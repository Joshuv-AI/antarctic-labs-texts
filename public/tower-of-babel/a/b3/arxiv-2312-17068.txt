Orbifolds and the modular curve
Juan Martín Pérez, Florent Schaffhauser

Abstract
. We provide an account of the construction of the moduli stack of elliptic curves as an analytic orbifold. While intimately linked to Thurston’s point of view on the subject (discrete groups acting properly and effectively on differentiable manifolds), the construction of the modular orbi-curve and its universal family of elliptic curves ends up requiring a bit more technology, in order to allow for non-effective actions.

Primary 14D23; Secondary 32G13

Introduction

Orbifolds were first introduced by Satake, under the name
V
-manifolds (
[
Satake_generalized_manifolds
]
), as a way to handle the
ramification
phenomena that occur in the general theory of group actions on manifolds. And it is generally accepted in the community that Satake chose that name in reference to the German word
Verzweigung
, which means ramification.
1
1

1

The second-named author believes he heard this explanation of the term in a talk by Professor Kaoru Ono, who asked Satake himself directly about it.
Now, ramification is a concept that is also present in the analytic or algebraic contexts, in particular to talk about morphisms that are
not étale
. And it is in fact remarkable that some of the concepts introduced by Satake and Thurston for differential-geometric purposes, namely orbifolds and their fundamental groups (
[
Satake_Gauss_Bonnet
,
Thurston_2002
]
), have natural algebro-geometric counterparts that can in turn be used to enrich our perspective and the scope of our methods in differential and analytic geometry. One of the goals of the present chapter is to illustrate this point of view through the example of the moduli space of elliptic curves.

It is well-known that elliptic curves can be classified by their
j
-invariant and this is the basis for the construction of the so-called modular curve, whose points parameterise isomorphism classes of elliptic curves (
[
McKean_Moll
]
). However, there are various reasons why we cannot quite content ourselves with the classical construction of this space. The main one, perhaps, being that the existence of a universal family of elliptic curves can only be obtained in the context of stacks, not manifolds. In this chapter, we will present a construction of the moduli stack of elliptic curves and show that it is in fact an analytic orbifold. This illustrates the need to go beyond the original definition of an orbifold because, in Thurston’s definition of the quotient orbifold
[X/G]
, the group
G
is supposed to act
effectively
on the manifold
X
, but to prove that the moduli stack of elliptic curves is analytic, one ends up describing it as the quotient stack
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
, where
\mathfrak{h}
is the upper half-plane in
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
acts on it by homographic transformations. This action is not effective, and in fact its kernel is precisely
\{\pm I_{2}\}
, so
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
acts effectively on
\mathfrak{h}
. While
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
are homeomorphic topological spaces, the quotient stacks
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
and
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
are not isomorphic, and only the first one is isomorphic to the moduli stack of elliptic curves, so the fact that
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is an orbifold in the sense of Thurston is not entirely satisfying here, even though it does get us the correct coarse moduli space. Resolving this issue by showing that
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
carries a canonical structure of non-effective orbifold is not quite good enough either, because of the difficulties that arise with the universal family. So the only way out of this seems to be considering the quotient stack
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
and showing that it is an orbifold in the stacky sense. Of course, there are deeper implications to this discussion, for instance the fact that the fundamental group of the moduli space of elliptic curves should be
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, not
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, because elliptic curves always have an Abelian group structure, so in particular an inversion map
x\longmapsto-x
, which is the map coming from the element
-I_{2}\in\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
acting trivially on
\mathfrak{h}
.

The example of the moduli space of elliptic curves illustrates how thinking about non-effective orbifolds as a particular class of stacks may be useful, even for classical problems. This suggests the need to go beyond Satake and Thurston’s original definitions, using the input from algebraic geometry to enrich the differential-geometric perspective on the notion of orbifold. Note that this is not the only occurrence of such a phenomenon. While no evidence suggests that the theory of the étale fundamental group of schemes may have been of inspiration to Thurston (except perhaps his construction of the universal cover as an inverse limit in
[
Thurston_2002
, Proposition 13.2.4, pp.305–307]
), the analogy is clear, and in fact illustrates a common trend between the two fields, which is to include more maps in the definition of a covering space in order to have a richer notion of fundamental group. Indeed, consider for instance the orthogonal projection from the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to the closed unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In the usual topology of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, this map is not a covering map (in fact, it is not even locally injective at points of the unit circle). But in algebraic geometry, it is the map induced on the set of closed points by the canonical projection
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}/\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
, which is an étale morphism of schemes (in particular, the étale fundamental group of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}
is not trivial, and since
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
is simply connected, it is in fact exactly
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
). If we view this example through the orbifold prism, the orbifold fundamental group, in the sense of Thurston, of the quotient of
S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
by the group generated by the reflection through the equator is indeed
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, just like in algebraic geometry. More generally, this leads to a richer notion of fundamental groups for all Klein surfaces (= quotients of Riemann surfaces by anti-holomorphic involutions), for which we refer for instance to
[
Sch_CRM
]
. In analytic and algebraic geometry too, it is useful to allow orbifolds: the fundamental group of the quotient of
\mathbb{C}
by the finite group
\mu_{n}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
acting by multiplication by a primitive
n
-th root of unity should be
\mu_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, not the trivial group. As a matter of fact, the canonical projection
\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}/\mu_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the typical case of a map which is ramified when seen as a morphism between smooth algebraic or analytic varieties (in which case it can be identified with the map
z\longmapsto z^{n}
), but which can be viewed as a covering map in the orbifold sense (see Example
3.9
).

Recall that a complex-analytic elliptic curve is a pair
(\mathscr{C},e)
where
\mathscr{C}
is a compact Riemann surface of genus
1
and
e
is a point in
\mathscr{C}
. A morphism between two elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1})
and
(\mathscr{C}_{2},e_{2})
is a holomorphic map
f:\mathscr{C}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}_{2}
such that
f(e_{1})=e_{2}
. We shall denote by
f:(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow(\mathscr{C}_{2},e_{2})
such a morphism. The main result that we wish to discuss in this chapter is stated as follows.

Theorem

(Theorem
5.23
)
.

The moduli stack of complex elliptic curves is a complex analytic stack, isomorphic to the orbifold
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})]
, where
\mathfrak{h}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \mathrm{Im}\,(z)>0\}
is the hyperbolic plane and

\mathbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}:a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z},\,ad-bc=1\right\}

is the so-called modular group, acting to the right on
\mathfrak{h}
by homographic transformations

z\cdot\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\,.

This moduli stack admits the (non-compact) Riemann surface
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{C}
as a coarse moduli space.

In Section
1
, we recall basic results on elliptic curves. Sections
2
and
3
contain the formal definition of a stack and an explanation of what it means for a stack defined on the category of complex analytic manifolds to be analytic, and to be an orbifold. In Sections
4
and
5
, we discuss the notion of moduli spaces for a complex analytic stack and we prove that the stack of elliptic curves is an analytic orbifold.

This chapter is entirely expository and makes no claims to originality: its main goal is to be self-contained enough in order to be useful to young researchers who are entering the field and are interested in the interactions between differential and algebraic geometry, all the while celebrating the vitality and scope of Thurston’s geometric vision.

1.
Complex elliptic curves

1.1.
Complex tori

Let us start by recalling the definition of a complex elliptic curve (in the analytic setting). We recall that the (geometric) genus of a compact connected Riemann surface
\mathscr{C}
is the dimension of the complex vector space
H^{0}(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}})
which, as
\mathscr{C}
is
1
-dimensional and compact, is the space of holomorphic
1
-forms on
\mathscr{C}
.

Definition 1.1
.

An analytic
complex elliptic curve
is a pair
(\mathscr{C},e)
where
\mathscr{C}
is a compact Riemann surface of genus
1
and
e
is a point in
\mathscr{C}
. A
morphism of elliptic curves
between
(\mathscr{C},e)
and
(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime})
is a holomorphic map
f:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
such that
f(e)=e^{\prime}
.

We now give the two sources of examples of complex elliptic curves that will be of interest to us: complex tori (Example
1.2
) and smooth plane cubics (Example
1.3
).

Example 1.2
.

Let us see
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
as a two-dimensional real vector space (equipped with its usual topology and additive group structure) and let
\Lambda
be a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
such that the quotient group
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
is compact (such a subgroup
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
will be called a (uniform)
lattice
). In particular, the group
\Lambda
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
2
. Note that there is a canonical marked point in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
in this case, namely
0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda
, the neutral element for the group law. Moreover, there is a (unique) Riemann surface structure on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
that makes the canonical projection
p:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
holomorphic. A holomorphic
1
-form on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
is necessarily closed, so it pulls back to an exact global
1
-form
f(z)\,dz
on the simply connected Riemann surface
\mathbb{C}
, with
f(z)
holomorphic and
\Lambda
-periodic (in the sense that, if
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
, then
f(z+\lambda)=f(z)
; this holds because
dz
is
\Lambda
-invariant for the action of
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{C}
by translation). Since
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
is compact, the holomorphic function
f
has to be bounded, therefore constant by Liouville’s theorem, and this proves that the space of holomorphic
1-
forms on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
has complex dimension 1, hence that we have constructed a complex elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda)
, which we will refer to as a (
1
-dimensional)
complex torus
.

The complex tori of Example
1.2
can be embedded onto non-singular complex projective sets using the Weierstrass
\wp
-function of the lattice
\Lambda
, namely

\wp_{\Lambda}(w)=\frac{1}{w^{2}}+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{(w-\lambda)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)

which, if we pick a basis
(\lambda,\mu)
of
\Lambda
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module, satisfies the equation

(1.1)

(\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(w))^{2}=f_{(\lambda,\mu)}\big(\wp_{\Lambda}(w)\big),

where

f_{(\lambda,\mu)}(x)=4(x-v_{1})(x-v_{2})(x-v_{3})

with

v_{1}=\wp_{\Lambda}\left(\textstyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{C},v_{2}=\wp_{\Lambda}\left(\textstyle\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{C}\ \text{and}\ v_{3}=\wp_{\Lambda}\left(\textstyle\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{C}\,,

thus inducing a holomorphic map

(1.2)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{C}/\Lambda&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{2}\\
w\text{ mod }\Lambda&\longmapsto&\left[\wp_{\Lambda}(w):\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(w):1\right]\end{array}

whose image, because of Equation (
1.1
) and up to normalisation of the basis
(\lambda,\mu)
, is a smooth projective curve of the type described in Equation (
1.3
).

Example 1.3
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
and consider the smooth projective curve

(1.3)

\mathscr{C}_{u}:=\left\{[x:y:z]\in\mathbb{CP}^{2}\ |\ y^{2}z=x(x-z)(x-uz)\right\}.

By the genus-degree formula, the smooth cubic
\mathscr{C}_{u}
has genus
g=\frac{(3-1)(3-2)}{2}=1
. And since the point
e:=[0:0:1]
belongs to
\mathscr{C}_{u}
for all
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
, we have defined a family of elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C}_{u},e)_{u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}}
, depending on the parameter
u
. This family is called the
Legendre family
(see also Example
5.2
).

Building on Example
1.3
, let us set
f_{u}(x)=x(x-1)(x-u)
. The classical theory of elliptic curves says that
\mathscr{C}_{u}
is a compactification of the plane cubic of equation
y^{2}=f_{u}(x)
. Moreover, the map
(x,y)\longmapsto x
induces a ramified double covering
p:\mathscr{C}_{u}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}
, with four branch points, so an application of the Riemann-Hurwitz formula shows that
\mathscr{C}_{u}
is homeomorphic to
S^{1}\times S^{1}
. If we pick a square root of
f_{u}
, the differential form
\frac{dt}{\sqrt{f_{u}(t)}}
on
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}
pulls back to a holomorphic
1
-form
\omega_{u}:=p^{*}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{f_{u}(t)}}
that can be extended holomorphically to
\mathscr{C}_{u}
. Then we can integrate this
1
-form along loops based at
e\in\mathscr{C}_{u}
and, since
\omega_{u}
is closed, obtain a group morphism

(1.4)

\Phi_{\omega_{u}}:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{u},e)&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}\\
\left[\gamma\right]&\longmapsto&\int_{\gamma}\omega_{u}\end{array}

whose image
\Lambda_{\omega_{u}}:=\mathrm{Im}\,(\Phi_{\omega_{u}})
is in fact a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
, called the
period lattice
. This induces a holomorphic map

(1.5)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathscr{C}_{u}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega_{u}}\\
x&\longmapsto&\int_{e}^{x}\omega_{u}\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega_{u}}\end{array}

which is an isomorphism of elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C}_{u},e)\simeq(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega_{u}},0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega_{u}})
. Note that if we choose a different non-zero holomorphic
1
-form
\omega
on
\mathscr{C}_{u}
, it will satisfy
\omega=\alpha\omega_{u}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, because the space of such forms is
1
-dimensional. Consequently, the lattices
\Lambda_{\omega_{u}}
and
\Lambda_{\omega}
satisfy
\Lambda_{\omega}=\alpha\Lambda_{\omega_{u}}
, so multiplication by
\alpha
in
\mathbb{C}
induces an isomorphism of elliptic curves
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega_{u}},0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega_{u}})\simeq(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega},0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega})
.

So, from the existence of the projective embedding (
1.2
) and the isomorphism (
1.5
), we see that there is a bijection

\big\{1\text{-dimensional complex tori}\big\}\,\big/\,\text{isomorphism}\simeq\big\{\text{smooth plane cubics}\big\}\,\big/\,\text{isomorphism}\,.

And as a matter of fact, every elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
in the sense of Definition
1.1
is of this form, meaning that it is isomorphic to a one-dimensional complex torus. One way to show this is to generalise the construction of the period subgroup (
1.4
) to the abstract setting: choose a generator
\omega
of the
1
-dimensional complex vector space
H^{0}(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}})
and show that the image of the group morphism
\Phi_{\omega}
is a lattice
\Lambda_{\omega}\subset\mathbb{C}
and that there is an isomorphism of elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C},e)\simeq(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega},0\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega})
. The issue here is that, with our definition of the genus, we do not yet know that
\mathscr{C}
is homeomorphic to
S^{1}\times S^{1}
, which makes it harder to prove that the period subgroup is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
. The way to solve this difficulty is to use Abel’s theorem, which gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a Weil divisor
D
on a compact Riemann surface
\mathscr{C}
to be a principal divisor, i.e. for
D
to be the divisor of zeros and poles of a meromorphic function on
\mathscr{C}
. More precisely, one can follow the approach in
[
Forster
, pp.163–173]
to show the following:

(1)

A Weil divisor
D\in\mathrm{Div}(\mathscr{C})
is the divisor of zeros and poles of a meromorphic function
f
on
\mathscr{C}
if and only if
D
is of degree
0
and there exists a chain
c\in C_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})
such that
\partial c=D
and
\int_{c}\omega=0
(Abel’s theorem in the
g=1
case,
[
Forster
, Theorem 20.7, p.163]
).

(2)

The period subgroup
\Lambda_{\omega}:=\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi_{\omega}\subset\mathbb{C}
is a lattice
[
Forster
, Theorem 21.4, p.168]
. This uses the previously stated version of Abel’s theorem, to prove that
\Lambda_{\omega}
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
.

One gets in this way a
1
-dimensional complex torus
\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega}
, namely the Jacobian variety of the elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
. It depends on
\omega
, but if we choose a different basis
\alpha\omega
of the complex vector space
H^{0}(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}})
, we obtain an isomorphic complex torus, as we have already seen. Moreover, the Riemann-Roch theorem has the following consequence.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e)
be an elliptic curve and let
\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathrm{Div}_{0}(\mathscr{C})/\mathrm{Div}_{P}(\mathscr{C})
be the group of degree
0
divisors modulo principal divisors. Then the map

\Psi:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathscr{C}&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C})\\
x&\longmapsto&[x]-[e]\end{array}

is bijective.

Proof.

The map
\Psi
is injective because, if
\Psi(x)=\Psi(x^{\prime})
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
, then the Weil divisor
[x]-[x^{\prime}]
would be principal, so there would exist a meromorphic function
f
on
\mathscr{C}
with exactly one pole, of order
1
, and the corresponding holomorphic map
f:\mathscr{C} \longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}
would be an isomorphism, contradicting the fact that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}})=1
.
Moreover, the map
\Psi
is surjective because every divisor
D
of degree
0
on
\mathscr{C}
is equivalent, modulo a principal divisor, to a divisor of the form
[x]-[e]
. Indeed, if we set
D^{\prime}=D+[e]
, then
\deg(D^{\prime})=\deg(D)+1=1
so, by the Riemann-Roch theorem,
\dim H^{0}(\mathscr{C};O_{\mathscr{C}}(D^{\prime}))\geqslant\deg(D^{\prime})=1
, meaning that there exists a non-zero meromorphic function
f
on
\mathscr{C}
whose poles are bounded by
D^{\prime}
, i.e. in terms of divisors,
(f)+D^{\prime}\geqslant 0
. Since
(f)+D^{\prime}
is an effective Weil divisor of degree
1
, there exists a point
x
in
\mathscr{C}
such that
(f)+D^{\prime}=[x]
, hence
D=D^{\prime}-[e]\sim_{(f)}[x]- [e]
.
∎

Remark 1.5
.

Lemma
1.4
shows in particular that an elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
has a natural structure of Abelian group, induced by the isomorphism
\Psi:(\mathscr{C},e)\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}(\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C}),0)
and the group structure on
\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C})
. In particular, an elliptic curve always has non-trivial automorphisms (namely the inversion map
D\longmapsto-D
, seen as a morphism of marked Riemann surfaces).

Finally, one can deduce from the above that a complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
is isomorphic to a complex torus, namely the Jacobian variety
\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega}
, defined by the period lattice. This proves in particular that
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C},e)\simeq\Lambda_{\omega}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. So we see from the classification of compact connected orientable surfaces that
\mathscr{C}
is homeomorphic to
S^{1}\times S^{1}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e)
be a complex elliptic curve and let
\omega
be a non-zero holomorphic
1
-form on
\mathscr{C}
. Let
\Lambda_{\omega}\subset\mathbb{C}
be the period lattice and let
\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega}
be the associated complex torus, i.e. the Jacobian variety of
(\mathscr{C},e)
. Then the map

F:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathscr{C}&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C})=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\omega}\\
x&\longmapsto&\int_{e}^{x}\omega\ \mathrm{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega}\end{array}

is an isomorphism of elliptic curves.

We refer to
[
Forster
, Theorems 21.7 p.171 and 21.10 p.172]
for a complete proof of Theorem
1.6
. Note that the map
F:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C})
is the composition of the map
\Psi:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C})
of Lemma
1.4
with the map

J:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C})&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C})\\
D=\partial c&\longmapsto&\int_{c}\omega\ \text{mod}\ \Lambda_{\omega}\end{array}

which is well-defined by definition of the period lattice (i.e. independent of the choice of the chain
c\in C_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})
such that
\partial c=D
), and injective in view of Abel’s theorem. The surjectivity of
J
is proven in
[
Forster
, Theorem 21.7 p.171]
and for the fact that the map
F:\mathscr{C}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Jac}(\mathscr{C})
is holomorphic, we refer to
[
Forster
, Theorem 21.4 p.168]
.

As an application, Theorem
1.6
gives us the structure of the automorphism group of a complex elliptic curve and of a compact Riemann surface of genus
1
without marked point. To obtain it, one just needs to observe that two complex tori
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{1}
and
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{2}
are isomorphic as complex elliptic curves if and only if there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
\alpha\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
, which can be deduced by lifting such an isomorphism to the universal cover
\mathbb{C}
of these tori and using the classification of automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, the automorphism group of the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0)
can be identified with the group of all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
\alpha\Lambda=\Lambda
. As
\Lambda
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
, the set of such
\alpha
is in fact a discrete subgroup of the circle group
S^{1}
, hence a finite rotation group, generated by a root of unity. If we do not ask to preserve the base point, then translations by an element
[z]\in\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
also induce automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e)
be a complex elliptic curve. Then there exists a natural number
n\geqslant 1
such that the automorphism group of the marked Riemann surface
(\mathscr{C},e)
is

(1.6)

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C},e)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.

Moreover, the automorphism group of the Riemann surface
\mathscr{C}
is the semi-direct product

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C})\simeq\mathscr{C}\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C},e)

where the group
\mathscr{C}\simeq\mathrm{Pic}_{0}(\mathscr{C})
acts on itself by translations and the structure of semi-direct product is given by the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C},e)
on
\mathscr{C}
. In particular, the group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C})
is a
1
-dimensional compact analytic Lie group over
\mathbb{C}
, whose identity component is isomorphic to
\mathscr{C}
.

Remark 1.8
.

We will see in Corollary
1.18
that, in fact, the natural number
n
in Equation (
1.6
) can only be
2
,
4
or
6
(never
1
!). In particular, we will give an alternate, more detailed proof of Corollary
1.7
.

So the conclusion we can draw from the present paragraph is two-folded: firstly, the classification of complex elliptic curves up to isomorphism can be reduced to the classification of complex tori, and secondly, such objects have non-trivial automorphisms as marked Riemann surfaces. As a consequence, even though we can always find, given a complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
, an isomorphism of elliptic curves with a complex torus
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
, this isomorphism will never be unique, because we can compose it with an automorphism of
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
. This is one of the reasons why constructing a moduli space of complex elliptic curves requires additional notions, namely framings, in order to get rid of non-trivial automorphisms.

1.2.
Framed elliptic curves

When faced with a moduli problem for objects that have non-trivial automorphisms, an idea that has proven useful is to add an extra structure to these objects. This is because the corresponding notion of isomorphism will then be more restrictive (since the extra structure needs to be preserved, too), so in particular there will be fewer automorphisms. In the best case scenario, there is a group action on these objects with extra structure, and two such objects with extra structure lie in the same orbit if and only if they are isomorphic as objects without extra structure, so the moduli space of objects without extra structure can be constructed as a quotient of the moduli space of objects with extra structure (provided the latter moduli space actually exists, of course). We will now illustrate this more concretely in the case of elliptic curves. We begin with the definition of an extra structure.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e)
be a complex elliptic curve. A
framing
of
(\mathscr{C},e)
is a basis
([a],[b])
of the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
H_{1}(\mathscr{C},\mathbb{Z})\simeq\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C},e)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with the additional property that

(1.7)

\text{for all non-zero }\omega\in H^{0}\big(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}}\big),\ \mathrm{Im}\,\textstyle\frac{\int_{a}\omega}{\int_{b}\omega}>0\,.

Since
H^{0}(\mathscr{C};\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}})
is one-dimensional by assumption, it suffices to test Condition (
1.7
) on a single non-zero holomorphic
1
-form
\omega
. A morphism of framed elliptic curves

f:\big(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b])\big)\longrightarrow\big(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime},([a^{\prime}],[b^{\prime}])\big)

is a morphism of elliptic curves
f:(\mathscr{C},e)\longrightarrow(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime})
such that
f_{*}([a])=[a^{\prime}]
and
f_{*}[b]=[b^{\prime}]
, where
f_{*}:H_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow H_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})
is the group morphism induced by
f
in homology.

Example 1.10
.

If
(\mathscr{C},e)=(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0)
for some lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
and
(\lambda,\mu)
is a basis of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda
that satisfies the additional positivity condition
\mathrm{Im}\,\frac{\mu}{\lambda}>0
, then
(\lambda,\mu)
defines a framing of
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0)
by letting
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\mu}
be the
1
-cycles in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
corresponding to the paths
t\longmapsto t\lambda
and
t\longmapsto t\mu
in
\mathbb{C}
. A lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
equipped with such a
direct basis

(\lambda,\mu)
is also called a
framed lattice
, and we shall sometimes identify a framing of
\Lambda
in this sense with the induced framing of
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
. So, when we write
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0,(\lambda,\mu))
, we mean the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0,(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\mu}))
. Note that if a non-zero holomorphic
1
-form
\omega
on
\mathscr{C}
is fixed, then we can define
(\lambda,\mu)
from
([a],[b])
by setting
\lambda:=\int_{a}\omega
and
\mu:=\int_{b}\omega
, but without such an
\omega
, we cannot recover
(\lambda,\mu)
from
(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\mu})
. Regardless, if
\Lambda^{\prime}=\alpha\Lambda
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, then the map
z\longmapsto\alpha z
induces an isomorphism of framed elliptic curves

\big(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0,(\lambda,\mu)\big)\simeq\big(\mathbb{C}/(\alpha\Lambda),0,(\alpha\lambda,\alpha\mu)\big).

In particular, if we set
\tau:=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}
, then
\tau
is a complex number with positive imaginary part, i.e.
\mathrm{Im}\,(\tau)>0
, and there is an associated lattice
\Lambda(\tau):=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}\tau\subset\mathbb{C}
with direct basis
(1,\tau)
such that the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda,0,(\lambda,\mu))
is isomorphic to the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
.

In view of Theorem
1.6
and Example
1.10
, a framed complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
is isomorphic to a framed complex torus of the form
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
where

\tau\in\mathfrak{h}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \mathrm{Im}\,(z)>0\}.

Indeed, it suffices to show that a complex elliptic curve admits a framing, which comes from the simple observation that if a basis
([a],[b])
for
H_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})
does not satisfy the positivity condition (
1.7
), then the basis
([b],[a])
does. Note that, a priori, given a framed elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
, there could be more than one
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
such that
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))\simeq(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
. But Lemma
1.11
shows that this is in fact not the case. In particular, a framed elliptic curve of the form
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
has no non-trivial automorphisms.

Lemma 1.11
.

The framed complex tori
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
and
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau^{\prime}),0,(1,\tau^{\prime}))
are isomorphic if and only if
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
.

Proof.

An isomorphism between such complex tori is induced by
z\longmapsto\alpha z
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, which respects the framing if and only if
\alpha=1
and
\tau^{\prime}=\tau
.
∎

Remark 1.12
.

It is not entirely apparent from the proof, but the positivity condition
\mathrm{Im}\,(\tau)>0
is necessary if we want a uniqueness result such as the one in Lemma
1.11
to hold. Indeed, if we had defined a framing of
\Lambda
simply as a basis of the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda
, then, even if we normalise the lattice so that the first vector in the basis be equal to
1
, two normalised framings of the complex torus
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
do not have to be equal: of the two bases
(1,\tau)
and
(\tau,1)\sim\big(1,\frac{1}{\tau}\big)
of the lattice
\Lambda(\tau)=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}\tau=\mathbb{Z}\tau\oplus\mathbb{Z}1
, only the first one satisfies the required positivity condition.

We then have the following classification result for framed elliptic curves, whose proof follows directly from the discussion above and Lemma
1.11
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
be a framed elliptic curve. Then there exists a unique

\tau\in\mathfrak{h}=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \mathrm{Im}\,(\tau)>0\big\}

and a unique isomorphism of framed elliptic curves

\big(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b])\big)\simeq\big(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau)\big).

In particular, framed elliptic curves have no non-trivial automorphisms:

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))=\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathscr{C}}\}.

As a consequence, the points of the Riemann surface

\mathfrak{h}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \mathrm{Im}\,(z)>0\}

represent isomorphism classes of framed elliptic curves. This parameter space of framed elliptic curves is called the
Teichmüller space
of elliptic curves, for reasons that will be explained next. Note that Theorem
1.13
constitutes an improvement on Theorem
1.6
, since we can now compare two given elliptic curves in a more formal sense because (at least when they are framed) these elliptic curves are represented by points
of a same space
, namely the upper half-plane
\mathfrak{h}
. The next step is to ask ourselves: when are two framed elliptic curves isomorphic as plain elliptic curves? This corresponds to forgetting the framing, so answering this question should also give us a sense of what the possible framings are on a given elliptic curve. More precisely, we will construct the set of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves as a quotient of the Teichmüller space
\mathfrak{h}
, so it will come equipped with a map from
\mathfrak{h}
to it, and the fibres of that map will be in bijection with the possible framings (the map in question being the one that forgets the frame on a given framed elliptic curve).

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
and
(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime},([a^{\prime}],[b^{\prime}]))
be framed elliptic curves and let
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
be the corresponding points in the Teichmüller space of elliptic curves. Then the unframed elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C},e)
and
(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime})
are isomorphic as elliptic curves if and only if there exists a matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})\text{ such that }\tau^{\prime}=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\,,

meaning that
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are related by a direct Möbius transformation with integral coefficients.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
1.13
, the framed elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
and
(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime},([a^{\prime}],[b^{\prime}]))
can be replaced by the framed elliptic curves
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
and
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau^{\prime}),0,(1,\tau^{\prime}))
and the latter are isomorphic as unframed elliptic curves if and only if there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
\alpha\Lambda(\tau)=\Lambda(\tau^{\prime})=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}\tau^{\prime}
. So there exist
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\tau^{\prime}=\alpha(a\tau+b)
and
1=\alpha(c\tau+d)
. This implies that

\alpha=\frac{1}{c\tau+d}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{\prime}=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\,.

Applying the same argument to
\Lambda(\tau)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\Lambda(\tau^{\prime})
, we see that the (Möbius) transformation
z\longmapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
is bijective, so the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}
is invertible, therefore has determinant
\pm 1\in\mathbb{Z}
. And since it sends
\tau
such that
\mathrm{Im}\,(\tau)>0
to
\tau^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{Im}\,(\tau^{\prime})>0
, it must have determinant
+1
.
∎

So the framed elliptic curves
(\mathscr{C},e,([a],[b]))
and
(\mathscr{C}^{\prime},e^{\prime},([a^{\prime}],[b^{\prime}]))
become isomorphic when forgetting the frame if and only if the points
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
lie in a same orbit of the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action on
\mathfrak{h}
defined by

(1.8)

z\cdot\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\,.

Note that this is a
right action
. Thanks to this, we can construct a set whose points are in bijection with
isomorphic classes of elliptic curves.

Corollary 1.15
.

The quotient set
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is the set of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves and we have a forgetful map

F:\mathfrak{h}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})

sending the isomorphism class of the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
to the isomorphism class of the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
. In particular, the orbit of a point
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
under the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action (
1.8
) is in bijection with the set of all framings on the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
, and the stabiliser group of
\tau
in
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the automorphism group of the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
.

1.3.
The modular curve

The
modular curve
is the quotient
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, of the Teichmüller space
\mathfrak{h}
by the action of the
modular group

\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. Note that Corollary
1.15
tells us that this quotient set should be the underlying set of the moduli space of elliptic curves.

In this section, we recall what the canonical structure of Riemann surface on
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is. But first, the mere fact that it is a quotient set already gives us insight into the classification of complex elliptic curves: by studying the action of the modular group on the Teichmüller space (stabilisers and fundamental domain), we can determine what the possible automorphism groups of complex elliptic curves are, and what the quotient may be as a Riemann surface, which turns out to be intimately related to the structure of the modular group itself. Note for instance that the centre of the modular group is the subgroup
\{\pm I_{2}\}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and that this subgroup acts trivially on the Teichmüller space. So every elliptic curve admits an order two automorphism. This is already apparent in Theorem
1.6
, which endows every Riemann surface of genus
1
with a marked point with a (non-canonical) structure of Abelian group: the associated order-two automorphism is just the inversion map
D\longmapsto(-D)
in this Abelian group. And if the elliptic curve is given by a smooth cubic equation
y^{2}z=f(x,z)
in the projective plane, then the involution is induced by the involution
[x:y:z]\longmapsto[x:(-y):z]
of
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{2}
. This also shows that the action of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathfrak{h}
is not faithful, as the centre of the modular group acts trivially on
\mathfrak{h}
. Hence an induced action of
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z}):=\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})/\{\pm I_{2}\}
, which turns out to be faithful. The important observation here is that the orbits of the
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action are the same as those of the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action but the stabilisers are not the same. In particular, the
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-stabiliser of a point
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
is
not
isomorphic to the automorphism group of the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
. The following result is classical (see for instance
[
Serre73
, Chapter VII.1 pp.77–79]
or
[
KConradSL2
]
for an exposition).

Theorem 1.16
.

The modular group
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is generated by the matrices

S=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&-1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad T=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\,,

hence also by the matrices
S
and
R:=ST=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&-1\\
1&1\end{array}\right)
. This induces the following presentations of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
by generators and relations:

\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})\ \simeq\ \big\langle\,S,R\ \big|\ S^{2}=R^{3},\ S^{4}=R^{6}=1\,\big\rangle\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\ast_{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}

and

\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})\ \simeq\ \big\langle\,[S],[R]\ \big|\ [S]^{2}=[R]^{3}=1\big\rangle\ \simeq\ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ast\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}.

Theorem
1.16
is about the algebraic structure of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. The matrices
S
and
R
show that this group is generated by an element of order four and an element of order six, which themselves satisfy the relation
S^{2}=R^{3}
(equal to
-I_{2}
). So
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is the amalgamated coproduct of the two Abelian groups
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, with the non-trivial element of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
being sent to
S^{2}
in
\langle S\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
and to
R^{3}
in
\langle R\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
. As a consequence, the group
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})/\{\pm I_{2}\}
is generated by the classes
[S]
and
[R]
, which are respectively of order
2
and
3
with no other relations between them, so
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is the coproduct of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
. This implies for instance that a character
\chi:\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
has image contained in the subgroup generated by a fourth root of unity and a sixth root of unity, so
\mathrm{Im}\,\chi\subset\mu_{12}(\mathbb{C})
. Geometrically, though, it is the action of the generators
S
and
T
that is the most convenient to visualise. Indeed,
S
acts on
\mathfrak{h}
via the Möbius transformation
[S]:z\longmapsto-\textstyle\frac{1}{z}
, which is a direct rotation of angle
\frac{\pi}{2}
about
i
, and
T
acts via
[T]:z\longmapsto z+1
, which is a translation. As a consequence,
R=ST
acts via
[R]:z\longmapsto\frac{1}{1-z}
, which is a rotation of angle
\frac{\pi}{3}
about
e^{i\pi/3}
. This leads to the usual construction of a fundamental domain for the action of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
(or equivalently
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, since the orbits are the same) on
\mathfrak{h}
.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let

\mathcal{F}:=\big\{\tau\in\mathfrak{h}\ |\ |\tau|\geqslant 1\text{ and }\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\mathrm{Re}\,(\tau)\leqslant\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\big\}\,.

Then the following properties hold:

(1)

Every
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-orbit in
\mathfrak{h}
intersects
\mathcal{F}
in at least one point and at most two points.

(2)

If an orbit intersects
\mathcal{F}
in two points, then these two points lie on the topological boundary
\partial\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{F}
.

(3)

The points of
\mathcal{F}
with non-trivial stabiliser in
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
are the points
i=e^{i\pi/2}
, whose stabiliser is the cyclic group of order
4
generated by
S
, and the points
j=e^{i2\pi/3}
and
-j^{2}=-\frac{1}{j}=e^{i\pi/3}=j\cdot T
, whose stabilisers are cyclic groups of order
6
, generated respectively by
TRT^{-1}=TS
and
R
.

Note that the point at infinity of
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
may be added to
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathbb{C}
and that the action of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathfrak{h}
by biholomorphisms extends to a conformal action on
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}:=\mathfrak{h}\cup\{\infty\}
. The stabiliser of the point at infinity is then the infinite cyclic group generated by
T
. Algebraically, the point at infinity no longer corresponds to an elliptic curve (smooth plane cubic with a marked point) but to a nodal plane cubic with a marked point (given in an affine chart by an equation of the form
y^{2}=x^{2}(x-1)
).

Corollary 1.18
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{F}
and let
\Lambda(\tau):=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}\tau
be the associated lattice in
\mathbb{C}
. Let
G:=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
be the automorphism group of the elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0)
. Then the following properties hold:

(1)

If
\tau\neq i,j,-j^{2}
, then
G\simeq\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

If
\tau=i
, then
G\simeq\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

If
\tau=j
or
-j^{2}
, then
G\simeq\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
.

The shape of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}=
gives us an indication of what the quotient space
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
might look like: a topological space homeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
, and this identification can be used to put a Riemann surface structure on
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. Thinking about
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
in terms of
\mathcal{F}
also suggests that there are three special points, namely
i
,
j
and
-j^{2}
, which are the points with non-trivial stabiliser in
\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, and this makes
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
an orbifold in the sense of Thurston. We will now show that
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{PSL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is also the quotient of a Riemann surface by the action of a
finite
group (Theorem
1.20
).

Remark 1.19
.

We will recall in Corollary
3.15
why
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
acts properly on
\mathfrak{h}
. By Theorem
1.16
, the group
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is a finitely generated subgroup of
\mathbf{GL}(2;\mathbb{C})
. So, by Selberg’s Lemma, it contains a torsion-free normal subgroup of finite index, say
\pi\lhd\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. The discrete group
\pi
, being a subgroup of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
, acts properly on
\mathfrak{h}
. Since
\pi
is torsion-free, it must therefore act freely on
\mathfrak{h}
(see Theorems
3.11
and
3.12
for further details on that). So the canonical projection
\mathfrak{h}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}/\pi=:\tilde{M}
is an analytic covering map between Riemann surfaces, and the topological space
M:=\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
is homeomorphic to
\tilde{M}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma:=\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})/\pi
is a finite group. A priori, there could exist several pairs
(\tilde{M},\Gamma)
consisting of a Riemann surface
\tilde{M}
and a finite group
\Gamma
acting on
M
by automorphisms, such that
\tilde{M}/\Gamma\simeq M
. An explicit such presentation is given in Theorem
1.20
.

Theorem 1.20
.

The set
M:=\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves is in bijection with the set
\tilde{M}/\Gamma
where
\tilde{M}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}
and
\Gamma
is the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{3}=\left\langle\sigma,\tau\right\rangle
, generated by the permutations
\sigma=(1\ 2)
and
\tau=(1\ 3)
and acting to the right on
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}
via
f_{\sigma}(u):=(1-u)
and
f_{\tau}(u):=\textstyle\frac{1}{u}\,
.

Proof.

As seen after Example
1.2
, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
can be embedded onto the smooth plane cubic of equation

y^{2}=4(x-v_{1}z)(x-v_{2}z)(x-v_{3}z)

in
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{2}
, with pairwise distinct coefficients

v_{1}=\wp_{\Lambda(\tau)}\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\right),\ v_{2}=\wp_{\Lambda(\tau)}\left(\textstyle\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\ \text{and}\ v_{3}=\wp_{\Lambda(\tau)}\left(\textstyle\frac{1+\tau}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{C}\,.

Let us set
u:=\textstyle\frac{v_{3}-v_{2}}{v_{1}-v_{2}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}
. This corresponds to sending
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
to
(1,0,u)
via the homographic transformation
z\longmapsto\textstyle\frac{z-v_{2}}{v_{1}-v_{2}}
, preserving
\infty\in\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}
. So we may also view
u
as a point of
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}\setminus\{0;1;\infty\}
, with limit cases
u=0,1,\infty
corresponding respectively to
v_{3}=v_{2}
,
v_{3}=v_{1}
and
v_{1}=v_{2}
. The point is that, if
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are tranposed by
\sigma=(1\ 2)\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}
, then
u
is changed to
f_{\sigma}(u)=1-u
, and if
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
are transposed by
\tau=(1\ 3)\in\mathfrak{S}_{3}
, then
u
is changed to
f_{\tau}(u)=\textstyle \frac{1}{u}
. In fact, the group
\left\langle f_{\sigma},f_{\tau}\right\rangle
is the group of automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}
which preserve the subset
\{0;1;\infty\}
. Note that this defines a right action of
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
on
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}\setminus\{0;1;\infty\}
because
f_{\sigma\tau}=f_{\tau}\circ f_{\sigma}
. Moreover, if
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
, then the corresponding
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{1}\setminus\{0;1;\infty\}
lies in the same
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-orbit as
u
if
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau)\simeq\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau^{\prime})
as elliptic curves. In view of Theorem
1.14
, this occurs if and only if
\tau^{\prime}=\textstyle \frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
for some
\textstyle\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. Thus, we have indeed proved that

(1.9)

\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})\simeq\big(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}\big)/\mathfrak{S}_{3}\,.

∎

Remark 1.21
.

The parameter
u\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}
that appears in the proof of Theorem

coincides with the parameter

in the Legendre family of Example

.

Based on the bijection (

), we can identify this set with the Riemann surface

in two equivalent ways. Either by defining an

-invariant surjection

whose fibres are precisely the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-orbits in

, or by defining a
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-invariant surjection
\hat{\lambda}:\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
whose fibres are precisely the
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
-orbits in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}
. The first method induces a bijection

sending the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-orbit of

to the

-invariant of the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
. The latter is constructed via the theory of modular forms and defined by converting Equation (
1.1
) into the equation

\wp^{\prime 2}(z)=4\wp^{3}(z)-g_{2}(\tau)\wp(z)-g_{3}(\tau)

where
g_{2}(\tau)=60G_{4}(\tau)
,
g_{3}(\tau)=140G_{6}(\tau)
and
G_{k}(\tau)
is the Eisenstein series

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},(m,n)\neq(0,0)}\frac{1}{(m\tau+n)^{k}}\,,

then setting
j(\tau)=\textstyle\frac{1728\,g_{2}(\tau)^{3}}{g_{2}(\tau)^{3}-27g_{3}(\tau)^{2}}
. For the former approach and the definition of a bijection

, recall that we want a surjective map
\hat{\lambda}:\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0;1\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
with generic fibres having six elements, and that is invariant under the transformations

and
f_{\tau}:u\longmapsto\textstyle\frac{1}{u}
. This leads to
\hat{\lambda}(u):=\textstyle \frac{(1+u(u-1))^{3}}{(u(u-1))^{2}}
, whose numerator is a polynomial of degree

which is non-zero when
u=0
or
u=1
. It can be checked that, indeed,
\hat{\lambda}\circ f_{\sigma}=\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\lambda}\circ f_{\tau}=\hat{\lambda}
. Finally, note that if we reduce the cubic equation

to an equation of the form

, so the

-invariant of the elliptic curve defined by the Legendre equation

is equal to

.

2.
Stacks

2.1.
Prestacks

Prestacks formalise the notion of
families
of geometric objects by building upon the intuition that a family is a special kind of map, one that you should be able to pull back. For instance, if a group
G
acts on a space
X
, there should be:

(1)

For all space
S
, a notion of family of
G
-orbits in
X
, parameterised by
S
, and a notion of isomorphism of such families.

(2)

For all morphism
f:T\longrightarrow S
and all family
P
parameterised by
S
, a family
f^{*}P
parameterised by
T
, such that there are natural isomorphisms
\mathrm{id}_{S}^{*}P\simeq P
and
(f\circ g)^{*}P\simeq g^{*}(f^{*}P)
for all morphism
g:U\longrightarrow T
.

.

If

is a Lie group acting by automorphisms on a manifold

where

-bundle on

-orbits in

-orbit in

in

. Moreover, if

is a morphism, then

-bundle on

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}

Example 2.3
.

A manifold

defines a prestack, denoted by
\underline{X}
, by setting
\underline{X}(S):=\mathrm{Mor}(S,X)=\{u:S\longrightarrow X\}
, with morphisms between

and

given by

\mathrm{Mor}_{\underline{X}(S)}(u,u^{\prime}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{\mathrm{id}_{S}\}&\text{if}\ u=u^{\prime}\,,\\
\emptyset&\text{if}\ u\neq u^{\prime}\,.\end{array}\right.

from manifolds to stacks. A prestack

is called a

. By Lemma

, a manifold

is defined up to canonical isomorphism.

2.2.
Stacks

There is an obvious analogy between Definition
2.2
and that of a presheaf on
\mathcal{A}n
. Based on that analogy, we can define stacks as prestacks that satisfy a descent condition with respect to open coverings of objects
S\in\mathcal{A}n
. The only subtlety is that we have to glue not only morphisms but also objects. In what follows, given a prestack
\mathcal{M}
on
\mathcal{A}n
, a morphism
f:T\longrightarrow S
in
\mathcal{A}n
, and a morphism
\varphi:A\longrightarrow B
in
\mathcal{M}(S)
, we denote by
A|_{T}
and
B|_{T}
the objects
f^{*}A
and
f^{*}B
of
\mathcal{M}(T)
, and by
\varphi|_{T}:A|_{T}\longrightarrow B|_{T}
the morphism
f_{\mathcal{M}}^{*}\varphi
in
\mathcal{M}(T)
.

Definition 2.7
.

A
stack
on
\mathcal{A}n
is a prestack
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{A}n^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow\text{Groupoids}
such that, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
and all open covering
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
of
S
, the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

(Gluing of morphisms). For all pair of objects
A,B\in\mathcal{M}(S)
and all family of morphisms

\varphi_{i}:A|_{S_{i}}\longrightarrow B|_{S_{i}}\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{M}(S_{i})

such that, for all
i,j
in
I
,
\varphi|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}=\varphi_{j}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}
, there exists a unique morphism
\varphi:A\longrightarrow B
in
\mathcal{M}(S)
such that, for all
i\in I
,
\varphi|_{S_{i}}=\varphi_{i}
.

(2)

(Gluing of objects). For all family of objects
(A_{i})_{i\in I}\in\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{M}(S_{i})
equipped with isomorphisms

\varphi_{ij}:A_{j}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}A_{i}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{M}(S_{i}\cap S_{j})

satisfying the cocycle conditions
\varphi_{ii}=\mathrm{id}_{A_{i}}
and
\varphi_{ij}\circ\varphi_{jk}=\varphi_{ik}
in
\mathcal{M}(S_{i}\cap S_{j}\cap S_{k})
for all triple
(i,j,k)
, there exists an object
A\in\mathcal{M}(S)
together with isomorphisms
\varphi_{i}:A|_{S_{i}}\longrightarrow A_{i}
in
\mathcal{M}(S_{i})
, such that, for all
(i,j)
, one has
\varphi_{i}\circ\varphi_{j}^{-1}=\varphi_{ij}
in
\mathcal{M}(S_{i}\cap S_{j})
.

A morphism of stacks
F:\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}
is a morphism of prestacks from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{N}
. We shall denote by
\mathcal{S}t
the
2
-category of stacks.

Remark 2.8
.

It is perhaps not easy to note at first, but an important feature of the definition of stacks is that objects are required to glue but without any requirement of uniqueness: in Point (2) of Definition
2.7
, the object
A
constructed from the
A_{i}
is “unique up to isomorphism”, by a direct application of Condition (1), but the isomorphisms
\varphi_{i}:A|_{S_{i}}\longrightarrow A_{i}
are not unique, which is the familiar condition from the theory of fibre bundles and is in fact what allows objects with non-trivial automorphisms to define stacks. We will for instance study the stack of elliptic curves from that point of view in Section
5
.

Proposition 2.9
.

The prestacks
[X/G]
,
BG
and
\underline{X}
introduced in Examples
2.1
and
2.3
are stacks on
\mathcal{A}n
.

Proof.

The prestacks
BG
and
\underline{X}
are special cases of the prestack
[X/G]
. We leave the proof that the prestack
[X/G]
is a stack as an exercise (it follows from the fact that principal
G
-bundles on
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, and morphisms between them, can be constructed by gluing).
∎

In line with the intuition that stacks are designed to formalise the idea of families as mathematical objects that can be

(prestack) and

.

2.3.
Analytic stacks

An analytic stack is a stack
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathcal{A}n
that admits a

(or

)

, where
\underline{X}

(henceforth, we shall often write simply

\underline{X}
associated to the space

). Before we can define more precisely what a presentation is, we need to introduce the following notion.

Definition 2.13
.

A morphism of stacks
F:\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is called

if, for all morphism
S\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}
with
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, there exists a complex analytic manifold

such that
S\times_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{M}\simeq M_{S}

In other words, for all
S\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
, the morphism of stacks
F:\mathcal{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
base-changes to a morphism of complex analytic manifolds
f_{S}:M_{S}\longrightarrow S
, as per the following
2

(2.6)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S\times_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{M}\simeq M_{S}}

Example 2.14
.

The computation of

Let

Let

Definition 2.17

An

\mathcal{X}
on
\mathcal{A}n

(1)

(2)

(3)

A morphism between analytic stacks

\mathcal{X}

The same definition works for a differentiable stack

such that

Note that a fibre product of analytic stacks is a stack over

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a stack on
\mathcal{A}n
. A substack
F:\mathcal{Y}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is called an
open substack
of
\mathcal{X}
if:

(1)

F
is representable (Definition

).

(2)

For all morphism of stacks
M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
M\in\mathcal{A}n
, the morphism
f_{M}:\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M\longrightarrow M
is an open morphism in
\mathcal{A}n
.

Note that Condition 2, which says that
f_{M}:\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M\longrightarrow M
is open in
\mathcal{A}n
, makes sense because the stack
\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M
is representable by a manifold (in this case because
F
is assumed to be a representable morphism; see Remark
3.7
for further comments).

Example 3.4
.

Let us retake Example
3.2
. We will show that, if
Y\subset X
is open and
G/H
is a
discrete
topological space, then
[Y/H]
is an open substack of
[X/G]
. This will apply in particular when
G
is a discrete group (see also Example
3.9
). Firstly, we have to show that the morphism (
3.1
) is representable and secondly, that it is open (this is where we shall use that
G/H
is discrete). For the first part, we compute the fibre product
[Y/H]\times_{[X/G]}M
for all morphism of stacks
M\longrightarrow[X/G]
with
M\in\mathcal{A}n
. Such a morphism is given by a principal
G
-bundle
P
over
M
, equipped with a
G
-equivariant morphism
P\longrightarrow X
in
\mathcal{A}n
. Likewise, an arbitrary morphism
S\longrightarrow[Y/H]
with
S\in\mathcal{A}n
is given by a principal
H
-bundle
Q
over
S
, equipped with an
H
-equivariant morphism
Q\longrightarrow Y
in
\mathcal{A}n
. So, given a morphism
u:S\longrightarrow M
in
\mathcal{A}n
, a morphism of stacks
S\longrightarrow[Y/H]\times_{[X/G]}M
is given by an isomorphism of principal
G
-bundles between
u^{*}P
and
Q\times_{H}G
over
S
, i.e. a reduction of structure group from
G
to
H
for the principal
G
-bundle
u^{*}P\longrightarrow S
. It is well-known that reductions of structure group are in bijection with sections of the associated fibre bundle
u^{*}P\times_{G}(G/H)\simeq u^{*}(P\times_{G}(G/H))
over
S
. Then, as in Example
2.11
, such sections are in bijection with morphisms of analytic manifolds from
S
to
P\times_{G}(G/H)
, which proves that the canonical morphism
F:[Y/H]\longrightarrow[X/G]
, defined in (
3.1
) by extension of structure group and inclusion of

in

, is representable (in particular, when
M=X
and

, then
P\times_{G}(G/H)=X\times(G/H)
). Since we now have a

-Cartesian diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\times_{G}(G/H)\simeq\left[Y/H\right]_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[Y/H\right]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[X/G\right]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}

we see that the morphism of stacks
[Y/H]\longrightarrow[X/G]
pulls back to the fibre bundle
P\times_{G}(G/H)\longrightarrow M
in
\mathcal{A}n
, which is indeed an open map
since the fibre, which is
G/H
, is discrete
.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that, if we take

in Example
3.4
, then the canonical morphism
[Y/G]\longrightarrow[X/G]
is an open

in the sense that, for all

, the morphism
[Y/G]_{M}\longrightarrow M
is an open embedding in
\mathcal{A}n
(this is obvious because
[Y/G]_{M}\simeq M
when

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a stack on
\mathcal{A}n
and assume that
\mathcal{X}
is an
analytic
stack with presentation
p:X\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
. A substack
F:\mathcal{Y}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is an
open substack
of
\mathcal{X}
if:

(1)

F
is representable.

(2)

The morphism
f_{X}:\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}X\longrightarrow X
is an open morphism in
\mathcal{A}n
.

Proof.

If
(\mathcal{Y},F)
is an open substack of the analytic stack
\mathcal{X}
, Conditions (1) and (2) of Proposition
3.6
are indeed met. To prove the converse, it suffices to show that, if
f_{X}:Y_{X}\longrightarrow X
is open, then
f_{M}:Y_{M}\longrightarrow M
is open for all
M\in\mathcal{A}n
, where, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, we use the notation
Y_{S}\in\mathcal{A}n
for the representable stack
\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}S
. To prove what we need to, note that, given a morphism of stacks
M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
, we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y_{X}\times_{X}X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{Y}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}}
open
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
open
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overset{\text{\tiny{surjective}}}{\text{\tiny{submersion}}}}

where the first vertical arrow is open because
f_{X}:Y_{X}\longrightarrow X
is open by the assumption made on

and since the property of being open is invariant by base change. The commutativity of the left square then implies that the second vertical arrow is open. Since
F:\mathcal{Y}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is a representable morphism of stacks, the stack

is representable by a manifold, and since the middle square is a pullback diagram, the morphism

∎

We leave it as an exercise to show that, if

Remark 3.7
.

Some comments are in order. For the sake of simplicity, we have omitted certain aspects of the definition of an analytic stack or of an open substack. For instance, in Definition
2.17
, it is often required that, for a stack
\mathcal{X}
over
\mathcal{A}n
to be an analytic stack,
every
morphism
M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
M
in
\mathcal{A}n
should be representable. So Condition (1) of Definition
2.17
becomes unnecessary. But of course it then becomes more difficult to show that a given stack over
\mathcal{A}n
is an analytic stack. In practice, one uses the fact that the following two conditions are equivalent (we refer to
[
Olsson2016
, Remark 8.1.6 p.170]
for a proof of this):

(1)

Every morphism
M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
M
in
\mathcal{A}n
is representable (meaning that, for all
N\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
N\in\mathcal{A}n
, the stack
M\times_{\mathcal{X}}N
is representable by a manifold).

(2)

The diagonal morphism
\Delta:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is representable.

So the more official version of Definition
2.17
is that a stack
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathcal{A}n
is called an analytic stack if the diagonal morphism
\Delta:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is representable and there exists a manifold
X\in\mathcal{A}n
and a (necessarily representable) surjective submersion
p:X\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
. The stack
\mathcal{X}
is called
separated
if the representable morphism
\Delta
is closed.

In the same way that we defined what it means to be an open substack of a stack
\mathcal{X}
, we can define what it means to be a
cover
of such a stack. Comments similar to Proposition
3.6
and Remark
3.7
apply when
\mathcal{X}
is an
analytic
stack, but we omit them here and only give the elementary definition.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a stack on
\mathcal{A}n
. A morphism of stacks
F:\mathcal{Y}\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is called a
cover
or
covering stack
of
\mathcal{X}
if:

(1)

F
is representable.

(2)

For all morphism of stacks
M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
M\in\mathcal{A}n
, the morphism
f_{M}:\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M\longrightarrow M
is a cover in
\mathcal{A}n
.

Such a cover is called a
finite cover
if, for all
M
, the covering map
f_{M}
is a finite cover in
\mathcal{A}n
.

Example 3.9
.

If
G
is a
discrete group
acting by automorphisms on the space
X\in\mathcal{A}n
, then the canonical morphism
X\longrightarrow[X/G]
(defined by the trivial cover
(X\times G)\longrightarrow X

[X/G]
. Indeed, if
M\in\mathcal{A}n
and
P\longrightarrow M
is the principal

, then

is isomorphic to

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[Y/H\right]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[Y/G\right]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[X/H\right]}

Note that a covering stack is in particular an open morphism of stacks because the covering maps
f_{M}:\mathcal{Y}\times_{\mathcal{X}}M\longrightarrow M
are all open morphisms in
\mathcal{A}n
. We are now ready to give the definition of an orbifold. The intuition if that an analytic stack is an orbifold if it admits an
open
covering of the form
[U/\Gamma]
, where
U\in\mathcal{A}n
and
\Gamma
is a
finite
group acting on
U
by automorphisms.

Definition 3.10
.

An
analytic orbifold
is an analytic stack
\mathcal{X}
(Definition
2.17
) equipped with a family of substacks of the form
\big(F_{i}:[U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}]\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}\big)_{i\in I}
such that:

(1)

For all
i\in I
, the pair
(U_{i},\Gamma_{i})
consists of a manifold
U_{i}\in\mathcal{A}n
and a
finite
group
\Gamma_{i}
acting on
U_{i}
by automorphisms.

(2)

For all
i\in I
, the substack
F_{i}:[U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}]\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is an open substack (Definition
3.3
).

(3)

The morphism of stacks
F:\sqcup_{i\in I}[U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}]\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
is surjective (Definition
2.16
).

The stack
\sqcup_{i\in I}[U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}]
appearing in Definition
3.10
is defined, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, by
\big(\sqcup_{i\in I}[U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}]\big)(S)=\sqcup_{i\in I}\big([U_{i}/\Gamma_{i}](S)\big)
. An ultimately equivalent but slightly more abstract definition of an analytic orbifold would be that of an analytic stack equipped with a (surjective) finite cover
F:M\longrightarrow\mathcal{X}
with
M\in\mathcal{A}n
. For instance, all manifolds have canonical orbifold structures. Either way, the main source of examples is obtained as an application of the following result, which is due to Thurston in the case when the group
G
acts effectively on the manifold
X

[
Thurston_2002
, Proposition 13.2.1, p.302]
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
X
be an analytic manifold and let
G
be a discrete group acting to the right on
X
by automorphisms. For all
x\in X
, we denote by

G_{x}:=\{g\in G\ |\ x\cdot g=x\}

the stabiliser of
x
in
G
. Assume that the following properties are satisfied:

(1)

For all
x\in X
, there exists an open neighbourhood
U_{x}
of
x
such that

g\in G_{x}\Rightarrow(U_{x}\cdot g)\subset U_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad g\notin G_{x}\Rightarrow(U_{x}\cdot g)\cap U_{x}=\emptyset.

(2)

For all
x\in X
, the group
G_{x}
is finite.

Then the quotient stack
[X/G]
is an orbifold.

Proof.

Since, for all
x\in X
, the subset
U_{x}\subset X
is
G_{x}
-invariant and open in
X
, we know from Example
3.4
that the quotient stack
[U_{x}/G_{x}]
defines an open substack of
[X/G]
. And since the
(U_{x})_{x\in X}
form an open covering of
X
, the canonical morphism of stacks
F:\sqcup_{x\in X}[U_{x}/G_{x}]\longrightarrow[X/G]
is surjective. Indeed, if
S\in\mathcal{A}n
and
(P,u)
is a pair consisting of a principal
G
-bundle
P
on
S
and a
G
-equivariant map
u:P\longrightarrow X
, we can take an open covering
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
of
S
such that, for all
i
, the principal
G
-bundle
P_{i}:=P|_{S_{i}}
is trivial. Let us fix a trivialisation
P_{i}\simeq(U_{i}\times G)
in which
u|_{P_{i}}:P_{i}\longrightarrow X
is the action map, and choose
x\in X
such that
x\in u(P_{i})
. Set then
Q_{i,x}:=(S_{i}\times G_{x})
. By Condition (1), an element
\xi\in u^{-1}(U_{x})\cap P_{i}
satisfies
g\cdot\xi\in u^{-1}(U_{x})\cap P_{i}
if and only if
g\in G_{x}
, so
u^{-1}(U_{x})\cap P_{i}\simeq Q_{i,x}
. So
P_{i}
lies in the essential image of the canonical functor

F_{x,S_{i}}:[U_{x}/G_{x}](S_{i})\longrightarrow[X/G](S_{i}),

hence also in the image of
F_{S}:\sqcup_{x\in X}[U_{x}/G_{x}](S_{i})\longrightarrow[X/G](S_{i})
, which proves that the morphism
F:\sqcup_{x\in X}[U_{x}/G_{x}]\longrightarrow[X/G]
is surjective in the sense of Definition
2.16
. Finally, since each
G_{x}
is finite by Condition (2), we have indeed shown that
[X/G]
is an orbifold.
∎

Example 3.12
.

If

to prove that
[X/G]
has a canonical orbifold structure. For instance, the quotient stack
[\mathbb{C}/\mu_{n}(\mathbb{C})]
of Example
3.9
is canonically an orbifold. We will see in Corollary

that the action of the discrete group
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
on the upper half-plane
\mathfrak{h}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \text{Im}\,z>0\}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem

, so the quotient stack
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
is also an orbifold, in a canonical way.

Remark 3.13
.

Note that we are not assuming that the action of
G
on
X
is effective, only that it has finite stabilisers. In particular, if
G
is a
finite
group, then the classifying stack
BG=[\bullet/G]
is an orbifold. When the action of the group
G
on the space
X
is effective, the group
G
can be identified with a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and the quotient stack
[X/G]
is called an
effective orbifold
.

To construct orbifolds, it suffices to find examples of actions of discrete groups on manifolds for which Conditions (1) and (2) of Theorem
3.11
are satisfied. An important tool to do that is the following result. We state it for a real Lie group
H
, but we are mostly interested here in the case where
H
is a
Hermitian group
, meaning that the symmetric space
X:=K\backslash H
has a canonical structure of complex analytic (in fact, Kähler) manifold, in which case the quotient stack
[X/G]
is indeed an analytic orbifold.

Theorem 3.14
.

[
Thurston_1997
, Corollary 3.5.11]

Let
H
be a real Lie group whose Lie algebra admits an
H
-invariant positive definite scalar product. Fix a maximal compact subgroup
K\subset H
and let us endow the homogeneous space
X:=K\backslash H
with its canonical manifold structure. Let
G\subset H
be a discrete subgroup of
H
. Then the canonical right action of
G
on
X
satisfies Conditions (1) and (2) of Theorem
3.11
. In particular, the quotient stack
[X/G]
has a canonical orbifold structure.

Corollary 3.15
.

Since the upper half-plane
\mathfrak{h}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \mathrm{Im}\,z>0\}
can be described as the homogeneous space
\mathbf{SO}(2)\backslash\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{R})
, the quotient stack
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
has a canonical orbifold structure.

Let us make a few remarks on the proof of Theorem
3.14
. Remarks
3.16
and
3.17
show that
proper, isometric
actions of
discrete subgroups of Lie groups
on Riemannian manifolds give rise to orbifolds. Together with Remark
3.18
, they can be formalised into a proof of Theorem
3.14
and Corollary
3.15
.

Remark 3.16
.

What is actually proven in
[
Thurston_1997
, Corollary 3.5.11]
is that, if
G\subset H
is a discrete subgroup of the Lie group
H
, the action of
G
on
K\backslash H
(or, as a matter of fact, on all manifold
X
on which
H
acts transitively with
compact
stabilisers
H_{x}
) satisfies the following condition:

(P)

For all compact subset
C\subset X
, the set
\{g\in G\ |\ C\cdot g\cap C\neq\emptyset\}
is finite.

An (effective) action of a group
G
by homeomorphisms on a locally compact space
X
is called
properly discontinuous
if it satisfies Property (P); see
[
Thurston_1997
, Definition 3.5.1.(v), p.153]
. If
G
is endowed with the
discrete
topology, this is equivalent to asking that the continuous map
X\times G\longrightarrow X\times X
given by
(x,g)\longmapsto(x,x\cdot g)
be
proper
. So giving a properly discontinuous action on a locally compact space is equivalent to giving a proper, continuous action of a discrete group on that space
[
Thurston_1997
, Exercise 3.5.2, p.154]
. Note that, for such an action, the stabilisers
G_{x}
are finite, so Condition (2) of Theorem
3.11
is indeed satisfied.

Remark 3.17
.

To prove Theorem
3.14
, we still need to show that if a discrete group
G
acts continuously and properly on a manifold
X
, then, as stated in the proof of
[
Thurston_2002
, Proposition 13.2.1, p.302]
, for all
x\in X
, there exists an open neighbourhood
U_{x}
of
x
in
X
which is
G_{x}
-invariant and disjoint from its translates by elements of
G
that are not in
G_{x}
(i.e. Condition (1) of Theorem
3.11
is also satisfied). In fact, to prove Theorem
3.14
, it suffices to do it in the case when there is a
G
-invariant distance
d
on
X
(i.e. the group
G
acts by isometries on
X
). And in that case we can take
U_{x}
to be a
Dirichlet domain

U_{x}=\mathcal{D}_{G}(x):=\{y\in X\ |\ \forall\,g\notin G_{x},d(y,x)<d(y,x\cdot g)\}.

Such a set is
G_{x}
-invariant because, if
y\in\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
and
h\in G_{x}
, the
G
-invariance of
d
gives, for all
g\notin G_{x}
,

d(y\cdot h,x\cdot g)=d(y,x\cdot gh^{-1})>d(y,x)=d(y,x\cdot h^{-1})=d(y\cdot h,x).

The set
\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
is also disjoint from its translates by elements of
G
that are not in
G_{x}
because, if
g\notin G_{x}
, then we cannot have both
y\in\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
and
(y\cdot g)\in\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
, as this would imply that

d(y,x)<d(y,x\cdot g^{-1})=d(y\cdot g,x)<d(y\cdot g,x\cdot g)=d(y,x).

So there only remains to prove that
\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
is open. For the sake of completeness, we sketch here the arguments to be found for instance in
[
Beardon
, Theorem 9.4.2, pp.227–228]
. Recall that
x
is fixed throughout. For all
g\notin G_{x}
, set

H_{g}(x):=\{y\in X\ |\ d(y,x)<d(y,x\cdot g)\}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{g}(x):=\{y\in X\ |\ d(y,x)=d(y,x\cdot g)\}.

In particular,
H_{g}(x)
is open,
L_{g}(x)
is closed and
\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)=\cap_{g\notin G_{x}}H_{g}(x)
. Since
G
is a discrete subgroup of a Lie group, it is countable. And since
G
acts properly on
X
, a compact set
C\subset X
can only intersect a finite number of
L_{g}(x)
. Indeed, this is a direct consequence of the fact that, if
G=\{g_{0},g_{1},\,\ldots\}
, then

\forall\,n\ |\ g_{n}\notin G_{x},d\big(x,L_{g_{n}}(x)\big)=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}d\big(x,x\cdot g_{n}\big)\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}+\infty.

Now let us fix
y\in\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)=\cap_{g\notin G_{x}}H_{g}(x)
and consider the compact disk
D[y;\varepsilon]
centred at
y
. As we have just seen, the set

\{g\notin G_{x}\ |\ D[y;\varepsilon]\cap L_{g}(x)\not=\emptyset\}=:\{g_{1},\,\ldots\,,g_{r}\}

is finite. Since
y\in H_{g}(x)
, we have that, for all
i\in\{1,\,\ldots\,,r\}
, there exists an integer
n_{i}\geqslant 1
such that the open disk
D(y,\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n_{i}}})
has an empty intersection with
L_{g_{i}}(x)
, and is therefore contained in
H_{g_{i}}(x)
. So, we can find an open neighbourhood
U_{y}:=\cap_{i=1}^{r}D(y_{i},\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n_{i}}})
such that, for all
g\notin G_{x}
,
U_{y}\subset H_{g}(x)
, i.e.
U_{y}\subset\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
, proving that
\mathcal{D}_{G}(x)
is indeed open.

Remark 3.18
.

Note that, in the situation of Theorem
3.14
, a
G
-invariant distance exists on

because
H
possesses a left-invariant Riemannian metric. Indeed we have assumed that the Lie algebra of
H
admits an
H
-invariant
positive definite scalar product (this holds for instance if

is a real form of a

complex Lie group, such as

in Corollary

indeed comes from its description as the homogeneous space

because of the exceptional isomorphism between

and the Hermitian real form

of
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{C})
.

Remark 3.19
.

As explained after the proof of Corollary 3.5.11 in

admits equivalent characterisations, all of them given in

. For instance the fact that

is a discrete subgroup of

, or the fact that the action is

For all

.

If a complex Lie group

Example 4.6
.

Let

act on

via

, which is a fixed point of the action, and the hyperbolas of equation

, for all

. The origin is contained in the closure of the only two non-closed orbits, which are the real and imaginary axes minus the origin. In particular, a
G
-invariant morphism

will take the same value on these three orbits, which will therefore be identified in the categorical quotient. This gives us the intuition that the categorical quotient in this example should be the set of closed orbits of the

\mathbb{C}
via the
G
-invariant function

. By construction, this set has strictly less elements than the whole set of orbits of the

act on
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}
via

, then the only closed orbit is
\{(0,0)\}
, which gives us the intuition that the categorical quotient will be a point in this case.

4.2.
GIT quotients

To make sense out of Example

O_{1}\sim O_{2}
if

is an equivalence relation on the set of
G
-orbits (the previous result on the closure of an orbit being used to prove that this relation is transitive).

(2)

The set of closed

-orbits in

, which by definition is the quotient of

by the previous equivalence relation, is a categorical quotient for the

. This quotient is called the

for the action of

on

.

There is in fact a more general situation, in which

can be embedded

-equivariantly onto a locally closed submanifold of some

with respect to a

and the canonical action of

on

. In that case, one has to restrict the action to the so-called

X^{ss}=\{x\in X\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{N-1}\ |\ 0\not\in\overline{G\cdot\tilde{x}},\text{ where }\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\text{ is a lift of }x\}.

If

and proceed as before to form the categorical quotient

X^{ss}=\big\{x\in X\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbf{P}^{N-1}\ |\ \forall\,\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{*}\longrightarrow G,\ \exists\,\mu\geqslant 0, \ \lim_{t\to 0}t^{\mu}\,\big(\lambda(t)\cdot\tilde{x}\big)\text{ exists in }\mathbb{C}^{N}\text{ and is}\neq 0\big\}.

Example 4.10
.

Let us equip

(2)

5.1.

Remark 5.3
.

In the general definition of a family of complex analytic manifolds, the condition that the holomorphic submersion
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
also be proper is often incorporated in the definition. In particular, with that definition, the fibres of
\pi
are automatically compact.

Definition 5.4
.

The prestack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
defined, for all complex analytic manifold

\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)=\big\{\text{analytic families\ of\ elliptic\ curves\ parameterised\ by}\ S\big\}

of elliptic curves.

The term

is justified by the following result.

Proposition 5.5
.

The prestack

Proof.

We have to check that the conditions of Definition

are satisfied by the prestack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
. This means showing that analytic families of elliptic curves parameterised by

, and morphisms between them, can be constructed by gluing. Explicitly, we want to show that, given an analytic manifold

and an open covering

, the following two properties hold:

(1)

are families of elliptic curves parameterised by

and
\varphi_{i}:E|_{S_{i}}\longrightarrow E^{\prime}|_{S_{i}}
are morphisms between the pullbacks of these two families to

\varphi_{i}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}=\varphi_{j}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}
over

, then there exists a unique morphism

in
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)
such that, for all

\varphi|_{ S_{i}}=\varphi_{i}
.

(2)

If, for all

, as well as isomorphisms

\varphi_{ij}:E_{j}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}E_{i}|_{S_{i}\cap S_{j}}

and

together with isomorphisms

The first property holds because we can simply define

and this gives a well-defined map that is a morphism of families of elliptic curves from

where

Remark 5.6
.

Note that if we had asked, in Definition
5.5
, that the holomorphic submersion

also be proper, Property (2) in the proof of Proposition

would be more difficult to show: we would also need to prove that the map (

We ultimately want to argue that the reason why the stack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is called a

on it. This will require an explanation, but the idea is that there exists, because of the geometric content behind the definition of

with the following property:

(5.3)

\begin{matrix}\text{For all }S\in\mathcal{A}n\text{ and all }E\in\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)\text{, there exists}\text{ a unique}\\
\text{morphism }f:S\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\text{ such that }f^{*}\mathcal{U}_{1,1}\simeq E.\end{matrix}

Note that, for Property (

) to make sense, we must provide a definition of a family of elliptic curves parameterised by a stack instead of a manifold. Once this is done, we can get an intuition of how we can define a universal family.

Definition 5.7
.

Let

be a stack over

5.2.
Families of framed elliptic curves

We now wish to study the representability of the moduli stack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
. As we shall see, this stack can be seen not to be representable for rather general and abstract reasons, which have to do with the fact that the objects that it parameterises, namely elliptic curves, have non-trivial automorphisms (Corollary
1.18
) and this prevents the moduli stack of elliptic curves from being representable (Proposition
5.14
). Since one can get rid of automorphisms by adding a framing to elliptic curves (Theorem
1.13
), a natural route to study
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is to introduce a
moduli stack of framed elliptic curves

\mathcal{M}_{1,1}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}
and relate it to
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
. We will ultimately show that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}
, in contrast to
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
, is representable (by the Teichmüller space
\mathfrak{h}
) and this will imply that the moduli stack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is isomorphic to the quotient stack
[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
. This last step is what shows that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is an orbifold in the sense of Definition
3.10
(in particular, an analytic stack with atlas
\mathfrak{h}
) and that it admits the modular curve
\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
as a coarse moduli space. But before we can get to all this, let us make a couple of remarks.

Remark 5.12
.

Let us assume that there exists a family of elliptic curves
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
(in the sense of Definition
5.7
) such that the associated morphism
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
(defined, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, by sending a morphism of stacks
f:S\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
to the family of elliptic curves
f^{*}\mathcal{U}_{1,1}\in\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)
) is the identity morphism. Then, as we have noted, the stack
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
is a universal family in the sense that it satisfies the universal property (
5.3
). As suggested by Example
5.10
, the representability of the universal family need not imply the representability of the moduli stack. In the reverse direction, however, the representability of
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
(by a manifold
M_{1,1}
, say) would imply that the universal family
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
is representable (by the fibre product
M_{1,1}\times_{\mathcal{M}_{1,1}}\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
, which is indeed a manifold because the morphism of stacks
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is by assumption representable).

Remark 5.13
.

More generally, if we can prove that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is an analytic stack, then the universal family
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
(which we have not yet constructed), is automatically an analytic stack. Indeed, since
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is representable, the fibre product of
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
with an atlas
X\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}
gives a manifold
X\times_{\mathcal{M}_{1,1}}\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
, which is an atlas for
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
because it is straightforward to check that all morphisms in the pullback diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\times_{\mathcal{M}_{1,1}}\mathcal{U}_{1,1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}_{1,1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}_{1,1}}

are representable surjective submersions.

Independently of these remarks, we can prove the following result.

Proposition 5.14
.

The moduli stack of elliptic curves
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is not representable by a manifold.

Proof.

Let us assume that the stack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is representable by a manifold
M_{1,1}\in\mathcal{A}n
, i.e. that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\simeq\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(-,M_{1,1})
. The contradiction will come from a special property of representable functors such as
\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(-,M_{1,1})
, namely that if
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is a covering map in
\mathcal{A}n
, then the induced map

(5.5)

f^{*}:\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(Y,M_{1,1})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(X,M_{1,1})

is injective. In fact, it suffices to know that this property holds for Galois covers
Y=X/G
where
G
is a finite group acting by automorphisms on
Y
. In this case, the image of the pullback map (
5.5
) is the set
\mathrm{Fix}_{G}\,\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(X,M_{1,1})
of
G
-invariant complex analytic maps from
X
to
M_{1,1}
and since such a map induces a well-defined complex analytic map from
Y
to
M_{1,1}
, the pullback map
f^{*}
is injective.

Thus, to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is not representable, it suffices to find a Galois covering map
\pi:X\longrightarrow X/G
and two non-isomorphic families of elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
on
X/G
such that
\pi^{*}E_{1}\simeq\pi^{*}E_{2}
as families of elliptic curves over
X
. Indeed, this will contradict the injectivity of
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(Y)\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(X)
which has to hold if we assume that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\simeq\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(-,M_{1,1})
, i.e. that
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
is representable. And this is when the fact that there are elliptic curves with non-trivial automorphisms comes in handy (to construct the families
E_{1},E_{2}
).

Let
\mathscr{C}
be the elliptic curve
\mathscr{C}:=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}i)
and set
X:=\mathscr{C}
. We consider the action of the group
G:=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
induced on
E:=X\times\mathscr{C}
by the involution

(5.6)

\big([x],[z]\big)\longmapsto\big([x+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}],[-z]\big)

and the quotient
E_{1}:=E/G
. Since
G
acts freely on
X
, the quotient
Y:=X/G
is a Riemann surface and the canonical projection
\pi:X\longrightarrow Y
is a two-to-one cover. Note that
Y
has genus
1
but does not carry a preferred marked point because
G
does not act on
X
by automorphisms of elliptic curves. Moreover, there is an induced complex analytic map
E_{1}\longrightarrow Y
, which is locally trivial with fibres isomorphic to
\mathscr{C}
. Since the action of
G
on the fibres of
E
is an action
by automorphisms of elliptic curves
, the map
E_{1}\longrightarrow Y
is in fact a family of elliptic curves. The point is that
E_{1}
is not trivial but pulls back to the trivial family
E=X\times\mathscr{C}
. Since the product family like
E_{2}:=Y\times\mathscr{C}
also pulls back to
E
, we see that the map
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(Y)\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(X)
is not injective.

Note that, to see that
E_{1}
is not trivial, it suffices to show that it does not carry a non-vanishing holomorphic volume form (which a product of two Riemann surfaces of genus
1
would), because otherwise the pullback of such a volume form to
E=X\times\mathscr{C}
would be
G
-invariant and equal to
cdx\wedge dz
for some constant
c\neq 0
, contradicting the fact that
dx\wedge dz
is
G
-anti-invariant with respect to the involution (
5.6
).
∎

Remark 5.15
.

The technique used in the proof of Proposition
5.14
can be adapted to show the non-representability of various moduli stacks. It rests on the fact that a representable stack
\mathcal{M}\simeq\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(-,M)
is a sheaf in various Grothendieck topologies on
\mathcal{A}n
, in particular the étale topology. So, to prove that a stack
\mathcal{M}
is not representable, it suffices to find an étale covering
\pi:Y\longrightarrow X
such that
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{M}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}(Y)
is not injective. Indeed, this injectivity is part of the conditions of being a sheaf: the restriction map (or pullback) to an open covering (or more generally an étale cover) is an injective map (if two sections of a sheaf coincide on every open of the cover, they are equal). Then, in order to show a lack of injectivity, the usual technique (as in the proof of Proposition
5.14
) consists in finding a non-trivial family whose pullback to a finite cover is trivial. The construction of such a family (called an
isotrivial family
) is then typically obtained by taking the quotient of a trivial family by a finite group acting
by automorphisms on the fibre
and freely on the base.

We now want to introduce families of
framed
elliptic curves. Recall that, in Definition
1.9
, we defined a framed elliptic curve to be a triple
(\mathscr{C},e,(\alpha,\beta))
consisting of an elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
equipped with a direct basis
(\alpha,\beta)
of
H_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})
, i.e. a basis satisfying the positivity condition (
1.7
), and this was convenient in order to classify framed elliptic curves and prove that they have no non-trivial automorphisms (Theorem
1.13
). While we can generalise this point of view to families of elliptic curves, it is not well-adapted to the more general moduli theory of higher genus curves (see also Remark
5.21
). So, before we proceed, we give an equivalent of a framed elliptic curve, based on the following result.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0},(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}))
and
(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1},(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}))
be framed elliptic curves. Then there exists an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f:(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\longrightarrow(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1})
such that
f_{*}(\alpha_{0})=\alpha_{1}
and
f_{*}(\beta_{0})=\beta_{1}
.

Note that the condition of being an orientation-preserving homeomorphism from
\mathscr{C}_{0}
to
\mathscr{C}_{1}
makes sense because complex analytic elliptic curves, like all Riemann surfaces, are canonically
oriented
topological surfaces. In the compact connected case, this means that a group isomorphism
H_{2}(\mathscr{C}_{i};\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}
has been chosen, and for a homeomorphism
f:\mathscr{C}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}_{1}
to be orientation-preserving means that the induced group isomorphism
f_{*}:H_{2}(\mathscr{C}_{0};\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow H_{2}(\mathscr{C}_{1};\mathbb{Z})
commutes to these identifications. Equivalently, the orientation of
\mathscr{C}_{i}
gives a notion of direct basis on
H_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{i};\mathbb{Z})
, and the induced group isomorphism
f_{*}:H_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{0};\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow H_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{1};\mathbb{Z})
sends a direct basis to a direct basis.

Proof of Lemma
5.16
.

By Theorem
1.13
, we can assume that
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0},(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}))
and
(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1},(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}))
are framed elliptic curves of the form
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau_{i}),0,(1,\tau))
for some
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{i})>0
. Then let us consider the map
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

z=x+iy\longmapsto\frac{(\tau_{1}-\overline{\tau_{0}})z-(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})\overline{z}}{\tau_{0}-\overline{\tau_{0}}}=x+\frac{\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})}{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{0})}y+i\frac{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1})}{\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{0})}y

which is the orientation-preserving invertible
\mathbb{R}
-linear map from
\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
to itself defined by the matrix

(5.7)

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{Re}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{0})/\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{0})\\
0&\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{1})/\mathrm{Im}(\tau_{0})\end{pmatrix}

and is the unique
\mathbb{R}
-linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to itself sending the basis
(1,\tau_{0})
to the basis
(1,\tau_{1})
. By construction, we have
\tilde{f}(m+n\tau_{0})=m+n\tau_{1}
, so
\tilde{f}
induces an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f:\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau_{0})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau_{1})
, sending
0
to
0
while also taking the framing
(1,\tau_{0})
to the framing
(1,\tau_{1})
.
∎

Note that, by the Cauchy-Riemann equations, the map
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic if and only if the matrix (
5.7
) is a similitude matrix, which happens if and only if
\tau_{0}=\tau_{1}
. This is consistent with the fact that framed elliptic curves have no non-trivial holomorphic automorphisms. More importantly for us, Lemma
5.16
implies that there is a bijection between framings of an elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
and homotopy classes of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms
f\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, where the latter notation means homeomorphisms
f:\mathscr{C}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathscr{C}_{0}
that preserve the canonical orientation of
\mathscr{C}_{0}
and that satisfy
f(e_{0})=e_{0}
. Indeed, the action of the
modular group

(5.8)

\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}):=\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\,/\,\text{homotopy}

on the set of direct bases of
H_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is transitive by Lemma
5.16
and free because, as
\mathscr{C}
has genus
1
, its universal cover is
\mathbb{C}
so an orientation-preserving self-homeomorphism of
(\mathscr{C},e)
lifts to a
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C},e)
-equivariant orientation-preserving self-homeomorphism of
(\mathbb{C},0)
and, as
\mathbb{C}
is contractible, two such transformations are
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C},e)
-equivariantly homotopic precisely when they induce the same group automorphism of
H_{1}(\mathscr{C};\mathbb{Z})
, which can be seen as a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
. Thus, in the situation of Lemma
5.16
, two orientation-preserving homeomorphisms
f,g:(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\longrightarrow(\mathscr{C}_{1},e_{1})
that both send
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
to
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
will satisfy
g^{-1}\circ f\sim\mathrm{id}_{\mathscr{C}_{0}}
, so
g
will indeed be homotopic to
f
in
\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
. Note that the fact that the modular group of a complex analytic elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
acts freely and transitively on the set of direct bases of
H_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{0};\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
also proves that

(5.9)

\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\simeq\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})

so Definition
5.8
is consistent with the terminology introduced in Section
1.3
. We can now redefine framed elliptic curves as follows (compare Definition
1.9
).

Definition 5.17
.

Let us fix a complex analytic elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
. Then, given a complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C},e)
, we define a
framing

[f]
of
(\mathscr{C},e)
to be a homotopy class of orientation-preserving homeomorphism, i.e.

[f]\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\big((\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}),(\mathscr{C},e)\big)\,/\,\text{homotopy}.

The set of all framings of
(\mathscr{C},e)
will be denoted by
\mathrm{Fr}_{(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}(\mathscr{C},e)
. It is acted upon freely and transitively by the modular group
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, via the right action defined, for all

[g]\in\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
by

(5.10)

[f]\cdot[g]:=[f\circ g].

Thanks to Definition
5.17
, we will define a framing of a family of elliptic curves
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e)
in the sense of Definition
5.1
essentially as a framing of the elliptic curve
(E_{s},e(s))
for all
s\in S
, i.e. as the data, for all
s\in S
, of a framing
\sigma(s)\in\mathrm{Fr}_{(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}(E_{s},e(s))
, where
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
is a fixed elliptic curve. In order for
\sigma(s)
to depend continuously on
s
, it suffices to construct a fibre bundle
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\longrightarrow S
whose fibre over
s\in S
is
\mathrm{Fr}_{(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}(E_{s},e(s))
and to define
\sigma
as a continuous section of that fibre bundle. We will follow Grothendieck’s construction in
[
Groth_constr_axiom_Teich
]
.

So let us start with a family of elliptic curves
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e)
. By Definition
5.1
, this means in particular that
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
is a surjective holomorphic submersion with compact connected fibres over the manifold
S
. By the so-called monotone-light factorisation (see for instance
[
Duda_Whyburn
, p.102]
), such a map is necessarily proper. But then, the fact that
\pi
is a submersion implies that, for all
z\in E
, there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
x:=\pi(z)
in
S
, an open neighbourhood
V\subset\pi^{-1}(U)
of
z
in
E
, an open subset
\Omega\subset\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
and a biholomorphic map
V\simeq U\times\Omega
over
U
(
[
Hubbard
, Proposition 6.2.3 p.240]
). Such a map is called
simple
in
[
Groth_constr_axiom_Teich
]
, where the base of a family
E\longrightarrow S
is a general complex analytic space, not necessarily a manifold. Note that, unless
V=\pi^{-1}(U)
and
\Omega=\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
, this is strictly weaker than
\pi
being a locally trivial holomorphic vector bundle. However, a simple holomorphic map
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
is a locally trivial
topological
fibre bundle. When
S
is a manifold, this is Ehresmann’s theorem, since a simple map is submersive, and for the case when
S
is an analytic space, which we will not require, we refer to
[
Groth_constr_axiom_Teich
, Lemma 2.1, p.3]
. The point here is that a family of elliptic curves is in particular a locally trivial continuous map
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
, whose fibres are oriented compact connected topological surfaces of genus
1
equipped with a marked point, meaning that for all
s\in S
, the set
\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}((\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}),(E_{s},e(s)))
is non-empty. In particular, we may view
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
as a topogical fibre bundle equipped with a continous section
e:S\longrightarrow E
, with typical fibre
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
and structure group
\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
. Then, by taking homotopy classes of the transition functions of
E
, we can associate to it the locally trivial topological fibre bundle with discrete fibres
p:\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\longrightarrow S
defined by

(5.11)

\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e):=\bigsqcup_{s\in S}\mathrm{Fr}_{(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}\big(E_{s},e(s)\big)

where

\mathrm{Fr}_{(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}(E_{s},e(s))=\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\big((\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}),(E_{s},e(s))\big)\,/\,\text{homotopy}

so
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\longrightarrow S
is actually a principal covering space of
S
, with structure group the discrete group
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
introduced in (
5.8
). We will call
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
the
frame bundle
of the family
(E,\pi,e)
. Its construction only depends on the homeomorphism class of the fixed complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, which is that of a torus
S^{1}\times S^{1}
, equipped with a marked point. This construction is functorial, in the sense that a morphism of families of elliptic curves
u:(E_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow(E_{2},e_{2})
(Definition
5.1
) gives rise to a morphism of principal bundles

\mathrm{Fr}\,(u):\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{2},e_{2})

induced fibrewise (for all
s\in S
) by the map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\Big(\big(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}\big),\big(E_{1}|_{\{s\}},e_{1}(s)\big)\Big)&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}\Big(\big(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0}\big),\big(E_{2}|_{\{s\}},e_{2}(s)\big)\Big)\\
f(s)&\longmapsto&u_{s}\circ f(s)\end{array}

where
u_{s}:E_{1}|_{\{s\}}\longrightarrow E_{2}|_{\{s\}}
is the isomorphism of elliptic curves induced by the isomorphism
u:(E_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow(E_{2},e_{2})
(recall that, by Definition
5.1
, all morphisms of families are isomorphisms).

Definition 5.18
.

Let us fix a complex analytic elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
. A
family of framed elliptic curves
is a quadruple
(E,\pi,e,\sigma)
where
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,\sigma)
is a family of elliptic curves parameterised by
S\in\mathcal{A}n
in the sense of Definition
5.1
and
\sigma:S\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
is a continuous section of the frame bundle
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
of the family
(E,\pi,e)
, meaning that, for all
s\in S
, we can think of
\sigma(s)
as (the homotopy class of) a homeomorphism
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\longrightarrow(E_{s},e(s))
. A morphism between families of framed elliptic curves

(E_{1},\pi_{1},e_{1},\sigma_{1})\longrightarrow(E_{2},\pi_{2},e_{2},\sigma_{2})

is a morphism of families of elliptic curves
u:(E_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow(E_{2},e_{2})
such that the induced morphism of principal covering spaces

\mathrm{Fr}\,(u):\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{1},e_{1})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{2},e_{2})

satisfies
\mathrm{Fr}\,(u)\circ\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{1},e_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Fr}\,(E_{2},e_{2})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Fr}\,(u)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma_{1}}

In particular, all morphisms of framed elliptic curves are isomorphisms, by definition. We shall often drop
\pi
from the notation and simply write
(E,e,\sigma)
for a family of framed elliptic curves parameterised by
S
.

Remark 5.19
.

Note that passing to homotopy classes in the definition of
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
gives us a locally trivial fibration, with the topological group
\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
replaced by the
discrete
group
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
as a fibre. In particular, we can endow
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
with a canonical structure of complex analytic manifold: the one with respect to which the local homeomorphism
p:\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\longrightarrow S
is holomorphic. It then becomes a local biholomorphism (
=
an étale analytic map) and the continuous sections of
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\longrightarrow S
are exactly the holomorphic sections with respect to that holomorphic structure.

If
f:T\longrightarrow S
is a morphism in
\mathcal{A}n
and
(E,e,\sigma)
is a family of framed elliptic curves on
S
, then the pullback family

(5.12)

f^{*}E:=\{(x,t)\in E\times T\ |\ \pi(x)=f(t)\}

defined in (
5.1
) is canonically framed by the section
f^{*}\sigma
of the frame bundle
f^{*}\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)=\mathrm{Fr}\,(f^{*}E,f^{*}e)
. The next result is then proved similarly to Proposition
5.5
.

Proposition 5.20
.

The functor

\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}:\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{A}n^{\text{op}}&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{Groupoids}\\
S&\longmapsto&\big\{\text{analytic families\ of\ framed\ elliptic\ curves\ parameterised\ by}\ S\big\}\end{array}

is a stack, called the
moduli stack of framed elliptic curves
.

It is also clear that there is a morphism of stacks from the moduli stack of framed elliptic curves to the moduli stack of elliptic curves, defined, for families parameterised by
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, by forgetting the frame,

F:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}(S)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)\\
(E,e,\sigma)&\longmapsto&(E,e)\end{array}

since this is compatible with taking pullbacks.

Remark 5.21
.

We could also have defined the objects in
\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}(S)
as triples
(E,e,\sigma^{\prime})
where
(E,e)
is a family of elliptic curves parameterised by
S\in\mathcal{A}n
and
\sigma^{\prime}
is a section of the bundle
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)\times_{\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})}H^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, associated to the principal
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
-bundle
\mathrm{Fr}\,(E,e)
via the action of the modular group on
H^{1}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, such that, for all
s\in S
, the element
\sigma^{\prime}(s)=(\alpha(s),\beta(s))
is a direct basis of

. The upshot is that this is the direct generalisation of Definition
1.9
to families of elliptic curves. The fact that one can use one bundle or the other is because, in genus
1
, the action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
on

is
faithful
, which is no longer the case in higher genus. Definition
5.18
, in contrast, generalises immediately to higher genus (where it is no longer necessary to provide the curves with a base point).

5.3.
Orbifold structure and coarse moduli space

Contrary to the moduli stack
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
, which is not representable by Proposition
5.14
, the moduli stack
\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is representable. To understand why, recall that, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
,

\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}(S)=\big\{(E,e,\sigma):\text{families of framed elliptic curves on }S\big\}

so, for all
s\in S
, the framed elliptic curves
(E_{s},e(s),\sigma(s))
should be isomorphic, by Theorem
1.13
, to the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau_{s}),0,(1,\tau_{s}))
, for a unique
\tau_{s}\in\mathfrak{h}
. This defines a map

(5.13)

f_{\sigma}:\begin{array}[]{rcl}S&\longrightarrow&\mathfrak{h}\\
s&\longmapsto&\tau_{s}\end{array}

corresponding to the family
(E,e,\sigma)
, which is in fact a morphism of complex analytic manifolds. By naturality with respect to
S
, the correspondence
(E,e,\sigma)\longmapsto f_{\sigma}
in (
5.13
) induces a morphism of stacks

(5.14)

\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(-,\mathfrak{h})

which will be seen to be an isomorphism if we can define a universal family of framed elliptic curves

\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}.

Indeed, if for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, the framed elliptic curve
(\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1})|_{\{s\}}
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau_{s}),0,(1,\tau_{s}))
, then by construction of the map
f_{\sigma}
introduced in (
5.13
), we have
f_{\sigma}^{*}\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\simeq(E,e,\sigma)
as families of framed elliptic curves, and
f_{\sigma}
is the unique map with that property. So
\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is indeed a universal family of framed elliptic curves and the morphism of stacks defined, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(S,\mathfrak{h})&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}(S)\\
(f:S\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h})&\longmapsto&f^{*}\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\end{array}

is an inverse to the morphism of stacks
(E,e,\sigma)\longmapsto f_{\sigma}
introduced in (
5.14
). To check that the previous considerations are correct and that
\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is indeed representable by
\mathfrak{h}
, we still need to:

(1)

Prove that the map
f_{\sigma}:S\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
constructed in (
5.13
) is indeed holomorphic.

(2)

Construct a universal family of framed elliptic curves over
\mathfrak{h}
, meaning a family
\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
such that, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, the fibre of
\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
at
\tau
is isomorphic to the framed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau))
.

For the first part, recall that, because of Definition
5.18
, the definition of the moduli stack of framed elliptic curves requires fixing an arbitrary complex elliptic curve
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
and that it is in fact only the homeomorphism type of that complex elliptic curve that matters. But the latter is uniquely determined: it is the homeomorphism type of the topological torus
S^{1}\times S^{1}
, with an arbitrary base point, for instance
(1,1)
. The point is that this topological torus comes equipped with a canonical direct homology basis
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for
H_{1}(S^{1}\times S^{1};\mathbb{Z})
, and that all other bases can be brought onto that one by an orientation-preserving self-homeomorphism of
S^{1}\times S^{1}
. Alternately, we can also choose
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
to be the complex torus corresponding to the lattice
\Lambda(i):=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}i\subset\mathbb{C}
, which comes equipped with the direct basis
(1,i)
. The point is that we can fix not only
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
but also a direct homology basis
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for
H_{1}(\mathscr{C}_{0};\mathbb{Z})
and, given a family of framed elliptic curves
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e,\sigma)
, use that direct basis
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
to define the map
\varphi_{\sigma}
explicitly as follows:

(5.15)

\varphi_{\sigma}:\begin{array}[]{rcl}S&\longrightarrow&\mathfrak{h}\\
s&\longmapsto&\tau_{s}:=\frac{\int_{\sigma(s)_{*}\beta_{0}}\omega_{s}}{\int_{\sigma(s)_{*}\alpha_{0}}\omega_{s}}=\frac{\int_{\beta_{0}}\sigma(s)^{*}\omega_{s}}{\int_{\alpha_{0}}\sigma(s)^{*}\omega_{s}}\end{array}

where
\sigma(s):\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(i)\longrightarrow E(s)
is the orientation-preserving homeomorphism determined by
\sigma
, and
\omega_{s}
is a continuous family of holomorphic
1
-form on the elliptic curve
E(s)
. This last part means that
\omega
is a section of the sheaf
\pi_{*}\Omega^{1}_{E/S}
, the push-forward of the sheaf of relative holomorphic differential forms of degree
1
of
E
over
S
. It makes sense to see these relative differentials as families of differential forms along the fibres because, as
\pi:E\longrightarrow S
is a submersion by assumption, the canonical sequence of
O_{E}
-modules

0\longrightarrow\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{S}\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}_{E/S}\longrightarrow 0

is exact and locally split (see for instance
[
BLR_Neron
, Proposition 5, p.37]
). It then follows that the map
\varphi_{\sigma}
defined in (
5.15
) is indeed holomorphic.

It therefore only remains to construct a universal family
\pi:\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
. Again, we do this explicitly. First we consider the (free and proper) action of the discrete Abelian group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on the product
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C}
defined as follows and which we write on the right. For all
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and all
(\tau,z)\in\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C}
, set

(5.16)

(\tau,z)\cdot(m,n):=(\tau,z+m\tau+n).

Next we take the quotient
manifold

(5.17)

\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}:=(\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{Z}^{2}

and consider the map
\pi:\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
induced by the first projection
(\tau,z)\longmapsto\tau
, which is a well-defined holomorphic submersion. The point is that, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, the identity map of
\mathbb{C}
induces an isomorphism of complex analytic manifolds

\pi^{-1}\big(\{\tau\}\big)\simeq\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau)

where
\Lambda(\tau)=\mathbb{Z}1\oplus\mathbb{Z}\tau
. Moreover, the family of complex tori
\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
has a canonical section
e:\mathfrak{h}\longrightarrow\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
, induced by the section
\tau\longmapsto(\tau,0)
of the first projection
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
. Finally, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, we can let

\sigma(\tau):\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(i)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau)

be the orientation-preserving homeomorphism defined as in the proof of Lemma
5.16
, by setting
\tau_{0}=i
and
\tau_{1}=\tau
in (
5.7
). We have therefore defined a family of framed elliptic curves
(\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1},e,\sigma)
in the sense of Definition
5.18
. And, by construction, we have, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
an isomorphism of framed elliptic curves

\Big(\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}\big|_{\{\tau\}},e(\tau),\sigma(\tau)\Big)\simeq\big(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(\tau),0,(1,\tau)\big)

so the family
\mathcal{U}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is indeed a universal family. We summarise the previous considerations as follows.

Theorem 5.22
.

The moduli stack of framed elliptic curves
\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is representable by the Teichmüller space
\mathfrak{h}
.

It remains to show that this representability theorem for
\mathcal{M}^{\,\mathrm{fr}}_{1,1}
is compatible with the action on
\mathfrak{h}
of the modular group
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
which, as in (
5.9
), we identify with
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
. First note that, for all
S\in\mathcal{A}n
, all family of elliptic curves
(E,e)
over
S
and all
s\in S
, the modular group
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
acts on the set of framings of the elliptic curve
(E_{s},e(s))
. Indeed, according to Definition
5.17
, a framing of
(E_{s},e(s))
is a homotopy class of orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f:\mathscr{C}_{0}\longrightarrow E_{s}
such that
f(e_{0})=e(s)
, and we have seen in (
5.10
) that there is a right action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
on the set of such framings, defined by
[f]\cdot[g]=[f\circ g]
. This induces an action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
on the set
\mathrm{Mor}_{\mathcal{A}n}(S,\mathfrak{h})
, under which the morphism
\varphi_{\sigma}:S\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
corresponding to the family
(E,e,\sigma)
is sent to the morphism
\varphi_{\sigma\cdot g}
corresponding to the family
(E,e,\sigma\cdot[g])
, where, for all
g\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
, we denote by
\sigma\cdot[g]
the following section of the frame bundle
\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)
introduced in (
5.11
):

(5.18)

(\sigma\cdot[g])(s)=\sigma(s)\cdot[g]\,.

There only remains to compute
\varphi_{E\cdot[g]}
more explicitly. Note that
\sigma\cdot[g]
is a framing of the
same
family of elliptic curves
(E,e)
. So, in view of Definition
5.15
and the fact that
(f\circ g)_{*}=f_{*}\circ g_{*}
in homology, we can use Theorem
1.14
to conclude that

(5.19)

\varphi_{\sigma\cdot[g]}(s)=\varphi_{\sigma}(s)\cdot[g],

where
\sigma\cdot[g]
is defined as in (
5.18
) and the action on the right-hand side is the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action on
\mathfrak{h}
defined in (
1.8
).

We are now in a position to prove that there is an isomorphism of stacks
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\simeq[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
, which by Corollary
3.15
will show that the moduli stack of elliptic curves is an orbifold, and to identify the universal family
\mathcal{U}_{1,1}
as an orbifold itself. In order to define a morphism of stacks
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\longrightarrow[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
, note that, if
S\in\mathcal{A}n
and
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e)\in\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)
is a family of elliptic curves parameterised by
S
, then there is a morphism of analytic varieties
u:\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
from the frame bundle of
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e)
to the Teichmüller space
\mathfrak{h}
, which is defined as follows. Recall that an element
[f]\in\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)
is, by Definition
5.17
and Equation (
5.11
), the homotopy class of an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f:(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})\longrightarrow(E_{\pi([f])},e(\pi([f])))
, where
(\mathscr{C}_{0},e_{0})
is our fixed elliptic curve
(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda(i),0)
and
p:\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)\longrightarrow S
is the canonical projection of the frame bundle. So we can define an analytic map
u:\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
by sending
[f]\in\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)
to the complex number
\tau_{p([f])}
defined in (
5.15
). For the same reason as in Equation (
5.19
), the map
u:\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{h}
thus constructed is
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-equivariant with respect to the
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action on
\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)
defined in (
5.10
) and the usual
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-action on
\mathfrak{h}
. And since
\mathrm{Fr}(E,e)
is a principal
\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})
-bundle on
S
and the contruction we have just given is compatible with pullbacks with respect to a morphism
T\longrightarrow S
, we have indeed a morphism of stacks
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}\longrightarrow[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})]
.

Theorem 5.23
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{A}n
. The natural correspondence

\Phi_{S}:\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(S)&\longrightarrow&[\mathfrak{h}/\mathbf{SL}(2;\mathbb{Z})](S)\\
(E,\pi:E\longrightarrow S,e)&\longmapsto&\left(&\end{array}
