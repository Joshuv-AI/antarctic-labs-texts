Efficient Multi-view Clustering via Unified and Discrete Bipartite Graph Learning
Si-Guo Fang, Dong Huang, Xiao-Sha Cai, Chang-Dong Wang, Chaobo He, and Yong Tang

Abstract
Although previous graph-based multi-view clustering algorithms have gained significant progress, most of them are still faced with three limitations. First, they often suffer from high computational complexity, which restricts their applications in large-scale scenarios. Second, they usually perform graph learning either at the single-view level or at the view-consensus level, but often neglect the possibility of the joint learning of single-view and consensus graphs. Third, many of them rely on the k k -means for discretization of the spectral embeddings, which lack the ability to directly learn the graph with discrete cluster structure. In light of this, this paper presents an efficient multi-view clustering approach via u nified and d iscrete b ipartite g raph l earning (UDBGL). Specifically, the anchor-based subspace learning is incorporated to learn the view-specific bipartite graphs from multiple views, upon which the bipartite graph fusion is leveraged to learn a view-consensus bipartite graph with adaptive weight learning. Further, the Laplacian rank constraint is imposed to ensure that the fused bipartite graph has discrete cluster structures (with a specific number of connected components). By simultaneously formulating the view-specific bipartite graph learning, the view-consensus bipartite graph learning, and the discrete cluster structure learning into a unified objective function, an efficient minimization algorithm is then designed to tackle this optimization problem and directly achieve a discrete clustering solution without requiring additional partitioning, which notably has linear time complexity in data size. Experiments on a variety of multi-view datasets demonstrate the robustness and efficiency of our UDBGL approach. The code is available at https://github.com/huangdonghere/UDBGL .

III-B

View-specific Subspace Learning with Anchors

Let a single-view data matrix be denoted as
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, which consists of
n
samples with
d
features. According to sparse subspace clustering (SSC)
[
12
]
, it is assumed that a data point can be written as a linear or affine combination of all other data points, i.e.,
\textbf{X}=\textbf{X}\textbf{Z}^{\top}+\textbf{E}
, where
\textbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the self-representation matrix with the constraint
z_{ii}=0
to enforce that each point cannot be represented by itself, and
\textbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is the error term. It aims to minimize the error term to find a better self-representation matrix, whose objective function can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{Z}}~

\displaystyle\left\|\textbf{X}-\textbf{X}\textbf{Z}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{Z}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\textbf{Z}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1};\forall i,z_{ii}=0,

(1)

where
\left\|\textbf{Z}\right\|_{F}^{2}
is the regularization term that prevents the model from the trivial solution, and
\alpha>0
is a hyper-parameter to control the influence of the regularization term. By constraining
\textbf{Z}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1}
, the elements in
Z
with
0\leq z_{ij}\leq 1
can represent the similarity between the
i
-th sample and the
j
-th sample. Thus
Z
can also be viewed as a similarity graph. Note that the elements in the
i
-th row of
Z
denote the linear weighting coefficients of the
i
-th sample reconstructed by the other sample points, i.e.,
\textbf{x}_{:i}\approx z_{i1}\textbf{x}_{:1}+z_{i2}\textbf{x}_{:2}+\cdots+z_{in}\textbf{x}_{:n}
. Here, the row constraint (via
\textbf{Z}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1}
) is utilized to ensure that each row of
Z
has at least one non-zero element.

For multi-view subspace clustering, it is challenging to fuse information from different views due to the potential inconsistency among multiple views
[
10
]
. Let
\textbf{X}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(v)}\times n}
denote the
v
-th data matrix of the multi-view dataset, where
d^{(v)}
is the number of features in the
v
-th view. Thus, we can perform the subspace learning on every single view as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{Z}^{(v)}}~

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{V}\left\{\left\|\textbf{X}^{(v)}-\textbf{X}^{(v)}(\textbf{Z}^{(v)})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle+\beta\mathcal{F}\left(\textbf{Z}^{(1)},\cdots,\textbf{Z}^{(V)}\right),

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1};\forall v,i,z_{ii}^{(v)}=0,

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}(\textbf{Z}^{(1)},\cdots,\textbf{Z}^{(V)})
is a regularization term which enforces the consistency of multiple self-representation matrices
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and
\beta>0
is a hyper-parameter to control the influence of the regularization term.

The above-mentioned model needs to learn multiple
n\times n
graphs, which takes at least
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time. In the general subspace learning, it is assumed that a sample can be written as a linear or affine combination of all other samples (as shown in the objective (
III-B
)). For the self-expressiveness property, there may be much redundant information in the original large-scale dataset. Instead of using the entire dataset as the dictionary, using a smaller number of samples as the dictionary may significantly reduce the computational cost while maintaining sufficient representation ability for the latent subspaces. Here, the small number of representative samples are referred to as the anchors. Recently, it has proved to be an efficient and effective strategy to utilize the anchor graph (which is a bipartite graph between the orginal samples and the anchors) rather than the
n\times n
full graph for large-scale multi-view datasets
[
27
,
22
]
. Specifically, a straightforward way to construct the anchors is to conduct the
k
-means clustering on the concatenated features and then split the obtained centers (i.e., anchors) of the concatenated features by views
[
28
]
.
Let
[\textbf{A}^{(1)};\textbf{A}^{(2)};\cdots;\textbf{A}^{(V)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{v}d^{(v)}\times m}
denote the anchor matrix for the dataset
[\textbf{X}^{(1)};\textbf{X}^{(2)};\cdots;\textbf{X}^{(V)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{v}d^{(v)}\times n}
, where
\textbf{A}^{(v)}
is the anchor matrix for the
v
-th view and
m
is the number of anchors. We extend the objective (
III-B
) from the conventional subspace learning to the anchor-based subspace learning by replacing the data matrix
\textbf{X}^{(v)}
with the anchor matrix
\textbf{A}^{(v)}
, and thus rewrite the objective function as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{Z}^{(v)}}~

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{V}\left\{\left\|\textbf{X}^{(v)}-\textbf{A}^{(v)}(\textbf{Z}^{(v)})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle+\beta\mathcal{F}\left(\textbf{Z}^{(1)},\cdots,\textbf{Z}^{(V)}\right),

\displaystyle s.t.~\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},

(3)

where the representation matrix
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
can be interpreted as a bipartite graph between the
n
original samples and the
m
anchors.

III-C

Rank-constrained Bipartite Graph Fusion

In this section, we proceed to fuse multiple bipartite graphs from multiple views into a unified bipartite graph. Notably, the multiple bipartite graphs are associated with adaptively-learned weights, and a Laplacian rank constraint is further imposed for learning a unified bipartite graph with discrete cluster structure.

Formally, the objective function for bipartite graph fusion can be formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{P},\bm{\delta}}~\left\|\sum_{v=1}^{V}\delta^{(v)}\textbf{Z}^{(v)}-\textbf{P}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0;\textbf{P}\in\bm{\Omega},

(4)

where
\bm{\delta}=[\delta^{(1)},\delta^{(2)},\cdots,\delta^{(V)}]^{\top}
is the view coefficient vector, and
\bm{\Omega}=\left\{\textbf{P}\mid\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0\right\}
ensures that
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
and
P
are at a similar scale.

After obtaining the unified bipartite graph
P
, the traditional strategy is to approximately construct a similarity graph among all samples through the obtained bipartite graph.
The doubly-stochastic similarity graph
\textbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

[
33
,
20
]
, whose summation of each row or each column equals to
1
, can be built as

\displaystyle\textbf{S}=

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\textbf{P}^{\top},

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle diag\left(\textbf{P}^{\top}\textbf{1}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

(6)

where the
diag\left(\cdot\right)
function returns a diagonal matrix whose main diagonal elements are the corresponding elements of input vector. Subsequently, the spectral clustering can be conducted on the similarity graph
S
, which takes at least
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}c)
time
[
27
]
, where
c
is the desired number of clusters. Regarding this, we redefine the similarity graph
S
in a different way from the aforementioned Eq. (
5
). That is

\displaystyle\textbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}&\textbf{P}\\
\textbf{P}^{\top}&\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}.

(7)

Lemma 1
.

[
34
,
22
]
Let
S
be an undirected graph with non-negative elements. The multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
of the normalized Laplacian matrix
\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}}
equals the number of connected components in the graph
S
.

In order to reduce the time complexity and directly achieve the discrete cluster structure without additional partitioning, we impose a rank constraint on the normalized Laplacian matrix (i.e.,
rank(\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}})=n+m-c
) of the similarity graph
S
, where
\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}}=\textbf{I}-\textbf{D}_{\textbf{S}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{S}\textbf{D}_{\textbf{S}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is the normalized Laplacian matrix, and
\textbf{D}_{\textbf{S}}=diag(\textbf{S}\textbf{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
is the degree matrix. According to Lemma
1
, the rank constraint enforces that the multiplicity of eigenvalue
0
of
\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}}
should be

, which leads to the learned graph
S
with exact

Further, since
S
and
P
represent the same graph, the
c

are guaranteed through the
c
-connected graph

P
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}=\{\textbf{P}\mid\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0;rank(\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}})=n+m-c\}.

(8)

Through the Laplacian rank constraint, the final clustering result can be directly gained from the bipartite graph
P
without additional partitioning. In particular, the bipartite graph
P
with the Laplacian low rank constraint will have a specific number of connected components
[
34
,
22
]
. By treating the samples in the same connected component as a cluster, the clustering result can then be obtained. Thereby, we rewrite the objective function of the bipartite graph fusion as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{P},\bm{\delta}}~

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{v=1}^{V}\delta^{(v)}\textbf{Z}^{(v)}-\textbf{P}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

\displaystyle s.t.~\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0;

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0;rank(\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}})=n+m-c.

(9)

Both the general graph and the bipartite graph can utilize the rank constraint to learn the discrete cluster structure, but they are quite different in the connectivity and optimization. In terms of the connectivity, the general graph constructs different connected components through the similarity between data samples, while in a bipartite graph it should be decided through the anchors whether two data samples in the bipartite graph are connected. That is, the connectivity between two samples should be achieved through one or more anchors. In terms of the optimization, the discrete cluster structure learning of a general graph is usually more time-consuming, while utilizing the bipartite graph structure can significantly reduce the computational complexity.

III-D

Multi-view Clustering via Unified and Discrete Bipartite Graph Learning

Though previous MVC methods based on bipartite graph learning have achieved considerable progress, yet most of them
either
pre-construct the view-specific bipartite graphs and then learn a unified bipartite graph
[
19
,
28
,
22
,
18
]

or
learn multiple view-specific bipartite graphs and then simply concatenate the learned view-specific bipartite graphs into a unified one
[

. However, the pre-constructed bipartite graphs lack the ability of adaptive learning and refining, while the simple concatenation cannot well explore the latent structures of multiple bipartite graphs. Surprisingly, there remains a challenging gap between the view-specific bipartite graph learning and the view-consensus bipartite graph learning in most of previous works
[

,
28
,
22
,
18
,
27
]

In this paper, we seek to unify the view-specific bipartite graph learning, the view-consensus bipartite graph learning, and the discrete cluster structure learning in a joint learning model. Particularly, the objective (
III-B
) (for anchor-based subspace learning) and the objective (

Formally, the objective function for the unified and discrete bipartite graph learning can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{Z}^{(v)},\textbf{P},\bm{\delta}}~

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{V}\left\{\left\|\textbf{X}^{(v)}-\textbf{A}^{(v)}(\textbf{Z}^{(v)})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\beta\left\|\sum_{v=1}^{V}\delta^{(v)}\textbf{Z}^{(v)}-\textbf{P}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1};\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0;\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0;

\displaystyle rank(\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}})=n+m-c.

(10)

In the next section, we will present a fast optimization algorithm to minimize this unified objective function, which enables the mutual promotion of the view-specific bipartite graphs and the view-consensus bipartite graph, and direct achieves the final clustering result without requiring additional graph partitioning.

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0;\textbf{F}^{\top}\textbf{F}=\textbf{I},

where
\textbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times c}
is the indicator matrix, and
\gamma
can be automatically determined based on the number of connected components of
P
. Further, we alternately optimize the variables
F
and
P
.

First, with
P
fixed, the subproblem w.r.t.
F
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{F}^{\top}\textbf{F}=\textbf{I}}~

\displaystyle Tr(\textbf{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}}\textbf{F})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\max_{\textbf{F}_{(n)}^{\top}\textbf{F}_{(n)}+\textbf{F}_{(m)}^{\top}\textbf{F}_{(m)}=\textbf{I}}

\displaystyle Tr(\textbf{F}_{(n)}^{\top}\textbf{D}_{(n)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{P}\textbf{D}_{(m)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{F}_{(m)}),

(13)

with

F

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{F}_{(n)}\\
\textbf{F}_{(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\textbf{F}_{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c},\textbf{F}_{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times c},

(14)

\displaystyle\textbf{D}_{\textbf{S}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{D}_{(n)}&\\
&\textbf{D}_{(m)}\\
\end{pmatrix},~\textbf{D}_{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\textbf{D}_{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

(15)

According to
[
19
]
, the optimal solutions to the problem (
IV-A
) can be obtained as
\textbf{F}_{(n)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\textbf{U}
and
\textbf{F}_{(m)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\textbf{V}
, where
U
and
V
are the largest
c
left and right singular vectors of
\textbf{D}_{(n)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{P}\textbf{D}_{(m)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, respectively.

Second, according to
[
22
]
, we can have the following equality:

\displaystyle Tr(\textbf{F}^{\top}\widetilde{\textbf{L}}_{\textbf{S}}\textbf{F})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\frac{\textbf{F}_{(n)}(i,:)}{\sqrt{\textbf{D}_{(n)}(i,i)}}-\frac{\textbf{F}_{(m)}(j,:)}{\sqrt{\textbf{D}_{(m)}(j,j)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}p_{ij}.

(16)

Let
q_{ij}=\left\|\frac{\textbf{F}_{(n)}(i,:)}{\sqrt{\textbf{D}_{(n)}(i,i)}}-\frac{\textbf{F}_{(m)}(j,:)}{\sqrt{\textbf{D}_{(m)}(j,j)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. With
F
fixed, the subproblem w.r.t.
P
can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{P}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1},\textbf{P}\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(b_{ij}-p_{ij}\right)^{2}+\gamma q_{ij}p_{ij}.

(17)

Because the optimization of each row of
P
in the subproblem (
17
) is independent, we optimize
P
by rows:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{p}_{i:}\textbf{1}=1,\textbf{p}_{i:}\geq 0}\left\|\textbf{p}_{i:}-(\textbf{b}_{i:}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\textbf{q}_{i:})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

Then the closed form solution
\textbf{p}_{i:}
can be obtained according to
[
36
]
.

IV-B

Update
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}

With the other variables fixed, the subproblem that only relates to
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{Z}^{(v)}}~

\displaystyle\left\|\textbf{X}^{(v)}-\textbf{A}^{(v)}(\textbf{Z}^{(v)})^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{V}\delta^{(i)}\textbf{Z}^{(i)}-\textbf{P}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle~\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\geq 0,\textbf{Z}^{(v)}\textbf{1}=\textbf{1}.

(19)

Since the optimization of each row of
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
in the subproblem (
IV-B
) is independent, we optimize
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
by rows:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}}~

\displaystyle\left\|\textbf{x}^{(v)}_{:j}-\textbf{A}^{(v)}(\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:})^{\top}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\left\|\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{V}\delta^{(i)}\textbf{z}^{(i)}_{j:}-\textbf{p}_{j:}\right\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle~\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}\geq 0,\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}\textbf{1}=1.

(20)

The above optimization problem (
IV-B
) can be easily formulated as the following quadratic programming (QP) problem

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}}~

\displaystyle\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}{\bar{\textbf{H}}}(\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:})^{\top}+\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}{\bar{\textbf{f}}},

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle~\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}\geq 0,\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{j:}\textbf{1}=1,

(21)

where

\displaystyle{\bar{\textbf{H}}}=

\displaystyle(\textbf{A}^{(v)})^{\top}\textbf{A}^{(v)}+\left(\alpha+\beta(\delta^{(v)})^{2}\right)\textbf{I},

\displaystyle{\bar{\textbf{f}}}=-2(\textbf{A}^{(v)})^{\top}

\displaystyle\textbf{x}^{(v)}_{:j}+2\beta\delta^{(v)}\left(\sum_{i\neq v}\delta^{(i)}(\textbf{z}^{(i)}_{j:})^{\top}-\textbf{p}_{j:}^{\top}\right).

(22)

The problem (
IV-B
) can be solved by using the augmented Lagrangian multiplier (ALM)
[
37
]
(please see Section
IV-C
for the details).

IV-C

Update
\bm{\delta}

With the other variables fixed, the subproblem that only relates to
\bm{\delta}
can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}}~

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{v=1}^{V}\delta^{(v)}\textbf{Z}^{(v)}-\textbf{P}\right\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle s.t.~~

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0.

(23)

In order to solve the subproblem (
IV-C
), we vectorize each matrix
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
into a vector
\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(v)}
, that is

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(v)}=\left[\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{:1};\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{:2};\cdots;\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{:m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times 1},

(24)

where
\textbf{z}^{(v)}_{:i}
represents the
i
-th column of
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
. Further, we stack the vectors
\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(v)}
for
V
views into a matrix
\hat{\textbf{Z}}=[\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(1)},\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(2)},\cdots,\hat{\textbf{z}}^{(V)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times V}
. Note that
\textbf{B}=\sum_{v=1}^{V}\delta^{(v)}\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
, and
\hat{\textbf{b}}
is the vectorization of
B
(similar to Eq. (
24
)), so we have
\hat{\textbf{b}}=\hat{\textbf{Z}}\bm{\delta}
. Similarly,
\hat{\textbf{p}}
is the vectorization of
P
, then the problem (
IV-C
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0}\left\|\hat{\textbf{Z}}\bm{\delta}-\hat{\textbf{p}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{H}\bm{\delta}-\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{f},

(25)

where
\textbf{H}=\hat{\textbf{Z}}^{\top}\hat{\textbf{Z}}
, and
\textbf{f}=2\hat{\textbf{Z}}^{\top}\hat{\textbf{p}}
. The objective (
IV-C
) is a convex quadratic programming with the semi-definite quadratic matrix
H
. Here, we use the augmented Lagrangian multiplier (ALM)
[
37
]
to optimize the objective (
IV-C
), whose solution is obtained by solving its equivalence:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0,\bm{\delta}=\bm{\rho}}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{H}\bm{\rho}-\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{f}.

(26)

The augmented Lagrangian function of the objective (
26
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0,\bm{\rho}}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{H}\bm{\rho}-\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{f}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{\delta}-\bm{\rho}+\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{\eta}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(27)

where the optimal solutions
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\rho}
can be optimized alternately. The third term of the objective (
27
) is a penalty term which ensures
\bm{\delta}=\bm{\rho}
. The penalty factor
\mu>0
is gradually increased in each iteration. Meanwhile,
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
can be updated by
\bm{\eta}\leftarrow\bm{\eta}+\mu(\bm{\delta}-\bm{\rho})
.

First, to update
\bm{\rho}
with
\bm{\delta}
fixed, the Lagrange function w.r.t.
\bm{\rho}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\rho})={\bm{\delta}^{*}}^{\top}\textbf{H}\bm{\rho}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{\delta}^{*}-\bm{\rho}+\frac{1}{\mu}\bm{\eta}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(28)

where
\bm{\delta}
is fixed with
\bm{\delta}^{*}
. By setting the derivative of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\rho})
w.r.t.
\bm{\rho}
to zero, we can obtain the optimal solution as

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}^{*}=\bm{\delta}^{*}+\frac{1}{\mu}(\bm{\eta}-\textbf{H}^{\top}\bm{\delta}^{*}).

(29)

Second, to update
\bm{\delta}
with fixed
\bm{\rho}^{*}
, the objective (
27
) can be transformed into solving the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\delta}^{\top}\textbf{1}=1,\bm{\delta}\geq 0}\left\|\bm{\delta}-\bm{\rho}^{*}+\frac{1}{\mu}(\bm{\eta}+\textbf{H}\bm{\rho}^{*}-\textbf{f})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

Thus, the problem (
30
) can be solved with a closed form solution according to
[
36
]
.

.

IV-D

Computational Complexity Analysis

In this section, we analyze the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm. The computational cost of UDBGL involves the computation of the preparation, the initialization, and the optimization of three variables. First, the normalization and the
k
-means take
\mathcal{O}(nd)
and
\mathcal{O}(ndmt_{1})
time, respectively, where
d=\sum_{v}d^{(v)}
and
t_{1}
is the number of
k
-means iterations. Second, the cost of updating
P
is
\mathcal{O}(nmct_{2}+nm^{2}t_{2}+m^{3}t_{2})
, where
t_{2}
is the iteration number of updating
P
. Third, the cost of updating
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
(for all
v
) is
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2}d)
. Finally, the cost of updating
\bm{\delta}
is
\mathcal{O}(V^{2}nm)
. Since
n\gg m
, and
c,V,t_{2}
are small constants, the time complexity of UDBGL is linear in
n
.

IV-E

Convergence analysis

In this section, we analyze the convergence property of the proposed optimization algorithm. In Section
IV-A
, we can obtain the optimal solutions for the subproblems of
F
and
P
. In order to obtain a bipartite graph with a specific number of connected components, we dynamically adjust the parameter
\gamma
, which leads to a bipartite graph with discrete cluster structure but may undermine the monotonicity of the objective function of the subproblem (
IV-A
). For updating
\textbf{Z}^{(v)}
, the QP problem (
IV-B
) can be proved to be convex. For any non-zero vector
\bar{\textbf{x}}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\textbf{x}}^{\top}\bar{\textbf{H}}\bar{\textbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\textbf{x}}^{\top}\{(\textbf{A}^{(v)})^{\top}\textbf{A}^{(v)}+(\alpha+\beta(\delta^{(v)})^{2})\textbf{I}\}\bar{\textbf{x}}

\displaystyle=||\textbf{A}^{(v)}\bar{\textbf{x}}||_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\beta(\delta^{(v)})^{2})||\bar{\textbf{x}}||_{2}^{2}\geq 0.

(31)

Thus, the matrix of quadratic form is positive semi-definite, and the objective function of the QP problem (
IV-B
) is convex. Note that the constraint functions are affine. Therefore, the QP problem (
IV-B
) is convex. Similarly, updating
\bm{\delta}
is also a convex optimization problem. To summarize, except for the dynamic adjustment of the parameter
\gamma
in the subproblem (
IV-A
), the optimal solutions for all the other subproblems can achieved. In the following, the empirical convergence of UDBGL will also be verified in Section
V-E
.

V-F

Ablation Study

In this section, we test the influence of the single-phase formulation of UDBGL by comparing it with the two-phase versions. Specifically, we design two variants by removing the view-specific graph learning part or by splitting the model into two phases in the ablation study. Our first variant is to first use the
K
-nearest neighbor (
K
-NN) graphs as the pre-constructed graphs and then perform graph fusion to learn a view-consensus graph with discrete cluster structure, which can be viewed as removing the view-specific graph learning part from UDBGL. The second variant is to treat the view-specific graph learning and the view-consensus graph learning in UDBGL as two separate phases, which first enforces subspace learning to obtain the view-specific bipartite graphs and then performs graph fusion to achieve the final clustering. As shown in Fig.
5
, the simultaneous learning of the view-specific graphs and the view-consensus graph can generally lead to more robust clustering performance than the two variants across the ten benchmark datasets. In particular, the clustering performances of the two variants have shown instability across different datasets, which means that they perform well on some datasets but may significantly decline on some other datasets (e.g., on the WebKB-Texas, MSRC-v1, Cora, Notting-Hill, and CIFAR-10 datasets). In comparison with the two variants, the unified formulation of UDBGL achieves consistently high-quality clustering performance across the ten datasets, which is probably due to its ability to learn the view-specific graphs and the view-consensus graph jointly and adaptively.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose an efficient MVC approach termed UDBGL. In particular, the anchor-based subspace learning is enforced to learn the view-specific bipartite graphs from multiple views, which are fused into a view-consensus bipartite graph with adaptive view weights. Remarkably, the view-specific bipartite graph learning and the view-consensus bipartite graph learning can promote each other mutually and adaptively, which are further coupled with the Laplacian low rank constraint to directly obtain the discrete cluster structure from the fused graph. An alternating minimization algorithm is designed to optimize the proposed model, whose computational complexity is linear to the data size. Extensive experiments on ten multi-view datasets, whose data sizes range from
187
to
60,000
, have demonstrated the clustering robustness and efficiency of our UDBGL approach over the state-of-the-art MVC approaches.
