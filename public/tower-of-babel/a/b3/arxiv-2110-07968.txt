On Epsilon Factorized Differential Equations for Elliptic Feynman Integrals
Hjalte Frellesvig

Abstract
In this paper we develop and demonstrate a method to obtain epsilon factorized differential equations for elliptic Feynman integrals. This method works by choosing an integral basis with the property that the period matrix obtained by integrating the basis over a complete set of integration cycles is diagonal. The method is a generalization of a similar method known to work for polylogarithmic Feynman integrals. We demonstrate the method explicitly for a number of Feynman integral families with an elliptic highest sector.

The method of differential equations
Barucchi:1973zm
;
KOTIKOV1991158
;
Gehrmann:1999as

This method works by taking the derivative with respect to a kinematic variable (or to several) of the members of a minimal basis of Feynman integrals for the problem, which we will refer to as master integrals. The result of taking that derivative may then be mapped back to the original master integrals using IBP identities

;
Laporta:2001dd

, and others

;

;

Henn:2013pwa
that for a large class of Feynman integrals in dimensional regularization (with
d=d_{0}-2\epsilon
), the solution of the system gets much simpler, in some cases trivialized, by choosing a special basis with the property that the differential equation system is epsilon factorized. Denoting the basis of master integrals as
J
, this means that the differential equation system in the kinematic variable

may be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}J

\displaystyle=\epsilon A^{(x)}J

(1)

where

In the cases discussed in ref.

A^{(x)}=\partial M/\partial x
with the property that the entries of

consist solely of logarithms of algebraic functions of the kinematics. A basis of master integrals for which the differential equations have these two properties (epsilon factorized, dlog form) is known as a canonical basis.

It is a well known fact that not all Feynman integrals can be brought to canonical form. One class (the simplest) of integrals for which a canonical form is unobtainable are those referred to as elliptic, a term that comes from the presence of elliptic curves at a cut surface. 1.1
Notation and conventions

We will in this work encounter the complete elliptic integrals of respectively first, second and third kind. They are defined as

\displaystyle K(k^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-k^{2}x^{2})}}

(2)

\displaystyle E(k^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\sqrt{1-k^{2}x^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

(3)

\displaystyle\Pi(n^{2},k^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(1-n^{2}x^{2})\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-k^{2}x^{2})}}

(4)

where we use the Mathematica convention in which the arguments are
k^{2}
and
n^{2}
.
The three complete elliptic integrals have the property that any integral of the form
\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{R}(x,Y)\mathrm{d}x
can be written in terms of them (along with elementary functions), where
\mathcal{R}
is any rational function,
Y
is the square root of a polynomial in
x
of degree three or four, and where
\mathcal{C}
is any closed contour.

We will in this work only discuss two-loop Feynman integrals, which can be written as

\displaystyle I_{\{a\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{2}}{\prod_{i}D_{i}^{a_{i}}}

(5)

where the propagators
D_{i}
are quadratic functions of the momenta on the form
k^{2}{-}m^{2}
and
d
is the spacetime dimension. As mentioned earlier, we will consider the integrals in dimensional regularization where
d=d_{0}-2\epsilon
with
d_{0}
being an integer, in this work either
2
or
4
.

We will consider our Feynman integrals in Baikov representation, in either its standard
Baikov:1996iu
;
Lee:2010wea
;
Grozin:2011mt
or loop-by-loop
Frellesvig:2017aai
;
Frellesvig:2021vem
version. Thus the integral may be written

\displaystyle I_{\{a\}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{u\,\mathrm{d}^{n}x}{\prod_{i}x_{i}^{a_{i}}}

(6)

where the
x_{i}
are Baikov variables (that equal the original propagators,
x_{i}=D_{i}
), the
\alpha_{j}
are generally irrational powers, and where
u
is a multivalued function that will have the general form

\displaystyle u=\prod_{j}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}

(7)

where the
\mathcal{B}_{j}
are polynomial functions called Baikov polynomials of the
x_{i}
. The prefactor
\mathcal{K}
will generally be given in terms of gamma functions of arguments linear in the spacetime dimension. In the examples we will look at,
\mathcal{K}
will be a
pure
function, by which we will mean a function with the property that the coefficients in the
\epsilon
expansion has uniform
weight

Duhr:2014woa
such that the coefficient of
\epsilon^{n}
has weight one higher than that of the coefficient of
\epsilon^{n-1}
and only numerical prefactors. We will not make any attempts at generalizing these notions to elliptic objects.

We will in this paper make significant use of generalized unitarity cuts, in particular
maximal cuts
which refers to cuts of all genuine propagators (and thus differs from the notation in the
\mathcal{N}{=}4
literature, in which a maximal is defined to fix all degrees of freedom). This is due to the fact that differential equations
Primo:2016ebd
, as well as IBP relations
Bosma:2017ens
, remain the same after such a cutting procedure, and indeed the Baikov parametrization is particularly suited for generalized unitarity cuts at the integral level
Frellesvig:2017aai
;
Harley:2017qut
.

In the examples in this paper, the maximal cut will leave a single integration to be done, bringing the integrals to a univariate form of eq. (
6
)

\displaystyle I_{\{a\}}|_{\text{max cut}}\

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\int_{\mathcal{C}}u\,\hat{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}z

(8)

where
z
is the remaining Baikov variable, and
\hat{\phi}
is a rational function of
z
and the kinematical variables. We will occasionally use the abbreviation
\phi:=\hat{\phi}\mathrm{d}z
. In general this notation follows that of the recent work on intersection theory and Feynman integrals such as refs.
Mastrolia:2018uzb
;
Frellesvig:2019kgj
.

We will also discuss various integration contours. We will name them with the symbols
\mathcal{C}
or
\gamma
, and use the notation
\mathcal{C}_{i}
for a contour surrounding a pole at
z=i
, and the notation
\mathcal{C}_{\text{i-j}}
for a contour surrounding a branch cut between the points
z=i
and
z=j
.

Notation - The sunrise integrals

We will in this paper work quite a lot with the sunrise integral with different mass distributions. Rather than repeating the same definitions over and over, let us write them here in the most general case:

The three-mass sunrise integral is defined by the complete set of propagators

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=k_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}-k_{2})^{2}-m_{2}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{3}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}+p)^{2}-m_{3}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{4}

\displaystyle=(k_{1})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{5}

\displaystyle=(k_{2}+p)^{2}\,,

(9)

with

, and where only the first three may appear as genuine propagators.

With these definition, the integrals are given as

\displaystyle I^{\text{sunrise}}_{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}a_{5}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}^{d}k_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{D_{4}^{-a_{4}}D_{5}^{-a_{5}}}{D_{1}^{a_{1}}D_{2}^{a_{2}}D_{3}^{a_{3}}}

(10)

We will always look at the case where

, when discussing the sunrise.

In the loop-by-loop Baikov representation, this integral may be written as

\displaystyle I^{\text{sunrise}}_{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{x_{4}^{-a_{4}}\,u\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x}{x_{1}^{a_{1}}x_{2}^{a_{2}}x_{3}^{a_{3}}}

(11)

where

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=x_{4}^{\epsilon}\times\big((m^{2}_{1}{-}m^{2}_{2}{+}x_{1}{-}x_{2})^{2}{-}2(m^{2}_{1}{+}m^{2}_{2}{+}x_{1}{+}x_{2})x_{4}{+}x_{4}^{2}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\times\big((m^{2}_{3}{-}s{+}x_{3})^{2}{-}2(m^{2}_{3}{+}s{+}x_{3})x_{4}{+}x_{4}^{2}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}

is the product of Baikov polynomials raised to their corresponding powers, and where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{4\epsilon}}{\pi\Gamma^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}-\epsilon)}

(13)

is a pure function. The choice

On the maximal cut (i.e. of

,

\displaystyle u|_{3\times\text{cut}}

\displaystyle=z^{\epsilon}\;\big((m^{2}_{1}{-}m^{2}_{2})^{2}{-}2(m^{2}_{1}{+}m^{2}_{2})z{+}z^{2}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\;\big((m^{2}_{3}{-}s)^{2}{-}2(m^{2}_{3}{+}s)z{+}z^{2}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}

\displaystyle I^{\text{sunrise}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\int_{\mathcal{C}}u|_{3\times\text{cut}}\,\hat{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}z

We note that

2
Motivation

In this section we will discuss two simple examples of non-elliptic Feynman integrals, and show how to find their canonical forms with the traditional method. This method will then be reformulated in terms of diagonalization of the period matrix, giving a formulation that generalizes directly to the elliptic case.

2.1
The double box

Let us as an illustration of the procedure discuss the well-known fully massless double box. That family of Feynman integrals (first computed in
Smirnov:1999gc
;
Smirnov:1999wz
) was used as the example in ref.
Henn:2013pwa
which introduced the concept of epsilon-factorized differential equations. It is given by the set of propagators

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}{+}p_{1})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{3}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}{+}p_{1}{+}p_{2})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{4}

\displaystyle=(k_{2}{+}p_{1}{+}p_{2})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{5}

\displaystyle=(k_{2}{-}p_{4})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{6}

\displaystyle=k_{2}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{7}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}{-}k_{2})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{8}

\displaystyle=(k_{1}{-}p_{4})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{9}

\displaystyle=(k_{2}{+}p_{1})^{2}\,,

(16)

of which only the first seven are allowed to appear as genuine propagators.

The family contains eight master integrals of which two are in the highest sector, and it is those two that will interest us in the following. In ref.
Henn:2013pwa
it was found that a set of integrals giving epsilon-factorized differential equations are

\displaystyle J_{1}=cs^{2}tI^{\text{db}}_{111111100}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{2}=cs^{2}I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}

(17)

where
c=\epsilon^{4}s^{-2\epsilon}
is a pure prefactor to be ignored in the following.
How can we know
a priori
the two prefactors
s^{2}t
and
s^{2}
?

One way is to perform the maximal cut (in the sense of cutting the seven actual propagators) of the integrals in the family. Using the loop-by-loop Baikov parametrization, this gives

\displaystyle I^{\text{db}}_{1111111a0}|_{\text{7-cut}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathcal{K}}{s^{2}}\int(z-t)^{-1-2\epsilon}z^{-a-1-\epsilon}(s+z)^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}z

(18)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}1+2\epsilon}s^{\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}2\epsilon}\,t^{\epsilon}\,(s{+}t)^{\epsilon}}{\pi^{3}\Gamma^{2}(\tfrac{1}{2}{-}\epsilon)}

(19)

is a pure function.

The prescription is now to factorize out the pure part of the integrand, which here corresponds to taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
of the object under the integral sign. This (ignoring the already pure
\mathcal{K}
) gives the integrand

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{-z^{-a}}{s^{2}z(z-t)}

(20)

Continuing the cutting procedure shows us that
I^{\text{db}}_{111111100}
has two residues. One at
z=0
evaluating to
1/(s^{2}t)
and the other at
z=t
evaluating to
-1/(s^{2}t)
. On the other hand
I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}
also has two residues, one at
z=t
evaluating to
-1/(s^{2})
and the other at
z=\infty
evaluating to
1/(s^{2})
. So we see that the two integrals become pure if
I^{\text{db}}_{111111100}
is given a prefactor of
s^{2}t
and
I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}
a prefactor of
s^{2}
, as it is given by eq. (
17
).

A different way of formulating the procedure is by writing up the period matrix for the two integrals, again after factorizing out and discarding the pure part of
u
. We pick the two integration cycles as
\gamma_{1}
being the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{0}
surrounding the
(z{=}0)
-pole and
\gamma_{2}
being
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
surrounding the
(z{=}\infty)
-pole, with the various contours shown on fig.
2
. Giving the two integrals prefactors
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
we get the period matrix

\displaystyle P_{ij}=\int_{\gamma_{j}}\!\!f_{i}\hat{\Phi}_{i}\mathrm{d}z\quad\Rightarrow\quad P=2\pi i\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{f_{1}}{s^{2}t}&0\\
0&\frac{f_{2}}{s^{2}}\end{array}\right]

and requiring the period matrix to be
2\pi i
times the unit matrix
I
fixes the
f_{i}
to the values found above.

Contours discussed for the double box

Making this procedure more general and algorithmic, we could also write each of the canonical integrals we want to find as general linear combinations of the two precanonical intermediate basis integrals
I^{\text{db}}_{111111100}
and
I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}
, that is

\displaystyle J_{1}=f_{11}I^{\text{db}}_{111111100}+f_{12}I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}

\displaystyle J_{2}=f_{21}I^{\text{db}}_{111111100}+f_{22}I^{\text{db}}_{1111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}

(23)

This gives the period matrix

\displaystyle P=2\pi i\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{f_{11}}{s^{2}t}&\frac{f_{12}}{s^{2}}\\[2.84526pt]

and once again imposing

fixes the

\displaystyle f_{11}=s^{2}t\,,\qquad f_{12}=0\,,\qquad f_{21}=0\,,\qquad f_{22}=s^{2}\,,

again corresponding to the prefactors given by eq. (

The two-mass non-elliptic sunrise

Let us look at another non-elliptic example that looks more similar to the elliptic examples we will encounter later.

We will make a choice of intermediate basis integrals (precanonicals) as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{1}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\phi}_{2}=z

\displaystyle g_{11}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{1}}\!U\hat{\phi}_{1}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{2\pi i}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{2}}\!U\hat{\phi}_{1}\mathrm{d}z=0

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{1}}\!U\hat{\phi}_{2}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{2\pi is}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}

\displaystyle g_{22}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{2}}\!U\hat{\phi}_{2}\mathrm{d}z=-2\pi i

\displaystyle J_{1}=f_{11}I^{\text{sne}}_{11100}+f_{12}I^{\text{sne}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}\qquad\quad\text{and}\qquad\quad J_{2}=f_{21}I^{\text{sne}}_{11100}+f_{22}I^{\text{sne}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{2\pi i(f_{11}+sf_{12})}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}&-2\pi if_{12}\\
\frac{2\pi i(f_{21}+sf_{22})}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}&-2\pi if_{22}\end{array}\right]

Solving for
P=2\pi iI
gives

\displaystyle f_{11}=\sqrt{s(s{-}4m^{2})}\,,\qquad f_{12}=0\,,\qquad f_{21}=s\,,\qquad f_{22}=-1\,,

(34)

corresponding to

\displaystyle J_{1}=\sqrt{s(s{-}4m^{2})}I^{\text{sne}}_{11100}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{2}=sI^{\text{sne}}_{11100}-I^{\text{sne}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}

(35)

With this we may compute the epsilon factorized differential equation matrix

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}J_{i}}{\mathrm{d}s}=\epsilon A_{ij}J_{j}\qquad\text{with}\qquad A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{4(s{-}m^{2})}{(4m^{2}{-}s)s}&\frac{3}{\sqrt{s(s{-}4m^{2})}}\\
\frac{-2}{\sqrt{s(s{-}4m^{2})}}&\frac{1}{s}\end{array}\right]

written disregarding integrals in lower sectors (here only the double-tadpole
I^{\text{sne}}_{11000}
) so we see that our method works in this case as well. This success is what motivates us to try the same approach in elliptic cases.

3
The proposed algorithm

Motivated by the examples in the previous section, we are now ready to formulate our algorithm. Having a set of
\nu
integrals of the form

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\int_{\mathcal{C}}u\hat{\varphi}_{i}\mathrm{d}^{n}x

(38)

where as discussed in section
1.1

u
is a multivalued function, and
\hat{\varphi}_{i}
a set of
\nu
rational (in
x
) functions, our claim is that if the integrand can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}u\hat{\varphi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma\hat{\Phi}_{i}

(39)

where
\sigma
is a pure function, and where the period matrix
P
with
P_{ij}=\int_{\gamma_{j}}\!\!\hat{\Phi}_{i}\mathrm{d}^{n}x
is proportional to the identity matrix as
P=(2\pi i)^{n}I
, then the integrals
J_{i}
will fulfill epsilon-factorized differential equations. In cases where
\mathcal{K}
is itself pure to begin with, it can be ignored in the above discussion.

What we will do in practice is to write the basis integrals as a linear combination of known intermediate basis integrals
I_{j}
(often called precanonical in the polylogarithmic case,) that is
J_{i}=f_{ij}I_{j}
or correspondingly as
\hat{\Phi}_{i}=f_{ij}\hat{\Phi}^{\text{int}}_{j}
, and then imposing
P=(2\pi i)^{n}I
will impose
\nu^{2}
constraints on the
f_{ij}
fixing them all uniquely.

In the examples in the following section, we will write the
\hat{\Phi}^{\text{int}}_{\;}
as
\hat{\Phi}^{\text{int}}_{i}=\hat{\phi}_{i}/Y
where
Y
is the square root of a (monic) polynomial of degree four, corresponding to the elliptic curve characterizing the problem.

4
Examples

In this section we will look at examples of elliptic Feynman integrals which we can bring into a form that has epsilon-factorized differential equations with the above algorithm. We will restrict the discussion to cases where the ellipticity is present in the highest sector only, and where a maximal cut can bring the integrals in that sector to a univariate form.

4.1
The same-mass elliptic sunrise

As a first example let us look at the most basic of elliptic Feynman integrals, the same-mass elliptic sunrise (s1m). This is defined as in section
1.2

5
Discussion

5.1
Form and integration of the differential equations

One topic that has been avoided so far in this paper, is how to solve the differential equations once the epsilon-factorized form has been obtained. For Feynman integrals in the traditional canonical form it is often straight forward to integrate them up order by order in the epsilon expansion, giving results in the function class of generalized polylogarithms
Goncharov:1998kja
, (at least in the case where all square-roots can be rationalized, which is however known to not always be possible
Brown:2020rda
). Many attempts have been made to extend this function class to elliptic cases and beyond
brown2011multiple
;
Adams:2014vja
;
Broedel:2017kkb
;
Adams:2017ejb
, yet none of those seem directly applicable to the form of the differential equations derived in this paper. Further investigations will be needed into how to best proceed analytically from the differential equations found here.

If, however, one is satisfied with a numerical approach, the development of techniques to numerically integrate this type of differential equations is developing quickly
Boughezal:2007ny
;
Czakon:2007qi
;
Aglietti:2007as
;
Lee:2017qql
;
Mandal:2018cdj
;
Moriello:2019yhu
;
Hidding:2020ytt
. Not all of these approaches require the differential equations to be epsilon factorized, but they all benefit from from it and work faster in that case, so even in the worst case scenario the techniques developed here can be used to speed up the numerics.

We do however notice one property that the differential equations found on the previous pages share with those in traditional canonical form and which might cause optimism for the prospect of analytical integration: They are free of higher poles at singular points including the point at infinity. This property is not obvious, it might appear as if for instance the
A_{11}
-element of the differential equation matrix found for the same-mass sunrise of eqs. (
55
) would have a higher pole in
\sqrt{s}=-m
. But that is not the case. Changing variable to
y=\sqrt{s}+m
, setting
m=1
, and expanding, that matrix element becomes

\displaystyle A^{\text{s1m};(y)}_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg(\big(6{-}2\pi i{+}6\log(y/4)\big)\frac{1}{y}+\tfrac{1}{4}\big(15{-}7\pi i{+}(33{-}6i\pi)\log(y/4){+}18\log^{2}(y/4)\big)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{64}\big(273{-}39i\pi{+}(309{-}60i\pi)\log(y/4){+}180\log^{2}(y/4)\big)y+\mathcal{O}(y^{2})\bigg)

(90)

and we see that no higher pole in
y
is present, the candidate having canceled with a term from the expansion of the elliptic integrals.

5.2
Freedom in basis choice

There is some amount of freedom in the algorithm discussed on the previous pages. This freedom corresponds to performing row operation on the period matrix. For instance one might define the master contours
\gamma_{i}
as some linear combination of the ones used in this paper. In all the elliptic examples we chose
\gamma_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{\text{ii-iii}}
and
\gamma_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{\text{i-ii}}
, but one might equally well have chosen for instance
\gamma_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{\text{iii-iv}}
which would yield a slightly different set of integrals in the end, and also there is nothing preventing the choice of some
\mathbb{C}
-linear combination of contours, such as
\gamma=\tfrac{5}{7}\mathcal{C}_{\text{ii-iii}}+\tfrac{8i}{13}\mathcal{C}_{\text{i-ii}}
. Yet these kinds of redefinitions seem to provide no simplification of the integrals or their differential equations. Likewise one might take linear combinations of the resulting integrals, since if the set
J
has epsilon factorized differential equations so will a
\mathbb{C}
-linear combination
\tilde{J}=BJ
with
B_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
. For example for the case of the two-mass elliptic sunrise, it seems from eqs. (
77
) as if it would provide a slight simplification to perform the replacement
J_{3}^{\text{s2m}}\rightarrow\tilde{J}_{3}^{\text{s2m}}=J_{3}^{\text{s2m}}-\tfrac{1}{2}J_{2}^{\text{s2m}}
.

Yet as mentioned in the introduction, there are previous examples in the literature in which elliptic Feynman integrals have been put into a form that allows for epsilon factorized differential equations, and which are not equal, or in a form related through the above mentioned transformations, to the expressions found here.
In particular the same-mass sunrise of section
4.1
has been discussed in ref.
Adams:2018yfj
, and the three-mass sunrise of section
4.4
has been discussed in ref.
Bogner:2019lfa
. It must be admitted that the integrals found in those references are “nicer” than those found with the algorithm outlined here. In particular the matrix of
f_{ij}
coefficients are in both cases found to be something more akin to a lower triangular form and the differential equation matrix is nicer as well as we will see. Focusing on the same-mass sunrise, the form found in ref.
Adams:2018yfj
has
f_{12}=0
which means that the master integrals there may be written

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{\text{s1m}}_{1}

\displaystyle=cf_{11}I^{\text{s1m}}_{11100}

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{\text{s1m}}_{2}

\displaystyle=c(f_{21}I^{\text{s1m}}_{11100}+f_{22}I^{\text{s1m}}_{21100})

(91)

where the values are found to

\displaystyle f_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\epsilon\pi R}{4K(k^{2})}

\displaystyle f_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{RE(k^{2})}{4\pi}+\frac{\big(3(m^{2}{-}s)(9m^{2}{-}s)+2\epsilon(45m^{4}{-}30m^{2}s{+}s^{2})\big)K(k^{2})}{12\pi R}

(92)

\displaystyle f_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}R(\sqrt{m^{2}}{-}\sqrt{s})(3\sqrt{m^{2}}{+}\sqrt{s})K(k^{2})}{\pi(\sqrt{m^{2}}{+}\sqrt{s})^{2}}

with the definitions of

. We see that these expressions are quite different from what was found in eqs. (

From this we may compute the differential equations. They may be written as

It would be great if some principle could be found to a priori generate the transformation between the forms discussed in this paper, and forms similar to the one discussed above.

5.3
The number of cycles

In principle there is a one-to-one correspondence between the number of master integrals and the number of independent cycles
Lee:2013hzt
;
Bitoun:2017nre
;
Frellesvig:2019uqt
. This correspondence is utilized by the Lee-Pomeransky criterion
Lee:2013hzt
, which states that the number of master integrals
\nu
is given as

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\text{number of solutions to ``}\omega=0\text{''}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\omega:=\mathrm{d}\text{log}(u)

(96)

We can test this for the examples discussed in secs.
2
and
4
of this paper. The result is that the numbers agree in all the cases, except for the same-mass sunrise of sec.
4.1
and the nonplanar double triangle of sec.
4.2
, which are the two cases in which an elliptic sector has two master integrals. For the case of the same-mass sunrise, this “miscounting” is well known, and is discussed in further detail in refs.
Frellesvig:2019kgj
;
Weinzierl:2020xyy
where it is shown to be caused by peculiarities in the interplay between the loop-by-loop Baikov parametrization and the maximal cut.

Focusing on the same-mass sunrise it might seem as if a valid third master integral would be
I_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}^{\text{s1m}}
corresponding to
\hat{\phi}_{3}{=}z
, with a corresponding third master contour surrounding the pole at infinity
\gamma_{3}{=}\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
making the set of cycles look as in the case of two-mass elliptic sunrise of fig.
6
. Yet we know from IBP relations that

\displaystyle I_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}^{\text{s1m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3m^{2}{+}s}{3}I_{11100}^{\text{s1m}}\,+\,\text{lower}

(97)

and it might be instructive to see how this relation is realized over the basic cycles. Using the
\hat{\phi}_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
from sec.
4.1
, and again defining
g_{ij}=\int_{\gamma_{j}}\hat{\phi}_{i}\mathrm{d}z/Y
, we get

\displaystyle g_{31}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{1}}\!\frac{\hat{\phi}_{3}\mathrm{d}z}{Y}=2(\sqrt{m^{2}}{-}\sqrt{s})\left(2\sqrt{m^{2}}K(k^{2})-(3\sqrt{m^{2}}{+}\sqrt{s})\Pi(n^{2},k^{2})\right)\!\big/R

\displaystyle g_{32}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{2}}\!\frac{\hat{\phi}_{3}\mathrm{d}z}{Y}=-4i\left(\sqrt{m^{2}}(\sqrt{m^{2}}{-}\sqrt{s})K(1{-}k^{2})+2\sqrt{m^{2}}\sqrt{s}\Pi(n^{\prime 2},1{-}k^{2})\right)\!\big/R+i\pi

(98)

\displaystyle g_{13}

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma_{3}}\!\frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}\mathrm{d}z}{Y}=0\qquad\qquad g_{23}=\int_{\gamma_{3}}\!\frac{\hat{\phi}_{2}\mathrm{d}z}{Y}=0\qquad\qquad g_{33}=\int_{\gamma_{3}}\!\frac{\hat{\phi}_{3}\mathrm{d}z}{Y}=-2\pi i

where
k^{2}
and
R
are as defined in sec.
4.1
, and where

\displaystyle n^{2}=\frac{4m^{2}}{(\sqrt{m^{2}}{+}\sqrt{s})^{2}}\qquad\quad\text{and}\qquad\quad n^{\prime 2}=\frac{(\sqrt{m^{2}}{-}\sqrt{s})^{2}}{(\sqrt{m^{2}}{+}\sqrt{s})^{2}}

(99)

If eq. (
97
) were to hold on the contour
\gamma_{j}
it would mean that
g_{3j}=((3m^{2}{+}s)/3)g_{1j}
, so let us investigate the status of that relation on each contour.

Starting by
\gamma_{1}
, it might seem unlikely for the relation to hold since
g_{31}
contains
\Pi(n^{2},k^{2})
, which
g_{11}
does not. But it does hold exactly, due to the relation

\displaystyle\Pi\!\left(\frac{4}{(1{+}x)^{2}},\frac{-16x}{(x{-}3)(1{+}x)^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2x}{3(x{-}1)}K\!\left(\frac{-16x}{(x{-}3)(1{+}x)^{3}}\right)

(100)

(valid for
x\in[-1,1]
). On the other hand for contour
\gamma_{3}
the relation clearly does not hold since
g_{13}=0
, while
g_{33}=-2\pi i
which is then the amount with which the relation is broken. At the last contour
\gamma_{2}
we get using a similar relation for a special point of
\Pi(n^{2},k^{2})
, that

\displaystyle g_{32}-\frac{3m^{2}{+}s}{3}g_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi i}{3}

(101)

so we see that when the peculiarities of the loop-by-loop Baikov parametrization ruins the counting of master contours, relations such as eq. (
97
) break only by a factor proportional to
2\pi i
.

For the nonplanar double triangle of sec.
4.2
the behaviour is extremely similar. There the extra IBP-derived integral relation relating an integral with a pole at infinity to the original two, is

\displaystyle I_{111111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}1}^{\text{npt}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-s}{2}I_{1111110}^{\text{npt}}\,+\,\text{lower}

(102)

and that relation holds exactly on contour
\gamma_{1}
and is broken with factors proportional to
2\pi i
on
\gamma_{2}
and a potential
\gamma_{3}
surrounding a pole at infinity. The main difference is that the
\Pi(n^{2},k^{2})
special value relation that makes this explicit is even simpler in that case.

5.4
Freedom in intermediate basis choice

One might consider if the intermediate bases chosen in the examples discussed in secs.
2
and
4
have some special property that allows for the algorithm to go through that would not be shared with any arbitrary basis choice. The answer to this is mostly no, any set of valid master integrals might be chosen as the intermediate basis. Let us illustrate that with an example, that of the two-mass elliptic sunrise of sec.
4.3
. Instead of the choice of (
I^{\text{s2m}}_{11100}
,
I^{\text{s2m}}_{21100}
,
I^{\text{s2m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}
) made in sec.
4.3
, one might instead pick for instance the set (
I^{\text{s2m}}_{11100}
,
I^{\text{s2m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}
,
I^{\text{s2m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}20}
) which is the choice naturally made by Kira.

With this one can go through the same procedure as before, define

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{i}=\tilde{f}_{i1}I^{\text{s2m}}_{11100}+\tilde{f}_{i2}I^{\text{s2m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}10}+\tilde{f}_{i3}I^{\text{s2m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}20}

(103)

compute the period matrix, and impose it to be proportional to
I
thereby fixing the
\tilde{f}_{ij}
. The resulting
\tilde{J}_{i}
equal those from sec.
4.3
up to integrals in lower sectors as well as terms of order
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{i}=J_{i}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)+\text{lower}

(104)

Additionally the new integrals have epsilon factorized differential equations
\mathrm{d}\tilde{J}_{i}/\mathrm{d}x=\epsilon\tilde{A}^{(x)}_{ij}\tilde{J}_{j}
in each of the three kinematic variables
x\in\{m_{a}^{2},m_{b}^{2},s\}
. The expressions for
\tilde{f}
and the
\tilde{A}^{(x)}
can be found in the file added to the arXiv version of this paper.

There are however cases in which not any choice of intermediate basis is equally convenient. This is true in the cases that have the “miscounting” of independent cycles discussed in sec.
5.3
, such as the same-mass elliptic sunrise. There we saw that a counting of independent cycles seems to include a third master contour surrounding a pole at infinity
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
, and that the relation reducing integrals with support there to integrals with support only on the original two
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ii-iii}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{i-ii}}
is realized only up to factors proportional to
2\pi i
. For the same-mass elliptic sunrise, (
I^{\text{s1m}}_{11100}
,
I^{\text{s1m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}20}
) which is chosen as the default by Kira would be a valid alternative to the intermediate basis discussed in section
4.1
. But since the integrand of
I^{\text{s1m}}_{111\scalebox{0.50}[0.75]{$\,-$}20}
has support on
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
, such a factor proportional to
2\pi i
has to be removed by hand before a basis with epsilon factorized differential equations is obtained.

5.5
Further discussion, open questions, and conclusions

In all the examples in this paper, we only discussed the highest sector for the integral families we looked at, which allowed us to only approach the system on its maximal cut. Yet for practical applications one would need the whole system of differential equations to be in epsilon-factorized form, not just the highest sector. Presumably the approach discussed here could be generalized away from the maximal cut, by introducing a multivariate notion of master contours, and indeed this is the approach taken in the prescriptive unitarity scheme
Bourjaily:2017wjl
;
Bourjaily:2021vyj
. Yet to bring subsectors into epsilon-factorized form, I do not believe this is needed. It will be enough to fit coefficients of lower sector integrals using an ansatz with its free coefficients fixed from imposing the epsilon-factorized property of the differential equations, starting from the highest subsectors going down, along the lines of the method proposed in ref.
Gehrmann:2014bfa
for the non-elliptic case.

Likewise the discussion here was limited to the case where all subsectors were free of elliptic contributions and could be put into canonical form in the traditional sense. For integrals for which that is not the case (such as the kite integrals that will be obtained by promoting
D_{4}
and
D_{5}
to genuine propagators for the elliptic sunrise integrals) presumably a similar approach can be taken, but that is a matter for further study.

The canonical form proposed in ref.
Henn:2013pwa
not only implied an epsilon-factorized form for the differential equations of eq. (
1
), but further imply that the distinct differential equations for each kinematic variable are joined in one differential of dlog-form
\mathrm{d}J=\epsilon(\mathrm{d}M)J
where
M
is a matrix containing only logarithms of algebraic functions of the kinematic variables. An obvious question is if the differential equations found here can likewise be unified into one differential form
\mathrm{d}J=\epsilon(\mathrm{d}M)J
, but where
M
obviously no longer will contain solely logarithms. In the polylogarithmic case, the arguments of those logarithms are the symbol letters
Goncharov:2010jf
;
Duhr:2012fh
of the problem, so an obvious open question is if the entries of
M
in the elliptic case, if it even exists, will be related to the entries of the elliptic generalization of the coaction
Broedel:2018iwv
in a similar straight forward fashion. For the polylogarithmic case, the fact that the algorithm discussed in this paper works in the first place, may be seen as following from the existence of an iterated dlog representation for the whole integral
Arkani-Hamed:2010pyv
;
WasserMSc
;
Herrmann:2019upk
;
Dlapa:2021qsl
, and the question of how such a representation might generalize to the elliptic case is presumably also linked to the above.

The answers to such questions will help clarify what the “correct” generalization of terms such as
pure

Broedel:2018qkq
,
canonical
,
weight
, etc. that have proven extremely helpful in the understanding of polylogarithmic Feynman integrals, will be to the elliptic case and beyond, if such generalizations can even be made in a useful way. I did not attempt to make any such generalizations in this work, but I hope that my approach contributes with insights that may help bring us to a point where such a step might be taken.

The algorithm presented in this paper is able to systematically bring differential equations for elliptic Feynman integrals into a form for which the differential equations are epsilon factorized. We demonstrated this for a number of examples of varying complexity, and it is the hope that this development will be one step on the way towards bringing the understanding of elliptic Feynman integrals to the same level as the polylogarithmic case.

Appendix A
A different form for the elliptic nonplanar double triangle

In the example discussed in section
5.2
, we saw that a valid form for the equal mass sunrise integral
Adams:2018yfj
resulting in an
\epsilon
-factorized differential equation could be made without the diagonal period matrix otherwise discussed in this paper. That example had
f_{12}=0
and
P_{11}
and
\text{det}(P)
both being constants. Using these properties as a guideline, we were able to find an epsilon factorized basis for the nonplanar double triangle of section
4.2
, which has two master integrals in the elliptic sector similarly to the equal mass sunrise.

We write the general form

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{\text{npt}}_{1}

\displaystyle=cf_{11}I^{\text{npt}}_{1111110}

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{\text{npt}}_{2}

\displaystyle=c(f_{21}I^{\text{npt}}_{1111110}+f_{22}I^{\text{npt}}_{2111110})

(105)

where we have used
f_{12}=0
. Imposing
P_{11}
and
\text{det}(P)
being constants puts two constraints on the remaining
f_{ij}
while the third may be fitted by requiring the differential equation to be
\epsilon
-factorized.
We find the values

\displaystyle f_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon\,s^{3/2}(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{+}\sqrt{s})}{4K(k^{2})}

\displaystyle f_{21}

\displaystyle=-s^{3/2}\left((\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{+}\sqrt{s})E(k^{2})+(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{-}\sqrt{s})\left(1+\epsilon\frac{24m^{2}{+}s}{m^{2}}\right)K(k^{2})\right)

(106)

\displaystyle f_{22}

\displaystyle=-s^{3/2}(16m^{2}{+}s)(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{-}\sqrt{s})K(k^{2})

which corresponds to the period matrix (given the two contours defined in eqs. (
63
))

\displaystyle P_{11}

\displaystyle=2\epsilon\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad P_{12}=-2i\epsilon\frac{K(1{-}k^{2})}{K(k^{2})}

\displaystyle P_{21}

\displaystyle=2\epsilon K(k^{2})\left(8E(k^{2})-\frac{(16m^{2}{+}s)(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{-}\sqrt{s})}{m^{2}(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{+}\sqrt{s})}K(k^{2})\right)

(107)

\displaystyle P_{22}

\displaystyle=4i\pi+2i\epsilon K(k^{2})\left(8E(1{-}k^{2})+\frac{\big((8m^{2}{+}s)\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}-(24m^{2}{+}s)\sqrt{s}\big)}{m^{2}(\sqrt{s}{+}\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s})}K(1{-}k^{2})\right)

with the property

\displaystyle\text{det}(P)

\displaystyle=8i\pi\epsilon(1{+}2\epsilon)

(108)

and we see the two requirements imposed above, being apparent.

The corresponding
s
-differential equation
\mathrm{d}\tilde{J}^{\text{npt}}_{i}/\mathrm{d}s=\epsilon\tilde{A}_{ij}\tilde{J}^{\text{npt}}_{j}
has

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(8m^{2}{+}s)}{s(16m^{2}{+}s)}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\;\tilde{A}_{12}=\frac{(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{+}\sqrt{s})^{2}}{8\sqrt{2}s(16m^{2}{+}s)K(k^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\sqrt{2}(8m^{2}{+}s)^{2}K(k^{2})^{2}}{s(16m^{2}{+}s)(\sqrt{16m^{2}{+}s}{+}\sqrt{s})^{2}}\qquad\qquad\qquad\tilde{A}_{22}=\frac{-(8m^{2}{+}s)}{s(16m^{2}{+}s)}

(109)

with a similar differential equation in the other variable
m^{2}
. This form of the differential equation resembles that given in eq. (
93

Adams:2018yfj
.

It would be interesting to generalize these considerations to a more general elliptic case, or preferably to find a principle that allows for reliably generating a transformation that can map between the forms discussed in the bulk of this paper and forms similar to the above, but to what extend that is possible is a question for the future.
