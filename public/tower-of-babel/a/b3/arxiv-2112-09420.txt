A random energy approach to deep learning Thanks: marsili@ictp.it
Rongrong Xie

Abstract
We study a generic ensemble of deep belief networks which is parametrized by the distribution of energy levels of the hidden states of each layer. We show that, within a random energy approach, statistical dependence can propagate from the visible to deep layers only if each layer is tuned close to the critical point during learning. As a consequence, efficiently trained learning machines are characterised by a broad distribution of energy levels. The analysis of Deep Belief Networks and Restricted Boltzmann Machines on different datasets confirms these conclusions.



1
Introduction

The study of ensembles of random systems can provide several insights on the properties of complex systems, such as heavy ions
[
29
]
, ecologies
[
15
]
, disordered materials
[
17
]
, satisfiability in computer science
[
18
]
and machine learning
[
30
]
. Indeed the collective behaviour of a system composed of many interacting degrees of freedom often does not depend on the specific realisation of the wiring of the interactions, but only on the statistical properties of the resulting energy landscape. In these circumstances, any realisation of a random system that shares the same statistical properties enjoys the same “typical" collective behaviour.

The Random Energy Model (REM)
[
6
]
is probably the simplest exemplar of this approach. It makes minimal assumptions on the network of interactions, because interactions of any order can occur among the variables
[
6
]
. It features a phase transition between a random (high temperature) phase and a low temperature frozen phase, which reproduces the gross features of more complex systems such as spin glasses.

Here we adopt the same approach to study systems that form an internal representation of a complex environment, such as deep belief networks (DBN). A DBN is composed of a stack of layers of variables, which interact only with variables in neighbouring layers, as shown in Fig.
1
a). The bottom layer is in contact with the data that the network seeks to learn, that is akin to the environment for a physical system. Typically the data has a non-trivial hidden structure of statistical dependencies, which the learning machine aims at extracting.

During training, the interaction strengths are adjusted in such a way that the distribution of internal states of the machine form a representation of the data, in the sense that when a state is drawn from this distribution and it is propagated to the bottom layer it reproduces the distribution of the data with which the machine had been trained. The distribution of internal states after training depends on the structure of the data, on the machine’s architecture and on the initial condition for the weights (which is generally assumed random, see e.g.
[
11
]
), and is generally characterised by a complex energy landscape, with effective interactions between variables of arbitrary order. In this paper, we’re going to consider this system as a draw from an ensemble of random systems in such a way that each layer can be thought of as a REM. This allows us to characterise the properties of learned representations within a rather general framework. Our main conclusion is that, in order for the information on the data’s statistical dependencies to propagate to the deep layers, each layer has to be tuned to the critical point.

There is extensive evidence that learning efficiency and criticality are related
[
12
,
3
,
21
]
. Critical models that are close to a phase transition or the edge of chaos are much better learners and generalisers than non-critical ones. The brain itself is conjectured to operate close to a critical state
[
2
,
20
]
. Criticality affords several benefits to information processing
[
25
]
but there is no agreed general rationale that explains the ubiquitously observed relation between criticality and efficiency in learning. For deep learning, in particular, Schoenholtz
et al.

[
24
]
reach conclusions similar to ours. The study deep (deterministic) neural networks with random Gaussian weights and argue that “only when the statistics of weights and variances is such that the network is close to the edge of chaos the signal propagates from the bottom to deep layers”. Yet their results apply to untrained networks with random weights. Our more general approach reaches the same conclusion for well trained deep (probabilistic) neural networks, irrespective of the statistics of the weights, of the data and of the architecture.
A similar REM approach can be extended to generic systems who form internal representations of their fluctuating environment, thereby offering a rationale for the observation of criticality in living systems
[
19
]
.

In what follows we shall first set the stage in Section
2
by introducing the main problem and the notations. Next we shall focus on a random energy description of learning machines in Section
3
. We shall end with a final discussion.

2
The general framework

In order to set the stage, let us discuss the generic properties of a systems with an architecture similar to that of deep belief networks (DBN)
[
22
]

\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{\ell+1},\mathbf{s}_{\ell},\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{1}

(1)

In Eq. (

(2)

is the marginal distribution of layer
\ell

\ell+1,\ell+2,\ldots
.

In an efficiently trained learning machine the statistical dependence needs to extend across different layers, in such a way that the state
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
of a deep layer should significantly depend on
\mathbf{s}_{0}
(see Fig.
1

obtained from Eq. (
1

Typical cases of interest are those where the data has a non-trivial structure. This is the case when the variation of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
across the samples spans a low dimensional manifold of intrinsic dimension
d
which is much smaller than the number
n_{0}
of components of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
. Ansuini
et al.

[
1
]
, for example, estimate that the MNIST dataset (
n_{0}=784
) spans a space with intrinsic dimension
d\simeq 13
. In loose terms, learning entails extracting a compressed representation
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
of the structure of statistical dependencies of the data.

2.1
Compression levels and the clamped distribution

A natural measure for the level of compression is the entropy of the internal representation of the data.
A proxy for this quantity is obtained considering the
distribution of clamped states

q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
. This is obtained presenting each data point
\mathbf{s}_{0}^{(i)}
on the visible layer and finding the most likely corresponding
clamped
state

\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{*}(\mathbf{s}_{0}^{(i)})={\rm arg}\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell}|\mathbf{s}_{0}^{(i)})

(3)

of layer
\ell
.
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
is the fraction of data points
\mathbf{s}_{0}^{(i)}
in the sample for which the clamped state coincides with
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
. The entropy

\hat{H}[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]=-\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})\log q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})

(4)

of the distribution of clamped states provides a measure of the compression level achieved in layer
\ell
.
When
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell}|\mathbf{s}_{0}^{(i)})
is a sharply peaked distribution, as is the case for well trained learning machines
[
26
,
27
]
,
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
is representative of the distribution obtained sampling the distribution
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell}|\mathbf{s}_{0})
of internal states, when
\mathbf{s}_{0}
is a sampled datapoint. In addition, a well trained machine is expected to reproduce the (unknown) distribution of the data by the model
p(\mathbf{s}_{0})
that is obtained from Eq. (
1
) by marginalisation over the hidden layers. In these cases, we expect that
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})\approx p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
approximates well the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
, and that the entropy
\hat{H}[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]
provides an estimate of the mutual information
I(\mathbf{s}_{\ell},\mathbf{s}_{0})=H[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]-H[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}|\mathbf{s}_{0}]
between the data and the internal representation, because
\hat{H}[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]\approx H[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]
and
H[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}|\mathbf{s}_{0}]\approx 0
.

\hat{H}[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]
as a function of layer depth
\ell

. As Fig.

H[\mathbf{s}]\leq\log N
, whereas untrained DBNs generate representations whose entropy is either close to the entropy of the data (
\log N

3
A random energy theory of deep learning

In order to discuss the statistical mechanics properties of the DBN, we consider it as a system in thermal equilibrium with a heat bath at inverse temperature
\beta=1
. Hence the Hamiltonian of the system is obtained by taking the logarithm of the joint distribution in Eq. (
1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log p(\mathbf{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{\ell})

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{1}}+\ldots+u_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}+E_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}-E_{0}.

(6)

Apart for a constant
E_{0}
, which plays no major role in what follows, the Hamiltonian is a
sum of a term
E_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}=E_{0}-\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
for layer
\ell
and of interaction terms

u_{\mathbf{s}_{k-1}|\mathbf{s}_{k}}=-\log p(\mathbf{s}_{k-1}|\mathbf{s}_{k})
between layers, for
k=1,\ldots,\ell
.

For a particular task, each of these terms takes a value which depends on the values of the weights with which the network is initialised and – for a trained network – on the dataset
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{0}
with which the DBN is trained.
In order to shed light on the generic nature of the energy landscape, we will compare the behaviour of
learning machines with that of models where the terms in Eq. (
6
) are taken at random from a distribution, as in random energy models
[
6
]
. As for models of spin glasses, the random energy assumption is questionable if taken too seriously. Yet, most of the results only depend on the degeneracy of energy levels (i.e. the number of states in a given energy range) so we believe that the conclusions that we shall derive provide a rather robust qualitative description of the statistical mechanics of probabilistic (deep) learning machines.

In what follows, we shall first discuss the properties of the internal layer
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
of a learning machine and then discuss the interaction with its environment, which is formed of the data and of the shallower layers.

3.1
Random energy description of a single layer

Let us first focus on the statistics of a generic hidden layer
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
. We shall drop the index
\ell
in this subsection.
In analogy with the REM, we assume that the energies
E_{\mathbf{s}}
are drawn from the distribution

P\{E_{\mathbf{s}}\leq-\Delta z\}=e^{-z^{\gamma}}\,,\qquad z\geq 0

(7)

independently for each state
\mathbf{s}
. The statistical properties of the learned model are parametrised by
\Delta>0
and
\gamma
, and they have been discussed in Ref.
[
6
,
16
]
for
\gamma=2
and in Ref.
[
14
]
for
\gamma>0
, to which we refer for technical details. The derivation of the main results is discussed in the Appendix. We shall confine our discussion to the case
\gamma\geq 1
, since it can be argued
[
14
]
that the properties of the REM with
\gamma<1
are not consistent with those of a system that learns.

The generic behaviour of the system in the thermodynamic limit, i.e. for
n\gg 1
, is characterised by a phase transition as
\Delta\to\Delta^{*}
, where

\Delta^{*}=\gamma\left(n\log 2\right)^{1-1/\gamma}.

(8)

For
\Delta<\Delta^{*}
the system exhibits a "high temperature" phase where the entropy

H[\mathbf{s}]\simeq\left[1-\left(\frac{\Delta}{\Delta^{*}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\right]n\log 2

(9)

is proportional to the number
n=n_{\ell}
of nodes (see
[
14
]
for details). The entropy
H[\mathbf{s}]
instead remains finite as
n\to\infty
for
\Delta>\Delta^{*}
. The transition is continuous but it becomes sharper and sharper as
\gamma\to 1^{+}
.
For finite systems,
H[\mathbf{s}]
exhibits a variation as a function of
\Delta
that become sharper and sharper in the neighbourhood of
\Delta^{*}
as
n
increases. The specific heat
C

which approaches
\Delta^{*}
as

grows large. The behaviour of

and

The critical point in the REM is located in the vicinity of the value

attains its maximum (see Fig.

(the activity of assembles of neurons in
[

]
). We observe that this construction explores the large deviation properties of the system, where the expected value of

) or large (
\beta<1
) values
2
2

2

This is not true when the construction is based on a distribution

of sampled states, as in Refs.

13
]

only contains information on the typical behaviour at

. Hence this construction singles out the compression level

as the order parameter of the transition. This is a natural choice in learning systems, that are built in order to extract the most compressed representation of high dimensional datasets. Cubero

show that this same construction implies that efficient codes in Minimum Description Length (MDL)

3.2
The relation between layers

The relation between internal states
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
and inputs
\mathbf{s}_{0}
can be studied by considering the most likely
clamped
states
\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell},\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell-1},\ldots\mathbf{s}^{*}_{1}
that the network associates to an input
\mathbf{s}_{0}
. These are the solution of the maximisation problem

\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell}(\mathbf{s}_{0})={\rm arg}\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}\left\{\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})+\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}}v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})\right\}

(10)

where
we defined

v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})=\max_{\mathbf{s}_{1}\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}}\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}).

(11)

The term
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})
characterises the “environment” with which the
\ell^{\rm th}
internal layer interacts with, and it satisfies a recursion relation

\displaystyle v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell})+\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}}\left[\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-3},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{1}}\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2},\mathbf{s}_{\ell-3},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1},\mathbf{s}_{\ell})\right]

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell})+\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}}\left[\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1})+v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})\right]

where we invoked the Markov property, i.e. the fact that
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1},\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
does not depend on
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
. Eq. (
12
) describes how the statistical dependencies propagate across layers and Eq. (
10
) how it determines the distribution of clamped states

q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})=P\{\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell}=\mathbf{s}_{\ell}\}.

(13)

Note that, if in some intermediate layer the optimisation on
\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}
in Eq. (
12
) is dominated by the first term
\log p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell-2}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1})
, then the dependence on
\mathbf{s}_{0}
of
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})
is lost. This means that the solution of Eq. (
10
) will also be independent of
\mathbf{s}_{0}
. In this case, the clamped distribution concentrates on just one state, which is what we observe in deep layers of untrained DBNs. On the other hand, if the optimisation in Eq. (
10
) is dominated by the term
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})
each different input will likely result in a different internal layer
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{*}
and
\hat{H}[\mathbf{s}_{\ell}]\simeq\log N
, as we observe in shallow layers of DBNs. These two extreme situations well characterise the behaviour of
H[\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell}]
observed in Fig.
1
for untrained learning machines.

Considering the input data
\mathbf{s}_{0}
as a random vector drawn from some (unknown) distribution implies that for all
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
and
\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}
,
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}
is a random variable that we assume is drawn independently from a distribution

P\{v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}>\Theta_{\ell}x-\bar{v}_{\ell}\}=e^{-x^{\theta_{\ell}}},\qquad(x\geq 0)\,,

(14)

where
\theta_{\ell}\geq 1
,
\Theta_{\ell}
sets the scale of the fluctuations of
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}
and
\bar{v}_{\ell}>0
is a constant that plays no role in what follows
3
3

3

\bar{v}_{\ell}
should be large enough so that
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}\leq 0
for all
\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n_{\ell-1}}
and
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n_{\ell}}
.
. Eq. (
14
) approximates the environment with which the
\ell^{\rm th}
layer interacts with a REM.

The distribution of clamped states can be computed as in Ref.
[
14
]
using results from extreme value theory
[
8
]
. Specifically, the intermediate maximisation of
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}
over
\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}
(see e.g. Eq.
10
) can be carried out explicitly, with the result

\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}}v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}\simeq a_{\ell}+\frac{\Theta_{\ell}}{\Theta^{*}_{\ell}}\eta_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}

where
a_{\ell}=\Theta_{\ell}(n_{\ell-1}\log 2)^{1/\theta_{\ell}}-\bar{v}_{\ell}
is a constant,
\eta_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}
is a random variable that follows a Gumbel distribution

P\{\eta_{\ell}<x\}=e^{-e^{-x}}.

(15)

and

\Theta^{*}_{\ell}=\theta_{\ell}(n_{\ell-1}\log 2)^{1-1/\theta_{\ell}}

(16)

coincides with the critical value of
\Delta^{*}
for
n=n_{\ell-1}
and
\theta_{\ell}=\gamma_{\ell}
(see Eq.
8
). Since the maximum is taken over
2^{n_{\ell-1}}
random variables, the characterisation provided by extreme value theory is accurate even for moderate values of
n_{\ell-1}
. This result can be used to compute the distribution of clamped states
\mathbf{s}^{*}_{\ell}
, as shown in Ref.
[
14
]
, i.e.

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\left\{\log p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}})+\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}}v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}\geq\log p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}})+\max_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}}v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}},~\forall\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}\right\}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\left\{\eta_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}}\leq\eta_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}+\beta\log\frac{p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}})}{p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}})},~\forall\mathbf{s}_{\ell}^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}})^{\beta},\qquad Z(\beta)=\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}p({\mathbf{s}_{\ell}})^{\beta}\,,

(18)

where we used Eq. (
15
) to compute Eq. (
17
) (see Appendix for more details). The exponent
\beta
is given by

\beta=\frac{\Theta^{*}_{\ell}}{\Theta_{\ell}}=\frac{\theta_{\ell}}{\Theta_{\ell}}(n_{\ell-1}\log 2)^{1-1/\theta_{\ell}}

(19)

that depends on the size
n_{\ell-1}
of layer
\ell-1
and on the parameter
\Theta_{\ell-1}
. Eq. (
18
) shows that the distribution of clamped states
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
coincides with the distribution
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
only when
\beta=1
, i.e. when the parameter
\Theta_{\ell}
is tuned to the parameter
\Theta^{*}_{\ell}
for which a REM with energies drawn from Eq. (
14
) is critical.
In other words, statistical dependence on the data
\mathbf{s}_{0}
can propagate to deep layers of a neural network only if the environment with which layer
\ell
interacts is tuned at the critical point, i.e.
\Theta_{\ell}\approx\Theta^{*}_{\ell}
, irrespective of the value of
\theta_{\ell}
.
If
\Theta_{\ell}>\Theta^{*}_{\ell}
the maximisation in Eq. (
10
) is dominated by the term
v_{\mathbf{s}_{\ell-1}|\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}
which results in a noisy clamped distribution
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
which is broader than
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
. If instead
\Theta_{\ell}\leq\Theta^{*}_{\ell}
the maximisation is dominated by the first term and
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
concentrates on the most probable values of
\mathbf{s}_{\ell}
, i.e. those with higher
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
.

Let us discuss the implications of these findings on the performance of the learning machines as a generative model.
Taking
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
as a projection of the data distribution in the
\ell^{\rm th}
layer, the case
\beta<1
corresponds to the situation where
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
is more sharply peaked than
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
. Hence the DBN will not reproduce the full variability of the data with which it has been trained. Rather it will predominantly generate the most likely patterns. This is what the authors of Ref.
[
26
]
observed when generating data points from deep layers of a DBN (see Fig.
1
b).
Conversely, for
\beta<1
the distribution
p(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
is flatter that
q(\mathbf{s}_{\ell})
. Noisy patters, which are generally unlikely in the data, are generated more frequently in this case. This behaviour is characteristic of shallow layers of DBN, as shown in Ref.
[
26
]
(see also Fig.
1
b).

4
Conclusion

Summarising, we study deep belief networks within a random energy ensemble approach. Each layer is described as a random energy model with a stretched exponential distribution of energies with parameter
\gamma
, as in Ref.
[
14
]
. Each layer features a phase transition to a “frozen” state for a particular value
\Delta^{*}
of the interaction strength, which depends on the number of hidden units and on
\gamma
(Eq.
16
). We show that
i)
the internal representation of hidden layers of well trained learning machines (DBN and RBM) on structured datasets (MNIST and Fashion MNIST) are best described by REMs with a parameter that is close to the critical point
\Delta^{*}
, irrespective of
\gamma
in a wide range of
n
. Untrained learning machines or machines trained on structureless data are instead best fitted by off-critical REMs. Furthermore,
ii)
in order to propagate the dependence on the data to the deep layers, each layer should interact with the data through a system (the environment) which should be tuned at the critical point of the corresponding REM.

Notice that close to the critical point
\Delta^{*}
, the distribution of energies of a REM is approximately flat, irrespective of the value of
\gamma
(see the Appendix). This prediction can be tested empirically, because, together with the relation between the clamped distribution and the distribution
p(\mathbf{s})
of Eq. (
18
), it implies that the distribution of clamped states should exhibit statistical criticality. Refs.
[
23
,
26
]
provide ample evidence that support this claim.
