An Axiomatic Characterization of CFMMs and Equivalence to Prediction Markets Conference: ; ;
Rafael Frongillo, Maneesha Papireddygari and Bo Waggoner

Abstract
. Constant-function market makers (CFMMs), such as Uniswap, are automated exchanges offering trades among a set of assets. We study their technical relationship to another class of automated market makers, cost-function prediction markets. We first introduce axioms for market makers and show that CFMMs with concave potential functions characterize “good” market makers according to these axioms. We then show that every such CFMM on n n assets is equivalent to a cost-function prediction market for events with n n outcomes. Our construction directly converts a CFMM into a prediction market and vice versa. Conceptually, our results show that desirable market-making axioms are equivalent to desirable information-elicitation axioms, i.e., markets are good at facilitating trade if and only if they are good at revealing beliefs. For example, we show that every CFMM implicitly defines a proper scoring rule for eliciting beliefs; the scoring rule for Uniswap is unusual, but known. From a technical standpoint, our results show how tools for prediction markets and CFMMs can interoperate. We illustrate this interoperability by showing how liquidity strategies from both literatures transfer to the other, yielding new market designs.



1.
Introduction

A prediction market is designed to elicit forecasts, such as for elections or sporting events.
Participants trade shares in
n
securities, each tied to one of the
n
possible outcomes, paying out $1 if that outcome occurs;
the prices form a probability distribution over the outcomes representing an aggregate belief.
To resolve thin market problems,
Hanson 2003
and
Chen and Pennock 2007
propose introducing
automated market makers
, mechanisms that offers to buy or sell any number of securities, with prices determined by the past history of transactions.
A significant amount of research has investigated the design of such markets based on convex
cost functions

C

(
Abernethy et al. 2013
;
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
)
.
To acquire a bundle of securities
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the trader pays
C({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}})-C({\mathbf{q}})
cash to the market maker, where
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the total numbers of securities sold so far.
A prominent example is the log market scoring rule (LMSR) due to
Hanson 2003
, given by
C({\mathbf{q}})=b\log\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{q_{i}/b}
for a liquidity parameter
b>0
.

More recently, inspired by blockchain applications, there has been significant theoretical and practical interest in the design of decentralized financial markets
(
Capponi and Jia 2021
;
Angeris et al. 2022
)
.
These markets allow trade between
n
assets without a fixed unit of exchange (“cash”).
Interestingly, like prediction markets, many decentralized exchanges also employ automated market makers, but for different reasons: automated market makers tend to have lower on-chain implementation costs than traditional order books
(
Jensen et al. 2021
, §4.1)
.
The dominant paradigm is the
constant-function market maker (CFMM)
based on a concave
potential function

\varphi
.
Here, if
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the amount of market maker holdings or “reserves” in the
n
assets, then a trade
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is accepted if
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, i.e., it keeps the potential function constant.
One of the most popular CFMMs is Uniswap where
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}
.

Despite clear similarities between these two settings, prior work has considered prediction markets to be only analogous to CFMMs, but not technically related.
1
1

1

To give two examples,
Angeris and Chitra 2020
argue that the two market makers can diverge, and
Bichuch and Feinstein 2022
state the following: “In a separate context, [automated market makers] for prediction markets were first proposed in
(
Hanson 2003
)
. Such a structure is fundamentally different from the constant function market makers considered herein insofar as a prediction market includes a terminal time at which bets are realized.”

Indeed, there are obvious differences between the settings: prediction markets rely on “cash”, while CFMMs do not require a special unit of account; prediction markets only offer certain types of outcome-dependent securities (and can manufacture as many of these as they want), while CFMMs deal in arbitrary assets in limited supply; and so on.
Furthermore, the
goals
of the two types of markets are very different: prediction markets seek to elicit information, whereas CFMMs seek to facilitate trade.

Nonetheless, we show a tight technical equivalence between prediction markets and CFMMs.
We give straightforward reductions to transform a cost function
C
for a prediction market into a potential function
\varphi
for a CFMM, and vice versa.
The reductions give one-to-one correspondence between trades placed in both markets, while preserving the relevant market properties.
An important implication is that markets designed to elicit information are also good at facilitating trade, and vice versa.
We illustrate our results with several examples; among them,
the prediction market equivalent to Uniswap, a new homogeneous CFMM from LMSR (Figure
1
), and a close connection between Brier score and a hybrid CFMM.
We hope our results can inspire new market designs and insights in both literatures.

1.1.
Outline of results

We begin in §
2
with an axiomatic characterization of the class of constant-function market makers (CFMMs) of interest in this paper.
Focusing on “vanilla” CFMMs that do not adapt liquidity, we show in Proposition
4
that an automated market maker satisfies a certain set of baseline axioms if and only if it can be represented by a CFMM with a concave, increasing
\varphi
.
This result assumes the initial reserves are fixed; we give a similar result in Theorem
5
allowing any initial reserves where
\varphi
is quasiconcave and continuous.

We give our main equivalence in §
3
.
We first recall that cost-function prediction markets, where
C
satisfies certain criteria, have been characterized by prior work as automated market makers satisfying a path independence axiom and an incentive-compatibility axiom (see Fact
1
).
We give a reduction from any such
C
to a concave, increasing
\varphi
, resulting in a CFMM that satisfies desirable market-making axioms (Theorem
3
).
Then, we give a reduction from any concave, increasing
\varphi
to a convex
C
satisfying the criteria, resulting in a prediction market with desirable incentive-compatibility axioms (Theorem
4
).
The conceptual implication is that design for good information-elicitation properties results in good market-making properties, and vice versa.
In fact, an immediate corollary is that any CFMM defines a
proper scoring rule
, a function for eliciting truthful predictions (Corollary
5
).

Our equivalence above is not fully satisfying in some cases, however, because for some initial conditions the resulting CFMMs can run out of their reserves.
In §
4
, we begin by characterizing markets that keep their reserves nonnegative in addition to our previous axioms.
We then give a more sophisticated reserves-aware reduction from prediction markets to CFMMs, using the
perspective transform
technique from convex analysis.
The result is a CFMM
\varphi
whose level sets are not parallel, as are those of cost functions (Figure
1
(L)), but instead “stretched”, with curvature adapting to the amount of reserves available (Figure
1
(M)).
We show that every 1-homogeneous (scale-symmetric) potential function, the dominant CFMM design in practice, can be obtained via our construction.

We conclude in §
5
with a discussion of other practical considerations, mainly
liquidity adaptation
.
In applications, CFMMs are designed to allow the reserves to grow (e.g. by lending), and the liquidity provided should grow as well
(
Angeris et al. 2020
)
.
Similarly, prediction-market research has studied how to automatically grow and adapt the liquidity of the market over time, again using the perspective transform
(
Abernethy et al. 2014
)
.
We begin an investigation of how these lines of research can inform each other.

1.2.
Equivalence in a nutshell

Let us give the key intuition for our equivalence.
First, to convert a prediction market into a CFMM, suppose we have cost function
C
.
We will turn it into a rule for pricing any set of
n
assets, without money.
Observe that a unit of cash is equivalent to the “grand bundle”, represented as the all-ones vector
\mathds{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, containing one share of each security: exactly one of them will pay off $1, and the rest $0, so the grand bundle is worth exactly $1.
We can therefore simulate transactions in a prediction market with only the
n
assets (securities) and no cash: a trade of
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in exchange for
c\in\mathbb{R}
units of cash can be expressed as the combined trade
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{r}}-c\mathds{1}
.

We can now simply set
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=-C(-{\mathbf{q}})
and, perhaps surprisingly, obtain a CFMM where valid trades are equivalent to those allowed by the original cost function.
The negations resolve a difference in sign conventions (i.e., whether
{\mathbf{q}}
is interpreted as a gain or loss) and convert the convex
C
to a concave
\varphi
.
We will show that concavity is equivalent to the desirable trading axiom of
demand responsiveness
; and
\varphi
also satisfies other nice trading axioms, like
liquidation
: the trader can purchase any bundle of assets for some amount of any given asset.
Perhaps surprisingly, these and other market-making axioms come “for free”, as long as
C
is a good prediction-market cost function.
One important exception is that ideally CFMMs do not deplete their reserves.
As described above, we will fix this issue with a more sophisticated construction based on the perspective transform that guarantees nonnegative reserves.
Figure
1
illustrates that construction applied to LMSR.

In the other direction, one can turn any CFMM into a prediction market with good elicitation properties.
Given a CFMM with potential function
\varphi
, we show that it has an equivalent cost function defined as follows: Let
C({\mathbf{q}})=c
for the constant
c
satisfying
\varphi(c\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
where
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of initial reserves and again
\mathds{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is grand bundle (the all-ones vector).
For example, as we recover in §
3.5
, the following cost function is equivalent to Uniswap
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}
when the initial reserves satisfy
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})=k
,

(1)

C({\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(q_{1}+q_{2}+\sqrt{4k^{2}+(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}}\right)~.\\

In fact, by standard prediction market facts, this general result implies that every “good” CFMM can be converted into a
proper scoring rule

(
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
)
;
we derive the scoring rule for Uniswap in §
3.5
.

1.3.
Related work

The literature on CFMMs contains several axiomatic results in a spirit related to our work in §
2
, e.g.
Angeris and Chitra 2020
;
Bichuch and Feinstein 2022
;
Schlegel et al. 2022
.
For example,
Bichuch and Feinstein 2022
characterize the set of “ideal” CFMMs from among the class of all CFMMs and
Schlegel et al. 2022
axiomatically characterize some sub-classes of CFMMs.
A major difference between our axiomatic results in §
2
and the above works is that we do not take the structure of CFMMs as given, for example, existence of a potential/utility function or its concavity.
We derive these attributes from the axioms directly.

Several authors have noted pieces of the equivalence we present.
Most technically related is
Chen and Pennock 2007
, which proposes the constant-utility market maker for prediction markets.
Their market maker accepts trades that, according to some subjective fixed belief (probability distribution) and risk-averse utility function, maintain its expected utility at a constant value.
Intriguingly, they observe in their eq. (14) that such a market maker with log utility is equivalent to a cost function of the form (
1
), which turns out to be the result of our conversion of Uniswap to a prediction market; see §
3.5
.
We emphasize that
Chen and Pennock 2007
, unlike the current paper, focused only on prediction markets, i.e. do not show how to use their market maker for more general classes of assets.
Within the prediction market context, they also only give equivalences to a certain class of cost functions, those corresponding to weighted pseudo-spherical scoring rules.

Other works have specifically connected market makers from decentralized finance and prediction markets.
For example,
Paradigm 2021
discusses how to apply the functional format of the LMSR as a potential function in a CFMM.
Similarly,
Manifold 2022
and
Gnosis 2020
apply Uniswap to the case where assets are contingent securities, obtaining a prediction market.
Such works can be described as applying a functional form from one context to a different context, without justifying why they will perform well in the new context.
In contrast, this work shows how to convert any cost function to a CFMM and vice versa via a reduction that guarantees to preserve axiomatic properties of the original.

Other technical tools come from the prediction market literature.
We rely specifically the cost-function market formulation
(
Chen and Pennock 2007
;
Abernethy et al. 2013
)
, axiomatic approaches to prediction markets
(
Abernethy et al. 2013
;
Abernethy et al. 2014
;
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
)
, and the duality between prediction markets and scoring rules
(
Hanson 2003
;
Abernethy et al. 2014
)
.
We also make use of constructions from the literature on financial risk measures, specifically obtaining a convex risk measure from a set of “acceptable” positions
(
Föllmer and Schied 2008
;
Föllmer and Weber 2015
)
; the connection between risk measures and prediction markets has been noted several times
(
Chen and Vaughan 2010
;
Agrawal et al. 2011
;
Frongillo and Reid 2015
)
.

Notation

Vectors are denoted in bold, e.g.
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The all-zeros vector is
{\mathbf{0}}=(0,\dots,0)
and the all-ones vector is
\mathds{1}=(1,\dots,1)
.
The vector
\bm{\delta}_{i}=(0,\dots,1,\dots,0)
, i.e. all-zero except for a one in the
i^{th}
position.

Comparison between vectors is pointwise, e.g.
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}

for all

, and similarly for
\succeq
.

Define

Let

.
We will use the following conditions.

•

2.
A Characterization of Fixed-Liquidity CFMMs

To lay a foundation, we consider market makers that have “fixed liquidity”: they do not accept loans nor charge transaction fees.
The fixed-liquidity case allows for a simple, universal characterization: if one wants an automated market to satisfy certain natural axioms, it
must
be a CFMM as we show in Theorem
5
.

2.1.
Automated market makers for general asset markets

We consider automated market makers that process trades sequentially.
There are
n
assets
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
A
trade
or
bundle

{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents
r_{i}
net units of each asset
i
being given to the market maker by a trader.
A positive
r_{i}
represents a net transfer from the trader to the market maker, and negative
r_{i}
represents a net transfer from the market maker to the trader.
A
history

h
is an ordered list of trades.
The empty history is denoted
\epsilon=()
.

A market maker is defined by a function
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
that specifies, for any valid history
h
, the set of valid trades that it is willing to accept from the next participant.
For each arriving participant
t=1,\dots
:

•

The current market history is
h_{t-1}
.

•

The valid trades are given by
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h_{t-1})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

Participant
t
selects some trade
{\mathbf{r}}_{t}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h_{t-1})
.

•

The market history is now
h_{t}=h_{t-1}\oplus{\mathbf{r}}_{t}
.

The market maker begins with some
initial reserves

{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
It will be convenient to include the initial reserves in the history, so in the above we would have
h_{0}=({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.
At any history
h
, we define the notation

{\mathbf{q}}_{h}=\mathsf{sum}(h)~,

meaning the current reserves are equal to the sum of the trades in the history.

Given a market maker defined by
\mathsf{ValTrades}
and a history
h
, we let
\mathsf{ValHist}(h)
denote the
valid histories extending
h
, i.e. all valid histories with
h
as a prefix.
Formally,
\mathsf{ValHist}(h)
is the smallest set containing
h
such that, for all
h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValHist}(h)
and all
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h^{\prime})
, we have
h^{\prime}\oplus{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValHist}(h)
.
In particular,
\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)
may be interpreted as the set of valid initial reserves for this market maker, and
\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
the set of valid histories.
However, we abuse notation slightly in that
\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
is defined not to include
\epsilon
itself, i.e. it only includes nonempty histories.

Examples and CFMMs.

The simplest example of an automated market maker is when
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
is a constant set that does not depend on
h
.
Such a market maker would offer a fixed exchange rate between assets.
However, we would like the exchange rates to adapt depending on demand.
In the context of decentralized finance, a common approach is the following.

Definition 0 (CFMM).

A
constant-function market maker (CFMM)
is a market such that

\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)=\left\{{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\varphi({\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})\right\},

for some function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, called the potential function.

In a CFMM, the market maker accepts any trade keeping the potential of its reserves
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
constant.
Some common examples are Uniswap or the constant product market where
\varphi({\mathbf{r}})=(r_{1}\cdot r_{2}\cdots r_{n})^{1/n}
, and its generalization Balancer or constant geometric mean market which has
\varphi({\mathbf{r}})=r_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot r_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots r_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha_{i}\geq 0\forall i
.

2.2.
Axioms

An axiom is a potentially desirable property for a market maker as defined by
\mathsf{ValTrades}
.
We begin with a few intuitive axioms.

Axiom 1 (
NoDominatedTrades
).

For all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
,
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
does not contain any
dominated trade
, i.e. an
{\mathbf{r}}
where, for some
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
, we have
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\succneqq{\mathbf{r}}
.

Assuming all assets have nonnegative value, a rational trader would never select a dominated trade.
Conversely, as typically
{\mathbf{0}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
, meaning a trader can chose not trade altogether, then under
NoDominatedTrades
no trader can take assets from the market maker without giving something in return.
This latter property is the typical condition of
no-arbitrage
(within the system) in the finance literature.

Axiom 2 (
PathIndependence
).

For all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
, for all
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h\oplus{\mathbf{r}})
, we have that
{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.

PathIndependence
ensures that a trader cannot profit by splitting a single large trade into two smaller sequential trades instead.
By backward induction, it implies that any
sequence
of trades can just as well be made in a single bundle:
if
h^{\prime}=h\oplus r_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus r_{j}
is a valid history, we must have
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}+\cdots+{\mathbf{r}}_{j}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.

Observation 1.

PathIndependence
implies the following inductive version: for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
and all
h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValHist}(h)
, we have
{\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.

Axiom 3 (
Liquidation
).

For all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
and all
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\succneqq{\mathbf{0}}
, there exists
\beta\geq 0
such that
{\mathbf{r}}-\beta\cdot{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.

In other words, a participant can supply any nonnegative bundle
{\mathbf{r}}
and specify a nonnegative demand bundle
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, and receive some multiple of
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
in return for
{\mathbf{r}}
.
The
Liquidation
axiom captures the point of a “market maker”, i.e., to offer to trade at some exhange rate between any pair of assets (or more generally, bundles).

Any market satisfying
Liquidation
and
NoDominatedTrades
must in particular offer the option to trade nothing; to see this set
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
for any nontrivial bundle
{\mathbf{r}}
.

Observation 2.

Liquidation
and
NoDominatedTrades
imply
{\mathbf{0}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
.
Furthermore, any
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)\setminus\{{\mathbf{0}}\}
can be written
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{+}-{\mathbf{r}}^{-}
where
{\mathbf{r}}^{+}=\max({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{-}=\min({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
satisfy
{\mathbf{r}}^{+},{\mathbf{r}}^{-}\succneqq{\mathbf{0}}
.

Axiom 4 (
DemandResponsiveness
).

For all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
, if
{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
for some
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}
, and if there exist
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h\oplus({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}))
, then
\beta\leq\alpha
.

In other words, if a participant supplies
{\mathbf{r}}
in return for
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
once, then the “exchange rate” should increase the second time in a row: more of
{\mathbf{r}}
is needed to buy a corresponding amount of
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
.

2.3.
Characterization of fixed-liquidity CFMMs

In this subsection we characterize automated market makers that satisfy the axioms above: CFMMs with increasing, concave potential functions.
In addition to setting the stage for the equivalence with prediction markets, this result places limits on the design space of automated market makers, or at least shows what axioms must be relinquished to move beyond the concave-CFMM framework.
We first prove that
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
, and
PathIndependence
already imply the market can be implemented as a CFMM for
some
potential function
\varphi
.

DemandResponsiveness
then gives concavity.
Finally, we extend these results from fixed initial market reserves to show that, when considering multiple initial reserves, the market is still without loss of generality a CFMM for some
quasiconcave
potential.

Lemma 0.

Fix the initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
If a market maker
\mathsf{ValTrades}
with
\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)=\{{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\}
satisfies
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
, and
PathIndependence
, then it is a CFMM for some potential function
\varphi
.

The key point of the proof is that there is a fixed set of reachable states of the market
\mathcal{R}
, and any of these is always reachable in a single trade regardless of the history.
We can then define
\varphi
to vanish exactly on that set.

Proof.

Let
h
be any valid history with initial reserves
q_{0}
.
We first prove that
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))\iff{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
, by induction on the length of
h
.
The base case
h=({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
is immediate.
For the inductive step, let
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
and consider a trade
{\mathbf{r}}
.
We show
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)\iff{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
.

(
\implies
)
Let
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.
By Observation
1
,
{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
.

(
\impliedby
)
Let
{\mathbf{r}}
be some trade such that
{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
.
By

{\mathbf{0}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}

{\mathbf{r}}_{+}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}_{-}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.
We will show

By Observation

{\mathbf{r}}_{+}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}_{-}+{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
.
If

Therefore, by

Now we show the market is a CFMM.
Define
\mathcal{R}=\{{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))\}

We proved above that
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)\iff{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
, which holds if and only if
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\in\mathcal{R}

Therefore, we can choose any potential function with
\mathcal{R}
as a level set, e.g.
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=0
if
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}
and
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=1

∎

In the proof of Lemma

,
\mathcal{R}
consists of the level set containing
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, i.e. the set of valid reserves the market is willing to maintain starting from
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
In general, given a function
\varphi
and initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, it will be useful to define the following notation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}\mid{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0}),{\mathbf{r}}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}\right\}.

Here,
\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
consists intuitively of valid reserves, plus all reserves that are weakly preferred by the market maker to some valid reserve.
The key geometrical point is that, under

,
\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
is a convex set:

Lemma 0.

Fix the initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
If a market maker

,

, and

The proof of Lemma

, as well as the rest of the claims in this section, can be found in Appendix

.
We obtain that, for a fixed initial starting point, a market maker satisfying our axioms can always be implemented as a CFMM with a concave potential.

Proposition 0.

Fix the initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
A market maker
\mathsf{ValTrades}
with
\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)=\{{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\}
satisfies
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
PathIndependence
, and
DemandResponsiveness
if and only if it can be implemented as a CFMM with an increasing, concave potential function
\varphi
.

If the market maker has multiple initial feasible reserves, then the market initiated from each one can be implemented as a CFMM with a concave potential.
But it is not clear that there is a single concave potential function that implements all of these markets simultaneously.
However, there at least exists a single
quasiconcave
potential function that implements the entire market as a CFMM simultaneously.
This statement holds as long as we assume the slightly stronger axiom of

, which enforces a relationship between the market when starting at different initial reserves.

Axiom 5 (

).

For all

with
\mathsf{sum}(h)=\mathsf{sum}(h^{\prime})
, we have
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)=\mathsf{ValTrades}(h^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, we have

for all

.

Theorem 5.

A market maker, with
\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfies

,
NoDominatedTrades
,
StrongPathIndependence
, and
DemandResponsiveness
if and only if it is a CFMM with an increasing, continuous, quasiconcave potential function

.

As the potential functions in Proposition

.
We conjecture that the answer is yes, in the sense that there are CFMMs with continuous, quasiconcave potential functions which cannot be “concavified” by any monotone transformation; see e.g.
Fenchel 1953
;
Connell and Rasmusen 2017
.

3.
Equivalence of CFMMs and Prediction Markets

The goal of CFMMs is to facilitate trade.
In contrast, a prediction market is designed to to elicit and aggregate information about future events.
We will now see that prediction markets (satisfying good elicitation axioms) are in a very strong sense equivalent to CFMMs (satisfying good trade axioms).
We give direct reductions to convert each into the other.
As a consequence, much existing research on design and properties of prediction markets can now transfer to CFMMs.

In §
3.1
, we define cost function prediction markets and recall that they characterize automated market makers satisfying elicitation axioms.
In §
3.2
, we take the key step of converting them into “cashless prediction markets”.
In §
3.3
, we give the reductions and equivalence between prediction markets and CFMMs.
§
3.4
discusses information-elicitation consequences, and §
3.5
gives examples.

3.1.
Cost function prediction markets

To begin, let us briefly review prediction markets for Arrow–Debreu (AD) securities, which are designed to elicit a full probability distribution over a future event.
Formally, let
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
be a set of outcomes, and let
Y
be the future event, a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{Y}
.
For example, in a tournament with
n
teams,
Y
can be the winner of the tournament, and
Y=y_{i}
is the outcome where team
i
wins.
The probability simplex on
\mathcal{Y}
is denoted
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
The AD prediction market is a market with
n
assets
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
.
When the outcome of
Y
is observed, each unit of
A_{i}
pays off to the owner one unit of cash (some fixed currency, such as US dollars) if
Y=y_{i}
, and pays off zero otherwise.
2
2

2

In more general prediction markets than the Arrow–Debreu case described here, there can be any number of assets that each pay off according to an arbitrary function of
Y
.

We allow traders to both buy and sell assets, including holding negative amounts, representing a short position.

By design, a trader’s fair price for
A_{i}
is therefore the probability
\Pr[Y=y_{i}]
according to their belief.
The current market prices for
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
form a “consensus” prediction in the form of a probability distribution over
\mathcal{Y}
.

A priori, as with general asset markets, it may seem that the valid trades and associated prices at each moment can be set in essentially any manner whatsoever.
It may therefore be surprising that, in order to successfully elicit and aggregate information while maintaining path independence, a market for contingent securities must take the form of a
cost-function market maker
, as we define next.
We then sketch the proof of this well-known characterization in Fact
1
.

The classic cost-function market maker for Arrow–Debreu securities, slightly generalized for our setting, operates as follows.

Definition 0.

Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex, increasing, and
\mathds{1}
-invariant.
Let
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an initial state.
The
AD market maker defined by
C
, with initial state
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, operates as follows.
At round
t=1,2,\dots

•

A trader can request any bundle of securities
{\mathbf{r}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

The market state updates to
{\mathbf{q}}_{t}={\mathbf{q}}_{t-1}+{\mathbf{r}}_{t}
.

•

The trader pays the market maker
C({\mathbf{q}}_{t})-C({\mathbf{q}}_{t-1})
in cash.

After an outcome of the form
Y=y_{i}
occurs, for each round
t
, the trader responsible for the trade
{\mathbf{r}}_{t}
is paid
({\mathbf{r}}_{t})_{i}
in cash, i.e. the number of shares purchased in outcome
y_{i}
.

When
C
is differentiable, the instantaneous prices of the securities are given by
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}=\nabla C({\mathbf{q}}_{t})
.
That is, the approximate cost of any bundle
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
is given by
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\cdot x
.
(The exact cost is given by integrating this trade from zero to
x
, resulting in
C({\mathbf{q}}_{t}+x)-C({\mathbf{q}}_{t})
.)
We can therefore regard
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}=\nabla C({\mathbf{q}}_{t})
as the market prediction.
Because of
\mathds{1}
-invariance,
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
is always a probability distribution.

We can cast cost-function market makers as a special case of the automated market maker framework in §
2
.
Specifically, represent cash by an asset
A_{\$}
, and consider the
n+1
assets
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},A_{\$}\}
.
Then we can restate Definition
1
as the following asset market
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}
over
\mathcal{A}
.
An important convention is that
prediction markets interpret states
{\mathbf{q}}
and trades
{\mathbf{r}}
as net transfers to the trader
, whereas our automated market maker follows the CFMM convention to interpret
{\mathbf{q}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}
as net amounts for the market maker.
Therefore, a negative sign is always required to translate between the settings.

•

The initial reserves are
(-{\mathbf{q}}_{0},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

•

Define
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}(h)=\left\{\left({\mathbf{r}},\,\alpha\right)\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})\right\}
.

In other words,
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}(h)
consists of any bundle of securities
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be sold, along with a payment in cash of
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
As mentioned, the input to
C
is negated because
C
expects as an argument the net bundle the market has
sold
rather than purchased.

A priori, the design space for prediction markets could include any automated market maker over any set of contingent securities.
From this perspective, it is perhaps surprising that, to achieve “good” elicitation of predictions according to a standard set of axioms, one
must
use a cost-function market maker.
That is, the following is known:

Fact 1 (
(
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
;
Abernethy et al. 2013
)
).

Let
M
be any automated market maker offering contingent securities for cash and satisfying these axioms:
(1) Path Independence;
(2) Incentive Compatibility for predicting
Y
, in the sense that:
(2a) Any history
h
of the market defines a probability distribution over
Y
(the “market prediction”), and
(2b) All possible predictions are achievable by some history, and
(2c) A trader maximizes expected net payment by moving the market prediction match their belief
p\in\Delta_{Y}
.
Then
M
is a cost-function AD prediction market.

Fact
1
is essentially a characterization: any cost-function AD prediction market satisfies the above axioms, if
C
is also differentiable with a certain set of gradients
(
Abernethy et al. 2013
)
.
For completeness, Appendix
B
includes a sketch of a proof.
The key ideas, synthesized from
(
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
;
Abernethy et al. 2013
)
, are these: Incentive Compatibility requires the market to use
proper scoring rules
, functions that induce truthful forecasts from individual experts (see Section
3.4
).
Path Independence then implies that market must use “chained” scoring rules as proposed by
(
Hanson 2003
)
.
Finally, it is known that Hanson’s scoring-rule markets are equivalent to cost function based prediction markets via convex duality
(
Abernethy et al. 2013
)
.

3.2.
Cashless prediction markets

A key step to translating between prediction markets and CFMMs is this observation: without loss of generality, an AD prediction market can drop cash from its assets
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n},A_{\$})
.
Because exactly one of the outcomes of
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
will occur, the “grand bundle” of securities
{\mathbf{r}}=\mathds{1}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is worth exactly one unit of cash to all traders; whichever outcome
Y=y_{i}
occurs, the
i
th security will pay out one unit of cash and the rest will pay out zero.

Specifically, we can define a “cashless prediction market” for any cost function
C
by taking a demand bundle
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
which would have had a price
\alpha
, and subtracting
\alpha
units of each security, becoming the bundle
({\mathbf{r}}_{1}-\alpha,\dots,{\mathbf{r}}_{n}-\alpha)={\mathbf{r}}-\alpha\mathds{1}
.
By
\mathds{1}
-invariance, the cost of this new trade must be zero, eliminating the need for cash.
With the negative sign to convert to the automated market maker framework, define for any cost function
C
:

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)=\left\{{\mathbf{r}}+\alpha\mathds{1}\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\text{ where $\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})$}.

In Appendix
C
, we show the equivalence of
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}
and
\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}
in more formality.
The key step is the following lemma, which will be useful for converting to a CFMM:

Lemma 0.

\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)=\{{\mathbf{r}}\mid C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})\}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)
, and write
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}+\alpha\mathds{1}
with
\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
Using ones-invariance,
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\alpha\mathds{1})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
Conversely, let
{\mathbf{r}}
be such that
C({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=C({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
Then, directly,
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)
, where we observe
\alpha=0
.
∎

3.3.
Equivalence between CFMMs and prediction markets

We now show that, in a strong sense, CFMMs and AD cost-function market makers are equivalent.
Specifically, every “good” prediction market mechanism for events on
n
outcomes defines, in a straightforward manner, a “good” CFMM on
n
assets; and vice versa.
Here, a “good” prediction market is one that satisfies the elicitation axioms we introduced in Subsection
2.2
, i.e. a cost-function market.
A “good” CFMM is one that satisfies the trading axioms, i.e. is defined by a concave, increasing
\varphi
.
Recall that
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}
is the market maker defined by the CFMM for
\varphi
, i.e.
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)=\{{\mathbf{r}}\mid\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})\}
.
Recall that
\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}
was defined in Equation
2
.

Theorem 3.

Let
C
define an AD cost-function prediction market.
Then the function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=-C(-{\mathbf{q}})

is concave and increasing, i.e. defines a CFMM.
Furthermore,
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}=\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}
.

Proof.

As
\varphi
is the negative of a convex function, it is concave.
Because
C
is increasing,
\varphi
is increasing.
By Lemma
2
,
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)\iff C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})\iff{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)
.
∎

Theorem 4.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be concave and increasing, defining a CFMM.
For any
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(3)

C({\mathbf{q}})=\inf\left\{c\in\mathbb{R}\mid\varphi(c\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}})\geq\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\right\}

is convex, increasing, and ones-invariant, i.e., defines an AD cost-function prediction market.
Furthermore, for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}_{\varphi}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
, we have
\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)=\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)
.

Proof.

The key observations come from the financial risk-measure literature, specifically “constant-risk” market makers
(
Frongillo and Reid 2015
, §2.1)

(
Chen and Pennock 2007
)
and standard constructions extending convex functions to be ones-invariant
(
Föllmer and Weber 2015
)
.
Specifically,
C
is increasing because
\varphi
is increasing, and ones-invariant immediately by construction.
We observe that the infimum is always achieved because
\varphi
, a concave function defined on all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is continuous.
For convexity, given
{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let
c=C({\mathbf{q}}),c^{\prime}=C({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
.
Let
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}=\lambda{\mathbf{q}}+(1-\lambda){\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
and
c^{*}=\lambda c+(1-\lambda)c^{\prime}
.
Then by concavity,
\varphi(c^{*}\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}^{*})\geq\lambda\varphi(c\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}})+(1-\lambda)\varphi(c^{\prime}\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.
Because
\varphi(c^{*}\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}^{*})\geq\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
, we have
C({\mathbf{q}}^{*})\leq c^{*}
, as desired.

Now,
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)
if and only if
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
by Lemma
2
.
Because
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{0})=0
, inductively we obtain
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)\iff C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=0
.
By construction of
C
and because
\varphi
is increasing,
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=0\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.
And by definition of a CFMM,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\iff{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)
.
∎

3.4.
CFMMs and scoring rules

Recall that a scoring rule is a function
S({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})
that evaluates the prediction
{\mathbf{p}}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
given an observed outcome
y_{i}
.
A scoring rule is
proper
if for all probability distributions
{\mathbf{p}}
, the expected score when
y_{i}\sim{\mathbf{p}}
is maximized by report
{\mathbf{p}}
.
It is
strictly
proper if
{\mathbf{p}}
is the unique maximizer.
It is well-known (see e.g.
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
) that a scoring rule is proper if and only if it can be written as an affine approximation to a particular convex
generating function

G({\mathbf{p}})
.

One implication of the above equivalence is that CFMMs are closely linked to proper scoring rules.
This connection emphasizes the surprising nature of the equivalence between cost functions and CFMMs, namely that CFMMs, while designed for a very different context than probabilistic forecasting,
characterize
the properties needed for eliciting forecasts.

Corollary 0.

Every CFMM for a concave, increasing
\varphi
and initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
is associated with a proper scoring rule.

The corollary follows immediately from known prediction-market equivalences between cost functions and scoring rules, discussed in Appendix
B.1
.
Briefly, every cost function
C
corresponds to the scoring rule generated by
G=C^{*}
, the convex conjugate of
C
, and vice versa.

As alluded to above, for strict properness, one needs additional conditions on
C
, namely that (1)
C
is differentiable, so that there is a unique market price at any given state, and (2) the set of gradients is equal to the probability simplex, so that any given market price/prediction is achievable by some set of trades.
These carry over into corresponding conditions on
\varphi
, as we explore in Appendix
B.2
.

3.5.
Examples

We now present two examples to illustrate our equivalence thus far.
We discuss these examples again in §
4.5
along with others.

LMSR
\to
CFMM

As a warm-up, let us see how the most popular cost function, the LMSR
C({\mathbf{q}})=b\log\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{q_{i}/b}
, can be interpreted as a CFMM.
Letting
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}})
, we have
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
, where
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
is the initial reserves.
If
k=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
, then
{\mathbf{r}}
is a valid trade if and only if
b\log\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-(q_{i}+r_{i})/b}=k\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-(q_{i}+r_{i})/b}=e^{k/b}
.
For two assets, this equation reduces to
e^{-(q_{1}+r_{1})/b}+e^{-(q_{1}+r_{1})/b}=e^{k/b}
(cf.
(
Paradigm 2021
)
).
See §
4.5
for a version which scales the liquidity
b
depending on
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.

Uniswap
\to
cost function / scoring rule

One of the most iconic CFMMs is Uniswap, with the potential function
\varphi_{U}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\varphi_{U}({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}
.
Interestingly,
\varphi_{U}
is not defined on all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and is only increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
; as such, typically one restricts to the latter space.
As we will see in §
4
, this restriction to
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
is typical for CFMMs used in practice, and does not pose a barrier for our equivalence.
Briefly, as long as we require
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, the market reserves will stay within
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, and the characterization from Theorem
5
and equivalence from Theorem
4
will still apply.

Let
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
and set
k=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})>0
.
Applying Theorem
4
, we obtain the cost function from eq. (
1
),

\displaystyle C_{k}({\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(q_{1}+q_{2}+\sqrt{4k^{2}+(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}}\right)~.

Let us verify the construction.
One can easily check that
C_{k}
is
\mathds{1}
-invariant and increasing.
Consequently, it suffices to verify a single level set of
C_{k}
; we will show
\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid C(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\}=\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}},\varphi_{U}({\mathbf{q}})=k\}
.

\displaystyle C_{k}(-{\mathbf{q}})=0

\displaystyle\iff\sqrt{4k^{2}+(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}}=(q_{1}+q_{2})

\displaystyle\iff 4k^{2}+(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}=(q_{1}+q_{2})^{2}\text{ and }q_{1},q_{2}>0

\displaystyle\iff 4k^{2}=4q_{1}q_{2}\text{ and }q_{1},q_{2}>0

\displaystyle\iff k=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}\text{ and }q_{1},q_{2}>0~.

We conclude that, indeed,
\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C_{k}}=\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi_{U}}
, as promised by Theorem
4
.
In other words, a prediction market run using
C_{k}
would behave exactly the same as Uniswap.

As discussed in §
1
, the expression for
C_{k}
appears in
Chen and Pennock 2007
.
There they show that a market maker keeping constant
\log
utility assuming the binary outcome would be drawn from the uniform distribution
\pi=(1/2,1/2)
.
Moreover, as observed by
Bichuch and Feinstein 2022
and
Othman 2021
, constant expected log utility under the uniform distribution gives rise to Uniswap:
(1/2)\log(q_{1})+(1/2)\log(q_{1})=c\iff\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}=e^{c}
.
(Changing this distribution
\pi
gives rise to more general forms of Uniswap, such as Balancer, which takes the form
\varphi_{\pi}({\mathbf{q}})=q_{1}^{\pi_{1}}q_{2}^{\pi_{2}}
.)
Amazingly, it appears that no one has yet made these two connections together, that Uniswap is equivalent to a cost-function prediction market.

In light of Corollary
5
, each level set of Uniswap must therefore be equivalent to a scoring rule market for some choice of proper scoring rule.
Let us now derive this family of scoring rules.
Taking the convex conjugate of
C_{k}
, we obtain the convex generating function

\displaystyle G_{k}({\mathbf{p}})=-2\sqrt{kp_{1}p_{2}}~.

The corresponding scoring rule is

\displaystyle S_{k}({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})=G({\mathbf{p}})+dG_{\mathbf{p}}(\bm{\delta}_{i}-{\mathbf{p}})=-k\sqrt{p_{i}/p_{j}}~,

where
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
.
This scoring rule is exactly the boosting loss of
Buja et al. 2005
, negated and scaled by
k
.
(This scoring rule also appears in a shifted form as “
\mathrm{hs}
” in
Ben-David and Blais 2020
.)
Interestingly, while in general each level set of
\varphi_{U}
could have corresponded to an entirely different scoring rule, instead each level set merely scales the same scoring rule.
In fact, as we will see in §
4.4
, this phenomenon is shared by all 1-homogeneous CFMMs.

4.
Bounded reserves and liquidity

CFMMs are defined in terms of the current reserves
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the vector of asset quantities available to trade.
Naturally, one might like to ensure
{\mathbf{q}}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}
at every state of the market, i.e., that the market maker can only trade assets it has, and not go short on any asset.
We now make this restriction explicit.

Axiom 6 (
BoundedReserves
).

\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\subseteq\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)
and for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
,
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}
.

A version of the
BoundedReserves
axiom appeared in
Angeris et al. 2022
, emphasizing its importance as most common potential functions are 1-homogeneous.
In this section, we first characterize all market makers satisfying
BoundedReserves
in addition to our other four axioms (Theorem
2
).
We then explore the relationship between
BoundedReserves
and
bounded worst-case loss
from the prediction market literature, finding the former to be a stronger condition.
In fact, CFMMs constructed from cost functions in our previous equivalence, Theorem
3
, can essentially never satisfy
BoundedReserves
.
Instead, we present a new construction leveraging a liquidity parameter from the prediction market literature, to convert any cost function to a CFMM satisfying
BoundedReserves
.
As we will see, this construction recovers every 1-homogeneous CFMM potential function, including nearly all CFMMs used in practice.
Moreover, leveraging Corollary
5
, these 1-homogeneous CFMMs are associated with a unique scoring rule, thus allowing us to convert between proper scoring rules and CFMMs satisfying
BoundedReserves
.

4.1.
Characterizing bounded reserves

We begin with an observation about CFMMs satisfying
BoundedReserves
: with
Liquidation
and
NoDominatedTrades
, the reserves of each asset must be
strictly
positive at all valid histories.

Lemma 0.

A market maker satisfying
BoundedReserves
,
Liquidation
and
NoDominatedTrades
must have
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\succ 0
for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
.

Proof.

Assume that for a history
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
, we have
({\mathbf{q}}_{h})_{i}=0
for some
i
.
For any quantity
t>0
of asset
j
an agent offers,
Liquidation
states that the market maker would have to quote a price for it in terms of asset
i
.
As the market maker does not have any of asset
i
, it must quote a price of zero to satisfy
BoundedReserves
.
If the agent asks to trade quantity
\frac{t}{2}
of asset
j
, the market maker can still only give zero units of asset
i
.
This trade of
\frac{t}{2}
for zero units of
i
therefore dominates
t
for zero, violating
NoDominatedTrades
.
Hence the reserves for an asset can never be empty.
∎

We will now characterize CFMMs satisfying all of our axioms.
In light of Lemma
1
, for any CFMM that satisfies these axioms, it must be the case that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
and
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
together imply
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
.
Intuitively, it follows that we may restrict attention to the strictly positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.
While
\varphi
will be increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, however, it might not be on its boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.
In fact, a prominent example is Uniswap,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}
, where
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=0
for
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
Yet the behavior of
\varphi
in a neighborhood of
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
turns out to be the key to whether
Liquidation
is satisfied.
To state our results, therefore, we restrict
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
without loss of generality, and then use the limits to control the behavior of
\varphi
near
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

Theorem 2.

A market maker satisfies
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
StrongPathIndependence
,
DemandResponsiveness
, and
BoundedReserves
if and only if the following hold:  (a)
\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
,  (b)  the market maker is a CFMM with an continuous, increasing, quasiconcave potential function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
, and  (c)
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+\alpha\mathds{1})=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\varphi(\alpha\mathds{1})
for all
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

First consider the forward direction, and assume we are given a market maker satisfying all 5 axioms.
For (a), we have
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\subseteq\mathsf{ValTrades}(\epsilon)
by
BoundedReserves
and the reverse inclusion from Lemma
1
.
For (b), we make use of the Lemma
5
, a more general version of the foreward direction for Theorem
5
(see §
A
).
Leveraging (a), the lemma states that the first 4 axioms imply a CFMM with an continuous, increasing, quasiconcave potential
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Finally, consider (c).
Denote
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}):=\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+\alpha\mathds{1})
for
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
; the limit exists as
\varphi
is increasing.
Then condition (c) can be succinctly written:
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
for all
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
A fact we will use is that for
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
{\mathbf{r}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})<\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}})
.
(Take
c=\min_{i}r_{i}>0
; as
\varphi
is increasing and by definition of
\overline{\varphi}
, we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})<\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+c\mathds{1})\leq\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}})
.)
As
\varphi
is increasing, we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})\geq\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
.
Now suppose for a contradiction we had some
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
with
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
.
By definiton of a limit, there exists
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})>\varphi(\epsilon\mathds{1})
.
Let
c=\max_{i}q_{i}+1
so that
c\mathds{1}\succ{\mathbf{q}}
.
Then by the fact above we have
\varphi(c\mathds{1})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})>\varphi(\epsilon\mathds{1})
.
By continuity of
\varphi
, there exists
c^{\prime}\in(\epsilon,c)
satisfying
\varphi(c^{\prime}\mathds{1})=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})
.
Let
i
be an asset such that
q_{i}=0
.
Now let us apply
Liquidation
at
h=(c^{\prime}\mathds{1})
to bundles
r=(c-c^{\prime})\mathds{1}
and
r^{\prime}=\bm{\delta}_{i}
, giving some
\beta\geq 0
such that
r-\beta r^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
.
Let
h^{\prime}=(c^{\prime}\mathds{1})\oplus(r-\beta r^{\prime})
be the resulting history.
Then
\varphi(c^{\prime}\mathds{1})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}})
by (b).
By Lemma
1
, we must have
0\prec{\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}}=c^{\prime}\mathds{1}+(r-\beta r^{\prime})=c\mathds{1}-\beta\bm{\delta}_{i}
, which implies
\beta<c
.
As
q_{i}=0
, we therefore have
{\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}}-{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, as the
i
th coordinate is
c-\beta>0
and all other coordinates are at least 1.
By the fact above,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})=\varphi(c^{\prime}\mathds{1})
, a contradiction.

For the reverse direction, let
\varphi
satisfying conditions (a), (b), and (c) be given.
Let
\overline{\varphi}
be defined as above.
We first show that we may assume without loss of generality that
\overline{\varphi}
is continous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
Let
f(\alpha)=\overline{\varphi}(\alpha\mathds{1})
for all
\alpha\geq 0
.
Then
f:[0,\infty)
is monotone increasing and continuous, and has a monotone increasing and continuous inverse.
Thus,
\varphi^{\prime}=f^{-1}\circ\varphi
satisfies conditions (a), (b), and (c) as well.
Moreover,
\varphi^{\prime}
satisfies
\varphi^{\prime}(\alpha\mathds{1})=\alpha
for all
\alpha>0
;
in other words,
\varphi^{\prime}
follows the construction of Theorem
5
.
Now as
\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}({\mathbf{0}})=0>-\infty
by the above, and
\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}})=\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}({\mathbf{0}})
for all
{\mathbf{q}}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
by (c),
Crouzeix 2005
states that
\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}
is continuous on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
As
\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}=\varphi^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, we have continuity on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

BoundedReserves
is trivially satisfied as
\varphi
is only defined (or equivalently, equal to
-\infty
) outside of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, so
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
and
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
immediately implies
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
.
It follows that the proofs for
DemandResponsiveness
,
NoDominatedTrades
, and
StrongPathIndependence
in Theorem
5
immediately extend to the case where the domain is restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, since they all begin with given elements of
\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
.
(The second condition of
StrongPathIndependence
also follows immediately from
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
.)

It remains therefore to show
Liquidation
.
We will use the fact that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
for all
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, which follows by a similar argument to the above: letting
c=\tfrac{1}{2}\min_{i}q_{i}>0
, we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})>\overline{\varphi}(c\mathds{1})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
.
Now let
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
and
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\succneqq{\mathbf{0}}
.
Then
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
as
\varphi
is defined only on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, giving
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
as well.
Thus
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h})>\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
.
Now let
\beta^{\prime}>0
be the unique real value such that
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta^{\prime}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
By (c), we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta^{\prime}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
.
Thus
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta^{\prime}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})<\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h})<\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-0\cdot{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
.
By continuity of
\overline{\varphi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
(see above), we have some
\beta\in(0,\beta^{\prime})
such that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h})=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
.
As
\beta<\beta^{\prime}
, we have
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
.
Thus
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
as well, giving
{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
, as desired.
∎

A
, we prove a more general version of some key steps of Theorem
2
for more general restrictions
S
on the reserves other than
S=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.
While from
BoundedReserves
this is perhaps the most interesting case, the entire theorem could in principle be generalized to other
S
which are “dominant domains” (Definition
4
); for example, condition (c) in the theorem would replace
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
with
\partial S
.

4.2.
Connection to worst-case loss and liquidity

When assets are contingent securities, it is perhaps more natural to ask how much money the market maker could lose in some run of the market and some outcome of nature, rather than tracking the number of securities “in reserve”.
This interpretation gives the following axiom, which is commonly found in the literature on prediction markets
(
Chen and Pennock 2007
;
Abernethy et al. 2013
;
Abernethy et al. 2014
;
Cummings et al. 2016
;
Frongillo and Waggoner 2017
)
.
Here we regard “money” as the grand bundle
\mathds{1}
(cf. §
3.2
).

Axiom 7 (
WorstCaseLoss
).

For all
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, there exists
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
with
h_{0}=({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
, we have
\mathsf{sum}(h)\succeq{\mathbf{q}}_{0}-b\,\mathds{1}
.

The
BoundedReserves
axiom can be regarded as a special case of
WorstCaseLoss
, where
b=\|{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})_{i}
, but the converse does not hold.
Indeed, essentially any interesting
\mathds{1}
-invariant cost function satisfying
WorstCaseLoss
does not satisfy
BoundedReserves
.
To see why, let
h=(\mathds{1})
and
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
be any non-dominated trade, i.e., with
{\mathbf{r}}\nsucceq{\mathbf{0}}
.
In other words, start with one of each asset, and take any nontrivial trade.
Let
i
be an asset with
r_{i}<0
, and now consider
h^{\prime}=(\alpha\mathds{1})
where
\alpha=|r_{i}|/2
.
Then by
\mathds{1}
-invariance, we still have
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h^{\prime})
, yet now
\mathsf{sum}(h^{\prime}\oplus{\mathbf{r}})_{i}=\alpha+r_{i}<0
, meaning the market maker is negative in its reserves of asset
i
.
Moreover, as our previous construction
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=-C(-{\mathbf{q}})
from Theorem
3
) gives a
\mathds{1}
-invariant market, we have yet to see how to convert a prediction market to a CFMM that respects the reserves regardless of where the market is initialized.

Fortunately, we can borrow ideas from the prediction market literature to make our construction reserves-aware.
Specifically, we will use the relationship between worst-case loss and
liquidity
, roughly the amount of assets available to buy or sell around a given price, which has been extensively studied
(
Chen and Pennock 2007
;
Abernethy et al. 2013
;
Abernethy et al. 2014
;
Cummings et al. 2016
;
Frongillo and Waggoner 2017
)
.
In the cost-function formulation, there is a simple and direct tradeoff: more liquidity leads to a higher worst-case loss.

Furthermore, there is a simple transformation from convex analysis that changes any given cost function’s liquidity: the perspective transform.
Given a convex function
C({\mathbf{q}})
, its
perspective transform
by
\alpha>0
is the function
C_{\alpha}({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha C({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
.
3
3

3

Often the perspective transform is given by
({\mathbf{q}},\alpha)\mapsto\alpha C({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
, a form we will use in Proposition
5
.

For example, for
C({\mathbf{q}})=\|{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}^{2}
, the perspective transform is
\alpha C({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\|{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}^{2}
.
As
\alpha\to\infty
, the liquidity increases: the gradient of
C
, i.e. the price, changes more slowly in response to a change in quantity
{\mathbf{q}}
.
But the worst-case loss increases as well.
One way to see the latter is via the convex duality relationship to proper scoring rules: the perspective transform on
C
is equivalent to a simple scaling of its corresponding proper scoring rule by
\alpha
.

4.3.
CFMM construction satisfying bounded reserves

Using the ideas above, we now provide a construction taking any cost function to a CFMM that satisfies
BoundedReserves
.
The magic is in “wrapping up” the level sets of
C_{\alpha}({\mathbf{q}}):=\alpha C({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
for different
\alpha
into a single function
\varphi
, so that starting at
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
implies a liquidity of
\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.
Furthermore, we ensure that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\to 0
as
{\mathbf{q}}\to{\mathbf{0}}
, so that a market starting with very low initial reserves quickly moves the price so as not to run out.

Figure 2.

Plots of Construction
1
for Uniswap (L), LMSR (M), and Brier score (R); see §
4.5
for how the construction was applied in each setting.
In each plot, the orange surface plots the
0
-level set of the perspective transform
T:(q_{1},q_{2},\alpha)\mapsto\alpha C(-(q_{1},q_{2})/\alpha)
, i.e., the set of triples
(q_{1},q_{2},\alpha)
such that
\alpha C(-(q_{1},q_{2})/\alpha)=0
.
By design, this set also happens to be the graph of
\varphi
resulting from Construction
1
, i.e., the set of triples
(q_{1},q_{2},\alpha)
such that
\varphi((q_{1},q_{2}))=\alpha
.
To emphasize this connection, the
\alpha
-level sets
\varphi
for
\alpha=0.2,0.6,1.0,1.4,1.8
are shown in blue.
(Technically, these level sets should be projected down to the plane on the bottom of the figure.)
As
T
is convex (a fact of perspective transforms), the sublevel set
T(q_{1},q_{2},\alpha)\leq 0
, the region below the surface, is a convex set.
We can see therefore that
\varphi
is always a concave function.

Construction 1.

Let
C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex, increasing,
\mathds{1}
-invariant cost function satisfying
C({\mathbf{0}})>0
.
Then define
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha
where
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
.

The assumption
C({\mathbf{0}})>0
is without loss of generality; otherwise, add a sufficiently large constant to
C
.
Similarly, while this construction centers around the level set
\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid C({\mathbf{q}})=0\}
, other level sets may be taken be shifting
C
by a constant.

Before arguing that this construction is well-defined and satisfies all our axioms, let us verify that it is behaving as we would hope.
Define
C_{\alpha}({\mathbf{q}}):=\alpha C({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
, which is
C
but with “liquidity level”
\alpha
.
Then by definition the
\alpha
-level set of
\varphi
matches the
0
-level set of
C_{\alpha}
, i.e.,
\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha\}=\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid C_{\alpha}(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\}
.
(See Figure
2
(L) for a visualization.)
If
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
satisfies
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})=\alpha
for
\alpha>0
, then we have
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)=\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C_{\alpha}}(h)
.
In other words,
\varphi
indeed has liquidity level
\alpha
when
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})=\alpha
.
Moreover, if
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}=\beta{\mathbf{q}}
, then
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})=\beta\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, so indeed the liquidity level drops to
0
as
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\to{\mathbf{0}}
, thereby protecting the reserves.
This scaling property is known as
1-homogeneity
, and plays a large role in our analysis; see Lemma
4
and §
4.4
.

Lemma 0.

The function
\varphi
from Construction
1
is well-defined.

Proof.

We must show that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
exists and is unique on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.
Let
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
.
First, define
t=C({\mathbf{0}})>0
.
By
\mathds{1}
-invariance,
C(-t\mathds{1})=0
.
Let
\beta_{1}=\min_{i}q_{i}
and
\beta_{2}=\max_{i}q_{i}
.
Define
\alpha_{1}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{t}
and
\alpha_{2}=\frac{\beta_{2}}{t}
.
We have
-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha_{1}\preceq-t\mathds{1}\preceq-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha_{2}
.
Because
C
is increasing,
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha_{1})\leq C(-t\mathds{1})\leq C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha_{2})
.
Because
C
is convex and defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is continuous.
Therefore,
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
is continuous in
\alpha
for
\alpha>0
.
So there exists
\alpha^{*}\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
such that
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha^{*})=C(-t\mathds{1})=0
.
Furthermore, because
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
is strictly decreasing in
\alpha
, this solution
\alpha^{*}
to
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
is unique.
∎

A useful fact about Construction
1
is that the resulting potential function
\varphi
is 1-homogeneous.
Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
is
1-homogeneous
if for all
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
and
\alpha>0
we have
f(\alpha{\mathbf{q}})=\alpha f({\mathbf{q}})
.
Such functions have useful symmetry for market making
(
Othman et al. 2010
)
; in particular, since their gradients are 0-homogeneous (invariant under scaling), the instantaneous exchange rates between any two assets remain constant when scaling up the reserves.
In part because of this appealing scale invariance, nearly all popular CFMM potential functions used in practice are 1-homogeneous
(
Angeris et al. 2022
)
.

Lemma 0.

Any
\varphi
resulting from Construction
1
is 1-homogeneous, concave, and increasing.

Proof.

Let
C
be the given cost function.

(Homogeneous)
Let
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, and
\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
.
Then
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
by definition of
\varphi
and the fact that it is well defined (Lemma
3
).
Further, for any
\beta>0
, we have
C(-\beta{\mathbf{q}}/(\alpha\beta))=0
, which implies
\varphi(\beta{\mathbf{q}})=\beta\alpha=\beta\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
.

(Increasing)
Let
{\mathbf{0}}\prec{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\precneqq{\mathbf{q}}
.
Let
\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, so that
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
.
As
C
is increasing, and
-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha\succneqq-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha
, we have
C(-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha)>C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
.
Now let
\alpha^{\prime}=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
, so that
C(-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha^{\prime})=0
.
Clearly
\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha
due to uniqueness in Lemma
3
, and if
\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
, we would have
-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha\precneqq-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha^{\prime}
yet
C(-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha)>0=C(-{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}/\alpha^{\prime})
, contradicting
C
being increasing.
We conclude
\alpha>\alpha^{\prime}
, or equivalently,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})>\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
, as desired.

(Concave)
It is well-known that the map
({\mathbf{q}},\alpha)\mapsto\alpha C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
is convex, as the perspective transform of the convex function
{\mathbf{q}}\mapsto C(-{\mathbf{q}})
.
Thus,
\{({\mathbf{q}},\alpha)\mid\alpha C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)\leq 0\}
is a convex set as a sublevel set of a convex function.
Yet this set is precisely the hypograph of
\varphi
, as
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\geq\alpha\iff C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)\leq 0\iff\alpha C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)\leq 0
, so
\{({\mathbf{q}},\alpha)\mid\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\geq\alpha\}=\{({\mathbf{q}},\alpha)\mid\alpha C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)\leq 0\}
.
As the hypograph of
\varphi
is convex,
\varphi
is a concave function.

∎

Proposition 0.

Let
C
satisfy the conditions of Construction
1
as well as
\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid C({\mathbf{q}})=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{<0}
.
Then the resulting
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
defines a CFMM satisfying
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
PathIndependence
,
DemandResponsiveness
, and
BoundedReserves
.

Proof.

We will establish conditions (a), (b), and (c) from Theorem
2
and then apply the result.
Part (a) is implied by the domain of
\varphi
.
Part (b) follows from Lemma
4
.
It thus suffices to show (c).

Let
\overline{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be the concave closure of
\varphi
.
Then
\overline{\varphi}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
; see the proof of Theorem
2
.
As
\varphi
is 1-homogeneous from Lemma
4
, we have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})=\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\varphi(\alpha\mathds{1})=\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\alpha\varphi(\mathds{1})=0
.
Now let
{\mathbf{q}}\succneqq{\mathbf{0}}
with
q_{i}=0
for some
i
.
Suppose for a contradiction that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha>0
.
Let
\{{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
converging to
{\mathbf{q}}
, so that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{k})\to\alpha
.
Then letting
\alpha_{k}=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{k})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{k})
, we have
-{\mathbf{q}}_{k}/\alpha_{k}\to-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha
.
By definition of
\varphi
, each point
-{\mathbf{q}}_{k}/\alpha_{k}
is an element of
Z:=\{{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid C({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=0\}
.
As
Z
is closed (by continuity of
C
), we must have
-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha\in Z
.
Yet by assumption,
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{<0}
, a contradiction as
-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha\not\prec{\mathbf{0}}
(recall that
q_{i}=0
).
We conclude that
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})\leq 0
.
As
\varphi>0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, by continuity we also have
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})\geq 0
.
Thus
\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}})=0\leq 0=\overline{\varphi}({\mathbf{0}})
, giving (c).
∎

4.4.
Homogeneous CFMMs and unique scoring rules

As essentially all CFMMs used in practice are 1-homogeneous
(
Angeris et al. 2022
)
, it is natural to ask whether Construction
1
could have produced them.
We now show the answer is yes: every 1-homogeneous (increasing, concave) potential function is the result of Construction
1
for some choice of cost function.

Proposition 0.

A potential
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
is the result of applying Construction
1
if and only if
\varphi
is 1-homogeneous, increasing, and concave.

Proof.

We have already shown in Lemma
4
and the proof of Proposition
5
that any potential resulting from the construction is 1-homogeneous, increasing, and concave.
Now let
\varphi
be a given 1-homogeneous, increasing, and concave potential.
As in Theorem
4
, define

C({\mathbf{q}})=\sup\left\{c\in\mathbb{R}\mid\varphi(-{\mathbf{q}}-c\mathds{1})\geq 1\right\}
.
Then for any
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, we have
C(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}}-0\mathds{1})=1\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=1
.
Now apply Construction
1
to
C
and obtain
\varphi^{\prime}
.
Then
C(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\iff C(-{\mathbf{q}}/1)=0\iff\varphi^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}})=1
.
We conclude
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=1\iff C(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\iff\varphi^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}})=1
.
As both
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are 1-homogeneous, they are fully determined by any level set, so must now be equal; formally, for any
{\mathbf{q}}\succ{\mathbf{0}}
, letting
\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, we have
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})/\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=1\implies\varphi^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=1\implies\varphi^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha\varphi^{\prime}({\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
.
∎

As a result of Proposition
6
, through Corollary
5
, every 1-homogeneous CFMM can be associated with a unique proper scoring rule; the various level sets of the potential merely correspond to scaling up the underlying scoring rule.
As we will see in §
4.5
, this fact can be used to construct useful CFMMs from the vast literature on proper scoring rules.

Corollary 0.

Every CFMM for a 1-homogeneous, concave, increasing
\varphi
is associated with a unique proper scoring rule.

Before moving to examples, let us briefly remark on connections to convex geometry.
Construction
1
can be regarded geometrically as a gauge function (also known as a Minkowski functional) on a star set, which is known to be a characterization of 1-homogeneous functions.
For any set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the gauge function (Minkowski functional) of
K
is defined as
g_{K}({\mathbf{q}})=\inf\{c>0\mid{\mathbf{q}}\in cK\}
.
Letting
K=\{{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid C(-{\mathbf{q}})\leq 0\}
, we have
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\inf\{c>0\mid{\mathbf{q}}/c\in K\}=g_{K}({\mathbf{q}})
.

4.5.
Examples

Uniswap

Let us perform Construction
1
on the cost function
C_{k}
we derived from Uniswap,
\varphi_{U}({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}
, given some level set
k>0
:

\displaystyle C_{k}({\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(q_{1}+q_{2}+\sqrt{4k^{2}+(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}}\right)~.

First, let us check that
C_{k}
satisfies the condition of Construction
1
: indeed,
C_{k}({\mathbf{0}})=k>0
.
From §
3.5
, we have
C_{k}(-{\mathbf{q}})=0\iff\varphi_{U}({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}=k
.
Thus
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0\iff\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}=\alpha k
.
Thus, the construction yields
\varphi_{k}({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}/k
, which is exactly
\varphi_{U}
when
k=1
, and a scaled version for other values.

LMSR

Let
C({\mathbf{q}})=b\log(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{q_{i}/b})
be LMSR.
Unlike our choice
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=-C(-{\mathbf{q}})
from §
3.5
, let us now consider a “reserves-aware” version, which will automatically scale liquidity with
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
As
C({\mathbf{0}})=b\log n>0
, Construction
1
applies, giving a potential
\varphi
.
See Figure
2
(M) for a visualization.
Moreover, the condition of Proposition
5
is also satisfied, as
C({\mathbf{q}})=0\implies\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{q_{i}}=1\implies q_{i}<0
for all
i
.
So the CFMM with potential
\varphi
satisfies all five of our axioms.
Unfortunately, unlike Uniswap, this
\varphi
does not permit a closed-form expression.
Nonetheless, as we discuss in §
5
, we believe it could be practical to deploy in real markets.

CFMM from Brier score

Let us see how to take one of the most popular scoring rules, the Brier (or quadratic) score,

(4)

S({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})=2{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\bm{\delta}_{i}-\|{\mathbf{p}}\|_{2}^{2}~,

and convert it to a CFMM using our chain of equivalences.
The corresponding convex generating function is
G({\mathbf{p}})=\|{\mathbf{p}}\|^{2}
.
The associated cost function
C
, the convex conjugate of
G
, does not have a convenient closed form in general; for
n=2
assets it can be written

(5)

C({\mathbf{q}})=\begin{cases}q_{1}&q_{1}-q_{2}\geq 2\\
q_{2}&q_{1}-q_{2}\leq-2\\
\tfrac{1}{8}\left((q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}+4(1+q_{1}+q_{2})\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}~.

From this form, one can see that
C
is not always increasing, as e.g. the price for asset 2 becomes 0 when
q_{1}-q_{2}\geq 2
.
The resulting market therefore does not satisfy
NoDominatedTrades
, as selling asset 2 for a price of 0 is dominated by doing nothing.

It turns out, however, that we may still apply Construction
1
to obtain a sensible CFMM; in this case, for
n=2
, we obtain

(6)

\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sqrt{q_{1}q_{2}}+\frac{q_{1}+q_{2}}{2}~,

the sum of the geometric and arithmetic means; see Figure
2
(R).
In fact, this potential function is a hybrid CFMM appearing already in the literature, e.g.
Angeris et al. 2022
.

Curve

Finally, let us consider Curve
(
Egorov 2019
)
, given by
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sum_{i}q_{i}+\sum_{i}(1/q_{i})
.
As noted by
Angeris et al. 2022
, this potential is not 1-homogeneous, and thus could not be produced by Construction
1
.
As a result, its level sets are not merely scaled copies of each other, but change as the value of
\varphi
grows.
(For instance, its 0-level set is the same as Uniswap with
k=1
, but no other level set is in common with Uniswap.)
Nonetheless, we may still apply Theorem
2
to see that
\varphi
satisfies all five of our axioms; to check condition (c), note that
\overline{\varphi}=-\infty
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.

5.
Adaptive Liquidity and Other Future Directions

The literature on automated market makers for decentralized exchanges has largely proceeded somewhat removed from the expansive literature on prediction markets.
We believe the results presented here will allow for these independent lines of research to merge and inform each other.
To conclude, we briefly discuss several avenues for future work, with a focus on liquidity adaptation.

Transaction fees

In practice, CFMMs often allow liquidity to change over time, in two ways: (1) they may charge a “transaction fee”, wherein traders must contribute directly to the reserves in addition to their trade, and (2) they may allow liquidity providers to contribute to the reserves in exchange for a dividend.
Let us first discuss the transaction fee.

Here the market designer chooses a parameter
\gamma\in(0,1]
, where lower
\gamma
corresponds to a higher fee.
The market maker then accepts any trade keeping the potential function
\varphi
constant after discounting the bundle given to the market maker by
\gamma
.
Formally, we can write

(7)

\displaystyle\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi,\gamma}(h)=\{{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\varphi(\mathsf{sum}(h)+\gamma{\mathbf{r}}_{+}-{\mathbf{r}}_{-})=\varphi(\mathsf{sum}(h))\}~,

where
{\mathbf{r}}_{+}=\max({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{-}=\min({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
.
To state this set of valid trades more naturally in terms of transaction fees, relative to the no-fee
\mathsf{ValTrades}
for the vanilla CFMM, we may write

(8)

\displaystyle\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi,\gamma}(h)=\{{\mathbf{r}}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}})\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)\}~,

where
\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}})=\beta{\mathbf{r}}_{+}
for
\beta=(1-\gamma)/\gamma>0
.
That is, a trader may choose any trade
{\mathbf{r}}
keeping
\varphi
constant, but then must also add
\beta{\mathbf{r}}_{+}
to the reserves.

Now suppose
\varphi
is increasing, concave, and 1-homogeneous, as is commonly the case, and as guaranteed by Construction
1
.
Then as
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})
, and
\varphi
is increasing, we will have
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}}))>\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
As we saw in §
4
,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
represents the liquidity level when the reserves are
{\mathbf{q}}
, so we conclude that the liquidity increases after each trade.
Moreover, as the fee-less CFMM satisfies
BoundedReserves
, and
\varphi
is increasing, it is easy to verify that
BoundedReserves
will still be satisfied with the transaction fee.
Thus, the transaction fee successsfully subsidizes the liquidity increase of the market without risking depleting the reserves.

Implicitly defined potential functions

Recall that the
\varphi
from Construnction
1
is only implicitly defined, as the solution
\varphi(\alpha)
to
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
for the given cost function
C
.
Thus, even if
C
is given explicitly,
\varphi
may not have a closed form.
While sometimes one can solve for
\varphi
explicitly, as we saw in §
4.5
, this is the case for the 1-homogeneous CFMM potential
\varphi
we derived from LMSR.
The lack of a closed form poses a challenge, as the transaction fee causes
\varphi
to change, and thus the value of
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
would need to be recalculated rather than being fixed ahead of time.

We now propose a straightforward workaround using the fact that
\varphi
is still implicitly defined by a known cost function
C
.
Suppose the current value
\alpha=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
is publicly known, and a trader wishes to purchase
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
, i.e., such that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\alpha
.
We will ask the trader to announce
{\mathbf{r}}
, as well as the value
\alpha^{\prime}=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}}))
, up to some error tolerance.
As
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)
is monotone in
\alpha
, the trader can easily compute
\alpha^{\prime}
within the desired accuracy given an expression for
C
.
Moreover, the two relevant conditions can be checked on-chain:
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})=\alpha
by
C(-({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}})/\alpha)=0
, and
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}}))=\alpha^{\prime}
by
C(-({\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}}))/\alpha^{\prime})=0
.

To illustrate, let
C
be LMSR, and
\varphi
the result of Construction
1
.
From the condition
C(-{\mathbf{q}}/\alpha)=0
, the level set
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\alpha
is
\{{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-q_{i}/\alpha}=1\}
.
The validity of
{\mathbf{r}}
and
\alpha^{\prime}
can be checked, via
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-(q_{i}+r_{i})/\alpha)\approx 1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(-(q_{i}+r_{i}+\mathsf{fee}({\mathbf{r}})_{i})/\alpha^{\prime})\approx 1
.
Thus, by asking a trader to compute a valid trade, as well as approximating the next value of
\varphi
, the market can proceed even without a closed form for
\varphi
.

Other forms of liquidity adaptation

The prediction market literature offers many other forms of transaction fees and schemes for liquidity adaptation; see e.g.
Othman et al. 2010
;
Othman and Sandholm 2012
;
Li and Vaughan 2013
.
Using the equivalence developed in this work, these market makers can be readily transformed into those for decentralized exchanges.
It would be particularly interesting to study the CFMM transaction fee within the volume-parameterized market (VPM) framework of
Abernethy et al. 2014
; we conjecture that it fails to satisfy their
shrinking spread
axiom (for any choice of volume function) but satisfies their others.
Conversely, the perspective market from that same paper may be of interest to the decentralized finance community.

Other directions

As mentioned above, many CFMMs allow liquidity providers to directly contribute to the reserves.
In light of our results, it would be interesting and potentially impactful to study the elicitation implications of these protocols from the side of liquidity providers.
Finally, while the above focuses on potential contributions from the prediction market literature to decentralized finance, contributions in the direction are likely to be fruitful as well.

Appendix A
Omitted Proofs for CFMM Characterization

To prove Lemma
3
, we separate out the following lemma, which will be useful later.

Lemma 0.

Suppose the CFMM defined by
\varphi
satisfies
PathIndependence
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
Liquidation
, and
DemandResponsiveness
.
For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{R}:=\{{\mathbf{q}}\mid\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=c\}
and let
\mathcal{R}^{+}:=\{{\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}\mid{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R},{\mathbf{r}}\preceq{\mathbf{0}}\}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}^{+}
is convex.

Proof.

We show that for any
{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, any convex combination
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}=(1-\lambda){\mathbf{q}}+\lambda{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
is in
\mathcal{R}^{+}
.
The lemma then follows because for any
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
of the form
{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{z},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
, with
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\geq{\mathbf{0}}
, the convex combination is
(1-\lambda){\mathbf{r}}+\lambda{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{q}}^{*}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{z}+\lambda\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
, which is in
\mathcal{R}^{+}
.

So let
{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and define
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{q}}
.
Then
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}({\mathbf{q}})
.
Let
{\mathbf{r}}^{+}=\max({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{-}=-\min({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{0}})
taken pointwise, so that
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{+}-{\mathbf{r}}^{-}
.
By
Liquidation
, there exists
\beta\geq 0
such that
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}:=\lambda{\mathbf{r}}^{+}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{-}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}({\mathbf{q}})
.
By
NoDominatedTrades
,
\beta\neq 0
as otherwise
\lambda{\mathbf{r}}^{+}
would be dominated by trade
{\mathbf{0}}
.
Let
{\mathbf{r}}_{2}=(1-\lambda){\mathbf{r}}^{+}-(1-\beta){\mathbf{r}}^{-}
.
Then
{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{1})
, because
{\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{1}+{\mathbf{r}}_{2}={\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, by
DemandResponsiveness
,
\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\leq\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}
, implying
\lambda\leq\beta
.

Observe that
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}={\mathbf{q}}+\lambda{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{q}}+\lambda{\mathbf{r}}^{+}-\lambda{\mathbf{r}}^{-}={\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{1}+(\beta-\lambda){\mathbf{r}}^{-}
.
Because
{\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\in\mathcal{R}
,
\beta\geq\lambda
, and
{\mathbf{r}}^{-}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}
, we have
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3
.

By Lemma
2
, the market can be implemented as a CFMM with potential
\varphi
.
Applying Lemma
1
to
c=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
in particular, we obtain
\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
is convex, as claimed.
∎

Proposition
4
will be proven by the following two lemmas.

Lemma 0.

Fix the initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
If a market maker
\mathsf{ValTrades}
with
\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)=\{({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\}
satisfies
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
PathIndependence
, and
DemandResponsiveness
, then it can be implemented as a CFMM with an increasing, concave potential function
\varphi
.

Proof.

Given a market
\mathsf{ValTrades}
satisfying the axioms, Lemma
3
implies it has a potential
\varphi^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi^{\prime}}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
is convex.
Again, for convenience, write
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\varphi^{\prime}}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
and
\mathcal{R}^{+}=\mathcal{R}^{+}_{\varphi^{\prime}}({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.
Define

\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\sup\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}\mid{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}\right\}.

This is a well-known construction in theory of risk measures
[
Föllmer and Schied 2008
,
Föllmer and Weber 2015
]
; we provide a proof that
\varphi
is increasing and concave for completeness.
First,
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
is well-defined, i.e. the supremum is over a nonempty set: for example, taking
u=\min_{i}({\mathbf{q}}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0})_{i}
, we find that
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\succeq{\mathbf{0}}
, i.e.
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}\succeq{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, so
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
.

Second, it implements
\mathsf{ValTrades}
as a CFMM.
By definition of the set of legal reserves
\mathcal{R}
, we have
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)\iff{\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{R}
.
So we must show
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\iff{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}
.
First, for any
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}
, including
{\mathbf{q}}={\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, we have
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=0
.
This follows because
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}\implies\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\geq 0
, but
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})>0
would violate
NoDominatedTrades
, as we would have
{\mathbf{q}}-\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
, which implies some trade in
\mathcal{R}
strictly dominates
{\mathbf{0}}
.
Second, if
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=0
, then
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
, and
{\mathbf{q}}
cannot dominate any member of
\mathcal{R}
(else
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})>0
), so it must be in
\mathcal{R}
.
We have shown
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{R}\iff\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=0=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}_{0})
.

Now we show
\varphi
is increasing.
Let
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\succeq{\mathbf{q}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{q}}
.
Let
u\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}^{+}
contains
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}^{+}
also contains, by upward closure,
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}+{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-u\mathds{1}
.
This proves
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})\geq\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, as the supremum is taken over a superset.
Furthermore, if
q^{\prime}\neq q
, then we claim
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})>\varphi({\mathbf{q}})
, i.e.
\varphi
is increasing.
If
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=u
, then
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-u\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{R}
, but then the trade
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
is dominated by the trade
{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-u\mathds{1}-{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
, both in
\mathsf{ValTrades}(({\mathbf{q}}_{0}))
, contradicting
NoDominatedTrades
.

Finally, for concavity, let
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
{\mathbf{q}}^{*}=(1-\lambda){\mathbf{q}}+\lambda{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}
.
Let
{\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1},{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-u^{\prime}\mathds{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{+}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}^{+}
also contains
(1-\lambda)({\mathbf{q}}-u\mathds{1})+\lambda({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}-u^{\prime}\mathds{1})={\mathbf{q}}^{*}-[(1-\lambda)u+\lambda u^{\prime}]\mathds{1}
, proving that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{*})\geq(1-\lambda)\varphi({\mathbf{q}})+\lambda\varphi({\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 0.

Fix the initial reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
.
If a market maker
\mathsf{ValTrades}
with
\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)=\{({\mathbf{q}}_{0})\}
is a CFMM with an increasing, concave potential function
\varphi
, then it satisfies
Liquidation
,
NoDominatedTrades
,
PathIndependence
, and
DemandResponsiveness
.

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathsf{ValHist}(\epsilon)
and, for short, let
{\mathbf{q}}={\mathbf{q}}_{h}
.

(
DemandResponsiveness

Hence from the supporting hyperplane inequality,
{\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta^{\prime}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\notin U_{{\mathbf{q}}}
, from which we have
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})>\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta^{\prime}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, again using the fact that
\varphi
is defined at the latter point.
As
\varphi
is increasing, we also have that
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})\leq\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}-0\cdot{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
.
Hence by continuity of
\varphi
, we can say that
\exists\beta\in[0,\beta^{\prime}]
s.t.
\varphi({\mathbf{q}})=\varphi({\mathbf{q}}+{\mathbf{r}}-\beta{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
which proves
Liquidation
.
∎

Appendix B
Information Elicitation

B.1.
Sketch for Fact
1

We recall that a
scoring rule
is a function
S:\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, assigning a score
S({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})
to each prediction
{\mathbf{p}}
when the obeserved outcome is
y_{i}
.
The scoring rule is
proper
if the expected score, with respect to any given belief
{\mathbf{p}}^{*}
, is maximized by reporting
{\mathbf{p}}={\mathbf{p}}^{*}
.
It is
strictly proper
if
{\mathbf{p}}={\mathbf{p}}^{*}
uniquely maximizes the expected score.
Examples of strictly proper scoring rules are the log score
S({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})=\log{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
and the Brier or quadratic score
S({\mathbf{p}},y_{i})=2{\mathbf{p}}_{i}-\sum_{j}{\mathbf{p}}_{j}^{2}
.

We now sketch the proof of Fact
1
for completeness.

Proof sketch for Fact
1
.

The first key step is Theorem 3.1 of
[
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
]
, which states that any market maker satisfying Path Independence and Incentive Compatibility must in fact be representable as a “scoring rule market”.
A scoring rule market, in our terminology, is an automated market maker including an asset of cash where
\mathsf{ValTrades}(h)
must map one-to-one to predictions
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
, and the net payoff for moving the market prediction from
{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}
to
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
must be given by the formula of
[
Hanson 2003
]
:

\text{net payoff}=S({\mathbf{p}}_{t},y_{i})-S({\mathbf{p}}_{t-1},y_{i}),

where
S
is a strictly proper scoring rule.
The intuition is that Incentive Compatibility is characterized by use of a strictly proper scoring rule in each round, while Path Independence imposes the requirement of “telescoping sums” for sequences of predictions, giving the above formula.

The second key step is given by classic results of
[
Chen and Pennock 2007
,
Abernethy et al. 2013
]
stating that scoring-rule markets are equivalent to cost-function markets.
In particular, a strictly proper scoring rule
S
corresponds via convex duality to a convex cost function
C
such that

S({\mathbf{p}}_{t},y_{i})-S({\mathbf{p}}_{t-1},y_{i})=({\mathbf{q}}_{t})_{i}-({\mathbf{q}}_{t-1})_{i}-\left[C({\mathbf{q}}_{t})-C({\mathbf{q}}_{t-1})\right].

(Also,
C
is differentiable and has a certain set of gradients, namely the probability simplex.)
Here convex duality gives a one-to-one correspondence between market states
{\mathbf{q}}_{t}
and predictions
{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
, such that the trade
{\mathbf{r}}
with
{\mathbf{q}}_{t}={\mathbf{q}}_{t-1}+{\mathbf{r}}
is equivalent to the prediction update
{\mathbf{p}}_{t-1}\to{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
.
In other words, the cost-function interface to the market is equivalent to the scoring-rule interface.
7
7

7

There is a difference in timing, because as usually implemented, a cost function collects the cash payment at the time of trade while the securities pay off at the end; the scoring rule assesses all payments at the end. For Fact
1
, we must either wait to collect the payment until the end, or assume that one unit of cash has constant utility over time (the usual assumption).

To complete the sketch, we observe the properties of
C
.
Convexity follows from the convex duality of scoring rules discussed above.
Increasing and ones-invariance follow from Incentive Compatibility, as follows.
Because each trade should correspond one-to-one to a feasible prediction, Incentive Compatibility implies there must be no available trades that make a guaranteed profit regardless of the outcome (what the prediction market literature calls an arbitrage opportunity).
Otherwise, traders with many different beliefs would all choose such a trade.
In particular, nonnegative trade bundles must cost a positive amount of money, which gives that
C
is increasing.
And
\alpha
units of the grand bundle
(1,\dots,1)
must cost exactly
\alpha
, since otherwise buying or short-selling it would guarantee profit; this yields ones-invariance.
∎

B.2.
CFMMs elicit ratios of valuations

A consequence of the equivalence is that CFMMs can be viewed as prediction markets.
But what exactly do they “predict”?
The answer is:
CFMMs elicit ratios of valuations.

To explain, let us return to the “cashless prediction market”, in which there are
n
assets.
Instead of payments in cash, each asset had a “price” in units of the grand bundle
(1,\dots,1)
.
Now generalize: consider a market with
n
arbitrary assets
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
with some nonnegative value.
One can similarly use a cost function
C
to define a cashless automated market maker with all prices given in units of the grand bundle
(1,\dots,1)
.

When
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
are contingent securities (e.g.
A_{i}
pays off equal to the number of points scored by team
i
during the season), this is known as a
ratio-of-expectations
market
[
Frongillo and Waggoner 2018
]
.
The market prices reflect the
ratio
between how the value of
A_{1}
and of the grand bundle
A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}
, because traders pay in units of the latter to obtain units of the former.
However, the market valuation of the grand bundle itself is never revealed.
Similarly, in a CFMM, two traders who value asset
A_{i}
at two times asset
A_{j}
will accept the same pairwise trades, even if their cash valuations of the grand bundle are very different.

So CFMMs elicit ratios of valuations.
For example, at any history
h
, a CFMM defines via
\mathsf{ValTrades}_{\varphi}(h)
a truthful mechanism to induce a single agent to reveal, not their valuation in cash of the assets, but their ratios of the value of each asset to the value of the grand bundle.
(This is an extension of the fact that CFMMs define proper scoring rules.).

It is not surprising that a CFMM’s prices reflect ratios of valuations of the assets.
What may be surprising is that,
if one designed a market maker with the intention of eliciting ratios of valuations
, it would result in a CFMM.
For example, a primary purpose of CFMMs is to “provide liquidity”, i.e. offer prices to exchange any asset
A_{i}
for another
A_{j}
.
Paying in units of the grand bundle clearly defeats this purpose, which seems to make a ratio-of-valuations market a poor choice.
However, Incentive Compatibility implies that a ratio-of-valuations market should be implemented by a cost-function, which is equivalent to a CFMM that satisfies
Liquidation
.
So good market-making axioms fall out “for free” from elicitation ones.

Appendix C
Cashless prediction markets

This section formalizes the equivalence of cost function prediction markets with and without cash.
Recall that, given a cost function
C
, it defines the following automated market maker on the
n+1
securities
A_{1},\dots,A_{n},\$
(Section
3.1
):

\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}(h)=\left\{({\mathbf{r}},\alpha)\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\text{ where $\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})$},

Then, we defined the cashless version on
n
securities
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
by Equation
2
, reproduced here:

\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)=\left\{{\mathbf{r}}+\alpha\mathds{1}\mid{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\text{ where $\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})$}.

First, we observe that any valid history and valid trade in the cashless market is a special case of a valid history and trade in the original market

Proposition 0.

Consider the cashless version of the market with a valid history
h
, reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}
, and valid trade
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)
, resulting in history
h^{\prime}
with reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}}={\mathbf{q}}_{h}+{\mathbf{r}}
.
Then there is a valid history of the original prediction market reserves
({\mathbf{q}}_{h},0)
and valid trade
({\mathbf{r}},0)
, resulting in reserves
({\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}},0)
.
Furthermore, for any realization
Y=y_{i}
, that trade’s net payoff is the same in both markets.

Proof.

Because
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathsf{ValTrades}^{\prime}_{C}(h)
, we can write
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}+\left(C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})\right)\mathds{1}
.
By ones-invariance,
C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
So if
({\mathbf{q}}_{h},0)
is a valid set of reserves in the original market, then
({\mathbf{r}},0)
is a valid trade in the original market.
Now by induction on the history, we get that valid histories in the two markets correspond.
Furthermore, the net payoff to the trader for the trade in either market, under any outcome
Y=y_{i}
, is
-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
, since the cash transfer is zero in the original market.
∎

Now, define
f:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
f({\mathbf{b}},a)={\mathbf{b}}+a\mathds{1}
.
We can show the more interesting direction of the equivalence as follows:

Proposition 0.

Consider a cost-function market with valid history
h
, current reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{h}
, and valid trade
({\mathbf{r}},\alpha)\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}(h)
, resulting in history
h^{\prime}
with reserves
{\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}}={\mathbf{q}}_{h}+(-{\mathbf{r}},\alpha)
.
Then in the cashless version, there is a valid history with reserves
f({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
and valid trade
f({\mathbf{r}},\alpha)
, resulting in a history with reserves
f({\mathbf{q}}_{h^{\prime}})
, such that for any outcome
Y=y_{i}
, the trade’s net payoff is the same as in the original market.

Proof.

If
({\mathbf{r}},\alpha)\in\mathsf{ValTrades}_{C}(h)
, then
\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
.
Suppose for now
f({\mathbf{q}}_{h})
are valid reserves in the cashless market.
Then the trade
f({\mathbf{r}},\alpha)={\mathbf{r}}+\alpha\mathds{1}
, with
\alpha=C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h}-{\mathbf{r}})-C(-{\mathbf{q}}_{h})
, proving that it is a valid trade in the cashless market.
Now by induction on the history, we get that valid histories in the two markets correspond.
Furthermore, the net payoff to the trader for the trade in either market, under any outcome
Y=y_{i}
, is
-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}-\alpha
.
In the original market, this occurs because the trader pays
\alpha
units of cash and additionally gives the bundle
{\mathbf{r}}
, which results in
-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
when
Y=y_{i}
.
In the cashless market, this occurs because the traders gives the bundle
{\mathbf{r}}+\alpha\mathds{1}
, which results in
-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}-\alpha
when
Y=y_{i}
.
∎
