Computing critical angles between two convex cones
Welington de OliveiraValentina SessaDavid Sossa

Abstract




Abstract
.

This paper addresses the numerical computation of critical angles between two convex cones in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces. We present a novel approach to computing these critical angles by reducing the problem to finding stationary points of a fractional programming problem. To efficiently compute these stationary points, we introduce a partial linearization-like algorithm that offers significant computational advantages. Solving a sequence of strictly convex subproblems with straightforward solutions in several settings gives the proposed algorithm high computational efficiency while delivering reliable results: our theoretical analysis demonstrates that the proposed algorithm asymptotically computes critical angles. Numerical experiments validate the efficiency of our approach, even when dealing with problems of relatively large dimensions: only a few seconds are necessary to compute critical angles between different types of cones in spaces of dimension 1000.

Mathematics Subject Classification
: 52A40; 90C26; 90C32; 90C46; 17C99

Keywords
: Critical angle; Convex cone; Fractional Programming; Optimality conditions; Euclidean Jordan algebra.

1
Introduction

In this work, we are concerned with the problem of computing critical (principal) angles between two closed convex cones
P\neq\emptyset
and
Q\neq\emptyset
in Euclidean spaces. This class of problems, investigated from the theoretical point of view in
[
32
]
and
[
33
]
,
finds applications in image classifications and other domains
[
37
]
.
In the more straightforward case where
P
and
Q
are linear subspaces, the concept of critical angles
has applications in statistics and numerical linear algebra.
Such a concept has been abundantly studied in the literature, both from a theoretical and computational
point of view
[
23
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no numerical procedure exists to compute critical angles when
P
and
Q
are not linear subspaces but convex cones in medium or high dimensional spaces. This work contributes to fill the gap by proposing an implementable algorithm for computing critical angles between two convex cones. Furthermore, our framework is general enough to cover several classes of cones, such as polyhedral, ellipsoidal, Loewnerian cones, and others. Indeed, our methodology applies to the vast family of LISC cones, which are closed convex cones that can be expressed as linear images of the symmetric cones of certain Euclidean Jordan algebras, plus some mild assumptions (see Definition
4
below).

By definition, the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
, in a Euclidean space
\mathbb{V}
equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, is the maximum value of
\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle
on
(P\cap S_{\mathbb{V}})\times(Q\cap S_{\mathbb{V}})
, where
S_{\mathbb{V}}
denotes the unit sphere of
\mathbb{V}
. Critical angles between
P
and
Q
are angles formed by vectors
u\in P
and
v\in Q
satisfying some complementarity conditions (see Definition
1
), which are necessary conditions for
u
and
v
to achieve the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
.

The study of critical angles for the case
P=Q
was first carried out by Iusem and Seeger
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Later, Seeger and Sossa expanded this study to two different cones
[
32
,
33
]
. See also the recent contributions of Orlitzky
[
24
]
and Bauschke et al.
[
3
]
.
Concerning the computation of critical angles, some methods were given in
[
33
]
for specially structured cones such as revolution cones, ellipsoidal, and cones of matrices. These methods are either applicable to specific instances of cones or limited to problems on spaces of small dimensions. In
[
32
]
, an exhaustive method was developed for computing all critical angles between polyhedral cones. Basically, one must select subsets of generators of the cones, and solve a generalized eigenvalue problem for each selection. This has a combinatorial nature and its numerical implementation is only possible for polyhedral cones with few generators.

In this work, we show that computing critical angles between two convex cones reduces to the problem of finding stationary points of a fractional programming (FP) problem, that is, the optimization of the ratio of two continuous functions over a nonempty, closed convex set
[
13
]
(see (
5
) for the precise definition).
When specialized to the family of LISC cones, the feasible set of our FP is the Cartesian product of spectraplexes (sets analogous to the standard simplex) of the involved Euclidean Jordan algebras. Thus, from the numerical point of view, our FP is easier to handle than the original maximal angle problem formulation, whose feasible set has spherical conditions.
Notably, most of the theoretical and algorithmic works in fractional programming are applied to the convex case, i.e., convex numerator and concave denominator. Dinkelbach’s method
[
9
,
36
]
is the classical approach used to solve convex fractional problems by tackling a parametric reformulation of the original FP problem.
Several iterative schemes based on Dinkelbach’s idea are proposed for special cases of convex FP
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
35
,
38
]
. In the recent paper
[
6
]
, an extrapolated proximal subgradient algorithm is applied to nonconvex and nonsmooth FPs. A disadvantage of that algorithm is that a nonconvex subproblem must be globally solved at every iteration, restricting thus the approach to a few particular FP cases. In theory, that algorithm could be applied to our FP formulation, but the structure of our problem is not (computationally) favorable to that approach.

This study addresses the challenges posed by our FP, a nonconvex FP problem that does not conform to the requirements of existing practical methodologies. Inspired by the recent paper
[
14
]
, we introduce a Sequential Regularized Partial Linearization algorithm to compute a stationary point of our FP formulation, thus a critical angle. Our algorithm, that features the main contribution of this work, solves a sequence of two independent convex subproblems, which are projections onto well-structured sets. The essential advantage of our approach lies in the fact that solving these subproblems requires minimal computational efforts as they have straightforward solutions in several settings of practical interest.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, we provide numerical results that focus on computing critical angles between LISC cones. These angles are commonly examined in the relevant literature, but a thorough numerical treatment of these problems has not been previously investigated.

This work is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the main definitions and preliminary results. The concept of LISC cones and relevant properties are presented in Section
3
. Section
4
clarifies the connection between critical angles and stationary points of the FP formulation. The proposed algorithm is presented in Section
5

along with a converge analysis. Numerical experiments are reported in Section
6
. Finally, some conclusions are given in Section
7
.

2
Main definitions and prerequisites

Let
\mathbb{V}
,
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
be Euclidean spaces. We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{V})
the set of all nontrivial closed convex cones in
\mathbb{V}
, that is,

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{V})=\{K\mbox{ closed convex cone in }\mathbb{V}:\,K\neq\{0\},\,K\neq\mathbb{V}\}.

(1)

The dual cone of
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{V})
is denoted by
K^{\ast}
:

K^{\ast}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{V}:\,\langle\zeta,u\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall\;u\in K\}.

Throughout this work,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
refers to the inner product of each Euclidean space that we are considering. The corresponding inner product to its space will be clear from the context. In particular, whenever we consider the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we assume that its inner product is
\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\top}y
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The norm
\|\cdot\|
refers to the Euclidean norm induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Given two cones
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{V})
, by definition, the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
is given by

\Theta(P,Q):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max&\displaystyle\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t.}&u\in P,\,\|u\|=1\\
&v\in Q,\,\|v\|=1.\end{array}\right.

(2)

As in
data analysis
, the cosinus similarity of two vectors
u,v
is defined as the cosinus of the angle
\theta
between them, i.e.,
\cos(\theta)=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}\textstyle{\|}v\textstyle{\|}}
. Recall that two proportional vectors have a cosinus similarity of 1, two orthogonal vectors have a similarity of 0, and two opposite vectors have a similarity of -1. Hence, the cosinus of the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
can be computed by solving the following (nonconvex) optimization problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t.}&u\in P,\,\|u\|=1,\\
&v\in Q,\,\|v\|=1.\end{array}\right.

(3)

The following definition is crucial for the remainder of this work.

Definition 1
.

(Maximal and critical angles between two cones).

1.

A pair
(u,v)\in\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}
solving problem (
3
) is called an
antipodal pair
of
(P,Q)
. The angle between an antipodal pair is called the maximal angle between the cones
P
and
Q
.

2.

A pair
(u,v)
is called a
critical pair
of
(P,Q)
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u\in P,\,\|u\|=1,\\[2.84526pt]
v\in Q,\,\|v\|=1,\\[2.84526pt]
v-\langle u,v\rangle u\in P^{\ast},\\[2.84526pt]
u-\langle u,v\rangle v\in Q^{\ast},\end{array}\right.

(4)

where
P^{\ast}
and
Q^{\ast}
are the dual cones of
P
and
Q
, respectively.
The angle between a critical pair is called a critical angle between the cones
P
and
Q
.

As we will see in Section
3
, in many important cases the cones
P

and
Q
are images of linear mappings applied to more structured/simpler cones
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
, i.e.,
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
and
Q=H(K_{\mathbb{Y}})
, with
G
and
H
linear applications of appropriated dimensions.
In these cases, it is computationally advantageous to work with the more structured cones
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
. As we will see in Section
4
, problem (
2
) can be reformulated as

\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\Phi(x,y):=\frac{\langle Gx,Hy\rangle}{\left\|Gx\right\|\left\|Hy\right\|}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1\\
&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1.\end{array}\right.

(5)

In this case,
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
are the symmetric cones of certain Euclidean Jordan algebras
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, and
e_{\mathbb{X}}
and
e_{\mathbb{Y}}
denote the unit elements of
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, respectively.

Observe that the optimization problems (
3
) and (
5
)
share a common structure. Indeed, they fit into the following more general class of problems:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\,\phi_{1}(x)=0\\
&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\,\phi_{2}(y)=0,\end{array}\right.

(6)

where
\Psi:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi_{1}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\phi_{2}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
are differentiable functions over the feasible set of (
6
).

Definition 2
.

We say that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a
stationary point
of (
6
) if there exist
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(\nabla_{x}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\gamma_{1}\nabla\phi_{1}(\bar{x})\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}}^{\ast},\\
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(\nabla_{y}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\gamma_{2}\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{y})\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\ast},\\
\phi_{1}(\bar{x})=0,\;\phi_{2}(\bar{y})=0,\end{array}\right.

(7)

where the notation
\perp
means orthogonality.

We now show that our concept of stationarity coincides with that of classic stationarity for the nonlinear optimization problem (
6
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
K_{\mathbb{X}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{X})
,
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Y})
and
suppose that
\Psi,\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are differentiable over the feasible set of problem (
6
). Then
the definition of stationarity given in (
7
) coincides with that of stationarity for the nonlinear programming problem (
6
).

Proof.

We can rewrite problem (
6
) with the help of the indicator function of the convex sets
C_{1}:=K_{\mathbb{X}}\times\mathbb{Y}
and
C_{2}:=\mathbb{X}\times K_{\mathbb{Y}}
as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)+i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\phi_{1}(x)=0,\;\phi_{2}(y)=0.\end{array}\right.

If the above problem has Lagrange multipliers
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
(e.g., if
(\nabla\phi_{1}(\bar{x}),\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{y}))\neq(0,0)
– satisfying thus the LICQ constraint qualification), then all of its (local) solutions must satisfy the KKT system (see for instance
[
29
, §10]
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0\in\partial^{\tt C}_{(x,y)}L(\bar{x},\bar{y},\gamma)\\
\bar{x}\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\,\phi_{1}(\bar{x})=0\\
\bar{y}\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\,\phi_{2}(\bar{y})=0,\end{array}\right.

where the Lagrangian function is given by

L(x,y,\gamma):=\Psi(x,y)+i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)+\gamma_{1}\phi(x)+\gamma_{2}\phi_{2}(y),

and
\partial^{\tt C}f
denotes the Clarke subdifferential of a locally Lipschitz function
f
. Observe that

\partial^{\tt C}_{(x,y)}L(x,y,\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}\Psi(x,y)\\
\nabla_{y}\Psi(x,y)\end{pmatrix}+\partial^{\tt C}[i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)]+\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\nabla\phi_{1}(x)\\
\gamma_{2}\nabla\phi_{2}(y)\\
\end{pmatrix},

and being
i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)
a convex function, its Clarke subdifferential coincides with that of the Convex Analysis. Thus,
\partial^{\tt C}[i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)]=\partial[i_{C_{1}}(x,y)+i_{C_{2}}(x,y)]=\partial i_{C_{2}}(x,y)+\partial i_{C_{2}}(x,y)
provided

{\tt ri\,dom}(i_{C_{1}})\cap{\tt ri\,dom}(i_{C_{2}})\neq\emptyset
(see
[
30
, Thm 23.8]
).
Furthermore,
{\tt ri\,dom}(i_{C_{1}})={\tt ri}K_{\mathbb{X}}\times\mathbb{Y}
and

{\tt ri\,dom}(i_{C_{2}})=\mathbb{X}\times{\tt ri}K_{\mathbb{Y}}
. As
K_{\mathbb{X}}\neq\{0\}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\neq\{0\}
(c.f. (
1
)) then

{\tt ri}K_{\mathbb{X}}\neq\emptyset
and
{\tt ri}K_{\mathbb{Y}}\neq\emptyset
. Recall that
C_{1}
is a convex set, and thus

\partial i_{C_{1}}(x,y)=N_{C_{1}}(x,y)=N_{K_{\mathbb{X}}}(x)\times 0
. Analogously,
\partial i_{C_{2}}(x,y)=0\times N_{K_{\mathbb{Y}}}(y)
.
Hence, the above KKT system becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0\in\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\\
\nabla_{y}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}N_{K_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bar{x})\\
N_{K_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bar{y})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\nabla\phi_{1}(\bar{x})\\
\gamma_{2}\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{y})\\
\end{pmatrix}\\
\bar{x}\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\,\phi_{1}(\bar{x})=0\\
\bar{y}\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\,\phi_{2}(\bar{y})=0.\end{array}\right.

(8)

Then, by using the well-known results in
[
11
, Prop. 1.1.3]
, we can see that the above system is nothing but the one given in (
7
).

∎

It is not surprising that (
4
) is a particular case of (
7
), as the following corollary shows.

Corollary 1
.

Under the setting of Theorem
1
,
suppose that
K_{\mathbb{X}}=P
,
K_{\mathbb{Y}}=Q
,
\Psi(x,y)=\langle x,y\rangle
,
\phi_{1}(x)=\textstyle{\|}x\textstyle{\|}-1
, and
\phi_{1}(y)=\textstyle{\|}y\textstyle{\|}-1
, i.e., problem (
6
) coincides with (
3
). Furthermore, suppose that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of problem (
6
). Then, the Lagrange multipliers
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
in (
7
) are given by

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=-\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle.

Proof.

In this setting, the Lagrange multipliers always exist due to the fact that

(\nabla\phi_{1}(\bar{x}),\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{y}))=({\bar{x}}/{\textstyle{\|}\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}},{\bar{y}}/{\textstyle{\|}\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}})\neq(0,0)
, i.e., the problem satisfies the LICQ constraint qualification.
Furthermore, system (
8
) becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0\in\begin{pmatrix}\bar{y}\\
\bar{x}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}N_{P}(\bar{x})\\
N_{Q}(\bar{y})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}\bar{x}/\textstyle{\|}\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}\\
\gamma_{2}\bar{y}/\textstyle{\|}\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}\\
\end{pmatrix}\\
\bar{x}\in P,\,\|\bar{x}\|=1\\
\bar{y}\in Q,\,\|\bar{y}\|=1.\end{array}\right.

Let
\zeta\in N_{P}(\bar{x})
such that
0=\bar{y}+\zeta+\gamma_{1}\bar{x}/\textstyle{\|}\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}
. By multiplying this equality by
\bar{x}
and recalling that
\|\bar{x}\|=1
, we get
0=\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle+\langle\bar{x},\zeta\rangle+\gamma_{1}
. As
\langle\bar{x},\zeta\rangle=0
, we have that

\gamma_{1}=-\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle
. Analogously,
\gamma_{2}=-\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle
.
∎

It is straightforward to see that, in the setting of the above corollary, the conditions in (
7
) boils down to (
4
).

3
LISC cones

This section presents the concept of LISC cones which are sets obtained as
Linear Image of Symmetric Cones
. Recall that a
symmetric cone
in a Euclidean space is a cone that is self-dual and homogeneous, see
[
12
, Section I.1]
. The symmetric cones possess a reach structure and they are central objects in many optimization problems such as linear programming, second-order cone programming, and semidefinite programming
[
39
,
1
,
28
]
. For those programs, the theory and the numerical resolution are well-developed and nowadays there are many solvers available to deal with them. It turns out that many convex cones arise as the linear image of symmetric ones. As we shall see in the next sections, the computation of critical angles for these cones can be performed by solving a sequence of (convex) symmetric cone programs.

3.1
Main definitions and some examples

It is known that every symmetric cone is obtained as the cone of square elements of a certain Euclidean Jordan Algebra (EJA), see
[
12
, Theorem III.3.1]
. Thus, to present our results we shall use the machinery of EJAs. We refer the reader to the book of Faraut and Korányi
[
12
]
for an exposition of the definitions and main results of this algebra.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be an EJA of rank
r
with unit element
e
, and equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and a Jordan product
\circ
.
We need to recall some ingredients from EJA concerning the spectral decomposition
[
12
, Theorem III.1.2]
. An element
c\in\mathbb{X}
is called
idempotent
if
c\circ c=c
. An idempotent element
c
is called
primitive
if it is non-zero and cannot be written as the sum of two non-zero idempotents. A collection of primitive idempotent elements
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\}
is called
Jordan frame
if
c_{i}\circ c_{j}=0
for all
i\neq j
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}=e
. Any element
x
in
\mathbb{X}
admits a spectral decomposition. That is, there exist scalars
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
(called the eigenvalues of
x
) and a Jordan frame
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\}
such that
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}
. The trace of
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}
is defined as
{\rm tr}(x)=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}
.

Definition 3

(Symmetric cone in
\mathbb{X}
)
.

We denote by
K_{\mathbb{X}}
the symmetric cone in
\mathbb{X}
, which corresponds to the cone of square elements of
\mathbb{X}
, that is,
K_{\mathbb{X}}=\{x\circ x:x\in\mathbb{X}\}
.

Observe that
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}\in K_{\mathbb{X}}
if and only if
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
.
Below, we provide some examples of EJAs and corresponding symmetric cones.

Example 1

(EJAs and corresponding symmetric cones)
.

(a)

The space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an EJA of rank
n
with unit element
{\bf 1}_{n}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
,
\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\top}y
, and
x\circ y=(x_{1}y_{1},\ldots,x_{n}y_{n})^{\top}
. Its associated symmetric cone is the nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

(b)

Alternatively,
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
is an EJA (Jordan spin algebra) of rank
2
with unit element given by
e=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{\top}
. For
x=(\xi,t),\,y=(\eta,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, the inner product is
\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\top}y
and
x\circ y=(s\xi+t\eta,x^{\top}y)
. Its corresponding symmetric cone is the
Lorentz cone
, which is given by
\mathcal{L}^{n}_{+}:=\{(\xi,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\xi\|\leq t\}
.

(c)

The space
\mathcal{S}^{n}
of the symmetric matrices of order
n
is an EJA of rank
n
with unit element
I
(the identity matrix of order
n
), equipped with the Frobenius (or trace) inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle={\rm Tr}(XY)
and the Jordan product
X\circ Y=(XY+YX)/2
. Its associated symmetric cone is the
positive semidefinite (SDP) cone
which is defined as follows,
\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\{X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}\,:\,u^{\top}Xu\geq 0,\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

A cone
P
in a Euclidean space
\mathbb{V}
is a LISC cone if it can be written as the linear image of some symmetric cone satisfying some mild assumptions. More precisely:

Definition 4
.

Let
P
be a closed convex cone in a Euclidean space
\mathbb{V}
. We say that
P
is a
LISC cone
if there exist a Euclidean Jordan algebra
\mathbb{X}
and a linear map
G:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{V}
such that
G(K_{\mathbb{X}})=P
, satisfying the following assumptions:

G(c)\neq 0
for every primitive idempotent element
c\in\mathbb{X}
.

(A1)

P
is pointed (i.e.
P\cap(-P)=\{0\}
).

(A2)

Observe that in the above definition, we are assuming that
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
is closed in advance. This is because in general, the linear image of
K_{\mathbb{X}}
may fail to be closed (cf.
[
26
]
).

There are vast classes of convex cones that are LISC cones. We now list some of them.

Example 2

(LISC cones)
.

(a)

Polyhedral cones

[
17
]
. A cone
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is polyhedral if there exist vectors
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(generators of
P
) such that

P={\rm cone}\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}:=\{\alpha_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}g_{p}:\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\forall i=1,\ldots,p\}.

Then, by taking
G:=[g_{1}\,\cdots\,g_{p}]
as the
n\times p
matrix formed by the generators of
P
, we have that
P=G(\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+})
. It is usual to assume that the generators of
P
are positive linear independent (i.e., if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}g_{i}=0
and
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
, then
\alpha_{i}=0
for all
i
). This property implies
(A1)
and
(A2)
. Indeed, the primitive idempotent elements of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
are
e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}
which are the elements of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Observe that
G(e_{i})=g_{i}\neq 0
for every
i
. Then,
(A1)
holds. On the other hand, suppose that
(A2)
is not satisfied. That is, suppose that
P
is not pointed. Then, there exists
u\neq 0
such that
u=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}g_{i}
and
-u=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}g_{i}
for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})g_{i}=0
. Since we assume that the generators of
P
are positive linear independents, we conclude that
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}=0
for all
i
. It means
u=0
which is a contradiction. Hence,
P
is a LISC cone with the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
as its associated symmetric cone.

(b)

Ellipsoidal cones

[
20
,
34
,
33
]
. A cone
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is ellipsoidal if there exists a symmetric positive definite matrix
A
of order
n-1
such that

P=\{(\xi,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:\sqrt{\langle\xi,A\xi}\rangle\leq t\}.

By taking the linear map
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by
G(\xi,t)=(A^{-1/2}\xi,t)
, we have that
P=G(\mathcal{L}^{n}_{+})
. Since
G
is invertible and
\mathcal{L}^{n}_{+}
is pointed, we have that
(A1)
and
(A2)
are satisfied. Thus,
P
is a LISC cone with the Lorentz cone
\mathcal{L}^{n}_{+}
as its associated symmetric cone.

(c)

Loewnerian cones

[
31
]
. By definition, a cone
P
in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
is Loewnerian if there exists an invertible linear map
G:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathcal{S}^{n}
such that

. Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)

As we will shortly see, our technique to find critical angles between a pair of LISC cones consists of reformulating problem (
2
) as a minimization problem over a convex set. More precisely, for a pair of LISC cones
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
and
Q=H(K_{\mathbb{Y}})
, the (bilinear) minimization problem (
3
) with nonconvex feasible set

S_{P,Q}:=\{(u,v)\in P\times Q:\|u\|=1,\,\|v\|=1\}

will be reformulated as a (fractional) minimization problem (
5
) with convex set

\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}:=\{(x,y)\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\times K_{\mathbb{Y}}:\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1,\,\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1\},

where
e_{\mathbb{X}}
and
e_{\mathbb{Y}}
are the unit elements of the EJAs
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, respectively.
Observe that
S_{P,Q}=S_{P}\times S_{Q}
, where

S_{P}:=\{u\in P:\|u\|=1\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{Q}:=\{v\in Q:\|v\|=1\}.

Analogously,
\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}\times\Delta_{\mathbb{Y}}
, where

\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}:=\{x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}:\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{Y}}:=\{y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}:\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1\}.

Here,
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{Y}}
are called the
spectraplexes
of
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, respectively.
A key fact to formulate the maximal angle problem as the minimization problem (
3
) is that each nonzero
u\in P
can be written as a positive multiple of an element of
S_{P}
; indeed,
u=\|u\|\frac{u}{\|u\|}
and
\frac{u}{\|u\|}\in S_{P}
(an analogous property
is for a nonzero
v\in Q
). In the same way, we must ensure that each nonzero
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}
can be written as a positive multiple of an element of
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
(an analogous property for a nonzero
y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}
). This is proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be an EJA of rank
r
with unit element
e_{\mathbb{X}}
. Let
K_{\mathbb{X}}
be the symmetric cone of
\mathbb{X}
. Then, the following statements are satisfied:

1.

\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle>0
for all
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
.

2.

\{\alpha x:x\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X}},\,\alpha>0\}=K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}
be a nonzero element in
K_{\mathbb{X}}
. Then,
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
, and not all of them are zero. Thus,

\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\|c_{i}\|^{2}\geq 0.

Then, if
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=0
we have that
\lambda_{i}\|c_{i}\|^{2}=0
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
. Since any primitive element is nonzero, we deduce that
\lambda_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
which contradicts the fact that
x
is nonzero. We conclude
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle>0
.

Let us prove the second part. Since
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
is contained in
K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
and because of the cone structure of
K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
, we have that
\{\alpha x:x\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X}},\,\alpha>0\}\subseteq K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
u\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
. From the previous part, we have that
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},u\rangle>0
. Then,
u=\alpha x
with
\alpha:=\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},u\rangle>0
and
x:=u/\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},u\rangle\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Now, to pass from problem (
2
) (which is equivalent to (
3
)) to the fractional one (
5
), we must ensure that
Gx\neq 0
for every
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
because we shall need to normalize

(an analogous observation is for

). The next lemma shows that assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
help us to achieve that property.
But before, observe that a general LISC cone can also be described by a sort of conic combination of a (possibly uncountable) set of vectors, similar to the polyhedral case. Indeed, let
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
denote the set of (ordered) Jordan frames of
\mathbb{X}
. That is,
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
if and only if
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\}
is a Jordan frame of
\mathbb{X}
. From the spectral decomposition property, it is not difficult to see that
K_{\mathbb{X}}
can be written as

K_{\mathbb{X}}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}c_{i}:\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\geq 0,(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}\right\}=\bigcup_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}}{\rm cone}\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\}.

Then, the LISC cone
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
can be written as

P=\bigcup_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}}{\rm cone}\{G(c_{1}),\ldots,G(c_{r})\}.

(9)

Observe that in the polyhedral case, the representation (
9
) coincides with the conic combination of its generators since the only Jordan frame of
\mathbb{R}^{r}
is its canonical basis.

Lemma 2
.

Let
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
be a LISC cone in
\mathbb{V}
with
\mathbb{X}
being an EJA of rank
r
. Then,
Gx\neq 0
for every
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Reasoning by contradiction, suppose that there is
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}c_{i}
in
K_{\mathbb{X}}\setminus\{0\}
such that
G(x)=0
. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}G(c_{i})=0
. Since
x
is nonzero, we may assume that
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
. Thus,

-G(c_{1})=\sum_{i=2}^{r}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)G(c_{i}).

Because of
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}
, we have that
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for every
i
. Then, from representation (
9
) we deduce that
-G(c_{1})\in P
. On the other hand, it is clear, also for the representation (
9
), that
G(c_{1})\in P
. Because of the assumption
(A1)
, we have that
G(c_{1})\neq 0
. We have proved that
P\cap(-P)
has a nonzero element which contradicts assumption
(A2)
.
∎

As a last ingredient of EJA, we now explain how to compute the orthogonal projection of
b\in\mathbb{X}
onto the spectraplex
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}=\{x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}:\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1\}

3.2
Projection onto spectraplexes

An EJA is said to be
scalarizable
if there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\langle x,y\rangle=\kappa{\rm tr}(x\circ y)
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{X}
. The number
\kappa
is called the scaling factor of the EJA. The EJAs in Example
1
are scalarizable: the scaling factors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{S}^{n}
are
1
,
1/2
and
1
, respectively. From now on, we assume that all the EJAs considered in this work are scalarizable. For
x\in\mathbb{X}
,
\lambda(x)
denotes the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
whose components are the eigenvalues of
x
arranged in nonincreasing order, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}(x)
are the eigenvalues of
x
. A set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{X}
is called
spectral set
if there exists a permutation invariant set
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
\Omega=\lambda^{-1}(\mathcal{Q})
. Observe that
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
is a spectral set whose associated permutation invariant set is

\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:\lambda~\geq~0,\>\langle{\bf 1}_{r},\lambda\rangle~=~\frac{1}{\kappa}\right\}.

Indeed,
x\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
if and only if
\lambda_{i}(x)\geq 0
for all
i
, and

1=\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=\kappa{\rm tr}(e_{\mathbb{X}}\circ x)=\kappa{\rm tr}(x)=\kappa(\lambda_{1}(x)+\cdots+\lambda_{r}(x)).

It means
\lambda(x)\geq 0
and
\langle{\bf 1}_{r},\lambda(x)\rangle=\frac{1}{\kappa}
. Thus,
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}=\lambda^{-1}(\Delta_{r}^{\kappa})
. Observe that when
\kappa=1
, which is the case for the algebras
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathcal{S}^{n}
,
\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}
coincides with the standard simplex

\Delta_{r}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:x~\geq~0,\>\langle{\bf 1}_{r},x\rangle~=~1\}.

Incidentally, we observe that
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
is compact. Indeed, it is clear that
\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}
is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Then, the compactness of
\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}
is transferred to its associated spectral set
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}

[
2
, Theorem 27]
. This result will be used later, so we state it as a proposition.

Proposition 1
.

The spectraplex
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
is compact in
\mathbb{X}
.

The next proposition shows that the computation of the orthogonal projection onto
\Delta_{\mathbb{X}}
is reduced to the computation of the orthogonal projection onto
\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}
which is easier to handle.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a scalarizable EJA with rank
r
and scaling factor
\kappa
. Let
b\in\mathbb{X}
with spectral decomposition
b=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(b)c_{i}
. Then,
\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\bar{\xi}_{i}c_{i}
is the solution of

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x-b\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X}},\end{array}

(10)

where
\bar{\xi}:=(\bar{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\xi}_{r})^{\top}
is the solution of

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\xi-\lambda(b)\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\xi\in\Delta_{r}^{\kappa}.\end{array}

(11)

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the proof of
[
27
, Proposition 10]
where the main tool is the commutation principle in EJA developed in
[
27
]
.
∎

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{V})
be LISC cones represented by
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
and
Q=H(K_{\mathbb{Y}})
, respectively. With these representations, the cosinus of the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
, formulated in (
3
), becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\langle Gx,Hy\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\;\|Gx\|=1,\\
&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\;\|Hy\|=1.\end{array}\right.

(15)

Lemma 3
.

The pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of (
15
) if and only if

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(G^{\top}H\bar{y}-\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle G^{\top}G\bar{x}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\\
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(H^{\top}G\bar{x}-\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle H^{\top}H\bar{y}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\\
\|G\bar{x}\|=1,\;\|H\bar{y}\|=1.\end{array}\right.

(16)

Proof.

Observe that for all
(x,y)
feasible to problem (
15
), the gradients of
\Psi(x,y)=\langle Gx,Hy\rangle
,
\phi_{1}(x):=\|Gx\|-1
and
\phi_{2}(y):=\|Hy\|-1
are all well defined and given by

\nabla\Psi(x,y)=\left(G^{\top}Hy,H^{\top}Gx\right),\;\nabla\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{G^{\top}Gx}{\|Gx\|}\;\mbox{and}\;\nabla\phi_{2}(y)=\frac{H^{\top}Hy}{\|Hy\|}.

It follows from (
7
) that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{x},\nabla_{x}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\rangle+\gamma_{1}\langle\bar{x},\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{x})\rangle=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle+\gamma_{1}\textstyle{\|}G\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle+\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{y},\nabla_{y}\Psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\rangle+\gamma_{2}\langle\bar{y},\nabla\phi_{2}(\bar{y})\rangle=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle+\gamma_{2}\textstyle{\|}H\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle+\gamma_{2}

i.e.,
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=-\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle
.
The result then follows from (
7
) by recalling that

K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
are self-dual, then
K_{\mathbb{X}}^{\ast}=K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\ast}=K_{\mathbb{Y}}
.

We have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{G^{\top}Hy}{\|Gx\|\|Hy\|}-\frac{\langle Gx,Hy\rangle}{\left\|Gx\right\|\left\|Hy\right\|}\frac{G^{\top}Gx}{\|Gx\|^{2}},

(18a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{H^{\top}Gx}{\|Gx\|\|Hy\|}-\frac{\langle Gx,Hy\rangle}{\left\|Gx\right\|\left\|Hy\right\|}\frac{H^{\top}Hy}{\|Hy\|^{2}}.

(18b)

Lemma 4
.

The pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
) if and only if

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(\frac{G^{\top}H\bar{y}}{\|H\bar{y}\|}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}\frac{G^{\top}G\bar{x}}{\|G\bar{x}\|}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\\
\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(\frac{H^{\top}G\bar{x}}{\|G\bar{x}\|}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}\frac{H^{\top}H\bar{y}}{\|H\bar{y}\|}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\\
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle=1,\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle=1.\end{array}\right.

(19)

Proof.

It follows from Definition
2
and the equations in (
18
) that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
) if, and only if, there exist
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(\frac{G^{\top}H\bar{y}}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}\frac{G^{\top}G\bar{x}}{\|G\bar{x}\|^{2}}+\gamma_{1}e_{\mathbb{X}}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}}^{\ast},

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(\frac{H^{\top}G\bar{x}}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}\frac{H^{\top}H\bar{y}}{\|H\bar{y}\|^{2}}+\gamma_{2}e_{\mathbb{Y}}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}^{\ast},

(21)

\displaystyle\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle=1,\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle=1.

(22)

Next we show that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
. From the orthogonality conditions (
20
) and (
21
) we have that
\gamma_{1}\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle=0
and
\gamma_{2}\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle=0
, respectively. Thus, because of (
22
) we deduce
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
. Finally, the system (
20
)-(
22
) becomes (
19
) by recalling that
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
are self-dual, and by removing w.l.o.g.
1/\textstyle{\|}G\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}
from equation (
20
) and
1/\textstyle{\|}H\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}
from equation (
21
).
∎

Theorem 2
.

If
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point (resp., solution) of the problem (
15
), then

(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\left(\frac{\bar{x}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle},\frac{\bar{y}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle}\right)

is a stationary point (resp., solution) of (
5
). Conversely, if
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
is a stationary point (resp., solution) of (
5
), then
(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=\left(\frac{\tilde{x}}{\|G\tilde{x}\|},\frac{\tilde{y}}{\|H\tilde{y}\|}\right)

is a stationary point (resp., solution) of (
15
).

Proof.

Let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
be a stationary point of (
15
). Then (
16
) holds. From Lemma
1
we have that
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle>0
and
\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle>0
. Thus, the pair
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\left(\frac{\bar{x}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle},\frac{\bar{y}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle}\right)
is well-defined. Observe that because of the unit norm conditions in (

) we get
\|G\tilde{x}\|=\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle^{-1}
and
\|H\tilde{y}\|=\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle^{-1}
. Then,
\bar{x}=\frac{\tilde{x}}{\|G\tilde{x}\|}
and
\bar{y}=\frac{\tilde{y}}{\|H\tilde{y}\|}
. By replacing these values in (
16
) we deduce that
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
satisfies (
19
). Therefore,
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
). The converse is proved analogously. In this case, when
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
),
G\tilde{x}\neq 0
and
H\tilde{y}\neq 0
thanks to Lemma
2
. Then,
(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=\left(\frac{\tilde{x}}{\|G\tilde{x}\|},\frac{\tilde{y}}{\|H\tilde{y}\|}\right)
is well-defined, and it turns out to be a stationary point of (
15
). The details are omitted.

Now, suppose that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a solution of (
15
). Let
(x,y)
be any feasible point of (
5
). Then,
\left(\frac{x}{\|Gx\|},\frac{y}{\|Hy\|}\right)
is well-defined and it is a feasible point of (
15
). Therefore,

\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle\leq\Phi(x,y),

for all feasible points
(x,y)
of (
5
). In particular,
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}):=\left(\frac{\bar{x}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle},\frac{\bar{y}}{\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle}\right)
is a feasible point of (
5
) and it attains the equality in the above displayed inequality. Thus
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
is a solution of (
5
). The converse is analogous.
∎

) and the problem of computing a critical angle between the cones

and

are made explicit in the following corollary.

.

If

is a stationary point (resp., solution) of (

), then
(u,v):=\left(\frac{G\tilde{x}}{\|G\tilde{x}\|},\frac{H\tilde{y}}{\|H\tilde{y}\|}\right)

is a critical

. Conversely, if

, then there exists a stationary point

of (

(u,v)
is given as above.

Proof.

Combine Theorem

with Proposition

.
∎

Hence, computing a critical angle between two cones
P=G(K_{\mathbb{X}})
and
Q=H(K_{\mathbb{Y}})
amounts to computing a stationary point of the fractional programming problem (
5
). The next section is dedicated to this task.

5
A sequential regularized partial linearization algorithm

In this section, we develop a numerical method to compute critical angles between a pair of LISC cones by computing stationary points of the fractional program (
5
). By using the well-known approach introduced by Dinkelbach in
[
9
]
, we consider the following parametric program, for
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
:

{\rm BCP}_{\delta}\;:\;\begin{cases}\min&f_{\delta}(x,y):=\langle Gx,Hy\rangle-\delta\left\|Gx\right\|\left\|Hy\right\|\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1,\\
&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1.\end{cases}

(23)

Remark 1
.

We use the notation
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
since that program is constituted by biconvex functions (see
[
16
]
for definitions and properties of biconvexity). Indeed,
\mathcal{B}(x,y):=\langle Gx,Hy\rangle
is biconvex because it is bilinear. Furthermore,
\mathcal{D}(x,y):=\left\|Gx\right\|\left\|Hy\right\|
is biconvex because its function at each variable is a multiple of the composition of the cartesian norm with a linear map which is a convex function.

Observe that
f_{\delta}
is differentiable at any
(x,y)
such that
Gx\neq 0
and
Hy\neq 0
. As discussed in the previous section, those conditions hold for every
(x,y)\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
. Thus,
f_{\delta}
is differentiable over the set
\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
of feasible points of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
, and the gradient is given by

\nabla f_{\delta}(x,y)=\left(\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}(x,y),\nabla_{y}f_{\delta}(x,y)\right)
, where

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}(x,y)=G^{\top}Hy-\delta\|Gx\|^{-1}\|Hy\|G^{\top}Gx,

(24a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}f_{\delta}(x,y)=H^{\top}Gx-\delta\|Hy\|^{-1}\|Gx\|H^{\top}Hy.

(24b)

The next theorem states that a stationary point of (
5
) can be computed as a stationary point of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
where
f_{\delta}
vanishes.

Theorem 3
.

The pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point (resp., solution) of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) with parameter
\delta:=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
if and only if
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point (resp., solution) of
FP
(
5
).

Proof.

Let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
be a stationary point of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
). From Definition
2
and because
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
are self-dual, we get

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(G^{\top}H\bar{y}-\delta\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}\|H\bar{y}\|G^{\top}G\bar{x}+\gamma_{1}e_{\mathbb{X}}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\\
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(H^{\top}G\bar{x}-\delta\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}\|G\bar{x}\|H^{\top}H\bar{y}+\gamma_{1}e_{\mathbb{Y}}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\\
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle=1,\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle=1.\end{array}\right.

From the orthogonality conditions above we deduce that

0=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle-\delta\textstyle{\|}H\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}\textstyle{\|}G\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}+\gamma_{1}

and
0=\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle-\delta\textstyle{\|}H\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}\textstyle{\|}G\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}+\gamma_{2}
. The definition of
\delta
then implies that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0
. The above system then becomes (with
\delta=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}K_{\mathbb{X}}\ni\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(G^{\top}H\bar{y}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|^{2}}G^{\top}G\bar{x}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\\
K_{\mathbb{Y}}\ni\bar{y}\;\perp\;\left(H^{\top}G\bar{x}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|^{2}}H^{\top}H\bar{y}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\\
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},\bar{x}\rangle=1,\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{y}\rangle=1.\end{array}\right.

As
\bar{x}\;\perp\;\left(G^{\top}H\bar{y}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|^{2}}G^{\top}G\bar{x}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}}
, it is equivalent to

\bar{x}\;\perp\;\frac{1}{\textstyle{\|}H\bar{y}\textstyle{\|}}\left(G^{\top}H\bar{y}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|^{2}}G^{\top}G\bar{x}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{X}}.

Analogously, we have
\bar{y}\;\perp\;\frac{1}{\textstyle{\|}G\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}}\left(H^{\top}G\bar{x}-\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|^{2}}H^{\top}H\bar{y}\right)\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}
. Then, we can deduce that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) with
\delta=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
if and only if
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
satisfies (
19
). Thus, the equivalence follows. Suppose that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a solution of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) with
\delta=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
. Then,
f_{\delta}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=0
. Furthermore, for any feasible point
(x,y)
of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) we get
f_{\delta}(x,y)\geq 0
, which means

\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\delta\leq\Phi(x,y).

(25)

Then, since problems
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) and FP (
5
) have the same feasible sets, we conclude from (
25
) that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a solution of FP (
5
). The converse follows the same steps but in reverse order.

∎

Corollary 3
.

If
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point (resp., solution) of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) with parameter
\delta:=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
, then
(u,v):=\left(\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}G\bar{x},\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}H\bar{y}\right)
is a critical (resp., antipodal) pair of
(P,Q)
. Conversely, if
(u,v)
is a critical (resp., antipodal) pair of
(P,Q)
, then there exists a stationary point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
of
{\rm BCP}_{\delta}
(
23
) with
\delta:=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\left\|G\bar{x}\right\|\left\|H\bar{y}\right\|}
and such that
(u,v)=\left(\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}G\bar{x},\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}H\bar{y}\right)
.

Proof.

It follows directly from Theorem
3
and Corollary
2
.
∎

Next, we formulate our algorithm to compute critical angles between a pair of LISC cones
(P,Q)=(G(K_{\mathbb{X}}),\,H(K_{\mathbb{Y}})).

Algorithm 1

Sequential Regularized Partial Linearization

1:

\triangleright\;

Step
0

2:

Choose
(x^{0},y^{0})\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
,
\beta>0
,
0<\alpha<1
,
0<\rho<1
, and prox-parameters
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\geq 0
.

3:

Set
k:=0
.

4:

\triangleright\;

Step
1

5:

Set

\delta_{k}:=\frac{\langle Gx^{k},Hy^{k}\rangle}{\|Gx^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|}.

6:

\triangleright\;

Step
2

7:

Let
L^{k}_{1}(x):=\left\langle Gx,Hy^{k}-\delta_{k}\|Gx^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|Gx^{k}\right\rangle
.

Compute a solution
\tilde{x}^{k}
to the convex program

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&L^{k}_{1}(x)+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\\
s.t.&x\in K_{\mathbb{X}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1.\end{array}

(26)

8:

Let
L^{k}_{2}(y):=\left\langle Hy,Gx^{k}-\delta_{k}\|Gx^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|^{-1}Hy^{k}\right\rangle
.

Compute a solution
\tilde{y}^{k}
to the convex program

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&L^{k}_{2}(y)+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\\
s.t.&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1.\end{array}

(27)

9:

Let
d_{1}^{k}:=\tilde{x}^{k}-x^{k}
and
d_{2}^{k}:=\tilde{y}^{k}-y^{k}
.

10:

\triangleright\;

Step
3

11:

If
L^{k}_{1}(d_{1}^{k})=0
and
L^{k}_{2}(d_{2}^{k})=0
, terminate.

Otherwise, let
t_{k}:=\beta\rho^{\ell_{k}}
, where
\ell_{k}
is the smallest nonnegative integer
\ell
such that

\Phi(x^{k}+t^{k}d^{k}_{1},y^{k}+t^{k}d^{k}_{2})\leq\Phi(x^{k},y^{k})+\alpha t_{k}\frac{L^{k}_{1}(d_{1}^{k})+L^{k}_{2}(d_{2}^{k})}{\|Gx^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|}

\left(x^{k+1},y^{k+1}\right):=(x^{k},y^{k})+t_{k}(d^{k}_{1},d^{k}_{2})
. Go to Step 1.

Some comments on Algorithm
1
are in order.

i)

The prox-parameters
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
can take arbitrary non-negative values. The choice
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
is covered by the convergence analysis given below because
\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
of (
5
) is compact (see Proposition
1
). However, our numerical experiments suggest that choosing the prox-parameters to be strictly positive is advantageous: it regularizes the iterative process in the sense that the algorithm performs fewer iterations to compute a stationary point within a given tolerance.

ii)

We highlight that Step 2 can be performed in parallel as the two subproblems are independent of each other.

iii)

For LISC cones, the interest of dealing with (
5
) instead of (
3
) is now evident: the cones
K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
K_{\mathbb{Y}}
are in general more structured, permitting (
26
) and (
27
) to have straightforward solutions in many settings of interest (c.f. Subsection
3.2
). If the considered cones are not LISC, then
G
and
H
are the identities operators in the corresponding spaces
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
, and thus
P=K_{\mathbb{X}}
and
Q=K_{\mathbb{Y}}
. Algorithm
1
is still applicable: for applications in which the subproblems do not have explicit solutions, we can use of off-the-shelf (linear, quadratic, conic, etc.) solvers to compute
\tilde{x}^{k}
and
\tilde{y}^{k}
.

iv)

In Step 3, we perform the standard Armijo line search rule. Indeed, the term
\frac{L^{k}_{1}(d_{1}^{k})+L^{k}_{2}(d_{2}^{k})}{\|Gx^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|}
coincides with
\left\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle
. As we show in Proposition
4
below,
d^{k}
is a descend direction for
\Phi
at
(x^{k},y^{k})
.

Lemma 5
.

We have that

L_{1}^{k}(d_{1}^{k})\leq-\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|d_{1}^{k}\|^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{2}^{k}(d_{2}^{k})\leq-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|d_{2}^{k}\|^{2}.

(28)

Furthermore, if
x^{k}
is not a solution to (
26
) or
y^{k}
is not a solution to (
27
), then

L_{1}^{k}(d_{1}^{k})+L_{2}^{k}(d_{2}^{k})<0.

(29)

Proof.

Since
\tilde{x}^{k}
solves (
26
) and
x^{k}
is feasible, we have that
L^{k}_{1}\left(\tilde{x}^{k}\right)+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|\tilde{x}^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}\leq L^{k}_{1}\left(x^{k}\right)
, with strict inequality if
x^{k}
is not a solution to (
26
).
As
L_{1}^{k}\left(d_{1}^{k}\right)=L^{k}_{1}\left(\tilde{x}^{k}\right)-L^{k}_{1}\left(x^{k}\right)
, we conclude that
L_{1}^{k}\left(d_{1}^{k}\right)\leq-\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|d_{1}^{k}\|^{2}
. Analogously, we get
L_{2}^{k}(d_{2}^{k})\leq-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|d_{2}^{k}\|^{2}
. Suppose that
x^{k}
is not a solution to (
26
) or
y^{k}
is not a solution to (
27
). Then, one of the inequalities in (
28
) holds strictly. It implies,

L_{1}^{k}(d_{1}^{k})+L_{2}^{k}(d_{2}^{k})<-\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|d_{1}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|d_{2}^{k}\|^{2}\leq 0.

∎

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
x^{k}
is not a solution to (
26
) or
y^{k}
is not a solution to (

). Then, the vector
\left(d_{1}^{k},d_{2}^{k}\right)
is a descent direction for the fractional function
\displaystyle\Phi
(given in (

)) at
(x^{k},y^{k})
.

Proof.

We must prove that

\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),(d_{1}^{k},d_{2}^{k})\rangle=\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{1}^{k}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{2}^{k}\rangle<0.

), we obtain,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{H^{\top}Gx^{k}}{\|Gx^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|}-\delta_{k}\frac{H^{\top}Hy^{k}}{\|Hy^{k}\|^{2}}

Then,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{1}^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|Gx^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|^{-1}L^{k}_{1}(d_{1}^{k}),

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{2}^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\|Gx^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|^{-1}L^{k}_{2}(d_{2}^{k}).

Since
x^{k}
is not a solution to (

) or

is not a solution to (

), we can use (

) to conclude (

).

Proposition 6
.

Let

(31)

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\left\langle Hy,G\bar{x}-\bar{\delta}\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}H\bar{y}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}\\
s.t.&y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}},\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1.\end{array}

(32)

Proof.

The pair
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
) if, and only if,
\langle\nabla f_{\bar{\delta}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),((x,y)-(\bar{x},\bar{y})\rangle\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
, which is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\langle\nabla_{x}f_{\bar{\delta}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),x-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}\;\mbox{ s.t. }\;\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1\\
\langle\nabla_{y}f_{\bar{\delta}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),y-\bar{y}\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall y\in K_{\mathbb{Y}}\;\mbox{ s.t. }\;\langle e_{\mathbb{Y}},y\rangle=1.\end{array}\right.

In view of (
24a
) and (
24b
), the above system is equivalent to saying that
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
are solutions to (
31
) and (
32
), respectively.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Every accumulation point
\left(\bar{x},\bar{y}\right)
of the sequence
\left\{\left(x^{k},y^{k}\right)\right\}
, generated by Algorithm
1
, is a stationary point of problem (
5
).

Proof.

Let
\left(\bar{x},\bar{y}\right)
be an accumulation point of
\left\{\left(x^{k},y^{k}\right)\right\}
(which is assured to exist because
\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
is compact, see Proposition
1
). By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may suppose that
(x^{k},y^{k})\to(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and
(\tilde{x}^{k},\tilde{y}^{k})\to(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
, as
k\to\infty
, where
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\in\Delta_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}
. Let
x\in K_{\mathbb{X}}
s.t.
\langle e_{\mathbb{X}},x\rangle=1
be arbitrary. Because
\tilde{x}^{k}
solves (
26
), we have that

\displaystyle\left\langle G\tilde{x}^{k},Hy^{k}-\delta_{k}\|Gx^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|Gx^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\textstyle{\|}\tilde{x}^{k}-x^{k}\textstyle{\|}^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\langle Gx,Hy^{k}-\delta_{k}\|Gx^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|Gx^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\textstyle{\|}x-x^{k}\textstyle{\|}^{2}.

By letting
k\to\infty
on (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle G\tilde{x},H\bar{y}-\bar{\delta}\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}\|H\bar{y}\|G\bar{x}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\textstyle{\|}\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\langle Gx,H\bar{y}-\bar{\delta}\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}\|H\bar{y}\|G\bar{x}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\textstyle{\|}x-\bar{x}\textstyle{\|}^{2}.

where

\bar{\delta}:=\frac{\langle G\bar{x},H\bar{y}\rangle}{\|G\bar{x}\|\|H\bar{y}\|}.

Thus, we deduce that
\tilde{x}
is a solution to (
31
).
On the other hand, from standard arguments on the Armijo rule we have that
\left\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),(d_{1}^{k},d_{2}^{k})\right\rangle\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. In Proposition
4
, we have shown that
\left\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{1}^{k}\right\rangle\leq 0
and
\left\langle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{2}^{k}\right\rangle\leq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{1}^{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle=0,

(34)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle\nabla_{y}\Phi(x^{k},y^{k}),d_{2}^{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla_{y}\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\tilde{y}-\bar{y}\right\rangle=0.

(35)

Since
\left\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\tilde{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle=\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}\bar{L}_{1}(\tilde{x}-\bar{x})
, from (
34
) we conclude

\bar{L}_{1}(\tilde{x})-\bar{L}_{1}(\bar{x})=\bar{L}_{1}(\tilde{x}-\bar{x})=0.

Thus, from Proposition
5
we conclude that not only
\tilde{x}
but also
\bar{x}
is solution of (
31
). In the same way, we can prove that both
\tilde{y}
and
\bar{y}
are solutions of (
32
). (We highlight that if
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
in Algorithm
1
, then
\bar{x}=\tilde{x}
and
\bar{y}=\tilde{y}
due to strongly convexity of the objective functions.)
Therefore, from Proposition
6
we conclude that
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a stationary point of (
5
).
∎

Finally, it follows from Theorem
4
and Corollary
3
that
Algorithm
1
asymptotically computes a critical pair

(u,v)=\left(\|G\bar{x}\|^{-1}G\bar{x},\|H\bar{y}\|^{-1}H\bar{y}\right)
of
(P,Q)
.

6.3
Critical angles between the SDP cone and the cone of nonnegative matrices

Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{n}
is the space of symmetric matrices of order
n
equipped with the trace inner product
\langle A,B\rangle={\rm Tr}(AB)
. Furthermore,

\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the SDP cone, and
\mathcal{N}_{n}
denotes the cone of nonnegative matrices in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{A\in\mathcal{S}^{n}\,:\,x^{\top}Ax\geq 0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}=\{B\in\mathcal{S}^{n}\,:\,\mbox{$B$ is nonnegative entrywise}\}.

Let
\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})
denote the maximal angle between
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}
. The computation of
\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})
was exhaustively treated in
[
15
]
and
[
33
]
. It is known that

\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})=(3/4)\pi,\mbox{ for }n=2,3,4,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})=\pi.

The exact computation of
\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})
for
n\geq 5
becomes difficult, and only lower bounds for certain values of
n
are known. As it is discussed in
[
18
]
, a motivation to compute
\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})
is that it provides a lower bound for the greatest possible angle between two matrices in the copositive cone
\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\{A\in\mathcal{S}^{n}\,:\,x^{\top}Ax\geq 0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\}
, cf.
[
15
]
. Our goal in this section is to use Algorithm
1
to improve the knowledge of these lower bounds.

It is clear that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a LISC cone since it is the symmetric cone of the algebra
\mathcal{S}^{n}
. In this case, its representation is

\mathcal{P}_{n}=G(\mathcal{P}_{n})

with
G:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathcal{S}^{n}
the identity map.

The cone
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is also LISC. Indeed, it can be expressed as

\mathcal{N}_{n}=H(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}),

where
N=n(n+1)/2
and
H:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathcal{S}^{n}
is the linear map constructed as follows: Let
\mathcal{E}:=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}\}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let
\{E_{i,j}\}_{i\leq i\leq j}
be the canonical basis of
\mathcal{S}^{n}
. That is,
E_{i,j}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
is the matrix whose entries are zeros except the entries corresponding to
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
in which take values equal to one. It is clear that
H
is completely defined by knowing the image of each element of its basis
\mathcal{E}
. In this way, we take the following assignment: for
k=1,\ldots,N
,

H(e_{k})=E_{i_{k},j_{k}},

where
i_{k}:=2k-\ell_{k}(\ell_{k}-1)/2
and
j_{k}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\{\ell(\ell+1)/2:\ell(\ell+1)/2\geq k\}.
Hence, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the value of
H(y)
is nothing but

H(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}y_{k}E_{i_{k},j_{k}}.

The mapping
H
is invertible since it maps the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
onto the canonical basis of
\mathcal{S}^{n}
. Hence, assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
are satisfied and
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is a LISC cone.

In the implementation of Algorithm
1
, we also need to know the map
H^{T}:\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
(the adjoint map of
H
). It is not difficult to see that it is given by

H^{T}(X)=(\langle X,E_{i_{1},j_{1}}\rangle,\ldots,\langle X,E_{i_{N},j_{N}}\rangle)^{T}.

In practice, the numerical evaluation of
H(y)
and
H^{T}(X)
is easy. To illustrate it, consider
n=3
. Then
N=6
and we have:

H\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\\
y_{3}\\
y_{4}\\
y_{5}\\
y_{6}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}&y_{4}\\
y_{2}&y_{3}&y_{5}\\
y_{4}&y_{5}&y_{6}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad H^{T}\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&x_{13}\\
x_{12}&x_{22}&x_{23}\\
x_{13}&x_{23}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}\\
2x_{12}\\
x_{22}\\
2x_{13}\\
2x_{23}\\
x_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, we are ready to implement Algorithm
1
to estimate
\Theta(\mathcal{P}_{n},\mathcal{N}_{n})
. Algorithm
1
generates the points
(X^{k},y^{k})
. Recall that
X^{k}
is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
y^{k}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In Step
2
of Algorithm
1
, for
\mu_{1}>0
, problem (
26
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|X-B^{k}\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&X\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\\
&{\rm Tr}(X)=1,\end{array}

where
B^{k}:=X^{k}-(1/\mu_{1})(Hy^{k}-\delta_{k}\|X^{k}\|^{-1}\|Hy^{k}\|X^{k})
. If
B^{k}
has the spectral decomposition, the solution to the above problem is
X^{k}=Q{\rm Diag}(\lambda(B^{k})Q^{T}
, with
Q
being some orthogonal matrix of order
n
. From Example
3
we have that
X^{k}=Q{\rm Diag}(\bar{x})Q^{T}
, where
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the solution of

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x-\lambda(B^{k})\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
&\langle{\bf 1}_{n},x\rangle=1.\end{array}

When
\mu_{2}>0
, problem (
27
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|y-b^{k}\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+},\\
&\langle{\bf 1}_{N},y\rangle=1,\end{array}

where
b^{k}:=y^{k}-(1/\mu_{2})H^{T}(X^{k}-\delta_{k}\|X^{k}\|\|Hy^{k}\|^{-1}Hy^{k})
.

The tolerances for the algorithm stopping test are
\epsilon_{1}=10^{-6}
,
\epsilon_{2}=10^{-6}
and
\epsilon_{3}=10^{-7}
.
We consider a maximum number of iterations equal to
5000
, and the value of the prox-parameters are chosen as
\mu_{1}=0.01
and
\mu_{2}=5
. For the initial point
(X^{0},y^{0})
, we set as
X^{0}=Diag(x^{0})
and, we generated
x^{0}
and
y^{0}
as two random points in the simplex. The largest dimension for the instances is
60
, because the subproblems to be solved at each iteration are more complex. In particular, the first requires the spectral decomposition of a matrix and a subsequent projection on the
n
-dimensional standard simplex. The second subproblem also consists in projecting on the standard simplex, but this time the dimension is much higher, that is
N=n(n+1)/2
(e.g., with
n=60
,
N=1830
).

Conclusions

In this work, we have investigated the problem of computing critical angles between pairs of convex cones in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
a) we have shown that the notion of critical angles considered in the literature is equivalent to that of (KKT) stationarity for a class of nonlinear optimization problems;
b) we have posed the problem in the fractional programming (FP) setting and proposed a specialized algorithm to compute stationary points, yielding thus critical angles to our original problem;
c) we have further specialized our analysis to the broad class of (LISC) convex cones derived as linear images of symmetric cones. Moreover, we have demonstrated that our FP formulation is well-defined and suitable for numerical optimization.

Our algorithm is the first numerical procedure for computing critical angles between pairs of convex cones. The approach requires solving two independent subproblems at every iteration to compute (with the aid of the Armijo line search) a new iterate. For the class of LISC cones, we have shown that solutions to such subproblems are projections onto well-structured convex sets. Otherwise, for more general cones, we can still apply off-the-shelf (linear, quadratic, conic, etc.) convex solvers to solve such subproblems. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that our approach asymptotically computes critical angles regardless of initialization.

Numerical experiments on different types of convex cones (polyhedral, ellipsoidal, and Loewnerian cones) in several dimensions illustrate the practical performance of our algorithm. Comparison with
IPOPT
have shown that exploiting the problem’s structure as done by our approach pays off: for the considered test problems, our algorithm is up to one hundred times faster than
IPOPT
while maintaining (sometimes even improving) the quality of computed critical angles.
