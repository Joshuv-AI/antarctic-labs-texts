1.
Introduction

1.1.
Set-up

Motivated by applications to the dynamical Mordell-Lang conjecture (for more details on this open problem in arithmetic dynamics, we refer the reader to
[
BGT16
]
), the authors
[
GOSS21
]
have recently
considered the question about representations of values of
polynomials
Q\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
as fixed linear combinations of powers of a prime
p
.
In particular, it is shown in
[
GOSS21
]
that for fixed coefficients
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}

and integral exponents
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}

the number of positive integers
n\leqslant N
for which
Q(n)
can be represented as

Q(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}p^{a_{i}k_{i}}

with some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is bounded by
O\left(\left(1+\log N\right)^{m}\right)

where the implied constant depends only on the initial data.
In fact it is easy to see that for
Q(n)=n
, this bound is tight. Furthermore,
a similar result is given in
[
GOSS21
]
for representations of the form

(1.1)

Q(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{i,j}\lambda_{j}^{a_{i}k_{i}},

with algebraic integers

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}
, each one of them of absolute value equal to
q
or

\sqrt{q}
(where
q
is a given power of a prime number).

Here we first consider representations of the form (
1.1
)
with
s=1
but arbitrary complex (rather than algebraic) parameters. We also generalise
this to approximations of polynomials rather than precise equalities, that is,
we consider inequalities of the form

(1.2)

\left|Q(n)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\lambda^{k_{i}}\right|\leqslant B,

with
Q\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
,
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m},\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and some
B\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

1.2.
Notation

We now recall that the notations
A=O(B)
,
A\ll B
and
B\geqslant A

are all equivalent to the inequality
|A|\leqslant cB
with some constant
c
.
Throughout this work all implied constants may depend on the polynomial
Q
and the
parameters
c_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
and
\lambda
in (
1.2
) and
also on
g
in (
1.4
) below.

For a finite set
{\mathscr{S}}
we use
\#{\mathscr{S}}
to denote its cardinality.

1.3.
New results

We remark that the argument of
[
GOSS21
]
is based on a result of
Laurent
[
Lau84
, Théorème 6]
, which required all parameters to be
defined over a number field; furthermore, the result of
[
Lau84
]
refers to equalities, not inequalities. Hence here we use a different approach to establish the
following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m},\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
B\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let
Q\in{\mathbb{C}}[X]
be a non-constant polynomial.
Then for
N\geqslant 2
we have

\#\{n\leqslant N:\penalty\ \text{(\ref{eq:QciglambdaB}\hbox{}) holds for some}\ k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\ll(\log N)^{m}.

We observe that the implied constant in Theorem
1.1
is effectively computable
in terms of the sizes of the initial data,
while in the result of
[
GOSS21
]
it is not.

We also note (see Example
2.1
) that if one considers inequalities of the form

(1.3)

\left|Q(n)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{i,j}\lambda_{j}^{a_{i}k_{i}}\right|\leqslant B,

for some arbitrary complex numbers
\lambda_{j}
, then one cannot expect a similar result as in Theorem
1.1
. More precisely, there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
each
sufficiently large integer
n
, there exists some positive integer
k_{n}
with the property that

\left|n-\frac{i}{\pi}\cdot\left(2^{k_{n}}-\lambda^{k_{n}}\right)\right|\leqslant\varepsilon,

see Example
2.1
for more details.

Furthermore, we consider the case of perfect squares and study relations of the form

(1.4)

n^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g^{k_{i}},

with non-zero integer coefficients
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
and an integer basis

g\geqslant 2
. Using the square-sieve of Heath-Brown
[
H-B84
]

we improve the exponent
m
of
\log N
.

For
m=2
, we write the equation (
1.4
) as
n^{2}=g^{k_{1}}\left(c_{1}+c_{2}g^{k_{2}-k_{1}}\right)

(with
k_{2}\geqslant k_{1}
).
Hence either
c_{1}+c_{2}g^{k_{2}-k_{1}}
or
c_{1}g+c_{2}g^{k_{2}-k_{1}+1}
is a
perfect square
j^{2}
for some
j\leqslant n\leqslant N
. Since the largest prime divisor of
j^{2}+c

for any
c\neq 0
tends to infinity with
j
, see
[
Kea69
]
, we see that

k_{2}-k_{1}
can take only finitely many values. Hence for
m=2
we have
O(\log N)
solution
to (
1.4
) with
n\leqslant N
.
This bound is obviously the
best possible as the example of the numbers
2^{k_{1}}+2^{k_{2}}
, with
k_{2}=k_{1}+3
and even

k_{1}
, shows. We also note that in
[
CGSZ
, Theorem 5.1 (B)]
, it is established even more generally the
precise
set of all positive integers
n
for which
u_{n}
is of the form
c_{1}g^{k_{1}}+c_{2}g^{k_{2}}
(for some given
g
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
), where
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is an arbitrary linear recurrence sequence (the result of
[
CGSZ
, Theorem 5.1 (B)]
is stated only when
g=p
is a prime number, but as remarked in
[
CGSZ
, Section 5]
, the method extends verbatim to an arbitrary integer
g
).

So we are mostly interested in the case of
m\geqslant 3
; furthermore, we note that for
m=3
(and in some cases, depending on
g
and the
c_{i}
, even for
m=4
), more precise results are available in the literature (see
[
CoZa13
]
). However, when
m\geqslant 5
, it is very difficult to find a precise description of all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n^{2}
is of the form (
1.4
) (for some given integers
g
and
c_{i}
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m\geqslant 3
and let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
and

g\geqslant 2
be integers.
Then for
N\geqslant 2
we have

\#\{n\leqslant N:\penalty\ \text{(\ref{eq:Qcig}\hbox{}) holds for some}\ k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\leqslant(\log N)^{m-\gamma_{m}+o(1)},

where

\gamma_{3}=\frac{677}{1969}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{m}=\frac{677m}{1323m+1354}\quad\text{for}\ m\geqslant 4.

We observe that
\gamma_{m}\to 677/1323=0.5117\ldots
as
m\to\infty
. Thus for large

m
Theorem
1.2
saves more than
1/2
compared to the general bound of Theorem
1.1
.
More precisely, simple calculations show that
\gamma_{m}>1/2
for
m\geqslant 44
.

We remark that the proof of Theorem
1.2
is based on some ideas and results
from
[
LuSh09
]
, later enhanced in
[
BaSh17
]
. The numerical constants come from the work
of Baker and Harman
[
BaHa98
]
on large prime divisors of shifted primes.

Furthermore, as in
[
BaSh17
]
we observe that under the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis
we can obtain a slightly larger value of
\gamma_{m}
.

On the other hand defining
s
as the largest integer with
s(s+1)/2\leqslant m
and considering numbers

\left(g^{h_{1}}+\ldots+g^{h_{s}}\right)^{2}
with

h_{i}\leqslant\frac{\log\left(N/s^{2}\right)}{2\log g},\quad i=1,\ldots,s,

we see that for at least one choice
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}>0
with
t\leqslant m

and
c_{1}+\ldots+c_{t}\leqslant s(s+1)/2
occurs at least
C_{0}(\log N)^{s}
times,
for some constant
C_{0}>0
, which shows that the best possible exponent in
any result of the type of Theorem
1.2
must grow with
m
(at least as
about
\sqrt{2m}
for large
m
).

Note that cycling over all
g^{m}
choices of

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\in\{0,\ldots,g-1\}^{m}

we obtain from Theorem
1.2
a result about the sparsity of the
values of
n
for which
n^{2}
has at most
m
non-zero digits to base
g
.
Various finiteness results on sparse digital representations of perfect powers
can be found in
[
BeBu14
,
BBM13
,
CoZa13
,
Mos21
]
.
Note that as we have just seen, in our setting of arbitrary
m

no finiteness result is possible, and hence we can use Theorem
1.2

to provide a counting result related to
such representations. More precisely we have the following straightforward consequence:

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
m\geqslant 3
and let
K\geqslant 1
and
g\geqslant 2
be integers. Then there are at most
K^{m-\gamma_{m}+o(1)}

integer squares with
g
-ary expansion of length
K
and with at most
m
non-zero digits.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1.
Counterexample to a possible extension to (
1.3
)

Before proceeding to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we provide the Example
2.1
(mentioned in Section
1.3
), which shows that one cannot expect to generalise Theorem
1.1

to (
1.3
), that is, to the case when we approximate
Q(n)
with a sum of powers of different
\lambda_{j}
.

Example 2.1
.

We consider the sequence of positive integers
\{b_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 2}
given by

b_{2}=2\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad b_{j+1}=2^{b_{j}}+b_{j}+1\text{ for }j\geqslant 2.

We let
\lambda=2\cdot e^{2\pi\alpha i}
, where

\alpha=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{j-1}{2^{b_{j}}}.

We let
n
be a positive integer and show that

(2.1)

n-\frac{i}{\pi}\cdot\left(2^{2^{b_{n}}}-\lambda^{2^{b_{n}}}\right)\ll\frac{n}{b_{n+1}}.

Indeed, we first notice that

\lambda^{2^{b_{n}}}=2^{2^{b_{n}}}\cdot e^{2\pi t_{n}i},

where

t_{n}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{j-1}{2^{b_{j}-b_{n}}}.

Then

\displaystyle 2^{2^{b_{n}}}-\lambda^{2^{b_{n}}}

\displaystyle=2^{2^{b_{n}}}\cdot\left(\left(1-\cos(2\pi t_{n})\right)-i\sin\left(2\pi t_{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2^{b_{n}}}\cdot 2\sin(\pi t_{n})\cdot\left(\sin(\pi t_{n})-i\cos(\pi t_{n})\right),

and so,

(2.2)

\frac{i}{\pi}\cdot\left(2^{2^{b_{n}}}-\lambda^{2^{b_{n}}}\right)=\frac{2^{1+2^{b_{n}}}\sin(\pi t_{n})}{\pi}\cdot e^{\pi t_{n}i}.

Now, by the definition of the rapidly increasing sequence
\{b_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 2}
, we have that

(2.3)

0<t_{n}-\frac{n}{2^{b_{n+1}-b_{n}}}<\frac{1}{2^{2^{b_{n+1}}}};

also, clearly,
t_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, we know that when
t
is close to
0
, then

(2.4)

|\sin t-t|\leqslant t^{2}.

So, using the inequalities (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), along with the fact that
b_{n+1}=2^{b_{n}}+b_{n}+1
, we get that

(2.5)

\left|n-\frac{2^{1+2^{b_{n}}}\sin\left(\pi t_{n}\right)}{\pi}\right|<2^{-b_{n}}\ll 1/b_{n+1}

for all
n
sufficiently large. Also, for
n
large, using the inequality (
2.3
) we have that

(2.6)

\left|1-e^{\pi t_{n}i}\right|<2^{-b_{n}}\ll 1/b_{n+1}.

Recalling (
2.2
) and using (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we derive (
2.1
).

Therefore, the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
cannot be generalised to inequalities (
1.3
) where we approximate a polynomial
Q(n)
with sums of powers of different
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}
.

Next we proceed to proving Theorem
1.1
.

2.2.
Preliminaries

We first note that if
|\lambda|=1
, then the inequality (
1.2
) yields that
|Q(n)|
is uniformly bounded above and therefore, we can only have finitely many
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying such inequality since
Q
is a non-constant polynomial. Furthermore, since the exponents
k_{i}
appearing in the inequality (
1.2
) are arbitrary integers, then without loss of generality, we may assume from now on that
|\lambda|>1
.

Now, if some of the exponents
k_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, from (
1.2
) were non-positive, then the absolute value of the corresponding terms
c_{i}\lambda^{k_{i}}
is uniformly bounded above. So, at the expense of replacing
B
by a larger constant (but depending only on the absolute values of the
c_{i}
), we may assume from now on, that each exponent
k_{i}
from (
1.2
) is positive.

Let
Q(X)=a_{d}X^{d}+\cdots+a_{1}X+a_{0}
for complex numbers
a_{0},\ldots,a_{d}
with
a_{d}\neq 0
.
There exists
N_{0}\geqslant 0
(depending only on
d
and the absolute values of the coefficients of
Q
) such that

(2.7)

|Q(n)|\leqslant 2\left|a_{d}n^{d}\right|\leqslant N_{0}\cdot n^{d}\text{ for each }n\geqslant N_{0}.

Furthermore, at the expense of replacing
N_{0}
by a larger positive integer (but still depending only on
d
, the absolute values of the coefficients of
Q
and also depending on
B
in this case), we may also assume that

(2.8)

|Q(n_{1}+n_{2})-Q(n_{1})|>2B\text{ for each }n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant N_{0}.

2.3.
Induction

We proceed to prove our desired result by induction on
m
.

We prove first the base case
m=1
, which also constitutes the inspiration for our proof for the general case in Theorem
1.1
. So, we have that
|Q(n)|=O(N^{d})
for each
0\leqslant n\leqslant N
(see also the inequality (
2.7
)). Therefore, for
n\leqslant N
satisfying the inequality

(2.9)

\left|Q(n)-c_{1}\lambda^{k_{1}}\right|\leqslant B

one has
|\lambda|^{k_{1}}=O\left(N^{d}\right)
and thus,
k_{1}=O\left(\log N\right)
. On the other hand, for a given
k_{1}
, the inequality (
2.9
) is satisfied by
O(1)
non-negative integers
n
(see also (
2.8
)), thus proving the desired bound in the case
m=1
.

So, suppose that the result is true for
m\leqslant s
and we prove that Theorem
1.1
holds when
m=s+1
; clearly we may assume each
c_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,s+1
is nonzero. Since there are
m
powers of
\lambda
in the inequality (
1.2
), then in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to prove that the set
{\mathscr{S}}_{0}
, consisting of all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which there exist integers

(2.10)

1\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant k_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{s+1}

such that

(2.11)

\left|Q(n)-\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}c_{j}\lambda^{k_{j}}\right|\leqslant B

satisfies

(2.12)

\left\{n\in{\mathscr{S}}_{0}:\penalty\ n\leqslant N\right\}\ll\left(\log N\right)^{s+1}.

Let
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
be sufficiently large (but depending only on
|\lambda|
, which is larger than
1
, and also depending on the absolute values of the
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s+1}
) such that we have

(2.13)

\frac{|c_{s+1}|}{2}\cdot|\lambda|^{k_{s+1}}\leqslant\left|\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}c_{j}\lambda^{k_{j}}\right|,

for all integers
k_{s+1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant k_{1}\geqslant 0
satisfying the
inequality (
2.11
) along with the inequality
k_{s+1}-k_{s}\geqslant\Delta
.

Now, we let
{\mathscr{U}}
be the subset of
{\mathscr{S}}_{0}
consisting of integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which one can find integers
k_{j}
satisfying (
2.11
) and in addition,
k_{s+1}-k_{s}<\Delta
. Then the existence of such a solution tuple
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s+1})
for each
n\in{\mathscr{U}}
means that

\left|Q(n)-\left(c_{1}\lambda^{k_{1}}+\cdots+c_{s-1}\lambda^{k_{s-1}}+\left(c_{s}+c_{s+1}\lambda^{k_{s+1}-k_{s}}\right)\lambda^{k_{s}}\right)\right|\leqslant B.

Because
k_{s+1}-k_{s}\in\{0,\ldots,\Delta-1\}
, applying the induction hypothesis for each of the possible
\Delta
values of
k_{s+1}-k_{s}
, we obtain the desired conclusion regarding the asymptotic growth given by (
2.12
) (furthermore, we actually get that the exponent from the right-hand side of the inequality (
2.12
) is
s=m-1
not
s+1=m
).

On the other hand, for each
n\in{\mathscr{S}}_{0}\setminus{\mathscr{U}}
satisfying
n\geqslant N_{0}
, we know that there must exist some tuple of nonnegative integers
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s+1})
satisfying (
2.11
) and in addition,
k_{s+1}-k_{s}\geqslant\Delta
. Then using both (
2.7
)
and (
2.13
), we get

\frac{|c_{s+1}|}{2}\cdot|\lambda|^{k_{s+1}}-B\leqslant\left|c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}c_{j}\lambda^{k_{j}}\right|-B\leqslant|Q(n)|\leqslant 2|a_{d}|\cdot n^{d},

which implies that

(2.14)

k_{s+1}\leqslant b_{0}\left(1+\log n\right)

for some positive real number
b_{0}
depending only on
B
,
|\lambda|
,
d
,
|a_{d}|
and
|c_{s+1}|
.

So, let
N
be an integer larger than
N_{0}
; then for each integer
N_{0}\leqslant n\leqslant N
contained in
{\mathscr{S}}_{0}\setminus{\mathscr{U}}
, we know there exists an
(s+1)
-tuple of integers
k_{i}
satisfying (
2.10
) and (
2.11
). Combining the fact that
1\leqslant k_{i}\leqslant k_{s+1}
with the inequality (
2.14
), we get that there are at most
\left(b_{0}\left(1+\log N\right)\right)^{s+1}
tuples
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s+1})
for which we could find some
n\in{\mathscr{S}}_{0}\setminus{\mathscr{U}}
satisfying the inequality
N_{0}\leqslant n\leqslant N
. However, since
n\geqslant N_{0}
, then the inequality (
2.8
) yields that for any such
(s+1)
-tuple of integers
k_{i}
, there are
at most

N_{0}
integers
n\in\left({\mathscr{S}}_{0}\setminus{\mathscr{U}}\right)\cap[N_{0},N]
satisfying (
2.11
) with respect to the tuple
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s+1})
. Hence, we get the inequality

\#\left\{n\leqslant N\colon\penalty\ n\in{\mathscr{S}}_{0}\setminus{\mathscr{U}}\right\}\leqslant N_{0}\cdot\left(1+\left(b_{0}\cdot\left(1+\log N\right)\right)^{s+1}\right).

for each positive integer
N\geqslant N_{0}
. This concludes our proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

3.
Construction and properties of the sieving set of primes

3.1.
Multiplicative orders

Let
\tau_{\ell}(g)
denote
the multiplicative order of an integer
g\geqslant 2
modulo a prime
\ell
, that is,
the smallest positive integer
\tau
for which
g^{\tau}\equiv 1\bmod\ell
.

Let
\alpha
be a fixed real number such that

(3.1)

\#\left\{\ell\leqslant z:\ell\penalty\ \text{is prime and\penalty\ }P(\ell-1)\geqslant\ell^{\alpha}\right\}\gg\frac{z}{\log z}

for all sufficiently large
z
, where
P(k)
denotes the largest
prime divisor of an integer
k\geqslant 2
, and the implied constant
depends only on
\alpha
.

We recall the following well known result which follows from
the divisibility
\tau_{\ell}(g)\mid\ell-1
(provided
\gcd(g,\ell)=1
)
and the bound

\#\left\{\ell\leqslant z:\ell\penalty\ \text{is prime,\penalty\ }P(\ell-1)>\ell^{1/2}\right\}=(1+o(1))\frac{z}{\log z}

as
z\to\infty
, which easily follows from a stronger result of
Erdős and Murty
[
ErMu96
, Theorem 3]
.
Details can be found in the work of
Kurlberg and Pomerance
[
KuPo05
, Lemma 20]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any fixed
\alpha\geqslant 1/2
satisfying (
3.1
)
and any fixed integer
g\geqslant 1
we have

\#\left\{\ell\leqslant z:\ell\penalty\ \text{is prime,\penalty\ }\tau_{\ell}(g)\geqslant\ell^{\alpha}\right\}\gg\frac{z}{\log z}

as
z\to\infty
.

For an integer
s\geqslant 1
we denote by
\nu_{2}(s)
the
2
-adic order of
s
, that is,
the largest power
\nu
such that
2^{\nu}\mid s
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any fixed
\alpha\geqslant 1/2
satisfying (
3.1
)
and any fixed integer
g\geqslant 1
there are some absolute
constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, such
that for every sufficiently large real number
z>1
, there exist some integer
u_{0}

and a set
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\subseteq[z,C_{1}z]
of primes of cardinality

\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\geqslant\frac{C_{2}z}{\log z\log\log z}

such that for every
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
we have

P(\ell-1)\geqslant z^{\alpha},\qquad P(\ell-1)\mid\tau_{\ell}(g),\qquad\nu_{2}\left(\tau_{\ell}(g)\right)=u_{0}.

Proof.

Lemma
3.1
obviously implies that for some absolute constants
C_{1},C_{3}
there are at least

C_{3}z/\log z
primes
\ell\in[z,C_{1}z]
satisfying only the first two conditions, see also
[
LuSh09
, Lemma 5.1]
.
Let
\overline{{\mathscr{L}}}_{z}
be this set.
Trivially, there are at at most
z/2^{v_{0}}
primes
\ell
with
\nu_{2}(\tau_{\ell}(g))\geqslant v_{0}
.
Hence taking a sufficiently large
C_{4}
, and
v_{0}=\left\lfloor C_{4}\log\log z\right\rfloor
,
we see that if we remove these primes from

\overline{{\mathscr{L}}}_{z}
we obtain the set of
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\subseteq\overline{{\mathscr{L}}}_{z}

of cardinality

\#\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\geqslant\#\overline{{\mathscr{L}}}_{z}-z/2^{v_{0}}\geqslant 0.5\#\overline{{\mathscr{L}}}_{z}\geqslant 0.5C_{3}z/\log z.

Since obviously
\nu_{2}\left(\tau_{\ell}(g)\right)\leqslant\nu_{2}\left(\ell-1\right)\leqslant v_{0}
, making a majority
decision we can find a set of
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
of cardinality

\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\geqslant\frac{\#\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}{v_{0}}\geqslant\frac{0.5C_{3}z}{v_{0}\log z}

with
\nu_{2}\left(\tau_{\ell}(g)\right)=u_{0}
for some fixed
u_{0}\leqslant v_{0}
for every
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
.
Taking
C_{2}=0.5C_{3}/C_{4}
we conclude the proof.
∎

We note that the
Brun-Titchmarsh
theorem (see
[
IwKo04
, Theorem 6.6]
) can be
used to remove
\log\log z
in the bound on
\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
of Lemma
3.2
.
However in our final result we do not try to optimise terms of this order, so we ignore this
and similar potential improvements.

We see that using a result of Baker and Harman
[
BaHa98
]
one can take

(3.2)

\alpha=0.677,

in Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
.

From now on, for any positive real number
z
, we fix a set
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
satisfying the conclusion
of Lemma
3.2
with
\alpha
given by (
3.2
).

3.3.
Bounds of some arithmetic sums

For an integer
K
we consider the set

(3.3)

{\mathscr{K}}={\mathscr{K}}_{m}(K)

where

(3.4)

{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(K)=\{0,\ldots,K\}^{m},

and for
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})\in{\mathscr{K}}
we define

(3.5)

F(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g^{k_{i}}.

For a real
z\geqslant 2
let
\omega_{z}\left(n\right)
be the number of distinct prime factors
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
of
n
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let an integer
K
and a real
z
be sufficiently large.
For
{\mathscr{K}}
and
F(\mathbf{k})

as in (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), respectively, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right)\ll\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}\right)\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}.

Proof.

We have

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right)\ll\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
\ell\mid F(\mathbf{k})\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}\\
\ell\mid F(\mathbf{k})\end{subarray}}1.

Clearly the last sum can be estimated as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}\\
\ell\mid F(\mathbf{k})\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leqslant(K+1)^{m-1}\left(\frac{K+1}{\tau_{\ell}(g)}+1\right)

\displaystyle\ll K^{m}\ell^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}\leqslant K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1},

and the result follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The proof of Lemma
3.3
appeals to essentially trivial bound

O\left(K^{m-1}\left(K/\tau_{\ell}(g)+1\right)\right)
on the number of solution to the congruence
F(\mathbf{k})\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}
,

\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}
. Using bounds of exponential sums one can obtain a better bound, which however does not improve our
final result (see also our Appendix).

For a real
\kappa
we define the sums

D_{\kappa}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
P(\ell-1)\neq P(r-1)\end{subarray}}\gcd\left(\ell-1,r-1\right)^{\kappa}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let a real
z
be sufficiently large.
Then for
\kappa\geqslant 1
we have

D_{\kappa}\leqslant z^{\kappa+\alpha-\alpha\kappa+1+o(1)}.

Proof.

Clearly for each pair of primes
(\ell,r)
in the sum
D_{\kappa}
we have

\gcd\left(\ell-1,r-1\right)\leqslant(\ell-1)/P(\ell-1)\leqslant H
for some integer
H\ll z^{1-\alpha}
.
Hence

D_{\kappa}\leqslant\sum_{h=1}^{H}h^{\kappa}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
P(\ell-1)\neq P(r-1)\\
\gcd\left(\ell-1,r-1\right)=h\end{subarray}}1.

We estimate the inner sum trivially as
O\left((z/h)^{2}\right)
and derive

D_{\kappa}\leqslant z^{2}\sum_{h=1}^{H}h^{\kappa-2}\leqslant z^{2+o(1)}H^{\kappa-1}\leqslant z^{2+(\kappa-1)(1-\alpha)+o(1)},

and the desired result follows.
∎

4.
Bounds of character sums

4.1.
Complete character sums with diagonal forms over finite fields

Let
q
be an odd prime power and let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
be the finite field of
q
elements.
We note that for the purpose of proving Theorem
1.2
, we only need to estimate the sums of this section
over a prime finite field. However, since our proofs work over arbitrary finite fields, we present them in this more general setting with the hope they would be of independent interest.

We let
m\geqslant 1
and
d\geqslant 2
be integers with
d
coprime with
q
.

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
denote the set
of multiplicative characters of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
and let
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}={\mathscr{X}}\setminus\{\chi_{0}\}
be the
set of non-principal characters, we refer to
[
IwKo04
, Chapter 3]
for a background on characters.
We also denote by
\eta\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
the quadratic characters (that is
\eta^{2}=\chi_{0}
).

We recall that the implied constant may depend on
m
(but not on
d
,
q
and other parameters).

We start with ‘pure’ bounds of sums of quadratic characters.

We note that in our next result we have an additional condition of
d
being an even
integer.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that the integer
d\geqslant 2
satisfies
\gcd(d,q)=1
and is even.
Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
. Then for

S=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right)

we have

\left|S\right|\leqslant d^{m-1}(q-1)q^{(m-1)/2}.

Proof.

The proof follows by induction on
m
. For
m=1
, since
d
is even, the sum becomes

\left|\sum_{x_{1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}\right)\right|=q-1.

We assume the bound true for
m-1
and we prove it for
m
. We have

\displaystyle S=\sum_{x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m-1}x_{m-1}^{d}\right).

By the induction hypothesis, the second sum in the above is bounded by
d^{m-2}(q-1)q^{(m-2)/2}
. Hence, we have

(4.1)

\left|S\right|\leqslant\left|S^{*}\right|+d^{m-2}(q-1)q^{(m-2)/2},

where

S^{*}=\sum_{x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right),

to which we apply
[
Kat02
, Theorem 2.1]
. Indeed, since
x_{m}\neq 0
in
S^{*}
, we make the transformation
x_{i}\to x_{i}x_{m}
,
i=1,\ldots,m-1
,
which does not change the sum. Moreover, since again
d
is even and
\eta(x_{m}^{d})=1
, we obtain

(4.2)

\begin{split}S^{*}&=\sum_{x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m-1}x_{m-1}^{d}+a_{m}\right)\\
&=(q-1)\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m-1}x_{m-1}^{d}+a_{m}\right).\end{split}

Let now

F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1})=a_{1}X_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m-1}X_{m-1}^{d}+a_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}].

We note that the equation
F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1})=0
defines a smooth hypersurface in the affine space
{\mathbb{A}}^{m-1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Indeed, considering the partial derivatives of
F
with respect to each variable
X_{i}
, we obtain that the only possible singular point would be
(0,\ldots,0)
. However, since
a_{m}\neq 0
, this point does not belong to the hypersurface
F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1})=0
.

Similarly, the equation given by the leading homogenous part of
F
,
a_{1}X_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m-1}X_{m-1}^{d}=0
, defines a smooth hypersurface in
the projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{m-2}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.

Applying now
[
Kat02
, Theorem 2.1]
, we conclude from (
4.2
) that

(4.3)

\left|S^{*}\right|\leqslant(d-1)^{m-1}(q-1)q^{(m-1)/2}.

Substituting (
4.3
) in (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle|S|

\displaystyle\leqslant(d-1)^{m-1}(q-1)q^{(m-1)/2}+d^{m-2}(q-1)q^{(m-2)/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant d^{m-2}(q-1)q^{(m-2)/2}\left((d-1)q^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\right).

Since
(d-1)q^{\frac{1}{2}}+1<dq^{1/2}
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Next we need the following bound on multidimensional sum of quadratic characters, twisted by
arbitrary characters. In the next result we do not use that
d
is even.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that the integer
d\geqslant 2
satisfies
\gcd(d,q)=1
.
Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
. Then for any
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{m}\in{\mathscr{X}}
we have

\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right)\chi_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\ldots\chi_{m}\left(x_{m}\right)\ll d^{m}q^{(m+1)/2}.

Proof.

First we note that if each
\chi_{i}
is equal to the principal character, then the result follows from Lemma
4.1
. So, from now on, we assume that not all of the characters
\chi_{i}
are equal to the principal character.

We have

(4.4)

\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\eta\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right)\chi_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\ldots\chi_{m}\left(x_{m}\right)=S_{1}-S_{0},

where

S_{0}=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\chi_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\ldots\chi_{m}\left(x_{m}\right)

and

S_{1}=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\\
a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}=y^{2}\end{subarray}}\chi_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\ldots\chi_{m}\left(x_{m}\right).

Indeed, we observe that each vector
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{m}
contributes
2\chi_{1}(x_{1})\ldots\chi_{m}(x_{m})
to the sum
S_{1}
.
It is also easy to see that
S_{0}
vanishes unless
\chi_{1}=\ldots=\chi_{m}=\chi_{0}
; therefore, due to our assumption from above, we get that
S_{0}=0
. We now fix a nontrivial additive character
\psi
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
By the orthogonality relation,

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda u\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\ u=0,\\
0,&\text{if}\ u\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*},\end{cases}

see
[
IwKo04
, Section 3.1]
.
Hence we write

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}-y^{2}\right)\right)\chi_{1}(x_{1})\ldots\chi_{m}(x_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\psi\left(-\lambda y^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda\left(a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+a_{m}x_{m}^{d}\right)\right)\chi_{1}(x_{1})\ldots\chi_{m}(x_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\psi\left(-\lambda y^{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda a_{i}x_{i}^{d}\right)\chi_{i}\left(x_{i}\right).

The contribution from the terms corresponding to
\lambda=0
is obviously equal to
\frac{q-1}{q}\cdot S_{0}=0
since
S_{0}=0
(because not all of the characters
\chi_{i}
are equal to the principal character).
Hence

(4.5)

S_{1}=W,

where

W=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\psi\left(-\lambda y^{2}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda a_{i}x_{i}^{d}\right)\chi_{i}\left(x_{i}\right).

Now the sum over
y
differs from the classical Gauss sums by only one term corresponding to
y=0
, and so we
have

(4.6)

\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}}\psi\left(-\lambda y^{2}\right)\ll q^{1/2},

see
[
IwKo04
, Theorem 3.4]
.
For the remaining sums, using that
\lambda a_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
we apply the Weil bound
[
Wei74
, Appendix 5, Example 12]

of mixed sums of additive and multiplicative characters which implies

(4.7)

\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\psi\left(\lambda a_{i}x_{i}^{d}\right)\chi_{i}\left(x_{i}\right)\ll dq^{1/2},

see also
[
Li96
, Chapter 6, Theorem 3]
.
Therefore, the bounds (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), combined together yield

W\ll d^{m}q^{(m+1)/2}.

and together with (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) we conclude the proof.
∎

We remark that some, or all, of the characters
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{m}\in{\mathscr{X}}

can be principal and that the implied constant from the conclusion of Lemma
4.2
depends only on
m
.

4.2.
Incomplete character sums with exponential functions

We now extend the definition of
\tau_{\ell}(g)
to orders modulo any
composite moduli
q
with
\gcd(g,q)=1
. We also use
(u/q)
to denote the

Jacobi symbol
modulo an odd
q
.

Here we need to obtain multidimensional analogues of the result on character sums from
[
BaSh17
, Section 3]
.
Although this does not require new ideas and can be achieved at the cost of merely typographical changes
we present some short proofs of these results.

As usual, we write
{\mathbf{\,e}}(t)=\exp(2\pi it)
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We use the following variant of the result of
[
BaSh17
, Lemma 3.1]
, which
in turn is based on some ideas of Korobov
[
Kor70
, Theorem 3]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
a_{1},b_{1}\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\vartheta\geqslant 2
.
Let
\ell
and
r
be distinct primes with

t_{\ell}=\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta),\qquad t_{r}=\tau_{r}(\vartheta),\qquad t=\tau_{\ell r}(\vartheta)

and such that

\gcd\left(\ell r,a_{1}\ldots a_{m}\vartheta\right)=\gcd\left(t_{\ell},t_{r}\right)=1.

We define integers
b_{i,\ell}
and
b_{i,r}
by the conditions

b_{i,\ell}t_{r}+b_{i,r}t_{\ell}\equiv b_{i}\pmod{t},\qquad 0\leqslant b_{i,\ell}<t_{\ell},\quad 0\leqslant b_{i,r}<t_{r},

for
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then,
for

S=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}=1}^{t}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{k_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{k_{m}}}{\ell r}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}k_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}k_{m}}{t}\right)

we have

S=S_{\ell}S_{r}

where

\displaystyle S_{\ell}=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=1}^{t_{\ell}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}}}{\ell}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1,\ell}x_{1}+\ldots+b_{m,\ell}x_{m}}{t_{\ell}}\right),

\displaystyle S_{r}=\sum_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=1}^{t_{r}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{y_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{y_{m}}}{r}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1,r}y_{1}+\ldots+b_{m,r}y_{m}}{t_{r}}\right).

Proof.

As in the proof of
[
BaSh17
, Lemma 3.1]
,
using that
\gcd(t_{\ell},t_{r})=1
, we see that
the integers

xt_{r}+yt_{\ell},\qquad 0\leqslant x<t_{\ell},\qquad\ 0\leqslant y<t_{r},

run through the complete residue system modulo

t=t_{\ell}t_{r}.

Moreover,

(4.8)

\vartheta^{xt_{r}+yt_{\ell}}\equiv\vartheta^{xt_{r}}\pmod{\ell},\qquad\vartheta^{xt_{r}+yt_{\ell}}\equiv\vartheta^{yt_{\ell}}\pmod{r},

and

(4.9)

{\mathbf{\,e}}(b(xt_{r}+yt_{\ell})/t)={\mathbf{\,e}}(bx/t_{\ell}){\mathbf{\,e}}(by/t_{r}).

Hence,

(4.10)

\begin{split}S&=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=1}^{t_{\ell}}\sum_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=1}^{t_{r}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{\ell r}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}\left(x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}\right)+\ldots+b_{m}\left(x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}\right)}{t}\right).\end{split}

Using the multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol, and recalling the congruences (
4.8
), we derive

(4.11)

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{\ell r}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad=\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{\ell}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{r}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad=\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}}}{\ell}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{r}\right).\end{split}

Furthermore, by (
4.9
) we have

(4.12)

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}\left(x_{1}t_{r}+y_{1}t_{\ell}\right)+\ldots+b_{m}\left(x_{m}t_{r}+y_{m}t_{\ell}\right)}{t}\right)\\
&\qquad\quad={\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}x_{m}}{t_{\ell}}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}t_{m}}{t_{r}}\right).\end{split}

Using (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) in (
4.10
), we see that the sum
S
can be decomposed
into a product of two sums as follows

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=1}^{t_{\ell}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}t_{r}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}t_{r}}}{\ell}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}x_{m}}{t_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\sum_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=1}^{t_{r}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{y_{1}t_{\ell}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{y_{m}t_{\ell}}}{r}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}t_{m}}{t_{r}}\right).

We now replace
x_{i}
with
x_{i}t_{r}^{-1}\pmod{t_{\ell}}

and
y_{i}
with
y_{i}t_{\ell}^{-1}\pmod{t_{r}}
,
and take into account that

b_{i}t_{r}^{-1}\equiv b_{i,\ell}\pmod{t_{\ell}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad b_{i}t_{\ell}^{-1}\equiv b_{i,r}\pmod{t_{r}},

for
i=1,\ldots,m
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Next we estimate the sums
S_{\ell}
and
S_{r}
which appear in Lemma
4.3
.
Namely we now establish an analogue of
[
BaSh17
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
a_{1},b_{1}\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\vartheta\geqslant 2
.
Let
\ell
be a prime with

t_{\ell}=\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)

and such that

\gcd\left(\ell,a_{1}\ldots a_{m}\vartheta\right)=1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gcd(t_{\ell},2)=1.

Then for

\displaystyle S_{\ell}=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=1}^{t_{\ell}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{x_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{x_{m}}}{\ell}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}x_{m}}{t_{\ell}}\right)

we have

S_{\ell}\ll\begin{cases}\ell^{(m+1)/2},&\text{for arbitrary}\ b_{1},\ldots,b_{m},\\
t_{\ell}\ell^{(m-1)/2},&\text{for}\ b_{1}=\ldots=b_{m}=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Denoting
d=(\ell-1)/t_{\ell}
,
we can write
\vartheta=\rho^{d}
with
some
primitive root
\rho
modulo
\ell
.
Then,

(4.13)

\begin{split}S_{\ell}&=\frac{1}{d^{m}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=1}^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{a_{1}\rho^{dx_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\rho^{dx_{m}}}{\ell}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{d\left(b_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}x_{m}\right)}{\ell-1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{d^{m}}\sum_{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}=1}^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{a_{1}w_{1}^{d}+\ldots+a_{m}w_{m}^{d}}{\ell}\right)\chi_{1}(w_{1})\ldots\chi_{m}(w_{m}),\end{split}

where for
w\in{\mathbb{F}}_{\ell}
we define
\chi_{i}
by

\chi_{i}(w)={\mathbf{\,e}}\left(b_{i}dx/(\ell-1)\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,m,

where
x
is any integer for which
w\equiv\rho^{x}\pmod{\ell}
.

As in the proof of
[
BaSh17
, Lemma 3.2]
we observe
\chi_{i}
is a multiplicative character of
{\mathbb{F}}_{\ell}
for
each
i=1,\ldots,m
. Recalling Lemma
4.2
, we derive from (
4.13
)

S_{\ell}\ll\frac{1}{d^{m}}d^{m}\ell^{(m+1)/2}=\ell^{(m+1)/2},

which establishes the desired bound for arbitrary
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For
b_{1}=\ldots=b_{m}=0
we observe that since
t_{\ell}
is odd,
d
is even and hence
we can use Lemma
4.1
instead of Lemma
4.2
.
Thus in this case (
4.13
) implies

S_{\ell}\ll\frac{1}{d^{m}}d^{m-1}\ell^{(m+1)/2}=\frac{1}{d}\ell^{(m+1)/2}\ll t_{\ell}\ell^{(m-1)/2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
combined together imply the following bound.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
a_{1},b_{1}\ldots,a_{m},b_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\vartheta\geqslant 2
.
Let
\ell
and
r
be distinct primes with

t_{\ell}=\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta),\qquad t_{r}=\tau_{r}(\vartheta),\qquad t=\tau_{\ell r}(\vartheta)

and such that

\gcd\left(\ell r,a_{1}\ldots a_{m}\vartheta\right)=\gcd\left(t_{\ell},t_{r}\right)=\gcd(t_{\ell}t_{r},2)=1.

Then,
for

S=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}=1}^{t}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{k_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{k_{m}}}{\ell r}\right){\mathbf{\,e}}\left(\frac{b_{1}k_{1}+\ldots+b_{m}k_{m}}{t}\right)

we have

S\ll\begin{cases}(\ell r)^{(m+1)/2},&\text{for arbitrary}\ b_{1},\ldots,b_{m},\\
t(\ell r)^{(m-1)/2},&\text{for}\ b_{1}=\ldots=b_{m}=0.\end{cases}

Clearly in Lemma
4.4
and Corollary
4.5
the parity condition on
multiplicative orders is important only in the case where
b_{1}=\ldots=b_{m}=0
,
as only these parts appeal to Lemma
4.1
(which required
d
to be even).

Combining Corollary
4.5
with the completing method, see
[
IwKo04
, Section 12.2]
,
we derive an analogue of
[
BaSh17
, Lemma 3.4]
, which is our
main technical tool.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\vartheta\geqslant 2
.
Let
\ell
and
r
be distinct primes with

\gcd\left(\ell r,a_{1}\ldots a_{m}\vartheta\right)=\gcd\left(\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta),\tau_{r}(\vartheta)\right)=\gcd(\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)\tau_{r}(\vartheta),2)=1.

Then, for any integers
L_{1},\ldots L_{m}\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{L_{1}}\ldots\sum_{k_{m}=1}^{L_{m}}\left(\frac{a_{1}\vartheta^{k_{1}}+\ldots+a_{m}\vartheta^{k_{m}}}{\ell r}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\ll L_{1}\ldots L_{m}t^{-m+1}(\ell r)^{(m-1)/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\left(L^{m-1}t^{-m+1}+1\right)(\ell r)^{(m+1)/2}\left(\log(\ell r)\right)^{m},

where

L=\max\{L_{1},\ldots L_{m}\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad t=\tau_{\ell r}(\vartheta),

and the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

Clearly we can split the above sum into
\left\lfloor L_{1}/t\right\rfloor\cdot\cdots\cdot\left\lfloor L_{m}/t\right\rfloor
complete sums, where each variable
runs over the complete residue system modulo
t
and into at most
O\left((L/t)^{m-1}+1\right)

incomplete sums over a complete residue system modulo
\ell r
.

By Corollary
4.5
each of these complete sums can be estimated as
O\left(t(\ell r)^{(m-1)/2}\right)
, so they contribute

O\left(L_{1}\ldots L_{m}t^{-m+1}(\ell r)^{(m-1)/2}\right)
in total.

By the standard completing
techniques, see, for example,
[
IwKo04
, Section 12.2]
, we derive from Corollary
4.5
that each incomplete
sum can be estimated as
O\left((\ell r)^{(m+1)/2}\left(\log(\ell r)\right)^{m}\right)
.
Therefore, in total they contribute

O\left(\left(L^{m-1}t^{-m+1}+1\right)(\ell r)^{(m+1)/2}\left(\log(\ell r)\right)^{m}\right)
.

Combining both contributions together, we conclude the proof.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

5.1.
Preliminary transformations

We can always assume that

k_{m}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant k_{1}.

We note that there is an integer constant
h_{0}
depending only on the initial data such that
if
k_{m}\geqslant k_{m-1}+h_{0}
then

n^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g^{k_{i}}\geqslant 0.5g^{k_{m}}

and hence for some

(5.1)

K\ll\log N

we have

(5.2)

k_{m}=\max\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\}\ll K.

On the other hand, for
k_{m}<k_{m-1}+h_{0}
, writing

k_{m}=k_{m-1}+h
,
h=0,\ldots,h_{0}-1
and

n^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g^{k_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m-2}c_{i}g^{k_{i}}+(c_{m-1}+c_{m}g^{h})g^{k_{m-1}}

by Theorem
1.1
we obtain at most
O\left((\log N)^{m-1}\right)
solutions
n\leqslant N
.

Hence we now estimate the number of solutions to (
1.4
) with (
5.2
)
(for
K
as in (
5.1
)).

We recall the notation (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), and we write (
1.4
)
as

(5.3)

n^{2}=F(\mathbf{k}).

We also recall the definitions of the set
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
in Section
3.2

and of
\omega_{z}(n)
from Section
3.3
.

To simplify the exposition everywhere below we replace logarithmic, and
double logarithmic factors of
z
with
z^{o(1)}
(implicitly assuming that
z\to\infty
).
In particular we simply write

(5.4)

\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}=z^{1+o(1)}.

Since
z^{o(1)}
also absorbs all implied constants, we use
\leqslant
instead
of
\ll
in the corresponding bounds.

5.2.
Sieving

Note that if
F(\mathbf{k})
is a perfect square, then we always have

\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)=\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}-\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right).

Hence

\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\leqslant\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|+\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right).

Denote by
{\mathscr{M}}
the set of values of
\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}
satisfying (
5.3
)
and let
M=\#{\mathscr{M}}
be its cardinality.
Invoking Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle M\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|+\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}\right)\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}.

Therefore either

(5.5)

M\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\ll\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|

or

(5.6)

M\ll K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}.

Assuming that (
5.5
) holds, by the Cauchy inequality

\left(M\#{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\right)^{2}\leqslant M\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{M}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|^{2}

and extending summation back to all
\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}
and using (
5.4
), we obtain

(5.7)

M\leqslant z^{-2+o(1)}W,

where

W=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\left|\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell}\right)\right|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\sum_{\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell r}\right).

Combining (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), we see that in any case we have

(5.8)

M\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}+z^{-2}W\right)z^{o(1)}.

We further split the sum
W
into two sums as
W=U+V
, where

(5.9)

\begin{split}U&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
P(\ell-1)=P(r-1)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell r}\right),\\
V&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
P(\ell-1)\neq P(r-1)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell r}\right).\end{split}

To estimate
U
(which also includes the diagonal case
\ell=r
), we use the trivial bound
(K+1)^{m}

on each inner sum, deriving that

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leqslant(K+1)^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
P(\ell-1)=P(r-1)\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\leqslant(K+1)^{m}\sum_{d\geqslant z^{\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}\\
\ell\equiv r\equiv 1\bmod d\end{subarray}}1\ll K^{m}\sum_{d\geqslant z^{\alpha}}\frac{z^{2}}{d^{2}}\ll K^{m}z^{2-\alpha}.

Hence we can write (
5.8
) as

(5.10)

M\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}+z^{-2}V\right)z^{o(1)}.

5.3.
Bounds of character sums

To estimate
V
, we first observe that for every
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
the inequality

\tau_{\ell}(g)\geqslant P(\ell-1)\geqslant\ell^{\alpha}
implies (since
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
)
that
P(\ell-1)\mid\tau_{\ell}(g)
.

Fix a pair
(\ell,r)\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}{\times}{\mathscr{L}}_{z}

with
P(\ell-1)\neq P(r-1)
and define

h=\gcd\left(\tau_{\ell}(g),\tau_{r}(g)\right)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\vartheta=g^{h}.

We observe that

\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)=\tau_{\ell}(g)/h\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\tau_{r}(\vartheta)=\tau_{r}(g)/h.

Furthermore, due to our choice of the set
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
in
Section
3.2
we have

\nu_{2}(\tau_{\ell}(g))=\nu_{2}(\tau_{r}(g))=\nu_{2}(h)

and hence both
\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)
and
\tau_{r}(\vartheta)
are odd.

We now write

(5.11)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell r}\right)=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}=1}^{h}T_{\ell,r}\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right),

where

\displaystyle T_{\ell,r}\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant(K-j_{1})/h}

\displaystyle\qquad\ldots\sum_{1\leqslant k_{m}\leqslant(K-j_{m})/h}\left(\frac{c_{1}g^{k_{1}h+j_{1}}+\ldots+c_{m}g^{k_{m}h+j_{m}}}{\ell r}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant k_{1}\leqslant(K-j_{1})/h}

\displaystyle\qquad\ldots\sum_{1\leqslant k_{m}\leqslant(K-j_{m})/h}\left(\frac{c_{1}g^{j_{1}}\vartheta^{k_{1}}+\ldots+c_{m}g^{j_{m}}\vartheta^{k_{m}}}{\ell r}\right).

We can certainly assume that
z
is large enough so that

\gcd(c_{1}\cdots c_{m},\ell r)=1

for
\ell,r\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
. Therefore
Lemma
4.6
applies to
T_{\ell,r}\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right)
and
implies

\displaystyle T_{\ell,r}\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\ll(K/h)^{m}\left(\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)\tau_{r}(\vartheta)\right)^{-m+1}(\ell r)^{(m-1)/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\left((K/h)^{m-1}\left(\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)\tau_{r}(\vartheta)\right)^{-m+1}+1\right)(\ell r)^{(m+1)/2}\left(\log(\ell r)\right)^{m}.

Using

\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta)\tau_{r}(\vartheta)\gg z^{2\alpha}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ell r\ll z^{2}

we see that

\displaystyle T_{\ell,r}\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\ \leqslant\left((K/h)^{m}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}+\left((K/h)^{m-1}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)+2}+z^{m+1}\right)\right)z^{o(1)}.

Therefore, after the substitution in (
5.11
) we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{k})}{\ell r}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}+K^{m-1}hz^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)+2}+h^{m}z^{m+1}\right)z^{o(1)}.

Since obviously
h\leqslant\gcd(\ell-1,r-1)
, from the definition of
V
in (
5.9
) and using (
5.4
),
we now derive

V\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}+D_{1}K^{m-1}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}+D_{m}z^{m-1}\right)z^{2+o(1)},

where
D_{1}
and
D_{m}
are as in Lemma
3.5
and thus we get

D_{1}\leqslant z^{2+o(1)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad D_{m}\leqslant z^{m+\alpha-\alpha m+1+o(1)}.

Therefore

V\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}+K^{m-1}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)+2}+z^{2m+\alpha-\alpha m}\right)z^{2+o(1)}.

which after the substitution in (
5.10
) yields

\displaystyle M\leqslant(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}

\displaystyle+K^{m}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}

\displaystyle+K^{m-1}z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)+2}+z^{2m+\alpha-\alpha m})z^{o(1)}.

5.4.
Optimisation

Clearly we can assume that

(5.12)

z\leqslant K^{1/(2-\alpha)}

as otherwise the last term
z^{2m+\alpha-\alpha m}

exceeds the trivial bound
K^{m}
.
Furthermore, for
m\geqslant 3
and
\alpha
as in (
3.2
), we have

(5.13)

(m-1)(2\alpha-1)>\alpha

and hence (using that
z^{(m-1)(1-2\alpha)}<z^{-\alpha}
due to the inequality (
5.13
)), we can simplify the above bound as follows:

M\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}+K^{m-1}z^{2-(m-1)(2\alpha-1)}+z^{2m+\alpha-\alpha m}\right)z^{o(1)}.

Moreover, since we have (
5.12
) and
\alpha<1
, we see that

K^{m}z^{-\alpha}>K^{m-1}

which means that

(5.14)

M\leqslant\left(K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1}z^{2-(m-1)(2\alpha-1)}+z^{2m+\alpha-\alpha m}\right)z^{o(1)}.

First we note that for
m\geqslant 5
, with our choice of
\alpha=0.677
in (
3.2
) along with our assumption (
5.12
),
we have that

K^{m}z^{-\alpha}>K^{m-1}z^{2-(m-1)(2\alpha-1)}

and thus the second term in (
5.14
) never dominates and we choose

z=K^{m/(2m+2\alpha-\alpha m)}

to balance the first and the third terms. Hence for if
m\geqslant 5
, we obtain:

(5.15)

M\leqslant K^{m-m\alpha/(2m+2\alpha-\alpha m)+o(1)}.

For
m=4
and with (
3.2
), direct calculations show that as for
m\geqslant 5
, it is better to balance the
first and the third terms in (
5.14
) (rather than the first and the second terms), hence (
5.15
)
also holds for
m=4
.

Finally, for
m=3
one checks that balancing the
first and the second terms in (
5.14
) with
z=K^{1/(4-3\alpha)}

leads to an optimal result

M\leqslant\left(K^{3-\alpha/(4-3\alpha)}+K^{(6-2\alpha)/(4-3\alpha)}\right)K^{o(1)}\leqslant K^{3-\alpha/(4-3\alpha)+o(1)},

which concludes the proof (see also (
5.1
)).

6.
Comments

The proof of Theorem
1.2
, depends on the bound

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}}\omega_{z}\left(F(\mathbf{k})\right)\leqslant\sum_{\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}}T_{m}(K,\ell),

where
T_{m}(K,\ell)
is the number of solutions to the congruence

F(\mathbf{k})\equiv 0\pmod{\ell},\qquad\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(K),

where
{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(K)
is given by (
3.4
), see the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
In fact, in the proof of Lemma
3.3
we use the trivial bound

(6.1)

T_{m}(K,\ell)\leqslant(K+1)^{m-1}\left(\frac{K+1}{\tau_{\ell}(g)}+1\right)\ll K^{m}z^{-\alpha}+K^{m-1},

which holds for any
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
and is the best possible for
m=2
.
For
m\geqslant 3
we get a better bound using exponential sum. This does not improve
our final result, however since it can be of independent interest and since it maybe becomes
important if better bounds of
W
in (
5.7
) become available (or maybe
with some other modifications of the argument) we present such a better bound in
Appendix
A
, see Lemma
A.2
.

The method of the proof of Theorem
1.2
also works for relations of the form

n^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}g_{i}^{k_{i}},

with integer coefficients
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
of the same sign and arbitrary
integer bases
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\geqslant 2
. Indeed,
in this case we still have a bound
O(\log N)
on the exponents

k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}
, which is important for our method.
It is an interesting open question to establish such a bound for arbitrary

c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
. Similarly, our method can also be used to estimate
the number of
n\leqslant N
which can be represented as

n^{2}=u_{1}+\ldots+u_{m}

for some
{\mathscr{S}}
-units

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
, that is, as a sum of
m
integers
which have all their prime factors from a prescribed finite set of primes
{\mathscr{S}}
. Again,
if negative values of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
are allowed then some additional arguments
are needed to bound the powers of primes in each
{\mathscr{S}}
-unit.

Furthermore, as in
[
BaSh17
]
we observe that under the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis
we can obtain a slightly large value of
\gamma_{m}
. We now recall that
p

is called a
Sophie Germain prime
if
p
and
2p+1
are both prime.
Under the assumption of the existence
of the expected number of Sophie Germain primes in intervals, or in fact of just
z^{1+o(1)}

such primes up to
z
, we can choose a set
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
in the argument of the proof
of Theorem
1.2
with any
\alpha<1
and we see that under this assumption
we can take
\gamma_{m}=m/(m+2)
for
m\geqslant 3
.

Finally, we note that other perfect powers
n^{\nu}
for a fixed
\nu=3,4,\ldots
, can be investigated
by our method. However, one needs a version of a result of Baker and Harman
[
BaHa98
]

for primes
\ell
in the arithmetic progression
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{\nu}
, so that there are
multiplicative characters modulo
\ell
of order
\nu
.

Appendix A
Congruences with exponential functions

First we recall the following special case of a classical result of Korobov
[
Kor72
, Lemma 2]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\vartheta\geqslant 2
.
Let
\ell
be a prime with

t=\tau_{\ell}(\vartheta),

and such that

\gcd\left(\ell,a\vartheta\right)=1.

Then, we have

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{t}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(a\vartheta^{k}/t\right)\right|\leqslant\ell^{1/2}.

We now have a bound on
T_{m}(K,\ell)
which improves (
6.1
) in some ranges.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
m\geqslant 3
. Then for
K\geqslant z
and
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
,
where
{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
is as in Section
3.2
, we have

T_{m}(K,\ell)\ll K^{m}z^{-1}+K^{m}z^{m/2-\alpha(m-1)-1}.

Proof.

Let
t=\tau_{\ell}(g)
; then since
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
, we know that
t\geqslant z^{\alpha}
. We also let

T_{m}(\ell)=T_{m}(t-1,\ell).

First we observe that
K\geqslant z\gg t
and thus

(A.1)

T_{m}(K,\ell)\leqslant\left(\frac{K+1}{t}+1\right)^{m}T_{m}(\ell)\ll K^{m}t^{-m}T_{m}(\ell).

Now, using the orthogonality of exponential functions, we write

T_{m}(\ell)=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(t-1)}\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(aF(\mathbf{k})/\ell\right),

where
{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(t-1)
consists of all
m
-tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})
of non-negative integers
k_{i}<t
.
Now changing the order of summation, we obtain

T_{m}(\ell)=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathscr{K}}_{m}(t-1)}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(aF(\mathbf{k})/\ell\right)=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{i}g^{k_{i}}/\ell\right).

The term corresponding to
a=0
is equal to
t^{m}/\ell
. We can
assume that
z
is large enough (as otherwise the bound is trivial) so that
\gcd(c_{1}\cdots c_{m},\ell)=1

for
\ell\in{\mathscr{L}}_{z}
. We apply now the bound of Lemma
A.1
to
m-2
sums
over
k_{3},\ldots,k_{m}
and derive

(A.2)

T_{m}(\ell)\leqslant t^{m}/\ell+\ell^{(m-2)/2}\frac{1}{\ell}R,

where

R=\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\left|\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{1}g^{k_{1}}/\ell\right)\right|\cdot\left|\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{2}g^{k_{2}}/\ell\right)\right|

(note that after an application of Lemma
A.1
we have added
the term corresponding to
a=0
back to the sum).
By the Cauchy inequality

R^{2}\leqslant\left(\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\left|\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{1}g^{k_{1}}/\ell\right)\right|^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\left|\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{2}g^{k_{2}}/\ell\right)\right|^{2}\right).

Using the orthogonality of exponential functions again, we derive

\sum_{a=0}^{\ell-1}\left|\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{t-1}{\mathbf{\,e}}\left(ac_{1}g^{k_{1}}/\ell\right)\right|^{2}=\ell t

and similarly for the sum over
k_{2}
. Hence
R\leqslant\ell t
, and after substitution in (
A.2
)
we derive

T_{m}(\ell)\leqslant t^{m}/\ell+\ell^{(m-2)/2}t,

which together with the inequality (
A.1
) and the fact that
t\geqslant z^{\alpha}
concludes the proof.
∎
