CALPHAD-informed phase-field modeling of grain boundary microchemistry and precipitation in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys
Chuanlai Liu, Alistair Garner, Huan Zhao, Philip B. Prangnell, Baptiste Gault, Dierk Raabe, Pratheek Shanthraj

Abstract
The grain boundary (GB) microchemistry and precipitation behaviour in high-strength Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys has an important influence on their mechanical and electrochemical properties. Simulation of the GB segregation, precipitation, and solute distribution in these alloys requires an accurate description of the thermodynamics and kinetics of this multi-component system. CALPHAD databases have been successfully developed for equilibrium thermodynamic calculations in complex multi-component systems, and in recent years have been combined with diffusion simulations. In this work, we have directly incorporated a CALPHAD database into a phase-field framework, to simulate, with high fidelity, the complex kinetics of the non-equilibrium GB microstructures that develop in these important commercial alloys during heat treatment. In particular, the influence of GB solute segregation, GB diffusion, precipitate number density, and far-field matrix composition, on the growth of a population of GB η \eta -precipitates, was systematically investigated in a model Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloy of near AA7050 composition. It is shown that the GB solute distribution in the early stages of ageing was highly heterogeneous and strongly affected by the distribution of GB η \eta -precipitates. Significant Mg and Cu GB segregation was predicted to remain during overageing, while Zn was rapidly depleted. This non-trivial GB segregation behaviour markedly influenced the resulting precipitate morphologies, but the overall precipitate transformation kinetics on a GB were relatively unaffected. Furthermore, solute depletion adjacent to the GB was largely determined by Zn and Mg diffusion, which will affect the development of precipitate free zones during the early stages of ageing. The simulation results were compared with scanning transmission electron microscopy and atom probe tomography characterisation of alloys of the similar composition, with good agreement.

2.1
The multi-phase-field model

A multi-phase, multi-component system with
\alpha=1~...~N
phases and
i=1~...~M
components is considered.
Vector-valued phase-fields
\bm{\varphi}
and phase compositions
\mathbf{c}_{\alpha}
are employed to describe the local volume fraction and solute compositions for each phase.
The order parameters and compositions fulfil the constraints,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}^{N}\varphi^{\alpha}=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\alpha}^{N}\varphi^{\alpha}c_{i}^{\alpha}=c_{i},

(1)

respectively.
The Gibbs free energy functional
for the
system is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{\varphi},\nabla\bm{\varphi},\mathbf{c}_{\alpha},\text{T})=\int_{V}\bigg(f_{\text{surf}}(\bm{\varphi},\nabla\bm{\varphi})+f_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}bulk}}}(\bm{\varphi},\mathbf{c}_{\alpha},\text{T})\bigg)\text{d}V,

(2)

where V is the domain of consideration, T is the temperature,
f_{\text{surf}}
and
f_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}bulk}}}
represents the interface and
bulk
free energy density, respectively.

The interface energy density is given by:

\displaystyle f_{\text{surf}}(\bm{\varphi},\nabla\bm{\varphi})=\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}^{N}\frac{4\sigma_{\alpha\beta}}{\eta_{\alpha\beta}}\bigg[-\frac{\eta_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\nabla\varphi_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\beta}+\varphi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}\bigg],

(3)

where,
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
and
\eta_{\alpha\beta}
is the interface energy and width between phase
\alpha
and phase
\beta
, respectively.

The bulk free energy contains both the elastic strain energy (
f_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}elas}}}
) and chemical free energy (
f_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}chem}}}
) contributions.
Since GB
\eta
-precipitates generally do not have specific orientation relations with the matrix and mainly have incoherent precipitate-matrix interfaces
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
, the elastic strain energy is only expected to play a minor role in the GB precipitation behaviour (see details in
Appendix

A
).
Therefore, it is physically reasonable to neglect the mechanical driving force for the present application to the simulation of GB precipitation in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys.
This simplification has also been widely employed in the mean-field GB precipitation models
[
20
,
21
]
.

The diffuse interface can be regarded as a mixture of the adjoining phases with equal chemical potentials, and the resulting chemical free energy density is given by:

\displaystyle f_{\text{chem}}(\bm{\varphi},\mathbf{c}_{\alpha},\text{T})=\sum_{\alpha}^{N}\varphi_{\alpha}f_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{c}_{\alpha},\text{T}).

(4)

The temporal and spatial evolution of the phase fields is driven by the minimization of the total free energy,
\mathcal{F}
, through over-damped relaxation:

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi_{\alpha}}=-\sum_{\beta=1}^{\tilde{N}}\frac{M_{\alpha\beta}}{\tilde{N}}\bigg[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\varphi_{\alpha}}-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\varphi_{\beta}}\bigg]=\sum_{\beta=1}^{\tilde{N}}\frac{M_{\alpha\beta}}{\tilde{N}}\bigg[\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\tilde{N}}[\sigma_{\beta\gamma}I_{\beta\gamma}-\sigma_{\alpha\gamma}I_{\alpha\gamma}]+\Delta G_{\alpha\beta}\bigg],

(5)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\gamma}:=\frac{8}{\eta_{\alpha\gamma}}\bigg[\frac{\eta_{\alpha\gamma}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\nabla^{2}\varphi_{\gamma}+\varphi_{\gamma}\bigg],

(6)

where
M_{\alpha\beta}
is the interface mobility,

\tilde{N}
is the number of active phases, and
I_{\alpha\beta}
are the generalised capillary terms.

The chemical driving force,
\Delta G_{\alpha\beta}
, is calculated as the derivative of the chemical free energy, with respect to the order parameters,
i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta G_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=-\bigg[\frac{\partial f_{\text{chem}}}{\partial\varphi_{\alpha}}-\frac{\partial f_{\text{chem}}}{\partial\varphi_{\beta}}\bigg]

(7)

\displaystyle=f_{\text{chem}}^{\beta}(\mathbf{c}_{\beta})-f_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{c}_{\alpha})-\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\bigg[\mu_{i}\bigg(c_{i}^{\beta}(\bm{\mu}^{\beta},\text{T})-c_{i}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mu}^{\alpha},\text{T})\bigg)\bigg],

where
the
vector-valued
\bm{\mu}^{\alpha}

refers to
the chemical potentials for each phase and
\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}^{\alpha}=\mu_{i}^{\beta}
is calculated relative to the solvent component
M
:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\alpha}=\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}}{\partial c_{i}^{\alpha}}\bigg)_{\text{T,P},c_{j}\neq c_{i}}-\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}}{\partial c_{M}^{\alpha}}\bigg)_{\text{T,P},c_{j}\neq c_{M}}.

(8)

When
\Delta G_{\alpha\beta}=0
,
Eq.

7
represents the common tangent construction, where the chemical free energy and chemical potential can be obtained from the CALPHAD database.

2.2
Multi-component diffusion and the CALPHAD model

Following
[
51
,
44
]
, a linear flux-force form is assumed for diffusion
of each component
,

\displaystyle\dot{c_{i}}(\bm{\mu})=\nabla\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}L_{ij}^{M}\nabla\mu_{j}.

(9)

The phenomenological chemical mobilities,
L_{ij}^{M}
, in
Eq.

9
are calculated as the volume-averaged chemical mobilities for each phase,
~{}^{\alpha}L_{ij}^{M}
,

\displaystyle L_{ij}^{M}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\varphi_{\alpha}~^{\alpha}L_{ij}^{M}.

(10)

The phase
specific
chemical mobilities,
~{}^{\alpha}L_{ij}^{M}
, can then be calculated from the atomic mobilities,

\displaystyle~{}^{\alpha}L_{ij}^{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(\delta_{jk}-c_{j}^{\alpha}\right)\left(\delta_{ki}-c_{i}^{\alpha}\right)c_{k}^{\alpha}M_{k}^{\alpha},

(11)

where the Kronecker delta
\delta_{ij}=1
when
i=j
, and
\delta_{ij}=0
when
i\neq j
.
M_{i}^{\alpha}
is the atomic mobility of component
i
in the phase
\alpha
,

\displaystyle M_{i}^{\alpha}=\Theta_{i}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{RT}\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{\Delta Q_{i}^{\alpha}}{RT}\bigg),

(12)

where
R
is the universal gas constant;
\Theta_{i}^{\alpha}
is the product of the atomic jump distance (squared) and the jump frequency,
and

\Delta Q_{i}^{\alpha}
is the diffusion activation energy of component
i
.

\Theta_{i}^{\alpha}
is set to unity, and the activation energy,
\Delta Q_{i}^{\alpha}
, is expressed as a function of
the
compositions and temperature in terms of a Redlich-Kister polynomial,

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}Q_{i}^{j}+\sum_{p}^{M-1}\sum_{j=p+1}^{M}c_{p}c_{j}\bigg[\sum_{r=0,1,2,...}~^{r}Q_{i}^{pj}(c_{p}-c_{j})^{r}\bigg],

(13)

where
Q_{i}^{j}
is the activation energy of species
,

i
,
in pure species
,

j
, and
~{}^{r}Q_{i}^{pj}
are binary interaction parameters.

Note that the composition fields,
c_{i}(\bm{\mu})
, in
Eq.

9
are implicit functions of chemical potentials, and the primary variables in the transport equations are the chemical potentials rather than the compositions.
However, the solution
for

Eq.

9
requires the inversion of the chemical potential relation in order to
be able to
express compositions
c_{i}:=c_{i}(\bm{\mu})
for
i=1,...,M-1
.
The numerical implementation of the substitutional phase and the stoichiometric phase in the chemical potential-based transport relations, and the GB segregation and diffusion will be described in the following sections.

2.2.1
Substitutional matrix phase

In the present work, the FCC-matrix phase is treated as a substitutional solid-solution.
The CALPHAD-based Gibbs free energy of the substitutional phase is given by,

\displaystyle\Omega f_{\text{chem}}^{\phi}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}~^{0}G_{i}^{\phi}+RT\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(c_{i}\text{ln}c_{i}\right)+~^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\phi}+G_{\text{mag}}^{\phi},

(14)

where
\Omega
is the molar volume and
~{}^{0}G_{i}^{\phi}
is the molar Gibbs free energy for
the
pure elements in a specified lattice structure.

G_{\text{mag}}^{\phi}
represents the magnetic contribution to the free energy, and does not need to be considered
for
application to Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys.
The excess term,
~{}^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\phi}
, expresses the non-ideal interactions between elements in the phase, and is modelled by the following Redlich-Kister polynomial:

\displaystyle~{}^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\phi}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{M}c_{i}c_{j}\left[\sum_{v=0}~^{v}L_{i,j}^{\phi}(c_{i}-c_{j})^{v}\right]

(15)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{M-2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{M-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{M}c_{i}c_{j}c_{k}\sum_{v=1}~^{v}L_{i,j,k}^{\phi}\left[c_{v}+\frac{1-c_{i}-c_{j}-c_{k}}{3}\right],

where
~{}^{v}L_{i,j}^{\phi}
and
~{}^{v}L_{i,j,k}^{\phi}
denote the binary and ternary interaction parameters. They are usually expressed as linear functions of temperature.

It is noteworthy that the up-hill diffusion arising from the miscibility gap is not expected for the current studies of GB
\eta
-precipitation in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys, based on an analysis of the chemical free energy of the solution matrix.
Therefore, the energy contribution from the gradient of concentrations (
i.e.
the forth order derivative with respect to concentrations in the Cahn-Hilliard model
[
52
,
44
]
) is not considered in the current work.

Based on the chemical free energy definition in
Eq.

14
and
Eq.

15
, the chemical potential for component
i
in the disordered phase is given by:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\alpha}=~^{0}G_{i}^{\alpha}-~^{0}G_{M}^{\alpha}+RT\text{ln}\frac{c_{i}^{\alpha}}{c_{M}^{\alpha}}+\bigg(\frac{\partial~^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}}{\partial c_{i}^{\alpha}}-\frac{\partial~^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}}{\partial c_{M}^{\alpha}}\bigg).

(16)

Solving
Eq.

9
requires inversion of
the
chemical potentials in order to express
c_{i}:=c_{i}(\bm{\mu})
for
i=1,...,M-1
.
This is achieved algorithmically, in a time-discrete context, through a semi-implicit splitting of the chemical potential relation:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})=\check{\mu}_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})+\hat{\mu}_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1}),

(17)

into a convex contribution:

\displaystyle\check{\mu}_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})=~^{0}G_{i}^{\alpha}-~^{0}G_{M}^{\alpha}+RT\text{ln}\frac{c_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})}{c_{M}^{\alpha}(t_{n})},

(18)

and a non-convex contribution:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})=\frac{\partial~^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})}{\partial c_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})}-\frac{\partial~^{\text{XS}}G_{\text{chem}}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})}{\partial c_{M}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})},

(19)

where
t_{n}
and
t_{n-1}
are consecutive discrete time intervals.
Based on this semi-implicit splitting, the compositions,
c_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})
, can then be expressed in terms of chemical potentials by the inversion of
Eq.

18
,

\displaystyle c_{m}^{\alpha}(t_{n})=\frac{\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{m}^{\alpha}(t_{n})-\left(~{}^{0}G_{m}^{\alpha}-~^{0}G_{M}^{\alpha}\right)-\hat{\mu}_{m}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})}{RT}\bigg)}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\text{exp}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n})-\left(~{}^{0}G_{i}^{\alpha}-~^{0}G_{M}^{\alpha}\right)-\hat{\mu}_{i}^{\alpha}(t_{n-1})}{RT}\bigg)}.

(20)

2.2.2
The
\eta
-precipitate phase

In the present work, the
\eta
-precipitate has been simplified as a stoichiometric compound, with little or no solubility range.

By neglecting
variation in
\eta
-precipitate chemistry the model complexity
and computation time can be substantially reduced

[
20
]
.
The chemical free energy corresponding to the stoichiometric composition at a given temperature in the CALPHAD definition
is
approximated
in the model
by a sharp parabolic function centred at this point,

\displaystyle\Omega f_{\text{chem}}^{\text{stoi}}=G_{\text{const}}(T)+\sum_{i}^{M}A_{i}^{\text{stoi}}(c_{i}^{\text{stoi}}-c_{i,\text{eq}}^{\text{stoi}})^{2},

(21)

where
G_{\text{const}}
is the chemical free energy of
the
\eta
-precipitate corresponding to the stoichiometric composition obtained from
the CALPHAD database,
c_{i,\text{eq}}^{\text{stoi}}
is the stoichiometric composition,
A_{i}^{\text{stoi}}
is a penalty chosen large enough to guarantee that the free energy increases rapidly as the composition deviates from the stoichiometric composition.
The chemical potential of species
i
of a stoichiometric phase can then be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\text{stoi}}=2A_{i}^{\text{stoi}}(c_{i}^{\text{stoi}}-c_{i,\text{eq}}^{\text{stoi}}).

(22)

The composition can be easily obtained from the chemical potential as:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\text{stoi}}=\frac{\mu_{i}^{\text{stoi}}}{2A_{i}^{\text{stoi}}}+c_{i,\text{eq}}^{\text{stoi}}.

(23)

2.2.3
Grain boundary segregation

GB segregation is assumed to result from
the
reduction in interface energy,
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
in
Eq.

3
, resulting from solute occupation of the boundary.
Following the CALPHAD approach, the reduction in interface energy can be expressed by a Redlich-Kister polynomial.
In the current formulation, however, the following first order dependence is used:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{0}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\Delta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{i},

(24)

where
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{0}
is the un-decorated GB energy, and
\Delta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{i}
is the relaxation in GB energy due to solute-GB interaction.
Substituting
Eq.

24
into
Eq.

3
results in an additional segregation term to be included in the chemical potential relations,
Eq.

16
and
Eq.

22
:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{seg}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{\text{surf}}}{\partial c_{i}}\bigg)_{\text{T,P},c_{j}\neq c_{i}}-\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{\text{surf}}}{\partial c_{M}}\bigg)_{\text{T,P},c_{j}\neq c_{M}}\approx\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}\varphi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\beta}\left(\Delta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{i}-\Delta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{M}\right),

(25)

where the gradient terms in
Eq.

3
are ignored for simplicity.

[

]
is implicitly satisfied in the current model, as the transport relations

4.3.1

The three-dimensional bi-crystal model, shown in

5

\times

Three phase-field order parameters were used in the present GB precipitation simulation studies,

two representing the two grains and a third representing the GB precipitate phase.

Periodic and constant chemical potential
boundary conditions were used in the simulations.
When applying periodic boundary conditions, the average solute compositions in the simulation domain remained constant during the ageing process, representing the extreme case
of
an ultrafine grain size.
In the
more realistic

of a
coarse grain size, the chemical potentials of all the solute elements (Zn, Mg, Cu) were fixed on the boundaries of the simulation box, which allow
ed
solute exchange with the far-field matrix.
The GB
\eta
-precipitates were modelled as a stoichiometric phase with a composition of
(\text{Zn}_{45.4},\text{Al}_{16.8},\text{Cu}_{4.5})\text{Mg}_{33.3}

120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}

5\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}

1000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
was used in the simulation.
These precipitate embryos were randomly distributed in the GB plane.
A matrix composition of 2.69 at.% Zn, 2.87 at.% Mg and 0.95 at.% Cu, and heat treatment temperature of
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}

the experimental data in

Section

3.1

-precipitates in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys generally do not have specific orientation relation with the matrix and the precipitate-matrix interfaces are usually incoherent

49

50

0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{J}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}

Appendix

1\text{\times}{10}^{-11}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}

The molar volume of the alloy,
\Omega

Discussion

5.1
Comparison and assessment of the simulations and experimental results

In the current work, both APT and STEM-EDS analysis show that gradual GB solute depletion occur
red
in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys during
artificial
ageing treatment
s
from the
initial segregation seen after quenching
, but at different rates with respect to the main elements of interest,
e.g.
the
peak
Mg and Cu
concentration on the GBs reduced
to about 3.0 at.% and 1.5 at.%, respectively.
While all the simulation cases considered here were able to predict this general trend, the difference
s
between the cases studied (
e.g.
GB diffusion, solute flux from the far-field matrix, precipitate number density)
were
significant and provide
d
insight into
some of
the observed variability
seen
in experimental results
[
13
,
14
,
5
,
11
]
.

Periodic and fixed boundary conditions were used in the simulations, which physically represent extremely fine and coarse grain sizes, respectively.
The simulation results show that the final Mg and Cu GB
segregation
after ageing was lower when fixed boundary conditions were used compared to with periodic boundary conditions,
i.e.
it decreases with increasing grain size.
Under periodic boundary conditions, the Zn/Cu ratio in the GB precipitate (10.1) was much higher than that in the overall domain (3.2).
Consequently, with the alloy composition investigated
,
which has an excess of Mg relative to the
\eta
-phase stoichiometry, Zn exhaustion was the precipitate growth-limiting process, leading to
an excess in residual
GB Cu.
However, as shown in
Fig.

13
(c), under the fixed boundary condition, the Zn/Cu ratio in the domain increased from 3.2 to 7.2 after
12.5\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
ageing, owing to the net influx of Zn into the domain to supply the growing GB precipitate
.

This occurred because of the Zn’s
higher diffusivity compared to Cu, which did not interact with the far-field matrix.

Allowing the possibility of supplying Zn into the domain
thus result
ed
in the continuous growth of the GB precipitates and consumption of the
excess
GB Cu.
Analogously,
the
greater influx of Zn than Mg
into the domain
resulted in an increase in the Zn/Mg ratio from 0.99 to 1.2, which was still smaller than the GB precipitate ratio of 1.4, and will consequently result in the consumption of the GB
excess
Mg.
These predictions are supported by similar observations of a decrease in Mg and Cu GB
segregation
with increasing grain size found from APT experiments on an ultrafine grained AA7136 alloy, processed by equal-channel angular pressing
[
13
]
, and AA7075, processed by high pressure torsion
[
14
]
.

In AA7050 alloy
STEM-EDS measured Mg and Cu residual segregation levels were found to be relatively constant along a GB after
T7651
overageing (
Fig.

3
), with no observable Zn excess, while the APT analysis, in
Fig.

2
(e), showed a slightly enriched Zn GB composition of 1.2 at.%.
This variability in GB Zn composition can be partly explained by the different sensitivities of the two experimental techniques, but could also be caused by the effect of the GB diffusion on the evolution of the GB solute composition.
As shown in
Fig.

9
(a) and
Fig.

11
(a), GB diffusion
was
able to significantly reduce the GB solute excess at a given ageing time, with Zn being the most sensitive followed by Mg, whereas Cu
was
relatively unaffected.
Due to the
ir
higher GB diffusion rate, HAGBs acting as solute collectors provide a fast path to transport solute to the precipitates, resulting in the more rapid consumption of the GB solute excess and a homogeneous distribution within the GB plane.
As the solute mobility can vary significantly depending on the structural state of individual GBs, it can be postulated that a range of Zn GB
segregation levels
may exist on different GBs,
which could contribute to
the observed discrepancy between the APT and STEM-EDS analysis, however, more experimental data is required to confirm this predicted effect.
The simulations also show a larger variability in Mg GB
segregation level
, with less variability in Cu.
These predictions are further supported by APT observations of a larger composition variation of GB Mg (0.9 at.%) compared to Cu (0.3 at.%) in
an ultrafine grained
AA7136 alloy
[
13
]
.

5.2
Role of grain boundary segregation and diffusion in corrosion

The evolution of the GB microchemistry and microstructure is important in understanding the corrosion behaviour of Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys.
It is generally accepted that GB Mg segregation is detrimental to SCC resistance
,
due to
a
strong Mg-H interaction
[
61
]
, while Cu enrichment on GBs and within GB precipitates can retard the anodic dissolution
of GB
\eta
-phase precipitates
and enhance GB cohesion
[
6
,
7
]
.
It has also been reported that the GB segregation of Zn may be responsible for hydrogen embrittlement and the high susceptibility to SCC of Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys
[
62
]
.
However, this has since been disputed by subsequent research that showed no correlation between Zn segregation and SCC
[
63
,
3
]
.
In the current work, phase-field simulations as well as APT and STEM-EDS characterisation
have
reveal
ed
that Cu and Mg moderately segregated
to
GB
s
in peak and overaged conditions,
while there was
little to no Zn
residual
segregation
, as any initially segregated Zn was rapidly consumed by the growing precipitates
.
This suggests that after overageing the SCC resistance in AA7050 alloys is predominantly affected by the Cu and Mg segregation, with Zn playing a more minor role.

The solute interaction terms used in the CALPHAD model, for AA7050 alloy at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, are only around
10.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the total Gibbs energy, which implies a limited solute interaction
between the solute species
on GBs.
Therefore, the observed GB segregation
was
strongly influenced by the multi-component diffusion between the GB and the matrix close to the GB and the rate of removal of solute by the growing GBPs, with
only
subtle co-segregation effects among different solutes.
As described in
Eq.

9
,
the diffusion of component
i
depends on
the chemical potential gradient
s

of all the components, which are correlated by
the mobility matrix.
For AA7050 at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, the mobilities for Cu in the matrix are
L_{\text{CuCu}}^{\text{FCC}}=

5\text{\times}{10}^{-28}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
,
L_{\text{CuMg}}^{\text{FCC}}=

-5.1\text{\times}{10}^{-28}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
,
L_{\text{CuZn}}^{\text{FCC}}=

-5.8\text{\times}{10}^{-28}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
, indicating that Cu transport is significantly affected by uphill diffusion along gradients in the Mg and Zn composition.
In addition, the cross-mobilities for Mg and Zn,
L_{\text{MgZn}}^{\text{FCC}}=

2.9\text{\times}{10}^{-27}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
, are approximately an order of magnitude smaller than their self-mobilities,
L_{\text{MgMg}}^{\text{FCC}}=

5\text{\times}{10}^{-26}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
and
L_{\text{ZnZn}}^{\text{FCC}}=

5.6\text{\times}{10}^{-26}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
, indicating that
the effect of
Mg and Zn inter-diffusion is limited.
Since the magnitude of the Cu mobility is relatively small at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, with
L_{\text{ZnZn}}^{FCC}/L_{\text{CuCu}}^{FCC}\approx
113, the large influx of Mg and Zn from the far-field matrix into the GB region
thus
resulted in a minor outflux of Cu to the neighbouring bulk, as shown in
Fig.

12
(c) and
Fig.

13
(c).
However, the GB self-mobility of Cu,
L_{\text{CuCu}}^{\text{GB}}=

1.1\text{\times}{10}^{-22}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
, is about 13 times the GB cross-mobilities,
L_{\text{CuMg}}^{\text{GB}}=

-8.1\text{\times}{10}^{-24}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
and
L_{\text{CuZn}}^{\text{GB}}=

-8.8\text{\times}{10}^{-24}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}\mathrm{mol}\text{\,}\mathrm{J}
, indicating that interdiffusion of Cu on the GB is limited.
The rate of diffusion of Cu to the
\eta
-precipitates has important consequences on SCC resistance, as it was reported that Cu enrichment of
\eta
-precipitates reduces their anodic dissolution rate
[
6
,
7
,
3
]
.
Uphill diffusion is also a potential mechanism for Cu leaching from
\eta
-precipitates, which would be detrimental to SCC resistance.
The results show that uphill diffusion of Cu is active in the bulk at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, but is minimal on the GB
plane
.
Increasing the ageing temperature to
180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
is therefore expected to be beneficial for SCC resistance, as it not only further minimizes the interdiffusion of Cu (
i.e.
reducing uphill diffusion of Cu) but also increases the mobility of Cu relative to Mg and Zn from
L_{\text{ZnZn}}^{FCC}/L_{\text{CuCu}}^{FCC}\approx
113 at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
to
L_{\text{ZnZn}}^{FCC}/L_{\text{CuCu}}^{FCC}\approx
6 at
180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
.

It also increases the equilibrium solubility of Cu in the
\eta
-phase which was not accounted for in the model
[
9
,
10
]
.

Therefore, higher ageing temperature
s
can increase the Cu/Zn ratio in the matrix adjacent to
a
GB, which can enhance the enrichment of Cu along GBs and consequently within GB precipitates.
Improvement of the SCC resistance has been observed through high temperature stages in multi-stage
[
64
]
and RRA
[
3
,
65
,
7
]
heat treatments in Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys, in agreement with this prediction.

The simulation results also indicate that GB diffusion ha
d
a large influence on the precipitate growth, with GB energetics having a minor effect.
On increasing GB diffusivity, the solute depletion zones of Mg and Cu increase
d
in size and distribute
d
more homogeneously across
a
GB, as shown in
Fig.

15
, indicating that GB diffusion can markedly accelerate the solute redistribution kinetics within the matrix adjacent to the GB.
With enhanced GB diffusion solute atoms from the matrix adjacent to the GB are collected onto the GB across a larger area through bulk diffusion and then diffuse rapidly along the GB to the precipitates by GB diffusion.
Since solute mobility on the GB can be 4 to 5 orders of magnitude larger compared to
in
the bulk, for only a
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
reduction of the solute migration energy, this effect is
very
important and GBs acting as solute collectors can effectively transport solutes across large distances, resulting in the accelerated growth of GB precipitates both along
,
as well as normal to
,
the GB.
This
effect
can thus explain the observation of the GB precipitates with much larger thickness than those nucleated in the matrix at the same time, as characterised by APT (
Fig.

1
(d)) and TEM experiments (
Fig.

3
(a)).

Finally, it should be noted that in these simulations to simplify the model a fixed stoichiometry was adopted for the
\eta
-phase, although this is unrealistic.
For example, it is known that in particular the Cu content of the GB precipitates increases with ageing time, owing to its lower diffusivity, and the equilibrium
\eta
-phase composition also changes with ageing temperature
[
9
,
10
]
.
This effect might be expected to increase the level of Cu on the GB plane at short ageing time
s
but should still converge with our simulations at longer ageing times
,
as we used the composition of the
\eta
-phase measured when it was close to equilibrium.
In addition, nucleation of precipitates in the matrix was ignored.
Matrix precipitation will act in competition with the GB precipitates for solute, and therefore inhibit their growth at longer ageing times; although to some extent this was replicated by the predictions
performed
with periodic boundary conditions.

Furthermore, the formation possibility of quench-induced GB precipitation occurring during cooling after solution treatment was not considered in the current phase-field simulations.
Owing to the quench sensitivity of Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys and the low cooling rate experienced in thick plates during quenching, large quench-induced precipitates (
e.g.

\eta
-phase, S-phase, T-phase) can also occur on GBs, prior to those that subsequently nucleate during ageing
[
66
,
4
]
.
These large quench-induced GB precipitates, being of the order of around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, can substantially consume the solutes along GBs and in the matrix adjacent to GBs, which will play an important role in the formation and compositional evolution of the subsequent age-induced GB precipitates and the formation of their associated precipitate free zones.

6
Conclusions

In this work, a
model involving
direct coupling of the phase-field method
with
CALPHAD
thermodynamic phase descriptions
has been developed to predict complex and transient microstructure transformations in multi-component engineering alloys, and an efficient numerical implementation has been proposed to solve the resulting evolution equations.

The developed model has been applied to simulation of the growth of a population of GB
\eta
-precipitates in
an
Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloy as an engineering case study.
This work
has
systematically investigated the influence of the GB solute segregation, GB diffusion, the far-field matrix and the GB precipitate nucleation rate on the GB precipitation and microchemistry evolution.
In agreement with APT and TEM observations, significant Mg and Cu GB segregation was predicted to remain after peak ageing, while Zn
was
depleted at an early stage in the
ageing
heat treatment.
Solute segregation and the respective very different mobilities of the species significantly affected the solute partitioning on GBs and the morphological evolution of GB precipitates, however, it had little influence on the average precipitate
transformation
kinetics.
The simulation results reveal
ed
that fast GB diffusion accelerate
d
precipitate growth by
more
effectively transporting solutes from far-field regions to the solute depletion zones surrounding the precipitates.
Cu diffusion
was
observed to be confined within the matrix adjacent to the GB due to its lower concentration in the precipitate
s
and low diffusivity, whereas the solute depletion zones of Mg and Zn reached beyond the matrix adjacent to the GB at an early stage on ageing at
120\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
.
Finally, it
was
found that the influence of precipitate nucleation on the local heterogeneity of the chemical composition evolution on the GB plane, and within the adjacent regions, can be significantly diminished by fast GB diffusion pathways.
