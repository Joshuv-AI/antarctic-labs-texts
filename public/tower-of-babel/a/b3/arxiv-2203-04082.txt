A concise formula for the Hessian determinant of a function parameterising a quadratic hypersurface Thanks: This work was supported by the Polish National Science Centre grants no. 2015/18/A/ST1/00553 and 2020/02/Y/ST1/00072. Numerical computations were performed within the Interdisciplinary Centre for Mathematical and Computational Modelling UW grant number G76-28.
Bartłomiej Zawalski

Abstract
. We will give a concise formula for the Hessian determinant of a smooth function y : ℝ n ⊇ Ω → ℝ y:\mathbb{R}^{n}\supseteq\Omega\to\mathbb{R} such that its graph is contained in a quadratic hypersurface. The proof will make heavy use of matrix algebra.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
15-A15, 15-A63

The aim of this work is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
y:\mathbb{R}^{n}\supseteq\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a function of class
C^{2}
defined on an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and satisfying a quadratic equation

(1)

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})=0,

where

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
y(\mathbf{x})\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega

is an augmented
(n+2)
-dimensional column vector and
\mathbf{Q}
is an arbitrary
\left(n+2\right)\times\left(n+2\right)
matrix. Then the following formula holds:

(2)

|\mp\mathbf{H}_{y}(\mathbf{x})|\cdot\Delta_{y}(\mathbf{x})^{n/2+1}=-\left|\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\right|,

where
\mathbf{H}_{y}
is the Hessian matrix of
y
,
\Delta_{y}
is the discriminant of the left hand side of (
1
) with respect to the variable
y
and the sign
\mp
depends on the selected branch of the square root function.

Here by
Hessian matrix
of
y
we mean a square
n\times n
matrix defined as follows:

(3)

\mathbf{H}_{y}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{1}\,\partial x_{2}}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{1}\,\partial x_{n}}\\[9.47217pt]
\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{2}\,\partial x_{1}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{2}^{2}}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{2}\,\partial x_{n}}\\[9.47217pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[9.47217pt]
\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{n}\,\partial x_{1}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{n}\,\partial x_{2}}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial x_{n}^{2}}\end{bmatrix},

and by
discriminant
of the quadratic polynomial
ay^{2}+by+c
with
a\neq 0
with respect to the variable
y
we mean

\Delta_{y}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}b^{2}-4ac.

In the course of the proof we will also use a vector differential operator, usually represented by the nabla symbol
\nabla
. It is defined in terms of partial derivative operators as

\nabla=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\end{bmatrix}.

In a convenient mathematical notation, we can consider e.g. a formal product of nabla with a scalar and a formal tensor product of nabla with a vector field. Namely,

\nabla y\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_{n}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\nabla\otimes\vec{v}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\vec{v}_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\vec{v}_{n}}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\vec{v}_{1}}{\partial x_{n}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\vec{v}_{n}}{\partial x_{n}}\end{bmatrix}.

In particular,

\mathbf{H}_{y}=\nabla\otimes\nabla y.

Proof.

To simplify the notation, we will henceforth skip the dependence on
\mathbf{x}
whenever it is clear from the context.

Since the formula (
2
) depends on
\mathbf{Q}
only through
\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
, without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathbf{Q}
is symmetric by taking its symmetric part

\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\right).

Thus we can write

\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{{=}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}&\mathbf{b}&\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}&d&e\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}&e&f\end{bmatrix}

as a symmetric block matrix, where
\mathbf{A}
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix,
\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}
are
n
-dimensional column vectors and
d,e,f
are scalars.

In terms of these new variables, equation (
1
) reads

(4)

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
y\\
1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{b}&\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}&d&e\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}&e&f\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
y\\
1\end{bmatrix}=dy^{2}+2\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+e\right)y+\left(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+f\right)

and hence its discriminant with respect to the variable
y
is itself a quadratic polynomial in
\mathbf{x}
given by

(5)

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}

\displaystyle=4\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+e\right)^{2}-4d\left(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+f\right)

\displaystyle=4\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}-d\mathbf{A}\right)\mathbf{x}+8\left(e\mathbf{b}^{\top}-d\mathbf{c}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}+4\left(e^{2}-df\right).

Denote its coefficients by

(6)

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}d\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}e\mathbf{b}-d\mathbf{c},\quad\nu\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}df-e^{2}

respectively, so that the equality

(7)

\Delta_{y}\mathrel{{=}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}}-4\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}+8\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-4\nu

holds.

Since the formula (
2
) is continuous in both
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{Q}
, it is enough to prove it for a dense subset of pairs
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Q})
. Hence without loss of generality we may assume that
d\neq 0
and
\Delta_{y}>0
. Solving (
4
) for
y
yields

y=\frac{-2\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+e\right)\pm\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}}{2d},

where the sign
\pm
depends on the selected branch of the square root function and is opposite to that in (
2
). Since the Hessian matrix does not depend on the linear part, we have

\pm\mathbf{H}_{y}=\frac{\nabla\otimes\nabla\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}}{2d}

with

(8)

|\pm\mathbf{H}_{y}|=2^{-n}d^{-n}\left|\nabla\otimes\nabla\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}\right|.

Now, for an arbitrary function
\Delta_{y}
, the Hessian matrix of
\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}
is given by

\nabla\otimes\nabla\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}=\nabla\otimes\frac{\nabla\Delta_{y}}{2{\Delta_{y}}^{1/2}}=\frac{2\Delta_{y}\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}-\nabla\Delta_{y}\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}}{4{\Delta_{y}}^{3/2}}

and thus is a linear combination of a square matrix
\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}
and a rank one matrix
\nabla\Delta_{y}\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}
. Its determinant can be computed using the following simple fact from linear algebra:

Lemma 2

(
[
1
, Theorem 18.1.1]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{R}
represent an
n\times n
matrix,
\mathbf{S}
an
n\times m
matrix,
\mathbf{T}
an
m\times m
matrix, and
\mathbf{U}
an
m\times n
matrix. If
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{T}
are non-singular, then

|\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{STU}|=|\mathbf{R}||\mathbf{T}||\mathbf{T}^{-1}+\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{S}|.

Recall that
\Delta_{y}
is a quadratic polynomial (
7
), in which case

\nabla\Delta_{y}=-8\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}+8\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}=-8\boldsymbol{\Lambda}.

Again, without loss of generality we may assume that
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is non-singular. Applying ?THM?
to

\mathbf{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}2\Delta_{y}\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y},\quad\mathbf{S}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-(\nabla\Delta_{y}),\quad\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{I}_{1},\quad\mathbf{U}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}

yields

(9)

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\otimes\nabla\sqrt{\Delta_{y}}\right|

\displaystyle=4^{-n}{\Delta_{y}}^{-3n/2}|\mathbf{R}|\left(1-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-n-1}{\Delta_{y}}^{-n/2-1}|\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}|\left(2\Delta_{y}-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})\right).

Further, without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathbf{A}
is non-singular. Applying ?THM?
to

\mathbf{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}d\mathbf{A},\quad\mathbf{S}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\mathbf{b},\quad\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{I}_{1},\quad\mathbf{U}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{b}^{\top}

yields

(10)

\displaystyle|\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}|

\displaystyle=(-8)^{n}|\mathbf{R}|\left(1-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)

\displaystyle=(-8)^{n}d^{n-1}|\mathbf{A}|\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right).

Moreover,

(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})=-8\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}+16\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-8\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}

and consequently

(11)

2\Delta_{y}-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})=-8\nu+8\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}

is a constant independent of
\mathbf{x}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=d\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}
is a linear combination of a square matrix
\mathbf{A}
and a rank one matrix
\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}
, its inverse can be computed using another simple fact from linear algebra:

Lemma 3

(
[
1
, Corollary 18.2.10]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{R}
represent an
n\times n
non-singular matrix, and let
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{u}
represent
n
-dimensional column vectors. Then
\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{s}\mathbf{u}^{\top}
is non-singular if and only if
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\neq-1
, in which case

\left(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{s}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}-\left(1+\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}.

Applying ?THM?
to

\mathbf{R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}d\mathbf{A},\quad\mathbf{s}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\mathbf{b},\quad\mathbf{u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{b}

yields

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{-1}+\left(1-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{-1}

\displaystyle=d^{-1}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}+\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=d^{-1}\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)^{-1}\left(\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\mathbf{A}^{-1}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right).

By combining (
10
) and (
11
) we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}|\left(2\Delta_{y}-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})\right)

\displaystyle\quad=(-8)^{n}d^{n-1}|\mathbf{A}|\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(-8\nu+8\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=(-8)^{n+1}d^{n-2}|\mathbf{A}|\cdot d\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\nu-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right).

Further, using (
12
) gives us

\displaystyle d\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\nu-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=d\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\nu-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\left(d\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle\quad=d\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\nu-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\left(\left(d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\mathbf{A}^{-1}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\mu},

which after applying the definitions (
6
) expands to

\displaystyle d^{3}f-d^{2}e^{2}-d^{2}f\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)+\cancel{de^{2}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}-\cancel{de^{2}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad+\cancel{e^{2}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}-\cancel{e^{2}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}+d^{2}e\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\cancel{de\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}+\cancel{de\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)}+d^{2}e\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\cancel{de\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)}+\cancel{de\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)}-d^{3}\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+d^{2}\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)-d^{2}\left(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\right)\left(\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right)

and then after cancellation can be put in the form
d^{2}\xi
, where

\xi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{vmatrix}d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}&e-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\\
e-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}&f-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\end{vmatrix}.

Thus we have eventually arrived at

(13)

\displaystyle|\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}|\left(2\Delta_{y}-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})\right)

\displaystyle=(-8)^{n+1}d^{n-2}|\mathbf{A}|\cdot d^{2}\xi

\displaystyle=(-8)^{n+1}d^{n}|\mathbf{A}|\xi.

On the other hand, since we assumed
\mathbf{A}
to be non-singular, by means of a finite sequence of elementary column operations we are able to eliminate
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{c}
from the last two columns of
\mathbf{Q}
. Indeed, both

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\end{bmatrix}

and

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\end{bmatrix}

are linear combinations of leading
n
columns of
\mathbf{Q}
with vectors of coefficients
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}
, respectively. Now, since the determinant is invariant under column addition, it follows that

|\mathbf{Q}|=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{b}&\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}&d&e\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}&e&f\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\top}&d-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}&e-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{c}^{\top}&e-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}&f-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}\end{vmatrix}.

Observe that the latter matrix is block-lower-triangular, in which case its determinant is simply equal to
|\mathbf{A}|\xi
. Therefore (
13
) reads

(14)

|\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y}|\left(2\Delta_{y}-(\nabla\Delta_{y})^{\top}(\nabla\otimes\nabla\Delta_{y})^{-1}(\nabla\Delta_{y})\right)=(-8)^{n+1}d^{n}|\mathbf{Q}|.

Finally, combining (
8
), (
9
) and (
14
) yields

\displaystyle|\pm\mathbf{H}_{y}|

\displaystyle=2^{-n}d^{-n}\cdot 2^{-n-1}{\Delta_{y}}^{-n/2-1}\cdot(-8)^{n+1}d^{n}|\mathbf{Q}|

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}{\Delta_{y}}^{-n/2-1}\cdot-2^{n+2}|\mathbf{Q}|,

which for symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is equivalent to (
2
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

Observe that if
|\mathbf{H}_{y}|
or
\Delta_{y}
takes a zero value anywhere on
\Omega
, then the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is singular and thus
|\mathbf{H}_{y}|
vanishes identically. Indeed, if
\Delta_{y}
vanishes identically, then
\mathbf{Q}
is a rank-one matrix, in which case
y
is affine and thus its Hessian determinant is zero. Othwerwise, as a non-zero quadratic polynomial,
\Delta_{y}
does not vanish on an open dense subset of
\Omega
, which implies that
|\mathbf{H}_{y}|
must be zero there. Hence, by continuity, it vanishes on the whole
\Omega
.

Remark
.

The sign
\mp
which depends on the selected branch of the square root function has any significance only for odd
n
.

Remark
.

Since the proof of ?THM?
was purely algebraic, the same result holds if we consider e.g. the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers instead of the field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers. However, note that both (
1
) and (
3
) should then be interpreted in the holomorphic sense, i.e. without conjugation.
