Statistical inverse learning problems with random observations
Abhishake, Tapio Helin, Nicole Mücke

Abstract
We provide an overview of recent progress in statistical inverse problems with random experimental design, covering both linear and nonlinear inverse problems. Different regularization schemes have been studied to produce robust and stable solutions. We discuss recent results in spectral regularization methods and regularization by projection, exploring both approaches within the context of Hilbert scales and presenting new insights particularly in regularization by projection. Additionally, we overview recent advancements in regularization using convex penalties. Convergence rates are analyzed in terms of the sample size in a probabilistic sense, yielding minimax rates in both expectation and probability. To achieve these results, the structure of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces is leveraged to establish minimax rates in the statistical learning setting. We detail the assumptions underpinning these key elements of our proofs. Finally, we demonstrate the application of these concepts to nonlinear inverse problems in pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) models, where the task is to predict changes in drug concentrations in patients.



1
Introduction

Statistical inverse learning refers to the process of inferring an unknown function
f^{\dagger}
by using statistical methods on point evaluations of a related function
g^{\dagger}
. These evaluations might be sparse or noisy, and the connection between
f^{\dagger}
and
g^{\dagger}
is established through an ill-posed mathematical model

A(f^{\dagger})=g^{\dagger},\qquad\text{for}\quad f^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}\quad\text{and}\quad g^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime},

(1.1)

where
A
can be a linear or nonlinear operator between a real separable Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and a reproducing kernel Hilber space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
represents the set of functions
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
, where
X
is a standard Borel space (the input space) and
\mathbb{R}
is a space of real numbers (the output space). The ill-posedness of (
1.1
) means the problem lacks a unique solution or is sensitive to data perturbations such as measurement noise.

In this work, we focus on random design regression in inverse problems, where the data
\mathbf{z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((x_{i},y_{i})}}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
emerges due to random observations

y_{i}=g^{\dagger}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,m,

(1.2)

where
\varepsilon_{i}
is the observational noise, and
m
denotes the sample size. Here, we assume that the random observations
(x_{i},y_{i})
are i.i.d. and follow the
unknown
probability distribution
\rho
, defined on the sample space
Z=X\times\mathbb{R}
. The key objective is to understand interplay between uncertainty of the design measure and optimal reconstruction rates.

Classical applications in inverse problems community often involve laboratory settings, where deterministic design of observational points
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}}}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
is possible. However, there is a growing literature considering inverse problems where observational design contains inherent uncertainties related to areas such as machine learning
[
13
,
42
,
67
]
and imaging
[
61
,
62
,
15
]
. Statistical inverse learning provides a principled approach to analyse concentration in such context.

The starting point in the inverse learning paradigm with random design is that we do not necessarily possess exact information regarding the distribution
\rho
. In this regard, our work differs from other avenues of random design regression in inverse problems such as
[
37
,
62
,
58
]
, where a specific (uniform) random design is assumed. Statistical inverse learning aims to assess how assumptions on
\rho
influence concentration of given statistical estimators.
That being said, it is known that under suitable regularity assumptions on
\rho
, the observation scheme in (
1.2
) is asymptotically equivalent (in a Le Cam sense of statistical experiments) to the full observation of
g^{\dagger}
corrupted by Gaussian noise with vanishing variance
[
69
]
.

The ill-posedness of inverse problems is usually mitigated by regularization techniques
[
40
]
which stabilize the reconstruction and prevent overfitting. These techniques introduce additional information or constraints to the problem to guide the solution towards a more reasonable and stable outcome. In this work, we overview recent concentration results for penalized least squares estimators

f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{f\in\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}}J_{\lambda}(f),\quad J_{\lambda}(f)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A(f)(x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}+\lambda G(f),

(1.3)

where the first term in
J_{\lambda}
is called the empirical error, the second term is the penalty term
G:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter that balances both terms. From the recent literature studying statistical inverse learning with random design, we overview three different regularization strategies: classical Tikhonov-type penalties, projection methods and general convex penalties. We separately consider results appearing for linear and non-linear forward mapping
A
.

Concentration results in inverse learning require assumptions limiting the complexity of the set of probability models that may produce the data
\mathbf{z}
.
Such assumptions are typically characterized by three conditions: First, a
source condition
limiting complexity of the source set (set of possible solutions
f^{\dagger}
), second, mapping properties of the forward operator, e.g.
smoothness condition
for linear operators and, third,
design measure

\nu
generating points
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\subset X^{m}
.
As we will see later, these conditions are often intertwined and the analysis of different penalties has been coupled with varying assumptions on these conditions. Here, with some cost of generality, we try to unify the exposition for clarity.

2.1
Problem setting

Statistical Model and Noise Assumption.

In the context of supervised inverse learning, the objective is to learn a function from random samples of input-output pairs
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Here, we assume that the input space
X
is a Polish space, such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the output space is denoted as
Y=\mathbb{R}
. The product space
Z=X\times\mathbb{R}
is equipped with a probability measure
\rho
, defined on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
X\times\mathbb{R}
.

The probability distribution
\rho
is only known through a training set
{\mathbf{z}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},y_{m})}}\right)\in Z^{m}
, consisting of pairs sampled independently and identically according to
\rho
. Given this training set
{\mathbf{z}}
, the goal in supervised inverse learning is to find an estimate
f_{{\bf z}}:X\to\mathbb{R}
so that, for a new unlabeled input
x\in X
, the value
A(f_{{\bf z}})(x)
serves as a reliable approximation of the true label
y
.

To formalize this objective, we introduce the notion of expected square loss for any measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
:

{\mathcal{E}}(f)=\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}(A(f)(x)-y)^{2}\,d\rho(x,y).

This loss function captures the discrepancy between the predicted values and the true labels, with pairs
(x,y)
that are more likely to be sampled exerting a greater contribution to the overall error.
The expected loss can be expressed in an alternative form, assuming that

\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}y^{2}\,d\rho(x,y)<\infty.

We recall a useful integral decomposition. Let
h:X\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. Then, the integral of
h
over
X\times\mathbb{R}
can be decomposed as

\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}h(x,y)\,d\rho(x,y)=\int_{X}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,y)\,d\rho(y|x)\,d\nu(x),

where
\nu
represents the marginal measure on
X
, and
\rho(\cdot|x)
is the conditional probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
given
x\in X
. Recall that the existence of
\rho(\cdot|x)
is ensured by our assumption on
Z
, see e.g.
[
71
]
.
Denoting by

g_{\rho}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\,d\rho(y|x),

the conditional mean function, the expected risk can be written as

\mathcal{E}(f)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{A(f)-g_{\rho}}}}\right\|^{2}_{\nu}+{\mathcal{E}}(g_{\rho}),

where we denote the
L^{2}
-norm on
X
by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{g}}}\right\|_{\nu}^{2}=\int_{X}|g(x)|^{2}\,d\nu(x)
.
Therefore, finding the minimizer of the expected risk is equivalent to identifying the minimizer of the first term on the right side in the above equation. In other words, the minimizers of expected risk typically coincide with the classical minimum norm solutions of inverse problems with the difference that data fidelity is weighted by the design measure. Moreover, unlike in statistical learning we are interested in providing convergence rates for the unknown
f
assuming some ground truth.

Remark 2.1
.

When there exists an element
f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{D}(A)~
such that
g_{\rho}=A(f_{\rho})
, then
f_{\rho}
is a minimizer of the expected risk. In the scenario of centered noise
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varepsilon d\rho(y|x)=0
, we obtain from the equations (
1.1
), (
1.2
) that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}yd\rho(y|x)=g^{\dagger}(x)=A(f^{\dagger})(x)
. Consequently, in this case for the injective operator
A
, the minimizer of the expected risk, denoted as
f_{\rho}
, is equal to
f^{\dagger}
.

In what follows, we state our assumptions on the true solution of the inverse problem (
1.1
) and the noise in (
1.2
), see also
[
13
]
.

Assumption 1

(True solution)
.

The conditional expectation of
y
given
x
, with respect to the probability distribution
\rho
, is guaranteed to exist almost surely (a.s.). Furthermore, there exists an element
f^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{D}(A)~
such that:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\,d\rho(y|x)=g^{\dagger}(x)=[A(f^{\dagger})](x),\text{ for all }x\in X.

The noise satisfies the following Bernstein condition:

Assumption 2

(Noise condition)
.

There exist positive constants
\Sigma>0
and
M>0
such that for any integer
l\geq 2

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y-[A(f^{\dagger})](x)}}\right\rvert^{l}d\rho(y|x)\leq\frac{1}{2}l!\Sigma^{2}M^{l-2},

\nu
-almost surely.

Assumption
2
characterizes the nature of the noise affecting the output
y
. This equation remains valid under various scenarios, including cases where the noise
\varepsilon
is bounded and follows either a Gaussian distribution or a sub-Gaussian distribution with zero mean and it is independent from
x

[
81
]
.

RKHS Structure.
We endow the space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
with an additional structure that allows us to exploit the rich theory of reproducing kernels.

Assumption 3

(RKHS Structure)
.

The space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is supposed to be a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) of functions
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
associated to a measurable bounded kernel
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
with
\kappa^{2}:=\sup\limits_{x\in X}K(x,x)<\infty.
We additionally assume that
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
can be continuously embedded into
\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\nu)
.
We denote the inclusion by
I_{\nu}:{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\nu)
.

We recall from
[
75
]
, Chapter 4, that this assumption is equivalent to assuming that for any
x\in X
, the evaluation functional

\displaystyle S_{x}:{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto S_{x}(g):=g(x)

(2.1)

is continuous. By Riesz’s representation theorem, there exists a unique
F^{\prime}_{x}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}

such that
g(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle g,F^{\prime}_{x}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}
and

K(x,x^{\prime})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle F^{\prime}_{x},F^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}\;,\quad x,x^{\prime}\in X.

In particular, for any
g\in\operatorname{Im}(A)
, we may write

g(x)=[A(f)](x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle A(f),F^{\prime}_{x}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}},

for some
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
and for all
x\in X
. If
A
is linear, we even have

[A(f)](x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,F_{x}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},\quad F_{x}:=A^{*}F^{\prime}_{x}\;.

(2.2)

From standard theory, we know that the feature map
x\mapsto F_{x}
gives rise to another
reproducing kernel

K_{A}(x,x^{\prime}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle F_{x},F_{x^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in X,

depending on the operator
A
. The associated RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}_{K_{A}}
coincides with the image of
{\mathcal{H}}
under
A
:

{\mathcal{H}}_{K_{A}}=\operatorname{Im}(A)
and
A
acts as a partial isometry, see
[
13
, Proposition 2.2]
, for an extended discussion.
Hence, in the linear case, we can forget about any structure on the space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
and consider
A
as an operator from
{\mathcal{H}}

to
{\mathcal{H}}_{K_{A}}
by assuming continuity of
S_{x}
on
\operatorname{Im}(A)
only, for any
x\in X
. We formulate
this as an independent assumption and will analyze this case in Section
3
more deeply.

Assumption 4

(Linear Case)
.

The operator
A:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}:=\operatorname{Im}(A)
is linear. Moreover, the evaluation functional

S_{x}:{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
2.1
) is continuous for all
x\in X
and the
map
x\mapsto S_{x}g
is measurable for all
g\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
.

We emphasize once more that in the general non-linear case in Assumption
3
, the kernel
K
and thus the RKHS do not depend on the
operator
A
, while in the linear case, the operator
A
is explicitly used to construct the kernel
K_{A}
.

Remark 2.2

(Equivalence with Classical Kernel Learning Setting)
.

Under Assumption
4
, the inverse problem (
1.1
) is naturally equivalent to the classical kernel learning problem (the
direct
problem) in non-parametric regression in

[
4
,
18
,
34
]
, that follows the model

y_{i}={g^{\dagger}}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}.

The goal is to estimate the function
g^{\dagger}
. Kernel methods
posit
that
g^{\dagger}
belongs to some reproducing
kernel Hilbert space and construct an estimate
\hat{g}
of
g^{\dagger}
based
on the observed data. The reconstruction error
\hat{g}-g^{\dagger}
is analyzed in
L^{2}(\nu)
-norm
or in RKHS-norm. In the setting of Assumption
4
and given the model (
1.1
), the linear operator
A
is a partial isometry and hence, defining
\hat{f}:=A^{+}\hat{g}
,
f^{\dagger}:=A^{+}g^{\dagger}
with pseudoinverse
A^{+}
gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f^{\dagger}-\hat{f}}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{g^{\dagger}-\hat{g}}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}},

see
[
13
]
.
Hence, a bound established for the direct learning
setting in the sense of the RKHS-norm reconstruction also applies to the
inverse problem reconstruction error.
Furthermore, the eigenvalue decay conditions and the source conditions involving the operator
T_{\nu}
introduced in Section
2.3
are, via the same isometry, equivalent to similar conditions involving the kernel integral operator in the direct learning setting, as considered, for instance, in
[
4
,
18
,
34
]
. It follows that estimates in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
-norm
available from those references are directly applicable to the inverse learning setting.

Remark 2.3
.

When employing kernel methods in statistical inverse learning problem (
1.1
), one tackles the ensuing minimization problem:

\inf\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((Af)(x)-y}}\right)^{2}d\rho(x,y).

Given Assumption
4
for
(Af)(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,F_{x}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, the previous expression can be reformulated as:

\inf\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,F_{x}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-y}}\right)^{2}d\rho(x,y).

By introducing an alternative probability measure
\rho^{\prime}(F_{x},y)=\rho(x,y)
, the above simplifies to the form:

\inf\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\int_{X\times\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,F_{x}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-y}}\right)^{2}d\rho^{\prime}(F_{x},y)=\inf\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\int_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,\xi}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-y}}\right)^{2}d\rho^{\prime}(\xi,y).

Therefore, minimizing the considered statistical inverse problem (
1.2
) in the linear case is equivalent to solving the following problem:

y_{i}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,\xi_{i}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

(2.3)

2.2
Hilbert Scales

In the context of regularized statistical inverse problems, the utilization of a Hilbert scale enables a delicate balance between the fidelity of the estimate to the observed data and the smoothness of the estimated function
[
60
,
36
]
.
Hilbert scales, comprised of a nested sequence of Hilbert spaces, facilitate systematic control over the smoothness and complexity of functions. Each space in the sequence is compactly embedded in the next, offering a structured approach to managing these properties.
Pioneering works by Tikhonov
[
78
,
79
]
, extended the regularization process to a more general optimization problem, minimizing a functional of the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{A(f)-g^{\dagger}}}}\right\|^{2}_{\nu}+\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{Lf}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}\,,\quad f\in{\mathcal{D}}(L)\;,

where
L:{\mathcal{D}}(L)\to{\mathcal{H}}
is an unbounded operator with domain
{\mathcal{D}}(L)
. A notable instance occurs when
L
is a differential operator, effectively enforcing smoothness in the approximation, given that the search space is a subset of
{\mathcal{D}}(L)
.

Remarkably, convergence of these regularized solutions can be achieved in the original
{\mathcal{H}}
-norm even when no solution of (
1.1
) is an element of
{\mathcal{D}}(L)
. In this case, we are in the setting of
oversmoothing
. We now give a rigorous definition:

Definition 2.4

(Hilbert Scale)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
and let
L:{\mathcal{D}}(L)\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a densely defined unbounded self-adjoint positive operator, with
{\mathcal{D}}(L)\subset{\mathcal{H}}
. We set

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}:=\bigcap_{a\in\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{D}}(L^{a})
and define on
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
the inner product and norm

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}}}\right\rangle_{a}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle L^{a}f_{1},L^{a}f_{2}}}\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\;,\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f}}}\right\|_{a}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{L^{a}f}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;.

The Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{a}
is defined as the completion of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
with respect to the norm
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\cdot}}}\right\|_{a}
. The scale
({\mathcal{H}}_{a})_{a\in\mathbb{R}}
is called the
Hilbert scale induced by
L
.

Note that by spectral theory, the powers
L^{a}
are defined on
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Moreover, for any
-\infty<a<a^{\prime}<\infty
, we have
{\mathcal{H}}_{a^{\prime}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{a}
, where the embedding is dense and continuous, see
[
36
, Section 8.4]
for mathematical details.

In many instances, it is assumed that the scale of Hilbert spaces corresponds to a scale of Sobolev spaces, and the smoothing properties of the underlying operator
A
are assessed in relation to this scale. Additionally, the smoothness of the solution is characterized by assuming it belongs to a specific space within this scale.
Additional examples of Hilbert Scales pertinent to learning in RKHSs can be found in the work of
[
59
]
.

There has been growing interest in regularization within Hilbert scales for statistical inverse problems, particularly in the context of the Bayesian approach
[
39
,
1
]
. Recently, this approach gained attention in statistical learning and statistical inverse learning
[
65
,
68
,
59
]
.

2.3
A Priori Assumptions

In classical statistical learning theory for non-parametric regression, the convergence speed of an estimator to the target function can be exceedingly slow as the sample size grows, a phenomenon known as the
No Free Lunch Theorem
. To address this issue, the set of all data-generating distributions is indirectly limited, for more efficient learning. A priori assumptions play a critical role in this process by enabling the inclusion of prior knowledge and information, ultimately leading to faster rates of convergence.

In what follows, we incorporate prior knowledge in two ways: First, we gauge the
degree of ill-posedness
of the inverse problem by assuming a certain decay behavior of the eigenvalues of a second moment operator associated to
A
. The faster the decay, the more ill-posed the problem is. Secondly, we impose a smoothness/ sparsity constraint on the target function
f^{\dagger}
. The smoother the target, the easier the reconstruction is. We now provide a formalization of both concepts utilizing the following covariance operator related to feature maps.

Definition 2.5

(Uncentered Covariance Operator)
.

Let
\Phi:X\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a measurable feature map with

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\Phi(x)}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}]<\infty.

(2.4)

The
uncentered covariance operator
associated to
\Phi
is defined to be the operator

T_{\nu}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
with

T_{\nu}:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\Phi(x)\otimes\Phi(x)]=\int_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\cdot,\Phi(x)}}\right\rangle\Phi(x)\;d\nu(x).

The operator
T_{\nu}
is positive, self-adjoint and compact, in particular trace class by (
2.4
) with
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma_{j}<\infty
, where
(\sigma_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the decreasing sequence of eigenvalues of
T_{\nu}
.

Depending on the context, linear or non-linear problems, we will consider different feature maps
\Phi
, giving rise to different operators
T_{\nu}
. However, the non-linear approach contains the linear approach as a special case, as exemplified below.

Example 2.6

(Linear Case)
.

If
A:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is linear, we let
\Phi(x):=A^{*}F_{x}^{\prime}=F_{x}
, see (
2.2
). Setting
B_{\nu}=I_{\nu}A
, we also have the alternative representation of the covariance operator
T_{\nu}=B_{\nu}^{*}B_{\nu}
, see e.g.
[
13
,
18
]
.

Example 2.7

(Non-Linear Case)
.

Assume that
A:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
is Fréchet differentiable at
f^{\dagger}
with derivative
A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger}):{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
. We let
\Phi(x):=A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})^{*}F^{\prime}_{x}
. Setting
B_{\nu}=I_{\nu}A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})
, we again obtain
T_{\nu}:=B_{\nu}^{*}B_{\nu}
. Note that we recover the linear case since
A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})=A
if
A
is linear.

In our given examples we observe that the second moment operator
T_{\nu}
depends on both, the marginal distribution
\nu
and the operator
A
itself, through the feature map
\Phi
. The properties of
T_{\nu}
are crucial for deriving fast rates.

Remark 2.8
.

(Normal Equation)
Let
A
be a linear operator, and let
f^{\dagger}
be the expected risk minimizer, i.e., the least-squares solution of the model (
1.1
) given by

f^{\dagger}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{H}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{B_{\nu}f-g^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\nu}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{f\in{\mathcal{H}}}{\mathcal{E}}(f)\;.

This minimization is equivalent to the validity of the Gaussian normal equation
[
36
]

T_{\nu}f^{\dagger}=B_{\nu}^{*}g^{\dagger}.

(2.5)

Eigenvalue Decay.

We impose a-priori assumptions on the set of marginal distributions on
X
by assuming a decay rate for the eigenvalues of the covariance operator
T_{\nu}
, reflecting the severity of the ill-posed nature of (
1.1
).

Assumption 5
.

Suppose the eigenvalues
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{i}}}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of the integral operator
T_{\nu}
follow a polynomial decay: For fixed positive constants
\alpha,\beta
and
b<1
, we asume that

(i)

\sigma_{i}\leq\beta i^{-\frac{1}{b}}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

(ii)

\alpha i^{-\frac{1}{b}}\leq\sigma_{i}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

(iii)

\exists\gamma>0,~i_{0}\geq 1~~\text{s.t.}~~\sigma_{i}\leq 2^{\gamma}\sigma_{2i}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Since the covariance operator
T_{\nu}
depends on the marginal
\nu
, Assumption
5
imposes a restriction on the class of marginal distributions
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
on
X
. We therefore introduce the following sub-classes:

{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{poly}}^{<}(\beta,b):=\{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\sigma_{i}\leq\beta i^{-\frac{1}{b}}\;\;\forall\;\;i\in\mathbb{N}\}\;,

(2.6)

{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{poly}}^{>}(\alpha,b):=\{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\sigma_{i}\geq\alpha i^{-\frac{1}{b}}\;\;\forall\;\;i\in\mathbb{N}\}\;,

(2.7)

{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{str}}^{>}(\gamma):=\{\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\sigma_{i}\leq 2^{\gamma}\sigma_{2i}\;\;\forall\;\;i\geq i_{0}\;,\;\;i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\}\;.

(2.8)

Remark 2.9

(Effective Dimension)
.

In statistical learning theory, assumptions about marginal distributions are expressed more broadly using the
effective dimension
, also known as
statistical dimension
, representing the effective number of degrees of freedom. In statistical modeling, particularly with high-dimensional data, the effective dimension denotes the parameters or features significantly influencing data variation. This is crucial when the actual data dimension is high, yet numerous features may be redundant or carry little information. For
\lambda>0
, it may be defined as

\mathcal{N}(\lambda):=\operatorname{Tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((T_{\nu}+\lambda I)^{-1}T_{\nu}}}\right)\;.

Since
T_{\nu}
is trace class and bounded, we immediately get

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)\leq\kappa^{2}\lambda^{-1}\;.

However, an upper bound for the decay rate of the eigenvalues of
T_{\nu}
implies an even sharper bound for
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda)
. Specifically, for any
\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{poly}}^{<}(\beta,b)
we find

\mathcal{N}(\lambda)\leq\frac{\beta b}{b-1}\lambda^{-b},

see
[
18
, Proposition 3]
. Note that in our context of inverse learning, bounds on
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda)
also depend on
A
, incorporating properties of the inverse problem into the statistical dimension naturally.

A-Priori Smoothness.
Another way to integrate prior knowledge into solving inverse problems is by means of
(approximate) source conditions
. In statistical inverse problems, a source condition describes the regularity or smoothness of the true solution or unknown parameter being estimated.
The choice of a source condition is motivated by the specific characteristics of the problem at hand and the available information about the true solution.

In the context of regularization of linear ill-posed inverse problems, the a-priori-smoothness is expressed in terms of powers of the covariance operator
T_{\nu}
, to be considered as a smoothing operator:

Assumption 6

(Source condition)
.

Let
r>0
,
R_{0}>0
. We assume that the solution
f^{\dagger}
is a member of the class
\Omega(r,R_{0},T_{\nu})
with

\Omega(r,R_{0},T_{\nu}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}:f=T_{\nu}^{r}v\text{ for some }v\in{\mathcal{H}}\text{ with }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{v}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R_{0}}}\right\}.

In the context of regularization schemes within Hilbert Scales, the source condition is considered in terms of the known unbounded operator
L
, which is involved in the regularization approach.

Assumption 7

(General Source Condition)
.

For
s>0
and
R_{0}>0
, we let

f^{\dagger}\in\Omega(s,R_{0},L^{-1}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}:f=L^{-s}v\text{ for some }v\in{\mathcal{H}}\text{ with }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R_{0}}}\right\}.

Source conditions are generally difficult to verify in practice or sometimes may not be pre-specified. In this case, the concept of
distance functions
can be employed to quantify the deviation from an assumed level of smoothness, serving as a benchmark. This benchmark can be chosen by the user. For a given
benchmark smoothness

p\geq 1
, we define the corresponding distance function as specified below.

Definition 2.10

(Distance Function)
.

Let
R>0
,
p\geq 1
and
L
be a generator of a Hilbert scale. We define the
distance function

d_{p}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle d_{p}(R)=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{L(f-f^{\dagger})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}:f=L^{-p}u\text{ for some }u\in{\mathcal{H}}\text{ with }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R}}\right\}.

(2.9)

The distance function is positive, decreasing, convex, and continuous for all values of
0\leq R<1
. Hence, the minimizer exists. As
R
approaches infinity, the distance function approaches
0
, as detailed in
[
44
]
. Note that the distance function can be bounded explicitly in terms of
R_{0}>0
provided
f^{\dagger}
satisfies a more general source condition Assumption
7
. More precisely, for
s<p
we have

d_{p}(R)\leq(R_{0})^{\frac{p-1}{p-s}}R^{\frac{1-s}{p-s}},

(2.10)

see
[
45
, Theorem 5.9]
. If
f^{\dagger}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
, i.e.
f^{\dagger}=L^{-p}u
for some
u\in{\mathcal{H}}
, then obviously
d_{p}(R)=0
.

In the Hilbert scale scenario when the source condition is considered in terms of
L^{-1}
, the following Link condition is essential to transfer the smoothness in terms of the covariance operator
L^{-1}T_{\nu}L^{-1}
to the smoothness with respect to the operator
L^{-1}
and vice versa.

Assumption 8

(Link condition)
.

For some
p\geq 1
,
0<a<\frac{1}{2}
and
1\leq c<\infty
we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{L^{-p}u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{(L^{-1}T_{\nu}L^{-1})^{ap}u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq c^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{L^{-p}u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad u\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.11)

Remark 2.11
.

The Weyl Monotonicity Theorem
[
7
, Cor. III.2.3]
establishes a relation between the eigenvalues
\sigma_{j}(L^{-1})
and
\sigma_{j}((L^{-1}T_{\nu}L^{-1})^{a})
, namely,

\sigma_{j}(L^{-1})\leq\sigma_{j}((L^{-1}T_{\nu}L^{-1})^{a})\leq\sigma_{j}(\beta L^{-1})\;,\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, we observe that when the eigenvalues of
L^{-1}
exhibit a decay rate, say,
{\mathcal{O}}(j^{-w})
for some
w>0
, the eigenvalues of
L^{-1}T_{\nu}L^{-1}
will follow a decay rate of
{\mathcal{O}}(j^{-\frac{w}{a}})
. What is more, one can show that
T_{\nu}
has a spectral decay
{\mathcal{O}}(j^{(2-\frac{1}{a})w})
.

Class of Models.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
and denote by
{\mathcal{K}}(\Omega)
the set of regular conditional distributions
\rho(\cdot|\cdot)
on
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R})\times X
such that (
1
) and (
2
) hold for some
f^{\dagger}\in\Omega
. Given
r>0
,
R_{0}>0
, the class of models considered in most cases below is defined by

{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}:=\{\rho(dx,dy)=\rho(dy|x)\nu(dx)\;:\;\rho(\cdot|\cdot)\in{\mathcal{K}}(\Omega)\;,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\}\;,

(2.12)

where
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
specifies the admissible design measures. This varies depending on the regularization method, see e.g. definitions
in (
2.6
)-(
2.8
) utilized in spectral regularization. The variable
\theta
represents parametrization for data-generating distributions and our study focuses on finding parameter choice rules that yield uniform concentration rates over the domain of
\theta
. For the rigorous definition of this idea, see next section for minimax optimality in learning. In practise, we mostly consider
\theta
as the pair
(\Gamma,R_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, i.e. the noise variance and a priori norm bound to
f^{\dagger}
, but other parametrizations could be considered as well.

We note that while
{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}
is specified by two main elements, the source set
\Omega
and the admissible design measures
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
, their definitions are often intertwined and are both often related to the mapping properties of the forward mapping.
In particular, for the convex penalties we will not specify
\Omega
and
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
but instead consider
{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}
generated by a set of pairs
(f^{\dagger},\nu)
satisfying suitable conditions.

2.4
Minimax optimality

In statistical (inverse) learning theory, the speed at which a learning algorithm
(\mathbf{z},\lambda)\mapsto f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}
converges to the true underlying model as the sample size increases is referred to as the
rate of convergence
.
It quantifies how quickly the learned model approaches the correct solution as more data becomes available.
The rate of convergence is a crucial concept in assessing the efficiency and performance of statistical learning methods. A faster rate of convergence implies that the estimator requires fewer observations to provide a more accurate estimate or model.
Typically, rates are discussed by means of probabilistic tail inequalities, involving the confidence level
\eta\in(0,1]
and the sample size
m\in\mathbb{N}
. More specifically, they take the form

\rho^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq Ca_{m}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\eta}}}\right)}}\right\}\geq 1-\eta.

(2.13)

Here,
\rho^{m}
is the joint distribution, the function
m\mapsto a_{m}
is a positive decreasing function and describes the specific rate as
m\to\infty
. Recall from the previous section that we restrict the class of admissible distributions to a subclass
{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}
, where
\theta
is a parameter belonging to a parameter space
\Theta
. Then, (
2.13
) allows to write

\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\rho^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\tau a_{m}}}\right\}\leq e^{-\frac{\tau}{C}}\;,

with
\tau=C\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\eta}}}\right)
.
Hence,

\lim\limits_{\tau\to\infty}\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\rho^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\tau a_{m}}}\right\}=0.

Integration immediately leads to a bound in expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}=

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}>t}}\right)dt}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{p}a_{m},

for some
C_{p}<\infty
, which implies

\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}}{a_{m}}\leq C_{p}<\infty.

This motivates the following definitions. We refer to
[
4
,
18
,
13
]
for more details.

Definition 2.12

(Upper Rate of Convergence)
.

A family of sequences
(a_{m,\theta})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
of positive numbers is called an
upper rate of convergence
in
\mathscr{L}^{p}(X,\nu)
for the reconstruction error, over the family of models

({\mathcal{M}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, for the sequence of estimated solutions
(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda_{m,\theta}})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
, using regularization parameters
(\lambda_{m,\theta})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
, if

\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda_{m,\theta}}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}}{a_{m,\theta}}<\infty.

Definition 2.13

(Weak and Strong Minimax Lower Rate of Convergence)
.

A family of sequences
(a_{m,\theta})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
of positive numbers is called a
weak minimax lower rate of convergence
in
\mathscr{L}^{p}(X,\nu)
for the reconstruction error, over the family of models
({\mathcal{M}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, if

\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}\inf\limits_{f_{\cdot}}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z}}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}}{a_{m,\theta}}>0.

where the infimum is taken over all estimators, i.e., measurable mappings
f_{\cdot}:(X\times\mathbb{R})^{m}\to\mathcal{H}
. It is called a
strong minimax lower rate of convergence
in
L^{p}
if

\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\liminf\limits_{m\to\infty}\inf\limits_{f_{\cdot}}\sup\limits_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z}}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}}{a_{m,\theta}}>0.

Definition 2.14

(Minimax Optimal Rate of Convergence)
.

The sequence of estimated solutions
(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda_{m,\theta}})_{m}
using the regularization parameters
(\lambda_{m},\theta)_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
is called
weak/strong minimax optimal
in
\mathscr{L}^{p}(X,\nu)
for the interpolation norm of parameter
s\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, over the model family
({\mathcal{M}}_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
, with rate of convergence given by the sequence
(a_{m,\theta})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
, if the latter is a weak/strong minimax lower rate as well as an upper rate for
(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda_{m,\theta}})_{(m,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\Theta}
.

3
Linear problems

In this Section we present the theory for regularization of linear inverse learning problems. We begin with introducing a suitable discretization strategy based on random sampling that is able to provide practical implementations of our regularized solutions. We move forward with presenting three major classes of regularization methods:
spectral regularization
,
regularization by projection
, and more generally
convex regularization
. For each of these methods we state convergence results and discuss their minimax optimality.

3.1
Discretization by Random Sampling

Discretization is a crucial step in solving inverse problems because it transforms complex, continuous mathematical models into a computationally tractable format that can be approached using numerical methods. In our framework, we employ the random sample
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((x_{i},y_{i})}}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
to construct a discretization of the operator
A
in the following way:
We define the

sampling operator

S_{\mathbf{x}}:{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
by

S_{\mathbf{x}}g:=(g(x_{1}),...,g(x_{m})).

We further define
B_{\mathbf{x}}:=S_{\mathbf{x}}A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, yielding an empirical approximation to
B_{\nu}
and
T_{\mathbf{x}}:=B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}B_{\mathbf{x}}
, yielding an approximation to the covariance
T_{\nu}
.

The empirical expected risk based on the sample is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{z}}(f)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([A(f)](x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{B_{\mathbf{x}}f-\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.1)

Minimizing
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{z}}
amounts to finding
f_{{\bf z}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
B_{\mathbf{x}}f_{{\bf z}}=\mathbf{y}
, which can be seen as a noisy, randomly discretized version of (
1.1
) and one would expect that with increasing sample size, model (
1.1
) will be better approximated by its empirical counterpart. However, it is not straightforward to measure the accuracy of the random approximation as the ranges of the operators defining the inverse problems are different. Considering the normal equations for both problems

T_{\nu}f^{\dagger}=B_{\nu}^{*}g^{\dagger},\quad T_{\mathbf{x}}f_{{\bf z}}=B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\mathbf{y}

(3.2)

suggest to compare both inverse problems by bounding the norms

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{T_{\nu}-T_{\mathbf{x}}}}}\right\|,\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{B_{\nu}^{*}g^{\dagger}-B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Since both norms are random quantities, estimating the perturbation due to random noise and random sampling requires probabilistic tools, such as concentration of measure results, see
[
18
,
34
]
. We provide a result that shows that
T_{\nu}
and
B_{\nu}^{*}g^{\dagger}
can be well approximated by their respective empirical
counterparts.

Proposition 3.1

(Lemma 9 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1]
and suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. With probability at least
1-\eta
we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{T_{\nu}-T_{\mathbf{x}}}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\log(4/\eta),

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{B_{\nu}^{*}g^{\dagger}-B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{M}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{\Sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}}}\right)\log(4/\eta)\;.

Remark 3.2

(Measure theoretic point of view)
.

If we let
\hat{\nu}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{j}}
denote the empirical measure of the input data, we can identify
\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\hat{\nu})
with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, equipped with the inner product
\langle{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}\rangle_{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}
. It is then straightforward to see that
the operators
B_{\nu}
,
T_{\nu}
reduce to their respective empirical counterparts when replacing
\nu
with
\hat{\nu}
, i.e.
B_{\hat{\nu}}=B_{\mathbf{x}}
and
T_{\hat{\nu}}=T_{\mathbf{x}}
.

3.2
Optimality of spectral regularization methods

Regularization strategies are essential in solving inverse problems to address issues related to ill-posedness, instability, and noise sensitivity in the input data. Regularization also helps to prevent overfitting by penalizing overly complex solutions, encouraging smoother or simpler solutions that are more likely to generalize well.

Common regularization strategies in the context of inverse problems include Tikhonov regularization (also known as ridge regression), total variation regularization, and sparse regularization. These techniques add mathematical constraints or penalties to the optimization problem, promoting solutions that are both faithful to the data and have desirable properties such as smoothness or sparsity. The choice of regularization method depends on the specific characteristics of the problem and the desired properties of the solution. In what follows, we will focus on spectral methods.

3.2.1
Spectral regularization methods

We give now the general definition of spectral methods, followed by classical examples.

Definition 3.3

(Spectral regularization)
.

We say that a family of functions
\{g_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
with

g_{\lambda}:[0,\kappa^{2}]\to\mathbb{R}
is a
spectral regularization scheme
or
spectral filter
if there exist constants
D<\infty
,
E<\infty
, and
\gamma_{0}<\infty
such that for all
\lambda>0

(i)

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert tg_{\lambda}(t)}}\right\rvert\leq D
,

(ii)

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert g_{\lambda}(t)}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{E}{\lambda}
and

(iii)

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert r_{\lambda}(t)}}\right\rvert\leq\gamma_{0}
, where
r_{\lambda}(t)=1-g_{\lambda}(t)t
.

Moreover, the maximal
q
satisfying the condition

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert r_{\lambda}(t)}}\right\rvert t^{q}\leq\gamma_{q}\lambda^{q},

for some constant
\gamma_{q}
and any
\lambda>0

is said to be the
qualification
of the regularization scheme
g_{\lambda}
.

Example 3.4

(Spectral cut-off)
.

Consider the spectral cut-off or truncated singular value decomposition (TSVD) defined by

g_{\lambda}(t)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}t^{-1}&,\quad\text{if}\quad t\geq\lambda;\\
0&,\quad\text{if}\quad t<\lambda\end{array}}}\right.

The qualification
q
could be any positive number and
D=E=\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{q}=1
.

Example 3.5

(Explicit regularization: (Iterated) ridge regression)
.

For
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the iterated Tikhonov regularization is given by the function

g_{\lambda}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\lambda^{i-1}(\lambda+t)^{-i}=\frac{1}{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{l}}{(\lambda+t)^{l}}}}\right)

In this case we have
q=l
,
D=\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{q}=1
, and
E=l
. For
l=1
, this scheme corresponds to the Tikhonov regularization or ridge regression.

Example 3.6

(Implicit regularization: Gradient decent)
.

Gradient descent with constant stepsize
\eta>0
, a.k.a. Landweber iteration, to the function
g_{\lambda}(t)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{u}\eta(1-\eta t)^{u-k}
with
\eta\in(0,\kappa^{2}]
with the choice
\lambda=(\eta u)^{-1}
. Here,
D=E=\gamma_{0}=1
and the qualification
q
can be any positive number with
\gamma_{q}=1
if
0<q\leq 1
and
\gamma_{q}=q^{q}
if
q>1
.

3.2.2
Classical Error Bounds

Equation (
3.2
) suggests to define our regularized empirical solution as given below.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\{g_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
be a family of filter functions defined on the spectrum of
T_{\nu}
and let
((x_{j},y_{j}))_{j=1}^{m}
be an i.i.d. sample. The regularized empirical estimator for the solution to the linear inverse problem (
1.1
) is defined by

f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}=g_{\lambda}(T_{\mathbf{x}})B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\mathbf{y}.

(3.3)

In what follows we present the rates of convergence for the reconstruction error for the estimator defined above.

Theorem 3.8

(Blanchard et al.
[
13
]
)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
(i),
6
are satisfied. Suppose further that
\lambda\geq m^{-\frac{1}{b+1}}
and the qualification is
q\geq r
.
For all
\eta\in(0,1]
, the regularized solution
f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}
(
3.3
) satisfies with probability at least
1-\eta
the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq

\displaystyle\;C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{0}\lambda^{r}+\frac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{m}\lambda^{\frac{b+1}{2}}}+\frac{R_{0}}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{M}{m\lambda}}}\right)\log^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{8}{\eta}}}\right),

(3.4)

where
C
depends on all model parameters
r,~\kappa,~\gamma_{0},\gamma_{q},~D,~E,~b,~\beta
.

Note that this upper bound is given in high probability.
Deriving the final rate of convergence requires to carefully select the regularization parameter
\lambda
, depending on the sample size and the a-priori assumptions. Setting
\theta=(\Sigma,R_{0})
, a straightforward calculation shows that the choice

\lambda_{m,\theta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Sigma}{R_{0}\sqrt{m}}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2r+b+1}}

(3.5)

equalizes the first and second term of (
3.4

3.2.3
Error Bounds in Hilbert Scales

Now, we analyze the regularization schemes in Hilbert Scales for the considered statistical inverse problem (

Regularization by projection is a classical method that has been studied widely

To set the stage, we assume that there is a sequence of nested subspaces of

Definition 3.16

Consider a series of finite-dimensional subspaces of

Given training data with

3.4
Convex regularization

Convex variational regularization techniques are of interest in various inverse problems, where certain features of the unknown, such as edges in imaging or sparsity in signal processing, needs to be emphasized.
In this section, we deviate briefly from the earlier setup and assume that the forward linear operator
A
maps a separable Banach space
{\mathcal{B}}
to a RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
.

Below, we discuss the characteristics of regulated solutions
f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}
obtained through the variational problem

f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{2m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A(f)(x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}+\lambda G(f)}}\right\}

(3.20)

Here,
G:{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\infty}}\right\}
represents a convex functional. Let us point out that penalties such as

G(f)=\frac{1}{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{B}}}^{p}

(3.21)

have been studied in statistical learning (see e.g.
[
57
,
76
]
).
However, in the inverse problems context much less is known and to the best of our knowledge their study with random design is so far limited to
[
14
,
5
]
.

The first article
[
14
]
leverages ideas from deterministic convex regularization theory, where Bregman distances
[
6
]
have become popular during last decade. While those techniques extend to more general penalties, here, we restrict to the
p
-homogeneous functional (
3.21
). Due to strict convexity for
p\geq 1
, the regularized solution
f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}
is unique. In addition, the mappings

(f,{\bf z})\mapsto\frac{1}{2m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A(f)(x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}+\lambda G(f)\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{z}}\mapsto f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}

(3.22)

are continuous and measurable, respectively (see comment in
[
14
, Sec. 4]
). Therefore, the learning problem in (
3.20
) becomes well-posed. Notice that
[
14
]
assumes more general range (Banach space) of operator
A
with similar continuous pointwise evaluation functionals. As we limit to RKHS-valued range, this simplifies the assumptions needed in
[
14
]
.

The optimality criterion associated with (
3.20
) is given by

B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}(B_{\mathbf{x}}f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}-\mathbf{y})+\lambda r_{{\bf z},\lambda}=0

(3.23)

for
r_{{\bf z},\lambda}\in\partial G(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda})
, where the dual operator
B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
maps from the RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
to the Banach dual
{\mathcal{B}}^{*}
and
\partial G
denotes the subdifferential set

\partial G(f)=\{r\in{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\;|\;G(f)-G(\tilde{f})\leq\langle r,f-\tilde{f}\rangle_{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\times{\mathcal{B}}}\;\text{for all}\;\tilde{f}\in{\mathcal{B}}\}.

For
r_{f}\in\partial G(f)
and
r_{\tilde{f}}\in\partial G(f)
we define symmetric Bregman distance between
f
and
\tilde{f}
as

D_{G}^{r_{f},r_{\tilde{f}}}(f,\tilde{f})=\langle r_{f}-r_{\tilde{f}},f-\tilde{f}\rangle_{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\times{\mathcal{B}}}

Following
[
14
]
we restrict to the case
1<p<2
. In consequence, the subdifferential elements
r_{f}\in\partial G(f)
are unique and in what follows we drop the dependence of Bregman distance on the subgradients and simplify notation writing
D_{G}(f,\tilde{f})
.

The crux of analysing the non-quadratic objective functional in (
3.20
) is that it does not give rise to similar explicit formula for the minimizer
f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}
as e.g. in the Tikhonov case in (
3.3
). However, as is shown in
[
14
]
, the Bregman-based reconstruction error can be separated into bias-variance type of decomposition. The bias is represented by the term

{\mathcal{R}}(\beta,{\mathbf{z}};f^{\dagger})=\inf_{{\bf w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G^{\ast}(R_{0}-B_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*}{\bf w})+\frac{\beta}{2m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{{\bf w}}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right],

(3.24)

where
G^{\ast}
is the convex conjugate of
G
.
The functional
{\mathcal{R}}
is closely related to the distance function in Definition
2.10
. However, notice carefully that unlike the distance function, the functional
{\mathcal{R}}
is dependent on the random sampling. Therefore, conditions for the source set
\Theta
and set of design measures
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
become intertwined without further assumptions on the structure of
G
.
Moreover, the error variance is represented by
G^{\ast}(B_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*}{\bf\epsilon})
. The general concentration result is given as follows:

Theorem 3.19

(
[
14
, Thm. 4.11]
)
.

Let
G
be defined in (
3.21
) and let
{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta}
,
\theta=(\Sigma,R_{0})
, consist of all data-generating distributions induced by conditions

G(f^{\dagger})\leq R_{0}

and

\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{R}}(\beta,{\mathbf{z}};f^{\dagger})\leq D_{1}\beta+D_{2}m^{-Q}

for some
R_{0},Q,D_{1},D_{2}>0
.
Moreover, let us assume that

\mathbb{E}G^{\ast}(B_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{*}{\bf\epsilon})\leq D_{3}m^{-\frac{q}{2}}

for some
D_{3}>0
, where
q
is the Hölder conjugate of
1<p<2
.
Then it follows that for the parameter choice rule

\lambda=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{D_{3}^{\frac{2}{q}}R_{0}^{-\frac{q+2}{q}}}{D_{1}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Sigma}{R_{0}\sqrt{m}}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}

(3.25)

we have

\sup_{(\Sigma,R_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}D_{G}(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda},f^{\dagger})}{a_{m,R_{0},\Sigma,p}}\leq C,

(3.26)

where the concentration rate is given by

a_{m,R_{0},\Sigma,p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Sigma}{R_{0}\sqrt{m}}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}

(3.27)

and the constant
C
depends on
D_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, and
p
.

For a more concrete result, let us consider wavelet decompositions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
{\mathcal{B}}=B_{pp}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=B_{p}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a Besov space
[
29
]
and

G(f)=\frac{1}{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f}}}\right\|_{B_{p}^{s}}^{p}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}c_{\alpha,p,s,d}|\langle f,\psi_{\alpha}\rangle|^{p}

(3.28)

for some
1<p<2
, where

c_{\alpha,p,s,d}=2^{|\alpha|d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)-1}}\right)}.

(3.29)

Here,
\psi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\alpha=1,...,\infty
, are suitably regular functions that form an orthonormal wavelet basis for
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with global indexing
\alpha
. The notation
|\alpha|
is used to denote the scale of the wavelet basis associated with the index
\alpha
. Rephrasing
[
14
, Cor. 5.6]
in terms of the upper rate of convergence yields the following statement.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
G
be defined by (
3.21
) and let the model set
{\mathcal{M}}
be induced by conditions

G(f)\leq R_{0}
and

\partial G(f)=\{B^{*}_{\nu}w\}\;\text{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w}}}\right\|\leq R_{0}.

Moreover, assume

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}c_{\alpha,q,-s,d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{A\psi_{\alpha}}}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}}^{q}<\infty,

where coefficients

are defined by (

).
Then for parameter choice rule

\lambda=R_{0}^{\frac{2}{3p}-\frac{1}{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Sigma}{R_{0}\sqrt{m}}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}

(3.30)

we have

\sup_{(\Sigma,R_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sup_{\rho\in{\mathcal{M}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}D_{G}(f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda},f^{\dagger})}{a_{m,R_{0},\Sigma,p}}\leq C

(3.31)

with the concentration rate

a_{m,R_{0},\Sigma,p}=R_{0}^{\frac{2+5p}{3p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Sigma}{R_{0}\sqrt{m}}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}.

(3.32)

Remark 3.21
.

As mentioned above, convex penalties in inverse problems, such as Lasso regularization, are often motivated by sparsity-type assumption regarding the unknown. While
Theorems
3.19
and
3.20
provide first attempt to formalize theory of convex penalties in statistical inverse learning with random design, the highly interesting case of
p=1
remains open and is not easily answered by the Bregman distance based techniques. Moreover, the Bregman distance for
1
-homogeneous functional (consider e.g. the
\ell^{1}
-norm) is typically weak as the subgradient set is not a singleton. We point out that variational source conditions have been successful in proving concentration rates in the norm for deterministic design in statistical inversion
[
84
]
and it would be tempting to use similar ideas for the random design problem.

3.5
Parameter choice rules and adaptivity

We recall that while tuning the regularization parameter is essential for regularization to work well, an a-priori choice of the regularization parameter is in general not feasible
in statistical problems since the choice necessarily depends on unknown structural properties such as smoothness of
f^{\dagger}
or eigenvalue decay, see e.g. equations (
3.5
), (
3.18
), Table
1
or equations (
3.25
), (
3.30
).
Parameter choice rules, also known as
model selection
or
hyperparameter selection
,
are crucial in the context of inverse problems
and play a vital role in finding stable,
reliable, and interpretable solutions, that depend on the data only and are free from additional unknown a-priori assumptions.

The aim is to construct estimators, i.e. to find a sequence of regularization parameters

(\lambda_{\mathbf{z}})_{m}
, without knowledge of a-priori assumptions, but depending
on the data
{\mathbf{z}}
, such that the sequence of regularized solutions
(f_{{\mathbf{z}},\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}})_{m}
is minimax optimal. Such a sequence of estimators is called
minimax optimal adaptive
.

We give a very brief overview about some classical model selection criteria used in inverse problems.

Unbiased risk estimation (URE).

A very natural idea is to estimate the unknown risk by a functional
U({\mathbf{z}},\lambda)
of the observations, and then to minimize this estimator of the risk. Ideally,
U
is an
unbiased
estimator of the risk, meaning that

{\mathcal{E}}(f_{{\mathbf{z}},\lambda})=\mathbb{E}[U({\mathbf{z}},\lambda)]\;,\quad\forall\;\;\lambda>0\;.

The principle of URE suggests to minimize over
\lambda\in\Lambda
, where
\Lambda
is a well-chosen finite grid of possible values for the regularization parameter:

\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}:=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}U({\mathbf{z}},\lambda)\;.

For inverse problems, this method was studied in
[
21
]
, where exact oracle inequalities for the mean square risk were obtained.

Hold-out and (generalized) cross-validation (GCV).

Cross-validation is a classical approach to select hyperparameters that is based on
data-splitting
. The idea behind GCV is to use the data to both train and validate the model, but with a focus on finding the optimal hyperparameters. The process involves iteratively fitting the model with different hyperparameter values and evaluating the model’s performance using a specific criterion. The simplist version is the
hold-out method
, where the available data are split into a training set
{\mathbf{z}}^{t}
of size
m_{1}
and validation set
{\mathbf{z}}^{v}
of size
m_{2}
, with
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
. The estimator
f_{{\mathbf{z}}^{t},\lambda}
is defined by means of the training data and is evaluated using the empirical risk with respect to the validation data:

\lambda_{{\mathbf{z}}}:=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\frac{1}{m_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}(f_{{\mathbf{z}}^{t},\lambda}(x_{j}^{v})-y_{j}^{v})^{2}\;.

This method works well in practice and
[
19
]
show minimax optimal rates for the
\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\nu)
error under polynomial eigendecay and a Hölder source condition.

Lepski’s balancing principle.

The
Lepski principle
, a.k.a. the
Goldenshluger-Lepski method
was introduced in the seminal paper
[
52
]
and does not use any data splitting. It is merely based on comparing estimators for different levels of regularization.
Surprisingly, the monotonicity of the approximation error suffices to provide the knowledge to choose the regularization parameter adaptively, provided an empirical approximation of the sample error
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbf{z}}
is available. More precisely, let
\Lambda
be a finite grid of possible values for
\lambda
. Set

J_{{\mathbf{z}}}(\Lambda)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(B_{\mathbf{x}}+\lambda^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{{\mathbf{z}},\lambda}-f_{{\mathbf{z}},\lambda^{\prime}})}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\leq c\;\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\;{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbf{z}}(\lambda^{\prime}),\quad\forall\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda,\,\lambda^{\prime}\leq\lambda}}\right\}\;.

The parameter choice based on Lepski’s principle is given by

{\lambda}_{{\mathbf{z}}}:=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}J_{{\mathbf{z}}}(\Lambda)\;.

In the context of learning theory, this method was analyzed in

[
31
]
,
[
54
]
and was finally improved by
[
12
]
.
This parameter choice rule is minimax optimal adaptive under various types of source conditions and eigenvalue decay.

Discrepancy principle and early stopping rules.

The above mentioned criteria require the computation of all estimators to be compared against each other.
For problems with high dimensions, this can incur a computationally prohibitive cost.
An alternative approach is the use of early stopping rules based on the discrepancy principle. These rules terminate the procedure at an iteration
t_{\mathbf{z}}
based
solely on iterates with index
t\leq t_{\mathbf{z}}
and potentially other quantities computed up to that point. Because they require
the calculation of significantly fewer iterates, early stopping rules offer the potential to achieve both computational
and statistical efficiency simultaneously. The stopping criterion is defined based on a validation performance: If the performance does not improve for a certain number of consecutive evaluations,
the training process is halted. A popular validation criterion, for instance, is based on the smoothed empirical residuals

\hat{R}_{t,s}:=||(B_{\mathbf{x}}B_{\mathbf{x}}^{*})^{s}({\mathbf{y}}-B_{\mathbf{x}}f_{{\mathbf{z}},t})||_{2}\;,

where
s\geq 0
and
f_{{\mathbf{z}},t}
is an iteratively defined estimator, as such as the last iterate of a gradient based method or truncated
SVD, see e.g.
[
86
,
74
,
9
,
10
]
. The iterative
process is stopped at the smallest
t_{\mathbf{z}}
, such that

\hat{R}_{t_{\mathbf{z}},s}\leq\tau\;,

for some pre-specified
\tau>0
. Provided that the inverse problem is moderately ill-posed,
it is known that this criterion leads to optimal adaptive bounds for
f_{{\mathbf{z}},t_{{\mathbf{z}}}}
for moderate smoothing, i.e.
s<b/2
, over Sobolev-type ellipsoids
[
74
]
.

We finally mention some recent results related to establishing data-driven approaches for finding the optimal regularization parameter, highlighting particularly the current need of a better understanding of such methods in modern machine learning:
[
2
]
focus on the
case where the regularization functional is not given a priori, but learned from data,
[
23
]
study a statistical learning approach,
based on empirical risk minimization, and
[
30
]
propose a novel method based on supervised learning to approximate the high-dimensional function, mapping noisy data into a good approximation to the optimal Tikhonov regularization parameter.

4
Non-linear problems

In this section, we discuss the statistical learning problem (
1.1
) when
A
is a nonlinear operator. However, in contrast to Section
3
, we only focus on Tikhonov regularization. We present classical error bounds for the reconstruction error in Section
4.1
as well as bounds for Tikhonov regularization in Hilbert scales in Section
4.2
.

For analyzing the non-linear inverse problem (
1.1
) we require the forward operator to be Fréchet differentiable (an assumption that will even be further relaxed below).

Assumption 9

(Non-linearity of the operator I)
.

We impose the following assumptions:

(i)

The domain
\mathcal{D}(A)
is convex with nonempty interior.

(ii)

The operator
A
is Fréchet differentiable.

(iii)

The Fréchet derivative
A^{\prime}(f)
of
A
at
f
is bounded in a ball
\mathcal{B}_{d}(f^{\dagger})
of radius
d:=4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f^{\dagger}-\bar{f}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, i.e., there exists
\mu_{1}<\infty
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{A^{\prime}(f)}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\leq\mu_{1}\qquad\forall~f\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}.~

Recall from Example
2.7
, the definition of the covariance operator

T_{\nu}:=B_{\nu}^{*}B_{\nu},\quad B_{\nu}=I_{\nu}A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger}).

4.1
Classical Error Bounds

The Tikhonov regularization scheme for this problem is again given by an optimization problem

f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{f\in\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([A(f)](x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}+\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f-\bar{f}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}}\right\}.

(4.1)

Here,
\bar{f}\in{\mathcal{H}}
denotes some initial guess of the solution
f^{\dagger}
, which offers the possibility to incorporate
a-priori
information. Note, however, due to the non-linearity of
A
, the solution to the minimization problem is not explicitly given and does not need to be unique.

To establish our error bounds we need additional assumptions on the non-linearity of
A
.

Assumption 10

(Non-linearity of the operator II)
.

We assume:

(i)

The operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\nu)
is weakly sequentially closed and one-to-one.

(ii)

There exists
\mu_{2}\geq 0
such that for
all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{d}(f^{\dagger})\cap\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}
we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{A(f)-A(f^{\dagger})-A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})(f-f^{\dagger})}}}\right\|_{\nu}\leq\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f-f^{\dagger}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

The first assumption ensures that there exists a global minimizer of the Tikhonov functional (
4.1
). If
\mathcal{D}(A)
is weakly closed (closed and convex) and
A
is weakly continuous, then weak sequential closedness is guaranteed. On the other hand, the second assumption helps to control the remainder term of the linearized problem. The second condition also holds true when the Fréchet derivative is Lipschitz in the operator norm (see
[
36
, Chapt. 10]
).

As in the linear case, we impose a source condition for
f^{\dagger}
to facilitate convergence.

Assumption 11

(Source condition)
.

Let
\bar{f}\in{\mathcal{H}}
. We assume that
f^{\dagger}-\bar{f}\in\Omega(r,R_{0},T_{\nu})
, for
r\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
and where the source set is defined in Assumption
6
.

Under the source condition
f^{\dagger}-\bar{f}=T_{\nu}^{r}u
for
r\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, we have that
f^{\dagger}-\bar{f}=T_{\nu}^{\frac{1}{2}}w
for
w=T_{\nu}^{r-\frac{1}{2}}u
satisfying

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{w}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{T_{\nu}}}}\right\|^{r-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{u}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\kappa^{2r-1}R_{0}\leq\bar{\kappa}R_{0}=:\mu_{3}\;,

with
\bar{\kappa}=\max\{1,\kappa\}
. We additionally assume that

R_{0}<\frac{1}{2\mu_{2}\bar{\kappa}},

(4.2)

implying that
2\mu_{2}\mu_{3}<1
. This assumption is a smallness condition that imposes a constraint between
\mu_{3}
and the non-linearity as measured by the parameter
\mu_{2}
in Assumption
9
(iii). It ensures that the initial guess is close enough to the true solution and the residual error on linearizing the nonlinear operator
A
at the solution is sufficiently small.

We need an additional restriction to achieve the lower bounds:

Assumption 12
.

Let us define
T=A^{\prime}(f)^{*}I_{\nu}^{*}I_{\nu}A^{\prime}(f)
and
\overline{T}=A^{\prime}(\bar{f})^{*}I_{\nu}^{*}I_{\nu}A^{\prime}(\bar{f})
. There exists a family of bounded linear operators
R_{f}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and
\zeta>0
such that

T^{r}=R_{f}\overline{T}^{r}\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{R_{f}-I}}}\right\|\leq\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{f-\bar{f}}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}},

where
f
belongs to the sufficiently large ball and
\bar{f}
is the initial guess.

Theorem 4.1

(Rastogi et al.

]

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
,
5

9

4.2
Error Bounds in Hilbert Scales

Let
L:{\mathcal{D}}(L)\subset{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
be an unbounded operator, generating a Hilbert scale.
The Tikhonov regularization scheme in Hilbert Scales amounts to minimize the regularized empirical risk

f_{\mathbf{z},\lambda}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\limits_{f\in\mathcal{D}(A)\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([A(f)](x_{i})-y_{i}}}\right)^{2}+\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{L(f-\bar{f})}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}}\right\},

(4.6)

enforcing smoothness and with initial guess
\bar{f}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)\cap{\mathcal{D}}(L)
. As above, due to the non-linearity of
A
, the solution to the minimization problem is not explicitly given and does not need to be unique. To establish error bounds also in this case, the nonlinear structure of the operator needs to be further specified.

Assumption 13

(Non-linearity of the operator III)
.

We suppose that:

(i)

The operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\cap\mathcal{D}(L)\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is weakly sequentially closed.

(ii)

Link condition: There exist constants
p\geq 1
and
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
such that for all
g\in\mathcal{H}
,

c_{1}\|g\|_{{-(p-1)}}\leq\|A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})g\|_{\nu}\leq c_{2}\|g\|_{{-(p-1)}}.

(iii)

The Fréchet derivative
A^{\prime}(f)
is Lipschitz continuous for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(A)\cap\mathcal{D}(L)~
, i.e., there exists a constant
\mu_{4}
such that

\|A^{\prime}(f^{\dagger})-A^{\prime}(f)\|_{-(p-1),\nu}\leq\mu_{4}\|f^{\dagger}-f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2c_{2}},

where the norm
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\cdot}}}\right\|_{-(p-1),\nu}
is defined for the operators
\mathcal{H}_{-(p-1)}
to
\mathscr{L}^{2}(X,\nu)
.

The link condition, described in Assumption
13
(ii) involves the interplay between the operator
L^{-1}
and the Fréchet derivative of
A
.

We incorporate a-priori smoothness in terms of a more general source condition.

Assumption 14

(Source condition)
.

Let
\bar{f}\in{\mathcal{D}}(L)
,
s\in[1,p+1]
for some benchmark smoothness
p\geq 1
. We assume that
f^{\dagger}-\bar{f}\in\Omega(s,R_{0},L^{-1})
, where the source set is defined in Assumption
7
.

Having now established all assumptions, we are ready to state an upper bound for the reconstruction error.

Theorem 4.6

(Rastogi et al.
[

)

Suppose Assumptions

–
3
,
5
,
9

in (

Applications

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) models are employed to predict the evolution of drug concentrations and effects in patients undergoing treatment. Covariate models tell us how patient attributes (covariates) influence model parameters and play a crucial role in PK/PD model construction. Common covariates are body weight, age, and levels of crucial biomarkers (e.g., plasma creatinine). The problem of interest is the determination of the functional relationship between covariates and parameters.

6
Conclusions and outlook for future directions

Motivated by the growing interest in supervised inverse learning with random design, we hope to have presented a thorough introduction to this modern research field. To analyze both linear and non-linear inverse problems, we have defined a general mathematical framework within reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, allowing us to appropriately consider the stochastic nature of the data.

For tackling linear inverse problems, we adopt a least squares approach with various types of regularization: spectral regularization, regularization by projection, and convex regularization. We discuss optimal rates of convergence for our estimators, initially dependent on unknown structural model assumptions such as the smoothness of the ground truth and the eigenvalue decay of the uncentered covariance operator (both describing the degree of ill-posedness of the inverse problem), ultimately leading to fast rates of convergence. To prevent overfitting, a proper choice of the regularization parameter is crucial. Since a-priori choices are not feasible in practical applications, we gave a brief overview of common data-driven approaches.

While inverse learning with a known linear forward operator is well developed for a fair amount of regularization methods, much less is known for inverse learning with a known forward non-linear operator. We discussed error bounds only for Tikhonov regularization in both the classical setting and within Hilbert scales using the least squares loss.
A Standard approach for handling non-linear problems is to consider linear approximations of the operator: Under the assumption of Fréchet differentiability of
A
and some Lipschitz conditions, the non-linearity is mild enough to obtain optimal bounds, matching those of the linear case. Notably, in the case where
A
is not Fréchet differentiable, error bounds can still be derived by means of conditional stability estimates.

Unfortunaltely, in the non-linear case, the Tikhonov regularized solution is not directly computable, and hence, other regularization approaches that take the non-linear structure into account and are efficiently implementable need to be analyzed further. Due to their practicability, the hope is to successfully apply kernel methods also to obtain approximate solutions that maintain statistical optimality. A major focus should be on the development of data-driven approaches that are also efficient, such as early stopping rules. We hope that our contribution can provide an impetus to further research in this area.
