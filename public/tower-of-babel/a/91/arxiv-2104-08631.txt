Training Humans to Train Robots Dynamic Motor Skills
Marina Y. Aoyama, Matthew Howard

Abstract
Learning from demonstration (LfD) is commonly considered to be a natural and intuitive way to allow novice users to teach motor skills to robots. However, it is important to acknowledge that the effectiveness of LfD is heavily dependent on the quality of teaching , something that may not be assured with novices. It remains an open question as to the most effective way of guiding demonstrators to produce informative demonstrations beyond ad hoc advice for specific teaching tasks. To this end, this paper investigates the use of machine teaching to derive an index for determining the quality of demonstrations and evaluates its use in guiding and training novices to become better teachers. Experiments with a simple learner robot suggest that guidance and training of teachers through the proposed approach can lead to up to 66.5% decrease in error in the learnt skill.

I
INTRODUCTION

As robots are integrated into many aspects of our life, their use by non-experts is becoming increasingly commonplace
[
1
]
. This has led to a growing demand for natural and intuitive means to engender robots with the motor skills to perform useful tasks.
Learning from demonstration (LfD)
is widely considered to be a promising approach to achieve this
[
2
,
3
]
.

At its core,
Learning from demonstration (LfD)
can be divided into two stages:
teaching
and
learning
. The teacher, who is usually an expert at performing the task, demonstrates the required motor skills to the robot learner. The latter records data from the demonstration and uses it to compute a skill model using an
LfD
algorithm
[
4
]
. While recent years has seen the development of learning algorithms capable of learning a wide range of complex tasks
[
2
,
5
]
, their performance is still highly dependent on the
quality of teaching
.

Although human teachers generally try to give informative demonstrations when teaching (rather than, say, randomly sampled data)
[
6
]
, recent studies have identified that it is often not intuitive for novice teachers to produce demonstrations of sufficient quality to enable effective learning. For example, in
[
7
]
none of the human teachers achieved the (provably) optimal strategy for teaching the concept of graspability (
i.e.,
whether an object fits in the hand) and
[
8
]
found significant inter-subject variability in ability when teaching a robot a pick and place task. Giving suboptimal demonstrations can result in poor performance of the robot and increase the time, effort and cost of teaching.

One way to address this, is to seek ways of generating data tailored to optimal performance of the learner robot. For example,
machine teaching

is an approach that, given knowledge of a learning algorithm, derives training data by solving a bilevel optimisation problem
[
9
,
10
]
. However, so far
machine teaching (MT)
has only been shown to be tractable for a limited set of problems.

With this in mind, this paper proposes the use of
MT
as a means to guide and train human teachers
, thereby improving the quality of their teaching, while retaining the benefit of their intuition and adaptability in teaching a wide variety of skills. To this end, this research

(i)

defines and solves the
MT
problem for teaching a wide class of dynamic motor skills,

(ii)

shows how the latter leads to a natural index to measure the quality of the demonstrations and

(iii)

evaluates the use of the latter as a means to guide and train human teachers

. Experiments are reported in which the effectiveness of training novice teachers (
n=32
) with the proposed index is evaluated and up to 66.5% reduction in the error of the robot-learnt skill is seen.

II
PROBLEM DEFINITION

This research aims to optimise the teaching of dynamic motor skills to torque-controlled robots by guiding and training human teachers to produce high quality demonstrations, in the form of data
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
, with bounded teaching effort. Specifically, the skills considered in this paper are those representable as a closed loop controller

u=\boldsymbol{\pi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\boldsymbol{x})
maps the system state
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{P}}}
to the desired action
u\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}}
are the skill parameters.

The demonstration set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
consists of a list of states
[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}}]=\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{P}}\times\mathcal{N}}
and the corresponding list of actions
(u_{1},...,u_{\mathcal{N}})^{\top}=\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}}
provided by the teacher to demonstrate the skill. These may take the form of trajectories or key frames sampled from the target behaviour.

Using this data, the learner then forms a model of the skill

\tilde{u}=\boldsymbol{\pi}(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

(2)

with which to reproduce the behaviour by estimating the parameters through some learning algorithm

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}).

(3)

A promising approach to deriving high quality teaching data, beyond simply relying on the intuition of demonstrators, is to make use of
MT
techniques. The latter use knowledge of the learning algorithm (
3
) and the target skill parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
to derive conditions on training data such that learning performance is optimised. In general,
MT
can be expressed as a bi-level optimisation problem and several formulations are possible. In this paper, the formulation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{D}}}\mathcal{\rho}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}})

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}})\leq n_{B}.

(6)

is used
[
11
]
. Here, the
teacher’s
problem (
4
) and (
6
) is to find the training set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
from the space of possible data sets
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{D}}
that minimises a
teaching risk function

\mathcal{\rho}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})
subject to a constraint on the effort expended represented by the
teaching effort function

\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}})
. The latter reflects the fact that in real-world robot
LfD
, typically providing demonstrations is costly (
e.g.,
in terms of the teacher’s time, equipment wear and tear and computational burden). In this paper, a fixed effort budget
n_{B}
is assumed, consisting of the maximum number of data points in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.
The
learner’s
problem (
5
) is to optimise the model of the skill (
i.e.,
the machine learning problem) based on the data
1
1

1

Note that, an implicit assumption here is that the model can be taught
only through demonstrations
and not by directly passing
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
to the learner. This reflects the fact that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
is typically only
implicitly
known to the demonstrator, through their own skill in performing the task.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
.

When the target model
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
is explicitly known and (
4
)-(
6
) can be solved, the machine teacher can create the optimal training data
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
. However, in many cases,

(i)

the target model is only known
implicitly
and

(ii)

the
MT
problem is intractable,

especially when the motor skills are complex or the learning algorithm does not have a closed-form solution. Therefore, this study examines if and how
the optimality benefits of machine teaching can be combined with the versatility of human teaching
. Specifically, the idea is to exploit human teachers’ (often implicit) knowledge of a broad range of motor skills, but shape their teaching strategies toward those deemed optimal according to
MT
, by training the human teachers. It is anticipated that this can lead to a significant improvement in the overall quality of teaching, for example, enabling humans to transfer optimal teaching of one motor skill to another, including those for which the
MT
problem cannot be directly solved.

To this end, the aims of this paper are twofold, namely,

(i)

to quantify the potential improvements to learner performance that may be achieved by optimising demonstrations through
MT
, and

(ii)

investigating the extent to which
MT
may be used to inform the training of non-experts to become skilled in teaching motor skills to robots

.

III
METHOD

In this section, the approach taken to formulating the
MT
problem for the teaching of dynamic motor skills is described alongside the key modelling assumptions.

III-A

Modelling the Learner

The learner’s goal is to learn a model of the target skill
{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
from demonstrations
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
using a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. As a simple but general model, in this paper, it is assumed that the learner approximates the target motor skill (
1
) with a
linear-in-the-parameters (LiP)
model

\tilde{u}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}}
is a vector of features or basis functions.

To learn (
7
) without overfitting, it is assumed that the learner forms the approximation through ridge regression, that is, by minimising the loss function

\min\limits_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}-u_{i})^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|^{2}

(8)

where
\lambda
is the regularisation parameter and
\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|
is the Mahalanobis norm of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The closed-form solution of (
8
) is

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}

(9)

where
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}\times\mathcal{{S}}}
is the identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}\times\mathcal{N}}
is a feature matrix mapped from
\boldsymbol{X}
through the basis functions
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

III-B

Modelling the Teacher

The teacher’s primary goal is to produce the demonstration set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
that enables the learner to estimate a model (
7
) that reproduces the the target skill (
1
) as closely as possible. To this end, the teacher’s model of the skill is

u={\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(10)

The teacher’s goal is to minimise the teaching risk function

\mathcal{\rho}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})=\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})^{\top}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})]

(11)

where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation over the error in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
model when there is Gaussian noise (assumed to originate from sensors or demonstrator errors)
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{\sigma})
on
\boldsymbol{u}
.

In line with the formulation of
MT
outlined in §
II
, the teacher must teach the target skill with a fixed budget of teaching effort. In the following, the teaching budget (
6
) is set as

\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}})=\mathcal{N}_{TD}

(12)

where
\mathcal{N}_{TD}
is the
teaching dimension
for the problem, defined as the minimum number of training items required to teach the target model to the learner. For the learner defined in §
III-A
, the teaching dimension is
\mathcal{{S}}

i.e.,
the dimensionality of the target model
[
12
]
.

III-C

Machine Teaching Problem

Combining (
9
), (
11
) and (
12
), the
MT
problem used in this paper is therefore

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{D}}}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})^{\top}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})]

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\quad}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}})=\mathcal{{S}}.

(14)

The solution to (
13
)-(
14
) is the optimal data set with which to teach the learner robot, and is therefore the gold standard of teaching to which human teachers should aspire. However, it should be noted that in general this solution is

(i)

non-unique, and

(ii)

hard to find in closed form for cases where
\mathcal{{S}}>2

. With this in mind, rather than prescribing a specific way of teaching (
i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
) when training teachers, the strategy taken in this paper is to find the
conditions for demonstration optimality
under (
13
)-(
14
). These conditions can then be used to guide teachers toward improving their teaching ability.

III-D

Condition for Optimal Demonstrations

In this section, the conditions for demonstration optimality are derived. As noted in §
III-C
, the general solution to for
\mathcal{{S}}>2
is challenging to find, so the approach taken here is to first perform a simple analysis of the conditions for optimal teaching under (
13
)-(
14
) to gain insight into the problem. It will then be shown that this approach is consistent with the analytically solvable case of
\mathcal{{S}}=2
.

III-D
1
Optimal Teaching for
\mathcal{{S}}>2

A consequence of the formulation (
13
)-(
14
) is that the feature matrix for the optimal data set is, by definition, square
i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{S}}\times\mathcal{{S}}}
. If it is further assumed that

(i)

the feature vectors are normalised (
i.e.,

0\leq\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\leq 1\ \forall\ \boldsymbol{x}
) and

(ii)

for the optimal data set regularisation is not needed (
i.e.,

\lambda=0
)

, the learning problem (
9
) can be reduced to

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}.

(15)

A condition for solving (
15
) is that the inverse exists, or equivalently

\det\boldsymbol{\Phi}\neq 0.

(16)

This leads to the following condition for optimal teaching:

Condition:

Optimal demonstrations maximise
\lvert\det\boldsymbol{\Phi}\rvert
.

Even when
\lambda\neq 0
, choices of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
with determinant close to zero risk numerical instability.

III-D
2
Optimal Teaching for
\mathcal{{S}}=2

In the case that
\mathcal{{S}}=2
, a general expression of the feature matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, assuming that the feature vectors are normalised (
i.e.,

0\leq\|\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\|\leq 1\ \forall\ \boldsymbol{x}
), is

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\cos\mathcal{\omega}\\
0&\sin\mathcal{\omega}\end{array}\right)

(17)

where
\mathcal{\omega}
is the angle between the feature vectors
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}
.

Using (
9
), (
11
) can be written
[
13
]

\mathcal{\rho}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})=\mathcal{\mathcal{\sigma}}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{b_{i}}{(b_{i}+\mathcal{\lambda})^{2}}+\mathcal{\lambda}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}+\mathcal{\lambda}\mathbf{I})^{-2}{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}

(18)

where
b_{i}
is the
i
th eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}
and
\mathcal{\mathcal{\sigma}}^{2}
is the variance of the noise on the training data (see (
10
)).
Since
\lambda
is small the second term, it is neglected here to give

\mathcal{\rho}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}})=\mathcal{\mathcal{\sigma}}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{b_{i}}{(b_{i}+\mathcal{\lambda})^{2}}

(19)

where

b_{1}=1+\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\mathcal{\omega}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad b_{2}=1-\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\mathcal{\omega}}.

(20)

From
(
19
)-(
20
),

\frac{d\mathcal{\rho}}{d\mathcal{\omega}}=\dfrac{4\mathcal{\mathcal{\sigma}}^{2}\cos\mathcal{\omega}\sin\mathcal{\omega}\left(\left(2\lambda+1\right)\sin^{2}\mathcal{\omega}-2\lambda^{3}-3\lambda^{2}-2\lambda\right)}{(\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\mathcal{\omega}}-\lambda-1)^{3}(\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\mathcal{\omega}}+\lambda+1)^{3}}.

(21)

It can be shown that
\mathcal{\omega}^{*}=\pi/2
satisfies
d\mathcal{\rho}/d\mathcal{\omega}=0
and
d^{2}\mathcal{\rho}/d\mathcal{\omega}^{2}>0
.

Moreover, it can be shown that for the feature matrix (
17
)

\lvert\det\boldsymbol{\Phi}\rvert=\lvert\sin(\mathcal{\omega})\rvert

(22)

which is maximised for
\mathcal{\omega}=\mathcal{\omega}^{*}
, hence lending support to the condition defined in §
III-D1
.

III-E

Teaching Quality Index

Having defined the conditions for optimal demonstration data in the preceding section, it is now possible to design a strategy for improving human teaching.
Specifically, the proposed
teaching quality index
is

s=100\lvert\det\boldsymbol{\Phi}\rvert

(23)

which is a score out of 100 (assuming normalised feature vectors).

The latter can be used either as an
evaluation
(
i.e.,
to
assess
teaching ability) or as
guidance
(
i.e.,

feedback
to teachers) to enable them to improve their teaching.

V
CONCLUSIONS

This paper designs a machine teaching problem for teaching motor skills to dynamically controlled robots through
LfD
, presents a condition for informative demonstration data using
machine teaching
and validates the use of the resulting teaching quality index to guide and train human teachers. Evaluation of the latter in teaching a dynamic motor skill to a simple robot suggest that its use can enable novice teachers to improve their selection of demonstrations.
Moreover, there is evidence that guidance enhances high-level learning to select good demonstrations to teach new motor skills.

In future work, this work may be extended to

(i)

teach more complex dynamic motor skills which require higher degrees of freedom robots or learning algorithms where no closed-form solutions are available and

(ii)

evaluate the effectiveness of different types of guidance and training methods using the
MT
framework.
