Abstract
We give an embedding of the Poincaré halfspace ℍ D \mathbb{H}^{D} into a discrete metric space based on a binary tiling of ℍ D \mathbb{H}^{D} , with additive distortion O ⁡ ( log ⁡ D ) O(\log D) . It yields the following results. We show that any subset P P of n n points in ℍ D \mathbb{H}^{D} can be embedded into a graph-metric with 2 O ⁡ ( D ) ​ n 2^{O(D)}n vertices and edges, and with additive distortion O ⁡ ( log ⁡ D ) O(\log D) . We also show how to construct, for any k k , an O ⁡ ( k ​ log ⁡ D ) O(k\log D) -purely additive spanner of P P with 2 O ⁡ ( D ) ​ n 2^{O(D)}n Steiner vertices and 2 O ⁡ ( D ) ​ n ⋅ λ k ​ ( n ) 2^{O(D)}n\cdot\lambda_{k}(n) edges, where λ k ​ ( n ) \lambda_{k}(n) is the k k th-row inverse Ackermann function. Finally, we show how to construct an approximate Voronoi diagram for P P of size 2 O ⁡ ( D ) ​ n 2^{O(D)}n . It allows us to answer approximate near-neighbor queries in 2 O ⁡ ( D ) + O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) 2^{O(D)}+O(\log n) time, with additive error O ⁡ ( log ⁡ D ) O(\log D) . These constructions can be done in 2 O ⁡ ( D ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n 2^{O(D)}n\log n time.



1
Introduction

The Poincaré halfplane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is perhaps the most common model of hyperbolic spaces, together with the
Poincaré disk which it is isometric to. The points of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are the points
(x,z)
,
z>0
of
the upper halfplane of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The shortest paths in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, called geodesics,
are arcs of circles orthogonal to
the
x
-axis (Figure
1
a) and the arc length is given by integrating
the relation
ds^{2}=(dx^{2}+dz^{2})/z^{2}
. More generally, the Poincaré halfspace is a
D
-dimensional
model of hyperbolic space, that consists of points
(x,z)
where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
and
z
is a positive
real number. In
\mathbb{H}^{D}
, the expression of
ds^{2}
is the same,
but
x
is now a point in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
, and geodesics are arcs of circles orthogonal to the hyperplane
z=0
.

Hyperbolic spaces behave very differently from Euclidean spaces in some respects. For instance, in fixed
dimension, the volume of a hyperbolic ball grows exponentially with its radius, while the radius
of a Euclidean ball grows polynomially. A triangle, formed by connecting three
points by the geodesic between each pair of these points, is thin in the sense that from any point
on an edge, there is a point on another edge at distance bounded by a constant.

As a consequence, hyperbolic spaces are sometimes more suitable than Euclidean spaces to represent
some types of data. It has been shown, for instance, that there are better embeddings of the internet
graph into hyperbolic spaces, compared with its embeddings into Euclidean spaces
[
12
]
. There has also been recent interest
in hyperbolic spaces in the context of artificial neural networks
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we present embeddings of finite subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
into graph metrics
with a linear number of edges, and additive distortion
O(\log D)
.
As an application, we present an approximate near-neighbor data structure with

O(\log D)
additive distortion. These two results have no multiplicative distortion.

:

(a) The Poincaré halfplane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

(\mathbb{B}^{2},d_{1})
, with a shortest path of length

.

1.1
Our results.

Given two metric spaces
(M,d)
and
(M^{\prime},d^{\prime})
, we say that a mapping
\varphi:M\to M^{\prime}

is an
embedding with additive distortion

\Delta

if for any two points
p,q\in M
, we have

d(p,q)-\Delta\leqslant d^{\prime}(\varphi(p),\varphi(q))\leqslant d(p,q)+\Delta.

Our first result (Theorem
14
) is an embedding of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
with additive distortion

O(\log D)
into a discrete metric space
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
that is obtained
from a binary tiling of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
. In two dimensions, this binary tiling uses boxes whose sizes increase
exponentially with the
z
-coordinate. (See Figure
1
b.). A point
is placed at the center of each box, and the distance between two points
p
and
q
is the minimum number
of box boundaries that are crossed when going from
p
to
q
.
(See Section
2
for a detailed description.)

Given a subset
P
of
n
points of a metric space
(M,d)
, a
purely-additive spanner
of
P

with distortion
\Delta
and
n^{\prime}

Steiner points
is a graph
G(V,E)
where
S\subset M
,
|S|=n^{\prime}
,
the points in
S
are called Steiner points,
V=P\cup S
, the length of any edge
pq
is
d(p,q)
, and the
shortest path distance
d_{G}(\cdot)
in this graph satisfies

d(p,q)\leqslant d_{G}(p,q)\leqslant d(p,q)+\Delta\quad\text{for all }p,q\in P.

We show, for any subset
P
of
n
points of
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
,
how to construct in
2^{O(D)}n\log n
time
a purely-additive spanner of
P
with
2^{O(D)}n
edges and Steiner vertices, and
distortion
2
. (Theorem
16
.)

Based on the two results above, we first obtain an embedding of any subset
P
of
n
points
of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
into a graph metric with
2^{O(D)}n
vertices and edges, and
additive distortion
O(\log D)
. We also obtain an
O(k\log D)
purely additive spanner of
P

with
2^{O(D)}n
vertices and
2^{O(D)}n\cdot\lambda_{k}(n)
Steiner vertices and edges, where
\lambda_{k}(n)

is the
k
th-row inverse Ackermann function.

Given a subset
P
of
n
points of a metric space
(M,d)
, and a query point
q\in P
,
a
nearest neighbor
of
q
is a point
p\in P
such that
d(p,q)
is minimum.
An
approximate nearest neighbor
(ANN) with additive distortion
\Delta
is a point

p^{\prime}\in P
such that
d(p^{\prime},q)\leqslant d(p,q)+\Delta
.

We give data structures for
answering ANN queries in
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
and in
\mathbb{H}^{D}
with
query time
2^{O(D)}+O(\log n)
and construction time
2^{O(D)}n\log n
. For
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
,
the additive distortion is 2, and for
\mathbb{H}^{D}
, it is
O(\log D)
.
(See Theorem
21
and Corollary
22
.)
These data structures are in fact Approximate Voronoi Diagrams (AVD): They give a partition
of the space into
2^{O(D)}n
regions, each region being associated with
2^{O(D)}
representative points,
and such that for any query point in one of these regions, one of the representative points
is an approximate near neighbor.

1.2
Comparison with previous work.

Spanners have been studied in the more general context
of an arbitrary weighted graph metric
[
1
]
. For instance, one can find a
t
-multiplicative
spanner of total weight
(1+1/t)
times the weight of a minimum spanning tree.

For Euclidean metrics, it was shown that there are spanners with a linear number
of edges, and multiplicative distortion arbitrarily close to 1
[
2
]
. One difference with
this paper is that we consider an
additive
distortion. In the worst case, one cannot hope to
find a non-trivial additive spanner for the Euclidean metric, because the additive error for a given
graph can be made arbitrarily larger by scaling up the input point set.

Gromov-hyperbolicity is a notion of hyperbolicity that applies to any metric space
(M,d)
,
including discrete metric spaces. These spaces have the property that for any 4 points
p,q,r,s\in M
,
the largest two sums of distances among
d(p,q)+d(r,s)
,
d(p,r)+d(q,s)
,
d(p,s)+d(q,r)
differ
by at most a constant
\delta
.
As shown by Gromov
[
6
]
, any metric space of
n
points with hyperbolicity
\delta
can be
embedded into a tree-metric with additive distortion
O(\delta\log n)
. The Poincaré half-space
has hyperbolicity
O(1)
, so this result applies to a more general type of hyperbolic spaces
than our embedding of
\mathbb{H}^{D}

into a graph metric with
O(n)
edges. On the other hand, we obtain an additive distortion
O(1)

in constant dimension
D=O(1)
, while Gromov’s construction gives
O(\log n)
in our case.
Chepoi et al.
[
4
]
give additive
O(\delta\log n)
spanners with
O(\delta n)
edges
for unit graph metrics (i.e. metrics for graphs where each edge weight is equal to 1) that are

\delta
-hyperbolic. Compared with our result, it allows arbitrary hyperbolicity, but it is restricted
to unit graphs, and the distortion is logarithmic.
Lee and Krautghamer
[
10
]
present multiplicative spanners with
O(n)
edges for locally doubling,
geodesic Gromov-hyperbolic spaces.

There has also been some work on problems other than spanners and embeddings in hyperbolic spaces.
Lee and Krautghamer
[
10
]
presented an ANN data structure for
geodesic and locally doubling Gromov-hyperbolic spaces, with
O(1)
additive error,

O(n^{2})
space usage and
O(\log^{2}n)
query time.
Kisfaludi-Bak et al. gave an algorithm for the TSP problem in
\mathbb{H}^{2}

[
8
]
.

More recently, Kisfaludi-Bak and van Wordragen
[
9
]
gave an ANN data
structure and Steiner spanners for
\mathbb{H}^{D}
with
multiplicative
error
(1+\varepsilon)
.
Their approach is based on the same binary tiling that we use, but they derive from it
a non-trivial type of quadtree that is taylored for providing multiplicative guarantees in
hyperbolic spaces. The lower bound
that they present on spanners without Steiner points imply that in order to an achieve additive error
as we do, Steiner points are also required.

1.3
Our approach.

We use an approximation of the Poincaré halfspace
(\mathbb{H}^{D},d_{H})

by a discrete metric space
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
where the points are centers of hypercubes (called
cells
)
whose sizes grow exponentially with
y
. (See Figure
1
b.)
The distance between two points
p,q
is the minimum number
of cells that are crossed when going from
p
to
q
.
This discrete model was mentioned,
for instance, by Cannon et al.
[
3
]
.
We present these models of hyperbolic spaces in Section
2
.

We introduce a different distance function
d_{2}
over
\mathbb{B}^{D}
: we go upwards from
p
and
q

until we reach adjacent squares, and then we connect the subpaths using a single horizontal edge.
(See Figure
1
c.).
This model is not a metric as it does not satisfy the triangle inequality.
In Section
3
, we show that
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
differ by at most 2.
Then in Section
4
, we show that over
\mathbb{B}^{D}
, the metric
d_{H}

differs from
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
by an additive error
O(\log D)
.

These discrete models lend themselves well to the use of quadtrees, and the efficient constructions
of our embeddings, spanners and ANN-data structure, as well as the bound on their size, are based
on compressed quadtrees.
In Section
5
, we present the compressed quadtree data structure that we use.

This structure allows us, in Section
6
, to show that the
overlay of the shortest paths according to
d_{2}
has linear complexity, and hence gives a 2-additive spanner of
linear size with respect to
d_{1}
. (See Figure
7
.)
In Section
7
, we use the spanner
we constructed in the discrete model to obtain an embedding with
O(\log D)
additive
distortion into the metric of a graph that has
2^{O(D)}n
vertices and edges.
Then using a transitive-closure spanner, we turn it into a spanner with respect to
d_{H}
.
This spanner is embedded in
\mathbb{H}^{D}
, and has
2^{O(D)}n
edges and Steiner points.

Our data structure for approximate near neighbor (ANN) searching is presented in Section
8
.
This data structure is an approximate Voronoi diagram for the discrete model
\mathbb{B}^{D}
,
that is based on compressed quadtrees. This construction is done in two steps. We first compute the
subdivision induced by the (minimal) quadtree that records the input point set
P
. Then we refine this
subdivision by adding additional quadtree boxes where it is needed.

The Poincaré halfplane
(\mathbb{H}^{2},d_{H})

It is a model of 2-dimensional hyperbolic space with constant negative curvature
-1

The halfplane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
consists of the points
p=(x(p),z(p))

.
The geodesics in
(\mathbb{H}^{2},d_{H})

x

(See Figure

More generally, when
D\geqslant 2

\mathbb{H}^{D}
consists of the points

p=(x(p),z(p))

The distance

p
to

d_{H}(p,q)=2\arsinh\left(\frac{1}{2}{\frac{\|pq\|}{\sqrt{z(p)z(q)}}}\right)

In the special case where

The geodesics in

The first discrete model
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
is defined
using a binary tiling of
\mathbb{H}^{D}

\mathcal{C}=[k_{1}2^{i},(k_{1}+1)2^{i}]\times[k_{2}2^{i},(k_{2}+1)2^{i}]\times\dots\times[k_{D-1}2^{i},(k_{D-1}+1)2^{i}]\times[2^{i},2^{i+1}]

The

The

of a cell

\mathcal{C}
. The

\mathcal{C}
,

has

\mathcal{C}
is either the parent of
\mathcal{C}
, or a parent of
an ancestor of
\mathcal{C}
. A descendant of
\mathcal{C}
is either a child of
\mathcal{C}
, or a descendant of a child
of
\mathcal{C}
.
The

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}

We denote by

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
.
The level
\mathrm{lev}(b(\mathcal{C}))

\mathcal{C}

3
Shortest paths in the discrete model

In this section, we study the structure, and give bounds on the length of
d_{1}
-paths
and
d_{2}
-paths.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
, and suppose that there is a
d_{1}
-path from
p
to

q
containing at least one upward move. Then there is a
d_{1}
-path from

p
to
q
whose first move is upward.

Proof.

Let
\rho=(p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
be a path of length
k=d_{1}(p_{0},p_{k})
from
p=p_{0}
to
q=p_{k}
,
containing at least one upward move. If the first move of
\rho
is upward, then we
are done. Otherwise,
Let
(p_{i},p_{i+1})
be the first upward move, hence
(p_{i-1},p_{i})
is horizontal
or downward. If
(p_{i-1},p_{i})
is downward, then we have
p_{i-1}=p_{i+1}
, so
the path obtained from
\rho
by
deleting
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
is a path from
p
to
q
of length
d_{1}(p,q)-2
,
a contradiction.

Now suppose that
(p_{i-1},p_{i})
is horizontal. As
p_{i-1}
and
p_{i}
are horizontal
neighbors, their parents either are equal, or are horizontal neighbors. So the parent of
p_{i-1}

is either
p_{i+1}
, or is
a horizontal neighbor of
p_{i+1}
. The parent of
p_{i-1}
cannot be
p_{i+1}
,
because if it were the case, we would obtain a path from
p
to
q
of length

d_{1}(p,q)-1
by deleting
p_{i}
from
\rho
. So the parent of
p_{i-1}
is a horizontal neighbor

p^{\prime}_{i}
of
p_{i+1}
. Hence, we obtain a path
\rho^{\prime}
from
p
to
q
of length
d_{1}(p,q)

by replacing
p_{i}
with
p^{\prime}_{i}
.

The first upward move of
\rho^{\prime}
is now
p_{i-1}p^{\prime}_{i}
,
hence it has been moved one position to the left. By repeating this process
i-1
times,
we find a
d_{1}
-path from
p
to
q
that starts with an
upward move.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
, there is a
d_{1}
-path from
p
to
q
that
consists of
m_{1}
upward moves, followed by
m_{2}
horizontal moves, and finally
m_{3}
downward
moves, where
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

By applying Lemma
1
repeatedly, we obtain a
d_{1}
-path from
p
to

q
consisting of a sequence
\alpha
of upward moves, followed by a sequence
\beta
of horizontal or
downward moves. Let
\gamma
be the path obtained by following
\beta
backward, hence

\gamma
consists of upward or horizontal moves. We apply Lemma
1

to
\gamma
, until we obtain a path
\gamma
consisting of upward moves followed by
horizontal moves. Then the path obtained by following
\alpha
, and then following
\gamma

backwards, has the desired property.
∎

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
be two points at the same level
i
.
Their
horizontal distance

\lambda(p,q)
is the length of a shortest path from
p
to
q

consisting of horizontal moves only. It is given by the expression

\lambda(p,q)=\|x(p)x(q)\|_{\infty}/2^{i}
where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is the
L^{\infty}
norm.

Lemma 3
.

If
p
and
q
are two points of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
and
\mathrm{lev}(p)=\mathrm{lev}(q)
, then

2\lambda(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})-1\leqslant\lambda(p,q)\leqslant 2\lambda(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})+1
,
where
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
are the parents of
p
and
q
, respectively

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{i}=\{-2^{i-1},0,2^{i-1}\}^{D-1}
, then we have
\{x(p^{\prime})-x(p),x(q^{\prime})-x(q)\}\subset\Delta_{i}
.
By the triangle inequality, it implies that

\|x(p^{\prime})x(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}-2^{i}\leqslant\|x(p)x(q)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|x(p^{\prime})x(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}+2^{i}.

The result follows by dividing these inequalities by
2^{i}
.
∎

We now obtain a recurrence relation for
d_{1}(p,q)
when
p
and
q
are at the same level.

Lemma 4
.

If
p
and
q
are two points of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
such that
\mathrm{lev}(p)=\mathrm{lev}(q)
,
and if
\lambda=\lambda(p,q)
is their horizontal distance, then

d_{1}(p,q)=\begin{cases}\lambda&\text{ if }\lambda\leqslant 4,\text{ and}\\
2+d_{1}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})&\text{ if }\lambda\geqslant 5.\end{cases}

where
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
are the respective parents of
p
and
q
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2
, there is a shortest path from
p
to
q

that either consists of horizontal moves only (type 1), or that goes through

p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
(type 2).

Any path of type 2 has length at least 3. So if
\lambda\leqslant 3
, there
is a shortest path of type 1, and thus
d_{1}(p,q)=\lambda
.

If
\lambda=4
, then by Lemma
3
, we have
\lambda(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\geqslant 2
,
so any path of type 2 has length more than 3. So there is a shortest path
of type 1, and thus

Compressed quadtrees

In this section, we present
compressed quadtrees
,
which will be needed for our graph metric embeddings and for approximate near neighbor searching.
Compressed quadtrees are presented in the book by Har-Peled
[
7
]
.
Our quadtrees will record points in
\mathbb{B}^{D}
, which correspond to quadtree boxes
in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
. (See the discussion below.) Hence, our quadtrees record a set of quadtree
boxes instead of recording a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
. This does not affect the
time and space bounds
[
7
, Lemma 2.11]
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cell of our binary tiling of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
.
The
vertical projection

\mathcal{C}_{x}
of
\mathcal{C}
is the set of the projections
x(p)
of
all the points
p\in\mathcal{C}
. Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{x}
is a box in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
, and more precisely,

\mathcal{C}_{x}
is of the form

[k_{1}2^{-i},(k_{1}+1)2^{-i}]\times\dots\times[k_{D-1}2^{-i},(k_{D-1}+1)2^{-i}]\text{ where }k_{1},\dots,k_{D-1}\in\mathbb{Z}.

A box
\mathcal{C}_{x}
of this form is called a
quadtree box
.
Thus, each point
b\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
corresponds to exactly one quadtree box

\mathcal{C}_{x}(b)\subset\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
, and the corresponding cell
\mathcal{C}(b)
of our binary tiling of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
.

It allows us extend the notions of children, parents, ancestors, descendants and levels to
quadtree boxes.
So for any two cells
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of our binary tiling, we say that
\mathcal{C}_{x}

is a child (resp. parent, ancestor, descendant) of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{x}
if
\mathcal{C}
is
a child (resp. parent, ancestor, descendant) of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. The level
\mathrm{lev}(\mathcal{C}_{x})

of the quadtree cell
\mathcal{C}_{x}
is
\mathrm{lev}(\mathcal{C})
.

Let
Q
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{B}^{D}
, such that
x(Q)\subset(0,1)^{D-1}
.
A compressed quadtree
\mathcal{T}
storing
Q
is a tree such that each node
\nu

of
\mathcal{T}
records one cell
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
of our binary tiling, and, equivalently,
the quadtree box
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
and the center point
b(\nu)=b(\mathcal{C})\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
.
This tree is constructed as follows.
A cell
\mathcal{C}
that contains no point of
Q

•

.

6
Spanner in the discrete model

In this section, we give a 2-additive spanner

Let

We construct the compressed quadtree

Let

For a given horizontal neighbor

.

7
Embedding into a graph metric and spanner for
\mathbb{H}^{D}

We can embed any set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{H}^{D}
into a graph metric as follows.
First we map each
p\in P
to the point
b(p)\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
. Then we construct the
spanner
S
for these points with respect to
d_{1}
as in Theorem
16
.
We multiply all the edge lengths of
S
by
\ln(2)
, thus obtaining a graph
S^{\prime}

such that the length
d_{S^{\prime}}(p,q)
of a shortest path in
S^{\prime}
between any two points

p,q\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
satisfies

\ln(2)\cdot d_{1}(p,q)\leqslant d_{S^{\prime}}(p,q)\leqslant\ln(2)\cdot d_{2}(p,q).

By Theorem
14
, this path length approximates
d_{H}(p,q)
within an additive
error
O(\log D)
. In summary:

Corollary 17
.

Given a set
P\subset\mathbb{H}^{D}
of
n
points, we can compute in time
2^{O(D)}n\log n
a
positively weighted graph
G(V,E)

that has
2^{O(D)}n
vertices and edges, and a mapping
b:P\to V
, such that

|d_{H}(p_{i},p_{j})-d_{G}(b(p_{i}),b(p_{j}))|=O(\log D)
for any
p_{i},p_{j}\in P
.

The graph from Corollary
17

is not a spanner in the sense that the length of an edge
b(p)b(q)
,
p,q\in\mathbb{H}^{D}
is

\ln(2)\cdot d_{1}(b(p),b(q))
instead
of

. If we set the edge weights to be
d_{H}(b(p),b(q))
for each edge

, then we introduce
a constant additive error for each edge of
S
, and since a shortest path in
S
may consist of
\Omega(n)

edges, we will no longer have
O(\log D)
additive error.

In order to obtain an additive spanner
for
P\subset\mathbb{H}^{D}
that is embedded in
\mathbb{H}^{D}
, we will add more edges, which will act as shortcuts.
So let
T
be the forest obtained by removing the horizontal edges from

, and let

be a fixed integer.
We orient all the edges of

upwards.
We construct a
k
-transitive closure spanner

of
S

[
11
,
13
]
. This graph

has
O(n\cdot\lambda_{k}(n))
edges, where
\lambda_{k}(n)
is the
k
-th row of the
inverse Ackermann function. The vertices of
T_{k}
are the points
b(p)
,
p\in P
and
the edges of
T_{k}
are a superset of the edges of
T
, also oriented upwards. The key property of

is that, if there is a path from vertex
p
to
q
in

p
to
q
of
length at most
k

k
-transitive closure spanner can be computed in
O(n\cdot\lambda_{k}(n))

time
[
13
, Algorithm L]
.

Our Spanner
S_{k}
is obtained from

by adding all the horizontal edges of
S
, and by adding
each point
p\in P
as a vertex, together with the edge
pb(p)
.
Each edge
pq
of

is assigned the weight
d_{H}(p,q)
. In particular, an edge
pb(p)
has
weight
O(\log D)
by Lemma
8
.

Let
p,q\in P
. We now prove that there is a path of length
d_{H}(p,q)+O(k\log D)
in

. By construction, there
is a path in

from

to

of length

that consists of a vertical path
\rho

b(p)

to a vertex
\bar{b}(p)
, then a horizontal edge from
\bar{b}(p)
to a vertex
\bar{b}(q)
,
followed by a vertical path
\rho^{\prime}

\bar{b}(q)
to

.

In

of at most

The length of each edge

in

, which is
\ln(2)\cdot d_{2}(v,w)+O(\log D)

.
So the length of

(i.e. the sum of the weights of its edges) is
\ln(2)\cdot d_{2}(b(p),\bar{b}(p))+O(k\log D)
. Similarly,
there is a path

from

to

in

,

and Theorem

.

Theorem 18
.

Let

be an integer, and let

a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{H}^{D}
. We can construct in

time an

purely additive spanner of

2^{O(D)}n

2^{O(D)}n\cdot\lambda_{k}(n)

8
Approximate Voronoi diagram (AVD)

In this section, we first show how to answer exact near-neighbor queries
in
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{2})
,
and then we briefly explain how this result yields ANN data structures for

(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})
and
\mathbb{H}^{D}
with additive error 2 and
O(\log D)
, respectively.

So let
P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{B}^{D}
.
For a query point
q\in\mathbb{B}^{D}
, we want to find a point
n_{2}(q)\in P

such that
d_{2}(q,n_{2}(q))
is minimum. As there can be several closest point to
q
,
we break the ties by taking
n_{2}(q)
to be the point
p_{i}
such that

d_{2}(p_{i},q)=\min_{j}d_{2}(p_{j},q)
and
i
is minimum.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
x(P)\subset[1/4,1/2]^{D-1}
.
If
x(q)\notin[0,1]^{D-1}
, then
n_{2}(q)
is the highest point in
P
, which
the data structure described below can return in constant time.

This data structure records an
approximate Voronoi diagram
(AVD), which is a partition
of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
in
2^{O(D)}n
regions
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
such that each region
V_{i}

is associated with a set
R_{i}\subset P
of at most
2^{O(D)}

representative points
.
For any point
q\in V_{i}
, we have
n_{2}(q)\in R_{i}
.

In the definition above, the AVD allows us to find an
exact
nearest
neighbor with respect to the second discrete model
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{2})
.
In the first discrete model
(\mathbb{B}^{D},d_{1})

and in
H^{D}
, it will allow us to find an approximate near-neighbor, with
additive error 2 and
O(\log D)
, respectively.

As in Section
6
, we first compute the compressed quadtree

\mathcal{T}
that records our input point set
P
. This quadtree
\mathcal{T}
has
O(n)
nodes, it can
be computed in time
O(Dn\log n)
time, and it allows us to perform
cell queries in
O(D\log n)
time.

Refinement step

Suppose that
\nu
is an ordinary node of
\mathcal{T}
. Let
b(\nu)
be the corresponding
point of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
, then
b(\nu)
is the only point of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
contained in
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
.
So we associate
\nu
with a Voronoi region consisting only of
b(\nu)
, and
with a single representative
n_{2}(b(\nu))
.

On the other hand, if
\nu
is a leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
, then the descendants of
\mathcal{C}(\nu)

do not correspond to nodes of
\mathcal{T}
. So we would like
\nu
to be associated with
a Voronoi region
V_{i}
that covers
b(\nu)
and all its descendants. The issue here is
that we may need
\Omega(n)
representatives for
V_{i}
, if for instance,
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)

is adjacent to
\Omega(n)
smaller, disjoint cells of
\mathcal{T}
.

In order to solve this problem, we will break
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
into smaller cells, by
inserting more boxes into our quadtree. We call this step of the construction
the
refinement step
. For each
node
\nu
of
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
is not empty,
we insert into our compressed quadtree all the boxes that are
horizontal neighbors of
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
, thus obtaining a quadtree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

that is associated with a finer subdivision of
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
.
(See Figure
8
.) In particular, for a compressed node
\nu
,
we insert the neighbors of the outer cell and the inner cell, that is, the horizontal neighbors
of
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
and
\mathcal{C}(\nu_{1})
, where
\nu_{1}
is the child of
\nu
.

Figure 8
:
(Left) The subdivision corresponding to the initial quadtree
\mathcal{T}
.
(Right) The subdivision after refinement, corresponding to the quadtree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

We say that two quadtree boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
are
adjacent
if they
intersect along their boundaries, but not in their interior.
The two lemmas below will help us bound the number of representative points for
each cell of our AVD.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\nu^{\prime}
be a leaf cell of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Then the number of
compressed nodes
\nu
of
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)

is adjacent to
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
is
2^{O(D)}
.

Figure 9
:
Proof of Lemma
19
. The node
\nu_{2}
is a compressed node.

Proof.

The leaf cells and the regions of the compressed nodes of
\mathcal{T}

form a subdivision of
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
.
The leaf cells and the regions of the compressed nodes of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}

form a refinement of this subdivision.
So there are at most
2^{O(D)}
such
cells
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
adjacent to
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
of size larger than or
equal to the size of
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
. So in the following, we consider
cells
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
whose size is smaller than the size of
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that there are at least
3^{D-1}
such nodes

\nu
such that the size of
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
is smaller than that of
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
.
Then two of them,
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu_{1})
and
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu_{2})
, must be contained
in a quadtree box
\mathcal{C}_{x}^{\prime\prime}
that has the same size as
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
and
is adjacent to it. (See Figure
9
.) This is impossible, as it
would have caused
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
to be partitioned at the refinement step. ∎

Lemma 20
.

Let
\nu^{\prime}
be a compressed node of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Then the number of
compressed nodes
\nu
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)

is adjacent to
\mathrm{rg}(\nu^{\prime})
is
2^{O(D)}
.

Proof.

The same proof as for Lemma
19
shows that there are
2^{O(D)}
such
cells that are adjacent to
\mathrm{rg}(\nu^{\prime})
along its outer boundary. A similar argument
again shows that there are
2^{O(D)}
such cells adjacent along the inner boundary.
∎

The approximate Voronoi diagram

Our AVD consists of one region
V(\nu^{\prime})
for each node
\nu^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
:

•

If
\nu^{\prime}
is an ordinary node, then the Voronoi region
V(\nu^{\prime})
is
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
.

•

If
\nu^{\prime}
is a leaf node, then the Voronoi region
V(\nu^{\prime})
is the union
of
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
and all its descendants.
In other words,
V(\nu^{\prime})
is the set of all the points that are
in or below
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
.

•

If
\nu^{\prime}
is a compressed node, let
\nu^{\prime}_{1}
be its child. Then
V(\nu^{\prime})
is
the set of all the points that are on or below
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
, and are not on or below

\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime}_{1})
.

Preprocessing

We first compute, for every node
\nu^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
is not empty,
the point
h(\nu^{\prime})
in
P

with highest
z
-coordinate that is recorded in the subtree rooted at
\nu^{\prime}
.
(Again, we break ties by taking the point
p_{i}
with smallest index
i
among
these highest points.)
We can compute all these points in linear time by traversing the tree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
from bottom
to top.

Next, we compute the
d_{2}
-nearest neighbor
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})=n_{2}(b(\nu^{\prime}))
for all the nodes
\nu^{\prime}
of

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
as follows. We compute these points by traversing the tree from top to bottom, so
when we compute
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
, we already know
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime\prime})
where
\nu^{\prime\prime}

is the parent of
\nu^{\prime}
. Let
\rho
be the
d_{2}
-shortest path from
b(\nu^{\prime})
to

n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
.
The 4 possible cases are listed below. We compute the nearest point for each case,
and record the nearest one as
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
.

1.

If
\rho
goes through
b(\nu^{\prime\prime})
, then

n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})=n_{2}(\nu^{\prime\prime})
, and we are done as we already know
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime\prime})
.

2.

If
\rho
is a downward path, or if
b(\nu)\in P
, then
n_{2}(\nu)=h(\nu)
.

3.

If
\rho
starts with a horizontal move from
b(\nu^{\prime})
, then

n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
is the highest point that is on or below a cell
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that
is a horizontal neighbor of
\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime})
. We can find this highest point
in
O(D\log n)
time by performing a cell query in
\mathcal{T}
, and since there are
2^{O(D)}

such cells
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we find
n_{2}(\nu)
in
2^{O(D)}\log n
time.

4.

Otherwise,
\rho
first goes upward from
b(\nu^{\prime})
, then follows a bridge
r^{\prime}r
,
and then goes downward to
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
. As
\rho
does not go through
b(\nu^{\prime\prime})
, it
follows that
\nu^{\prime\prime}
is a compressed node. Then
\mathcal{C}(r)
is not stored
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, because if it were the case, we would have created a node
for
\mathcal{C}(r^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, and
\nu^{\prime\prime}
could not be the parent of
\nu^{\prime}
.
So
r
is in
V(\nu)
for some
compressed node
\nu
of
\mathcal{T}
, and we have
n_{2}(b(\nu))=h(\nu)
.
As
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
must be adjacent, by Lemma
20
,
there are
2^{O(D)}
candidates for
\nu
, so we can find it in
2^{O(D)}\log n
time.

Finding the representative points

We now explain how we choose the representative points for each region
V(\nu^{\prime})
.
For each node
\nu^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, we pick
n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
as a representative point.
If
\nu^{\prime}
is an ordinary cell, then we do not add any other representative point.

If
\nu^{\prime}
is a leaf node or a compressed node, then for each compressed node
\nu
of

\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
is adjacent to
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
or
\mathrm{rg}(\nu^{\prime})
, respectively,
we add
h(\nu)

as a representative point. By Lemma
19
and Lemma
20
, there
are
2^{O(D)}
such points.

We now prove that for each point
q
in a Voronoi cell
V(\nu^{\prime})
,
one of these representative points is

n_{2}(q)
. So suppose that
n_{2}(q)\neq n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
, and thus
\nu^{\prime}
is
not an ordinary node. Let
\rho
be the
d_{2}
-path
from
q
to
n_{2}(q)
. As
\nu^{\prime}
is either a leaf node or a compressed node,
there is no point in
P
below
q
, so
\rho
cannot be a downward path.
As
n_{2}(q)\neq n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
, it cannot be an upward path either. So it must have
a bridge
r^{\prime}r
, where
r
is on or above
n_{2}(q)
. If
\mathcal{C}(r)
is recorded
in a node
\nu(r)
of
\mathcal{T}
, then at the refinement stage, we
must have inserted into
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
the node
\nu(r^{\prime})
such that

\mathcal{C}(\nu(r^{\prime}))=\mathcal{C}(r^{\prime})
, and then
n_{2}(q)=n_{2}(\nu^{\prime})
, a contradiction.
Therefore,
\mathcal{C}(r)
is not recorded in
\mathcal{T}
. Hence,
r
is in

\mathrm{rg}(\nu)
for some compressed node
\nu
of
\mathcal{T}
, such that

\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu)
is adjacent to
\mathcal{C}_{x}(\nu^{\prime})
. It follows that
n_{2}(q)=h(\nu)
,
which is one of our representative points.

Result

As
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
records
2^{O(D)}n
nodes, it can be
computed in
2^{O(D)}n\log n
time, and it can be queries in
O(D+\log n)

time. Since our data structure returns an exact nearest neighbor with respect to
d_{2}
,
by Theorem
6
, it returns an approximate near neighbor with respect to

d_{1}
, with additive error 2.
So we obtained the following result:

Theorem 21
.

Let
P
be a subset of
\mathbb{B}^{D}
of size
n
.
Then we can compute in
2^{O(D)}n\log n
time an AVD of
P
with
2^{O(D)}n
regions,
and
2^{O(D)}
representative points per region.
Using this diagram, given a query point
q\in\mathbb{H}^{D}
,
a point
n_{2}(q)\in P
such that
d_{2}(q,n_{2}(q))
is minimum can be returned
in
2^{O(D)}+O(\log n)
time.
This point satisfies
d_{1}(q,n_{2}(q))\leqslant d_{1}(q,n_{1}(q))+2
,
where
n_{1}(q)
is a point in
P
such that
d_{1}(q,n_{1}(q))
is minimum.

The discrete AVD presented above can also be turned into an AVD for a set of points
P

in
H^{D}
, by constructing the
d_{2}
-AVD for the points
\{b(p):p\in P\}
,
and replacing each point
p
in a Voronoi region by the whole box
\mathcal{C}(p)
, and thus
we obtain a partition of
[0,1]^{D-1}\times(0,2]\subset\mathbb{H}^{D}
.
Then for a query point
q\in H^{D}
, we return a point in
P
such
that
b(p)=n_{2}(b(q))
.
Theorem
14
implies that this point is an

O(\log D)
-additive approximate near neighbor:

Corollary 22
.

Let
P
be a subset of
\mathbb{H}^{D}
of size
n
.
Then we can compute in
2^{O(D)}n\log n
time an AVD of
P
with
2^{O(D)}n
regions,
and
2^{O(D)}
representative points per region.
Using this diagram, for any query point
q
,
we can return in
2^{O(D)}+O(\log n)
time
a point
r\in P
such that
d_{H}(q,r)\leqslant d_{H}(q,n_{H}(q))+O(\log D)
,
where
n_{H}(q)
is a the point in
P
such that
d_{H}(q,n_{H}(q))
is minimum.
