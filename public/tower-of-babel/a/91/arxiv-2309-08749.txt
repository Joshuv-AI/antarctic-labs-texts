A Hermitian refinement of symplectic Clifford analysis
David Eelbode, Guner Muarem

Abstract
In this paper we develop the Hermitian refinement of symplectic Clifford analysis, by introducing a complex structure 𝕁 \mathbb{J} on the canonical symplectic manifold ( ℝ 2 ​ n , ω 0 ) (\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega_{0}) . This gives rise to two symplectic Dirac operators D s D_{s} and D t D_{t} (in the sense of Habermann [ 8 ] ), leading to a 𝔲 ⁡ ( n ) \mathfrak{u}(n) -invariant system of equations on ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} . We discuss the solution space for this system, culminating in a Fischer decomposition for the space of polynomials on ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} with values in the symplectic spinors. To make this decomposition explicit, we will construct the associated embedding factors using a transvector algebra.

Introduction

This paper is to be situated in the framework of so-called symplectic (Clifford) analysis, a function theory which focuses on solutions for the symplectic Dirac operator
D_{s}
(see
[
8
]
for basic information). This is a first-order differential operator which generalises the classical Dirac operator

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathsf{Spin}(n)

In this paper, we will consider a symmetry reduction from the symplectic Lie group

Before introducing this symplectic analogue, we will shortly review the basics of the Hermitian refinement of (classical) Clifford analysis, breaking down the symmetry of the orthogonal group

\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)

\mathsf{U}(n)\times\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)

2
Hermitian Clifford analysis

The key ingredient in the transition from the classical case to Hermitean refinement is a complex structure
\mathbb{J}
, given by

\mathbb{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathsf{SO}(2n)\ .

On the standard orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
this complex structure
\mathbb{J}
thus acts as follows:
\mathbb{J}(e_{k})=-e_{k+n}
and
\mathbb{J}(e_{k+n})=e_{k}
, for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Note that the coordinates will be referred to as
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1}\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
in this paper. The (classical) Dirac operator
\underline{\partial}_{x}
and its (Fischer) dual
\underline{x}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
are given by

\displaystyle\underline{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(e_{k}x_{k}+e_{n+k}y_{k}\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\underline{\partial}_{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(e_{k}\partial_{x_{k}}+e_{n+k}\partial_{y_{k}}\big)\ ,

and using the complex structure
\mathbb{J}
one can define a twisted (or rotated) version:

\displaystyle\underline{x}^{\mathbb{J}}:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{J}[\underline{x}]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(y_{k}e_{2k-1}-x_{k}e_{2k})

\displaystyle{\partial^{\mathbb{J}}_{\underline{x}}}:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{J}[\underline{\partial}_{x}]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(e_{2k-1}\partial_{y_{k}}-e_{2k}\partial_{x_{k}})\ .

Note that
\mathbb{J}
acts on the basis vectors
e_{k}
here. In the definition below,
\mathbb{S}
stands for the space of Dirac spinors on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
(i.e. the sum of positive and negative Weyl spinors), typically realised using a minimal left ideal in the complex Clifford algebra
\mathbb{C}_{2n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
\mathbb{S}
-valued function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
satisfying the equations
\underline{\partial}_{x}f={\partial^{\mathbb{J}}_{\underline{x}}}f=0
is called
Hermitian monogenic
(or
h
-monogenic). One further defines the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}^{h}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S}):=\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S})\cap\big(\ker(\underline{\partial}_{x})\cap\ker(\partial^{\mathbb{J}}_{\underline{x}})\big)\ ,

containing
k
-homogeneous polynomials which are
h
-monogenic.

There is another way to introduce a pair of Hermitian Dirac operators, using the Witt basis for
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
:

Definition 2.2
.

The Witt basis vectors for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{k}-ie_{n+k}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\dagger}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{k}+ie_{n+k}\right)\ .

The Hermitian Dirac operators and their adjoints are then introduced as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\underline{\partial}_{z},\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger})=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\dagger}\partial_{z_{k}},\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathfrak{f}_{k}\partial_{\overline{z}_{k}}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad(\underline{z},\underline{z}^{\dagger})=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathfrak{f}_{k}z_{k},\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\dagger}\overline{z}_{k}\right)\ .

The Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
can then be written as
\underline{\partial}_{x}=2(\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}-\underline{\partial}_{z})
, and similarly for its adjoint
\underline{x}=\underline{z}-\underline{z}^{\dagger}
. This shows why Hermitian Clifford analysis is a
refinement
of classical Clifford analysis:
h
-monogenics always belong to the kernel of the Dirac operator
\underline{\partial}_{x}
, but not necessarily the other way around.

Remark 2.3
.

The operators
\underline{\partial}_{z}
and
\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}
can be used to factorise the Laplacian
\Delta
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, since
\Delta=4(\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}\underline{\partial}_{z}+\underline{\partial}_{z}\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger})=4\{\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger},\underline{\partial}_{z}\}
. This implies that every
h
-monogenic function is harmonic.

The Witt basis can be used to define a simple model for the spinor space
\mathbb{S}
in the orthogonal setting. Indeed, one has that
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{C}_{2n}I
where
I
is the primitive idempotent
I=I_{1}\cdots I_{n}
with
I_{k}=\mathfrak{f}_{k}\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{\dagger}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Due to the fact that
\mathfrak{f}_{k}I=0
, the spinor space
\mathbb{S}
reduces to the action of the (complex) Grassmann algebra
\Lambda^{\dagger}_{n}
spanned by the elements
\{\mathfrak{f}^{\dagger}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, which means that we can write
\mathbb{S}=\Lambda^{\dagger}_{n}I
. This implies that the spinor space decomposes into ‘homogeneous parts’ as

\mathbb{S}=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n}\mathbb{S}_{(r)}=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n}(\Lambda^{\dagger}_{n})^{r}I

where
(\Lambda^{\dagger}_{n})^{r}
consists of all
r
-vectors in
\Lambda^{\dagger}_{n}
. This decomposition is certainly not spin-invariant, but it does carry a (left) multiplicative
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-action, see e.g.
[
2
]
. The invariant subspaces
\mathbb{S}_{(r)}
can be characterised as eigenspaces of the so-called
spin-Euler operator

\beta=n-\sum_{k}I_{k}
, measuring the ‘degree of homogeneity’
r
.

Now that we have introduced the Hermitian Dirac operators
\underline{\partial}_{z}
and
\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}
and discussed the decomposition of the spinor space
\mathbb{S}
into
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-irreducible representations, we focus on the algebra generated by these Dirac operators and their (Fischer) duals. Recall that in the orthogonal case the operators
\underline{\partial}_{x}
and
\underline{x}
give rise to the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)
, whereas in the Hermitian setting one obtains the algebra
\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)
. For further details, such as the definitions for the generators and their (anti-)commutation relations, we refer to
[
2
]
. There, one can also find the following theorem:

Theorem 2.4

(Hermitian Howe duality)
.

The space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S})
of spinor-valued polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
can be decomposed in a multiplicity-free way, using the Howe dual pair
\operatorname{\mathsf{U}}(n)\times\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)
.

Using the identification
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\leftrightarrow(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, where we have put
z_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}
, we get a canonical identification

\displaystyle P(\underline{x},\underline{y})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\ \leftrightarrow\ P(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})\ .

Further structure can then be imposed on this space of polynomials:

Definition 2.5
.

Let
a,b
be two non-negative integers. A polynomial
P(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})
is said to be
bi-homogeneous
of degree
(a,b)
if

\displaystyle P(\lambda\underline{z},\overline{\lambda}\overline{\underline{z}})=\lambda^{a}\overline{\lambda}^{b}P(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})

for all
\underline{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. We denote the space of the
bi-homogeneous
polynomials of degree
(a,b)
by
\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})
or
\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})
.

Remark 2.6
.

Recall that in the classical case, the degree of homogeneity of a polynomial
P_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C})
was measured by the Euler operator
\mathbb{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}
. In the Hermitian setting, one defines the so-called
h
-Euler operators
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}_{z}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{z}_{j}\partial_{\overline{z}_{j}}\ ,

the eigenvalues of which are precisely the bi-degree parameters
a
and
b
.

Definition 2.7
.

We define
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})
as the space of
harmonic polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
of bi-degree

(a,b)
. This means that
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C}):=\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\cap\ker(\Delta)
.

Denoting the space of
k
-harmonic polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
by means of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})
, we then have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{k=a+b}\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\ ,

which expresses a irreducible representation for
\mathsf{SO}(2n)
at the left-hand side (harmonic polynomials of degree
k
) as a direct sum of irreducible representations for the subgroup
\mathsf{U}(n)
at the right-hand side. A key result in understanding the Hermitean refinement of the Fischer decomposition is the following theorem:

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose
H_{a,b}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})\in\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}
with
0<r<n
and
a,b>0
, then
H_{a,b}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle H_{a,b}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})

\displaystyle=M_{a,b}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})+\underline{z}M_{a-1,b}^{(r+1)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})+\underline{z}^{\dagger}M_{a,b-1}^{(r-1)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})

\displaystyle+\left((a+r-1)\underline{z}\underline{z}^{\dagger}-(b+m-r-1)\underline{z}^{\dagger}\underline{z}\right)M_{a-1,b-1}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}}),

where all
M_{a,b}^{(r)}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})\in\ker(\underline{\partial}_{z})\cap\ker(\underline{\partial}^{\dagger}_{z})
, i.e. are
h
-monogenic.

Remark 2.9
.

There are a few trivial cases for the decomposition above, which explains the restrictions on the parameters
a,b
and
r
(see
[
2
]
for more details).

3
Hermitian symplectic Dirac operators

Just like in the previous section, we start by introducing the rotated (or ‘twisted’) symplectic Dirac operator and its dual, using the complex structure
\mathbb{J}
from above. For that purpose, we recall their definitions (see
[
8
]
):

\displaystyle D_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(iq_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}-\partial_{q_{j}}\partial_{x_{j}}\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad X_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(y_{j}\partial_{q_{j}}+iq_{j}x_{j}\big)\ .

These operators act on symplectic spinor-valued functions
f(x_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, whereby the symplectic spinor space can be realised as the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C})
of rapidly decreasing functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. These then correspond to the smooth vectors of the oscillator representation for the metaplectic group
\mathsf{Mp}(2n)
, see for instance
[
8
,
11
]
. We will use the variable
\underline{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the argument for these functions, and use the symbol
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
to refer to this spinor space (where the infinity symbol stresses the fact that the underlying metaplectic representation is infinite-dimensional).

Remark 3.1
.

We denote the even and odd part of
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
, containing even and odd functions respectively, by means of
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{-}
. These spaces then define the (positive and negative) spinor representations for the symplectic group
\mathsf{Sp}(2n)
, or its associated Lie algebra. The highest weights of these representations are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{+}\leftrightarrow\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{sp}(2n)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{-}\leftrightarrow\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{3}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{sp}(2n)}\ ,

and they provide examples of so-called
completely pointed modules
, see
[
3
,
4
]
.

Now that we have defined the values of the functions that will be considered in this paper, we can use the complex structure
\mathbb{J}
, acting on the column vector
(q_{j},-i\partial_{q_{j}})^{T}
, to arrive at the following operators:

Definition 3.2
.

The twisted symplectic Dirac operator
D_{t}
and its (Fischer) dual
X_{t}
are given by:

\displaystyle D_{t}:=\mathbb{J}[D_{s}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(iq_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}+\partial_{y_{j}}\partial_{q_{j}}\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad X_{t}:=\mathbb{J}[X_{s}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(x_{j}\partial_{q_{j}}-iy_{j}q_{j}\big)\ .

Remark 3.3
.

The operator
D_{t}
was often ignored up to this point in the context of symplectic Clifford analysis for
D_{s}
, mostly because the former operator does not have the
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
-invariance properties of the latter. The operator
D_{t}
is indeed
not
invariant under the realisation for
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
which appears in the setting of
D_{s}
(see the first set of operators in the lemma below). However, when switching to
another
realisation for this symplectic Lie algebra (the second set of operators in the lemma below),
D_{t}

will
be invariant. As is to be expected,
D_{s}
will in turn not be invariant under this new realisation. We would thus argue that both operators are symplectically invariant, just like Habermann mentions in
[
8
]
, under the caveat that one has to specify for which realisation. Recall that this invariance amounts to saying that the generators below commute with the symplectic Dirac operators, so e.g.
[D_{s},X_{jk}]=[D_{t},\tilde{Y}_{jk}]=0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have the following two realisations of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
on the space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
of symplectic spinor-valued polynomials

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{jk}=x_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}-y_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}-(q_{k}\partial_{q_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk})&1\leq j\leq k\leq n\\
Y_{jk}=x_{j}\partial_{y_{k}}+x_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}+i\partial_{q_{j}}\partial_{q_{k}}&j<k=1,\dots,n\\
Z_{jk}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}+y_{k}\partial_{x_{j}}+iq_{j}q_{k}&j<k=1,\dots,n\\
Y_{jj}=x_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}+\frac{i}{2}\partial_{q_{j}}^{2}&j=1,\dots,n\\
Z_{jj}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}+\frac{i}{2}q_{j}^{2}&j=1,\dots,n\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{X}_{jk}=x_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}-y_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}+q_{k}\partial_{q_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{jk}&1\leq j\leq k\leq n\\
\tilde{Y}_{jk}=x_{j}\partial_{y_{k}}+x_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}-iq_{j}q_{k}&j<k=1,\dots,n\\
\tilde{Z}_{jk}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}+y_{k}\partial_{x_{j}}-i\partial_{q_{j}}\partial_{q_{k}}&j<k=1,\dots,n\\
\tilde{Y}_{jj}=x_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}-\frac{i}{2}q_{j}^{2}&j=1,\dots,n\\
\tilde{Z}_{jj}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}-\frac{i}{2}\partial_{q_{j}}^{2}&j=1,\dots,n\end{cases}

The symplectic Dirac operator
D_{s}
is invariant under the first realisation, the operator
D_{t}
under the second.

Proof.

This follows from straightforward commutation relations.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The capital letters used to denote these operators are referring to the letters used in a typical matrix realisation (see e.g.
[
7
]
), so that the Lie algebra homomorphism becomes immediately clear.

We will now perform a symmetry reduction, such that the operators
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
both become invariant under
one and the same
group (or associated Lie algebra). To see that this must be the unitary group, we will first switch to matrices and characterise all symplectic matrices which commute with the complex structure
\mathbb{J}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have that
\mathsf{Sp}_{\mathbb{J}}(2n):=\{M\in\mathsf{Sp}(2n)\mid M\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{J}M\}

defines a realisation for the unitary Lie group
\mathsf{U}(n)
.

Proof.

In order to see this, assume that
M
is of the block-form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
A,B,C
and
D
are
(n\times n)-
matrices. There are two conditions to be met here:
M^{T}\mathbb{J}M=\mathbb{J}
(being symplectic) and
M\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{J}M
. The latter tells us that
A=D
and
B=-C
, and plugging the second condition in the first immediately yields that
M^{T}M=\textup{Id}
gives the identity matrix. This is precisely the condition required to be a unitary matrix, with the well-known group isomorphism
\Phi:\mathsf{Sp}_{\mathbb{J}}(2n,\mathbb{R})\to\mathsf{U}(n):M\mapsto A+iB
.
∎

On the level of operators acting on functions, we get the following:

Lemma 3.7
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}(n)
can be realised in terms of the differential operators acting on the space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{jk}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}-x_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}+y_{k}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{y_{k}}+i(q_{j}q_{k}-\partial_{q_{j}}\partial_{q_{k}})&\quad 1\leq j<k\leq n\\
B_{jj}=y_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}+\frac{i}{2}\left(q_{j}^{2}-\partial_{q_{j}}^{2}\right)&\quad 1\leq j\leq n\\
C_{jk}=x_{j}\partial_{x_{k}}-x_{k}\partial_{x_{j}}+y_{j}\partial_{y_{k}}-y_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}+q_{j}\partial_{q_{k}}-q_{k}\partial_{q_{j}}&\quad 1\leq j<k\leq n\end{cases}

Proof.

One can use the previous lemma to obtain the matrix realisation, and use lemma
3.4
to obtain the corresponding differential operators. Alternatively, one can also employ the fact that the operator
\sum_{j}B_{jj}
corresponds to the symplectic structure
\mathbb{J}
from the previous lemma, and use this to obtain the required linear combinations which commute with
\sum_{j}B_{jj}
.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

The operators
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
are invariant differential operators with respect to the realisation for
\mathfrak{u}(n)
from above.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
commute with all the operators from Lemma
3.7
.
∎

This means that we can now refine the
\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
-invariant equation
D_{s}f=0
, by introducing a system of two
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-invariant equations:
D_{s}f=
and
D_{t}f=0
, for an
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
-valued function
f
(note that we will mostly stick to polynomials in this paper). On the analogy with the classical case, we have the following:

Definition 3.9
.

A function which is a solution for both
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
is called a
Hermitian symplectic monogenic
(or
h
-symplectic monogenic for short).

These two symplectic Dirac operators
(D_{s},D_{t})
will serve as the analogue of the operators
(\underline{\partial}_{x},\underline{\partial}_{x}^{\mathbb{J}})
. One can develop the analogy with the classical situation even further by introducing the symplectic analogues of the operators
\underline{\partial}_{z}
and
\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}
, see (
2
).
Recall that these Hermitian Dirac operators
\underline{\partial}_{z}
and
\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}
were introduced using the Witt basis vectors. In the symplectic setting, we will do something similar by introducing the operators
D_{z}
and
D_{z}^{\dagger}
, known in the literature as the
symplectic Dolbeault operators

[
10
]
. These are defined using the lowering operator
L_{j}=q_{j}+\partial_{q_{j}}
and raising operator
R_{j}=q_{j}-\partial_{q_{j}}

(1\leq j\leq n)
acting on symplectic spinors.

Definition 3.10
.

The symplectic Dolbeault operators are defined as follows:

\displaystyle D_{z}:=\frac{1}{2}(D_{s}+iD_{t})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}=-\langle\underline{L},\underline{\partial}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\dagger}:=\frac{1}{2}(D_{s}-iD_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}\partial_{{\overline{z}}_{j}}=\langle\underline{R},\underline{\partial}_{\overline{z}}\rangle

\displaystyle X_{z}:=\frac{1}{2}(X_{s}+iX_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}{\overline{z}_{j}}=\frac{i}{2}\langle\underline{L},\underline{\overline{z}}\rangle

\displaystyle X_{z}^{\dagger}:=\frac{1}{2}(X_{s}-iX_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}{{z}_{j}}=\frac{i}{2}\langle\underline{R},\underline{z}\rangle\ ,

where we used the short-hand notation notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the inner product.

The general philosophy in passing from the orthogonal setting to the symplectic one often amounts to replacing an anti-commutator
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
by a commutator
[\cdot,\cdot]
. As a simple example we mention the definition for the Clifford algebra versus the Weyl algebra. A similar thing happens for the factorisation of the Laplace operator. Whereas the formula
\Delta=4\{\underline{\partial}_{z},\underline{\partial}_{z}^{\dagger}\}
expresses
\Delta
as an anti-commutator, a simple computation leads to the commutator relation

Lemma 3.11

We have the inclusion
\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\subseteq\ker(\Delta)
.

This shows that also Hermitian symplectic Clifford analysis is a

5
Decomposition of the symplectic spinor space

In Section 2 we studied the decomposition of the (standard) spinor space
\mathbb{S}
in terms of the spin-Euler operator
\beta
. In this section, we will do the same thing for the infinite-dimensional symplectic spinor space
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
. For this we will need the powers
\rho^{k}
of the determinant representation (where
k\in\mathbb{R}
), given by

\displaystyle\rho^{k}:\mathfrak{u}(n)\to\mathbb{C}:M\mapsto\det(M)^{k}\ ,

with highest weight
(k,k,\dots,k)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}
. Note that these weights correspond to the action of a Cartan algebra for the complexification of
\mathfrak{u}(n)
, where the elements
H_{j}:=iB_{jj}
are chosen (see Lemma
3.7
). As for
\mathbb{C}
-valued polynomials, we first mention the following well-known result:

Theorem 5.1
.

The space
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})
of bi-homogeneous harmonics of degree
(a,b)
is an irreducible
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-modules of highest weight
(a,0,\dots,0,-b)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}
and has highest weight vector
w_{a,b}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})=z_{1}^{a}\overline{z}_{n}^{b}
. Moreover, the dimension of the module is given by

\dim(\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C}))=\frac{n+a+b-1}{n-1}{a+n-2\choose n-2}{b+n-2\choose n-2}.

Next, we consider the branching of the infinite-dimensional symplectic spinors, when considered as a
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-module.

Theorem 5.2
.

The symplectic spinor space
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
decomposes as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{\infty}\bigg\downarrow^{\mathfrak{sp}(2n)}_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2}-r\right)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\ .

The space
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
hereby has dimension
{n+r-1\choose r}
.

Proof.

The branching of the metaplectic representation
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}=\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{+}\oplus\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{-}
to the maximal compact subalgebra
\mathfrak{u}(n)\subseteq\mathfrak{sp}(2n)
was treated in
[
9
]
, and is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{+}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\to\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\otimes\bigoplus_{r\in 2\mathbb{N}}(0,0,\dots,-r)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{-}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\to\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\otimes\bigoplus_{r\in 2\mathbb{N}+1}(0,0,\dots,-r)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}

Working out these tensor products leads to the desired result. The dimension of these spaces can be calculated using the Weyl dimension formula:

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}=\prod_{j=2}^{n}\frac{j+k-1}{j-1}={n+r-1\choose r}\ ,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Note that this dimension coincides with the dimension of the space
\mathcal{P}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})
of
r
-homogeneous polynomials. This is not a coincidence, since the spaces
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
are known as the eigenspaces for the bosonic quantum oscillator.

In the following scheme we summarise the comparison between classical spinors in
\mathbb{S}
and symplectic spinors in
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
:

Lemma 5.4
.

The unitary representation
\mathbb{S}_{(r)}^{\infty}
is generated by the weight vector

\displaystyle w(\underline{q})=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\underline{q}|^{2}}\mathsf{H}_{r}(q_{n})\ ,

where
\mathsf{H}_{r}(q_{n})
is a Hermite polynomial of degree
r

q_{n}
.

Proof.

For all indices
1\leq j<n
we directly obtain that

\displaystyle H_{j}w(\underline{q})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{q_{j}}^{2}-q_{j}^{2}\right)w(\underline{q})=-\frac{1}{2}w(\underline{q})\ .

The action of the last Cartan element
H_{n}
is slightly more complicated:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{q_{n}}^{2}-q_{n}^{2}\right)w(\underline{q})=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\underline{q}|^{2}}\left(4(r-1)r\mathsf{H}_{r-2}\left(q_{n}\right)-\mathsf{H}_{r}\left(q_{n}\right)-4q_{n}r\mathsf{H}_{r-1}\left(q_{n}\right)\right)\ .

Using the recursion relation for Hermite polynomials, which says that

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{r}(q_{n})=2q_{n}\mathsf{H}_{r-1}(q_{n})-2(r-1)\mathsf{H}_{r-2}(q_{n})\ ,

we can rewrite this action as

\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{q_{n}}^{2}-q_{n}^{2}\right)w(\underline{q})=-\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\underline{q}|^{2}}(2r+1)\mathsf{H}_{r}(q_{n})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(r+\frac{1}{2}\right)w(\underline{q})\ .

One further verifies that
w(\underline{q})
is a solution for the positive root vectors, which finishes the proof.
∎

6
The symplectic Hermitian Fischer decomposition

Now that we have obtained the reduction of the symplectic spinors
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
in terms of the spaces
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
and have identified our candidate for the Howe dual pair as
\mathsf{U}(n)\times\mathfrak{su}(1,2)
, we are ready to formulate the Fischer decomposition for the space
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}^{\infty}
. In terms of
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-irreducible representations, we need to compute the tensor product

(6.1)

\displaystyle(a,0,\dots,0,-b)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\otimes\left(-\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{2r+1}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\ .

Our strategy is the following:

(i)

First we will compute
(a,0,\dots,0,-b)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\otimes(0,\cdots,0,-r)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}
using a suitable ‘dualisation argument’ and then determine the tensor product of the resulting summands with the determinant representation
\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

(ii)

Of course, the procedure in step (i) yield a lot of information, but it only gives the decomposition of the tensor product
up to an isomorphism
. In order to make the isomorphism into an equality (as was in Theorem
2.8
) we need the so-called
embedding factors
which will turn out to be unitary invariant differential operators.

For two irreducible representations for
\mathfrak{u}(n)
with highest weights
\alpha
and
\beta
, the tensor product decomposes as
\alpha\otimes\beta\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma}\mu_{\gamma}\gamma
, where
\mu_{\gamma}
is the multiplicity. As a particular case, we mention the following result (from
[
13
]
):

\displaystyle(m,0,\dots,0)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\otimes(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\cong\bigoplus\>(\beta_{1}+\nu_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}+\nu_{n})_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}

where the summation is such that
\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{n}=m
. Moreover, we have the conditions
0\leq\nu_{1}\leq m
and
0\leq\nu_{k}\leq\beta_{k-1}-\beta_{k}
for
2\leq k\leq n
. Invoking the fact that
(V\otimes W)^{\ast}\cong V^{\ast}\otimes W^{\ast}
(the dualisation mentioned above), it then suffices to put

\displaystyle m=r\quad\mbox{and}\quad(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})=(b,0,\ldots,0,-a)

in the formula above, after which one can take the dual (or contragradient) and a tensor product with the (one-dimensional) determinant representation
\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}
to arrive at the theorem below. Note that from now on, we restrict ourselves to the case
n\geq 3
.

Theorem 6.1
.

We have the following tensor product decomposition:

\mathcal{H}_{a,b}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}^{\infty}\cong\bigoplus_{j=0}^{a}\bigoplus_{i=0}^{b}\left(a-j-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},\cdots,-i-\frac{1}{2},i+j-b-r-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{\mathfrak{u}(n)}\ ,

whereby the indices
i
and
j
satisfy the additional constraint
i+j\leq r
.

Remark 6.2
.

If
a,b\geq r
, the direct sum at the right-hand side has a maximal number of summands, equal to the triangular number
t_{r+1}=\frac{1}{2}(r+1)(r+2)
. This should be contrasted with the classical case (the case
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}
in Hermitean Clifford analysis), where the maximal number of summands is always bounded by
4
.

As usual in the framework of Fischer decompositions, we will now try to make the information in the theorem above more explicit.

Remark 6.3
.

Let us for instance calculate
\mathcal{H}_{1,2}(\mathbb{C}^{3},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(3)}^{\infty}
. Using the formula from above we have:

\displaystyle(1,0,-2)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}\otimes(0,0,-3)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\cong(1,0,-5)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}\oplus(1,-1,-4)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}\oplus(1,-2,-3)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\oplus(0,0,-4)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}\oplus(0,-1,-3)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime}\oplus(0,-2,-2)_{\mathfrak{u}(3)}^{\prime},

where we have introduced the shorthand notation
(a,b,c)^{\prime}:=\left(a-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2},c-\frac{1}{2}\right)
.

Before we can express the summands in the decomposition for
\mathbb{S}^{(r)}
-valued harmonics in terms of
h
-monogenics, we introduce the following:

Definition 6.4
.

For positive integers
a,b
and
r
with
b\leq r
we use the notation
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}
for the irreducible representation highest weight

\displaystyle\left(a-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},\ldots,-b-\frac{1}{2},-r-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ .

We restrict ourselves to the case
n\geq 3
here.

The dimension of these spaces follows from the Weyl dimension formula, for which we refer to a standard textbook:

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
a,b,r\geq 0
be arbitrary integers with
b\leq r
, then the dimension of the
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-irreducible representation
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}
is given by

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}=\frac{\Gamma(a+n-2)\Gamma(b+n-2)\Gamma(r+n-1)}{\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(b+1)\Gamma(r+2)}\frac{(a+b+n-2)(a+r+n-1)(r-b+1)}{(n-1)!(n-2)!(n-3)!}\ .

Although this is not absolutely necessary, one can now use the formula above to verify Theorem
6.1
by a dimensional argument. Indeed, using symbolic computation software (the authors used Mathematica), one can verify that the following numerical equality holds:

Lemma 6.6
.

For arbitrary positive integers
a,b,r\geq 0
for which
b\leq r
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{b}\sum_{j=0}^{a}\dim\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

\displaystyle={r+n-1\choose n-1}\frac{(a+n+n-1)\Gamma(a+n-1)\Gamma(b+n-1)}{(n-1)!(n-2)!\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(b+1)}\ ,

whereby the indices
i
and
j
satisfy the additional constraint
i+j\leq r
.

Our goal for the rest of this section is to show that these spaces are precisely the spaces of (symplectic)
h
-monogenics of a degree
(a,b;r)
, so that we can also define these spaces in an equivalent way as follows:

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)})\cap\big(\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\big)\ .

Remark 6.7
.

Note that the condition
b\leq r
on the parameters may seem a bit strange, since this implies that spaces of
h
-monogenics will not always exist. However, a similar phenomenon also occurs in the classical case. One can for instance see that there are no
h
-monogenic polynomials of degree
(a,0)
taking values in the homogeneous spinor space
\mathbb{S}_{(0)}
. So even in Hermitean Clifford analysis, the existence of the spaces
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(j)}
is governed by restrictions on the parameters
(a,b;j)
.

The inclusion from left to right in formula (
6.2
) is rather immediate, the opposite direction requires more work. To see why

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S}_{(r)}^{\infty})\cap\big(\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\big)\ ,

it suffices to note that we have an explicit model for the highest weight vector which generates the representation under the action of the negative root vectors in
\mathfrak{u}(n)
. Indeed, recalling the definition for the (raising) operators
R_{j}=q_{j}-\partial_{q_{j}}
and the Hermite polynomials
\mathsf{H}_{k}(t)
, it is not hard to see that the function

\displaystyle w_{a,b;r}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})

\displaystyle:=z_{1}^{a}\big(\overline{z}_{n-1}R_{n}-\overline{z}_{n}R_{n-1}\big)^{b}\mathsf{H}_{r-b}(q_{n})e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\underline{q}|^{2}}\ ,

satisfies the necessary requirements. It suffices to consider the action of the positive root operators

\displaystyle C_{jk}+iA_{jk}=2\big(z_{j}\partial_{z_{k}}-\overline{z}_{k}\partial_{\overline{z}_{j}})+(q_{j}+\partial_{q_{j}})(\partial_{q_{k}}-q_{k})\ ,

where
1\leq j<k\leq n
, and the Cartan elements

\displaystyle H_{j}=H_{j}=-i(z_{j}\partial_{z_{j}}-\overline{z}_{j}\partial_{{\overline{z}}_{j}})+\frac{i}{2}\left(q_{j}^{2}-\partial_{q_{j}}^{2}\right)

for
1\leq j\leq n
. The former all act trivially on
w_{a,b;r}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})
, whereas the latter indeed give the desired eigenvalues. Next, we prove that this function is also a solution the symplectic Dirac operators. One has that

\displaystyle D_{z}w_{a,b;r}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})

\displaystyle=az_{1}^{a-1}L_{1}(\overline{z}_{n-1}R_{n}-\overline{z}_{n}R_{n-1})^{b}\mathsf{H}_{r-b}(q_{n})e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\underline{q}|}=0\ ,

since the lowering operator
L_{1}
acts on the Gaussian only (here we have used the fact that
n\geq 3
, so that we can commute
L_{1}
with the operators
R_{n}
and
R_{n-1}
). A similar calculation for the operator
D_{z}^{\dagger}
then proves that
w_{a,b;r}(\underline{z},\overline{\underline{z}})
is indeed
h
-monogenic.

To prove the opposite inclusion in equation (
6.2
), we will argue as follows: given the fact that symplectic
h
-monogenics are always harmonic, we certainly know that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)})\cap\big(\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\big)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\ ,

whereby we assume that
b\leq r
. Now, we know how the right-hand side decomposes (see Theorem
6.1
), so any polynomial
P(\underline{x},\underline{y})
of degree
(a,b)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
taking values in
\mathbb{S}^{(r)}
which satisfies
D_{s}P=D_{t}P=0
can be written as a linear combination of elements belonging to the spaces predicted by this theorem. However, in what follows we will prove that there is a
unique
summand in the decomposition containing
h
-monogenics, and that all the other summands are orthogonal (in a suitable sense) to the space of
h
-monogenics. This will thus force
P(\underline{x},\underline{y})
to be an element of that unique component, which will turn out to be the one generated by
w_{a,b;r}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})
.

The main idea behind the proof announced in the previous paragraph involves the construction of the so-called ‘embedding factors’. These are (in full generality) the
\mathfrak{u}(n)
-invariant linear maps injecting
\gamma
into
\alpha\otimes\beta\cong\bigoplus\mu_{\gamma}\gamma
. For example, the vector variable
\underline{z}
appearing in Theorem
2.8
can be seen as an example of such an embedding map, sending
h
-monogenics of certain degree into the tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}
. In the setting of Howe dual pairs, these embedding factors are typically given by the operators dual (in the sense of the Fischer inner product) to the differential operators defining the (monogenic) solution spaces. This is also the case in the present situation, since one can show the following:

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
0\leq\ell\leq b
and
0\leq k\leq b
, then the following properties hold:

\displaystyle X_{z}^{\ell}\mathcal{M}_{a,b-\ell}^{(r+\ell)}\in\mathcal{H}_{a,b}\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(X_{z}^{\dagger})^{k}\mathcal{M}_{a-k,b}^{(r-k)}\in\mathcal{H}_{a,b}\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\ .

Note that these statements only hold if the parameters characterising the
\mathcal{M}
-spaces make sense, so
b-\ell\leq r+\ell
and
b\leq r-k
respectively.

Proof.

It is clear that the operator
X_{z}^{\ell}
raises the bi-degree from
(a,b-\ell)
to
(a,b)
, and that the spinor-degree is lowered from
r+\ell
to
r
. It thus suffices to show that the result is indeed harmonic. To that end, we note that
[\Delta_{z},X_{z}]=-D_{z}
(where we have put
\Delta_{z}=\sum\partial_{z_{j}}\bar{\partial}_{z_{j}}
) and
[D_{z},X_{z}]=0
. A similar calculation for the operator
X_{z}^{\dagger}
then proves the lemma.
∎

It is now tempting to think that one has a handle on all embedding factors, through combinations of powers of
X_{z}
and
X_{z}^{\dagger}
. Unfortunately, the situation is slightly more complicated, because a simple calculation shows that for instance the product operator
X_{z}X_{z}^{\dagger}
cannot be used to embed
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}
into the space of
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
-valued harmonics. It turns out that one needs a slightly more complicated algebraic structure to characterise the embedding factors, expressed in terms of a so-called transvector algebra. Note that this (quadratic) algebra already appeared in the setting of Howe dualities in harmonic analysis for the Laplace operator, see for instance
[
5
]
. The construction of this type of algebra hinges upon the existence of a reductive subalgebra inside a Lie algebra, hence the following result:

Lemma 6.9
.

The Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
is reductive in
\mathfrak{su}(1,2)
.

Proof.

We need to prove that
\mathfrak{su}(1,2)
decomposes as
\mathfrak{sl}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{t}
, such that the subspace
\mathfrak{t}
caries the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
. If we now define
\mathfrak{t}:=\textup{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mathcal{O},X_{s},X_{t},D_{s},D_{t}\big)
and
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
as the subalgebra generated by the remaining operators
\Delta,r^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}+n
, the result follows from instance from the isomorphism constructed in Theorem
4.3
.
∎

For the most general definition of a transvector algebra, or the (intimitely related) Mickelsson algebra, we refer to the literature: see for instance
[
12
,
14
]
. In the present paper we will proceed by constructing this algebra through its generators, which is typically done in terms of the extremal projection operator. Denoting our transvector algebra by means of
\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{su}(1,2),\mathfrak{sl}(2))
, we note that its generators are constructed in terms of the extremal projector

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}

\displaystyle:=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{\Gamma(H+2)}{\Gamma(H+2+j)}Y^{j}X^{j}\ ,

whereby the (suitably normalised) operators
X,Y
and
H
are respectively given by
X=\frac{1}{2}\Delta
,
Y=-\frac{1}{2}r^{2}
and
H=-(\mathbb{E}+n)
. Note that there is no need to care about convergence issues for the formal series above, since we will never need more than a finite number of terms. Also the factors in the denominator are harmless, because the previous observation will always allow us to get rid of the denominators after multiplication with a suitable factor (technically speaking, this amounts to going from a transvector to a Mickelsson algebra). The upshot is that although the operators in the subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}\subseteq\mathfrak{su}(1,2)
do not map harmonics to harmonics, one does have that
\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}(\mathfrak{t})\subseteq\mbox{End}(\ker(\Delta))
. So the operators in
\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}(\mathfrak{t})
and products thereof will be our embedding factors. Let us then prove the following technical result:

Lemma 6.10
.

(i)

The transvector projections of the operators
X_{s}
and
X_{t}
, acting on harmonic functions, can be rescaled using the factor
H+2
, such that

\displaystyle-(H+2)\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(YX-(H+2)\right)X_{s}=(\mathbb{E}+n-2)X_{s}-\frac{1}{2}r^{2}D_{t}

\displaystyle-(H+2)\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(YX-(H+2)\right)X_{t}=(\mathbb{E}+n-2)X_{t}+\frac{1}{2}r^{2}D_{s}\ .

In other words, only the first two terms of the extremal projection operator
\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}
are required to do the projection.

(ii)

These rescaled operators commute in
\operatorname{End}(\ker(\Delta))
.

Proof.

The first statement (i) follows from the fact that
[\Delta,[\Delta,X_{s}]]=0
(and similarly for
X_{t}
). This thus means that the infinite sum in the extremal projector reduces to a finite sum of two terms only. The explicit expression then follows from straightforward calculations, hereby taking into account that everything is to be seen as an operator acting on
\ker(\Delta)
. For the second statement (ii) we will make use of the commutator equality

[AB,CD]=A[B,C]D+[A,C]BD+CA[B,D]+C[A,D]B

which allows us to say that

\displaystyle[\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{s},\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}[X_{s},\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}]X_{t}+\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}^{2}[X_{s},X_{t}]+\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}[\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)},X_{t}]X_{s}\ .

Note that we can commute any factor involving the Euler operator
\mathbb{E}
through the product
r^{2}\Delta
, because it is homogeneous of degree zero, and that
[r^{2},X_{s}]=0
such that

\displaystyle[\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)},X_{s}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\left[(\mathbb{E}+n-2)^{-1}r^{2}\Delta,X_{s}\right]=-\frac{1}{4}r^{2}\left[\Delta(\mathbb{E}+n-2)^{-1}r^{2},X_{s}\right]\ .

This is enough to see why
\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}[X_{s},\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}]=0
(and similarly for the last term in the righ-hand side above). Indeed, we can hereby make use of the fact
\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}r^{2}=0
, which is one of the defining properties for the extremal projection operator. This also gets rid of the middle term, as
[X_{s},X_{t}]\sim r^{2}
. Finally, it suffices to multiply the (trivial) commutator
[\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{s},\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{t}]
from the left with
(H+2)(H+1)
to see that also the rescaled operators will commute.
∎

One can obviously also consider the projections of
X_{z}
and
X_{z}^{\dagger}
. Briefly denoting the rescaled projection operator by means of
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}=-(H+2)\pi_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}
, we get the following:

Definition 6.11
.

The operators
\mathbb{X}_{z}
and
\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{z}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{z}=(\mathbb{E}+n-2)X_{z}+\frac{i}{2}|\underline{z}|^{2}D_{z}

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{\pi}_{\mathfrak{sl}(2)}X_{z}^{\dagger}=(\mathbb{E}+n-2)X_{z}^{\dagger}-\frac{i}{2}|\underline{z}|^{2}D_{z}^{\dagger}\ .

In view of Lemma
6.10
it is clear that also
[\mathbb{X}_{z},\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}]=0
. In particular, one can thus consider the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}_{z},\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}]
in these operators, defining a subalgebra of the endomorphism algebra End
(\ker(\Delta))
. Note that in switching from
(X_{s},X_{t})
to
(X_{z},X_{z}^{\dagger})
we introduced a complex unit, which means that
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}_{z},\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}]
should be seen as a subalgebra of the complexified transvector algebra
Z(\mathfrak{su}(1,2),\mathfrak{sl}(2))\otimes\mathbb{C}
here. Monomials in the algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}_{z},\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}]
are precisely the embedding operators we are after, hence the following definition:

Definition 6.12
.

For all positive integers
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we define the maps

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}:=\mathbb{X}^{k}(\mathbb{X}^{\dagger})^{\ell}\in\mbox{End}\big(\ker(\Delta)\big)\ ,

whereby
\mathcal{I}_{0,0}=\textup{Id}
is the identity map. Each of these maps is invariant with respect to
\mathfrak{u}(n)
, in the sense that
\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}
commutes with all the generators.

It is clear that the map
\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}
raises the bi-degree of homogeneity in
(\underline{z},\underline{z}^{\dagger})
from
(a,b)
to
(a+\ell,b+k)
, and it maps spinors in
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
to elements in
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r-k+\ell)}
. As such, these maps can indeed be used as embedding maps:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}:\mathcal{M}_{a-\ell,b-k}^{(r+k-\ell)}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\ ,

where we momentarily ignore the conditions on the parameters (depending on the values of the parameters, the space at the left-hand side of the arrow may not exist). To finish the goal we have set right before Remark
6.7
, the following will come in handy:

Lemma 6.13
.

For all
(k,\ell)\neq(0,0)
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}:\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\longrightarrow\big(\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\big)^{\perp}\ ,

whereby the orthogonality is expressed with respect to the Fischer inner product, defined on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}
by means of

\displaystyle\langle P(\underline{x},\underline{y})\otimes\psi(\underline{q}),Q(\underline{x},\underline{y})\otimes\varphi(\underline{q})\rangle:=\big(P(\underline{\partial}_{y},-\underline{\partial}_{x})Q(\underline{x},\underline{y})\big)\bigg|_{x=y=0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\overline{\psi}(\underline{q})\varphi(\underline{q})d\underline{q}\ .

Proof.

Because the operator
\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}
is a monomial in the operators
\mathbb{X}_{z}
and
\mathbb{X}_{z}^{\dagger}
(note that we exclude the identity operator
\mathcal{I}_{0,0}
here), it is sufficient to investigate
\mathcal{I}_{1,0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{0,1}
separately. These operators are projections (up to a multiplicative constant, coming from the Euler factor
H+n
) of suitable linear combinations of
X_{s}
and
X_{t}
, so we can even restrict our attention to these operators instead. The result then follows from the fact that
X_{s}
(resp.
X_{t}
) is the Fischer adjoint of
D_{s}
(resp.
D_{t}
).
∎

This allows us to prove the following:

Lemma 6.14
.

For all positive integers
b\leq r
, the space
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
can be defined as the space of
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
-valued
h
-monogenic polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
of bi-degree
(a,b)
.

Proof.

Since any
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
-valued polynomial
P_{a,b}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})
of bi-degree
(a,b)
in the kernel of the operators
D_{s}
and
D_{t}
is also harmonic, we know that it must belong to one of the spaces appearing at the right-hand side of Theorem
6.1
, embedded using one of the mappings
\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}
constructed above. However, we have just shown that the only map that preserves the property of being symplectic
h
-monogenic is
\mathcal{I}_{0,0}
, which means that
P_{a,b}(\underline{x},\underline{y};\underline{q})\in\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
.
∎

Putting everything together then leads to the following refinement of Theorem
6.1
:

Theorem 6.15

(Symplectic Hermitian Fischer decomposition)
.

We have the following explicit decomposition for the space of spinor-valued harmonics:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(r)}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{a}\bigoplus_{i=0}^{b}\mathcal{I}_{b-i,j}\mathcal{M}_{a-j,i}^{(b+r-(i+j))}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\ ,

where the indices are constrained by the condition
i+j\leq r
. This is a decomposition of
\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
-valued harmonics into symplectic
h
-monogenics.

Proof.

The decomposition itself follows from
6.1
, together with the observation that the maps
\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell}
belong to End
(\ker(\Delta))
and therefore have the desired mapping properties. The last statement (i.e. the connection with symplectic
h
-monogenics) follows from the previous lemma.
∎

Despite the obvious similarities between the theorem above and the classical Fischer decompositions (for the harmonic and monogenic case), there are also a few crucial differences. First of all, note that when the space
\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(r)}
is included in the tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}
, then this space is
not
given by the Cartan product

\displaystyle(a,0,\ldots,0,-b)\boxtimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\cong\left(a-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},\ldots,-\frac{1}{2},-b-r-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ .

This is clearly different from the aforementioned Fischer decompositions, in which the monogenics were always embedded as a Cartan product. Moreover, the vector space of (
r
-homogeneous) spinor-valued harmonic polynomials does not always contain symplectic
h
-monogenics. Put differently, for certain choices of indices
(a,b;r)
one has that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{a,b}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(r)}\subseteq\big(\ker(D_{s})\cap\ker(D_{t})\big)^{\perp}\ .

As a simple example, consider
\mathcal{H}_{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\otimes\mathbb{S}^{\infty}_{(0)}=\mathbb{X}_{z}\mathcal{M}_{0,0}^{(1)}
. This is reminiscent of the situation in (standard) Hermitean Clifford analysis, where one for instance has that
\mathcal{H}_{1,0}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\otimes\mathbb{S}_{(0)}=\underline{z}\mathcal{M}_{0,0}^{(1)}
.

\mathbb{J}

\mathbb{R}^{2n}

D_{s}f=D_{t}f=0

\mathfrak{su}(1,2)

\mathsf{U}(n)

\mathsf{Sp}(n)=\mathsf{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathsf{U}(2n)
, see

1
]
). The symplectic analogue of this function theory, the empty slot in the overview, will be the topic of further investigation.
