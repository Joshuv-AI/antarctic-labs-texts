Harmonic LLMs are Trustworthy
Nicholas S. Kersting, Mohammad Rahman, Suchismitha Vedala, Yang Wang

Abstract




Abstract

We introduce an intuitive method to test the robustness (stability and explainability) of any black-box LLM in real-time via its local deviation from harmoniticity, denoted as
\gamma
. To the best of our knowledge this is the first completely model-agnostic and unsupervised method of measuring the robustness of any given response from an LLM, based upon the model itself conforming to a purely mathematical standard. To show general application and immediacy of results, we measure
\gamma
in 10 popular LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude-2.1, Claude-3.0, GPT-4, GPT-4o, Smaug-72B, Mixtral-8x7B, Llama2-7B, Mistral-7B and MPT-7B) across thousands of queries in three objective domains: WebQA, ProgrammingQA, and TruthfulQA. Across all models and domains tested, human annotation confirms that
\gamma\to 0
indicates trustworthiness, and conversely searching higher values of
\gamma
easily exposes examples of hallucination, a fact that enables efficient adversarial prompt generation through stochastic gradient ascent in
\gamma
. The low-
\gamma
leaders among the models in the respective domains are GPT-4o, GPT-4, and Smaug-72B, providing evidence that mid-size open-source models can win out against large commercial models.

1
Motivation and Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are notoriously confident in their answers
[
1
]
, which poses a dilemma for the user asking factual-type questions: how can I trust it? With hallucination a real and common phenomena across the LLM landscape, there is to date no reliable method to gauge the trustworthiness of a response
prima facae
without having to conduct independent research or cross-checking.
What is missing in every user’s LLM experience is a ‘trustworthiness score’ that tells them whether the response can be accepted as-is. The goal, of course, is to have an LLM which gets mostly high trustworthiness scores so the user has less of a burden to do follow-up verification.

We have found such a trustworthiness score is possible through the method of Harmonic Robustness, introduced in a previous work
[
2
]
to measure robustness of predictive machine learning models. In that work, we demonstrated that computing the anharmoniticity (
\gamma
) in any black-box model was correlated with robustness (stability and explainability) of the model in feature space. The current work extends the application of Harmonic Robustness to LLMs, which, as next-token prediction engines, are formally no different from predictive machine learning models, but which possess several distinguishing characteristics that warrant this separate, dedicated treatment.
The reader may of course consult
[
2
]
for the full treatment of Harmonic Robustness, but we will briefly summarize below for the present discussion.

The essence of Harmonic Robustness is to measure how close a machine learning function
f
is to being harmonic, i.e., satisfying

\nabla^{2}f=0

(1.1)

with the gradient taken over all feature dimensions.
Crucially, it turns out this condition is equivalent to the statement

f(x)=\frac{1}{S_{r,n}}\int_{B(x,r)}fd\Omega_{r,n}

(1.2)

which, in plain English, says that the value of the function at any point is the average of the function over any ball surrounding the point
1
1

1

An easy informal way to see this equivalence is to recognize (
1.1
) as the divergence of the gradient (
\nabla^{2}f=\bigtriangledown\cdot\bigtriangledown f
): the gradient expressing the change of
f
in all directions, if its divergence is zero then ‘change in f’ neither flows into or out of any given point, hence the average change of
f
on any ball around that point is 0, relative to its value at the point.
. We define the degree to which this ‘mean value property’ (
1.2
) is satisfied at a point
x
in input space as

\gamma(x)\equiv|f(x)-\frac{1}{S_{r,n}}\int_{B(x,r)}fd\Omega_{r,n}|

(1.3)

which allows us to summarize how ‘harmonic’
f
is across feature space given a fixed ball radius
r
. This is interesting because the mean value property is actually a very strong statement of stability and explainability:
harmonic functions smoothly interpolate between boundary values and exhibit no intermediate oscillations; because the value at any point is the average of values around that point, there is a certain maximal ‘fairness’ at play which lends itself easily to explainability. Applying (
1.3
) to classical ML models allows one to measure failure of robustness in feature space, identifying points where one should not "trust" the output of the model.

There are several caveats, none of them insurmountable, to applying (
1.3
) to an LLM for the same purpose of identifying "trustworthiness" of outputs. Firstly, an LLM with a non-zero temperature parameter isn’t strictly a mathematical function, as the same input may produce several outputs in a stochastic fashion, so any further discussion must either reduce temperature fluctuations to a benign level, or eliminate them altogether. We choose the latter route in the following, focusing on T=0. Even so, an LLM in practice maps strings to strings, and we now have to interpret (
1.3
) in that language: what is the ball
B(x,r)
of radius
r
about a string
x
? How do we compute the difference of two strings or take the average, etc.? If we are able to directly access the input layer of the LLM, then we might pass simplicial perturbations of the token embeddings to form the ball; the outputs would in general have different numbers of tokens, however, so direct averaging or comparison would not be possible. We can get around this by using a separate semantic embedding to convert each of the outputs to a single embedding vector, then, just as in our previous work
[
2
]
with high dimensional functions such as image classifiers, we simply average over the perturbed output embeddings to directly subtract from the original output embedding, giving an n-dimensional
\overrightarrow{\gamma}
— unlike an image classifier, however, where each output dimension represents a very interpretible class logit, the individual dimensions of a natural language embedding do not carry any special meaning, so we might take either the magnitude of
\overrightarrow{\gamma}
or, better, the angle between the original output embedding and average perturbed output embedding as our anharmoniticity metric, for angles between embeddings
do
encode semantic closeness.

If we cannot directly access the input embeddings of the LLM, which is generally the case for, e.g., commercial black-box models, we are indeed restricted to string inputs, and we have to somehow generate many perturbations of the input string that effectively amount to simplicial perturbations in token embedding space. This is in fact the assumption of the present work, and we will see there is a trick to accomplish this.

The trick is to add random characters to the input string -- we will see this does effectively perturb the input embedding roughly isotropically as long as these random characters do not add real semantic meaning to the input string. One way to guarantee the random characters are non-semantic is to ensure they never occur in any meaningful way in an LLM’s actual training data. For this purpose, we will choose the non-semantic and non-printing ASCII control characters (ASCII 0-31), which typically do not occur with any semantic correlations in the web text and corpora that known LLMs are trained on
2
2

2

One could also, for example, choose random exotic UTF-8 characters, but these might carry unintentional semantics. It turns out, however, that this is an effective method of finding adversarial examples (see Section
5
).
.

Intuitively, then,
\gamma
will be measuring the stability of the LLM output to random non-printing character additions to the input. It is reasonable to assume that if there is significant variation of the output, the LLM is unstable in the region of its input, and one cannot trust the response. On the other hand, if the output is consistent, giving
\gamma=0
, we can at least be assured that the LLM response is stable and in line with its training data. To the extent we believe the training data represents trustworthy world knowledge, statistically speaking, we can trust this response.

Note that we are measuring sensitivity to the harmonic condition, and not just any directional sensitivity, which, for some use cases may be naturally large. A simple arithmetic problem of the form “What is 5 + k * x" can be made as sensitive as you like to perturbation in
x
by increasing the value of
k
. That is what we would call explainable or “natural sensitivity". A violation of the harmonic condition, however, is not so explainable, and it is our hypothesis that this kind of “unnatural sensitivity" as parameterized by
\gamma
points to hallucination.

In the following, then, we hope to demonstrate the simplicity and effectiveness of
\gamma
as a proxy for measuring trustworthiness in LLMs. After reviewing Related Work in Section
2
, we describe the adaption of Harmonic Robustness to LLMs in more technical detail in Section
3
, and demonstrate in Section
4
how
\gamma
does in fact correlate with trustworthiness in human annotation experiments across several popular LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude-2.1, Claude-3.0, GPT-4, GPT-4o, Smaug-72B, Mixtral-8x7B, Llama2-7B, Mistral-7B and MPT-7B) in three domains: WebQA, ProgrammingQA, and TruthfulQA, corresponding to Internet trivia, programming tasks, and commonsense questions, respectively. Section
5
demonstrates that stochastically following the gradient of
\gamma
, like in
[
2
]
, affords an effective adversarial example generator. We wrap up discussion and offer future directions in Section
6
.

As we discussed in the Introduction, our adaption of (
1.3

As stated, Algorithm
1
permits some freedom of implementation. Given a target LLM, assumed to be black-box, one still needs to choose the Embedding for converting textual outputs to vectors; probably any reasonably-trained embedding will suffice here, though it is good practice to use the best available. We choose the industry standard "ada-002," for example. Next, one must decide how to implement the "ballPoints" functional approximation to the ball of strings surrounding the input text. Our ansatz is that appending random ASCII control characters to the end of the input string provides a sufficient ball approximation.
We tested, for example, that generating N=10 perturbations of the input string by appending 1-3 random characters in the ASCII range 0-31 provided adequate isotropy in embedding space, a core requirement of the Harmonic Robustness technique
3
3

3

To demonstrate this, we considered “words” consisting of 1-3 random ASCII control characters appended to 100 random sentences from the TruthfulQA
[
22
]
dataset. For each input sentence, the average “ada-002” embedding vector of N=10 such randomly perturbed output sentences was within a cone angle less than 11 degrees.
. We could have but did not need to improve on this with different numbers of more systematically-chosen random characters and generating more perturbations (i.e. higher N) as well.

Note our rubric changes somewhat for coding-type questions. Code is not trivia-QA — there is far less room for variation in response, and a single typo can cause the whole program to fail. An answer should address all the conditions of the problem, but a generic punt is OK: not answering or saying "I don’t know" is an honest, trustworthy response. If, however, the answer contains code, it must actually run and match the output given; if it is conceptual or pseudocode, it must look reasonable. We thus also did not hold annotators to the standard of the ground-truth answers for this corpus, as these were actually generated by CodeLlama-70B and full of copious errors.

Composite annotation results are shown in
Fig.
1
. We compute Fleiss’ Kappa at about 0.6 for all three domains, with annotators typically differing by much less than a point on any given answer, all indicating consistent inter-annotator agreement. The reader may consult the Appendix for examples of annotations from each model and category. The average rating for the larger models (ChatGPT, Claude-2.1, Claude-3.0, GPT-4o, GPT-4, Smaug-72B, and Mixtral-8x7B) was generally high (>4), indicating these models perform acceptably on the chosen domains. The smaller Mistral-7B, Llama2-7B and MPT-7B models, however, were of rather lower quality, corroborating the conventional wisdom in current AI development that one does sacrifice some quality in using a small number of parameters.

That said, two clear patterns emerge in the annotation scores across all models and domains: first, the lowest
\gamma
-scoring responses (below, say 0.05) are of higher quality than higher-
\gamma

\gamma\to 0
) LLMs are trustworthy. Secondly, quality more-or-less falls with increasing
\gamma

\gamma

\gamma

Finally, a peripheral, but interesting result of this work is a demonstration that the
\gamma
metric provides a way to efficiently find adversarial prompts. As we showed before in
[
2
]
with image classification, we can follow the gradient of
\gamma
to find adversarial examples which tend to occur with higher frequency along this gradient.

near its initial value), but by adding random UTF-8 characters because these do carry some random semantic weight. After the first iteration we already get some false content, and after two iterations we’re seeing full-blown hallucination.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

We applied a generic technique based on harmonic functions to test the stability of answers from LLMs. This is of great interest lately as these latter enjoy far wider adoption than probably any other machine-learning model in the past, yet the stability/hallucination problem is a major block in factual usage of LLMs, whether in a typical QA-type application, for coding suggestions, or summarization (however, for purely creative applications of LLMs, e.g., writing stories, poetry, and the like, hallucination is actually a feature).

Defining the anharmoniticity (
\gamma
) for LLMs as the sine of the angle between average embeddings was somewhat arbitrary; other definitions can and should be tried for even better correlation with trustworthiness. One could, for example, use a smaller auxiliary LLM or even just BERT to compare semantics of the original output versus the surrounding ‘ball’ outputs.

The choice of random ASCII control characters was also for simplicity — as noted there are other ways to perturb the input text, e.g., with rarely-used UTF8 characters or taking misspellings, synonyms or paraphrases. Whatever the perturbation scheme, one should verify that there is at least approximate isotropic perturbation in embedding space.

We also chose N=10 perturbations of each input as a middle-ground between what would be a more accurate representation of the ball with say, N=100 perturbations (suitable for offline analysis), and the minimal choice of N=1, which is probably affordable in a real-time inference setting. This latter may be rather effective in quickly and cheaply identifying large hallucinations, for if
\gamma
with N=100 is truly large for a given query, the probability that one random perturbation of the input gives a different answer with nearly the same
\gamma
is quite high.

Regardless of the approximations we employed, because the method is unsupervised and easy to implement, it can readily enjoy usage as a quality metric to, e.g. continuously scan for LLM weakness in various domains, so
\gamma
-threshold checks may trigger an automatic retraining process for the LLM to improve upon an area in which the training data provides ambiguous results. Model builders can publish a
\gamma
-distribution similar to those in Fig.
2
across various domains as part of their model card, and with white-box access to the layers wherein one can directly perturb input embeddings with much better isotropy the precision of the metric should be greater.

As we showed one can design adversarial “jailbreaking" prompts following the gradient of
\gamma
, perhaps conversely one can design stable prompts which are relatively immune to perturbations, again using
\gamma
as a sort of penetration-testing metric. This would fulfill another need of the industry to design prompts in e.g., a chatbot, that would be immmune to malicious prompt injection attacks.

In closing, the foregoing has demonstrated the usefulness of
\gamma
as a gauge of trustworthiness in factual domains. We have seen that
\gamma\to 0
generally does imply trustworthiness, but it is important to keep in mind some limitations. Firstly, there is mild domain and model dependence: some domains may be softer e.g. the arts, social sciences and humanities can have larger
\gamma
while still being truthful, while physics and math not so much as there is less room for variation of response in those disciplines. Perhaps, as in
[
2
]
, one can introduce auxiliary dimensions to construct a “Gamma Map" to handle this. Also some models may be more expressive (e.g. Claude) even when temperature is set to 0 in the API. Thus it was not clear that less stability implied more false information, since many outputs could express the same semantic content in many ways, and a simple embedding may be too crude to capture this. We again could use a separate semantic interpreter instead of the sine of the angle between embeddings, but we wanted this initial study to be as conceptually simple as feasible, leaving room for future improvements and iterations. It is important to note that there is no “magic" value of
\gamma
above which you should definitely doubt the answer, but rather
\gamma\sim 0
should be as truthful as the model can be. Here “trustworthy" means in accordance with training data, which everything of course depends on. If the training data is biased, the best one can hope for is a stable, but biased answer. For example, in Table
13
we show a query to ChatGPT which gives a totally wrong answer even though
\gamma=0
, explainably due to the biased training of the model. Despite this anomaly,
we go with the hypothesis that, with enough training data coming from varied sources, stability of an LLM converges on truthfulness as agreed upon in the Internet (which, as we saw, may be at least explainably-biased).
And let us not forget that stability itself is a feature of Responsible AI.

Finally, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time a model-agnostic (black-box) metric exists to measure trustworthiness of a response from an LLM in a standardized way. Our hope is that by building this basic metric, the industry will open up to improving this and other ways of testing reliability of AI systems for the good of future AI usage.
