Squeezed ensembles and anomalous dynamic roughening in interacting integrable chains Preprint: APS/123-QED
Guillaume Cecile, Jacopo De Nardis, Enej Ilievski

Abstract
It is widely accepted that local subsystems in isolated integrable quantum systems equilibrate to generalized Gibbs ensembles. Here, we demonstrate the failure of canonical generalized thermalization for a particular class of initial states in certain types of interacting integrable models. Particularly, we show that in the easy-axis regime of the quantum XXZ chain, pure non-equilibrium initial states with no magnetic fluctuations instead locally relax to squeezed generalized Gibbs ensembles, referring to exotic equilibrium states governed by non-local equilibrium Hamiltonians with sub-extensive charge fluctuations that violate the self-affine scaling. The behaviour at the isotropic point is exceptional and depends on the initial state. We find that relaxation from the Néel state is governed by extensive fluctuations and a super-diffusive dynamical exponent compatible with the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality. On the other hand, there are other non-fluctuating initial states that display diffusive scaling. Our predictions can be directly tested in state-of-the-art cold atomic experimental settings.

Thermalization in isolated extended systems primarily concerns the stationary values of local observables following a quench from a pure initial state. Local, but large, subsystems thermalize whenever the reduced density matrices are described by canonical Gibbs ensembles or, in the case of integrable models, the generalized Gibbs ensembles
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Vidmar and Rigol 2016
;
Essler and Fagotti 2016
, characterized by finite densities of local charges and possess extensive, strictly positive, fluctuations (i.e. static charge susceptibilities).

In this work, we revisit the problem of thermalization in integrable models. We specifically consider interacting quantum spin chains with a global
U(1)
charge
Q

Q

not
a faithful GGE, but rather a non-canonical ensemble generated by a super-extensive effective Hamiltonian. Such ensembles possess a
divergent

U(1)
chemical potential indicative of sub-extensive charge fluctuations. We dub such states suggestively as
squeezed GGEs
(SGGEs).

GGEs for interacting integrable systems.—

In the scope of the standard quantum quench protocol, we consider integrable interacting quantum spin chains with an internal (charge) degree of freedom. For simplicity, we assume the system possesses a single
U(1)
charge (i.e. no nesting) and consider only a class of product pure initial states

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}^{\otimes n}

\Lambda_{\ell}
of size

\varrho_{\ell}(t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}{\rm Tr}_{\bar{\Lambda}_{\ell}}\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}
,
where the trace is over the complementary lattice
\bar{\Lambda}_{\ell}
. At late times,
\varrho_{\ell}(t)
is expected to relax towards a GGE,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\varrho_{\ell}(t)=\varrho_{\rm GGE}

I_{i}
of the model
Ilievski et al. 2015a
;
Ilievski et al. 2016a

\varrho_{\rm GGE}=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}_{L}\exp{(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}I_{i}+h\,Q)}
(cf.
SM

Generalized Gibbs ensembles admit several equivalent descriptions

;
Ilievski and Quinn 2019

via
\rho^{\rm tot}_{s}(u)=p^{\prime}_{s}(u)/2\pi

Crucially, the coarse-grained information stored in state functions

is sufficient to uniquely fix all local correlation functions in a GGE

;

Squeezed GGEs.—

We consider quantum quenches from a class of pure initial states
\ket{\Psi}
with vanishing charge cumulants,
c^{(n)}=(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\lambda)^{n}F_{Q}(\lambda)|_{\lambda=0}=0
,
with the scaled cumulant generating function
F_{Q}(\lambda)\equiv\lim_{L\to\infty}L^{-1}\log\bra{\Psi}e^{\lambda\,Q}\ket{\Psi}
.
In spite of
c^{(n)}
remaining globally conserved at all times, any subsystem of length
\ell
will in general possess positive time-dependent cumulants
W^{(n)}(\ell,t)=\bra{\Psi(t)}Q^{n}_{\ell}\ket{\Psi(t)}
. Accordingly, one expects the emergent local equilibrium state to exhibit strictly positive
cumulants, namely
\chi^{(n)}=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\ell^{-1}W^{(n)}(\ell,t)>0
. Surprisingly, however, this is not what happens in the easy axis regime
\Delta>1
, where
W^{2}(\ell,t)
instead behaves anomalously, scaling sub-extensively with
\ell
.

We now shortly discuss how such a dynamical suppression of magnetic fluctuations in local equilibrium states is subtly related to ‘
freezing
’ of the mode occupations
n_{s}
; instead of diminishing with increasing
s
,
n_{s}
are found to converge toward non-trivial limiting functions (attractors) depending on whether
s
is even or odd, see. Fig.
2
.
As a corollary, the infinite sum over the quasiparticle spectrum becomes divergent,
\mathfrak{f}\to\infty
. The free-energy density
f
nonetheless remains finite. Indeed, infinitely many contributions can be resumed using certain kernel identities (cf.
SM
for details), signifying that
f
is manifestly finite in both canonical GGEs and squeezed ensembles. Importantly, however, a
divergent

\mathfrak{f}
implies
h=\infty
. In simple terms, a singular
U(1)
chemical potential means that the steady-state reduced density matrix is subject to a microcanonical constraint, i.e. confined within a fixed magnetisation sector of the Hilbert space.

Before heading on to explicit examples, there are several key remarks in order: (i) although the employed TBA formulae are strictly applicable only for canonical GGEs
Ilievski et al. 2017
;
Ilievski and Quinn 2019
, one can always regularize a divergent
\mathfrak{f}
by introducing an appropriate twist (say
\tau
) that renders
\mathfrak{f}
and hence also
\chi^{(n)}
finite, removing the twist only at the end; (ii) we emphasize that
\langle Q\rangle=0
does not generally imply
h=0
in a GGE. In fact, for
\Delta>1
and
\tau>0
we find instead
\mathfrak{f}(\tau)<\infty
, but with
\mathfrak{f}(\tau)
and
h=h(\tau)
both diverging as
\tau\to 0
, see
SM
; (iii) the peculiar freezing phenomenon cannot take place in integrable systems with a finite number of bound states since
\mathfrak{f}
cannot grow unboundedly. Hence, there is no freezing taking place in the gapless regime with
|\Delta|<1
and, for the same reason, this effect is genuinely due to attractive interaction (see also
SM
); (iv) our conclusions apply likewise to non-fluctuating magnetized states with
c^{(1)}\neq 0
upon subtracting the first moment in eq. (
2
),
Q_{\ell}\rightarrow Q_{\ell}-\langle Q_{\ell}\rangle
.

Proceeding now to explicit examples, we focus our analysis on simple initial valence-bond product states of two-site (
b=2
) blocks

\ket{\Psi_{N}}=\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\otimes L/2},\quad\ket{\Psi_{D}}=\Big[\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big]^{\otimes L/2},

(5)

allowing for explicit analytic computation of state functions in a recursive manner

Our main conclusions nevertheless hold very generally, i.e. are valid for other initial product states that only involve eigenstates with the same value of
Q

Pozsgay et al. 2019

Static spin susceptibility.—

Dynamical suppression of magnetic fluctuations in SGGEs implies a vanishing static spin susceptibility, namely
\chi=0
, given by the exact formula
Doyon 2020

\chi=\sum_{s\geq 1}\int\mathrm{d}u\,\chi_{s}(u)[m^{\rm dr}_{s}(u)]^{2},

(6)

where
\chi_{s}(u)\equiv\rho_{s}(u)(1-n_{s}(u))
are the single-mode susceptibilities and
m^{\rm dr}_{s}
denote the dressed magnetizations of quasiparticles (computed by solving the dressing equations
SM
).
Despite
m^{\rm dr}_{s}
all vanish upon approaching an unmagnetized state,
q\equiv\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\langle Q_{\ell}\rangle/\ell=0
, absence of uniform convergence requires regularization when evaluating Eq. (
6
). In order to reinstate finite magnetization density and finite fluctuations we employ
twisted
initial states. For instance, we use the twisted Néel state
\ket{\Psi_{N}(\tau)}=\ket{\psi_{N}(\tau)}^{\otimes L/2}
, where

\ket{\varphi_{N}(\tau)}\simeq\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\varphi_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\ket{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
,
with amplitudes
\varphi_{\uparrow\uparrow}(\tau)=-\varphi^{-1}_{\downarrow\downarrow}(\tau)=e^{\tau}
,
\varphi_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\tau)=-\varphi^{-1}_{\downarrow\uparrow}(\tau)=\cot{(\tau/2)}
depending on ‘twist’ parameter
\tau>0
. Such twisting in particular ensures that the mode occupation functions experience exponential decay for large
s
, while
m^{\rm dr}_{s}\sim q\,s^{2}
for small/intermediate
s
, mirroring thermal states. Unlike in thermal (Gibbs) states, where the density decay algebraically as
\rho_{s}(u)\sim s^{-3}
, which gives a finite limit for the susceptibility
\lim_{q\to 0}\chi>0
, SGGEs (in
\Delta>1
regime) instead generically exhibit exponential falloff
\rho_{s}(u)\sim e^{-\xi s}
.
Consequently, the zero-twist limit can be interchanged with the infinite sum over
s
, yielding
\chi=0
. Such exponential suppression of the densities is not incompatible with the observed freezing of the
n_{s}
at large
s
since the effective Brillouin zone (i.e. the Jacobian
k^{\prime}_{s}(u)
) available for quasiparticles with large
s
shrinks exponentially in SGGEs. Indeed, in this limit only the giant quasiparticles that carry finite effective magnetization, telling that they become effectively pinned locally in space and consequently preclude the distribution of magnetic fluctuations through the system.

Spin diffusion.—

We now briefly examine the diffusion constant
\mathfrak{D}
, using the following exact mode resolution
Nardis et al. 2019
;
De Nardis et al. 2019
;
Gopalakrishnan and Vasseur 2019

\mathfrak{D}=\sum_{s\geq 1}\int\mathrm{d}u\chi_{s}(u)|v^{\rm eff}_{s}(u)|\mu^{2}_{s},

(7)

with effective velocities
v_{s}^{\rm eff}(u)
(computed from the dressed dispersion relations, see
SM
) and magnetic moments
\mu_{s}\equiv\partial_{q}m_{s}^{\rm dr}|_{q=0}
. Using the general scaling
\mu_{s}\sim s^{2}
, alongside
|v^{\rm eff}_{s}|\sim e^{-\kappa s}
for
\Delta>1
, we readily conclude that
\mathfrak{D}>0
in SGGEs. However, as we explain shortly, we find a clear signature of anomalous diffusive behavior. We also note that DC spin conductivity
\sigma
vanishes identically, that is
\sigma=\mathfrak{D}\chi=0
.

To confirm our theoretical predictions, and to additionally infer the scaling properties of finite subsystems, we carry out numerical simulations using the matrix product states (MPS) with the iTensor library

For both Néel (see Fig.

followed by saturation to a value
\sim\ell^{2\zeta}
with the approximate fitted exponent

(affording to ignore still very pronounced finite

3
) in the case of ballistic dynamical exponent

, but not with

associated with diffusive processes. This leads us to rule out the self-affine structure of the magnetic fluctuations in SGGEs, signalling the absence of normal spin diffusion. Lastly, we also verify that the norm of the equilibrium Hamiltonian

The isotropic limit
\Delta\to 1
requires special attention due to an enhanced non-abelian symmetry

;

;

;

On the other hand, hydrodynamic relaxation from pure states is much less explored, and our work partially fills this void. Indeed, it turns out, somewhat surprisingly, that in the case of isotropic interaction the non-fluctuating initial states can exhibit different qualitative behavior. , implying a logarithmic divergence of

Conclusions.—
By considering a class of non-fluctuating initial product states, we demonstrated that interacting integrable systems hosting infinitely many bound states can evade thermalization to canonical GGEs. We found that local subsystems instead relax towards unorthodox states called squeezed GGEs. The latter feature sub-extensive magnetic fluctuations (signalled by a divergent
U(1)
chemical potential), and the approach to equilibrium violates the Family-Vicsek scaling hypothesis. Another distinguished property of SGGEs are non-decaying, so-called ‘frozen’, mode occupations of giant quasiparticles, causing an emergent large-scale semi-classical description
De Nardis et al. 2020
to break down. We are hopeful that the state-of-the-art quantum simulators
Trotzky et al. 2012
;
Scherg et al. 2021
;
Wei et al. 2022
and modern quantum processors
Chertkov et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2018

can provide a test bed and an ideal opportunity to verify our predictions, as they do not suffer from the rapid growth of entanglement generated by the quantum quench.

Several recent studies reported anomalous behaviour of macroscopic fluctuating quantities, such as the full counting statistics (FCS) (together with Rényi entropy
Alba 2019
;
Bertini et al. 2022
) of the charge transfer in quantum and classical Heisenberg chains

Appendix B
Generalized Gibbs ensembles in the Heisenberg chain

Here we provide a compressed summary of generalized Gibbs ensembles in the anisotropic Heisenberg XXZ spin chain in the language of Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz. Specializing to regime
|\Delta|\geq 1
, we discuss the analytic properties of various state functions and derive the formulae for the free-energy density.
For definiteness, we consider the isotropic interaction
\Delta=1
first, and later in a separate section discuss appropriate modifications to describe the model with
\Delta>1
.
We shall make extensive use the following compact notations for the scalar product and convolution,

g\circ h\equiv\sum_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}ug_{s}(u)h_{s}(u),\qquad g\star h\equiv\sum_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}ug_{s}(u-u^{\prime})h_{s^{\prime}}(u^{\prime}),

(SB.1)

respectively, for any set of real (dummy) functions
f,g,h
supported on the real rapidity axis
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we employ a compact notation for imaginary shifts by
\pm{\rm i}/2
, that is
g^{\pm}(u)\equiv g(u\pm\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})
and
g^{[\pm n]}(u)\equiv g(u\pm n\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})
.

Commuting fused transfer matrices.

Considering the unitary irreducible spin-
s/2
representations
\mathcal{V}_{s}
of dimension
s+1

with spin generators
\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{s}
,
the quasilocal conserved charges of the spin-
1/2
Heisenberg chain on
L
sites with the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathcal{V}^{\otimes L}_{1}
, are derived from the fused transfer matrices
\hat{T}_{s}(u)
,

\hat{T}_{s}(u)={\rm Tr}_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}\bigotimes_{L}\hat{L}_{s,1}(u),

(SB.2)

where

\hat{L}_{s,1}(u)=u\,\mathds{1}+2{\rm i}\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{s}\otimes\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{1},

(SB.3)

are quantum Lax operators acting on the tensor product
\mathcal{V}_{s}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{1}
,
while traces in Eq. (
SB.2
) are over the common auxiliary space
\mathcal{V}_{s}
,
u\in\mathbb{C}
is a general complex spectral parameter.
Mutual commutativity of
\hat{T}_{s}(u)

[\hat{T}_{s}(u),\hat{T}_{s^{\prime}}(u^{\prime})]=0,\qquad s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

(SB.4)

with
\hat{T}_{0}(u)=u^{L}
, is ensured by the Yang–Baxter equations. For more details, we direct the reader to
Ilievski et al. 2015a
;
Ilievski et al. 2016a
.

Quasilocal charges.

The quasilocal conserved charges correspond to logarithmic derivatives, that is

\hat{X}_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{u}\log{\frac{\hat{T}^{+}_{s}(u)}{\phi_{s}(u)}},

(SB.5)

where
\phi_{s}(\theta)
is a convenient normalization,
and
u\in\mathcal{P}
lies in the physical strip

\mathcal{P}=\{u\in\mathbb{C};|{\rm Im}(u)|<1/2\}.

(SB.6)

In the thermodynamic limit, magnon rapidities
\{u_{i}\}
(Bethe roots) organize into Bethe strings,

\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\{u_{i}\}\rightarrow\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{i=1}^{M_{j}}\bigcup_{k=1}^{j}\{u_{j,i}+\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}(j+1-2k)\},

(SB.7)

representing bound-state excitations. Generic thermodynamic eigenstates involve
M=\sum_{s}M_{s}
magnons, partitioned into
M_{s}\sim\mathcal{O}(L)
strings (up to corrections that are exponentially small in
L
).

Bound state undergo elastic collisions. The elementary scattering amplitude of two magnons is simple rational function depending on the rapidity difference
u
,

S(u)=\frac{u-{\rm i}}{u+{\rm i}},

(SB.8)

The fused scattering amplitude associated with a
j
-string and a
k
-string read

S_{j,\ell}(u)=\prod_{a=1}^{j}\prod_{b=1}^{\ell}S(u+(j-\ell-2a+2b)\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}),

(SB.9)

with convention that
S(0)=-1
. The differential scattering phases defined the set of kernels

K_{j,\ell}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\partial_{u}\log S_{j,k}(u).

(SB.10)

In the large-
L
limit, the separation between nearby strings with rapidities
u_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
decreases as
1/L
, allowing to introduce densities of Bethe strings
\rho_{s}(u)
.
The Bethe equations

e^{{\rm i}k(u_{i})L}\prod_{j=1}^{M}S(u_{i}-u_{j})=-1,

(SB.11)

can then be formulated as the Bethe–Yang integral equations

\rho_{s}+\bar{\rho}_{s}=K_{s}-K_{s,s^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}},

(SB.12)

where

K_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}k^{\prime}_{s}.

(SB.13)

String-charge duality.

Expressed in terms of the root densities, eigenvalues of
\hat{X}_{s}(u)
read

X_{s}=G_{ss^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}},

(SB.14)

where

G_{s,s^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\rm min}(s,s^{\prime})}K_{s+s^{\prime}+1-2j},

(SB.15)

represents the ‘bare energy tensor’. In terms of the
s
-kernel,

s(u)=\frac{1}{2\cosh{(\pi u)}}.

(SB.16)

we can write it as

G_{s,s^{\prime}}=(1+K)_{s,s^{\prime}}\star s.

(SB.17)

Eq. (
SB.14
) is referred to as the string-charge duality. By introducing the discrete d’Alembertian operator
\square
, acting on any set of dummy functions
g_{s}
as

(\square g)_{s}=\square_{ss^{\prime}}g_{s^{\prime}}=(s^{-1}\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}-I_{s,s^{\prime}})\star g_{s^{\prime}},

(SB.18)

the inverse of Eq. (
SB.14
) reads

\rho_{s}=\square X_{s}.

(SB.19)

We have simultaneously introduced the infinite dimensional incidence matrix
I_{s,s^{\prime}}
of
{\rm A}_{\infty}
root system,

I_{s,s^{\prime}}\equiv\delta_{s,s^{\prime}-1}+\delta_{s,s^{\prime}+1}.

(SB.20)

Notice that the left inverse
s^{-1}
of the convolution kernel
s(u)
,
s^{-1}\star s=\delta
, acts as an imaginary shift in the complex
\theta
-plane, which has to be taken with the
\epsilon
-prescription,

(s^{-1}\star g)(\theta)=g(u+\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}-{\rm i}\epsilon)+g(u-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}+{\rm i}\epsilon),

(SB.21)

in order to avoid touching the boundary of the physical strip
\mathcal{P}
.
In fact,
s^{-1}
is the pseudoinverse since it involves a non-trivial nullspace, i.e. there exist functions
\zeta
such that
s^{-1}\star\zeta=0
.

Analytic parametrization.

Following Ref.
Ilievski et al. 2017
, we being by writing the reduced density matrix of a GGE for a spin chain of length
L
in the form

\hat{\varrho}=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}_{L}\exp{\left[-\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int{\rm d}u\lambda_{s}(\theta)\hat{X}_{s}(\theta)-h\,\hat{S}^{z}_{\rm tot}\right]},

(SB.22)

where functions
\lambda_{s}(\theta)
play the role of Lagrange multipliers. The distinguished global
U(1)
charge,i.e. total magnetization, couples to chemical potential
h
.

As pointed out in Ref.
Ilievski et al. 2017
, the above representation of a GGE is not completely general. Indeed, the corresponding thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-functions (to be introduced shortly) will always be
holomorphic
in the physical strip
\mathcal{P}
in the complex
\theta
-plane. Generic
\mathcal{Y}
-functions are however meromorphic, i.e. they involve isolated zeros and poles in the interior of
\mathcal{P}
. To accommodate for this, one has to include the charges
\hat{X}_{s}(u)
with complex arguments
u\in\mathcal{P}
, combined into conjugate pairs to ensure hermiticity. Accordingly, by changing the basis and introducing conserved operators

\hat{\rho}_{s}=\square\hat{X}_{s},

(SB.23)

the GGE density matrix can be cast in the form

\hat{\rho}_{\rm GGE}=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}_{L}\exp{\left[-\mu_{s}\circ\hat{\rho}_{s}-h\,\hat{S}^{z}_{\rm tot}\right]}.

(SB.24)

Now
\mu_{s}(\theta)
can be interpreted as fugacities ascribed to quasiparticles, i.e. magnonic bound states. In terms of original Lagrange multipliers, we have the relation

\mu_{s}=G_{ss^{\prime}}\star\lambda_{s^{\prime}}+h\,s.

(SB.25)

Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz.

In the limit of large system size
L
, the finite-volume partition sum

\mathcal{Z}_{L}={\rm Tr}\,\hat{\varrho}_{\rm GGE}

exhibits exponential growth,

\mathcal{Z}_{L}\asymp e^{-L\,f},

(SB.26)

where

f=-\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\log\mathcal{Z}_{L},

(SB.27)

is the free energy per site. To compute
f
, we employ the saddle-point technique called the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz. To this end, we begin by casting the partition function as a functional integral,

\mathcal{Z}[\{\rho_{s}\}]\equiv\int\mathcal{D}[\{\rho_{s}\}]e^{-L\,\mathcal{F}[\{\rho_{s}\}]},

(SB.28)

in terms of the free-energy functional

\mathcal{F}[\{\rho_{s}\}]=\mathcal{E}[\{\rho_{s}\}]-\mathcal{S}_{\rm YY}[\{\rho_{s}\}]+h\left[\frac{1}{2}-\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}u\,s\,\rho_{s}(u)\right],

(SB.29)

with

\mathcal{E}[\{\rho_{s}\}]=\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}u\,\mu_{s}(u)\rho_{s}(u),\qquad\mathcal{S}_{\rm YY}[\{\rho_{s}\}]=\sum_{s}\int{\rm d}u\,\mathfrak{s}_{s}(u),

(SB.30)

where each mode contributes to the density of entropy a weight

\mathfrak{s}_{s}(u)=(\rho_{s}(u)+\bar{\rho}_{s}(u))\log{\big[(\rho_{s}(u)+\bar{\rho}_{s}(u))\big]}-\rho_{s}(u)\log{(\rho_{s}(u))}-\bar{\rho}_{s}(u)\log{(\bar{\rho}_{s}(u))}.

(SB.31)

The variational variables are only the root densities
\rho_{s}
represent, whereas the hole densities
\bar{\rho}_{s}
are related to
\rho_{s}

via the Bethe–Yang integral equations,

\rho_{s}+\bar{\rho}_{s}=K_{s}-K_{ss^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}},

(SB.32)

and thus we have

\delta\bar{\rho}_{s}=-(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\delta\rho_{s^{\prime}}.

(SB.33)

Free-energy density is obtained by variational optimization of the free-energy functional
\mathfrak{F}
, that is

f=\mathcal{F}[\{\rho_{s}\}]_{\rho_{s}=\rho^{*}_{s}},

(SB.34)

where
\rho^{*}_{s}
represent the saddle-point densities.
Affording a slight abuse of notation, we shall subsequently operate only with the saddle-point quantities and omit the star symbol.
By introducing the thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-functions,

\mathcal{Y}_{s}(u)\equiv\frac{\bar{\rho}_{s}(u)}{\rho_{s}(u)},

(SB.35)

and rewriting the entropic contribution compactly as

\mathfrak{s}_{s}=\rho_{s}\log{[1+\mathcal{Y}_{s}]}+\bar{\rho}_{s}\log{[1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s}]},

(SB.36)

the saddle-point condition

\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho_{s}|_{\rho^{*}_{s}}=0,

(SB.37)

yields, upon substituting
\delta\bar{\rho}_{s}
with
\delta\rho_{s}
,

0=\mu_{s}-s\,h+\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}-(1+K)_{s,s^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}.

(SB.38)

These saddle-point equations are the celebrated
canonical TBA equations

\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\mu_{s}-h\,s+K_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}}).

(SB.39)

The logarithm of
\mathcal{Y}
-functions determined the dressed quasiparticle energies via

\varepsilon_{s}(u)=\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}(u).

(SB.40)

The effective velocity of quasiparticle propagation
are the computed based on the dressed dispersion relations, namely

v_{s}^{\rm eff}(u)=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{s}}{\partial p_{s}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{s}^{\prime}(u)}{p_{s}^{\prime}(u)},

(SB.41)

where dressed momenta
p^{\prime}_{s}(u)
coincide with the total state densities,

p^{\prime}_{s}(u)=2\pi(\rho_{s}+\bar{\rho}_{s}).

(SB.42)

Free energy.

In terms of the thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-functions, the density of free energy can be expressed as

f=\frac{h}{2}+(\mu_{s}-h\,s)\circ\rho_{s}-\rho_{s}\circ\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}-\bar{\rho}_{s}\circ\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}.

(SB.43)

Using the Bethe–Yang equations
\bar{\rho}_{s}=K_{s}-(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}}
, we can eliminate
\bar{\rho}_{s}
and recast the above expression in the form

f=\frac{h}{2}+(\mu_{s}-h\,s-\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})})\circ\rho_{s}-K_{s}\circ\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}+(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}}\circ\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}.

(SB.44)

The last term can be simplified using the symmetry
K_{ss^{\prime}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=K_{s^{\prime}s}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
, yielding

(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\rho_{s^{\prime}}\circ\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}=\rho_{s}\circ(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})=\rho_{s}\circ\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}-\rho_{s}\circ(\mu_{s}-h\,s),

(SB.45)

where in the second line we have made use of the TBA equations
K_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}=\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}-(\mu_{s}-hs)
.
This means that in the above expression for
f
two terms get cancelled out, leaving us with the simple result

f=\frac{h}{2}-K_{s}\circ\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}.

(SB.46)

The outlined derivation only makes use of kernel identities is thus completely
general
and free of any analyticity assumptions.

Resumation.

We now show how to resolve the infinite summation to obtain a simple compact expression for
f
.
To this end, we insert the resolution of the identity
(1-R)_{ss^{\prime\prime}}\star(1+K)_{s^{\prime\prime}s}=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
and rewrite
f
as

f=\frac{h}{2}-K_{s}\circ(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\big[(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}\big].

(SB.47)

The term in the square bracket can now again be simplified by using the TBA equations,

\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}=\mu_{s}-h\,s+(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}
, yielding

f=\frac{h}{2}-K_{s}\circ(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\big[\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}-\mu_{s^{\prime}}+hs^{\prime}\big].

(SB.48)

Using further that
(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\alpha\,s^{\prime}=0
for any constant
\alpha
, we arrive at

f^{*}=\frac{h}{2}-\big[\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}-\mu_{s}\big]\circ(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star K_{s^{\prime}}.

(SB.49)

Here we encounter the following subtlety: the convolution kernel
(1-R)
is not the proper inverse of Fredholm kernel
(1+K)
since it possesses a non-trivial null space.
Evidently, upon convolving with
(1-R)
the
h
-dependent term in the square bracket gets
erased
. In effect, the resulting expression for
f^{*}
does in fact not equal
f
.
To better elucidate this point, notice that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mu_{s}=0
, along with
\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}(h)\sim-h\,s
at large
s
, imply that
\lim_{s\to\infty}[\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})}-\mu_{s^{\prime}}+hs^{\prime}\big]=0
. On the other hand,
after acting with the convolution we ended up with
\lim_{s\to\infty}s^{-1}[\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})}-\mu_{s}\big]=-h
.
This issue can be elegantly circumvented by introducing regularized
\mathcal{Y}
-functions,

\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}=\frac{\mathcal{Y}_{s}}{\mathcal{Y}^{\infty}_{s}},\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{\infty}_{s}=e^{-h\,s},

(SB.50)

with unit asymptotics,
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}=1
writing

\log(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s})-\mu_{s}+h\,s=\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})}+\log\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}-\mu_{s},

(SB.51)

such that we can now safely convolve with
(1-R)
without losing any information, yielding

f=\frac{h}{2}-[\log(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s})+\log\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}-\mu_{s}]\circ(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star K_{s^{\prime}}s.

(SB.52)

By finally using the identity
(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star K_{s^{\prime}}=\delta_{s,1}s
, we obtain

f=\frac{h}{2}+s\circ\big(\mu_{1}-\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{1})}\big)+s\circ\log\mathcal{Y}_{1}-h\circ s,

(SB.53)

which, upon cancelling the first term with the last one using
1\circ s=1/2
, brings us to the final compact result

f=s\circ\big(\mu_{1}-\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{1})}\big).

(SB.54)

Remarkably, there is no explicit
h
-dependence in this formula, unlike in the original (canonical) formula.
Let us also stress again that we have performed all the computations independently of any state-specific information. Formula (
SB.54
) follows purely from the formal structure of the canonical TBA equations and kernel identities stemming from the underlying fusion rules.

For practical purposes, it is beneficial to replace the initial source term
\mu_{1}(\theta)
with the local source term
d_{1}(u)
.
This can be achieved via the identity

s\circ\mu_{1}=d_{1}\circ[(1+K_{2})\star s],

(SB.55)

which follows as a consequence of
\mu_{1}=(1+K_{1,1})\star d_{1}
. Using further that
(1+K_{2})\star s=K_{1}
, we arrive at

f=K_{1}\circ d_{1}-s\circ\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{1})}.

(SB.56)

The key advantage of this formula is that now
d_{1}
can be retrieved entirely
from the analytic data of the initial
\mathcal{Y}
-function without ever needing to compute
\mu_{1}
.

Decoupled TBA equations.

In order to fully exhibit the analytic structure of the
\mathcal{Y}
-functions, we proceed by recasting the TBA equations in the so-called decoupled form.
To this end, we rewrite the canonical TBA equations in the form

(1+K_{ss^{\prime}})\star\log\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}}=\mu_{s}-h\,s+K_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})},

(SB.57)

and by subsequently applying the pseudo-inverse
(1-R)
we obtain the equivalent decoupled form

\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}=d_{s}+I_{ss^{\prime}}s\star\log{(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})},

(SB.58)

with local source terms

d_{s}=(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\mu_{s^{\prime}}.

(SB.59)

Here we again encounter the “nullspace problem”; we have to ensure the solution to the decoupled equations matches the original solution of the canonical equations. To achieve this, we request the large-
s
dependence

\mathcal{Y}_{s}\sim e^{-h\,}s.

(SB.60)

The local source terms admit the following general decomposition,

d_{s}=s\star\lambda_{s}+\zeta_{s},

(SB.61)

where
\zeta
-functions lie entirely in the nullspace of the pseudo-inverse
s^{-1}
, namely

s^{-1}\star\zeta_{s}=0.

(SB.62)

Finally, we recast the local TBA equations into equivalent functional relations, written in terms of complex
\mathcal{Y}
-functions obtained via
analytically continuation from the real rapidity axis. The procedure goes as follows. Operating first on both sides by
s^{-1}
and taking the exponent, we readily obtain

\mathcal{Y}^{+}_{s}(u-{\rm i}\epsilon)\mathcal{Y}^{-}_{s}(u-{\rm i}\epsilon)=e^{\lambda_{s}(u)}\log{[(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s-1}(u))(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s+1}(u))]},

(SB.63)

using the usual compact notation for imaginary shift
g^{\pm}(u)\equiv g(u\pm\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})
.
An infinitesimal regulator
\epsilon
is required here to avoid touching the boundary of the physical strip
\mathcal{P}
. The above equations, which generally involve state-dependent node terms, and called the modified
\mathcal{Y}
-system.

The local TBA equations can be transformed back to the canonical TBA equations as follows. First, the
\mathcal{Y}
-functions appearing
on left-hand side of

\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}-I_{ss^{\prime}}s\star\log\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}}=d_{s}+I_{ss^{\prime}}s\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})},

(SB.64)

are replaced with the regularized ones and written as
(1-R)_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s^{\prime}}
. Next, convolving both sides
with
(1+K)
and using
(1+K)_{s,s^{\prime\prime}}\star I_{s^{\prime\prime},s^{\prime}}s=K_{ss^{\prime}}
yields

\log\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}=\mu_{s}+K_{ss^{\prime}}\star\log{(1+1/\mathcal{Y}_{s^{\prime}})},

(SB.65)

where

\mu_{s}=(1+K)_{ss^{\prime}}\star d_{s^{\prime}}.

(SB.66)

Lastly, one replaces
\mathcal{Y}^{\rm reg}_{s}
with
e^{h\,s}\mathcal{Y}_{s}
to recover the missing term
-h\,s
.

Anisotropic easy-axis regime

The construction from the previous section extends with little modifications. Parametrizing anistotropy as
\Delta=\cosh{(\eta)}
with
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
the Brillouin zone now wraps to a circle of circumference
\pi
. We pick the fundamental zone, i.e. work with rapidities in the range
u\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. Analytic continuation
to complex
u
now extends to
|{\rm Im}(u)|<\eta/2
, i.e. the physical strip is a cylinder

\mathcal{P}_{\eta}\equiv(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\times(-{\rm i}\eta/2,{\rm i}\eta/2)
. Compactification of rapidities implies the
k
-space becomes a discrete lattice, with the forward and backward discrete Fourier transforms reading

f(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)e^{-{\rm i}\,2ku},\qquad\hat{f}(k)=\int^{\pi/2}_{-\pi/2}{\rm d}uf(u)e^{{\rm i}\,2uk}.

(SB.67)

The fundamental kernel inversion identity is structurally preserved under
\eta
-deformation,

(1+K)^{-1}=(1-R),

(SB.68)

except that this time the Fredholm resolvent
R=I_{s,s^{\prime}}\mathfrak{s}
involves a more complicated
\mathfrak{s}
-kernel, namely a doubly-periodic function. Its Fourier representation is however very simple,

\hat{\mathfrak{s}}(k)=\frac{1}{2\cosh{(k\eta)}},

(SB.69)

and therefore we can immediately give a series representation

\mathfrak{s}(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}\,2ku}}{2\cosh{(k\eta)}}.

(SB.70)

To derive the
\mathcal{Y}
-system functional relations, we require analytic continuation to the boundaries of
\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
. In analogy with the isotropic case, we introduce the discrete shift, denoted by
s^{-1}
, once again taken with the
\epsilon
-prescription

[s^{-1}_{\eta}\star f](u)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\big[f(u+\tfrac{{\rm i}\eta}{2}-{\rm i}\epsilon)+f(u-\tfrac{{\rm i}\eta}{2}+{\rm i}\epsilon)\big],

(SB.71)

Analogously to the undeformed case, we have

s^{-1}\star\mathfrak{s}=\delta
and
thus the following useful identity,

s^{-1}\star(\mathfrak{s}\star f)=f\qquad{\rm for}\quad u\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2),

(SB.72)

which holds for any dummy function
f
. This follows immediately from
\pm{\rm i}\eta/2
shifts
and by recalling the resolution of the Dirac delta
\delta(u)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{{\rm i}\,2ku}
.

To find the rapidity space representation of kernel
\hat{\mathfrak{s}}(k)
, we make use of the Jacobi elliptic functions.
To this end, we introduce the elliptic integral

K(m)=\int^{\pi/2}_{0}\frac{{\rm d}\varphi}{\sqrt{1-m\sin^{2}(\varphi)}},\qquad K^{\prime}(m)\equiv K(1-m),

(SB.73)

and recall the series expansion

\frac{2K}{\pi}{\rm dn}(\upsilon)=1+4\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\cos{(2n\tfrac{\pi}{2K}\upsilon)}}{q^{n}+q^{-n}},

(SB.74)

with nome
q=\exp{(-\pi K^{\prime}/K)}
. Setting
q=e^{\eta}
and introducing
u=(\pi/2K)\upsilon
,
we can rewrite it as a Fourier series,

\mathfrak{s}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{2K}{\pi}{\rm dn}\Big(\frac{2K}{\pi}u\Big)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[1+2\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\cos{(2ku)}}{\cosh{(k\eta)}}\right]=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\cos{(2ku)}}{2\cosh{(k\eta)}}.

(SB.75)

For generic macrostates, the
\mathcal{Y}
-system relations are modified by the presence of additional node data, namely

\mathcal{Y}^{+}_{s}\mathcal{Y}^{-}_{s}=e^{\lambda_{s}}(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s-1})(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s+1}).

(SB.76)

Taking the logarithm and subsequently undoing the contour shifts by convolving with
\mathfrak{s}
, we arrive at a local form of the TBA equations

\log\mathcal{Y}_{s}=d_{s}+\log{[(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s-1})(1+\mathcal{Y}_{s+1})]},

(SB.77)

with source terms
d_{s}
related to
\lambda_{s}
via

d_{s}=\mathfrak{s}\star\lambda_{s}+\zeta_{s}.

(SB.78)

In the same way as in the isotropic case, the local source terms
d_{s}
pick up additional nullspace contributions
\zeta_{s}
,

s^{-1}_{\eta}\star\zeta_{s}=0,

(SB.79)

whenever the
\mathcal{Y}
-function contains a zero or a pole inside
\mathcal{P}
. On the other hand, Lagrange multipliers that determine the so-called node data instead span the kernel of
s^{-1}_{\eta}
and are therefore retrieved by virtue
s^{-1}_{\eta}\star(\mathfrak{s}\star\lambda_{s})=\lambda_{s}
.
We must accordingly find which doubly-periodic functions span
{\rm ker}(s^{-1}_{\eta})
. In addition, in order to be able to factor the analytic data out of
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, we need the
\eta
-deformed
\tau
-function, say
\tau_{\eta}(u;w)
to obey the identity
\tau^{+}(u-w)\tau^{-}(u-w)=1
, such that in the
\eta\to 0
limit we recover

\tau_{\eta}(u;w)\to\tau(u;w)=\tanh{(\tfrac{\pi}{2}(u-w))}
. Moreover, by sending
w
towards the strip boundary
we have to retrieve
\mathfrak{s}
.

To deduce the explicit form of the basis functions for nullspace of
s^{-1}_{\eta})
, it will suffice to have a brief look at the thermal Gibbs case.
The associated partition function corresponds to the large-
N
limit of
\mathcal{Z}^{(N)}_{L}
associated with a classical vertex model in the torus topology with physical circumference
L
and perpendicular (fictitious) dimension
N
, understood as the Trotter parameter. The general construction of the isotropic Heisenberg model can be found in Ref.
Ilievski and Quinn 2019
which, mostly for compactness of presentation, we do not review here.

The analytic structure of the initial
\mathcal{Y}
-function, denoted by
\mathcal{Y}^{(N)}_{1}
, is inherited from the leading eigenvalue of the fundamental column transfer matrix. For finite
N
,
the initial
\mathcal{Y}
-function (describing the truncated Gibbs state) involves a zero of degree
2N
located at
\theta=\pm{\rm i}(\eta/2\mp\alpha_{N})
and no other analytic data inside
\mathcal{P}
, with
\alpha_{N}\equiv\beta/2N
.
In the large-
N
limit, the solution converges to the Gibbs state with the local source term coupling to the initial node becomes

d^{\rm Gibbs}_{1}(\theta)=-4\pi\beta\,\mathfrak{s}(\theta).

(SB.80)

This can be easily seen by performing the limit in Fourier space,

\displaystyle-2\pi N\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2{\rm i}k\theta}\frac{\sinh{(2k\alpha_{N})}}{k\cosh{(k\eta)}}

\displaystyle=-4\pi\beta\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\sinh{(2k\alpha_{N})}}{2k\alpha_{N}}\hat{\mathfrak{s}}(k)\to-4\pi\beta\mathfrak{s}(k),

(SB.81)

showing that finite-
N
regularization is in fact the discrete approximation to the Dirac
\delta
-function. We thus conclude that the initial
\lambda
-function of the GGE,
namely
\lambda_{1}(u)\simeq\delta(u)
. Recalling that
\mu_{1}\simeq\lambda_{1}\star\mathfrak{s}
, we infer that

s^{-1}_{\eta}\star\mathfrak{s}=\delta
fall outside the nullspace of
s^{-1}_{\eta}
.
This explains the general mechanism of how the node data arises from the analytic data of
\mathcal{Y}
-functions
\mathcal{Y}^{(N)}_{s}
in the scaling limit. In this regard, it is necessary that
a degree
N
zero or pole approaching in a
1/N
fashion towards the boundary
\partial\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
; contributions from isolated zeros of poles of finite order would otherwise be negligible.
The upshot of the above analysis is that any individual zero or a pole located at
w\in\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
(restricted to the upper half,
{\rm Im}(w)>0
) contributes a term

\hat{\zeta}(k;v)=-2\pi\frac{\sinh(-2{\rm i}kv)}{k}=2\pi{\rm i}\frac{\sin{(2kv)}}{k},

(SB.82)

where the parameter
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\eta}
is related to
w
via

w=\frac{{\rm i}\eta}{2}-v,\qquad{\rm Im}(v)\leq\frac{\eta}{2}.

(SB.83)

We note that
v
represents an inhomogeneity of the column transfer matrix. The thermal Gibbs case is a special case where zeros are located at
w_{N}={\rm i}\eta/2-{\rm i}\alpha_{N}
, corresponding to
v_{N}={\rm i}\alpha_{N}
.

To give a concrete example, we briefly consider the Dimer state. The corresponding
\mathcal{Y}
-functions involve a double zero at the origin (
v={\rm i}\eta/2
) and a double zero at the edge of the Brillouin zone at
u=\pm\eta/2
(
v={\rm i}\eta/2+\pi/2
) for all the nodes
s\in\mathbb{N}
. This results in the source term of the form

d^{\rm D}_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{d}^{\rm D}_{s}(k),

(SB.84)

with

\hat{d}^{\rm D}_{s}(k)=[\hat{\zeta}(k;{\rm i}\eta/2)+\hat{\zeta}(k;{\rm i}\eta/2+\pi/2)]\hat{\mathfrak{s}}(k)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\tanh{(k\eta)}}{k}\Big(-1-(-1)^{k}\Big).

(SB.85)

Appendix C
Thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-system

Here we summarize how to compute the thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-functions
\mathcal{Y}_{s}(u)
from the expectation values of the quasilocal charges
\hat{X}_{s}(u)
on the initial state
\ket{\Psi}
.

We employ the transfer matrix method, assuming the state has a product form, i.e. can be written as
\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}^{\otimes L/b}
with period
b\in\mathbb{N}
. First, we define a family of the double-row transfer matrices

\mathbb{T}^{\Psi}_{s}(u;\alpha)\equiv\bra{\psi}\bigotimes_{b}\mathbb{L}_{s}(u;\alpha)\ket{\psi},

(SC.86)

with Lax operators

\mathbb{L}_{s}(u;\alpha)\equiv\frac{L^{-}_{s}(u)L^{+}(u+\alpha)}{L^{[-s-1]}_{0}(u)L^{[s+1]}_{0}(u+\alpha)},

(SC.87)

acting of the tensor product
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{V}^{\otimes 2}_{s}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{s}
is an auxiliary unitary spin-
s/2
representation.
We have introduced an additional parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
formally acting as source coupling to
\hat{X}_{s}(u)
.
The partition function
\mathcal{Z}^{\Psi}_{s}(u;\alpha)
can be computed by iteration, yielding

\mathcal{Z}^{\Psi}_{s}(u;\alpha)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}{\rm Tr}_{\mathcal{V}^{\otimes 2}_{s}}[\mathbb{T}^{\Psi}_{s}(u;\alpha)]^{L/b}.

(SC.88)

The average values of quasilocal charges

X^{\Psi}_{s}(u)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\bra{\Psi}\hat{X}_{s}(u)\ket{\Psi},

(SC.89)

are therefore given by

X^{\Psi}_{s}(u)=-{\rm i}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{Z}^{\Psi}_{s}(u;\alpha)|_{\alpha=0}.

(SC.90)

The information stored in the set of complex functions
\{X^{\Psi}_{s}(u)\}
with
u\in\mathcal{P}
uniquely fixes an equilibrium macrostate. By employing the string-charge duality
Ilievski et al. 2016b
, the root and hole densities of Bethe strings are given by

\displaystyle\rho^{\Psi}_{s}(u)

\displaystyle=\square X^{\Psi}_{s}(u)=X^{\Psi}_{s}(u+\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})+X^{\Psi}_{s}(u-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})-X^{\Psi}_{s-1}(u)-X^{\Psi}_{s+1}(u),

(SC.91)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{\Psi}_{s}(u)

\displaystyle=K_{s}(u)-X^{\Psi}_{s}(u+\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2})-X^{\Psi}_{s}(u-\tfrac{{\rm i}}{2}),

(SC.92)

from where we readily obtain

\mathcal{Y}^{\Psi}_{s}(u)=\frac{\bar{\rho}^{\Psi}_{s}(u)}{\rho^{\Psi}_{s}(u)}.

(SC.93)

For meromorphic solutions, i.e. macrostate whose
\mathcal{Y}
-functions only involve the analytic data (zeros and poles inside the physical strip
\mathcal{P}
) and no node data (cf. Refs.
Ilievski et al. 2017
;
Ilievski and Quinn 2019
), the thermodynamic
\mathcal{Y}
-function obey the canonical
\mathcal{Y}
-system relations (without additional node terms). In this case, it suffices to infer the initial
\mathcal{Y}
-function, while the higher ones can be obtained in a closed analytic form simply by iteration, namely

\mathcal{Y}_{s}(u)=\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{+}_{s-1}(u)\mathcal{Y}^{-}_{s-1}(u)}{1+\mathcal{Y}_{s-2}(u)}-1,\qquad s\geq 2.

(SC.94)

In particular, the entire family of two-site integrable boundary states, representing generic product states with
b=2
, always yields such meromorphic solutions.
In general, states with
b=2
involve also non-trivial node data. In such cases, an iterative solution of the
\mathcal{Y}
-system hierarchy is no longer possible and one has to compute the
\mathcal{Y}
-functions one by one.

Numerical solution of the
\mathcal{Y}
-system

The recursive relation (
SC.94
) allows obtaining all
\mathcal{Y}
-functions once we know explicitly the first one for
s=1
. Focusing first on the gapped regime,
\Delta=\cosh{(\eta)}
with
\eta>0
, we employ the outlined procedure to deduce
\mathcal{Y}_{1}(u)
, with
u\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]

(suppressing additional dependence on
\eta
and twisting parameters).

•

Dimer state
\ket{\rm D}=\Big[\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big]^{\otimes L/2}
, with

\mathcal{Y}^{\rm D}_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\tan^{2}(u)\csc(u-{\rm i}\eta)\csc(u+{\rm i}\eta)(\cosh(2\eta)+3\cos(2u)+2).

(SC.95)

•

Néel state
\ket{\rm N}=\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\otimes L/2}
, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\rm N}_{s}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\sin^{2}(2u)\csc(u-{\rm i}\eta)\csc(u+{\rm i}\eta)\sec(u-{\rm i}\eta)\sec(u+{\rm i}\eta)

\displaystyle\times\text{csch}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\eta-2{\rm i}u)\right)\text{csch}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\eta+2{\rm i}u)\right)(\cosh(\eta)+2\cosh(3\eta)-3\cos(2u)).

(SC.96)

•

One-parameter family of twisted Néel states

\ket{{\rm N}(\tau)}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{{\rm N}(\tau)}}\left[e^{\tau}\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}+\cot{(\tau/2)}\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\tan{(\tau/2)}\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}-e^{-\tau}\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right],

(SC.97)

with normalization
\mathcal{N}_{{\rm N}(\tau)}=2\cosh{(2\tau)}+\cot^{2}{(\tau/2)}+\tan^{2}{(\tau/2)}
,
recovering
\ket{\rm N}
as
\tau\to 0
.
The first
\mathcal{Y}
-function is quite lengthy and we thus do not display it here.

•

Two-parameter family of twisted Dimer states

\ket{{\rm D}(\tau;\gamma)}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{{\rm D}(\tau;\gamma)}}\left[\frac{e^{\gamma}}{\cosh{(\eta)}}\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}+\frac{{\rm i}}{\sinh{(\eta/2)}}\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\frac{{\rm i}}{\sinh{(\eta/2)}}\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}-\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\cosh{(\eta)}}\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right],

(SC.98)

with normalization
\mathcal{N}_{{\rm D}(\tau;\gamma)}=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{({\rm csch}^{2}(\eta/2)+\cosh{(2\gamma)}{\rm sech}^{2}{(1/\tau)})}
,
recovering the Dimer state
\ket{\rm D}
as
\tau\to 0
. The initial
\mathcal{Y}
-function reads

\displaystyle 32e^{4\gamma}(1+\mathcal{Y}^{{\rm D}(\tau;\gamma)}_{s}(u))

\displaystyle=-\sec^{2}(u)\csc(u-{\rm i}\eta)\csc(u+{\rm i}\eta)\sec^{2}\left(u-\frac{{\rm i}}{\tau}\right)\sec^{2}\left(u+\frac{{\rm i}}{\tau}\right)(\cos(4u)-\cosh(2\eta))

\displaystyle\times\left(\left(1+e^{4\gamma}\right)\cosh(\eta)+2e^{2\gamma}\cosh\left(\frac{2}{\tau}\right)-e^{4\gamma}\cos(2u-{\rm i}\eta)-\cos(2u-{\rm i}\eta)+2e^{2\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\left(1+e^{4\gamma}\right)\cosh(\eta)+2e^{2\gamma}\cosh\left(\frac{2}{\tau}\right)-e^{4\gamma}\cos(2u+{\rm i}\eta)-\cos(2u+{\rm i}\eta)+2e^{2\gamma}\right).

In our applications, we set
\gamma=1
.

To numerically generate the higher
\mathcal{Y}
-functions we use the following procedure:

1.

We create a matrix
Y_{i,1,k}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}(u_{i}+(N_{\rm shift}/2-k){\rm i}\eta/2)
where the rapidities
u
are discretized by Gaussian quadrature with
N_{\rm point}
, the integer
k\in[0,N_{\rm shift}-1]
, and with the integer
s\in[0,S_{\rm{max}}]
.

2.

Using the convention that
\mathcal{Y}_{0}(u)=0
, we then calculate the corresponding values starting on the plane
s=1
and
s=2
, and generate for
s\geq 2
as

Y_{i,j,k}=\frac{Y_{i,j-1,k-1}Y_{i,j+1,k+1}}{1+Y_{i,j-2,k}}-1.

(SC.99)

3.

In the matrix
Y_{i,j,k}
, we select the plane corresponding to
k=N_{\rm shift}/2
.

We remind there is a one-to-one correspondence between the occupation (filling) functions and the
\mathcal{Y}
-functions:

n_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{1+\mathcal{Y}_{s}(u)}.

(SC.100)
