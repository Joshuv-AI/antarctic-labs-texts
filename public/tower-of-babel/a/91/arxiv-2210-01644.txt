1.
Introduction

This paper concerns the generalization to infinite-dimensional symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebras of the construction of Chevalley groups associated to finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebras.

Let
A
be a symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Kac–Moody algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
associated with
A
. We denote simple roots by
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}
, the set of roots by
\Delta
, the set of positive (respectively negative) roots by
\Delta_{\pm}
,
and the set of real roots by
\Delta^{\re}
. If
A
is not of finite type, then
\mathfrak{g}
is infinite dimensional.
Let
V=V^{\lambda}
be an integrable highest-weight
\mathfrak{g}
-module with highest weight
\lambda
, highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
, and defining homomorphism
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\End(V^{\lambda})
.
Let
\chi_{\pm\alpha_{i}}(t)\in\operatorname{Aut}(V)
denote
\exp(t\rho(e_{i}))
(resp.
\exp(t\rho(f_{i}))
), for each
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, where
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}
and
e_{i}
(resp.
f_{i}
) is the Chevalley–Serre generator corresponding to the simple root
\pm\alpha_{i}
.
A (split)
minimal representation-theoretic Kac–Moody group
is

G({\mathbb{Q}})=G^{\lambda}({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t),\ \chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\rangle.

We let
{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
denote the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-form of the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
(
[
Tit87
]
; see also Section
3
below).
As in
[
CG03
]
, we set
V_{\mathbb{Z}}=\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}.

The subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
is

\displaystyle G({\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t),\ \chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\ t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\rangle

and the
Chevalley subgroup
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is the subgroup the stabilizes the integral lattice
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
:

\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=\{g\in G({\mathbb{Q}})\mid g\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

This work concerns the question of
integrality
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. By this we mean:

‘Is
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
?’

It is easy to show that
G({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
. Thus it remains to determine if
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

The answer to this question has not yet been established for any family of Kac–Moody groups. Here we consider integrality of certain subgroups of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. We call a subgroup
M
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})

integral
if
M\cap G({\mathbb{Z}})=M\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
.
This is equivalent to showing that for all
g\in M
,
g\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
g\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
Note that
M_{1}\subseteq M_{2}
and
M_{2}
integral implies that
M_{1}
is integral.
However
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
integral
does not
imply that the subgroup
\langle M_{1},M_{2}\rangle
generated by
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is integral.

In the finite dimensional case, the property of integrality for semi-simple algebraic groups
G({\mathbb{Q}})
, with root system
\Delta
and Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, was established in
[
Che95
]
. Chevalley constructed (what was his notion of) an affine group scheme associated to
G({\mathbb{Q}})
and
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and showed that its coordinate algebra is generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This was a starting point for Grothendieck and Demazure who organized the SGA3 seminar (
[
DG70
]
) where they developed the theory of split reductive group schemes over arbitrary base schemes. Demazure and Grothendieck’s theory assumed known the classification of reductive groups over an algebraically closed field due primarily to Chevalley, which also holds over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
L
be a lattice satisfying
Q\subseteq L\subseteq P
where
Q
is the root lattice and
P
is the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Chevalley showed that (
\Delta
,
L
) defines an affine group scheme
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}})=\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{Spec}({\mathbb{Z}}),G_{{\mathbb{Z}}})

is its set of integral points (
[
Che95
]
). The group
G_{{\mathbb{Z}}}({\mathbb{Q}})\cong G({\mathbb{Q}})
is now known as a Chevalley group. If
L=Q
, then
G_{{\mathbb{Z}}}({\mathbb{Q}})
is called
adjoint
and if
L=P
, then
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
is called
simply connected
. It follows that the group
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
is generated by the automorphisms

\{\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t),\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid\ t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\},

so if
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
is simply connected, it is integral.

To our knowledge, there is no proof in the literature that
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
for semi-simple linear algebraic groups
G({\mathbb{Q}})
that doesn’t use the language of group schemes. In certain cases, it is straightforward to verify integrality using properties of the linear algebraic group and underlying representation. For example, it is easy to see that
\operatorname{SL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the stabilizer of the standard lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
. See also
[
Sou07
]
, where Soulé showed that the group
\mathbb{E}_{7}({\mathbb{Z}})
coincides with
\mathbb{E}_{7}({\mathbb{R}})\cap\operatorname{Sp}_{56}({\mathbb{Z}})
where
\mathbb{E}_{7}({\mathbb{R}})
is the non-compact real form and
\operatorname{Sp}_{56}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the stabilizer of the standard lattice (and the canonical symplectic form) in the fundamental representation of dimension 56 of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{e}_{7}
.

The methods used to prove integrality in the finite dimensional case do not extend to Kac–Moody groups
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. Here we address the question of integrality of subgroups of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
using only the definition of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
acting on the integrable highest weight module
V
. We introduce the following slight modification of the notion of integrality.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
M
be a subgroup of the Kac–Moody group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
and let
v_{0}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. We say that
M
is
strongly integral with respect to
v_{0}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
if, for all
g\in M
,
g\cdot v_{0}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
g\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

This is stronger than integrality since it only requires us to test the action on a single vector
v_{0}
, rather than on the entire set
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. It is easy to see that strong integrality implies integrality (Lemma
4.1
).

The (positive) unipotent subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is

U=U({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\rangle.

Unipotent subgroups play an important role in the study of the structure and representation theory of Kac–Moody groups. They are constituents of group decompositions such as Iwasawa and Birkhoff decompositions, which provide
important tools for studying Kac–Moody groups and their applications.

In this paper, as a first step towards proving integrality of
U
, we prove strong integrality of inversion subgroups of
U
, which are defined as follows. For
w
in the Weyl group
W
, the
inversion subgroup

U_{(w)}
is given as

U_{(w)}=\langle U_{\beta}\mid\beta\in\Phi_{(w)}\rangle,

where

\Phi_{(w)}=\{\beta\in\Delta_{+}\mid w^{-1}\beta\in\Delta_{-}\}
and
U_{\beta}=\{\chi_{\beta}(t)\mid t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\}
for
\chi_{\beta}(t)
as in Subsection
3.2
.

We write
\widetilde{w}
for the usual lift of
w
to
G({\mathbb{Q}})
(see Subsection
3.2
).
Our main result is the following (Section
6
).

Theorem 1.2
.

(Strong integrality of inversion subgroups)
Let
G({\mathbb{Q}})
be as above. Let
U
be the positive unipotent subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. For
w\in W
,

U_{(w)}
is strongly integral with respect to
\widetilde{w}v_{\lambda}
.

If the matrix
A
is of finite type (see Subsection
2.1
), then the group

G({\mathbb{Q}})=G^{\lambda}({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t),\ \chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\rangle

is a semi-simple linear algebraic group. Furthermore, if the set of weights of
V=V^{\lambda}
contains all the fundamental weights, then
G({\mathbb{Q}})
coincides with the simply connected Chevalley group
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
of type
A
.

Our methods give a proof of integrality of unipotent subgroups of simply connected Chevalley groups
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})\cong G({\mathbb{Q}})
, using only the action of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
on
V
. As a corollary of Theorem
1.2
, we have the following (Subsection
7.1
).

Corollary 1.3
.

Suppose that the matrix
A
has finite type. Suppose also that the set of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. Then the positive unipotent subgroup
U
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is integral.

Theorem
1.2
also yields integrality of other subgroups of
U
.
In Subsection
7.2
we prove:

Theorem 1.4
.

If
M
is a subgroup of
U
generated by commuting real root subgroups, then

M
is integral.

In Section
7.3
, we consider the case where
G
has rank 2. In this case
U=U_{1}{\Large{*}}\,U_{2}
where each
U_{i}
is generated by ‘half’ the positive real roots (see
[
CKMS21
]
).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G({\mathbb{Q}})
be of rank 2 with positive unipotent subgroup
U=U_{1}{\Large{*}}\,U_{2}
where
U_{i}
are abelian if
A
is symmetric, and nilpotent of class 2 if
A
is not symmetric. Then the groups
U_{i}
are integral.

Though our methods do not currently extend to a proof of integrality for
U
,
we conjecture
1

U
.
It would then be straightforward to show that
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})

2.
Kac–Moody Algebras

In this section, we review some facts about Kac-Moody algebras and their representations.

2.1.
Generalized Cartan matrices

Let
I=\{1,2,\dots,\ell\}
and let
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
be a
generalized Cartan matrix
. That is,

\displaystyle a_{ij}

\displaystyle\in{{\mathbb{Z}}};

\displaystyle a_{ii}

\displaystyle=2;

\displaystyle a_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq 0,\text{ for }i\neq j;\text{ and}

\displaystyle a_{ij}

\displaystyle=0\iff a_{ji}=0,

for all
i,j\in I
.
The matrix
A
is
symmetrizable
if there exist positive rational numbers
q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}
, such that
the matrix
\operatorname{diag}(q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell})A
is symmetric.
We say that
A
is of
finite type
if
A
is positive definite, and that
A
is of
affine type
if
A
is positive semidefinite but not positive definite. If
A
is not of finite or affine type, we say that
A
has
indefinite type
.
In particular,
A
is of
hyperbolic type
if
A
is neither of finite nor affine type, but every proper, indecomposable submatrix is either of finite or of affine type.

We assume throughout that
A
is a symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix.

2.2.
Generators and relations for
\mathfrak{g}

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space of dimension
2\ell-\rank(A)
, and let
\langle\circ,\circ\rangle:\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}\times\mathfrak{h}\to{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the natural nondegenerate bilinear pairing between
\mathfrak{h}
and its dual.
Fix
simple roots

\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
and
simple coroots

\Pi^{\vee}=\{\alpha_{1}^{\vee},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}^{\vee}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
such that
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\vee}
are linearly independent, and
\langle\alpha_{j},\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle=\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{i}^{\vee})=a_{ij}
.

As in
[
Moo67
]
,
[
Moo68
]
, and
[
Kac90
, Theorem 9.11]
, the associated
Kac–Moody algebra

\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with generating set

\mathfrak{h}\cup\{e_{i},f_{i}\mid i\in I\}
and defining relations:

\displaystyle[h,h^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle[h,e_{i}]

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle e_{i};

\displaystyle[h,f_{i}]

\displaystyle=-\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle f_{i};

\displaystyle[e_{i},f_{i}]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}^{\vee};

\displaystyle[e_{i},f_{j}]

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle(\operatorname{ad}e_{i})^{-a_{ij}+1}(e_{j})

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle(\operatorname{ad}f_{i})^{-a_{ij}+1}(f_{j})

\displaystyle=0;

for
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
, and
i,j\in I
with
i\neq j
.

2.3.
Roots and the Weyl group

The
roots
of
\mathfrak{g}
are the nonzero

\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
for which the corresponding root space

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for all $h\in\mathfrak{h}$}\}

is nontrivial. The simple roots
\alpha_{i}
have root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}={\mathbb{Q}}e_{i}
.
Every root
\alpha
can be written in the form
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}k_{i}\alpha_{i}
where the
k_{i}
are integers, with either all
k_{i}\geq 0
, in which case
\alpha
is called
positive
, or all
k_{i}\leq 0
, in which case
\alpha
is called
negative
. We denote the set of roots by
\Delta
and the set of positive (resp. negative) roots by
\Delta_{+}
(resp.
\Delta_{-}
).
The
root lattice
is
Q:=\mathbb{Z}\Pi\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and the
coroot lattice
is
Q^{\vee}:=\mathbb{Z}\Pi^{\vee}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has a triangular decomposition (
[
Kac90
, Theorem 1.2]
)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+},

where

\mathfrak{n}^{+}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{n}^{-}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta_{-}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

Since
A
is symmetrizable,
\mathfrak{g}
admits a nondegenerate symmetric invariant bilinear form
(\circ,\circ)
satisfying

\displaystyle\left(\alpha^{\vee}_{i},\alpha^{\vee}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{ij},

\displaystyle\left({e}_{i},{f}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{n}_{+}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{n}_{-}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

We define the
simple reflection

w_{i}:\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}\to\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
by

w_{i}(v)=v-\langle v,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle\alpha_{i}.

The group
W\subseteq\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{h}^{\ast})
generated by the simple reflections is called the
Weyl group
. The induced bilinear form on
{\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}}
is
W
-invariant.
The group
W
comes equipped with a length function

\ell\colon W\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

defined as
\ell(w)=k
, where
k
is the smallest number such that
w
is the product of
k
simple root reflections.
Such a minimal length word

w=w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k}}

is called a
reduced word
.

A root
\alpha\in\Delta
is called
real
if there exists
w\in W
such that
w\alpha
is a
simple root. A root
\alpha
which is not real is called

imaginary
.
We denote by
\Delta^{\re}
the set of real roots and
\Delta^{\im}
the
set of imaginary roots. We have
\Delta^{\re}=W\Pi
. Similarly define sets
\Delta^{\re}_{\pm}:=\Delta^{\re}\cap\Delta_{\pm}

and
\Delta^{\im}_{\pm}:=\Delta^{\im}\cap\Delta_{\pm}
.
For
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
, the reflection in
\alpha
is defined by

w_{\alpha}(v)=v-\langle v,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\alpha,

where
\alpha^{\vee}
is the usual coroot of
\alpha
defined using the bilinear form
(\circ,\circ)
on
\mathfrak{h}
.
Since
w\alpha=\alpha_{i}
for some
w\in W
,
i\in I
, we have
w_{\alpha}=ww_{i}w^{-1}\in W
.

2.4.
Integrable highest weight modules

The
weight lattice

P\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is the dual lattice of
Q^{\vee}
, that is,

P=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}\mid\langle\lambda,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ i\in I\}.

An element
\lambda\in P
is
dominant
if
\langle\lambda,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle\geq 0
for all
i\in I
, and is
regular
if
\langle\lambda,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle\neq 0
for all
i\in I
. We denote the set of dominant elements of
P

by
P_{+}
and write
Q_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\alpha_{i}
.
The
dominance order
on
\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is defined by
\lambda<\mu
if
\mu-\lambda\in Q_{+}
.

Let
V
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module with defining homomorphism

\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\End(V)
.
Then the
weight space
of
\mu\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
is

V_{\mu}:=\{v\in V\mid\rho(h)(v)=\mu(h)v\text{ for all $h\in\mathfrak{h}$}\},

and the set of
weights
of
V
is

\wts(V)=\left\{\mu\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}\mid V_{\mu}\neq 0\right\}.

Recall also that
x\in\mathfrak{g}
acts
locally nilpotently
on
V
if, for each
v\in V
, there is a natural number
n
such that

\rho(x)^{n}(v)=0
.
Then
V
is called an
integrable
module if
it is a direct sum of its weight spaces, that is

V=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\wts(V)}V_{\mu}
,
and each generator

e_{i}
or
f_{i}
acts locally nilpotently on
V
.

We call
\lambda\in\wts(V)
the
highest weight
of
V
if it is largest element of
\wts(V)
in the dominance order.
If
V
has a highest weight we call it a
highest weight module
.
If
\mu
is a weight of a highest weight module
V
, then
\mu=\lambda-\beta
for some
\beta\in Q_{+}
.
Among all modules with highest weight
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
, there is a unique irreducible
\mathfrak{g}
–module (Prop. 9.3,
[
Kac90
]
), which we denote by
V^{\lambda}
.
The module
V^{\lambda}
is integrable if and only if
\lambda\in P_{+}
, that is,
\lambda
is a dominant weight (Lemma 10.1,
[
Kac90
]
). In this case, we get
\wts(V^{\lambda})\subseteq P
.

From now on, we take
V=V^{\lambda}
to be an integrable highest weight
\mathfrak{g}
-module over
\mathbb{Q}
for a fixed dominant, regular weight
\lambda
and a fixed highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
. For
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
v\in V
, we denote the action of
x
on
v
by
x\cdot v:=\rho(x)(v)
.

3.
Representation-theoretic Kac–Moody groups

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Kac–Moody algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix
A
.
Let
V=V^{\lambda}
to be an integrable highest weight
\mathfrak{g}
-module over
\mathbb{Q}
for a fixed dominant, regular weight
\lambda
and let
v_{\lambda}
be a fixed highest weight vector .

The Chevalley involution
\omega
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
with
\omega^{2}=1
,
\omega(h)=-h
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}
, and
\omega(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\Delta
.

3.1.
{\mathbb{Z}}
–forms

Let
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathfrak{g})
be the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Let

•

{\mathcal{U}}^{+}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subalgebra generated by

\dfrac{{e_{i}}^{m}}{m!}
for
i\in I

and
m\geq 0
;

•

{\mathcal{U}}^{-}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subalgebra generated by

\dfrac{{f_{i}}^{m}}{m!}
for
i\in I

and
m\geq 0
; and

•

{\mathcal{U}}^{0}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subalgebra generated by

\left(\begin{matrix}h\\
m\end{matrix}\right):=\dfrac{h(h-1)\dots(h-m+1)}{m!},

for

h\in Q^{\vee}
and
m\geq 0
.

As in
[
CG03
]
and
[
Tit81
]
,
[
Tit87
]
, the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-form of
\mathcal{U}
is
{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subalgebra generated by
{\mathcal{U}}^{+}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
{\mathcal{U}}^{-}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, and
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
By
[
Tit87
]
, we have
\mathcal{U}^{-}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{0}\otimes\mathcal{U}^{+}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cong\mathcal{U}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

We define

\mathfrak{g}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\ =\ \mathfrak{g}\cap{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{h}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\ =\ \mathfrak{h}\cap{\mathcal{U}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}.

Note that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Fix the highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
in
V
.
We have

\mathcal{U}^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\mathbb{Z}v_{\lambda}

since every
x_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}_{+}
annihilates
v_{\lambda}
. Also

\mathcal{U}^{0}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\mathbb{Z}v_{\lambda}

since
{\mathcal{U}}^{0}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
acts on
v_{\lambda}
as scalar multiplication by an integer. Therefore

\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\mathcal{U}^{-}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}.

We define the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-form of
V
to be

V_{\mathbb{Z}}:=\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\mathcal{U}^{-}_{\mathbb{Z}}\cdot v_{\lambda}

and note that

V=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}.

For
\mu\in\wts(V)
, set
V_{\mu,\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mu}\cap V_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

3.2.
The groups
G({\mathbb{Q}})
and
G({\mathbb{Z}})

For
i\in I
and
t\in\mathbb{Q}
set

\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)=\exp(t\rho(e_{i}))=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\frac{t^{m}\rho\left(e_{i}\right)^{m}}{m!},\quad\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)=\exp(t\rho(f_{i}))=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\frac{t^{m}\rho\left(f_{i}\right)^{m}}{m!},

where
\rho
is the defining homomorphism for
V=V^{\lambda}
.
Since
V
is integrable, the
\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)
are elements of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
.
We let
G(\mathbb{Q})\leq\operatorname{GL}(V)
be the group generated by
\chi_{\pm\alpha_{i}}(t)
for
i\in I
and
t\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We refer to
G({\mathbb{Q}})=G^{\lambda}(\mathbb{Q})
as a
minimal representation-theoretic Kac–Moody group
.
Similarly we define
G(\mathbb{Z})
as the subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
generated by
\chi_{\pm\alpha_{i}}(t)
for
i\in I
and
t\in\mathbb{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
We denote the action of
g\in G
on
v\in V
by
g\cdot v
.

For each
i\in I
and
t\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, we define

\widetilde{w}_{i}(t)=\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(-t^{-1})\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)

and set
\widetilde{w}_{i}=\widetilde{w}_{i}(1)
.
Next set

h_{\alpha_{i}}(t)\ =\ \widetilde{w}_{i}(t)\widetilde{w}_{i}(1)^{-1}

for
t\in Q^{\times}
and
i\in I
, and let
H\subseteq G
be the subgroup generated by the
h_{\alpha_{i}}(t)
.
Let
N
denote the subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
generated by
H
and the
\widetilde{w}_{i}
. By Lemma 4 in Section 5.4 of
[
Tit87
]
, there exists a unique endomorphism

N\longrightarrow W

with kernel
H
.
For
w\in W
, take a reduced word
w=w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k}}
and define

\widetilde{w}:=\widetilde{w}_{i_{1}}\widetilde{w}_{i_{2}}\cdots\widetilde{w}_{i_{k}}.

Now
\widetilde{w}_{i}\in G(\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
and so

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}

for all
w\in W
.

Note that the adjoint representation makes
\mathfrak{g}
into a
G
-module and we also get
\text{Ad}(\widetilde{w})(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}})=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}
as in
[
MP95
]
.
Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}^{\re}
, then
\alpha=w\alpha_{i}
for some
w\in W
and
i\in I
.
We define the
root vectors
(
[
MP95
]
, Section 4.1) by

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}:=\text{Ad}(\widetilde{w})(e_{i})\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{-\alpha}:=\text{Ad}(\widetilde{w})(f_{i})\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}},

so that
x_{-\alpha}=-\omega(x_{\alpha})
and
[x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha}]=\alpha^{\vee}
.
In particular,
x_{\alpha_{i}}=e_{i}
and
x_{-\alpha_{i}}=f_{i}
. The set
\{x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha},\ \alpha^{\vee}\}

forms a basis for a subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
w\in W
,
\mu\in\wts(V)
,
\alpha\in\Delta_{+}^{\re}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

(a)

\widetilde{w}\cdot V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}=V_{w\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

(b)

V_{w\lambda,{\mathbb{Z}}}=\widetilde{w}\cdot V_{\lambda,{\mathbb{Z}}}={\mathbb{Z}}v_{w\lambda}
where
v_{w\lambda}=\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}
.

(c)

\dfrac{{x_{\pm\alpha}}^{m}}{m!}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\dfrac{{x_{\pm\alpha}}^{m}}{m!}\cdot V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq V_{\mu\pm m\alpha,{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Proof.

Lemma 3.8(a) in
[
Kac90
]
implies
\widetilde{w_{i}}\cdot V_{\mu}=V_{w_{i}\mu}
for
i\in I
, and so

\widetilde{w}\cdot V_{\mu}=V_{w\mu}
by induction.
Combining this with
\widetilde{w}\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
V=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\wts(V)}V_{\mu}
we get (a).
Parts (b) and (c) are now straightforward.
∎

For
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, set

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(t):=\exp(t\rho(x_{\alpha}))=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}\frac{t^{m}\rho\left(x_{\alpha}\right)^{m}}{m!}\in\operatorname{GL}(V).

We have

\chi_{\alpha}(t)=\widetilde{w}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)\widetilde{w}^{-1}\in G
.
We define the (real)
root group
and its
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subgroup as

U_{\alpha}=\{\chi_{\alpha}(t)\mid t\in\mathbb{Q}\}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{\alpha}(\mathbb{Z})=\{\chi_{\alpha}(t)\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Given a set of positive real roots
\Omega\subseteq\Delta_{+}^{\re}
, we define

U_{\Omega}=\langle U_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Omega\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad U_{\Omega}({\mathbb{Z}})=\langle U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}})\mid\alpha\in\Omega\rangle.

In particular, the
unipotent subgroup
is
U:=U_{\Delta_{+}^{\re}}
and its
{\mathbb{Z}}
-subgroup is

U({\mathbb{Z}}):=U_{\Delta_{+}^{\re}}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
It is clear that
U_{\Omega}({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq U_{\Omega}\cap G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
The reverse inclusion is conjectured to be true for reasonable choices of
\Omega
but will not be needed for the results in this paper.

We end this section with the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
u\in U
,
u\cdot v_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove this statement for a generator
\chi_{\alpha}(t)
of
U_{\alpha}\subseteq U
. Now

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(t)\cdot v_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}+tx_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\lambda}+t^{2}\,\frac{{x_{\alpha}}^{2}}{2!}\cdot v_{\lambda}+\dots+t^{m}\,\frac{{x_{\alpha}}^{m}}{m!}\cdot v_{\lambda},

for some
m
since
x_{\alpha}
acts nilpotently.
But
{x_{\alpha}}^{i}\cdot v_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda+i\alpha}=0
, since
\lambda+i\alpha
is not a weight of
V
for
i>0
. Thus,

\chi_{\alpha}(t)\cdot v_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}.

∎

4.
Strong integrality of real root groups

Throughout this section, fix
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
.
The main result of this section is to prove strong integrality of the real root group

U_{\alpha}=\{\chi_{\alpha}(t)\mid t\in\mathbb{Q}\}.

This provides the base case for Theorem
1.2
.
Integrality of
U_{\alpha}
will follow from the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Strong integrality implies integrality.

Proof.

Let
M\subseteq G
be strongly integral with respect to
v_{0}\in V^{\lambda}
.
Let
g\in M\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
. Then
g\cdot v\in V^{\lambda}_{\mathbb{Z}}
for all
v\in V^{\lambda}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. In particular
g\cdot v_{0}\in V^{\lambda}_{\mathbb{Z}}
and so
g\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
by strong integrality.
∎

Define

x_{\alpha}^{[m]}:=\frac{{x_{\alpha}}^{m}}{m!}.

The following technical lemma is essential to our integrality results:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a weight such that
\mu+\alpha
is not a weight of
V
.
Further assume that
v_{\mu}\in V_{\mu}
and
n:=\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle>0
.
Then

x_{\alpha}^{[n]}x_{-\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu}=v_{\mu}.

Proof.

First we prove that

[x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}]=k{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}\left(\alpha^{\vee}-(k-1)\right)

by induction on
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle[x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}]

\displaystyle=x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}-{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}-{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}+{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}-{x_{-\alpha}}^{k}x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=[x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}]{x_{-\alpha}}+{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}[x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}]

\displaystyle=(k-1){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-2}\left(\alpha^{\vee}-k+2\right){x_{-\alpha}}+{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}\alpha^{\vee}

\displaystyle=(k-1){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-2}(\alpha^{\vee}x_{-\alpha})-(k-1)(k-2){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}+{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}m\alpha^{\vee}

\displaystyle=(k-1){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-2}(x_{-\alpha}\alpha^{\vee}-2x_{-\alpha})-(k-1)(k-2){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}+{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}\alpha^{\vee}

\displaystyle=k{x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}\alpha^{\vee}-k(k-1){x_{-\alpha}}^{k-1}.

Note that
x_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\mu}\in V_{\mu+\alpha}=0
, so
x_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\mu}=0
.
Also
\alpha^{\vee}\cdot v_{\mu}=\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle v_{\mu}=nv_{\mu}
.
Next we prove

\displaystyle{x_{\alpha}}^{m}{x_{-\alpha}}^{m}\cdot v_{\mu}=m!\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(n-j)\cdot v_{\mu}.

by induction on
m
:

\displaystyle{x_{\alpha}}^{m}{x_{-\alpha}}^{m}\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle={x_{\alpha}}^{m-1}\left([x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}^{m}]+{x_{-\alpha}}^{m}x_{\alpha}\right)\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle={x_{\alpha}}^{m-1}[x_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}^{m}]\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle={x_{\alpha}}^{m-1}{x_{-\alpha}}^{m-1}m\left(\alpha^{\vee}-(m-1)\right)\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=m\left(n-(m-1)\right){x_{\alpha}}^{m-1}{x_{-\alpha}}^{m-1}\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=m!\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(n-j)\cdot v_{\mu}.

Now take
m=n
to get

{x_{\alpha}}^{n}{x_{-\alpha}}^{n}\cdot v_{\mu}=n!\,n!\,v_{\mu}
, so
x_{\alpha}^{[n]}x_{-\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu}=v_{\mu}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Lemma 7.2 of
[
Ste67
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
and
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}
.
If
\mu
is a weight of
V
and
v\in V_{\mu}
, then

\chi_{\alpha}(t)\cdot v=v+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}t^{m}(x_{\alpha}^{[m]}\cdot v),

where
x_{\alpha}^{[m]}\cdot v\in V_{\mu+m\alpha}
, and only finitely many of the terms
x_{\alpha}^{[m]}\cdot v
are non-zero.
If moreover
v\in V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}
, then
x_{\alpha}^{[m]}\cdot v\in V_{\mu+m\alpha,{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Proof.

If
v\in V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
then
x_{\alpha}^{[m]}\cdot v\in V_{\mu+m\alpha,{\mathbb{Z}}}
by Lemma
3.1
(
c
).
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
.
If
\chi_{\alpha}(t)\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\lambda}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
\chi_{\alpha}(t)\in U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be positive. Suppose
\chi_{\alpha}(t)\cdot v_{0}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
where
v_{0}:=\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\lambda}
.
Let
n=\langle\lambda,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle
so that
w_{\alpha}\lambda=\lambda-n\alpha
.
Note that
n>0
since
\lambda
is regular.
Now
V_{\lambda-n\alpha,{\mathbb{Z}}}={\mathbb{Z}}v_{0}
by Lemma
3.1
(
b
).
But
x_{-\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\lambda}
is also in
V_{\lambda-n\alpha,{\mathbb{Z}}}

by Lemma
3.1
(
c
), so
x_{-\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\lambda}=mv_{0}

for some integer
m
.
We have

\chi_{\alpha}(t)\cdot v_{0}=v_{0}+tx_{\alpha}\cdot v_{0}+t^{2}x_{\alpha}^{[2]}\cdot v_{0}+\dots+t^{n}x_{\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{0}.

Now consider the
V_{\lambda,{\mathbb{Z}}}
component:

t^{n}x_{\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{0}=\frac{t^{n}}{m}x_{\alpha}^{[n]}x_{-\alpha}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\frac{t^{n}}{m}v_{\lambda}

by Lemma
4.2
.
Hence

\frac{t^{n}}{m}
is an integer, so
t^{n}
is an integer, and so
t
is an integer.
The proof for negative
\alpha
is similar.
∎

Since
U_{\alpha}({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq U_{\alpha}\cap G({\mathbb{Z}})
, we get:

Corollary 4.5
.

If
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
, then
U_{\alpha}

is strongly integral with respect to
\widetilde{w}_{\alpha}\cdot v_{\lambda}
.
Hence
U_{\alpha}
is integral.

It follows immediately that for
\alpha\in\Delta^{\re}
and
u\in U({\mathbb{Z}})
, if
\chi_{\alpha}(s)u\in\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
then
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

:

Lemma 5.2
.

(a)

.

We equip

with an ordering

\alpha_{i_{1}}\prec w_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}}\prec w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\alpha_{i_{3}}\prec\cdots\prec w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k-2}}w_{i_{k-1}}\alpha_{k},

as described by Papi in
[

6.
Integrality of inversion subgroups

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we need a preliminary result, which we discuss next.
Define

x_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}^{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}:=x_{\beta_{1}}^{[i_{1}]}x_{\beta_{2}}^{[i_{2}]}\cdots x_{\beta_{k}}^{[i_{k}]}.

The proof of the following is similar to Lemma
4.3
:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\beta_{i}\in\Delta^{\re}_{+}
and
t_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
If
\mu
is a weight of
V
and
v\in V_{\mu}
, then

{\prod}_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{\beta_{i}}(t_{i})\cdot v={{\sum}}_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}t_{1}^{i_{1}}t_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{i_{k}}\left(x_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}^{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}\cdot v\right)

where
x_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}^{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}\cdot v\in V_{\mu+i_{1}\beta_{1}+i_{2}\beta_{2}+i_{k}\beta_{k}}
and only finitely of the terms
x_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}^{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}\cdot v
are non-zero.
If moreover
v\in V_{\mu,\mathbb{Z}}
, then
x_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}^{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}\cdot v\in V_{\mu+i_{1}\beta_{1}+i_{2}\beta_{2}+i_{k}\beta_{k},\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
w\in W
and

u_{(w)}\in U_{(w)}
. If
u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
u_{(w)}\in U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Theorem
1.2
follows immediately from this result since
U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq U_{(w)}\cap G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k=\ell(w)
. The base case
\ell(w)=1
is just Proposition
4.4

for a simple root
\alpha_{i}
.
So assume
\ell(w)\geq 2
and the inductive hypothesis.
Let
\Phi_{(w)}=\{\beta_{1},\dots\beta_{k}\}
in the usual order.
So
\beta:=\beta_{1}
is a simple root and
w=w_{\beta}w^{\prime\prime}
with
\ell(w^{\prime\prime})=k-1
,
and
\Phi_{(w^{\prime\prime})}=\{w_{\beta}\beta_{2},\dots,w_{\beta}\beta_{k}\}
by Lemma
5.1
(
d
).
In particular,
w_{\beta}\beta_{2},\dots,w_{\beta}\beta_{k}
are positive roots.
Write

u_{(w)}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{\beta_{i}}(t_{i})=\chi_{\beta}(t)\prod_{i=2}^{k}\chi_{\beta_{i}}(t_{i}),

for
t=t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{k}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Write
\mu=w^{\prime\prime}\lambda
and
v_{\mu}=\widetilde{w}^{\prime\prime}\cdot v_{\lambda}
.
Let
n=\langle\mu,\beta^{\vee}\rangle
, so that
w\lambda=w_{\beta}\mu=\mu-n\beta
.
Let
v_{\mu-n\beta}=\widetilde{w}_{\beta}\cdot v_{\mu}=\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}
.
Then

\displaystyle u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=u_{(w)}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}={{\sum}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\end{subarray}}t_{1}^{i_{1}}t_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{i_{k}}\left(x^{[i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}_{\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=t^{n}x_{\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}+{{\sum}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq n},\;i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\\
\text{at least one non-zero}\end{subarray}}t^{n-i}t_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{i_{k}}\left(x^{[n-i,i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}_{\beta,\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}\right),

where we have substituted
i=n-i_{1}
and extracted the term with
i,i_{2},\dots,i_{k}=0
from the sum.
Now
t^{n}x_{\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}\in V_{\mu}
and
x^{[n-i,i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]}_{\beta,\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k}}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}\in V_{\nu}
for
\nu=\mu-i\beta+\sum_{j=2}^{k}i_{j}\beta_{j}
.
Now

\displaystyle(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\lambda-(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(i\beta)+(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{k}i_{j}\beta_{j}\right)\geq\lambda-i(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(\beta),

but
(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(\beta)>0
, so we must have
i\geq 0
for
\nu
to be a weight of
V
.
So we can take
i
to be in the range
0\leq i\leq n
.
If
\mu=\nu
, then
i\beta=\sum_{j=2}^{k}i_{j}\beta_{j}
,
but
w_{\beta}\left(i_{j}\beta_{j}\right)\geq 0
and
w_{\beta}(i\beta)\leq 0
, so
i,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=0
.
Hence the
V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}
component of
u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
is just

t^{n}x_{\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}
.

Now
V_{\mu,{\mathbb{Z}}}={\mathbb{Z}}v_{\mu}
, so

t^{n}x_{\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}=mv_{\mu}

for some integer
m
.
Also

x_{-\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu}\in V_{\mu-n\beta,{\mathbb{Z}}}={\mathbb{Z}}v_{\mu-n\beta},

so

x_{-\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu}=m^{\prime}v_{\mu-n\beta}
for some integer
m^{\prime}
.
Hence

mm^{\prime}v_{\mu-n\beta}=mx_{-\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu}=t^{n}x_{-\beta}^{[n]}x_{\beta}^{[n]}\cdot v_{\mu-n\beta}=t^{n}v_{\mu-n\beta}

by Lemma
4.2
for
\alpha=-\beta
, since
\mu-n\beta-\beta\notin\wts(V)
as

w^{-1}(\mu-n\beta-\beta)=w^{-1}(w\lambda-\beta)=\lambda-w^{-1}\beta>\lambda
.
Hence
t^{n}=mm^{\prime}
is an integer, so
t
is an integer.

Now

u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}=\chi_{\beta}(t)\prod_{i=2}^{k}\chi_{\beta_{i}}(t_{i})\,\widetilde{w}_{\beta}\widetilde{w}^{\prime\prime}=\chi_{\beta}(t)\widetilde{w}_{\beta}u_{(w^{\prime\prime})}\widetilde{w}^{\prime\prime},

where

u_{(w^{\prime\prime})}=\prod_{i=2}^{k}\chi_{w_{\beta}\beta_{i}}(t_{i})\in U_{(w^{\prime\prime})}
.
Now
\chi_{\beta}(t)\widetilde{w}_{\beta}\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
, so

u_{(w^{\prime\prime})}\widetilde{w}^{\prime\prime}\cdot v_{\lambda}=\left(\chi_{\beta}(t)\widetilde{w}_{\beta}\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}\right)\in V_{\mathbb{Z}},

and so
u_{(w^{\prime\prime})}\in U_{(w^{\prime\prime})}({\mathbb{Z}})
by induction. Hence
u_{(w)}=\chi_{\beta}(t)\widetilde{w}_{\beta}u_{(w^{\prime\prime})}\widetilde{w}_{\beta}^{-1}\in U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
∎

7.
Further integrality results

7.1.
Integrality of finite dimensional unipotent groups

For this section, we assume that
A
has finite type. In this case,
A
is a Cartan matrix and
\mathfrak{g}
is a finite dimensional semi-simple Lie algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The group construction in Section
3
can be carried out with the same external data: a highest weight representation
V
with highest weight
\lambda
, a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-form of the universal enveloping algebra (constructed by Cartier and Kostant, see
[
Kos66
]
) and a lattice
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
in
V
(called an
admissible lattice
in
[
Che95
]
).

The group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is then a semi-simple algebraic group and
G({\mathbb{Q}})\cong G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
, where
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
is Chevalley’s group scheme, now known as the Chevalley–Demazure group scheme (
[
Che05
]
).

Our construction of the group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
does not explicitly involve a lattice
L
between the root lattice and the weight lattice, as in Chevalley’s construction. With the assumption that the set of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights, our group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
coincides with Chevalley’s simply connected group
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
.

As a corollary of Theorem
1.2
, we have the following.

Corollary 7.1
.

Suppose that the matrix
A
has finite type. Suppose also that the set of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. Then the unipotent subgroup
U
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is integral.

Proof.

Since
A
has finite type, the Weyl group
W
is finite. Moreover,
W
contains the longest element, denoted
w_{0}
, which flips all positive roots to negative roots. That is
\Phi_{w_{0}}=\Delta^{+}
, which gives
U_{w_{0}}=U
. Combining this with Theorem
1.2
and Lemma
4.1
,
it follows that
U
is integral.
∎

7.2.
Integrality of groups generated by commutating real root subgroups

We can now prove the result from Section
1
that gives integrality of subgroups of
U
generated by commuting real root groups.

Theorem
1.4
.

Let
U
be the positive unipotent subgroup of
G=G^{\lambda}({\mathbb{Q}})
.
If
M
is a subgroup of
U
generated by commuting real root subgroups, then

M
is integral.

Proof.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that

\displaystyle u=\chi_{\beta_{1}}(t_{\beta_{1}})\chi_{\beta_{2}}(t_{\beta_{2}})\cdots\chi_{\beta_{N}}(t_{\beta_{N}})\in M

is the shortest word such that

u\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}
but
u\notin U({\mathbb{Z}})
.
By Corollary
4.5
, we must have
N>1
.
Now
\Omega:=\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{N}\}\subseteq\Delta^{\re}_{+}
is a finite set of real roots whose corresponding root subgroups commute with each other.
By part (b) of Lemma
5.2
, there exists
w\in W
such that
\Omega\cap\Phi_{(w)}\neq\emptyset
.
Using the commutating of the real root groups
U_{\beta_{j}}
, we can rearrange the product above as

u=u_{(w)}u^{(w)}=u^{(w)}u_{(w)},

where
u_{(w)}=\prod_{\beta\in\Omega\cap\Phi_{(w)}}\chi_{\beta}(t_{\beta})
and

u^{(w)}=\prod_{\beta\in\Omega\setminus\Phi_{(w)}}\chi_{\beta}(t_{\beta})
.

Now we have

\displaystyle u\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}=u_{(w)}u^{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}=u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot(\widetilde{w}^{-1}u^{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}).

But
\widetilde{w}^{-1}u^{(w)}\widetilde{w}\in U
, so

\widetilde{w}^{-1}u^{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}=v_{\lambda}

by Lemma
3.2
.
So
u_{(w)}\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}=u\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}\in V_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
But
U_{(w)}
is strongly integral with respect to
\widetilde{w}\cdot v_{\lambda}

by Theorem
1.2
, so
u_{(w)}\in U(\mathbb{Z})
.

Hence
{u_{(w)}}^{-1}\in U(\mathbb{Z})
and so

u^{(w)}\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=u\cdot({u_{(w)}}^{-1}\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}})=u\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}.

Since
\Omega\cap\Phi_{(w)}
is nonempty,
u^{(w)}=\prod_{\beta\in\Omega\setminus\Phi_{(w)}}\chi_{\beta}(t_{\beta})
is a shorter word than
u
and so our assumption implies
u^{(w)}\in U(\mathbb{Z})
.
But now

u=u_{(w)}u^{(w)}\in U(\mathbb{Z})
, which is a contradiction.
∎

7.3.
Integrality of subgroups of
U
in the rank
2
Kac–Moody case

We start with a method for constructing infinite-dimensional integral unipotent subgroups.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{n}\subseteq\Delta_{+}^{\re}
where
\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega_{2}\subseteq\cdots
and
assume that each
U_{\Omega_{n}}
is integral.
Then
U_{\Omega}=\langle U_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Omega\rangle
is integral.

Proof.

Take
u\in U_{\Omega}
with

u\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then

u=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{\beta_{i}}(a_{i})

for some
\beta_{i}\in\Omega
,
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. But now the finite set of roots
\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N}\}
must be contained
in
\Omega_{n}
for some
n
.
Hence
u\in U_{\Omega_{n}}
, which is an integral subgroup. So
u\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
∎

One way to construct such sets is by taking a sequence of simple reflections
w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}},\dots
such that every initial subsequence gives a reduced word
w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{n}}\in W
. Then
each group
U_{(w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{n}})}
is integral by Theorem
1.2
. Now take

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Phi_{(w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{n}})}=\{\alpha_{i_{1}},\;w_{i_{1}}\alpha_{i_{2}},\;w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\alpha_{i_{3}},\;\dots,\;w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k-2}}w_{i_{k-1}}\alpha_{k},\;\dots\}.

Then the subgroup
U_{\Omega}
is integral.

Now suppose that
\mathfrak{g}
has rank 2.
A detailed description of the structure of
\Delta^{\re}
and
U
in rank 2 can be found in
[
CKMS21
]
.
We define

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}:=\{\alpha_{1},\ w_{1}\alpha_{2},\ w_{1}w_{2}\alpha_{1},\;\dots\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega_{2}:=\{\alpha_{2},\ w_{2}\alpha_{1},\ w_{2}w_{1}\alpha_{2},\;\dots\}.

In fact,
\Omega_{1}
(resp.
\Omega_{2}
) is the set of all positive real roots on the lower (resp. upper)
branches of the hyperbolas defined in
[
CKMS21
]
.

In this case
U=U_{1}{\Large{*}}\,U_{2}
, where for
i=1,2
, the groups
U_{i}:=U_{\Omega_{i}}
are abelian if
A
is symmetric, and nilpotent of class 2 if
A
is not symmetric (see
[
CKMS21
]
).

The following theorem follows immediately from Proposition
7.2
.

Theorem
1.5
.

Suppose that
G({\mathbb{Q}})
has rank 2 with
U=U_{1}{\Large{*}}\,U_{2}
, where
U_{i}=U_{\Omega_{i}}
. Then the groups
U_{i}
are integral.
