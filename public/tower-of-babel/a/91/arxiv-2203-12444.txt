Volume Approximation of Strongly ℂ {\mathbb{C}} -Convex Domains by Random Polyhedra
Siva Athreya, Purvi Gupta, D. Yogeshwaran

Abstract
. Polyhedral-type approximations of convex-like domains in ℂ d {\mathbb{C}}^{d} have been considered recently by the second author. In particular, the decay rate of the error in optimal volume approximation as a function of the number of facets has been obtained. In this article, we take these studies further by investigating polyhedra constructed using random points (Poisson or binomial process) on the boundary of a strongly ℂ {\mathbb{C}} -convex domain. We determine the rate of error in volume approximation of the domain by random polyhedra, and conjecture the precise value of the minimal limiting constant. Analogous to the real case, the exponent appearing in the error rate of random volume approximation coincides with that of optimal volume approximation, and can be interpreted in terms of the Hausdorff dimension of a naturally-occurring metric space. Moreover, the limiting constant is conjectured to depend on the Möbius-Fefferman measure , which is a complex analogue of the Blaschke surface area measure. Finally, we also prove L 1 L^{1} -convergence, variance bounds, and normal approximation.

1.
Introduction

Approximation of convex bodies by (convex) polyhedra is an important topic in convex geometry; see
Gruber 1993a
and
Bronstein 2008
. Motivated by both theoretical and practical considerations, this has led to numerous studies of approximation of convex bodies by random polyhedra; see
Reitzner 2010
and
Hug 2013
. A fascinating feature of such studies is that for both deterministic and random approximations, the asymptotic rates and leading constants are rather well understood. For instance, the least possible volume discrepancy between a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the convex hull of
n
points on its boundary decays like
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{R}}(K)n^{-2/(d-1)}
, where the limiting constant
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
is given in terms of the Blaschke affine surface area measure of
K
. This was shown by
McClure and Vitale 1975
for
d=2
, and by
Gruber 1993b
for all
d
. On the other hand, the least possible volume discrepancy between
K
and the convex hull of
n
i.i.d. random points chosen according to a continuous density on its boundary decays like
v^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}(K)n^{-2/(d-1)}
, where
v^{*}_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
differs from
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
only by a dimensional constant. Here, “least” is to be understood as over all possible continuous densities on the bounday. This was proven by
Schneider 1988
in the case of
d=2
,
Müller 1990
for balls in all dimensions, and by
Schütt and Werner 2000
and
Reitzner 2002
for general convex domains (under different regularity assumptions). Similar behaviour has also been observed for circumscribed polytopes in
[
14
]
and random circumscribed polytopes in
Böröczky Jr. and Reitzner 2004
.

While there are several notions of convexity in several complex variables, the asymptotic analysis of polyhedral approximations in the complex setting is rather underexplored. Deterministic volume approximations for two different classes of (convex-like) domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
were investigated by the second author in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
. In both cases, it was either shown or conjectured that for a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the error in volume approximation decays like
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)n^{-1/d}
, where
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)
depends on complex analogues of the Blaschke surface area, i.e., the Fefferman hypersurface measure in the former case, and the Möbius–Fefferman hypersurface measure in the latter case. In this article, we initiate the study of random polyhedral approximations in the complex setting by exploring random analogues of the approximation considered in
[
16
]
. We determine the rate of approximation, and make a conjecture for the limiting constant; see Page
7
for an an overview of the results.

1.1.
Context and Contribution

Convex geometry has had fruitful interactions separately with probability and several complex variables. We envisage that the joint interaction between the three subjects could be equally exciting. Apart from mere mathematical curiosity, the motivation behind considering
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is to enlarge the class of convex-like domains for which approximation problems can be studied. In geometric terms,
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is the natural analogue of classical convexity in complex projective geometry. Although there is a developing literature on random polytopes in non-Euclidean geometry (see, for example,
Besau and Thäle 2020
,
Besau et al. 2020
), our work is fundamentally different in that the underlying notion of convexity itself is non-Euclidean (complex projective).

Strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains.

Recall that for a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, convexity and strong convexity are characterized by the semi-positive definiteness and positive definiteness, respectively, of the second fundamental form of
bK
, the boundary of
K
(viewed as a smooth manifold), at all points of
bK
. Analogous to this, a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
(
strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
), if for all
w\in bD
,
the restriction of the second fundamental form of
bD
to
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
is semi-positive definite (positive definite), where
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
is the maximal complex subspace of the real tangent space of
bD
at
w
when viewed as a subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We refer to
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
as the
complex tangent space
to
bD
at
w
. In this paper, we work with bounded strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. However, for the sake of convenience and illustration, the examples given in Figures
1
,
3
and
4
are of unbounded strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains. The planar case (
d=1
) appears to be simpler since complex tangent spaces reduce to points, so there is no complex geometry to speak of; compare Figure
2
(
d=1
) and Figures
3
and
4
(
d=2
). Thus, the case
d=1
calls for a separate investigation, and we exclusively deal with the case
d>1
in this paper.

{\mathbb{C}}

\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}
are always
{\mathbb{C}}

{\mathbb{C}}

From this point of view, we shortly introduce a class of polyhedral sets that is invariant under the intermediate group of complex affine transformations. In complex analysis,
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity plays a significant role in questions of duality, interpolation, integral representations of holomorphic functions and metric geometry; see
Andersson et al. 2012
,
Barrett 2016
,
Lanzani and Stein 2014
and
Lempert 1984
, for instance.

Induced polyhedra.

In the real setting, given a finite set
\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}
on the boundary of a real convex domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the simplest construction of a convex polyhedron is to take the convex hull
P_{\text{i}}(\varphi)
of the point set
\varphi
, i.e.,
P_{\text{i}}(\varphi)
is the intersection of all the convex sets containing
\varphi
. In the complex setting, since the intersection of two
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex sets need not be
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex, there is no notion of a “
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex hull”; see
[
1
, Section 2.2]
. A more natural construction of polyhedra is suggested by the following construction of circumscribed polyhedra in the real setting. Given a finite set
\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}
on the boundary of a strongly convex domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
denote the the intersection of those
n
half-spaces determined by the tangent planes to
bK
at
w^{1},...,w^{n}
that contain
K
. Analogous to this construction, a class of “complex” polyhedra are defined in
[
16
]
as follows. Given a strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
, a
source set

\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}\subset bD
and a
size function

\boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, let
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
be the union of those connected components of

(1.1)

\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\text{dist}(z,\mathcal{H}_{w^{j}}bD)>\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})\right\},

that intersect
D
, where
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
is the complex tangent space to
bD
at
w
; see Section
2.1
. The set (
1.1
) is
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex
and hence,
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is
weakly
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly convex
; see
[
1
, Section 2.1]
. We call
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
an
induced polyhedron with at most
n
facets
. Though “
D
-induced polyhedra” may be a more precise term, we drop the domain-dependence for convenience. In comparison,
P_{\text{i}}(\varphi)
and
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
have at most
n
vertices and at most
n
facets, respectively. Geometrically, the role of half-spaces is played by complements of ‘tubes’ of radii
\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{\cdot})
with central axes
\mathcal{H}_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}bD
. See Figure
2
for illustrations of induced polyhedra in dimension one. In higher dimensions, induced polyhedra are hard to draw, but see Figures
3
and
4
for illustrations of these ‘tubes’ that generate the polyhedra.

Induced polyhedra are special cases of what are known as
analytic polyhedra
in the complex analysis literature. However, as pointed out in
[
15
,
16
]
, the full class of analytic polyhedra is not suitable for the kind of approximation problems we are interested in. The size function may seem as an artefact of the complex setting since it is absent in the construction of
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
above. However, convex polyhedra with nontrivial size functions have appeared in the real setting; see
Ludwig 1999
and
Glasauer and Schneider 1996
.

Random polyhedra and the metric of approximation.

We wish to discuss a random analogue of the following question investigated in
[
16
]
: given a bounded strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
, determine the asymptotics as
n\rightarrow\infty
of

(1.2)

v_{n}(D):=\inf\{\lambda(D\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})):\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}\subset bD,\ \boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty),\ P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\subset D\},

where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We know from
[
16
]
that

(1.3)

v_{n}(D)\sim v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)n^{-1/d}\qquad\text{as}\ n\to\infty,

for some domain-dependent constant
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)
.

We randomize
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
by taking
\varphi
to be random point sets. Let
\{W^{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
, be i.i.d. points sampled according to a probability density
f(\cdot)
with respect to the surface area measure
\sigma
on
bD
. We either take the random point set to be
\varphi=\eta_{n}:=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{N_{n}}\}
(Poisson process) where
N_{n}
is an independent Poisson(
n
) random variable, or
\varphi=\beta_{n}:=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{n}\}
(binomial process). We shortly comment on the choice of
\boldsymbol{\delta}
.

As with (
1.2
), we use the Lebesgue volume
\lambda
to measure the error of approximation of
D
by
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
. Since we are interested in randomly contained polyhedra but a random
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
(in the sense defined above) may not be contained in
D
, we choose the following metric whereby the polyhedron is penalised if it is not contained in
D
(or is unbounded):

(1.4)

\delta_{V}\big(D,P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\big):=\lambda(D\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}))\mathbf{1}(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\subset D)+\lambda(D)\mathbf{1}(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\subsetneq D).

In the real case, such a penalty is unnecessary for inscribed polyhedra, but is used for randomly circumscribed polyhedra; see
Böröczky Jr. and Reitzner 2004
. An alternative would be the symmetric difference of
D
and
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
, as considered by
Ludwig 1999
,
Ludwig et al. 2006
and
Grote and Werner 2018
in the real case, which is a subject for future investigation.

Overview of the results.

The first step towards understanding the random equivalent of
v_{n}(D)
is to investigate the asymptotics of
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
and
\delta_{V}(D,P(\beta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
for a given sequence of functions
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
. Since we want to study randomly contained polyhedra, we assume that the polyhedra are contained in
D
with high probability, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. For this, it is essential to assume that
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=g(z)(\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/d}
for a function
g:bD\to(0,\infty)
; see Proposition
2.6
. For technical reasons, we assume that
g
is
\alpha
-Hölder for some
0<\alpha<1
.

Our first main result, Theorem
2.3
, is that when
\varphi_{n}
is either
\eta_{n}
(Poisson) or
\beta_{n}
(binomial), and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
is as above,

(1.5)

\Big(\frac{n}{\log n}\Big)^{1/d}\delta_{V}(D,P(\varphi_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\overset{p}{\rightarrow}
denotes convergence in probability. Under further assumptions on the rate of convergence of the containment probabilities
\mathbb{P}(P(\varphi_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)
, we

1.

strengthen
(
1.5
)
to
L^{1}
-convergence (Theorem
2.4
),

2.

obtain variance (upper and lower) bounds for
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
, and variance upper bounds for
\delta_{V}(D,P(\beta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
(Theorem
2.4
), and

3.

bound the rate of normal approxiation of
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
(Theorem
2.5
).

Our variance upper and lower bounds for
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
differ only by logarithmic factors. We are unable to obtain good variance lower bounds for
\delta_{V}(D,P(\beta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
. We prove a normal approximation result for
\delta_{V}(D,P(\beta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
assuming a suitable lower bound on the variance; see Theorem
2.5
.

Optimal random approximations: a conjecture

In problems of approximation, it is of interest to compare the rate of random approximations to that of optimal approximations. Note that if we ignore the domain-dependent constant in (
1.5
), we obtain that the approximation rate is
(n^{-1}\log n)^{1/d}
, which differs from the rate of optimal approximation in (
1.3
) by a logarithmic factor.
This additional factor is necessitated by the need for the containment condition on the underlying random polyhedra. In fact, once the containment condition is translated into a coverage condition on the boundary (Lemma
3.14
), the additional logarithmic factor is no longer surprising in view of coverage results of
Flatto and Newman 1977
,
Hall 1985
and
Janson 1986
(although these do not apply directly to our setting).

Now, we compare the limiting constants in (
1.3
) and (
1.5
). At first glance, it appears that the choice of density
f
is absent in the limiting constant in (
1.5
). But, it is implicit in the condition that
\mathbb{P}(P(\varphi_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. This raises the questions of:
(a)
the best possible limiting constant in (
1.5
), i.e. ,

v_{D}^{*}(f)=\inf\left\{\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z):\mbox{$g$ is continuous and}\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P(\varphi_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)=1\right\}

for a fixed continuous density
f
, and
(b)
the least possible constant,
v_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{*}(D)
, over all such
f
. The proofs of
[
10
,
17
,
19
]
for coverage results in the real setting suggest the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a bounded strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domain, and
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a continuous probability density function. Then,

(1.6)

v^{*}_{D}(f)=(h_{d}\kappa_{2d-2})^{-1/d}\int_{bD}\frac{(16\nu_{D}(z))^{\frac{1}{2d}}}{f(z)^{\frac{1}{d}}}\,d\sigma(z),

where
\nu_{D}
is the complex-restricted curvature of
bD
(see Definition
B.3
),
\kappa_{d}
is the Lebesgue volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
h_{d}=2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{d}\!\theta\>\text{d}\theta
.

Note that the right-hand side of (
1.6
) is minimized when

f=(16\nu_{D})^{\frac{1}{2d+2}}\left(\int_{bD}(16\nu_{D})^{\frac{1}{2d+2}}d\sigma\right)^{-1},

i.e.,
f\text{d}\sigma
is the so-called normalized Möbius-Fefferman measure, which appears in
[
16
]
in relation to
v_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{\sharp}(D)
, and thus
v_{{\mathbb{C}}}^{*}(D)=(h_{d}\kappa_{2d-2})^{-1/d}\left(\int_{bD}(16\nu_{D})^{\frac{1}{2d+2}}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{d+1}{d}}.

This is expected to differ from the constant
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)
in (
1.3
) only by a dimensional constant. See Section
2.3
for a remark on this conjecture.

Comparison with real random polyhedra.

Needless to say, the literature on random real polyhedra is very large owing to the numerous ways of constructing convex polyhedra. Nevertheless, there are some recurring features, within the context of which we place our results.

As noted above, the exponent of
n
in the rate of optimal approximations (
1.3
) and random approximations (
1.5
) is the same, i.e.,
2/2d
. This matching of exponents in the optimal and random rates is seen in the (real) case of approximations by
P_{\text{i}}(\varphi)
and
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
as well, where the exponent is
2/(d-1)
. The quantities
2d
and
d-1
in the exponents
1/d
in the complex setting and
2/(d-1)
in the real setting, respectively, can be unified when interpreted in terms of the Hausdorff dimensions of naturally-occuring metric space in these problems; see
[
15
, Section 1]
. The appearance of the logarithmic factor in (
1.5
) is a point of departure from the case of
P_{\text{i}}(\varphi)
and
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
. However, similar logarithmic factors appear even in the real setting in the case of polyhedral constructions for which containment needs to be imposed; e.g., see
[
11
, Theorems 4 and 5]
. Finally, though the optimal and random limiting constants in the complex setting (
v^{\sharp}_{\mathbb{C}}(D)
and
v^{*}_{D}
) are not as well-understood as in many models of real polyhedra, we have some evidence to believe that they too will only differ by a dimensional constant, as in the real case, e.g., compare the constants in
[
14
]
(best inscribed and circumscribed) and
[
39
,
31
,
6
]
(random inscribed and circumscribed).

So far, we have only placed the convergence in probability result (Theorem
2.3
) into context. Our expectation, variance and normal approximation results also compare well with what is known in the real setting, but we postpone the details to Section
2.3
.

Overview of the proofs.

The two key ideas underpinning our proofs are that the complement of an induced polyehdron in
D
can be expressed as a union of finitely many ‘cuts’ i.e., sets of the form
\left\{z\in D:\text{dist}(z,\mathcal{H}_{w}bD)<\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)\right\}
,
and that a cut is well-approximated by a ‘tubular cut’. The volume of a union of tubular cuts is then esitmated via a Weyl-type tube formula for non-uniform tubes due to
Roccaforte 2013
. This requires analysis of an appropriate depth function and the so-called visibility regions. The results on convergence in probability and
L^{1}
-convergence follow from these estimates (and suitable assumptions on the containment probabilities
\mathbb{P}(P(\varphi_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)
). This approach is in the spirit of
[
6
]
, where Steiner’s tube formula is used to study volume approximations of randomly circumscribed (real) polyhedra. For variance bounds and normal approximation, we use the Efron–Stein, Poincaré, and second-order Poincaré inequalities along with first-order estimates on the volumes of cuts and tubular cuts. Some of these geometric estimates were already available in the literature (Lemmas
3.2
,
3.3
,
3.5
,
3.13
and
3.15
), while the rest are either new or are quantitatively precise versions of what was known previously. We believe that these estimates are of independent interest in the study of
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains.

2.
Limit theorems for random polyhedra

In this section, we formally introduce our model of random polyhedra and state our main results. In Section
2.1
, we define the class of polyhedral objects under consideration. In Section
2.2
, we state the main limit theorems for the volume approximation. In Section
2.3
, we make some observations related to the main theorems. For reference, a list of notation (such as for complex derivatives and gradients) is provided at the beginning of Section
3
.

2.1.
Polyhedra induced by strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains

Recall that a set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is (real) convex if and only if the intersection of
K
with every real line is connected. In analogy with this, a subset
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
is said to be
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
if for every
complex
affine line
L
,
D\cap L
is connected and simply connected, i.e., the complement of
D
in the extended line
L\cup\{\infty\}
is connected. A domain (i.e., a connected and open set) in
{\mathbb{C}}
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex precisely when it is simply connected. In higher dimensions, while all
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
are topologically trivial, i.e., homeomorphic to the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the converse is far from true.
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex sets satisfy many complex analogues of properties displayed by convex sets. As mentioned in Section
1
,
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is invariant under projective (or linear fractional) transformations, i.e., mappings of the form (
B.1
). Akin to the polar of a convex set, one may define the dual complement of a
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex set; see
[
1
, Def. 2.1.1]
. Moreover, any open or compact
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex set
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfies a complex version of the Hahn–Banach separation property:
\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus D
is a union of complex hyperplanes in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We direct the reader to
[
1
]
for a more detailed discussion of
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity and related notions.

We now consider
\mathcal{C}^{2}
domains, i.e., those of the form
D=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\rho(z)<0\}
, where
\rho
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
whose gradient is nonvanishing on
bD
. Here,
\rho
is called a
defining function of
D
. At any point
w\in bD
, the
real tangent space
of
bD
at
w
is the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space

\mathcal{T}_{w}bD=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\operatorname{Re}\left<\partial\rho(w),v\right>=0\},

while the
complex tangent space
of
bD
at
w
is the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space

\mathcal{H}_{w}bD=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\left<\partial\rho(w),v\right>=0\}.

We note that the complex tangent space at
w
is the maximal subspace of the real tangent space at
w
that is invariant under the action of multiplication by
i
.

Recall that
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
as above is real convex if and only if the Hessian of
\rho
when restricted to
\mathcal{T}_{w}bD
is semi-positive definite for every
w\in bD
. In the same way,
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
as above is
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex if and only if the Hessian of
\rho
when restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
is semi-positive definite for every
w\in bD
. This can be expressed in terms of complex derivatives as follows:

Q_{w}(v,v):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\rho}}{\partial{z_{j}}\partial{\overline{z}_{k}}}(w)v_{j}\overline{v}_{k}+\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\rho}}{\partial{z_{j}}\partial{z_{k}}}(w)v_{j}v_{k}\geq 0\quad\forall w\in bD,v\in\mathcal{H}_{w}bD.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, and
\rho
be as above. Then
D
is said to be
strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
if, for each
w\in bD
,
Q_{w}
is positive definite when restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{w}bD
.

Any such
D
has the property that the complex tangent hyperplane
w+\mathcal{H}_{w}bD=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\left<\partial\rho(w),w-z\right>=0\}
at any
w\in bD
intersects
\overline{D}
only at
w
; see
[
1
, Sec. 2.5]
.
By the continuity of the second-order derivatives, strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is equivalent to the existence of a continuous function
c:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that

(2.1)

Q_{w}(v,v)\geq c(w)||v||^{2}\quad\forall w\in bD,v\in\mathcal{H}_{w}bD.

Thus, when
D
is bounded, strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity is equivalent to
strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear convexity
(in the sense of
[
22
]
), i.e., there exists a
\tilde{c}>0
such that

(2.2)

\left|\left<\partial\rho(w),w-z\right>\right|\geq\tilde{c}||w-z||^{2}\quad\forall(z,w)\in\overline{D}\times bD.

See Proposition 3.1 (and the subsequent remarks) in
[
22
]
. In general, for a fixed
w\in bD
, (
2.1
) implies (
2.2
) only for
z
near
w
, but since the left hand side of (
2.2
) is positive on
\overline{D}\setminus\{w\}
, the compactness of
\overline{D}
grants (
2.2
) for all
z\in\overline{D}
. In geometric terms,
D
is strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex if
bD
is locally equivalent to a strongly real convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
via a linear fractional transformation. For more insight into the geometry of such domains, see Lemma
3.3
, Lemma
3.13
, and Appendix
B
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a bounded strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domain and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Given a defining function
\rho
of
D
,
\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}\subset bD
and a function
\boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, let
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
be the union of those connected components of

(2.3)

\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\frac{\left|\left<\partial\rho(w^{j}),w^{j}-z\right>\right|}{||\partial\rho(w^{j})||}>\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})\right\},

that intersect
D
. To place (
2.3
) into context, we note that if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
(real-)convex domain with defining function
r
, then any
n
-faceted (real-)convex polyhedron
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be written as

P=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left<\nabla r(y^{j}),y^{j}-x\right>>\delta_{j},j=1,...,n\right\}

for some
y^{1},...,y^{n}\in bK
and
\delta_{1},...,\delta_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In particular, if
\delta_{1}=\cdots=\delta_{n}=0
, then
P
is the circumscribed polyhedron
P_{\text{c}}(\varphi)
for
\varphi=\{y^{1},...,y^{n}\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
D
as above, an
induced polyhedron
is a nonempty set of the form
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
. We say that
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is an
induced polyhedron contained
in
D
, if
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\subset D
. We call
\varphi
the
source set
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}
the
size function
of
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
, respectively.

Note that, for any
w\in bD
, the set

F_{w}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\frac{\left|\left<\partial\rho(w),w-z\right>\right|}{||\partial\rho(w)||}=\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)\right\}

is a real hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
that is foliated by complex hyperplanes, and divides
\mathbb{C}^{d}
into two unbounded
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains. Thus,
F_{w}
can be viewed as a complex analogue of a real hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In view of this, we refer to any nonempty set
F_{w_{j}}\cap bP(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
,
j=1,...,n
, as a
facet
of
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
. The elements of the collection

\mathscr{P}_{n}(D):=\big\{P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}):\varphi\subset bD,\ |\varphi|\leq n,\ \text{and}\ \boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)\big\}

are called induced polyhedra with at most
n
facets. This class is invariant under complex affine transformations, i.e., given a source set
\varphi
on
bD
, size function
\boldsymbol{\delta}
, and
A\in\operatorname{GL}(d;{\mathbb{C}})
, there exists a size function
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}
such that
A(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}))=P(A(\varphi);\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime})
. This follows from (
B.2
).

2.2.
Main results

We briefly introduce the Poisson and binomial processes, and then state our main results for random polyhedra constructed on these point processes. See Appendix
A
for a more formal definition of point processes and a list of results regarding them that we use in this paper.

Let
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a probability density function i.e.,
\int_{bD}f(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)=1
. For
n\geq 1
, let
\eta_{n}
be a Poisson process with intensity measure
nf\sigma
. From the standard representation of a Poisson process, we can assume that
\eta_{n}=\{W^{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N_{n}}
, where
W^{i}
are i.i.d. with probability density
f
, and
N_{n}
is an independent Poisson random variable with mean
n
. A second point process of interest is the binomial process. We say that a process
\beta_{n}
is a binomial process on
bD
with
n
points and density
f
if
\beta_{n}=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{n}\}
where, once again,
W^{i}
are i.i.d. points with probability distribution
f\sigma
.

For a random polyhedral approximation of
D
, we consider an induced polyhedron whose source set is a point process on
bD
(either
\eta_{n}
or
\beta_{n}
) and size function is
\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
, where we impose the following assumption on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
:

(2.4)

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(w)=g(w)\delta_{n}{,}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\delta_{n}:=\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}

and
g
is a fixed positive function on
bD
such that
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(bD)
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

Our main objective in this paper is to understand the asymptotics of the volume of the portion of
D
that is left uncovered by the random polyhedra
P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})
or
P(\beta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Two of our main results are convergence in probability, and under further assumptions,
L^{1}
-convergence of the random variables
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
and
\delta_{V}(D,P(\beta_{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
, where recall from (
1.4
) that

\delta_{V}(D,P)=\lambda(D\setminus P)\mathbf{1}(P\subset D)+\lambda(D)\mathbf{1}(P\subsetneq D).

The rest are variance bounds and normal approximation for these random variables.

Theorem 2.3

(
Convergence in Probability).

Let
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a bounded probability density function that is bounded away from
0
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=g\delta_{n}
be as in (
2.4
). Further, assume that
g
is such that

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)\rightarrow 1\mbox{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

Then,

(2.6)

{\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{1/d}}\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\mbox{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

If (
2.5
) holds for
\beta_{n}
, then (
2.6
) holds for
\beta_{n}
.

The above theorem gives a simple condition under which the error in volume approximation decays to zero at a fixed rate as the number of facets of the random polyhedra increase. While (
2.6
) gives the precise rate of volume approximation for a fixed
f
and
g
, it leaves open the question of the the best possible rate of volume approximation among all random polyhedra; see Conjecture
1.1
.

Theorem 2.4

(
L^{1}
-Convergence and Variance Bounds).

Let
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a bounded probability density function that is bounded away from
0
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=g\delta_{n}
be as in (
2.4
). Further, assume that
g
is such that

(2.7)

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)=1-o\left(n^{-\left(1+\frac{2}{d}+\epsilon\right)}\right)\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty,

for some
\epsilon>0
. Then,

(2.8)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|{\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{1/d}}\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\right|=0

and there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(2.9)

c_{1}{\frac{1}{n^{1+2/d}(\log n)^{2+2/d}}}\leq\BV[\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\leq c_{2}{\frac{(\log n)^{2+2/d}}{n^{1+2/d}}},\quad n\geq 1.

If (
2.7
) holds for
\beta_{n}
, then (
2.8
) and the upper bound in (
2.9
) hold for
\beta_{n}
.

Note that the assumption in (
2.7
) is to ensure that the dominating contribution is from the volume of the random polyhedron and not the non-containment event. Our proofs give explicit bounds in terms of non-containment probabilities, and it may be possible to weaken (
2.7
). For instance, a quick inspection of the proof of Theorem
2.4
shows that to obtain the expectation asymptotics in (
2.8
), it suffices to assume that the non-containment probability is
o\Big((\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/d}\Big)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, but we have assumed (
2.7
) to give a combined result for expectation and variance.

To state a normal approximation result, we fix a metric of approximation for random variables. Given two random variables,
X
and
Y
,
the Wasserstein distance

d_{W}(X,Y)
is defined as

(2.10)

d_{W}(X,Y):=\sup_{h\in\mathrm{Lip}(1)}|\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]|,

where
\mathrm{Lip}(1)
is the class of all functions
h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with Lipschitz constant at most
1
. The Wasserstein distance metrizes the topology of weak convergence i.e., if
d_{W}(X_{n},X)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
then
X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}X
as
n\to\infty
where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution or weak convergence. Henceforth,
Z
denotes a standard normal variable.

Theorem 2.5

(
Central Limit Theorem).

Let
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a bounded probability density function that is bounded away from
0
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=g\delta_{n}
be as in (
2.4
). Further, assume that
g
is such that (
2.7
) holds for some
\epsilon>0
. Then,

(2.11)

d_{W}\left(\frac{\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-{\mathbb{E}}[\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}},Z\right)\leq C{\frac{(\log n)^{6+6/d}}{n^{\frac{\min\{\epsilon,1\}}{2}}}},\quad n\geq 1.

If (
2.7
) and the lower bound in (
2.9
) hold for
\beta_{n}
, then (
2.11
) holds for
\beta_{n}
.

We have made an assumption on the decay of
\delta_{n}
in (
2.4
), and on the asymptotic behavior of containment probabilities, as in (
2.5
) and (
2.7
). The following lemma assures us that these assumptions are compatible as well as essential.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be a bounded probability density function that is bounded away from
0
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}=g\delta_{n}
with
\delta_{n}
as in (
2.4
) and
g
is a continuous function. Then, the following hold.

(a)

There exists a
c_{D}>0
such that if
\min_{z\in bD}g(z)<c_{D}
, then

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)\rightarrow 0\mbox{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

(b)

For any
\alpha>0
, there exists a
C_{D}>0
such that if
\min_{z\in bD}g(z)>C_{D}
, then

(2.12)

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)=1-{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\alpha})\mbox{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

(c)

Both (a) and (b) also hold when
\eta_{n}
is replaced by
\beta_{n}
.

It is clear from our proof that Part
(b)
holds more generally for any measurable
g
. A more careful analysis of our proofs can yield estimates on
c_{D},C_{D}
but given that these are likely to be far from the sharp bounds, we avoid quantifying them here.

2.3.
Remarks

We now remark in detail about the assumptions in our results, the nature of results and how they compare to real inscribed polyhedra.

Containment condition.
Theorem
2.3
assumes that containment happens with high probability i.e., (
2.5
) holds. Suppose not i.e.,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)<1
. Then we can show that along some subsequence,
\delta_{V}(D,P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
converges in probability to a non-degenerate random variable supported on
\{0,\lambda(D)\}
. Thus, the error doesn’t converge to
0
if (
2.5
) is violated.

Optimizing the size function.
As mentioned earlier, non-trivial size functions occur in similar models in the real case. However, in the real case, one is able to obtain the decay rate of the optimal
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
with explicit constants because the transition from containment probability to coverage probability on the boundary is done via a suitable Riemannian metric, following which the probabilities are estimated using the classical coverage results of
[
10
,
17
,
19
]
. In our case, the rate of decay of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}
, i.e., Lemma
2.6
, is also obtained by reducing the question of containment to that of coverage of the boundary by the so-called caps of the approximating polyhedra. However, due to the inhomogeneous nature of the caps, this is at best a coverage problem in a sub-Riemannian metric, for which no coverage results of the type in
[
10
,
17
,
19
]
are available. This is one of the obstacles in proving Conjecture
1.1
.

Comparison with real polyhedra.
In Section
1.1
, we have already compared Theorem
2.3
with the results known for randomly inscribed and circumscribed polyhedra in the real setting. We now compare the other limit theorems in this paper with those for randomly inscribed polyhedra (see
[
31
,
32
,
34
,
35
,
41
,
43
]
). Recall that in these models,
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth convex body,
the polyhedron, say
P_{n}
, is constructed as the convex hull of a point process (Poisson or binomial) on
bK
, and one studies the asymptotics of
V_{n}:=\lambda(K\setminus P_{n})
. It is known that

(a)

\mathbb{E}[V_{n}]\sim v^{*}_{{\mathbb{R}}}n^{-2/(d-1)}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
(
[
31
, Theorem 1]
),

(b)

\BV[V_{n}]=\Theta(n^{-1-4/(d-1)})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
(
[
32
, Theorem 8]
and
[
35
, Theorem 1.1]
),

(c)

d_{K}\left(\frac{V_{n}-\mathbb{E}[V_{n}]}{\sqrt{BV[V_{n}]}},Z\right)\leq Cn^{-1/2}(\log n)^{3(1+2/(d-1))}
(
[
41
, Theorem 1 and Section 4.1]
),

where
d_{K}
is the Kolmogorov distance and
v^{*}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
is an explicit constant described in terms of the Blaschke surface area measure. Up to logarithmic factors, our expectation asymptotics and variance bounds resemble the above results with the exponent
(d-1)
replaced by
2d
. The error in normal approximation has an extra
\epsilon
dependence owing to assumption (
2.7
). An expectation result similar to
(a)
is proven for randomly circumscribed polytopes in
[
6
]
, but we are not aware of variance bounds or a central limit theorem for this model. Similar results are known if the point process (Poisson or binomial) is in
K
(see
[
33
]
), where the exponent is
(d+1)
instead of
(d-1)
. Exact variance asymptotics have also been obtained for real random polytopes in
[
8
, Corollary 1.1 and 1.2]
(see also
[
40
, Chapter 5]
) via scaling transforms.

On the metric of normal approximation.
Our proof for the normal approximation result (Theorem
2.5
) uses the second-order Poincaré inequalities for Wasserstein distance in
[
24
,
21
]
(see Appendix
A
). One can instead use the second-order Poincaré inequalities for the stronger Kolmogorov distance from
[
24
,
21
]
but this involves bounding some additional terms. Though this should be possible using our current estimates, we avoid doing so here. We expect that the rate of convergence will be of same order even under Kolmogorov distance.

Geometric estimates

In this section, we lay the groundwork for the deterministic aspects of our main proofs. Subsection

3.7
and
3.10
and Propositions

and

Notation
.

The following notation will be used throughout the paper.

(i)

For a point

•

z

•

•

(ii)

Theorem 3.6

(
[

)

Let

3.2.

In view of Theorem

D
between two (inner) tubular neighborhoods of the boundary of

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
D
be as in Definition
3.1
,
\delta>0
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}=g\delta
, for some positive function

(3.5)

\displaystyle R_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\leq C_{D}||\boldsymbol{\delta}||_{\infty},

(3.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq r_{\infty}(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta})-g(\zeta)\delta\leq C_{D}||g||_{\alpha}^{2}\delta^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

To prove the above theorem, we need estimates on

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

.

\delta-|L(z,w)|\leq r_{w}(z;\delta)\leq\sqrt{\delta^{2}-|L(z,w)|^{2}}+C_{D}\delta^{2}.

By Lemmas

\displaystyle r_{0}(z;\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{r\geq 0:|z_{d}+r\hat{\eta}_{d}(z)|=\delta\right\}.

Thus,

\displaystyle x_{d}\partial_{x_{d}}f(z^{\prime},x_{d})-y_{d}

\displaystyle-c_{D}||z^{\prime}||^{2}+ex_{d}^{2}+h(z^{\prime},x_{d}),

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}^{2}(\zeta,w)-\mathtt{d}^{2}(z,w)-\mathtt{d}^{2}(\zeta,z)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left<\frac{\partial\rho(w)}{||\partial\rho(w)||},w-\zeta\right>-\left<\frac{\partial\rho(w)}{||\partial\rho(w)||},w-z\right>-\left<\frac{\partial\rho(z)}{||\partial\rho(z)||},z-\zeta\right>\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\left<\partial\rho(w)-\partial\rho(z),z-\zeta\right>\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim||w-z||\times||z-\zeta||

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathtt{d}(z,w)\mathtt{d}(\zeta,z),

where the final two steps follow from the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness of
\rho
and (
3.3
), respectively. Thus, we obtain
(c)
.
∎

We now observe that the containment condition
P(\varphi;\delta)\subset D
can indeed be reduced to a covering condition on
bD
in terms of the caps of
P(\varphi;\delta)
, which are
\mathtt{d}
-balls since

S(w;\delta):=\{\zeta\in bD:\mathtt{d}(\zeta,w)\leq\sqrt{\delta}\}.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
D
be as in Definition
3.1
,
\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{n}\}\subset bD
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. Then,
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is contained in
D
if and only if
bD=S(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
, where

S(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}S(w^{j};\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})).

Proof.

Recall that
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is the union of those connected components of

\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\left|L(z,w^{j})\right|>\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})\right\}

that intersect
D
. Since
S(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})=bD\setminus\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
,
bD\neq S(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
if and only if
bD\cap\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is nonempty. However, if
z\in bD\cap\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
, then
z
must be contained in a connected component of
\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
that intersects
D
, i.e.,
bD\cap\widetilde{P}(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})=bD\cap P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
. Thus, it suffices to show that
P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\subset D
if and only if
bD\cap P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
is empty. The forward implication is trivial. For the converse, we argue by contraposition: if
P
is a connected component of
P(\varphi;\delta)
that is not contained in
D
, then it must intersect
bD
. Otherwise
P
is the disjoint union of the nonempty open sets
P\cap D
and
P\cap(\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\overline{D})
which is impossible.
∎

In order to study the covering problem on
bD
, we need the existence of a ‘good’ cover of
bD
. The existence of this cover is due to the fact that balls in the quasimetric
\mathtt{d}_{D}
satisfy a Vitali-type covering lemma; see
[
20
, Lemma 4.1.1]
for a proof that generalizes to quasimetrics.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
D
be as in Definition
3.1
. There is a constant
0<\mathfrak{K}=\mathfrak{K}_{D}\leq 1/9
such that for any
\delta>0
, there exist
m=m_{\delta}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
p^{1},...,p^{m}\in bD
such that

(a)

bD=S(p^{1};\delta)\cup\cdots\cup S(p^{m};\delta)
, and

(b)

S(p^{1},\mathfrak{K}\>\delta),...,S(p^{m};\mathfrak{K}\>\delta)
are pairwise disjoint.

The dilation constant in the above lemma depends on the quasimetric constant
\mathfrak{q}_{D}
. In the case of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
,
\mathtt{d}
is in fact a metric on
b\mathbb{B}^{d}
(e.g., see
[
37
, Section 5.1]
), so we may choose
\mathfrak{q}_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}
and
\mathfrak{K}_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}
as
1
and
1/9
, respectively.

3.5.
Nearest-neighbor estimates

So far, we have focussed on estimates for individual cuts and caps, but for variance bounds and normal approximation results, it is crucial to understand volume and area contributions of intersecting cuts and caps. In this subsection, we collect a couple of such estimates.

First, we quantify the following statement: cuts based at points that are sufficiently far away from each other (in
\mathtt{d}
) cannot intersect.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
D
be as in Definition
3.1
. There are constants
\delta_{D},A_{D}>0
such that

\sigma\{\zeta\in bD:C(\zeta;\delta)\cap C(w;\delta)\neq\emptyset\}\leq A_{D}\delta^{d}\qquad\forall w\in bD,\ \delta<\delta_{D}.

Proof.

Let
C_{D},\delta_{D}
be as in Corollary
3.9
. Let
w,\zeta\in bD
be such that
C(w;\delta)\cap C(\zeta;\delta)\neq\emptyset
for some
\delta<\delta_{D}
. Then,
T_{C_{D}\delta}(S(w;C_{D}\delta))\cap T_{C_{D}\delta}(S(\zeta;C_{D}\delta))\neq\emptyset
. We may assume that each
z\in T_{C_{D}\delta}(bD)
admits a unique projection onto
bD
. Then, there is a
z\in S(w;C_{D}\delta)\cap S(\zeta;C_{D}\delta)
. By the quasi-symmetry of
\mathtt{d}
,
\zeta\in S(w;C_{D}\delta)
, and the claim follows from (
3.12
).
∎

Next, we bound (from below) the volume increment caused by adding a cut at a point that is sufficiently isolated from the rest of the source points. Note that this result is stated for tubular cuts, which suffices for our purposes.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
D
be as in Definition
3.1
. Let
\boldsymbol{\delta}=g\delta
for some positive function
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(bD)
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\delta>0
. There exist
b_{D},B_{g,D},t_{g,D}>0
such that for all
w\in bD
and
t<t_{g,D}
,

\lambda\left(TC(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\setminus\bigcup_{\zeta\in bD\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))}TC(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))\right)\geq b_{D}\left(t^{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)\right)^{d+1},\quad\delta<B_{g,D}t^{4/\alpha}.

Proof.

Let
C_{D}
and
\delta_{D}
be as in the proof of Proposition
3.16
. Observe that for any
w\in bD
,

\{\zeta\in bD:TC(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\cap TC(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))\neq\emptyset\}\subset\{\zeta\in bD:C(w;||g||_{\infty}\delta)\cap C(\zeta;||g||_{\infty}\delta)\neq\emptyset\},

and the latter set is contained in
S(w;C_{g,D}\delta)
, as shown in the proof of Proposition
3.16
. Thus, it suffices to work with
\zeta\in S(w;C_{g,D}\delta)\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))
to establish the claim.

We now bound from below
r_{w}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))-r_{\zeta}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))
for certain
z
and
\zeta
near
w
. Paraphrasing (
3.7
) in terms of the quasimetric
\mathtt{d}
, we have that for
w\in bD
,
z\in S(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))
, and
\delta<\delta_{g,D}
,

(3.23)

\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)-\mathtt{d}(z,w)^{2}\leq r_{w}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\leq\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)^{2}-\mathtt{d}(z,w)^{4}}+C_{g,D}\delta^{2}.

Fix
s,t>0
such that
s<t<1
. Then, if
z\in S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))
and
\zeta\in bD\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))
,

\sqrt{t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}\leq\mathtt{d}(\zeta,w)\leq\mathfrak{q}^{2}\mathtt{d}(z,\zeta)+\mathfrak{q}\mathtt{d}(z,w)\leq\mathfrak{q}^{2}\mathtt{d}(z,\zeta)+\mathfrak{q}^{2}\sqrt{s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)},

where
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{q}_{D}\geq 1
is the quasi-metric constant in Lemma
3.13
. Thus,

\mathtt{d}(z,\zeta)\geq\left(\mathfrak{q}^{-2}\sqrt{t}-\sqrt{s}\right)\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}>0,\qquad\text{for}\ s<t/\mathfrak{q}^{4}.

Now, setting
s=t^{2}/4\mathfrak{q}^{8}
, it follows from (
3.23
) and the lower bound on
\mathtt{d}(z,\zeta)
that for
\zeta\in S(w;C_{g,D}\delta)\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))
and
z\in S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\cap S(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))
,

(3.24)

r_{w}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\geq\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)R(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{\zeta}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))\leq\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)r_{\zeta}(t),

where
R(t)=1-t^{2}/4\mathfrak{q}^{8}
and

r_{\zeta}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{g(\zeta)^{2}}{g(w)^{2}}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\mathfrak{q}^{2}}-\frac{t}{2\mathfrak{q}^{4}}\right)^{4}}+C_{g,D}\delta
.

Now, by the assumption on
g
, we obtain that
|g(\zeta)-g(w)|\leq C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
, for all
w\in bD
,
\zeta\in S(w;C_{g,D}\delta)
, and
\delta<\delta_{g,D}
. Thus, for
w\in bD,\zeta\in S(w;C_{g,D}\delta)
, and
\delta<\delta_{g,D}
,

g(\zeta)^{2}\leq g(w)^{2}+C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

Therefore,

(3.25)

\displaystyle r_{\zeta}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\mathfrak{q}^{2}}-\frac{t}{2\mathfrak{q}^{4}}\right)^{4}}+C_{g,D}\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\mathfrak{q}^{2}}-\frac{t}{2\mathfrak{q}^{4}}\right)^{4}+C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=:r(t),

where we have used that
\sqrt{1-b+a}\leq 1-\frac{b}{2}+a
, whenever
b<3/4
and
a>0
. Now, for sufficiently small
t
,

\displaystyle R(t)-r(t)=\left(\frac{(\sqrt{t}-t/2\mathfrak{q}^{2})^{4}}{2\mathfrak{q}^{8}}-\frac{t^{2}}{4\mathfrak{q}^{8}}-C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)\geq\left(\frac{t^{2}}{8\mathfrak{q}^{8}}-C_{g,D}\delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right).

Thus, there are
a_{D},A_{g,D},t_{g,D}>0
so that
R(t)-r(t)\geq a_{D}t^{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)
for all
\delta<A_{g,D}t^{4/\alpha}
,
t<t_{g,D}
.

Now, let
\pi_{bD}(\xi)
be the (uniquely determined) projection of
\xi
onto
bD
for
\xi
sufficiently close to
bD
. Define
T:=T_{R(t)\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)))\setminus T_{r(t)\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)))
,
where
T_{*}(\cdot)
is as in Definition
3.1
. By (
3.24
), we have that

\displaystyle T_{R(t)\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}\big(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\big)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle TC(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)),

\displaystyle T_{r(t)\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)}\big(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\big)

\displaystyle\supset

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\zeta\in bD\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))}\left\{\xi\in TC(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta)):\pi_{bD}(\xi)\in S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\right\}.

Thus,
T\subset TC(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\setminus\bigcup_{\zeta\in bD\setminus S(w;t\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))}TC(\zeta;\boldsymbol{\delta}(\zeta))
, and it suffices to produce a lower bound for
\lambda(T)
. We will use Roccaforte’s formula, i.e., Theorem
3.6
for this.

From (
3.12
),
\sigma(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)))\geq\widetilde{a}_{D}(t^{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))^{d}
for some
\widetilde{a}_{D}>0
. Moreover,
|R(t)|,|r(t)|\leq 2
for all
t<t_{g,D}
, and the functions
s_{1},...,s_{2d-1}
as in Theorem
3.6
are bounded on
bD
. Thus, by Theorem
3.6
and the above estimates, there are constants
b_{D},B_{g,D}>0
so that

\displaystyle\lambda(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)(R(t)-r(t))\int\limits_{S(w;s\delta)}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{(-\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))^{j+1}}{j+1}\frac{R(t)^{j+1}-r(t)^{j+1}}{R(t)-r(t)}s_{j}(z)\right)d\sigma(z)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle b_{D}\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)(R(t)-r(t))\sigma(S(w;s\boldsymbol{\delta}(w)))\geq b_{D}(t^{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))^{d+1}

for all
\delta<B_{g,D}t^{4/\alpha}
and
t<t_{g,D}
.
∎

4.
Proofs of limit theorems

In this section, we present the proofs of the main results of this paper, namely those stated in Section
2.2
. We first collect some useful observations regarding the notions introduced in Definition
3.1
.
We use the notation introduced in Definition
3.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-domain as in Definition
3.1
,
\varphi=\{w^{1},\ldots,w^{m}\}\subset bD
be a source set, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}:bD\to(0,\infty)
be a size function. Since
bD
and
\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:|L(z,w)|=\delta\}
,
w\in bD
,
\delta>0
, are smooth real hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, they have zero Lebesgue volume. Thus,

(4.1)

\lambda(D\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}))=\lambda\left(\overline{D}\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\right)=\lambda\left(D\setminus\overline{P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})}\right).

Note that

(4.2)

D\setminus\overline{P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\left\{z\in D:|L(z,w^{j})|<\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})\right\}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}C(w^{j};\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})).

On the other hand, recalling that
\overline{C(w;\delta)}\supset TC(w;\delta)
, we have that

(4.3)

\overline{D}\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\left\{z\in\overline{D}:|L(z,w^{j})|\leq\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})\right\}\supset\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}TC(w^{j};\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})).

Setting

C(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}C(w^{j};\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}):=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}TC(w^{j};\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j})),

we obtain from (
4.1
), (
4.3
), and (
4.2
) that

(4.4)

\lambda\left(TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\right)\leq\lambda\left(D\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\right)=\lambda\left(C(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\right)=\lambda\left(\overline{C(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})}\right).

In order to compute
\lambda\left(TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\right)
using the tube formula in Theorem
3.6
, we express
TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
as a
g
-tube of
bD
for some non-negative
g
. For this, set
r^{+}(z)=\max\{r(z),0\}
, where
r(z):=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}r_{w^{j}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w^{j}))
. Then,
T_{r}=TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
and
T_{r^{+}}=TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\cup bD
.
Thus,

(4.5)

\lambda(TC(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}))=\lambda(T_{r})=\lambda(T_{r^{+}}).

Finally, we note that (
4.4
) helps estimate
\delta_{V}(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}),D)
in terms of volumes of cuts since

(4.6)

\displaystyle\delta_{V}(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}),D)-\lambda(D\setminus P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda(P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\cap D)\mathbf{1}[P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\not\subset D]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda(D)\mathbf{1}[P(\varphi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\not\subset D].

In all the proofs below, we first handle the case when the source set of the random polyhedron is
\eta_{n}
, a Poission point process. Subsequently, the changes required to tackle the case of the source set being
\beta_{n}
, a binomial point process, is discussed. For brevity, we set

\delta_{V}(n):=\delta_{V}\left(D,P(\varphi_{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\right),

where
\varphi_{n}
is either
\eta_{n}
or
\beta_{n}
, depending on the context. We also denote the probability measure
f\sigma
by
\sigma_{f}
.

4.1.
Theorem
2.3
: convergence in probability

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
\eta_{n}
be a Poisson point process on
bD
such that (
2.5
) holds, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset D)\rightarrow 1\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Step 1. Approximating by tubular cuts
. First, we observe that by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)}{\delta_{n}}-\int_{bD}g(z)d\sigma(z)\right|\leq\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|+\left|\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|

(4.7)

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}-\int_{bD}g(z)d\sigma(z)\right|.

Now, from (
4.4
) and (
4.6
), we have that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|>\epsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D\right).

Thus, by the containment assumption on
P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})
,

(4.8)

\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Next, invoking (
4.4
) and (
3.14
), we have that

(4.9)

0\leq\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|\eta_{n}|}\lambda\big(C(W^{i};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\setminus TC(W^{i};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\big)\leq C_{D}|\eta_{n}|\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}\|_{\infty}^{2d+1},

for some
C_{D}>0
. Thus, taking expectation on both sides, we have that

(4.10)

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|\leq C_{D}\|g\|_{\infty}^{2d}n\delta_{n}^{2d}=C_{D}\|g\|_{\infty}^{2d}\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{n}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. Thus, by Markov’s inequality, (
4.1
), (
4.8
), and (
4.10
), (
2.6
) follows if

(4.11)

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}-\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\right|\rightarrow 0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

This reduces to estimating the expectation of a depth function associated to
P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})
.

Step 2. Replacing volume by depth.
For
z\in bD
, let
r_{n}(z):=\max_{1\leq j\leq|\eta_{n}|}r_{W^{j}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(W^{j}))
and
r_{n}^{+}(z)=\max\{r_{n}(z),0\}.
Then, by (
4.5
) the statement (
4.11
) is equivalent to

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\lambda(T_{r^{+}_{n}})}{\delta_{n}}-\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\right|\to 0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Using the tube formula in Theorem
3.6
we have that

(4.13)

\lambda(T_{r^{+}_{n}})=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j+1}\int_{bD}r^{+}_{n}(z)^{j+1}s_{j}(z)\,\text{d}\sigma(z).

Now, recalling that
r_{n}^{+}(z)\leq r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})
for all
z\in bD
, and that
r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\leq C\delta_{n}
(see (
3.6
)), we have that for any
j\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\left|\int_{bD}r^{+}_{n}(z)^{j+1}s_{j}(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\right|\leq\int_{bD}\frac{r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})^{j+1}}{\delta_{n}}|s_{j}(z)|\,\text{d}\sigma(z)\to 0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Thus, since
s_{0}\equiv 1
, to prove (
4.12
), it suffices to show that

(4.14)

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\int_{bD}r^{+}_{n}(z)\,\text{d}\sigma(z)-\int_{bD}g(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\right|\to 0\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty.

Now, by (
3.6
),
r^{+}_{n}(z)\leq r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\leq g(z)\delta_{n}+C_{D}\parallel g\parallel_{\alpha}^{2}\delta_{n}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}
for some
C_{D}>0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}r^{+}_{n}(z)-g(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}r^{+}_{n}(z)-g(z)-C_{D}\parallel g\parallel_{\alpha}^{2}\delta_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right|+C_{D}\parallel g\parallel_{\alpha}^{2}\delta_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=g(z)-\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}r^{+}_{n}(z)+2C_{D}\parallel g\parallel_{\alpha}^{2}\delta_{n}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

Thus, by the triangle inequality, Fubini’s theorem, and the dominated convergence theorem, (
4.14
) follows if we show that for all
z\in bD
,

(4.15)

{{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[r^{+}_{n}(z)]}{\delta_{n}}=g(z).}}

Step 3. Expectation of depth.
Fix
z\in bD
. Since
\{r_{n}(z)\geq s\}=\{r^{+}_{n}(z)\geq s\}
for
s>0
, we have that,

\mathbb{E}[r^{+}_{n}(z)]=\int_{0}^{r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})}\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq s)\text{d}s.

Thus, since
r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\geq g(z)\delta_{n}
, we have that

(4.16)

0\leq\mathbb{E}[r^{+}_{n}(z)]-\int_{0}^{g(z)\delta_{n}}\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq s)\text{d}s\leq(r_{\infty}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})-g(z)\delta_{n}).

By (
3.6
), it suffices to prove (
4.15
) with
\mathbb{E}[r^{+}_{n}(z)]
replaced by the integral in (
4.16
).

Now, recall that
\{w\in bD:r_{w}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(w))\geq s\}=G_{s}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta})
, the
(\boldsymbol{\delta},s)
-visibility region at
z
. Thus,

(4.17)

\mathbb{P}\left(r_{W^{i}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}(W^{i}))<s\right)=\mathbb{P}(W^{i}\notin G_{s}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}))=1-\sigma_{f}(G_{s}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta})),

where recall
\sigma_{f}=f\text{d}\sigma
is the probability distribution of the
W^{i}
’s. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq s)=1-\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)<s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{|\eta_{n}|}\{r_{W^{i}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(W^{i}))<s\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{|\eta_{n}|}\mathbb{P}\Big(r_{W^{i}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(W^{i}))<s\Big)\right],

where, in the last step, we have conditioned on
|\eta_{n}|
and used the independence of
|\eta_{n}|
and
W^{i}
’s. Now, by (
4.17
), and the fact that
\mathbb{E}[t^{|\eta_{n}|}]=e^{-n(1-t)}
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{g(z)\delta_{n}}\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq s)\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{g(z)\delta_{n}}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\Big[(1-\sigma_{f}(G_{s}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))^{|\eta_{n}|}\Big]\right)\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{g(z)\delta_{n}}\big(1-e^{-n\sigma_{f}(G_{s}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}\big)\text{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(z)\delta_{n}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-e^{-n\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}\right)\text{d}t,

where, in the final step, we use the change of variable
s\mapsto tg(z)\delta_{n}
.
Now, setting
c_{f}:=\inf_{w\in bD}f(w)
, we have that

c_{f}\sigma(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\leq\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\sigma(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})).

Now, recall that (
3.22
) grants constants
C_{D},K_{D}>0
such that for
n
large and
t\leq 1-C_{D}\sqrt{\delta_{n}}
,

|\sigma(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-h_{d}(t)k_{d}(z)g(z)^{d}\delta_{n}^{d}|\leq K_{D}\delta_{n}^{d+\alpha/2}.

Therefore, since
\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain that for any
t<1
,

(4.19)

n\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\to\infty\ \text{as}\ n\to\infty.

In other words,
1-e^{-n\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
for any
t<1
. Thus, by (
4.18
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

(4.20)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}\int_{0}^{g(z)\delta_{n}}\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq s)\text{d}s=\lim_{n\to\infty}g(z)\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-e^{-n\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}\right)\text{d}t=g(z).

Substituting (
4.20
) into (
4.16
) yields (
4.15
). This completes the proof in the case of
\eta_{n}
.

Now, assume that
\beta_{n}
is a binomial point process satisfying (the analogue of) (
2.5
). The proof of (
2.6
) for
\beta_{n}
is identical to the above argument until (
4.16
) as one does not rely on the properties of Poisson point processes until that stage. For the rest of the proof, it suffices to show that

(4.21)

\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)\geq tg(z)\delta_{n})\rightarrow 1\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty

for any
t<1
, where
r_{n}(z)=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}r_{W^{j}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(W^{j}))
. Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(r_{n}(z)<tg(z)\delta_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\cap_{i=1}^{n}\{r_{W^{i}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(W^{i}))<tg(z)\delta_{n}\})=\big(1-\sigma_{f}(G_{tg(z)\delta_{n}}(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\big)^{n},

which converges to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
for any
t<1
by (
4.19
). Thus, (
4.21
) holds, and the proof is complete.
∎

4.2.
Theorem
2.4
:
L^{1}
-convergence and variance bounds

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let
\eta_{n}
be a Poisson process on
bD
satisfying (
2.7
), i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)=o\left(n^{-\left(1+\frac{2}{d}+\epsilon\right)}\right)\ \text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty,

for some
\varepsilon>0
. Recall that
\delta_{V}(n)=\delta_{V}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}),D)
.

Part 1.
L^{1}
-Convergence.
We take expectations on both sides of the inequality (
4.1
). The expectations of the second and third terms of the right-hand side of (
4.1
) converge to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
as proved in Section
4.1
; see (
4.10
) and (
4.11
). Moreover, by (
4.6
) and (
2.7
), we have that

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))}{\delta_{n}}\right|\leq\lambda(D)\frac{\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)}{\delta_{n}}=o\left(n^{-\left(1+\frac{1}{d}+\varepsilon\right)}(\log n)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\right)

as
n\to\infty
. Thus, (
2.8
) follows.

Part 2. Variance bounds.
The rest of the proof is devoted to establishing (
2.9
). This will be done in three steps. First, we approximate the variance of
\delta_{V}(n)
by that of
\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
, the volume of tubular cuts. This approximation is done by assuming (
2.9
) for
\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
. Next, we derive variance upper bounds for the volume of tubular cuts. Finally, we derive variance lower bounds for the volume of tubular cuts. Thus, we prove (
2.9
) for
\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
and complete the proof.

Step 1. Approximating by tubular cuts.
First, we recall the standard variance-covariance inequality. Let
X,Y
be random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty
. Then

\displaystyle|\BV[X]-\BV[Y]|

\displaystyle=|\BV[X-Y]+2\BC(X-Y,Y)|

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leq\BV[X-Y]+2\sqrt{\BV[X-Y]\BV[Y]}

Now, by (
4.22
) and (
4.6
),

\displaystyle\left|\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]-\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\BV[\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]+2\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\BV[\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))|^{2}]+2\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\delta_{n}))]\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{V}(n)-\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))|^{2}]}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(D)^{2}\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)+2\lambda(D)\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)}.

We will now complete the proof of (
2.9
) assuming that there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, such that

(4.24)

C_{1}\frac{1}{n^{(1+2/d)}(\log n)^{2(1+1/d)}}\leq\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\leq C_{2}\frac{(\log n)^{2(1+1/d)}}{n^{(1+2/d)}}.

We defer the proof of (
4.24
) to Step 2 and Step 3 below. Since
\BV[X]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
, by (
4.9
), we have that

\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\leq Cn^{2}\delta_{n}^{4d+2},

for some
C=C_{g,D}
. Combining this with the lower bound in (
4.24
) yields that

\displaystyle\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{2}\delta_{n}^{4d+2}\left({\frac{n\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}}{(\log n)^{4(d+1)/d}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\frac{(\log n)^{6+\frac{4}{d}}}{n}.

Thus, we have established

(4.25)

\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]=o(\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]){\quad\text{as}\ n\rightarrow\infty}.

Now, (
4.25
) along with the variance-covariance bound (
4.22
) implies that

(4.26)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}=1.

Then, using assumption (
2.7
) in (
4.23
) and combining it with (
4.24
) and (
4.26
), (
2.9
) follows.

Step 2. Proof of upper bound for
\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]
in (
4.24
).
We use the Poincaré inequality i.e., the upper bound in (
A.1
). Now,

\displaystyle D_{z}\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\lambda(TC(\eta_{n}\cup\{z\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\setminus TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))=\lambda(TC(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))\setminus TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})).

Thus, by (
3.13
), there is a constant
C>0
(independent of
z
) such that

(4.27)

\displaystyle|D_{z}\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))|^{2}\leq\lambda(TC(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z)))^{2}\leq\lambda(C(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z)))^{2}\leq C\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}.

Since
\eta_{n}
has intensity measure
nf\sigma
, we have from (
A.1
) that

\displaystyle\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]

\displaystyle\leq Cn\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}\int_{bD}f(z)\text{d}\sigma(z)\leq Cn\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}.

The upper bound in (
4.24
) follows since
\delta_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/d}
.

Step 3. Proof of lower bound for
\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]
in (
4.24
).
We shall show that there is a
C>0
such that

(4.28)

\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\geq C\frac{n\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}}{(\log n)^{4(d+1)/d}}.

The lower bound in (
4.24
) will then follow since
\delta_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/d}
.

We use the lower bound in (
A.1
), for which we must estimate the appropritate difference operator. Given any
s>0
, we have that

\displaystyle D_{z}\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\lambda(TC(\eta_{n}\cup\{z\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\setminus TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))

(4.29)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda(TC(\eta_{n}\cup\{z\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\setminus TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\mathbf{1}[\eta_{n}\cap S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))=\emptyset].

By Proposition
3.17
, there exist constants
t,B,c>0
(depending only on
g
and
D
) such that for any
s<t
, if
\delta_{n}<Bs^{4/\alpha}
,
z\in bD
,
\varphi=\{w^{1},...,w^{m}\}\subset bD
and
\varphi\cap S(z;s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))=\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle\lambda\big(TC(\phi\cup\{z\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\setminus TC(\phi;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\big)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\left(TC(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))\setminus\bigcup_{w\in bD\setminus S(z;s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))}TC(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(w))\right)

\displaystyle\geq cs^{2(d+1)}\delta_{n}^{d+1}.

Applying this to (
4.2
), we obtain that for any
s<t
, if
\delta_{n}<Bs^{4/\alpha}
, then

(4.30)

D_{z}\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\geq cs^{2(d+1)}\delta_{n}^{d+1}\mathbf{1}[\eta_{n}\cap S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))=\emptyset].

Now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\eta_{n}\cap S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))=\emptyset)^{2}

\displaystyle=\exp\{-2n\sigma_{f}(S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z)))\}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\{-cn\sigma(S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z)))\}\qquad\mbox{($f$ is bounded above)}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\{-cns^{d}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z)^{d}\}\qquad\qquad\quad\mbox{(by \eqref{eq:cap})}

\displaystyle\geq n^{-cg(z)^{d}s^{d}}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,(\delta_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/d})

\displaystyle\geq n^{-\widetilde{c}s^{d}},

where
\widetilde{c}=c||g||_{\infty}<\infty
. Thus, by (
4.30
), for some
c_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{bD}\left(\mathbb{E}[D_{z}\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\right)^{2}\text{d}\sigma_{f}(z)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle cs^{4(d+1)}n\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)}\int_{bD}\mathbb{P}(\eta_{n}\cap S(z,s\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))=\emptyset)^{2}\text{d}\sigma_{f}(z)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c_{1}s^{4(d+1)}n^{1-cs^{d}}\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)},

if
\delta_{n}<Bs^{4/\alpha}
and
n
is sufficiently large.
Thus, invoking the lower bound in (
A.1
),

(4.31)

\BV[\lambda(TC(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]\geq c_{1}s^{4(d+1)}n^{1-cs^{d}}\delta_{n}^{2(d+1)},

if
\delta_{n}<Bs^{4/\alpha}
and
n
is sufficiently large. Since
\delta_{n}=(\frac{\log n}{n})^{\frac{1}{d}}
, (
4.31
) holds when
s=\left(\frac{4(d+1)}{cd\log(n)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
and
n
is sufficiently large, and yields (
4.28
). Though we have not used the fact, it can be verified that this choice of
s
is the optimal one.

Now, assume that
\beta_{n}
is a binomial point process satisfying (the analogue of) (
2.7
). The proof of
L^{1}
-convergence, i.e., (
2.8
) for
\beta_{n}
follows as in Step 1 above. The upper bound on the variance in (
2.9
) can be derived as done in Step 2 above with the following modification. Instead of the Poincaré inequality, apply the Efron–Stein inequality (
A.3
) to
F(\cdot)=\lambda(C(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
, and use the fact that (
4.27
) holds for
\lambda(C(\beta_{n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
as well.
∎

4.3.
Theorem
2.5
: central limit theorem

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

The strategy of the proof is to reduce (
2.11
) to a central limit theorem for
\lambda(C(\varphi_{n};\delta_{n}))
, where
\varphi_{n}
is either
\eta_{n}
or
\beta_{n}
. The latter is then handled using second-order Poincaré inequalities (Theorem
A.2
in the case of
\eta_{n}
, and Theorem
A.3
in the case of
\beta_{n}
).

Case I. The source set is
\eta_{n}
.
Let
\eta_{n}
be a Poisson process on
bD
satisfying (
2.7
). Recall that
\delta_{V}(n)=\delta_{V}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}),D)
, and the Wasserstein distance is as in (
2.10
). We will often invoke the following simple observation.

(4.32)

d_{W}(X,Y)\leq\sup_{h\in\mathrm{Lip}(1)}\mathbb{E}|h(X)-h(Y)|\leq\mathbb{E}|X-Y|.

By the triangle inequality, we have that

d_{W}\Big(\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{V}(n)]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}},Z\Big)\leq\text{I}+\text{II}+\text{III},

where

I

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle d_{W}\left(\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{V}(n)]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}},\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}\right),

II

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle d_{W}\left(\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}},\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}}\right),

III

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle d_{W}\left(\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}},Z\right).

Part 1. Upper bound for I
. Using(
4.32
), we have that

I

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\delta_{V}(n)-\mathbb{E}[\delta_{V}(n)]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}-\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\lambda(D)\frac{\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)}{\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{(by \eqref{e:delvvolp} and \eqref{E:upperlowerbounds})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{n^{-(1+2/d+\epsilon)}}{\sqrt{n^{-(1+2/d)}(\log n)^{-2(1+1/d)}}}\qquad\qquad\,\,\mbox{(by \eqref{e:covexpvarpoi} and \eqref{e:varasymptotpoi})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\frac{(\log n)^{(1+1/d)}}{n^{\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d}}}.

Part 2. Upper bound for II
. Denoting
\dfrac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}}
by
X_{n}
, we have that

(4.33)

II

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{W}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}\,X_{n},X_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}}-1\right|\mathbb{E}|X_{n}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}-1\right|\frac{1}{\left|\sqrt{{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}}+1\right|}\mathbb{E}|X_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}-1\right|,

where, in the last step, we have used the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality to observe that,

\mathbb{E}|X_{n}|=\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\sqrt{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}}\right]^{2}}=1.

Using the variance-covariance bound (
4.22
) as done in (
4.23
), we have that

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}}-1\right|=\frac{|\BV[\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))]-\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]|}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}

(4.34)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leq\frac{\lambda(D)^{2}\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)+2\lambda(D)\sqrt{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]\mathbb{P}(P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D)}}{\BV[\delta_{V}(n)]}.

Combining this with (
4.33
), and invoking the bounds in (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), we have that

II

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left({n^{-(1+2/d+\epsilon)}}{n^{(1+\frac{2}{d})}(\log n)^{2(1+\frac{1}{d})}}+\sqrt{{n^{-(1+2/d+\epsilon)}}{n^{(1+\frac{2}{d})}(\log n)^{2(1+\frac{1}{d})}}}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\,\frac{(\log n)^{(1+\frac{1}{d})}}{n^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}}.

Part 3. Upper bound for III
. We use Theorem
A.2
, for which must derive bounds for
T_{i}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))
,
i=1,2,3
, as defined in Theorem
A.2
. Note that in the case of
\eta_{n}
, the intensity measure is
\mu=n\sigma_{f}=nf\sigma
.

To compute
T_{1}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))
and
T_{2}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))
, we need to bound the second-difference operator of
\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))
. Note that for
z,w\in bD
,

\displaystyle\Big|D^{2}_{z,w}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\lambda(C(\eta_{n}\cup\{z,w\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(C(\eta_{n}\cup\{w\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))-\lambda(C(\eta_{n}\cup\{z\};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))+\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(C(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))\cap C(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(w)))\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\mbox{(by the inclusion-exclusion principle)}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{n}^{d+1}\mathbf{1}[C(z;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(z))\cap C(w;\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}(w))\neq\emptyset]\qquad\mbox{(by \eqref{eq:cut})}

(4.35)

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{n}^{d+1}\mathbf{1}[C(z;\|g\|_{\infty}\delta_{n})\cap C(w;\|g\|_{\infty}\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset].

Thus,

\displaystyle A(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3}):=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big(D^{2}_{z^{1},z^{2}}\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\big)^{2}\big(D^{2}_{z^{2},z^{3}}\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}))\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n}^{4(d+1)}\mathbf{1}\left[z^{1}\in N(z^{2};||g||_{\infty}\delta_{n})\right]\mathbf{1}\left[z^{3}\in N(z^{2};||g||_{\infty}\delta_{n})\right].

where

N(z;\delta):=\{w:C(w;\delta)\cap C(z;\delta)\neq\emptyset\}.
Also, from (
4.27
), we have that

B(z^{1},z^{2}):=\mathbb{E}[(D^{1}_{z^{1}}\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))^{2}(D^{1}_{z^{2}}\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))^{2}]\leq C\delta_{n}^{4(d+1)}.

Therefore, by Proposition
3.16
and the fact that
||g||_{\infty}<\infty
,

\displaystyle T_{1}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(bD)^{3}}A(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})^{1/2}B(z^{1},z^{2})^{1/2}d\mu^{3}(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{3}\delta_{n}^{4(d+1)}\int_{bD}\sigma\left(N(z;||g||_{\infty}\delta_{n})\right)^{2}\text{d}\sigma_{f}(z)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{3}\delta_{n}^{6d+4}.

In a similar fashion, we have that

\displaystyle T_{2}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(bD)^{3}}A(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})d\mu^{3}(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cn^{3}\delta_{n}^{4(d+1)}\int\sigma(N(z;\|g\|_{\infty}\delta_{n}))^{2}\text{d}\sigma_{f}(z)\leq Cn^{3}\delta_{n}^{6d+4}.

Finally, an upper bound for
T_{3}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))
follows easily from (
4.27
):

(4.36)

T_{3}(\lambda(C(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})))\leq Cn\delta_{n}^{3(d+1)}.

Now, using that
\delta_{n}=((\log n)/n)^{1/d}

4.4.
Proposition

: conditions for coverage

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.

The strategy of the proof is to use Lemma
3.14
to convert containment probabilities into converage probabilities. We handle the latter by dividing
bD
suitably and using a classical balls into bins approach for coverage problems.

Proof of
(a)
.
Let
\eta_{n}
be a Poisson process on
bD
. We claim that it suffices to show the existence of a

such that

(4.46)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(S(z;\varepsilon)\setminus S(\eta_{n};c_{D}\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset\Big)=1,\qquad\text{{for some}}\ z\in bD,\varepsilon>0.

To see this, assume (
4.46
) and suppose
\min_{z\in bD}g(z)<c_{D}
. Then, there exist
z_{0}\in bD
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\max_{w\in S(z_{0};2\varepsilon_{0})}g(w)<c_{D}
. Moreover, since
||g||_{\infty}<\infty
and
\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
as

, there is an
n_{g,\varepsilon}
large enough so that if

, then

S(\eta_{n}\cap S(z_{0};2\varepsilon_{0});\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\subset S(\eta_{n};c_{D}\delta_{n})

S(z_{0};\varepsilon_{0})\setminus S(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})=S(z_{0};\varepsilon_{0})\setminus S(\eta_{n}\cap S(z_{0};2\varepsilon_{0});\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\neq\emptyset.

In other words, for any
n\geq n_{g,\varepsilon}
,
S(z_{0};\varepsilon_{0})\setminus S(\eta_{n};c_{D}\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset
implies that
S(z_{0};\varepsilon_{0})\setminus S(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\neq\emptyset
, which (by Lemma
3.14
) further implies that
P(\eta_{n};\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n})\not\subset D
. Thus, (
4.46
) implies
(a)
.

We now establish (
4.46
). Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{p^{1},\ldots,p^{m}\}\subset bD
be as in Lemma
3.15
corresponding to
\delta=\delta_{n}
. Note that both
m
and the
p^{j}
’s change with

, but we do not indicate this in the notation for convenience. Now, given
z\in bD
and
\varepsilon>0
, set

\ell_{n}:=\ell_{\delta_{n},\varepsilon,z}=|\{j:p^{j}\in S(z;\epsilon)\}|.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\{1,...,\ell_{n}\}=\{j:p^{j}\in S(z;\varepsilon)\}
. We claim that, in order to prove (
4.46
), it suffices to show the existence of a
b=b_{D}>0
such that for all
z\in bD
and
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability there exists a
j\in\{1,...\ell_{n}\}
such that
\eta_{n}\cap S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})=\emptyset
, i.e.,

(4.47)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\left\{\eta_{n}\cap S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})=\emptyset\right\}\right)=1.

To see this, set
z=z_{0}
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
. Now, suppose
\eta_{n}\cap S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})=\emptyset
for some
j\in\{1,...,\ell_{n}\}
, where
\ell_{n}=\ell_{\delta_{n},\varepsilon_{0},z_{0}}
. Then, by the quasi-symmetry of the quasimetric
\mathtt{d}
, there is a
c_{D}>0
depending only on
\mathfrak{K}
and

such that
\min\{\mathtt{d}(p^{j},W):W\in\eta_{n}\}>c_{D}\delta_{n}
, i.e.,

. In other words, (
4.47
) implies (
4.46
).

Finally, it remains to prove (
4.47
). Let
z\in bD
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let

. Using that the caps

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\left\{\eta_{n}\cap S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\eta_{n}\cap S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})\neq\emptyset)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\left(1-e^{-n\sigma_{f}(S(p^{j};b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n}))}\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\ell_{n}}\left(1-e^{-nC_{f,D}(b\mathfrak{K}\delta_{n})^{d}}\right)

.

Appendix A
Background on Poisson and Binomial processes

We now collect some notions and results on Poisson and binomial processes that are used in the article; see
[
23
,
30
]
for more details.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be as defined above, and
{\mathcal{B}}:=\mathcal{B}(bD)
be the Borel
\sigma
-field on

bD
. Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be the set of finite counting measures on
bD
,
and
{\mathcal{N}}
be the evaluation
\sigma
-field i.e., the
\sigma
-field of subsets of
{\mathbf{N}}
generated by
the sets of the form
\{\varphi\in{\mathbf{N}}:\varphi(B)=k\}
, with
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
.

Definition A.1
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space. A
point
process

\eta
on
bD
is a random element of
({\mathbf{N}},{\mathcal{N}})
,
i.e.
\eta:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbf{N}}
is measurable. The intensity measure of a point process
\eta
is the measure
\lambda
defined as
\lambda(B)={\mathbb{E}}[\eta(B)],B\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

Let
\lambda
be a finite measure. A point process
\eta
is said to be
a Poisson process on
bD
with intensity measure
\lambda
if

(a)

for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
, the random variable
\eta(B)
has a Poisson distribution with mean
\lambda(B)
;

(b)

for all
m\geq 1
and all pairwise disjoint subsets
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\in\mathcal{B}
, the random variables
\eta(B_{1}),\ldots,\eta(B_{m})
are independent (
complete independence
).

An elementary way to construct a Poissson process with a finite intensity measure
\lambda
on
bD
is as follows. Let
W^{n},n\geq 1
, be i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\frac{\lambda(\cdot)}{\lambda(bD)}
and
N
be an independent Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda(bD)
. Then
\eta=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{N}\}
is a Poisson process with intensity measure
\lambda
. We also represent
\eta
as a counting measure, i.e.,
\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{W^{i}}.
We always work with this explicit construction of the Poisson process for convenience.

We recall the so-called difference operators
[
23
, Section 18.1]
. Let
z\in bD,\phi\in{\mathbf{N}}
and
F:{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function. The first and second-order difference operators are defined as

\displaystyle D_{z}F(\phi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle F(\phi\cup\{z\})-F(\phi),

\displaystyle D^{2}_{z,w}F(\phi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle D_{z}(D_{w}F(\phi))=D_{w}(D_{z}F(\phi))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(\phi\cup\{z,w\})-F(\phi\cup\{w\})-F(\phi\cup\{z\})+F(\phi).

Let
F:{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable and square-integrable function with respect to
\eta
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[F(\eta)^{2}]<\infty
. From
[
23
, Exercise 18.8 and Theorem 18.7]
, we have the following bounds on variance

(A.1)

\int(\mathbb{E}[D_{z}F(\eta)])^{2}\text{d}\lambda(z)\leq\BV(F(\eta))\leq\int\mathbb{E}[(D_{z}F(\eta))^{2}]\text{d}\lambda(z).

The upper bound is the well-known Poincaré inequality. We now state a normal approximation result in the Wasserstein metric (
2.10
) for
F(\eta)
.

Theorem A.2
.

(Second-order Poincaré inequality,
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
)

Let
F:{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function with
\mathbb{E}[F(\eta)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[F(\eta)^{2}]<\infty
. Define

\displaystyle T_{1}(F)

\displaystyle:=\left[\int\left(\mathbb{E}[(D^{2}_{z^{1},z^{2}}F)^{2}(D^{2}_{z^{2},z^{3}}F)^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}[(D_{z^{1}}F)^{2}(D_{z^{2}}F)^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\text{d}\lambda^{3}(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})\right]^{1/2},

\displaystyle T_{2}(F)

\displaystyle:=\left[\int\mathbb{E}[(D^{2}_{z^{1},z^{2}}F)^{2}(D^{2}_{z^{1},z^{3}}F)^{2}]\text{d}\lambda^{3}(z^{1},z^{2},z^{3})\right]^{1/2},

\displaystyle T_{3}(F)

\displaystyle:=\int\mathbb{E}[|D_{z}F|^{3}]\text{d}\lambda(z),

where
\eta
has been omitted in the argument of
F
for convenience. Then

(A.2)

d_{W}\left(\frac{F(\eta)}{\BV[F(\eta)]^{1/2}},Z\right)\leq\frac{2T_{1}(F)+T_{2}(F)}{\BV[F(\eta)]}+\frac{T_{3}(F)}{\BV[F(\eta)]^{3/2}}.

We now state similar results for the Binomial process
\beta_{n}
. Recall that
\beta_{n}=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{n}\}
with
n
i.i.d. points sampled according to the distribution
f\sigma
where
f
is a probability density. Let
F:{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function. From the Efron–Stein inequality (
[
21
, Remark 2.5]
), one can derive the following standard variance upper bound for
F(\beta_{n})
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[F(\beta_{n})^{2}]<\infty
.

(A.3)

\BV(F(\beta_{n}))\leq n\int\mathbb{E}[(D_{z}F(\beta_{n-1}))^{2}]f(z)\text{d}\sigma(z).

We now state a normal approximation theorem for functionals of binomial process, after introducing the necessary notation. The theorem is obtained by combining the bounds in
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
(see also
[
21
, Remark 5.4]
). Let
{\mathbf{W}}=(W^{1},\ldots,W^{n})
and let
{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime\prime}
be independent copies of
{\mathbf{W}}
. We say that
{\mathbf{Z}}=(Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{n})
is
a recombination of
{\mathbf{W}}
if
{Z^{i}}\in\{W^{i},(W^{\prime})^{i},(W^{\prime\prime})^{i}\}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. We denote the set of possible recombinations of
{\mathbf{W}}
as
Rec({\mathbf{W}})
. Let
{\mathbf{W}}^{(i)}=(\ldots,W^{i-1},W^{i+1},\ldots),1\leq i\leq n
, be the vector obtained by dropping the
i
th co-ordinate from
{\mathbf{W}}
. Similarly, let
{\mathbf{W}}^{(i,j)}=({\mathbf{W}}^{(i)})^{(j)},1\leq i\neq j\leq n
, be the vector obtained by dropping the
i
th and
j
th co-ordinates from
{\mathbf{W}}
. We abuse notation and set
F(\mathbf{V})=F(\{V^{1},\ldots,V^{m}\})
for any vector
\mathbf{V}=(V^{1},...,V^{m})
. In other words, we view vectors as point sets when applying
F
. For
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
, define

\displaystyle D_{i}F({\mathbf{W}})

\displaystyle:=F({\mathbf{W}})-F({\mathbf{W}}^{(i)}),

\displaystyle D_{i,j}F({\mathbf{W}})=D_{j,i}F({\mathbf{W}})

\displaystyle:=F({\mathbf{W}})-F({\mathbf{W}}^{(i)})-F({\mathbf{W}}^{(j)})+F({\mathbf{W}}^{(i,j)}).

Theorem A.3
.

(
[
42
, Lemma 2.3]
)

Let
F:{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function with
\mathbb{E}[F(\beta_{n})]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[F(\beta_{n})^{2}]<\infty
. Define

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{1}(F)

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}[D_{1,2}F({\mathbf{Y}})\neq 0]D_{1}f({\mathbf{Z}})^{2}D_{2}f({\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime})^{2}\right]:{\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}},{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\in Rec({\mathbf{W}})\right\},

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{2}(F)

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}[D_{1,2}F({\mathbf{Y}})\neq 0,D_{1,3}F({\mathbf{Y}}^{\prime})\neq 0]D_{2}f({\mathbf{Z}})^{2}D_{2}f({\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime})^{2}\right]:{\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}},{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}\in Rec({\mathbf{W}})\right\},

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{3}(F)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[|D_{1}F({\mathbf{W}})|^{4}],

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{4}(F)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[|D_{1}F({\mathbf{W}})|^{3}].

Then, there is a finite constant
C>0
such that

(A.4)

d_{W}\left(\frac{F(\beta_{n})}{\BV[F(\beta_{n})]^{1/2}},Z\right)\leq C\frac{\sqrt{n^{2}T^{\prime}_{1}(F)}+\sqrt{n^{3/2}T^{\prime}_{2}(F)}+\sqrt{nT^{\prime}_{3}(F)}}{\BV(F(\beta_{n}))}+\frac{nT^{\prime}_{4}(F)}{\BV(F(\beta_{n}))^{3/2}}.

While we have only used bounds in terms of the first and second-order difference operators, we note that higher-order difference operators may be defined recursively and are important ingredients in the stochastic analysis of Poisson and binomial processes; see
[

,
30
,
21
]
.

Appendix B
The Möbius geometry of strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains

In Section
3
, we often use the unitary invariance of strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity and induced polyhedra to simplify certain computations. We give here the proofs of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, which are key to such simplifications. In fact, we prove a slight generalization of Lemma
3.2
to demonstrate why the class of polyhedra considered in this paper are invariant under the group of complex affine transformations, but not the full group of linear fractional transformations. We also elaborate on the curvature function
\nu_{D}
that appears as a constant in the geometric estimates. Most of these results are implicitly present in the literature, but we gather them here for easy reference.

The transformation law in Part
(b)
of Lemma
3.2
is a special case of a more general transformation law, for which we need the following definition.

Definition B.1
.

A
linear fractional transformation
(or
Möbius transformation
) on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a map of the form

(B.1)

\mathcal{M}_{\beta}:z\mapsto\left(\frac{b_{10}+b_{11}z_{1}+\cdots b_{1d}z_{d}}{b_{00}+b_{01}z_{1}+\cdots+b_{0d}z_{d}},...,\frac{b_{d0}+b_{d1}z_{1}+\cdots+b_{dd}z_{d}}{b_{00}+b_{01}z_{1}+\cdots+b_{0d}z_{d}}\right),

where
\beta=(b_{jk})_{0\leq j,k\leq d}\in\operatorname{GL}(d+1;{\mathbb{C}})
. The function
(z_{1},...,z_{d})\mapsto b_{00}+b_{01}z_{1}+\cdots+b_{0d}z_{d}
is denoted by
\beta_{0}
, and
\ell_{\beta}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\beta_{0}(z)=0\}
is the set of indeterminacy of
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
.

Any affine transformation on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a linear fractional transformation. Lemma
3.2
is thus a corollary of the following result, which is implicitly present in
[
22
, Proposition 4.1]
and
[
5
, Theorem 3]
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
D
and
\rho
be as in Definition
3.1
.

(a)

For any defining function
\widetilde{\rho}
of
D
,
L_{\widetilde{\rho}}(z,w)=L_{\rho}(z,w)
for all
(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\times bD
.

(b)

Suppose
\beta\in\operatorname{GL}(d+1;{\mathbb{C}})
such that
\overline{D}\cap\ell_{\beta}=\emptyset
. Then, for
\rho^{*}=\rho\circ\mathcal{M}_{\beta}^{-1}
,

(B.2)

L_{\rho}(z,w)=\frac{||\partial\rho^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w))||}{||\partial\rho(w)||}\frac{\beta_{0}(z)}{\beta_{0}(w)}L_{\rho^{*}}(\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(z),\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w)),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\ell_{\beta},w\in bD.

Proof.

To prove
(a)
, note that there exists a positive
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function
h
defined on some neighborhood
U
of
bD
, such that,
\widetilde{\rho}=h\rho
. Thus, since
\rho
vanishes on
bD
,
\partial\widetilde{\rho}(w)=h(w)\partial\rho(w)
for any
w\in bD
, and the claim follows.

For
(b)
, first observe that
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w)=(\mathcal{M}^{1}_{\beta}(w),...,\mathcal{M}^{d}_{\beta}(w))=({\beta_{0}(w)})^{-1}\Big({\boldsymbol{b}+w\cdot\widetilde{\beta}^{\operatorname{T}}}\Big)
, and

(D_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}_{\beta})(w):=\left(\dfrac{\partial{\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\beta}(w)}}{\partial{z_{k}}}\right)_{1\leq j,k\leq d}=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)}\widetilde{\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{b}^{\operatorname{T}}+\widetilde{\beta}\cdot w^{\operatorname{T}}\big)\cdot(b_{01},...,b_{0d}),

where
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{10},...,b_{d0})
and
\widetilde{\beta}
is the principal minor
(b_{jk})_{1\leq j,k\leq d}
. Now, setting
w^{*}=\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w)
and denoting the product of two matrices
A
and
B
by
A\cdot B
, we get that

\displaystyle||\partial\rho(w)||L_{\rho}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial(\rho^{*}\circ\mathcal{M}_{\beta})(w)\cdot(w-z)^{\operatorname{T}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\rho^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w))\cdot(D_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}_{\beta})(w)\cdot(w-z)^{\operatorname{T}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)}\widetilde{\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{b}^{\operatorname{T}}+\widetilde{\beta}\cdot w^{\operatorname{T}}\big)\cdot(b_{01},...,b_{0d})\right)\cdot(w-z)^{\operatorname{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{{\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)}\widetilde{\beta}\cdot(w^{\operatorname{T}}-z^{\operatorname{T}})-\frac{\beta_{0}(w)-\beta_{0}(z)}{\beta_{0}(w)^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{b}^{\operatorname{T}}+\widetilde{\beta}\cdot w^{\operatorname{T}}\big)\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}(w)}(\boldsymbol{b}^{\operatorname{T}}+\widetilde{\beta}\cdot z^{\operatorname{T}})+\frac{\beta_{0}(z)}{\beta_{0}(w)^{2}}\big(\boldsymbol{b}^{\operatorname{T}}+\widetilde{\beta}\cdot w^{\operatorname{T}}\big)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{||\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})||}\frac{\beta_{0}(z)}{\beta_{0}(w)}\left<\frac{\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})}{||\partial\rho^{*}(w^{*})||},\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(w)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(z)\right>,

which is precisely (
B.2
).
∎

Next, as an analogue of the Gaussian curvature for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the determinant of the (self-adjoint) linear operator
\pi\circ S:\mathcal{H}_{w}M\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{w}M
, where
S:\mathcal{T}_{w}M\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{w}M
is the shape operator, and
\pi:\mathcal{T}_{w}M\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{w}M
is the orthogonal projection with respect to the standard Euclidean inner product on
\mathcal{T}_{w}M
(viewed as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
). A more computationally convenient definition of this second-order quantity is as follows.

Definition B.3
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\rho
be as above. For any
w\in bD
, we define the
complex-restricted curvature
of
bD
at
w
as

\nu_{D}(w)=(-1)^{d+1}\frac{\det\mathcal{Q}[\rho](w)}{||\nabla\rho(w)||^{2d+2}},

where
\mathcal{Q}[\rho]
is the
(2d+2)\times(2d+2)
-matrix function

\mathcal{Q}[\rho]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c | c|c|c}0&0&\rho_{k}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&\rho_{\,\overline{k}}\\
\hline\cr\rho_{j}&0&\rho_{j,k}&\rho_{j,\overline{k}}\\
\hline\cr 0&\rho_{\,\overline{j}}&\rho_{\,\overline{j},k}&\rho_{\,\overline{j},\overline{k}}\end{array}\right)_{1\leq j,k\leq d},

where
\rho_{j}=\frac{\partial{\rho}}{\partial{z_{j}}}
,
\rho_{j,\overline{k}}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\rho}}{\partial{z_{j}}\text{ }\partial{\overline{z}_{k}}}
, for
1\leq j,k\leq d
.

It is easy to check that
\nu_{D}
is independent of the choice of defining function, a positive function for strongly
{\mathbb{C}}
-convex domains, and invariant under unitary transformations; see
[
16
]
. We now prove Lemma
3.3
, which employs a standard trick in the local study of smooth hypersurfaces; see
[
44
, Lemma 4.1]
, and the comments preceding Remark 8 in
[
2
]
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.
Recall that
A\cdot B
denotes the product of two matrices
A
and
B
. First, we choose an
\varepsilon_{D}>0
so that
B_{w}(\varepsilon_{D})\cap bD
is a graph over the tangent space at
w
for all
w\in bD
. Now, after a unitary transformation, we may assume that
w=0
,
\mathcal{T}_{0}bD=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\operatorname{Im}z_{d}=0\}
and
\hat{\eta}(0)=(0,...,0,-i)
. Then,
B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D})\cap D=\{z\in B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D}):\rho(z)<0\}
, where

\rho(z)=-y_{d}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d-1}a_{j,k}z_{j}\overline{z}_{k}+\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d-1}b_{j,k}z_{j}z_{k}+\operatorname{Im}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}c_{j}z_{j}x_{d}+ex_{d}^{2}+O||(z^{\prime},x_{d})||^{3},

for some
a_{j,k},b_{j,k},c_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
j,k=1,...,d-1
,
e\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

•

A=(a_{j,k})
is Hermitian,

•

B=(b_{j,k})
is symmetric,

•

|\operatorname{Re}(z^{\prime}\cdot B\cdot z^{\prime\,{\operatorname{T}}})|<\overline{z}^{\prime}\cdot A\cdot z^{\prime\,{\operatorname{T}}}
for all nonzero
z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d-1}
.

Now, let
U
be a unitary matrix in
\operatorname{GL}(d-1;{\mathbb{C}})
such that
U^{*}\cdot A\cdot U
is diagonal, and the entries of the symmetric matrix
U^{\operatorname{T}}\cdot B\cdot U
are real. Then, after applying the unitary transformation
U_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}U^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}_{d-1\times 1}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, we obtain that
B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D})\cap U_{1}(\Omega)=\{z\in B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D}):\rho_{1}(z)<0\}
for

\rho_{1}(z)=-y_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\alpha_{j}|z_{j}|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d-1}\widetilde{b}_{j,k}z_{j}z_{k}+\operatorname{Im}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\widetilde{c}_{j}z_{j}u_{d}+eu_{d}^{2}+O||(z^{\prime},x_{d})||^{3},

where
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d-1}>0
are the eigenvalues of
A
,
\widetilde{B}=(\widetilde{b}_{j,k})
is a real symmetric matrix, and
\widetilde{c}=(\widetilde{c}_{1},...,\widetilde{c}_{d-1})=(c_{1},...,c_{d-1})\cdot~U
. Finally, let
R
be a real orthogonal matrix that diagonalizes
\widetilde{B}
. Letting
U_{w}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}R^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}_{d-1\times 1}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\cdot U_{1}
, we get that
B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D})\cap U_{w}(\Omega)=\{\rho_{2}(z)<0\}
for

\rho_{2}(z)=-y_{d}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d-1}(\alpha_{j}|z_{j}|^{2}+\beta_{j}(\operatorname{Re}z_{j}^{2}))+\operatorname{Im}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\gamma_{j}z_{j}x_{d}+ex_{d}^{2}+O||(z^{\prime},x_{d})||^{3},

where
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{d-1}
are the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{B}
, and
(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{d-1})=\widetilde{c}\cdot R
. Since unitary transformations preserve strong
{\mathbb{C}}
-convexity, we have that
0\leq\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j}
for all
j=1,...,d-1
.

Now, by the unitary invariance of
\nu_{D}
,
\nu_{D}(w)=\nu_{B_{0}(\varepsilon_{D})\cap U_{w}(D)}(0)
, and a direct computation shows that the latter is equal to
\frac{1}{16}\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}(\alpha_{j}^{2}-\beta_{j}^{2})
.
∎

Appendix C
Area and volumes estimates for model domains

\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\lambda\big(C^{D_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}(0;\delta)\big)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\alpha_{j}^{-1}\right)\lambda\big(C^{D_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}(0;\delta)\big),

\displaystyle\left|\sigma(S^{D_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}(0;\delta))-\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\alpha_{j}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{2d-1}\big(S^{D_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}(0;\delta)\big)\right|\leq\delta\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\alpha_{j}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{2d-1}\big(S^{D_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}}(0;\delta)\big).

∎

Theorem C.2
.

Let
D_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
be as above,
\delta>0
, and
t\in[0,1)
.Then, there is a
C_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}>0
such that for
\delta<\frac{1}{2\max_{j}\alpha_{j}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}h_{d}(t)\delta^{d}\leq\sigma(G_{t\delta}(0;\delta))\leq\frac{\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}h_{d}(t)\delta^{d}+C_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\delta^{d+1},

where
h_{d}(t)=2\int_{0}^{\sin^{-1}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}(\cos\theta-t)^{d-1}\cos\theta\,d\theta
.

Proof.

Since
G_{t\delta}(0;\delta)\subset bD
, it is the graph of
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
over
\widetilde{G}_{t\delta}(0;\delta)
, which is given by

\displaystyle\left\{(z^{\prime},x_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:(t\delta+q_{1}(z^{\prime}))^{2}+\left(x_{d}+q_{2}(z^{\prime})\right)^{2}<\delta^{2}(1+4q_{3}(z^{\prime}))\right\},

where
q_{1}(z^{\prime})~=~\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\left(\alpha_{j}|z_{j}|^{2}+\beta_{j}\operatorname{Re}\overline{z}_{j}^{2}\right)
,
q_{2}(z^{\prime})=2\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\beta_{j}\operatorname{Im}z_{j}^{2}
, and
q_{3}(z^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}|\alpha_{j}z_{j}+\beta_{j}\overline{z}_{j}|^{2}
. Note that
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\geq 0
.

Now, setting
A=\max_{j}\alpha_{j}
and recalling that
\beta_{j}<\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,...,d-1
, we have that
q_{3}(w^{\prime})\leq 2Aq_{1}(w^{\prime})
. Thus, if
z\in G_{t\delta}(0;\delta)
,

q_{3}(z^{\prime})^{2}\leq 4A^{2}\delta^{2}(1+4q_{3}(z^{\prime})),

i.e.,
q_{3}(z^{\prime})\leq 2A\delta+16A^{2}\delta^{2}
. Thus, for
\delta<1/(2A)
,
K_{t\delta,\delta}\subseteq\widetilde{G}_{t\delta}(0;\delta)\subseteq K_{t\delta,\delta(1+8A\delta)}
,
where,

K_{\varepsilon,\eta}=\left\{(z^{\prime},x_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:(\varepsilon+q_{1}(z^{\prime}))^{2}+\left(x_{d}+q_{2}(z^{\prime})\right)^{2}<\eta^{2}\right\},\quad 0<\varepsilon<\eta.

Consequently, for
\delta<1/(2A)
,

(C.6)

\int\limits_{K_{t\delta,\delta}}d\lambda_{2d-1}(z^{\prime},x_{d})\leq\int\limits_{\widetilde{G}_{t\delta}(0;\delta)}\sqrt{1+4q_{3}(w^{\prime})}\,d\lambda_{2d-1}(z^{\prime},x_{d})\leq\int\limits_{K_{t\delta,\delta+8A\delta^{2}}}(1+8A\delta)d\lambda_{2d-1}(z^{\prime},x_{d}),

where the term in the center is
\sigma\big(G_{t\delta}(0;\delta)\big)
. It now suffices to compute
\lambda_{2d-1}(K_{\varepsilon,\eta}\big)
. Now, setting

\xi_{j}=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}}\,\operatorname{Re}z_{j}+i\sqrt{\alpha_{j}-\beta_{j}}\,\operatorname{Im}z_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq d-1
, and
\tau=x_{d}+2\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\beta_{j}\operatorname{Im}w_{j}^{2}
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda_{2d-1}(K_{\varepsilon,\eta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\lambda_{2d-1}\left\{(\xi,\tau)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}:(\varepsilon+||\xi||^{2})^{2}+\tau^{2}<\eta^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}\int\limits_{0}^{\sqrt{\eta^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}}(\sqrt{\eta^{2}-\tau^{2}}-\varepsilon)^{d-1}\,d\tau=\frac{\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}\,\eta^{d}h_{d}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\eta}\right),

where
h_{d}(t)=2\int_{0}^{\sin^{-1}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}(\cos\theta-t)^{d-1}\cos\theta\,d\theta
.

A few comments about
h_{d}
are in order. Note that
h_{1}(t)=2\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
,
t\in[0,1]
. When
d\geq 2
,
h_{d}
is a decreasing Lipschitz function on
[0,1]
with
h_{d}(1)=0
. This follows from observing that
h^{\prime}_{d}=-(d-1)h_{d-1}(t)
, which is negative and bounded (in absolute value) on
(0,1)
. Thus, for any
t\in(0,1)
, there is an
r\in(t(1+8A\delta)^{-1},t)
such that

h_{d}\left(\frac{t\delta}{\delta+8A\delta^{2}}\right)=h_{d}(t)-h_{d}^{\prime}(r)\frac{8At\delta}{1+8A\delta}\leq h_{d}(t)+(d-1)h_{d-1}(0)\frac{8At\delta}{1+8A\delta}.

Combining this with (
C.6
) gives that

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}h_{d}(t)\delta^{d}\leq\sigma\big(G_{t\delta}(0;\delta)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{2d-2}}{\sqrt{v_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}}\delta^{d}(1+8A\delta)^{d}\left(h_{d}(t)+(d-1)h_{d-1}(0)\frac{8At\delta}{1+8A\delta}\right),

which yields our claim.
∎
