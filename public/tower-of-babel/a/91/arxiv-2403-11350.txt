Robustness of data-driven approaches in limited angle tomography
Yiran Wang and Yimin Zhong

Abstract
. The limited angle Radon transform is notoriously difficult to invert due to its ill-posedness. In this work, we give a mathematical explanation that data-driven approaches can stably reconstruct more information compared to traditional methods like filtered backprojection. In addition, we use experiments based on the U-Net neural network to validate our theory.



1.
Introduction

Consider the limited angle Radon transform on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Let
(x_{1},x_{2})
be the coordinate for
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. For
\delta\in(0,\pi/2),
let
{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}=\{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}:|\theta|\leq\delta\}.
We use the parallel beam parametrization of the Radon transform. Let
v\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}
and
v^{\perp}
be the unit vector orthogonal to
v
so that
\{v,v^{\perp}\}
has the same orientation as the coordinate frame. The limited angle Radon transform of a function
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is

(1)

R_{\delta}f(p,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\chi_{\delta}(v)f(pv+tv^{\perp})dt,\quad v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1},p\in{\mathbb{R}}

where
\chi_{\delta}
is the characteristic function of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}.
For simplicity, we assume that
f
is supported in the unit disk
{\mathcal{B}}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x|<1\}
where
|\cdot|
stands for the Euclidean norm. It is a classical result that
R_{\delta}
is injective on compactly supported distributions, for instance,
[
13
,
21
]
. However,
the inverse problem of recovering
f
from
R_{\delta}f
is severely ill-posed. This was demonstrated from the behavior of the singular values in
[
4
]
. On the microlocal level, it is clear that some information of
f
is lost. In particular, Quinto in
[
15
]
gave a microlocal description of the singularities of
f
that can be recovered, called “visible singularities”, and those that cannot, called “invisible singularities”, see Section 2 for more details. Due to the ill-posedness, traditional methods such as filtered back-projection or iterative methods for solving a variational regularized problem (e.g., a sparsity enforcing approach solved with ISTA, see e.g.
[
3
,
5
]
) often produce artifacts in the reconstruction, and it is very challenging to recover the invisible information.

Recently, there have been increasing efforts in applying data-driven approaches such as deep neural networks to limited angle tomography. Among the already vast literature, we mention
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
17
]
which are more relevant to our work, and
[
14
, Section II.B]
for more references. Multiple numerical experiments reported that data-driven approaches can significantly reduce the artifact phenomena and even learn the invisible singularities. The success seems difficult to understand from the existing mathematical theory. Also, the robustness of data-driven approaches is a concern, see for example
[
12
]
. It is suspected that data-driven approaches should be tailored to specific tasks, not for general image reconstruction. But a precise understanding is still missing.

The goal of this paper is to provide a mathematical explanation. It is useful to interpret the instability of the limited angle Radon transform from the following viewpoint: there is no stability estimate of the form

(2)

\|f\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq C(s,t)\|R_{\delta}f\|_{H^{s+t}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})}

for any
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, see Section VI.2 of
[
13
]
. This is because, roughly speaking, after taking the limited angle Radon transform, the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
of
f
is lost in a conic region (corresponding to the missing angles), see Figure
1
. Recovery of
\hat{f}
in the rest of the Fourier space (
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}
in Figure
1
) however is stable, see Theorem
3.1
. This result is sharp. For data-driven approaches, we consider
f
in a training data set
{\mathcal{D}}
and show that
\hat{f}
can be stably recovered in a larger cone (
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta+\epsilon}
in Figure
1
), see Theorem
3.2
. The size of the cone depends on
{\mathcal{D}}
and it is possible to cover the whole Fourier space for a certain
{\mathcal{D}}
. This explains the phenomena of “learning the invisible”. We remark that our theory does not rely on the specific structure of the neural network, at least for supervised learning. In Section
4
, we use a simple U-Net to validate our theory. The concluding summary is offered in Section
5
.

Figure 1.
Illustration of the increased stability in the Fourier space. Traditional methods can recover
\hat{f}
in the cone
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}
(defined in Section
3
). Data-driven approaches can recover
\hat{f}
in a larger cone
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta+\epsilon}
. The size of the cone depends on the specific training set.

2.
The visible and invisible singularities

In
[
16
]
, Quinto described some principles for recovering features of an object from limited X-ray CT. Roughly speaking, if a boundary of a feature of the body is tangent to a line in a limited data set, then that boundary should be easy to reconstruct (called visible boundaries). Otherwise, the boundary should be difficult to reconstruct from the limited data (i.e. invisible boundaries). We use the FBP reconstruction to demonstrate the principle.

Let
\theta\in(-\delta,\delta)
and define the limited angle back-projection for
R_{\delta}
as

R^{*}_{\delta}g(x)=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}g(v(\theta),x\cdot v(\theta))d\theta.

Let
\Lambda
be the Riesz potential

\Lambda g(v,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\tau(p-s)}|\tau|g(v,s)dsd\tau.

Then we consider the following FBP for limited angle transform

(3)

L_{\delta}f=\frac{1}{4\pi}R_{\delta}^{*}\Lambda R_{\delta}f.

Note that this is not an inversion formula. We describe what can be reconstructed from this formula.

We introduce some notions from microlocal analysis. Let
f
be a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\xi_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\{0\}
. By definition,
f
is smooth at
x_{0}
in direction
\xi_{0}
if there is a smooth function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
with
\phi(x_{0})\neq 0
and an open cone
{\mathscr{V}}
containing
\xi_{0}
such that for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is
C_{N}
such that

|\widehat{\phi f}(\xi)|\leq C_{N}(1+|\xi|)^{-N},\text{ for all }\xi\in{\mathscr{V}},

where
\hat{}
denotes the Fourier transform in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

If
f
is not smooth at
x_{0}
in direction
\xi_{0}
, then
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\text{WF}(f)
the wavefront set of
f
. Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\text{WF}(f).
We say
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is a
visible singularity
of
f
(for
L_{\delta}
) if
\xi_{0}
is parallel to some
v\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}
. We say
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is an
invisible singularity
of
f
if
\xi_{0}
is not parallel for any
v\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}
. We have

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 2 of
[
16
]
)
.

Let
f
be a function of compact support and
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\text{WF}(f)
.

(1)

If
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is a visible singularity of
f
then
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in\text{WF}(L_{\delta}f)
.

(2)

If
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is an invisible singularity of
f
then
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\notin\text{WF}(L_{\delta}f)
.

We remark that it is possible to obtain the more precise microlocal Sobolev regularity of the visible singularities, see Theorem 3.1 of
[
16
]
.
Within the microlocal framework, one can also explain the artifact phenomena. For our setup, the artifact can occur on lines parallel to
v\in\partial{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}
and the lines are tangent to the boundary of some feature in the object, see Principle 2 of
[
15
]
.

3.
The stable reconstruction

We establish a quantitative estimate for recovering visible singularities. Our starting point is the simple injectivity proof for
R_{\delta}
, see
[
13
, Theorem 3.4]
.
The transform
R_{\delta}f(p,v)
is a function defined on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}.
We take the Fourier transform of
R_{\delta}f
in
p
to get

(4)

\displaystyle\widehat{R_{\delta}f}(\tau,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\imath p\tau}\chi_{\delta}(v)f(pv+tv^{\perp})dtdp

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\imath(pv+tv^{\perp})\cdot\tau v}\chi_{\delta}(v)f(pv+tv^{\perp})dtdp=\hat{f}(\tau v)\chi_{\delta}(v).

Here,
\imath^{2}=-1
. Because
R_{\delta}f(p,v)=0
, we get
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
for
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
parallel to
v
. Thus
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
in a cone
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}=\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\xi/|\xi|\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\delta}\}
with non-empty interior for
\delta>0
. Because
\hat{f}
is analytic in
\xi
, we conclude that
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.
So
f=0
after taking the inverse Fourier transform.

We can further derive some stability estimates. Let
\widetilde{\chi}_{\delta}
be the characteristic function of
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Note that
\widetilde{\chi}_{\delta}(\xi)=\chi_{\delta}(\xi/|\xi|),\xi\neq 0
. For simplicity, we use the same notation
\chi_{\delta}
for both characteristic functions. It should be clear from the context which one we are referring to. Also, we use
\chi_{\delta}(D)
for the Fourier multiplier of
\chi_{\delta}(\xi)
defined by
\chi_{\delta}(D)f=(\chi_{\delta}\hat{f})^{\vee}
where
∨
denotes the inverse Fourier transform.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
\delta\in(0,\pi/2]
and
f\in H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}),
we have

(5)

\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq\|R_{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})}.

Proof.

Applying Plancherel’s theorem and using (
4
), we get

(6)

\displaystyle\|R_{\delta}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})}=

\displaystyle\|\widehat{R_{\delta}f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})}=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\chi_{\delta}(v)|\hat{f}(\tau v)|^{2}d\tau dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}}|\xi|^{-1}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\geq\|\chi_{\delta}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}^{2}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

This theorem is a quantitative version of Theorem
2.1
on stably recovering visible singularities of
f
. It is also clear that the stability estimate (
2
) fails because of
(1-\chi_{\delta}(D))f
which sits in the kernel of
R_{\delta}.

We seek improvement of the estimate to include invisible singularities. For
\epsilon>0
small we define
\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)
as a Fourier multiplier of
\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\xi),\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. For
N\geq 0
, we define

(7)

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{B}}):\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leq N\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}}\}.\end{gathered}

Here, we recall that
{\mathcal{B}}
is the unit disk in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.
Observe that
L^{2}({\mathcal{B}})=\cup_{N\geq 0}{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}.

The following theorem says that for functions in
{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}
, the stability estimate (
5
) can be improved.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
N\geq 0
, there is
\epsilon>0
small such that

(8)

\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq 2\|R_{\delta}f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})}

for all
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}.

Proof.

We decompose

(9)

R_{\delta}f=R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f+R_{\delta}(1-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D))f.

Note that the second term on the right vanishes because
(1-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D))f
is in the kernel of
R_{\delta}.
So we estimate the first term.
Using (
4
), we have

(10)

\displaystyle R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f(p,v)-R_{\delta+\epsilon}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f(p,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\imath p\tau}(\chi_{\delta}(v)-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(v))\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\tau v)\hat{f}(\tau v)d\tau.

Note that
|\chi_{\delta}(v)-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(v)|
is bounded by one and supported in a set of
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
with measure
2\epsilon
. Also, the left-hand side of (
10
) is compactly supported in
p
. Denote
U_{\lambda}=\{|\tau|\leq\lambda\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, where we choose
\lambda=(2\pi\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then using Plancherel’s theorem again, we get

(11)

\displaystyle\|R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f-R_{\delta+\epsilon}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}|(\chi_{\delta}(v)-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(v))\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\tau v)\hat{f}(\tau v)|^{2}dvd\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{U_{\lambda}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}|(\chi_{\delta}(v)-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(v))\hat{f}(\tau v)|^{2}dvd\tau+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\pi\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\int_{U_{\lambda}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}(\chi_{\delta}(v)-\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(v))^{2}dvd\tau+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(4\pi\epsilon\lambda+\frac{1}{\lambda})\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 4N^{2}\sqrt{\pi\epsilon}\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|^{2}_{H^{-1/2}}.

In the derivation, we used the fact that
\|\hat{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\pi}\|h\|_{L^{2}}
for
h
compactly supported in
\mathcal{B}
and the inequality

\displaystyle\int_{|\tau|>\lambda}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}|\hat{f}(\tau v)|^{2}dvd\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{|\tau|>\lambda}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}|\tau||\hat{f}(\tau v)|^{2}dvd\tau\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We can apply Theorem
3.1
to derive that

\displaystyle\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{\delta+\epsilon}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}+\|R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f-R_{\delta+\epsilon}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}}+2N(\pi\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}}.

For
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
2N(\pi\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{4}}<\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain

(12)

\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq 2\|R_{\delta}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1})},

which finishes the proof.
∎

We remark that the choices of the Sobolev norms in the condition in (
7
) are for simplicity only. The analysis can be adapted to other Sobolev spaces by slightly modifying the proofs of Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
. For general data-driven methods, if there exists an appropriate pair of
(N,\epsilon)
such that the whole image data set (including training and testing)
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}\subset H^{-1/2}
, then for any limited angle Radon transform measurement
F^{\ast}=R_{\delta}f^{\ast}+\eta
,
f^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{D}}
and
\eta
is the additive noise, the optimization

(13)

\min_{{f}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{N,\epsilon}}\|R_{\delta}f-F^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}}

provides a stable reconstruction for
\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f^{\ast}
in
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, which reveals the information in a wider cone
\mathcal{K}_{\delta+\epsilon}\supset\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
. Regarding the neural network approaches, there are extensive experiments
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
8
,
11
]
with different structures (e.g. U-Net
[
18
]
, It-Net
[
6
]
). Typically, the training data set is generated from a low dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{D}_{N,\epsilon}
and the neural network
\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
tries to learn the inversion operator by finding the optimal weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
that minimizes the loss function

(14)

\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}F_{j})-f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

over the training data set
\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and the corresponding measurements
\{F_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. The operator
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
is a preprocessing operator (e.g. FBP or a learnable layer). Suppose the neural network is well trained on this data set so that
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
is a good approximation of
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}R_{\delta}
. In principle, this would happen when the network architecture has the universal approximation property (see e.g.
[
22
]
for convolutional neural networks, and
[
10
]
for feedforward networks), and the property is achieved through the training. Then the learned inversion operator
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
would potentially satisfy the improved stability estimate (
8
) which can explain the stable reconstruction of certain invisible singularities observed in the experimental studies
[
12
]
.

4.3.
The robustness

We consider the violation of the condition in (
7
). For
a>0
, we select the characteristic function for the disk centered at
(0,0)
with radius
a>0
,

f_{a}(x)=1\text{ for }|x|<a\text{ and }f_{a}(x)=0\text{ for }|x|\geq a.

Taking the Fourier transform in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and using polar coordinates, we get

\begin{gathered}\hat{f}_{a}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}\int_{0}^{a}e^{-ir\omega\cdot\xi}rdrd\omega=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-ir\omega\cdot a\xi}a^{2}rdrd\omega=a^{2}\hat{f}_{1}(a\xi).\end{gathered}

Below, we use homogeneous Sobolev spaces to compute

\begin{gathered}\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f_{a}\|_{\dot{H}^{-1/2}}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\xi|^{-1}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\xi)^{2}|\hat{f}_{a}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\\
=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\xi|^{-1}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\xi)^{2}a^{4}|\hat{f}_{1}(a\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\xi/a|^{-1}\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(\xi/a)^{2}a^{2}|\hat{f}_{1}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.\end{gathered}

We see that for
a
large,

\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f_{a}\|_{\dot{H}^{-1/2}}\simeq a^{3/2}\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f_{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{-1/2}}.

Similarly, we have
\|f_{a}\|_{L^{2}}\simeq a\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}}
. If
\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}}=C_{0}\|\chi_{\delta+\epsilon}(D)f_{1}\|_{H^{-1/2}}
is true for some
C_{0}>0
, then for
f_{a}
, the condition in (
7
) will be violated when
a
is large.
We show the test result for this example in Figure
7
. As
a
increases, we start to see some instability in the network reconstruction, and eventually, the artifacts appear around the missing angles, which agrees with our theory.

4.4.
Further discussions

The examples presented in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
indicate that the trained neural network pre-composed with the FBP could be a good approximation of
R_{\delta}^{-1}
. However, we do not claim that every trained network shares the same property. To prevent overfitting, we stop the training after a small number of iterations, see Figure
5
. Although training the network for a longer time may keep training errors decreasing, the test errors on the test set and general images in Section
4.3
usually worsen. We also emphasize that the choice of activation function is essential in the U-Net. First, the activation has to be an affine mapping. Without the nonlinearity in the activations, the effect of the convolution layers can be viewed as a single convolution kernel
\mathcal{K}
. Assuming the noiseless data that
F_{j}=R_{\delta}f_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,n
, then using Plancherel’s theorem, the loss function becomes

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{K}(x-y)\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}F_{j}(y)dy-f_{j}(x)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(\zeta)\widehat{f_{j}}(\zeta)\chi_{\delta}(\zeta)-\widehat{f_{j}}(\zeta)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

It implies that the minimization of the loss function cannot recover any information outside the cone
\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
, see Figure
10
for the reconstructions for the same examples as Figure
6
but using U-Net without nonlinear activation functions.

To demonstrate the potential effectiveness of the activation function, we consider a simplified shallow U-Net that skips all downsampling and upsampling blocks (only consists of the bottleneck and output layer). For analysis purposes, we minimized the loss function by gradient flow.

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}g:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\sigma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}\ast g\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{a}_{i}\sigma\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}\ast g\right),

where
\widetilde{a}_{i}:=\sqrt{n}a_{i}
,
\sigma(x)=\max(0,x)+\alpha\min(0,x)
with typical value
\alpha=10^{-2}
, and each
K_{ij}
is a convolutional kernel function. The loss function becomes

(17)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}R_{\delta}f(x)-f(x)|^{2}dx,

where
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{2}
denotes the support of the data set
\mathcal{D}
. Initially, the
band-limited
kernel functions
\{K_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
are generated from the random field

K(x,\omega)=\sigma_{K}\sum_{|\zeta|\leq B}Z_{\zeta}(\omega)e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot x},

where
B
denotes a sufficiently wide band limit,
\sigma_{K}
is a configuration constant, and
Z_{\zeta}(\omega)=\overline{Z_{-\zeta}(\omega)}
are i.i.d standard normal variables (real and imaginary parts) on the half-plane, except
|\zeta|=0
. In the regime of the neural tangent kernel (NTK)
[
19
,
20
]
that
n\to\infty
, the kernel functions
K_{ij}
can be regarded as frozen and the only trainable parameters become
\{\widetilde{a}_{i}\}_{i=1,\cdots,n}
. For any
f\in\mathcal{D}
,

(18)

\displaystyle K(x,\omega)\ast\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}R_{\delta}f

\displaystyle=\sigma_{K}\sum_{|\zeta|\leq B}Z_{\zeta}(\omega)e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot x}\ast\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}R_{\delta}f

\displaystyle=\sigma_{K}\sum_{|\zeta|\leq B}Z_{\zeta}(\omega)\widehat{f}(\zeta)\chi_{\delta}(\zeta)e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot x},

which defines another random field, denoted by
K_{\delta}(x,f,\omega)
, with correlation function

(19)

\rho_{\delta}(x-y,f,h)=\frac{1}{\|f\|_{\delta}\|h\|_{\delta}}\sum_{|\zeta|\leq B,\zeta\in\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}\widehat{f}(\zeta)\overline{\widehat{h}(\zeta)}e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot(x-y)},

where
\|f\|_{\delta}:=\sqrt{\sum_{\zeta\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\mathcal{K}_{\delta}}|\widehat{f}(\zeta)|^{2}}
.
Without the activation function, this correlation has a large kernel space containing Fourier modes
e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot x}
that
\zeta\notin\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
.
After applying the nonlinear activation function
\sigma
, we denote
\widetilde{\rho}_{\delta}
as the new correlation function for the transformed field
\sigma(K_{\delta}(x,f,\omega))
, which satisfies

(20)

\widetilde{\rho}_{\delta}:=\rho_{\delta}+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{\pi(1+\alpha^{2})}(\sqrt{1-\rho_{\delta}^{2}}-\rho_{\delta}\cos^{-1}(\rho_{\delta})).

Then, the gradient flow for the loss function
\mathcal{L}
becomes

(21)

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt}=-\frac{2\sigma_{K}^{2}}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{f,h\in\mathcal{D}}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}T_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(x)\widetilde{\rho}_{\delta}(x-y,f,h)T_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}h(y)dxdy,

where
T_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f=\|f\|_{\delta}(N_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f-f)
. Moreover, if we decompose the correlation function into Fourier basis

(22)

\widetilde{\rho}_{\delta}(x-y,f,h)=\sum_{\zeta\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}C_{\zeta}(f,h)e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot(x-y)},

then if each
|\mathcal{D}|\times|\mathcal{D}|
matrix
C_{\zeta}(f,h)
,
f,h\in\mathcal{D}
is strictly positive definite, then the loss function
\mathcal{L}
will eventually converge to zero.

As a special example, if the data set
\mathcal{D}
satisfies that
\rho_{\delta}(s,f,h)\equiv 0
for any pair
f\neq h\in\mathcal{D}
, then we only have to focus on the diagonal part
\rho_{\delta}(s,f,f)
. When
|s|\ll 1
, we can expand
\rho_{\delta}(s,f,f)=1-\frac{A}{2}s^{2}+\mathcal{O}(s^{4})
for a constant
A>0
depending on
f
, which means

(23)

\widetilde{\rho}_{\delta}(s,f,f)={\rho}_{\delta}(s,f,f)+\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{\pi(1+\alpha^{2})}\frac{A^{3/2}}{3}|s|s^{2}+\mathcal{O}(s^{4}).

The new kernel function finds a mild non-smoothness at
s=0
, which implies that if
|\zeta|
is sufficiently large, the Fourier modes
e^{i2\pi\zeta\cdot x}
cannot stay inside the kernel space. Hence, the nonlinear activation enables the network to fit some information beyond the cone
\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
under the NTK setting. Nevertheless, in this regime, the network often has severe overfitting, hence the early stopping strategy is necessary. The discussion of the training mechanism in a more general setting is beyond the scope of this work.

5.
Conclusion

In this note, we provided a mathematical explanation of the robustness of data-driven approaches based on deep learning for limited-angle tomography. We showed that prior conditions on the training data set can lead to a stable reconstruction of some invisible singularities. We also tested the theory using a simple U-Net with a pre-composed FBP. For data sets
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}_{N,\epsilon}
(see (
7
)) with a relatively small
N
(e.g. the public data set from
[
3
]
), the numerical experiments demonstrate stable reconstruction beyond the visible angle. For data sets with growing
N
(e.g. Figure
7
), the reconstructed images suffer severely from the artifacts. These numerical experiments agree with our analysis in Section 3.
