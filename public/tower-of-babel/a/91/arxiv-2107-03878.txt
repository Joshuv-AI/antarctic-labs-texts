BMS 3 Mechanics and the Black Hole Interior
Marc Geiller, Etera R. Livine, Francesco Sartini

Abstract
The spacetime in the interior of a black hole can be described by an homogeneous line element, for which the Einstein–Hilbert action reduces to a one-dimensional mechanical model. We have shown in [ 1 ] that this model exhibits a symmetry under the ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) -dimensional Poincaré group. Here we explain how this can be understood as a broken infinite-dimensional BMS 3 symmetry. This is done by reinterpreting the action for the model as a geometric action for BMS 3 , where the configuration space variables are elements of the algebra 𝔟 ​ 𝔪 ​ 𝔰 3 \mathfrak{bms}_{3} and the equations of motion transform as coadjoint vectors. The Poincaré subgroup then arises as the stabilizer of the vacuum orbit. This symmetry breaking is analogous to what happens with the Schwarzian action in AdS 2 JT gravity, although in the present case there is no direct interpretation in terms of boundary symmetries. This observation, together with the fact that other lower-dimensional gravitational models (such as the BTZ black hole) possess the same broken BMS 3 symmetries, provides yet another illustration of the ubiquitous role played by this group.

2  The black hole interior spacetime

In this first section we present the classical model underlying our construction, namely the homogeneous black hole interior spacetime. We start by explaining the choice of lapse and clock, and analyse the resulting Hamiltonian. We then present the symmetry reduced action in proper time and recall how the resulting equations of motion reconstruct the black hole interior spacetime.

Homogeneous metric, choice of time and Hamiltonian

The region inside the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole of mass
M
is described by the Kantowski–Sachs metric, with the homogeneous though anisotropic line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\left(\frac{2M}{T}-1\right)^{-1}\mathrm{d}T^{2}+\left(\frac{2M}{T}-1\right)\mathrm{d}r^{2}+T^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-N(t)^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{8V_{2}(t)}{V_{1}(t)}\mathrm{d}x^{2}+V_{1}(t)\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}\,,

(2.2)

playing the role of an IR cutoff. Restricting the integration range to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{EH}}^{(t)}[N,V_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi G}\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,R

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\mathrm{d}t\,L_{0}\left[\frac{V_{2}(4N^{2}V_{1}+V_{1}^{\prime 2})-2V_{1}V_{1}^{\prime}V_{2}^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{2}N(V_{1}^{3}V_{2})^{1/2}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}N}\big(\sqrt{V_{1}V_{2}}\big)^{\prime}\right)\right]\,,

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2}(\tau-\tau_{0})^{2}\,,

Symmetries and transformations of the action

The previous work

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{lcl}V_{1}&\mapsto&\widetilde{V}_{1}=V_{1}\,,\\

sector we obtain a symmetry if
g
has a vanishing third derivative, meaning that it is a second degree polynomial. This shows that the subgroup of (
3.1
) which describes the symmetries of (
2.8
) is given by
\text{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, which is the
2+1
Poincaré group
[
1
]
.

When the transformation
\text{Ad}_{f,g}
does not belong to the Poincaré subgroup, we see that the general BMS transformation produces terms proportional to the dynamical variables
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
in the action. As explained in appendix
C
, terms in
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
also appear when introducing a cosmological constant or a scalar field in the model. While it is tempting to interpret the BMS transformation as producing a cosmological constant term or a scalar field, this interpretation does not strictly hold since, as can be seen on (
C.2
) a scalar field comes with specific time derivatives which cannot be obtained from
f
and
g
in (
3.15
). However, in the case of pure supertranslations
g
(i.e. with
f=\mathbb{I})
one can really interpret the term
g^{(3)}V_{1}
as the contribution of a cosmological constant. The action of a further finite supertranslation then shifts the value of this cosmological constant.

As an alternative to this interpretation in terms of a cosmological constant and a scalar field, we can also create a fully BMS invariant action by including extra fields in the theory. These have to transform in a way which compensates the Schwarzian and the
g^{(3)}
produces by a finite BMS transformation. This is achieved by the invariant action

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{inv}}[V_{i},\Phi,\Psi]\coloneqq{\mathcal{S}}_{0}[V_{i}]+\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[(V_{2}+\dot{\Psi}V_{1}-\Psi\dot{V}_{1})\text{Sch}[\Phi]+V_{1}\Psi^{(3)}\right],

(3.16)

provided the new fields
\Phi
and
\Psi
(which can be seen as Stückelberg fields for the BMS symmetry) transform as

\displaystyle\text{Ad}_{f,g}:\,

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Phi&\mapsto&\widetilde{\Phi}=\Phi\circ f^{-1}\,,\\
\Psi&\mapsto&\widetilde{\Psi}=g+(\dot{f}\Psi)\circ f^{-1}\,.\\
\end{array}\right.

This BMS invariant action can be seen as the extension from the Virasoro group to the BMS group of the conformally-invariant action for FLRW cosmology that was introduced in
[
53
]
. The cosmological model can be understood as the
V_{1}=0
regime of the theory with
V_{2}
playing the role of the cosmological volume. In that case, there are no supertranslations but only superrotations and the conformally-invariant cosmological action was defined by introducing solely an extra
V_{2}\,\text{Sch}[\Phi]
term.

In terms of group representation, this means that the fields are embedded in the BMS
3
group itself:

\displaystyle(\Phi,\Psi)\in\text{BMS}_{3}=\text{Diff}(S^{1})\ltimes_{\text{Ad}}\text{Vect}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}.

(3.19)

This will inevitably modify the equations of motion for the initial fields
V_{i}
, and we postpone the analysis of this fully invariant theory to appendix
D
.

Note that here we have simply introduced the BMS invariant action as an example of a 1-dimensional system with full BMS symmetry. This is to be contrasted with the 2-dimensional BMS–Liouville theory (
3.36
) which will also have a close relationship with the action
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
we started from.

3.1.2
Infinitesimal transformations

We now move on to the study of the infinitesimal transformations. Since the supertranslations are Abelian, their infinitesimal generator, which we will denote
\alpha
, acts in the same way as the finite generator
g
. For the superrotations, we parametrize the transformation infinitesimally as
f(\tau)=\tau+\epsilon X(\tau)
. Then the infinitesimal version of (
3.1
) reproduces the
\mathfrak{bms}_{3}
algebra (
A.19
). We then obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{X}V_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\text{Ad}_{f,0}V_{i}-V_{i}}{\epsilon}=\dot{X}V_{i}-X\dot{V}_{i}=[V_{i},X],

(3.20)

and

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}V_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.21a)

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}V_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha\dot{V}_{1}-\dot{\alpha}V_{1}=-[V_{1},\alpha].

(3.21b)

With this, the infinitesimal variation of the action is

\displaystyle\delta_{X,\alpha}{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[V_{2}X^{(3)}-V_{1}\alpha^{(3)}\right]+\mathrm{d}\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left[\frac{\dot{V}_{1}}{2V_{1}^{2}}\Big(2XV_{1}\dot{V}_{2}-\dot{V}_{1}(\alpha V_{1}+XV_{2})\Big)+\ddot{\alpha}V_{1}-\ddot{X}V_{2}\right].

(3.22)

Once again, we see that the Poincaré subgroup, which at the infinitesimal level has generators such that
X^{(3)}=0=\alpha^{(3)}
, generates symmetries of the system since this variation then reduces to a total derivative.

Hamiltonian formulation and generators

Having studied the action of finite and infinitesimal BMS transformations on our system
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
, we can now study the generators in the (covariant) Hamiltonian formulation. From the general variational expression we can read the symplectic potential
\theta
. Its expression can be simplified if we change variables and introduce

\displaystyle\varphi=-\log V_{1}\,,\qquad\qquad\xi=\frac{V_{2}}{V_{1}}\,.

(3.23)

In terms of these variables
J
and
P
in (
2.14
) become

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{\varphi}^{2}-2\ddot{\varphi}\right)\,,\qquad\qquad J

\displaystyle=\ddot{\xi}-\dot{\xi}\dot{\varphi}\,,

(3.24)

and the equations of motion are
J=0=P
. One can note that in terms of
W
defined by
\dot{W}=e^{\varphi}
we have
P=-\text{Sch}[W]
. The transformations (
3.1
) become

\displaystyle\text{Ad}_{f,g}^{-1}:\,

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{lcl}\varphi&\mapsto&\widetilde{\varphi}=\varphi\circ f+\log\dot{f}\,,\\
\xi&\mapsto&\widetilde{\xi}=\left(\xi+g\dot{\varphi}+\dot{g}\right)\circ f\,,\\
\end{array}\right.

and we see in particular that under
D_{f}
the variables
\varphi
and
\xi
transform respectively as a connection and a scalar. The infinitesimal transformations (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) take the form

\displaystyle\delta_{X,\alpha}\,\varphi

\displaystyle=-X\dot{\varphi}-\dot{X}\,,

(3.27a)

\displaystyle\delta_{X,\alpha}\,\xi

\displaystyle=-X\dot{\xi}-\alpha\dot{\varphi}-\dot{\alpha}\,.

(3.27b)

Rewriting (
2.14c
) in terms of
\varphi
and
\xi
, we find the symplectic structure

\displaystyle\omega=\delta\theta=-\delta\dot{\xi}\,\delta(e^{-\varphi})+\delta\dot{\varphi}\,\delta(\xi e^{-\varphi})\,.

(3.28)

From this we can rewrite the initial momenta as

\displaystyle P_{1}=-\dot{\xi}=\frac{\dot{V}_{1}V_{2}-\dot{V}_{2}V_{1}}{V_{1}^{2}}\,\qquad\qquad P_{2}=\dot{\varphi}=-\frac{\dot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}\,,\qquad\qquad\{V_{i},P_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\,.

(3.29)

With the symplectic structure at our disposal, we can ask whether there are integrable generators associated with the infinitesimal transformations (
3.27
). This is found by contracting
\omega
with the variations
\delta_{X,\alpha}
, and then using the equations of motion to get rid of the second order derivatives. Quite surprisingly, even if the general BMS transformations do not describe symmetries of the action, they have an integrable generator. Separating the superrotations and supertranslations, we find

\displaystyle\delta_{X}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\omega

\displaystyle=-\delta_{X}\dot{\xi}\,\delta(e^{-\varphi})+\delta_{X}\dot{\varphi}\,\delta(\xi e^{-\varphi})+\delta\dot{\xi}\,\delta_{X}(e^{-\varphi})-\delta\dot{\varphi}\,\delta_{X}(\xi e^{-\varphi})

(3.30a)

\displaystyle=-\delta\left(e^{-\varphi}\left(\frac{\xi}{2}\dot{\varphi}^{2}-\dot{\varphi}\dot{\xi}\right)X+e^{-\varphi}(\xi\dot{\varphi}-\dot{\xi})\dot{X}+e^{-\varphi}\xi\ddot{X}\right)

(3.30b)

\displaystyle=-\delta\left(P_{2}\left(P_{1}V_{1}+\frac{1}{2}P_{2}V_{2}\right)X+(V_{2}P_{2}+V_{1}P_{1})\dot{X}+V_{2}\ddot{X}\right)

(3.30c)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\delta{\mathcal{D}}_{X}\,,

(3.30d)

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\omega

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\alpha}\dot{\xi}\,\delta(e^{-\varphi})+\delta_{\alpha}\dot{\varphi}\,\delta(\xi e^{-\varphi})+\delta\dot{\xi}\,\delta_{\alpha}(e^{-\varphi})-\delta\dot{\varphi}\,\delta_{\alpha}(\xi e^{-\varphi})

(3.30e)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\varphi}\dot{\varphi}^{2}\alpha+e^{-\varphi}\dot{\varphi}\dot{\alpha}+e^{-\varphi}\ddot{\alpha}\right)

(3.30f)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(\frac{1}{2}{P_{2}}^{2}V_{1}\alpha+P_{2}V_{1}\dot{\alpha}+V_{1}\ddot{\alpha}\right)

(3.30g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\delta{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}\,.

(3.30h)

It is now interesting to compute the Poisson brackets between these generators. This can be one either by contracting twice the symplectic structure with a variation
\delta_{X,\alpha}
, or using the phase space expression for the generators and the usual definition of Poisson brackets. For example we have

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{D}}_{X},{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}\}=-\delta_{X}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\delta_{Y}\lrcorner\,\!\!\!\lrcorner\,\omega=\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\mathrm{d}V_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}P_{i}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{D}}_{X}}{\mathrm{d}P_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}V_{i}}\,.

(3.31)

Explicitly, we find

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{D}}_{X},{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}\}

\displaystyle=-{\mathcal{D}}_{[X,Y]}+\big(XY^{(3)}-YX^{(3)}\big)V_{2}\,,

(3.32a)

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha},{\mathcal{T}}_{\beta}\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(3.32b)

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{D}}_{X},{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}\}

\displaystyle=-{\mathcal{T}}_{[X,\alpha]}+\big(\alpha X^{(3)}-X\alpha^{(3)}\big)V_{1}\,,

(3.32c)

where
[X,Y]=X\dot{Y}-Y\dot{X}
and similarly for
[X,\alpha]
.

These brackets are consistent with the equations of motion. For example, one can study the superrotation associated with a constant shift in time. This actually corresponds to the Hamiltonian of the system, i.e.
{\mathcal{D}}_{X(\tau)=1}=H
, as one can also check using a Legendre transform of the Lagrangian. Note that this is slightly peculiar since in the usual spacetime picture the Hamiltonian belongs to the Abelian sector of the symmetry algebra (i.e. Poincaré time translations), whereas here it belongs to the superrotations. For a general (time-dependent) phase space function
{\mathcal{O}}(\tau)
, the time evolution is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{O}}\eqqcolon\mathrm{d}_{\tau}{\mathcal{O}}=\partial_{\tau}{\mathcal{O}}+\{{\mathcal{O}},H\}\,.

(3.33)

Using the commutation relations (
3.32
) applied to
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
, and the fact that e.g.
\partial_{\tau}{\mathcal{D}}_{X}={\mathcal{D}}_{\dot{X}}
, we then find

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\tau}{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}{\mathcal{D}}_{X}-\{H,{\mathcal{D}}_{X}\}=V_{2}X^{(3)},

(3.34a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\tau}{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}-\{H,{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}\}=-V_{1}\alpha^{(3)},

(3.34b)

which is consistent with the equations of motion.

In spite of this consistency, we see of course that the bracket (
3.32
) between the generators
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
do not close because of a remaining field-dependency on the right-hand side (although up to the
V_{i}
’s this would have been the centrally-extended BMS
3
algebra). This is to be expected since
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
, although they are integrable, do not generate symmetries of the theory. Consistently, we recover a closed algebra for the Poincaré generators since these have a vanishing third derivative.

One way to obtain charges closing on the BMS
3
algebra is of course to consider a BMS invariant field theory. Although we have proposed a 1-dimensional such theory in (
3.16
), it turns out that a simpler field theory which has an interesting relationship with the black hole interior action (
2.8
) is the 2-dimensional BMS–Liouville theory of
[
59
,
44
,
16
]
. In terms of the scalar
\xi
and the connection
\varphi
its action is
4
4

4

The fields now depends on two variables, but the symmetries are still in the form (
3.2
), with

\displaystyle X(u,\tau)=Y(\tau)\,,\qquad\qquad\alpha(u,\tau)=T(\tau)+u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}Y(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,.

(3.35)

Usually the
\tau
coordinate is compactified and represented by an angle
\phi
, but here we keep the name
\tau
for a better comparison with our case.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{BMS}}=\iint\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\tau\big[\partial_{\tau}\xi\,\partial_{u}\varphi-(\partial_{\tau}\varphi)^{2}\big]\,.

(3.36)

Performing the Hamiltonian analysis of this action with respect to the new variable

, we find that the conserved charges generating the BMS

symmetries are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}=\int\mathrm{d}\tau\big[JX+P\alpha\big],

(3.37)

where
J
and

are precisely the quantities (

), whose vanishing is equivalent to the equations of motion. This can also be seen by computing the charges as the contraction of the transformations (

) with the symplectic structure
\omega=\delta(2\varphi-\xi)\delta\dot{\varphi}
coming from (

(X,\alpha)
in the adjoint representation and elements
(J,P)
in the coadjoint representation. This particular relationship between the charges of the 2d BMS

invariant theory and the equations of motion of the 1d model (

4  Coadjoint representation of BMS
3
and central charges

The understanding of coadjoint orbits of the symmetry groups is crucial in order to go towards the quantum theory. In particular the classification of the orbits of semi-direct product groups by their little groups will naturally leads to irreducible representation of the full group. In the case of the BMS
3
group, a detailed discussion is presented in
[
40
]
. In appendix
A
we report the key ingredients about the coadjoint orbits which we need in the present work.

The main goal of this section is to explain how the identification of the gravitational fields with the
\mathfrak{bms}_{3}
Lie algebra elements naturally leads to the introduction of 2-forms on the circle that transform as covectors under the group action. Moreover, we show here that the action itself can be seen as a bilinear form between vectors and covectors. For this, let us first consider the equations of motion for the configuration fields
V_{i}
, written in the form

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{\ddot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}-\frac{\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{2V_{1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{\varphi}^{2}-2\ddot{\varphi}\right)\,,

(4.1a)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{V}_{2}\dot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\ddot{V_{2}}}{V_{1}}+\frac{V_{2}}{V_{1}}\left(\frac{\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{V_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\ddot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}\right)=\ddot{\xi}-\dot{\xi}\dot{\varphi}\,.

(4.1b)

A straightforward calculation reveals that
J
and
P
transform exactly as in the coadjoint representation of the centrally-extended group
\text{BMS}_{3}
, i.e. as (
A.23
), with central charges given by
5
5

5

The central charge
c_{2}
appears here as a dimensionless quantity, while in 3-dimensional gravity it is usually normalized to
c_{2}=3/G
. Nevertheless, by a constant and dimensionfull rescaling of
P
in (
4.1a
) we can arbitrarily change the value of
c_{2}
(and its dimension). The normalization in 3-dimensional gravity comes from the fact that the zero mode of the supermomentum represents the Bondi mass aspect, while here the on-shell value of
P
is zero, so its dimension is not important. We keep the choice leading to ligher notations.

\displaystyle c_{1}=0\qquad\qquad c_{2}=1.

(4.2)

More precisely, defining the coadjoint action as

\displaystyle\text{Ad}^{*}_{f^{-1},g}(J,P)\coloneqq\left(\text{D}_{f^{-1}}\circ\text{T}_{g}\right)(J,P)\eqqcolon(\tilde{J},\tilde{P})\,,

(4.3)

we find the transformation laws

\displaystyle\tilde{P}=\dot{f}^{2}(P\circ f)-\,\text{Sch}[f]\,,\qquad\qquad\tilde{J}=\dot{f}^{2}\left(J+g\dot{P}+2\dot{g}P-g^{(3)}\right)\circ f\,.

(4.4)

As a remark, one can note that the other central charge can be switched on by the shift
J\to J+cP
, which leads to
c_{1}=c
. This shift can be obtained by adding a term
cPV_{1}
to the action (
2.8
). Remarkably, this shift does not affect the equations of motion and only modifies the symplectic potential. This comes from the fact that

\displaystyle\delta(PV_{1})=P\delta V_{1}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left(\delta\dot{V}_{1}-\frac{\delta V_{1}\dot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}\right),

(4.5)

so that the variation of the new Lagrangian is
\delta({\mathcal{L}}_{0}+cPV_{1})=(J+cP)\delta V_{1}+P\delta V_{2}+\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\bar{\theta}
, where
\bar{\theta}
is the sum of the boundary term (
4.5
) and the potential (
2.14c
) coming from
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
. The equations of motion therefore combine to give once again
J=0
and
P=0
. It is very intriguing that this mechanism is the same as in 3-dimensional gravity, where the BMS
3
central charge
c_{1}
(or a chiral mismatch
c_{+}\neq c_{-}
between the two Brown–Henneaux central charges in the AdS
3
case) can be switched on by adding a Chern–Simons and a torsion term (together these constitute Witten’s exotic Lagrangian
[
60
]
) to the first order Lagrangian, without however modifying the equations of motion
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
.

By adding an appropriate boundary term to the initial action (
2.8
), it is also possible to write a new action as the bilinear form between vectors and covectors. This rewriting is done by defining

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}[V_{i}]

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{S}}_{0}[V_{i}]-\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left(\frac{\dot{V}_{1}V_{2}}{V_{1}}\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\frac{V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{2V_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\dot{V}_{1}\dot{V}_{2}}{V_{1}}+\ddot{V}_{2}\right]

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\big[JV_{1}+PV_{2}\big].

(4.8)

By construction this action leads to the same equations of motion as
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
. It is however remarkable that the explicit variation is again of the form

\displaystyle\delta{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\,\big[J\delta V_{1}+P\delta V_{2}+\mathrm{d}_{\tau}\tilde{\theta}\,\big]\,,

(4.9)

even though
J
and
P
(expressed in terms of the
V_{i}
’s as in (
4.1
)) have of course been varied in (

) to obtain this expression! Consistently, the only difference with (
2.13
) is the form of the symplectic potential (which we have not displayed here because it won’t play a role in what follows).

The result is the 1-dimensional action (
4.6
) for the black hole interior in proper time gauge. This action, which is
not

invariant, is however equivalent to the charge expression (
3.37
) for the 2-dimensional BMS
3
invariant theory (
3.36
), provided we identify the infinitesimal parameters
(X,\alpha)
in the charge with the algebra element
(V_{1},V_{2})
. We note that this new action transforms under BMS

as

\displaystyle\text{Ad}^{*}_{(f^{-1},g)}{\mathcal{S}}[V_{i},\tau]={\mathcal{S}}[V_{i},\tilde{\tau}]-\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tilde{\tau}\left[(V_{2}-\dot{g}V_{1}+g\dot{V}_{1})\,\text{Sch}[f^{-1}]-V_{1}g^{(3)}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\tau}}(gV_{1}P)\right],

(4.10)

which as expected differs from (
3.15
) only by a boundary term.

We can now give a nice interpretation of the Poincaré symmetry subgroup as an orbit stabilizer. So far we have embedded the fields
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
within the
{\mathfrak{bms}}_{3}
Lie algebra. We can build corresponding group elements by considering the functions

\displaystyle f(\tau)\coloneqq\int^{\tau}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{V_{1}(s)}\,,\qquad\qquad g(\tau)\coloneqq\int^{\tau}\mathrm{d}s\,\left(\frac{V_{2}}{V_{1}}\right)\circ f^{(-1)}(s)\,.

(4.11)

Transforming these quantities under BMS
3
reveals that they are indeed group elements. If we now insert these functions into the transformation law (
4.4
) for the covectors, starting from the point
(P=0,J=0)
we find that the transformed pair
(\tilde{J},\tilde{P})
is precisely equal to the right-hand side of (
4.1

(f,g)
given above we have

\displaystyle-\text{Sch}[f]=\frac{\ddot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}-\frac{\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{2V_{1}^{2}}=P\,,\qquad\qquad-\dot{f}^{2}\,g^{(3)}\circ f=-\frac{\dot{V}_{2}\dot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\ddot{V_{2}}}{V_{1}}+\frac{V_{2}}{V_{1}}\left(\frac{\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{V_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\ddot{V}_{1}}{V_{1}}\right)=J.

(4.12)

This shows that demanding that the fields satisfy the equations of motion is equivalent to the condition that (
P=0,J=0
) be preserved under (
4.4
), which means in turn that
(f,g)
belongs to the stabilizer of the orbit. In general the coadjoint orbit of BMS
3
is defined by the Virasoro orbit spanned by the supermomentum

. These orbits are usually classified for the diffeomorphism group of the circle, while here we are dealing with functions on the real line since
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We can however compactify the proper time with

the reparametrization
\tau\to\arctan\theta/2

(P=0,J=0)
to
(P=-1/2,J=0)
, i.e. the constant representative of the so called

[

5  Discussion

In
[
1
]
we have studied the ISO(2,1) (i.e.
(2+1)
-dimensional Poincaré) symmetry of the homogeneous action (
2.8
) describing the black hole interior spacetime, that is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\dot{V}_{1}(V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}-2V_{1}\dot{V}_{2})}{2V_{1}^{2}}\,,

(
2.8
)

where
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are components of the 4-metric.
This previous work has hinted at the fact that the infinite-dimensional extension BMS
3
of the Poincaré group could also play a role in understanding the origin and the role of this symmetry. In the present paper, we have pushed along this direction and studied the action (
2.8
)
for the geometry inside the black hole
from the point of view of BMS
3
.

For this, we have shown in section
3
how finite and infinitesimal BMS
3
transformations act on (
2.8
). These transformations do not leave the action invariant, and only the subgroup of transformations corresponding to Poincaré does. Nevertheless, in the case of the supertranslations, one can interpret the non-invariance of the action as creating a term corresponding to a cosmological constant. Acting with a further supertranslation then preserves the form of the Lagrangian while however changing the value of the cosmological “constant”. We have shown that even if the BMS transformations are not strictly speaking symmetries of the action, they nevertheless admit integrable generators on the phase space of the theory. Their charge algebra, given by (
3.32
), does however fail to reproduce the centrally-extended
\mathfrak{bms}_{3}
. This is to be expected since these are indeed not symmetries of the theory. We have explained how a fully BMS-invariant action (
3.16
) can be obtained by promoting the BMS
3
group elements to dynamical variables. This higher order action was then studied in appendix
D
. Aside from this 1-dimensional invariant action, there are 2-dimensional geometrical actions typically arising in studies of the boundary dynamics of 3-dimensional gravity
[
16
,
15
,
59
]
. This is for example the BMS
3
invariant action (
3.36
). Intriguingly, the charges of this 2-dimensional action are written in terms of coadjoint vectors which turn out to be precisely the equations of motion of our starting point action (
2.8
). This has motivated the study of the coadjoint representation of BMS
3
in section
4
. Note that for the action (
3.16
) the BMS symmetries are
gauge symmetries
, while in the case of (
3.36
) they are
physical symmetries
.

We have then shown that the action (
2.8
) for the black hole interior can be rewritten (up to a boundary term innocent for the equations of motion) as the compact geometric action

{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\frac{V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}^{2}}{2V_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\dot{V}_{1}\dot{V}_{2}}{V_{1}}+\ddot{V}_{2}\right]=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\big[JV_{1}+PV_{2}\big]\,.

(
4.6
)

This is made possible by the fact that
J
and
P
(which are defined in terms of
V_{1},V_{2}
and their time derivatives)
are coadjoint vectors under BMS
3
with central charge
c_{2}=1
, while the configuration variables
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are elements of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{bms}_{3}
.
Remarkably, the variation of this action (
4.6
) leads to the field equations
J=P=0
, which are exactly equivalent to the original equations of motion.
This property of the geometric action also enables to turn on the other central charge
c_{1}
by adding a term to the Lagrangian without however modifying the equations of motion. In this construction, the Poincaré subgroup corresponds to the stabilizer of the vacuum orbit of BMS
3
.

These results show that even if the action (
2.8
) is only invariant under Poincaré symmetries, there is a meaningful way in which one can understand this invariance as a broken BMS
3
symmetry. This confirms the intuition that this latter symmetry group does indeed plays a foundational role in the physics of the black hole interior, although it does not appear here in the more usual way as an asymptotic or horizon boundary symmetries. It is also intriguing, as shown in appendix
B
, that other gravitational systems in 4, 3, and 2 spacetime dimensions involve the same action (
2.8
) and therefore admit the same symmetry structure.

In order to investigate further the origin and the physical role of these symmetries (both the Poincaré and the extended BMS one), there are several directions to be developed:

•

One should investigate to which physical systems correspond the other BMS coadjoint orbits. A related question is how the phase space structure of these coadjoint orbits can be used to define a quantization of the system. This can already be investigated in terms of Poincaré representations
[
64
]
, but the embedding into BMS could allow to shift the Casimirs and to describe new physical processes related to the dynamics of the black hole interior.

•

An intriguing question remains that of the relationship between the BMS group appearing in the present context and that appearing for asymptotic or near-horizon symmetries. It is particularly interesting how the Poincaré symmetry of (
2.8
) can be seen as a broken BMS symmetry as in the case of SL
(2,\mathbb{R})
and the Schwarzian action in JT gravity.

•

A related question is that of understanding BMS-invariant actions in mechanics and field theory. It could be that (
2.8
) descends from a higher-dimensional BMS-invariant action with a gauge-fixing and a dimensional reduction.

•

We have seen that the BMS transformations do not leave the action invariant, but that in a sense (which is clear for supertranslations) they generate new terms which can be interpreted e.g. as a cosmological constant. Then the BMS transformations generate a flow in a space of theories, and preserve the form of the action while changing its couplings. It would be very interesting to investigate the extension of Noether’s theorem(s) to this type of transformations (which are very similar to renormalization flow transformations).

•

Finally, another possible generalization of this work, motivated in parts by the symmetry structure of 3-dimensional gravity, is to study an extension of (
2.8
) which admits as a symmetry group
\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
instead of Poincaré’s
\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and therefore involves a double Virasoro algebra instead of BMS
3
. In the case of 3-dimensional gravity, this is done by turning on the cosmological constant, while in the case of the black hole interior, this is evidently not so. There are however other simple terms to be added to the action (
2.8
) in order to obtain this deformation to double Virasoro. The gravitational interpretation of these terms in the present context is however not yet clear, but is the subject of ongoing work.

Appendix A
The Virasoro and BMS
3
groups

In this appendix we recall basic facts about the Virasoro and the BMS
3
groups, as well as their centrally-extended version. We give in particular the form of the coadjoint action used in the main text.

The Virasoro group

We recall here some basic properties of the Virasoro group as the central extension of the group of diffeomorphisms of the circle
S^{1}
. We denote by
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
the space of vector fields on the circle endowed with the bracket

\displaystyle\big[f(\theta)\,\partial_{\theta},\,g(\theta)\,\partial_{\theta}\big]=(fg^{\prime}-gf^{\prime})\partial_{\theta}\qquad\qquad f\,\partial_{\theta},\,g\,\partial_{\theta}\in\text{Vect}(S^{1})\,,

(A.1)

with
f
and
g
periodic functions of
\theta
with period
2\pi
. A standard basis is given by the Fourier modes
\ell_{n}\coloneqq ie^{in\theta}\partial_{\theta}
, with the commutation relations
[\ell_{n},\ell_{m}]=-i(n-m)\ell_{n+m}
. This is the Witt algebra. Moreover, the vector space
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
can been seen as the space of generators of the (orientation preserving) diffeomorphisms on the circle, denoted by
\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
. Conversely, the diffeomorphisms can be endowed with a Lie group structure, whose algebra is
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
.

A.1.1
Tensor densities on
S^{1}

We denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}
the space of tensor densities of degree
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. It consists of elements of the form
\alpha=\alpha(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta^{\lambda}
. Diffeomorphisms act by the adjoint action as

\displaystyle\alpha(\theta)\xrightarrow{\varphi\,\in\,\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})}\text{Ad}^{*}_{\varphi^{-1}}\alpha(\theta)=\left(\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\right)^{\lambda}\alpha\left(\varphi(\theta)\right).

(A.2)

The infinitesimal version gives the adjoint action of
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
on densities, i.e. the Lie derivative of the differential along a vector field. Taking
\varphi=\mathbb{I}+\epsilon X
, with
\epsilon\to 0
and
X=X(\theta)\partial_{\theta}
, we get

\displaystyle L^{(\lambda)}_{X}\alpha\coloneqq\text{ad}^{*}_{X}\alpha=X\alpha^{\prime}+\lambda X^{\prime}\alpha.

(A.3)

We can naturally define a bilinear form on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}\times{\mathcal{F}}_{1-\lambda}
which is invariant under Lie derivative as

\displaystyle\braket{\alpha,\beta}\coloneqq\int_{S^{1}}\alpha\otimes\beta\ \qquad\qquad\forall\ \alpha\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\lambda}\,,\ \beta\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1-\lambda}\,.

(A.4)

We then identify the dual of
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
with the space of quadratic differentials
p=p(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
, and we have

\displaystyle\braket{p,v}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,p(\theta)v(\theta)\qquad\qquad\forall\ v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}\,.

(A.5)

This means that the action of the vector fields on
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
coincides with the co-adjoint action, i.e.

\displaystyle\braket{\text{ad}^{*}_{X}(p),Y}=-\braket{p,[X,Y]}\qquad\forall\ X,Y\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}\,,\ p\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}\,.

(A.6)

A.1.2
Central extension

We recall that the only non-trivial cocycle on
\text{Vect}(S^{1})
is given by the Gelfand–Fuchs 2-cocycle

\displaystyle\omega:\text{Vect}(S^{1})\times\text{Vect}(S^{1})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}

(A.7)

\displaystyle(X,Y)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,X^{\prime}Y^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\theta(X^{\prime}Y^{\prime\prime}-X^{\prime\prime}Y^{\prime})\,.

The Virasoro algebra is the central extension of the algebra of vector fields, defined on the vector space
\widehat{\text{Vect}(S^{1})}\coloneqq\text{Vect}(S^{1})\oplus{\mathbb{R}}
, with the bracket

\displaystyle\big[(X,a),(Y,b)\big]\coloneqq\big([X,Y],\omega(X,Y)\big)\qquad\qquad\forall\ a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,\ X,Y\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}\,.

(A.8)

The corresponding group is introduced via the Bott–Thurston 2-cocycle in
\mathrm{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
, which is

\displaystyle B:\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\times\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}

(A.9)

\displaystyle(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\theta\log(\varphi^{\prime}\circ\psi)\left(\log(\psi^{\prime})\right)^{\prime}\,.

We then define the Virasoro group as
\widehat{\text{Diff}^{+}}(S^{1})=\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\times{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with the group law

\displaystyle(\varphi,a)\circ(\psi,b)=\big(\varphi\circ\psi,b+B(\varphi,\psi)\big)\,.

(A.10)

Consistently, we recover the Virasoro algebra by the observation that the infinitesimal limit of
B
is
\omega
. Indeed, defining the flows
\varphi_{t}
and
\psi_{s}
corresponding respectively to the vector fields
X(\theta)\partial_{\theta}
and
Y(\theta)\partial_{\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle\omega(X,Y)=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t}\big(B(\varphi_{t},\psi_{s})-B(\psi_{s},\varphi_{t})\big)\Big|_{t=s=0}\,.

(A.11)

A.1.3
Coadjoint action

With the extended Lie bracket and the bilinear form at our disposal, we can compute the coadjoint action of the Virasoro algebra on quadratic forms. Taking the adjoint action of the algebra on itself, we define its coadjoint action as in (
A.6
). A straightforward calculation leads to

\displaystyle\text{ad}^{*}_{(X,a)}(p,c)=\big(p^{\prime}X+2X^{\prime}p-cX^{(3)},0\big)\qquad\qquad\forall\ X\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-1}\,,\ p\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}\,,\ a,c\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(A.12)

Its exponential gives the group coadjoint action

\displaystyle\text{Ad}^{*}_{(f^{-1},a)}(p,c)=\big(p\bullet f-c\,{\mathcal{S}}[f],\,c\big)\qquad\qquad\forall\ f\in\mathrm{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})\,,\ p\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}\,,\ a,c\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,

(A.13)

where

\displaystyle p\bullet f=f^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}(p\circ f)\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{S}}[f]=\text{Sch}[f]\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}=\left[\frac{f^{(3)}}{f^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}\,.

(A.14)

Finally, introducing
h=f^{\prime}
, we recall that the Schwarzian derivative satisfies

\displaystyle\text{Sch}[f]

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{h^{\prime}}{h}\right)^{2}=(\log h)^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\big((\log h)^{\prime}\big)^{2}\,,

(A.15a)

\displaystyle\text{Sch}[f\circ g]

\displaystyle=\text{Sch}[g]+(g^{\prime})^{2}\,\text{Sch}[f]\circ g\,,

(A.15b)

\displaystyle\text{Sch}[f^{-1}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\text{Sch}[f]}{h^{2}}\,.

(A.15c)

The BMS
3
group

The centrally-extended BMS
3
group is the semidirect product, under the adjoint action, of the Virasoro group and its algebra seen as an Abelian vector group. This is

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{BMS}_{3}}=\widehat{\text{Diff}^{+}}(S^{1})\ltimes_{\text{Ad}}\widehat{\text{Vect}}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}\,.

(A.16)

We will (abusively) refer to the first factor as superrotations, and to the second one as supertranslations. Its elements are quadruples
(f,a;g,b)
with
f\in\text{Diff}^{+}(S^{1})
,
g\in\text{Vect}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}
, and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The adjoint action of the Virasoro group reads

\displaystyle\text{Ad}_{f}(g,b)=\left((gf^{\prime})\circ f^{-1},b-\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,\text{Sch}[f]g\right)\,.

(A.17)

The corresponding algebra is

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathfrak{bms}_{3}}=\widehat{\text{Vect}}(S^{1})\oplus_{\text{ad}}\widehat{\text{Vect}}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}\,,

(A.18)

whose elements are again quadruples
(X,a;\alpha,b)
with
X\in\text{Vect}(S^{1})
,
\alpha\in\text{Vect}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}
, and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The commutation relations are

\displaystyle\big[(X,a;\alpha,b),(Y,r;\beta,s)\big]=\big([X,Y],\omega(X,Y);[X,\beta]-[Y,\alpha],\omega(X,\beta)-\omega(Y,\alpha)\big)\,.

(A.19)

As always, we can define an adjoint action of the group on its algebra by conjugation, which simply consists in exponentiating the commutation relation above. In the specific case studied here this gives

\displaystyle\text{Ad}_{(f;g)}(X;\alpha)=\left(\text{Ad}_{f}X;\text{Ad}_{f}\alpha+[\text{Ad}_{f}X,g]\right)\,.

(A.20)

We are now interested in the coadjoint representation. The dual of the algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{bms}_{3}}
is the space
\widehat{\text{Vect}}(S^{1})^{*}\oplus\widehat{\text{Vect}}(S^{1})_{\text{ab}}^{*}
, paired with the algebra element via the bilinear form

\displaystyle\big\{(J,c_{1};P,c_{2}),(X,a;\alpha,b)\big\}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(JX+P\alpha\right)\mathrm{d}\theta+c_{1}a+c_{2}b\,.

(A.21)

This leads to the coadjoint representations of
\widehat{\text{BMS}_{3}}
, which is

\displaystyle\text{Ad}^{*}_{f^{-1},g}(J,c_{1};P,c_{2})=\left(\tilde{J},c_{1};\tilde{P},c_{2}\right)

(A.22)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{P}=f^{\prime 2}(P\circ f)-c_{2}\,\text{Sch}[f]\,,\qquad\qquad\tilde{J}=f^{\prime 2}\left(J+gP^{\prime}+2g^{\prime}P-c_{2}g^{(3)}\right)\circ f-c_{1}\,\text{Sch}[f]\,.

(A.23)

This is what we use in the main text, for example in (
4.4
), where there however the prime
′
has become dot
\cdot
since the variable is the proper time
\tau
instead of the angle
\theta
.

Appendix B
Other gravitational systems

In this section we briefly present some other gravitational systems which exhibit the same algebraic properties (encoded in the Poincaré symmetry and its embedding into BMS
3
) as the action
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
which was our starting point.

4d black hole in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates

The first example is the 4-dimensional black hole spacetime written in Eddington–Finkelstein null coordinates. This is described by the line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\text{EF}}=\frac{8V_{2}(r)}{V_{1}(r)}\mathrm{d}u^{2}+4L_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}r+V_{1}(r)\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}\,.

(B.1)

Up to a boundary term, with
\tau
replaced by
r
, and with the non-compact coordinate
u
regularized to
u\in[0,L_{0}]
, the Einstein–Hilbert action evaluated on this line element gives
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
, i.e.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{EH}}^{(\text{EF})}=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}r\,\left[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{\dot{V}_{1}(V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}-2V_{1}\dot{V}_{2})}{2V_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(\frac{V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}+V_{1}\dot{V}_{2}}{V_{1}}\right)\right]\,,

(B.2)

where the dot denotes derivative with respect to
r
.

3d BTZ black hole interior

Going lower in dimensions, it is interesting that the 3-dimensional BTZ black hole, which is defined in AdS spacetime, also leads to the action
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
. To see this, we consider the 3-dimensional line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\text{BTZ}}

\displaystyle=-N^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{2V_{2}}{\sqrt{V_{1}}}\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\sqrt{V_{1}}(\mathrm{d}\phi+N_{\phi}\mathrm{d}r)^{2},

(B.3)

\displaystyle=-N^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\left(\frac{2V_{2}}{\sqrt{V_{1}}}+N_{\phi}^{2}\sqrt{V_{1}}\right)\mathrm{d}r^{2}+2N_{\phi}\sqrt{V_{1}}\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\phi+\sqrt{V_{1}}\mathrm{d}\phi^{2},

(B.4)

with coefficients depending only on
t
, and where we have included angular momentum
N_{\phi}
. With this line element we find
\sqrt{-g}=N\sqrt{2V_{2}}
and

\displaystyle\sqrt{-g}\,R=\frac{V^{\prime}_{1}(V_{2}V^{\prime}_{1}-2V_{1}V^{\prime}_{2})+2V_{1}(V_{1}N_{\phi}^{\prime})^{2}}{4\sqrt{2}NV_{1}^{2}\sqrt{V_{2}}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}V^{\prime}_{2}}{N\sqrt{V_{2}}}\right).

(B.5)

We remark that the 3-dimensional Lagrangian has only a kinetic term and no potential. This is feature of 3-dimensional gravity, where the potential term is actually provided by the cosmological constant volume term. Dropping the boundary term above, and integrating over
r\in[0,L_{0}]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
we find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{3d}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{M}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,(R+2\Lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}t\,L_{0}\left[\frac{V^{\prime}_{1}(V_{2}V^{\prime}_{1}-2V_{1}V^{\prime}_{2})+2V_{1}(V_{1}N_{\phi}^{\prime})^{2}}{4\sqrt{2}NV_{1}^{2}\sqrt{V_{2}}}+\Lambda N\sqrt{8V_{2}}\right].

(B.6)

Gauge fixing the lapse to introduce proper time
\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{N\sqrt{8V_{2}}}{L_{0}}\mathrm{d}t
, we find once again the action
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
up to some differences:

•

The constant term
L_{0}^{2}
is now multiplied by the cosmological constant
\Lambda
.

•

There is an additional kinetic term depending on the angular momentum. This term is actually covariant under the BMS transformations, provided that
N_{\phi}
is a scalar under superrotations and does not transform under supertranslations, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}_{f,g}N_{\phi}(\tau)=N_{\phi}\circ f^{-1}.

(B.7)

2d dilatonic gravity

Finally, another example leading to
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
can be found in 2-dimensional dilatonic gravity. In this context, we consider the 2-parameter family of actions

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{2d}}=\int\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\sqrt{-g}\,\Big(\Phi^{2}R+\alpha\Phi^{\alpha-2}+2\beta(\nabla\Phi)^{2}\Big).

(B.8)

This includes the spherically-symmetric section of 4d gravity
(\alpha=2,\beta=1)
, the CGHS model
(\alpha=4,\beta=2)
, and JT gravity
(\alpha=4,\beta=0)
. Let us then consider a gauge where the dilaton depends only on
r
, and with 2-dimensional metrics of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=A(r)\mathrm{d}u^{2}+2B(r)\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}r\,.

(B.9)

If we now choose the fields as

\displaystyle A(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\alpha}V_{1}^{\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-\beta)}}V_{2}^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha-\beta}}\,,

(B.10a)

\displaystyle B(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{0}}{\alpha}V_{1}^{\frac{\alpha-2}{2(\alpha-\beta)}}V_{2}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}}\,,

(B.10b)

\displaystyle\Phi(r)

\displaystyle=V_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha-2\beta}}V_{2}^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}}\,,

(B.10c)

the dilatonic action reduces again to
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
up to a boundary term if we choose
u\in[0,L_{0}]
, and replace
\tau
with
r
. More precisely, we find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{2d}}=\int\mathrm{d}r\,\left[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{\dot{V}_{1}(V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}-2V_{1}\dot{V}_{2})}{2V_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(\alpha\frac{V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}}{2V_{1}}-\beta\dot{V}_{2}\right)\right]\,,

(B.11)

where dot now denotes derivation with respect to
r
.

Appendix C
Cosmological constant and scalar field

In this appendix we briefly explain how the action (
2.8
) is modified in the presence of a cosmological constant and an homogeneous scalar field. This shows that the contribution of these matter sources is proportional to
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
in the action, just like the extra terms which have been produced by the action of a finite BMS transformation in (
3.15
).

Using the metric with redefined lapse
N_{\text{p}}
as in (
2.5
), working with proper time
\tau
and introducing the IR cutoff
L_{0}
to regulate the integration over
x
, the inclusion of a cosmological constant is done by adding to the Einstein–Hilbert action the volume term

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda}=\frac{1}{8\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\Lambda=\int\mathrm{d}\tau\,L_{0}V_{1}\Lambda\,.

(C.1)

Similarly, the contribution of a minimally-coupled scalar field
\phi
in the spherically-symmetric and homogeneous spacetime (
2.5
) is described by the action

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\phi}=-\frac{1}{16\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi)^{2}-{\mathcal{V}}(\phi)\right)=\frac{1}{2G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left(\dot{\phi}^{2}V_{2}+L_{0}V_{1}{\mathcal{V}}(\phi)\right)\,.

(C.2)

Comparing these two actions with the transformation (
3.15
), one can see that for pure supertranslations (i.e. with
f=\mathbb{I}
) we can interpret the third derivative of
g
as an effective cosmological constant, while for pure superrotations (i.e. with
g=0
) we can interpret the term created by the superrotation as the kinetic term of a scalar field (up to a redefinition
\text{Sch}[f^{-1}]\mapsto\dot{\phi}^{2}
).

Appendix D
1d BMS
3
invariant action

This appendix is devoted to the study of some properties of the full BMS invariant theory (
3.16
), which is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{inv}}[V_{i},\Phi,\Psi]=\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\frac{\dot{V}_{1}(V_{2}\dot{V}_{1}-2V_{1}\dot{V}_{2})}{2V_{1}^{2}}+(V_{2}+\dot{\Psi}V_{1}-\Psi\dot{V}_{1})\text{Sch}[\Phi]+V_{1}\Psi^{(3)}\right].

(D.1)

We first study the Lagrangian equations of motion and symmetries, and then perform the Hamiltonian analysis.

Lagrangian analysis

First of all we need to remark that the field
\Phi
does not appear in the Lagrangian without derivatives, implying the existence of a ghost zero mode for
\Phi
. On the other hand, both
\Psi
and
\Phi
appear with third derivatives. Moreover, the action exhibits an infinite-dimensional gauge symmetry (under BMS
3
), which will be reflected in the freedom to arbitrarily choose two of the fields in the solutions.

In spite of these peculiarities, the equations of motion takes nevertheless a remarkably simple form in terms of the coadjoint vectors defined in (
2.14a
) and (
2.14b
). They are given by

\displaystyle 0\equiv{\mathcal{E}}_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=J+\Psi\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\,\text{Sch}[\Phi]}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+2\dot{\Psi}\,\text{Sch}[\Phi]+\Psi^{(3)}\,,

(D.2a)

\displaystyle 0\equiv{\mathcal{E}}_{V_{2}}

\displaystyle=P+\text{Sch}[\Phi]\,.

(D.2b)

The other two equations of motion arise as a consequence of these first two. Indeed, one an check that we have

\displaystyle 0\equiv{\mathcal{E}}_{\Psi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{V_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left({\mathcal{E}}_{V_{2}}V_{1}^{2}\right)\,,

(D.3a)

\displaystyle 0\equiv\dot{\Phi}{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{V_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left({\mathcal{E}}_{V_{1}}V_{1}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{V_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left({\mathcal{E}}_{V_{2}}V_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\Psi}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left[\frac{\Psi^{2}}{V_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left({\mathcal{E}}_{V_{2}}V_{1}^{2}\right)\right]\,.

(D.3b)

This makes manifest that the theory posses gauge symmetries, provided by the BMS
3
group. The equations of motion are solved by the 2-parameter family of solutions

\displaystyle V_{1}=\frac{1}{\dot{\Phi}}\,,\qquad\qquad V_{2}=\frac{\mathfrak{c_{1}}+\mathfrak{c_{2}}\Phi}{\dot{\Phi}}-\frac{1}{\dot{\Phi}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\big(\Psi\dot{\Phi}\big)\,,

(D.4)

for some constant
\mathfrak{c}_{i}
’s. Moreover, we have that different solutions can be mapped into each other according to the monodromy condition satisfied by the supermomentum
P
(one can compare the equations of motion with (
4.4
) in order to see this), meaning that choosing a particular solution is equivalent to picking up a point on a coadjoint orbit of BMS
3
entirely determined by the constants of integration
\mathfrak{c}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2}
.

We can also compute the Noether charges associated to the BMS
3
symmetry. They are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

\displaystyle=-X\left(V_{1}\big(J-\Psi\,\dot{P}+2\dot{\Psi}\,\text{Sch}[\Phi]+\Psi^{(3)}\big)+(V_{2}-2\Psi\dot{V}_{1})\big(P+\text{Sch}[\Phi]\big)\right)\,,

(D.5a)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,V_{1}\big(P+\text{Sch}[\Phi]\big)\,,

(D.5b)

that trivially vanishes on-shell, as expected for a gauge symmetry charges.

Hamiltonian analysis

Since the Lagrangian in (
3.16
) is higher-order, in order to perform the Hamiltonian analysis we need to follow the Ostrogradski algorithm and introduce auxiliary fields with appropriate constraints. First, we therefore define

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}=\dot{\Phi}\,,\qquad\qquad\Phi_{2}=\ddot{\Phi}\,,\qquad\qquad\Psi_{1}=\dot{\Psi}\,,\qquad\qquad\Psi_{2}=\ddot{\Psi}\,.

(D.6)

With this, the action (
3.16
) can then be rewritten with at most first order derivatives as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{inv}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{S}}_{0}+\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[(V_{2}+\Psi_{1}V_{1}-\Psi\dot{V}_{1})\left(\frac{\dot{\Phi}_{2}}{\Phi_{1}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\Phi_{2}}{\Phi_{1}}\right)^{2}\right)+V_{1}\dot{\Psi}_{2}\right.

(D.7)

\displaystyle\phantom{={\mathcal{S}}_{0}+\frac{1}{G}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\right.}+\Pi(\dot{\Phi}-\Phi_{1})+\Pi_{1}(\dot{\Phi}_{1}-\Phi_{2})+\Xi(\dot{\Psi}-\Psi_{1})+\Xi_{1}(\dot{\Psi}_{1}-\Psi_{2})\Bigg]\,.

(D.8)

Here the second line is the set of constraints, imposed with Lagrange multiplier
(\Pi,\Pi_{1},\Xi,\Xi_{1})
, ensuring that the auxiliary fields can be eliminated in order to go back to the initial expression (
3.16
). As expected, because this theory has gauge symmetries it is a constrained system. In order to see this in the Hamiltonian formulation, we perform the Legendre transform and introduce a 16-dimensional phase space with canonical pairs

\displaystyle\big\{(\Phi,\Pi),(\Phi_{1},\Pi_{1}),(\Phi_{2},\Pi_{2}),(\Psi,\Xi),(\Psi_{1},\Xi_{1}),(\Psi_{2},\Xi_{2}),(V_{1},P_{1}),(V_{2},P_{2})\big\}\,,

(D.9)

with

\displaystyle\big\{\Phi_{i},\Pi_{i}\big\}=\big\{\Psi_{i},\Xi_{i}\big\}=\big\{V_{i},P_{i}\big\}=1\,.

(D.10)

The extended Hamiltonian is then found to be

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\dot{\Phi}_{2}\chi_{1}+\dot{\Psi}_{2}\chi_{2}\,,

(D.11)

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=-P_{2}\left(V_{1}P_{1}+\frac{1}{2}V_{2}P_{2}\right)+(V_{2}+\Psi_{1}V_{1}+\Psi V_{1}P_{2})\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\Phi_{2}}{\Phi_{1}}\right)^{2}+\Pi\Phi_{1}+\Pi_{1}\Phi_{2}+\Xi\Psi_{1}+\Xi_{1}\Psi_{2}\,,

where
\dot{\Phi}_{2}
and
\dot{\Psi}_{2}
are Lagrange multipliers imposing the primary constraints

\displaystyle\chi_{1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{2}-\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}}(V_{2}+\Psi_{1}V_{1}+\Psi V_{1}P_{2})\,,

(D.12a)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}

\displaystyle=V_{1}-\Xi_{2}\,.

(D.12b)

We can now apply Dirac’s algorithm and evolve the primary constraints with respect to the extended Hamiltonian. This evolution generates two secondary constraints given by

\displaystyle\chi_{3}

\displaystyle=\big\{\chi_{1},H_{\text{tot}}\big\}=\frac{1}{\Phi_{1}}\left(V_{1}P_{1}+V_{2}P_{2}-V_{1}\Psi_{2}+\frac{1}{2}V_{1}P_{2}^{2}\Psi\right)-\frac{2\Phi_{2}}{\Phi_{1}^{2}}(V_{2}+\Psi_{1}V_{1}+\Psi V_{1}P_{2})-\Pi_{1}\,,

(D.13a)

\displaystyle\chi_{4}

\displaystyle=\{\chi_{2},H_{\text{tot}}\}=-P_{2}V_{1}+\Xi_{1}\,.

(D.13b)

The evolution of these secondary constraints then leads to two tertiary constraints given by

\displaystyle\chi_{5}=\{\chi_{3},H_{\text{tot}}\}

\displaystyle=\Pi-\frac{1}{2\Phi_{1}}P_{2}\big(P_{2}V_{2}-V_{1}(P_{2}\Psi_{1}-2P_{1}+2\Psi_{2})\big)

(D.14a)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\ }+\frac{\Phi_{2}}{2\Phi_{1}^{2}}\big(2P_{2}V_{2}+V_{1}(2P_{1}+P_{2}^{2}\Psi-2\Psi_{2})\big)-\frac{\Phi_{2}^{2}}{2\Phi_{1}^{3}}\big(V_{2}+V_{1}(P_{2}\Psi+\Psi_{1})\big)\,,

(D.14b)

\displaystyle\chi_{6}=\{\chi_{4},H_{\text{tot}}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}P_{2}^{2}V_{1}-\Xi\,,

(D.14c)

\displaystyle H_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\mu_{i}\chi_{i}\,.

(D.14d)

The algorithm then stops without giving any conditions on the Lagrange multipliers or on the fields. All the constraints are first class: their brackets weakly vanish, and for
\chi_{5}
and
\chi_{6}
they actually strongly vanish.

Now that we know the first class constraints, we need to find which combination generates the infinitesimal BMS
3
gauge transformations. A straightforward calculation shows that these Hamiltonian generators are given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{X}

\displaystyle=X(-\Phi_{1}\chi_{5}+\chi_{2}\dot{\Psi}_{2}-\dot{\Phi}_{2}\chi_{1}+\Phi_{2}\chi_{3}+\Psi_{1}\chi_{6}-\Psi_{2}\chi_{4})+

(D.15a)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\ }+\dot{X}(\Phi_{1}\chi_{3}-2\Phi_{2}\chi_{1}+\Psi_{2}\chi_{2}-\Psi\chi_{6})+\ddot{X}(\Psi\Phi_{4}-\Psi_{1}\chi_{2}-\Phi_{1}\chi_{1})-\Psi\chi_{2}X^{(3)}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\alpha\chi_{6}+\dot{\alpha}\chi_{4}-\ddot{\alpha}\chi_{2}\,.

(D.15b)

\displaystyle(\Psi,\Pi_{i},\Xi_{i})\to 0\,,\qquad\qquad\Phi(\tau)\to\tau\,.
