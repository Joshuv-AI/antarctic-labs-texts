A ∞ A_{\infty} -Deformations and their Derived Categories
Jasper van de Kreeke

Abstract
Handling curved A ∞ A_{\infty} -deformations is challenging and defining their derived categories seems impossible. In this paper, we show how to welcome the curvature and build derived categories despite the apparent difficulties. We explicitly describe their A ∞ A_{\infty} -structure by a deformed Kadeishvili theorem. This paper starts with a review of A ∞ A_{\infty} -deformations for non-specialists. We explain why A ∞ A_{\infty} -deformations require curvature and why curvature supposedly causes problems. We prove that infinitesimally curved A ∞ A_{\infty} -deformations are in fact non-problematic and provide the correct definitions for their twisted completion and minimal model. The classical Kadeishvili theorem collapses for curved deformations. We show how to adapt it by iteratively optimizing the curvature and changing the homological splitting infinitesimally. The final A ∞ A_{\infty} -structure on the minimal model is constructed in terms of trees.

A_{\infty}
-categories and their deformations

A_{\infty}
-categories are a type of category used to store homotopical information. In comparison with the better-known dg categories, they allow for more flexibility. While dg categories come with only two operations, the differential and the composition, an
A_{\infty}
-category allows room for higher products
\mu^{3},\mu^{4},\ldots
. The flexibility entails that many different
A_{\infty}
-categories are equivalent to a given one, varying in size and amount of nonzero higher products.

Deformation theory is a means of capturing all ways to algebraically or geometrically deform objects. The language of
A_{\infty}
-categories makes it possible to define immediately an algebraic deformation theory over a given local ring
B
. The idea is to look for all ways to deform the
A_{\infty}
-products
\mu^{1},\mu^{2},\ldots
of
\mathcal{C}
and allow additional curvature
\mu^{0}
in such a way that the products satisfy the “curved
A_{\infty}
-relations” and reduce to the
A_{\infty}
-structure of
\mathcal{C}
once the maximal ideal of
B
is divided out.

Minimal models and derived categories

Given an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}
and an equivalent
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{D}
, one also says that
\mathcal{D}
is a different model for
\mathcal{C}
. Switching to a different model of a given
A_{\infty}
-category is often tedious because it requires computing the higher products on the model. The Kadeishvili construction is an explicit method to construct these higher products. The idea is originally due to Kadeishvili, but has been worked out explicitly in
[
13
, Chapter 6, 3.3.2]
.

Minimal models are a way to decrease the size of the hom spaces of an
A_{\infty}
-category. An
A_{\infty}
-category is called minimal if its differential
\mu^{1}
vanishes. A minimal model for
\mathcal{C}
is by definition any
A_{\infty}
-category
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
which is quasi-isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}
and whose differential
\mu^{1}_{\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}}
vanishes. Thanks to the Kadeishvili construction, every
A_{\infty}
-category clearly has a minimal model: We take the cohomology of all the hom spaces and equip them with the
A_{\infty}
-structure produced by the Kadeishvili construction.

Twisted completion is a way to form an
A_{\infty}
-category of formal chain complexes of objects. The idea is to include arbitrary formal sums of shifted objects together with an upper-triangular matrix which represents the differential. Derived categories of
A_{\infty}
-categories are defined by combining the twisted completion and minimal model construction. More precisely, the derived category of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as the
A_{\infty}
-category
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
.

The curvature problem and derived categories

In formal
A_{\infty}
-deformation theory, one regards infinitesimal deformations of a given
A_{\infty}
-structure such that the
A_{\infty}\,
-relations are preserved. An issue is that only deforming the
A_{\infty}
-structure does not give a notion of deformations that is invariant under quasi-equivalence of
A_{\infty}
-categories. In order to obtain a notion invariant under quasi-equivalence, one needs to permit the deformation to have curvature. More precisely, the curvature must be infinitesimal in the sense that it lies in a multiple of the maximal ideal of the local ring. Infinitesimal curvature is inevitable for a good notion of
A_{\infty}
-deformations.

Dealing with curvature is however regarded as tedious, because the curvature prevents the differential from squaring to zero. The presence of curvature is often referred to as the “curvature problem”. A main question is how to gauge away the curvature or otherwise how to deal with the remaining curvature. An instance of the uncurving problem has been studied by Lowen and Van den Bergh
[
15
]
.

Unfortunately the deformation makes it difficult to speak about twisted completions, minimal models and derived categories: Twisted complexes are classically assumed to satisfy the Maurer-Cartan equation, a condition which ensures that the category
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
becomes curvature-free. Minimal models require that the differential squares to zero in order to be able to take cohomology. Derived categories require both the twisted complex and minimal model construction to be well-defined.

Results

The aim of the present paper is to show that deformations of
A_{\infty}
-categories have derived categories. The first step is to define the twisted completion and minimal model of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories in an abstract way. This abstract method entails first forming the twisted completion or minimal model of the
A_{\infty}
-category without deformation and afterwards pushing the deformation onto it. The second step is to describe twisted completion and minimal model explicitly. For the twisted completion, the construction is tricky but straight-forward. For the minimal model, the construction is a fine-tuned variant of the Kadeishvili construction.

Abstract definition for twisted completion

The most conceptual way to define

\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}

Explicit construction for twisted completion

The explicit way to define
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is by gathering the same objects as
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, namely those twisted complexes satisfying the non-deformed Maurer-Cartan equation
\mu^{1}(\delta)+\mu^{2}(\delta,\delta)+\ldots=0
. This is not the same as gathering twisted complexes with twisted differential
\delta
satisfying the deformed Maurer-Cartan equation
\mu^{0}_{q}+\mu^{1}_{q}(\delta)+\ldots=0
. In contrast to
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, the category
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
inherits infinitesimal curvature, stemming precisely from the infinitesimal failure of the twisted differentials to satisfy the deformed Maurer-Cartan equation.

Abstract definition for the minimal model

The most conceptual way to define
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
consists of taking any minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
and inducing the deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
onto
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
via any quasi-isomorphism
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. This abstract approach means that the “minimal model”
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
may have infinitesimal curvature as well as a residual infinitesimal differential. This is not the same as taking cohomology of the hom complexes
(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X,Y),\mu^{1}_{q})
. In fact, the deformed differential of an
A_{\infty}
-deformation need not even square to zero because of the curvature.

Explicit construction for the minimal model

Minimal models of
A_{\infty}
-categories can classically be computed by means of homological splittings and Kadeishvili trees. In our deformed Kadeishvili theorem, we show that this method carries over to the deformed case. The starting point is an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}
together with an deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The difficulties encountered in constructing the minimal model are the presence of curvature
\mu^{0}_{q}
, the fact that the deformed differential
\mu^{1}_{q}
does not square to zero and the fact that
\mu^{1}_{q}
is not compatible with the homological splitting chosen for
\mathcal{C}
. In
section 6
, we show how to adapt the Kadeishvili construction to these special circumstances.

Our construction can be summarized as follows:

1.

Choose a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}
.

2.

Perform an infinitesimal change on the homological splitting in order to adapt it to
\mu^{1}_{q}
.

3.

Gauge away part of the deformation’s curvature.

4.

Repeat steps 2 and 3 indefinitely. Take the limit of this process.

5.

Calculate the deformed codifferential
h_{q}
and projection
\pi_{q}
.

6.

Define the structure of
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
by sums over deformed Kadeishvili trees.

Relation to the literature

The theory of
A_{\infty}
-deformations presented in
section 3
and
4
is known to experts. In the literature the topic is however under-represented, to the extent that it is hard to pinpoint a single location where
A_{\infty}
-deformations are defined. In what follows, we summarize three articles which gave rise to the understanding of the theory presented in this paper.

Barmeier and Wang

In
[
1
]
, Barmeier and Wang prove the power of
L_{\infty}
-morphisms in deformation theory of ordinary algebras. They depart from a quiver algebra with relations
A=\mathbb{C}Q/I
, where the ideal
I
is supposed to come from a reduction system. In order to classify all deformations of
A
, they replace the Hochschild DGLA
\operatorname{HC}(A)
by a quasi-isomorphic
L_{\infty}
-algebra

.

To define the Hochschild DGLA, we typically use the bar resolution. Barmeier and Wang demonstrate that this resolution can be substituted by any other one, in particular the very simple bimodule resolution
P_{\bullet}
of Chouhy-Solotar, see
[
1
, Section 4.2]
. The cochain map between resolutions immediately gives a quasi-isomorphism of complexes

\operatorname{Hom}_{A\otimes A^{\mathsf{opp}}}\left(\bigoplus\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}A\otimes A^{\otimes_{i}}\otimes A,A\right)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Hom}_{A\otimes A^{\mathsf{opp}}}(P_{\bullet},A).

(1.1)

The left-hand side already being a DGLA, the
L_{\infty}
-structure transfer theorem induces an
L_{\infty}
-structure on the right-hand side such that (
1.1
) becomes a quasi-isomorphism of
L_{\infty}
-algebras. Barmeier and Wang then compute part of the
L_{\infty}
-structure on the right-hand side, just enough to classify all of its Maurer-Cartan elements. All deformations of
A
can then be written down explicitly.

Barmeier and Wang’s realization that

-quasi-isomorphisms transport Maurer-Cartan elements has greatly helped us shape the curved
A_{\infty}
-deformation theory of
section 4
. There, we make constant use of the fact that quasi-equivalences of
A_{\infty}
-categories induces
L_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphisms of their Hochschild DGLAs:

F:\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{D}\quad\rightsquigarrow\quad F_{*}:\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D}).

Correspondingly, their sets of Maurer-Cartan elements over any deformation base
B
match:

\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}),B)\xrightarrow{\sim}\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D}),B).

This is the main principle that lets us push deformations to and fro between different categories. For example, it lets us seamlessly reduce an
A_{\infty}
-category to a skeleton of non-isomorphic objects without changing its deformation theory. It enable us to prove that if an object
X\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
is uncurvable, then all objects
Y\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
quasi-isomorphic to
X
are uncurvable as well.

The work of Lowen and Van den Bergh illustrates that curvature is essential in the notion of

Uncurving is a crucial contribution to our deformed Kadeishvili construction. The presence of curvature namely allows the differential of an

Preliminaries on
A_{\infty}
-categories

The notion of
A_{\infty}
-category is now widely used as a homological-algebraic tool to study symplectic and algebraic geometry. Its relation to dg categories, triangulated categories and
\infty
-categories can be described as follows:

•

DG categories are more rigid than
A_{\infty}
-categories. DG quasi-isomorphisms cannot be quasi-inverted on the dg level, while they always have a quasi-inverse if one interprets the dg structures as
A_{\infty}
-categories. In particular, zigzags of dg quasi-isomorphisms can be resolved into a single
A_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism. DG structures are very easy to work with, for instance homotopy colimits are easy to calculate due to the well-known Tabuada model structure
[
20
,
10
]
.

•

Triangulated categories are weaker than
A_{\infty}
-categories. They do not remember any of the “higher structure” that
A_{\infty}
-categories contain. The higher structure on an
A_{\infty}
-category makes the entire category reconstructible from a subcategory of generators. There is an
A_{\infty}
notion of derived category
\operatorname{D}\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, whose “lower structure”
(\operatorname{D}\mathcal{C})^{0}
is a triangulated category
[
12
]
.

•

Stable
\infty
-categories also remember the homotopy information, similar to
A_{\infty}
-categories. A priori they lack the linearity over a base field, which can be added afterwards. In fact, there is a direct correspondence between
k
-linear stable
\infty
-categories and
A_{\infty}
-categories over
k
, given in one direction by taking the derived category
[
6
,
7
]
.

Next we recall the twisted completion

Definition 2.9

A

The notion of a
curved
A_{\infty}
-category
is a generalization of ordinary
A_{\infty}
-category. In contrast to an ordinary
A_{\infty}
-category, a curved

-category also has an element
\mu^{0}_{X}
of degree
2
associated with every object

. The required curved

-relations are the same as those of an
A_{\infty}
-category, in particular allowing

to appear as inner

. The first two curved

-relations read

\mu^{1}(\mu^{0})=0,\quad\mu^{1}(\mu^{1}(a))+(-1)^{\|a\|}\mu^{2}(\mu^{0},a)+\mu^{2}(a,\mu^{0})=0.

Curved
A_{\infty}
-categories are however ill-behaved: They do not have a notion of derived category. One may form the twisted completion, but if one enforces the Maurer-Cartan equation
\mu^{0}+\mu^{1}(\delta)+\ldots=0
, very few objects remain and the construction is not functorial. If one instead discards the Maurer-Cartan requirement for twisted complexes, one obtains a category
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
with curvature, the curvature given by the Maurer-Cartan formula

. This category however has no minimal model
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, because curvature prevents us from bringing
\mu^{1}
to zero. In short, curved
A_{\infty}
-categories are not useful, except for the purpose of matrix factorizations.

We would like to stress that infinitesimal curvature however does not hurt. In
section 3.2
, we recall what infinitesimal curvature entails and we explain in
section 5
that infinitesimally curved
A_{\infty}
-categories do have derived categories.

The material in the entire section is not original. For instance, Seidel already mentioned curved

In this section, we will be comparing different deformations with another. We should set some terminology right before we get started. A “deformation base”

will be a complete local Noetherian unital
\mathbb{C}
-algebra with residue field

over

can be interpreted as object of two different universes: either as deformation of

-category. Let us depict this pictorially:
deformations of

\mathcal{C}_{q}

\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}

This perspective makes a difference: The correct notion for two deformations

3.1
The completed tensor product

In this section, we recall the completed tensor products of the form
B\widehat{\otimes}X
, where
B
is a local algebra and
X
is a vector space. This serves as a preparation for
section 3.2
where we define
A_{\infty}
-deformations.

Throughout this paper, we deform over local rings like
\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket
. There are a few more conditions we put on the local ring: In order to work with the
A_{\infty}
-formalism we need the ring to be a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra. In order to speak of a special fiber, or algebraically of an
\mathfrak{m}
-adic leading term, we need to require that its residue field is
\mathbb{C}
itself. As is customary in deformation theory, we shall also require that the ring be complete and Noetherian. We have decided to give the type of local rings a name in this paper:

Definition 3.1
.

A
deformation base
is a complete local Noetherian unital
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
B
with residue field
B/\mathfrak{m}=\mathbb{C}
. The maximal ideal is always denoted
\mathfrak{m}
.

Remark 3.2
.

By the Cohen structure theorem, every deformation base is of the form
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket/I
with
I
denoting some ideal.

The idea behind formal
A_{\infty}
-deformations is to tensor the hom spaces with a deformation base. One then looks at
A_{\infty}
-structures on the enlarged collection of hom spaces which reduce to the original
A_{\infty}
-structure once the maximal ideal is divided out. The construction of these tensored hom spaces makes use of the completed tensor product, which we now recall.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
B
be a deformation base and
X
a vector space. Then the
completed tensor product

B\widehat{\otimes}X
is the
B
-module limit

B\widehat{\otimes}X=\lim(\ldots\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{1}\otimes X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{0}\otimes X),

For simplicity, we write
\mathfrak{m}^{k}X
to denote the infinitesimal part
\mathfrak{m}^{k}X=\mathfrak{m}^{k}\widehat{\otimes}X\subseteq B\widehat{\otimes}X
.

Remark 3.4
.

In case
B=\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket
, the completed tensor product
B\widehat{\otimes}X
equals the even more well-known space
X\llbracket q\rrbracket
. The space
X\llbracket q\rrbracket
consists of formal
X
-valued power series in one variable. This way
X\llbracket q\rrbracket
becomes naturally a
\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket
-module. The space is different from
\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket\otimes X
. In fact, elements of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket\otimes X
are only those power series which can be written as a finite sum of pure tensors
a\otimes x
. Simply speaking, in
\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket\otimes X
, the power series entries are divided into finitely many partitions in which all coefficients are interrelated. Meanwhile in
X\llbracket q\rrbracket
any entries can be chosen at random. However if
X
is finite-dimensional, then
X\llbracket q\rrbracket=\mathbb{C}\llbracket q\rrbracket\otimes X
.

There are two popular ways of defining formal deformations of an associative algebra
A
. The first definition asks for a product
\mu_{q}:A\otimes A\to B\widehat{\otimes}A
and the second definition asks for a product
\mu_{q}:(B\widehat{\otimes}A)\otimes(B\widehat{\otimes}A)\to B\widehat{\otimes}A
. The first definition immediately gives rise to a Maurer-Cartan element, while in the second definition associativity is formulated more naturally. In what follows, we shall explain briefly why both are equivalent.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
B
be a deformation base. Then the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology on
B
is the topology on
B
generated by open neighborhoods
x+\mathfrak{m}^{k}\subseteq B
for
x\in B
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology on
B\widehat{\otimes}X
is generated by the open neighborhoods
x+\mathfrak{m}^{k}\widehat{\otimes}X\subseteq B\widehat{\otimes}X
for every
x\in B\widehat{\otimes}X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\,
. A map
\varphi:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is
continuous
if it is continuous with respect to the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topologies. A map
\varphi:(B\widehat{\otimes}X_{k})\otimes\ldots\otimes(B\widehat{\otimes}X_{1})\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is
continuous
if for every
1\leq i\leq k
and every sequence of elements
x_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{i},\ldots,x_{k}
the map

\mu(x_{k},\ldots,-,\ldots,x_{1}):B\widehat{\otimes}X_{i}\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y

is continuous.

Remark 3.6
.

It is well-known that the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology turns
B\widehat{\otimes}X
into a sequential Hausdorff space. It is also well-known that
B\widehat{\otimes}X
can simultaneously be interpreted as limit and completion. More precisely,
B\widehat{\otimes}X
is the completion of
B\otimes X
with respect to the so-called
\mathfrak{m}
-adic metric on
B\otimes X
. For convenience, we may from time to time use expressions like
x=\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{k})
to indicate
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{k}X
.

Remark 3.7
.

Every element in
B\widehat{\otimes}X
can be written as a series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}m_{i}x_{i}
. Here
m_{i}
is a sequence of elements
m_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}
and
x_{i}
is a sequence of elements
x_{i}\in X
. We have used the notation
m_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}
to indicate that
m_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{k_{i}}
for some sequence
(k_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
k_{i}\to\infty
.

Remark 3.8
.

Let
\varphi:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
be a
B
-linear map. If
\varphi(\mathfrak{m}^{k}X)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{k}Y
, then
\varphi
is continuous.

Whenever
X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is a linear map, we can extend it uniquely to a
B
-linear continuous map
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. Conversely, we can restrict any
B
-linear map
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
to a linear map
X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. Restriction and extension are in fact inverse to each other, providing a one-to-one correspondence:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
B
be a deformation base and
X,Y
be vector spaces. Then a
B
-linear map
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is automatically continuous. There is a one-to-one correspondence between:

•

linear maps
X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
,

•

B
-linear maps
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
,

•

B~
-linear continuous maps
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
.

Proof.

The proof consists of three parts: First, we show that every
B
-linear map
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is continuous. Second, we recall how to extend a map
X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
to
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. Third, we comment on the one-to-one aspect of the claim.

For the first step, we show that any
B
-linear map
\varphi:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is continuous. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim that
\varphi(\mathfrak{m}^{k}X)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{k}Y
. The idea is to exploit the Cohen structure theorem. Write
B=\mathbb{C}\llbracket q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\rrbracket/I
, and regard the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
. With this in mind, we can write any element
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{k}X
as a series

x=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}m_{i}\tilde{m}_{i}y_{i}.

Here
m_{i}
is a monomial of degree
k
in the variables
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
, the letter
\tilde{m}_{i}
denotes a sequence
\tilde{m}_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}
, and
y_{i}\in Y
. We conclude

x=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{monomials }M\\
\text{ of degree }k\end{subarray}}M\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\geq 0\\
m_{i}=M\end{subarray}}\tilde{m}_{i}y_{i}.

The outer sum is finite. For every monomial
M
of degree
k
, the inner sum is an element
x_{M}\in B\widehat{\otimes}X
. We get that

\varphi(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{monomials }M\\
\text{ of degree }k\end{subarray}}M\varphi(x_{M}).

We conclude that
\varphi(x)\in\mathfrak{m}^{k}Y
. This shows
\varphi(\mathfrak{m}^{k}X)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{k}Y
. In particular,
\varphi
is continuous.

For the second step, denote by
\pi_{i}
the projection maps
\pi_{i}:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes X
or
B\widehat{\otimes}Y\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y
and by
\pi_{ij}
the projection maps
\pi_{ij}:B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{j}\otimes Y
for
i>j
. Note that
(\pi_{ij}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{Y})\circ\pi_{i}=\pi_{j}
for
i>j
.

Let now
F:X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
be a linear map. In order to define a map
\hat{F}:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
, we shall build maps
\hat{F}_{i}:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y
and then use the universal property to combine them into
\hat{F}
.

Let us now construct auxiliary maps
F_{i}
and the maps
\hat{F}_{i}
. Define
F_{i}:B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y
as the
B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}
-linear extension of
\pi_{i}F:X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y
. Then let
\hat{F}_{i}:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}\otimes Y
be the composition
F_{i}\pi_{i}
. More directly, we could write
\hat{F}_{i}(z)=F(\pi_{i}(z))
, where on the right-hand side
F
is interpreted
B/\mathfrak{m}^{i}
-linearly.

We claim that
\hat{F}_{j}=(\pi_{ij}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{Y})\circ\hat{F}_{i}
for
i>j
. Indeed,

\pi_{ij}(\hat{F}_{i}(z))=(\pi_{ij}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{Y})(F(\pi_{i}(z))=F((\pi_{ij}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{X})(\pi_{i}(z)))=F(\pi_{j}(z))=\hat{F}_{j}(z).

This proves that the family of maps
\{\hat{F}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is compatible with the projections
\pi_{ij}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{Y}
. By the universal property of
B\widehat{\otimes}Y
, this family of maps factors through the limit
B\widehat{\otimes}Y
, yielding a
B
-linear map

\hat{F}:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y,\text{ with }\pi_{i}\hat{F}=\hat{F}_{i}.

The map
\hat{F}
can also be written explicitly as follows: Let
x=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}m_{i}x_{i}
be an element of
B\widehat{\otimes}X
, with
m_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}
and
x_{i}\in X
. Then
\hat{F}(\sum m_{i}x_{i})=\sum m_{i}F(x_{i})
.

As third step of the proof, we comment on the one-to-one aspect in the claim. The only remaining statement to explain is that for a given map
F:X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
, there is only one single
B
-linear continuous extension to a map
B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. But this is obvious, since
B
-linearity already determines the value on
B\otimes X
and continuity then determines the value on all of
B\widehat{\otimes}X
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We shall provide a few more standard utilities. Recall that a map
\varphi:X\to Y
of topological spaces is an
embedding
if it is a homeomorphism onto its image, the image being equipped with subspace topology. The
leading term
of a
B
-linear map
\varphi:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to\,B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is the map
\varphi_{0}:X\to Y
given by the composition
\varphi_{0}=\pi\varphi|_{X}
, where
\pi:B\widehat{\otimes}Y\to Y
denotes the standard projection.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
X,Y
be vector spaces and
\varphi:B\widehat{\otimes}X\to B\widehat{\otimes}Y
be
B
-linear with injective leading term. Then
\varphi
is an embedding and its image is closed. If the leading term is surjective, then
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Before we dive into the proof, we start with an observation regarding the leading term. Let
\varphi_{0}:X\to Y
be the leading term of
\varphi
and define for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
the map

\varphi_{i}:\mathfrak{m}^{i}/\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}\otimes X\to\mathfrak{m}^{i}/\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}\otimes Y

(3.1)

induced from
\varphi
. Our observation is that
\varphi_{i}
is in fact equal to
\mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{m}^{i}/\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}}\otimes\varphi_{0}
.

We are now ready to start the proof. First we show that
\varphi
is injective. Second we show that
\varphi^{-1}:\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)\to B\widehat{\otimes}X
is continuous. Third we show that
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
is closed. Fourth we prove
\varphi
surjective if
\varphi_{0}
is surjective.

For the first part of the proof, we show that
\varphi
injective. Let
x\in B\widehat{\otimes}X
with
\varphi(x)=0
. By induction, we show that
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
. For
i=0
this is clear. As induction hypothesis, assume that
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
. Then
\varphi_{i}([x])=[\varphi(x)]=0
. Since
\varphi_{i}
is injective, we obtain
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}X
. This finishes the induction. In consequence we have
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
and therefore
x=0
. This proves
\varphi
injective.

For the second part of the proof, we show that
\varphi^{-1}
preserves the filtration by
\mathfrak{m}
. In other words, we show that
\varphi(x)\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}Y
implies
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
. We do this by induction over
i\in\mathbb{N}\,
. For
i=0
this is clear. Assume this statements holds for a certain
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Now let
\varphi(x)\in\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}Y
. Then
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
by induction hypothesis. We get
\varphi_{i}([x])=0
, since
\varphi(x)\in\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}Y
. Since
\varphi_{i}
is injective, we get
x\in\mathfrak{m}^{i+1}X
. This finishes the induction. Finally, we have shown
\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{m}^{i}Y)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{i}X
. This renders
\varphi^{-1}:\varphi(B\widehat{\otimes}X)\to B\widehat{\otimes}X
continuous.

For the third part of the proof, we check that
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
is closed. Since
B\widehat{\otimes}Y
is a sequential space, it suffices to check that any sequence
\varphi(x_{n})\subseteq\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
converging in
B\widehat{\otimes}Y
converges in
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
. Pick a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B\widehat{\otimes}X
and assume
\varphi(x_{n})\to y\in B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. This makes
\varphi(x_{n})-y\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}Y
. We have
\varphi(x_{n})-\varphi(x_{m})\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}Y
and by continuity of
\varphi^{-1}
we get
x_{n}-x_{m}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\to\infty}
. In particuar,
x_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence and converges to some
x\in B\widehat{\otimes}X
. We get
\varphi(x)=\lim\varphi(x_{n})=y
and therefore
y\in\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
. This proves
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)
closed.

For the fourth part of the proof, consider
y\in B\widehat{\otimes}Y
. We construct inductively a sequence
(x_{n})
with
x_{n}\in\mathfrak{m}^{n}\widehat{\otimes}X
such that
\varphi(\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i})=\pi_{n}(y)
. For
i=0
, let
x_{0}\in X
be defined as
x_{0}=\varphi_{0}^{-1}(\pi_{0}(y))
. Now assume the sequence has been constructed for indices until
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Put
z\coloneqq y-\varphi(\sum^{n}x_{i})\in\mathfrak{m}^{n}Y
. Since
\varphi_{0}
is an isomorphism, the map
\varphi_{n}
from (
3.1
) is an isomorphism as well. Put

x_{n+1}^{\prime}\coloneqq\varphi_{n+1}^{-1}(z)\in\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}/\mathfrak{m}^{n+2}X.

Let
x_{n+1}\in\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}X
be any lift of
x_{n+1}^{\prime}
. Then we have

\varphi\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}x_{i}\right)=y-z+\varphi(x_{n+1})=y+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{n+2}).

This finishes the inductive construction of the sequence
(x_{i})
. Finally, the series
x=\sum x_{i}
converges and
\varphi(x)=y
. This proves
\varphi
surjective.
∎

The pathway to defining
A_{\infty}
-deformations is now clear: When
\mathcal{C}
is an
A_{\infty}\,
-category, a deformation of
\mathcal{C}
will always be modeled on the collection of enlarged hom spaces
\{B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)\}_{X,Y\in\mathcal{C}}
. Any
B
-multilinear product on these hom spaces is automatically continuous. Similarly, functors of
A_{\infty}
-deformations will be defined as maps between tensor products of the enlarged hom spaces and will be automatically continuous as well.

3.2
Curved deformations

In this section, we give a quick definition of curved deformations of
A_{\infty}
-categories. We have already used this notion in
[
4
]
. The notion is not surprising and known to experts. Despite being uncomfortable to work with, curvature comes naturally via the Hochschild complex and is necessary if one aims at deformation theory invariant under quasi-equivalences. The curvature in
A_{\infty}
-deformations will always be infinitesimal, which we consider harmless in contrast with the different notion of curved
A_{\infty}
-categories
[
5
]
.

Let us recall the setup of Hochschild cohomology in the classical case. Let
A
be an associative algebra. As the reader probably knows, Hochschild cohomology
\operatorname{HH}^{2}(A)
captures the associative deformations of the algebra over
\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})
. More precisely, a 2-cochain
\nu\in\operatorname{HC}^{2}(A)
is a cocycle if and only if
\mu+\varepsilon\nu
is an associative product on
A\oplus A\varepsilon
(note
\varepsilon^{2}=0
). Classical Hochschild cohomology in other degrees than 2 helps characterize the deformation problem, but provides no actual deformations.

Since Hochschild’s original definition in 1946, Hochschild cohomology has been generalized to
A_{\infty}
-categories. The trick is to use the same formula for the differential, with two adaptations: include also higher products
\mu^{k}
instead of only
\mu^{2}
, and use grading induced from the shift
\mathcal{C}[1]
. We will make this precise in
section 4.1
.

What should an
A_{\infty}
-deformation be then? The naive answer would be to allow deformations of all products
\mu^{k}
. This is however too shortsighted: We contend that for a notion of
A_{\infty}
-deformations whose infinitesimal deformations are classified by a cochain complex (or better a DGLA), the
A_{\infty}
-Hochschild complex is the natural choice. Its Maurer-Cartan elements consist however not only of deformations to the products
\mu^{k}
with
k\geq 1
, but also introduce curvature
\mu^{0}
.

We are now ready to define
A_{\infty}
-deformations precisely.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
category with products
\mu
and
B
a deformation base. An (infinitesimally curved)
deformation

\mathcal{C}_{q}
of
\mathcal{C}
consists of

•

The same objects as
\mathcal{C}
,

•

Hom spaces
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X,Y)=B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
for
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
,

•

B
-multilinear products of degree
2-k

\mu_{q}^{k}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X_{k},X_{k+1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X_{1},X_{2})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X_{1},X_{k+1}),~k\geq 1

•

Curvature of degree
2
for every object
X\in\mathcal{C}

\mu_{q,X}^{0}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X,X),

such that
\mu_{q}
reduces to
\mu
once the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq R
is divided out, and
\mu_{q}
satisfies the curved
A_{\infty}
(
cA_{\infty}
) relations

\sum_{k\geq l\geq m\geq 0}(-1)^{\|a_{m}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|}\mu_{q}(a_{k},\ldots,\mu_{q}(a_{l},\ldots),a_{m},\ldots,a_{1})=0.

The deformation is
unital
if the deformed higher products still satisfy the unitality axioms

\mu^{2}_{q}(a,\mathrm{id}_{X})=a,~\mu^{2}_{q}(\mathrm{id}_{Y},a)=(-1)^{|a|}a,~\mu^{\geq 3}_{q}(\ldots,\mathrm{id}_{X},\ldots)=0.

We use the terms
A_{\infty}
-deformation and deformation of an
A_{\infty}
-category interchangeably. Whenever we speak of deformations of
A_{\infty}
-categories in this paper, they are allowed to be (infinitesimally) curved.

Remark 3.12
.

As explained in
section 3.1
, the product
\mu^{k}_{q}
is automatically
\mathfrak{m}
-adically continuous. Spelling out this continuity requirement, the datum of the map
\mu^{k}
is equivalent to the datum of merely multilinear maps

\mu_{q}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{k},X_{k+1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{2})~\to~B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{k+1}).

There is a notion for two objects to be quasi-isomorphic in an
A_{\infty}
-deformation. We provide here an ad-hoc definition which seems odd at first, but we will encounter evidence in
section 5
and
6
which supports correctness of the definition.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
be two objects. Then
X
and
Y
are
quasi-isomorphic
if they are quasi-isomorphic in
\mathcal{C}
.

It sometimes comes handy to work with deformations that include more objects than
\mathcal{C}
does. We fix terminology as follows:

Definition 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category. Let
O
be an arbitrary set of objects and
F:O\to\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
a map. Then the
object-cloned version

F^{*}\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{C}
is the
A_{\infty}\,
-category given by object set
O
, hom spaces

\operatorname{Hom}_{F^{*}\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(F(X),F(Y)),\quad X,Y\in O,

and products simply given by the same composition as in
\mathcal{C}
.

An
A_{\infty}
-deformation of
\mathcal{C}
always gives an induced deformation of
F^{*}\mathcal{C}
. This provides a map from deformations of
\mathcal{C}
to deformations of
F^{*}\mathcal{C}
. In case
F
is surjective, the categories
\mathcal{C}
and
F^{*}\mathcal{C}
are equivalent and as we elaborate in
section 4
actually have the same deformation theory. For this reason, we fix the following terminology:

Definition 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
B
a deformation base. Let
O
be an arbitrary set and
F:O\to\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{C}
a map. An
object-cloning deformation
is a deformation
\mathcal{D}_{q}
of
\mathcal{D}=F^{*}\mathcal{C}
. The object-cloning deformation is
essentially surjective
if
F:\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{D}\to\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal{C}
reaches all objects of
\mathcal{C}
up to isomorphism.

3.3
Twisted completion

In this section, we define the twisted completion of a deformed
A_{\infty}
-category. The starting point is an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}
together with a deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The aim is to define a deformation
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
which incorporates the deformation given by
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. More formally, the aim is to push the deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
onto
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
along the inclusion
\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
which is a derived equivalence. Since we only explain the pushforward of deformations in
section 4
, we give here a direct and explicit definition of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

We start by defining additive completions for deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. The aim is to define an additive completion
\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}
in such a way that it is a deformation of
\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}
. This is straight-forward:

Definition 3.16
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Then the
additive completion

\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the deformation of
\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}
given by the following deformed product:

\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}^{k}(\alpha_{k},\ldots,\alpha_{1})=\sum(-1)^{\sum_{j<i}\|\alpha_{i}\|l_{j}}\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}^{k}(\alpha_{k},\ldots,\alpha_{1}),

with the same sign convention as in the non-deformed case.

Remark 3.17
.

The only difference between
\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}}
and
\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}
lies in using the non-deformed product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
for the former and the deformed product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
for the latter.

We now extend the twisted completion construction to the case of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. The aim is to define a twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
in such a way that it is a deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
. In particular, the category
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
should have the same objects as
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
. With this in mind, we define:

Definition 3.18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
category with products
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation with products
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
. Then the
twisted completion

\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the (possibly curved) deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
given by the deformed products

\mu_{\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}}^{k}(\alpha_{k},\ldots,\alpha_{1})=\sum_{n_{0},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 0}\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{n_{k}},\alpha_{k},\ldots,\alpha_{1},\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{n_{0}}).

Remark 3.19
.

As one may have expected, the product
\mu_{\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}}
now simply uses
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
instead of
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
. The set of objects of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
may however be surprising: The objects of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are not formed with twisted differentials
\delta\in\operatorname{Hom}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X,X)
. Instead, the objects of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are twisted complexes
(X,\delta)\in\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, in other words, their
\delta
-differential must lie in
\operatorname{Hom}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,X)
. It is easily checked that
\mu_{\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}}
satisfies the (curved)
A_{\infty}
-axioms, rendering
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
a genuine
A_{\infty}
-deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3.20
.

Denoting the twisted completion of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
by
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
constitutes a slight abuse of notation: “
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
” suggests that twisted complexes are to be taken with the
\delta
-differential formed from values in
\mathcal{C}_{q}
, which is not the case. A more proper notation for the twisted completion of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
would have been
(\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C})_{q}
, which we however found too complicated.

Remark 3.21
.

Typical objects in
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
have curvature. Indeed, according to
Definition 3.18
, an object
(X,\delta)\in\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
has curvature

\mu^{0}_{(X,\delta)}=\mu^{0}_{X,\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}+\mu^{1}_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\delta)+\mu^{2}_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\delta,\delta)+\ldots.

The curvature
\mu^{0}_{X,\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}
can be spelled out more concretely as the sum of the curvatures of all constituents
A_{i}
of
X
. The differential
\mu^{1}_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\delta)
is concretely the sum of the deformed differentials
\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
applied to each entry of
\delta
as a matrix. Whatever the value of
\mu^{0}_{(X,\delta)}
adds up to, the reader can see that it does typically not vanish because the twisted differential
\delta\in\operatorname{Hom}^{1}(X,X)
only satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation with respect to the non-deformed differential
\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Remark 3.22
.

A popular way to form twisted completions of curved categories is to pick only curvature-free twisted complexes. This might be the way to luck in case of curved
A_{\infty}
-categories, because a curved twisted completion cannot be passed to the minimal model. In contrast, for
A_{\infty}
-deformations, curvature on
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is not problematic at all. Picking all twisted complexes of
\mathcal{C}
is in fact necessary in order to render
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
.

3.4
Functors

In this section, we define the notion of functors between deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. These functors serve as a framework for gauge equivalences, quasi-equivalences and pushforwards of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. This class of functors is presumably known to experts. Our sign conventions are those of
[
8
]
and
[
2
, Section 3.1.4/symplectic]
.

Based on the classical definition of
A_{\infty}
-functors which we recalled in
Definition 2.3
, we are now ready to explain the natural extension to the deformed case. We fix the definition as follows:

Definition 3.23
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be two
A_{\infty}
-categories and
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{D}_{q}
deformations. A
functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories
consists of a map
F_{q}:\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{C})\to\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal{D})
together with for every
k\geq 1
a
B
-multilinear degree
1-k
map

F^{k}_{q}:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X_{k},X_{k+1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X_{1},X_{2})\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(F_{q}X_{1},F_{q}X_{k+1})

and infinitesimal curvature
F^{0}_{q,X}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{Hom}^{1}_{\mathcal{D}}(F_{q}X,F_{q}X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{C}
, such that the curved
A_{\infty}
-functor relations hold:

\sum_{0\leq j\leq i\leq k}(-1)^{\|a_{j}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|}F_{q}(a_{k},\ldots,a_{i+1},\mu_{q}(a_{i},\ldots,a_{j+1}),a_{j},\ldots,a_{1})\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\geq 0\\
1=j_{1}\leq\ldots\leq j_{l}\leq k\end{subarray}}\mu_{q}(F_{q}(a_{k},\ldots,a_{j_{l}}),\ldots,F_{q}(\ldots,a_{j_{2}}),F_{q}(\ldots,a_{j_{1}})).

Remark 3.24
.

A functor
F_{q}
of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories consists of maps between hom spaces which are allowed to have deformed (nonconstant) terms themselves. As we have seen in
section 3.1
, the maps
F_{q}^{k}
are automatically continuous. Apart from the components
F_{q}^{\geq 1}
, the functor is allowed to have infinitesimal curvature
F^{0}_{q}
. This curvature is a feature of the deformed world where infinitesimal curvature is not only welcome, but is necessary. The first two curved
A_{\infty}
-functor relations read

\displaystyle F^{0}_{q}+F^{1}_{q}(\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q},X})

\displaystyle=\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(F^{0}_{q,X}),

\displaystyle F^{1}_{q}(\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(a))+(-1)^{\|a\|}F^{2}_{q}(\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q},Y},a)+F^{2}_{q}(a,\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q},X})

\displaystyle=\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(F^{1}_{q}(a))+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(F^{0}_{q,Y},F^{1}_{q}(a))

\displaystyle\quad+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(F^{1}_{q}(a),F^{0}_{q,X}),\quad\forall~a:X\to Y.

We can interpret a functor
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
as an extension of an ordinary
A_{\infty}
-functor
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
. Indeed, when forgetting the terms of
F=\{F_{q}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
in higher
\mathfrak{m}
-adic order, we get a collection of maps
F=\{F^{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Since
F_{q}
satisfies the curved
A_{\infty}
-functor relations, its reduction
F
satisfies the ordinary
A_{\infty}
-functor relations. We fix this terminology as follows:

Definition 3.25
.

Let
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
be a functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. Then its
leading term
is the functor
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
obtained by dividing out the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
.

Let us now define various notions of equivalences between deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. The aim is to transfer the notions of equivalences between
A_{\infty}
-categories recalled in
Definition 2.4
and
2.5
to the world of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. The main idea is to declare a functor
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
an isomorphism if its leading term
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is an isomorphism. Note that this definition is not vacuous: It still requires the functor
F_{q}
to satisfy the (curved)
A_{\infty}
-relations, but displays the isomorphism property as a side issue which is dealt with on the leading term part.

Definition 3.26
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be
A_{\infty}
-categories and
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{D}_{q}
deformations. Let
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
be a functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories and denote by
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
its leading term. Then
F_{q}
is

•

an
isomorphism
if
F
is an isomorphism,

•

a
quasi-isomorphism
if
F
is a quasi-isomorphism,

•

a
quasi-equivalence
if
F
is a quasi-equivalence,

•

a
derived equivalence
if
F
is a derived equivalence.

Two deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
are

•

isomorphic
if there is an isomorphism
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
,

•

quasi-isomorphic
if there is a quasi-isomorphism
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
,

•

quasi-equivalent
if there is a quasi-equivalence
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
,

•

derived equivalent
if
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent.

We explain in
Lemma 4.19
why relations such as quasi-equivalence and derived equivalence are equivalence relations among deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. Among the
A_{\infty}
-deformations of one single category
\mathcal{C}
, there is one further notion of equivalence, known as gauge equivalence:

Definition 3.27
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
be deformations. Then a
gauge equivalence
between
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
is a functor
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories whose leading term
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
is the identity.

We elaborate gauge equivalence further in the context of the Hochschild DGLA in
section 4.2
.

4
The lens of DGLAs

In this section, we show how to abstractly transfer
A_{\infty}
-deformations from one category onto another.

In
section 4.1
, we recall how to view
A_{\infty}
-deformations as Maurer-Cartan elements via the so-called Hochschild DGLA. In
section 4.2
, we recall the notion of gauge equivalence of Maurer-Cartan elements and explain its application in the specific case of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. In
section 4.3
, we recall the generalization of DGLAs known as
L_{\infty}
-algebras. In
section 4.4
, we explain how to push forward deformations between quasi-equivalent categories.

For the interpretation of gauge equivalences and pushforwards of deformations in terms of
L_{\infty}
-theory we will not provide any proof. Rather, we state in
section 4.4
a collection of axioms on the Hochschild DGLA which we will just assume in this paper.

The material in this section is not original. The Hochschild DGLA is classical at least in the case of ordinary algebras. Keller proved its invariance under quasi-equivalence in the dg case in
[
9
]
.

Instead, this section serves to support a very specific viewpoint on
A_{\infty}
-deformations. While there is widespread belief that curvature is a hindrance, we want to demonstrate here that it is at least possible to live peacefully with infinitesimal curvature. Let us present which section supports which claim: From
section 3.3
we learn that the twisted completion of an
A_{\infty}
-deformation can be formed even under the presence of infinitesimal curvature. This construction is entirely natural in the sense that the deformed twisted completion is a deformation of the twisted completion of the non-deformed
A_{\infty}
-category. From
section 4.1
we learn that infinitesimal curvature is inevitable for a good notion of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. The core summary is that curvature does not hurt for constructions with
A_{\infty}
-categories because it enters the picture automatically when a deformation is induced from one category to another.

4.1
The Hochschild DGLA

In this section, we recall how to view
A_{\infty}
-deformations from the DGLA point of view. The material is all known to experts and nicely shows how curvature enters the picture. Useful references include
[
16
, Chapter V]
and
[
21
]
.

Before we recall the concept of DGLA and Maurer-Cartan elements, let us summarize the philosophy: The aim is to capture every deformation problem by a DGLA. The solutions of the deformation problem should then correspond to Maurer-Cartan elements of the DGLA. This empowers the mathematician to use the force of DGLA theory. Standard questions in DGLAs include: to find a quasi-isomorphism
F:L\to L^{\prime}
between two DGLAs, or to classify all Maurer-Cartan elements of
L
up to gauge equivalence.
deformation problem
DGLA

2. algebraic power
3. harvest

We are now ready to recall the notion of DGLAs and Maurer-Cartan elements.

Definition 4.1
.

A
DG Lie algebra
(DGLA) is a
\mathbb{Z}
- or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space
L
together with

•

a differential
d:L^{i}\to L^{i+1}
,

•

a bracket
[-,-]:L\times L\to L
of degree zero,

satisfying skew-symmetry, Leibniz rule and Jacobi identity:

\displaystyle[a,b]=(-1)^{|a||b|+1}[b,a],

\displaystyle d([a,b])=[da,b]+(-1)^{|a|}[a,db],

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a||c|}[a,[b,c]]+(-1)^{|b||a|}[b,[c,a]]+(-1)^{|c||b|}[c,[a,b]]=0.

For example, the bracket
[-,-]
is commutative on odd elements. With this consideration, we recall the definition of Maurer-Cartan elements:

Definition 4.2
.

Let
L
be a DGLA and
B
a deformation base. Regard the tensored DGLA
B\widehat{\otimes}L
with differential
d
and bracket
[-,-]
simply extended continuously. A
Maurer-Cartan element
of
L
over
B
is an element
\nu\in B\widehat{\otimes}L^{1}
which satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation

d\nu+\frac{1}{2}[\nu,\nu]=0.

The set of Maurer-Cartan elements of
L
over
B
is denoted
\operatorname{MC}(L,B)
. In case the DGLA
L
is
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded, a Maurer-Cartan element is supposed to lie in
B\widehat{\otimes}L^{\operatorname{odd}}
.

In the rest of this section, we will make sense of these definitions in the case of the so-called Hochschild DGLA:

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
- or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded
A_{\infty}
-category. Then its Hochschild complex
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
is the graded vector space

\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{1},\ldots,X_{k+1}\in\mathcal{C}\\
k\geq 0\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Hom}\big(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{k},X_{k+1})[1]\otimes\ldots\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{2})[1],~\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{k+1})[1]\big).

Here
[1]
denotes the left-shift and
\|a\|=|a|-1
denotes the reduced degree of a morphism
a\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
. The grading
\|\cdot\|
on
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
is the one induced from the shifted degrees of the hom spaces of
\mathcal{C}
. In other words, we have

\|\eta(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})\|=\|\eta\|+\|a_{k}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|,\quad\eta\in\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}).

For
\eta,\omega\in\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
, the Gerstenhaber product
\mu\cdot\omega\in\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
is defined as

(\eta\cdot\omega)(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1})=\sum(-1)^{(\|a_{l}\|+\ldots+\|a_{1}\|)\|\omega\|}\eta(a_{k},\ldots,\omega(\ldots),a_{l},\ldots,a_{1}).

The
Hochschild DGLA
is the following
\mathbb{Z}
- or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded DGLA structure on
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
: The bracket on
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
is the Gerstenhaber bracket

[\eta,\omega]=\eta\cdot\omega-(-1)^{\|\omega\|\|\eta\|}\omega\cdot\eta.

The differential on
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
consists of commuting with the product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}\in\operatorname{HC}^{1}(\mathcal{C})
:

d\nu=[\mu_{\mathcal{C}},\nu].

Remark 4.4
.

It is not hard to check that
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
is indeed a DGLA. The reader who wishes to perform the computation is advised to write all double brackets in terms of Gerstenhaber products and use the associator relation

\displaystyle(a\cdot b)\cdot c-a\cdot(b\cdot c)

\displaystyle=\sum(-1)^{\|b\|(\|a_{1}\|+\ldots+\|a_{i}\|)+\|c\|(\|a_{1}\|+\ldots+\|a_{j}\|)+\|b\|\|c\|}a(\ldots,b(\ldots),a_{i},\ldots,c(\ldots),a_{j},\ldots)

\displaystyle+\sum(-1)^{\|b\|(\|a_{1}\|+\ldots+\|a_{j}\|)+\|c\|(\|a_{1}\|+\ldots+\|a_{i}\|)}a(\ldots,c(\ldots),a_{i},\ldots,b(\ldots),a_{j},\ldots)).

The sum in these formulas runs over all ways to insert
b
and
c
into
a
. Despite the way the formulas are written, it is not necessary that any elements
a_{i}
or
a_{j}
actually lie in between or behind
b
and
c
. This is merely an artifact needed to define the sign right: the sign shall be the total reduced degree of all elements coming after
b
or after
c
, respectively.

Remark 4.5
.

In the terminology of the Hochschild DGLA
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
, we can interpret
A_{\infty}
-deformations of
\mathcal{C}
precisely as Maurer-Cartan elements of
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
. We start by observing that the
A_{\infty}
-product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
amounts to an element
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}\in\operatorname{HC}^{1}(\mathcal{C})
and the
A_{\infty}
-relations translate to
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot\mu_{\mathcal{C}}=0
.

Now let
\mathcal{C}_{q}
be a deformation of
\mathcal{C}
. Then the curved
A_{\infty}
-relations for
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
translate to
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\cdot\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}=0
. Decompose
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}=\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\nu
as non-deformed part
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
plus deformation
\nu\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{HC}^{1}(\mathcal{C})
. Given that
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
already satisfies the
A_{\infty}
-relation
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}\cdot\mu_{\mathcal{C}}=0
, the element
\nu
itself satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation

0=(\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\nu)\cdot(\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\nu)=d\nu+[\nu,\nu]/2.

Conversely, pick a Maurer-Cartan element
\nu\in\operatorname{MC}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}),B)
. According to
Lemma 3.9
, the element
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\nu
extends in a multilinear and
\mathfrak{m}
-adically continuous way to a collection of mappings

\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}^{k\geq 0}:(B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{k},X_{k+1}))\otimes\ldots\otimes(B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{2}))\to B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X_{1},X_{k+1}).

The Maurer-Cartan identity for
\nu
makes that
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
satisfies the curved
A_{\infty}
-relations.

Remark 4.6
.

In case
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded DGLA, then Maurer-Cartan elements of
L
are by definition odd elements
\nu\in\mathfrak{m}L^{\operatorname{odd}}
with
d\nu+[\nu,\nu]/2=0
. For example, our deformation
\operatorname{Gtl}_{q}Q
is only a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded deformation of
\operatorname{Gtl}Q
. In the context of deformations, we have to view both
\operatorname{Gtl}Q
and its Hochschild DGLA
\operatorname{HC}(\operatorname{Gtl}Q)
as
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded.

Remark 4.7
.

For ordinary algebras, which are concentrated in degree zero and have vanishing higher products, the Hochschild cohomology is typically defined without the shifts. This results in a grading difference of
1
from what we present here. For example, the center of the algebra is the classical zeroth Hochschild cohomology. In our
A_{\infty}
-setting, this cohomology will rather be found in degree
-1
.

4.2
Gauge equivalence

In this section, we recall the notion of gauge equivalence. By virtue of algebraic deformation theory, we have two ways of defining this equivalence: via an explicit definition and via the Hochschild DGLA. Both notions are defined here in parallel. Useful references are
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
.

Gauge equivalence
by functor
F:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
Gauge equivalence
as MC elements
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\sim\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}

Recall from
Definition 3.27
that a gauge equivalence between two deformations
\mathcal{C}_{q}
,
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
of an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}
consists of a functor
F:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
whose leading term is the identity.

Remark 4.8
.

The idea behind
Definition 3.27
is that the set of automorphisms
\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
as deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories is rather large. The leading term of an automorphism can be any automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
, which is not interesting from the perspective of deformation theory. Therefore one restricts to those functors whose leading term is the identity on
\mathcal{C}
.

After the success of the notion of gauge equivalence throughout mathematics and physics, a definition has also been captured in the abstract DGLA approach. The idea here is that the gauging functor
F_{q}
can be viewed as an element of a “gauge group”. The infinitesimal action of this gauge group can be described purely in terms of the DGLA:

Definition 4.9
.

Let
L
be a DGLA and
B
a deformation base. Then there is a group action by
\exp(\mathfrak{m}L^{0})
on
B\widehat{\otimes}L^{1}
with infinitesimal generator

\varphi.\nu=d\varphi+[\nu,\varphi]\in B\widehat{\otimes}L^{1},\quad\nu\in\mathfrak{m}L^{0}.

The group action preserves the set of Maurer-Cartan elements
\operatorname{MC}(L;B)
. Two Maurer-Cartan elements
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\operatorname{MC}(L;B)
are
gauge-equivalent
if they lie in the same orbit. The set of Maurer-Cartan elements up to gauge-equivalence is denoted
\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(L;B)
.

Remark 4.10
.

In case
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded DGLA, then the gauge group is
\exp(\mathfrak{m}L^{\operatorname{even}})
. This group acts on Maurer-Cartan elements of
L
, which are by definition odd elements
\nu\in\mathfrak{m}L^{\operatorname{odd}}
with
d\nu+[\nu,\nu]/2=0
.

Let us compare the infinitesimal generators of the action, at the non-deformed product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
: An “infinitesimal functor”
\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon F
pushes the non-deformed product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
to some
\mu^{\prime}
such that
(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon F)\cdot\mu_{\mathcal{C}}=\mu^{\prime}\circ(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon F)
. Here
\circ
denotes functor composition, in contrast to the Gerstenhaber product “
\cdot
”. Setting
\varepsilon^{2}=0
, we read off

\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\varepsilon[\mu_{\mathcal{C}},F].

Meanwhile, the trivial deformation
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
corresponds to the zero Maurer-Cartan element in
\operatorname{MC}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}))
. Gauging it by
\varepsilon F
under
\varepsilon^{2}=0
gives the element

(\varepsilon F).0=0+d(\varepsilon F)+[0,\varepsilon F]=\varepsilon[\mu_{\mathcal{C}},F].

This element corresponds to the deformation
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\varepsilon[\mu_{\mathcal{C}},F]
. We see that gauging a deformation by a gauge functor
\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon F
is the same as gauging its corresponding Maurer-Cartan element in the Hochschild DGLA:
Infinitesimal functor
\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon F
\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+\varepsilon[\mu_{\mathcal{C}},F]
Infinitesimal DGLA gauge
\varepsilon F
\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathcal{C}}+(d(\varepsilon F)+[0,\varepsilon F])

More precisely, two deformations are gauge equivalent in the sense of
Definition 3.27
if and only if their corresponding Maurer-Cartan elements are gauge equivalent in the sense of
Definition 4.9
.

Remark 4.11
.

A_{\infty}
-Hochschild cohomology of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as the cohomology of
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
, merely regarded as a cochain complex instead of DGLA. This way, Hochschild cohomology is the linear approximation of Maurer-Cartan elements up to gauge equivalence.

4.3
L_{\infty}
-algebras

In this section we recall the formalism of
L_{\infty}
-algebras. In our context of
A_{\infty}
-categories, we namely want to transport deformations from one category to another, so that one needs morphisms between their Hochschild DGLAs. The world of DGLAs and their morphisms is quite restrictive, but there is a more flexible version of DGLAs known as
L_{\infty}
-algebras. We finish this section with a definition and brief discussion of the
L_{\infty}
-theory. We follow version 3 of
[
1
]
. The reader be cautioned that version 4 of that paper has changed signs.

Definition 4.12
.

An
L_{\infty}
-algebra
is a graded vector space
L
together with multilinear maps

l_{k}:\underbrace{L\times\ldots\times L}_{k\text{ times}}\to L

of degree
2-k
which are graded skew-symmetric and satisfy the higher Jacobi identities:

\displaystyle l_{k}(x_{s(1)},\ldots,x_{s(k)})=\chi(s)l_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k+1\\
i,j\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{s\in S_{i,k-i}}(-1)^{i(n-i)}\chi(s)l_{j}(l_{i}(x_{s(1)},\ldots,x_{s(i)}),x_{s(i+1)},\ldots,x_{s(k)})=0.

Here
S_{i,k-i}
denotes the set of shuffles, i.e.
s\in S_{k}
with
s(1)<\ldots<s(i)
and
s(i+1)<\ldots<s(k)
. The sign
\chi(s)
is the product of the signum of
s
and the Koszul sign of
s
. The Koszul sign of a transposition
(i~j)
is
(-1)^{|x_{i}||x_{j}|}
, and this rule is extended multiplicatively to arbitrary permutations.

With this sign convention, a DGLA as in
Definition 4.1
is simply an
L_{\infty}
-algebra without higher products. In particular, the Hochschild DGLA can automatically be regarded as an
L_{\infty}
-algebra.

Morphisms between
L_{\infty}
-algebras are indeed more flexible than between DGLAs:
L_{\infty}
-morphisms are allowed to have higher components, just like
A_{\infty}
-functors allow for higher components.

Definition 4.13
.

A
morphism of
L_{\infty}
-algebras

\varphi:L\to L^{\prime}
is given by multilinear maps

\varphi^{k}:L\times\ldots\times L\to L

of degree
1-k
for all
k\geq 1
such that
\varphi(x_{s(1)},\ldots,x_{s(k)})=\chi(s)\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
for any
s\in S_{k}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=k+1\\
i,j\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{s\in S_{i,n-i}}(-1)^{i(k-i)}\chi(s)\varphi(l(x_{s(1)},\ldots,x_{s(i)}),x_{s(i+1)},\ldots,x_{s(k)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq k\\
i_{1}+\ldots+i_{r}=k\end{subarray}}\sum_{t}(-1)^{u}\chi(t)l^{\prime}_{r}(\varphi(x_{t(1)},\ldots,x_{t(i_{1})}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{t(i_{1}+\ldots+i_{r-1}+1)},\ldots,x_{t(k)})),

where
t
runs over all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
-shuffles for which

t(i_{1}+\ldots+i_{l-1}+1)<t(i_{1}+\ldots+i_{l}+1).

and
u=(r-1)(i_{1}-1)+\ldots+2(i_{r-2}-1)+(i_{r-1}-1)
. A morphism
\varphi:L\to L^{\prime}
of
L_{\infty}
-algebras is a
quasi-isomorphism
if
\varphi^{1}
is a quasi-isomorphism of complexes.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
L
be an
L_{\infty}
-algebra and
B
a deformation base. Then a
Maurer-Cartan element
is an element
x\in\mathfrak{m}\widehat{\otimes}L^{1}
satisfying the Maurer-Cartan equation

\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{l_{k}(x,\ldots,x)}{k!}=0.

We write
\operatorname{MC}(L,B)
for the set of Maurer-Cartan elements of
L
over
B
.

In contrast to the DGLA case, there is no gauge group acting on the Maurer-Cartan elements. Instead, one should regard an equivalence relation of “homotopy”. All we should assume here is that the notion of homotopy exists and gives rise to a quotient set
\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(L,B)
, just as in the DGLA case.

4.4
Axioms on
A_{\infty}
-deformations

There is a slight gap in our treatment of
A_{\infty}
-deformations: We cannot prove invariance of the Hochschild DGLA under quasi-equivalences. While derived invariance is known in the dg case due to
[
9
]
, according to private communication with Keller there is no literature available for the
A_{\infty}
-case. We do not fill the gap here. Instead, we state a small set of axioms in this section which we will simply assume for the purpose of
section 5.4
and
5.2
.

The motivation for our axioms is that we need to be able to push deformations from one category to another. Assume two
A_{\infty}
-categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are quasi-equivalent by means of a quasi-equivalence
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
. Intuition says that a deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
of
\mathcal{C}
can be “pushed” via
F
to a deformation
\mathcal{D}_{q}=F_{*}(\mathcal{C}_{q})
of
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
still quasi-equivalent to each other by a deformation of the functor
F
. We formalize this notion as follows:

Definition 4.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
be a deformation. Let
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a quasi-equivalence. Then we call any deformation
\mathcal{D}_{q}
of
\mathcal{D}
a
naive pushforward
of the deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
along
F
if there exists a functor
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
with leading term
F
.

Remark 4.16
.

If forming Hochschild DGLAs were functorial, pushforwards would be easy. Indeed, a quasi-equivalence
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
would ideally induce a quasi-isomorphism of
L_{\infty}
-algebras
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})\to\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D})
and therefore a bijection
\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\mathcal{C},B)\to\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\mathcal{D},B)
of Maurer-Cartan elements. The naive pushforward of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
would then simply be obtained as the image under this map of the Maurer-Cartan elemement defined by
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. It is an awkward fact of algebra that however neither the Hochschild DGLA nor Hochschild cohomology is functorial. For instance, even the center
\operatorname{HH}^{0}(A)=Z(A)
of an algebra
A
is not functorial a property. Two quasi-equivalent
A_{\infty}
-categories however have quasi-isomorphic Hochschild DGLAs. At least, this is a folklore statement, with actual proof only available by Keller in the dg case
[
9
]
.

This definition of naive pushforwards raises many detail questions. For instance, let
G:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{E}
be yet another quasi-equivalence. Then we are interested in the question whether the double pushforward of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
along
F
and
G
is gauge-equivalent to the single pushforward of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
along
GF
. We shall provide answer to this question in the form of axioms:

Convention 4.17
.

We assume the following axioms regarding the Hochschild DGLA: Let
B
be a fixed deformation base. Then:

(A1)

Two
A_{\infty}
-deformations
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
over
B
are gauge equivalent if and only if they are gauge equivalent as Maurer-Cartan elements of
\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})
.

(A2)

Let
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be a quasi-equivalence of
A_{\infty}
-categories. Then there exists a quasi-isomorphism of
L_{\infty}
-algebras
F_{*}:\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C})\to\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D})
.

(A3)

We call
F_{*}
the pushforward of
F
. The pushforward is noncanonical. However its induced map
(F_{*})^{\operatorname{MC}}:\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}),B)\to\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D}),B)
has the following property:
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is a naive pushforward of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
along
F
if and only if
\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}=F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})
.

(A4)

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
be a subcategory such that the inclusion
i:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a quasi-equivalence. Then
i_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}|_{\mathcal{C}})=\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}
.

(A5)

Push-forward is functorial on Maurer-Cartan elements:
(GF)_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})=G_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})
.

Remark 4.18
.

There is a slight abuse of the notation in
Convention 4.17
. Where we have written
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})
, we actually mean the Maurer-Cartan element
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}-\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
instead of
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
. In fact, the element
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\in\,B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{HC}^{1}(\mathcal{C})
is not a Maurer-Cartan element itself. In similar abuse of notation, we may occasionally write
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mathcal{C}_{q})
instead of
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})
.

As an application of
Convention 4.17
, we show here that quasi-equivalence of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories is an equivalence relation. By quasi-equivalence of two deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories, we refer to the relation defined in
Definition 3.26
. A proof without assuming the axioms would either need to deal with complicated explicit constructions, or make use of an
\infty
-categorical level. In other words, assuming the axioms seems to be a healthy midway for the scope of the paper.

Lemma 4.19
.

Quasi-equivalence of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories is an equivalence relation. Even stronger, for every quasi-equivalence
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
there exists a quasi-equivalence
G:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
such that
G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. Derived equivalence is an equivalence relation as well.

Proof.

We need to prove reflexivity, transitivity and symmetry of the quasi-equivalence relation. The first two properties are easy: The identity functor obviously provides a quasi-equivalence from any deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
to itself. And if
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
and
G_{q}:\mathcal{D}_{q}\to\mathcal{E}_{q}
are quasi-equivalences, then the composition
G_{q}F_{g}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{E}_{q}
is a quasi-equivalence as well. We conclude that only symmetry remains to be proven.

The proof of symmetry consists of five steps: First, we define a set of “good” quasi-equivalences
F
for which there exists a quasi-equivalence
G
with
G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. The second, third and fourth step establish basic properties of this “good” set. In the fifth step, we show that those properties already make every quasi-equivalence lie in
\mathcal{F}
.

As step 1, let us recall our context. We are interested in the set of quasi-equivalences
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
such that there exists a quasi-equivalence
G:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
with
G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. Denote this set by
\mathcal{F}
:

\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}\text{ q.e.}~|~\exists G:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}\text{ q.e.}:G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}\}.

Our aim is to show that any quasi-equivalence lies in
\mathcal{F}
. Steps 2, 3, 4 are devoted to proving several properties of
\mathcal{F}
.

As step 2, we prove the property

F\in\mathcal{F}\text{ with }G\text{ q.e. such that }G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{*}G_{*}=\mathrm{id}\text{ and }G\in\mathcal{F}.

Indeed, pick
F
as on the left-hand side. Since both
F
and
G
are quasi-equivalences, both pushforwards
F_{*}
and
G_{*}
are bijections. Together with
G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
, we conclude that
G_{*}
and
F_{*}
are simply inverses to each other. In other words,
F_{*}G_{*}=\mathrm{id}
holds as well. We conclude that
G\in\mathcal{F}
.

As step 3, we prove for composable quasi-equivalences
F,G
the property

F,G\in\mathcal{F}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad GF\in\mathcal{F}.

One should think of this as a strong version of the two-out-of-three property. To prove it, pick
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
with
F^{\prime}_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
and
G^{\prime}_{*}G_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. We get
(F^{\prime}G^{\prime})_{*}(GF)_{*}=F^{\prime}_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
, which renders
GF\in\mathcal{F}
. Conversely assume
GF\in\mathcal{F}
with
H_{*}(GF)_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. Then
(HG)_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
, hence
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Step 2 implies
F_{*}(HG)_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. In other words
(FH)_{*}G_{*}=\mathrm{id}
, hence
G\in\mathcal{F}
. We conclude that both
F
and
G
lie in
\mathcal{F}
, as desired.

As step 4, we show that the following lie in
\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\text{quasi-equivalences with a one-sided inverse},

\displaystyle\text{inclusions of skeletal subcategories},

\displaystyle\text{inclusion }i:\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}\text{ and projection }\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}.

To this end, assume
F
and
G
are quasi-equivalences with
FG=\operatorname{Id}
. Combining
\operatorname{Id}\in\mathcal{F}
with step 3, we deduce
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, isomorphisms fall under this regime. Inclusions of skeletal subcategories also fall under this regime, since a skeletal subcategory
S\subseteq\mathcal{C}
produces a quasi-equivalence
\mathcal{C}\to S
which reduces to the identity on
S
. Now regard a category
\mathcal{C}
and its minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. The minimal model is not unique, but every minimal model comes with quasi-isomorphisms
i:\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
and
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. Regard the map
\pi i:\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. Since both
i
and
\pi
are quasi-isomorphisms,
\pi i
is a quasi-isomorphism as well. Moreover,
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
is already a minimal category, hence
\pi i
is an isomorphism. We conclude that
\pi i\in\mathcal{F}
. By step 3, we deduce that both
\pi
and
i
lie in
\mathcal{F}
. This finishes step 4.

As final step 5, we prove that any quasi-equivalence lies in
\mathcal{F}
. To this end, let
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be any quasi-equivalence. Our strategy is to build a diagram to whose arrows we can apply step 3 and 4 to deduce that
F
also lies in
\mathcal{F}
. In order to write down the diagram, pick minimal models
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
and
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}
, together with inclusion map
i_{\mathcal{C}}:\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
and projection
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{D}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}
. Define
F^{\prime}\coloneqq\pi_{\mathcal{D}}Fi_{\mathcal{C}}
. This gives a diagram, commutative by definition,
\mathcal{C}
\mathcal{D}
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}

Choose a skeletal subcategory
S_{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
and set
S_{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq F^{\prime}(S_{\mathcal{C}})
. Then
S_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}
is a skeletal subcategory as well: Any object
X\in\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}
is isomorphic to some
F^{\prime}(Y)
, and
Y
in turn is isomorphic to some
Z\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
, hence
X\cong F^{\prime}(Z)\in S_{\mathcal{D}}
. Moreover if
Y,Z\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
and
F^{\prime}(Y)\cong F^{\prime}(Z)
, then
Y\cong Z
. This implies
Y=Z
because
S_{\mathcal{C}}
is a skeleton. In total, we conclude that
S_{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\operatorname{H}\mathcal{D}
is a skeletal subcategory, and we obtain a restricted quasi-equivalence
F^{\prime\prime}:S_{\mathcal{C}}\to S_{\mathcal{D}}
. Putting everything together, we have the commutative diagram
\mathcal{C}

Here we denoted by

. The top and bottom square are strictly commutative by definition of

.

We now count everything together:
F^{\prime\prime}
is an isomorphism and

J,F^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. By step 3, we get

. A double application of step 3 renders

. Since

. In other words, for every
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
there exists a
G:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
such that
G_{*}F_{*}=\mathrm{id}
. In particular, this shows quasi-equivalence is an equivalence relation.

Let us now explain why derived equivalence is an equivalence relation as well. Indeed,
\mathcal{C}_{q}

if
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent. Since we have just shown that quasi-equivalence is an equivalence relation, we conclude that derived equivalence is an equivalence relation. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

Pushing deformations from one category to another is not only possible via quasi-equivalences. We can also push forward deformations from one category to a derived equivalent category. Namely, let
F:\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}
be a quasi-equivalence and let
\mathcal{C}_{q}
be a deformation of
\mathcal{C}
. Then the twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
from
Definition 3.18
is canonically a deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
. The pushforward
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}
on Maurer-Cartan elements now transports this deformation
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
to a deformation
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}})
of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}
. Restricting this deformation to
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}
gives a deformation of
\mathcal{D}
. Via the route of twisted completion, pushforward and restriction, the deformation
\mathcal{D}_{q}
corresponds to the deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. We may equally call
\mathcal{D}_{q}
the pushforward of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
along
F
.

5
Derived categories of
A_{\infty}
-deformations

In this section, we define and prove existence of derived categories of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. In
section 5.1
, we revisit the notion of twisted completion from
section 3.3
. In
section 5.2
, we define minimal models for
A_{\infty}
-deformations, in such a way that the minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
is a deformation of
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. In
section 5.3
, we combine both steps and define the derived category of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
to be
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
, the minimal model of the twisted completion. In
section 5.4
we comment on uncurving of
A_{\infty}
-deformations and on uncurvable objects.

5.1
Twisted completion

In this section, we revisit the twisted completion construction for deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories from an abstract perspective. While in
section 3.3
we already defined
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
in an ad-hoc way, in the present section we have the additional abstract operations of
section 4
available. We explain that
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the pushforward of the deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
onto
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
along the derived equivalence
\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
. This section is meant to illustrate that the story of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is well-rounded and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is correctly defined by
Definition 3.18
.

To start with, let us recall the additive completion and twisted completion from
Definition 3.18
. The additive completion
\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the deformation of
\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}
whose deformed products are simply induced from those of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
whose deformed products are defined by embracing the deformed products of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
with the
\delta
-matrix. Note that the objects of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are by definition the same as those of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, namely twisted complexes
(A_{1}[k_{1}]\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{n}[k_{n}],\delta)
where
\delta
satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation with respect to
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
. The category
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is a curved deformation and its curvature is given by the failure of
\delta
to satisfy the Maurer-Cartan equation with respect to
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
.

With the help of
Definition 3.26
, we can put the definition of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
into a better framing. The easiest way to make this precise is to note that the inclusion
\mathcal{C}\to\,\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
is trivially a derived equivalence. The twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
satisfies precisely the deformed analog of this classical statement:

Lemma 5.1
.

The inclusion
\mathcal{C}_{q}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is a derived equivalence of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories.

Proof.

Due to our definition of quasi-equivalences of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories, this statement is trivial. Indeed, regard the inclusion functor
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. This is by definition a functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. Its leading term is the standard inclusion
F:\mathcal{C}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
. Ultimately, the functor
F
is a derived equivalence and we conclude that
F_{q}
is a derived equivalence according to
Definition 3.26
.
∎

In this section, let us go beyond the definition of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and define a few more utilities. It is possible to define a variant
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
of the twisted completion of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. It is not a deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
, but rather an object-cloning deformation in the sense of
Definition 3.15
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. The objects of
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are be pairs

(X,\delta=\delta_{0}+\delta^{\prime}),\quad X\in\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C},\quad\delta_{0}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X,X),\quad\delta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X,X).

Only the leading part
\delta_{0}
is required to be upper triangular and satisfy the Maurer-Cartan equation with respect to
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
. The hom spaces and higher products of
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
shall be defined by embracing with
\delta
as in
Definition 3.18
.

Remark 5.3
.

In an object
(X,\delta_{0}+\delta^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
, the infinitesimal part
\delta^{\prime}
can also lie below the diagonal. The category
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is clearly a deformed
A_{\infty}
-category. When dividing out the maximal ideal we do not exactly reach
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
though, but a version of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
with lots of isomorphic objects, one copy for every choice of infinitesimal terms being added in the
\delta
matrix. This makes
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
an essentially surjective object-cloning deformation of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
along the cloning map
O:\operatorname{Ob}(\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q})\to\operatorname{Ob}(\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C})
given by
(X,\delta_{0}+\delta^{\prime})\mapsto(X,\delta_{0})
.

Remark 5.4
.

Let us check that products of this deformed
A_{\infty}
-category
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are still well-defined: Regard a sequence of
k
compatible entries of
\delta
. Then at least
k-d
of them are infinitesimal, where
d
is the dimension of the
\delta
-matrix. We conclude that a term

\mu_{q}(\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{k_{n+1}\text{ times}},a_{n},\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{k_{n}\text{ times}},\ldots,a_{1},\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{k_{1}\text{ times}})

lies in the
K\coloneqq(k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n+1}-(n+1)d)
-th power of the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
. When the number
k_{1}+\ldots+k_{n+1}
of total
\delta
insertions goes to infinity, this exponent
K
tends towards infinity as well. This renders all products in
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
well-defined.

The category
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
allows us to define twisted complexes more liberally than in
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Nevertheless both categories are essentially equal:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Then
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent as deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. Moreover, let
S\coloneqq\{(X_{i},\delta_{i})\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
be a collection of objects in
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\delta_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}^{1}(X_{i})
be infinitesimal terms. Then the category

S^{\prime}=\{(X_{i},\delta_{i}+\delta_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}

is gauge equivalent to
S
.

Proof.

For the first part, regard the inclusion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Upon dividing out the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, this inclusion reduces to the inclusion of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
into a version of
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
with lots of copies of objects. We conclude that the inclusion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is a quasi-equivalence of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories.

For the second part, build the functor

F_{q}:S^{\prime}\to S,\quad F^{0}_{q,(X_{i},\delta_{i}+\delta_{i}^{\prime})}=\delta_{i}^{\prime},\quad F_{q}^{1}=\operatorname{Id},\quad F_{q}^{\geq 2}=0.

This functor is the identity on objects and indeed satisfies the curved
A_{\infty}
-functor relations:

\displaystyle\sum\mu_{S}(\underbrace{F_{q}^{0},\ldots,F_{q}^{0}}_{\geq 0\text{ times}},F_{q}^{1}(a_{k}),

\displaystyle\underbrace{F_{q}^{0},\ldots,F_{q}^{0}}_{\geq 0\text{ times}},\ldots,F^{1}(a_{1}),\underbrace{F_{q}^{0},\ldots,F_{q}^{0}}_{\geq 0\text{ times}})

\displaystyle=\sum\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\underbrace{\delta_{i},\delta_{i}^{\prime},\ldots}_{\text{any mix}},a_{k},\ldots,a_{1},\underbrace{\delta_{i},\delta_{i}^{\prime},\ldots}_{\text{any mix}})

\displaystyle=\sum\mu_{\operatorname{Add}\mathcal{C}_{q}}(\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{\geq 0\text{ times}},a_{k},\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{\geq 0\text{ times}},\ldots,a_{1},\underbrace{\delta,\ldots,\delta}_{\geq 0\text{ times}})

\displaystyle=F_{q}^{1}(\mu_{S^{\prime}}(a_{k},\ldots,a_{1}).

This shows that
F_{q}
is a gauge-equivalence
S^{\prime}\to S
.
∎

5.2
Minimal models

In this section we define a notion of minimal model for deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. The definition might differ from the reader’s expectation. Indeed, a minimal model according to our definition need not have vanishing differential. We finish the section by explaining why every
A_{\infty}
-deformation has a minimal model.

In
Definition 5.6
, we define the notion of minimal model for
A_{\infty}
-deformations. The aim is to have a definition which is compatible with the classical notion and make minimal models exist for any deformation.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be
A_{\infty}
-categories and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
deformations. Then
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is a
minimal model
for
\mathcal{C}_{q}
if
\mathcal{D}
is a minimal category and there is a functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories

F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}

whose leading term is a quasi-isomorphism
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
. We denote any minimal model of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
by
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

Let us discuss
Definition 5.6
. Classical minimal models of
A_{\infty}
-categories have vanishing curvature
\mu^{0}
and differential
\mu^{1}
by definition. This is not the case anymore for minimal models of deformations. Instead, we require that
\mathcal{D}
itself is minimal, while
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is allowed to have both curvature and nonvanishing differential. Of course, the curvature and differential of
\mathcal{D}_{q}
are both infinitesimal, since
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is a deformation of the minimal
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{D}_{q},X}

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(X,X),\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}(x)

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(X,Y),\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{D},~x\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(X,Y).

At this point, writing
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
for a minimal model constitutes abuse of notation, since we have not explained their existence. This is done in
Corollary 5.9
.

Remark 5.7
.

An alternative definition is obtained by requiring that the map
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
induced by
F
be only a quasi-equivalence. The difference in the two notions is merely cosmetic. While in the version of
Definition 5.6
the objects of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are required to match, a definition requiring quasi-equivalence allows for additional bloat: One may add any amount of quasi-isomorphic objects to both
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

There are several equivalent ways of characterizing minimal models. Recall the notion of quasi-isomorphism from
Definition 3.26
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be
A_{\infty}
-categories and
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{D}_{q}
deformations. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathcal{D}_{q}
is a minimal model for
\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

2.

\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-isomorphic, and
\mathcal{D}
is minimal.

3.

\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-isomorphic, and
\mathcal{D}
is a minimal model for
\mathcal{C}
.

4.

There is a quasi-isomorphism
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
such that
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})=\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}
and
\mathcal{D}
is minimal.

Here
F_{*}^{\operatorname{MC}}:\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{C}),B)\to\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D}),B)
denotes the push-forward map of Maurer-Cartan elements along
F
.

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of the axioms stated in
Convention 4.17
.
∎

A consequence of
Lemma 5.8
is that minimal models always exist:

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category. Then any deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
has a minimal model.

Proof.

Pick any (classical) minimal model
\mathcal{D}
for
\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathcal{D}
is a minimal category and there is an
A_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism
F:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
. Now pick the push-forward

\mu_{\mathcal{D}_{q}}\coloneqq F_{*}(\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})\in\overline{\operatorname{MC}}(\operatorname{HC}(\mathcal{D}),B).

This Maurer-Cartan element defines a deformation
\mathcal{D}_{q}
of
\mathcal{D}
. This satisfies statement
4
of
Lemma 5.8
.
∎

5.3
Derived categories

In this section, we define derived categories of
A_{\infty}
-deformations. The starting point is an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}
and a deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. In
section 3.3
we have already defined the twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. In
section 5.2
we have introduced the notion of minimal models for
A_{\infty}
-deformations. By applying it to
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
, we obtain the derived category
\operatorname{D}\mathcal{C}_{q}\coloneqq\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Then its
derived category
is the
A_{\infty}
-deformation
\operatorname{D}\mathcal{C}_{q}\coloneqq\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
of
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
.

Derived categories are not unique. For instance, any deformation of
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}
which is gauge equivalent to a given model
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is a derived category for
\mathcal{C}
as well.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Then the derived category
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is defined uniquely up to quasi-isomorphism.

Proof.

According to our definition, the twisted completion
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is defined uniquely. However, the minimal model
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is not unique. Instead, minimal models are only defined up to quasi-isomorphism. This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be
A_{\infty}
-categories and
\mathcal{C}_{q},\mathcal{D}_{q}
deformations. If
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{D}_{q}
are derived equivalent, then
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent.

Proof.

By
Definition 3.26
, the deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent. By definition
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are quasi-isomorphic. Similarly
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-isomorphic. By
Lemma 4.19
, quasi-equivalence is an equivalence relation and hence
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
are quasi-equivalent.
∎

The proof above is inconvenient in that it seems to give no clue about an actual quasi-equivalence
\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
. However we can construct such a functor by properly inverting and chaining the given information. Indeed, the minimal models by definition come with quasi-isomorphisms
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}
. As we see in the proof of
Lemma 4.19
, the quasi-isomorphism
\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}
has a kind of quasi-inverse. By chaining up all functors, we now obtain an explicit quasi-equivalence

\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\operatorname{Tw}\mathcal{D}_{q}.

5.4
The theory of uncurving

In this section, we recollect uncurving theory for
A_{\infty}
-deformations. The starting point is an arbitrary deformed
A_{\infty}
-category with curvature. We are interested in the question which objects become curvature-free once we apply a gauge functor to the category. In the present section, we explain this question and define terminology. We prove elementary properties. Uncurving has been studied in the literature, for instance under the name of the “curvature problem” in
[
15
]
.

It is our aim to explain in how far a gauge equivalence can change the curvature of a deformation. As a starting point, let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
category. Let
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
be two deformations of
\mathcal{C}
connected by a gauge equivalence
F:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
. The curvature of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
are related by the zeroth (curved)
A_{\infty}
-functor relation:

F^{1}(\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})=\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}+\mu^{1}(F^{0})+\mu^{2}(F^{0},F^{0})+\ldots~.

The map
F^{1}
has leading term the identity and is then a linear isomorphism by
Lemma 3.10
. This means that the curvature
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
depends only on
F^{0}
and
F^{1}
, and not on the higher components
F^{\geq 2}
. If we approximate
F^{1}
as the identity, we conclude that uncurving essentially depends only on the choice of
F^{0}
. This gives rise to the following definition:

Definition 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
X\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
X
is
uncurvable
if there exists an
S\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}^{1}(X)
such that

\mu^{0}_{X}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(S)+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(S,S)+\ldots=0.

We will now prove several basic properties regarding uncurvable objects.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
F:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\mathcal{D}_{q}
be a functor of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories. If
X\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
is uncurvable, then so is
F(X)
.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}^{1}(X)
be the uncurving morphism, that is

\mu^{0}_{X}+\mu^{1}(S)+\mu^{2}(S,S)+\ldots=0.

Now set

T:=F^{0}_{X}+F^{1}(S)+F^{2}(S,S)+\ldots\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}^{1}(F(X)).

We claim that
T
is an uncurving morphism for
F(X)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{F(X)}+\mu^{1}(T)+\mu^{2}(T,T)+\ldots

\displaystyle=\big(\mu^{0}_{F(X)}+\mu^{1}(F^{0}_{X})+\mu^{2}(F^{0}_{X},F^{0}_{X})+\ldots\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\mu^{1}(F^{1}(S))+\mu^{2}(F^{1}(S),F^{0}_{X})+\mu^{2}(F^{0}_{X},F^{1}(S))+\ldots\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\mu^{1}(F^{2}(S,S))+\mu^{2}(F^{1}(S),F^{1}(S))+\mu^{2}(F^{2}(S,S),F^{0}_{X})+\ldots\big)+\ldots

We apply the curved
A_{\infty}
rule to these terms and continue

\displaystyle=F^{1}\big(\mu^{0}_{X}+\mu^{1}(S)+\mu^{2}(S,S)+\ldots\big)+F^{2}\big(\mu^{0}_{X}+\mu^{1}(S)+\mu^{2}(S,S)+\ldots,S\big)

\displaystyle\quad+F^{2}\big(S,\mu^{0}_{X}+\mu^{1}(S)+\mu^{2}(S,S)+\ldots\big)+\ldots

\displaystyle=F^{1}(0)+F^{2}(0,S)+F^{2}(S,0)+F^{3}(0,S,S)+\ldots=0.

This shows that
T
is an uncurving morphism for
F(X)
.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Assume
\mathcal{C}
is minimal,
X\cong Y
in
\mathcal{C}
and
X
is uncurvable. Then so is
Y
.

Proof.

Regard the embedding

\{Y\}_{q}\to\{X,Y\}_{q},

where both sides are defined as full subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The embedding is an equivalence, since it reduces to the inclusion
\{Y\}\to\{X,Y\}
which is essentially surjective in cohomology. By
Lemma 4.19
, there is a quasi-equivalence in opposite direction

\{X,Y\}_{q}\to\{Y\}_{q}.

In particular, it maps
X
to
Y
. Since
X
is uncurvable, an application of
Lemma 5.14
shows that
Y
is uncurvable.
∎

The next lemma concerns uncurving of objects in minimal models. As a preparation let us recall that a minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
comes with a choice of quasi-isomorphism
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Correspondingly, the objects of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
are identified via
F_{q}
.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Then an object
X
is uncurvable in
\mathcal{C}_{q}
if and only if it is uncurvable in
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{q}:\mathcal{C}_{q}\to\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
be a quasi-isomorphism and let
X\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
. We need to show that
X
is uncurvable if and only if
F_{q}(X)
is uncurvable. By
Lemma 5.14
,
F_{q}(X)
is clearly uncurvable if
X
is uncurvable. For the other direction, regard the restriction
F_{q}|_{\{X\}}:\{X\}_{q}\to\{F_{q}(X)\}_{q}
. It is a quasi-isomorphism and by
Lemma 4.19
there exists a quasi-isomorphism
\{F_{q}(X)\}_{q}\to\{X\}_{q}
in opposite direction. Now if
F_{q}(X)
is uncurvable, then by
Lemma 5.14
also
X
is uncurvable. This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.17
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. If
X
and
Y
are quasi-isomorphic in
\mathcal{C}
, then
X
is uncurvable if and only if
Y
is uncurvable.

Proof.

Pick a minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The objects
X
and
Y
are isomorphic in
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. Combining
Lemma 5.15

and
5.16
, we conclude

X\in\mathcal{C}_{q}\text{ uncurvable }\Leftrightarrow X\in\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}\text{ uncurvable }\Leftrightarrow Y\in\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}\text{ uncurvable }\Leftrightarrow Y\in\mathcal{C}_{q}\text{ uncurvable}.

This chain of equivalences proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 5.17
might be slightly surprising. It is entirely irrelevant for uncurvability how

and

get deformed themselves, the only relevant measure is whether they are quasi-equivalent in
\mathcal{C}
. This supports

where we defined two objects

to be quasi-isomorphic already if they are quasi-isomorphic in
\mathcal{C}
.

6
A deformed Kadeishvili theorem

The aim of this section is to prove a deformed Kadeishvili theorem. The classical Kadeishvili theorem states that every
A_{\infty}
-category has a minimal model, and the minimal model can be computed by a construction with trees. The starting point for the present section is an arbitrary deformed
A_{\infty}
-category. In particular, it may contain curvature and its differential need not square to zero. As we have already explained in
section 5.2
, every deformed
A_{\infty}
-category has a minimal model.

In the present section we construct a minimal model
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}_{q}
explicitly for any deformed
A_{\infty}
-category. Our approach is to construct the minimal model via trees. The bottleneck in comparison with the classical case is the curvature and the failure of the differential to square to zero. We are therefore forced to analyze the shape of the differential in detail and build methods that are robust enough to work with less premises than the classical Kadeishvili theorem.

6.1
Homological splittings

In this section we recall the notion of homological splittings. The idea is to split a cochain complex into three direct summands in terms of which the differential becomes easy to describe. This is a classical notion, often just referred to as a “split”

3

Definition 6.1

We frequently denote a homological splitting of
\mathcal{C}

understood implicitly.

Given a category

6.2
The classical Kadeishvili theorem

In this section we recall the classical Kadeishvili theorem. This serves as a preparation for our deformed Kadeishvili theorem and fixes pieces of notation. We follow the construction by means of trees, as given in
[

To start with, we recall the standard notion of minimal

Definition 6.5

An

\mathcal{C}

. Specifically, the

6.3
Deformed differentials

In this section, we analyze differentials of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories in detail. The starting point is a deformed
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}_{q}
together with a homological splitting of
\mathcal{C}
. The homological splitting of
\mathcal{C}
is naturally not a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. However, one may try find a decomposition of the hom spaces of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
with properties that at least resemble those of a homological splitting. The idea is to deform the spaces involved in the homological splitting in order to account for the deformed differential. In the present section, we construct these deformed decompositions and state all properties.

Homological splitting
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}=H\oplus I\oplus R
Deformed decomposition
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}=H_{q}\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
upon deformation

The direct approach of
section 6.2
fails in the deformed context. In fact, one of the reasons the construction with trees works well in the classical case is that we have
\mu^{1}(H)=0
. However, it need not be the case that
\mu^{1}_{q}(H)=0
. This makes the direct description of the minimal model by Kadeishvili trees fail in the deformed context.

The present section provides a workaround. A glance at
[
13
, Chapter 6, 3.3]
shows that it suffices to require
\mu^{1}_{q}(H)\subseteq H
instead of
\mu^{1}_{q}(H)=0
. Even better, we may try in the deformed context to find an infinitesimal deformation
H_{q}
of
H
with
\mu^{1}_{q}(H_{q})\subseteq H_{q}
. Exploiting this observation is the strategy of our deformed Kadeishvili theorem. The present section is devoted to finding this deformation
H_{q}
.

A point of attention is the requirement of a minimal model of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
to be a deformation of
\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}
. This entails that the hom spaces be identified as
B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{H}\mathcal{C}}
and that the leading term of the products is the product
\mu_{\mathcal{C}}
. The present section has been purpose-built to keep track of the identification of
H_{q}
and
B\widehat{\otimes}H
.

In order to find
H_{q}
, we have to analyze the precise shape of the differential
\mu^{1}_{q}
. In
section 6.1
, we have seen that a differential on an ordinary
A_{\infty}
-category can be written in matrix form. Most matrix entries vanish because of the
A_{\infty}
-relations. For deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories, we can still write down
\mu^{1}_{q}
in matrix form with respect to
H\oplus I\oplus R
, although no entries vanish by default. A first step is to change
I
to
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
, which already renders two matrix entries zero:

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
H\oplus I\oplus R
be a homological splitting of
\mathcal{C}
. Then the differential
\mu^{1}_{q}
restricted to
B\widehat{\otimes}R
is injective:

\mu^{1}_{q}:B\widehat{\otimes}R~\xrightarrow{\sim}~\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R),

(6.2)

and we have a direct sum decomposition of
B
-modules

B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}=(B\widehat{\otimes}H)\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R).

(6.3)

With respect to this decomposition,
\mu^{1}_{q}
takes the shape

\mu^{1}_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}D&*&0\\
\mu^{1}_{q}E&*&*\\
F&*&0\end{pmatrix}

(6.4)

for some operators

\displaystyle D:B\widehat{\otimes}H

\displaystyle\to B\widehat{\otimes}H,

\displaystyle E:B\widehat{\otimes}H

\displaystyle\to B\widehat{\otimes}R,

\displaystyle F:B\widehat{\otimes}H

\displaystyle\to B\widehat{\otimes}R.

Proof.

First of all, regard the map

\mu^{1}_{q}:B\widehat{\otimes}R\to B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}.

It is
B
-linear and has leading term the injective map
\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}|_{R}
. By
Lemma 3.10
, it is an embedding. This establishes the first claim.

Second, let us prove the direct sum decomposition. Intuitively, changing the summand
\mu^{1}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
to
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
constitutes only an infinitesimal change and should leave the decomposition intact. Formally, define the map

\displaystyle\psi:\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}

\displaystyle\to B\widehat{\otimes}H+\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)+B\widehat{\otimes}R\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}},

\displaystyle(h,\mu^{1}(r),r^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mapsto h+\mu^{1}_{q}(r)+r^{\prime},\quad\text{for}\quad h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H,\quad r\in B\widehat{\otimes}R,\quad r^{\prime}\in B\widehat{\otimes}R.

The map
\psi
has leading term the identity. By
Lemma 3.9
, it is an isomorphism onto
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
. In particular, this already establishes that (
6.3
) is a sum decomosition, not necessarily direct. To prove the sum decomposition direct, let
h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H
,
\mu^{1}_{q}(r)\in\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
and
r^{\prime}\in B\widehat{\otimes}R
with
h+\mu^{1}_{q}(r)+r^{\prime}=0
in
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
. This implies
\psi(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r),r^{\prime})=0
and finally
h=r=r^{\prime}=0
since
\psi
is an isomorphism and
\mu^{1}_{q}|_{B\widehat{\otimes}R}
is injective. We conclude that (
6.3
) is a direct sum decomposition.

To obtain the claimed matrix presentation of
\mu^{1}_{q}
, simply define
D
and
F
as
\mu^{1}_{B\widehat{\otimes}H}
followed by the projections to
B\widehat{\otimes}H
and
B\widehat{\otimes}R
, respectively. Define
E
as
\mu^{1}_{B\widehat{\otimes}H}
followed by projection to
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
and the inverse of
\mu^{1}_{q}:B\widehat{\otimes}R\xrightarrow{\sim}\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
. The vanishing of the two indicated matrix entries is immediate, since
\mu^{1}_{q}
sends
B\widehat{\otimes}R
to
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
by definition. This settles all claims.
∎

We are now ready to define
H_{q}
.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Assume
H\oplus I\oplus R
is a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}
. Let
D,E,F
denote the operators from
Lemma 6.11
. Put

H_{q}\coloneqq\{h-Eh~|~h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H\}.

Then we have a direct sum decomposition

B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}=H_{q}\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R).

(6.5)

It holds that
\mu^{1}_{q}(H_{q})\subseteq H_{q}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
. With respect to this decomposition,
\mu^{1}_{q}
has the shape

\mu^{1}_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*&0\\
*&*&*\\
0&*&0\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

The decomposition is achieved easily as in the proof of
Lemma 6.11
. Namely,
H_{q}
and
B\widehat{\otimes}H
only differ by
R
-terms. To show
\mu^{1}_{q}(H_{q})\subseteq H_{q}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
, we calculate

\mu^{1}_{q}(h-Eh)=Dh+\mu^{1}_{q}E(h)+Fh-\mu^{1}_{q}(Eh)=Dh+Fh\in B\widehat{\otimes}H\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R=H_{q}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R.

This finishes the proof.
∎

The decomposition (

) plays a crucial role throughout this paper. It is not a homological splitting of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
in any sense, since for example
\mu^{1}_{q}
need not vanish on

. The decomposition is however an important prerequisite for our deformed Kadeishvili theorem. In particular, whenever computing minimal models of deformed
A_{\infty}
-categories, this decomposition needs to be calculated first.

There is a natural identification between
B\widehat{\otimes}H
and
H_{q}
. The identification associated an element
h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H
with
h-Eh\in H_{q}
. Since
Eh\in B\widehat{\otimes}R
, we can recover
h
from
h-Eh
by stripping off the
R
component. This identification plays an important role in this paper. Another important role is played by the map
\mu^{1}_{q}:B\widehat{\otimes}R\to\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
. We call the inverse of this map the deformed codifferential. Let us fix all important notions in the following definition.

Definition 6.13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
H\oplus I\oplus R
a homological splitting. Let
\mathcal{C}_{q}
be a deformation of
\mathcal{C}
. The
deformed decomposition
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the collection of direct sum decompositions (

) of all hom spaces in
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The
deformed counterpart
of an element

is the element
h-Eh\in H_{q}
. The correspondence between
H_{q}
and
B\widehat{\otimes}H
is denoted

\displaystyle\varphi:H_{q}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{~~~\sim~~~}B\widehat{\otimes}H,

\displaystyle h-Eh

\displaystyle\xmapsto{~~~\phantom{\sim}~~~}h.

The
deformed codifferential
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the
R
-linear map

h_{q}=(\mu^{1}_{q}|_{B\widehat{\otimes}R})^{-1}:\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\xrightarrow{~~~\phantom{\sim}~~~}B\widehat{\otimes}R.

The
deformed projection
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
is the
R
-linear map

\pi_{q}:H_{q}\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\to H_{q}.

6.4
Optimizing curvature

In this section, we show how to optimize curvature of an
A_{\infty}
-category. In general, it is not possible to remove curvature from an
A_{\infty}
-deformation entirely. For the purposes of our Kadeishvili theorem, it is however important to tame the curvature as much as possible. In this section, we show that the curvature of any deformation can be reduced sufficiently for our purpose of constructing a deformed Kadeishvili theorem.

Deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
Deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
with optimal curvature

Let us start by fixing our terminology:

Definition 6.14
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
H\oplus I\oplus R
be a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}
and
H_{q}\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
be the associated deformed decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{q}
has
optimal curvature
if
\mu^{0}_{q}\in H_{q}
.

In the remainder of the section, we show how to gauge an arbitrary deformed
A_{\infty}
-category such that its curvature becomes optimal. We also explain why optimal curvature is the best we can expect. The idea to optimize the curvature is to apply successive gauges. All gauges will be gauge functors
F
of the form
F^{1}=\operatorname{Id}
and
F^{0}=r
and have no higher components. We may also call such functors “uncurving gauges” because they are strong at reducing curvature. The following definition settles our terminology.

Definition 6.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
r=\{r_{X}\}_{X\in\mathcal{C}}
be an element consisting of
r_{X}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{C}
. Then the
uncurving
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}

by

r
is the category
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
obtained from adding
r_{X}
as twisted differential to every
X\in\mathcal{C}_{q}
:

\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{(X,r_{X})~|~X\in\mathcal{C}\}\subseteq\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}.

The notation
\operatorname{Tw}^{\prime}\mathcal{C}_{q}
is taken from
Definition 5.2
.

Remark 6.16
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
be the uncurving of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
by
r
. Then the curvature of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
is

\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}=\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(r)+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(r,r)+\ldots.

The uncurving
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
is naturally a deformation of
\mathcal{C}
and comes with a gauge equivalence

F:\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{C}_{q},\quad\text{given by}\quad F^{0}\coloneqq r,\quad F^{1}\coloneqq\operatorname{Id},\quad F^{\geq 2}\coloneqq 0.

The category
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
can also be defined by forcing this particular map
F
to be a functor of deformations. More on uncurving can be found in
section 5.4
, which focuses on cases where uncurving removes the curvature entirely.

Remark 6.17
.

The name “uncurving” for the gauge in
Definition 6.15
is a slight abuse of terminology: The curvature
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}
will not vanish, but has the chance to be less than
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
. The term uncurving generally refer to any procedure of reducing curvature, while
Definition 6.15
restricts usage of the term to a particular class of functors. According to the explanation in
section 5.4
, this particular class of functors is however the only one that essentially changes curvature, therefore we have adopted the name “uncurving”.

Remark 6.18
.

We can now explain the name “optimal curvature”. In fact, any other deformation gauge equivalent to a deformation with optimal curvature will generally have more curvature. To see this, regard a deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
with optimal curvature. Its curvature already lies in
H_{q}
. If we apply uncurving by an element
r
, the new curvature is
\mu^{0}_{q}+\mu^{1}_{q}(r)+\ldots
. If we choose
r\in B\widehat{\otimes}R
, then
\mu^{1}_{q}(r)
naturally lies in
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
which already downgrades the curvature. If we choose
r\in\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
or
r\in H_{q}
, then
\mu^{1}_{q}(r)
typically contains components from
R
or
\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
as well. The additional summands
\mu^{2}_{q}(r,r)
even worsen the situation. We see that
\mu^{0}_{q}\in H_{q}
is generally the best achievable.

In the remainder of this section, we prove that any deformed
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{C}_{q}
has an uncurving with optimal curvature. The idea is to apply repeated uncurving by elements
s
which lie in increasingly high order of
\mathfrak{m}
. We take our clue from inspecting the curvature
\mu_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}^{0}=\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(s)+\ldots
.
To get
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}
as close to zero as possible, write
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}=h+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(r)+r^{\prime}
in terms of the deformed decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
and choose
s=-r
. The curvature of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}
then reads

\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\prime}}=h+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(r)+r^{\prime}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(-r)+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(-r,-r)+\ldots=h+r^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2}).

This is very productive strategy, since the new curvature has lost its
\mu^{1}_{q}(R)
component in lowest order. Our idea is to repeat this procedure to eliminate also the higher order terms. A repeated approach is indeed necessary because newly arising curvature terms like
\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(r,r)
may behave unpredictably.

Definition 6.19
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category with deformation
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Let
H\oplus I\oplus R
be a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}
. The
curvature optimization procedure
is the following inductive procedure, starting with
i=0
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(0)}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{q}
.

1.

Form the deformed decomposition
H_{q}^{(i)}\oplus\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}
.

2.

Write the curvature as
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}=h^{(i)}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(r^{(i)})+r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}
in terms of the decomposition.

3.

Define
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i+1)}
to be the uncurving of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}
by
-r^{(i)}
.

4.

Repeat.

Remark 6.20
.

The definition of
r^{(i)}
in terms of
\mathcal{C}_{q}{}^{(i)}
can also be written elegantly as
r^{(i)}=h_{q}{}^{(i)}(\mu^{0}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}{}^{(i)}})
, where
h_{q}{}^{(i)}
is the deformed codifferential of
\mathcal{C}_{q}{}^{(i)}
. The letters
h^{(i)}
,
r^{(i)}
and
r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}
are actually families parametrized by objects
X\in\mathcal{C}
. In the statement of
Lemma 6.21
, we combine this shorthand with the shorthand notation
\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}=\{\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)\}_{X\in\mathcal{C}}
. For instance,
r^{(i)}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i}}\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}
is to be understood as
r^{(i)}_{X}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i}}\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{C}
.

After running the curvature optimization procedure, we expect the gauges
r^{(i)}
to combine together to one large gauge. We expect the categories
\mathcal{C}_{q}{}^{(i)}
to converge to a limit category
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
. We also expect the curvature of
\mathcal{C}_{q}{}^{(i)}
to converge to the curvature of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
and the deformed decompositions of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}
to converge to the deformed decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}},

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}},

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\big(H_{q}^{(i)},\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(B\widehat{\otimes}R),(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\big)

\displaystyle=\big(H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}},\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(B\widehat{\otimes}R),(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\big).

Ultimately, we hope to find
\mu^{0}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}\in H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
. The next lemma makes this precise, and also shows that we have in fact reached
\mu^{0}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}\in H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
as desired.

Lemma 6.21
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a deformation. Let
H\oplus I\oplus R
be a homological splitting for
\mathcal{C}
. Let
r^{(i)}
be the sequence obtained from applying the curvature optimization procedure to
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. Then it holds that
r^{(i)}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i}}\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}
. Set
r=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}r^{(i)}\in\mathfrak{m}\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}
and define
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
as the uncurving of
\mathcal{C}_{q}
by
-r
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
has optimal curvature and comes with an gauge equivalence

F:\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\to\mathcal{C}_{q},\quad\text{given by}\quad F^{0}=-r,\quad F^{1}=\operatorname{Id},\quad F^{\geq 2}=0.

Proof.

We divide the proof into three parts. In the first part of the proof we show
r^{(i)}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i}})
. Denote by
H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
the deformed decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
. In the second part of the proof, we show that the curvature
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}
lies in
H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
. In the third part of the proof we conclude that the curvature actually lies in
H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
.

For the first part, let us show
r^{(i)}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i}}\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{C}}
by induction. For
i=0
, the statement holds. Assume it holds for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i+1)}
is the uncurving of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}
by
-r^{(i)}
. Its curvature is

\displaystyle\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i+1)}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(-r^{(i)})+\mu^{2}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(-r^{(i)},-r^{(i)})+\ldots

\displaystyle=h^{(i)}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(r^{(i)})+r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}-\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(r^{(i)})+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i+1}})

\displaystyle=h^{(i)}+r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i+1}}).

To make statements on
r^{(i+1)}
, write
h_{q}^{(i+1)}
for the deformed codifferential of
\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i+1)}
. Then

\displaystyle r^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=h_{q}^{(i+1)}(\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i+1)}})

\displaystyle=h_{q}^{(i+1)}\big(h^{(i)}+r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i+1}})\big)

\displaystyle=0+0+\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i+1}}).

In the last row, we have used that

h^{(i)},r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}\in H_{q}^{(i)}\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)=(B\widehat{\otimes}H)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)=H_{q}^{(i+1)}\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R).

We have also used that
h_{q}^{(i+1)}:B\widehat{\otimes}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}\to B\widehat{\otimes}R
preserves the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic filtration. The reason is that
h_{q}^{(i+1)}
is
B
-linear and automatically continuous according to
Lemma 3.9
. In total, we arrive at
r^{(i+1)}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{i+1}}R
as claimed. This finishes the induction.

For the second part of the proof, we show that
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}
lies in
H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
. For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, regard

\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}=h^{(i)}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(r^{(i)})+r^{(i)}{}^{\prime}.

The left-hand side converges to
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}
. The third term on the right-hand side converges to zero. Together this means

(B\widehat{\otimes}H)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\ni h^{(i)}+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{(i)}}(r^{(i)})\xrightarrow{i\to\infty}\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}.

We conclude

\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}\in(B\widehat{\otimes}H)\oplus(B\widehat{\otimes}R)=H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R.

This finishes the second part of the proof.

For the third part of the proof, we show that
\mu^{0}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}\in H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
. The idea is to show that the
R
component of
\mu^{0}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}
vanishes. In fact, this is an easy a posteriori observation: Write this curvature as
h+r
with
h\in\,H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
and
r\in B\widehat{\otimes}R
. Then

0=\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}})=\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(h)+\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(r).

On the right hand side, the first summand lies in
H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
and the second summand lies in
\mu^{1}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)
. Correspondingly, both summands vanish. While for
h
this is a weak statement, we immediately derive
r=0
since
\mu^{1}{}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}
is injective on
B\widehat{\otimes}R
. This shows
\mu^{0}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}}\in H_{q}^{\bm{\mathrm{opt}}}
and finishes the proof.
∎

In this section, we examine the deformed Kadeishvili construction in a special case. The starting point is a curvature-free deformed

Lemma 6.35
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
A_{\infty}
-category and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
a curvature-free deformation. Choose a homological splitting
H\oplus I\oplus R
. Denote by
D,E,F
the operators from
Lemma 6.11
. Then we have a natural quasi-isomorphism of
B
-modules

\operatorname{H}\big(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}},\mu^{1}_{q}\big)~\cong~\operatorname{H}\big(B\widehat{\otimes}H,D\big).

(6.8)

In particular if
D=0
and
H(X,Y)
is finite-dimensional, then
\operatorname{H}(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}(X,Y),\mu^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}})
is a projective
B
-module.

Proof.

To equate the two cohomology modules, we provide explicit morphisms
\varphi,\psi
of chain complexes in both directions. Next, we check that the maps actually commute with the differential. We finally show that in cohomology, both compositions
\varphi\psi
and
\psi\varphi
descend to the identity.

Our first step is to give explicit morphisms of chain complexes. In terms of the decomposition (
6.3
) of
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}}
into
B\widehat{\otimes}H\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
, put

\displaystyle\varphi:(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}},\mu^{1}_{q})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(B\widehat{\otimes}H,D),

\displaystyle(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)

\displaystyle\longmapsto h,\qquad\text{for}\quad h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H,\quad r,r^{\prime}\in B\widehat{\otimes}R,

\displaystyle\psi:(B\widehat{\otimes}H,D)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}_{q}},\mu^{1}_{q}),

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\longmapsto(h,0,-Eh),\qquad\text{for}\quad h\in B\widehat{\otimes}H.

We are now ready to check that both
\varphi
and
\psi
are chain maps. Indeed, we have

\varphi(\mu^{1}_{q}(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r))=\varphi(Dh,\mu^{1}_{q}(Eh)+\mu^{1}_{q}(r),Fh)=Dh=D\varphi(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)

and

\psi(Dh)=(Dh,0,-EDh)=(Dh,0,Fh)=\mu^{1}_{q}(h,0,-Eh)=\mu^{1}_{q}(\psi(h)).

In the above calculations, we have written elements of the direct sum
B\widehat{\otimes}H\oplus\mu^{1}_{q}(B\widehat{\otimes}R)\oplus B\widehat{\otimes}R
as tuples instead of sums.

The next step is to calculate
\varphi\psi
and
\psi\varphi
and verify that they descend to the identity on cohomology. For
\varphi\psi
, this is trivial since
\varphi\psi=\mathrm{id}
. For the other composition
\psi\varphi
, we pick an element
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\mu^{1}_{q})
and check whether
\psi(\varphi(x))-x
lies in the image of
\mu^{1}_{q}
. First of all, write
x=(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)
and note that
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\mu^{1}_{q})
implies
Dh=0
,
r+Eh=0
and
Fh=0
. We obtain

\psi(\varphi(x))-x=\psi(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)-(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)=(h,0,-Eh)-(h,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),r)=(0,\mu^{1}_{q}(r^{\prime}),0).

The expression on the right is simply the image
\mu^{1}_{q}(0,0,r^{\prime})
. We conclude that
\varphi
and
\psi
are quasi-inverse to each other. This establishes the desired quasi-isomorphism (
6.8
). In case
D=0
and
H(X,Y)
is finite-dimensional, the cohomology is simply
B\otimes H(X,Y)
which is projective. This finishes the proof.
∎
