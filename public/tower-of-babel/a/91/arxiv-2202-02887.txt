Monte Carlo Methods for Estimating the Diagonal of a Real Symmetric Matrix
Eric Hallman, Ilse C.F. Ipsen, Arvind K. Saibaba

Abstract
For real symmetric matrices that are accessible only through matrix vector products, we present Monte Carlo estimators for computing the diagonal elements. Our probabilistic bounds for normwise absolute and relative errors apply to Monte Carlo estimators based on random Rademacher, sparse Rademacher, normalized and unnormalized Gaussian vectors, and to vectors with bounded fourth moments. The novel use of matrix concentration inequalities in our proofs represents a systematic model for future analyses. Our bounds mostly do not depend on the matrix dimension, target different error measures than existing work, and imply that the accuracy of the estimators increases with the diagonal dominance of the matrix. An application to derivative-based global sensitivity metrics corroborates this, as do numerical experiments on synthetic test matrices. We recommend against the use in practice of sparse Rademacher vectors, which are the basis for many randomized sketching and sampling algorithms, because they tend to deliver barely a digit of accuracy even under large sampling amounts.

Introduction

We compute the diagonal elements of symmetric matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

with Monte Carlo estimators of the form

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}}

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}
are independent random vectors. This approach is crucial
when the elements of
\boldsymbol{A}
are available only
implicitly, via matrix vector products.

Estimating the diagonal elements of a matrix is important in many areas of science and engineering:
In electronic structure calculations, one computes the diagonal
elements of a projector onto the smallest eigenvectors of a Hamiltonian matrix
[
4
]
.
In statistics, leverage scores for column subset selection can be computed from the diagonals of the projector onto the column space. In Bayesian inverse problems, the diagonal elements of the posterior covariance are computed with matrix-free estimators. Diagonal, or Jacobi preconditioners can accelerate the convergence of iterative linear solvers
[
21
]
. More recently, diagonal estimators have been used to accelerate second order optimization techniques for machine learning
[
22
]
. In network science, subgraph centrality measures
and ranks the importance of the network nodes based on the diagonal of a scaled exponential of the adjacency matrix. In sensitivity analysis, Monte Carlo diagonal estimators efficiently compute the derivative-based global sensitivity metrics
[
6
,
10
]
.

Diagonal estimation is related to trace estimation. Once the diagonal elements are known, the trace can be computed from their sum. Therefore, estimators for the diagonal
of a matrix can be easily adapted to trace estimators. Monte Carlo methods were first proposed by Hutchinson
[
8
]
, and subsequently improved and expanded to different distributions
[
2
,
7
,
16
]
. Applications of trace estimators, reviewed in
[
19
]
, include estimating density of states, log determinants, and Schatten
p
-norms.

Literature review

To our knowledge, Monte Carlo diagonal estimators were first proposed by Bekas, Kokiopoulou, and Saad
[
4
]
, and a sufficient condition was given for a Monte Carlo estimator to be unbiased. However, this paper identified that large offdiagonal entries can result in large relative errors and developed probing methods to mitigate the effects of the offdiagonal entries. This idea is further explored in the following works
[
11
,
9
]
.

We are aware of a recent paper
[
3
]
as the only other work to analyze the number of samples required for a relative
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.
In contrast to
[
3
]
, our proofs are the first to exploit matrix concentration inequalities to impose a systematic structure that can serve as a model for future analyses and allow us to analyze the normwise errors in a different norm. We analyze more general distributions such as random vectors with bounded fourth moments and sparse Rademacher vectors with a user-specified sparsity parameter, and—in contrast to
[
3
]
—focus on un-normalized estimators. Most of our bounds do not show an explicit dependence on the matrix dimension which is desirable for large-scale problems.

2.1
Notation

The
Schur product
(or

Hadamard
, or

elementwise product
)
of
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is denoted by

\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and has elements

c_{ij}=a_{ij}b_{ij}\qquad 1\leq 1\leq m,\quad 1\leq j\leq n.

For
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{C}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, the Schur product is commutative and distributive,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}\circ\boldsymbol{A},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}\circ(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{C})=\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{A}\circ\boldsymbol{C}.

Following MATLAB convention, we define

\diag(\boldsymbol{A})=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&\cdots&a_{nn}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

as the column vector of diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
.
The operator
\diag
is overloaded,
and
\diag(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
represents a
diagonal matrix whose
diagonal elements are the elements of the vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\equiv\diag(\diag(\boldsymbol{A}))=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(1)

represents the diagonal matrix whose diagonal
elements are the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
In other words,
\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}
zeros out the offdiagonal
elements of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

If the first factor in a Schur product is a square matrix

\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and the second factor
an outer product involving
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}\circ(\boldsymbol{xy}^{\top})=\diag(\boldsymbol{x})\,\boldsymbol{M}\,\diag(\boldsymbol{y}).

(2)

For symmetric matrices
\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
the partial order
\boldsymbol{A}\preceq\boldsymbol{B}
,
or equivalently
\boldsymbol{B}\succeq\boldsymbol{A}
,
says that
\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{A}
is positive semidefinite.
If
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are positive semidefinite, then
\boldsymbol{A}\preceq\boldsymbol{B}
implies
\boldsymbol{A}^{1/2}\preceq\boldsymbol{B}^{1/2}
.

The
intrinsic dimension
of a nonzero symmetric positive semidefinite matrix

\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is

\intdim(\boldsymbol{A})\equiv\frac{\trace(\boldsymbol{A})}{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{2}},\qquad\text{with}\quad 1\leq\intdim(\boldsymbol{A})\leq\rank(\boldsymbol{A})\leq n.

If, additionally,
\boldsymbol{A}
is a diagonal matrix, then

\intdim(\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}}{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{ii}}.

The columns of

\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{a}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
1\leq j\leq n
, and the columns of the identity

\boldsymbol{I}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The transpose of
\boldsymbol{A}
is
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}
.

2.2
Concentration inequalities

We rely on two scalar and two matrix concentration inequalities.

Markov’s inequality
[
15
, Section 3.1]
bounds the
the probability that a random variable exceeds a constant.

Theorem 1
(Markov’s inequality).

If
Z
is a non-negative random variable, then for
t>0

\mathbb{P}[Z\geq t]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]}{t^{2}}.

Hoeffding’s inequality for general bounded random variables

[
20
, Theorem 2.2.6]
bounds the
probability that a sum of scalar random variables exceeds its mean.

Theorem 2
(Scalar Hoeffding inequality).

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}
be independent random variables, bounded by

m_{k}\leq Z_{k}\leq M_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq N
, with
sum
Z\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}{Z_{k}}
. Then for
t>0

\myP\left[|Z-\E[Z]|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2t^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(M_{k}-m_{k})^{2}}}\right).

Next are two bounds
for sums of independent symmetric matrix-valued random variables.
The first is a matrix Bernstein concentration inequality

[
18
, Theorems 7.3.1 and 7.7.1]

for sums of independent, symmetric, bounded, zero-mean random matrices.

Theorem 3
(Matrix Bernstein inequality).

Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be independent
symmetric random matrices with

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}]=\boldsymbol{0},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{2}\leq L\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

Let the sum
\boldsymbol{S}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
have a matrix-valued variance that is majorized by

\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\boldsymbol{V}\succeq\Var(\boldsymbol{S})=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}^{2}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}].

Abbreviate
\nu\equiv\|\boldsymbol{V}\|_{2}
and
d\equiv\intdim(\boldsymbol{V})
.
Then for
t>0

\myP\left[\|\boldsymbol{S}\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 8d\,\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}\right).

(3)

Proof.

In
[
18
, Theorems 7.3.1 and 7.7.1]
it is shown that (
3
)
holds, provided
t\geq\sqrt{\nu}+\frac{L}{3}
.
We show that (
3
) always holds and the lower bound on
t
is not necessary.
To see this, note that

\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}

decreases monotonically as
t
increases. Therefore we can bound it from below
as long as
t<\sqrt{\nu}+L/3
, by

\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}>\frac{-(\sqrt{\nu}+L/3)^{2}}{2\left(\nu+(L/3)(\sqrt{\nu}+L/3)\right)}\geq-\frac{2}{3}.

The second inequality comes from
setting
x=\frac{L}{3\sqrt{\nu}}
and noting that
f(x)=-\frac{(1+x)^{2}}{2(1+x+x^{2})}
has a minimum
at
\hat{x}=1
where
f(\hat{x})=-2/3
, and
\sqrt{\nu}=L/3
. Substituting the lower bound into
Theorem
3
gives

8d\,\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}\right)\geq 8\exp(-2/3)>4.

But now (
3
) holds trivially since

\myP\left[\|\boldsymbol{S}\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 1<4<8d\,\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2(\nu+Lt/3)}\right).

The second matrix concentration inequality
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]

bounds the mean of the squared norm of the sum of symmetric random matrices.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

with
n\geq 3
be independent symmetric random matrices with zero mean. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2e\ln{n}}\>\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}+4e\ln{n}\>\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

2.3
Setup for the analysis

Our Monte Carlo estimators
compute the diagonal elements
of a symmetric
matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

by means of matrix vector products with
\boldsymbol{A}
.
It samples
N
independent random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and approximates the
vector of diagonal elements
\diag(\boldsymbol{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

by the mean

\diag(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\left((\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\circ\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\qquad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

To see this, apply (
1
) and (
2
) to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\boldsymbol{I}\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\diag((\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\circ\boldsymbol{w}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\diag\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{((\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\circ\boldsymbol{w}_{k})}\right)=\diag\left(\diag(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Alternately, the diagonal elements of the estimators can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})_{ii}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left((\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\circ\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

We measure the cost of a diagonal estimator by the number
N

of samples. To assess the accuracy, we introduce
a relative error in the form of
normwise and componentwise

(\epsilon,\delta)
estimators, which extend the notion of
(\epsilon,\delta)
trace estimator from
[
2
,
16
]
.

Definition 5
(Normwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator).

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be symmetric.
Given user-specified parameters
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
, we say
that
\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})
is a
normwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator for the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{A}
, if

\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\,\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In other words, for a user-specified failure probability
\delta>0
and tolerance
\epsilon>0
, the normwise relative error of
the diagonal estimator is, with
probability at most
1-\delta
, at most
\epsilon
.
The two-norm in Definition
5
can be replaced by any matrix-
p
norm, because
the
p
-norm of a diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is

\|\boldsymbol{D}\|_{p}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{|d_{ii}|}
for
p\geq 1
. Next we define a componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.

Definition 6
(Componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator).

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be symmetric.
Given user-specified parameters
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
and diagonal
element
a_{ii}\neq 0
of
\boldsymbol{A}
, we say
that
\widehat{a}_{ii}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})_{ii}
is a
componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator for
a_{ii}
, if

|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}|\leq\epsilon\,|a_{ii}|

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Standard Rademacher vectors

Definition 7
.

A
Rademacher random variable
takes on the
values
\pm 1
with equal probability 1/2.
A
Rademacher vector
is a random vector whose elements are independent
Rademacher random variables.

Standard Rademacher vectors have the advantage of cheap matrix vector products and immediately recovering diagonal matrices.

Remark 3.1
.

The elements
w_{j}
of a Rademacher vector
\boldsymbol{w}
have the following properties:

1.

Zero mean:
\mathbb{E}[w_{j}]=0

2.

Constant square:
w_{j}^{2}=1

3.

Independence:
\mathbb{E}[w_{j}w_{i}]=0
for
i\neq j
.

Remark 3.2
.

Standard Rademacher vectors recover a diagonal matrix with a single sample,
N=1
.

To see this, let
\boldsymbol{A}=\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be diagonal, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a Rademacher vector.
Remark
3.1
implies that
\boldsymbol{Aw}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has diagonal elements

a_{ii}w_{i}^{2}=a_{ii}
,
1\leq i\leq n
.

As a consequence, we can focus the analysis of standard Rademacher-based estimators on non-diagonal matrices.
The results below are special cases of
those for sparse Rademacher vectors in section
3.2
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric, and

K_{1}\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\|_{2},\qquad K_{2}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty},\qquad d\equiv(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}-\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2})/K_{1}.

If

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}
is a Monte Carlo estimator
with independent Rademacher vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

1\leq k\leq N
, then
the probability that
the absolute error exceeds
t>0
is at most

\myP\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 8d\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(K_{1}+tK_{2}/3)}\right).

Proof.

This is the special case
s=1
of Theorem
11
.

The constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
represent the absolute deviation
of
\boldsymbol{A}
from diagonality, and more specifically the degree of diagonal dominance of
\boldsymbol{A}
in the absolute sense.
Theorem
8
implies that the Rademacher
estimator has a small absolute error when applied
to strongly diagonally dominant matrices. In other words,
the normwise absolute error in the Rademacher estimator
decreases with increasing diagonal dominance of
\boldsymbol{A}
in the absolute sense.

We determine the least sampling amount
required for the Monte Carlo estimator with Rademacher vectors to be a normwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator.

Corollary 9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric.
Let

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{K_{1}}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad\Delta_{2}\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}},\qquad d\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}-\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2}}{K_{1}},

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}

with independent Rademacher vectors

(4)

then

.

Proof.

This is the special case
s=1
of Corollary
12
.

The constants
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
in

represent the

and

in Theorem

represent the relative deviation
of

For Rademacher vectors that are

The random vectors in

1

This concept was extended in

Definition 10
.

A

A
Sparse Rademacher vector
is a random vector whose elements are

The properties of sparse Rademacher vectors
are almost the same as those of the original Rademacher vectors in Remark

Remark 3.3
.

The elements of a sparse Rademacher vector
\boldsymbol{w}
with parameter
s\geq 1

have the following properties

1.

Zero mean:
\E[w_{j}]=0

2.

Unit variance
\E[w_{j}^{2}]=1

3.

Independence: For
i\neq j
,
and integer
\ell\geq 1

\mathbb{E}[w_{i}^{2}w_{j}^{2}]=1,\qquad\mathbb{E}[w_{i}^{\ell}w_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[w_{i}w_{j}^{\ell}]=0.

The case

corresponds to the original Rademacher vectors (Definition
7
), while
s=3
corresponds to
the choice in
[
1
]
.

Below is the extension of Theorem

to sparse Rademacher vectors with

.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric, and
and let

\displaystyle K_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\|_{2},\qquad K_{2}(s)\equiv\|s\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle d(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{K_{1}(s)}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}+(s-2)\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2}.

Let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}}
be a Monte Carlo
estimator with independent sparse Rademacher random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
with integer parameter
s\geq 1
.
Then the probability that
the absolute error exceeds
t>0
is at most

\myP\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 8d(s)\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(K_{1}(s)+tK_{2}(s)/3)}\right).

Proof.

Define the random diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\left(\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N,

and their sum

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{S}_{k}=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}=\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}).

(5)

Before applying Corollary
3
, we need
to verify the assumptions for the Bernstein inequality.

1. Expectation

With (
2
), the first summand in
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
equals

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})=\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\boldsymbol{I}\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})=\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}),

and with (
1
) this gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

(6)

The linearity of expectation implies

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}]=\frac{1}{N}\left(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})]}_{\boldsymbol{T}}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

Remark
3.3
implies that element
(i,j)
of

\boldsymbol{T}\equiv\mathbb{E}[\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})]
equals

\displaystyle t_{ij}=\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})_{i}\,\boldsymbol{I}_{ij}\,(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}]=\boldsymbol{I}_{ij}a_{ij}\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\ 1\leq j\leq n.

(7)

Hence
\boldsymbol{T}=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}
, and

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}]=\frac{1}{N}(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A})=\boldsymbol{0}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
Therefore, the random matrices have zero mean,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}]=\boldsymbol{0}
;
and so has their sum,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}]=\boldsymbol{0}
.

2. Boundedness

From (
6
) follows that the diagonal matrices
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
have diagonal elements

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{S}_{k})_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left((\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}-a_{ii}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\left((\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}}-a_{ii}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(a_{ii}((\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}^{2}-1)+(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}\,\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}}\right),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\quad 1\leq k\leq N.

Since
s\geq 1
is an integer,

|((\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{ii}^{2}-1)a_{ii}|\leq(s-1)|a_{ii}|
holds for any value of

(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}
, and

\displaystyle|(\boldsymbol{S}_{k})_{ii}|\leq\frac{1}{N}\left((s-1)|a_{ii}|+s\sum_{j\neq i}{|a_{ij}|}\right),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,\quad 1\leq k\leq N.

Since the matrix infinity norm is absolute,
with
\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{\infty}=\|\,|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}|\,\|_{\infty}

we can bound the two-norms of the diagonal matrices
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
by

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{\frac{1}{N}\left((s-1)|a_{ii}|+s\sum_{j\neq i}{|a_{ij}|}\right)}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{\frac{1}{N}\left(-|a_{ii}|+s\sum_{j=1}^{n}{|a_{ij}|}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\|s\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}=\frac{K_{2}(s)}{N},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

Set
L(s)\equiv K_{2}(s)/N
where
K_{2}(s)>0
since
\boldsymbol{A}
is not diagonal.

3. Variance

In (
6
) abbreviate
\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\equiv\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k})
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\equiv\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})
, and
\boldsymbol{D}\equiv\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})
, and write
the summands in
\Var[\boldsymbol{Z}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}]}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}-\boldsymbol{D}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left((\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{2}-2\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}+\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N,

taking advantage of the fact that diagonal matrices commute.
The expectation is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{2}\right]-2\boldsymbol{D}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\right]+\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

(8)

To determine
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}]
, look at the individual diagonal elements
and abbreviate
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})_{ii}={u}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Au}={u}_{i}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}}=a_{ii}{u}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{i}{u}_{j}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(9)

Remark
3.3
implies for
the expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})_{ii}\right]=a_{ii}\,\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{i}^{2}\right]+\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}\,\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{i}{u}_{j}\right]}=a_{ii},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Thus
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\right]=\boldsymbol{D}
.
Insert this into (
8
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{2}\right]-\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

(10)

To determine
\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{2}\right]
,
start from the individual diagonal elements in (
9
)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})_{ii}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{ii}{u}_{i}^{2}+{u}_{i}\,\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{a_{ii}^{2}{u}_{i}^{4}}_{\alpha}+\underbrace{2a_{ii}{u}_{i}^{3}\,\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}}}_{\beta}+\underbrace{{u}_{i}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}}\right)^{2}}_{\gamma}\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

In the expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})_{ii}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\alpha]+\mathbb{E}[\beta]+\mathbb{E}[\gamma]

(11)

we inspect each summand in turn.

Definition
10
implies for the fourth moment

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha]=a_{ii}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{i}^{4}\right]=a_{ii}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2s}\left(-\sqrt{s}\right)^{4}+\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right)0+\frac{1}{2s}\left(\sqrt{s}\right)^{4}\right)=a_{ii}^{2}s.

(12)

Since
{u}_{i}
is independent from

{u}_{j}
for
j\neq i

Remark
3.3
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\beta]=2a_{ii}\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{i}^{3}{u}_{j}\right]}_{=0}}=0.

(13)

Collect the independent elements of
\boldsymbol{u}
,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle={u}_{i}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell\neq i}{a_{i\ell}{u}_{\ell}}\right)={u}_{i}^{2}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}{u}_{j}\left(a_{ij}{u}_{j}+\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}{a_{i\ell}{u}_{\ell}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}^{2}{u}_{j}^{2}{u}_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{j\neq i}{\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}{a_{ij}a_{i\ell}{u}_{j}{u}_{\ell}{u}_{i}^{2}}}

and apply Remark
3.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\gamma]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}^{2}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{j}^{2}{u}_{i}^{2}\right]}_{=1}}+\sum_{j\neq i}{\sum_{\ell\neq i,j}{a_{ij}a_{i\ell}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}_{j}{u}_{\ell}{u}_{i}^{2}\right]}_{=0}}}=\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}^{2}}

(14)

Substitute (
12
), (
13
) and (
14
) into (
11
). Then Remark
3.3
and the
symmetry of
\boldsymbol{A}
imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})_{ii}^{2}\right]=sa_{ii}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}^{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}^{2}}+(s-1)a_{ii}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+(s-1)|a_{ii}|^{2}.

With
\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})=\diag\begin{pmatrix}(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\|\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

this gives for the whole diagonal matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})^{2}]=\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-1)\boldsymbol{D}^{2},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

At last, substitute this into the
variance (
10
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-1)\boldsymbol{D}^{2}-\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

Sum up the individual variances,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}(s)\equiv\Var[\boldsymbol{Z}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}]}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\right).

Since
\boldsymbol{V}(s)
is diagonal, its norm is

\nu(s)\equiv\|\boldsymbol{V}(s)\|_{2}=\frac{1}{N}\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\|_{2}=\frac{K_{1}(s)}{N},

where
K_{1}(s)>0
since
\boldsymbol{A}
is not diagonal.
The intrinsic dimension of
\boldsymbol{V}(s)
is

\displaystyle d(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\intdim(\boldsymbol{V})=\frac{\trace(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\boldsymbol{D}^{2})}{N\nu(s)}=\frac{1}{K_{1}(s)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\left(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+(s-2)|a_{jj}|^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K_{1}(s)}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}+(s-2)\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2}.

4. Apply Theorem
3

Substituting
L(s)=K_{2}(s)/N
and
\nu(s)=K_{1}(s)/N
into Theorem
3

and remembering that the sum
\boldsymbol{Z}
in (
5
) has zero mean gives

\myP\left[\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}\geq t\right]=\myP\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 8d(s)\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(K_{1}(s)+tK_{2}(s)/3)}\right).

In the special case
s=1
of standard
Rademacher vectors,
Theorem
11

reduces to Theorem
8
.
However, sparse Rademacher Monte Carlo estimators with
s>1
do, in general, not recover a diagonal matrix with a single sample,
N=1
.

As
s
increases, so do
the constants
K_{1}(s)
and
K_{2}(s)
, and the upper bound on

\myP\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\geq t\right]
.
In other words, the sparser the vectors

\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
, the less accurate the Monte Carlo estimate
\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})
.

Remark 3.4
(Non-integer sparsity levels).

The restriction to integers
s
in
Theorem
11
is
relevant only for
1<s<2
. More generally, Theorem
11

holds for
s=1
, and any real number
s\geq 2
.

The extension below of Corollary
9

presents the minimal sampling amount that
makes the sparse Rademacher Monte Carlo estimator
a normwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator.

Corollary 12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric, and let

\displaystyle K_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(s-2)\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\|_{2},\qquad\Delta_{1}(s)\equiv\frac{K_{1}(s)}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\|s\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}},\qquad d\equiv\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}+(s-2)\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2}}{K_{1}(s)}.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}}
be a Monte Carlo
estimator with independent sparse Rademacher random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
with integer parameter
s\geq 1
. Pick
\epsilon>0
. For any
0<\delta<1
, if the sampling amount is at least

N\geq\frac{\Delta_{2}(s)}{3\epsilon^{2}}\left(2\epsilon+6\,\frac{\Delta_{1}(s)}{\Delta_{2}(s)}\right)\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)},

then
\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\,\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Denote the bound
for the failure probability in Theorem
11
by

\delta\equiv 8d(s)\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(K_{1}(s)+tK_{2}(s)/3)}\right),

and solve it for
t
,

\displaystyle t=\frac{K_{2}(s)}{3N}\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}+\sqrt{\frac{K_{2}(s)^{2}}{9N^{2}}\ln^{2}(8d(s)/\delta)+\frac{2K_{1}(s)}{N}\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}}.

We can restate Theorem
8
in terms of the failure
probability: With probability at most
\delta
, the normwise absolute
error exceeds
\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\geq t
, where

\displaystyle t=\frac{K_{2}(s)}{3N}\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}+\sqrt{\frac{K_{2}(s)^{2}}{9N^{2}}\ln^{2}(8d(s)/\delta)+\frac{2K_{1}(s)}{N}\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}}.

And in terms of the success probability:
With probability at least
1-\delta
, the normwise absolute
error is bounded above by
\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})\|_{2}\leq t
.
Converting this absolute error into a relative one
requires
t\leq\epsilon\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}
, in other words,

\frac{t}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}=\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+\beta\gamma}\leq\epsilon,

where

\displaystyle\gamma\equiv\frac{K_{2}(s)\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}}{3N\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}},\qquad\beta\equiv\frac{6K_{1}(s)}{K_{2}(s)\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}

Solving for
\gamma
gives
\gamma\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\epsilon+\beta}
,
which implies

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\geq\frac{K_{2}(s)}{3\epsilon^{2}\,\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}\left(2\epsilon+\frac{6K_{1}(s)}{K_{2}(s)\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}\right)\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\epsilon^{2}}\,\frac{K_{2}(s)}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}\left(2\epsilon+6\,\frac{K_{1}(s)}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}}\frac{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}{K_{2}(s)}\right)\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{2}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\left(2\epsilon+6\,\frac{\Delta_{1}(s)}{\Delta_{2}(s)}\right)\ln{(8d(s)/\delta)},

where we exploited the norms of diagonal matrices,

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{1}(s)}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad\Delta_{2}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}}=\frac{K_{2}(s)}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}}.

Corollary
12

suggests that increasing the sparsity parameter
s

could on the one hand
lower the computational cost per sample, but on the
other hand increase the sampling amount for the same accuracy.

4.1

We bound the expectation of the squared absolute
error (Theorem

) for
Monte Carlo estimators based on

\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
(assumed to have independent entries with zero mean and variance
1
) with bounded
fourth moment,
1\leq k\leq N
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}\right]<+\infty.

(15)

These include standard
Rademacher (section

)

Theorem 13
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
n\geq 3

be symmetric, and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}}

be a Monte Carlo
estimator with independent random vectors

\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
, that have
independent elements
with zero mean and variance 1.
If the vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
also
have a bounded fourth moment (
15
), then

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\,\left(\sqrt{\frac{8e\ln n}{N}}+\frac{8e\ln n}{N}\right)\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

We make use of matrix concentration inequalities but follow the spirit of the analysis in
[
5
]
.

1. Symmetrization

Write the normwise error by exploiting diagonal Schur products (
1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}\right)},

and take expectations of the squared norms

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

From the assumption that
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
has
independent elements with zero mean and and variance 1 follows

\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})\right]=\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A}
.
Hence the matrix random variables

\boldsymbol{X}_{k}\equiv\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})-\boldsymbol{I}\circ\boldsymbol{A},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N

have zero mean.
We use symmetrization
[
20
, Lemma 6.4.2]
to create
symmetric
random variables
\varepsilon_{k}\,\boldsymbol{X}_{k}
,
where
\varepsilon_{k}
are independent symmetric Bernoulli random variables,
that is, they are Rademacher variables
as in Definition
7
. The
Rademacher variables
\varepsilon_{k}
are independent of each other and also independent of the random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
.
Remark
3.1
implies
\E[\varepsilon_{k}]=0
, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\varepsilon_{k}\,\boldsymbol{I}\circ{\boldsymbol{A}}}\right]=\boldsymbol{0}.

(16)

Then
[
20
, Lemma 6.4.2]
,
(
16
) and (
2
) imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{X}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\varepsilon_{k}\,\boldsymbol{I}\circ(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}\equiv\varepsilon_{k}\,\diag(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k})\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})

are symmetric random matrices, and
the second and third expectations range over all random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
and
all Rademacher variables
\varepsilon_{k}
.

2. Concentration inequality

Applying Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and Theorem
4

to the sum
\boldsymbol{Z}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2e\ln{n}}\>\left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}^{2}]}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}+4e\ln{n}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(18)

We bound the expectations that represent the matrix variance and
the maximal two-norm separately.

3. Variance

As in item 3 of the proof of Theorem
11
abbreviate

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{k}\equiv\diag(\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}),\qquad\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\equiv\diag(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}),\qquad\boldsymbol{O}\equiv\diag\left(\begin{bmatrix}\|\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\|_{1}&\cdots&\|\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\|_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right).

With this notation,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{2}]
. Consider the diagonal term

(\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{2})_{ii}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}\right)^{2}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{1}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{2}\preceq\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}\boldsymbol{O}^{2}
. The symmetry of
\boldsymbol{A}

implies
\boldsymbol{O}\preceq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\boldsymbol{I}
.
Combining the two inequalities gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{D}_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{2}]\preceq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}^{2}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}\right]\boldsymbol{I}.

(19)

Now take square roots,

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}^{2}]}\right)^{1/2}\preceq\sqrt{N}\>\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]^{1/2}\boldsymbol{I},

and bound the norm,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}^{2}]}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}\>\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]^{1/2}.

(20)

4: Maximal two-norm

In analogy to (
19
), we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}^{2}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right],

and its square root

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{Y}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]^{1/2}.

(21)

5. Putting everything together

Substitute the variance bound (
20
) and the norm bound (
21
)
into the expectation (
18
) for the sum

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}]^{1/2}\leq\left(\sqrt{2e\ln{n}}\,N^{1/2}+4e\ln{n}\right)\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]^{1/2},

substitute this, in turn, into the expectation (
17
) for
the absolute error,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\frac{2}{N}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{2}{N}\>\left(\sqrt{2e\ln{n}}\,N^{1/2}+4e\ln{n}\right)\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}\>\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]^{1/2},

and simplify.

We determine the minimal sampling amount for
Gaussian Monte Carlo estimators to be

(\epsilon,\delta)

Corollary 14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
n\geq 3

be symmetric, and let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}}

be a Monte Carlo
estimator with independent Gaussian
random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
Pick
\epsilon>0
.
For any
0<\delta<1
, if the sampling amount

N
satisfies
{8e\ln n}\leq N\leq n
, and
is at least

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{128\,(e\,\ln{n})^{3}}{\epsilon^{2}\,\delta}\>\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2},

(22)

then
\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

For Gaussian random vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
,

[
5
, (3.7)]
implies

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq N}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{4}}\right]\right)^{1/2}\leq e\ln{(nN)}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}{|\boldsymbol{I}_{ij}|}=e\ln{(nN)}.

Substituting this into Theorem
13
gives

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}}+\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}\right)\>e\ln{(nN})\>\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}.

Square both sides and apply Markov’s inequality (Theorem
1
) to the random variable

Z\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}
using

t\equiv\epsilon\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}\geq\epsilon\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}\right]

(23)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}}+\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}\right)^{2}\>(e\ln{(nN)})^{2}\>\frac{\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\,\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty}^{2}}.

Substituting the assumption

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}}+\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}\right)^{2}\>(e\ln{(nN)})^{2}\leq\left(2\sqrt{\frac{8e\ln{n}}{N}}\right)^{2}(2e\ln{n})^{2}=\frac{128\,(e\,\ln{n})^{3}}{N}.

Substitute this, in turn, into (

N

5.1
Standard Rademacher
vectors

We present a componentwise worst case absolute error
bound (Corollary
15
), and a
bound on the minimal sampling amount that makes the Rademacher Monte Carlo
estimator a
componentwise
(
\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator (Theorem
17
).

Theorem
8

is identical to the following worst case componentwise bound.

Corollary 15
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric, and let

K_{1}\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})^{2}\|_{2},\quad K_{2}\equiv\|\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{\infty},\quad d\equiv\frac{1}{K_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{A}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{F}^{2}.

If
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}
is a Monte Carlo estimator
with independent Rademacher vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
, then
the probability that
the absolute error exceeds
t>0
is at most

\myP\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{|a_{ii}-\widehat{a}_{ii}|}\geq t\right]\leq 8d\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(K_{1}+tK_{2}/3)}\right).

Proof.

In Theorem
8
, the
p
-norm of the diagonal matrix

\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{A})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}})
is a largest
magnitude diagonal element.

In contrast to Corollary
15
, the
next bound depends on the particular diagonal element. We first require a lemma on the independence of products of Rademacher variables.

Lemma 16
.

Let
Z_{1},W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n}
be independent Rademacher variables. Then the products

X_{1}\equiv ZW_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\equiv ZW_{n}

are also independent Rademacher variables.

Proof.

For any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\{-1,+1\}
, using the law of total probability, the joint probability mass function satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\cap_{i=1}^{n}\,\{X_{i}=x_{i}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\{-1,+1\}}\mathbb{P}\left[\cap_{i=1}^{n}\,\{X_{i}=x_{i}\}|Z=z\right]\mathbb{P}[Z=z]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\cap_{i=1}^{n}\,\{W_{i}=-x_{i}\}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left[\cap_{i=1}^{n}\,\{W_{i}=x_{i}\}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{-n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left[X_{i}=x_{i}\right].

Since the joint PMF factorizes, the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric,
and let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}

be a Monte Carlo estimator
with independent Rademacher vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
The probability that the absolute error
exceeds
t>0
is at most

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\,(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2})}\right),\qquad 1\leq j\leq n.

(24)

Proof.

Fix
i
, for some
1\leq i\leq n
.
The properties in Remark
3.1
allow us
to split off the original diagonal element from the estimator,

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}\right)_{ii}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}\,(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}}

\displaystyle=a_{ii}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\underbrace{\frac{a_{ij}}{N}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})_{j}}_{Z_{kj}^{(i)}}.

Lemma
16
implies that for fixed
i
, the
Z_{kj}^{(i)}
are independent.
Remark
3.1
implies that they
have zero mean, and are bounded by

\displaystyle-\tfrac{|a_{ij}|}{N}\leq Z_{kj}^{(i)}\leq\tfrac{|a_{ij}|}{N},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N,\quad 1\leq j\leq n,\quad j\neq i.

Hence the absolute error

\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}

is a sum of independent bounded zero-mean random variables, and we can apply
Hoeffding’s inequality in Theorem
2

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2t^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\frac{2}{N}{|a_{ij}|}\right)^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{2}}\right).

At last, write

\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}
.

Theorem
17
implies that the accuracy for estimating
a single diagonal element depends only on the magnitude of the off-diagonal elements in the corresponding row and column.

We determine the minimal sampling amount
required to make
the Rademacher Monte Carlo estimator a componentwise (
\epsilon,\delta)

diagonal estimator. For symmetric matrices, this result coincides with the bound in Equation 40 of
[
3
]
but uses a different proof technique.

Corollary 18
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric,
and let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{Aw}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}^{\top}

be a Monte Carlo estimator
with independent Rademacher vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
Pick
\epsilon>0
, and a diagonal element
a_{ii}\neq 0
of
\boldsymbol{A}
. For any
0<\delta<1
, if the sampling amount is at least

N\geq\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}{a_{ii}^{2}}\right)\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}},

then
|a_{jj}-\widehat{a}_{jj}|\leq\epsilon|a_{jj}|
holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Define the 2-norm offdiagonal column sums

\mathrm{off}_{i}\equiv(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2})^{1/2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

and denote the bound for the failure probability in Theorem
17
by

\delta\equiv 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\,\mathrm{off}_{i}^{2}}\right),

and solve it for
t
,

t=\sqrt{\frac{2\,\mathrm{off}_{i}^{2}}{N}\ln(2/\delta)}.

Restate Theorem
17
in terms of the failure probability: With probability at most
1-\delta
, the absolute error of a specific diagonal element is bounded above by

|a_{ii}-\widehat{a}_{ii}|\leq t=\sqrt{\frac{2\,\mathrm{off}_{i}^{2}}{N}\ln(2/\delta)}.

Converting this absolute error into a relative error requires
t\leq\epsilon|a_{ii}|
, which implies

N\geq\left(\frac{\mathrm{off}_{i}}{|a_{ii}|}\right)^{2}\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}.

The minimal sampling amount for
computing
a_{ii}
with
the Rademacher Monte Carlo estimator
depends on

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}{|a_{ii}|}
, which
represents the relative 2-norm deviation of the
i
th column
and row of
\boldsymbol{A}
from diagonality.
Thus, the more diagonal the
i
th row and column, the fewer samples are required for
a
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.

5.2
Gaussian vectors

We present a componentwise absolute error
bound (Theorem
19
)
for Gaussian Monte Carlo estimators,
and a bound on the minimal sampling amount that makes the Gaussian
Monte Carlo estimator a componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimators
(Corollary
20
).
Our bounds are derived from and identical to bounds for trace estimators in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric,

L_{1i}\equiv|a_{ii}|+\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2},\qquad L_{i2}\equiv|a_{ii}|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

and let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

be a Monte Carlo estimator with
independent Gaussian vectors

\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
If
t>0
, then

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}\right|>t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2(L_{i2}+t\,L_{i1})}\right),\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

(25)

Proof.

Fix
i
for some
1\leq i\leq n
.
Write the diagonal element as an inner product

(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{j}}=\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{k},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N

involving the symmetric matrix

\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}0&&\frac{1}{2}a_{1i}&&0\\
&&\vdots&&\\
\frac{1}{2}a_{i1}&\cdots&a_{ii}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}a_{in}\\
&&\vdots&&\\
0&&\frac{1}{2}a_{ni}&&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}\quad\text{with}\quad\trace(\boldsymbol{B}_{i})=a_{ii}.

We can think of

\widehat{a}_{ii}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}}\right)_{ii}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\left(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\right)_{ii}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}}

as a Monte Carlo estimator
for
\trace(\boldsymbol{B}_{i})
, and apply
the bound for Gaussian trace estimators

[
7
, Theorem 1]

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}\right|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{4\|\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}+4t\|\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\|_{2}}\right),

where

\|\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|a_{ii}|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\frac{L_{i2}}{2},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{B}_{i}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|a_{ii}|+\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}\right)=\frac{L_{i1}}{2}.

Theorem
19

implies that with Gaussian vectors, the accuracy for estimating
a single diagonal element depends on the magnitude of all elements in the corresponding row and column. By contrast, the bounds of Theorem
17
for Rademacher vectors depend only on the magnitude of the off-diagonal elements.

We determine the minimal sampling amount required to make the
Gaussian Monte Carlo estimator a componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.

Corollary 20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric,

\Delta_{1i}\equiv 1+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}}{|a_{ii}|},\qquad\Delta_{i2}\equiv 1+\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}}{|a_{ii}|}\right)^{2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

and let

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

be a Monte Carlo estimator with
independent Gaussian vectors

\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
1\leq k\leq N
.
Pick
\epsilon>0
, and a diagonal element
a_{ii}\neq 0
of
\boldsymbol{A}
. For any
0<\delta<1
, if the sampling amount is at
least

N\geq(\Delta_{2i}+\Delta_{1i}\epsilon)\,\frac{2\ln{(2/\delta)}}{\epsilon^{2}}

then
|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}|\leq\epsilon\,|a_{ii}|
holds with probability
at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the lower bound for
N
in
[
7
, Theorem 1]
.

The required sampling amount for computing
a_{ii}
with the Gaussian Monte Carlo
estimator depends on
\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}/|a_{ii}|
which can be interpreted as the 2-norm
derivation of the
i
th column and row of
\boldsymbol{A}
from diagonality. The more diagonal
the
i
th row and column, the smaller the sampling amount for the
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.

5.3
Normalized Gaussian vectors

We extend and complete the analysis
in
[
4
]
for a Monte Carlo estimator based on normalized
Gaussian vectors,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\right)\oslash\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\right),

(26)

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are independent random
vectors, and
\oslash
denotes elementwise division.
We derive the
distribution of the componentwise absolute errors (Lemma
21
),
followed by a bound (Theorem
22
).

We represent the distribution for the absolute errors in the
diagonal elements in terms
of a
Student
t
-distribution
with
N\geq 1
degrees of freedom
[
13
, Definition 7.3.3]
,

T_{N}\equiv\frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/N}},

(27)

where
Z
is a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1)

random variable, and
U
an independent chi-square random variable
with
N
degrees of freedom.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be symmetric, and (
26
)
be a Monte Carlo estimator
where
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are independent Gaussian random
vectors,
1\leq k\leq N
.
The absolute errors in the diagonal elements
are distributed as

\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}\ \sim\ \sqrt{\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}{N}}\,T_{N},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Proof.

Due to the normalization in the
denominator, we can extract the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{A}
from the diagonal elements
of the Monte Carlo estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}
,

\widehat{a}_{ii}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a_{ij}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{j}}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}^{2}}}=a_{ii}+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{j}}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}^{2}}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Normalize across the
i
th elements of the Gaussian vectors
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}
to unit vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

with elements

(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{k}\equiv\frac{(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{z}_{k^{\prime}})_{i}^{2}}}},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N,\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

Use the denominator to normalize the
i
th component in the
i
th
absolute error,

\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{k}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{N}{(\boldsymbol{z}_{k^{\prime}})_{i}^{2}}}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(28)

The rotational invariance of the standard Gaussian distribution guarantees the
independence of the direction vectors

\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
and radial components
\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}^{2}}
; see
[
20
, Exercise 3.3.6]
.
Hence, the numerator and denominator in (
28
)
are independent. We can rewrite (
28
) as

\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}=\frac{Z_{i}}{\sqrt{U_{i}/N}},

where we define the random variables
Z_{i}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}{a_{ij}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{k}}/\sqrt{N}
and
U_{i}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})_{i}^{2}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
The random variable
U_{i}
has a chi-square distribution with
N
degrees of freedom.
The conditional distribution of
Z_{i}
given
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
is Gaussian (see e.g. Exercise 3.3.3(a) of
[
20
]
), with zero mean and variance

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{k}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}).

Therefore,
Z_{i}|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{N}(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}))
. However, the conditional distribution is independent of
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
, so this is also the unconditional distribution. The claim then follows from (
27
).

Remark 5.1
.

It was observed in
[
3
]
that the square of the error
(\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii})^{2}
has a scaled
F
-distribution. We note that the square of a Student
t
-distribution is specifically a scaled
F
-distribution with one degree of freedom in the numerator. Moreover, for a single sample
N=1
the error has a Cauchy distribution, which has undefined mean and variance.

If
N
is large, then the
t
-distribution
T_{N}
can be approximated by a standard normal distribution. However,
T_{N}
has wider tails, thus somewhat weaker tail bounds. Existing tail bounds for the Student
t
-distribution
imply the following concentration inequality
for error bounds.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be symmetric, and (
26
)
be a Monte Carlo estimator
where
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are independent Gaussian random
vectors,
1\leq k\leq N
.
For any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}|>t\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\,(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2})}{\pi N}}\,\frac{1}{t}\,\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Proof.

The probability density function of
T_{N}
is
[
17
]

f_{N}(x)=c_{N}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{N}\right)^{-\frac{N+1}{2}}\qquad\text{where}\quad c_{N}\equiv\frac{\Gamma((N+1)/2)}{\Gamma(N/2)\sqrt{N\pi}},

and
1/\pi\leq c_{N}\leq 1/\sqrt{2\pi}
. If
F_{N}(x)
is the cumulative distribution
function for
T_{N}
then by

[
17
, Theorem 3.1]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii})>\sqrt{\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}{N}}x\right]=

\displaystyle 1-F_{N}(x)<\frac{f_{N}(x)}{x}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{N}}{x}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{N}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}.

Take
x=t\,\sqrt{\frac{N}{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}}
and bound the upper tail with
c_{N}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}
, to obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[(\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii})>t\right]\leq\frac{1}{t}\sqrt{\frac{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}{2\pi N}}\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}.

Since
T_{N}
is symmetric about the origin, the lower tail has the same bound. Using a union bound gives the desired inequality.

We determine a sampling amount sufficient to make the normalized Gaussian Monte Carlo estimator a componentwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
estimator.

Corollary 23
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be non-diagonal symmetric, and let

\Psi_{i}\equiv\frac{|a_{ii}|}{(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2})^{1/2}},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

and let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}
be defined as in (
26
). Pick
\epsilon>0
and a diagonal element
a_{ii}\neq 0
of
\boldsymbol{A}
. For any
0<\delta<1
, if the sampling amount is positive and at least

N\geq 1+2\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\delta\epsilon\Psi_{i}}\right)/\ln(1+\epsilon^{2}\Psi_{i}^{2}),

then
|\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii}|\leq\epsilon|a_{ii}|
holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

In Theorem
22
, set
t=\epsilon|a_{ii}|
. If the sampling number satisfies the desired bound, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\widehat{a}_{ii}-a_{ii})>t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\,(\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2})}{\pi N}}\,\frac{1}{t}\,\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{ii}^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\epsilon\Psi_{i}\sqrt{N}}\left(1+\epsilon^{2}\Psi_{i}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\epsilon\Psi_{i}}\left(1+\epsilon^{2}\Psi_{i}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}}

where the final inequality holds since
N\geq 1
by assumption. Set the failure probability
\delta
to the right hand side as

\delta\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\epsilon\Psi_{i}}\left(1+\epsilon^{2}\Psi_{i}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{N-1}{2}},

and solve for
N
.

The larger the value of
\Psi_{i}
(the same measure of diagonal dominance that appears in Corollary
18
), the smaller the sampling amount for the (
\epsilon
,
\delta
) estimator.

6
Application: Monte Carlo estimators for a derivative-based global sensitivity metric

We bound the absolute error (Theorem
24
)
in a Monte Carlo estimator for global sensitivity analysis,
and more specifically for a derivative-based global sensitivity metric (DGSM)
of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
whose partial derivatives are square integrable with respect to a probability density function

\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The DGSM is equal to the diagonal
\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})

of the matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})]^{\top}\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
is well-defined, symmetric positive semidefinite,
and can be interpreted as a second moment matrix of the gradient.
We compute the DGSM with the Monte Carlo estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}\qquad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\equiv\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})

(29)

and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq N
, are independent samples from the distribution
of
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Below is a normwise bound
for the error in the DGSM computed by the Monte Carlo estimator (
29
). Its
derivation is related to the analysis in
[
12
, Section 4]
.

Theorem 24
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

have square integrable partial derivatives with respect to the probability
density function
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\leq\beta
almost surely,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
be the Monte Carlo estimator in (
29
), and

\displaystyle c_{\max}

\displaystyle\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2}\qquad S_{1}\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\left(\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\right)\|_{2}

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv c_{\max}+\beta^{2},\qquad d\equiv\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{ii}(\beta^{2}-c_{ii})}{S_{1}}.

If
c_{\max}>0
and
S_{1}>0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq 8d\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{S_{1}+S_{2}t/3}\right).

Proof.

Before applying the matrix Bernstein inequality in
Theorem
3
, we need to verify the assumptions.
The Monte Carlo estimate
\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})

is an unbiased estimator of the DGSM
\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})
whose largest diagonal
element is

c_{\max}=\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{|c_{ii}|}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left[(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}))_{i}^{2}\right]\leq\beta^{2}.

The absolute error in the DGSM computed by the Monte Carlo estimator
(
29
) is

\boldsymbol{Z}=\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{S}_{k},\qquad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\right).

The summands
\boldsymbol{S}_{k}
have zero mean and are bounded by

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2}+\beta^{2}}{N}=\frac{c_{\max}+\beta^{2}}{N}=\frac{S_{2}}{N},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

We let
L=S_{2}/N
so that
\|\boldsymbol{S}_{k}\|_{2}\leq L
. The variance is

\Vy[\boldsymbol{Z}]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{S}_{k}^{2}]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right].

(30)

Linearity of the expectation,
the majorization
\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\preceq\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{I}
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})]=\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})

and commutativity of diagonal matrices imply for the summands,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})^{2}+(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}))^{2}-2\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\,\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\top}))^{2}\right]-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq\beta^{2}\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})^{2},\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.

Substitute the above into (
30
)

\displaystyle\Vy[\boldsymbol{Z}]\preceq\boldsymbol{V}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\left(\beta^{2}\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})^{2}\right),

and apply Theorem
3

with
\nu=\|\boldsymbol{V}\|_{2}=S_{1}/N
and
d=\intdim(\boldsymbol{V})
.

Below is the minimal sampling amount that
makes the Monte Carlo estimator in (
29
)
a normwise
(\epsilon,\delta)
diagonal estimator.

Corollary 25
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

have square integrable partial derivatives with respect to the probability
density function
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x})
,

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\leq\beta
almost surely,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
be the Monte Carlo estimator in (
29
), and

\displaystyle c_{\max}

\displaystyle\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2},\qquad S_{1}\equiv\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\left(\beta^{2}\boldsymbol{I}-\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\right)\|_{2}

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv c_{\max}+\beta^{2},\qquad d\equiv\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{ii}}{S_{1}}.

Pick
\epsilon>0
.
If
c_{\max}>0
and
S_{1}>0
, then
for any
0<\delta<1
, if the
sampling amount is at least

N\geq\frac{S_{2}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\left(2\epsilon+\frac{6S_{1}}{c_{\max}\,S_{2}}\right)\ln(8d/\delta),

then
\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})-\mathcal{D}\,(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{C})\|_{2}
holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
corollary

9
but is based instead on
theorem

24
.

We determine the constants in Corollary

for two different functions

,
and random variables

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

from a uniform distribution over

Linear Function

Let
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{h}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
with
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

almost surely. The second moment matrix
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{hh}^{\top}
has a largest diagonal entry

. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
have entries
v_{i}=h_{i}^{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\infty}^{2}-h_{i}^{2}\right)
for
1\leq i\leq n
. The constants in Corollary
25
are

S_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\infty},\quad S_{2}=2\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\infty}^{2},\quad d=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}}{S_{1}}.

Quadratic function

Let
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{x}
where
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}

is a symmetric square root of the positive semidefinite matrix
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{S}^{2}
. Then
\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\leq\beta\equiv\|\boldsymbol{S}\|_{\infty}
almost surely. The second moment matrix
\boldsymbol{C}=\frac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{M}

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

for
1\leq i\leq n
. The constants in Corollary
25
are

S_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\infty},\quad S_{2}=\frac{1}{3}\,\|\mathcal{D}\,(\boldsymbol{M})\|_{\infty}+\|\boldsymbol{S}\|_{\infty}^{2},\quad d=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}}{S_{1}}.

8
Conclusion and future work

This paper derives probabilistic bounds for the Monte Carlo diagonal estimators; the bounds come in two flavors, normwise and componentwise bounds for the absolute and relative errors of the Monte Carlo estimators. There are several avenues for future work. First, it would be interesting to study the accuracy of the diagonal estimator for matrix functions when a polynomial, or rational approximation to the matrix function is used. Second, we are currently pursuing the extension of the analysis of the Monte Carlo diagonal estimators to estimators for the selected entries (possibly including offdiagonal entries) of a matrix.
