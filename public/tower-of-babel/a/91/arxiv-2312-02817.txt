Quantum simulation for time-dependent Hamiltonians – with applications to non-autonomous ordinary and partial differential equations
Yu Cao, Shi Jin, Nana Liu

Abstract
Non-autonomous dynamical systems appear in a very wide range of interesting applications, both in classical and quantum dynamics, where in the latter case it corresponds to having a time-dependent Hamiltonian. However, the quantum simulation of these systems often needs to appeal to rather complicated procedures involving the Dyson series, considerations of time-ordering, requirement of time steps to be discrete and/or requiring multiple measurements and postselection. These procedures are generally much more complicated than the quantum simulation of time-independent Hamiltonians. Here we propose an alternative formalism that turns any non-autonomous unitary dynamical system into an autonomous unitary system, i.e., quantum system with a time- independent Hamiltonian, in one higher dimension, while keeping time continuous . This makes the simulation with time-dependent Hamiltonians not much more difficult than that of time-independent Hamiltonians, and can also be framed in terms of an analogue quantum system evolving continuously in time. We show how our new quantum protocol for time-dependent Hamiltonians can be performed in a resource-efficient way and without measurements, and can be made possible on either continuous-variable, qubit or hybrid systems. Combined with a technique called Schrödingerisation, this dilation technique can be applied to the quantum simulation of any linear ODEs and PDEs, and nonlinear ODEs and certain nonlinear PDEs, with time-dependent coefficients.



I
Introduction

Non-autonomous dynamical systems
Kloeden and Rasmussen 2011
;
Kiguradze and Chanturia 2012
;
Gunzburger et al. 2007
appear in broad areas of applications, in both classical
Clemson and Stefanovska 2014
and quantum physical systems
Poulin et al. 2011
, in adiabatic quantum simulation
Albash and Lidar 2018
, in biological systems
Thieme 2000
;
Coutinhoa et al. 2006
, and in finance such as the Black-Scholes equations with time-dependent coefficients – for example the risk-free interest rate
Lo et al. 2003
. Numerical simulation of such systems are challenging
Gunzburger et al. 2007
, especially when the time-dependent operator in the system do not commute at different times. In this case, the evolution operator needs time-ordering which is usually interpreted by Dyson’s theory
Reed and Simon 1975
, in the case of unitary dynamics. Quantum simulation of such systems – with time-dependent Hamiltonians – also become more difficult. For instance, when using Trotter splitting, each step in the split involves a different Hamiltonian, corresponding to
different quantum circuits at different times.

In this paper we introduce a strategy based on extending non-autonomous dynamical systems, including both ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and partial differential equations (PDEs), to new systems of autonomous PDEs in one higher dimension. This new dimension, which can be treated like a spatial dimension, gives rise to an extra linear convection term and acts like a clock parameter. While previous classical methods
Guckenheimer and Holmes 2013
;
Wiggins and Zeidler 1991
have also used such a clock parameter, they turn non-autonomous systems of linear ODEs into a system of nonlinear ODEs in one higher dimension. In our approach, the autonomous PDEs in one higher dimension
remains linear
. Such linearity not only reduces resources but is also much more suitable for quantum simulation.

This idea can be naturally used for unitary dynamics, allowing us to avoid using time ordering and Dyson’s series – which is the foundation of many of the existing quantum algorithms for time-dependent Hamiltonians (e.g .,
Poulin et al. 2011
;
Wiebe et al. 2010
;
Low and Wiebe 2018
;
Berry et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
An et al. 2021
;
An et al. 2022
) in constructing similarly efficient quantum algorithms. Our formulation has several key benefits: 1) We do not require discrete time-steps at the outset, which is necessary, for instance, for a formalism based on Dyson’s series. Our formulation instead transforms the original problem into unitary evolution with a time-independent Hamiltonian in
continuous time
using only one additional mode, thus making it also a candidate for analogue quantum simulation. 2) Unlike many previous methods, we do not require any measurements or postselection. 3) The initial ancilla states can also be a fully mixed state, which can further simplify implementation. 4) Furthermore, unlike some previous algorithms, we also do not require extra access to operations dealing with time-ordering or the requirement of implementing a different unitary at a different time-step.

We can easily extend this formalism also to non-unitary dynamics, while still easily maintaining continuity in the time parameter, as well as all other parameters. For this we will use the Schrödingerisation technique
Jin and Liu 2023
;
Jin et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2023a
, which can turn any non-unitary dynamics into unitary dynamics while preserving continuity in time. Schrödingerisation can thus be directly integrated with our new method. This means that no discretisation of either time or spatial parameters at any stage is necessary for the formalism to apply.

Our protocol finds applications not only in linear ODEs and PDES with time-dependent coefficients, but also some nonlinear PDEs including scalar hyperbolic balance laws, and Hamilton-Jacobi equations. These systems can be transferred to linear transport equations via the level set techniques
Jin and Osher 2003
;
Jin and Liu 2022
, and then the aforementioned strategy applies.

Before moving on, we also mention here a few related, but in-equivalent, works in the mathematical physics community and in the foundations of quantum physics. To the best of our knowledge, Howland firstly proposed an augmented Hamiltonian, also known as
“Howland’s clock Hamiltonian”
to study scattering theories
Howland 1974
. This formalism was recently adopted by e.g.,
Burgarth et al. 2023
to construct a dilation method for open quantum systems. However, these tools were used more as a mathematical method for the study of quantum systems rather than an explicit proposal of a quantum protocol to allow the quantum simulation of non-autonomous systems, which we demonstrate explicitly in this paper along with various applications. We emphasise that having the same Hamiltonian does not mean that one has the same protocol. In the foundations of quantum physics community, a related set of works relies on the Page-Wooters mechanism
Page and Wootters 1983
, which looks at turning time-dependent quantum dynamics into time-independent quantum dynamics, rather than just autonomous quantum mechanics. It can be used for instance in finding a quantum description for time
Giovannetti et al. 2015
. We briefly summarise the mechanism in Appendix
C
. Although it is clear that it is distinct from our proposal, it is interesting to speculate on how our protocol could inform future studies in this foundational area.

Although our initial formulation is chiefly based on continuous-variables, this is for the purpose of conveying the central ideas in as clean a manner as possible. Our approach can be easily adopted to either continuous-variable quantum systems (qumodes), qubits or hybrid qubit-qumode formulations, which we describe later in the paper.

In Section

we will formulate our main idea of turning a non-autonomous unitary system into a linear autonomous PDE system with unitary dynamics, so this applies also to classical simulation. In Section
III
we describe how general linear non-autonomous systems can be mapped onto quantum simulation with a time-independent Hamiltonian operating only on one extra mode. We look at non-autonomous unitary dynamics and non-unitary dynamics separately, where in the latter case we apply Schrödingerisation. In this section, we also describe the qubit formulation and hybrid formulations. See Figures
1
and
2
for a summary of the protocols. In Section
IV
we describe applications to quantum systems and more general linear and nonlinear ODEs and PDEs. In Section

VI
.

Notation

Here we summarise our notation below. A vector
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
is time-dependent and in the ket-notation
|u(t)\rangle\equiv\boldsymbol{u}(t)/\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|
denotes its normalised counterpart, where
\|.\|
is the
l_{2}
-norm. When
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
obeys unitary evolution, then
\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|=\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|
, where we can assume
\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|=1
unless otherwise stated, so
|u(t)\rangle=\boldsymbol{u}(t)
. According to the context,
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
could act on either finite-dimensional or infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Quantum information processing protocols are often introduced in terms of qubits, which are the quantum counterparts to classical bit-strings
\{0,1\}^{\otimes m}
. A qubit uses the eigenbasis
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
for the computational basis. An
J
-qubit system is a tensor product of
J
qubits which lives in
2^{J}
-dimensional Hilbert space, so
|u(t)\rangle\propto\sum_{j=1}^{J}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(t)|j\rangle
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(t)
is the
j^{\text{th}}
-component of
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
. A continuous-variable (CV) quantum state, or
‘qumode’
, on the other hand, spans an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. A qumode is the quantum analogue of a continuous classical degree of freedom, like position, momentum or energy before being quantised. A qumode is equipped with observables with a continuous spectrum, such as the position
\hat{x}
and momentum
\hat{p}
observables of a quantum particle. Its eigenbasis can be chosen to be for instance
\{|x\rangle\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
, which are the eigenstates of
\hat{x}
. A system of
D
-qumodes is a tensor product of
D
qumodes. The qubit and qumode language can be easily interchanged as well, in the limit where we are restricted to a truncation of the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. In this paper, although most of the language is in the qumode of CV language, this is done for simplicity only, and the results are easily translated into that of qubits.

In this paper, we use
[\hat{A},\hat{B}]=\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{B}\hat{A}
to denote commutation relations and
\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\}=\hat{A}\hat{B}+\hat{B}\hat{A}
to denote anticommutation relations.

II
Transforming a non-autonomous system to an autonomous system with unitary dynamics

We consider a general linear non-autonomous dynamical system
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}(t)}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t)\qquad\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}.

(1)

Here
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
can be either a finite-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}u_{j}(t)|j\rangle
(for
J
-dimensional ODEs), or an infinite-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}u(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})|x_{1},\cdots,x_{D}\rangle dx_{1}\cdots dx_{D}
, where
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
are differential operators with (non-linear) time-dependent coefficients.
Our aim is to transform this into an autonomous system with unitary dynamics.

Our first observation is that any system in Eq. (
1
) can be easily transformed into a linear non-autonomous system with unitary dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{y}(0)=\boldsymbol{y}_{0}.\end{aligned}

(2)

This is clear in the case when
\boldsymbol{A}
is Hermitian:
\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}(t)
, which describes a quantum system
\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\boldsymbol{y}(t)
evolving unitarily under a time-dependent Hamiltonian. The second case is the more general
\boldsymbol{A}(t)\neq\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t)
scenario. In this case we can use a method called Schrödingerisation
Jin and Liu 2023
;
Jin et al. 2023a
;
Jin et al. 2022
, where
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
can be easily constructed from
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
and acts on a Hilbert space with only one extra mode. Then there is another simple step to recover
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
from
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
. We will review this Schrödingerisation method in Section
III
.

We consider the time-dependent Hamiltonian system in Eq (
2
).
The solution to Eq. (
2
) can be written as

\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\mathcal{U}_{t,0}\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\qquad

(3)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t,s}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{s}^{t}\boldsymbol{H}(\tau)d\tau}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}e^{-i\boldsymbol{H}(t_{N})\Delta t}\cdots e^{-i\boldsymbol{H}(t_{1})\Delta t}=I+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-i)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{s}^{t}dt_{1}\cdots\int_{s}^{t}dt_{n}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{H}(t_{1})\cdots\boldsymbol{H}(t_{n}),

and
\mathcal{T}
is the chronological time-ordering operator.
Note that in the case where
[\boldsymbol{H}(t),\boldsymbol{H}(t^{\prime})]=0
, then no time-ordering is required since
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
commutes at different times. It is important to note that when
[\boldsymbol{H}(t),\boldsymbol{H}(t^{\prime})]\neq 0
,
\exp(-i\int_0^t \vect{H}(\tau)d\tau)
is
not
a solution to Eq. (
2
) and the time-ordering operation is necessary to define an ordering of the operations
\exp(-i\vect{H}(t_i) \Delta t)
where
t_{N}>t_{N-1}>\cdots>t_{1}
with
t=N\Delta t
.

The unitary operator
\mathcal{U}_{t,s}
satisfies the following properties
Reed and Simon 1975
:

Lemma 1
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{U}_{t,s}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\mathcal{U}_{t,s},\qquad\frac{d\mathcal{U}_{s,t}}{dt}=i\,\mathcal{U}_{s,t}\boldsymbol{H}(t),\qquad\mathcal{U}_{t,t}=\mathbf{1},

(4)

and for any
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t,s}=\mathcal{U}_{t,s^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}_{s^{\prime},s},\qquad\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{t,s}=\mathcal{U}_{s,t}.

(5)

However, solving the problem in Eq. (
2
) through this time-ordering is complicated. The difficulty for this non-autonomous system is that one needs to evolve according to the time-dependent operator
\mathcal{T}\exp(-i\int_{0}^t\vect{H}(\tau)d\tau)
in a chronological way. For instance, in quantum simulation, not only are time-ordered oracles necessary, but also at each time-interval, a
different
Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}(t_{i})
, corresponding to a different gate, is required.

In this section we instead propose a reformulation of the initial value problem in Eq. (
2
) to arrive at a dynamics that will only evolve according to a
time-independent
linear operator. In the case of Hamiltonian simulation for instance, this gives rise to a corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian. The idea is to introduce a new time variable
s
so that the problem becomes a new PDE system defined in one higher dimension, now with
time-independent
coefficients!

Theorem 2
.

For the non-autonomous system in Eq.(
2
), we introduce the following initial-value problem of an autonomous PDE

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}{\partial s}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(s)\boldsymbol{w}\\
&\boldsymbol{w}(0,s)=G(s)\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}

(6)

The analytical solution to this problem is

\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)=G(s-t)\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{s-t}^{s}\boldsymbol{H}(\tau)d\tau}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{H}(s-t+\tau)d\tau}.

(7)

When
G(s)=\delta(s)
, one can easily recover
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
in Eq. (
2
) from
\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)
using

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)\,ds.

(8)

Alternatively, when
G(s)=1
,
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
can be recovered with
\boldsymbol{w}(t,s=t)=\boldsymbol{y}(t)
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We will now prove that
\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)=G(\sigma)\mathcal{U}_{s,\sigma}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
solves Eq. (
6
) using
\sigma=s-t
. The LHS of Eq. (
6
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial G(\sigma)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial G(\sigma)}{\partial s}\right)\mathcal{U}_{s,\sigma}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}+G(\sigma)\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{s,\sigma}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{s,\sigma}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{y}_{0}

\displaystyle=G(\sigma)\left(\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{s,0}}{\partial s}\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}+\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{y}_{0}

\displaystyle=G(\sigma)\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{s,0}}{\partial s}\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}+G(\sigma)\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{y}_{0}

\displaystyle=-i\boldsymbol{H}(s)\boldsymbol{w}+G(\sigma)\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{y}_{0}

\displaystyle=-i\boldsymbol{H}(s)\boldsymbol{w}.

Here in the first line the first term in brackets is zero, since letting
\sigma=s-t
, clearly
\partial G(\sigma)/\partial s=-\partial G(\sigma)/\partial t
. In the second term we used the expansion
\mathcal{U}_{s,\sigma}=\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}
. In the third line we used the definition for
\mathcal{U}_{s,0}
which obeys Eq. (
4
) and
G(\sigma)\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)
. The second term in the third line goes to zero since
\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}/{\partial s}=-\partial\mathcal{U}_{0,\sigma}/{\partial t}
.

When
G(s)=\delta(s)
, Theorem
2
easily follows by integrating
\int ds\,\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)=\int ds\,\delta(s-t)\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}=\mathcal{U}_{t,0}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{y}(t)
.

When
G(s)=1
,
\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)=\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
, so
\boldsymbol{w}(t,s=t)=\mathcal{U}_{t,0}\boldsymbol{y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{y}(t)
.
∎

Remark 3
.

While Theorem
2
holds for Eq. (
2
) with unitary dynamics, the same result can also hold for non-unitary dynamics or
\boldsymbol{H}(t)\neq\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t)
in the case when
[\boldsymbol{H}(t),\boldsymbol{H}(t^{\prime})]=0
for all

. In this case no time-ordering is required in the solution, and
\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}=\exp(-i\int_{s-t}^s \vect{H}(\tau) d\tau)=\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\mathcal{U}_{0,s-t}
. Then it is clear that the same proof for Theorem

still holds. However, in the more general case
[\boldsymbol{H}(t),\boldsymbol{H}(t^{\prime})]\neq 0

\boldsymbol{H}(t)\neq\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t)
, we can no longer write
\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}=\mathcal{U}_{s,0}\mathcal{U}_{0,s-t}
and the time-ordered Dyson series itself may not even converge. We leave further investigation on this topic to future work.

III
Quantum simulation of a linear non-autonomous system using a time-independent Hamiltonian

We distinguish two kinds of non-autonomous dynamical systems. One obeys unitary dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{y}(0)=\boldsymbol{y}_{0}.\end{aligned}

(13)

and can describe closed quantum systems. The second more general dynamical system does not obey unitary dynamics

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}(t)}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}(t)\neq\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}.\end{aligned}

(14)

For systems obeying unitary dynamics, we only need to apply the formalism in Section
II
for transforming a non-autonomous system into an autonomous system with unitary dynamics. For systems obeying non-unitary dynamics, the Schödingerisation procedure is required to turn Eq. (
14
) into Eq. (
13
). We will look at the quantum simulation of these cases in the following.

III.1
Non-autonomous unitary dynamics

We are given a system
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
evolving under unitary dynamics in Eq. (
13
), so no Schrödingerisation is required.
To apply the formulation in Theorem
2
, we first define
\boldsymbol{\bar{w}}(t)\equiv\int ds\,\boldsymbol{w}(t,s)|s\rangle
and our aim is to first prepare the quantum state
|\bar{w}(t)\rangle=\boldsymbol{\bar{w}}(t)/\|\boldsymbol{\bar{w}}(t)\|
using a
time-independent
Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}

III.2
Non-autonomous non-unitary dynamics

For a general linear dynamical system

\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}(t)\neq\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t),

(18)

so we use the following decomposition

\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t),\qquad{\text{where}}\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{A}(t)+\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t))=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}_{1}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)=\frac{i}{2}(\boldsymbol{A}(t)-\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(t))=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}_{2}(t).

To turn this system into Eq. (
13
), we can apply Schrödingerisation
Jin et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2023a
;
Jin and Liu 2023
. The first step is the application of a warped phase transformation to
\boldsymbol{u}
by defining a new parameter
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, where for
\xi>0
, and let
\boldsymbol{v}(t,\xi)=\exp(-\xi)\boldsymbol{u}(t)
. We then extend evenly to
\xi<0
, see details in
Jin et al. 2023a
. Then Eq. (
18
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\xi}-i\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)\boldsymbol{v},\qquad\boldsymbol{v}(0,\xi)=e^{-|\xi|}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}.

Taking the Fourier transform of
\boldsymbol{v}(t)
with respect to
\xi
,
\boldsymbol{v}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
, gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{\partial t}=-i(\eta\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t))\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=-i\boldsymbol{H}_{\eta}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},

(19)

where
\eta\in(-\infty,\infty)
is the Fourier mode. Now the new Hamiltonian of the system is
\boldsymbol{H}_{\eta}(t)=\eta\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}_{\eta}^{\dagger}(t)
. The only difference is that now this is a system of Schrödinger type equations, one for each
\eta
. It can be represented by augmenting
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
by a single continuous-variable mode
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, and
\hat{\eta}
acts on
\boldsymbol{\eta}
by
\hat{\eta}\boldsymbol{\eta}=\eta\boldsymbol{\eta}
. Note that we have a corresponding conjugate operator
\hat{\xi}
where
[\hat{\xi},\hat{\eta}]=iI_{\eta}
and
\hat{\xi}
acts on mode
\boldsymbol{\xi}
by
\hat{\xi}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\xi\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Since
\eta,\xi
are conjugate variables, we can write
\langle\eta|\xi\rangle=\exp(-i\xi\eta)
. Defining
\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\int\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t,\eta)|\eta\rangle d\eta
, we then arrive at Eq. (
2
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\boldsymbol{y},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{y}_{0}=|\Xi\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\qquad|\Xi\rangle=\int\frac{2}{1+\eta^{2}}|\eta\rangle d\eta=\int e^{-|\xi|}|\xi\rangle d\xi,\end{aligned}

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
is the time-dependent Hamiltonian and
I_{\eta}
is the identity operation acting on the ancillary
\boldsymbol{\eta}
mode. To recover
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
from
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
, we just need to apply an inverse Fourier transform to
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
with respect to
\eta
to get
\boldsymbol{v}(t,\xi)
and then to recover
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{v}(t,\xi)d\xi.

However, Eq. (
20

|y(t)\rangle
using the time-dependent
\boldsymbol{H}(t)

20
) is now in the form of Eq. (
13

20

|y(t)\rangle
using a time-independent Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
. Then the state
|u(t)\rangle\equiv\boldsymbol{u}(t)/\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|
can be recovered from
|y(t)\rangle
through a measurement procedure. See Figure

III.3
Preparing
|u(t)\rangle
with an (imperfect) auxiliary clock register

The first protocol in Theorems
9
and
10
that prepares
|y(t)\rangle
by tracing out the clock register
|s\rangle
entirely is generally simpler for implementation, since no measurement process is required at the end. An important observation from Theorems
9
and
10
is that only the diagonal components
g(s,s)
of the initial ancilla state
\rho_{0}
matter for retrieving
|y(t)\rangle
– and subsequently
|u(t)\rangle
– rather than any off-diagonal term
g(s,s^{\prime})
for
s\neq s^{\prime}
. This means it is not the quantum coherence that acts as a resource but rather the ‘classical precision’ in the clock register
|s\rangle
through using
g(s,s)=\delta(s)
. Since
g(s,s)=\delta(s)
refers to a clock register with infinite precision, this is not possible to implement, and we will instead prepare physically realisable states, so we require
\delta\rightarrow\delta_{\omega}
. In an implementation, we can also prepare a mixed state
\rho_{0}=\int ds\delta_{\omega}(s)|s\rangle\langle s|
instead and this achieves the same precision as when using a pure state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=\int ds\,\sqrt{\delta_{\omega}(s)}|s\rangle
, since we retrieve the same
\gamma(t)=\text{Tr}_{s}(\sigma(t))
state in cases.

We want to bound the quantum fidelity
\text{Fid}(.\,,.)
between the target
|u(t)\rangle
and the true output state
\gamma(t)=\text{Tr}_{s}(\sigma(t))
, using the first protocol in Theorems
9
and
10
. For simplicity, we will not include the part requiring Schrödingerisation and work with
\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}(t)
, so
|u(t)\rangle=|y(t)\rangle
and

\displaystyle\text{Fid}(\gamma(t),|y(t)\rangle)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\ (\gamma(t)|y(t)\rangle\langle y(t)|)=\int ds\,g(s-t,s-t)|\langle y(t)|\mathcal{U}_{s,s-t}|y_{0}\rangle|^{2}\geq 1-\zeta.

When
g(s,s)=\delta(s)
, then clearly
\text{Fid}(\gamma(t),|y(t)\rangle)=1
. However, if we can only access an approximation,
g(s,s)=\delta_{\omega}(s)
. As we see from Lemma
11
, this will give
1-\text{Fid}(\gamma(t),|y(t)\rangle)\approx O(\omega^{2})
, thus we need to choose widths
\omega\lesssim O(\sqrt{\zeta})
.

The second protocol in Theorems
9
and
10
that require a final projective measurement in
|s=t\rangle
doesn’t exist in the limit of
g(0,0)=1
. Instead, suppose we prepare a thermal (Gaussian) state with

\displaystyle g(s,s)

\displaystyle=\exp(-s^2/2\omega^2)/\sqrt{2\pi\omega^{2}},\ \forall s\text{ and }g(s,s^{\prime})=0,\ \forall s\neq s^{\prime}.

Then by measuring the last register in
|s=t\rangle
, we obtain
|y(t)\rangle
with probability
g(0,0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi\omega^{2}}
, which is only possible with perfect final measurement. In this case, although a larger
\omega
corresponding to a higher temperature state which might be simpler to prepare in some cases, a final more precise measurement is still required. Furthermore, the larger the
\omega
, the smaller
g(0,0)
so the final probability of retrieval of
|y(t)\rangle
and hence
|u(t)\rangle
will be multiplied by the factor
\omega
. Thus, in most cases where there is not much difference in resource in the preparation of
\rho_{0}
, which does not require any quantum coherence, the first protocol is preferred.

III.5
Hybrid system

We can also consider continuous-variable discrete-variable hybrid protocols, where
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
acts on both continuous-variable (qumode) or discrete-variable (qubit) degrees of freedom. The first scenario is if the clock register is kept as a continuous variable, whereas the time-dependent
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
acts on finite-dimensional Hilbert space. In this case only a single qumode is required and is especially suitable for non-autonomous ODEs. Alternatively, one can keep
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
acting only on qumodes whereas the clock register is discretised, in the way outlined in Section
III.4
. This can be suitable for example for non-autonomous PDEs where the discretisation of the PDE can bring problems, but we know that the preparation of
\rho_{0}
is simpler in the qubit setting, since it’s just a classical mixture of
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=-N/2}^{N/2}
states. We will examine these various settings for different problems and different platforms in upcoming work.

See Figure
1
for a schematic diagram of the process in Theorem

to retrieve the approximation to

when the clock mode is replaced by qubits.

IV
Applications

Here we will provide applications to general non-autonomous PDEs including quantum dynamics with time-dependent Hamiltonians, any linear non-autonomous PDE as well as non-autonomous nonlinear scalar hyperbolic and Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs and systems of nonlinear ODEs.

We will retain the language of continuous-variable qumodes only for simplicity. However, the formalism can be easily translated into qubit-based systems. For example, the auxiliary clock register can easily be discretised so the single qumode can be replaced by
\log_{2}N
qubits where
N
is the size of the discretisation, defined in Section
III.4
. Either a single qumode or
\log_{2}N
qubits is the maximum amount of extra spatial resources necessary to turn a non-autonomous unitary system into an autonomous one.

We can apply the method reviewed in Section
III
to Schrödingerise linear non-autonomous PDEs. The corresponding time-dependent Hamiltonian is then given in Eq. (
20
). If the time-dependent parameters of this Hamiltonian can be directly manipulated by good quantum control protocols, then no other methods are necessary. However, if a physical architecture is not capable to engineer the time-dependent control of relevant parameters, then we must dilate the system to obtain a time-independent Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
, formulated in Theorem
9
for quantum dynamics and Theorem
10
for more general linear dynamics. We will write down the corresponding
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
for different non-autonomous PDEs.

IV.1
Quantum dynamics

We begin with applications to time-dependent Schrödinger’s equations governing non-autonomous quantum systems. This can also be applied to Schrödinger’s equations with complex absorbing potentials and even open quantum systems with time-dependent Hamiltonians and system-environment interactions.

IV.1.1
Closed quantum systems

There are many problems in quantum physics that involve time-dependent Hamiltonians. Here we will only briefly mention an application relevant for computation is adiabatic quantum computation
Albash and Lidar 2018
, and leave descriptions of other applications to future work. Here the time dependence in the simplest case has the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}(t)=(1-t)\boldsymbol{H}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{H}_{f},\qquad t\in[0,1],

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{f}
are the initial and final Hamiltonians, and the ground state of
\boldsymbol{H}_{f}
can embed the solution of certain computational problems. Even for such a simple time-dependency, it is clear that the Hamiltonians don’t commute at different times

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{H}(t),\boldsymbol{H}(t^{\prime})]=(t^{\prime}-t)[\boldsymbol{H}_{0},\boldsymbol{H}_{f}]\neq 0,\qquad t\neq t^{\prime},

since we don’t want the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian to coincide with the ground state of the final Hamiltonian. From our formalism, this evolution can be achieved using a time-independent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\boldsymbol{H}(\hat{s})=\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{s}+\boldsymbol{H}_{f}\otimes\hat{s},

where the auxiliary mode can be either presented by a continuous variable or
\log_{2}N
qubits (where in the latter case
\hat{p}_{s}\rightarrow\hat{P}_{s}
and
\hat{s}\rightarrow\hat{S}
), depending on the physical implementation. More details on the relevance of our protocol for adiabatic quantum computing will be in an upcoming paper.

IV.1.2
Open quantum systems

We consider time-dependent Schrödinger’s equations with a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, as a way to model open quantum systems
de Morisson Faria and Fring 2006
;
Rotter 2009

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\boldsymbol{u}(t)=-i\boldsymbol{V}(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{V}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}_{1}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}_{2}(t),

(25)

where the
“effective Hamiltonian”

\boldsymbol{V}
is non-Hermitian. This is known as the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian formalism. This formalism can arise from e.g., a non-trace-preserving quantum channel
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\boldsymbol{M}\rho\boldsymbol{M}^{\dagger}
such that
\boldsymbol{M}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{M}\leq I
; one can rewrite this quantum channel by
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=e^{-i\boldsymbol{V}}\rho\ e^{i\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}}
where the effective Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{V}=i\log(\vect{M})
is non-Hermitian in general. The operator
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
can be physically interpreted as the Hamiltonian for the original closed quantum system, whereas the term
\boldsymbol{A}_{2}
can be viewed as a correction term from the system-environment interaction
Rotter 2009
.
The dissipative rate of the system is governed by

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\norm{\vect{u}(t)}^{2}=2\bra{\vect{u}(t)}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\ket{\vect{u}(t)}.

This means that for applications of Eq. (
25
) to open quantum systems,
\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\neq 0
. From Eq. (
21
), we see that the quantum state
|u(t)\rangle
can be prepared using two ancillary qumodes where the augmented Hamiltonian takes the following form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}

\displaystyle=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s}),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{V}(\hat{s})+\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}(\hat{s})),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(s)=\frac{i}{2}(\boldsymbol{V}(\hat{s})-\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}(\hat{s})).

We note that Eq. (
25
) is also relevant to modelling quantum systems with time-dependent Hamiltonians and with artificial boundary conditions like complex absorbing potentials
Jin et al. 2023b
;
An et al. 2023
.

IV.2
General ODEs and PDEs

IV.2.1
Linear ODEs

A system of
J
first-order linear ODEs can be written in the form

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u},\qquad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{J},

where here
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
is now a finite-matrix and does not consist of operators acting in infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. In the hybrid continuous-variable discrete-variable setting where we can assume the clock mode to be represented by a qumode, whereas
\boldsymbol{u}
remains discrete-variable (i.e., represented by qubits), the formulation in Theorem
10
can be directly applied, so the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s}),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{A}(\hat{s})+\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(\hat{s})),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})=\frac{i}{2}(\boldsymbol{A}(\hat{s})-\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}(\hat{s})).

This can easily extend to the fully discrete setting by discretising
s
and using the formulation in Section
III.4
.

Higher-order derivative cases can also be easily treated by adding more qubits, following methods in
Jin and Liu 2023
. For instance, we can look at second-order ODES for
\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{u}}{dt^{2}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}+i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u}=0,\qquad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(t)=(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\dagger}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(t)=i(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\dagger}(t),

We can dilate the system
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\boldsymbol{u}(t)\otimes|0\rangle+(d\boldsymbol{u}/dt)\otimes|1\rangle
. Then the following equation for
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
can be obtained

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}}{dt}=\begin{pmatrix}d\boldsymbol{u}/dt\\
d^{2}\boldsymbol{u}/dt^{2}\end{pmatrix}=-i\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{y},\qquad\boldsymbol{V}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&iI\\
\boldsymbol{A}(t)&-i\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)\end{pmatrix},\\
&\boldsymbol{V}(t)=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(t)=(\boldsymbol{V}+\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger})/2=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\dagger},\boldsymbol{V}_{2}=i(\boldsymbol{V}-\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger})/2=\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\dagger},\\
&\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger})/2=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{\dagger},\boldsymbol{A}_{2}=i(\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger})/2=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}^{\dagger},\\
&\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}+iI\\
\boldsymbol{A}-iI&-i(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger})\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\otimes\sigma_{y}-\frac{I}{2}\otimes\sigma_{y}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I-\sigma_{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(t)=\frac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}+iI\\
\boldsymbol{A}+iI&-i(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger})\end{pmatrix}=-\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\otimes\sigma_{y}-\frac{I}{2}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I-\sigma_{z}).\end{aligned}

(26)

Applying Schrödingerisation to
d\boldsymbol{y}/dt=-i\boldsymbol{V}(t)\boldsymbol{y}

\boldsymbol{y}(t)\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)
obeying

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}+i(\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(t)\otimes\hat{\eta}+\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(t)\otimes I)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(t)\otimes\hat{\eta}+\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(t)\otimes I=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t).

It is straightforward to generalise to higher-order derivatives in

For a system of non-autonomous nonlinear ODEs, we will treat later in Section

IV.2.2

Consider a linear homogeneous non-autonomous PDE for

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{k,j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial^{k}u}{\partial x_{j}^{k}}+b(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u=0,

To embed this problem into a quantum simulator, without discretising

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\int dx\,u(t,x)|x\rangle,\qquad x\equiv(x_{1},\cdots,x_{D}).

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{k,j}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})i^{k+1}\hat{p}_{j}^{k}-ib(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}).

Here

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s}).

For example, we can look at first-order homogeneous time-dependent PDEs for

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}+b(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u=0,\qquad a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},b\in\mathbb{C}.

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}=\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\hat{p}_{j}-ib(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\otimes\hat{\eta}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)\otimes I=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger},\\
&\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{p}_{j},a_{j}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\}+\frac{i}{2}(b^{*}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})-b(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})),\\

Thus the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{f}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\left(\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[a_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}),\hat{p}_{j}]+\frac{1}{2}(b(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})+b^{*}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}))\right)\\
&+I_{\eta}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{p}_{j},a_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\}+\frac{i}{2}(b^{*}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})-b(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}))\right)\end{aligned}

(29)

where we made the replacement
t\rightarrow\hat{s}
in
a(t,\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{D})
and
b(t,\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{D})
. Note that since
\hat{s}
in
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
in Eq. (
29
) only acts on the clock mode, the operation commutes with
\hat{p}_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,D
. Thus if
a_{j}(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})
has terms that only depend on
t
, then those terms vanish in the commutation relation, and they will not contribute to the term in
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
containing
\hat{\eta}
. However, if
b(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})
has terms that only depend on
t
, then these terms do still show up in the time-independent Hamiltonian containing
\hat{\eta}
.

The simplest example is considering the convection equation with constant
a_{j}
in space, which varies linearly in time so
a_{j}=k_{j}t
,
k_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
b=0
. The corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian is then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+I_{\eta}\otimes\sum_{j=1}^{D}\hat{p}_{j}\otimes k_{j}\hat{s},

which only involves Gaussian operations. A slightly more involved example is that of a
D
-dimensional Liouville equation where
a_{j}=k_{j}x_{j}t
and
b=lt
where
k_{j},l\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
reduces to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{D}\mathbf{1}\otimes k_{j}\hat{s}+\mathbf{1}\otimes l\hat{s}\right)+I_{\eta}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}(\hat{x}_{j}\hat{p}_{j}+\hat{p}_{j}\hat{x}_{j})\otimes k_{j}\hat{s},

where the time-independent Hamiltonian has a non-Gaussian term.

We can also look at other examples. For instance, consider the
D
-dimensional general heat equation with a source term

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}D_{ij}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\right)+V(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u=0,\qquad D_{ij}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})>0,\,V(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\in\mathbb{R},

where
D_{ij}
are the components of a symmetric positive definite diffusion matrix. In this case the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s}),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{D}\hat{p}_{i}D_{ij}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\hat{p}_{j}+V(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}).

We can also consider the Fokker-Planck equation, which gives the time evolution of the probability density function
u(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})
of the velocity of a particle under the impact of drag and random forces. In the Fokker-Planck equation below,
\mu_{j}
are the components of the drift vector and
D_{j}
are the components of the diffusion vector

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(\mu_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u)-\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}(D_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u)=0,\qquad\mu_{j},D_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

The drift vector and diffusion coefficients
D_{j}
only have real-valued components, and
D_{j}>0
. The corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian then becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s}),

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{p}_{j},\mu_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[\hat{p}^{2}_{j},D_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[\mu_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}),\hat{p}_{j}]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{p}_{j}^{2},D_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\}.

Suppose we have additive noise
D_{j}=a_{j}
and when the drift is linear in time

\mu_{j}=c_{j}t,\qquad\boldsymbol{A}=-\sum_{j=1}^{D}c_{j}\hat{p}_{j}\hat{s}-i\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j}\hat{p}_{j}^{2},

and the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian in
D+2
qumodes is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\left(c_{j}I-a_{j}\hat{p}_{j}^{2}\right)\otimes I_{s}+I_{\eta}\otimes\sum_{j=1}^{D}c_{j}\hat{p}_{j}\otimes\hat{s}.

IV.2.3
Linear inhomogeneous PDEs with first-order time-derivative

A linear inhomogeneous PDE for
u(x,t)
with first derivative in
t
with an inhomogeneous term
f\neq 0
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{k,j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial^{k}u}{\partial x_{j}^{k}}+b(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u=f(t,x_{1},...,x_{D}),

(30)

where the coefficients satisfy
\text{sign}(a_{2k,j})=(-1)^{k}
. We can rewrite this as

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{f}(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}).

In the case where the time-dependence of the inhomogeneous term can be factored out like
f(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})=\exp(g_1(t))g_{2}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})
, then we only need to augment the system by a single qubit and perform the dilation
\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{u}\otimes|0\rangle+\boldsymbol{f}\otimes|1\rangle
and we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\boldsymbol{y}}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}\\
\boldsymbol{f}\end{pmatrix}=-i\boldsymbol{B}(t)\boldsymbol{y},\qquad\boldsymbol{B}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}(t)&iI\\
0&idg_{1}(t)/dt\end{pmatrix},\\
&\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{B}(t)=\boldsymbol{B}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{B}_{1}(t)=(\boldsymbol{B}(t)+\boldsymbol{B}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{B}_{1}^{\dagger}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(t)=i(\boldsymbol{B}(t)-\boldsymbol{B}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{B}_{2}^{\dagger}(t),\\
&\boldsymbol{B}_{1}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)&iI/2\\
-iI/2&\frac{i}{2}\frac{d(g_{1}-g_{1}^{*})}{dt}\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I+\sigma_{z})+\frac{I}{2}\otimes\sigma_{y}+\frac{i}{2}\frac{d(g_{1}-g_{1}^{*})}{dt}\mathbf{1}\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I-\sigma_{z}),\\
&\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)&-I/2\\
-I/2&\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(g_{1}+g_{1}^{*})}{dt}\end{pmatrix}=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I+\sigma_{z})-\frac{I}{2}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{d(g_{1}+g_{1}^{*})}{dt}\mathbf{1}\otimes\frac{1}{2}(I-\sigma_{z}),\end{aligned}

(31)

where
I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Note that the functions
dg_{1}/dt
and
dg_{1}^{*}/dt
can be computed classically offline. Then the time-dependent Hamiltonian after Schrödingerisation acting on
D+1
qumodes and a single qubit is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(t)+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{1}(t).

The corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian from Theorem
10
is then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{2}(\hat{s})+\mathbf{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{1}(\hat{s}),

which is a Hamiltonian acting on a system of
D+2
qumodes and a single qubit.

IV.2.4
Linear homogeneous PDEs with second-order time derivatives and beyond

It is also straightforward to extend to higher-order derivatives in time, following the same methods in
Jin and Liu 2023
. We look at the following homogeneous linear PDE (includes for example the wave equation as a special case)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+c_{0}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}\partial t}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j,l}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial^{l}u}{\partial x_{j}^{l}}+b(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u=0,

(32)

where the coefficients
a_{j,l}<0
when
l
is an even integer.
One can rewrite Eq. (
32
) into the following equation for
\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{u}}{dt^{2}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}+i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{u}=0,\qquad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)\equiv\boldsymbol{c}_{0}(t)+i\sum_{j=1}^{D}\boldsymbol{c}_{j}(t)(I^{\otimes j-1}\otimes\hat{p}_{j}\otimes I^{\otimes D-j}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(t)-i\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(t),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(t)=(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\dagger}(t),\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(t)=i(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\dagger}(t))/2=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\dagger}(t),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(t)=-i\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j,l}(t,\hat{x_{1}},...,\hat{x}_{D})(i\hat{p}_{j})^{l}+b(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\right)

where
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}(t)=c_{i}(t,\hat{x_{1}},...,\hat{x}_{D})
. From Section
IV.2.1
, we can dilate the system
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\boldsymbol{u}(t)\otimes|0\rangle+(d\boldsymbol{u}/dt)\otimes|1\rangle
. The formulation with Section
IV.2.1
is the same, except here
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
act on infinite dimensional Hilbert space. Then the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(\hat{s})+\mathbf{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\hat{s}),

with
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\hat{s}),\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\hat{s})
given in Eq. (
26
). This is easily generalisable to higher-order derivatives in
t
. If there is at most an
n^{\text{th}}
-order derivative in
t
, then we need
\log_{2}(n)
auxiliary qubits.

For instance, consider a
D
-dimensional wave equation of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{D}a_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=-V(t,x_{1},...,x_{D})u,\qquad u(0,x),\qquad x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},V\in\mathbb{R}.

In this case

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{1}(\hat{s})=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})\otimes\sigma_{x}+\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})-\frac{I}{2}\right)\otimes\sigma_{y},\qquad\boldsymbol{V}_{2}(t)=-\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})\otimes\sigma_{y}+\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+\frac{I}{2}\right)\otimes\sigma_{x},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s})=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[\hat{p}^{2}_{j},a_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})],\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{p}_{j}^{2},a_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D})\}+V(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D}).

IV.2.5
Nonlinear ODEs

Suppose we have a system of
J
nonlinear ODEs for
\gamma_{n}(t)
where each
\gamma_{n}(t)
obeys a nonlinear ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d\gamma_{n}(t)}{dt}=F_{n}(t,\gamma_{0},...,\gamma_{J-1}),\qquad n=0,...,J-1,

and
F_{n}
is a nonlinear function of its arguments. Here we introduce
J
auxiliary variables
q_{0},...,q_{J-1}
and a function
\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{J-1})
defined by

\displaystyle\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{J-1})=\prod_{n=0}^{J-1}\delta(q_{n}-\gamma_{n}(t)),\qquad q_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.

Then it is simple to check that
\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{N-1})
satisfies, in the weak sense
Jin and Osher 2003
;
Jin et al. 2023a
, the
linear

N+1
-dimensional PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{J-1})}{\partial t}+\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{n}}(F_{n}(t,q_{0},...,q_{J-1})\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{N-1}))=0.

(33)

We can treat this linear PDE with the same methods as before. We can define
\hat{q}_{n}
to be a quadrature operator whose eigenstate is
|q_{n}\rangle
and its conjugate quadrature operator as
\hat{Q}_{n}
. Thus
[\hat{q}_{n},\hat{Q}_{n}]=i
and we can use the correspondence
-i\hat{Q}_{n}\leftrightarrow\partial/\partial q_{n}
. Then defining
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)\equiv\int\Phi(t,q_{0},...,q_{N-1})|q_{0},...,q_{N-1}\rangle dq_{0}...dq_{N-1}
, we see that Eq. (
33
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t),\qquad\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\hat{Q}_{n}F_{n}(t,\hat{q}_{0},...,\hat{q}_{N-1}).

Using Schrödingerisation
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t)\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\otimes\hat{\eta}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)\otimes I=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}\{\hat{Q}_{n},F_{n}(t,\hat{q}_{0},...,\hat{q}_{J-1})\},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{J-1}[\hat{Q}_{n},F_{n}(t,\hat{q}_{0},...,\hat{q}_{J-1})],

where
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
is a time-dependent Hamiltonian on
J+1
qumodes. From Theorem
10
, the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian on
J+2
qumodes is then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(\hat{s})+I_{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(\hat{s}).

This method can also be applied to an approximation of general nonlinear PDEs, which will first be discretised spatially to form a system of ODEs. Here
J
can be larger or smaller depending on the discretisation technique.

IV.2.6
Nonlinear PDEs

There are some nonlinear PDEs where one does not need to take spatial discretisation first to form a system of nonlinear ODEs. Such problems belong to the cases where we can find a linear representation in higher dimension which is linear and equivalent to the original PDEs. These include the scalar hyperbolic and Hamilton-Jacobi equations
Jin and Liu 2022
;
Jin et al. 2023c
.

Scalar nonlinear hyperbolic PDEs

A
D
-dimensional scalar nonlinear hyperbolic PDEs for
u(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D})
with explicit time-dependence can be written

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}F_{j}(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D},u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}+Q(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},u)=0,\qquad u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),

(34)

Following the same formalism as in
Jin and Liu 2022
, we introduce a level set function
\phi(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi)
, where
\chi\in\mathbb{R}
. It is defined so its zero level set is the solution

u
:

\phi(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi)=0\quad{\text{at}}\quad\chi=u(t,x_{1},...,x_{D}).

By defining

\displaystyle\Psi(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi)=\delta(\phi(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi)),

it can shown that
\Psi
(like
\phi
) evolves according to the linear PDE in one extra dimension

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}F_{j}(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D},\chi)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{j}}+Q(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D},\chi)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\chi}=0

\displaystyle\Psi(0,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi)=\delta(\chi-u_{0}(x_{1},...,x_{D})).

If
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t)\equiv\int dx_{1}\cdots dx_{D}d\chi\Psi(t,x_{1},\cdots,x_{D},\chi)|x_{1},\cdots,x_{D},\chi\rangle
, then it satisfies the following linear ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{\Psi},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}F_{j}(t,\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\hat{p}_{j}+Q(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\hat{\zeta},

where
i\hat{p}_{j}=\partial/\partial x_{j}
,
i\hat{\zeta}=\partial/\partial\chi
. We can apply Schrödingerisation to this system
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\rightarrow\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)\otimes\hat{\eta}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)\otimes I=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{1}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}F_{j}(t,\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\hat{p}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{\zeta},Q(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}(t)=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[\hat{\zeta},Q(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})].

Here
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
consists of
D+1
modes and the Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}
operates on
D+2
modes. Then from Theorem
10
, the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian acting on
D+3
qumodes is then

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\bar{H}}&=I_{\eta}\otimes\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\hat{\eta}\otimes\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}[\hat{\zeta},Q(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})]\\
&\qquad+I_{\eta}\otimes\left(\sum_{j=1}^{D}F_{j}(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\hat{p}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{D}\{\hat{\zeta},Q(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{\chi})\}\right).\end{aligned}

Hamilton-Jacobi equations

A similar methodology applies to nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi equations. If
S
obeys the nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi PDE and
u=\gradient S
, then
u
solves the nonlinear hyperbolic system of conservation equations in gradient form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}H(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},u_{1},...,u_{D})=0,\qquad\forall j=1,...,D,\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}

where the function
H=H(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},p_{1},...,p_{2})
is a Hamiltonian. We now introduce
D
variables
\chi_{1},...,\chi_{D}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then one can define a system of level set functions
\phi=(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{D})
where each
\phi_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi_{1},...,\chi_{D})=0
at
\chi_{j}=u_{j}
. We can then define

\displaystyle\Psi(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi_{1},...,\chi_{D})=\prod_{j=1}^{D}\delta(\phi_{j}(t,x_{1},...,x_{D},\chi_{1},...,\chi_{D})),\qquad\Psi(t=0)=\prod_{j=1}^{D}\delta(\chi_{j}-u_{j}(t=0)).

which obeys the following linear transport PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\chi_{j}}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{D}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\chi_{j}}=0.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{dt}=-i\boldsymbol{H}(t)\boldsymbol{\Psi},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(t)=i\sum_{j=1}^{D}(\hat{\zeta}_{j}H(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})\hat{p}_{j}-\hat{p}_{j}H(t,\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})\hat{\zeta}_{j})=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t).

which acts on
2D
qumodes. Since
\boldsymbol{H}(t)=\boldsymbol{H}^{\dagger}(t)
because
H
is interpreted as a Hamiltonian and each of the quadratures is hermitian, no Schrödingerisation is required and we can directly apply Theorem
10
to obtain the corresponding time-independent Hamiltonian on
2D+1
qumodes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\boldsymbol{H}(\hat{s}),\qquad\boldsymbol{H}(\hat{s})=i\sum_{j=1}^{D}(\hat{\zeta}_{j}H(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})\hat{p}_{j}-\hat{p}_{j}H(\hat{s},\hat{x}_{1},...,\hat{x}_{D},\hat{p}_{1},...,\hat{p}_{D})\hat{\zeta}_{j}).

For both the scalar hyperbolic and Hamilton-Jacobi equations, observables of the PDE can be recovered from
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
using techniques in
Jin and Liu 2022
.

We point out that the level set methods for the above quasi-linear PDEs produce
multi-valued
solutions
Jin and Osher 2003
, not the viscosity solutions
Crandall and Lions 1983
;
Lax 1973
. The multi-valued solution corresponds to solutions obtained by the method of characteristics,
and has applications in semi-classical limit of quantum mechanics, geometric optics, and seismic waves (for multiple arrivals), and high-frequency limits of general linear waves, for examples
Jin and Osher 2003
.

V
Numerical experiments

We use a classical computer to emulate the above quantum algorithms for a few time-dependent dynamics with easily accessible solutions for validation: a closed Hamiltonian dynamics, a dynamics for open quantum system characterised via a non-Hermitian linear operator, as well as a 1D Fokker-Planck equation. Emulating high-dimensional PDEs and quantifying the actual quantum resources necessary in practice are interesting, but are beyond the scope of this current work, and we shall leave this for future investigations.

Simulation details

-qumode to

V.2
A Hamiltonian system

We consider a simple example for numerical validation. The dimension
n=2
and the time-dependent Hamiltonian is
H(s)=ag(s)\boldsymbol{h}
where
\boldsymbol{h}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{X}+\frac{1}{3}\sigma_{Y}+\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{Z}

VI
Discussion

We have shown how to turn any linear non-autonomous unitary system into an autonomous system obeying unitary dynamics, in one higher dimension. Combined with our method for turning any linear dynamical system into one obeying unitary dynamics in one higher dimension (Schrödingerisation), this means that the evolution of any linear system with time-dependent coefficients can be simulated on a quantum simulator with a single time-independent Hamiltonian with at most two additional modes for homogeneous systems with a first-order time derivative. For inhomogeneous systems, there is an additional single qubit, and one additional qubit for each higher-order time derivative. This means that any methods for the simulation of time-independent Hamiltonians can be immediately adapted to this time-dependent setting.

Apart from applications to ODEs and PDEs, there are many other interesting applications to explore in future work. These include further applications to both closed and open quantum systems, adiabatic computation, optimisation, and time-dependent iterative methods, that can also be applied to stochastic problems and applications of random matrices and many more.

Appendix A
Alternative protocol for the special case of the Hamiltonian that commutes at different times

In the most general case,
[\boldsymbol{H}_{\tau},\boldsymbol{H}_{\tau^{\prime}}]\neq 0
. We can also have a simplified scenario where we have the commutativity between the Hamiltonian at different times
[\boldsymbol{H}_{\tau},\boldsymbol{H}_{\tau^{\prime}}]=0
, which can be satisfied for
\boldsymbol{H}(t)=g(t)\boldsymbol{h}
where
\boldsymbol{h}
is a time-independent Hamiltonian. Then the corresponding unitary operation is

\displaystyle U(t)=e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}g(\tau)d\tau\boldsymbol{h}}=e^{-iG(t)t\boldsymbol{h}},\qquad G(t)\equiv\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}g(\tau)d\tau.

where no time-ordering operation is required since the Hamiltonian commutes at different times.
Our aim is to simulate the time evolution

\displaystyle U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle=|\psi(t)\rangle.

Now let us define two new time-independent Hamiltonians that act on the original system plus an ancillary mode

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}=\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s},\qquad U_{1}(t)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{1}t}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{2}=\boldsymbol{h}\otimes G(\hat{s}),\qquad U_{2}(t)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{2}t},\qquad[\hat{x},\hat{p}_{x}]=i\mathbf{1},

where
\hat{s}
is a quadrature operator acting on the ancillary mode. We now define an initial state

\displaystyle|0_{s}\rangle|\psi(0)\rangle

where
|0_{s}\rangle
is the
s=0
eigenstate so
\hat{s}|0_{s}\rangle=0
.

Applying the unitary
U_{2}(t)U_{1}(t)
to this initial state

\displaystyle U_{2}(t)U_{1}(t)|0_{s}\rangle|\psi(0)\rangle=U_{2}(t)|t_{s}\rangle|\psi(0)\rangle=e^{-iG(t)t\boldsymbol{h}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{s}}|t_{s}\rangle|\psi(0)\rangle.

Tracing out the first (ancillary) register, we end up with the state

\displaystyle e^{-iG(t)t\boldsymbol{h}}|\psi(0)\rangle=U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle,

which is the initial state that we desired.

This means that with the simulation of the unitary system of
D
qumodes with a time-dependent Hamiltonian, then we need only
D+1
qumodes and access to the two unitary operations. The most difficult part is to engineer the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{2}
when
G(t)
is a complicated function of
t
. We note that this is a different method to the method considered in the main text. This method is more closely akin to solving the first equation first, then inserting the constant value into the second equation in

\displaystyle\frac{ds}{dt}=1,\quad s_{0}=t_{0}

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{H}(s)\boldsymbol{u}.

By solving the first equation first, one gets
s=T
for some final time
T
. This is equivalent to the
U_{1}(T)
step. Then this means
H(s)\rightarrow H(T)
becomes a time-independent Hamiltonian (since
T
is now a constant). This method is not equivalent to that considered in the main text since in general
[\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s},\boldsymbol{H}(\hat{s})]\neq 0
.

Appendix C
Page-Wotters mechanism

Although at first our protocol might seem similar to the Page-Wotters mechanism, it is in fact a different protocol, but which still recovers the state
|y(t)\rangle
. The Page-Wooter’s mechanism relies on the preparation of the
ground state
of
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}=\mathbf{1}\otimes\hat{p}_{s}+\boldsymbol{H}(\hat{s})
. Here we discretise in
s
and can write the ground state as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}|\Psi\rangle=0,\qquad|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=-N/2}^{N/2}|y(s_{i})\rangle\otimes|i\rangle,

which is a quantum superposition of the time-dependent state
|y(t)\rangle
entangled with the clock register. To recover
|y(t)\rangle
, one would require a method to prepare the ground state of a given
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}
(which can even be NP-hard depending on the Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}(t)
). Given
|\Psi\rangle
, one would further need a projective measurement onto
|s=t\rangle
so

\displaystyle|y(t)\rangle\propto\langle s=t|\Psi\rangle.

This is clearly very resource intensive compared to our protocol. Its objective is also different to ours, namely to create a time-independent state
|\Psi\rangle
, whereas we only aim to create a state
|y(t)\rangle
that evolves according to a time-independent Hamiltonian.
