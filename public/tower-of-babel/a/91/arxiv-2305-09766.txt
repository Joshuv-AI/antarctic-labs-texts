Stopping Times of Boundaries: Relaxation and Continuity
H. Mete Soner, Valentin Tissot-Daguette

Abstract
We study the properties of the free boundaries and the corresponding hitting times in the context of optimal stopping in discrete time. We first prove the continuity of the map from the boundaries to the expected value of the corresponding stopping policy both in the supremum norm and also in a weaker, novel topology induced by the relaxed L ∞ L^{\infty} metric that we introduce. The latter is particularly useful when the optimal stopping boundary is only proved to be semicontinuous. Secondly, we study the connection between the hitting times, and their relaxations as widely employed in recent numerical methods. All these results together with the universal approximation capability of neural networks and the notion of inf/sup convolution are then used to provide a convergence analysis for the algorithm in [Reppen, Soner, and Tissot-Daguette, Neural Optimal Stopping Boundary, 2025] for the numerical resolution of the exercise regions arising in the analysis of Bermudan type option.



Keywords:
Hitting times, relaxed stopping rules, universal approximation theorem,

inf/sup convolution, convergence analysis, Bermudan options.

Introduction

The optimal stopping problem, given in its simplest form by

\sup\left\{v(\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\tau,X_{\tau})]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \tau=\text{ stopping time}\right\},

(1.1)

for a stochastic process
X
and
a reward function
\varphi
,
plays a central role in stochastic optimal control and has applications
in numerous quantitative fields.
In mathematical statistics, the theory of optimal stopping
can be utilized to address sequential tests, such as the quickest detection of a Wiener process

[
34
,
27
]
.
In mathematical finance, optimal stopping problems emerge naturally from
the pricing of American or Bermudan contingent claims

[
9
,
18
,
27
,
33
]
.
In this context,
\varphi
is the payoff of the claim,
X
describes
the price evolution of the underlying asset, and the optimal value in
(
1.1
) gives the initial price of the contract.
While the initial motivation of this paper is to provide
a convergence study of the neural stopping boundary
algorithm of
[
29
,
30
]
,
our analysis is essentially applicable to all scenarios
where the exit times play a central role. Consequently,
we present our findings in this broader context, then
analyze the mentioned algorithm using these general results.

The unifying structure is the approximate parameterization of
the
stopping boundaries

by feedforward artificial neural networks,
and the central question revolves around their asymptotic effectiveness.
In fact, the algorithm developed in
[
30
]

computes the
stopping
(or
exercise
)
region

for Bermudan options.

Following
[
30
]
,
we assume that in an appropriate coordinate system,
the optimal stopping region is the
epigraph

of a function
f^{\diamond}
. In other words, stopping occurs the first time the underlying process lies above the boundary
f^{\diamond}
.

Consequently, the hitting time of
f^{\diamond}
is optimal for (
1.1
).
Hence, we can restrict the set of stopping times in (
1.1
) to hitting times of boundaries.
Namely,

\sup\left\{v(\tau_{f})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\tau_{f},X_{\tau_{f}})]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ f=\text{ stopping boundary}\right\},

(1.2)

where
\tau_{f}
is the hitting time of the epigraph (or hypograph) of
f
.
In the neural boundary algorithm, networks approximate
f^{\diamond}
,
and the convergence analysis of this method
relies mainly on the properties of the relaxation of the hitting times,
and the continuity of the map from the boundaries
to the expected value of the corresponding stopping policy,
as proved in
Sections

3
and
4
, respectively.

It is well-known that, training with
stochastic gradient descent of loss functions
computed via hitting times results in vanishing gradient,
and as in the
deep optimal stopping
of
[
2
,
3
]
and in
[
30
]
,
relaxed stopping must be introduced.
Relegating the technical details to
Section

3
,
the relaxed version of problem (
1.1
) is given by,

\sup\left\{v(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}\left[\int\varphi(t,X_{t})\mu(dt)\right]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \mu=\text{ random probability measure}\right\}.

(1.3)

Also called
convexification

[
1
]
or
compactification

[
8
]
, relaxation procedures such as (
1.3
) are
classically used in stochastic control problems to prove the existence of an optimal solution
[
11
,
14
,
24
]
.
Indeed, relaxation convexifies the problem allowing
for numerous methods and tools for its analysis.
Moreover, as we prove in
Proposition

3.1
,
both (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) achieve the same optimal value.
Indeed, this holds essentially in all other contexts;
see, e.g.,
[
13
,
15
]
and
[
19
, Lemma 1.5.2.]

for continuous time optimal stopping, and

[
8
,
11
,
14
]
for general stochastic optimal control.

In the neural optimal stopping boundary method,
the stopping policy is relaxed by introducing a so-called
fuzzy
region around the boundary
with a small width
\varepsilon>0
. Concretely, the fuzzy region separates the continuation and stopping regions through a gradual transition:
the stopping rule remains the same outside the fuzzy region, while inside,
the usual binary decision (”exercise or continue”) is replaced by stopping intensities proportional to the signed distance to the boundary.

This procedure generates
relaxed
hitting times of boundaries
-
namely random
probability measures supported on the set of exercise dates.
The relaxed problem (
1.3
) thus becomes

\sup\left\{v(\mu_{f}^{\varepsilon})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}\left[\int\varphi(t,X_{t}){\mu_{f}^{\varepsilon}}(dt)\right]\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ {\mu_{f}^{\varepsilon}}=\text{relaxed hitting time of $f$}\right\}.

(1.4)

As shifting the boundary ever so slightly changes the stopping intensities inside the fuzzy region, the above relaxation makes stochastic gradient methods applicable.
The fuzzy region is akin to the
mushy zone

encountered in phase-field models
[
4
,
36
,
38
]
, a physical phenomenon characterized by the coexistence of two phases (typically solid and liquid) within a material.

The introduction of a fuzzy region is critical in other deep learning algorithms as well, such as occupation networks in computer vision
[
26
]
or deep level-set functions for solving Stefan problems
[
35
]
.

Our central results of continuity are proved in

Section

4
. In particular,
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
establish the continuity
of the value functional
\frakF\ni f\mapsto v(\tau_{f})
,
where
\frakF
is a set of stopping boundaries

that are lower semi-continuous
.
Formally, these results are
in the following form.

Theorem
.

(Continuity of
\bm{f\mapsto v(\tau_{f})}
)

Let
\frakm
be a metric on
\frakF
.
Then for all
\delta>0
, there exists
\iota>0
such that for all
f,g\in\frakF
,

\frakm(f,g)\leq\iota\ \Longrightarrow\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{g})|\leq\delta.

Indeed, while
Theorem

4.1
employs the supremum norm,
we consider a weaker topology induced by the
relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric

of Definition
4.1

in
Theorem

4.2
to accommodate boundaries that are only semi-continuous.
Concretely, this metric is defined as

\frakm(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frakm_{d}(f,g)+\frakm_{d}(g,f),\quad\;\;\frakm_{d}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{\psi\in\Psi}\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\psi(\xi))-g(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big],

where
\Psi
contains all endofunctions
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\calE
.
The above metric discussed further in subsection
4.1
is
similar to the Skorokhod
J1
distance for càdlàg processes,
and compares boundaries in
\frakF
uniformly,
possibly after a local transformation of the domain.
It is also inspired by the inf/sup convolution, which is an effective regularization
tool widely used in the theory of viscosity solutions
[
10
,
21
]
. We shall prove in
Lemma

4.1
that the ”directed” map

\frakm_{d}
is an
asymmetric metric
; see
Mennucci 2013
.

An immediate corollary,
Corollary

4.1
,

to the continuity results

is the convergence of the value (
1.4
)
associated to the relaxed stopping times
to the value using the same interface as the width
\varepsilon
of the fuzzy region tends to zero.

In
Section

5
, we combine the above results and provide a convergence
analysis of the
neural optimal stopping boundary
algorithm
[
30
]
.
Briefly, this algorithm looks for a
neural stopping boundary
,
namely a
feedforward neural network

g^{\theta}

such that

{v(\mu_{g^{\theta}}^{\varepsilon})}\approx v(\tau_{g^{\theta}})\approx\sup_{f\in\frakF}v(\tau_{f})\ =v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}}).

The parameter vector
\theta
is then trained by
maximizing the relaxed reward
v(\mu_{g^{\theta}}^{\varepsilon})
using stochastic gradient ascent.
The following result, stated precisely in
Theorem

5.1
, implies
the convergence of the algorithm employed in
[
30
]
.

Theorem
.

(Convergence)

For all
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a feedforward
neural network
g^{\theta}
such that
{v(\mu_{g^{\theta}}^{\varepsilon})}\geq v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}})-\delta
.

When the optimal stopping boundary
f^{\diamond}
is continuous,
one can apply the Universal Approximation
Theorem
[
6
,
16
]
stating that any continuous function can be
approximated arbitrarily well by feedforward neural networks in compact sets.
This together with the continuity of
f\mapsto v(\tau_{f})
,
implies the above result.
However, the optimal stopping boundary
f^{\diamond}

may fail to be continuous, preventing the use of the Universal Approximation Theorem.
Nevertheless, under mild assumptions, the stopping boundary is
shown to be semicontinuous in
Proposition

2.2
.

Motivated by this, we first extend in
Theorem

5.2
the Universal Approximation Theorem
by proving that semicontinuous functions can be
approximated by neural networks in the

relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric
introduced in Definition
4.1
.
This is achieved through inf-sup convolutions
recalled in
Section

5.2
.
This approximation together
with
Theorem

4.2
and the semicontinuity of
f^{\diamond}

enables us to prove the above
theorem.
Incidentally, we suggest that the universal approximation
proved in
Theorem

5.2
,
is an interesting result in its own right
and can be applied to other tasks involving the approximation of semicontinuous functions.

There is a recent body of literature on the convergence of deep learning methods in stochastic optimal control:
Huré et al. 2021
study the convergence of discrete time stochastic control problems involving policy and/or value iteration;
Lapeyre and Lelong 2021
prove the convergence of the Longstaff-Schwartz algorithm
[
23
]
using neural networks to approximate the continuation value;

Gonon 2022
sheds light on the expressivity and maximal size of neural networks to produce optimal stopping rules.
Additionally,
[
2
, Proposition 4]
of the
deep optimal stopping
paper
provies the near optimality of deep stopping strategies.
A common property used in all these papers is dynamic programming,
while the neural optimal stopping boundary algorithm

[
30
]
constructs an optimal stopping rule in a forward fashion.
Consequently, our convergence analysis necessitates
other techniques and tools, and differs greatly from previous works.

Structure of the paper.
In
Section

2
, we introduce the optimal stopping problem, our main structural assumption on the exercise region, and give financial examples. In

2
Optimal stopping

Let
\calT\subset[0,\infty)
a finite subset of time indices containing
0
and

(\Omega,\F=(\calF_{t})_{t\in\calT},\Q)
a filtered probability space.
The state
X=(X_{t})_{t\in\calT}

is an
\F
-adapted, Markov process which take values in a Euclidean space
\calX
. We assume for concreteness that
\F
is the filtration generated by
X
.
Given a
reward (or payoff) function

\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\calX\to\R
, the associated
optimal stopping problem
is to

\text{maximize}\ \ v(\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\tau,X_{\tau})],\qquad\text{over}\ \tau\,\in\,\vartheta(\calT),

(2.1)

where

\vartheta(\calT)
is the set of

\calT
-valued
\F
-stopping times.
Problem
(
2.1
)
is well-defined (and non-trivial) if
\max_{t\in\calT}|\varphi(t,X_{t})|\in L^{1}(\Q)
. We write
v^{\diamond}
for the optimal value of (
2.1
). The following assumption on
\varphi
and the dynamics of
X
will prove useful.

Assumption 2.1
.

There exists
a>0

such that

\varphi(t,\cdot)\in\calL_{a}\ \forall\ t\in\calT
, where

\calL_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{\phi\in\calC(\calX;\R)\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ |\phi(x)|\leq C[1+|x|^{a}]\ \ \text{for some}\ C>0\right\}.

(2.2)

Also, we suppose that
\phi\in\calL_{a}\Longrightarrow K_{t,s}\phi\in\calL_{a}

\forall\ t,s\in\calT
, with the transition semigroup

K_{t,s}\phi(x)=\E[\phi(X_{s})\mid X_{t}=x],\quad x\in\calX,\quad t<s.

(2.3)

Allowing the second component of
K_{\cdot,\cdot}
in (
2.3
) to be a stopping time,
define the value function

v^{\diamond}(t,x)=\sup_{t\leq\tau\in\vartheta(\calT)}K_{t,\tau}\varphi(\cdot,x)\ ,

(2.4)

and stopping region

\calS_{t}=\{x\in\calX\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\varphi(t,x)=v^{\diamond}(t,x)\},\ t\in\calT.

We then have the following result, proved in
[
30
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
the value function
v(t,\cdot)
belongs to
\calL_{a}
for each
t\in\calT
. In particular,
v^{\diamond}(t,\cdot)
is continuous and the stopping region
\calS_{t}
is a relatively closed subset of
\calX

.

Similar to
[
30
]
, we make the following structural assumption yielding the existence of a
stopping boundary
(or
free boundary
) delimiting the stopping region in a problem specific coordinate system.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exist measurable functions

\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calX\to\R_{+},\quad\Xi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calX\to\calE\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Xi(\calX),\quad f^{\diamond}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\ \calE\to[0,\infty],

such that
A=(\Xi,\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calX\to\calE\times\R_{+}
is a homeomorphism
and for every
t\in\calT
,

\calS_{t}=\{x\in\calX\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \eta\left(f^{\diamond}(t,\Xi(x))-\alpha(x)\right)\leq 0\},\qquad\eta\in\{-1,1\}.

(2.5)

We suppose that the
latent space

\calE
is itself a subset of a Euclidean space (e.g.
\R^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
) and write
|\xi|
for the norm of
\xi\in\calE
.
Under the above assumption, the
optimal stopping boundary
is thus given by

\displaystyle f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)=\begin{cases}\inf\{a\geq 0\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ A^{-1}(\xi,a)\in\calS_{t}\},&\eta=1,\\[5.0pt]
\sup\{a\geq 0\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ A^{-1}(\xi,a)\in\calS_{t}\},&\eta=-1.\end{cases}

(2.6)

\eta=1

\calS_{t}

f^{\diamond}(t,\cdot)

\eta=-1

For completeness, next we prove the following simple properties of
f^{\diamond}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For every
t\in\calT
, the map
\xi\mapsto f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
is lower (respectively upper) semicontinuous when
\eta=1
(resp.
\eta=-1
). Moreover, if the payoff function
\varphi
is convex in
x
, then
f^{\diamond}
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\calT
and prove the result for
\eta=1
. Similar arguments can be applied for the case
\eta=-1
.
Let
\xi\in\calE
and
(\xi_{n})\subset\calE
such that
\xi_{n}\to\xi
and fix
\varepsilon>0
. In light of
(
2.6
)
, there exists
a_{n}\in\R_{+}
such that
x_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A^{-1}(\xi_{n},a_{n})\in\calS_{t}
and
f^{\diamond}(t,\xi_{n})\geq a_{n}-\varepsilon
for every
n\in\N
. Defining
a=\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n},
then

\liminf_{n\to\infty}f^{\diamond}(t,\xi_{n})\geq a-\varepsilon.

Next, the continuity of
A^{-1}
yields
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{n_{k}}=x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A^{-1}(\xi,a)
for a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that
a_{n_{k}}\to a
. As
(x_{n_{k}})\subset\calS_{t}
and

is closed (see
Proposition

2.1
), then
x\in\calS_{t}
. Therefore
a\geq f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
, giving

\liminf_{n\to\infty}f^{\diamond}(t,\xi_{n})\geq f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)-\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
indeed shows the lower semicontinuity of
\xi\mapsto f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
.

For the second assertion, it is shown in
[
5
]
that the convexity of
x\mapsto\varphi(t,x)
carries over to
\calS_{t}
. In particular,
f^{\diamond}
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

In the remainder of the paper,
to simplify the exposition we assume that
\eta=1
, and the case
\eta=-1
follows
mutadis mutandis
. For fixed maps
(\Xi,\alpha)
that satisfies Assumption
2.2
with
\eta=1
, the corresponding optimal stopping boundary
f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
is therefore lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) in
\xi

, and we set

\frakF\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\ \calE\to[0,\infty],\;f(t,\cdot)\text{ l.s.c. }\forall t\ \in\calT\}.

(2.7)

We associate to each
f\in\frakF
the

\frakS_{t}(f)=\{x\in\calX\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \alpha(x)\geq f(t,\Xi(x))\},

(2.8)

and the

\tau_{f}=\inf\{t\in\calT\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X_{t}\in\frakS_{t}(f)\}
. Assumption
2.2
implies in particular that it is enough to consider hitting times of boundaries to achieve the optimal value in (
2.1
). Namely,

v^{\diamond}=\sup_{f\in\frakF}v(\tau_{f})=v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}}),

as already shown in
[
30

. Problem (

) is therefore equivalent to finding
f\in\frakF
such that
\frakS_{t}(f)=\calS_{t}
for all
t\in\calT
.

Financial examples

describes the price process of
m\geq 2
financial assets. The state space is therefore
\calX=\R^{m}_{+}
. Moreover, consider payoff functions of the form

\varphi(t,x)=e^{-rt}(\alpha(x)-\kappa)^{+},

(2.9)

where

is the

of the option, and

, and the homeomorphism

. See Theorem 4.1 in
[

]
for a complete statement.

.

Consider a

, which is obtained by choosing

3
Relaxed stopping rules

To find an optimal boundary with stochastic gradient techniques as in
Section

5
, the optimal stopping problem needs to be relaxed. Indeed, the map
f\mapsto\tau_{f}(\omega)
is piecewise constant for
\Q
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
as the hitting time of
f
, taking values in the discrete set
\calT
, is typically unchanged when the stopping boundary varies slightly. This in turn leads to severe vanishing gradient problems. We overcome this difficulty by approximating
\tau_{f}(\omega)
by a sequence of relaxed stopping rules based on
fuzzy regions
parametrized by
\varepsilon>0
,

\frakB_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frakS_{t}(f-\varepsilon)\setminus\frakS_{t}(f)=\{x\in\calX\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ d(t,x;f)\in(0,\varepsilon]\},

(3.1)

where
d\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\calX\times\frakF\to[0,\infty]
is the distance from
x
to
\frakS_{t}(f)
in the coordinate system
(\Xi,\alpha)
,

d(t,x;f)=(f(t,\Xi(x))-\alpha(x))^{+},\quad(t,x,f)\in\calT\times\calX\times\frakF,

(3.2)

and an illustration is given in the left panel of
Fig.

3
.

We continue by first defining the general relaxed stopping rules, and then the specific one induced by the fuzzy region. Let
\calP(\calT)
be the set of probability measures on
\calT
and
\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)
denotes the set of
\calP(\calT)-
valued random variables

\mu(\omega)=\sum_{t\in\calT}P_{t}(\omega)\delta_{t},\quad\omega\in\Omega,\qquad(\delta_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\text{Dirac measure at $t$})

such that the process
(P_{t})_{t\in\calT}
is
\F
-adapted.
In this context,
\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)
is the set of
relaxed
(or

)
\F

\calT

with
\delta_{\tau}\in\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)

\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)
contains all
\F
-stopping times. Indeed, this procedure is effectively a

of the stopping times as every relaxed stopping rule is a random convex combination of the Dirac measures
(\delta_{t})_{t\in\calT}
.
We can now define the value functional

v(\mu)=\E^{\Q}\left[\int_{\calT}\varphi(t,X_{t})\mu(dt)\right],\quad\mu\in\underline{\vartheta}(\calT).

(3.3)

The

amounts to maximizing

]

]

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be the value of the classical optimal stopping problem (

(3.4)

Moreover, any maximizer

of

is a stopping time, i.e.
\mu^{\diamond}=\delta_{\tau^{\diamond}}
for some

Proof.

See

We next construct a relaxed stopping rule from a given boundary
f\in\frakF
and a fuzziness parameter
\varepsilon>0
. Set
\calT_{t-}=\calT\cap\ [0,t)\
for

and
\calT_{0-}=\varnothing
, and recursively define the

representing stopping probabilities by,

\displaystyle P^{\varepsilon}_{f,t}=\underbrace{p_{f,t}^{\varepsilon}}_{\text{stop at $t$}}\ \ \underbrace{\Big(1-\sum_{s\in\calT_{t-}}P_{f,s}^{\varepsilon}\Big)}_{\text{continue until $t$}},\qquad p_{f,t}^{\varepsilon}=(\chi^{\varepsilon}\circ d)(t,X_{t};f),

(3.5)

with the relaxed phase indicator function
\chi^{\varepsilon}(\delta)=(1-\delta/\varepsilon)^{+}\wedge 1
.
Specifically,
\chi^{\varepsilon}
is equal to one in the stopping region
\frakS_{t}(f)
, it is linearly decreasing in the fuzzy region
\frakB_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)
, and is zero otherwise. The right panel of
Fig.

displays the composition
\chi^{\varepsilon}\circ d
in the space
\Xi(\calX)\times\alpha(\calX)
.
We can now define the relaxed stopping rule associated to

as

\mu^{\varepsilon}_{f}(\omega)=\sum_{t\in\calT}P^{\varepsilon}_{f,t}(\omega)\ \delta_{t}.

(3.6)

in the coordinates

\frakS_{t}(f)

\chi^{\varepsilon}(\delta)=(1-\delta/\varepsilon)^{+}\wedge 1

Using (

), we directly verify that
\mu_{f}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)(\calT)=1
. Additionally, as for each

,
P^{\varepsilon}_{f,t}
depends only on

. Hence,

is a relaxed

To complete the approximation, in

we prove the following convergence which was already argued in

v(\mu_{f}^{\varepsilon})\longrightarrow v(\tau_{f})\quad\text{as }\ \varepsilon\downarrow 0.

(3.7)

That is, the value of the relaxed stopping rule

converges to the expected reward from the hitting time of

when the fuzzy parameter

decreases to zero.

The above result is a byproduct of the continuity of

, proved in the next section.
Towards our main results
Theorems

4.1
and
4.2
, we start with a
continuity lemma for relaxed stopping rules with respect to
the
total variation distance
of probability measures,

\textnormal{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t\in\calT}|\mu(\{t\})-\mu^{\prime}(\{t\})|\in[0,1],\qquad\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\calP(\calT).

Note that when
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)
, then
\textnormal{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is the random variable
\omega\mapsto\textnormal{TV}(\mu(\omega),\mu^{\prime}(\omega))
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exists
C\in(0,\infty)
depending on
\varphi
such that

|v(\mu)-v(\mu^{\prime})|\leq C\ \lVert\textnormal{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Q)},\quad\forall\ \mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\underline{\vartheta}(\calT).

(3.8)

In particular,

|v(\tau)-v(\tau^{\prime})|\leq C\Q(\tau\neq\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}
for any stopping times
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\vartheta(\calT)
.

Proof.

Define
\Phi=\sum_{t\in\calT}|\varphi(t,X_{t})|
and
recall from Assumption
2.1
that
\varphi(t,\cdot)\in\calL_{a}
for some
a>0
. Since
\phi\in\calL_{a}
implies
\phi^{2}\in\calL_{2a}
, we obtain for some constant
C\in(0,\infty)
that

\E[\Phi^{2}]\leq|\calT|\sum_{t\in\calT}\E^{\Q}[\varphi(t,X_{t})^{2}]=|\calT|\sum_{t\in\calT}\underbrace{K_{0,t}\varphi^{2}(t,x_{0})}_{\in\ \calL_{2a}}\leq C\sum_{t\in\calT}[1+|x_{0}|^{2a}]<\infty.

Thus
\Phi\in L^{2}(\Q)
. Next, observe that

\displaystyle|v(\mu)-v(\mu^{\prime})|\leq\E^{\Q}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\varphi(t,X_{t})|\ |\mu-\mu^{\prime}|(dt)\right]\leq 2\E^{\Q}\left[\Phi\ \text{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\right].

As
\text{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq 1\in L^{2}(\Q)
, the result follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and setting
C=2\lVert\Phi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Q)}
. For the second claim, it suffices to observe that
\text{TV}(\delta_{\tau},\delta_{\tau^{\prime}})=\mathds{1}_{\{\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}\}}
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Set
\overline{\textnormal{TV}}_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lVert\textnormal{TV}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\lVert_{L^{2}(\Q)}
for relaxed stopping rules
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\underline{\vartheta}(\calT)
. Then,
Lemma

3.1
states that
\mu\mapsto v(\mu)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\overline{\textnormal{TV}}_{2}
.

First, we
state
a distributional assumption on the process
\alpha(X)=(\alpha(X_{t}))_{t\in\calT}
.

Assumption 4.1
.

For every
t\in\calT
, the conditional law of
\alpha(X_{t})
given
\Xi(X_{t})

is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure,
and admits a modulus of continuity
\rho_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
satisfying,

\Q(\alpha(X_{t})\in[a,a+\iota]\ \large{|}\ \Xi(X_{t}))\leq\rho_{t}(\iota),\hskip 23.49976pt\Q-a.s,\;\forall a,\iota\geq 0.

Example 3
.

Let us verify Assumption
4.1
for max-call options on
d=2
assets seen in
Example

, namely
\alpha(x)=x_{1}\vee x_{2}
and
\Xi(x)=\frac{x}{\alpha(x)}
. We also suppose that the law of
X_{t}=(X_{t}^{1},X_{t}^{2})
has no atoms and denote the joint density of
X_{t}
by
\phi_{t}
. If
\Xi(X_{t})\in\calE_{b}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(1,\xi_{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\xi_{2}\leq b\}
, then
\alpha(X_{t})=X_{t}^{1}
so that

\displaystyle\Q(\alpha(X_{t})\leq a\ \large{|}\ \Xi(X_{t})\in\calE_{b})=\Q(X_{t}^{1}\leq a\ \large{|}\ X_{t}^{2}\leq bX_{t}^{1})=\frac{1}{\Q(X_{t}^{2}\leq bX_{t}^{1})}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{bx_{1}}\phi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{2}dx_{1}.

Hence for all
b\in\R
such that
\Q(X_{t}^{2}\leq bX_{t}^{1})>0
, we have
\Q(\alpha(X_{t})\in[a,a+\iota]\ \large{|}\ \Xi(X_{t})\in\calE_{b})\leq\rho_{t}(\iota)
as claimed.

We are now ready to state our first continuity result. Throughout, we adopt the notation
\calK\subset\subset\calE
if
\calK
is a compact subset of
\calE
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
C\in(0,\infty)
be as in
Lemma

3.1
and fix
\delta>0
. Then,
under Assumptions
4.1
,
there exists a compact set
\calK\subset\subset\calE
such that for all
\iota>0
and

,

\lVert f-f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota\ \Longrightarrow\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{f^{\prime}})|\leq C\sqrt{\rho(\iota)+\delta},

(4.1)

where
\rho_{t}
is the modulus of continuity of

a\mapsto\Q(\alpha(X_{t})\in[0,a]\ |\ \Xi(X_{t}))
and

\rho(\iota)=\sum_{t\in\calT}\rho_{t}(\iota)
. If
\calE
is compact,
then

is uniformly continuous in the supremum distance.

Remark 4.1
.

If Assumption
4.1
holds and for all
t\in\calT
,
the density
\phi_{t}(x)dx\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Q(\alpha(X_{t})\in dx)
is bounded, then the cumulative distribution function of
\alpha(X_{t})
is Lipschitz continuous. In particular, its modulus of continuity satisfies
\rho_{t}(\iota)\leq\lVert\phi_{t}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\R)}\iota

, we can thus rewrite the right side of (

) as

\lVert f-f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota\;\Longrightarrow\;|v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{f^{\prime}})|\leq C\sqrt{c\ \iota+\delta}.

(4.2)

Figure 4
:
Illustration of
the symmetric difference between the stopping regions
\frakS_{t}(f)
and
\frakS_{t}(f^{\prime})
(purple region) contained in the interval

coincide in the red (stop) and blue (continue) region.

Proof.

(
Theorem

5.1
)
Step
{1}
.

Since
\calE=\Xi(\calX)
is locally compact (being the continuous image of a Euclidean space),
every finite Borel measure on it
is tight.
Hence, there exists
\calK\subset\subset\calE
such that

\Q(\Omega_{\calK})\geq 1-\delta,\quad\Omega_{\calK}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\omega\in\Omega\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \Xi(X_{t}(\omega))\in\calK\;\ \forall t\in\calT\}.

(4.3)

Step
{2}
.
Fix

First, note that the event

4.1
Relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric and second continuity result

The continuity result in
Theorem

4.1
only applies when boundaries are close in the
L^{\infty}
sense.
Although reasonable for continuous boundaries, this condition is typically too restrictive
for semicontinuous boundaries. We therefore introduce a weaker topology
induced by the following metric.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\R
, the
relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric
is defined as

\frakm(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frakm_{d}(f,g)+\frakm_{d}(g,f)
,
where

\frakm_{d}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{\psi\in\Psi}\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\psi(\xi))-g(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big],

and
\Psi
is the set of all endofunctions
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\calE
.
For
f,g\in\frakF
, we also write

\frakm(f,g)=\max_{t\in\calT}\frakm(f(t,\cdot),g(t,\cdot)).

(4.8)

We denote the relaxed
L^{\infty}
distance on any compact subset

\calK
by
\frakm_{\calK}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frakm(f\mathds{1}_{\calK},g\mathds{1}_{\calK})
.

We note that the epigraph,
\text{epi}(f)
, of a lower semi-continuous function
f

is closed and for any
\xi\in\calE
,
\inf_{\psi\in\Psi}\big[(f(\psi(\xi))-g(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big]

is equal to the distance from
(\xi,g(\xi))
to
\text{epi}(f)
.

The relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric comes as a natural notion for semicontinuous functions
and is closely related to
inf convolutions
discussed in
Section

5.2
.
The transformations in
\Psi
echo the reparametrization maps appearing in the
Skorokhod
J1
distance for càdlàg processes.
A notable difference is that unlike
the Skorokhod metric,
\psi
needs not be invertible. Additionally,
the
“directed”
function

\frakm_{d}
is an asymmetric metric studied in
[
25
]

as we prove below.

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\frakm_{d}
is an
asymmetric metric, i.e., it has the following properties:

•

\frakm_{d}(f,g)=0
if and only if
f\leq g
on
\calE
.

•

(triangle inequality)

\frakm_{d}(f,h)\leq\frakm_{d}(f,g)+\frakm_{d}(g,h)
, for any
f,g,h\in\frakF
.

Consequently,
\frakm
is a metric on
\frakF
and also dominated by the supremum distance.

Proof.

First statement follows directly from the definition.
Fix
f,g,h\in\frakF
. For
\psi,\phi\in\Psi
, set
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\phi\circ\psi
.
Then,
\varphi\in\Psi
and we have

\displaystyle\frakm_{d}(f,h)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\varphi(\xi))-h(\xi))^{+}+|\varphi(\xi)-\xi|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\phi(\psi(\xi))-g(\psi(\xi)))^{+}+(g(\psi(\xi))-h(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|+|\phi(\psi(\xi))-\psi(\xi)|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\phi(\psi(\xi))-g(\psi(\xi)))^{+}+|\phi(\psi(\xi))-\psi(\xi)|\big]+\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\big[(g(\psi(\xi))-h(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\phi(\xi^{\prime}))-g(\xi^{\prime}))^{+}+|\phi(\xi^{\prime})-\xi^{\prime}|\big]+\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\big[(g(\psi(\xi))-h(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big].

Since above holds for every
\psi,\phi\in\Psi
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\frakm_{d}(f,h)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\phi\in\Psi}\ \sup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\calE}\ \big[(f(\phi(\xi^{\prime}))-g(\xi^{\prime}))^{+}+|\phi(\xi^{\prime})-\xi^{\prime}|\big]+\inf_{\psi\in\Psi}\sup_{\xi\in\calE}\ \big[(g(\psi(\xi))-h(\xi))^{+}+|\psi(\xi)-\xi|\big]

\displaystyle=\frakm_{d}(f,g)+\frakm_{d}(g,h).

Then, the symmetrized function
\frakm
is a metric.
∎

Next, we adapt
Theorem

4.1
to obtain a continuity type result of

f\mapsto v(\tau_{f})
with respect to the relaxed
L^{\infty}
metric.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
C\in(0,\infty)
be as in
Lemma

3.1
and fix
\delta>0
.
Then, under Assumptions
4.1
,
there exists

\calK\subset\subset\calE
such that for all

f\in\frakF
and a
sequence
f_{n}\leq f
in
\frakF
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frakm_{\calK}(f,f_{n})=0\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{f_{n}})|\leq C\sqrt{\delta}.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, there exists
\calK\subset\subset\calE
such that

\Q(\Omega_{\calK})\geq 1-\delta,\quad\Omega_{\calK}=\{\omega\in\Omega\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ \Xi(X_{t}(\omega))\in\calK\;\ \forall t\in\calT\}.

Consider a sequence
f_{n}\leq f
in
\frakF
, such that

\frakm_{\calK}(f,f_{n})
converges to zero. Then, there are
\psi_{n}\in\Psi
satisfying,

c_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\xi\in\calK}\ \big[(f(\psi_{n}(\xi))-f_{n}(\xi))^{+}+|\psi_{n}(\xi)-\xi|\big]\longrightarrow 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Since by (
2.7
)
f
is lower semi-continuous and
c_{n}
tends to zero, for every
\xi\in\calK
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}f(\xi)-f_{n}(\xi)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ (f(\xi)-f(\psi_{n}(\xi)))+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ (f(\psi_{n}(\xi))-f_{n}(\xi))

\displaystyle\leq f(\xi)-\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(\psi_{n}(\xi))+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ (f(\psi_{n}(\xi))-f_{n}(\xi))^{+}\leq 0.

(4.9)

Following the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\{\tau_{f}\neq\tau_{f_{n}}\}\subseteq\Omega_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{t\in\calT}\big\{X_{t}\in\frakS_{t}(f)\triangle\frakS_{t}(f_{n})\big\}=\bigcup_{t\in\calT}\big\{f_{n}(t,\Xi(X_{t}))\leq\alpha(X_{t})<f(t,\Xi(X_{t}))\big\}.

Set
\chi_{n}
to be the characteristic function of the set

\Omega_{n}\cap\Omega_{\calK}
.
In view of (
4.9
),
\chi_{n}
converges to zero.

Then, from the bounded convergence theorem, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \Q(\Omega_{n}\cap\Omega_{\calK})=0.

Following the same rationale as in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{f_{n}})|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ C\left[\Q(\Omega_{n}\cap\Omega_{\calK})+\delta\right]^{1/2}=C\sqrt{\delta}.

∎

5
Application to neural stopping boundaries

In this section, we apply the continuity results to prove the convergence
of the
Neural Optimal Stopping Boundary method
developed in

[
30
]
. We outline the algorithm for the convenience of the reader.
Under a suitable coordinate system
(\alpha,\Xi)
as in Assumption
2.2
, the algorithm looks for a
neural stopping boundary
, namely a feedforward neural network
g^{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\calE\to\R_{+}
in a (sufficiently large) hypothesis space
\frakN
such that

v(\tau_{g^{\theta}})\approx\sup_{f\in\frakF}v(\tau_{f})\ =v^{\diamond}.

Additional details on the architecture and size of the neural networks
for this problem are given in
[
30
]
.
The parameter vector
\theta
is thereafter trained using stochastic gradient ascent. As explained at the beginning of
Section

3
, the stopping rule needs to be relaxed to avoid vanishing gradient issues.
For some fixed fuzzy region width
\varepsilon>0
, we therefore
aim to

\text{maximize}\ \ v(\mu_{g}^{\varepsilon})=\E^{\Q}\left[\int_{\calT}\varphi(t,X_{t})\mu_{g}^{\varepsilon}(dt)\right]\qquad\text{over}\ g\in\frakN,

(5.1)

with the relaxed stopping rule
\mu_{g}^{\varepsilon}
given in (
3.6
).
The vanishing gradient problem is henceforth resolved and owing to well-known convergence results in stochastic optimization
[
31
,
32
]
, we can find for every
\gamma>0
an

\gamma-
maximizer

g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\frakN
, that is

v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq\sup_{g\in\frakN}\ v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g})-\gamma.

(5.2)

Finally, the value of the optimal stopping problem is computed using the strict stopping rule
\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}}
instead of
\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}}
. The method is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.
For further details, see Appendix C in
[
30
]
. The goal of this section is to show that
v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})
can be made arbitrarily close to the optimal value
v^{\diamond}
when
\varepsilon,\gamma
are small enough.

Remark 5.1
.

When computing the value associated to the trained stopping rule, say
\tau_{g}
, an additional inaccuracy comes from the Monte Carlo simulations of the process; see step III in
Algorithm

1
. There is nevertheless a clear control of this error. Indeed, it is classical that

|v(\tau_{g})-\hat{v}_{J}(\tau_{g})|=\calO(J^{-1/2}),

when the random reward
\varphi(\tau_{g},X_{\tau_{g}})
is square integrable. Under Assumption
4.1
, this is indeed the case (see also the proof of
Lemma

3.1
).

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
30
]
, a two-sided fuzzy region is considered, namely

\{x\in\calX\ \mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ |d(t,x;f)|<\varepsilon\},

where
d(t,\cdot;f)
is the
signed distance function
to
\partial\frakS_{t}(f)
.
Although the two representations are equivalent, one-sided fuzzy regions are more practical towards a convergence analysis.

5.1
Convergence analysis: continuous boundaries

We first outline our strategy to prove convergence of the Neural Optimal Stopping Boundary method when the optimal stopping boundary
f^{\diamond}
is continuous. First, the hypothesis space
\frakN
(neural networks in this case) must satisfy a universal approximation property; see Assumption
5.1
below. Thus, there exists
g^{\diamond}\in\frakN
arbitrarily close to
f^{\diamond}
in compact sets. We can therefore invoke our first continuity result (
Theorem

4.1
) to ensure that the values of the stopping strategies
\tau_{f^{\diamond}}
,
\tau_{g^{\diamond}}
are close as well. Next, we relax the problem and use
Corollary

4.1
to guarantee that
v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\diamond}})\approx v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})
for small fuzzy parameter
\varepsilon
. We then train the neural boundary to construct
g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\frakN
such that
v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\diamond}})\approx v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})
and finally conclude that
v(\mu_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}}^{\varepsilon})\approx v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})
in view of
Corollary

4.1
. The above steps can be summarized as follows:

\displaystyle v^{\diamond}

\displaystyle=v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}})

\displaystyle\approx v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\ \textnormal{approximation and continuity (Assumption \ref{asm:UAP} and \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{thm:unifcontV})}

\displaystyle\approx v(\mu_{g^{\diamond}}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\ \textnormal{relaxation (\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{cor:relaxUnif})}

\displaystyle\approx v(\mu_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\ \textnormal{stochastic optimization \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{Number}{RobbinsMonro,RobbinsSiegmund}{}{}]}}

\displaystyle\approx v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}}).

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\ \textnormal{relaxation (\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{cor:relaxUnif})}

We start with the approximation of
f^{\diamond}
. Let
\frakF_{c}=\{f\in\frakF\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f(t,\cdot)\in\calC(\calE)\;\forall t\in\calT\}
denotes the set of continuous boundaries and consider a hypothesis space
\frakN\subset\frakF_{c}
satisfying the following
universal approximation property
.

Assumption 5.1
.

For all

f\in\frakF_{c}
, compact set
\calK\subset\subset\calE
and
\iota>0
, there exists
g\in\frakN
such that

\lVert f-g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota.

(5.3)

We refer to elements in a family
\frakN\subset\frakF_{c}
such that
Assumption
5.1
holds as
universal approximators
. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem gives an example of universal approximators, namely the polynomials. This class, however, is numerically unsuitable when the Hausdorff dimension of
\calE
is large. In our case,
\frakN
is evidently the family of all feedforward neural networks
g^{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calT\times\ \calE\to\R_{+}
with finite-dimensional parameter vector
\theta
. Assumption
5.1
follows classically from the
Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT) for neural networks
[
6
,
16
]
.

In this section, we assume that
f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
is continuous in
\xi
, i.e.
f^{\diamond}\in\frakF_{c}
. We can therefore directly use
f^{\diamond}
in Assumption
5.1
. Together with the results from
Section

4
, the next convergence theorem follows naturally.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider
\frakN\subset\frakF_{c}
satisfying Assumption
5.1

and suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds.
Further assume that the
optimal stopping boundary is continuous, i.e.,
f^{\diamond}\in\frakF_{c}
.
Then, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon,\gamma>0
such that any
g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\frakN
with the property

v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq\sup_{g\in\frakN}\ v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g})-\gamma

also satisfies,

v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq v^{\diamond}-\delta=v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}})-\delta=\sup_{f\in\frakF}v(\tau_{f})-\delta.

Proof.

Step
{1}
.
Let
\delta^{\prime}>0
be arbitrary. From
Theorem

4.1
, there exist
\delta^{\prime\prime}>0
,
\calK\subseteq\calE
compact, and
\iota>0
such that

\lVert f-f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota\ \Longrightarrow\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{f^{\prime}})|\leq C\sqrt{\rho(\iota)+\delta^{\prime\prime}}\leq\delta^{\prime}.

(5.4)

Invoking Assumption
5.1
, there exists
g^{\diamond}\in\frakN
such that

\lVert f^{\diamond}-g^{\diamond}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota.

We conclude from
Theorem

4.1
that
v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})\geq v^{\diamond}-\delta^{\prime}
.

Step
{2}
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, set

v^{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{g\in\frakN}v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g})
.
For any
f\in\frakF
,
Corollary

4.1
implies that

v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{f})\to v(\tau_{f})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
In particular, taking
f=g^{\diamond}
from Step 1 yields

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}v^{\varepsilon}\geq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}v(\mu_{g^{\diamond}}^{\varepsilon})=v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}}).

Thus, one can pick
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
such that
v^{\varepsilon}\geq v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})-\delta^{\prime}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\bar{\varepsilon})
.

Step
{3}
.
Using again
Corollary

4.1
, there exists
\ \varepsilon\in(0,\bar{\varepsilon})
such that for all
f\in\frakF
,

|v(\tau_{f})-v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{f})|\leq C\sqrt{\rho(\varepsilon)}\leq\delta^{\prime}.

(5.5)

Consequently, if
g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\frakN
is a
\gamma-
maximizer of
v^{\varepsilon}
, then

v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})-\delta^{\prime}\geq v^{\varepsilon}-\gamma-\delta^{\prime}.

Step
{4}
.

Collecting the above inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq v^{\varepsilon}-\gamma-\delta^{\prime}\geq v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})-\gamma-2\delta^{\prime}\geq v^{\diamond}-\gamma-3\delta^{\prime}.

We can therefore set
\delta^{\prime}=\gamma=\frac{\delta}{4}
.
∎

5.2
Convergence analysis: semicontinuous boundaries

This section consider optimal boundaries
f^{\diamond}=f^{\diamond}(t,\xi)
that are
only not necessarily continuous and are only
lower continuous in
\xi
. To project
f^{\diamond}
onto
\frakN
, we first carefully construct a sequence of
continuous
boundaries converging to
f^{\diamond}
. These continuous boundaries can thereafter be approximated by elements in
\frakN
. To construct such sequence, we utilize the notion of
inf/sup convolution
, which is central in the theory of viscosity solutions; see
[
10
, Section V.5]
and
[
21
]
.

Let
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\R_{+}
be a lower semicontinuous function and define its
inf convolution
as

h_{\delta}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{\xi^{\prime}\in\calE}\left[h(\xi^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|^{2}\right],\quad\delta>0.

(5.6)

It is easily seen that
h_{\delta}
is semiconcave, hence continuous.
For
f\in\frakF
, the inf convolution of
f
is understood as
f_{\delta}(t,\cdot)=f(t,\cdot)_{\delta}
for each
t\in\calT
. The next proposition shows that
f_{\delta}\uparrow f
as
\delta\to 0
; see
Fig.

5
. In particular, the hitting time
\tau_{f_{\delta}}
of
\frakS(f_{\delta})
increases to
\tau_{f}
.

Figure 5
:
Inf convolution of
f(t,\cdot)
for some
t\in\calT
. The monotonicity of
\delta\to f_{\delta}
implies that the hitting time
\tau_{f_{\delta}}
increases to
\tau_{f}
(pathwise) as
\delta\downarrow 0
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\R_{+}
be lower semicontinuous and
h_{\delta}
be its inf convolution with parameter
\delta
as in (
5.6
).
Then
h_{\delta}\uparrow h
pointwise.

Proof.

It is clear that
h_{\delta}
increases with
\delta
, hence any sequence
(h_{\delta_{n}})
with
\delta_{l}\downarrow 0
is non-decreasing.
Let
\xi_{n}
be a
\delta_{n}-
minimizer of
h_{\delta_{n}}(\xi)
, i.e.
h(\xi_{n})+\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}|\xi-\xi_{n}|^{2}\leq h_{\delta_{n}}(\xi)+\delta_{n}
. Then,

h(\xi)\geq h_{\delta_{n}}(\xi)\geq h(\xi_{n})+\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}|\xi-\xi_{n}|^{2}-\delta_{n}\geq\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}|\xi-\xi_{n}|^{2}-\delta_{n},

which implies that
|\xi-\xi_{n}|^{2}\leq\delta_{n}(\delta_{n}+h(\xi))
. Therefore
\xi_{n}\to\xi
, and the lower semicontinuity of
h
gives

h(\xi)\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}h(\xi_{n})\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}\left[h(\xi_{n})+\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}|\xi-\xi_{n}|^{2}\right]\leq\liminf_{l\to\infty}\left[h_{\delta_{n}}(\xi)+\delta_{n}\right]\leq h(\xi).

∎

More can be said when restricting l.s.c. boundaries to a compact set. Accordingly, we define

h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{\xi^{\prime}\in\calK}\left[h(\xi^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|^{2}\right],\quad\delta>0,\quad\xi\in\calK\subset\subset\calE.

(5.7)

The inf convolution of
f\in\frakF
in
\calK
is understood as
f_{\delta}^{\calK}(t,\cdot)=f(t,\cdot)_{\delta}^{\calK}
for each
t\in\calT
.
Since
\xi^{\prime}\mapsto h(\xi^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|^{2}
is also l.s.c., it attains its minimum on
\calK
.
This leads us to the following corollary. Throughout, we write
B_{r}(\xi)
for the closed ball of radius
r\geq 0
centered at
\xi\in\calE
.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\calK\subset\subset\calE
and a lower semicontinuous function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\R_{+}
such that
c_{\calK}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calK)}<\infty
. Then
\forall\ \delta>0
,
\xi\in\calK
, any minimizer achieving
h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)
in (
5.7
) is contained in
B_{\sqrt{\delta c_{\calK}}}(\xi)
.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{\delta}\in\calK
be any minimizer in (
5.7
), i.e.
h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)=h(\xi_{\delta})+\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi_{\delta}-\xi|^{2}
. Then

c_{\calK}\geq h(\xi)\geq h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)=h(\xi_{\delta})+\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi_{\delta}-\xi|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi_{\delta}-\xi|^{2}.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

When the stopping region is the hypograph of the optimal boundary (
\eta=-1
in
2.2
) one can employ
sup convolutions
instead, that is

h^{\delta}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\ \calE}\left[h(\xi^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\delta}|\xi^{\prime}-\xi|^{2}\right]\geq h(\xi),\quad\delta>0.

We may also define the restriction
h^{\delta}_{\calK}
to some compact set
\calK\subset\subset\calE
. This case is simpler since u.s.c functions are automatically bounded above in compact sets.

The next lemma states that inf convolutions approximate lower semicontinuous functions arbitrarily well with respect to the relaxed
L^{\infty}
distance seen in
Definition

4.1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\calK
be a compact subset of
\calE
,

h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calE\to\R
be bounded and lower semi-continuous,
and
h_{\delta}^{\calK}
be as in (
5.7
).
Then, for any
\iota>0
, there exists
\delta_{\iota,\calK}>0
such that

\frakm_{\calK}(h,h_{\delta}^{\calK})\leq\iota,\qquad\forall\ \delta\in(0,\delta_{\iota,\calK}].

Proof.

Fix
\iota>0
and
\calK\subset\subset\calE
.
Set
c_{\calK}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calK)}
.
For
\xi\in\calK
, choose
\psi_{\delta}(\xi)
satisfying,

h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)=h(\psi_{\delta}(\xi))+\frac{1}{\delta}|\psi_{\delta}(\xi)-\xi|^{2}.

Then,
c_{\calK}\geq h(\xi)\geq h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)\geq h(\psi_{\delta}(\xi))\geq-c_{\calK}
,
or equivalently,
|h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)|\leq c_{\calK}
. Additionaly,

|\psi_{\delta}(\xi)-\xi|^{2}\leq(h(\xi)-h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi))\delta\leq 2c_{\calK}\delta.

Moreover, since
h(\psi_{\delta}(\xi))\leq h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi)\leq h(\xi)
,

\displaystyle\frakm_{\calK}(h,h_{\delta}^{\calK})\leq\sup_{\xi\in\calK}\left[(h(\psi_{\delta}(\xi))-h_{\delta}^{\calK}(\xi))^{+}+|\psi_{\delta}(\xi)-\xi|\right]=\sup_{\xi\in\calK}|\psi_{\delta}(\xi)-\xi|\leq(2c_{\calK}\delta)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.

We now set
\delta_{\iota,\calK}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\iota^{2}/(2c_{\calK})
to complete the proof.
∎

The next approximation result follows immediately.

Theorem 5.2
.

(Universal approximation of lower semicontinuous functions).

Let
\frakN\subseteq\frakF_{c}
be a hypothesis space satisfying Assumption
5.1
.
Then, for all
\iota>0
,
\calK\subset\subset\calE

and
f\in\frakF
that is bounded in
\calK\times\calT
,
there exists
g\in\frakN
such that
\frakm_{\calK}(f,g)\leq\iota.

Proof.

Fix
\iota>0
and
\calK\subset\subset\calE
.
In view of
Lemma

5.1
,

\frakm_{\calK}(f,f_{\delta}^{\calK})\leq\iota/2

for some
\delta>0
. Moreover, as
f_{\delta}^{\calK}(t,\xi)
is continuous in
\xi
, we can invoke Assumption
5.1
giving the existence of
g\in\frakN\subseteq\frakF_{c}
such that

\lVert f_{\delta}^{\calK}-g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\leq\iota/2
.
Since
\frakm
is dominated by the supremum norm,
the triangular inequality yields

\frakm_{\calK}(f,g)\leq\frakm_{\calK}(f,f_{\delta}^{\calK})+\frakm_{\calK}(f_{\delta}^{\calK},g)\leq\frac{1}{2}\iota+\lVert f_{\delta}^{\calK}-g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\calT\times\calK)}\\
\leq\iota.

∎

Finally, we obtain the following convergence theorem for lower semicontinuous boundaries.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\frakN\subset\frakF_{c}
be a family of universal approximators,
and
v^{\diamond}
be the value function defined in (
2.4
).
Under Assumption
4.1
, for every
\delta>0
,
there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta),\gamma=\gamma(\delta)>0
such that
any function
g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}\in\frakN

with the property

v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq\sup_{g\in\frakN}\ v(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{g})-\gamma

also satisfies,

v(\tau_{g^{\varepsilon,\gamma}})\geq v^{\diamond}-\delta=v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}})-\delta=\sup_{f\in\frakF}v(\tau_{f})-\delta.

Proof.

Let
\delta^{\prime}>0
be arbitrary. In view of
Theorem

4.2
,
there are
\delta^{\prime\prime}>0
,
\calK\subset\subset\calE
,
\iota>0
such that

\frakm_{\calK}(f,g)\leq\iota\ \Longrightarrow\ |v(\tau_{f})-v(\tau_{g})|\leq C\sqrt{\rho(\iota)+\delta^{\prime\prime}}\leq\delta^{\prime},\quad\forall f,g\in\frakF.

(5.8)

As the optimal boundary
f^{\diamond}\in\frakF
is locally bounded, we apply

Theorem

5.2
to construct
g^{\diamond}\in\frakN
such that

\frakm_{\calK}(f^{\diamond},g^{\diamond})\leq\iota
.
We conclude from (
5.8
) that
v(\tau_{g^{\diamond}})\geq v(\tau_{f^{\diamond}})-\delta^{\prime}=v^{\diamond}-\delta^{\prime}
. As
g^{\diamond}
is
continuous, we can follow the
Steps 2, 3, and 4 in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
.
∎

6
Conclusion

We analyze hitting times of boundaries arising in optimal stopping.
After introducing relaxed stopping rules and establishing preliminary results, the continuity of the value functional
f\mapsto v(\tau_{f})
is shown with respect to the topology of uniform convergence and the novel relaxed
L^{\infty}
distance.
The main results are later applied to study the convergence of the neural optimal stopping boundary method
[
30
]
. When the optimal stopping boundary is continuous, we can directly invoke the universal approximation theorem of neural networks and obtain a convergence theorem. When the optimal stopping boundary is strictly semicontinuous, inf/sup convolutions are combined with the relaxed
L^{\infty}
distance, leading to a more general result.

A natural extension of the present work would be to show similar continuity and relaxation results for optimal stopping problems in continuous time. In particular, it would be worth investigating the convergence of the neural optimal stopping method for
American
options, namely when
\calT=[0,T]
.
