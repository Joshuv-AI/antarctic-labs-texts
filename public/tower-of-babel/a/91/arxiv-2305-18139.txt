1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider a class of stochastic differential equations (abbreviated as SDEs), whose drift coefficients belong to some Besov spaces with negative orders, driven by some symmetric non-degenerate
\alpha
-stable processes. In the following, we first introduce the motivation of this paper in subsection
1.1
, and state concerned problems in subsection
1.2
. Then we briefly overview the relevant literature concerning well-posedness and Euler-Maruyama approximations for SDEs with irregular or singular coefficients in subsection
1.3
, and the contributions of this paper are also listed in the same subsection. Finally, we show our main results in subsection
1.4
.

1.1.
Motivation

In recent decades, there has been increasing theoretical interest in the context of regularization by noise, which is a phenomenon where stochastic differential equations behave quite differently from their deterministic counterparts, nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs for short), when the coefficient
b
is not Lipschitz. Specifically, well-posedness for ODEs may not hold if the coefficient is not Lipschitz. However, Veretennikov
[
Ve79
]
and Krylov and Röckner
[
KR05
]
obtained that irregular additive Brownian noise can make SDEs well-posed even when the coefficients are bounded or
L^{p}
-integrable. Building on these results, there have been numerous developments, such as a series of works by Zhang and et al.
[
Zh05
,
Zh11
,
XXZZ20
]
, for cases involving multiplicative noise. We also refer to the introduction of
[
HZ23
]
for more recent references concerning regularization by noise.

Apart from motivations arising from regularization by noise, SDEs with distributional drift often model random irregular media and exhibit different behaviors. For example, Brox diffusion, as studied in
[
HLM17
]
, superdiffusive phenomena
[
CHT22
,
CMOW23
]
, random directed polymers
[
DD16
]
, and self-attracting Brownian motion in a random medium
[
CC18
]
. For further references on the motivations for studying SDEs with distributional drifts, see
[
AGI22
]
.

1.2.
Problem statement

Nowadays, researchers have increasingly focused on SDEs driven by
\alpha
-stable processes, which are a typical class of heavy-tailed distributions. These jump processes can model collisions in certain physical systems, such as those described by the Boltzmann equation (see e.g.
[
Ta78
]
), and appear in geoscience models like the Surface Quasi-Geostrophic system (see e.g.
[
HRZ24
, Theorem 6.4]
). Moreover, heavy-tailed distributions are useful in social and economic fields, such as Pareto distributions modeling income (see e.g.
[
Ar15
]
) and applications in insurance and finance (see
[
BG87
]
for example).

In this paper, we consider the well-posedness and the Euler-Maruyama approximation of the following SDE in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d\geqslant 1
):

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma(t,X_{t-}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t}^{(\alpha)},\ \ X_{0}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where

belongs to
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
with some

(here,

are Besov spaces, see

below), and

is a

Here

is a

with the Lévy measure given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\nu^{(\alpha)}(A)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}\frac{1_{A}(r\theta)\Sigma({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\theta)}{r^{1+\alpha}}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,\quad A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

where

(H

)
There is a constant

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1}|\xi|\leqslant|\sigma(t,x)\xi|\leqslant{c}_{0}|\xi|,\ \ \hbox{and}\ \ |\nabla_{x}\sigma(t,x)|\leqslant{c}_{0}.

The Lévy measure is

1.2.1.
SDEs and the related PDEs

Given
T>0
, let
{\mathbf{D}}:=D([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the space of all c
\rm\grave{a}
dl
\rm\grave{a}
g (right continuous with left limits) functions from
[0,T]
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

{\mathbf{D}}
by
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{D}})
. We let the map
\pi_{t}:{\mathbf{D}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the coordinate process defined by

\displaystyle\pi_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t},\ \ \forall t\in[0,T],\,\omega\in{\mathbf{D}}.

Define the natural filtration by

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}:=\sigma(\pi_{u},0\leqslant u\leqslant t).

It is well-known that when the drift term
b
is a good enough function, the infinitesimal generator of the solution
X_{t}
is given by
{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma,b}:={\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}+b\cdot\nabla
,
where

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}f(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(f(x+\sigma(t,x)z)-f(x)-\sigma(t,x)z\cdot\nabla f(x))\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

Moreover, the weak solution of SDE (
1.1
) on
[0,T]
is equivalent to the martingale solution related to the operator
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma,b}
. That is the distribution of the process
X
,
{\mathbb{P}}\circ X^{-1}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbf{D}})
, satisfies that for any

\displaystyle M_{t}:=g(t,\pi_{t})-g(0,\pi_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{s}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma,b})g(s,\pi_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\ \ 0\leqslant t\leqslant T,

is a

b
is some distribution rather than a real function, the drift term

is not well-defined due to the singularity of

, which causes that the domain of the operator
(\partial_{s}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma,b}):L^{\infty}_{T}C({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to L^{\infty}_{T}C({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

with
((\partial_{s}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma,b})^{-1}f,f)
. More precisely, we consider the following backward parabolic PDE

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\Big(}\partial_{t}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}+b\cdot\nabla{\Big)}u=g,\quad u(T)=0,

where
b,g\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty})
with some
\beta\in(0,(\alpha-1)/2)
. Formally, based on the Schauders estimate,
u=(\partial_{s}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma,b})^{-1}g\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{B}}^{\alpha-\beta}_{\infty,\infty})
is a Hölder continuous function in the space variable

is to keep

Now we state the following definition of generalized martingale solutions.

(Generalized martingale solution)
.

Let

(i)

(ii)

For any

\displaystyle M_{t}:=u(t,\pi_{t})-u(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}f(r,\pi_{r}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

is a martingale under

with respect to the filtration

, where

is the unique solution of PDE (
1.5

The set of all the generalized martingale solutions is denoted by

Remark 1.2
.

This kind of generalized martingale problems is in the sense of Either and Kurtz
[

, Section 4.3, p.173]
. When

[

,
CC18
,
KP20

HZZZ24
]
, for
SDEs with singular drifts.

Therefore, it is clear that our first key step is to solve the nonlocal PDE (
1.5
). After establishing the well-posedness result for PDEs (see
Theorem 3.4
), we arrive at our first main result, the unique generalized martingale solution in the sense of
1.1
, which is presented in
Theorem 1.3
. Let us look back to the PDE (
1.5
). Actually, there have been some works concerning on the well-posedness and Schauder’s estimates of non-local PDE (
1.5
) such as
[
LZ22
,
SX23
]
, where the authors studied the problems by replacing
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}u
(see (

)) with

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}}^{(\alpha)}_{\kappa}u(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u(t,x+z)-u(t,x)-\nabla u(t,x)\cdot z)\kappa(t,x,z)\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

which is evidently different with our case. Particularly, in
[
LZ22
]
, considering
\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)=|z|^{-d-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z
where the spherical measure
\Sigma
is absolutely continuous to the Lebesgue measure, through the following change of variables:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}f(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}f(x+z)-f(x)-z\cdot\nabla f(x){\Big)}\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z}{|\sigma^{-1}(t,x)z|^{d+\alpha}|\det\sigma(t,x)|},\end{split}

the authors established Schauder’s estimates, where

\kappa(t,x,z):=|z|^{d+\alpha}/(|\sigma^{-1}(t,x)z|^{d+\alpha}|\det\sigma(t,x)|).

On the one hand, not all
\alpha
-stable Lévy measures can be calculated as above. For instance, the cylindrical
\alpha
-stable Lévy measure:

\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}{z}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z_{1})\cdots\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z_{i-1})\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z_{i}}{|z_{i}|^{1+\alpha}}\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z_{i+1})\cdots\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z_{d}),

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure at zero and

\Sigma({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{e_{i}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\theta),\ \ e_{i}=(0,..,1(i{\rm th}),...,0),

satisfies the condition
(ND)
, but the relation (
1.7
) does no longer hold in this situation. On the other hand, after obtaining a aprior estimates, before showing the well-posedness, we need to make sure that the diffusion term
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}u
is well-defined, since the natural domain of
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}
is
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha+\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon>0
) but the solution
u
of PDE (
1.5
) at most has the regularity
u\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha-\beta}
by the Schauder estimate. As a matter of fact, the same problem appears in
[
LZ22
]
. As we mentioned before, the authors in
[
LZ22
]
considered the nonlocal operator in the shape of
{\mathscr{A}}^{(\alpha)}_{\kappa}\approx\kappa\Delta^{\alpha/2}
, where
\Delta^{\alpha/2}
is the usual fractional Laplacian, and then the boundness of the linear operator
{\mathscr{A}}^{(\alpha)}_{\kappa}:{\mathbf{B}}^{\alpha-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}\to{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}
is given by

\displaystyle\|\kappa\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}}\lesssim\|\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}}\|\kappa\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\beta+\varepsilon}_{\infty,\infty}},\ \ \epsilon>0,

which can extend the domain of operator
{\mathscr{A}}^{(\alpha)}_{\kappa}
from
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha+\varepsilon}
to
{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha-\beta}
whereas

. However, it doesn’t work anymore for our operator

given by (

After a bit long trip in the mathematical theory of PDEs, we finally arrive at the area of SDEs which we truly care about (see

). Actually, each cylindrical

-stable Lévy measure mentioned above are associated to a purely jumped Lévy process

-stable processes, appears naturally in the study of

[

]
for example), in which we can see that the joint independence of

plays a vital role in the following

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X^{N,i}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq i}K(X^{N,i}_{t}-X^{N,j}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{i}_{t},

where

is the interaction kernel, and

is a family of independent

-stable processes, which stands for the random phenomenon like collisions between two particles.

1.2.2.
The Euler-Maruyama approximations

Our second aim is to investigate the Euler-Maruyama scheme for SDE (
1.1
) when
\sigma={\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}
and
b=b(x)\in{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}
with some
\beta\in(0,(\alpha-1)/2)
, where
{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}
denotes the identity
d\times d
-matrix. The first question is how to define the Euler-Maruyama scheme since
b(x)
makes no sense for any single point. To this end, we introduce the following modification:
Modifying
b(x)
in the sense of

\displaystyle b_{m}:=b*{\mathcal{K}}_{m},\quad m>0,

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}(x):=m^{d}{\mathcal{K}}(mx)
with some good kernel
{\mathcal{K}}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
satisfying
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathcal{K}}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=1
, we consider the following modified Euler’s scheme for SDE (
1.1
): for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(1.8)

\displaystyle X^{m,n}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b_{m}(X^{m,n}_{\phi_{n}(t)}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+L_{t}^{(\alpha)},\ \ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

where
\phi_{n}(t):=k/n
when
t\in[k/n,(k+1)/n)
with
k=0,1,2,....,n
.

Thanks to the stability estimates (see
Theorem 4.4
), a by-product in the well-posedness part, we only need to give a quantitative result for the difference between
X^{m,n}_{t}
and the
X^{m}_{t}
, where
X^{m}_{t}
is the solution of the following classical SDE:

\displaystyle X^{m}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b_{m}(X^{m}_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+L_{t}^{(\alpha)}.

The key technique used in this task is the so-called Itô-Tanaka trick, which has been widely applied in the literature to obtain quantitative estimates of the Euler approximation, like
[
TT90
,
MP91
,
Hol22
,
FJM24
,
SH24
]
. Let us take a brief look at this trick. Considering the function

, which solves the following PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{m}={\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{{\mathbb{I}}}u_{m},\quad u_{m}(0)=\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{b},

and using Itô’s formula to
s\to u(t-s,X^{m}_{s})
and
s\to u(t-s,X^{m,n}_{s})
respectively, one sees that

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X^{m,n}_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X^{m}_{t})|=

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\Big((b_{m}\cdot\nabla u(t-s))(X^{m,n}_{s})-(b_{m}\cdot\nabla u(t-s))(X^{m}_{s})\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(b_{m}(X^{m,n}_{\phi_{n}(s)})-b_{m}(X^{m,n}_{s})\right)\cdot\nabla u(t-s,X^{m,n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|b_{m}\cdot\nabla u(t-s)\|_{\infty}\|{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m,n}_{s})^{-1}-{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m}_{s})^{-1}\|_{\rm var}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle+\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{1}}{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u(t-s)\|_{\infty}|X^{m,n}_{\phi_{n}(s)}-X^{m,n}_{s}|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

where

is the total variation distance.
Since
\|\nabla u(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-1/\alpha}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
(see (
2.21
)), taking the supremum of
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=1
, we have that for
\alpha>1
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m,n}_{t})^{-1}-{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m}_{t})^{-1}\|_{\rm var}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m,n}_{s})^{-1}-{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m}_{s})^{-1}\|_{\rm var}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle+t^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{1}}(\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}n^{-1}+n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}),

which, by Gronwall’s inequality of Volterra’s type (see
[
MR3906348
, Theorem 3.2]
or
[
Zh10
, Lemma 2.2]
), derives that there are two constants
c_{0}=c_{0}(\|b_{m}\|_{\infty})>0
and
c_{1}=c_{1}(\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{1}})>0
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m,n}_{t})^{-1}-{\mathbb{P}}\circ(X^{m}_{t})^{-1}\|_{\rm var}\leqslant c_{1}{\rm e}^{c_{0}}t^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}

Noting that
\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}\lesssim m^{\beta}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}}
, one sees that in (
1.9
), the item
{\rm e}^{c_{0}}
gives a massive quantity like
\exp\{m^{\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha-1}}\}
(cf.
[
MR3906348
, Theorem 3.2]
). To chancel it we need to assume that the modifier parameter
m\sim(\ln n)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha\beta}}
, where
n
is the discretization parameter. Back to the distributional framework, here are main two questions:

i)

whether we can reduce the norm of
\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{1}}
to
\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}
;

ii)

whether we can obtain a polynomial-dependency of the modifier parameter

instead of
{\rm e}^{c_{0}}
in (

).

For the first question, a first answer has been given by Gyöngy and Krylov
[

]
that

converges in probability to
X^{m}
whereas the driven noise is Brownian motion and

is only bounded. For the second question, in fact, there have been already a vast of results investigating the quantitative estimate of the Euler-Maruyama approximation for non-continuous drift

inspired by the regularity from the semigroup. We will discuss the literature in detail in the subsection

. Now our tasks in this paper are to figure out an upper bound
assuming that
m=n^{\gamma}
with some

, and to show (
1.9
) with the constant being polynomial of

(see

). As a result, we obtain our second main result

.

1.3.
Relevant literature and our contributions

1.3.1.
Well-posedness of SDEs driven by Brownian noises

Under the framework of distributional drifts, there has been extensive literature on Brownian motion cases. As the best of our knowledge, the earliest works are
[
BC01
]
by (Bass and Chen) and
[
FRW03
]
(by Flandoli, Russo, and Wolf), which both focused on one-dimensional time-independent multiplicative noise. For further studies on the one-dimensional case, see
[
FRW04
,
CE05
,
HZ07
,
HLM17
]
. In the multi-dimensional case, Zhang and Zhao
[
ZZ17
]
achieved well-posedness for the zero-energy solution (see also
[
ZZ18
]
). Meanwhile, Flandoli, Issolio, and Russo
[
FIR17
]
introduced the concept of the virtual solution (a kind of weak solution) and established the well-posedness when the drift
b
has
-\beta
-Hölder regularity with
\beta<1/2
. Furthermore, for
\beta\in(1/2,2/3)
, Delarue and Diel
[
DD16
]
and Cannizzaro and Chouk
[
CC18
]
, using rough path theory and paracontrolled calculus, respectively, obtained the well-posedness for one-dimensional and multidimensional cases when the drift
b
represents some certain Gaussian noise. Recently, when the drift coefficient
b
is divergence-free and the diffusion coefficient
\sigma\equiv{\mathbb{I}}
, Gräfner and Perkowski
[
GP23
]
and the first named author and Zhang
[
HZ23
]
established the well-posedness of energy solutions and weak solutions, respectively, for all
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Notably, the well-posedness of SDEs with singular drifts, in the most of the results mentioned above, strongly depends on the solvability of the related second order parabolic PDE (such as (
1.5
) with a local operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}
) which are known as backward Kolmogorov equations in the literature. For instance,
[
Ve79
,
KR05
,
XXZZ20
,
FIR17

1.3.2.
Well-posedness of SDEs driven by Lévy noises

Actually, the approach to solving the well-posedness of SDEs driven by
\alpha
-stable processes is similar to those driven by Brownian motions, that is researchers usually focus on PDE (
1.5
) before showing the well-posedness of SDE (
1.1
). Due to the comparison between the diffusion part
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}
(which increases the regularity of the solution) and the transport part
b\cdot\nabla
(which maintains the singularity), three classifications arise:

•

subcritical case:
\alpha\in(1,2)
;

•

critical case:
\alpha=1
;

•

supercritical case:
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

For more discussion on the such classifications, see
[
HWW20
]
for example. It is worth pointing out that the well-posedness of non-local PDEs like (

) itself gained popularity after the influential paper by Caffarelli and Silvestre
[
CS07
]
.

In the following, we outline the literature on the well-posedness of the
\alpha
-stable-driven SDEs and the coresponding nonlocal PDEs based on the above categorization. Before that, we introduce some abbreviations for simplicity:

•

SW
= the strong well-posedness of SDEs (existence of a strong solution and pathwise uniqueness);

•

WW
= the weak well-posedness of SDE s (existence of a weak solution and uniqueness in law);

•

= the well-posedness of PDEs;

•

= Schauder’s estimates of PDEs.

Under the framework distributional drifts, compared with continuous noises, there are fewer results for the jumped cases. Thus, before talking about the distributional ones, we would like to overview some results about the case of
\alpha
-stable noises where the drift

is singular or irregular but is a function.

In the subcritical case

, when

and

were obtained by Priola

obtained

in the case of cylindrical

-stable measures. Moreover, it is worth pointing out that, when

Now we turn to the distributional case. Considering the additive noise that is

1.3.3.
Convergence rates of the Euler-Maruyama approximation.

First of all, let us introduce some results on the strong Euler-Maruyama approximation rate about the usual SDEs when the drift term
b
is a function but may be non-Lipschitz.

So far, via the Zvonkin transform or the Itô-Tanaka trick, regarding the Euler-Maruyama approximation for SDEs driven
\alpha
-stable processes with
\alpha\in(1,2)
, there have been many results achieving the following strong (path) convergence rate for the
\beta
-Hölder continuous drift
b
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X^{m,n}_{t}-X_{t}^{m}|^{p}\right]\leqslant c_{\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}}n^{-c_{\alpha,\beta,p}}.

In the framework of additive noises,
[
MT17
]
obtained
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}=(\beta/2)\wedge(1/p)
with
\beta>2-\alpha
for truncated symmetric
\alpha
-stable processes, and
[
KS19
]
got
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}=(\beta/\alpha)\wedge(1/p)
for a broader class of Lévy processes and
\beta>2/\alpha-1
. Moreover, considering multiplicative standard
\alpha
-stable noises,
[
MX18
]
established the strong convergence rate
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}=(\beta/\alpha)\wedge(1/p)
for
\beta>1-\alpha/2
, relying on the Itô-Tanaka trick as well. Readers are also referred to the work
[
HSY21
]
, in which
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}=(\beta/\alpha)\wedge(1/p)
with
\beta>1-\alpha/2
and drifts are Sobolev, that is the drift may be discontinuous.

Recently, employing a significantly different method, the stochastic sewing lemma (introduced in
[
Le20
]
), which is useful for the models driven by noises exhibiting scaling properties like fractional Brownian motions and
\alpha
-stable processes, Butkovsky, Dareiotis, and Gerencsér
[
BDG22
]
obtained the convergence rate
c_{\alpha,\beta,p}=1/2+(\beta/\alpha)\wedge(1/2)
with
\alpha\in[2/3,2]
, which essentially improved the convergence rate by an additional
1/2
.
As a matter of fact, after that the stochastic sewing lemma had been obtained in
[
Le20
]
, compared to earlier works (see eg.
[
MT17
]
for continuous drifts and

[
DG20
]
for discontinuous drifts), numerous results, such as
[
BDG21
,
LL21
,
DGL23
]
, achieved a similar
1/2
rate improvement on the strong Euler-Maruyama approximation, concerning the SDEs driven by Brownian motions (i.e.
2
-stable processes) or fractional Brownian motions.

In this paper, we consider the following weak convergence rate:

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X^{m,n}_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t}^{m})|\leqslant C_{\|b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbb{X}}}n^{-c_{\alpha,\beta}},

where
{\mathbb{X}}
is some suitable space. When
b\in\mathbf{C}^{\beta}
with some
\beta>0
and
\mathbb{X}=\mathbf{C}^{\alpha+\beta}
, there have been many results (see
[
MZ11
]
and references therein for example) considering the weak convergence rate, where the rate
c_{\alpha,\beta}
is usually proportional to
\beta
and approaches zero as
\beta
tends to zero. Consequently, the question of whether these weak rates can be further improved was raised in the introduction of
[
BDG22
]
. A new approach introduced in
[
BJ22
]
is using the classical Itô-Tanaka trick and delicate heat kernel calculations to achieve rate
1/2
for the case driven by Brownian motions with drifts being bounded and
\mathbb{X}=L^{\infty}
(i.e. the total variation distance). Moreover, such approach was extended to the
L^{q}_{t}L^{p}
drifts in
[
JM24
]
, and then to the
\alpha
-stable case in
[
FJM24
]
and
[
SH24
]
, respectively, for
L^{q}_{t}L^{p}
drifts and bounded density-dependent drifts. When
b
is merely bounded, the rate in
[
FJM24
]
is
(\alpha-1)/\alpha+\epsilon
for any small
\epsilon>0
, and
[
SH24
]
drops the
\epsilon>0
that is obtaining a rate of
(\alpha-1)/\alpha
, which coincides with the rate
1/2
in
[
BJ22
]
when
\alpha=2
.

Contributions.
The contributions of this paper to the Euler-Maruyama approximation are as follows:

(i)

We establish a quantitative estimate in

for bounded drifts, where the dependence of the constant on

is evident. This dependence plays a significant role in our distributional drift case as discussed in subsection

(ii)

The Euler-Maruyama approximation for SDEs driven by

-stable processes with distributional drifts is obtained in

2.1.
Besov spaces

In this subsection, we introduce Besov spaces. Let
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the Schwartz space of all rapidly decreasing functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

the dual space of
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
called Schwartz generalized function (or tempered distribution) space. Given
f\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
and the inverse Fourier transform
\check{f}
are defined by

\hat{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-i\xi\cdot x}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

\check{f}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{i\xi\cdot x}f(\xi){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

For every
f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the Fourier and the inverse transforms are defined by

\displaystyle{\langle}\hat{f},\varphi{\rangle}:={\langle}f,\hat{\varphi}{\rangle},\qquad{\langle}\check{f},\varphi{\rangle}:={\langle}f,\check{\varphi}{\rangle},\ \ \forall\varphi\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Let
\chi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a radial smooth function with

\displaystyle\chi(\xi)=\begin{cases}1,&\ \ |\xi|\leqslant 1,\\
0,&\ \ |\xi|>3/2.\end{cases}

For
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define
\psi(\xi):=\chi(\xi)-\chi(2\xi)
and for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(\xi){:=}\psi(2^{-j}\xi).

Let
B_{r}:=\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid|\xi|\leqslant r\}
for
r>0
. It is easy to see that
\psi\geqslant 0
, supp
\psi\subset B_{3/2}/B_{1/2}
, and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\chi(2\xi)+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\psi_{j}(\xi)=\chi(2^{-k}\xi)\to 1,\ \ \hbox{as}\ \ k\to\infty.

Since
\check{\psi}_{j}(y)=2^{jd}\check{\psi}(2^{j}y),j\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{\theta}|\nabla^{k}\check{\psi}_{j}|(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant{c}2^{(k-\theta)j},\ \ \theta>0,\ \ k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

where the constant
c
is equal to
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{\theta}|\nabla^{k}\check{\psi}|(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
and
\nabla^{k}
stands for the
k
-order gradient. The block operators
{\mathcal{R}}_{j},j\geqslant 0
are defined on
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f(x):=(\psi_{j}\hat{f})^{\check{\,}}(x)=\check{\psi}_{j}*f(x)=2^{jd}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\check{\psi}(2^{j}y)f(x-y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,

and

{\mathcal{R}}_{-1}f(x):=(\chi(2\cdot)\hat{f})^{\check{\,}}(x)=(\chi(2\cdot))\check{}*f(x).

Then by (
2.1
),

(2.3)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{j\geqslant-1}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f.

Remark 2.1
.

For
j\geqslant-1
, by definitions, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}={\mathcal{R}}_{j}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j},\quad\text{where $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}{:=}\sum_{\ell=-1}^{1}{\mathcal{R}}_{j+\ell}$ with ${\mathcal{R}}_{-2}:=0$},

and
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
is symmetric in the sense of

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f(x)g(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x){\mathcal{R}}_{j}g(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,\ \ f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\,g\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Now we state the definitions of Besov spaces.

Definition 2.2

(Besov spaces)
.

For every
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, the Besov space
{\mathbf{B}}_{p,q}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is defined by

{\mathbf{B}}_{p,q}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\Big\{f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\big|\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s}_{p,q}}:={\Big[}\sum_{j\geqslant-1}\left(2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p}\right)^{q}{\Big]}^{1/q}<\infty\Big\}.

If
p=q=\infty
, it is in the sense

{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\Big\{f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\big|\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s}_{\infty,\infty}}:=\sup_{j\geqslant-1}2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{\infty}<\infty\Big\}.

This definition and Young’s inequality ensure that for any
s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
i=0,1,2
, with
s_{0}<s_{1}<s_{2}
and
\kappa>0
, there is a constant
c_{\kappa}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s_{1}}}\leqslant\kappa\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s_{2}}}+c_{\kappa}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s_{0}}},

In addition, for any
s_{2}>s_{1}>0
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s_{1}}}\leqslant\kappa\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s_{2}}}+c_{\kappa}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Recall the following Bernstein’s inequality (cf.
[
BCD11
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.3

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, there is a constant
c=c(d,k)>0
such that for all
j\geqslant-1
and
1\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant\infty
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{2}}\lesssim_{c}2^{(k+d(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}))j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{1}}.

In particular, for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
1\leqslant p,q\leqslant\infty
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s}_{p,q}}\lesssim_{c}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s+k}_{p,q}}.

It is worth discussing here the equivalence between the Besov and Hölder spaces, which will be used in various contexts in this paper without much explanation. For
s>0
, let
{\mathbf{C}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the classical
s
-order Hölder space consisting of all measurable functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{s}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{[s]}\|\nabla^{j}f\|_{\infty}+[\nabla^{[s]}f]_{{\mathbf{C}}^{s-[s]}}<\infty,

where
[s]
denotes the greatest integer not more than
s
, and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f(x)|,\quad[f]_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}}:=\sup_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\|f(\cdot+h)-f(\cdot)\|_{\infty}}{|h|^{\gamma}},~\gamma\in(0,1).

If
s>0
and
s\notin{\mathbb{N}}
, we have the following equivalence between
{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
{\mathbf{C}}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
: (cf.
[
Tr92
]
)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s}}\asymp\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{s}}.

However, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we only have one side control that is

\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{n}_{\infty,\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{n}}.

At the end of this subsection, we introduce the following product law (cf.
[
GIP15
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
Bo81
]
) and a interpolation inequality (cf.
[
BCD11
, Theorem 2.80]
).

Lemma 2.4

(Product laws)
.

For any
s>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
c=c(s,\varepsilon)>0
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-s}_{\infty,\infty}}\lesssim_{c}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s+\varepsilon}_{\infty,\infty}}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-s}_{\infty,\infty}}.

Lemma 2.5

(Interpolation inequality)
.

Let
s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s_{2}>s_{1}
. For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, there is a constant
c=c(s_{1},s_{2},p)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{B_{p,1}^{\theta s_{1}+(1-\theta)s_{2}}}\lesssim_{c}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s_{1}}_{p,\infty}}^{\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s_{2}}_{p,\infty}}^{1-\theta}.

Furthermore, for any
s_{2}>0>s_{1}
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}\lesssim_{c}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s_{1}}_{\infty,\infty}}^{\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s_{2}}_{\infty,\infty}}^{1-\theta},

where
\theta=s_{2}/(s_{2}-s_{1})
.

2.2.
\alpha
-stable processes

We call a
\sigma
-finite positive measure

on

a Lévy measure if

\displaystyle\nu(\{0\})=0,\ \ \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big(1\wedge|z|^{2}\big)\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)<+\infty.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Let
L^{(\alpha)}_{t}
be a
d
-dimensional
\alpha
-stable process with Lévy measure (or
\alpha
-stable measure)

defined as (

). We say an
\alpha

(ND)
holds. Note that
\alpha
-stable process

has the scaling property,

(2.9)

\displaystyle(L_{t}^{(\alpha)})_{t\geqslant 0}\overset{(d)}{=}(\lambda^{-1/\alpha}L^{(\alpha)}_{\lambda t})_{t\geqslant 0},\ \ \forall\,\lambda>0,

and for any
\gamma_{2}>\alpha>\gamma_{1}\geqslant 0
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\int_{|z|\leqslant 1}|z|^{\gamma_{2}}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{|z|>1}|z|^{\gamma_{1}}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)<\infty.

Moreover, it is easy to see that for any
\lambda>0
and

,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(1\wedge|\lambda z|^{p})\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)=\lambda^{\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(1\wedge|z|^{p})\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

Let
N({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)
be the associated Poisson random measure defined by

N((0,t]\times A):=\sum_{s\in(0,t]}{\mathbf{1}}_{A}(L_{s}^{(\alpha)}-L^{(\alpha)}_{s-}),\ \ A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}),t>0.

By Lévy-Itô’s decomposition (cf.
[

\displaystyle L^{(\alpha)}_{t}=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\varepsilon<|z|\leqslant 1}z\tilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|z|>1}zN({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

where

is the compensated Poisson random measure.

In the sequel, we always assume that
\nu^{(\alpha)}
is symmetric. Hence, we can write

(2.12)

\displaystyle L^{(\alpha)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|z|\leqslant c}z\tilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|z|>c}zN({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),\ \ \forall c>0.

The following moment estimate is taken from
[
CZ18b
, Lemma 2.4]
with some slight modification. The proof is similar, so we omit it.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
T,\delta>0
. Assume that

are two bounded stopping times and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}g(r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}L^{(\alpha)}_{r}\right|^{p}\lesssim_{c}\delta^{p/\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}^{p}.

2.3.
Time-dependent Lévy-type operators

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
L^{(\alpha)}
be an
\alpha
-stable process having symmetric non-degenerate Lévy measure
\nu^{(\alpha)}
. In this subsection, we start with the following time-inhomogeneous Lévy process: for

(2.14)

\displaystyle L^{\sigma}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{r}^{(\alpha)}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sigma_{r}z\tilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

where

is a bounded measurable function. Define

\displaystyle P^{\sigma}_{s,t}f(x):={\mathbb{E}}(x+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{r}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{r}^{(\alpha)})

for all

. By Itô’s formula (cf.
[

, Theorem 5.1 of Chapter II]
), one sees that

\partial_{t}P^{\sigma}_{s,t}f(x)={\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma_{t}}P^{\sigma}_{s,t}f(x),

where

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma_{t}}f(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}f(x+\sigma_{t}z)-f(x)-\sigma_{t}z\cdot\nabla f(x){\Big)}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

Below, we always make the following assumption in this subsection:

\bf({\mathbf{H}}0)
There is a constant
a_{0}>1
such that

a_{0}^{-1}|\xi|\leqslant|\sigma_{t}\xi|\leqslant a_{0}|\xi|,\ \ \forall(t,\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Under the assumptions
\bf({\mathbf{H}}0)
and
\bf(ND)

[
Sa99
, Theorem 8.1]

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}{\rm e}^{i\xi\cdot L^{\sigma}_{t}}|\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\left(t\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\cos(\xi\cdot\sigma_{t}z)-1)\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\left(-{t}|\xi|^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}\frac{1-\cos(\xi/|\xi|\cdot\sigma_{t}r\theta)}{r^{1+\alpha}}\Sigma({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\theta){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\leqslant{\rm e}^{-ct|\xi|^{\alpha}},

where the constant

depends only on

and

. Hence, by
[
Sa99
, Proposition 28.1]
, the random variable

defined by (

given by Fourier’s inverse transform

p^{\sigma}(t,x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\rm e}^{-ix\cdot\xi}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm e}^{i\xi\cdot L^{\sigma}_{t}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,\ \ \forall t>0,

and the partial derivatives of

at any orders tend to

as

. Furthermore, by
[

, Lemma 3.2]
or
[

, for each

,

satisfies that for any

and

,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{\beta}|\nabla^{k}p^{\sigma}(t,x)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lesssim_{c}t^{-\frac{k-\beta}{\alpha}},

where

.

We need the following heat kernel estimates in integral form with Littlewood-Paley’s decomposition, which is obtained in
[

, Lemma 3.3 ]
(see also
[

Lemma 2.7

(Heat kernel estimates)
.

Let

holds with constant

. Let

be the density of the random variable

(i)

There is a constant

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{-1}p^{\sigma}_{s,t}\|_{1}\leqslant c.

(ii)

and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{\gamma}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}p^{\sigma}_{s,t}(x)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lesssim_{c}(t-s)^{-\frac{\vartheta-\gamma}{\alpha}}2^{-j\vartheta},

where the block operators

(iii)

For each
\gamma\in[0,\alpha)
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{\gamma}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}p^{\sigma}_{s,t}(x)|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim_{c}2^{-j\alpha}.

In particular, when

is always equal to the identity matrix

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}f(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}f(x+z)-f(x)-z\cdot\nabla f(x){\Big)}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

which is exactly the infinitesimal generator of the

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u={\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}u,\quad u(0)=f,

where

[

, Theorem 5.1 of Chapter II]

\displaystyle u(t,x)={\mathbb{E}}f(x+L_{t}^{(\alpha)})=(p_{t}*f)(x),

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}u(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-\frac{k}{\alpha}}\|f\|_{\infty}.

We also have the following useful estimates.

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
T>0
. For
k=0,1
, there is a constant
c>0
such that for all
0<s<t\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}u(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-\frac{k+\alpha}{\alpha}}\|f\|_{\infty}

and

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}u(t)-\nabla^{k}u(s)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\left[s^{-\frac{k}{\alpha}}\wedge(s^{-\frac{k+\alpha}{\alpha}}(t-s))\right]\|f\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Observe that, by (
2.19
) and Bernstein’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}h\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}h\|_{\infty}\wedge{\Big[}|z|^{2}\|\nabla^{2}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}h\|_{\infty}{\Big]}{\Big)}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}1\wedge|2^{j}z|^{2}{\Big)}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)\lesssim 2^{\alpha j}\|h\|_{\infty}.

Hence, by (
2.3
) and (
2.17
) and Bernstein’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}u(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geqslant-1}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}(\nabla^{k}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u)\|_{\infty}\overset{\eqref{eq:XM100}}{\lesssim}\sum_{j\geqslant-1}2^{(k+\alpha)j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}p_{t}\|_{1}\|f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geqslant-1}2^{(k+\alpha)j}\left([2^{-(k+\alpha+1)j}t^{-\frac{k+\alpha+1}{\alpha}}]\wedge 1\right)\|f\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-\frac{k+\alpha}{\alpha}}\|f\|_{\infty},

where we used the following estimate in the last step: for any
0<\beta<\gamma
and
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 0}2^{\beta j}\left([2^{-\gamma j}\lambda]\wedge 1\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\wedge 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}2^{\beta s}\left([2^{-\gamma s}\lambda]\wedge 1\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda\wedge 1+\lambda^{\frac{\beta}{\gamma}}\int_{\lambda^{-1/\gamma}}^{\infty}r^{\beta-1}\left(r^{-\gamma}\wedge 1\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{\beta}{\gamma}}.

Hence, for all
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}u(t,x)-\nabla^{k}u(s,x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\nabla^{k}\partial_{r}u(r,x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right|=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\nabla^{k}{\mathscr{L}}_{{\mathbb{I}}_{d\times d}}^{(\alpha)}u(r,x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{s}^{t}r^{-\frac{k+\alpha}{\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\lesssim s^{-\frac{k+\alpha}{\alpha}}(t-s)\|f\|_{\infty},

which, combining with (
2.21
), deduces the desired result (
2.22
).
∎

3.
Nonlocal equations with singular Lévy measures

Fix
\alpha\in(1,2)
. Let
\sigma(t,x):{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{M}}_{non}^{d}
be a bounded measurable function. In this section, we establish Schauder’s estimate and obtain the well-posedness for the following non-local parabolic equation with time-dependent variable diffusion coefficient
\sigma_{t}(x):=\sigma(t,x)
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u={\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}u+b\cdot\nabla u+f,\ \ u(0)=u_{0},

where
b,f\in L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}
with some
\beta\in(0,\frac{\alpha-1}{2})
,
\sigma
satisfies the condition
(H
σ
)
with constant
c_{0}
, and
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}
is given by (
1.4
):

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}g(t,x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\Big(}g(t,x+\sigma_{t}(x)z)-g(t,x)-\sigma_{t}(x)z\cdot\nabla g(t,x){\Big)}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

Here
\nu^{(\alpha)}
is defined by (
1.2
) and satisfies condition
(ND)
.

It is well known that the best regularity of the solution
u(t)
is in
{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}
, but the domain of the operator
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma_{t}}
is
\cup_{\varepsilon>0}{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha+\varepsilon}
. Therefore, in order to define the solutions of PDE (
3.1
), we fisrt extend the domain of
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma_{t}}
. Before that, we introduce some notations. For any
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|z|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}c_{0}^{-1}
, define

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{y}(x):=\sigma(t,x+y),\ \ \hbox{and}\ \ \Lambda_{\sigma_{t}^{y},z}(x):=x+\sigma_{t}^{y}(x)z.

By
[
Zh13
, Lemma 2.1]
, it is easy to see that for any
t\geqslant 0
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leqslant|\Lambda_{t,z}^{y}(x_{1})-\Lambda_{t,z}^{y}(x_{2})|\leqslant 2|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

Define

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma_{t}^{y},z}f(x):=f(x+\sigma_{t}^{y}(x)z)-f(x+\sigma_{t}^{y}(0)z)-(\sigma_{t}^{y}(x)-\sigma_{t}^{y}(0))z\cdot\nabla f(x).

Then, by
[
CHZ20
, Lemma 2.2]
, for any
f\in C^{1}
,
g\in W^{2,1}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle|{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma_{t}^{y},z}f,g{\rangle}|\leqslant{c}_{d,\theta}|z|^{1+\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\theta}}{\Big(}\sum_{j=0}^{1}\mu_{j}(|\nabla^{j}g|)+\mu_{1+\theta}(|\nabla^{2}g|)^{\theta}\mu_{1+\theta}(|\nabla g|)^{1-\theta}{\Big)},

where the constant
c_{d,\theta}>0
is independent of the variables
t,y,z
, and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x):=(|x|\wedge 1)^{\theta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,\ \ \hbox{and}\ \ \mu_{\theta}(f):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x).

In particular,

(3.5)

\displaystyle|{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma_{t}^{y},z}f,g{\rangle}|\leqslant c_{d}|z|^{2}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\mu_{j}(|\nabla^{j}g|).

The following lemma is crucial to give
3.2
.

Lemma 3.1

(Boundedness of operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}
)
.

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
. Under the condition
(H
σ
)
, there is a constant
c=c(\alpha,\beta,c_{0})>0
such that for any
u\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
t\geqslant 0
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}u(t)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}}\lesssim_{c}\|u(t)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}},

where
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}_{\sigma}
is given by (
3.2
).

Proof.

For simplicity, we drop the time variable
t
and the superscript
\alpha
in
\nu^{(\alpha)}
in the following proof. Observe that

{\mathcal{R}}_{j}{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}u(x)={\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)+[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}]u(x),

where
[{\mathscr{A}}_{1},{\mathscr{A}}_{2}]:={\mathscr{A}}_{1}{\mathscr{A}}_{2}-{\mathscr{A}}_{2}{\mathscr{A}}_{1}
called commutator operator. Define

u^{x}(y):=u(y+x).

Recall the definition (
2.2
) of block operators
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
. By the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\check{\psi}_{j}(x-y){\Big(}u(y+\sigma(y)z)-u(y+\sigma(x)z)-(\sigma(y)-\sigma(x))z\cdot\nabla u(y){\Big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

\displaystyle\check{\psi}_{j}(-y){\Big(}u^{x}(y+\sigma^{x}(y)z)-u^{x}(y+\sigma^{x}(0)z)-(\sigma^{x}(y)-\sigma^{x}(0))z\cdot\nabla u^{x}(y){\Big)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}y,

which yields

[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}]u(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma^{x},z}u^{x},\check{\psi}_{j}(-\cdot){\rangle}\nu^{(\alpha)}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z).

For
|z|\leqslant\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2c_{0}}
, based on the fact

\displaystyle\mu_{\delta}(|\nabla^{k}\check{\psi}_{j}|)\lesssim 2^{(k-\delta)j},\ \ \delta\geqslant 0,~~k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

and (
3.3
) with
\theta\in(\alpha-1,\alpha-\beta)
, one sees that

\displaystyle\sup_{x}|{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma^{x},z}u^{x},\check{\psi}_{j}(-\cdot){\rangle}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{1+\theta}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}}{\Big(}1+2^{\theta(2-1-\theta)j}2^{(1-\theta)(1-1-\theta)j}{\Big)}\lesssim|z|^{1+\theta}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}}.

For
|z|>\delta
, by the mean-value theorem and (
1.3
), we get

\displaystyle\sup_{x}|{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma^{x},z}u^{x},\check{\psi}_{j}(-\cdot){\rangle}|\leqslant 2c_{0}|z|\|\nabla u\|_{\infty}\|\check{\psi}\|_{1}\lesssim|z|\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}},

where we used the fact
\alpha-\beta>1
. Hence, by (
2.10
), we obtain that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}]u\|_{\infty}\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sup_{x}|{\langle}{\mathscr{D}}_{\sigma^{x},z}u^{x},\check{\psi}_{j}(-\cdot){\rangle}|\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)\lesssim\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}}.

On the other hand, through Bernstein’s inequality
2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x+\sigma(x)z)-{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)-\sigma(x)z\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)|\lesssim(|2^{j}z|\wedge|2^{j}z|^{2})\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty},

which implies that

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(|2^{j}z|\wedge|2^{j}z|^{2})\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)\lesssim 2^{\alpha j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty},

provided by the scaling property (
2.9
) and (
2.10
).
Therefore, combining this with (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}^{(\alpha)}u\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\alpha-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}}+2^{\alpha j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty}\lesssim 2^{\beta j}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\alpha-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}},

which derives (
3.6
) by taking the supremum of
j
. The proof is completed.
∎

Based on

from

to
{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}
with

Definition 3.2

(Solutions)
.

Let

c=c(d,\alpha,T,\delta,\beta,\|b\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}})>0
such that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\in(0,T]
,

\|{\mathbf{P}}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(t)\|_{\rm var}\lesssim_{c}\|b\|_{\infty}^{1+\delta}n^{-\delta}+\|b\|_{\infty}n^{-\delta/\alpha}+(1+\|b\|_{\infty}^{2})n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\rm var}
denotes the total variance of measures.

Remark 5.2
.

Taking
\delta=\alpha-1-\beta
in (
5.5
), one sees that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{P}}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(t)\|_{\rm var}\lesssim_{c}\|b\|_{\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}n^{-(\alpha-1-\beta)}+\|b\|_{\infty}n^{-\frac{\alpha-\beta-1}{\alpha}}+(1+\|b\|_{\infty}^{2})n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

Proof of
Theorem 5.1

.

By the weak uniqueness of SDEs (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we assume
b\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. It suffices to estimate

\left|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X^{n}_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t})\right|

for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Use Itô-Tanaka trick by considering the following backward PDE with the terminal condition
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}u^{t}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}u^{t}+b\cdot\nabla u^{t}=0,\quad u^{t}_{t}=\varphi,

where
u^{t}
is the shifted function
u^{t}(s,x):=u(t-s,x)
with
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T
, and
{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}
is the infinitesimal generator of
L_{t}^{(\alpha)}
(see (
2.19
)). It follows (
3.27
) that for any
\beta\in(0,(\alpha-1)/2)
and
\delta\in[0,\alpha-\beta]
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|u^{t}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\delta}}\lesssim(t-s)^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha}}\|\varphi\|_{\infty},

where the implicit constant in the above inequality only depends on
d,\alpha,T,\delta,\beta,\|b\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta}_{\infty,\infty}}
. By the same argument as the one in the proof of
Theorem 4.4
, adopting Itô’s formula (cf.
[
IW89
, Theorem 5.1 of Chapter II]
) to
u^{t}(s,X^{n}_{s})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X^{n}_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t})=

\displaystyle\,{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(b(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)})-b(X^{n}_{r})\right)\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r,X^{n}_{r}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}b(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)})\cdot\left(\nabla u^{t}(r,X^{n}_{r})-\nabla u^{t}(r,X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)})\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left({\mathbb{E}}(b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r))(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)})-{\mathbb{E}}(b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r))(X^{n}_{r}))\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{1}+{\mathscr{I}}_{2}.

\bullet

For
{\mathscr{I}}_{1}
, by Bernstein’s inequality, (
5.4
) and (
5.1
), one sees that for any
\delta\in(0,\alpha-1-\beta]
,

\displaystyle|{\mathscr{I}}_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u^{t}(r)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\delta}}{\mathbb{E}}|X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)}-X^{n}_{r}|^{\delta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{-\frac{1+\delta}{\alpha}}{\mathbb{E}}|X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)}-X^{n}_{r}|^{\delta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:mo}}{\lesssim}\|b\|_{\infty}(\|b\|_{\infty}^{\delta}n^{-\delta}+n^{-\delta/\alpha})t^{\frac{\alpha-1-\delta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}r^{\frac{\alpha-1-\delta}{\alpha}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Consequently, we get that for any
\delta\in(0,\alpha-1-\beta]
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle|{\mathscr{I}}_{1}|\lesssim\|b\|_{\infty}^{1+\delta}n^{-\delta}+\|b\|_{\infty}n^{-\delta/\alpha}.

\bullet

As for
{\mathscr{I}}_{2}
, the estimate of

\left|{\mathbb{E}}(b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r))(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(r)})-{\mathbb{E}}(b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r))(X^{n}_{r}))\right|

is the key ingredient. Using the Itô-Tanaka trick again, we consider the following equation:

(5.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}w^{r}+{\mathscr{L}}^{(\alpha)}w^{r}=0,\quad w^{r}(r)=f,

where
w^{r}(s,x):=w(r-s,x)
is the shifted function with
0<s<r\leqslant T
and
f\in C^{\infty}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. In the last, readers will see that the terminal condition
f
is taken as
b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r)
. Applying Itô’s formula (cf.
[
IW89
, Theorem 5.1 of Chapter II]
) to
w^{r}(s,X^{n}_{s})
and by (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r})={\mathbb{E}}w^{r}(r,X^{n}_{r})=w^{r}(0,x)+{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{r}b(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(s)})\cdot\nabla w^{r}(s,X^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

which drives that for any
r_{2}>r_{1}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{2}})-{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{1}})\right|\leqslant&\,|w(r_{2},x)-w(r_{1},x)|+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}b(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(s)})\cdot\nabla w(r_{2}-s,X^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|\\
&+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}b(X^{n}_{\phi_{n}(s)})\cdot(\nabla w(r_{2}-s,X^{n}_{s})-\nabla w(r_{1}-s,X^{n}_{s})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right|\\
\lesssim&\|w(r_{2})-w(r_{1})\|_{\infty}+\|b\|_{\infty}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\|\nabla w(r_{2}-s)\|_{\infty}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\\
&+\|b\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\|\nabla w(r_{2}-s)-\nabla w(r_{1}-s)\|_{\infty}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\\
\end{split}

Furthermore, based on (
2.22
) and (
2.21
), we get that for all
0<r_{1}<r_{2}\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{2}})-{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{1}})\right|\lesssim&\left[1\wedge((r_{2}-{r_{1}}){r_{1}}^{-1})\right]\|f\|_{\infty}+\|b\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}(r_{2}-{r_{1}})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}+1}\\
&+\|b\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{r_{1}}\left[({r_{1}}-s)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\wedge((r_{2}-{r_{1}})({r_{1}}-s)^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}})\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.\end{split}

Noticing that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{2}-r_{1}}s^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+(r_{2}-{r_{1}})\int_{(r_{2}-r_{1})\wedge r_{1}}^{r_{1}}s^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim(r_{2}-r_{1})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}+1},

one sees that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{2}})-{\mathbb{E}}f(X^{n}_{r_{1}})\right|\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}\left(\left[1\wedge((r_{2}-{r_{1}}){r_{1}}^{-1})\right]+\|b\|_{\infty}(r_{2}-{r_{1}})^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}+1}\right).

Taking the place of
(f(\cdot),r_{1},r_{2})
by
((b\cdot\nabla u^{t}(r))(\cdot),\phi_{n}(r),r)
in (
5.7
), and noticing that, by (
2.7
) and Bernstein’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla u^{t}(r)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla u^{t}(r)\|^{1/2}_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-(\alpha-\beta)+1}}\|\nabla u^{t}(r)\|^{1/2}_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{(\alpha-\beta)-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u^{t}(r)\|^{1/2}_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{2-(\alpha-\beta)}}\|u^{t}(r)\|^{1/2}_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DH12}}}{{\lesssim}}(t-r)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}},

we infer that

\displaystyle|{\mathscr{I}}_{2}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u^{t}(r)\|_{\infty}\left(\left[1\wedge((nr)^{-1})\right]+\|b\|_{\infty}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}\|b\|_{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}r^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\|b\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}(\|b\|_{\infty}+\|b\|_{\infty}^{2}),

where we used the fact
|\phi_{n}(r)-r|\leqslant 1/n
, and the definitions of Beta functions (
1.10
).

Finally, combining the above calculations, we establish the desired estimates.
∎

Proof of
Theorem 1.5

Let
X^{m}_{t}
be the solution to the following classical SDE:

\displaystyle X_{t}^{m}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b_{m}(X^{m}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+L_{t}^{(\alpha)}.

Denote
{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(t):={\mathbb{P}}\circ(X_{t}^{m})^{-1}
.
According to the stability estimates
Theorem 4.4
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and

, we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}(t)\|_{\rm{var}}\lesssim t^{\frac{\alpha-1-2\theta-\varepsilon}{\alpha}}\|b-b_{m}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\theta}}\lesssim t^{\frac{\alpha-1-2\theta-\varepsilon}{\alpha}}m^{-{(\theta-\beta)}}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}}.

Noting
\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}\lesssim m^{\beta}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}}=n^{\beta\gamma}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{-\beta}}
, and applying
Theorem 5.1
with
\delta=\alpha-1-\beta
, for any
\gamma\in(0,\frac{\alpha-1}{2\alpha\beta})
, we get that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{P}}_{m,n}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}(t)\|_{\rm{var}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|{\mathbf{P}}_{m,n}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(t)\|_{\rm{var}}+\|{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(t)-{\mathbf{P}}(t)\|_{\rm{var}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\Big(}\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}^{\alpha-\beta}n^{-(\alpha-1-\beta)}+\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}n^{-\frac{\alpha-1-\beta}{\alpha}}+(1+\|b_{m}\|_{\infty}^{2})n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}{\Big)}+t^{\frac{\alpha-1-2\theta-\varepsilon}{\alpha}}m^{-{\theta-\beta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}+\beta(\gamma+\gamma\vee\frac{1}{\alpha})}+t^{\frac{\alpha-1-2\theta-\varepsilon}{\alpha}}n^{-\gamma(\theta-\beta)}.

Appendix A

Lemma A.1
.

Let
T>0
. For any
0<\beta,\gamma<1<\alpha
, there is a constant
c>0
such that for all
\lambda>0
and
t\in(0,T]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left[(\lambda s^{-\alpha})\wedge s^{-\beta}\right](t-s)^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim_{c}\lambda^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}}t^{-\gamma}.

Proof.

First of all, by a change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left[(\lambda s^{-\alpha})\wedge s^{-\beta}\right](t-s)^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=t^{1-\gamma-\beta}\int_{0}^{1}\left[(\lambda t^{-(\alpha-\beta)}s^{-\alpha})\wedge s^{-\beta}\right](1-s)^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Therefore, it is sufficient to show

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left[(\lambda s^{-\alpha})\wedge s^{-\beta}\right](1-s)^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}}.

Indeed, for any
0<\beta,\gamma<1<\alpha
,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\left[(\lambda s^{-\alpha})\wedge s^{-\beta}\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\left[(\lambda(\tfrac{1}{2})^{-\alpha})\wedge(\tfrac{1}{2})^{-\beta}\right]\int_{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{1}(1-s)^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{1}{2\lambda^{1/(\alpha-\beta)}}}r^{-\beta}\left[r^{-\alpha+\beta}\wedge 1\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\lambda\wedge 1

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{-\beta}\left[r^{-\alpha+\beta}\wedge 1\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\lambda\wedge 1\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}},

where we used the change of variable
s=\lambda^{1/(\alpha-\beta)}r
in the second inequality. This completes the proof.
∎
