Origins of Proof Theory

The story of modern proof theory is usually tied to the renowned mathematician David Hilbert (1862-1943), who initiated what is known as
Hilbert’s Program
in the foundations of mathematics. Following the discovery of set-theoretic paradoxes, such as Russell’s paradox, concerns and uncertainties arose regarding the consistency of various branches of mathematics.
In reaction to these emerging doubts at the beginning of the 20th century, Hilbert put forward a way to provide mathematics with a secure basis by proposing to formalize mathematics in an axiomatic theory whose consistency can be proved by finitary means. Such an achievement would clearly establish the consistency of mathematics once and for all; this solution would put the nagging doubts about the correctness of our abstract mathematical constructions to rest.
To elucidate, Hilbert’s proposal was to prove the consistency of more complicated systems in terms of simpler systems. Ultimately, the consistency of all of mathematics could be reduced to basic arithmetic.

As we know, Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems, only a decade after its birth, put an end to Hilbert’s program, at least to the program in its strict sense. To understand Gödel’s results, suppose a consistent “strong enough” system is given. Gödel’s first theorem states that such a system can never be complete. Consequently, it is not possible to formalize all mathematical true
statements within a formal system.
Gödel’s second theorem states that such a system cannot prove its own consistency. Accordingly, Hilbert’s assumption that a finitistic system can be used to prove the consistency of itself and more powerful theories, such as set theory, is refuted. To put it another way, if Peano Arithmetic
PA
is not able to prove its own consistency, how could a finitary theory, much weaker than
PA
, be able to prove it? This shows that, at least for theories to which Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems apply, Hilbert’s aims are unattainable. Still, it is not to be excluded that there are other finitary means available to prove the consistency of
PA
; means that are not formalizable in
PA
. But the general opinion is that Hilbert’s Program, in its strict sense, did not survive Gödel’s famous results.

But there is something that did survive, namely the study of formal proofs and proof systems, and their use in the foundations of mathematics, as well as in numerous other disciplines, such as computer science, philosophy, and linguistics.
Someone who has been very important for this survival is Gerhard Gentzen. In the 1930s, Gentzen showed that although
PA
cannot prove the consistency of
PA
, an extension of it by the (suitably coded) statement that the ordinal
\varepsilon_{0}
is well-founded

This field of analyzing proofs, nowadays called
Proof Theory
, is large and has connections to almost all other areas in logic and applications in many others, for instance in theorem proving and logic programming. One therefore could say that Proof Theory is one of the most successful programs based on a wrong idea.

The subject of these short lecture notes is a recently emerging area within proof theory, called
Universal Proof Theory
. There are at least three fundamental problems in this area worth investigating. The first one is the
existence problem

2
Flavours of Proof Theory

Looking at the field of Proof Theory today one can distinguish several lines of research. These lines are all concerned with formal proofs and proof systems, but with quite different aims and focussing, not exclusively but in the main, on quite different theories. The areas are not disjoint, but it can be worthwhile to determine them and place proof-theoretic results in one or more of these areas.

What is often called
Classical Proof Theory
is a continuation of Hilbert’s Program. It is concerned with the relative consistency and strength of foundational theories and fragments thereof, in line with the early consistency result by Gentzen mentioned above.
Set theories and theories of arithmetic are among the main theories that are investigated. These investigations are focused on the ordinal analysis of such theories, their strength with respect to a weak basic theory, as in reverse mathematics,
or their relative strength via reductions, what
prawitz74
calls
Reductive Proof Theory
.

prawitz74
contrasts this with
General Proof Theory
where proofs, and consequence relations, are the main topic of study, in their own right. This naturally leads to more fine-grained proof systems, since what is provable is less the issue than the nature and structure of proofs.

Structural Proof Theory
received its name in
negri&vonplato01
and is concerned with the combinatorial analysis of the structure of proofs, and its main methodologies are cut elimination and normalization. Structural Proof Theory is very close in spirit to General Proof Theory. However, it focuses more on the proof systems that Gentzen developed very early on. As a result of their beauty and the insight that they provide in proofs, Gentzen’s systems form the inspiration for many proof systems that are investigated and applied today.

2.1
Universal Proof Theory

Universal proof theory is concerned with classes of logics and the variety of proof systems for them. Rather than on foundational theories, its focus is more on the numerous logics that occur in computer science, philosophy, mathematics, and linguistics. These logics come with their own questions.
Here consistency is not an issue but other questions become important. For example:
the complexity of the decidability of these logics, (not) having interpolation, and the analyticity of the proofs. Foundational theories also occur as objects of study, but the aspects that one is interested in are mostly of a logical nature and are shared with many theories, such as the tautologies or the admissible rules of a theory. But a large part of the research is about logics: epistemic logics, intermediate logics, modal logics, substructural logics, temporal logics, and so on.

These logics are mostly decidable, and many of the well-known ones have sophisticated proof systems, such as sequent calculi (Section
6
). Such proof systems can be used to prove properties about the logics and to compare them. Proof systems that stand out in this respect and at the same are close to actual logical reasoning are Natural Deduction and Sequent Calculi, both introduced by
gentzen35
.
These two kinds of proof systems are closely related and both elegant and natural.

In the introduction, we described three of the main aims of Universal Proof Theory. In these notes we focus on the first one, the
existence problem
, which is discussed in detail in

Namely, to establish, ideally for large classes of logics, whether these logics have good proof systems. Of course, such a question

It is worth mentioning that the Weakness of Hilbert systems is not on the positive side, i.e., in the sense of proving theorems. After proving some, and not many meta theorems, which is the hard part, we can prove theorems rather quickly.
Some common meta theorems are:

•

The deduction theorem:

\Gamma,\varphi\vdash\psi
if and only if
\Gamma\vdash\varphi\to\psi

•

Contraposition: If
\Gamma,\varphi\vdash\psi
then
\Gamma,\neg\psi\vdash\neg\varphi

The problem appears when we want to show a formula is not provable. Then you have to consider all the proofs, and as we observed, guessing the structure of the proof is a very combinatorially complicated task.
In this sense, Gentzen observed that Hilbert systems are not useful in achieving the goal of proving consistency. Therefore, he introduced two other proof systems, natural deduction, and sequent calculi, to study proofs systematically. Being in a transparent context, they are regarded as elegant systems.

5
Natural Deduction

When it comes to choosing proof systems, two main strategies exist depending on the trade-off between logical axioms and rules of inference. Hilbert-style proof systems include many schemes as logical axioms and as few rules of inference as possible. Natural deduction systems and sequent calculi take the opposite strategy, including many inference rules and few or no logical axioms.
Gentzen developed the proof systems
Natural Deduction
(
ND
) as a faithful representation of the logical reasoning used in mathematics. As opposed to Hilbert-style proof systems, in Natural Deduction, logical reasoning is close to “natural” reasoning: starting from assumptions and reaching conclusions using inference rules. Like in “real” mathematics, derivations in
ND
contain assumptions and case distinctions.

A
deduction
(or
derivation

{\mathcal{D}}

assumptions
and the formula at the root (i.e., node at the bottom of the tree) is the
conclusion
. Assumptions can be either
open
or
closed
. An assumption
\varphi
that is closed is depicted as
[\varphi]
, usually superscripted by a symbol
a,b,\dots
or by a natural number. The closure is indicated by writing the superscript of the formula at the inference rule. When all assumptions in a deduction are closed, we call the deduction a
proof
and the deduction tree a
proof tree
.

5.2
The Curry-Howard Isomorphism

The Curry-Howard isomorphism (also known as the Curry–Howard correspondence, or the proofs-as-programs and propositions-as-types interpretation) stands as a noteworthy connection between two seemingly disparate realms: intuitionistic natural deduction and type theory. Its essence is the observation that every derivation in the implicational fragment of intuitionistic natural deduction (
NDi
) corresponds precisely to a term in the simply typed lambda calculus, and vice versa. This profound connection, initially highlighted by the mathematician Haskell Curry and the logician William Alvin Howard, underscores the functional nature of implication. Moreover, it supports the idea that intuitionistic logic captures constructive reasoning. Here, an implication is considered to be a construction or a function that maps proofs of the antecedent to proofs of the conclusion. The isomorphism has been extended to other logical operators as well.

In essence, this correspondence immerses intuitionistic logic with a computational flavor, as it aligns logical derivations with program constructions. As a consequence, the Curry-Howard isomorphism enriches our understanding of logic and enhances our ability to reason about programs. This deep insight has led to advances in a range of disciplines, from type theory and programming languages to formal verification and proof theory. For a gentle introduction to the topic, see
(
sorensen&urzyczun06
)
.

\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsubsection}{toc}{Complexity Measures}
The {degree} of a formula $\varphi$ is inductively defined as $d(\bot)=d(\top)=0$, $d(p)=1$, $d(\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi)=d(\varphi)+1$ for $\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\in\{\Box,\Diamond\}$, and $d(\varphi\circ\psi)=d(\varphi)+d(\psi)+1$ for $\circ\in\{\wedge,\vee,\rightarrow\}$.
In the setting of intuitionistic logic we need another order on formulas, the {weight function} $w(\cdot)$ from \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{dyckhoff92}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} extended to modalities: the weight of an atom and the constants $\bot$ and $\top$ is 1, $w(\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi)=w(\varphi)+1$, and $w(\varphi\circ\psi)=w(\varphi)+w(\psi)+i$, where $i=1$ in case $\circ\in\{\vee,\rightarrow\}$ and $i=2$ otherwise.
\par We use the following orderings on sequents: $S_{0}<_{d}S_{1}$ ($S_{0}<_{w}S_{1}$) if and only if
$S_{0}^{a}\cup S_{0}^{s}<S_{1}^{a}\cup S_{1}^{s}$, where $<$ is the order on multisets determined by degree (weight) as in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{dershowitz&manna79}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}: for multisets $\Gamma,\Delta$ we have $\Delta<\Gamma$ if $\Delta$ is the result of replacing one or more formulas in $\Gamma$ by zero or more formulas of lower degree (weight).
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Sequent Calculi}
A {\it sequent calculus} (or {\it calculus} for short) is a finite set of (sequent) axioms and (sequent) rules, where an axiom is a single meta-sequent and a rule is an expression of the form
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.15pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$S_{1}\dots S_{n}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to36.77377pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 9.13069pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$S_{0}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$where $S_{0},\dots,S_{n}$ are meta-sequents. In each rule, the \emph{principal}
or \emph{main} formulas are defined as the formulas in the
conclusion of the rule which are not in the context.
\par A calculus is {\it single-conclusion} if every meta-sequent in its axioms and rules is single-conclusion. Meta-sequents above the line are called the \emph{premises} and the meta-sequent below the line is called the \emph{conclusion} of the rule.
\par We define $\sigma S$ to be the {\it$\sigma$-instance} of $S$ and $\sigma S_{1}\dots\sigma S_{n}/\sigma S_{0}$ to be the {\it$\sigma$-instance} of rule $S_{1}\dots S_{n}/S_{0}$.
Recall the definition of substitution from Section~\ref{sec:logics}.
\par A {\it derivation} (or \emph{proof}) of $S$ in a calculus {\sf G}\ is a tree labelled with sequents in such a way that the root has the label $S$ and there is a substitution $\sigma$ such that all leaves of the tree are $\sigma$-instances of axioms of {\sf G}\ and for every non-leaf node in the tree with label $S^{\prime}$ and immediate successors labelled $S_{1},\dots,S_{n}$, there is a rule in {\sf G}\ such that $S_{1}\dots S_{n}/S^{\prime}$ is a $\sigma$-instance of that rule. A sequent $S$ is {\it derivable} (or \emph{provable}) in {\sf G}\ if it has a derivation in {\sf G}, in which case we write $\vdash_{\sf G}S$. The {\it depth} of a derivation is the length of its longest branch. We write $\vdash_{m}S$ to denote that $S$ has a derivation of depth at most $m$. A rule $R$ is \emph{admissible} in ${\sf G}$, if for any $\sigma$-instance $S_{1}\dots S_{n}/S_{0}$ of $R$, if for any $1\leq i\leq n$ we have $\vdash_{\sf G}S_{i}$ then $\vdash_{\sf G}S_{0}$.
The rule $R$ is \emph{depth-preserving admissible} in ${\sf G}$, if for all $m$ and $1\leq i\leq n$ we have ${\sf G}\vdash_{m}S_{i}$ then ${\sf G}\vdash_{m}S_{0}$.
\par\par We say that a calculus {\sf G}\ is a {\it calculus for logic {\sf L}} or \emph{{\sf L}\ is the logic of {\sf G}} if the formula interpretation of the sequents provable in {\sf G}\ are derivable in {\sf L}:
$$\vdash_{\sf G}(\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta)\text{ if and only if }\vdash_{\sf L}(\bigwedge\Gamma\to\bigvee\Delta).$$Specifically, {\sf G}\ derives the sequent version of all formulas that are derivable in {\sf L}:
$$\vdash_{\sf L}\varphi\text{ if and only if }\vdash_{\sf G}(\ \Rightarrow\varphi).$$\par Let $G$ and $H$ be two sequent calculi. We say $H$ is an \emph{extension} of $G$ when $G\subseteq H$.

The difference between a sequence of formulas and a multiset of formulas is that in a sequence the order of formulas matters. Gentzen's calculus {\sf LK}\ consists of {\sf G1c}\ (but then with $\Gamma$ and $\Delta$ standing for sequences of formulas) plus the following two rules of {\it Exchange}:
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.89354pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\varphi,\psi,\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to67.2218pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$LE$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\psi,\varphi,\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.89354pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta,\varphi,\psi,\Sigma$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to66.96626pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$RE$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta,\psi,\varphi,\Sigma$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$Nowadays, it is more common to use sequents that consist of multisets or even sets, and in this paper, we consider the multiset version most of the time.
\par Second, besides the weakening, contraction, and exchange rules, {\sf LK}\ contains one other structural rule, the {\it Cut rule}:
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.84352pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta,\varphi$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\varphi,\Sigma\Rightarrow\Pi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to106.63934pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt\footnotesize Cut}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 24.46152pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Sigma\Rightarrow\Delta,\Pi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$\par The formula $\varphi$ is the {\it cutformula} of the inference. The Cut rule is structural in that no connective is introduced or appears. But it is special in another sense too: there is a formula, namely $\varphi$, that ``disappears'' in the conclusion. For all the other rules in the calculus ${\sf LK}$, and thus for all the rules of {\sf G1c}, any formula that occurs in the premises of a rule occurs as a subformula in the conclusion, where we consider $\varphi(t)$ to be a subformula of $\exists x\varphi(x)$ and $\forall x\varphi(x)$. This property is the {\it subformula property}, which {\sf G1c}\ thus satisfies and {\sf LK}\ does not.
\par Note that, therefore, in any derivation in {\sf G1c}\ of a sequent $S$, any predicate that occurs in the proof occurs in $S$. One could see this as a form of {\it purity of proof}, a notion in (the philosophy of) mathematics, where a proof of a theorem is {\it pure} if it does not use notions alien to the theorem. An example of a proof that is not pure is the topological proof of the infinitude of primes. For more on the notion of purity, see e.g. \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{arana&detlefsen11,iemhoff17a,kahle&pulcini18}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\par Thus, the absence of the Cut rule has interesting consequences. But so does having the Cut rule in a sequent calculus. On a very general level, the Cut rule is considered to capture the use of lemmas in mathematics: here, the cut formula $\varphi$ is the lemma, and if the mathematician shows that assumptions $\Gamma$ imply the lemma and that the lemma implies $\psi$, then the mathematician is justified to conclude that $\psi$ follows from assumptions $\Gamma$:
$$\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.24075pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\text{lemma}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\text{lemma}\Rightarrow\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to135.04506pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt\footnotesize Cut}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 49.36284pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$The beautiful and extremely useful fact about the Cut rule is that it can be eliminated from proofs in {\sf LK}, as shown by \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{gentzen35}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. If ${\sf LK^{-}}$ denotes ${\sf LK}$ minus the Cut rule, then
\par\begin{theorem}{(Gentzen)}
If $\vdash_{{\sf LK}}S$, then $\vdash_{{\sf LK^{-}}}S$.
\end{theorem}Theorem \ref{thm: cut elim}, as stated above, shows the admissibility of cut in {\sf LK}. Gentzen indeed proved something stronger, namely the \emph{cut-elimination} theorem. He provided an algorithm to transform any proof $\pi$ of a sequent $S$ in {\sf LK}\ into a proof $\pi^{\prime}$ of $S$ in ${\sf LK^{-}}$, which means that the cut rule is not used in $\pi^{\prime}$. His proof was based on certain global transformations as well as some local transformation steps, including the permutation of a rule upwards or replacing a cut on a formula with some cuts on its immediate subformulas.
\par In the systems {\sf G1c},{\sf G1i}, and their propositional parts {\sf G1cp},{\sf G1ip}, the same holds, but since the cut rule is not a rule in these systems, one speaks of {\it cut-admissibility} rather than cut-elimination ({\sf{\sf G}+Cut} denotes the sequent calculus {\sf G}\ to which the Cut rule is added):
\par\begin{theorem}For any ${\sf G}\in\{{\sf G1c},{\sf G1i},{\sf G1cp},{\sf G1ip}\}$:
$\vdash_{\sf{\sf G}+Cut}S$ implies $\vdash_{\sf{\sf G}}S$.
\end{theorem}\par A derivation without cuts is {\it cut-free}.
\par Because of the admissibility of Cut we can use or omit it whenever the setting requires so. When we easily want to combine derivations to form new ones, Cut comes in handy: when we have shown that $\varphi\Rightarrow\psi$ and $\neg\varphi\Rightarrow\psi$, then we have a derivation of $(\ \Rightarrow\psi)$ from a derivation of $(\ \Rightarrow\varphi\vee\neg\varphi)$, an application of $(L\vee)$ and Cut. It may well be that a cut-free derivation of $\psi$ has a very different form and cannot be obtained easily from the given derivations.
\par On the other hand, suppose we want to show the consistency of {\sf CQC}\ by using {\sf G1c}+{\sf Cut}. Then, the structural rules and Cut, in particular, are a nuisance because they spoil bottom-up proof search in the calculus. This becomes clear when it is shown that the calculus that is introduced next, {\sf G3cp}, has good computational properties. For now, let us say that having a calculus with many of the good properties of {\sf G1c}\ but without the structural rules is highly desirable. This brings us to the sequent calculus {\sf G3c}\ given in Figure~\ref{fig:Gthq}. Its intuitionistic variant {\sf G3i}\ is given in Figure~\ref{fig:Gthqi}, and is essentially nothing but {\sf G3c}\ with the requirement that all sequents are single-conclusion, and a reformulation of $(R\vee)$ and $(L\!\rightarrow)$. The first one needs no argument, and the form of the second one will become clear in Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion}.
\par{\sf G3cp}\ ({\sf G3ip}) is the propositional version of {\sf G3c}\ ({\sf G3i}), so for the propositional language and without the rules for the quantifiers. For the curious reader, there are also sequent calculi ${\sf G2cp}$ and ${\sf G2ip}$, which are obtained from ${\sf G1cp}$ and ${\sf G1ip}$, respectively, by considering a generalized version of their axioms and leaving out the weakening rules. There are a few things to note here. Indeed, {\sf G3c}\ contains no structural rules. The axioms have changed in that they are generalized to have arbitrary multiset variables on both sides. The rules for the connectives remain unchanged except for $(R\vee)$. For {\sf G3i}, there are two more differences with {\sf G1i}, and that is the duplicate of the principal formula in the premise of $(R\exists)$ and $(L\forall)$, which causes a {\it hidden contraction}. In the proof of the admissibility of the structural rules in {\sf G3cp}\ and {\sf G3c}, Lemma~\ref{lem:structuralrules}, we will see the reason for these adjustments. But first, we need the inversion lemma.
\par\par\par\begin{lemma}({\it Inversion Lemma})\\
Let $\vdash$ denote derivability in {\sf G3c}\ (for $\vdash_{n}$ see Subsection~\ref{sec:seqcaldef}). Then,
\begin{description}\description@item@[$(L\en)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\wedge\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi,\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(R\en)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\wedge\psi,\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\Delta$ and $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\psi,\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(L\of)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\vee\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$ and $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(R\of)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi,\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(L\!\imp)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\rightarrow\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\Delta$ and $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(R\!\imp)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\rightarrow\psi,\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\Rightarrow\psi,\Delta$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(L\E)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\exists x\varphi(x)\Rightarrow\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi(y)\Rightarrow\Delta$ for all $y$ not in $\Gamma,\Delta,\varphi$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[$(R\A)$]$\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\forall x\varphi(x),\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi(y),\Delta$ for all $y$ not in $\Gamma,\Delta,\varphi$.
\end{description}The same holds for {\sf G3ip}, in which case sequents are single-conclusion, which means that in {\sf G3ip}\, $(R\vee)$ does not hold, and in the case $(L\!\rightarrow)$ only $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$ can be concluded from $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\varphi\rightarrow\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$.
\end{lemma}\begin{proof}Left to the reader, or see Proposition 3.5.4 in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{troelstra&schwichtenberg00}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\end{proof}\par\par\begin{lemma}({\it Weakening and Contraction Lemma})
\\
Weakening and Contraction are depth-preserving admissible in {\sf G3cp}\ and {\sf G3c}, and in {\sf G3ip}\ and {\sf G3i}.
\end{lemma}\begin{proof}We treat {\sf G3c}, the other three calculi can be treated in the same way. $\vdash$ stands for derivability in {\sf G3c}, and $\vdash_{n}S$ means that $S$ has a derivation of depth at most $n$ in {\sf G3c}.
\par{\it Weakening} We have to show that if $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta$, then $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ and $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$, for any formula $\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}$. We prove this by induction on $n$. If $\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta$ is an axiom, then $\Gamma,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ and $\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$ are instances of the same axioms, which proves the statement. If $\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta$ is not an axiom, consider the last inference of the derivation. We distinguish by cases according to the rule $R$ of which the last inference is an instance. Suppose $R$ is $L\vee$, then the last inference looks as follows, where $\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi\vee\psi$:
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.43019pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to112.11432pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$L\vee$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 23.48444pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi\vee\psi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$By the induction hypothesis $\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ and $\Gamma^{\prime},\psi,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ have derivations of at most depth $n-1$. An application of $L\vee$ to these sequents shows that $\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi\vee\psi,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ has a derivation of depth at most $n$. The case for right weakening is analogous. The other connectives and the quantifiers can be treated in exactly the same way. Note that for the strong quantifiers (i.e., positive occurrences of the universal quantifier and
negative occurrences of the existential quantifier), with the condition on the variable, the eigenvariables may have to be changed in order to not interfere with the variables in $\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}$. We leave the details to the reader.
\par{\it Contraction} We have to show that $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}\Rightarrow\Delta$, and that $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$ implies $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$. We treat the second case, contraction on the right, and use induction on $n$. If $\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$ is an axiom, then $\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$ is an instance of the same axiom, which proves the statement. If $\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta$ is not an axiom, consider the last inference of the derivation. We distinguish by cases according to the rule $R$ of which the last inference is an instance. Suppose $R$ is $R\vee$, then there are two possibilities for the last inference, either $\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}$ is the main formula or not:
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.43019pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 3.08331pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to81.81894pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\vee$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi,\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.43019pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 3.08331pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\varphi\vee\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to91.60916pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\vee$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi,\varphi\vee\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$In the first case, the induction hypothesis applies to the premise, and then an application of $R\vee$ gives
$\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi,\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta^{\prime}$, as desired. In the second case we can apply the Inversion Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion} to the premise $\vdash_{n-1}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\varphi\vee\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$ and obtain $\vdash_{n-1}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\varphi,\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$. By applying the induction hypothesis twice, once to $\varphi$ and once to $\psi$, we obtain
$\vdash_{n-1}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi,\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$. An application of $R\vee$ proves $\vdash_{n}\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi,\Delta^{\prime}$.
\par The other connectives can be treated in exactly the same way.
In the treatment of $L\!\rightarrow$ for ${\sf G3ip}$ the use of the main formula in the premise will become clear.
\par From the quantifiers, we only treat $R\exists$. Thus, the last inference can have the following two forms:
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.38574pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi(t),\exists x\varphi(x),\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to110.55351pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\exists$}}}\vskip-0.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 10.34299pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\exists x\varphi(x),\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}},\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ \ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.38574pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi(t),\exists x\varphi(x),\exists x\varphi(x),\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to128.39233pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\exists$}}}\vskip-0.27591pt\hbox{\hskip 10.34299pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow\exists x\varphi(x),\exists x\varphi(x),\Delta^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$In both cases, we can apply the induction hypothesis to the premise to remove a $\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}$ or $\exists x\varphi(x)$, respectively, and then apply $R\exists$ to obtain the desired conclusion.
\end{proof}\par\begin{remark}Note that in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:structuralrules} it is essential that in $R\exists$ the main formula is copied in the premise. The same remark holds for $L\forall$.
\end{remark}\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Cut-elimination}
We have seen that weakening and contraction are admissible in the four calculi {\sf G3cp}, {\sf G3c}, {\sf G3ip}, and {\sf G3i}.
Also, the other admissible rule, Cut, is admissible in them, but that is far harder to prove. We do not give the proof here, there are many in the literature, for example in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{troelstra&schwichtenberg00}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\par\par\begin{theorem}({\it Cut-elimination})
\\
For {\sf X} being any of the calculi {\sf G3cp}, {\sf G3c}, {\sf G3ip}, {\sf G3i}, and for all sequents $S$:
$$\vdash_{\sf X+Cut}S\ \text{ implies }\vdash_{\sf X}S.$$In other words, Cut is admissible in these calculi. Moreover, there is an algorithm that given a proof of $S$ in $\sf X+Cut$ provides a proof for $S$ in $\sf X$.
\end{theorem}\par Thus, with the {\sf G3}-calculi we have proof systems without structural rules but with the power of these rules. A striking example is {\sf G3cp}, in which proof search is terminating without any side conditions on the search (for a definition of termination, see Subsection \ref{Sec: Terminating}). Proof search in {\sf G3ip}\ is also terminating, but under an additional side condition that we will not dwell on here.
\par The cut-elimination has numerous consequences, among which are the following two. Theorem~\ref{thm:dec} illustrates the usefulness of sequent calculi to prove properties about a logic, a phenomenon we also encounter in the sections on interpolation. Theorem~\ref{thm:equal} shows that for {\sf CPC}\ as well as {\sf IPC}\ there exist two sequent calculi, each of which has properties that make it useful in a given context, as explained in Subsection~\ref{sec:structural}.
\par\begin{theorem}Derivability in {\sf G3cp}\ and {\sf G3ip}\ is decidable. Thus, {\sf CPC}\ and {\sf IPC}\ are decidable.
\end{theorem}\par\begin{proof}For the decidability of {\sf IPC}\ via a bottom-up proof search in {\sf G3ip}\ see Theorem 4.2.6 in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{troelstra&schwichtenberg00}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}. The result for {\sf G3cp}\ and {\sf CPC}\ is similar and in fact easier.
\end{proof}\par\begin{theorem}The sequent calculi {\sf G1cp}({\sf G1c}) and {\sf G3cp}({\sf G3c}) prove the same sequents. So do {\sf G1ip}({\sf G1i}) and {\sf G3ip}({\sf G3i}).
\end{theorem}\par\begin{proof}See Proposition 3.5.9 in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{troelstra&schwichtenberg00}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\end{proof}\par Theorem~\ref{thm:equal} implies that {\sf G1ip}\ does not derive the law of excluded middle and Peirce's law, as predicted in Subsection~\ref{sec:gonei}. This follows from the fact that {\sf G3ip}\ does not derive these principles, a fact that is not hard to establish, given that {\sf G3ip}\ does not contain structural rules: a proof of $(\ \Rightarrow p\vee\neg p)$ in {\sf G3ip}\ necessarily has to end with an application of $R\vee$, but then the premise would be $(\ \Rightarrow p)$ or $(\ \Rightarrow\neg p)$, two clearly underivable sequents. The argument for Peirce's law is similar.
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Sequent Calculi for Other Logics}
\par We have seen several elegant sequent calculi for classical and intuitionistic logic. What is a beautiful fact is that there are many logics, be it intermediate, modal, conditional, or substructural, that have sequent calculi that are extensions of or are similar to the calculi we have seen. These calculi are as well-behaved as the ones discussed above, and therefore useful in the study of the mentioned logics.
\par The discussion below describes sequent calculi for intermediate, modal, and conditional logics. Because of the lack of space, the usual calculi for standard substructural logics are not given. They are as elegant and useful as the calculi that are discussed here, pointers to the literature are given below and see \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Ono}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Existence of Sequent Calculi}
We have introduced proof systems for {\sf IPC}\ and {\sf CPC}, namely the sequent calculi {\sf G1cp}, {\sf G3cp}, {\sf G1ip}, {\sf G3ip}, and argued that they are good proof systems, at least in a given context.
Thus, the question arises: which other logics have sequent calculi with certain desirable properties, such as the absence but admissibility of structural rules, or termination?
A positive answer to that question for a given logic is often provided by developing a proof system for that logic and proving that it has the desirable properties one wishes it to have. But for a negative answer, in case the logic does not have good sequent calculi, it is not as clear how one should obtain such a result. How to prove that no sequent calculus from the class of calculi considered ``good'' can be a calculus for the logic?
\par Although the answer to that question naturally depends on the precise definition of ``good'' that one wishes to use, the general methodology to approach this question as described in this section applies to various interpretations of ``good''. However, because the method can be better explained when applied to a concrete case, we consider a calculus good when it is terminating, and comment on the general case later on.
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Terminating Calculi}
\par A given calculus {\sf G}\ for a (modal) propositional logic is {\it terminating} if it satisfies the following properties:
\par\begin{description}\description@item@[finite]{\sf G}\ consists of finitely many axioms and rules;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[instance finite]for every sequent $S$ there are at most finitely many instances of rules in {\sf G}\ of which $S$ is the conclusion;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[well-ordered]there is a well-order $<$ on sequents such that
\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@[$\circ$]every proper subsequent comes before the sequent in $<$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[$\circ$]for every rule in {\sf G}\ the premises come before the conclusion in $<$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[$\circ$]any sequent of the form $(\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta,\Lambda)$, where $\Pi\cup\Lambda$ is nonempty comes before a sequent of the form $(\Gamma,\Box\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta,\Box\Lambda)$.
\end{itemize}\end{description}\par Thus, we arrive at the question of which logics have a terminating calculus, and in particular at the problem of how to establish, for logics that do not have such calculi, the fact that they do not. The latter results, stating that a certain logic does not have a terminating calculus, we call {\it negative results}.

\par When turning to logics based on intuitionistic logic one naturally turns to the calculus {\sf G3ip}\ for {\sf IPC}\ and tries to extend it to a calculus for the logic one is considering, it being intermediate or an intuitionistic modal logic. \par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Method Towards Negative Results}
How can one prove negative results, results stating that certain logics do not have terminating calculi? Here we introduce a possible method, one that is easy to explain but requires ingenuity to apply. It can be applied not only to the class of terminating sequent calculi, but to any class of sequent calculi $\mathcal{S}$ that one is interested in, and we will formulate it in this general form.
\par Consider a class of sequent calculi $\mathcal{S}$ (e.g.\ the terminating sequent calculi) and a class of logics $\mathcal{C}$ (e.g.\ intermediate, modal, etc.). Suppose that there is a property of logics $\mathcal{P}$ and a subset $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ of $\mathcal{C}$ such that one can show the following:
\begin{description}\description@item@[\rm(I)]Any logic in $\mathcal{C}$ that has a sequent calculus in $\mathcal{S}$ has property $\mathcal{P}$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[\rm(II)]No logic in $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ has property $\mathcal{P}$.
\end{description}Then, one can conclude by simple contraposition:
\begin{description}\description@item@[\rm(III)]No logic in $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ has a sequent calculus in $\mathcal{S}$ (conclusion).
\end{description}If the property $\mathcal{P}$ is rare, meaning that not many logics in $\mathcal{C}$ have $\mathcal{P}$, and the class of logics $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ and the class of calculi $\mathcal{S}$ are large, then the conclusion states that there is a large class of logics, namely $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$, such that for no logic in that class can there be a sequent calculus in the large class $\mathcal{S}$. Exactly the kind of negative results we are looking for.
Thus, the general aim is to find $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ that are large and a property $\mathcal{P}$ that is relatively rare among the logics in $\mathcal{C}$.
\par In this paper, the property $\mathcal{P}$ that we are mostly considering is (Craig, uniform, Lyndon) interpolation, defined in Subsections~\ref{sec:inter} and \ref{sec:uip} and Section~\ref{sec:positive}. The class of sequent calculi $\mathcal{S}$ is, in most settings, a subclass of the (terminating) calculi, usually the largest class of (terminating) calculi to which the method has been successfully applied, but not ruling out that there are more calculi for which (I) holds. The results of (II) we mainly obtain from the literature. These are the results stating that certain logics do not have (Craig, uniform, Lyndon) interpolation. Since these results are often proved with tools from algebraic logic, the whole method shows a nice interplay between that area and proof theory.
\par Uniform (Lyndon) interpolation is rarer than interpolation and therefore the better choice for the property $\mathcal{P}$. However, for this exposition, we have chosen to illustrate the method for the case that $\mathcal{P}$ is the property of interpolation. The advantage is that this case still shows the spirit of the method, but without the technical complexities and conceptual difficulties that come with the study of uniform interpolation. References to papers on the full method will be given during the exposition.
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Intermediate Logics}
We describe the details of the above method, introduced in Subsection~\ref{sec:method}, to obtain negative results for the class of intermediate logics ($\mathcal{C}$). The class of calculi $\mathcal{S}$ that we use is a subclass of the class of terminating sequent calculi, to be defined below. As the property $\mathcal{P}$ we use interpolation.
\par Let us remind the reader that a propositional (modal) logic ${\sf L}$ has {\it Craig Interpolation Property} (CIP), also called {interpolation}, if for every implication $\varphi\rightarrow\psi$ that is derivable in ${\sf L}$ there exists a formula $\alpha$ (the {\it interpolant}) such that both $\varphi\rightarrow\alpha$ and $\alpha\rightarrow\psi$ are derivable in ${\sf L}$ and $\alpha$ is in the {\it common language} of $\varphi$ and $\psi$, meaning that every atom that occurs in $\alpha$ occurs in both $\varphi$ and $\psi$.
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsubsection}{toc}{Sequent Interpolation for \Gthi\ }
We start with a proof-theoretic proof of interpolation for {\sf IPC}. The proof uses the calculus {\sf G3ip}\ and the following sequent version from which the standard interpolation property follows.
A calculus {\sf G}\ has {\it sequent interpolation} if for every partitioned sequent $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ (partitions are defined in Subsection~\ref{sec:seq}) there is a formula $\alpha$ in the common language of $(\Gamma\Rightarrow\ )$ and $(\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta)$ such that $\Gamma\Rightarrow\alpha$ and $\Pi,\alpha\Rightarrow\Delta$ are derivable in {\sf G}. Here the common language of $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ is by definition the common language of $\Gamma\Rightarrow\ $ and $\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$, which means the atoms that occur in some formula in $\Gamma$ as well as in some formula of $\Pi$ or $\Delta$.
\par The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dycint}, which states that {\sf G3ip}\ has sequent interpolation, uses two lemmas that we prove first.
\par\begin{lemma}Every partition of every instance of an axiom of {\sf G3ip}\ has sequent interpolation.
\end{lemma}\begin{proof}Let $S=(\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta)$ be a partition of an instance of an axiom of {\sf G3ip}.
In case $S$ is an instance of $L\bot$, then the interpolant can be $\top$ in case $\bot\in\Pi$ and $\bot$ otherwise. In case $S$ is an instance of the other axiom, there are several cases to consider: if $p\in\Pi\cap\Delta$, then $\top$ is an interpolant, and if $p\in\Gamma\cap\Delta$, then $p$ is in the common language and an interpolant.
\end{proof}\par\begin{lemma}For every instance of a rule ${\mathcal{R}}$ in {\sf G3ip}, for every partition $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ of its conclusion there are partitions $\Gamma_{i};\Pi_{i}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}$ of its $n$ premises and a formula $\gamma(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})$ such that $\gamma(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})$ is an interpolant of $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ whenever $\alpha_{i}$ is an interpolant of $\Gamma_{i};\Pi_{i}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}$ for every $i=1,\dots,n$.
\end{lemma}\begin{proof}We treat the case that ${\mathcal{R}}$ is $R\vee$, $R\wedge$, and $R\!\rightarrow$. In the first case, the last inference is
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.84352pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 8.1562pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to61.56906pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\vee$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$Let $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi$ be the partition of the conclusion. Consider the partition $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi$ of the premise and suppose $\beta$ is its interpolant. Thus, $\Gamma\Rightarrow\beta$ and $\Pi,\beta\Rightarrow\varphi$ are derivable, which implies that so is $\Pi,\beta\Rightarrow\varphi\vee\psi$. Hence, $\beta$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion and let $\gamma(p_{1})$ be $p_{1}$.
In the second case, the last inference is
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.84352pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi_{2}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to113.66699pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\wedge$}}}\vskip-0.23146pt\hbox{\hskip 22.72401pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$Let $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}$ be the partition of the conclusion. Consider the partitions $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi_{i}$ of the premises and suppose the $\alpha_{i}$ are their interpolants. Thus, $\Gamma\Rightarrow\alpha_{i}$ and $\Pi,\alpha_{i}\Rightarrow\varphi$ are derivable, which implies that so are $\Gamma\Rightarrow\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}$ and $\Pi,\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\Rightarrow\varphi\wedge\psi$. Hence, $\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion and let $\gamma(p_{1},p_{2})$ be $p_{1}\wedge p_{2}$.
\par In the third case, $R\!\rightarrow$, the last inference is of the form
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.75908pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 2.82637pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi,\varphi\Rightarrow\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.36592pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to61.05518pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.3659pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$R\!\rightarrow$}}}\vskip-0.3659pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi\rightarrow\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$Let $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\varphi\to\psi$ be the partition of the conclusion. Consider the partition $\Gamma;\Pi,\varphi\Rightarrow\psi$ of the premise and let $\beta$ be its interpolant. Hence, we have derivable sequents $\Gamma\Rightarrow\beta$ and $\Pi,\varphi,\beta\Rightarrow\psi$. Thus, $\beta$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion, as $\beta$ is in the common language of the partition of the premise, and let $\gamma(p_{1})$ be $p_{1}$.
\end{proof}\par\begin{theorem}{\sf G3ip}\ has sequent interpolation: For every partitioned sequent $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ derivable in {\sf G3ip}\ there is a formula $\alpha$ in the common language of $(\Gamma\Rightarrow\ )$ and $(\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta)$ such that $\Gamma\Rightarrow\alpha$ and $\Pi,\alpha\Rightarrow\Delta$ are derivable in ${\sf G3ip}$.
\end{theorem}\begin{proof}We use induction on the depth of the derivation of $S=(\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta)$. The case that $S$ is an instance of an axiom is covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:axint}. If $S$ is not an axiom and the conclusion of an application of a rule in {\sf G3ip}, then Lemma~\ref{lem:dycint} applies, which completes the proof.
\end{proof}\par The above is a constructive proof of the following well-known theorem for {\sf IPC}. \par\par\begin{corollary}{\sf IPC}\ has Craig interpolation.
\end{corollary}\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsubsection}{toc}{Generalization of Sequent Interpolation for \Gdyc}
A similar result as in Theorem \ref{thm:dycint} can be proved for the terminating sequent calculus {\sf G4ip}.
\begin{theorem}{\sf G4ip}\ has sequent interpolation: For every partitioned sequent $\Gamma;\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$ derivable in {\sf G4ip}\ there is a formula $\alpha$ in the common language of $(\Gamma\Rightarrow\ )$ and $(\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta)$ such that $\Gamma\Rightarrow\alpha$ and $\Pi,\alpha\Rightarrow\Delta$ are derivable in ${\sf G4ip}$.
\end{theorem}\begin{proof}We use the analog of Lemma \ref{lem:dycint} for {\sf G4ip}.
The new cases for the rule ${\mathcal{R}}$, compared to the proof of Lemma \ref{lem:dycint}, are the rules $Lp\!\rightarrow$ and $L\!\rightarrow\rightarrow$.
\par In the case $Lp\!\rightarrow$, the last inference is of the form
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.51796pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 7.2036pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime},p,\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-1.14369pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to85.84602pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 1.58813pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$Lp\!\rightarrow$}}}\vskip-1.14369pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime},p,p\rightarrow\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$In case $p,p\rightarrow\varphi$ belongs to the same partition, the interpolant of the conclusion is the same for the premise. We consider the two remaining cases. First suppose the partition of $S$ is $\Gamma^{\prime},p;\Pi^{\prime},p\rightarrow\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$ and let $\beta$ be the interpolant of the premise which is partitioned as $\Gamma^{\prime},p;\Pi^{\prime},\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta$. Since $p$ belongs to the common language of the partitioned $S$, $\beta\wedge p$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion and we take
$\gamma(p_{1})$ to be $p_{1}\wedge p$. In case the partition of $S$ is $\Gamma^{\prime},p\rightarrow\varphi;\Pi^{\prime},p\Rightarrow\Delta$, partition the premise as $\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi;\Pi^{\prime},p\Rightarrow\Delta$ and let $\beta$ be its interpolant. Since $p$ belongs to the common language of $S$, $p\rightarrow\beta$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion and we take $\gamma(p_{1})$ to be $p\rightarrow p_{1}$.
\par In the case $L\!\rightarrow\rightarrow$, the last inference is of the form
$$\small\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.29573pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime},\varphi_{2}\rightarrow\varphi_{3}\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime},\varphi_{3}\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.36592pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to181.23454pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.3659pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt{\footnotesize$L\!\rightarrow\rightarrow$}}}\vskip-0.3659pt\hbox{\hskip 35.91835pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime},(\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2})\rightarrow\varphi_{3}\Rightarrow\Delta$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$First, suppose the partition of $S$ is $\Gamma^{\prime};(\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2})\rightarrow\varphi_{3},\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Delta$ and let $\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}$ be the interpolants of the premises, which are partitioned as $\Gamma^{\prime};\varphi_{2}\rightarrow\varphi_{3},\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}$ and $\Gamma^{\prime};\varphi_{3},\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Delta$. Thus, $\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}$ satisfies the conditions of being an interpolant for the conclusion and we take $\gamma(p_{1},p_{2})$ to be $p_{1}\wedge p_{2}$, noting that $\alpha$ indeed belongs to the common language of the partitioned conclusion.
\par Second, suppose the partition of $S$ is $\Gamma^{\prime},(\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2})\rightarrow\varphi_{3};\Pi\Rightarrow\Delta$. Then, we consider
the partition $\Pi^{\prime};\varphi_{2}\rightarrow\varphi_{3},\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}$ of the left premise with interpolant $\alpha_{1}$. For the right premise we consider the partition $\Gamma^{\prime},\varphi_{3};\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\Delta$ and its interpolant $\alpha_{2}$. Thus, the following sequents are all derivable.
$$\small\begin{array}[]{llll}(1)&\Pi^{\prime}\Rightarrow\alpha_{1}&(2)&\alpha_{1},\varphi_{2}\rightarrow\varphi_{3},\Gamma^{\prime}\Rightarrow\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2}\\

$$\small\begin{array}[]{lll}(5)&\Gamma^{\prime},\alpha_{1},(\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2})\rightarrow\varphi_{3}\Rightarrow\alpha_{2}&\text{by }(2)\ \&\ (3)\\
(6)&\Gamma^{\prime},(\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\varphi_{2})\rightarrow\varphi_{3}\Rightarrow\alpha_{1}\rightarrow\alpha_{2}&\text{by }(5)\\

We let $\gamma(p_{1},p_{2})$ be $p_{1}\rightarrow p_{2}$.
\end{proof}\par If one considers the proof of sequent interpolation for {\sf G4ip}\ (Theorem~\ref{thm:G4ip}) then the cases $Lp\!\rightarrow$ and $L\!\rightarrow\rightarrow$ seem specific and the others general. This brings us to define a large and general class of rules, the semi-analytic rules. \par\begin{definition}In the following definitions, expression $\langle\,\langle\,\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{ik}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$ is short for the sets of premises
$$\langle\,\Gamma_{1},\overline{\varphi}_{1k}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{1k}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{1}}\ \ \dots\ \ \langle\,\Gamma_{m},\overline{\varphi}_{mk}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{mk}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{m}},$$where $\langle\,\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{ik}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{i}}$ is short for the set of premises
$$\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{i1}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{i1}\ \ \Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{i2}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{i2}\ \ \dots\ \ \Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ia_{i}}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{ia_{i}}.$$Likewise for expressions $\langle\,\langle\,\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$. By $\overline{\psi}$, we mean a multiset of formulas $\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\}$.
\par A sequent rule ${\mathcal{R}}$ is {left semi-analytic} if it is of the following form:
\begin{equation}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 12.58022pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\Pi_{j},\overline{\psi}_{jl}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{jl}\,\rangle_{l=1}^{b_{j}}\,\rangle_{j=1}^{n}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.9148pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to229.7919pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt${\mathcal{R}}$}}\vskip-0.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 31.68939pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{m},\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{n},\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces\end{equation}A {right semi-analytic} rule is of the following form:
\begin{equation}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.8352pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\Gamma_{i},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{ik}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{a_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.9148pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to107.83458pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt${\mathcal{R}}$}}\vskip-0.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 19.77203pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{m}\Rightarrow\varphi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces\end{equation}Here the $\Gamma_{i},\Pi_{j},\Delta_{i}$ are multiset variables, and we have the following respective variable conditions for the left and the right rules:
$$\bigcup_{ik}V(\overline{\varphi}_{ik})\cup\bigcup_{jl}V(\overline{\psi}_{jl})\cup\bigcup_{jl}V(\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{jl})\subseteq V(\varphi)\ ,\ \bigcup_{ik}V(\overline{\varphi}_{ik})\cup\bigcup_{ik}V(\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{ik})\subseteq V(\varphi).$$Recall that we consider single-conclusion sequents, so that the $\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{m}$ are empty or consist of one formula, meaning that at most one of the $\Delta_{i}$ can be nonempty. A left semi-analytic rule as above is {\it context-sharing} if $m=n$ and $\Gamma_{i}=\Pi_{i}$ for all $i$.
A rule is {\it semi-analytic} if it is either right or left semi-analytic.
\end{definition}\par Note that the left and right conjunction and disjunction rules and the right implication rule of {\sf G3ip}\ (and thus of {\sf G4ip}) are semi-analytic rules. The rules $L\!\rightarrow$ of {\sf G3ip}\ and $L\!\rightarrow\rightarrow$ of {\sf G4ip}\ are context-sharing semi-analytic rules. Regarding the other left implication rules of {\sf G4ip}, the rule $Lp\!\rightarrow$ is not semi-analytic, but the rules $L\wedge\!\rightarrow$ and $L\vee\!\rightarrow$ are.

$$\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.84412pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\alpha,\Pi_{i}^{\prime},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{i}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip-0.9148pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to115.50584pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt${\mathcal{R}}$}}\vskip-0.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 13.1389pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\alpha^{m},\Pi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\Pi_{m}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\varphi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$Hence, $\vdash_{{\sf L}}\alpha,\Pi_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\Pi_{m}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\varphi$.

\end{enumerate}\end{lemma}\begin{proof}1. Let $\gamma$ denote $\bigwedge_{ik}\alpha_{ik}\wedge\bigwedge_{jl}\beta_{jl}$. By assumption we have
$$\vdash_{{\sf L}}\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\alpha_{ik}\ ,\ \vdash_{{\sf L}}\alpha_{ik},\overline{\varphi}_{ik},\Gamma_{i}^{\prime\prime}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}\ ,\ \vdash_{{\sf L}}\Pi_{j}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\beta_{jl}\ ,\ \vdash_{{\sf L}}\beta_{jl},\overline{\psi}_{jl},\Pi_{j}^{\prime\prime}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{j}.$$Hence, $\vdash_{{\sf L}}\overline{\Gamma}_{i}^{\prime},\overline{\Pi}_{i}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\gamma$. $$\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 11.84412pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\gamma,\Gamma_{i}^{\prime\prime},\overline{\varphi}_{ik}\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}\,\rangle_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\,\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\langle\,\langle\,\gamma,\Pi_{j}^{\prime\prime},\overline{\psi}_{jl}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{j}\,\rangle_{l=1}^{n_{j}}\,\rangle_{j=1}^{n}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip-0.9148pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to249.14526pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower 2.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 3.0pt${\mathcal{R}}$}}\vskip-0.91478pt\hbox{\hskip 33.41286pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\gamma^{x},\Gamma_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,\Gamma_{m}^{\prime\prime},\Pi_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,\Pi_{n}^{\prime\prime},\varphi\Rightarrow\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{m}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\ignorespaces$$We can conclude that $\gamma$ is the desired interpolant, as the condition on its atoms follows from that of its conjuncts.
\par The proof of 2.\ is analogous.
\end{proof}\par Similar to the semi-analytic rules we define a class of axioms that includes the axioms of {\sf G3ip}\ or {\sf G4ip}\ and such that every partition of a sequent in the class has sequent interpolation.
\par\begin{definition}A sequent is a {\it focused} axiom if it has one of the following forms, where $|\Delta|\leq 1$:

\end{definition}\par\begin{lemma}Every partition of every focused axiom has sequent interpolation.
\end{lemma}\begin{proof}Straightforward checking of all cases.
\end{proof}\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsubsection}{toc}{Positive and Negative Results via Interpolation}

\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Negative Results via Uniform Interpolation}
Having seen the method at work for the class of intermediate logics, the question of whether it can be adapted to other logics naturally arises. What about normal modal logics, substructural logics, non-normal modal logics, and intuitionistic modal logics? To obtain negative results for these logics with the method described in Subsection~\ref{sec:method}, one could still use Craig interpolation as the property $\mathcal{P}$. But as was explained there, the more rare the property $\mathcal{P}$ is for the logics that one considers, the stronger the negative result is. We therefore turn to a strengthening of CIP, uniform interpolation, defined in the next section. For intermediate logics, the two notions coincide, but, as we will see, for modal logics this is no longer the case, and therefore, using the stronger notion effectively strengthens our results.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsubsection}{toc}{Uniform Interpolation}
A logic ${\sf L}$ has the {Uniform Interpolation Property (UIP)} if for any formula $\varphi$ and any atom $p$ there exist two formulas, usually denoted by $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi$ and $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi$, in the language of the logic, that do not contain $p$ or atoms not in $\varphi$ and such that for all $\psi$ not containing $p$:
$$\vdash\psi\rightarrow\varphi\ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \vdash\psi\rightarrow\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi\ \ \ \ \ \ \vdash\varphi\rightarrow\psi\ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \vdash\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi\rightarrow\psi.$$Given a formula $\varphi$, its {universal uniform interpolant with respect to $p_{1}\dots p_{n}$} is $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp_{1}\dots p_{n}\varphi$, which is short for $\forall p_{1}(\forall p_{2}(\dots(\forall p_{n}\varphi)\dots)$, and its {existential uniform interpolant with respect to $p_{1}\dots p_{n}$} is $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp_{1}\dots p_{n}\varphi$, which is short for $\exists p_{1}(\exists p_{2}(\dots(\exists p_{n}\varphi)\dots)$. The requirements above could be replaced by the following four requirements, that explain the names of the uniform interpolants:
$$\vdash\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\ \ \ \ \ \ \vdash\psi\rightarrow\varphi\ \Rightarrow\ \vdash\psi\rightarrow\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi.$$$$\vdash\varphi\rightarrow\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi\ \ \ \ \ \ \vdash\varphi\rightarrow\psi\ \Rightarrow\ \vdash\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi\rightarrow\psi.$$In classical logic, one only needs one quantifier, as $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt$ can be defined as $\neg\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\neg$ and vice versa. Although in the intuitionistic setting $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt$ can also be defined in terms of $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt$, namely as $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi=\forall q(\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt(\varphi\rightarrow q)\rightarrow q)$ for a $q$ not in $\varphi$, having it as a separate quantifier is convenient in the proof-theoretic approach presented here (we follow \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{pitts92}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, which also uses both quantifiers).
\par It is easy to see that uniform interpolation implies interpolation. For suppose $\vdash_{\sf L}\varphi\rightarrow\psi$, and let $\bar{p}=p_{1},\dots,p_{n}$ be all the atoms that occur in $\psi$ but not in $\varphi$. Hence, $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp_{1}\dots p_{n}\psi\rightarrow\psi$ and $\varphi\rightarrow\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp_{1}\dots p_{n}\psi$ are derivable in {\sf L}. Since all atoms in $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp_{1}\dots p_{n}\psi$ have to occur in both $\varphi$ and $\psi$, it is indeed an interpolant for $\varphi\rightarrow\psi$.
\par To define uniform interpolants in the setting of sequents, we introduce the notion of a partition, which applies to sequents and rules.
A {partition} of a sequent $S$ is an ordered pair $(S^{r},S^{i})$ ($i$ for {interpolant}, $r$ for {rest}) such that $S=S^{r}\cdot S^{i}$, where the {multiplication} of the sequents $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ is defined as $S_{1}\cdot S_{2}:=S_{1}^{a}\cup S_{2}^{a}\Rightarrow S_{1}^{s}\cup S_{2}^{s}$. It is a {$p$--partition} if $p$ does not occur in $S^{r}$.
A sequent calculus {\sf G}\ has {\it uniform sequent interpolation} if for all sequents $S$ and all atoms $p$ there are formulas $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$ and $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$ in ${\mathcal{L}}$ that do not contain $p$ nor any atoms that do not occur in $S$, and such that the following holds, where $\vdash$ stands for derivability in {\sf G}:
\par\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@[($\A$l)]$\vdash S^{a},\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS\Rightarrow S^{s}$;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[($\E$r)]$\vdash S^{a}\Rightarrow\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[($\A\E$)]If $S$ is derivable, for all $p$--partitions $(S^{r},S^{i})$ of $S$: $\vdash S^{r}\cdot(\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i}\Rightarrow\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i})$.
\end{itemize}In the case of a single-conclusion sequent calculus, the last requirement is replaced by
$$\begin{array}[]{ll}\vdash S^{r}\cdot(\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i}\Rightarrow\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i})&\text{if $S^{s}\neq\varnothing$ and $S^{rs}=\varnothing$}\\
\vdash S^{r}\cdot(\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i}\Rightarrow\ )&\text{if $S^{s}=\varnothing$ or $S^{rs}\neq\varnothing$.}\end{array}$$Properties ($\forall$l) and ($\exists$r) are the {independent} (from partitions) {interpolant properties}, and ($\forall\exists$) is the {dependent interpolant property}. It is not hard to see that whenever a logic ${\sf L}$ has a sequent calculus that has uniform sequent interpolation, ${\sf L}$ has uniform interpolation: for $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\psi$ we take $\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt(\ \Rightarrow\psi)$ and for $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt\varphi$ we take $\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905pt(\varphi\Rightarrow\ )$.
\par\begin{example}In classical logic:
$$\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{sequent }S&\forall\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS&\exists\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS\\
\\
\text{ if }\vdash S&\top&\bot\\
p\Rightarrow q&q&\neg q\\
q\Rightarrow p&\neg q&q\end{array}$$\end{example}\par\par For intermediate logics, the two notions of interpolation coincide (\cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{ghilardi&zawadowski02}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}), but for modal logics they do not. For example, {\sf K4}\ and {\sf S4}\ have interpolation but not uniform interpolation \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{bilkova07, ghilardi&zawadowski95}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}. The difference between the two notions also shows in the difficulty of proving them. For interpolation, these are often relatively straightforward proofs but proofs of uniform interpolation tend to be hard. The proof by \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{pitts92}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} for {\sf IPC}\ is complicated, as are the semantics proofs of uniform interpolation for modal logics that appeared after that. Pitts' proof is proof-theoretic and forms the inspiration for the proof-theoretic proofs of the theorems presented in this exposition.

The method presented in Section~\ref{sec:exiseqcal} can also be used to prove positive instead of negative results, meaning results stating that a logic actually does have uniform interpolation. In this section, we state some of the results of this form.
\par\par The flexibility of the method presented here is illustrated by the fact that it can be used to prove positive results (results stating that a logic has UIP) for many logics as well. We state some of the results of this form.
In most cases, it appears that we can even prove a strengthening of uniform interpolation, namely uniform Lyndon interpolation,
in which the interpolant respects the polarity of propositional variables (the definition follows below). It first occurred in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{kurahashi20}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, where it was shown that several normal modal logics, including $\mathsf{K}$ and $\mathsf{KD}$, have that property.
We define uniform Lyndon interpolation for sequent calculi, but from that definition, one can easily read off the corresponding notion for logics.
\par Given a sequent $S$, let $V^{+}(\varphi)$ ($V^{-}(\varphi)$) denote the atoms that occur positively (negatively) in $S$ (defined as usual, reading $\rightarrow$ for $\Rightarrow$, but see Definition 1 in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai&iemhoff}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} for the precise definition).
A sequent calculus has {\it uniform Lyndon interpolation} ULIP if for both $\circ=+$ and $\circ=-$, for all sequents $S$ and all atoms $p\in V^{\circ}(\varphi)$, there are formulas $\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$ and $\exists^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$ in ${\mathcal{L}}$ such that $V^{\star}(\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS)\subseteq V^{\star}(S)$ and $V^{\star}(\exists^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS)\subseteq V^{\star}(S)$ for all $\star\in\{+,-\}$, and $p\not\in V^{+}(\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS)$ in case $\circ=+$ and $p\not\in V^{-}(\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS)$ in case $\circ=-$, and such that the following holds, where $\vdash$ stands for derivability in {\sf G}:
\par\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@[($\A$l)]$\vdash S^{a},\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS\Rightarrow S^{s}$;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[($\E$r)]$\vdash S^{a}\Rightarrow\exists^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS$;
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[($\A\E$)]If $S$ is derivable, for all partitions $(S^{r},S^{i})$ of $S$ such that $p\not\in V^{\circ}(S^{r})$: $\vdash S^{r}\cdot(\exists^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i}\Rightarrow\forall^{\circ}\hskip-0.28453ptp\hskip 0.56905ptS^{i})$.
\end{itemize}The single-conclusion version can be obtained from it in the same way as for UIP in Subsection~\ref{sec:uip}.
\par It seems likely that the positive results given below can be generalized in such a way that one can obtain negative results from them as well. However, at this stage, such generalization has not yet been carried out and we therefore only present the positive results that have already been obtained.
\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Non-normal Modal Logics}
\par Non-normal modal logics are modal logics in which the $K$-axiom, i.e.\ the axiom $\Box(\varphi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\psi)$, does not hold, but a weaker version that is given by the following $E$-rule does:
\begin{center}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.47221pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 7.7778pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to52.16663pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Box\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\psi$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}
\ignorespaces\end{center}Thus, the \emph{minimal non-normal modal logic}, {\sf E}, consists of classical propositional logic plus the $E$-rule above.
Over the last decades, non-normal modal logics have emerged in various fields, such as game theory and epistemic and deontic logic \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Chellas80}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}. Two well-known non-normal modal logics that are investigated in this paper are natural weakenings of the principle
$\Box(\varphi\wedge\psi)\leftrightarrow(\Box\varphi\wedge\Box\psi)$ that implies the principle $K$ over {\sf E}. Namely, the two principles:
$$(M)\ \ \ \Box(\varphi\wedge\psi)\rightarrow(\Box\varphi\wedge\Box\psi)\ \ \ \ \ \ (C)\ \ \ (\Box\varphi\wedge\Box\psi)\rightarrow\Box(\varphi\wedge\psi).$$Another principle of the logic {\sf K}\ that is often considered is
$$(N)\ \ \ \raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}\top.$$Because the $K$-axiom holds in the traditional relational semantics for modal logic, non-normal modal logics require different semantics, of which the most well-known is neighborhood semantics. As we do not need semantics in this paper, we refer the interested reader to the textbook \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Pacuit11}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\par The method that we used to prove ULIP for intermediate and normal (intuitionistic) modal logics also applies here to non-normal modal logics:
\par\begin{theorem}\cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai&iemhoff}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}
The non-normal modal logics $\mathsf{E}$, $\mathsf{M}$, $\mathsf{EN}$, $\mathsf{MN}$, $\mathsf{MC}$ have uniform Lyndon interpolation.
\end{theorem}Some of these results have already been obtained in the literature.
That the logics $\mathsf{E}$, $\mathsf{M}$, and $\mathsf{MC}$ have UIP has already been established in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Pattinson13,Santocanale&Venema10}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, but that they have uniform Lyndon interpolation is, as far as we know, a new insight. In \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Orlandelli21}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} it is shown that $\mathsf{E}$, $\mathsf{M}$, $\mathsf{MC}$, $\mathsf{EN}$, $\mathsf{MN}$ have Craig interpolation. The proof that these logics have UIP is not a mere extension of the proof that they have interpolation but requires a different approach.
\par The proof is constructive in that the uniform interpolants can be constructed explicitly from the proof.
\par\begin{theorem}\cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai&iemhoff}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}
The non-normal modal logics $\mathsf{EC}$ and $\mathsf{ECN}$ do not have Craig interpolation, and thus not uniform (Lyndon) interpolation.
\end{theorem}\par The logics in the theorem above would thus be excellent candidates of non-normal modal logics to obtain negative results for in the style of Section~\ref{sec:exiseqcal}. But that we leave for future work.
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Conditional Logics}
Conditional logics formalize reasoning about conditional statements, which are statements of the form {if $\varphi$ were the case, then $\psi$}, denoted as $\varphi\triangleright\psi$. This includes formal reasoning about counterfactuals, which are instances of conditional statements in which the antecedent is false. Examples of well-known conditional logics are the logics {\sf CE} and {\sf CK}. The logic {\sf CE} consists of the classical propositional logic plus the following {\it CE}-rule, which could be seen as the conditional counterpart of the $E$-rule above (by reading the $\varphi_{i}$ as $\top$):
\begin{center}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.47221pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.55087pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\varphi_{0}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{1}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\psi_{0}\leftrightarrow\psi_{1}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to103.16667pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$(\varphi_{1}\triangleright\psi_{1})\leftrightarrow(\varphi_{0}\triangleright\psi_{0})$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}
\ignorespaces\end{center}The conditional logic {\sf CK} is the result of adding to classical propositional logic the following {\it CK}-rule, which derives the conditional counterpart of $K$:
\begin{center}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.75pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\{\varphi_{0}\leftrightarrow\varphi_{i}\}_{i\in I}$}\hskip 4.0pt}\hbox{\hskip 14.45377pt}\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\rightarrow\psi_{0}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to138.36407pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 15.1301pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\{\varphi_{i}\triangleright\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\rightarrow(\varphi_{0}\triangleright\psi_{0})$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}
\ignorespaces\end{center}where $I$ is a possibly empty finite set.

\par By using the same technique as for Theorem~\ref{thm:non-normal} we can prove the counterpart of that theorem for conditional logics. Also in this case the uniform interpolants can be constructed explicitly from the proof.
\par\par\par\begin{theorem}\cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai&iemhoff22}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}
The conditional logics $\mathsf{CE}$, $\mathsf{CM}$, $\mathsf{CEN}$, $\mathsf{CMN}$, $\mathsf{CMC}$, $\mathsf{CK}$, $\mathsf{CKID}$ have uniform Lyndon interpolation.

The conditional logics {\sf CKCEM} and {\sf CKCEMID} have UIP but not ULIP.
\end{theorem}\par\begin{theorem}\cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai&iemhoff22}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}
The conditional logics $\mathsf{CEC}$ and $\mathsf{CECN}$ do not have Craig interpolation.
\end{theorem}\par As in the corresponding case for non-normal modal logics, the last theorem suggests that by generalizing Theorem~\ref{thm:conditional} one can obtain negative results for $\mathsf{CEC}$ and $\mathsf{CECN}$. But, again as in the case of non-normal logics, we leave that for future work.
\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Lax Logic}
\par Finally, we present a positive result for a single modal logic that occurs in several areas of logic. This logic,
Lax logic {\sf LL}, has a single unary modal operator $\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}$ and is axiomatized by the principles of intuitionistic propositional logic {\sf IPC}\ plus the following three modal principles:
$$\varphi\rightarrow\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi\ \ (\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}R)\ \ \ \ \ \raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi\rightarrow\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi\ \ (\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}M)\ \ \ \ \ \raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi\wedge\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\psi\rightarrow\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}(\varphi\wedge\psi)\ \ (\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}S)$$In type theory, the modal operator describes a certain type constructor, and there exists a Curry-Howard-like correspondence between Lax Logic and the computational lambda calculus \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Bentonetal98}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}. In algebraic logic, the operator is a so-called nucleus, and in recent work in that area, embeddings of superintuitionistic logics into extensions of {\sf LL}\ have been used to provide new characterizations of subframe superintuitionistic logics \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Bezhanishvilietal19}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, while in \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Melzer20}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} the method of canonical formulas for Lax Logic has been developed. The origins of the modality can be traced back to \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Curry57}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} but the logic received its name {Lax Logic} in the PhD-thesis of \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Mendler93}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, where the logic was introduced in the setting of hardware verification. In that context, Lax Logic is used to model reasoning about statements of the form {$\varphi$ holds under some constraint}, denoted as $\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\ocircle$}\varphi$. Further investigation led to \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{Fairtlough&Mendler94, Fairtlough&Mendler97}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}, in which, among other things, a sequent calculus for the logic is provided.

We have seen the method described in Subsection~\ref{sec:method} at work for various classes of logics. Recall that in the most general terms, the method aims to prove, for a given class $\mathcal{S}$ of sequent calculi, and given classes $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}$ of logics, and a certain property $\mathcal{P}$ of logics:
\begin{description}\description@item@[\rm(I)]Any logic in $\mathcal{C}$ that has a sequent calculus in $\mathcal{S}$ has property $\mathcal{P}$;
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\description@item@[\rm(II)]No logic in $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ has property $\mathcal{P}$.
\end{description}So one can conclude by contraposition the negative result:
\begin{description}\description@item@[\rm(III)]No logic in $\mathcal{C^{\prime}}$ has a sequent calculus in $\mathcal{S}$.
\end{description}\par In Section~\ref{sec:exiseqcal} we have applied the method for the case that $\mathcal{P}$ is interpolation or uniform (Lyndon) interpolation and obtained negative results of the form (III) for intermediate logics and various classes of (intuitionistic) modal logics. In Section~\ref{sec:positive} we presented positive results showing that various non-normal modal and conditional logics have uniform (Lyndon) interpolation. The proofs of these facts are based on the techniques developed for (I), still with $\mathcal{P}$ being uniform (Lyndon) interpolation.
\par\par This choice for $\mathcal{P}$ is convenient because uniform interpolation seems to be a rare property so that one can conclude results of the form (III) for broad classes of logics. However, there is no reason to believe that the method only works for interpolation properties. And in fact, it does not, as was shown by \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{jalali&tabatabai22}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. The class of logics they consider is that of intuitionistic modal logics in the language ${\mathcal{L}}=\{\wedge,\vee,\rightarrow,\top,\bot,\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$},\Diamond\}$. For the property $\mathcal{P}$ we turn to the realm of admissible rules.
\par Given an intuitionistic modal logic ${\sf L}$ in ${\mathcal{L}}$, a rule $\varphi\ /\ \psi$ is {\it admissible} in ${\sf L}$, denoted $\varphi\makebox{\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\scriptsize$\ \mid$}\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\footnotesize$\!\sim\,$}}_{{\sf L}}\psi$, if for every ${\mathcal{L}}$-substitution $\sigma$: $\vdash_{{\sf L}}\sigma\varphi$ implies $\vdash_{{\sf L}}\sigma\psi$. Here $\sigma:{\cal F}_{\mathcal{L}}\rightarrow{\cal F}_{\mathcal{L}}$ is an ${\mathcal{L}}$-substitution if it commutes with the connectives and modal operators. Clearly, if $\vdash_{\sf L}\varphi\rightarrow\psi$, then $\varphi\makebox{\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\scriptsize$\ \mid$}\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\footnotesize$\!\sim\,$}}_{{\sf L}}\psi$. But the converse is not always the case. For example, in ${\sf IPC}$ and ${\sf iK}_{\Box}$ the Kreisel-Putnam rule
$$\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.75891pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 20.49301pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\neg\varphi\rightarrow\psi\vee\psi^{\prime}$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to101.36086pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$(\neg\varphi\rightarrow\psi)\vee(\neg\varphi\rightarrow\psi^{\prime})$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$is admissible, but not derivable. For more on the notion of admissibility, see \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{iemhoff15,iemhoff16b,rybakov97}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}.
\par Here we consider the notion of admissibility in the setting of sequent calculi and combine it with the notion of feasibility in the following definition. In it we use the notion of a Harrop formula \cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{troelstra&vandalen14}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})} but then extended to our modal language: the set of {\it Harrop formulas} is inductively defined as the smallest class of formulas containing the atoms and constants of ${\mathcal{L}}$, is closed under $\wedge,\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}$ and implications $\varphi\rightarrow\psi$ in which $\psi$ is a Harrop formula.
\par\begin{definition}Consider a sequent calculus {\sf G}. We denote with $\vdash^{\pi}S$ that $\pi$ is a proof in {\sf G}\ of $S$.
{\sf G}\ has the {\it feasible Visser-Harrop property}, if there is a polynomial time algorithm $f$ such that for all $\Gamma,\varphi_{i},\psi_{i},\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{j}$, where $\Gamma$ consists of Harrop formulas, and any proof $\pi$ in {\sf G}:
$$\small\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{ if }&\vdash^{\pi}\Gamma,\{\varphi_{i}\rightarrow\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{1}\vee\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{2},\\
\text{ then }&\vdash^{f(\pi)}\Gamma,\{\varphi_{i}\rightarrow\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\Rightarrow\raisebox{1.54996pt}{\mbox{$\chi$}}_{j}\text{ for some $j\in\{1,2\}$}\\
\text{ or }&\vdash^{f(\pi)}\Gamma,\{\varphi_{i}\rightarrow\psi_{i}\}_{i\in I}\Rightarrow\varphi_{i}\text{ for some $i\in I$}.\end{array}$$In case $I$ is empty, it is called the {\it feasible disjunction property} of {\sf G}.
\end{definition}\par Returning to our method, let property $\mathcal{P}$ be the feasible Visser-Harrop property, and let the class of logics $\mathcal{C}$ be the logics in language ${\mathcal{L}}$ that are equal to or extend {\sf CK}, which extends {\sf IPC}\ by Modus Ponens, Necessitation, and the axioms
$$\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}(\varphi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}\varphi\rightarrow\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}\psi)\ \ \ \ \ \ \raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}(\varphi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\Diamond\varphi\rightarrow\Diamond\psi)$$Define the sequent calculus $\mathsf{CK}$ as the calculus that extends {\sf LJ}\ by the two modal rules that correspond to the axioms above:
$$\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.41666pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 6.38895pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma\Rightarrow p$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to47.6146pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}\Gamma\Rightarrow\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}p$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces\ \ \ \ \ignorespaces\ignorespaces\lower 14.41666pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 10.55559pt\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Gamma,p\Rightarrow q$}\hskip 4.0pt}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to65.2152pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\raisebox{0.28453pt}{$\Box$}\Gamma,\Diamond p\Rightarrow\Diamond q$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}\ignorespaces$$Note that the logic and sequent calculus $\mathsf{CK}$ defined here are different from the conditional logic $\mathsf{CK}$ defined and used in Subsection \ref{sec:conditional}. However, this abuse of notation will cause no confusion, as from now on, we will only work with $\mathsf{CK}$ as defined here.
The class of sequent calculi we are interested in extends $\mathsf{CK}$ with possibly a finite number of \emph{constructive} axioms, defined below.
We call this class of calculi $\mathcal{CK}$. Note that these calculi are allowed to contain the Cut rule.
\begin{definition}We define the following three sets of formulas:
\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@(\emph{Basic})
$B:=p\mid\top\mid\bot\mid B\wedge B\mid B\vee B\mid\Diamond B\text{, with }p\text{ an atom}$
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@(\emph{Almost positive})
$P:=B\mid P\wedge P\mid P\vee P\mid\Box P\mid\Diamond P\mid B\to P,$ with $B$ a basic formula
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@(\emph{Constructive})
$C:=B\mid C\wedge C\mid\Box C\mid P\to C,$ with $B$ a basic and $P$ an almost positive formula.
\end{itemize}An axiom is called \emph{constructive} if it is of the form $\Gamma\Rightarrow\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is a constructive formula.
\end{definition}The following are examples of formulas that are basic, almost positive, and constructive: $p$, $(p\wedge q)$, $(p\vee q)$, $(p\to q)$, $\neg p$, $\Box p$, $\Diamond p$.
The formula $p\vee\neg p$ is almost positive but not constructive. The formulas $\neg\neg p$ and $(p\to q)\to r$ are constructive but not almost positive.
\par\par Denote the Kripke frame that has only one reflexive node by $\mathcal{K}_{r}$ and the Kripke frame that has one irreflexive node by $\mathcal{K}_{i}$. If the class of calculi $\mathcal{CK}$, satisfies a mild technical condition, namely being \emph{$T$-free} or \emph{$T$-full}, then it has the feasible disjunction property.
\par\begin{definition}Let {\sf G}\ be a sequent calculus such that $\mathsf{CK}\subseteq{\sf G}$ and ${\sf L}$ be a logic such that ${\sf CK}\subseteq{\sf L}$. We say {\sf G}\ (resp. {\sf L}) is \emph{$T$-free} if it is valid in $\mathcal{K}_{i}$. We say {\sf G}\ (resp. {\sf L}) is \emph{$T$-full} if it is valid in $\mathcal{K}_{r}$ and both $T_{a}=(\Box p\to p)$ and $T_{b}=(p\to\Diamond p)$ are provable in it (resp. $T_{a},T_{b}\in{\sf L}$).
\end{definition}\par\begin{theorem}\cite[citep]{(\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{jalali&tabatabai22}{\@@citephrase{, }}{})}
Any calculus {\sf G}\ in $\mathcal{CK}$ that is either $T$-free or $T$-full, has the feasible disjunction property.
\end{theorem}\par Recall that a logic $L$ has the \emph{disjunction property (DP)} if $A\vee B\in L$ implies either $A\in L$ or $B\in L$. Define a \emph{Visser rules} as
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}[]{c}$V_{n}$ \quad \ignorespaces
\ignorespaces\lower 14.75pt\hbox{\vbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Rightarrow\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\left(p_{i}\rightarrow q_{i}\right)\rightarrow p_{n+1}\vee p_{n+2}\right)\vee r$}\hskip 4.0pt}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 0.0pt\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}\hbox to151.17781pt{\leaders{\hrule}{\hfill}}\lower-0.50186pt\hbox{}}\vskip 2.0pt\hbox{\hskip 7.08192pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 4.0pt\hbox{$\Rightarrow\bigvee_{j=1}^{n+2}\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\left(p_{i}\rightarrow q_{i}\right)\rightarrow p_{j}\right)\vee r$}\hskip 4.0pt}}}}}\end{tabular}\end{center}for $n\geq 1$. By abuse of notation, if the logic $L$ has DP, we say $L$ admits $V_{0}$.
We say the logic $L$ \emph{admits all Visser's rules} when it admits all $V_{n}$ for $n\geq 0$.
\par\begin{corollary}If ${\sf L}$ is a $T$-free or $T$-full logic in which at least one Visser rule is not admissible, then ${\sf L}$ does
not have a sequent calculus in $\mathcal{CK}$.
\end{corollary}\par The paper by \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{jalali&tabatabai22}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} contains many further results that we cannot do justice here. But we hope that with this selection of results from it, we have illustrated a variant of the general method and shown how also, in this case, strong negative and positive results can be obtained from it.
