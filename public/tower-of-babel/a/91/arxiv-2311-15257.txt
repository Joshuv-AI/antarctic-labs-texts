Career Modeling with Missing Data and Traces
Théo Voldoire, Robin J. Ryder, Ryan Lahfa

Abstract




The Case of Public-Private Paths of Elite Civil Servants in France

Abstract

Many social scientists study the career trajectories of populations of interest, such as economic and administrative elites. However, data to document such processes are rarely completely available, which motivates the adoption of inference tools that can account for large numbers of missing values. Taking the example of public-private paths of elite civil servants in France, we introduce binary Markov switching models to perform Bayesian data augmentation.
Our procedure relies on two data sources:
(1) detailed observations of a small number of individual trajectories, and
(2) less informative “traces” left by all individuals, which we model for imputation of missing data.
An advantage of this model class is that it maintains the properties of hidden Markov models and enables a tailored sampler to target the posterior, while allowing for varying parameters across individuals and time. We provide two applied studies which demonstrate this can be used to properly test substantive hypotheses, and expand the social scientific literature in various ways. We notably show that the rate at which ENA graduates exit the French public sector has not increased since 1990, but that the rate at which they come back has increased.

Keywords:
Bayesian Markov Switching Model, Career Modeling, Public-Private Paths, Civil Servants, Missing Values

Introduction

Many important questions in the social sciences require understanding career trajectories of certain populations. This is an important aspect when describing political
(
Jäckle and
Kerby 2018
)
, economic
(
Dudouet
et al. 2010
)
, academic
(
Angermuller and
Blanchard 2023
)
and administrative
(
Rouban 2014
)
elites, but also when studying specific professions like musicians
(
Abbott and
Hrycak 1990
)
, journalists
(
Machut and
Bastin 2023
)
, or even athletes
(
Li
et al. 2018
)
. Studying careers is seen as interesting in its own right, but also to describe broader social mechanisms such as lobbying and revolving doors
(
Blanes i Vidal et al. 2012
)
or the spatial structuring of finance
(
Bühlmann et al. 2024
)
.

However, a key problem in career modeling is data missingness. It is very rare that all data relevant to the career of a certain population are available in a public record or can even be collected because of resource constraints, which is problematic for two reasons. First, for a given defined population, inference can be made more difficult and less powerful if a majority of individuals cannot be observed. This reduces the breadth of questions that can be answered using career modeling, as it makes the testing of interesting interaction effects impossible in practice. Second, researchers often choose to only study populations that can be entirely observed. This forces them to study the groups that are the most visible, such as top political elites; it thus constrains the breadth of questions that can be addressed using career modeling.

Our article has two aims. First, we propose a general methodology employing hidden Markov models with covariates and autoregressive components, and showcase, using plausible synthetic data examples, why they are required for such a modeling task while remaining simple enough for substantive hypothesis testing. The procedure relies on (i) a fraction of the population being well-observed using a first (possibly hand collected) data source, and (ii) the usage of partial “traces” from a digital or administrative record that are modeled to input the missing information for the remaining majority of individuals. Second, we provide two applied studies showcasing the usefulness of these models, which expand the social scientific literature of administrative elites in meaningful ways.

Our leading example will be the study of public-private paths of elite civil servants in France. Paths of civil servants into the private sector are seen as important both by the general public and in the social science literature, where many publications that quantitatively study French high civil servants tackle the issue directly
(
Bouzidi et al. 2010
;
Kolopp 2021
;
Rouban 2014
)
. Public-private paths broadly understood for policy-relevant positions are sometimes described using the term “revolving doors”, because these back-and-forth switches resemble a physical revolving door
(
Seabrooke and
Tsingou 2021
;
France and
Vauchez 2017
)
, but other scholars define revolving doors to be a much more precise and limited in scope phenomenon. Unassuming about this definitional debate, we denote this problem as the study of public-private paths of French elite civil servants, and acknowledge that our article falls in the first category of works.

The phenomenon of public-private paths of civil servants demonstrates well the usefulness of the general method because it shares many difficulties with other settings in career analysis. First, it is relatively easy to describe the phenomenon for a portion of elite civil servants, by collecting data from digital networking websites such as LinkedIn on which they are registered. However, a majority of them do not have a networking digital profile, and further, it is not always the case that a profile is fully completed from the starting point of one’s career. Second, most civil servants, to various degrees, leave “traces” of their activity, when they sign legal documents, or obtain important promotions or bonuses. We showcase that it is possible to reconstruct trajectories of large civil servant populations and draw interpretations that contribute to the social scientific literature. In addition, our population is voluntarily more diverse and an order of magnitude larger compared to usual prosopography studies of the domain, to demonstrate that our methodology is well suited for analyses that are also targeted at less visible, larger professional groups.

2
General methodology

2.1
Problem setting

2.1.1
Detailed and trace data sources

The core of our approach stems from the tension between data quality and quantity. In the majority of cases, it is difficult to obtain data that exhibit both high precision to document career trajectories, and high coverage. However, there often exists at least two data sources with opposed strengths and weaknesses:

•

Detailed data source.
One data source exhibits
low coverage
but is
very informative
on the individuals it documents. This usually corresponds to data collected by hand for a small number of individuals, but can also be taken automatically from digital or administrative sources, such as professional networking websites.

•

Trace data source.
Another data source exhibits
high coverage
but is
not very informative
taken on its own and requires statistical elaboration for analysis. This concerns larger administrative or digital records, which we will describe as collections of “traces”: we know that individuals in a certain career position should regularly leave traces of their activity in these records, but the rate at which this happens varies across people and time.

Let us provide several examples taken from the introduction. When studying journalists’ affiliations, we know that some of them have digital profiles on networking websites (detailed data source), or we can spend some time hand-crafting a career database on a subpopulation; but we also know that journalists regularly publish newspaper articles with their name and affiliation (trace data source). We can apply the same logic to academic researchers, even if the rate at which they publish is lower: modeling the process by which individuals emit traces needs to be informed by data, and this motivates our mathematical framework. For board members in the economic elite, one might hand-construct a dataset (detailed data source); but we might also find their names with affiliation in documents at the end of every board meeting (trace data source), which might vary in frequency from company to period. Our leading example will be the case of elite civil servants in the public and private sectors: a good portion of them have a digital presence (detailed data source); but for many groups, we know they leave traces of their activity in administrative records when they get certain promotions or take certain decisions in the public sector (trace data source). For all the examples cited in the introduction, one could find credible data sources that would match this setting.

2.1.2
Formalization

We now turn to the formalization of this setting. We aim to study the career trajectory of
N
individuals
i\in\{1,...,N\}
in discrete time
t\in\{1,...,T\}
. We denote the career position of individual
i
in time
t
as
X_{i,t}\in\mathcal{X}
, with
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},...,x_{K}\}
a known finite state space of size
K
. In our application,
\mathcal{X}
is equal to
\{0,1\}
, which corresponds to public and private sectors, but this can describe many other settings: position in a given newsroom or media type for a journalist, stage of the career for an academic, company for a corporate manager…
As we will use a model in the family of Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), we expect that choosing a higher cardinality for
\mathcal{X}
will incur further assumptions so that the parameters governing the behavior remain of moderate effective dimension.
The ensemble of all trajectories at all time points is denoted
\textbf{X}=(X_{i,t})_{i,t}
. Observe that time here is discrete: our techniques may be generalized with continuous time, but we do not find a sufficient interest given the prohibitive cost that would incur.
In our applications,
t
corresponds to a six-month period; other choices could be reasonable depending on the substantive research question at hand.
We assume that
X
is partially observed, thanks to the
detailed data source
: for some (but not all) values of
i
and
t
, we observe the value of
X_{i,t}
.

We now need to connect it to information found in the
trace data source
. The trace data source is described using a second set of time series,
\textbf{Y}=(Y_{i,t})_{i,t}
, which take value
Y_{i,t}\in\mathcal{Y}
for each
i
at time
t
. The state space
\mathcal{Y}
may here be either discrete or continuous, one-dimensional or multi-dimensional. In our application, it will simply be a binary variable describing whether an administrative trace can be found for individual
i
at time
t
. This could correspond to the number of articles published by a journalist or an academic, an encoding of an athlete’s participation in different competitions, or a binary encoding on whether there was a document including the name of a board member in the given period.
Unlike
X
, we assume that
Y
is fully observed.

Lastly, this setting is described with covariates
\textbf{W}=(W_{i,t})_{i,t}
which are of interest to the researchers or which are deemed important to control for, and which may vary across individuals and time. A typical example will be to include the sex of individuals, their age, or their initial diplomas, to see if career behavior differs across these characterics. Importantly, these covariates will be used both to model the behavior of career paths
X
, and the relationship between paths
X
and traces
Y
, and so may include both substantive and nuisance characteristics. We will show it is possible and important to include transformations of lagged values of
Y_{i,t}
into
W_{i,t}
when describing the relationship between
X
and
Y
.

2.2
Model building with Markov switching models and inference

2.2.1
Related works and inspirations

Our modeling approach is inspired by duration analysis models and their connection to sequence analysis, and by recent developments using Markov switching models in realistic applied settings.

When working with social sequence data, many questions are fundamentally tied to duration. For example, duration analysis models have been applied the durability of political elites such as ministers
(
King
et al. 1990
;
Bienen and Van de
Walle 1991
)
. In addition, many applications find interest in the duration individuals may stay in more than one type of position:
Machut and
Bastin 2023
are interested in the career instability of journalists and how transitions across steps of the careers are accelerating with time;
Angermuller and
Blanchard 2023
(
Angermuller and
Blanchard 2023
) are interested in the mobility of academic researchers across statuses, institutions, and countries; and we are interested in both the time elite civil servants spend in the public sector and the time they take before coming back. In our specific application, we complement the scholarship on the tenure of administrative elites using traditional duration analysis
(
Fleischer 2016
)
since we also consider the time taken before coming back, and similar generalizations for the other examples can be provided by modeling as a whole trajectories
X
instead of only focusing on a single duration problem.

One traditional solution is to study discrete trajectories as Markov models, or, in the case there are missing values, as hidden Markov models (HMM). A complication is that we aim to retain a traditional strength of duration analyses, namely, the ability to flexibly include covariates whose importance can be assessed by statistical tests, as in proportional hazard models
(
Box-Steffensmeier et al. 2007
)
. A second inspiration of this work thus consists of using hidden Markov models with covariates, meaning that the transition rates of
X
and emission probabilities of
Y
can vary across individuals and time. This class of models, otherwise called Markov switching, has been regularly used in recent applied statistics works.
Wang and
Puterman 1999
used Markov Poisson regression models for discrete time series, and
Langrock et al. 2017
proposed generalized additive models with B-splines for additional covariate flexibility. Recent applications include financial and economic forecasting
(
Meligkotsidou
and Dellaportas 2011
)
, biometrics and environmental data
(
Wong 2001
)
, and shooting performance in sports
(
Sandri
et al. 2020
)
. These applications exhibit how Markov switching models produce good estimates yet remain easy to handle.

2.2.2
Formal presentation

For each individual
i
, we assume that the trajectory
(X_{t}^{i})_{t}
is a Markov chain with non-homogeneous transition kernel
P_{i,t}=(\gamma_{i,t}^{x,x^{\prime}})_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}
. For each starting point
x
, we model the transition probabilities using a generalized linear model (GLM) for the multinomial distribution:

(\gamma_{i,t}^{x,x_{1}},...,\gamma_{i,t}^{x,x_{K}})=\text{softmax}((W_{i,t}^{T}\beta^{x,x_{1}},...,W_{i,t}^{T}\beta^{x,x_{K}})),

(1)

with
W_{i,t}
the aforementioned vector of covariates, and
(\beta^{x,x^{\prime}})_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}
vectors of parameters.
Notice that we may have different parameters
\beta^{x,x^{\prime}}
for different transitions
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, but that the parameters
\beta^{x,x^{\prime}}
do not depend on time. Time is rather encoded here as a variable in the covariate vectors
W_{i,t}
. Notice also that, for a state space of size
K
, there are only
K\times(K-1)
vectors of parameters
(\beta^{x,x^{\prime}})
to model. In the specific case of career modeling, where individuals do not move at every step, it is reasonable to take the absence of change
x=x^{\prime}
as the reference category.

For every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we next need to model the trace-emission process, that is the distribution of
Y_{i,t}
conditional on
X_{i,t}=x
. We will use a parametric family with likelihood
\mathcal{H}
and parameter
\lambda_{i,t}
specified via a GLM model:

\lambda_{i,t}:=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}|X_{i,t}=x,\widetilde{W}_{i,t}]=g(\widetilde{W}_{i,t}^{T}\eta^{x}),

(2)

with
\widetilde{W}_{i,t}
the vector of covariates,
(\eta^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
vectors of parameters, and
g
a link function. We stress that we use the notation
\widetilde{W}_{i,t}
to communicate that when estimating emission probabilities, it is possible to include lagged values of
(Y_{i,t^{\prime}})_{t^{\prime}<t}
inside
\widetilde{W}_{i,t}
, in addition to the standard covariates in
{W}_{i,t}
. Formally,
\widetilde{W}_{i,t}=[W_{i,t}^{T},f(Y_{i,1},...,Y_{i,t-1})]^{T}
, with
f
any user-specified transformation. We discuss why this auto-correlation feature is essential for such applications in subsection
2.2.3
.

1
.
The likelihood is available in closed form for all parameter vectors for transitions
(\beta^{x,x^{\prime}})_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}
:

\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\mathcal{L}(\beta^{x,.}|\textbf{X, W})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
X_{i,t}=x\end{subarray}}^{T-1}\prod_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\gamma_{i,t}^{x,x^{\prime}}\right)^{1_{\{X_{i,t+1}=x^{\prime}\}}}

(3)

and for emissions
(\eta^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}

\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\mathcal{L}(\eta^{x}|\textbf{X, Y, W})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
X_{i,t}=x\end{subarray}}^{T}\mathcal{H}(Y_{i,t}|\lambda_{i,t}^{x}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the likelihood function for the trace-emission GLM model, which will be practical for exploring the space of parameters with a custom sampler.

We purposefully introduced the Markov switching model in a general fashion, but it is now useful to provide details on one useful specificity of applications for career modeling. In many instances, certain values taken by
X_{i,t}\in\mathcal{X}
will be tied to certain values or a certain support of
Y_{i,t}
,
deterministically
.
For example: when studying traces in the public sector record, we know that if an individual is working in the private sector (denoted here as
X_{i,t}=\text{Private}
)
then there cannot be any traces in the public sector record. Formally,
X_{i,t}=\text{Private}
implies
Y_{i,t}=0
. This can be applied to many cases where individuals cannot hold multiple positions at the same time: journalists who have a full-time contract, managers in companies, politicians and their political party.

There is no need to actively model the emission parameters
\eta^{x}
in cases
x\in\mathcal{X}
where we have such information (e.g.
\eta^{Private}
for the civil servants), since, because of our external knowledge, the conditional distribution of
Y_{i,t}
given
X_{i,t}=x
is deterministic. This reduces the number of parameters to estimate and enables the seamless integration of expert knowledge. Additionally, when trying to reconstruct trajectories
X
from traces
Y
, this will provide an unusually strong conditioning compared to usual applications of HMMs.
This is a reason why our procedure works well in practice. As a whole, this application specificity thus facilitates both inference of parameters and reconstruction of trajectories.

2.2.3
Motivation from a complexity point of view

The model we suggest is purposefully more complex than a Hidden Markov Model (HMM). Indeed, several issues arise from simply using a HMM in our application. One is substantive: a key interest is to describe how career behavior varies across individuals, groups, and time; thus we naturally need to include covariates in the models. Another is more technical and concerns traces. A key component of the problem is that it is essential that the trace-emission process be well modeled: otherwise, errors here will be communicated to the rest of the procedure, which will result both in a poor reconstruction of
X
and in biased inference of the
(\beta^{x,x^{\prime}})
.
We present for now the substantive reason for why it is essential to have covariates and lagged values of traces when modeling the trace-emission process. We defer examples based on synthetic data to section
3.2
as they require more notation.

Two problems make the inclusion of covariates and lagged values essential in modeling the trace-emission process: heterogeneity and self-excitation.
In all the examples cited above, we expect heterogeneity between individuals emitting traces. Some journalists and academics write more articles than others, some civil servants make more important decisions and are promoted more often than their peers, and some athletes participate in more competitions than their competitors. If we do not account for this, our inference will confound the trace frequency variety and the actual behavior of careers. Furthermore, in most of the examples we cited, it is expected that some traces will be tied or will lead to other traces in quick succession, a fact that can either be described as self-excitation or time heterogeneity. Politicians writing political posts will lead to more posting, civil servants joining a committee will lead to more announcements when the committee arrives at a position, and journalists writing a piece on some hot-button issue can be expected to write more in the following weeks or months. Not accounting for that will lead to overestimating the probability of finding traces at any given time, and thus to overestimating the number of transitions across states of trajectories
X
. For this reason, using a simple HMM is not sufficient, and there is a need for both covariates and transformations of lagged values of traces.

On the other hand, it might be argued that a more complex model is called for.
We resist the urge to go for more complex models, because Markov switching models exhibit properties that greatly facilitate the inference and enable us to efficiently target the posterior in a Bayesian framework. In particular, their factorization structure still enables the usage of the Baum-Welch algorithm when reconstructing sequences
X
from traces
Y
. One can take any presentation of HMM reconstruction (e.g.
Marin and
Robert 2014
,
Marin and
Robert 2014
), simply subscript the transition and emission rates, and perform the reconstruction unchanged. In fact, this would even work for rates that are obtained with more complex models than a GLM, such as neural networks
(
Tran
et al. 2016
)
, as long as the independence structure described in Figure
1
holds. We do not consider such extensions here, and remain in the GLM realm as our goal is to test substantive hypotheses.

This last point connects to one final discussion of the goals of the analysis. If the goal were only to reconstruct the trajectories
X
with the highest accuracy at the individual level, other solutions from (Bayesian) machine learning would probably be better suited, using recent developments in deep learning for sequences and inverse problems. However, our primary goal here is to provide a method that enables the testing of substantive research hypotheses, reconstruction being only secondary and used in an MCMC sampler. We still report quantities related to reconstruction accuracy with cross-validation experiments, but our main interest throughout is to enable the unbiased and precise inference of substantively motivated parameters. Yet, more complex developments could be motivated for the trace emission part (since we consider emission parameters
\eta^{x}
to be nuisance), and for the career trajectory part (notably using varying-length Markov models, see e.g.
Le Picard and
Rossi 2023
,
Le Picard and
Rossi 2023
).

2.2.4
MCMC inference sampler

We propose a tailored estimation scheme by MCMC to target the posterior, consisting of one Gibbs step for generating the missing values of
X
, and Metropolis-within-Gibbs steps for exploring the parameter spaces. This contrasts with a recent work from
Michelot 2022
which proposes a faster (but non-exact) estimation using the Laplace approximation of the posterior.
We give here a general presentation on our algorithm, but note that our implementation (available at
https://github.com/theovoldoire/Hidden_Careers_Public
) is more problem-specific: in our implementation, we only consider a state space of dimension 2, and perform several simplifications similar to those discussed in section
2.2.2
and introduced in section
3
. The extension to the general case would be straightforward.

Gibbs step for unobserved values of
X
.
The Gibbs step consists of simulating missing values of
X
given observed values of
X
, traces
Y
, and parameters
(\beta^{x,x^{\prime}})_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}
,
(\eta^{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
, and consists of two stages.
In the first stage, we compute the transition parameters
\gamma_{i,t}^{x,x^{\prime}}
for all individuals
i
, time
t
, and states
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
; and the conditional probabilities
p(Y_{i,t}|X_{i,t}=x,\widetilde{W}_{i,t},\eta^{x})
provided by the expected value
\lambda_{i,t}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}|X_{i,t}=x,\widetilde{W}_{i,t},\eta^{x}]=g(\widetilde{W}_{i,t}^{T}\eta^{x})
, for all
i,t
and states
x
.
In the second stage, we use the Baum-Welch algorithm.
Our implementation is based on the R package
HMM

(
Himmelmann 2022
)
, for which we simply added a time subscript on parameters; note that the Baum-Welch algorithm allows for transition and emission parameters which are not constant across time and units.

Metropolis-within-Gibbs step for parameters
. We take inspiration from
Marin and
Robert 2014
and apply the same method successively to different parameters of interest. Iterating on
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we update separately each vector of parameters, first
(\beta^{x,x_{1}},...,\beta^{x,x_{K}})
jointly, and then
\eta^{x}
.
For each update of an arbitrary parameter vector
\mu
, we proceed as follows: compute the MLE
\hat{\mu}
for this given parameter using either equation
3
or
4
and compute the associated covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}
. Second, run for
M_{\text{metropolis}}
iterations a Metropolis-Hastings random walk with the proposition
\tilde{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\tau^{2}\hat{\Sigma})
, with
\tau
a hyperparameter that we set to 1 after experimentation. After
M_{\text{metropolis}}
iterations, keep the final value of parameter
\mu
as the new value. In our applications, visual checks indicated that it was sufficient to take
M_{\text{metropolis}}=30
.

3
Public-Private paths in French elite civil servants

In this second part of the article, we showcase the general methodology on real data taken from the social and political sciences: public-private paths of civil servants in France. While our focus is on the inferential process itself, we also discuss the applied literature and extend it in several manners.

3.1
Presentation of the studies

3.1.1
General framing

Paths of civil servants in the private sector in France have been studied by multiple authors
(
Bouzidi et al. 2010
;
Kolopp 2021
;
Rouban 2014
)
and have been a source of interest in the general public. We explore two aspects assessed as interesting in the literature but not yet described, or not described with such precision: how the structure of public-private paths has evolved since 1990
(
Bouzidi et al. 2010
)
; and how patterns of these paths vary across many organizations
(
Charle 1987
)
or individual characteristics like gender
(
Kolopp 2021
)
. To this end, we will study two populations of elite civil servants: former students of the National School of Administration (École Nationale d’Administration, hereafter ENA) in Section
3.3
, and members of powerful services (listed below) in Section
3.4
.
Previous studies have focused on detailed data sources only: well described career paths for a small number of people. As such, they have not explored thoroughly these questions, which require large amounts of data, and very diverse data.

We review the relevant applied literature in sections
3.3
and
3.4
. We first introduce our data sources along some particularities of the analysis. Details for how we constructed and assembled the population can be found in Appendix
A
.

3.1.2
Detailed and trace data sources

Recall that we combine two data sources: a detailed data set for a smaller number of individuals, and a trace data set which covers the entire population but with less detail. Quantitative details on the size of each population and their coverage in the detailed source are provided in sections
3.3.1
,
3.4.1
and in Appendix
B
.

Our detailed data come from the website LinkedIn. LinkedIn is a
social media platform oriented towards professional networking, where
users describe their past work experience on their profile.
Many managers, both in the public and private sectors, have a
profile, but not the entirety of the population. In addition, as these data are auto-biographical, individuals may choose to not include all relevant information: there are gaps in profiles, which means that there might be missing data even for individuals with a LinkedIn profile. This means that even when an individual is in this detailed data source, it can still be useful to complement the analysis with a trace data source.

Our trace data source is the Official Journal of the French Republic (hereafter JO or “Journal Officiel”), an administrative record with a significant legal value that includes new pieces of regulation, executive decisions and nominations.
This source documents nominations for many top-level positions in public organizations. It also includes, in certain cases, information about promotions, other career changes (such as retirement) and decisions taken by authorities that might involve named civil servants. Importantly, certain key positions are systematically described, and this source can thus be used to define exhaustive and representative populations. However, not all public positions are described, and there is often no duration attached to these positions, which explains why it may only be treated as a traces dataset. The source was digitized starting in 1990, and this defines our temporal frame (1990-2022).

3.1.3
Specificities in the implementation

Indices and span of the analysis
. We consider the trajectories of individuals through time
t=1,...,T
, where an increment by one unit corresponds to 6 months:
t=1
corresponds to January-June 1990, and
T=68
corresponds to July-December 2022. Each individual
i
is modeled on an interval
[T_{min}^{i},T_{\max}^{i}]
. We let
T_{\min}^{i}
be their first membership-defining trace (see Appendix
A
). If the individual has a trace that states they retired, we use that date for
T_{\max}^{i}
; else we let
T_{\max}^{i}=T
, where
T
corresponds to our last observation time (July-December 2022). Individuals are thus modeled on different spans, a fact that is possible in the general framework but makes notation more cumbersome.

State spaces and parameters
. As our aim is to describe public-private paths, we study the state space
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
, with
0
corresponding to the public sector and
1
to the private sector. It is possible to describe heterogeneity of the public sector by including information in the covariates
W
and without increasing the size of the state space. We thus have two parameters of interest indexed by individuals and time:
\gamma_{i,t}^{0}:=\gamma_{i,t}^{0\rightarrow 1}
the probability to exit the public sector at time
t
, and
\gamma_{i,t}^{1}:=\gamma_{i,t}^{1\rightarrow 0}
the probability for an individual who had previously left to the private sector to return to the public sector. Finally, we describe traces using simply a dichotomous variable as this led to better results: we take
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
, with
Y_{i,t}=1
if we observe at least one trace in JO in the given six-months interval and
Y_{i,t}=0
otherwise. This is modeled as
Y_{i,t}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\lambda_{i,t})
.

Configuration for autoregressive traces
. We code three variables to describe the autoregressive aspect of traces. Two are given by
A_{i,t}(\varphi)=({\sum_{0<s\leq t}\varphi^{s}Y_{t-s}^{i}})/({\sum_{0<s\leq t}\varphi^{s}})
, where we set
\varphi=1
to prioritize long-term information and
\varphi=0.8
for medium-term information.
A_{i,t}(1)
and
A_{i,t}(0.8)
take values between
0
and
1
. For short-term information, we create a transformation of the time lapsed since the last trace

L_{i,t}=\sqrt{\max\{p\ |\ \forall s<p,Y_{t-s}=0\ \text{and}\ Y_{t-p}=1\}-1}.

(5)

These three variables provide a low-dimensional and efficient encoding of past traces and tackle the issue of the varying size of past values, but many other encodings could be motivated since these are not our parameters of interest.
We used the cross-validation reconstruction loss criterion introduced in section
3.3.3
to evaluate different possible values for
\varphi
(we tried
\varphi=0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9
) and found that
\varphi=0.8
minimized the loss.

Handling of boundary effects
. An issue is that when
t
is low, there are not many past trace variables. This becomes problematic because a specificity of our setting is that we use traces both to define the population (as the starting positions we are interested in are systematically described) and as a complementary source (for all traces). This means that by construction,
Y_{i,T^{i}_{\min}}=1
, which needs to be taken into account. As a solution, we ignore this first trace (setting the value to zero), and include specific covariates to handle points before any traces were found:
\textbf{1}_{\{A_{t,i}(1)=0\}}
, and
L_{t,i}\times\textbf{1}_{\{A_{t,i}(1)=0\}}
. A more complex handling is required when including time-sensitive coefficients, and we include instead an interaction between B-splines for age and the indicator
\textbf{1}_{\{A_{t,i}(1)=0\}}
(see below, models 3, 4, 5 of Study 1).

Prior distributions and convergence
. Results presented throughout are with non-informative, flat (improper) prior distributions. Other tests were performed with low-informative priors, e.g.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma=5
for model 3 in study 1, with unchanged results. Contrary to fully unsupervised methods where the dependency on the prior choice could be higher, our setting only exhibits a small dependency, as points taken from the detailed data source are not simulated an thus reduce the relative importance played by the prior in the posterior distribution. We run our MCMC sampler on 5000 iterations and discard the first 200 as a burn-in period. Visual checks show that this is sufficient to guarantee convergence and the MCMC sampler mixes well. Throughout studies 1 and 2, the ESS of all parameters is above 200, and the
\hat{R}
below
1.006
, indicating that the Markov chain has converged and has appropriately explored the posterior distribution.

3.3
Study 1. ENA graduates and temporary exit

3.3.1
Motivation and population definition

The National School of Administration ENA was founded in 1945 to select and train high civil servants for the French state. Graduates of this school (around 80 to 100 a year) play a central role in the political and administrative life of the country, and they constitute a natural population when studying elite civil servants
(
Bouzidi et al. 2010
;
Rouban 2014
)
. We study students admitted between 1990 and 2019, before the school was replaced by the National Institute for Public Service (INSP) in 2021
1
1

1

Data taken from Journal Officiel were first assembled and cleaned up by Nathann Cohen and put up on an API on the website steinertriples. Throughout the article, we rely on the amazing meta-data provided by this API, in addition to our own data cleaning procedures.
.
We only keep track of individuals who graduated from ENA and whose first position was working for the state, which we operationalize by only keeping individuals for which at least one trace can be found in the three years following their graduation. The number of students identified using this method by year of admission can be found in Appendix
B
and forms an exhaustive total of
N=2715
.

ENA graduates constitute a natural population when studying public-private career paths, as they hold a central position in the organization of the French state.
Rouban 2014
conducted a survey over 6 student year-groups (
N=620
) who graduated in 1969-1970, 1989-90, and 1999-2000. He reports that, 10 years after graduating, 8.3% of students from the first two cohorts were working in a private company, compared to 14.5% for the two next cohorts, and 9.5% for the last two, which indicates year-to-year variation but not a clear trend towards increasing exit rate to the private sector.

The results of
Rouban 2014
seem to show that the average time taken for an ENA graduate before leaving the public sector has drastically decreased over time, which would imply a stark increase in the exit rate from the state. We must however note that their methodology does not allow for comparing different cohorts. Indeed, many statistics considered in this study, like average time to leave to the private sector, are subject to right-censoring; this was not accounted for or acknowledged.
This is problematic as the observation frames are significantly different across cohorts: it was, at the time of publication, 44 years for the 1970 cohort, but only 14 years for the 2000 cohort. We assume the difference with our results is due to our methodology properly taking into account the different lengths of observation frames. We conclude that the variation reported by
Rouban 2014
is artefactual. The other quantitative conclusions of
Rouban 2014
are not subject to this bias, and are coherent with our results.

Bouzidi et al. 2010
focus on a subgroup of ENA graduates, of the “civil administrator” status working in the Finance Ministry, between 1960 and 2002 (
N=438
), using hand-collected data.
They show that, on their sample, about 40% of individuals went at least once into the private sector 20 years after graduating.
The advantage of their approach is that they used a formal duration analysis model and are not susceptible to right-censoring biases. They however do not attempt to estimate whether there was an acceleration or not on their period of study, and stress that doing such inference is difficult for later cohorts because of right-censoring. They also estimate time spent in the private sector using simulations using two Weilbull distributions, one for the public sector and one for the private sector. Our work can be seen as a discretization of such a procedure that also enables missing data imputation and inclusion of covariates.

We build our first study from these surveys and provide two improvements. First, thanks to our methodology, we can broaden the scope and analyze all students who graduated between 1991 and 2021, including the 55% do not have a LinkedIn profile, as well as the individuals who have a partially incomplete LinkedIn profile.
In addition, since our modeling accounts allows individuals to leave for the private sector and then return to the public sector, we can measure more precisely the public exit rate, and distinguish between individuals the public sector permanently or temporarily.

3.3.2
Models

We implement four initial configurations which are described here from simplest to most complex. Models 1 and 2 are introduced for pedagogical purposes.
Model 1
is a standard hidden Markov model: the covariate vector
W_{i,t}
only includes the intercept.
Model 2
is a Markov switching model for which only the autocorrelation covariates described in section
3.1.3
are included for the estimation of
\lambda
.

Models 3, 4 (and 5) include time-specific interest covariates and notably age. Age is encoded as time elapsed since the individual was admitted to ENA, on a range from 0 to 1 (with 1 corresponding to the entire time frame, 32 years). Age is modeled in a semi-parametric fashion, using B-splines of degree 2, with 5 degrees of freedom including an intercept, and boundary knots in 0 and 1, leading to
g=1,...,5
covariates
S_{i,t}^{g}
in the linear model (with the R package
splines
). We do this in order to saturate the model on the age dimension, so we can safely identify an effect related to cohort or period effects. Using splines works better than multiple powers of age: under this parameterization, parameters are less correlated, so the sampler mixes faster. As the spline basis already includes an intercept, no general intercept is included.
Recall from our discussion in Section
3.1.3
that we have to use a more complex scheme to handle boundary effects for
\lambda
, and interact these five covariates with
\mathbb{I}_{A(1)=0}
for all three models. More specifically,
Model 3
adds a time-varying component depending on the age of individuals for
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
.
Model 4
adds a cohort effect for the estimation of
\gamma^{0}
,
\gamma^{1}
, and
\lambda
. Cohort is encoded as the time at which the individual was admitted at ENA, ranging between 0 and 1.

Finally, we showcase that it is possible to answer questions that are less descriptive and more centered on substantive social mechanisms by studying the dependence between public-private paths and electoral cycles. We implement an additional
Model 5
, which is similar to Model 4, except for the fact that instead of a cohort effect, we estimate a period effect using a dummy variable

with value
1

0
else. Election time points correspond to Jan-Jun 1995, 2002, 2007, 2012, 2017, 2022 (French presidential elections). This dummy is shifted by one unit (so
t+1
or

) for
\lambda
.

The change in accuracy from Model 2 to Model 3 is small in size, but our interest is rather to perform hypothesis testing when including the additional age coefficients. Overall, this indicates that one could obtain better reconstruction by adopting more complex models, but this would be at the cost of model interpretability and hypothesis testing, which is our main focus.

3.3.5
Discussion of the substantive results

We conclude that
exit rates from the state have not increased on the period
for former ENA students, contrary to what we expected before conducting this study. If any change was to be inferred from data, it would rather be that elite civil servants have now a lower probability of leaving for the private sector than before, but it is possible to remain undecisive. Furthermore, the rate of former civil servants coming back to work in the public sector after leaving has significantly increased over the period. In other words,
when civil servants leave for the private sector, they now have a higher chance of coming back.
This is notably a contribution with respect to
(
Bouzidi et al. 2010
)
, who stressed that they could not study such evolutions given the constraints of their data despite the demands of their journal referees, and showcases the advantages of studying larger groups via data augmentation.

In addition, if there seems to be an interaction between political majority changes with election years and revolving doors for this population, it seems to affect the chance of coming back from the private sector than the probability of leaving.

3.4
Study 2. Public-private paths across the state

Many social scientists have early insisted on the usefulness of adopting a comparative point of view when describing civil servants populations and public-private paths
(
Charle 1987
)
. We are able with our method to describe the heterogeneity of public-private paths across the State and how they interact with gender.

3.4.1
Population definition

Top administrators are divided between groups and services that are sometimes tied to certain special statutes. While recent regulatory changes have attempted to increase mobility between them and foster a common organizational culture, they remain useful categories to base our approach upon.

Our study focuses on the following groups: three inspection groups whose mission is to audit public organizations organized thematically around financial affairs (igf, Inspection Générale des Finances), social affairs (igas, Inspection Générale des Affaires Sociales), and administration (iga, Inspection Générale de l’Administration); the Cour des Comptes (ccomptes), the supreme financial jurisdiction with a similar financial auditing function; the Conseil d’État (ce), whose members serve both as judges in the Supreme Administrative Court and as legal advisors to the government; the
préfets
(cprefet), state administration representatives in regions and departments; ambassadors (cdiplo, Corps Diplomatique); administrators of the national statistical and economics office (insee) who can serve key roles in the definition of economic policies; and finally, directors and assistant directors of two powerful services of the Ministry of the Economy, treasury (dgtresor), and public finances (dgfip).

This selection is voluntarily diverse and encompassing, as one modeling goal is to describe how public-private mobility is a heterogeneous phenomenon. We describe in Appendix
A
the method we use to link individuals to each group. The total number of individuals in each group alongside the proportion of them matched to a digital profile is given in Appendix
B
. Note that some individuals are affiliated with multiple services because their affiliation may change over time, a technicality we discuss below. The number of missing values is greater than in study 1, reaching 75%, which makes data augmentation even more valuable.

3.4.2
Relevant applied literature and hypotheses

Different researchers have studied the trajectories of high civil servants in France by defining populations based on organizational belonging at a certain time point, especially by focusing on their past trajectory.

Rouban 2010
studied the finance general inspection (igf) on the
1958-2008 period. igf appears to have an important place in the
revolving doors phenomenon, as they identified that over the 578
inspectors present in the organization between 1958-2009, a majority had
wandered at least once in the private sector (62%). They identify a
discontinuity around 1990, with a massification of individuals having
done a business school before attending ENA and then igf. They also observed
after 2000 a growing proportion of igf members who have had an
experience in the private sector before coming back, which seems quite
aligned with results obtained in our first study with a positive cohort
effect for
\gamma^{1}
. However, we underlie that this study too is subject to bias because of the lack of accounting of right-censoring.

Kolopp 2021
conducted a study on the general direction of the treasury
(dgtresor) in the 1965-2010 period. They exhibited a gendered behavior: men are more likely than women to leave for the most prestigious private industries (especially banking); on the other hand, they do not seem to show gender differences in the overall probability of leaving the public sector. They describe that differentiated practices generate unequal outcomes at different points in careers, for example with the stress on international mobility for career advancement and differences in professional networks. If we have reasons to assume these factors also play a significant role for other organizations, their strength may vary, and other factors might come into play. Even if we don’t differentiate within private sector activities, we will compare behavior between men and women across the different organizations of our sample.

We attempt to build upon their results and propose a complete and
systematic comparison across the different organizations we mentioned.
This has value in the sense that it can qualify statements regarding the
most prestigious organizations that are represented in these studies:
are they really that special compared to other powerful services? For
example, are the Finance Inspection and Treasury that much more affected
by revolving doors compared to other services in the economy ministry or
other inspections? We can also test statements linked to individual
properties: do men have higher chances to leave for the private
sector, and then come back?

Models

We encode organizational membership using a set of

3.5
Discussion on the substantive results

We find three profiles of organizations depending on the chance of leaving and the chance of coming back: one with high chances of leaving but low chances of coming back; one with low chances of leaving but high chances of coming back; and Inspection of Finances standing out from the rest with high chances of both
to leave and to come back. Women have overall less chance to leave for the private sector than men, but the situation is not constant across public organizations.

4
General discussion

We have proposed a general methodology for studying career trajectories on large populations, relevant for many problems in the social sciences. This method relies on two data sources, one with high precision but low coverage, and another, consisting of imperfect traces, but with high coverage. Markov switching models with autoregressive components constitute a powerful and flexible tool that can be fruitfully used to analyze this kind of data. They can be well estimated using a custom MCMC sampler combining a Gibbs step and a Metropolis-within-Gibbs step, but remain flexible enough to test substantive research hypotheses. In addition, we showcased the issues of using misspecified HMMs using synthetic data examples and showed one could gain statistical power using our method.

We have showcased the usefulness of our approach in two applied studies on French civil servants and their trajectory into the private sector, extending the social scientific literature along the way. An innovation in our applied studies is found in the way data were collected and cleaned. Our philosophy was to automate as much work as possible, for example in the name-matching phase, which enabled us to study a population an order of magnitude larger than what is usually done in traditional elite research (prosopography studies rarely exceed a few hundred individuals). However, we were not able to automate everything away, like public-private organization name classification, and the main limitation would again become human time if a researcher attempted to scale up this work by another order of magnitude. Our MCMC sampling procedure is, compared to approximate calculations, more costly, which could also be a problem when trying to scale up this approach. We found that the main cost of the procedure was in the computation of the MLE in the initialization of each Metropolis step, in addition to the random walk which required multiple computations of the likelihood. The cost of the Gibbs step was less important as it was very easy to parallelize it across individuals. Enabling a better scaling of the procedure could enable to study of a larger population, at a finer scale than based on six-month increments.

Appendix A
Data assemblage

This appendix documents the various procedures we used to assemble the dataset. To assemble data, we queried all traces of the JO linked to the corresponding organizations and manually crafted a rule-based approach to only keep those that define membership. All traces and LinkedIn profiles are attached to a full name. There is therefore a high risk of data being tarnished by homonyms. In this section, we describe the careful procedure used to eliminate this risk as much as possible, while preserving information.

A.1
Selection of administrative
traces

For every person in our population, we query the Journal Officiel to obtain every administrative order carrying the same name, which yields a first set of traces we name
S_{1}
. As many people have perfect homonyms, we have to filter this set. Most traces are tagged with one or multiple organizations, which range from small bureaus to the entire police force. To reduce this risk of perfect homonyms, we only keep traces linked to organizations significantly over-represented in
S_{1}
compared to the entire Journal Officiel. For each organization with the set
O
of traces linked to it, we compute:

\frac{\mathbb{P}[O|S_{1}]}{\mathbb{P}[O]}=\frac{\#\{\text{traces linked to org}\ O\}\cap S_{1}}{\sum_{x}\#\{\text{traces linked to org}\ x\}\cap S_{1}}/\frac{\#\{\text{traces linked to org}\ O\}}{\sum_{x}\#\{\text{traces linked to org}\ x\}}

which can be interpreted as a relative risk score. We only keep traces linked to organizations with a score above 2, or above 1 if they have fewer than 1000 traces, or above 0.5 if they have fewer than 100 traces. This effectively removes organizations such as the army or the police where many promotions are described for the entire force, and
constitute the main sources of perfect homonyms in
S_{1}
. For traces that are not linked to any organization, we also include them if they are a “délégation de signature” (meaning an authority delegates administrative power to the civil servant, a practice that remains limited in scope) or if it states an admission into a “grand corps”. This enables a good tradeoff between minimizing the homonymy risk and keeping as much data as possible, and yields a second set of traces
S_{2}
. For the first population, we then manually search for every individual for which we cannot find any trace upon graduation, and document the cases of name-changing for individuals who got married during their time as students (around 200 individuals) and add the relevant traces to the total after filtering them using the same procedure. For the second population, if a trace in
S_{2}
documents the person has had another name, we also add the relevant traces after passing them through the same filters as before (this also represented around 200 individuals). This yields our final set of traces
S_{3}
.

A.2
Linkage with digital profiles

For each person in our population, we search on LinkedIn for up to three profiles that share the same name, download them, and then only keep the ones that list an experience in our starting set of organizations. For example, if an individual is included because they are part of the Statistical office, we only keep profiles with such an affiliation, using a rule-based approach. When more than one profile qualifies, we score the candidate profiles based on their affiliations and only keep the one with the highest score. Overall, we designed our scheme to minimize false matchings between sources rather than maximizing the number of profiles that would be matched; manual inspection shows that it is very robust to homonymy problems in the matching between the two sources. The proportion of individuals that could be matched with a digital profile for subgroups can be found in Appendix
B
.

For each profile linked to an individual, we obtain a list of dated professional positions that are shared between the public and private sectors (or “spheres”, see
Daho and
Gally 2018
;
France and
Vauchez 2017
). We classify organization names between public and private positions, using a rule-based method, progressively adding filters to capture all public organizations. This strategy enabled us to cover manually the entire sample. Companies entirely owned by the state (e.g. La Poste, France Television) are also coded as public, alongside companies with the “EPIC” status (e.g. SNCF, OSEO). Contrary to
Daho and
Gally 2018
, companies that only have the state as a minority shareholder (e.g. Renault, TotalEnergies) are coded as private. In addition, positions at Universities and research bodies are not coded as either public or private: it appears they correspond to associate teaching positions that do not constitute the main occupation for people in our population, and so we leave them out. This reasoning leads us to also not code implication in a political campaign, as this is often superposed to other engagements. The same goes for positions in associations, naturally including charities, but also positions in unions, which include corporate representation bodies such as Medef. Finally, to ensure homogeneity with Journal Officiel, we do not include positions in world organizations (e.g. World Health Organization). This strategy of not coding uncertain cases is enabled by our modeling approach, as it doesn’t require full trajectories, and reduces the impact of such decisions on results. Without claiming this coding scheme is perfect, these decisions only have a minor impact on the substantial research findings, and all 1488 coding filters will be made fully accessible in the appendix for re-use.

and

.

C.2
Data heterogeneity concerns and modular inference

In our inference procedure, we use both values of
(X_{t}^{i})
which have been observed (on LinkedIn), and values which have been simulated from the appropriate conditional; both types of values will be used to infer the parameters of interest. As our two data sources are of very different nature, the heterogeneity it may create can be disquieting. Even in the slightest misspecification case, simulated data could be a lot less trustworthy than observed data, and errors on the former could be propagated to the entire estimation procedure. In addition, even if we attempted to
classify digital data so it would match as best as possible administrative accounts, some residual artefactual differences could still be observed.

This problem of having multiple, heterogeneous data modules is common and has been discussed in the Bayesian literature
Jacob
et al. 2017
. For each configuration, we consider two settings: (a) all values are treated equally; (b) simulated values are given a weight of 0.01, whereas observed values are given a weight of 1. This second strategy effectively grants all importance to individuals who have a digital profile.

Simulations in modular inference control condition leads to a similar conclusion on
\gamma^{1}
, with a posterior on
\gamma^{0}
a little more shifted to the negative side.
