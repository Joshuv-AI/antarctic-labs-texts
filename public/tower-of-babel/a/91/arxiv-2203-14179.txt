Ginzburg-Landau Equations on Non-compact Riemann Surfaces
Nicholas M. Ercolani, Israel Michael Sigal, Jingxuan Zhang

Abstract
. We study the Ginzburg-Landau equations on line bundles over non-compact Riemann surfaces with constant negative curvature. We prove existence of solutions with energy strictly less than that of the constant curvature (magnetic field) one. These solutions are the non-commutative generalizations of the Abrikosov vortex lattice of superconductivity. Conjecturally, they are (local) minimizers of the Ginzburg-Landau energy. We obtain precise asymptotic expansions of these solutions and their energies in terms of the curvature of the underlying Riemann surface. Among other things, our result shows the spontaneous breaking of the gauge-translational symmetry of the Ginzburg-Landau equations.

2.3.

In this subsection, we state the extension of the Chern-Weil

Recall that

Let

(

and the topology of the line bundle

Indeed,

7.
Extension to the case
\dim K>1

In this section, we illustrate how to extend the main existence results in Section
1
to the degenerate case, with
D=\dim K>1
. We also show how to extend energy estimates to this more general setting (in Section
7.2
).

7.1.
Existence theory

We now make some remarks on the possibility of solving the bifurcation equations (
4.7
) in the case when
D>1
where
D
is the complex dimension of
K
. The Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction detailed in section
4.2
carries over mutatis mutandis for
D>1
. The essential change comes in section

4.3
, as
we will now choose a hermitian orthonormal basis
\xi_{j}\,\,\,1\leq j\leq D
for
K
. Define

v=v_{st}=(\phi(s),\gamma(t))
,
where
s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{D})
,
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\dim\Omega})
,

\phi(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}s_{j}\xi_{j}
, and
\gamma(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\dim\Omega}t_{k}\eta_{k}
as in (
4.32
).

Taking inner products of the bifurcation equation (
4.30
)
respectively with
\xi_{j}
and
\eta_{k}
, this equation can be rewritten in terms of the coordinates
s,t,r
as

(7.1)

\displaystyle(\kappa^{2}r-b)s_{j}{+H_{\psi,j}(s,t,r)}

\displaystyle=0,\quad j=1,\ldots,D,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\dim\Omega}B_{kl}(s)t_{l}+H_{\alpha,k}(s,t,r)

\displaystyle=0,\quad k=1,\ldots,\dim\Omega,

where
B_{k,\ell}(s)=\langle\eta_{k},|\phi(s)|^{2}\eta_{\ell}\rangle_{\vec{L}^{2}}.

The remainders satisfy the estimates

(7.3)

\displaystyle H_{\psi,j}(s,t,r)=

\displaystyle O(\left\lvert s\right\rvert\left\lvert t\right\rvert)+O(\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{3}),

(7.4)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,k}(s,t,r)=

\displaystyle O(\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{2})+O(\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{4}),

and similarly for their derivatives.

The solution of (
7.2
) is based on the following classical result of Krasnoselski that reduces, under appropriate conditions, a vector bifurcation problem to a scalar one.

Theorem 7.1

(
[
6
, Chapt. 4 ]
)
.

Let
E:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be
C^{2}
of the form

(7.5)

\displaystyle E(\ell,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell s+H(\ell,s)

where
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ell\in\mathbb{R}
and
H(\ell,s)=O(|s|^{2})
as
|s|\to 0
uniformly in a neighborhood of
\ell=0
.
Assume that
n
is odd; then,
(\ell,s)=(0,0)
is a bifurcation point of
E(\ell,s)
; i.e., there are solutions
(\ell,s)
with
s\neq 0
of the equation
E(\ell,s)=0

in every neighborhood of
(\ell,s)=(0,0)
.

Remark 13
.

We note that (
7.5
) is the standard normal form for a nonlinear problem whose linearization has
\ell=0
as an isolated eigenvalue of multiplicity

n\geq 1
.

We defer the elegant proof of Theorem
7.1
to an Appendix
D
, and turn to applying it to our problem.

The first step is to consider solutions of (
7.1
). We find that

Lemma 7.2
.

Eq. (
7.2
) has a unique solution
t=t(r,s)
in a small neighbourhood of
(b/\kappa^{2},0)
, such that
st(s,r)
is
C^{2}
.

We momentarily defer the proof of this lemma in order to apply it to solving the full bifurcation problem. As a consequence of the lemma, insertion of
t(s,r)
into the higher order terms (
7.3
) of (
7.1
) results in a biifurcation equation that is
C^{2}
which will enable us to satisfy regularity conditions of Theorem
7.1
. We now want to rewrite (
7.1
) in terms of real variables by setting
s_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}
. We also apply a constant gauge transformation to reduce
s_{1}
to
s_{1}=x_{1}
. With this gauge fix in place, we
take
s
to denote the coordinates,
s=(x_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\dots x_{D},y_{D})
, which coordinatizes
\mathbb{R}^{2D-1}
. So in our application
n=2D-1
which
is odd so that condition of the theorem will be satisfied. With this gauge fix in place, (
7.1
) is precisely in the normal form (
7.5
). Indeed, setting
\ell=\kappa^{2}r-b
and substituting the solution
t=t(r,s)
found in Lemma
7.2
to (
7.1
), the equation takes the vector form

(7.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(\ell,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell s+H(s,r)=0

where
H(s,r)=O(|s|^{2})
.
Since
\tilde{G}(\ell,s)
is
C^{2}
, all conditions of Theorem
7.1
are satisfied. This establishes the existence of a supercritical zero. Given that, an application of the vector implicit function theorem yields the existence of a smooth branch of zeroes extending from the bifurcation point to the supercritical zero. Hence, Proposition 4.1 extends to be true for
D>1
.

As a brief illustration we note how Theorem
7.1
, for the case
D=1
aligns with our earlier proof of Lemma
4.4
. From the proof of Theorem
7.1
given in Appendix
D
, when
n=1
, the constraint simply reduces to
s\hat{E}(s)=0
. So for each
s\neq 0
,
\hat{E}(s)=0
.
It follows that the unique
C^{2}
curve
\ell(s)
found in the proof of Theorem
7.1
determines the branch of supercritical solutions to the bifurcation equation. Applying this to (
7.6
), this is the branch
\kappa^{2}r(s)-b
, determining a unique
r(s)
that satisfies

(7.7)

r(s)=\frac{b}{\kappa^{2}}+O(\left\lvert s\right\rvert^{2}).

We now turn to the proof of the Lemma
7.2
.

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

We consider the quadratic form associated to the leading terms of (
7.2
),

(7.8)

\displaystyle\Phi(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle B(s)t,t\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{\dim\Omega}t_{k}t_{\ell}B_{k\ell}(s)

(7.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\omega,|\phi(s)|^{2}\omega\rangle_{\vec{L}^{2}}

It is manifest from this that
\Phi
is real valued and, away from
s=0
, defined, differentiable and
positive definite as a quadratic form in
{t}
.

To understand
\Phi
in a neighborhood of
s=0
we do a (real) algebro-geometric blow-up of
s
-space at the origin.
This construction replaces the origin by a compact hypersurface diffeomorphic to the projective space of all local directions through the origin. More precisely, in our situation, the blow-up is defined as follows. Let
n=2D-1
and
consider the product space

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
coordinatized by the affine coordinates
\vec{w}=(\vec{x},\vec{y})
and

\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
coordinatized by the
homogeneous
coordinates
\vec{z}=[z_{1},\dots z_{n}]
. The blow-up of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
at the
origin, which we’ll denote by
M
, is the closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
defined by the equations

\{w_{i}z_{j}-w_{j}z_{i}=0\,\,|\,\,i,j=1,\dots,n\}
. One has a natural map
\pi:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
induced by projection onto the first factor. It is
fairly straightforward to check that away from
\vec{w}=0
,
\pi
is a diffeomorphism. However,
\pi^{-1}(0)\simeq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
; it consists of all
points of the form
0\times[z_{1},\dots z_{n}]
. Now to see that points of
\pi^{-1}(0)
are in 1:1 correspondence with the set of lines through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, note that a line
L
through the origin is parametrically given by
w_{i}=c_{i}\sigma
where the
c_{i}
are not all zero. Then consider the lift of this line to
\tilde{L}=\pi^{-1}(L-0)
in
M-\pi^{-1}(0)
whose parametrization is
w_{i}=c_{i}\sigma,z_{i}=c_{i}\sigma
. However, since
z_{i}
are homogeneous coordinates, one may as well take this parametrization to be
w_{i}=c_{i}\sigma,z_{i}=c_{i}
. These equations are well-defined for
\sigma=0
, defining the closure of
\tilde{L}
in
M
which meets
\pi^{-1}(0)
in the point
[c_{1},\dots,c_{n}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. This defines the mapping,
L\to[c_{1},\dots,c_{n}]
that gives
the 1:1 correspondence between lines through the origin and points of
\pi^{-1}(0)
. For further explanation and details we refer the reader to

[
18
]
on which the above is based.

Applying this to
\Phi
, we set
A_{jm}=\int_{F_{\Sigma}}\xi_{m}\bar{\xi}_{j}\omega\wedge*\omega=\mu_{jm}+i\nu_{jm}
. Then one has a representation of
\Phi
as

(7.10)

\displaystyle\Phi(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq m\leq D}\left(A_{jm}s_{j}\bar{s}_{m}+\bar{A}_{jm}\bar{s}_{j}s_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mu_{jm}(x_{j}x_{m}+y_{j}y_{m})-2\nu_{jm}(y_{j}x_{m}-x_{j}y_{m}).

To lift
\Phi
to
M
we set
\vec{c}=[a_{1},a_{2},b_{2},\dots a_{D},b_{D}]
. Applying the corresponding parametrization in
\Phi
one has

(7.11)

\displaystyle\Phi(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sigma^{2}\left(\mu_{jm}(a_{j}a_{m}+b_{j}b_{m})-\nu_{jm}(b_{j}a_{m}-a_{j}b_{m})\right).

Under the blow-up change of coordinates to
(\vec{c},\sigma,r)
, the higher order terms scale similarly but involve powers of
\sigma
greater than 2.
Consequently, the terms in
(1/\sigma^{2})
times the quadratic form associated to (
7.2
) are polynomial and so these equations are clearly
C^{2}
in the blow-up coordinates. They have a leading term that is independent of
\sigma
. Hence the positivity seen
in (
7.9
) is inherited by
(1/\sigma^{2})\Phi(s,t)
from (
7.11
). But the latter is defined just in terms of
\vec{c}
which are coordinates on a compact projective space. So by continuity,
(1/\sigma^{2})\Phi(s,t)
realizes its infimum at some point in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. But
this infimum is bounded away from zero. So
B(s)
is globally invertible and, by the implicit function theorem,
t
is smoothly defined in the blow-up coordinates and
t
is
O(\sigma^{2})
in these coordinates and so vanishes in a continuous fashion as
\sigma\to 0
. Hence
t
on the blowup
M
can be pushed forward under
\pi
to
t(s,r)
in the original
s
-space. Similarly for the first derivatives of
t(s,r)
and the second derivatives of
st(s,r)
.
∎

7.2.
Energy estimates

It is possible to extend the energy estimate proved in Corollary
1.6
to the degenerate case.
In general, if one drops the second part in condition (
1.14
),
then, for the solution
(\psi_{s(r)},a_{s(r)})
constructed in (
1.22
), we have,

(7.12)

{\mathcal{E}(\psi_{s(r)},a_{s(r)},h_{r})\geq\mathcal{E}(0,a^{b_{r}},h_{r})}{}-\frac{{\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert_{r}}}{4}\frac{\left\lvert\kappa^{2}-{b_{r}}\right\rvert^{2}}{(\kappa^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2})\beta(r)+\tfrac{1}{2}}+O(\left\lvert\kappa^{2}-{b_{r}}\right\rvert^{3})

for
\kappa\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
, and the opposite inequality for
\kappa<1/\sqrt{2}
.

Indeed, for
\dim K>1
, we have
\beta(r)\leq\langle\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{4}\rangle
for all
\phi\in K,\,\langle\left\lvert\phi\right\rvert^{2}\rangle=1
. This, together with (
6.16
), gives (
7.12
) depending on the sign of
\kappa^{2}-\frac{1}{2}
.

One can also introduce the ‘upper Abrikosov function’,

(7.13)

\displaystyle\beta_{+}(r):={\max\left\{{\langle\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{4}\rangle}:\xi\in K(r),\langle\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\rangle=1\right\}.}

which leads to the upper energy bound, for

,

(7.14)

{\mathcal{E}(\psi_{s(r)},a_{s(r)},h_{r})\leq\mathcal{E}(0,a^{b_{r}},h_{r})}{}-\frac{{\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert_{r}}}{4}\frac{\left\lvert\kappa^{2}-{b_{r}}\right\rvert^{2}}{(\kappa^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2})\beta_{+}(r)+\tfrac{1}{2}}+O(\left\lvert\kappa^{2}-{b_{r}}\right\rvert^{3}).

For
\kappa_{c+}(r):=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{+}(r)}\right)}
, estimate (

) ensures that

\mathcal{E}(\psi_{s(r)},a_{s(r)},h_{r})<\mathcal{E}(0,a^{b_{r}},h_{r})

.

Appendix A
Standard definitions and notation

We sketch very briefly some notions and definitions relevant for us .
To fix ideas, in what follows,

is a line bundle over a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, with fibers isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
and the gauge group

.

A.1.
Connections

A connection (gauge field),
a
, is a real-valued one-form with certain transformation properties.
For a connection
a
, defines the covariant derivative,
\nabla_{a}
, on sections of
E
, which can be written locally as
\nabla_{a}\psi=\nabla\psi-ia\psi
.
Here
\nabla
is a fixed connection (say, the Levi-Civita one).

A connection
a
defines the curvature two-form
F_{a}:=da
.
A connection
a
on
E
is said to be a
constant
curvature connection
if its curvature is of the form

(A.1)

\displaystyle da=b\omega,

for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\omega
is the standard hyperbolic
2
-form on
\Sigma
.

By
[
5
, Lem. 3.2]
, a constant
curvature connection,
a
, solves the (
static
) Maxwell equation

(A.2)

d^{*}da=0.

Let
x^{i},\,i=1,2
be a local coordinate on
\Sigma
.
With the summation convention understood, we can write a connection (gauge field),
a
,
and the covariant derivative,
\nabla_{a}
, as
a=a_{i}dx^{i}
and
\nabla_{a}=\nabla_{i}dx^{i}
, where

\nabla_{j}:=\partial_{i}-ia_{j}
.
Let
d
be the exterior derivative on
\Sigma
.
Then, for a section
\psi
and 1-form
B=B_{i}dx^{i}
,

d_{a}\psi=\nabla_{i}\psi dx^{i},\ dB=\partial_{i}B_{j}dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j},\ d^{*}B=-\partial_{i}B_{i}

and the curvature,
F_{a}=da
, of

is given by

(A.3)

\displaystyle F_{a}=\big[\nabla_{i},\nabla_{j}\big]dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j},\ \quad\big[\nabla_{i},\nabla_{j}\big]=\partial_{i}a_{j}-\partial_{j}a_{i}.

A.2.
Automorphy factor

Let
\Sigma=\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
be a non-compact Riemann surface of the form (
2.1
).
Let

\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
be the first fundamental group of
\Sigma
,
which we identify with

.
A map

\rho(\gamma,z):\pi_{1}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{H}\cong\Gamma\times\mathbb{H}\to U(1)

Appendix B

In this section, we reproduce with minor modifications results of
[
5

on classification of automorphy factors and constant curvature connections.

Recall that a character of
\Gamma
is a homomorphism
\chi:\Gamma\rightarrow U(1)
. For other standard definitions, see Appendix
A

Theorem B.1

(classification of automorphy factor)
.

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
\rho_{\beta}:PGL(2,\mathbb{R})\times{\mathbb{H}}\rightarrow U(1)
, given by

(B.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(\gamma,z)

\displaystyle=\biggr[\frac{c+d}{c\bar{z}+d}\biggl]^{\beta},\quad\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in PGL(2,\mathbb{R}),

satisfies the co-cycle condition

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(s\cdot t,z)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\beta}(s,t\cdot z)\rho_{\beta}(t,z),\quad\forall s,t\in PGL(2,\mathbb{R}).

Consequently, for any Fuchsian group
\Gamma
s.t.
\Sigma:={\mathbb{H}}/\Gamma
has finite area and
b:=\frac{2\pi n}{|\Sigma|}
, and any character
\chi:\Gamma\rightarrow U(1)
,
the map
\rho_{b,\chi}:\Gamma\times{\mathbb{H}}\rightarrow U(1)
given by

(B.2)

\displaystyle\rho_{b,\chi}(\gamma,z)

\displaystyle=\chi(\gamma)\rho_{b}(\gamma,z),\quad\gamma\in\Gamma,

is also an automorphy factor.
Two automorphy factors related as in (
B.2
) are said to be equivalent.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b:=\frac{2\pi n}{|\Sigma|}
.
Then, the first Chern number of
\rho_{b,\chi}
is
c_{1}(\rho_{b,\chi})=n
. Hence any automorphy factor
\rho:\Gamma\times{\mathbb{H}}\rightarrow U(1)
of degree
n
is equivalent to
\rho_{b}
as in (
A.5
).

Proof.

Let
s=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ t=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}e&f\\
g&h\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
. Using (
B.1
), we compute
for any
\beta
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}(s\cdot t,z)

\displaystyle=\biggr[\frac{(ce+dg)z+(cf+dh)}{(ce+dg)\overline{z}+(cf+dh)}\biggl]^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\biggr[\frac{c(ez+f)+d(gz+h)}{(c(e\overline{z}+f)+d(g\overline{z}+h)}\biggl]^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\biggr[\frac{c\frac{ez+f}{gz+h}+d}{c\frac{e\overline{z}+f}{g\overline{z}+h}+d}\biggl]^{\beta}\biggr[\frac{gz+h}{g\overline{z}+h}\biggl]^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\beta}(s,t\cdot z)\rho_{\beta}(t,z).

Using the formula for the Chern class,

, of a co-cycle

(see
[

]

is the area of

Theorem B.2

(classification of constant curvature connections)
.

For any

\displaystyle A^{b}=by^{-1}dx,

The description in the above theorem does not depend at all on the complex structure of the underlying Riemann surface.

Proof of Theorem

We begin with some preliminary constructions.
We consider the trivial bundle,
\tilde{E}:={\mathbb{H}}\times\mathbb{C}
with the standard complex structure on

associated to the standard hyperbolic metric

Since we work here on a global product space, it is natural to take the fiber metric to be induced from the metric on the base. So we take the metric on the fiber

Let the connection

\partial_{A}^{\prime}

Recall from Section

According to (

In the remaining of this proof, we omit the superindex

Proof of constant curvature.

Using that
\omega=\frac{i}{2}\operatorname{Im}(z)^{-2}dz\wedge d\overline{z}
, we find

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}A_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\frac{ib}{z-\overline{z}}d\bar{z}\wedge dz=\frac{-ib}{(z-\overline{z})^{2}}dz\wedge d\overline{z}=\frac{ib}{4\operatorname{Im}(z)^{2}}dz\wedge d\overline{z}=\frac{b}{2}\omega.

Since

, this gives the desired result.

Proof of uniqueness.

If
A
and

, satisfy

then
d(A-B)=0
. It follows from the simple connectedness of

that

and

This completes the proof.

Proof of equivariance.
For a generic isometry

\frac{\partial s(z)}{\partial z}=(\gamma z+\delta)^{-2}
and
\operatorname{Im}(s(z))=\frac{\operatorname{Im}(z)}{|\gamma z+\delta|^{2}}

\displaystyle s^{*}\bar{A}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{2\operatorname{Im}(s(z))}\overline{\frac{\partial s(z)}{\partial z}}d\overline{z}=\frac{k|\gamma z+\delta|^{2}}{2\operatorname{Im}(z)}\frac{d\overline{z}}{(\gamma\overline{z}+\delta)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{2\operatorname{Im}(z)}\frac{\gamma z+\delta}{\gamma\overline{z}+\delta}d\overline{z}=\frac{b}{2\operatorname{Im}(z)}d\overline{z}+\frac{b(\gamma z-\gamma\overline{z})}{2\operatorname{Im}(z)(\gamma\overline{z}+\delta)}d\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}_{c}+\frac{ib\gamma}{\gamma\overline{z}+\delta}d\overline{z}=:\bar{A}_{c}+\overline{\partial}\tilde{f}_{s},

where
\tilde{f}_{s}
is the function defined by the last relation, i.e.
\overline{\partial}\tilde{f}_{s}=\frac{ib\gamma}{\gamma\overline{z}+\delta}d\overline{z}.

Solving this equation, we find

\tilde{f_{s}}=b\text{ln}(\gamma\overline{z}+\delta)+c_{s}.

Now, we define
f_{s}:=2\operatorname{Re}\tilde{f_{s}}
and use that
\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\partial}\tilde{f_{s}})=d(\operatorname{Re}\tilde{f_{s}})

Appendix C
Chern-Weil Correspondence

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

The argument below, due to D. Chouchkov, is streamlined from classical results from
[
17
, Chap. II.4]
.

Let
F_{\Sigma}\subset\mathbb{H}
be a fundamental domain of
\Gamma
.
Let
A_{i},B_{i},i=1,\dots g,
and
S_{i},i=1,\dots m,
be the hyperbolic and parabolic generators of
\Gamma
, respectively (no elliptic ones by assumption).
Let
\alpha_{i},\alpha^{\prime}_{i},\beta_{i},\beta^{\prime}_{i},i=1,\dots g,
and
\delta_{j},\delta_{j}^{\prime},j=1,\dots m
be the sides of
F_{\Sigma}
.

As
in the beginning of Section
3
,
we have the following relations:

A_{i}^{*}\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{i}^{\prime},B_{i}^{*}\beta_{i}=-\beta_{i}^{\prime},i=1,\ldots g,\text{ and }S_{j}^{*}\delta_{j}=-\delta_{j}^{\prime},j=1,\dots m.

as well as the decomposition

(C.1)

\displaystyle\partial F_{\Sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha^{\prime}_{i}+\beta_{i}+\beta^{\prime}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\delta_{j}+\delta_{j}^{\prime}).

Here the plus sign denotes disjoint union.

Let
A
be an equivariant
1
-form on
F_{\Sigma}
that
corresponds to the connection
a
on
E\to\Sigma
,
as in Sections
A.2
–
A.3
.
By definition (
A.9
), the theorem is proved once we establish

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial F_{\Sigma}}A=c_{1}(\sigma).

Since the
1
-form
A
is gauge-equivariant, we have

(C.2)

\gamma^{*}A=A-i\rho_{\gamma}^{-1}d\rho_{\gamma},\ \forall\gamma\in\Gamma,\,z\in\partial F_{\Sigma}.

This, together with the definition
\rho(\gamma,z)=e^{i\sigma_{\gamma}(z)}
, implies

\int_{\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{\prime}}A=\int_{\alpha_{i}}(A-A_{i}^{*}A)=-\int_{\alpha_{i}}d\sigma_{A_{i}}=\sigma_{A_{i}}(v_{i})-\sigma_{A_{i}}(v_{i+1})\quad(i=1,\dots g),

where
v_{i},v_{i+1}
are the vertices of
F_{\Sigma}
spanned by the side
\alpha_{i}
. Similarly we proceed with
\int_{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}^{\prime}}A
and
\int_{\delta_{j}+\delta_{j}^{\prime}}A
. In the last case, since the gauge exponent
\sigma_{S_{i}}(z)
is independent of
z
, we have
\int_{\delta_{j}+\delta_{j}^{\prime}}A=0
, so these term do not contribute.

Summing up these contributions and using (
C.1
), we arrive at

\int_{\partial F_{\Sigma}}A=\sum_{1}^{g}[\sigma_{A_{i}}(v_{i})-\sigma_{A_{i}}(v_{i+1})+\sigma_{B_{i}}(w_{i})-\sigma_{B_{i}}(w_{i+1})],

where
v_{i},v_{i+1}
and
w_{i},w_{i+1}

are the vertices of
F_{\sigma}
spanned by
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
,
respectively.
Note that the vertices
v_{i},v_{i+1}
,
w_{i},w_{i+1}
and
u_{i},u_{i+1}
are related as
w_{i}=v_{i+1},w_{i+1}=A_{i}v_{i+1}
and
v_{i+1}=B_{i}^{-1}v_{i}
, which gives

(C.3)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial F_{\Sigma}}A=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{1}^{g}[\sigma_{A_{i}}(v_{i})-\sigma_{A_{i}}(B_{i}^{-1}v_{i})+\sigma_{B_{i}}(B_{i}^{-1}v_{i})-\sigma_{B_{i}}(A_{i}B_{i}^{-1}v_{i})].

By construction,

v_{i}=B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}B_{i}^{-1}C_{i+1}\dots C_{g}z_{0}=A_{i}^{-1}C_{i}\dots C_{g}z_{0},

with
C_{i}:=A_{i}B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}B_{i}^{-1}\text{ and some }z_{0}\in F_{\Sigma}.

Hence, r.h.s. of (
C.3
) agrees with the definition of the first Chern number

c_{1}
in (
A.8
). This proves the theorem. ∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
7.1

One reduces to a scalar bifurcation problem by considering the scalar-valued function

(D.1)

\displaystyle\phi(\ell,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|s|^{2}}\langle s,\ell s+H(\ell,s)\rangle.

Manifestly,
\phi(0,0)=0
and
\partial\phi(0,0)/\partial\ell=1
. It then follows from the implicit function theorem that there exists a unique function
\ell(s)
,
for small
s
, such that
\phi(\ell(s),s)=0
. Set
\hat{E}(s)=E(\ell(s),s)
, where, recall,
E(\ell,s)=\ell s+H(\ell,s)
, and let
S_{\epsilon}:=\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lvert s\right\rvert=\epsilon\right\}
be the
\epsilon
-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
\hat{E}(s)
, regarded as a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is everywhere tangent to
S_{\epsilon}
. To see this, simply note that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\ell(s),s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|s|^{2}}\langle s,\ell(s)s+H(\ell(s),s)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|s|^{2}}\langle s,\hat{E}(s)\rangle,

which precisely states that the vector field
\hat{E}(s)
is everywhere tangent to the sphere. Since
n
is odd, the sphere is even dimensional. But every
vector field on an even dimensional sphere has as zero vector, i.e.
\hat{E}(s)=0
has a solution on
S_{\epsilon}
. The theorem follows.
