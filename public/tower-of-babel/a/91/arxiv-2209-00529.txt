Abstract
Tree-based demappers for multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) detection such as the sphere decoder can achieve near-optimal performance but incur high computational cost due to their sequential nature. In this paper, we propose the perturbed linear demapper (PLM), which is a novel data-driven model for computing soft outputs in parallel. To achieve this, the PLM learns a distribution centered on an initial linear estimate and a log-likelihood ratio clipping parameter using end-to-end Bayesian optimization. Furthermore, we show that lattice-reduction can be naturally incorporated into the PLM pipeline, which allows to trade off computational cost against coded block error rate reduction. We find that the optimized PLM can achieve near maximum-likelihood (ML) performance in Rayleigh channels, making it an efficient alternative to tree-based demappers.



I
Introduction

Multiple-input and multiple-output detection is a fundamental problem in modern wireless communication enabling high data rates for transmission of multiple streams over multiple antennas
[
1
]
. MIMO demappers are typically required to calculate soft probability estimates of transmitted bits, which can then be used as informative priors for
error correction codes
, such as LDPC codes. This, however, comes at the cost of increased computational complexity
[
2
]
, which is why it is crucial to develop fixed, low complexity detectors
[
3
]
that can achieve near-maximum-likelihood (near-ML) performance in terms of coded bit error rate (BER).

The simplest of such low complexity detectors are
linear MIMO detectors
, based on the Zero-Forcing (ZF) or Minimum-Mean Square Error (MMSE) paradigm. Linear MIMO detectors fail to account for inter-stream interference and for this reason are not typically considered a viable candidate for obtaining soft outputs in high correlation channels. To tackle this, tree based methods iteratively search the solution space for a set of likely candidates and have proven to attain near-ML performance at the cost of added complexity
[
4
,
5
]
. Nonetheless, the search procedure of these methods relies on manually designed heuristics, which offers little flexibility in terms of end-to-end optimization with respect to different channel statistics and error correction procedures.
Recently, there has been an increased interest in learning-based methods for MIMO detection, which have been shown to achieve state of the art results for predicting hard-output solutions
[
6
,
7
]
.
On the other hand, the design of learnable soft-output MIMO demappers with low complexity poses a much larger challenge and has yet to be explored.

To circumvent this issue of complexity, we propose the
perturbed linear demapper
, which learns a parametric distribution centered on a lattice reduced hard-output solution to generate a diverse set of candidates. Previously, lattice reduction (LR) aided linear demappers
[
8
]
have been shown to improve soft MIMO detection by perturbing an initial solution
[
9
]
or employing Schnorr-Euchner enumeration to obtain a set of most likely transmitted symbols
[
10
]
. Our key insight is that this idea can be generalized using minimal added complexity to allow for more flexibility as well as generate more reliable log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) for subsequent error correction. We achieve this by jointly learning parameters for (i) LLR clipping and (ii) the shape of the perturbation distribution using Bayesian optimization (BO)
[
11
]
on simulated data.

Contributions:
We take a soft-output LR-aided linear detector with LLR clipping on the output and use Bayesian optimization to select an optimal perturbation distribution and LLR clipping parameters with respect to the bit error rate, post-LDPC decoding. We show that our method achieves comparable BER to the low-complexity tree-based Parallel Fixed-Complexity Sphere Decoder (PFSD)
[
5
]
, while adding the benefit
of a candidate generation that is fully parallelizable and adaptable to channel statistics.

II-A

MIMO Demapping

We consider MIMO systems based on the famous V-BLAST architecture
[
12
]
. This is a multiple antenna system with
M
transmit and
N\geq M
receive antennas. A coded bit-stream is mapped to transmit symbol vectors
{\mathbf{x}}\in\bm{\Omega}^{M}
, where
\bm{\Omega}\subset\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}(\bm{1}+i\bm{1})
is a
2^{Q}
-QAM complex constellation for
\alpha>0
and even
Q
, the number of bits per symbol
x_{j}
.
\alpha
is chosen such that QAM symbols are normalized to unit power per dimension (i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{H}]={\mathbf{I}}_{M}
). Each symbol vector
{\mathbf{x}}
has a corresponding bit-decoding in
\{-1,1\}^{M\times Q}
, where
x_{j,b}
is the
b
th
bit in the
j
th
dimension/layer of
{\mathbf{x}}
. We assume a flat-fading environment with a block fading channel (constant gains per frame), changing independently frame-to-frame. We therefore model a single slot of the time-discrete complex baseband model as a linear system with additive white complex Gaussian noise (AWGN):

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{n}}.

(1)

{\mathbf{H}}
is an
N\times M
matrix, representing a Rayleigh fading channel, with i.i.d. complex Gaussian fading gain entries
H_{ij}\sim{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1/\sigma^{2})
for
\sigma>0
and
{\mathbf{n}}
is a zero-mean circular-symmetric complex Gaussian noise vector with identity covariance, (i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{n}}^{H}]={\mathbf{I}}_{N}
). This channel model has per-stream received SNR proportional to
\log(1/\sigma^{2})
. We also assume perfect channel estimation at the receiver. While some works lift the
M\times N
complex system to a
2M\times 2N
real system, we instead choose to perform all calculations in complex space.

In soft-output MIMO detection, we aim to compute bitwise log-likelihood ratios (LLRs)

\displaystyle L(x_{j,b})=\log\frac{p(x_{j,b}=1|{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{H}})}{p(x_{j,b}=-1|{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{H}})},

(2)

which can be fed as soft-information into a downstream error correction algorithm. This contrasts the hard-output problem, known as
integer least squares

[
13
]
, where we solve

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\bm{\Omega}^{M}}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}.

(3)

Given our AWGN model, the LLRs can be rewritten

\displaystyle L(x_{j,b})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{+}}-\lambda_{{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{-}}

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}\exp\left\{-\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\right\}

(5)

where
{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{+}\subset\bm{\Omega}^{M}
is the subset of points
{\mathbf{x}}
with bit
x_{j,b}=1
and likewise for
{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{-}
and
x_{j,b}=-1
. The corresponding bit of the larger of the two log likelihoods (in absolute value) is referred to as the
hypothesis bit
and the smaller, the
counter-hypothesis bit
. A large part of the complexity of soft-output demappers is in computing counterhypothesis bits, since while a single hypothesis vector
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
contains all the hypothesis bits, the counterhypothesis bits may be scattered across up to
MQ
counterhypothesis vectors. Sometimes, Equation
5
is replaced with the
max-log approximation
, which approximates the sum by the highest mass point as

\displaystyle\lambda_{{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}

\displaystyle\simeq\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}\left\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}.

(6)

This approximation, a variant of the hard-output problem, was shown in
[
2
]
to only incur a small performance loss of about 0.25 dB. Even with this simplification, however, computing the solution to Equation
6
is itself an NP-hard problem.

II-B

Linear Receivers

Linear detectors demap the received vector
{\mathbf{y}}
in two steps: left-multiplying Equation
1
by a linear filter matrix
{\mathbf{G}}
, followed by quantization to the constellation. Mathematically

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{linear}}=\lfloor{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}\rceil_{\bm{\Omega}^{M}}

(7)

where
\lfloor\bullet\rceil_{\bm{\Omega}^{M}}
denotes quantization and remapping into the constellation
\bm{\Omega}^{M}
. Different linear detectors make different choices about
{\mathbf{G}}
. For a finite set
{\mathcal{X}}
we define the quantization operator for a real scalar variable
x
as

\displaystyle\lfloor x\rceil_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\min_{y\in{\mathcal{X}}}|x-y|.

(8)

For complex scalars, we quantize the real and imaginary components independently and for vectors, we quantize each dimension independently too.

II-B
1
Zero-Forcing Detector

The Zero-Forcing detector uses the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the channel matrix:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}_{\text{ZF}}={\mathbf{H}}^{+}=({\mathbf{H}}^{H}{\mathbf{H}})^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{H}.

(9)

II-B
2
MMSE Detector

The MMSE detector is a more sophisticated linear receiver, which takes into account of the noise statistics of the channel. This sets

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}_{\text{MMSE}}=({\mathbf{H}}^{H}{\mathbf{H}}+{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}^{H}.

(10)

Note that we normalize the channel to use unit variance noise in our model, so
\sigma^{2}
is implicitly defined in
{\mathbf{H}}
. We can also write the detector as a Zero-Forcing detector over the
extended channel
, where

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathbf{G}}_{\text{MMSE}}}=\underline{{\mathbf{H}}}^{+},\quad\underline{{\mathbf{H}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{H}}\\
{\mathbf{I}}_{M\times M}\end{bmatrix},\quad\underline{{\mathbf{y}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{y}}\\
\bm{0}_{M}\end{bmatrix},

(11)

satisfying the identity

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}_{\text{MMSE}}{\mathbf{y}}=\underline{{\mathbf{G}}_{\text{MMSE}}}\,\underline{{\mathbf{y}}}.

(12)

II-B
3
Soft-output ZF/MMSE Detector

The above linear receivers only produce hard outputs. We can compute soft-outputs by rewriting the channel model as
[
14
]

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}=x_{j}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}+\left(\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}{\mathbf{h}}_{i}+{\mathbf{n}}\right)=x_{j}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}+{\mathbf{n}}_{j},

(13)

for
{\mathbf{h}}_{1},{\mathbf{h}}_{2},...
the columns of
{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{n}}_{j}
a new noise vector treating layers
i\neq j
as interference. If we marginalize out the symbols
x_{i\neq j}
, we are left with a new channel for
p({\mathbf{y}}|x_{j}{\mathbf{h}}_{j})
with covariance
{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{I}}+\sum_{i\neq j}{\mathbf{h}}_{i}{\mathbf{h}}_{i}^{H}
. We can turn this into a one dimensional problem by left-multiplying by a per-channel decorrelating filter
{\mathbf{w}}_{j}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{y}}=x_{j}{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}+{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{n}}_{j},\quad{\mathbf{w}}_{j}={\mathbf{K}}^{-1}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}

(14)

and approximating
p({\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{y}}|x_{j}{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{h}}_{j})
with a 1D complex Gaussian with matched variance. We then have

\displaystyle\lambda_{{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}\simeq\log\sum_{x_{j}\in\bm{\Omega}_{b}^{\pm}}\exp\left\{-({\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{y}}-x_{j}{\mathbf{w}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{h}}_{j})^{2}/\sigma_{j}^{2}\right\}

(15)

where
\sigma_{j}^{2}={\mathbf{h}}_{j}^{H}{\mathbf{K}}^{-1}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}
is the variance
{\mathbf{K}}
projected on to
{\mathbf{w}}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{b}^{\pm}
is the subset of constellation points with bit
b
set to
\pm 1
. If instead of using
({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{y}})
in the above we used
(\underline{{\mathbf{H}}},\underline{{\mathbf{y}}})
, then we would have a soft-output MMSE detector.

II-C

Lattice Reduction

The columns of
{\mathbf{H}}
form the
basis
of a
lattice

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}{\mathbf{h}}_{j}\middle|x_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}},\,j=1,...,M\right\}.

(16)

The lattice basis is non-unique, and we can transform between bases by right-multiplication of
{\mathbf{H}}
with a
unimodular matrix

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}\in GL(M,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}}).

(17)

where
GL(M,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}})
, the
\emph{generallineargroup}
over the complex integers, is the set of all matrices with complex integer entries and unit determinant. Each element of
GL(M,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}})
, thus each unimodular matrix, has an inverse, which is also unimodular. Due to the unit determinant, right-multiplication by
{\mathbf{U}}
is volume-preserving.

The unimodular matrices provide a change of basis for the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
and thus a reparametrization of the channel
{\mathbf{H}}
as
{\mathbf{H}}\mapsto{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}
. Crucially, it turns out that most MIMO detectors
1
1

1

A counterexample is a maximum likelihood detector, such as the sphere decoder.
are not invariant under this reparameterization, indicating that certain choices of
{\mathbf{U}}
are better than others. Concretely, if the columns of
{\mathbf{H}}
are orthogonal, there is no inter-stream interference and linear detectors are optimal. It has therefore been shown
[
8
]
that a good choice of
{\mathbf{U}}
is one that approximately orthogonalizes the columns of
{\mathbf{H}}
, measured in terms of the
log orthogonality defect

\displaystyle\log\delta({\mathbf{H}})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\log\|{\mathbf{h}}_{j}\|-\frac{1}{2}\log\det{\mathbf{H}}^{H}{\mathbf{H}},

(18)

a non-negative quantity, reaching zero when the columns of
{\mathbf{H}}
are orthogonal. Detectors using the lattice reduced channel matrix
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}
are called
LR-aided

[
8
]
. We can apply LR to linear detectors as shown in figure
1
. The only differences are that we use a different channel
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}
and quantize to the
reduced lattice

{\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{L}}
before transforming back and bounding to the original constellation
\bm{\Omega}^{M}
.
If we let
\bm{\beta}=\frac{\alpha}{2}(\bm{1}+i\bm{1})
, we can efficiently compute the lattice quantization operator of a lattice
{\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{L}}
as
[
9
]

\displaystyle\lfloor{\mathbf{z}}\rceil_{{\mathbf{U}}{\mathcal{L}}}=\alpha\lfloor\alpha^{-1}({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\beta})\rceil_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{m}}+{\mathbf{U}}\bm{\beta}.

(19)

III
Perturbed Linear Detection

III-A

Motivation

The complexity of the log-sum-exp computation in Equation
5
is determined by the size of the search-space
|{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}|=|\bm{\Omega}^{M}|/2=2^{QM-1}
, scaling exponentially in
M
and
Q
. In practice, to reduce this complexity we approximate it by replacing each set
{\mathcal{X}}_{j,b}^{\pm}
with a smaller
candidate set

{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}
. LLR computation can then be broken down into two steps:

1.

Candidate set
{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}
generation

2.

Compute LLR
\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}
: Equation
5
or Equation
6
.

For the candidate set generation, we propose a new class of model, which we call a
perturbed linear model
(PLM). For the LLR computation, we leverage classical methods, simply plugging our candidate sets
{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}
into Equation
5
or Equation
6
.

III-B

Perturbed Linear Model

The PLM generates a candidate set
{\mathcal{B}}
by taking the output
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}
of a linear receiver, adding small perturbations
\bm{\delta}
to it, and then quantizes back on to the constellation

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\lfloor{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}+\bm{\delta}\rceil_{\bm{\Omega}^{M}}\right\}_{\bm{\delta}\in\bm{\Delta}\cup\{\bm{0}\}}.

(20)

Using samples from the set
\bm{\Delta}\cup\bm{0}
instead of
\bm{\Delta}
ensures that we always include the Babai-estimate of equation
7
. We can then sift this set
{\mathcal{B}}
into individual candidate sets for each bit
j,b
set to
\pm 1
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{B}}\mid x_{j,b}=\pm 1\}.

(21)

Of course,
{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{+}\,\dot{\cup}\,{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{-}={\mathcal{B}}
for all
j
and
b
. The motivation for this is that the hypothesis
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
lies close to the linear receiver output
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}
, and can be found within a ball centered on
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}
spanned by
\bm{\Delta}
. Finding the counter-hypotheses, however, is non-trivial but we can search for them in a region around the hypothesis. Thus how we choose to populate
\bm{\Delta}
is important.

We consider three different choices for perturbation generation. Firstly, we sample in an ellipsoid (
\bm{\delta}_{e}
), given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{e}

\displaystyle=r{\mathbf{G}}(u^{-2M\kappa}{\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle u\sim{\mathcal{U}}(0,1),{\mathbf{r}}\sim{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{S}}_{M}

(22)

where
r>0
is a radius parameter,
u
is a real uniform random variable in interval
[0,1]
,
\kappa>0
is a radial weighting factor and
{\mathbf{r}}
is a random vector drawn from
{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{S}}_{M}
, the uniform distribution on a complex sphere of dimension
M
. The parameters
r
and
\kappa
are learnable. Equation
22
draws samples from a ball distribution and passes them through
{\mathbf{G}}
, which makes the distribution of samples ellipsoidal. When
\kappa>1
, the density of samples close to 0 increases. We correlate dimensions of samples with
{\mathbf{G}}
since, say, for Zero-Forcing detectors with
\rm{\mathbf{H}}^{+}
, decoded symbol vectors have the form
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{n}}
, which is centered on
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}
with additive noise
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{n}}
.
Second, we consider sampling a non-isotropic Gaussian (
\bm{\delta}_{g}
) defined as:

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{g}

\displaystyle=r{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{g}}\sim{\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1^{2}),

(23)

where
{\mathbf{g}}
is a complex Gaussian vector from a zero-centered unit-variance circular symmetric complex density. Equation
23
draws standard complex Gaussian samples and passes them through
{\mathbf{G}}
as well. Similarly,
r
is a learnable parameter.

Finally, we consider determinstic perturbations in the form of a
K
-QAM-constellation in each dimension (
\bm{\delta}_{s}
):

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{s}

\displaystyle=q{\mathbf{e}}_{i}

\displaystyle i\in\{1,...,M\},q\in\bm{\Omega}_{K}

(24)

where
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
is a one-hot vector with a one in position
i
, and
\bm{\Omega}_{K}
is a one dimensional
K
-QAM constellation. Equation
24
generates candidates by drawing a square around
{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{y}}
in each dimension, which is a similar setup to
[
9
]
. However,
[
9
]
does not study larger QAM-constellation sizes and thus expands only 4 points in each dimension. A key benefit of the perturbative method is that the generation of perturbations can be parallelized, leading to fast implementation.

III-C

LLR computation

After subset generation, we compute LLRs using the max-log approximation
[
15
]
as

\displaystyle\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{\pm}}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}.

(25)

In some cases, one of the sets
{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{+}
or
{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{-}
is empty and the corresponding
\lambda
defaults to
\infty
. We found we could improve our LLR outputs, when used for downstream LDPC decoding with LLR clipping to a default value
L_{\text{max}}
, such that

\displaystyle L(x_{j,b})=\min(\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{+}}-\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}}_{j,b}^{-}},L_{\text{max}}).

(26)

We optimize
L_{\text{max}}
for each SNR, storing it in a lookup table.

III-D

Bayesian Optimization

The whole demapper from input bits to output bits after LDPC decoding can be treated as a black box depending on a few parameters. These are, namely,
L_{\text{max}}
and the perturbation distribution parameters
r
and
\kappa
. We denote all these parameters
\bm{\theta}=\{r,\kappa,L_{\text{max}}\}
. We optimize these, using Bayesian optimization (BO)
[
16
]
, a machine learning-based global optimization technique. In particular, for each SNR we measure the coded BER
L_{\text{BER}}
averaged over a subset of
B
instances of the channel in Equation
1
for a given value of
\bm{\theta}
.
Note that here we use BO as an optimizer for the learning problem of finding parameters
\bm{\theta}
that give small
L_{\text{BER}}
when estimated during deployment over a test set of channel instances.
In more details,
we fit a Gaussian process
[
17
]
with Matérn covariance kernel to this training data of input–output pairs
\{(\bm{\theta}_{k},L_{\text{BER},k})\}_{k}
, which provides a differentiable Bayesian regression estimator of the
\bm{\theta}\mapsto L_{\text{BER},k}
mapping. This can then be used to choose the next value of
\bm{\theta}
in the optimization process by maximizing an acquisition function. Our implementation used the openly available
Scikit-optimize
library
2
2

2

https://github.com/scikit-optimize/scikit-optimize
.

The BO process is run as a separate ‘offline’ step to find the optimal values (up to noise and optimization constraints) of
\bm{\theta}
for every SNR. At deployment time, we simply look up each
\bm{\theta}
given the SNR of the channel, which in this work we assume to be known. Also, our approach does not require differentiability of the pipeline and is far more efficient than running a grid-search, which becomes computationally infeasible in problems of even just a handful of variables.

III-E

Efficient Lattice Reduction

The PLM does not require LR to operate, but we found that including an LR step to preprocess the channel helps. In particular it is important to identify a good hypothesis, around which the perturbation distribution is defined. In the LR-aided setup the channel
{\mathbf{H}}
is replaced with
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{U}}^{-1}
and we change quantization accordingly, see Section
II-C
.

LR itself is an NP-hard problem and thus we opt for an efficient approximate algorithm to compute
{\mathbf{U}}
. We use the method in Algorithm 5 of
[
18
]
, which is low-, fixed-complexity and parallelizable. Empirically, we find the required number of Gram-Schmidt iterations to be
\lceil\sqrt{M}\rceil
. Note that we only need to compute
{\mathbf{U}}
once for each
{\mathbf{H}}
and in a practical setting the cost of LR will be amortized across multiple channel uses, where
{\mathbf{H}}
is assumed to be constant; although, in our setup we opt of a worst-case scenario and recompute
{\mathbf{H}}
per transmission.

•

Furthermore, we provide example FLOP counts for

IV-B

Efficacy of lattice reduction

When comparing our lattice-reduced PLM against its non lattice-reduced counterpart Figure
3(b)
, we see that LR helps in terms of BLER. This performance boost comes merely from the difference in quantization regions induced by LR. Aside from this, the learned perturbations with and without LR are similar, aside from their different basis representation. While this performance gain of 0.5-1.0 dB is significant, we note however, that the PLM without LR still performs surprisingly well and at the same time yields only half of the complexity due to the omission of the remapping step.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed the perturbed linear demapper (PLM), a data-driven model for soft-output MIMO detection. In order to efficiently generate a list of candidates for the LLR computation, the model makes use of parametric distributions for perturbing the output solution of a simple linear demapper. We show that we can leverage Bayesian optimization to learn optimal perturbation and postprocessing parameters to achieve near-ML performance in terms of coded BLER.
Our method achieves comparable BLER to PFSD
[
5
]
, but adds the benefit of a fully parallelizable candidate generation as well as offers adaptability to channel statistics. Furthermore, the PLM can utilize lattice-reduction to strictly improve its performance at some added complexity cost.

It is a question for future work to find if there are better perturbation distributions, which are performant in terms of BLER and also in terms of sample efficiency when we reduce the number of candidates in the set
{\mathcal{B}}
. In particular, we consider the parametrization of perturbations using neural networks as an especially promising future direction.
