Sparse random graphs with many triangles
Suman Chakraborty, Remco van der Hofstad, Frank den Hollander

Abstract
. In this paper we consider the Erdős-Rényi random graph in the sparse regime in the limit as the number of vertices n n tends to infinity. We are interested in what this graph looks like when it contains many triangles, in two settings. First, we derive asymptotically sharp bounds on the probability that the graph contains a large number of triangles. We show that, conditionally on this event, with high probability the graph contains an almost complete subgraph, i.e., the triangles form a near-clique, and has the same local limit as the original Erdős-Rényi random graph. Second, we derive asymptotically sharp bounds on the probability that the graph contains a large number of vertices that are part of a triangle. If order n n vertices are in triangles, then the local limit (provided it exists) is different from that of the Erdős-Rényi random graph. Our results shed light on the challenges that arise in the description of real-world networks, which often are sparse, yet highly clustered , and on exponential random graphs, which often are used to model such networks.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Background and motivation

Let
G_{n,p_{n}}
be the Erdős-Rényi random graph on
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
vertices, where each edge is present with probability
p_{n}
independently of each other. In this article we consider the sparse regime:
p_{n}=\lambda/n
for some fixed
\lambda>0
. (We call a graph
sparse
when the expected number of edges is of the same order as the number of vertices in the graph.)

Triangles.

It is well-known that a typical outcome of
G_{n,p_{n}}
with
p_{n}=\lambda/n
has roughly
\lambda n/2
many edges and is locally tree-like, i.e., for each fixed
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the proportion of vertices whose
r
-neighborhood contains a cycle vanishes with high probability (see, for example, Bollobás
[
10
]
, Frieze and Karoński
[
22
]
, Janson, Ruciński and Luczak
[
32
]
, van der Hofstad
[
27
]
). Here ‘with high probability’ means with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, many networks observed in practice are far from locally tree-like (see Newman
[
40
]
and the references therein). In particular, they often contain a large number of triangles. This motivates our first question:
How unlikely is it to observe a large number of triangles in a typical outcome of
G_{n,p_{n}}
, and what does the graph looks like if we condition
G_{n,p_{n}}
to have a large number of triangles?

In this article we derive asymptotically sharp upper and lower bounds on the logarithmic probability that
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a large number of triangles. Furthermore, we show that if we condition
G_{n,p_{n}}
to have a large number of triangles, then with high probability it contains a small ‘almost-complete’ subgraph, while a typical outcome is still locally tree-like. This is rather disappointing, as in practice a substantial number of vertices of the graph are part of some triangle (Newman
[
40
]
, Rapoport
[
41
]
, Serrano and Boguña
[
44
]
, Watts and Strogatz
[
48
]
). This motivates our second question:
What is the probability that a typical outcome of
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a large number of vertices that are part of some triangle?
We derive sharp upper and lower bounds on the logarithmic upper tail probability of this event as well. Although we have not been able to prove structural results for this setting, our proof of upper and lower bounds is based on the Ansatz that if
S
is the subset of vertices of
G_{n,p_{n}}
that are part of some triangle, then
S
contains a subgraph that has approximately
|S|/3
vertex-disjoint triangles.

Subgraphs.

For a small fixed graph
H
, let
N(H,G)
be the number of copies of
H
in
G
. For various values of
p_{n}
, the distribution of
N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})
has been studied extensively. The first results appeared in the seminal paper by Erdős and Rényi
[
20
]
. For
H
a small complete graph, the probability of
\{N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})>0\}
was studied by Schürger
[
43
]
. This work was later extended to other subgraphs by Bollobás in
[
9
]
. Ruciński
[
42
]
obtained a necessary and sufficient condition for
asymptotic normality
of
N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})
.

Sharp estimates of probabilities of the form
\mathbb{P}\left(N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})\leq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})]\right)
were obtained in Janson, Ruciński, and Luczak
[
32
]
. For a long time, no sharp estimate was available on ‘upper tail’ probabilities of the form
\mathbb{P}\left(N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})]\right)
. When
p\in(0,1)
is independent of
n
, asymptotically sharp estimates of
\log{\mathbb{P}\left(N(H,G_{n,p})\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[N(H,G_{n,p})]\right)}
were obtained by Chatterjee and Varadhan
[
15
]
using the theory of dense graph limits
[
36
]
, and Szemerédi’s regularity lemma
[
46
]
. Chatterjee and Varadhan
[
15
]
, and Lubetzky and Zhao
[
37
]
studied the structure of
G_{n,p}
conditioned on large subgraph counts as well. Due to the poor quantitative estimates in Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, the arguments in
[
15
]
did not extend easily when
p=p_{n}
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
faster than a negative power of
\log{n}
(even extending the arguments in
[
15
]
to the regime when
p_{n}
is a negative power of
\log{n}
required a weaker version of the Szemerédi’s regularity lemma; see
[
38
]
).

In the regime where
(\log n)/n\ll p_{n}\ll 1
, initial attempts using standard concentration inequalities (such as Hoeffding
[
26
]
, Talagrand
[
47
]
, Kim and Vu
[
35
]
) yielded estimates that were far from optimal. The estimates obtained with these approaches appeared in the article appropriately titled ‘The infamous upper tail’ by Janson and Ruciński
[
31
]
. In Janson, Oleszkiewicz, and Ruciński
[
30
]
, an upper and a lower bound on
\log{\mathbb{P}\left(N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[N(H,G_{n,p_{n}})]\right)}
were obtained that differed by a multiplicative factor
\log(1/p_{n})
.

When
H=K_{r}
is an
r
-clique (complete subgraph on
r
vertices), the mis-matching factor
\log(1/p_{n})
was removed by Chatterjee
[
12
]
and by DeMarco and Kahn
[
18
]
, who proved the correct order of magnitude of the logarithmic upper tail probability. Finally, after a series of improvements (Augeri
[
2
]
, Chatterjee and Dembo
[
13
]
, Cook and Dembo
[
17
]
, Eldan
[
19
]
), Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
approximated
\log{\mathbb{P}\left(N(K_{r},G_{n,p_{n}})\right)}
in terms of a variational problem for
(\log n)/n\ll p_{n}\ll 1
. The variational problem was independently solved by Lubetzky and Zhao
[
38
]
yielding the estimate

\log{\mathbb{P}\left(N(K_{r},G_{n,p_{n}})\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[N(K_{r},G_{n,p_{n}})]\right)}\approx-C(\varepsilon)n^{2}p_{n}^{2}\log(1/p_{n}),

(1.1)

for
(\log n)/n\ll p_{n}\ll 1
, where
C(\varepsilon)>0
is a constant.

In their attempt to prove estimates of the form (
1.1
), Chatterjee and Dembo
[
13
]
initiated the study of
non-linear large deviations
, i.e., large deviations of non-linear functions of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables. Since then the study of non-linear large deviations has received vast attention (see Augeri
[
2
]
, Basak and Basu
[
4
]
, Chatterjee and Dembo
[
13
]
, Cook and Dembo
[
17
]
, Eldan
[
19
]
, Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
). These works also consider graphs other than
K_{r}
, and the related variational problem was solved in Bhattacharya, Ganguly, Lubetzky, and Zhao
[
7
]
.

Unfortunately, to our knowledge none of these previous works consider the sparse regime
p=p_{n}=\lambda/n
.

Back to triangles.

It is well-known that, in the sparse regime, in
G_{n,p_{n}}
the number of triangles
T=N(K_{3},G_{n,p_{n}})
converges in law to a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda^{3}/6
. In our first main result, we derive sharp upper and lower bounds on the probability of rare events of the form
\{T\geq k_{n}\}
, where
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive real numbers that tend to infinity. Roughly, we show that, as long as
k_{n}
is growing fast enough,

\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}[6k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n}).

(1.2)

The
lower bound
on
\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})
expressed by (
1.2
) comes from the observation that if
[(6k_{n})^{1/3}]
forms a clique, then we must have
T\geq k_{n}
. The probability that
[(6k_{n})^{1/3}]
forms a clique is roughly
\exp{\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}[6k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\right)}
. The matching
upper bound
expressed by (
1.2
) is more difficult, and to derive it we adopt the theory of non-linear large deviations developed in Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
. As mentioned before, the study of small subgraphs is a classical topic in random graph theory. In particular, the estimate (
1.2
) is a new contribution towards the understanding of the rare event that ‘an unusually large number of triangles appears in a sparse random graph’.

In this regard, Collet and Eckmann
[
16
]
considered a closely related problem. We need some notation to discuss their result. For
\alpha,\beta>0
, let
\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,\beta)
be the set of graphs on
n
vertices,
\alpha n
edges, and
\beta n
triangles, and let
\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha)
be the set of graphs on
n
vertices, and
\alpha n
edges. Collet and Eckmann
[
16
]
showed that
\log{|\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,\beta)|}\approx\log{|\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha)|}
. Note that when
\alpha
is independent of
n
,
\log{|\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha)|}
is of order
n\log{n}
. This suggests that it might be possible to show that
-\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq\beta n)=o(n\log{n})
but we were unable to improve this further using the techniques in
[
16
]
, whereas, using (
1.2
) we can obtain much sharper estimate, that is,

\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq\beta n)\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}[6\beta n]^{2/3}\log(n).

Motivated by their results the authors in
[
16
]
suggested that “the triangles seem to cluster even at low density”. As far as we know the present paper is the first one to provide rigorous, and quantitative, evidence to this claim in the sparse regime. We describe these in the following paragraph.

Equation (
1.2
) provides a rough answer to the question how unlikely it is that a sparse graph with a large number of triangles is actually an outcome of a sparse Erdős-Rényi graph. We also provide a description of the random graph
conditionally
on the event
\{T\geq k_{n}\}
. Roughly, this asserts that with high probability
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a subgraph
G
with approximately
(6k_{n})^{2/3}/2
edges with minimum degree approximately
(6k_{n})^{1/3}
, which suggests that
G
is an ‘almost complete’ subgraph. Although this provides structural information of
G_{n,p_{n}}
conditionally on
\{T\geq k_{n}\}
, we also show that this does not affect the
local structure
of the graph. Indeed, conditionally on
\{T\geq k_{n}\}
,
G_{n,p_{n}}
still converges locally in probability to the same Galton-Watson tree as the original Erdős-Rényi random graph. Unfortunately, this is generally not the case in applications (for example, Newman
[
40
]
proposed a random graph model where each vertex
i\in[n]
participates in
t_{i}
-triangles and
s_{i}
-edges that are not part of any triangle).

The above failure motivates us to study the number of vertices that are part of some triangle in
G_{n,p_{n}}
, denoted by
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})
. We show that, for appropriately growing
k_{n}
,

{\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})}\approx-\frac{1}{3}k_{n}\log k_{n}.

(1.3)

As far as we know, this estimate is the first of its kind. To see where it comes from, note that if, in a graph
G
, there is a set
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k_{n}
such that
S
is an union of vertex-disjoint triangles, no other edge is present in
[S]
, and
V\backslash S
is triangle free, then clearly
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)\geq k_{n}
, and it is relatively straightforward to obtain a lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})
. However,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)
does not seem to be a bounded-degree polynomial function of the entries of the adjacency matrix of
G
. Therefore, unlike
T
, the theory of non-linear large deviations in Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
is not readily applicable in this case. To derive an upper bound we instead develop some new combinatorial tools, in particular, a
graph decomposition lemma
that we believe to be of independent interest.

Real-world networks.

Large-number-of-triangles events have practical importance. Many networks observed in practice are sparse, while at the same time they exhibit transitivity (also called clustering), in the sense that two neighbors of the same vertex are more likely to also be neighbors of one another. (See Newman
[
40
]
, Rapoport
[
41
]
, Serrano and Boguña
[
44
]
, Watts and Strogatz
[
48
]
for more on this topic.) As a result, these graphs contain a large number of triangles, and many vertices in them lie in triangles. This is another motivation behind (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Large-deviation-type estimates of the form (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are sometimes helpful to study the corresponding exponential random graph models. In dense settings, this connection has been investigated in Chatterjee and Diaconis
[
14
]
, Chatterjee and Dembo
[
13
]
, Bhamidi, Chakraborty, Cranmer and Desmarais
[
6
]
. Some potential implications of our results regarding exponential random graph models are discussed in Section
1.6
. In particular, we propose a new exponential random graph model, evaluate its normalizing constant, and show that this model undergoes an intriguing phase transition.

Organisation of this section.

Section

has provided us with the background and the motivation for the paper. Section
1.2
introduces notation. In Section
1.3
, we estimate the probability for the Erdős-Rényi random graph to have many triangles. In Section
1.4
, we study the structure of the graph conditionally on this event. In Section
1.5
, we estimate the probability for the Erdős-Rényi random graph to have many vertices in triangles. Sections

contain quick previews of the structure of the proofs of the main results presented there, which are written out in Sections
2
–
7
. We close in Section
1.6
with a discussion, formulating relevant extensions and open problems, addressing the relevance of our results for real-world networks, and giving a brief outline of the remainder of the paper.

1.2.
Introductory notation

A graph
G=(V,E)
consists of a set of vertices
V=V(G)
and a set of edges
E=E(G)
connecting pairs of vertices. Abbreviate

e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}=|E(G)|
.

In the complete graph on
n
vertices, written
K_{n}
, all possible edges are present. In the Erdős-Rényi random graph on
n
vertices with retention probability
p\in[0,1]
, written
G_{n,p}
, edges are present with probability
p
and absent with probability
1-p
, independently for different edges. The law of
G_{n,p}
is written
\mathbb{P}
. We denote the law of
G_{n,p}
conditionally on the event that it contains a given graph
G
with
V(G)\subseteq[n]
by

\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\mid G_{n,p}\supseteq G).

(1.4)

Further, we let
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
denote the (conditional) expectation w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
.

Remark 1.1
.

[Induced subgraph]

For a graph
G
with
V(G)\subseteq[n]
,
G_{n,p}\supseteq G
in (
1.4
) simply means that the induced subgraph of
G_{n,p}
on the vertex set
V(G)
contains
G
as a subgraph.
\blacksquare

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, and
U\subseteq V
,
G[U]
will denote the subgraph induced in
G
by
U
. For two sequences of non-negative real numbers
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
will mean
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=0
. For any two sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
a_{n}\approx b_{n}
means
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=1
.

1.3.
Probability to have many triangles

Let
T
be the number of triangles in
G_{n,p}
. In what follows, we fix
\lambda>0
, and put

p_{n}=\frac{\lambda}{n},\qquad\varepsilon_{n}=k_{n}^{-2/3},

(1.5)

where
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive reals that can be chosen freely such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
. In order to state our main results on the large deviations for the number of triangles, we state the relevant
variational problem.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in(0,1)
and
a,k>0
, define

\Phi_{n,p,k}(a):=\min\{e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p)\colon\,G\subseteq K_{n},\,\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq ak\}.

(1.6)

The following theorem provide asymptotically sharp upper and lower bounds on the probability of the event that
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains at least
ak_{n}
triangles:

Theorem 1.2
.

[Large deviations for the number of triangles]

Suppose that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
. Fix a sequence of positive reals
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}=0
, and such that
w_{n}k_{n}\geq{n\choose 3}(p_{n})^{C}
for some constant
C>0
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log{n})/(w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3})=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log w_{n})/\log(1/p_{n})=0
. Then, for
n
large enough,

-(1+\varepsilon_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})\leq\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\leq-(1-\psi_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-w_{n}),

(1.7)

for a sequence
\psi_{n}=o(1)
, where, for
\lim_{n\to\infty}

w_{n}k_{n}^{1/3}=\infty
,

\tfrac{1}{2}[6a(1-w_{n})k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\leq\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}[6a(1+w_{n})k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n}).

(1.8)

Remark 1.3
.

[Structure of the proof of Theorem
1.2
]

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is given in three theorems stated separately below:

(a) The lower bound in (
1.7
) in Theorem
1.2
is stated more generally in Theorem
2.1
in Section
2
. In Theorem
2.1
, we estimate the probability
\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})
for a sequence of positive real numbers
k_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, and the estimate is expressed in terms of a perturbation of the variational formula given in (
1.6
).

(b) The upper bound in (
1.7
) in Theorem
1.2
is stated more generally in Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
, and we refer to there for a more detailed discussion. For the upper bound we require the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log{n})/

(w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3})=0
, which implies that we need
k_{n}
to grow faster than
(\log n)^{3}
. On the other hand, the restriction on the perturbation term
w_{n}
is somewhat more restrictive. We also need
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log w_{n})/

\log(1/p_{n})=0
, which implies that
w_{n}
can not converge to zero faster than
n^{-C}
for any
C>0
. Note that one can insert
a=1
to obtain an estimate of the form
\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})
.

(c) The asymptotics of the variational problem in (
1.6
), as described in (
1.8
), is stated in Theorem
4.1
in Section
4
.
\blacksquare

Remark 1.4
.

[Conditions of Theorem
1.2
]

The conditions in Theorem
1.2
ensure that the upper and lower bounds in (
1.7
) are asymptotically equal up to the leading order term. For this we require that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}=0
,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
, and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}=0
. These give rise to the conditions in Theorem
1.2
. More precisely,

(a) the term
\psi_{n}
in the upper bound in (
1.7
) converges to zero when
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log{n})/(w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3})=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log w_{n})/\log(1/p_{n})=0
. An explicit expression for
\psi_{n}
is given in Theorem
3.1
.

(b) the lower bound in (
1.7
) holds even when
\varepsilon_{n}
does not vanish as
n\to\infty
, but in order to get a lower bound that matches with the upper bound we need
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
.

(c) since
T
converges to a Poisson distribution with parameter
\tfrac{\lambda^{3}}{6}
as
n\to\infty
, the upper bound in (
1.7
) is not true when
\varepsilon_{n}
does not vanish as
n\to\infty
.

1.4.
Graph structure conditionally on having many triangles

We need the notion of a near-clique:

Definition 1.5
.

[Near-clique]

For
\delta>0
,
G
is called an
(\delta,m)
-clique
if
G
has minimum degree
(1-4\delta^{1/2})(2e_{\scriptscriptstyle G})^{1/2}
, and
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}=m
.

\spadesuit

The following theorem shows that if we condition the random graph to have at least
ak_{n}
triangles, then with high probability the graph contains an almost complete subgraph (see Section
5
for finer results), while at the same time it locally still looks like the unconditional Erdős-Rényi random graph.

Theorem 1.6
.

[Structure of the graph conditionally on having many triangles]

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.2
, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
and a sequence
\psi_{n}=o(1)
such that, for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm contains no }\left(C(\psi_{n}+w_{n}),m_{n}\right)\text{\rm-clique}\mid T\geq ak_{n}\right)\leq\delta,

(1.9)

where
m_{n}:=\tfrac{1}{2}[6(a-2w_{n})k_{n}(1-Ck_{n}^{-1/3})]^{2/3}
. At the same time, the Erdős-Rényi random graph, conditionally on
T\geq ak_{n}
, converges locally in probability to a
\text{Poi}(\lambda)
branching process, just like the unconditional Erdős-Rényi random graph.

Remark 1.7
.

[Structure of the proof of Theorem
1.6
]

(a) Theorem
1.6
shows that the triangles in the Erdős-Rényi random graph conditionally on having many triangles are highly localized: They are almost all in a near-clique, and thus the remainder of the graph is not affected by the conditioning.

(b) The proof of Theorem
1.6
is given in Section
5
, and is organised as follows. In Theorem
5.1
in Section
5.1
, we prove a finer result on the existence of a near-clique. In Theorem
5.6
in Section
5.2
, we identify the local limit of the Erdős-Rényi random graph conditionally on having many triangles. There we also define more precisely what local convergence in probability means. In this proof, we crucially rely on Bordenave and Caputo
[
11
, Theorem 1.8]
, which identifies the large deviation principle for the local limit of the Erdős-Rényi random graph. Both results are finer than the one stated in Theorem
1.6
.

\blacksquare

1.5.
Large deviations for the number of vertices in triangles

Let
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)
be the number of vertices in
G
that are part of a triangle. The following theorem derives a large deviation estimate on the probability that the Erdős-Rényi random graph has at least
k_{n}
vertices that are part of a triangle:

Theorem 1.8
.

[Large deviations for the number of vertices in triangles]

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}/\log n=\infty
. Then, for
n
large enough,

-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})-Ck_{n}\leq\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\leq-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})+Ck_{n},

(1.10)

for some constant
C>0
.

Remark 1.9
.

[Structure of the proof of Theorem
1.8
]

(a) Comparing Theorem
1.8
to Theorem
1.2
, we see that the large deviations for the number of vertices in triangles and for the number of triangles are completely different: It is much more unlikely that there are many vertices in triangles than that there are many triangles. This is exemplified by Theorem
1.6
, which shows that the many triangles only involve few vertices.

(b) The proof of Theorem
1.8
is given in Section
6
, and is organised as follows. In Theorem
6.1
in Section
6.1
, we prove a finer upper bound on the number of vertices in triangles. Its proof is obtained by considering graphs in which all triangles are
disjoint
, and showing that the probability of such graphs is close to that of the upper bound. In Theorem
6.3
in Section
6.2
, we prove a finer upper bound on the probability of having many vertices in triangles. Its proof relies on a
decomposition
of the graph when there are many vertices in triangles, stated in Lemma
6.8
in Section
6.2
, that is interesting in its own right.
\blacksquare

1.6.
Discussion

In this section, we discuss our main results, mention some of their consequences, and list some open problems.

Large deviations for the number of triangles.

In Theorem
1.2
(see also Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
), we have obtained sharp upper and lower bounds on the probability
\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})
for a sequence of non-negative real numbers
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. It is easy to see that, under the assumption
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}k_{n}^{-1/3}\log{n}=0
, Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
imply the weaker statement

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})}{k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})}=-\tfrac{1}{2}6^{2/3}.

(1.11)

Although the expression in (
1.11
) is much cleaner, the statements in Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
explicitly spell out the error terms. The proofs of these theorems are based on the theory of non-linear large deviations developed in Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
(which was partly based on Janson, Oleszkiewicz, and Ruciński
[
30
]
).

Large deviations for the number of vertices in triangles.

For
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
, we can use Theorem
1.8
to write a limiting statement as well. For a sequence
k_{n}\to\infty
, such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{k_{n}}{\log n}=\infty
, and
k_{n}\leq n
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})}{k_{n}\log(k_{n})}=-\tfrac{1}{3}.

(1.12)

Unfortunately,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
(unlike
T
) does not appear to be a bounded-degree polynomial function of the entries of the adjacency matrix, and thus the existing methods are not readily applicable. More precisely, if
G
is a simple graph on
n
vertices with adjacency matrix
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
, then we can write

V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\prod_{j=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-a_{ij}a_{ik}a_{jk})\right),

(1.13)

which is a polynomial function of the entries of the matrix
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
, and the degree of this polynomial depends on
n
, whereas the theory developed in Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
25
]
is only applicable to bounded-degree polynomials with non-negative coefficients. Also, it does not appear straightforward to apply the techniques in Chatterjee
[
13
]
(in particular, we were not able to verify the condition on
f
in
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
). To get around this issue we have developed some novel
combinatorial
tools. Roughly, we have computed the number of graphs on
n
vertices with
m
edges such that exactly
q
vertices are part of some triangle. In order to do so, we proved a
greedy decomposition lemma
(Lemma
6.8
in Section
6.2
) for graphs whose
q
vertices are part of some triangle and contain no ‘extra’ edges. These techniques are a new contribution to the theory of non-linear large deviations, and we hope to develop them further in future work.

Extension: Local limit Erdős-Rényi conditioned on many vertices in triangles.

It would be interesting to also investigate the
local limit
of the Erdős-Rényi random graph conditionally on having many vertices in triangles. It is not hard to show that this local limit does not change when
k_{n}
is such that
k_{n}\log{n}=o(n)
. However, this rules out the interesting case where
k_{n}=\Theta(n),
in which case this local limit should be
different
. We leave the proof of such a result to future work. Note, however, that it suffices to show that the local limit
exists
. Indeed, assume that the local limit exists along a subsequence, and that
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq an
for some
a\in(0,1)
. Then, a uniform vertex will, with probability at least
a
, be part of a triangle. Since the Erdős-Rényi random graph is locally tree-like, this means that the limit is different. As such, a main ingredient would be to show that the Erdős-Rényi random graph conditionally on having at least
an
vertices in triangles is
tight
in the local convergence topology.

Extension: Cliques for many triangles.

In Theorem
1.6
, we show that, conditionally on there being many triangles, the graph whp contains a near-clique. We conjecture that this result can be considerably sharpened. Indeed, we expect that there is a clique of size
a_{n}
, where
a_{n}
is the largest natural number such that
a_{n}(a_{n}-1)(a_{n}-2)/6\leq ak_{n}
. This is
much
sharper than the near-clique result in Theorem
1.6
.

Extension: Beyond triangles.

We expect that some of our results can be relatively straightforwardly extended beyond triangles. Indeed, Theorem
1.2
should apply also when dealing with larger cliques, when the variational problem in (
1.6
) is appropriately adapted. We expect that this is not quite the case for Theorem
1.8
. The proof of Theorem
1.8
crucially relies on a counting argument based on the decomposition Lemma
6.8
in Section
6.2
. Although this lemma extends to higher-order cliques, the counting argument becomes much more difficult.

Extension: Other ranges of
p_{n}
.

We believe that Theorem
1.2
can be extended to larger values of
p_{n}
by choosing a suitable sequence
k_{n}
, but Theorem
1.8
does not seem to extend easily. Indeed, in our proof (see Lemma
6.2
in Section
6.2
) we have used the fact that the probability for the graph to be triangle free is bounded below by an absolute constant, and the proof of Lemma
6.14
in Section
6.2
also suggests that if we let
\lambda
grow like a power of
n
, then it will affect the upper bound in Theorem
1.8
. It is still possible to let
\lambda
grow slowly, say, like
\log{n}
, but we do not pursue this in this paper.

Highly-clustered real-world networks.

In many real-world networks there are order
n
triangles, as well as order
n
vertices in triangles. Thus, from a practical perspective, the setting where
k_{n}
is of order
n
is the most relevant. Comparing Theorems
1.2
and
1.8
, we see that conditioning on many vertices in triangles is a
much more effective way to create a highly-clustered graph
than conditioning on having many triangles. Furthermore, Theorem
1.8
also explains that the logarithm of the probability for an Erdős-Rényi random graph to be highly clustered is of the order
n\log{n}
, a regime that has not yet attracted much attention. Indeed, Bordenave and Caputo
[
11
]
show that the probability for the local limit of the Erdős-Rényi random graph to be unequal to a Poisson branching process is exponentially small, but
only when
the alternative local limit is a tree itself. It would be highly interesting to investigate the probability that the local limit is not a tree in more detail, and the exponential rate should be
n\log{n}
.

Exponential random graphs as real-world network models.

Exponential random graphs models (ERGM) are popular for modelling real-world networks. Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the space of all simple graphs on the vertex set
[n]
, which has
2^{n\choose 2}
elements. The ERGM can be represented by the following exponential form

\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\exp{\left(T(G)\right)},\,\,G\in\mathcal{G}_{n},

(1.14)

where
T(G)
is a function on the space of graphs, and
Z_{n}
is the normalizing constant (also called the partition function). Examples of
T
include subgraph counts such as the number of edges, triangles, cycles, etc. Such models were first studied in Holland and Leinhardt
[
29
]
, Frank and Strauss
[
21
]
. Several new sufficient statistics were introduced in Snijders, Pattison, Robins and Handcock
[
45
]
. The evaluation of
Z_{n}
is one of the fundamental (and often difficult) problems. In the dense regime the first such result was obtained by Chatterjee and Diaconis
[
14
]
. While ERGMs are well-understood in the dense regime, there are hardly any results on sparse random graphs (a recent result in the sparse regime appeared in Mukherjee
[
39
]
, and a related model called the random triangle model was studied by Jonasson
[
33
]
, and Häggström and Jonasson
[
24
]
). Unfortunately, even dense exponential random graphs are problematic, as shown by Bhamidi, Bresler, and Sly
[
5
]
: either they locally look like dense Erdős-Rényi random graphs, or the mixing times of Glauber dynamics or Metropolis-Hasting dynamics on these graphs are exponentially large.

Phase transitions for exponential random graphs.

Our results have an interesting tale to tell about exponential random graphs. First, we could consider exponential random graphs given by the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
defined in terms of its Radon-Nikodym derivative w.r.t. the law
\mathbb{P}
of the Erdős-Rényi random graph with
p=\lambda/n
as

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\beta}}{d\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}})}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}},

(1.15)

where
Z_{n}(\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}})
is a normalizing constant or
partition function
and
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}\geq 0
is an appropriate sequence. Theorem
1.8
suggests that this measure is well-defined for
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}=\beta\log{n}
, and that it will indeed give rise to a linear number of vertices in triangles. Note that the
\beta\log{n}
scaling in the exponential is crucial to make the measure well-defined in the large-graph limit. ERGMs often exhibit
phase transitions
(also called degeneracy) at some values of the parameters.(see Chatterjee and Diaconis
[
14
]
, Bhamidi, Chakraborty, Cranmer, and Desmarais
[
6
]
, Mukherjee
[
39
]
). Roughly, this means that a slight change in the values of some parameter has a drastic effect on a typical outcome of the model.

The above phenomenon is observed in various models in statistical physics (see Georgii
[
23
]
), and is intimately related to the properties of the associated partition function. Here we investigate the scaling of the partition function, and show that the model proposed in (
1.15
) undergoes a phase transition:

Corollary 1.10
.

[Exponential graph with the number of vertices in triangles]

Consider the exponential random graph model defined in (
1.15
) with
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}=\beta\log{n}
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n\log{n}}\log{Z_{n}(\beta\log n)}=(\beta-\tfrac{1}{3})_{+},

(1.16)

where
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. Moreover, when
\beta<\tfrac{1}{3}
,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}/n
converges in probability to
0
, and when
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}/n
converges in probability to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

Note that Corollary
1.10
not only determines the scaling of the partition function, it also shows that the model undergoes a phase transition: when
\beta<\tfrac{1}{3}
, in a typical realization from
\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}
very few vertices are part of some triangle, while when
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
, almost all vertices are part of some triangle. The proof of Corollary
1.10
is given in Section
7
. Finally, Corollary
1.10
and Theorem
1.8
suggest that interesting behaviour arises for
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}=\frac{1}{3}\log{n}+\beta
. For this we would need to study
\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k_{n})
up to order
n
, which is beyond the scope of the present paper.

Alternatively, we could consider the measure

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}})}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}}T-\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}S},

(1.17)

where
S
is the number of subgraphs
K_{4}\backslash e
, the complete subgraph on
4
vertices minus an edge. These subgraphs are sometimes called
diamonds
. Again, we take
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}}=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\log{n}
and
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\log{n}
with
\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle T},\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\geq 0
, as suggested by Theorem
1.8
, when we wish to have order
n
vertices in triangles. When
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}=0
, Theorem
1.2
suggests that the graph will be complete whp, as this gives a contribution
n(n-1)(n-2)/6
in the exponent, which is so large that it cannot be beaten by the entropy of graphs that miss a substantial number of edges. However, when
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\log{n}
with
\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle S}>0
, the
-\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}S
term strongly penalises graph configurations with large cliques, and thus ‘tames’ the triangle count. As a result, we are tempted to believe that the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}
is concentrated on graph configurations with a linear number of triangles in a linear number of vertices. This would lead to sparse exponential random graphs that are
not locally tree-like
. It would be of great interest to see whether such results can be proved. They would be extremely relevant for the study of sparse exponential random graphs. Unfortunately, we do not know how to prove a result like Corollary
1.10
for
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle T}}=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\log{n}
and
\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle S}}=\beta_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\log{n}
in this model.

2.
Lower bound on the large deviations for the number of triangles

In this section, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
. The following theorem describes a more precise result:

Theorem 2.1
.

[Large deviation lower bound]

Suppose that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
. Then, for every sequence of positive reals
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}

w_{n}=0
, and such that
w_{n}k_{n}\geq{n\choose 3}(p_{n})^{C}
for some constant
C>0
, for
n
large enough,

\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\geq-(1+\varepsilon_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n}).

(2.1)

Remark 2.2
.

[Parameters in Theorem
2.1
]

In Theorem
2.1
we estimate the probability
\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})
for a sequence of positive real numbers
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
, and the estimate is expressed in terms of a perturbation of the variational formula in (
1.6
). As noted before, the condition
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
requires that
k_{n}
grows faster than
(\log{n})^{3/2}
. The variational formula is perturbed by
w_{n}
, and the condition
w_{n}k_{n}\geq{n\choose 3}(p_{n})^{C}
requires that the perturbation is not too small. More precisely, this condition implies that
w_{n}\geq\frac{1}{n^{C}k_{n}}
for some constant
C>0
as
n\to\infty
. Also note that we can easily estimate
\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})
by simply picking
a=1
. We have chosen to express the theorem in the current form because it makes the perturbation argument in our proof notationally cleaner.
\blacksquare

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is based on the idea that
\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})
is dominated by the presence of a subgraph
G
that minimizes an appropriate perturbation of (
1.6
). This subgraph is very dense, as we prove later on. In the rest of this section we formalize this idea.

Let
G
be the graph that attains the minimum in the definition of
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})
in (
1.6
). First note that, by the definition of conditional probability,

\displaystyle-\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})

\displaystyle\leq-\log\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\supseteq G)-\log\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T\geq ak_{n})

(2.2)

\displaystyle=e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p_{n})-\log\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T\geq ak_{n}).

Therefore, by construction, we have
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p_{n})=\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})
. We will estimate
\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T\geq ak_{n})
in (
2.2
), for which we use the following lemma, whose proof is postponed to the end of this section.

Lemma 2.3
.

[Lower bound on the edge count for graphs with many triangles]

Let
G
be any simple graph on
n
vertices with adjacency matrix
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
. Let
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
and
T_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
be the number of edges and triangles in
G
, respectively. Then
\frac{1}{6}(2e_{\scriptscriptstyle G})^{3/2}\geq T_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
.

We use the standard notation where
K_{r}
denotes the complete graph on
r
vertices, and
K_{r,s}
denotes the complete bipartite graph with
r
vertices in one part and
s
vertices in the other part. Now, using the fact that
G
is the graph that attains the minimum in the definition of
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})
(see (
1.6
)), we get

ak_{n}\leq(a+w_{n})k_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\leq N(K_{1,2},G)\,p_{n}+N(K_{2},G)\,np_{n}^{2}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3},

(2.3)

where
N(H,G)
is the number of copies of
H
in
G
, and we recall (
1.5
). Note that
N(K_{1,2},G)\leq N(K_{2},G)|V(G)|
(since each edge in
G
can be in at most
|V(G)|
many
K_{1,2}
’s). Hence, for some absolute constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle ak_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\leq N(K_{2},G)|V(G)|\,p_{n}+N(K_{2},G)\,np_{n}^{2}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda+\tfrac{1}{n}\lambda^{2}\right)N(K_{2},G)+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}\leq C\left(N(K_{2},G)\right)^{3/2},

where in the third inequality we use Lemma
2.3
. This gives
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\geq Ck_{n}^{2/3}
for some constant
C
. Again using the fact that
G
is the graph that attains the minimum in the definition of
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})
, we get

(a+w_{n})k_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\leq{n\choose 3}\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T\geq ak_{n})+ak_{n},

(2.5)

and so

\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T\geq ak_{n})\geq w_{n}k_{n}/{n\choose 3}.

(2.6)

Therefore, substituting (
2.6
) into (
2.2
), we get

\displaystyle-\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\leq e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p_{n})+C\log(1/p_{n})

(2.7)

for some constant
C
. Here we use our assumption that
{n\choose 3}/w_{n}k_{n}\leq\left(1/p_{n}\right)^{C}
for some constant
C>0
. By construction,
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n})=e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p_{n})
, and we have already observed that
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\geq Ck_{n}^{2/3}
. Therefore

-\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\leq(1+\varepsilon_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a+w_{n}),

(2.8)

which completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
. We conclude this section with the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle 6T_{\scriptscriptstyle G}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}a_{ij}a_{jk}a_{ki}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{jk}a_{ki}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{jk}a_{ki}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{jk}\sum_{l=1}^{n}a_{li}\Big)^{1/2}=\left(2e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\right)^{3/2}.

∎

3.
Upper bound on the large deviations for the number of triangles

In this section, we prove the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
. The following theorem describes a more precise result:

Theorem 3.1
.

[Large deviation upper bound]

For every
\delta>0
and every sequence of positive reals
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}

w_{n}=0
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log{n})/(w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3})=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log w_{n})/

\log(1/p_{n})=0
, for
n
large enough,

\log\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\leq-(1-\psi_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-2w_{n})+\log(1+\delta),

(3.1)

where

\psi_{n}=\frac{C^{\prime}\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}+\frac{\log[C^{\prime}w_{n}^{-5}\log^{3}(1/p_{n})]}{\log(1/p_{n})}

(3.2)

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
.

Remark 3.2
.

[Parameters in Theorem
3.1
]

In Theorem
3.1
we require
k_{n}
to grow faster than
(\log{n})^{3}
as
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, the restriction on the perturbation term
w_{n}
is somewhat more restrictive. More precisely, we need
w_{n}k_{n}^{1/6}\gg(\log n)^{1/2}
. We also need
(\log w_{n})/\log(1/p_{n})\rightarrow 0
, which implies that
w_{n}
cannot converge to zero faster than
n^{-C}
for any
C>0
. Again, in Theorem
3.1
, we can pick
a=1
to obtain an estimate on
\mathbb{P}(T\geq k_{n})
.
\blacksquare

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.1
. This is done in two steps. In the first step, in Section
3.1
, we show that if a large number of triangles are present in
G_{n,p_{n}}
, then the graph contains a particular subgraph called a ‘seed’ (Definition
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
), which contains a specific subgraph called a ‘core’ (Definition
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
). We next obtain an upper bound on the number of cores (Lemma
3.7
). In the second step, in Section
3.2
, we upper bound the probability of
G_{n,p_{n}}
having at least
ak_{n}
many triangles by the probability of
G_{n,p_{n}}
having the appropriate number of cores, and then use the estimates obtained in the first step to obtain a sharp upper bound on this probability.

3.1.
Triangles are created by seeds

In this section, we first show that if
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a large number of triangles, then it must contain a particular subgraph called ‘seed’ with ‘appropriately high’ probability.

Definition 3.3
.

[Seed]

For
C
sufficiently large and for a sequence of positive reals
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}=0
, we call
G
a
seed
if the following hold:

(S1)

\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq(a-w_{n})k_{n}
.

(S2)

e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\leq{Ca}{w_{n}^{-1}}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
.
\spadesuit

The following lemma reduces the study of triangles to the existence of a seed. We obtain a quantitative estimate on the probability that
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a large number of triangles, but does not contain a seed of appropriate size. The proof is an adaptation of
[
25
]
, which in turn was based on a classical moment argument in
[
30
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

[Unlikely not to contain a seed]

Let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}=0
. Then for every positive integer
\ell\leq\frac{Ca}{3}{w_{n}^{-1}}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
, and every
a>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm contains no seed}\right)\leq\left(1-\tfrac{w_{n}}{a}\right)^{\ell}

(3.3)

for any
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
. Consequently,

\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})\leq(1+\xi_{n})\,\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm contains a seed}),

(3.4)

and

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm contains no seed}\right)\leq\xi_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n}\right),

(3.5)

where

\xi_{n}=\exp\left([\tfrac{1}{2}(6a)^{2/3}-\tfrac{1}{3}C]\,k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\right).

(3.6)

Proof.

We use a bound on the moments of the number of triangles, on the event that
G_{n,p_{n}}
does not contain a seed. For this, let
Z
be the indicator of the event that
G_{n,p_{n}}
does not contain a seed. Fix a positive integer
\ell
that satisfies
\ell\leq\frac{1}{3}Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
. Then
TZ\geq 0
and
Z^{\ell}=Z
, so that, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains no seed}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(TZ\geq ak_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(T^{\ell}Z\right)}{(ak_{n})^{\ell}}.

(3.7)

Also note that we can write
T=\sum_{t}Y_{t}
, where the sum runs over all triangles in
K_{n}
, and
Y_{t}
is the indicator that
t
belongs to
G_{n,p_{n}}
.

Now let
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
be the indicator of the event that
G\cap G_{n,p_{n}}
does not contain a seed. This gives
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\leq Z_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{\prime}}
when
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
. Also, since
Z=Z_{\scriptscriptstyle K_{n}}
for
k\in[\ell]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T^{k}Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k}}Z)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k-1}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}})\sum_{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{k}}\mid Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k-1}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}}=1).

Note that the first sum runs over all
t_{1},\cdots,t_{k-1}
such that the event
Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k-1}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}}=1
has positive probability. This implies that
t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}
(seen as a graph containing the edges and vertices in
t_{i}
for
i\in[k-1]
) does not contain a seed. Also note that
e_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}}\leq 3(k-1)\leq 3\ell\leq Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
, and so

\sum_{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{k}}\mid Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k-1}}=1)=\mathbb{E}_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}}(T)<(a-w_{n})k_{n},

(3.9)

because otherwise
t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}
would be a seed. Therefore

\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k}})<(a-w_{n})k_{n}\sum_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}(Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\cdots Y_{t_{k-1}}Z_{t_{1}\cup\cdots\cup t_{k-1}}).

(3.10)

Repeating this argument, we obtain
\mathbb{E}(T^{\ell}Z))\leq(a-w_{n})^{\ell}k_{n}^{\ell}
. Therefore, using (
3.7
), we can write

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains no seed}\right)\leq\left(1-\tfrac{w_{n}}{a}\right)^{\ell}.

(3.11)

Finally, putting
\ell=\frac{1}{3}Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
, we get

\left(\tfrac{a-w_{n}}{a}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{3}Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})}=\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{3}Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\log{\left(\tfrac{a-w_{n}}{a}\right)}\right).

(3.12)

Since
\log(1-x)\leq-x
,
x\in(0,1)
, we arrive at

\left(\tfrac{a-w_{n}}{a}\right)^{\ell}\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}Ck_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\right)\leq\xi_{n}\,\mathbb{P}\left(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a clique of size }(6ak_{n})^{1/3}\right),

(3.13)

where
\xi_{n}
is given in (
3.6
). Note that a clique of size
(6ak_{n})^{1/3}
is a seed, and such a clique contains
ak_{n}
many triangles. This concludes the proofs of (
3.5
) and (
3.4
).
∎

It turns out that the existence of a seed implies existence of a minimal type of seed that we call a
core
:

Definition 3.5
.

[Core]

A graph
G^{*}
is called a
core
if the following conditions hold:

(C1)

\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}(T)\geq(a-2w_{n})k_{n}
.

(C2)

e_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}\leq{Ca}{w_{n}^{-1}}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
.

(C3)

\min_{e\in E(G^{*})}(\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}(T)-\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}\backslash e}(T))\geq w_{n}^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{1/3}}{Ca\log(1/p_{n})}
.

Moreover, we call a core
G^{*}
an
m
-core
when it has exactly
m
-edges.
\spadesuit

Intuitively, a core can be thought of as a
minimal
version of a seed. We will shortly see that obtaining an upper bound on the number of cores is a relatively accessible task. First, let us show that every seed
G
contains a core
G^{*}
:

Lemma 3.6
.

[Seeds contain cores]

Every seed
G
contains a core
G^{*}
.

Proof.

We start with a seed
G
, and recursively remove edges to create a core. For this, we define a sequence of graphs
G=G_{0}\supseteq G_{1}\supset\cdots\supseteq G_{s}=G^{*}
. Here,
G_{i+1}=G_{i}\backslash e
for some edge in
e\in G_{i}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G_{i}}(T)-\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G_{i}\backslash e}(T)<w_{n}^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{1/3}}{Ca\log(1/p_{n})}
. We continue deleting edges as long as there is such an
e
. We stop when such an
e
no longer exists, and denote the resulting graph by
G^{*}
. Therefore,
G^{*}
satisfies Definition
3.5
(C3) and (C2) by construction. Also note that
s\leq e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\leq{Ca}{w_{n}^{-1}}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
since
G
is a seed, and therefore

\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)-\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G_{i}}(T)-\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G_{i+1}}(T)\right)<sw_{n}^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{1/3}}{Ca\log(1/p_{n})}\leq w_{n}k_{n}.

(3.14)

Now, since
G
is a seed, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq(a-w_{n})k_{n}
. Combining this with (
3.14
), we get
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}(T)\geq(a-2w_{n})k_{n}
, which is Definition
3.5
(C1).
∎

In the following lemma we obtain an upper bound on the number of
m
-cores that plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
3.1
:

Lemma 3.7
.

[Bound on the number of cores]

Let
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}=0
. Then the number of
m
-cores is bounded above by
(1/p_{n})^{m\Psi_{n}}
, where

\Psi_{n}=\frac{1}{\log(1/p_{n})}\left(\tfrac{C^{\prime}}{t_{n}}\log{n}+\log\left(C^{\prime}\tfrac{m}{t_{n}^{2}}\right)\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{n}:=w_{n}^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{1/3}}{Ca\log(1/p_{n})}.

(3.15)

Proof.

For an
m
-core
G^{*}
, let
A_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}\subseteq V(G^{*})
be the set of vertices with degree at least
t_{n}-C(\lambda)
, where
C(\lambda)=\lambda+\lambda^{2}
. Since
G^{*}
has
m
edges,

\left|A_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}\right|\leq 2m\left(t_{n}-C(\lambda)\right)^{-1}:=\ell.

(3.16)

We will show that the endpoints of every edge in
G^{*}
lie in
A_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}
. To see why, fix an edge
e\in E(G^{*})
. For every non-empty graph
F\subseteq K_{3}
, let
N(F,G^{*},e)
be the number of (induced unlabelled) copies of
F
in
G^{*}
containing
e
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}(T)-\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}\backslash e}(T)\leq\Big(N(K_{3},G^{*},e)+N(K_{1,2},G^{*},e)\,p_{n}+np_{n}^{2}\Big)(1-p_{n}).

(3.17)

By Definition
3.5
(C3),

t_{n}\leq N(K_{3},G^{*},e)+N(K_{1,2},G^{*},e)\,p_{n}+np_{n}^{2}.

(3.18)

Since
p_{n}=\lambda/n
, and since there can be at most
n
many
K_{1,2}
’s that contains a given edge
e
, we get
N(K_{3},G^{*},e)\geq t_{n}-C(\lambda)
. Moreover, if
e=uv
, then
N(K_{3},G^{*},e)\leq\min\{d_{u},d_{v}\}
, where
d_{u}
and
d_{v}
are the degree of
u
and
v
in
G^{*}
respectively. Therefore
\min\{d_{u},d_{v}\}\geq t_{n}-C(\lambda)
. Thus, both
u
and
v
are in
A_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}
.

For a set
A\subseteq[n]
, the number of
m
-cores
G^{*}
that satisfy
A_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}\subseteq A
is bounded above by
{{\ell^{2}}/{2}\choose m}
, where
|A|=\ell
. Therefore the number of cores with
m
edges is bounded above by

{n\choose\ell}{{\ell^{2}}/{2}\choose m}\leq n^{\ell}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}\ell^{2}}{2m}\right)^{m}.

(3.19)

Since
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}=0
, we have
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}t_{n}=\infty
. Thus,
\ell\leq C^{\prime}\frac{m}{t_{n}}
for large enough
n
, and therefore

n^{\ell}=\exp\left(\ell\log{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(C^{\prime}\tfrac{m}{t_{n}}\log{n}\right),

and

\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{e}\ell^{2}}{2m}\right)^{m}=\exp\left(m\log\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{e}\ell^{2}}{2m}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(m\log\left(C^{\prime}\tfrac{m}{t_{n}^{2}}\right)\right).

Therefore the number of
m
-cores is at most
(1/p_{n})^{m\Psi_{n}}
, where
\Psi_{n}
is defined in (
3.15
).
∎

3.2.
Upper bound on the upper tail probability

In this section, we use the lemmas proved in Section
3.1
to obtain a sharp bound on the upper tail probability, and thereby prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By Lemma
3.4
, Lemma
3.6
and a union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a seed})

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a core})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains an }m\text{-core}).

Again via a union bound and Lemma
3.7
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{n}^{m}\,|\,\{G^{*}\subseteq K_{n}\colon\,G^{*}\text{ is an }m\text{-core}\}\,|

(3.21)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\sum_{m\geq m_{\min}}p_{n}^{(1-\Psi_{n})m}.

Here,
m_{\min}
is the minimum number of edges in the core. We next prove a lower bound of
m_{\min}
. First, recall from (
1.6
) that
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-2w_{n})\leq e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log(1/p_{n})
for any graph
G
with
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq(a-2w_{n})k_{n}
. Further, since by Definition
3.5
(C1),
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G^{*}}(T)\geq(a-2w_{n})k_{n}
, we get
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-2w_{n})\leq m_{\min}\log(1/p_{n})
, and therefore

m_{\min}\geq\frac{\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-2w_{n})}{\log(1/p_{n})}.

(3.22)

Also, using the fact that
m\leq Caw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})
, and substituting
t_{n}:=w_{n}^{2}\frac{k_{n}^{1/3}}{Ca\log(1/p_{n})}
, we get

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(1/p_{n})}\left(\frac{1}{t_{n}}\log n+\log\left(C^{\prime}\tfrac{m}{t_{n}^{2}}\right)\right)

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}+\frac{\log[C^{\prime}w_{n}^{-5}(\log(1/p_{n}))^{3}]}{\log(1/p_{n})}:=\psi_{n}.

By our assumptions we have
\psi_{n}=o(1)
. Now using (
3.21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\xi_{n})\frac{p_{n}^{(1-\psi_{n})m_{\min}}}{1-p_{n}^{(1-\psi_{n})}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\exp\left(-(1-\psi_{n})m_{\min}\log(1/p_{n})\right)

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\exp\left(-(1-\psi_{n})\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a-2w_{n})\right),

as required.
∎

4.
The variational problem

In this section, we prove (
1.8
) in Theorem
1.2
. Note that an explicit solution of the variational problem in (
1.6
) would give rise to an explicit asymptotic formula for the upper tail probabilities in Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
. Indeed, we have already expressed the upper and lower bounds on the logarithmic probability of the event
\{T\geq ak_{n}\}
in terms of
\Phi_{n,p,k}(a)
with appropriate choices of the parameters. In this section, we obtain sharp estimates of the quantity
\Phi_{n,p,k}(a)
for choices of parameters that cover both Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
.

The following theorem provides asymptotically sharp upper and lower bounds on
\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a)
under a milder condition than those needed in Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

[Asymptotics for the variational problem]

For every sequence of positive reals
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}

w_{n}k_{n}^{1/3}=\infty
, for
n
large enough,

\tfrac{1}{2}[6a(1-w_{n})k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\leq\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}[6a(1+w_{n})k_{n}]^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n}).

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}k_{n}^{1/3}=\infty
. Note that if
G
is a complete graph on
(6a(1+w_{n})k_{n})^{1/3}
vertices, then
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq ak_{n}
for
n
sufficiently large. Moreover, we can find such a complete graph even when
6ak_{n}
is not a cube of some number, since the nearest cube larger than
6ak_{n}
is within
6ak_{n}+C^{\prime}k_{n}^{2/3}
for some constant
C^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\Phi_{n,p_{n},k_{n}}(a)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1+w_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n}).

(4.2)

For the lower bound, suppose that
G
is a graph that satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq ak_{n}
. Then
\Phi_{n,p,k}(a)\geq e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log\left(1/p\right)
. We are now left to find an appropriate lower bound on
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
. Since
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq ak_{n}
,

ak_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\leq N(K_{1,2},G)\,p_{n}+N(K_{2},G)\,np_{n}^{2}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}.

(4.3)

Here,
N(K_{1,2},G)\leq N(K_{2},G)|V(G)|
(since each edge in
G
can be in at most
|V(G)|
many
K_{1,2}
’s), and therefore

\displaystyle ak_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\leq N(K_{2},G)|V(G)|\,p_{n}+N(K_{2},G)\,np_{n}^{2}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda+n^{-1}\lambda^{2}\right)N(K_{2},G)+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}.

Now assume by contradiction that
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}=N(K_{2},G)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1-w_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}
. Then Lemma
2.3
gives
N(K_{3},G)\leq a(1-w_{n})k_{n}
, and the right-hand side of (
4.4
) is bounded above by
a(1-w_{n})k_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1-w_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}=ak_{n}-r_{n}
, where
\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=\infty
by our assumption that
\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n}k_{n}^{1/3}=\infty
, which contradicts (
4.4
). Therefore
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}=N(K_{2},G)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1-w_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}
, which together with
\Phi_{n,p,k}(a)\geq e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\log\left(1/p\right)
completes the proof of the lower bound.
∎

5.
Structure of random graphs with many triangles

In this section, we investigate the structure of
G_{n,p_{n}}

conditionally
on
T\geq ak_{n}
as stated in Theorem
1.6
. In Section
5.1
, we show that, conditionally on
T\geq ak_{n}
, there is a near-clique (recall Definition
1.5
). In Section
5.2
, we study the local structure of the Erdős-Rényi random graph conditionally on
T\geq ak_{n}
, and show that its local limit exists and is the same as that for the original Erdős-Rényi random graph.

5.1.
Localization of the triangles: Existence of near-cliques

The following theorem states a more precise version of (
1.9
) in Theorem
1.6
, where we recall the notion of a
(\delta,m)
-clique in Definition
1.5
:

Theorem 5.1
.

[Existence of a near-clique]

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\left(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm~contains no }\left(C(\psi_{n}+w_{n}),m_{n}\right)\text{\rm-clique}\mid T\geq ak_{n}\right)\leq\delta,

(5.1)

where
m_{n}:=\tfrac{1}{2}[6(a-2w_{n})k_{n}(1-Ck_{n}^{-1/3})]^{2/3}
, and

\psi_{n}=\frac{C^{\prime}\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}+\frac{\log[C^{\prime}w_{n}^{-5}(\log(1/p_{n}))^{3}]}{\log(1/p_{n})}

(5.2)

is defined in (
3.23
).

The proof will follow from Propositions
5.3
and
5.5
below.

We start by setting the stage. We will work with
\psi_{n}
-minimal cores, which are cores that do not have too many edges (recall also Definition
3.5
for the definition of a core).

Definition 5.2
.

[
\psi_{n}
-minimal cores]

For a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}=0
,
G
is called a
\psi_{n}
-minimal core if
G
is a core with
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1+\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}
.

Note that the upper bound in
e_{\scriptscriptstyle G}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1+\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}
is chosen in relation to the event
T\geq ak_{n}
of
interest. The following lemma reveals the structure of the graph when it has many triangles:

Proposition 5.3
.

[Unlikely not to have a
\psi_{n}
-minimal core]

For every
a,\delta>0
and every sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in Definition
3.5
of the core satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log{n}}{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{1/3}}

=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\log w_{n})/\log(1/p_{n})=0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm~contains no }2\psi_{n}\text{-minimal core}\right)\leq\delta\,\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n}).

(5.3)

Proof.

For any sequence of positive reals
\psi_{n}^{\prime}
, we can split

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains no }\psi_{n}^{\prime}\text{-minimal core}\right)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains no core}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ has a core that is not }\psi_{n}^{\prime}\text{-minimal}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Term-I}+\text{Term-II}.

We bound both contributions. For
Term-I
, we start by recalling that (
3.5
) in Lemma
3.4
implies

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains no seed}\right)\leq\xi_{n}\,\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n}),

(5.5)

where

\xi_{n}:=\exp\left([\tfrac{1}{2}(6(a-w_{n}))^{2/3}-\tfrac{1}{3}C]\,k_{n}^{2/3}\log(1/p_{n})\right).

(5.6)

By Lemma
3.6
,

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n},\,G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm contains no core}\right)\leq\xi_{n}\,\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n}).

(5.7)

Hence we can use (
5.7
) to get

\text{Term-I}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\,\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n}).

(5.8)

This bounds
Term-I
. For
Term-II
, using the union bound, we have

\text{Term-II}\leq\sum_{m\geq\frac{1}{2}(6a(1+\psi_{n}^{\prime})k_{n})^{2/3}}p_{n}^{m}\,|\,\{G^{*}\subseteq K_{n}\colon\,G^{*}\text{ is an }m\text{-core}\}\,|.

(5.9)

We take
\psi_{n}^{\prime}=2\psi_{n}
, recall (
3.23
) and note that, under the assumptions in Proposition
5.3
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}

=0
. By Lemma
3.7
(see, in particular, (

) in its proof),

\displaystyle\text{Term-II}\leq\sum_{m\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1+2\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}}p_{n}^{m(1-\psi_{n})}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,p_{n}^{\tfrac{1}{2}(6a(1+2\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}(1-\psi_{n})}.

(5.10)

Note that
(1+2x)^{2/3}(1-x)=((1+2x)(1-x))^{2/3}(1-x)^{1/3}
, which gives

for

sufficiently small. Since

, using this in (

), we get

\text{Term-II}\leq p_{n}^{\tfrac{1}{2}(6ak_{n})^{2/3}+c\psi_{n}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon\,p_{n}^{\tfrac{1}{2}(6ak_{n})^{2/3}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\,\mathbb{P}(T\geq ak_{n}).

(5.11)

In the last equation we use that
\psi_{n}\geq\frac{\log{C^{\prime}(\log(1/p_{n}))}}{\log(1/p_{n})}
, which gives
p_{n}^{\psi_{n}}\leq\exp{(-\log{C^{\prime}(\log(1/p_{n}))})}
, and so
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{n}^{\psi_{n}}=0
. This bounds
Term-II
.

Combining (
5.8
) and (

), we arrive at the claim.
∎

We continue by studying subgraphs of

-minimal cores. Proposition

below proves that a subgraph exists with a high minimal degree. Let

denote the set of embeddings of a graph

in

, i.e., the set of edge-preserving injective maps from

. We use the following theorem from

(also proved in

]
):

Proposition 5.4
.

[Existence of subgraph with high minimal-degree under embeddings bound]

If a graph

(5.12)

for some

, then

with minimum degree

.

.

[Existence of subgraph of
\psi_{n}
-minimal core with high minimal-degree]

A
\psi_{n}
-minimal core

whose minimum degree is at least
(1-4(C(\psi_{n}+w_{n}))^{1/2})(2e_{\scriptscriptstyle G})^{1/2}
.

Proof.

A

-minimal core has at most

edges and, by Definition

of a core, also
\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)\geq(a-2w_{n})k_{n}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(a-2w_{n})k_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(T)

\displaystyle\leq N(K_{1,2},G)\,p_{n}+N(K_{2},G)\,np_{n}^{2}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda(6a(1+\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}+\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-1}\lambda^{2}(6a(1+\psi_{n})k_{n})^{2/3}+N(K_{3},G)+\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}.

5.2.
The local limit of the random graph conditionally on many triangles

In this section, we show that even if we condition the graph to have at least
ak_{n}
triangles with
k_{n}
not too large, then the local structure of the graph remains unaffected. To describe the result, we introduce some notation and define local convergence. We refer to
[
1
]
or
[
28
, Chapter 2]
for more details.

A
rooted graph

(G,o)
is a locally-finite (i.e., the degrees of all the vertices are finite) and connected graph
G=(V,E)
with a distinguished vertex
o\in V
, called the root. For an integer
r\geq 0
, let
(G,o)_{r}
be the induced subgraph on the vertex set
\{u\in V\colon{\rm dist}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(o,u)\leq r\}
, where
{\rm dist}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}
is the usual graph distance in
G
.

Two rooted graphs
(G_{i},o_{i})=(V_{i},E_{i},o_{i})
,
i=1,2
, are
isomorphic
if there exists a bijection
\sigma\colon\,V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
such that
\sigma(o_{1})=o_{2}
, and
\{u,v\}\in E_{1}
if and only if
\{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)\}\in E_{2}
. It is easy to check that the rooted isomorphism is an equivalence relation, which we denote by
(G_{1},o_{1})\simeq(G_{2},o_{2})
. An equivalence class of rooted graphs is sometimes just called an unlabelled rooted graph.

Let
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
be the set of all locally finite, connected and unlabelled rooted graphs. For
\gamma\in\mathscr{G}^{\star}
and an integer
h\geq 0
, let
\gamma_{h}
denote the unlabelled rooted graph obtained by removing all the vertices, and all the edges incident to them, that are at graph-distance larger than
h
from the root.

The
local topology
is the smallest topology such that for any
\gamma\in\mathscr{G}^{\star}
the
\mathscr{G}^{\star}\to\{0,1\}
function
f(g)=\mathbf{1}(g_{h}=\gamma_{h})
is continuous. This topology is metrizable, and with this topology the space
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
is complete and separable
[
1
]
. Write
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
to denote the space of probability measures on
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
, and equip
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
with the topology of weak convergence.

Fix a finite graph
G=(V,E)
. The empirical neighborhood distribution of
U(G)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
is defined by

U(G):=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}\delta_{[G,v]},

(5.16)

where
[G,v]\in\mathscr{G}^{\star}
denotes the equivalence class of
(G(v),v)
and
\delta_{g}
is the Dirac mass at
g\in\mathscr{G}^{\star}
. For a sequence of finite graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we say that
G_{n}
converges to
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
in the
local weak sense
if
U(G_{n})
converges to
\rho
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
, i.e., if for all bounded and continuous functions
h\colon\mathscr{G}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}h([G,v])\Big]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[h(G,o)],

(5.17)

where the expectation is w.r.t. the (possibly random) graph
G_{n}
. We further say that
G_{n}
converges
locally in probability
to
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
if for all bounded and continuous functions
h\colon\mathscr{G}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}h([G,v])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[h(G,o)].

(5.18)

Now consider
\text{UGW}(\text{Poi}(\lambda))\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
, the Galton-Watson tree where the root has
\text{Poi}(\lambda)
many children, and all the children have
\text{Poi}(\lambda)
many children independently, and so on. Our main result on local convergence is the following theorem:

Theorem 5.6
.

[Local convergence conditionally on many triangles]

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}^{2/3}\log p_{n}/n=0
. Then, under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, conditionally on
T\geq ak_{n}
,
G_{n,p_{n}}
converges locally in probability to
\text{\rm UGW}(\text{\rm Poi}(\lambda)
.

Our proof crucially relies on
[
11
, Theorem 1.8]
. In order to show that the local structure of the random graph does not change under the presence of a large number of triangles, we compute the probability that the random graph is concentrated around the Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution Poi
(\lambda)
. We rely on the following result from
[
11
]
:

Proposition 5.7
.

[
11
, Theorem 1.8]

Fix
\lambda>0
, and let
G_{n,p_{n}}
be the random graph on
n
vertices with
p_{n}=\lambda/n
. Then
U(G_{n,p_{n}})
satisfies the large deviation principle in
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
with rate
n
and with a good rate function
I
, i.e.,
I
is lower semi-continuous and has compact level sets. Further,

I(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}d\log{\lambda}-\Sigma\left(\rho\right),

(5.19)

where
d:=\mathbbm{E}_{\rho}(\text{\rm deg}_{\scriptscriptstyle G}(o))
is the degree of
o
in
G
, with the convention that
\Sigma\left(\rho\right)=0
if
d=0
. The quantity
\Sigma\left(\rho\right)
will be defined below.

To describe
\Sigma\left(\rho\right)
appearing in the rate function, we need some further notation. Let
\mathcal{G}_{n,m}
be the set of graphs on vertex set
[n]
and
m
edges. If
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
, then define

\mathcal{G}_{n,m}(\rho,\varepsilon):=\{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n,m}:U(G)\in B(\rho,\varepsilon)\},

(5.20)

where
B(\rho,\varepsilon)
denotes the
\varepsilon
-open ball around
\rho
in Lévy-Prokhorov metric on
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
.

For the convenience of the reader we recall the definition of the Lévy-Prokhorov metric. Consider a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
be the Borel sigma-field of the space
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
equipped with local topology, and define its
\varepsilon
-neighborhood by

A^{\varepsilon}=\cup_{g\in A}{B}_{\mathscr{G}^{\star}}(g,\varepsilon),

(5.21)

where
{B}_{\mathscr{G}^{\star}}(g,\varepsilon)
is an open ball in
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
of radius
\varepsilon
around
g
. Then the Lévy-Prokhorov metric
\pi\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})^{2}\to[0,\infty)
is defined as follows: For
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
,

\pi(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0:\rho_{1}(a)\leq\rho_{2}(A^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\text{ and }\rho_{2}(a)\leq\rho_{1}(A^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\text{ for all }A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})\}.

(5.22)

Since
\mathscr{G}^{\star}
is separable in the local topology, the topology induced by
\pi
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
is equivalent to the topology of weak convergence on
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
(see
[
8
]
). We write

\Sigma(\rho,\varepsilon)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{|\mathcal{G}_{n,m}(\rho,\varepsilon)|}-m\log{n}}{n}.

(5.23)

Finally,

\Sigma(\rho):=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\Sigma(\rho,\varepsilon).

(5.24)

Write
s(d)=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\log{d}
. We are now ready to state and prove the main proposition in this section, which will immediately prove Theorem
5.6
:

Proposition 5.8
.

[Exponential concentration around
\text{\rm UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda))
]

For every
\varepsilon>0
, and
n\geq n(\varepsilon)
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in B^{c}(\text{\rm UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda)),\varepsilon)\right)\leq\exp\left(-C_{\varepsilon}n\right).

(5.25)

Proof.

We use the exponential tightness of a sequence of probability measures satisfying a large deviation upper bound with a rate function that has compact level sets, as stated in Proposition
5.7
. This implies that, for each positive integer
l\geq 1
, there is a compact set
C_{l}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{G}^{\star})
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\rho\in C_{l}^{c}\right)\leq-l.

(5.26)

Now let
S_{l}=C_{l}\cap B^{c}(\text{\rm UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda)),\varepsilon)
. Then, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
and
n\geq n(\varepsilon)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in B^{c}(\text{\rm UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda)),\varepsilon)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in S_{l}\right)+\exp(-C_{l}n),

(5.27)

for some constant
C_{l}>0
. Note that
S_{l}
is a compact set (since it is a closed subset of a compact set) Using Proposition
5.7
again we have, for any
\delta>0
and
n\geq n(\delta)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in S_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\inf_{\rho\in S_{l}}I(\rho)+\delta

\displaystyle=-\min_{\rho\in S_{l}}I(\rho)+\delta.

(5.28)

In the second line, we have used the fact that
I
is lower semi-continuous, and therefore the minimum is attained on a compact set.

We are done if we can show that
\min_{\rho\in S_{l}}I(\rho)>0
. First, let us recall from
[
3
, Remark 4.12]
that UGW
(\text{\rm Poi}(d))

uniquely
maximizes
\Sigma(\rho)
among all measures
\rho
with mean degree
d
. Also recall from
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
that
\Sigma(\rho)\leq s(d)
, where
s(d)=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\log{d}
. Therefore,
\Sigma(\rho)<s(d)
for all
\rho
except
\rho
= UGW
(\text{Poi}(d))
.

Let
\rho^{\prime}
be a minimizer of
I
on
S_{l}
having mean degree
d
. Since
\rho^{\prime}\neq
UGW
(\text{Poi}(\lambda))
, there can be two cases:

Case I:

\rho^{\prime}
has mean degree
d\neq\lambda
.

In this case,

\displaystyle\min_{\rho\in S_{l}}I(\rho)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}d\log{\lambda}-\max_{\rho\in S_{l}}\Sigma\left(\rho\right)

(5.29)

\displaystyle\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}d\log{\lambda}-s(d)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}d\log{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{2}d+\tfrac{1}{2}d\log{d}>0.

Case II:

\rho^{\prime}
has mean degree
d=\lambda
.

In this case, since
\rho^{\prime}\neq
UGW
(\text{\rm Poi}(\lambda))
, we must have
\Sigma(\rho^{\prime})<s(\lambda)
, and therefore

\displaystyle\min_{\rho\in S_{l}}I(\rho)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\log{\lambda}-\max_{\rho\in S_{l}}\Sigma\left(\rho\right)

(5.30)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\log{\lambda}-\Sigma\left(\rho^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle>\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\log{\lambda}-s(\lambda)=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

We can now complete the proof of Theorem
5.6
:

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

First note that

\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in B^{c}(\text{UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda)),\varepsilon)\mid T\geq ak_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(U(G_{n})\in B^{c}(\text{\rm UGW}({\rm Poi}(\lambda)),\varepsilon)\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq ak_{n}\right)}.

(5.31)

We can upper bound the numerator in the right-hand side of (
5.31
) by Proposition
5.8
, and lower bound the denominator by Theorems
2.1
and
4.1
.
∎

6.
Large deviations for the number of vertices in triangles

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.8
by providing sharp upper and lower bounds of the probability that at least
k_{n}
vertices are part of some triangle in
G_{n,p_{n}}
. Roughly, the proof is based on the idea that if
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains
k_{n}
vertices that are part of some triangle, then there are approximately
k_{n}/3
disjoint triangles in the graph.

The proof of the lower bound in Section
6.1
is relatively easy and involves computing an appropriate lower bound of the probability of the aforementioned event. The upper bound in Section
6.2
is much more delicate. For this we carefully compute the number of ways a graph on
n
vertices can contain
k_{n}
many vertices that are part of a triangle. To do this, we find it more convenient to work with a ‘basic graph’. Roughly, basic graphs do not contain any
unnecessary edges
. More precisely, the edges that are not contributing to the formation of any triangle in the graph are removed. Interestingly, these basic graphs admit a structural decomposition, which in turn enables us to compute the number of basic graphs with a given number of edges. Using this fact, we compute the upper bound of the probability that in
G_{n,p_{n}}
the
k_{n}
vertices are part of some triangle. The details of the proof are presented in Section
6.2
.

6.1.
Proof of the lower bound in Theorem
1.8

In this section, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.8
, by proving the following more precise theorem:

Theorem 6.1
.

[Large deviation lower bound on the number of vertices in triangles]

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
and
k_{n}\leq n
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\geq-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})-Ck_{n}.

(6.1)

For the lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})
, we construct an appropriate lower bounding event. For the rest of the proof, we assume that
k_{n}
is divisible by
3
. It is easy to see that this does not affect our estimates. Indeed, if
k_{n}
is not divisible by
3
, then
k_{n}+t
must be divisible by
3
, where
t=1
or
2
, and then
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n}+t)
, and the contribution of
t
is easily seen to be negligible.

Define the event
\mathcal{T}_{S}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{S}

\displaystyle:=\{S\text{ is a union of vertex-disjoint triangles and no other edge is present in }G[S]\}

(6.2)

\displaystyle\cap\{G\backslash E(G[S])\text{ is triangle-free}\}.

Note that the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{S}
is valid only when
|S|
is divisible by
3
, and that
\mathcal{T}_{S}
and
\mathcal{T}_{S^{\prime}}
are
disjoint
for any different sets
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
.

Lemma 6.2
.

[Only vertex-disjoint triangles]

For
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=k_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{S})\geq\frac{k_{n}!}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})!}}p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}\,\tfrac{9}{10}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}\right).

(6.3)

Proof.

First observe that

A_{S}\cap B_{S}\cap C_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{S},

(6.4)

where

\displaystyle A_{S}

\displaystyle:=\{[S]\text{ is a union of vertex-disjoint triangles and no other edge is present in }G[S]\},

(6.5)

\displaystyle B_{S}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{there is no edge between }V\backslash S\text{ and }S\},

\displaystyle C_{S}

\displaystyle:=\{G[V\backslash S]\text{ is triangle-free}\}.

Then
A_{S}
,
B_{S}
,
C_{S}
are independent events, so that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{S})\geq\mathbb{P}(A_{S})\mathbb{P}(B_{S})\mathbb{P}(C_{S}).

(6.6)

Since the distribution of the number of triangles converges to a Poisson distribution with mean
\frac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}
, for large enough
n
we have
\mathbb{P}(C_{S})\geq 0.9\exp\left(-\frac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}\right)
. It is easy to see that
\mathbb{P}(B_{S})=(1-p_{n})^{(n-k_{n})k_{n}}
. Finally, for
\mathbb{P}(A_{S})
we assume that
k_{n}
is divisible by
3
, and estimate

\mathbb{P}(A_{S})=\frac{k_{n}!}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}}(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}}.

(6.7)

We combine this with

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{S})\geq\frac{k_{n}!}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}}(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}\,0.9\,\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}\lambda^{3}\right)

(6.8)

to arrive at the claim.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof of Theorem
6.1
:

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Note that

\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(\cup_{|S|=k_{n}}\mathcal{T}_{S})=\sum_{|S|=k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{S}).

(6.9)

By Lemma
6.2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})

\displaystyle\geq C\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k_{n}+1)}{(3!)^{\frac{k_{n}}{3}}\frac{k_{n}}{3}!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}

(6.10)

\displaystyle=C\frac{n!}{(n-k_{n})!(3!)^{\frac{k_{n}}{3}}\frac{k_{n}}{3}!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}

for some constant
C>0
. Using Stirling’s approximation,

\sqrt{2\pi}L^{L+{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-L}\leq L!\leq\mathrm{e}L^{L+{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-L},

(6.11)

we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})

\displaystyle\geq C\frac{n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{k_{n}}}{(n-k_{n})^{n-k_{n}+\frac{1}{2}}(3!)^{\frac{k_{n}}{3}}\frac{k_{n}}{3}!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}

(6.12)

\displaystyle\geq C\frac{n^{k_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{k_{n}}}{(3!)^{\frac{k_{n}}{3}}\frac{k_{n}}{3}!}\,p_{n}^{k_{n}}(1-p_{n})^{{k_{n}\choose 2}-k_{n}+(n-k_{n})k_{n}}.

Using Stirling’s approximation in (
6.11
) again, and the fact that
\log(1-x)\geq-2x
for
0<x<\tfrac{1}{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})

\displaystyle\geq C\exp\left(k_{n}\log{n}-Ck_{n}-k_{n}\log{n}-\tfrac{2}{n}(n-k_{n})k_{n}\right)

(6.13)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})\right).

Therefore, possibly after enlarging
C
, arrive at

\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\geq C\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})-Ck_{n}\right)

(6.14)

as required.
∎

6.2.
Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.8

In this section, we prove the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})
stated in Theorem
1.8
:

Theorem 6.3
.

[Upper bound on the probability of many vertices in triangles]

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{k_{n}}{\log n}=\infty
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\log\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})\leq-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})+Ck_{n}.

(6.15)

First we develop some combinatorial tools that we will need along the way. We begin with a simple lemma that provides a lower bound on the number of edges for a graph that has
q
-vertices in triangles:

Lemma 6.4
.

[Edges and triangles]

Let
G
be a graph with
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=q
. Then
|E(G)|\geq q
.

Proof.

Let
d_{v}
denote the degree of vertex
v\in[n]
. If
v
is part of a triangle, then
d_{v}\geq 2
. Let
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{q}
be the vertices that are part of a triangle. Then

2|E(G)|=\sum_{v\in V(G)}d_{v}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{q}d_{v_{j}}\geq 2q.

(6.16)

∎

We find it more convenient to work with a basic subgraph of a graph with at least
q
vertices that are part of a triangle:

Definition 6.5
.

[
q
-basic graph]

A subgraph
G\subseteq K_{n}
is called
q
-basic
if
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=q
and
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G\backslash e)<q
for all edges
e\in G
. Here,
G\backslash e
denotes the graph with the edge
e
removed.

\spadesuit

Remark 6.6
.

[Lower bound on edges in
q
-basic graphs]

Note that every edge in a
q
-basic graph is part of some triangle, otherwise we can delete the edge without removing any triangle.
\blacksquare

The next lemma shows that whenever there are
q
-vertices in a graph
G
that are part of a triangle, then it contains a
q
-basic subgraph:

Lemma 6.7
.

[There is a
q
-basic graph spanning the vertices in triangles]

If
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=q
, then there is a subgraph
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
that is a
q
-basic graph.

Proof.

Starting with the graph
G
, we delete an edge
e
such that
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G\backslash e)=q
. We repeat this until there is no such edge left. Let us denote this graph by
G^{\prime}
. At this point, by our construction,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G)=q
but
V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G^{\prime}\backslash e)<q
for every
e\in G^{\prime}
. Therefore, by Definition
6.5
,
G^{\prime}
a
q
-basic graph.
∎

The following is our key decomposition lemma of a
q
-basic graph, which will be seen to be instrumental in counting their number. This lemma gives us the rough structure of a
q
-basic graph. For a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
and
u,v,w\in V(G)
,
w
is called a
co-neighbor
of a pair of vertices
u
and
v
if
(u,w),(v,w)\in E(G)
. Also, for
U\subseteq V
,
G[U]
is the induced subgraph of
G
on the vertex set
U
. Finally,
U\subseteq V
is called an
independent set
if there is no edge in
G[U]
.

Lemma 6.8
.

[Decomposition lemma of
q
-basic graphs]

The vertices of a
q
-basic graph
G
can be partitioned into three disjoint parts
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
such that the following properties hold:

–

G[V_{1}]
is a union of vertex-disjoint triangles.

–

G[V(G)\backslash V_{1}]
does not contain a triangle.

–

G[V_{2}]
is a union of vertex-disjoint edges, and the endpoints of each edge in
G[V_{2}]
have a co-neighbour in
V_{1}
.

–

G[V_{3}]
is an independent set, and each vertex in
V_{3}
is either a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge in
V_{1}
, or a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Proof.

First, we extract the vertex-disjoint triangles from
G
, one by one in an arbitrary order, and stop when no more triangles can be extracted. The set of vertices of the extracted triangles is denoted by
V_{1}
. Clearly, there is no triangle left in the graph induced by
G[V(G)\backslash V_{1}]
.

Next, starting from
G[V(G)\backslash V_{1}]
, we extract vertex-disjoint edges, one by one in an arbitrary order, and stop when no vertex-disjoint edge is left. The set of vertices in the extracted edges is denoted by
V_{2}
. Note that every endpoint of an edge in
G[V_{2}]
has a co-neighbour in
V_{1}
, because otherwise the edge is not part of any triangle, which is impossible by Remark
6.6
.

Finally, put
V_{3}:=V(G)\backslash(V_{1}\cup V_{2})
. Note that
G[V_{3}]
is an independent set, because otherwise there is an edge in
G[V_{3}]
whose endpoints, by construction, would have been added to
V_{2}
. Also, each vertex is in a triangle and therefore each vertex in
G[V_{3}]
is either a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge in
V_{1}
, or a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Indeed, the other choice is impossible: if a vertex in
G[V_{3}]
is a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge in
V_{2}
, then we get a triangle outside
V_{1}
, which violates our construction.
∎

Lemma
6.8
gives us a natural way to decompose the
q
-basic graphs in terms of the sizes of
V_{1},V_{2}
and
V_{3}
.

Definition 6.9
.

[
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
configuration of
q
-basic graphs]

A
q
-basic graph
G
has an
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
configuration if there is a decomposition as in Lemma
6.8
with
|V_{i}|=\ell_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
. (Note that
q=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
.)

\spadesuit

Definition
6.9
is useful to upper bound the number of edges in
q
-basic graphs:

Lemma 6.10
.

[Upper bound on edges in
q
-basic graphs]

A
q
-basic graph has at most
3q
edges.

Proof.

Suppose that the
q
-basic graph has an
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
configuration. Then
G[V_{1}]
has
\ell_{1}/3
disjoint triangles, which contribute
\ell_{1}
edges. Note that these edges put all vertices in
V_{1}
in some triangle.

Next,
\ell_{2}/2
edges in
G[V_{2}]
are due to its
\ell_{2}/2
disjoint edges. Since, by Lemma
6.8
, the pair of endpoints of each edge in
V_{2}
has a co-neighbor in
V_{1}
, they contribute another
\ell_{2}
edges. Therefore in total we get
{3\ell_{2}}/2
edges, and all vertices in
V_{2}
are now part of some triangle.

Finally, since each vertex in
V_{3}
is an independent set, and all vertices are in some triangle, the maximum number of edges needed to put each vertex in a triangle is
3\ell_{3}
. At this point all vertices in
V_{3}
are part of some triangle as well.

Since the graph is
q
-basic, there are no more edges present in the graph (recall Remark
6.6
). Therefore the graph has at most
\ell_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\ell_{2}+3\ell_{3}
edges. Since
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=q
, we arrive at the conclusion that a
q
-basic graph has at most
3q
edges.
∎

The next lemma counts the number of
q
-basic graphs with configuration
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
.

Lemma 6.11
.

[The number of
q
-basic graphs in configuration]

The number of
q
-basic subgraphs of
K_{n}
with configuration
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
is at most

\tfrac{n^{q}}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}\times\ell_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}\times\left(3q\right)^{\ell_{3}}.

(6.17)

Proof.

Using Lemma
6.8
, we get that the number of
q
-basic subgraphs of
K_{n}
with configuration
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
is at most

\tfrac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-\ell_{3}+1)}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}\ell_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}\left(|E\left(V_{1}\right)|+|E\left(V_{1},V_{2}\right)|\right)^{\ell_{3}}.

(6.18)

First, let us explain the combinatorial factor

\tfrac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-\ell_{3}+1)}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}.

(6.19)

The numerator counts the number of ways to select the
q=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
vertices, the term
(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!
in the denominator counts the number of permutation of the
\ell_{1}/3
triangles in
V_{1}
, while
(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}
counts the permutations of the vertices of each of the individual triangles. Similarly,
(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!
accounts for the permutation of the edges in
V_{2}
, and
(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}
accounts for the permutations of the vertices of each of the individual edges. Finally,
\ell_{3}!
counts the permutations of the vertices in
V_{3}
.

Second,
\ell_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}
is the number of ways the endpoints of the
\ell_{2}/2
vertex-disjoint edges can select a co-neighbor from
V_{1}
, and
\left(|E\left(V_{1}\right)|+|E\left(V_{1},V_{2}\right)|\right)^{\ell_{3}}
is the number of ways a vertex in
V_{3}
can be part of some triangle, i.e., each vertex in
V_{3}
is either a co-neighbor of an edge in
G[V_{1}]
or a co-neighbor of an edge between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Using Lemma
6.10
, this can be bounded from above by

\tfrac{n^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}}}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}\times\ell_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}\times\left(3q\right)^{\ell_{3}}.

(6.20)

∎

Before investigating the number of
q
-basic graphs with
m
edges, we study a minimization problem that appears in it:

Lemma 6.12
.

[Optimization problem]

For
q
sufficiently large, the minimum value of the function

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\tfrac{1}{3}x_{1}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}x_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2}x_{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}x_{2})+x_{3}\log x_{3}

(6.21)

subject to the constraint
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=q
is at least
\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3}\log{q})\log(\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3})\log q)
.
Further, the maximizers satisfy
q-q^{2/3}\log q\leq x_{1}\leq q
and
0\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\leq q^{2/3}\log q
.

Proof.

We use the Lagrange multiplier method. Define

L:=\tfrac{1}{3}x_{1}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}x_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2}x_{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}x_{2})+x_{3}\log x_{3}+\mu(q-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}).

(6.22)

Differentiating
L
with respect to
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
, we get

\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{1}}=\tfrac{1}{3}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}x_{1})+\tfrac{1}{3}-\mu,\,\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}x_{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}-\mu,\,\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{3}}=\log x_{3}+1-\mu,

(6.23)

with
\mu
the Lagrange multiplier. Setting these derivatives equal to zero, and substituting them into
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=q
, we get

3\mathrm{e}^{3\mu-1}\left(1+\tfrac{2}{3}\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}+\tfrac{1}{3}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu}\right)=q.

(6.24)

Note that the left-hand side of (
6.24
) is strictly increasing in
\mu
, and therefore there is a unique solution to (
6.24
). Moreover, the solution must be less than
\mu_{U}
, where
3\exp{\left(3\mu_{U}-1\right)}=q
.

Next, we show that when
q
is large enough, the solution is larger than
\mu_{L}
, where
3\exp{\left(3\mu_{L}-1\right)}=q-q^{2/3}\log{q}
. To see why, substitute
\mu_{L}
into (
6.24
), to get that the left-hand side is equal to

\left(q-q^{2/3}\log{q}\right)\left(1+\tfrac{2}{3}\left(\tfrac{3}{(q-q^{2/3}\log{q})\mathrm{e}}\right)^{1/3}+\tfrac{1}{3}\left(\tfrac{3}{(q-q^{2/3}\log{q})\mathrm{e}}\right)^{2/3}\right).

(6.25)

Now using the fact that
q-q^{2/3}\log{q}\geq 0.9q
for
q
sufficiently large, for some constant
C
, we see that the last display is bounded above by

\left(q-q^{2/3}\log{q}\right)\left(1+\frac{C}{q^{1/3}}+\frac{C}{q^{2/3}}\right)<q.

(6.26)

Hence, for
q
sufficiently large, the solution of (
6.24
) lies in the interval

(\mu_{L},\mu_{U})=\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3}\log{q}))+\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{3}\log\tfrac{1}{2}q+\tfrac{1}{3}\right).

(6.27)

Therefore
q-q^{2/3}\log q\leq x_{1}\leq q
, and consequently
0\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\leq q^{2/3}\log q
. Hence the minimum value of
f
is at least
\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3}\log q)\log(\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3}\log q))
.
∎

The next lemma bounds the number of
q
-basic graphs in terms of the number of its edges.

Lemma 6.13
.

[The number of
q
-basic graphs in terms of edges]

For
q
sufficiently large, the number of
q
-basic subgraphs of
K_{n}
with
m
edges is at most

C(3q)^{3}\exp\left(m\log n-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+16q\right)

(6.28)

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Proof.

First note that the number of edges in a
q
-basic graph with configuration
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
is at least

\ell_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\ell_{2}+2\ell_{3}.

(6.29)

To see why, observe that
\ell_{1}
edges in
G[V_{1}]
are contributed by
\ell_{1}/3
disjoint triangles, and
\ell_{2}/2
edges in
G[V_{2}]
. Since the endpoints of each edge in
V_{2}
have a co-neighbour in
V_{1}
, they contribute another
\ell_{2}
edges. Moreover, since each vertex in
V_{3}
is either a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge in
V_{1}
or a co-neighbour of the endpoints of an edge between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, they contribute at least
2\ell_{3}
edges. Therefore, via Lemma
6.11
, the number of
q
-basic graphs with
m
edges is at most

\sum_{\ell_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\ell_{2}+2\ell_{3}\leq m}\tfrac{n^{q}}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{1}!}\times\ell_{1}^{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}\times\left(3q\right)^{\ell_{3}}.

(6.30)

Since
\ell_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\ell_{2}+2\ell_{3}\leq m
and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=q
, we get that
q\leq m-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}-\ell_{3}
. Further, using
m\leq 3q
, we get that there are at most
(3q)^{3}
terms in the sum. Therefore we can bound the expression in the last display crudely by

(3q)^{3}\,\tfrac{\exp\left((m-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}-\ell_{3})\log{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}\log{\ell_{1}}+\ell_{3}\log{3q}\right)}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}.

(6.31)

Using that
\ell_{1},q\leq n
, we see that this can by bounded by

\displaystyle(3q)^{3}\tfrac{\exp\left(m\log{n}+\ell_{3}\log 3\right)}{(3!)^{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}\times(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})!\times(2!)^{{\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}}}\times(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})!\times\ell_{3}!}

(6.32)

\displaystyle\leq C(3q)^{3}\exp\left(m\log{n}-\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log{\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}}+4\ell_{1}-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})+{\ell_{2}}-\left(\ell_{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log{\ell_{3}}+3\ell_{3}\right)

for some absolute constant
C
. We can simplify (
6.32
) further by enlarging
C
, and using the bound
\ell_{i}\leq q
for the smaller order terms. This gives the bound

\exp\left(m\log n-\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})-\ell_{3}\log{\ell_{3}}+15q\right),

(6.33)

where we use Stirling’s approximation in (
6.11
). Now note that in (
6.32
) the term
\frac{1}{3}\ell_{1}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})
is asymptotically the smallest (see Lemma
6.12
for the formal proof), i.e., subject to
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}={q}
the minimum value of
\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}\ell_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell_{2})+\ell_{3}\log{\ell_{3}}
is bounded below by
\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3}\log{q})\log(\tfrac{1}{3}(q-q^{2/3})\log q)
for
q
sufficiently large
q
. Therefore (
6.32
) can be bounded from above by

C(3q)^{3}\exp\left(m\log n-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+16q\right),

(6.34)

as required.
∎

We next estimate the probability that
G_{n,p_{n}}
contains a
q
-basic subgraph of
K_{n}
:

Lemma 6.14
.

[Upper bound on the probability to contain a
q
-basic subgraph]

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all sufficiently large integers
q
,

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{\rm~contains a }q\text{-basic graph of }K_{n})\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+Cq\right).

(6.35)

Proof.

Using Lemmas
6.4
,
6.10
and
6.13
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a }q\text{-basic subgraph of }K_{n})

(6.36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=q}^{3q}\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a }q\text{-basic subgraph of }K_{n}\text{ with }m\text{ edges})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=q}^{3q}p_{n}^{m}\,\#\{q\text{-basic subgraphs of }K_{n}\text{ with }m\text{ edges}\}

\displaystyle=C(3q)^{2}\sum_{m=q}^{3q}\exp\left(m\log\lambda-m\log n+m\log n-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+16q\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+Cq\right)

for some constant
C>0
.
∎

We are now ready to estimate
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq k_{n})
as in Theorem
6.3
:

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

Using Lemma
6.7
and the union bound, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})\geq ak_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=k_{n}}^{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(G_{n,p_{n}})=q)

(6.37)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=k_{n}}^{n}\mathbb{P}(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ contains a }q\text{-basic subgraph of }K_{n}).

Using Lemma
6.14
, we bound (
6.37
) from above by

\sum_{q=k_{n}}^{n}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}q\log(\tfrac{1}{3}q)+Cq\right)\leq n\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n}\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k_{n})+Ck_{n}\right),

(6.38)

as required.
∎

7.
Phase transition in exponential random graphs: Proof of Corollary
1.10

In this section, we apply Theorem
1.8
to analyze the exponential random graph model
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
defined in (
1.15
) by proving Corollary
1.10
.

Proof of Corollary
1.10
.

We rely on Theorem
1.8
and simple bounds. We first note that, by Jensen,

\log Z_{n}(\beta\log n)=\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}]=o(n\log{n}),

(7.1)

since
\mathbb{P}(1\text{ is in a triangle})=o(1)
.

This proves the lower bound for
\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
. Further, by Theorem
1.8
we can lower bound

\log Z_{n}(\beta\log n)\geq\log\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=n\}}]=\beta n\log{n}+\log{\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\geq n)}\geq(1+o(1))(\beta-\tfrac{1}{3})n\log{n}.

(7.2)

This proves the lower bound
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3},
and completes the proof of the lower bound.

For the upper bound, we estimate

Z_{n}(\beta\log n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k)\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}k}\leq n\max_{k\in[n]}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\geq k)\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}k}.

(7.3)

Using Theorem
1.8
, we get that for
\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
the maximum is achieved for
k=k_{n}=o(n)
, while for
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
the maximum is achieved for
k=n
. To see this in more detail, note that when
k=k_{n}=o(n)
, we can simply bound
\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\geq k_{n})\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{n,{\scriptscriptstyle V}}k_{n}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta k_{n}\log{n}}
, while when
k_{n}=cn
for some constant
c\in(0,1]
we can use Theorem
1.8
.

We next proceed to prove the second part of the corollary in two cases:

Case I:

\beta<\tfrac{1}{3}
. By (
7.1
)
and using the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}
, we get

\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}\left(\frac{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{n}\geq\varepsilon\right)=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta\log n)}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\geq\varepsilon n\}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\log{n}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta\log n)}\sum_{k\geq\varepsilon n}\mathrm{e}^{\beta k\log{n}}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k).

(7.4)

Using Theorem
1.8
, we can bound

\sum_{k\geq\varepsilon n}\mathrm{e}^{\beta k\log{n}}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k)\leq n\max_{k\geq\varepsilon n}\exp{\left(\beta k\log{n}-\tfrac{1}{3}k\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k)+Ck\right)}.

Since
\beta<\tfrac{1}{3}
, the maximum is attained at
\varepsilon n
and therefore, using (
7.1
), we get that (
7.4
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}\left(\frac{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{n}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{o(n\log{n})}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\varepsilon n\log{n}-\frac{\varepsilon n\log{n}}{3}+Cn},

(7.5)

for some constant
C>0
. Since
\beta<\tfrac{1}{3}
, the right-hand side tends to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Case II:

\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
. We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}\left(\frac{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{n}<1-\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta\log n)}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}<(1-\varepsilon)n\}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\log{n}V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)

(7.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\beta\log n)}\sum_{q<(1-\varepsilon)n}\mathrm{e}^{\beta k\log{n}}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k).

Observe that

\sum_{q<(1-\varepsilon)n}\mathrm{e}^{\beta k\log{n}}\mathbb{P}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=k)\leq n\max_{k<(1-\varepsilon)n}\exp{\left(\beta k\log{n}-\tfrac{1}{3}k\log(\tfrac{1}{3}k)+Ck\right)}.

(7.7)

Since
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
, the maximum is attained at
k=(1-\varepsilon)n
. Using (
7.2
) we get

Z_{n}(\beta\log n)\geq\exp((\beta-\tfrac{1}{3})n\log{n}(1+o(1))),

(7.8)

and therefore we can upper bound (
7.6
) by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\beta\log{n}}\left(\frac{V_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{n}<1-\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-(\beta-\tfrac{1}{3})n\log{n}(1+o(1))+\beta(1-\varepsilon)n\log{n}-(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n}{3}\log{n}+Cn\right)}

(7.9)

\displaystyle=\exp{\left(-(\beta-\tfrac{1}{3})o(1)n\log{n}-\beta\varepsilon n\log{n}+\varepsilon\frac{n}{3}\log{n}+Cn\right)}

for some constant
C>0
. Since
\beta>\tfrac{1}{3}
, the right-hand side tends to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
∎
