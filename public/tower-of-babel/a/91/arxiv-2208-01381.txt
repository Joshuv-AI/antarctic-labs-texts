Classical flows of vector fields with exponential or sub-exponential summability Thanks: Corresponding author: S. Nicolussi Golo.
Luigi Ambrosio, Sebastiano Nicolussi Golo, Francesco Serra Cassano

Abstract
. We show that vector fields b b whose spatial derivative D x ​ b D_{x}b satisfies a Orlicz summability condition have a spatially continuous representative and are well-posed. For the case of sub-exponential summability, their flows satisfy a Lusin (N) condition in a quantitative form, too. Furthermore, we prove that if D x ​ b D_{x}b satisfies a suitable exponential summability condition then the flow associated to b b has Sobolev regularity, without assuming boundedness of div x ​ b {\rm div}_{x}b . We then apply these results to the representation and Sobolev regularity of weak solutions of the Cauchy problem for the transport and continuity equations.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we are concerned with the study of the existence and uniqueness of classical solutions of the Cauchy problem for
the ODE system

(1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{\,=\,}l}\dot{\gamma}(t)&b(t,\gamma(t))\\
\gamma(s)&x\,,\end{array}\right.

with
x\in\Omega
, an open domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
s\in I
, an open interval in
\mathbb{R}
, and
b:\,I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a
continuous, possibly non-autonomous vector field
Even though we will mostly deal with the case when
b
is continuous,
we will point out which proofs can easily be adapted to the case when
b
is only measurable with respect to
t
. If solutions to (
1
) exist and are unique for every
s
and
x
, we say that the vector field
b
is
well-posed
in
I\times\Omega
(or in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
, see Definition
3.1
for a more precise statement). For every well posed vector field
b:\,I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have a
flow
, that is, a map

X:I\times I\times\Omega\to\Omega
, defined as
X(t,s,x):=\gamma(t)
where
\gamma
is the unique absolutely continuous solution of (
1
).
More precisely, for each
t,\,s\in I
we denote by
\Omega_{(t,s)}\subset\Omega
the open set of all
x\in\Omega
such that the path starting at
x
at time
s
can be extended until time
t
(see Section
3.2
and Remark
3.3
).
Then
X(t,s,\cdot)
is a well defined homeomorphism
\Omega_{(t,s)}\to\Omega_{(s,t)}
(see Remark
3.3
).

Di Perna–Lions
[
17
]
carried out a pioneering and far-reaching theory by introducing a generalized notion of flow for vector fields
b\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,T);W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}))
with important applications to the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for the Cauchy problem of the
transport equation
associated to a weakly differentiable vector field
b
, that is,

(2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+b\cdot D_{x}u=0&\text{ in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$ }\\
u(0,\cdot)=\bar{u}\,.\end{cases}

The theory was later remarkably extended by the first author
[
2
]
to vector fields
b(t,\cdot)
with
BV
regularity.
In these works, the regularity of
b
is paired with the boundedness of its spatial divergence, that is

(3)

{\rm div}_{x}b\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})),

which ensures the existence and uniqueness of the generalized flow of
b
.
If (
3
) does not hold, then uniqueness of the flow may fail,
as it was already shown in
[
17
, Section IV.1]
.
The existence and uniqueness of a generalized flow associated to a weakly regular vector field
b
has been the object of an intensive study with applications to the Cauchy problem for the transport equation as well as for the
continuity equation
associated to
b
, that is,

(4)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}_{x}(b\rho)=0&\text{ in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$ }\\
\rho(0,\cdot)=\bar{\rho}&\text{ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$\ . }\end{cases}

Existence, uniqueness and regularity of solutions of these three problems (
1
), (
2
) and (
4
) are connected with each other.
In particular, the existence of a unique flow
X
with enough regularity implies existence and uniqueness of solutions to both the transport equation and the continuity equation. A fairly complete account of the development in this topic can be found in
[
5
]
and references therein.
A sample of the literature on this subject is
[
4
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
13
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
27
,
29
,
28
]
.

Our contribution focuses on two problems.
First,
we want to weaken the boundedness assumption
on the divergence (
3
).
We will show in Theorem
A
that sub-exponential summability of
\|D_{x}b\|
guarantees the existence of a unique
classical
flow (in the Di Perna–Lions–Ambrosio theory, flows have a weaker definition).

Second, we want to find conditions on
b
for the flow to have Sobolev regularity,
instead of just
L^{p}
integrability.
It is well-known that high
L^{p}
integrability of matrix Jacobian
D_{x}b
, even coupled with (
3
), is not enough in order to provide Sobolev regularity of the flow
X
(see, for instance,
[
26
]
).
A strategy used in the recent papers
[
13
,
8
]
was to strengthen the hypotheses by requiring exponential summability of
\|D_{x}b\|
.
We refer in particular to the recent paper
[
8
]
, where it has been shown that that
b
has a unique flow with Sobolev regularity under the condition

(5)

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\exp(\beta\|D_{x}b(t,x)\|)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm div}_{x}b\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}),

for some
\beta>0
, where
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is the
n
-dimensional torus.
We prove analogous results without conditions on the divergence of
b
in Theorem
D
, see also Remark
6.3
.

Our results are of three types.
We first provide integral conditions of sub-exponential type on
Db
that ensure well-posedness.
Then, we study the Sobolev regularity of the homeomorphisms
X(t,s,\cdot)
.
Finally, we apply these results to both the transport equation and the continuity equation.

1.1.
Well-posedness

Let us focus, first, on the well-posedness. If
\left\|D_{x}b\right\|
satisfies an
exponential summability
,
that is, conditions of the form

(6)

\int_{I}\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\beta\|D_{x}b(t,x)\|\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t<+\infty

for some
\beta>\,0
, then it is well-known that
b
is well-posed.
Indeed, in this case, the vector field
b(t,\cdot)
satisfies a so-called Log-Lipschitz condition;
see, for instance,
[
8
,
32
]
.
However, reformulating the condition of exponential summability
in a Orlicz-like form, we extend the result to some sub-exponential cases.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Theta:[0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
be a non decreasing locally Lipschitz function.
Assume that

(
A
.I)

if
n>\,1
, there exists
\alpha\in(1,\frac{n}{n-1})
such that
\Theta^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}
is convex,
while, if
n=1
,
\Theta
is convex;

(
A
.II)

there exists
C_{\Theta}\geq 1
such that
\Theta:\,[C_{\Theta},+\infty)\to[\Theta(C_{\theta}),+\infty)
is bijective and

(7)

\Theta(s_{1})\Theta(s_{2})\leq\Theta(C_{\Theta}\,s_{1}\,s_{2})\text{ for all }s_{1},s_{2}\geq C_{\Theta}\,;

(
A
.III)

\displaystyle{\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\Theta^{\prime}(s)}{s\Theta(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s=+\infty}
.

Let
b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and assume that
for every
o\in\Omega
there exist
c>0
,
R>0
such that
B(o,2R)\subset\Omega
and
the function

(8)

t\mapsto\psi(t):=\,\int_{B(o,2R)}\Theta(c\|D_{x}b(t,z)\|)\,\mathrm{d}z

belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
.
Then
b(t,\cdot):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a continuous representative

\tilde{b}(t,\cdot)
for a.e.
t\in I
and
\tilde{b}
is well-posed in
I\times\Omega
.
Moreover, if there exists
m\in L^{1}(I)
such that

(9)

|b(t,x)|\leq\,m(t)\quad\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }t\in I,\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }x\in B(o,R)\,,

then
\tilde{b}
is also well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
.

Notice that a byproduct of the proof of Theorem
A

is that the Sobolev-Orlicz space
W^{1}L_{\Theta}(\Omega)
embeds in
C^{0}(\Omega)
, with modulus of continuity that depends only on
\Theta
.
See
[
7
, Section 2.6]
for the definition of
W^{1}L_{\Theta}(\Omega)
.

The proof is inspired from
[
32
]
.
In fact, we shall prove that a continuous representative
\tilde{b}(t,\cdot)
of
b(t,\cdot)
satisfies the Osgood’s criterion (see
[
23
, Chap. III, Corollary 6.2]
or Proposition
3.2
below). The proof of this result is given in Section
4
.

We note in Proposition
4.4
that if an increasing function
\Theta

satisfies condition
(.III)
of Theorem
A
,
then it does not have polynomial growth.
Examples of functions
\Theta
satisfying the properties
(.I)
-
(.III)

are of the form

(10)

\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)=\exp\left(\frac{s}{\log(s)\log\log(s)\dots(\underbrace{\log\dots\log}_{\text{ $k$-times}}s)^{\beta}}\right)\quad\text{ for }s\geq\bar{s},

with
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)=\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(\bar{s})
for
s<\bar{s}
, where
\bar{s}
is large enough,
k\geq\,1
is an integer and
0\leq\beta\leq 1
,
see Proposition
4.6
. Notice that the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
as
s\to\infty
is almost sharp in order that assumption
(.III)
holds, see Remark
4.7
.
If
\mathscr{E}_{k,1}(c\left\|D_{x}b\right\|)\in\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)

for some
c>0
, we say that
\left\|D_{x}b\right\|
satisfies a
subexponential summability
of order
k
.
Therefore, Theorem
A
shows that, if
\left\|D_{x}b\right\|
has
subexponential summability
, then
b
has a
classical
unique flow.
However, we stress that, under the hypothesis of Theorem
A
,
\left\|D_{x}b\right\|
does not need not be in
L^{p}_{\rm loc}(I\times\Omega)
for each
p>1
(see Remark
4.5
).

1.2.
Regularity

Moving on to the regularity of the flow, we can prove that,
if
D_{x}b
satisfies a subexponential summability of order
1
, the associated flow
X(t,s,\cdot)

satisfies a weak regularity property, namely it maps the Lebesgue measure into absolutely continuous measures.
Notice that, in this case,
\left\|D_{x}b(t,\cdot)\right\|
does belong to
L^{p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
for every
p>1
, for almost every
t\in I
.
A quantitative version,
that we obtain adapting
[
11
]
, is the following.

Theorem B
.

Let
b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.
Suppose that

(11)

\frac{|b(t,x)|}{1+|x|\,\log^{+}|x|}\in L^{1}(I;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\,,

and

(12)

\int_{I}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(\frac{\|D_{x}b\|}{\log^{+}\|D_{x}b\|}\right)(t,x)\,d\gamma_{n}(x)dt<\,+\infty,

where
\gamma_{n}
is the Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
namely

\gamma_{n}:=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{L}^{n}\,

with
\mathcal{L}^{n}
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then the space continuous representative
\tilde{b}
, granted by the Sobolev embedding, is well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
and the associated flow
X
of
\tilde{b}
is globally defined, that is,

X:\overline{I}\times\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, for every
t,\,s\in\overline{I}
, the image measure
X(t,s,\cdot)_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}

and there exists a positive constant
\alpha_{0}(t,s)>0
such that

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}}(X(t,s,\cdot)_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n})\in\mathrm{L}^{\Phi_{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for each
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}(s,t)
, where
\mathrm{L}^{\Phi_{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the Orlicz space with

\Phi_{\alpha}:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty),\qquad\Phi_{\alpha}(w):=w\exp((\log^{+}w)^{\alpha}),

and
\alpha_{0}:I\times I\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous with
\alpha_{0}(t,t)=1
for every
t\in I
.

See Section
5
for the proof.
By trivial considerations, in the one-dimensional case, we can improve Theorem
B
to absolutely continuity of the flow.

Theorem C
.

If
n=1
and
b
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
B
, then, for every
t,\,s\in I
,
the map
X(t,s,\cdot)
is an absolutely continuous homeomorphism between intervals of
\mathbb{R}
.

Sobolev regularity stated in Theorem
C
is sharp, as we show in Example
8.2
.
We don’t know whether Theorem
C
can be extended to
the case of sub-exponential summability of order
k>1
.

In higher dimensions, for subexponential summability,
we have a partial negative result with an example in Section
8.2
,
or the example constructed in
[
8
]
, see also Remark
7.2
:

there are vector fields satisfying a subexponential summability of order
1
whose flow is not in
W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
for
any
p>n
.
However, it remains open whether vector fields satisfying a subexponential summability of order
1
can fail to have the flow in
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.

On the other hand, in higher dimensions, for exponential summability we have a positive result:

Theorem D
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded open sets and let
b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be bounded.
Assume that for some
p>2n
the vector field
b
satisfies the global geometric condition

(13)

\Lambda_{p}:=\int_{\Omega}\int_{I}\max\left\{\ell^{\frac{n}{n-p}},\frac{({\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega))^{\frac{n}{n-p}}}{(\sup|b|)^{\frac{n}{n-p}}}\right\}\exp\left(\frac{\ell p^{2}}{p-n}\|D_{x}b(s,x)\|\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty,

with
\ell
equal to the length of
I
.
Then the space-continuous representative
\tilde{b}
is well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega

thanks to Theorem
A
and,
in addition, for every
t\in{I}
and for almost every
s\in I
, one has

(14)

\text{for a.e.\penalty\ }s\in I,\quad X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}(\Omega_{(t,s)};\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad X(s,t,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}(\Omega_{(s,t)};\mathbb{R}^{n}),

with

(15)

\int_{I}\int_{\Omega_{(t,s)}}\|D_{x}X(t,s,x)\|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\ell^{\frac{n}{p-n}}\Lambda_{p}.

With regards to (
14
),
a lower Sobolev regularity for the flow can be proved for all pairs of times
s,t\in I
, see Corollary
6.9
.
For
b\in C^{0}(I;C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, we have a more detailed statement in Theorem
6.1
.
We may also consider the case when
b:I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the support of
b(t,\cdot)
is contained in a
compact set independent of
t
. See also Remarks
6.3
and
6.2
.

Theorem E
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and let
b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}))

be a bounded vector field. Assume that there exist a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p>n
such that

(16)

\mathtt{spt}(b(t,\cdot))\subset\Omega\quad\text{for each $t\in I$}

and (
6
) holds with
\beta=\,{\ell p^{2}}/{(p-n)}
and
\ell
equal to the length of
I
.

Then the space-continuous representative
\tilde{b}
, understood as vector field in
I\times\Omega
, is well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega

thanks to Theorem
A
,
\Omega_{(t,s)}=\,\Omega
and for every
t,\,s\in\overline{I}
one has

(17)

\begin{split}&X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{with}\\
&\int_{\Omega}\|D_{x}X(t,s,x)\|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{I}\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\frac{\ell p^{2}}{p-n}\|D_{x}b(v,y)\|\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}v.\end{split}

Notice also that, in the previous theorem, we obviously have that the flow map
X:\,\overline{I}\times\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
b
is
identically equal to the identity on
\overline{I}\times\overline{I}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega})
.

Remark 1.1
.

(1) Since
{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
is bounded,
(
13
) can be stated in the equivalent form

\int_{\Omega}\int_{I}({\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega))^{n/(n-p)}\exp\left(\frac{\ell p^{2}}{p-n}\|D_{x}b(s,x)\|\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

We used that specific form for the purpose of the estimate (
14
).

(2) If
\alpha=n/(p-n)
is smaller than 1 (i.e.,
p>2n
) and
\partial\Omega
is regular, then the geometric condition (
13
), thanks to the
Hölder inequality, is implied by the simpler condition

(18)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{I}\exp\left(c\|D_{x}b(s,x)\|\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty

provided
c>(1/\alpha)^{\prime}p^{2}/(p-n)
, where
(1/\alpha)^{\prime}=(1-\alpha)^{-1}
is the dual exponent.

We also point out that the Sobolev exponent
p
, related to the constant in the exponential
integrability condition in (
13
), may not be sharp.
In other words, we can have
\Lambda_{p}=\infty
but
X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}(\Omega_{(t,s)};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see Example
8.3
.

Several regularity properties of
X
follow from Theorem
D
, see Corollaries
6.7
,
6.8
and
6.10
. In particular we show that, as in Theorem
B
,
X(t,s,\cdot)_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n}\ll\mathcal{L}^{n}
, for each

t,\,s\in\overline{I}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Suppose that
b\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n}))

has compact support and (
18
) holds for some
c>0
.
For every
p>2n
, we get (
17
) on subintervals of
I
of length
\ell
such that

\frac{\ell p^{2}}{p-n}=c
.
Since
\frac{p^{2}}{p-n}>4n
for
p>2n
, then
\ell<\frac{c}{4n}
.

1.3.
Transport and continuity equations

Finally, we apply Theorem
B
and Theorem
D
to the transport equation (
2
) and the continuity equation (
4
), respectively. By standard methods, we provide existence, uniqueness, representation and regularity of solutions.

In
[
12
, Theorem 1]
, uniqueness of weak solutions
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))

has been proved, provided that the spatial derivatives of
b
satisfies a sub-exponential summability and
\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Our next result provides even in this context the classical representation of this unique solution in terms of the flow map,
granted by Theorem
B
.

Theorem F

(Representation of the solutions for the transport equation)
.

Let
b
be a vector field as in Theorem
B
, and let
X
be the flow associated to the continuous
representative
b
.
Then, for each
\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for each
t\in[0,T]
, the function

(19)

v(t,x):=\,\bar{u}\left(X(t,0,\cdot)^{-1}(x)\right)\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

is the unique weak solution in
L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
of the Cauchy problem for the transport equation (
2
),
understood in the sense of distributions.

By means of the theory of maps with finite distortion, see for instance
[
25
]
,
we can also show Sobolev regularity of the solution
v
to the transport equation,
see Corollary
7.1
.

By applying the well-posedness and representation results proved in
[
3
,
14
]
(see Theorem
7.4
and Remark
7.5
), we make more explicit the representation of the weak solutions of (
4
), under the assumptions of
Theorem
B
and Theorem
E
. It is useful to deal with the case where
\rho(t,\cdot)
belongs to the space of signed Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite total variation, which we will denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Theorem G

(Representation of the solutions for the continuity equation)
.

Let
I=(0,T)
, let
b:I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector field with
b(t,\cdot)
continuous for a.e.
t\in I
.

(i)

Suppose that
b
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
B
and let
X
be the flow associated to
b
. Then, for each signed measure
\bar{\rho}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(20)

\rho_{t}=\,\rho(t,\cdot)=\,X(t,0,\cdot)_{\#}\bar{\rho}\quad t\in[0,T]

is the unique weak solution for the Cauchy problem (
4
) in
L^{\infty}((0,T);\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

Moreover, if
\bar{\rho}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\rho_{t}
can be represented as

(21)

\rho_{t}:=(\bar{\rho}J_{X,t})\circ X(0,t,\cdot)\,,

where
J_{X,t}(y)=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}X(t,0,\cdot)_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}}(X(t,0,y))
.
In particular we have
\rho\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

(ii)

If
b
satisfies the stronger hypotheses of Theorem
E
and
\bar{\rho}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
the unique weak solution for the Cauchy problem (
4
) can also be represented as

(22)

\rho_{t}=\,\frac{\bar{\rho}}{J_{X(t,0,\cdot)}}\circ X(0,t,\cdot)\,,

where
J_{X(t,0,\cdot)}
denotes the Jacobian of homeomorphism
X(t,0,\cdot):\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see (
24
)).

Notice that existence and uniqueness of distributional solutions of continuity equation (
4
) has been obtained in
[
15
]
by weakening assumption (
3
).
More precisely, the authors showed that if
\bar{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

(23)

{\rm div}_{x}b\in(BMO\cap L^{1})(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

then there exists a unique distributional solution
\rho\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the Cauchy problem (
4
)
However, we point out that condition (
6
) does not imply (

), see Example

2.1.
Weak derivatives of homeomorphisms

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and
\Psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
D\Psi
the weak differential of
\Psi
.
When a point-wise analysis is needed, we assume that
D\Psi(x)
is equal to the classical differential of
\Psi
at every
x\in\Omega
where
\Psi
is differentiable.
1
1

1

Notice that
the distributional derivative of a Sobolev function
\Psi
agrees with the classical derivative at a.e.
x
where
\Psi
is differentiable.
If the reader needs an argument, let us point out that the approximate differential of
\Psi
agrees with the distributional derivative almost everywhere by
[
18
, Theorem 6.1.4, page 233]
, and that the classical derivative is equal to the approximate differential wherever it exists.

The
Jacobian
of
\Psi
is

(24)

J_{\Psi}(x)=\det(D\Psi(x))\,.

A map
\Psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies the
Lusin (N) condition
if for all
E\subset\Omega
the vanishing of
\mathcal{L}^{n}(E)
implies
\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Psi(E))=0
.
An
embedding
is a homeomorphism onto its image.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
25
, Lemma A.29]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
\Psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an embedding that is differentiable at
x
.
The map
\Psi^{-1}
is differentiable at
\Psi(x)
if and only if
\det(D\Psi(x))\neq 0
.
In this case, we have

D(\Psi^{-1})(\Psi(x))=(D\Psi(x))^{-1}.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
25
, Theorem A.35]
: Area Formula)
.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open,
\Psi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,+\infty]
is a Borel function, then

(25)

\int_{\Omega}\eta(\Psi(x))|J_{\Psi}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(y)N(\Psi,\Omega,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
N(\Psi,\Omega,y)=\#(\Psi^{-1}(y)\cap\Omega)
is the cardinality of the set
\Psi^{-1}(y)\cap\Omega
.
If
\Psi
satisfies also the Lusin (N) condition then equality holds in (
25
).

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that the Lusin (N) condition for a homeomorphism
\Psi:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is equivalent to assume that the push-forward measure
(\Psi^{-1})_{\#}(\mathcal{L}^{n}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Omega^{\prime})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}
.
An abstract area-type formula with respect to a general measure
\mu
is also introduced in Lemma
7.6
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
25
, Theorem 4.2 and Theorem 6.2.1]
)
.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open,
\Psi\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
p>n
, then the continuous
representative
\tilde{\Psi}
is differentiable at
a.e.
x\in\Omega
and

2.2.
Mappings of finite distortion

We denote by
\|M\|
the operator norm of a linear map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
m\times n
-matrix, that is,

\|M\|:=\sup\left\{|Mv|:v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ |v|=1\right\},

where
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean norm. With this choice of the norm,
one can easily show the following inequalities (the first one is called
Hadamard’s inequality
):

(26)

|\det M|\leq\Pi_{i=1}^{n}|Me_{i}|\leq\|M\|^{n}.

Definition 2.5
.

A
map of finite distortion
on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a function
\Psi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that there exists
K:\Omega\to[1,+\infty)
with

(27)

\|D\Psi(x)\|^{n}\leq K(x)J_{\Psi}(x)\qquad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ }x\in\Omega.

For
q\geq 1
, the
q
-distortion function
of a map of finite distortion
\Psi
is

K_{q}^{\Psi}(x):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\|D\Psi(x)\|^{q}}{J_{\Psi}(x)}}&\text{ if }J_{\Psi}(x)\neq 0,\\
1&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Our main reference on maps of finite distortion is
[
25
]
.
Notice that
K_{n}^{\Psi}
is the optimal distortion function for the inequality (
27
) to hold.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and
\Psi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an embedding with
J_{\Psi}>0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
,
then
\Psi
has finite distortion.

Proof.

The function
K^{\Psi}_{n}
is finite almost everywhere and (
27
) holds whenever
J_{\Psi}(x)>0
.
Since
J_{\Psi}>0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
, then
\Psi
has finite distortion.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Psi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
be a homeomorphism between open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

q>0
and
p>n
.
Suppose that

\Psi\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\Psi^{-1}\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Psi(\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{and }J_{\Psi}(x)>0\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }x\in\Omega.

Then
\Psi
is a homeomorphism of finite distortion and

K^{\Psi}_{q}\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)

with
r=\left(\frac{q}{p}+\frac{n}{p-n}\right)^{-1}
, which may be smaller than 1.

Proof.

The map
\Psi
has finite distortion by Lemma
2.6
.
Let
r>0
and
U\Subset\Omega
.
By Hölder inequality, we have

(28)

\int_{U}|K^{\Psi}_{q}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\int_{U}\|D\Psi\|^{rq\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\alpha}\left(\int_{U}|J\Psi|^{-r\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\beta}

for
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
with
\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=1
.
If
rq\alpha=p
, then the first term of the right-hand side is finite.

By Lemma
2.4
, both
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
are differentiable almost everywhere.
Therefore, using Lemma
2.1
, it follows that, for almost every
x\in U
,

|J\Psi(x)|^{-1}=|J\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))|.

Using the area inequality (
25
), we have for all
\gamma>1

\displaystyle\int_{U}|J\Psi(x)|^{-\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{U}|J\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))|^{\gamma+1}|J\Psi(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Psi(U)}|J\Psi^{-1}(y)|^{\gamma+1}\,dy\leq\int_{\Psi(U)}\|D\Psi^{-1}(y)\|^{n(\gamma+1)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where we used (
26
) in the last step.
Now, if
\gamma=r\beta
, then

\int_{U}|J\Psi|^{-r\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Psi(U)}\|D\Psi^{-1}(y)\|^{n(r\beta+1)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which is finite when
n(r\beta+1)=p
.

Finally, solving the three equations
\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}=1
,
rq\alpha=p
and
n(r\beta+1)=p
in
\alpha,\beta
and
r
, we get

r=\left(\frac{q}{p}+\frac{n}{p-n}\right)^{-1}

and
\alpha=\frac{p}{rq}=1+\frac{pn}{q(p-n)}>1
, by the assumption
p>n
.
Therefore, we get from (
28
) that
\int_{U}|K_{\Psi}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.
∎

Proposition 2.8

(
[
25
, Theorem 5.13 and equation (5.18)]
: Regularity of composition)
.

Let
\Psi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
be a homeomorphism between open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and let
1\leq\,p<\,q<\,\infty
.
Define the composition operator
T_{\Psi}u=u\circ\Psi
for

.
Suppose that
\Psi
has finite distortion and

(29)

K^{\Psi}_{q}\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{p}{q-p}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega_{1}).

Then

T_{\Psi}
is continuous from
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,q}(\Omega_{2})
to
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,p}(\Omega_{1})

for every
1\leq q\leq\infty

where the operator norm on

3.1.
Well-posedness of vector fields

We denote by
B(x,r)
the Euclidean ball of radius
r
and center
x
.
Recall that a vector field
b(t,x)
is said to be
autonomous
if it does not depend on
t
,

non-autonomous
if it may depend on
t
.

Definition 3.1

(Well posedness)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open,
I\subset\mathbb{R}
an open interval and
b:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a function.
We say that
b
is
well-posed in
I\times\Omega
if for every
(s,x)\in I\times\Omega
there exist
\epsilon>0
and, in the interval

(s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon)\subset I
, a unique absolutely continuous solution

\gamma:\,(s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon)\to\Omega
of (
1
), that is,
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\,b(t,\gamma(t))
for
a.e.
t\in(s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon)
and
\gamma(s)=x
.
Analogously, we say that
b
is
well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
if for every
(s,x)\in\overline{I}\times\Omega

there exist
\epsilon>0
and, in the interval
I\cap(s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon)
, a unique absolutely continuous solution

\gamma:\,I\cap(s-\epsilon,s+\epsilon)\to\Omega
of (
1
).

The following classical result provides a sufficient condition for well-posedness, in local and global form
with respect to the time variable.
The proof is rather classical and well known, see for instance
[
19
,
23
]
.

Proposition 3.2

(Well-posedness with Osgood Condition)
.

Let
\omega:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be non-decreasing with
\omega(0)=0
,
\omega(t)>0
for
t>0
and

(30)

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\omega(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t=+\infty.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval,
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open set.
Let
b:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function such that:

(a)

there exists a positive function
\phi\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that

(31)

|b(t,x)-b(t,y)|\leq\phi(t)\omega(|x-y|)

for all
t\in I
and
x,\,y\in\Omega
;

(b)

for every
x\in\Omega
, the function
t\mapsto b(t,x)
is measurable;

(c)

there exists
m\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that
|b(t,x)|\leq m(t)
for all
(t,x)\in I\times\Omega
.

Then the following hold:

(i)

The vector field
b
is well posed in
I\times\Omega
.
For
(s,x)\in I\times\Omega
, denote by
\gamma_{(s,x)}
the maximal solution of (
1
) with
\gamma_{(s,x)}(s)=x
and by
I_{(s,x)}
its maximal domain of existence.
Define also
\alpha(s,t)=\inf I_{(s,x)}
and
\beta(s,t)=\sup I_{(s,x)}
.

(ii)

If
\beta(s,x)\neq\sup I
, then
\lim_{t\to\beta(s,x)}\gamma_{(s,x)}
exists and belongs to
\partial\Omega
.
Similarly for
\alpha(s,x)\neq\inf I
.

(iii)

If
(s_{j},x_{j})\in I\times\Omega
is a sequence converging to
(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})\in I\times\Omega
,
with
\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{(s_{j},x_{j})}:I_{(s_{j},x_{j})}\to\Omega
the corresponding maximal solutions,
j\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,
and if
s_{\infty}\in J\Subset I_{(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})}
, then
J\subset I_{(s_{j},x_{j})}
for
j
large enough and
\gamma_{j}
converge to
\gamma_{\infty}
uniformly on
J
.

In particular,
\alpha
is upper semicontinuous and
\beta
is lower semicontinuous.

(iv)

If
m\in L^{1}(I)
, then
\gamma_{(s,x)}
is well defined also for
(s,x)\in\partial I\times\Omega
and the limits

\lim_{t\to\beta(s,x)}\gamma_{(s,x)}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\alpha(s,x)}\gamma_{(s,x)}(t)

exist in
\bar{\Omega}
.
In particular,
b
is well posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
.

3.2.
The flow of a well-posed vector field

Fix an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and a well-posed vector field
b:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For every
(s,x)\in I\times\Omega
, let
I_{(s,x)}=\,(\alpha(s,x),\beta(s,x))\subset I
be the maximal open interval of existence of a solution
\gamma_{(s,x)}:I_{(s,x)}\to\Omega
to the Cauchy problem (
1
) with initial datum
\gamma_{(s,x)}(s)=x
.
Define

\mathscr{D}_{b}:=\left\{(t,s,x):(s,x)\in I\times\Omega,\ t\in I_{(s,x)}\right\}\subset I\times I\times\Omega,

and the
flow

X:\mathscr{D}_{b}\to\Omega
of
b
as
X(t,s,x)=\gamma_{(s,x)}(t)
.
For every
(t,s)\in I\times I
, we define
\Omega_{(t,s)}=\{x\in\Omega:(t,s,x)\in\mathscr{D}_{b}\}=\{x\in\Omega:t\in I_{(s,x)}\}
(which is possibly empty) and
X_{(t,s)}:\Omega_{(t,s)}\to\Omega
as
X_{(t,s)}(x)=X(t,s,x)
.

An analogous notation can be used for the case of vector fields well posed in
\overline{I}\times\Omega
, just allowing
s
and

t
to belong also to
\partial I
.

We recall several well-known facts about flows of vector fields,
referring to fix the ideas to the weaker case of well posedness in

I\times\Omega
. See for instance
[
23
]
for reference.

Remark 3.3
.

If
b:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
3.2
,
then the sets
\mathscr{D}_{b}
and
\Omega_{(t,s)}
are open
and
X
is continuous,
thanks to Proposition
3.2
(iii)
.

Uniqueness gives the semigroup-type property

(32)

X(t_{3},t_{2},X(t_{2},t_{1},x))=X(t_{3},t_{1},x)\ ,

whenever the expressions make sense.
Moreover, the map
X_{(t,s)}
is a homeomorphism
\Omega_{(t,s)}\to\Omega_{(s,t)}
with inverse
X_{(s,t)}
.
Finally, if
k\geq 1
and
b\in C^{k}(I\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
X\in C^{k+1}(\mathscr{D}_{b};\Omega)
.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
23
, Theorem 3.1, Chap. V]
)
.

Any
b\in C^{0}(I;C^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is well posed in
I\times\Omega
and the homeomorphisms

X_{(t,s)}:\Omega_{(t,s)}\to\Omega_{(s,t)}
are of class
C^{1}
.

Fix
(s,x)\in I\times\Omega
and set the following functions defined on
I_{(s,x)}

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle:=\,D_{x}X(t,s,x),

\displaystyle B(t)

\displaystyle:=\,(D_{x}b)(t,X(t,s,x)),

\displaystyle J(t)

\displaystyle:=\det(D_{x}X(t,s,x)),

\displaystyle\beta(t)

\displaystyle:={\rm div}_{x}(b)(t,X(t,s,x))=\mathrm{trace}(B(t)).

Then
y
and
J
are the unique
C^{1}
solutions of the following initial value problems:

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(t)&=B(t)y(t),\\
y(s)&=\mathrm{Id},\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}\dot{J}(t)&=\beta(t)J(t),\\
J(s)&=1.\end{cases}

When considering smooth approximations of vector fields,
we need to control the convergence of the corresponding flows, as in Lemma
3.5
below.
The following results are fairly well-known, but in absence of a reference precisely adapted to our purposes
we provide sketch of proofs for the reader’s convenience.
An analogous statement holds
for vector fields well posed in
\overline{I}
, considering the additional
points
(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})\in\partial I\times\Omega
and the stronger convergence
in
L^{1}(I;C(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for any
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\{b_{h}\}_{h\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}}
be well posed vector fields in
I
and denote
by
I^{h}_{(s,x)}
their maximal existence times and by
X^{h}
their flows.
If
b_{h}\to b_{\infty}
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;C(\Omega^{\prime};\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for any
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega

and
(s_{h},x_{h})\to(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})\in I\times\Omega

as
h\to\infty
, then for any compact interval
J\subset I^{\infty}_{(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})}
one has

J\subset I^{h}_{(s_{h},x_{h})}\qquad\text{for $h$ large enough}

and
X^{h}(\cdot,s_{h},x_{h})
converge uniformly to
X^{\infty}(\cdot,s_{\infty},x_{\infty})
on
J
.

Proof.

Let us consider the compact curve
\Gamma=\{(v,X^{\infty}(v,s_{\infty},x_{\infty})):\ v\in J\}

and let
U
be the open set
J^{\prime}\times\Omega^{\prime}
, with
J\Subset J^{\prime}\Subset I
and
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
chosen in such a way
that
\Gamma\subset U
.
By assumption,

\int_{J^{\prime}}\sup_{\Omega^{\prime}}|b_{h}(t,\cdot)-b(t,\cdot)|\,\mathrm{d}t
is infinitesimal as
h\to\infty
. Let us consider the maximal solutions
\gamma_{h}(v)
for the ODE relative to

b_{h}
, starting from
x_{h}
at
s_{h}
and with graph remaining in
U
(in particular, restrictions
of
X^{h}(\cdot,s_{h},x_{h})
), and let
J_{h}\subset I^{h}_{(s_{h},x_{h})}
their maximal existence
intervals. Since the curves
\gamma_{h}
are
equicontinuous, it is clear that limit points of the graph of these curves
exist, and that any limit point is the graph of a solution
\gamma_{\infty}
to the ODE relative to
b_{\infty}
,
with
\gamma_{\infty}(s_{\infty})=x_{\infty}
, defined on an interval
J_{\infty}
and with the
property that, as
v
tends to one, if any, of the extreme points of
J_{\infty}
different from
\partial I
,
(v,\gamma_{\infty}(v))
tends to

\partial U
. Since
U\Subset I\times\Omega
, the well-posedness of
b_{\infty}
yields that the curve
\gamma_{\infty}
is
not only a restriction of the maximal curve
X^{\infty}(v,s_{\infty},x_{\infty})
,
v\in I^{\infty}_{(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})}
, but also contains the
curve
X^{\infty}(v,s_{\infty},x_{\infty})
,
v\in J
, since this restricted curve does not touch the boundary of
U
.
∎

The previous lemma grants, in particular, the lower semicontinuity of
(s,x)\mapsto{\rm length}(I_{(s,x)})
in
I\times\Omega
.
Moreover, a simple contradiction argument gives

(33)

\begin{split}\text{ for any compact interval $J\subset I^{\infty}_{(s_{\infty},x_{\infty})}$ and any open set $A\Subset\Omega$, one has}\\
\exists\,\bar{h}\text{ such that }J\subset I^{h}_{(s,x)}\qquad\text{for each $h>\bar{h}$ and $(s,x)\in J\times A$}\,;\end{split}

(34)

A\Subset\Omega^{\infty}_{(t,s)}\quad\text{implies}\quad A\Subset\Omega^{h}_{(t,s)}\quad\text{for $h$ large enough}

and the uniform convergence of
X_{h}(t,s,\cdot)
to
X_{\infty}(t,s,\cdot)
on

.

4.
Well-posedness with Orlicz condition

In this section we are going to prove Theorem
A
.
We fix some dimensional constants for

.
Let
\omega_{n}=|B(0,1)|
be the volume of the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma_{n-1}=n\omega_{n}
the perimeter of
B(0,1)
;
let
\tau_{n}=|B(0,1)\cap B(q,1)|
for any

is the constant from Lemma

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
b\in W^{1,1}(B(x,r);\mathbb{R}^{n})
and assume that
x
is a Lebesgue point of
b
. Then,
for some dimensional constant
C_{n}
, one has

(35)

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B(x,r)}|b(x)-b(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C_{n}\int_{B(x,r)}\frac{\|Db(y)\|}{|x-y|^{n-1}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Proof.

We assume for simplicity
n\geq 2
.
From the same argument of
[
18
, Lemma 1, Sec.4.5.2]
, based on a radial integration,
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
r>0
,
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and

f\in C^{1}(B(x,r))
we have

(36)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(s)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j}^{p}}{j^{2}x_{j}^{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j}^{p-2}}{j^{2}}

is finite if and only if
p\leq 2
.

4.1.
A class of subexponential summability types

Examples of functions
\Theta
satisfying the properties
listed in Theorem
A

are of the form
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)
as in (
10
), as we will show in this section.
It is clear that the subexponential summability
of type
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
implies the subexponential summability of type
\mathscr{E}_{k^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}
for all
\beta\leq\beta^{\prime}\leq 1
and
k^{\prime}\geq k
.

If
\left\|D_{x}b\right\|
satisfies an exponential summability, then it is well-known that
b
is well-posed. Indeed, in this case, the vector field
b
satisfies a so-called Log-Lipschitz condition (see, for instance,
[
8
,
32
]
).
Theorem
A
extends the well-posedness to subexponential summability order
k\geq 1
.
We will show in Section
8.2
that the upper bound on
\beta
for the subexponential summability order
1
is in fact necessary; see also
[
12
, Section 6]
,
[
11
, p. 1240]
.

Let us now show that
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
A
. Let us introduce some notation in order to better represent
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
. Let us denote by
E_{k}:\,\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the
k
th
-iterated exponential function
, that is, by induction on
k
,

E_{1}(s):=\exp(s),\quad E_{k+1}(s):=\exp\left(E_{k}(s)\right)\text{ if }s\in\mathbb{R},\,k\geq\,1.

Since
\lim_{s\to-\infty}E_{1}(s)=0
, notice that
\lim_{s\to-\infty}E_{k}(s)=E_{k-1}(0)=:s_{k}
for all
k>1
, and that
\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{k}=\infty
.
Denote by
L_{k}:E_{k}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
the
k
th
-iterated logarithm function
as the inverse of
E_{k}
, that is

L_{k}(s):=E_{k}^{-1}(s)\text{ if }s\in E_{k}(\mathbb{R})=(s_{k},+\infty)\,.

Define then
P_{k}(s):=\prod_{j=1}^{k}L_{j}(s)
, so that one can easily check that

L_{k+1}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{sP_{k}(s)},\text{ so that }P^{\prime}_{k}(s)=\frac{P_{k}(s)}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{P_{j}(s)}.

With this notation, we have

\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)=\exp\left(\frac{s}{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}^{\beta}(s)}\right).

A direct computation shows that

(52)

\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}^{\prime}=\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}H_{k,\beta}\quad\text{ where }\quad H_{k,\beta}=\frac{1}{P_{k-1}L_{k}^{\beta}}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{P_{j}}-\frac{\beta}{P_{k}}\right),

and that

(53)

H_{k,\beta}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{sP_{k-1}L_{k}^{\beta}}\bigg(-\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{P_{j}}+\frac{\beta}{P_{k}}\Big)\Big(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{P_{j}}-\frac{\beta}{P_{k}}\Big)+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{P_{j}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{P_{i}}+\frac{\beta}{P_{k}}\Big)\bigg).

Proposition 4.6
.

The function
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}:\,\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
A
for each integer
k\geq\,1
and
0\leq\,\beta\leq\,1
.

Proof.

Monotonicity of
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
:

From (
52
) it is evident that
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}^{\prime}
is positive for
s
large enough and thus
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}
is
strictly increasing in
[\alpha,+\infty)
for
\alpha
large enough.

Verification of
(.I)
:

If
\gamma>0
, then

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\,\mathrm{d}s^{2}}\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)^{\gamma}=\gamma\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)^{\gamma}\left(\gamma H_{k,\beta}^{2}+H_{k,\beta}^{\prime}\right).

Because of the presence of the factor
1/s
in (
53
), one can see that

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{H_{k,\beta}^{\prime}}{H_{k,\beta}^{2}}=0
and thus
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\,\mathrm{d}s^{2}}\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)^{\gamma}
is positive for
s

large enough.
Thus we obtain that
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)^{\gamma}
is convex for
s
large.

Verification of
(.II)
:

Firstly, notice that
\log(st)=\log(s)+\log(t)\leq\log(s)\log(t)
for all
s,t>e
.
Therefore, by induction on
k
, we have
L_{k}(st)\leq L_{k}(s)L_{k}(t)
for all
s,t>E_{n}(1)
.
Hence,
P_{k}(st)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}L_{j}(st)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}L_{j}(s)L_{j}(t)=P_{k}(s)P_{k}(t)
,
or all
s,t>E_{n}(1)
.

Secondly, condition (
7
), i.e.,
\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(ts)\leq\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(t)
for
s,t
large enough, is equivalent to

\frac{s}{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}}+\frac{t}{P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}\leq\frac{st}{P_{k-1}(st)L_{k}(st)^{\beta}},

that is

(54)

\frac{P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}{t}+\frac{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}}{s}\leq\frac{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}{P_{k-1}(st)L_{k}(st)^{\beta}}.

Thirdly, on the one hand we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}{t}=0
, and thus the left-hand side of (
54
) smaller than 1 for
s
and
t
large.
On the other hand, by the initial observation,

\frac{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}{P_{k-1}(st)L_{k}(st)^{\beta}}\geq\frac{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}{P_{k-1}(s)P_{k-1}(t)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}L_{k}(t)^{\beta}}=1.

So, inequality (
54
) holds true and so condition (
7
).

Verification of
(.III)
:

Notice that, by (
52
), for
s>E_{k}(1)
,

\frac{\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}^{\prime}(s)}{s\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)}\sim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{sP_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}}.

Thus, since
\int_{E_{k}(1)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{sP_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\infty
if and only if
\beta\leq 1
, we also have
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}^{\prime}(s)}{s\mathscr{E}_{k,\beta}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s=+\infty
if and only if
\beta\leq 1
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Observe that, if
(.III)
holds, then

(55)

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{P_{k-1}(s)L_{k}(s)^{1+\alpha}\Theta^{\prime}(s)}{\Theta(s)}=\infty\text{ for every }\alpha>0\text{ and }k\geq 1\,.

5.
Regularity of the flow with subexponential summability

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

and its consequence in dimension 1 as written in Theorem
C
.
Let us recall that, if
\Phi:\,[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is an increasing homeomorphism, so that
\Phi(0)=\,0
and

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\Phi(t)=+\infty
, the
Orlicz space

\mathrm{L}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the space of measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the
Luxembourg norm

\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\Phi}}:=\,\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Phi\left(\frac{f(x)}{\lambda}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\,1\right\}

is finite.
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
will denote, as usual, the space of measurable functions
f
such that
f\,\chi_{K}\in\mathrm{L}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for each compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If

(56)

\Phi(t)=\exp\left(\frac{t}{\log^{+}t}\right)-1,

then we will denote the obtained
\mathrm{L}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(respectively
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
) by
{\rm Exp}\bigl({L}/{\log L}\bigr)
(respectively
{\rm Exp}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bigl({L}/{\log L}\bigr)
). When changing the reference measure from the Lebegues measure to a Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will denote
\mathrm{L}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu)
(respectively
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu)
) the associated Orlicz space and, if
\Phi
is as in (
56
), by
{\rm Exp}_{\mu}\bigl({L}/{\log L}\bigr)
(respectively
{\rm Exp}_{\mu,\mathrm{loc}}\bigl({L}/{\log L}\bigr)
).

Proof of Theorem
B
.

We assume with no loss of generality that
b
coincides
with its space continuous representative.
It is easy to see that, from assumptions (
11
) and (
12
), it follows that, for a given
R>\,0
and

m(t):=\,(1+R\log^{+}R)\,\sup_{x\in B(0,R)}\frac{|b(t,x)|}{1+|x|\log^{+}|x|}\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }t\in I\,,

then

(57)

m\in L^{1}(I)\text{ and }|b(t,x)|\leq\,m(t)\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }t\in I,\text{ for each }x\in B(0,R)

and

(58)

\exp\left(\frac{\|D_{x}b\|}{\log^{+}\|D_{x}b\|}\right)\in L^{1}(I;L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))\,.

Thus, by (
57
) and (
58
), the vector field
b
is well-posed in
\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
by Theorem
A
and Proposition
4.6
. Moreover the flow
X
associated to

is globally defined thanks
to the growth condition (

), so that
X\in C^{0}(\overline{I}\times\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})

(see, for instance,
[
23
, Theorem 5.1, Chap. III]
).
In particular,

satisfies the assumptions of
[
11
, Main Theorem]
. Therefore
the push-forward measure
X(t,s,\cdot)_{\#}\gamma_{n}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Gaussian measure

\gamma_{n}
with density
w(t,s)
belonging to
\mathrm{L}^{\Phi_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma_{n})
for each
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}(s,t)
, where

\alpha_{0}(t,s)=\exp\left(-16\,e^{2}\int_{s}^{t}\|{\rm div}_{\gamma_{n}}b(v,\cdot)\|_{{\rm Exp}_{\gamma_{n}}\left(\frac{L}{\log L}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}v\right),

where

that is,

is the adjoint of the gradient operator with respect to the measure
\gamma_{n}
.
Notice that

is a continuous function with
\alpha_{0}(t,t)=1
for every

.

To complete the proof, it is enough to show that

(59)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}}(X(t,s,\cdot)_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n})(x)=D(x)w(t,s)(x)\quad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

where
D(x)=\exp\left({\frac{|X(s,t,x)|^{2}-|x|^{2}}{2}}\right)

and
{\frac{d}{d\mathcal{L}^{n}}}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}
.
Indeed, since the map
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is continuous and since
\mathrm{L}^{\Phi_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu)\subset\mathrm{L}^{\Phi_{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
we can conclude (
B
).

In order to prove (
59
), fix

and set

.
By the differentiation theorem for Radon measures (see, for instance,
[
6
]
), we have

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{n}}\psi_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{n}=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(x,r))},

for almost every

.
Notice that

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(x,r))}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\gamma_{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}\frac{\gamma_{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\gamma_{n}(B(x,r))}\frac{\gamma_{n}(B(x,r)))}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(x,r))},

where, again by differentiation theorem for Radon measures,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{n}(B(x,r)))}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\gamma_{n}(B(x,r))}

\displaystyle=w(t,s)(x),

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}{\mathcal{L}^{n}(\psi(B(x,r)))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\psi(x)|^{2}}{2}\right).

A short computation leads to (
59
).
∎

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{x}X[\phi]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathscr{D}_{b}}D_{x}X\partial_{t}\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathscr{D}_{b}}D_{x}X_{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathscr{D}_{b}}D_{x}b_{\epsilon}(t,X_{\epsilon}(t,s,x))D_{x}X_{\epsilon}(t,s,x)\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x.

Now,
X_{\epsilon}\to X
uniformly on compact sets and
D_{x}b_{\epsilon}\to D_{x}b
in
\mathrm{L}^{q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathscr{D}_{b})
for all
q
.
So, using Hölder inequality and the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we obtain that the limit above is

\partial_{t}D_{x}X[\phi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2+n}}D_{x}b(t,X(t,s,x))D_{x}X(t,s,x)\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}x.

In other words,
\partial_{t}D_{x}X=D_{x}b(t,X(t,s,x))D_{x}X(t,s,x)
.
This shows that
y
is solution to the Cauchy system (
87
).
Since
B
is integrable, then we get (
89
) by integrating this Cauchy system.

Finally, the validity of (
88
) follows in a standard way from (
87
) and
(
88
) implies (
90
).
∎

,

Corollary 6.10
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
D
, we have

X\in W^{1,p}(\mathscr{D}_{b};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

We know that

is continuous and, from (

.
Moreover, by the identity

, we get

\partial_{s}X(t,s,X(s,v,x))+D_{x}X(t,s,X(s,v,x))b(s,X(s,v,x))=0,

for all

and

Therefore,
\partial_{s}X(t,s,y)=-D_{x}X(t,s,y)b(s,y)

.

7.
Applications to PDEs

In this section we apply the Sobolev regularity of flows for getting the representation of weak solutions of the Cauchy problems both for the transport and continuity equations.

Proof of Theorem
F
.

By
[
12
, Theorem 1]
, we can infer the uniqueness of weak solutions
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
for (
2
), provided that the spatial derivative of
b
satisfies a sub-exponential summability and
\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Therefore we have only to show that the function
v
in (
19
) is a weak solution of (
2
), that is,

(92)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\,\left(\partial_{t}\varphi+{\rm div}(b\,\varphi)\,\right)dtdx=\,-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{u}\,\varphi(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x

for each
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We divide the proof in two steps.

1st step.

Let us assume that
\bar{u}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Let
b_{\epsilon}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the family of vector fields defined as

b_{\epsilon}(t,x):=\,(\tilde{b}_{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)*\rho_{\epsilon})(x)\text{ if }(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

where

\tilde{b}_{\epsilon}(t,x):=\,(\tilde{b}_{\epsilon,1}(t,x),\dots,\tilde{b}_{\epsilon,n}(t,x))\,,

\tilde{b}_{\epsilon,i}(t,x):=\,\max\{\{\min\{b_{i}(t,x),1/\epsilon\},-1/\epsilon\}\text{ if }(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\,i=1,\dots,n\,,

and
(\rho_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}
denotes a family of mollifiers depending on the space variable
x
. Then,
standard properties of convolutions yield

(93)

|b_{\epsilon}(t,x)|\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\epsilon}\text{ for each }(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

(94)

b_{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }D_{x}b_{\epsilon}(t,x)=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D_{x}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)\,\tilde{b}_{\epsilon}(t,y)\,dy\quad\forall(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

(95)

\text{ $b_{\epsilon}(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is Lipschitz continuous, uniformly with respect to $t\in I$},

(96)

b_{\epsilon}\to b\quad\text{in $L^{1}(I;L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ as $\epsilon\to 0$, whenever $\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\,.

Then, from (
95
) we get that
b_{\epsilon}
is well-posed and the flow maps
X_{\epsilon}
associated to
b_{\epsilon}
is globally defined, that is,
X_{\epsilon}:\,\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and is Lipschitz regular.
Define

v_{\epsilon}(t,x)=\bar{u}(X_{\epsilon}(0,t,x)).

By (
96
), Lemma
3.5
and the subsequent remark,
X_{\epsilon}\to X
uniformly on compact subsets
of
\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since
\bar{u}
is assumed to be continuous, we obtain that
v_{\epsilon}\to v
uniformly on compact sets of
\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, since
\bar{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, by the dominated convergence theorem, we can also assume that
v_{\epsilon}\to v
in
L^{2}
on each compact set of
\overline{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

By the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz theory (see, for instance,
[
5
, Section 2]
), it is well-known that
v_{\epsilon}

is a classical solution to (
2
) with
b_{\epsilon}
in place of
b
and, in particular, a weak solution, i.e.,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v_{\epsilon}\left(\partial_{t}\varphi+{\rm div}(b_{\epsilon}\varphi)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{u}(x)\varphi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

for each
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
\varphi
has compact support in
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is easy to check that

{\rm div}(b_{\epsilon}\varphi)\to{\rm div}(b\varphi)
in
L^{2}
. Hence

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v_{\epsilon}\left(\partial_{t}\varphi+{\rm div}(b_{\epsilon}\varphi)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\left(\partial_{t}\varphi+{\rm div}(b\varphi)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

that is,
v
is a weak solution to (
2
).

2nd step.

Let
\bar{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and, by mollification in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\bar{u}_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with
j\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence of functions that converges to
\bar{u}
almost everywhere and such that
\|\bar{u}_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leq\|\bar{u}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}
for every
j
.

Define

v_{j}(t,x)=\bar{u}_{j}(X(0,t,x)).

By Theorem
B
, for every
t
the homeomorphism
X(0,t,\cdot)
satisfies the Lusin (N) condition,
and therefore
v_{j}(t,\cdot)\to v(t,\cdot)
almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since this is true for every
t
, we get that
v_{j}\to v
almost everywhere in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, it is clear that
\|v_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leq\|\bar{u}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}
for all
j
.

By the previous step, we have, for all
j
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v_{j}\left(\partial_{t}\varphi+{\rm div}(b\varphi)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{u}_{j}(x)\varphi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

for each
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We can now apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem and pass to the limit
j\to\infty
to obtain that
v
is a weak solution to (
2
).
∎

8.
Examples

Most of the examples we provide are in low dimension.
However, notice that we can always extend the examples to higher dimensions as follows.
If
b(t,x)
is a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
m>0
, then define the vector field
h(t,(x,y)):=(b(t,x),0)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
If

is the flow of

, then

Z(t,s,(x,y)):=(X(t,s,x),y)
is the flow of

.
Notice that

if and only if

.

8.1.
Well-posedness does not imply absolute continuity of the flow

Here we show that

there is a vector field
b\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
that is well-posed, but whose flow on
\mathbb{R}
is not absolutely continuous.

This shows that Theorems
B
and
C
are not a direct consequence only of the well-posedness.

A first example is given as follows.
It is well-known that a quasisymmetric homeomorphism
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
need not be absolutely continuous (see, for instance,
[
24
, p. 107]
).
Moreover, by
[
31
, p. 250]
, for each quasisymmetric homeomorphism
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
there is a well -posed continuous vector field

8.2.
Sub-exponential condition does not imply high Sobolev regularity

Here we show that

there is a vector field
b\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})

that satisfies
(
12
), but
the flow of
b
is no better that
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

The same example allows us to show that

the upper bound on
\beta
in
Proposition
4.6

is necessary for the well-posedness
.

Given
\alpha\geq 1
, let
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the autonomous vector field on
\mathbb{R}
given by

b(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\leq 0\text{ or }x\geq e^{-e},\\
\beta x\log\frac{1}{x}(\log\log\frac{1}{x}-1)^{\alpha}&\text{ if }0<x<e^{-e}.\end{cases}

We clearly have that

b\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
,
and that
\mathtt{spt}(b)=[0,e^{-e}]
.
Moreover,

D_{x}b(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x<0\text{ or }x>e^{-e},\\
\beta\log\frac{1}{x}(\log\log\frac{1}{x})^{\alpha}(1+R(x))&\text{ if }0<x<e^{-e},\end{cases}

where
R
is a remainder satisfying
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}R(x)=0
.
It is easy to see that

(103)

\exp\left(\frac{\left|D_{x}b\right|}{\left(\log^{+}\left|D_{x}b\right|\right)^{\alpha}}\right)\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}).

Let us consider now the case
\alpha=1
.
Then
b
satisfies (
12
) and thus, by Theorem
C
,
b
is well-posed and the unique flow
X
of
b
satisfies
X(t,s)\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for every
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.
In fact, one can explicitly compute
X
by separation of variables:
for every
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
,

X(t,s,x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if }x\leq 0,\\
\exp\left(-e\left(\frac{1}{e}\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{k(t,s)}\right)&\text{ if }0<x<e^{-e},\\
x&\text{ if }x\geq e^{-e},\end{cases}

where
k(t,s)=\exp(s-t)
.
The spatial derivative of
X
for
0<x<e^{-e}
is

D_{x}X(t,s,x)=\exp\left(-e\left(\frac{1}{e}\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{k(t,s)}\right)k(t,s)\exp\left(-k(t,s)+1\right)\left(\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{k(t,s)-1}\frac{1}{x}.

If we now choose
s=0
and
t>0
, then it is easy to see that, for every
p>1
,

\int_{0}^{e^{-e}}|D_{x}X(t,0,x)|^{p}dx=+\infty;

in particular,
X(t,0,\cdot)\notin W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
whenever
p>1
.
Notice also that
X(t,0,\cdot)\notin C^{0,\gamma}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{x})
for all
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

In the other case, when
\alpha>1
, the vector field
b

103
) and (
8
)
with
\Theta

) (but with
\beta>1
).

b
is not well-posed any more.

and

satisfy
\gamma^{\prime}=b(\gamma)
and
\gamma(0)=0
.

8.3.
Exponential summability does not imply the divergence in
BMO
and it is only sufficient for the Sobolev regularity

Here we show that
there is a vector field
b
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
E
such that
{\rm div}_{x}b\notin BMO(\mathbb{R})
.

On the other hand
there exist
\ell>0
and an exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
such that, for each
t,\,s\in[-\ell/2,\ell/2]
,

X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
,
even though
\exp(\beta|D_{x}b|)\notin L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
if
\beta:=\,\frac{\ell p^{2}}{p-1}
.

Let
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the autonomous vector field on
\mathbb{R}
given by

b(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\leq 0\text{ or }x\geq e,\\
x\log\displaystyle{\frac{e}{x}}\,&\text{ if }0<x<e.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
b\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
, and that
\mathtt{spt}(b)=\mathbb{R}\times[0,e]
.
Moreover,

D_{x}b(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x<0\text{ or }x>e,\\
\log\frac{1}{x}&\text{ if }0<x<e.\end{cases}

Let
I:=\,(-\ell/2,\ell/2)
and
\Omega:=\,(-1,3)
. Observe that, in this case, condition (
6
) amounts to

(104)

\beta:=\,\frac{\ell\,p^{2}}{p-1}<1.

It is also easy to check (see
[
22
, Example 7.1.4]
) that

D_{x}b(\cdot)\notin BMO(\mathbb{R})\,.

Using (
104
) and Theorem
E
, one easily proves that
b:I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is well-posed.
In fact, we can integrate
b
by separation of variables and obtain

X(t,s,x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{ if }x\leq 0,\\
e\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{k(t,s)}&\text{ if }0<x<e,\\
x&\text{ if }x\geq e,\end{cases}

where
k(t,s)=\exp(s-t)
.
Moreover

D_{x}X(t,s,x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x<0,\\
k(t,s)\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{{k(t,s)-1}}&\text{ if }0<x<e,\\
1&\text{ if }x>e.\end{cases}

It follows that
X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if

(105)

(\exp(s-t)-1)q>-1,\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad\begin{cases}q<\frac{1}{1-\exp(s-t)}&\text{ if }s<t,\\
q\geq 1&\text{ if }s>t.\end{cases}

This example shows how the Sobolev regularity of
X(t,0,\cdot)
can deteriorate with time.
It also shows that condition (
6
) is only sufficient:
indeed, if
\ell\geq 1/4
then (
104
) is not satisfied by any
p
, but
X(t,s,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
, for each
t,\,s\in[-\ell/2,\ell/2]
, if
1<\,p<\,\frac{1}{1-\exp(-\ell)}
. Thus Theorem
E
does not apply. Notice also that, by (
105
), if
t>\,0
,
X(t,0,\cdot)\notin W^{1,q}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
for any
q\geq\,\frac{1}{1-\exp(-t)}
.
