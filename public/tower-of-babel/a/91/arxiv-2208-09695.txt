Two-phase flows with bulk-surface interaction: thermodynamically consistent Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard models with dynamic boundary conditions
Andrea Giorgini, Patrik Knopf

Abstract
We derive a novel thermodynamically consistent Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system with dynamic boundary conditions. This model describes the motion of viscous incompressible binary fluids with different densities. In contrast to previous models in the literature, our new model allows for surface diffusion, a variable contact angle between the diffuse interface and the boundary, and mass transfer between bulk and surface. In particular, this transfer of material is subject to a mass conservation law including both a bulk and a surface contribution. The derivation is carried out by means of local energy dissipation laws and the Lagrange multiplier approach. Next, in the case of fluids with matched densities, we show the existence of global weak solutions in two and three dimensions as well as the uniqueness of weak solutions in two dimensions. Keywords: Two-phase flows, Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system, bulk-surface interaction, dynamic boundary conditions Mathematics Subject Classification: 35D30, 35Q35, 76D03, 76D05, 76T06, 76T99.

Introduction

The description of multi-phase flows is a primal topic in modern continuum fluid dynamics with enormous applications in biology, chemistry and engineering. Two major approaches have been developed according to the representation of the interface separating the different components (see
[
19
,
5
,
30
,
46
]
and the references therein): Sharp Interface (SI) versus Diffuse Interface (DI) methods (see Figure
1
). In the former, the interface is represented by a hypersurface in the surrounding domain, which leads to the formulation of free boundary problems. In the latter, also called phase-field method, the interface is represented by a layer with finite thickness, corresponding to the level sets of the concentration function whose evolution is governed by a macroscopic equation. The main advantage of this approach is the Eulerian formulation of the phase-field equation, which avoids tracking the interface as in free boundary problems. Although conceptually opposites, these approaches are strictly related since a proper scaling of DI systems approximates SI models in the so-called
sharp interface limit
(see, e.g.,
[
5
]
).

Even though the AGG model ((
1.2
),(
1.3
)) is already very advanced and is capable of describing the complex case where the two fluids have different specific densities, it still inherits some limitations from the underlying (convective) Cahn–Hilliard equation with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. The main limitations are:

(L1)

The boundary condition (
1.3b
) enforces the diffuse interface separating both fluids to always intersect the boundary
\Gamma
at a perfect angle of ninety degrees. Of course, this restrictive condition will not always be satisfied in concrete applications as the contact angle of the interface might deviate from ninety degrees and even change dynamically over the course of time.
Moreover, as discussed in
[
47
]
, the no-slip boundary condition (
1.3a

(L2)

The boundary condition (
1.3c

\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0

\Sigma

1.2

1.3

[

Due to these well-known limitations of the Cahn–Hilliard model, and in order to describe short-range interactions between bulk and surface more precisely, physicists proposed that the total free energy should contain an additional contribution on the surface being also of Ginzburg–Landau type (see, e.g.,
[
10

\displaystyle E_{\text{free}}(\phi,\psi):=E_{\text{bulk}}(\phi)+E_{\text{surf}}(\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptE_{\text{surf}}(\psi):=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\delta\kappa}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}S,

\displaystyle\phi|_{\Sigma}=\psi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$}.

In this paper, we derive and study a new class of thermodynamically consistent Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard systems with dynamic boundary conditions. Our model derivation is based on local mass balance laws in the bulk and on the surface in which the mass fluxes are still to be determined. Arguing similarly as in
[
6
]

Sufficiently regular solutions of system (
1.17
) satisfy the
mass conservation law

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\beta\int_{\Omega}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}S=\beta\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}S&&\quad\text{if $L\in[0,\infty)$,}\\
&\int_{\Omega}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Gamma}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}S=\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}S&&\quad\text{if $L=\infty$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(1.19)

and the
energy dissipation law

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\psi)&=-\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\phi)\,\left|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\psi)\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S-\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad-\int_{\Gamma}m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S-\mathds{h}(L)\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\theta-\mu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S,\end{aligned}

(1.20)

where

\displaystyle\mathds{h}(L)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $L=0$ or $L=\infty$},\\
L^{-1}&\text{if $L\in(0,\infty)$},\end{cases}

(1.21)

as long as the solution exists.

After presenting the model derivation of the system (
1.17
) in Section
2
, we will study the case of fluids having matched densities, namely
\rho\equiv\tilde{\rho}_{1}=\tilde{\rho}_{2}
. This means that the flux
\mathbf{J}
vanishes. We rewrite the system (
1.17
) as follows

\displaystyle\rho\,\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\rho\,\textnormal{div}\big({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)-\textnormal{div}\big(2\nu(\phi)\,\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\nabla p=-\varepsilon\,\textnormal{div}\big(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.22a)

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.22b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})=\textnormal{div}\big(m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.22c)

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta\phi+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(\phi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.22d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\tau})=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\big)-\beta m_{\Omega}(\psi)\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.22e)

\displaystyle\theta=-\delta\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\delta^{-1}G^{\prime}(\psi)+\varepsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.22f)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\phi|_{\Gamma}=\psi,\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{cases}Lm_{\Omega}(\psi)\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=\beta\theta-\mu&\text{if $L\in[0,\infty)$},\\
m_{\Omega}(\psi)\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0&\text{if $L=\infty$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.22g)

\displaystyle\big[2\nu(\psi)(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+\gamma(\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big]_{\tau}=\big[-\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\big]_{\tau}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.22h)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{t=0}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\phi|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},

(1.22i)

\displaystyle\psi|_{t=0}=\psi_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Gamma$}.

(1.22j)

The model (
1.22
) can be regarded as a generalization of the model H with dynamic boundary conditions of Cahn–Hilliard type.
We will focus on the mathematical analysis of system (
1.22
) in the case
L=0
and
\beta>0
(see system (
3.12
) in Section
3
). In Theorem
3.3
, we first prove the existence of global weak solutions to system (
3.12
) with regular (polynomial-like) potentials
F
and
G
in bounded Lipschitz domains (cf. (

A2

)–(

A5

) below for the specific assumptions). Weak solutions satisfy the
variational formulation
of the problem (
3.12
) and a
weak energy dissipation law
(see (
3.18
)). A weak solution is obtained as the limit of smooth solutions to suitably regularized problems based on a Faedo–Galerkin scheme, where appropriate compactness properties are derived on the basis of uniform and global estimates gained from the energy dissipation law (
1.20
).
However, an ad hoc construction of the solution ansatz is required since both
(\phi,\psi)
and
(\mu,\theta)
need to be approximated in the same finite-dimensional subspace in order to show the validity of (
1.20
) for such regularized solutions (cf. the change of variables (
3.22
)). In comparison with the works in literature
[
25
,
26
,
23
]
, our argument is rather simple and relies only on a one-level Faedo–Galerkin approximation. Furthermore, it is shown that
(\mu,\theta)\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathcal{L}^{2})
, which is a novel regularity for NSCH systems (and even CH equations) with regular potentials (cf., for instance,
[
11
,
22
]
).
Next, in Theorem
3.4
, we then establish the uniqueness of the weak solutions in the two dimensional case. This is the first uniqueness result in the literature for a NSCH system with dynamic boundary conditions. It is important to underline that the nonlinear coupling in the NSCH system with dynamic boundary conditions (
1.22
) is much stronger than in the model H (
1.1
). Roughly speaking, this is due to the elastic (surface) term
[\psi\nabla\theta]_{\tau}
in (
1.22h
),
in addition to the classical capillarity (bulk) term
-\varepsilon\textnormal{div}(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)
(also referred to as
Korteweg force
).
More precisely, gradient terms arising from equivalent formulations of the
Korteweg force
(cf. (
3.11
)) disappear in bulk integrals in view of the incompressibility constraint
\textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}=0
.
Instead, surface integrals involving the product between a gradient function and
{\boldsymbol{v}}
does not vanish since
\textnormal{div}_{\tau}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\neq 0
on
\Sigma
. For this reason,
the term
[\psi\nabla\theta]_{\tau}
cannot be handled as in
[
33
]
through dual estimates.
Therefore, the appropriate functional to control the difference of two solutions corresponds to the total energy. This functional is capable of reproducing the same cancellation of the nonlinear terms as in the derivation of (
1.20
).
In order to rigorously justify this fact, two additional assumptions need to be imposed: the domain
\Omega
has a
C^{3}
-boundary, which is needed to gain further spatial regularity of
(\phi,\psi)
and to recover the relations of the bulk/surface chemical potentials
(\mu,\theta)
almost everywhere; the relation between bulk and surface potentials
F(s)=\beta G(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, which enables us to apply a particular chain formula (see Appendix
A
).

2
Model derivation

In this section we will derive the novel Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system with dynamic boundary conditions (
1.17
).

2.1
Considerations based on local mass balance laws

We consider the time evolution of two fluids (indexed with
i=1,2
) in a (sufficiently smooth) domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
on a time interval
[0,T]
with
T>0
. We write
Q=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma=\Gamma\times(0,T)
, where
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.
Let
\rho_{i}:Q\cup\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, denote the mass densities of these two fluids.
The local mass balance equations in the bulk read as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}\,\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}=0,\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.1)

Here,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}
are the mass fluxes. As we want to allow for a transfer of material between bulk and surface, the local mass balance equation on the boundary is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}=\beta\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n},\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.2)

In this relation,
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}
stand for the mass fluxes on the boundary. The right-hand side
\beta\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}
describes the transfer of mass between bulk and surface. Here, the constant
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
acts as a weight of this mass transfer.
Introducing the individual velocities
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}:Q\cup\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,2
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}{\rho_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},\hskip 20.00003pt{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}}{\rho_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,

the mass balances (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}(\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\beta\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.4)

Let now
\phi_{i}:Q\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, denote the volume fractions of the two fluids in the bulk and on the boundary, respectively.
Provided that each fluid has a constant density
\tilde{\rho}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, the volume fractions can be identified as

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}\hskip 20.00003pt\psi_{i}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$,}\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2.

(2.5)

In particular, this naturally entails the trace relation

\displaystyle\phi|_{\Sigma}=\psi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.6)

Under the assumption that the excess volume is zero, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}=1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}\hskip 20.00003pt\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}=1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.7)

We further set

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}\hskip 20.00003pt\psi=\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.8)

Furthermore, let
{\boldsymbol{v}}:Q\cup\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the volume averaged velocity field associated with the two fluids.
It can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}+\phi_{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}+\psi_{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.10)

We assume that both fluids cannot permeate the boundary
\Gamma
, which leads to the condition

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mathbf{n}}={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.11)

Hence,
{\boldsymbol{v}}
is identical to its tangential component, that is,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\tau}={\boldsymbol{v}}-({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}={\boldsymbol{v}}
on
\Sigma
. We further write

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}-\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}-\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$,}

(2.13)

to denote the mass fluxes relative to the volume averaged velocity. The mass balance equations (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) can thus be expressed as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}(\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{i}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\rho_{i}{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathbf{J}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.15)

In this context,
\mathbf{J}_{i}
and
\mathbf{K}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, can be regarded as diffusive flow rates. We now define the total mass density as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q\cup\Sigma$.}

(2.16)

This leads to the relations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\textnormal{div}(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}(\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{2})

\displaystyle=0

in
Q
,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{K}_{1}+\mathbf{K}_{2})

\displaystyle=\beta(\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{n}

on
\Sigma
.

(2.18)

Next, assuming a conservation of linear momentum with respect to the velocity field
{\boldsymbol{v}}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}})=\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{T}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.19)

Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}
denotes the stress tensor that needs to be specified by constitutive assumptions. In the following, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}_{1}+\mathbf{K}_{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.20)

Using (
2.17
) to rewrite (
2.19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})&=\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+(\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}){\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&=\textnormal{div}(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes\mathbf{J})-\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{J}\quad\text{in $Q$.}\end{aligned}

(2.21)

This allows us to define the objective tensor

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes\mathbf{J}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.22)

which is frame indifferent. Hence, (
2.21
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\,(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}}+\mathbf{J})=\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{T}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.23)

We now multiply (
2.3
) by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, and we add the resulting equations. Recalling (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), this yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}&=\partial_{t}(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})+\textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&=\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}\right)+\textnormal{div}\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}\right)=0&&\quad\text{in $Q$,}\end{aligned}

(2.24)

which means that the volume averaged velocity field is divergence-free.
Multiplying (
2.4
) by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, and adding the resulting equations, we infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}&=\partial_{t}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&=\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}\right)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}\right)\\
&=\beta\left(\frac{\mathbf{J}_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}+\frac{\mathbf{J}_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}&&\quad\text{on $\Sigma$.}\end{aligned}

(2.25)

We now define

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{J}_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbf{J}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}

in
Q
,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{K}_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbf{K}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}

on
\Sigma
.

(2.27)

We next multiply (
2.14
) by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
. Subtracting the resulting equations, we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.28)

Moreover, multiplying (
2.14
) by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
, adding the resulting equations, and recalling (
2.7
) and (
2.24
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2})=\partial_{t}(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})+\textnormal{div}\big((\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2})=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.29)

Proceeding similarly with (
2.15
), and recalling (
2.7
) and (
2.25

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}=\beta\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$,}

(2.30)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2})&=\partial_{t}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big((\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)\\
&\quad+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2})-\beta(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{on $\Sigma$.}\end{aligned}

(2.31)

Using (
2.26
) and (
2.29
), we infer that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}&=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tilde{\rho}_{2}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tilde{\rho}_{2}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\bigg]\\[4.30554pt]
&=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}&&\quad\text{in $Q$.}\end{aligned}

(2.32)

This means that

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.33)

In the same fashion, we derive the identity

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.34)

From (

) and (

\displaystyle\rho(\phi)=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\phi+\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.35)

and due to (

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\rho(\phi)|_{\Sigma}=\rho(\psi)=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\psi+\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.36)

Plugging (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.37)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.38)

2.2
Local energy dissipation laws

Proceeding as in
[
6
]
, we introduce the following energy density in the bulk:

\displaystyle e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)=\frac{\rho(\phi)}{2}\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}+f(\phi,\nabla\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.39)

The first summand on the right-hand side stands for the kinetic energy density whereas the second summand denotes the free energy density in the bulk.
On the boundary, we introduce the additional energy density

\displaystyle e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)=g(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$,}

(2.40)

where
g
stands for the free energy density on the surface.

We now consider an arbitrary test volume
V(t)\subset\Omega
,

t\in[0,T]
, that is transported by the flow associated with
{\boldsymbol{v}}
. Let
\nu
denote the outer unit normal vector field of
V(t)
.
In an isothermal situation, the second law of thermodynamics leads to the dissipation inequality

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}S\right]\\
&\qquad+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Omega}\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\nu\;\mathrm{d}S+\int_{\partial_{\Gamma}(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma)}\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot\nu_{\Gamma}\;\mathrm{d}S_{\Gamma}.\end{aligned}

(2.41)

Here
\mathbf{J}_{e}
and
\mathbf{K}_{e}
are energy fluxes that will be specified later,
and we assume that
\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
.
As we consider a closed system, there is no transfer of energy over the boundary
\Gamma
and thus, the domain of the first integral in the second line is just
\partial V(t)\cap\Omega
instead of
\partial V(t)
. We further point out that
\partial_{\Gamma}(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma)\subseteq\Gamma
is to be understood as the relative boundary of the set
\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma
within the submanifold
\Gamma
, and
\nu_{\Gamma}
stands for the corresponding outer unit normal vector field. Applying Gauß’s divergence theorem on both integrals in the second line, and recalling that
\nu=\mathbf{n}
on
\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma
, we reformulate (
2.41
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}S\right]\\
&\qquad+\int_{V(t)}\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\mathrm{d}S.\end{aligned}

(2.42)

Applying the Reynolds transport theorem, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{V(t)}\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\big(e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)\;\mathrm{d}x.

(2.43)

Similarly, using the transport theorem for evolving hypersurfaces (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorem 32]
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}S=\int_{V(t)\cap\Gamma}\partial_{t}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)\;\mathrm{d}S.

(2.44)

Combining (
2.42
), (
2.43
) and (
2.44
), we thus get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\int_{V(t)}\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\big(e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}\partial_{t}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\mathrm{d}S.\end{aligned}

(2.45)

In particular, the above inequality holds true for all test volumes
V(t)\subset\Omega
with
\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma=\emptyset
. We thus infer the local dissipation law in the bulk, which reads as

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}:=\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\big(e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.46)

Let now
\alpha>0
be arbitrary and suppose that
V(t)\subset\Omega
is a test volume with
\left|V(t)\right|
being sufficiently small such that

\displaystyle\int_{V(t)}\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\;\mathrm{d}x<\alpha.

Then, we use (
2.45
) to infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma}\partial_{t}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\mathrm{d}S<\alpha.

Since
\alpha>0
and the test volume
V(t)
were arbitrary (except for the above restriction on
V(t)
), we conclude the following local dissipation law on the boundary:

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}:=\partial_{t}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.47)

2.3
Completion of the model derivation by the Lagrange multiplier approach

We now complete the model derivation by means of the
Lagrange multiplier approach
. We introduce the functions
\mu
and
\theta
that will be fixed in the subsequent approach. In view of the identities (
2.37
) and (
2.38
), the local energy dissipation laws (
2.46
) and (
2.47
) can be written as

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\big(e_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\mu\big(\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\big)

in
Q
,

(2.48)

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(e_{\Gamma}(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\theta\big(\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}({\boldsymbol{v}}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-\beta\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big)

on
\Sigma
.

(2.49)

Here, the functions
\mu
and
\theta
can be understood as Lagrange multipliers.

In the following, for brevity, we will just write
\rho
,
f
and
g
instead of
\rho(\phi)
,
f(\phi,\nabla\phi)
and
g(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)
.
By the definition of the energy densities
e_{\Omega}
and
e_{\Gamma}
(see (
2.39
) and (
2.40
)), we rewrite (
2.3
) and (
2.3
) as

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}\big)+\textnormal{div}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\partial_{t}f+\textnormal{div}(f{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\mu\big(\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\big)

in
Q
,

(2.50)

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}g+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(g{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\theta\big(\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-\beta\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\big)

on
\Sigma
.

(2.51)

By the chain rule, the derivatives

\partial_{t}f
and
\partial_{t}g

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}f\,\partial_{t}\phi+\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\,\partial_{t}\nabla\phi

in
Q
,

(2.52)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g

\displaystyle=\partial_{\psi}g\,\partial_{t}\psi+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\,\partial_{t}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi

on
\Sigma
.

(2.53)

In the following, for any function
h:Q\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the notation

\displaystyle D_{t}h=\partial_{t}h+(v\cdot\nabla)h

to denote its material derivative.
In the bulk, we proceed exactly as in
[
6
]
to reformulate (
2.3
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\textnormal{div}\big[\mathbf{J}_{e}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{T}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}+\partial_{\nabla\phi}fD_{t}\phi\big]\\
&\quad+\big[\partial_{\phi}f-\textnormal{div}(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f)-\mu\big]D_{t}\phi\\
&\quad-\big[\mathbf{T}+\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big]:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\phi}&&\quad\text{in $Q$.}\end{aligned}

(2.54)

To ensure (
2.54
), we now choose the chemical potential
\mu
and the energy flux
\mathbf{J}_{e}
as

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}f-\textnormal{div}\big(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big)

in
Q
,

(2.55)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{T}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}-\partial_{\nabla\phi}fD_{t}\phi

in
Q
.

(2.56)

By these choices, the first two lines of the right-hand side in (
2.54
) vanish.
We further assume the mass flux
\mathbf{J}_{\phi}
to be of Fick’s type, that is

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.57)

where
m_{\Omega}=m_{\Omega}(\phi)
is a nonnegative function representing the mobility.
Hence, (
2.54
) reduces to

\displaystyle 0\geq-\big[\mathbf{T}+\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big]:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.58)

We now define the tensor

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{T}+p\mathbf{I}+\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

Here, the variable
p
denotes the pressure, and
\mathbf{I}
denotes the identity matrix.
\mathbf{S}
is the viscous stress tensor that corresponds to irreversible changes of the energy due to friction. For Newtonian fluids,
\mathbf{S}
is usually chosen as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}},

(2.59)

where
\nu=\nu(\phi)
is a nonnegative function representing the viscosity of the fluids, and
\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}
is the symmetric gradient of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
. By this choice, (
2.58
) is satisfied since we obtain

\displaystyle 0\geq-\nu(\phi)\left|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.60)

by means of the identity
p\mathbf{I}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}=p\,\textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}=0
in
Q
.

We now assume that the energy density
f
is of Ginzburg–Landau type, that is

\displaystyle f(\phi,\nabla\phi)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.61)

Here, the parameter
\varepsilon>0
is related to the thickness of the diffuse interface that separates the two fluids, and
F
is a potential that usually exhibits a double-well structure. Hence, the chemical potential
\mu
reads as

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta\phi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F^{\prime}(\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.62)

and the total stress tensor
\mathbf{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{T}&=\mathbf{S}-p\mathbf{I}-\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\\
&=2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}-p\mathbf{I}-\varepsilon\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\quad\text{in $Q$.}\end{aligned}

(2.63)

Plugging (
2.57
) into (
2.37
), we further obtain the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}(\phi{\boldsymbol{v}})=\textnormal{div}\big(m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.64)

Eventually, recalling (
2.22
) and (
2.63
), we use (
2.19
) to derive the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}\big({\boldsymbol{v}}\otimes(\rho(\phi){\boldsymbol{v}}+\mathbf{J})\big)-\textnormal{div}\big(2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\big)+\nabla p=-\varepsilon\textnormal{div}(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$,}

(2.65)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$.}

(2.66)

We now consider the local energy dissipation law (
2.3
) on the boundary.
Recalling the formulas for
\mathbf{J}_{e}
(see (
2.56
)) and
\mathbf{T}
(see (
2.63
)), we infer that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\partial_{\psi}g\,\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\partial_{t}\psi\big)-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(g{\boldsymbol{v}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}\\
&\quad-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n})\,({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}})+2\nu(\phi)\big(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n}\big)\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-\varepsilon(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\mathbf{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\\
&\quad+(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n})\partial_{t}\psi+(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n})({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)-\theta\partial_{t}\psi-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\theta\psi{\boldsymbol{v}})+\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&\quad-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{K}_{\psi}\theta)+\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}+\beta\theta\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\\[4.30554pt]
&=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big[\mathbf{K}_{e}+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\partial_{t}\psi+g{\boldsymbol{v}}-\theta\psi{\boldsymbol{v}}-\theta\mathbf{K}_{\psi}\big]\\
&\quad+\big[\partial_{\psi}g-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)+\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n}-\theta\big]\partial_{t}\psi\\
&\quad+\big[2\nu(\psi)(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&\quad+(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n})({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)-\varepsilon(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\mathbf{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
&\quad+\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}+(\beta\theta-\mu)\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\end{aligned}

(2.67)

on
\Sigma
.
Here, we have used that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\phi={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\big[\nabla\phi-\mathbf{n}(\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n})\big]={\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla\phi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$}

(2.68)

due to the definition of the surface gradient and the boundary condition (
2.11
).
In order to ensure that the inequality (
2.67
) is fulfilled, we choose the chemical potential
\theta
, the mass flux
\mathbf{K}_{\psi}
, and the energy flux
\mathbf{K}_{e}
as follows:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\partial_{\psi}g-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)+\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n}

on
\Sigma
,

(2.69)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=-m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta

on
\Sigma
,

(2.70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{e}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}gD_{t}\psi+\theta\psi{\boldsymbol{v}}+\theta\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-g{\boldsymbol{v}}

on
\Sigma
.

(2.71)

To ensure the condition
K_{e}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\Sigma
, we need to assume at this point that
\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\Sigma
.

In (
2.70
),
m_{\Gamma}=m_{\Gamma}(\psi)
is a nonnegative function representing the mobility.
This means that the first two lines of the right-hand side in (
2.67
) vanish.
Moreover, since
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\mathbf{n}}=0
(see (
2.11
)), we have
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{\tau}
and thus,

\displaystyle\big[2\nu(\psi)(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\big[2\nu(\psi)(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big]_{\tau}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\tau}

Therefore, in order to ensure that (
2.67
) is satisfied, we make the constitutive assumption

\displaystyle\big[2\nu(\psi)(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{n}){\boldsymbol{v}}\big]_{\tau}=-\gamma(\psi){\boldsymbol{v}}_{\tau}.

(2.72)

where
\gamma=\gamma(\psi)
is a nonnegative function.
This equation can be regarded as an inhomogeneous Navier slip boundary condition.
We now assume that the energy density
g
is also of Ginzburg–Landau type, that is

\displaystyle g(\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)=\frac{\kappa\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.73)

Here,
\delta>0
is related to the thickness of the diffuse interface on the boundary,
\kappa\geq 0
acts as a weight for surface diffusion effects, and
G
is a potential that usually exhibits a double-well structure. Hence, recalling the definition of the energy density
f
(see (
2.61
)), the chemical potential
\theta
is given as

\displaystyle\theta=-\kappa\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\frac{1}{\delta}G^{\prime}(\psi)-\varepsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.74)

Thanks to (
2.68
), we further have

\displaystyle(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\cdot\mathbf{n})({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)=\varepsilon(\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n})({\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\nabla\phi)=\varepsilon(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\mathbf{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.75)

Hence, (
2.67
) reduces to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq-\gamma(\psi)\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|^{2}-m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}-(\beta\theta-\mu)\,m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.76)

The first two terms on the right-hand side are clearly nonpositive as
\gamma(\psi)
and
m_{\Gamma}(\psi)
are nonnegative. This means that (
2.76
) is fulfilled if one of the following boundary conditions holds:

\displaystyle\beta\theta-\mu

\displaystyle=0

on
\Sigma
,

(2.77a)

\displaystyle m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{L}(\beta\theta-\mu)

on
\Sigma
for a constant
L\in(0,\infty)
,

(2.77b)

\displaystyle m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

on
\Sigma
.

(2.77c)

Finally, by substituting (
2.57
) and (
2.70
) into (
2.38
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\boldsymbol{v}})=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta)-\beta m_{\Omega}(\psi)\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$.}

(2.78)

Collecting (
2.65
), (
2.24
), (
2.66
), (
2.64
), (
2.62
), (
2.78
), (
2.74
), (
2.11
), (
2.6
), (
2.77
) and (
2.72
), and imposing the initial conditions

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{t=0}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\phi|_{t=0}=\phi_{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},\hskip 20.00003pt\psi|_{t=0}=\psi_{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$,}

(2.79)

for prescribed initial data
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}
,
\phi_{0}
and
\psi_{0}
, we have thus derived the system (
1.17
).

3
Mathematical analysis: the case of matched densities

3.1
Notation and preliminaries

We fix some notation and assumptions that are supposed to hold throughout the remainder of this paper.

Notation.

(\mathrm{N1})

The set of natural numbers excluding zero is denoted by
\mathbb{N}
, and we write
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

(\mathrm{N2})

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\left\{2,3\right\}
. We write
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
.
For any real numbers
k\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces for functions defined on
\Omega
with values in
\mathbb{R}
are denoted as
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
. We write
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}
to denote the standard norms on these spaces. If
p=2
, we use the notation
H^{k}(\Omega)=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. We point out that
H^{0}(\Omega)
can be identified with
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Analogously, the
Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma
are denoted by
L^{p}(\Gamma)
and
W^{k,p}(\Gamma)
with corresponding norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Gamma)}
, respectively.
In the case of vector fields on
\Omega
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n>1
, we use the notation
\mathbf{L}^{p}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{W}^{k,p}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{H}^{k}(\Omega)
. For simplicity, their norms are denoted as in the scalar case by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
,
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}
, respectively.

Let
I
be a closed interval in
\mathbb{R}
and
X
be a Banach space. The space
C(I;X)
denotes the set of continuous functions from
I
to
X
and, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
C^{k}(I;X)
denotes the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable functions from
I
to
X
. In particular, we simply write
C(I)
and
C^{k}(I)
if
X=\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
C_{w}(I;X)
denotes the space of functions mapping from
I
to
X
which are continuous on
I
with respect to the weak topology on
X
. This means that for any function
f\in C_{w}(I;X)
and every sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
I
with
t_{k}\to t
in
I
as
k\to\infty
, it holds
f(t_{k})\to f(t)
weakly in
X
as
k\to\infty
. Furthermore, for any real numbers
k\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, the Bochner spaces of functions defined on an interval
I
in
\mathbb{R}
with values in
X
are denoted by
L^{p}(I;X)
and
W^{k,p}(I;X)
.

(\mathrm{N3})

For any Banach space
X
, we write
X^{\prime}
to denote its dual space. The associated duality pairing of elements
\phi\in X^{\prime}
and
\zeta\in X
is denoted as
\langle\phi,\zeta\rangle_{X}
. If
X
is a Hilbert space, we write
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
to denote its inner product.

(\mathrm{N4})

For any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
, we define

\displaystyle\langle u\rangle_{\Omega}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\langle u,1\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}&\text{ if }u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime},\\
\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\int_{\Omega}u\;\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }u\in L^{1}(\Omega)\end{cases}

to denote the (generalized) spatial mean of
u
. Here,
\left|\Omega\right|
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
.
The spatial mean of a function
v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)^{\prime}
(or
v\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
, respectively) is denoted as
\langle v\rangle_{\Gamma}
and defined analogously.

(\mathrm{N5})

For any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
and
d\in\{2,3\}
, we introduce the space

\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega):=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):\ \textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\ \text{in}\ \Omega,\ {\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma\right\}=\overline{C_{0,\sigma}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C_{0,\sigma}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\left\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):\ \textnormal{div}\,{\boldsymbol{v}}=0\ \text{in}\ \Omega\right\}
. Moreover, we define

\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)=\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega).

Assumptions.

To prove the existence of weak solutions in the case of matched densities, we make the following assumptions:

(\mathrm{A1})

We consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
and a final time
T>0
. We further use the notation

\displaystyle Q:=\Omega\times(0,T),\hskip 10.00002pt\Sigma:=\Gamma\times(0,T).

(\mathrm{A2})

The constants occurring in the system (
1.22
) satisfy
\varepsilon,\delta,\beta,\kappa>0
and
L=0
.
Since the choice of
\delta
,
\varepsilon
and
\kappa
has no impact on the mathematical analysis, we will simply set
\delta=\varepsilon=\kappa=1
without loss of generality.

(\mathrm{A3})

The potentials
F:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
and
G:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
are twice continuously differentiable
and there exist two exponents
p
and
q
satisfying

\displaystyle p\in\begin{cases}[2,\infty),&\text{if $d=2$,}\\
[2,6],&\text{if $d=3$,}\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptq\in[2,\infty)

as well as constants

Preliminaries.

We next introduce several function spaces, inner products, norms and operators that will be used throughout this paper.

(\mathrm{P1})

For any real numbers
k\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p}:=L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{p}(\Gamma),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{H}^{k}:=H^{k}(\Omega)\times H^{k}(\Gamma),

and we identify
\mathcal{L}^{2}
with
\mathcal{H}^{0}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}^{k}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\big((\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\big(\phi,\zeta\big)_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\big(\psi,\xi\big)_{H^{k}(\Gamma)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}$,}

and its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{1/2}
.

(\mathrm{P2})

For
\beta>0
, we introduce the subspace

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}:=\left\{(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}\;\big|\;\phi|_{\Gamma}=\beta\psi\;\;\text{a.e. on}\;\Gamma\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{1},

endowed with the inner product

\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

and its induced norm.
The space
{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}
is a Hilbert space. Moreover, we define the product

\displaystyle\big<(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}:=\left(\phi,\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\psi,\xi\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

for all
(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. By means of the Riesz representation theorem, this product can be extended to a duality pairing on
{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}^{\prime}}\times{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}
, which will also be denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}
.

In particular, the spaces
\big({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}},\mathcal{L}^{2},{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}^{\prime}}\big)
form a Gelfand triplet, and the operator norm on
{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}^{\prime}}
is given by

\displaystyle\|(\phi,\psi)\|_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}^{\prime}}}:=\sup\Big\{\,\big|\langle(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\rangle_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}\big|\;\Big|\;(\zeta,\xi)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}\text{ with }\|(\zeta,\xi)\|_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}=1\Big\},

for all
(\phi,\psi)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}^{\prime}}
.

(\mathrm{P3})

We further recall the following
bulk-surface Poincaré inequalities
:

(\mathrm{P3.1})

For any
\alpha>0
, there exists a constant
C_{P}
depending only on
\alpha
and
\Omega
such that

\|(\phi,\psi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq C_{P}\|(\nabla\phi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

(3.9)

for all
(\phi,\psi)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
with

\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}=0
.

This bulk-surface Poincaré inequality is a special case of the one established in
[
40
, Lemma A.1]
(with the parameters therein being chosen as
K=0
and
\alpha,\beta>0
).

(\mathrm{P3.2})

There exists a constant
C_{P}^{\prime}
depending only on
\Omega
such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}^{\prime}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)

(3.10)

for all
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

This bulk-surface Poincaré inequality is established in
[
53
, Chapter II, Section 1.4]
.

(3.25c)

for all test functions
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
and
(\zeta,\xi),\,(\eta,\vartheta)\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
.

We now prove the existence of a weak solution of system (
3.23
) (in the aforementioned sense) and afterwards,
we use the change of variables (
3.22
) to infer the existence of a weak solution to the original model (
3.12
).
The weak formulation (
3.25
) can indeed be discretized by a suitable Faedo–Galerkin scheme.

Step 1: Discretization via a bulk-surface Faedo–Galerkin scheme.

It has already been established in
[
40
, Theorem 4.4]
that the second-order elliptic eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\zeta&=\lambda\zeta&&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
-\Delta_{\Gamma}\xi+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\zeta&=\lambda\xi&&\text{on $\Gamma$},\\
\zeta|_{\Gamma}&=\alpha\xi&&\text{on $\Gamma$}\end{aligned}\right.

(3.26)

has countably many eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and the corresponding eigenfunctions
\{(\zeta_{k},\xi_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}

can be chosen such that they form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}^{2}
.
Here, we fix the eigenfunction associated with the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=0
as

\displaystyle(\zeta_{1},\xi_{1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|}}(\alpha,1).

(3.27)

Moreover, in view of their construction (see
[
40
]
), the eigenfunctions
(\zeta_{k},\xi_{k})
with
k\geq 2
satisfy the mean value constraint

\displaystyle\alpha|\Omega|{\langle\zeta_{k}\rangle}_{\Omega}+|\gamma|{\langle\xi_{k}\rangle}_{\Gamma}=0.

(3.28)

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the finite dimensional subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{k}:=\text{span}\,\{(\zeta_{1},\xi_{1}),...,(\zeta_{k},\xi_{k})\}\subset{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}},

(3.29)

and we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\zeta,\xi)=\big(\mathbb{P}^{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\zeta,\xi),\mathbb{P}^{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\zeta,\xi)\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any $(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}$},

to denote the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.

Next, we consider the Stokes operator with Navier boundary conditions (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Appendix A]
)

\mathbf{A}:\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})\subset\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega):\hskip 10.00002pt{\boldsymbol{v}}\mapsto\mathbf{A}{\boldsymbol{v}}:=-\mathbb{P}\textnormal{div}(2D{\boldsymbol{v}}),

(3.30)

where
\mathbb{P}
is the Leray projection operator, with domain

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})=\Big\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega):\big[2(\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathbf{n})+{\boldsymbol{v}}\big]_{\tau}=0\;\text{on $\Gamma$}\Big\}.

(3.31)

Since
\mathbf{A}
is a positive and self-adjoint operator on
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
with compact inverse, and

\left(\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega),\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{A})\right)_{\frac{1}{2},2}=\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega),

there exists a sequence of countably many positive eigenvalues
\big\{\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\big\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the corresponding eigenfunctions
\{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
,
which are determined by

\int_{\Omega}2D{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\;\mathrm{d}S=\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all ${\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)$},

(3.32)

can be chosen in such a way that they form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the finite dimensional subspace

\mathcal{U}_{k}=\text{span}\,\{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\}\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega),

and the
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{U}_{k}
is denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{k}}
.

Next, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,T]
, we make the ansatz

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{k}_{i}(t)\,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega$},

(3.33a)

\displaystyle\big(\phi_{k}(t),\Psi_{k}(t)\big)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b^{k}_{i}(t)\,(\zeta_{i},\xi_{i}),

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega\times\Gamma$},

(3.33b)

\displaystyle\big(\mu_{k}(t),\Theta_{k}(t)\big)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c^{k}_{i}(t)\,(\zeta_{i},\xi_{i}),

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega\times\Gamma$}.

(3.33c)

Here, the scalar, time-dependent coefficients
a^{k}_{i}
,
b^{k}_{i}
and
c^{k}_{i}
,
i=1,...,k
are assumed to be continuously differentiable functions that are still to be determined. They need to be designed in such a way that the discretized weak formulation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\langle\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{w}}\rangle_{\mathbf{H}_{\textnormal{div}}^{1}(\Omega)}-\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k})\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\phi_{k})\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}:\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{w}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k}\nabla\phi_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\alpha\Psi_{k}){\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}+\Psi_{k}\nabla_{\Gamma}\Theta_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{w}}\;\mathrm{d}S,\end{aligned}

(3.34a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\big<(\partial_{t}\phi_{k},\partial_{t}\Psi_{k}),(\zeta,\xi)\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}-\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S\\
&\quad=-\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi_{k})\nabla\mu_{k}:\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}n_{\Gamma}(\Psi_{k})\nabla_{\Gamma}\Theta_{k}:\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S,\end{aligned}

(3.34b)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\mu_{k}\cdot\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\Theta_{k}\cdot\xi\;\mathrm{d}S\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{k}\cdot\nabla\zeta+F^{\prime}(\phi_{k})\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\alpha^{2}\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi+\alpha G^{\prime}(\alpha\Psi_{k})\xi\;\mathrm{d}S\end{aligned}

(3.34c)

holds for all
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}
and all
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}
,
supplemented with the initial conditions

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{k}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0})\in\mathcal{U}_{k}

in
\Omega
,

(3.35)

\displaystyle(\phi_{k},\Psi_{k})|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega\times\Gamma$},

(3.36)

where
\Psi_{0}:=\alpha^{-1}\psi_{0}
.

Let now
\mathbf{a}^{k}:=(a_{1}^{k},...,a_{k}^{k})
,
\mathbf{b}^{k}:=(b_{1}^{k},...,b_{k}^{k})
and
\mathbf{c}^{k}:=(c_{1}^{k},...,c_{k}^{k})
denote the coefficient vectors.
Testing (
3.34a
) with
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}
and (
3.34b
) with
(\zeta_{1},\xi_{1}),...,(\zeta_{k},\xi_{k})
, we conclude that
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
is determined by a system of
2k
ordinary differential equations whose right-hand side depends continuously on
\mathbf{a}^{k}
,
\mathbf{b}^{k}
and
\mathbf{c}^{k}
. Due to (
3.35
) and (
3.36
), this ODE system is subject to the initial condition

\displaystyle[\mathbf{a}^{k}]_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{k}(0)=\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\big)_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti\in\{1,...,k\},

\displaystyle[\mathbf{b}^{k}]_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=b_{i}^{k}(0)=\big((\phi_{0},\Psi_{0}),(\zeta_{i},\xi_{i})\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pti\in\{1,...,k\}.

Moreover, testing (
3.34c
) with
(\zeta_{1},\xi_{1}),...,(\zeta_{k},\xi_{k})
, we infer that
\mathbf{c}^{k}
is explicitly given by a system of
k
algebraic equations whose right-hand side depends continuously on
\mathbf{b}^{k}
. We now replace the variable
\mathbf{c}^{k}
appearing in the right-hand side of the aforementioned ODE system to obtain a closed
2k
-dimensional ODE system describing
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
whose right-hand side depends continuously on
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
. We can thus apply the Cauchy–Peano theorem to infer the existence of at least one local solution

(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}:[0,T_{k}^{*})\cap[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2k}

with
T_{k}^{*}>0
to the corresponding initial value problem. Without loss of generality, we assume that
T_{k}^{*}\leq T
and that
(\mathbf{a}^{k},\mathbf{b}^{k})^{\top}
is right-maximal, meaning that
T_{k}^{*}
is chosen as large as possible. Now, we can reconstruct
\mathbf{c}^{k}:[0,T_{k}^{*})\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
by the aforementioned system of
k
algebraic equations. In view of (
3.33
), we thus obtain functions

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\in C^{1}\big([0,T_{k}^{*});\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)\big),\hskip 10.00002pt(\phi_{k},\Psi_{k})\in C^{1}\big([0,T_{k}^{*});{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})\in C^{1}\big([0,T_{k}^{*});{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}\big),

which fulfill the discretized weak formulation (
3.34
) on the time interval
[0,T_{k}^{*})
.

Step 2: Uniform estimates.

We establish suitable estimates for each approximate solution
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k},\phi_{k},\psi_{k},\mu_{k},\theta_{k})
which are uniform with respect to the index
k
.
In particular, let
T_{k}<T_{k}^{*}
be arbitrary. In the following, let
C
denote generic positive constants depending only on the initial data and the constants introduced in (

A2

)–(

A5

) including the final time
T
, but is independent of
k
and
T_{k}
.

Testing (
3.34a
) with
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}
, (
3.34b
) with
(\mu_{k},\theta_{k})
, and (
3.34c
) with
-(\partial_{t}\phi_{k},\partial_{t}\Psi_{k})
, adding the resulting equations, and integrating with respect to time from
0
to
t
,
we derive the discretized energy identity

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t),\phi_{k}(t),\alpha\Psi_{k}(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}2\nu(\phi_{k})\,\left|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\gamma(\alpha\Psi_{k})\left|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}m_{\Omega}(\phi_{k})\left|\nabla\mu_{k}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}n_{\Gamma}(\psi_{k})\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Theta_{k}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S\;\mathrm{d}t\\[4.30554pt]
&=E\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(0),\phi_{k}(0),\alpha\Psi_{k}(0))\end{aligned}

(3.37)

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
. Here,
E
stands for the total energy introduced in (
3.13
).
Due to the growth conditions on
F
and
G
(see (

A3

)), the Sobolev embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Gamma)
for any
1\leq p<\infty
, and the properties of the projections
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{k}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}
,
we use the initial conditions (
3.35
) and (
3.36
) to infer that

\displaystyle E\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(0),\phi_{k}(0),\alpha\Psi_{k}(0))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{k}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mathbb{P}^{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\mathbb{P}^{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+c_{F}\left(|\Omega|+\|\mathbb{P}^{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\right)+c_{G}\left(|\Gamma|+\|\mathbb{P}^{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{L^{q}(\Gamma)}^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\left(1+\|\mathbb{P}^{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{p}\right)+C\left(1+\|\mathbb{P}^{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+C\left(1+\|(\phi_{0},\Psi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{p}\right)+C\left(1+\|(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{q}\right),

which, in turn, entails that

\displaystyle E\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(0),\phi_{k}(0),\alpha\Psi_{k}(0))\leq C.

(3.38)

Recalling that
F,G\geq 0
and that the functions
\nu
,
\gamma
,
m_{\Omega}
and
m_{\Gamma}
(and thus also
n_{\Gamma}
) are uniformly positive (see (

A4

)–(

A5

)), we conclude the uniform bounds

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.39)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.40)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mu_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq C.

(3.41)

Testing (
3.34a
) with the first eigenfunction
(\zeta_{1},\xi_{1})
(see (
3.27
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\alpha\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}(t)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}(t)\;\mathrm{d}S\right)=0,

which implies

\alpha\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}(t)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}(t)\;\mathrm{d}S=\alpha\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\Psi_{0}\;\mathrm{d}S,

(3.42)

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
.
Here, we have used that

\displaystyle\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\langle\mathbb{P}^{\Omega}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\mathbb{P}^{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0})\rangle_{\Gamma}=\big(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\phi_{0},\Psi_{0}),(\alpha,1)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\big((\phi_{0},\Psi_{0}),(\alpha,1)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\Gamma},

which follows by means of (
3.27
) and (
3.28
).
Defining the constant

c_{m}:=\frac{\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi_{0}\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\Psi_{0}\rangle_{\Gamma}}{\alpha^{2}\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|},

we have
(\alpha c_{m},c_{m})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
and invoking (
3.42
), a straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\big(\phi_{k}(t)-\alpha c_{m},\Psi_{k}(t)-c_{m}\big)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi_{k}(t)-\alpha c_{m}\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\Psi_{k}(t)-c_{m}\rangle_{\Gamma}=0

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
.
Hence, applying the bulk-surface variant of the Poincaré inequality (
3.9
) (with
\beta=\alpha^{2}
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\|(\phi_{k},\Psi_{k})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\phi_{k}-\alpha c_{m},\Psi_{k}-c_{m})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\|(\alpha c_{m},c_{m})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P}\|(\nabla\phi_{k},\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+C

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
.
In view of (
3.40
), we thus conclude

\displaystyle\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};H^{1}(\Gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq C.

(3.43)

Next, we derive a uniform estimate for
(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})
in the full
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-norm. For this aim, we test (
3.34c
) by

(\zeta,\xi):=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})
, where
(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
is an arbitrary test function.
By the growth conditions (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) from (

A3

) and the Sobolev embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Gamma)
for any
1\leq p<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left|\big<(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k}),(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\right|&=\left|\big<(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k}),(\zeta,\xi)\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\right|\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\zeta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|F^{\prime}(\phi_{k})\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\Omega)}\|\zeta\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad+\alpha^{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\alpha\|G^{\prime}(\alpha\Psi_{k})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
&\leq C\left(1+\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{5}\right)\|(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\\
&\quad+C\left(1+\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\|\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{2q}(\Gamma)}^{q}\right)\|(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\\
&\leq C\left(1+\|\phi_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{5}+\|\Psi_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{q}\right)\|(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}},\end{aligned}

(3.44)

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
.
Taking the supremum over all
(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
with
\|(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\|_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\leq 1
,
and exploiting (
3.43
), we find that

\|(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime})}\leq C.

(3.45)

By a duality argument, we infer the existence of a positive constant
C_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}
such that

\|(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}=\big<(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi}),(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\leq C_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\|(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime}}\|(\nabla\bar{\zeta},\nabla_{\Gamma}\bar{\xi})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+C_{{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}}\|(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\|_{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{\prime}}^{2}

(3.46)

for all
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
.
Hence, applying (
3.46
) with
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})=(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})
and using (
3.41
) and (
3.45
), we arrive at

\|(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})\|_{L^{4}(0,T_{k};\mathcal{L}^{2})}\leq C.

(3.47)

In addition, we infer from (
3.41
) and (
3.47
) that

\displaystyle\|(\mu_{k},\Theta_{k})\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};\mathcal{H}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C.

(3.48)

Lastly, we derive uniform estimates on the time derivatives. Therefore, let
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
and
(\zeta^{*},\xi^{*})\in{\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}}
be arbitrary test functions. We now test (
3.34a
) with
{\boldsymbol{w}}:=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{k}}({\boldsymbol{w}}^{*})
.

In three dimensions, recalling that the functions
\nu
and
\gamma
are bounded, we use Hölder’s inequality, the continuous embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Gamma)
as well as the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality to obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left|\big<\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\big>_{\mathbf{H}_{\textnormal{div}}^{1}(\Omega)}\right|&=\left|\big<\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{w}}\big>_{\mathbf{H}_{\textnormal{div}}^{1}(\Omega)}\right|\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+\|\mu_{k}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}+C\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
&\qquad+\|\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{4}(\Gamma)}\|\nabla\Theta_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\|_{L^{4}(\Gamma)}\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{3}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+C\|\mu_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+\|\Psi_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\|\Theta_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\end{aligned}

for all
t\in[0,T_{k}]
.
Taking the supremum over all
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
with
\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, taking the power
\frac{4}{3}
and integrating over
[0,T_{k}]
, we now deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T_{k};\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)^{\prime})}^{\frac{4}{3}}&\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{k};L^{2}(\Omega))}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{k};H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\\

Appendix A
A chain rule formula.

Let
\kappa
and
\sigma
be two positive constants. We define the function
\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S,\quad&&\text{if }(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma},\\
&+\infty,\quad&&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(A.1)

Proposition A.1
.

For any
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
and
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
, the function
\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
is convex, lower semicontinuous and proper. The subgradient
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}=\partial\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
is given by

\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi)=(-\Delta\phi,-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma})$},

(A.2)

where

D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa})=\mathcal{H}^{2}\cap\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}.

(A.3)

It is easy to see that
\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
is convex and proper. The lower semicontinuity of
\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
immediately follows from the weak lower semicontinuity of the norm with respect to the weak convergence.

Let us now consider the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}:D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma})\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}
as defined in (
A.2
)–(
A.3
). First of all, we observe that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
is well defined since
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
for any
\phi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.
For any
(\phi,\psi)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma})
and
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi),(\phi,\psi)-(\zeta,\xi))_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\Delta\phi(\phi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}(-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi)(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\phi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi(\phi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\Gamma}\kappa\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S+\int_{\Gamma}\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\phi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}x-\sigma\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\Gamma}\kappa\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S+\sigma\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\phi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\kappa\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}(\psi-\xi)\;\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S.

Thus, we found

(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi),(\phi,\psi)-(\zeta,\xi))_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\geq\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi)-\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\zeta,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}),\ (\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}$.}

The above inequality can be easily extended to

(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi),(\phi,\psi)-(\zeta,\xi))_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\geq\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi)-\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\zeta,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}),\ (\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}$.}

This implies that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\subset\partial\Phi_{\kappa}
, i.e.,
D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa})\subset D(\partial\Phi_{\kappa})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}(\phi,\psi)\in\partial\Phi_{\kappa}(\phi,\psi)
for all
(\phi,\psi)\in D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa})
.

In order to conclude that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}=\partial\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
, we are left to show that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}
is maximal monotone in
\mathcal{L}^{2}\times\mathcal{L}^{2}
, namely
R(I+A_{\kappa}^{\sigma})=\mathcal{L}^{2}
. For this purpose, let us fix
(f,g)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and consider the equation
(\phi,\psi)+\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}(\phi,\psi)=(f,g)
, which reads as

\displaystyle\phi-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(A.4a)

\displaystyle\psi-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(A.4b)

\displaystyle\phi|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\sigma\psi

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

(A.4c)

A pair
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
is a corresponding weak solution if

\big((\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\kappa\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S=\big((f,g),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

(A.5)

for all
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
.
The existence and uniqueness of
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
solving (
A.5
) follows directly from the Lax-Milgram theorem. Next, using regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
40
, Theorem 3.3]
), where
(f-\phi,g-\psi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
is interpreted as the source term,
we infer that
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
and (
A.4
) holds almost everywhere. This entails the desired conclusion. ∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
and
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
. Assume that
(\phi,\psi)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma})\cap H^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{\prime})
such that
(-\Delta\phi,-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma})
. Then, the chain rule formula

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\right]=\big<(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi),(-\Delta\phi,-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi)\big>_{\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}}

(A.6)

holds for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

We define the function
\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(\phi,\psi)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\phi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{2}|\psi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S,\quad&&\text{if }(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2},\\
&+\infty,\quad&&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(A.7)

It is easily seen that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is convex, lower semicontinuous and proper. In addition, the subgradient
\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}
,
\mathcal{B}(\phi,\psi)=(\phi,\psi)
is Lipschitz in
\mathcal{H}^{0}
. Let us now consider

\Phi^{\star}=\Phi_{\kappa}^{\sigma}+\widetilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, which clearly is a convex, lower semicontinuous, proper functional. Moreover, we have

\Phi^{\star}(\phi,\psi)\geq\widetilde{\Phi}(\phi,\psi)=\|(\phi,\psi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}$.}

Thanks to
[
51
, Lemma 2.1, Chapter IV]
, we infer that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}+\mathcal{B}
is a maximal monotone operator with domain

D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma}+\mathcal{B})=D(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}^{\sigma})
, which coincides with
\partial\Phi^{\star}
. In light of our assumptions, we recall that

(\phi,\psi)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma})\cap H^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{\prime})
. Then, since
\phi|_{\Gamma}=\sigma\psi
on
\Gamma
, we observe that

\partial\Phi^{\star}(\phi,\psi)=(-\Delta\phi+\phi,-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi+\psi)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}).

Therefore, we are in position to apply
[
48
, Lemma 4.1]
, which entails that

is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
and the chain rule

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\right]\\
&\quad=\big<(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi),(-\Delta\phi+\phi,-\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\sigma\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi+\psi)\big>_{\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}}\end{split}

(A.8)

holds almost everywhere in
[0,T]
.
On the other hand, by the classical chain rule formula (or, alternatively, by using
[
48
, Lemma 4.1]
with
\mathcal{J}=\widetilde{\Phi}
), we know that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\right]=\big<(\partial_{t}\phi,\partial_{t}\psi),(\phi,\psi)\big>_{\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}}

(A.9)

almost everywhere in
[0,T]
