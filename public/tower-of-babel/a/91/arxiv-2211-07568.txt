A block–diagonal form for four–component operators describing graphene quantum dots
Rafael D. Benguria 1, Edgardo Stockmeyer 2, Cristóbal Vallejos 3, Hanne Van Den Bosch 4

Abstract
. We consider four–component Dirac operators on domains in the plane. With suitable boundary conditions, these operators describe graphene quantum dots. The most general boundary conditions are defined by a matrix depending on four real parameters. For operators with constant boundary parameters we show that the Hamiltonian is unitary equivalent to two copies of the two–component operator. This allows to extend the known results for this type of operators to the four–component case. As an application, we identify the boundary conditions from the tight–binding model for graphene that give rise to a block–diagonal operator in the continuum limit.



1.
Introduction

Low energy electronic excitations in graphene are described by a massless Dirac operator acting on four–component spinors
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
The four components take into account a degree of freedom for each of the points in the unit celll of the honeycomb lattice, sometimes called pseudospin, and a degree of freedom for quasiparticles with momenta near the unequivalent Dirac points at the corners of the hexagonal Brillouin zone, the so-called valleys. In the valley–isotropic representation, the Hamiltonian describing these excitations is a direct sum of two two-dimensional Dirac operators, so we define the differential expression

\displaystyle H=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix T & 0 \\ 0 & T\endlx@physics@matrix)\,,\quad T=-i\bm{\sigma}\cdot\gradient=-i\,\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & \partial_1-i\partial_2 \\ \partial_1+i\partial_2 & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

(1)

Here, we write
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})^{\top}
where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are the first two Pauli matrices and we use the usual representation,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0&1 \\ 1&0\endlx@physics@matrix)\,,\quad\sigma_{2}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & -i \\ i &0\endlx@physics@matrix)\,,\quad\sigma_{3}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1 &0 \\ 0 & -1\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

When describing electrons confined to a piece of graphene with boundary, suitable boundary conditions must be imposed. Three of these are commonly used in the physics literature: the so-called zigzag, armchair, and infinite mass boundary conditions. The choice of boundary conditions is relevant both from a physical and a mathematical point of view. From the mathematical point of view, they determine the regularity of spinors in the domain of the Hamiltonian and its spectrum. The spectrum and the related transport properties determine the behaviour of the graphene quantum dot when used, for instance, as a single electron transistor.

T

In the first part of this article, we study the most general family of local boundary conditions for the four–component operator given, for instance, in

In the second part of this article, we specialize to the case of a terminated honeycomb lattice and study the boundary conditions there. For edges perpendicular to the carbon bonds, a block diagonal operator with

Set up and boundary conditions.

Throughout this paper,
\Omega

domain. For each point

Such a boundary matrix takes the form

m_{\eta}(s):=\cos\eta\,\quantity(\bm\sigma\cdot\bm t(s))+\sin\eta\,\sigma_{3}\,.

(2)

Here, we write

\operatorname{Dom}(T_{\eta}):=\{\phi\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{2})\,|\,m_{\eta}\phi=\phi\text{ at }\partial\Omega\}\,.

The following result follows from
[

]

[

]

Theorem
.

The operator

. In the case

(resp.
\operatorname{Dom}^{\rm max}(\partial_{z*})\oplus H^{1}_{0}
).

For the graphene Dirac operator

2.
A Unitary Transformation and Its Consequences

Proposition 2.1
.

For
\vec{\bm{\nu}}=(\cos\phi_{\nu}\cos\theta_{\nu},\sin\phi_{\nu}\cos\theta_{\nu},\sin\theta_{\nu})^{\top}
, define

U_{\vec{\bm{\nu}}}:=\exp(i\frac{\theta_\nu}{2}\sigma_2)\exp(i\frac{\phi_\nu}{2}\sigma_3)\otimes\sigma_{0}.

Then

\displaystyle U_{\vec{\bm{\nu}}}M_{\Gamma}U_{\vec{\bm{\nu}}}^{*}=m_{\eta_{+}}\oplus m_{\eta_{-}},\quad\text{ and }\quad U_{\vec{\bm{\nu}}}H_{\Gamma}U_{\vec{\bm{\nu}}}^{*}=T_{\eta_{+}}\oplus T_{\eta_{-}},

with
\eta_{\pm}:=-\Theta\pm\quantity(\pi/2-\Lambda)
.

Proof.

We will frequently use the property

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD).

As the first step, we consider the matrix

\displaystyle U_{\phi_{\nu}}:=e^{i\frac{\phi_{\nu}}{2}\sigma_{3}}\otimes\sigma_{0}\,

defining a unitary transformation.
This transformation can be interpreted as a clockwise rotation of the

The first term of

is invariant under this transformation, while for the second term, we have that

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\phi_{\nu}}{2}\sigma_{3}}(\vec{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot\vec{\bm{\nu}})e^{-i\frac{\phi_{\nu}}{2}\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix e^{i\frac{\phi_\nu}{2}} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\frac{\phi_\nu}{2}}\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\cos\theta_\nu& \sin\theta_\nu e^{-i\phi_\nu} \\ \sin\theta_\nu e^{i\phi_\nu} & -\cos\theta_\nu\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix e^{-i\frac{\phi_\nu}{2}} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\frac{\phi_\nu}{2}}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\cos\theta_\nu& \sin\theta_\nu\\ \sin\theta_\nu& -\cos\theta_\nu\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

One could therefore restrict our parameters to the case
\phi_{\nu}=0
, i.e., confining

to the
(x,z)
plane. Now, we write

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\cos\theta_\nu& \sin\theta_\nu\\ \sin\theta_\nu& -\cos\theta_\nu\endlx@physics@matrix)=\cos\theta_{\nu}\,\sigma_{3}+\sin\theta_{\nu}\,\sigma_{1}=e^{-i\frac{\theta_{\nu}}{2}\sigma_{2}}\sigma_{3}e^{i\frac{\theta_{\nu}}{2}\sigma_{2}}\,,

which motivates the definition

. This matrix defines a unitary transformation that leaves the first term of

invariant and it transforms the second term of

into the case

(i.e.,

).

After the two transformations, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{\theta_{\nu}}U_{\phi_{\nu}}M_{\Gamma}U_{\phi_{\nu}}^{\ast}U_{\theta_{\nu}}^{\ast}=\sin\Lambda\,\quantity(\sigma_0 \otimes(\vec{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot\vec{\bm{n}}_1 ))+\cos\Lambda\,\quantity( \sigma_3\otimes(\vec{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot\vec{\bm{n}}_2 ))=m_{\eta_{+}}\oplus m_{\eta_{-}}\,.

Using the parameterization of

\displaystyle m_{\eta_{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\sin\Lambda\cos\Theta\pm\cos\Lambda\sin\Theta)(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{t})+\quantity(-\sin\Lambda\sin\Theta\pm\cos\Lambda\cos\Theta)\sigma_{3}

.
Finally, the differential expression

is invariant under the transformation

, which maps

onto
\operatorname{Dom}(T_{\eta_{+}})\oplus\operatorname{Dom}(T_{\eta_{-}})

Boundary Conditions for Armchair Edges

In this section, we study boundary conditions arising from the tight-binding model for a terminated honeycomb lattice.
Our goal is to obtain the boundary condition that holds in the discrete setting and express it in the parametric form
M_{\Gamma}

Then, in a formal scaling limit, the tight–binding operator on the domain under consideration converges to a Dirac operator with this boundary condition.

i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, with
\bm{\vec{c}_{i}}=(c_{i1},c_{i2},c_{i3})^{\top}

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}(\sigma_{i}\otimes\bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{\vec{c}}_{i})=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot(\bm{\vec{c}}_0 + \bm{\vec{c}}_3) & \bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot(\bm{\vec{c}}_1 - i\bm{\vec{c}}_2) \\

Using the relation (
A.3a
), we explicitly obtain that

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \sigma_0\qty((\bm{\vec{c}}_0 + \bm{\vec{c}}_3)^2 + \bm{\vec{c}}_1^{\ 2} + \bm{\vec{c}}_2^{\ 2} ) +2 \bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot(\bm{\vec{c}}_1\times\bm{\vec{c}}_2)&

The condition

implies that
\bm{\vec{c}}_{0}=c_{0}\bm{\vec{n}}_{1}
is orthogonal to

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma}=\sin\Lambda\,\quantity(\sigma_0 \otimes(\bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{\vec{n}}_1 ))+\cos\Lambda\,\quantity( (\bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{\vec{\nu}} ) \otimes\, (\bm{\vec{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{\vec{n}}_2 ))\,,

Appendix B
Derivation of the Dirac Equation

We use the conventions introduced in Figure
1
. Integer indices
n,m
label each unit cell, the position of its centre is defined as
\bm{r}_{nm}:=n\bm{a}_{1}+m\bm{a}_{2}
.
In a scaling limit,
\bm{r}_{nm}
becomes a continuous variable and therefore it is convenient to write the discrete wavefunction at a lattice site as
\psi_{A}(\bm{r})
and
\psi_{B}(\bm{r})
. The tight–binding Hamiltonian at a site
A(B)
depends on the sum of the wave–function at its nearest neighbours on the
B(A)
–sublattice.

\displaystyle\left(H_{\mathrm{t.b.}}\psi\right)_{A}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=t\quantity(\psi_B(\bm{r}) + \psi_B(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_1)+\psi_B(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_2))\,,

\displaystyle\left(H_{\mathrm{t.b.}}\psi\right)_{B}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=t\quantity(\psi_A(\bm{r}) + \psi_A(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_1)+\psi_A(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_2))\,.

The energies are given by
\pm\absolutevalue{f(\bm{k})}
, with
f(\bm{k})=t\quantity(1+e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_2}+e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{a}_1})
and
\bm{k}
in the first Brillouin zone (FBZ).

The restriction to low energies amounts to replacing each of these wave–functions by plane waves with momenta
\pm\bm{K}
, which are the so–called Dirac points in the FBZ, defined as the wave–vectors where the energy vanishes:
f(\pm\bm{K})=0
. To simplify the calculations, we have chosen the non–equivalent Dirac points as the two corners of the FBZ lying in the vertical axis (see Figure
1
):
\xi\bm{K}=\xi\quantity(0,-\frac{4\pi}{3a})^{\top}
, where
\xi=\pm
is the valley index. Thus, the Ansatz for the wavefunction
\psi_{X}
becomes

\displaystyle\psi_{X}(\bm{r})=e^{i\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\Psi_{X}^{+}(\bm{r})+e^{-i\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\Psi_{X}^{-}(\bm{r}),\quad X=A,B.

Replacing the above in the tight–binding Hamiltonian at a site
A
, we get

\displaystyle\left(H_{\mathrm{t.b.}}\psi\right)_{A}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=t\sum\limits_{\xi=\pm}e^{i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\left(\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r})+e^{-i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{a_{1}}}\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_{1})+e^{-i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{a_{2}}}\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_{2})\right)\,.

Next, we aproximate
\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r}-\bm{a}_{j})
by its first–order Taylor expansion. The constant terms vanish by the definition of the Dirac points, and we are left with

\displaystyle\left(H_{\mathrm{t.b.}}\psi\right)_{A}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\approx-t\sum\limits_{\xi=\pm}e^{i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\left(e^{-i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{a_{1}}}\bm{a}_{1}+e^{-i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{a_{2}}}\bm{a}_{2}\right)\cdot\gradient\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r})\,,

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}ta\sum\limits_{\xi=\pm}e^{i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\quantity( \partial_1-i\xi\partial_2 )\Psi_{B}^{\xi}(\bm{r})\,.

In the last line we used that
\bm{a}_{1}=a/2(\sqrt{3},1)^{\top}
and
\bm{a}_{2}=a/2(\sqrt{3},-1)^{\top}
. This expression leads to the definition
v_{F}=\sqrt{3}\,ta/2
, the Fermi velocity in graphene. By symmetry of the operator (or the analogous computation), for a
B
-site we obtain

\displaystyle\left(H_{\mathrm{t.b.}}\psi\right)_{B}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\approx v_{F}\sum\limits_{\xi=\pm}e^{i\xi\bm{K}\cdot\bm{r}}\quantity( \partial_1+i\xi\partial_2 )\Psi_{A}^{\xi}(\bm{r})\,.

Thus, upon defining the spinor
\Psi(\mathbf{r})=\quantity(\Psi^{+}_A(\bm{r}),-i\Psi^{+}_B(\bm{r}),i\Psi^{-}_B(\bm{r}),-\Psi^{-}_A(\bm{r}))^{\top}
in terms of the four amplitudes, we obtain the effective Hamiltonian that acts as

\displaystyle H\Psi(\mathbf{r})=-iv_{F}\quantity(\sigma_0 \otimes(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\grad))\Psi(\mathbf{r})\,.

Finally, we set
v_{F}=1
to recover equation (
1
).
