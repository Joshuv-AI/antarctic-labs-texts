Abstract
. Spectral properties of Schrödinger operators on compact metric graphs are studied and special emphasis is put on differences in the spectral behavior between different classes of vertex conditions. We survey recent results especially for δ \delta and δ ′ \delta^{\prime} couplings and demonstrate the spectral properties on many examples. Amongst other things, properties of the ground state eigenvalue and eigenfunction and the spectral behavior under various perturbations of the metric graph or the vertex conditions are considered.

In this survey we review different aspects of the spectral theory for self-adjoint Schrödinger operators on metric graphs. While some of the results presented here are valid for general self-adjoint vertex conditions, in many cases we put emphasis on the comparison between
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
vertex conditions. Among other things, we focus on the effect of surgery principles. These may be divided basically into two groups: on the one hand manipulations which change the total length of the graph such as

•

increasing the length of one or all edges,

•

adding a new edge or attaching another graph,

•

inserting another graph into a vertex,

•

shrinking some edge lengths to zero.

And on the other hand surgical operations which preserve the total length of the graph, for instance

•

changing the coupling condition or its strength at a vertex,

•

joining vertices,

•

unfolding parallel or pendant edges.

In addition, we study properties of the ground state eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenfunction, which especially are of some use to the above-named surgery principles. Moreover, we discuss bounds for the lowest eigenvalue of the Laplacian with
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
vertex conditions, where some of the above surgery principles can be of use.

This article has mostly the character of a survey. Many of the results come from recent literature in this or a similar form, though we in some cases provide slight generalizations and modified proofs. However, we complement these results by some modest additions such as Theorem
4.13
or Theorem
5.4
and a bunch of illuminating examples and counterexamples. In addition, we formulate some open questions.

Let us outline the content of this article. In Section
2

3

\delta

\delta^{\prime}

2.
Schrödinger operators on metric graphs

2.1.
Metric graphs

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph constituted by a finite set of vertices
{\mathcal{V}}:={\mathcal{V}}(\Gamma)
, a finite set of edges
{\mathcal{E}}:={\mathcal{E}}(\Gamma)
and a length function
L\colon{\mathcal{E}}\to(0,\infty)
. Upon parametrizing each edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
by the interval
[0,L(e)]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and considering
\Gamma
as the one-dimensional simplicial complex consisting of the intervals
[0,L(e)]
,
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
, with corresponding coupling at the vertices,
\Gamma
becomes a compact metric space, and we will refer to such
\Gamma
as a compact metric graph. For each vertex
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
we denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{v}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
the set of edges incident to
v
and by
\deg(v)
the degree of
v
. We write
v=o(e)
or
v=t(e)
and say that the edge
e
originates from or terminates at the vertex
v
, respectively, if
v
corresponds to the endpoint
0
respectively
L(e)
according to our parametrization.
We will denote by

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Gamma):=\bigoplus_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}L^{2}(0,L(e))

the usual
L^{2}
space on
\Gamma
, and by

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{k}(\Gamma):=\bigoplus_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}H^{k}(0,L(e))

the
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space of oder
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
and
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
we write
f_{e}
for the restriction of
f
to the edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
(i.e. the interval
(0,L(e))
corresponding to
e
).

2.2.
Schrödinger operators on metric graphs

In this paper we focus on self-adjoint Schrödinger operators in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
subject to self-adjoint coupling conditions on the vertices; that is, we consider Schrödinger operators in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
acting as

\displaystyle({\mathcal{L}}f)_{e}:=-f_{e}^{\prime\prime}+q_{e}f_{e},\quad e\in{\mathcal{E}},

(2.1)

with a real-valued potential
q=(q_{e})_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}
, where we assume that
q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. This assumption is made for reasons of simplicity; many of the results discussed in this survey extend naturally to larger classes of potentials such as
L^{1}(\Gamma)
.

Next we specify vertex conditions with which
{\mathcal{L}}
will constitute a self-adjoint operator. For
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{l}\}:=\{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{v}:\;v=o(e)\}
and
\{e_{l+1},\dots e_{m}\}:=\{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{v}:\;v=t(e)\}
be enumerations of the sets of edges originating from and terminating at
v
, respectively. Note that, in the special case that an edge
e
is a loop, i.e.
o(e)=t(e)
,
e
will belong to both sets. For
f\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
we write

\displaystyle F(v):=\begin{pmatrix}f_{e_{1}}(0)\\
\vdots\\
f_{e_{l}}(0)\\
f_{e_{l+1}}(L(e_{l+1}))\\
\vdots\\
f_{e_{m}}(L(e_{m}))\end{pmatrix}

for the collection of boundary values of the restriction of
f
to the adjacent edges at
v
. Moreover, for
f\in\widetilde{H}^{2}(\Gamma)
we write

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(v):=\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}_{e_{1}}(0)\\
\vdots\\
f^{\prime}_{e_{l}}(0)\\
-f^{\prime}_{e_{l+1}}(L(e_{l+1}))\\
\vdots\\
-f^{\prime}_{e_{m}}(L(e_{m}))\end{pmatrix},

for the collection of derivatives of
f
at
v
in the direction pointing out of
v
into the edges. The following description of all self-adjoint incarnations of
{\mathcal{L}}
in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
with local coupling conditions is standard.

Proposition 2.1

(e.g.
[
14
, Theorem 1.4.4]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph,
q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
be real-valued and
{\mathcal{L}}
be the Schrödinger differential expression in (
2.1
). For each vertex
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
let
P_{v,\rm D},P_{v,\rm N}
and
P_{v,\rm R}
be orthogonal projections in
\mathbb{C}^{\deg(v)}
with mutually orthogonal ranges such that
P_{v,\rm D}+P_{v,\rm N}+P_{v,\rm R}=I
and let
\Lambda_{v}
be a self-adjoint, invertible operator in
{\text{\rm ran\,}}P_{v,\rm R}
.Then the operator
H
in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
given by

\displaystyle Hf

\displaystyle:={\mathcal{L}}f,

\displaystyle{\text{\rm dom\,}}H

\displaystyle:=\Big\{f\in\widetilde{H}^{2}(\Gamma):P_{v,\rm D}F(v)=0,P_{v,\rm N}F^{\prime}(v)=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad P_{v,\rm R}F^{\prime}(v)=\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v)~\text{for each}~v\in{\mathcal{V}}\Big\},

is self-adjoint (and each self-adjoint realization of
{\mathcal{L}}
in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
subject to local coupling conditions can be written in this form). Furthermore, the closed quadratic form
h
corresponding to the operator
H
in the sense of, e.g.,
[
34
, Chapter VI, Theorem 2.1]
is given by

\displaystyle h(f)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma}|f^{\prime}|^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}q|f|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\big\langle\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v),P_{v,\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle,

\displaystyle{\text{\rm dom\,}}h

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma):P_{v,\rm D}F(v)=0~\text{for each}~v\in{\mathcal{V}}\right\}.

By a standard compact embedding argument the spectrum of the Hamiltonian
H
on the compact metric graph
\Gamma
is always purely discrete and bounded from below, see, e.g.,
[
48
, Corollary 10 and Theorem 18]
. We denote by

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(H)\leq\lambda_{2}(H)\leq\dots

the eigenvalues of
H
in non-decreasing order and counted with multiplicities. If
q\geq 0
and
\Lambda_{v}
is non-negative for each vertex
v
then all eigenvalues are non-negative. We will frequently make use of the fact that these eigenvalues may be expressed via the min-max principle

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(H)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq{\text{\rm dom\,}}h\\
\dim F=k\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in F\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{h(f)}{\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}}

for
k=1,2,\dots
.

We will put special emphasis on so-called
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
coupling conditions and their special variants, namely continuity-Kirchhoff and anti-Kirchhoff conditions. To specify those within the framework of Proposition
2.1
, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}:={\mathcal{P}}_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{d}&\dots&\frac{1}{d}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{d}&\dots&\frac{1}{d}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{Q}}:={\mathcal{Q}}_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{d-1}{d}&-\frac{1}{d}&\dots&-\frac{1}{d}\\
-\frac{1}{d}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&-\frac{1}{d}\\
-\frac{1}{d}&\dots&-\frac{1}{d}&\frac{d-1}{d}\end{pmatrix}

the orthogonal projections onto
\spann\{(1,1,\dots,1)^{\top}\}
and onto its orthogonal complement
(\spann\{(1,1,\dots,1)^{\top}\})^{\perp}
, respectively.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
d:=\deg(v)>0
.
Then the vertex conditions at
v
are called

(a)

continuity-Kirchhoff conditions
if
P_{v,\rm D}={\mathcal{Q}}
,
P_{v,\rm N}={\mathcal{P}}
and
P_{v,\rm R}=0
;

(b)

\delta
coupling conditions
with
strength

\alpha_{v}\in\mathbb{R}
if
P_{v,\rm D}={\mathcal{Q}}
,
P_{v,\rm N}=0
,
P_{v,\rm R}={\mathcal{P}}
and
\Lambda_{v}
is the multiplication by
\frac{\alpha_{v}}{d}
;

(c)

anti-Kirchhoff conditions
if
P_{v,\rm N}={\mathcal{Q}}
,
P_{v,\rm D}={\mathcal{P}}
and
P_{v,\rm R}=0
;

(d)

\delta^{\prime}
coupling conditions
with
strength

\beta_{v}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
if
P_{v,\rm N}={\mathcal{Q}}
,
P_{v,\rm D}=0
,
P_{v,\rm R}={\mathcal{P}}
and
\Lambda_{v}
is the multiplication by
\frac{d}{\beta_{v}}
.

The vertex conditions given in Definition
2.2
can be written more explicitly. In fact,
f
satisfies
\delta
vertex conditions with strength
\alpha_{v}
at a vertex
v
if and only if
F(v)
is equal to a constant vector, whose value we denote
f(v)
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}F_{j}^{\prime}(v)=\alpha_{v}f(v).

In particular, continuity-Kirchhoff coupling conditions equal
\delta
coupling conditions with strength
\alpha_{v}=0
. On the other hand, formally setting
\alpha_{v}=\infty
results in a Dirichlet condition at
v
, i.e.
f(v)=0
.

Furthermore,
f
satisfies
\delta^{\prime}
coupling conditions with strength
\beta_{v}
at
v
if and only if
F^{\prime}(v)
is equal to a constant vector, whose value we denote
f^{\prime}(v)
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{d}F_{j}(v)=\beta_{v}f^{\prime}(v).

In particular, an anti-Kirchhoff coupling condition can be interpreted as a
\delta^{\prime}
coupling condition with strength
\beta_{v}=0
. Formally setting
\beta_{v}=\infty
results in a Neumann condition at
v
, i.e.
f^{\prime}(v)=0
.

For
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
couplings, the vertex term in the quadratic form
h
in Proposition
2.1
looks as follows.

Lemma 2.3

(e.g.
[
69
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph, let
H
be a self-adjoint Schrödinger operator in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
as in Proposition
2.1
, and let
h
be the corresponding quadratic form. Furthermore, let
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
and let the vertex conditions for
H
at
v
be given in terms of
P_{v,\rm D},P_{v,\rm N},P_{v,\rm R}
and
\Lambda_{v}
.
Then the following assertions hold for each
f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}h
.

(i)

If a continuity-Kirchhoff condition is imposed at
v
, then
f
is continuous at
v
and

\displaystyle\big\langle\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v),P_{v,\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle=0.

(ii)

If a
\delta
coupling condition with strength
\alpha_{v}
is imposed at
v
, then
f
is continuous at
v
and

\displaystyle\big\langle\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v),P_{v,\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle=\alpha_{v}|f(v)|^{2}.

(iii)

If an anti-Kirchhoff condition is imposed at
v
, then
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}F_{j}(v)=0
and

\displaystyle\big\langle\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v),P_{v,\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle=0.

(iv)

If a
\delta^{\prime}
coupling condition with strength
\beta_{v}
is imposed at
v
, then
f
does not satisfy any vertex conditions at
v
and

\displaystyle\big\langle\Lambda_{v}P_{v,\rm R}F(v),P_{v,\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle=\frac{1}{\beta_{v}}\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}F_{j}(v)\bigg|^{2}.

3.
Properties of the ground state

In this section we review properties of the first eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenfunctions in case that the same coupling conditions are imposed at each vertex, either
\delta
or
\delta^{\prime}
.

3.1.
Positivity of the first eigenfunction

Here we discuss Perron-Frobenius (or Courant) type properties of quantum graphs with
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
vertex conditions. For, e.g., Schrödinger operators on Euclidean domains with suitable boundary conditions, it is well known that the first eigenvalue has multiplicity one and the corresponding eigenfunction can be chosen positive. This turns out to be correct for
\delta
coupling conditions, but is no longer true for
\delta^{\prime}
conditions, as we will see below.

The following theorem corresponds to
[
21
, Theorem 3.2]
, see also
[
52
, Corollary 1]
; for the convenience of the reader, we provide a proof.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected compact metric graph, let
q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
be real-valued and let
H
be the Schrödinger operator on
\Gamma
defined in Proposition
2.1
and assume that
\delta
coupling conditions are imposed at all vertices. Then the eigenspace of
H
corresponding to the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(H)
is one-dimensional. Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunction does not have any zero in
\Gamma
and, hence, can be chosen positive.

Proof.

Let
f\in\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))
,
f\neq 0
, and let
g:=|f|
. Then
|g^{\prime}|\leq|f^{\prime}|
and therefore

\displaystyle h(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}|g^{\prime}|^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}q|g|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{v}|g(v)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma}|f^{\prime}|^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}q|f|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{v}|f(v)|^{2}=h(f),

and as
f
minimizes the Rayleigh quotient, the same is true for
g
. Hence
g
is a non-trivial element of
\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))
; in particular,
g
satisfies
\delta
coupling conditions. Note that
g
does not vanish on any vertex. Indeed,
g(0)=0
and the coupling conditions imply

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}G_{j}^{\prime}(v)=0,

and since
g
vanishes at
v
and is nonnegative everywhere, it follows
g_{e}^{\prime}(v)=0
for each edge
e
incident to
v
. In particular,
g
vanishes on each edge incident to
v
, and it follows
g(w)=0
for each vertex
w
which is connected to
v
by an edge. Successively the same argument yields
g=0
identically on
\Gamma
as
\Gamma
is connected, a contradiction. As every non-vertex point on
\Gamma
can be interpreted as a vertex of degree two equipped with
\delta
conditions of strength zero, it follows that
g
has no zero on
\Gamma
, and this implies that the arbitrarily chosen eigenfunction
f
has no zero.

Finally, having two linearly independent eigenfunctions
f,k
would imply that for any chosen
x_{0}\in\Gamma
the function

\displaystyle f-\frac{f(x_{0})}{k(x_{0})}k

belongs to
\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))
, is nontrivial and vanishes at
x_{0}
, a contradiction. Thus
\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))
is one-dimensional.
∎

The case of
\delta^{\prime}
coupling conditions is fundamentally different, as the following example shows.

Example 3.2
.

Consider a 3-star graph formed of three copies of the interval
[0,1]
coupled to each other at their zero endpoints, see Figure
1
.

0

Figure 1.
The equilateral 3-star graph.

If we impose Neumann boundary conditions at the degree-one vertices (which can be read as
\delta^{\prime}
conditions with strength
\infty
) and a
\delta^{\prime}
coupling condition with a strength
\beta\geq 0
at the degree-three vertex, then the corresponding quadratic form for the Laplacian
H
is non-negative, i.e.
\lambda_{1}(H)\geq 0
. Moreover, the function
f
which is constantly
1
on one of the edges,
-1
on another and zero on the remaining edge satisfies all imposed vertex conditions. As
f^{\prime\prime}=0
on every edge, we have
f\in\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))=\ker H
. But
f
is positive on one edge and negative on another, that is, there exist ground state eigenfunctions without fixed sign, and they can even be constantly zero on a whole edge. Furthermore, by interchanging the roles of the edges, one sees that the eigenspace is two-dimensional.

For the study of a larger class of vertex conditions that allow for uniqueness and positivity of the ground state eigenfunction we refer the reader to
[

]
.

3.2.
Existence of negative eigenvalues

For a quantum graph with
\delta
or
\delta^{\prime}
vertex conditions, it is not necessarily true that existence of one negative coupling coefficient implies that the lowest eigenvalue is negative; cf. Example
3.6
below. In the following, sufficient conditions for existence of a negative eigenvalue are provided.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph and
H
a self-adjoint Schrödinger operator on
\Gamma
as defined in Proposition
2.1
. Assume that at each vertex
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
a
\delta
coupling condition of strength
\alpha_{v}
is imposed. If

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}q+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{v}<0,

then
\lambda_{1}(H)<0
.

Proof.

By plugging the constant function
f=1
into the quadratic form we get

\displaystyle h(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}|f^{\prime}|^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}q|f|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{v}|f(v)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}q+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\alpha_{v}<0.

This implies
\lambda_{1}(H)<0
.
∎

For
\delta^{\prime}
couplings, a more localized condition for existence of negative eigenvalues can be given.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph and
H
a self-adjoint Schrödinger operator on
\Gamma
as defined in Proposition
2.1
. Assume that at each vertex
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
a
\delta^{\prime}
coupling condition of strength
\beta_{v}
is imposed. If for some edge
e_{0}
,

\displaystyle\int_{e_{0}}q+\frac{1}{\beta_{o(e_{0})}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{t(e_{0})}}<0,

then
\lambda_{1}(H)<0
.

Proof.

This time we may use the function
f
being constantly equal to one on
e_{0}
and zero otherwise. Then
f
belongs to
{\text{\rm dom\,}}h
and

\displaystyle h(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}|f^{\prime}|^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}q|f|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\frac{1}{\beta_{v}}\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}F_{j}(v)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{e_{0}}q+\frac{1}{\beta_{o(e_{0})}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{t(e_{0})}}<0.

Thus
\lambda_{1}(H)<0
.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected compact metric graph and
H
the Laplacian (i.e. the Schrödinger operator with constant zero potential) on
\Gamma
with
\delta^{\prime}
coupling conditions at each vertex
v
, where
\beta_{v}=1
for all
v\neq v_{0}
and
\beta_{v_{0}}=-1/2
, for a selected vertex
v_{0}
. If
e_{0}
is any edge incident to
v_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{o(e_{0})}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{t(e_{0})}}=-2+1<0.

Therefore, Theorem
3.4
yields
\lambda_{1}(H)<0
.

The following example shows that the analogous condition for
\delta
vertex conditions is not sufficient for existence of negative eigenvalues.

Example 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be any compact metric graph. Consider again the case
q=0
constantly, and suppose that at each vertex
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
a
\delta
coupling condition of strength
\alpha_{v}
is imposed. Assume that for some

\alpha_{v_{0}}<0

are all positive. Our aim is to show that

depends continuously on the coupling coefficients,

We finally point out that the number of negative eigenvalues of Schrödinger operators on metric graphs with general self-adjoint vertex conditions was studied in

.

4.
Graph manipulations changing the total length of the graph

In the following we review the effect of certain geometric manipulations of a given metric graph onto the spectrum of a Schrödinger operator on the graph. We start with manipulations which change the total length of the metric graph

. These include increasing or shrinking the length of one or all edges, attaching new edges or graphs, or inserting a graph at a vertex.

4.1.
Increasing the length of one edge

Here, we consider the change of the eigenvalues in case we increase the length of one edge. For the Laplacian with continuity-Kirchhoff,
\delta
or Dirichlet vertex conditions, the following theorem can be found in
[

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph,

real-valued and

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle h(f)\leq\lambda_{k}(H)\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}\quad\text{for all}~f\in F.

on

4.2.

Here, we consider the change of the eigenvalues in case we increase the length of all edges simultaneously. Iterating the results in Subsection

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}\int_{0}^{tL(e)}|\widetilde{f}^{\prime}|^{2}+\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}\int_{0}^{tL(e)}\widetilde{q}_{e}|\widetilde{f}_{e}|^{2}+\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\big\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{v}P_{v,\rm R}\widetilde{F}(v),P_{v,\rm R}\widetilde{F}(v)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\beta_{v}}\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v)}\widetilde{F}_{j}(v)&\text{for a $\delta^{\prime}$ condition at $v$},\\
\alpha_{v}|f(v)|^{2}&\text{for a $\delta$ condition at $v$}.\end{cases}

Note that

\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\widehat{q}|\widetilde{f}|^{2}\begin{cases}=0&\text{for a $\delta^{\prime}$ condition at $v$},\\
\leq 0&\text{for a $\delta$ condition at $v$}.\end{cases}

Moreover, note that the vertex terms at vertices
w\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{\widehat{v}\}
are

\langle\widehat{\Lambda}_{w}\widehat{P}_{w,\rm R}\widehat{F}(w),\widehat{P}_{w,\rm R}F(w)\rangle\begin{cases}=0&\text{for a $\delta^{\prime}$ condition at $v$},\\
\leq 0&\text{for a $\delta$ condition at $v$},\end{cases}

according to the assumptions of the theorem. Thus,

\widetilde{h}(\widetilde{f})\leq h(f)\leq\lambda_{k}(H)\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}\leq\lambda_{k}(H)\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}|\widetilde{f}|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{F}.

The min-max principle yields the assertion.
∎

4.6.
Shrinking edge lengths to zero

Here we review some results from
[
15
]
, where shrinkage of edge lengths to zero has been considered. More precisely, in
[
15
]
the natural limit vertex conditions when some edge lengths shrink to zero were identified, and convergence of the corresponding Schrödinger operators was shown under certain conditions. For simplicity of presentation we do restrict ourselves here to the potential-free case of the Laplacian,
q=0
identically.

We change now slightly the perspective in the following sense. Up to now, we viewed Schrödinger operators on metric graphs as having coupling conditions at the vertices, where the boundary values of functions came from all edges adjacent to a given vertex. Now, we focus on each edge separately such that functions in
\widetilde{H}^{2}
have two boundary values on each edge for the function as well as for the (inward) derivative, one for the left endpoint and one for the right endpoint, respectively. Since the sum of the vertex degrees is twice the number of edges for each graph, taking all boundary values at all vertices corresponds to taking all boundary values for all edges, so this change of perspective just yields a reordering of the boundary values (now sorted by edges and not by vertices).

In order to formulate a variant of the main result of
[
15
]
, some notation has to be introduced.
For a compact metric graph
\Gamma
with total number of edges
E=|{\mathcal{E}}|
, let
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
be a set of selected edges, which we are going to shrink to zero. For each edge
e
denote by
l(e)
and

the left, respectively right, end point of the interval with which
e
is identified, and let

\displaystyle\partial\Gamma:=\bigcup_{e\in{\mathcal{E}}}\{l(e),r(e)\};

we distinguish all end points, even if they possibly correspond to the same points on the real line according to the parametrizations of the edges. Then for any function
f\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
we may consider a vector

\displaystyle f|_{\partial\Gamma}\in\mathbb{C}^{2E}

of evaluations of
f
at all end points of all edges. Note that
f|_{\partial\Gamma}
describes the same boundary values as
(F(v))_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}

for some self-adjoint Schrödinger operator

as in Proposition
2.1
, then the vertex conditions may be expressed in terms of the vectors

and

describes the same boundary values for the derivatives as

\displaystyle D_{0}

\displaystyle:=D_{0}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}):=\{F\in\mathbb{C}^{2E}:\;F_{l(e)}=F_{r(e)},\;e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\},

\displaystyle N_{0}

\displaystyle:=N_{0}({\mathcal{E}}_{0}):=\{F^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{2E}:\;F^{\prime}_{l(e)}=-F^{\prime}_{r(e)},\;e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\}.

holds for all

. If the eigenvalue

and its associated vector

. Moreover, denote by

for

\displaystyle h(f)-\widetilde{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\Lambda_{v_{0}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v),P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle-\big\langle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{v_{0}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v),P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v)\big\rangle\geq 0.

Assume now that for some

is simple and equality holds in (

\displaystyle\lambda_{k-1}(\widetilde{H})\leq\lambda_{k-1}(H)<\lambda_{k}(H)=\lambda_{k}(\widetilde{H});

in particular, for all nontrivial

formed of the span of

for

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}(f)\leq\lambda_{k-1}(H)\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}<\lambda_{k}(H)\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}.

Therefore the assumption
\lambda_{k}(\widetilde{H})=\lambda_{k}(H)
implies equality in (

) for all

. This implies

\displaystyle P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\in\ker(\Lambda_{v_{0}}-\widetilde{\Lambda}_{v_{0}}).

for all
f\in\ker(H-\lambda_{1}(H))
and proves the second assertion.

Closely related to the change of the coupling strength at a vertex considered in the previous section is replacing a

Let
\Gamma

q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)

v_{0}\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Denote by

Proof.

Let

\widetilde{h}
be the quadratic forms associated with

\displaystyle h(f)-\widetilde{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{v_{0}}|f(v_{0})|^{2}-\frac{1}{\beta_{v_{0}}}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{\deg(v_{0})}F_{j}(v_{0})\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{v_{0}}|f(v_{0})|^{2}-\frac{\deg(v_{0})^{2}}{\beta_{v_{0}}}|f(v_{0})|^{2}\geq 0,

The case of an anti-Kirchhoff condition,

Example 5.5
.

Consider a star graph with three edges of arbitrary lengths and the zero potential on them. Impose Neumann vertex conditions at the degree-one vertices. For the operator

, we impose a continuity-Kirchhoff vertex condition at the central vertex, while

is equipped with an anti-Kirchhoff condition there. As earlier, any function which is constantly

Consider, on the other hand, the Laplacian on an interval and fix a Neumann boundary condition at the left end point for both

and
\widetilde{H}

. Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(H)=0<\frac{\pi^{2}}{4L^{2}}=\lambda_{1}(\widetilde{H}),

where

Complementary to this example, it should be mentioned that in certain situations continuity-Kirchhoff and anti-Kirchhoff vertex conditions lead to the same positive eigenvalues; cf.
[

]
.

Theorem 5.9

Let

are parallel. Let

be a Schrödinger operator on
\Gamma
with real potential

and with

be the graph obtained by unfolding the parallel edges

be the Schrödinger operator on

with the same potential as for

on all edges except for the new edge

and the constant zero-potential on

. Moreover, for

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\widetilde{H})\leq\lambda_{1}(H)

holds. If

, then even

Proof.

We will prove the theorem in the case

. The general case can be obtained by iterating the following procedure. Let
v_{1}
and

are distinct vertices; the case in which

By Theorem

we can choose

strictly positive on
\Gamma
. Consider the restrictions

and

of

, respectively. Let, without loss of generality,

\displaystyle a:=f_{1}(0)=f_{2}(0)>0,\qquad b:=f_{1}(L(e_{1}))=f_{2}(L(e_{2}))>0,

and assume without loss of generality

. Moreover, choose

such that

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{0})=a\quad\text{and}\quad f_{1}([x_{0},L(e_{1})])=[a,b].

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

\lambda_{1}(H)\geq 0
. In this case, the quadratic form
h
corresponding to

satisfies

. Define a function
\widetilde{f}
on the graph

by letting it be equal to

on all edges except
\widehat{e}
(after identification of these edges with edges of

in the natural way). On
\widehat{e}

\widehat{f}
by inserting the edge

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{\widehat{e}}(x)=\begin{cases}f_{1}(x),&x\in[0,x_{0}],\\

\displaystyle\int_{e_{1}\cup e_{2}}|f^{\prime}|^{2}\geq\int_{\widehat{e}}|\widetilde{f}^{\prime}|^{2}.

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}-\int_{e_{1}\cup e_{2}}|f|^{2}+\int_{\widehat{e}}|\widetilde{f}|^{2}\geq\int_{\Gamma}|f|^{2}

6.
Hadamard-type formulas

In this section we study Haramard-type formulas, i.e. the variation of eigenvalues and eigenvectors with respect to perturbations of the vertex conditions.

Remark 6.1
.

In general, the vertex conditions are parametrized by certain matrices, i.e. by elements of a Banach space, and differentiating has to be understood in the sense of, e.g., the Fréchet derivative: let
X
be a Banach space,
U\subseteq X
open, and
F\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
Fréchet differentiable; cf.
[
75
, Section 2.1]
. Then
F^{\prime}
is a mapping from
U
to
{\mathcal{L}}(X,\mathbb{C})
, where
{\mathcal{L}}(X,\mathbb{C})
denotes the space of bounded linear functionals on
X
. Thus, for
x\in U
,
F^{\prime}(x)\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
is linear and bounded. In our case,
X
will be finite-dimensional,
X=\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
for some
d
, and then for
x\in U
we can represent
F^{\prime}(x)
by its matrix of partial derivatives.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact metric graph,
q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
real,
v_{0}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
H
the Schrödinger operator on
\Gamma
with potential
q
, arbitrary self-adjoint coupling conditions as in Proposition
2.1
at the vertices
v\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{v_{0}\}
and a self-adjoint coupling condition with non-trivial Robin part,
P_{v_{0},\rm R}\neq 0
, at the vertex
v_{0}
. Denote by
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
the self-adjoint, invertible coupling operator in
{\text{\rm ran\,}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}
.
Let
\lambda
be a simple eigenvalue of
H
and
f
be a corresponding normalized eigenfunction.
Then
\lambda
and
f
are differentiable w.r.t.
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
and we have

\lambda^{\prime}(\Lambda_{v_{0}})\colon\widetilde{\Lambda_{v_{0}}}\mapsto\big\langle\widetilde{\Lambda_{v_{0}}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0}),P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\big\rangle

that is,

\lambda^{\prime}(\Lambda_{v_{0}})(\widetilde{\Lambda_{v_{0}}})=\big\langle\widetilde{\Lambda_{v_{0}}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0}),P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\big\rangle

for all operators
\widetilde{\Lambda_{v_{0}}}
in
{\text{\rm ran\,}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}
.

Remark 6.3
.

We would like to point out that
{\text{\rm ran\,}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}
is a finite-dimensional vector space, implying that
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
can be represented as a Hermitian matrix. Hence, differentiating with respect to
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
in fact is just differentiating on a finite-dimensional space.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

By
[
13
, Theorem 3.8 and Theorem 3.10]
and the different equivalent ways to describe vertex conditions (see e.g.
[
49
, Theorem 5]
) the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are differentiable with respect to
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
. Let
d:=\dim{\text{\rm ran\,}}P_{v_{0},\rm R}>0
and
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. We abbreviate
\partial_{jk}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\Lambda_{v_{0}})_{j,k}}
for the partial derivatives.

We check that partial derivatives of
f
w.r.t. components of the parameter again belong to
{\text{\rm dom\,}}h
. It suffices to check that the derivative is in
\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
, as the condition
P_{v,\rm D}F(v)=0
for all
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
is a closed condition and thus stable under taking partial derivatives w.r.t. parameters.
Note that on each edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
,
f
is a linear combination of two basis functions solving the eigenvalue equation
-f^{\prime\prime}+qf=\lambda f
; the dependence of
f
on
\Lambda_{v_{0}}
is only present in the coefficients of these basis functions. Thus, on each edge,
\partial_{jk}f
is again in
H^{1}
on each edge (as edges have finite length). Thus,
\partial_{jk}f\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
, and hence
\partial_{jk}f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}h
.

Since
f
is normalized, we obtain

2\Real\langle f,\partial_{jk}f\rangle=0,\quad j,k=1,\dots,d.

Moreover, as
h(f)=\lambda
, we get

\partial_{jk}\lambda(\Lambda_{v_{0}})=\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{k}\overline{\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{j}}+2\Real h(f,\partial_{jk}f).

Now, since
f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}H
with
Hf=\lambda f
and
\partial_{jk}f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}h
, we observe

\Real h(f,\partial_{jk}f)=\Real\langle Hf,\partial_{jk}f\rangle=\lambda\Real\langle f,\partial_{jk}f\rangle=0,

and therefore

\partial_{jk}\lambda(\Lambda_{v_{0}})=\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{k}\overline{\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{j}}.

We note that this partial derivative is actually constant.

With respect to the canonical basis
\bigl((\delta_{jk,lm})_{l,m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}:j,k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\bigr)
of
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, we observe

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\Lambda_{v_{0}})((\delta_{jk,lm}))

\displaystyle=\partial_{jk}\lambda(\Lambda_{v_{0}})=\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{k}\overline{\bigl(P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\bigr)_{j}}

\displaystyle=\langle((\delta_{jk,lm})P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0}),P_{v_{0},\rm R}F(v_{0})\big\rangle.

This yields the assertion.
