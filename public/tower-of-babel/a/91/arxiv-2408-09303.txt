Primordial black hole numbers: standard formulas and charts
Eemeli Tomberg

Abstract
This brief note presents standard computations of primordial black hole mass M M given perturbations of scale k k , and their late-time abundance Ω PBH \Omega_{\text{PBH}} given their initial density fraction β \beta . I recap the assumptions made in these computations and present formulas and reference charts useful for a working cosmologist.

When strong primordial perturbations enter the Hubble radius in the early universe, they may collapse into primordial black holes (PBHs). The time of entry depends on the perturbation scale
k
and determines the mass of the PBHs,
M
. It also determines how much the initial PBH density gets diluted as space expands, relating the initial PBH mass fraction
\beta
to the late-time mass fraction
\Omega_{\text{PBH}}
. The formulas relating
M
to
k
and
\Omega_{\text{PBH}}
to
M
and
\beta
are standard (see e.g.
[
6
,
7
]
), but the results may vary by up to a few orders of magnitude depending on the assumptions made, complicating comparisons between studies.
In this note, I derive these formulas in detail and present them in various easy-to-use numerical forms. The note is meant as a reference for the results and a resource for comparing PBH computations made under different assumptions. 3
PBH abundance

I assume the PBHs form instantaneously with mass
M
when the scale
k
re-enters the Hubble radius. I call their initial energy density fraction
\beta
, as is standard in the literature. The initial PBH energy density is then
\beta\times\rho
. Today, the energy density has diluted by factor
a^{3}
and the total energy density of the universe is
3H_{0}^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}
. Today’s PBH density parameter is then

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{PBH},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\rho a^{3}}{3H_{0}^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1/4}\pi^{2}\beta\gamma^{1/2}g_{*}^{3/4}g_{*s0}T_{0}^{3}}{3\sqrt{3}5^{3/4}g_{*s}H_{0}^{2}\sqrt{MM_{\mathrm{Pl}}}}

\displaystyle=$9.16\text{\times}{10}^{7}$\times\beta\gamma^{1/2}\quantity(\frac{M}{\Msolar})^{-1/2}\quantity(\frac{g_{*s}^4g_{*}^{-3}}{106.75})^{-1/4}\frac{H_{0}}{$\quantity(\frac{H_0}{\SI{70}{km/s/Mpc}})\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c}$}^{-2}

\displaystyle=$4.09\text{\times}{10}^{14}$\times\beta\gamma^{1/2}\frac{M}{${10}^{\quantity(\frac{M}{\SI{e20}{g}})}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}$}^{-1/2}\quantity(\frac{g_{*s}^4g_{*}^{-3}}{106.75})^{-1/4}\frac{H_{0}}{$\quantity(\frac{H_0}{\SI{70}{km/s/Mpc}})\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{c}$}^{-2}\,,

where I used the results of the previous section.

For a wide PBH mass distribution, it is conventional to keep track of the contribution to
\Omega_{\text{PBH}}
from PBHs in a mass bin of logarithmic width
\differential\ln M
and define

f_{\text{PBH}}(M)\equiv\frac{1}{\Omega_{\text{DM}}}\frac{\differential\Omega_{\text{PBH},0}}{\differential\ln M}\,.

(3.2)

The total PBH dark matter fraction is then

f_{\text{PBH}}^{\text{tot}}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\text{PBH},0}}{\Omega_{\text{DM}}}=\int\differential\ln M\,f_{\text{PBH}}(M)\,.

(3.3)

PBH abundance bounds are often given in terms of
f_{\text{PBH}}
.

In the above computation, I anchored the early universe quantities to physical scales by using the cosmological observables today, in particular,
T_{0}
and
H_{0}
. In the literature, the computation is sometimes anchored to the matter–radiation equality instead, see e.g.
[
19

20

. Other anchorings are also possible. Instead of
T_{0}
and
H_{0}
, one simply has to know the redshift (or
k
) and the energy densities of all the matter components at the reference scale.

4
Beyond the standard scenario

In this note, I have dealt with three sets of linked variables:
M
,
\rho
, and
H
linked by geometry and the Friedmann equations;
a
and
z=a_{0}/a-1
; and
N
,
f_{\text{GW}}
, and
k
linked directly by (
2.3
) and
f_{\text{GW}}=k
. The last are related to the others through
k=aH
, but the rest of the relations go through the temperature
T
, and they may be modified in non-standard cosmological scenarios. Relating
a
to
T
by (
2.4
) requires a thermal bath undergoing adiabatic expansion, isolated from possible extra sectors (up to changes in
g_{*s}
). Further relating
T
to
M
through (
2.1
) requires the thermal bath to dominate over other sectors. Hidden sectors decoupled from the Standard Model can severe these relations, as can non-standard expansion history, such as an early period of matter or dark energy domination or kination. In such cases, equations (
2.5
) and (
3.1
) have to be reworked.
