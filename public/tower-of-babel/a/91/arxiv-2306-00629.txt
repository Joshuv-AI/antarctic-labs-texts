Identifiability and Generalizability in Constrained Inverse Reinforcement Learning
Andreas Schlaginhaufen, Maryam Kamgarpour

Abstract
Two main challenges in Reinforcement Learning (RL) are designing appropriate reward functions and ensuring the safety of the learned policy. To address these challenges, we present a theoretical framework for Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) in constrained Markov decision processes. From a convex-analytic perspective, we extend prior results on reward identifiability and generalizability to both the constrained setting and a more general class of regularizations. In particular, we show that identifiability up to potential shaping ( Cao et al. 2021 ) is a consequence of entropy regularization and may generally no longer hold for other regularizations or in the presence of safety constraints. We also show that to ensure generalizability to new transition laws and constraints, the true reward must be identified up to a constant. Additionally, we derive a finite sample guarantee for the suboptimality of the learned rewards, and validate our results in a gridworld environment.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been successfully applied to many artificial intelligence tasks such as playing the games of Chess, Go, and Starcraft
(
Silver et al. 2016
)
, control of humanoid robots
(
Akkaya et al. 2019
)
, or fine-tuning of large language models
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
. However, two of the key challenges in bringing RL to the real world include designing suitable reward functions for the problem at hand and guaranteeing safety of the learned policy.

While a surge of recent work addresses safe RL, past work has focused on the case in which the reward function is known. In many cases, such as autonomous driving or human-robot interactions, the reward functions are a priori unknown and are hard to design. The framework of inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) addresses learning reward functions based on expert demonstrations. However, most past work on IRL is not incorporating safety explicitly. While safety can be learned implicitly by learning a reward that penalizes unsafe behavior,
incorporating safety in IRL is essential in ensuring that the learned rewards can be transferred to new tasks efficiently
.

Related Work

Safe RL for Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) has been extensively studied and various notions of safety have been considered
(
Garcıa & Fernández 2015
)
. A well-established framework for safety is Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs)
(
Altman 1999
)
. In this setting, safety is defined through a set of cost functions on the state and actions, whose expectations should be bounded by a predefined threshold. The CMDP approach has been broadly applied to safe RL
(
Achiam et al. 2017
;
Chow et al. 2018
;
Turchetta et al. 2020
)
.

The above safe RL works all assume that the reward is known or can be evaluated along the MDP trajectories. Learning rewards from a data set of expert demonstrations – i.e. the concept of IRL – was first introduced by
Russell 1998
. Whereas imitation learning
(
Pomerleau 1988
;
Syed & Schapire 2007
;
Ho & Ermon 2016
;
Garg et al. 2021
)
attempts to directly recover the expert’s policy, the goal of IRL is to learn the expert’s latent reward function. The main motivation for IRL is that the reward, being independent of the transition law, provides the most succinct and transferable description of a task
(
Ng et al. 2000
)
.

A fundamental challenge in IRL is that in general many rewards can generate a given optimal behavior, making it difficult to recover the true underlying reward function. In particular,
Ng et al. 1999
show that the set of optimal policies is always invariant under the so-called potential shaping transformations. Additionally, the non-uniqueness of the optimal policy corresponding to some reward can lead to trivial solutions to the IRL problem. For instance, for a constant reward all policies are optimal, but such a reward is most likely not informative for the expert’s task. Various methods have been proposed to deal with the above two degeneracies. These include approaches based on margin maximization
(
Abbeel & Ng 2004
;
Ratliff et al. 2006
)
or Bayesian reasoning
(
Ramachandran & Amir 2007
;
Choi & Kim 2012
)
. One promising approach is Maximum Causal Entropy IRL (MCE-IRL)
(
Ziebart et al. 2010
;
Zhou et al. 2017
)
, which ensures uniqueness of the optimal policy via entropy regularization and has led to state-of-the-art imitation learning methods
(
Ho & Ermon 2016
;
Garg et al. 2021
)
. MCE-IRL provably recovers the expert’s true reward up to potential shaping transformations
Cao et al. 2021
. Moreover, the MCE-IRL framework has been extended to more general policy regularizations
Jeon et al. 2020
, but the question of identifiability up to potential shaping transformations remains unanswered in this more general setting.

While both IRL and safe RL problems have been studied extensively, less work has focused on safety aspects in IRL. Robustness to transition laws has been addressed by
(
Fu et al. 2017
;
Viano et al. 2021
)
. Motivated by safety and risk considerations,
Majumdar et al. 2017
suggest that the expert may not be simply minimizing an expected cumulative cost (corresponding to a constraint), but some general coherent risk measure of that cost. Moreover,
Tschiatschek et al. 2019
provide a learner-aware MCE-IRL framework in which the learner has their own preference constraints, such as safety. Most recently,
Malik et al. 2021
assume a known reward and address the problem of learning only the constraints from demonstrations, whereas
Ding & Xue 2022
consider IRL with combinatorial constraints. These recent works incorporate safety in various approaches but do not focus on identifiability and generalizability aspects.

Contribution

Approaching the CMDP problem as a linear program in the so-called occupancy measure, we present a constrained IRL framework for arbitrary convex regularizations of the occupancy measure. In this general setting, we then address the questions of identifiability and generalizability to new transition laws and constraints. In particular, we first show that arbitrary (strictly) convex policy regularizations
(
Geist et al. 2019
)
yield a (strictly) convex regularization of the occupancy measure, and are hence naturally incorporated in our framework (Proposition
3.1
). Our first main result (Theorem
4.5
) is a complete characterization of the set of rewards for which a given expert is optimal. Notably, we show that identifiability up to potential shaping transformations in MCE-IRL is a consequence of entropy regularizations and generally no longer holds for other regularizations (such as e.g. sparse Tsallis entropy
(
Lee et al. 2018a
)
), nor in the constrained setting. Our second main result (Theorem
4.12
) shows that generalizability to new transition laws and safety constraints is only possible if the expert’s reward is recovered up to a constant. Furthermore, we provide a verifiable sufficient condition for generalizability. Our proof techniques, based on convex analysis, unify and generalize past work. In Section
5
, we address the finite sample setting and provide a novel result for the number of expert demonstrations needed to recover a reward whose optimal policy is close to the expert’s policy. In Section
6
, we experimentally verify our results in a gridworld environment.

Convex Viewpoint

Convex Reformulation

While the optimization problem (
2

(

4
Constrained IRL

Problem Formulation

Given a CMDP without reward
M\setminus\bm{r}=\left({\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\bm{P},\bm{\nu}_{0},\bm{\Psi},\bm{b},\gamma}\right)
and a data set of demonstrations
\mathcal{D}=\left\{{(s^{i}_{t},a^{i}_{t})_{t=0}^{T}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
from some expert
\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}

\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{nm}
for which the expert is optimal. Unless stated otherwise, we let the reward class
\mathcal{R}
be an arbitrary convex set. Clearly, the above IRL problem only has a solution under the following realizability assumption.

Assumption 4.1

(Realizability)
.

Assume the expert is optimal for some
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{R}
i.e.
\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}\in\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})

Next, we show that the constrained IRL problem can be formulated as an optimization problem. We first consider an idealized setting, where we are given access to the expert’s true occupancy measure

(IRL)

Identifiability

Trivially, the optimal occupancy measure in (
P
) is invariant to constant shifts of the reward. Furthermore,
Ng et al. 1999
show that if the reward is allowed to depend on the consecutive state
s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)
, the so-called
potential shaping transformations

\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})\mapsto\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})+\bm{\eta}(s)-\gamma\bm{\eta}(s^{\prime})
leave the optimal occupancy measure in (
P
) invariant. Using the conversion
\bm{r}(s,a)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
, potential shaping reduces in our setting to
\bm{r}\mapsto\bm{r}+\Delta_{\bm{r}}
with

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{r}}\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\big\{\Delta_{\bm{r}}\in

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{nm}:\;\Delta_{\bm{r}}(s,a)=\bm{\eta}(s)

(12)

\displaystyle-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bm{\eta}(s^{\prime}),\;\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\big\}

\displaystyle=\spn(\bm{E}

\displaystyle-\gamma\bm{P}).\vskip-2.84544pt

As a consequence, we expect
\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\supseteq\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\mathcal{U}
. A recent result by
Cao et al. 2021
and
Skalse et al. 2022
shows that for standard unconstrained MCE-IRL it holds

\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})=\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\mathcal{U}=\beta\log\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}}+\mathcal{U},

(13)

with
\beta(\log\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}})(s,a)=\beta\log\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}}(a|s)=\nabla f(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})(s,a)
. In other words, for
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}^{nm}
the expert’s reward can be identified up to potential shaping in MCE-IRL. Example
4.4
below shows that for MCE-IRL identifiability up to potential shaping is lost when there are active safety constraints

Consider a single state CMDP with
\mathcal{A}=\left\{{a_{1},a_{2}}\right\}

\bm{\mu}(a_{2})\leq 3/4

\bm{\Psi}=\begin{bmatrix}0,1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and

\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}=\bm{\pi}

\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}

Our main result of this section shows that more generally identifiability up to potential shaping is lost whenever there are active inequality constraints. In particular, under Assumption
3.2
an occupancy measure
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}
is optimal for some reward if and only if this reward is contained in the Minkowski sum of the subdifferential of

\mathcal{F}
at
\bm{\mu}

\mathcal{U}
and additional conic combinations of the gradients of active inequality constraints. The latter become nonzero when
\bm{\mu}
lies on the relative boundary of the feasible set. In this case, we may have
\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\supset\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\mathcal{U}
and
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\notin\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let Assumption
3.2
hold and consider
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
\mathcal{I}(\bm{\mu})
and
\mathcal{J}(\bm{\mu})
denote the set of indices of active inequality constraints under
\bm{\mu}
i.e.
\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{b}_{i}
and
\bm{\mu}(s,a)=0
if and only if

and
(s,a)\in\mathcal{J}(\bm{\mu})

\bm{\mu}\in\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{r})\iff\bm{r}\in\partial f(\bm{\mu})+N_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu}),

(14)

where
N_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu})=\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\cone\left({\left\{{\bm{\Psi}_{i}}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\bm{\mu})}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\cone\left({\left\{{-\bm{e}_{s,a}}\right\}_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{J}(\bm{\mu})}}\right).

Here,
\bm{e}_{s,a}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm}
denote the standard unit vectors with
\bm{e}_{s,a}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=1
if
(s,a)=(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
and
\bm{e}_{s,a}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=0
otherwise.

Remark 4.6
.

Note that in the differentiable case, i.e. if

\nabla h(\bm{\mu})^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}^{\prime}-\bm{\mu}}\right)\leq 0,\forall\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F},

for maximization of

over the set
\mathcal{F}

\bm{\mu}

\mathcal{M}

Generalizability

As for our initial goal of learning a reward generalizing to new transition laws and constraints, the result of Corollary
4.7
is problematic, since the set
\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
depends on both the transition law and the constraints. To make this dependency explicit, we will throughout this section denote
\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}},\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}
for the solution maps corresponding to the transition law
\bm{P}
and constraint threshold
\bm{b}
. Additionally, we let
\mathfrak{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{nm\times n}
be the set of all transition laws. Moreover, we introduce the following notion of generalizability.

Definition 4.11

(Generalizability)
.

Fix some transition law and constraint threshold
(\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0})
. We say that IRL generalizes to
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, if

\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\prime}),\;\forall\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P},\forall\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B},

(18)

for any pair of rewards
\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
.

Definition
4.11
requires all rewards recovered via IRL to yield the same optimal occupancy measures for all
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}
. The subsequent result shows that, under Assumption
4.9
, generalization to a neighborhood of new transition laws and arbitrary constraint thresholds is possible if and only if the expert’s reward is recovered up to a constant.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let Assumptions
3.2
,
3.3
,
4.1
,
4.9
hold for
(\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0})
and let
\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}\in\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})
for some
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{R}
. Consider an arbitrary neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{nm\times n}
of
\bm{P}_{0}
. Then, IRL generalizes to
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{O}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\cap\mathfrak{P}
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
if and only if

\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\spn(\bm{1}_{nm}).

(19)

Note that since
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
, Theorem
4.12
considers generalizability to all possible constraint thresholds -- in particular to the unconstrained setting.

For the unrestricted reward class
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}^{nm}
, we expect the same result to hold even if
\mathcal{B}
is only a neighborhood of constraint thresholds. However, a rigorous proof would require continuity of
\bm{P}\mapsto\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}}(\bm{r})
, which we leave open to future work.

is to show that in any neighborhood of
\bm{P}_{0}
we can find

generalize to both
\bm{P}_{1}
and
\bm{P}_{2}
. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel result – even in the context of unconstrained IRL. For more details about the proof and a brief discussion about the connection to recent results on identifiability from multiple experts
(
Cao et al. 2021

Rolland et al. 2022

In light of Theorem
4.12
, the following corollary shows that for the unrestricted reward class, IRL is not generalizing to a neighborhood of new transition laws and arbitrary constraints.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let Assumption
3.2
and

\mathcal{B}

is a consequence of

.

Let Assumptions

Ignoring the Constraints

As for strictly convex regularization, we have the equivalence
\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{r})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{r}-\bm{\Psi}\bm{\xi}^{*})
(Proposition
3.4
), we may ignore safety constraints in IRL and recover the modified reward
\bm{r}-\bm{\Psi}\bm{\xi}^{*}
via unconstrained IRL. However, this comes with two caveats. First, the reward class needs to be sufficiently expressive to implicitly account for the safety constraints. Second, as illustrated in Example
4.4
,
\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
may not contain the expert’s reward in case of active safety constraints and hence fail to generalize to the unconstrained setting.

Finite Sample Setting

Practical Inverse Reinforcement Learning

In practice, we only have access to a finite data set of demonstrations
\mathcal{D}=\left\{{(s^{i}_{t},a^{i}_{t})_{t=0}^{T}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
. In this case, the expert occupancy measure
\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}
can be estimated via
(
Abbeel & Ng 2004
)

\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(s,a)\vcentcolon=\dfrac{(1-\gamma)}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}\mathbbm{1}(s_{t}^{i}=s,a_{t}^{i}=a),

(22)

where
\mathbbm{1}
is an indicator function. Swapping the order of minimization and maximization using Sion’s min-max theorem
(
Sion 1958
)
, we can interpret the resulting min-max problem as the dual of an occupancy measure matching problem
(
Syed & Schapire 2007
;
Syed et al. 2008
;
Ho & Ermon 2016
)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}}\;\;\bm{r}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}}\right)-f(\bm{\mu})

(23)

\displaystyle=-\max_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\min_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}}\;\;\left[{-\bm{r}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}}\right)+f(\bm{\mu})}\right]

\displaystyle=-\min_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}}\left[{\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{\mu},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})+f(\bm{\mu})}\right].

Here,
\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{\mu},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})\vcentcolon=\max_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\bm{r}^{\top}\left({\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}-\bm{\mu}}\right)
is an integral probability metric
(
Müller 1997
)
measuring the distance from
\bm{\mu}
to the empirical expert occupancy measure. For different choices of
\mathcal{R}
different distance measures arise. The choice
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}^{nm}
yields a characteristic function with
\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{\mu},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})=0
if
\bm{\mu}=\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{\mu},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})=\infty
otherwise
(
Boyd et al. 2004
)
. For the bounded linear feature classes
\mathcal{R}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{r}_{\bm{w}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{w}:\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times d},\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert\leq 1}\right\}
we get
\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{\mu},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})=\left\lVert\bm{\Phi}^{\top}(\bm{\mu}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}})\right\rVert_{*}
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{*}
denotes the dual norm to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. Thus, for
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
we recover feature expectation matching in the 2-norm
(
Abbeel & Ng 2004
)
and for
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
in the
\infty
-norm
(
Syed et al. 2008
)
. Other choices of
\mathcal{R}
lead to other distance measures such as the Wasserstein-1 distance or the maximum mean discrepancy (for an overview see
(
Xiao et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2019
;
Swamy et al. 2021
)
). In the following, we focus on the choice

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}}\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{r}_{\bm{w}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{w}:\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times d},\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert_{1}\leq 1}\right\}.

(24)

We will see that bounding the reward class as above enables us to derive a bound for the sample complexity of IRL.

7
Limitations and Future Work

For ease of exposition, we limit the scope of this paper to discrete state and action spaces. However, an interesting direction for future research would be to extend our results to the continuous setting, where the CMDP problem can be formulated as an infinite-dimensional convex optimization problem involving the occupancy measure
(
Altman 1999
)
. Furthermore, our results are based on optimal solutions and rewards, but in practical settings, we hardly ever obtain optimal solutions and approximately optimal solutions are the norm. Hence, examining identifiability and generalizability in an approximate setting would be valuable for practical applications and may reveal valuable insights on how to choose the regularization
f
. Finally, Proposition
4.14
provides a sufficient condition for generalizability, but checking the rank condition (
21
) requires knowledge of the transition law and the constraints. To alleviate this, it may be helpful to learn a reward from multiple experts with different transition laws and constraints.

8
Summary

In this paper, we present a constrained IRL framework for CMDPs with arbitrary convex regularizations of the occupancy measure. From a convex-analytic viewpoint, we address identifiability and generalizability to new transition laws and constraints. Our results indicate that identifiability of rewards up to potential shaping is contingent on the use of entropy regularizations and that generalizability to new transition laws and constraints is only possible when the expert’s reward is identified up to a constant. Based on these insights, we provide a sufficient condition for identifiability and generalizability. Furthermore, we show a novel result on the number of expert trajectories required to recover a reward whose optimal policy is close to the expert’s policy. Lastly, we showcase the applicability of our results in a gridworld experiment.

B.1
Entropy Regularization

In their work on regularized MDPs
Geist et al. 2019
consider a family of regularized MDPs with the objective

\max_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}J(\bm{\pi},\bm{r}),

(34)

where
J(\bm{\pi},\bm{r})\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}}\left[{\bm{r}(s,a)-\Omega(\bm{\pi}(\cdot|s))}\right]
and
\Omega:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex. Defining the optimal value and q-value function

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{*}(s)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\max_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}}\left[{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left[{\bm{r}(s_{t},a_{t})-\Omega(\bm{\pi}(\cdot|s_{t}))}\right]\bigg|s_{0}=s}\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{q}^{*}(s,a)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bm{r}(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bm{v}^{*}(s^{\prime}),

(36)

the optimal policy can be shown to be
\bm{\pi}^{*}(\cdot|s)=\nabla\Omega^{*}(\bm{q}^{*}(s,\cdot))
, where
\nabla\Omega^{*}
is the gradient of the convex conjugate
\Omega^{*}(\bm{q}^{*}(s,\cdot))\vcentcolon=\max_{\bm{d}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{q}^{*}(s,\cdot)}^{\top}\bm{d}-\Omega(\bm{d})
. For the entropy regularization
\Omega(\bm{d})=-\beta H(\bm{d})
with
\beta>0
, the optimal policy can be shown to have the soft-max form

\bm{\pi}^{*}(a|s)=\dfrac{\exp\left({\bm{q}^{*}(s,a)/\beta}\right)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp\left({\bm{q}^{*}(s,a^{\prime})/\beta}\right)}.

(37)

Accordingly, entropy regularization forces the optimal policy to always assign a non-zero probability to each action regularizing the optimal policy towards the uniform distribution. Similar to unregularized MDPs, the optimal policy in entropy regularized MDPs can be computed via value or policy iteration
(
Ziebart et al. 2010
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
.

In the occupancy measure, entropy regularization in the policy takes the form

f(\bm{\mu})=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\bm{\mu}}\left[{H\left(\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}}(\cdot|s)\right)}\right]=\beta\sum_{s,a}\bm{\mu}(s,a)\log\left({\dfrac{\bm{\mu}(s,a)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\bm{\mu}(s,a^{\prime})}}\right).

(38)

In order to incorporate prior knowledge about the expert’s policy, we may also consider the relative entropy regularization

f(\bm{\mu})=\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\bm{\mu}}\left[{D_{\text{KL}}\left(\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}}(\cdot|s)||\bm{\pi}_{0}(\cdot|s)\right)}\right],

(39)

where

with

is the KL divergence or relative entropy and
\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Pi
is some some reference policy. The following corollary shows that, under Slater’s condition, entropy and relative entropy regularization in the policy are indeed satisfying Assumption
4.9
. By Corollary
4.10
this implies that the optimal occupancy measure lies in the relative interior of
\mathcal{M}

(48)

. In particular, this implies that
\bm{\nu}(s)>0

If

\bm{\mathbbm{P}}_{\bm{\nu}_{0}}^{\bm{\pi}^{\bar{\bm{\mu}}}}(s_{T}=s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{0},\ldots,s_{T-1}\\

This implies that there is at least one path

with non-zero probability under

\bm{\nu}_{0}(s_{0})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\pi}^{\bar{\bm{\mu}}}(a_{t-1}|s_{t-1})\bm{P}(s_{t}|s_{t-1},a_{t-1})>0.

Proposition

B.4
Remarks on Strong Duality

Whereas strong duality holds also for unregularized CMDPs
Altman 1999
, unique recovery of the optimal occupancy measure from an unconstrained RL problem (
56
) is a consequence of the strictly convex regularization. To illustrate this, consider the following simple example.

Example B.4
.

Consider a single state MDP with
\mathcal{A}=\left\{{a_{1},a_{2}}\right\}
. The reward is defined via
\bm{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}(a_{1}),\bm{r}(a_{2})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}0,1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and there is no regularization. Furthermore, the agent needs to respect the constraints
\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}0,1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}(a_{2})\leq 3/4
. In this single state setting
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}(a)=\bm{\pi}(a)
and
\mathcal{M}=\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
. Clearly, the unique primal optimal solution is
\bm{\mu}^{*}(a_{1})=1/4
and
\bm{\mu}^{*}(a_{2})=3/4
. This is a key difference to the unconstrained setting where always a deterministic optimal policy exists. Thus,
\bm{\mu}^{*}
cannot be realized as the unique optimum of an unconstrained, unregularized MDP, but only as the convex combination of multiple deterministic solutions. Indeed relaxing the safety constraint yields the Lagrangian
L(\bm{\mu},\xi)=(\bm{r}-\bm{\Psi}\xi)^{\top}\bm{\mu}+\xi b=(1-\xi)\bm{\mu}(a_{2})+3\xi/4
and the dual function

g(\xi)=\max_{\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}L(\bm{\mu},\xi)=\begin{cases}1-\xi/4\quad&,\xi\leq 1\\
3\xi/4\quad&,\xi>1.\end{cases}

(59)

Thus, there is a unique dual optimum
\xi^{*}=\argmin_{\xi\geq 0}g(\xi)=1
, leading to the dual optimal value
1/2
, which is equal to the primal optimum due to strong duality. However, for the reward
\bm{r}-\bm{\Psi}\xi=\begin{bmatrix}0,0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
not only
\bm{\mu}^{*}
, but all
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
are optimal in the unconstrained problem – even those with
\bm{\mu}(a_{2})>3/4
that are primal infeasible.

C.1
Preliminaries from Convex Analysis

Throughout this section, we introduce a few additional tools from convex analysis which turn out to be useful for the proof of Theorem
4.5
. In convex analysis it is standard to extend convex functions over the entire space by setting their value to
+\infty
outside of their domain. This leads us to extended real value functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
. Their effective domain is defined as
\dom h\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{x}:h(\bm{x})<\infty}\right\}
, and a convex function
h
is said to be proper if
h>-\infty
and
\dom h\neq\emptyset
. Furthermore,
h
is referred to as closed if its epigraph
\left\{{\left({\bm{x},y}\right):\bm{x}\in\dom h,y\geq h(\bm{x})}\right\}
is a closed set. For instance,
h
is closed if it is continuous and
\dom h
is a closed set
(
Boyd et al. 2004
)
. Next, we introduce the two key tools needed for the proof of Theorem
4.5
– convex conjugates and the Moreau-Rockafeller theorem.

Convex Conjugate

The convex conjugate
h^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
of
h
is defined as

h^{*}(\bm{y})\vcentcolon=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{x}-h(\bm{x}).

(60)

If
h
is closed proper convex, it holds
h^{**}=h
. Moreover, the following optimality conditions hold.

Theorem C.1

(
Rockafellar 1970
)
.

For any proper convex function
h^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
it holds

h^{*}(\bm{y})=\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{x}-h(\bm{x})\quad\iff\quad\bm{y}\in\partial h(\bm{x}).

(61)

If additionally
h
is closed, then

h^{*}(\bm{y})=\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{x}-h(\bm{x})\quad\iff\quad\bm{y}\in\partial h(\bm{x})\quad\iff\quad\bm{x}\in\partial h^{*}(\bm{y}).

(62)

Moreau-Rockafeller Theorem

The following theorem gives sufficient conditions under which the sum of subdifferentials of two functions is equal to the subdifferential of the sum of the two functions.

Theorem C.2

(
Rockafellar 1970
)
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}
be proper convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
h\vcentcolon=h_{1}+h_{2}
. If
\relint(\dom h_{1})
and
\relint(\dom h_{2})
, have a point in common then

\partial h(\bm{x})=\partial h_{1}(\bm{x})+\partial h_{2}(\bm{x}),\,\forall\bm{x}.

(63)

If
h_{1}
is polyhedral (i.e. its epigraph is polyhedral) then it is enough if the sets
\dom h_{1}
and
\relint(\dom h_{2})
have a point in common.

Proof of Proposition

Throughout this paper our focus lies on state-action rewards
\bm{r}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R},(s,a)\mapsto\bm{r}(s,a)
that are naturally arising in the convex analytic approach to CMDPs (see (

(
Altman 1999
)
), and as the dual variables to the occupancy measure matching problem (see (

(
Ho & Ermon 2016

(

Skalse et al. 2022

\bar{\bm{r}}
is equivalent to a CMDP problem with the state-action reward
\bm{r}(s,a)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
. Nevertheless, in practice the transition law is typically unknown and it may in certain cases be easier to specify a state-action-state reward. To relate our identifiability results to this setting, we make use of the following vector notation for a state-action-state reward
\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})

\bar{\bm{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{r}}_{s^{\prime}_{1}}^{\top},\ldots,\bar{\bm{r}}_{s^{\prime}_{n}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}m},\;\text{ with }\bar{\bm{r}}_{s^{\prime}}=\bar{\bm{r}}(\cdot,\cdot,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{nm},

(73)

and define the linear mapping
\mathbf{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}m}\to\mathbb{R}^{nm}
via
(\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}})(s,a)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\bm{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bar{\bm{r}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
. Furthermore, we denote
\overline{\mathsf{RL}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{\bm{r}})\vcentcolon=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}})
for the CMDP solution map for state-action-state rewards. The following corollary shows that identifiability of state-action-state rewards can be reduced to identifiability of state-action rewards – and hence to the result of Theorem
4.5
.

Corollary C.4
.

Let Assumption
3.2
hold and consider
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then,

\bm{\mu}\in\overline{\mathsf{RL}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{\bm{r}})\iff\bar{\bm{r}}\in\left\{{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{r}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{r}\end{bmatrix}\bigg|\bm{r}\in\partial f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})}\right\}+\mathcal{V},

(74)

where
\mathcal{V}=\ker\bm{A}
with
\dim\mathcal{V}=n(nm-1)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.5
, we have
\bm{\mu}\in\overline{\mathsf{RL}}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{\bm{r}})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}})
if and only if
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}\in\partial f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})
. It therefore suffices to show that
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{r}
if and only if
\bar{\bm{r}}=\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}+\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime}
with
\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}=[\bm{r},\ldots,\bm{r}]^{\top}
and
\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime}\in\ker\bm{A}
. If
\bar{\bm{r}}=\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}+\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime}
with
\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}=[\bm{r},\ldots,\bm{r}]^{\top}
and
\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime}\in\ker\bm{A}
, then it follows from
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}=\bm{r}
that
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{r}+\bm{0}
. Conversely, if
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{r}
, then
\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}=[\bm{r},\ldots,\bm{r}]^{\top}
also satisfies
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}=\bm{r}
, and thus
\bar{\bm{r}}-\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}\in\ker\bm{A}
.

Finally, since for
\bar{\bm{r}}=[\bm{r},\ldots,\bm{r}]^{\top}
we have
\bm{A}\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{r}
, the mapping
\bm{A}
is surjective and thus
\dim\mathcal{V}=n(nm-1)
.
∎

From a more abstract perspective, Corollary
C.4
makes use of the fact that the image
\textbf{im}\bm{A}=\mathbb{R}^{nm}
of
\bm{A}
is isomorphic to the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}m}/\ker\bm{A}

(
Halmos 2017
)
, where
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}m}/\ker\bm{A}
is the set of equivalence classes
[\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}]\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bar{\bm{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}m}:\bar{\bm{r}}=\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime}+\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime},\bar{\bm{r}}^{\prime\prime}\in\ker\bm{A}}\right\}
.

For unconstrained MDPs
Skalse et al. 2022
discuss invariances of optimal policies to reward transformation for state-action-state rewards. They introduce the additional invariances along the linear subspace
\mathcal{V}=\ker\mathbf{A}
as
s^{\prime}
-redistribution
. Moreover, they show that identifying a state-action-state reward up to
s^{\prime}
-redistribution is not sufficient for generalizability to new environments. In contrast, our result in Theorem
4.12
shows that for state-action rewards identifying the rewards up to potential shaping is not enough for generalizability and that we instead need to recover the expert’s reward up to a constant. However, this result is not extending immediately to the state-action-state setting, and one would need to modify the proof of Theorem
4.12
to additionally account for the space
\mathcal{V}
in order to make a statement about generalizability.

C.6
Proof of Theorem
4.12

Theorem
4.12

Let Assumption
3.2
,
3.3
,
4.1
,
4.9
be satisfied for
(\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0})
and let
\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}\in\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})
for some
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{R}
. Consider an arbitrary neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{nm\times n}
of
\bm{P}_{0}
. Then, IRL generalizes to
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{O}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\cap\mathfrak{P}
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
if and only if

\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\spn(\bm{1}_{nm}).

(79)

Proof.

The
if
direction is trivial, since addition of a constant is not changing the set of optimal occupancy measures. Hence, if
\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\spn(\bm{1}_{nm})
, then IRL generalizes to any arbitrary set of transition laws and constraint thresholds.

To prove the
only if
direction, we proceed in the following steps:

1.

Show that Slater’s condition is still satisfied in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\bm{P}_{0}
.

2.

Apply Theorem
4.5
to rewrite generalizability as a condition on the rewards.

3.

Construct
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}
such that only
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\spn\left({\mathbf{1}_{nm}}\right)
generalize to
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}
.

4.

Use
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}
to construct
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
such that only
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\spn\left({\mathbf{1}_{nm}}\right)
generalize to
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}
.

Step 1:

By Assumption
3.2
(Slater’s condition) there is some occupancy measure
\bar{\bm{\mu}}\in\relint\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}
where

\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm}:\bm{\mu}\geq\bm{0},(\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P}_{0})^{\top}\bm{\mu}=(1-\gamma)\bm{\nu}_{0},\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{\mu}\leq\bm{b}_{0}}\right\}.

(80)

Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}}
for
\bm{b}\geq\bm{b}_{0}
. Thus, Slater’s condition remains to hold when the constraint threshold is relaxed. Furthermore, for
\bm{P}\in\mathfrak{P}
consider the orthogonal projection

\operatorname{Proj}^{\bm{P}}(\bar{\bm{\mu}})\vcentcolon=\argmin_{\bm{\mu}:(\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P})^{\top}\bm{\mu}=(1-\gamma)\bm{\nu}_{0}}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}\right\rVert_{2},

(81)

of
\bar{\bm{\mu}}
onto the affine hull of
\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}_{0}}
. Since
(\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P})
has full rank, the projection
\operatorname{Proj}^{\bm{P}}(\bar{\bm{\mu}})
is continuous in
\bm{P}

(
Penrose 1955
;
Ding 1993
)
. Therefore, if
\bm{P}
is sufficiently close to
\bm{P}_{0}
, we have
\operatorname{Proj}^{\bm{P}}(\bar{\bm{\mu}})>\bm{0}
and
\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\operatorname{Proj}^{\bm{P}}(\bar{\bm{\mu}})<\bm{b}_{0}
i.e.
\operatorname{Proj}^{\bm{P}}(\bar{\bm{\mu}})\in\relint\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}_{0}}
. Hence, there exists some neighborhood
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\bm{P}_{0}}
of
\bm{P}_{0}
such that
\relint\mathcal{F}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}_{0}}\neq\emptyset
for all
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\cap\mathfrak{P}
.

Step 2:

Since Assumption
3.3
(strict convexity) holds, all sets
\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})
are singleton for any
\bm{r},\bm{P},\bm{b}
. Throughout this proof we therefore interpret
\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}
as a single-valued mapping and write
\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})
instead of
\left\{{\bm{\mu}}\right\}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})
.

Now, let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{\bm{P}_{0}}\cap\mathfrak{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}:\bm{b}\geq\bm{b}_{0}}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. Due to Assumption
4.1
(realizability) and Proposition
4.2
we have
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
. Hence, the following chain of implications holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\prime}),\;\forall\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}),\forall\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P},\forall\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}

(82)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\iff}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}}),\;\forall\bm{r}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}),\forall\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P},\forall\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\implies}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r})=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}}),\;\forall\bm{r}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}}),\forall\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\forall\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\iff}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\left[{\partial f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}+\mathcal{C}^{\bm{b}}(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})}\right]_{\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})},\;\forall\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\forall\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iv)}}{{\iff}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\bigcap_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\left[{\partial f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}+\mathcal{C}^{\bm{b}}(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})}\right]_{\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(v)}}{{\iff}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\bigcap_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\left[{\nabla f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}+\mathcal{C}^{\bm{b}}(\bm{\mu})}\right]_{\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})}.

Here,
(i)
holds since
\bm{r}^{\text{E}}\in\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})
,
(ii)
follows from
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, and
(iii)
is a consequence of Theorem
4.5
which applies since Assumption
3.2
(Slater’s condition) is satisfied for all
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
(iv)
follows from the definition of the intersection, and
(v)
from differentiability and Assumption
4.9
which ensures
\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mu})=\bm{0}
.

Next, we recall that
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Thus, we may choose a large enough
\bar{\bm{b}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
such that the set of active safety constraints is empty for any
\bm{\mu}
and hence
\mathcal{C}^{\bar{\bm{b}}}(\bm{\mu})=\bm{0}
. This allows us to further simplify (
82
) to:

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\bigcap_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\left[{\nabla f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}+\mathcal{C}^{\bm{b}}(\bm{\mu})}\right]_{\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bm{b}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})}

(83)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\left[{\nabla f(\bm{\mu})+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}}\right]_{\bm{\mu}=\mathsf{RL}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P},\bar{\bm{b}}}(\bm{r}^{\text{E}})}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathsf{IRL}_{\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F}}^{\bm{P}_{0},\bm{b}_{0}}(\bm{\mu}^{\text{E}})\subseteq\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\left[{\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}}\right]=\bm{r}^{\text{E}}+\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that
\bigcap_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}\subseteq\spn\bm{1}_{nm}
. In particular, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}_{2}}=\spn\left({\mathbf{1}_{nm}}\right)
for two
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. To that end, we will continue by first showing that there are
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}}=\spn\left({\mathbf{1}_{nm}}\right)
.

Step 3:
Note that, as shown by
Rolland et al. 2022
, the condition
\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}}=\spn\left({\mathbf{1}_{nm}}\right)
is equivalent to

\rank\begin{bmatrix}\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=2n-1.

(84)

This follows from the two facts that
(a)
for any
\bm{P}\in\mathfrak{P}
we have
\bm{1}_{nm}\in\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}}
and
(b)
the condition (
84
) is equivalent to
\dim(\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}})=1
. Here,
(a)
holds since

(\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P})\bm{1}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}(\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{P}_{a_{1}})\bm{1}_{n}\\
\vdots\\
(\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{P}_{a_{m}})\bm{1}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\gamma)\bm{1}_{n}\\
\vdots\\
(1-\gamma)\bm{1}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=(1-\gamma)\bm{1}_{nm},

(85)

where we use that
\bm{1}_{n}
is for both
\bm{I}_{n}
and
\bm{P}_{a_{i}},i=1,...,m
an eigenvector to the eigenvalue
1
. Furthermore,
(b)
is a consequence of

\text{dim}(\spn\bm{A}_{1}\cap\spn\bm{A}_{2})=(\rank\bm{A}_{1}+\rank\bm{A}_{2})-\rank\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{1},\bm{A}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(86)

for the two matrices
\bm{A}_{i}=\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{i},i=1,2
. Therefore, the goal for this step is to prove the following claim:

Claim
There exist
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}
such that
\rank\begin{bmatrix}\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=2n-1
.

Note that since the vector
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{1}_{n}^{\top},-\bm{1}_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
lies in the kernel of
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
, the rank cannot be larger than
2n-1
. Moreover, since the rank of a matrix is larger or equal than the rank of any submatrix, it suffices to prove the claim for
m=2
. To this end, let
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}
be defined as follows

\bar{\bm{P}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n}\\
\bm{D}\end{bmatrix},\;\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{D}\\
\bm{I}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

(87)

where

\bm{D}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&1&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

(88)

It then holds
\rank(\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{D})=n-1
as is readily seen since
\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{D}
is an upper triangular matrix in row echelon form. Now, in order to prove that the rank of

C\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bm{E}-\gamma\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n},

(89)

equals
2n-1
, we show that the first
2n-1
columns of
C
are linearly independent. To this end, let
C^{(-2n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times(2n-1)}
denote the submatrix obtained by removing the last column of
C
. Moreover, let
\bm{z}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}^{\top}&\bm{y}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
with
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The columns of
C^{(-2n)}
are linearly independent if

C^{(-2n)}\bm{z}=\bm{0}\implies\bm{z}=\bm{0}.

(90)

Plugging in the definition of
\bar{\bm{P}}_{1},\bar{\bm{P}}_{2}
it holds

\displaystyle C^{(-2n)}\bm{z}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\gamma)\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D}^{(-n)}\\
\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D}&(1-\gamma)\bm{I}_{n}^{(-n)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\gamma)\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D}\\
\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D}&(1-\gamma)\bm{I}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}\\
\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(91)

where we again use the notation
\bm{B}^{(-n)}
to denote the submatrix of some matrix
\bm{B}
obtained when removing the

-th column. Therefore, we can rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle\bm{x}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}-\gamma\left({\bm{x}+\bm{D}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

(92)

\displaystyle\bm{x}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}-\gamma\left({\bm{D}\bm{x}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

Substituting
\tilde{\bm{x}}\vcentcolon=\bm{x}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}
we get

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\tilde{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\bm{D}-\bm{I}_{n})\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

(93)

\displaystyle(\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D})\tilde{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{D}-\bm{I}_{n})\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

This implies

(1-\gamma)\tilde{\bm{x}}+(\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D})\tilde{\bm{x}}=2(\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\tilde{\bm{D}})\tilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{0},

(94)

for
\tilde{\bm{D}}\vcentcolon=(\bm{I}_{n}+\bm{D})/2
. Since
\tilde{\bm{D}}
is again a row stochastic matrix,
\bm{I}_{n}-\gamma\tilde{\bm{D}}
is invertible and thus
\tilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{0}
. Moreover, since
\rank(\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{D})=n-1
and
(\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{D})\bm{1}_{n}=\bm{0}
, we have
\ker(\bm{I}_{n}-\bm{D})=\spn(\bm{1}_{n})
. In light of

(\bm{D}-\bm{I}_{n})\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0},

(95)

this implies that
\bm{y}=\bm{0}
, which proves the claim.

Step 4:

Equipped with the above claim, the final step of the proof is to show that there are
\bm{P}_{1},\bm{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
such that

.\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}_{1}}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bm{P}_{2}}=\spn(\bm{1}_{nm}).

(96)

Analogously to the previous step, we will show that

\rank\begin{bmatrix}\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P}_{1},\bm{E}-\gamma\bm{P}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=2n-1.

(97)

For this purpose, we choose two arbitrary (and possibly equal)

Appendix E
Algorithm

We present the policy based algorithm for entropy regularization as used in the experiments. To this end, recall the min-max problem (
23
)

\min_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{F}}\;\;\bm{r}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}}\right)-f(\bm{\mu}),

(123)

and the entropy regularization
f(\bm{\mu})=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\bm{\mu}}\left[{H\left(\bm{\pi}^{\bm{\mu}}(\cdot|s)\right)}\right]
. Applying Proposition
3.4
this is equivalent to

Using the one-to-one mapping between policies and occupancy measures and the linear reward class

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\vcentcolon=\left\{{\bm{r}_{\bm{w}}=\bm{\Phi}\bm{w}:\bm{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times d},\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert\leq c}\right\},

(125)

this can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\xi}\geq\bm{0},\bm{r}\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}\bm{r}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}}\right)-f(\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}})+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\left({\bm{b}-\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\xi}\geq\bm{0},\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert\leq c}\max_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\left({\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\text{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}}\right)-f(\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}})+\bm{\xi}^{\top}\left({\bm{b}-\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\pi}}}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\xi}\geq\bm{0},\left\lVert\bm{w}\right\rVert\leq c}\max_{\bm{\pi}\in\Pi}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{\pi},\bm{w},\bm{\xi}),

with the Lagrangian

(
