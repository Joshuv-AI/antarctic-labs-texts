Quantum vs classical Markov chains; Exactly solvable examples


Abstract
A coinless quantisation procedure of general reversible Markov chains on graphs is presented. A quantum Hamiltonian ℋ \mathcal{H} is obtained by a similarity transformation of the fundamental transition probability matrix K K in terms of the square root of the reversible distribution. The evolution of the classical and quantum Markov chains is described by the solutions of the eigenvalue problem of the quantum Hamiltonian ℋ \mathcal{H} . About twenty plus exactly solvable Markov chains based on the hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme, derived by Odake-Sasaki, would provide a good window for scrutinising the quantum/classical contrast of Markov chains. Among them five explicit examples, related to the Krawtchouk, Hahn, q q -Hahn, Charlier and Meixner, are demonstrated to illustrate the actual calculations.

2
Problem setting, classical

In order to formulate the quantum version of the general stationary Markov chains on graphs,
let us first recapitulate the well-known problem setting of the classical Markov chains on
a graph
G=(V,E)
,
[
2
]
.
The set of vertices
V
of the graph
G
is is either finite or semi-infinite;

V=\{0,1,\ldots,N\}:\quad\text{finite},\qquad V=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\quad\text{semi-infinite}.

(2.1)

For analytic treatments I use
x,y,..
as representing the the vertices of
V
,
x,y\in{V}

rather than the conventional
n
and
m
etc. The set of edges of
G
is denoted by
E
.
In quantum mechanics with a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
, time evolution is usually denoted by

\langle{\rm final\ state}|e^{-i\mathcal{H}t}|{\rm initial\ state}\rangle
.
Likewise I use the convention that the transition probability matrix per unit time interval
K(x,y)
on
V
means
the transition from an initial vertex
y
to a final vertex
x
.
In probability community, the opposite convention is dominant.
The matrix
K
is non-negative and satisfies the conservation of probability

K(x,y)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}K(x,y)=1.

(2.2)

The Schrödinger equation is time-reversal invariant.
With quantum treatments in mind, the following condition is imposed.

Condition 2.1

The Markov chain described by
K
is connected and reversible.
That is,
K
has a stationary (detailed balanced)
distribution
\pi(x)
satisfying

K(x,y)\pi(y)=K(y,x)\pi(x),\qquad\forall x,y\in{V},\quad\pi(x)>0,\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi(x)=1.

(2.3)

A simple random walk on a finite connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
without a self-loop
becomes reversible for the following choices
[
31
,
33
]
,

K(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{d(y)}&\text{if}\ (x,y)\in{E}\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,\qquad\pi(x)=\frac{d(x)}{2m},

(2.4)

in which
d(x)
is the degree of the vertex
x
,
i.e.
the number of edges connected to
x
,
and
m
is the total number of edges
m=\#(E)
.
Since there are
d(y)
vertices directly connected to
y
,
\sum_{x\in{V}}K(x,y)=1

and
\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi(x)=1
. The reversibility condition (
2.3
) is trivially satisfied.

The right and left eigenvectors of
K
of eigenvalue 1 are easy to find.

Proposition 2.2

The right and left eigenvectors of
K
of eigenvalue 1 are
\pi(x)
and 1,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K(x,y)\pi(y)

\displaystyle=\pi(x)\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)=\pi(x),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)v_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)=1=v_{0}(x),\quad v_{0}(x)\equiv 1.

(2.6)

It should be stressed that
v_{0}(x)\equiv 1
is not an eigenvector when the graph G
is infinite, as it is not square summable
v_{0}(x)\notin\ell^{2}(V)
.

As a sequel to
v_{0}(x)
, let us consider the general solutions of the left eigenvalue problem
of
K
,

\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)v_{n}(y)=\kappa(n)v_{n}(x),\quad n\in{V},\quad\kappa(0)=1.

(2.7)

Next let us introduce the square root of the stationary distribution

\pi(x)
and a diagonal matrix
\Phi
on V,

\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\pi(x)},\quad\Phi(x,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\hat{\phi}_{0}(x),\quad\Phi(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}0\ \ (x\neq y).

(2.8)

Proposition 2.3

By dividing both sides of the reversibility condition (
2.3
) by

\sqrt{\pi(x)\pi(y)}
, a non-negative and real symmetric matrix
\mathcal{T}
is obtained,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}K(x,y)\sqrt{\pi(y)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(y)}}K(y,x)\sqrt{\pi(x)}=\mathcal{T}(y,x),

(2.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\mathcal{T}=\Phi^{-1}K\Phi,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{T}(x,y)\sqrt{\pi(y)}v_{n}(y)=\sqrt{\pi(x)}\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)v_{n}(y)=\kappa(n)\sqrt{\pi(x)}v_{n}(x),\quad n\in{V},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bigl(\mathcal{T}\phi_{n}\bigr)(x)=\kappa(n)\phi_{n}(x),\quad\phi_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}v_{n}(x)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)v_{n}(x),\qquad\qquad\ \,n\in{V},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\bigl(K\hat{\phi}_{0}\phi_{n}\bigr)(x)=\sum_{y\in{V}}K(x,y)\pi(y)v_{n}(y)=\pi(x)\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)v_{n}(y)=\kappa(n)\pi(x)v_{n}(x)

(2.13)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\phi_{n}(x),\hskip 207.7052ptn\in{V}.

(2.14)

The non-negative and real symmetric matrix corresponding to
\mathcal{T}
was reported in
[
2
]
.

The vectors
\{\phi_{n}\}
are eigenvectors of
\mathcal{T}
for finite graphs
G
. For infinite graphs they have to be
square summable,
\phi_{n}\in\ell^{2}(V)
,

\displaystyle(\phi_{n},\phi_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in{V}}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{m}(x)=\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi(x)v_{n}(x)v_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{d_{n}^{2}}\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V}.

(2.15)

Here the normalisation constants
\{d_{n}\}
are positive
d_{n}>0
.
In case of degenerate eigenvalues, the orthogonalisation is assumed.
For the exactly solvable examples introduced in section four, the orthogonality of the eigenvectors is
guaranteed.

This leads to the following

Proposition 2.4

K
and
\mathcal{T}
share all the eigenvalues
\{\kappa(n)\}
(
2.7
)
which are real and Perron-Frobenius theorem tells

-1\leq\kappa(n)\leq 1,\quad n\in{V},

(2.16)

and the eigenvectors are related by the multiplication of
\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)
,

\bigl(\mathcal{T}\phi_{n}\bigr)(x)=\kappa(n)\phi_{n}(x),\quad\bigl(K\hat{\phi}_{0}\phi_{n}\bigr)(x)=\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\phi_{n}(x),\quad n\in{V}.

(2.17)

If
K
is a positive matrix,
-1
does not belong to the set of eigenvalues,
-1<\kappa(n)
.
In all the exactly solvable examples to be introduced in section four,
K
is a positive matrix.

A candidate for a possible quantum Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
of the classical Markov chain
K

is introduced in the following.

Proposition 2.5

A real symmetric and positive semi-definite matrix
\mathcal{H}
on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(V)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{1}-\mathcal{T}=\Phi^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{1}-K\right)\Phi.

(2.18)

It shares all the eigenvectors with
\mathcal{T}
,

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{H}\phi_{n}\bigr)(x)=\mathcal{E}(n)\phi_{n}(x),\qquad 0\leq\mathcal{E}(n)=1-\kappa(n)\leq 2,\quad n\in{V}.

(2.19)

Here
\boldsymbol{1}
is the identity matrix on
\ell^{2}(V)
. In theories of random walk on infinite graphs

[
33
]

K-\boldsymbol{1}
is the Laplacian
and the negative Laplacian is a good candidate of a quantum Hamiltonian.
In terms of the orthonormal basis
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
of
\ell^{2}(V)
,

\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\phi_{n}(x)d_{n},\quad n\in{V},

(2.20)

the spectral representations of
\mathcal{H}
and
K
read as follows,

Proposition 2.6

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{V}}\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y),\qquad\qquad x,y\in{V},

(2.21)

\displaystyle K(x,y)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{0}(y)^{-1}\sum_{n\in{V}}\kappa(n)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y),\quad x,y\in{V}.

(2.22)

3
The evolution of Markov chains

Some fundamental formulas of the evolution of the reversible Markov chains described by

K(x,y)
(
2.2
)–(
2.22
) are listed here, classical vs quantum.

3.1
Classical evolution

Let
\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)\geq 0
(
\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=1
)
be the probability distribution over
V
at
\ell
-th time step.
The next step distribution is given by

\mathcal{P}(x;\ell+1)=\sum_{y\in{V}}K(x,y)\mathcal{P}(y;\ell),

(3.1)

and the condition
\sum_{x\in{V}}K(x,y)=1
(
2.2
) guarantees the conservation of probability

\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x;\ell+1)=1

during the evolution.

The initial value problem is to determine
\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)
for given
\mathcal{P}(x;0)
,

\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=\sum_{y\in{V}}(K)^{\ell}(x,y)\mathcal{P}(y;0),\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x;0)=1,\quad\mathcal{P}(x;0)\geq 0.

(3.2)

The solutions are summarised by the following well known

Theorem 3.1

In terms of the spectral representation of
K
(
2.22
)
the solution of the initial value problem of the classical Markov chain
is given by

\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n\in{V}}c_{n}\bigl(\kappa(n)\bigr)^{\ell}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),

(3.3)

in which
\{c_{n}\}
are determined as the expansion coefficients of the initial distribution
\mathcal{P}(x;0)
,

\mathcal{P}(x;0)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n\in{V}}c_{n}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\Rightarrow c_{0}=1,\ c_{n}=\sum_{x\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)^{-1}\mathcal{P}(x;0),\quad n=1,\ldots.

(3.4)

The transition probability from
y
to
x
after
\ell
steps is

\mathcal{P}(x,y;\ell)=(K)^{\ell}(x,y)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{0}(y)^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{X}}(\kappa(n))^{\ell}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y).

(3.5)

If
K
is positive, the eigenvalue
-1
is absent and the reversible distribution
\pi(x)

(
2.3
) becomes the stationary distribution after large steps,

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=\pi(x),\qquad\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(x,y;\ell)=\pi(x).

(3.6)

3.2
Quantum evolution

The quantum Markov chain is described in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(V)

with the orthonormal basis

|x\rangle,\quad\langle y|x\rangle=\delta_{y\,x},\quad x,y\in{V}.

It is expressed in the conventional vector notation

|x\rangle\equiv e_{x}=\vskip-4.0pt\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&\!\!{0}&{\cdots}&{x}&x+1&{\cdots}&\\
{}^{t}(&\!\!0&\cdots&1&0&\cdots&)\\
&&&&&&\end{array}\ \Longrightarrow\sum_{x\in{V}}|x\rangle\langle x|=\boldsymbol{1}.\vskip-10.0pt

(3.7)

Another orthonormal basis is the normalised eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
,

\{\hat{\phi}_{n}\}
,
(\hat{\phi}_{m},\hat{\phi}_{n})=\delta_{m\,n}
,
n,m\in{V}
(
2.20
).
By using the notation
|x\rangle
, it can be expressed by
\|n\rangle\!\rangle
,

\|n\rangle\!\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)|x\rangle\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \langle x\|n\rangle\!\rangle=\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),\qquad\langle\!\langle m\|n\rangle\!\rangle=\delta_{m\,n},\quad n,m\in{V}.

(3.8)

Corresponding to (
3.7
) one has

\sum_{n\in{V}}\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|=\boldsymbol{1},

(3.9)

which is the completeness relation

\sum_{n\in{V}}\langle x\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|y\rangle=\sum_{n\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\delta_{x\,y},\quad x,y\in{V}.

(3.10)

The spectral representations of the Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}
(
2.21
) in this notation reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{V}}\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathcal{H}(x,y)=\langle x|\mathcal{H}|y\rangle\ =\sum_{n\in{V}}\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y).

(3.11)

The evolution of the discrete time system is governed by a unitary matrix
U
acting on
\ell^{2}(V)

which is the exponentiation of
\mathcal{H}
,

|\psi(\ell+1)\rangle=U|\psi(\ell)\rangle,\quad U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}e^{-i\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))}\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|\ .

(3.12)

The normalised general initial state
|\psi(0)\rangle
can be represented as

\displaystyle|\psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in{V}}\varphi(z)|z\rangle,\quad\ \varphi(z)\in\mathbb{C},\quad\ \sum_{z\in{V}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}=1.

(3.13)

Corresponding to
Theorem
3.1
, the quantum version is the following

Theorem 3.2

In terms of the spectral representation of the quantum Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
(
2.21
), the solution of the initial value problem is given by

\displaystyle|\psi(\ell)\rangle=U^{\ell}|\psi(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|\psi(0)\rangle

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,z\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}\|n\rangle\!\rangle\hat{\phi}_{n}(z)\varphi(z).

(3.15)

The time evolution is causal and the non-causal effects of branching appear at the measurements.
For the initial state
|\psi(0)\rangle=|y\rangle
(
\varphi(z)=\delta_{z\,y}
)
at
\ell=0
, the probability amplitude of arriving at
the state
|x\rangle
at
\ell
-th step is

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y;\ell)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\langle x|U^{\ell}|y\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}\langle x\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|y\rangle=\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y).

(3.16)

The probability of measuring the state
|x\rangle
at
\ell
-th step is

\displaystyle|\Psi(x,y;\ell)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{n\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)^{2}\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)^{2}+2\!\!\sum_{n>m\in{V}}\!\!\cos[\bigl(\kappa(n)\!-\!\kappa(m)\bigr)\ell]\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{m}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)\hat{\phi}_{m}(y).

(3.17)

Various stochastic quantities can be calculated based on these expressions.
Since pure state quantum mechanics is time-reversal invariant,
the above expressions do not converge as
\ell\to\infty
.
In other words, there is no stationary (or terminal, limiting) distribution.
The long time average is

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\ell=0}^{T}|\Psi(x,y;\ell)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)^{2}\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)^{2},

(3.18)

which is symmetric in
x
and
y
.

Remark 3.3

It should be stressed that in the coinless quantum formulations, the continuous time and the discrete time results
look almost identical when expressed in terms of the spectral representations.

Explicitly solvable examples; Quantum cases

In
[
24
]
Odake and myself reported about two dozens of reversible classical Markov chains of
finite and infinite dimensions.
They are explicitly solved in the sense all the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are
obtained analytically.
These Markov chains are generated by five different types of convolutions of the orthogonality measures

[
9
]

of hypergeometric polynomials of the Askey scheme
[
5
,
14
,
16
,
22
]
,
e.g.

the Krawtchouk, Hahn and
q
-Hahn polynomials etc.
Some of these Markov chains are generalisations of the earlier works
[
10
,
12
,
13
]
.
Those including
q
-polynomials are totally new.
The
K
of these solvable Markov chains contain many adjustable free parameters
so that they could enjoy wide ranges of applications in various disciplines.
The eigenvalues of
K
are terminated (
q
)-hypergeometric functions, see (
4.4
), (
4.19
),
(
4.34
), (
4.49
), (
4.61
) and
most of them are expressed as monomials of the powers and (
q
)-shifted factorials of the contained parameters,
see (
4.4
), (
4.34
), (
4.49
), (
4.61
).
The eigenvectors are the (
q
-)orthogonal polynomials themselves.

The main claim of this paper is the following.

Theorem 4.1

All of the classical reversible Markov chains reported by Odake and myself
[
24
]

provide exactly solvable quantum Markov chains without a coin.
Since the entire set of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors is available analytically,
the formulas of
Theorem
3.2
apply directly.
Various quantities, e.g. hitting time, mixing time, sampling time, filling time,
dispersion time etc
[

]

can be calculated explicitly.

Below I replicate some typical examples of the solvable Markov chains from
[
24
]
to show
how the formulas in

3.2
look like.
The first three correspond to finite Markov chains and

is the maximal coordinate of the vertices.
The next two are infinite ones.
Throughout this section, the

is suppressed
for simplicity of presentation.

4.1
Type (i) convolution,
q
-Hahn

The type (i) convolution for constructing
K
was employed in (3.1) of
[
13
]

and (1.1) of
[
12
]
in connection with the ‘cumulative Bernoulli trials’ of Krawtchouk processes.
But the convolution works for other finite polynomials. Here I demonstrate it for the
q
-Hahn polynomials,
see §4.1.3 of
[

]
for more details.
The fundamental transition probability matrix
K
for the
q
-Hahn is

K_{qH}(x,y)=\sum_{z=0}^{\min(x,y)}\pi_{qH}(x-z,N-z,b,c)\pi_{qH}(z,y,a,b),\quad x,y\in{V},

(4.1)

in which
\pi_{qH}
is the normalised orthogonality measure of the
q
-Hahn polynomial,

\displaystyle 0<\pi_{qH}(x,N,a,b)=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,N\,}{x}\frac{(a\,;q)_{x}\,(b\,;q)_{N-x}a^{N-x}}{(ab\,;q)_{N}},\quad\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,N\,}{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{(q\,;q)_{N}}{(q\,;q)_{x}\,(q\,;q)_{N-x}},

(4.2)

\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{qH}(x,N,a,b)=1
, with the parameter ranges
0<a<1
,
b<1
.
It is easy to see the conservation of the probability

\sum_{x\in{V}}K_{qH}(x,y)=1
.
The reversible distribution is
\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c)
,

K_{qH}(x,y)\pi_{qH}(y,N,ab,c)=K_{qH}(y,x)\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c).

(4.3)

It can be verified by using (
4.2
) without performing the summation explicitly.
The eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\kappa(n)={}_{3}\phi_{2}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n},\,abcq^{n-1},\,b}{ab,\,bc}\Bigm|q\,;q\Bigr)=\frac{b^{n}(a\,;q)_{n}(c\,;q)_{n}}{(ab\,;q)_{n}(bc\,;q)_{n}},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\hskip 82.51299pt\check{P}_{n}(x,ab,c),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{qH}(x,y)\pi_{qH}(y,N,ab,c)\check{P}_{n}(y,ab,c)=\kappa(n)\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c)\check{P}_{n}(x,ab,c).

(4.6)

The explicit form of the polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)
and its normalisation constant are

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)=P_{n}\bigl(\eta(x),a,b\bigr)={}_{3}\phi_{2}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n},\,abq^{n-1},\,q^{-x}}{a,\,q^{-N}}\Bigm|q\,;q\Bigr),\quad\eta(x)=q^{-x}-1,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{qH}(x,N,a,b)\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)\check{P}_{m}(x,a,b)=\frac{1}{d_{n}(a,b)^{2}}\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V},

(4.8)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188ptd_{n}(a,b)^{2}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,N\,}{n}\frac{(a,abq^{-1}\,;q)_{n}}{(abq^{N},b\,;q)_{n}\,a^{n}}\frac{1-abq^{2n-1}}{1-abq^{-1}}.

(4.9)

The normalised eigenvectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{qH}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{qH}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c)}}\left(\boldsymbol{1}-K_{qH}(x,y)\right)\sqrt{\pi_{qH}(y,N,ab,c)},

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c)}\,d_{n}(ab,c)\,\check{P}_{n}(x,ab,c),

(4.11)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{qH}\hat{\phi}_{n}\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{H}_{qH}(x,y)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x).

(4.12)

The probability amplitude
\Psi(x,y;\ell)
(
3.16
) becomes simple when
y=0
,
since
\check{P}_{n}(0)=1
.

\displaystyle\Psi(x,0;\ell)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{qH}(x,N,ab,c)\pi_{qH}(0,N,ab,c)}\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}d_{n}(ab,c)^{2}\check{P}_{n}(x,ab,c),

(4.13)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\pi_{qH}(0,N,ab,c)=\frac{(c\,;q)_{N}(ab)^{N}}{(abc\,;q)_{N}},\quad\pi_{qH}(N,N,ab,c)=\frac{(ab\,;q)_{N}}{(abc\,;q)_{N}}.

(4.14)

It is further simplified when
x=N
, as

\check{P}_{n}(N,ab,c)=(-ab)^{n}q^{n(n-1)/2}\frac{(c\,;q)_{n}}{(ab\,;q)_{n}}.

(4.15)

4.2
Type (ii) convolution, Hahn

The type (ii) convolution for the Hahn polynomial is a three parameter generalisation of
the one parameter model of Burnside process by Diaconis and Zhong
[
10
]
.
The convolution also works for the Krawtchouk polynomial but not for the
q
-Hahn. See §4.2.2 of
[
24
]

for more details.
The fundamental transition probability matrix
K
for the Hahn is

K_{H}(x,y)=\!\!\!\sum_{z=\max(0,x+y-N)}^{\min(x,y)}\!\!\!\pi_{H}(x-z,N-y,b,c)\pi_{H}(z,y,a,b),

(4.16)

in which
\pi_{H}
is the normalised orthogonality measure of the Hahn polynomial,

\displaystyle 0<\pi_{H}(x,N,a,b)=\binom{N}{x}\frac{(a)_{x}\,(b)_{N-x}}{(a+b)_{N}},\quad 0<a,b,\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{H}(x,N,a,b)=1.

(4.17)

It is easy to see the conservation of the probability

\sum_{x\in{V}}K_{H}(x,y)=1
.
The reversible distribution is
\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c)
,

K_{H}(x,y)\pi_{H}(y,N,a+b,b+c)=K_{H}(y,x)\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c),

(4.18)

which can be verified by using (
4.17
) without performing the summation explicitly.
The eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\kappa(n)\!=\!{}_{3}F_{2}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n,\,n+a+2b+c-1,\,b}{a+b,\,b+c}\Bigm|1\Bigr)\!\!=\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}\frac{(b)_{k}(n+a+2b+c-1)_{k}}{(a+b)_{k}(b+c)_{k}},

(4.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\check{P}_{n}(x,a+b,b+c),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{H}(x,y)\pi_{H}(y,N,a+b,b+c)\check{P}_{n}(y,a+b,b+c)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\kappa(n)\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c)\check{P}_{n}(x,a+b,b+c).

(4.21)

The explicit form of the polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)
and its normalisation constant are

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)=P_{n}(x,a,b)={}_{3}F_{2}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n,\,n+a+b-1,\,-x}{a,\,-N}\Bigm|1\Bigr),

(4.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{H}(x,N,a,b)\check{P}_{n}(x,a,b)\check{P}_{m}(x,a,b)=\frac{1}{d_{n}(a,b)^{2}}\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V},

(4.23)

\displaystyle d_{n}(a,b)^{2}=\binom{N}{n}\frac{(a)_{n}\,(2n+a+b-1)(a+b)_{N}}{(b)_{n}\,(n+a+b-1)_{N+1}}.

(4.24)

The normalised eigenvectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{H}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{H}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c)}}\left(\boldsymbol{1}-K_{H}(x,y)\right)\sqrt{\pi_{H}(y,N,a+b,b+c)},

(4.25)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c)}\,d_{n}(a+b,b+c)\,\check{P}_{n}(x,a+b,b+c),

(4.26)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{H}\hat{\phi}_{n}\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{H}_{H}(x,y)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x).

(4.27)

The probability amplitude
\Psi(x,y;\ell)
(
3.16
) becomes simple when
y=0
,
since
\check{P}_{n}(0)=1
,

\displaystyle\Psi(x,0;\ell)=\sqrt{\pi_{H}(x,N,a+b,b+c)\pi_{H}(0,N,a+b,b+c)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}d_{n}(a+b,b+c)^{2}\check{P}_{n}(x,a+b,b+c),

(4.28)

\displaystyle\pi_{H}(0,N,a+b,b+c)=\frac{(b+c)_{N}}{(a+2b+c)_{N}},\quad\pi_{H}(N,N,a+b,b+c)=\frac{(a+b)_{N}}{(a+2b+c)_{N}}.

(4.29)

It is further simplified when
x=N
, as

\check{P}_{n}(N,a+b,b+c)=(-1)^{n}\frac{(b+c)_{n}}{(a+b)_{n}}.

(4.30)

4.3
Type (iii) convolution, Krawtchouk

The Markov chain generated by type (iii) convolution was, to the best of my knowledge,
first introduced in
[
24
]
.
Here is an example using the Krawtchouk polynomial.
The convolution is applicable to the Hahn and

q
-Hahn polynomials as well. See §4.3 of
[
24
]
for more details.
The fundamental transition probability matrix
K
for the Krawtchouk is

\displaystyle K_{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=\max(x,y)}^{N}\pi_{K}(x,z,b)\pi_{K}(z-y,N-y,a),\quad 0<a,b<1,

(4.31)

in which
\pi_{K}
is the normalised orthogonality measure of the Krawtchouk polynomial,

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(x,N,a)=\binom{N}{x}a^{x}(1-a)^{N-x},\quad 0<a<1,\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{K}(x,N,a)=1.

(4.32)

The reversible distribution is
\pi_{K}(x,N,p)
,

\displaystyle K_{K}(x,y)\pi_{K}(y,N,p)=K_{K}(y,x)\pi_{K}(x,N,p),\qquad p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{ab}{1-b+ab},

(4.33)

which can be verified by using (
4.32
) without performing the summation explicitly.
The eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\kappa(n)={}_{1}F_{0}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n}{-}\Bigm|abp^{-1}\Bigr)=(1-a)^{n}b^{n},\qquad\check{P}_{n}(x,p),

(4.34)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{K}(x,y)\pi_{K}(y,N,p)\check{P}_{n}(y,p)=\kappa(n)\pi_{K}(x,N,p)\check{P}_{n}(x,p).

(4.35)

The explicit form of the polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x,a)
and its normalisation constant are

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(x,a)=P_{n}(x,a)={}_{2}F_{1}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n,\,-x}{-N}\Bigm|a^{-1}\Bigr),\quad P_{n}(x,a)=P_{x}(n,a),

(4.36)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{K}(x,N,a)\check{P}_{n}(x,a)\check{P}_{m}(x,a)=\frac{1}{d_{n}(a)^{2}}\,\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V},

(4.37)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188ptd_{n}(a)^{2}=\binom{N}{n}\Bigl(\frac{a}{1-a}\Bigr)^{n}.

(4.38)

The normalised eigenvectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{K}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{K}(x,N,p)}}\left(\boldsymbol{1}-K_{K}(x,y)\right)\sqrt{\pi_{K}(y,N,p)},

(4.39)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{K}(x,N,p)}\,d_{n}(p)\check{P}_{n}(x,p),

(4.40)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{K}\hat{\phi}_{n}\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{H}_{K}(x,y)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x).

(4.41)

The probability amplitude
\Psi(x,y;\ell)
(
3.16
) becomes simple when
y=0
,
since
\check{P}_{n}(0)=1
,

\displaystyle\Psi(x,0;\ell)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{K}(x,N,p)\pi_{K}(0,N,p)}\,\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}d_{n}(p)^{2}\check{P}_{n}(x,p),

(4.42)

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(0,N,p)=(1-p)^{N},\quad\pi_{K}(N,N,p)=p^{N}.

(4.43)

It is further simplified when
x=N
, as

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(N,p)=(-1)^{n}(p^{-1}-1)^{n},

(4.44)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\Psi(N,0;\ell)=\frac{\bigl(ab(1-b)\bigr)^{N/2}}{(1-b+ab)^{N}}\sum_{n\in{V}}(-1)^{n}e^{-i(1-(1-a)^{n}b^{n})\ell}\binom{N}{n}.

(4.45)

4.4
Type (i) convolution, Charlier

The Charlier polynomial is obtained from the Krawtchouk polynomial by a certain limit
including
N\to\infty

[
16
]
.
This Markov chain is obtained from that of type (i) Krawtchouk, §4.1.1
[
13
]
.
The fundamental transition probability matrix
K
for the Charlier is,

K_{C}(x,y)=\sum_{z=0}^{\min(x,y)}\pi_{C}(x-z,b)\pi_{K}(z,y,a),\quad x,y\in{V},\quad 0<a,b,

(4.46)

in which
\pi_{C}
is the normalised orthogonality measure of the Charlier polynomial,

\displaystyle\pi_{C}(x,a)=\frac{a^{x}e^{-a}}{x!},\quad 0<a,\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{C}(x,a)=1.

(4.47)

The reversible distribution is
\pi_{C}(x,p)
,

K_{C}(x,y)\pi_{C}(y,p)=K_{C}(y,x)\pi_{C}(x,p),\quad p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{b}{1-a}.

(4.48)

It can be verified by using (
4.47
) without performing the summation explicitly.
The eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\kappa(n)={}_{1}F_{0}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n}{-}\Bigm|bp^{-1}\Bigr)=a^{n},\qquad\check{P}_{n}(y,p),

(4.49)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{C}(x,y)\pi_{C}(y,p)\check{P}_{n}(y,p)=\kappa(n)\pi_{C}(x,p)\check{P}_{n}(x,p).

(4.50)

The explicit form of the polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x,a)
and its normalisation constant are

\displaystyle\check{P}_{n}(x,a)=P_{n}(x,a)={}_{2}F_{0}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n,\,-x}{-}\Bigm|-a^{-1}\Bigr),\quad P_{n}(x,a)=P_{x}(n,a),

(4.51)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi_{C}(x,a)\check{P}_{n}(x,a)\check{P}_{m}(x,a)=\frac{1}{d_{n}(a)^{2}}\delta_{n\,m},\quad d_{n}(a)^{2}=\frac{a^{n}}{n!},\quad n,m\in{V}.

(4.52)

The normalised eigenvectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{C}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{C}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi_{C}(x,p)}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{1}-K_{C}(x,y)\bigr)\sqrt{\pi_{C}(y,p)},

(4.53)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{C}(x,p)}\,d_{n}(p)\check{P}_{n}(x,p),

(4.54)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{C}\hat{\phi}_{n}\bigr)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{H}_{C}(x,y)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\bigl(1-\kappa(n)\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x).

(4.55)

The probability amplitude
\Psi(x,y;\ell)
(
3.16
) becomes simple when
y=0
,

\check{P}_{n}(0)=1
,

\displaystyle\Psi(x,0;\ell)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi_{C}(x,p)\pi_{C}(0,p)}\,\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-i(1-\kappa(n))\ell}d_{n}(p)^{2}\check{P}_{n}(x,p),

(4.56)

\displaystyle\pi_{C}(0,p)=e^{-p}.

\pi_{M}(x,a+b,c)

5
Comments

A few comments are in order.

•

I follow the standard convention of quantum mechanics,
\langle{\rm final\ state}|e^{-i\mathcal{H}t}|{\rm initial\ state}\rangle
.
Correspondingly, the fundamental transition probability matrix
K(x,y)
means the transition from the initial vertex
y

to the final vertex
x
.

•

The main point of the exact solvability of the
K
constructed by the five types of convolutions shown in
[
24
]

is that the orthogonal polynomial
\check{P}_{n}(x)
is the left eigenvector of
K
,

\sum_{y\in{V}}K(y,x)\check{P}_{n}(y)=\kappa(n)\check{P}_{n}(x)\quad(n\in{V}).

(
[
24
]
.2.12)

The proof is given in Appendix of
[
24
]
.
In most cases, the eigenvalue
\kappa(n)
is known, without calculation,
from the factorised form of the transition probability matrix
K
due to the convolution.

•

Time averaged distributions of quantum walks were reported in many papers.
It should be stressed that these results are initial state specific and not universal, cf (
3.18
).

•

It should be noted that, because of the context, the present definition of

d_{n}^{2}
corresponds to
d_{n}^{2}/d_{0}^{2}
in the accompanying paper on the quantum birth and death process

[
28
]

and also previous series of papers

[
22
,
26
,
23
]
.

Appendix

Basic definitions related to the (
q
)-hypergeometric functions

\circ
shifted factorial
(a)_{n}
:

(a)_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(a+k-1)=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)=\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}.

(A.1)

\circ

q
-shifted factorial
(a\,;q)_{n}
:

(a\,;q)_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1-aq^{k-1})=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1}).

(A.2)

\circ
hypergeometric function
{}_{r}F_{s}
:

{}_{r}F_{s}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\,\cdots,a_r}{b_1,\,\cdots,b_s}\Bigm|z\Bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1},\,\cdots,a_{r})_{n}}{(b_{1},\,\cdots,b_{s})_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\,,

(A.3)

where
(a_{1},\,\cdots,a_{r})_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(a_{j})_{n}=(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{r})_{n}
.

\circ

q
-hypergeometric function (the basic hypergeometric function)

{}_{r}\phi_{s}
:

{}_{r}\phi_{s}\Bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\,\cdots,a_r}{b_1,\,\cdots,b_s}\Bigm|q\,;z\Bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1},\,\cdots,a_{r}\,;q)_{n}}{(b_{1},\,\cdots,b_{s}\,;q)_{n}}(-1)^{(1+s-r)n}q^{(1+s-r)n(n-1)/2}\frac{z^{n}}{(q\,;q)_{n}}\,,

(A.4)

where
(a_{1},\,\cdots,a_{r}\,;q)_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(a_{j}\,;q)_{n}=(a_{1}\,;q)_{n}\cdots(a_{r}\,;q)_{n}
.
