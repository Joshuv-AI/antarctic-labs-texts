Thompson Sampling for Stochastic Bandits with Noisy Contexts: An Information-Theoretic Regret Analysis
Sharu Theresa Jose, Shana Moothedath

Abstract
We study stochastic linear contextual bandits (CB) where the agent observes a noisy version of the true context through a noise channel with unknown channel parameter. Our objective is to design an action policy that can “approximate” that of a Bayesian oracle that has access to the reward model and the noise channel parameter. We introduce a modified Thompson sampling algorithm and analyze its Bayesian cumulative regret with respect to the oracle action policy via information-theoretic tools. For d d -dimensional Gaussian bandits with Gaussian context noise, our information-theoretic analysis shows that the Bayesian cumulative regret scales as O ⁡ ( d ​ T ) O(d\sqrt{T}) , where T T is the horizon. We also consider the problem setting where the agent observes the true context with some delay after receiving the reward, and show that delayed true contexts lead to lower regret. Finally, we empirically demonstrate the performance of the proposed algorithms against baselines.



I
Introduction

Decision-making in the face of uncertainty is a widespread challenge found across various domains such as control and robotics
(
Srivastava et al. 2014
)
, clinical trials
(
Aziz et al. 2021
)
, communications
(
Anandkumar et al. 2011
)
, and ecology
(
Srivastava et al. 2013
)
.
To tackle this challenge, learning algorithms have been developed to uncover effective policies for optimal decision-making. One notable framework for addressing this is contextual bandits (CBs), which captures the essence of sequential decision-making by incorporating side information, termed
context

(
Bubeck and Cesa-Bianchi 2012
)
.
In the standard CB model, an agent interacts with the environment over numerous rounds.
In each round, the environment presents a context to the agent based on which the agent chooses an action and receives a reward from the environment. The goal of the agent is to design a policy for action selection that can maximize the cumulative mean reward accrued over a
T
-length horizon.

While most prior research on CBs has primarily focused on models with known exact contexts
(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Chu et al. 2011
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
)
, in many real-world applications, the contexts are noisy, e.g. imprecise measurement of patient conditions in clinical trials, weather or stock market predictions. In such scenarios when the exact contexts are unknown, the agent must utilize the observed noisy contexts to estimate the mean reward associated with the true context. However, this results in a biased estimate that renders the application of standard CB algorithms unsuitable. Consequently, recent efforts have been made to develop CB algorithms tailored to noisy context settings.

Motivation and Problem Setting:
In this work, inspired by
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
, we consider the following noisy CB setting. In each round, the environment samples a true context vector
c_{t}
from a
context distribution
that is
known
to the agent. The agent, however, does not observe the true context, but observes a noisy context
\hat{c}_{t}
obtained as the output of a noise channel
P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})
parameterized by
\gamma^{*}
. The agent is aware of the noise present but does not know the channel parameter
\gamma^{*}
. Following
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
, we consider Gaussian noise channels for our regret analysis.

Based on the observed noisy contexts, the agent chooses an action
a_{t}
and observes a reward
r_{t}
corresponding to the true context. We consider a linear bandit whose mean reward
\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}
is determined by an unknown reward parameter
\theta^{*}
. The goal of the agent is to design an action policy that minimizes the
Bayesian cumulative regret
with respect to the action policy of a Bayesian oracle. The oracle has access to the
reward model and the channel parameter
\gamma^{*}
, and uses the predictive distribution
of the true context given the observed noisy context
to select an action.

Our setting differs from
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
in that we assume noisy contexts rather than noisy feature vectors and that the agent knows the context distribution. The noise model, incorporating noise in the feature vector, allows
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
to transform the original problem into a different CB problem that estimates a modified reward parameter.
Such a transformation however is not straightforward in our setting with noisy contexts, where we wish to analyze the Bayesian regret. Additionally, we propose a de-noising approach to estimate the predictive distribution of the true context from given noisy contexts, offering potential benefits for future analyses.

The assumption of known context distribution follows from
(
Kirschner and Krause 2019
)
. This can be motivated by considering the example of an online recommendation engine that pre-process the user account registration information or contexts (e.g., age, gender, device, location, item preferences) to group them into different clusters
(
Roy and Dutta 2022
)
. The engine can then infer the ‘empirical’ distribution of users within each cluster to define a context distribution over true contextual information. A noisy contextual information scenario occurs when a guest with different preferences logs into a user’s account.

Challenges and Novelty:
Different from existing works that developed UCB-based algorithms, we propose a fully Bayesian TS algorithm that approximates the Bayesian oracle policy. The proposed algorithm differs from the standard contextual TS
(
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
)
in the following aspects. Firstly, since the true context vectors are not accessible at each round and the channel parameter
\gamma^{*}
is unknown, the agent uses its knowledge of the context distribution and the past observed noisy contexts to infer a
predictive posterior
distribution of the true context from the current observed noisy context. The inferred predictive distribution is then used to choose the action. This
de-noising
step enables our algorithm to ‘approximate’ the oracle action policy that uses knowledge of the channel parameter
\gamma^{*}
to implement
exact de-noising
.
Secondly, the reward
r_{t}
received by the agent corresponds to the unobserved true context
c_{t}
. Hence, the agent cannot accurately evaluate the posterior distribution of
\theta^{*}
and sample from it as is done in standard contextual TS. Instead, our algorithm proposes to use a sampling distribution that ‘approximates’ the posterior.

Moreover, different from existing works that focus on frequentist regret analysis, we derive novel
information-theoretic
bounds on the
Bayesian cumulative regret
of our algorithm. For Gaussian bandits, our information-theoretic regret bounds scale as
O(d\sqrt{T})
where
d,T
denotes the context vector’s dimension and time horizon, respectively. Furthermore, our Bayesian regret analysis shows that the
posterior mismatch
, resulting due to replacing the true posterior distribution with a sampling distribution, results in an approximation error that is captured via the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the distributions.
We also
extend our algorithm to a setting where the agent
observes the true context after a delay following the reward reception
(
Kirschner and Krause 2019
)
, and show that delayed true contexts
result in reduced Bayesian regret.

Problem Setting

In this section, we present the stochastic linear CB problem studied in this paper. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the action set with
K

\mathcal{C}
denote the (possibly infinite) set of
d

t\in\mathbb{N}

c_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
according to a
context distribution

P(c)
defined over the space
\mathcal{C}
of context vectors. The context distribution
P(c)
is known to the agent. The agent, however, does not observe the true context
c_{t}
drawn by the environment. Instead, it observes a noisy version
\hat{c}_{t}
of the true context, obtained as the output of a noisy, stochastic channel
P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})
with the true context
c_{t}
as the input. The noise channel
P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})
is parameterized by the
noise channel parameter

\gamma^{*}
that is
unknown
to the agent.

Having observed the noisy context
\hat{c}_{t}
at iteration
t
, the agent chooses an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
according to an
action policy

\pi_{t}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t})
. The action policy may be stochastic describing a probability distribution over the set
\mathcal{A}
of actions.
Corresponding to the chosen action
a_{t}
, the agent receives a reward from the environment given by

\displaystyle r_{t}=f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})+\xi_{t},

(1)

where
f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})=\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}
is the linear
mean-reward function
and
\xi_{t}
is a zero-mean reward noise variable. The mean reward function
f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})
is defined via the
feature map

\phi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, that maps the action and true context to an
m
-dimensional feature vector, and via the reward parameter
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that is
unknown
to the agent.

We call the noisy CB problem described above
CBs with unobserved true context
(see Setting
1
) since the agent does not observe the true context
c_{t}
and the selection of action is based solely on the observed noisy context. Accordingly, at the end of iteration
t
, the agent has accrued the history
\mathcal{H}_{t,r,a,\hat{c}}=\{r_{\tau},a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=1}^{t}
of observed reward-action-noisy context tuples. The action policy
\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t+1})
at
(t+1)^{\rm th}
iteration may depend on the history
\mathcal{H}_{t,r,a,\hat{c}}
.

. We call this setting

II-A

Bayesian Cumulative Regret

The cumulative regret of an action policy
\pi_{t}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t})
quantifies how different the mean reward accumulated over
T
iterations is from that accrued by a baseline action policy
\pi^{*}_{t}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t})
. In this work, we consider as baseline the action policy of an
oracle
that has access to the channel noise parameter
\gamma^{*}
, reward parameter
\theta^{*}
, the context distribution
P(c)
and the noise channel likelihood
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*}).
Accordingly, at each iteration
t
, the oracle can infer the
exact predictive distribution

P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
of the true context from the observed noisy context
\hat{c}_{t}
via Baye’s rule as

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})=\frac{P(c_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})}{P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})}.

(2)

Here,
P(c_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})=P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})
is the joint distribution of the true and noisy contexts given the noise channel parameter
\gamma^{*}
, and
P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})
is the distribution obtained by marginalizing
P(c_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})
over the true contexts,

i.e.
,

\displaystyle P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t})}[P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})],

(3)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\bullet}[\cdot]
denotes expectation with respect to
`\bullet
’.
The oracle action policy then adopts an action

\displaystyle a^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})}[\phi(a,c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}]

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*},

(4)

at iteration
t
, where
\psi(a,\hat{c}|\gamma^{*}):=\mathbb{E}_{P(c|\hat{c},\gamma^{*})}[\phi(a,c)]
. Note that as in
(
Kim et al. 2023
;
Park and Faradonbeh 2021
)
, we do not choose the stronger oracle action policy of
\arg\max_{a\in{\cal A}}\phi(a,c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}
, that requires access to the true context
c_{t}
, as it is generally not achievable by an agent that observes only noisy context
\hat{c}_{t}
and has no access to
\gamma^{*}
.

For fixed parameters
\theta^{*}
and
\gamma^{*}
, we define the cumulative regret of the action policy
\pi_{t}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t})
as

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\scalebox{0.95}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*})\hskip-2.84526pt=\hskip-2.84526pt\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hskip-2.84526pt\phi(a^{*}_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}\hskip-2.84526pt-\hskip-2.84526pt\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}\bigl|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}\Bigr]$}},

(5)

the expected difference in mean rewards of the oracle decision policy and the agent’s decision policy over
T
iterations. The expectation is taken over the randomness in the selection of actions
a^{*}_{t}
and
a_{t}
, as well as true context
c_{t}
.
Equivalently, the cumulative regret of (
5
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*})

\displaystyle=\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(a^{*}_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}\bigl|\hat{c}_{t},a_{t}\bigr]\bigl|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(a^{*}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}\hskip-1.99168pt-\hskip-1.99168pt\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}\bigl|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*}\bigr].

(6)

Our focus in this work is on a
Bayesian framework
where we assume that the reward parameter
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
and channel noise parameter
\gamma^{*}\in\Gamma
are independently sampled by the environment from prior distributions
P(\theta^{*})
, defined on the set
\Theta
of reward parameters, and
P(\gamma^{*})
, defined on the set
\Gamma
of channel noise parameters, respectively. The agent has knowledge of the prior distributions, the reward likelihood in (
1
) and the noise channel likelihood
P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})
, although it does not observe the sampled
\gamma^{*}
and
\theta^{*}
. Using the above prior distributions, we define
Bayesian cumulative regret
of the action policy
\pi_{t}(\cdot|\hat{c}_{t})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi|\theta^{*},\gamma^{*})],

(7)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the priors
P(\theta^{*})
and
P(\gamma^{*})
.

In the next sections, we present our novel TS algorithms to minimize the Bayesian cumulative regret for the two problem settings considered in this paper.

The proposed algorithm implements two steps in each iteration

P(c)

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})=\mathbb{E}_{P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})}[P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})],

Linear-Gaussian Stochastic CBs

, we show that the above choice of sampling distribution is indeed ‘close’ to the true posterior.

III-B

Bayesian Regret Analysis

In this section, we derive information-theoertic upper bounds on the Bayesian regret (
7
) of the modified TS algorithm for Gaussian CBs. To this end, we first outline the key information-theoretic tools required to derive our bound.

III-B
1
Preliminaries

To start, let
P(x)
and
Q(x)
denote two probability distributions defined over the space
{\cal X}
of random variables
x
. Then, the Kullback Leibler (KL)-divergence between the distributions
P(x)
and
Q(x)
is defined as

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P(x)||Q(x))=\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}\biggl[\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\biggr]$}},

(17)

if
P(x)
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q(x)
, and takes value
\infty
otherwise. If
x
and
y
denote two random variables described by the joint probability distribution
P(x,y)
, the mutual information
I(x;y)
between
x
and
y
is defined as
I(x;y)=D_{\rm KL}(P(x,y)\|P(x)P(y))
, where
P(x)
(and
P(y)
) is the marginal distribution of
x
(and
y
). More generally, for three random variables
x
,
y
and
z
with joint distribution
P(x,y,z)
, the conditional mutual information
I(x;y|z)
between
x
and
y
given
z
evaluates as
I(x;y|z)=\mathbb{E}_{P(z)}[D_{KL}(P(x,y|z)\|P(x|z)P(y|z))]
where
P(x|z)
and
P(y|z)
are conditional distributions.
We will also use the following variational representation of the KL-divergence, also termed the
Donskar-Varadhan (DV)
inequality,

\displaystyle\hskip-9.95845ptD_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))\hskip-2.84526pt\geq\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[f(x)]\hskip-2.84526pt-\hskip-2.84526pt\log\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(f(x))],

(18)

which holds for any measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satifying the inequality
\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(f(x))]<\infty
.

III-B
2
Information-Theoretic Bayesian Regret Bounds

To analyze the Bayesian regret of the proposed algorithm for Gaussian contextual bandits, we start by defining

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}

(19)

as the action that maximizes the mean reward
\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}
corresponding to reward parameter
\theta^{*}
.
Using the above, the Bayesian cumulative regret (
7
) for the proposed TS algorithm
\pi^{\rm{TS}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm{TS}})=\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm CB}}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE1}}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE2}},\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{where}

(20)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm CB}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(\hat{a}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr]$}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE1}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(a^{*}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(\hat{a}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr],

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr].

In (
20
), the first term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm CB}}
quantifies the Bayesian regret of our action policy (
11
) with respect to the action policy (
19
) for a CB with mean reward function
\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}
. The second term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE1}}
accounts for the average difference in the cumulative mean rewards of the oracle optimal action policy (
4
), evaluated using the exact predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
, and our action policy (
11
), that uses the inferred predictive posterior distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})
. In this sense,
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE1}}
captures the error in approximating the exact predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
via the inferred predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{\hat{c}})
. The third term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE2}}
similarly accounts for the average approximation error.
To derive an upper bound on the Bayesian regret
\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm TS})
, we separately upper bound each of the three terms in (
20
). To this end, we make the following assumption.

Assumption III.1
.

The feature map
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
has bounded norm, i.e.,
\|\phi(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{2}\leq 1
.

The following lemma presents an upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm CB}}
.

Lemma III.1
.

Under Assumption
III.1
, the following upper bound holds if
\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{d}{T}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.9}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm CB}}\hskip-1.42271pt\leq\hskip-1.42271ptU(m,\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{T})\hskip-4.2679pt+\hskip-4.2679pt\sqrt{2d\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{t}}$}}\scalebox{0.9}{\mbox{$\displaystyle+\sqrt{2d\sigma^{2}\Bigl(T\log(K)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{t}\Bigr)}$}},

(21)

where
D_{t}=\mathbb{E}[D_{\rm KL}(P_{t}(\theta^{*})\|\bar{P}_{t}(\theta^{*}))]
and

\displaystyle U(m,\lambda)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.9}{\mbox{$\displaystyle=\hskip-2.84526pt\sqrt{2Tm\sigma^{2}\min\{m,2+2\log K\}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{T\lambda}{m\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)}$}}.

(22)

In particular, if the feature map
\phi(a,c)=G(a)c
with
G(a)
being a
d\times d
matrix satisfying Assumption
III.1
with
m=d
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{t}\leq\frac{dT}{4}.

(23)

To derive the upper bound in (
21
), we leverage results from
Neu et al. 2022
that studies information-theoretic Bayesian regret of standard contextual TS algorithms. However, the results do not directly apply to our algorithm due to the
posterior mismatch
between the sampling distribution
\bar{P}_{t}(\theta^{*})
and the true posterior distribution
P_{t}(\theta^{*})
. Consequently, our upper bound (
21
) consists of three terms: the first term, defined as in (
22
), corresponds to the upper bound on the Bayesian regret of contextual TS that assumes
\bar{P}_{t}(\theta^{*})
as the true posterior. This can be obtained by applying
(
Neu et al. 2022
, Cor. 2)
. The second and third terms account for the posterior mismatch via the expected KL-divergence
D_{t}
between the true posterior
P_{t}(\theta^{*})
and the sampling distribution
\bar{P}_{t}(\theta^{*})
.
In particular, when the feature map
\phi(a,c)
is linear in
c
, the above expected KL divergence can be bounded as in (
23
) provided that the prior
P(\theta^{*})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\lambda\mathbb{I})
is sufficiently concentrated. This ensures that the contribution of posterior mismatch to the Bayes regret scales as
O(d\sqrt{T})
.

The following lemma gives an upper bound on the sum
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE1}}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE2}}
.

Lemma III.2
.

Under Assumption
III.1
, the following upper bound holds for
\delta\in(0,1)
if
\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{d}{T}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE1}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE2}\leq 2\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE1}\leq 4\delta^{2}\sqrt{\frac{md\sigma^{2}T}{2\pi}}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle+2\sqrt{4md\sigma^{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{2m}{\delta}\Bigr)\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(\gamma^{*};c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})}$}}.

(24)

In particular, if
\phi(a,c)=G(a)c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies Assumption
III.1
with
m=d
,
\Sigma_{c}=\sigma^{2}_{c}\mathbb{I}
,
\Sigma_{n}=\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbb{I}
, and
\Sigma_{\gamma}=\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}\mathbb{I}
, where
\sigma^{2}_{n},\sigma^{2}_{c},\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}>0
, we have the tigher bound of

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE1}}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm EE2}}\leq 2\sqrt{2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(\gamma^{*};c_{t}|\hat{c},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})}$}},

(25)

where
L=dK\nu\Bigl(\sigma^{2}_{n}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{c}\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}}{T(\sigma^{2}_{c}+\sigma^{2}_{n})}+\sigma^{2}_{c}\frac{\log(T-1)}{T}\Bigr)

1
1

1

\nu=\sigma^{2}\sigma^{2}_{c}/(\sigma^{2}_{c}+\sigma^{2}_{n})\max_{a}{\rm Tr}(G(a)^{\top}G(a))
, with

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(\gamma^{*};c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})\leq\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{c}}{2\sigma^{2}_{n}}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}}{\sigma^{2}_{c}+\sigma^{2}_{n}}+\log(T-1)\Bigr)$}}.

Lemma
III.2
shows that the error in approximating
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
with
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})
, on average, can be quantified via the conditional mutual information
I(\gamma^{*};c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,\hat{c}})
between
\gamma^{*}
and true context
c_{t}
given knowledge of observed noisy contexts upto and including iteration
t
.
Combining Lemma
III.1
and Lemma
III.2
gives us the following upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm{TS}})
.

Theorem III.1
.

Assume that
\phi(a,c)=G(a)c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Sigma_{c}=\sigma^{2}_{c}\mathbb{I}
,
\Sigma_{n}=\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbb{I}
, and

, where
\sigma^{2}_{n},\sigma^{2}_{c},\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}>0
. If
\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{d}{T}\leq 1
, we have the following upper bound,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm{TS}})\leq U(d,d\sigma^{2}/T)+\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}\Bigl(2T\log(K)+\frac{dT}{2}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.9}{\mbox{$\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{d^{2}T\sigma^{2}}{2}}+2\sqrt{2L\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{c}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}+\sigma^{2}_{c}}+\log(T-1)\Bigr)}$}},

where
L
is as defined in Lemma
III.2
.

The theorem above shows that the proposed TS algorithm achieves
O(d\sqrt{T})
regret for feature maps linear in context vector when the prior
P(\theta^{*})
is highly informative.

IV
TS for CB with Delayed True Contexts

In this section, we consider the CBs with delayed true context setting where the agent observes the true context
c_{t}
after it observes the reward
r_{t}
corresponding to the chosen action
a_{t}
. Note that at the time of choosing action
a_{t}
, the agent has access only to noisy contexts. We specialize our TS algorithm to this setting, and call it Algorithm 2 (or
\pi^{\rm TS}_{\rm delay})
.

Algorithm 2 follows similar steps as in Algorithm 1. However, different from Algorithm 1, at
t
th iteration, the agent knows the history
\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}}
of true contexts in addition to that of noisy contexts. Consequently, in the
de-noising
step, the agent
evaluates the predictive posterior distribution as

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})=\mathbb{E}_{P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})}[P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})],

(26)

where
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
is as defined in (
2
) and posterior distribution
P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})
is obtained via Baye’s rule as

P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})\propto P(\gamma^{*})\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}P(c_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}|\gamma^{*})
using the history of true and noisy contexts.

For Gaussian context noise as considered in Sec.
III-A
,
the predictive posterior distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{V}_{t},\tilde{R}_{t}^{-1})
is multivariate Gaussian with the inverse covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\tilde{R_{t}}

\displaystyle=M-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}^{-1},

(27)

and the mean vector

\displaystyle\tilde{V_{t}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{R}_{t}^{-1}\Bigl(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\hat{c}_{t}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}(\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau})

\displaystyle-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}M^{-1}(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\hat{c}_{t})\Bigr),

(28)

where
M=\Sigma_{c}^{-1}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}
and
\tilde{H}_{t}=\Sigma_{n}^{-1}M^{-1}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+(t-1)\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+\Sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}
. Derivation can be found in App.
B-B4
.

Following the denoising step, the next step in Algorithm 2 is a conventional Thompson sampling step, thanks to access to delayed true contexts. Consequently, the agent can evaluate the posterior distribution
P(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,c})
with known contexts as in (
9
) and use it to sample
\theta_{t}\sim P(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,c})
. For Gaussian bandit with Gaussian prior on
\theta^{*}
, the posterior distribution
P(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,c})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t-1},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1})
is a multivariate Gaussian distribution whose inverse covariance matrix and mean respectively evaluate as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\phi(a_{\tau},c_{\tau})\phi(a_{\tau},c_{\tau})^{\top}

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}r_{\tau}\phi(a_{\tau},c_{\tau})\Bigr).

(30)

Using the sampled
\theta_{t}
and the obtained predictive posterior distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})
, the agent then chooses action
a_{t}
as

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta_{t},

(31)

where we use the expected feature map
\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}}):=\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})}[\phi(a_{t},c_{t})]
.

IV-A

Information-Theoretic Bayesian Regret Bounds

In this section, we derive an information-theoretic upper bound on the Bayesian regret (
7
) of Algorithm 2 for Gaussian CBs. To prevent overloading notation, we re-use

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}

(32)

to define the optimal action maximizing the mean reward
\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}
.

To derive the upper bound, we first decompose the Bayesian cumulative regret (
7
) of Algorithm 2 (
\pi^{\rm{TS}}_{\rm delay}
), similar to (
20
), into the following three terms,

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm{TS}}_{\rm delay})=\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,EE1}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,EE2}\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{where}$}},

(33)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(\hat{a}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr]$}},

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,EE1}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(a^{*}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(\hat{a}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr]$}},

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,EE2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr]$}}.

An upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}(\pi^{\rm{TS}}_{\rm delay})
can be obtained by separately bound each of the three terms in (
33
).

In (
33
), the first term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
corresponds to the Bayesian cumulative regret of a standard contextual TS algorithm that uses
\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
as the mean reward function. Note that due to availability of delayed true contexts, there is no posterior mismatch in Algorithm 2. Hence, we apply
(
Neu et al. 2022
, Cor. 3)
to yield the following upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
.

Lemma IV.1
.

Under Assumption
III.1
, the following upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
holds for
\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}

\displaystyle\leq U(m,\lambda),

(34)

where
U(m,\lambda)
is defined as in (
22
).

Lemma
IV.1
gives a tighter bound in comparison to Lemma
III.1
where the posterior mismatch results in additional error terms in the regret bound.

We now upper bound the second term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,EE1}
of (
33
), which similar to the term
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm EE1}
in (
20
), captures the error in approximating the exact predictive distribution

via the inferred predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})

I(\gamma^{*};\mathcal{H}_{T,c,\hat{c}})
between
\gamma^{*}
and the

Lemma IV.2
.

Under Assumption
III.1
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have the following upper bound,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm d,EE1}}+\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm d,EE2}}\leq 2\mathcal{R}^{T}_{{\rm d,EE1}}

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{m\lambda T\log\Bigl(\frac{2m}{\delta}\Bigr)I(\gamma^{*};\mathcal{H}_{T,c,\hat{c}})}+2T\delta^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2m\lambda}{\pi}}$}}.

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.96}{\mbox{$\displaystyle I(\gamma^{*};\mathcal{H}_{T,c,\hat{c}})=\frac{d}{2}\log\Bigl(1+(T-1)\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\Bigr)$}}.

IV.1

Theorem IV.1
.

Under Assumption
III.1

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Definition A.1

(Sub-Gaussian Random Variable)
.

A random variable
y
is said to be
s^{2}
-sub-Gaussian with respect to the distribution
P(y)
if the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P(y)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda(y-\mathbb{E}_{P(y)}[y])]\leq\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda^{2}s^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(36)

Lemma A.1

(Change of Measure Inequality)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote a real-valued function. Let
P(x)
and
Q(x)
be two probability distributions defined on the space of
x
. If
g(x)
is
s^{2}
-sub-Gaussian with respect to
Q(x)
, then the following inequality holds,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[g(x)]|\leq\sqrt{2s^{2}D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))}.

(37)

Proof.

The inequality (
37
) follows by using the Donsker-Varadhan inequality (
18
) with
f(x)=\lambda g(x)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. This yields that

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda g(x))]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\lambda^{2}\frac{s^{2}}{2}

(38)

where the last inequality follows from the assumption of sub-Gaussianity. Rearranging, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\lambda g(x)]\leq\lambda^{2}\frac{s^{2}}{2}+D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x)).

(39)

For
\lambda>0
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[g(x)]\leq\lambda\frac{s^{2}}{2}+\frac{D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))}{\lambda},

(40)

and optimizing over
\lambda>0
then yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[g(x)]\leq\sqrt{2s^{2}D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))}.

(41)

Similarly, for
\lambda<0
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[g(x)]\leq\sqrt{2s^{2}D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))}.

(42)

Lemma A.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be distributed according to
Q(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(x_{i}|\mu_{i},\sigma^{2}_{i})
, i.e., each element of the random vector is independently distributed according to a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{i}
and variance
\sigma^{2}_{i}
. Let
g(x)=\max_{i=1,\ldots n}x_{i}
denote the maximum of
n
Gaussian random variables. Then, the following inequality holds for
\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda g(x))]\leq\log n+\lambda\max_{i}\mu_{i}+\lambda^{2}\frac{\max_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{2}.

(43)

For any distribution
P(x)
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q(x)
, we then have the following change of measure inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[g(x)]\leq\sqrt{2\Bigl(\log n+D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))\Bigr)\max_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}}.

(44)

Proof.

The proof of inequality (
43
) follows from standard analysis (see
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
). We present it here for the sake of completeness. The following sequence of relations hold for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda g(x))]=\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\max_{i}\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda x_{i})]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q(x_{i})}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda x_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}\Bigl(\lambda\mu_{i}+\lambda^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}/2\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq n\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda\max_{i}\mu_{i}+\lambda^{2}\max_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}/2\Bigr).

(45)

Taking logarithm on both sides of the inequality yields the upper bound in (
43
). We now apply the DV inequality (
18
) as in (
38
). This yields that

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}(P(x)\|Q(x))

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\log\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\mathop{\hbox{\rm exp}}(\lambda g(x))]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\log n-\lambda\max_{i}\mu_{i}-\lambda^{2}\frac{\max_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}\mathbb{E}_{P(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\lambda g(x)]-\log n-\lambda^{2}\frac{\max_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{2},

(46)

where the inequality in
(a)
follows from (
43
). The inequality in
(b)
follows from observing that
\max_{i}x_{i}\geq x_{i}
for all
i
, whereby we get that
\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\max_{i}x_{i}]\geq\mu_{i}
which holds for all
i
. The latter inequality implies that
\mathbb{E}_{Q(x)}[\max_{i}x_{i}]\geq\max_{i}\mu_{i}
. Re-arranging and optimizing over
\lambda\geq 0
then yields the required inequality in (
44
).

Derivation of Posterior and Predictive Posterior Distributions

In this section, we provide detailed derivation of posterior predictive distribution for Gaussian bandits. To this end, we first derive the exact predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
.

B-B
1
Derivation of
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})

We begin by noting that

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})=\frac{P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})}{P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})}

\displaystyle\propto P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{c},\Sigma_{c})\mathcal{N}(c_{t}+\gamma^{*},\Sigma_{n}).

Subsequently,

\displaystyle\log(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*}))

\displaystyle\propto\log(P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*}))

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl((c_{t}-\mu_{c})^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}(c_{t}-\mu)+(\hat{c}_{t}-c_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-c_{t}-\gamma^{*})\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(c_{t}^{\top}(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1})c_{t}-\Bigl(\mu_{c}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}+(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\Bigr)c_{t}

\displaystyle-c_{t}^{\top}\Bigl(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\Bigr)+(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(c_{t}^{\top}Mc_{t}-A_{t}^{\top}Mc_{t}-c_{t}^{\top}MA+A_{t}^{\top}MA-A_{t}^{\top}MA+(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\biggr),

(47)

where we have defined

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{c}^{-1}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}

(48)

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=(M^{-1})^{\top}\Bigl(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\Bigr).

(49)

From (
47
), we get that

\displaystyle\log(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*}))\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(c_{t}^{\top}Mc_{t}-A_{t}^{\top}Mc_{t}-c_{t}^{\top}MA+A_{t}^{\top}MA\biggr).

This implies that

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(A_{t},M^{-1}).

(50)

B-B
2
Derivation of
P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})

We now derive the distribution
P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})
which is defined in (
3
) as

\displaystyle P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t})}[P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})].

Hence,
P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})
can be obtained by marginalizing the joint distribution
P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*})=P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})
over
c_{t}
. To this end, we use (
47
) to get,

\displaystyle\log(P(c_{t})P(\hat{c}_{t}|c_{t},\gamma^{*}))=\log(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*}))

\displaystyle\propto\log(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*}))-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-A_{t}^{\top}MA+(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\Bigr),

which implies that

\displaystyle\log(P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*}))

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-A_{t}^{\top}MA+(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{t}-\gamma^{*})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\hat{c}_{t}^{\top}\Bigl(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\Bigr)\hat{c}_{t}-\hat{c}_{t}^{\top}\Bigl(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}(-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*}+\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c})+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*}\Bigr)

\displaystyle-\Bigl((\mu_{c}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}-\gamma^{*\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1})(M^{-1})\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+\gamma^{*\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\Bigr)\hat{c}\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\hat{c}_{t}^{\top}G\hat{c}_{t}-F^{\top}G\hat{c}_{t}-G^{\top}F\hat{c}_{t}\biggr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl((\hat{c}_{t}-F)^{\top}G(\hat{c}_{t}-F)\biggr),

where

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{n}^{-1}-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}

(51)

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=(G^{-1})^{\top}\Bigl(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}(-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*}+\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c})+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*}\Bigr)=(G^{-1})^{\top}(G\gamma^{*}+\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}).

(52)

Thus,

\displaystyle P(\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})=\mathcal{N}(F,G^{-1}).

(53)

B-B
3
Derivation of
P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})

We now derive the posterior distribution
P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})
. To this end, we use Baye’s theorem as

\displaystyle P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}P(\hat{c}_{\tau}|c_{\tau},\gamma^{*})P(\gamma^{*})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\mathcal{N}(c_{\tau}+\gamma^{*},\Sigma_{n})\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\Sigma_{\gamma}).

We then have,

\displaystyle\log P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\biggl((\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau}-\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau}-\gamma^{*})\biggr)+\gamma^{*\top}\Sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}\gamma^{*}\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\biggl((-\hat{c}_{\tau}+c_{\tau}+\gamma^{*})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(-\hat{c}_{\tau}+c_{\tau}+\gamma^{*})\biggr)+\gamma^{*\top}\Sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}\gamma^{*}\biggr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\gamma^{*\top}((t-1)\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+\Sigma_{\gamma}^{-1})\gamma^{*}-\gamma^{*\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}(\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau}))-(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}(\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau}))^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*}\biggr).

Consequently, we get that,

\displaystyle P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})=\mathcal{N}(\gamma^{*}|Y_{t},W_{t}^{-1})

(54)

where

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=(t-1)\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+\Sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}

(55)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=(W_{t}^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}(\hat{c}_{\tau}-c_{\tau}).

(56)

B-B
4
Derivation of Posterior Predictive Distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})

Using results from previous subsections, we are now ready to derive the posterior predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})
. Note that
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})=\mathbb{E}_{P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})}[P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})]
. We then have the following set of relations:

\displaystyle\log\Bigl(P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl((c_{t}-A_{t})^{\top}M(c_{t}-A_{t})+(\gamma^{*}-Y_{t})^{\top}W_{t}(\gamma^{*}-Y_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl((c_{t}-D-E_{t}+(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*})^{\top}M(c_{t}-D-E_{t}+(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\gamma^{*})+(\gamma^{*}-Y_{t})^{\top}W_{t}(\gamma^{*}-Y_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\gamma^{*\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+W_{t})\gamma^{*}-\gamma^{*\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(D+E_{t}-c_{t})+W_{t}Y_{t})-((D+E_{t}-c_{t})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+Y_{t}^{\top}W_{t})\gamma^{*}

\displaystyle+(c_{t}-D-E_{t})^{\top}M(c_{t}-D-E_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\gamma^{*\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\gamma^{*}-\gamma^{*\top}\tilde{H}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{J}_{t}-\tilde{J}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\gamma^{*}+\tilde{J}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\tilde{J}_{t}-\tilde{J}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\tilde{J}_{t}+(c_{t}-D-E_{t})^{\top}M(c_{t}-D-E_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\propto\log(P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t,c,\hat{c}}))-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-\tilde{J}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\tilde{J}_{t}+(c_{t}-D-E_{t})^{\top}M(c_{t}-D-E_{t})\Bigr)

where
D=(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mu_{c}
,
E_{t}=(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\hat{c}_{t}
,
\tilde{H}_{t}=\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}+W_{t}
and
\tilde{J}_{t}=(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(D+E_{t}-c_{t})+W_{t}Y_{t})
.

Since

\log\Bigl(P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})\Bigr)=\log\Bigl(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})P(\gamma^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t,c,\hat{c}})\Bigr)
, we get that

\displaystyle\log(P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}}))

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(-\tilde{J}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{H}_{t}\tilde{J}_{t}+(c_{t}-D-E_{t})^{\top}M(c_{t}-D-E_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\propto-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(c_{t}^{\top}\Bigl(M-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}\Bigr)c_{t}-c_{t}^{\top}\Bigl(M(D+E_{t})-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(D+E_{t})+W_{t}Y_{t})\Bigr)

\displaystyle-\Bigl(M(D+E_{t})-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(D+E_{t})+W_{t}Y_{t})\Bigr)^{\top}c_{t}\Bigr).

This gives that

\displaystyle P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{V}_{t},\tilde{R}_{t}^{-1})\quad\mbox{where}

(57)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{t}

\displaystyle=M-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}

(58)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{R}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}\Bigl(M(D+E_{t})-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\tilde{H}_{t}^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(D+E_{t})+W_{t}Y_{t})\Bigr).

(59)

B-C

Proof of Lemma
IV.1

We now present the proof of Lemma
IV.1
. To this end, we first recall that
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,c,\hat{c}}\cup\hat{c}_{t}
, and we denote
P_{t}(\theta^{*})
as the posterior distribution
P(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,c})
of
\theta^{*}
given the history of observed reward-action-context tuples. We can then equivalently write
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[\psi(\hat{a}_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}-\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})^{\top}\theta^{*}\Bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbb{E}_{t}\underbrace{\Bigl[f(\theta^{*},\hat{a}_{t},c_{t})-f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})\Bigr]}_{:=\Delta_{t}}\biggr],

(60)

where
\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{c,\hat{c}})=\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1,c,\hat{c}})}[\phi(a,c_{t})]
is as defined in (

) and we have used
f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})=\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\theta^{*}
to denote the mean-reward function.
To obtain an upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
, we define the following
lifted information ratio
as in
(
Neu et al. 2022
)
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}=\frac{(\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}])^{2}}{\Lambda_{t}}

(61)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[\Bigl(f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})-\bar{f}(a_{t},c_{t})\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr],

(62)

with
\bar{f}(a_{t},c_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{t}[f(\theta^{*},a_{t},c_{t})|a_{t},c_{t}]
denoting the expectation of mean reward with respect to the posterior distribution
P_{t}(\theta^{*})
.
Subsequently, we get the following upper bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\sqrt{\Gamma_{t}\Lambda_{t}}\biggr]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Gamma_{t}\Bigr]\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Lambda_{t}\Bigr]\Bigr]},

(63)

where the last inequality follows by an application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. An upper bound on
\mathcal{R}^{T}_{\rm d,CB}
then follows by obtaining an upper bound on the lifted information ratio
\Gamma_{t}
as well as on
\Lambda_{t}.

We first evaluate the term
\Lambda_{t}
. To this end, note that
\bar{f}(a_{t},c_{t})=\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}
, with
\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}
defined as in (
30
). Using this, we get

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[(\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}(\theta^{*}-\tilde{\mu}_{t-1}))^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}(\theta^{*}-\tilde{\mu}_{t-1})(\theta^{*}-\tilde{\mu}_{t-1})^{\top}\phi(a_{t},c_{t})\Bigr]

(64)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[\phi(a_{t},c_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\phi(a_{t},c_{t})\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Bigl[\|\phi(a_{t},c_{t})\|_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}\Bigr]

(65)

where
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t-1}
is as in (
29
), and the third equality follows since conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
,
(a_{t},c_{t})
is independent of
\theta^{*}
. Subsequently, we can apply the elliptical potential lemma
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
, Lemma 19.4)
using the assumption that
\|\phi(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{2}\leq 1
and that
\sigma^{2}/\lambda\geq 1
. This results in

D-A

Gaussian Bandits

For Gaussian bandits, we implemented the baselines as explained below.

TS_naive
:

This algorithm implements the following action policy at each iteration
t
,

\displaystyle a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\phi(a,\hat{c}_{t})^{\top}\theta_{t},

where
\theta_{t}
is sampled from a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t-1,{\rm naive}},\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm naive}}^{-1})
with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm naive}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\phi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau})\phi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau})^{\top}

\displaystyle\mu_{t-1,{\rm naive}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm naive}}^{-1}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}r_{\tau}\phi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau})\Bigr).

TS_oracle
:

In this baseline, the agent has knowledge of the true predictive distribution
P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})
. Consequently, at each iteration
t
, the algorithm chooses action

\displaystyle a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta_{t},

where
\theta_{t}
is sampled from a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t-1,{\rm poc}},\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm poc}}^{-1})
with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm poc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\psi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}|\gamma^{*})\psi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}

\displaystyle\mu_{t-1,{\rm poc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{t-1,{\rm poc}}^{-1}}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}r_{\tau}\psi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}|\gamma^{*})\Bigr).

D-B

Logistic Bandits

In the case of logistic bandits, we implemented the baselines as explained below.

TS_naive
:

This algorithm considers the following sampling distribution:

\displaystyle Q(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,\hat{c}})\propto P(\theta^{*})\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}{\rm Ber}(\mu(\phi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau})^{\top}\theta^{*})).

However, due to the non-conjugateness of Gaussian prior
P(\theta^{*})
and Bernoulli reward likelihood, sampling from the above posterior distribution is not straightforward. Consequently, we adopt the Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) sampling approach from
Xu et al. 2022
.
To sample
\theta_{t}

at iteration
t
, we run LMC for
I=50
iterations with learning rate
\eta_{t}=0.2/t
and inverse temperature parameter
\beta^{-1}=0.001
. Then,
\theta_{t}
is chosen as the output of the LMC after
I=50
iterations. Using the sampled
\theta_{t}
, the algorithm then chooses the action
a_{t}
as

\displaystyle a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\phi(a,\hat{c}_{t})^{\top}\theta_{t}.

TS_oracle
:

This algorithm considers the following sampling distribution:

\displaystyle Q(\theta^{*}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1,r,a,\hat{c}})\propto P(\theta^{*})\prod_{\tau=1}^{t-1}{\rm Ber}(\mu(\psi(a_{\tau},\hat{c}_{\tau}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta^{*})),

where
\psi(a_{t},\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{P(c_{t}|\hat{c}_{t},\gamma^{*})}[\phi(a_{t},c_{t})]
is the expected feature map under the posterior predictive distribution with known
\gamma^{*}
. As before, to sample from the above distribution, we use
I=50
iterations of LMC with learning rate
\eta_{t}=0.2/t
and inverse temperature parameter
\beta^{-1}=0.001
. Using the sampled
\theta_{t}
, the algorithm then chooses the action
a_{t}
as

\displaystyle a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\psi(a,\hat{c}_{t}|\gamma^{*})^{\top}\theta_{t}.
