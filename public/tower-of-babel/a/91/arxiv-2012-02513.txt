Complexity of fixed point counting problems in Boolean Networks Thanks: This work extends some early results announced in [ 11 ] .
Florian Bridoux, Amélia Durbec, Kevin Perrot, Adrien Richard

Abstract
A Boolean network (BN) with n n components is a discrete dynamical system described by the successive iterations of a function f : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } n f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n} . This model finds applications in biology, where fixed points play a central role. For example, in genetic regulations, they correspond to cell phenotypes. In this context, experiments reveal the existence of positive or negative influences among components. The digraph of influences is called signed interaction digraph (SID), and one SID may correspond to a large number of BNs. The present work opens a new perspective on the well-established study of fixed points in BNs. When biologists discover the SID of a BN they do not know, they may ask: given that SID, can it correspond to a BN having at least/at most k k fixed points? Depending on the input, we prove that these problems are in 𝖯 \mathrm{\sf P} or complete for 𝖭𝖯 \mathrm{\sf NP} , 𝖭𝖯 𝖭𝖯 \mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf NP}} , 𝖭𝖯 # ​ 𝖯 \mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}} or 𝖭𝖤𝖷𝖯𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤 \mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME} .



1
Introduction

A
Boolean network
(BN) with
n
components is a discrete dynamical system described by the successive iterations of a function

f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n},\qquad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto f(x)=(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{n}(x)).

The structure of the network is often described by a signed digraph
D
, called
signed interaction digraph
(SID) of
f
, catching effective positive and negative dependencies among components: the vertex set is
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and, for all
i,j\in[n]
, there is a positive (resp. negative, zero) arc from
j
to
i
if
f_{i}(x)
depends essentially on
x_{j}
and is an increasing (resp. decreasing, non-monotone) function of
x_{j}
. The SID provides a very rough information about
f
since, given a SID
D
, the set
\mathrm{F}(D)
of BNs whose SID is
D
is generally huge.

BNs have many applications. In particular, since the seminal papers of Kauffman
[
25
,
26
]
and Thomas
[
49
,
50
]
, they are very classical models for the dynamics of gene networks. In this context, the first reliable experimental information often concern the SID of the network, while the actual dynamics are very difficult to observe
[
51
,
29
]
. One is thus faced with the following question:
What can be said about the dynamics described by
f
according to
D
only?

Among the many dynamical properties that can be studied, fixed points are of special interest. For instance, in the context of gene networks, they correspond to stable patterns of gene expression at the basis of particular cellular phenotypes
[
50
,
3
]
. Furthermore, from a theoretical point of view, fixed points are among the very few properties invariant under the classical update schedules (
e.g
parallel, sequential, asynchronous) that we can use to define a dynamics from
f

[
8
]
. As such, they are arguably the property which has been the most thoroughly studied. The number of fixed points and its maximization in particular is the subject of a stream of work,
e.g.
in
[
43
,
7
,
39
,
5
,
20
,
9
,
19
,
10
]
.

From the computational complexity point of view, previous works essentially focused on decision problems of the following form: given
f
and a dynamical property
P
, what is the complexity of deciding if the dynamics described by
f
has the property
P
. For instance, it is well-known that deciding if
f
has a fixed point is
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-complete in general (see
[
28
]
and the references therein), and in
\mathrm{\sf P}
for some families of BNs, such as non-expansive BNs
[
16
]
. However, as mentioned above, in practice,
f
is often unknown while its SID is well approximated. Hence, a more natural question is: given a SID
D
and a dynamical property
P
, what is the complexity of deciding if the dynamics described by some
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
has the property
P
. To the best of our knowledge, there is, perhaps surprisingly, almost no work concerning this kind of decision problems; we can however cite
[
14
]
which, in the continuous setting, studies the complexity of an optimization problem based on the SID and dynamical properties related to monotonicity.

In this paper, we study this class of decision problems, focusing on the maximum and minimum number of fixed points. More precisely, given a SID
D
, we respectively denote by
\phi^{\max}(D)
and
\phi^{\min}(D)
the maximum and minimum number of fixed points in a BN
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
, and we study the complexity of deciding if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
or
\phi^{\min}(D)<k
.

Organization of the paper and summary of contributions

After the preliminaries given in Section
2

\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k

\mathrm{\sf P}

k=1
and, in Section

\mathrm{\sf NP}

42

and the references therein. Section

considers the case where

is part of the input. We prove that, given a SID

1.2
Synopsis of proof techniques

It is well known that the number of fixed points of a BN is strongly related to the cycles of its SID, see
[
42
]
for a review. Let us define the sign of a cycle as the product of the sign of its arcs. For instance, it is well known that
\phi^{\max}(D)\leq 1
(resp.
\phi^{\min}(D)\geq 1
) if all the cycles of
D
are negative (resp. positive), and these inequalities are strict if
D
is strongly connected. The first bound has the following well known generalization:
\phi^{\max}(D)\leq 2^{\tau}
if we can obtain a SID with only negative cycles by deleting
\tau
vertices in
D
.

To prove that deciding if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
, we establish a kind of converse of the first bound. First, we prove that we can compute in polynomial time a SID
D^{\prime}
, without zero sign, such that
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 1
if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
. Hence, we can assume that
D
has no zero sign. Under this assumption, we prove that
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
if and only if each non-trivial initial strongly connected component of
D
contains a positive cycle. By a deep result in graph theory, one can decide in polynomial time if a SID has a positive cycle, and it follows that deciding if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
.

All the other results are exact complexity results and, in each case, the main step is the lower bound, which is always done with a reduction from some variations of the SAT problem. The most important case is the reduction used to prove that it is
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-hard to decide if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
. The reduction is roughly as follows. Given a CNF formula
\psi
over a set of
n
variables we construct in polynomial time a strongly connected SID
H_{\psi}
with only positive arcs, with
2n
particular vertices encoding the positive and negative literals, and with
m
particular vertices
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
corresponding to the clauses, such that every cycle of
D
contains these
m
vertices. By the results mentioned above, we have
2\geq\phi^{\max}(H_{\psi})\geq\phi^{\min}(H_{\psi})\geq 2
, and thus
\phi^{\max}(H_{\psi})=2
. Let
H^{\prime}_{\psi}
be obtained from
H_{\psi}
by adding, for
1\leq s\leq m
, a new vertex
\mu_{s}
, a positive arcs from each literal contained in the
s
th clause to
\mu_{s}
, and a negative arc from
\mu_{s}
to
c_{s}
. Then one easily shows that
\phi^{\max}(H^{\prime}_{\psi})<2
: in some sense, the negative arcs “destroy” the positive cycles, and the system behaves as if there are only negative cycles. But now, let
D_{\psi}
be obtained from
H^{\prime}_{\psi}
by adding new vertices
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
and a positive (resp. negative) arc from
\lambda_{r}
to the positive (resp. negative) literal corresponding to the
r
th variable. Then the situation is in-between: we can have
\max(D_{\psi})=2
or
\max(D_{\psi})<2
depending on
\psi
and the initial choice for
H_{\psi}
. However, by choosing
H_{\psi}
carefully, we have that
\max(D_{\psi})=2
if
\psi
is satisfiable and
\max(D_{\psi})<2
otherwise, and it is thus
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-hard to decide if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
.

Furthermore, if
\psi
is a
3
-CNF then
\Delta(D_{\psi})\leq 3
, so the complexity lower bound still holds when the maximum in-degree is bounded. With few modifications, we can decrease the maximum in-degree to
2
without modifying the complexity lower bound. In addition, for a fixed
k>2
, by adding
\lceil\log_{2}k\rceil-1
new vertices in
D_{\psi}
, and a positive loop on each new vertex, we obtain a SID
D^{\prime}_{\psi}
such that
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime}_{\psi})\geq k
if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D_{\psi})\geq 2
. Thus, deciding if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
for each fixed
k\geq 2
is
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-hard, even if the maximum in-degree of
D
is bounded. When
k
is part of the input and the in-degree is bounded, the reduction is still obtained from minor modifications of
D_{\psi}
. In that case, the reduction is from
Existential-Majority-SAT
, which is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-complete. This problem asks, given a CNF formula
\psi
over a set of
n
variables and an integer
1\leq s\leq n
, if there exists an assignment
z
of the first
s
variables such that
\psi
is satisfied by the majority of the
2^{n-s}
assignments extending
z
.

When
k
is part of the input and the in-degree is unbounded, the reduction differs from the previous case and is much more technical. This reduction is from
Succinct-3SAT
, a classical
\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}
-complete problem. It asks, given a succinct representation of a
3
-CNF formula
\Psi
, if
\Psi
is satisfiable. First, we translate the succinct representation under the form of a BN
h
. The formula
\Psi
is then encoded by the fixed points of
h
: to each clause, and each position in that clause, there is a fixed point encoding the literal contained in the clause in that position. In turn, these fixed points are encoded into a CNF formula
\phi
(whose variables are the vertices of the SID of
h
). We then construct a SID
D_{\Psi}
essentially from the union of the SID of
h
and the SID
D_{\psi}
described above, where
\psi
is obtained from
\phi
by adding a few extra clauses. It then appears that if the number of fixed points of a BN
f\in F(D_{\Psi})
is sufficiently large, then the fixed points of
f
extend (in some way) those of
h
(thanks to the formula
\phi
contained in
\psi
), and thus encode
\Psi
and, furthermore, we can extract from
f
an assignment for
\Psi
satisfying many clauses. Conversely, if many clauses of
\Psi
can be simultaneously satisfied, then one can construct a BN
f\in F(D_{\Psi})
with many fixed points. We then obtain that
\Psi
is satisfiable if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D_{\Psi})\geq k
for some
k
depending on
D_{\Psi}
. This shows that, when
k
is part of the input, it is
\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}
-hard to decide if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
.

The duality between positive and negative cycles, highlighted by the basic results given at the beginning of this subsection, allows us to adapt, with few modifications, all above constructions for the study of the minimum number of fixed points. In each reduction, it is enough to take one of the constructions and inverse the sign of a single arc. The analysis is then rather easy, because based on results concerning the maximum number of fixed points. Even if the constructions and the analysis are similar to the maximum number of fixed points, the complexity results differ. In particular, when
k
is fixed and the maximum in-degree is bounded, the reduction is from
Quantified satisfiability
with 2 alternating quantifiers, which is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf NP}}
-complete. This problem asks, given a CNF formula
\psi
over a set of
n
variables and an integer
1\leq s\leq n
, if there exists an assignment
z
of the first
s
variables such that
\psi
is satisfied by all the assignments extending
z
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Configurations

Given a finite set
V
, a
configuration
on
V
is an element
x\in\{0,1\}^{V}
that assigns a
state

x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
to every
i\in V
. For
a\in\{0,1\}
, we write
x=a
to mean that
x_{i}=a
for all
i\in V
. Given an enumeration
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}
of the elements of
V
, we use the one-line notation writing
x=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\dots x_{i_{n}}
. For
I\subseteq V
, we denote by
x_{I}
the restriction of
x
on
I
, that is, the configuration
y\in\{0,1\}^{I}
such that
y_{i}=x_{i}
for all
i\in I
; and
x

extends
a configuration
y\in\{0,1\}^{I}
if
x_{I}=y
. For every
i\in V
, we denote the
i
-base vector by
e_{i}
, that is,
(e_{i})_{i}=1
and
(e_{i})_{j}=0
for all
j\neq i
. Given
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{V}
, we denote by
x{\oplus}y
the configuration
z
on
V
such that
z_{i}=x_{i}{\oplus}y_{i}
for all
i\in V
, where the addition is computed modulo two. Hence,
x{\oplus}e_{i}
is the configuration obtained from
x
by flipping the state of
i
only. We write
x\leq y
to means that
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in V
.

2.2
Boolean networks

A
Boolean network
(BN) with component set
V
is a function
f:\{0,1\}^{V}\to\{0,1\}^{V}
. Given an initial configuration
x^{0}
on
V
, the dynamics of the network is described by the successive iterations of
f
, that is,
x^{t+1}=f(x^{t})
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, the state of component
i
evolves according to the
local function

f_{i}:\{0,1\}^{V}\to\{0,1\}
, which is the coordinate
i
of
f
, that is,
f_{i}(x)=f(x)_{i}
. More generally, given
I\subseteq V
, we denote by
f_{I}
the function from
\{0,1\}^{V}
to
\{0,1\}^{I}
defined by
f_{I}(x)=f(x)_{I}
. If
y\in\{0,1\}^{I}
then
f_{I}=y
means that
f_{I}
is a constant function that always returns
y
.

2.3
Signed digraphs

A
signed digraph

D
consists of a finite set of vertices
V_{D}
, a set of arcs
A\subseteq V_{D}\times V_{D}
and an arc-labeling function
\sigma
from
A
to
\{-1,0,1\}
, which gives a sign (negative, zero or positive) to each arc
(j,i)
, denoted
\sigma_{ji}
. In the following, it will be convenient to set
\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=0
if
\sigma_{ji}\geq 0
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=1
otherwise. We say that
D
is
nice
if it has no zero arc, and
full-positive
if it has only positive arcs. Given a vertex
i
, we denote by
N_{D}(i)
the set of in-neighbors of
i
and, for
s\in\{-1,0,1\}
, we denote by
N^{s}_{D}(i)
the set of in-neighbors
j
of
i
with
\sigma_{ji}=s
. We drop
D
in the previous notations when it is clear from the context. We call
N^{1}(i)
,
N^{0}(i)
and
N^{-1}(i)
the set of positive, zero and negative in-neighbors of
i
, respectively. We say that
i
is a
source
if it has no in-neighbor. The maximum in-degree of
D
is denoted
\Delta(D)
. Cycles and paths are always directed and without repeated vertices. The
sign
of a cycle or a path is the product of the signs of its arcs. We say that
i
has a
positive loop
if
D
has a positive arc from
i
to itself (a positive cycle of length one). Given
I\subseteq V_{D}
, we denote by
D\setminus I
the signed digraph obtained from
D
by deleting the vertices in
I
(with the attached arcs). We say that a signed digraph
D^{\prime}
is a
spanning subgraph
of
D
if
D^{\prime}
can be obtained from
D
by removing arcs only. We say that
I
is a
positive feedback vertex set
of
D
if
D\setminus I
has only negative cycles (or is acyclic). The minimum size of a positive feedback vertex set of
D
is denoted
\tau^{+}(D)
.

2.4
Signed interaction digraphs

The
signed interaction digraph
(SID) of a BN
f
with component set
V
is the signed digraph
D_{f}
with vertex set
V_{D}=V
defined as follows. First, given
i,j\in V
, there is an arc
(j,i)
if and only if there exists a configuration
x
on
V
such that
f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})\neq f_{i}(x)
(
i.e.
the local function
f_{i}
depends on component
j
). Second, the sign
\sigma_{ji}
of an arc
(j,i)
depends on whether the local function
f_{i}
is increasing or decreasing with respect to component
j
, and is defined as

\sigma_{ji}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\quad\text{if $f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})\geq f_{i}(x)$ for all $x\in\{0,1\}^{V}$ with $x_{j}=0$,}\\
-1&\quad\text{if $f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})\leq f_{i}(x)$ for all $x\in\{0,1\}^{V}$ with $x_{j}=0$,}\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Hence, a positive (resp. negative, zero) arc from
j
to
i
means that
f_{i}(x)
is an increasing (resp. decreasing, non-monotone) function of
x_{j}
.

Example 3
.

The SID of the BN
f
given in Example

D
given in Example

. One can easily deduce that from description of the three local functions by logical formulas. First, we have
f_{1}(x)=\neg x_{1}\land\neg x_{3}
, thus
f_{1}(x)
only depends on
x_{1}
,
x_{3}
, and is decreasing with
x_{1},x_{2}
. Hence, in the SID of

, vertex

has two in-coming arcs, from vertex
1
and vertex

f_{2}(x)=x_{1}\lor x_{3}
, thus
f_{2}(x)

x_{1}
,

, and is increasing with
x_{1},x_{3}
. Hence, in the SID of

, vertex

f_{3}(x)

x_{2}

x_{2},x_{3}
. Hence, in the SID of

2
and vertex

A signed digraph is called a SID if it is the SID of at least one BN. Such signed digraphs are characterized below (as a consequence, every nice signed digraph is a SID).

Proposition 1

(
[
36
]
)
.

A signed digraph
D
is a SID if and only if
|N^{0}_{D}(i)|\neq 1
or
|N_{D}(i)|\geq 3
for all vertex
i
.

A fundamental remark regarding the present work is that multiple BNs may have the same SID. Given a SID
D
, we denote by
\mathrm{F}(D)
the corresponding BNs:

\mathrm{F}(D)=\{f:\{0,1\}^{V_{D}}\to\{0,1\}^{V_{D}}\mid D_{f}=D\}.

The size of
\mathrm{F}(D)
is generally large: there are
2^{n2^{n}}
BNs with
n
components and only
3^{n}
signed digraphs with
n
vertices. Given
i\in V_{D}
, we denote by
\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)
the possible local functions for
i
, that is,

\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)=\{f_{i}\mid f\in\mathrm{F}(D)\}.

The size of
\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)
is doubly exponential according to the in-degree
d
of
i
in
D
, thus it scales as the number of Boolean functions on
d
variables,
2^{2^{d}}
. The precise value of
|\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)|
is not trivial and has been extensively studied when
i
has only positive in-neighbors, see
A006126
on the OEIS
[
1
]
.

In the following, we will often consider vertices with in-degree
\leq 2
and, in that case, the situation is clear. If
i
is a source, then there are only two possible local functions, the two constant local functions. If
i
has a unique in-neighbor, say
j
, then it is not a zero in-neighbor by Proposition
1
, and there is a unique possible local function: we have
f_{i}(x)=x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}
. If
i
has only positive or negative in-neighbors, we say that
f_{i}
is the AND (resp. OR) function if it is the ordinary logical and (resp. or) but inputs with a negative sign are flipped, that is,

f_{i}(x)=\bigwedge_{j\in N(i)}x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}\qquad\text{(resp.}~f_{i}(x)=\bigvee_{j\in N(i)}x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}~\text{)}.

(If
i
is of in-degree one, the AND and OR functions are identical.) Now, if
i
is of in-degree two and has no zero in-neighbor, there are only two possibles local functions: the AND function and the OR function.

2.5
Fixed points and decision problems

A
fixed point
of
f
is a configuration
x
such that
f(x)=x
. We denote by
\phi(f)
the number of fixed points of
f
. In this paper, we are interested in decision problems related to the maximum and minimum number of fixed points of BNs in
\mathrm{F}(D)
, denoted

\begin{array}[]{l}\phi^{\max}(D)=\max\left\{\phi(f)\mid f\in\mathrm{F}(D)\right\},\\[5.69054pt]
\phi^{\min}(D)=\min\left\{\phi(f)\mid f\in\mathrm{F}(D)\right\}.\end{array}

.

of Example

, we have
\phi^{\min}(D)=0
and
\phi^{\max}(D)=1
. This can be easily deduce from the table given in Example
4
.

More precisely, we will study the complexity of deciding if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
or
\phi^{\min}(D)<k
, where
k
is a positive integer, fixed or not. This gives the following decision problems.

k
-Maximum Fixed Point Problem
(
k
-
MaxFPP
)

Input:
a SID
D
.

Question:

\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
?

Maximum Fixed Point Problem
(
MaxFPP
)

Input:
a SID
D
and an integer
k\geq 1
.

Question:

\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
?

k
-Minimum Fixed Point Problem
(
k
-
MinFPP
)

Input:
a SID
D
.

Question:

\phi^{\min}(D)<k
?

Minimum Fixed Point Problem
(
MinFPP
)

Input:
a SID
D
and an integer
k\geq 1
.

Question:

\phi^{\min}(D)<k
?

All these problems are in
\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}
(they can be decided in exponential time on a non-deterministic Turing machine). For instance the problem
MaxFPP
can be decided as follows. Given a SID
D
with
n
vertices and an integer
k
:

1.

Choose non-deterministically a BN
f
with component set
V_{D}
; this can be done in exponential time since
f
can be represented using
n2^{n}
bits.

2.

Compute the SID
D_{f}
of
f
; this can be done in exponential time by comparing
f_{i}(x)
and
f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})
for all configurations
x
on
V_{D}
and vertices
i,j\in V_{D}
.

3.

Compute
\phi(f)
by considering each of the
2^{n}
configurations.

4.

Accept if and only if
\phi(f)\geq k
and
D_{f}=D
.

This non-deterministic exponential time algorithm has an accepting branch if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
. Therefore, the problem
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}
. This algorithm can be adapted to the other problems.

However, as we will see later, this complexity bound can be refined for some problems or when we restrict the problems to some subclass of SIDs, such as SIDs with a maximum in-degree bounded by a constant
d
. This is because the SIDs of this subclass only admit a simple exponential number of BNs. Indeed, if
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
and
\Delta(D)\leq d
, then each local function
f_{i}
can be regarded as a Boolean function with
|N(i)|\leq d
inputs. Consequently, there are at most
c=2^{2^{d}}
possible choices for each local function, and
|\mathrm{F}(D)|\leq c^{n}
is a simple exponential.

Remark
1
below shows that the restriction to SIDs
D
with maximum in-degree
\leq 1
reduces drastically the hardness of the problems. Therefore, in the rest of the article, we will only consider SIDs whose maximum in-degree is bounded by a constant
d\geq 2
or not bounded at all.

Remark 1
.

The decisions problems
k
-
MaxFPP
,
MaxFPP
,
k
-
MinFPP
and
MinFPP
restricted to SIDs
D
such that
\Delta(D)\leq 1
are in
\mathrm{\sf P}
. Indeed, if
\Delta(D)\leq 1
then the numbers
c^{+}
and
c^{-}
of positive and negative cycles can be computed in polynomial time (and
c^{-}+c^{+}
is at most the number of vertices). Furthermore, there is a unique
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
, and one easily check that

\phi(f)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $c^{-}>0$},\\
2^{c^{+}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

As a consequence, computing
\phi(f)
, which is obviously equal to both
\phi^{\max}(D)
and
\phi^{\min}(D)
, can be done in polynomial time, and it is sufficient to compare this value to
k
in order to answer any problem.

2.6
Basic results

Cycles of
D
are known to play a fundamental role concerning the number of fixed points (and the above remark already shows this). A basic result by Robert is that if
D
is acyclic, then every
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
has a unique fixed point (thus
\phi^{\max}(D)=\phi^{\min}(D)=1
). Considering positive and negative cycles, Aracena
[
3
]
proved the following other basic results, that show a kind of duality between the two types of cycle (note that Robert’s result is an immediate consequence of the first two items).

Theorem 1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
D
be a SID.

1.

If
D
has only negative cycles then
\phi^{\max}(D)\leq 1
.

2.

If
D
has only positive cycles then
\phi^{\min}(D)\geq 1
.

3.

If
D
is strongly connected and has only negative cycles then
\phi^{\max}(D)<1
.

4.

If
D
is strongly connected and has only positive cycles then
\phi^{\min}(D)>1
.

The first item can be widely generalized into the following bound (many extensions or improvements for particular classes of SIDs exist; see
[
40
,
19
,
10
]
for instance).

Theorem 2

(Positive feedback bound
[
3
]
)
.

For every SID
D
we have
\phi^{\max}(D)\leq 2^{\tau^{+}(D)}
.

The positive feedback bound is an immediate consequence of the following lemma, used many times in the following.

Lemma 1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
D
be a SID, let
I
be a positive feedback vertex set of
D
and
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
. If
f
has distinct fixed points
x,y
, then
x_{I}\neq y_{I}
.

The proof of all the previous results uses at some point the following lemma, that we will also use intensively (the first item appears in
[
19
]
and the second is a consequence of the first; we give a proof for completeness). Given a SID
D
and
i\in V_{D}
, we define the partial order
\leq^{D}_{i}
on
\{0,1\}^{V_{D}}
by

x\leq^{D}_{i}y\iff x_{N^{1}(i)}\leq y_{N^{1}(i)}\text{ and }y_{N^{-1}(i)}\leq x_{N^{-1}(i)}\text{ and }x_{N^{0}(i)}=y_{N^{0}(i)}.

Lemma 2
.

Let
D
be a SID with sign function
\sigma
, and let
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
. For every
i\in V_{D}
, we have the following properties:

1.

The local function
f_{i}
is non-decreasing with respect to
\leq^{D}_{i}
, that is, for any configurations
x,y
on
V_{D}
we have

x\leq^{D}_{i}y\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(y).

2.

If
i
has at least one in-neighbor and at most one zero in-neighbor then, for any configuration
x
on
V_{D}
, there is a non-zero in-neighbor
j
of
i
such that
f_{i}(x)=x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}
.

Proof.

To prove the first property, let
x,y
be configurations on
V_{D}
such that
x\leq^{D}_{i}y
. Let
\Delta(x,y)
be the set of
j\in V_{D}
such that
x_{j}\neq y_{j}
. We proceed by induction on
|\Delta(x,y)|
, the Hamming distance between
x
and
y
. If
|\Delta(x,y)|=0
then
x=y
so
f_{i}(x)=f_{i}(y)
. Otherwise, there exists
j\in\Delta(x,y)
and we set
z=x{\oplus}e_{j}
. Since
z_{j}=y_{j}
, it is clear that
z\leq^{D}_{i}y
. Furthermore, we have
\Delta(z,y)=\Delta(x,y)\setminus\{j\}
so
f_{i}(z)\leq f_{i}(y)
by induction hypothesis. If
j\not\in N(i)
, we have
f_{i}(x)=f_{i}(z)\leq f_{i}(y)
. Otherwise,
j
is either a positive or a negative in-neighbor of
i
(since
x_{j}\neq y_{j}
and
x\leq^{D}_{i}y
). If
j
is a positive in-neighbor, then
x_{j}<y_{j}=z_{j}
so
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(z)
, and we deduce that
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(y)
. If
j
is a negative in-neighbor, then
x_{j}>y_{j}=z_{j}
, so
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(z)
and we have again
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(y)
. This concludes the induction step.

We now prove the second property. Suppose that
f_{i}(x)=0
and suppose,
for a contradiction, that there is no non-zero in-neighbor
j
of
i

such that
x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=0
. This means that

x_{N^{1}(i)}=1
and
x_{N^{-1}(i)}=0
or, equivalently, that
x
is a

\leq^{D}_{i}
-maximal configuration. If
i
has no zero in-neighbor, for
any configuration
y
we have
y\leq^{D}_{i}x
and we deduce from the
first property that
f_{i}(y)=0
. Hence,
f_{i}
is the 0 constant
function, which is a contradiction since
i
has at least one
in-neighbor. Suppose now that
i
has a unique zero in-neighbor, say

k
. For any configuration
y
with
y_{k}=x_{k}
we have
y\leq^{D}_{i}x

and we deduce from the first property that
f_{i}(y)=0
, so that

f_{i}(y)\leq f_{i}(y{\oplus}e_{k})
. It follows that
k
is a positive
in-neighbor if
x_{k}=0
and a negative in-neighbor if
x_{k}=1
, a
contradiction. The case
f_{i}(x)=1
is similar.
∎

3
k
-Maximum Fixed Point Problem
for
k=1

In this Section, we are interested in the problem of deciding if a SID
D
admits a BN with at least one fixed point. This is the only decision problem we consider for which we do not prove tight complexity bounds. More precisely, Theorem
3
shows that the problem is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
, but it remains open to know whether it is
\mathrm{\sf P}
-hard.

Theorem 3
.

1
-
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
.

The next lemma shows that we can efficiently transform any SID
D
into a nice SID
D^{\prime}
such that
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 1
. As a consequence, in order to prove that
1
-
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
, we can consider that the input SID is nice.

Lemma 3
.

Let
D
be a SID and let
D^{\prime}
be the nice SID obtained from
D
by deleting the set of arcs
(j,i)
such that
i
has at least two zero in-neighbors in
D
or such that
j
is the unique zero in-neighbor of
i
in
D
. We have

\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1\iff\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 1.

Proof.

Suppose that
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
and let
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with a fixed point
y
. We define componentwise a BN
f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{F}(D^{\prime})
that admits
y
as fixed point. Let
i
be any vertex. We consider three cases:

1.

If
i
has no zero in-neighbor in
D
, then
i
has the same in-coming arcs in
D
and
D^{\prime}
thus we can set
f^{\prime}_{i}=f_{i}
, so that
f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=f_{i}(y)=y_{i}
.

2.

If
i
has a unique zero in-neighbor in
D
, then
f^{\prime}_{i}
is defined as the AND
function if
y_{i}=0
, and the OR function otherwise. Suppose first that

y_{i}=0
. Then
f_{i}(y)=0
so, by the second property of
Lemma
2
, there is a non-zero in-neighbor
j
of
i

such that
y_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=0
, where
\sigma
is the sign function
of
D
. Since
f^{\prime}_{i}
is the AND function, we deduce that
f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=0
. We
prove similarly that
f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=1
when
y_{i}=1
.

3.

If
i
has at least two zero in-neighbors in
D
, then
i
is a source of
D^{\prime}
so
f^{\prime}_{i}
has to be a constant function. We then set
f^{\prime}_{i}=y_{i}
, so that
f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=y_{i}
.

In this way,
f^{\prime}
is a BN in
\mathrm{F}(D^{\prime})
which has
y
as fixed point. Hence,
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 1
.

Conversely, suppose that
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 1
and let
f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{F}(D^{\prime})
with a fixed point
y
. We define componentwise a BN
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
that admits
y
as fixed point. Let
i
be any vertex. We consider three cases:

1.

If
i
has no zero in-neighbor in
D
, then
i
has the same in-coming arcs in
D
and
D^{\prime}
thus we can set
f_{i}=f^{\prime}_{i}
, so that
f_{i}(y)=f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=y_{i}
.

2.

Suppose that
i
has a unique zero in-neighbor in
D
, say
k
. Note that
i

has at least two non-zero in-neighbors in
D
by Proposition
1
.
Suppose that
y_{i}=0
. Then
f^{\prime}_{i}(y)=0
so, by the second property of
Lemma
2
, there is a non-zero in-neighbor
j
of
i

such that
y_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=0
. We then define
f_{i}
by:

f_{i}(x)=\bigl((x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}\big)\vee(x_{k}{\oplus}y_{k})\bigr)\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{\ell\in N_{D}(i)\setminus\{j,k\}}\bigl((x_{\ell}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell i})\vee(x_{k}{\oplus}\neg y_{k})\bigr).

It is easy to check that
f_{i}\in\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)
and, since the first term of the conjunction vanishes for
x=y
, we have
f_{i}(y)=0=y_{i}
. If
y_{i}=1
we prove similarly that there is
f_{i}\in\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)
with
f_{i}(y)=1
(in that case, there is a non-zero in-neighbor
j
such that
y_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=1
and
f_{i}
is defined as above by swapping
\wedge
and
\vee
, and by swapping
y_{k}
and
\neg y_{k}
).

3.

Suppose that
i
has at least two zero in-neighbors in
D
. If
y_{i}=0
we define
f_{i}
by:

f_{i}(x)=\left(\bigoplus\limits_{j\in N^{0}_{D}(i)}x_{j}{\oplus}y_{j}\right)\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{j\in N_{D}(i)\setminus N^{0}_{D}(i)}(x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}).

It is easy to check that
f_{i}\in\mathrm{F}_{i}(D)
and, since the first term of
the conjunction vanishes for
x=y
, we have
f_{i}(y)=0=y_{i}
. The case

y_{i}=1
is symmetric (with
\vee
instead of
\wedge
and
\neg y_{j}

instead of
y_{j}
).

In this way,
f
is a BN in
\mathrm{F}(D)
which has
y
as fixed point. Hence,
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
.
∎

We now give a graph-theoretical characterization of the nice SIDs
D
such that
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
. We need some additional definitions. A strongly connected component
H
in a signed digraph
D
is
trivial
if it has a unique vertex and no arc, and
initial
if
D
has no arc
(i,j)
where
j
is in
H
but not
i
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
D
be a nice SID. We have
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 1
if and only if each non-trivial initial strongly connected component of
D
has a positive cycle.

Proof.

The left to right implication has been proven by Aracena
in
[
5
, Corollary 3]
and is an easy consequence of the third item in
Theorem
1
. We present a version rewritten with the
notations of this paper. Let
\sigma
be the sign function of
D
, and let
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with a fixed point
x
. Consider an arbitrary non-trivial
initial strongly connected component
H
of
D
, and let us prove that
H

has a positive cycle. Since
H
is non-trivial, by the second property of
Lemma
2
, each vertex
i
in
H
has a non-zero
in-neighbor
j
such that
x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=f_{i}(x)=x_{i}
, and
j
is
necessarily in
H
since
H
is initial. We deduce that
H
has a spanning
subgraph
H^{\prime}
in which each vertex is of in-degree one, and such that

x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=x_{i}
for any arc
(j,i)
of
H^{\prime}
. Hence, for any
vertices
i,j
in
H
, if
x_{j}=x_{i}
then any walk in
H^{\prime}
from
j
to
i

visits an even number of negative arcs. In particular, any cycle of
H^{\prime}
is
positive. Since
H^{\prime}
has no source,
H^{\prime}
has a cycle, which is positive.
Thus,
H
has a positive cycle as desired.

Conversely, suppose that each non-trivial initial strongly connected component
of
D
has a positive cycle. Then it is easy to see that
D
has a spanning
subgraph
D^{\prime}
with only positive cycles, and with the same sources as
D
. Let
any
f^{\prime}\in\mathrm{F}(D^{\prime})
. By the second item of Theorem

1
,
f^{\prime}
has at least one fixed point, say
x
. We then
define
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
componentwise as follows. For any vertex
i
, if

i
is a source, then we set
f_{i}=x_{i}
, so that
f_{i}(x)=x_{i}
. Otherwise, by the
second property of Lemma
2
,
i
has an in-neighbor
j
in

D^{\prime}
such that
x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=f^{\prime}_{i}(x)=x_{i}
. We then define
f_{i}
as
the AND function if
x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=x_{i}=0
and the OR function if

x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=x_{i}=1
. It is then clear that
f_{i}(x)=x_{i}
. Hence,

x
is a fixed point of
f
.∎

. It finally checks that these non-deterministic guesses do not
contradict the first property of Lemma
2
. If so, this
partial knowledge of the local functions can be extended into a BN in

\mathrm{F}(D)
with the
k
guessed fixed points,
i.e.

is a true
instance. More precisely, the algorithm is as follows:

1.

Choose non-deterministically

distinct configurations
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}
on
V_{D}
and, for every
i\in V_{D}
, compute the sets
F_{i}=\{x^{\ell}\,|\,x^{\ell}_{i}=0,\ell\in[k]\}
and
T_{i}=\{x^{\ell}\,|\,x^{\ell}_{i}=1,\ell\in[k]\}
.

2.

For each non-negative arc
(j,i)
of
D
, choose non-deterministically a configuration

on
V_{D}
with
x^{ji+}_{j}=0
. Then add
x^{ji+}
in the set

and
x^{ji+}{\oplus}e_{j}
in the set

.

3.

For each non-positive arc
(j,i)
of

, choose non-deterministically a configuration

on
V_{D}
with
x^{ji-}_{j}=0
. Then add
x^{ji-}
in the set

and
x^{ji-}{\oplus}e_{j}
in the set

.

4.

Accept if and only if there is no

,
x\in F_{i}
and
y\in T_{i}
such that

.

This algorithm runs in
O(|V_{D}|\Delta(D)^{3})
, which is actually the time complexity of the last item, the most consuming one. Indeed, we have
|F_{i}||T_{i}|\leq(\Delta(D)+k)^{2}
, and the relation

can be checked in
O(\Delta(D))
.

We will prove that there is an accepting branch if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
.

Suppose first that there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with at least
k
fixed points.
One can consider the following execution. First, the configurations

x^{1},\dots,x^{k}
chosen in the first step are fixed points of
f
. Second, for
each non-negative arc
(j,i)
of
D
, the configuration
x^{ji+}
chosen in
the second step is such that
f_{i}(x^{ji+})<f_{i}(x^{ji+}{\oplus}e_{j})
; this
configuration exists since
D
is the SID of
f
. Third, for each
non-positive arc
(j,i)
, the configuration
x^{ji-}
chosen in the third
step is such that
f_{i}(x^{ji-})>f_{i}(x^{ji-}{\oplus}e_{j})
; this configuration
exists since
D
is the SID of
f
. Hence, for all
i\in V_{D}
,
x\in F_{i}
and

y\in T_{i}
we have
f_{i}(x)=0
and
f_{i}(y)=1
and we deduce from the first
property of Lemma
2
that
y\not\leq^{D}_{i}x
, so the
algorithm as an accepting branch.

For the other direction, suppose that the algorithm has an accepting branch. Let us prove that there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with at least
k
fixed points. For each
i\in V_{D}
, we define
f_{i}
has follows: for all configurations
x
on
V_{D}
,

f_{i}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if there is $y\in T_{i}$ such that $y\leq^{D}_{i}x$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
f_{i}(x)=1
if
x\in T_{i}
. Furthermore, if
x\in F_{i}
then, since we are considering an accepting branch, there is no
y\in T_{i}
such that
y\leq^{D}_{i}x
, and we deduce that
f_{i}(x)=0
. Consequently,
f_{i}(x^{\ell})=x^{\ell}_{i}
for
1\leq\ell\leq k
. Thus,
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}
are fixed points of
f
.

It remains to prove that the SID
D_{f}
of
f
is equal to

. Suppose first that
D_{f}
has a non-negative arc
(j,i)
. Then there is a configuration
x
with
x_{j}=0
such that
f_{i}(x)<f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})
. We deduce that
x\not\leq^{D}_{i}y\leq^{D}_{i}x{\oplus}e_{j}
for some
y\in T_{i}
. So
(j,i)
is an arc of
D
and, since
x_{j}\leq y_{j}
and
y\leq^{D}_{i}x{\oplus}e_{j}
, if this arc is negative then
y\leq^{D}_{i}x
, a contradiction. Thus,
(j,i)
is a non-negative arc of
D
. We prove similarly that every non-positive arc of
D_{f}
is a non-positive arc of
D
. Now, suppose that
D
has a non-negative arc
(j,i)
and let
x=x^{ji+}
be the corresponding configuration chosen in the second step. We have
x\in F_{i}
and
x{\oplus}e_{j}\in T_{i}
, so
f_{i}(x)<f_{i}(x{\oplus}e_{j})
and since
x_{j}=0
we deduce that
(j,i)
is a non-negative arc of

. We prove similarly that every non-positive arc of
D
is a non-positive arc of

. This proves that

.
∎

4.2
Lower bound

We now prove the lower bound. We first show that the problem can be reduced to
k=2
: by the following lemma,
2
-
MaxFPP
is as hard as
k
-
MaxFPP
for all
k>2
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
k>2
and let
D
be any SID. Let
D^{\prime}
be the SID obtained from
D
by adding
\lceil\log_{2}k\rceil-1
new vertices and a positive loop on each new vertex. We have

\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq k\iff\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2.

Proof.

Let
\ell=\lceil\log_{2}k\rceil
so that
2^{\ell-1}<k\leq 2^{\ell}
and
\ell\geq 2
. Let
H
be the SID with
\ell-1
vertices and a positive loop on each vertex. Then
\mathrm{F}(H)
contains a unique BN, which is the identity, thus
\phi^{\max}(H)=2^{\ell-1}
. Since
D^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of
D
and
H
,

\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})=\phi^{\max}(D)\cdot\phi^{\max}(H)=\phi^{\max}(D)\cdot 2^{\ell-1}.

Thus,
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\geq 2^{\ell}\geq k
if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
, and
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})\leq 2^{\ell-1}<k
otherwise.
∎

It remains to prove the case
k=2
: it is
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-hard to decide if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
. This is the main contribution of the paper. First, from a technical point
of view, the reduction used for this decision problem will be adapted for all
the other hardness results of this paper. Second, from a more general point of
view, many works have been devoted to the study of the SID of dynamical systems
with multiple steady states, both in the continuous and discrete setting, see

[
46
,
39
,
46
,
27
,
42
]
and the references therein. The basic
observation, answering a conjecture of the biologist Thomas, is that a
non-negative cycle must be present. The biological motivation behind is that
dynamical systems (in particular BNs) with multiple steady states (fixed points
in the discrete setting) should account for very important biological
phenomena: cell differentiation processes. In our setting, the principal
observation we just mention is the first item of Theorem

1
: if
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
then
D
has a non-negative
cycle. This necessary condition for
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
(which can be checked in
polynomial time) is obviously not sufficient, and it is natural to seek for a
characterization. By proving that it is NP-hard to decide that
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq 2
, we show that any such characterization is difficult to check. Together with
the complexity upper bound established above, this answers a question stated in

[
41
]
.

The rough idea is the following. We know that if a SID
D_{1}
has no source and
is full-positive then
\phi^{\min}(D_{1})\geq 2
(this is an easy consequence of the
last item of Theorem
1
). More precisely, every

f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{1})
has two distinct fixed points
x
and
y
such that

x\leq y
. If, in addition,
D_{1}
has a positive feedback vertex set of size
one, then
\phi^{\max}(D_{1})\leq 2
thus every
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{1})
has
exactly
two distinct fixed points
x
and
y
such that
x\leq y
. The
simplest example is the full-positive cycle. Now, if we add some negative arcs
in
D_{1}
, without producing additional positive cycles, this can only reduce
the maximum number of fixed points. In many cases the reduction is effective:
the resulting SID
D_{2}
is such that
\phi^{\max}(D_{2})\leq 1
. The simplest example
is a full-positive cycle plus any negative arc. The idea is then to add to
D_{2}

some additional sources that “control” the negative arcs in such a way that
some BNs behave as if their SID were
D_{1}
(the negative arcs are “dynamically
absent”) and have thus two fixed points, while some others behave as if their
SID were
D_{2}
(the negative arcs are “dynamically present”) and have at
most one fixed point. If this “control” is possible if and only if rather
particular graphical conditions are satisfied, then some complexity lower bound
should be obtained. Actually, the reduction consists in encoding, in these
graphical conditions, a formula in conjunctive normal form.

So consider a CNF formula
\psi
over a set
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
of
n
variables and with a set of
m
clauses
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. To each variable
\lambda\in\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is associated a positive literal
\lambda^{+}
and a negative literal
\lambda^{-}

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{-}

\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{+}\cup\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{-}
. If each clause is of size at most
3
, then
\psi
is a
3
-CNF formula. An

for

on a set

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\subseteq V

is satisfied by

if

, and a negative literal
\lambda^{-}
is satisfied by

if

. A clause is satisfied by

if at least one of its literals is satisfied by

is

by

(or

is a

for

is satisfied by

is

This construction works intuitively as follows. For the first direction, we consider an assignment
z\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and we construct
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
, in such a way that, at the first iteration, each source
\lambda_{r}

This is like if, in three iterations, all the negative arcs “disappear” leaving “free” a full-positive cycle. has at least one fixed point even if

is not a true assignment. These two properties will be very useful for the other hardness results. Below, we do not described formally this “three iterations process”. We only prove what we need for the following.

Lemma 7
.

Let
z\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
defined by:
f_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z
and, for all
i\in U_{\psi}
,
f_{i}
is the AND function if
i\in\boldsymbol{\ell}
and the OR function otherwise. Then
f
has at least one fixed point, and if
\psi
is satisfied by
z
, then
f
has at least two fixed points.

Proof.

Let
x
be the configuration on
V_{\psi}
defined by
x_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z
and
x_{U_{\psi}}=1
. First,
f(x)_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z=x_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Second,
f_{\ell_{0}}(x)=x_{c_{1}}=1=x_{\ell_{0}}
and, for all
r\in[n]
,
f_{\ell_{r}}(x)=x_{\lambda^{+}_{r}}\land x_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=1\land 1=1=x_{\ell_{r}}
. Third, any vertex
i\in U_{\psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\ell}
has a positive in-neighbor
j\in U_{\psi}
. Since
f_{i}
is the OR function and
x_{j}=1
, we have
f_{i}(x)=1=x_{i}
. Thus,
f(x)=x
.

Let
y
be the configuration on
V_{\psi}
be defined by:
y_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z
and, for all
i\in U_{\psi}
,

y_{i}=1\iff\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\textrm{$i$ is a clause $\mu_{s}$, or}\\
\textrm{$i$ is a positive literal $\lambda^{+}_{r}$ such that $z_{\lambda_{r}}=1$, or}\\
\textrm{$i$ is a negative literal $\lambda^{-}_{r}$ such that $z_{\lambda_{r}}=0$.}\end{array}\right.

Note that
y_{\lambda^{+}_{r}}=z_{\lambda_{r}}\neq y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}
for all

, so
x\neq y
. Suppose that
\psi
is satisfied by

, and let us prove that

is a fixed point of

. It is clear that
f(y)_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z=y_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Let
r\in[n]
. Since exactly one of
y_{\lambda^{+}_{r}},y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}
is
0
, we have
f_{\ell_{r}}(y)=y_{\lambda^{+}_{r}}\land y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=0=y_{\ell_{r}}
; and
f_{\ell_{0}}(y)=y_{c_{1}}=0=y_{\ell_{0}}
. Then

\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{\lambda^{+}_{r}}(y)=y_{\lambda_{r}}\lor y_{\ell_{r-1}}=z_{\lambda_{r}}\lor 0=z_{\lambda_{r}}=y_{\lambda^{+}_{r}},\\[2.84526pt]
f_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}(y)=\neg y_{\lambda_{r}}\lor y_{\ell_{r-1}}=\neg z_{\lambda_{r}}\lor 0=\neg z_{\lambda_{r}}=y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}.\end{array}

Let

. Since
\psi

,
\mu_{s}
has at least one in-neighbor which is a positive literal

with

. Hence,
\mu_{s}
has an in-neighbor

and since

is the OR function we deduce that
f_{\mu_{s}}(y)=1=y_{\mu_{s}}
. We finally prove that

by induction on

from

to

means

, we have

by induction so

. Thus,
f(y)=y
.
∎

For the other direction, we suppose that there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})

. Then we prove that either

or

where

or

has an in-neighbor (a positive or negative literal associated to some variable

Lemma 8
.

Let
D
be a nice SID, without two vertex-disjoint positive cycles, such that every positive cycle is full-positive and, for any negative arc
(j,i)
, either

. Let

with two fixed point

and

•

We have

or
y\leq x
.

•

If

.

Let

be the set of vertices

such that
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
. We prove
that each

x_{j}{\oplus}\tilde{\sigma}_{ji}=x_{i}
. Let

and suppose that
x_{i}<y_{i}
, the

f_{i}(x)<f_{i}(y)

.

We deduce that

of

, each vertex is of in-degree one,

has no other cycle (since otherwise

or

. Then

and
(j,i)
is negative, which is a contradiction since
y_{i}=1
. Thus,
x_{j}=y_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
D
be a SID and
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with two fixed points
x,y
such that
x\leq y
. Every vertex
i
with only positive in-neighbors such that
x_{i}=y_{i}
has a positive in-neighbor
j
such that
x_{j}=y_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
i
be as in the statement. If
x_{i}=y_{i}=0
then, by
Lemma
2
,
i
has a positive in-neighbor
j
such that

y_{j}=f_{i}(y)=y_{i}=0
, and since
x\leq y
we have
x_{j}=y_{j}=0
. If
x_{i}=y_{i}=1

then, by Lemma
2
,
i
has a positive
in-neighbor
j
such that
x_{j}=f_{i}(x)=x_{i}=1
, and since
x\leq y
we have

x_{j}=y_{j}=1
.
∎

We go back to our construction
D_{\psi}
and prove a kind of converse of Lemma
7
. We need a definition. For every
r\in[n]
,
\ell_{r}
is of in-degree two in
D_{\psi}
, so
f_{\ell_{r}}
is either the AND function or the OR function; we then define
\epsilon(f)\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
as follows: for all
r\in[n]
,

\epsilon(f)_{\lambda_{r}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\textrm{if $f_{\ell_{r}}$ is the OR function,}\\[2.84526pt]
1&\textrm{if $f_{\ell_{r}}$ is the AND function.}\end{array}\right.

Lemma 10
.

If
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
has distinct fixed points
x
and
y
, then
\psi
is satisfied by
x_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\oplus}\epsilon(f)
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
and
\epsilon=\epsilon(f)
. Suppose that
f
has distinct fixed points
x
and
y
. By Lemma
8
, we have
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
. Suppose, without loss, that
x\leq y
. Since vertices in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are sources, there is
z\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
such that
x_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=y_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z
. Note also that, for every
i\in\boldsymbol{\ell}
,
\{i\}
is a positive feedback vertex, and thus
x_{i}<y_{i}
by Lemma
1
.

Consider any clause
\mu_{s}
, and let us prove that it is satisfied by
z\oplus\epsilon
. By Lemma
8
, we have
x_{\mu_{s}}=y_{\mu_{s}}
and, by Lemma
9
,
\mu_{s}
has an in-neighbor
i
such that
x_{i}=y_{i}
. This in-neighbor
i
is a positive or negative literal contained in
\mu_{s}
and associated with some variable, say
\lambda_{r}
. We prove that this literal is satisfied by
z\oplus\epsilon
, and so is
\mu_{s}
. We consider two cases:

1.

Suppose that
i=\lambda^{+}_{r}
. Since
x_{\ell_{r-1}}<y_{\ell_{r-1}}
, if
f_{i}
is the OR function we have

x_{i}=f_{i}(x)=x_{\lambda_{r}}\lor x_{\ell_{r-1}}=x_{\lambda_{r}}\lor 0=x_{\lambda_{r}}=z_{\lambda_{r}},

and if
f_{i}
is the AND function we have

y_{i}=f_{i}(y)=y_{\lambda_{r}}\land y_{\ell_{r-1}}=y_{\lambda_{r}}\land 1=y_{\lambda_{r}}=z_{\lambda_{r}}.

Since
x_{i}=y_{i}
, we have
z_{\lambda_{r}}=x_{i}=y_{i}
and consider two cases.

(a)

If
z_{\lambda_{r}}=1
then
x_{i}=1
so, if
f_{\ell_{r}}
is the OR function, then

x_{\ell_{r}}=f_{\ell_{r}}(x)=x_{i}\lor x_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=1\lor x_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=1,

and this contradicts
x_{\ell_{r}}<y_{\ell_{r}}
. Hence,
f_{\ell_{r}}
is the AND function so
\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=0
.

(b)

If
z_{\lambda_{r}}=0
then
y_{i}=0
so, if
f_{\ell_{r}}
is the AND function, then

x_{\ell_{r}}=f_{\ell_{r}}(y)=y_{i}\land y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=0\land y_{\lambda^{-}_{r}}=0,

and this contradicts
x_{\ell_{r}}<y_{\ell_{r}}
. Hence,
f_{\ell_{r}}
is the OR function so
\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=1
.

In both cases we have
z_{\lambda_{r}}{\oplus}\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=1
thus
i=\lambda^{+}_{r}
is satisfied by
z\oplus\epsilon
.

2.

Suppose that
i=\lambda^{-}_{r}
. Since
x_{\ell_{r-1}}<y_{\ell_{r-1}}
, if
f_{i}
is the OR function we have

x_{i}=f_{i}(x)=\neg x_{\lambda_{r}}\lor x_{\ell_{r-1}}=\neg x_{\lambda_{r}}\lor 0=\neg x_{\lambda_{r}}=\neg z_{\lambda_{r}},

and if
f_{i}
is the AND function we have

y_{i}=f_{i}(y)=\neg y_{\lambda_{r}}\land y_{\ell_{r-1}}=\neg y_{\lambda_{r}}\land 1=\neg y_{\lambda_{r}}=\neg z_{\lambda_{r}}.

We deduce that
z_{\lambda_{r}}\neq x_{i}=y_{i}
. If
z_{\lambda_{r}}=0
then
x_{i}=1
and we deduce as in the first case that
\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=0
. If
z_{i}=1
then
y_{i}=0
and we deduce as in the first case that
\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=1
. In both cases we have
z_{\lambda_{r}}{\oplus}\epsilon_{\lambda_{r}}=0
thus
i=\lambda^{-}_{r}
is satisfied by
z{\oplus}\epsilon
.

∎

We can now prove the main property of
D_{\psi}
.

Lemma 11
.

\phi^{\max}(D_{\psi})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2&\textrm{if $\psi$ is satisfiable},\\
1&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
and the positive feedback bound, we have
1\leq\phi^{\max}(D_{\psi})\leq 2
. By Lemma
10
, if
\phi^{\max}(D_{\psi})=2
then
\psi
is satisfiable, and the converse is given by Lemma
7
.
∎

The following lemma is useful to reduce the maximum in-degree without modifying the maximum and minimum number of fixed points. The proof is a simple exercise and is omitted. The transformation described in the statement is illustrated Figure
2
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
D
be a SID and let
I
be the set of vertices with at least three in-neighbors. Suppose that each vertex in
I
has only positive in-neighbors. Let
D^{\prime}
be obtained from
D
by doing the following operations for each
i\in I
. Denoting
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
the in-neighbors of
i
,

•

we remove the arc from
j_{\ell}
to
i
for
1\leq\ell<k
,

•

we add a new vertex
j^{\prime}_{\ell}
and a positive arc from
j_{\ell}
to
j^{\prime}_{\ell}
for
1<\ell<k
, and

•

setting
j^{\prime}_{1}=j_{1}
and
j^{\prime}_{k}=i
, we add a positive arc from
j^{\prime}_{\ell}
to
j^{\prime}_{\ell+1}
for
1\leq\ell<k
.

Then
\Delta(D^{\prime})\leq 2
and
D
has at most
|V_{D}|+|I|\Delta(D)-2|I|
vertices. Furthermore if there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with
\phi^{\max}(D)
fixed points such that
f_{i}
is the OR function for all
i\in I
, then
\phi^{\max}(D^{\prime})=\phi^{\max}(D)
. Similarly, if there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with
\phi^{\min}(D)
fixed points such that
f_{i}
is the OR function for all
i\in I
, then
\phi^{\min}(D^{\prime})=\phi^{\min}(D)
.

4.3
Extensions

We now present easy extensions of the key Lemmas
7
and
10
,
which will be useful for the other hardness results. We start with some definitions.

Definition 2

(Extensions of
D_{\psi}
and partial fixed points)
.

An extension of
D_{\psi}
is a SID
D
which is the union of
D_{\psi}
and a SID
H
with
V_{H}\cap V_{\psi}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\lambda}
; equivalently:

•

D_{\psi}
is a subgraph of
D
(
i.e.
each arc of
D_{\psi}
is in
D
with the same sign), and

•

in- and out-neighbors of vertices in
U_{\psi}
are identical in
D
and
D_{\psi}
.

Let
D
be an extension of
D_{\psi}
and
I=V_{D}\setminus U_{\psi}
. Given
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
, we say that a configuration
z
on
I
is a partial fixed point of
f
if
f(x)_{I}=x_{I}=z
for some configuration
x
on
V_{D}
(which thus extends
z
); since there is no arc from
U_{\psi}
to
I
, if
z
is a partial fixed point, then
f(x)_{I}=z
for every configuration
x
on
V_{D}
extending
z
.

Note that every fixed point extends a partial fixed point. Note also that

D_{\psi}
is an extension of itself with
I=\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, and each

f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
has a unique partial fixed point: the configuration

z
on
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
such that
f_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z
. If
f
is as in
Lemma
7
and
\psi
is satisfied by
z
, then
z
can be extended
into two (global) fixed points. Conversely, in any case, by
Lemma
10
, if
z
can be extended into two (global) fixed points,
then
\psi
is satisfied by
z{\oplus}\epsilon(f)
. The next two lemmas show that,
more generally, if the SID of
f
is an extension of
D_{\psi}
, then these
properties remain true for
every
partial fixed point
z
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
D
be an extension of
D_{\psi}
. Let
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
such that, for all
i\in U_{\psi}
,
f_{i}
is the AND function if
i\in\boldsymbol{\ell}
and the OR function otherwise. Let
z
be a partial fixed point of
f
. Then
f
has at least one fixed point extending
z
and, if
\psi
is satisfied by
z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, then
f
has at least two fixed points extending
z
.

Proof.

Let
I=V_{D}\setminus U_{\psi}
and let
\tilde{f}\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
be defined by
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and, for all
i\in U_{\psi}
,
\tilde{f}_{i}
is the AND function if
i\in\boldsymbol{\ell}
and the OR function otherwise. By Lemma
7
,
\tilde{f}
has a fixed point
\tilde{x}
. Let
x
be the configuration on
V_{D}
defined by
x_{I}=z
and
x_{U_{\psi}}=\tilde{x}_{U_{\psi}}
. Since
z
is a partial fixed point, we have
f(x)_{I}=x_{I}
, and since each vertex
i\in U_{\psi}
has the same in-neighbors in
D
and
D_{\psi}
, and since
f_{i}
and
\tilde{f}_{i}
are either both AND functions or both OR functions, we have
f_{i}(x)=\tilde{f}_{i}(\tilde{x})=\tilde{x}_{i}=x_{i}
. Thus,
x
is a fixed point of
f
. By Lemma
7
, if
\psi
is satisfied by
z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, then
\tilde{f}
has a fixed point
\tilde{y}\neq\tilde{x}
. Similarly, the configuration
y
on
V_{D}
defined by
y_{I}=z
and
y_{U_{\psi}}=\tilde{y}_{U_{\psi}}
is a fixed point of
f
. Since
\tilde{y}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\tilde{x}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, we have
\tilde{x}_{U_{\psi}}\neq\tilde{y}_{U_{\psi}}
and thus
x\neq y
.
∎

If
D
is an extension of
D_{\psi}
, each vertex
\ell_{r}
is of in-degree two in
D
thus, for each
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
,
f_{\ell_{r}}
is either the AND function or the OR function. Then, as previously, we define
\epsilon(f)\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
by
\epsilon(f)_{\lambda_{r}}=0
if
f_{\ell_{r}}
is the OR function and
\epsilon(f)_{\lambda_{r}}=1
otherwise.

Lemma 15
.

Let
D
be an extension of
D_{\psi}
. Choose
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
and let
z
be a partial fixed point of
f
. Then
f
has at most two fixed points extending
z
, and if
f
has two fixed points extending
z
, then
\psi
is satisfied by
z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\oplus}\epsilon(f)
.

Proof.

Let
I=V_{D}\setminus U_{\psi}
. Since
z\in\{0,1\}^{I}
and
D\setminus I
has a positive feedback vertex set of size one, by Lemma
1
,
f
has at most two fixed points extending
z
. Let
\tilde{f}
be the BN with component set
V_{\psi}
defined by:
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and for all configurations
x
on
V_{D}
,
\tilde{f}(x_{V_{\psi}})_{U_{\psi}}=f(x)_{U_{\psi}}
; there is no ambiguity since all the in-neighbors of vertices in
U_{\psi}
are in
V_{\psi}
. Since vertices in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are sources of
D_{\psi}
and since every vertex in
U_{\psi}
have exactly the same in-neighbors in
D
and
D_{\psi}
it is clear that
\tilde{f}\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi})
. Suppose now that
f
has two distinct fixed points
x,y
extending
z
. We easily check that the restrictions
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}
of
x,y
on
V_{\psi}
are fixed points of
\tilde{f}
and that
\epsilon(\tilde{f})=\epsilon(f)
. Since
x_{I}=y_{I}=z
and
x\neq y
, we have
\tilde{x}\neq\tilde{y}
thus, by Lemma
10
,
\psi
is satisfied by
\tilde{x}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\oplus}\epsilon(\tilde{f})=z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\oplus}\epsilon(f)
.
∎

5
Maximum Fixed Point Problem

In this section, we study the case where
k
is not a constant but a parameter of the problem.
Contrary to the two previous sections, we will see here that the restriction to SIDs with a bounded maximum in-degree reduces the complexity of the problem.

5.1
\Delta(D)
bounded

In this first subsection, we study the problem
MaxFPP
for the family of SIDs with a maximum in-degree bounded by a constant
d\geq 2
, and prove that it is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-complete. To introduce this complexity class, let us first recall that problems in
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
consist in counting the number of certificates of decision problems in
\mathrm{\sf NP}
(the number of accepting branches of a non-deterministic polynomial time algorithm). The canonical
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
-complete problem is
#SAT
, which asks for the number of satisfying assignments of an input
3
-CNF formula. The class
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
then corresponds to decision problems computable in polynomial time by a non-deterministic Turing machine with an oracle in
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
(a “black box” answering any problem in the class
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
without using any resource).

Theorem 6
.

When
\Delta(D)\leq d
,
MaxFPP
is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-complete.

We first prove the upper bound.

Lemma 16
.

When
\Delta(D)\leq d
,
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
.

Proof.

Let
d\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Consider the algorithm, which takes as input a SID
D
with
\Delta(D)\leq d
and an integer
k
, and proceeds as follows.

1.

Choose non-deterministically a BN
f
with component set
V_{D}
such that, for all
i\in V_{D}
, the local function
f_{i}
only depends on components in
N(i)
; this can be done in linear time since each local function
f_{i}
can be represented using
2^{|N(i)|}\leq 2^{d}
bits.

2.

Compute the SID
D_{f}
of
f
; this can be done in quadratic time since, to compute the in-neighbors of each
i\in V_{D}
and the corresponding signs, we only have to consider
2^{|N(i)|}\leq 2^{d}
configurations (for each configuration
x
on
N(i)
and
j\in N(i)
, we compare
f_{i}(\tilde{x})
and
f_{i}(\tilde{x}{\oplus}e_{j})
where
\tilde{x}
is any configuration on
V_{D}
extending
x
).

3.

Compute
\phi(f)
, the number of fixed points of
f
, with a call to the
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
oracle (the problem of deciding if
f
has a fixed point is trivially in
\mathrm{\sf NP}
: choose non-deterministically a configuration, and accept if and only if it is a fixed point).

4.

Accept if and only if
\phi(f)\geq k
and
D_{f}=D
.

This non-deterministic polynomial time algorithm has an accepting branch if and only
\phi^{\max}(D)\geq k
, so
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
.
∎

For the lower bound, it is convenient to define
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
in another way. The class
\mathrm{\sf PP}
regroups decision problems decided by a probabilistic Turing machine in polynomial time, with a probability of error less than a half.
The canonical
\mathrm{\sf PP}
-complete decision problem is
Majority-SAT
, which asks if the majority of the assignments of a given CNF formula are satisfying. The class
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf PP}}
then corresponds to decision problems computable in polynomial time by a non-deterministic Turing machine with an oracle in
\mathrm{\sf PP}
. It is well known that
\mathrm{\sf P}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}=\mathrm{\sf P}^{\mathrm{\sf PP}}

[
35
]
, and from that we easily deduce that
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}=\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf PP}}
. The following problem is known to be
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf PP}}
-complete
[
31
,
2
]
, and thus also
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-complete.

Existential-Majority-SAT
(
E-MajSAT
)

Input:
a CNF formula
\psi
over
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
and
s\in[n]
.

Question:
does there exist
z^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\}
, such that
\psi
is satisfied by the majority of the assignments
z\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
extending
z^{\prime}
?

Lemma 17
.

When
\Delta(D)\leq d
,
MaxFPP
is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-hard.

Proof.

We present a reduction from
E-MajSAT
. Let
\psi
be a CNF formula over the set of variables

. Let

.

Let

obtained from

by adding, for every

. Let us prove that:

Let

\alpha(z^{\prime})=\alpha^{*}
. Let

such that

and, for all

,

and the OR function otherwise. Since each vertex in

is the set of partial fixed points of

has a fixed point extending

.

For the other direction, choose any

and set
\epsilon=\epsilon(f)
. Since vertices in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
are sources,

for some

. Since each vertex in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\setminus\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
has a positive loop and no other in-coming arc,

is the set of partial fixed points of

. By Lemma

, for every

,

has at most two fixed points extending

has two fixed points extending

\psi

. Since every fixed point extends a partial fixed point, we deduce that

.

Consequently, we have
\alpha^{*}\geq 2^{n-s-1}
if and only if
\phi^{\max}(D_{\psi,s})\geq 3\cdot 2^{n-s-1}
. Thus,
(\psi,s)
is a true instance of

if and only if
(D_{\psi,s},k)
is a true instance of

, where

.

We have
\Delta(D_{\psi,s})\leq 2n
but, using Lemma

with

and at most

vertices such that

(for that we use the fact, showed above, that there is

with

fixed points where

is the OR function for every vertex

of in-degree at least three).

Let

•

In this section, we study decision problems related to the minimum number of fixed points, and we obtain the following tight complexity results. A new complexity class is involved:

Let

Lemma 23

Let

As argued in Section

For

\alpha(z^{\prime})=\alpha^{*}

f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi,s})
such that
f_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}=z^{\prime}
and, for all

,

is the AND function if

and the OR function otherwise. Since each vertex in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\setminus\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
has a positive loop and no other in-coming arc,

is the set of partial fixed points of

. Hence, by Lemma
24
, for every

,

has at most

fixed points, and thus
\phi^{\min}(D_{\psi,s})\leq 2^{n-s}-\alpha(z^{\prime})=2^{n-s}-\alpha^{*}
.

For the other direction, let any
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi,s})
and set

\epsilon=\epsilon(f)
. Since vertices in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
are sources,

f_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}=z^{\prime}
for some
z^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}}
. Since each vertex in

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\setminus\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
has a positive loop and no other in-coming arc,

E(z^{\prime})
is the set of partial fixed points of
f
. By Lemma

25
, for every
z\in E(z^{\prime})
, if
\psi
is not satisfied by

, then
f
has a fixed point extending

has at
least
2^{n-s}-\alpha(z^{\prime}{\oplus}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})
fixed points, and since

\alpha(z^{\prime}{\oplus}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}})\leq\alpha^{*}
, we deduce that

\phi^{\min}(D_{\psi,s})\geq 2^{n-s}-\alpha^{*}
.

We have
\Delta(D_{\psi,s})\leq 2n
but, using Lemma
12
, we can obtain from
D_{\psi,s}
a SID

with
\Delta(D^{\prime}_{\psi,s})\leq 2
and at most

vertices such that

\phi^{\min}(D^{\prime}_{\psi,s})=\phi^{\min}(D_{\psi,s})=2^{n-s}-\alpha^{*}.

(For that we use the fact, showed above, that there is
f\in\mathrm{F}(D_{\psi,s})
with
\phi^{\min}(D_{\psi,s})
fixed points where

is the OR function for every vertex

of in-degree three.)

Consequently, we have
\alpha^{*}\geq 2^{n-s-1}
if and only if
\phi^{\min}(D^{\prime}_{\psi,s})\leq 2^{n-s-1}
. Thus,
(\psi,s)
is a true instance of

if and only if
(D^{\prime}_{\psi,s},k)

, where

if

,

is
\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf\#P}}
-hard.

An other consequence is that
\phi^{\min}(D^{\prime}_{\psi,s})=0
if and only if
\alpha^{*}=2^{n-s}
, that is, there is a partial assignment

of the variables in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
such that
\psi

. Thus,
(\psi,s)
is a true instance of

is a true instance of

\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf NP}}
-hard. We then deduce from Lemma

that, when

\mathrm{\sf NP}^{\mathrm{\sf NP}}
-hard for all

∎

It remains to prove that, in the general case,

Definition 8

(

Proof.

has the AND function if

Conversely, suppose that

7
Conclusion and perspectives

In this paper, we studied the algorithmic complexity of many decision problems related to counting the number of fixed points of a BN from its SID only. Except for
1
-
MaxFPP
, we proved exact complexity bounds for each one of these problems,
revealing a large range of complexities, some classes having a pretty scarce literature.

The function problems of computing
\phi^{\max}(D)
or
\phi^{\min}(D)
can, quite classically, be seen as
n
(since the result ranges from
0
to
2^{n}
, but the case
2^{n}
can be treated separately) decision problems providing the bits of the answer by binary search. Table
1
gives the worst case complexity of computing each of these bits in the different cases (minimum/maximum, degree bounded/unbounded). Note that, even though they do not intuitively correspond to counting problems, computing
\phi^{\max}(D)
or
\phi^{\min}(D)
are proven to be
\mathrm{\sf\#P}
-hard problems even for bounded degree, from the proofs of Lemmas
17
and
27
.

Even if the problems we studied are natural, they have not been considered
before. Indeed, most of the works on the complexity of BNs are of the form:
does a BN
f
satisfies a given dynamical property
P
? Here, we studied
problems of the form: does a SID
D
admits a BN
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)

satisfying a given property
P
? In some cases, this new problem can be easier.
For instance, the problem of deciding if a BN (encoded as a concatenation of
local functions in conjunctive normal form) has at least one fixed point is

\mathrm{\sf NP}
-complete
[
28
]
, but we showed that deciding if there exists a BN
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
with at least one fixed point is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
. Conversely, the
problem of deciding if a BN
f
has no fixed point is in
\mathrm{\sf coNP}
, whereas it is

\mathrm{\sf NEXPTIME}
-complete to decide if there is a BN
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
without
fixed point.

This new theme opens many further investigations. First, we proved that
1
-
MaxFPP
is equivalent (up to a polynomial reduction) to the problem of finding an even cycle in a digraph. This problem is known to be in
\mathrm{\sf P}

[
32
,
45
]
but, to the best of our knowledge, no work has been done to show its
\mathsf{C}
-hardness for any complexity class
\mathsf{C}
. It would be interesting to find such lower bounds, because this problem is equivalent to many other decision problems of graph theory (
[
45
]
lists several of them).

Furthermore, we could study the effect of other restrictions on the SIDs considered. For instance, what happens for the family of SIDs with positives arcs only? It is an interesting problem, because the BN having such SIDs are monotone, and since Tarski
[
48
]
monotone networks received great attention
[
10
,
44
,
4
,
6
,
33
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. We hope to be able to adapt our constructions in order to fit this new constraint. We conjecture that, in this case, there is an integer
k_{0}
such that
k
-
MaxFPP
is in
\mathrm{\sf P}
if
k\leq k_{0}
, and
\mathrm{\sf NP}
-complete otherwise.

As further variations, we could study unsigned interaction digraph, or consider

automata network
instead of Boolean network (where a component can take
more than two states), or consider BNs that may ignore some arcs of the SID to
get closer to real experimental conditions (where arcs of the SID are not
always a hundred percent accurate). This could affect the problems’
difficulties drastically, and may reveal relations to
network coding

problems in information theory
[
30
,
20
,
18
]
.

Finally, we could think of new problems where, given a SID
D
, we want to decide other properties shared by all
f\in\mathrm{F}(D)
. Indeed, there are many other interesting BN properties, such as the number or the size of the limit cycles, the size of their transients… we could also look for properties of their basins of attraction, some complexity results already being known for threshold networks
[
17
]
.

As mentioned in the introduction, there are many update schedules that can be used to describe a dynamics from
f
, the most classical ones being the parallel, block-sequential, and asynchronous update schedules
[
43
,
8
,
42
]
. With the parallel (or synchronous) update schedule, the dynamics is described by the digraph with an arc from
x
to
f(x)
for all configurations
x
, as in Example
1
. For the other schedules, we need a notation. Let
V
be the component set of
f
and
I\subseteq V
. Let
f^{I}(x)
be the configuration
y
on
V
such that
y_{i}=f_{i}(x)
for
i\in I
, and
y_{i}=x_{i}
for
i\not\in I
. Hence,
f^{I}(x)
is obtained from
x
by updating in parallel components in
I
. A block-sequential update schedule is then an ordered partition
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
of
V
and the resulting dynamics is the parallel dynamics of the BN
h=f^{I_{k}}\circ\dots\circ f^{I_{2}}\circ f^{I_{1}}
. Finally, the asynchronous dynamics of
f
is described by the digraph with an arc from
x
to
f^{\{i\}}(x)
for all configurations
x
and components
i
. In every case, attractors correspond to the terminal strongly connected components (and are also called limit cycles in the first two cases). In each case, the fixed points of
f
correspond to attractors of size one and are, in this sense, invariant
[
21
]
. But the other attractors, and their basins, can change a lot according to the update schedule used
[
15
]
. Hence, when studying dynamical properties not involving only fixed points, one has to fix the update schedule (but any deterministic dynamics can be obtained by a network updated in parallel, which can be constructed in polynomial time
[
43
]
; hence in deterministic cases such as block-sequential, it may be more meaningful to quantify over the possible update schedules within the class under consideration, in the problem definition), and complexity results, as well as proof technics, should highly depend on the choice
[
37
,
13
,
38
,
12
]
.
