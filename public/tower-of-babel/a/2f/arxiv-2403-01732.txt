Motion of sharp interface of Allen-Cahn equation with anisotropic nonlinear diffusion
Tadahisa Funaki ∗, Hyunjoon Park †

Abstract
We consider the Allen-Cahn equation with nonlinear anisotropic diffusion and derive anisotropic direction-dependent curvature flow under the sharp interface limit. The anisotropic curvature flow was already studied, but its derivation is new. We prove both generation and propagation of the interface. For the proof we construct sub- and super-solutions applying the comparison theorem. The problem discussed in this article naturally appeared in the study of the interacting particle systems, especially of non-gradient type. The Allen-Cahn equation obtained from systems of gradient type has a simpler nonlinearity in diffusion and leads to isotropic mean-curvature flow. We extend those results to anisotropic situations.

Introduction

The Allen-Cahn equation with nonlinear diffusion has a natural physical
background. However, compared to those with linear diffusion,
they seem to be less studied. In general, nonlinear partial differential
equations, which describe macroscopic phenomena, are derived from
microscopic systems via a certain scaling limit especially under an
averaging effect due to local ergodicity of the system;
see
[
17
]
,
[
15
]
. In particular,
the linear Laplacian arises at macroscopic level when molecules at
microscopic level evolve independently. However, if molecules evolve
with interaction, we obtain a nonlinear Laplacian instead of linear.
Especially when the interaction of microscopic particles has
a special structure called of gradient type, which gives a good
cancellation in scaling limit, we obtain nonlinearity of the form

\Delta\varphi(u)
for particle density
u
with a certain nonlinear
increasing function
\varphi
; see Remark
1.1
-case (i),

[
9
]
(writing
P
instead of
\varphi
). More generally,
from microscopic systems called of non-gradient type, we obtain
a nonlinear differential operator of second order

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{i}}\{D_{ij}(u)\partial_{x_{j}}u\}

of divergence form. The diffusion coefficients
\{D_{ij}(u)\}

are known to be described by the so-called Green-Kubo formula;
see
[
17
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
. The reaction term
f(u)
in the Allen-Cahn
equation reflects the creation and annihilation of microscopic
particles.

In this article we study the Allen-Cahn equation with nonlinear anisotropic diffusion. Namely, we consider the following Cauchy problem of partial differential equation:

(
P^{\varepsilon}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}(u^{\varepsilon}):=\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{i}}\big\{D_{ij}(u^{\varepsilon})\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\varepsilon}\big\}-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f(u^{\varepsilon})&\text{in}~\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\dfrac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on}~\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

where
N\geq 2
is the spatial dimension,
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
\varepsilon
is a small positive number,
\nu
is the outward normal vector on the boundary
\partial\Omega
and
u_{0}(x)
is a bounded and
C^{2}
function in
\Omega
. The function
f
is a bistable reaction term with three roots
f(\alpha_{+})=f(\alpha)=f(\alpha_{-})=0,\alpha_{-}<\alpha<\alpha_{+}
and satisfying

(1.1)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha_{\pm})<0,~\nu:=f^{\prime}(\alpha)>0,~f\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}).

The term
(D_{ij}(s))_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is symmetric and strictly positive definite matrix for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We further assume the existence of some positive constants
c_{D},C_{D}>0
satisfying

(1.2)

\displaystyle c_{D}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}D_{ij}(s)\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\leq C_{D},~\|D_{ij}\|_{C^{3}({\mathbb{R}})}\leq C_{D}

where
s\in{\mathbb{R}},\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},|\eta|=1
. Moreover, we assume equipotential condition to
D_{ij}
and
f
:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha_{-}}^{\alpha_{+}}D_{ij}(s)f(s)ds=0,

for all
1\leq i,j\leq N
.

For the initial condition
u_{0}(x)
we assume that
u_{0}\in C^{2}(\Omega)
. Throughout the paper, we define
c_{0}
as follows:

(1.4)

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle:=||u_{0}||_{C^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}.

Furthermore, we define
\Gamma_{0}
by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}:=\{x\in\Omega:u_{0}(x)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\}

and we suppose
\Gamma_{0}
is a
C^{4+\nu},0<\nu<1
, hypersurface without boundary such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}\Subset\Omega,\nabla u_{0}(x)\cdot n(x)\neq 0\text{ if}~x\in\Gamma_{0},

(1.6)

\displaystyle u_{0}>\alpha\text{ in }\Omega_{0}^{+},~~~~~~u_{0}<\alpha\text{ in }\Omega_{0}^{-},

where
\Omega_{0}^{-}
denotes the region enclosed by
\Gamma_{0}
,
\Omega_{0}^{+}
the region enclosed between
\partial\Omega
and
\Gamma_{0}
and
n
is the outward normal vector to
\Gamma_{0}
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the reaction term prevails over the diffusion term, thus the limit solution will take either
\alpha_{+}
or
\alpha_{-}
and a hypersurface
\Gamma_{t}
(which we call an interface) occurs that separates the two stable steady states. From this observation we expect two stages to take place for the solution

of (

P
ε

\varepsilon^{-2}f(u)
, hence the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
can be approximated by ordinary differential equation
u_{t}=\varepsilon^{-2}f(u)
. This implies that the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
quickly converges close to either
{\alpha_{+}}
or
{\alpha_{-}}
, creating a steep transition layer. (II) After the creation of the steep transition layer, it starts to propagate. And, from the limiting behavior of
u^{\varepsilon}
, one can expect that the movement of this steep transition layer can be described by an interface
\Gamma_{t}
. In fact, the interface propagates according to the following motion equation (see Section
2
for details)

(
P^{0}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{n}=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mu_{ij}(n)\partial_{x_{i}}n_{j}&\text{on~}\Gamma_{t},\\
\Gamma_{t}\Big|_{t=0}=\Gamma_{0},\end{cases}

where

is the outward normal velocity of
\Gamma_{t}
,

is the outward normal vector to
\Gamma_{t}
, and

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}(e)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda(e)}\int_{\alpha_{-}}^{\alpha_{+}}\left[D_{ij}(s)\sqrt{W_{e}(s)}-\dfrac{\partial_{e_{i}}(W_{e}(s))}{2}\partial_{e_{j}}\left(\dfrac{a_{e}(s)}{\sqrt{W_{e}(s)}}\right)\right]ds,

\displaystyle\lambda(e)

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha_{-}}^{\alpha_{+}}\sqrt{W_{e}(s)}ds,~W_{e}(s)=-2\int_{\alpha_{-}}^{s}a_{e}(s)f(s)ds,~a_{e}(s)=e\cdot D(s)e.

In our setting, the matrix
(\mu_{ij})
becomes dependent on

which gives an anisotropic feature to the interface motion. The well-posedness of the problem (

P
0

) will be shown in Section
2

be the time that
\Gamma_{t}
exists on
[0,T]
and denote
\Omega_{t}^{-}
be the region enclosed by
\Gamma_{t}
and
\Omega_{t}^{+}
be the region enclosed by
\Gamma_{t}
and
\partial\Omega
.

Remark 1.1
.

The motion equation (

P
0

) is general in the sense that it matches with the motion equation of the Allen-Cahn equation with (i) isotropic nonlinear diffusion or (ii) anisotropic diffusion without the nonlinear diffusivity.
In the case of (i), where

with a smooth increasing function

and

if

, we no longer have

vanishes and the motion equation becomes

\displaystyle V_{n}=-\tilde{\lambda}\kappa,

where

is a mean curvature and
\tilde{\lambda}
is some constant which depends on
{\varphi}
and

, and this coincides with the result of
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
11
]
.
In the case of (ii), the matrix
D_{ij}
no longer depends on

and the term

depends only on

, thus these terms can be considered as constant during the computation of
\mu_{ij}
, which yields

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}(e)=D_{ij}-{\partial_{e_{i}}(\sqrt{a_{e}})\partial_{e_{j}}(\sqrt{a_{e}})}.

This implies that
\mu_{ij}
becomes independent from the reaction term

and the resulting motion equation can be simplified to

\displaystyle\dfrac{V_{n}}{\sqrt{a_{n}}}=-{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial_{n}a_{n}}{2\sqrt{a_{n}}}\right),

which coincides with the motion equation introduced in
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
, where

with

.

The aim of this article is to rigorously prove that the solution

of (

{\alpha_{-}}&\text{in}~\Omega_{t}^{-},\end{cases}~~\text{for}~t\in[0,T].

\displaystyle{\alpha_{-}}-\eta_{g}\leq u^{\varepsilon}(x,t^{\varepsilon})\leq{\alpha_{+}}+\eta_{g},

(ii)

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(x,t^{\varepsilon})\geq{\alpha_{+}}-\eta_{g},

2
Formal asymptotic expansion

In this section we give a formal asymptotic expansion to derive the interface motion corresponding to Problem (

P
ε

) using the argument introduced in
[
16
]
. Even though the computation in this section is formal, it provides a helpful intuition for the analysis in later sections.

We start from the assumption that
d^{\varepsilon}=d^{\varepsilon}(x,t)
is the signed distance function to the interface
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega,u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\alpha\}
defined by

\displaystyle d^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\begin{cases}{\rm dist}(v,\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})&\text{for}~x\in\Omega^{\varepsilon,+}_{t}\\
-{\rm dist}(v,\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})&\text{for}~x\in\Omega^{\varepsilon,-}_{t},\end{cases}

where
\Omega^{\varepsilon,+}_{t}
is the area enclosed by
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}
and
\Omega^{\varepsilon,-}_{t}
is the area enclosed between
\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}
. Following the idea of
[

]
we assume that

has the expansion

\displaystyle d^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=d_{0}(x,t)+\varepsilon d_{1}(x,t)+\varepsilon^{2}d_{2}(x,t)+\cdots,

and define
\Gamma_{t}=\{x\in\Omega,d_{0}(x,t)=0\}
. In this way,
\Gamma_{t}
represents the interface of

as

and

can be considered as the signed distance function of
\Gamma_{t}
.

We assume that

also has similar expansion to

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\alpha_{\pm}+\varepsilon u_{1}^{\pm}(x,t)+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}^{\pm}(x,t)+\cdots~\text{in}~Q_{T}^{\pm},~~Q_{T}^{\pm}=\cup_{0<t\leq T}\big(\Omega_{t}^{\pm}\times\{t\}\big).

Similarly, we assume

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)={U_{0}}(z,x,t;e)+\varepsilon U_{1}(z,x,t;e)+\varepsilon^{2}U_{2}(z,x,t;e)+\cdots,z=\frac{d^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle{U_{0}}(\pm\infty,x,t;e)=\alpha_{\pm},~~U_{i}(\pm\infty,x,t;e)=u_{i}^{\pm}(x,t),

Under this assumption, we search for the suitable candidate of

and

by substituting the inner expansion into the equation (

P
ε

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{U_{0z}}\partial_{t}d^{\varepsilon}+O(1),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f({U_{0}})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f^{\prime}({U_{0}})U_{1}+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(D_{ij}({U_{0}}){U_{0z}}\Big)_{z}\left(\partial_{x_{i}}d_{0}\,\partial_{x_{j}}d_{0}\right.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(a_{e}({U_{0}})\psi)_{zz}+f^{\prime}({U_{0}})\psi=G(z),~~z\in{\mathbb{R}}\\

where

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}(e)=\mu_{ij}^{1}(e)+\mu_{ij}^{2}(e),

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}^{1}(e)={\lambda(e)}^{-1}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}A_{e}(U_{0})_{z}D_{ij}(U_{0}){U_{0z}}dz},

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}^{2}(e)=2{\lambda(e)}^{-1}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}A_{e}(U_{0})_{z}\big(e_{k}D_{ik}(U_{0})U_{0e_{j}}\big)_{z}dz},
