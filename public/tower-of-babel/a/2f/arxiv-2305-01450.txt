Abstract
. In the present paper we compute Alexander polynomials for certain classes of conic-line arrangements in the complex projective plane which are related to pencils. We prove two general results for curve arrangements coming from Halphen pencils of index k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . Then we apply them to the Hesse or the Chilean arrangement of 12 conics and to some of its degenerations. The results are completed by computations using computer algebra. In particular, we construct conic-line arrangements which are non-reduced pencil-type arrangements and have as roots of their Alexander polynomials roots of unity of order 7. Such roots are not known and are conjectured not to exist in the class of line arrangements.

1.
Introduction

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the graded polynomial ring in the variables
x,y,z
with the complex coefficients and let
C:f=0
be a reduced curve of degree
d
in the complex projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}=\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
If the curve
C
is reducible, one also calls it as a curve arrangement.
Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in S_{m}
be two homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
, without common factor, and consider the associated pencil of degree
m
curves

{\mathcal{P}}:sg_{1}+tg_{2}=0,

where
(s:t)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We assume that the generic member of
{\mathcal{P}}
is irreducible.
Such a pencil is called a
Halphen pencil of index

k
if one has
g_{2}=h^{k}
for an integer
k>1
and a polynomial
h
which is not a power of another polynomial.
A curve
C
is called a
reduced (resp. non-reduced) pencil-type arrangement associated with
\ell
members
of
{\mathcal{P}}
if there is a number
\ell\geq 3
of reduced members (resp. members with at least one member having a multiple irreducible component) of the pencil
{\mathcal{P}}
such that
C
is the union of these
\ell
members (after replacing all the multiple components by their reductions). For instance, the monomial arrangement

{\mathcal{M}}_{m}:(x^{m}-y^{m})(y^{m}-z^{m})(x^{m}-z^{m})=0

is a reduced pencil-type line arrangement with
\ell=3
members associated to the pencil

{\mathcal{P}}:s(x^{m}-y^{m})+t(y^{m}-z^{m})=0
, while the full monomial arrangement

{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}_{m}:xyz(x^{m}-y^{m})(y^{m}-z^{m})(x^{m}-z^{m})=0

is a non-reduced pencil-type line arrangement with
\ell=3
associated to the pencil

{\mathcal{P}}:sz^{m}(x^{m}-y^{m})+tx^{m}(y^{m}-z^{m})=0
.
Such pencil-type arrangements play a key role in the theory of line arrangements, see for instance
[
21
,
12
]
, and their relation to the freeness was considered in
[
13
,
41
]
.

We consider the complement
U=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus C
and let
F:f=1
be the corresponding Milnor fiber in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, with the usual monodromy action
h:F\to F
. One can also consider the characteristic polynomials of the monodromy, namely

(1.1)

\Delta^{j}_{C}(t)=\det(t\cdot Id-h^{j}\,|\,H^{j}(F,\mathbb{C})),

for
j\in\{0,1,2\}
. It is clear that, when the curve
C
is reduced, one has
\Delta^{0}_{C}(t)=t-1
, and moreover

(1.2)

\Delta^{0}_{C}(t)\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)^{-1}\Delta^{2}_{C}(t)=(t^{d}-1)^{\chi(U)},

where
\chi(U)
denotes the Euler characteristic of the complement
U
, see for instance
[
8
, Proposition 4.1.21]
. Let us recall that

(1.3)

\chi(U)=(d-1)(d-2)+1-\mu(C),

where
\mu(C)
is the total Milnor number of
C
, which is the sum of the Milnor numbers
\mu(C,p)
for all the singular points
p
of
C
.
It follows that the polynomial
\Delta(t)=\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
, also called the Alexander polynomial of
C
– see
[
23
,
24
,
33
]
, determines the remaining polynomial
\Delta^{2}_{C}(t)
if one knows
\mu(C)
.

When
\chi(U)\leq 0
, it is known that all the irreducible components
C_{i}
of
C
are rational, see
[
22
,
25
,
42
]
, and then
\Delta(t)
tends to be quite large. If these components
C_{i}
are smooth,
being rational means that they are either lines or conics, so
C
is a conic-line arrangement.
However, it is easy to show that for any line arrangement
C
with
d=\deg(C)\geq 5
and without points of multiplicity
\geq d-1
one has

(1.4)

\chi(U)\geq d-4\geq 1,

see Proposition
2.3
.
Note that the arrangements of
d
lines having points of multiplicity
\geq d-1
or such that
d<5
are easy to describe, and their monodromy is well-known. In fact, for most line arrangements
C
, the Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
is trivial, namely it satisfies

(1.5)

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)=(t-1)^{r-1},

where
r
is the number of irreducible components of
C
, see for instance
[
5
,
27
,
39
]
. Even when the Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
is non-trivial, possible roots of
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
seem to be very restricted. For instance, we have the following conjecture by Papadima and Suciu
[
29
, Conjecture 1.9]
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a root of the Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
of a line arrangement
C
, not a union of concurrent lines.
If the order of
{\alpha}
has the form
p^{s}
, with
p
a prime number and

s>0
an integer, then
p^{s}\in\{2,3,4\}
.

Moreover, all known examples of line arrangements with non trivial Alexander polynomials come from pencil-type arrangements, see
[
21
,
39
,
40
]
. More precisely, if a
reduced pencil-type line arrangement
consists of
\ell
members of a pencil, then the corresponding Alexander polynomial has as roots any primitive root of order
\ell
, see Corollary
3.2
.
On the other hand, for a
non-reduced pencil-type line arrangement

consisting of
\ell
members, the roots of unity of order
\ell
are not always roots of its Alexander polynomial. For instance, for the full monomial arrangement
{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{M}}_{m}
the cubic roots of unity are roots of the Alexander polynomial if and only if
m\equiv 1
mod
3
, see
[
15
, Remark 3.4]
.
Since for any line arrangement of pencil-type, except the union of concurrent lines, one has
\ell\leq 4
, this was perhaps the motivation for Conjecture
1.1
.
The first author has constructed in
[
14
]
some conic-line arrangements
C
, related to Halphen pencils of conics of index 2, with
\chi(U)\leq 1
and such that

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
admits as roots any root of unity of order
d=\deg(C)
, see Remark
4.9
for additional information.

In this note we construct (reduced and non-reduced) pencil-type conic-line arrangements related to pencils of conics and sextics, with non-trivial Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
and such that
\chi(U)>0
. For instance, the Hesse arrangement of conics
C=\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)
from Theorem
5.4
satisfies
\chi(U)=54
, consists of
\ell=4
reduced members in a pencil of sextics, and
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
has roots of order
8
. This arrangement has non quasi homogeneous singularities, but which are locally topological equivalent to quasi homogeneous singularities.
It seems therefore that Conjecture
1.1
has nothing to do with the positivity of
\chi(U)
or the local topological properties of singularities, but is likely to be a property specific to line arrangements.

It is an interesting question to see how the freeness of a curve
C
is reflected in the topological properties of
U
and
F
, see for instance
[
2
]
.
In this note we show, in particular, that some new free and nearly free conic-line arrangements lead to arrangements with non-trivial Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
.
The freeness of conic-line arrangements is also discussed in
[
38
]
, from a different perspective and with an aim towards Terao’s freeness conjecture.

Let us briefly present an outline of our note. In Section 2, we review some basic facts on the Alexander polynomials of curve arrangements
C
and prove the inequality (
1.4
) in Proposition
2.3
.
In Section 3, we discuss the relation between Alexander polynomials and pencil-type arrangements. We recall the basic facts in Theorem
3.1
and prove two of our main results, namely Theorem

3.3
and Theorem
3.4
which explain the additional information provided by
Halphen pencils. Using these results, we can predict the
existence of certain roots of the Alexander polynomials in all the cases discussed in this paper
, see Remarks
4.9
,
6.3
and
6.5
.

In Section 4, we consider conic-line arrangements associated to pencils of conics. Their freeness, except from the case of the generic pencil of Type I, see Theorem
4.3
, is not a consequence of the results in
[
13
,
41
]
.
Both the freeness and the Alexander polynomials are computed using
the algebra software
Singular
via the algorithms developed in

[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, which are recalled in Section 2.

In Section 5, we discuss the main example of our paper, the 1-parameter family of Hesse arrangements
\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)
for

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-2,-2\omega,-2\omega^{2},0,1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}

with
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
, of
12
smooth conics in the plane
[
19
,
26
]
, which is both free and has an interesting Alexander polynomial. In Section 6, we consider some degenerations of the Hesse arrangement
\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)
, obtained by setting
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
for

\lambda_{0}\in\{-2,-2\omega,-2\omega^{2},0,1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}

in the equation
f(\lambda)=0
.
For each of these values of
\lambda
, the defining equation
f(\lambda)=0
has 3 linear factors which occurs with multiplicity 2, hence
f(\lambda)=0
is in fact a multiarrangement
{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda)
formed by 9 smooth conics and 3 double lines. From the point of view of pencils of sextics, these degenerations are union of 4 members (one double and 3 reduced) of a Halphen pencil of sextics of index 2.
We denote by
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
the
non-reduced pencil-type
conic-line arrangement
with is obtained from
{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda)
by replacing the 3 double lines by the corresponding reduced lines, and by
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
the
reduced pencil-type
subarrangement of
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
consisting just of the 3 reduced members.
These 14 new arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
have very interesting properties themselves. For instance, Theorem
6.1
says that all the seven conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
are free, and only three of the seven conic arrangements

{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
, namely those corresponding to
\lambda^{3}=1
, are free.
Finally, Theorem
6.2
and Theorem
6.4
say that unit roots of order 6 (resp. 7) occur as roots of the Alexander polynomial of the arrangements
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
(resp.
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
).
It is an interesting fact that the combinatorics and the local type of singularities of the arrangements

{\mathcal{A}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
(resp.
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
) are very different, but nevertheless one has

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(0)}(t)=\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(1)}(t)\text{ and }\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(0)}(t)=\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(1)}(t).

One explanation of this fact comes by looking at the associated pencils of sextics: both
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
consist
of 3 reduced fibers, and
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
are obtained by adding the reduce curve coming from the double cubic in the pencil, which is in fact a triangle.

2.
Some general properties of Alexander polynomials of curve arrangements

In order to construct conic-line arrangements
C
with non-trivial Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
, the following result is quite useful, see
[
9
, Section 6.4]
. We recall that for any isolated plane curve singularity
(X,0)
, there is a Milnor fiber
F_{0}
and a monodromy operator
h_{0}:F_{0}\to F_{0}
, giving rise to a local Alexander polynomial

(2.1)

\Delta^{1}_{(X,0)}(t)=\det(t\cdot{\rm Id}-h^{1}_{0}\,|\,H^{1}(F_{0},\mathbb{C})).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
C=\cup_{i=1}^{s}C_{i}
be a curve arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that the curve
C_{1}
is irreducible. For any singular point
p
of
C
situated on
C_{1}
, let
\Delta^{1}_{(C,p)}(t)
be the Alexander polynomial of the singularity
(C,p)
. Then the following conditions hold.

(1)

Any root of the Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
is a root of unity of order
d=\deg(C)
.

(2)

The Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
divides the product
\prod_{p}\Delta^{1}_{(C,p)}(t)
, where

p
runs through all the singularities of
C
situated on
C_{1}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Here are some examples of local Alexander polynomials. For the simple singularity
A_{n}:u^{2}+v^{n+1}=0
, the corresponding Alexander polynomial is

\Delta^{1}_{(A_{n})}(t)=\frac{t^{n+1}-(-1)^{n+1}}{t+1}.

For an ordinary
m
-multiple point
(X_{m},0)
, that is when there are
m
smooth branches meeting transversally in pairs at a given point, we have

\Delta^{1}_{(X_{m},0)}(t)=(t^{m}-1)^{m-2}(t-1).

To get these formulas, we refer to the proof of Theorem (3.4.10) in
[
8
]
.

Theorem
2.1
gives necessary conditions to get interesting eigenvalues for the monodromy. However, these conditions are far from being sufficient. To make sure that a root of unity
{\alpha}
such that
{\alpha}^{d}=1
is a root of the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
, we can follow
two approaches
.

The first approach
is to compute the Alexander polynomials of plane curves using two Singular codes, which can be found at

https://math.unice.fr/~dimca/singular.html

The first one, the code
monof3(q1,q2)
, computes the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
for a
free
curve
C
and is very rapid. Its construction is described in
[
16
,
12
]
, see also
[
17
, Remark 4.4]
.

The second one, the code
mono3\_wh()
, described in
[
17
,
12
]
, computes the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
for a
any
reduced curve
C
. Moreover, it computes the total Milnor number
\mu(C)
and then, using the equalities (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), one can determine the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{2}_{C}(t)
as well (which is in fact also given by this code). However, for this second code, make sure that the coordinates
x,y,z
are chosen such that the line at infinity
L:z=0
contains no singularities of the curve
C
. This is achieved by making a linear substitution
z\mapsto ax+by+z
with
a,b
being general enough.
Otherwise the computation of
\mu(C)
gives a wrong value.

More precisely, let

(2.2)

{\alpha}_{q}=\exp(-2\pi iq/d)

be a root of unity of order
d=\deg C
, where
0\leq q\leq d
and let
m({\alpha}_{q})
be the multiplicity of

{\alpha}_{q}
as a root of the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{C}(t)
.
One has
{\alpha}_{0}={\alpha}_{d}=1
and

(2.3)

m(1)=b_{1}(U)=r-1,

where
r
is the number of irreducible components of
C
.
The output of the code
monof3(q1,q2)
with
q1=3
and
q2=d
is a Table, where the first column contains the values of
q
from
3
to
d
,
and the before last column contains the data

n_{2}(q)
. We set
n_{2}(0)=n_{2}(1)=n_{2}(2)=0
. Then
[
17
, Remark 4.4]
yields

(2.4)

m({\alpha}_{q})=n_{2}(q)+n_{2}(d-q)\text{ for any }q=0,\ldots,d.

The output of the code
mono3_{w}h()
, when the curve has some non quasi homogeneous singularities, that is when
\tau(C)<\mu(C)
, is first the total Tjurina number
\tau(C)
and the total Milnor
\mu(C)
. Then there is again a Table, where the first column contains the values of
q
from
3
to
d
,
and the before last column contains the data

H^{1}_{q}
. We set
H^{1}_{0}=H^{1}_{1}=H^{1}_{2}=0
. Then
[
17
, Remark 4.4]
yields

(2.5)

m({\alpha}_{q})=H^{1}_{q}+H^{1}_{d-q}\text{ for any }q=0,\ldots,d.

When all the singularities of
C
are quasi homogeneous, then there are additional information in the output, but for the computation of
m({\alpha}_{q})
one can use the data displayed in the first part of Table 1 and the formula (
2.5
).

The interested reader can find alternative codes for the computation of Alexander polynomials obtained by Morihiko Saito in
[
36
,
37
]
.

The second approach
is to use the following formula for the multiplicity
m({\alpha})
of
{\alpha}
as a root of the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{C}(t)
, namely

(2.6)

m({\alpha})=\dim H^{1}(U,{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}),

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
is the rank one local system whose monodromy around each irreducible component of
C
is multiplication by
{\alpha}
,
see
[
12
, Proposition 5.4]
. This approach is particularly useful when
the curve
C
is the union of some members in a pencil of curves, as we explain in the next section.

We end this section with the following easy result.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
C:f=0
be an arrangement of
d
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, without points of multiplicity
\geq d-1
. If
d=\deg(f)\geq 5
and
U=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus C
, then

\chi(U)\geq d-4\geq 1.

Proof.

Since
C
has no points of multiplicity
\geq d-1
, one has
r={\rm mdr}(f)\geq 2
(regarding
r
, see discussion below Definition
4.1
).
Furthermore, the singular points of
C
are quasi homogeneous, so we have

\mu(C)=\tau(C)\leq\tau(d,r)_{max}\leq\tau(d,2)_{max}=d^{2}-4d+7,

and this follows from
[
20
]
. Hence we have

\chi(U)=(d-1)(d-2)+1-\mu(C)\geq(d-1)(d-2)+1-(d^{2}-4d+7)=d-4.

∎

Note that the arrangements of
d
-lines having points of multiplicity
\geq d-1
or such that
d<5
are easily described, see for instance
[
12
, Cor. 8.1 and Cor. 8.2]
, and their monodromy is well known.

3.
Pencils of curves and Alexander polynomials

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in S_{m}
be two linearly independent homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
, consider the associated pencil

{\mathcal{P}}:sg_{1}+tg_{2}=0,

and assume that the generic member of
{\mathcal{P}}
is irreducible.
If the curve
C
is a
pencil-type arrangement associated with
\ell\geq 3
(reduced or non-reduced) members
of
{\mathcal{P}}
, then we have a regular mapping

(3.1)

\theta:U\to T,\ \ \theta(x,y,z)=(g_{1}(x,y,z):g_{2}(x,y,z)),

where
T
is obtained from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
by deleting the
\ell
points corresponding to the
\ell
members of
{\mathcal{P}}
used to construct
C
. Note that one has

\chi(T)=2-\ell.

We recall the following key result, see
[
12
, Theorem 6.9, Corollary 6.3 and Theorem 6.10]
. For all the details we refer to
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a rank one local system on
U
and let
\mathbb{F}
be the generic fiber of
\theta
. If
{\mathcal{L}}|_{\mathbb{F}}=\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}}
is the trivial local system,
then for any rank one local system
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
on
T
one has

\dim H^{1}(U,{\mathcal{L}}\otimes\theta^{*}{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})\geq-\chi(T)=\ell-2\geq 1.

The first inequality is strict when
{\mathcal{L}}
is not the pull-back under
\theta
of a rank one local system on
T
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Any root of unity
{\alpha}
of order
\ell
is a root for the Alexander polynomial of a reduced pencil-type arrangement associated with
\ell
members of
{\mathcal{P}}
and moreover
m({\alpha})\geq\ell-2
.

Proof.

It is enough to take in Theorem
3.1

{\mathcal{L}}=\mathbb{C}_{U}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
the rank one local system on
T
with monodromy about each deleted point a primitive root of unity of order
\ell
.
∎

When
{\mathcal{P}}
is a Halphen pencil of index
k
, then the map
\theta
has a multiple fiber, namely
F_{0}=\theta^{-1}(1:0)
, of order
k
. Then the following result show the existence of roots of Alexander polynomial of higher order and higher multiplicity for the associated reduced pencil-type arrangements.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a Halphen pencil of index
k
and let
C
be a reduced pencil-type arrangement associated with
\ell
members of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Then the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{C}(t)
has, among its roots, any primitive root of unity
{\alpha}
of order
k\ell
and its multiplicity satisfies
m({\alpha})\geq\ell-1.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.1
in the following way. Let
{\alpha}
be any primitive root of unity of order
k\ell
and consider the rank one local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\prime}
on
T^{\prime}=T\setminus\{(1:0)\}
whose monodromies are as follows:

(1)

About each of the
\ell
points corresponding the the member of

{\mathcal{P}}
which are in
C
the monodromy is multiplication by
{\alpha}
.

(2)

About the point
(1:0)
the monodromy is
{\beta}={\alpha}^{-\ell}
.

Since the product of all these monodromies is equal to
1
, this local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\prime}
is well-defined. Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{\prime}=\theta^{*}({\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\prime})
be the pull-back of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\prime}
under the obvious restriction of
\theta
to
U^{\prime}=\theta^{-1}(T^{\prime})
. Each irreducible component
C^{\prime}
of the multiple fiber

F_{0}
has a multiplicity of the form
ka
for some integer
a\geq 1
.
It follows that the monodromy of the local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{\prime}
about
C^{\prime}
is

{\beta}^{ka}={\alpha}^{-\ell ka}=1.

It follows that the local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{\prime}
can be extended over the fiber
F_{0}
, that is, there is a local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
on
U
whose restriction to
U^{\prime}
is exactly
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{\prime}
. It is clear that this local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
satisfies the equality (
2.6
). On the other hand,
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
is obviously not the pull-back of a rank one local system on
T
. The claims then follow from Theorem
3.1
, where we take
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}_{T}
. The property
{\mathcal{L}}|_{\mathbb{F}}=\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{F}}
holds, since the generic fiber of
\theta
is the same as the generic fiber of
\theta^{\prime}=\theta|U^{\prime}
.

∎

We also have the following result on the simplest non-reduced pencil-type arrangements.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a Halphen pencil of index
k
given by

{\mathcal{P}}:sg_{1}+tg_{2}=0,
with
g_{2}=h^{k}
and
h
a polynomial without multiple factors.
Let
C
be a non-reduced pencil-type arrangement associated with
\ell-1\geq 2
reduced members of
{\mathcal{P}}
and the reduced curve
h=0
. Then the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{C}(t)
has, among its roots, any root of unity
{\alpha}
of order
n=k(\ell-1)+1
and its multiplicity satisfies
m({\alpha})\geq\ell-2.

Proof.

Let
{\alpha}
be any root of unity of order
n

(1)

4.
Free curves and pencils of conics

In this section we recall first the definition of free and nearly free curves.
In the sequel, many of the constructed curves have not only intresting Alexander polynomials, but turn out to be either free or nearly free.
Let
C:f=0
be a reduced curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
of degree
d
defined by
f\in S
. Denote by
M(f):=S/J_{f}
the Milnor algebra.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
C
is
m
-syzygy when
M(f)
has the following minimal graded free resolution

0\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m-2}S(-e_{i})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}S(1-d-d_{i})\rightarrow S^{3}(1-d)\rightarrow S\rightarrow M(F)\rightarrow 0

with
e_{1}\leq e_{2}\leq...\leq e_{m-2}
and
1\leq d_{1}\leq...\leq d_{m}
.

In the setting of the above definition, we define the minimal degree of the Jacobian relations among the partial derivatives of
f
, namely

{\rm mdr}(f):=d_{1}.

Definition 4.2
.

We say that

•

C
is free if and only if
m=2
and
d_{1}+d_{2}=d-1
.

•

C
is nearly-free if and only if
m=3
,
d_{1}+d_{2}=d
,
d_{2}=d_{3}
.

In both cases, we call the pair
(d_{1},d_{2})
the exponents of the curve
C
.
Finally, for a reduced curve

let us denote by

the total Tjurina number of

and by

the total Milnor number of

are quasi homogeneous, then

, and it follows from
[
35
]
.
There are characterization of free and nearly free curves in terms of
the total Tjurina number – see
[
11
,
20
]
for all necessary details.

In the remaining of this section we apply Theorem
3.1
to curves coming from pencils of conics.

{\mathcal{P}}:tq_{1}+sq_{2}=0

With the above notation, the following 5 cases occur for a pencil of conics

Here are some equations for each type of pencil of conics listed above, see

{\rm Type\,I}:q_{1}=x^{2}-y^{2},q_{2}=y^{2}-z^{2},

where the indices

Remark 5.2

Let

generated by

. As an example, the map

in each of these four

given by

W=\{(\lambda,[x:y:z])\in X\ |\ f(\lambda)(x,y,z)\neq 0\}.

It is easy to check, using Proposition
5.1
(2), that for any singular point

p(\lambda)\in\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)
the singularity
(\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda),p(\lambda))
gives rise to a
\mu
-constant family of singularities with
\lambda
varying in
\Lambda
.
It is known that a
\mu
-constant family of isolated plane curve singularities is in fact
\mu^{*}
-constant, see for instance
[
8
, Remark (1.2.15)]
,
and hence it gives rise to a Whitney regular stratification,
[
8
, Theorem (1.2.13)]
. It follows that the 2-dimensional strata
Z_{j}
are Whitney regular over the 1-dimensional strata, and hence the stratification of
X
described above is Whitney regular. Since the projection
{\rm pr}_{1}
is clearly proper and submersive when restricted to any stratum, we get a
topologically trivial family via Thom’s First Isotopy Lemma, see for instance
[
8
, Theorem (1.3.5)]
.
It follows that the topological type of the pair
(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda))
does not depend on
\lambda\in\Lambda
, and hence the same applies to the complement

U(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)
.
Recall the definition of
{\alpha}_{q}
from (
2.2
). The multiplicity
m({\alpha}_{q})
of the root
{\alpha}_{q}
of the Alexander polynomial

\Delta^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)}(t)

can be computed using the formula (
2.6
), with
{\alpha}={\alpha}_{q}
and the local system
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}
denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}({\lambda})
to stress the dependence on
{\lambda}
.
It follows that the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)}(t)

is constant when
\lambda
varies in
\Lambda
. Indeed, for any two values
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\Lambda
, there is a stratified homeomorphism

\phi:(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda))\to(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda^{\prime})),

which implies that the homeomorphism obtained by restriction

\phi:U(\lambda)\to U(\lambda^{\prime})

preserves the rank one local systems used to compute the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of the monodromy
h
, namely

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}(\lambda)=\phi^{*}({\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}(\lambda^{\prime})).

Since the cohomology groups
H^{1}(U(\lambda),{\mathcal{L}}_{{\alpha}}(\lambda))
behave well with respect to pull-back and homotopy equivalence, see
for instance
[
9
, Remark 2.5.12]
, our claim that the Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{12}(\lambda)}(t)

is constant follows.

Then we can take, for instance,
\lambda=2
, and the corresponding Alexander polynomial
\Delta^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{12}(2)}(t)
is obtained using the code
monof3(q1,q2)
with
q1=3
and
q2=24
, since this curve is free by Remark
5.3
.
∎

6.
The degenerations of the family of Hesse arrangements of conics

We have seen in the previous section that there are 7 special values for

\lambda
which must be excluded in Theorem
5.4
. As explained briefly in the proof of Proposition
5.1
, for each of these values of
\lambda
, the defining equation
f(\lambda)=0
has 3 linear factors which occurs with exponents 2, hence
f(\lambda)=0
is in fact a multiarrangement
{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda)
formed by 9 smooth conics and 3 double lines.
We denote in this section by
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
the non-reduced pencil type conic-line arrangement obtained from
{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda)
by replacing the 3 double lines by the corresponding reduced lines, and by
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
the reduced pencil-type subarrangement of
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
consisting just of the 9 conics.
These new arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
can be regarded as degenerations of the Hesse arrangement of conics
\mathcal{C}_{12}
and they have very interesting properties themselves,
as the following results show.

Theorem 6.1
.

The degenerations
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\{-2,-2\omega,-2\omega^{2},0,1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
are free conic-line arrangements with exponents
(7,13)
.
Moreover, the degenerations
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
are free conic arrangements with exponents
(7,10)
.

Proof.

To fix our ideas, consider the case
\lambda=0
. If we set
\lambda=0
in the polynomial
f(\lambda)
, we get

{\mathcal{C}}(0):f(0)=0
, where
f(0)(x,y,z)=x^{2}y^{2}z^{2}\cdot g(x,y,z)
with

g=s_{1}(0)s_{3}(0)s_{4}(0).

Indeed, in this case
s_{2}(0)=-x^{2}y^{2}z^{2}
.
It follows that one has

{\mathcal{A}}(0):xyz\cdot g(x,y,z)=0\text{ and }{\mathcal{B}}(0):g(x,y,z)=0.

The claim about the freeness of the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
follows using
a direct computation via
Singular
. One can also check that the arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
is neither free, nor nearly free. The remaining 6 special values of
\lambda
can be treated exactly in the same way.
∎

It turns out that the new conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)
and
{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
have interesting Alexander polynomials. As a sample of this claim, we have the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

The degeneration
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
has 9 ordinary singularities of multiplicity 7 and 12 nodes
A_{1}
. Moreover

\tau({\mathcal{A}}(0))=309<336=\mu({\mathcal{A}}(0))

and its Alexander polynomial is given by

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(0)}(t)=(t-1)^{9}(t^{7}-1)^{2}.

The degeneration
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
has 9 ordinary singularities of multiplicity 6 and 9 nodes
A_{1}
. Moreover

\tau({\mathcal{B}}(0))=216<234=\mu({\mathcal{B}}(0))

and its Alexander polynomial is given by

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(0)}(t)=(t-1)^{7}(t^{3}-1)(t^{3}+1)^{2}.

In particular,
\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(0)}(t)
(resp.
\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(0)}(t))
has as roots some roots of unity of order
7
(resp.
6
).

Proof.

The Alexander polynomial for the free conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
can be computed using the code
monof3(q1,q2)
, with
q1=3
and
q2=\deg{\mathcal{A}}(0)=21
. The code
mono3\_wh()
takes longer time, but also gives the value
\mu({\mathcal{A}}(0))=336
, which can be obtained also by looking at the singularities of
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
. The 9 ordinary singularities of multiplicity 7 are exactly the points
p_{j}(0)
for
j=1,\ldots,12
from (
5.2
) and the 12 nodes
A_{1}
are exactly the points in (
5.3
).
The value
\tau({\mathcal{A}}(0))
comes from the freeness property in Theorem
6.1
using
[
20
]
, or from the code
mono3\_wh()
.

All the claims for the non free conic arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
follow by using the code

mono3\_wh()
. The 9 ordinary singularities of multiplicity 6 are exactly the points
p_{j}(0)
for
j=1,\ldots,9
from (
5.2
) and the 9 nodes
A_{1}
are exactly the points in (
5.3
) except the points
(1:0:0),(0:1:0)
and
(0:0:1)
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The
existence
of roots
{\alpha}
of order 7 and resp. 6 in this case can be seen using our approach with rank one local systems. Consider first the conic arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(0)
, which is the union of 3 reduced members in the pencil
{\mathcal{P}}(0)
from (
5.4
). It follows from
[
19

that one member of this pencil is a sextic with 9 cusps, and hence the generic member is irreducible.
This pencil has a double fiber

3.3
with

.
For the conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(0)

.

Theorem 6.4
.

The degeneration
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
has three singularities of multiplicity 11 and nine nodes
A_{1}
. Moreover

\tau({\mathcal{A}}(1))=309<363=\mu({\mathcal{A}}(1))

and its Alexander polynomial is given by

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(1)}(t)=(t-1)^{9}(t^{7}-1)^{2}.

The degeneration
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
of the Hesse arrangement of conics
has three singularities of multiplicity 9 and nine nodes
A_{1}
. Moreover

\tau({\mathcal{B}}(1))=216<255=\mu({\mathcal{B}}(1))

and its Alexander polynomial is given by

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(1)}(t)=(t-1)^{7}(t^{3}-1)(t^{3}+1)^{2}.

In particular,
\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}(1)}(t)
(resp.
\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}(1)}(t))
has as roots some roots of unity of order
7
(resp.
6
).

Proof.

If we set
\lambda=1
in the polynomial
f(\lambda)
, since

s_{1}(1)=(x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3}-3xyz)^{2},

we get

f(1)(x,y,z)=(x+y+z)^{2}(x+\omega y+\omega^{2}z)^{2}(x+\omega^{2}y+\omega z)^{2}\cdot h(x,y,z)

with

It follows that one has

{\mathcal{A}}(1):(x+y+z)(x+\omega y+\omega^{2}z)(x+\omega^{2}y+\omega z)h(x,y,z)=0\text{ and }{\mathcal{B}}(1):h(x,y,z)=0.

The new features of this case are the following. First of all, when we set
\lambda=1
, the 9 points in (
5.2
) collapse at 3 points, namely

a=(1:1:1)=p_{1}(1)=p_{2}(1)=p_{3}(1),

Remark
5.2
.
Next, three conics degenerate to double lines, namely

f_{1}(1)=\ell_{1}^{2}\text{ where }\ell_{1}=x+\omega y+\omega^{2}z,\ \ f_{2}(1)=\ell_{2}^{2}\text{ where }\ell_{2}=x+\omega^{2}y+\omega z,

for

Remark 6.6
.

All the singularities of a line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are both weighted homogeneous and
ordinary multiple points
. In fact, these singularities are
homogeneous, which implies that they are ordinary multiple points
.
The conic-line arrangements constructed in
[
14
]
have singularities which are
not ordinary multiple points
.
On the other hand, the conic-line arrangements constructed from conic pencils of Type I in Propositions
4.5
and
4.6
have singularities which are
homogeneous, and hence ordinary multiple points
. And the conic-line arrangements in Theorems
5.4
and
6.2
have singularities which are

ordinary multiple points, but some of them not weighted homogeneous
.

Finally, the conic arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
and the conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(1)

have some singularities which are not topologically equivalent to ordinary multiple points. One way to see this using Alexander polynomials is as follows. Note that in the case of the conic arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
, which has three singular points of multiplicity
9
,
the Alexander polynomial has roots of order
6
. The local Alexander polynomial of an ordinary point of multiplicity
9
has only roots of order
9
, as shown in Remark
2.2
. This property is preserved for a singularity which is topologically equivalent to an ordinary point of multiplicity
9
. Since the other singularities of
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
are nodes
A_{1}
,
with trivial local Alexander polynomial, our claim follows from Theorem
2.1
, (2). A similar approach proves the claim for the conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
, which has three singular points of multiplicity 11.

Remark 6.7
.

The conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
can be called the
Cremona-Hesse arrangement
for the following reason. Consider the projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with coordinates
(u:v:w)
, the Cremona birational morphism

\phi:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2},\ (u:v:w)\mapsto(vw:uw:uv),

and the Hesse line arrangement

(6.1)

{\mathcal{H}}:h(u,v,w)=uvw\left((u^{3}+v^{3}+w^{3})^{3}-27u^{3}v^{3}w^{3}\right)=0.

The Hesse line arrangement plays a key role in hyperplane arrangement theory, being the only known 4-net, see
[
21
]
. It has 12 nodes and 9 points of multiplicity 4, it is free with exponents
(4,7)
and the corresponding Alexander polynomial is

\Delta^{1}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(t)=(t-1)^{9}(t^{4}-1)^{2},

see
[
4
,
28
]
or
[
12
, Theorem 8.19]
.
Note that

h(u,v,w)=uvw(u+v+w)(u+\omega v+\omega^{2}w)(u+\omega^{2}v+\omega w)

(\omega u+v+w)(u+\omega v+w)(u+v+\omega w)

(\omega^{2}u+v+w)(u+\omega^{2}v+w)(u+v+\omega^{2}w).

Consider the scheme pull-back
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}({\mathcal{H}}):h^{\prime}(u,v,w)=0
,
where

h^{\prime}(u,v,w)=h(vw:uw:uv).

Then the scheme
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
is projectively equivalent to the scheme
{\mathcal{C}}(1):f(1)=0
considered above, that is the two defining ideals map to each other under some automorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. More precisely, one has

(6.2)

f(1)(x,y,z)=h^{\prime}(x+\omega y+\omega^{2}z,x+\omega^{2}y+\omega z,x+y+z).

It follows that the conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
is projectively equivalent to the conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}_{red}
, obtained from the scheme

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
by replacing the double lines by reduced lines. In particular, the conic arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}(1)
is projectively equivalent to a conic arrangement obtained by taking the 3 members of the pencil of sextics

s(x^{3}y^{3}+y^{3}z^{3}+x^{3}z^{3})-3tx^{2}y^{2}z^{2},

corresponding to
s=1
and
t^{3}=1
.

Remark 6.8
.

Both conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
and
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
are not reduced pencil-type arrangements, i.e. they are not unions of
reduced
members of a pencil. Since
\deg{\mathcal{A}}(0)=\deg{\mathcal{A}}(1)=21
, there are only two possibilities for
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
or
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
to be reduced pencil-type arrangements, namely the following.

(1)

{\mathcal{A}}(0)
or
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
are union of 7 reduced members in a pencil of cubics. Since
{\mathcal{A}}(0)
or
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
have only 3 lines, and no irreducible cubic as components, this case is impossible.

(2)

{\mathcal{A}}(0)
or
{\mathcal{A}}(1)
are unions of 3 reduced members in a pencil
of curves of degree 7. This would imply, using Theorem
3.1
, that the corresponding Alexander polynomials have as roots any cubic root of unity, which is not the case.
