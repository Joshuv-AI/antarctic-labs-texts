HIGHER DEGREE ERDŐS-GINZBURG-ZIV CONSTANTS
Yair Caro

Abstract




Abstract

We generalize the notion of Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv constants – along the same lines we generalized in earlier work the notion of Davenport constants – to a “higher degree” and obtain various lower and upper bounds. These bounds are sometimes exact as is the case for certain finite commutative rings of prime power cardinality. We also consider to what extent a theorem due independently to W.D. Gao and the first author that relates these two parameters extends to this higher degree setting. Two simple examples that capture the essence of these higher degree Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv constants are the following. 1) Let
\nu_{p}(m)
denote the
p-
adic valuation of the integer
m
. Suppose we have integers
t|{m\choose 2}
and
n=t+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
, then every sequence
S
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of length
|S|\geq n
contains a subsequence
S^{\prime}
of length
t
for which
\sum_{a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{m}}\in S^{\prime}}a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{m}}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and this is sharp. 2) Suppose
k=3^{\alpha}
for some integer
\alpha\geq 2
. Then every sequence
S
over
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
of length
|S|\geq k+6
contains a subsequence
S^{\prime}
of length
k
for which
\sum_{a_{h},a_{i},a_{j}\in S^{\prime}}a_{h}a_{i}a_{j}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. These examples illustrate that if a sequence of elements from a finite commutative ring is long enough, certain symmetric expressions (symmetric polynomials) have to vanish on the elements of a subsequence of prescribed length. The Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv Theorem is just the case where a sequence of length
2n–1
over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
contains a subsequence
S^{\prime}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
of length
n
that vanishes when substituted in the linear symmetric polynomial
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}.

Before giving the proof of this theorem, we need to remind the reader of some classical results.

For
m\geq 0

elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
m
is the sum of all distinct products of
m
distinct variables. Thus,
e_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=1,e_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n},e_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}
and, so on, until,
e_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}
. The
m

is
p_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{m}
.

We now state a historical set of relations between the elementary symmetric polynomials and the power sum polynomials. These 17th-century relations are independently due to Albert Girard and Isaac Newton and known as the Girard-Newton formulae (or sometimes Newton’s identities); for more about these identities, see
[
3
]
.

Theorem 2.11

(Girard-Newton formulae)
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
1\leq m\leq n
, we have

me_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{i-1}e_{m-i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

(2)

We may rewrite Equation
2
in a manner that is independent of the number of variables, that is, we may rewrite Equation
2
in the ring of symmetric functions as

me_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{i-1}e_{m-i}p_{i}.

(3)

One may use the Girard-Newton formulae to recursively express elementary symmetric polynomials in terms of power sums as follows.

e_{m}=(-1)^{m}\sum\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(-p_{i})^{j_{i}}}{j_{i}!i^{j_{i}}},

(4)

where the sum extends over all solutions to
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+mj_{m}=m
such that
j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\geq 0
. For example, we have
e_{1}=p_{1},~e_{2}=\frac{1}{2}p_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p_{2},~e_{3}=\frac{1}{6}p_{1}^{3}-\frac{1}{2}p_{1}p_{2}+\frac{1}{3}p_{3},~e_{4}=\frac{1}{24}p_{1}^{4}-\frac{1}{4}p_{1}^{2}p_{2}+\frac{1}{8}p_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}p_{1}p_{3}-\frac{1}{4}p_{4}
. If we multiply both sides of Equation
4
by
m!
, then we obtain on the right side integer coefficients.

Notice that for Equation
4
, each term in the sum of the right side is a product that contains at most
m
distinct power sum polynomials. For a fixed
m
we call a set
T
of power sum polynomials a
dominating set for
e_{m}
if each term in the sum contains at least one member of
T
. Let
t({m})
denote the size of the smallest dominating set. For
m=1
, the only dominating set is
\{p_{1}\}
, and so
t(1)=1
. For
m=2
, the only dominating set is
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
, and so
t(2)=2
. For
m=3
, any dominating set must contain both
p_{1}
and
p_{3}
and
\{p_{1},p_{3}\}
is a dominating set, and so
t(3)=2
. More generally, the following was determined previously.

Lemma 2.12

(
[
9
]
)
.

We have
t({m})=\frac{m+2}{2}
when
m
is even,
t({m})=\frac{m+1}{2}
when
m
is odd.

We are now able to give the proof of Theorem
2.10

Proof.

Let

2.1.1
Exact determination for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

In order to prove a generalization of the Caro-Gao Theorem for the cyclic group of order
2
, we need some preparatory lemmas. To prove these lemmas, we provide some necessary background.

Let
p
be a prime number and
n>1

p
-adic valuation of

, denoted
\nu_{p}(n)
, is the exponent of
p
in the canonical decomposition in prime numbers of

(and if
p
does not divide

, then
\nu_{p}(n)=0
). The base-
p
expansion of

is written as such,
n=a_{k}p^{k}+a_{k-1}p^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}p+a_{0}
.

Theorem 2.14

(E. Kummer, 1852
[
19
]
)
.

The
p-
adic valuation of the binomial coefficient
{n\choose m}
is equal to the number of ‘carry-overs’ when performing the addition in base
p
of
n-m
and
m
.

Recall Inequality
1
:
\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{n}},{m})\geq L({n},{m})
and the discussion that precedes it. In particular, we have
\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{2}},{m})\geq L({2},{m})
. Notice that any sequence
S
containing the element
0
has a subsequence
S
for which
e_{m}(S^{\prime})=0
. Any sequence
S
not containing
0
and of length
L({2},{m})
, we have
e_{m}(S)\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

Lemma 2.15
.

\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{2}},{m})=m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}.

Proof.

Let
m
be a positive integer with
2-
adic valuation
\nu_{2}(m)
.

Notice that any sequence
S
containing the element
0
has a subsequence
S
for which
e_{m}(S^{\prime})=0
. We will show that any sequence
S
not containing
0
and of length
m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)})
, that is
S=(1^{m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}})
we have
e_{m}(S)={m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}\choose m}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and sequences of shorter length do not have this property.

We begin by showing that for
m+1\leq j<m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
we have
{j\choose m}\not\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

We will use Kummer’s Theorem to compute
2
-adic valuation of this binomial coefficient
{j\choose m}
: it is equal to the number of ‘carry-overs’ when performing the addition in base
2
of
j-m
and
m
. Begin by noticing that base-2 expansion of
m
has
0^{\prime}
s to the right of position
\nu_{2}(m)
(and a
1
in position
\nu_{2}(m)
). That is, the base-
2
expansion of
m
ends with
0\cdot 2^{\nu_{2}(m)-1}+\cdots+0\cdot 2^{0}
. As
m+1\leq j<m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
, we have
1\leq j-m<2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
. Thus, the base-
2
expansion does not have a

to the left of position
\nu_{2}(m)
. Thus, when we add
m
and
j-m
in base-
2
, there are no ‘carry-overs’. By Kummer’s Theorem, the
2
-adic valuation of
{j\choose m}
is
0
. That is,
2
does not divide
{j\choose m}
for
m+1\leq j<m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
.

Now consider
j=m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
. In this case,
j-m=2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
. Thus, the base-
2
expansion of
j-m
is
1\cdot 2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
. As
m
has a
1
in position
\nu_{2}(m)
, when we add
m
and

in base-

there is at least one ‘carry-over’. Thus, some positive power of
2
divides
{m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}\choose m}
.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Suppose that
i\geq m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}
. Then for some

in the interval
[i-2^{\nu_{2}(m)},\ldots,i]
, we have

.

Proof.

As the interval has length

, there exists some integer
j
with a
0
in position
\nu_{2}(m)
of its base-
2
expansion and

. As
m
has a

in position
\nu_{2}(m)
,
j-m
has

in position
\nu_{2}(m)
of the base-
2
expansion. Thus, when we add
m
and
j-m
in base-

there is at least one ‘carry-over’. Thus, by Kummer’s Theorem, some positive power of

.
∎

.

For

\mathsf{EGZ}({t},{2},{m})=t+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}=t+\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{2}},{m})-m.

of length

i\geq t\geq m+1
.

There exists the subsequence

. As

, we have

.

2.

i\leq m-1

Then the number of

s that

contains is at least

equals

.

3.

m\leq i\leq m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}-1

Then the number of

contains is at least

m+2^{\nu_{2}(m)}\leq i\leq t-1

Then the number of

2.2
Prime power parameters yield a Caro-Gao-type theorem

In this subsection, we investigate the
\mathsf{EGZ}({t},{k},{m})
when the parameters are restricted to being powers of the same prime. Central to establishing our results here and later is the use of a tool from the polynomial method tool-kit, as follows.

Theorem 2.18

(U. Schauz
[
23
]
, D. Brink
[
6
]
)
.

Let
P_{1}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}),\ldots,P_{r}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]
be
polynomials, let
p
be a prime, let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
such that for each
i
,
the elements of
A_{i}
are pairwise incongruent modulo
p
, and put

A=\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
.
Let

Z_{A}=\{x\in A\mid P_{j}(x)\equiv 0\pmod{p^{v_{j}}}\ \forall 1\leq j\leq r\},\ \mathbf{z}_{A}=\#Z_{A}.

a) If
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(p^{v_{j}}-1)\deg(P_{j})<\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\#A_{i}-1\right)
, then
\mathbf{z}_{A}\neq 1
.

b) (
Boolean Case
) If
A=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(p^{v_{j}}-1)\deg(P_{j})<n
, then
\mathbf{z}_{A}\neq 1
.

When we apply Theorem
2.18
, most notably the Boolean Case, we will use a system of polynomials to encode the combinatorial problem in the zero set of this system. When applied, the Boolean Case will guarantee the existence of a non-zero boolean vector in the zero set. If the
j^{th}
-entry of this vector is
1
, then this will correspond to selecting the
j^{th}
-entry of a given sequence
S
whereas
0
corresponds to not selecting this entry. In the proofs that make use of this theorem, we will write it so that the first polynomial (or set of polynomials) that we give will ensure that we pick out a subsequence that sums to the zero-element and the second polynomial that we give will ensure that the subsequence that we pick out is of the desired length. We note that Theorem
2.18
has been generalized (see
[
10
]
), though the statement given here is sufficient for our purposes.

To warm the reader to the employment of this method, we begin with an example.

Example 2.19
.

We compute
\mathsf{EGZ}({16},{8},{2})
. First, note that
16\in S({8},{2})
. Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{30}
be integers.

Let

and

. We seek a particular type of member of the set of shared zeros of

2.18
. First note that the zero-vector is a shared zero of this polynomial system. Note that the hypothesis of Theorem

. Thus, there exists a shared zero other than the zero-vector. This boolean vector of length

must have precisely
16

1^{\prime}

.

We now show that

\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{p^{s}})=p^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(p^{\alpha_{i}}-1)+1\right)=p^{s}\mathsf{D}(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathsf{EGZ}({t},{\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{p^{s}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle t+p^{s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(p^{\alpha_{i}}-1)+1\right)-p^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t+p^{s}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(p^{\alpha_{i}}-1).

3.

Part 1 with
m=p^{s}
and Part 2 together imply the result.

∎

An immediate consequence is another Caro-Gao-type statement.

Corollary 2.22
.

Let
p
be a prime. Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}
be a
p-
group of rank
r
. Let
h=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\alpha_{i}}
. Suppose that
p^{h}>\sum_{j=1}^{r}p^{s}(p^{\alpha_{j}}-1)
. Then
\mathsf{EGZ}({p^{h}},{\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{p^{s}})=p^{h}+\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{p^{s}})-p^{s}
.

Proof.

It was shown in
[
9
]
that
\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{p^{s}})=p^{s}(\sum_{j=1}^{r}(p^{\alpha_{j}}-1)+1)
. Thus, by Theorem
2.21
.3, the result follows immediately.
∎

Theorem 2.23
.

Let
p
be a prime such that
p>m
. Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}
be a
p-
group of rank
r
. Let
h=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\alpha_{i}}
and suppose that
p^{h}>(\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor+1)(\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{\alpha_{i}}-1)
. Then
\mathsf{EGZ}({p^{h}},{\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}},{m})\leq p^{h}+(\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor+1)(\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{\alpha_{i}}-1)
.

Proof.

Let
\omega=p^{h}+(\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor+1)(\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{\alpha_{i}}-1)
.
Let
S=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\omega})
be a sequence over
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}
, where
g_{i}=(a^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,a^{(r)}_{i})
.

For each coordinate
j=1,\ldots,r
, we define a set of
\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor+1
linear polynomials as follows. Let
u\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor,m\}
. Then define

P_{j,u}=\sum_{i=1}^{\omega}(a^{(j)}_{i})^{u}x_{i}.

Define another linear polynomial

Q=\sum_{i=1}^{\omega}x_{i}.

We seek a particular type of member of the set of shared zeros of
P_{j,u}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{\alpha_{j}}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor,m\}
and
Q\equiv 0\pmod{p^{h}}
. We use the Boolean Case of Theorem
2.18
.

First note that the zero-vector is a shared zero of this polynomial system. Note that the hypothesis of Theorem
2.18
is satisfied, that is, we have

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle(p^{h}-1)deg(Q)+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{u\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor,m\}}(p^{\alpha_{j}}-1)deg(P_{j,u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{h}-1+(\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor+1)(\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{\alpha_{j}}-1).

Thus, there exists a shared zero other than the zero-vector. This boolean vector of length
\omega
must have precisely
p^{h}

1^{\prime}
s in it as
Q\equiv 0\pmod{p^{h}}
and by the hypothesis
2p^{h}>\omega
.

We now must show that these
1^{\prime}
s select a length-
p^{h}
subsequence
S^{\prime}
of the above list of such that
e_{m}(S^{\prime})
equals the zero-element in
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{1}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{p^{\alpha_{r}}}
.

We point out that
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor},p_{m}\}
is a minimum dominating set for the elementary symmetric polynomial
e_{m}
(see Section 4 of
[
9
]
). By the Newton-Girard formulae,
m!e_{m}
may be written as a sum of products, where each product has an integer coefficient and each product contains at least one of the elements from the above dominating set. Now as each of these polynomials in the dominating set evaluate to
0
on
S^{\prime}
, so does
m!e_{m}
. The hypothesis that
p>m
, implies that
\gcd(p,m!)=1
. This now implies that
e_{m}(S^{\prime})
equals the zero-element.

∎

\mathsf{EGZ}({t},{q},{q})=t+q^{2}-q=t+\mathsf{D}({\mathbb{Z}_{q}},{q})-q.
