On the preferred extensions of argumentation frameworks: bijections with naive sets
Mohammed Elaroussi, Lhouari Nourine, Mohammed Said Radjef, Simon Vilmin

Abstract
This paper deals with the problem of finding the preferred extensions of an argumentation framework by means of a bijection with the naive sets of another framework. First, we consider the case where an argumentation framework is naive-bijective: its naive sets and preferred extensions are equal. Recognizing naive-bijective argumentation frameworks is hard, but we show that it is tractable for frameworks with bounded in-degree. Next, we give a bijection between the preferred extensions of an argumentation framework being admissible-closed (the intersection of two admissible sets is admissible) and the naive sets of another framework on the same set of arguments. On the other hand, we prove that identifying admissible-closed argumentation frameworks is coNP -complete. At last, we introduce the notion of irreducible self-defending sets as those that are not the union of others. It turns out there exists a bijection between the preferred extensions of an argumentation framework and the naive sets of a framework on its irreducible self-defending sets. Consequently, the preferred extensions of argumentation frameworks with some lattice properties can be listed with polynomial delay and polynomial space. Keywords: argumentation framework, preferred extensions, naive sets, casting, irreducible self-defending sets.



1
Introduction

Abstract argumentation frameworks
[
Dun95
]
have become a major
research trend within the field of
artificial intelligence
[
BCD07
,
RS09
]
.
They also provide tools to study other areas such as decision support systems

[
AP09
]
, machine learning
[
CT16
]
, and agent
interaction in multi-agent systems
[
MP09
]
.
In particular, Dung
[
Dun95
]
shows how
n
-person games

[
VNM47
]
, logic default reasoning
[
Pol87
]
and logic
programming
[
Apt90
]
can be seen as instances of abstract argumentation.

Informally, an abstract argumentation framework consists of a set
of arguments together with a binary relation representing attacks between arguments.
The aim of an argument framework is to evaluate the acceptability (or the relevance) of
groups of arguments.
On this purpose, Dung
[
Dun95
]
introduces
admissible sets
.
An admissible set is a collection of arguments that are not attacking each other
(conflict-free set) and that are counterattacking all externals attackers
(self-defending set).
Then, Dung distinguishes several types of admissible sets called
extensions
, which
express different perspectives on arguments that are collectively acceptable.
Examples of extensions are preferred, stable, complete, or grounded extensions.
In particular, preferred extensions represent inclusion-wise maximal admissible sets, and
hence the largest (
w.r.t
set-inclusion) acceptable collections of arguments.
We illustrate these notions with the help of an example of Baroni et al.

[
BTV20
]
about vegetarianism.

Example 1
.

Let us consider the next four arguments:

(A)

“a vegetarian diet should be adopted because it is generally
healthier”;

(B)

“a vegetarian diet should not be adopted because it can promote
specific health problems”;

(C)

“we should not eat animals because animals have rights because they
are sentient beings”;

(D)

“animals cannot possess rights because they have no moral judgment”.

Following Baroni et al.
[
BTV20
]
, the arguments
A
and
B
attack
each other as well as the arguments
C
and
D
, and
C
attacks
B
.

The set
\{D,B\}
is admissible: neither
D
nor
B
attacks the other (or
itself) and
D
defends
B
against
C
.
In fact,
\{D,B\}
is preferred as adding either
A
or
C
would break the
conflict-free set property.
On the other hand,
\{B\}
is not admissible since there is no defense against
C
.
The set
\{C,D\}
is not admissible either as
C
attacks
D
.

Naturally, extensions are at the core of numerous decision and search
problems revolving around argumentation frameworks.
Unfortunately, most of these problems are hard to solve for several argumentation
extensions
[
DD17
,
GLMW20
,
KPW17
]
.
Nevertheless, these hardness results have motivated the search for tractable classes of
argumentation frameworks among which we quote the frameworks being without even cycles

[
DBC01
]
, symmetric
[
CMDM05
]
or
bipartite
[
Dun07
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the enumeration of the preferred extensions
of an argumentation framework, being a hard problem
[
DT96
]
.
Even though preferred extensions are only as informative as (maximal)
conflic-free sets within the context of logic-based frameworks

[
CW
,
Amg12
]
, their enumeration
remains one of the most general problem in argumentation frameworks.
Indeed, several recent works contributed to this task as well as the enumeration of other
extensions in argumentation frameworks
[
AGP19
,
CVG18
,
CDG+15
,
ENR21
]
.
Enumerating preferred extensions provides complete information concerning the
justification status of
arguments and allows to easily resolve other significant argumentation frameworks
problems.

In this context, our strategy draws inspiration from the work of Dunne
et al.

[
DDLW15
]
on realizability and recasting problems for argumentation
frameworks.
More precisely, we seek to express the set of preferred extensions of a given framework
using a bijective correspondence with the inclusion-wise maximal conflict-free
sets, called the naive sets, of another argumentation
framework.
For example, symmetric argumentation frameworks admit a bijection between their
naive sets and
preferred extensions
[
CMDM05
]
.
Since it is possible to list the naive sets of an argumentation framework with
polynomial delay and space
[
JYP88
]
, our approach can be used to
identify new classes of frameworks where the preferred extensions can be enumerated
efficiently.
On this purpose, we obtain the following contributions:

1. Naive-bijective argumentation frameworks.

(Section

3
).
An argumentation framework is
naive-bijective
if its naive sets are
equal
to its
preferred extensions.
For instance, symmetric argumentation frameworks are naive-bijective

[
CMDM05
]
. This is also the case of every logic-based argumentation framework, as
demonstrated for example by Amgoud
[
Amg12
]
.
Nevertheless, the class of naive-bijective argumentation frameworks is not very well
characterized.
We give an exact characterization of naive-bijective argumentation frameworks.
We deduce an algorithm to recognize this class of argumentation frameworks, running
in
polynomial time in frameworks with bounded in-degree.
In contrast, we prove that this recognition problem is
coNP
-complete in general.

2. Naive-recasting and admissible-closed argumentation frameworks.

(Section
4
).
An argumentation framework is
naive-recasting
if its preferred extensions are
the
naive sets of an other argumentation framework.
This class of argumentation framework has been introduced by Dunne
et al.

[
DDLW15
]
, and studied in a series of works
[
BDL+14
,
DSLW16
,
Püh20
]
.
In particular, the authors in
[
DDLW15
]
show that deciding
whether a given argumentation framework is naive-recasting is
coNP
-hard.
In our contribution, we introduce
admissible-closed
argumentation frameworks.
An argumentation framework belongs to this class if its admissible sets are
closed
under intersection.
Using implicational systems
[
ENR21
,
Wil17
]
, we
show that admissible-closed argumentation frameworks are naive-recasting.
Eventually, we prove that recognizing admissible-closed argumentation frameworks is a

coNP
-complete problem.

3. Irreducible self-defending sets.

(Section
5
).
Using the lattice structure of self-defending sets
[
Dun95
,
Grä11
]
we introduce irreducible self-defending sets.
A self-defending set is irreducible if it cannot be obtained as the union of other
self-defending sets.
We construct a bijection between the preferred extensions of an argumentation
framework
and the naive sets of another framework on its irreducible self-defending sets.
We deduce that when an argumentation framework meets some lattice properties, it is
possible to enumerate its preferred extensions with polynomial delay and polynomial
space.

Background

All the objects considered in this paper are finite. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set.
We denote by
2^{\mathcal{A}}
the powerset of
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.
For convenience, and mostly in examples and figures, we may write
S
as the
concatenation of its elements.
That is, if
S=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, we may write it
x_{1}\dots x_{n}
.
The
complement

\bar{S}
of
S
in
\mathcal{A}
is given by
\bar{S}=\mathcal{A}\setminus S
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{A}}
be a set system over
\mathcal{A}
.
The complementary set of
\mathcal{F}
(with respect to
\mathcal{A}
) is the set system
\bar{\mathcal{F}}

defined by
\bar{\mathcal{F}}=\{\bar{S}\mid S\in\mathcal{F}\}
.

Argumentation frameworks.

We follow
[
Dun95
]
.
An
argumentation framework
is
a pair
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
where
\mathcal{A}
is a set of arguments, and
\mathcal{R}

(attacks) is a binary relation on
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
.
A pair
(x,y
) in
\mathcal{R}
depicts an attack of the argument
x
against the argument
y
.
We can represent an argumentation framework
F
by a directed graph
G=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R})
, known as the
attack graph
of
F
.
Given a subset
S
of arguments, we denote by
S^{+}
the set of arguments attacked by

S
, i.e.
S^{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{A}\mid\exists y\in S,(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}\}
.
Likewise, the set of arguments that attack at least one argument of
S
is denoted by

S^{-}
, that is
S^{-}=\{x\in\mathcal{A}\mid\exists y\in S,(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}\}
.
In case
S
is a singleton
\{y\}
, we write
y^{-}
as a shortcut for
\{y\}^{-}
.
Finally, we put
\Gamma(S)=S^{+}\cup S^{-}
.

Given a set of arguments S of
\mathcal{A}
, an argument
x\in\mathcal{A}
is
acceptable
with respect to
S
if for each
y\in\mathcal{A}
,
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
implies that
(z,y)\in\mathcal{R}
for some
z
in
S
.
We say that
S
is
conflict-free
(an
independent set
in graph theory’s
terminology) if
S\cap S^{+}=\emptyset
.
We denote by
\mathrm{CF}(F)
the family of all conflict-free sets of
F
.
We call
\mathcal{S}(F)
the set of all self-attacking arguments, that is
\mathcal{S}(F)=\{x\in\mathcal{A}\mid(x,x)\in\mathcal{R}\}
.
The inclusion-wise maximal conflict-free subsets of
\mathcal{A}
are called
naive
sets

[
BDKT97
]
(
maximal independent set
in graph theoretic
terms).
The family of all naive sets of
F
is denoted by
\mathrm{NAIV}(F)
.
A set
S\subseteq\mathcal{A}
is
self-defending
if
S^{-}\subseteq S^{+}
.
The family of all self-defending sets of
F
is denoted by

\mathrm{SD}(F)
.
Using conflict-free and acceptable sets, Dung
[
Dun95
]

defines several extensions.
A conflict-free set
S\subseteq A
is
admissible
if

S^{-}\subseteq S^{+}
.
The family of all admissible sets of
F
is denoted by

\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
The inclusion-wise maximal admissible subsets of
\mathcal{A}
are called
preferred

extensions, and the family of all preferred extensions of
F
is
denoted by
\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.
We refer the reader to Baroni
et al.

[
BCG11
]

for more discussion on semantics.

Closure systems and implicational systems.

We use terminology from
[
Grä11
,
Wil17
]
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set system over
\mathcal{A}
.
The pair
\langle\mathcal{F},\subseteq\rangle

An

and

In

An

.

3
Naive-bijective argumentation frameworks

In this section, we show that checking whether the family of preferred extensions of a
given
argumentation framework is equal to the family of its naive sets is a

coNP
-complete problem.
In contrast, we give a polynomial time algorithm to analyze the bounded in-degree
argumentation frameworks.

Definition 1
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
We say that
F
is
naive-bijective
if
\mathrm{NAIV}(F)=\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.

In order to illustrate the above definition, we provide the following examples.

Example 5

(Running Example)
.

The argumentation framework
F
of Example
2
is naive-bijective.
Indeed, we have
\mathrm{NAIV}(F)=\mathrm{PREF}(F)=\{13,45,25\}
.

Example 6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2,3\}
and
\mathcal{R}=\{(1,2),(2,3)\}
.
The set
\{2\}
is naive, but not self-defending.
Thus, the argumentation framework
\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
is not naive-bijective.

Since naive sets can be enumerated with polynomial delay and space
[
JYP88
]
,
naive-bijective argumentation frameworks are particularly interesting for the enumeration
of preferred extensions.

The following lemma provides a simple case where an argumentation framework fails to be
naive-bijective.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework and let
\Sigma
be its
associated implicational system, obtained with Theorem
2.1
.
If
\emptyset^{\Sigma}\nsubseteq\mathcal{S}(F)
, then
F
is not naive-bijective.

Proof.

The result follows from the fact that an argument
x\in\emptyset^{\Sigma}\setminus\mathcal{S}(F)
belongs to at least one conflict-free set in
F
and not to any admissible
set in
F
.
∎

An argument
x
in
\emptyset^{\Sigma}
cannot appear in a self-defending set.
In particular, it is not contained in any admissible set.
Thus, adding the self-attack
(x,x)
to
\mathcal{R}
does not change
\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
Therefore, an argumentation framework where
\emptyset^{\Sigma}\nsubseteq\mathcal{S}(F)
can
be
transformed in polynomial time to an argumentation framework satisfying
\emptyset^{\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(F)
without changing the admissible and preferred extensions.
This is done by adding a self-attack to each argument
x
in

\emptyset^{\Sigma}
.

Example 7
.

Consider the argumentation framework of Example
6
.

F
can be transformed into a naive-bijective argumentation framework.
Indeed,
2\in\emptyset^{\Sigma}
as
1^{-}=\emptyset
, and
\{2\}
is the unique naive set
which is not a preferred extension of
F
.
Thus, adding the loop
(2,2)
to
\mathcal{R}
does not change
\mathrm{PREF}(F)
, while it removes

\{2\}
from
\mathrm{NAIV}(F)
.
As a result,
F
becomes naive-bijective.

The next theorem characterizes naive-bijective argumentation frameworks.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework and let
\Sigma
be its
associated implicational system, obtained with Theorem
2.1
.
Suppose that
\emptyset^{\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(F)
.
Then,
F
is not naive-bijective if and only if for some
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
such
that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
, there exists a conflict-free set
S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
satisfying:

•

S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
,

•

S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
for each
z\in y^{-}
.

Proof.

We begin with the if part.
Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
and
suppose that there exists
S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
such that

S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
and for all
z\in y^{-}
,
S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
.
Then, there exists a naive set
S^{\prime}
such that
S\cup\{x\}\subseteq S^{\prime}
and

S^{\prime}\cap y^{-}=\emptyset
, i.e. such that
y
attacks
x
and
y
is not attacked by

S^{\prime}
.
Thus,
S^{\prime}
is not self-defending and hence it is not preferred either.

We move to the only if part.
Assume that
F
is not naive-bijective.
Then, there exists a naive set
S^{\prime}
which is not self-defending.
More precisely, there exists
x\in S^{\prime}
and
y\notin S^{\prime}
such that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and

S^{\prime}\cap y^{-}=\emptyset
.
In particular, we have
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
.
Now, let
S=S^{\prime}\cap\Gamma(y^{-})
.
Since
S^{\prime}\cap y^{-}=\emptyset
, we have that
S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
.
We show that
S
satisfies the two conditions of the theorem:

•

S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
.
It follows from
S\cup\{x\}\subseteq S^{\prime}
and the fact that
S^{\prime}
is conflict-free.

•

S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
for each
z\in y^{-}
.
Since
S^{\prime}
is a naive set and
S^{\prime}\cap y^{-}=\emptyset
, for every
z\in y^{-}
we have
that
S^{\prime}\cup\{z\}\notin\mathrm{CF}(F)
.
Thus,
S^{\prime}
contains some
t\in\Gamma(z)
.
As
\Gamma(z)\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})
, we deduce that
t\in S^{\prime}\cap\Gamma(y^{-})=S

and hence that
S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
.

In other words, there exists
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
with
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
,
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
and
such that some
S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
satisfies
S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
and
S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
for each
z\in y^{-}
.
This concludes the proof of the theorem.

∎

Now we consider the problem of recognizing naive-bijective argumentation
frameworks.
Formally, the problem reads as follows:

Naive-bijective argumentation framework recognition (
NAF
)

Input:

An argumentation framework
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
.

Question:

Is
F
naive-bijective ?

On the basis of Theorem
3.1
, we deduce the following strategy to solve

NAF
.
For each
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
, check
whether there exists a
conflict-free set
S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
such that
S\cup\{x\}
is a conflict-free set of
F
and for all
z\in y^{-}
,
S\cap\Gamma(z)\neq\emptyset
. Return
“No”
if such a
S
exists and
“Yes”
otherwise.
Quite clearly, this algorithm has exponential-time complexity.
Yet, it runs in polynomial time if the in-degree of
F
is constant.

Proposition 1
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
If the in-degree of
F
is bounded by a constant, there exists a polynomial time
algorithm (in the size of
F
) to check whether
F
is naive-bijective .

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
.
By assumption,
y^{-}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\}
for some constant
k
.
For convenience, let
\mathcal{T}=\{S\subseteq\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}\mid S\cap\Gamma(z_{i})\neq\emptyset\text{ for each }z_{i}\in y^{-}\}
.
According to Theorem
3.1
,
F
is not naive-bijective if there
exists
some conflict-free set
S\in\mathcal{T}
such that
S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
.
Since every subset of a conflict-free set is conflict-free, it is sufficient to check the
existence of such a
S
in
\min_{\subseteq}(\mathcal{T})
, the collection of inclusion-wise
minimal elements of
\mathcal{T}
.
Let
S\in\min_{\subseteq}(\mathcal{T})
.
Since
|y^{-}|=k
and by definition of
\mathcal{T}
,
S
has size at most
k
.
Hence,
\min_{\subseteq}(\mathcal{T})
can be computed in polynomial time by checking all the
subsets of
\Gamma(y^{-})\setminus y^{-}
of size at most
k
.
Then, for each
S\in\min_{\subseteq}(\mathcal{T})
, testing whether
S\cup\{x\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
also requires polynomial time in the size of
F
.

Applying this algorithm for each

such that
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
and
(x,y)\notin\mathcal{R}
, we can verify the condition of Theorem

3.1
in polynomial time in the size of

We settle the general hardness of

in the subsequent theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

The problem
NAF
is
coNP
-complete.

Proof.

The proof is a reduction from 3-SAT.
Let

be a CNF formula, where
each clause

\{x_{1},...,x_{n},\neg x_{1},...,\neg x_{n}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=

\displaystyle\{x,y\}\cup\{C_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}

\displaystyle\cup\{x_{j},\neg x_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq n\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=

\displaystyle\{(y,x)\}\cup\{(C_{i},y),(y,C_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}

.

.

.
The second case is,
y\notin S
.

x\in S
and

is not admissible in
F
if and only if
S\cap\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}=\emptyset
.
By construction of

, it follows that
S
is not admissible if and only if for
each

C_{i}

1\leq i\leq m

1\leq j\leq n

\ell_{j}\in C_{i}

for each

4
Naive-recasting and admissible-closed argumentation frameworks

In this section, we study the class of argumentation frameworks whose preferred
extensions are the naive sets of another argumentation framework.
This class of argumentation frameworks is called naive-recasting and defined formally as
follows.

Definition 2
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework. We say that
F
is

naive-recasting
if there exists an argumentation framework
F^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\rangle
such that
\mathrm{PREF}(F)=\mathrm{NAIV}(F^{\prime})
.

Dunne
et al.

[
DDLW15
]
show that it is
coNP
-hard to
recognize
naive-recasting argumentation frameworks.
This problem is defined as follows:

Naive-recasting argumentation framework recognition (
NRAF
)

Input:

An argumentation framework
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
.

Question:

Is
F
naive-recasting?

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
We prove that
F
is naive-recasting in the restricted case where
\mathrm{ADM}(F)
is
closed under intersection.
First, we formally define this class of argumentation frameworks.

Definition 3
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
We say that
F
is
admissible-closed
if its admissible sets are closed
under
intersection.

Let us now provide some examples to illustrate the above definition.

Example 8

(Running Example)
.

The argumentation framework
F
of Example
2
is not admissible-closed.
Indeed,
45
and
25
are admissible but
45\cap 25=5
is not self-defending.

Example 9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2,3,4\}
and
\mathcal{R}=\{(1,3),(3,1),(1,2),(3,2),(3,4)\}
.
Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
.
We have
\mathrm{ADM}(F)=\{\emptyset,1,3,14\}
.
Since
1\cap 3=14\cap 3=\emptyset
and
1\cap 14=1
, we derive that
F
is
admissible-closed.

In the following lemma, we show that each admissible-closed argumentation framework has a
family of self-defending sets closed under union and intersection and includes all its
admissible sets.

Lemma 2
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an admissible-closed argumentation framework. Then,
there exists a family of self-defending sets
SD^{\prime}(F)\subseteq SD(F)
closed under
union and intersection such that
SD^{\prime}(F)\cap CF(F)
=
ADM(F)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
be the set system obtained from
\mathrm{ADM}(F)
after closing under
union,
that is
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)=\{\bigcup\mathcal{I}\mid\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathrm{ADM}(F)\}
.
Since self-defending sets are closed under union
[
Dun95
]

and

\mathrm{ADM}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{SD}(F)
,
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{SD}(F)
readily holds.
Then, the fact that
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)\cap\mathrm{CF}(F)=\mathrm{ADM}(F)
follows from
\mathrm{ADM}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{SD}(F)
and
\mathrm{SD}(F)\cap\mathrm{CF}(F)=\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.

It remains to prove that
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
is closed under intersection.
Let
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
.
We show that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\in\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
.
By construction of
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
,
S_{1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
and
S_{2}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}B_{j}
with
A_{i},B_{j}\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
for every
1\leq i\leq k

and
every
1\leq j\leq\ell
.
Therefore, we have:

S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right)\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}B_{j}\right)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}(A_{i}\cap B_{j})

However,
\mathrm{ADM}(F)
is closed under intersection by assumption.
Consequently,
A_{i}\cap B_{j}\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
for every
1\leq i\leq k
and every
1\leq j\leq\ell
.
We deduce that
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\in\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
as required.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Every admissible-closed argumentation framework is naive-recasting.

Proof.

Consider an admissible-closed argumentation framework
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
.
According to Lemma
2
, there is a collection of
self-defending
sets
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{SD}(F)
which completely
includes
\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
Since
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
is closed under union and intersection, it is possible to
represent the closure system
\langle\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F),\subseteq\rangle
by an
implicational system
\Sigma_{d}
with singleton premises
[
Wil17
]
.
Let
S^{*}
be the inclusion-wise maximal set of
\mathrm{SD}^{\prime}(F)
.
Consider now the argumentation framework
F^{\prime}=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\rangle
constructed
from

F
,
\Sigma_{d}
and
S^{*}
, by extending
\mathcal{R}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
with the following rules:

Rule 1:
if
x\in\mathcal{A}\setminus S^{*}
, then add
(x,x)
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

Rule 2:
if
x\rightarrow z\in\Sigma_{d}
and
y\in\Gamma(z)
,
then
add
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

To show that
F
is naive-recasting, it is sufficient to prove

\mathrm{NAIV}(F^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{ADM}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{CF}(F^{\prime})
.

We begin with the first inclusion.
Let
S\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F^{\prime})
.
Since
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
,
S\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
is clear.
By
Rule 1
,
S\subseteq S^{*}
.
We show that
S
is closed for
\Sigma_{d}
and hence that
S
is self-defending in
F
,
in
virtue of Lemma
2
.
Let
x\rightarrow z
be an implication of
\Sigma_{d}
such that
x\in S
.
Since
S
is conflict-free in
F^{\prime}
,
Rule 2
implies that
S\cap\Gamma(z)=\emptyset
.
Thus,
S\cup\{z\}
is also conflict-free in
F^{\prime}
.
As
S
is an inclusion-wise maximal conflict-free set of
F^{\prime}
, we deduce that
z\in S
.
Therefore,
S
is a closed set of
\Sigma_{d}
so that
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
holds.
Consequently, we have
S\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
as required.

We move to the second inclusion.
Let
S\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
We show that
S\in\mathrm{CF}(F^{\prime})
.
By assumption on
F
,
S\subseteq S^{*}
and
S
is a closed set of
\Sigma_{d}
.
Suppose for contradiction that
S
is not conflict-free in
F^{\prime}
.
Since
S
is conflict-free in
F
and
S\subseteq S^{*}
, it must be that
S

contains a
pair
(x,y)
of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
which has been added by
Rule 2
.
Consequently, there exists an implication
x\rightarrow z
in
\Sigma_{d}
such that
x\in S

and
y\in\Gamma(z)
.
However,
S
is a closed set of
\Sigma_{d}
by assumption on
F
and by construction
of
\Sigma_{d}
.
Therefore,
x\in S
implies
z\in S
so that
\{y,z\}\subseteq S
also holds.
As
y\in\Gamma(z)
in
F
, this contradicts
S\in\mathrm{CF}(F)
.
We deduce that
S\in\mathrm{CF}(F^{\prime})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

In the following result, we show that it is
coNP
-complete to recognize
admissible-closed argumentation frameworks.

Admissible-closed argumentation framework recognition (
ACAF
)

Input:

An argumentation framework
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
.

Question:

Is
F
admissible-closed?

Theorem 4.2
.

The problem
ACAF
is
coNP
-complete.

Proof.

The proof is a reduction from 3-SAT.
Let
C=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}
be a CNF formula, where
each clause
C_{i}
is a disjunction of three literals from
\{x_{1},...,x_{n},\neg x_{1},...,\neg x_{n}\}
.
Let
F
be the argumentation framework
\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
where (see also

):

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=

\displaystyle\{x,y,z_{1},z_{2}\}\cup\{C_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}

\displaystyle\cup\{x_{j},\neg x_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq n\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=

\displaystyle\{(x_{j},C_{i}),(C_{i},x_{j})\mid x_{j}\text{ occurs in }C_{i}\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(\neg x_{j},C_{i}),(C_{i},\neg x_{j})\mid\neg x_{j}\text{ occurs in }C_{i}\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(z_{1},y),(z_{2},y),(z_{1},z_{2}),(z_{2},z_{1}),(y,x)\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(C_{i},z_{1})\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(x_{j},\neg x_{j}),(\neg x_{j},x_{j})\mid 1\leq j\leq n\}

The construction of
F
can be done in polynomial time in the size of
C
.
Using the same reasoning as in Theorem

, we can show that

S

if and only if

is
satisfiable.
It remains to show that

does not

We prove the only if part using contrapositive.
Assume that there exists an admissible set
S
such that

Since

and

is admissible,
z_{2}\notin S
.
As
\{x,z_{2}\}
is also admissible, we have that
S\cap\{x,z_{2}\}
is not

x\in S\cap\{x,z_{2}\}
and
y^{-}\cap S=\emptyset
.
Hence,

is not admissible-closed.

We move to the if part.
Suppose that

does not exists.
By assumption, an admissible set which contains

also contains
z_{2}
.
Moreover,

and
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
so that no admissible set
in

contains

such

5
Irreducible self-defending sets and preferred extensions

In this section, we introduce the irreducible self-defending sets of an argumentation
framework.
They are those self-defending sets that cannot be obtained as the union of other
self-defending sets.
In lattice theoretic terms
[
Grä11
]
, they are the
join-irreducible elements of the lattice of self-defending sets.
Using these irreducible elements, we construct a new argumentation framework whose naive
sets are in a one-to-one correspondence with the preferred extensions of the input
framework.
We deduce an algorithm to list the preferred extensions of an argumentation framework
using its irreducible self-defending sets.
This algorithm has polynomial-delay and polynomial space when the irreducible
sets are given.
First, we formally define irreducible self-defending sets.

Definition 4
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
A self-defending set
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
with
S\neq\emptyset
is
irreducible

if
for every
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
,
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
implies
S=S_{1}
or
S=S_{2}
.
We denote by
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
the family of irreducible self-defending sets of
F
.

Remark 1
.

The
initial admissible sets

[
XC18
]
are irreducible
self-defending
sets.
A set of arguments is an
initial admissible set
if it is an
inclusion-wise
minimal
non-empty admissible set.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
The family
\mathrm{SD}(F)
can be generated from
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
by taking the union
of all possible subsets of
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
, that is
\mathrm{SD}(F)=\{\bigcup\mathcal{I}\mid\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathrm{IRR}(F)\}
, see
[
Grä11
]
.
Since a self-defending set in
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
cannot be obtained by union,

\mathrm{IRR}(F)
is in fact the most compact representation of
\mathrm{SD}(F)
by self-defending
sets.
For a given
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
, we put
\mathrm{IRR}(S)=\{S^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)\mid S^{\prime}\subseteq S\}
.
We have
S=\bigcup\mathrm{IRR}(S)
.

Remark 2
.

This representation by irreducible elements is common in
several fields of computer science where lattices are used (see e.g.

[
DF12
,
Gan10
,
Mar75
,
Wil17
]
).

Example 10

(Running Example)
.

In the argumentation framework
F
of Example
2
(see also Figure

2
), we have
\mathrm{IRR}(F)=\{4,45,2,25,13,124\}
.
For example,
245
is a self-defending set which is not irreducible.
It is obtained as the union of
25
and
24
.

Let
F=\langle A,\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
We construct an argumentation framework
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}=\langle\mathrm{IRR}(F),\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}\rangle
from which
\mathrm{PREF}(F)
can be easily recovered with
\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
.
We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
A self-defending set
S
is not admissible if and only if there exists
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathrm{IRR}(S)
such that
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is not an admissible set of
F
.

Proof.

The if part is clear.
Let
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
with
S\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
By definition,
S
contains some pair
(x,y)
such that
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
.
Since
S=\bigcup\mathrm{IRR}(S)
, there exist
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathrm{IRR}(S)
such that
x\in S_{1}
and
y\in S_{2}
(possibly
S_{1}=S_{2}
).
Thus,
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is a self-defending set which is not admissible,
concluding the proof.
∎

In the next lemma, we use Lemma
3
to devise an argumentation
framework
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
on
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
and show the relationship between

\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
and
\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
F=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle
be an argumentation framework.
Let
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}=\langle\mathrm{IRR}(F),\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}\rangle
be a new argumentation
framework
where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}=\{(S_{1},S_{2})\mid S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)\text{ and }S_{1}\cup S_{2}\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)\}
.
Then, the following equality holds and it is moreover a bijection between

\mathrm{PREF}(F)

and
\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
:

\mathrm{PREF}(F)=\left\{\bigcup\mathcal{I}\mid\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})\right\}

Proof.

First, we show the equality.
Let
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
be the argumentation framework defined in the lemma.
We begin with the
\subseteq
part of the equality.
Let
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
and consider
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
.
We show that
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
is a naive set of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Since
S
is admissible,
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
is a conflict-free extension of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
by
Lemma
3
and by construction of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
If
\mathrm{IRR}(S)=\mathrm{IRR}(F)
, then it is clearly the unique naive set of

F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Suppose now there exists
S^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)
such that
S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{IRR}(S)
and
consider
the set
S^{\prime\prime}=S^{\prime}\cup\bigcup\mathrm{IRR}(S)=S^{\prime}\cup S
.
Since the union of two self-defending set is self-defending,
S^{\prime\prime}
is a
self-defending
set of
F
.
Moreover, we have
S\subset S^{\prime\prime}
by definition of
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
.
Since
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
and
S\subset S^{\prime\prime}
, we have
S^{\prime\prime}\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
Thus, there exists a pair
(x,y)
in
\mathcal{R}
such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq S^{\prime\prime}
while

\{x,y\}\nsubseteq S
.
As
S
is admissible and
S^{\prime\prime}=S\cup S^{\prime}
we deduce that either
x\in S^{\prime}
or
y\in S^{\prime}
.
If both
x
and
y
belong to
S^{\prime}
, then
\mathrm{IRR}(S)\cup\{S^{\prime}\}
is not a
conflict-free extension of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Assume now that
x\in S^{\prime}
but
y\notin S^{\prime}
, without loss of generality.
Observe that we must have
y\in S
as otherwise
\{x,y\}\subseteq S^{\prime\prime}
would not
hold.
Because
S=\bigcup\mathrm{IRR}(S)
and
y\in S
, we deduce that there exists

T\in\mathrm{IRR}(S)
such that
y\in T
.
Hence,
\{x,y\}\subseteq S^{\prime}\cup T
which entails that
(T,S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}
and
\mathrm{IRR}(S)\cup\{S^{\prime}\}
is not a conflict-free extension of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Consequently, for every
S^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)\setminus\mathrm{IRR}(S)
, we have that
\mathrm{IRR}(S)\cup\{S^{\prime}\}
is not a conflict-free extension of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Together with the fact that
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
is conflict-free, we conclude that
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
is
a naive set of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
as expected.

We move to the
\supseteq
part.
Let
\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
.
Let
S=\bigcup\mathcal{I}
.
We show that
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.
Since
\mathrm{IRR}(F)
generates
\mathrm{SD}(F)
,
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
readily holds.
Assume for contradiction that
S\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
Then, there exists
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq S
.
Since
S=\bigcup\mathcal{I}
, there exists
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.
In particular, it must be that
(S_{1},S_{2})\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}
by construction of

F_{\mathrm{IRR}}

so that
\mathcal{I}
is not a conflict-free extension of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
, a contradiction.
Thus,
S\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
must hold.
Now let
S^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)
such that
S^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}
.
If such a
S^{\prime}
does not exist, the result
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
is clear.
Otherwise, we have two cases.
First,
\{S^{\prime}\}\notin\mathrm{CF}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
.
Then
S\cup S^{\prime}
is not admissible in
F
by construction of
F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
Second,
\{S^{\prime}\}\in\mathrm{CF}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
.
Since
\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
, we have that
S\cup\{S^{\prime}\}
contains some pair of

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{IRR}}
and hence that
S\cup S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
again by construction of

F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
.
In both cases, we deduce that
S\cup S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
As
S\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
, we deduce that
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
as expected.
This concludes the
\supseteq
part of the proof, and the equality of the lemma holds.

Now we show that this equality defines a bijection between
\mathrm{PREF}(F)
and

\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
.
As
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
is uniquely defined for every
S\in\mathrm{SD}(F)
, it is sufficient to
prove
that for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F_{\mathrm{IRR}})
,
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{IRR}(S)
for some
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.
Let
\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F)
and let
S=\bigcup\mathcal{I}
.
By previous discussion
S\in\mathrm{PREF}(F)
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathrm{IRR}(S)
by definition of
\mathrm{IRR}(S)
.
To prove that
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{IRR}(S)
, we show that
S^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}
implies
S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{IRR}(S)
, for every
S^{\prime}\in\mathrm{IRR}(F)
.
First, observe that if
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{IRR}(F)
, the implication is clear.
Now assume there exists some
S^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{IRR}(F)\setminus\mathcal{I}
.
We show that
S\cup S^{\prime}\neq S
.
Because
\mathcal{I}\in\mathrm{NAIV}(F)
and
S=\bigcup\mathcal{I}
, it follows from the construction of

F_{\mathrm{IRR}}
that
S\cup S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{ADM}(F)
.
As
S\in\mathrm{ADM}(F)
, we deduce that
S\cup S^{\prime}\neq S
must hold.
Thus,
S^{\prime}\notin\mathrm{IRR}(S)
for every
S^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}
.
We obtain
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{IRR}(S)
, concluding the proof.
∎

6
Conclusion and further works

In this paper we studied the problem of listing the preferred extensions of an
argumentation framework, being a hard problem
[
DT96
]
.
We identified cases where this task can be reduced to the enumeration of the naive sets
of a possibly different framework.
As this latter problem can be solved with polynomial delay and space

[
JYP88
]
, our contribution sheds the light on new properties making
the enumeration of preferred extensions tractable.
These properties pave the way for future research, which we will briefly discuss now.

Being naive-bijective is perhaps the most direct way to relate preferred extensions to
naive sets.
Even though this property is hard to recognize in general, our characterization provides
new insights into the structure of naive-bijective frameworks.
For future work it will be interesting to study this characterization in conjunction with
other properties from particular classes of argumentation frameworks.
By doing so, one may be able to identify further cases where the problem
NAF
can
be solved in polynomial time, as we did with bounded in-degree argumentation frameworks.

As for admissible-closed argumentation frameworks, we showed that they are
naive-recasting.
In fact, an admissible-closed framework
F
can even be recasted in a framework
F^{\prime}

on the same set of arguments.
This implies that
F^{\prime}
has polynomial size with respect to
F
.
Thus, being able to compute
F^{\prime}
in polynomial time yields another polynomial delay and
space algorithm to list the preferred extensions of
\mathcal{F}
.
As such, the open question of Section
4
about the complexity of
finding
F^{\prime}
is a particularly important part of continuing studies.
Identifying cases where
ACAF
can be solved in polynomial time, much as
NAF

for naive-bijective frameworks, is another direction for future investigation.

At last, irreducible self-defending sets are a new point of view on the structure of
argumentation frameworks.
The results from Section
5
show that a small portion of
self-defending sets are sufficient to efficiently compute the preferred extensions of a
given framework.
Thus, these results suggest to study argumentation frameworks not only from their graph
perspective, but also from the lattice and implicational point of view.
Specifically, finding properties of lattices and argumentation frameworks where
irreducible sets can be computed efficiently is an intriguing question for future work.
