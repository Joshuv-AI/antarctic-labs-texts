Abstract
. Given a poset P P , the set Γ ⁡ ( P ) \Gamma(P) of all Scott closed sets ordered by inclusion forms a complete lattice. A subcategory 𝐂 \mathbf{C} of 𝐏𝐨𝐬 d \mathbf{Pos}_{d} (the category of posets and Scott-continuous maps) is said to be Γ \Gamma -faithful if for any posets P P and Q Q in 𝐂 \mathbf{C} , Γ ⁡ ( P ) ≅ Γ ⁡ ( Q ) \Gamma(P)\cong\Gamma(Q) implies P ≅ Q P\cong Q . It is known that the category of all continuous dcpos and the category of bounded complete dcpos are Γ \Gamma -faithful, while 𝐏𝐨𝐬 d \mathbf{Pos}_{d} is not. Ho & Zhao (2009) asked whether the category 𝐃𝐂𝐏𝐎 \mathbf{DCPO} of dcpos is Γ \Gamma -faithful. In this paper, we answer this question in the negative by exhibiting a counterexample. To achieve this, we introduce a new subcategory of dcpos which is Γ \Gamma -faithful. This subcategory subsumes all currently known Γ \Gamma -faithful subcategories. With this new concept in mind, we construct the desired counterexample which relies heavily on Johnstone’s famous dcpo which is not sober in its Scott topology.

Introduction

The collection,
\Gamma(X)

of

If a lattice

{qu}

Which lattices have topological representations?

Seymour Papert was the first to characterize such lattices as those which are complete, distributive, and have a base consisting of irreducible elements,

]

We can also ask how much of the topological structure of a space is encoded in its closed set lattice. Following Wolfgang Thron,
[
12

, we say that two topological spaces

This then leads us to:

{qu}

Which classes

\forall X,Y\in\mathbf{C}.\;\Gamma(X)\cong\Gamma(Y)\implies X\cong Y\;?

Sober topological spaces are exactly those that can be fully reconstructed from their closed set lattices,

of sober spaces is a natural choice in answer to Question

at the same time, in other words,

The two questions above can also be asked in the context of a particular class of topological spaces. The ones we have in mind were introduced by Dana Scott,

We may now relativize our definitions to the context of Scott spaces. We say that a lattice

Although some work has been done on this problem,

2.
A positive result

2.1.
Irreducible sets

A nonempty subset
A
of a topological space
(X;\tau)
is called
irreducible
if whenever
A\subseteq B\cup C
for closed sets
B
and
C
,
A\subseteq B
or
A\subseteq C
follows. We say that
A
is
closed irreducible
if it is closed and irreducible. The set of all closed irreducible subsets of
X
is denoted by
\widehat{X}
. The following two facts about irreducible sets follow immediately from the definition:

•

If
A
is irreducible then so is its topological closure.

•

The direct image of an irreducible set under a continuous function is again irreducible.

In this paper we are exclusively interested in dcpos and the Scott topology on them. When we say “(closed) irreducible” in this context we always mean (closed) irreducible with respect to the Scott topology. It is a fact that every directed set in a dcpo is irreducible in this sense, while a set that is directed and closed is of course a principal ideal, that is, of the form
\mathord{\downarrow}x
. Peter Johnstone discovered in 1981,
[
8
]
, that a dcpo may have irreducible sets which are not directed, and closed irreducible sets which are not principal ideals; we will discuss his famous example in Section
3
and someone not familiar with it may want to have a look at it before reading on.

Given that we may view irreducible sets as a generalisation of directed sets, the following definition suggests itself:

{defi}

A dcpo
(D;\leq)
is called
strongly complete
if every irreducible subset of
D
has a supremum. In this case we also say that
D
is an
scpo
. A subset of an scpo is called
strongly closed
if it closed under the formation of suprema of irreducible subsets.

Note that despite this terminology, strongly complete partial orders are a long way from being complete in the sense of lattice theory.

\;\;\widehat{D}\cong\widehat{E}\iff\Gamma(D)\cong\Gamma(E)

{defi}

At the juncture, the curious reader must be wondering why we have not yet given an example of a dcpo which is not dominated. Now, as we showed, any dcpo whose Scott topology is sober is dominated and so a non-dominated dcpo, if it exists, must be non-sober. Peter Johnstone,

3.
Johnstone’s counterexample revisited

We denote with
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
the
ordered set
of natural numbers
augmented with a largest element
\infty
. When we write

we
mean the natural numbers as a set, that is,
discretely ordered
.
Johnstone’s counterexample
\mathcal{S}
, depicted in
Figure

, is based on the ordered
set
{\mathbb{N}}\times\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
, that is, a countable collection of infinite
chains. We call the chain
C_{m}:={\{m\}}\times\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
the

m
-th component
of
\mathcal{S}
. Similarly, for a fixed
element

we call the set
L_{n}:={\mathbb{N}}\times{\{n\}}
the

n
-th level
. So
(m,n)
is the unique element in the
m
-th
component on level
n
. We call the elements on the
\infty
-th level

limit points
, and refer to all the others as
finite
elements
.

The order on
\mathcal{S}
is given by the product order plus the
stipulation that the limit point
(m,\infty)
of the
m
-th component
be above every element of levels
1
to
m
. We express this formally as follows (where
m,m^{\prime},n,n^{\prime}
are natural numbers):

•

(m,n)<_{1}(m,n^{\prime})
if
n<n^{\prime}

•

(m,n)<_{2}(m,\infty)

•

(m,n)<_{3}(m^{\prime},\infty)
if
n\leq m^{\prime}
.

A simple case distinction shows that
{<}\mathrel{\mathord{:}\mathord{=}}{<_{1}}\cup{<_{2}}\cup{<_{3}}
is transitive and irreflexive, hence
{\leq}\mathrel{\mathord{:}\mathord{=}}({<}\cup{=})
is an order relation on
\mathcal{S}
.

Figure 1.
Johnstone’s non-sober
dcpo
\mathcal{S}
.

As the Hasse diagram makes clear, the only non-trivial (i.e., not containing their supremum) directed sets
of
\mathcal{S}
are the chains contained in a component, with supremum the
component’s limit point. Since every finite element
(m,n)
is also
below the limit points
(m^{\prime},\infty)
, where
n\leq m^{\prime}
, but not below
any other finite element outside its own component, we see that none
of them is
compact
in the sense of domain theory. It follows
that
\mathcal{S}
is highly non-algebraic, and indeed it couldn’t be algebraic
as algebraic dcpos are always sober spaces in their Scott topology.

Let us take a closer look at the Scott topology on
\mathcal{S}
. The two
defining conditions of Scott-closed sets manifest themselves in the
following properties:

•

Closed sets are lower sets: if the set contains a limit
point
(m,\infty)
, then it must contain the component
C_{m}
and all
levels
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
.

•

Closed sets are closed under the formation of limits: if the set
contains infinitely many elements of any one component then it must
contain the limit point of that component.

Taken together, we obtain the following principle (which plays a
crucial role in our construction as well):

(\dagger)

If a Scott-closed subset of
\mathcal{S}
contains
infinitely many limit points, then it equals
\mathcal{S}
.

This is because such a set contains infinitely many levels by the
first property of Scott-closed sets, which means that it contains
infinitely many elements of every component, and therefore contains
the limit points of all components by the second property. Applying
the first property again we see that it contains everything.

It is now easy to see that
\mathcal{S}
itself is an irreducible closed set:
If we cover
\mathcal{S}
with two closed subsets then at least one of them
must contain infinitely many limit points. By
(\dagger)
that set
then is already all of
\mathcal{S}
. Of course,
\mathcal{S}
is not the closure of a
singleton since there is no largest element, so we may conclude, as
Peter Johnstone did in
[
8
]
, that
\mathcal{S}
is not sober in
its Scott topology. In the terminology of the last section, it also
follows that
\mathcal{S}
is not strongly complete. In preparation for our own counterexample, let us prove that
\mathcal{S}
is the
only
closed subset which is not a point closure.

Proposition 17
.

The closed irreducible subsets of
\mathcal{S}
are
\mathcal{S}
itself and the
closures of singleton sets.

Proof 3.1
.

Assume that
A

\mathcal{S}
. We distinguish

Case 1:

contains no limit points. Let

The only non-trivial directed sets in

By Theorem

Recall that the unit

Final remarks

We have shown that the class of dominated dcpos is
\Gamma
-faithful and also seen that it is quite encompassing (Theorem
14
). One may wonder whether there are other natural classes of dcpos on which
\Gamma
is faithful or whether
\mathbf{domDCPO}
is in some sense “maximal.” Strictly speaking, the answer to this question is no, since the singleton class

is (trivially)

. What is needed, then, is a proper definition of “maximal” before any attempt can be made to establish its veracity.
