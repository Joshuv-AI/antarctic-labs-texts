1.
Introduction and main results

Study of spectral statistics of large random matrices are of significant interest. There is a host of results regarding the typical behavior of spectral observables of random matrices. However, results on atypical behaviors, such as large deviations, of spectral observables, e.g. extreme eigenvalues, the
esd
(empirical spectral distribution), are few and far between. Using an explicit formula for the joint density of the eigenvalues, the large deviations of the largest eigenvalue and of the
esd
of
goe
(Gaussian orthogonal ensemble) matrices were obtained in
[
11
,
12
]
. Beyond such exactly solvable models of random matrices, the first breakthrough was due to Bordenave and Caputo
[
18
]
, where they derived large deviations of the
esd
of Wigner matrices with stretched exponential tails. There they showed that the large deviation event is created by a relatively few large entries. This was later extended for the largest eigenvalue by Augeri
[
3
]
. Very recently, Guionnet and Husson
[
27
]
, using asymptotics of
spherical integrals
, derived the large deviations for the largest eigenvalue for Wigner matrices with entries possessing a
sharp sub-Gausian tail
(e.g. Rademacher distribution), and this has been extended to the case of entries with sub-Gaussian tails, see
[
5
]
.

The results mentioned thus far are in the context of
dense
random matrices. The goal of this article is to derive the large deviations of the largest eigenvalue of a class of
sparse
random matrices, namely, the adjacency matrices of Erdős-Rényi graphs. An Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
on
n
vertices is the random graph obtained by joining the edge between each pair of vertices with probability
p=p(n)\in(0,1)
, and independently of every other pair. Here we consider the case
p\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Before moving further let us mention that the typical behavior of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
, the spectral radius of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, is well understood: It follows from
[
30
]
and
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
that, with
d=np
,

\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+o(1)){d}&\mbox{ if }{d}\gg\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}},\\
(1+o(1))\max\{{d},\sqrt{\mathfrak{L}_{p}}\}&\mbox{ if }{d}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}},\\
(1+o(1))\sqrt{\mathfrak{L}_{p}}&\mbox{ if }{d}\ll\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\mbox{ and }\log n\gg\log(1/{d}),\end{array}\right.

almost surely (see Section
1.5
for the notational conventions used in this paper), where

\mathfrak{L}_{p}:=\frac{\log n}{\log\log n-\log{d}}.

The typical behaviors of the extreme eigenvalues have been further extended to the inhomogeneous setting by
[
13
,
14
]
.

Over the last decade or so there has been extensive research on understanding the large deviation phenomena in random graph models. Chatterjee and Varadhan
[
22
]
first successfully considered the problem of upper tail large deviations of triangle counts in dense
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, i.e.
p\asymp 1
. To tackle this problem,
[
22
]
introduced a general framework for large deviation principle that uses Szemerédi’s regularity lemma
[
36
]
and the theory of graph limits
[
19
,
32
,
33
]
. They expressed the large deviation rate function as the solution of a
mean-field variational problem
, and obtained the structure of the random graph conditioned on the large deviation event in the
replica symmetry region
. Later this was extended by Lubetzky and Zhao
[
34
]
for
nice graph parameters
, e.g. regular subgraph densities and the spectral radius. They also identified the phase boundary between the replica symmetry and symmetry breaking regimes.

In a breakthrough work Chatterjee and Dembo
[
21
]
proposed a new framework of nonlinear large deviations that can be applied to a host of problems, including, in particular, the upper tail large deviations of subgraph counts in sparse
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. Similar to the dense setting, here also the large deviation rate function is given by the solution of an appropriate mean-field variational problem, which was solved in
[
17
]
. The results of
[
21
]
have been extended and improved by
[
4
,
23
,
25
]
.

The recent work
[
23
]
, in addition to the large deviations of homomorphism densities, derives the large deviation of the upper tail of the spectral radius of an Erdős-Rényi graph where the rate function is again shown to be the solution of some mean-field variational problem. The solution to this variational problem was identified in
[
15
]
. These two results together imply that

(1.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}(\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))\geqslant(1+\delta){d})}{{d^{2}}\log(1/p)}=\min\left\{\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{2},\delta(1+\delta)\right\},\quad\text{ for }{n^{1/2}\ll d\ll n},

and
\delta>0
. On the other hand, in
[
16
]
, using a completely different approach, it has been shown that

(1.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}(\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p)\geqslant(1+\delta)\sqrt{\mathfrak{L}_{p}})}{\log n}=2\delta+\delta^{2},

for
\delta>0
and
d
such that

(1.3)

\log n\gg\log(1/{d})\quad\text{ and }\quad{d}\ll\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}.

In this paper we will derive large deviations of the spectral radius in the intermediate regime of sparsity, i.e. for
d
such that

(1.4)

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\ll{d}\leqslant n^{1/2-o(1)}.

Therefore,
the results of this paper together with
[
15
,
16
,
23
]
resolve the upper tail large deviations for the spectral radius of eigenvalues in the entire sparse regime, except for a couple of boundary cases
. The lower tail large deviations of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
for
p\ll 1
have been settled in
[
16
,
23
]
.

Informal summary of the main result

This paper shows that for
d
such that (
1.4
) holds and
{d}\gg n^{1/3}
the
\log
-probability of the upper tail of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
, upon ignoring smaller order terms, equals to that of
planting
a
clique
on
\lceil(1+\delta){d}\rceil
vertices, while if
{d}\ll n^{1/3}
and (
1.4
) hold then the same equals to that of the existence of a vertex with degree greater than
(1+\delta)^{2}n^{2}p^{2}
. See Theorem
1.1
for a precise statement.

Perhaps, at the very first glance, the transition of the large deviation behavior at
d\asymp n^{1/3}
may seem surprising. However, observe that the
\log
-large deviation probability should be at least as large as the maximum of the
\log
-probabilities of the two events described above (in Theorem
1.1
we show that this is also essentially the upper bound).
For
d\ll n^{1/3}
the probability of the second event dominates that of the first, and hence the transition.

It is instructive to note that the existence of a high degree vertex continues to be the
primary reason
for an atypical large value of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
in the regime (
1.3
) (see (
1.2
)), although the threshold on the degree of a vertex to be called it a high degree degree vertex needs to be changed appropriately in that regime. On the other hand, by (
1.1
), the large deviations probability of the upper tail event for
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
, in the regime
n^{1/2}\ll{d}\ll n
, equals, asymptotically, to that of planting either a clique or a
hub
of appropriate sizes. It is also worth noting that this second event ceases to be a viable option if
{d}\ll n^{1/2}
and therefore one does not encounter it in the regime covered by (
1.4
).

Large deviation probability and mean field approximation

As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
and some additional work we establish that in the entire regime (
1.4
) the
\log
-probability of the upper tail of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
is asymptotically approximated by the solution of the mean-field variational problem (see Theorem
1.8
(a)).
Prior to this work, the solution to the mean-field variational problem for the spectral radius
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, in the regime given by (
1.4
), was not known in the literature.

It is also worth adding that our approach of deriving the large deviation bound is somewhat reverse in nature compared to the other works in the area, e.g.
[
4
,
21
,
23
,
25
]
where the
\log
-probability is shown to be asymptotically equal to the solution of the mean-field variational problem, and the variational problem is solved separately in
[
15
,
35
]
.

Large deviations of homomorphism counts in cycle graphs

Finally, let us add that Theorem
1.5
of this article together with
[
8
]
show that in a certain regime of sparsity
the large deviation rate functions for the upper tail of subgraph counts and homomorphism counts for
C_{2t}
differ but the large deviation speeds of these two events remain the same
.
This difference in the rate function is probably due to lack of low complexity of the gradient of the homomorphism count function in the above mentioned regime of
{d}
. See Section
1.2
for further discussions.

The upper tail large deviations of homomorphism counts of
r
-regular graphs in the regime
p\ll n^{-1/r}
were not known previously. We believe that, with some additional efforts, Theorem
1.5
extended to cover the case of all
r
-regular graphs in the above mentioned regime.

1.1.
Main results

The following is the main result on the upper tail large deviations of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. Fix
\delta>0
.
Recall
d=np
. Let
d
be such that

(1.5)

{d\gg\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log d}{\log n}=\alpha\in[0,1/2)}.

(a)

If
\alpha\in[0,1/3)
then

(1.6)

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant(1+\delta){d}\right)}{{d^{2}}\log{d}}=(1+\delta)^{2}.

(b)

If
\alpha\in[1/3,1/2)
then

(1.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant(1+\delta){d}\right)}{{d^{2}}\log(1/p)}=\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{2}.

Remark 1.2
.

The reader may check that the negative of the
\log
-probability of having a vertex of degree
u^{2}d^{2}
in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, for any
u>0
, is approximately
u^{2}d^{2}\log d
, while that for having a clique with
ud
many vertices is roughly
\frac{1}{2}u^{2}d^{2}\log(1/p)
. Thus the preceding theorem shows that for
\alpha<1/3
the upper large deviation event is primarily due to the presence of a high degree vertex while in the other regime that is due to the presence of a large clique.

Let us further add that the proofs for the cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha>0
are different. In Section
2
we will prove Theorem
1.1
for
d
such that

(1.8)

{d\gg\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\log{d}\ll\log(1/p)}.

The reader can note that the condition
\log d\ll\log(1/p)
is equivalent to the condition
\alpha=0
. For the other case, i.e. when
\alpha>0
, we will prove a slightly wider regime than described in Theorem
1.1
. Namely, we will show in Section
3
that

(1.9)

-\log\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant(1+\delta){d}\right)=(1+o(1))\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta)^{2}{d^{2}}\log(1/p),(1+\delta)^{2}{d^{2}}\log{d}\right\},

for
{d}
such that

(1.10)

\log({d})\gtrsim\log n\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{d}\leqslant n^{1/2}(\log n)^{-\omega(1)}.

When
\alpha=0
Theorem
1.1
can be strengthened to derive the typical structure of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
conditioned on the atypical event that its largest eigenvalue is large. In particular, we obtain the following result. For ease in writing, let us introduce the following notation: For
\delta,\xi>0
and
\mathbb{G}_{n}\subset K_{n}
we set

{\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta):=\{\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant(1+\delta){d}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\rm UT}_{\Delta}(\xi):=\{\Delta(\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant\xi{d^{2}}\}.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
d
satisfy (
1.8
) and
\mathbb{G}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{G}(n,p)
.
Then for any
\delta>0
and
\chi\in(0,1)
,

(1.11)

\mathbb{P}\left({\rm UT}_{\Delta}((1+\delta)^{2}(1-\chi))\mid{\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta)\right)\to 1,\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Observe that Corollary
1.3
treats the sparsity regime (
1.8
) and shows that in that regime the upper tail large deviation event of the largest eigenvalue is (primarily) due to the presence of a very large degree.
We expect that an appropriate analogue of Corollary
1.3
should hold for the entire regime (
1.4
). See Section
1.3
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\alpha>0
hinges on understanding the upper tail large deviations of the homomorphism counts of even cycles in Erdős-Rényi graphs. First let us provide necessary definitions.

Definition 1.4

(Homomorphism counts and labelled copies)
.

Given graphs
H
and
{\sf G}
we write
{\rm Hom}(H,{\sf G})
to denote the number of homomorphisms of
H
into
{\sf G}
. That is,

(1.12)

{\rm Hom}(H,{\sf G}):=\sum_{\varphi:V(H)\mapsto V({\sf G})}\prod_{{\bm{e}}=(x,y)\in E(H)}a^{\sf G}_{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)},

where the sum runs over
all
maps
\varphi
from
V(H)
to
V({\sf G})
and
\{a^{\sf G}_{u,v}\}
are the entries of the adjacency matrix of
{\sf G}
.

There is a closely related notion to the homomorphism counts, known as the number of labelled copies of
H
in
{\sf G}
, which is defined as follows:

N(H,{\sf G}):=\sum_{\varphi:V(H)\hookrightarrow V({\sf G})}\prod_{{\bm{e}}=(x,y)\in E(H)}a^{\sf G}_{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)}.

Here the sum is over all
injective
maps.

Below is the result on the upper tail large deviations of homomorphism counts of
C_{2t}
in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, where for an integer
s\geqslant 3
we write
C_{s}
to denote the cycle graph of length
s
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix
t\geqslant 3
and let
{d}
be such that

(1.13)

{nd^{t}}\ll{d^{2t}}.

Then, for
\mathbb{G}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{G}(n,p)
,

(1.14)

\mathbb{E}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n})]=(1+o(1)){d^{2t}}.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\log d/\log n=\alpha

then, for any
\widehat{\delta}>0
,

(1.15)

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left({\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n})\geqslant(1+\widehat{\delta}){d^{2t}}\right)}{{d^{2}}\log(1/p)}=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\delta}^{1/t}\cdot\min\left\{2^{1-1/t}\cdot{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}},1\right\}.

The case
t=2
is excluded from Theorem
1.5
, as for
t=2
there is no
{d}
satisfying (
1.13
) such that
{d}\ll n^{1/2}
. Let us note that, similar to (
1.7
), one also sees a dichotomous large deviations behavior (
1.15
). Recall that previous works (cf.
[
4
,
23
,
24
]
) considered upper tail large deviations of

.

It can be noted that in the regime
\log{d}\gtrsim\log n
a large atypical value of the spectral radius produces the same for

. This observation will be used to prove Theorem

for

Theorems

and

will be extended below (cf. Section

1.2.
Connection to the naïve mean-field approximation

For a function
h:\{0,1\}^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and the uniform measure
\mu
on
{\sf C}_{N}:=\{0,1\}^{N}
, the
Gibbs variational principle
states that

(1.18)

Z_{h}:=\log\int e^{h}d\mu=\sup_{\nu}\left\{\int hd\nu-D_{KL}(\nu\|\mu)\right\},

where
D_{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
denotes the
Kulback-Leibler divergence
and the supremum in (
1.18
) is taken over all probability measures
\nu
on
{\sf C}_{N}
. If the supremum in (
1.18
) is replaced by product measures on
{\sf C}_{N}
, in statistical mechanics, the approximation is termed as the
naïve mean-field approximation
.
Over the past ten years there have been several works, in different settings, attempting to find sufficient conditions on
h(\cdot)
, e.g. appropriate
low-complexity
conditions on the (discrete) gradient of
h(\cdot)
, such that the mean-field approximation is asymptotically tight for the
log-partition function

Z_{h}
(see
[
4
,
7
,
9
,
21
,
25
,
37
]
).

A natural extension is to ask whether the mean-field approximation is asymptotically tight for
\mu_{p}(f\geqslant(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f])
, where
f:[0,1]^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is some ‘nice’ function,
\delta>0
, and
\mu_{p}
is the product of
N
i.i.d.
\dBer(p)
measures. A heuristic computation shows that if the mean-field approximation is believed to be hold then one should have that

(1.19)

\log\mathbb{P}(f\geqslant(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f])=-(1+o(1))\Psi_{p}(f,\delta),

where

(1.20)

\Psi_{p}(f,\delta):=\inf\left\{I_{p}({\bm{\xi}}):{\bm{\xi}}\in[0,1]^{N}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}[f]\geqslant(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f]\right\}.

I_{p}({\bm{\xi}}):=\sum_{\upalpha=1}^{N}I_{p}(\xi_{i}),\quad{\bm{\xi}}:=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{N}),\quad\text{and}\quad I_{p}(x):=x\log\frac{x}{p}+(1-x)\log\frac{1-x}{1-p}\text{ for }x\in[0,1],

with the convention
0\log 0=0
and the probability measure
\mu_{\bm{\xi}}:=\otimes_{\upalpha=1}^{N}\dBer(\xi_{\upalpha})
. Functions that are of interests and fit into this framework include
N({\sf H},\mathbb{G}_{n})
,
{\rm Hom}({\sf H},\mathbb{G}_{n})
, and
\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n})
for
{\sf H}\subset K_{n}
and
\mathbb{G}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{G}(n,p)
(set
N=\binom{n}{2}
and identify
\binom{n}{2}
possible edges of
\mathbb{G}_{n}
to
[N]
). As already mentioned above, the mean-field approximation is shown to be asymptotically tight for large deviations of homomorphism counts and of the largest eigenvalue in sparse
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
for various ranges of sparsity (cf.
[
4
,
21
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
). One may enquire if the same phenomenon continues to hold under the setting of this paper. The following result confirms that.

Theorem 1.8
.

Fix
\delta,\widehat{\delta}>0
,
\chi\in(0,1)
, and
t\geqslant 3
.

(a)

Let
{d}
be such that

\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\ll{d}\leqslant n^{1/2}(\log n)^{-\omega(1)}.

Then, for all large
n
,

(1.21)

\Psi_{p}(\lambda(\cdot),\delta(1-\chi))\leqslant-\log\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta))\leqslant(1+\chi)\Psi_{p}(\lambda(\cdot),\delta(1+\chi)).

(b)

Let
{d}
satisfy (
1.16
). Then, for all large
n
,

(1.22)

\Psi_{p}({\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\cdot),\widehat{\delta}(1-\chi))\leqslant-\log\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant(1+\chi)\Psi_{p}({\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\cdot),\widehat{\delta}(1+\chi)).

The reader may guess that the lower bounds in (
1.21
) and (
1.22
) are immediate from Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
. To prove the upper bound one needs to show that certain ‘error term’ is small, and for that one needs bounds on
\Var_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}(f(\mathbb{G}_{n}))
for
f(\cdot)=\lambda(\cdot)
and
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\cdot)
(see Lemma
5.1
). This error term arises as an indicator function needs to be approximated by an exponential function.

A different formulation of the mean-field variational problem

The variational problem (
1.20
) is slightly different than the one considered in
[
4
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. There they show that the
\log
-probability is approximated by

(1.23)

\Phi_{p}(f,\delta):=\inf\left\{I_{p}({\bm{\xi}}):{\bm{\xi}}\in[0,1]^{N}\text{ and }f({\bm{\xi}})\geqslant(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f]\right\},

for various ranges of sparsity. The variational problems (
1.20
) and (
1.23
) coincide with each other when
|f({\bm{\xi}})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}[f]|\ll\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f]
. Examples of such
f(\cdot)
include functions that are linear in each variable such as
f(\cdot)=N({\sf H},\cdot)
. Another well known criteria is the ‘low-complexity’ of
\nabla f(\cdot)
. However, in the absence of suitable low-complexity criteria, (
1.20
) and (
1.23
) may have a completely different behavior, and (
1.23
) may not even represent the correct large deviation behavior. To see this, we observe that from the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
it follows that for
f(\cdot)=\lambda(\cdot)
and
p
such that
{d}\gg 1
the first term in the
rhs
of (
1.9
) equals
(1+o(1))\Phi_{p}(f,\delta)\asymp{d^{2}}\log(1/p)
. If
\log{d}<\log n/3
then the second term in (
1.9
) is strictly smaller than the first term. Therefore, by Theorems
1.1
and
1.8
(a), in that regime
\Psi_{p}(f,\delta)
is strictly smaller than
\Phi_{p}(f,\delta)
, and moreover in the regime
\log{d}\ll\log n
they are of different orders of magnitude.

Emergence of non-planted optimizers

It has been shown in
[
15
,
17
,
35
]
that any minimizer
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}
of
\Phi_{p}(f,\delta)
, for
f(\cdot)=\lambda(\cdot)
and
{\rm Hom}({\sf H},\cdot)
satisfy
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}\in\{p,1\}^{N}
. Therefore, such a
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}
is also a minimizer of the variational problem

(1.24)

\widehat{\Phi}_{p}(f,\delta):=\inf\left\{e({\sf G})\log(1/p):{\sf G}\subset K_{n}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}[f]\geqslant(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f]\right\},

where
\mathbb{E}_{{\sf G}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f({\bm{\zeta}})|\zeta_{\upalpha}=1;\upalpha\in E({\sf G})]
. To see this for any
{\bm{\xi}}\in\{p,1\}^{N}
one associates a graph
{\sf G}({\bm{\xi}})\subset K_{n}
by letting
E({\sf G}({\bm{\xi}}))=\{\upalpha\in[N]:\xi_{\upalpha}=1\}
.

For any
{\bm{\xi}}\in[0,1]^{N}
the probability measure
\mu_{\bm{\xi}}
can be naturally associated to the inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathbb{G}(n,{\bm{\xi}})
with edge probabilities given by
\xi_{\upalpha}
for
\upalpha\in[N]
. Thus, for an optimizer
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}\in\{p,1\}^{N}
the edges in
{\sf G}({\bm{\xi}}_{\star})\subset\mathbb{G}(n,{\bm{\xi}}_{\star})
are present with probability one. Therefore, in the literature the edge set
E({\sf G}({\bm{\xi}}_{\star}))
is commonly termed as
planted
and by an abuse of terminology we term the associated optimizer
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}
a
planted optimizer
. Such is the case for the upper tail large deviations of
\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p))
in the regime
n^{1/2}\ll d\ll n
, where the
\log
-large deviations probability is asymptotically equal to the variational problem
\Phi_{p}(\lambda(\cdot),\cdot)
whose optimizers are planted and the corresponding planted structures induced by
{\sf G}({\bm{\xi}}_{\star})
are either a clique or a hub of appropriate sizes (see (
1.1
)).

In contrast, the proof of Theorem
1.8
will show that, in certain sub regimes of
d
, an optimizer for the variational problem
\Psi_{p}(f(\cdot),\cdot)
, both for
f(\cdot)=\lambda(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)={\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\cdot)
, is
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
where
\widehat{\xi}_{i,j}=p+\uptau np^{2}{\bf 1}_{i=1}
, for
i\neq j\in[n]
, for some appropriate choices of
\uptau>0
. Clearly the optimizer
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
is a non-planted optimizer. Therefore, unlike in the regime,
n^{1/2}\ll d\ll n
, we see an emergence of non-planted optimizers in the regime considered in this article.

1.3.
Extensions and open problems

Note that Theorem
1.1
leaves out two boundary cases. If
{d}\asymp\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}
it is natural to predict the large deviation speed to be
\log n
and the rate function should be some combination of the rate function in Theorem
1.1
for
\alpha=0
and
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
. We expect Theorem
1.1
to extend for
{d}
such that
n^{-1/2-o(1)}\leqslant{d}\ll n^{1/2}
as well.
It seems that one can combine the ideas of Lemma
3.6
and those in
[
28
, Section 7]
to treat this regime. However, for the sake of brevity and clarity of the presentation we have not attempted this here. Next, in the regime
p\asymp n^{-1/2}
one is expected to encounter some integrality issue, as was seen in
[
28
, Theorem 1.7]
.

Corollary
1.3
provides a description of the typical behavior of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
conditioned on
{\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta)
in the regime (
1.8
). The same behavior should extend for
{d}\ll n^{1/3}
. See Remark
4.4
for the challenge on proving the same. It is expected that in the regime
n^{1/3}\ll{d}\ll n^{1/2}
the graph
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, conditioned on
{\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta)
, should typically contain an almost-clique of an appropriate size.
Same is believed to hold for
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))
for
{d}
in the regime not covered in Corollary
1.7
.
This would require analyzing the near-minimizers of (
1.23
) (or (
1.24
)). See the discussion in
[
28
, Section 10]
in this regard.

Another natural question would be to extend Theorem
1.5
for all regular graphs. It would be interesting to check if there is a sparsity regime where the large deviation rate functions for the upper tails of
{\rm Hom}(H,\mathbb{G}(n,p))
and
N(H,\mathbb{G}(n,p))
are different, but the speed is the same. We expect this behavior to depend on whether
H
is bipartite or not. For example, preliminary computations show that
H=C_{2t+1}
upper tails of
{\rm Hom}(H,\mathbb{G}(n,p))
and
N(H,\mathbb{G}(n,p))
share the same large deviation speed and rate function for all
{d}
such that
{d}\geqslant(\log n)^{C}
, for some
C<\infty
. It remains to be investigated whether the same should hold in the entire ‘localized regime’.

A related problem is the large deviations of the largest eigenvalue of diluted Wigner matrices, i.e. matrices of the form
W_{n}\circ B_{n}
, where
W_{n}
is a Wigner matrix and
B_{n}
is a symmetric matrix with i.i.d.
\dBer(p)
entries on the diagonal and above the diagonal positions, and
p\to 0
with
n\to\infty
. The case of
p\asymp 1/n
and the entries of
W_{n}
are standard Gaussian was recently dealt in
[
26
]
.
This problem is closely related to the problem of studying large deviations of the second largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. In this direction a very recent progress has been made in
[
6
]
.

1.4.
Outline of the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and
1.5
, and Corollary
1.7

The proof of Theorem
1.1
(a) splits into two parts:
\alpha=0
and
\alpha>0
. To prove Theorem
1.1
(a) for
\alpha=0
we split the random graph
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
into vertices of high, moderate, and low degrees (see Definition
2.3
). At a high level, the idea of decomposition may similar to those in
[
16
,
30
]
. However, let us emphasize that the threshold used to define such subsets of vertices, as well as the arguments employed here are completely different from those in
[
16
,
30
]
. For example, the key ideas in
[
16
]
are to show that (i) there are no ‘large’ cycles, and (ii) any vertex is not incident to too many edge disjoint ‘small’ cycles, at the large deviation scale. These do not hold for the entirety of the regime (
1.8
). Hence, we need new ideas.

Bounding the spectral radius of different subgraphs of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
require several different approaches. Below we illustrate some of them. In Lemma
2.8
we show that it is unlikely, at the large deviations scale, to have a connected component
{\sf G}
of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
such that all its vertices are of moderate degree and
v({\sf G})\gg{d}\log{d}
. Using this, the fact that
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{2e({\sf G})}
, and the Chernoff bound we then argue that the subgraph of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
spanned by moderate degree vertices has a negligible spectral radius.

To bound the spectral radius of a certain subgraph
{\sf G}_{0}
of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
such that all its vertices are of low degree (see the proof of Theorem
1.1
(a) for
\alpha=0
for a precise definition of this subgraph) we employ the following ‘bootstrap’ strategy: using a bound on the number of high degree vertices (see Lemma
2.7
) we first show that for such a graph, say
\widetilde{\sf G}
, at the large deviations scale, one must have that
e(\widetilde{\sf G})\leqslant{d}^{C}
, for some absolute constant
C<\infty
. Then, we argue that if
\lambda(\widetilde{\sf G})
is non-negligible (i.e.
\gtrsim{d}
) then, upon excluding an event of negligible probability (at the large deviations scale), one can procure a subgraph
\widetilde{\sf G}^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{\sf G}
such that
e(\widetilde{\sf G}^{\prime})\leqslant{d}^{C-1}
and
\lambda(\widetilde{\sf G}^{\prime})
is still non-negligible (see Lemma
2.9
). We iterate this argument to finally obtain a subgraph
\widetilde{\sf G}_{0}\subset\widetilde{\sf G}
, with minimum degree two, such that
e(\widetilde{\sf G}_{0})\leqslant{d}^{2}
and
\lambda(\widetilde{\sf G}_{0})\gtrsim{d}
. Applying a standard Binomial tail probability bound we then deduce that the number of excess edges of
\widetilde{\sf G}_{0}
(i.e.
e(\widetilde{\sf G}_{0})-v(\widetilde{\sf G}_{0})
) should not be large, see Lemma
2.6
. However, by Lemma
2.2
(viii) this upper bound then yields that
\lambda(\widetilde{\sf G}_{0})/{d}
must be negligible thereby showing that the spectral radius of
{\sf G}_{0}
must be negligible as well.

To treat the spectral radius of the bipartite subgraph of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
with vertex bipartition consisting of moderate and low degree vertices we use the following idea: we apply estimates on the number of moderate degree vertices in the two-neighborhood of any given vertex (see Lemma
2.4
) and a bound on the spectral radius of a bipartite graph
(see Lemma
2.2
(vii)) to deduce that the spectral radius to be non-negligible there must be two sets of vertices of small sizes containing too many edges between them. By a standard bound on Binomial tail probability, and a union bound, this event turns out to be unlikely at the large deviations scale. To treat the rest of the subgraphs of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
we employ estimates that are already mentioned above, i.e. Lemmas
2.4
, and
2.6
-
2.9
. See Section
2
for further details.

Let us now move to describe the main ideas of the proof of Theorem
1.5
. From this Theorem
1.1
for
\alpha>0
essentially follows upon letting
t\to\infty
. To derive Theorem
1.5
we follow the general strategy developed in
[
28
,
8
]
. However,
[
28
,
8
]
deal with the upper tail large deviations of
N(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))
(in fact they cover all regular graphs), while here we are interested in that of
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))
. Since the large deviation results in these two cases differ, in the sparsity regime we consider, the argument require considerable modifications. As in
[
28
,
8
]
, in the first step we apply Markov’s inequality to deduce that conditioned on
{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})
the random graph contains some
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
such that
e({\sf G})\lesssim{d}^{2}\log(1/p)
and the conditional expectation of
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))
given
{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p)
is at least
\widehat{\delta}(1-o(1)){d}^{2t}
, holds with probability
1-o(1)
. The class of such graphs
{\sf G}
will be called pre-seed graphs (see Definition
3.1
). This step allows us to reduce the configuration space to a smaller one that is ‘responsible’ for the large deviation event.

The next step is to reduce the configuration space further to the set
\mathbb{G}(n,p)\supset{\sf G}
where
{\sf G}
runs over the set of all
core graphs
(see Definition
3.5
). At a high level this step can be thought of procuring an appropriate net of pre-seed graphs so that one can perform a union bound. This step was almost trivial in
[
8
]
. However, as
\varphi
in (
1.12
) is allowed to be non-injective some of the edges of
C_{2t}
may get mapped to the same edge in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, and thus the number of ‘independent’ edges at our disposal may decrease.
To overcome this challenge we introduce a notion of generating and non-generating edges and vertices (see Definition
3.12
) and perform a combinatorial analysis. See Section
3.2
.

Next, we split the set of core graphs into two further subsets:
e({\sf G})\gg{d}^{2}
and
e({\sf G})=O({d}^{2})
. Using yet another combinatorial argument the first subset can be shown to be unlikely at the large deviations scale. Applying the pigeonhole principle and the fact that
x\mapsto x^{1/t}+(1-x)^{1/t},x\in[0,1],t\geqslant 2
, is strictly concave we further deduce that there exists a splitting for any core graph
{\sf G}
belonging to the second subset (upon excluding a negligible number of edges) to
{\sf G}^{\prime}
and
{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}
such that the number of homomorphisms of
C_{2t}
in at least one of them is at least
\widehat{\delta}(1-o(1)){d}^{2t}
. Furthermore, all the vertices of
{\sf G}^{\prime}
are of moderately large degrees, while
{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}
is a bipartite graph with parts
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
such that
\max_{u\in U_{1}}\deg_{{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}}(u)=O(1)
, and
\min_{u\in U_{2}}\deg_{{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}}(u)\gtrsim{d}^{2}
. Using appropriate lower bounds on
e({\sf G}^{\prime})
and
e({\sf G}^{\prime\prime})
, and a bound on the number of such graphs (see Lemma
3.17
) we complete the argument.

To prove Corollary
1.7
we need to strengthen some of the above steps. In fact, we argue that under the set up of Corollary
1.7
, conditioned on
{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})
, the number of homomorphisms of
C_{2t}
in
{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}
is
\widehat{\delta}(1-o(1)){d}^{2t}
, and
e({\sf G}^{\prime\prime})-|U_{1}|\ll{d}^{2}
, with probability
1-o(1)
. This shows that almost all the excess homomorpshims in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
is due to the stars centered at vertices of
U_{2}
, i.e. vertices with degree
\gtrsim{d}^{2}
. Using this finding together with an estimate on the Binomial tail probability and the
strict
convexity of the Binomial rate function we deduce that there must be a single large degree vertex, thereby yielding Corollary
1.7
.

\alpha=0
and Corollary

First we derive the large deviation lower bound. This will be straightforward. To derive that bound we will need estimates on the binomial upper tail probability. We use the following result.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
2
, Lemma 4.7.2]
)
.

Let
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\dBin(N,s)
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in(0,1)
. Fix any
\uplambda\in(s,1]
. Then

\frac{\exp(-NI_{s}(\uplambda))}{\sqrt{8N\uplambda(1-\uplambda)}}\leqslant\mathbb{P}(X\geqslant\uplambda N)\leqslant\exp(-NI_{s}(\uplambda)),

where
I_{s}(\cdot)
is the binary relative entropy function. That is,

(2.1)

I_{s}(\uplambda):=\uplambda\log\frac{\uplambda}{s}+(1-\uplambda)\log\frac{1-\uplambda}{1-s}.

For any
\gamma>0
such that
(1+\gamma)s\leqslant 1
one can also derive from above the following well known Chernoff bound:

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}(\dBin(N,s)\geqslant(1+\gamma)Ns)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2+\gamma}Ns\right).

The next lemma provides bounds on the top eigenvalue of a graph in terms of various graph parameters. It will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, for the lower bound and later more extensively for the upper bound.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a graph with
{\bm{e}}
edges, maximum degree
\Delta
, and minimum degree
\updelta
. Then the following bounds hold for the top eigenvalue
\lambda({\sf G})
of the adjacency matrix of
{\sf G}
.

(i)

\sqrt{\Delta}\leqslant\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\min\{\Delta,\sqrt{2{\bm{e}}}\}.

(ii)

If
E({\sf G})=\cup_{i}E({\sf G}_{i})
then
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sum_{i}\lambda({\sf G}_{i})
. Additionally, if the collection of graphs
\{{\sf G}_{i}\}
are vertex disjoint then
\lambda({\sf G})=\max_{i}\lambda({\sf G}_{i})
.

(iii)

If
{\sf G}
is a forest then
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant 2\sqrt{\Delta-1}
.

(iv)

If
{\sf G}
is a star then
\lambda({\sf G})=\sqrt{\Delta}
.

(v)

If
{\sf G}
is a bipartite graph such that the degrees on both sides of the bipartition are bounded by
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
respectively, then
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}}
.

(vi)

Let
{\sf G}^{\prime}\subset{\sf G}
then
\lambda({\sf G}^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda({\sf G})
.

(vii)

Let
{\sf G}
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. That is, all its edges are between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Then

\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\min_{i\in\{1,2\}}\max_{v\in V_{i}}\sqrt{\sum_{u:u\sim v}\deg_{\sf G}(u)}.

(viii)

If
{\sf G}
is a graph with
\updelta\geqslant 2
then we have

\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{2({\bm{e}}-{\bm{v}})+\Delta+2},

where
{\bm{v}}
is the number of vertices in
{\sf G}
.

Most of the bounds in Lemma
2.2
are well known. The proofs of the rest are provided in Appendix
A
.

Proof of Theorem

(i) we have that

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta))\geqslant\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{\Delta}((1+\delta)^{2}))\geqslant\mathbb{P}(\deg_{\mathbb{G}(n,p)}(v)\geqslant(1+\delta)^{2}{d^{2}}),

where
v\in[n]
is an arbitrarily chosen vertex. Now the desired lower bound follows upon noting that

(2.4)

I_{s}(\uplambda)=\uplambda\log\frac{\uplambda}{s}\cdot(1+o(1)),\quad\text{ for }0<s\ll\uplambda\leqslant 1,

where
I_{s}(\uplambda)
is as in (
2.1
).
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the upper bound. As outlined in Section
1.4
this will require us to split the graph appropriately and show that the spectral radius of the random subgraph obtained upon removing the one neighborhoods of high degree vertices is small in the large deviations scale. The following definition provides the necessary splitting of a graph
{\sf G}
.

Definition 2.3

(Decomposition of the graph)
.

Fix
\eta,\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
. Given any graph
{\sf G}
(possibly random) with vertex set
V({\sf G})\subset[n]
we define the vertices of high, low, and moderate degrees, to be denoted by
V_{H}({\sf G}),V_{L}({\sf G})
, and
V_{M}({\sf G})
, respectively, as follows:

V_{H}({\sf G}):=\left\{v\in V({\sf G}):\deg_{\sf G}(v)\geqslant\varpi_{n}\right\},

where

\varpi_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{d}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}},&\mbox{ if }\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}\ll{d}\leqslant(\log n)^{1-\eta/2},\\
{d}^{1+\eta}&\mbox{ if }(\log n)^{1-\eta/2}\leqslant{d}\mbox{ and }\log{d}\ll\log n,\end{array}\right.

V_{L}({\sf G}):=\left\{v\in V({\sf G}):\deg_{\sf G}(v)\leqslant(1+\delta(1-\varepsilon)){d}\right\},

, and

Denote

. Now we describe the necessary decomposition of the graph

:

•

,
{\sf G}_{M}:={\sf G}[V_{M}]

Similarly as above we define

Our task is to show that

To carry out this task we need a few lemmas. The first lemma yields a bound on the number of moderate and high degree vertices in the two neighborhood of any given vertex. To state this lemma let us introduce the following notation.

For any

We similarly define
\mathcal{N}_{H}(v,{\sf G})
and
\mathcal{N}_{H}^{(2)}(v,{\sf G})

In the rest of the section we will always assume that

\alpha=0\quad\text{ and }\quad d\gg\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}}.

To avoid repetition, we have chosen not to include the above assumption in the statements of the rest of the lemmas in this section.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
\delta>0

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}(\exists v\in[n]:|\mathcal{N}_{M}^{(2)}(v)|\geqslant C_{1}{d}\log{d})\leqslant\exp(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d^{2}}\log{d})

Lemma

Next, fix

For any

The next lemma provides a bound on the number of high degree vertices which together with the lemma above will be used in the proof of

Lemma 2.7

The following probability bound holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(|V_{H}|\geqslant{d}^{1-\eta/2}\right)}{{d}^{2}\log{d}}=-\infty.

In the following lemma we will show that any connected component of
{\sf G}_{M}
(the underlying graph is again

) cannot have too many vertices. This is needed to obtain a desired bound on

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\exists S\subset V_{M}:|S|\geqslant C_{3}{d}\log{d}\text{ and }{\sf G}[S]\text{ is connected}\right\}.

Lemma 2.8
.

There exists
C_{3}=C_{3}(\delta)<\infty

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M})\leqslant\exp(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d}^{2}\log{d}).

The goal of the next lemma is to carry out iterative pruning procedure that will be needed to tackle

. To state this result we need to introduce a couple more notation.

Fix

and
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)

\mathcal{C}_{\ell}:=\left\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\in\mathscr{C}_{\ell}\text{ and }\lambda({\sf G})\geqslant\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{\ell-1}}{d}\right\}.

.

For any
\ell\in[L-2]

The proofs of these lemmas are postponed to Section

. Below, using these lemmas, we complete the proof of the large deviations upper bound.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\alpha>0
(upper bound).

Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
We will show that

\lambda(\mathbb{G}(n,p)\backslash\widetilde{\sf F}_{HL_{1}}(\mathbb{G}(n,p)))\geqslant\varepsilon{d}

with a negligible probability at the large deviations scale. This will yield the upper bound.

Step 1.
We claim that

(2.8)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max\{\lambda({\sf G}_{H}),\lambda({\sf G}_{HM})\}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d}^{2}\log{d}\right).

By Lemma
2.2

\lambda({\sf G}_{H})\leqslant\max_{v\in V_{H}}|\mathcal{N}_{H}(v)|\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda({\sf G}_{HM})\leqslant\sqrt{\max_{v\in V_{M}}|\mathcal{N}_{H}(v)|\cdot\max_{u\in V_{H}}|\mathcal{N}_{M}({u})|}.

Therefore, (
2.8

2.4
(see also Remark

).

Step 2.
We next claim that

(2.9)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda({\sf G}_{M})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d}^{2}\log{d}\right).

Since the top eigenvalue of a graph is the maximum of the top eigenvalues of its connected components (see Lemma
2.2
) we obtain that

(2.10)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda({\sf G}_{M})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\exists S\subset V_{M}:{\sf G}[S]\text{ is connected and }\lambda({\sf G}[S])\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\\
\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\exists S\subset[n]:|S|\leqslant C_{3}{d}\log{d}\text{ and }\lambda({\sf G}[S])\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}).

The bound on the second term in the
rhs
of (
2.10
) follows from Lemma
2.8
. To bound the first term we note that, for any
c_{\star}>0
, upon taking a union bound,

(2.11)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists S\subset[n]:|S|\leqslant C_{3}{d}\log{d}\text{ and }e(S)\geqslant c_{\star}{d}^{2}\right)\leqslant\sum_{w\geqslant c_{\star}{d}^{2}}\sum_{s\leqslant C_{3}{d}\log{d}}\binom{n}{s}\binom{s^{2}}{w}p^{w}\\
\leqslant\sum_{w\geqslant c_{\star}{d}^{2}}\sum_{s\leqslant C_{3}{d}\log{d}}\exp\left(s\log n+w\log(2es)-w\log(1/p)\right)\\
\leqslant O({d}\log{d})\sum_{w\geqslant c_{\star}{d}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{w}{2}\log n\right)\leqslant n^{3}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\star}}{2}{d}^{2}\log n\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\star}}{4}{d}^{2}\log n\right),

where in the second inequality we used Stirling’s approximation, and the third inequality is due to the facts that
s=O({d}\log{d})
,
\log{d}\ll\log n
,
{d}=np\gg 1
, and
w\geqslant c_{\star}{d}^{2}
.

Upon setting
c_{\star}=\varepsilon\delta/450
, we observe that (
2.9
) follows from the fact that
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{2e({\sf G})}
(see the upper bound in Lemma
2.2
(i)), (

)-(
2.11
), and Lemma
2.8
.

Step 3.
We aim to show that, for all large

,

(2.12)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda({\sf G}_{L_{1}}\cup{\sf G}_{L_{1}L_{2}})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d}^{2}\log{d}\right).

We will first find a bound for
\lambda(\widehat{\sf G}_{L_{1}}\cup\widehat{\sf G}_{L_{1}L_{2}})
. As the rest of the subgraph

is a forest, the rest will have a small spectral radius. Putting these two pieces together we will get (
2.12
).

To this end, we begin by noting that

(2.13)

\mathbb{P}(\Delta(\mathbb{G}(n,p))\geqslant{d}^{2+\eta/3})\leqslant n\mathbb{P}(\dBin(n-1,p)\geqslant{d}^{2+\eta/3})\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{d}^{2+\eta/3}\log{d}\right),

which is negligible at the large deviations scale. As every vertex in

must be connected to some vertex in

we observe that on the event

. By a same reasoning, on the event

, we also have that

\left|\left\{u\in V_{L_{2}}:(u,v)\in E(\mathbb{G}(n,p))\text{ for some }v\in V_{L_{1}}\right\}\right|\leqslant\left[\max_{v\in V_{L_{1}}}\deg(v)\right]\cdot|V_{L_{1}}|\leqslant{d}^{4-\eta/12}.

Thus, on the event
\Omega_{0}
, we have that
v({\sf G}_{L_{1}}\cup{\sf G}_{L_{1}L_{2}})\leqslant{d}^{4}
. Therefore, setting
L=4
and
\alpha=\varepsilon\delta/30
, and applying Lemma

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda(\widehat{\sf G}_{L_{1}}\cup\widehat{\sf G}_{L_{1}L_{2}})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{30}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{3})+2\exp(-c_{1}\varepsilon^{2}{d}^{2}\log n),

for some constant

, depending only on
\delta
. Observe that, by (

(2.15)

{-\log\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}^{c})}\gg{{d}^{2}\log{d}}.

On the other hand, by Lemma

Therefore, we find that

for a suitably chosen

Recall that

Our next goal is to derive that

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda({\sf G}_{ML})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta{d}}{15}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-2(1+\delta)^{2}{d}^{2}\log{d}\right).

Fix any

. As

(see Definition

(2.19)

\widetilde{\Omega}_{0}\backslash\left\{\exists v^{\prime}\in[n]:|\mathcal{N}_{M}^{(2)}(v^{\prime})|\geqslant C_{1}{d}\log{d}\right\}\\

where

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\Omega}_{0})\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{u}\leqslant\varpi_{n}\\

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

This is a simple application of the binomial tail probability bound. Indeed, fix a set
\mathcal{U}\subset[n]
of cardinality
t
. Recall
\varpi_{n}
from (
2.5
). Observe that

(2.26)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\deg(u)\geqslant\varpi_{n}t\right)=\mathbb{P}(2e(\mathcal{U})+e(\mathcal{U},[n]\backslash\mathcal{U})\geqslant\varpi_{n}t)\\
\leqslant\mathbb{P}(e(\mathcal{U})\geqslant\varpi_{n}t/3)+\mathbb{P}(e(\mathcal{U},[n]\backslash\mathcal{U})\geqslant\varpi_{n}t/3).

As
\varpi_{n}\gg{d}
, and

e(\mathcal{U},[n]\backslash\mathcal{U})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\dBin(t(n-t),p)\quad\text{ and }\quad e(\mathcal{U})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\dBin\left(\binom{t}{2},p\right),

using Lemma
2.1
(see also (
2.4
)) we obtain that, there exists some
c>0
, depending only on
\eta
, such that the
lhs
of (
2.26
) is bounded above by
\exp(-c\varpi_{n}t\log\log n)
. Hence, applying the union bound we now deduce that

\mathbb{P}(|V_{H}|\geqslant{d}^{1-\eta/2})\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{U}\subset[n]\\
|\mathcal{U}|={d}^{1-\eta/2}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\deg(u)\geqslant\varpi_{n}{d}^{1-\eta/2}\right)\\
\leqslant\exp\left(-{d}^{1-\eta/2}(c\varpi_{n}\log\log n-\log n)\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}{d}^{1-\eta/2}\varpi_{n}\log\log n\right)\\
\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}{d}^{2+\eta/2}\log\log n\right),

where in the penultimate step we have used that
\varpi_{n}\log\log n\gg\log n
(follows from (
2.5
) and that
{d}\gg\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}
) and in the last step we used that
\varpi_{n}\geqslant{d}^{1+\eta}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next we prove Lemma
2.6
. This also uses the Binomial tail bound of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
2.6

1.16
). Then

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant(1+o(1))\cdot\mathbb{P}(\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is a pre-seed graph}).

The proof of Lemma
3.2
relies on the following result from
[
28
]
. To state the result we need the following notation: For a function
F(Y)
, where
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N})
is a random vector, and
I\subset[N]
, we use the shorthand
\mathbb{E}_{I}[F]
to denote the conditional expectation of
F
given
\{Y_{i},i\in I\}
.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
28
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

Let
Y:=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N})
be a random vector taking values in
\{0,1\}^{N}
and
F:=F(Y)
be a nonzero polynomial with nonnegative coefficient of degree at most
{\widehat{d}}
. Then for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
\widehat{\delta}>0
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(F\geqslant(1+\widehat{\delta})\mathbb{E}[F]\text{ and }Y_{I}=0\text{ for all }I\in\mathcal{I}\right)\leqslant\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon\widehat{\delta}}{1+\widehat{\delta}}\right)^{\ell},

where

\mathcal{I}:=\left\{I\subset[N]:|I|\leqslant{\widehat{d}}\ell\text{ and }\mathbb{E}_{I}[F]\geqslant(1+\widehat{\delta}(1-\varepsilon))\mathbb{E}[F]\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad Y_{I}:=\prod_{i\in I}Y_{i}.

Lemma
3.3
essentially says that the upper tail event of a function
F
, a ‘low’ degree polynomial of Boolean variables with nonnegative coefficients, when viewed as a subset of the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{N}
, excluding a set of small probability, can be covered by a union over a collection of sub cubes of ‘small’ codimension such that the (conditional) average of
F
on each of those sub cubes is large. In the context of the Erdős-Rényi graph this translates to the existence of a pre-seed of subgraph of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
.
The proof of Lemma
3.3
follows from a bound on high moments of
F(Y){\bf 1}(Y_{I}=0\text{ for all }I\in\mathcal{I})
and Markov’s inequality.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We set
F={\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))
and
N=\binom{n}{2}
. Identifying the set
\{(i,j),i<j\in[n]\}
with
[N]
we apply Lemma
3.3
with
\ell=\bar{C}t^{-1}{d}^{2}\log(1/p)
to deduce that

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}\left({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})\cap\left\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ a pre-seed graph}\right\}^{\complement}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\bar{C}t^{-1}{d}^{2}\log(1/p)}\ll\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})),

where the last step follows upon choosing
\bar{C}
choosing sufficiently large, and from the lower bound on the probability of
{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})
(proved above). This immediately implies that

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant o(1)\cdot\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))+\mathbb{P}\left({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})\cap\left\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ a pre-seed graph}\right\}\right),

which in turn yields the desired upper bound on the probability of
{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})
.
∎

The condition (PS1) in Definition
3.1
is difficult to work with. Below we will show that, in the regime (
1.16
), graphs satisfying (PS1) admit a nicer description which we define below.

Definition 3.4

(Seed graph)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\widehat{\delta},t
, and
\bar{C}
be as in Definition
3.1
. A graph
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
is said to be a seed graph if the followings hold:

(S1)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\geqslant\widehat{\delta}(1-2\varepsilon){d}^{2t}
.

(S2)

e({\sf G})\leqslant\bar{C}{d}^{2}\log(1/p)
.

By Lemma
3.2
and the discussion above it follows that to upper bound the probability of
{\rm UT}_{t}
we need the same for the existence of seed subgraph of
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. The latter probability can be naïvely bounded by bounding the cardinality of the number of seed graphs of a given size, using that each edge in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
appears independent with probability
p
, and then taking a union bound over the set of possible sizes of seed graphs. However, such a naïve approach do not give a tight upper bound. Instead, in a very broad sense, we first find a suitable ‘net’ for the set of all seed graphs and then carry out the union bound over those net elements. Such nets will be obtained in stages. In the first stage we obtain the net of all seed graphs by simply deleting those edges that
do not account for many
homomorphism counts. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.5

(Core graph)
.

With
\varepsilon,\widehat{\delta},t
, and
\bar{C}
as in Definition
3.1
we define a graph
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
to be a core graph if

(C1)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\geqslant\widehat{\delta}(1-3\varepsilon){d}^{2t}
,

(C2)

e({\sf G})\leqslant\bar{C}{d}^{2}\log(1/p)
,

and

(C3)

\min_{{\bf e}\in E({\sf G})}{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},{\bf e})\geqslant\widehat{\delta}\varepsilon{d}^{2t}/(\bar{C}{d}^{2}\log(1/p))
,

where for an edge
{\bf e}\in E({\sf G})
the notation
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},{\bf e})
denotes the number of homomorphisms of
C_{2t}
in
{\sf G}
that contain the edge
{\bf e}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
{d}
satisfy (
1.16
). Then, for all large
n
,

\left\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is pre-seed}\right\}\subset\left\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is core}\right\}.

The proof of Lemma
3.6
is postponed to Section
3.2
. It needs combinatorial arguments. Equipped with Lemma
3.6
we observe that it suffices to bound the probability of the existence of a core graph in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. Again a naïve union bound does not suffice. So we split the set of core graphs into two subsets: (i) core graphs with a large number of edges and (ii) core graphs with
O({d}^{2})
many edges. By counting the number of core graphs with a given a number of edges and a union bound (where a lower bound on the number of edges becomes handy) we show below that the existence of the first set of graphs is unlikely at the large deviations scale. To treat the second set of graphs we extract yet another net for core graphs by keeping only those edges that participate in even a larger number of homomorphism counts. These latter set of graphs will be termed as strong-core graphs.

Definition 3.7

(Strong-core graph)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\widehat{\delta}
, and
t
be as in Definition
3.1
, and
\bar{C}_{\star}:=\bar{C}_{\star}(\widehat{\delta},t)<\infty
be a large constant, depending on
\widehat{\delta}
and
t
, and the ratio
\log d/\log n
. We define a graph
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
to be a strong-core graph if

(SC1)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\geqslant\widehat{\delta}(1-{6}\varepsilon){d}^{2t}
,

(SC2)

e({\sf G})\leqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}
,

and

(SC3)

\min_{{\bf e}\in E({\sf G})}{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},{\bf e})\geqslant(\widehat{\delta}\varepsilon/\bar{C}_{\star})\cdot{d}^{2t-2}
.

The choice of
\bar{C}_{\star}
will also be made precise in the proof. Note the difference in the lower bounds in (C3) and (SC3) in Definitions
3.5
and
3.7
, respectively.

Lemma 3.8
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for
{d}
satisfying (
1.16
) and
\varepsilon\leqslant 1/(36t)
we have that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is core and }e({\sf G})\geqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}\right)}{{d}^{2}\log{d}}\leqslant-{\frac{\bar{C}_{\star}}{20}}.

Lemma 3.9
.

Consider the same setup as in Lemma
3.8
. Then, there exists some absolute constant
\beta<\infty
such that

\log\mathbb{P}\left(\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is strong-core}\right)\leqslant\exp(-\phi_{t}(\widehat{\delta})(1-\beta t\varepsilon)),

for all large
n
, where

(3.6)

\phi_{t}(\widehat{\delta}):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\delta}^{1/t}\log(1/p),\left(\frac{\widehat{\delta}}{2}\right)^{1/t}\log{d}\right\}{d}^{2}.

Proofs of Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
are postponed to Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, respectively.
Equipped with Lemmas
3.2
,
3.6
,
3.8
, and
3.9
we now complete the proof of the large deviation upper bound.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
(upper bound).

Using Lemmas
3.2
and
3.6
we derive that

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}(\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is core}).

Therefore, once we show that

(3.7)

\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ is core and }e({\sf G})\leqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}\}\subset\{\exists{\sf G}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p):{\sf G}\text{ strong-core}\},

application of Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
, with
\bar{C}_{\star}=C\widehat{\delta}^{1/t}
for some large absolute constant
C<\infty
, would yield that

(3.8)

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant\exp(-\phi_{t}(\widehat{\delta})(1-2\beta t\varepsilon)),

for all large

. To prove (
3.7
) we consider the subgraph
{\sf G}^{\prime}\subset{\sf G}
obtained by iteratively deleting edges
{\bf e}
of
{\sf G}
such that
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},{\bf e})\leqslant(\widehat{\delta}\varepsilon/\bar{C}_{\star})\cdot{d}^{t-2}
. Using triangle inequality it follows that
{\sf G}^{\prime}
is indeed a strong-core graph.
Thus (
3.7
) holds and the proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

For the rest of the paper we set

.

Next, using Theorem

Proof of Theorem

Recall that it suffices to prove (

\left\{K_{[\lceil(1+\delta){d}\rceil+1]}\subset\mathbb{G}(n,p)\right\}\cup{\rm UT}_{\Delta}(\delta)\subset{\rm UT}_{\lambda}(\delta).

Turning to prove the upper bound we note that for any graph

Now, as

3.2.
Bounds on expected homomorphism counts

In this section our goal is to derive Lemma
3.6
. To prove Lemma
3.6
we will need a couple of results. Comparing Definitions
3.1
and
3.4
we find that we need an asymptotic estimate on
\mathbb{E}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))]
. The next lemma provides that necessary bound.

To state the result we need the notion of quotient graphs. For
\pi
a partition of
V(H)
we let
H/\pi
to be the
quotient graph
obtained from
H
by identifying vertices within parts of the partition
\pi
and deleting multiple edges (but keeping self-loops). Note that if some part of
\pi
contain vertices that do not form an independent set of
H
then the quotient graph
H/\pi
possesses self-loops. By an abuse of notation, quotient graphs that do not possess any self-loops will be termed, for convenience,
simple quotient graphs
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Fix
t\geqslant 2
.

(a)

Let
H=C_{2t}/\pi
be a simple quotient subgraph of
C_{2t}
. If
H
is a tree then
v(H)\leqslant t+1
. Furthermore, the number of simple quotient subgraphs of
C_{2t}
that are trees and have (vertex) size
(t+1)
is
\binom{2t}{t}/(t+1)
.

(b)

Let
p\in(0,1)
such that
{d}\gg 1
. Then

\mathbb{E}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))]=(1+o(1))\cdot\left({d}^{2t}+\frac{\binom{2t}{t}}{t+1}n{d}^{t}\right).

The proof of Lemma
3.11
uses a counting argument that has some similarity with the counting argument used in the proof of Wigner’s semicircle law. To prove Lemma
3.6
we also need to show that
\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))]=(1+o(1)){\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})
(recall Definitions
3.1
and
3.4
again).

Using (
1.12
) one can write
\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p))]
as a sum over subgraphs
{\sf H}\subset C_{2t}
such that
E({\sf H})
is mapped to
E({\sf G})
and the rest of the edges of
C_{2t}
are mapped to
K_{n}\backslash{\sf G}
via maps
\varphi:V(C_{2t})\mapsto[n]
. If
\varphi
were an injective map then while computing
\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}(\cdot)

all
the edges of
C_{2t}
that are not mapped to those of
{\sf G}
retain their independence. Thus in the case of subgraph counts this step was almost a triviality.

However, a homomorphism
\varphi
not necessarily being an injective map the edges of
C_{2t}
that are not mapped to
E({\sf G})
need not be mapped to distinct edges of
K_{n}\backslash{\sf G}
, and hence all these edges may not be “free” and some may get “frozen” via
\varphi
. To tackle this new difficulty one needs an additional combinatorial analysis. We require a few definitions to carry out this step.

Definition 3.12

(Equivalence classes of maps, and generating and non-generating edges and vertices)
.

Let
\varphi:V(C_{2t})\mapsto[n]
be a map such that no two adjacent vertices of
C_{2t}
are mapped to the same element in
[n]
. It naturally induces a map from
E(C_{2t})
to
\binom{[n]}{2}
, which, by a slight abuse of notation, will be continued to be denoted by
\varphi
.

For
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
and
H\subset C_{2t}
we let
\mathcal{S}({H},{\sf G})
to be the set of all maps
\varphi
such that
\varphi(E({H}))\subset E({\sf G})
and
\varphi(E(\bar{H}))\subset E(\bar{{\sf G}})
, where
\bar{H}:=C_{2t}\backslash{H}
and
\bar{\sf G}:=K_{n}\backslash{\sf G}
.

Notice that
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}({H},{\sf G})
further induces equivalence classes on
E(\bar{H})
. Namely,

e_{i},e_{j}\in E(\bar{H})
are said to be equivalent iff
\varphi(e_{i})=\varphi(e_{j})
. Inside each equivalence class an arbitrarily chosen edge, e.g. the edge with the smallest index under the canonical labelling of
E(C_{2t})
(see Definition
3.13
below), is said to be
generating
(or
free
), while the others are said to be
non-generating
(or
frozen
).

This also allows us to extend the notion of free/frozen for vertices that are incident to some edge in
E(\bar{H})
: Both end points of a non-generating edge are set to be non-generating (or frozen), while the rest of the vertices are termed to be generating (or free).

Definition 3.13

(Types of edges)
.

For convenience, let us put the canonical labelling on
C_{2t}
. That is,
V(C_{2t})=[2t]
and
E(C_{2t})=\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2t}
, where
e_{i}=(i,i+1)
for
i\in[2t-1]
and
e_{2t}=(2t,1)
. For
i\in[2t]
, the vertex
i
will be said to be the left end point of the edge
e_{i}
, while the vertex in the other end of
e_{i}
will be its right end point.

Fix
H\subset C_{2t}
.
We classify the edges of
\bar{H}
as follows. If both end points of the edge
{\bf e}\in E(\bar{H})
are in
V({H})
then we classify
{\bf e}
as a
type I edge
. If
{\bf e}
is such that only its left end point belongs to
V({H})
then we classify it as a
type II edge
. When only the right end point of
{\bf e}
belongs to
V({\sf H})
we term that to be a
type III edge
. The rest of the edges of
\bar{H}
are
type IV
edges.

Definition 3.14
.

Fix
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
and
H\subset C_{2t}
. Define

\psi(H)=\psi_{\sf G}(H):=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G})}\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}\left[\prod_{{\bf e}=(x,y)\in E(C_{2t})}a^{\mathbb{G}}_{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)}\right].

Let
{\bm{f}}:=(\mathfrak{f}_{1},\mathfrak{f}_{2},\mathfrak{f}_{3},\mathfrak{f}_{4})
, where
\mathfrak{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
, and define
\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},{\bm{f}})
to be the subset of all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G})
such that the number of frozen edges of Type
i
equals
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
. Set

(3.11)

\psi(H,{\bm{f}})=\psi_{\sf G}(H,{\bm{f}}):=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},{\bm{f}})}\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}\left[\prod_{{\bf e}=(x,y)\in E(C_{2t})}a^{\mathbb{G}}_{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)}\right].

Equipped with the above definitions we have the following result.

Lemma 3.15
.

Fix
\varnothing\neq H\subsetneq C_{2t}
,
{\sf G}\subset K_{n}
, and
{\bm{f}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant}^{4}
such that
H
does not contain any isolated vertex and
e({\sf G})=O({d}^{2}\log n)
. Then, for
p\in(0,1)
such that
(\log n)^{t}\ll{d}\ll n^{1/2}(\log n)^{-2t}
we have

(3.12)

\psi(H,{\bm{f}})=o({d}^{2t}).

Let us postpone the proof of Lemma
3.15
to later and now provide the proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
(using Lemmas
3.11
and
3.15
).

As
\max_{i=1}^{4}\mathfrak{f}_{i}\lesssim t
, it follows from Definition
3.14
and Lemma

that

\mathbb{E}_{\sf G}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}(n,p)]=\sum_{H:H\subset C_{2t}}\psi_{\sf G}(H)=\psi_{\sf G}(C_{2t})+(1+o(1)){d}^{2t}={\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})+(1+o(1)){d}^{2t},

where the sum over

C_{2t}
that do not possess any isolated vertex. Therefore, by Lemma

(b) we deduce that any pre-seed graph
{\sf G}
is a seed graph, for

as in (

) and all large

. Now upon deleting edges

. Since

, by triangle inequality we obtain that (C1) also holds for

∎

It remains to prove Lemmas
3.11
and

. First, we prove Lemma

. It uses a counting argument.

Proof of Lemma
3.15
.

To derive (

) we will split the sum over
\varphi
into subsets of
\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},{\bm{f}})

.

\psi(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})
to be the

of (

We now turn to prove (

) for

. Observe that conditioned on the edges in

, for any

\psi(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{{\bf e}=(x,y)\in E(\bar{H})}a^{\mathbb{G}}_{\varphi(x),\varphi(y)}\right]=\widetilde{\psi}(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})\cdot p^{|E(\bar{H})\backslash\mathcal{F}|},

where

. As

|E(\bar{H})\backslash\mathcal{F}|=2t-e(H)-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathfrak{f}_{i},

it now remains to find an appropriate bound on

, where
V(\mathcal{F})
is the set of frozen vertices. Recalling Definition
3.12
, as
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})
, we observe that
\varphi(V_{\star}\cup\widehat{V})
determines the values of
\varphi(V(\mathcal{F}))
. Therefore

\mathcal{S}(H,{\sf G},\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})\subset\left\{\varphi_{\star}\Big|\,\varphi_{\star}:V_{\star}\mapsto V({\sf G}),\varphi_{\star}(E(H_{\star}))\subset E({\sf G})\right\}\times\left\{\widehat{\varphi}\Big|\,\widehat{\varphi}:\widehat{V}\mapsto[n]\right\},

where
H_{\star}:={\sf G}[V_{\star}]
. Hence

(3.15)

\widetilde{\psi}(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})\leqslant n^{\widehat{v}}\cdot{\rm Hom}(H_{\star},{\sf G}),

where
\widehat{v}:=|\widehat{V}|
.

Since
\varnothing\neq H\subsetneq C_{2t}
and
H
does not contain isolated vertices it is easy to see that
H
must be a vertex disjoint union of paths and hence, by the definition of
H_{\star}
, so is
H_{\star}
. Let us assume that
H=\cup_{i=1}^{k}P_{s_{i}}
for some
k\geqslant 1
and
s_{i}\geqslant 1
for
i\in[k]
, where
P_{\ell}
denotes a path of length
\ell
and the union is a vertex disjoint union. Recall Definition
3.13
and note that each frozen edge appearing immediately after a path
P_{s_{i}}
, for some
i\in[k]
(when
C_{2t}
is traversed from the lowest indexed edge to the highest indexed edge in its canonical labelling) must be either a type I or type II edge, while each frozen edge immediately before a path
P_{s_{i}}
should be either a type I and or type III edge. This yields that

(3.16)

\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}\leqslant k.

In fact
\mathfrak{f}_{1}
in the
lhs
above can be replaced by the total number of type I edges.
We next claim that

(3.17)

\widehat{v}\leqslant 2t-v(H)-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}-\mathfrak{f}_{4}

and

(3.18)

{\rm Hom}(H_{\star},{\sf G})\lesssim n^{v(H)}p^{e(H)}\cdot(np^{2})^{k}\cdot{d}^{-(\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3})}\cdot(\log n)^{3t}.

Before proving (
3.17
)-(
3.18
) let us use these bounds to derive the bound (
3.12
) for
\psi(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})
. Using (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) and (
3.17
)-(
3.18
) we obtain that

\psi(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})\lesssim{d}^{2t}\cdot(\log n)^{3t}\cdot(np^{2})^{k-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\wedge\mathfrak{f}_{3}}\cdot{d}^{-(\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}+\mathfrak{f}_{4})}.

If
\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{4}>0
then the lower bound on
{d}
yields the desired bound on
\psi(H,\mathcal{P},{\bm{m}})
. Consider remaining case, i.e. when
\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{4}=0
. As there are only Type I edges, at least one of them must a generating edge. Thus, by (
3.16
) we find
\mathfrak{f}_{1}<k
and therefore the upper bound
d
yields the desired bound.

To complete the proof of the lemma it remains to derive (
3.17
)-(
3.18
). To prove (
3.17
) pick any frozen edge that is of type either II or IV. Consider the vertex on its right end point. Observe that such vertices are distinct as we traverse over all type II and IV frozen edges. Furthermore, all these vertices are in
([2t]\backslash V(H))\cap V(\mathcal{F})
. Therefore,
\widehat{v}\leqslant 2t-v(H)-\mathfrak{f}_{2}-\mathfrak{f}_{4}
. Now considering type III and IV frozen edges and the vertices at the left end point of these edges, and repeating the same argument as above we arrive at the bound (
3.17
).

Turning to prove (
3.18
) we observe that, for
\ell\geqslant-1
,

(3.19)

{\rm Hom}(P_{\ell},{\sf G})\leqslant(2e({\sf G}))^{\lceil\frac{\ell+1}{2}\rceil}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{\rm Hom}({\sf H}_{1}\cup{\sf H}_{2},{\sf G})\leqslant{\rm Hom}({\sf H}_{1},{\sf G})\cdot{\rm Hom}({\sf H}_{2},{\sf G}),

where for notational convenience we set
P_{0}
to be the graph containing a single isolated vertex and
P_{-1}
to be the empty graph, and
{\sf H}_{1}
and
{\sf H}_{2}
vertex disjoint. We then recall the definitions of
V_{\star}
and
H_{\star}
from above to find that
H_{\star}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}P_{\widetilde{s}_{i}}
for some sequence
\{\widetilde{s}_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}
such that
-1\leqslant\widetilde{s}_{i}\leqslant s_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
. We claim that

(3.20)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{s}_{i}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}=e(H)-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}=v(H)-k-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}.

The equalities in (
3.20
) follow from the fact that
H
is a vertex disjoint union of paths
\{P_{s_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}
with
s_{i}\geqslant 1
. To prove the inequality in (
3.20
) we consider type I or II frozen edges. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the vertices at the left end point of such edges. Clearly
\mathcal{R}\subset V(H)\cap V(F)
, all elements of
\mathcal{R}
are distinct, and
|V(H)\cap V(F)|\geqslant|\mathcal{R}|=\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}
. By a similar argument we also have that
|V(H)\cap V(F)|\geqslant\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{3}
. Thus
v(H_{\star})\leqslant v(H)-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}
. Then using that
v(P_{\ell})=\ell+1
for all
\ell\geqslant-1
we obtain the inequality in (
3.20
).

Equipped with (
3.20
) we apply (
3.19
) to deduce that

{\rm Hom}(H_{\star},{\sf G})\lesssim e({\sf G})^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\frac{\widetilde{s}_{i}}{2}+1)}\lesssim{d}^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{s}_{i}+2k}\cdot(\log n)^{3t}\leqslant{d}^{v(H)+k-\mathfrak{f}_{1}-\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3}}\cdot(\log n)^{3t}\\
=n^{v(H)}p^{e(H)}(np^{2})^{k}{d}^{-(\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}\vee\mathfrak{f}_{3})}\cdot(\log n)^{3t},

where in the second step we used that
e({\sf G})=O({d}^{2}\log n)
,
v(H)\leqslant 2t
, and
k\leqslant t
, and in the last step we have used
v(H)=e(H)+k
. This yields (
3.18
). The proof of the lemma is now complete.
∎

We end this section with the proof of Lemma
3.11
. To prove part (a) we will need to use some standard notion that are used in the proof of Wigner’s semicircle law, such as words, and graphs associated to words. These are borrowed from
[
1
, Chapter 2.1.3]
. Readers familiar with these notions can skip the definition below and move straight to the proof of Lemma
3.11
.

Definition 3.16
.

Given a finite set
\mathscr{S}
, an
\mathscr{S}
-word
w
is a finite sequence of letters, elements of
\mathscr{S}
, i.e.
w=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The length of
w
,
\ell(w)
, is defined to be
k
and its weight, to be denoted by
{\rm wt}(w)
, is the number of distinct letters in
{\rm supp}(w):=\{s_{i},i\in[k]\}
. The word
w
is closed if
s_{1}=s_{k}
. Two
\mathscr{S}
-words are equivalent if there is a bijection on
\mathscr{S}
which maps one word to the other. If
\mathscr{S}
is clear from the context, we refer to
w
simply as a word.

For a word
w
as above we let
G(w)
to be the graph associated with it whose vertex set is
{\rm supp}(w)
and the edge set is
\{\{s_{i},s_{i+1}\},i\in[k]\}
. Note that for a closed word
w
this defines a path starting and ending at the same vertex. For an edge
{\bf e}\in E(G(w))
we define
\mathscr{N}_{\bf e}(w)
to be the number of times this path traverses the edge
{\bf e}
.

We then let
\mathscr{V}_{k,r}
to be a set of representatives for the equivalence classes of closed
[r]
-words
w
with
\ell(w)=k+1
and
{\rm wt}(w)=r
. Define
\mathscr{W}_{k,r}\subset\mathscr{V}_{k,r}
be the collection of all
w
such that
\mathscr{N}_{\bf e}(w)\geqslant 2
for all
{\bf e}\in E(G(w))
. The set
\mathscr{W}_{k,k/2+1}
(
k
must be even) is said to be the set of all Wigner words.

Proof of Lemma
3.11
.

Fix a partition
\pi
so that the quotient subgraph
C_{2t}/\pi
is simple. Our strategy would be to associate any such simple quotient graph to
G(w)
for some appropriately chosen closed word
w
, and then use properties of
G(w)
.

Towards this end, we observe that for any partition
\pi
of
[2t]
there is a natural choice of a closed word
w=w(\pi)=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{2t+1}
such that
{\rm wt}(w)
is the number of parts in the partition
\pi
, and
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i}\}
is a discrete interval in
\mathbb{N}
for all
i\in[2t+1]
. Let us illustrate it through examples: If
\pi_{1}=\{\{1,3,5\},\{2\},\{4\},\{6\}\}
and
\pi_{2}=\{\{1,3\},\{2,4\},\{5\},\{6\}\}
then
w(\pi_{1})=1213141
and
w_{2}(\pi)=1212341
. It can be argued that that this map
\pi\mapsto w(\pi)
is a bijection between the set of all
\pi
such that its number of parts is
r
and the set words
w\in\mathscr{V}_{2t,r}
such that
{\rm supp}(w)=[r]
. Furthermore, the graphs
G(w(\pi))
(when one edge is kept for each set of parallel edges) and
C_{2t}/\pi
are isomorphic.

Let
\pi
be such that
\mathscr{N}_{\bf e}(w(\pi))=1
for some edge
{\bf e}\in G(w(\pi))
. We claim that
G(w(\pi))
(or equivalently
C_{2t}/\pi
) must contain a cycle. To see this, for ease of writing assuming that
{\bf e}_{0}=(\llbracket 1\rrbracket,\llbracket 2t\rrbracket)
is traversed only once we observe that the path induced by the set of edges
\mathfrak{W}:=\{(\llbracket i\rrbracket,\llbracket i+1\rrbracket),i\in[2t-1]\}
, where
\llbracket j\rrbracket
denotes the equivalence class containing the vertex
j
induced by
\pi
, does not contain the edge
{\bf e}_{0}
. Since the vertices
\llbracket 1\rrbracket
and
\llbracket 2t\rrbracket
are connected via the edges in
\mathfrak{W}
we deduce that there are two paths between
\llbracket 1\rrbracket
and
\llbracket 2t\rrbracket
yielding the claim.

On the other hand
\mathscr{N}_{\bf e}(w(\pi))\geqslant 1
for all edge
{\bf e}
,
e(G(w(\pi)))\geqslant{\rm wt}(w)-1
and
\sum_{\bf e}\mathscr{N}_{\bf e}(w(\pi))=2t
, where the lower bound on
e(G(w(\pi)))
is due to the fact that
G(w(\pi))
is connected. This shows that for any
r\geqslant t+2
such that
{\rm wt}(w(\pi))=r
there exists some edge
{\bf e}_{\star}
such that
\mathscr{N}_{{\bf e}_{\star}}(w(\pi))=1
. Hence, we conclude that any simple quotient graph
H=C_{2t}/\pi
with
v(H)>t+1
cannot be a tree.

It additionally follows from above that the set of
\pi
for which that
C_{2t}/\pi
is a tree on
(t+1)
vertices has a bijection with
\mathscr{W}_{2t,t+1}
. So, the conclusion on the number of trees follows from the one-to-one correspondence between Wigner words and Dyck paths (cf.
[
1
, Proof of Lemma 2.1.6]
). This completes part (a).

The proof of (b) uses the following well known identity:

(3.21)

{\rm Hom}({\sf H},{\sf G})=\sum_{\pi}N({\sf H}/\pi,{\sf G}),

where the sum is over all partition
\pi
of the vertex set
V({\sf H})
(cf.
[
31
, Chapter 5]
), and we recall that
N({\sf H},{\sf G})
denotes the number of labelled copies of
{\sf H}
in
{\sf G}
. Note that if
{\sf G}
does not possess any self-loop then the sum in (
3.21
) can be restricted to a sum over
\pi
such that the graph
{\sf H}/\pi
is a simple quotient graph. By part (a), for a simple quotient graph
H=C_{2t}/\pi
either
e(H)\geqslant v(H)
or
e(H)=v(H)-1
only if
v(H)\leqslant t+1
. Since for any
H
one has that
\mathbb{E}N(H,\mathbb{G}(n,p))=n^{v(H)}p^{e(H)}(1+o(1))
, denoting
\mathscr{T}
to be the set of all trees on
(t+1)
vertices and
\upphi
to be the trivial partition (i.e. consisting of only singletons), and using that
np\gg 1
we deduce that

\sum_{\pi:\pi\neq\upphi,C_{2t}/\pi\notin\mathscr{T}}\mathbb{E}[N(C_{2t}/\pi,\mathbb{G}(n,p)]=o({d}^{2t})+o(n{d}^{t}).

Finally using the bound on the number of simple quotient graphs
C_{2t}/\pi\in\mathscr{T}
and (
3.21
) the proof completes.
∎

3.3.
Cores with many edges are unlikely

In this section we prove Lemma
3.8
. This is based on a combinatorial argument that requires the following result on the number of graphs with certain specified properties.

Lemma 3.17
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
{d}
be such that
(\log n)^{2t}\ll{d}\lesssim n^{1/2}
, and
\mathfrak{D}:=\mathfrak{D}(\varepsilon)
and
D:=D(\varepsilon):=\lceil 32/\varepsilon\rceil
be intergers such that
\mathfrak{D}\geqslant D
. Denote

\mathcal{V}_{1}:=\mathcal{V}_{1}({\sf G}):=\{v\in V({\sf G}):\deg_{\sf G}(v)\leqslant\mathfrak{D}\}

and
\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}:=V({\sf G})\backslash\mathcal{V}_{1}
. Let
\mathcal{N}({\bm{e}},{\bm{v}},\mathfrak{D})
be the number of core graphs
{\sf G}
with
e({\sf G})={\bm{e}}
and
v({\mathcal{V}}_{1}({\sf G}))={\bm{v}}
. Then, for all large
n
, we have

\mathcal{N}({\bm{e}},{\bm{v}},\mathfrak{D})\leqslant\binom{n}{{\bm{v}}}\cdot\exp(\varepsilon{\bm{e}}\log(1/p)).

Proceeding similarly as in the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 4.7]
, and using the lemma below instead of
[
8
, Lemma 3.6]
, the proof of Lemma
3.17
follows. Therefore, we spare the details.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
{d}
satisfy (
1.16
) and
{\sf G}
be a core graph. Then, for every edge
{\bf e}=(u,v)\in E({\sf G})

\deg_{\sf G}(u)\deg_{\sf G}(v)\geqslant\frac{\widetilde{c}_{0}(\varepsilon,t){d}^{2}}{(\log n)^{2t}},

for some constant
\widetilde{c}_{0}(\varepsilon,t)>0
.

The proof of Lemma
3.18
is postponed to Appendix
B
. We now proceed to prove Lemma
3.8
.

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1}({\sf G})
be as in Lemma
3.17
with
\mathfrak{D}=D
. Note that, by Lemma
3.18
, there are no edges
{\sf G}
with both end points in
\mathcal{V}_{1}
. Therefore,
e({\sf G})\geqslant e(\mathcal{V}_{1}({\sf G}),\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}({\sf G}))\geqslant v(\mathcal{V}_{1}({\sf G}))
.
We claim that

(3.22)

\mathcal{N}({\bm{e}},{\bm{v}},D)\leqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left(2(1/3+\varepsilon){\bm{e}}\log(1/p)\right),&\mbox{ if }{\bm{v}}\leqslant{\bm{e}}/3,\\
\exp\left(\left\{(1+2\varepsilon)\log(1/p)-\log{d}/3\right\}{\bm{e}}\right),&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

As the probability of any graph
{\sf G}
with
{\bm{e}}
edges is
p^{\bm{e}}
, and upon using that

t^{-1}\log(1/p)\leqslant\log{d}\leqslant\log(1/p)
, for
{d}
satisfying (
1.16
), the proof of this lemma follows by taking a union over
({\bm{e}}_{11},{\bm{e}}_{12},{\bm{v}})
such that
{\bm{e}}\geqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}
, and
{\bm{e}}_{11},{\bm{e}}_{12}
, and
{\bm{v}}\leqslant n^{2}
.

Thus, it remains to prove (
3.22
). Since
{d}\ll n^{1/2}
the upper bound in (
3.22
) is immediate from Lemma
3.17
when
{\bm{v}}\leqslant{\bm{e}}/3
. Turning to prove the remaining case we apply Lemma
3.17
again, together with Stirling’s approximation, to obtain that

\mathcal{N}({\bm{e}},{\bm{v}},D)\leqslant\left(\frac{en}{{\bm{v}}}\right)^{\bm{v}}\cdot\exp(\varepsilon{\bm{e}}\log(1/p))\leqslant\left(\frac{3en}{\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}}\right)^{\bm{v}}\cdot\exp(\varepsilon{\bm{e}}\log(1/p))\\
\leqslant\exp\left(\left\{(1+2\varepsilon)\log(1/p)-\log{d}/3\right\}{\bm{e}}\right),

where in the last two steps we have used that
{\bm{v}}\geqslant{\bm{e}}/3
, and in the penultimate and the final steps we have also used that
{\bm{e}}\geqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}
and
v(\mathcal{V}_{1}({\sf G}))\leqslant e({\sf G})
, respectively. This completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Probability upper bound on the existence of strong-core graphs

In this section we prove Lemma
3.9
. This will require that for all but a few edges in a strong-core graph, the product of the degrees of two adjacent vertices satisfy a tight upper and lower bound in the leading order. First we define the set of “bad” and “good” edges.

Definition 3.19
.

Fix
C_{0}<\infty
. Let
{\sf G}_{\rm high}\subset{\sf G}
be the subgraph spanned by the edges
{\bf e}=(u,v)\in E({\sf G})
for which

(3.23)

\deg_{\sf G}(u)\deg_{\sf G}(v)\geqslant C_{0}{d}^{2},

and set
{\sf G}_{\rm low}:={\sf G}\backslash{\sf G}_{\rm high}
.

The next lemma shows that a careful choice of
C_{0}
in (
3.23
) yields that
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})
is almost the same as
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G}_{\rm low})
, while keeping the number of edges in
{\sf G}_{\rm high}
small. It also provides a lower bound on the product of the degree of two adjacent vertices. The proof is postponed to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Consider the same setup as in Lemma
3.8
. Let
{\sf G}
be a strong-core graph. There exist constants
0<c_{0}(\varepsilon,t),C_{0}(\varepsilon,t)<\infty
such that the followings hold:

(a)

For every edge
{\bf e}=(u,v)\in E({\sf G})

\deg_{\sf G}(u)\deg_{\sf G}(v)\geqslant{c_{0}(\varepsilon,t){d}^{2}}.

(b)

Let
{\sf G}_{\rm high}={\sf G}_{\rm high}(\varepsilon,t)
and
{\sf G}_{\rm low}={\sf G}_{\rm low}(\varepsilon,t)
be as in Definition
3.19
with
C_{0}=C_{0}(\varepsilon,t)
in (
3.23
). Then

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G}_{\rm low})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon){\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad e({\sf G}_{\rm high})\leqslant\varepsilon e({\sf G}).

We now prove Lemma
3.9
.

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

In the first step we decompose a strong-core graph
{\sf G}
into three subgraphs: one is a bipartite graph with edges only between vertices of low and high degrees (by Lemma
3.20
), the second subgraph consisting of vertices of large degrees, and the third consisting of negligible many edges. Furthermore, almost all the homomorphisms of
C_{2t}
into
{\sf G}
are contained in the first two subgraphs. In the second step using that a large number of homomorphism implies a lower bound on the number of the edges of a graph (both for bipartite and non-bipartite graphs), the upper bound on the number of core graphs with a given number of edges, and a union bound we complete the proof.

To carry out these steps we need to introduce several notation.
Let
D=D(\varepsilon)
be as in Lemma
3.17
, and
C_{0}=C_{0}(\varepsilon,t)
and
c_{0}=c_{0}(\varepsilon,t)
be as in Lemma
3.20
. Set
D_{0}:=0
,

(3.24)

\widetilde{C}=\widetilde{C}(\varepsilon,t):=(t-1)\left(\left\lceil\frac{(2\bar{C}_{\star})^{t}}{\varepsilon\widehat{\delta}}\right\rceil+2\right),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad D_{i}:=D\cdot\left(\frac{C_{0}}{c_{0}}\right)^{i-1},\text{ for }i\in[\widetilde{C}].

Define
\widetilde{V}_{0}:=\varnothing
. Then, for
i\in[\widetilde{C}]
we (iteratively) define

V_{i}=V_{i}({\sf G}):=\left\{v\in V({\sf G}_{\rm low}):D_{i-1}+1\leqslant\deg_{\sf G}(v)\leqslant D_{i}\right\}

and

\widetilde{V}_{i}=\widetilde{V}_{i}({\sf G}):=\left\{v\in V({\sf G}_{\rm low}):(u,v)\in E({\sf G}_{\rm low})\text{ for some }u\in V_{i}\right\}\backslash\widetilde{V}_{i-1}.

We further define
{\sf H}_{i}
to be the subgraph (of
{\sf G}_{\rm low}
) spanned by the edges that are incident to some vertex in
\cup_{j=1}^{i}V_{j}
, and
\bar{\sf H}_{i}={\sf G}_{\rm low}\backslash{\sf H}_{i}
. Note that, by Lemma
3.20
,
\{{\sf H}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\widetilde{C}}
are bipartite graphs.

We now claim that there exists an
i\in[\widetilde{C}]
such that

(3.25)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{i})+{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\bar{\sf H}_{i})\geqslant\widehat{\delta}(1-8\varepsilon){d}^{2t}.

To derive this claim we observe that there exists an
i\in[\widetilde{C}-t+1]
such that

(3.26)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{i+(t-1)})-{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{i})\leqslant\varepsilon\widehat{\delta}{d}^{2t}.

Otherwise, as for a graph
{\sf G}
and a bipartite graph
\widetilde{\sf G}
one has that

(3.27)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\leqslant(2e({\sf G}))^{t}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\widetilde{\sf G})\leqslant 2(e(\widetilde{\sf G}))^{t},

it follows that

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\geqslant\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\widetilde{C}/(t-1)-1}{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{k(t-1)+1})-{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{(t-1)(k-1)+1})\right]+{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf H}_{1})\\
\geqslant\left(\frac{\widetilde{C}}{t-1}-1\right)\varepsilon\widehat{\delta}{d}^{2t}>(2\bar{C}_{\star})^{t}{d}^{2t},

(recall the definition of
\widetilde{C}
) yielding a contradiction to the fact that
e({\sf G})\leqslant\bar{C}_{\star}{d}^{2}
.

Recalling the definition of
\{D_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\widetilde{C}}
, and using the tight upper and lower bounds on the product of the degrees of adjacent vertices in
{\sf G}_{\rm low}
given by Lemma
3.20
, we further deduce that any edge in
\bar{\sf H}_{k}
that is incident to some edge in
{\sf H}_{k}
must be contained in
{\sf H}_{k+1}
. Therefore, as the distance between any two vertices of same parity in
C_{2t}
is at most
(t-1)
, by induction we derive that any homomorphism of
C_{2t}
that uses edges of both
{\sf H}_{i}
and
\bar{\sf H}_{i}
must be contained in
{\sf H}_{i+(t-1)}
, but not in
{\sf H}_{i}
. Hence, by (
3.26
), we also obtain that the number of such homomorphisms is at most
\varepsilon\widehat{\delta}{d}^{2t}
. As any homomorphism of
C_{2t}
into
{\sf G}_{\rm low}
must either be fully contained in
{\sf H}_{i}
or
\bar{\sf H}_{i}
, or must use edges of both
{\sf H}_{i}
and
\bar{\sf H}_{i}
, an application of Lemma
3.20
(b) now yields the claim (
3.25
).
This concludes the first step.

It now remains to find an upper bound on the probability of the existence of a strong-core subgraph
\mathbb{G}(n,p)

4.
Proof of Corollary
1.7

We start with some auxiliary results that help us to identify and eliminate a few unlikely events at the large deviations scale. The first result shows that if the number of edges of a bipartite graph
{\sf G}
, with vertex bipartition
V({\sf G})=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
, does not exceed much from the size of
U_{1}
then almost all homomorphisms of
C_{2t}
in
{\sf G}
must be due to the stars centered at the vertices in
U_{2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
. Assume that
e({\sf G})-|U_{1}|\leqslant\upalpha e({\sf G})

for some
\upalpha>0
. Then

(4.1)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})\leqslant 2\upalpha te({\sf G})^{t}+2\sum_{w\in U_{2}}\deg_{\sf G}(w)^{t}.

Proof.

Consider the canonical labelling of the vertices and edges of
C_{2t}
(recall Definition
3.13
). Fix
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{t}\in U_{1}
(not necessarily distinct) and let
\underline{u}:=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{t})
. Denote
{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},\underline{u})
to be the cardinality of
\mathscr{H}_{\underline{u}}
, the set of homomorphisms
\varphi
of
C_{2t}
into
{\sf G}
such that
\varphi(2i-1)=u_{i}
for
i\in[t]
. Since
{\sf G}
is bipartite it follows that

(4.2)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G})=2\sum_{\underline{u}\in U_{1}^{t}}{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},\underline{u}).

We will show that

(4.3)

{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},{\sf G},\underline{u})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{t}(\deg_{\sf G}(u_{i})-1)\cdot\left[\prod_{j\neq i}\deg_{\sf G}(u_{j})\right]+\sum_{w\in U_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}a_{u_{i},w}^{\sf G},

for
\underline{u}\in U_{1}^{t}
, where
\{a_{u,v}^{\sf G}\}
denotes the adjacency matrix of
{\sf G}
. Sum the second term in the
rhs
of (
4.3
) over all
\underline{u}\in U_{1}^{t}
, and apply (
4.2
) to obtain the second term in the
rhs
of (
4.1
). On the other hand, summing the first term in the
rhs
of (
4.3
), and using the assumption
e({\sf G})-|U_{1}|\leqslant\upalpha e({\sf G})
and (
4.2
) we obtain the first term in the
rhs
of (
4.1
).

Turning to prove (
4.3
) we split
\mathscr{H}_{\underline{u}}
into two further subsets
\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}:=\mathscr{H}_{\underline{u}}\backslash\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
, where
\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
is the set of homomorphisms
\varphi
such that the cardinality of the set
\{\varphi(2i)\}_{i=1}^{t}
is one. Thus,
\varphi\in\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
implies that
\varphi(2i)=w
, for some
w\in U_{2}
, and
a_{u_{i},w}^{\sf G}=1
for all
i\in[t]
. This indeed shows that
|\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}|
is bounded by the second term in the
rhs
of (
4.3
).

To establish the bound on
\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
we observe that
\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{t}\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{{\underline{u}},i}
, where
\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{{\underline{u}},i}
is the set of
\varphi\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\underline{u}}
such that
\varphi(2i)\neq\varphi(2i-2)
(the vertex
0
is to be understood as the vertex
2t
). We claim that

(4.4)

|\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{{\underline{u}},i}|\leqslant(\deg_{\sf G}(u_{i})-1)\cdot\left[\prod_{j\neq i}\deg_{\sf G}(u_{j})\right].

Let us prove this claim for
i=2
. The other cases are similar. The number of choices of
\varphi(2)
is bounded by
\deg_{\sf G}(u_{1})
. Pick one such choice and let
\varphi(2)=w
for some
w\in U_{2}
. Now, by definition, for any
\varphi\in\widehat{\mathscr{H}}_{{\underline{u}},2}
we have that
\varphi(4)\in U_{2}\backslash\{w\}
. So, the number of choices of
\varphi(4)
is at most
\deg_{\sf G}(u_{2})-1
. Continuing this argument we get the claim. Now summing (
4.4
) over
i\in[t]
we obtain the first term in the
rhs
of (
4.3
). This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
1.8

The lower bounds in (
1.21
) and (

), as will be seen below, follow from Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
. To prove the upper bound we will need the following bounds on the variances.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\delta,\widehat{\delta},
and
t
be as in Theorem
1.8
. For any
\chi\in(-1,1)
define

(5.1)

{\sf S}_{\widehat{\delta},t,\chi}:=\left\{{\bm{\xi}}\in[0,1]^{N}:\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}[{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n})]\geqslant(1+\widehat{\delta}(1+\chi)){d}^{2t}\right\}.

(a)

For any
{\bm{\xi}}\in[0,1]^{N}

\Var_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}(\lambda(\mathbb{G}_{n}))\lesssim 1.

(b)

Let
p\in(0,1)
be such that
np\gg\sqrt{\log n}
. Then, for any fixed
\chi\in(-1,1)
,

(5.2)

\sup_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}\in{\sf S}_{\widehat{\delta},t,\chi}}\frac{\Var_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}({\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n}))}{(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}{\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n}))^{2}}=o(1).

Proof.

The proofs of both parts will be consequences of Efron-Stein inequality (cf.
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
). In fact, part (a) has been already worked out in
[
20
, Example 3.14]
. So we will only prove part (b).

Let
A_{n}=\{a_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
be the random matrix which is the adjacency matrix of
\mathbb{G}_{n}
, where
\mathbb{G}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{G}(n,{\bm{\xi}})
. For
i<j\in[n]
we let
A_{n}^{(i,j)}
to be the symmetric random matrix obtained from
A_{n}
by replacing
a_{i,j}
with
\widehat{a}_{i,j}
, an independent copy of
a_{i,j}
.
By
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
we have that

(5.3)

\Var_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}({\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n}))=\Var_{\mu_{\bm{\xi}}}[\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<j\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Tr\left(A_{n}^{2t}-(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{2t}\right)\right)^{2}\right].

Observe that only the
(i,j)
-th and the
(j,i)
-th entries of
A_{n}-A_{n}^{(i,j)}
are non-zero. Therefore, noting that

\Tr(B_{1}^{2t})-\Tr(B_{2}^{2t})=\sum_{k=1}^{2t}\Tr(B_{2}^{k-1}B_{1}^{2t-k}(B_{1}-B_{2})),

for any two square matrices
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, we derive that

\Tr\left(A_{n}^{2t}-(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{2t}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{2t}\left[((A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k-1}A_{n}^{2t-k})_{i,j}(a_{i,j}-\widehat{a}_{i,j})+((A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k-1}A_{n}^{2t-k})_{j,i}(a_{i,j}-\widehat{a}_{i,j})\right].

Hence, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
|a_{i,j}-\widehat{a}_{i,j}|\leqslant 1
we obtain that

(5.4)

\sum_{i<j\in[n]}\left(\Tr\left(A_{n}^{2t}-(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{2t}\right)\right)^{2}\leqslant 4t\sum_{k=1}^{2t}\sum_{i\neq j}\left(((A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k-1}A_{n}^{2t-k})_{i,j}\right)^{2}.

So, in the light of (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) we deduce that it suffices to show that

(5.5)

\mathfrak{I}_{k}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\neq j}\left(((A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k-1}A_{n}^{2t-k})_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]\ll\left(\mathbb{E}\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})\right)^{2},

for all
k\in[2t]
. Since
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}A_{n}^{(i,j)}
it is enough to prove (
5.5
) for
k\in[t]
.

We will prove (
5.5
) by an induction on
k
. We start with
k=1
. Notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\neq j}\left((A_{n}^{2t-1})_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}^{2t-1}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\|^{2(t-1)}\|A_{n}^{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\right],

where we have used that
\|B_{1}B_{2}\|_{\rm HS}\leqslant\|B_{1}\|\cdot\|B_{2}\|_{\rm HS}
. Denote

\mathscr{A}:=\left\{\max\left\{\max_{i<j\in[n]}\{\|A_{n}^{(i,j)}\|\},\|A_{n}\|\right\}\leqslant 2(\mathbb{E}[\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})])^{1/(2t)}\right\}.

Since for a
n\times n
symmetric matrix
B
we have that
\|B\|=\max\{\lambda_{1}(B),-\lambda_{n}(B)\}
we get from
[
20
, Example 8.7]
that

(5.6)

\mathbb{P}(\|A_{n}\|\geqslant 2\theta_{t})\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\|A_{n}\|\geqslant\mathbb{E}\|A_{n}\|+\theta_{t})\leqslant\exp(-2c\theta_{t}^{2}),

for some absolute constant
c>0
, where
\theta_{t}:=(\mathbb{E}[\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})])^{1/(2t)}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\|A_{n}\|
. Using that
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}A_{n}^{(i,j)}
,
\theta_{t}\gtrsim{d}\gg\sqrt{\log n}
for
\mu_{\bm{\xi}}\in{\sf S}_{\widehat{\delta},t,\chi}
, and a union bound we obtain from (
5.6
) that

(5.7)

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{A}^{\complement})\leqslant\exp(-c\theta_{t}^{2}).

Since
A_{n}
is a matrix with entries bounded by one it follows that
\|A_{n}\|\leqslant\|A_{n}\|_{\rm HS}\leqslant n
. Therefore, as
\theta_{t}\gg\sqrt{\log n}
an application of (
5.7
) yields that

(5.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\|^{2(t-1)}\|A_{n}^{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}{\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}^{\complement}}\right]=o(1).

On the other hand,

(5.9)

\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\|^{2(t-1)}\|A_{n}^{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}{\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}}\right]\lesssim\theta_{t}^{2(t-1)}\mathbb{E}[\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})]\ll\theta_{t}^{4t}.

Combining (
5.8
)-(
5.9
) we have (
5.5
) for
k=1
. Now let us assume that (
5.5
) holds for some
k=k_{0}<t
. We proceed to prove that the same holds for
k=k_{0}+1
. To this end, observe that

\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}}A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}=\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}}\right]_{i,j}+\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}(\widehat{a}_{i,j}-a_{i,j})\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}\\
+\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,i}(\widehat{a}_{i,j}-a_{i,j})\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{j,j}.

Hence

(5.10)

\mathfrak{I}_{k_{0}+1}\lesssim\mathfrak{I}_{k_{0}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{4}\mathfrak{J}^{(\ell)},

where

\mathfrak{J}^{(1)}:=\sum_{i\neq j}\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}^{2}\widehat{a}_{i,j}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}^{2},\,\mathfrak{J}^{(2)}:=\sum_{i\neq j}\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}^{2}a_{i,j}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,j}^{2},

\mathfrak{J}^{(3)}:=\sum_{i\neq j}\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,i}^{2}\widehat{a}_{i,j}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{j,j}^{2},\,\text{ and }\,\mathfrak{J}^{(4)}:=\sum_{i\neq j}\left[(A_{n}^{(i,j)})^{k_{0}-1}\right]_{i,i}^{2}a_{i,j}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{j,j}^{2}.

Now note that

(5.11)

\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{J}^{(1)}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}^{(i,j)}\|^{2(k_{0}-1)}\|A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}^{(i,j)}\|^{2(k_{0}-1)}\|A_{n}\|^{2(t-k_{0}-1)}\|A_{n}^{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\right]\\
\leqslant o(1)+\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}^{(i,j)}\|^{2(k_{0}-1)}\|A_{n}\|^{2(t-k_{0}-1)}\|A_{n}^{t}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}{\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}}\right]\lesssim o(1)+\theta_{t}^{2t-4}\mathbb{E}[\Tr(A_{n}^{2t})]\ll\theta_{t}^{4t},

where in the third step we use (
5.7
) and argue similarly as in (
5.8
). Notice that the same argument can be repeated to show that the same bound holds for
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{J}^{(2)}]
. Next, as
\|A_{n}\|,\|A_{n}^{(i,j)}\|\leqslant n
, using (
5.7
) and that
\{\widehat{a}_{i,j}\}_{i<j\in[n]}
and
A_{n}
are independent we see that

(5.12)

\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{J}^{(3)}]\lesssim o(1)+np\theta_{t}^{2(k_{0}-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{j,j}^{2}\right){\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}}\right]\\
\lesssim o(1)+\theta_{t}^{2k_{0}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}{\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}}\right]\ll\theta_{t}^{4t}.

To bound
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{J}^{(4)}]
we use that
\sum_{i}a_{i,j}\leqslant\|A_{n}\|^{2}
, for any
j\in[n]
, and proceed similarly as above to find that

(5.13)

\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{J}^{(4)}]\lesssim o(1)+\theta_{t}^{2k_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[A_{n}^{2t-k_{0}-1}\right]_{j,j}^{2}\right){\bf 1}_{\mathscr{A}}\right]\ll\theta_{t}^{4t}.

Thus, from (

) holds for

t\geqslant 3
. Let
f(\mathbb{G}_{n}):={\rm Hom}(C_{2t},\mathbb{G}_{n})
and use the shorthand
\Psi:=\Psi_{p}(f,\widehat{\delta}(1+\chi))
. Let
{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}\in[0,1]^{N}
(with
N=\binom{n}{2}
) such that
\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}\in{\sf S}_{\widehat{\delta},t,\chi}
and
I_{p}({\bm{\xi}}_{\star})\leqslant(1+\chi/2)\Psi
. Fix some large absolute constant
C>1
. Define

\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left({\frac{f(\mathbb{G}_{n})}{(1+\widehat{\delta}(1+\chi))\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f]}-1}\right)\Big/\left({\frac{\chi\widehat{\delta}}{1+\widehat{\delta}(1+\chi)}}\right)&\mbox{ if }\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[f]\leqslant C(1+\widehat{\delta})\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[f],\\
\left(\frac{f(\mathbb{G}_{n})}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[f]}-1\right)\Big/\left(1-C^{-1}\right)&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Let

h(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2x+1&\mbox{ for }x\leqslant-1,\\
-x^{2}&\mbox{ for }x\in[-1,0],\\
0&\mbox{ for }x\geqslant 0.\end{array}\right.

and

\widehat{h}(\mathbb{G}_{n}):=2\Psi h(\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n})).

Observe that
h^{\prime}(x)=-2(x\vee(-1)){\bf 1}(x\leqslant 0)
. Therefore, by Taylor’s theorem we find that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[\widehat{h}(\mathbb{G}_{n})]=2\Psi\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[h(\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n}))]\geqslant 2\Psi h(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n})])-2\Psi\Var_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}(\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n}))=-2\Psi\Var_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}(\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n})),

where in the last step we have used that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n})]\geqslant 0
.

On the other hand, by the definition of
\widehat{h}(\cdot)
we find that
\widehat{h}\leqslant-2\Psi
on the event
{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})^{\complement}
. As
\widehat{h}\leqslant 0
we deduce that

(5.17)

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[\exp(\widehat{h}(\mathbb{G}_{n}))]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{p}}[\exp(\widehat{h}(\mathbb{G}_{n})){\bf 1}_{{\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta})^{\complement}}]\\
\geqslant\exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}[\widehat{h}(\mathbb{G}_{n})]-I_{p}({\bm{\xi}}_{\star})\right)-\exp(-2\Psi)\\
\geqslant\exp(-(1+\chi/2)\Psi-2\Psi\Var_{\mu_{{\bm{\xi}}_{\star}}}(\widehat{f}(\mathbb{G}_{n})))-\exp(-2\Psi)\geqslant\exp(-(1+3\chi/4)\Psi)-\exp(-2\Psi),

for all large
n
, where in the last step we use Lemma
5.1
(b).

By Theorem
1.5
we have that
\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\leqslant e^{-\omega(\log n)}
. Therefore (
5.17
) shows that either
\Psi\geqslant\omega(\log n)
or
1-\exp(-(1-3\chi/4)\Psi)\leqslant\exp(-\omega(\log n))
. If the latter condition holds then we have that

\Psi\lesssim-\log(1-\exp(-\omega(\log n)))\asymp\exp(-\omega(\log n)).

Since
p\gtrsim n^{-1}
this yields a contradiction to Lemma
5.2
(b). Thus, we must have that
\Psi\geqslant\omega(\log n)\gg 1
. Plugging this lower bound on
\Psi
in (
5.17
) we finally deduce that

\mathbb{P}({\rm UT}_{t}(\widehat{\delta}))\geqslant\exp(-(1+\chi)\Psi),

for all large
n
, yielding the upper bound in (
1.22
). This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2.2

The proof of
\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{2{\bm{e}}}
is trivial. Indeed, denoting
\mathfrak{a}_{i,j}
to be the
(i,j)
-th entry of the adjacency matrix of
{\sf G}
we note that

\lambda({\sf G})\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}({\sf G})}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}\mathfrak{a}_{i,j}}=\sqrt{2{\bm{e}}}.

The rest of (i), and (ii)-(v) are taken from
[
30
, Proposition 3.1]
. The proof of (vi) is standard. It relies on the variational representation of eigenvalues and
the fact that the Perron-Frobenius eigenvector of a matrix with nonnegative entires has nonnegative entries. We omit further details.

We are not able to locate the proof of (vii) in the literature. It follows from an intermediate step in the proof of (viii). A bound similar to (vii) was derived in
[
29
]
for
\updelta=1
. We adapt their proof to deduce our bound for
\updelta\geqslant 2
.
Turning to do that, for ease in writing let us assume that
{\sf G}
is a graph on
[N]
. Let
\rho
be an eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix
\mathfrak{A}
and
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})
be the corresponding eigenvector of unit Euclidean norm. Then for any
i\in[N]
we have that

(A.1)

\rho^{2}x_{i}^{2}=|(\mathfrak{A}x)_{i}|^{2}=\left(\sum_{j:j\sim i}x_{j}\right)^{2}\leqslant\deg_{\sf G}(i)\cdot\left(\sum_{j:j\sim i}x_{j}^{2}\right).

Now summing the both sides of (
A.1
) over
i\in[N]
we deduce that

(A.2)

\rho^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}\left(\sum_{i:i\sim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\right)=2{\bm{e}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}\left(\sum_{i:i\nsim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\right).

Observe that, as
\updelta\geqslant 2
,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}\left(\sum_{i:i\nsim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\deg_{\sf G}(i)x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\nsim i\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}x_{j}^{2}\right)\\
\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\deg_{\sf G}(i)x_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:j\nsim i\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}x_{j}^{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\deg_{\sf G}(i)x_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(N-\deg_{\sf G}(i)-1)x_{i}^{2}\\
=2(N-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\deg_{\sf G}(i)x_{i}^{2}\geqslant 2(N-1)-\Delta.

Plugging this bound in (
A.2
) the desired bound on
\lambda({\sf G})
follows.

It remains to prove (vii). Our starting point is (
A.1
). Recall that
{\sf G}
is a bipartite graph with vertex partition
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Summing the both sides of (
A.1
) over
i\in V_{1}
we obtain

(A.3)

\rho^{2}\sum_{i\in V_{1}}x_{i}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\in V_{2}}x_{j}^{2}\left(\sum_{i:i\sim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\right)\leqslant\left[\max_{j\in V_{2}}\left(\sum_{i:i\sim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)\right)\right]\cdot\sum_{j\in V_{2}}x_{j}^{2}.

Since
{\sf G}
is a bipartite graph, upon permuting the vertices if required, we have that its adjacency matrix
\mathfrak{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathfrak{A}_{0}\\
\mathfrak{A}^{\sf T}_{0}&0\end{bmatrix}
for some matrix
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
of dimension
|V_{1}|\times|V_{2}|
. It is straightforward to see that
\rho
is a singular value of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
if and only if
\pm\rho
are eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{A}
. Furthermore, if
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{z}}
are the left and right singular vectors of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
corresponding to the singular value
\rho
, then
\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{y}}\\
\pm{\bm{z}}\end{pmatrix}
are the eigenvectors of
\mathfrak{A}
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm\rho
. This, immediately implies that
\sum_{i\in V_{1}}x_{i}^{2}=\sum_{j\in V_{2}}x_{j}^{2}
. Thus, (
A.3
) yields that

\rho\leqslant\max_{j\in V_{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i:i\sim j}\deg_{\sf G}(i)}.

Now reversing the roles of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
the desired bound on
\lambda({\sf G})
follows. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Local Homomorphism count bounds and proofs of Lemmas
3.18
and
3.20

We start with a couple of local homomorphism bounds.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
H
be a
\Delta
-regular graph. For every graph
{\sf G}
and an edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E({\sf G})
we have

\left|{\rm Hom}(H,{\sf G},e)\right|\leqslant 4e_{H}\cdot(2e_{\sf G})^{\frac{v_{H}}{2}-\frac{2\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\cdot(4\deg_{\sf G}(u)\cdot\deg_{\sf G}(v))^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
H
be a
\Delta
-regular graph. For every graph
{\sf G}
and
{\sf G}^{\prime}\subset{\sf G}
we have

\sum_{e\in E({\sf G}^{\prime})}\left|{\rm Hom}(H,{\sf G},e)\right|\leqslant e_{H}\cdot(2e_{G})^{v_{H}/2}\cdot\left(\frac{e_{{\sf G}^{\prime}}}{e_{\sf G}}\right)^{1/\Delta}.

Notice that analogs of Lemmas
B.1
and
B.2
are proved in
[
28
, Section 5.3]
for subgraph counts. To prove these two results we follow the same route.
We need the following abstract result.

Lemma B.3
.

H
be a
\Delta
-regular graph and
\mathcal{E}
be a collection of homomorphisms of
H
into
{\sf G}
. For every edge

, we set

\mathcal{E}_{e}:=\{\{\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\},\varphi\in\mathcal{E}\}.

Then

|\mathcal{E}|\leqslant\prod_{e\in E(H)}(2|\mathcal{E}_{e}|)^{1/\Delta}.

Using Shearer’s inequality the same bound was shown to hold in
[
28
]
(see Lemma 5.10 there) when
\mathcal{E}
is a collection of embeddings of
H
into
{\sf G}
. The same argument works in our setup. So we omit the details.

Equipped with Lemma
B.3
one can proceed as in the proofs of to

28

and

28

B.1
and
B.2

The proof of Lemma
3.20
now follows upon using Lemmas
B.1
and
B.2
(instead of
[
28

8

.
