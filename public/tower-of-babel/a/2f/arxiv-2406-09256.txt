Integral solutions to systems of diagonal equations
Nick Rome, Shuntaro Yamagishi

Abstract
. In this paper, we obtain an asymptotic formula for the number of integral solutions to a system of diagonal equations. We obtain an asymptotic formula for the number of solutions with variables restricted to smooth numbers as well. We improve the required number of variables compared to previous results by incorporating recent progress on Waring’s problem and the resolution of the main conjecture in Vinogradov’s mean value theorem.



1.
Introduction

Consider the system of equations defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle m_{1,1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+m_{1,n}x_{n}^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle m_{R,1}x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+m_{R,n}x_{n}^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{R},

which we denote by
M\mathbf{x}^{d}=\bm{\mu}
,
where
M=[m_{i,j}]_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant R\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}
is the coefficient matrix with integer entries,

\mathbf{x}^{d}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{d}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}^{d}\end{bmatrix}

and

\bm{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{R}.

The system of diagonal equations (
1.1
) with
\bm{\mu}=\mathbf{0}
was first studied by Davenport and Lewis
[
DL69
, Lemma 32]

who established the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Davenport and Lewis)
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
\bm{\mu}=\mathbf{0}
. Suppose that all
n
variables occur explicitly in the equations (
1.1
). Suppose that any linear combination, not identically zero, of
the
R
rows of
M
contains more than
(2H+3d-1)R
non-zero entries, where
H=\lfloor 3d\log Rd\rfloor
. Suppose the equations (
1.1
) have a non-singular solution in every
p
-adic field, and further, if
d
is even, a real non-singular solution. Then the equations (
1.1
) have infinitely many solutions in integers.

In fact, they obtained an asymptotic formula for the number of solutions.
Their main results
[
DL69
, Theorems 1 and 2]
are consequences of this theorem and require

n\geqslant\begin{cases}\lfloor 9R^{2}d\log(3Rd)\rfloor&\mbox{if $d$ is odd},\\
\lfloor 48R^{2}d^{3}\log(3Rd^{2})\rfloor&\mbox{if $d\geqslant 4$ is even},\end{cases}

for the conclusions to hold. By incorporating the breakthrough on Waring’s problem by Vaughan
[
Vau89
]
, Brüdern and Cook
[
BC92
]

improved the number of variables required to

n>n_{0}(d)R,

where
n_{0}(d)=2d(\log d+O(\log\log d))
, under a suitable “rank condition” on the coefficient matrix
M
.
They also obtained an asymptotic formula for the number of solutions but with variables restricted to smooth numbers, which in turn provided a lower bound for the number of solutions in positive integers.

Since the release of these two papers, there has been great progress regarding Waring’s problem
(for example, by Wooley
[
Woo92
]
,
[
Woo95
]
and more recently by Wooley and Brüdern
[
BW23
]
)
and also the resolution of the main conjecture in Vinogradov’s mean value theorem (see the work by Bourgain, Demeter and Guth
[
BDG16
]
,
and by Wooley
[
Woo16a
,
Woo19
]
). The purpose of this paper is to incorporate these recent progress to improve the required number of variables
in both the setting of solutions in positive integers as in
[
DL69
]

and in the smooth numbers as in
[
BC92
]
.

There have been a number of results regarding pairs of diagonal equations,
in which the improvements have been achieved by making use of various developments in the theory of smooth Weyl sums.
For example, the work of Parsell
[
Par02
]
on pairs of equations of small degrees,
Parsell and Wooley
[
PW02
]
on pairs of quintic equations,
and
Brüdern and Wooley
[
BW07
]
on pairs of cubic equations.
For larger systems of diagonal equations, there are the papers of
Brüdern and Wooley
[
BW16
]
on systems of cubic equations,
and
of Brandes and Parsell
[
BP17
]

and Brandes and Wooley
[
BW21
]

on systems of equations involving different degrees. These works assume that the system is
“highly non-singular” which is to say that any
R\times R
submatrix of the coefficient matrix is invertible.
Our work is instead in line with
[
DL69
]
and
[
BC92
]
which hold for systems of diagonal equations of the same (arbitrary) degree with slightly less restrictive conditions on the underlying coefficient matrix.

For
X\geqslant 1
and
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the following counting function

\displaystyle N(\mathfrak{B};X)=\#\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X])^{n}:M\mathbf{x}^{d}=\bm{\mu}\}.

Instead of restricting the singularity of the variety defined by our system of equations,
as in the work of Birch
[
Bir62
]
,
we will require a condition on how well the underlying coefficient matrix can be partitioned.

Definition 1.2
.

For an
R\times n
matrix
A
with
n\geqslant R
, we define
\Psi(A)
to be the largest integer
\mathfrak{T}
such that there exists

\{\mathfrak{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{T}}\},

where each
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
is a linearly independent set of
R
columns of
A
and
\mathfrak{D}_{i}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{j}\neq\varnothing
if
i\neq j
.

Remark 1.3
.

There are at least two ways to obtain lower bounds for

: by studying the ranks of submatrices (thanks to a result of Low–Pitman–Wolff
[
LPW88
, Lemma 1]
) as in
[

]
, or by algorithmically enumerating sets of
R
linearly independent columns as in
[

]
. In these two papers,
lower bounds of the form a constant times

were obtained for coefficient matrices related to
n\times n
magic squares.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Weyl sums

In this section, we collect two results which are the main ingredients to prove Theorem
1.4
.
Both are consequences of the resolution of the main conjecture in Vinogradov’s mean value theorem (by Bourgain, Demeter and Guth
[
BDG16
]
and by Wooley
[
Woo16a
,
Woo19
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose that there exist
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(q,a)=1
such that
|\alpha-a/q|\leqslant q^{-2}
and
q\leqslant X^{d}
.
We define

\lambda(d)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}&\mbox{if }2\leqslant d\leqslant 5,\\
\frac{1}{d(d-1)}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then

\left|\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant X}e(\alpha x^{d})\right|\ll X^{1+\varepsilon}(q^{-1}+X^{-1}+qX^{-d})^{\lambda(d)},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

The bound for
2\leqslant d\leqslant 5
is the classic Weyl’s inequality
[
Vau97
, Lemma 2.4]
.
The other estimate for larger
d
is a consequence of the resolution of the main conjecture in Vinogradov’s mean value theorem (c.f.
[
BY21
, Lemma 2.4]
).
∎

Let us define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\theta(d)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }2d+2\geqslant\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor,\\
2&\mbox{if }2d+2<\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
s
a real number such that

s\geqslant T_{\text{int.}}(d)=\min\left\{2^{d},d^{2}-d+2\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor-\theta(d),d^{2}+1-\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j\leqslant d-1\\
2^{j}\leqslant d^{2}\end{subarray}}\left\lceil\frac{dj-2^{j}}{d+1-j}\right\rceil\right\}.

Then

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant X}e(\alpha x^{d})\right|^{s}\mathrm{d}\alpha\ll X^{s-d+\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

The first bound
s\geqslant 2^{d}
is the classical version of Hua’s lemma
[
Vau97
, Lemma 2.5]
, while
the other two bounds are consequences of the resolution of the main conjecture in Vinogradov’s mean value theorem.
The second bound can be found in
[
Woo19
, Cor. 14.7]
, on noting that the bound for
s_{0}
in the statement is given by

s_{0}\leqslant\lfloor s_{0}\rfloor+1\leqslant d^{2}-d+2\lfloor\sqrt{2d+2}\rfloor-\theta(d)

as explained in the proof. The third bound essentially follows from
[
Woo12
, Thm. 4.1]
; it can be seen in the proof that the
integral over the minor arcs satisfies
\ll X^{s-d+\varepsilon}
, while over the major arcs the same estimate follows
by combining familiar estimates from the major arc analysis in the theory of Waring’s problem (see
[
Vau97
, Section 4]
).
It can be verified that the values of
T_{\text{int.}}(d)
are precisely as in Table 1 (see the paragraph following
[
Woo19
, Cor. 14.7]

and the proof of
[
Woo12
, Thm. 4.1]
).
∎

2.2.
Smooth Weyl sums

In this section, we record some key estimates regarding the smooth Weyl sums needed to prove Theorem
1.5
.
Let
d\geqslant 3
. We let

f(\alpha;X,Z)=\sum_{x\in\mathscr{A}(X,Z)}e(\alpha x^{d}).

We first need two estimates from
[
BC92
]
.
We begin with
[
BC92
, Lemma 3]
which is obtained by combining
[
Vau89
, Theorem 1.8]
and
[
VW91
, Lemma 7.2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
BC92
, Lemma 3]

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small.
Suppose
\eta>0
is sufficiently small.
Then there exists
\gamma=\gamma(d)>0
such that
given
\alpha\in[0,1]
one of the following two alternatives holds:

(i)

we have
|f(\alpha;X,X^{\eta})|<X^{1-\gamma}
;

(ii)

there exist
0\leqslant a\leqslant q
,
\gcd(q,a)=1
such that

f(\alpha;X,X^{\eta})\ll q^{\varepsilon}X(q+X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{-\frac{1}{2d}}(\log X)^{3}.

The following is
[
BC92
, Lemma 4]
, which is a special case of
[
VW91
, Lemma 8.5]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
BC92
, Lemma 4]

Let
d\geqslant 3
.
Suppose
\eta>0
is sufficiently small.
Let
A_{0}>0
. Suppose
\gcd(q,a)=1
,
1\leqslant q\leqslant(\log X)^{A_{0}}
and
|q\alpha-a|\leqslant(\log X)^{A_{0}}X^{-d}
.
Then

f(\alpha;X,X^{\eta})\ll Xq^{\varepsilon}(q+X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{-\frac{1}{d}},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Given a real parameter
\mathfrak{L}\geqslant 1
, we define

\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}}=\bigcup_{1\leqslant q\leqslant\mathfrak{L}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant a\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}\{\theta\in[0,1]:|q\theta-a|<\mathfrak{L}X^{-d}\}.

We make use of the previous two lemmas to prove the following.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\delta>0
,
A=2d\delta
and
\mathfrak{L}=(\log X)^{A}
.
Suppose
\eta>0
is sufficiently small.
If

|f(\alpha;X,X^{\eta})|>X(\log X)^{-\delta}

holds for
X\geqslant 1
sufficiently large,
then

\alpha\in\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}}.

Proof.

Since we are in alternative (ii) of Lemma
2.3
, it follows that

X(\log X)^{-\delta}<Cq^{\varepsilon}X(q+X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{-\frac{1}{2d}}(\log X)^{3},

for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and some
C>0
, which in turn implies

q^{\frac{1}{2d}}<Cq^{\varepsilon}(\log X)^{\delta+3}

and

(X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{\frac{1}{2d}}<Cq^{\varepsilon}(\log X)^{\delta+3}.

Therefore, by setting
A_{0}=(\delta+3)4d
, we obtain

1\leqslant q<(\log X)^{4d(\delta+3)}
,
\gcd(q,a)=1
and
|q\alpha-a|<(\log X)^{A_{0}}X^{-d}
.
It then follows from Lemma
2.4
that

X(\log X)^{-\delta}<C_{1}q^{\varepsilon}X(q+X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{-\frac{1}{d}},

for some
C_{1}=C_{1}(d,\delta,\varepsilon)>0
, which in turn implies

q^{\frac{1}{d}}<C_{1}q^{\varepsilon}(\log X)^{\delta}

and

(X^{d}|q\alpha-a|)^{\frac{1}{d}}<C_{1}q^{\varepsilon}(\log X)^{\delta}.

Therefore, for
\mathfrak{L}=(\log X)^{A}
with
A=2d\delta
, it follows that

\alpha\in\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}}

as desired.
∎

3.
The Hardy-Littlewood circle method

Let
\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N}
or
\mathscr{A}(X,X^{\eta})
. Throughout the remainder of the paper, unless stated otherwise, we assume
d\geqslant 2
if
\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N}
, and
d\geqslant 3
if
\mathfrak{B}=\mathscr{A}(X,X^{\eta})

Our main tool
to study
N(\mathfrak{B};X)

is the Hardy–Littlewood circle method and
the key input are the estimates regarding the associated exponential sums.
In contrast to the exposition in
[
BC92
]
, we find it more natural to index our exponential sums by the columns of the corresponding coefficient matrix.
For
\bm{\theta}\in[0,1]^{R}
and
\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)
, we introduce the exponential sum

S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})=S_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathfrak{B};\bm{\theta})=\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\theta}x^{d}).

Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle N(\mathfrak{B};X)=\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}S_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathfrak{B};\bm{\theta})\cdot e\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\theta_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}.

We set

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}=\begin{cases}X^{\delta}&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N},\\
(\log X)^{A}&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathscr{A}(X,X^{\eta}),\end{cases}

where

are to be chosen in due course.
We define the major arcs

\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}=\bigcup_{1\leqslant q\leqslant\mathfrak{L}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{R}\\
0\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\{\bm{\theta}\in[0,1]^{R}:|q\theta_{i}-a_{i}|<\mathfrak{L}X^{-d}\ (1\leqslant i\leqslant R)\},

and the minor arcs

\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{L}}=[0,1]^{R}\setminus\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}.

From here on out, we will use the following notation for simplicity.

Definition 3.1
.

We let
T
be a natural number such that
\Psi(M)\geqslant T
.

3.1.
The minor arc estimate

The following lemma allows us to understand when a phase of the form
\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\theta}
belongs to
[0,1]\setminus\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}}
.
Given a set of vectors
\mathfrak{D}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{R}\}
, we denote by
M(\mathfrak{D})=[\mathbf{c}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{c}_{R}]

the matrix with these vectors as its columns.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{R}\}\subseteq\textnormal{Col}(M)
be a set of
R
linearly independent vectors.
Suppose
X\geqslant 1
is sufficiently large.
If
\mathbf{c}_{i}\cdot\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}^{1/(R+1)}}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
,
then
\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}
.

Proof.

We have

\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\mathbf{c}_{1}\cdot\bm{\theta}\\
\vdots\\
q_{R}\mathbf{c}_{R}\cdot\bm{\theta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}+E_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{R}+E_{R}\end{bmatrix}

for some
1\leqslant q_{i}\leqslant\mathfrak{L}^{1/(R+1)}
and
1\leqslant a_{i}\leqslant q_{i}
such that
\gcd(a_{i},q_{i})=1

and
|E_{i}|<\mathfrak{L}^{1/(R+1)}X^{-d}
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
. Then

q_{1}\cdots q_{R}M(\mathfrak{D})^{t}\bm{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\cdots q_{R}(a_{1}+E_{1})/q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
q_{1}\cdots q_{R}(a_{R}+E_{R})/q_{R}\end{bmatrix},

and the result follows
by multiplying both sides of the equation by the inverse of
M(\mathfrak{D})^{t}
on the left
and simplifying the resulting equation.
∎

We are now ready to bound the contribution from the minor arcs.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that

T\geqslant\begin{cases}T_{int.}(d)+1&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N},\\
T_{smo.}(d)+1&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathscr{A}(X,X^{\eta}).\end{cases}

Suppose
\eta>0
is sufficiently small.
Then, we may choose
\delta,A>0
such that there exists
\gamma>0
satisfying

\int_{\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{L}}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathfrak{B};\bm{\theta})|\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\ll X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-\gamma}.

Proof.

Let

\mathfrak{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{D}_{T}

be pairwise disjoint sets of
R
linearly independent columns of
M
.
We begin by applying Lemma
3.2
with
\mathfrak{D}_{T}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{R}\}
.
Given
\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{L}}
, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that there exists
1\leqslant i\leqslant R
such that
\mathbf{c}_{i}\cdot\bm{\theta}\not\in\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L^{\prime}}}
with
\mathfrak{L^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{L}^{1/(R+1)}
. Extracting the contribution from this column, we have the bound

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{L}}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})|\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle X^{R-1}\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}}}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\alpha x^{d})\right|\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in\text{Col}(M)\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{T}\end{subarray}}|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})|\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}.

Then bounding the contribution from any column which does not belong to
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{D}_{T-1}
trivially gives a bound for the integral over the minor arcs of

X^{n-(T-1)R-1}\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}}}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\alpha x^{d})\right|\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\ \left|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})\right|\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality this is bounded by

X^{n-(T-1)R-1}\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]\setminus\mathfrak{N}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}}}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\alpha x^{d})\right|\prod_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\ \left|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})\right|^{T-1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\right)^{1/(T-1)}.

Since the columns in
\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}
are linearly independent, by a linear change of variables we obtain

\int_{[0,1]^{R}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\ \left|S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})\right|^{T-1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\ll\prod_{i=1}^{R}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\lambda_{i}x^{d})\right|^{T-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i},

for each
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant T-1
.
We may now apply the bounds from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
or from Lemmas
2.5
and
2.6
, depending on
\mathfrak{B}
, to conclude the proof.
∎

3.2.
Major arc analysis

We define

\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{+}=\bigcup_{1\leqslant q\leqslant\mathfrak{L}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{R}\\
0\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\{\bm{\theta}\in[0,1]^{R}:|q\theta_{i}-a_{i}|<q\mathfrak{L}X^{-d}\ (1\leqslant i\leqslant R)\},

which clearly satisfies
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{+}
. For any
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we introduce the standard notation

S(q,a)=\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant q}e_{q}(ax^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad I(\beta)=\int_{0}^{1}e(\beta\xi^{d})\mathrm{d}\xi.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta=\alpha-a/q
.
Then

\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant X}e(\alpha x^{d})=Xq^{-1}S(q,a)I(X^{d}\beta)+O\left(\frac{q}{\gcd(q,a)}(1+X^{d}|\beta|)\right).

Proof.

The statement with the additional hypothesis
\gcd(q,a)=1
follows from
[
Vau97
, Theorem 4.1]
.
Suppose
\gcd(q,a)=g
and let
q_{0}=q/g
and
a_{0}=a/g
.
Then

q^{-1}S(q,a)=q^{-1}\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant q}e_{q}(ax^{d})=q^{-1}\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant q}e_{q_{0}}(a_{0}x^{d})=q^{-1}g\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant q_{0}}e_{q_{0}}(a_{0}x^{d})=q_{0}^{-1}S(q_{0},a_{0}).

Therefore, we see that we may remove the coprimality condition.
∎

For the smooth Weyl sums we have the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
1\leqslant q\leqslant Z
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\beta=\alpha-a/q
.
Then

f(\alpha;X,Z)=q^{-1}S(q,a)w(\beta)+O\left(\frac{qX}{\gcd(q,a)\log X}(1+X^{d}|\beta|)\right),

where

w(\beta)=\sum_{Z^{d}<m\leqslant X^{d}}\frac{1}{d}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}\varrho\left(\frac{\log m}{d\log Z}\right)e(\beta m)

and
\varrho
is the Dickman’s function (for example, see
[
Vau89
, pp.53]
).

Proof.

The statement with the additional hypothesis
\gcd(q,a)=1
is precisely
[
Vau89
, Lemma 5.4]
.
The coprimality condition may be removed in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
|\beta|<\mathfrak{L}X^{-d}
and
w
be as in Lemma
3.5
.
Then

w(\beta)=\varrho\left(\frac{d\log X}{d\log Z}\right)XI(X^{d}\beta)+O\left(\frac{X}{\log Z}+Z\right).

Proof.

Let us denote

P(y)=\sum_{Z^{d}<m\leqslant y}\frac{1}{d}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}e(\beta m).

Then, by summation by parts, it follows that

\displaystyle w(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z^{d}<m\leqslant X^{d}}\frac{1}{d}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}e(\beta m)\varrho\left(\frac{\log m}{d\log Z}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(X^{d})\varrho\left(\frac{d\log X}{d\log Z}\right)+O\left(1+\int_{Z^{d}}^{X^{d}}|P(y)|\frac{1}{y\log Z}\mathrm{d}y\right).

Since
|P(y)|\ll y^{\frac{1}{d}}
, we have

\int_{Z^{d}}^{X^{d}}|P(y)|\frac{1}{y\log Z}dy\ll\frac{1}{\log Z}\int_{Z^{d}}^{X^{d}}y^{\frac{1}{d}-1}\mathrm{d}y\ll\frac{X}{\log Z}.

Therefore, we obtain

w(\beta)=\varrho\left(\frac{d\log X}{d\log Z}\right)\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant X^{d}}\frac{1}{d}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}e(\beta m)+O\left(\frac{X}{\log Z}+Z\right).

By the mean value theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant X^{d}}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}e(\beta m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\int_{1}^{X^{d}}x^{\frac{1}{d}-1}e(\beta x)\mathrm{d}x+O\left(1+\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant X^{d}}m^{\frac{1}{d}-1}(m^{-1}+|\beta|)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{X}e(\beta t^{d})\mathrm{d}t+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\int_{0}^{1}e(X^{d}\beta y^{d})\mathrm{d}y+O(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle XI(X^{d}\beta)+O(1).

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\eta>0
be sufficiently small and

(3.3)

\displaystyle C_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N},\\
\varrho(1/\eta)&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathscr{A}(X,X^{\eta}).\end{cases}

Let

Suppose that

\left|\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{B}\cap[1,X]}e(\alpha x^{d})-C_{\mathfrak{B}}Xq^{-1}S(q,a)I(X^{d}\beta)\right|\ll\begin{cases}\mathfrak{L}^{2}\phantom{pp}&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N},\\

We define the truncated singular series

\mathfrak{S}(B)=\sum_{1\leqslant q\leqslant B}q^{-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\eta>0
be sufficiently small and
C_{\mathfrak{B}}
as in (

Then

\int_{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{+}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})\cdot e\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\theta_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}=C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\mathfrak{I}(\mathfrak{L})+O(X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-1}).

Proof.

First, if
\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{+}
then there exist
0\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q
such that
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1
and

\left|\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\theta}-\frac{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{a}}{q}\right|<C\mathfrak{L}X^{-d},

where
C>0
is a constant depending only on
\mathbf{c}
; therefore,
\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\theta}
, reduced modulo
1

with

\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\gamma}
and
C\mathfrak{L}

and

Thus we may apply Lemma

for any

and
\bm{\theta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{+}

\begin{cases}O(X^{n-dR-1}\mathfrak{L}^{2R+3})&\mbox{if }\mathfrak{B}=\mathbb{N},\\

As a result we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{+}_{\mathfrak{L}}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}S_{\mathbf{c}}(\bm{\theta})\cdot e\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\theta_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}S_{\mathbf{c}}(\mathbf{a}/q)\cdot e_{q}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}a_{i}\right)\times

\displaystyle C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}X^{n}\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|<\mathfrak{L}X^{-d}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}I(X^{d}\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\gamma})\cdot e\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}+O(X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|<\mathfrak{L}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}I(\mathbf{c}\cdot\bm{\gamma})\cdot e\left(-\frac{1}{X^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}+O(X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\mathfrak{I}(\mathfrak{L})+O(X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-1}),

which completes the claim.
∎

4.
Singular series and singular integral

Let us denote

A(q)=q^{-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}S(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})\cdot e_{q}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}a_{i}\right).

We define the singular series as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}A(q)=\lim_{B\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{S}(B).

In the following lemma, we bound the quantity
A(q)
in order to show that the singular series does indeed converge absolutely.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

A(q)\ll q^{-R(\frac{T}{d}-1)}.

Proof.

By
[
Dav05
, Lemma 6.4]
, we have

|S(q,\mathbf{a}.\mathbf{c})|=|S(q/\gcd(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}),\mathbf{a}/\gcd(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}))|\ll\left(\frac{q}{\gcd(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}.

We know that there exist pairwise disjoint sets
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\ldots\mathfrak{D}_{T}
of
R
linearly independent columns of
M
.
Applying Hölder’s inequality, it follows that

(4.2)

\displaystyle|A(q)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle q^{-TR}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{T}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}|S(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})|

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{-TR}\prod_{\ell=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}|S(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})|^{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{-\frac{TR}{d}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\gcd(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})^{-\frac{(d-1)T}{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}.

Let
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant T
and denote
\mathbf{b}=M(\mathfrak{D}_{\ell})^{t}\mathbf{a}
.
Then it is clear that
|\mathbf{b}|\ll q
. Since
M(\mathfrak{D}_{\ell})^{t}
is invertible, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\mathbf{a}\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,\mathbf{a})=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\gcd(q,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c})^{-\frac{(d-1)T}{d}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{b}\leqslant q}\prod_{i=1}^{R}\gcd(q,b_{i})^{-\frac{(d-1)T}{d}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{i}|q\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant R\end{subarray}}(g_{1}\cdots g_{R})^{-\frac{(d-1)T}{d}}\frac{q^{R}}{g_{1}\cdots g_{R}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{R}.

The result follows on substituting this estimate into (
4.2
).
∎

Using this lemma we may extend the truncated singular series.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose

T>\frac{d(R+1)}{R}.

Then

\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(B)+O(B^{1-R(\frac{T}{d}-1)})

for any
B\geqslant 1
. In fact,

\mathfrak{S}=\prod_{p\textnormal{ prime}}\chi(p),

where

\chi(p)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A(p^{k}).

Proof.

The statement is obtained by Lemma
4.1
as follows

|\mathfrak{S}-\mathfrak{S}(B)|\leqslant\sum_{q>B}|A(q)|\ll\sum_{q>B}q^{-R(\frac{T}{d}-1)}\ll B^{1-R(\frac{T}{d}-1)}.

Since
A(q_{1}q_{2})=A(q_{1})A(q_{2})
for any coprime positive integers
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, we also have

\mathfrak{S}=\prod_{p\textnormal{ prime}}\chi(p)

as desired.
∎

Similarly, we define the singular integral as

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{R}}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}I(\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c})\cdot e\left(-\frac{1}{X^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{R}\mu_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}=\lim_{B\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{I}(B).

We may also extend the truncated singular integral.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
T>d
.
Then

\mathfrak{I}(B)=\mathfrak{I}+O(B^{1-\frac{T}{d}})

for any
B>1
.

Proof.

We begin with the bound

(4.4)

\displaystyle I(\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c})=\int_{0}^{1}e(\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}\xi^{d})\mathrm{d}\xi\ll\min\{1,|\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}|^{-1/d}\},

which for instance can be found in
[
Dav05
, p. 21]
or
[
Vau97
, Lemma 2.8]
.
We know that there exist pairwise disjoint sets
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\ldots\mathfrak{D}_{T}
of
R
linearly independent columns of
M
.
It then follows by Hölder’s inequality that

(4.5)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(B)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant B}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\textnormal{Col}(M)}\min\{1,|\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}|^{-1/d}\}\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant B}\prod_{\ell=1}^{T}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\min\{1,|\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}|^{-1/d}\}\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{T}\left(\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant B}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\min\{1,|\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{T}}.

By the change of variable
\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}=M(\mathfrak{D}_{\ell})^{t}\bm{\gamma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{|\bm{\gamma}|\geqslant B}\prod_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathfrak{D}_{\ell}}\min\{1,|\bm{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{c}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\int_{|\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}|\gg B}\min\{1,|\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\cdots\min\{1,|\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}>\cdots>\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\geqslant 0\\
\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}\gg B\end{subarray}}\min\{1,|\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\cdots\min\{1,|\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}|^{-1/d}\}^{T}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle B^{1-\frac{T}{d}}

for each
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant T
. On substituting this estimate into (
4.5
), it follows that

|\mathfrak{I}-\mathfrak{I}(B)|\ll B^{1-\frac{T}{d}}.

∎

Proof of Theorems

On combining Propositions

3.8
, we have

N(\mathfrak{B};X)=C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{n}X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\mathfrak{I}(\mathfrak{L})+O(X^{n-dR}\mathfrak{L}^{-\gamma}),

for some
\gamma>0
.
Lastly, we obtain from Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
that

\mathfrak{S}(\mathfrak{L})\mathfrak{I}(\mathfrak{L})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}+O\left(\mathfrak{L}^{1-R(\frac{T}{d}-1)}+\mathfrak{L}^{1-\frac{T}{d}}\right).

These two equations together give the desired asymptotic formula.
∎
