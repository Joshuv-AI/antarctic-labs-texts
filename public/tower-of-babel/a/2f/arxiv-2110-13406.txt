Towards Optimal Variance Reduction in Online Controlled Experiments
Ying Jin, Shan Ba

Abstract
We study optimal variance reduction solutions for count and ratio metrics in online controlled experiments. Our methods leverage flexible machine learning tools to incorporate covariates that are independent from the treatment but have predictive power for the outcomes, and employ the cross-fitting technique to remove the bias in complex machine learning models. We establish CLT-type asymptotic inference based on our estimators under mild convergence conditions. Our procedures are optimal (efficient) for the corresponding targets as long as the machine learning estimators are consistent, without any requirement for their convergence rates. In complement to the general optimal procedure, we also derive a linear adjustment method for ratio metrics as a special case that is computationally efficient and can flexibly incorporate any pre-treatment covariates. We evaluate the proposed variance reduction procedures with comprehensive simulation studies and provide practical suggestions regarding commonly adopted assumptions in computing ratio metrics. When tested on real online experiment data from LinkedIn, the proposed optimal procedure for ratio metrics can reduce up to 80% of variance compared to the standard difference-in-mean estimator and also further reduce up to 30% of variance compared to the CUPED approach by going beyond linearity and incorporating a large number of extra covariates.



Keywords
: A/B test, randomized experiments, variance reduction, semiparametric efficiency, causal inference, covariate adjustment, ratio metrics.

1
Introduction

Online controlled experiments, also known as A/B tests, are extensively used in tech companies to assess the impacts of product changes on business metrics.
Although online experiments can involve millions of users, their sensitivity is still a major challenge because
the treatment effect is often small
compared to the noise.
Failing to detect even small differences in key metrics can have significant business implications
(
Kohavi et al. 2020
)
and it is crucial to develop
powerful
statistical tools to quickly capture nonzero effects
with fewer samples
and shorter
experimentation turn-around time.

To improve the sensitivity of online controlled experiments, variance reduction techniques are commonly used which leverage relevant covariates to
remove explainable variance in
the outcomes.
For count metrics,
many variance reduction solutions have been developed in the literature based on classical linear adjustment

(
Yang and Tsiatis 2001
;
Freedman 2008
;
Lin 2013
;
Deng et al. 2013
)

and
machine learning tools
(
Hosseini and Najmi 2019
;
Guo et al. 2021
)
.
However,
the optimality of
variance reduction procedures has not been thoroughly studied and using suboptimal procedures may lead
to unnecessary costs.
In addition to count metrics, online experiments also often involve ratio metrics
(
Deng et al. 2017
)
whose variance reduction is more complex but much less studied. As we will discuss in Section
2.1
, ratio metrics are similar to the setting of cluster randomized experiments. Existing variance reduction solutions for ratio metric are mostly extensions to those of count metrics
(
Deng et al. 2013
)
and
a rigorous
statistical framework for (optimal) variance reduction
remains absent.

In this paper, we target at the natural but unanswered question:

With access to a set of covariates that are independent of the treatment in the experiment, what is the optimal estimator for comparing the outcomes of treatment and control groups?

We study the optimal variance reduction procedures for both
count and ratio metrics that are ubiquitous
in online controlled experiments in the industry.
The optimality we focus on is
semiparametric efficiency
(
Bickel et al. 1993
)
.
Given an estimand that
arises from the comparison of experiment outcomes,
our goal is to
develop an estimator with the smallest
asymptotic variance among all asymptotically unbiased estimators.
We propose procedures that reduce variance
of treatment effect estimation by incorporating flexible ML regressors with rigorous statistical guarantee.
Based on classical semiparametric statistics theory,
we establish the optimality of our procedures under mild conditions.
For ratio metrics,
in addition to the optimal (and perhaps nonlinear)
approaches,
we also propose a computationally efficient
linear adjustment method
which, to the best of our knowledge, are not available in the literature.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we introduce the definition of count and ratio metrics in online experiments and provide an overview of
the related literature.
We develop the variance reduction procedures, asymptotic inference and optimality properties for count metrics in Section
3
, and for ratio metrics in Section
4
and
5
. Section
6
is devoted to simulation studies and Section
7
illustrates the performance of our methods using real online experiments from LinkedIn.
Finally, we give concluding remarks in Section
8
.

2
Problem setting and related work

The
randomization unit
and the
analysis unit

are two important concepts for online experiments. The most common online experiments are randomized by users, while sometimes the experiments can also use alternative randomization units.
For example, in online experiments for enterprise products, the randomization unit typically needs to be a cluster of users (such as
an enterprise account or contract) because users in the same contract must have access to the same product feature.
In another scenario where it is infeasible to identify/track users in a web service, the randomization unit is often chosen to be a service request or a pageview.
The analysis unit of an experiment, on the other hand, may not necessarily be the same as the experiment’s randomization unit. For example, in a cluster randomized experiment for enterprise products, we can choose the analysis unit at either the cluster level (e.g., revenue per cluster) or the individual user level (e.g., revenue per user). Depending on whether the randomization
and the analysis units coincide, metrics in online experiments can be classified into different types, for which different analysis procedures are needed.

2.1
Count and ratio metrics

To formalize the
problem setting, we adopt the potential outcomes framework
and follow the standard Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)
(
Imbens and Rubin 2015
)
so that there is no interference among
the randomization units.
We also take a super-population perspective
where the randomization units can be viewed as i.i.d., but the
analysis units may not.

Count metrics.

The most common metrics are count metrics, whose analysis unit matches the randomization unit in the experiment. For example, in online experiments that are randomized by users, count metrics are those defined on the user level such as revenue per user, pageviews per user, number of clicks per user, etc. Because the analysis units
are the same as the the i.i.d. randomization units,
the variance of count metrics
can be estimated directly by the sample variance formula.

Formally, suppose there are
n
units in the experiment,
for which we have access to i.i.d. observations
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},T_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
from an unknown distribution, where

X_{i}
is the pre-treatment covariates,

Y_{i}
is the measured metric and
T_{i}
is the treatment indicator.
Following the standard practice
of online controlled experiments,
we assume the treatment indicators
T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}
Bernoulli(
p
)
for
p\in(0,1)
and
are independent of all other information.
The units in the treated group (
T_{i}=1
) receive the treatment,
and the units in the control group (
T_{i}=0
) do not.
The outcomes
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are then measured
after the experiment.
Under the potential outcome framework,
each unit has two potential outcomes
(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0))
,
where
Y_{i}(1)
is the outcome that unit
i
exhibits
under treatment, and
Y_{i}(0)
is that under control.
For each unit, we only observe one potential outcome
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})

under SUTVA
(
Imbens and Rubin 2015
)
.
Typically, count metrics are aggregated by sample means
such as
\frac{1}{\sum_{i}T_{i}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}Y_{i}
for
the units in the treatment or control groups,
and the difference between two groups
shows the causal effect of the treatment.
The corresponding estimand is

\displaystyle\tau=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big],

where the expectations are taken with respect to
the distribution that the units are from.

Ratio metrics.

When the analysis unit is at a lower level
than the experiments’ randomization unit, the metric of interest is a ratio metric (because it can be expressed as a ratio of two count metrics), whose
unique structure requires distinct techniques for
inference.

In user-randomized experiments, the click-through rate (average number of clicks per pageview, computed as number of clicks /number of pageviews) is an example of ratio metric
whose analysis unit is at the pageview level.
Because the experiment is randomized by users and
different pageviews of the same user are correlated,
only the outcomes aggregated at the user level (randomization unit level) can be viewed as i.i.d..
For analysis purposes, the click-through rate
can be equivalently viewed as a ratio of
two user-level count metrics
(number of clicks per user
/number of pageviews per user).
Consider another experiment for enterprise products which needs to be randomized by contracts to ensure that all users within each contract receive the same treatment assignment. Revenue per contract is a standard count metric in this experiment, but in practice we are often more interested in analyzing revenue per user, which is a ratio metric. Because only the contracts are i.i.d. and users under each contract are not independent, variance of the revenue per user cannot be directly calculated by the sample variance formula. Instead, by viewing revenue per user as a “ratio” of two contract-level count metrics (revenue per contract/number of users per contract), we can estimate its variance based on the delta method
(
Deng et al. 2017
)
.
More broadly, this setting is similar to the cluster randomized experiments in causal inference
(
Green and Vavreck 2008
;
Middleton and Aronow 2015
)
as each randomization unit can be viewed as a cluster of analysis units.

Formally, let
i=1,\dots,n
be i.i.d. randomization units in the experiment from some distribution
\mathbb{P}
. They are randomly allocated to treated or control groups, indicated by
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Here we assume
T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Bernoulli}(p)
for some
p\in(0,1)
and are independent from all other information.
Suppose two count metrics
Y_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are measured for each randomization unit
i
(e.g.,
Y_{i}
is the total number of clicks of user
i
and
Z_{i}
is the total number of pageviews of user
i
). The ratio metrics (e.g., click through rate) for the treated and control groups are defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}Y_{i}}{\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}Z_{i}}=~\frac{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}Y_{i}}{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}Z_{i}},\quad\frac{\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Y_{i}}{\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Z_{i}}=~\frac{\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Y_{i}}{\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Z_{i}},

where
Y_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are aggregated
across all randomization units
in the treatment/control groups first before taking the ratio.
Because randomization units are i.i.d.,
the law of large numbers implies that the
population-level comparison
target (the limit
of difference in ratio metrics)
is
the difference in ratios of expectations

\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=1]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=1]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=0]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=0]}.

In practice, the expectation of
Z_{i}
is always nonzero and hence both the population-level and sample-level ratios are well defined.
Note that we do not define ratio metrics for the treatment and control groups as
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\frac{Y_{i}}{Z_{i}}
and
\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\frac{Y_{i}}{Z_{i}}
, because some individual
Z_{i}
may be zero and the corresponding individual ratio
Y_{i}/Z_{i}
is not well-defined.
The previous definition of ratio metrics can actually be viewed as a weighted average of
Y_{i}/Z_{i}
with
the weights proportional to
Z_{i}
(e.g.,
giving more weights to more active users):

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Y_{i}}{\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Z_{i}}=\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\underbrace{\frac{Z_{i}}{\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Z_{i}}}_{\textrm{"weights"}}\times\underbrace{\frac{Y_{i}}{Z_{i}}}_{\textrm{ratio for randomization unit $i$}}.

Ratio metrics can also be grouped into two types depending on their assumptions:
(1) The first type allows both the metric
Z
in the denominator and the metric
Y
in the numerator to be changed by the treatment.
The above click-through rate example belongs to this type.
(2) The second type assumes that the metric
Z
in the denominator is a random variable associated with the experiment unit, but is
not
changed by the treatment.

Deng et al. 2017
refers to (2)
as the
stable denominator assumption
(SDA in the following).
SDA is plausible when the ratio metric mainly uses
Z
in the denominator as a normalization factor
to standardize the changes in the numerator metric
Y
.
For example, when we are interested in a user-level metric “revenue per user” but the experiment needs to be randomized by clusters of users, we can choose
Z_{i}
as the number of active users in an cluster.
This assumption always needs to be checked in practice using a separate test on
Z_{i}
.
If the SDA is violated, the ratio metric itself is hard to interpret: we do not know whether an increase in the ratio is good (e.g., due to an increase in revenue) or bad (e.g., due to a decrease in the number of active users).
For instance, a bad treatment which decreases the number of active users in the contracts can instead yield a higher revenue-per-user ratio as the remaining users most likely are the most active ones.
When the SDA is violated, the analysis should also emphasize on studying the count metric change in the numerator
Y
and in the denominator
Z
separately before drawing conclusions.

We now define the estimands for the two types of ratio metrics under the potential outcomes framework.
For type (1) ratio metric, each unit has potential outcomes (
Y_{i}(1)
,
Y_{i}(0)
,
Z_{i}(1)
,
Z_{i}(0)
), where we observe
(Y_{i},Z_{i})=(Y_{i}(T_{i}),Z_{i}(T_{i}))
.
The estimand is

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]},

(2.1)

where the expectation is with respect to
the distribution the units are from.
In the type (2) ratio metric, each unit has potential outcomes
(Y(1),Y(0))
so that
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})
while
Z
is a plain random variable, and the estimand is

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}.

(2.2)

In this work, we will consider both types of estimands for the ratio metrics and offer practical recommendations. As we will show in our numerical experiments,
the assumption (2) should be made with caution.

2.2
Related work

This work falls into the general randomized experiment setting in causal inference
(
Imbens and Rubin 2015
)
,
whereas we study the post-hoc variance reduction
instead of the
experimental design strategies.
In addition, our method leverages
machine learning models to
learn the conditional relation between the potential outcomes
and the covariates,
which is similar to but with distinct goal from
the investigation
of treatment effect heterogeneity in causal inference

(
Athey and Imbens 2016
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2017
;
Imai and Ratkovic 2013
;
Künzel et al. 2019
;
Nie and Wager 2020
;
Wager and Athey 2018
)
: we fit
the conditional mean functions of the potential outcomes to
remove
explainable variations in the outcomes, rather than
to investigate the heterogeneity of the conditional treatment effect.

This work adds to the rich literature of
variance reduction with covariate adjustment
in randomized experiments.
Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) has a long history of
application in physical experiments
(
Wu and Hamada 2009
)
.
The classic linear regression adjustment
(
Yang and Tsiatis 2001
;
Freedman 2008
;
Lin 2013
)
is shown to work even when the linear model is misspecified.

Deng et al. 2013
proposes to use pre-treatment data as the regression covariates and the corresponding variance reduction method, called CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data), has been widely used in the industry.
The blossom of ML research also inspires
a line of recent work on using ML tools for variance reduction, including
Hosseini and Najmi 2019
;
Cohen and Fogarty 2020
;
Guo et al. 2021
and the references therein,
but few of them focus on the optimality of the variance reduction procedure.
In particular, among the existing works for count metrics,
both
Guo et al. 2021
and
Cohen and Fogarty 2020
uses predicted outcomes from ML estimators as covariates in a linear regression adjustment.
Their intuitions are to create more relevant features with ML tools to improve upon CUPED, but not aimed at optimality.
As would be discussed in details at the end of Section
3.3
, careful considerations are needed for optimality and the method in
Guo et al. 2021
can not be semiparametrically efficient in general.

Hosseini and Najmi 2019
discusses
ratio metrics and provides methods
to obtain unbiased estimators with machine learning tools.
However, to our knowledge,
rigorous and explicit statistical inference guarantees and
the optimality of the procedures are not provided.

Our approach for count metrics is asymptotically
the same as the augmented inverse propensity weighting (AIPW) estimator
(
Robins et al. 1994
)
,
whose well-established semiparametric efficiency
(
Hahn 1998
)
result
forms the basis of our optimality guarantee.
For count metrics, we develop valid inference procedures
in randomized experiments (
L_{2}

convergence in probability to any fixed function),
which is much weaker than
the pointwise convergence condition
to true conditional mean functions
as is often required in observational studies
(
Nichols 2007
;
Schuler and Rose 2017
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)

or the investigation of heterogeneous treatment effects
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2017
;
Künzel et al. 2019
;
Athey and Wager 2019
;
Nie and Wager 2020
;
Kennedy 2020
)
,
and also differs from the traditional approach of Donsker conditions and empirical process theory
(
Andrews 1994
;
Van der Vaart 2000
;
Van Der Vaart et al. 1996
)
to
control errors in estimating nuisance components (the conditional mean functions in our setting).

Our estimators for ratio metrics
as well as its inference and optimality results
are new to the literature.
Intuitively, they all have a fit-and-debias flavor related to the AIPW estimator
(
Robins et al. 1994
)
.
The optimality theories we develop for ratio metrics finds roots in
the classical semiparametric efficiency theory
(
Bickel et al. 1993
;
Hahn 1998
)
.

3
Variance reduction for count metrics

We begin our discussion with
intuitions on how machine learning can assist
variance reduction for count metrics.
To estimate the treatment effect
\tau=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]
,
an ideal “estimator” is

\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{ideal}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0))

which directly compares pairs of potential outcomes,
although it is not rigorously an estimator since

Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
are not simultaneously observable.
This “estimator” is ideal for two reasons: (1) large sample size: both the treated and the control group leverage
n
observations; (2) paired comparison: the variance of the individual treatment effect
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
is typically smaller than the variances of
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
alone.
A standard Difference-in-Mean (DiM) estimator
compares the averages of the two groups:

\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{DiM}}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}Y_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Y_{i}(0),

which has larger variance than
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{ideal}}

by
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
While
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{ideal}}
is not computable,
a natural idea is to impute the unobserved potential outcomes
based on covariates
X_{i}
.
Let

\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\cdot)
be some machine learning
predictors for
Y(1)
and
Y(0)
based on
X
, respectively.
By naively plugging in the predictors, we obtain

\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{Y}_{i}(1)-\widehat{Y}_{i}(0)\big),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i}(1)=\begin{cases}Y_{i}(1),\quad&T_{i}=1\\
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i}),\quad&T_{i}=0\end{cases},\qquad\widehat{Y}_{i}(0)=\begin{cases}Y_{i}(0),\quad&T_{i}=0\\
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}),\quad&T_{i}=1\end{cases}.

The simplicity of
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}

comes with two problems. First, without a well-posed parametric model, the convergence rate of
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\cdot)
is often slower than
n^{-1/2}
,
hence
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}
can introduce “
regressor bias
”
that is even larger than the variance.
Secondly, another “
double-dipping bias
” occurs as the same dataset is used both for model-fitting and for prediction,
hence

\{\widehat{\mu}_{w}(X_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are no longer independent copies, posing challenges for statistical analysis.
We will develop debiasing techniques for these issues.

We also note that
the above intuition
is related to
Guo and Basse 2021
;
Cohen and Fogarty 2020

where machine learning predictions
are used to impute the counterfactuals.
However, our work takes a specific approach
that aims at optimality.
The conditions for valid inference also differ from them.

3.1
Estimation procedure

Our proposed variance reduction procedure
improves upon the naive
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}
in two aspects: it eliminates the “regressor bias” by adding a de-biasing term and employs the cross-fitting technique
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
to
correct for the “double-dipping bias”.

The first step is to randomly split
the original dataset
\mathcal{D}=(Y_{i},T_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
into
K
(roughly) equal-sized folds
\{\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\}_{1\leq k\leq K}
with sizes
n_{k}=|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}|
, each fold containing
n_{k,t}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}T_{i}
treated samples and
n_{k,c}=n_{k}-n_{k,t}
control samples.
Here the random splitting is conducted separately in the treated and control groups to achieve
balanced numbers of treated samples
across folds. In practice,
K=2
generally works well.

The second step is cross-fitting
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
. Let
\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}=\mathcal{D}\backslash\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
denote all data after holding out the
k
-th fold.
For each
k\in[K]
, fit a function
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{(k)}(x)
for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1){\,|\,}X=x]
using
\big\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\colon T_{i}=1,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\big\}
, and fit a function
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{(k)}(x)
for
\mathbb{E}[Y(0){\,|\,}X=x]
using
\big\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\colon T_{i}=0,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\big\}
. Then apply the fitted functions to the held-out samples in
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
to generate out-of-sample predictions
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{(k)}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{(k)}(X_{i})
for
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
.
These out-of-sample predictions are independent conditional on
\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}
, helping alleviate the “double-dipping bias”.

Finally, we estimate
\tau
via

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}~\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)},\quad\text{where}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=1,\\
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=0,\\
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big).

As will be shown later, this estimator is finite-sample unbiased and one could also obtain similar performance by the following estimator

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big),

(3.2)

which might have an
O(1/n)
bias from unequal fold sizes.
Note that
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
adds a de-biasing term to the naive plug-in estimator

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}\approx\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}+\frac{n_{c}}{n\cdot n_{t}}\sum_{i~{\scriptsize\textrm{treated}}}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{n_{t}}{n\cdot n_{c}}\sum_{i~{\scriptsize\textrm{control}}}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big).

In this equation,
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{plug-in}}
uses machine learning tools to
impute the unobserved potential outcomes, and the remaining two terms serve as corrections to the “regressor bias” in fitting the mean functions.
As will be seen in Section
4
and
5
,
the debiasing technique
is also useful when developing estimators
for ratio metrics with careful considerations on the imputation of the predicted values.
The proposed procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

\widehat{\mu}_{w}(x)\to\mu_{w}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y(w){\,|\,}X=x]
for
w\in\{0,1\}
,
one can show that the asymptotic variance of
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Var(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}})\approx\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\Var\big(\mu_{1}(X)-\mu_{0}(X)\big)}_{\textrm{(i) predictable part}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\Var\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1}(X)\big)+\frac{1}{n_{c}}\Var\big(Y(0)-\mu_{0}(X)\big)}_{\textrm{(ii) irreducible variance}}

In the above decomposition, the (i) predictable part echoes our intuitions of imputing
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
with predicted values, thereby inheriting the advantages of
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{ideal}}
of increased sample size and decreased single-term variance when the treatment effects are typically small.
In the meantime, (i) is the variance of the projection of
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
on the
X
-space, which is
the best effort to predict
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
with
X_{i}
.
The (ii) is the variance that cannot be eliminated by the information of
X
embodied in the
n_{t}
treated samples and
n_{c}
control samples.
In other words, one might view
\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}
as the best efforts towards exploiting the information in
X
.
As the variance in (i) is deflated by a larger factor
n
,
predictors with smaller variance in (ii), i.e., more accurate ML predictors
\mu_{w}(x)
, are desirable.

Remark 3.1
.

For the case of count metrics, our estimator is
asymptotically equivalent to the AIPW estimator
(
Robins et al. 1994
)
, whose
semiparametric efficiency
(
Hahn 1998
)
directly implies the
optimality of our estimator.
As will be shown in Section
3.2
and
3.3
, the differences here include 1) we establish
valid inference as long as the ML estimator
converges to deterministic functions
without consistency,
and 2) we establish the optimality under a
weaker condition of
L_{2}
convergence in probability.
Our debiasing term
is also related to the “prediction unbiasedness” condition in
Guo and Basse 2021
.

When we restrict
\widehat{\mu}_{w}
in Algorithm
1
to be a linear function of
x
,
our estimator is connected to the well-known CUPED estimator
(
Deng et al. 2013
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{CUPED}}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\theta}(X_{i}-\bar{X})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\theta}(X_{i}-\bar{X})\big),

(3.3)

where
\widehat{\theta}
is the OLS projection of
Y_{i}
(the pooled outcomes of control and treated groups) on (centered) pre-treatment metric
X_{i}
. The connection can be easily established as follows. By replacing
\mu_{w}(x)
with the linear predictor
\beta_{w}^{\top}x
(
w
= 0 or 1) where
\beta_{w}
is the least-square linear coefficient of
Y(w)
on
X
, the proposed optimal estimator

\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
in (
3.2
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\beta_{1}^{\top}(X_{i}-\bar{X})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\beta_{0}^{\top}(X_{i}-\bar{X})\big).

This improves upon CUPED by running separate regressions in the treated and control groups. This estimator enjoys the agnostic property
(
Lin 2013
)
: it leads to no larger variance than the diff-in-mean estimator without any assumptions, while the vanilla CUPED in (
3.3
) may not. In such low-dimensional case, due to the low complexity of linear function classes, the double-dipping bias is not a concern and cross-fitting is not necessary.
But for high-dimensional linear regression such as the LASSO,
it is important to use our proposed algorithm to cross-fit and debias with
\widehat{\mu}
being the LASSO estimator, otherwise the bias induced by
the high dimensional regression could be of the same order as the variance.

3.2
Unbiasedness and asymptotic inference

In this part, we establish the finite-sample unbiasedness and asymptotic inference for our procedures for count metrics.
In the following theorem, we show that
\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}
is unbiased and

\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
has a negligible
O(1/n)
bias due to potentially
unequally-sized folds.
Such finite-sample unbiasedness holds for any machine learning regressors.

Theorem 3.2

(Finite-sample unbiasedness)
.

Suppose
(X_{i},Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}

are independent of the treatment assignments
{\mathcal{T}}=\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}{\,|\,}{\mathcal{T}}]=\tau

for any
n
. Furthermore,
suppose there exists an absolute constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\big|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{(k)}(X_{i}){\,|\,}\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}]\big|\leq c_{0}

and
\big|\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w)]\big|\leq c_{0}
for all
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Then
\big|\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}{\,|\,}{\mathcal{T}}]-\tau\big|\leq c/\min\{n_{t},n_{c}\}
for some absolute constant
c>0
.

We now study the asymptotic inference for our estimators.
We assume the convergence of
\widehat{\mu}_{1}
,
\widehat{\mu}_{0}
to fixed functions (this is mild because we do not require it to converge to the true conditional mean functions) and the treatment assignment mechanism.

Assumption 3.3

(Convergence)
.

There exists two fixed functions
\mu^{*}_{1}(\cdot)
and
\mu^{*}_{0}(\cdot)
, so that
\|\widehat{\mu}^{(k)}_{1}-\mu^{*}_{1}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
and
\|\widehat{\mu}^{(k)}_{0}-\mu^{*}_{0}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for all
k\in[K]
.

Assumption 3.4

(Treatment assignment mechanism)
.

Assume
n_{t}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
for some fixed
p\in(0,1)
, so that
n_{k,t}/n_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
for all
k\in[K]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Asymptotic confidence intervals)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.3
and
3.4
hold. Then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}-\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2})
and
\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}-\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{p}\Var\big(Y_{i}(1)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{1-p}\Var\big(Y_{i}(0)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\big).

Furthermore, define the variance estimator

\widehat{\sigma}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}=\frac{n}{n_{t}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}(A_{i}-\bar{A})^{2}+\frac{n}{n_{c}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-T_{i})(B_{i}-\bar{B})^{2},

where
\bar{A}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}A_{i}
,
\bar{B}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-T_{i})B_{i}
, and

A_{i}=Y_{i}(1)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}),

B_{i}=Y_{i}(0)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}).

Then
\widehat{\sigma}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\sigma_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{2}
,
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\textrm{Debias}}/\sqrt{n}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\textrm{Debias}}/\sqrt{n}
are both asymptotically valid
(1-\alpha)
confidence intervals for
\tau=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]
.

3.3
Optimality: semiparametric efficiency

Returning to our motivating question, we establish the optimality of our procedure, building upon semiparametric statistics theory
(
Bickel et al. 1993
;
Robins et al. 1994
;
Hahn 1998
)
. We are to show that the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}
is no larger than any ‘regular’ estimators (roughly speaking, those asymptotically linear ones with the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i},Y_{i},T_{i})+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
for some function
\phi
, including the ones obtained from linear regression as in
Deng et al. 2013
;
Guo et al. 2021
;
Cohen and Fogarty 2020
).
Due to the limit of paper length, we omit the formal backgrounds on semiparametric efficiency in the main text and defer the discussions to
Appendix
E.1
,
which includes notions of regular non-parametric space and efficient influence functions.

We impose the following condition on the consistency of the estimators
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(\cdot),~\widehat{\mu}_{0}(\cdot)
.

Assumption 3.6

(Consistency)
.

Let
\mu_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(1){\,|\,}X=x]
and
\mu_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(0){\,|\,}X=x]
be the two true mean functions. Suppose
\|\widehat{\mu}^{(k)}_{w}-\mu_{w}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for
w\in\{0,1\}
and all
k\in[K]
.

Assumption
3.6
is a mild condition
without any requirement of the convergence rates.
This is in contrast to
the conditions on convergence rates (though doubly-robust properties help relax them to lower orders) for observational studies
(
Nichols 2007
;
Schuler and Rose 2017
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)

where the propensity score function
e(x)=\mathbb{P}(T=1{\,|\,}X=x)
also needs to be estimated. Also, we do not need the convergence to be point-wise.

Theorem 3.7

(Semiparametric efficiency)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.4
and
3.6
hold. Then the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
in Theorem
3.5

is the semiparametric variance bound for
\tau=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]
.

Theorem
3.7
, together with Theorem
3.5
,
indicates that the covariates
X_{i}
should
be chosen to be powerful predictors for the outcomes,
such that
\Var(Y(w)-\mu_{w}(X))
is relatively small for
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Since our estimator is finite-sample unbiased conditional on
{\mathcal{T}}
, if we view
{\mathcal{T}}
as fixed,
it has the smallest variance among all asymptotically unbiased regular estimators.
This result might be of interest for two-sample mean test
and completely randomized experiments as well;
in the latter case,
units in the treated and control
groups still have i.i.d. potential outcomes
if we assume the units are i.i.d. before treatment assignment.

We take a moment here to compare Theorem
3.7

to other machine-learning empowered methods in the literature
(
Cohen and Fogarty 2020
;
Guo et al. 2021
;
Hosseini and Najmi 2019
)
.
As indicated by (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), our estimator is essentially a linear combination of
Y_{i}
,
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})
that achieves smallest variance.
Thus, it can be obtained from a linear regression
when Assumption
3.6
holds.
Referring to (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), for optimality, one needs to include both
\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})
in the regression terms and run the regression separately on treated and control groups. Therefore, although machine learning helps to exploit nonlinearity, the regression in
Guo et al. 2021

with one single predictor for
Y_{i}
(not separately for
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
) would not achieve optimality in general, unless
\mu_{1}(x)
and
\mu_{0}(x)
are completely colinear. This issue is the same for
Hosseini and Najmi 2019
where only one predictor is used.
The method in
Cohen and Fogarty 2020
may be optimal,
which, however,
requires a slightly stronger condition of
L_{4}
-distance convergence.

4
Variance reduction for ratio metrics: without SDA

We now
study variance reduction procedures for ratio metrics introduced in Section
2.1
whose denominator
Z_{i}=Z_{i}(T_{i})
can be changed by the treatment (without SDA).
The results are new to the literature while sharing similar ideas to
our results for count metrics.

4.1
Estimation procedure

The first step
is the
K
-fold sample splitting for
\mathcal{D}=(Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

as introduced in Section
3.1
.
The second step, cross-fitting, needs
careful consideration: for each
k\in[K]
, we use the data
\big\{(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i})\colon T_{i}=1,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\big\}
to obtain estimators
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y,(k)}(x)
for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1){\,|\,}X=x]
and
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z,(k)}(x)
for
\mathbb{E}[Z(1){\,|\,}X=x]
. Likewise, we use
\big\{(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i})\colon T_{i}=0,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\big\}
to obtain
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y,(k)}(x)
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z,(k)}(x)
. Then, we
calculate predictions
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y,(k)}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z,(k)}(X_{i})
for all
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
,
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Finally, we estimate
\delta
in (
2.1
) by

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}},

(4.1)

where
A_{i}=\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{\widehat{p}}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})\big)
,

B_{i}=\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{\widehat{p}}\big(Z_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z}(X_{i})\big)
,

C_{i}=\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\widehat{p}}\big(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y}(X_{i})\big)
, and

D_{i}=\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\widehat{p}}\big(Z_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z}(X_{i})\big)
for
\widehat{p}=n_{t}/n
.
The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
2
.
Compared to

\widehat{\delta}_{{\textrm{DiM}}}:=\frac{\sum_{i~\textrm{treatment}}Y_{i}}{\sum_{i~\textrm{treatment}}Z_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Y_{i}}{\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}Z_{i}},

our estimator
substitutes the sample means of the treated and control groups with average of the fit-and-debias predictions for all
n
units, which is similar to our estimator for count metrics.

Input: Dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(Y_{i},X_{i},Z_{i},T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, number of folds
K
.

2:

Randomly split
\mathcal{D}
into
K
folds
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,K
.

3:

for

k=1,\dots,K

do

4:

Use all
(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i})
with
T_{i}=1
and
i\notin\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
to obtain
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y,(k)}(x)
and
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z,(k)}(x)
;

5:

Use all
(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i})
with
T_{i}=0
and
i\notin\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
to obtain
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y,(k)}(x)
and
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z,(k)}(x)
;

6:

Compute
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y,(k)}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z}(X_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z,(k)}(X_{i})
for all
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
and
w\in\{0,1\}
.

7:

end

for

Compute estimator
\widehat{\delta}
on
\mathcal{D}
according to (
4.1
).

4.2
Asymptotic inference

The analysis of ratio metrics is naturally asymptotic (
Deng et al. 2017
), and thus
we focus more on the asymptotic properties of the proposed estimator.

We impose the following conditions on the treatment assignment mechanism and convergence of cross-fitted functions. In Assumption
4.2
, we only require the convergence of estimated functions to deterministic functions, not the true conditional mean functions. This is a mild condition that holds for general machine learning regression methods.

Assumption 4.1

(Data Generating Process)
.

(X_{i},Z_{i}(0),Z_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
and the treatment assignments
T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\textrm{Bernoulli}(p)
are independent of all other random variables.

Assumption 4.2

(Convergence)
.

There exists some fixed fuctions
\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(\cdot)
,
\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(\cdot),\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(\cdot)
,
\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(\cdot)
, so that both
\|\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y,(k)}-\mu_{w,Y}^{*}\|_{2},\|\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z,(k)}-\mu_{w,Z}^{*}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for
w\in\{0,1\}
.

In preparation for inferential guarantees,
we define the influence function

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{i}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]^{2}}B_{i}^{*}-\frac{C_{i}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]^{2}}D_{i}^{*},

(4.2)

where
A_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big),

B_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]-\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big)
,

C_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]-\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big)
,
and
D_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]-\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big)
.
Here
\widetilde{\mu}_{w,Y}^{*}(X_{i})=\mu_{w,Y}^{*}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{w,Y}^{*}(X_{i})]
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{w,Z}^{*}(X_{i})=\mu_{w,Z}^{*}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{w,Z}^{*}(X_{i})]
,
w\in\{0,1\}
are the centered limiting functions. The following theorem establishes the asymptotic confidence intervals.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
hold, and let
\widehat{\delta}
be the output of Algorithm
2
. Then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\delta}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{\delta}^{2}=\Var(\phi_{\delta}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i}))
.
Moreover, define the variance estimator

\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(d_{1,i}-\bar{d}_{1}\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(d_{0,i}-\bar{d}_{0}\big)^{2}
, where

d_{1,i}=-\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}\bar{Z}(1)}\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})+\frac{n}{n_{t}\bar{Z}(1)}Y_{i}+\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}}\frac{\bar{Y}(1)}{\bar{Z}(1)^{2}}\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z}(X_{i})-\frac{n}{n_{t}}\frac{\bar{Y}(1)}{\bar{Z}(1)^{2}}Z_{i}-\frac{1}{\bar{Z}(0)}\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y}(X_{i})+\frac{\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}(0)^{2}}\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z}(X_{i}),
and

d_{0,i}=\frac{1}{\bar{Z}(1)}\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\frac{\bar{Y}(1)}{\bar{Z}(1)^{2}}\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z}(X_{i})-\frac{n}{n_{c}\bar{Z}(0)}Y_{i}+\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}\bar{Z}(0)}\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y}(X_{i})+\frac{n}{n_{c}}\frac{\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}(0)^{2}}Z_{i}-\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}}\frac{\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}(0)^{2}}\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z}(X_{i}).

Then
\widehat{\delta}\pm\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}\cdot z_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt{n}
is an asymptotically valid
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
\delta
.

4.3
Optimality

Our estimator is optimal (semiparametric efficient) when the estimated functions are consistent.
We begin with mild convergence assumptions.

Assumption 4.4
.

\|\widehat{\mu}^{Y,(k)}_{w}-\mu_{w,Y}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
and
\|\widehat{\mu}^{Z,(k)}_{0}-\mu_{w,Z}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for all
k\in[K]
and
w\in\{0,1\}
, where

\mu_{w,Y}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(w){\,|\,}X=x]
and
\mu_{w,Z}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Z(w){\,|\,}X=x]
.

We are to show that the efficient influence function for the estimation of
\delta
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})=\frac{A_{i}^{{\dagger}}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]^{2}}B_{i}^{{\dagger}}-\frac{C_{i}^{{\dagger}}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]^{2}}D_{i}^{{\dagger}},

(4.3)

where

A_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\mu_{1,Y}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{1,Y}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]
,

B_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\mu_{1,Z}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(Z_{i}-\mu_{1,Z}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]
,

C_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{0,Y}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]
,

D_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(Z_{i}-\mu_{0,Z}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]
.
The following theorem shows that under consistency, the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\delta}
coincides with the variance of
\phi_{\delta,{\dagger}}
, which is also the efficient variance bound. Thus, the optimality of the proposed procedure is established under appropriate conditions.

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.1
and
4.4
hold. Then it holds that
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\delta,{\dagger}}^{2})
, where

\sigma_{\delta,{\dagger}}^{2}=\Var\big(\phi_{\delta,{\dagger}}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big)
(c.f. (
4.2
))
is the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound for
\delta=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]/\mathbb{E}[Z(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]/\mathbb{E}[Z(0)]
.

Theorem
4.5
is based on
a more general result (Theorem
E.7
in the appendix) in situations
where the treatment assignment might depend on
X
.
It may be of independent interest for
efficient estimation of ratio metrics in
stratified experiments and observational studies.

4.4
Special case: optimal linear adjustment for ratio metrics

As a special case of Alg.
2
,
we derive an optimal
linear adjustment
method
for ratio metrics, which is computationally efficient and has several advantages over the existing approaches in the literature.

We suppose
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for some fixed
p
(with intercept). In Alg.
2
,
let
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y,(k)}(x)=\widehat{\beta}_{Y,w,(k)}^{\top}x
for all
w\in\{0,1\}
and
k=1,\dots,K
, where
\widehat{\beta}_{Y,w,(k)}
is the OLS coefficient of
\{Y_{i}\colon T_{i}=w,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\}
on
\{X_{i}\colon T_{i}=w,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}\}
. The fitted function
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z,(k)}(x)
can be similarly obtained.
As we have discussed at the end of Section
3.1
,
in this fixed-
p
setting, because of
the low complexity of linear function classes,
the cross-fitting step may not be needed and we can further simplify the approach by letting
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Y}(x)=\widehat{\beta}_{Y,w}^{\top}x
in Algorithm
2
,
where
\widehat{\beta}_{Y,w}
is the empirical OLS coefficient of
\{Y_{i}\colon T_{i}=w\}

on
\{X_{i}\colon T_{i}=w\}
for
w\in\{0,1\}
, i.e.,
running linear regressions separately on treated and control groups
without sample splitting.
The imputations
\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{Z}(x)
can be similarly obtained.
One can show that the two estimators with or without sample splitting are asymptotically equivalent due to the convergence property of linear regression coefficients.
Both estimators admit the asymptotic linear expansion (up to additive constants)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\alpha_{1,*}^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha_{0,*}^{\top}X_{i}+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(\Gamma_{i}-\alpha_{1,*}^{\top}X_{i})-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(\Gamma_{i}-\alpha_{0,*}^{\top}X_{i})\Big)+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}),

where
\Gamma_{i}=\frac{T_{i}Y_{i}}{\mathbb{E}[Z(1)]}-\frac{T_{i}Z_{i}\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z(1)]^{2}}-\frac{(1-T_{i})Y_{i}}{\mathbb{E}[Z(0)]}+\frac{(1-T_{i})Z_{i}\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z(0)]^{2}}
,
and
\alpha_{1,*}
,
\alpha_{0,*}
are the population OLS coefficients
of
\Gamma_{i}
on
X_{i}
in treated and control groups.
Because of the “agnostic” property of linear adjustment
(
Lin 2013
)
,
the variance of our estimator is always no larger than that of the diff-in-mean estimator.
Moreover, our estimator achieves
semiparametric efficiency if the actual conditional means are linear.
Our linear adjustment approach improves
in several aspects upon
the ratio-metric extention of CUPED
(
Deng et al. 2013
)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{T_{i}=1}Y_{i}}{\sum_{T_{i}=1}Z_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{T_{i}=0}Y_{i}}{\sum_{T_{i}=0}Z_{i}}-\widehat{\theta}\cdot\Bigg(\frac{\sum_{T_{i}=1}\widetilde{Y}_{i}}{\sum_{T_{i}=1}\widetilde{Z}_{i}}-\frac{\sum_{T_{i}=0}\widetilde{Y}_{i}}{\sum_{T_{i}=0}\widetilde{Z}_{i}}\Bigg)

(4.4)

for some
\widehat{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\widetilde{Y}_{i}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
are pre-treatment versions of
Y_{i}
and
Z_{i}
.

(i)

Firstly, the estimator (
4.4
) only works with pre-treatment metrics as covariates, while our method incorporates arbitrary covariates, hence more flexible and powerful.

(ii)

Secondly, our method provably reduces variance compared to the diff-in-mean estimator because it runs
separate regressions in the treated and control groups.
A single
\widehat{\theta}
in (
4.4
) is not guaranteed to reduce variance in some adversarial settings
(
Lin 2013
)
.

(iii)

Thirdly, the linear expansion of the estimator in (
4.4
) (assuming
\widehat{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta
) is

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(\Gamma_{i}-\theta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\big)-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(\Gamma_{i}-\theta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\big)\big]+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}),

where
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}
is similarly defined as
\Gamma_{i}
with
Y_{i},Z_{i}
replaced by
\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i}
, and
\theta
is the OLS coefficient of
\Gamma_{i}
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}
.
Our estimator achieves the OLS projection of
\Gamma_{i}
on the whole linear space of
X_{i}
,
while (
4.4
) only projects on the linear space of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}
,
a subspace of
X_{i}

when
X_{i}
contains
\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i}
. Therefore, our estimator achieves more reduction of variance.

Real examples

In this section, we provide two real examples at LinkedIn:
one applying Alg.
1
to analyze
the count metrics in a LinkedIn feed experiment,

7.1
Count metrics in a LinkedIn feed experiment

The LinkedIn feed is an online system exposing members to contents posted in their network, including career news, ideas, questions, and jobs in
the form of short text, articles, images, and videos. ML algorithms are used to rank the tens of thousands of candidate updates for each member to help them discover the most relevant contents.
We consider an experiment on LinkedIn’s feed homepage where members are randomly assigned into a treatment group and a control group. The treatment group is assigned a new version of feed relevance algorithm,
which would be compared to the baseline algorithm in the control group.
The goal of the experiment is to understand how the new ranking algorithm would impact the revenue from feed. As discussed in
Deng et al. 2013
, the effectiveness of variance reduction using CUPED depends on the linear correlation of the experiment outcome with the pre-experiment metric.
It is challenging to reduce the variance for revenue
because member’s revenue is a volatile metric whose autocorrelation
across different time periods is weak.
We hope to improve the performance of variance reduction by incorporating
more covariates and
exploiting nonlinearity with ML methods.

The experiment takes a random sample of
n=400000
members from the LinkedIn online feed traffic
and remove outliers whose revenues are above the
99.5\%
quantile. We implement Alg.
1
with gradient boosting in the
scikit-learn
Python library and compare it to CUPED (with separate linear regressions on treated and control outcomes) as well as the diff-in-mean estimator.
To illustrate the advantage of incorporating side information,
the CUPED method only uses pre-treatment revenue metric, whilst
two sets of covariates are used in Alg
1
:
1) pre-treatment revenue metric;
2) pre-treatment revenue metric and other member attributes including country code, industry, membership status, job seeker class, profile viewer count, connection count, network density, etc., where categorical features are transformed into binary variables with one-hot encoding.

With only pre-treatment revenue metrics, CUPED reduces
15.91\%
of variance compared to the diff-in-mean estimator, whereas Alg.
1
reduces
19.77\%
by exploiting nonlinear relations with gradient boosting models.
By further incorporating member attribute covariates, Alg.
1
reduces
22.22\%
of variance compared to the diff-in-mean estimator,
showing the efficiency gained from more side information.

8
Conclusions

We establish a rigorous statistical framework for variance reduction of count and ratio metrics that are popular in online controlled experiments.
We
propose variance reduction methods that utilize flexible
ML tools to incorporate large numbers of covariates,
yielding unbiased estimators for treatment effects under mild conditions.
Based on semiparametric efficiency theory, we establish the optimality of the proposed procedures, sheding light on ideal solutions for variance reduction.
Simulation studies
illustrate the performance of our methods and also give practical suggestions for ratio metrics under different assumptions.
Finally,
two real online experiments from LinkedIn are used to illustrate the applicability of our methods.

Our current work opens several interesting avenues for future work.
Firstly,
direct extension of the current work is
to add a linear regression step on top of our methods to
utilize the “agnostic” property of linear adjustment.
This might help in practice when the ML estimators are much off;
however, this does not help with optimality, since
under the consistency condition for the ML predictor,
adding a linear regression step
does not change the asymptotic behavior of the estimator.
Another line of future work is variance reduction with limited capacity of ML estimators
— the consistency condition
may not be satisfied
especially when the ML estimator is chosen among a function class that does not cover the true conditional mean function.
In this case, it would be of interest to investigate optimal solutions given such constraints.
Another possible extension is to study robust solutions
for ratio metrics.
As illustrated in the simulations, erroneously making the SDA might hurt the validity of the optimal procedure for
\delta^{\prime}
in (
2.2
).
A procedure that yields valid inference for the target
\delta^{\prime}
even when the SDA is violated might be desired. Instead of targeting at optimality, it would be interesting to find a robust solution that is “optimal” in a possibly more restrictive sense.

A.1
Asymptotic inference

To establish asymptotically valid inference procedure, we impose several conditions on the data generating process and convergence of the estimators.

Assumption A.1

(Data Generating Process)
.

Suppose
(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
and
T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\textrm{Bernoulli}(p)
which are independent of all other random variables.

Assumption A.2

(Convergence)
.

There exists some fixed fuctions
\mu_{1}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\mu_{0}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
so that
\|\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{(k)}-\mu_{w}^{*}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for all
k\in[K]
and
w\in\{0,1\}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
L_{2}
norm on the probability space of
(X_{i},Z_{i})
.

Under the above two conditions, we are to show that the estimator
\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}
has influence function

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})=\frac{\Gamma_{i}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]^{2}}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\big),\quad\text{where}

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big),

and
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(w)=Y_{i}(w)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w)]
,
\widetilde{\mu}_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})=\mu_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})]
for
w\in\{0,1\}
are centered random variables.
The following theorem establishes the asymptotic behavior of the proposed estimator, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
C.2
.

Theorem A.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
A.1
and
A.2
hold, and let
\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}
be the output of Algorithm
3
. Then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}=\Var\big(\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big)

for the influence function (
A.1
).
Furthermore, define the variance estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(g_{1,i}-\bar{g}_{1}(1)\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(g_{0,i}-\bar{g}_{0}(0)\big)^{2}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\textrm{where}~~g_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{Z}}\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\frac{n}{n_{t}\bar{Z}}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{\bar{Y}(1)-\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}^{2}}Z_{i},

\displaystyle g_{0,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{Z}}\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{n}{n_{c}\bar{Z}}\big({Y}_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{\bar{Y}(1)-\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}^{2}}Z_{i}.

Then
\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}\pm\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta^{\prime}}\cdot z_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt{n}
is an asymptotically valid
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
\delta^{\prime}
.

A.2
Optimality

In this part, we show the semiparametric efficiency of
the proposed estimator under similar consistency conditions.
To begin with, we impose the following two assumptions on the data generating process and consistency.

Assumption A.4
.

Denote
\mu_{w}(x,z)=\mathbb{E}[Y(w){\,|\,}X=x,Z=z]
for all
w\in\{0,1\}
. Suppose
\|\widehat{\mu}_{w}^{(k)}-\mu_{w}\|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
for all
k\in[K]
and
w\in\{0,1\}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
L_{2}
-norm in the probability space of
(X_{i},Z_{i})
.

To begin with, we define the efficient influence function for estimating
\delta^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta^{\prime},{\dagger}}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})=\frac{\Gamma_{i}^{{\dagger}}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]^{2}}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]),

(A.3)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}^{{\dagger}}=\mu_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{T_{i}}{p}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big).

The following theorem establishes the semiparametric efficiency of
\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}
under the consistency assumption
A.4
by referring to a more general result (c.f. Theorem
A.5
)
which provides efficiency bounds under
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i}
. The latter works for more general settings than the randomized experiment protocol, which might be of independent interest for observational studies.

Theorem A.5
.

Suppose Assumptions
A.1
and
A.4
hold. Then it holds that
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\delta^{\prime},{\dagger}}^{2})
, where

\sigma_{\delta^{\prime},{\dagger}}^{2}=\Var\big(\phi_{\delta^{\prime},{\dagger}}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big)

for
\phi_{\delta^{\prime},{\dagger}}
defined in (
A.3
)
is the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound for
\delta^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]/\mathbb{E}[Z]
.

Theorem
A.5
sheds light on the ideal procedure of variance reduction
for ratio metrics with stable denominators: one should aim to fit the two conditional mean functions of the numerator
Y
on both
(X,Z)
in the fit-and-plugin framework, where the denominator
Z_{i}
should be pooled to estimate the expectation.

The procedure proposed here is different from the optimal one for a changing
Z
in Section
4
due to differeces in the estimands and
probabilistic nature of the data-generating processes.
Although it seems plausible that pooling all data to estimate the denominator (and making more assumptions) should lead
to an estimator with smaller variance, it is not always the case
and might even hurt the validity of the procedure if the assumption is false imposed.
We will elaborate on this issue by simulation studies in Section
6.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Recall that
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,K
are the
K
randomly-split folds, with
n_{k}=|\mathcal{D}^{(k)}|
,
n_{k,t}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}T_{i}
and
n_{k,c}=n_{k}-n_{k,t}
. Here
\lfloor n/K\rfloor\leq n_{k}\leq\lfloor n/K\rfloor+1
. For each
k\in[K]
, since the functions
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{(k)}
,
\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{(k)}
are fitted with data in
\mathcal{D}\backslash\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=0}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)\,\bigg|\,{\mathcal{T}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)\,\big|\,{\mathcal{T}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)\big].

Here the first equality follows from the tower property of conditional expectations, as well as the i.i.d. of
(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}))
conditional on
\mathcal{D}^{(-k)},{\mathcal{T}}
thanks to the cross-fitting technique and the independence of
{\mathcal{T}}
. The second equality follows from the independence of
\widehat{\mu}_{1},\widehat{\mu}_{0}
and
X_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
due to cross-fitting, and the last equality follows from the independence of
{\mathcal{T}}
and
(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0))
. Since
\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}
in (
3.1
) is the average of all
\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}{\,|\,}{\mathcal{T}}]=\delta
for any sample size
n
.

We then consider
\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}
. Note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)\Big)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{c}}\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=0}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}~\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{c}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=0}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)\Big).

By the conditions in (i), we know

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big|\leq\frac{2}{n_{t}},\quad\Big|\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{c}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big|\leq\frac{2}{n_{c}}.

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}\,\big|\,{\mathcal{T}}\big]-\delta\Big|\leq

\displaystyle~\Bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\big)\Big)\,\bigg|\,{\mathcal{T}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{c}}-\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=0}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\big)\Big)\,\bigg|\,{\mathcal{T}}\bigg]\Bigg|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\Bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})\,\big|\,\mathcal{D}^{(-k)},{\mathcal{T}}\big]\,\bigg|\,{\mathcal{T}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{c}}-\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i})\,\big|\,\mathcal{D}^{(-k)},{\mathcal{T}}\big]\,\bigg|\,{\mathcal{T}}\bigg]\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\cdot\frac{2}{\min\{n_{t},n_{c}\}}\cdot 2c_{0}:=\frac{c}{\min\{n_{t},n_{c}\}}

for absolute constant

. Here the first equality follows from the tower property and the i.i.d. of

conditional on

B.2
Proof of asymptotic inference

Proof of Theorem

We first investigate the behavior of
\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=1,\\

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{k,t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=1,\\

\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}
and
{\mathcal{T}}

\Delta_{0}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\Big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{k}}{n_{k,t}}T_{i}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{k}}{n_{k,c}}(1-T_{i})\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)\bigg]

On the other hand, by (

\displaystyle n_{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[\big(\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{c}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=1,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}\Big(-\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{k,t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)+\frac{1}{n_{k,c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T_{i}=0,i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}\Big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{k,c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)\bigg)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\Big(-\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{k,t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}\bigg]+\frac{n_{k,c}}{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\Big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{k,t}}{n_{k,c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}\bigg]

. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, for any

We now consider the behavior of

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}-\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}T_{i}\Big(-\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)+(1-T_{i})\Big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)

Using the aforementioned notations, for each

\displaystyle n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{k,t}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\Big(-\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}\bigg]+\frac{n_{k,c}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\Big(\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{n_{k,t}(n_{k,t}-1)}{n}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[-\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big]\bigg)^{2}+\frac{n_{k,c}(n_{k,c}-1)}{n}\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big]\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{2n_{k,t}n_{k,c}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[-\frac{n_{c}}{n_{t}}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})-\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{c}\Big[\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n_{c}}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big]=o_{P}(1)

by similar arguments as before. Therefore, we have
\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}-\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{*}\big)=o_{P}(1)
, both marginally and conditionally on
{\mathcal{T}}
.

We now show the asymptotic equivalence of
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}
. Note that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}}{K}-\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{Kn_{k}}-\frac{1}{n}\bigg)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\mu^{*}_{1}(X_{i})-\mu^{*}_{0}(X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{Kn_{k,t}}-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\bigg)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}T_{i}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\mu^{*}_{1}(X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{Kn_{k,c}}-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\bigg)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}T_{i}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\mu^{*}_{0}(X_{i})\big).

Here

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{Kn_{k}}-\frac{1}{n}\bigg)=\frac{n-Kn_{k}}{\sqrt{n}\cdot Kn_{k}}=O\big(n^{-3/2}\big)

since
|n-Kn_{k}|\leq 1
. Therefore we have (under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{Kn_{k}}-\frac{1}{n}\bigg)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)}}\big(\mu^{*}_{1}(X_{i})-\mu^{*}_{0}(X_{i})\big)=o_{P}(1).

With similar arguments for the other two terms, we have
\sqrt{n}\big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}}{K}-\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{(*,k)}\big)=o_{P}(1)
both marginally and conditionally on
{\mathcal{T}}
. Adding up the
K
terms, we have
\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}\big)=o_{P}(1)
. To summarize, so far we have shown that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}\big),~\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}\big),~\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}\big)=o_{P}(1)

both marginally and conditionally on
{\mathcal{T}}
. Therefore, to study the asymptotic behavior of
\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
, it suffices to focus on the oracle
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}
. Conditionally on
{\mathcal{T}}
, i.e., fixing the treatment and control indices, we know

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\Big(Y_{i}(1)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\Big)+\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\Big(-Y_{i}(0)+\frac{n_{c}}{n}\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n}\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\Big),

where the two summations are mutually independent. Furthermore, by law of large numbers and
n_{t}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\Big(\frac{n_{c}}{n}-(1-p)\Big)\Big(\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})\big]\Big)=o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}).

With similar arguments for other terms, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}-\delta\big)

\displaystyle=o_{P}(1)+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{i~\textrm{treated}}\Big(Y_{i}(1)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})\big]-p\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i~\textrm{control}}\Big(-Y_{i}(0)+(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})+p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})+\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})\big]-p\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})\big]\Big).

Since
n_{t}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
, by the central limit theorem, we know

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{*}-\delta\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}),

(B.2)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{p}\Var\Big(Y_{i}(1)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\Big)+\frac{1}{1-p}\Var\Big(Y_{i}(0)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\Big).

By the asymptotic equivalences established before, we also have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}-\delta\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}),\quad\sqrt{n}\big(\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}-\delta\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2})

for the same
\sigma_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}
in (
B.2
). Such variance can be consistently estimated via

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}=\frac{n}{n_{t}^{2}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(A_{i}-\bar{A}(1)\big)^{2}+\frac{n}{n_{c}^{2}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(B_{i}-\bar{B}(0)\big)^{2},

where, recalling the notations,
\bar{A}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}
,
\bar{B}(0)=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}B_{i}
, and

\displaystyle A_{i}=Y_{i}(1)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}),\quad B_{i}=Y_{i}(0)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i}).

To see the consistency, we write
A_{i}=A_{1,i}+E_{1,i}
, where

\displaystyle A_{1,i}=Y_{i}(1)-\frac{n_{c}}{n}\mu^{*}_{1}(X_{i})-\frac{n_{t}}{n}\mu^{*}_{0}(X_{i}).

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}\bigg|=\bigg|\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}E_{1,i}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}.

Again by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Big(\frac{n_{c}}{n}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})+\frac{n_{t}}{n}\Delta_{0}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{n_{c}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})^{2}+\frac{n_{t}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})^{2}.

For each
k\in[K]
, by Assumption
3.3
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Delta_{1}(X_{i})^{2}\bigg]=2\big\|\Delta_{1}\big\|_{2}^{2}=o_{P}(1).

Applying Lemma
F.1
and with similar arguments, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}\leq\frac{n_{c}^{2}}{n^{2}}\cdot o_{P}(1)+\frac{n_{t}^{2}}{n^{2}}\cdot o_{P}(1)=o_{P}(1).

It’s not hard to see that
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}=O_{P}(1)
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1).

(B.3)

Meanwhile, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{1,i}^{2}}=o_{P}(1),

hence

\displaystyle\bar{A}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}+o_{P}(1)=\bar{A^{*}}(0)+o_{P}(1).

(B.4)

Combining equations (
B.3
) and (
B.4
), we know

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(A_{i}-\bar{A}(1)\big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1).

Furthermore, we write
A_{1,i}=A_{2,i}+E_{2,i}
, where

A_{2,i}=Y_{i}(1)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i}).

Here

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Big(\frac{n_{t}}{n}-p\Big)^{2}\big(\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})\big)^{2}=o_{P}(1),

since
n_{t}/n-p=o_{P}(1)
. By similar arguments as for
A_{i}
and
A_{i,1}
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{2,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{1,i}+o_{P}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{2,i}+o_{P}(1).

Therefore by the law of large numbers, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(A_{i}-\bar{A}(1)\big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{2,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}A_{2,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1)=\Var(A_{2,i})+o_{P}(1).

By same arguments to
B_{i}
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(B_{i}-\bar{B}(0)\big)^{2}=\Var(B_{2,i})+o_{P}(1),

where
B_{2,i}=Y_{i}(0)-(1-p)\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i})-p\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i})
. Further since
n/n_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}1/p
and
n/n_{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}1/(1-p)
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{\textrm{Debias}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n_{t}}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(A_{i}-\bar{A}(1)\big)^{2}+\frac{n}{n_{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(B_{i}-\bar{B}(0)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\Var(A_{2,i})+\frac{1}{1-p}\Var(B_{2,i})+o_{P}(1)=\sigma_{{\textrm{Debias}}}^{2}+o_{P}(1).

Therefore, we complete the proof of Theorem
3.5
.
∎

We treat the two terms (i) and (ii) separately. Firstly, we denote

4.2

k\in[K]

K

since

\textrm{(i)}=o_{P}(1)

(ii)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\big[\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{np}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\bar{A}=o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)+\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]+\bar{A^{*}}.

Following exactly the same arguments, we can show that

\displaystyle\bar{B}

\displaystyle=o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)+\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{i}(1)\big]+\bar{B^{*}},

\displaystyle\bar{C}

\displaystyle=o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)+\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]+\bar{C^{*}},

\displaystyle\bar{D}

\displaystyle=o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)+\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{i}(0)\big]+\bar{D^{*}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}=\bar{A}/\bar{B}-\bar{C}/\bar{D}=o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)+\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]+\bar{A^{*}}}{\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{i}(1)\big]+\bar{B^{*}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]+\bar{C^{*}}}{\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{i}(0)\big]+\bar{D^{*}}}=\widehat{\delta}^{*}+o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big).

Moerover, by Delta method or Taylor expansion for
\widehat{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}=\widehat{\delta}^{*}+o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)=\delta+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\delta}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})+o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big),

Writing
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(w)=Y_{i}(w)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w)]

\widetilde{Z}_{i}(w)=Z_{i}(w)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(w)]
for
w\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})=T_{i}\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})+(1-T_{i})\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i}),

where

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=\theta_{A}\Big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta_{B}\Big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\big(\widetilde{Z}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big)\Big)+\theta_{C}\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\theta_{D}\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i}),

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=\theta_{A}\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\theta_{B}\widetilde{\mu}_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\theta_{C}\Big(\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{1-p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta_{D}\Big(\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{1-p}\big(\widetilde{Z}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\Big).

Therefore, since
T_{i}
are independent of all other random variables, we have

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{\delta}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})\big)+(1-p)\cdot\Var\big(\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i})\big).

Since
\widehat{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
, it suffices to show that
\widehat{V}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})\big)
and
\widehat{V}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\Var\big(\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i})\big)
. We showcase the first convergence result, while the second follows exactly the same arguments. Since adding constants to random variables does not change the variance, we have

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})\big)=\mathbb{E}[D_{1,i}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[D_{1,i}]^{2}=o_{P}(1)+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}\Big)^{2}

(C.1)

according to the law of large numbers,
where

\displaystyle D_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\theta_{A}\Big(\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\theta_{B}\Big(\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{p}\big({Z}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big)\Big)+\theta_{C}\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\theta_{D}\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})

\displaystyle=\theta_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{\theta_{A}}{p}Y_{i}(1)+\theta_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{\theta_{B}}{p}Z_{i}(1)+\theta_{C}\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\theta_{D}\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i}).

Meanwhile, the law of large numbers gives
\widehat{\theta}_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{A}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{B}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{C}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{D}
for

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{A}=1/\bar{Z}(1),\quad\widehat{\theta}_{B}=-\bar{Y}(1)/\bar{Z}(1)^{2},\quad\widehat{\theta}_{C}=-1/\bar{Z}(0),\quad\widehat{\theta}_{D}=\bar{Y}(0)/\bar{Z}(0)^{2}.

Writing
D_{1,i}=D_{2,i}+E_{2,i}
, where

\displaystyle D_{2,i}=\widehat{\theta}_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{A}}{\widehat{p}}Y_{i}(1)+\widehat{\theta}_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{B}}{\widehat{p}}Z_{i}(1)+\widehat{\theta}_{C}\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\widehat{\theta}_{D}\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i}).

Then by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}\bigg|=\bigg|\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}E_{2,i}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}.

Again by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\bigg[\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)-\theta_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\bigg)\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\Big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{A}}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{\theta_{A}}{p}\Big)Y_{i}(1)+\big(\widehat{\theta}_{C}-\theta_{C}\big)\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)-\theta_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\bigg)\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\Big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{B}}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{\theta_{B}}{p}\Big)Z_{i}(1)+\big(\widehat{\theta}_{D}-\theta_{D}\big)\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)-\theta_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\bigg)^{2}\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})^{2}+\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{A}}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{\theta_{A}}{p}\Big)^{2}Y_{i}(1)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\bigg(\widehat{\theta}_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)-\theta_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\bigg)^{2}\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})^{2}+\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{B}}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{\theta_{B}}{p}\Big)^{2}Z_{i}(1)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(\widehat{\theta}_{C}-\theta_{C}\big)^{2}\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})^{2}+\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(\widehat{\theta}_{D}-\theta_{D}\big)\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i}).

(C.2)

Since
\widehat{\theta}_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{A}
and
\widehat{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
, by the law of large numbers we have

\displaystyle\frac{6}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\widehat{\theta}_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)=o_{P}(1).

Similarly, all terms in the right-most handed equation (
C.2
) are
o_{P}(1)
, which implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1).

Now writing
D_{2,i}=D_{3,i}+E_{3,i}
so that

\displaystyle D_{3,i}=\widehat{\theta}_{A}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)\widehat{\mu}_{1,Y}(X_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{A}}{\widehat{p}}Y_{i}(1)+\widehat{\theta}_{B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)\widehat{\mu}_{1,Z}(X_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{B}}{\widehat{p}}Z_{i}(1)+\widehat{\theta}_{C}\widehat{\mu}_{0,Y}(X_{i})+\widehat{\theta}_{D}\widehat{\mu}_{0,Z}(X_{i}).

It’s straightforward to see that
D_{3,i}=d_{1,i}
for
d_{1,i}
defined in
Theorem
4.3
.
Similar arguments yield

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|-\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}E_{3,i}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}.

Here similar to the arguments in (
C.2
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\theta}_{A}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)^{2}\cdot\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}+\widehat{\theta}_{B}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)^{2}\cdot\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Z}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\widehat{\theta}_{C}^{2}\cdot\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}+\widehat{\theta}_{D}^{2}\cdot\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{0}^{Z}(X_{i})-\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}.

(C.3)

We decompose into the
K
cross-fitting folds, so that

\displaystyle\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}.

Note that under
\mathbb{E}_{c}
, the conditional expectation given
\mathcal{D}^{(-k)}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
, the summation terms
\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}
for

and
T_{i}=1
are i.i.d., and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c}\bigg[\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}\bigg]=\frac{4n_{k,t}}{n_{t}}\big\|\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y,(k)}-\widehat{\mu}_{1,Y}^{*}\big\|_{2}=o_{P}(1).

By a standard argument on a.s. convergence and convergence in probability, we have

\displaystyle\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{(k)},T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}=o_{P}(1)

for all

. Adding them up, we have

\displaystyle\frac{4}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big[\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{Y}(X_{i})-\mu_{1,Y}^{*}(X_{i})\big]^{2}=o_{P}(1).

Exactly the same arguments for the rest terms in (
C.3
) leads to
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}=o_{P}(1)
. Also it’s straightforward that
\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}=O_{P}(1)
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}^{2}+o_{P}(1).

(C.4)

Moreover, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle o_{P}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}\geq\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}\bigg)^{2},\quad o_{P}(1)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}\geq\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}\bigg)^{2},

which indicates

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}+o_{P}(1).

(C.5)

Combining equations (
C.1
), (
C.4
) and (
C.5
), we have

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1),X_{i},T_{i})\big)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{3,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1)=\widehat{V}_{1}+o_{P}(1),

By similar arguments for
\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i})
, we can show

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0),X_{i},T_{i})\big)=\widehat{V}_{0}+o_{P}(1).

Furthermore, the fact that
\widehat{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
establishes the consistency of
\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}^{2}
for
\sigma_{\delta}^{2}
. By Slutsky’s theorem,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)/\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,1),

Therefore, we conclude the proof of Theorem
4.3
.
∎

C.2
Proof of asymptotic inference: under SDA

Proof of Theorem
A.3
.

Firstly, we note that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}
coincides with
\widetilde{\theta}_{\textrm{Debias}}
defined in (
3.2
), except that
\widehat{\mu}_{w}(X_{i})
is substitued by
\widehat{\mu}_{w}(X_{i},Z_{i})
here for
w\in\{0,1\}
. Therefore, by exactly the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
for
\widetilde{\theta}_{{\textrm{Debias}}}
, we can show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(Y_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(Y_{i}(0)-\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+o_{P}\big(1/\sqrt{n}\big),

where
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(w)=Y_{i}(w)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w)]
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})=\mu_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})]
for
w\in\{0,1\}
are centered random variables. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\big(\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\frac{T_{i}}{\widehat{p}}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-\widehat{p}}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}^{*}+\Big(\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(\frac{1}{1-\widehat{p}}-\frac{1}{1-p}\Big)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-T_{i})\cdot\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big),

where
\Gamma_{i}^{*}
is defined in (
A.1
).
Since
\widehat{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
and
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(w)-\widetilde{\mu}_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})
are i.i.d. with mean zero, the last two terms in the above summation are both
o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(1)\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{i}(0)\big]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}^{*}+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}).

By a standard argument of delta method or Taylor expansion, we see that

\displaystyle\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}^{*}+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}])}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})+o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}).

This leads to the asymptotic distribution
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2})
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}=\Var\big(\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big).

We now proceed to establish the asymptotic consistency of the variance estimator. To this end, writing
1/\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=\theta_{1}
and
-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]^{2}}=\theta_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=T_{i}\cdot\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})+(1-T_{i})\cdot\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i}),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\theta_{1}\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{\theta_{1}}{p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\theta_{2}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\big),

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\widetilde{\mu}_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\theta_{1}\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\frac{\theta_{1}}{1-p}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{i}(0)-\widetilde{\mu}_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\theta_{2}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\big).

Since
T_{i}
’s are independent of all other random variables, we have

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{\delta}^{\prime}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big)=p\cdot\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})\big)+(1-p)\cdot\Var\big(\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})\big).

Since adding constants does not change variance, the law of large numbers implies

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\Var\Big(\theta_{1}\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\theta_{1}\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{\theta_{1}}{p}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\theta_{2}Z_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}(D_{i}^{*})^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{i}^{*}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1),

where we write

\displaystyle D_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\theta_{1}\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{\theta_{1}}{p}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\theta_{2}Z_{i}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad D_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\theta}_{1}\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widehat{\theta}_{1}\widehat{\mu}_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{1}}{\widehat{p}}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\widehat{\mu}_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\widehat{\theta}_{2}Z_{i},

with
\widehat{\theta}_{1}=1/\bar{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{1}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{2}=-\frac{\bar{Y}(1)-\bar{Y}(0)}{\bar{Z}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\theta_{2}
and
\widehat{p}=n_{t}/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
being consistent estimators. Now we define
D_{1,i}=D_{2,i}+E_{2,i}
, where

\displaystyle D_{2,i}=\widehat{\theta}_{1}\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\widehat{\theta}_{1}\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{1}}{\widehat{p}}\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+\widehat{\theta}_{2}Z_{i}.

Also write
\Delta_{w}(X_{i},Z_{i})=\widehat{\mu}_{w}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mu_{w}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})
for
w\in\{0,1\}
. Then by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}\leq\widehat{\theta}_{1}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{p}}\Big)^{2}\cdot\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Delta_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})^{2}+\widehat{\theta}_{1}^{2}\cdot\frac{2}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}\Delta_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})^{2}.

Using similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}^{2}=o_{P}(1),\quad\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{2,i}=o_{P}(1),

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1).

Writing
D_{2,i}=D_{i}^{*}+E_{3,i}
, where

\displaystyle E_{3,i}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{1})\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})-(\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{1})\mu_{0}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})+\Big(\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{1}}{\widehat{p}}-\frac{\theta_{1}}{p}\Big)\big({Y}_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}^{*}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)+(\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{2})Z_{i},

similar arguments as the proof of Theorem
4.3
lead to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}^{2}=o_{P}(1),\quad\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}E_{3,i}=o_{P}(1),

and (noting that
D_{1,i}=g_{1,i}
as defined in (
A.2
))

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{1}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}(D_{i}^{*})^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{i}^{*}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{2,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}D_{1,i}\Big)^{2}+o_{P}(1)=\widehat{V}_{1}+o_{P}(1)

for
\widehat{V}_{1}
defined in (
A.2
). With exactly the same arguments, we can show

\displaystyle\Var\big(\phi_{0}(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i},X_{i})\big)=\widehat{V}_{0}+o_{P}(1).

Combining the above two results and the fact that
\widehat{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}p
, we know
\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\sigma_{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}
. By Slutsky’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\delta}^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime})/\widehat{\sigma}_{\delta^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,1),

which establishes the validity of confidence interval. Therefore, we complete the proof of Theorem
A.3
.

D.1
Semiparametric efficiency for count metric

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\Var\Big(Y_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}(X_{i})+p\big[\mu_{1}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big]\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{1-p}\Var\Big(Y_{i}(0)-\mu_{0}(X_{i})-(1-p)\big[\mu_{1}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\Var\big(Y_{i}(1)-\mu_{1}(X_{i})\big)+p\cdot\Var\big(\mu_{1}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{1-p}\Var\big(Y_{i}(0)-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big)+(1-p)\cdot\Var\big(\mu_{1}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}\big[\Var(Y_{i}(1){\,|\,}X_{i})]+\frac{1}{1-p}\mathbb{E}\big[\Var(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i})]+\Var\big(\mu_{1}(X_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i})\big)=V^{*}.

Therefore,
\sigma_{*}^{2}
is the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound for
\tau
in this case.
∎

Appendix E
Semiparametric variance bounds

In this part, we establish semiparametric variance bounds for the estimands
\tau
,
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
, which forms the basis for the optimality of the proposed procedures.

E.1
Backgrounds and definitions

We first introduce basic definitions that are necessary for semiparametric variance bounds from the literature of semiparametric efficiency. Let
\mu
be a fixed
\sigma
-finite measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
, and let
L_{2}(\mu)
be the space of all
L_{2}
-integrable functions with respect to
\mu
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
dominated by
\mu
, which can be equivalently viewed as a subset of
L_{2}(\mu)
via the embedding

\displaystyle P~\to~p=\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}}P}{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu}~\to~r=\sqrt{p}\in L_{2}(\mu)

(E.1)

for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}}P}{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu}
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative.

Definition E.1

(Regular parametric space)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is a collection of measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
dominated by
\mu
, which is parametrized by
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathcal{R}^{k}
, so that
\mathcal{Q}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
. Denote the embedding
r(\theta)=\sqrt{p(\theta)}\in L_{2}(\mu)
, where
p(\theta)=\frac{{\,\mathrm{d}}P_{\theta}}{{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu}
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative. We say the parametrization
\theta\to p(\theta)
is
regular
if
\Theta
is open, and for any
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, the map
\theta\to r(\theta)
is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta_{0}
with derivative
\dot{r}(\theta_{0})\in L_{2}(\mu)^{k}
, and the matrix
\int\dot{r}(\theta_{0})\dot{r}(\theta_{0})^{\top}{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu
is non-singular.

Definition E.2

(Regular non-parametric space)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
dominated by
\mu
. An element
P_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
is
regular
if it is contained in a regular parametric subspace of
\mathcal{P}
. We say
\mathcal{P}
is
regular
if every point
P\in\mathcal{P}
is regular.

Definition E.3

(Tangent space)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
dominated by
\mu
, which can be viewed as a subset
\mathcal{R}
of
L_{2}(\mu)
via the embedding in Equation (
E.1
). For a fixed
P_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
, we define the
tangent set
at
P_{0}
as the union of all (one-dimensional) tangent spaces of curves
C\subset\mathcal{P}
passing through
P_{0}
. We define
tangent space
at
P_{0}
as the closed linear span of the tangent set at
P_{0}
.

Note that in the above definition, everything considered is in the Hilbert space
L_{2}(\mu)
.

Definition E.4

(Pathwise differentiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a regular nonparametric model, and
\beta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{R}
be a functional on
\mathcal{P}
. Under the embedding in Equation (
E.1
), let
\beta
also denote the mapping
\beta:L_{2}(\mu)\to\mathcal{R}
with
\beta(r(P))=\beta(P)
. For a fixed
P_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\dot{\mathcal{P}}
denote the tangent space of
\mathcal{P}
at
P_{0}
, and let
r_{0}=r(P_{0})
be its embedding in
L_{2}(\mu)
, with tangent space
\dot{\mathcal{R}}
at
r_{0}
.
We say
\beta
is pathwise differentiable on
\mathcal{P}
at
P_{0}
(or
r_{0}
) if there exists a bounded linear functional
\dot{\beta}(r_{0}):\dot{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{R}
so that

\displaystyle{\beta}(r(\eta))=\beta(r_{0})+\eta\dot{\beta}(r_{0})(t)+o(|\eta|)

for any curve
r(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{R}
passing through
r_{0}=r(0)
and
\dot{r}(0)=t
. We also define
\dot{\beta}(P_{0}):\dot{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{R}
by
\dot{\beta}(P_{0})(h)=\dot{\beta}(r_{0})(hr_{0}/2)
, so that
\beta(P_{\eta})=\beta(P_{0})+\eta\dot{\beta}(P_{0})(h)+o(|\eta|)
, where
P_{\eta}
is a curve in
\mathcal{P}
corresponding to
r(\eta)
in
\mathcal{R}
and
h=2t/r_{0}
.

The following definition gives the notion of efficient influence function by defining it as the projection of pathwise derivative on the tangent space, and the efficient variance, or semiparametric variance bound, is defined as the variance of efficient influence function. The efficient variance is actually a lower bound for all asymptotically linear estimators, hence any asymptotically linear estimator whose asymptotic variance coincide with the efficient variance is semiparametrically efficient. More detailed results are in the textbook
Bickel et al. 1993
.

Definition E.5

(Efficient influence function)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a regular nonparametric model, and
\beta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{R}
be a functional on
\mathcal{P}
that is pathwise differentiable at
P_{0}
with derivative
\dot{\beta}(P_{0})
. We define the
efficient influence function
as
\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,P_{0}{\,|\,}\beta,\mathcal{P})=\Pi(\dot{\beta}(P_{0}){\,|\,}\dot{\mathcal{P}})
, the projection of
\dot{\beta}(P_{0})\in L_{2}(\mu)
onto the tangent space
\dot{\mathcal{P}}
. We also define the
efficient variance
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{\beta}=\|\widetilde{\phi}(\cdot,P_{0}{\,|\,}\beta,\mathcal{P})\|_{0}^{2}~,

where the norm
\|\cdot\|_{0}
is defined as
\|f\|_{0}^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle_{0}=\int f^{2}{\,\mathrm{d}}P_{0}
for any
f\in L_{2}(\mu)
.

Based on these definitions, given an estimand
\beta=\beta(\mathcal{P})
, we outline the following protocol of providing semiparametric variance bounds, which will be applied in Appedix
E.3
and
E.4
.

•

Step 1: Characterize parametric submodels
\{P_{\theta}\colon\theta\in\Theta\}
for
\mathcal{P}
.

•

Step 2: Find the tangent space
{\mathcal{T}}
of any regular parametric submodel
\{P_{\theta}\colon\theta\in\Theta\}
.

•

Step 3: Find pathwise derivative
{\,\mathrm{d}}\beta(\theta)/{\,\mathrm{d}}\theta
of the estimand at the parametric submodel.

•

Step 4: Project the pathwise derivative, as a member in the Hilbert space
L_{2}(\mu)
, onto the tangent space. Then the projection is the efficient influence function, whose variance is the efficient variance.

E.2
Semiparametric variance bound for
\tau

The semiparametric variance bound for
\tau
has been established in a general setting, as in the following lemma.

Lemma E.6

(Theorem 1 in
Hahn 1998
)
.

Suppose
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i}
, and let
e(x)=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1{\,|\,}X_{i}=x)
be the propensity score. Then the asymptotic variance bounds for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\Var(Y_{i}(1){\,|\,}X_{i})}{e(X_{i})}+\frac{\Var(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i})}{1-e(X_{i})}+\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1){\,|\,}X_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i}]-\tau\big)^{2}\bigg].

E.3
Semiparametric variance bound for
\delta

We establish the efficient influence function and efficient variance for
\delta
in the general setting following the protocol in Appedix
E.1
. Recall that
\mu_{w,Y}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w){\,|\,}X_{i}=x]
and
\mu_{w,Z}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(w){\,|\,}X_{i}=x]
,

Theorem E.7
.

Suppose
(X_{i},Z_{i}(0),Z_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
and
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(1),Z_{i}(0)){\,|\,}X_{i}
. Let
e(x)=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1{\,|\,}X_{i}=x)
be the propensity score. Then the efficient influence function for
\delta=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]/\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]/\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]
is
\phi_{\delta}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})
, where

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{i}^{**}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)]^{2}}B_{i}^{**}-\frac{C_{i}^{**}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)]^{2}}D_{i}^{**},

(E.2)

\displaystyle\textrm{where}\quad A_{i}^{**}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1,Y}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{e(X_{i})}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{1,Y}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)],

\displaystyle B_{i}^{**}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1,Z}(X_{i})+\frac{T_{i}}{e(X_{i})}\big(Z_{i}-\mu_{1,Z}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(1)],

\displaystyle C_{i}^{**}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0,Y}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-e(X_{i})}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{0,Y}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)],

\displaystyle D_{i}^{**}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0,Z}^{*}(X_{i})+\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-e(X_{i})}\big(Z_{i}-\mu_{0,Z}(X_{i})\big)-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(0)].

The asymptotic variance bound for
\delta
is
V_{\delta}^{*}=\Var(\phi_{\delta}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i}))
.

Proof of Theorem
E.7
.

We follow the general protocol in Appendix

, which is similar to

Step 1: Parametric submodel.

Under the strong ignorability
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(1),Z_{i}(0)){\,|\,}X_{i}
, the density of the joint distribution of
(X_{i},Z_{i}(0),Z_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{q}(x,y_{0},y_{1},z_{0},z_{1},t)=f(x)\cdot f(y_{0},y_{1},z_{0},z_{1}{\,|\,}x)\cdot e(x)^{t}(1-e(x))^{1-t},

where
f(y_{0},y_{1},z_{0},z_{1}{\,|\,}x)
is the conditional density function. The joint distribution of the observed quantities
(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})
where
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})
and
Z_{i}=Z_{i}(T_{i})
is thus

\displaystyle q(x,y,z,t)=\big[f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x)e(x)\big]^{t}\cdot\big[f_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x)(1-e(x))\big]^{1-t}f(x),

where
f_{w}(y,z{\,|\,}x)
is the conditional density function of
Y(w),Z(w)
given
X
for
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Then a regular parametric submodel containing
\mathbb{P}
is characterized by

\displaystyle q(x,y,z,t;\theta)=\big[f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)e(x,\theta)\big]^{t}\cdot\big[f_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)(1-e(x,\theta))\big]^{1-t}f(x,\theta),

where the submodel is parametrizedby
\theta\in\Theta
, and the density equals the true density
q(x,y,z,t)
when
\theta=\theta_{0}
. Also, the functions
f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)
,
f_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)
,
f(x,\theta)
and

is differentiable w.r.t.
\theta
at
\theta_{0}
.

Step 2: Tangent space.

By a straightforward calculation, the score function at
\mathbb{P}(\theta)
with density

is

\displaystyle s(\theta;x,y,z,t)=t\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}{f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}+(1-t)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}{f_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}+\frac{t-e(x,\theta)}{e(x,\theta)(1-e(x,\theta))}\cdot\dot{e}(x,\theta)+\frac{\dot{f}(x,\theta)}{f(x,\theta)},

where

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{w}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f_{w}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta),~w\in\{0,1\},\quad\dot{e}(x,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{e}(x,\theta),\quad\dot{f}(x,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(x,\theta).

Therefore, the tangent space at
\mathbb{P}
is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}=\Big\{

\displaystyle t\cdot s_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x)+(1-t)\cdot s_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x)+a(x)\big(t-e(x)\big)+s(x)\colon

(E.3)

\displaystyle\quad\int s_{w}(y,z{\,|\,}x)f_{w}(y,z{\,|\,}x){\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z=0,\forall w\in\{0,1\},~\forall x,z,

(E.4)

\displaystyle\qquad~\int s(x)f(x){\,\mathrm{d}}x=0,~a(x)\text{ is square integrable}\Big\}.

Step 3: Pathwise derivative.

The target
\delta
can be written as

\delta(\theta)=\frac{\int yf_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z}{\int zf_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z}-\frac{\int yf_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z}{\int zf_{0}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta(\theta)}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[Z(1)]}\bigg(\int y\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z+\int yf_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)\dot{f}(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z(1)]^{2}}\bigg(\int z\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z+\int zf_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)\dot{f}(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z\bigg)

(E.5)

, we only show the first equation, and others follow exactly the same arguments. Note that

\displaystyle\int y\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z+\int yf_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)\dot{f}(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle=\int y\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}{f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)}\cdot f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)f(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z+\int y\frac{\dot{f}(x,\theta)}{{f}(x,\theta)}\cdot f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta){f}(x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1),Z(1){\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1),Z(1){\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\bigg].

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[A_{i}^{**}\cdot s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot T_{i}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot(1-T_{i})\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{T_{i}-e(X_{i})}{e(X_{i})(1-e(X_{i}))}\cdot\dot{e}(X_{i},\theta)\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X_{i},\theta)}{f(X_{i},\theta)}\bigg].

By the definition of
A_{i}^{**}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot T_{i}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot T\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T}{e(X)}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg],

where by the tower property and takeout property of conditional expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot T\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot\mathbb{E}[T{\,|\,}X]\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\,\Big|\,X\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot e(X)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]=0,

where the last equality follows the fact that for any measurable function
g(\cdot)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[g(X)\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]=\int_{x}g(x)f(x,\theta)\int_{y,z}\dot{f}_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z{\,\mathrm{d}}x=0.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T}{e(X)}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1),Z(1){\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1),Z(1){\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg].

Meanwhile, still by the conditional independence of
Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(1)
and
T_{i}
given
X_{i}
as well as properties of conditional expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot(1-T_{i})\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot(1-T)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\big)\cdot\big(1-e(X)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y,Z{\,|\,}X,\theta)}\bigg]=0.

For the third term,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{T_{i}-e(X_{i})}{e(X_{i})(1-e(X_{i}))}\cdot\dot{e}(X_{i},\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\Big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(Y-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\Big)\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[T{\,|\,}X]-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y(1){\,|\,}X]-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,\theta)\bigg]=0.

Here the second equality follows from the tower property and takeout property and the fact that
TY=TY(1)
, the forth equality follows from the conditional independence of
Y(1)
and
T
given
X
as well as the tower property and takeout property. The last term satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[A_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X_{i},\theta)}{f(X_{i},\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1,Y}(X)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]\Big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(Y-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X)}\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1,Y}(X)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\bigg].

The above derivation uses the fact that
TY=TY(1)
,
Y(1)\perp\!\!\!\perp T{\,|\,}X
and

\mathbb{E}\big[g(X)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,\theta)}{f(X,\theta)}\big]=0

for all measurable function
g(\cdot)
. Combining the four terms, we arrive at the first of the desired relations. By symmetric roles of
Y_{i},Z_{i}
and
T_{i},1-T_{i}
, the other four follow similarly. Thus, we complete the proof that
\phi_{\delta}^{*}
is the projection of
\partial\delta(\theta)/\partial\theta
onto
{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence,
\phi_{\delta}^{*}
is the efficient influence function, and its variance is the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound for estimating
\delta
.
∎

E.4
Semiparametric variance bound for
\delta^{\prime}

Similar to the case of
\delta
, we establish the efficient influence function and efficient variance for
\delta^{\prime}
in the general setting following the protocol in Appedix
E.1
. Recall that
\mu_{w}(x,z)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(w){\,|\,}X_{i}=x,Z_{i}=z]
,
w\in\{0,1\}
are the true conditional mean functions.

Theorem E.8
.

Suppose
(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
and
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)){\,|\,}(X_{i},Z_{i})
. Let
e(x,z)=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=1{\,|\,}X_{i}=x,Z_{i}=z)
be the propensity score. Then the efficient influence function for
\delta^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]/\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]/\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]
is
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})
, where

\displaystyle\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{i}^{**}}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]^{2}}\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]),

(E.6)

\displaystyle\textrm{where}\quad\Gamma_{i}^{**}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\mu_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})-\big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)]\big)

(E.7)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{T_{i}}{e(X_{i},Z_{i})}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{1}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big)-\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-e(X_{i},Z_{i})}\big(Y_{i}-\mu_{0}(X_{i},Z_{i})\big).

The asymptotic variance bound for
\delta
is
V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}=\Var(\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i}))
.

Proof of Theorem
E.8
.

The proof follows the protocol in Appendix
E.1
.

Step 1: Parametric submodel.

Under the strong ignorability
T_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)){\,|\,}(X_{i},Z_{i})
, the density of the joint distribution of
(X_{i},Z_{i},Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1),T_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{q}(x,y_{0},y_{1},z,t)=f(x,z)\cdot f(y_{0},y_{1},{\,|\,}x,z)\cdot e(x)^{t}(1-e(x))^{1-t},

where
f(y_{0},y_{1}{\,|\,}x,z)
is the conditional density function. The joint distribution of the observed quantities
(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})
where
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})
is

\displaystyle q(x,y,z,t)=\big[f_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z)e(x,z)\big]^{t}\cdot\big[f_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z)(1-e(x,z))\big]^{1-t}f(x,z),

where
f_{w}(y{\,|\,}x,z)
is the conditional density function of
Y(w)
given
X,Z
for
w\in\{0,1\}
.
Then a regular parametric submodel containing
\mathbb{P}
is characterized by

\displaystyle q(x,y,z,t;\theta)=\big[f_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)e(x,z,\theta)\big]^{t}\cdot\big[f_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)(1-e(x,z,\theta))\big]^{1-t}f(x,z,\theta),

where the submodel is parametrizedby
\theta\in\Theta
, and the density equals the true density
q(x,y,z,t)
when
\theta=\theta_{0}
. Also, the functions
f_{1}(y,z{\,|\,}x,\theta)
,
f_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)
,
f(x,z,\theta)
and
e(x,z,\theta)
are differentiable w.r.t.
\theta
at
\theta=\theta_{0}
.

Step 2: Tangent space.

By the joint distribution of the observable, the score function at
\theta
or
\mathbb{P}(\theta)
is

\displaystyle s(\theta;x,y,z,t)=t\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)}{f_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)}+(1-t)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)}{f_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)}+\frac{t-e(x,z,\theta)}{e(x,z,\theta)(1-e(x,z,\theta))}\cdot\dot{e}(x,z,\theta)+\frac{\dot{f}(x,z,\theta)}{f(x,z,\theta)},

(E.8)

where

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{w}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f_{w}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta),~w\in\{0,1\},\quad\dot{e}(x,z,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}{e}(x,z,\theta),\quad\dot{f}(x,z,\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(x,z,\theta).

Therefore, the tangent space
{\mathcal{T}}
at
\theta
is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}=\Big\{

\displaystyle t\cdot s_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z)+(1-t)\cdot s_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z)+a(x,z)\big(t-e(x,z)\big)+s(x,z)\colon

(E.9)

\displaystyle\int s_{w}(y{\,|\,}x,z)f_{w}(y{\,|\,}x,z){\,\mathrm{d}}y=0,\forall w\in\{0,1\},~\forall x,z,

(E.10)

\displaystyle\qquad~\int s(x,z)f(x,z){\,\mathrm{d}}x=0,~a(x,z)\text{ is square integrable}\Big\}.

Step 3: Pathwise derivative.

The target
\delta^{\prime}
can be parametrized as

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{\int yf_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)f(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z-\int yf_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)f(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z}{\int zf(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}z}.

Therefore, the pathwise derivative at
\theta
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[Z]}\bigg(\int y\dot{f}_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)f(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z-\int y\dot{f}_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)f(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[Z]}\bigg(\int y{f}_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)\dot{f}(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z-\int y{f}_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z,\theta)\dot{f}(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}y{\,\mathrm{d}}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}\int z\dot{f}(x,z,\theta){\,\mathrm{d}}x{\,\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[Z]}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(0)\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[Z]}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-Y(0)\big)\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{{f}(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]+\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]}{\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{{f}(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg],

where the second equality follows similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
E.7
.

Step 4: Projection onto tangent space.

We show that
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}
is in
{\mathcal{T}}
and is the projection of
\frac{\partial\delta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}
onto
{\mathcal{T}}
. Firstly, note that
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}\in{\mathcal{T}}
by setting
a(x,z)\equiv 0
and

\displaystyle s_{1}(y{\,|\,}x,z)=\frac{y-\mu_{1}(x,z)}{e(x,z)},\quad s_{0}(y{\,|\,}x,z)=\frac{y-\mu_{0}(x,z)}{1-e(x,z)},

\displaystyle s(x,z)=\mu_{1}(x,z)-\mu_{0}(x,z)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]+\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]

in the specification of
E.9
. Then to show that
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}
is the projection of
\frac{\partial\delta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}
onto
{\mathcal{T}}
, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})\cdot\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}(Y_{i},Z_{i},X_{i},T_{i})\big]

(E.11)

for the score function in (
E.8
). To this end, we now show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\big)\cdot s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{{f}(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma_{i}^{**}\cdot s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(0)\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-Y(0)\big)\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{{f}(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg].

For the first equation, note that
\mathbb{E}[s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})]=0
, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\big)\cdot s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})]=\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{i}\cdot s(\theta;X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},T_{i})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[T\cdot Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}+(1-T)\cdot Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[Z\cdot\frac{T-e(X,Z,\theta)}{e(X,Z,\theta)(1-e(X,Z,\theta))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,Z,\theta)+Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[e(X,Z)\cdot Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}+(1-e(X,Z))\cdot Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{0}(Y(0){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[Z\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[T{\,|\,}X,Z]-e(X,Z,\theta)}{e(X,Z,\theta)(1-e(X,Z,\theta))}\cdot\dot{e}(X,Z,\theta)+Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(x,Z,\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Z\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg],

where the third equality follows from the conditional independence of
T
and
Y(1),Y(0)
given
X,Z
and the last equality follows the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[g(X,Z)\frac{\dot{f}_{w}(Y(w){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{w}(Y(w){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]=0,\quad\forall~w\in\{0,1\}

(E.12)

and any measurable function
g(\cdot,\cdot)
. For the second equality, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Gamma_{i}^{**}\cdot T_{i}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y_{i}(1){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}{f_{1}(Y_{i}(1){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1}(X,Z)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]+\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]\Big)\cdot T\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X,Z)}\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1}(X,Z)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]+\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]\Big)\cdot e(X,Z)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(1)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}{f_{1}(Y(1){\,|\,}X,Z,\theta)}\bigg].

Here the second equality follows from the conditional independence of
T
and
Y(1),Y(0)
given
X,Z
and the last equality follows from (
E.12
). Following exactly the same arguments, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Gamma_{i}^{**}\cdot(1-T_{i})\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}{f_{0}(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y(0)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}_{0}(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}{f_{0}(Y_{i}(0){\,|\,}X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}\bigg].

Meanwhile, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Gamma_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}{f(X_{i},Z_{i},\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1}(X,Z)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]+\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]\Big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X,Z)}\cdot\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1-T}{1-e(X,Z)}\cdot\big(Y(0)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1}(X,Z)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)\Big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(Y(1)-Y(0)\big)\cdot\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\bigg],

where the second equality follows from the conditional independence and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{\dot{f}(X,Z,\theta)}{f(X,Z,\theta)}\big]=0
, and the last equality follows from the takeout and tower property of conditional expectations. Also,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Gamma_{i}^{**}\cdot\frac{T_{i}-e(X_{i},Z_{i})}{e(X_{i},Z_{i})(1-e(X_{i},Z_{i}))}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\mu_{1}(X,Z)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]+\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]\Big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X,Z)}{e(X,Z)(1-e(X,Z))}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{T}{e(X,Z)}\cdot\big(Y(1)-\mu_{1}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X,Z)}{e(X,Z)(1-e(X,Z))}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1-T}{1-e(X,Z)}\cdot\big(Y(0)-\mu_{0}(X,Z)\big)\cdot\frac{T-e(X,Z)}{e(X,Z)(1-e(X,Z))}\bigg]=0,

where all the three terms in the summation equals zero because of the tower and takeout property of conditional expectations. Putting them together, we’ve proved (
E.11
). Therefore,
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}
is the projection of
\frac{\partial\delta^{\prime}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}
onto
{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence,
\phi_{\delta^{\prime}}^{*}
is the efficient influence function, and its variance is the semiparametric asymptotic variance bound for estimating
\delta^{\prime}
.
∎

Appendix F
Technical lemmas

Lemma F.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be a sequence of
\sigma
-algebra, and let
A_{n}\geq 0
be a sequence of nonnegative random variables. If
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}{\,|\,}\mathcal{F}_{n}]=o_{P}(1)
, then
A_{n}=o_{P}(1)
.

Proof of Lemma
F.1
.

By Markov’s inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle B_{n}:=\mathbb{P}(A_{n}>\epsilon{\,|\,}\mathcal{F}_{n})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[A_{n}{\,|\,}\mathcal{F}_{n}]}{\epsilon}=o_{P}(1),

and
B_{n}\in[0,1]
are bounded random variables. For any subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{N}
, since
B_{n_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
, there exists a subsequence
\{n_{k_{i}}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
B_{n_{k_{i}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\to}}0
as
i\to\infty
. By the dominated convergence theorem, we have
\mathbb{E}[B_{n_{k_{i}}}]\to 0
, or equivalently,

\mathbb{P}(A_{n_{k_{i}}}>\epsilon)\to 0.

Therefore, for any subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{N}
, there exists a subsequence
\{n_{k_{i}}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
A_{n_{k_{i}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
as
i\to\infty
. By the arbitrariness of
\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, we know
A_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
as
n\to\infty
, which completes the proof.
∎
