Unique continuation for the wave equation based on a discontinuous Galerkin time discretization Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Erik Burman, Janosch Preuss

Abstract
We consider a stable unique continuation problem for the wave equation where the initial data is lacking and the solution is reconstructed using measurements in some subset of the bulk domain. Typically fairly sophisticated space-time methods have been used in previous work to obtain stable and accurate solutions to this reconstruction problem. Here we propose to solve the problem using a standard discontinuous Galerkin method for the temporal discretization and continuous finite elements for the space discretization. Error estimates are established under a geometric control condition. We also investigate two preconditioning strategies which can be used to solve the arising globally coupled space-time system by means of simple time-stepping procedures. Our numerical experiments test the performance of these strategies and highlight the importance of the geometric control condition for reconstructing the solution beyond the data domain.



keywords

Unique continuation, data assimilation, wave equation, finite element method, discontinuous Galerkin, preconditioning, geometric control condition

Introduction

In this article we consider a Lipschitz-stable unique continuation problem for the wave equation.
For a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
, with smooth strictly convex boundary
\partial\Omega
, and
T>0
we define the space-time
cylinder
Q=(0,T)\times\Omega
with lateral boundary
\Sigma:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
.
Analogously, for a subset
\omega\subset\Omega
we define the data domain
\omega_{T}:=(0,T)\times\omega
.
We are now concerned with the following data assimilation problem:
Find
u:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle(\Box u,u)

\displaystyle=(0,0)\text{ in }Q\times\Sigma,

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=u_{\omega}\text{ in }\omega_{T},

(2)

where
\Box u:=\partial_{t}^{2}u-\triangle u
and
u_{\omega}
is the known data. Note that only the restriction of

u
to the data set is given, whereas the wave-displacement and velocity at time
t=0
in
\Omega
are
unknown.

Problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
enjoys Lipschitz stability if the
set
\omega_{T}\subset Q
fulfills the so called geometric control condition (GCC), see
[
3
,
4
]
for a precise definition thereof.

Theorem 1
(Lipschitz stability).

Assume that
\omega_{T}\subset Q
fulfills the GCC.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
\phi\in H^{1}(Q)
the following stability estimate holds:

\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}\phi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}\leqslant C\left(\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega_{T})}+\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}+\left\lVert\Box\phi\right\rVert_{H^{-1}(Q)}\right).

(3)

Proof.

See
[
8
, Remark A.5]
.

Unless otherwise stated, below we assume that the GGC holds for the chosen geometry.

1.1
Literature review

Recently, at least five different methods
[
13
,
9
,
7
,
19
,
14
]
for the numerical solution of problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
have been proposed.
Before discussing these approaches let us briefly explain a common principle that drives the design of all numerical methods based on the stability estimate
eq.

3
. To this end, let
u_{h}\in H^{1}(Q)
be some discrete approximation of the solution
u
of problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
.
The idea is then to apply
Theorem

1
to
\phi=u-u_{h}
, which leads to an error estimate provided that the terms on the right hand side of
eq.

3
, in particular the PDE residual
\left\lVert\Box(u-u_{h})\right\rVert_{H^{-1}(Q)}
, can be controlled. Let us distinguish the recently proposed methods based on how they strive to
achieve this aim.

•

The first method proposed in
[
13
]
in fact preceeded the stability estimate in
eq.

3
. Here the discretization was required to fulfill

\Box u_{h}\in L^{2}(0,T,H^{-1}(\Omega))
to repect the PDE constraint. To this end,
C^{1}
-conforming (space-time) elements have been used. Verifying discrete inf-sup stability in
this setting is an intricate problem. Alternatively, the authors propose a stabilized formulation which is shown to convergence under an additional regularity assumption on
an auxiliary variable, see
[
13
, Proposition 2]
.

•

Subsequently, a class of methods
[
9
,
7
]
based on a primal-dual stabilized finite element framework introduced in
[
5
]
were proposed. Here, the problem is formulated as a PDE constrained optimization problem and discretized. Then, (weakly)-consistent stabilization terms are added to the discretization which allow to control the PDE residual without having to evaluate dual norms or to use exotic discretization spaces.
Based on this strategy, in reference
[
9
]
a piecewise affine continuous finite element method in space was coupled with backward differences in time to yield a provably convergent
first order method for problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
.
We point out that even though backward differences in time were used, this is not a time-stepping method, as the solution of a globally coupled space-time system is required.

Since the method of
[
9
]
ultimately leads to space-time systems anyhow, it seems natural to treat problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2

directly using a full space-time discretization. In fact, such an approach has been considered in reference
[
7
]
. Therein the authors proposed an arbitrary order
H^{1}(Q)
-conforming
stabilized finite element method and derived optimal error estimates. An extension of the method to a closely related control problem has been presented in
[
8
]
.

•

Whereas the methods introduced in
[
9
,
7
]
avoid a direct evaluation of the PDE residual
\left\lVert\Box(u-u_{h})\right\rVert_{H^{-1}(Q)}
, in reference
[
14
]
a least-squares finite element method has been introduced which minimizes this quantity directly. The authors deal with the arising challenges of having to prove technical inf-sup conditions and the need to replace the negative index Sobolev norms by computable quantities to be able to solve the problem numerically. We remark that the framework introduced in this reference is fairly general which allows the additional treatment of data assimilation for the heat equation and the Cauchy problem for Poisson’s equation in this publication.

•

Finally, physics informed neural networks have been proposed in reference
[
19
]
to solve problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
and similar data assimilation problems. Here, the PDE residual is minimized in the
L^{2}
instead of the
H^{-1}
-norm on a set of training points and estimates on the generalization error of these networks have been derived based on
Theorem

1
. Let us mention that, even though this approach in its present form can yield accurate results for problems enjoying robust stability, it has recently been shown that it is not suitable yet to solve Helmholtz problems posed in geometrical configurations with poor stability, see reference
[
20
]
.

1.2
Motivation for and novelty of this paper

The methods discussed in
section

1.1
share the common feature of treating problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
in a space-time fashion.
This is reasonable since initial data
u(0,\cdot),u_{t}(0,\cdot)
for starting a time-marching procedure is not available.
Also, in order to apply
Theorem

1
to the error, one needs to ensure that
u_{h}
is in
H^{1}(Q)
, which renders the use of conforming space-time discretizations very natural and appealing.
However, space-time methods also come with some considerable challenges. Applying for example the method from
[
7
]
to a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
would
require to generate a non-trivial (recall that
\partial\Omega
is smooth) four-dimensional space-time mesh and a corresponding discretization which can be challenging to realize with common finite element software. Whereas this algorithmic challenge may be overcome, solving the arising extremely large and ill-conditioned linear systems appears like
a more daunting obstacle. Therefore, we want to investigate in this article to what extent problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
is amenable to the solution with computationally less-demanding time-marching methods.

To this end, we consider a fairly flexible time discretization provided by a discontinuous Galerkin method (dG), which is very well-established in the literature, see e.g.
[
16
,
17
,
15
]
. The dG method is known to allow for time-marching procedures provided that only weak continuity in time is imposed by the variational formulation. The analysis presented in this article shows however, that some stronger constraints which couple the problem globally in time cannot be avoided if one wants to preserve the optimal error estimates from reference
[
7
]
. The contribution of this manuscript is to identify these couplings explictly and then to introduce small perturbations thereof for which time-marching is possible.
These approximate solvers can then be used as preconditioners to solve the original globally-coupled space-time problem iteratively.
The advantage of this strategy is that it allows to solve problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
using a standard time-marching procedure and that it avoids space-time discretizations. Now we can compute solutions in spatially-three dimensional domains (
d=3
) up to moderate levels of precision. The current limitations of our approach are that the preconditioners lack robustness with respect refinement of the discretization and that adaptive refinement strategies have not been considered yet.

We remark that a dG method in time for an ill-posed heat equation has already been analyzed in reference
[
6
]
. However, the authors did not make use of the dG in time structure for solving the linear systems. This idea appears novel to us.

1.3
Structure of this paper

To show the generality of the approach with respect to time discretisation we consider both a semi-discretisation in space, with time continuous and a fully-discrete formulation with a discontinuous Galerkin approximation in time.
We introduce these methods in
section

2
and establish some auxiliary results valid for both approaches. The semi-discrete method in which time is still considered to be a continuous variable is analyzed in
section

3
, whereas analysis of the fully-discrete method can be found in
section

4
. The separate treatment of these two approaches allows us to identify very clearly the mechanism which couples provably convergent approximations of
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
globally in time at the discrete level.
Based on these insights, we propose two preconditioning strategies in
section

5
which can be used for the iterative solution of the arising linear systems. In
section

6
we investigate their performance in numerical experiments and explore further aspects which go beyond our analytical results. For instance, we investigate how the numerical method behaves when the GCC (which is crucial for the analysis) is violated. This article is accompanied by a
appendix

A
in which interpolation into tensor-product space-time finite element spaces is discussed.

2.1.2
Partition of the time axis

We introduce a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T
of the time axis into subintervals
I_{n}:=(t_{n},t_{n+1}),n=0,\ldots,N-1
.
For simplicity all time intervals are assumed to be of equal length

The discretization of the time axis implies a corresponding partition of the space-time cylinder into time slabs

Q^{n}:=I_{n}\times\Omega,\;\Sigma^{n}=I_{n}\times\Sigma,\;n=0,\ldots,N-1,\quad Q:=\cup_{n=0}^{N-1}Q^{n},\;\Sigma:=\cup_{n=0}^{N-1}\Sigma^{n}.

(5)

The space-time integrals on the slabs are denoted by

(u,v)_{Q^{n}}:=\int\limits_{I_{n}}\int\limits_{\Omega}uv\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t,\;a(u,v)_{Q^{n}}:=\!\int\limits_{I_{n}}\int\limits_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t,\;(u,v)_{\Sigma^{n}}=\int\limits_{I_{n}}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}uv\;\mathrm{d}S\;\mathrm{d}t

\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{Q^{n}}:=(v,v)_{Q^{n}}

The data domain and integrals thereon are defined similarly

\omega^{n}:=I_{n}\times\omega,\quad(u,v)_{\omega^{n}}:=\int\limits_{I_{n}}\int\limits_{\omega}uv\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}t,\quad\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\omega_{T}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}(u,v)_{\omega^{n}}.

A semi-discrete conforming FEM

Let us first ignore the partition of the time axis in our ansatz space and define

W^{{k}}_{h}:=H^{1}\left(0,T;V_{h}^{k}\right).

(6)

(8)

for
\underline{\mathbf{U_{h}}}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k}}_{h}
and
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}\in
for
\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}}}_{h}
with

denotes the outer normal vector of
\Omega

Note that for a sufficiently regular solution

1
we obtain for

via integration by parts in space that

We will search for approximate solutions of problem
eq.

1
-
eq.

2
as stationary points of the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\underline{u}_{1}-u_{\omega}\right\rVert_{\omega_{T}}^{2}+A[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]+\frac{1}{2}S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h})-\frac{1}{2}S_{h}^{\ast}(\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}),\quad

(9)

for
(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\in\mathcal{W}^{{k}}_{h}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}}}_{h}
.
Here, the first and second term of the Lagrangian incorporate the data and PDE constraints, respectively. The contributions
S_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
S_{h}^{\ast}(\cdot,\cdot)
represent stabilization terms which are defined as follows. We define a spatial continuous interior penalty term
J(\cdot,\cdot)
, which penalizes the jump of the gradient
\llbracket\nabla\underline{u}_{1}\rrbracket
over interior facets, a Galerkin-least squares term

which enforces the PDE at the element level, a term

which ensures that
\underline{u}_{2}=\partial_{t}\underline{u}_{1}
and a term for boundary stability:

\displaystyle J(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}h(\llbracket\nabla\underline{u}_{1}\rrbracket,\llbracket\nabla\underline{w}_{1}\rrbracket)_{F}\;\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle G(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h^{2}(\partial_{t}\underline{u}_{2}-\Delta\underline{u}_{1},\partial_{t}\underline{w}_{2}-\Delta\underline{w}_{1})_{K}\;\mathrm{d}t,\quad

\displaystyle I_{0}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}(\underline{u}_{2}-\partial_{t}\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{2}-\partial_{t}\underline{w}_{1})_{Q^{n}},\quad R(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}h^{-1}(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{1})_{\Sigma^{n}}.

The complete primal stabilization is given as a sum of these terms:

S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=J(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+G(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+R(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+I_{0}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}).

(10)

To stabilize the dual variable
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}
we will use:

S^{\ast}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}\big\{(\underline{y}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{Q^{n}}+a(\underline{y}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{Q^{n}}+(\underline{y}_{2},\underline{z}_{2})_{Q^{n}}+h^{-1}(\underline{y}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{\Sigma^{n}}\big\}.

(11)

These stabilizations give rise to semi-norms, respectively norms:

\left\lvert\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}\right\rvert_{S_{h}}:=S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h})^{1/2},\quad\left\lVert\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}\right\rVert_{S_{h}^{\ast}}:=S_{h}^{\ast}(\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})^{1/2}.

(12)

We will later see that the following expression defines a norm on
\mathcal{W}^{{k}}_{h}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}}}_{h}
:

|\hskip-0.85358pt\|(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\|\hskip-0.85358pt|^{2}_{h}:=\left\lvert\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}\right\rvert_{S_{h}}^{2}+\left\lVert\underline{u}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{\omega_{T}}+\left\lVert\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}\right\rVert_{S_{h}^{\ast}}^{2}.

(13)

The first order optimality conditions characterizing critical points of the Lagrangian take the form:
Find
(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\in\mathcal{W}^{{k}}_{h}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}}}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}+A[\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]+S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

\displaystyle=(u_{\omega},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}},\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k}}_{h},

\displaystyle A[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]-S_{h}^{\ast}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}}}_{h}.

(14)

This can be written in a more compact way:

B_{h}[(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}),(\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h})]=(u_{\omega_{T}},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}},

(15)

where

\displaystyle B_{h}[(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}),(\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h})]:=

\displaystyle(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}+A[\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]+S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

\displaystyle+A[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]-S_{h}^{\ast}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}).

(16)

In

2.3
A fully-discrete FEM using dG in time

To discretize time, let
\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})
denote the space of polynomials up to degree
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
on
I_{n}
and define a discontinuous (in-time) finite element space:

W^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}:=\otimes_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes V_{h}^{k},\quad\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}:=W^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}\times W^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t},\quad q\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that functions in
\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}
are allowed to be discontinuous between time-slabs.
Hence, we require a notation for jumps in time.
Let

v^{n}_{\pm}(x):=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}v(x,t_{n}\pm s),\text{ and }\llbracket v^{n}\rrbracket:=v_{+}^{n}-v_{-}^{n}.

(17)

Since continuity in time is now no longer ensured by the spaces, we will need to augment the semi-discrete Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
from

9
by a stabilization term
S^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\Delta t}
which imposes sufficient temporal regularity of the primal variable, i.e.

\mathcal{L}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}):=\mathcal{L}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})+\frac{1}{2}S^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\Delta t}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}),\qquad(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\in\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}},{q_{\ast}}}_{h,\Delta t},

(18)

where

S^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\Delta t}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\underline{I}_{1}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+\underline{I}_{2}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

(19)

\displaystyle\underline{I}_{1}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\{\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\llbracket\underline{u}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket,\llbracket\underline{w}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket)_{\Omega}+\Delta t(\llbracket\nabla\underline{u}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket,\llbracket\nabla\underline{w}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket)_{\Omega}\right\},

\displaystyle\underline{I}_{2}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\llbracket\underline{u}_{2}^{n}\rrbracket,\llbracket\underline{w}_{2}^{n}\rrbracket)_{\Omega}.

The first order optimality conditions then take the form:
Find
(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\in\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}},{q_{\ast}}}_{h,\Delta t}

B[(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}),(\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h})]=(u_{\omega},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}

5
Preconditioning strategies

The fully-discrete method defined in
eq.

20
is globally coupled in time. This is due to the presence of the term
S^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\Delta t}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})
defined in
eq.

19
, which contains jump operators on both variables
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
and
\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}
. Consequently, solving
eq.

20
with a direct solver would require to assemble and factorize a
d+1
-dimensional linear system, which is prohibitively expensive for
d=3
. Hence, iterative solutions strategies must be investigated.

In this setting Krylov methods such as GMRes are very attractive as they only require matrix-vector multiplications. Note here that application of the space-time matrix resulting from
eq.

20
can be easily implemented time-slab wise which obliterates the need for assembling an enormous space-time system. Unfortunately, an unpreconditioned GMRes iteration is not suitable for solving our problem even in the simplest possible setting as
fig.

1
demonstrates. The column ‘NoPre’ in the table gives the GMRes iteration numbers for a simple problem on the unit interval discretized with piecewise linear elements, which are almost as high as the total number of degrees of freedom (‘ndof’ in the second column). Hence, a preconditioning strategy is required. In this chapter we propose two simple strategies based on time-marching procedures.

5.1

A simple preconditioner can be obtained by relaxing the bi-directional coupling
\uparrow\downarrow
in
S^{\uparrow\downarrow}
to a forward coupling
\uparrow
in time only. This is achieved by replacing
S^{\uparrow\downarrow}
in
eq.

21
by

S^{\uparrow}_{\Delta t}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=\underline{I}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+\underline{I}_{2}^{\uparrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}),

(32)

\displaystyle\underline{I}_{1}^{\uparrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\{\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\llbracket\underline{u}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket,\underline{w}_{1,+}^{n})_{\Omega}+\Delta t(\llbracket\nabla\underline{u}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket,\nabla\underline{w}_{1,+}^{n})_{\Omega}\right\},

\displaystyle\underline{I}_{2}^{\uparrow}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\llbracket\underline{u}_{2}^{n}\rrbracket,\underline{w}_{2,+}^{n})_{\Omega}.

This allows to solve for
(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})
time-slab by time-slab, i.e. starting with
Q^{0}
and progressing one slab
Q^{n}
at a time. Indeed, if the solution on
Q^{n-1}
has been determined then in particular
\underline{u}_{j,-}^{n}
for
j=1,2
are known and can be shifted to the right hand side of the linear system as is common practice in the dG time-stepping method, see e.g. the monograph of Thomée
[
25
]
for details. This means that the cost of one application of the preconditioner is the same as the solution of a wave equation with a dG time-stepping method.

Since the dual stabilization and the slab-local part
S_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
of the primal stabilization remain fully active in the preconditioner, it remains possible to choose the polynomial degrees of primal and dual variable independent of each other.

•

In the following we will refer to the strategy using the full dual order, i.e.
k_{\ast}=k
and
q_{\ast}=q
, as ‘M-f’.

•

The choice using minimal dual order
k_{\ast}=1,q_{\ast}=0
will be denoted by ‘M-l’.

5.2
Decoupled forward-backward solve

The strategy proposed in the previous subsection treats primal and dual variable in a monolithic fashion. Alternatively, we can (approximately) solve for these variables separately by exploiting the fact that
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}
converges to zero, see
lemma

13
. Hence, we may set
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}=0
in
eq.

20

and solve for
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
and subsequently determine a corrected
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}
based on
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
. This requires, however, that
A[\cdot,\cdot]
on its own provides a consistent and stable discretization of the wave equation which is currently not the case. Consequently, we have to replace
A[\cdot,\cdot]
by an enriched bilinear form
\tilde{A}[\cdot,\cdot]
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]

\displaystyle=A[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]

\displaystyle+(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{y}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}+\frac{\lambda}{h}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{y}_{1})_{\Sigma^{n}}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\{(\llbracket\underline{u}_{1}^{n}\rrbracket,\underline{y}_{2,+}^{n})_{\Omega}+(\llbracket\underline{u}_{2}^{n}\rrbracket,\underline{y}_{1,+}^{n})_{\Omega}\right\}

(33)

with the Nitsche parameter
\lambda>0
sufficiently large as usual. We remark that this enriched bilinear form is a minor modification of a method originally proposed and analyzed in
[
17
]
. In order to account for the presence of the jumps terms in
\tilde{A}[\cdot,\cdot]
, the dual stabilization has to be adapted as follows:

\tilde{S}^{\ast}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})=S^{\ast}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})+\Delta t\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}\left\{(\underline{y}_{1,+}^{n},\underline{z}_{1,+}^{n})_{\Omega}+(\underline{y}_{2,+}^{n},\underline{z}_{2,+}^{n})_{\Omega}\right\}.

In
\tilde{A}
we have also added a Luenberger observer type term on
\omega_{T}
. It is known that this can improve the stability of the corrected dynamics (see
[
11
,
12
]
), which may improve the convergence of the iterations. Since the perturbation is consistent it is straightforward to include in the error analysis above.
The first-order optimality conditions then become:
Find
(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})\in\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}\times\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}},{q_{\ast}}}_{h,\Delta t}
such that

\displaystyle(\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}+\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]+\tilde{S}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})

\displaystyle=(u_{\omega},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}},\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t},

(34)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]-\tilde{S}_{h}^{\ast}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h})

\displaystyle=(u_{\omega},\underline{y}_{1})_{\omega_{T}},\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}},{q_{\ast}}}_{h,\Delta t},

(35)

for
\tilde{S}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}):=S_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})+S^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\Delta t}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h})
.
Assuming
k=k_{\ast}
and
q=q_{\ast}
we propose the following preconditioning strategy, which we will refer to as ‘DFB’.

1.

Set
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}=0
in
eq.

35
and solve

\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}]=(u_{\omega},\underline{y}_{1})_{\omega_{T}},\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{Y}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k_{\ast}},{q_{\ast}}}_{h,\Delta t}

(36)

by marching forward in time to obtain
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
.

2.

Then insert
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h}
in
eq.

34
and solve

\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]=(u_{\omega}-\underline{u}_{1},\underline{w}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}-\tilde{S}_{h}(\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}),\quad\forall\;\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h}\in\mathcal{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t},

(37)

backward in time to obtain
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}
.

We point out that we integrate by parts in
eq.

37
to write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\tilde{A}[\underline{\mathbf{W}}_{h},\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}]=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}\big\{-(\underline{w}_{2},\partial_{t}\underline{z}_{1}+\underline{z}_{2})_{Q^{n}}+a(\underline{w}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{Q^{n}}-(\underline{w}_{1},\partial_{t}\underline{z}_{2})_{Q^{n}}\\
&-(\nabla\underline{w}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{n},\underline{z}_{1})_{\Sigma^{n}}+\frac{\lambda}{h}(\underline{w}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{\Sigma^{n}}\big\}+(\underline{w}_{1},\underline{z}_{1})_{\omega_{T}}-(\underline{w}_{2,+}^{0},\underline{z}_{1,+}^{0})_{\Omega}-(\underline{w}_{1,+}^{0},\underline{z}_{2,+}^{0})_{\Omega}\\
&+(\underline{w}_{2,-}^{N},\underline{z}_{1,-}^{N})_{\Omega}+(\underline{w}_{1,-}^{N},\underline{z}_{2,-}^{N})_{\Omega}-\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-2}\left\{(\underline{w}_{2,-}^{n+1},\llbracket\underline{z}_{1}^{n+1}\rrbracket)_{\Omega}+(\underline{w}_{1,-}^{n+1},\llbracket\underline{z}_{2}^{n+1}\rrbracket)_{\Omega}\right\},\end{aligned}

which then indeed allows to obtain
\underline{\mathbf{Z}}_{h}
time-slab wise by starting at
n=N-1
and progressing backwards in time.
We also mention that this strategy is limited to the setting in which primal and dual polynomial degrees coincide as we need to make sure that the linear systems in
eq.

36
and
eq.

37
are both well-posed.

5.3
Comparison

Let us briefly compare the proposed strategies. Both approaches only require ingredients available in standard dG time-stepping methods. In particular, only linear systems on the time slabs
Q^{n}
need to be solved. When the full polynomial degree for the dual variables is used, i.e.
k_{\ast}=k
and
q_{\ast}=q
, then the linear systems on the slabs to be inverted in the decoupled method (‘DFB’) are half the size of the ones in the monolithic method (‘M-f’). This is because of the monolithic treatment of primal and dual variable in the latter. However, if the variant ‘M-l’ of the monolithic method with minimal dual polynomial order is used, then the difference in size between the linear systems quickly diminishes as the primal polynomial order increases. Therefore, it seems fair to focus in the numerical experiments presented in
section

6
on a comparison between the ‘DFB’ and ‘M-l’ methods.

Appendix A
Interpolation in space-time

In reference
[
21
]
an interpolation theory for unfitted space-time finite element spaces has been developed.
Here we recall the results for the fitted case and give some extensions which are
required for the applications in this paper. The final aim of this appendix is to prove
lemma

5
and
lemma

11
.

We first define interpolation on the extended time slices

\hat{Q}^{n}=I_{n}\times\hat{\Omega}
and
\hat{Q}=I\times\hat{\Omega}

and then obtain the result on the orignal domains by means of a Sobolev extension argument.
To this end, let

\hat{V}_{h}^{k}:=\{u\in H^{1}(\hat{\Omega})\mid u|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(K)\;\forall K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},\qquad\hat{W}^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}:=\otimes_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k}.

We start in
section

A.1
by defining a purely temporal interpolation operator and introduce its purely spatial counterpart in
section

A.2
.
In
section

A.3
we then concatenate these operators to achieve space-time interpolation. To seprate spatial and temporal continuity, we define
t
-anisotropic Sobolev spaces

H^{q,k}(\hat{Q}):=\left\{u\mid\partial_{t}^{p}D_{x}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(\hat{Q}),\frac{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert}{k}+\frac{p}{q}\leqslant 1\right\}.

(39)

For
q=k
the isotropic Sobolev spaces are retained:
H^{k,k}(\hat{Q})=H^{k}(\hat{Q})
.

A.1
Interpolation in time

For
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
define
\pi^{q}_{n}:H^{0,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
by

(\pi^{q}_{n}(u)-u,v)_{\hat{Q}^{n}}=0\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}).

(40)

On sufficiently smooth functions,
\pi^{q}_{n}
coincides with a standard temporal
L^{2}(I_{n})
-projection
I^{q}_{n}
defined by

(I^{q}_{n}u,\chi)_{I_{n}}=(u,\chi)_{I_{n}}\quad\forall\;\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n}).

Lemma 15
.

For
u\in C^{0}(\hat{\Omega};L^{2}(I_{n}))
there holds

\pi^{q}_{n}u=I_{n}^{q}u\text{ in the }L^{2}(\hat{Q}^{n})\text{ sense.}

Moreover, for
u\in H^{0,1}(\hat{Q}^{n})
the temporal interpolation commutes with spatial derivatives

\partial_{x_{j}}\pi^{q}_{n}u=\pi^{q}_{n}\partial_{x_{j}}u,\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

Proof.

See
[
21
, Lemma 3.14]
.

We denote the temporal interpolation error by
\mathcal{L}^{q}_{n}:=\mathrm{id}-\pi^{q}_{n}
.
The stability and approximation properties of
\pi^{q}_{n}
are studied below.

Lemma 16
.

The following stability and approximation results hold.

(a)

\left\lVert\pi^{q}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\quad\forall u\in L^{2}(\hat{Q}^{n})
for
n=1,\ldots,N-1
.

(b)

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\pi^{q}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}\quad\forall u\in H^{1,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})
for
n=1,\ldots,N-1
.

(c)

For
n=1,\ldots,N-1
we have
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{q}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\Delta{t}^{l}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}

\forall u\in H^{l,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})
and
l\in\{0,\ldots,q+1\}
.

(d)

For
n=1,\ldots,N-1
we have
\left\lVert\partial_{t}\mathcal{L}^{q}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\Delta{t}^{l-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
for all
u\in H^{l,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})
and
l\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
.

Proof.

The results for
\Pi^{q}_{n}
have been shown in
[
21
, Lemma 3.15]
.
Note that in this reference the results (c) and (d) are only stated for
l\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
and
l\in\{2,\ldots,q+1\}
, respectively.
However, the case
l=0
in (c) immediately follows from (a) and the case
l=1
in (d) follows from (b).

A.2
Interpolation in space

In this section we consider a generic interval
\tilde{I}\subset(0,T)
and set
\tilde{Q}=\tilde{I}\times\hat{\Omega}
. Later, we will choose
\tilde{I}=(0,T)
to define an interpolation operator into the semi-discrete space
W^{{k}}_{h}
and
\tilde{I}=I_{n}
to define a time-slab-wise interpolation into the fully-discrete space
W^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}
.

Define the projector
\pi^{k}:L^{2}(\tilde{Q})\rightarrow L^{2}(\tilde{I})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
, so that for
u\in L^{2}(\tilde{Q})

(\pi^{k}(u)-u,v_{h})_{\tilde{Q}}=0,\quad\text{ for all }v_{h}\in L^{2}(\tilde{I})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k}.

(41)

On sufficiently smooth functions
\pi^{k}
coincides with a spatial
L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
projector
I^{k}:L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})\rightarrow\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
I^{k}:L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})\rightarrow\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
be the spatial
L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
projection operator. Then there holds for
u\in C^{0}(\tilde{I},L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}))

\pi^{k}u=I^{k}u\quad\text{in the }L^{2}(\tilde{Q})\text{ sense.}

(42)

Further, for
u\in H^{1,0}(\tilde{Q})
there holds:

\pi^{k}\partial_{t}u=\partial_{t}\pi^{k}u.

(43)

Proof.

See
[
18
, Lemma 3.3.5]
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{k}=\mathrm{id}-\pi^{k}
denote the spatial interpolation error. The next lemma shows that the projection into the semi-discrete space is stable and has the expected approximation properties.

Lemma 18
.

For the projector
\pi^{k}:L^{2}(\tilde{Q})\rightarrow L^{2}(\tilde{I})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
there hold the following stability and approximation results:

(a)

\left\lVert\pi^{k}u\right\rVert_{\tilde{Q}}\leqslant\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\tilde{Q}}\quad\forall u\in L^{2}(\tilde{Q})
,

(b)

\left\lVert\nabla\pi^{k}u\right\rVert_{\tilde{Q}}\leqslant C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,1}(\tilde{Q})}\quad\forall u\in H^{0,1}(\tilde{Q})
,

(c)

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{\tilde{Q}}\leqslant Ch^{s}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q})}\quad\forall u\in H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q}),\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
,

(d)

\left\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{\tilde{Q}}\leqslant Ch^{s-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s,0}(\tilde{Q})}\quad\forall u\in H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q}),\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
.

(e)

Also, for all
\forall u\in H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q})\cap C^{0}(\tilde{I},H^{2}(\hat{\Omega}))
and
s\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}
it holds that

\left(\int\limits_{\tilde{I}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}\leqslant Ch^{s-2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q})}.

Proof.

We only show (e) since the results (a)-(d) have already been established in
[
21
, Lemma 3.17]
.
Note that since
u\in C^{0}(\tilde{I},L^{2}(\hat{\Omega}))
we have
\mathcal{L}^{k}u=u-I^{k}u
.
Moreover, as
u(t,\cdot)\in H^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
and
d\leqslant 3
the standard Lagrange interpolation operator
I^{L}:H^{2}(\hat{\Omega})\rightarrow\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
is well-defined
and has the approximation property

\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}\leqslant Ch^{s-2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}(K)},\;\text{for }s\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}.

(44)

Then we have

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}\leqslant\left\lVert u-I^{L}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}+\left\lVert I^{L}u-I^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}.

The integrand in the second term is a discrete function to which the usual inverse inequalities can be applied:

\displaystyle\left\lVert I^{L}u-I^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}

\displaystyle\leqslant h^{-2}C\left\lVert I^{L}u-I^{k}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}

\displaystyle\leqslant h^{-2}C\left(\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}+\left\lVert I^{k}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}\right).

Note that
\left\lVert I^{k}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}\leqslant\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}
by the best approximation property of the

L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})
-projection.
Therefore, summing up over
K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
yields

h^{-2}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert I^{k}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}=h^{-2}\left\lVert I^{k}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}^{2}\leqslant h^{-2}\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{\Omega})}^{2}.

Combining these results it follows that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\tilde{I}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int\limits_{\tilde{I}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\{\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}+h^{-2}\left\lVert I^{L}u-u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant Ch^{2(s-2)}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,s}(\tilde{Q})}^{2},\;s\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}.

Using the result established above it is easy to deduce the interpolation results
required for analysis of the semi-discretization.

Proof of
lemma

5
for
\Pi_{h}=\Pi_{h}^{k}
:

To define an interpolation operator into
W^{{k}}_{h}
, we precompose
\pi^{k}
defined on
\tilde{I}=(0,T)
with a continuous (and degree independent) Sobolev extension operator
\mathcal{E}:H^{s}(Q)\rightarrow H^{s}(\hat{Q})
, see
[
24
, Theorem 5, Chapter VI]
. That is, let
\Pi_{h}^{k}u:=(\pi^{k}\mathcal{E}u)|_{Q}
. Then by construction
\Pi_{h}^{k}u\in L^{2}(\tilde{I})\otimes V_{h}^{k}
. Thanks to
lemma

17
we have for sufficiently regular
u
that

\partial_{t}\Pi_{h}^{k}u=(\partial_{t}\pi^{k}\mathcal{E}u)|_{Q}.=(\pi^{k}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}u)|_{Q}\in L^{2}(\tilde{I})\otimes V_{h}^{k}.

Hence,
\Pi_{h}^{k}u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;V_{h}^{k}\right)=W^{{k}}_{h}
.
To apply the interpolation results established in
lemma

18
to
\Pi_{h}^{k}
, we note that for any
D\in\{\mathrm{id},\nabla,\partial_{t}\}

we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert D(\Pi_{h}^{k}u-u)\right\rVert_{Q}=\left\lVert D(\pi^{k}\mathcal{E}u-\mathcal{E}u)\right\rVert_{Q}\leqslant\left\lVert D(\pi^{k}\mathcal{E}u-\mathcal{E}u)\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}}.

Using the interpolation results from
lemma

18
then allows to estimate the last expression by some power of
h

times
C\left\lVert\mathcal{E}u\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\hat{Q})}
which can be bounded by
C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}(Q)}
thanks to continuity of the Sobolev extension operator. Using this argument the interpolation results in
lemma

5
follow from
lemma

18
.

A.3
Interpolation in space-time

To define an interpolation operator into the fully-discrete spaces
W^{{k},{q}}_{h,\Delta t}
, some additional work is necessary. If we apply the results from
section

A.2
on
\tilde{I}=I_{n}
we obtain an interpolation operator
\pi^{k}_{n}
into
L^{2}(I_{n})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k}
for
n=0,\ldots,N-1
. The space-time interpolation operator
\pi^{q,k}_{n}
is then obtained by concatenating the purely spatial and temporal operators introduced in the previous two subsections. We define

\pi_{n}^{q,k}:H^{0,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes\hat{V}_{h}^{k},\quad\pi^{q,k}_{n}=\pi^{q}_{n}\circ\pi^{k}_{n}

(45)

and obtain the global operator by means of restriction to the time-slabs:

\Pi^{q,k}_{n}:H^{0,0}(\hat{Q})\rightarrow\hat{W}^{{q},{k}}_{h,\Delta t},\quad\Pi^{q,k}_{n}u=\pi^{q,k}_{n}u|_{\hat{Q}^{n}}.

(46)

An important ingredient for establishing the approximation results of these operators is that spatial derivatives commute with the temporal interpolation and vice versa (cf.
lemma

15
and
lemma

17
).

Theorem 19
.

For
l_{q},l_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
define
l_{\max}=\max{\{l_{q},l_{k}\}}
.
The following stability results and approximation error bounds hold:

(a)

\left\lVert\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{Q}^{n})}\leqslant\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
and
\left\lVert\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\hat{Q}^{n})}\leqslant C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
.

(b)

\left\lVert u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}}+h^{l_{k}}\right)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{\max}}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
,
for
l_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
.

(c)

\left\lVert\partial_{t}(u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u)\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}-1}+h^{l_{k}}\right)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{\max}+1}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
, for
l_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{2,\ldots,q+1\}
.

(d)

\left\lVert\nabla{(u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u)}\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}}+h^{l_{k}-1}\right)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{\max}+1}(\hat{Q}^{n})}
, for
l_{k}\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
.

(e)

For
u\in H^{l_{\max}+2}(\hat{Q}^{n})\cap C^{0}(I_{n},H^{2}(\hat{\Omega}))
we have

\left(\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}}+h^{l_{k}-2}\right)\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{\max}+2}(\hat{Q}^{n})}

for
l_{k}\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
.

Proof.

We only show (a) and (e) as the other results have been established in
[
21
, Corollary 3.19]
.

(a)

From
lemma

16
(a) and
lemma

18
(a) we obtain

\left\lVert\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}=\left\lVert\pi^{q}_{n}\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant\left\lVert\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}.

By using that spatial derivatives commute with the interpolation in time (see
lemma

15
) and applying
the stability results of
lemma

16
(a) and
lemma

18
(b)
we get

\left\lVert\nabla\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}=\left\lVert\pi^{q}_{n}\nabla\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant\left\lVert\nabla\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,1}(\hat{Q}^{n})}.

From the stability of the temporal interpolation,
lemma

16
(b), we have

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{Q^{n}}=\left\lVert\partial_{t}\pi^{q}_{n}(\pi^{k}_{n}u)\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C\left\lVert\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{H^{1,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}.

Since temporal derivatives commute with the spatial interpolation (as shown in
lemma

17
), we have

\left\lVert\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{H^{1,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}^{2}=\left\lVert\pi^{k}_{n}\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}_{n}}^{2}+\left\lVert\pi^{k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}_{n}}^{2}\leqslant C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{1,0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}^{2}

by means of
lemma

18
(a).

(e)

We utilize the splitting
u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u=\mathcal{L}^{q}u+\pi^{q}_{n}\mathcal{L}^{k}u
.
Hence,

\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\leqslant C\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{n}}\left\{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{q}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}+\left\lVert\pi^{q}\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\right\}.

Since spatial derivatives commute with
\mathcal{L}^{q}
(see
lemma

15
), we obtain from
lemma

16
(c):

\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{q}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\Delta{t}^{2l_{q}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{q},2}(\hat{Q}^{n})}^{2}\quad l_{q}\in\{0,\ldots,q+1\}.

For the other term we use stability of the temporal interpolation (see the
lemma

16
(a)) and

lemma

18
(e) to obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert\pi^{q}_{n}\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\limits_{I_{n}}\sum\limits_{K\in\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(K)}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant Ch^{2(l_{k}-2)}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})}^{2}

for
l_{k}\in\{2,\ldots,k+1\}
which yields the desired result.

To derive a bound on the approximation error at discrete time levels from the previous results, we need an additional discrete inverse inequality in time:

Lemma 20
(Discrete inverse inequality in time).

Let
M
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left\lVert u(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{M}\leqslant C\Delta{t}^{-1/2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{I_{n}\times M}

(47)

for all
u\in\mathcal{P}^{q}(I_{n})\otimes L^{2}(M)
and
\tau\in\{t_{n},t_{n+1}\}
.

Proof.

See
[
21
, Lemma 3.20]
. In this reference the case
\tau=t_{n}
has been shown, however, the proof for
\tau=t_{n+1}
is completely analogous.

Lemma 21
(Approximation error bounds at fixed time levels).

At the top and bottom of the time slice the following approximation bounds for the traces hold true:

(a)

For
u\in H^{l_{q},0}(\hat{Q}^{n})\cap H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})
we have

\left\lVert(u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u)(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\hat{\Omega}}\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}-1/2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{q},0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}+\Delta{t}^{-1/2}h^{l_{k}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})}\right),

(48)

with
\tau\in\{t_{n},t_{n+1}\}
for
l_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,k_{s}+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
,

(b)

For
u\in H^{l_{q},0}(\hat{Q}^{n})\cap H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla(u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u)(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\hat{\Omega}}

(49)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\Delta{t}^{l_{q}-1/2}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{H^{l_{q},0}(\hat{Q}^{n})}+\Delta{t}^{-1/2}h^{l_{k}-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})}\right),

with
\tau\in\{t_{n},t_{n+1}\}
, for
l_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,k_{s}+1\}
and
l_{q}\in\{1,\ldots,q+1\}
.

Proof.

We will show (b). The proof of (a) is analogous and can be found in
[
21
, Lemma 3.21]
.
Recall the splitting
u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u=\mathcal{L}^{q}u+\pi^{q}_{n}\mathcal{L}^{k}u

An application of the triangle inequality yields

\left\lVert\nabla(u-\pi^{q,k}_{n}u)(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\hat{\Omega}}\leqslant\left\lVert\nabla(\mathcal{L}^{q}u)(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\hat{\Omega}}+\left\lVert\nabla(\pi^{q}_{n}\mathcal{L}^{k}u)(\tau,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\hat{\Omega}}

To treat the second term, we can apply the discrete inverse inequality in time, see the

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\Delta t)^{-1/2}\left\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}^{k}u\right\rVert_{\hat{Q}^{n}}\leqslant C(\Delta t)^{-1/2}h^{l_{k}-1}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{0,l_{k}}(\hat{Q}^{n})}

for

The transformed function and operator are given by

for

.
Combining these estimates yields the claim.
