Probabilistic Unrolling: Scalable, Inverse-Free Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Latent Gaussian Models
Alexander Lin, Bahareh Tolooshams, Yves Atchadé, Demba Ba

Abstract
Latent Gaussian models have a rich history in statistics and machine learning, with applications ranging from factor analysis to compressed sensing to time series analysis. The classical method for maximizing the likelihood of these models is the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm. For problems with high-dimensional latent variables and large datasets, EM scales poorly because it needs to invert as many large covariance matrices as the number of data points. We introduce probabilistic unrolling , a method that combines Monte Carlo sampling with iterative linear solvers to circumvent matrix inversion. Our theoretical analyses reveal that unrolling and backpropagation through the iterations of the solver can accelerate gradient estimation for maximum likelihood estimation. In experiments on simulated and real data, we demonstrate that probabilistic unrolling learns latent Gaussian models up to an order of magnitude faster than gradient EM, with minimal losses in model performance.



1
Introduction

Latent variable models with Gaussian prior and Gaussian likelihood, i.e.
latent Gaussian models
(LGMs), are popular and powerful tools within statistics and machine learning. They have found applications in many settings, such as factor analysis
(
Basilevsky 2009
)
, sparse Bayesian learning
(
Tipping 2001
)
, state-space models
(
Durbin & Koopman 2012
)
, and neural linear models
(
Ober & Rasmussen 2019
)
. In these models, the means and/or covariances of the Gaussian distributions are functions of parameters that must be optimized to fit observed data.

The expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
is a popular way to optimize the parameters by maximum likelihood estimation. One variant called
gradient EM

(
Lange 1995
)
implements the M-step through a single iteration of gradient descent. For problems with high-dimensional latent variables and many training examples, gradient EM scales poorly due to the need to invert as many large covariance matrices as the number of examples.

Advances in numerical linear algebra have demonstrated, in various contexts, that iterative solvers often provide a much faster alternative to matrix inversion
(
Saad 2003
;
Ubaru et al. 2017
;
Gardner et al. 2018
;
Lin et al. 2022b
)
. A separate, burgeoning literature on unrolled optimization has shown theoretical and practical benefits to differentiating through the iterations of deterministic optimizers
(
Maclaurin et al. 2015
;
Shaban et al. 2019
;
Ablin et al. 2020
;
Tolooshams & Ba 2022
;
Malézieux et al. 2021
)
. This literature begs questions as to the potential benefits, in a latent variable setting, of unrolling the iterations of a sampler (i.e. stochastic solver), and differentiating through them.

Contributions
We introduce
probabilistic unrolling
, a computational framework that accelerates maximum likelihood estimation for large-scale, high-dimensional LGMs.
Our method provides a way to run gradient EM without matrix inversions. Specifically, we design iterative linear solvers to yield the probabilistic quantities needed by the EM algorithm (i.e. posterior means and covariance samples). Our method reduces the complexity of gradient EM from a cubic function of the latent dimension to a quadratic function in the general case, and a linear function in special cases.

We theoretically analyze the faithfulness of probabilistic unrolling to gradient EM when encountering two sources of error: (a) the
statistical error
from using a finite number of covariance samples, and (b) the
optimization error
from stopping the solver before convergence. We provide bounds for both of these factors, producing insights on how to pick the number of samples and the number of solver iterations. Finally, we show that our method can further improve its approximation to the true EM gradient by backpropagating through the unrolled iterations of the solver.

Probabilistic unrolling can be viewed as training a recurrent network in which each layer applies a matrix operation from the unrolled linear solver.
We implement this highly structured architecture in modern deep learning frameworks to further benefit from GPU acceleration. We perform several experiments with simulated and real data, showing that probabilistic unrolling can fit LGMs of practical interest up to
70
times faster than gradient EM. Our code is available at
https://github.com/al5250/prob-unroll
.

Background: Latent Gaussian Model

Let
\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
denote
N
i.i.d. observations, each associated with a latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}
. In a LGM, the
prior
on each latent variable and
likelihood
(i.e. conditional distribution) of each observation both follow Gaussian distributions,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}),

(1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}),\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

The prior and likelihood depend on a set of
canonical parameters
(
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and a diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
) that form the means and covariances of the Gaussian distributions. The canonical parameters are themselves functions of the model’s
free parameters

\boldsymbol{\theta}
, which are individual values that can be learned through maximum likelihood estimation.

Examples

The LGM (
1
) generalizes many models within statistics and machine learning. Some famous examples include
(a)
factor analysis
, a probabilistic generalization of PCA
(
Basilevsky 2009
)
, (b)
sparse Bayesian learning
, a Bayesian approach to compressed sensing
(
Wipf & Rao 2004
)
, and (c)
state-space models
, one of the most popular class of probabilistic time series models
(
Durbin & Koopman 2012
)
. With the advent of deep learning, the LGM class has broadened to include complex, non-linear structures such as (d)
neural linear models
, i.e. neural networks whose trainable weights correspond to free parameters
(
Ober & Rasmussen 2019
)
. For each of these models (and others), we work out the definition of free parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and how they map to the canonical parameters in Appendix
A
.

Missing Data
In many applications of LGMs,
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
may have missing values, i.e. we may not observe all its entries. To account for missing data, we assume that for each
n
, we observe
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
,
where the mask
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{n}\times M}
is a row-wise subset of the
M\times M
identity matrix.

EM Inference
To fit the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
to data
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(N)}
, we perform maximum likelihood estimation or, equivalently, minimize the negative log-likelihood,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\log p(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\log\int p(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\theta})d\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}.

Due to the latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}
, one common approach to minimizing (
2
) is to use the
expectation-maximization
(EM) algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
. EM revolves around the
\mathcal{Q}
-function, which is defined for any
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\}\in\Theta\times\Theta
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{q}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}),

(3)

\displaystyle{q}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})}[-\log p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})].

The
\mathcal{Q}
-function is called the
expected complete-data negative log-likelihood
because it averages the negative log-likelihood of the observed data
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
and the unobserved data
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}
over all possible realizations of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}

(
Bishop & Nasrabadi 2006
, Ch. 9)
. EM iterations repeatedly alternate between constructing
\mathcal{Q}
and minimizing it to make progress on
\mathcal{L}
: Given a current solution
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}}
, the
E
-step computes the posterior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})
to form the function
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})
, defined for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
. The
M
-step then finds a new solution
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{new}}
such that
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{new}}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})\leq\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})
. This guarantees that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{new}})\leq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})
.

Variants of EM differ in how they implement the
M
-step.
Classical EM

(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
solves an optimization problem, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{new}}:=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}})
.
We focus on a computationally-simpler alternative called
gradient EM

(
Lange 1995
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{new}}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}}-\alpha\cdot\nabla_{1}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\text{old}}),

(4)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the step size and
\nabla_{1}\mathcal{Q}
means the gradient with respect to the first argument of
\mathcal{Q}
, as defined in (
3
).

EM for the LGM
For latent Gaussian models,
\mathcal{Q}
and its gradient are computable in closed-form. Each
{q}^{(n)}
in (
3
) simplifies to (dropping the index
n
for convenience):

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}

(5)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}})+c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},

where, for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, we define the quantities

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

and the posterior
p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}},\boldsymbol{\theta})\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}.

(7)

The derivation for equations (
5
)-(
7
) is given in Appendix
B
.

Computational Challenges
Gradient EM involves computing the gradient of (
5
) (which we call the
exact gradient
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\nabla_{1}q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(8)

Since
\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
depends on the posterior moments
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, it requires inverting a large matrix of size
D\times D
. This has time cost
\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
and storage cost
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
, which becomes prohibitive for large
D
. Furthermore, for
N
different data vectors, we need to compute
N
posterior moments
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, which requires
N
separate matrix inversions.

We now arrive at the main goal of the paper: In the ensuing sections, we introduce a computational framework called
probabilistic unrolling
that can provably accelerate gradient EM by avoiding explicit matrix inversions. This allows us to fit latent Gaussian models at substantially greater scale in high dimensions
D
and for large dataset sizes
N
.

3
Method: Probabilistic Unrolling

Probabilistic unrolling circumvents matrix inversion by iteratively solving multiple linear systems in parallel. We design the systems to perform posterior inference, i.e. the solutions are the posterior mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and covariance samples distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. We use these quantities to estimate the EM objective (
5
) and its gradient
\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(
8
). This process requires less time and memory than computing
\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
directly. We also show that backpropagating through the linear solvers further improves our estimation of
\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

From a deep learning perspective, the overall method looks like a recurrent network (Fig.
1
). We can view the unrolled sequence of solver iterations as a
recurrent encoder
, with weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, that takes the observed data
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}
and refines
hidden states
representing the distribution
p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}},\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The hidden states are then passed through an
output layer
, also parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, to evaluate the loss (
5
). Training this network is equivalent to running gradient EM for the LGM.

Figure 1:
The probabilistic unrolling architecture: The data point
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}
, mask
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, and parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
define the linear operator
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and construct the matrix
\mathbb{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. A linear solver, unrolled for
I
steps, solves the matrix equation
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, yielding the posterior mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and samples
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
with covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. These posterior quantities are used to compute either the output gradient (
16
) or network gradient (
17
) to approximate the true EM gradient.

3.1
Monte Carlo Gradient EM

In high-dimensional settings, inverting a matrix to compute
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the main bottleneck of (
5
). The first step of our method replaces the trace term containing
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with an unbiased estimator. Given any square matrix
\mathbb{A}
and a sample
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

(
Skilling 1989
;
Hutchinson 1989
)
. Using
K>1
independent samples
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,\boldsymbol{\theta}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{K,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
(to reduce variance) leads to the following approximation of (
5
),

\displaystyle q^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}-\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}

(9)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}+c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}.

Eq. (
9
) satisfies
\mathbb{E}[q^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})]=q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})
, where the expectation is taken with respect to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,\boldsymbol{\theta}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{K,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We now define the
Monte Carlo gradient

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\nabla_{1}q^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(10)

which can take the place of
\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for updating
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in gradient EM.
The estimator satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\boldsymbol{g}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Constructing Samples
The question remains as to how we draw each sample
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Consider independent random vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, and let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}.

(11)

It follows from properties of Gaussian random vectors that
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, where
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is defined in (
6
). Then, we let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K.

(12)

As a result,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
has covariance
\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

3.2
Linear Systems and Iterative Solvers

Although the large covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is no longer explicitly written in the new objective (
9
), it still appears in the definitions for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in (
7
) and (
12
), respectively. In this section, we show how to obtain
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}

without
explicitly forming the covariance matrix.

First, we cast
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as the solutions to linear systems,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hskip 15.00002pt\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

(13)

where
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}
is defined in (
6
). Then, we solve (
13
) using an
iterative linear solver

(
Saad 2003
)
. For a system
\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
, iterative solvers refine a solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{\langle i\rangle}
over iterations
i=1,\ldots,I
until
\boldsymbol{x}^{\langle I\rangle}\approx\mathbb{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}
. At iteration
i
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\langle i+1\rangle}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\langle i\rangle}+\boldsymbol{p}^{\langle i\rangle},

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{p}^{\langle i\rangle}
is the search direction. Different solvers vary in how they construct
\boldsymbol{p}^{\langle i\rangle}
. Examples of popular solvers include
gradient descent
,
steepest descent
, and
conjugate gradient

(
Saad 2003
)
, which we review in Appendix
D
.

3.3
Gradients from Truncated Linear Solvers

High-dimensional latent spaces
D
may require a large number of iterations
I
(hence a high computational cost) to obtain exact solutions. Thus, in practice, it is desirable to run the solver for small
I
, which leads to approximations
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\langle I\rangle}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
of the true quantities
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. This section proposes two ways to obtain an approximate EM gradient from these partial solutions
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\langle I\rangle}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. We defer a theoretical analysis of the gradient error to Section
5
.

First, we substitute
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\langle I\rangle}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
in (
9
), i.e.

\displaystyle q^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\langle I\rangle})^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\langle I\rangle}

(15)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\langle I\rangle})^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}^{\langle I\rangle}+c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},

which satisfies
\lim_{I\to\infty}q^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})=q^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})
.

Option 1: Output Gradient
We can take the gradient of (
15
) in a manner similar to (
10
) to obtain the
output gradient

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\nabla_{1}{q}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(16)

which satisifies
\lim_{I\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
We interpret this gradient as backpropagating through only the output layer of the architecture in Fig.
1
, hence the terminology.

Option 2: Network Gradient
Since the inputs to the output layer
(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\langle I\rangle}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}})
are themselves functions of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, a natural question arises as to the benefits of additionally propagating the gradient through these quantities (and the linear solver). This leads to the
network gradient

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[{q}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\langle I\rangle}\right],

(17)

which backpropagates through the whole architecture in Fig.
1
. There are two changes that (
17
) makes to (
16
): (a) the use of
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
instead of
\nabla_{1}
means that (
17
) differentiates with respect to both variables in (
15
) (not just the first argument); (b) (
17
) has an extra term with
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
, which is absent from (
16
) but is necessary in (
17
) to ensure
\lim_{I\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(short proof in Appendix
C
; longer proof in Appendix
E.3
). In Section
5.2
, we will show that compared to the output gradient
\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}
, the network gradient
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}
exhibits a “super-efficiency” phenomenon
(
Ablin et al. 2020
;
Tolooshams & Ba 2022
)
, which means that it converges faster to
\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}
.

3.4
Full Algorithm

The probabilistic unrolling algorithm is given in Algorithm
1
. The
LinearSolver
step depends on the particular choice of solver; options include gradient descent, steepest descent, and conjugate gradient. In addition to circumventing matrix inversion, probabilistic unrolling provides several computational benefits over EM, which we explain below.

Covariance-Free Computation.
The iterative solvers eliminate the need to explicitly form the
D\times D

covariance matrix
\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(or even its inverse
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
). At each iteration
i
, a linear solver simply needs to compute matrix-vector products of the form
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{v}
, for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, efficiently. For an LGM, the matrix
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a highly-structured function of its canonical parameters and the data mask
\mathbb{\Omega}
, as shown in (
6
).

Exploiting LGM Structure.
In many cases, the canonical parameters of the LGM exhibit additional structure, such as diagonal, Toeplitz, low rank, and sparse structure, to name a few examples. This can significantly reduce the computational and storage costs of each iteration of the linear solver. For example, in applications of sparse Bayesian learning
(
Lin et al. 2022b
)
,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and its transpose often arise as Fourier-like operators. Efficient algorithms, in both computation and storage, exist for applying such operators to vectors.
For a single linear system, the time cost of the solver is
\mathcal{O}(I\tau_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, where
I
is the number of iterations and
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the time needed to compute the matrix-vector multiplication
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{v}
. The space cost is
\mathcal{O}(D+\omega_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, where
\omega_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the space needed to store the canonical parameters.

Amenability to Parallelization.
Iterative solvers are simple and straightforward to parallelize for solving multiple linear systems (e.g. in (
13
)) through
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=[\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{K,\boldsymbol{\theta}}],

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=[\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}|\cdots|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{K}].\vskip-5.69054pt

For example,
Gardner et al. 2018
and
Lin et al. 2022b
show how to parallelize the preconditioned conjugate gradient algorithm to solve for
\mathbb{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. They demonstrate that matrix-based parallelization is especially suitable for multi-core hardware, such as graphics processing units. In this work, we go a step further by parallelizing the solver across data points
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
to obtain solutions
\{\mathbb{X}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
for every
n
. By (
6
), the operators
\{\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
only differ in the masks
\{\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Thus, the total storage needed for performing
NK
matrix-vector multiplications with
\{\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is only

4
Related Work

Efficient Learning with Linear Solvers.
Using iterative solvers to circumvent matrix inversion is a widely-known technique within numerical linear algebra
(
Saad 2003
;
Halko et al. 2011
)
. Recently, solvers such as the Lanczos algorithm
(
Lanczos 1950
)
and conjugate gradient (CG)
(
Hestenes & Stiefel 1952
)
have become popular for accelerating gradient-based learning for Gaussian processes
(
Dong et al. 2017
;
Gardner et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Wenger et al. 2022
)
. In addition,
Lin et al. 2022b
and
Lin et al. 2022c
used CG to accelerate the classical EM algorithm for sparse Bayesian learning. Many of these works consider when the number of data vectors
N=1
, as opposed to the setting of the LGM where
N
can be large. They also do not consider the idea of backpropagation through the solver.

Backpropagating through Optimization Algorithms.
Automatic differentiation (or “backpropagation”)
(
Baydin et al. 2018
)
has been widely used and studied in machine learning
(
Domke 2012
;
Deledalle et al. 2014
;
Shaban et al. 2019
)
.
Domke 2012
studied truncated backpropagation as a replacement for implicit differentiation
(
Foo et al. 2007
;
Blondel et al. 2022
;
Bertrand et al. 2022
, e.g.)
when performing incomplete energy minimization.
Shaban et al. 2019
studied the use of truncated backpropagation for parameter estimation using unrolled networks. Backpropagating through an unrolled parameter estimation mapping has also been applied to hyperparameter optimization
(
Maclaurin et al. 2015
;
Franceschi et al. 2018
)
, and constructing generative adversarial networks
(
Metz et al. 2016
)
.

Ablin et al. 2020
theoretically studied how backpropagation can accelerate gradient estimation for bilevel (i.e. min-min) optimization problems, in the setting where the inner and outer objectives are the same, and when the inner optimization algorithm is gradient descent. Moreover,
Tolooshams & Ba 2022
;
Malézieux et al. 2021
studied the acceleration phenomenon for the sparse coding problem. This paper differs from the aforementioned prior work as follows: (a) probabilistic unrolling is designed for the specific setting of the LGM (as opposed to the general energy minimization problem of
Domke 2012
), and contains a novel Monte Carlo sampling step to avoid inversion of the covariance matrix, (b) the fact that our inner optimization originates from this sampling step
necessitates statistical considerations and analyses absent from previous work, (c) we extend the result of
Ablin et al. 2020
, showing that backpropagation can accelerate gradient estimation even in cases in which the inner and outer objectives are different, and (d) we provide gradient convergence analysis for steepest descent (an algorithm that is more sophisticated than gradient descent, requiring analysis of backpropagation through the step size).

Unrolled Networks.
Our interpretation of unrolled solvers as a deep neural network is known as unrolled/unfolded networks in the literature.
Gregor & LeCun 2010
introduced this approach for solving the sparse coding problem. Prior works designed and studied deep unrolled networks
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Ablin et al. 2019
)
. Moreover, unrolled networks have found advantages in various applications such as compressed sensing MRI
(
Sun et al. 2016
)
, Poisson image denoising
(
Tolooshams et al. 2020
)
, and pattern learning from physiological data
(
Malézieux et al. 2021
)
.

Variational EM and Variational Auto-Encoders.
Variational inference (VI) is a popular approach for approximating posterior distributions with simpler surrogates. Using VI for the E-Step of EM leads to the
variational EM (VEM)
algorithm
(
Murphy 2023
, Sec. 10.3.5)
, which is a potential alternative to probabilistic unrolling for accelerating EM inference. VEM is more flexible than probabilistic unrolling because it can perform inference for models outside the LGM family. However, the most common form of VEM learns a “mean-field” approximation to the posterior, which does not model covariance between latent variables and therefore biases the learning process away from the negative log-likelihood objective
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(
2
)
(
Lin et al. 2022b
)
; in contrast, probabilistic unrolling captures rich covariance structure using samples from the true posterior and like EM, still optimizes
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as its central objective. The variational auto-encoder (VAE)
(
Kingma & Welling 2013
)
is one of the most widely-used instances of VEM that trains a deep neural network to perform VI. Although VAEs are efficient tools for inference, they (a) require a separate inference network that is different from the generative model, increasing the number of parameters for training, and (b) require custom design of this network’s architecture (e.g. layers, activations, etc.). In contrast, the probabilistic unrolling architecture (Fig.
1
) is based on an interpretable linear solver that uses the same parameters as the generative model.

5
Theoretical Analysis

How well probabilistic unrolling approximates the exact EM gradient depends on the number of solver iterations, and the quality of the Monte Carlo approximation. We conduct a theoretical analysis of these two sources of error. We begin by defining population-level quantities for each gradient,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{g}^{\star,(n)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{g}^{\#,(n)}

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle,(n)}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle,(n)},

where
\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{1}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the exact gradient EM update of (
4
). We denote the approximate gradient after
I
iterations of probabilistic unrolling by
\boldsymbol{h}^{\langle I\rangle}
, with variants
\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
and
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
corresponding, respectively, to the output and network gradients defined previously. The quantity of interest is

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert\leq\underbrace{\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}\rVert}_{\text{statistical error}}+\underbrace{\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert}_{\text{optimization error}},\vskip-5.69054pt

(19)

which decomposes into two terms. The first term, which we name
statistical error
, comes from approximating
\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}
with Monte Carlo samples. We name the second term
optimization error
: this term captures the error due to performing a finite number
I
of iterations of the linear solver.

5.1
Statistical Error

Given
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, we first bound
\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rVert_{\infty}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
N
be the number of data points,
K
be the number of samples for each data point, and
L
be the dimesionality of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. For every
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{(n,\ell)}:=(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{1/2}\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{1/2},

(20)

where
\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is defined in (
6
),
\mathbb{\Sigma}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is defined in (
7
), and
\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta_{\ell}}
is the
D\times D
matrix of partial derivatives of the entries of
\mathbb{A}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with respect to
\theta_{\ell}
. Let
\xi:=\max_{\ell}\max_{n}\lVert\mathbb{M}^{(n,\ell)}\rVert_{F}
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
denotes the spectral norm and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
denotes Frobenius norm.

Then, there is an absolute constant
C
such that if the number of Monte Carlo samples
K
satisfies

\displaystyle K\geq\frac{\log(4NL)}{C}\max_{\ell}\left(\frac{\max_{n}\lVert\mathbb{M}^{(n,\ell)}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{n}\lVert\mathbb{M}^{(n,\ell)}\rVert_{F}^{2}}\right),

(21)

it follows that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}\rVert_{\infty}>\xi\sqrt{\frac{\log(4NL)}{CNK}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{N}.

(22)

We give the proof in Appendix
E.1
. The implication of Prop.
5.1
is that with high probability,
\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}
is close to
\boldsymbol{h}^{\star}
. The condition in (
21
) is a mild Monte Carlo sample size requirement and is satisfied for instance if
K\geq\frac{\log(4NL)}{C\max(1,\kappa N)}
, where
\kappa
is any number such that for all
\ell,n,n^{\prime}
,
\frac{\lVert\mathbb{M}^{(n,\ell)}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert\mathbb{M}^{(n^{\prime},\ell)}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\geq\kappa.

5.2
Optimization Error

Next, we bound optimization error
\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{h}^{\langle I\rangle}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rVert_{2}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
I
denote the number of linear solver iterations. Then, the output gradient
\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
and the network gradient
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
converge to
\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}
with the following rates:

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\rho^{I}),

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(I\rho^{2I}),

where
\rho<1
is the solver convergence rate. For gradient descent (GD) and steepest descent (SD), these rates are

\displaystyle\rho_{\textsc{GD}}:=\frac{\iota-1}{\iota},

\displaystyle\rho_{\textsc{SD}}:=\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1},

(23)

where
\iota
denotes the condition number (i.e. ratio between largest and smallest eigenvalues) of the matrix
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(
6
).

From Prop.
5.2
, we draw three conclusions: First, both the output gradient
\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
and the network gradient
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
converge to
\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}
as
I\to\infty
. Second,
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
achieves asymptotically better estimation of
\boldsymbol{h}^{\#}
(compared to
\boldsymbol{\widehat{h}}^{\langle I\rangle}
). Third, the results suggest that the error in both gradients can be decreased by the use of solvers that converge faster than gradient descent, e.g., using steepest descent (as shown in Prop.
5.2
), or conjugate gradient (CG), which has convergence rate
\rho_{\text{CG}}=\frac{\sqrt{\iota}-1}{\sqrt{\iota}+1}

(
Shewchuk et al. 1994
)
.

The proof of Prop.
5.2
is given in Appendix
E.2
. It relies on a connection we build between probabilistic unrolling and
bilevel optimization
, i.e. minimizing functions defined as a minimum
(
Ablin et al. 2020
)
. Probabilistic unrolling (
15
) is an instance of bilevel optimization in which the outer level optimizes the EM objective by estimating its gradient with respect to parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. This gradient is itself dependent on the solutions
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
of
K+1
linear systems, each equivalent to minimizing an inner quadratic function that depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. As part of our proof, we introduce the following two lemmas, which may be of broader interest beyond our particular setting of probabilistic unrolling for LGMs. The first result (Lemma
5.3
, proof in Appendix
E.3
) is a general statement on gradient convergence for bilevel optimization problems; it extends Prop. 2.2 of
Ablin et al. 2020
to settings in which the outer and inner objectives have different forms.
The second result (Lemma
5.4
, proof in Appendix
E.4
) analyzes Jacobian convergence for iterative solvers based on gradient descent and steepest descent.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider a bilevel optimization problem with outer objective
r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and inner objective
s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})
,

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}:=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(24)

in which the gradients
\{\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}),\nabla_{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})\}
and the second derivatives
\{\nabla_{22}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}),\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})\}
are Lipschitz continuous in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}\coloneqq\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})
be the desired gradient. Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}
denote an approximation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}
obtained from running an iterative (and differentiable) optimizer for
I
steps. We use
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}
to define two approximate gradients: (1) the analytic gradient (called “output gradient” in our work)
\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})
and (2) the automatic gradient (called “network gradient” in our work)
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})
. Additionally, define the Jacobians
\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\coloneqq\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}\coloneqq\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and let
\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}
be bounded (i.e.
\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}\leq J_{M}
). If the outer and inner objectives share second-order derivatives, i.e.
\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})=\nabla_{12}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})
, then the analytic and automatic gradients converge at the following rates:

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\widehat{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}),

(25)

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\rVert_{2}).

Lemma 5.4
.

Given the bilevel optimization problem from Prop.
5.3
, let the inner objective
s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
be a strongly convex quadratic function with positive definite
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, let
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\textsc{LinearSolver}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},I)
be the output of an
I
-step linear solver used to approximate
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}:=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Then, for gradient descent and steepest descent as the linear solver, the Jacobian error is the following function of solver error:
\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(I\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2})
.

Taken together, Prop.
5.1
and
5.2
offer insights in choosing the number of unrolling iterations
I
. Since the overall gradient error is the sum of the optimization and statistical errors
1
1

1

\ell_{2}
-norm is an upper-bound on the
\ell_{\infty}

) in
\ell_{\infty}

We perform experiments on several LGM applications, ranging from recovering unknown parameters to solving inverse problems to predicting movie ratings. We demonstrate that probabilistic unrolling provides significant scalability over EM, without loss in model performance. In all instances of EM, we use a single gradient step for the M-Step update (i.e. gradient EM). We implement all algorithms in PyTorch and on a single Nvidia T4 GPU with 16 GB RAM.

The main hyperparameters for probabilistic unrolling are the number of samples
K
and the number of solver iterations
I
. When solving a linear system
\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}
, we let
I
be just large enough so that the residual error
\lVert\boldsymbol{b}-\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is below some small threshold (i.e.
10^{-8}
). We set
K
based on our theoretical analysis (
21
). We keep
K
small if either the number of data points
N
is large or the number of parameters
L
is small; otherwise we increase
K
. In our experiments, we find that having
I
and
K
in the range [10, 30] is sufficient even when
D
increases to (tens of) thousands of dimensions.

6.1
Parameter Recovery for Noisy AR Models

The noisy auto-regressive (AR) model is a time series model with applications in radar
(
Çayır & Candan 2021
)
and biomedical imaging
(
Luo et al. 2020
)
. A noisy AR model of order
P
for a time series
\boldsymbol{y}:=\{y_{d}\}_{d=1}^{D}
is written as

\displaystyle\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{P}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{0},\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),\quad\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P},

(26)

\displaystyle z_{d}=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\phi_{p}\cdot z_{d-p}+w_{d},\quad w_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\kappa),\quad P<d\leq D,

\displaystyle y_{d}=z_{d}+v_{d},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad v_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda),\quad 1\leq d\leq D.

The initial covariance matrix
\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is some function of the AR coefficients
\boldsymbol{\phi}:=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{P}\}
that ensures stationarity for the latent process (see Appendix
F.1.1
for details).
The model’s free parameters are
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda,\kappa\}
. We can write this model as an LGM (
1
),
where
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{I},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\lambda^{-1}\mathbb{I}
, and
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a function of
\{\boldsymbol{\phi},\kappa\}
.

Complexity Comparison.
Using matrix inversion, exact-gradient EM will require
\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
-time and
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
-space. In comparison, probabilistic unrolling scales with the time
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and space
\omega_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
needed for matrix-vector multiplication with the posterior inverse-covariance matrix
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(
6
). For the noisy AR model of order
P
,
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a banded matrix with
2P+1
non-zero bands (derivation given in Appendix
F.1.1
). As a result,
\tau_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{O}(DP+P^{3})
and
\omega_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathcal{O}(DP+P^{2})
, which is much more efficient than EM.
2
2

2

We note that instead of using matrix inversion, we could cast (
26
) as a state-space model and use a Kalman smoother to run exact-gradient EM in
\mathcal{O}(DP^{3})
-time and
\mathcal{O}(DP^{2})
-space (see Appendix
F.1.2
). However, unlike probabilistic unrolling, the Kalman filter is a sequential algorithm and does not parallelize across
D
.

Setup and Results.
We compare the accuracy and speed of exact-gradient EM and probabilistic unrolling in parameter recovery for noisy AR models of order
P=5
. First, we randomly sample a set of true parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\star},\lambda^{\star},\kappa^{\star}\}
, generate
N=5
time series according to (
26
), and randomly mask out 10% of the observations from each time series to create
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(5)}
. Then, we perform maximum likelihood estimation using either gradient EM or probabilistic unrolling to produce parameter estimates
\{\boldsymbol{\hat{\phi}},\hat{\lambda},\hat{\kappa}\}
. We measure accuracy using the normalized root-mean-square error (NRMSE)
r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}):=\lVert\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\rVert_{2}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\rVert_{2}\times 100\%
. For probabilistic unrolling, we use
K=10
Monte Carlo samples, unroll
I=30
iterations of the conjugate gradient solver, and use the network gradient. Other details can be found in Appendix
F.1.3
. We report results for different values of
D
in Table
2
. Probabilistic unrolling consistently matches the performance of gradient EM, while being up to 47 times faster. For each
D
, we report the smaller of the times between EM with matrix inversion and EM with a Kalman smoother (see Appendix
F.1.2
). Typically, inversion is faster for smaller
D
while using the Kalman smoother is faster for larger
D
. Probabilistic unrolling is faster than both of these for all
D
. We additionally perform comparisons between probabilistic unrolling and variational EM (as implemented through the variational auto-encoder (VAE)
(
Kingma & Welling 2013
)
) in Appendix
F.1.4
.

With applications from radio astronomy
(
Wiaux et al. 2009
)
to MRI
(
Lustig et al. 2008
)
, compressed sensing (CS) is a technique for reconstructing sparse, high-dimensional signals
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{(n)}
from measurements
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}
.
Bayesian compressed sensing

(
Ji et al. 2008b
;
Bilgic et al. 2011
;
Lin et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2022a
)
is an approach to CS that employs the sparse Bayesian learning model
(
Wipf & Rao 2004
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{-1}),

\displaystyle n=1,\ldots,N

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)},\beta^{-1}\mathbb{I}),

\displaystyle n=1,\ldots,N,

where each
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is an unknown signal,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a measurement associated of the signal, and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
is a so-called measurement matrix. The free parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of the model are
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. When a common sparsity pattern underlies the observations
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, maximum likelihood estimation will push many of the entries
\alpha_{m}

to adopt large values, tending to
\infty
, and, thus, encouraging sparsity of samples from the posterior
p(\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta)

(
Yee & Atchadé 2017
)
. The mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}
of each posterior is then used as an estimate for
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{(n)}

(
Ji et al. 2008a
)
.

Complexity Comparison.
In several applications (e.g. MRI, astronomy), each
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
is the Fourier transform and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
is a random undersampling mask. Thus, (
27
) is an instance of the LGM, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\beta\}
. Using gradient EM to fit
\boldsymbol{\theta}
requires
\mathcal{O}(D^{3})
-time and
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
-space. On the other hand, probabilistic unrolling scales with the complexity needed to apply
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(
6
) to vectors; this is dominated by the Fourier transform
\mathbb{\Phi}
, which only requires
\mathcal{O}(D\log D)
-time and
\mathcal{O}(D)
-space.

Setup and Results.
We perform CS experiments on NIST
(
Grother 1995
)
, a dataset of handwritten digits. For each digit type (i.e. 0 through 9), we sample
N=10
images
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{(n)}
of size
128\times 128
, which are high-dimensional signals with
D=16{,}384
pixels. Each image is naturally sparse because most pixels are zero. For each
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{(n)}
, we randomly undersample its 2D Fourier transform by 15
\%
(i.e.
M=0.15D
) and add noise to construct the measurement
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}
. Then, we fit a Bayesian compressed sensing model (
27
) to
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
to obtain reconstructions
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. We measure success using the NRMSE between
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{ND}
are the concatenations of
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and the true signals
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, respectively. For probabilistic unrolling, we use
K=30
samples,
I=25
iterations of preconditioned conjugate gradient, and the network gradient. More details can be found in Appendix
F.2.2
. Results averaged over the 10 different digit types are given in Table
3
. We find that probabilistic unrolling and gradient EM have similar error. However, probabilistic unrolling is approximately 70 times faster than gradient EM, even after we accelerate EM using the Woodbury matrix identity (see Appendix
F.2.1
).

Collaborative Filtering through Factor Analysis

The goal of recommender systems is to predict user ratings for various items. One common approach is
collaborative filtering
, in which we pool together incomplete ratings data for
M
items across
N
users to infer how all users would rate all items. One of the central challenges of collaborative filtering is the inherent sparsity of the data – for every user, we typically only observe ratings for a small fraction of items, leading to large amounts of missing data
(
Rendle et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2021
)
. In this section, we use
factor analysis
models for collaborative filtering. Factor analysis is a Bayesian analog of matrix factorization, one of the state-of-the-art methods for recommender systems
(
Koren et al. 2009
;
Lawrence & Urtasun 2009
;
Rendle et al. 2019
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be the ratings for user
n
across
M
movies. Only part of this vector is known:
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{n}}
, where
M_{n}<M
. The factor analysis model is written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbb{I}),

(28)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}+\boldsymbol{\eta}),\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(n)})^{\top}),

where each
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
for
D<M
is a set of latent factors for user
n
. The free parameters of this model are
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{\mathbb{\Phi},\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\Psi}\}
, where
\mathbb{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
is a diagonal
M\times M
matrix. After estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, we can predict any unknown rating
{y}^{(n)}_{m}\not\in\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)}
using the mean of the distribution
p({y}^{(n)}_{m}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{\theta})
(i.e.
{\hat{y}}^{(n)}_{m}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\eta_{m}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is defined by (
7
) and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{m}
is the
m
-th row of
\mathbb{\Phi}
).

Setup and Results.
We perform collaborative filtering experiments on MovieLens
(

)
, a group of successively larger datasets with

. Further experimental details can be found in Appendix

7
Conclusion

We introduced
probabilistic unrolling
, a computational framework for accelerating gradient-based maximum likelihood estimation for a large class of latent variable models with Gaussian prior and Gaussian likelihood. Our method combines Monte Carlo sampling with iterative solvers and unrolled optimization, leading to a novel means of backpropagating through a sampling algorithm. Our theoretical analyses demonstrated that this can accelerate gradient estimation and, hence, maximum likelihood estimation. Our analyses provide insight into the relationship between the number of solver iterations, i.e. network depth, the number of Monte Carlo samples, and the gradient approximation error. In the future, we will consider extensions of probabilistic unrolling to other classes of probabilistic latent variable models.

Factor Analysis

This model sets
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\mathbb{I}

and

Probabilistic PCA

Probabilistic principal components analysis is a slight variation of factor analysis in which

has a single free parameter

that determines its constant diagonal values

tipping1999probabilistic
)
.

Sparse Bayesian Learning

This model assumes that
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0}

, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
determines the prior precision
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
determines the likelihood precision
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\beta\mathbb{I}
.

)

State-Space Model

This is a popular time series model that generalizes other popular variants (e.g. auto-regressive processes, moving average processes)

Durbin & Koopman 2012
)

n

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{A}\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}_{m-1}\boldsymbol{+}\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}_{m},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbb{Q}),

(29)

\displaystyle{y}^{(n)}_{m}

29
) in the form of (
1
) by defining
\boldsymbol{z}^{(n)}
(with length

Given a dataset of covariates and response variables
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{M},y_{M})

\boldsymbol{x}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

\displaystyle y_{m}=\boldsymbol{x}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}+\varepsilon_{m},

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\alpha\mathbb{I}

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\beta\mathbb{I}

where

\boldsymbol{\phi}

.

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}},\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log p(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\log p(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(34)

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log p(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\log p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

(35)

\displaystyle\cong-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top})^{-1}

\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\cong-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top})

(36)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

We can then combine terms based on whether they are quadratic, linear, or constant functions of
\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta})\cong-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}-c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(38)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}},\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})}[-\log p(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})]\cong\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}})}\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}+c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}\right]

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+c_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})+\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}\cdot\nabla_{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}),

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\nabla_{1}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})-\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}))

\nabla_{22}s
, we have another quadratic bound

\displaystyle\lVert\text{(Term B)}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}\left(\nabla_{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\nabla_{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})-\nabla_{22}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})\right)\rVert_{2}\leq J_{M}\cdot\frac{L^{s}_{22}}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(82)

Finally,

\displaystyle\lVert\text{(Term C)}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}\nabla_{22}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})+\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\nabla_{22}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\nabla_{12}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})\right)(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})\rVert_{2}

(83)

\displaystyle=\lVert((\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#})\nabla_{22}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})+(\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\nabla_{12}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})))(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq L^{s}_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})-\nabla_{12}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

Hence, under the sufficient condition of
s
and
r
sharing the same second order derivatives, i.e.
\nabla_{12}^{2}r(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})=\nabla_{12}^{2}s(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})
, the automatic gradient converges as

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{g}^{\#}-\boldsymbol{\widetilde{g}}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\lVert\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}).

(84)

∎

E.4
Lemma
5.4

This section proves the convergence rate of the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}
to
\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}
for gradient descent and steepest descent. See Appendix
D
for a summary of these algorithms.

E.4.1
Solver Convergence

For completeness, we begin by proving the convergence rate of the linear solver (i.e. how fast
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}
converges to
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle\#\rangle}
).

Gradient Descent

For gradient descent, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\alpha(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

(85)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\alpha(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\text{GD}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2},

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the constant step size of gradient descent. To ensure convergence, we require
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}
, where
\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is the largest eigenvalue of matrix
\mathbb{A}_{\theta}
. Assuming
\alpha=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}
, the spectral norm
\rho_{\text{GD}}
(or largest eigenvalue) of the symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{GD}}=1-\frac{\lambda_{\text{min}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}=\frac{\iota-1}{\iota},

(86)

where
\iota:=\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\lambda_{\text{min}}(\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}
is defined as the condition number of
\mathbb{A}_{\theta}
.
This leads to the following rate of convergence:

\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\rho_{\text{GD}}^{I}).

(87)

Steepest Descent

For steepest descent, let
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\}_{j}
be the set of eigenvectors of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, with
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{2}=1
and corresponding eigenvalues of
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{p}
. We define the error at iteration
I
by
\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}
. We express this error as a linear combination of the eigenvectors,

\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}=\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

(88)

for some coefficients
\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\langle I\rangle}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{D}
.
Now, we define the following residual

\boldsymbol{r}^{\langle I\rangle}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}=-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}=-\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{j}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}.

(89)

This gives us
\lVert\boldsymbol{r}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\langle I\rangle\top}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{r}^{\langle I\rangle}=\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}
. Hence, we can express the optimal step-size
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}
as

\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}=\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}.

(90)

Now, we show that the updates of steepest descent are contractive,

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#})\rVert_{2}

(91)

\displaystyle=\lVert(\mathbb{I}-\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\langle I\rangle}\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2},

where

\omega^{\langle I\rangle}=\frac{\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\sum_{a}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{a}\rVert_{2}}.

(92)

We are now left to find an upper bound on
\omega^{\langle I\rangle}
that goes to zero as
I
increases. We derive results for
D=2
. The condition number of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is
\iota\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}>1
. We denote
\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\coloneq=\frac{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}}{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}}
. We have

\displaystyle\omega^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{j}\lambda^{3}_{j}}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\sum_{a}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{a}\boldsymbol{v}_{a}\rVert_{2}}

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{2}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{2}_{2}}{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{3}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{3}_{2}}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\sum_{b}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\lambda_{2}(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}\lambda_{b})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\lambda_{2}(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}\lambda_{1})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\lambda_{2}(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}\lambda_{2})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-(\iota\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-(\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})})\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta_{i2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}(\iota\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}(\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}.

The worst convergence (i.e. an upper bound) is achieved when
\iota=\tau^{\langle I\rangle}
. Hence, we write the upper bound on
\omega^{\langle I\rangle}
as follows:

\displaystyle\omega^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}(\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{\iota^{2}+\iota^{2}}{(\iota^{3}+\iota^{2})}(\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{2\iota^{2}}{\iota^{2}(1+\iota)}(\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\frac{2\iota}{1+\iota}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert\frac{1-\iota}{\iota+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\frac{\iota(\iota-1)}{\iota+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\iota\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}.

Hence,

\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}\right)\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}.

(95)

We denote
\rho_{\text{SD}}\coloneqq\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}
(which is a faster rate than
\rho_{\text{GD}}
), and write

\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\rho_{\text{SD}}^{I}).

(96)

This bound also holds for
D>2
; the proof steps are similar (e.g. see Section 9.2 of
Shewchuk et al. 1994
).

E.4.2
Jacobian Convergence

We now analyze the convergence rate of the Jacobian.
Convergence studies in this context date back to the seminal work of
gilbert1992automatic
. We drop the subscript
\boldsymbol{\theta}
when referring to
(j,k)
-entry of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for ease of notation. Given matrix
\mathbb{A}
, we have

\partial\lambda_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{\top}\partial\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

(97)

\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=(\lambda_{j}\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{{\dagger}}\partial\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

(98)

\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}=\mathbb{H}^{jk},

(99)

where
{\dagger}
denotes pseudo inverse and
\mathbb{H}
is a zero matrix except at the
(j,k)
-entry, which is
1
. The above holds when the eigenvalues and vectors are distinct. We denote the Jacobian error by
\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}:=\boldsymbol{J}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{J}^{\#}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Given the expression
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}=\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}
, we first focus on writing the Jacobian error with respect to
\mathbb{A}_{jk}
, denoted by
\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}^{\langle I\rangle}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}
. Then, we use this Jacobian error to find the bound on the Jacobian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as
\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}=\sum_{jk}\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}
.

Gradient Descent

For gradient descent, we start with the recursion below,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I+1\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\langle I\rangle}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\#}).

(100)

Then, we take the derivative with respect to
\mathbb{A}_{jk}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}+\frac{\partial(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

(101)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}-\alpha\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}.

We use the eigendecomposition of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{V}\mathbb{\Sigma}\mathbb{V}^{\top}
(i.e.
\mathbb{V}
is the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and
\mathbb{\Sigma}
is a diagonal matrix of
\mathbb{A}
eigenvalues.This leads to the bound

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbb{I}-\alpha\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

(102)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\text{GD}}\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha\mathbb{H}^{jk}\rVert_{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}.

Unrolling the recursion gives us

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(I\rho_{\text{GD}}^{I}).

(103)

Given
\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}=\sum_{jk}\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}
, for gradient descent, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(I\rho_{\text{GD}}^{I}).

(104)

Steepest Descent

For steepest descent, we analyze Jacobian convergence under two different scenarios: (a) the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is
not
propagated through the adaptive step size
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}
, and (b) the gradient is propagated through
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}
(i.e. a more sophisticated scenario). In both of these scenarios, we show that the Jacobian converges at the same asymptotic rate. We express the Jacobian error as

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}_{b}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

(105)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(\lambda_{b}\boldsymbol{I}-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{{\dagger}}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(\lambda_{b}\boldsymbol{I}-\mathbb{V}\mathbb{\Sigma}\mathbb{V}^{\top})^{{\dagger}}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(\mathbb{V}\mathbb{\Lambda}_{b}\mathbb{V}^{\top})\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{d},

where
\mathbb{\Lambda}_{b}
is a diagonal matrix with
q_{bd}:=\frac{1}{\lambda_{b}-\lambda_{d}}
is its
d
-th diagonal entry, and
0
on
b
-th diagonal entry. We substitute this expression into the Jacobian recursion,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}+\frac{\partial(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}.

(106)

Scenario (a).

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

(107)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})(1-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\lambda_{d})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})(1-\frac{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{2}_{o}}{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{3}_{o}}\lambda_{d})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}.

Similar to the analysis for the solver error, we consider the case when
D=2
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}(1-\frac{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{2}_{o}}{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{3}_{o}}\lambda_{d})-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

(108)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{d}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{bd}\boldsymbol{v}_{jd}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{d}(1-\frac{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{2}_{o}}{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{3}_{o}}\lambda_{d})-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{2}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{2}_{2}}{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{3}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{3}_{2}}\lambda_{1})+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{2}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{2}_{2}}{\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{1}\lambda^{3}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{2}\lambda^{3}_{2}}\lambda_{2})\right)-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\lambda_{2}(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}\lambda_{1})+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\lambda_{2}(\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2})}\lambda_{2})\right)-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}\iota)+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}})\right)-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}.

Now, for the upper bound, we set
\iota=\tau^{\langle I\rangle}

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}\iota)+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}})\right)-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

(109)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}\iota)+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{\iota^{2}+\tau^{\langle I\rangle 2}}{\iota^{3}+\tau_{i}^{2}})\right)\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\frac{2}{1+\iota}\iota)+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(1-\frac{2}{1+\iota})\right)\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(\frac{1-\iota}{\iota+1})+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1})\right)\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha_{i}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}\sum_{b}\left(\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j1}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\zeta^{\langle I\rangle}_{b}(q_{b2}\boldsymbol{v}_{j2}\boldsymbol{v}_{bk})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\iota-1}{\iota+1}\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}.

Unrolling the recursion, we have

\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(I\rho_{\text{SD}}^{I}).

(110)

Hence,

\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}(I\rho_{\text{SD}}^{I}).

(111)

Scenario (b).
In scenario (a), we have
\frac{(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}=-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}
with bounded norm of
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}
. In scenario (b), we have

\frac{\partial(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}=\frac{\partial\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\frac{\partial(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\frac{\partial(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}=-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}-\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}.

(112)

This leads to the following,

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\langle I+1\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbb{B}^{\langle I\rangle}_{\mathbb{A}_{jk}}-(\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}\mathbb{H}^{jk}+\mathbb{A}\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}})\boldsymbol{e}^{\langle I\rangle}.

(113)

It remains to be shown that the norm of
\mathbb{A}\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}
can be upper-bounded. Then, the Jacobian error is upper-bonded by the same order of convergence as in scenario (a),

\lVert\mathbb{A}\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rVert_{2}\lVert\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}=\lambda_{1}\lVert\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}.

(114)

We write
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}
in terms of eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(i.e.
\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}=\frac{\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{2}_{o}}{\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{b}\lambda^{3}_{b}}
) and take the derivative, i.e.

\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}=\frac{\left(\sum_{o}2\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda_{o}\boldsymbol{v}_{o}^{\top}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_{o}\right)\left(\sum_{b}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{b}\lambda^{3}_{b}\right)-\left(\sum_{o}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{o}\lambda^{2}_{o}\right)\left(\sum_{b}3\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{b}\lambda^{2}_{b}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{\top}\mathbb{H}^{jk}\boldsymbol{v}_{b}\right)}{(\sum_{m}\zeta^{\langle I\rangle 2}_{m}\lambda^{3}_{m})^{2}}.

(115)

Given the above, there exist a constant that bounds the norm of this derivative. We denote this constant by
\lVert\frac{\partial\alpha^{\langle I\rangle}}{\partial\mathbb{A}_{jk}}\rVert_{2}\leq M_{\alpha_{\text{div}}}
.

F.1.1
Form of Prior Precision
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

From (
26
), let us define
\boldsymbol{z}_{\leq P}:=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{P}\}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{>P}:=\{z_{P+1},\ldots,z_{D}\}
and
\boldsymbol{w}:=\{w_{P+1},\ldots,w_{D}\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{\leq P}\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{>P}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{\leq P}\\
\boldsymbol{w}\end{bmatrix},

(116)

where
\mathbb{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{(D-P)\times P}
and
\mathbb{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{(D-P)\times(D-P)}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}:=\begin{bmatrix}-\phi_{P}&-\phi_{P-1}&\ldots&-\phi_{1}\\
0&-\phi_{P}&\ldots&-\phi_{2}\\
0&0&\ldots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&-\phi_{P}\\
0&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\ldots&0\\
-\phi_{1}&1&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
-\phi_{P-1}&-\phi_{P-2}&-\phi_{P-3}&\ldots&0\\
-\phi_{P}&-\phi_{P-1}&-\phi_{P-2}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix}.

(117)

Observe that
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{\leq P}\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{>P}\end{bmatrix}
is a multivariate Gaussian with mean
\boldsymbol{0}
and inverse-covariance
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(i.e. our object of interest). Similarly,
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{\leq P}\\
\boldsymbol{w}\end{bmatrix}
is also a multivariate Gaussian with mean
\boldsymbol{0}
and inverse-covariance
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\sigma^{-2}\mathbb{I}\end{bmatrix}
. Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\mathbb{D}
for some matrix
\mathbb{D}
. The change-of-variables formula for probability distributions then tells us that

\displaystyle\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\kappa^{-1}\mathbb{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\kappa}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{D}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\mathbb{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}.

(118)

(Note that change-of-variables also tells us that
\log\det\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\log\det\mathbb{D}-T\log\kappa
, which we need to compute the term
\boldsymbol{c}_{\theta}
.)

galbraith1974inverses
show that for a
stationary
AR process, we have
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{L}_{P}^{\top}\mathbb{L}_{P}-\mathbb{H}_{P}^{\top}\mathbb{H}_{P}
, where
\mathbb{H}_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
is the first
P
rows of
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{L}_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
is the top-left
P\times P
block of
\mathbb{L}
.

Next, let
\mathbb{D}
be factorized as
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{R}^{\top}=\mathbb{D}
, which we can obtain through Cholesky decomposition. This implies that
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{X}^{\top}\mathbb{X}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{R}&\mathbb{0}\\
\mathbb{H}&\mathbb{L}\end{bmatrix}.

(119)

We can verify that
\mathbb{X}
is a lower triangular and banded matrix with
P+1
non-zero bands below the diagonal. Similarly
\mathbb{X}^{\top}
is an upper triangular and banded matrix with
P+1
non-zero bands above the diagonal. It follows that
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
has
2P+1
non-zero bands. In turn, this implies that
\mathbb{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in (
6
) also has
2P+1
non-zero bands (since the other part of the sum is a diagonal matrix).

To provide another baseline of comparison for probabilistic unrolling, we fit the model in (

2

F.2.1
Woodbury Matrix Identity for Exact-Gradient EM

The Woodbury matrix identity is a property from linear algebra that allows us to compute
\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for Bayesian compressed sensing by inverting a
M\times M
matrix instead of a
D\times D
one. Since typically
M<D
in compressed sensing, this can lead to computational savings in time from a practical standpoint (but perhaps not an asymptotic one, because
M
often needs to grow linearly with
D
in compressed sensing applications). Note that it does not lead to computational savings in space, because the full matrix
\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is still computed in the end.

Instead of computing
\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
through (
7
), we equivalently have

\displaystyle\mathbb{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}-\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\mathbb{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top}(\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top}+\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}\mathbb{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Omega}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbb{\Omega}\mathbb{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}.

(125)

Note that for Bayesian compressed sensing, both
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}
and
\mathbb{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}
can be cheaply computed, because these are both diagonal matrices.

F.3
Collaborative Filtering through Factor Analysis

F.3.1
Experimental Settings

The MovieLens datasets are accessed at
https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/
.

We follow the general experimental setup of
Sedhain et al. 2015
. For each dataset, we perform a 90%-10% train-test split of the ratings data. We then set aside 10% of the training set as a validation set. Both models (gradient EM and probabilistic unrolling) are trained with the Adam optimizer, learning rate 0.001, and a user mini-batch size of 25 (with gradient accumulation over four mini-batches for an overall batch size of 100). For the ML-1M dataset, we train the model for 20 epochs and evaluate the model on the validation set every 500 gradient steps. For the ML-10 M and ML-25 M datasets, we train the model for 5 epochs and evaluate the model on the validation set every 2,000 gradient steps. The checkpoint with the best validation set RMSE is used for final evaluation on the test set.

During training, we only train on users with at least one rating in the training set. Thus, there may be users in the validation/test sets that do not appear during training. Following
Sedhain et al. 2015
, we always predict a rating of 3 for these users. All other model rating predictions are clipped to be in the range [1.0, 5.0] before evaluation of RMSE.

F.3.2
Comparison with VAEs

Similar to Appendix
F.1.4
, we report results for a VAE baseline on the collaborative filtering task. Searching over architectural options, we find that the best-performing architecture had a two-layer encoder with
3{,}000
units in the hidden layer and ReLU activations. However, even with our extensive tuning, we observe that VAEs generally perform worse and have higher computational costs in comparison to probabilistic unrolling. The VAE obtains 0.8849 RMSE (compared to 0.8436 for PU) on MovieLens 1-m and 0.8366 RMSE (compared to 0.7796 for PU) on MovieLens 10-m. The VAE also has approximately 1.5x the time cost and 2x the memory cost of PU (due to the separate inference encoder network).
