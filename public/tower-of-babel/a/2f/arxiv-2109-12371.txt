Characterising rectifiable metric spaces using tangent spaces
David Bate

Abstract
We characterise rectifiable subsets of a complete metric space X X in terms of local approximation, with respect to the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, by an n n -dimensional Banach space. In fact, if E ⊂ X E\subset X with ℋ n ​ ( E ) < ∞ \mathcal{H}^{n}(E)<\infty and has positive lower density almost everywhere, we prove that it is sufficient that, at almost every point and each sufficiently small scale, E E is approximated by a bi-Lipschitz image of Euclidean space. We also introduce a generalisation of Preiss’s tangent measures that is suitable for the setting of arbitrary metric spaces and formulate our characterisation in terms of tangent measures. This definition is equivalent to that of Preiss when the ambient space is Euclidean, and equivalent to the measured Gromov–Hausdorff tangent space when the measure is doubling.

Recently there has been significant interest in studying analysis on metric spaces.
Inevitably, such study leads to questions regarding the geometric nature of rectifiable subsets of a metric space.
For a metric space

measure zero, by countably many Lipschitz images of

Kirchheim

From this description it is possible to deduce the existence of a tangent structure of a rectifiable metric space akin to an approximate tangent plane after two modifications.

Firstly, one must replace the Hausdorff distance

The question of whether an

More recently, this question appeared in the work of Ambrosio, Brué and Semola

To date the question is unknown for any ambient metric space that is not a Euclidean space (in this case

In this paper we answer this question affirmatively.

Similarly, we define

We will often omit notation for the metric

By a

.
A set

The set of all

For

2.2
Doubling measures

Much of the theory of tangent measures requires the original measure to be asymptotically doubling.

Definition 2.7
.

For
M\geq 1
,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is
M
-doubling
if

0<\mu(B(x,2r))\leq M\mu(B(x,r))<\infty

for each
x\in X
and each
r>0
.
A
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is
asymptotically doubling
if

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}<\infty\quad\text{for }\mu\text{-a.e. }x\in X.

To proceed we need the following standard facts.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
.
For any
r>0
,

x\mapsto\mu(U(x,r))\text{ is lower semicontinuous}

(10)

and

x\mapsto\mu(B(x,r))\text{ is upper semicontinuous}.

(11)

Consequently, for any
R,M,s>0
the set

A:=\{x\in X:\mu(B(x,2r))\leq M\mu(B(x,r))\ \forall 0<r<R\}

is a Borel set, as are

B:=\{x\in X:\mu(B(x,r))<Mr^{s}\ \forall 0<r<R\}

and

C:=\{x\in X:\mu(B(x,r))>Mr^{s}\ \forall 0<r<R\}.

Proof.

If
x_{i}\to x
with
d(x_{i},x)
monotonically decreasing then

U(x,r-d(x,x_{i}))

monotonically increases to
U(x,r)
and is contained in
U(x_{i},r)
, proving (
10
).
Similarly,

B(x,r+d(x,x_{i}))

monotonically decreases to
B(x,r)
and contains in
B(x_{i},r)
, proving (
11
).
Consequently, for any
R,M>0
and
\delta\geq 0
, the set

A_{R,M,\delta}:=\{x\in X:\mu(U(x,(2+\delta)r))\leq M\mu(B(x,r))\ \forall 0<r<R\}

is closed, as is

A=\bigcap_{\delta\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}}A_{R,M,\delta}.

The proof that
B
and
C
are Borel is similar.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
.
Suppose that for some
M,R>0
a set
Y\subset X
satisfies

0<\mu(B(x,2r))\leq M\mu(B(x,r))<\infty\quad\forall 0<r<R,\ \forall x\in Y.

(12)

Then for any
x\in Y
and
r<R/2
,
B(x,r)\cap Y
is contained in
M^{4}
balls of radius
r/2
centred at points of
B(x,r)\cap Y
.
In particular, for any
\epsilon>0
,
B(x,r)\cap Y
is contained in
M^{-4\log_{2}\epsilon}
balls of radius
\epsilon r
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a maximal disjoint
r/4
-net of
B(x,r)\cap Y
.
Then for any
y\in\mathcal{N}
, (
12
) gives

\mu(B(y,r/4))\geq\frac{\mu(B(y,r/2))}{M}\geq\frac{\mu(B(y,r))}{M^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu(B(y,2r))}{M^{3}}\geq\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{M^{3}}.

(13)

For
y\in\mathcal{N}
the
B(y,r/4)
are disjoint subsets of
B(x,2r)
.
Therefore, if
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\in\mathcal{N}
,

\mu(B(x,2r))\geq\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(y_{i},r/4)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu(B(y_{i},r/4))\\
\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mu(B(x,r))}{M^{3}}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mu(B(x,2r))}{M^{4}}

using (
13
) for the penultimate inequality and (
12
) for the final inequality.
Thus
N\leq M^{4}
.

The statement about covering by balls of radius
\epsilon r
follows by induction. ∎

Theorem 2.10
.

Any asymptotically doubling
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)
satisfies the Lebesgue density theorem.
That is, for any
S\subset X
and for
\mu
-a.e.

,

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu(S\cap B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=1.

Such an

is a
Lebesgue density point
of
S
.

Proof.

This is a standard result for doubling measures.
First one proves the Vitali covering theorem
(
MR1800917
, Theorem 1.6)
and uses it to deduce the Lebesgue differentiation theorem
(
MR1800917
, Theorem 1.8)
and hence the Lebesgue density theorem for Borel sets

.

The proof for asymptotically doubling measures is the same since the proof of the Vitali covering theorem works, with minor modifications, only assuming an almost everywhere countable decomposition into Borel sets, each satisfying (

.
Assuming

is asymptotically doubling,

gives such a decomposition of

.

The statement for arbitrary

with

.
∎

2.3
Prokhorov’s theorem

A sequence
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)

weak*
converges to
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
if, for every bounded and continuous

with bounded support,

\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}\to\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

We write
\mu_{i}\to\mu
to denote this convergence.

Recall that, if
\mu_{i}\to\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
and

, then for every

\mu(U(x,r))\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(U(x_{i},r))

(14)

and

\mu(B(x,r))\geq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(B(x_{i},r)).

(15)

Indeed, these follow by combining the standard proof of lower/upper semicontinuity for a fixed open/closed set and the proof of
eqs.

10
and
11
.

Recall Prokhorov’s theorem, reformulated to describe convergence in

Theorem 2.11

(Theorem 2.3.4

Let

A set

•

\{\mu(B(x,r)):\mu\in\mathcal{S}\}

•

There exists a compact

(16)

Definition 2.12
.

Let

be a metric space and

.
For

, and

.

Note that

Note that, for any Borel

(18)

The reason for allowing the Lipschitz constants of the functions to increase when defining

.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
x\in X
and
L>0
.
Convergence in
F_{x}
, equivalently
F_{x}^{L}
, implies weak* convergence in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
and, if

is complete and separable, the converse is true.

Proof.

Equivalence of convergence in
F_{x}
and
F_{x}^{L}
follows from (
17
).

Suppose
F_{x}(\mu_{i},\mu)\to 0
and that
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded and continuous with
\operatorname{spt}g\subset B(x,r)
.
The fact that

\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}\to\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu

follows from a standard approximation argument
(
MR2401600
, Section 5.1)
.
Indeed, observe that the sequence

g_{k}(y):=\inf\{g(w)+kd(w,y):w\in X\}

monotonically increases to

and that each

is

\operatorname{spt}g_{k}\subset B(x,r+\|g\|_{\infty}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\liminf_{i\to\infty}\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\liminf_{i\to\infty}\int g_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int g_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int g\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

using monotonicity of the integral for the inequality, the fact that
F_{x}(\mu_{i},\mu)\to 0
for the first equality and the monotone convergence theorem for the final equality.
The reverse
“limsup”
equality follows from a similar argument that involves approximating

from above.

Now suppose that
X
is complete and separable, that
\mu_{i}\to\mu
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
but

.
By taking a (non-relabelled) subsequence, there exist an

such that

for all

.
That is, for each

there exists an
r

with

such that

\left|\int g_{i}\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{i}-\mu)\right|\geq 1/r.

Set
r^{\prime}=r+1/r
. By
Theorem

, there exist an

and a compact

such that, for all

,
\mu_{i}(B(x,r^{\prime}))\leq M
and

\mu_{i}(B(x,r^{\prime})\setminus K)<1/8r.

(19)

By increasing

if necessary, we may also suppose

The Arzelà–Ascoli theorem implies that, after taking a further subsequence, the

converge uniformly on

with
\operatorname{spt}g\subset B(x,r)

By

Then, for any sufficiently large

\displaystyle\left|\int g\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{i}-\mu)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\int_{K}g\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{i}-\mu)\right|-1/4r

Lemma 2.14
.

For any metric space
X
,
x\in X
and
L>0
,

F_{x}
and
F_{x}^{L}
are metrics on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
.

is complete and separable then so are
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X),F_{x})
and
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X),F_{x}^{L})
.

Proof.

Certainly

are positive, finite on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(X)
and symmetric.

, then the monotonicity of

.
Consequently, the monotone convergence theorem implies

for all closed and bounded

and hence
\mu(B)=\nu(B)
for all Borel

.
Since
\mu
and

are both Borel regular, this implies

.

To see that

satisfies the triangle inequality, let

.
Otherwise, for

\displaystyle\leq F_{x}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}},\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}}(\mu,\lambda)+F_{x}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}},\frac{1}{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}}(\lambda,\nu)

2.4
Gromov distances, embeddings and convergence

Pointed Gromov–Hausdorff
convergence
of metric spaces is prevalent in the literature.
However, the metric defining this convergence is less commonly used.
In this subsection we recall Gromov’s metric for pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and prove some basic facts about it that are missing from the literature (namely that it is a complete metric on the isometry classes of pointed metric spaces).
Our presentation of this is sufficiently general so that the corresponding statements for the pointed measured Gromov–Hausdorff distance, and our metric
d_{*}
, easily follow (see
Section

4
).

We use the following modification of the Hausdorff distance.

Definition 2.15

(Section 6

)
.

Let
Z
be a metric space,
z\in Z
and
X,Y\subset Z
.
Define
H_{z}(X,Y)
to be the infimum over all
0<\epsilon<1/2
for which

X\cap B(z,1/\epsilon)\subset B(Y,\epsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad Y\cap B(z,1/\epsilon)\subset B(X,\epsilon).

(20)

If no such
0<\epsilon<1/2
exists, set
H_{z}(X,Y)=1/2
.
Note that, if (
20
) is satisfied for some
\epsilon>0
then it is satisfied for all
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon
.

Lemma 2.16
.

For any metric space
Z
and
z\in Z
,
H_{z}
is a pseudometric on the power set of
Z
and is a metric on the closed subsets of
Z
.
If
Z
is complete then so is the set of closed subsets of

equipped with

.

Proof.

Let
\zeta
be the metric on
Z
, let
X,Y,W\subset Z
and let

d_{XW}=H_{z}(X,W)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{WY}=H_{z}(W,Y).

If one of
d_{XW}
or
d_{WY}
equals
1/2
there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, let
0<\delta<1/2-\min\{d_{XZ},d_{ZY}\}
.
If

x\in X\cap B(z,1/(d_{XZ}+d_{ZY}+2\delta))

(21)

then (
20
) for
X
and
W
gives a

with

\zeta(w,x)\leq d_{XW}+\delta.

(22)

In particular, since
d_{XW},d_{WZ}<1/2-\delta
,

\zeta(w,z)\leq\zeta(w,x)+\zeta(x,z)\leq d_{XW}+\delta+1/(d_{XW}+d_{WY}+2\delta)\leq 1/(d_{WY}+\delta).

Therefore (
20
) for
W
and
Y
gives a

with

\zeta(w,y)\leq d_{WY}+\delta.

(23)

In particular,

\zeta(x,w)\leq d_{XW}+d_{WY}+2\delta.

(24)

Thus

and

show that (
20
) holds between

and

for
\epsilon=d_{XW}+d_{WY}+2\delta
.
Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, this completes the proof.

If
C\subset Z
is closed and

, then for any

there exist

with

.
Since

is closed,

and so

.
Consequently,

is a metric on the closed subsets of

.

Completeness follows from the same argument as the regular Hausdorff metric
(

, Proposition 7.3.7)
.
∎

We require the following standard construction.

.

Let

4.2
Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of large subsets

Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of pointed metric measure spaces is used throughout analysis.
However, the requirement that the underlying metric spaces must Gromov–Hausdorff converge is too rigid for some uses in geometric measure theory.
For example, it requires that every sequence of null sets must Gromov–Hausdorff converge to some limit.

For a concrete example, consider the following.

Example 4.8
.

Let
X=\{0,1\}
with
d(0,1)=1
and for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mu_{i}(\{0\})=1
and
\mu(\{1\})=1/i
.
Then the sequence
(X,d,\mu_{i},0)
Gromov–Hausdorff converges to
(X,d,\delta_{0},0)
but
not
to
(\{0\},\delta_{0},0)
, which is the more natural choice.
That is, Gromov–Hausdorff convergence may require one to consider a larger metric space than simply the support of the limit measure.

For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
X_{i}
be an
i
-point metric space including a point
0
, equipped with the discrete metric
d_{i}
.
Then the sequence
(X_{i},d_{i},\delta_{0},0)
has no Gromov–Hausdorff limit, even though a one point metric space with a Dirac measure is the natural candidate from a measure theoretic point of view.

To construct a 1-rectifiable metric space with no Gromov–Hausdorff tangents at any point, one takes an isometric copy of an interval and scatters
“dust”
around it at all scales as in the previous example.

Rather than Gromov–Hausdorff convergence we will use the following.

Definition 4.9
.

For pointed metric measure spaces
(X,\mu,x)
and
(Y,\nu,y)
define

d_{*}((X,\mu,x),(Y,\nu,y))=G_{0,1}((X,\mu,x),(Y,\nu,y)).

Note that
d_{*}\leq d_{\mathrm{pmGH}}
.
Also, for any Borel
x\in S\subset X
, (
18
) implies

d_{*}((X,\mu,x),(S,\mu|_{S},x))\leq\inf\{r>0:\mu(B(x,1/r)\setminus S)<r\}.

(84)

Although
d_{*}
does not require the underlying sets to be close in the sense of
d_{\mathrm{pGH}}
, we can recover large subsets which are.

) in
Theorem

1.2
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
E\subset X
an
n
-rectifiable set with
\mathcal{H}^{n}(E)<\infty
.
For any
x\in E
that satisfies the conclusion of
Theorem

2.4
and
Lemma

2.3

item

1
,

T_{r}(X,d,\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{E},x)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x},\mathcal{H}^{n}/2^{n},0)

(126)

as
r\to 0
.
In particular, for
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-a.e.
x\in E
,

\operatorname{Tan}(X,d,\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{E},x)=\{(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x},\mathcal{H}^{n}/2^{n},0)\}.

Proof.

Let
x\in E
be as in the hypotheses and
f_{x},\|\cdot\|_{x},C_{x}
as in the conclusion of
Theorem

2.4
.
For each
r>0
, let

\epsilon_{r}=\sup\left\{\left|1-\frac{\|f_{x}(y)-f_{x}(z)\|_{x}}{d(y,z)}\right|:y\neq z\in C_{x}\cap B(x,\sqrt{r})\right\},

so that
\epsilon_{r}\to 0
as
r\to 0
.
Also let
f_{r}=f_{x}/r
and

C_{r}=C_{x}\cap B(x,\sqrt{r}).

First note that, for any
y,z\in C_{r}
with
d(y,z)/r\leq\min\{r^{-1/2},\epsilon_{r}^{-1/2}\}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d(y,z)}{r}-\|f_{r}(y)-f_{r}(z)\|_{x}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{|d(y,z)-\|f_{x}(y)-f_{x}(z)\|_{x}|}{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{r}d(y,z)}{r}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{r}}.

(127)

Now observe that
f_{r}\colon(C_{r},d/r)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x})
is
L_{r}
-bi-Lipschitz, for

L_{r}=\frac{1+\epsilon_{r}}{1-\epsilon_{r}^{2}}\to 1.

Therefore

\frac{1}{L_{r}^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}|_{f_{r}(C_{r})}\leq(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{r}|_{C_{r}})\leq L_{r}^{n}\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}|_{f_{r}(C_{r})},

(128)

where
\mathcal{H}^{n}_{r}
denotes
\mathcal{H}^{n}
on the metric space
(C_{r},d/r)
and
\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}
denotes
\mathcal{H}^{n}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x})
.
However,

(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{r}|_{C_{r}})=(f_{r})_{\#}\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{C_{r}}}{r^{n}}\right)=\frac{(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}|_{C_{r}})}{r^{n}},

where
\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}
denotes
\mathcal{H}^{n}
on
(X,d)
.
Also observe, for any
R>0
, by
lemmas

2.5
and
9
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}(B(0,R)\setminus f_{r}(C_{r}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}(B(0,R)\setminus f_{r}(C_{r}\cap B(x,Rr/L_{r})))

(129)

\displaystyle\leq(2R)^{n}-\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}(f_{r}(C_{r}\cap B(x,Rr/L_{r})))

\displaystyle\leq(2R)^{n}-\frac{L_{r}^{n}}{r^{n}}\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}(C_{r}\cap B(x,Rr/L_{r}))

\displaystyle\to(2R)^{n}-(2R)^{n}=0

Let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}}
denote the total variation of elements of
\mathcal{M}((B(0,R),\|\cdot\|_{x}))
.
Then
eqs.

129
and
128
imply

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}|_{C_{r}})}{r^{n}}-\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}|_{C_{r}})}{r^{n}}-\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}|_{f_{r}(C_{r})}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}|_{f_{r}(C_{r})}-\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\leq(L_{r}^{n}-1)\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}(B(0,R))+\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}(B(0,R)\setminus f_{r}(C_{r}))\to 0.

That is,

\frac{(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}_{d}|_{C_{r}})}{(2r)^{n}}\to\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}}{2^{n}}\quad\text{strongly on }B(0,R).

(130)

Equation

130
has two consequences.
Firstly, it shows that, for any
R,\delta>0
,

B(f_{r}(C_{r}),\delta)\supset B(0,R)

for sufficiently small
r>0
.
Together with (
127
), this shows that there exist
\epsilon^{\prime}_{r}\to 0
such that

f_{r}\colon(C_{r},d/r)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x})\quad\text{is an }\epsilon^{\prime}_{r}\text{-isometry.}

(131)

Equation

130
also implies that

\frac{(f_{r})_{\#}(\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{C_{r}})}{r^{n}}\to\mathcal{H}^{n}_{x}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x}).

(132)

Corollaries

4.4
,
131
and
132
then imply

d_{\mathrm{pmGH}}(T_{r}(C_{r},d,\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{C_{r}},x),(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{x},\mathcal{H}^{n}/2^{n},0))\to 0

and so
eqs.

9
and
4.11
imply (
126
).
∎
