The Fine-Grained Hardness of Sparse Linear Regression
Aparna Gupte, Vinod Vaikuntanathan

Abstract
Sparse linear regression is the well-studied inference problem where one is given a design matrix 𝐀 ∈ ℝ M × N \mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N} and a response vector 𝐛 ∈ ℝ M \mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M} , and the goal is to find a solution 𝐱 ∈ ℝ N \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N} which is k k -sparse (that is, it has at most k k non-zero coordinates) and minimizes the prediction error ‖ 𝐀𝐱 − 𝐛 ‖ 2 \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2} . On the one hand, the problem is known to be 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{NP} -hard which tells us that no polynomial-time algorithm exists unless 𝒫 = 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{P}=\mathcal{NP} . On the other hand, the best known algorithms for the problem do a brute-force search among N k N^{k} possibilities. In this work, we show that there are no better-than-brute-force algorithms, assuming any one of a variety of popular conjectures including the weighted k k -clique conjecture from the area of fine-grained complexity, or the hardness of the closest vector problem from the geometry of numbers. We also show the impossibility of better-than-brute-force algorithms when the prediction error is measured in other ℓ p \ell_{p} norms, assuming the strong exponential-time hypothesis.



1
Introduction

High-dimensional inference problems come up naturally in many fields of science and engineering including computational biology and genomics, natural language processing, medical imaging and remote sensing. A well-known example is the standard linear regression problem where one is given data items
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and responses
b_{1},\ldots,b_{M}\in\mathbb{R}
where each
b_{i}\approx\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{*}\rangle
for a hidden parameter vector
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. When
N\gg M
, the model is unidentifiable; however, in various application domains (such as the ones above), it makes sense to require that
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is sparse, that is, it has only a few non-zero coordinates. The resulting problem is called
sparse linear regression
which can be formulated as finding

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\hskip 14.45377pt\mbox{ such that }\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0}\leq k

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is the design matrix with
\mathbf{a}_{i}
as its rows,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 1}
is the response vector consisting of the
b_{i}
’s as entries, and
k
is the sparsity parameter.

This problem is non-convex and finding an exact as well as approximate solution is known to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
Nat95
,
DMA97
,
FKT15
]
.
Despite this negative result, when the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
is particularly well-conditioned, polynomial-time algorithms based on
\ell_{1}
-relaxation, such as basis pursuit and Lasso estimators
[
Tib96
,
CDS98
]
as well as the Dantzig selector
[
CT07
]
have been shown to meet the minimax-optimal lower bound for sparse linear regression. This is also the setting of compressed sensing, which has seen much exciting work in the last two decades.

However, in several high-dimensional regression problems in data science, there is no such guarantee on the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
, as the features could be correlated. As a result, the problem formulation with general design matrices is also of great interest, and is the main focus of this paper.

On the one hand, the NP-hardness of this problem
[
Nat95
,
DMA97
,
FKT15
]
rules out algorithms that run in time polynomial in
M,N
and
k
. On the other hand, the best known algorithm for this problem is trivial brute-force search that runs in time
O(N^{k})
. Concretely, one can enumerate over all possible
\binom{N}{k}
supports, and run an ordinary least squares regression with an
M\times k
design matrix, which results in a
N^{k}(M+k)^{O(1)}
time algorithm
[
HPIM18
]
. Improving this to, say,
O(N^{0.999k})
or even a modest
O(N^{k-1})
seems out of reach. This state of affairs motivates our question:

Can
k
-sparse linear regression be solved in time
N^{(1-\epsilon)k}
for
some
constant
\epsilon>0
?

Our main result in this paper shows that the answer is “no” assuming popular conjectures from the field of fine-grained algorithms and complexity
[
Wil18
]
.

First, we show that it is hard to solve
k
-SLR in the
\ell_{2}
norm faster than
O(N^{k-\epsilon})
for any constant
\epsilon>0
, assuming either the min-weight
k
-clique hypothesis
[
Wil18
]
or the conjectured hardness of the lattice closest vector problem
[
BGS17
]
.
The min-weight
k
-clique problem asks to find a clique with
k
vertices with the smallest total edge weight, in a weighted undirected graph. The best known algorithm is exhaustive search which runs in time
O(N^{k})
on
N
-node graphs
[
Wil18
]
. The closest vector problem is a long-studied problem in the geometry of numbers
[
Min10
,
MG02
]
, which asks to find the closest lattice point to a given target vector, given an arbitrary
n
-dimensional integer lattice. The best known algorithm runs in time
O(2^{n})

[
ADS15
]
, and improving it is a long-standing challenge with considerable impact in the area of combinatorial optimization and lattice-based cryptography
[
BGS17
]
.
(For a more formal description of these problems, we refer the reader to Section
2
).

Secondly, we show that it is hard to solve
k
-SLR in
\ell_{p}
norms (including
p=1
and odd
p>2
) faster than
O(N^{(1-\epsilon)k})
under the strong exponential-time hypothesis (SETH)
[
IP01
]
. The strong exponential-time hypothesis, formulated by Impagliazzo and Paturi, conjectures that the satisfiability problem (SAT), the cornerstone of computer science, cannot be sped up significantly. More precisely, SETH states that for every
\epsilon>0
, there is an
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k
-SAT on
n
variables cannot be solved in time
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
.

These results together settle the exact complexity of the
k
-sparse linear regression problem in the worst-case, assuming popular conjectures in fine-grained complexity and the geometry of numbers. Under these conjectures, there are
no better-than-brute-force
algorithms for sparse linear regression.

A Window into our Techniques: Translating Integrality into Sparsity.

The hardness of problems such as the closest vector problem are closely connected to integer linear programming, where hardness arises from the fact that one has to come up with
integer solutions
to an optimization problem. On the other hand, there is no such integrality condition in
k
-sparse linear regression; rather, the hardness seems to arise from the fact that we insist on
sparse
solutions. Thus, a key technical contribution of our work is showing several ways to map two apparently different sources of hardness, namely integrality and sparsity.

In the rest of the paper, we provide a self-contained exposition of our results. We start with definitions in Section
2
, and proceed to show the reduction from the min-weight
k
-clique problem to sparse linear regression in Section
3
and the reduction from the lattice closest vector problem in Section
4
. Finally, in Section
5
, we also show that there are no sub-quadratic
N^{2-\epsilon}
-time algorithms for
k
-SLR when
k=2
, assuming the strong exponential-time hypothesis.

1.1
Related work

Lower bounds.

Natarajan
[
Nat95
]
showed the
\mathcal{NP}
-hardness of solving sparse linear regression, which rules out
(Nk)^{c}
-time algorithms for any absolute constant
c
. Har-Peled, Indyk and Mahabadi
[
HPIM18
]
showed the hardness of solving
k
-SLR faster than
O(N^{k/2})
time, under the well-known
k
-SUM conjecture from fine-grained complexity. However, both results left a gap between the best known upper bound which is brute-force search that runs in
N^{k}
time, and a lower bound of
N^{k/2}
.

Algorithms.

Har-Peled, Indyk and Mahabadi
[
HPIM18
]
also give an algorithm for
approximate
sparse linear regression
with pre-processing
that runs in time slightly better than brute force, namely in time
\tilde{O}(N^{k-1})
. Gupte and Lu
[
GL20
]
showed a slight improvement to the standard
k
-SLR problem in the
noiseless
setting, namely they showed an
O(N^{k-1})
time algorithm. They also give a
N^{\Omega(k)}
-time lower bound in the noiseless setting, under the Exponential Time Hypothesis. In contrast to both these works, our result shows that even such a modest improvement is impossible to achieve for the standard and well-studied
k
-SLR problem. That is, we show that
k
-SLR cannot be solved in time
O(N^{k-\epsilon})
for any constant
\epsilon>0
, under the popular min-weight
k
-clique conjecture
[
Wil18
]
from fine-grained complexity.

Compressed Sensing and the LASSO.

While the focus of this paper is the worst-case complexity of sparse linear regression, its
average-case
complexity has been the object of much study. Methods based on
\ell_{1}
-relaxation, such as basis pursuit and Lasso estimators
[
Tib96
,
CDS98
]
as well as the Dantzig selector
[
CT07
]
have been shown to meet the minimax-optimal lower bound in some cases. In particular, they focus on the setting where
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w}
where the noise vector
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
comes from a Gaussian distribution. While these methods perform well when the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
is well-conditioned in an appropriate sense, Zhang, Wainwright and Jordan
[
ZWJ14
]
show that such a condition is necessary unless
\mathcal{NP}\subseteq\mathcal{P}/\mathsf{poly}
. Recently, and concurrently with this work, Kelner et al
[
KKMR21
]
show random-design instances of sparse linear regression that are hard for a particular class of algorithms, namely preconditioned Lasso. In contrast, we show hardness for
worst-case
design matrices, but with respect to
any algorithm
that runs faster than trivial brute force search.

Further afield, the areas of fine-grained complexity and the geometry of numbers (or the study of integer lattices) have recently been shown to have interesting connections to statistical problems. For example, Backurs, Indyk and Schmidt
[
BIS17
]
show that certain empirical risk minimization (ERM) problems cannot be solved in sub-quadratic time, under the SETH conjecture. In another result, Backurs and Tzamos
[
BT17
]
showed that the classical Viterbi algorithm for finding the most likely path in a Hidden Markov Model is in fact optimal under the min-weight
k
-clique hypothesis which we rely on in this work. Bruna, Regev, Song and Tang
[
BRST20
]
recently showed the hardness of problems in robust statistics and learning mixtures of Gaussians, assuming the hardness of lattice problems.

2
Preliminaries and definitions

Notations.

Bold lower-case letters, such as
\mathbf{x}
, denote (column) vectors. Bold uppercase letters, such as
\mathbf{A}
, denote matrices.
\mathbb{R}
stands for the set of real numbers,
\mathbb{Z}
for the set of integers, and
\mathbb{R}^{+}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
) for the set of non-negative real numbers (resp. integers). We will let
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}
denote the
\ell_{p}
norm of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, that is,
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}=\big(\sum_{i\in[N]}|x_{i}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}
. The Hamming weight of
\mathbf{x}
, also called its
\ell_{0}
norm, is simply the number of non-zero coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
. A vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is called
k
-sparse if its Hamming weight is at most
k
, that is, if
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0}\leq k
.

2.1
Sparse linear regression

We now define the sparse linear regression problem.

Definition 1

(
k
-SLR
p
)
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the
k
-sparse linear regression problem with respect to the
\ell_{p}
norm is defined as follows.
Given a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
, a target vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and a number
\delta>0
, distinguish between:

•

a
YES
instance, where there is some
k
-sparse
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{p}\leq\delta
; and

•

a
NO
instance, where for all
k
-sparse
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{p}>\delta
.

k
-sparse linear regression can be trivially solved in time
N^{k}
. In this work, we are interested in determining if there are significantly faster algorithms. We will sometimes also give the algorithms more power, by allowing them unbounded time to preprocess the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
in an “offline” phase, and require them to be fast only in an “online” phase where they are given
\mathbf{b}
. We call this variant
sparse linear regression with preprocessing
.

2.2
Fine-grained complexity and its hypotheses

Fine-grained complexity is the study of exact runtimes of algorithms for various problems. We present the relevant problems and results here, and refer the reader to the survey by Vassilevska-Williams
[
Wil18
]
for a detailed exposition.

k
-SAT and the SETH Hypothesis.

A central problem in fine-grained complexity is the study of the satisfiability problem, and the associated strong exponential-time hypothesis.
Given a CNF-SAT formula on
n
variables with each clause having size at most
k
, the
k
-SAT problem asks to determine whether the formula is satisfiable or not.

Impagliazzo and Paturi
[
IP01
]
introduced the strong exponential time hypothesis, conjecturing the fine-grained hardness of
k
-SAT.

Definition 2

(Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH))
.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists an integer
k\geq 3
such that no (randomized) algorithm can solve
k
-SAT on
n
variables in
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
time.

We also use the non-uniform version of SETH to show hardness of sparse linear regression with preprocessing.

Definition 3

(Non-uniform SETH)
.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists an integer
k\geq 3
such that no family of circuits of size
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
solves
k
-SAT on
n
variables.

The Weighted
k
-Clique Conjecture.

Suppose we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=N
nodes. For a constant
k\geq 3
, a
k
-clique of
G
is a fully connected subgraph of
G
of size
k
. The
k
-clique problem asks to distinguish between
YES
instances, where
G
contains a
k
-clique, and
NO
instances, where
G
does not contain any
k
-clique. Given an
edge-weighted
graph and a threshold weight
W
, the Min-Weight
k
-Clique problem asks to distinguish between
YES
instances which are graphs
G
that contain a
k
-Clique of weight at most
W
, and
NO
instances where
G
does not contain any
k
-clique of weight at most
W
.

Definition 4

(Min-weight-
k
-clique)
.

Given a graph
G
with integer edge weights and a weight threshold
W
, determine whether
G
has a
k
-clique with weight at most
W
or not.

The naive algorithm for both problems requires enumerating all
\binom{N}{k}
subsets of vertices of size
k
, and this takes time
O(N^{k})
. In the case of the
k
-clique conjecture, slightly faster algorithms that run in time
O(N^{\omega k/3})
are known
[
NP85
]
, where
\omega<2.373
is the matrix multiplication constant.
However, no non-trivial algorithms are known for the weighted
k
-clique problem. It is conjectured that the problem of finding the minimum weight
k
-clique requires time
N^{k-o(1)}

[
Wil18
]
.

Under the min-weight
k
-clique hypothesis, Backurs and Tzamos
[
BT17
]
showed that the classical Viterbi algorithm for finding the most likely path in a Hidden Markov Model is in fact optimal. Under this hypothesis, tight lower bounds are also known for the the local alignment problem
[
AWW14
]
from computational biology and the Maximum Weight Rectangle problem
[
BDT16
]
from computational geometry.

Definition 5

(Min-weight
k
-clique hypothesis)
.

The Min-Weight
k
-Clique problem on
N
node graphs with edge weights in
\{-N^{100k},\ldots,N^{100k}\}
requires (randomized)
N^{k-o(1)}
time.

We note that the following variants of the min-weight
k
-clique problem are as hard as the original one. First, we can assume that the edges are non-negative and in the range
\{1,\ldots,2N^{100k}+1\}
. To see this, let
w^{*}
be the minimum weight of the edges in a graph. Create a new graph
G^{\prime}
with the same vertex and edge sets. The edge weights of
G^{\prime}
are obtained by adding
|w^{*}|+1
to the weight of the corresponding edge in
G
. Then
G^{\prime}
has a
k
-clique of weight at most
W+\binom{k}{2}(|w^{*}|+1)
, if and only if
G
has a
k
-clique of weight at most
W
.

Secondly, we can assume that there is a partition of the vertex set into
k
subsets such that the
k
-clique has one vertex from each subset.
To see this, suppose there is a
k
-clique
K
in
G
with weight at most
W
. Then, we claim that we can assume that the vertex set
V
is partitioned into
k
subsets
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots,V_{k}
such
|K\cap V_{i}|=1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
To see this, randomly partition the vertices into
k
subsets, by assigning each vertex to a subset uniformly and independently at random. Then, for any given
k
-clique (and in particular, the min-weight one), the probability that it has exactly one vertex in each subset is
\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{k-i}{k}\right)=\frac{k!}{k^{k}}
, which is at least
\sqrt{2\pi k}\cdot e^{-k}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12k+1}}>e^{-k}
by Stirling’s formula. Repeating this
O(e^{k})
many times completes the argument. Note that although the number of repetitions is exponential in
k
, it is independent of
N
, and so is sufficient for our purposes.

2.3
The closest vector problem

A lattice
\mathcal{L}
is the set of the integer linear combinations of a set of linearly independent vectors (a basis)
\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{n})
where each
\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})=\left\{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right\}.

Given a lattice basis
\mathbf{B}
, and a target vector
\mathbf{t}
, define
\dist_{p}(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}),\mathbf{t})=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t}\|_{p}
to be the minimum
\ell_{p}
distance from
\mathbf{t}
to
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
.

We follow
[
BGS17
]
for definitions of lattice problems.

Definition 6

(
\gamma
-approximate Closest Vector Problem with respect to the
\ell_{p}
norm (
\gamma
-
\text{CVP}_{p}
))
.

For any
\gamma\geq 1
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, given a lattice
\mathcal{L}
(specified by its basis
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
), a target vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a number
r>0
, distinguish between a
YES
instance, where
\dist_{p}(\mathcal{L},\mathbf{t})\leq r
and a
NO
instance, where
\dist_{p}(\mathcal{L},\mathbf{t})>\gamma r
.

We refer to the exact version of the problem, when
\gamma=1
, as
\text{CVP}_{p}
.

The best known algorithm for exact CVP
2
runs in time
2^{n+o(n)}
time
[
ADS15
]
. However, for
p\neq 2
, the best known algorithms for CVP
p
are slower, i.e., they run in time
n^{O(n)}

[
Kan83
,
MW14
]
.
Bennett, Golovnev and Stephens-Davidowitz
[
BGS17
]
proved an SETH-based hardness bound for the Closest Vector Problem. In particular, for many
p
, including odd integers
p\geq 1
and
p=\infty
, they show that
\text{CVP}_{p}
cannot be solved in time
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
for any
\epsilon>0
, unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) is false.

Theorem 1

(SETH-hardness of CVP
p

[
BGS17
]
)
.

For every
\epsilon>0
and every integer
p\geq 1,p\notin 2\mathbb{Z}
there is no
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
algorithm for CVP
p
, unless SETH is false.

CVP with preprocessing (CVPP) refers to algorithms for the closest vector problem which are allowed an unbounded-time offline preprocessing phase where they get the basis
\mathbf{B}
, and are required to be fast only in an online phase where they receive the target vector
\mathbf{t}
and the distance threshold
r
. Aggarwal, Bennett, Golovnev and Stephens-Davidowitz show the following hardness results for CVPP
p
.

Theorem 2

(
[
ABGSD21
]
)
.

For every
\epsilon>0
and every integer
p\geq 1,p\notin 2\mathbb{Z}
there is no
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
algorithm for CVPP
p
, unless non-uniform SETH is false.

Examining their proof more carefully, we notice that they prove a stronger result. In particular, they show SETH-hardness of the following problem, which we refer to as
(0,1)
-
\text{CVP}_{p}
.

Definition 7

(
(0,1)
-
\text{CVP}_{p}
)
.

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the
(0,1)
-Closest Vector Problem with respect to the
\ell_{p}
norm is the following promise problem. Given a lattice
\mathcal{L}
specified by a basis
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, a target vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two numbers
r,\tau>0
, distinguish between the following two cases:

•

YES
instances, where there exists some
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{t}\|\leq r
; and

•

NO
instances, where for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{t}\|\geq r+\tau.

Their method does not show hardness of
\text{CVP}_{2}
(or
(0,1)
-
\text{CVP}_{2}
). On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, there is no
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
-time algorithm for
(0,1)
-CVP
2
for any
\epsilon>0
. Indeed, as we observe in Section
A
, such an algorithm would imply a better algorithm for Weighted Max-Cut.

3
Optimal worst-case hardness of sparse linear regression under the min-weight
k
-clique hypothesis

The main result of this section is the theorem below, which shows that there are no
better-than-brute-force
algorithms for worst-case sparse linear regression, unless the Min-weight
k
-clique conjecture is false.

Theorem 3
.

For any integer
k\geq 4
, the
k
-SLR problem in the Euclidean (
\ell_{2}
) norm requires
N^{k-o(1)}
(randomized) time, unless the Min-Weight
k
-clique conjecture is false.

Proof.

We give a reduction from any Min-weight
k
-clique instance
G=(V,E)
with positive edge weights to a
k
-SLR
2
instance
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},\delta)
as follows.
First, we set large numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, whose significance will be clear in the sequel:

\alpha=\sqrt{\max\left\{1,\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}+8W\right\}},\quad\beta=\sqrt{\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}+8W+\alpha^{2}Z}\cdot\max\left\{8Z,50\left(\alpha^{2}Z+\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}\right)\right\},

where we define
Z=\left|\{(u,v)\notin E\mid u,v\in V\}\right|
to be the number of non-edges in the graph
G
.

We enumerate the unordered pairs of vertices
(u_{i_{1}},u_{i_{2}})
with index
i\in\left[\binom{N}{2}\right]
where
u_{i_{1}},u_{i_{2}}\in V
. Let edge
e
have weight
w_{e}
.
We first describe how we construct
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}
which have the following structure:

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{D}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{d}\end{pmatrix}

The submatrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{N}{2}\times N}
contains one row for each unordered pair of vertices and one column for each vertex in
V
. This submatrix
\mathbf{C}
, along with the target subvector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{N}{2}}
, encodes the
k
-clique instance. The submatrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times N}
and its corresponding target vector
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
ensure that any solution must have exactly
k
non-zero entries, all of which are close to
1
.

For each unordered pair of vertices
(u_{i_{1}},u_{i_{2}})\notin E
corresponding to a non-edge, we add a row to the matrix
\mathbf{C}

4
Optimal worst-case hardness of sparse linear regression under the hardness of lattice problems

Our main theorem in this section is a fine-grained reduction from the closest vector problem (CVP) in any norm to sparse linear regression (SLR) over the same norm. Using Theorem
1

k
-
\mbox{SLR}_{p}
(defined on odd norms) that runs in time better than

for any constant
\epsilon>0

While CVP and SLR look similar at a high level, our reduction has to bridge a significant difference between the problems:

Theorem 4
.

For any integer

5
SETH-hardness of

-SLR

Here we define the Orthogonal Vectors problem (OV), and show that a subquadratic algorithm for
2
-sparse linear regression in the
2
-norm violates the Orthogonal Vectors Hypothesis and, by a result of

Let

of

|U|=|V|=n
, distinguish between:

•

\mathbf{u}\in U

\mathbf{v}\in V

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{u}[i]\mathbf{v}[i]=0

Definition 8

.

The Orthogonal Vectors Hypothesis is implied by the strong exponential time hypothesis, as the following theorem states.

Theorem 5

(SETH-hardness of Orthogonal Vectors
[
WY14
]
)
.

If there is an algorithm that solves OV in
n^{2-\epsilon}\poly(d)
time, for some
\epsilon>0
then SETH is false.

With this background, we are ready to state our main result of this section, and its corollary, which guarantees the hardness of
2
-sparse linear regression under SETH or the weaker Orthogonal Vectors Hypothesis.

Proposition 1
.

There is an efficient reduction from Orthogonal Vectors with
2
sets of
n

to
2
-sparse linear regression in the
2
-norm, with a

Corollary 2
.

Suppose there is an algorithm that can solve

-sparse linear regression in time

. Then, the Orthogonal Vectors Hypothesis and SETH are false.

Appendix A
Reduction from Weighted Max-cut to CVP

In this section, we define the weighted max-cut problem, and recall a reduction that shows how any improvement in algorithms for the closest vector problem in the
\ell_{2}
-norm would imply a better algorithm for weighted max-cut than what is currently known. This reduction, along with Theorem
4
gives further evidence of the
N^{k}
-hardness of the
k
-sparse linear regression problem in the
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Suppose we are given a weighted undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and a weight function
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, where edge

between vertices

\mathsf{Maxcut}(G):=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}}\sum_{i,j}(1-x_{i}x_{j})\cdot w(e_{ij})

(Weighted Max-Cut)
.

Given a weighted undirected graph
G=(V,E)
on
|V|=n
vertices, where edge

YES

\mathsf{Maxcut}(G)\geq W
and a
NO

\mathsf{Maxcut}(G)<W
.

The naive enumeration algorithm runs in time
2^{n}
, and an improvement to even

for any
\epsilon>0

[
Wil04
]
.

Theorem 6
.

There is an efficient reduction from the weighted max-cut problem on a graph of
n

(0,1)
-closest vector problem in the
\ell_{2}
-norm on an
n

Proof.

W
of Max-cut. Suppose that
G

with the index

. We index vertices
u_{j}\in V
with

.
We construct an instance
(\mathbf{B},\mathbf{t},r,\tau)
of
(0,1)
-CVP
2

\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{D}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{d}\end{pmatrix},\quad r^{2}=\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}-W+n\alpha^{2},\quad\tau=\sqrt{r^{2}+1}-r

For each edge
e_{i}=(u_{j_{1}},u_{j_{2}})\in E

w_{e_{i}}

i

\mathbf{B}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. The entries of the
i

\mathbf{c}

\mathbf{C}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{w_{e_{i}}}&j\in\{j_{1},j_{2}\}\\

Also, let
\mathbf{D}=2\alpha I_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{t}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(u,v)\notin C}w_{e}+n\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}-W+n\alpha^{2}=r^{2}.

\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{t}\|_{2}\geq r+\tau

Now, we show soundness. Suppose there is some vector

Setting

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{t}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=(u,v)\notin C^{*}}w_{e}+n\alpha^{2}=\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}-W^{*}+n\alpha^{2}\leq r^{2},

This reduction runs in time
O\left(n(m+n)\right)\leq O(n^{3})
, and this completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof for Section
5

Proof for Proposition
1
.

We give a reduction from OV to
2
-sparse linear regression in the
2
-norm.
Suppose we are given an OV instance consisting of two sets
U,V
of
d
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}\in U
and
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}\in V
, each of size
n
.
Define the normalized vectors
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|}\mathbf{u}_{i}
and

\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|}\mathbf{v}_{i}
such that
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{2}=1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then construct an instance
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},\delta)
of
2
-sparse linear regression as follows.

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}|&&|&|&&|\\
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{n}&\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{n}\\
|&&|&|&&|\\
\alpha&\ldots&\alpha&\alpha&\ldots&\alpha\\
0&\ldots&0&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix},\quad b=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\alpha\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix},\quad\delta=\sqrt{2},

where we set
\alpha=16\delta d
, whose significance will become clear later.

We first show completeness. Suppose there are two vectors
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in U,\mathbf{v}_{j}\in V
such that
\left\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle=0
. Then, construct the vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that

x_{\ell}=\begin{cases}1&\ell\in\{i,n+j\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}_{2}=\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}+\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}=\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}\|^{2}_{2}+\|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}+2\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle=2=\delta^{2}
.

We now show soundness. Suppose that there is some vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_{2}\leq\delta
.
First, we claim that
\mathbf{x}
must have at least one non-zero entry indexed from the index set
I_{1}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and at least one non-zero entry indexed from
I_{2}:=\{n+1\ldots,2n\}
.
Suppose for contradiction that
x_{i}=0
for all
i\in I_{1}
. Then,
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|\geq\alpha>\delta
. Using a similar argument for
I_{2}
, and because
\mathbf{x}
is
2
-sparse, we know that
\mathbf{x}
must have exactly two non-zero entries, one indexed from each of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.
Let the two non-zero entries be
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, with
i\in I_{1}
and
j\in I_{2}
.

Next, we claim that the two non-zero entries have to be close to
1
. Specifically,
x_{i},x_{j}\in\left[1-2\delta/\alpha,1+2\delta/\alpha\right]
. Otherwise, we get a contradiction since
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|\geq 2\delta
.

Note that since the vectors of the OV instance
\mathbf{u}\in U,\mathbf{v}\in V
have
0,1
entries, we know that either
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle=0
or
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq 1
. Translating this to a statement about the normalized vectors, we know that either
\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle=0
or
\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle\geq 1/d
.

Now, we can lower and upper bound the prediction error
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|
as follows. By assumption, we know that
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}\leq\delta^{2}=2
. Further,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|x_{i}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}+x_{j}\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\|^{2}=x^{2}_{i}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i}\|^{2}+x^{2}_{j}\|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\|^{2}+2x_{i}x_{j}\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle=x_{i}^{2}+x^{2}_{j}+2x_{i}x_{j}\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 2\left(1-\frac{2\delta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}+2\left(1-\frac{2\delta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle\geq 2\left(1-\frac{4\delta}{\alpha}\right)+2\left(1-\frac{4\delta}{\alpha}\right)\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 2\left(1-\frac{4\delta}{\alpha}\right)+\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle.

Combining the upper and lower bounds from above,
\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle\leq\frac{8\delta}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2d}<1/d
. So, it must be true that
\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle=0
, and so there is an orthogonal pair of vectors.

This reduction runs in time
O(nd)
time, so an algorithm that solves
2
-sparse linear regression in time
O(N^{2-\epsilon})
for some
\epsilon>0
will solve OV in time
O(n^{2-\epsilon})
. This completes the proof.
∎
