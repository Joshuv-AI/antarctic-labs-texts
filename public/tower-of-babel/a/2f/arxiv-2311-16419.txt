A review on the charging station planning and fleet operation for electric freight vehicles
Md Rakibul Alam, Zhaomiao Guo

Abstract
Freight electrification introduces new opportunities and challenges for planning and operation. Although research on charging infrastructure planning and operation is widely available for general electric vehicles, unique physical and operational characteristics of EFV coupled with specific patterns of logistics require dedicated research. This paper presents a comprehensive literature review to gain a better understanding of the state-of-the-art research efforts related to planning (charging station siting and sizing) and operation (routing, charge scheduling, platoon scheduling, and fleet sizing) for EFVs. We classified the existing literature based on the research topics, innovations, methodologies, and solution approaches, and future research directions are identified. Different types of methodologies, such as heuristic, simulation, and mathematical programming approaches, were applied in the reviewed literature where mathematical models account for the majority. We further narrated the specific modeling considerations for different logistic patterns and research goals with proper reasoning. To solve the proposed models, different solution approaches, including exact algorithms, metaheuristic algorithms, and software simulation, were evaluated in terms of applicability, advantages, and disadvantages. This paper helps to draw more attention to the planning and operation issues and solutions for freight electrification and facilitates future studies on EFV to ensure a smooth transition to a clean freight system.

RQ1: What justifies the necessity for dedicated research into EFVs?

The travel patterns of freight vehicles differ significantly from those of passenger vehicles. Freight vehicle trips are more frequent and adhere to tight pickup and delivery schedules, in contrast to the less frequent and less rigorously scheduled trips made by passenger vehicles, with the exception of commercial passenger vehicles like ridesourcing and dial-a-ride services. The more frequent freight trips necessitate more charging due to lower energy efficiency resulting from the heavy freight payload.

Unlike private
EV
users who have the flexibility to choose
CS
with varying speeds, such as Level 1, Level 2, and fast chargers, based on their contingencies and specific time of day,
EFV
require a dense distribution of
DCFC
(
DCFC

Speth et al. 2022
. While interstates and highways feature
DCFC
stations, most of these are presently inadequate and cause challenges in accommodating and maneuvering large
EFV

Hurtado-Beltran et al. 2021
. Furthermore, freight drivers require mandatory rest periods, particularly during long-distance middle-mile freight deliveries. These unique characteristics of
EFV
demand special attention when planning CS infrastructure and operational scheduling for
EFV
. In addition, different freight logistics patterns demand modifications in operational planning.

The extensive literature on
EV
has primarily concentrated on passenger vehicles within the context of
CS
planning
[
Alam et al. 2022
,
Cui et al. 2019
,
Liu et al. 2019
,
Arslan and Karaşan 2016
,
Guo et al. 2016
,
Baouche et al. 2014
]
.
Unplanned CS allocation has been identified as a key hindrance to
EV
adoption
Xu et al. 2022
that can be equally true for
EFV
. This issue can be exacerbated when operational scheduling for
EFV
is implemented without considering the unique characteristics of these vehicles.

4.5
Platoon scheduling

Although the long charging duration of

On one hand, the energy saving by platooning will significantly reduce the need for en-route charging and avoid shipment delays. On the other hand,

5.2
Mathematical programming model

The above-mentioned coverage-based and queue-based approaches provide good guidance for
CS
planning, however, the decisions made are not guaranteed to be system optimal.

To quantitatively optimize the sitting and sizing decisions of
CS
, mathematical programming approaches can be used. The application of the mathematical programming for allocation of
CS
were proposed in

.

In contrast to modeling most of the operational problems using holistic mathematical programming approaches such as
MIP
(
MIP
),
IP
(
IP
),
MINLP
(
MINLP
) etc., some studies used heuristic procedures to determine “good” routing strategies, which may not be optimal.
Montoya et al. 2016
implemented
MSH
(
MSH
) (introduced in
Mendoza and Villegas 2013
) for
EFV
routing problems.
MSH
has two phases: sampling and assembling. During the sampling phase, routes from the traveling-salesman-problem tours are sampled, whereas the assembling phase uses the set-partitioning formulation. The first phase builds a pool of routes via a set of randomized route-first cluster-second heuristics and the second phase assembles a routing decision by solving a set partitioning formulation over the routes that are stored in the pool. The former controls the feasibility and the cost of each route while the latter controls the feasibility and the cost of the whole solution.
Andelmin and Bartolini 2019
implemented a multi-start local search heuristic that is built based on the idea of a multi-start method proposed in
Martí et al. 2013
. Among the three phases of the multi-start local-search heuristic, the first two phases iteratively construct new solutions which are further improved by the local search algorithm and finally, all vehicles’ routes associated with improved solutions are stored in a route pool. The set of optimal routes from this route pool is decided from a set partitioning algorithm in the third phase, which is further improved by local search algorithms.

Reyes-Rubiano et al. 2019

5.3
Simulation-based approach

Although mathematical programming is a rigorous quantitative approach for making optimal operational decisions, this approach could be computationally expensive for large-scale problems in the real world and formulating such models may become challenging for very complex systems and introducing the uncertainty. In contrast to the pursuit of optimal decisions, utilizing simulation models can prove to be an effective method for comprehending the intricate dynamics exhibited by a system when the simulation environment can be effectively calibrated.

For example, after siting
CS

Jahangir Samet et al. 2021
implemented simulation model (

[

In context of operational scheduling problem, both commercial and open-source simulation software were used.

used commercial transportation planning software TransCAD (which has built-in route optimization solving feature) for

However, it’s worth noting that these software solutions are not specifically designed for

Survey approach

Constraints

CS

A set of constraints were formulated for planning the deployment of

Operational scheduling

Freight network constraints
:
Freight networks can be modeled as directional graphs. To ensure the realistic representation of freight operation, the following graph constraints are typically imposed (e.g.,
[
Yilmaz and Kalayci 2022
,
Schneider et al. 2014
,
Ahmadi et al. 2021
]
).

•

In-degree and out-degree constraints define that exactly one edge enters and leaves each node of the freight network associated with each customer, respectively. This constraint ensures that
EFV
departs after visiting a customer or
CS
and flow conservation is guaranteed in routing.

•

To run the shipment cycle on a regular basis,
EFV
should return to its origin after completing the deliveries along the route. This property can be ensured by constraining that the number of
EFV
leaving the depot equals the number of
EFV
entering the depot.

•

Sub-tour elimination constraint prevents a trip visiting few cities in the given set and return to the starting city.

•

Each customer is visited exactly once which enforces the connectivity of customer visits so that overall freight transportation is organized and streamlined.

Freight load constraints
:
Freight load constraints are crucial for tracking freight load while
EFV
moving along the route and satisfying customer demand. Some typical constraints found in the existing literature (e.g.,
Yilmaz and Kalayci 2022
,
Schneider et al. 2014
,
Ahmadi et al. 2021
,
Arias-Londoño et al. 2021
) are as follows.

•

The freight flowing through each edge is less than the freight capacity of the
EFV
.

•

The amount of freight to be delivered by an
EFV
after visiting the
CS
remains the same because charging has no impact on freight load
[
Yilmaz and Kalayci 2022
]
.

•

The weight of the
EFV
right after leaving the depot is equal to the sum of the freight weight and
EFV
’s own dead weight.

Battery
SOC
constraints
:
The constraints for tracking battery
SOC
are fundamental to model the energy dynamics and charging behavior of
EFV
. Dynamics of battery
SOC
can be modeled by the following constraints
[
Van Duin et al. 2013
,
Pelletier et al. 2018
,
Yilmaz and Kalayci 2022
]

•

The real-time battery energy of each
EFV
shall not exceed its battery capacity. Based on the distance traveled, the battery
SOC
is reduced along the route unless charged. The remaining distance (i.e., the distance that can be traveled by the remaining battery
SOC
) is also monitored. The initial remaining distance is equal to the maximum battery range of the
EFV
. The remaining distance is updated by subtracting the distance traveled between nodes based on MPGe. No trip shall be conducted with a trip distance exceeding the remaining distance of a vehicle.

•

Without loss of generality, a constraint is imposed to ensure that the
SOC
needs to be greater than a minimum percentage and below the maximum percentage of battery capacity. For example,
EFV
are not allowed to reach extremely low
SOC
or constantly be fully charged to enhance the longevity of battery performance and/or emulate real-life user behavior.
However, full charging is assumed at en-route
CS
by some existing studies (e.g.,
Elahi and Avakh Darestani 2022
).

•

In charging scheduling problem, each
EFV
will occupy at most one charger per period
[
Pelletier et al. 2018
]
. Such constraint is needed to determine the charging availability and charger allocation.

•

To address the variable charging speed based on different battery
SOC
, a charger-specific piece-wise linear function needs to be implemented to formulate a

(
LP
) model.

•

Majority of the studies assume that
EFV
leave the depot fully charged. A constraint is required to ensure this initial condition.

•

Considering smart charging with
V2G
capability where bi-directional power flow is considered, in the real-time operational scheduling mode, the lower bound of charged power (i.e., the upper bound of discharged power) at a particular timestamp for an
EFV
is determined by the availability of the
EFV
at the CS and the maximum discharge power at that particular timestamp. On the other hand, the upper bound of charge power is determined by the availability of the
EFV
at the CS and the maximum charge power
[
Al-Hanahi et al. 2022
]
.

Fleet sizing constraints
:
For the fleet sizing problem, the number of utilized
EFV
per period can not exceed the available number of

[
Elahi and Avakh Darestani 2022
,
Yeomans 2021
,

The total of

Coupling

The majority of the constraints are found similar to mathematical programming in

9
Conclusions and RQ7: What are the potential research areas and challenges that have not been adequately explored

Transportation electrification in the freight sector is a possible trend in the near future. This paper reviews the existing research on the challenges and opportunities of adopting electric vehicles in freight transportation. In particular, we focus on three major themes, including
CS
planning,
EFV
operational scheduling, and the coupling of
CS
planning and operation, with a detailed classification of the proposed methodologies and solution approaches. Based on our review, we identified further research directions that can further facilitate freight electrification, as summarized below.

1.

The existing research typically focuses on centralized decision-makers to optimize the planning and operational decisions. However, these decisions can involve multiple stakeholders, such as charging station providers, power utilities, and freight companies, who make decisions in a decentralized manner to optimize their own objectives but whose decisions will interact with each other. The application of a game theoretical modeling framework can be an effective strategy to better understand their decentralized interaction and derive effective policy incentives to guide the system evolvement for societal benefits.

2.

The existing literature mainly focuses on the planning and operation of
EFV
considering transportation systems. However, freight electrification may have significant impacts on both power transmission and distribution systems due to higher charging power and larger battery capacity. In addition to planning charging stations, a necessary power system upgrade may be needed to support the additional charging power. Furthermore, considering
V2G
,
EFV
fleet can be valuable distributed energy resources to support power system operation compared with passenger vehicles because of larger battery capacity and more predictable travel and charging patterns.

3.

Most of the studies focus on planning and operation of
EFV
for normal scenarios considering economic efficiency. However, freight electrification could face significant challenges during disruptions due to the urgency of good delivery and potential charging inaccessibility due to power or transportation disruptions. More studies will be needed to consider the challenges and opportunities of
EFV
during emergencies to enhance system reliability and resilience.

4.

From a modeling perspective, there are some critical features that can be further considered to shed light on the efficiency and feasibility of adopting
EFV
, including the uncertainties of charger/driver availability, the influence of charging/electricity prices, human-drive v.s. automated, special types of logistics (e.g., logistics for humanitarian, human organs, refrigerated goods, etc), energy consumption factors (e.g., road pavement condition
[
Alam et al. 2020
]
, seasonal and geospatial impact
[
Hao et al. 2020
]
), and land use restriction.

5.

Although existing studies have proposed different computational algorithms, there is still a lack of understanding on the comparison of computational performance and solution qualities among different solution approaches. A comparison using standardized models and benchmark test systems for different type of problems will be beneficial.

6.

Solutions of planning/operational decision models can be validated through field experiments (e.g., pilot studies). Validated results from pilot studies will further encourage more stakeholders in freight systems to consider
EFV
in their decision-making process and promote
EFV
adoption.
