Relating incompatibility, noncommutativity, uncertainty and Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassicality
Stephan De Bièvre

Abstract
We provide an in-depth study of the recently introduced notion of completely incompatible observables and its links to the support uncertainty and to the Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassicality of pure quantum states. The latter notion has recently been proven central to a number of issues in quantum information theory and quantum metrology. In this last context, it was shown that a quantum advantage requires the use of Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassical states. We establish sharp bounds of very general validity that imply that the support uncertainty is an efficient Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassicality witness. When adapted to completely incompatible observables that are close to mutually unbiased ones, this bound allows us to fully characterize the Kirkwood-Dirac classical states as the eigenvectors of the two observables. We show furthermore that complete incompatibility implies several weaker notions of incompatibility, among which features a strong form of noncommutativity.

Introduction

Among the salient characteristics of quantum mechanics, distinguishing it from classical mechanics, feature prominently the incompatibility and noncommutativity of two observables and the associated uncertainty principles. They are, together with entanglement, the main ingredients for the study of the classical-quantum transition within the quantum state space. It was pointed out in

The latter provides a mathematical expression to the physical idea that the measurement of a second observable after the measurement of a first one always perturbs the result obtained in the first measurement, whatever the pre-measurement state. Complete incompatibility was then shown to lead to a strong uncertainty relation for all pure states which in turn was proven to be linked to the notion of Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassicality. It is the goal of this paper to further explore the consequences of the complete incompatibility of two observables and its relation to various weaker notions of incompatibility (Proposition
7
), as well as to deepen the logical links between this notion, the uncertainty inherent in quantum states, and their Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassicality. One of the main results of our analysis is that when two observables are completely incompatible and – in a sense we make precise – close to mutually unbiased, then all states, except the eigenstates of the two observables, are Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassical (Theorem

When two observables do not satisfy the stringent complete incompatibility condition, our results still permit to partially characterize the KD-nonclassical states (Theorem

We shall work in the context of quantum mechanics on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
of finite dimension

{\mathcal{A}}=\{|a_{i}\rangle|1\leq i\leq d\}

{\mathcal{B}}=\{|b_{j}\rangle|1\leq j\leq d\}

We show in particular that when two observables are completely incompatible, no spectral projector of

B

To study the link of complete incompatibility with uncertainty, we proceed as follows. Given a state
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we define
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)
(respectively
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
) to be the number of nonvanishing components of
\psi
on
{\mathcal{A}}
(respectively
{\mathcal{B}}

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=\sharp\{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\mid\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle\not=0\},\quad n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=\sharp\{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\mid\langle b_{j}|\psi\rangle\not=0\}.

We then introduce the uncertainty diagram of the pair
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})
to be the set of points
(n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}})\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket^{2}
in the
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}n_{{\mathcal{B}}}
-plane for which there exists
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
so that
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}
,
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{B}}}
. Whereas for an arbitrary choice of bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
the uncertainty diagram can have a complex structure, we show it can be easily characterized in the case the two bases are completely incompatible. Indeed, in that case, it is composed of
all
points
(n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}})\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket^{2}
for which
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}\geq d+1
(Theorem
1
). This last inequality, which is in fact equivalent to complete incompatibility, can be interpreted as an uncertainty relation: it says
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)
and
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
cannot both be small.

To illustrate these first results, we analyse the case where
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are mutually unbiased bases (MUB), meaning
|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
, for all
i,j
. Those have attracted considerable attention over the years

,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
and many open questions concerning their classification remain in dimensions
d\geq 6

[
25
]
.
The MUB are sometimes referred to as “complementary” or “maximally incompatible”
[
23
]
because if a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
yields the outcome
|a_{i}\rangle
, then a subsequent measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
yields any of the outcomes
|b_{j}\rangle
with the same probability. It turns out, nevertheless, that not all MUB are completely incompatible, as already pointed out in
[
19
]
. Whereas, in dimensions

this is easily seen to be the case, no MUB are completely incompatible when
d=4

[
19
]
. In higher dimension, the situation is unclear: it is in particular not known in which dimension there exist MUB that are completely incompatible. We show in this paper (Theorem

{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
for which
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}:=\min_{i,j}|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}-\delta
, for any
\delta>0
. In other words, there exist completely incompatible bases that are arbitrary close to being mutually unbiased.

Having explained that the complete incompatibility of two observables is equivalent to a strong uncertainty relation for all states
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we turn to the central question of this paper, which are the links between complete incompatibility, uncertainty and “nonclassicality”. For that purpose, we use the notion of nonclassicality associated to the Kirkwood-Dirac distribution.
Recall that, given
{\mathcal{H}}
and two orthonormal bases
{\mathcal{A}}=\{|a_{i}\rangle\}
and
{\mathcal{B}}=\{|b_{j}\rangle\}
, the Kirkwood-Dirac (KD) distribution of a state
\psi

[
26
,
27
]
,

Q(\psi)_{ij}=\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle,1\leq i,j\leq d,

(1)

is a quasi-probability distribution somewhat similar in spirit to the Wigner distribution or Glauber-Sudarshan
P
-function
[
28
,
29
]
in continuous variable quantum mechanics, which are intimately linked to the choice of two conjugate quadratures
X
and
P
.
It is complex-valued and satisfies

\sum_{ij}Q(\psi)_{ij}=1,

with marginals

\sum_{j}Q(\psi)_{ij}=|\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle|^{2},\sum_{i}Q(\psi)_{ij}=|\langle b_{i}|\psi\rangle|^{2}.

A state
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
is said to be
KD classical
if its KD distribution is real nonnegative everywhere; in other words, if its KD distribution is a probability distribution. Otherwise it is
KD nonclassical
. KD nonclassicality is used in quantum tomography
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
as well as in the theory and applications of weak measurements, (non)contextuality, and their relation to nonclassical effects in quantum mechanics
[
2
,
5
,
4
,
8
]
. Also, KD nonclassicality has been shown to be an essential ingredient necessary to obtain an operational quantum advantage in postselected metrology
[
15
,
16
]
.

For these reasons, the question of how to characterize the KD-nonclassical states poses itself naturally. One way to do this is to identify a nonclassicality witness. By this, we mean a quantity depending on the state
|\psi\rangle
that can be computed or measured without determining the KD-distribution of the state, and whose value can reveal its KD nonclassicality. It is crucial to keep in mind that the KD distribution and hence the KD nonclassicality of
|\psi\rangle
depend not only on
|\psi\rangle
, but also on the two bases
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
used to define it, the choice of which is in applications linked to the identification of two observables
A
and
B
of which they are eigenbases. The questions that arise are therefore: under what conditions on the bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
do there exist KD-nonclassical states, how many are there, where in Hilbert space can they be found and how strongly nonclassical are they?

A form of incompatibility or of noncommutativity is needed between the projectors
|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|
and
|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|
for KD-nonclassical states to exist. Indeed, if for example
A
and
B
have nondegenerate spectra, and if they are compatible in the usual sense that
[A,B]=0
, then all those projectors commute, and therefore each
|a_{i}\rangle
is up to a phase equal to some
|b_{j}\rangle
. It is then immediate from (
1
) that
Q_{ij}(\psi)\geq 0
for all
\psi
so that there are no KD-nonclassical states in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Under the assumption that
A
and
B
are incompatible in the sense that they do not commute, a sufficient but non-optimal condition for a state to be KD nonclassical was given in
[
16
]
. (See Theorem
15
below.) In the present paper, we sharpen and optimize that result in several ways. In particular, our main results under the assumption that the bases are completely incompatible are summarized in the following theorem. It is a corollary of Theorem
8
and Theorem
13
.

Theorem 1
.

Two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are COINC if and only if for all
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\geq d+1
.

In that case:

(i)

For all
1\leq n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}}\leq d
with
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}\geq d+1
,
\exists\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{B}}}
.

(ii)

If
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi):=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>d+1
, then
\psi
is KD nonclassical.

(iii)

If a state
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
is KD classical, then
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi):=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=d+1
.

(iv)

If in addition
{\mathcal{A}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}
are such that

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}:=\min_{ij}|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|^{2}>\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}{1+\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\right),

(2)

and hence in particular when they are mutually unbiased, then the only classical states are the vectors in
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
.

The first part of this result can be suggestively paraphrased as saying that, when
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are completely incompatible, all states, except possible those that have minimal support uncertainty, are KD nonclassical. Part (ii) in particular shows that the
support uncertainty

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi):=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
is a KD-nonclassicality witness. According to (iv), if in addition
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is close to its maximal possible value
1/\sqrt{d}
, only the elements of the two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are classical.

Since complete incompatibility is a stringent condition on the bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, not necessarily satisfied in all situations of interest, it is important to understand what can be said about the KD-nonclassical states in such situations as well. It was shown in
[
19
]
that, if the bases satisfy the weaker incompatibility condition that

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}:=\min_{i,j}|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|>0,

then part (ii) and (iii) of Theorem
1
still hold (but not (i)). In other words, under that condition, the support uncertainty is still a KD-nonclassicality witness. We show here this remains true in much greater generality, even if
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=0
, but provided not too many of the overlaps
\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle
vanish (Theorem
12
). We further establish a sharpening of the bound in (ii) of the above theorem: provided
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is sufficiently close to
1/\sqrt{d}
, any
|\psi\rangle
that is not an eigenvector of one of the two observables and satisfies
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>d
is nonclassical (Theorem
13
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we define the mathematical framework within which we shall work. Section

briefly reviews the standard notions of incompatibility that appear in the literature and their link with noncommutativity. Section

This sets the stage for our definition and characterization of complete incompatibility in Section
5

6

7
we establish under which conditions the support uncertainty of a state can serve as a KD nonclassicality witness. This allows us to provide a complete characterization of all KD-classical states under suitable conditions on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
. Some final conclusions are drawn in Section
8
.

Setup

In this preliminary section, we describe the basic objects entering our study and fix the notation we shall use. Throughout, we will consider two distinct orthonormal bases
{\mathcal{A}}=\{|a_{i}\rangle|1\leq i\leq d\}
and
{\mathcal{B}}=\{|b_{j}\rangle|1\leq j\leq d\}
of a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Given two such bases,
we define their transition matrix
U_{ij}=\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle
.
Introducing

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}:=\min_{i,j}|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|,\quad M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=\max_{i,j}|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|,

one has

0\leq m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}\leq d^{-1/2}\leq M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}\leq 1,

(3)

which follows immediately from the fact that the transition matrix is unitary so that

d=\Tr\mathbb{I}=\sum_{i,j}|\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle|^{2}.

Clearly

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0\Rightarrow M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1.

(4)

The condition
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
means
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
have no basis vectors in common. We will see below (Proposition
7
) that both the conditions
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
and
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
can be viewed as a form of incompatibility between the two bases, with
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
being the stronger one of the two.

Considering a column of
U
containing a term for which
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|
, one has

1\geq M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}+m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}(d-1)

(5)

and similarly, considering a column of
U
containing a term for which
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|
, one has

1\leq m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}+M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}(d-1).

(6)

Hence

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Leftrightarrow M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Leftrightarrow m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}};

any of these conditions characterizes mutually unbiased bases.

When two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are specified, we can associate to each
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
its
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
-
representations
, which are the
vectors of their components
u(\psi)=(\langle a_{1}|\psi\rangle,\dots,\langle a_{d}|\psi\rangle)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
on the
{\mathcal{A}}
basis and
v(\psi)=(\langle b_{1}|\psi\rangle,\dots,\langle b_{d}|\psi\rangle)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
on the
{\mathcal{B}}
basis. One has

u(\psi)=Uv(\psi)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}|u_{i}(\psi)|^{2}=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|v_{j}(\psi)|^{2}.

(7)

Given a state
\psi
, so that
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
,
|u_{i}(\psi)|^{2}
and
|v_{j}(\psi)|^{2}
form two probabilities representing uncertainties which can be measured in various ways. We will come back to this extensively below and in particular study the joint uncertainty with respect to both bases.

Given bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, we introduce a family of orthogonal projectors as follows. For every
S,T\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket:=\{1,2,\dots,d\}
,

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)=\sum_{i\in S}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|,\quad{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)=\sum_{j\in T}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|.

In some cases the two bases
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
arise as the eigenbases of two self-adjoint operators
A
and
B
on
{\mathcal{H}}
. If their eigenvalues are non-degenerate, then the corresponding bases are unique (up to a global phase and possible reordering of the basis vectors), but not otherwise. In what follows, given two observables
A
and
B
, we shall always assume a particular eigenbasis has been chosen for each. In many examples of interest, however, the two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are not associated to observables, as we shall see.
If a basis is given, we will say a self-adjoint operator
A
with eigenvalues
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\dots<\alpha_{L}
is adapted to the basis
{\mathcal{A}}
if the latter is a basis of eigenvectors for
A
. We can associate a projective partition of unity to such an adapted observable by considering the partition of
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
defined by

S_{\ell}=\{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\mid A|a_{i}\rangle=\alpha_{\ell}|a_{i}\rangle\},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq L.

(8)

The
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})
are then the eigenprojectors of
A
and one has

\cup_{\ell}S_{\ell}=\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad\sum_{\ell}{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})=\mathbb{I}.

Finally, there is a unique projection-valued measure associated to
A
in the usual way. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then we introduce the spectral projector of
A
for
I
to be the operator
\Pi(A\in I)
defined as follows:

\Pi(A\in I)=\sum_{\alpha_{\ell}\in I}{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell}).

This is the sum of the projectors onto the basis vectors whose corresponding eigenvalue is in
I
.
Note that these spectral projectors are all of the type
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)
for some
S\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
.

These considerations lead to the following description of quantum measurements associated to a basis
{\mathcal{A}}
, that we need for our discussion of both uncertainty and incompatibility.
We only need some basic elements of the quantum theory of projective measurements as discussed in in
[
1
,
30
,
31
]
, that we briefly recall.

We in particular only consider selective measurements: the outcome of an individual measurement is always recorded. Given a basis
{\mathcal{A}}
, one can associate to every partition
S_{1},\dots,S_{L}
of the index set
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
a family of projectors
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{1}),\dots{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{L})
forming a projective partition of unity in the sense that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})=\mathbb{I}.

To such a family corresponds a selective projective measurement the outcomes of which are the
S_{\ell}
. In the case where the
S_{\ell}
are as defined in (
8
), this is commonly referred to as a measurement of
A
. The outcomes
S_{\ell}
are then identified with the eigenvalues
\alpha_{\ell}
. If initially the system is in the state
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
and the outcome
S_{\ell}
is realized then the post-measurement state is the pure state
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})\psi/\|{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})\psi\|
. The probability of this outcome is
\|{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S_{\ell})\psi\|^{2}
. This is what we shall refer to as a measurement in the basis
{\mathcal{A}}
. When
L=d
and
S_{\ell}=\{\ell\}
, for
\ell=1,\dots,d
, we will say the measurement is finegrained. Otherwise it is coarsegrained. When
L=2
, a case we will frequently consider, the corresponding measurement has only two possible outcomes and one has
S_{1}=S,S_{2}=S^{\textrm{c}}
, for some
S\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
. In line with the preceding terminology, we can also refer to this as a measurement of
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)
. Indeed, the projector

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)
has two eigenvalues,
1
and
0
, that correspond to the outcomes
S
and
S^{\textrm{c}}
.

Note that the transition matrix
U
depends on the order in which the basis vectors are listed. None of the definitions or results in this work will depend on that order and we will freely reorder the bases when it is convenient. Similarly, the results only depend on the basis vectors up to a global phase. In particular, if
k
of the basis vectors of
{\mathcal{A}}
coincide with
k
of those of
{\mathcal{B}}
then one can consider
|a_{i}\rangle=|b_{i}\rangle
for

, after a possible reordering and adjusting of the phases. From the point of view of incompatibility, uncertainty and KD nonclassicality, nothing interesting then happens on the subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
spanned by the first
k
basis vectors and the entire analysis in this paper can then be done on the
d-k
-dimensional complementary subspace. In particular, one then has

Q_{ij}(\psi)=|\langle\psi|a_{i}\rangle|^{2}\delta_{ij},\quad\forall 1\leq i,j\leq k.

More generally, when
U
is block-diagonal, then so is
Q(\psi)
and the analysis should be performed for each block separately.

For later use, we introduce the following notation. For
S,T\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
we introduce the
|S|\times|T|
matrix
U(S,T)
with entries

U(S,T)_{k\ell}=\langle a_{i_{k}}|b_{j_{\ell}}\rangle,

(9)

where
1\leq k\leq|S|,1\leq\ell\leq|T|
, and
i_{k}\in S,j_{\ell}\in T
. Note that Rank
U(S,T)\leq\min\{|S|,|T|\}
. Also, in what follows

{\mathcal{H}}(S,T)={\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}.

(10)

3
(In)compatibility and (non)commutativity

To study (in)compatibility, we need to discuss repeated successive measurements in the bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, a subject we turn to in this section.
We start with a brief but critical analysis of the standard relationship between compatibility and commutativity of observables
[
32
,
31
]
, which is helpful in motivating the notion of complete incompatibility introduced in Section
5
.

Suppose on a state
\psi
successive measurements in
{\mathcal{A}}
, then
{\mathcal{B}}
, yield the outcomes
S
, then
T
.
We will say these outcomes are compatible if, when measuring again in
{\mathcal{A}}
after having obtained
T
, the outcome
S
occurs with probability one. This means that the measurement of
{\mathcal{B}}
after the first measurement of
{\mathcal{A}}
has not disturbed the first outcome. Repeating measurements in
{\mathcal{A}}
and/or
{\mathcal{B}}
will then systematically yield the same outcomes, always with probability one. As a result, we adopt the following definition:

Definition 2
.

S,T
are compatible outcomes for a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
followed by one in
{\mathcal{B}}
if

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\subset{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}.

(11)

Indeed, if the pre-measurement state is
\psi
, the (nonnormalized) state after the first two measurements is
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi
. For the second measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
to yield
S
with probability one for all such pre-measurement states
\psi
, one needs that
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
for all such
\psi
. This justifies the definition. For brevity, we will simply say
S
and
T
are compatible.

Eq. (
11
) is equivalent to
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0
:

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\subset{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\Leftrightarrow[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0.

(12)

Indeed, Eq. (
11
) means
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)
leaves
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
invariant, which implies it also leaves its orthogonal complement
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S^{\textrm{c}}){\mathcal{H}}
invariant so that
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S^{\textrm{c}})=0
. Hence

\displaystyle{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi={\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi-{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S^{\textrm{c}})\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi.

The converse implication is obvious. One then also has

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\subset{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\Leftrightarrow[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0\Leftrightarrow{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}\subset{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}.

(13)

As a result, if first a measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
is made, and the outcome
T
obtained, then a subsequent measurement of
{\mathcal{A}}
with outcome
S
does not perturb the first measurement. In other words, if
S,T
are compatible outcomes for a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
followed by one in
{\mathcal{B}}
, then
T,S
are compatible outcomes for a measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
followed by one in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

We will say two observables
A
and
B
are compatible when all outcomes associated to their eigenvalues
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{k}
(see Eq. (
8
)) are compatible in the sense of Eq. (
11
). In view of what precedes, this means
A
and
B
are compatible if and only if they commute. Mathematically, this is a very strong, restrictive property. It entails a number of further physical properties such as joint measurability and measurement non-disturbance
[
3
,
6
,
17
]
.

Note that
S
and
T
are mutually exclusive when, after a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
yielded the outcome
S
, a measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
cannot yield the outcome
T
. This is equivalent to
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}=0
. This, in turn, implies Eq. (
11
), so that, in this sense, mutually exclusive events are compatible. While this sounds odd, considering the standard everyday meaning of the words “compatible” and “mutually exclusive”, it is implicit in all discussions on quantum mechanics and we shall also adopt this practice. Indeed, it often occurs that two observables commute, while many outcomes
\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{k}
are mutually exclusive.

What happens if
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
? Suppose a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
on the pre-measurement state
\psi
yields the outcome
S
so that the post-measurement state is, up to normalization,
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi
. Suppose that a subsequent measurement in
B
yields the outcome
T
, and the post-measurement state is
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi
. As we saw, this second measurement has not perturbed the first
if and only if

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi={\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi,

(14)

which is equivalent to

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
. Do such states exist? The answer is yes, if and only if

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}\not=\{0\}.

(15)

In that case, taking
0\not=\phi\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}
,
\phi^{\prime}\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S^{\textrm{c}}){\mathcal{H}}
, and setting
\psi=\phi+\phi^{\prime}
, one has indeed Eq. (
14
). Note that Eq. (
14
) is equivalent to the statement

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)\psi\in{\mathrm{Ker}}[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)].

Of course, even though
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
, generally the kernel of
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]
is not trivial. In fact, it is always true that

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathrm{Ker}}[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)].

In conclusion, if
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}\not=\{0\}
, then, for any state
\psi\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}
, repeated successive measurements
in
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
will give with probability one the outcomes
S
and
T
. In other words, even if the commutator does not vanish, it is still possible for the measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
to yield the outcome
T
, without perturbing the previous outcome
S
. However, if
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
, there also exist pre-measurement states
\psi
for which this is not true. To see this, note that, since the projectors don’t commute, we know
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
is not invariant under
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)
. So there exists
\psi\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
so that
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi\not\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
. If initially the system is in such a state
\psi
, the outcome of an
{\mathcal{A}}
measurement is
S
. Since
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi\not\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
, we know
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi\not=0
, so that the probability that a measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
yields
T
does not vanish. When this occurs, the post-measurement state is, up to normalization,
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)\psi\not\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
. Hence the probability that a subsequent measurement of
A
yields
S
is strictly less than one.

In conclusion, when two projectors
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)
and
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)
do not commute, it may still happen for some pre-measurement states that repeated successive measurements in
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
systematically yield the same outcomes
S
and
T
. However, this does no longer happen for every pre-measurement state that yields the outcomes
S
and
T
in the first two measurements.

A simple but instructive example illustrating this situation is this:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}(|a_{1}\rangle\langle a_{1}|+|a_{2}\rangle\langle a_{2}|)+a_{3}|a_{3}\rangle\langle a_{3}|,\quad a_{1}<a_{3},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{1}(|b_{1}\rangle\langle b_{1}|+|b_{2}\rangle\langle b_{2}|)+b_{3}|b_{3}\rangle\langle b_{3}|,\quad b_{1}<b_{3},

with

|b_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|a_{1}\rangle+|a_{2}\rangle),\quad|b_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|a_{1}\rangle-|a_{2}\rangle)+|a_{3}\rangle\right),\quad|b_{3}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|a_{1}\rangle-|a_{2}\rangle)-|a_{3}\rangle\right).

Let
S=\{1,2\},T=\{1,2\}
, so that
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S)
and
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)
are the eigenprojectors of
A
,
B
with eigenvalue
a_{1},b_{1}
. They do not commute but

{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}|b_{1}\rangle
.
Now, let
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|b_{1}\rangle+|a_{3}\rangle)
. Then a measurement of
A
yields the outcome
a_{1}
with probability
1/2
; the post-measurement state is then
|b_{1}\rangle
. A subsequent measurement of
B
yields the outcome
b_{1}
without altering the state and hence without altering the outcomes of any further measurements of
A
, which will always yield
a_{1}
. In this situation, the measurement of
B
has not disturbed the one of
A
. If, on the other hand, the pre-measurement state is

|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|a_{2}\rangle+|a_{3}\rangle)
, then a first measurement of
A
still yields
a_{1}
with probability
1/2
, but now the resulting state
is
|a_{2}\rangle
and

|a_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|b_{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|b_{2}\rangle+|b_{3}\rangle)\right).

A subsequent measurement of
B
yields
b_{1}
with probability
3/4
and the resulting state is then

\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}(|b_{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|b_{2}\rangle).

This is not an eigenvector of
A
and a second measurement of
A
only yields
a_{1}
again with probability
5/6
(and
a_{3}
with probability
1/6
). In other words, this time the measurement of
B
has perturbed the initial value obtained for
A
.
This example thus illustrates that indeed, when two operators do not commute, successive measurements may or may not perturb outcomes previously obtained.

It is common practice in quantum mechanics to identify the incompatibility of observables with their noncommutativity
[
31
]
.
In view of the previous discussion, this yields a rather weak notion of incompatible since it means that in some – but not in all – circumstances the measurement of the second observable may perturb the outcome of a previous measurement of the first observable. This is of course the result of the fact that incompatibility is defined here as the negation of compatibility, which is a very strong notion: it requires that a measurement of
B

never
disturbs a previous measurement of
A
.

In what follows, we will say two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}
are compatible if
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0
for all
S,T\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
.
And we will say two such bases are incompatible if this is not true; in other words, if
there exist

S,T\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
so that
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
.
We will see below that, to connect incompatibility to uncertainty and nonclassicality, a stronger notion of incompatibility is needed.

4
Uncertainties

It is well known that one way to understand the Heisenberg uncertainty relation is to view it as
a property of the Fourier transform saying, loosely speaking, that a function and its Fourier transform cannot both be sharply localized. When made precise, this statement can take many forms, thoroughly reviewed in
[
33
]
(See also
[
34
]
). The Heisenberg uncertainty relation (in the form proven by Weyl
[
35
]
) expresses the localization of the wave function in terms of the variances of its position and momentum distributions. Another form was proven much more recently and states that a wave function and its Fourier transform cannot both have their support in sets of finite volume. In other words, they cannot both vanish outside a region of finite volume. This formulation is very similar to the general support uncertainty principles that we will consider below (Section
4.3
) in the finite dimensional setting. They have attracted attention more recently, in the context of signal analysis
[
36
,
37
]
, in the theory of the Fourier analysis on finite groups
[
38
,
34
]
, and in the study of nonclassicality in quantum systems with a finite dimensional Hilbert space
[
19
]
, which is our focus here.

In order to give perspective on the notion of support uncertainty, we first briefly review some aspects of the Heisenberg, Robertson, and entropic uncertainty relations. In Section
4.1
we recall the well-known link between noncommutativity and uncertainty as expressed in the Robertson uncertainty relation and show that noncommutativity alone does not lead to very strong information about the uncertainty inherent in a state. We briefly review entropic uncertainty relations in Section
4.2
and turn to the support uncertainty relation in Section
4.3
.

4.1
The Heisenberg and Robertson uncertainty relations

Rather than viewing the original Heisenberg uncertainty relation as a property of the Fourier transform, one can alternatively (and equivalently) see it as a bound on the uncertainties of an arbitrary state
|\psi\rangle
with respect to two conjugate variables
Q
and
P
satisfying the canonical commutation relation
[Q,P]=i
. This alternative viewpoint leads to the generalization shown by Robertson
[
39
]
, and which concerns arbitrary noncommuting observables
A
and
B
:

\Delta A\Delta B\geq\frac{1}{2}|\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle|,\quad\textrm{where}\ C=-i[A,B].

(16)

It relates the precision with which we can know the value of two noncommuting observables
A
and
B
when the system under consideration is in a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
. More precisely, when the right hand side of this uncertainty relation does not vanish, the inequality can be interpreted as providing a limit on the statistical variation of the outcomes of measurements of
A
and
B
on an ensemble of systems in state
|\psi\rangle

[
30
,
40
]
. In other words, it quantifies to what extent the two observables are incompatible in this state: the sharper the knowledge on one, the less sharp the knowledge on the other. However, in finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, this can never give a useful lower bound on the product of the variances
for all states
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
at once
. Indeed, when
|\psi\rangle
is an eigenstate of
A
or of
B
, the right hand side vanishes and the inequality does not give a useful lower bound on the variances at all. In other words, in a finite dimensional Hilbert space, one always has that

\min_{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1}|\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle|=0.

(17)

In fact, since
\Tr C=0
, the self-adjoint operator
C
cannot have purely positive or purely negative spectrum: if it has a positive eigenvalue, it must also have a negative one, and vice-versa. As a result, the quadratic form
\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle
cannot be positive or negative definite, which again implies (
17
).
As a result, to get any information on the uncertainty on the knowledge of the values of the observables
A
and
B
in the state
|\psi\rangle
, one needs to know something about the state
|\psi\rangle
, namely the value of
\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle
. When it vanishes, no information is obtained. In this sense, the noncommutativity of
A
and
B
alone does not provide automatically an information on the degree of incompatibility of the two observables for any state
|\psi\rangle
. Again we see here that the common practice of equating the “incompatibility” of two quantum observables with the noncommutativity of the corresponding observables, recalled in the previous section, yields only limited information and suggests stronger notions of incompatibility are needed. We will extensively come back to this point below.

One may think the above observations are particular to the finite dimensional situation, but as we now briefly indicate, they are not. Let us therefore consider two observables represented by bounded self-adjoint operators
A
and
B
on an infinite dimensional Hilbert space. They may not have any normalizable eigenvectors, and only continuous spectrum. The above arguments leading to (
18
) do then not apply but one still has the following simple result, closely related to Putnam’s theorem
[
41
]
. The proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
A
and
B
be self-adjoint bounded operators on a finite or infinite dimensional Hilbert space and let
C=-i[A,B]
. Then

\inf_{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1}|\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle|=0.

(18)

Note that (
18
) again implies that the Heisenberg-Robertson uncertainty relation in Eq. (
16
) does not give a uniform bound on the variances for all
|\psi\rangle
at once so that, here as well, noncommutativity alone does not imply an information on the degree of incompatibility of the observables
A
and
B
.

Note that, if
A=Q
and
B=P
, then
C=\mathbb{I}
and of course (
18
) does not hold in that case. A slight variation on this example is
A=h(Q)
,
B=P
, so that
C=h^{\prime}(Q)
. Assuming
h^{\prime}(q)>\delta>0
for all
q
, again (
18
) does not hold. As a concrete example, one may consider
h(q)=q+a\sin q
with
a<1
. Then
h^{\prime}(q)\geq(1-a)>0
and
\inf_{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1}|\langle\psi|C|\psi\rangle|=1-a
. Of course, in none of these cases are
A
and
B
bounded.
These examples show that, in infinite dimension, the commutator
C
can be a positive definite operator which has positive spectrum bounded away from zero. This cannot happen however, if both
A
and
B
are bounded, as a result of (
18
). In that case, it is still possible for
C
to be non-negative, meaning that its spectrum is non-negative, and includes
0
so that (
18
) still holds. Examples of such operators are given in
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
.

The conclusion we draw is therefore that, generally, the fact that two observables do not commute does not necessarily provide a useful bound
on the uncertainties inherent in their measurement on an arbitrary state, as measured by the variances. As in the discussion in the previous section, this implies again that the standard notion of incompatibility of two observables (namely their noncommutativity) does not imply much about such uncertainty. These observations provide a first motivation to look for other means than the variance to express the uncertainty inherent in a state with regards to the measurement of two observables.

Before doing so, let us point out that, in finite dimension, there is a second drawback to the use of variances to measure uncertainties. Indeed, when measurements are made in two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
that are not naturally associated with observables
A
and
B
, then mean values and variances cannot be meaningfully defined
[
45
,
46
]
. Different approaches to the quantification of uncertainty are then needed. The entropic and support uncertainty relations provide such alternatives, as discussed next.

4.2
Entropic uncertainty relations

The two shortcomings of (
16
) in finite dimensional Hilbert space pointed out above are one of the reasons that entropic uncertainty relations have been developed in this context
[
45
,
46
,
40
]
. The best known of those is the Maassen-Uffink uncertainty relation which reads

H_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=H_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+H_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)\geq\ln M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{-2},

(19)

where
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
are two orthonormal bases of
{\mathcal{H}}
, as above, and

H_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)=-\sum_{i}|\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\ln|\langle a_{i}\psi\rangle|^{2},\quad H_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)=-\sum_{i}|\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\ln|\langle a_{i}\psi\rangle|^{2}

are the Shannon entropies associated to the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
-representations of
|\psi\rangle
. Note that, when
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
are the eigenbases of two observables
A
and
B
, the entropic uncertainty
H_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
does not depend on the spectra of
A
and
B
, only on the eigenbases, contrary to the variances
\Delta A,\Delta B
. In addition,
H_{\mathcal{A}}
and
H_{\mathcal{B}}
are insensitive to permutations of the basis vectors and to global phase changes.

The entropy
H_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)
vanishes if and only if
|\psi\rangle
equals one of the basis vectors; in that case the uncertainty inherent in
|\psi\rangle
with respect to an
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurement vanishes. An analogous statement holds for
H_{\mathcal{B}}
. The entropy
H_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
yields a measure of the joint uncertainty inherent in
|\psi\rangle
with respect to
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
measurements. It vanishes iff
|\psi\rangle
is a common basis vector of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
. In fact, such a vector exists if and only if
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
.
In that case, the Maassen-Uffink uncertainty relation provides no information, since the Shannon entropies are always nonnegative. When
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
, on the other hand, it provides a uniform lower bound valid for all
|\psi\rangle
, similar to the Heisenberg uncertainty relation for conjugate observables. We will see below that the condition
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
can be interpreted as a – very weak – incompatibility condition between the bases: see Proposition
7
.
The smaller
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
, the stronger the bound obtained. From (
3
) one concludes therefore that the strongest entropic uncertainty relation is obtained when
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
, in which case all overlaps have the same amplitude and the bases are mutually unbiased. The lower bound is then reached (at least) by the basis vectors.
We note however that, even when
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
, the bound is not always optimal and may not be reached, as extensively discussed in
[
47
]
.

4.3
The support uncertainty relation

Another uncertainty relation that has been used in various contexts other than quantum mechanics is the support uncertainty relation (see Eq. (
21
) below), first introduced in
[
36
]
in the context of the Fourier transform on finite groups. It has been shown to have much wider validity, however: see
[
37
,
34
]
.

To describe it, we introduce first the
{\mathcal{A}}
-support and
{\mathcal{B}}
-support of
|\psi\rangle
:

S_{\psi}=\{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\mid\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle\not=0\},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad T_{\psi}=\{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\mid\langle b_{j}|\psi\rangle\not=0\}.

They are the supports of the state
|\psi\rangle
in the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
-representations introduced in Eq. (
7
). One can also think of
S_{\psi}
as the set of possible outcomes of a fine-grained measurement in the
{\mathcal{A}}
-basis with the pre-measurement state
|\psi\rangle
. We then define

n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)=|S_{\psi}|\not=0,\ n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)=|T_{\psi}|\not=0,

(20)

where
|S|
denotes the number of elements in
S
.
Then
n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)
(respectively
n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
) is the number of nonvanishing entries of
u(\psi)
(respectively
v(\psi)
);
n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)
is therefore the size of the support or the “spread” of the probability distribution
|u_{i}(\psi)|^{2}
(respectively
|v_{j}(\psi)|^{2}
) of the state
|\psi\rangle
in the
{\mathcal{A}}
- (respectively
{\mathcal{B}}
)-representations. As such
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)
and
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
are measures of the localization of
|\psi\rangle
in the respective representations. They can be thought of as providing a measure of the uncertainty on the outcomes of a measurement in the
{\mathcal{A}}
-basis, respectively
{\mathcal{B}}
-basis when the system is in the state
|\psi\rangle
.

In our notation the support uncertainty relation then reads

n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)\geq M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{-2}.

(21)

The analogy with the Heisenberg-Robinson uncertainty relation is clear: it says that the supports of
|\psi\rangle
in the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and in the
{\mathcal{B}}
-representation cannot both be small since their product is bounded below. Like the Robertson uncertainty principle, it has a very simple proof, that we provide for completeness in Appendix
B
. (See also
[
19
]
for an alternatiive argument.) It can also be seen as an immediate consequence of the more sophisticated
Maassen-Uffink bound since the Shannon entropy is well-known to have the property that

H_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)\leq\ln(n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)),\quad H_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)\leq\ln(n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)).

Unlike what happens in the Heisenberg-Robinson uncertainty relation, and similarly to what happens for the Maassen-Uffink entropic inequality, the lower bound in Eq. (
21
) has the advantage that it does not depend on the state
|\psi\rangle
, and only depends on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
through the maximum value
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
of the matrix elements of
U
. It is also not necessarily saturated, as we will see below. Like the Maassen-Uffink bound, it is without interest if
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
, since both
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)
and
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
are positive integers.

For the purpose of relating uncertainty to incompatibility and KD nonclassicality, it turns out to be more instructive to consider lower bounds on the sum

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi):=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi),

(22)

that we shall refer to as the
support uncertainty
of
|\psi\rangle
, rather than on the product
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
. Both such bounds yield information on the tradeoff between uncertainty in the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
-representations of the state, but, as we shall establish, the sum provides a more natural and direct link with a strong form of incompatibility and KD nonclassicality.
The situation is not unlike the one in quantum optics, where the sum of the variances
(\Delta Q)^{2}
and
(\Delta P)^{2}
of two conjugate quadratures
Q,P
of the field yields a measure of the uncertainty and of the optical nonclassicality of the state referred to as its
total noise

[
48
]
. It satisfies the lower bound
(\Delta Q)^{2}+(\Delta P)^{2}\geq 1
for all states.

For later purposes, we also introduce the
minimal support uncertainty

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}
of the two bases
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
as

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}=\min_{|\psi\rangle\not=0}n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi).

(23)

Clearly

2\leq n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}\leq d+1.

The lower bound is obvious since for any nonzero
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)\geq 1
and
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\geq 1
. For the upper bound, consider any basis vector
|a_{i}\rangle
. Then
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(|a_{i}\rangle)=1
,
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(|a_{i}\rangle)\leq d
. So
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(|a_{i}\rangle)\leq d+1
, which yields the upper bound. Note that

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}\geq 3\Leftrightarrow M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1.

(24)

Indeed,
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}\geq 3
is equivalent to the fact that each
{\mathcal{A}}
basis vector is the linear combination of at least two
{\mathcal{B}}
-basis vectors and vice versa. Hence
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
. The converse is obvious. More generally, Eq. (
21
) implies that

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{\min}\geq\frac{2}{M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}},

(25)

which is again not sharp in general, as can be observed in Fig.
1
. See also the next subsection.

We have already pointed out that the Maassen-Uffink and support uncertainty relations provide no information when
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
. This is not surprising.
Indeed, when
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
,
{\mathcal{A}}
has
k\geq 1
basis vectors
|a_{i}\rangle
that are equal (up to an irrelevant phase) to
k
basis vectors
|b_{j}\rangle
. It is then not possible to get a nontrivial bound constraining the uncertainties
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
since they can both be simultaneously equal to
1
. As anticipated at the end of Section
2
, after reordering the basis vectors and adjusting their phase, we can then assume
|a_{1}\rangle=|b_{1}\rangle,\dots,|a_{k}\rangle=|b_{k}\rangle
and split the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
into the subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
generated by the first
k
basis vectors and its orthogonal complement
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{\perp}
, of dimension
d^{\prime}=d-k
.
The analysis of the uncertainty inherent in a state
|\psi\rangle
can then be done separately for its component in each of these subspaces.

4.4
Uncertainty diagrams

Given two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, their
uncertainty diagram
UNCD
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})
was defined in
[
19
]
to be the subset of all points
(n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}})
in the first quadrant of the
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}n_{{\mathcal{B}}}
-plane for which there exists a state
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
so that

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),\quad n_{{\mathcal{B}}}=n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi),

(26)

In other words,

{\mathrm{UNCD}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})=\{(n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}|\exists\psi\in{\mathcal{H}},n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{A}}},n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{B}}}\}.

(27)

One can also view the uncertainty diagram is the image of the map
|\psi\rangle\to(n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi))\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket^{2}
. As we will see, the uncertainty diagram is a useful practical and visual tool to study the mutual uncertainty of a state with respect to the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
- representations. Analogous uncertainty diagrams for entropic uncertainty measures were introduced in
[
47
]
.
In view of Eq. (
21
), the uncertainty diagram always lies above the hyperbola

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}n_{{\mathcal{B}}}=M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{-2}.

This lower bound is not necessarily reached, of course, since in general
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{-2}
is not an integer.

Three examples of uncertainty diagrams are presented in Fig.

. In the left panel a spin
1
system is considered, for which
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
. We choose for
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
the eigenbases of
J_{z}
and
J_{x}
, where

J_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\

We write
|z,\epsilon\rangle
and
|x,\epsilon\rangle
,
\epsilon=1,0,-1
for the corresponding eigenvectors. The transition matrix from the
J_{z}
basis to the
J_{x}
basis is then

U=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{2}&1\\
\sqrt{2}&0&-\sqrt{2}\\

In this case,
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=0
,
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1/\sqrt{2}
and the support uncertainty relation reads
n_{z}n_{x}(\psi)\geq 2
. The corresponding lower bound on the uncertainty diagram is therefore
n_{z}n_{x}=2
. Note that
J_{x}
and

do not commute so that the bases are incompatible. Details of the computations underlying the left panel of Fig.

are given in Appendix

.

In general, given two bases

5
Complete incompatibility

5.1
Complete incompatibility: definition, interpretation.

The previous sections make it clear that incompatiblity, understood as noncommutativity, is a rather weak notion. On the one hand, it only implies that a measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}

may
perturb a previously obtained outcome in a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
. On the other hand, it provides only limited information on the (minimal) uncertainty inherent in an arbitrary given state. To avoid these shortcomings, the following very stringent notion of incompatibility was introduced in
[
19
]
.

Definition 4
.

We say that two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are completely incompatible (COINC) if and only if all index sets
S,T
in
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
for which
|S|+|T|\leq d
have the property that
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}=\{0\}
.

It follows from the discussion in Section
3
, and particularly the one concerning Eq. (
15
), that this mathematical property has a direct interpretation in terms of the incompatibility of repeated successive measurements in the
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
bases. In short, complete incompatibility of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
means the following. Suppose that the system is prepared in
any
state
|\psi\rangle
and a measurement in the
{\mathcal{A}}
basis yielding the outcome
S
is followed by one in the
{\mathcal{B}}
basis yielding the outcome
T
. In that case, this second measurement has
always
a nonvanishing probability to perturb the result of the first, provided
|S|+|T|\leq d
. Since Eq. (
15
) allows for the possibility of the second measurement not to perturb the first for certain pre-measurement states, it is by negating this condition that a very strong notion of incompatibility is obtained. This is all the more true since the condition is imposed for all outcomes
S,T
, with
|S|+|T|\leq d
.

Note that the restriction
|S|+|T|\leq d
is needed since, when
|S|+|T|>d
, the intersection
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}
is necessarily nontrivial because the dimension of
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
is
|S|
and that of
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}
is
|T|
. What this expresses is the intuitively expected fact that if the measurements are too coarsegrained, then it may happen that one does not perturb the other. Note also that it would be sufficient in the definition to require
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}=\{0\}
for all
S,T
so that
|S|+|T|=d
, since the more general requirement follows from this immediately.

The condition
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(T){\mathcal{H}}=\{0\}
can also be understood geometrically, a viewpoint that sometimes helps the intuition. Given
S
, respectively
T
, one can think of
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
, respectively
{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T){\mathcal{H}}
as “coordinate vector spaces” for the
{\mathcal{A}}
-, respectively
{\mathcal{B}}
-representations. Indeed, analogously with the “
xy
-plane”, which corresponds to points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that have vanishing
z
-coordinate, these spaces contain all states that have vanishing coordinates corresponding to indices in
S^{c}
, respectively
T^{c}
. The condition that
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(S){\mathcal{H}}\cap\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(T){\mathcal{H}}=\{0\}
for all
|S|+|T|\leq d
can then be understood as saying that these coordinate spaces for the
{\mathcal{A}}
- and
{\mathcal{B}}
-representation have a trivial intersection. To put it differently, the coordinate spaces for
{\mathcal{A}}
sit askew in the coordinate spaces for
{\mathcal{B}}
, and vice versa.

The definition of COINC bases depends on the two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
only through the unitary transition matrix between them, defined as
U|a_{j}\rangle=|b_{j}\rangle
, with matrix elements
U_{ij}=\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle
in the
{\mathcal{A}}
-basis. This can be seen as follows. If
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
have transition matrix
U
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}
have transition matrix
U^{\prime}
, then
U=U^{\prime}
if and only if there exists a unitary map
V
so that
|a^{\prime}_{i}\rangle=V|a_{i}\rangle
and
|b^{\prime}_{i}\rangle=V|b_{i}\rangle
. But in that case, clearly,
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are COINC if and only if
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}
are.
Conversely, given an arbitrary unitary
d\times d
matrix
U
, one can always view it as the transition matrix between the canonical basis of
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
(taken to be
{\mathcal{A}}
) and the basis composed of the columns of
U
(taken to be
{\mathcal{B}}
). In view of this, we will say a unitary matrix
U
is COINC whenever these two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are. Depending on the situation at hand, it is sometimes more convenient to think in terms of two bases
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
and sometimes in terms of a unitary matrix
U
, without reference to the bases. Note finally that the complete incompatibility of two bases is also not affected by a renumbering, nor by global phase changes of the basis vectors.

5.2
Complete incompatibility: characterization and examples

A number of examples of COINC bases were given in
[
19
]
.
It is first of all easy to see that in dimension
d=2,3
, two bases are COINC if and only if
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
, in other words when the transition matrix has no zeros. This is no longer true in higher dimension as we shall show in Proposition
7
below. For example, it is shown in
[
19
]
using result of Tao
[
49
]
that, when the transition matrix
U
is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), the bases are COINC if and only if
d
is prime.
Other explicit examples of bases that are COINC don’t seem to be very easy to come by. It was nevertheless illustrated numerically in
[
19
]
that small perturbations of MUB for
d=4
tend to be COINC. Theorem
5
below explains this numerical observation in much more generality.

Theorem 5
.

The set of completely incompatible unitary
d\times d
matrices is open and dense in the set of all unitary matrices.

The proof, given in Appendix
F
, relies on the following algebraic characterization of complete incompatibility proven in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 6
.

Two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are COINC if and only if none of the minors of their transition matrix
U
vanishes.

To make this paper self-contained we also provide a proof of the lemma in Appendix
F
.

The theorem may seem somewhat surprising at first sight, since we insisted on the fact that complete incompatibility is a strong notion of incompatibility. To understand why it is true, one may consider Lemma
6
. A minor is a multi-variable polynomial in the matrix elements of
U
and its zeros form a very “thin” set: essentially it is a hypersurface of co-dimension
1
. Hence, since small perturbations of
U
will typically change the values of all its minors, those that initially vanish will typically become nonvanishing. On the other hand, if the perturbation is small enough, those that do not vanish initially will remain nonvanishing. This suggest that the set of COINC transition matrices is dense. This is precisely what we prove in Appendix
F
. The proof is somewhat technical because of the need to ensure that the perturbations considered preserve the unitarity. By the same reasoning, the set of COINC transition matrices is dense. Indeed, if no minor of
U
vanishes, then this will remain true for all small perturbations of
U
. The situation is similar to what happens when one considers commuting self-adjoint
d\times d
matrices, which are considered compatible. Those also form a very thin subset of all pairs of self-adjoint matrices and again, a slight perturbation will typically render them noncommuting, hence incompatible. And in this context also, when two observables do not commute, small perturbations of them will still not commute. This being said, the set of completely incompatible observables is considerably smaller than the set of merely incompatible ones, as will become clear in the following section.

5.3
A hierarchy of incompatibilities

As a first illustration of the strength of the notion of complete incompatibility, let us consider the case where
A
and
B
are observables with non-degenerate spectrum and eigenbases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
. Saying that
A
and
B
are incompatible in the standard sense that they do not commute then means that there exist at least one
i,j
so that
[|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|,\langle b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|]\not=0
. This is clearly much weaker than requiring that this needs to be true for all
i,j
which in turn is weaker than requiring that
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
for all
S,T\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
with
|S|+|T|\leq d
.
This is readily illustrated on a spin
1
system, so that
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}^{3}
, taking
{\mathcal{A}}
to be the eigenbasis of
J_{z}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
of
J_{x}
.
In spite of the fact that
J_{x}
and
J_{z}
do not commute,
the corresponding eigenbases are not COINC. To see this, one may note that
|x,0\rangle\in{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S){\mathcal{H}}
, with
S=\{-1,1\}
. So, if first a coarse-grained measurement is made of
J_{z}
on an arbitrary state
|\psi\rangle
, with two possible outcomes “
J_{z}=0
” and “
J_{z}\not=0
”, and the outcome is “
J_{z}\not=0
”, then a subsequent fine-grained measurement of
J_{x}
yielding the outcome “
J_{x}=0
” will not perturb the first measurement.
This is reflected in the fact that the outcomes
T=\{0\}
and
S=\{-1,1\}
are compatible in the sense of Definition
2
:
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0
.

To shed further light on the definition of complete incompatibility and to justify the use of this terminology, we will now show that Definition
4
implies three properties that can be seen as weaker manifestations of incompatibility.

Proposition 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
be two orthonormal bases on a
d\geq 2
dimensional Hilbert space. Consider the following statements:

(i)

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are COINC.

(ii)

For all
S,T\subset\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
, with
1\leq|S|,|T|<d
,
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]\not=0
.

(iii)

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
.

(iv)

M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
.

Then (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv).

In addition

•

When
d=2
, one has (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv);

•

When
d=3
, one has (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv), but (iv) does not imply (iii);

•

When
d\geq 4
, one has (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv), but (iv) does not imply (iii) and (iii) does not imply (ii);

•

When
d=4
or
d=6
, (ii) does not imply (i).

The proof of the proposition is given below, but we first discuss its meaning by interpreting each of the four statements (i)-(iv) as a manifestation of incompatibility.
The interpretation of (i) was given above. We need to understand the meaning of (ii), which is a strong statement of “noncommutativity”. Indeed, when two operators
A
and
B
commute, then all their spectral projectors commute. Saying that they don’t commute means therefore that there exist some spectral projectors that don’t commute; (ii) is much stronger, since it requires no spectral projectors to commute.
For example, the spin 1 bases discussed above clearly do not satisfy (ii), as explained above.

To see that (ii) implies also a strong notion of incompatibility, first recall that we showed two outcomes
S
and
T
are compatible (Definition
2
) if and only if
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0
(see Eq. (
12
)). So (ii) can be paraphrased by saying that no two outcomes
S
and
T
are compatible for a measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
followed by one in
{\mathcal{B}}
. This is indeed a strong notion of incompatibility. It can in fact be viewed as the negation of a weak form of compatibility given by the negation of (ii): there exist nontrivial
S,T
so that
[{\Pi_{{\mathcal{A}}}}(S),{\Pi_{{\mathcal{B}}}}(T)]=0
. However, note that (ii) only means that for any two such outcomes, it
may
happen – depending on the pre-measurement state – that the occurrence of the second one perturbs the first one. In other words, the second statement guarantees the possible perturbation of the first measurement by the second for all outcomes, but not for all states. One then understands why the second assertion is a weaker form of incompatibility than the first. Indeed, when (i) holds, the second outcome
always
perturbs the first one, as explained above. In Appendix
we give examples of bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
satisfying (ii) but not (i) in dimensions
4
and
6
.
We conjecture it is true that (ii) does not imply (i) in all dimensions
d\geq 4
, but we have not produced such examples in other dimensions than
4
and
6
. Note that checking condition (ii) in higher dimension is necessarily more involved since there are then many more choices of
S
and
T
.

Statement (iv), namely
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
guarantees that none of the basis vectors of
{\mathcal{A}}
is a basis vector of
{\mathcal{B}}
, and vice versa. This means that, when a first fine-grained measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
leaves the system in a basis state of
{\mathcal{A}}
, a subsequent measurement of
{\mathcal{B}}
has at least two possible outcomes with nonzero probability and therefore necessarily disturbs the first measurement, which is indeed a manifestation of incompatibility. So a fine-grained measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
then necessarily perturbs a previous fine-grained measurement in
{\mathcal{A}}
. In particular, if
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are eigenbases of two observables
A
and
B
with nondegenerate spectra, then (iv) implies the noncommutativity of all
|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|
and
|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|
which implies the noncommutativity of
A
and
B

[
50
]
. The converse is not true:
A
and
B
may not commute and nevertheless have some eigenvectors in common, in which case
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
.

Statement (iii) is a strengthening of (iv). It is sometimes referred to as a condition of “complementarity” for the two bases. This is because the condition
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
implies that, when the system is in one of the basis states of
{\mathcal{A}}
and a fine-grained measurement is made in
{\mathcal{B}}
, then any outcome
|b_{j}\rangle
may occur with nonvanishing probability. And vice versa. In that sense, there is uncertainty in the outcome of this second measurement, and this uncertainty is larger when
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is larger. Some authors
[
23
]
reserve the term “complementary” or “maximally incompatible” for mutually unbiased bases, for which these probabilities are the same for all
j
so that
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
takes on its maximal possible value
1/\sqrt{d}
. When (iii) holds, repeated successive fine-grained measurements of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
do not yield the same result with probability one. In fact, they can give any result and in that sense there is incompatibility between
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
. The proposition asserts this form of incompatibility does not imply the stronger condition on the commutators in (ii), in any dimension
d\geq 4
.

We now turn to the proof of Proposition
7
which contains instructive examples and counterexamples.

\Rightarrow

The uncertainty diagram of COINC bases

In this section we analyze what the complete incompatibility of two bases implies for their uncertainty diagram: we will see it can be completely determined and that it has a very simple form.

Theorem 8

Part (ii) of the theorem is essentially a rephrasing of the definition of the total incompatibility of two bases in terms of the localization properties of all states in the

6
Relating complete incompatibility and mutual unbiasedness

We have justified our definition of complete incompatibility of two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
(Definition
4
) through an analysis of
the effect of a
{\mathcal{B}}
-measurement on previously obtained information in an
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurement. We have seen that, essentially, two bases are COINC if and only if the measurement in
{\mathcal{B}}
always perturbs this previous information, whatever the outcome of the
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurement, which can be coarse grained or fine grained.

One can, alternatively, consider only the situations where the
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurement is fine grained, so that the state after the
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurement is
|a_{i}\rangle
, and require that the subsequent fine-grained
{\mathcal{B}}
measurement has a maximally uncertain outcome, which means that
|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|=1/\sqrt{d}
, so that all outcomes
b_{j}
are equally likely. This idea has lead to the definition of MUB which are, as already mentioned, bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
for which the transition matrix has the property that
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1/\sqrt{d}=m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
. MUB have attracted attention because of their potential use in various quantum information protocols
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
One may note that, when dealing with two conjugate (and hence continuous) variables
X
and
P
, an analogous property holds; indeed
|\langle x|p\rangle|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
in that case, expressing the idea that when the localization in
X
is perfect, the uncertainty in
P
is maximal and vice versa. The definition of MUB captures this same phenomenon: perfect localization in the
{\mathcal{A}}
-representation leads to maximal uncertainty in the
{\mathcal{B}}
-representation. However, conjugate variables have an additional property: their support in the
X
- and
P
-representations cannot be both finite.
Indeed, it is well known that there do not exist states
|\psi\rangle
for which the
X
-representation
\langle x|\psi\rangle
is localized inside some bounded set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
and the
P
-representation
\langle p|\hat{\psi}\rangle
is localized in some bounded set
T\subset\mathbb{R}

[
33
]
.
In fact, if the
X
-support of
|\psi\rangle
is bounded, its
P
-support must be unbounded. The definition of complete incompatibility naturally transcribes this second property to a somewhat analogous statement in the finite-dimensional case.
However, for the DFT in finite dimension, it follows from
[
38
]
and Theorem
8
that it is COINC if and only if the dimension is prime. This shows not all MUB are COINC. We point out that, on the contrary, the noncommutativity/incompatibility measures introduced in
[
18
]
are maximal on MUB and therefore do not distinguish between them.

More generally, one may therefore ask the question under what circumstances MUB are COINC. It is easy to see that in dimenion
d=2,3,5
all MUB are COINC, whereas it was shown in
[
19
]
that in dimension
d=4
, none of them are. In fact, we showed above that, in dimension
d=4
, no MUB satisfy the weaker condition (ii) of Proposition
7
. In higher dimension, the situation is not clear, in particular because a complete characterization of all MUB is not known for
d\geq 6
.

Two questions arise therefore naturally:

“In which dimensions do their exist MUB that are COINC?”

and

“In which dimensions are all MUB COINC?”

The answer to the first question certainly includes all prime dimensions since, as already mentioned, the DFT is COINC in prime dimension. Answering this question is probably not easy since a complete characterization of all MUB does not exist. Similarly, the only dimensions eligible for a positive answer to the second question are the prime dimensions. Again, in dimensions
2,3,5
the assertion is valid, but in higher prime dimensions the answer is not known, to the best of our knowledge.

While these questions seem difficult, as an immediate consequence of Theorem
5
, one nevertheless has that, arbitrarily close to any MUB, there are always bases that are COINC, in any dimension. This is the content of the following theorem, proven in Appendix
F
.

Theorem 10
.

For all
d\geq 2
and for all
0<m<\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
there exist bases
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
that are COINC and whose transition matrix
U
satisfies

m\leq m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}\leq 1/{\sqrt{d}}
.

, as we will see in examples below. Theorem
12
is an immediate consequence of the following more technical statement, which is of interest in its own right.

Proposition 11
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
be two orthonormal bases on a
d\geq 2
dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, with transition matrix
U
. Let
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
and suppose

\displaystyle\max\{n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi\}>\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\},

(31)

\displaystyle n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)>d+1,

(32)

then
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
is KD nonclassical.

The proof of this result is given below.

The first condition on
|\psi\rangle
, Eq. (
31
), is implied by the second in those cases where there are not “too many” zeros in
U
. This idea is made precise in the following theorem.

Theorem 12
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
be two orthonormal bases on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
of dimension
d\geq 2
, with transition matrix
U
.
Suppose

\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\}\leq\frac{d+1}{2}
.
Then, if
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies Eq. (
32
), it is KD nonclassical.

This result was proven in
[
19
]
for all
d\geq 2
, but only under the restrictive hypothesis that
U
has no zeros, in which case Eq. (
31
) is automatically satisfied for any
|\psi\rangle
.
The case
d=2
is particular and fully covered by the following remarks. Note that when
d=2
, then
Z=\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\}
. Since a unitary two by two matrix cannot have exactly one zero, it has either one or two zeros. The hypothesis
\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\}\leq\frac{d+1}{2}=3/2
therefore corresponds to the case where
U
has no zeros at all. In that case the theorem, together with the fact that the basis states are classical, implies that the only nonclassical states are those with
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=2=n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
. If on the contrary
U
does have two zeros, then it is (equivalent to) the identity matrix, and then there are no nonclassical states at all.

Proof of Theorem
12
.
We use Proposition
11
. It is enough to show that when
\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\}\leq\frac{d+1}{2}
, Eq. (
31
) is implied by Eq. (
32
). Suppose therefore that Eq. (
32
) holds. Then
\max\{n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\}>\frac{d+1}{2}\geq\max\{Z_{\textrm{r}},Z_{\textrm{c}}\}
, which proves the result.
∎

Here are two examples with
d=6
, that satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem:

U_{6}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1&1&1&1\\
1&0&1&-1&1&-1\\
1&1&0&-1&-1&1\\
1&-1&-1&0&1&1\\
1&1&-1&1&0&-1\\
1&-1&1&1&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad U^{\prime}_{6}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1&1&1&1\\
1&0&1&-1&1&-1\\
1&1&0&1&-1&-1\\
1&-1&1&0&-1&1\\
1&1&-1&-1&0&1\\
1&-1&-1&1&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(33)

Indeed,
Z=6,Z_{\textrm{r}}=2=Z_{\textrm{c}}
.
These examples can be extended to arbitrarily high dimension, as follows. Let us write
|a_{i}\rangle,|b_{j}\rangle
for two bases of
\mathbb{C}^{6}
having
U_{6}
as transition matrix. Let
{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{C}^{6}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
and let
V
be the unitary
k
by
k
transition matrix of two bases
|a^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle,|b^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle
of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
, that we assume to not have any zeros. Hence
U_{6}\otimes V
is the transition matrix between the bases
|a_{i},a^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle,|b_{i},b^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle
.
Then each basis vector
|b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle
has exactly
k
zero matrix elements on the basis
|a_{i},a^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle
. So the total number of zeros in the transition matrix is
Z=6k^{2}
and any
2
such basis vectors have
2k
vanishing components implying
Z_{\textrm{c}}=2k
. Similarly
Z_{\textrm{r}}=2k
. Since the dimension of the Hilbert space is
d=6k
, the condition in Theorem
12
holds for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
. These examples show that the number of zeros in any given column can in fact be very large, and proportional to the length of the column (which is
6k
in these examples).

When the two bases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are mutually unbiased, or in a suitable sense close to mutually unbiased, a stronger result can be proven, that we now turn to.

Theorem 13
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}
be two orthonormal bases on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
of dimension
d\geq 3
, with transition matrix
U
. Suppose

\frac{d-1}{d+1}<\left(\frac{m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}{M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}\right)^{2}\leq 1.

(34)

Suppose
|\psi\rangle
is KD classical. Then either
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}
or
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq d
.
Consequently, if
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are in addition COINC, then the only classical states are the basis states.

We excluded the case
d=2
from the statement because in that case, if
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
, we know from Theorem
12
that, if
|\psi\rangle
is classical, then
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq 3
. This, in turn, implies that
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}
, since
d=2
, proving the result. So the additional constraint on
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}/M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is not relevant when
d=2
.

The theorem implies that for the DFT in prime dimension, which is both mutually unbiased and COINC, only the basis vectors are classical. This can be observed in the rightmost panel of Fig.
1
for
d=7
.

Note that
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1/\sqrt{d}
is equivalent to
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
and hence to the bases being mutually unbiased. The theorem completely characterizes the classical states of COINC bases that have a large
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
, meaning that
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is close to its maximal possible value
1/\sqrt{d}
, attained only for MUB.
More precisely, suppose that, for some
0\leq\delta<1
,

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}>\frac{1}{d}(1-\delta).

Then the normalization of the columns of
U
implies that

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}(d-1)+M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}\leq 1,

so that

\left(\frac{m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}{M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}\right)^{2}>\frac{1}{1+\frac{\delta d}{1-\delta}}.

Hence, provided

\delta<\frac{2}{d(d-1)}\frac{1}{1+\frac{2}{d(d-1)}},

hypothesis Eq. (
34
) is satisfied. This proves Theorem
1
(iv).
Note that, while this is an increasingly restrictive condition as the dimension
d
grows, we know such
U
exist by Theorem
10
.

The proof of Theorem
13
relies on the arguments in the proof of Proposition
11
that we therefore prove first.

Proof (of Proposition
11
)
The proof follows the strategy used in
[
16
]
and
[
19
]
. We proceed by contradiction and suppose
|\psi\rangle
is KD classical and that hypothesis (
31
) holds. We need to prove that

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq d+1.

When
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=1
, or
n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=1
,
|\psi\rangle
is one of the basis states and the result is then immediate. We can therefore assume that

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi),n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\geq 2
. Since the KD distribution (see Eq. (
1
)) is insensitive to global phase rotations
|a_{i}\rangle\to\exp(i\phi_{i})|a_{i}\rangle,|b_{j}\rangle\to\exp(i\phi_{j}^{\prime})|b_{j}\rangle
, we can suppose that all
\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle
and
\langle\psi|b_{j}\rangle
are nonnegative (hence real) for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. Possibly reordering the basis vectors, we can suppose that
\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle\not=0\not=\langle b_{j}|\psi\rangle
for
1\leq i\leq n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi),1\leq j\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
whereas all other
\langle a_{i}|\psi\rangle
,
\langle b_{j}|\psi\rangle
vanish. By hypothesis, the KD distribution of
|\psi\rangle
is real and nonnegative. Hence, for the same range of
i
and
j
, we can conclude
\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle
is real and nonnegative.

Now, assume
2\leq n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)=d
. Then the matrix
U
contains two columns with real nonnegative entries. Since by hypothesis (
31
), there are at most
Z_{c}<d
zeros in these two columns of
U
this is in contradiction with the fact that those columns are orthogonal.

Let us therefore assume that
2\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)\leq n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)<d
.
Then, for
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
, we have

0=\langle b_{j}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)}\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle+\sum_{i=n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+1}^{d}\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle.

(35)

We first show that for all
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
, one has

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)}\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle>0.

(36)

To see this, note first that, for any fixed pair
j\not=j^{\prime}
, the sum in (
36
) contains
n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)
nonnegative terms. If at least one of those is positive, the sum is positive. But this is the case, since at most
Z_{c}
of these terms can vanish, and
Z_{c}<n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)
by hypothesis (
31
). This proves Eq. (
36
).

It then follows from Eq. (
35
) that, for all
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
, one has

\sum_{i=n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+1}^{d}\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle<0.

Defining, for each
1\leq j\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)
the vector
v_{j}=(\langle a_{n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+1}|b_{j}\rangle,\dots,\langle a_{d}|b_{j}\rangle)\in\mathbb{C}^{d-n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)}
we see from the above that
\langle v_{j}|v_{j^{\prime}}\rangle<0
. It then follows from Lemma
14
below that

n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)\leq d-n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+1.

This proves the result.

The case where
n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)\leq n_{\mathcal{B}}(\psi)<d
is treated similarly, inverting the roles of the columns and the rows.
∎

Proof (of Theorem
13
)

We proceed as in the proof of Proposition
11
.
We can again suppose
2\leq n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)<d
. Note that this implies that
|\psi\rangle
is not one of the basis vectors since
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
. Eq. (
35
) now yields

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)\leq|\sum_{i=n_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi)+1}^{d}\langle b_{j}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|b_{j^{\prime}}\rangle|\leq M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{2}(d-n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)).

Using that
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)\leq n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)
, this implies

n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq\frac{2d}{1+\left(\frac{m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}{M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}}\right)^{2}}<\frac{2d}{1+\frac{d-1}{d+1}}=d+1.

It follows that
n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)\leq d
, which is the desired result. The second statement of the Theorem now follows from
Theorem
8
.
∎

The following lemma can be understood geometrically as putting an upper bound on the number of vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
that can have an obtuse angle between them, two by two. It is a refinement of a result in
[
16
]
, where only part (i) of the Lemma was proven. It appeared in the Supplementary Material of
[
19
]
, we repeat the proof here for completeness.

Lemma 14
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
and
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Then the following holds:

(i) If
\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle\leq 0
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, then
k\leq 2n
.

(ii) If
\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle<0
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, then
k\leq n+1
.

The proof follows from an induction argument, given below. For
n=1
, there is no difference between (i) and (ii). Indeed, one may note that one can always take
v_{1}>0
, by applying a common phase rotation to all
v_{i}
, which does not change the inner products
\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle
between them. Hence
v_{j}<0
for all
j\not=1
. But if
k>2
, then this contradicts the requirement that
v_{2}v_{3}\leq 0
. So
k\leq 2
when
n=1
. For arbitrary
n
, it is clear the upper bound in (i) is reached by taking for example
v_{1}=e_{1}=-v_{2},v_{3}=e_{2}=-v_{4},\dots v_{2n}=-e_{n}
. Some of the
v_{i}
are then orthogonal, so that this set does not satisfy the hypothesis of (ii). When
n=2
, one can understand geometrically why in (ii) the upper bound is only
k=3
and not
k=4
, as in (i). For that purpose, let us reason as if we were working in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, not
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. By applying a rotation to all
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can consider
v_{1}=e_{1}
. Let
v_{j}=(\cos\theta_{j},\sin\theta_{j})
. Since we are in the plane, the hypothesis then implies that the angles between
v_{j}
and
v_{1}
must be larger than
\pi/2
for all
2\leq j\leq k
, so that
\theta_{j}\in]-\pi/2,\pi/2[
. But the hypothesis further implies
|\theta_{j}-\theta_{j^{\prime}}|>\frac{\pi}{2}
, which can only be true if
j
takes at most
2
values so that
k\leq 3
.

Proof (of Lemma
14
)
(i)
The proof goes by induction. We have seen the result holds for
n=1
. Suppose the result holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
. We show it holds for
n+1
. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. As above, we can suppose
v_{1}=a_{1}e_{1}
,
a_{1}>0
. Write
v_{j}=a_{j}e_{1}+w_{j}
, with
\langle w_{j},e_{1}\rangle=0
, for all
j=2,\dots,k
. By hypothesis,
a_{j}\leq 0
for all
2\leq j\leq k
. As a result, for all
2\leq i<j\leq k
,

0\geq\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle=a_{i}a_{j}+\langle w_{i}|w_{j}\rangle.

(37)

Hence, for all
2\leq i<j\leq k
,
\langle w_{i}|w_{j}\rangle\leq 0
.
Note that at most one of the
w_{j}
can vanish. Indeed, if two of them vanish, say
w_{2}=0=w_{3}
, then
a_{2}\not=0\not=a_{3}
and hence
\langle v_{2}|v_{3}\rangle=a_{2}a_{3}>0
, which contradicts the hypothesis. We conclude that among the
(k-1)
vectors
w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, there are at least
(k-2)
that don’t vanish and since their mutual inner products are all non-positive, the induction hypothesis allows to conclude that
k-2\leq 2n
so that
k\leq 2(n+1)
.

(ii) The argument is similar and proceeds again by induction.
This time, by hypothesis, all
a_{j}<0
, for
j=2,\dots,k
: none of the
a_{j}
can vanish. Now suppose one of the
w_{j}
vanishes:
w_{2}=0
. Then, for
j=3,\dots,k
since
\langle v_{1}|v_{j}\rangle=a_{1}a_{j}<0,\langle v_{2}|v_{j}\rangle=a_{2}a_{j}<0
, which is impossible since
a_{1}>0,a_{2}<0
. Consequently, none of the
w_{j},j=2,\dots k
vanishes. We have found therefore
(k-1)
non vanishing vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with a negative inner product. So
k-1\leq n+1
or
k\leq(n+1)+1
. ∎

Theorem
12
provides an improvement on existing results that we now further analyze.
First, as already mentioned, the result was proven in
[
19
]
under the much stricter hypothesis that
Z=0
,
i.e.

m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
. Recall from Proposition
7
that this constitutes a weak incompatibility condition on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
.

In
[
16
]
, on the other hand, the following closely related result was obtained, which imposes no condition on the zeros of
U
:

Theorem 15
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be orthonormal bases in a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
. Suppose

M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}<1
. Then, if
|\psi\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies

n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)=n_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\psi)+n_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>\lfloor 3d/2\rfloor,

(38)

then
|\psi\rangle
is KD nonclassical.

To compare Theorem
12
and Theorem
15
, we first remark that, in the latter result, the hypothesis on
U
is essentially empty, so that it has a very general applicability. Indeed, if
M_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=1
, some of the
|a_{i}\rangle
are equal, up to a phase, to some of the
|b_{j}\rangle
, and then one can split the Hilbert space into a direct sum and study the nonclassicality on the subspace orthogonal to the common basis vectors, as already pointed out above. On the other hand, the conclusion of KD nonclassicality in Theorem
15
is obtained only on states
|\psi\rangle
for which
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>\lfloor 3d/2\rfloor
; this is a more restrictive family of states than those satisfying the bound
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>d+1
required in Theorem
12
as soon as
d\geq 4
and it becomes increasingly restrictive as
d
grows. In fact is easy to show that, when
d=3
, Theorem
12
and Theorem
15
are equivalent. To see this, one needs to remark that in that case, the condition on the zeros of the matrix
U
in Theorem
12
does not actually constitute a restriction.
We refer to Appendix
G
for the details of the argument.

In the high-dimensional examples above, where
d=6k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
, and
U=U_{6}\otimes V
, Theorem
12
guarantees the KD nonclassicality of all states
|\psi\rangle
for which
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>6k+1
, whereas Theorem
15
only concludes this when
n_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}(\psi)>9k
.

8
Conclusion

We have analyzed the Kirkwood-Dirac quasi-probability distributions associated with two observables
A
and
B
, with eigenbases
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
on a finite dimensional Hilbert space of states
{\mathcal{H}}
. We have characterized the Kirkwood-Dirac (non)classical states in terms of their support uncertainty and shown the special role played by the complete incompatibility of the observables in this analysis. In particular, when the observables are both completely incompatible and (close to) mutually unbiased, we have shown that the only Kirkwood-Dirac classical states are the basis vectors of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
.

A number of questions remain open. Some, relating to the precise link between mutual unbiasedness and complete incompatibility, have been suggested in Section
6
. We have also not attempted to establish a hierarchy among the Kirkwood-Dirac nonclassical states, a question of obvious interest. Finally, the extension of our results to mixed states remains a subject for further research.
