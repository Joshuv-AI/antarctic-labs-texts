Bayesian Inference for Multivariate Monotone Densities Thanks: The research was supported in part by NSF Grant number DMS-1916419.
Kang Wang, Subhashis Ghosal

Abstract
We consider a nonparametric Bayesian approach to estimation and testing for a multivariate monotone density. Instead of following the conventional Bayesian route of putting a prior distribution complying with the monotonicity restriction, we put a prior on the step heights through binning and a Dirichlet distribution. An arbitrary piece-wise constant probability density is converted to a monotone one by a projection map, taking its 𝕃 1 \mathbb{L}_{1} -projection onto the space of monotone functions, which is subsequently normalized to integrate to one. We construct consistent Bayesian tests to test multivariate monotonicity of a probability density based on the 𝕃 1 \mathbb{L}_{1} -distance to the class of monotone functions. The test is shown to have a size going to zero and high power against alternatives sufficiently separated from the null hypothesis. To obtain a Bayesian credible interval for the value of the density function at an interior point with guaranteed asymptotic frequentist coverage, we consider a posterior quantile interval of an induced map transforming the function value to its value optimized over certain blocks. The limiting coverage is explicitly calculated and is seen to be higher than the credibility level used in the construction. By exploring the asymptotic relationship between the coverage and the credibility, we show that a desired asymptomatic coverage can be obtained exactly by starting with an appropriate credibility level. Keywords: Bayesian tests for monotonicity; Contraction rate; Credible intervals; Limiting coverage; Multivariate density estimation.

Introduction

The problem of estimating a probability density from observed data has been well-studied theoretically, and the technique has been applied in diverse fields. Usually, density is estimated assuming the smoothness of the function, but in many contexts, structural properties like monotonicity, convexity, or log-concavity occur naturally. Under such a setting, a density function may be estimated accurately without even continuity or smoothness. For both monotone regression and monotone density, it was observed that the limiting coverage of the credible interval based on projection-posterior quantiles is higher than the credibility level, but a predetermined asymptotic coverage level may be assured by starting with a slightly lower appropriate credibility level. They obtained Bernstein-von Mises theorems which, in particular, show that the posterior contracts at the parametric rate and Bayesian credible intervals have the correct asymptotic frequentist coverage. The Bayesian multivariate monotone regression problem was treated by

In the same setting, frequentist coverage of a Bayesian credible interval of the function value at a point using a more general “immersion-posterior” approach was studied by

In this paper, we develop a Bayesian method to make inferences on the joint probability density function of several random variables. Instead of making a global smoothness assumption, we assume that the density function decreases in each argument. We shall pursue the projection-posterior approach, or use a more general immersion map to induce a posterior distribution from an unrestricted posterior distribution supported on piece-wise constant functions on small hyperrectangles. More precisely, we consider the setting where we observe independent and identically distributed random samples from an unknown probability density supported on
[0,1]^{d}
which is monotonically decreasing in each coordinate. To put a prior on the probability density function, we partition
[0,1]^{d}
into hyperrectangles and let the density function have constant values on each hyperrectangle. However, instead of imposing the shape constraints, we first put a Dirichlet distribution on the vector of (normalized) step-heights of the piece-wise representation as the prior. By posterior conjugacy, the corresponding unrestricted posterior distribution also follows a Dirichlet distribution on the unit simplex. Since the unrestricted posterior for the density function is not supported within the space of multivariate monotone densities, we comply with this restriction by applying a transformation on the samples from the unrestricted posterior, which maps a density to a multivariate monotone density.
Depending on the purpose of the inference, we use different transformations. To obtain a global posterior contraction rate with respect to the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric, we use an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-projection of a density function onto the space of monotone functions. For a sampled density from the posterior distribution, the monotone projection results in a nonnegative monotone step function, which need not be a density though. This deficiency is rectified by combining it with a renormalization map. We show that the resulting induced posterior from this immersion map obtained by an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-projection and renormalization contracts at the optimal rate provided that the number of bins is chosen optimally. We then use this result to construct a Bayesian test for multivariate monotonicity of the density. We reject the hypothesis of monotonicity if the posterior probability that the distance between the density following the unrestricted posterior and the space of multivariate monotone functions is smaller than an appropriate threshold given by the posterior contraction rate is smaller than a predetermined level. We show that the resulting Bayesian test has size approaching zero, power at any fixed alternative approaching one, and the last assertion holds even for smooth alternative densities approaching the null hypothesis of multivariate monotone functions if they are separated sufficiently well. We further present a variation of the test such that the required separation with the null hypothesis for the power to approach one adapts to the smoothness of the underlying density function. It may be noted that no test, Bayesian or frequentist, seems available to test for multivariate monotonicity of a density in the literature. Finally, we address the important problem of uncertainty quantification of the function value at a given point. In the Bayesian approach, a credible interval, typically based on posterior quantiles is used to quantify the uncertainty about a parameter, but its frequentist coverage may be significantly different. Bayesian credible intervals with asymptotically adequate frequentist coverage are highly desirable. We obtain the asymptotic coverage of a Bayesian credible interval with endpoints at certain quantiles of an immersion-posterior distribution induced by a min-max, or max-min, block operation. We observe that the asymptotic coverage is slightly higher than the credibility level used in the construction. This is similar to the phenomenon observed for the univariate case in
[
11
]
, except that the immersion map is different from the
\mathbb{L}_{2}
-projection map. As in the univariate case, the limiting coverage depends only on the credibility level used for a given smoothness level of the density function at the point of interest, not on any nuisance parameter. Hence the correct asymptotic coverage can be achieved by starting with an appropriate lower credibility level.

J\asymp n^{1/(2+d)}
used to construct the test leads to the suboptimal contraction rate
n^{-\alpha/(2+d)}
. However, the assumed choice is essential to obtain the optimal posterior contraction rate at multivariate monotone functions since otherwise, at some monotone true density, the posterior will not concentrate within its
M_{n}n^{-1/(2+d)}
-neighborhood, and hence the size of the Bayesian test will tend to one. The problem can be rectified by also putting an appropriate prior on
J
but at the expense of higher computational complexity. Therefore, fixed
J
can not give rise to the optimal separation rate for some classes of smooth functions, but with a random
J
, the required separation rate adapts optimally with
\alpha
within a logarithmic factor.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
J
be endowed with a prior
\pi
such that

e^{-b_{1}J^{d}\log J}\leq\pi(J)\leq e^{-b_{2}J^{d}\log J}

for some
b_{1}
and
b_{2}>0
, and suppose that
a_{2}n^{-a_{3}}\leq\alpha_{\bm{j}}\leq a_{1}
, for some constants
a_{1},a_{2}
, and
a_{3}>0
. Consider the test
\phi_{n}=\mathbbm{1}\{\Pi(d_{1}(g,\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq M_{0}\sqrt{(J^{d}\log n)/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})<\gamma\}
for testing the hypothesis of multivariate monotonicity of
g
, where
M_{0}
is a sufficiently large constant and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is predetermined.
Then

(i)

for fixed
g_{0}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}
and
g_{0}
bounded away from zero,
\mathrm{E}_{0}(\phi_{n})\to 0
;

(ii)

for fixed density
g_{0}
bounded away from zero and not belonging to the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-closure of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
,
\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n})\to 0
;

(iii)

there exists a sufficiently large constant
C>0
depending only on
\alpha
,
L
, and
d
, such that
\sup\{\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n}):g_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\alpha,L),g_{0}\geq l>0,d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{G}^{*})>C(n/\log n)^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}\}\to 0
.

4
Coverage of pointwise credible intervals

To obtain a Bayesian credible interval for
g(\bm{x}_{0})
at an interior point
\bm{x}_{0}\in(0,1)^{d}
with an asserted frequentist coverage, we use quantiles of immersion posteriors induced by the immersion maps via the max-min (or min-max) operation over blocks containing
\bm{x}_{0}
. The immersion map is partly inspired by the operation used in the estimator of the monotone function value at a point proposed by
[
18
]
. The asymptotic distribution of such block estimators was studied by
[
27
]
for the multivariate monotone regression problem. We drew on their method in the proof of the following theorem.

Throughout this section,
\bm{J}
is taken as deterministic and changing with the sample size. We allow
J_{k}
to take different values adapting to the different local smoothness levels along each coordinate.
Let
\bm{j}_{0}=\left\lceil\bm{x}_{0}\circ\bm{J}\right\rceil
so that
\bm{x}_{0}\in I_{\bm{j}_{0}}
. Since
\bm{x}_{0}
is arbitrary so far, we can define an immersion map
\iota
by the value of
\iota(g)
at
\bm{x}_{0}
for any
g=(\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k})\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}}

with
\bm{\theta}
from the the unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k}}
. Specifically, we consider the min-max immersion map
\overline{\iota}
defined by
\overline{\iota}(g)(\bm{x}_{0})=\theta_{\bm{j}_{0}}^{\star}
, where

\displaystyle\theta_{\bm{j}_{0}}^{\star}=\min_{\bm{j}_{1}\preceq\bm{j}_{0}}\max_{\bm{j}_{0}\preceq\bm{j}_{2}}\frac{\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{j}_{1}:\bm{j}_{2}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(j_{2,k}-j_{1,k}+1)}.

(4.1)

It is clear that
\theta_{\bm{j}_{0}}^{\star}
is uniquely defined although there can be multiple pairs of
\bm{j}_{2}
and
\bm{j}_{1}
maximizing and minimizing the average of
{\theta_{\bm{j}}}
in the last display.
It is not hard to see that
\bm{\theta}^{\star}:=(\theta_{\bm{j}}^{\star}:\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}])\in\mathcal{C}_{\bm{J}}
.
If we switch the order of maximization and minimization in the transformation in (
4.1
), we obtain the max-min immersion map
\underline{\iota}
defined by
\underline{\iota}(g)(\bm{x}_{0})=\theta_{\bm{j}_{0}}^{\dagger}
, where

\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}_{\bm{j}}=\max_{\bm{j}\preceq\bm{j}_{2}}\min_{\bm{j}_{1}\preceq\bm{j}}\frac{\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{j}_{1}:\bm{j}_{2}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(j_{2,k}-j_{1,k}+1)}.

(4.2)

It again follows that
\bm{\theta}^{\dagger}:=(\theta_{\bm{j}}^{\star}:\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}])\in\mathcal{C}_{\bm{J}}
,
but
\bm{\theta}^{\dagger}
is possibly different from
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. As both operations result in monotone outcomes, the average immersion map
(\overline{\iota}+\underline{\iota})/2
will also map piece-wise constant functions to monotone piece-wise constant functions. For a given
\bm{x}_{0}
, we shall obtain asymptotic frequentist coverage of Bayesian credible intervals of
g(\bm{x}_{0})
based on the quantiles of its immersion posterior distribution using the immersion maps
\overline{\iota}
,
\underline{\iota}
and
(\overline{\iota}+\underline{\iota})/2
.

We shall make the following assumptions on the prior and the true probability density function.

Assumption 1
.

The parameters of the prior distribution satisfy
\max_{\bm{j}}\alpha_{\bm{j}}\leq a_{1}
for some positive constant
a_{1}
.

Assumption 2
.

The true density
g_{0}
is a coordinate-wise nonincreasing function on
[0,1]^{d}
.
g_{0}
is continuously differentiable in a small neighborhood of
\bm{x}_{0}\in(0,1)^{d}
. For every
1\leq k\leq d
, there exists some
\eta_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
\partial_{k}^{m_{k}}g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})=0
for
m_{k}=1,\ldots,\eta_{k}-1
and
\partial_{k}^{\eta_{k}}g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})\neq 0
, and
for any
t>0
, with
M_{0}=\{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}/\eta_{k}\leq 1\}
,

\displaystyle\sup\big\{\big|g_{0}(\bm{x})-\sum_{\bm{m}\in M_{0}}\frac{\partial^{\bm{m}}g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})}{\bm{m}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})^{\bm{m}}\big|:|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}|\preceq t\bm{r}_{n}\big\}=o(\omega_{n}),

where

\omega_{n}\downarrow 0
and
\bm{r}_{n}=(\omega_{n}^{1/\eta_{1}},\ldots,\omega_{n}^{1/\eta_{d}})^{\mathrm{T}}
.

Assumption
2
is adapted from
[
27
]
and some unique features, essential for the proof, of multivariate monotone functions follow from this assumption, see Lemma 1 of
[
27
]
.
Assumption
2
holds generally when
g_{0}
has smoothness of order
\max\{\eta_{k}:1\leq k\leq d\}
at
\bm{x}_{0}
.

To state the theorems on limiting coverage, we introduce some stochastic processes. Let
H_{1}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
H_{2}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
be two independent centered Gaussian processes
indexed by
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}

with the covariance structure

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(H_{i}(\bm{u},\bm{v}),H_{i}(\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime}))=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(u_{k}\wedge u^{\prime}_{k}+v_{k}\wedge v^{\prime}_{k}),

for
(\bm{u},\bm{v}),(\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
and
i=1,2
. Then define a Gaussian process by the relation

\displaystyle U(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\frac{\sqrt{g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})}H_{1}(\bm{u},\bm{v})}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(u_{k}+v_{k})}+\frac{\sqrt{g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})}H_{2}(\bm{u},\bm{v})}{\prod_{k=1}^{d}(u_{k}+v_{k})}+\sum_{\bm{m}\in M}\frac{\partial_{k}^{m_{k}}g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})}{(\bm{m}+\bm{1})!}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{v_{k}^{m_{k}+1}-(-u)^{m_{k}}}{u_{k}+v_{k}},

where
M=\{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}/\eta_{k}=1\}
. For each of the three immersion maps, let the images be denoted by
g^{\star}=\overline{\iota}(g)
,
g^{\dagger}=\underline{\iota}(g)
and
\bar{g}=((\overline{\iota}+\underline{\iota})/2)(g)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and

\omega_{n}=n^{-1/(2+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\eta_{k}^{-1})}
and

\bm{r}_{n}=(\omega_{n}^{1/\eta_{1}},\ldots,\omega_{n}^{1/\eta_{d}})^{{\mathrm{T}}}
. If
J_{k}\gg r_{n,k}^{-1}
and
\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k}\ll n\omega_{n}
, then for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi(\omega_{n}^{-1}(g^{\star}(\bm{x}_{0})-g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0}))\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\rightsquigarrow\mathrm{P}\big(\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq z|H_{1}\big),

\displaystyle\Pi(\omega_{n}^{-1}(g^{\dagger}(\bm{x}_{0})-g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0}))\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\rightsquigarrow\mathrm{P}\big(\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq z|H_{1}\big),

\displaystyle\Pi(\omega_{n}^{-1}(\bar{g}(\bm{x}_{0})-g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0}))\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\rightsquigarrow\mathrm{P}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})+\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\leq z|H_{1}\big).

The corresponding immersion-posterior
(1-\gamma)
-quantiles are

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{n,\gamma}=\inf\{z:\Pi(g^{\star}(\bm{x}_{0})\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\geq 1-\gamma\},

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{n,\gamma}=\inf\{z:\Pi(g^{\dagger}(\bm{x}_{0})\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\geq 1-\gamma\},

\displaystyle Q^{(3)}_{n,\gamma}=\inf\{z:\Pi(\bar{g}(\bm{x}_{0})\leq z|\mathbb{D}_{n})\geq 1-\gamma\}.

Theorem
4.1
gives the coverage of the corresponding immersion-posterior credible intervals in terms of the distributions of the following random variables:

\displaystyle Z_{B}^{(1)}=\mathrm{P}(\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq 0|H_{1}),

\displaystyle Z_{B}^{(2)}=\mathrm{P}(\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})\leq 0|H_{1}),

\displaystyle Z_{B}^{(3)}=\mathrm{P}(\tfrac{1}{2}(\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v})\sup_{\bm{v}\succeq\bm{0}}\inf_{\bm{u}\succeq\bm{0}}U(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\leq 0|H_{1}).

Corollary 4.2
.

The asymptotic coverage of one-sided immersion-posterior credible intervals is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{0}(g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})\leq Q^{(l)}_{n,\gamma})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{P}(Z_{B}^{(l)}\leq 1-\gamma),

(4.3)

and that of two-sided immersion-posterior credible intervals are

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{0}(g_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})\in[Q^{(l)}_{n,(1-\gamma)/2},Q^{(l)}_{n,\gamma/2}])

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{P}(\gamma/2\leq Z_{B}^{(l)}\leq 1-\gamma/2),

(4.4)

for
l=1,2,3
.

Thus the coverage depends only on the distributions of
Z_{B}^{(l)}
,
l=1,2,3
, which do not involve any model parameters. The distribution functions of
Z_{B}^{(l)}
at a set of most commonly used points under several local smoothness levels of
g_{0}
at
\bm{x}_{0}
were tabulated in
[
39
]
. From their tables, we can conclude that if
\bm{\eta}=(1,1)
, the asymptotic coverage of the three
95\%
one-sided credible intervals considered here are
96.6\%
,
97.5\%
, and
96.8\%
, respectively. To target a specific asymptotic frequentist coverage,
[
39
]
tabulated values of the inverted distribution functions of
Z_{B}^{(1)}
,
Z_{B}^{(2)}
and
Z_{B}^{(3)}
at several points and back-calculated the credibility level necessary for certain standard confidence levels. For example, if we use the symmetrized immersion-posterior quantile intervals with the targeted coverage of
95\%
, the required two endpoints are
Q^{(3)}_{n,0.959}
and
Q^{(3)}_{n,0.041}
, resulting in a shorter interval than the nominal
95\%
credible interval.

In addition to evaluating the proposed methods when the sample size is finite, we also compare the

-metric between the immersion posterior samples and the true data generating density function and the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric between the unrestricted posterior samples and the true data generating density function in the simulation studies.

For each data set, we generate
1000
posterior samples and calculate

\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric to the true probability density. We repeat this process for

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

For part (i), let
\bm{x}\in I_{\bm{j}}
. Then, as
h
is constant over
I_{\bm{j}}
, by the definition of
s_{\bm{J}}

\displaystyle\left|s_{\bm{J}}(\bm{x})-h(\bm{x})\right|^{p}=\big|\big(\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k}\big)\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}(s-h)\big|^{p}\leq\big(\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k}\big)\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|s-h|^{p},

(6.1)

by Jensen’s inequality. By taking integral on both sides of (
6.1
) over
I_{\bm{j}}
, we have
\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|s_{\bm{J}}-h|^{p}\leq\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|s-h|^{p}
. Part (i) now follows then by summing over all
\bm{j}
on both sides of the inequality.

For parts (ii) and (iii), if
s
is a multivariate monotone nonincreasing function, then
\{b_{j}\}
is a monotonically decreasing array in terms of the natural partial order on the indices since, by the translation of
s
,

\displaystyle b_{\bm{j}_{1}}=\int_{I_{\bm{j}_{1}}}s(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\geq\int_{I_{\bm{j}_{1}}}s(\bm{x}+(\bm{j}_{2}-\bm{j}_{1})/\bm{J})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{I_{\bm{j}_{2}}}s(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=b_{\bm{j}_{2}},

when
\bm{j}_{2}\succeq\bm{j}_{1}
. Additionally, it is clear that if
s\geq 0
,
b_{\bm{j}}\geq 0
for all
\bm{j}
, and
\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}(\prod_{k=1}^{d}J_{k})b_{\bm{j}}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}s
.
∎

The main ideas of the proofs of Theorems
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
are very similar to their univariate counterpart in
[
11
]
, but the associated bounds will have to be reestablished in the multidimensional case. For the sake of self-contentedness, we present the proofs in brief.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\tilde{g}
be an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-projection of
g
to
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, from which we obtain the normalized
g^{\ast}
. Since

\|g^{\ast}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|\tilde{g}-g_{0}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{g}-g^{\ast}\|_{1}
,
and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{g}-g^{\ast}\|_{1}=\int\big|\tilde{g}-\frac{\tilde{g}}{\int\tilde{g}}\big|=\int\tilde{g}\big|1-\frac{1}{\int\tilde{g}}\big|=\big|\int\tilde{g}-\int g_{0}\big|\leq\|\tilde{g}-g_{0}\|_{1},

we have
\|g^{\ast}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq 2\|\tilde{g}-g_{0}\|_{1}
. Moreover,
\|\tilde{g}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|\tilde{g}-g\|_{1}+\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq 2\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}
by the triangle inequality and the definition of projection. Combining
\|g^{\ast}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq 4\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}
, hence the immersion posterior contraction rate is inherited from the unrestricted posterior contraction rate.

Let
g_{0,J}=J^{d}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}}
, where
\theta_{0,\bm{j}}=\mathrm{P}_{0}(I_{\bm{j}})
.
For every
\bm{j}
and
\bm{x}\in I_{\bm{j}}
,
|g_{0,J}(\bm{x})-g_{0}(\bm{x})|=|J^{d}\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}g_{0}-g_{0}(\bm{x})|\leq g_{0}(({\bm{j}}-\bm{1})/J)-g_{0}({\bm{j}}/J)
as
g_{0}
is coordinate-wise decreasing. Then, splitting the integral over each
I_{\bm{j}}
,

\displaystyle\|g_{0,J}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq J^{-d}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}[g_{0}(({\bm{j}}-\bm{1})/J)-g_{0}({\bm{j}}/J)]\leq dJ^{-1}(g_{0}(\bm{0})-g_{0}(\bm{1})),

by the telescoping property of the series corresponding to the points
\{\ldots,\bm{j},\bm{j}+\bm{1},\bm{j}+2\cdot\bm{1},\ldots\}
, and there are no more than
dJ^{d-1}
such series in the above summation.

Since
\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{1}+\|g_{0,J}-g_{0}\|_{1}
, we only have to show that for every
M_{n}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{1}\geq M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to 0.

(6.2)

As the
\mathbb{L}_{2}
-norm dominates the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-norm, it suffices to show that
\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{2}\geq M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to 0
. Observing that

\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{2}^{2}=J^{d}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}|\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}|^{2}
, it is enough to verify the bounds

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\mathrm{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})=O_{P}(n^{-1}),\quad\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}|\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}|^{2}=O_{P}(n^{-1})

in view of the Markov inequality and a standard variance-bias decomposition. Since
\text{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\leq(\alpha_{\bm{j}}+N_{\bm{j}})/(\alpha_{\cdot}+n)^{2}
, the first part of the assertion is immediately obtained as
\alpha_{\bm{j}}\leq a_{1}<\infty
,
J^{d}\leq n
and
\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}=n
. For the second claim, note that
\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})=(\alpha_{\bm{j}}+N_{\bm{j}})/(\alpha_{\cdot}+n)
and
N_{\bm{j}}\sim\text{Bin}(n,\theta_{0,\bm{j}})
, where
\alpha_{\cdot}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\alpha_{\bm{j}}\leq J^{d}a_{1}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n}))

\displaystyle=n\theta_{0,\bm{j}}(1-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})/(\alpha_{0}+n)^{2}\leq\theta_{0,\bm{j}}/n\leq J^{-d}g_{0}(\bm{0})/n,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{E}_{0}(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n}))-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{\bm{j}}-\alpha_{0}\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}/(\alpha_{0}+n)^{2}\leq 2a_{1}^{2}(1+g_{0}^{2}(\bm{0}))/n^{2},

as
\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\leq J^{-d}g_{0}(\bm{0})
. Now summing over
\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]
, we obtain the result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

(i) Since
d_{1}(g,\mathcal{F})=\|g-\tilde{g}\|_{1}\leq\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}
, the first assertion follows from Theorem
3.1
.

(ii) We claim that
d_{1}(g_{0},{\mathcal{F}}^{*})>0
. If not, for every
\eta>0
, there exists
f\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
such that
\|f-g_{0}\|\leq\eta/2
. Then
f_{+}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
and
\|f_{+}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|f-g_{0}\|_{1}
, where
f_{+}
is the positive part of
f
. Further,
\bar{f}=f_{+}/\|f_{+}\|_{1}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}
and, since
\bar{f}-f_{+}=f_{+}(1-\|f_{+}\|_{1})/\|f_{+}\|_{1}
and
\|g_{0}\|_{1}=1
,

\displaystyle\|\bar{f}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\|\bar{f}-f_{+}\|_{1}+\|f_{+}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\left|\|g_{0}\|_{1}-\|f_{+}\|_{1}\right|+\|f_{+}-g_{0}\|_{1}

is bounded by
2\|f_{+}-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq 2\|f-g_{0}\|_{1}\leq\eta
, contradicting the assumption that
g_{0}
does not belong to the closure of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
. Thus
d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{F}^{*})>0
. Hence by the triangle inequality and the definition of the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-projection,

\displaystyle d_{1}(g,\mathcal{F}^{*})\geq\|g_{0}-\tilde{g}\|_{1}-\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}\geq d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{F}^{*})-\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}.

(6.3)

To conclude the consistency of the test, it now suffices to show that the posterior distribution of
g
is consistent at
g_{0}
with respect to the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric. By the martingale convergence theorem, we have
\|g_{0}-g_{0,J}\|_{1}\to 0
as
J\to\infty
. Observing (
6.2
) does not require monotonicity, posterior consistency at
g_{0}
follows.

(iii) Uniformly over
g_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\alpha,L)
, we have
\|g_{0}-g_{0,J}\|_{1}\lesssim J^{-\alpha}
. Combined with (
6.2
), this gives the posterior contraction rate
\max\{J^{-\alpha},\sqrt{J^{d}/n}\}
at
g_{0}
, which is optimized to
n^{-\alpha/(2+d)}
for the choice
J\asymp n^{1/(2+d)}
. As in the proof of part (ii), we can conclude that
d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{F}^{*})\geq\rho_{n}(\alpha)/2
if
d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{G}^{*})>\rho_{n}(\alpha)
. By (
6.3
),

\Pi(d_{1}(g,\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq M_{n}n^{-1/(2+d)}|\mathbb{D}_{n})
is bounded by

\Pi(\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}\geq d_{1}(g_{0},\mathcal{F}^{*})-M_{n}n^{-1/(2+d)}|\mathbb{D}_{n})
.
When
\alpha<1
,
n^{-\alpha/(d+2)}\gg M_{n}n^{-1/(d+2)}
and hence the bound goes to
0
in
\mathrm{P}_{0}
-probability with the choice of
\rho_{n}(\alpha)=Cn^{-\alpha/(2+d)}
for any fixed
C>0
. When
\alpha=1
, the bound is at most
\Pi(\|g-g_{0}\|_{1}\geq(C/2-1)M_{n}n^{-1/(2+d)}|\mathbb{D}_{n})
, which converges to zero in
\mathrm{P}_{0}
-probability.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

(i) By the definition of the piece-wise constant approximation,
g_{0,J}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}\subset\mathcal{F}^{*}
if
g_{0}\in\mathcal{G}^{*}
. Then we have
d_{1}(g,\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{1}
for every
J>0
.
Then for part (i), we assert that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{0}(\Pi(\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{1}>M_{0}\sqrt{(J^{d}\log n)/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\leq 1-\gamma)\to 1.

(6.4)

To this end, we shall show that for
J_{n}^{d}\lesssim n
,

(a)

\Pi(\|g-g_{0,J}\|_{1}>M_{0}\sqrt{J^{d}\log n/n},J\leq J_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to_{\mathrm{P}_{0}}0
;

(b)

\Pi(J>J_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to_{\mathrm{P}_{0}}0
for
J_{n}\asymp(n/\log n)^{1/(d+1)}
.

As the
\mathbb{L}_{2}
-metric dominates the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric on
[0,1]^{d}
,
the posterior probability in (a) is bounded by

\sum_{J=1}^{J_{n}}A_{n,J}(M_{0})\pi(J|\mathbb{D}_{n})
,
where
A_{n,J}(M_{0})=\Pi(\int|g-g_{0,J}|^{2}>M_{0}^{2}(J^{d}\log n)/n|J,\mathbb{D}_{n})
.
By Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle A_{n,J}(M_{0})\leq\frac{n}{M_{0}^{2}J^{d}\log n}\big[\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\mathrm{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})+\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}\big].

(6.5)

Using the bounds established in the proof of (
6.2
), the first term inside the bracket is
O(n^{-1})
uniformly for all
J
, while we can decompose
\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}
as the sum of
(\alpha_{\bm{j}}-\alpha_{\cdot}N_{\bm{j}}/n)/(\alpha_{\cdot}+n)
and
N_{\bm{j}}/n-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}
with
\alpha_{\cdot}:=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\alpha_{\bm{j}}\leq a_{1}J^{d}
. The sum of squares of the first term over
\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]
is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{2\big[\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\alpha_{\bm{j}}^{2}+\alpha_{\cdot}^{2}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}^{2}/n^{2}\big]}{(\alpha_{\cdot}+n)^{2}}\leq\frac{2[a_{1}^{2}J^{d}+(a_{1}J^{d})^{2}n\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}/n^{2}]}{(\alpha_{\cdot}+n)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{J^{d}}{n}

uniformly for all
\bm{j}
and all
J
.
Using Bennett’s inequality (cf. Proposition A.6.2 of
[
38
]
) and summing over
\bm{j}
, we conclude that simultaneously for all
J\leq J_{n}
,

\max\{|N_{j}/n-\theta_{0j}|:\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]\}\lesssim\sqrt{(\log n)/(nJ^{d})}

if
J_{n}^{d}\log n\lesssim n
. This gives that the expression in (
6.5
) is bounded by a constant multiple of
M_{0}^{-2}
, and hence can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
M_{0}
sufficiently large.
Thus, claim (a) follows.

To establish (b), we apply the general theory of posterior contraction in
[
20
]
. Because
g_{0}
is bounded below, any
g
sufficiently uniformly close is also bounded below, and so
g_{0}/g
is uniformly bounded. Using standard relations between Kullback-Leibler divergences, the Hellinger distance and the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-distance, and recalling that the piece-wise constant approximation
g_{0,J}
is
O(J^{-1})
close to
g_{0}
in
\mathbb{L}_{1}
, to estimate the prior
\epsilon_{n}
-ball probability necessary for the application of the general theory, it suffices to lower bound
\Pi(J=J_{n}^{*})\Pi(\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}_{n}^{*}]}|\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}|\leq\epsilon_{n})
, where
J_{n}^{*}\asymp\epsilon_{n}^{-1}
. By the assumption on the prior on
J
and the Dirichlet small ball probability estimate (cf. Lemma G.13 of
[
20
]
), a lower bound is
e^{-c(J_{n}^{*})^{d}\log n}
for some
c>0
provided that
\epsilon_{n}
is lower bounded by some negative power of
n
. Thus (b) holds if
J_{n}
is chosen a sufficiently large constant multiple of
J_{n}^{*}
by Theorem 8.20 of
[
20
]
. Now, with these estimates, proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, the conclusion in (i) can be established.

For part (ii), in view of the martingale convergence theorem, for any given
\epsilon>0
,
\|g_{0}-g_{0,J}\|_{1}<\epsilon
when
J\geq J_{0}
, say. Then we proceed as in part (i) with a fixed small
\epsilon
,
J_{n}^{*}=J_{0}
and establish that (a) and (b) hold for
J_{n}
a multiple of
(n/\log n)^{1/d}
, where the constant of proportionality is taken to be sufficiently small depending on
\epsilon
.

The proof of part (iii) also proceeds similarly after establishing the posterior contraction rate
\epsilon_{n}\asymp(n/\log n)^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}
by standard arguments for Hölder functions. The estimates used in the proof imply that (a) and (b) hold for
J_{n}
a sufficiently large constant multiple of
(n/\log n)^{1/(2\alpha+d)}
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is long, so we separate it into several key steps. These are stated as separate lemmas below, under the setup and assumptions of Theorem

. We first introduce some notations and preliminaries.

For
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

1\leq k\leq d\end{subarray}}H(\bm{0},\bm{v})\leq 0\Big)\leq\mathrm{P}\Big(\max_{0\leq v_{d}\leq 1}H(\bm{0},(1,\ldots,1,v_{d}))\leq 0\Big)=0

by the fact that

\mathrm{P}\Big(\max_{0\leq v_{d}\leq 1}H(\bm{0},(1,\ldots,1,v_{d}))\leq\rho\Big)=1-2\mathrm{P}(H(\bm{0},\bm{1})\geq\rho),

and the continuity of normal distribution.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

If
U\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\Delta,1)
, then for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

\mathrm{E}(U-\mathrm{E}(U))^{2m}\lesssim\Delta^{m}
as
\Delta\to\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose
n_{\Delta}\leq\Delta<n_{\Delta}+1
for some integer
n_{\Delta}
. We will assume that
\Delta>n_{\Delta}
in this proof. If
\Delta
is an integer, the argument can proceed in a similar and simpler way.
Let
Z_{i}
denote independent random variables with standard exponential distribution for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\Delta}
. Let
Z_{n_{\Delta}+1}\sim\text{Gamma}(\Delta-n_{\Delta},1)
. Then
U
is equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}Z_{i}
in distribution. By Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality, there exists a constant
C_{m}>0
depending only on
m
such that,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(U-\mathrm{E}(U))^{2m}=\mathrm{E}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}(Z_{i}-\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}))\big]^{2m}\leq C_{m}\mathrm{E}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}(Z_{i}-\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}))^{2}\big]^{m}.

(A.2)

By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\big[\frac{1}{n_{\Delta}+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}(Z_{i}-\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}))^{2}\big]^{m}\leq\frac{1}{n_{\Delta}+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}(Z_{i}-\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}))^{2m}.

The the right-hand side of (
A.2
) is bounded by
(n_{\Delta}+1)^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\Delta}+1}\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}-\mathrm{E}(Z_{i}))^{2m}
. For fixed
m
, the lemma follows when
\Delta\to\infty
.
∎
