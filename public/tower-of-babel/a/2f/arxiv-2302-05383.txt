Discovering Sparse Representations of Lie Groups with Machine Learning
Roy T. Forestano, Konstantin T. Matchev, Katia Matcheva, Alexander Roman, Eyup B. Unlu, Sarunas Verner

Abstract
Recent work has used deep learning to derive symmetry transformations, which preserve conserved quantities, and to obtain the corresponding algebras of generators. In this letter, we extend this technique to derive sparse representations of arbitrary Lie algebras. We show that our method reproduces the canonical (sparse) representations of the generators of the Lorentz group, as well as the U ⁡ ( n ) U(n) and S ​ U ​ ( n ) SU(n) families of Lie groups. This approach is completely general and can be used to find the infinitesimal generators for any Lie group.



I
Introduction

The transformative advances in theoretical physics at the turn of the 20
th
century came with the realization that symmetry is a primary feature of nature that constrains the allowable dynamical laws
Gross 1996
. By Noether’s theorem
Noether 1918
, the presence of a continuous symmetry in the problem implies a conservation law which is universally applicable and indispensable in understanding the system’s behavior and evolution. Symmetries can be found at all scales — from the microscopic description of subatomic particles in the Standard Model to the large-scale structure of the Universe. In particle physics, symmetries provide an organizing principle behind the observed particle zoo and its interactions, and guide model-builders in the search for viable extensions of the Standard Model
Csáki et al. 2018
.

The formal study of symmetries has historically been done within the framework of group theory
Wigner et al. 1959
. While virtually all types of classical Lie groups find some applications in various physics subfields, the most commonly encountered Lie groups in particle physics are the special orthogonal groups
SO(n)
and the special unitary groups
SU(n)
. In particular, the rotation group
SO(3)
reflects the isotropy of space and all fundamental laws of motion, the Lorentz group
SO(1,3)
is the symmetry group of spacetime in special relativity, and
SU(2)
and
SU(3)
are the Standard Model gauge groups responsible for the weak and strong interactions, respectively. An advanced course in group theory is a necessary tool in the arsenal of the aspiring physics graduate student.

Recently there has been increased interest in the application of machine learning (ML) for the discovery and identification of symmetries in data
Iten et al. 2020
;
Wetzel et al. 2020
;
Krippendorf and Syvaeri 2020
;
Liu and Tegmark 2021
;
Barenboim et al. 2021
;
Dillon et al. 2022
;
Liu and Tegmark 2022
;
Desai et al. 2022
;
Craven et al. 2022
;
Moskalev et al. 2022
;
Forestano et al. 2023
;
Roman et al. 2023
. Relevant applications of ML to group theory include computing tensor products and branching rules of irreducible representations of Lie groups
Chen et al. 2021
, and testing and/or deriving Lie group generators of a symmetry present in the data
Liu and Tegmark 2022
;
Moskalev et al. 2022
;
Forestano et al. 2023
;
Roman et al. 2023
. In this letter we extend the method of
Forestano et al. 2023
;
Roman et al. 2023
to derive
sparse
representations of arbitrary Lie algebras. We shall demonstrate that, with the appropriate loss function modifications discussed in Section
II
, one can use machine learning to derive from first principles the canonical (sparse) form of the generators for the Lorentz group (Section
III
), as well as for the
U(n)
and
SU(n)
families (Section
IV
). As emphasized in the summary Section
V
, the described method is completely general and can be applied to
any
other classical Lie group.

II
Method

We begin by briefly summarizing the symmetry finding procedure outlined in Ref.
Forestano et al. 2023
. The classical groups are the linear groups of transformations over the reals
\mathbb{R}
, the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, and quaternions
\mathbb{H}
. Therefore, a symmetry transformation acts on a feature vector
{\mathbf{x}}\equiv\{x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\ldots,x^{(n)}\}
, where
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
in our
SO(n)
and
SU(n)
examples, respectively. In order to capture the effect of a group transformation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
or
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we consider a representative set of
m
points
\left\{\mathbf{x}\right\}\equiv\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\right\}
sampled from some finite domain. The choice of a sampling distribution and/or the size and location of the domain is inconsequential. For definiteness we use a standard normal distribution with
m=100
.

The classical groups are defined in terms of polynomial invariants over their respective fields. For example,
O(n)
preserves the values of the polynomial oracle

\varphi_{O}(\mathbf{x})\;\equiv\;|\mathbf{x}|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[x^{(j)}]^{2},\quad x^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

the Lorentz group in
n=4
dimensions preserves

\varphi_{L}(\mathbf{x})\;\equiv\;\bigl(x^{(1)}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(x^{(2)}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(x^{(3)}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(x^{(4)}\bigr)^{2},\quad x^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R},

(2)

while
U(n)
preserves

\varphi_{U}(\mathbf{x})\;\equiv\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(x^{(j)}\bigl)^{\ast}x^{(j)},\quad x^{(j)}\in\mathbb{C}.

(3)

A symmetry transformation
\mathbf{f}
is a map
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
which preserves the respective oracle
(
1
-
3
) everywhere, or in our case, for each of the sampled
m
points:

\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i})\;\equiv\;\varphi(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))=\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\quad\forall i\,=\,1,2,\ldots,m\,.

(4)

In order to focus on the
generators
of the group of symmetry transformations,
\mathbf{f}
is linearized by considering infinitesimal transformations
\delta{\mathbf{f}}
in the vicinity of the identity transformation
\mathbb{I}
:

\delta{\mathbf{f}}\;\equiv\;\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon\,{\mathbb{G}}\,,

(5)

where
\varepsilon
is an infinitesimal parameter and
{\mathbb{G}}
is a
n\times n
matrix. Upon training with a suitable loss function
L({\mathbb{G}},\{\mathbf{x}\})
, the components of
{\mathbb{G}}
are driven to their
trained
values, thus producing one symmetry generator
Forestano et al. 2023

\mathbb{J}\;\equiv\;\argmin_{\mathbb{G}}\Bigl(L({\mathbb{G}},\{\mathbf{x}\})\Bigr).

(6)

By repeating the above procedure
N_{g}
times under different initial conditions
{\mathbb{G}}_{0}
for the candidate generator components, or for different random seeds and hyperparameters, one obtains a whole set of
N_{g}
generators
\mathbb{J}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,2,\ldots,N_{g}
. The loss function is chosen to ensure that the obtained generators have the following properties:

1.

Invariance
, i.e., preserving the oracle values for all sampled datapoints
\left\{\mathbf{x}\right\}
under the set
\{\mathbb{G}\}
of all candidate transformations
\mathbb{G}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,2,\ldots,N_{g}
:

L_{\text{inv}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\},\{\mathbf{x}\}\bigl)=\frac{1}{m\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Bigl[\varphi\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\varepsilon{\mathbb{G}_{\alpha}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigr)-\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i})\Bigr]^{2}\,,

(7)

where “
\cdot
” denotes ordinary tensor multiplication.

2.

Orthogonality
, which guarantees that the obtained generators are distinct:

L_{\text{ortho}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\}\bigr)=\Bigl[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{g}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=\alpha+1\end{subarray}}^{N_{g}}\text{Tr}\bigl({\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\mathbb{G}}_{\beta}^{T}\bigr)\Bigr]^{2}.

(8)

3.

Normalization
, ensuring that the transformation is not trivial:

L_{\text{norm}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\}\bigr)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{g}}\Bigl[\text{Tr}\Bigl({\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}\cdot{\mathbb{G}}^{T}_{\alpha}\Bigr)-2\Bigr]^{2}.

(9)

4.

Closure of the algebra
. This tests whether the set of candidate generators
\{\mathbb{G}\}
forms a closed algebra

\bigl[\mathbb{G}_{\alpha},\mathbb{G}_{\beta}\bigr]=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{N_{g}}a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\mathbb{G}_{\gamma}

with some structure constants
a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}
:

L_{\text{closure}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\},a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\bigr)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{g}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta=\alpha+1\end{subarray}}^{N_{g}}\Tr\left(\mathbb{C}_{\alpha\beta}\cdot\mathbb{C}_{\alpha\beta}^{T}\right),

(10)

where the closure mismatch is defined by

\mathbb{C}_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\},a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\bigr)\equiv\bigl[\mathbb{G}_{\alpha},\mathbb{G}_{\beta}\bigr]-\sum_{\gamma=1}^{N_{g}}a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\mathbb{G}_{\gamma}.

(11)

The loss function in
Forestano et al. 2023
was the sum of those four terms with suitable weight factors (hyperparameters)
h_{\text{ortho}}
,
h_{\text{norm}}
and
h_{\text{closure}}
:

L_{4}=L_{\text{inv}}+h_{\text{ortho}}\,L_{\text{ortho}}+h_{\text{norm}}\,L_{\text{norm}}+h_{\text{closure}}\,L_{\text{closure}}.

(12)

The analysis in
Forestano et al. 2023
demonstrated that this method is capable of learning the symmetry groups (and their subgroups) generated by the oracles (
1
) and (
2
) without any prior assumptions, in a fully agnostic fashion. Since the method is completely general, however, the representations learned in
Forestano et al. 2023
were not conveniently aligned with the axes in the feature space, thus differing from the nice conventional results found in the textbooks. To rectify this problem, here we introduce an additional loss term designed to encourage sparsity:

L_{\text{sparsity}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\}\bigr)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{g}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}^{n}\left|{\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}^{(jk)}{\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}^{(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}\right|\Bigl(1-\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\Bigr),

(13)

where
{\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}^{(jk)}
denotes the
jk
-component of
\mathbb{G}_{\alpha}
.
The full loss function is therefore

L\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\},a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\bigr)=L_{4}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\},a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\bigr)+h_{\text{sparsity}}\,L_{\text{sparsity}}\bigl(\{\mathbb{G}\}\bigr)

(14)

and the minimization is performed over all generator components
{\mathbb{G}}_{\alpha}^{(jk)}
and structure constants
a_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}
, using the
Adam
optimizer with a learning rate between
0.0001
and
0.01
. Unless otherwise specified, the hyperparameters
h
were held fixed to 1.

IV
The
U(n)
and
SU(n)
family

We now turn our attention to the
U(n)
and
SU(n)
families which were not considered in
Forestano et al. 2023
or elsewhere in the current context. The two families are related as
U(n)=SU(n)\times U(1)
, where the
U(1)
factor accounts for the
\det U=1
condition. Since the invariant polynomial (
3
) is defined over the field of complex numbers, we need to make a few adjustments to the procedure in Section
II
: i) create a dataset
\{\mathbf{x}\}
which now consists of
m

n
-dimensional complex vectors; ii) use the complex form of the functions defined in the loss function by replacing any products of
\mathbb{G}\mathbb{G}
with
\mathbb{G}\mathbb{G}^{\ast}
; and iii) insert the conventional imaginary unit factor in front of
\varepsilon
in (
5
) as
\delta{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbb{I}+i\varepsilon\,{\mathbb{G}}
. Upon training with
n^{2}
generators, we will find the algebra of
U(n)
. In order to find the algebra of
SU(n)
, we take advantage of the ability of our method to find sets of generators forming closed algebras. Therefore, all we need to do is reduce the number of generators to
n^{2}-1
and proceed as before.

V
Summary and Outlook

In this letter we demonstrated how to use machine learning to derive sparse representations of the generators of an arbitrary Lie algebra. The key observation is that a Lie group preserves a certain invariant polynomial under the group action on the vector space (for the examples considered here, those were the oracle functions (
1
-
3
)). As shown in
Forestano et al. 2023
, one can design suitable loss functions for the generator components, so that minimization of the loss function leads to a set of valid generators. In this letter we extended the approach of
Forestano et al. 2023
by adding terms to the loss function that encourage finding sparse representations. We validated our method for the Lorentz group (in Section
III
) and for the
U(n)
and
SU(n)
family of Lie groups (in Section
IV
). Although not shown here, we have also verified the procedure on all of the
SO(n)
examples considered in
Forestano et al. 2023
.

We should note that all of our numerical work was done on a personal laptop, and did not use any high performance facilities. All models were trained for 5,000 epochs and the typical training times ranged from a few minutes to a couple of hours for the case of
SU(6)
. While the method is applicable to arbitrarily large groups, in practice the training in such cases may become relatively slow and could benefit from parallelization, further code optimization, further hyperparameter tuning, and better understanding of the loss landscape. Examples of much larger groups, like the exceptional Lie groups
Ramond 1976
, will be explored in future work.
