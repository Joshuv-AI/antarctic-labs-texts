Long time asymptotics of mixed-type Kimura diffusions
Guillaume Bal, Binglu Chen, Zhongjian Wang

Abstract
This paper concerns the long-time asymptotics of diffusions with degenerate coefficients at the domain’s boundary. Degenerate diffusion operators with mixed linear and quadratic degeneracies find applications in the analysis of asymmetric transport at edges separating topological insulators. In one space dimension, we characterize all possible invariant measures for such a class of operators and in all cases show exponential convergence of the Green’s kernel to such invariant measures. We generalize the results to a class of two-dimensional operators including those used in the analysis of topological insulators. Several numerical simulations illustrate our theoretical findings. Key Words: Degenerate Diffusion Operators; Invariant Measure; Long Time Asymptotics; Fredholm Index; Lyapunov Functions. MSC : 35K65, 60J70, 47D06, 37C40.

Introduction

This paper analyzes the long time behavior of diffusion processes with infinitesimal generator given by a mixed type Kimura operator
L
on one-dimensional and two-dimensional manifolds with corners.

In two-space dimensions, the mixed type Kimura operator
L
acts on functions defined on a manifold with corner
P
. A boundary point
p\in bP
has a relatively open neighborhood
V
that is homomorphic to either
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, where we assume that
p
maps to
(0,0)
. We say that
p
is a corner in the first case and an edge point in the second case. More details are presented in Section
5
.

In this paper,
L
is a degenerate second-order differential operator such that for each edge, the coefficients of the normal part of the second-order term vanish to order one or two. For an edge point
p
, when written in an adapted system of local coordinates on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
,
L
takes the form:

\displaystyle L=a(x,y)x^{m}\partial_{xx}+b(x,y)x^{m-1}\partial_{xy}+c(x,y)\partial_{yy}+d(x,y)x^{m-1}\partial_{x}+e(x,y)\partial_{y},

(1)

where we assume that
a,b,c,d,e
are smooth functions and that
a(x,y)>0
and
c(x,y)>0
. Also
m\in\{1,2\}
. When
m=1
, the edge
x=0
is of Kimura type in that the coefficients vanish linearly towards it. When
m=2
, the edge
x=0
is of quadratic type as the coefficients now vanish quadratically.

For a corner
p
, as an intersection point of two edges,
L
has the following normal form:

\displaystyle L=a(x,y)x^{m}\partial_{xx}+b(x,y)x^{m-1}y^{n-1}\partial_{xy}+c(x,y)y^{n}\partial_{yy}+d(x,y)x^{m-1}\partial_{x}+e(x,y)y^{n-1}\partial_{y},

(2)

when written in an adapted system of local coordinates on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, where
a(x,y)>0
,
c(x,y)>0
,
and
m,n\in\{1,2\}
.

Associated to the operator
L
is a
C^{0}
semigroup
\mathcal{Q}_{t}=e^{tL}
solution operator of the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=Lu

with initial conditions
u(x,0)=f(x)
at

. The operator
e^{tL}
and some of its properties are presented in detail in
[
10
]
. The main objective of this paper is to analyze the long time behavior of

, and in particular convergence to appropriately defined invariant measures.

It turns out that the number of possible invariant measures and their type (absolutely continuous with respect to one-dimensional or two-dimensional Lebesgue measures or not) strongly depend on the structure of the coefficients
(a,b,c,d,e)
. We thus distinguish the different boundary types that influence the long time asymptotics of transition probabilities.

Definition 1.1
.

A Kimura edge

is called a tangent (Kimura) edge when

A quadratic edge

\frac{d(0,y)}{a(0,y)}<1
, a transverse (quadratic) edge when
\frac{d(0,y)}{a(0,y)}>1
, and a neutral (quadratic) edge when
\frac{d(0,y)}{a(0,y)}=1
.

We assume that:

Assumption 1.1
.

Every edge is either tangent, transverse, or neutral.

Note that we do not consider the setting with
d(0,y)<0
on a Kimura edge. In such a situation, diffusive particles pushed by the drift term

have a positive probability of escaping the domain

. We would then need to augment the diffusion operator with appropriate boundary conditions.

The long-time analysis in two-dimensions for a class of operators including a specific example of interest in the field of topological insulators
[
6

[

]
, the degenerate diffusion equation describes reflection coefficients of wavefields propagating in heterogeneous media, which model the separation between topological insulators; see

Before addressing two-dimensional operators, we consider the simpler one-dimensional setting, where we need to consider ten different scenarios, listed in table

To describe all invariant measure on the interval

Definition 1.2

When

The quadratic endpoint and the tangent Kimura endpoint are sticky boundary points in the sense that the Dirac measure supported on them is an invariant measure. When both endpoints are transverse, there is another invariant measure

In both cases, starting from a point in

In two space dimensions, we do not consider all possible invariant measures as a function of the nature of the drift terms

For

The setting of

; see Remark

There is a large literature on the analysis of the long-time behavior of
e^{tL}
when
L
is non-degenerate and when
L
is of Kimura type. In the latter case,
L
is the generalized Kimura operator studied in
[
16
]
. In that work,

is analyzed on a weighted Hölder space, denoted by
L_{\gamma}:C^{0,2+\gamma}(P)\to C^{0,\gamma}(P)

is then Fredholm of index zero, which is used to characterize the nullspace of an adjoint operator and show that the non-zero spectrum lies in a half plane
\text{Re}\ \mu<\eta<0
so that for

,

converges to a stable limiting solution at an exponential rate.

However, in the presence of quadratic edge/point,

is not Fredholm. The reason is that near such quadratic edges or points, the operator may be modeled by an elliptic (non-degenerate) operator on an infinite domain (with thus continuous spectrum in the vicinity of the origin). We thus need another approach that builds on the following previous works. In
[
1
]
, the growth bound of a strongly continuous positive semigroup is associated with the Lyapunov function. In
[
4
]
, for a time continuous Markov process admitting a (unique) ergodic invariant measure, the rate of convergence to equilibrium is studied using a weaker version of a Lyapunov function called a
\phi

An outline of the rest of this paper is as follows. The semigroup
e^{tL}
is analyzed in section

The operator

. For the class of operators satisfying Assumption

Numerical simulations of stochastic differential equations presented in section

\displaystyle\underset{t\to 0+}{\lim}||\mathcal{Q}_{t}f-f||_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}=0.

(5)

Proof.

If both endpoints are of Kimura type,

is the 1D Kimura operator. By
[
15
, Sec 9, Theorem 1]
,

defines a positive and strongly continuous semi-group on

for

. If both endpoints are of quadratic type, we can regard

as a uniform parabolic operator on

(using, e.g., a change of variables

[

For the remaining case, we might as well assume that

is a Kimura endpoint and

a quadratic endpoint. We intend to build the global solution out of local solution near the boundary. Let
([0,1-\eta],\phi_{0}),([\eta,1],\phi_{1})

small be the coordinate charts so that pulling back

to these coordinate charts gives two local operators

\displaystyle L_{0}=x\partial^{2}_{x}+b_{0}\partial_{x}+xc(x)\partial_{x},\quad x\in[0,\phi_{0}(1-\eta)),

\displaystyle L_{1}=\partial_{z}^{2}+d(z)\partial_{z},\quad z\in(\phi_{1}(\eta),\infty).

We extend these two local operators to the whole sample space

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{0}=x\partial^{2}_{x}+b_{0}\partial_{x}+xc(x)\varphi_{0}(x)\partial_{x},\quad\widetilde{L}_{1}=\partial_{z}^{2}+d(z)\varphi_{1}(z)\partial_{z}

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{for}\ x\in[0,\phi_{0}(1-2\eta)]\\
0&\text{for}\ x>\phi_{0}(1-\eta),\end{array}\end{cases}\varphi_{1}(z)=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{for}\ z\in[\phi_{1}(2\eta),\infty)\\
0&\text{for}\ z<\phi_{1}(\eta).\end{array}\end{cases}

Let
\widetilde{Q}^{0}_{t},\widetilde{Q}^{1}_{t}
be the solution operators of
\widetilde{L}_{0},\widetilde{L}_{1}
and denote their kernels by
\widetilde{q}^{0}_{t},\widetilde{q}^{1}_{t}
respectively. Define smooth cutoff functions

\displaystyle\text{supp}\psi_{0}\subset[0,1-2\eta],\ \text{supp}\psi_{1}\subset[2\eta,1],\ \psi_{0}|_{\text{supp}\chi}\equiv 1,\ \psi_{1}|_{\text{supp}(1-\chi)}\equiv 1.

(6)

Given

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{t}f=\psi_{0}\widetilde{Q}^{0}_{t}[\chi f]+\psi_{1}\widetilde{Q}^{1}_{t}[(1-\chi)f],

\displaystyle A_{t}g=\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{Q}_{t-s}g(s)ds.

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-L)\widetilde{Q}_{t}f=E^{0}_{t}f:=[\psi_{0},L]\widetilde{Q}^{0}_{t}[\chi f]+[\psi_{1},L]\widetilde{Q}^{1}_{t}[(1-\chi)f],

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-L)A_{t}g=(Id-E_{t})g:=g-[\psi_{0},L]A^{0}_{t}[\chi g]-[\psi_{1},L]A^{1}_{t}[(1-\chi)g].

Our choice of

\text{dist}(\text{supp}[\psi_{0},L],\text{supp}\chi)>0

, which ensures that

are bounded operators with operator norms bounded by

for some constant

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t}f=\widetilde{Q}_{t}f-A_{t}(Id-E_{t})^{-1}E^{0}_{t}f.

Since both

\underset{t\to 0}{\lim}\ ||\widetilde{Q}_{t}f-f||_{C^{0}}=0.

||A_{t}(Id-E_{t})^{-1}E^{0}_{t}||_{C^{0}([0,1])\to(C^{0}([0,1]),t)}=o(t).

Therefore (

\displaystyle q_{t}(x,y)=\widetilde{q}_{t}(x,y)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{q}_{t-s}(x,z)h_{s}(z,y)dzds.

4
Exponential convergence to invariant measures

In this section we study the convergence rate to the invariant measure in two different cases.
First, when there is at least one tangent boundary, then the invariant measure is Dirac measure(s) at the tangent boundary(ies) and quadratic endpoints (if any). To estimate the convergence rate we use Lyapunov functions constructed in Section
4.1.2
. These Lyapunov functions display different asymptotic behaviors at different boundary endpoints, and turn out to lie in the function spaces
C(\alpha,\beta)
(see Definition
4.1
). We show that the growth bound of the generator of
\mathcal{Q}_{t}
on
C(\alpha,\beta)
is not larger than the Lyapunov function rate (
56
), and thus negative. From this, we conclude that the transition probability converges exponentially to the corresponding invariant measure in a Wasserstein distance. The main results are summarized in Theorem
4.1
.

In the second case where both boundary points are transverse, the invariant measures include a unique invariant measure
\mu
supported on the whole domain
[0,1]
and Dirac measures at quadratic endpoints (if any). Motivated by underdamped Langevin dynamics, we wish to prove and then apply a Poincaré inequality in
L^{2}(\mu)
to show exponential convergence.
Using tools developed in
[
3
]
, we obtain a (W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality; see Definition
4.2
. The main tools we use are the local Lyapunov function, which is Lyapunov except on a small subset
\mathcal{C}
and a local Poincaré inequality on
\mathcal{C}^{c}
; see Definition
124
. Finally we apply the (W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality to show that
\mu
satisfies the Poincaré inequality and for any probability measure

(56)

C(\alpha,\beta)

]

Proof.

Our proof is a modification of the proof of

Given

Let

2.

\forall g\in C(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle P_{u}(f)\cdot u-f\geq 0,\ (P_{u}(f)\cdot u-f)(x_{0})=0.

4.1.2
Estimation of
\lambda_{0}
and rate of convergence

In this part, we show that
\lambda_{0}<0
, which implies the exponential convergence to
\delta
measures at tangent boundaries. In order to do this, we construct
u
that satisfies
Lu<\lambda u
for some
\lambda<0
. The strategy of construction is to first use the behavior of
L
in the vicinity of two the boundary points to construct
u
near the boundaries and then find a connecting interior function.

Boundary Construction

\mathit{1.\ Quadratic\ endpoint}
.
In the vicinity of quadratic point,
L=x^{2}\partial_{xx}+(b(x)+1)x\partial_{x}
and recall
{b_{-}}=b(0)
. Let

\displaystyle u=Ax^{c},A>0.

Taking
c=\frac{-{b_{-}}}{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{Lu}{u}=(c+b(x))c,

is negative in a neighborhood of 0.

\mathit{2.Kimura\ endpoint}.

In the vicinity of a Kimura point,
L=x\partial_{xx}+b(x)\partial_{x}
with
b=b(0)
. Let

\displaystyle\mathit{u=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cc}Ax^{c},\ 0<c<1&b=0\\
A(1-x)^{c},\ c>0&b>0\end{array}\end{cases}}.

In both cases

\displaystyle\frac{Lu}{u}=(c-1+b(x))cx^{-1}

\displaystyle\frac{Lu}{u}=c(1-x)^{-2}[(c-1)x-b(x)(1-x)]

are negative in a neighborhood of 0.

Interior Construction

Suppose we have constructed
u
in
[0,x_{1}]
and
[x_{2},1]
. We now need to construct an interior function
u
that satisfies the boundary conditions. Inside
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, suppose
L
has the form

\displaystyle L=\partial_{xx}+b(x)\partial_{x},

where
b(x)
is smooth on
[x_{1},x_{2}]
.
Let

\displaystyle B(x)=\int_{x_{1}}^{x}b(t)dt,

(58)

then
B
is smooth on
[x_{1},x_{2}]
. We want
Lu
strictly negative, that is

\displaystyle u_{xx}+b(x)u_{x}<0.

It is equivalent to
e^{B(x)}u_{x}
being a decreasing function, i.e. there exists some positive function
f
on
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, such that

\displaystyle(e^{B(x)}u_{x})^{\prime}=-f.

(59)

Case I: one tangent, one transverse points

Without loss of generality, we assume a tangent boundary at
x=0
. Now by construction of
u
at both boundaries,
u_{x}(x_{1})>0
,
u_{x}(x_{2})>0
. We first modify the constant
A
in the construction near
x=1
such that

\displaystyle e^{B(x_{2})}u_{x}(x_{2})<e^{B(x_{1})}u_{x}(x_{1}).

Next, by (
59
),
f(x_{1})=-e^{B(x_{1})}Lu(x_{1})>0,\ f(x_{2})=-e^{B(x_{2})}Lu(x_{2})>0
are two positive fixed constants. Then we can set

to be a positive function with

fixed and

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}f(t)dt=e^{B(x_{1})}u_{x}(x_{1})-e^{B(x_{2})}u_{x}(x_{2}).

Such an

guarantees that
e^{B(x)}u_{x}(x)>e^{B(x_{2})}u_{x}(x_{2})>0
for

which guarantees positiveness of

and hence positiveness of

.

Case II: two tangent boundaries

By construction of
u
at both boundaries,
u_{x}(x_{1})>0,\ u_{x}(x_{2})<0
. We directly get
e^{B(x_{2})}u_{x}(x_{2})<e^{B(x_{1})}u_{x}(x_{1})
.

Denoting

, then
F(x)
is a differentiable increasing function on

with

. Then by considering derivatives of

\displaystyle u_{x}(x)=e^{-B(x)}\left[e^{B(x_{1})}u_{x}(x_{1})-F(x)\right]

we know
u
is first increasing then decreasing on
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, which implies
u
is lower bounded by
\min(u(x_{1}),u(x_{2}))
. Hence we only need to assign a positive
f
such that

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}f(t)dt=e^{B(x_{1})}u_{x}(x_{1})-e^{B(x_{2})}u_{x}(x_{2}).

Theorem 4.1
.

If there is only one tangent endpoint

and

, such that for any probability measure

If there is only one tangent endpoint, say

,

\displaystyle f=f_{0}+f(0),f_{0}\in C(\alpha,\beta).

\displaystyle||\mathcal{Q}_{t}f-f(0)||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}=||\mathcal{Q}_{t}f_{0}||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}\leq Me^{\lambda_{0}t}||f_{0}||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(x)-f(0)|\leq MC_{\alpha,\beta}e^{\lambda_{0}t}x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}.

(60)

. Hence for any non-quadratic point

converges to

x=1

for

.

If both endpoints are tangent, by Theorem

\displaystyle f=f_{0}+(1-S_{0})f(0)+S_{0}f(1),\quad f_{0}\in C(\alpha,\beta).

Again, by Proposition

\displaystyle||\mathcal{Q}_{t}f-(1-S_{0})f(0)-S_{0}f(1)||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}=||\mathcal{Q}_{t}f_{0}||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}\leq Me^{\lambda_{0}t}||f_{0}||_{C(\alpha,\beta)}

Recall that we have constructed the invariant measure

4.2.1
(W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality

Consider
U(z)=\int_{0}^{z}b(s)ds
with
b(s)
negative when
s\to-\infty
and positive when
s\to\infty
. Then
U
grows linearly to
+\infty
near

.
L
can be taken as

\displaystyle L_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{zz}-\nabla U(z)\partial_{z}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}^{*}\partial_{z}

with
\partial^{*}_{z}=\partial_{z}+2\nabla U(z)
on a probability space
(X,\mu)
, with
\mu
the invariant measure

\displaystyle\mu(dz)=\frac{e^{-2U(z)}}{Z}dz

(61)

where the normalizing constant
Z<\infty
due to the linear growth of
U
.
The main advantage of switching to
L^{2}(\mu)
is

L
is self adjoint on
L^{2}(\mu)
. Indeed

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{z}\varphi)\phi d\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(-\partial_{z}\phi+2\phi\nabla U(z))d\mu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\partial^{*}_{z}\phi d\mu

so that

\displaystyle(\varphi,L\phi)_{L^{2}(\mu)}=(L\varphi,\phi)_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

For some function
W\in D(L)
with
W\geq 1
, let
I_{W}(t)=\int Q^{2}_{t}fWd\mu
. Then
[
3
]

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{W}(t)=-\int W|\nabla(Q_{t}f)|^{2}d\mu+\int WLQ^{2}_{t}fd\mu.

(62)

This leads to the definition
(W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality
:

Definition 4.2
.

We say that
\mu
satisfies a (W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality, if there exists
W\in D(L),W\geq 1
and a constant
C_{LP}
such that for all nice
f
with
\int fd\mu=0
,

\displaystyle\int f^{2}Wd\mu\leq C_{LP}\int\left(W|\nabla f|^{2}-WLf^{2}\right)d\mu.

(63)

Local Poincaré inequality

In the transverse case, we can not find a global Lyapunov function. We have to weaken the condition to define a
local Lyapunov function

V
as

\displaystyle LV\leq-\alpha V+\beta\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}},\ V\geq 1

(64)

for some set
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 4.3

(Local Poincaré inequality)
.

Let
{\Omega}
be a subset of the whole space
X
(we will use
\mathcal{C}\subset{\Omega}
). We say that
\mu
satisfies a
local Poincaré inequality on
{\Omega}
if there exists some constant
\kappa_{\Omega}
such that for all nice
f
with

\int_{X}fd\mu=0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{2}d\mu\leq\kappa_{U}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu+\frac{1}{\mu({\Omega})}\left(\int_{\Omega}fd\mu\right)^{2}.

(65)

We now show the:

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that there is some local Lyapunov function
V\geq 1
under some set
\mathcal{C}
such that (
64
) holds, and
\mu
satisfies a local Poincaré inequality on
{\Omega}\supset\mathcal{C}
with moreover

\displaystyle\beta\mu({\Omega}^{c})<\alpha\mu({\Omega}).

(66)

Then we can find
\lambda\geq 0
such that if
W=V+\lambda
, then
\mu
satisfies a (W)-Lyapunov-Poincaré inequality.

Proof.

Multiply (
64
) by
f^{2}
and integrate to get

\displaystyle\int_{X}f^{2}LVd\mu\leq-\alpha\int_{X}f^{2}Vd\mu+\beta\int_{C}f^{2}d\mu.

Let
\int_{X}fd\mu=0
. The local Poincaré inequality implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{\Omega}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu+\frac{1}{\mu({\Omega})}\left(\int_{\Omega}fd\mu\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{\Omega}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu+\frac{1}{\mu({\Omega})}\left(-\int_{{\Omega}^{c}}fd\mu\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{\Omega}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu+\frac{\mu({\Omega}^{c})}{\mu({\Omega})}\left(\int_{{\Omega}^{c}}f^{2}d\mu\right),

so that

\displaystyle\int_{X}f^{2}\left(LV+\alpha V\right)d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\beta\int_{C}f^{2}d\mu\leq\beta\int_{\Omega}f^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\beta\kappa_{U}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu+\frac{\beta\mu({\Omega}^{c})}{\mu({\Omega})}\left(\int_{{\Omega}^{c}}f^{2}Vd\mu\right).

We re-organize the inequality as,

\displaystyle\alpha\left(1-\frac{\beta\mu({\Omega}^{c})}{\alpha\mu({\Omega})}\right)\int_{X}f^{2}Vd\mu

\displaystyle\leq\beta\kappa_{\Omega}\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu-\int_{X}f^{2}LVd\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}(V+\lambda)d\mu-\int_{X}f^{2}L(V+\lambda)d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{X}|\nabla f|^{2}(V+\lambda)d\mu-\int_{X}Lf^{2}(V+\lambda)d\mu.

Here we take
\ \lambda=(\beta\kappa_{U}-1)_{+}
so that
\beta\kappa_{\Omega}\leq V+\lambda
.
Since,

Exponential convergence to invariant measure

First, there exists a tubular neighborhood of

with

with respect to

\displaystyle q_{h}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{1}p_{t}(p,x,y)h(y)dy.

(

We already know that there exists a unique invariant measure

does not satisfy the logarithmic Sobolev inequality. So in the proof of the following theorem, we employ an interpolation divergence as constructed in
[

.

For

\displaystyle\psi(u)=3+\left(\frac{5}{2}-\frac{3}{r-1}\right)(u-3)+\frac{9}{r(r-1)}\left[\left(\frac{u}{3}\right)^{r}-1\right],u\geq 0.

(83)

Such
\psi
is convex,
\psi(1)=0
and
\psi(u)\sim\frac{9}{r(r-1)3^{r}}u^{r}
at
+\infty

Lemma 5.1
.

[

There exists a constant

such that for two probability measures

\displaystyle||P-Q||_{TV}\leq C_{\psi}\sqrt{\int\psi\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)dQ}.

If
\mu
satisfies a Poincaré inequality, then for

satisfying

, we have that

satisfies the

-inequality:

\displaystyle\int\psi(h)\mu\leq C_{\psi}\int\psi^{\prime\prime}(h)|\partial_{x}h|^{2}\mu.

(84)

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume for some

that

\displaystyle h\geq 0,\ h|_{H\times[0,A]}\equiv 1\ \text{for}\ y\in[0,1],\ h|_{y=1},h^{\prime}|_{y=1}\equiv 0.

(85)

Fix a point

\forall\epsilon>0

Proof.

Three are three types of boundary of

. We first take a coordinate change at quadratic endpoints. By abuse of notation, we still denote

the coordinate along

and

is mapped to

On

\displaystyle L=a(x,y)\partial_{xx}+b(x,y)y^{m}\partial_{yy}+c(x,y)y\partial_{xy}+d(x,y)\partial_{x}+e(x,y)^{m-1}\partial_{y},\ \ m\in\{1,2\}.

The proofs for the cases

(87)

6
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present numerical simulations that illustrates the long time behaviors obtained in earlier sections. Since the distributions may converge to lower dimensional manifolds at tangent boundaries, we adopt the probabilistic representation
[
35
]
that considers the stochastic process governed by the generator
L
. Comparing with solving PDE directly on meshgrids, the probabilistic representation can be numerically implemented in a way respecting the local coordinate chart at degenerated boundaries. See
[
28
,
18
]
for examples of stability of long time behaviour simulation by numerical probabilistic approach.

Following the theoretical derivations, we start with examples in one space dimension before considering a two dimensional case.

We present the Lagrangian scheme in detail in the one-dimensional setting.

6.1
One dimensional cases with mixed boundary conditions

We first consider a general model operator that is of Kimura type at
x=0
and of quadratic type at
x=1
. Specifically,

\displaystyle L=(c_{0}(1-x)^{2}-c_{1}x(1-x))\partial_{x}+x(1-x)^{2}\partial_{x}^{2},

(102)

where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
denote the strength of convection near boundaries. We recall that a Kimura boundary at
x=0
is transverse when
c_{0}>0
and tangent when
c_{0}=0
; the quadratic boundary at
x=0
is transverse when
c_{1}>1
and tangent when
c_{1}<1
. When both boundaries are transverse,
L
admits an invariant measure

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{Z}x^{c_{0}-1}(1-x)^{c_{1}-2}.

(103)

To present the convergence result, we use particles to represent the distribution
q(t,x)
defined by

\displaystyle q(t,x)=\int_{0}^{1}p_{t}(x,y)q_{0}(x)dy,

(104)

where
q_{0}
is the initial distribution and

. We denote the process as
X_{\cdot}

has initial distribution
q_{0}
, and

follows stochastic differential equation (SDE) in the Itô sense
[
35
]

\displaystyle dX=(c_{0}(1-X)^{2}-c_{1}X(1-X))dt+\sqrt{2X(1-X)^{2}}dW_{t},

(105)

where
W_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion. The probability density function of
X_{t}
is then

We now develop a modified Euler Maruyama (EM) scheme
[
24
]
to generate approximated realizations of the process
X
and then approximate
q
by the empirical distribution of such generated realizations. First,
X_{n}
denotes the approximated process at time

is a fixed constant during the computation. Since the degeneracy of

only happens at boundaries, we apply a standard EM scheme when

is ‘far’ from the boundary.

In practice, when

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=X_{n}+(c_{0}(1-X_{n})^{2}-c_{1}X_{n}(1-X_{n}))\Delta t+\sqrt{2X_{n}(1-X_{n})^{2}}\sqrt{\Delta t}\mathcal{N},

(106)

where
\mathcal{N}
denotes a generated standard Gaussian distribution.

When

, the particle is considered as approaching the Kimura boundary at
x=0
. The leading order of SDE turns to be

\displaystyle dX=c_{0}dt,

which provides a strong forcing so that the position of particles remain strictly positive. To this end, we consider an implicit scheme,

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=X_{n}+(c_{0}(1-X_{n})^{2}-c_{1}X_{n}(1-X_{n}))\Delta t+\sqrt{2X_{n+1}(1-X_{n})^{2}}\sqrt{\Delta t}\mathcal{N},

which may be explicitly solved by

\displaystyle\sqrt{X_{n+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{2\Delta t}\Delta W(1-X_{n})+\sqrt{(\sqrt{2\Delta t}\Delta W(1-X_{n}))+4(X_{n}+(c_{0}(1-X_{n})^{2}-c_{1}X_{n}(1-X_{n}))\Delta t)}}{2}.

(107)

When

, the particle is considered as approaching the quadratic boundary at

. The leading order of the SDE is

\displaystyle dX=-c_{1}(1-X)dt+\sqrt{2}(1-X)dW_{t}.

It is then natural to apply a logarithm transform over

, i.e., let

. Then by the Itô formula,

follows

\displaystyle dY=(c_{0}(1-X)-c_{1}X)dt+\sqrt{2X}dW_{t}+Xdt,

(108)

where

. This is not a degenerate SDE since here

is assumed to be near

.

Proof.

We can follow the proof of

d\mu
on

induces a Riemannian metric
dV
on

. For each Kimura boundary surface

m+n=2

\displaystyle\underset{i=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}+\underset{i,j=1}{\overset{m,n}{\sum}}b(x,y)x_{i}y_{j}\partial_{x_{i}y_{j}}+\underset{j=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}c(x,y)y_{j}^{2}\partial_{y_{j}y_{j}}

\displaystyle+\underset{i=1}{\overset{m}{\sum}}d(x,y)\partial_{x_{i}}+\underset{j=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}e(x,y)y_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}.

The weighted measure

is a multiple by a smooth function of

\displaystyle d\mu(x,y)=\underset{i=1}{\overset{m}{\Pi}}x_{i}^{d_{i}(x,y)-1}dx_{i}\underset{j=1}{\overset{n}{\Pi}}\frac{1}{y_{j}}dy_{j}.
