Quantum Many-Body Scars from Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen States in Bilayer Systems
Julia Wildeboer, Christopher M. Langlett, Zhi-Cheng Yang, Alexey V. Gorshkov, Thomas Iadecola, Shenglong Xu

Abstract
Quantum many-body scar states are special eigenstates of nonintegrable models with distinctive entanglement features that give rise to infinitely long-lived coherent dynamics under quantum quenches from certain initial states. We elaborate on a construction of quantum many-body scar states in which they emerge from Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) states in systems with two layers, wherein the two layers are maximally entangled. We apply this construction to spin systems as well as systems of itinerant fermions and bosons and demonstrate how symmetries can be harnessed to enhance its versatility. We show that several well known examples of quantum many-body scars, including the tower of states in the spin-1 XY model and the η \eta -pairing states in the Fermi-Hubbard model, can be understood within this formalism. We also demonstrate how an infinite tower of many-body scar states can emerge in bilayer Bose-Hubbard models with charge conservation.



I
Introduction

Quantum many-body scar (QMBS) states are special highly excited eigenstates with atypical properties relative to other eigenstates at the same energy density
Serbyn et al. 2021
;
Moudgalya et al. 2022
;
Chandran et al. 2022
.
Such eigenstates emerge in quantum many-body systems that are nonintegrable and are expected to obey the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH)
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
, which states that all eigenstates in a small energy window should exhibit observable properties that are identical in the thermodynamic limit.
In ETH-obeying systems, quantum dynamics from typical initial states exhibits relaxation to a local thermal equilibrium dictated by the eigenstates with which the initial state has overlap
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Polkovnikov et al. 2011
.
QMBS states typically constitute a vanishing fraction of all eigenstates in the thermodynamic limit and display no dynamical signature for generic initial states.
However, for special initial states that have overlap either predominantly
Turner et al. 2018
or exclusively
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
;
Iadecola and Schecter 2020
with the scar states, the resulting dynamics exhibit distinctive coherent features that are absent in generic quench experiments.
QMBS states often appear in “towers” consisting of a number of states scaling polynomially with system size
Moudgalya et al. 2018a
;
Moudgalya et al. 2018b
;
Turner et al. 2018
;
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
;
Iadecola and Schecter 2020
;
Shibata et al. 2020
;
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Mark et al. 2020
;
Moudgalya et al. 2020a
;
Chattopadhyay et al. 2020
;
Pakrouski et al. 2020
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
;
Ren et al. 2021
;
O’Dea et al. 2020
;
Tang et al. 2021
.
When these states are equally spaced in energy, the resulting dynamics becomes perfectly oscillatory, leading to sharp experimental signatures of these rare eigenstates
Bernien et al. 2017
;
Su et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2022
.
These coherent dynamics may enable applications of QMBS states in quantum sensing protocols
Dooley 2021
;
Desaules et al. 2022
;
Dooley et al. 2022
.

One important research direction in this field is the development of systematic constructions of QMBS state towers with distinctive dynamical signatures.
Techniques for achieving this include group-theoretic constructions
Pakrouski et al. 2020
;
Ren et al. 2021
;
O’Dea et al. 2020
, matrix product state methods
Moudgalya et al. 2020b
;
Ren et al. 2022
, and projector embeddings
Shiraishi and Mori 2017
;
Shiraishi 2019
;
Choi et al. 2019
;
Ok et al. 2019
;
Srivatsa et al. 2020
;
Wildeboer et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2020
;
Banerjee and Sen 2021
;
Biswas et al. 2022
.
These methods provide insight into manifestations of QMBS states in previously unexplored models, which constitutes another important direction of ongoing research that is connected to efforts to realize such states in experiments.
While QMBS states have been heavily studied in one-dimensional (1D) systems, their two-dimensional (2D) counterparts have also begun to attract attention
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
;
Ok et al. 2019
;
Srivatsa et al. 2020
;
Wildeboer et al. 2021
;
Ren et al. 2022
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
, particularly in light of recent experimental progress
Bluvstein et al. 2021
.

Generally QMBS states are identified in a many-body spectrum through their subextensive entanglement scaling, which contrasts with the volume law entanglement scaling of typical finite-energy-density eigenstates.
However, sub-volume-law entanglement entropy is not a necessary condition for an eigenstate to be a QMBS
Langlett et al. 2022
;
Schindler et al. 2022
;
Srivatsa et al. 2022
.
In Ref.
Langlett et al. 2022
, it was demonstrated that there exists a class of QMBS states that are highly entangled while retaining a simple entanglement structure, and that can be embedded into many-body Hamiltonians with a suitable bipartite structure.
These states, dubbed “rainbow scars” for their connection to the rainbow state and its long-range entanglement structure
Langlett et al. 2022
;
Ramírez et al. 2014
;
Vitagliano et al. 2010
;
Srivatsa et al. 2022
, display extensive entanglement scaling for generic bipartitions, going against the standard definition of QMBS states.
Thus, entanglement may be a good indicator but not a definitive method to judge if a quantum state is indeed a nonthermal QMBS state.

In this work, we apply the tools developed in Ref.
Langlett et al. 2022
to construct a wide variety of models with QMBS states.
We focus our attention on bilayer quantum many-body systems, which can arise both in systems with two layers separated in real space as well as systems where two internal states, such as spin states, play the role of the two layers.
We show that Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) states in which the two layers are maximally entangled give rise to QMBS states, and refer to these scar states as “EPR scars”
to distinguish their geometric structure from that of the rainbow state.
We construct these EPR scars for spin systems like those considered in Ref.
Langlett et al. 2022
, as well as for itinerant fermionic and bosonic degrees of freedom, focusing in particular on 2D examples that cannot be mapped to local spin systems.
We lay out the general construction in Sec.
II
.

Our main conclusions are twofold. First, we show that several known examples of QMBS states fall into the EPR scar framework.
In Sec.
III
, we show that scars in the spin-
1
XY model
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
can be recast as EPR scars upon mapping the spin-
1
system to a bilayer spin-
\frac{1}{2}
system projected onto the local triplet sector.
In Sec.
IV
, we show that the celebrated
\eta
-pairing tower of states
Yang 1989
;
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Moudgalya et al. 2020a
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
also falls into this construction, in addition to laying out generalizations beyond the original Fermi-Hubbard model that include spin-orbit coupling
Moudgalya et al. 2020a
;
Li 2020
and superconducting pairing
Pakrouski et al. 2021
terms.
In both of these examples the bilayer structure is emergent, stemming from the rewriting in terms of spin-
\frac{1}{2}
’s for the spin-
1
XY model, and from the spin index for
\eta
-pairing.
Second, in Sec.
V
, we construct an
infinite-dimensional
tower of states in bilayer Bose-Hubbard systems.
In the simplest case, this tower emerges in systems where the intra and interlayer interactions have opposite signs, a scenario that could be engineered in optical lattices for mixtures of two bosonic species.

II
Bilayer Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Quantum Many-Body Scar States

In this section we describe the construction of EPR scar states in bilayer systems and consider how different choices of bipartition influence entanglement calculations.
Subsequently we describe a general construction of parent Hamiltonians in doubled Hilbert spaces that is independent of the concrete details of the single system Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

II.1
The EPR State and its Entanglement Structure

We begin by considering two identical quantum systems, labeled
1
and
2
, which may either be spatially separated (e.g., the two layers of a bilayer system) or constructed using an internal degree of freedom (e.g., electron spin).
The Hilbert space
\mathbbm{H}_{1(2)}
of each system is spanned by an identical basis of states
\ket{n}_{1(2)}
.
The single-system Hilbert space
\mathbbm{H}_{1(2)}
need not have a local tensor product structure, the lack of which could arise from projection into a symmetry sector or from kinetic constraints.
A general state in the doubled Hilbert space
\mathbbm{H}=\mathbbm{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{2}
is given by
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n,\tilde{n}}\psi_{n,\tilde{n}}\ket{n}_{1}\otimes\ket{\tilde n}_{2}
.
The many-body EPR state is a pure state defined in the doubled Hilbert space as

\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}\ket{n}_{1}\otimes\ket{n}_{2}\,.

(1)

Because of its unique entanglement structure, the EPR state and its finite temperature variant called the thermofield-double state,

\ket{\text{TFD}(\beta)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}/2}\ket{n}_{1}\otimes\ket{n}_{2}\,,

(2)

where
Z=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}}
is the partition function at inverse temperature
\beta
, are of great research interest across multiple fields
Cottrell et al. 2019
;
Hartman and Maldacena 2013
;
Papadodimas and Raju 2015
;
Schuster et al. 2021
;
Nezami et al. 2021
.
The EPR state that is the focus of our work is simply the infinite-temperature thermofield-double state:
\ket{\text{EPR}}=\ket{\text{TFD}(\beta=0)}
.
In the rest of paper, we drop the subscript labelling the two copies
1
and
2
unless ambiguity arises.

Now we consider the entanglement structure of
\ket{\text{EPR}}
.
Throughout the paper, we quantify the entanglement between a subregion
A
and its complement
B
with the von-Neumann entropy
S^{\rm vN}=-\rm Tr(\rho_{A}\ln\rho_{A})
, where
\rho_{A}
is the reduced density matrix of subregion
A
computed from an eigenstate
|E\rangle
, i.e.

\rho_{A}={\rm Tr}_{B}\left(\ket{E}\bra{E}\right)
.
We note that, by construction, the two copies of the EPR state Eq. (
1
) share the maximal entanglement entropy
S^{\rm vN}=\ln[\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})]
,
where region
A
is defined as in Fig.
1
(a).

In the simplest case, each Hilbert space
\mathbbm{H}_{1(2)}
has a tensor product structure.
For concreteness, let us consider the Hilbert space of
N
qubits with dimension
2^{N}
for each copy (generalization to local dimension larger than
2
is straightforward).
In this case, the sum in Eq. (
1
) can be carried out independently for each pair of qubits, and
the EPR state becomes a product of local EPR pairs,

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ket{0_{i}}\otimes\ket{0_{i}}+\ket{1_{i}}\otimes\ket{1_{i}}\right)\,.

(3)

The bipartition in Fig.
1
(a) cuts through
N
EPR pairs, leading to the maximal entanglement entropy
S^{\rm vN}=N\ln 2
between the two copies. On the other hand, the bipartite entanglement in Fig.
1
(b) is zero because of the tensor product structure of the Hilbert space.

Now we consider the EPR state in Eq. (
1
) for more general Hilbert spaces without tensor product structure.
As mentioned before, by construction, the bipartition in Fig.
1
(a) always leads to the maximal entanglement entropy
S^{\rm vN}=\ln\rm dim(\mathbbm{H})
.
However, the entanglement entropy for the bipartition in Fig.
1
(b) is in general nonzero for Hilbert spaces that are not factorized.
A common approach to realizing such a Hilbert space is to add a constraint, e.g. from symmetries or kinetic constraints, to the Hilbert space of
N
qubits.
Suppose the constraint is implemented by fixing some set
\{p\}
of conserved quantities in
\mathbbm{H}_{1(2)}
, and let
\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}
be the projector into the constrained Hilbert space.
Assuming a bipartition of the type shown in Fig.
1
(b), the EPR state can then be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}}\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}\ket{n}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}\ket{n}_{2}

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}}\sum_{n_{A},n_{B}}\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}\ket{n_A n_B}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}\ket{n_A n_B}_{2}

where
\ket{n}
now lives in the Hilbert space of qubits and can be split into two parts
\ket{n_A}
and
\ket{n_B}
. The only nonzero matrix elements of the density matrix
\rho_{A}
of region
A
are then given by

\bra{n_A}_{1}\otimes\bra{n_A}_{2}\rho_{A}\ket{n'_A }_{1}\otimes\ket{n'_A}_{2}=\sum_{n_{B}}p_{n_{A}n_{B}}p_{n_{A^{\prime}}n_{B}}\,,

(5)

where the matrix element
p_{n_{A}n_{B}}
equals one up to normalization for states
\ket{n_A n_B}
within the constrained Hilbert space and zero otherwise.

From the above calculation, it becomes clear that the entanglement entropy of
\ket{\text{EPR}}
for the bipartition in Fig.
1
(b) is the same as that of the following state defined in a single copy of the system,

\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}}\sum_{n}\mathcal{P}_{\{p\}}\ket{n}\,.

(6)

Without the constraint imposed by the set
\{p\}
, this state is the
x
-basis product state
\ket{+}\cdots\ket{+}
, where
\ket{+}=(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
.
Adding a constraint results in entanglement.
For instance, in the case of U(1) symmetry, the projection of this state into a generic magnetization sector (i.e., one with a finite magnetization density above a fully polarized state) has bipartite entanglement entropy of order
\ln N
.
Another example for a system with a constrained Hilbert space is
the well-known PXP model, which captures the physics of Rydberg atoms
in the regime when two neighboring atoms cannot be simultaneously in the
excited state
Lesanovsky 2011
;
Turner et al. 2018
;
Bluvstein et al. 2021
;
Khemani et al. 2019
;
Lin and Motrunich 2019
. Here the single-layer state and thus the constrained EPR states display an area law entanglement entropy.

.
(a) A bipartition of type I, where subregions
A

(b) Contrarily a bipartition of type II includes degrees of freedom from both layers in each subregion (

and different

II.2
Constructing the Parent Hamiltonian for the EPR Scar State

Because of the maximal entanglement between the two copies, the EPR state in Eq. (
1
) has the special property that applying an operator
\mathcal{O}
to one copy is equivalent to applying its transpose
\mathcal{O}^{T}
to the other copy.
To show this, consider the single-copy operator
\mathcal{O}=\sum_{mn}\ket{m}O_{mn}\bra{n}
and its transpose
\mathcal{O}^{T}=\sum_{mn}\ket{m }O_{nm}\bra{n}
, which we take to act within
\mathbbm{H}_{1}
. Using the definition of the EPR state in Eq. (
1
), one can show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\otimes\mathbbm{I}\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm dim(\mathbbm{H}_{1})}}\sum\limits_{mn}O_{mn}\ket{m}\otimes\ket{n}

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{O}^{T}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,.

Based on this property, one can construct a family of interacting parent Hamiltonians for which
\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an exact eigenstate.
Since
\ket{\text{EPR}}
has a very simple entanglement structure described in the last section, it is not thermal and becomes a QMBS state, which we call the EPR scar state, when the parent Hamiltonian is nonintegrable.

We now outline the construction of the parent Hamiltonian.
Consider the Hamiltonian in the doubled Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{12}\,.

(8)

In the above,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are the Hamiltonians within each copy and
\mathcal{H}_{12}
describes the interaction between the two copies, which can be expanded in the basis
\{\mathcal{O}_{A}\}
of one-copy Hermitian operators as
\sum_{A,B}\lambda_{AB}\mathcal{O}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}
.
Using Eq. (
7
) to move the nontrivial actions of
\mathcal{H}
on
|{\rm EPR}\rangle
to a single copy, we find that the general condition for the EPR state to be an eigenstate of
\mathcal{H}
with energy
E
is that

\mathcal{H}_{1}+\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*}+\sum\limits_{A,B}\lambda_{AB}\mathcal{O}_{A}\mathcal{O}_{B}^{*}=E\mathbbm{I}\,.

(9)

Notice that
\sum_{A,B}\lambda_{AB}\mathcal{O}_{A}\mathcal{O}_{B}
now acts on a single copy, and hence this is an operator equation in a single copy of the Hilbert space.
The construction Eq. (
8
) is valid in arbitrary spatial dimensions.
In our previous work
Langlett et al. 2022
, we considered a less general construction also requiring
\mathcal{H}_{1}+\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*}=0
.
The general construction can be further enriched by considering local unitary transformations and symmetries.
In the following sections, we demonstrate the general construction using specific examples with different Hilbert space structures including spins, fermions, and bosons and uncover EPR scars in some well-studied many-body Hamiltonians.

III
Spin Systems

In the first part of this section devoted to
spin systems we formulate the EPR scar construction for spin-
S
degrees of freedom.
We then proceed to demonstrate that the tower of QMBS states in the spin-
1
XY model
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
can be recast as EPR scars in a bilayer spin-
\frac{1}{2}
system, which in 1D reduces to a ladder geometry.
In the process, we illustrate how the general construction outlined in Sec.
II
can be enriched by unitary transformations and U(1) symmetry.
Finally, we consider a bilayer Heisenberg model on the triangular lattice and illustrate the even richer interplay of SU(2) symmetry with our construction.

III.1
Spin EPR State

We start by considering a spin-
S
model on a lattice of
N
sites with
S^{z}_{i}\in\{-S,\dots,+S\}
,
\forall

i=1,\ldots,N
.
For such a system, the EPR state in the doubled Hilbert space takes the form

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{\left(2S+1\right)^{N/2}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=-S}^{+S}\ket{m_{i}}\otimes\ket{m_{i}}=\frac{1}{\left(2S+1\right)^{N/2}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{m=-S}^{S}\Gamma(S,m)\left(S_{i}^{+}\otimes S_{i}^{+}\right)^{m+S}\ket{-S_i}\otimes\ket{-S_i}

(10)

with

\Gamma(S,m)=\frac{(S-m)!}{(2S)!(m+S)!}
and
S^{+}_{j}=S^{x}_{j}+iS^{y}_{j}
.
In this spin EPR state, spins from Hilbert spaces
\mathbbm{H}_{1}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{2}
that share the same site index
i
are perfectly correlated.

In cases where the parent Hamiltonian of the EPR state (
10
) respects a global symmetry, the projection of
\ket{\text{EPR}}
into each symmetry sector becomes an independent eigenstate of the Hamiltonian.
For example, it is easy to see that, when multiplied out, the state
\ket{\text{EPR}}
(
10
) consists of several terms that each can be associated with an eigenvalue of the total magnetization operator

\displaystyle S^{z}_{\rm tot}=\sum_{i}\left(S_{i}^{z}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes S_{i}^{z}\right).

(11)

Consequently, in models with a U(1) symmetry generated by
S^{z}_{\rm tot}
, the projections of the EPR state (
10
) into each magnetization sector become scar states themselves.
More generally, as we will see in Secs.
III.2
and
III.3
and in Appendix
B
, the total
number of scar states into which Eq. (
10
) separates
depends on the symmetries of the underlying bilayer construction.
For example, for a bilayer spin-
\frac{1}{2}
system with only U(1) symmetry, we will see that the number of EPR scars is
N+1
.

III.2
Spin-1 XY Model: U(1) Symmetry

An early example of an exact QMBS tower and the associated periodic dynamics was found in Ref.
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
, which studied the spin-1 XY model on a
D
-dimensional hypercubic lattice.
The model hosts a tower of scar states with anomalously low entanglement entropy, with each scar state obtained from the previous one through a raising operator due to an emergent
SU(2)
algebra.
Furthermore, time evolution from an initial state has finite
overlap with the tower and exhibits
perfect periodic revivals, while generic initial states rapidly thermalize as dictated by ETH.
We will show how these scars are in fact EPR scars.

For concreteness, let us first consider the spin-1 XY model on a 1D chain with
N
sites given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{S=1}_{\text{XY}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(S^{x}_{i}S^{x}_{i+1}+S^{y}_{i}S^{y}_{i+1}\right)\,.

(12)

We take
N
to be even for convenience.
In the following, we map the spin-
1
chain onto a ladder composed of spin-
\frac{1}{2}
degrees of freedom (see Fig.
2
) and demonstrate that the new Hamiltonian obeys the operator equation in Eq. (
9
) after a local unitary transformation.
As a result, the EPR state emerges as a scarred eigenstate of the original Hamiltonian.

First, we replace each spin-
1
operator by a sum of two spin-
\frac{1}{2}
Pauli operators:

S_{i}^{\alpha}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\sigma_{i}^{\alpha})\,,

(13)

where
\sigma^{\alpha}_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{I}
and
\mathbbm{I}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha}_{i}
(
\alpha=x,y,z
) are the Pauli operators on site
i
of the top and bottom leg.

The spin-
1
Hamiltonian then becomes a ladder Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}&(\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma^{x}_{i+1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma^{x}_{i+1})\\
+&(\sigma^{x}_{i}\otimes\sigma^{x}_{i+1}+\sigma^{x}_{i+1}\otimes\sigma^{x}_{i})+(x\rightarrow y)\,.\end{split}

(14)

By construction, the ladder Hamiltonian commutes with the total spin on each rung, which takes value
0
(singlet) or
1
(triplet).
The subsector with all triplets coincides with the original spin-
1
Hamiltonian.
This subsector is defined by the global projector
\mathcal{P}^{(1)}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{i}

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(1)}_{i}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sigma^{\alpha}_{i}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha}_{i}+\frac{3}{4}\,.

(15)

Equipped with Eqs. (
12
)-(
15
), it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1}=\mathcal{P}^{(1)}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2}\right)\mathcal{P}^{(1)}\,.

(16)

At first glance, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
14
) does not obey the general construction in Eq. (
9
). However, there exists a simple local unitary transformation bringing it to the required form. Consider the unitary transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathbbm{I}\otimes\prod_{i=1}^{N/2}\sigma^{z}_{2i-1}\,.

(17)

Under this transformation,
\mathbbm{I}\otimes\sigma_{i}^{x}
and
\mathbbm{I}\otimes\sigma_{i}^{y}
acquire a minus sign for odd

i
.
Now we split the transformed ladder Hamiltonian into terms within each leg and terms connecting the two legs, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\,\mathcal{H}_{\rm XY}^{S=1/2}\,\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{12}\,,

(18)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{H}_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{i+1}^{x}+\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{i+1}^{y}\right),\\
&\mathcal{H}_{2}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{i+1}^{x}+\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{i+1}^{y}\right),\\
&\mathcal{H}_{12}=\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathrm{odd}}\left(\sigma_{i}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{i+1}^{x}-\sigma_{i+1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{i}^{x}\right)\\
&\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,-\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathrm{even}}\left(\sigma_{i}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{i+1}^{x}-\sigma_{i+1}^{x}\otimes\sigma_{i}^{x}\right)+(x\rightarrow y)\,.\end{split}

(19)

This transformed Hamiltonian obeys Eq. (
9
) with
E=0
.
Therefore, the unconstrained EPR state in Eq. (
3
) is a zero-energy eigenstate of the transformed ladder Hamiltonian, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=0
.
Multiplying both sides by
\mathcal{C}
and using
\mathcal{C}^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
, we find that the transformed EPR state

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\ket{0_i}\otimes\ket{0_{i}}+(-1)^{i}\ket{1_i}\otimes\ket{1_{i}})

(20)

is an eigenstate of the original ladder Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=0\,.

(21)

Note that
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
lives in the subsector of
\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2}
that
coincides with the spin-
1
Hamiltonian.
It turns out the above construction can be generalized to the spin-
1
XY model on any bipartite lattice, including the
D
-dimensional hypercubic lattice studied in Ref.
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
.
The only required modification is that the product in
\mathcal{C}
should be taken only over one of the sublattices.
We henceforth restrict to the 1D case for simplicity.

To connect the EPR scar state
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
to the tower of states found in Ref.
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
, we rewrite Eq. (
20
) in the spin-1 language:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\ket{+1_i}+(-1)^{i}\ket{-1_{i}})\,.

(22)

Next, note that
\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1}

(\mathcal{H}_{\text{XY}}^{S=1/2})
conserves the total magnetization
\sum_{i}S^{z}_{i}
.
The projection of
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
into each magnetization sector therefore remains an eigenstate.
Notice that
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
overlaps with only the even magnetization sectors, leading to
N+1
degenerate scarred eigenstates including the two fully polarized states
\ket{+1\cdots+1}
and
\ket{-1\cdots-1}
.
Explicitly, we can write
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=(-1)^{N/2}\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}\ket{\mathcal{S}_n},

(23)

where

\displaystyle\ket{\mathcal{S}_n}=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}}(J^{+})^{n}\ket{-1\cdots-1}

(24)

is the (normalized) projection of
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
into the sector with magnetization
2n-N
.
The raising operator
J^{+}
is given by

\displaystyle J^{+}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-1)^{i}(S_{i}^{+})^{2}\,.

(25)

The states
\ket{\mathcal S_n}
are precisely the scar states constructed by other means in Ref.
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
.
They are degenerate eigenstates of
\mathcal{H}^{S=1}_{\rm XY}
, but adding a magnetic field
h_{z}\sum_{i}S^{z}_{i}
lifts the degeneracy and results in a tower of states with equal energy spacing
2h_{z}
.
The state
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
belongs to the family of initial states shown in Ref.
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
to exhibit perfect periodic revivals with period
\pi/h_{z}
under a quantum quench.

Finally, we remark that the
(N+1)
-fold degeneracy is a generic feature of the EPR scars in systems with U(1) symmetry.
In Appendix
B
, we consider two square-lattice Heisenberg layers coupled by an Ising interaction as another example of a spin system with a U(1) EPR scar tower.

Having studied a U(1) symmetric spin model in the previous section, we now move on to an SU(2) symmetric spin model and show how the SU(2) symmetry leads to an EPR scar tower with a rich structure.
We consider the following bilayer triangular-lattice Heisenberg model consisting of exchange-coupled ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic layers,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}&=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{12}\,.\end{split}

(26)

The individual Hamiltonians take the form

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}\,\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}\,,\,\,\,\,\,\mathcal{H}_{2}=-\mathcal{H}_{1},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{12}=\lambda\sum_{i}\vec{S}_{i}\otimes\vec{S}_{i}\,.

The operators
S^{z}_{i}

\frac{1}{2}

This model has an SU(2) symmetry with commuting generators

\displaystyle

\displaystyle S_{\rm tot}^{z}=S^{z,1}_{\rm tot}+S^{z,2}_{\rm tot}=\sum_{i}S_{i}^{z}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes S_{i}^{z},

(28)

\displaystyle S_{\rm tot}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i}\vec{S}_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\vec{S}_{i}\right)^{2},

which correspond respectively to the magnetization and the total spin.
With SU(2) symmetry, the unconstrained EPR state of
2N

\displaystyle N_{\rm scars}=\frac{1}{4}\times\begin{cases}(N+1)(N+3)&\text{for }N\text{ odd}\\
(N+2)^{2}&\text{for }N\text{ even}\end{cases}\,.

IV
Fermionic Systems

In this section, we apply the general construction outlined in Sec.
II
to spinful fermionic systems, where we take advantage of the internal spin degree of freedom to obtain the desired doubled Hilbert space structure.

IV.1
Fermionic EPR State

A generic spinful fermionic Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is written in terms of the fermionic creation (annihilation) operators
c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}
(
c_{i,\sigma}
) on site
i
with spin
\sigma=\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
.
To directly apply the formalism in Sec.
II
and decompose the full Hilbert space as
\mathbbm{H}_{\uparrow}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{\downarrow}
, one has to be careful about fermionic anticommutation; fermionic operators with opposite spins anticommute with each other, therefore they do not act totally independently on the two copies of the Hilbert space.
To mitigate this problem, we introduce two flavors of spinless fermionic operators
\psi_{i,1}
and
\psi_{i,2}
.
They are related to the original spinful fermions via

\displaystyle\psi_{i,1}=c_{i,\uparrow},

(32)

\displaystyle\psi_{i,2}=(-1)^{N_{\uparrow}}c_{i,\downarrow},

where
N_{\uparrow}=\sum_{j}c^{\dagger}_{j,\uparrow}c_{j,\uparrow}
, and
(-1)^{N_{\uparrow}}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}
is the parity of the spin-up fermions. One can readily check using
\{c_{i,\uparrow},\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}\}=0
that they satisfy the commutation relations:

\displaystyle\{\psi_{i,1},\psi_{j,1}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{ij},\quad\{\psi_{i,2},\psi_{j,2}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{ij},\quad[\psi_{i,1},\psi_{j,2}]=0\,.

(33)

Therefore,
\psi_{i,1}
and
\psi_{i,2}
have
fermionic
self-statistics and
bosonic
mutual statistics.
Hence, one can treat the Hilbert space spanned by the two flavors of fermions as independent and construct EPR scars in a way analagous to spin systems.
The inverse mapping of Eq. (
32
) is given by

\displaystyle c_{i,\uparrow}=\psi_{i,1},

(34)

\displaystyle c_{i,\downarrow}=(-1)^{N_{1}}\psi_{i,2},

where
N_{1}=\sum_{j}\psi_{j,1}^{\dagger}\psi_{j,1}
. In what follows, we shall represent these two flavors of fermions using notation consistent with previous sections, i.e.

\displaystyle\psi_{i,1}\rightarrow\psi_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I},

(35)

\displaystyle\psi_{i,2}\rightarrow\mathbb{I}\otimes\psi_{i}\,.

The unconstrained EPR state Eq. (
3
) in
\mathbbm{H}_{\uparrow}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{\downarrow}
can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\otimes\psi^{\dagger}_{i})\ket{0}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}e^{\sum\limits_{i}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\psi_{i}^{\dagger}}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}e^{\sum_{i}-\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow}}\ket{0},

where
\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\equiv\psi_{i,1}^{\dagger}\psi_{i,2}^{\dagger}=-\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i,\downarrow}^{\dagger}
, and

\ket{0}\equiv\bigotimes^{N}_{i=1}\ket{0_i}\otimes\ket{0_i}

is the
vacuum for
2N
sites on the two layers.
In the presence of U(1) charge conservation, the projection of
\ket{\text{EPR}}
into each charge sector also becomes an eigenstate, leading to a U(1) tower of EPR scars of the following form upon expanding the exponential function in Eq. (
36
):

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}}\left(\sum_{i}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i,\downarrow}^{\dagger}\right)^{n}\ket{0}\,.

(37)

Note that in each charge sector the factor
-\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}
becomes an overall phase of
\pm 1
and can therefore be omitted.
The particle number of the state
\ket{\text{EPR}}_{n}
is
2n
for
0\leq n\leq N
. As a result, there are
N+1
states in the U(1) tower.

In the following, we show that applying appropriate transformations to
\ket{\text{EPR}}
yields two towers of exact eigenstates in the Fermi-Hubbard model.
One unitary transformation, which is well defined only on bipartite lattices, yields the celebrated
\eta
-pairing states
Yang 1989
.
The other transformation, which can be defined on any lattice, yields another tower of eigenstates that was also considered in Refs.
Pakrouski et al. 2020
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
.
We further give the necessary conditions for the EPR state to remain an eigenstate of the most general quadratic interaction, including spin-orbit coupling
Li 2020
and superconducting pairing
Pakrouski et al. 2021
terms.

IV.2
Fermi-Hubbard Model

The Fermi-Hubbard model on an arbitrary lattice with
N
sites is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-t\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\sigma,i}c_{\sigma,j}+\text{H.c.}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle+U\sum_{i}\left(n_{\uparrow,i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,\left(n_{\downarrow,i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,,

where we label sites with indices
i,j
and where
\langle ij\rangle
denotes a pair of nearest-neighbor sites.
The operators
n_{\sigma,i}=c^{\dagger}_{\sigma,i}c_{\sigma,i}
are the standard number operators.
The Hubbard onsite interaction in Eq. (
38
) is formulated in such a way that it invariant under particle-hole symmetry as is the kinetic part.
To check whether
\mathcal{H}
obeys the general criterion in Eq. (
9
), it is easier to rewrite it in terms of the spinless fermion operators
\psi_{i}
. We also define the spinless number operator
n_{i}=\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{i}
.
With these operators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\downarrow}+\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow\downarrow}+\frac{NU}{4}\,,

(39)

where

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow}=\mathcal{H}_{\downarrow}=-t\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}(\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{j}+\text{H.c.})-\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i},

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow\downarrow}=U\sum_{i}n_{i}\otimes n_{i}\,.

When deriving these terms, we used the identity
\mathcal{F}^{2}=\mathbbm{I}
so
\mathcal{F}
does not appear.
Although these terms do not obey the criterion (
9
), we demonstrate below that there exist simple unitary transformations bringing the Hamiltonian to the required form.
We consider two transformations.
The first one, denoted
\mathcal{C}
, is similar to the chiral transformation used in our treatment of the spin-1 XY model in Sec.
III.2
and works for bipartite lattices.
The second one, denoted
\pi
, is a particle-hole transformation and works for any lattice.

IV.2.1
Chiral Transformation

For a bipartite lattice, we can associate each site
i
with one of the two sublattices
A
or
B
.
We define the chiral transformation
\mathcal{C}
to act on one of the spin species (say
\sigma=\downarrow
) and on one of the sublattices (say
B
), so that

\mathcal{C}:c_{\downarrow,i}\rightarrow-c_{\downarrow,i}
for
i\in B
,
or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\mathbbm{I}\otimes\psi_{i}\rightarrow-\mathbbm{I}\otimes\psi_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in B\,.

(41)

The nearest neighbor hopping term in
\mathcal{H}_{\downarrow}
links the two sublattices and therefore acquires a minus sign under the transformation.
The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
40
) becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow}\mathcal{C}=-t\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}(\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{j}+\text{H.c.})-\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i},

(42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}_{\downarrow}\mathcal{C}=+t\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}(\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{j}+\text{H.c.})-\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow\downarrow}\mathcal{C}=U\sum_{i}n_{i}\otimes n_{i}\,.

Using the property (
7
) of the EPR state and
n_{i}^{2}=n_{i}
, one can verify that Eq. (
42
) obeys the general criterion Eq. (
9
) with
E=0
.
Hence, taking account the constant piece
NU/4
, we have demonstrated that the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state is an eigenstate of the Hubbard model on arbitrary bipartite lattices, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{NU}{4}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,.

(43)

The
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state takes the following form in terms of the original
c
operators:

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{1}-\xi_{i}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow})\ket{0},

(44)

where
\xi_{i}=+1
(
-1
) for
i\in A
(
B
).

The Hubbard model separately conserves the number of spin-up fermions
N_{\uparrow}
and the number of spin-down fermions
N_{\downarrow}
.
In the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state, a spin up creation operator and a spin down creation operator always appear together. As a result,
N_{\uparrow}=N_{\downarrow}=n
, ranging from 0 to
N
.
Projecting
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
into each allowed total charge sector yields the following modification of Eq. (
37
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}}\left(\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\,c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i,\downarrow}^{\dagger}\right)^{n}\ket{0}\,.

(45)

This tower of
N+1
states is nothing but the
\eta
-pairing states.
The construction of this tower in the Hubbard model is very similar to that in the spin 1 XY model discussed in Sec.
III.2
, consistent with the connection found in Ref.
Mark and Motrunich 2020
.

IV.2.2
Particle-Hole Transformation

Now we consider another transformation, the particle-hole transformation
\pi
, which we apply to one of the spin species (say,
\sigma=\downarrow
) .
This transformation exchanges creation and annihilation operators,
\pi:c^{\dagger}_{\downarrow,i}\leftrightarrow c_{\downarrow,i}
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathcal{\pi}:\mathbbm{I}\otimes\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\leftrightarrow\mathbbm{I}\otimes\psi_{i}\,.

(46)

This transformation flips the sign of
t
for the spin-down fermions as well as that of the interaction strength
U
. Under the transformation, the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow}\pi=-t\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}(\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{j}+\text{H.c.})+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i},

(47)

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{H}_{\downarrow}\pi=+t\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}(\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\psi_{j}+\text{H.c.})+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i},

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{H}_{\uparrow\downarrow}\pi=-U\sum_{i}n_{i}\otimes n_{i}\,,

which obeys the general criterion (
9
) with
E=0
. Therefore, the
\mathcal{\pi}
-transformed EPR state is an eigenstate of the Hubbard model on arbitrary lattices,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathcal{\pi}\ket{\text{EPR}}=-\frac{NU}{4}\mathcal{\pi}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,.

(48)

The minus sign comes from the fact that the constant piece in the Hamiltonian changes sign under
\pi
.
The
\mathcal{\pi}
-transformed EPR state takes the following form in terms of the original
c
operators:

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}_{\pi}=\mathcal{\pi}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbb{1}-\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\uparrow}c_{i,\downarrow})\ket{\Omega}\,,

(49)

where the particle-hole-transformed vacuum

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega}=\prod_{i}c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow}\ket{0}

(50)

is the spin polarized state in which each site is occupied by a spin-down fermion.
Since
c^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}
appears together with another
c_{\downarrow}
operator, the
\mathcal{\pi}
transformed state has a fixed total particle number
N_{\uparrow}+N_{\downarrow}=N
. However, the total magnetization
N_{\uparrow}-N_{\downarrow}
varies from
-N
to
N
in steps of 2, leading to a new tower of
N+1
states
Pakrouski et al. 2020
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\pi}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!\sqrt{\binom{N}{n}}}\left(\sum_{i}c^{\dagger}_{i,\uparrow}c_{i,\downarrow}\right)^{n}\ket{\Omega}

(51)

with total magnetization
-N+2n
. That is distinct from the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR states which instead has varying charge but zero magnetization.

We remark that in the standard Hubbard model, both the
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{\pi}
towers of states are not regarded as many-body scars, since their existence is enforced by symmetry.
For instance, the Hubbard model has a spin SU(2) symmetry, and the
\mathcal{\pi}
tower of
N+1
states are the unique states with maximal total spin
N/2
and their respective magnetizations.
Furthermore, on bipartite lattices, the Hubbard model acquires another SU(2) symmetry of the charge degrees of freedom
Yang 1989
, and the
\mathcal{C}
tower of
N+1
states are enforced in an analogous fashion.
However, a variety of extended Hubbard models are known in which these enforcing symmetries are broken such that these two towers become genuine scar towers
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Moudgalya et al. 2020a
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
.

We have shown that the standard Hubbard model on a bipartite lattice hosts two towers of states from the
\mathcal{C}
- and
\mathcal{\pi}

To visualize these towers, we consider the Hubbard model on the square lattice in the presence of a chemical potential
\mu

in the

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-t\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\sigma,i}c_{\sigma,j}+\text{H.c.}\right)

(52)

\displaystyle+U\sum_{i}\left(n_{\uparrow,i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,\left(n_{\downarrow,i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

The chemical potential

\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle E^{\mathcal{C}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{NU}{4}-2\mu n,

(53)

\displaystyle E^{\mathcal{\pi}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{U}{4}-\mu+h\right)N-2hn

for
0\leq n\leq N
.

We explicitly verify the two towers by diagonalizing Eq. (

) on a
3\times 2

,

and

Note that the entanglement entropy for the type-I bipartition is interpreted in this setting as an entanglement in spin space, rather than real space.
As expected, the entanglement entropy for the type-I bipartition takes the maximum value allowed within each symmetry sector, while that for the type-II bipartition is markedly smaller for the scar states than for typical eigenstates.

IV.3
Generalized Fermi-Hubbard Models

In this section we generalize the Fermi-Hubbard model (
38
) to include spin-orbit coupling and superconducting pairing terms and derive the conditions under which the EPR state and its
\mathcal{C}
- and
\pi
-transformed variants are eigenstates.
We work on an arbitrary lattice and consider a generic Hamiltonian of the following form

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm k}+\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}\,,

(54)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\rm k}
is quadratic in fermion operators and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}=\sum_{i}U_{i}(n_{i,\uparrow}-1/2)(n_{i,\downarrow}-1/2)
.

\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an eigenstate of
\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}
with eigenvalue
\sum_{i}U_{i}/4
.
The quadratic part
\mathcal{H}_{\rm k}
contains hopping, spin-orbit coupling, and superconducting pairing terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\rm k}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}\left(t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j\sigma^{\prime}}+\text{H.c.}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}\left(\Delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{j,\sigma^{\prime}}+\text{H.c.}\right)\,.

Here
t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}~(\Delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij})
are spin- and position-dependent hopping (pairing) strengths for
i\neq j
, and the Hamiltonian is manifestly Hermitian in this form.
We emphasize that the terms
t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ii}
correspond not to hopping but rather to a chemical potential when
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
or a magnetic field for
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
.
The parameterization of
t
and
\Delta
contains a redundancy that is removed by setting

\displaystyle t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}=\left(t^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}_{ji}\right)^{*},\quad\Delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}=-\Delta^{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}_{ji}\,.

(56)

Demanding that Eq. (
55
) satisfy Eq. (
9
) when written in terms of the
\psi
operators, we find the following constraints on
t
and
\Delta
such that
\mathcal{H}\ket{\text{EPR}}
is confined to a two dimensional subspace spanned by
\ket{\text{EPR}}
and its parity partner
\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}
(see Appendix
C
for details):

\displaystyle

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}+t^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ji}=0,\quad\quad\Delta^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}+\Delta^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ji}=0,

(57)

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}-t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ji}=0,\quad\quad\Delta^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}+\Delta^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ij}=0\,.

Note that the above criteria are not satisfied for the standard Fermi-Hubbard model (
38
)—for example, the above conditions imply that the two spin species have hopping amplitudes of opposite signs.
To ensure that
\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an exact eigenstate there is an additional constraint, namely,
\sum_{i}\Delta^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ii}=0
.

In the most general case, the model (
54
) does not conserve U(1) charge owing to the pairing terms
\Delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}
, and only the global fermion parity is conserved.
In this case, the only eigenstates guaranteed by Eq. (
57
) are
\ket{\text{EPR}}
and its parity partner.
If instead
\Delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}=0
and charge is conserved, we recover the U(1) EPR towers obtained from projecting these states onto each U(1) sector.

In order to connect the general Hamiltonian Eq. (
54
) to the standard Fermi-Hubbard model, we consider the effect of the particle-hole transformation
\pi
and the chiral transformation
\mathcal{C}
on the above conditions.
We first discuss the transformation
\pi:c_{i,\downarrow}\leftrightarrow c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow}
, whose action on the EPR state is shown in Eq. (
49
).
Under this transformation, the constraints Eq. (
57
) become

\displaystyle

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}-t^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ij}=0,\quad\quad\Delta^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}+\Delta^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ji}=0,

(58)

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}+t^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ij}=0,\quad\quad\Delta^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}-\Delta^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ji}=0\,.

Notice that, under this transformation, the spin-orbit coupling and pairing between opposite spins exchange with one another.
As with the constraints (
57
), the constraints (
58
) only ensure that
\mathcal{H}\pi\ket{\text{EPR}}
resides in a two-dimensional subspace spanned by
\pi\ket{\text{EPR}}
and
\pi\mathcal{F}^{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}
. To ensure that
\pi\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an eigenstate we must add the condition
\sum_{i}t^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ii}=0
.

We now make a connection with the
\mathcal{C}
-EPR tower and the
\eta
-pairing states, and specialize to the case of a Bravais lattice in which site
i
is located at position
\bm{r}_{i}
.
We consider the transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\bm{Q}}:\begin{cases}c_{i,\downarrow}\\
c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow}\end{cases}\to\quad\begin{cases}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\,c_{i,\downarrow}\\
e^{-i\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\,c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow}\end{cases}\,,

(59)

where
\bm{Q}
is a generic momentum vector.
The transformed EPR state becomes,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\bm{Q}}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{1}-e^{-i\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{i,\uparrow}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{i,\downarrow})\ket{0}\,.

(60)

This transformed state generalizes the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state in Eq. (
44
)—for example, on the 2D square lattice, choosing
\bm{Q}=(\pi,\pi)
(in units where the lattice spacing is one) results in Eq. (
44
).
Under this transformation, the constraints in Eq. (
57
) become

\displaystyle

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}=-t^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ji}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})},\quad

\displaystyle\Delta^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{ij}=-\Delta^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{ji}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{j})},

(61)

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}=t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ji}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})},\quad

\displaystyle\Delta^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}=-\Delta^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ij}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{j})}\,.

The EPR state
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{Q}}\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an exact eigenstate when
\sum_{i}\Delta^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{ii}e^{i\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}=0
.
Note that, when
\bm{Q}=0
, the above constraints reduce to those in Eq. (
57
).

As a specific example of applying Eq. (
61
), first demand that the couplings are translation invariant, i.e,
t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{ij}\equiv t^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{i-j}
.
Since the phase factors on the
\Delta
constraints oscillate with
(\bm{r}_{i}+\bm{r}_{j})
, breaking the assumption of translation invariance, we set the pairing terms to zero.
We now rewrite the remaining two conditions in momentum space as follows:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle t^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{\bm{k}}=-t^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{-\bm{k}+\bm{Q}},\quad t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\bm{k}}=t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{-\bm{k}+\bm{Q}}\,.

(62)

These constraints are the same as the ones found in Ref.
Li 2020
guaranteeing that the
\eta
-pairing states are eigenstates of the Fermi-Hubbard model with spin-orbit coupling.
More generic Fermi-Hubbard models are realized through the use of the constraints in conjunction with unitary transformations, such as the Hirsch model
Hirsch 1989
;
Hirsch and Marsiglio 1989
;
Duan 2007
;
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Bhattacharyya and Sil 1999
.

The aim of the present section is to formulate the EPR scar construction in bilayer systems of itinerant bosonic degrees of freedom.
In the case of U(1) symmetry, we uncover an
infinite
tower of EPR scar states labeled by the total U(1) charge, which is unique to the bosonic case.
We then discuss different options for the interlayer interaction and comment on experimental settings for possible realization and detection.

V.1
Bosonic EPR State

Consider a bosonic model on a lattice of
N
sites with annihilation (creation) operators
b_{i}
(
b_{i}^{\dagger}
) defined on each site
i
that satisfy the canonical commutation relation
[b_{i},b_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ij}
.
Defining the normalized local number states for individual sites in
\mathbbm{H}_{1(2)}
,

\displaystyle\ket{M_i}=\frac{(b^{\dagger}_{i})^{M}}{\sqrt{M!}}\ket{0_i}\,,

(63)

we can write the EPR state in the doubled Hilbert space as

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes^{N}_{i=1}\left(\sum^{\infty}_{M=0}\ket{M_i}\otimes\ket{M_i}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{N} b_{i}^{\dagger} \otimes b_{i}^{\dagger})\ket{0}\,,

where
\ket{0}\equiv\bigotimes^{N}_{i=1}\ket{0_i}\otimes\ket{0_i}
is the vacuum state.
Note that this state is not normalizable, a feature unique to bosonic systems due to their infinite-dimensional local Hilbert space.
However, in the presence of U(1) symmetry, the projection of this state into each charge sector is normalizable.
Expanding the exponential in the second line of Eq. (
64
), we find an
infinite
tower of states spanned by

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}=\frac{1}{M!\sqrt{\binom{N+M-1}{M}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes b_{i}^{\dagger}\right)^{M}\ket{0}\,,

(65)

which are normalized states with fixed boson number
2M
.

V.2
Bose-Hubbard Models

Now we are ready to construct a bosonic many-body
bilayer model that realizes this infinite tower of states.
Consider the following bilayer Bose-Hubbard model

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{12}

(66)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(t_{ij}b^{\dagger}_{i}b_{j}+\text{H.c.}\right)+\sum_{i}U_{i}n_{i}\left(n_{i}-1\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{2}

\displaystyle=+\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(t^{*}_{ij}b^{\dagger}_{i}b_{j}+\text{H.c.}\right)+\sum_{i}U_{i}n_{i}\left(n_{i}-1\right)

where
n_{i}=b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i}
is the number operator,
t_{ij}
are nearest-neighbor hoppings which in general can be complex, and
U_{i}
is the onsite interaction strength.
The Hamiltonian Eq. (
67
) conserves the boson number
\mathcal{M}=\sum_{i}n_{i}
in each layer.
One choice of interlayer coupling such that
\ket{\text{EPR}}
is an eigenstate is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{12}=\lambda\sum_{i}\left(n_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{I}-\mathbbm{I}\otimes n_{i}\right)^{2}\,,

(68)

which explicitly penalizes differences in particle number between corresponding sites in different layers.
It is then straightforward to show, e.g. using Eq. (
7
), that
\mathcal{H}_{12}\ket{\text{EPR}}=0
, such that Eq. (
9
) is satisfied with
E=0
.

The hopping terms in the two layers are related by complex conjugation with a minus sign, and thus become the same if
t_{ij}
is purely imaginary. When
t_{ij}
is real, the
relative minus sign can be partially removed on bipartite lattices with sublattices
A
and
B
by performing a chiral transformation on bosons in one of the layers,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\mathbbm{I}\otimes b_{i}\rightarrow-\mathbbm{I}\otimes b_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in B\,.

(69)

This transformation commutes with
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{12}
but changes the sign of the hopping term in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}_{2}\mathcal{C}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}t_{ij}\left(b^{\dagger}_{i}b_{j}+\text{H.c.}\right)-\sum_{i}U_{i}n_{i}\left(n_{i}-1\right)\,.

(70)

The
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \xi_i b_{i}^{\dagger} \otimes b_{i}^{\dagger})\ket{0}\,,

(71)

where
\xi_{i}=+1
(
-1
) for
i\in A
(
B
), and the associated U(1) tower of states,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}=\frac{1}{M!\sqrt{\binom{N+M-1}{M}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}b_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes b_{i}^{\dagger}\right)^{M}\ket{0}\,,

(72)

are then eigenstates of
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{C}
.

To visualize the U(1) scar tower of
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{C}
, we perform an
exact diagonalization study. For practical reasons, we limit the number of bosons to
a maximum of
M_{\rm max}
per layer, i.e., we consider the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed version of the truncated EPR state

\displaystyle\ket{\text{EPR}}_{0:M_{\rm max}}=\Gamma(N,M_{\rm Max})\sum_{M=0}^{M_{\rm max}}\sqrt{\binom{N+M-1}{M}}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}

(73)

with a normalization factor of

\Gamma(N,M_{\rm Max})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{N+M_{\rm max}}{M_{\rm max}}}}
.
For the numerical study, we set
M_{\rm max}=3
; we then expect four EPR scar states with
0\leq M\leq 3
. We consider a bilayer square lattice where each layer is of dimensions
(L_{x},L_{y})=(3,2)
.
The site-dependent Hubbard interaction
U_{i}
and hopping matrix elements
t_{ij}
are uniformly drawn from the interval
[0.9,1.1]
while the interlayer interaction strength
\lambda=1
.
Since the states
\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}
described by Eq. (
65
) are eigenstates of Eq. (
66
) with the same energy, we numerically scrutinize a slightly modified Hamiltonian by adding a chemical potential to Eq. (
66
) so that the newly designed Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes n_{i},

(74)

with
\mu=1.0
, hosts a tower of EPR scars with equal energy spacing.

In Fig.
5
(a), we plot the overlap between Eq. (
73
) and each eigenstate of the bilayer system as a function of energy.
The four scars with boson numbers
2M
(
M=0,1,2,3
, with data from different sectors plotted in different colors) are clearly visible and are located at energies
\mu M
.
Note that the overlap of the scar states with
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{0:3}
increases monotonically with particle number,
since the total number of configurations grows as more bosons are added.
We also compute the entanglement entropy for bipartitions of types I and II in Fig.
5
(b) and (c), respectively.
As in Secs.
III
and
IV
, the bipartition-dependence of the entanglement entropy characteristic of EPR scars is again clearly visible.

We close this section by summarizing two alternative parent Hamiltonians for EPR scar states in Bose-Hubbard systems.
We present these Hamiltonians assuming a bipartite lattice so that hoppings of the same sign can be used in both layers; the relevant scar state is then the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state.

The first parent Hamiltonian we consider is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}t_{ij}(b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\text{H.c.})+U\sum\limits_{i}n_{i}(n_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{2}

(75)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{12}

\displaystyle=-2U\sum_{i}n_{i}\otimes n_{i}\,.

This is simply a system of two identical Bose-Hubbard layers coupled by an interlayer density-density interaction.
The hopping terms in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
cancel when acting on the state
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
, however the interaction terms do not as they are invariant under
\mathcal{C}
.
However, the coefficient of the interaction term in
\mathcal{H}_{12}
is chosen such that it cancels the intralayer interaction terms upon applying Eq. (
7
):

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}_{1}+\mathcal{H}_{2}^{*}-2U\sum_{i}n_{i}^{2})\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=-2U\sum_{i}n_{i}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,.

(76)

The leftover term is just a chemical potential that is fixed within each U(1) charge sector.
Therefore the states in the infinite U(1)
\mathcal{C}
-EPR tower Eq. (
72
) are eigenstates of the Hamiltonian defined by Eq. (
V.2
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}=-2UM\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}_{M}\,.

(77)

The second parent Hamiltonian we consider is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{\langle ij\rangle}t_{ij}(b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\text{H.c.})+U\sum\limits_{i}n_{i}(n_{i}-1)

(78)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i}\left[(b_{i}^{\dagger})^{2}+b_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{12}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{i}(b_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes b_{i}+b_{i}\otimes b_{i}^{\dagger})\,.

In this case, the layers are coupled by an interlayer tunneling, which is present in experimental realizations of Bose-Hubbard bilayers
Preiss et al. 2015
.
The U(1)-nonconserving term
\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{i}\left[(b_{i}^{\dagger})^{2}+b_{i}^{2}\right]
in both layers is necessary to retain
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
as an eigenstate in the presence of interlayer tunneling. Such pair creation-annihilation Hamiltonians can be engineered, for example, in photonic
Leghtas et al. 2015
and atomic
Zhang et al. 2021
systems using a coherent state of another species of bosons that can be coherently converted into a pair of
b_{i}
bosons.
Since the operators
b_{i}
and
b_{i}^{\dagger}
are real in the Fock basis, mapping
\mathcal{H}_{12}
onto a single layer using Eq. (
7
) yields
-\lambda\sum_{i}\left[(b_{i}^{\dagger})^{2}+b_{i}^{2}\right]
, which then cancels against the corresponding terms in
\mathcal{H}_{1(2)}
when
\mathcal{H}
is applied to
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
.
Note that the model (
78
) conserves the parity of the total number of bosons—thus, there are two EPR scar states corresponding to the projection of
\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}
into each parity sector.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we elaborated on a construction
Langlett et al. 2022
in which maximally entangled EPR states between two copies of a quantum many-body system give rise to scar states with a distinctive entanglement structure.
We applied this construction to systems of spins, fermions, and bosons and demonstrated that it can be used to obtain several well-known examples of scar states, including the towers of states in the spin-1 XY
Schecter and Iadecola 2019
and Fermi-Hubbard
Yang 1989
;
Mark and Motrunich 2020
;
Moudgalya et al. 2020a
;
Pakrouski et al. 2020
;
Pakrouski et al. 2021
models.
We also demonstrated a qualitatively distinct

infinite
tower of states in number-conserving bosonic models.

Our work motivates several directions of future research.
One direction is to apply the construction to constrained degrees of freedom such as anyons and dimers, the latter of which can be realized experimentally in systems of Rydberg atoms
Verresen et al. 2021
;
Semeghini et al. 2021
.
Our results on bosonic systems also raise the possibility of finding scar states in Bose-Hubbard bilayers
Preiss et al. 2015
or multispecies mixtures.
An important concern in experimental realizations is the ability to prepare a state having large overlap with the EPR state for a subsequent quantum quench, which reveals coherent dynamical signatures of these scar states
Langlett et al. 2022
.
A variety of state preparation protocols are possible depending on the nature of the experimental apparatus.
For example, in hybrid analog-digital setups such as the one detailed in Ref.
Bluvstein et al. 2022
, the EPR state can be prepared using digital gates before performing an analog quantum quench.
An alternative possibility is to prepare the EPR states adiabatically using an appropriately engineered Hamiltonian.
Another intriguing option is to use non-unitary methods, e.g. as described in Ref.
Agarwal et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Bilayer Square-Lattice Heisenberg Model: U(1) symmetry

To emphasize that the number of EPR scars does not depend on the geometry but rather on the symmetries of the Hamiltonian, we study in this Appendix the Heisenberg model on a square lattice utilizing our bilayer prescription.
For this example, we take the Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{I}+\mathbbm{I}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{12}\,,

(79)

where

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}\,\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{j}^{-}+S_{i}^{-}S_{j}^{+}\right)+\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Delta_{ij}S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z},

(80)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{2}=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}\,\left(S_{i}^{+}S_{j}^{-}+S_{i}^{-}S_{j}^{+}\right)-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Delta_{ij}S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{12}=\lambda\sum_{i}S_{i}^{z}\otimes S_{i}^{z}\,.

Notice that, unlike in Eq. (
27
), we assume interlayer Ising—rather than Heisenberg—coupling.
The minus sign from the construction relating the single-copy Hamiltonians is partially gauged away under the transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:\mathbbm{I}\otimes S^{\pm}_{i}\rightarrow-\mathbbm{I}\otimes S^{\pm}_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in B\,,

(81)

which acts on
one
sublattice (
B
) of
one
layer.
Consequently, the intralayer couplings
J_{ij}
in Eq. (
80
) appear with the same sign, while the parameters
\Delta_{ij}
in the two layers are still required to appear with opposite signs.
Similar to the case described in Sec.
III.2
the
\mathcal{C}
-transformed EPR state is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\ket{\text{EPR}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N/2}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\ket{0_i}\otimes\ket{0_{i}}+(-1)^{i}\ket{1_i}\otimes\ket{1_{i}})\,.

(82)

79

Appendix C
Generalized Fermion Constraints

C.1
Deriving the EPR constraints.

In this appendix, we give an explicit example of how the constraints in Eqs. (
57
)–(
61
) are derived and how the action of
\mathcal{H}
on the EPR state is constrained to a two-dimensional EPR scar subspace unless an additional condition is imposed.
As our example, we will derive the third constraint in Eq. (
58
) on the spin-orbit coupling constants
t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}
.
Consider the Hamiltonian under the
\pi
transformation:

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{H}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\pi=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+(t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}c_{j,\downarrow}c_{i,\uparrow}\,.

(86)

In the following derivation, we will use the following forms of Eq. (
7
):

\displaystyle

\displaystyle c_{i,\downarrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}\ket{\text{EPR}}

(87)

\displaystyle c_{i,\downarrow}^{\dagger}\ket{\text{EPR}}=\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,,

which follows from applying Eq. (
7
) to the
\psi
fermions and rewriting the result in terms of
c
fermions
We now give a step-by-step procedure for determining the constraints on
t^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{ij}
:

\displaystyle\pi\mathcal{H}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\pi\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\bigg[t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\downarrow}^{\dagger}-(t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}c_{i,\uparrow}c_{j,\downarrow}\bigg]\ket{\text{EPR}}

(88)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j}\bigg[t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\uparrow}-(t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}c_{j,\uparrow}^{\dagger}\bigg]\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j}\bigg[t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\uparrow}+(t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{j,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i,\uparrow}\bigg]\ket{\text{EPR}}+\sum_{i}(t_{ii}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j}\bigg[t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}+(t_{ji}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}\bigg]\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}+\sum_{i}(t_{ii}^{\uparrow\downarrow})^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}\,.

In the first line above we anticommuted the second set of fermionic operators. In the second line we applied the identity Eq. (
87
) and moved the parity operator to the left using the relations
[\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow},c_{i,\downarrow}~(c_{i,\downarrow}^{\dagger})]=0
and
\{\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow},c_{i,\uparrow}~(c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger})\}=0
.
In the third line, we normal ordered the second term, giving rise to an extra term
\sim\mathcal{F}_{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}
.
To arrive at the constraint, we set the first term in the final line to zero and find that
\left(t_{ij}^{\uparrow\downarrow}+t_{ij}^{\downarrow\uparrow}\right)=0
, which matches the main text.
When this constraint is satisfied, we find that
\pi\mathcal{H}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\pi
maps
\ket{\text{EPR}}
to its parity partner
\mathcal{F}^{\uparrow}\ket{\text{EPR}}
(up to a constant).
To obtain the EPR state as a true eigenstate, we must additionally demand that
\sum_{i}t_{ii}^{\uparrow\downarrow}=0
.
All other constraints found in the main text are derived in a similar manner to the above.
