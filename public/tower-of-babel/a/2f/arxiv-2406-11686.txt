The Role of Inherent Bellman Error in Offline Reinforcement Learning with Linear Function Approximation
Noah Golowich, Ankur Moitra

Abstract
In this paper, we study the offline RL problem with linear function approximation. Our main structural assumption is that the MDP has low inherent Bellman error , which stipulates that linear value functions have linear Bellman backups with respect to the greedy policy. This assumption is natural in that it is essentially the minimal assumption required for value iteration to succeed. We give a computationally efficient algorithm which succeeds under a single-policy coverage condition on the dataset, namely which outputs a policy whose value is at least that of any policy which is well-covered by the dataset. Even in the setting when the inherent Bellman error is 0 (termed linear Bellman completeness ), our algorithm yields the first known guarantee under single-policy coverage. In the setting of positive inherent Bellman error ε 𝖡𝖤 > 0 \varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}>0 , we show that the suboptimality error of our algorithm scales with ε 𝖡𝖤 \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}} . Furthermore, we prove that the scaling of the suboptimality with ε 𝖡𝖤 \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}} cannot be improved for any algorithm. Our lower bound stands in contrast to many other settings in reinforcement learning with misspecification, where one can typically obtain performance that degrades linearly with the misspecification error.

Introduction

The study of
reinforcement learning (RL)
focuses on the problem of sequential decision making in a stateful and stochastic environment, typically modeled as a
Markov Decision Process (MDP)
. An agent aims to maximize its expected reward over a finite time horizon
H
, also known as its
value
, by choosing a
policy
, or a mapping from states to actions. In the
offline
(or
batch
)
RL
problem, an agent’s only knowledge of its environment comes from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
consisting of samples drawn from the state transition distributions and reward functions of the MDP. Given
\mathcal{D}
, the learning algorithm aims to compute a policy
\hat{\pi}
whose value is at least as good as that of some
reference policy

\pi^{\star}
.

A key challenge in offline RL is understanding how to choose the policy
\hat{\pi}
when the dataset
\mathcal{D}
exhibits incomplete coverage of the environment, meaning that the transitions from many states are not represented in
\mathcal{D}
. The naive approach to this problem would proceed via some variant of
value iteration
. At each step
h=H,H-1,\ldots,1
of the time horizon, given a policy acting at steps
h^{\prime}>h
, value iteration uses
\mathcal{D}
to compute estimates of the
value function
of the policy, which maps each state-action pair to the policy’s expected reward starting at that state-ation pair. Value iteration then greedily chooses a policy at step
h
which maximizes this estimated value, and proceeds to step
h-1
. Unfortunately, this approach suffers from the fact that state-action pairs which are
undercovered
by the dataset
\mathcal{D}
may have, due to random fluctuations in
\mathcal{D}
, overly optimistic estimates of their value. The chosen policy
\hat{\pi}
will then aim to visit such state-action pairs, which could in fact be very suboptimal.

A common approach to correcting the above issue is the principle of
pessimism
(e.g.,
[
FMP19
,
XCJ+21
,
LSAB20
,
YTY+20
,
JYW21
]
), which chooses
\hat{\pi}
so as to maximize an
underestimate
of its value, where the underestimate is chosen according to constraints that force it to be consistent with the dataset. By ensuring that
\hat{\pi}
takes actions whose value is robust to random fluctuations in the dataset, pessimistic algorithms typically ensure a guarantee of the following form: for any policy
\pi^{\star}
whose state-action pairs are well-covered by
\mathcal{D}
, the value of
\hat{\pi}
is guaranteed to be at least that of
\pi^{\star}
. The assumption made on
\pi^{\star}
here is typically known as a
single-policy coverage
condition (formalized in
Definition

2.2
); along with several variants, it has come to represent a gold standard for obtaining offline RL guarantees.

Function approximation & misspecification in offline RL.

As the state and action spaces encountered in practice tend to be large or infinite, much of the theoretical work on offline RL makes
function approximation
assumptions, which introduce function classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
for steps
h\in[H]
, whose elements can be used to approximate the value function for a policy. Due to the complexity of the offline RL problem, our understanding of the optimal guarantees attainable remains limited even for the fundamental special case in which the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
are
linear
, which is the focus of this paper. Concretely, letting the state and action spaces be denoted by
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{A}
, respectively, we assume the following: for some known
feature mappings

\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
is the class of functions
(x,a)\mapsto\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w\rangle
, where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
belongs to some bounded set. As an added benefit of focusing on the linear setting, we will be able to obtain end-to-end computationally efficient algorithms, without reliance on a regression oracle for
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
.

It is unreasonable to expect that elements of the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
coincide exactly with the actual value functions for the underlying MDP. Therefore, it is essential to understand the price paid by having
misspecification error
in
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
. To quantify this misspecification error we use the
inherent Bellman error

[
MS08
,
Mun05
]
, denoted
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, which describes the maximum distance between the Bellman backup of any function in
\mathcal{Q}_{h+1}
with respect to the greedy policy and a best-approximating function in
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
. The inherent Bellman error is particularly natural since it is exactly what quantifies the degree to which value iteration succeeds: in particular, the regression problems solved by value iteration are
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
-approximatly well-specified. As a result, it can be shown that, if
\mathcal{D}
covers the entire state space in an appropriate sense and the inherent Bellman error is bounded by
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, then value iteration produces a policy whose suboptimality may be bounded by
\poly(d,H)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}

[
MS08
]
. Moreover, the linear growth of suboptimality error with respect to
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
cannot be improved in general
[
TvR96
]
.

The results of
[
MS08
,
Mun05
]
discussed above serve as a useful sanity check on the reasonableness of low inherent Bellman error, but do little to address the problems encountered in typical offline RL situations as a result of their stringent assumption that
\mathcal{D}
covers the full state space. In most such settings, ranging from autonomous driving to healthcare, one should expect the offline data to be gathered in regions of the state space that result from executing reasonably good policies. (For instance, one should not expect much offline data involving states corresponding to driving a car off a cliff.) Thus, positive results under only single-policy coverage conditions are much more desirable.
Our main goal is to address the following question:
is boundedness of the inherent Bellman error sufficient for computationally efficient offline RL under only a single-policy coverage condition?
Prior to our work, this question was open even for the special case of 0 inherent Bellman error, which is typically known as
linear Bellman completeness
.

1.1
Main results

Our first result is a positive answer to our main question; to state it, we need the following notation (introduced formally in
Section

2
). An offline RL algorithm takes as input a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of size
n
, consisting of
n
tuples
(h,x,a,r,x^{\prime})
, denoting a sample of the transition at step
h\in[H]
: namely, at state
x
, action
a
was taken, reward
r
was received, and the next state observed was
x^{\prime}
. For each
h\in[H]
, let
\Sigma_{h}
denote the unnormalized covariance matrix of feature vectors in
\mathcal{D}
at step
h
. Moreover, for a policy
\pi
, let
\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi}:=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\|\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})]\|_{n\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}
denote the
coverage parameter
for
\pi
with respect to
\mathcal{D}
, which measures to what degree vectors in
\mathcal{D}
extend in the direction of a typical feature vector drawn from
\pi
. We denote the inherent Bellman error of the MDP by
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}\geq 0
.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal version of
Theorem

4.1
)
.

There is an algorithm (namely,
Algorithm

1
) which given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
as input, outputs a policy
\hat{\pi}
at random so that for any policy
\pi^{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}^{\pi^{\star}}(x_{1})-V_{1}^{\hat{\pi}}(x_{1})\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi^{\star}}\cdot\poly(d,H)\cdot\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Moreover,
Algorithm

1
runs in time
\poly(d,H,n)
.

A notable feature of
Theorem

1.1
is the fact that the suboptimality of
\hat{\pi}
scales with
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}
, which contrasts with the
linear
scaling in
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
seen in classic works on offline RL
[
MS08
,
Mun05
]
and recent works studying
online RL
under low inherent Bellman error
[
ZLKB20a
,
ZLKB20b
]
, as well as linear dependence on the misspecification error for other types of misspecification in both online and offline RL settings
[
XCJ+21
,
ZWB21
,
NTA23
,
JYWJ20a
,
WL21
]
.

Note that, in
[
XCJ+21
]
, the realizability error
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F}}
and policy completeness error
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}}
appear in a square root (see Theorem 3.1 of
[
XCJ+21
]
), since the quantities
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}}
actually represent the mean
squared
errors. Moreover, the cube-root dependence on
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F}},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}}
in
[

]
is suboptimal and is improved in
[
NTA23

.
We show below that, perhaps surprisingly, the square-root dependence on
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
cannot be improved, even in a statistical sense:

Theorem 1.2

(Informal version of
Theorem

5.1
)
.

Fix
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and set
d=H=2
. There are feature mappings
\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
h\in[H]
, where
|\mathcal{A}|=4
, so that for any (randomized) offline RL algorithm
\mathfrak{A}
, the following holds. There is some MDP with inherent Bellman error bounded by
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, together with some policy
\pi^{\star}
so that
\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi^{\star}}=O(1)
yet the output policy
\hat{\pi}
of
\mathfrak{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V_{1}^{\pi^{\star}}(x_{1})-V_{1}^{\hat{\pi}}(x_{1})\right]\geq\Omega\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

We emphasize that
Theorem

1.2
establishes a surprising separation between offline and online RL: whereas in the online setting, as mentioned above, one can learn a policy whose suboptimality scales linearly with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, the optimal scaling in the offline setting is linear in
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}
. Thus,
misspecification is more expensive in the offline setting
, i.e., when one is not allowed to adaptively gather data.

Relation to prior work.

[
WFK21
,
Zan21
]
showed exponential lower bounds for offline RL under the assumption of
all-policy realizability
, which stipulates that the
Q
-value function of all policies is linear (i.e., belongs to
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
).
This lower bound is incomparable to that of
Theorem

1.2
: whereas the inherent Bellman error of the instances in
[
WFK21
,
Zan21
]
satisfies
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}=\Omega(1)
(so that lower bounds of
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
are indistinguishable), the instance used to prove
Theorem

1.2
does not satisfy all-policy realizability. Moreover, the lower bounds are unrelated on a technical level.

A recent line of work has investigated a strengthening of all-policy realizability under which offline RL can be achieved, known as
Bellman restricted closedness
(or often simply as
(policy) completeness
). Under this condition, there are statistically efficient algorithms for offline RL with only single-policy coverage, for general function classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}

[
ZWB21
,
XCJ+21
,
CXJA22
,
NTA23
]
. Moreover, given a regression oracle for the class
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
which implements a variant of regularized least-squares, many of these works
[
XCJ+21
,
CXJA22
,
NTA23
]
show that the same offline RL guarantee can be obtained in an oracle-efficient manner.
Since regularized least-squares is computationally efficient when
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
is linear, it follows that, under Bellman restricted closedness computationally efficient offline RL algorithms are known
[
XCJ+21
,
CXJA22
,
NTA23
,
ZWB21
]
.

For a class
\Pi
of policies, an MDP satisfies
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness if the Bellman backup of any function in
\mathcal{Q}_{h+1}
,
with respect to any policy in
\Pi
, belongs to
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
(see
Definition

C.2
). Generally speaking, the above papers achieving the strongest bounds assume
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness for the class
\Pi^{\mathsf{soft}}
of softmax policies (
Definition

C.1
)
[
ZWB21
,
NTA23
]
. While technically speaking such an assumption is incomparable with linear Bellman completeness (i.e., 0 inherent Bellman error), the latter has the advantage of being universal in the sense that it is defined so as to allow the fundamental approach of
value iteration
to succeed. In contrast,
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness only enjoys a similar “universality” property when one takes
\Pi
to be the class of
all Markov policies
, in which case
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness allows
policy iteration
to succeed
[
DKWY20
, Theorem D.1]
.
However, as we discuss further in
Appendix

A
, in this case
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closendess becomes significantly stronger than linear Bellman completeness: in fact, if each state has two distinct feature vectors, then it becomes equivalent to the linear MDP assumption
[
JYWJ20b
, Proposition 5.1]
.

Remark 1.1

(Confluence of terminology)
.

Due to an unfortunate confluence of terminology, some prior works in the literature (e.g.,
[
US22
]
) refer to the setting of
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness when the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
are linear as “linear Bellman completeness”. We do not use this convention, and use “linear Bellman completeness” to refer to the setting when the inherent Bellman error is
0
.

Finally, we remark that in the construction used to prove
Theorem

1.2
, the comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
is not the optimal policy in the MDP. This observation suggests the following intriguing open problem: if one further assumes that
\pi^{\star}
is the optimal policy, then can one improve the
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}
upper bound in
Theorem

1.1
to be linear in
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, or does an analogue of
Theorem

1.2
continue to hold?

1.2
Overview of techniques

Proof idea of the upper bound.

A key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
(the formal version of
Theorem

1.1
) is a new structural condition (
Theorem

4.2
below) proving that MDPs with low inherent Bellman error satisfy
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness for a certain class
\Pi
consisting of
perturbed linear policies
. To understand this result, we first consider the special case of linear Bellman completeness, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}=0
. In this case, we show (as a special case of
Theorem

4.2
) that
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}
-Bellman restricted closedness holds, where
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}
denotes the class of linear policies, namely those of the form
x\mapsto\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta\rangle
, for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Unfortunately, when
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
is positive but small,
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}
-Bellman restricted closedness may no longer hold even in an approximate sense. We correct for this deficiency by modifying the policy class
\Pi^{\mathsf{lin}}
to instead consist of perturbed linear policies:
a perturbed linear policy at step
h
,
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, is indexed by a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a scalar
\sigma>0
. It chooses an action by first drawing a random vector
\theta
from the normal distribution with mean
v
and covariance
\sigma^{2}\cdot I_{d}
, and then chooses
\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}),\theta\rangle
. We denote the set of such policies by
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
.

Theorem 1.3

(
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
-Bellman restricted closedness; informal version of
Corollary

3.2
)
.

Suppose that
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
and
h\in[H-1]
. Then there is a matrix
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
so that, for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\left|\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w\rangle-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)\\
a^{\prime}\sim\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\left[\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),w\rangle\right]\right|\leq\tilde{O}\left(\|w\|_{2}d^{3/2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)\right)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}.

(1)

Previous work
[
ZWB21
]
shows that, under Bellman restricted closedness with respect to a
softmax
policy class, an actor-critic method suffices to obtain offline RL guarantees under single-policy coverage. While Bellman restricted closedness does not hold in general for the softmax policy class under the assumption of linear Bellman completeness (see
Lemma

C.1
), we prove
Theorem

1.1
by adapting the actor-critic method in
[
ZWB21
]
to work instead with the set of perturbed linear policies. To explain the requisite modifications, we briefly overview the actor-critic method: roughly speaking, the overall goal is to solve the problem
\max_{\pi}\min_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi)}V^{\scriptscriptstyle{M,\pi}}(x_{1})
, where
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi)
indicates a set of MDPs which, under trajectories drawn from
\pi
, are statistically consistent with the dataset
\mathcal{D}
. Moreover,
V^{\scriptscriptstyle{M,\pi}}(x_{1})
denotes the value of policy
\pi
in MDP
M
. Minimization over
M\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi)
corresponds to the pessimism principle, and standard arguments
[
XCJ+21
,
ZWB21
]
show that an exact solution to this max-min problem would solve the offline RL task.

To solve this max-min problem in a computationally efficient manner, the actor-critic method uses the “no-regret learning vs. best response” approach: a sequence of policies
\pi^{(t)}
and MDPs
M^{(t)}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi^{(t)})
is generated in the following manner. At each step
t
, a no-regret learning algorithm, called the
Actor
, generates a policy
\pi^{(t)}
; in response, an optimization algorithm, called the
Critic
, chooses
M^{(t)}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi^{(t)})
so as to minimize
V^{\scriptscriptstyle{M^{(t)},\pi^{(t)}}}(x_{1})
. If
T
is chosen sufficiently large, then, as shown in
[
ZWB21
]
, a policy drawn uniformly from
\{\pi^{(t)}\ :\ t\in[T]\}
will have sufficiently large value in the true MDP.

The
Actor
algorithm in
[
ZWB21
]
uses the exponential weights algorithm at each state to update the policies
\pi^{(t)}
, which leads
\pi^{(t)}
to be softmax policies. We replace exponential weights with a variant of the
expected follow-the-perturbed leader (FTPL)
algorithm
[
Haz17
, Algorithm 16]
, which has the key advantage that the policies it produces are perturbed linear policies, as opposed to softmax policies. The optimal choice of the standard deviation of the perturbation turns out to be
\sigma\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}
, which together with
Theorem

4.2
and appropriate modifications of the analysis in
[
ZWB21
]
suffices to prove
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof idea of the lower bound.

To explain the idea behind
Theorem

1.2
, we first consider the following “toy setup”: suppose that
H=d=2
,
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
, that rewards at step
h=1
are known to be 0, and rewards at step
h=2
are known to be induced by a coefficient vector
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}\in\{(1,1),(-1,1)\}
. Concretely, this type of uncertainty in
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}
may be implemented by having a dataset
\mathcal{D}
for which all feature vectors at step 2 are parallel to
(0,1)
; thus, the first coordinate of
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}
may remain unknown.

Consider an MDP with states
x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
so that, for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
(x_{1},a)
transitions to either
x_{2}
or
x_{2}^{\prime}
, but which one is unknown. Moreover suppose that feature vectors at
x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime}
are given as follows: for
a\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x_{2},a)=(1-2a,1-(1-2a)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}/2),\quad\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{\prime},a)=(1-2a,1+(1-2a)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}/2).

(2)

We may ensure that the transitions described above are consistent with the requirement that the inherent Bellman error be bounded by
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, since the feature vector
\phi_{2}(x_{2},a)
is within distance
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
from
\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{\prime},a)
for each
a
.

At a high level, our lower bound results from the following consideration: suppose the policy we wish to compete with at step 2, namely
\pi_{2}^{\star}
, takes a uniformly random action at step 2 (so that its expected feature vector at step 2 is
(0,1)
). Not knowing any information about the first coordinate of
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}
, a natural choice for
\hat{\pi}_{2}
is the “naive” policy that maximizes the reward in the one “known” direction
(0,1)
, i.e., let
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
be the linear policy which, at state
x
, chooses
\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),(0,1)\rangle
. However, this choice suffers from the issue that, due to the
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
perturbation between
\phi_{2}(x_{2},a)
and
\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{\prime},a)
,
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
will choose an action
a
at step 2, whose feature vector is either
(1,1+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}/2)
(if
x_{1}
transitions to
x_{2}^{\prime}
) or
(-1,1+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}/2)
(if
x_{1}
transitions to
x_{2}
). By choosing
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}=(-1,1)
in the former case and
\theta^{\mathsf{r}}_{2}=(1,1)
in the latter case, we can thus force
this naive choice
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
to have suboptimality
-\Omega(1)
compared to
\pi_{2}^{\star}
. This issue stems from the fact that the policy
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
is extremely brittle to small perturbations of
\phi_{2}(x_{2},a)
: a change of size
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
in each of the feature vectors leads to a
\Omega(1)
-size change in the actual feature vector chosen by
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
.

Of course, in this specific example, one can simply instead set
\hat{\pi}_{2}
to be the policy which chooses each action at step 2 with probability
1/2
, which will lead to a policy
\hat{\pi}
whose value is within
O(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}})
of
\pi^{\star}
. Roughly speaking, doing so corresponds to considering a perturbed linear policy, in the vein of
Theorem

4.2
. We can show, however, that such a perturbation must hurt the value of
\hat{\pi}
, for some alternative choice of MDP. Formally, we add transitions to states
x_{2}^{(\ell)}
at step 2 with state-action feature vectors
\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{(\ell)},a)=(1-2a,1\pm(1-2a)\cdot\ell\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}})
, for each value of
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}}\rfloor\}
. A suboptimality of
\Omega(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}})
, with respect to some reference policy
\pi^{\star}
, arises because avoiding it would require
\hat{\pi}_{2}
to act in a way consistent with the naive policy
\hat{\pi}_{2}^{\mathsf{naive}}
(i.e., without perturbation) at states with features
\phi_{\ell}
, for
\ell=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}})
. (At such states, the second component of the feature vectors deviates from
1
by enough that it cannot be “ignored”.) But because the MDP has inherent Bellman error of
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, similar reasoning to the previous paragraph shows that the algorithm’s output policy
\hat{\pi}
cannot act significantly differently at states with feature vectors
\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{(\ell)},a),\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{(\ell-1)},a)
, for each value of
\ell
and
a
. Since, per the previous paragraph, the algorithm must add large perturbations to
\hat{\pi}_{2}
for
\ell=O(1)
, we will ultimately reach a contradiction. There are many details we have omitted from this high-level description, such as ensuring that
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the requisite coverage property with respect to
\pi^{\star}
, and the fact that we wish to allow the algorithm to output arbitrary choices of
\hat{\pi}
, and not just perturbed linear policies – the full details are in
Section

5
.

, we introduce preliminaries. In

, we prove

, and in

Preliminaries

We consider the standard setting of a
finite-horizon Markov decision process
, which consists of a tuple
M=(H,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},(P_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}})_{h=1}^{H},(r_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}})_{h=1}^{H},x_{1})
, where
H\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the
horizon
,
\mathcal{X}
denotes the
state set
,
\mathcal{A}
denotes the
action set
,
P_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}(\cdot\mid x,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denote the
transition kernels
(for
h\in[H]
),
r_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
denote the
reward functions
(for
h\in[H]
), and
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
denotes the initial state. We omit the superscript
M
from these notations when its value is clear.

A
Markov policy
(or simply
policy
)
\pi
is a tuple
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H})
, where
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
for each
h\in[H]
. We let
\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
denote the set of Markov policies. A policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
defines a distribution over
trajectories

(x_{1},a_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{H},a_{H},r_{H})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times[0,1])^{H}
, in the following manner: for each
h\in[H]
, given the state
x_{h}
, an action
a_{h}
is drawn according to
a_{h}\sim\pi_{h}(x_{h})
, a reward
r_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})
is received, and the subsequent state
x_{h+1}
is generated according to
x_{h}\sim P_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}(\cdot\mid x_{h},a_{h})
. We use the notation
\mathbb{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M,\pi}}[\cdot]
to denote expectaton under the draw of a trajectory from policy
\pi
in the MDP
M
, and we write
\mathbb{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\pi}}[\cdot]
if the value of
M
is clear.

Fix an MDP
M
. The
Q
-value function
and
V
-value function
associated to a policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
in MDP
M
are defined as follows: for
h\in[H],x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a):=r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{g=h+1}^{H}r_{g}(x_{g},a_{g})\mid(x_{h},a_{h})=(x,a)\right],\qquad V_{h}^{\pi}(x):=Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,\pi_{h}(x)),

where for a function
Q:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
Q(x,\pi_{h}(x)):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{h}(x)}[Q(x,a)]
. We use the convention that all rewards and value functions evaluate to 0 at step
H+1
.

Given
h\in[H]
, a function
Q_{h+1}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, and a policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
, the
Bellman backup of
Q_{h+1}
with respect to
\pi
is the function
Q_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
Q_{h}(x,a):=r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[Q_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime}))]
. It is straightforward to see that, for any
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
,
Q_{h}^{\pi}
is the Bellman backup of
Q_{h+1}^{\pi}
with respect to
\pi
, for each
h\in[H]
.

The
optimal policy

\pi^{\mathsf{opt}}\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
is defined as
\pi^{\mathsf{opt}}:=\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}}V_{1}^{\pi}(x_{1})
.

2.1
Inherent Bellman Error

MDPs encountered in practical scenarios tend to have enormous state and action spaces. To address this challenge, it is common to use
function approximation
assumptions, which consider function classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
and posit that the value functions for the optimal policy belong to
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
, i.e.,
Q_{h}^{\pi^{\mathsf{opt}}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}
for
h\in[H]
. As our goal is to obtain provable
end-to-end computationally efficient algorithms
for offline RL, without reliance on intractable regression oracles, we focus on the setting when the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
are linear. In particular, for some dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
together with
known
feature mappings
\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we take
\mathcal{Q}_{h}:=\{(x,a)\mapsto\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w\rangle\ :\ w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. For simplicity of notation, we use the convention that
\phi_{H+1}(x,a)
is the all-zeros vector for each
x,a
.

Generally speaking, prior work on offline RL in the linear setting
[
ZWB21
,
XCJ+21
,
CXJA22
,
GNOP23
,
NTA23
]
interprets elements of the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
as approximations of the
Q
-value functions
Q_{h}^{\pi}
, for all
\pi
belonging to some subset
\Pi\subseteq\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
consisting of policies whose values the learning algorithm wishes to compete with. Accordingly, these works make the assumption of
\Pi
-realizability
, which posits that for all
\pi\in\Pi
and
h\in[H]
,
Q_{h}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}
. This assumption is natural in that it is sufficient for correctness of the
Least-Squares Policy Iteration (LSPI)

[
LP03
]
(which assumes knowledge of the transitions and rewards of
M
) for finding an optimal policy
[
DKWY20
, Theorem D.1]
. However, as shown in
[
WFK21
,
AJX20
,
Zan21
]
,
\Pi
-realizability alone is insufficient for offline RL to succeed with polynomial sample complexity under our desired single-policy coverage condition.

In fact, these results rule out offline RL even under a stronger
all-policy
coverage condition.
Thus, it is typical to make the stronger assumption of
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness

[
ZWB21
]
(also known as
policy completeness
), namely that for all
\pi\in\Pi
and
Q_{h+1}\in\mathcal{Q}_{h+1}
, there is some

so that
Q_{h}(x,a)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[r_{h}(x,a)+Q_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime}))]

2.3
The offline learning problem

In the
offline learning model
, the algorithm is not allowed to interact with the environment. Rather, it is given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
consisting of tuples
(h,x,a,r,x^{\prime})
, where
r=r_{h}(x,a)
and
x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(\cdot|x,a)
. We allow the values of
h,x,a
in the dataset
\mathcal{D}
to be chosen in an arbitrary adaptive manner, as formalized by the following assumption:

Assumption 2.3
.

We assume the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(h_{i},x_{i},a_{i},r_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is drawn from a distribution satisfying the following conditions. For
i\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
denote the sigma-algebra generated by
\{(h_{j},x_{j},a_{j},r_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\}_{j=1}^{i-1}\cup\{(h_{i},x_{i},a_{i})\}
. We assume that, for each
i\in[n]
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, the reward
r_{i}
is equal to
r_{h_{i}}(x_{i},a_{i})=\langle\phi_{h_{i}}(x_{i},a_{i}),\theta_{h_{i}}^{\mathsf{r}}\rangle
, and
x_{i}^{\prime}\sim P_{h_{i}}(x_{i},a_{i})
.

We remark that
Assumption

2.3
is essentially the same as Assumption 1 of
[
ZWB21
]
.
Based on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the algorithm must output a policy
\hat{\pi}
whose value,
V_{1}^{\hat{\pi}}(x_{1})
is as large as possible. Of course, it may be impossible to make
V_{1}^{\hat{\pi}}(x_{1})
very large if the dataset
\mathcal{D}
does not include states
(h_{i},x_{i},a_{i})
which explore certain directions of the feature space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A large number of conditions have been proposed in the offline RL literature which capture the degree to which
\mathcal{D}
exhibits good “coverage” properties of the state space. Our bounds depend on one of the weakest such conditions, namely the following variant of
single-policy coverage
, which is identical to that in
[
ZWB21
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Coverage parameter)
.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}
as in
Assumption

2.3
, we define
\Sigma_{h}:=\sum_{i:h_{i}=h}\phi_{h_{i}}(x_{i},a_{i})\phi_{h_{i}}(x_{i},a_{i})^{\top}
. For a policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\mathsf{M}}
, its

for the dataset
\mathcal{D}
is

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi}:=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left\|\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})]\right\|_{n\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}.

In words,
\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi}
measures the degree to which an average feature vector drawn from
\pi
at each step

lines up with directions spanned by feature vectors in
\mathcal{D}
. We refer to
[
GNOP23
, Section 6]
,
[
NTA23
, Section 4]
, and
[
Jia23
]
for a detailed comparison between
\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi}
and other coverage parameters considered in prior work. As discussed there, assuming boundedness of
\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{D},\pi}
is essentially the
weakest
coverage assumption in the literature, as many previous works (e.g.,
[
JYW21
]
) require instead boundedness of
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\|\phi_{h}(x_{h},a_{h})\|_{n\Sigma_{h}^{-1}}]
, where the norm is
inside
the expectation.

In this section, we prove
Theorem

4.2
(restated below formally as

3.2
), which shows that the Bellman backup of any linear function at step

. The main ingredient in the proof of
Corollary

3.2
is

3.1
below, which shows that the expected feature vector induced by a perturbed linear policy at step

is a linear transformation of the state-action feature vector at step
h
.
Lemma

3.1
is a generalization of Lemma 4.3 of
[

]
, which treats the special case of
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}=0
and used the result to develop an efficient algorithm for the related setting of
online RL
under linear Bellman completeness.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that the MDP
M
has inherent bellman error bounded by
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
, and fix
\sigma>0
. Then for each
h\in[H]
and

, there is a linear map

so that for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\left\|L_{h,w,\sigma}^{\top}\cdot\phi_{h}(x,a)-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{h}(x,a)}[\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1,w,\sigma}(x^{\prime}))]\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\ref{lem:ibe-linear}}\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}d^{3/2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{d\log(d/(\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}\sigma))}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\right),

for some constant
C_{\ref{lem:ibe-linear}}

w,w^{\prime},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
for which
\pi_{h+1,w,\sigma}(x^{\prime})=\pi_{h+1,w^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}
for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
L_{h,w,\sigma}=L_{h,w^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}

4.1
Algorithm description

In our setting, the
Actor
algorithm and its associated
Critic
(
Algorithm

2
) function similarly to the
Actor
and
Critic
algorithms in
[
ZWB21
]
. For an appropriate choice of the number of iterations
T
,
Actor
repeats the following steps
T
times: at each iteration
t\in[T]
,
Actor
lets
\theta_{h}^{(t)}
be the sum of vectors
w_{h}^{(s)}
for iterations
s<t
, and lets
\pi_{h}^{(t)}
be a perturbed linear policy with mean vector
\theta_{h}^{(t)}
. The vector
\theta_{h}^{(t)}
represents an aggregation of the pessimistic estimates of the value function produced by the
Critic
at previous iterations. In turn, at time step
t
, the
Critic
(
Algorithm

2
), given
\pi^{(t)}=(\pi_{1}^{(t)},\ldots,\pi_{H}^{(t)})
as input, then produces vectors
w_{h}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
h\in[H]
, which constitute a solution to the optimization problem
Eq.

19
. The program
Eq.

19
has as its objective to minimize the value at the initial state (namely,
Eq.

19a
), subject to constraints that force
w_{h}^{(t)}
to have Bellman backups with respect to
\pi^{(t)}
which are consistent with
\mathcal{D}
(
Eqs.

19b
,
19c
and
19d
).

The main difference between
Algorithms

1
and
2
and the approach in
[
ZWB21
]
is the choice of the policies
\pi_{h}^{(t)}
in the
Actor
(
Algorithm

1
). While we take
\pi_{h}^{(t)}
to be a perturbed linear policy corresponding to
\theta_{h}^{(t)}
and an appropriate choice of the noise parameter
\eta
, in
[
ZWB21
]
,
\pi_{h}^{(t)}
was taken to be a softmax policy corresponding to
\theta_{h}^{(t)}
, with an appropriate choice of temperature. As we discuss further in
Section

4.3
, our choice of
\pi_{h}^{(t)}
implicitly leads
\pi_{h}^{(t)}(x)
to implement the no-regret
follow-the-perturbed-leader
algorithm at each state
x
. In contrast, the softmax policy from
[
ZWB21
]
corresponds to the exponential weights algorithm.
This difference is crucial to allow us to use
Corollary

3.2
to ensure that the program
Eq.

19
is feasible and outputs a pessimistic estimate of the value function
V_{1}^{\pi^{(t)}}(x_{1})
, for each
t\in[T]
. The settings of the parameters in our algorithms are given in
Definition

4.2
below.

Remark 4.1

(Relation to prior work: Bellman restricted closedness)
.

We point out that many works on offline RL consider similar actor-critic methods to ours in the setting of general function approximation
[
ZWB21
,
XCJ+21
,
CXJA22
,
NTA23
]
, generally under the assumption of
\Pi
-Bellman restricted closedness for various policy classes
\Pi
. While some of these results are sufficiently general to be instantiated in our setting (of low inherent Bellman error) using
Theorem

4.2
, which establishes (approximate)
\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
-Bellman restricted closedness when
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}
is small, none of the resulting implications will be as strong as
Theorem

4.1
, either suffering from suboptimal statistical rates or computational intractability.
We defer detailed discussion to
Appendix

A
.

Definition 4.2

(Parameter settings for the algorithms)
.

Given

specifying the dimension, horizon, and boundedness parameters for the unknown MDP
M
, as well as dataset size

and error parameters

and

and its analysis:

•

.

•

•

•

•

•

•

is a constant chosen sufficiently large so as to ensure

4.2

Consider a tuple

\pi

\displaystyle r_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M^{f,\pi}}}(x,a)=f_{h}(x,a)-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}[f_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime}))].

(20)

Lemma 4.3

(Lemma 1 of
[

]
)

Fix any

with

, and write

\displaystyle\forall h\in[H],\ (x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A},\qquad Q_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M^{\prime},\pi}}(x,a)=f_{h}(x,a),

which implies in particular that

The proof of

[

]
. Next, we establish the following straightforward guarantee for

:

Lemma 4.4
.

For any

Review of FTPL.

For

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\int_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mu(\rho)-\mu(\rho-v)|d\rho\leq

We consider the setting of

Lemma 4.10
.

For any
\eta>0
,

Proof.

We first note that

\displaystyle 2D_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathcal{N}(0,\eta^{2}I_{d})},{\mathcal{N}(v,\eta^{2}I_{d})})

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\KL({\mathcal{N}(0,\eta^{2}I_{d})}||{\mathcal{N}(v,\eta^{2}I_{d})})}=\|v\|_{2}/\eta,

Appendix A
Detailed comparison to related work

In this section, we discuss prior work on offline RL with function approximation and compare existing provable guarantees to our own.

Actor-critic methods.

As mentioned above, the most closely related work is
[
ZWB21
]
, which proves an analogous upper bound to ours for the special case of linear MDPs. More generally, the results of
[
ZWB21
]
apply to the broader class of MDPs satisfying
Bellman restricted closedness
, which requires that the Bellman backup of any linear function under
any policy
is (approximately) linear in the features. In contrast, the weaker condition of low inherent Bellman error (
Assumption

2.1
) requires that the Bellman backup of any linear function under the
single policy
induced by that linear function is approximately linear. Intuitively, the fact that Bellman restricted closedness requires that Bellman backups under all poicies be linear places it much “closer” to the assumption of linear (i.e., low-rank) MDPs than to low inherent Bellman error. This intuition is formalized in
[
JYWJ20b
, Proposition 5.1]
, which shows that under the mild additional assumption that each state has at least two distinct feature vectors, then Bellman restricted closedness implies that the MDP is a linear MDP.

Several works have studied actor-critic methods in the setting of general function approximation, beginning with
[
XCJ+21
]
. In particular,
[
XCJ+21
]
considers the setting of a general function class
\mathcal{F}\subset[0,1]^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
and policy class
\Pi\subset\Delta(\mathcal{A})^{\mathcal{X}}
, and assumes that
(\mathcal{F},\Pi)
satisfy approximate realizability and completeness.
4
4

4

Formally, to satisfy the realizability and completeness assumptions of
[
XCJ+21
]
, it suffices that for all
h\in[H]
,
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi,f_{h+1}\in\mathcal{F}}\inf_{f_{h}\in\mathcal{F}}\|f_{h}-\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}f_{h+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
.
Their first result, namely
[
XCJ+21
, Theorem 3.1]
, establishes a
computationally inefficient
algorithm for offline RL based on the principle of Bellman-consistent pessimism. One may use
Corollary

3.2
together with an appropriate choice of
\sigma
to instantiate their result with
\Pi=\Pi^{\mathsf{Plin},{\sigma}}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{(x,a)\mapsto\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w\rangle\ :\ h\in[H],w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
to obtain a computationally inefficient version of
Theorem

4.1
.
5
5

5

The resulting single-policy coverage parameter is somewhat larger than our own, though a subsequent observation by the authors of
[
XCJ+21
]
(see
[
Jia23
]
) leads to a tightening of their analysis which results in a matching coverage parameter.
Finally,
[
XCJ+21
]
establish an algorithm,
PSPI
, which is oracle-efficient given an oracle for
\mathcal{F}
which can solve a certain regularized least-squares problem. However, this result requires taking
\Pi
to be a softmax policy class, for which approximate Bellman completeness does not in general hold under
Assumption

2.1
(see
Lemma

C.1
). Thus,
PSPI
cannot be combined with
Corollary

3.2
to achieve an efficient algorithm under the assumption of low inherent Bellman error. (Moreover, their rate of
O(n^{-1/3})

[
XCJ+21
, Corollary 5]
is suboptimal.)

Subsequently,
[
CXJA22
]
considered a similar setting, with general function and policy classes
(\mathcal{F},\Pi)
satisfying approximate realizability and completeness. Their algorithm,
ATAC
, implements the actor using a generic no-regret learning algorithm, and implements the critic by solving a Lagrange relaxation of a least-squares regression problem. When combined with our main structural result,
Corollary

3.2
, it is possible to use
ATAC
to obtain an end-to-end computationally efficient learning algorithm for offline RL under
Assumption

2.1
. In particular, one may take
\Pi
to be the class of perturbed linear policies, the no-regret learning algorithm to be expected FTPL (i.e.,
Algorithm

4
), and one may implement the critic, which a priori appears to require minimizing a nonconvex quadratic function, using the approach in
[
XCJ+21
, Appendix D]
(see also
[
ASM06
]
). However, due to the Lagrangian term in the critic’s optimization problem, the resulting rate (see
[
CXJA22
, Theorem 5]
) is worse than ours, scaling with
O(n^{-1/3})
. We remark that
[
CXJA22
]
also considers the problem of
robust policy improvement
, which shows that if the data is drawn from a behavior policy, then the algorithm’s output performs nearly as well as the behavior policy, with no assumptions on the hyperparameters, no dependence on any concentrability coefficient, and no assumption of Bellman completeness. Recently,
[
NTA23
]
has given a refined analysis of
ATAC
which obtains the optimal
O(n^{-1/2})
statistical rate, though only in the case when the policy class is the softmax policy class and Bellman completeness holds with respect to this class (which is not the case under
Assumption

2.1
).

Finally,
[
ZRJ23
]
introduces an algorithm,
A-Crab
, which is similar to
ATAC
but incorporates the idea of
marginalized importance sampling
. As we discuss in the following paragraph, this approach cannot be instantiated in the setting of low inherent Bellman error to yield computationally or statistically efficient guarantees for offline RL in full generality.

Approaches via marginalized importance weighting.

In addition to
A-Crab
, many other works, starting with
[
XMW19
]
have considered the approach of marginalized importance sampling (MIS), which introduces an additional bounded function class
\mathcal{W}
consisting of
importance weights
. Elements of
\mathcal{W}
may be interpreted as possible values for the density ratio between the state-action visitation distribution of the policy
\pi^{\star}
one wishes to compete with and the data distribution. A line of work, including
CORAL

[
RZY+23
]
,
PRO-RL

[
ZHH+22
]
,
PABC

[
CJ22
]
,
MLB-PO

[
JH20
]
and that of
[
OPZZ23
]
, makes the assumption that
\mathcal{W}
contains the density ratio for
\pi^{\star}
, amongst other assumptions. Generally speaking, these algorithms implement pessimism for offline RL using primal-dual methods applied to the linear programming formulation of policy optimization. The introduction of
\mathcal{W}
allows many of them to establish upper bounds even in the absence of Bellman completeness.

Several factors prevent such approaches from implying bounds similar to our own for the setting of MDPs satisfying linear Bellman completeness. First, all of the above works assume the existence of an oracle for optimizing over the class
\mathcal{W}
, which would translate into an intractable nonlinear optimization problem in the setting of linear Bellman completeness. More fundamentally, it can be impossible to satisfy the assumption of realizability with respect to
\mathcal{W}
: even if we only wish to compete with a single policy
\pi^{\star}
, there is a class
\mathcal{M}
of linear Bellman complete MDPs so that there is no distribution
\mu_{h}
for which
\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\left\|\frac{d_{h}^{\pi^{\star},M}(x,a)}{\mu_{h}(x,a)}\right\|_{\infty}
is bounded.
6
6

6

For instance, consider a class of MDPs for which an initial state-action pair
(x_{1},a_{1})
transitions deterministically to any of infinitely many copies of some state, denoted
x_{2}^{1},x_{2}^{2},\ldots
, each of which is equivalent in the sense that
\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{i},a)=\phi_{2}(x_{2}^{j},a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
i\neq j
.
Since the above results require that
\frac{d_{h}^{\pi^{\star},M}(x,a)}{\mu_{h}(x,a)}
belongs to
\mathcal{W}
for any
M\in\mathcal{M}
, a bounded class
\mathcal{W}
of importance weights, satisfying realizability for the model class
\mathcal{M}
,
does not exist
.

[
UKLS23
]
analyses a primal-dual approach of a slightly different nature, but with the common goal of relaxing Bellman completeness at the cost of assuming realizability of a suitable class of Lagrange multipliers. Finally,
[
GNOP23
]
uses a similar primal-dual method applied to the LP formulation of policy optimization as many of the above approaches, but only treats the special case of linear MDPs. Due to this additional structure,
[
GNOP23
]
does not need to explicitly make any assumptions regarding a class
\mathcal{W}
.

Linear MDPs: pessimistic value iteration.

In contrast to the above approaches, which phrase the problem of finding a pessimistic value function as a
global
optimization problem, a line of work, including
PEVI

[
JYW21
]
,
VAPVI

[
YDWW22
]
,
R-LSVI

[
ZCZS22
]
, and
LinPEVI-ADV

[
XZS+23
]
has considered a
local
approach to implementing pessimism. In particular, these algorithms perform value iteration but subtract ‘‘penalties’’ at each state which are inversely proportional to how well the state is visited in the given dataset. Since the penalties do not necessarily have Bellman backups which are linear functions, these approaches do not directly generalize to the setting of linear Bellman complete MDPs.
7
7

7

Moreover, a necessary truncation step in pessimistic value iteration presents another obstacle to extending this approach to our setting.
The approach of pessimistic value iteration has also been extended to settings with nonlinear function approximation
[
DZHG23
]
.

Additional approaches for offline RL with function approximation.

An older line of work
[
MS08
,
CJ19
]
has studied offline RL under the stronger assumption of
all policy concentrability
, meaning that the data distribution covers the state-action distribution of
any
policy. These approaches proceed via variants of fitted
Q
-iteration, and therefore require approximate realizability and Bellman completeness in the setting of general function approximation.

[
XJ21
]
show that the assumption of Bellman completeness can be avoided under an even stronger concentrability assumption.
[
LSAB20
]
analyzes pessimistic variants of value and policy iteration with only single-policy concentrability, under somewhat non-standard assumptions regarding completeness with respect to truncated Bellman backups. In the special case of tabular MDPs,
[
RZM+21
,
SLW+22
,
XJW+21
]
have focused on obtaining the optimal polynomial dependence on the various problem parameters, under single-policy concentrability. Finally, several works have considered model-based offline RL
[
RB12
,
CUS+21
,
US22
,
BXB+23
]
, which construct an estimate of all of the MDP’s transitions and rewards (perhaps pessimistically) as opposed to estimating the value functions.

Finally, we mention that in a distinct setting to ours (namely, that of nonlinear dynamical systems),
[
BJP+23
]
use the idea of injecting Gaussian noise into the learned policy to establish guarantees (see Definition 5.3 therein). This technique is analogous to our technique of using perturbed linear policies.

Lower bounds.

[
WFK21
,
AJX20
]
show an exponential lower bound for offline RL even when the value function for
any
policy is assumed to be linear in some known features, and when the distribution of the data has good coverage of
all
feature directions.
[
Zan21
]
shows a similar exponential lower bound, but which is stronger in that it holds no matter how the distribution of offline data is chosen. Finally,
[
FKSX21
]
shows a lower bound for offline RL in a nonlinear setting when the stronger assumption of
concentrability
is made. Taken together, these results may be seen as motivating the assumption of Bellman completeness: when only realizability (as well as an appropriate coverage or concentrability notion) is assumed, little is possible.

.

Appendix C
Bellman restricted closedness

In this section, we show that linear Bellman completeness does not, in general, imply that Bellman restricted closedness holds, even when the policy class is restricted to be softmax policies. We first make the requisite definitions.

Definition C.1

(Softmax policy class)
.

Given feature mappings
(\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d})_{h\in[H]}
, the associated
softmax policy class

\Pi^{\mathsf{soft}}
consists of the set of all policies
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H})
, for which there is some
\eta>0
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that for all
h\in[H]
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{h}(a|x)=\frac{\exp(\eta\cdot\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),w_{h}\rangle)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(\eta\cdot\langle\phi_{h}(x,a^{\prime}),w_{h}\rangle)}.

If
\pi_{h}
satisfies the above display, we write
\pi_{h}=\pi^{\mathsf{soft}}_{h}[{w_{h},\eta}]
.

Definition C.2

(Bellman restricted closedness)
.

An MDP
M
is said to satisfy
Bellman restricted closendess
with respect to
d
-dimensional feature mappings
(\phi_{h})_{h\in[H]}
for a policy class
\Pi^{\prime}
if for each
\pi\in\Pi^{\prime}
, there are mappings
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that the following holds for each
h\in[H]
:

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}\left|\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pi}w\rangle-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{h}(x,a)}\left[r_{h}(x,a)+\langle\phi_{h+1}(x^{\prime},\pi_{h+1}(x^{\prime})),w\rangle\right]\right|=0.

Lemma C.1
.

There is an MDP with
H=2
,
A=3
together with feature mappings in
d=1
dimension which satisfies linear Bellman completeness (i.e.,
Assumption

2.1
with
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{BE}}=0
) but not Bellman restricted closedness (
Definition

C.2
) with respect to the softmax policy class
\Pi^{\mathsf{soft}}
.

Proof.

Consider the MDP with
H=2,\mathcal{A}=\{1,2,3\},d=1
,
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2}\}
, and

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x,a)=1\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},1)=1,\ \phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},2)=0,\ \phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},3)=-1

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},1)=1,\ \phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},2)=1,\phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},3)=-1.

All rewards are 0. The transitions are as follows: for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
(x,a)
transitions to
x
at step 1. By defining
\mathcal{T}_{1}w=|w|
, we may ensure that linear Bellman completeness holds with respect to the above feature mappings: indeed, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}w\cdot\phi_{2}(x,a)=|w|=\langle\phi_{1}(x,a^{\prime}),\mathcal{T}_{1}w\rangle
for all
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Now let
\pi_{2}:=\pi^{\mathsf{soft}}_{2}[1,1]
(see
Definition

C.1
) and
w=1
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\pi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{1})),w\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{e-e^{-1}}{e+1+e^{-1}}

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},\pi_{2}(\mathfrak{s}_{2})),w\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{2e}{2e+e^{-1}}.

Since
\frac{e-e^{-1}}{e+1+e^{-1}}\neq\frac{2e}{2e+e^{-1}}
, and
\phi_{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},a)=\phi_{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},a)
for all
a
, Bellman restricted closedness cannot hold (even up to constant approximation error).
∎
