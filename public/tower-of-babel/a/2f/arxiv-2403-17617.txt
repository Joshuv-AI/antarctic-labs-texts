Topological Levinson’s theorem in presence of embedded thresholds and discontinuities of the scattering matrix
V. Austen, D. Parra, A. Rennie, S. Richard

Abstract
A family of discrete Schrödinger operators is investigated through scattering theory. The continuous spectrum of these operators exhibit changes of multiplicity, and some of these operators possess resonances at thresholds. It is shown that the corresponding wave operators belong to an explicitly constructed C ∗ C^{*} -algebra, whose K-theory is carefully analysed. An index theorem is deduced from these investigations, which corresponds to a topological version of Levinson’s theorem in presence of embedded thresholds, resonances, and changes of multiplicity of the scattering matrices. In the second half of the paper, very detailed computations for the simplest realisation of this family of operators are provided. In particular, a surface of resonances is exhibited, probably for the first time. For Levinson’s theorem, it is shown that contributions due to resonances at the lowest value and at the highest value of the continuous spectrum play an essential role.

Introduction

In this paper we prove a version of Levinson’s theorem for scattering systems with
non-constant multiplicity of the continuous spectrum. These systems appeared first in
[
19
]
.
Levinson’s theorem relates the number of bound states of a quantum
mechanical system to the scattering part of the system.
The original formulation was by N. Levinson in
[
18
]
in the context of
a Schrödinger operator with a spherically symmetric potential.
In
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, a topological interpretation of Levinson’s theorem was
proposed. The topological approach consists in constructing a
C^{*}
-algebraic framework for the scattering system involving not
only the scattering operator, but also new operators that describe the system at thresholds energies. The number of bound states is the Fredholm
index of one wave operator, evaluated via a winding number.

The review paper
[
23
]
describes the topological approach and applications to several classical models, see also
[
1
,
2
,
11
,
27
]
for similar investigations.
A common feature of the examples considered in
[
23
]
, and all previous examples,
is that the continuous spectrum of the underlying self-adjoint operators is made of one connected set, with no change of multiplicity.
The topological approach has also been applied to more exotic situations, for example to Dirac
operators (with an underlying spectrum made of two disjoint connected parts)
[
21
]
,
to systems with embedded or complex eigenvalues
[
20
,
24
]
, or to systems with an infinite number of eigenvalues
[
9
,
10
]
.

The non-constancy of the multiplicity of continuous spectrum incorporated here has various drastic effects: the spectral representation of the
underlying unperturbed operator takes place in a direct integral with fibres
of non-constant dimension; the scattering matrices are acting on different Hilbert spaces with
jumps in their dimension; the
C^{*}
-algebras containing the wave operators and the scattering operator have
a non-trivial internal structure. The notion of continuity of the scattering operator,
which has played a crucial role in all investigations on Levinson’s theorem, has
also to be suitably adapted.

These interesting new features arise in
a family of systems that were introduced and partially studied in
[
19
]
as parts of a discrete model
of scattering theory in a half-space, see also
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
25
,
26
]
.
Here we consider each system individually, meaning
that we have at hand a family of scattering systems indexed by a parameter
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
Each system carries changes of multiplicity in the continuous spectrum,
and accordingly embedded thresholds. The resulting scattering matrices
act on some finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
with their dimension depending on the energy parameter. Even if a global notion of continuity
can not be defined in this setting, it has been shown in
[
19
, Sec. 3.3]
that a local notion of continuity holds, together with the existence of left or right limits at the points corresponding
to a change of multiplicity (or equivalently to a change of dimension).

The
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
containing the wave operator is built from functions of energy (with values in matrices of varying dimension) as well as functions of the appropriate derivative on the energy spectrum. The bound states are computed as the winding number of the image of the wave operator in the quotient of
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
by the compact operators. The quotient algebra consists of continuous matrix-valued functions on non-trivially overlapping loops (topologically speaking), where the overlaps happen along the energy spectrum of the system. The additional non-overlapping parts of the loops appear at opening ​/ ​closing of channels, and restore continuity so that the winding number can be meaningfully discussed.
The proof that the quotient algebra can be viewed in this way is the first appearance of “twisted commutators”
[
5
]
in scattering theory.
As a result, we obtain a numerical equality between the number of eigenvalues of the interacting Hamiltonian and the winding number of the operator made of the scattering operator together with the opening ​/ ​closing operators. Resonances at thresholds (embedded or not) and possibly embedded eigenvalues are automatically included in our computation.
At the
C^{*}
-algebraic level, note that the direct integrals of
C^{*}
-algebras arising here can be studied using the formalism of pullback algebras, see for example
[
8
, Ex. 4.10.22]
, allowing the
K
-theory to be computed.

Let us now be more precise about the content of this paper.
In Section
2
we introduce the model we shall consider
and recall the main properties exhibited in
[
19
]
. Roughly speaking, the unperturbed
system
H_{0}^{\theta}
is a discrete magnetic Schrödinger operator acting
on a cylinder of the form
\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
for any integer
N\geq 2
, with a constant magnetic
field in the
\mathbb{N}
direction encoded by
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. The perturbed system
H^{\theta}
is a finite rank perturbation with perturbation supported on
\{0\}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.
The full spectral and scattering theory for the pair of operators
(H^{\theta},H^{\theta}_{0})

has been investigated in
[
19
]
, and in particular an explicit formula
for the wave operator is recalled in Theorem
2.3
.
Precise formulas for the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
are also presented, and the changes of multiplicity already appear in the spectral
decomposition of
H_{0}^{\theta}
, and hence persist throughout the subsequent analysis.

In Section
3
we define two
C^{*}
-algebras: the
C^{*}
-algebra

\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
which contains (after a unitary transformation) the scattering
operator
S^{\theta}
; and the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
which contains (after a unitary transformation) the wave operator
W_{-}^{\theta}
. These algebras are constructed in the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
, which explains their dependence on
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
This section also contains a first description of the quotient algebra
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}
of
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}

by the ideal of compact operators. In particular, the proof that
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}
consists of matrix-valued functions on a compact Hausdorff space uses techniques to analyse commutators that have not appeared before in the scattering literature.

The quotient algebra
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}
can be thought of as
an upside down comb
with
2N
teeth: the first
N
of them support the operators corresponding to the opening of new channels of scattering, while the last
N
teeth support the operators closing channels of scattering.
The structure of the quotient algebra allows
us to analyse the various possible behaviours of the system at embedded thresholds.
The
K
-theory for the algebra
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}
is investigated in Section
4
. The algebra is efficiently described by assembling matrix-valued functions on intervals by gluing them at boundaries. This allows the Mayer-Vietoris theorem to be used to compute the
K
-theory inductively.

Section
5
contains the newly developed topological Levinson’s theorem,
in presence of embedded thresholds and discontinuities of the scattering matrix.
The number of bound states
\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})
is given by

\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})=N-\frac{\#\big\{j\mid\mathfrak{s}_{jj}(\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2)=1\big\}}{2}+\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\to\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)\big),

where
\mathfrak{s}_{jj}(\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2)
corresponds to a distinguished entry of the scattering matrix at the threshold energy
\lambda^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2
, and where
\mathrm{Var}\big(\lambda\mapsto\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)

is the total variation of the argument of the piecewise continuous function
\lambda\mapsto\det S^{\theta}(\lambda)
.
We refer to Theorem
5.2
for the details and for more explanations.
The proof is based on the usual winding number argument.
Again, its striking feature is its topological nature, despite the non-continuity of the
scattering matrix, and even the change of dimension of these matrices.
In this section, we also describe the behaviour of the scattering matrix
at the opening or at the closing of a new channel of scattering.

Finally, Section
6
illustrates
our findings for
N=2
. In this case, all computations can be explicitly performed.
The importance of the operators related to the opening or the closing of channels is highlighted. The section also contains an illustration of a surface of resonances.
For each set of parameters (which define the finite rank perturbation) in an open set, there
exists a unique
\theta\in(0,\pi)
for which the scattering system exhibit a resonance at
the lowest value of its continuous spectrum. By collecting these exceptional values of
\theta
one gets the surface
of resonances presented in Figure
4
.
For a fixed set of parameters, we also provide an illustration of the non-trivial dependence
on
\theta
for the number of bound states of
H^{\theta}
, see Figure
5
.
Note that a reader interested only in topological results can skip this rather long section,
but we hope that a reader interested in explicitly solvable models will enjoy it.

As a conclusion, this work illustrates the flexibility of the algebraic approach in proving index theorems in scattering theory. The success in dealing with
change of multiplicity opens the door for various new applications, such as the

N
-body problem or highly anisotropic systems.

2
The model and the existing results

In this section, we introduce the quantum system and give a rather complete description of the results obtained in
[
19
]
, to which we refer for the details. The following exposition is partially based on the short review paper
[
22
]
.

We set
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. In the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}:=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

we consider the discrete Neumann
adjacency operator whose action on
\phi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
is described by

\big(\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\;\!\phi\big)(n)=\begin{cases}2^{1/2}\;\!\phi(1)&\hbox{if $n=0$}\\
2^{1/2}\;\!\phi(0)+\phi(2)&\hbox{if $n=1$}\\
\phi(n+1)+\phi(n-1)&\hbox{if $n\geq 2$.}\end{cases}

For any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq 2
and for any fixed
\theta\in(0,\pi)

we also consider the
N\times N
Hermitian matrix

A^{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0&\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\theta}\\
1&0&1&\ddots&&0\\
0&1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&1&0\\
0&&\ddots&1&0&1\\
\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\theta}&0&\cdots&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

These two ingredients lead to the self-adjoint operator
H_{0}(\theta)

acting on
\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
as

H_{0}(\theta):=\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}

with
1_{N}
the
N\times N
identity matrix.

This operator can be viewed as a discrete magnetic adjacency operator, see Remark
2.1
. It is also the one which appears in a direct integral decomposition of
an operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z})
through a Bloch-Floquet
transformation, see
[
19
, Sec. 2]
. We do not emphasise this decomposition
in the present work, but concentrate on each individual operator living on the fibers
of a direct integral.

The perturbed operator
H(\theta)
describing the discrete quantum model is then given by

H(\theta):=H_{0}(\theta)+V,

where
V
is the multiplication operator by a nonzero, matrix-valued function
with support on
\{0\}\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, there exists a nonzero function

v:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for
\psi\in\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}

one has

\big(H(\theta)\psi\big)_{j}(n)=\big(H_{0}(\theta)\psi\big)_{j}(n)+\delta_{0,n}\;\!v(j)\;\!\psi_{j}(0),

with
\delta_{0,n}
the Kronecker delta function.

Remark 2.1
.

H_{0}(\theta)
can be interpreted as a magnetic adjacency operator on the Cayley graph of the semi-group
\mathbb{N}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
with respect to a magnetic field constant in the
\mathbb{N}
direction. In particular, it correspond to choosing a magnetic potential supported only on edges of the form
\big((n,N),(n,1)\big)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In this representation, the perturbation
V
corresponds
to a multiplicative perturbation with support on
\{0\}\times(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
.

We now set

\mathfrak{h}:=\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits\big([0,\pi),\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)

and define a transformation in the
\mathbb{N}
-variable

\mathcal{G}:\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)\to\mathfrak{h}

given for
\psi\in\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)_{\rm fin}
(the finitely supported functions),
\omega\in[0,\pi)
, and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
by

\big(\mathcal{G}\psi\big)_{j}(\omega):=2^{1/2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\cos(n\;\!\omega)\psi_{j}(n)+\psi_{j}(0).

The transformation
\mathcal{G}
extends to a unitary operator from

\ell^{2}\big(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
to
\mathfrak{h}
, which we denote by the same
symbol, and a direct computation gives the equality

\mathcal{G}\;\!H_{0}(\theta)\;\!\mathcal{G}^{*}=H^{\theta}_{0}\quad\hbox{with}\quad H^{\theta}_{0}:=2\cos(\Omega)\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}

with the operator
2\cos(\Omega)
of multiplication by the function

[0,\pi)\ni\omega\mapsto 2\cos(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
.

Through the same unitary transformation, the operator
H(\theta)

is unitarily equivalent to

H^{\theta}:=2\cos(\Omega)\otimes 1_{N}+A^{\theta}+\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)P_{0}

with

\big(\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)\;\!\mathfrak{f}\big)_{j}:=v(j)\;\!\mathfrak{f}_{j}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\big(P_{0}\;\!\mathfrak{f}\big)_{j}:=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathfrak{f}_{j}(\omega)\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\pi}

for
\mathfrak{f}\in\mathfrak{h}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Observe that the term
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(v)P_{0}
corresponds to a finite rank
perturbation, and therefore
H^{\theta}
and
H_{0}^{\theta}

differ only by a finite rank operator.

Let us now move to spectral results.
A direct inspection shows that the matrix
A^{\theta}
has eigenvalues

\lambda_{j}^{\theta}:=2\cos\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi\;\!j}{N}\right),\quad j\in\{1,\dots,N\},

with corresponding eigenvectors
\xi_{j}^{\theta}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
having components

\big(\xi_{j}^{\theta}\big)_{k}:=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i(\theta+2\pi j)k/N}
,
j,k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Using the
notation
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\equiv\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j}|
for the orthogonal projection associated to
\xi_{j}^{\theta}
, one has

A^{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\;\!\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}
.

The next step consists in exhibiting the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
.
For that purpose, we first define for

\theta\in(0,\pi)
and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the sets

I_{j}^{\theta}:=\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\big)\quad\hbox{and}\quad I^{\theta}:=\cup_{j=1}^{N}I^{\theta}_{j},

with
\lambda_{j}^{\theta}
the eigenvalues of
A^{\theta}
.
Also, we consider for
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
the fiber Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda):=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\nolimits\big\{\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid\hbox{$j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ such that
$\lambda\in I_{j}^{\theta}$}\big\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N},

and the corresponding direct integral Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}:=\int_{I^{\theta}}^{\oplus}\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)\;\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Then, the map
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathscr{H}^{\theta}

acting on
\mathfrak{f}\in\mathfrak{h}
and for a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
as

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}\mathfrak{f}\big)(\lambda):=\pi^{-1/2}\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I_{j}^{\theta}\}}\big(4-\big(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{2}\big)^{-1/4}\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\mathfrak{f}\left(\arccos\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right).

is unitary.
In addition,
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}
diagonalises the Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{\theta}
, namely for all

\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}H_{0}^{\theta}\;\!(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\zeta\big)(\lambda)=\lambda\;\!\zeta(\lambda)=\big(X^{\theta}\zeta\big)(\lambda),

with
X^{\theta}
the (bounded) operator of multiplication by the variable in

\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.
One infers that

H_{0}^{\theta}
has purely absolutely continuous spectrum equal to

\textstyle\sigma(H_{0}^{\theta})=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{Ran}}\nolimits(X^{\theta})}=\overline{I^{\theta}}=\big[\big(\min_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)-2,\big(\max_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\big)+2\big]\subset[-4,4].

Note that we use the notation
\overline{I}
for the closure of a set
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.

The spectral representation of
H^{\theta}_{0}
leads also naturally to the
notion of thresholds: these real values correspond to a change
of multiplicity of the spectrum.
Clearly, the set
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
of thresholds for the operator
H_{0}^{\theta}

is given by

\mathcal{T}^{\theta}:=\big\{\lambda_{j}^{\theta}\pm 2\mid j\in\{1,\dots,N\}\big\}.

(2.1)

Remark 2.2
.

By looking at these thresholds, one observes that there is a qualitative change between
these thresholds for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and for
\theta=0
or
\theta=\pi
.
Indeed, for
\theta\in\{0,\pi\}
, some of these thresholds coincide, while it is not the case for

\theta\in(0,\pi)
. This multiplicity different from
1
has
several implications in the following constructions, and for simplicity we have decided
not to consider these pathological cases here.
Note also that the matrices obtained for
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
are unitarily equivalent to some matrices with
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. Again for simplicity, we restrict our attention
to
\theta\in(0,\pi)
only.

The main spectral result for
H^{\theta}
has been presented in
[
19
, Prop. 1.2]
,
which we recall when necessary, see Proposition
6.9
. About scattering theory, since the difference
H^{\theta}-H^{\theta}_{0}
is a finite rank
operator we observe that the wave operators

W_{\pm}^{\theta}:=\slim_{t\to\pm\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH^{\theta}}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH^{\theta}_{0}}

exist and are complete, see
[
13
, Thm X.4.4]
. As a consequence, the scattering operator

S^{\theta}:=(W_{+}^{\theta})^{*}W_{-}^{\theta}

is a unitary operator in
\mathfrak{h}
commuting with
H^{\theta}_{0}
, and thus
S^{\theta}
is
decomposable in the spectral representation of
H^{\theta}_{0}
, that is
for
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and a.e.
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
, one has

\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}S^{\theta}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\zeta\big)(\lambda)=S^{\theta}(\lambda)\zeta(\lambda),

with the scattering matrix
S^{\theta}(\lambda)
a unitary operator in
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)
.

For
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and for

\lambda\in\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)

let us define the channel scattering matrix

S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{jj^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}S^{\theta}(\lambda)\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}

and consider the map

\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)\ni\lambda\mapsto S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{jj^{\prime}}\in\mathscr{B}\big(\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\mathbb{C}^{N};\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\mathbb{C}^{N}\big).

The continuity of the scattering matrix at embedded eigenvalues has been shown in

[
19
, Thm 3.10]
, while its behaviour at thresholds has been studied in

[
19
, Thm 3.9]
.
This latter result can be summarised as follows: For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
,
a channel can already be opened at the
energy
\lambda
(in which case the existence and the equality of the
limits from the right and from the left is proved), it can open at the energy
\lambda
(in
which case only the existence of the limit from the right is proved), or it can
close at the energy
\lambda
(in which case only the existence of the
limit from the left is proved).

Let us finally return to the wave operator
W_{-}^{\theta}
, which will be further investigated in the sequel. By using the stationary approach of scattering theory
and by looking at the representation of the wave operator inside
the spectral representation of
H_{0}^{\theta}
, the operator
W_{-}^{\theta}

can be explicitely computed, modulo compact operators. More precisely
if we set
\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})
for the set of compact operators on
\mathfrak{h}
, then
the following statement has been proved in
[
19
, Thm 1.5]
:

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, one has the equality

W_{-}^{\theta}-1=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi\mathfrak{D})-i\cosh(\pi\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\tanh(\mathfrak{X})\big)(S^{\theta}-1)+\mathfrak{K}^{\theta},

with
\mathfrak{K}^{\theta}\in\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})
and where
\mathfrak{X}
and
\mathfrak{D}
are representations
of the canonical position and momentum operators in the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}
.

Let us emphasise that this result is at the root of the subsequent investigations.

3
The
C^{*}
-algebraic framework

In the section we construct the algebraic framework and a
C^{*}
-algebra
which is going to contain the operator
W_{-}^{\theta}
for any fixed
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.
We also compute the quotient of this
C^{*}
-algebra by the ideal of compact operators.
Most of the notations are directly borrowed from Section
2
.

We firstly exhibit an algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
of multiplication operators acting on
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}\subset\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
with
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
if for any
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big[a\zeta\big](\lambda)\equiv\big[a(X^{\theta})\zeta\big](\lambda)=a(\lambda)\zeta(\lambda)

and for
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the maps

I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\ni\lambda\mapsto a_{jj^{\prime}}(\lambda):=\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}a(\lambda)\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\in\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}(\lambda)\big)\subset\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})

(3.1)

belongs to
C_{0}\left(I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta};\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\right)
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
while it belongs to
C\left(\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}};\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\right)
if
j=j^{\prime}
.

In other words, the function defined in (
3.1
)
is a continuous function vanishing at the boundaries of the intersection
I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}
when these two intervals are not equal, and has limits at the boundaries of these intervals when they coincide.

Remark 3.2
.

In the sequel, we shall use the notation
a_{jj^{\prime}}
for
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}a\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}

even if this notation is slightly misleading. Indeed,
a_{jj^{\prime}}(\lambda)
exists if and only if

\lambda\in\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}}
.
If
\lambda\not\in\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}}
or if
\lambda\not\in\overline{I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}}
, then the expression containing

a_{jj^{\prime}}(\lambda)
should be interpreted as
0
.
More precisely, with the standard bra-ket notation, we shall write

a_{jj^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|
,
where
\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}
denotes the multiplication operator by a function belonging to

C_{0}\left(I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta};\mathbb{C}\right)
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
, while it belongs to
C\big(\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}};\mathbb{C}\big)
if
j=j^{\prime}
.
Thus, with these notations and for any
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

a(\lambda)=\sum_{\{j,j^{\prime}\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j},\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\}}\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}(\lambda)\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|

By the above abuse of notation, we shall conveniently write

a=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\otimes\;\!\frac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|
.

The interest of the algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
comes from the following statement, which can be directly inferred from

​:

Proposition 3.3
.

The multiplication operator defined by the map

belongs to
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
.

For subsequent constructions, we need to know that some specific functions also belong
to
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
. For this, we recall the definition of a useful unitary map, introduced in

[
19
, Sec. 4.1]
.
For
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
we define the unitary map

\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
given on
\zeta\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})
and for

by

\big[\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\zeta\big](s):=\tfrac{2^{1/2}}{\cosh(s)}\;\!\zeta\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(s)\big).

(3.2)

Its adjoint
(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})
is then given on

\big[(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}f\big](\lambda)=\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}f\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right).

(3.3)

Based on this, we define the unitary map

\mathcal{V}^{\theta}:\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
acting on
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
as

\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\zeta|_{I^{\theta}_{j}},

with adjoint
(\mathcal{V}^{\theta})^{*}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})\to\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
acting on

f\in\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
and for
\lambda\in I^{\theta}

\big[(\mathcal{V}^{\theta})^{*}f\big](\lambda)=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big((\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)f\right](\lambda).

In the sequel, let
X
denote the self-adjoint operator of multiplication by the variable in

\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
.
We are now ready to prove the following lemma:

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big[\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda)=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\tfrac{X^{\theta}-\lambda^{\theta}_{j}}{2}\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}\zeta\right](\lambda).

Proof.

From the definitions of the unitary maps one infers that

\displaystyle\big[\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big((\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\right](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big[\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\big]\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\big[\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\zeta\big]\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\tfrac{2^{-1/2}}{\left(1-\Big(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\Big)^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\frac{2^{1/2}}{\cosh\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)}\big[\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\big[\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda),

where the relation
\frac{1}{\cosh(s)^{2}}=1-\tanh(s)^{2}
has been used for the last equality.
This leads directly to the statement.
∎

As a consequence of this lemma one easily infers that

\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}
belongs to the algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
introduced above. In fact, in the matrix formulation mentioned in Remark

3.2
, this operator corresponds to a diagonal multiplication operator.

Our next aim is to introduce a
C^{*}
-algebra containing the wave operators
W_{\pm}^{\theta}
.
For that purpose, we introduce in the Hilbert space
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
a specific family of convolution operators. More precisely, let
D
stand for the self-adjoint realization of the operator
-i\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
. Clearly,
X
and
D
satisfy the canonical commutation relation. By functional calculus, we then define the bounded convolution operator
\eta(D)
with
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
, the algebra of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
having a limit at
-\infty
and vanishing at
+\infty
.
In
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{N})
we finally introduce the family of operators

\eta(D)\otimes 1_{N}

with
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
.
These operators naturally generate a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{C}
isomorphic to
C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
.
Clearly, the operators
\frac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi D)\big)\otimes 1_{N}
and
\cosh(\pi D)^{-1}\otimes 1_{N}
belong to

\mathcal{C}
. These two operators will subsequently play an important role.

For
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
, let us now look at the image of

\eta(D)\otimes 1_{N}
in
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.
For this, we recall that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the first Sobolev space on
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

(i)

For
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the operator

D^{\theta}_{j}:=\big(\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{*}D\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}
is self-adjoint on
(\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
and the following equality holds:

D^{\theta}_{j}=2\bigg(1-\Big(\tfrac{X^{\theta}_{j}-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\Big)^{2}\bigg)\left(-i\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\right)+i\Big(\tfrac{X^{\theta}_{j}-\lambda^{\theta}_{j}}{2}\Big)

where
X^{\theta}_{j}
denotes the operator of multiplication by the variable in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})
,

(ii)

For any
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
we set

\eta(D^{\theta}):=\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\eta(D)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta},

(3.4)

and for
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and for
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\big[\eta(D^{\theta})\zeta\big](\lambda)=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\big[\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\zeta\big](\lambda).

Proof.

i) The self-adjointness of
D^{\theta}_{j}
on
(\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
directly follows from the self-adjointness of
D
on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, for
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}\big(I_{j}^{\theta}\big)
one has

\displaystyle\big[\big(\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{*}D\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\big[D\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\zeta\big]\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\big[\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\zeta\big]^{\prime}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-i\left(\tfrac{2}{4-(\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left[\tfrac{2^{1/2}}{\cosh(\cdot)}\zeta\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(\cdot)\big)\right]^{\prime}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-i\tfrac{1}{\left(1-\Big(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\Big)^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\Big[\tfrac{-\tanh(\cdot)}{\cosh(\cdot)}\zeta\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(\cdot)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\tfrac{1-\tanh(\cdot)^{2}}{\cosh(\cdot)}\zeta^{\prime}\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(\cdot)\big)\Big]\left(\mathop{\mathrm{arctanh}}\nolimits\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=i\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\zeta(\lambda)-2i\bigg(1-\left(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda_{j}^{\theta}}{2}\right)^{2}\bigg)\;\!\zeta^{\prime}(\lambda),

which leads directly to the statement.

ii) For
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\displaystyle\big[\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\eta(D)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big((\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\big(\eta(D)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\right](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}\eta(D)\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\zeta\right](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j})^{*}\eta(D)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}_{j}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\zeta\right](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\left[\big(\eta(D_{j}^{\theta})\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)\zeta\right](\lambda),

which corresponds to the statement.
∎

Let us now introduce our main
C^{*}
-algebra. We recall that
\eta(D^{\theta})
has been introduced in (
3.4
).
We set

\mathcal{E}^{\theta}:=C^{*}\Big(\eta(D^{\theta})\;\!a+b\mid\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big),a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta},b\in\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)\Big)^{+}

which is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)

containing the ideal
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
of compact operators on
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
.
Here, the exponent
+
means that
\mathbb{C}
times the identity of
\mathscr{B}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
have been added to the algebra, turning it into a unital
C^{*}
-algebra.
Observe that for a typical element

\eta(D^{\theta})\;\!a
with
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
and
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
,
and for
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
and
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
, one has

\displaystyle\big[\eta(D^{\theta})\;\!a\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\big[\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\otimes\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\big)a\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\big[\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!a_{jj^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N}\big[\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\big)\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|\zeta\big](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\big[\big(\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\big)\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|\zeta\big](\lambda),

(3.5)

where the notation introduced in Remark
3.2
has been used.

Our main interest in this
C^{*}
-algebra is based on the following result, which is a direct
consequence of Theorem
2.3
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, the wave operators
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}W_{-}^{\theta}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}
belongs to
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
.

Proof.

Let us start by recalling the main formula for the wave operator valid for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
​:

W_{-}^{\theta}-1=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi\mathfrak{D})-i\cosh(\pi\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\tanh(\mathfrak{X})\big)(S^{\theta}-1)+\mathfrak{K}^{\theta},

with
\mathfrak{K}^{\theta}\in\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})
. By looking at the r.h.s. through the unitary conjugation by
\mathscr{F}^{\theta}
, one ends up with the expression in
\mathscr{H}^{\theta}
​:

\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1-\tanh(\pi D^{\theta})-i\cosh(\pi D^{\theta})^{-1}\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\Big)(S^{\theta}(X^{\theta})-1)+k^{\theta},

(3.6)

with
k^{\theta}\in\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
.

Clearly, the functions
1-\tanh(\pi\cdot)
and
\cosh(\pi\cdot)^{-1}
belong to

C_{0}\big([-\infty,+\infty)\big)
.
As already mentioned in Proposition
3.3
, the map
\lambda\mapsto S^{\theta}(\lambda)
belongs
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
, and the same is true for the constant function
1
. As a consequence, the map
\lambda\mapsto S^{\theta}(\lambda)-1
belongs to
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
, or equivalently the multiplication operator
S^{\theta}(X^{\theta})-1
belongs to
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
.
Furthermore and as mentioned after Lemma
3.4
, the operator

\big(\mathcal{V}^{\theta}\big)^{*}\big(\tanh(X)\otimes 1_{N}\big)\mathcal{V}^{\theta}
also belongs to the
algebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
.
Thus, the leading term in (
3.6
) belongs to
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
by inspection of its different factors, and the remainder term
k^{\theta}
also belongs to
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
because it is a compact contribution.
∎

Our next task is to compute the quotient of the algebra
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}
by the set of compact operators.
For that purpose, we firstly show that if some restrictions are imposed on the functions
\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}
and
\eta
appearing in (
3.5
),
then the corresponding operator is compact. For this we introduce an ideal in
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
, namely

\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{0}:=\Big\{a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}\mid a_{jj}\in C_{0}\left(I_{j}^{\theta};\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\right)\hbox{ for all }j\in\{1,\dots,N\}\Big\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{0}
and
\eta\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
the operator
\eta(D^{\theta})a
belongs
to
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
.

Proof.

Let us first observe that the condition
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{0}
means that all multiplication operators

\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}
introduced in Remark
3.2
are defined by functions in
C_{0}\left(I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta};\mathbb{C}\right)
, for all
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Thus, according to (
3.5
) it is enough to show that each operator

\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\,\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}:\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta})\to\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})
is compact. However, since
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\subset\overline{I_{j}^{\theta}\cap I_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}}
,
it is simpler to show that
\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\,\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\in\mathscr{K}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(I_{j}^{\theta})\big)
.
Now, if we apply the unitary transforms defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)
one gets

\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\;\!\eta(D^{\theta}_{j})\,\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\;\!\big(\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\theta}\big)^{*}=\eta(D)\;\!\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(X)\big).

(3.7)

Since
\eta
and
\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}\big(\lambda_{j}^{\theta}+2\tanh(\cdot)\big)
belong to
C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
the r.h.s. of (
3.7
) is known to be a compact operator in
\mathop{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R})
.
Thus, each summand in (
3.5
) is unitarily equivalent to a compact operator, which means
that
\eta(D^{\theta})a
is compact.
∎

Let us supplement the previous result with a compactness result about commutators.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,\infty)\big)
and
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
, the commutator

[\eta(D^{\theta}),a]
belongs to
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathscr{H}^{\theta}\big)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
F^{\theta}:=D^{\theta}\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}
has compact commutator with elements of
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
. For if this is true, then any polynomial in
F^{\theta}
compactly commutes with
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
.
If
\eta\in C_{0}\big([-\infty,\infty)\big)
then there exists a unique
h\in C_{0}\big([-1,1)\big)
such that
\eta(D^{\theta})=h(F^{\theta})
. By Stone-Weierstrass theorem, any
h\in C_{0}\big([-1,1)\big)
can be approximated by a polynomial on
(-1,1)
. Then, one concludes the proof by recalling that the set of compact operators is norm closed.

Now, with the notation of Remark
3.2
and for any
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
,
let us define
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)
by

\big[\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big](\lambda):=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\mathfrak{a}_{jj}(\lambda)\otimes\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j},\qquad\forall\lambda\in I^{\theta}.

We also consider the diagonal multiplication operator
\Lambda\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{0}

given for any
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
by

\Lambda^{\theta}(\lambda):=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\Lambda^{\theta}_{j}(\lambda)\otimes\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}

with
\Lambda^{\theta}_{j}(\lambda):=1-\Big(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda^{\theta}_{j}}{2}\Big)^{2}
.
For
\epsilon>0
small, let
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
be an open
\epsilon
-neighbourhood of
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
, where the set of thresholds
\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
has been defined in (
2.1
).
We then set
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
for the set of
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}

satisfying
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\big(a-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big)\subset I^{\theta}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
.
For
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
we define

\rho^{\theta}(a):={\rm diag}(a)+\Lambda^{\theta}\big(a-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big)\big(\Lambda^{\theta}\big)^{-1}.

With the notations introduced before it means that for any
\lambda\in I^{\theta}
one has

\displaystyle\big[\rho^{\theta}(a)\big](\lambda)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}\mathfrak{a}_{jj}(\lambda)\otimes\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\{j\neq j^{\prime}\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j},\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\}}\Lambda^{\theta}_{j}(\lambda)\mathfrak{a}_{jj^{\prime}}(\lambda)\big(\Lambda^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}(\lambda)\big)^{-1}\otimes\;\!\tfrac{1}{N}|\xi^{\theta}_{j}\rangle\langle\xi^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}|.

One observes that
\rho^{\theta}
is an algebra homomorphism on the subalgebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
,
but it is not a
*
-homomorphism. With a grain of salt, the expression
\rho^{\theta}(a)

could simply be written
\Lambda^{\theta}a\big(\Lambda^{\theta}\big)^{-1}
, but the diagonal
elements have to be suitably interpreted (the cancellation of two factors before the evaluation
at thresholds).

Let us also consider the subalgebra
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon,1}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
of those
a
whose derivatives
a^{\prime}
exist, are continuous and satisfy

\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits(a^{\prime})\subset I^{\theta}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
.
Our interest in this algebra is that any element of
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}
is a norm limit of elements in
\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon,1}
as
\epsilon\searrow 0
.
If we define the additional diagonal multiplication operator

\Omega^{\theta}(\lambda)=\sum_{\{j\mid\lambda\in I^{\theta}_{j}\}}i\Big(\tfrac{\lambda-\lambda^{\theta}_{j}}{2}\Big)\otimes\;\!\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j},\qquad\forall\lambda\in I^{\theta},

then one readily checks that for
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\theta}_{\epsilon,1}
one has in the form sense
on the domain of
D^{\theta}

\displaystyle D^{\theta}a-\rho^{\theta}(a)D^{\theta}

\displaystyle=-2i\Lambda^{\theta}a^{\prime}+\Omega^{\theta}a-\rho^{\theta}(a)\Omega^{\theta}

\displaystyle=-2i\Lambda^{\theta}a^{\prime}+\Omega^{\theta}a-\rho^{\theta}\big(a-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big)\Omega^{\theta}-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\Omega^{\theta}

\displaystyle=-2i\Lambda^{\theta}a^{\prime}+\Omega^{\theta}\big(a-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big)-\rho^{\theta}\big(a-\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\nolimits(a)\big)\Omega^{\theta}.

Clearly, the resulting expression is bounded, and the same notation is kept
for the bounded operator extending it continuously. One also observes that
this operator corresponds to a multiplication operator with compact support.
By adapting the argument of Connes-Moscovici
[
5
, Prop. 3.2]
we finally write

\displaystyle\big[F^{\theta},a\big]=

\displaystyle\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}D^{\theta}a-a\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}D^{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\big(D^{\theta}a-\rho^{\theta}(a)D^{\theta}\big)

(3.8)

\displaystyle+\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\Big(\rho^{\theta}(a)-\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{1/2}a\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\Big)D^{\theta}.

(3.9)

In order to analyse these terms, let us firstly observe that
(D^{\theta})^{2}
is a second order uniformly elliptic pseudodifferential operator on
I^{\theta}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
, and so the inverse of
1+(D^{\theta})^{2}
is a pseudodifferential operator of order
-2
.
By using the formula

\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-1/2}\big(1+\lambda+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

one then infers that
\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{-1/2}
is a pseudodifferential operator of order
-1
, and accordingly that
\big(1+(D^{\theta})^{2}\big)^{1/2}
is a pseudodifferential operator of order
1
.
Note that these properties hold locally on the domain
I^{\theta}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
.

Now, the term in (
3.8
) is a compact operator since the second factor is a multiplication operator with support in
I^{\theta}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\theta}_{\epsilon}
and since the first factor is the pseudodifferential operator of order
-1
studied above.
For the term (

5
Topological Levinson’s theorem

In this section we provide the topolgical version of Levinson’s theorem, linking
the number of bound states to an expression involving the image of
W^{\theta}_{-}

in the quotient algebra. First of all, we provide a statement about the behavior
of the scattering matrix at thresholds. It shows that the expression obtained for
the wave operator
W_{-}^{\theta}
is rather rigid and imposes a strict behaviour at thresholds.
For its statement we define the scalar valued function
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{jj}^{\theta}
by the relation

\tilde{S}^{\theta}(\lambda)_{jj}=:\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{jj}^{\theta}(\lambda)\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{j}^{\theta}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
one has
\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}\pm 2)\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

Since
W_{-}^{\theta}
is a Fredholm operator of norm 1, the image

q^{\theta}\big(\mathscr{F}^{\theta}W_{-}^{\theta}(\mathscr{F}^{\theta})^{*}\big)
is a unitary operator, and therefore
its restrictions on all components of
\mathcal{Q}^{\theta}
must be unitary.
The restrictions on
\rightarrow_{j}
,
\rightarrow_{N}
, and
\rightarrow^{j}
do not impose
any conditions, since the scattering operator is unitary valued, see Lemma
3.11
.
On the other hand, by checking that the restrictions on
\downarrow_{j}
and
on
\uparrow^{j}
, the conditions appearing in the statement have to be imposed.

Starting with (
3.16
), the restriction on
\downarrow_{j}
, we can rewrite this operator
as a scalar function multiplying a rank one projection. By imposing that the operator is unitary valued, one infers that

\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\eta_{-}(s)\big)\big({\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}-2)-1\big)\Big)\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\eta_{-}(s)\big)\big(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}-2)-1\big)\Big)^{*}=1

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Some direct computations lead then to the condition

\Im\big(\eta_{-}(s)\big)\Im\big({\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}-2)\big)=0

for any

, meaning that
{\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}-2)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
{\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}-2)
is also unitary valued,
the only solutions are the ones given in the statement. A similar argument holds for

{\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}}^{\theta}_{jj}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\theta}_{j}+2)
, starting with the restriction on

\uparrow^{j}
.
∎

Up to now, only the values of the scattering matrix at thresholds have been provided.
Additional information can be deduced from this computation, but some notations have

Firstly, let

A precise (and rather long) asymptotic expansion of this has been provided in

, Prop. 3.4]

as
\varepsilon
approaches
0

\lambda
in the spectrum of
H^{\theta}
.
Based on the previous proof, more precise information can be obtained in our current
framework, namely for

.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and consider
a\geq b\geq 0
with
a>0
. Let us assume that

\Xi_{+}(\theta)\neq\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{2\varrho}.

(6.7)

Then the map
\kappa\mapsto M^{\theta}(\lambda^{\theta}_{1}-2-\kappa^{2})
admits an holomorphic extension in some sufficiently small neighbourhood of
0
for

-\kappa^{2}\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
(the closure of
\mathbb{C}_{+}
).
If, in contrast

\Xi_{+}(\theta)=\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{2\varrho},

(6.8)

the map
\kappa\mapsto M^{\theta}(\lambda^{\theta}_{1}-2-\kappa^{2})
admits an meromorphic extension in some sufficiently small neighbourhood of
0
in

\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
, with its unique pole located at
0
and of order
1
(in the variable
\kappa
).

Before giving the proof, let us stress that the pole of order
1
(in the variable
\kappa
) does not correspond to an eigenvalue of
H^{\theta}
at
\lambda^{\theta}_{1}-2
, but corresponds to a resonance. Indeed, an eigenvalue would necessitate a pole of order
2
in
\kappa
,
as for the resolvent
\big(H^{\theta}-(\lambda^{\theta}_{1}-2-\kappa^{2})\big)^{-1}
.
In particular, this statement rules out the existence of an eigenvalue at the bottom
of the continuous spectrum of
H^{\theta}
.

Proof.

We first observe that (
6.7
) corresponds to the generic case, and hence to
S_{1}=0
. Then, by examining the expansion of
M^{\theta}(\lambda^{\theta}_{1}-2-\kappa^{2})
provided in
[
19
, (3.17)]
, we infers that
S_{1}=0
leads to a regular expression, without the singularities in
\tfrac{1}{k}
and in
\tfrac{1}{k^{2}}
.
In contrast, if we assume (
6.8
) we have shown in the previous proof that
S_{1}\neq 0
but
S_{2}=0
, see (
6.6
). Hence, by looking again at
[
19
, (3.17)]
, we deduce that the singularity in
\tfrac{1}{k^{2}}
vanishes while the one in
\tfrac{1}{k}
does not.
∎

6.2
The scattering matrix
S^{\theta}(\lambda)

In this section we provide explicit formulas for
S^{\theta}(\lambda)
.
The starting point is a stationary representation of the scattering matrix.
Namely, let us also define for
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
the operator

\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\in\mathscr{B}\big(\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\;\!\mathbb{C}^{N};\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\;\!\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
by

\delta_{jj^{\prime}}:=1
if

and
\delta_{jj^{\prime}}:=0
otherwise. Then, for

\lambda\in\big(I^{\theta}_{j}\cap I^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}\big)\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)

the channel scattering matrix

S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{jj^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j}S^{\theta}(\lambda)\mathcal{P}^{\theta}_{j^{\prime}}
satisfies the
formula

S^{\theta}(\lambda)_{jj^{\prime}}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}-2i\;\!\beta_{j}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}\;\!M^{\theta}(\lambda+i0)\mathfrak{v}\;\!\mathcal{P}_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}\;\!\beta_{j^{\prime}}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{-1},

(6.9)

with

M^{\theta}(\lambda+i0)=\big(\mathfrak{u}+G(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda-i0)^{-1}G^{*}\big)^{-1}.

The operator
M^{\theta}(\lambda+i0)
belongs to
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
for

\lambda\notin\big(\tau^{\theta}\cup\sigma_{\rm p}(H^{\theta})\big)

and by
[
19
, Lem. 3.1]
one has

\mathfrak{u}+G(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda-i0)^{-1}G^{*}=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{u}+\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{1}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{2}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}&\hbox{ for }\ \lambda<\lambda_{1}^{\theta}-2,\\
\mathfrak{u}+i\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{1}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{2}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}&\hbox{ for }\ \lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{2}^{\theta}-2),\\
\mathfrak{u}+i\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{1}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}+i\frac{\mathfrak{v}\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\theta}\mathfrak{v}}{\beta_{2}^{\theta}(\lambda)^{2}}&\hbox{ for }\ \lambda\in(\lambda_{2}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{1}^{\theta}+2),\\

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{2}^{\theta}+2)\backslash\{\lambda_{2}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{1}^{\theta}+2\}

belongs to
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})

\mathfrak{v}M^{\theta}(\lambda+i0)\mathfrak{v}=\frac{1}{Q^{\theta}(\lambda)}\begin{pmatrix}u_{2}a^{2}+\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{2}T_{+}^{\theta}(\lambda)&\frac{a^{2}b^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}}{2}T_{-}^{\theta}(\lambda)\\

(6.12)

We first observe that

\mathfrak{u}+G(H_{0}^{\theta}-\lambda-i0)^{-1}G^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}+\frac{a^{2}}{2}T_{+}^{\theta}(\lambda)&-\frac{ab\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}}{2}T_{-}^{\theta}(\lambda)\\

Expressions (
6.11
) and (

) are then readily obtained.

In only remains to check that

, which corresponds to the absence of embedded eigenvalues.
Since
\lambda\not\in\mathcal{T}^{\theta}
, the condition

(6.13)

Case:
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{\theta}-2,\lambda_{2}^{\theta}-2)

and

, or
u_{2}=-1
and

Case:

From the second equation we infer that

Case:

which can be treated as the first case.
