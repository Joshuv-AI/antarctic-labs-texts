The method of stochastic characteristics for linear second-order hypoelliptic equations
Juraj Földes, David P. Herzog email: foldes@virginia.edu, dherzog@iastate.edu

Abstract
. We study hypoelliptic stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and their connection to degenerate-elliptic boundary value problems on bounded or unbounded domains. In particular, we provide probabilistic conditions that guarantee that the formal stochastic representation of a solution is smooth on the interior of the domain and continuously approaches the prescribed boundary data at a given boundary point. The main general results are proved using fine properties of the process stopped at the boundary of the domain combined with hypoellipticity of the operators associated to the SDE. The main general results are then applied to deduce properties of the associated Green’s functions and to obtain a generalization of Bony’s Harnack inequality. We moreover revisit the transience and recurrence dichotomy for hypoelliptic diffusions and its relationship to invariant measures.

1.3.
Previous results and layout of the paper

Despite these issues in the hypoelliptic setting, understanding when the equation or boundary conditions in (

,

]
.
Direct applications also occur in the ergodic theory of SDEs, where functionals of return times to compact sets can be seen as the formal stochastic representations corresponding
to equations of the form (

[
HM11
,
RB06
,

]
.

Historically, various aspects of the problem (
1.2
) in the hypoelliptic setting have drawn interest from researchers dating back to Kolmogorov
[
Kol34
]
, who gave the first known example of a hypoelliptic diffusion that is not uniformly elliptic. Later, the seminal work of Hörmander
[
Hör67
]
provided an efficient tool to determine hypoellipticity of

by locally calculating the Lie algebra of vector fields
that create the operator

in the form (

), see
Section

2.4
for further information. For classical well-posedness of the problem (

), the pioneering work of Bony

is fundamental. There, Bony gives conditions on a bounded open set

)

a critical assumption guaranteeing continuity on

Such an assumption is often not satisfied for many hypoelliptic operators of interest,
for example if the noise is additive (that is, spatially constant), see

,

More recently, the hypoelliptic Dirichlet problem (

]

Similar to Bony’s work, the results in

Perhaps more closely related to the present paper is the work of Kogoj

]

Note that

However, applications to solvability of (

Another goal of the present work is to provide a relatively self-contained presentation on the topic in this paper, referring primarily to graduate-level textbooks to obtain needed results. Our hope is that the level of the paper is similar to the level in Oksendal’s book

Lemma 2.4

\displaystyle w_{k}:=\inf_{x\in\partial\mathcal{X}_{k}}w(x)\to\infty\,\,\text{ as }\,\,k\to\infty\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Lw\leq Cw+D\,\,\text{ on }\,\,\mathcal{X}.

2.3

\displaystyle w_{k}e^{-Ct}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}w(x_{t\wedge\tau_{k}})e^{-C(t\wedge\tau_{k})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}<t\}}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}w(x_{t\wedge\tau_{k}})e^{-C(t\wedge\tau_{k})}

\displaystyle=w(x)+\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{k}}-Cw(x_{s})e^{-Cs}+e^{-Cs}Lw(x_{s})\,ds

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t}:=x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}},\,\,t\geq 0.

Because

is a well-defined Markov process distributed on

for all times

.
We let
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
and

,

, respectively denote the Markov semigroup and transitions associated to

Remark 2.5
.

For the process

with

2.4.
Hypoellipticity

Let us first define precisely the term
hypoelliptic
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
be non-empty, open set and
M
be a differential operator with coefficients belonging to
C^{\infty}(V)
.
We say that
M
is
hypoelliptic
on
V
if for any distribution
v
on
V
with
Mv\in C^{\infty}(W)
for some
W\subset V
nonempty open, we have
v\in C^{\infty}(W)
.

Fundamental to our analysis are the smoothing properties of the differential operator
L
afforded by hypoellipticity.
In the classical paper
[
Hör67
]
, Hörmander (see Theorem
2.9
below) provides a sufficient condition for hypoellipticity on open
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
for operators
M
of the form

(2.11)

\displaystyle M=a+X_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}X_{\ell}^{2},

where
a\in C^{\infty}(V;\mathbf{R})
and
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}
are
C^{\infty}
vector fields on
V
.

After some algebraic manipulations, note that

\displaystyle L,L^{*},\partial_{t}\pm L,\partial_{t}\pm L^{*},

where
L
is as in (
1.1
) and
L^{*}
denotes the formal
L^{2}(dx)
-adjoint of
L
, can all be written in the form (
2.11
) on the respective open sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X},(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{X},(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{X}
. For example, observe that if we let
X_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{m}}\sigma_{ji}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
, and
Y_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{m}}b_{j}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}
, then

(2.12)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=Y_{0}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{ji}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}(\sigma_{\ell i}(x))\bigg]\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\ell}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}X_{j}^{2}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=:X_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}X_{j}^{2}.

To introduce Hörmander’s condition which implies hypoellipticity, we first define
the
Lie bracket

[X,Y]
of differentiable vector fields
X=\sum_{i}X^{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
and
Y=\sum_{j}Y^{j}\partial_{x_{j}}
on an open set in
\mathbf{R}^{k}
by

\displaystyle[X,Y]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(X^{i}\partial_{x_{i}}(Y^{j})-Y^{i}\partial_{x_{i}}(X^{j}))\partial_{x_{j}}.

Definition 2.7
.

Suppose
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
is an open set and
M
is an operator of the form (
2.11
), where
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are

C^{\infty}
vector fields on
V
and
a\in C^{\infty}(V)
. Define the following
C^{\infty}
vector fields on
V
:

(2.14)

\displaystyle X_{j},

\displaystyle j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}

\displaystyle[X_{i},X_{j}],

\displaystyle i,j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}

\displaystyle[[X_{i},X_{j}],X_{k}],

\displaystyle i,j,k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,m\}

If the vector fields in (
2.14
) span the tangent space at all points in
V
, we say
M
satisfies the
Hörmander condition
on
V
.

Remark 2.8
.

If
M
of the form (
2.11
) is elliptic, then
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m}
span the tangent space at all points in
V
. Thus the brackets in (
2.14
) can
be viewed as a generalization
of ellipticity. Relating this back to
L
in (
1.1
) and the equation (
1.3
), one can interpret Lie brackets in (
2.13
) as a propagation of the randomness implicitly through the equation.

Hörmander’s theorem provides even more refined estimates on the smoothing effect along every bracket in the list (
2.14
). Essentially, the more brackets one takes to reach a certain direction,
the smaller the smoothing effect occurs along that direction. While we do not state this general result rigorously, we need below the following simpler version to establish a generalization of Bony’s Harnack inequality in
Section

5
.

Theorem 2.9

(Hörmander 1967
[
Hör67
]
)
.

If
M
defined in (
2.11
) satisfies the Hörmander condition on a nonempty open set
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
, then
M
is hypoelliptic on
V
. More specifically, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(V)
with
\psi_{2}=1
on an open neighborhood of
\text{\emph{supp}}(\psi_{1})
we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{1}u\|_{H^{s+\delta}(\mathbf{R}^{k})}\leq C_{s}(\|\psi_{2}Mu\|_{H^{s}(\mathbf{R}^{k})}+\|\psi_{2}u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbf{R}^{k})})

for any
s\in\mathbf{R}
and any distribution
u
on
V
with
\psi_{2}u,\psi_{2}Mu\in H^{s}(\mathbf{R}^{k})
. In (
2.15
),
C_{s}
is a constant depending only on
s
,
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
,
and the domain
V
and all functions are assumed to be zero outside of their compact supports.

Remark 2.10
.

Note that hypoellipticity is a consequence of the inequality (
2.15
) by a bootstrapping argument.
As remarked above, although we mostly use hypoellipticity somewhat independently of Hörmander’s result, we need the precise estimate (
2.15
) for the proof of a generalization of Bony’s Harnack inequality
[
Bon69
]
.

Often, instead of listing operators that are hypoelliptic, we make a simper hypothesis. See the
parabolic Hörmander condition
below, which ensures that an entire list of operators is hypoelliptic.

Definition 2.11
.

Suppose that
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
is open and
M
is an operator of the form (
2.11
) where the
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are
C^{\infty}
vector fields on

V
and
a\in C^{\infty}(V)
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m}\}
and for

inductively define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k}=\{[X_{i},X]\,:\,X\in\mathcal{V}_{k-1},i=0,1,2,\ldots,m\}\qquad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{V}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{k}\,.

If
\mathcal{V}
spans
\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
at all points in

, then we say that

satisfies the

on

.

Remark 2.12
.

Observe that for

to satisfy the parabolic Hörmander condition, one cannot include

in the list of a spanning set. Rather,

must be first commuted with another vector field, for example

.

Example 2.13
.

Let

\displaystyle M=\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}+\partial_{x_{1}}=:X_{1}^{2}+X_{0}.

Then,

\displaystyle L,\,L^{*},\,L+\beta,\,L^{*}+\beta,\,\partial_{t}\pm L,\,\partial_{t}\pm L^{*}

where

are hypoelliptic on the respective sets

(2.17)

\displaystyle V,\,V,\,V,\,V,\,(0,\infty)\times V,\,(0,\infty)\times V.

Below, we will see that
hypoellipticity of the operators in (

.

3.
Remarks on Boundary Behavior

In order to solve the equation (
1.2
) in the classical sense, understanding the behavior of the process
x_{t}
satisfying the equation (
1.3
) near the boundary
\partial U
is critical.
In this section, we explore various conditions related to boundary behavior used in the literature to ensure well-posedness of equation (
1.2
) (in the classical sense) when
L
fails to be uniformly elliptic.

3.1.
Nice points and regular points

Definition 3.1
.

We call
x_{*}\in\partial U

nice
if there exists an open neighborhood
U_{x_{*}}\subset\mathcal{X}
of
x_{*}
and a function
w\in C^{2}(U_{x_{*}})
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

w>0
on
U_{x_{*}}\setminus\{x_{*}\}
and
w(x_{*})=0
;

(ii)

Lw<0
on
U_{x_{*}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

We show in
3.9
below that if
x_{*}\in\partial U
is nice and the relevant hypoellipticity is satisfied, then the process
x_{t}
exits
U
instantaneously when started from
x_{*}
.
However, proving that
x_{*}
is nice; that is, finding a Lyapunov function
w
in
3.1
, can be highly nontrivial or even impossible. One can ensure
x_{*}\in\partial U
is nice provided
\partial U
has an exterior normal vector to
U
at
x_{*}
and randomness pointing in the direction of the normal vector (see
3.10
below).
Intuitively, the process
x_{t}
projected onto this normal direction for small times behaves like a scaled one-dimensional Brownian motion. Then, the process must exit the domain instantaneously as the one-dimensional Brownian motion has no preferred direction and dominates the motion in small times. See
[
CFH21
, Section 7]
for further details.

Remark 3.3
.

If
U
is furthermore assumed to be a bounded and
L
is assumed to be hypoelliptic, then one can show that the Dirichlet problem (
1.2
) with
f\equiv 0
, has a unique classical solution if all points on the boundary are nice. Although the main result in
[
Ram97
]
establishes this fact, it is not exactly stated in this way.

In this paper, we find it more convenient to phrase our hypotheses in terms of stopping times for the process
x_{t}
solving (
1.3
). Hence, we define

(3.1)

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,x_{t}\notin\overline{U}\}\,,

and recall the first positive exit time
\tau
from
U
defined in equation (
2.2
).

Definition 3.4
.

We call an interior point
x\in U

regular
if
\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\overline{\tau}\}=1
. A boundary point
x_{*}\in\partial U
is called
regular
if
\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\overline{\tau}=0\}=1
. Points on
\overline{U}
are called
irregular
otherwise. We call the set
U

interior regular
if all
x\in U
are regular. We call
U

boundary regular
if all
x_{*}\in\partial U
are regular.

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that if
x\in U
is regular, then the process
x_{t}
started from
x
exits
U
and
\overline{U}
at the same time. In particular,
x_{t}
cannot reach
\partial U
and return to the interior
of
U
with positive probability.
On the other hand,
x_{*}\in\partial U
being regular means
x_{t}
initiated at
x_{*}
must exit
\overline{U}
instantaneously.

Remark 3.6
.

Note that the event
\{\overline{\tau}=0\}
belongs to the
germ sigma field

\bigcap_{t>0}\mathcal{F}_{t}
, hence has probability
0
or
1
by Blumenthal’s
0
-
1
law. Thus,

x_{*}\in\partial U
being regular is equivalent to
\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\overline{\tau}=0\}>0
.

Using the strong Markov property for
x_{t}
, the next result states that boundary regular implies interior regular.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
U
is boundary regular, then
U
is interior regular.

Proof.

Let
x\in U
and suppose
\tau=\infty
. Then
\overline{\tau}\geq\tau=\infty
, so
\tau=\overline{\tau}
. If the event
\{\tau<\infty\}
has positive probability, then the strong Markov property
and boundary regularity gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\overline{\tau},\,\tau<\infty\}=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau=\overline{\tau},\,\tau<\infty\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]=\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau<\infty\}}\mathbf{P}_{x_{\tau}}\{\overline{\tau}=0\}=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau<\infty\}.

This finishes the proof since

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\overline{\tau}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\overline{\tau},\,\tau=\infty\}+\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\overline{\tau},\,\tau<\infty\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\infty\}+\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau<\infty\}=1.

∎

As the next example shows, the converse of
3.7
is false even if the parabolic Hörmander condition is satisfied.

Example 3.8
.

Let
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}
be the interior of the open square with vertices
(-1,1)
,
(1,1)
,
(1,-1)
,
(-1,-1)
, and consider the following SDE on
U

\displaystyle dx^{1}_{t}

\displaystyle=-(x_{t}^{2})^{2}\,dt\,,

\displaystyle dx^{2}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t}\,,

where
B_{t}
is a standard, one-dimensional Brownian motion. Let
I
be the open segment connecting
(1,-1)
and
(1,1)
.
Note that all points on
I\subset\partial U
are not regular since
x_{t}^{1}
is decreasing for all times. On the other hand,
x_{t}=(x_{t}^{1},x_{t}^{2})
has generator

\displaystyle L=-(x^{2})^{2}\partial_{x^{1}}+\partial_{x^{2}}^{2}=:X_{0}+X_{1}^{2},

which satisfies the parabolic Hörmander condition on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
since

X_{1}=\partial_{x^{2}}\,\,\text{ and }\,\,[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{0}]]=-2\partial_{x^{1}}

span the tangent space at all points in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
. Also, observe that the process initiated at
x\in U
can only exit
U
on
\partial U\setminus I
.
Hence,
U
is interior regular since every point on
\partial U\setminus I
is regular.

Next, we clarify a the relationship between boundary regular and nice.

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose
x_{*}\in\partial U
is nice and that
\partial_{t}\pm L
and
\partial_{t}\pm L^{*}
are hypoelliptic on
(0,\infty)\times B_{\delta}(x_{*})
for some
\delta>0
such that

.
Then,
x_{*}
is regular.

Proof.

Let
x_{*}\in\partial U
be nice and fix
\delta>0
such that
\partial_{t}\pm L^{*}
,
\partial_{t}\pm L^{*}
are hypoelliptic on
(0,\infty)\times B_{\delta}(x_{*})
. Choose an open neighborhood
U_{x_{*}}
of
x_{*}
such that there exists a function
w\in C^{2}(U_{x_{*}})
satisfying properties (i) and (ii) in
3.1
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
V:=U_{x_{*}}=B_{\delta}(x_{*})
.
It follows that the distribution
\mu_{t}
of the stopped process

x_{t}^{V}:=x_{t\wedge\tau_{V}}
(see
4.3
) satisfies Fokker-Planck equation

(\partial_{t}+L^{*})(\mu_{t})=0
in the sense of distributions on
(0,\infty)\times V
. Thus, when restricted to subsets of
(0,\infty)\times V
,
\mu_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure with density
p_{t}
which is smooth on
(0,\infty)\times V
. In other words, the law of
x_{t}^{V}
restricted to subsets of
V
, for fixed

, is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
V
.

If
x_{*}
is not regular, then
\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\overline{\tau}>0\}=1
. Thus, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})\subset V
and
\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})}<\overline{\tau}\}>0
, for otherwise
the distribution of
x_{t}^{V}
started at

would have non-zero mass concentrated at

, violating the absolute continuity of
\mu_{t}
above.
For
\sigma(t):=t\wedge\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})}\wedge\overline{\tau}
, Dynkin’s formula (see (
2.8
)) and positivity of

on
\partial B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})
gives

\displaystyle c\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})}<\overline{\tau}\wedge t\}\leq\mathbf{E}_{x_{*}}w(x_{\sigma(t)})\leq w(x_{*})=0

for some constant
c>0
independent of

we obtain
\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(x_{*})}<\overline{\tau}\}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Fix
x_{*}\in\partial U
and as in
3.9
suppose that
\partial_{t}\pm L
and
\partial_{t}\pm L^{*}
are hypoelliptic on

for some

such that

Moreover, suppose that

satisfies the

at

Then, if the noise has a non-zero component in

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{\mathfrak{m}}(\sigma(x_{*})\sigma^{T}(x_{*}))_{ij}v_{i}(x_{*})v_{j}(x_{*})>0.

Then, by choosing

\displaystyle w(x)=e^{-\beta|x^{\prime}-x_{*}|^{2}}-e^{-\beta|x^{\prime}-x|^{2}}

Note that (

3.2.
Modification of the domain

We conclude this section by restating
[

in our context. Intuitively, it asserts that starting from a

In order to state the result, we say that

.

Suppose that

with piecewise linear boundary

such that
V_{\epsilon}
and
V_{\epsilon}^{c}
are boundary regular for
x_{t}
and
|U|-|V_{\epsilon}|<\epsilon
. Here,
|\cdot|
denotes Lebesgue measure on
\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
.

To prove
3.11
one defines
V_{\epsilon}
to be the interior of the convex hull of a sufficient number of points
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n(\epsilon)}\in\partial U
selected independently and randomly according to Hausdorff measure on
\partial U
. One can then show that with probability one, the faces generated by these points on
\partial V_{\epsilon}
are not parallel to the fixed vector
v
in
3.11
, so that the process exits
\overline{V_{\epsilon}}
instantaneously when started there. The fact that the volumes of
V_{\epsilon}
and
U
are close is intuitive provided
n(\epsilon)

is large enough, which was rigorously proved in
[
SW03
]
.

4.
Interior regularity of functionals of
\tau

Our goal in this section is to obtain interior smoothness of various functionals of the first positive exit time
\tau
from
U
. Given the relevant hypoellipticity, the key first step is to show that the law of the stopped process
\tilde{x}_{t}
, introduced in
Section

2.3
, when restricted to Borel subsets of
U
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{U}
, has a density
\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
with respect to Lebesgue measure on
U
. Furthermore, the mapping
(t,x,y)\mapsto\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U\times U)
and satisfies, respectively, the forward and backward Kolmogorov equations in the classical sense:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)=L_{y}^{*}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\,\,\,\text{ on }\,\,\,(0,\infty)\times U,\,x\in U\text{ fixed},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)=L_{x}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\,\,\,\text{ on }\,\,\,(0,\infty)\times U,\,y\in U\text{ fixed}.

This is done in
Section

4.2
. In
Section

4.3
, we deduce interior smoothness and the equations satisfied by

(4.3)

\displaystyle x\mapsto\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi(\tau):U\to\mathbf{R}

for some choices of smooth
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbf{R}
, for example
\varphi(x)=x^{k}
,
k\in\mathbf{N}
or
\varphi(x)=\mathbf{1}_{(t,\infty)}(x)
for fixed
t
.

First, however, we need to establish a few auxiliary results.

4.1.
Auxiliary results

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that conditions
(U0)
and
(NE)
are satisfied. Then, the following assertions hold true.

(i)

For any
t>0
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
M>0
we have

\lim_{y\to x}\mathbf{E}\bigg\{\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|x_{s}(x)-x_{s}(y)|^{2}\wedge M\bigg\}=0\,,

where
s\mapsto x_{s}(z)
satisfies (
1.3
)
with
x_{0}(z)=z
.

(ii)

If
x_{*}\in\partial U
is regular, then for any
\epsilon,\delta>0
, there is
\rho>0
such that

\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau\geq\delta\}\leq\epsilon
for any
x\in U
with
|x-x_{*}|<\rho
. That is, as
x\rightarrow x_{*}\in\partial U,x\in U
,
\tau(x)\rightarrow\tau(x_{*})
in probability.

Proof.

The proof of part (i) follows a standard Grönwall-type comparison argument. Let
\tau_{k}=\tau_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}
and we denote the dependence of
\tau_{k}
on
the initial condition
x
of
x_{t}
by writing
\tau_{k}(x)
. Observe that for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\leq\tau_{k}(x)\wedge\tau_{k}(y)
, the processes
x_{s}(x)
and
x_{s}(y)
respectively agree pathwise with processes
x_{s,k}(x)
and
x_{s,k}(y)
satisfying Itô SDEs with globally Lipschitz coefficients (cf.
2.2
). Thus if
\Delta_{t}=x_{t}(x)-x_{t}(y)
and
\Delta_{t,k}=x_{t,k}(x)-x_{t,k}(y)
, then for any
\sigma\leq t\wedge\tau_{k}(x)\wedge\tau_{k}(y)
with
t\geq 0
deterministic, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,\sigma]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\leq\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s,k}|^{2}.

Hence, to estimate
\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,\sigma]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}
it suffices, for a given deterministic time
t\geq 0
to estimate
\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}
, where
\Delta_{t}=x_{t}(x)-x_{t}(y)
satisfies

(4.4)

\Delta_{t}=x-y+\int_{0}^{t}[b(x_{s}(x))-b(x_{s}(y))]\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}[\sigma(x_{s}(x))-\sigma(x_{s}(y))]\,d\mathbf{W}_{s}

with globally Lipschitz
b,\sigma
on
\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
and Lipschitz constants depending on
k
.
Hence, there exists a constant
Q_{k}>0
such that

\displaystyle|b(x)-b(y)|+|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\leq Q_{k}|x-y|\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}.

Using Doob’s maximal inequality and Itô isometry, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 9|x-y|^{2}+9Q_{k}^{2}\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left(\int_{0}^{s}|x_{u}(x)-x_{u}(y)|\,du\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+9\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|\int_{0}^{s}(\sigma(x_{u}(x))-\sigma(x_{u}(y)))d\mathbf{W}_{u}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 9|x-y|^{2}+C_{k}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,u]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}du

for some constant
C_{k}(t)
depending only on
k,t
.
Consequently by Grönwall’s inequality, we have

(4.5)

\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\leq 9|x-y|^{2}e^{C_{k}(t)t}=:|x-y|^{2}D_{k}(t).

Now if
b,\sigma
are no longer globally Lipschitz and
\delta_{k}=\text{dist}(\mathcal{X}_{k},\partial\mathcal{X}_{k+1})>0
,
we have by Chebyshev’s inequality and (
4.5
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\wedge M

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\wedge M(\textbf{1}\{\tau_{k}(x)<t\}+\textbf{1}\{\tau_{k}(x)\geq t\})

\displaystyle\leq M\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{k}<t\}+\mathbf{E}_{x}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s,k}|^{2}\mathbf{1}\{\tau_{k}(x)\geq t,\tau_{k+1}(y)\geq t\}+M\mathbf{P}\{\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\Delta_{s,k}|^{2}>\delta_{k}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq M\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{k}<t\}+D_{k}(t)|x-y|^{2}\,+\frac{|x-y|^{2}D_{k}(t)}{\delta_{k}^{2}}M.

Passing
y\to x
and then
k\to\infty
using nonexplosivity of
x_{t}(x)
, we obtain (i).

To obtain (ii), suppose there exist
\epsilon_{0},\delta_{0}>0
and
a sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset U
with
x_{n}\rightarrow x_{*}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x_{n}}\{\tau\geq\delta_{0}\}\geq\epsilon_{0}

for all
n
. For any
\epsilon>0
, let
U^{+}_{\epsilon}
be given by

(4.6)

\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}^{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,:\,\text{dist}(x,\overline{U})<\epsilon\}.

By path continuity,
\overline{\tau}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\tau_{U_{\epsilon}^{+}}
almost surely. Since
x_{*}
is regular, there is
\epsilon\in(0,1)
so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x_{*}}\{\tau_{U_{\epsilon}^{+}}<\delta_{0}/2\}\geq 1-\epsilon_{0}/2\,,

and consequently for any
n

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{\tau(x_{n})\geq\delta_{0},\tau_{U_{\epsilon}^{+}}(x_{*})<\delta_{0}/2\}\geq\epsilon_{0}/2\,,

where the initial condition is indicated in the stopping times above. By conclusion (i) with
M=1
, and
\epsilon<1

for any large enough
n
one has
\mathbf{P}\{\sup_{s\in[0,\delta_{0}/2]}|x_{s}(x_{n})-x_{s}(x_{*})|>\epsilon/2\}\leq\epsilon_{0}/4
. But then

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left\{\tau(x_{n})\geq\delta_{0},\tau_{U_{\epsilon}^{+}}(x_{*})<\frac{\delta_{0}}{2},\sup_{s\in[0,\delta_{0}/2]}|x_{s}(x_{n})-x_{s}(x_{*})|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{P}\left\{\sup_{s\in[0,\delta_{0}/2]}|x_{s}(x_{n})-x_{s}(x_{*})|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right\}\leq 0+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{4},

a contradiction.
∎

In addition to
4.1
, we need one more auxiliary result. Although this result is basic, it is used repeatedly throughout the paper.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
V\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
be open and let
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbf{N}
, be linear second-order differential operators with
C^{\infty}(V)
coefficients. Suppose that
\{v_{n}\}
be a uniformly bounded sequence of measurable functions
v_{n}:V\to\mathbf{R}
and that the following conditions are satisfied:

-

For every
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(V)
,
(\mathcal{L}_{n}-\mathcal{L})^{*}\phi\to 0
as
n\to\infty
in
L^{1}(V,dx)
where the
*
denotes the formal adjoint with respect to
L^{2}(V,dx)
-inner product;

-

For some measurable function
f
on
V
which is bounded on compact subsets of
V
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}v_{n}\to-f
as
n\rightarrow\infty
on
V
in the sense of distributions.

-

v_{n}
converges to
v_{\infty}
in the sense of distributions on
V
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Then,
\mathcal{L}v_{\infty}=-f
on
V
in the sense of distributions. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic on
V
and
f\in C^{\infty}(V)
, then
v_{\infty}\in C^{\infty}(V)
and
\mathcal{L}v_{\infty}=-f
on
V
in the classical sense.

Proof.

For fixed
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(V)
, conditions of the statement and the Dominated Convergence Theorem imply

\displaystyle\int_{V}v_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi\,dx=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{V}v_{n}\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{V}v_{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}\varphi-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{U}v_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{n}-\mathcal{L})^{*}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{V}-f\varphi\,dx.

Thus
\mathcal{L}v_{\infty}=-f
on
V
in the sense of distributions. The remaining assertion is an immediate consequence of hypoellipticity.
∎

4.2.
The forward and backward Kolmogorov equations for the stopped process

Recall that if
x_{t}
is nonexplosive, then the stopped process
\tilde{x}_{t}
, see (
2.10
), is a well-defined, continuous-time Markov process distributed on
\overline{U}
. Moreover, the associated semigroup and transitions are respectively denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
.

Our main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
(U0)
,
(NE)
, and
(L2)
are satisfied.

(i)

For all
x\in U
and
t>0
, the restriction of the measure
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
to Borel subsets of
U
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
U
with density
\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
, and for fixed
x\in U
the mapping
(t,y)\mapsto\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
. Furthermore, the forward Kolmogorov equation (
4.1
) is satisfied in the classical sense.

(ii)

If
(L3)
is furthermore satisfied, then
(t,x,y)\mapsto\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
belongs to
C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U\times U)
and the backward Kolmogorov equation (
4.2
) is satisfied in the classical sense.

Remark 4.4
.

The above result is natural and understood by experts in the field. However, we found it difficult to locate a complete proof of part (ii) as it is much more subtle than part (i). While part (i) follows almost immediately from Dynkin’s formula, part (ii) requires several nontrivial approximations and steps.

Remark 4.5
.

Intuitively the result above holds because, while the measure
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
has a singular component on the boundary if the process exits
U
by time
t
with positive probability,
when restricted to subsets of
U
this singularity is not seen.

Remark 4.6
.

Note that in the statement above we may take
U=\mathcal{X}
, in which case
\tilde{x}_{t}
coincides with the original process
x_{t}
on
\mathcal{X}
, and thus the statement is about the law of
x_{t}
in
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof of
4.3
.

To prove part (i), fix
t>0
,
x\in U
, and take any
\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,t]\times U)\cap C^{\infty}_{0}((0,t)\times U)
. Thus, in particular,
\psi
is compactly supported in
(0,t)\times U
.
Then, Dynkin’s formula yields

\displaystyle 0=\mathbf{E}_{x}\psi(t,\tilde{x}_{t})=\mathbf{E}_{x}\psi(t,x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}})

\displaystyle=\psi(0,x)+\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{0}}(L+\partial_{s})\psi(s,x_{s})\,ds

\displaystyle=0+\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t}(L+\partial_{s})\psi(s,\tilde{x}_{s})\,ds\,,

where
\tau_{0}
from (
2.9
) is the first exit time from
U
.
Hence,

\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t}(L+\partial_{s})\psi(s,\tilde{x}_{s})=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}(L+\partial_{s})\psi(s,y)\tilde{p}_{s}(x,dy)ds=0

for any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,t)\times U)
. That is,
(L^{*}_{y}-\partial_{t})(\tilde{p}_{t}(x,dy))=0
on
(0,\infty)\times U
in the sense of distributions. By hypoellipticity of
\partial_{t}-L^{*}
on
(0,\infty)\times U
as in
(L2)
, part (i) follows.

Part (ii) is more involved as there are several layers of approximations. We divide the proof into two main steps.

Step 1
. Let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and consider the mapping
(t,x)\mapsto u(t,x):=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\psi(x)
. We claim that

u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}u=Lu
in the classical sense on
(0,\infty)\times U
.

Step 2
.We prove part (ii) by taking a sequence of approximations of
\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
of the form
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\psi_{n}(x)
,
\psi_{n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
.

Proof of Step 1
. First observe that since
x_{t}
is nonexplosive, the stopped process
\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}}
with initial condition
\tilde{x}_{0}=x\in U
satisfies the Itô SDE

\displaystyle d\tilde{x}_{t}=b(\tilde{x}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{t})\,dt+\sigma(\tilde{x}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{t})\,d\mathbf{W}_{t}.

We first show
u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}u=Lu
in the classical sense on
(0,\infty)\times U
in the special case when
b,\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
are bounded with bounded derivatives of all orders.

Recall that
U_{n}
is a sequence of bounded open sets with
\overline{U}_{n}\subset U
and
U_{n}\uparrow U
as
n\to\infty
. Suppose that
\varphi_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U};[0,1])
satisfies
\varphi_{n}(x)=1
on
U_{n}
and
\varphi_{n}(x)=0
for
x\in U_{n+1}^{c}
. Consider a sequence of approximating processes
x_{t}^{n}
with
x_{0}^{n}=x
and

(4.7)

\displaystyle dx_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=b(x_{t}^{n})\varphi_{n}(x_{t}^{n})\,dt+\sigma(x_{t}^{n})\varphi_{n}(x_{t}^{n})\,d\mathbf{W}_{t}.

Claim.

If
b,\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
are bounded with bounded derivatives of all orders, then
for any
t>0
fixed,

(4.8)

\mathbf{E}_{x}\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|\tilde{x}_{r}-x_{r}^{n}|^{2}\to 0\,\,\text{ as }\,\,n\to\infty.

Proof of (
4.8
).

Observe that

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t}-x_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n})[b(\tilde{x}_{s})-b(x_{s}^{n})]\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n})[\sigma(\tilde{x}_{s})-\sigma(x_{s}^{n})]\,d\mathbf{W}_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}(1-\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n}))\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})b(\tilde{x}_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}(1-\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n}))\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})\sigma(\tilde{x}_{s})\,d\mathbf{W}_{s}.

Hence, squaring both sides, taking the supremum and using Doob’s maximal inequality we find that for all
t\leq T

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|\tilde{x}_{r}-x_{r}^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\sup_{r\in[0,s]}|\tilde{x}_{r}-x_{r}^{n}|^{2}\,ds+C_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[(1-\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n}))\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})]^{2}\,ds\,,

where the constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(T,\|b\|_{\text{Lip}},\|\sigma\|_{\text{Lip}})>0
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(T,\|b\|_{L^{\infty}},\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}})>0
do
not
depend on
n
. Grönwall’s inequality then implies that for all
t\leq T

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|\tilde{x}_{r}-x_{r}^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}e^{tC_{1}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[(1-\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n}))\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})]^{2}\,ds\,.

Next, observe that if
\tilde{x}_{s}(\omega)\in U
for some
s\geq 0
, then by path continuity and the definition of the stopped process,
\tilde{x}_{u}(\omega)\in U_{n_{0}}
for all
u\in[0,s]
for some

n_{0}=n_{0}(\omega,s)\in\mathbf{N}
. Hence, by definition,
x_{u}^{n}(\omega)\equiv\tilde{x}_{u}(\omega)
for all
u\in[0,s]
and
n\geq n_{0}
, and consequently by the Bounded Convergence Theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[(1-\varphi_{n}(x_{s}^{n}))\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})]^{2}\,ds=0\,.

Hence,
\mathbf{E}\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|\tilde{x}_{r}-x_{r}^{n}|^{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, establishing (
4.8
).
∎

Let
u_{n}(t,x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\psi(x_{t}^{n})
. It follows that for fixed
t>0
, the mapping
x\mapsto u_{n}(t,x)
belongs to the space
C^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})

[
Fri75
, Theorem 5.5]
. Define an extended version
A_{n}
of the generator of
x_{t}^{n}
by

(4.9)

\displaystyle A_{n}g(x):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{x}g(x_{t}^{n})-g(x)}{t}\,,

where the domain of
A_{n}
consists of all measurable functions
g:\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}\to\mathbf{R}
such that the limit in (
4.9
) exists for each
x
.
Next, by
[
Oks13
, Theorem 8.1.1]
, for fixed
x\in\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
,
t\mapsto u_{n}(t,x)\in C^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})
and by Markov property
u_{n}
satisfies on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
the generalized backward equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}=A_{n}u_{n}.

Since
x\mapsto u_{n}(t,x)
is globally
C^{2}
, Dynkin’s formula implies that
A_{n}u=L_{n}u
where
L_{n}
is the
classical
generator of
x_{t}^{n}
, which is a second-order differential operator with
C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})
coefficients. Moreover, these coefficients agree with the coefficients of

on
U_{n}
.
In particular, by
(L3)
,
L-\partial_{t}
is hypoelliptic on
(0,\infty)\times U_{n}
, and therefore
u_{n}\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U_{n})
and
\partial_{t}u_{n}=Lu_{n}
on
(0,\infty)\times U_{n}
in the classical sense.
To obtain a similar result for
u(t,x)=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\psi(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\psi(\tilde{x}_{t})
, we note that
u_{n}
is uniformly bounded. Furthermore,
u_{n}\to u
pointwise as
n\to\infty
since by Claim

(4.10)

\displaystyle|u_{n}(t,x)-u(t,x)|^{2}\leq\|\psi\|_{\text{Lip}}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{x}|\tilde{x_{t}}-x_{t}^{n}|^{2}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. By
4.2
,
u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}u=Lu
in the classical sense on
(0,\infty)\times U
. Thus Step 1 is completed in the case when
b,\sigma
have bounded derivatives of all orders.

To complete Step 1, assume that
b\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X};\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})
and
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X};M_{\mathfrak{m}\times r})
are not necessarily bounded and
x_{t}
is nonexplosive. For the sequence

\{U_{n}\}
above, note that the restrictions of
b,\sigma
to
U_{n}
can be extended to functions on
\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
which are bounded with bounded derivatives all orders. Setting
\tau_{n}=\inf\{t>0\,:\,\tilde{x}_{t}\notin U_{n}\}
, we have shown above that if
\tilde{x}_{t}^{n}:=\tilde{x}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}
, then
(t,x)\mapsto\tilde{u}_{n}(t,x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\psi(\tilde{x}_{t}^{n})
satisfies
\tilde{u}_{n}\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U_{n})
and
\partial_{t}\tilde{u}_{n}=L\tilde{u}_{n}
on
(0,\infty)\times U_{n}
in the classical sense. However, since

\psi
is bounded, then
\tilde{u}_{n}
is also uniformly bounded in

and by (

),

\tilde{u}_{n}\to u
pointwise on
(0,\infty)\times U
. Step 1 now follows after applying
4.2
.

Proof of Step 2
. Let
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}};[0,\infty))
with
\int_{\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\chi(x)\,dx=1
. Let
\chi_{\epsilon}(x)=\epsilon^{-\mathfrak{m}}\chi(\epsilon^{-1}x)
and extend
\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
to be zero for all
(t,x,y)\notin(0,\infty)\times U\times U
. For any
(t,x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}\times\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
, define

(4.11)

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y):=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,z)\chi_{\epsilon}(y-z)\,dz.

Consider any bounded open
V\subset U
with
\overline{V}\subset U
.
For each fixed
y\in\overline{V}
, there is
\epsilon_{0}(y)>0
small enough such that
z\mapsto\chi_{\epsilon}(y-z)\in C_{0}^{\infty}
is compactly supported in
U
for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}(y)]
. It follows by compactness of
\overline{V}
, that we may choose
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
z\mapsto\chi_{\epsilon}(y-z)
is compactly supported in
U
for all
y\in\overline{V}
and all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
. Thus, for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
and
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times U
, the formula (
4.11
) makes sense and
y\mapsto u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)\in C^{\infty}(V)
. Also, by Step 1, for each
y\in V
and
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
,

(t,x)\mapsto u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)=L_{x}u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)
on
(0,\infty)\times U
in the classical sense.
To conclude the result, it suffices to show
for any
\varphi\in C_{0}((0,\infty)\times U\times V)
with
\varphi
supported in
K\subset(0,\infty)\times U\times V
compact, that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{K}\varphi(t,x,y)[u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)-\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)]dx\,dy\,dt=0\,.

Indeed, simply replace
\varphi
in the above formula by
(\partial_{t}-L_{x})^{*}\varphi
, where
\varphi
is smooth with compact support.

First, since
\int_{\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}\chi(z)\,dz=1
, a standard substitution gives for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
and
y\in V

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)-\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}}[\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y-\epsilon z)-\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)]\chi(z)\,dz.

Next, let
\hat{p}_{t}(x,y)=\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)
on
K
and
\hat{p}_{t}(x,y)=0
otherwise.
By Tonelli’s theorem,
(t,x,y)\mapsto\hat{p}_{t}(x,y)\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}\times(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{K}\varphi(t,x,y)[u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)-\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)]\,dx\,dy\,dt\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbf{R}\times(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})^{3}}|\hat{p}_{t}(x,y-\epsilon z)-\hat{p}_{t}(x,y)|\chi(z)dz\,dx\,dy\,dt.

Since
\hat{p}_{t}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}\times(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})^{2})
, for any
\delta>0
there is
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})^{3})
within
L^{1}
distance
\delta
of
\hat{p}_{t}
. Thus,
by translation invariance and Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{K}\varphi(t,x,y)[u_{\epsilon}(t,x,y)-\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)]\,dx\,dy\,dt\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{(\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}})^{4}}|\psi(t,x,y-\epsilon z)-\psi(t,x,y)|\chi(z)dz\,dx\,dy\,dt.

Since
\psi
is compactly supported, passing
\epsilon\to 0
, using Dominated Convergence theorem, and then
\delta\to 0
finishes the proof of
4.3
(ii).
∎

4.3.
Interior smoothness of functionals of the first positive exit time

In this section we analyze quantities of the form

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi(\tau),\,\,x\in U,

for various functions
\varphi
and
\tau
as in (
2.2
). The first two choices

\varphi(x)=\mathbf{1}\{|x|<\infty\}\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\varphi(x)=x,

that respectively yield in (
4.12
)

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau<\infty\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau

are of significant importance for analyzing recurrence and transience in
Section

7
below.
Throughout, we assume the parabolic Hörmander condition
(PH)
for simplicity, which in particular implies that
(L1)
–
(L4)
are satisfied.

Proposition 4.7
.

Suppose that
(U00)
,
(NE)
, and
(PH)
are satisfied. For any
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times U
, define

(4.14)

\displaystyle u_{1}(t,x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad u_{2}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\infty\}.

Then:

(i)

u_{1}\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}u_{1}=Lu_{1}
on
(0,\infty)\times U
in the classical sense;

(ii)

u_{2}\in C^{\infty}(U)
and
Lu_{2}=0
on
U
in the classical sense.

Proof.

For (i), observe that for any
x\in U
,
u_{1}(t,x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,U)
where
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
is the transition kernel of the stopped process
\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}}
. Let
K\subset U
be compact and by
4.3

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,K)=\int_{K}\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)\,dy
. Furthermore, by
4.3
and the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
it follows that
(t,x)\mapsto\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,K)
is
C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U)
and
\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,K)=L\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,K)
on
(0,\infty)\times U
in the classical sense. To obtain the same result for
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,U)
, let
K_{n}\subset U
be a sequence of compact sets with
K_{n}\uparrow U
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, by the monotone convergence theorem,
the sequence
(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,K_{n}))_{n}
is uniformly bounded and converges pointwise to
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,U)
. The assertion (i) follows after applying
4.2
.

To obtain (ii), first observe that

u_{2}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=\infty\}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,U).

Thus we seek to apply an argument similar to that in
4.2
. To this end, note that

\displaystyle\bar{u}(t,x):=\int_{t}^{t+1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}(x,U)\,ds\rightarrow u_{2}(x)\,\,\,\text{ pointwise as }\,\,\,t\to\infty

and that
\bar{u}(t,x)
is uniformly bounded on
[0,\infty)\times U
. Thus, for any function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and any
t>0
, using integration by parts and the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{U}(L-\partial_{s})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}(x,U)\varphi(x)\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\bar{u}(t,x)L^{*}\varphi(x)\,dx-\int_{U}[\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t+1}(x,U)-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,U)]\varphi(x)\,dx.

By passing
t\rightarrow\infty
and
using the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
u_{2}
solves
Lu_{2}=0
in the sense of distributions on
U
. Part (ii) follows by hypoellipticity of
L
on
U
.
∎

We next state and prove a regularity result for
\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau
; that is, for
\varphi(x)=x
in (
4.12
).

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that
(U00)
,
(NE)
, and
(PH)
are satisfied and
\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau<\infty\}=1
for all
x\in U
.

(i)

If
U_{0}\subset U
is bounded open with
\overline{U_{0}}\subset U
, then
v_{0}(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau_{U_{0}}
is bounded on
\overline{U_{0}}
. Furthermore,
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}(U_{0})
and
Lv_{0}=-1
on
U_{0}
in the classical sense.

(ii)

Let
U_{0}\subset U
be an open set and suppose that
v(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau
is finite on a dense set
D\subset U_{0}
. Then,
v\in C^{\infty}(U_{0})
and
v
solves
Lv=-1
on
U_{0}
in the classical sense.

Remark 4.9
.

The technical hypothesis in (ii) that
v
is finite on a dense set and not everywhere
is needed below in
Section

7
. We defer the proof of
4.8
(ii) until after we prove a hypoelliptic Harnack inequality (
5.5
).

Proof of
4.8
(i).

Let
\sigma=\tau_{U_{0}}
and note that
\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau<\infty\}=1
imply that for all
x\in\overline{U_{0}}
, there exists
t>0,\alpha\in(0,1/2)
such that
\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>t\}\leq(1-2\alpha)
. By
4.7
(i), there exists
\epsilon=\epsilon(x)>0
such that
\mathbf{P}_{y}\{\sigma>t\}\leq(1-\alpha)
for all
y\in B_{\epsilon}(x)
. Using compactness of
\overline{U_{0}}
and taking a finite subcover, it follows that there exists
t_{*}>0,\alpha_{*}\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbf{P}_{y}\{\sigma>t_{*}\}\leq(1-\alpha_{*})
for all
y\in\overline{U_{0}}
. Since
\tau
is almost surely finite, so is
\sigma
by

U_{0}\subset U
and
path continuity. Thus, for any
x\in\overline{U_{0}}
we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma\mathbf{1}\{\sigma\in[(m-1)t_{*},mt_{*})\}\leq 3t_{*}+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}mt_{*}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>(m-2)t_{*}\}.

By the Markov property, for
x\in\overline{U_{0}}
and
m\in\mathbf{N}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>mt_{*}\}=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma>mt_{*}\}|\mathcal{F}_{(m-1)t_{*}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma>(m-1)t_{*}\}\mathbf{P}_{x_{(m-1)t_{*}}}\{\sigma>t_{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{*})\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>(m-1)t_{*}\}\leq(1-\alpha_{*})^{m}

and (
4.15
) implies for all
x\in\overline{U_{0}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma\leq 3t_{*}+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}mt_{*}(1-\alpha_{*})^{m-2},

which is finite and bounded independently of
x\in\overline{U_{0}}
.

Next, in order to show that
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}(U_{0})
and satisfies
Lv_{0}=-1
on
U_{0}
in the classical sense, observe that

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>t\}\,dt=:\int_{0}^{\infty}u(t,x)\,dt.

4.7
yields
u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times U_{0})
and
Lu=\partial_{t}u
on
U_{0}
in the classical sense. Thus if
v_{n}(x)=\int_{1/n}^{n}u(t,x)\,dt
for
n\in\mathbf{N}
, it follows that
v_{n}\in C^{\infty}(U_{0})
and for all
x\in U_{0}

\displaystyle Lv_{n}(x)=\int_{\frac{1}{n}}^{n}Lu(t,x)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{n}}^{n}\partial_{t}u(t,x)\,dt

\displaystyle=u(n,x)-u(1/n,x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>n\}-\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\sigma>1/n\}\to-1

as
n\to\infty
in the sense of distributions on
U_{0}
.
Since the sequence
v_{n}
is uniformly bounded by bounded
v_{0}
and
v_{n}\rightarrow v_{0}
pointwise on
U_{0}
, the result follows from
4.2
.
∎

We next consider the expression (
4.12
) for more general
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
. First we need an auxiliary lemma, which is a representation result for the expected value of certain random variables.

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose that
(U00)
and
(NE)
are met and let
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbf{R}
be
C^{1}
, strictly increasing and satisfy
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Then

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi(\tau)=\varphi(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt.

Proof.

Note that by shifting the formula (
4.16
), it suffices to assume that
\varphi(0)=0
. Since
\varphi
is
C^{1}
, strictly increasing with
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
,
\varphi
has a
C^{1}
inverse
\varphi^{-1}
mapping
[0,\infty)
onto
[0,\infty)
. Moreover,
\varphi^{-1}
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
. It thus follows that
\varphi^{-1}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}(\varphi(\tau)\wedge N)=\int_{0}^{N}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\varphi(\tau)>t\}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{N}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>\varphi^{-1}(t)\}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\varphi^{-1}(N)}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt.

Passing
N\to\infty
and using the Monotone Convergence Theorem finishes the proof.
∎

Next, we state and prove our main result for general
\varphi
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that
(U00)
,
(NE)
, and
(PH)
are satisfied. Let
\varphi\in C^{2}([0,\infty);\mathbf{R})
be strictly increasing with
\varphi(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
with derivative
\varphi^{\prime}\in C^{1}([0,\infty);\mathbf{R})
which is strictly increasing with
\varphi^{\prime}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Suppose furthermore that

v_{1}(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi(\tau)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad v_{2}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)

are bounded on compact subsets of
U
. Then
Lv_{1}=-v_{2}
on
U
in the sense of distributions.

Remark 4.12
.

Compare the equation
Lv_{1}=-v_{2}
with
Lv=-1
when
\varphi(x)=x
. Although such a
\varphi
does not satisfy the hypotheses above, we still have the analogous conclusion.

Proof of
4.11
.

By
4.10
, we have

\displaystyle v_{1}(x)=\varphi(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt\qquad\text{ and }\qquad v_{2}(x)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt.

In the latter formula, simply apply
4.10
to
\varphi^{\prime}
instead of
\varphi
.
Consider the sequence of functions on
U
given by

\displaystyle v_{1,n}(x):=\varphi(0)+\int_{1/n}^{n}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt\,.

Observe that by
4.7
,
v_{1,n}\in C^{\infty}(U)
and

\displaystyle Lv_{1,n}(x)=\int_{1/n}^{n}\varphi^{\prime}(t)L_{x}(\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\})\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{1/n}^{n}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\partial_{t}(\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\})\,dt

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(n)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>n\}-\varphi^{\prime}(1/n)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>1/n\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{1/n}^{n}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>t\}\,dt\,.

Since for any
x\in U
we have
\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)<\infty
, it follows that

(4.17)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(n)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>n\}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}_{x}\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq n\}=0

on
U
. Furthermore, since
\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau=0\}=0
for any
x\in U
, it also follows that on

(4.18)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(1/n)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>1/n\}=\varphi^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau>0\}=\varphi^{\prime}(0).\,

In addition,
\varphi^{\prime}
is strictly increasing, and therefore
\varphi^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
and by the monotone convergence theorem,
Lv_{1,n}\to-v_{2}
pointwise as
n\to\infty
on
U
.
Since
v_{1},v_{2}
are bounded on compact subsets of
U
and
v_{1,n}\to v_{1}
as

,
4.2
implies
Lv_{1}=-v_{2}
on

in the sense of distributions.
∎

Let us note some more specific examples and results that follow as an immediate consequence of the previous results.

Corollary 4.13
.

Suppose that
(U00)
,
(NE)
, and

are satisfied. Then:

(i)

If for some

,

,

,

. Then

and

.

5.
Green’s functions and Bony’s Harnack inequality

In this section, we explore
Green’s functions
in the hypoelliptic setting for an open set
U
. As a consequence, we generalize Bony’s form of the Harnack inequality
[
Bon69
]
.

Let
\beta>0
be a constant and suppose
f\in C^{\infty}(U)\cap B(\overline{U})
. Often (see
[
Bon69
]
) one refers to the
Green’s operator

G_{\beta}
as the ‘mapping’
f\mapsto v
, where
v

is the ‘unique’ solution of the Poisson problem

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (L-\beta)v&=-f\qquad&&\text{ on }U\,,\\
u&=0\qquad&&\text{ on }\partial U.\end{aligned}\right.

When
\beta=0
and
L
is hypoelliptic on
U
, the uniqueness of solutions of (
5.1
) heavily depends on the structure of the diffusion
x_{t}
driven by
L
near
\partial U
. This is also the case when (
5.1
) and
\beta>0
. Here, we employ stochastic methods to define and deduce properties of
G_{\beta}f
without the uniqueness.

Formally, our ‘best guess’ of solution of (
5.1
) would be

(5.2)

\displaystyle v(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds\,,

where
\tau=\inf\{t>0\,:\,x_{t}\notin U\}
. The expression (
5.2
) is motivated by a formal application of
Itô’s formula to the function
e^{-\beta t}v(x_{t})
, where
v
is a presumed classical solution of (
5.1
). Even though it is not clear nor necessarily true that
v
solves (
5.1
),

v
in (
5.2
) is well-defined
for any function
f\in B(\overline{U})
. Note that
v
in (
5.2
) is well-defined even if
\tau=\infty
,
due to the presence of the exponentially decaying factor
e^{-\beta s}
.

Remark 5.1
.

Under further conditions on the stopping time
\tau
, one can define
G_{0}
or even sometimes
G_{-\beta}
for
\beta>0
small enough. Here, for simplicity, we keep
\beta

positive to avoid any further complexities of assumptions on
\tau
. Additionally, under similar assumptions, one can replace
\beta
by a spatially dependent function
\beta=\beta(x)\in C(\overline{U})
to arrive at the Feynman-Kac formula

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\beta(x_{v})\,dv}\,ds,

provided (
5.3
) makes sense.

5.1.
Definition and properties of
G_{\beta}

Given the preliminary remarks above, we now define
G_{\beta}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(U00)
and
(NE)
be satisfied. For any
\beta>0
and any
f:\overline{U}\to[0,\infty)
measurable, define
G_{\beta}f:U\to[0,+\infty]
by

(5.4)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}f(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds.

If
f:\overline{U}\to\mathbf{R}
is measurable and
G_{\beta}|f|(x)<\infty
for all
x\in U
, we define

(5.5)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}f(x):=G_{\beta}f_{+}(x)-G_{\beta}f_{-}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds

where
f_{+}
,
f_{-}
denote the positive and negative parts of
f
, respectively.
We call
G_{\beta}
the
Green’s operator of order

\beta>0
. Let

\mathcal{D}_{\beta}:=\{f:\overline{U}\to\mathbf{R}\text{ measurable}\,:\,x\mapsto G_{\beta}|f|(x)\text{ is bounded on compacts in }U\}

be the
domain
of
G_{\beta}
.

Remark 5.3
.

Observe that the expression (
5.5
) is better behaved compared with the formal stochastic representation corresponding to
the usual Poisson problem (i.e. with
\beta=0
) due to the presence of the exponentially decaying factor
e^{-\beta s}
.

Often it is convenient to express the operator
G_{\beta}f
as a traditional integral operator, meaning that for any

reasonable
function
f
on
\overline{U}
and any
x\in U
:

(5.6)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}f(x)=\int_{\overline{U}}f(y)g_{\beta}(x,y)\,dy

for some kernel
g_{\beta}
to be referred to as the
Green’s function
associated to
G_{\beta}
. However, we must be careful as there are subtleties in defining
g_{\beta}(x,y)
for
y\in\partial U
and for
x=y
in
U
.

To obtain a workable expression for
g_{\beta}
, under the assumptions
(U00)
and
(NE)
we recall the stopped process
\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}}
from
(
2.10
) distributed on
\overline{U}
,
where
\tau_{0}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,x_{t}\notin U\}
. We also recall its transition kernel
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
defined on Borel subsets of
\overline{U}
. Note that, for any
x\in U
and any
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
, the Dominated Convergence theorem implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}f(\tilde{x}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{(\tau_{0}-\delta)\vee 0}f(\tilde{x}_{s})\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{(\tau_{0}-\delta)\vee 0}f(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}f(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds.

Hence, for any
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
and
x\in U
, we have by Fubini’s theorem

(5.7)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}f(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}f(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{U}(\tilde{x}_{s})f(\tilde{x}_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{U}f(y)\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}(x,dy)e^{-\beta s}\,ds=\int_{U}f(y)\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{s}(x,dy)e^{-\beta s}\,ds

Theorem 5.4
.

Let

(ii)

The mapping

, where

(iii)

For any

(L_{x}-\beta)G_{\beta}f=-f
on

(iv)

If

on

Proof.

First, we show (i). By

(i), (ii). Thus, if

Hence, by the Radon-Nikodym theorem, for each

,

has density

which belongs to

. This concludes (i).

Next, we establish parts (ii) and (iii) simultaneously. Note that for any

, we have for any

(5.9)

\displaystyle G_{\beta}((L-\beta)f)(x)=\int_{U}(L-\beta)f(y)g_{\beta}(x,y)dy.

On the other hand, because

) applied to

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}f(x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}})e^{-\beta(t\wedge\tau_{0})}

\displaystyle=f(x)+\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{0}}(L-\beta)f(x_{s})e^{-\beta s}ds.

Since

\mathbf{E}_{x}f(x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}})e^{-\beta(t\wedge\tau_{0})}\to 0.

By (

) and (

\displaystyle G_{\beta}(L-\beta)f(x)=-f(x)

.

To obtain regularity in the

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\int_{U}h(x)f(y)\int_{1/n}^{n}\partial_{t}(\tilde{p}_{t}(x,y)e^{-\beta t})\,dt\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U}h(x)[e^{-\beta n}\mathbf{E}_{x}f(\tilde{x}_{n})-e^{-\beta/n}\mathbf{E}_{x}f(\tilde{x}_{1/n})]\,dx.

Passing

satisfies

in the sense of distributions on

To finish the rest of (ii), let

Since

and

(5.15)

\displaystyle(L-\beta)_{x}g_{\beta}(x,y)=-\delta_{y}(x)

on

Finally, for part (iv), for

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbf{E}_{x}e^{-\beta t\wedge\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{n}}u(x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{n}})=u(x)-\beta\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{n}}u(x_{s})e^{-\beta s}\,ds.

We now apply

Our assumptions are weaker as we do not assume the uniqueness of the solution of (

Before we proceed, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{U}=\{u\in C^{2}(U;[0,\infty))\,:\,Lu=0\text{ on }U\}.

Of course, if

Theorem 5.5

(Harnack inequality)
.

Suppose that
(U00)
,
(NE)
, and
(PH)
are satisfied. Consider any compact set

, any set

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in K}|D^{\alpha}u(x)|\leq c[u(y_{1})+u(y_{2})+\cdots+u(y_{k})]

Proof.

From this point in the paper, the argument is a slight modification of the proof of
[

. Fix any multi-index

. Let

We show that there exist an open neighborhood

\displaystyle u(y)\geq c\sup_{x\in V}|D^{\alpha}u(x)|\,\,\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{H}_{U}.

The result then follows from (

.
By
5.4

, we obtain for all

\displaystyle u(y)\geq\beta\int_{U}u(z)g_{\beta}(y,z)\,dz.

We next claim that there exists

Thus fix

of

.
In order to bound the integral

) to obtain
for any

and any open neighborhood

of

For sufficiently negative

, and
there exists

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in V}|D^{\alpha}u(x)|\leq C\int_{X}u(x)\,dx\leq\frac{C}{\beta c}u(y).

∎

Given the previous result, we return to the proof of

(ii).

6.
General results on the formal stochastic solution

6.1.
Preliminary remarks

Throughout this section, we assume that
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
is non-empty, open set with non-empty complement
U^{c}
.
We return to one of our main goals: satisfaction of (
1.2
) by the formal stochastic representation, which for the Poisson equation
is given by (provided it makes sense)

(6.1)

\displaystyle v_{\text{stoc},0}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})\,ds=\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}f(\tilde{x}_{s})\,ds,\,\,x\in U,

where
\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t\wedge\tau_{0}}
is the process stopped at the boundary

.
For example if

,
v(x):=\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau=\mathbf{E}_{x}\tau_{0}=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}1\,ds
,

and as long as the hypotheses of

(ii) are satisfied,

and

in the classical sense. Thus, we expect that under analogous assumptions,
v_{\text{stoc},0}\in C^{\infty}(U)
and
Lv_{\text{stoc},0}=-f
in the classical sense on
U
.

Additionally, if

for the formal stochastic solution of the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle u_{\text{stoc},0}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}g(x_{\tau}),

Dynkin’s formula applied to

yields for any
x\in U
,

\displaystyle u_{\text{stoc},0}(x,t):=\mathbf{E}_{x}g(x_{\tau\wedge t})

\displaystyle=g(x)+\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge t}Lg(x_{s})\,ds.

Thus by formally passing

, rearranging and using (

), one has that

satisfies

, and interior smoothness and

on

in the classical sense follows.
Then, by an approximation argument and

, the same properties follow
for more general

.

6.2.
Interior smoothness for

and

We begin with the interior smoothness for

. Suppose

and
(NE)

\displaystyle G_{0}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})\,ds\,,

where

For any

\displaystyle G_{0}f(x):=G_{0}f_{+}(x)-G_{0}f_{-}(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\tau}f(x_{s})\,ds

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}(U)=\{f:\overline{U}\to\mathbf{R}\text{ measurable}\,:\,x\mapsto G_{0}|f|(x)\text{ is bounded on compact sets in }U\}.

Theorem 6.1

(Interior Smoothness for

)

Let

(U00)

(NE)

\displaystyle Lv_{\text{stoc},0}=-f\,\,\text{ on }\,\,U

Suppose

(Interior smoothness for

Note that by the Tietze Extension Theorem we can replace the assumption

7.
The Transience and Recurrence Dichotomy for Degenerate Diffusions

The goal of this section is to carefully establish the dichotomy between transience and recurrence for degenerate diffusions by adapting the classic cycle constructions of Khasminskii
[
Kha60
,
Has60
]
, which was carried out in the setting of elliptic diffusions on Euclidean space. Note that this has been done previously using the language of invariant control sets as in
[
Kli87
]
. However, we found a gap in the arguments in
[
Kli87
]
that we could not fix in an obvious way (see
7.22
below).
Moreover, some regularity claims in
[
Kli87
]
could not be verified without calculations analogous to ones
in the previous sections. Although it is known that there are alternative, probabilistic paths which circumnavigate these issues (we refer, in particular, to the work of Harris
[
Har56
]
, the survey paper of Baxendale
[
Bax11
]
and the work of Meyn-Tweedie
[
MT12
]
), here we establish the dichotomy using classical ideas of Khasminskii and regularity properties established above.
Note that this approach traces back to Maruyama and Tanaka
[
MT57
]
and Watanabe
[
WM58
]

in the case of a one-dimensional, elliptic diffusion. We also refer to the works
[
AKDR66
,
GT15
]
which we found helpful.

7.1.
Nice diffusions

In this subsection, we briefly introduce the structural assumptions we make on the diffusion
x_{t}
satisfying (
1.3
) in this section. Recall that by condition
(NE)
,
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
for all
t\geq 0
and any
initial condition
x_{0}=x\in\mathcal{X}
.

In order to formulate our results, we need a notion of
irreducibility
of
x_{t}
as introduced in the following definition.

Definition 7.1
.

Suppose that condition
(NE)
is satisfied. We call
x_{t}

irreducible
if
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
\delta>0
we have
\mathbf{P}_{y}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(x)^{c}}<\infty\}>0
.

Note that irreducibility means that, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, the process started at
y\in\mathcal{X}
enters an arbitrarily small neighborhood of
x\in\mathcal{X}
with positive probability. Thus,
the process can transition between arbitrarily small neighborhoods of any two points in
\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 7.2
.

Comparing terminology, if
x_{t}
is irreducible in the sense of
7.1
, then
\mathcal{X}
is the unique invariant control set of

as in
[
Kli87
]
.
Certainly, the methods used below can be applied in more general settings, e.g.
if there is more than one invariant control set or if the process
x_{t}
eventually enters an invariant control set from a larger set to not return to other parts of space. For our purposes, one irreducible set
\mathcal{X}
is sufficient.

We are ready to introduce the assumptions we impose on the diffusion
x_{t}
in this section.

Definition 7.3
.

We say that the diffusion
x_{t}
is
nice
if the following conditions are met:

(i)

Condition
(NE)
is satisfied;

(ii)

The generator
L
of

is satisfies the parabolic Hörmander condition on
\mathcal{X}
as in
2.11
;

(iii)

x_{t}
is irreducible as in
7.1
.

One key property of a nice diffusion employed below is that the process leaves bounded sets in
\mathcal{X}
sufficiently fast.

Proposition 7.4
.

Suppose that
x_{t}
is a nice diffusion and
U\subset\mathcal{X}
is nonempty, open and bounded with
\overline{U}\subset\mathcal{X}
. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\overline{U}}\mathbf{E}_{x}e^{\delta\tau_{\overline{U}}}<\infty.

Proof.

The proof of this result follows a similar reasoning used in the proof of
4.8
(i). Let
z\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\overline{U}
and fix
\epsilon>0
such that
B_{\epsilon}(z)\subset\mathcal{X}\setminus\overline{U}
. By irreducibility of

, for all
x\in\overline{U}
there exists
t=t(x)>0
and
\alpha=\alpha(x)\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(z)^{c}}>t\}\leq 1-\alpha.

Applying
4.7
(i) along with the parabolic Hörmander condition on
\mathcal{X}
and compactness of

, there exists

and
\alpha_{*}\in(0,1)
independent of

so that

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{\overline{U}}>t_{*}\}\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{B_{\epsilon}(z)^{c}}>t_{*}\}\leq 1-\alpha_{*}\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,x\in\overline{U}.

Following the proof of
4.8
(i), the Markov property implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{\overline{U}}\geq mt_{*}\}\leq(1-\alpha_{*})^{m-1}

for all

and all

. Hence

,
\mathbf{P}_{x}
-almost surely. Furthermore, choosing

small enough so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}e^{\delta\tau_{\overline{U}}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{x}e^{\delta\tau_{\overline{U}}}\mathbf{1}\{(m-1)t_{*}\leq\tau_{\overline{U}}<mt_{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{\delta mt_{*}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\{\tau_{\overline{U}}\geq(m-1)t_{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{*})^{-2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(e^{\delta t_{*}}(1-\alpha_{*}))^{m}<\infty.

∎

7.2.
Recurrence and transience for nice diffusions

We start with the definition of transience and recurrence.

Definition 7.6
.

Suppose the diffusion
x_{t}
is nice.
We say that a point

\delta>0

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{y}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(x)^{c}}<\infty\}=1.

Our next goal is to prove that points in

Proposition 7.7

Assume

is nice.
If

is recurrent, then all points in
\mathcal{X}
are recurrent. Consequently, either all points in
\mathcal{X}
are recurrent or all points in

Proof.

Suppose

is recurrent and let

with

. We show that

is also recurrent. Suppose that
\delta>0
is any positive real number such that

and

By irreducibility in

and path continuity (if

such that

(7.4)

\displaystyle 2\alpha:=P_{t_{*}}(x,B_{\delta}(y))>0.

Since

is continuous at

In particular, there exists

such that

and

for all

. Define stopping times

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,x_{t}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}+t_{*}

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\geq\sigma_{2}\,:\,x_{t}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)\}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle\sigma_{2k}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2k-1}+t_{*}

\displaystyle\sigma_{2k+1}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\geq\sigma_{2k}\,:\,x_{t}\in B_{\epsilon}(x)\}.

for

Then for all

is almost surely finite since

is nice and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{w}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>\sigma_{2j+2}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{w}\mathbf{E}_{w}[\mathbf{1}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>\sigma_{2j+2}\}|\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{2j+1}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{w}\mathbf{1}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>\sigma_{2j+1}\}\mathbf{E}_{x_{\sigma_{2j+1}}}\mathbf{1}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>t_{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\mathbf{P}_{w}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>\sigma_{2j}\}.

Thus, by induction,
\mathbf{P}_{w}\{\tau_{B_{\delta}(y)^{c}}>\sigma_{2j}\}\leq(1-\alpha)^{j}
for all

7.3.
Invariant measures

A central interest in the theory of stochastic differential equations is the large-time behavior of the process
x_{t}
. In particular, we are interested in the relationship between
recurrence, transience and the existence of
invariant measures
. Such measures are the random analogues of equilibrium points of deterministic ordinary differential equations.

To introduce invariant measures, throughout this section we again assume
x_{t}
is a nice diffusion. In particular,
x_{t}
is non-explosive process on
\mathcal{X}
,
and consequently the process
x_{t}
is Markov with Markov semigroup
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Recalling that
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Borel sigma algebra
of subsets of
\mathcal{X}
, we call a positive,
\mathcal{B}
-measure
\mu
an
invariant measure
,
if
\mu\mathcal{P}_{t}=\mu
for all
t\geq 0
, where
\mu\mathcal{P}_{t}
was defined in (
2.6
). An invariant measure
\mu
with
\mu(\mathcal{X})=1
is called an
invariant probability measure
.

Remark 7.12
.

Observe that if
\mu
is an invariant probability measure, the equality
\mu\mathcal{P}_{t}=\mu
for all
t\geq 0
means that the process
x_{t}
with
initial distribution
\mu
has the distribution
\mu
for all times
t\geq 0
. In other words, the statistics remain invariant under the dynamics.

Remark 7.13
.

It is common in the literature to implicitly assume that
an invariant measure is an invariant probability measure. However, below we need to distinguish between invariant measures which are probability distributions and those which are not.

We first show that a nice diffusion
x_{t}
which is transient cannot have an invariant probability measure.

Corollary 7.14
.

If
x_{t}
is nice diffusion which is transient, then
x_{t}
cannot have an invariant probability measure.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that there exists an invariant probability measure
\mu
. Then, there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbf{R}^{\mathfrak{m}}
with
K\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\mu(K)>0
. Since
\mu
is invariant,

\displaystyle 0<\mu(K)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)P_{t}(x,K)\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Using the Bounded Convergence Theorem and
7.11
, it follows that

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(dx)P_{t}(x,K)\rightarrow 0

as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Hence,
\mu(K)=0
, a contradiction.
∎

On the other hand, when
x_{t}
is recurrent, one can always construct a
\sigma
-finite invariant measure using an embedded Markov chain via cycles.

Proposition 7.19

Suppose

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(B)=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\nu(dx)\mathbf{E}_{x}\sigma^{B}\qquad\textrm{for any }B\in\mathcal{B}\,,

.
We may assume without loss of generality that

Since any invariant probability measure is a solution of

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(B)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\mu}(dx)\mathcal{P}_{t}(x,B)\geq\int_{W^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}(dx)\mathcal{P}_{t}(x,B)\geq cc^{\prime}|W^{\prime}|>0\,,

If there is more than one invariant probability measure, then by standard arguments we can choose two distinct ergodic invariant measures, which are in particular
