Effective Boundary Conditions for the Fisher-KPP Equation on a Domain with 3-dimensional Optimally Aligned Coating 0 0 footnotetext: E-mail addresses: gengxingri@u.nus.edu.
Xingri Geng, [2mm] Department of Mathematics, Southern University of Science, Technology, Shenzhen, P.R. China

Abstract
We consider the Fisher-KPP equation on a three-dimensional domain surrounded by a thin layer whose diffusion rates are drastically different from that in the bulk. The bulk is isotropic, while the layer is considered to be anisotropic and “optimally aligned”, where the normal direction is always an eigenvector of the diffusion tensor. To see the effect of the layer, we derive effective boundary conditions (EBCs) by the limiting solution of the Fisher-KPP equation as the thickness of the layer shrinks to zero. These EBCs contain some exotic boundary conditions including the Dirichlet-to-Neumann mapping and the Fractional Laplacian. Moreover, we emphasize that each EBC keeps effective indefinitely, even as time approaches infinity.



Keywords.
thin layer, asymptotic behavior, eigenvalue problem, effective boundary conditions, lifespan.

Introduction

Motivated by the scenario of a nature reserve surrounded by a road/buffer zone. The diffusion rate is isotropic in the nature reserve, while it is anisotropic and drastically different in the road/buffer zone. Moreover, the road/buffer zone is thin compared to the scale of the nature reserve, resulting in multi-scales in the spatial variable. The difference in diffusion rate and spatial size leads to computational difficulty. To deal with such situations, we view the road/buffer zone as a thickless surface as its thickness shrinks to zero, on which “effective boundary conditions” (EBCs) are imposed. These EBCs not only provide an alternative way for numerical computation but also give us an analytic interpretation of the effects of the road/buffer zone.

In this paper, we consider the Fisher-KPP equation on a 3-dimensional domain surrounded by a thin coating (see Figure

&=|H-h|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}e_{n}(s)g_{n}\frac{-4}{(e^{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}h^{\prime}}-e^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}h^{\prime}})^{2}}\\
&=O(|H-h|),\end{split}

for some

between

and
H
, which means that
\mathcal{J}^{h}[g]\to\mathcal{J}^{H}[g]
uniformly as
h\to H
, and
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}[g]=-(-\Delta_{\Gamma})^{1/2}g.

In what follows, we will estimate the size of the term

for a sufficiently small

as

, then it holds from (

\begin{split}\sqrt{\sigma\mu}\Psi_{R}(s,0)&=\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\left(-g(s)+O(h^{2})\right).\end{split}

(3.9)

On the other hand, if

as

\begin{split}\|\Psi_{R}(s,0)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma))}&\leq\frac{2}{R}\|\Psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2})}+R\|\Psi_{RR}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2})}\leq\frac{3\|g\|_{C^{2}(\Gamma)}}{R},\end{split}

from which it turns out that

\sqrt{\sigma\mu}\|\Psi_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}=\frac{O(1)\sqrt{\sigma\mu}}{R}.

(3.10)

Remark 3.1

Then, as

where

(\lambda_{1},e_{1})
is the principal eigenpair of

(4.3)

subject to the effective boundary conditions listed in Table

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Denote by
(\lambda_{1}^{D},e_{1}^{D})
the principal eigenpair of (

) subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition. Taking a special test function

in (

0,&\mbox{$x\in\Omega_{2},$}&\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

we are led to

\lambda_{1}^{\delta}<\frac{k\int_{\Omega_{1}}|\nabla e_{1}^{D}|^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega_{1}}(e_{1}^{D})^{2}dx}=\lambda_{1}^{D}.

Moreover, it holds from (
4.2
) that
\lambda_{1}^{\delta}>0
. Then after passing to a subsequence of
\delta>0
, we have

\lambda_{1}^{\delta}\to\;\text{ some }\;\lambda_{1}^{0}.

Subsequently, it is easy to see that

\lambda_{1}^{\delta}=k\int_{\Omega_{1}}|\nabla e_{1}^{\delta}|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\sigma(e_{1}^{\delta})_{r}^{2}+\mu|\nabla_{s}e_{1}^{\delta}|^{2}dx\leq\lambda_{1}^{D},

(4.5)

from which

is bounded in

.
Thus, after passing to a further subsequence of
\delta\to 0
, we have

e_{1}^{\delta}\to\;\text{some}\;e_{1}^{0}

weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
and strongly in

Consequently, similar to the parabolic case in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we can show that

is a weak solution of (

) subject to the effective boundary conditions in Table
1
with the corresponding
\lambda_{1}^{0}

, after which it holds that

is the eigenfunction of
\lambda_{1}^{0}
.

In view of (
2.4
), we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{2}}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx=&\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}(1+H(s)r+\kappa(s)r^{2})drds\\
=&\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\int_{r}^{\delta}(e_{1}^{\delta})_{\tau}d\tau\right)^{2}(1+H(s)r+\kappa(s)r^{2})drds\\
\leq&C\delta\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{\delta}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}_{\tau}d\tau\right)(1+H(s)r+\kappa(s)r^{2})drds\\
\leq&C\delta^{2}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}_{r}drds,\end{split}

(4.6)

from which, together with (

), we have

\int_{\Omega_{2}}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx\leq C\frac{\delta^{2}}{\sigma}\to 0\;\text{ as }\;\delta\to 0,

in the case of
\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to\alpha\in(0,\infty]
. Combining (
4.5
) and (

) with Lemma
4.1
, as
\delta\to 0

\int_{\Omega_{2}}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx\leq C\delta\to 0

in the case of
\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to 0
. Thus, we obtain
e_{1}^{0}\not\equiv 0
and
||e_{1}^{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}=1
, resulting from

1=\int_{\Omega}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx=\int_{\Omega_{1}}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega_{2}}(e_{1}^{\delta})^{2}dx.

Consequently, by the uniqueness of the principal eigenpair, it follows that
(\lambda_{1},e_{1})=(\lambda_{1}^{0},e_{1}^{0})
and all above convergences hold without passing to a subsequence.
∎

We now turn to the positive steady state of (

(iii)

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
A(x)
is given in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Assume that
\sigma
and
\mu
satisfy

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sigma\mu=\gamma\in[0,\infty],\quad\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\sigma}{\delta}=\alpha\in[0,\infty].

Then, as
\delta\to 0
,
U\to V
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{1})
, where
V
is the unique positive solution of

-k\Delta V=V(1-V),\;x\in\Omega_{1}

(4.12)

subject to the effective boundary conditions listed in Table
1
(with
v
replaced by
V
).

Remark 4.1
.

As the results about
U
, it holds that the positive steady state
V
exists if and only if the principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}<1
; if
V
exists, then it is unique; as
t\to\infty
,
v\to V
if
V
exists (
v\to 0
if
V
does not exist). Moreover, if
\alpha=0
, then
V\equiv 1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

By performing the process similar to the parabolic case in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we can show that after passing to a subsequence of
\delta\to 0
,

U\to\;\text{ some }\;U_{0}\;\text{ in }\;L^{2}(\Omega_{1}),

where
U_{0}
is a weak solution of (
4.12
) (
U_{0}
may be 0 in
\Omega_{1}
). It is easy to see that the weak solution of (
4.12
) is unique. Thus, all above convergences hold without passing to a subsequence. We next will show that
U_{0}\not\equiv 0
to ensure that
U_{0}
is positive, namely,
U_{0}>0
in
\Omega_{1}
. Thus, we have
U_{0}=V
.

In what follows, it suffices to prove that
U_{0}\not\equiv 0
due to the fact
U\geq 0
. In the case of
\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, we can construct a lower solution by defining

\underline{U}(x)=\varepsilon e_{1}^{\delta}(x)

where
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small. For small
\delta
, it holds from Lemma
4.1
that

-\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla\underline{U})-\underline{U}(1-\underline{U})=\underline{U}(\lambda_{1}^{\delta}-1+\varepsilon e_{1}^{\delta})\leq 0.

Since
U\equiv 1
is a upper solution, it follows from the lower and upper solutions method that

U\geq\underline{U}=\varepsilon e_{1}^{\delta},

from which we have
U_{0}\geq\underline{U}>0
in
\Omega_{1}
.

On the other hand, in the case of
\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to\alpha\in(0,\infty]
as
\delta\to 0
, we will prove
U_{0}>0
by contradiction. If not, then
U_{0}\equiv 0
. Thus, let
W=\frac{U}{||U||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}>0
, and by (
4.11
),
W
satisfies the following elliptic problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llr}-\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla W)=W(1-U),&\mbox{$x\in\Omega,$}\\
W=0,&\mbox{$x\in\partial\Omega.$}&\\
\end{array}\right.

Multiplying both sides by
W
and performing integration by parts, we have

k\int_{\Omega_{1}}|\nabla W|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega_{2}}A(x)\nabla W\cdot\nabla Wdx+\int_{\Omega}W^{2}(U-1)dx=0,

(4.13)

from which
\{W\}_{\delta>0}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{1})
. As a result, after passing to a subsequence of
\delta
,
W\to
some
W_{0}
weakly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{1})
and strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{1})
. Moreover, similar to (
4.6
), we get

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{2}}W^{2}dx=&\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}W^{2}(1+H(s)r+\kappa(s)r^{2})drds\\
\leq&C(\delta^{2})\int_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\delta}W^{2}_{r}drds\\
=&O(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\sigma})\to 0\;\text{ as }\;\delta\to 0.\end{split}

(4.14)

Consequently, we have
||W_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}=1
because of the fact
||W||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
. Following the process as in the parabolic case in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we can show that
W_{0}
is a weak solution of

-k\Delta W_{0}=W_{0},\quad x\in\Omega_{1},

subject to the corresponding effective boundary conditions in Table
1
(with
v
replaced by
W_{0}
). This means that
\lambda_{1}=1
, which contradicts the necessary condition for the existence of
V
.

∎

4.2
Main proof of Theorem
1.2

In the remaining part, our primary objective is to establish the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which unveils the infinite lifespan of each EBC as in Table

.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

If the initial value
u_{0}\equiv 0
, then it is easy to see that
u=v=0
in
\Omega_{1}\times(0,\infty)
, which implies this theorem trivially. Thus, we always have the assumption of
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
.

Case 1.

\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, i.e.,
\alpha=0
.

According to Theorem
4.1
, as
\delta\to 0
, it holds that
\lambda_{1}^{\delta}\to\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the principal eigenvalue of (
4.3
) with the corresponding effective boundary conditions (second column in Table
1
). By Lemma
4.1
, it turns out that
\lambda_{1}=0
. Thus, for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, it is easy to see that
\lambda_{1}^{\delta}<1
, implying that (
4.12
) has a unique positive steady state solution
U
. Moreover, it follows from Theorem
4.2
and Remark
4.1
that as
\delta\to 0
,
U\to V
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{1})
with
V\equiv 1
.

Subsequently, we consider the eigenvalue problem of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llr}-\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla u)-u(1-U)=\eta u,&\mbox{$x\in\Omega,$}\\
u=0,&\mbox{$x\in\partial\Omega,$}&\\
\end{array}\right.

(4.15)

from which
(\eta_{1},u)=(0,U)
is the principal eigenpair with
U>0
in
\Omega
. Let
\widetilde{u}=u-U
and then
\widetilde{u}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llr}\widetilde{u}_{t}-\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla\widetilde{u})=\widetilde{u}(1-u-U),&\mbox{$(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),$}\\
\widetilde{u}=u_{0}-U,&\mbox{$(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty).$}&\\
\end{array}\right.

(4.16)

Multiplying (
4.16
) by
\widetilde{u}
and integrating by parts in

, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}^{2}dx=&-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\widetilde{u}\cdot A\nabla\widetilde{u}+\widetilde{u}^{2}(1-U)dx-\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}^{2}udx\\
\leq&-\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{u}^{2}udx\\
\leq&0,\end{split}

(4.17)

which results from Lemma
2.1
and the variational characterization of
\eta_{1}
as

0=\eta_{1}=\underset{0\not\equiv u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}{\inf}\frac{\int_{\Omega}A\nabla u\cdot\nabla u+u^{2}(U-1)dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}dx}.

(4.18)

∎

Consequently, given a fixed
T>0
, and for any
t\geq T
, (
4.17
) gives

\begin{split}||u(\cdot,t)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\leq&||u(\cdot,t)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq&||u(\cdot,T)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq&||u(\cdot,T)-v(\cdot,T)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||v(\cdot,T)-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
&+||U-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||u(\cdot,T)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}.\end{split}

(4.19)

Since
\lambda_{1}=0
and
u_{0}\geq 0,\not\equiv 0
, as
t\to\infty
, it holds that

v(x,t)\to V\equiv 1\;\text{ uniformly in }\;\overline{\Omega}_{1},

which means that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some large
T_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

|v(x,t)-V(x)|<\varepsilon,\quad\forall x\in\overline{\Omega}_{1},

provided
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}
. Thus, we have

||v(\cdot,t)-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\leq|\Omega_{1}|^{1/2}\varepsilon

for
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}
. Furthermore, for a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, thanks to Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
2.1
, we are led to

\begin{split}||u(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})-v(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\leq\varepsilon,\end{split}

(4.20)

and

\begin{split}||u(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

(4.21)

As a result, for any
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}
, due to Theorem
4.2
and above estimates, (
4.19
) gives

\begin{split}||u(\cdot,t)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\leq&||u(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})-v(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||v(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
&+||U-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||u(\cdot,T)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}\\
\leq&C\varepsilon.\end{split}

(4.22)

Therefore, in view of (
4.19
)-(
4.21
), we have

\begin{split}&\underset{t\in[T_{\varepsilon},\infty]}{\max}||u(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
\leq&\underset{t\in[T_{\varepsilon},\infty]}{\max}||u(\cdot,t)-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+\underset{t\in[T_{\varepsilon},\infty]}{\max}||v(\cdot,t)-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||U-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
\leq&||u(\cdot,T_{\varepsilon})-U||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+\underset{t\in[T_{\varepsilon},\infty]}{\max}||v(\cdot,t)-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+||U-V||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
\leq&C\varepsilon.\\
\end{split}

(4.23)

Thus, for a small
\delta>0
, it holds that

\begin{split}\underset{0\leq t\leq\infty}{\max}||u(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}&\leq\underset{t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon}]}{\max}||u(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}+\underset{t\in[T_{\varepsilon},\infty]}{\max}||u(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,t)||_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}\\
&\leq C\varepsilon,\end{split}

(4.24)

which completes the proof of the first case.

Case 2.

\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\to\alpha\in(0,\infty)

.

Thanks to Theorem
4.1

If
