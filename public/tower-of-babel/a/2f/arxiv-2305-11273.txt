Stirling-Euler-Mahonian Triples of Permutation Statistics
Frederick Butler

Abstract
Two well-known distributions in the study of permutation statistics are the Mahonian and Eulerian distributions. Mahonian statistics include the major index MAJ \mathrm{MAJ} and the number of inversions INV \mathrm{INV} , while examples of Eulerian statistics are the number of descents des \mathrm{des} and excedances exc \mathrm{exc} . Also of interest are pairs of permutation statistics that have the same joint distribution as ( des , MAJ ) (\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ}) ; these are called Euler Mahonian pairs. In this paper we define a Stirling statistic as one that is equidistributed with the number of right-to-left minima rlmin \mathrm{rlmin} and a Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triple as a triple of statistics with the same trivariate distribution as ( rlmin , des , MAJ ) (\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ}) . We prove that several triples involving well-known statistics are Stirling-Euler-Mahonian. A general bijective technique is demonstrated for any Mahonian statistic that has a permutation code. Finally, we note several properties of Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples that follow from earlier results.



1
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the set of all permutations of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. A
permutation statistic
is a function
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
. While permutation statistics can have any distribution on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, much research has focused on the Mahonian and Eulerian distributions.

A permutation statistic
\mathrm{MST}
is said to be
Mahonian
if

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)}=(1+q)(1+q+q^{2})\cdots(1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}).

(1)

Following the convention established in
[
5
]
, we will denote Mahonian statistics in all capital letters. We give two classic examples of Mahonian statistics.

Let
\sigma_{i}
denote the
i
th entry of the word representing the permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. An
inversion
in
\sigma
is a pair
j<k
such that
\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{k}
. We use
\#X
to denote the cardinality of the set
X
. The statistic
\mathrm{INV}
defined as

\mathrm{INV}(\sigma)=\#\{j<k\,|\,\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{k}\}

is called the
inversion number
of
\sigma
. The inversion number was proven to be Mahonian by Rodrigues in
[
14
]
.

The
major index

\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)
is given by

\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{j+1}}j.

Note
\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)
can be described as the sum of the descents of
\sigma
(we define descents below). The statistic
\mathrm{MAJ}
was proven to be Mahonian in
[
12
]
by MacMahon, after whom both the statistic and the distribution are named (MacMahon was a major in the British military). The first bijective proof of this fact was given by Foata in
[
7
]
.

A
descent
of
\sigma
is a position
j
for which
\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{j+1}
. The value
\sigma_{j}
is called the
descent top
and the value
\sigma_{j+1}
the
descent bottom
. We’ll use these terms again in Section
5
. The
descent number

\mathrm{des}(\sigma)=\#\{j\,|\,\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{j+1}\}

counts the number of descents in
\sigma
.

A permutation statistic
\mathrm{est}
is said to be
Eulerian
if it has the same distribution as
\mathrm{des}
; that is,

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}x^{\mathrm{est}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}x^{\mathrm{des}(\sigma)}.

(2)

The polynomials in (
2
) are called the
Eulerian polynomials
. While there is no known closed formula for (
2
) similar to that in (
1
), there is an exponential generating function whose coefficients are the Eulerian polynomials; see
[
8
]
.

A well-known Eulerian statistic is
\mathrm{exc}
, which we define now. An
excedance
of
\sigma
is a position
j
for which
\sigma_{j}>j
. Using similar language to that for descents, we will refer to the value
\sigma_{j}
as the
excedance top
(we will use this terminology more in Sections
4
and
5
). The
excedance number

\mathrm{exc}(\sigma)=\#\{j\,|\,\sigma_{j}>j\}

counts the number of excedances in
\sigma
. MacMahon first proved
\mathrm{exc}
is Eulerian in
[
12
]
, while Foata offered a combinatorial proof in
[
6
]
.

Eventually research turned to the study bivariate distributions. A pair of permutation statistics
(\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
is
Euler-Mahonian
if it is equidistributed with
(\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ})
; that is,

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}x^{\mathrm{est}(\sigma)}q^{\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}x^{\mathrm{des}(\sigma)}q^{\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)}

(3)

Note (
3
) implies that
\mathrm{est}
must be Eulerian and
\mathrm{MST}
must be Mahonian. There are several known Euler-Mahonian pairs of statistics. These include
(\mathrm{exc},\mathrm{DEN})
, where
\mathrm{DEN}
is
Denert’s statistic
described in Section
4
;
(\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAK})
with
\mathrm{MAK}
as defined in Section
5
; and
(\mathrm{stc},\mathrm{INV})
, where
\mathrm{stc}
is discussed in Section
3
. The pair
(\mathrm{exc},\mathrm{DEN})
was first proven to be Euler-Mahonian in
[
9
]
; this fact was proven bijectively in
[
10
]
. The other two pairs were proven Euler-Mahonian in
[
5
]
and
[
16
]
, respectively.

We will call a permutation statistic
\mathrm{sst}
a
Stirling
statistic if

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}y^{\mathrm{sst}(\sigma)}=y(y+1)(y+2)\cdots(y+n-1).

(4)

That is,
\mathrm{sst}
has the same generating function as the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind. See
[
17
, Proposition 1.3.7]
.

We describe several examples of Stirling statistics. The first is the
number of cycles
in a decomposition of
\sigma
into disjoint cycles, including those of length
1
, which is denoted
\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)
. A value
\sigma_{j}
is a
right-to-left minimum
of
\sigma
if it is smaller than all values to its right in the word for
\sigma
, so
\sigma_{j}<\sigma_{k}
for all
k>j
. The statistic
\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)
is defined to equal the number of right-to-left minima of
\sigma
. Through symmetry it is easy to show that
\mathrm{rlmin}
has the same distribution on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as the number of
right-to-left maxima
,
left-to-right minima
, and
left-to-right maxima
(see the proof of
[
17
, Proposition 1.3.1]
). It can then be established that
\mathrm{cyc}
and
\mathrm{rlmin}
both satisfy (
4
) via
[
17
, Corollary 1.3.11 ]
.

We will now define a
Stirling-Euler-Mahonian
triple of statistics
(\mathrm{sst},\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
as one that has the same trivariate distribution as the triple
(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ})
; that is,

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}y^{\mathrm{sst}(\sigma)}x^{\mathrm{est}(\sigma)}q^{\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}y^{\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)}x^{\mathrm{des}(\sigma)}q^{\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)}.

(5)

Note (
5
) implies that
\mathrm{sst}
must be a Stirling statistic,
\mathrm{est}
must be Eulerian, and
\mathrm{MST}
must be Mahonian. What we call Stirling-Euler-Mahonian above was first introduced as
cycle-Euler-Mahonian
in
[
1
]
. We feel the revised term better reflects the more general nature of the statistic
\mathrm{sst}
.

Our main goal in this paper will be to prove that several triples involving various permutation statistics are Stirling-Euler-Mahonian. We give a general technique for doing so for any Mahonian permutation statistic that has a permutation code, which we define in the next section. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we define permutation codes and introduce the theorems we will be using to prove the main results of this paper. In the next several sections, we will use these techniques to find Stirling-Euler-Mahonian mates for known Mahonian statistics: the inversion number
\mathrm{INV}
in Section
3
; Denert’s statistic
\mathrm{DEN}
in Section
4
(where we give two such mates); Foata and Zeilberger’s
\mathrm{MAK}

[
9
]
in Section
5
; and Petersen and Wilson’s sorting index
\mathrm{SOR}
in Section
6
. Finally in Section
7
, we prove several properties of Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples that follow from earlier results.

2
Permutation codes and
\mathrm{MAJcode}

In this section we introduce the tools and theorems we will use to prove the main results of this paper. Let
E_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-tuples of integers
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})
satisfying
0\leq e_{j}\leq j-1
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
. Note that
\#E_{n}=n!
. For any
n
-tuple
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})\in E_{n}
, let

\mathrm{sum}((e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}))=e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{n}.

For a Mahonian statistic
\mathrm{MST}
, a bijection
\mathrm{MSTcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
with the property that
\mathrm{sum}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)
is called a
permutation code
for
\mathrm{MST}
.

We will define such a code for
\mathrm{MAJ}
using the work of Skandera
[
16
]
. What we will call the
\mathrm{MAJcode}
, Skandera refers to as the major index table. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and define
\sigma^{(j)}
to be the subword of
\sigma
containing only the values
1,2,\ldots j
. Let

m_{j}(\sigma)=\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma^{(j)})-\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma^{(j-1)}).

Then we can define

\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma)=(m_{1}(\sigma),m_{2}(\sigma),\ldots,m_{n}(\sigma)).

For example
\mathrm{MAJcode}(354162)=(0,0,1,1,3,5)
; see Table
1
for more details. Note that
\mathrm{sum}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(354162))=\mathrm{sum}((0,0,1,1,3,5))=10=\mathrm{MAJ}(354162)
. Skandera
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
proves that
\mathrm{MAJcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
is a bijection. It is clear from the definition that for any
\sigma
,
\mathrm{sum}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)
.

Let
\mathrm{zer}
represent the number of zeros in the code of a permutation. Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

We’ll use induction on
n
. When
n=1
,
\sigma=1
has
\mathrm{MAJ}
-code equal to
0
and the result clearly holds.

Now assume the theorem holds for
n\leq k
, and let
\sigma\in S_{k+1}
. Consider
\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma)=(m_{1}(\sigma),m_{2}(\sigma),\ldots,m_{k}(\sigma),m_{k+1}(\sigma))
. Let
\pi
be the permutation obtained by removing
k+1
from
\sigma
. By the induction hypothesis,
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\pi))=\mathrm{rlmin}(\pi)
. It is clear from the definition that
\mathrm{MAJcode}(\pi)=(m_{1}(\sigma),m_{2}(\sigma),\ldots,m_{k}(\sigma)).
Now replace
k+1
in its original position to form
\sigma
. If
k+1
goes into any of the positions
1,2,\ldots,k
of
\pi
, it will increase
\mathrm{MAJ}
and thus we’ll have
m_{k+1}(\sigma)\neq 0
. So
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\pi))
. It also will not increase the number of right-to-left minima, since
k+1
will always have at least one of the smaller values
1,2,\ldots,k
to its right, so
\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)=\mathrm{rlmin}(\pi)
. Thus in this case,
\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)=\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))
as desired. If
k+1
goes into position
k+1
of
\pi
, it will not increase
\mathrm{MAJ}
and thus we’ll have
m_{k+1}(\sigma)=0
. So
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\pi))+1
. It will also increase the number of right-to-left minima, since the last entry of a permutation is always a right-to-left minimum, so
\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)=\mathrm{rlmin}(\pi)+1
. Thus in this case
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)
, and the result is true in general as desired.
∎

Skandera
[
16
]
also defines a function
\mathrm{st}:E_{n}\to\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
such that

\mathrm{st}((e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}))=\ell,

where
\ell
is the largest number such that
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n})
contains a subsequence
(e_{j_{1}},e_{j_{2}},\ldots,e_{j_{\ell}})
with the property that
e_{j_{k}}\geq k
for
1\leq k\leq\ell
. Note there may be several subsequences with this property. For example
\mathrm{st}((0,0,1,1,3,5))=3
with
(1,3,5)
such a subsequence. Skandera proves the following useful result (see the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{des}(\sigma)
.

We see that
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MAJcode}((354162)))=3=\mathrm{des}(354162)
. Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
provide our bijective method for finding Stirling-Euler-Mahonian mates for any Mahonian statistic
\mathrm{MST}
with a code
\mathrm{MSTcode}
, which we detail in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathrm{MST}
be a Mahonian statistic with permutation code
\mathrm{MSTcode}
. Then the triple
(\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode}),\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode}),\mathrm{MST})
is Stirling-Euler-Mahonian.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{MSTcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
and
\mathrm{MAJcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
are both bijections, the map
\mathcal{C}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
defined by

\mathcal{C}=(\mathrm{MSTcode})^{-1}\circ\mathrm{MAJcode}

is also a bijection. Then
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma))
, so
\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)=\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma)))
by Proposition
2.1
. Also
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma)))
, so
\mathrm{des}(\sigma)=\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma)))
by Proposition
2.2
. Finally,
\mathrm{sum}(\mathrm{MAJcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{sum}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma)))
, so
\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)=\mathrm{MST}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma))
. Thus this bijection has the property that

(\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma),\mathrm{des}(\sigma),\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma))=\\
(\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma)),\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma))),\mathrm{MST}(\mathcal{C}(\sigma))),

for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, making
(\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode}),\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode}),\mathrm{MST})
a Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triple as desired.
∎

Note that Theorem
2.3
is an extension of Corollary 3.2 of
[
16
]
. We will use Theorem
2.3
throughout the rest of this paper, finding familiar interpretations for
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{MSTcode})
or
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{MSTcode})
whenever possible.

3
A Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triple for
\mathrm{INV}

We begin with a simple application of Theorem
2.3
. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation. For each
j
,
1\leq j\leq n
, we can let

i_{j}(\sigma)=\#\{k>j|\sigma_{j}>\sigma_{k}\}.

The value
i_{j}(\sigma)
is counting the number of inversions in
\sigma
for which
\sigma_{j}
is the bigger number. Then define
\mathrm{INVcode}(\sigma)=(i_{n}(\sigma),i_{n-1}(\sigma),\ldots,i_{1}(\sigma))
. The
i_{j}
terms are written with indices in descending order so that
\mathrm{INVcode}(\sigma)
is an element of
E_{n}
. Note that
\mathrm{INVcode}
is referred to as the Lehmer code in the literature
[
11
]
. For example,
\mathrm{INVcode}(341625)=(0,0,2,0,2,2)
.

We state the following well-known theorem and give a brief proof.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{INVcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

We have
i_{j}(\sigma)=0
if and only if there are no entries to right of
\sigma_{j}
that are less than
\sigma_{j}
, which is true if and only if
\sigma_{j}
is a right-to-left minimum.
∎

Skandera defines
\mathrm{stc}(\sigma)=\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{INVcode}(\sigma))
in
[
16
]
. It is not known to be equal to any familiar Eulerian statistic calculated directly from the permutation
\sigma
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The triple
(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{stc},\mathrm{INV})
is Stirling-Euler-Mahonian.

Proof.

Follows directly from Theorem
2.3
and Proposition
3.1
.
∎

4
Two Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples for
\mathrm{DEN}

4.1
Two definitions of
\mathrm{DEN}

Denert’s statistic

\mathrm{DEN}
has two equivalent definitions, both of which will be useful to us. The first definition is given by

\mathrm{DEN}(\sigma)=\#\{j<k\,|\,\sigma_{k}<\sigma_{j}\leq k\}+\\
\#\{j<k\,|\,\sigma_{j}\leq k<\sigma_{k}\}+\#\{j<k\,|\,k<\sigma_{k}<\sigma_{j}\}.

(6)

The second formulation of
\mathrm{DEN}
is easier to work with, but first requires some definitions. For a permutation
\sigma
, the
excedance subword

\sigma_{E}
is the subword of all excedances tops in the order that they occur in
\sigma
, and the
non-excedance subword

\sigma_{N}
is the subword of all non-excedance tops in the order that they occur in
\sigma
. Then

\mathrm{DEN}(\sigma)=\mathrm{INV}(\sigma_{E})+\mathrm{INV}(\sigma_{N})+\sum_{\sigma_{j}>j}j.

(7)

Note the last term in (
7
) is the sum of the excedances of
\sigma
. See
[
9
]
for a proof of the equivalence of definitions (
6
) and (
7
), along with a proof that
\mathrm{DEN}
is Mahonian.

For example, for
\sigma=354162
using the first definition of
\mathrm{DEN}
we get
\mathrm{DEN}(354162)=6+5+1=12
. Alternately using the second definition, we have
\sigma_{E}=3546
,
\sigma_{N}=12
,
\mathrm{INV}(\sigma_{E})=1
, and
\mathrm{INV}(\sigma_{N})=0
, so
\mathrm{DEN}(354162)=1+0+1+2+3+5=12
.

Denert’s statistic has two known permutation codes, which lead to different results related to
\mathrm{DEN}
and Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples.

4.2
\mathrm{DENcode}

The first code for
\mathrm{DEN}
comes from Foata and Zeilberger
[
9
]
, which we will denote
\mathrm{DENcode}
. It is based on the definition of
\mathrm{DEN}
given in (
6
). We let

d_{j}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}\#\{k<j\,|\,\sigma_{j}<\sigma_{k}\leq j\}&\mathrm{if}\,\sigma_{j}\leq j\\
\#\{k<j\,|\,\sigma_{k}\leq j\}+\#\{k<j\,|\,\sigma_{j}<\sigma_{k}\}&\mathrm{if}\,\sigma_{j}>j\end{cases}.

Note the value of
d_{j}(\sigma)
is just the contribution of position
j
to
\mathrm{DEN}(\sigma)
from (
6
). Using this fact it is easy to see that
0\leq d_{j}(\sigma)\leq j-1
. We can then define
\mathrm{DENcode}(\sigma)=(d_{1}(\sigma),d_{2}(\sigma),\ldots,d_{n}(\sigma))
. For example,
\mathrm{DENcode}(341625)=(0,0,0,3,2,1)
. This map is shown to be invertible in
[
9
]
, making it clear that
\mathrm{DENcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
is a bijection. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

The triple
(\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{DENcode}),\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{DENcode}),\mathrm{DEN})
is Stirling-Euler-Mahonian.

Proof.

Follows directly from Theorem
2.3
.
∎

We checked the values of the statistics
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{DENcode})
and
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{DENcode})
on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
for
n=2,3,4
in the FindStat database for combinatorial statistics
[
15
]
and received no direct matches for either. This provides evidence that
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{DENcode})
is not equal to any familiar Stirling statistic, nor is
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{DENcode})
equal to any familiar Eulerian statistic.

4.3
\mathrm{HANcode}

The second code for
\mathrm{DEN}
is due to Han
[
10
]
, so we will denote it
\mathrm{HANcode}
. It is based on the definition of
\mathrm{DEN}
given in (
7
). It has a complicated recursive definition as a function
E_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
(instead of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
as we have seen with other examples above). But since the function defined is a bijection, it still represents a pairing between a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and a unique element
\mathrm{HANcode}(\sigma)\in E_{n}
. We will describe Han’s recursive process.

When
n=1
, the only permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
is
\sigma=1
and
\mathrm{DEN}(\sigma)=0
, so we have

\mathrm{HANcode}(\sigma)=0.

For
n\geq 2
, Han defines a function
\Psi:\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}\times\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
which allows us to recursively find the permutation associated to a given
\mathrm{HANcode}
in
E_{n}
. Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
. If
s=0
, then
\Psi(\sigma,s)=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n-1}n
.

If
s\neq 0
, first we must compute the
\nu(\sigma_{r})
values of
\sigma
for
1\leq r\leq n-1
, where

\nu(\sigma_{r})=\begin{cases}\#\{j\geq\sigma_{r}|j\ \textrm{is an excedance of}\ \sigma\}&\textrm{if}\ r\ \textrm{is an excedance of}\ \sigma\\
\#\{j\leq\sigma_{r}|j\ \textrm{is a non-excedance of}\ \sigma\}+\mathrm{exc}(\sigma)&\textrm{if}\ r\ \textrm{is a non-excedance of}\ \sigma\end{cases}

Note the
\nu(\sigma_{r})
values equal to
1,2,\ldots,\mathrm{exc}(\sigma)
will correspond to the excedance tops of
\sigma
from largest to smallest, and the
\nu(\sigma_{r})
values equal to
exc(\sigma)+1,\ldots,n
will correspond to the non-excedance tops of
\sigma
from smallest to largest. Next underline each excedance top
\sigma_{j}
such that
\sigma_{j}\geq\nu^{-1}(s)
, and let

\{\nu^{-1}(1),\nu^{-1}(2),\ldots,\nu^{-1}(m)\}

(8)

be these underlined values. Then swap excedance tops in the following fashion:
n
replaces
\nu^{-1}(1)
(the largest underlined value, from the remarks above),
\nu^{-1}(1)
replaces
\nu^{-1}(2)
, …,
\nu^{-1}(m-1)
replaces
\nu^{-1}(m)
. Finally, insert the value
p=\nu^{-1}(m)
in position
\nu^{-1}(s)
. Then
\Psi(\sigma,s)
is the permutation obtained through this procedure.

The statistic
\mathrm{MAK}
was first introduced in
[

\mathrm{MAK}
from
[

descent block
of a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a maximal decreasing subword of
\sigma

embraces

\sigma_{j}
of
\sigma

\sigma_{j}
is strictly between the opener and closer of the block. The
right embracing number
of a value
\sigma_{j}
of
\sigma

\mathrm{Rem}(\sigma_{j})

\sigma_{j}
in
\sigma
that embrace
\sigma_{j}
. We can then define

\mathrm{MAK}(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma_{j+1}>\sigma_{j}}\sigma_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Rem}(\sigma_{j}).

Note the first term is just the sum of all the descent bottoms of
\sigma
.

For example, consider the permutation
\sigma=354162

,
\mathrm{Rem}(5)=1

\mathrm{Rem}(6)=0

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Phi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the bijection described above and suppose

\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)=\mathrm{rlmin}(\Phi(\sigma))
.

Proof.

Let
\tau=\Phi(\sigma)
. Suppose
\sigma_{i}
is a right-to-left minimum of
\sigma
. Since
\sigma_{i}
is smaller than all value to its right in
\sigma
, in particular
\sigma_{i+1}>\sigma_{i}
. Thus
\sigma_{i}
is the closer for a (possibly outsider) descent block in
\sigma
, and hence
\sigma_{i}
must be a non-descent top of
\sigma
. Therefore the image
\tau_{j}
of
\sigma_{i}
under the map
\Phi
is in the subword
g^{\prime}
. Additionally,
\sigma_{i}
being smaller than every entry to its right implies that no descent block to the right of
\sigma_{i}
can embrace
\sigma_{i}
. Thus the right embracing number of
\sigma_{i}
in
\sigma
is
0
. Hence the location of
\tau_{j}
in the subword
g^{\prime}
under the map
\Phi
is chosen as to have inversion top number equal to
0
. This implies that no entry in
\tau^{\prime}
to the right of
\tau_{j}
is less than
\tau_{j}
, hence
\tau_{j}
is a right-to-left minimum of
\tau^{\prime}
. We will now show that
\tau_{j}
is also a right-to-left minimum of
\tau
.

We know
\tau_{j}
must be in the subword
g^{\prime}
, but more generally all the right-to-left minima of
\tau^{\prime}
must be in the subword
g^{\prime}
. The right-to-left minima in a permutation must appear in ascending order when reading the permutation word from left to right, since a right-to-left minimum cannot have a smaller right-to-left minimum to its right. So the right-to-left minima in
g^{\prime}
must appear in ascending order. Upon placing the top row of
\tau^{\prime}
in ascending order to obtain
\tau
, because the entries in
g
are in ascending order it is clear that the values of
g^{\prime}
will remain in the same order relative to one another in
\tau
. The only way
\tau_{j}
could fail to be a right-to-left minimum of
\tau
would be if there were a right-to-left minimum smaller than
\tau_{j}
to the right of
\tau_{j}
in the word for
\tau
. This cannot happen with any values from the subword
g^{\prime}
for the reasons given above. All of the values in
f^{\prime}
are excedance tops in
\tau^{\prime}
, and will remain excedance tops when reordered to produce
\tau
. So none of the values from
f^{\prime}
can be right-to-left minima to the right of
\tau_{j}
, again by Lemma
4.3
. Hence
\tau_{j}
has no right-to-left minimum smaller than
\tau_{j}
to its right in the word for
\tau
. Thus
\tau_{j}
is a right-to-left minimum of
\tau
.

Now suppose
\tau_{j}
is a right-to-left minimum of
\tau
. By Lemma
4.3
,
\tau_{j}
must be a non-excedance top of
\tau
. Also since
\tau_{j}
is less than all entries to its right, the inversion top number of
\tau_{j}
is
0
. Hence under the bijection
\Phi
,
\tau_{j}
is the image of an entry
\sigma_{i}
that is a non-descent top with right embracing number
0
. Since
\sigma_{i}
is a non-descent top, note we must have that
\tau_{j}
is in the word
g^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{i+1}>\sigma_{i}
. Let
\sigma_{k}
be the opener of the descent block with closer
\sigma_{i+1}
. But then we must have
\sigma_{k}>\sigma_{i}
, otherwise the descent block will embrace
\sigma_{i}
, contradicting that the right embracing number of
\sigma_{i}
is
0
. We also must have that
\sigma_{k+1}>\sigma_{k}
since
\sigma_{k+1}
begins a new descent block. But then we also have that
\sigma_{k+1}>\sigma_{i}
, so the opener of the descent block with closer
\sigma_{k+1}
must also be greater than
\sigma_{i}
, otherwise this descent block will embrace
\sigma_{i}
. We continue in this manner until we consider all entries to the right of
\sigma_{i}
, all of which must be greater than
\sigma_{i}
. Thus
\sigma_{i}
is a right-to-left minimum of
\sigma
.

Since the right-to-left minima of
\sigma
are in one-to-one correspondence with the right-to-left minima of

, we conclude that

.

that is the smallest number of steps away from

when moving from
j
to
k
along the cycle
C
. We then define the sorting index by the equation

\mathrm{SOR}(\sigma)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j-c_{j}).

See
[
13
,
18
]
for more on
\mathrm{SOR}
, including a proof that it is Mahonian. As an example, we see that
\sigma=354162
has disjoint cycle decomposition
(134)(256)
, so
c_{1}(\sigma)=1
,
c_{2}(\sigma)=2
,
c_{3}(\sigma)=1
,
c_{4}(\sigma)=1
,
c_{5}(\sigma)=2
, and
c_{6}(\sigma)=2
. Hence

\mathrm{SOR}(354162)=(1-1)+(2-2)+(3-1)+(4-1)+(5-2)+(6-2)=12
.

It is clear from the definition that
1\leq c_{j}\leq j
for all
j
, so
0\leq j-c_{j}\leq j-1
. We let

s_{j}(\sigma)=j-c_{j}(\sigma)

and define
\mathrm{SORcode}(\sigma)=(s_{1}(\sigma),s_{2}(\sigma),\ldots,s_{n}(\sigma))
. This is the
B
-code of
[
4
]
, where it is proven that
\mathrm{SORcode}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to E_{n}
is a bijection. For example,
\mathrm{SORcode}(341625)=(0,0,2,2,3,1)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{SORcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

If
j
is the smallest value in its cycle
C
, then we get that
c_{j}(\sigma)=j
and thus
s_{j}(\sigma)=0
. If
j
is not the smallest value in its cycle then
c_{j}(\sigma)<j
, so
s_{j}(\sigma)>0
. Hence
\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{SORcode}(\sigma))=\mathrm{cyc}(\sigma)
as desired.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

The triple
(\mathrm{cyc},\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{SORcode}),\mathrm{SOR})
is Stirling-Euler-Mahonian.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem
2.3
and Proposition
6.1
.
∎

We checked the values of the statistic
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{SORcode})
on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
for
n=2,3,4
in the FindStat database for combinatorial statistics
[
15
]
and received no direct matches. This provides evidence that
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{SORcode})
is not equal to any familiar Eulerian statistic.

7
Properties of Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples

In this paper we have established that the following triples of permutation statistics are Stirling-Euler-Mahonian:

•

(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{stc},\mathrm{INV})
,

•

(\mathrm{zer}(\mathrm{DENcode}),\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{DENcode}),\mathrm{DEN})
,

•

(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{exc},\mathrm{DEN})
,

•

(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAK})
, and

•

(\mathrm{cyc},\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{SORcode}),\mathrm{SOR})
.

In addition, it was shown that the triple of statistics

(\mathrm{cyc}(\mathrm{DG}),\mathrm{des},\mathrm{s}_{\mathbb{T}_{n},\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{DG})+\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{DG}))

described in
[
1
]
is Stirling-Euler-Mahonian. The results in this section apply to these or any other Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triples.

Let
f(q)=\sum_{j=M}^{N}a_{j}q^{j}
be a polynomial in
q
with
a_{M},a_{N}\neq 0
. We call
M+N
the
virtual degree
of
f(q)
. The polynomial
f(q)
is called
symmetric
provided
a_{N-j}=a_{M+j}
for
0\leq j\leq N-M
, and
unimodal
provided there exists a
P
,
M\leq P\leq N
, such that
a_{M}\leq\cdots\leq a_{P}\geq a_{P+1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{N}.
Recall the
q
-analog
of the natural number
m
is given by
[m]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{m-1}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(\mathrm{sst},\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
be any Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triple of permutation statistics. Then for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomial

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ \mathrm{est}(\sigma)=k-1}[m]_{q}^{\mathrm{sst}(\sigma)}q^{(n-\mathrm{sst}(\sigma))(m-1)+\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)}

in
q
is symmetric and unimodal with virtual degree
n(m+k-2)+(k-1)(m-1)
.

Proof.

In
[
2
]
it was proven that

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ \mathrm{des}(\sigma)=k-1}[m]_{q}^{\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)}q^{(n-\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma))(m-1)+\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)}

is symmetric and unimodal with virtual degree
n(m+k-2)+(k-1)(m-1)
. But
(\mathrm{sst},\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
is equidistributed with
(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ})
, so the result holds.
∎

For the next theorem, we need to define several additional
q
-analogs. For any real number
y
, the
q
-analog of the real number
y
is given by

[y]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{y}}{1-q}.

Note this generalizes the definition of the
q
-analog of a natural number. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the
q
-analog of
m!
is given by
[m]_{q}!=[1]_{q}[2]_{q}\cdots[m-1]_{q}[m]_{q}
. The
q
-analog of the binomial coefficient
is

{k\brack j}=\frac{[k]_{q}[k-1]_{q}\cdots[k-j+1]_{q}}{[j]_{q}!}.

Note this definition makes sense even if
k
is a real number.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
(\mathrm{sst},\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
be any Stirling-Euler-Mahonian triple of permutation statistics. Then for any
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k\leq n
we have

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ \mathrm{est}(\sigma)=k-1}[y]_{q}^{\mathrm{sst}(\sigma)}q^{(n-\mathrm{sst}(\sigma))(y-1)+\mathrm{MST}(\sigma)}=\\
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{y+n\brack k-j-1}{y+j-1\brack j}(-1)^{k-j-1}q^{\binom{k-j-1}{2}}[y+j]_{q}^{n}.

Proof.

In
[
3
]
it was proven that

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ \mathrm{des}(\sigma)=k-1}[y]_{q}^{\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)}q^{(n-\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma))(y-1)+\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)}=\\
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{y+n\brack k-j-1}{y+j-1\brack j}(-1)^{k-j-1}q^{\binom{k-j-1}{2}}[y+j]_{q}^{n}.

But
(\mathrm{sst},\mathrm{est},\mathrm{MST})
is equidistributed with
(\mathrm{rlmin},\mathrm{des},\mathrm{MAJ})
, so the result holds.
∎

In
[

]
the
q,y
-Eulerian numbers

E_{n,k}(q,y)

But since

Theorem 7.3
.

Let

be as defined in (

Since

E_{n,k}(q,y)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ \mathrm{des}(\sigma)=k-1}[y]_{q}^{\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma)}q^{(n-\mathrm{rlmin}(\sigma))(y-1)+\mathrm{MAJ}(\sigma)}.

In
