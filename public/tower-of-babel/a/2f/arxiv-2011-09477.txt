Dark Matter Detection With Bound Nuclear Targets: The Poisson Phonon Tail
Yonatan Kahn, Gordan Krnjaic, Bashi Mandava

Abstract
Dark matter (DM) scattering with nuclei in solid-state systems may produce elastic nuclear recoil at high energies and single-phonon excitation at low energies. When the dark matter momentum is comparable to the momentum spread of nuclei bound in a lattice, q 0 = 2 ​ m N ​ ω 0 q_{0}=\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}} where m N m_{N} is the mass of the nucleus and ω 0 \omega_{0} is the optical phonon energy, an intermediate scattering regime characterized by multi-phonon excitations emerges. We study a greatly simplified model of a single nucleus in a harmonic potential and show that, while the mean energy deposited for a given momentum transfer q q is equal to the elastic value q 2 / ( 2 ​ m N ) q^{2}/(2m_{N}) , the phonon occupation number follows a Poisson distribution and thus the energy spread is Δ ​ E = q ​ ω 0 / ( 2 ​ m N ) \Delta E=q\sqrt{\omega_{0}/(2m_{N})} . This observation suggests that low-threshold calorimetric detectors may have significantly increased sensitivity to sub-GeV DM compared to the expectation from elastic scattering, even when the energy threshold is above the single-phonon energy, by exploiting the tail of the Poisson distribution for phonons above the elastic energy. We use a simple model of electronic excitations to argue that this multi-phonon signal will also accompany ionization signals induced from DM-electron scattering or the Migdal effect. In well-motivated models where DM couples to a heavy, kinetically-mixed dark photon, we show that these signals can probe experimental milestones for cosmological DM production via thermal freeze-out, including the thermal target for Majorana fermion DM.

The strategy of searching for dark matter (DM) by detecting the energy imparted to a nucleus during a scattering process dates back to the original proposals of Goodman and Witten
Goodman and Witten 1985
and Drukier, Freese, and Spergel
Drukier et al. 1986
and forms the basis for the multi-ton liquid noble experiments XENONnT
Aprile et al. 2020
and LZ
Akerib et al. 2020
. In recent years, a vast landscape of plausible DM models has opened up at much lighter DM masses compared to the GeV-scale WIMPs these experiments were originally designed to search for (see
Battaglieri et al. 2017
and references therein), necessitating a rethinking of the basic assumptions that went into the calculations of the nuclear scattering rates. In particular, for
elastic
scattering of free nuclei, the energy transfer between the DM and nucleus is inefficient if the DM mass is much below the nucleus mass: for a xenon nucleus of mass
m_{N}=122\ {\rm GeV}
and DM of mass
m_{\chi}=100\ {\rm MeV}
, the typical momentum transfer is
q\sim m_{\chi}v\sim 150\ {\rm keV}
, and the recoiling nucleus has energy
q^{2}/(2m_{N})\sim 0.1\ {\rm eV}
, well below the thresholds of typical noble liquid detectors.

However, as emphasized in recent work
Trickle et al. 2020
;
Griffin et al. 2020
, a nucleus in a lattice is not a free particle, and therefore the elastic scattering argument does not necessarily apply for solid-state detectors. Indeed, an individual nucleus in a solid is subject to a harmonic oscillator potential from its neighboring atoms

\displaystyle V(r)\approx\frac{1}{2}m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}r^{2},

(1)

where
\omega_{0}\sim 60\ {\rm meV}
is a typical optical phonon energy for silicon (for other materials,
\omega_{0}\in[20\ {\rm meV},140\ {\rm meV}]
). This potential is short-range, acting only over interatomic distances
a\sim({\rm keV})^{-1}
, and thus the displacement energy required to remove a nucleus from its lattice site is
E_{d}\sim\frac{1}{2}m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}a^{2}\sim\pazocal{O}(10\ {\rm eV})
. Only for recoil energies
E_{R}
well above
E_{d}
may the final-state nucleus be well-approximated as a free particle; below
E_{d}
, the final-state energy spectrum is that of a harmonic oscillator, and of course the initial state is the ground state of a harmonic oscillator rather than a zero-momentum plane wave. This observation has previously been made in the context of single-phonon excitations in solids
Knapen et al. 2018
;
Griffin et al. 2018
, where DM with de Broglie wavelength exceeding the interatomic spacing interacts primarily with collective modes of many oscillating nuclei, which are quantized into a phonon spectrum.

In this Letter, we study the intermediate regime where the momentum transfer satisfies
q\gg 1/a
, such that the interaction is localized to a single lattice site, but
E_{R}\lesssim E_{d}
so that
both
the initial and final nuclear states belong to the harmonic oscillator spectrum. We construct a toy model of a solid-state detector by considering the non-relativistic single-particle quantum mechanics of a nucleus subject to the potential (
1
). The characteristic momentum spread of the ground state is

\displaystyle q_{0}=\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}},

(2)

where
q_{0}\approx 56\ {\rm keV}
for silicon. We will show that when a momentum transfer
q>q_{0}
is kinematically allowed, the typical energy deposited is

\displaystyle\overline{E}_{R}=\left(\frac{q}{q_{0}}\right)^{2}\omega_{0}=\frac{q^{2}}{2m_{N}}.

(3)

This is the energy expected from elastic scattering, but here it may be interpreted as the production of a multi-phonon state with occupation number
n=(q/q_{0})^{2}
.

We will show that in this greatly simplified model of a single oscillator, the distribution of phonon number is
exactly
Poissonian, so that unlike elastic scattering where only a single energy
E
is allowed for a given
q
, there is a spread
\Delta n=q/q_{0}
corresponding to an energy spread

\displaystyle\Delta E_{R}=\left(\frac{q}{q_{0}}\right)\omega_{0}=q\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{0}}{2m_{N}}}.

(4)

This observation extends the reach of solid-state nuclear recoil detectors to sub-GeV DM compared to previous analyses which assumed the kinematics of elastic scattering. As a cross-check, we show that our results parametrically reduce to the previously-calculated single-phonon rate when
q\ll q_{0}
, offering a pleasing interpretation of the single-phonon rate as an upward Poisson fluctuation when the elastic energy is well below the phonon energy; our results converge exactly on the expected elastic recoil spectrum when
q\gg q_{0}
. Our work complements that of
Trickle et al. 2020
;
Griffin et al. 2020
by explicitly showing the transition between the single-phonon and elastic regimes in a simple model (see also
Campbell-Deem et al. 2020
which considers production of multiple acoustic phonons for
q\ll q_{0}
). We will show that when the DM-nucleus interaction is mediated by a heavy dark photon, the full single- and multi-phonon spectrum can probe a wide range of thermal relic targets, including Majorana DM which suffers a velocity-suppressed cross section.

Harmonic Oscillator Model

We consider a single nucleus of mass
m_{N}
subject to a 3-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator potential:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{N}^{2}}{2m_{N}}+\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{2}.

(5)

The energy eigenstates are
|\vec{n}\rangle
, where
\vec{n}=\{n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}\}
, with energies
E_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega_{0}
, where
n=n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}
. To model galactic DM particles
\chi
scattering from this nucleus, we follow
Essig et al. 2016
and write the total scattering rate for a detector with
N_{T}
nuclear targets as

\displaystyle R=N_{T}\frac{\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{p}}{8\pi\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{\mathbf{q}}}{q}\sum_{n}|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}\eta(v_{\rm min}^{(n)}),

(6)

where
\rho_{\chi}/m_{\chi}=0.3\,{\rm cm}^{-3}({\rm GeV}/m_{\chi})
is the local DM number density
de Salas 2020
,
Z
is the atomic number of the target and
\overline{\sigma}_{p}
is a fiducial DM-proton cross section; for concreteness we restrict our attention to a contact interaction between DM and protons, but our results are easily generalizable to other interactions. We do not include a nuclear form factor because the momentum transfers we consider for sub-GeV DM will always be smaller than the inverse nuclear size,
q\ll 1/R_{0}\sim\pazocal{O}(10\ {\rm MeV})
.

\displaystyle|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}=\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}|\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle|^{2}\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\eta(v_{\rm min}^{(n)})=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{\mathbf{v}}}{v}f_{\chi}(\mathbf{v})\,\Theta\!\left(v-v_{\rm min}^{(n)}\right)\penalty\ ,

\displaystyle v_{\rm min}^{(n)}=\frac{n\omega_{0}}{q}+\frac{q}{2m_{\chi}}\penalty\ ,

n

We can calculate the form factor analytically using either momentum-space wavefunctions or the harmonic oscillator algebra. Due to the isotropy of the harmonic oscillator, the angular integrals can also be performed analytically. Defining an angular average
|f(n,q)|^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\mathbf{q}}|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}

\displaystyle|f(n,q)|^{2}=\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{q}{q_{0}}\right)^{2n}\!\!e^{-q^{2}\!/q_{0}^{2}}\penalty\ .

(10)

While to our knowledge this result has not appeared before in the DM literature, it is known in the case of neutron scattering, see for example
Schober 2014

Performing the angular average in Eq. (
6
) and using in Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{dR}{dq}=\!\frac{N_{T}\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{p}}{2\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\sum_{n}\frac{q}{n!}\left(\frac{q}{q_{0}}\right)^{2n}\!\!e^{-q^{2}\!/q_{0}^{2}}\,\eta(v_{\rm min}^{(n)}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

The form factor in (
10

n
with mean
\bar{n}=q^{2}\!/q_{0}^{2}=q^{2}/(2m_{N}\omega_{0})
. Therefore, the mean energy deposited in a DM-nuclear scattering is
\bar{n}\omega_{0}=q^{2}/(2m_{N})
, the elastic value. In the limit
q^{2}\gg q_{0}^{2}
, the Poisson distribution approaches a delta function
\delta(n-\bar{n})
; taking the continuum limit
n\to E_{R}/\omega_{0}
,
\sum_{n}\to\int dn
in Eq. (
6
),
and replacing
qdq=m_{N}dE_{R}
, we recover the usual elastic recoil spectrum

\displaystyle\frac{dR}{dE_{R}}\approx N_{T}m_{N}\frac{\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{p}}{2\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\,\eta(v_{\rm min}^{(\bar{n})}),

(12)

with
v_{\rm min}^{(\bar{n})}=\sqrt{m_{N}E_{R}/(2\mu_{\chi N}^{2})}

\omega_{0}\ll E_{R}<E_{d}
such that the nucleus remains bound in the harmonic oscillator potential.

Consider now the opposite limit,
q^{2}\ll q_{0}^{2}
. From
Knapen et al. 2018
;
Griffin et al. 2018
;
Trickle et al. 2020
;
Griffin et al. 2020

n=1
phonons when
\bar{n}\ll 1
. While the highest-probability outcome is producing no phonons,
n=0

n=1
, with larger phonon numbers strongly suppressed by powers of
q^{2}\!/q_{0}^{2}\ll 1
.

The advantage of our model is that we can now seamlessly interpolate between the single-phonon regime
q^{2}\ll q_{0}^{2}
and the elastic regime
q^{2}\gg q_{0}^{2}
. When
q^{2}\sim q_{0}^{2}
, as is the case for kinematics of 50 MeV DM in the Standard Halo Model, the Poissonian fluctuations in phonon number become important. In the right panel of Fig.
2
we show representative quantized spectra that illustrate this behavior. Indeed, consider a detector with threshold close to
\omega_{0}
. If the scattering were purely elastic, DM with maximum momentum

could not produce a detectable nuclear recoil. However, for

, the Poisson probability for
n=2

n=1

A similar version of this argument applies to detectors with thresholds somewhat above the optical phonon energy: the Poisson tail of events with large
n
permits sensitivity to smaller DM masses than would be expected based on elastic kinematics alone.

Migdal Effect and Electron Scattering

Nearly a century ago it was pointed out by Migdal
Migdal 1939
that a sudden impulse delivered to the nucleus could result in electronic transitions in atoms. In recent years this observation has become increasingly relevant for light DM searches because the ionization energy may greatly exceed the elastic energy (for calorimetric detectors) or quenched elastic energy (for ionization detectors) from nuclear recoil, improving the possibility of detecting nuclear recoil events through secondary ionization even if the nuclear recoil energy is below the detector threshold.

However, as noted in
Baxter et al. 2020
, the theoretical formalism of the Migdal effect only applies to isolated atoms, where the energy eigenstates are free-particle plane waves and thus a unitary transformation may be applied to transform to the frame of the recoiling nucleus. The overlap between this frame and the stationary frame leads to a factor of
\exp(i\mathbf{q}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{e})
in matrix elements between electronic states, where
\mathbf{q}_{e}\equiv(m_{e}/m_{N})\mathbf{q}
is suppressed from the physical momentum transfer
\mathbf{q}
by the ratio of electron to nuclear masses. Despite the rather limited validity of the formalism, theoretical estimates of Migdal rates have been performed for semiconductors
Ibe et al. 2018
, including for the valence bands
Essig et al. 2020
, where the effective mass of the electron may differ considerably from the vacuum mass
m_{e}
used in the standard Migdal calculation.

While we do not attempt to provide a rigorous derivation of the Migdal effect in solid-state systems in this paper (especially because many-body electron states such as the plasmon may contribute significantly
Kurinsky et al. 2020
;
Kozaczuk and Lin 2020
), we consider a simplified model in the spirit of our nuclear scattering analysis, a nucleus in a harmonic potential which exerts a potential
V_{e}
on a single electron:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{N}^{2}}{2m_{N}}+\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{2}+V_{e}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e}).

(16)

Transforming to relative and center-of-mass coordinates,
\hat{\mathbf{r}}\equiv\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e}
,
\hat{\mathbf{R}}=(m_{N}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}+m_{e}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e})/(m_{N}+m_{e})
, the Hamiltonian becomes separable up to small perturbations (see the Supplementary Material),
and the eigenstates can be written
|\Psi\rangle=|\vec{n};\psi_{e}\rangle
, where the
first label is the harmonic oscillator state and
\psi_{e}(\mathbf{r})
is the electronic
wavefunction. The generalized transition amplitudes from Eq. (
7

factorize into oscillator and ionization terms

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|\Psi\rangle\simeq\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{R}}}|0\rangle\langle\psi_{e}^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}_{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}|\psi_{e}\rangle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

We see that in this model, the electronic spectrum is unmodified to leading order in

\displaystyle v_{\rm min}^{(n,e)}=\frac{E_{e}+n\omega_{0}}{q}+\frac{q}{2m_{\chi}}.

Compared to previous results on isolated atoms

\mathbf{r}_{e}
is unity up to
\pazocal{O}(m_{e}/m_{N})
, the electron scattering matrix element is independent of the vacuum electron mass to leading order and can be computed entirely with band-structure wavefunctions which fully take into account the effective mass.

Our arguments about electronic excitations are fairly general but rely on an electronic potential which is a function only of
\mathbf{r}_{N}-\mathbf{r}_{e}
and a system which is separable enough that it can be treated as a two-body problem and decomposed into relative and center-of-mass coordinates. At this point it is unclear to us how well these assumptions hold in realistic solid-state systems (at the very least, this analysis completely ignores electron-phonon interactions and anisotropies due to the lattice structure which are likely to couple the two spectra at some level beyond simple kinematics), but regardless, our analysis of the nuclear scattering matrix element suggests that the Poisson tail may help push the nuclear recoil energies above threshold. A detector capable of converting ionization energy to phonons, such as the low-threshold calorimetric detectors used by SuperCDMS
Kurinsky et al. 2017
;
Agnese et al. 2018
;
Alkhatib et al. 2020
and EDELWEISS
Armengaud et al. 2019
;
Arnaud et al. 2020
, could observe the Poisson phonon spectrum of nuclear recoil simultaneously with the electron-hole pairs created by ionization, which could help distinguish between true scattering events and low-momentum-transfer background processes such as charge leakage.

Conclusions

In this Letter we have constructed a simple quantum-mechanical model which describes the multi-phonon regime of DM-nuclear scattering in solid-state systems, interpolating between the elastic regime and single-phonon production when the energy deposit is less than the displacement energy
E_{d}
. Our key finding is that when the elastic energy
q^{2}/2m_{N}
is close to the optical phonon energy
\omega_{0}
, there is an order-1 variance in the number of phonons produced, such that the sensitivity of low-threshold detectors to low-mass DM is stronger than previously expected from elastic kinematics alone. This observation suggests that detectors with an especially large optical phonon energy, such as diamond
Kurinsky et al. 2019
, may be able to take advantage of these Poisson fluctuations even when the detector threshold is somewhat above the single-phonon energy.

As an illustrative example, we have shown that plausible next-generation calorimeter detectors with
\sim
eV scale
thresholds can exploit this irreducible effect to greatly enhance their sensitivity
to sub-GeV DM. Remarkably, the potential gains identified here could enable such detectors to probe the full thermal
relic parameter space for Majorana DM candidates with mass between 10 MeV and 1 GeV freezing out via kinetically-mixed dark photons, corresponding to parameter space which is far below current direct detection limits.

Finally, according to
Schober 2014
, a more realistic model would incorporate a nontrivial phonon density of states and anharmonicities which broaden the phonon spectrum, but we expect that the parametric scaling of our result would persist, at least for monoatomic crystals. Crystals with more than one atom per unit cell, in particular polar materials where dark photons couple to optical phonons even at low
q

Knapen et al. 2018
;
Griffin et al. 2018
;
Trickle et al. 2020
;
Griffin et al. 2020
, may exhibit similar behavior but deserve a dedicated analysis, especially because of their directional detection capabilities. We emphasize here that the rates we have computed are only
lower
bounds on the total nuclear scattering rates for sub-GeV DM. Indeed, DM heavier than about 10 MeV has sufficient kinetic energy to displace a nucleus from its lattice site, and the fact that neither the initial nor the final states are free plane waves may allow for the possibility of inelastic scattering when the elastic rate below threshold is zero. This would give a spectrum of recoil events with
E_{N}>E_{d}
additive to the one we consider here. We plan to investigate this possibility in future work.

II
1. Deriving The Scattering Rate

The transition rate between nuclear states induced by DM-nuclear scattering can be computed using Fermi’s Golden Rule. Following
Essig et al. 2016
, the scattering cross section times velocity
for the inelastic
\chi(\mathbf{p})N(0)\to\chi(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})N(\vec{n})
transition
between individual oscillator levels
0\to(\vec{n})
can be written in relativistic normalization as

\displaystyle\sigma v_{\{0\to\vec{n}\}}=\frac{1}{4E_{\chi}E_{N}}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{4E^{\prime}_{\chi}E^{\prime}_{N}}(2\pi)\delta(E_{f}-E_{i})\overline{|{\pazocal M}(q)|^{2}},

(S1)

where primes denote final-state quantities. To calculate the momentum-space matrix element
\pazocal M(q)
,
we begin by postulating a contact interaction between dark matter and nuclei of the form

\displaystyle\hat{V}(\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N})=\frac{{\pazocal M}_{N}}{4m_{N}m_{\chi}}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N}),

(S2)

where

\mathbf{r}_{\chi}
,
\mathbf{r}_{N}
are the DM and nuclear coordinates, respectively, and

{\pazocal M}_{N}
is the dimensionless relativistic scattering matrix element between DM and a free nucleus, which is simply a constant for a contact interaction. For simplicity we consider the case of scattering mediated through a heavy dark photon, such that the fundamental interaction is between DM and protons; the matrix elements are related by

\displaystyle{\pazocal M}_{N}=Z\frac{m_{N}}{m_{p}}{\pazocal M}_{p}.

(S3)

From this we can define the fiducial single
proton cross section

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}_{p}\equiv\frac{\mu_{\chi p}^{2}|\overline{{\pazocal M}_{p}|^{2}}}{16\pi m_{\chi}^{2}m_{p}^{2}}\penalty\ .

(S4)

Using relativistic state normalization, the initial/final state wave functions in position space are

\displaystyle|\mathbf{p};0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2m_{\chi}}\sqrt{2m_{N}}\,\psi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\,e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\chi}}

(S5)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{p}^{\prime};\vec{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2m_{\chi}}\sqrt{2m_{N}}\,\psi_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\,e^{i\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\chi}},

(S6)

where we treat the dark matter as a free-particle plane wave and the
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{N})
are harmonic
oscillator wavefunctions. The matrix element can then be written

\displaystyle{\pazocal M}(q)=\langle\mathbf{p}^{\prime};\vec{n}|\hat{V}|\mathbf{p};0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\pazocal M}_{N}\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{N}\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{\chi}\,e^{i(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\chi}}\psi^{*}_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\delta^{3}(\mathbf{r}_{\chi}-\mathbf{r}_{N})\psi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{N})

(S7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\pazocal M}_{N}\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{N}\psi^{*}_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{N}}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\equiv{\pazocal M}_{N}\langle\vec{n}|\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle\penalty\ ,

where
\mathbf{q}\equiv\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is the momentum transferred from the dark matter to the harmonic oscillator system.
The harmonic oscillator matrix element can be written in momentum space as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\vec{n}|\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{N}\psi^{*}_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\,e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{N}}\psi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{N})

(S8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{N}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{N}}\tilde{\phi}^{*}_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{p})\tilde{\phi}_{0}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\tilde{\phi}^{*}_{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{p})\tilde{\phi}_{0}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,

where
\tilde{\phi}_{n}
are harmonic oscillator wave functions
in momentum space.
Note that, for comparison with the derivation in
Essig et al. 2016
,
here we adopt a normalization convention for which

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{r}\,\psi^{*}_{m}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r})=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\tilde{\phi}^{*}_{m}(\mathbf{q})\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\mathbf{q})=\delta_{mn}\penalty\ .

(S9)

Putting it all together, we
insert
{\pazocal M}(q)
back into Eq. (
S1
) and replace
E_{\chi},E_{\chi}^{\prime}\to m_{\chi}
,
E_{N},E^{\prime}_{N}\to m_{N}
in the non-relativistic limit. Squaring and summing over all oscillator levels such that
n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n
yields the form factor
|f(\mathbf{q},n)|^{2}
defined in Eq. (
7
). Summing over all allowed energies (labeled by
n
) and scattering targets and integrating over the DM velocity distribution, we obtain the usual rate formula
Essig et al. 2016

stated in Eq. (
6
):

\displaystyle R=N_{T}\frac{\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\bar{\sigma}_{p}}{8\pi\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{q}}{q}\sum_{n}\,|f(\mathbf{q},n)|^{2}\eta(v_{\rm min}^{(n)})\penalty\ ,

(S10)

where
\eta=\langle v^{-1}\theta(v-v_{\rm min})\rangle
is the inverse mean speed and
v_{\rm min}

is the minimum DM velocity required to upscatter the nucleus into a state with energy
E_{n}=(n+1/2)\omega_{0}
. Note that our derivation here is essentially identical to that of
Essig et al. 2016
, replacing DM scattering from bound electrons with DM scattering from bound nuclei. The only essential difference is the factor of
Z^{2}
arising from coherent scattering over all protons in the nucleus.

III
2. Evaluating The Matrix Element

III.1
A. Momentum Space Wavefunctions

We begin by constructing the harmonic oscillator wavefunctions in momentum space, which is convenient for evaluating the matrix element where the argument of one wavefunction is translated by the momentum transfer
\mathbf{q}
.
In momentum space, the position and momentum operators are represented as

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbf{p}}}\rightarrow\mathbf{p}
,

\hat{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rightarrow i\mathbf{\nabla}_{p}
,
so the time independent Schrödinger equation with
\hbar=1
becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m_{N}}-\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\nabla^{2}\right)\phi(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=x,y,z}\left(\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{N}}-\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{i}^{2}}\right)\phi(\mathbf{p})=E\phi(\mathbf{p})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\

(S11)

which we have written in terms of separable solutions
\phi(\mathbf{p})=\phi(p_{x})\phi(p_{y})\phi(p_{z})
,
each satisfying
\hat{H}\phi_{i}=E_{i}\phi_{i}
, where
E=E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}
. Defining dimensionless quantities
\tilde{p}\equiv p/\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}
and
\varepsilon\equiv E/\omega_{0}
, each eigenvalue equation becomes

\phi^{\prime\prime}+(2\varepsilon-\tilde{p}^{2})\phi=0,

where
′
denotes differentiation with respect to
\tilde{p}
.
For each
i=x,y,z
, the normalized solutions satisfy

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\left(p_{i}\right)=A_{n}\exp\left(-\dfrac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)H_{n}\!\left(\dfrac{p_{i}}{\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ A_{n}=\dfrac{A_{0}}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(S12)

where
H_{n}
is an Hermite polynomial. In particular, the ground state has a Gaussian profile,

\displaystyle\phi_{0}\left(p_{i}\right)=A_{0}\operatorname{exp}\left(-\dfrac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ A_{0}=\left(m_{N}\omega_{0}\pi\right)^{-1/4}\penalty\ .

(S13)

with momentum spread of order
q_{0}=\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}
as claimed.
Note that the normalized wavefunctions (
S12
) satisfy the usual non-relativistic normalization convention

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{p}\,\phi_{m}^{*}(\mathbf{p})\phi_{n}(\mathbf{p})=\delta_{mn},

(S14)

which differs from the convention in Eq. (
S9
) by
\tilde{\phi}_{n}=(2\pi)^{3/2}\phi_{n}
which is more common in relativistic treatments. Throughout this paper
(including the remaining Supplementary Material), we use the convention in Eq. (
S14
).

III.2
B. Poisson Distribution

According to Eq. (
S8
), we may write the matrix element between harmonic oscillator states in momentum space as

\displaystyle\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle=\int dp_{x}dp_{y}dp_{z}\,\phi^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{p})\phi_{0}(\mathbf{p}-\bf q)\penalty\ .

(S15)

Since the wave functions are separable in Cartesian coordinates, we need only evaluate the integral for a single component. For ease of notation, we will do this for the
x
-coordinate and write
n\equiv n_{x}
:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{x}\phi^{*}_{n}(p_{x})\phi_{0}(p_{x}-q_{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{0}A_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{x}\exp{\left(-\frac{p_{x}^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)}H_{n}\left(\frac{p_{x}}{\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)\exp{\left(-\frac{(p_{x}-q_{x})^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)}\penalty\ .

(S16)

Defining the dimensionless variables

a\equiv p_{x}/\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}
and
b\equiv q_{x}/\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}
,
the right-hand side of Eq. (
S16
) is

\displaystyle A_{0}A_{n}\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}da\exp\left(-a^{2}+ab\right)H_{n}(a)\penalty\ ,

(S17)

so our task reduces to evaluating the expression

\displaystyle I_{n}(b)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}da\exp\left(-a^{2}+ba\right)H_{n}(a).

(S18)

Note that the integrand is related to the generating functions for Hermite polynomials:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-t^{2}+2at\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}H_{n}(a)\penalty\ .

(S19)

By performing a summation over all
I_{n}(b)
as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}I_{n}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}da\exp\left(-a^{2}+ba\right)H_{n}(a)

(S20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}da\operatorname{exp}\left(-a^{2}+ba-t^{2}+2at\right)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}}{4}\right)\exp(bt)\penalty\ ,

and using the Taylor expansion of
\exp(bt)
on the right hand side, we can read off the expression for individual
I_{n}(b)
by matching terms of equal
n
in the
summation:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}I_{n}(b)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}}{4}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}b^{n}\penalty\ \penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \penalty\ I_{n}(b)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}}{4}\right)b^{n}.

(S21)

Using this result to evaluate our original Cartesian integral, we obtain (now restoring the index
n_{x}
)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp_{x}\phi^{*}_{n_{x}}(p_{x})\phi_{0}(p_{x}-q_{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{0}A_{n_{x}}\sqrt{\pi m_{N}\omega_{0}}\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{4m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{q_{x}}{\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)^{n_{x}}.

(S22)

Since the integrals in Eq. (
S15
) are identical in
p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}
, we can use
Eq. (
S12
) to write their product as

\displaystyle\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{\hat{q}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[2^{n}(n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!)\right]^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{-q^{2}}{4m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{q}{\sqrt{m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)^{n}\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{n_{x}}(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{n_{y}}(\cos\theta\right)^{n_{z}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

(S23)

where
n=n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}
and
we have written the momentum components
q_{i}
in spherical coordinates for future
convenience. Squaring and summing over degenerate states, the form factor for exciting the
n^{\text{th}}
energy level of the harmonic oscillator is given by

|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}=\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\left(\frac{1}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}\right)\exp\left(\frac{-q^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{q}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)^{2n}\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{2n_{x}}(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{2n_{y}}(\cos\theta\right)^{2n_{z}},

(S24)

which resembles a Poisson distribution up to the angular factors. We now turn to
performing an angular average of this expression to justify the full Poissonian form
shown in Eq. (
10
).

III.3
C. Angular Integrals

Since phonon directions are not observable, we are interested in computing
the angular average
\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\mathbf{q}}|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}
, summing
over all occupation numbers that satisfy the constraint
n=n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{x}
. To average the angular part of Eq. (
S24
), we need to compute

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\frac{1}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,(\sin\theta)^{2n_{x}+2n_{y}+1}(\cos\theta)^{2n_{z}}(\sin\phi)^{2n_{y}}(\cos\phi)^{2n_{x}}

(S25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\frac{1}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{2n_{x}}(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{2n_{y}}(\cos\theta)^{2n_{z}}\penalty\ .

Let
(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{2}=A
,
(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{2}=B
and
(\cos\theta)^{2}=C
. The integrand with the summation is

\displaystyle K_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\frac{1}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{2n_{x}}(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{2n_{y}}(\cos\theta)^{2n_{z}}

(S26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\frac{1}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}A^{n_{x}}B^{n_{y}}C^{n_{z}}.

Multiplying and dividing by
n!
, we have

K_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}=n}\frac{n!}{n_{x}!n_{y}!n_{z}!}A^{n_{x}}B^{n_{y}}C^{n_{z}},

(S27)

where the summation is simply the multinomial expansion,

\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{m}=n}\frac{n!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots k_{m}!}x_{1}^{k_{1}}x_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots x_{m}^{k_{m}}\\
=\left(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{m}\right)^{n}.

(S28)

With this reorganization of terms, our integrand of interest simplifies considerably:

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\left(A+B+C\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\left[(\sin\theta\cos\phi)^{2}+(\sin\theta\sin\phi)^{2}+(\cos\theta)^{2}\right]^{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\penalty\ .

(S29)

Using this form, Eq. (
S25
) becomes

F_{n}=\frac{1}{4\pi n!}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\ =\frac{1}{n!},

(S30)

as claimed in Eq. (
10
).

III.4
D. Operator Algebra

We can obtain the same result by using the algebra of creation and annihilation operators. The three-dimensional harmonic oscillator can be separated into three mutually-commuting sets of creation and annihilation operators,

[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}]=1,\qquad i=1,2,3,

(S31)

in terms of which the position and momentum operators
\hat{x}_{i}
and
\hat{p}_{i}
can be written

\hat{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\left(\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\right),\qquad\hat{p}_{i}=i\sqrt{\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}}{2}}\left(-\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\right).

(S32)

Since
\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{N}=q_{x}\hat{x}_{N}+q_{y}\hat{y}_{N}+q_{z}\hat{z}_{N}
and the spatial operators commute with each other, without loss of generality we can simply compute

|\langle 0|e^{iq_{x}\hat{x}_{N}}|n_{x}\rangle|^{2}

(S33)

and copy the result for
n_{y}
and
n_{z}
; multiplying these together gives the desired result for arbitrary
n
.

First let’s write the exponential operator in terms of creation and annihilation operators:

\exp({iq_{x}\hat{x}_{N}})=\exp\left[\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\right],

(S34)

where we have dropped subscript on
a
for convenience. Using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
for any operators
A
and
B
, we can write

\exp(A)\exp(B)=\exp\left(A+B+\frac{1}{2}[A,B]+\frac{1}{12}[A,[A,B]]-\frac{1}{12}[B,[A,B]]\dots\right)\penalty\ .

(S35)

Identifying
A\to\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
B\to\hat{a}
, and noting that
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
is a
c
-number, the
series in Eq. (
S34
) truncates after the third term. Indeed, the third term is just a number, so we have the exact result

e^{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}e^{\hat{a}}=e^{\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}}\penalty\ ,

(S36)

and since the last factor is just a
c
-number,
this can be written as

e^{\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}}=e^{\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}e^{\hat{a}}.

(S37)

Note that this is the same argument presented in
Trickle et al. 2020
to compute the amplitude for single-phonon production, only restricted here to the greatly simplified context of a single 3-dimensional oscillator.

Since we are interested in simplifying
e^{iq_{x}\hat{x}_{N}}=e^{i\kappa(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})}
, where
\kappa=iq_{x}/\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}
, the relevant commutator is
[\kappa a,\kappa a^{\dagger}]=\kappa^{2}
. Following the above argument yields

\exp\left[\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\right]=\exp\left(-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{4m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\exp\left(\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\hat{a}\right)\penalty\ .

(S38)

Consider taking the matrix element of this operator between the states
\langle 0|
and
|n_{x}\rangle
. Acting on
|n_{x}\rangle
on the left, we have to get to the state
|0\rangle
to have a nonzero matrix element with
\langle 0|
. The only way to get there is to act
n_{x}
times with
\hat{a}
and zero times with
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
. This means we take the
n_{x}^{\rm th}
term from the right-most exponential series, and the
0^{\rm th}
term from the middle exponential.
Since these operators satisfy

\hat{a}^{n}_{x}|n_{x}\rangle=\sqrt{n_{x}!}|0\rangle,

(S39)

we can act on our initial and final states to obtain

\left\langle 0\left|\exp\left(-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{4m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)\exp\left(\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\hat{a}\right)\right|n_{x}\right\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{n_{x}!}}{n_{x}!}\left(\frac{iq_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)^{n_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{4m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right)\penalty\ .

(S40)

Taking the modulus squared of this expression gives

|\langle 0|e^{iq_{x}\hat{x}_{N}}|n_{x}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{x}!}\left(\frac{q_{x}}{\sqrt{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}}\right)^{2n_{x}}\exp\left(-\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{2m_{N}\omega_{0}}\right),

(S41)

and multiplying identical expression for the
n_{y}
and
n_{z}
contributions recovers (
S24
). In fact, from Eq. (
S41
) we can see that the Cartesian occupation numbers
n_{x}
,
n_{y}
, and
n_{z}
are also Poisson-distributed, so their sum
n=n_{x}+n_{y}+n_{z}
will also be Poisson, confirming our more detailed calculation.

IV
3. Generalizing the Migdal Effect

Dark matter scattering off bound nuclear targets can also yield electronic energy in the
form of Migdal ionization. However, unlike previous studies of this effect
Ibe et al. 2018
, here the
nucleus is not a free particle, so here we revisit and generalize this result with a harmonic oscillator
dispersion relation for the nuclear target. Rather than considering the final state in the boosted frame of the recoiling nucleus, we will decompose the problem into relative coordinates in the lab frame, which more easily generalizes for a bound nucleus.

IV.1
A. Choosing Coordinates

We begin by first considering a simple atomic system in which
a single electron and nucleus are bound by a potential
V_{e}
,
and the nucleus is held in place by a harmonic oscillator potential. The Hamiltonian
for this system
can be written

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{N}^{2}}{2m_{N}}+\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{e}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}^{2}+V_{e}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e}).

(S42)

Transforming to relative and center-of-mass coordinates,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\mathbf{R}}=\frac{m_{N}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}+m_{e}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{e}}{m_{N}+m_{e}},

(S43)

the Hamiltonian becomes
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\Delta\hat{H}
, where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{R}^{2}}{2(m_{N}+m_{e})}+\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{r}^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{m_{N}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{2}+V_{e}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}),\penalty\ \penalty\

(S44)

where
\mu=m_{e}m_{N}(m_{e}+m_{N})\approx m_{e}
is the electron-nucleus reduced mass, and expanding the original harmonic oscillator term gives

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\omega_{0}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}+\frac{\mu^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}}{2m_{N}}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\penalty\ .

(S45)

Written in this way,
\hat{H}_{0}
is separable and can be solved by
\Psi(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r})=\psi_{N}(\mathbf{R})\psi_{e}(\mathbf{r})
, where
\psi_{N}
is a simple harmonic oscillator wavefunction for the nucleus
N
and
\psi_{e}
an electronic wavefunction. The terms in
\Delta\hat{H}
are suppressed by powers of
m_{e}/m_{N}\ll 1
and can be treated as small perturbations. In particular, the first-order energy shift is parametrically

\displaystyle\Delta E=\langle\Psi|\Delta\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle\sim\frac{m_{e}^{2}}{m_{N}}\omega_{0}^{2}a^{2}\penalty\ ,

(S46)

where we have assumed that the typical spread in position space of the electronic wavefunction is of order the lattice spacing
a
; note that
\Delta E
independent of the harmonic oscillator level
N
because
\langle\hat{\mathbf{R}}\rangle=0
in any stationary state, so only the second term in
\Delta\hat{H}
contributes. For
\omega_{0}\sim 50\ {\rm meV}
and
m_{N}=28\ {\rm GeV}
as for silicon, we have
\Delta E\sim 25\ {\rm neV}\ll\omega_{0}
and thus we are justified in ignoring the perturbation and treating the nuclear spectrum as purely a harmonic oscillator spectrum.

IV.2
B. Including Dark Matter

Equipped with this formalism, we can now include the contact interaction from Eq. (
S2
) that couples
the DM to the nucleus and
repeat the argument that culminates in
Eq. (
S10
) in Sec. 1 of this supplement with the initial/final states

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2m_{\chi}}\sqrt{2m_{N}}\,\psi_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\psi_{e}(\mathbf{r}_{e})\,e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\chi}}

(S47)

\displaystyle|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2m_{\chi}}\sqrt{2m_{N}}\,\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}_{N})\psi_{e}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}_{e})\,e^{i\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\chi}},

(S48)

where we have merely extended Eq. (
S5
) to include electron wave functions which have non-relativistic normalization to match the conventions of
Ibe et al. 2018
. Since the DM potential
is only a function of
\mathbf{r}_{N}-\mathbf{r}_{\chi}
, all the steps leading up to Eq. (
S7
) are identical
and the
\psi_{e}
states are spectators up until the last step
where, instead of
\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|0\rangle
,
we get

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{N}}|\Psi\rangle=\langle\vec{n}|e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{R}}}|0\rangle\langle\psi_{e}^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}_{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}|\psi_{e}\rangle,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbf{q}_{e}\equiv\left(\frac{m_{e}}{m_{N}}\right)\mathbf{q}\penalty\ .

(S49)

Upon squaring this result, the first factor recovers Eq. (
S24
) and the
second factor is the same ionization probability
found in Ref.
Ibe et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle Z_{f}(\mathbf{q}_{e})\equiv\langle\psi_{e}^{\prime}|e^{i\mathbf{q}_{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}|\psi_{e}\rangle.

(S50)

Although here we have only considered single-electron atoms, the calculation straightforwardly generalizes to atomic systems
with multiple electrons at relative coordinates
\mathbf{r}_{j}
:

\displaystyle Z_{f}(\mathbf{q}_{e})\to\int\prod_{j}d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{j}\psi_{e}^{\prime*}(\mathbf{r}_{j})\,\exp\biggl(i\sum_{j}\mathbf{q}_{e}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}\biggr)\psi_{e}(\mathbf{r}_{j}).

(S51)

Thus, including the electronic matrix elements, the total scattering rate for the Migdal effect now becomes

\displaystyle R=N_{T}\frac{\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{p}}{8\pi\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{\mathbf{q}}}{q}\sum_{n}|f(n,\mathbf{q})|^{2}\,\sum_{f}|Z_{f}(\mathbf{q}_{e})|^{2}\,\eta(v_{\rm min}^{n,e})\penalty\ ,

(S52)

where the sum on
f
includes all allowed electron final states and
the additional electronic energy
E_{e}
released in this inelastic process shifts
the minimum velocity required to undergo a given transition

\displaystyle v_{\rm min}^{(n,e)}=\frac{E_{e}+n\omega_{0}}{q}+\frac{q}{2m_{\chi}}.

(S53)

For systems with spherically symmetric ionization probabilities,

Z_{f}
depends only on
E_{e}
(as in
Ibe et al. 2018
with free atoms), so the electron term factorizes
from
the
\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{\mathbf{q}}
angular average that yields the Poisson distribution
in Eq. (
10
). In this case, the differential scattering rate becomes

\displaystyle\frac{dR}{dqdE_{e}}=\!\frac{N_{T}\rho_{\chi}}{m_{\chi}}\frac{Z^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{p}}{2\mu_{\chi p}^{2}}\sum_{n}q|f(n,q)|^{2}\sum_{f}\frac{d|Z_{f}|^{2}}{dE_{e}}\eta(v_{\rm min}^{n,e}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

(S54)

which recovers the expression in Eq. (
18
). However, in general, it need not
be the case that these terms factorize (in particular, the valence electrons in semiconductors are not necessarily in spherically-symmetric states) and the final distribution will involve
an integration over the combined oscillator and ionization probabilities.
For a detailed discussion of the Migdal effect in semiconductors see
Knapen et al. 2020
.
