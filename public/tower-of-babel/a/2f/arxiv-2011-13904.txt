Abstract
. In this paper, our discussion mainly focuses on equations with energy supercritical nonlinearities. We establish probabilistic global well-posedness (GWP) results for the cubic Schrödinger equation with any fractional power of the Laplacian in all dimensions. We consider both low and high regularities in the radial setting, in dimension ≥ 2 \geq 2 . In the high regularity result, an Inviscid - Infinite dimensional (IID) limit is employed while in the low regularity global well-posedness result, we make use of the Skorokhod representation theorem. The IID limit is presented in details as an independent approach that applies to a wide range of Hamiltonian PDEs. Moreover we discuss the adaptation to the periodic settings, in any dimension, for smooth regularities. Keywords : Fractional NLS, compact manifold, invariant measure, almost sure global well-posedness. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 35A01, 35Q55, 35R11, 60H15, 37K06, 37L50.



1.
Introduction

In this work, we consider the initial value problem of the cubic fractional nonlinear Schrödinger equation (FNLS)

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}u=-{\textbf{i}}((-\Delta)^{\sigma}u+|u|^{2}u),\quad u\big|_{t=0}=u_{0}

on both the
d-
dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, and the unit ball
B^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, supplemented with a radial assumption and a Dirichlet boundary condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle u\Big|_{\partial B^{d}}=0.

Here
\sigma\in(0,1]
. When
\sigma=1
we have the standard Schrödinger equation. The FNLS equation satisfies the following conservation laws referred as the mass and the energy

(1.3)

\displaystyle M(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

(1.4)

\displaystyle E(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}.

It also enjoys the scaling invariance given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle v=\lambda^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}x,\lambda^{\sigma}t).

Consequently, its critical regularity is given by the Sobolev space
H^{s_{c}}
where

(1.6)

\displaystyle s_{c}=\frac{d}{2}-\sigma.

Data of regularity weaker than
s_{c}
(resp. stronger than
s_{c}
) are called supercritical (resp. subcritical). The equation is called energy-supercritical (resp. energy-criticical, energy-subcritical) if
s_{c}>\sigma
(resp.
s_{c}=\sigma
,
s_{c}<\sigma
); that is,
\sigma<\frac{d}{4}
(resp.
\sigma=\frac{d}{4}
,
\sigma>\frac{d}{4}
). Since
\sigma\in(0,1)
, the cubic FNLS equations have energy-supercritical ranges in all dimensions
d\leq 3
, and is fully energy-supercritical in dimensions
d\geq 4.
In this paper we address all the scenarios listed above. We construct global solutions by mean of an invariant measure argument and establish long-time dynamics properties.

1.1.
Motivation

In recent decades, there has been of great interest in using fractional Laplacians to model
physical phenomena. The fractional quantum mechanics was introduced by Laskin
[
42
]
as a generalization of the standard quantum mechanics. This generalization operates on the Feynman path integral formulation by replacing the Brownian motion with a general Levy flight. As a consequence, one obtains fractional versions of the fundamental Schrödinger equation. That means the Laplace operator
(-\Delta)
arising from the Gaussian kernel used in the standard theory is replaced by its fractional powers
(-\Delta)^{\sigma}
, where
0<\sigma<1
, as such operators naturally generate Levy flights.
Also, it turns out that the equation (
1.1
) and its discrete versions are relevant in molecular biology as they have been proposed to describe the charge transport between base pairs in the DNA molecule where typical long range interactions occur
[
46
]
. The continuum limit for discrete FNLS was studied rigorously first in
[
36
]
. See also
[
27
,
28
,
33
]
for recent works on the continuum limits.

The FNLS equation does not enjoy the strong dispersion estimates as the classical NLS does. The bounded domain setting naturally highlights this lack of dispersion. This makes both the well-posedness and the long-time behavior more difficult than the Euclidean setting on one hand, an the classical NLS on the other hand.

The problem of understanding long-time behavior of dispersive equations on bounded domains is widely open. While on Euclidean spaces scattering turns out to apply to most of the defocusing contexts, on bounded domains one does not expect such scattering and there is no well-established general asymptotic theory. It is however expected that solutions would generically exhibit weak turbulence

1

. See for example
[

,
31
,

,
7

,

and references therein for results in the direction of weak turbulence. A way to detect a weak turbulent behavior is to analyze the higher order Sobolev norms of the solutions and determine whether or not the quantity

(1.7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{|t|\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}

is infinite for some

.

In this paper, we prove probabilistic global well-posedness for (

) by mean of invariant measures and discuss the long-time behavior by employing ergodic theorems. In particular, we show that a slightly different version of (

1.3.
Invariant measure as a tool of globalization

Bourgain
[
5
]
performed an ingenious argument based on the invariance of the Gibbs measure of the nonlinear one-dimensional NLS to prove global existence on a rich set of rough data. The Gibbs measure of the one-dimensional NLS is supported on the space
H^{\frac{1}{2}-}
and was constructed by Lebowitz, Rose and Speer
[
44
]
. The non existence of a conservation law at this level of regularity was a serious issue for the globalization. The method introduced by Bourgain to solve this issue was to derive individual bounds from the the statistical control given by the Fernique theorem. A crucial fact is that these bounds do not depend on the dimension of the Galerkin projections of the equation which are used in the construction of the Gibbs measure. With such estimates, the local solutions of the equation are globalized by an iteration argument based on comparison with the global solutions of the finite-dimensional projections. This is how an invariant measure can play the role of a conservation law. More precisely, we should say how the role played by a conservation law survive through the invariant measure; indeed such quantity are essentially constructed by the use of a conservation laws and can be seen as their statistical duals. This approach is widely exploited in different contexts. For instance, we refer the reader to the papers
[
56
,
57
,
9
,
11
,
22
,
6
]
and references therein.

A second approach based on a fluctuation-dissipation method was introduced by Kuksin
[
38
]
in the context of the two-dimensional Euler equation, and developed in the context of the cubic NLS by Kuksin and Shirikyan
[
39
]
. This method uses a compactness argument and relies on stochastic analysis with an inviscid limit. The Hamiltonian equation is viewed as a limit as viscosity goes to 0 of an appropriately scaled fluctuation-dissipation equation that enjoys a stationary measure for any given vicosity. By compactness, this family of stationary measures admit at least one accumulation point as the viscosity vanishes, this turns out to be an invariant measure for the limiting equation. The scaling of the fluctuations with respect to the dissipation is such that one obtains estimates not depending on the viscosity. These estimates allow to perform a globalization argument. See
[
51
,
53
,
25
,
40
]
for works related to this method.

The two approaches discussed above were developed on various setting. However for energy supercritical equations, both approaches come across serious obstructions. The first author initiated a new approach that combines the two methods and applied it to the energy-supercritical NLS
[
52
]
, this approach was developed further by the authors in
[
54
]
. This approach utilizes a fluctuation-dissipation argument on the Galerkin approximations of the equation, we then have a double approximation: a finite-dimensional one and a viscous one. Two limits are considered in the following order: (i) an inviscid limit to recover the Galerkin projection of the Hamiltonian equation, (ii) then an infinite-dimensional limit to recover the equation itself. In the infinite-dimensional limit, a Bourgain type iteration is used. Overall, different difficulties arise in adapting the Bourgain strategy to a non Gaussian situation. That is why new ingredients were involved. This method is what we refer to
Inviscid-Infinite-dimensional limit
, or simply the “IID” limit. See also the work
[
43
]
for a similar procedure. We perform in Section
3
a general and independent version of the IID limit.

1.6.
Comparison with
[
52
,
54
]

The probabilistic technique employed in this paper is closed to
[
52
,
54
]
. It is worth mentioning the novelty in the present work beside the fact that fractional NLS equations are much less understood than the standard NLS which motivated our interest to the problem considered here, and the presentation of the IID limit in a general form that is independent of characteristics of (

). A main difference
from

is the fact that the result of Theorem
1.3
goes below the energy space, and
s
can go all the way toward

. The major issue in achieving this is the uniqueness in low regularity. Our strategy is to control the gradient of the nonlinearity, that is the term
|u|^{2}
, and then to combine it with the radial Sobolev embedding and an approximation argument. This control is anticipated in the preparation of the dissipation operator.

Let us present the dissipation operator (see (
4.4
)) on which the low regularity result (see e.g. Section
6
) is based, in particular:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u)=e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u+(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u\right)\quad 0<s\leq 1+\sigma,\ \ \sigma\in(0,1].

The dissipation rate
\mathcal{E}(u)
of the energy will be of regularity
s
which, for
s<\sigma
, is weaker than the energy. Since
\mathcal{E}(u)
is the highest regular quantity controlled, we are then in new ranges of regularity compared to
[
52
,
54
]
. To deal with these ranges we need new inputs at different levels of the proof. For instance, the large data argument relies on the identity (
4.51
) whose proof cannot be achieved by using the approach of
[
52
]
and
[
54
]
(where the dissipation was of positive order and then the dissipation rate of the energy was smoother than the energy itself which made it useful in the derivation of the identity concerning the dissipation rate of the mass). We, instead, introduce a modified approach which use a careful cutoff on the frequencies.

It is worth mentioning the central quantity that we manage to control at the finite-dimensional level (the control is however uniform in the dimension) by the use of a fluctuation-dissipation strategy:

(1.18)

\displaystyle e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\||u|^{2}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\||u|^{2}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s-\sigma}}\right).

Below is the role of each term in the quantity above:

•

The term
\|u\|_{H^{s}}
determines the minimal regularity of the measure

•

The term
\||u|^{2}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}+\||u|^{2}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s-\sigma}}
combined with the radial Sobolev inequality and an approximation argument allows to obtain uniqueness of solutions in Theorem
1.3
(see Subsection

or

, this results in a control of gradient of the nonlinearity

far from the origin of the ball, and then an approximation argument introduce in our previous work
[
54
]
is employed.

•

The term
\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
combined with the Skorokhod representation theorem and a compactness argument allows to pass to the limit

and prove the existence of solutions.

•

The term
e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}
is employed in the Bourgain argument to obtain, in particular, the concave bounds on the solutions claimed in Theorem
1.1
.

3.
Description of the Inviscid-Infinite dimensional (IID) limit

The IID limit combines a fluctuation-dissipation argument and an abstract version of the Bourgain’s invariant measure globalization
[
5
]
.

We consider a Hamiltonian equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}u=JH^{\prime}(u)

where
J
is a skew-adjoint operator on suitable spaces,
H(u)
is the hamiltonian function,
H^{\prime}
is the derivative with respect to
u
(or a function of
u
, for instance to its complex conjugate). We can, for simplicity, assume the following form for the Hamiltonian

(3.2)

\displaystyle H(u)=E_{K}(u)+E_{p}(u),

where
E_{K}
and
E_{p}
refer to the kinetic energy of the system (having a quadratic power) and the potential energy, respectively. In the sequel we construct a general framework allowing to apply the Bourgain’s invariant measure argument to the context of a general probability measure (not necessarily Gaussian based). We then present the strategy of the construction of the required measures.

3.1.
Abstract version of Bourgain’s invariant measure argument

Let
\Pi^{N}
be the projection on the
N
-dimensional space
E^{N}
spanned by the first
N
eigenfunctions
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{N}\}
. Consider the Galerkin projections of (
3.1
)

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}u=\Pi^{N}JH^{\prime}(u).

The equation (
3.1
) will be seen as (
3.3
) with
N=\infty
.

Assumption 1

(Uniform local well-posedness)
.

The equation (
3.3
) is uniformly (in
N
) locally wellposed in the Cauchy-Hadamard sense on some Sobolev space
H^{s}
. And there is a function
f
independent of
N
, such that for any given data
u_{0}\in\Pi^{N}H^{s}
, the time existence
T(u_{0})
of the corresponding solution
u
is at least
f(\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}})
. Also, the following estimate holds

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}\quad\text{for all $|t|\leq T(u_{0})$}.

We denote by
\phi^{N}_{t}
the local flow of (
3.3
), for
N=\infty
we set
\phi_{t}=\phi^{\infty}_{t}
.

For
N<\infty
, we assume that
\phi^{N}_{t}
is defined globally in time.

Remark 3.1
.

For the power-like nonlinear Schrödinger equation,
f(x)
is of the form

where
r
is related to the power of the nonlinearity.

Assumption 2

(Convergence)
.

For any
u_{0}\in H^{s}
, any sequence
(u_{0}^{N})_{N}
such that

(1)

for any
N
,
u_{0}^{N}\in\Pi^{N}H^{s}
;

(2)

(u_{0}^{N})
converges to
u_{0}
in
H^{s}
;

then
\phi^{N}_{t}u^{N}
converges

in
C_{t}H^{s-}.

Assumption 3

(Invariant measure)
.

For any
N
, there is a measure
\mu_{N}
invariant under the projection equations (
3.3
) and satisfying

(1)

There is an increasing one-to-one function

and a function

such that we have the uniform bound

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{E^{N}}g(\|u\|_{H^{s}})h(\|u\|_{H^{s_{0}}})\mu^{N}(du)\leq C,

where
C>0
is independent of
N
and
s_{0}
is some fixed number. We then have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{E^{N}}e^{\ln(1+g(\|u\|_{H^{s}}))}h(\|u\|_{H^{s_{0}}})\mu^{N}(du)\leq 1+C.

We set
\tilde{g}(\|u\|_{H^{s}})=\ln(1+g(\|u\|_{H^{s}}));
4
4

4

Remark that

is increasing and one-to-one from

to

.

(2)

The following limit holds

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}e^{-2i}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-j}}{f\circ\tilde{g}^{-1}(2(i+j))}=0.

We set the number

(3.8)

\displaystyle\kappa(i):=e^{-2i}\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-j}}{f\circ\tilde{g}^{-1}(2(i+j))}.

Remark 3.2

For the case of Gibbs measures for NLS like equations,

is of the form

is a constant.

The Prokhorov theorem (Theorem

as an accumulation point when

.

Under the Assumptions

2

(2)

In particular,

\displaystyle\mu(\cup_{i\geq 1}\overline{\Sigma_{r,i}})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu(\overline{\Sigma_{r,i}}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mu(\cup_{i\geq 1}\overline{\Sigma_{r,i}})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu(\overline{\Sigma_{r,i}})\geq\lim_{i\to\infty}(1-Ca^{-1}\kappa(i))=1.

Since
\mu
is a probability measure, we obtain

\displaystyle 1\geq\mu(\Sigma_{r})\geq 1.

This finishes the proof of Proposition

.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let

Proof of Proposition

.

For

(recall that

(1)

u_{0}\in H^{s}

(2)

there is

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{H^{r}}\leq 2\tilde{g}^{-1}(1+i).

3.2.
Construction of the required invariant measures

Here we present a strategy to achieve the fulfillment of Assumption
3
. It is based on a fluctuation-dissipation method.

Let us introduce a probabilistic setting and some notations that are going to be used from this section on. First,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
is a complete probability space. If
E
is a Banach space, we can define random variables

as Bochner measurable functions with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{B}(E)

\mathcal{B}(E)

For every positive integer

Throughout the sequel, we use

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle=\re\int u\bar{v}\,dx,\quad\text{for all $u,v\in L^{2}$}.

Ocassionally this notation is used to write a duality bracket when the context is not confusing.

We set the fluctuation-dissipation equation

(3.51)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}u=\Pi^{N}JH^{\prime}(u)-\alpha\mathcal{L}(u)+\sqrt{\alpha}dW^{N},

where
\mathcal{L}(u)
is a dissipation operator.

Assumption 5

(Exponential moment)
.

We assume that
\mathcal{L}(u)
satisfies the dissipation inequality

(3.52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=E^{\prime}(u,\mathcal{L}(u))\geq e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s}})}\|u\|_{H^{s_{0}}}^{2},

where
\xi^{-1}(x)\geq x
is a one-to-one convex function from
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

We assume also that the (local existence) function

defined in Assumption

for some

.

Assumption 6
.

The equation (
3.51

that is:

For every
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
u_{0}
in

, we have

(1)

for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega\in\Omega

(2)

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{2}}f(w)T^{N}_{t,\alpha}(v,dw)\quad L^{\infty}(L^{2},{\mathbb{R}})\to L^{\infty}(L^{2},{\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle P^{N*}_{t,\alpha}\lambda(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{L^{2}}\lambda(dw)T^{N}_{t,\alpha}(w,\Gamma)\quad\mathfrak{p}(L^{2})\to\mathfrak{p}(L^{2}).

(3.56)

\displaystyle P^{N}_{t,\alpha}f(v)={\mathbb{E}}f(u^{N}_{\alpha}(t,v)).

Using the continuity of the solution

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)\geq\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\right)-\tilde{C}.

For

,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\langle(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u,\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\rangle+\langle\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u,(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u\rangle+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

We are able to give a useful estimate of
\mathcal{E}(u)
in the following two cases:

(a)

For
\max(\frac{1}{2},\sigma)\leq s\leq 1+\sigma:

In order the treat the term
\langle(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u,\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\rangle
and
\langle\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u,(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u\rangle
, we use the Córdoba-Córdoba inequality ( see Corollary
2.7
) since
s-\sigma\in[0,1]
,
s
being in
[\max(\frac{1}{2},\sigma),1+\sigma]
. We have the following

(4.12)

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u,|u|^{2}u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s-\sigma}{2}}|u|^{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u,|u|^{2}u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}|u|^{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We finally obtain that

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\||u|^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\||u|^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{s-\sigma}}^{2}\right).

(b)

For
0<s\leq\sigma:
Since
\sigma
still lies in
(0,1]
, we can use the Córdoba-Córdoba inequality to obtain

(4.15)

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{\sigma}u,|u|^{2}u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}|u|^{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

However, we cannot use the Córdoba-Córdoba inequality for
(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}
in the term
\langle(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u,|u|^{2}u\rangle
since
s\leq\sigma
. We instead employ a direct estimation (using in particular the Sobolev embedding
L^{4}=W^{0,4}\subset W^{s-\sigma,4}
):

(4.16)

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u,|u|^{2}u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-\|(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}u\|_{L^{4}}\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{3}\geq-C\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}.

We arrive at

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\||u|^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}-C\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}\right).

Now, let us set

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u):=M^{\prime}(u,\mathcal{L}(u)),

where
M(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We obtain that

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u)=e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\|u\|_{H^{s-\sigma}}^{2}\right).

This gives us that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(\||u|^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\right)-\frac{C}{2}\mathcal{M}(u).

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that for
\sigma<\frac{1}{2}
, we do not have an estimate of
\mathcal{E}(u)
for
s\in(\sigma,d/2)
. So we construct global solutions in that case using the arguments in Section
6
. Surprisingly enough, we construct solutions, for such
\sigma
, in lower regularities
s\in(0,\sigma)
since in that cases the need estimates are obtained through the control of
\mathcal{E}(u)
established above (see Section
6
).

For any
\sigma\in(0,1]
, Assumption
5
is now fulfilled for
s\in(d/2,\infty)\cup[\max(1/2,\sigma),1+\sigma]\cup[0,\sigma]
. Notice that the term
-\frac{C}{2}\mathcal{M}(u)
in (
4.20
) does not have any obstruction because it is controlled under expectation (see (
4.55
) for details of the Itô estimate on
M(u)
). It’s expectation is just added the to right hand side of (
3.60
) and similarly for absolute
\tilde{C}
in (
4.10
).

In particular, we notice that

(4.21)

\displaystyle\|u^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq(\|u^{2}\|_{\dot{H}^{\sigma}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\|\Pi^{N}|u|^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2})e^{\xi^{-1}(\|u\|_{H^{s-}})}\leq 2\mathcal{E}(u)+C\mathcal{M}(u),

where
C=C(s,\sigma)
does not depend on
(u,N,\alpha)
.

4.2.
Fulfillment of Assumption
6
(The stochastic GWP)

For simplicity we present the proof of stochastic GWP in the case
0\leq s\leq 1+\sigma
, we then consider the corresponding dissipation
\mathcal{L}(u)
. The other case involves a simpler dissipation operator and can be treated similarily.

We follow the well-known Da Prato-Debussche decomposition
[
19
]
(also known as Bourgain decomposition
[
6
]
). Let us consider the following linear stochastic equation

(4.22)

\displaystyle dz=-{\textbf{i}}(-\Delta)^{\sigma}zdt+\sqrt{\alpha}dW^{N},\quad z\Big|_{t=0}=0.

This equation admits a unique solution known as stochastic convolution:

(4.23)

\displaystyle z(t)=\sqrt{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-{\textbf{i}}(t-s)(-\Delta)^{\sigma}}dW^{N}(s).

It follows the Itô theory that
z(t)
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}-
martingale. The Doob maximal inequality combined with the Itô isometry shows that, for any
T>0

(4.24)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|z(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{T}\sqrt{\alpha}.

We notice that
z
exists globally in
t
for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. Let
u_{0}
be
E^{N}-
valued
\mathcal{F}_{0}-
independent random variable. Fix
\omega
such
z^{\omega}
exists globally in time, and consider the nonlinear deterministic equation

(4.25)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}v=F(v,z),\quad v(t=0)=u_{0}^{\omega},

where
F(v,z)=-{\textbf{i}}((-\Delta)^{\sigma}v+|v+z|^{2}(v+z))-\alpha e^{\xi^{-1}(\|v+z\|_{H^{s-}})}[\Pi^{N}|v+z|^{2}(v+z)+(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}(v+z)]
.

We remark that for a global solution
v
to (
4.25
), we have that
u=v+z
is a global solution to (
4.5
) supplemented with the initial condition
u(t=0)=u_{0}^{\omega}
.

The local existence for (
4.25
) follows from the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem since
F(v,z)
is smooth. Let us show that this local solution is in fact global. By an standard iteration argument, it suffices to show that the
L^{2}
-norm does not blow up in finite time. We have that

(4.26)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=-2{\textbf{i}}\langle v,\Pi^{N}|v+z|^{2}(v+z)\rangle-2\alpha e^{\xi^{-1}(\|v+z\|_{H^{s-}})}[\langle v,\Pi^{N}|v+z|^{2}(v+z)\rangle+\langle v,(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}(v+z)\rangle].

By adding
z-z
and using Cauchy-Schwarz, we obtain

(4.27)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{N}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{4}}{\alpha}+e^{\xi^{-1}(\|v+z\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(-\|v\|_{H^{s-\sigma}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{N}(\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|z\|_{L^{4}}^{4})\right).

We then have two complementary cases for a
t\in[0,T]
:

(1)

either
\|v\|_{H^{s-\sigma}}^{2}\leq\tilde{C}_{N}(\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|z\|_{L^{4}}^{4})
; in this case

(4.28)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C^{0}_{\alpha}(\omega,T),

(2)

or
\|v\|_{H^{s-\sigma}}^{2}>\tilde{C}_{N}(\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|z\|_{L^{4}}^{4})
; in this case

(4.29)

\displaystyle e^{\xi^{-1}(\|v+z\|_{H^{s-}})}\left(-\|v\|_{H^{s-\sigma}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{N}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq 0,

and

(4.30)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C^{1}_{\alpha}(\omega,T).

Taking
C_{\alpha}=\max(C^{0}_{\alpha},C^{1}_{\alpha})
, we obtain the wished finiteness of
\|v\|_{L^{2}}
.

Now, using the mean value theorem and the Gronwall lemma, we find for
u
and
v
two solutions to (
4.5
) that

(4.31)

\displaystyle\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u(0)-v(0)\|_{L^{2}}e^{\sup_{x\in D}\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(ru+(1-r)v)|dr},

where
f(u)=F(u,0).

Standard arguments show that the constructed solution
u
is adapted to
\sigma(u_{0},\mathcal{F}_{t}).

4.3.
Fulfillment of Assumption
7
(Zero viscosity limit,
\alpha\to 0
)

The difficulty in the convergence of Assumption
7
is the fact that both
P^{N*}_{t,\alpha_{k}}
and
\mu^{N}_{\alpha_{k}}
depend on
\alpha_{k}
, we then need some uniformity in the convergence
u^{N}_{\alpha_{k}}(t,\cdot)\to\phi^{N}_{t}(\cdot)
as
\alpha_{k}\to 0
. The following lemma gives the needed uniform convergence. To simplify the notation, we use the abuse of notation:
P^{N*}_{t,\alpha_{k}}=:P^{N*}_{t,k}
,
\mu^{N}_{\alpha_{k}}=:\mu^{N}_{k}
and
u_{\alpha_{k}}=:u_{k}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
T>0
. For any
R>0
, any
r>0
,

(4.32)

\displaystyle\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}(L^{2})}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|u_{k}(t,\Pi^{N}u_{0})-\phi^{N}_{t}\Pi^{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}1_{S_{r,k}(t)}\right)\to 0,\quad\text{as $k\to\infty$},

where

(4.33)

\displaystyle S_{r,k}(t)=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\ |\ \text{max}\left(\|z_{\alpha_{k}}^{\omega}(t)\|_{L^{2}},\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}a_{m}\langle u,e_{m}\rangle d\beta_{s}^{\omega}\right|\right)\leq r\right\}.

We postpone the proof of Lemma
4.3
. We remark that, using the Ito isometry and the Chebyshev inequality we have

(4.34)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(1-1_{S_{r,k}}(t))\leq\frac{Ct}{r^{2}}

where
C
does not depend on
(r,k,t)
.

Let
f:L^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded Lipschitz function. Without any loss of generality we can assume that
f
is bounded by
1
and its Lipschitz constant is also
1
. We have

(4.35)

\displaystyle\langle P^{N*}_{t,k}\mu^{N}_{k},f\rangle-\langle P^{N*}_{t}\mu^{N},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mu^{N}_{k},P^{N}_{t,k}f\rangle-\langle\mu^{N},P^{N}_{t}f\rangle

(4.36)

\displaystyle=\langle\mu^{N}_{k},(P^{N}_{t,k}-P^{N}_{t})f\rangle-\langle\mu^{N}-\mu^{N}_{k},P^{N}_{t}f\rangle

(4.37)

\displaystyle=A-B.

We see that
B\to 0
as
k\to\infty
according to the weak convergence
\mu^{N}_{k}\to\mu^{N}.

Using the boundedness of
f
we obtain

(4.38)

\displaystyle|A|\leq\int_{B_{R}(L^{2})}|P^{N}_{t,k}f(w)-P^{N}_{t}f(w)|\mu^{N}_{k}(dw)+2\mu^{N}_{k}(L^{2}\backslash B_{R}(L^{2}))=:A_{1}+A_{2}.

Now from (
3.60
),

(4.39)

\displaystyle\int_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\mu^{N}_{k}(du)\leq\int_{L^{2}}\mathcal{E}(u)\mu^{N}_{k}(du)\leq C,

where
C
does not depend on
(N,\alpha).
Combining this with the Chebyshev inequality we obtain that

(4.40)

\displaystyle A_{2}\leq\frac{2C}{R^{2}}.

Using the boundedness and Lipschitz properties of
f
, we obtain

(4.41)

\displaystyle A_{1}\leq A_{1,1}+A_{1,2}

where

(4.42)

\displaystyle A_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(L^{2})}\|{\mathbb{E}}u_{k}^{N}(t,\Pi^{N}w)-\phi_{t}w\|_{L^{2}}1_{S_{r}}\mu^{N}_{k}(dw)

(4.43)

\displaystyle A_{1,2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{L^{2}\backslash B_{R}(L^{2})}{\mathbb{E}}(1-1_{S_{r}})\mu^{N}_{k}(dw).

Using the (
4.34
) we obtain that

(4.44)

\displaystyle A_{1,2}\leq\frac{2Ct}{r^{2}}.

We finally obtain

(4.45)

\displaystyle|A|\leq A_{1,1}+\frac{2Ct}{r^{2}}+\frac{2C}{R^{2}}.

We pass to the limit on
k
first, by applying apply Lemma
4.3
, we obtain
A_{1,1}\to 0
. Then we take the limits
r\to\infty
and
R\to\infty
, we arrive at the conclusion of Assumption
7
.

Now let us prove Lemma (
4.3
):

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Let
u_{0}\in B_{R}(L^{2})
and
u_{k}
and
u
be the solutions of (
4.5
) (with viscosity
\alpha_{k}
) and (
4.1
) starting at
u_{0}
, respectively. Recall that
u_{k}
can be decomposed as
u_{k}=v_{k}+z_{k}
where
v_{k}
is the solution of (
4.25
) with initial datum
u_{0}
and
z_{k}
is given by (
4.23
) with
\alpha_{k}
. In order to prove Lemma
4.3
, it suffice to show that

(4.46)

\displaystyle\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\|v_{k}-u\|_{L^{2}}1_{S_{r,k}(t)}\right)\to 0,\quad\text{as $k\to\infty$}.

Indeed, we already have by (
4.24
) that
{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|z_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Set
w_{k}=v_{k}-u
. We will treat the case
s\leq 1+\sigma
which is more delicate. We then consider the equation satisfied by
w_{k}
:

(4.47)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}w_{k}={\textbf{i}}\Delta w_{k}+\Pi^{N}[w_{k}f(v_{k},u,z_{k})+z_{k}g(v_{k},z_{k})]-\alpha_{k}e^{\xi^{-1}(\|v_{k}+z_{k}\|_{H^{s-}})}(-\Delta)^{s-\sigma}(v_{k}+z_{k}),

where
f
and
g
is a homogeneous of degree
2
complex polynomial. We claim that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2}}=0
almost surely. Indeed, by taking the dot product with
w_{k}
, we obtain after the use of the Gronwall inequality,

(4.48)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{T(1+z_{N}^{2}+\|f(v_{k},z_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}})}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|z\|_{L^{2}}^{2}dt+\alpha_{k}T\right)\left(1+e^{C(N)(\xi^{-1}(\|v_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}})+\xi^{-1}(\|z_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}))}\right).

We pass to the limit
k\to\infty
with the use of (
4.24
) to obtain the claim.

Now, writing the Itô formula for
\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{M}(u_{k})\,d\tau=\|\Pi^{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha_{k}\frac{A_{0}^{N}}{2}t+2\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\sum_{m\leq N}a_{m}\int_{0}^{t}\langle u_{k},e_{m}\rangle\,d\beta_{m},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u)=M^{\prime}(u,\mathcal{L}(u))\geq 0.

Since
\alpha_{k}\leq 1,
we have that, on the set
S_{r,k}(t),

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\Pi^{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(r,N)t,

where
C(r,N)
does not depend on
k.
Hence we see that, on
S_{r,k}(t)
,

\displaystyle\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}}+\|z_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}}+2\|z_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+3C(r,N)t.

In particular, we have the following two estimates:

(4.49)

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 1}\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\mathbb{1}_{S_{r,k}}\leq R+3C(r,N)T,

Hence coming back to (
4.48
) and using the (deterministic) conservation
\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}=\|P_{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
and the estimate (
4.49
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\mathbb{1}_{S_{r}}\leq A(R,N,r,T)(\alpha_{k}+\|z_{k}\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}).

Therefore, using again the bound (
4.24
), we obtain the almost sure convergence
\|z_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
(as
k\to\infty
, up to a subsequence), we obtain then the almost sure convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\mathbb{1}_{S_{r}}=0.

Now, we use (
4.49
) and the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{u_{0}\in B_{R}}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}\mathbb{1}_{S_{r}}\to 0,\quad as\ k\to\infty.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is finished.
∎

Estimates

We have that

\displaystyle\int_{L^{2}}\mathcal{M}(u)\mu(du)=\frac{A_{0}}{2}.

Proof of Proposition

.

We present the more difficult case in which the dissipation is given by

The other case can be proved using a similar procedure.
We split the proof in different steps:

(1)

Step 1: Identity for the

, where

(2)

(3)

(c)

(2)

\displaystyle J_{12}(\underline{N})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\times B^{d}}Tu_{0}(\nabla u_{1})\cdot(\nabla u_{2})u_{3}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{0}^{2}}\operatorname{}\left|J_{11}(\underline{N})\right|\lesssim(\frac{N_{1}}{N_{0}})^{2}(N_{2}N_{3})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\varepsilon}\prod_{j=0}^{3}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{j}\right\|_{X_{\sigma}^{0,b^{\prime}}}.

\displaystyle\lesssim N_{1}N_{2}(N_{2}N_{3})^{\frac{d}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{3}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{j}\right\|_{X_{\sigma}^{0,\frac{1}{4}}}.

5.3.
Local well-posedness

We now turn to the proof of the main result in this section: local well-posedness.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{s}
. We first define a map

\displaystyle F(u)(t):=S_{\sigma}(t)u_{0}-{\textbf{i}}\int_{0}^{t}S_{\sigma}(t-s)\operatorname{}\left|u\right|^{2}u\,ds.

We prove this locally well-posedness theory using a standard fixed point argument.
Let

and

with

is a contraction mapping from

onto itself.

Define

.

(1)

is a self map from
B(0,R)\subset X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})
onto itself.

For
\delta<1
, by Lemma
2.4
, Proposition
5.10
and Sobolev embedding

\displaystyle\operatorname{}\left\|F(u)\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}}+c_{1}\delta^{1-b-b^{\prime}}\operatorname{}\left\|\operatorname{}\left|u\right|^{2}u\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,-b^{\prime}}(B^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}}+c_{2}\delta^{1-b-b^{\prime}}\delta^{3(b-b^{\prime})}\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}}+c_{2}\delta^{1+2b-4b^{\prime}}R^{3}.

Taking

(2)

F
is a contraction mapping.

2.4
and Proposition

\displaystyle\operatorname{}\left\|F(u)-F(v)\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\delta^{1+2b-4b^{\prime}}(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}^{2}+\operatorname{}\left\|v\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}^{2})\operatorname{}\left\|u-v\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}

Now we choose

(5.12)

As a consequence of the fixed point argument, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}\leq 2c_{0}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}}.

(3)

Stability.

If
u
and

are two solutions to (
1.1
) with initial data

and

respectively. Then by (
2
) and the choice of
\delta_{R}
(

\displaystyle\operatorname{}\left\|u-v\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\operatorname{}\left\|u_{0}-v_{0}\right\|_{H^{s}}+c_{4}\delta^{1+2b-4b^{\prime}}R^{2}\operatorname{}\left\|u-v\right\|_{X_{\sigma,\delta}^{s,b}(B^{d})},

\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
.

Proof of Proposition
6.5
.

Let us take
\theta=\frac{1}{2}+
and set

(6.21)

\displaystyle\gamma=\begin{cases}(-\ln\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}})^{\frac{1}{4(2\theta-d)}}&\text{if $0<\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}<1$}\\
0&\text{if $w_{0}=0$}.\end{cases}

Since
\theta<\frac{d}{2}
, we see that
\gamma\to 0
as
\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
. So we can consider
\gamma<<1
and take, as above, the function
\chi_{\gamma}=1_{\{r\geq\gamma\}}
. Using the radial Sobolev inequality (
2.9
), we have

(6.22)

\displaystyle\|w\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|w_{0}\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\gamma^{2(2\theta-d)}\int_{0}^{t}(\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2})\|w\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}d\tau.

By the Gronwall inequality we obtain

(6.23)

\displaystyle\|w\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|w_{0}\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}e^{\gamma^{2(2\theta-d)}C\int_{0}^{t}(\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2})d\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\|w_{0}\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}e^{\gamma^{4(2\theta-d)}}e^{C^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}d\tau\right)^{2}}

(6.24)

\displaystyle\leq e^{C^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}d\tau\right)^{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore

(6.25)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{C^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}d\tau\right)^{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\|w(1-\chi_{\gamma})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(6.26)

\displaystyle\leq e^{C^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}d\tau\right)^{2}}\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\|1-\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|w\|_{L^{4}}^{2}.

Since
\|1-\chi_{\gamma}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\to 0
as
\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\to 0
we arrive at the result of Proposition
6.5
.
∎

Notice that Remark
4.5
applies also in the present situation. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Appendix A
Results on probability measures and stochastic processes

In this section
(E,\|.\|)
is a Banach space,
C_{b}(E)
denotes the space of bounded continuous functions
f:E\to{\mathbb{R}}.
We present some useful standard results from measure theory.

A.1.
Convergence of measures

Definition A.1
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures and
\mu
a Borel probability measure on
E
. We say that
(\mu_{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
if for all
f\in C_{b}(E)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}f(x)\mu_{n}(dx)=\int_{E}f(x)\mu(dx).

We write
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu.

Definition A.2
.

A family
\Lambda
of Borel probability measures on
E
is said to be tight if for all
\epsilon>0
there is a compact set
K_{\epsilon}\subset E
such that for all
\mu\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\mu(K_{\epsilon})\geq 1-\epsilon.

Theorem A.3

(Portmanteau theorem, see Theorem 11.1.1 in
[
24
]
)
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of probability measures and
\mu
a probability measure on
E
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
,

(2)

for all open sets
U
,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(U)\geq\mu(U)
,

(3)

for all closed sets
F
,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(F)\leq\mu(F)
.

Theorem A.4

(Prokhorov theorem, see Theorem 2.3 in
[
20
]
)
.

A set
\Lambda
of Borel probability measures is relatively compact in
E
if and only if it is tight.

Theorem A.5

(Skorokhod representation theorem, see Theorem 2.4 in
[
20
]
)
.

For any sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
of Borel probability measures on
E
converging weakly to
\mu
, there is a probability space
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}^{0},{\mathbb{P}}_{0})
, and random variables
X,\ X_{1},\cdots
on
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}^{0},{\mathbb{P}}_{0})
such that

(1)

\mathcal{L}(X_{n})=\mu_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}(X)=\mu
,

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}X_{n}=X
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}-
almost surely.

Here
\mathcal{L}(Y)
stands for the law of the random variable
Y.

A.2.
Stochastic processes

Recall that if
(X_{t})_{t}
is an
E-
valued martingale, then since
\|.\|
is a convex function, we have that
(\|X_{t}\|)_{t}
is a submartingale (by Jensen inequality). Here is a version of the Doob maximal inequality (see Theorem 3.8 in
[
35
]
):

Theorem A.6

(Doob maximal inequality)
.

We have that for
p>1.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X(t)\|\right)^{p}\leq\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}{\mathbb{E}}\|X(T)\|^{p}.

The following can be found in Chapters 3 and 4 in
[
47
]
.

Definition A.7
.

An
N
-dimensional Itô process is a proces
X=(X_{t})_{t}
of the form

(A.1)

\displaystyle X(t)=X(0)+\int_{0}^{t}u(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,dB(s)

where
B=(B_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is an
N
-dimension Brownian motion,
u=(u_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is an
N
-dimensional stochastic process and
v=(v_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is a
N\times N
matrix that both are adapted with respect to
(B_{t})_{t}
and satisfy the following

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|u(s)|+|v(s)|^{2}\,ds<\infty\quad\forall t>0\right)

\displaystyle=1.

A short notation for (
A.1
) is given by

\displaystyle dX=u\,dt+v\,dB.

Theorem A.8

(
N
-dimensional Itô formula)
.

Let
X
be a
N
-dimensional Itô process as in (
A.1
). Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
C^{2}
function, then
f(X)
is a
1
-dimensional Itô process and satisfy

\displaystyle df(X)=\nabla f(X)\cdot dX+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}f(X)\,dX_{i}dX_{j},

with the properties
dB_{i}\,dB_{j}=\delta_{ij}\,dt
,
dt\,dB_{i}=0
.

Using the properties above, we can remark that in the particular case where
v
is diagonal (as in this paper), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}f(X)\,dX_{i}dX_{j}=\sum_{i}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}f(X)v_{i}^{2}\,dt.

Appendix B
Some important lemmas

In this section
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Feller semi-group (a Markov semi-group satisfying the Feller property) on a Banach space
X
,
P_{t}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
P_{t}
.

Lemma B.1

(Krylov-Bogoliubov argument)
.

If there exists
t_{n}\to\infty
and
\mu\in\mathfrak{P}(X)
such that
\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rightharpoonup\mu
in
X
, then
P_{t}^{*}\mu=\mu
for all
t\geq 0
.
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure at 0.

Proof of Lemma
B
.

Note that
P_{r}f\in C_{b}
. We have

\displaystyle\langle f,P_{r}^{*}\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle P_{r}f,\mu\rangle=\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle P_{r}f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle=\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,P_{r}^{*}\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t+r}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle=\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{r}^{t_{n}+r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle+\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle-\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\mu\rangle

where
\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle=0
and
\lim_{t_{n}\to\infty}\langle f,\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt\rangle=0
, since
\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt
and
\int_{0}^{r}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt
are bounded. Therefore,
P_{r}^{*}\mu=\mu
. Now the proof of Lemma
B
is finished.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Set

\displaystyle\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}P_{t}^{*}\delta_{0}\,dt.

Assume that
X
is compactly embedded into a Banach space
X_{0}
and

\displaystyle\int_{X}f(\|u\|_{X})\nu_{n}(du)\leq C

for some function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\infty
, and
C
does not depend on
n
. Then there exists
\mu\in\mathfrak{P}(X)
such that
\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
weakly on
X_{0}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Consider
B_{R}(X)
, by Markov inequality

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(X\setminus B_{R})=\nu_{n}(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X}>R)\leq\frac{C}{f(R)},

then
(\nu_{n})
is tight in
X_{0}
. Now, the Prokhorov theorem (see Theorem
A.4
) implies the existence of
\mu
in
\mathfrak{P}(X_{0})
.

Now, let us show that
\mu(X)=1
. It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\int_{X_{0}}f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\mu(du)\leq C<\infty

since, with such property, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(B_{R}^{c})\leq\frac{\int_{X_{0}}f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\mu(du)}{f(R)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }R\to\infty.

Now, let
\chi_{R}
be a
C^{\infty}
function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\chi_{R}=1
on
[0,R]
and
\chi_{R}=0
on
[R+1,\infty)
.

\displaystyle\int_{X_{0}}f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\chi_{R}(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X_{0}})\nu_{n}(du)\leq\int_{X_{0}}f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\nu_{n}(du)\leq C.

Since
f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\chi_{R}(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X_{0}})
is bounded continuous on
X_{0}
, by Fatou’s lemma, we have

\displaystyle\int_{X_{0}}f(\operatorname{}\left\|u\right\|_{X})\mu(du)\leq C.

The proof of Lemma
B.2
is finished.
∎
