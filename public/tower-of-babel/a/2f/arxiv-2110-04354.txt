2
Quantum Doubles and Casimir operators

Given two associative unital algebras
A
and
B
, we assume that there exists a
permutation map

\sigma:A\otimes B\to B\otimes A

preserving the algebraic structures of
A
and
B
. If these algebras are defined via relations on their generators, this property means that the relations are preserved by
\sigma

(for a more detailed definition see
[
GS2
]
). Using the map
\sigma
, one can convert the linear space
B\otimes A
into the unital associative algebra with the product
defined by the rule:

(b_{1}\otimes a_{1})*(b_{2}\otimes a_{2})=(m_{B}\otimes m_{A})\circ(b_{1}\otimes\sigma(a_{1}\otimes b_{2})\otimes a_{2}),

where
m_{B}
and
m_{A}
are the product operations in the algebras
B
and
A
respectively. Such an algebra
B\otimes A
is called a
quantum double
(QD) of the algebras

A
and
B
if the map
\sigma
is different from a (super-)flip. We denote the corresponding QD as
(A,B,\sigma)
or simply
(A,B)
.

The algebras
A\simeq 1_{B}\otimes A
and
B\simeq B\otimes 1_{A}
are subalgebras of the QD
(A,B)
with respect to the above
*
product since by definition

\sigma(1_{A}\otimes b)=b\otimes 1_{A}
and
\sigma(a\otimes 1_{B})=1_{B}\otimes a
for any
a\in A
,
b\in B
(see
[
GS2
]
). The QD products
*
of elements of the
subalgebra
A
and elements of the subalgebra
B
will be called the
permutation relations
induced by the map
\sigma
:

(1_{B}\otimes a)*(b\otimes 1_{A})=\sigma(a\otimes b).

If the algebra
A
is endowed with a counit
\varepsilon_{A}:A\to{{C}}
(a one-dimensional representation), it is possible to define an action
\triangleright:A\otimes B\rightarrow B
of the
algebra
A
onto
B
by setting

a\triangleright b:=(\mathrm{Id}\otimes\varepsilon_{A})\sigma(a\otimes b),\quad\forall\,a\in A,\,b\in B.

(2.1)

A typical example of a QD is the so-called Heisenberg double
[
IP
]
, in which the roles of the algebras
A
and
B
are played respectively by an RE algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
and
the so-called RTT algebra, defined via the same Hecke symmetry
R
.

In
[
GPS2
,
GPS3
]
we have introduced a QD
(A,B,\sigma)
, where
A=\hat{{\cal L}}(R)
,
B={\cal{M}}(R)
with the generating matrices
\hat{L}
and
M
respectively and the permutation map

\sigma:\,R\hat{L}_{1}RM_{1}\to M_{1}R\hat{L}_{1}R^{-1}+RM_{1}1_{A}.

(2.2)

By contrast with the mentioned Heisenberg double, this one is composed of two copies of RE algebras.

Also, we have introduced the matrix
D=M^{-1}\hat{L}
, whose entries are interpreted as
q
-partial derivatives in the entries of the matrix
M
. The matrix
D
was shown to
satisfy the matrix relation

]
:

R^{-1}D_{1}R^{-1}D_{1}=D_{1}R^{-1}D_{1}R^{-1}.

The permutation relations between the matrices
D
and

read as follows

D_{1}RM_{1}R=RM_{1}R^{-1}D_{1}+R\,1_{B}1_{A}.

(2.3)

Below, we shall omit the unit factors
1_{A}
and
1_{B}
in order to simplify the formulas.

Note that our treatment of the entries of the matrix

as analogs of partial derivatives is motivated by the following reason. If

2.3

of the matrix

are just the usual partial derivatives in the entries of the matrix

Observe that a particular case of such a QD, corresponding to the Quantum Group

Let us go back to the QD
(\hat{{\cal L}}(R),{\cal{M}}(R),\sigma)

\sigma:RL_{1}RM_{1}\,\to\,M_{1}RL_{1}R^{-1}.

The full list of the defining relations of this QD is as follows

RL_{1}RL_{1}=L_{1}RL_{1}R,\qquad RM_{1}RM_{1}=M_{1}RM_{1}R,

RL_{1}RM_{1}=M_{1}RL_{1}R^{-1}.

(2.4)

The equality (

) is a permutation relation between subalgebras
{\cal{L}}(R)
and
{\cal{M}}(R)
.

This QD will play the main role in our considerations below. In particular we perform the spectral analysis of the Casimir operators of the subalgebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
acting

in accordance with the rule (

The technical tools for describing a representation of
{\cal{L}}(R)
in
{\cal{M}}(R)
are given by the so-called
R
-matrix representations of the Hecke algebras
H_{k}(q)
,
k\geq 1
.
Recall that a Hecke algebra
H_{k}(q)
is generated by elements
1,\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k-1}
subject to the relations

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\tau_{i}\,\tau_{i+1}\,\tau_{i}=\tau_{i+1}\,\tau_{i}\,\tau_{i+1},&&1\leq i\leq k-2\\
\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}\tau_{i}\,\tau_{j}=\tau_{j}\,\tau_{i},&&|i-j|\geq 2\\
\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}(q\,1-\tau_{i})(q^{-1}\,1+\tau_{i})=0,&&q\in{{C}}\setminus\{\pm 1,0\}.\end{array}

A maximal commutative subalgebra in the Hecke algebra
H_{k}(q)
is generated by the so-called
Jucys-Murphy
elements
J_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
, which are defined
by the recurrence relation (see
[
OP
]
for detail):

J_{1}=1,\quad J_{i}=\tau_{i-1}J_{i-1}\tau_{i-1},\quad 2\leq i\leq k.

With any Hecke symmetry
R
we can associate the
R
-matrix representation of the Hecke algebra
H_{k}(q)
in the space
V^{\otimes k}
by the following
map

\tau_{i}\,\mapsto\,R_{i}:=I^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes R\otimes I^{\otimes(k-i-1)}\in\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes k}),\quad 1\leq i\leq k-1.

Besides, we introduce the “copies” of the generating matrix
L
of the RE algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
:

L_{\overline{1}}=L_{\underline{1}}=L_{1},\qquad L_{\overline{i+1}}=R_{i}L_{\overline{i}}R_{i}^{-1},\qquad L_{\underline{i+1}}=R_{i}^{-1}L_{\underline{i}}R_{i},\quad i\geq 1.

The same notation will be used for the generating matrix
M
of the RE algebra
{\cal{M}}(R)
.

With this notation we can rewrite the permutation relation (
2.4
) using the
R
-matrix representation of the Jucys-Murphy elements:

L_{\underline{2}}M_{1}=R_{1}^{-2}M_{1}L_{\overline{2}}=J_{2}^{-1}M_{1}L_{\overline{2}}.

This relation gives rise to the following generalizations valid for a chain of the “copies” of the matrix
M
:

L_{\underline{k+1}}M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}}=J_{k+1}^{-1}M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}}L_{\overline{k+1}}.

(2.5)

Now, introduce the counit
\varepsilon:{\cal{L}}(R)\rightarrow{{C}}
in the algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
:

\varepsilon(l_{i}^{j})=\delta_{i}^{j},\quad\varepsilon(1)=1_{{C}},\quad\varepsilon(a\cdot b)=\varepsilon(a)\,\varepsilon(b),\quad\forall\,a,b\in{\cal{L}}(R).

According to the scheme described above we get the action of the algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
onto
{\cal{M}}(R)
:

L_{\underline{k+1}}\triangleright M_{1}\dots M_{\overline{k}}=J_{k+1}^{-1}M_{1}\dots M_{\overline{k}}.

(2.6)

Hereafter, the notation
\triangleright
means that in any entry of the matrix

the element of the algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
is applied as an

{\cal{M}}(R)
.

Proposition 4

Proposition 5

Let
\lambda\vdash k
be a partition and
(\lambda,a)
be a standard Young table corresponding to the Young diagram of the partition

.
Then the following relation holds

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L\triangleright P_{({\lambda},a)}(R)M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}}=\chi_{{\lambda}}(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L)P_{({\lambda},a)}(R)M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}},

where

\chi_{{\lambda}}(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L)=\frac{N_{q}}{q^{N}}-\frac{\nu}{q^{2N}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\,q^{-2c(i)},

(3.2)

the sum is taken over all boxes of the table
({\lambda},a)
and

is the content of the box in which the integer

Note, that in (

\lambda

(\lambda,a)

Proof.
Take into account that due to the braid relarion on
R

\begin{array}[]{l}R_{1}^{\pm 1}R_{2}^{\pm 1}\dots R_{k}^{\pm 1}R_{i}=R_{i+1}R_{1}^{\pm 1}R_{2}^{\pm 1}\dots R_{k}^{\pm 1}\qquad 1\leq\forall\,i\leq k-1,\\
\rule{0.0pt}{17.07164pt}R_{k}^{\pm 1}\dots R_{2}^{\pm 1}R_{1}^{\pm 1}R_{j}=R_{j-1}R_{k}^{\pm 1}\dots R_{2}^{\pm 1}R_{1}^{\pm 1}\qquad 2\leq\forall\,j\leq k.\end{array}

Therefore, the matrix
L_{\underline{k+1}}=R_{k}^{-1}\dots R_{1}^{-1}L_{1}R_{1}\dots R_{k}
commute with any polynomial
Q
in the matrices
R_{i}
,

L_{\underline{k+1}}Q(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{k-1})=Q(R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{k-1})L_{\underline{k+1}}.

In the
R
-matrix representation any primitive idempotent

which is a polynomial in the matrices
R_{i}

. So, taking into account (

L_{\underline{k+1}}\triangleright P_{({\lambda},a)}(R)M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}}=P_{({\lambda},a)}(R)J_{k+1}^{-1}M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\dots M_{\overline{k}}.

(3.3)

Besides, it can be shown (see

So, on applying the

Proposition 9

Let
\lambda=({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2},\dots,{\lambda}_{N})
be a partition. Then the
{\lambda}
-character
(
3.2
)
of the Casimir element
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L
can be written in the following form

\chi_{{\lambda}}(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L)=q^{-1}\,\sum_{k=1}^{N}q^{-2({\lambda}_{k}+N-k)}.

(3.4)

Proof.
Let us multiply formula (
3.2
) by
q
and calculate the sums of terms
q^{-2c(i)}
corresponding to each row of the Young diagram
\lambda
.
For such a partial sum corresponding to a
j

\nu\,q^{1-2N}\left(q^{2(j-1)}+q^{2(j-2)}+\dots+q^{2(j-{\lambda}_{j})}\right)=q^{-2(N-j)}-q^{-2({\lambda}_{j}+N-j)}.

So, the second term of (
3.2
), multiplied by
q
, is equal to the expression

\nu\,q^{1-2N}\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}q^{-2c(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(q^{-2(N-j)}-q^{-2({\lambda}_{j}+N-j)}\right),

(3.5)

where
s
is the number of non-trivial components of the partition
{\lambda}=({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2},\dots,{\lambda}_{s},0,\dots,0)
,
{\lambda}_{s}>0

Taking into account that

q^{1-N}N_{q}=1+q^{-2}+\dots+q^{-2(N-1)},

we arrive at the final result:

q\,\chi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L)=1+q^{-2}+\dots+q^{-2(N-s-1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}q^{-2({\lambda}_{j}+N-j)}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q^{-2({\lambda}_{k}+N-k)},

since
\lambda_{k}=0
for
s+1\leq k\leq N

4
Higher Casimir and cut-and-join operators

In this section we shall consider the general case of a
GL_{m}
type Hecke symmetry with an arbitrary rank
m\leq N
. For such a symmetry
R
the generating matrix
L
of
the algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
is subject to the matrix Cayley-Hamilton (CH) identity (see for instance
[
GPS1
]
and the references therein):

L^{m}-qe_{1}(L)L^{m-1}+q^{2}e_{2}(L)L^{m-2}+\dots+(-q)^{m-1}e_{m-1}(L)L+(-q)^{m}e_{m}(L)\,I=0.

(4.1)

The corresponding
characteristic polynomial

Q(t)
meeting the relation
Q(L)=0
is of the form:

Q(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\,t^{m-k}(-q)^{k}e_{k}(L).

Its roots
\{\mu_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq m}
are called the
eigenvalues
of the matrix
L
. These roots are elements of a central extension of the algebra
{\cal{L}}(R)
.
Thus, we have

q^{k}e_{k}(L)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq m}\mu_{i_{1}}\dots\mu_{i_{k}}.

(4.2)

In particular,
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L=e_{1}(L)=q^{-1}\,\sum_{k=1}^{m}\,\mu_{k}
.

It is tempting to assign to the eigenvalues
\mu_{i}
their
{\lambda}
-characters
\chi_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})
whose values would be consistent with the relations (
4.2
).

Conjecture 11

Given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m})
, we set

\chi_{\lambda}(\mu_{i})=q^{-2({\lambda}_{i}+m-i)}.

This assignement is consistent with the relations
(
4.2
)
, that is

\chi_{{\lambda}}(e_{k}(L))=q^{-k}\,\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq m}\chi_{{\lambda}}(\mu_{i_{1}})\dots\chi_{{\lambda}}(\mu_{i_{k}}).

In Proposition
9
this conjecture was proved for the particular case
k=1
. Also, we checked this conjecture in a few low-dimensional cases.

Remark 12

As was shown by straightforward calculations, the
{\lambda}
-character of the Casimir operator
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}L
does not depend on the Young table
(\lambda,a)
. So far
we do not have analogous explicit calculations for the characters of the higher Casimir operators. Nevertheless, it is not difficult to see that the
{\lambda}
-characters of the these operators
do not depend on the table indeed. It is due to the property, mentioned above, that the idempotents
P_{({\lambda},a)}
, corresponding to different tables, are conjugated to each other.

Assuming Conjecture
11
to be true, we become able to compute the
{\lambda}
-characters of the elementary symmetric polynomials and consequently of the power
sums since these two families are related by
q
-analogs of the Newton relations. The reader is referred to
[
GPS4
]
for more details on the relations between these families.

Note that the matrix
\hat{L}
is also subject to a version of the CH identity. It can be readily obtained from (
4.1
) and (
1.4
). Let
\hat{\mu}_{i}
be the eigenvalues of the characteristic polynomial for the matrix
\hat{L}
. They are related to the eigenvalues of the matrix
L
by the formula

\mu_{i}=1-\nu\hat{\mu}_{i}.

Thus, we get

\chi_{\lambda}({\hat{\mu}_{k}})=\frac{1-q^{-2\,({\lambda}_{k}+m-k)}}{q-q^{-1}}=q^{-(\lambda_{k}+m-k)}(\lambda_{k}+m-k)_{q},\quad 1\leq k\leq m.

If
m=N
and
R\to P
as
q\to 1
, then by passing to the limit
q\rightarrow 1
in the above relation we obtain the
{\lambda}
-characters of the eigenvalues
\hat{\mu}_{k}
of the matrix
\hat{L}
generating the algebra
U(gl(N))
:

\chi_{\lambda}({\hat{\mu}_{k}})={\lambda}_{k}+N-k,\quad 1\leq k\leq N.

Note that a similar result is also valid for the generating matrix
\hat{L}
of the modified RE algebra, associated with any involutive symmetry
R

(i.e.
R^{2}=I
), provided
R
is the
q\to 1
limit of a
GL_{N}
type Hecke symmetry.

Now, we pass to construction of the higher cut-and-join operators
W^{\Delta}
, which are similar to those (
1.1
) but with a new meaning of the matrix
\hat{L}
, the traces and
the normal ordering. In our setting the matrix
\hat{L}
, giving rise to the cut-and-join operators, equals to the matrix product
MD
, where
D
and
M
are the generating matrices
of the algebras
{\cal{L}}(R^{-1})
and
{\cal{M}}(R)
respectively, their permutation relations are given by (
2.3
).

Thus, in order to introduce the quantum analogs of the operators
W^{\Delta}
we have to define the quantum normal ordering rule. For this purpose we replace each matrix
\hat{L}

in the definition of
W^{\Delta}
by the product
MD
and then, in full analogy with the classical pattern, we push all the matrices
D
through all the matrices
M
to the most right
position by means of the following permutation relations:

D_{1}RM_{1}R=RM_{1}R^{-1}D_{1}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad D_{1}RM_{1}=RM_{1}R^{-1}D_{1}R^{-1}.

(4.3)

These permutation relations are obtained from (
2.3
) by omitting the last (constant) term. Thus, we define the
q
-analog of the normal ordering for
the product of
q
-differential operators.

Besides, similarly to the classical case considered in
[
MMZ
]
, the cut-and-join operators thus defined can be expressed in terms of the quantum Casimir ones.

Let us consider a few examples. Namely, we compute the operators
W^{\Delta}
for
\Delta=(2,0,\dots,0)
,
\Delta=(1,1,0,\dots,0)
, and
\Delta=(3,0,\dots,0)
.

Example 13

For
\Delta=(2,0,\dots,0)
the cut-and-join operator reads
W^{(2)}=\,:\!\!\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{2}\!\!:
. In order to get the explicit expression for the normal
ordered form, we use the identity

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(\hat{L}_{1}\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}R_{1})=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(\hat{L}_{1}R_{1}\hat{L}_{1})=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}D_{1}R_{1}M_{1}D_{1})

and apply the permutation relations (
4.3
):

\displaystyle W^{(2)}=:\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{2}:\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!:\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}{\underline{D_{1}R_{1}M_{1}}}D_{1}):=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}R_{1}M_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}\,D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1})=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}D_{\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}).

A straightforward computation leads to the following connection between
W^{(2)}
and the Casimir operators:

W^{(2)}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{2}-q^{-m}m_{q}\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}.

(4.4)

Example 14

For
\Delta=(1,1,0,\dots,0)
we have the cut-and-join operator

W^{(1,1)}=:(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L})^{2}:=:\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(\hat{L}_{1}\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}):.

Its explicit form is

\displaystyle W^{(1,1)}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!:\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}{\underline{D_{1}R_{1}M_{1}}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}):=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}R_{1}M_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{R(12)}(M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}D_{\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}).

Also, we get the following expression of the operator
W^{(1,1)}
in terms of the Casimir operators:

W^{(1,1)}=(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L})^{2}-q^{-2m}\,\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}.

(4.5)

Example 15

At last, we give the explicit form of the cut-and-join operator
W^{(3)}=:\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{3}:
, corresponding to
\Delta=(3,0,\dots,0)
. To this end we use the identity

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{3}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(123)}(\hat{L}_{1}\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}\hat{L}_{\overline{3}}R_{2}R_{1})

and then apply the relations (
4.3
). Omitting the straightforward calculations we give the answer

W^{(3)}=:\mathrm{Tr}_{R}(\hat{L}^{3}):=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(123)}(M_{1}M_{\overline{2}}M_{\overline{3}}D_{\overline{3}}D_{\overline{2}}D_{1}(R_{2}^{-1}R_{1}^{-1})^{2}).

The expression of
W^{(3)}
via the Casimir operators is as follows

W^{(3)}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{3}-2\frac{m_{q}}{q^{m}}\,\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}^{2}-\frac{1}{q^{2m}}\,(\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L})^{2}+(q^{-4m}+q^{-2m}m_{q}^{2}\,)\,\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\hat{L}.

(4.6)

At last, we point out that the
{\lambda}
-characters of all operators under consideration do not depend on the concrete form of the initial Hecke symmetry
R
, but only on its rank.

As we said above, the classical cut-and-join operators are usually expressed in terms of the power sums
p_{k}=\mathrm{Tr}(M^{k})
,
k\geq 1
, and the partial derivatives in
p_{k}
. In our
setting such an expression is impossible and the action of the cut-and-join operators on the power sums
p_{k}
(and functions in
p_{k}
) has to be computed directly. We plan to consider this problem in our subsequent publications.
