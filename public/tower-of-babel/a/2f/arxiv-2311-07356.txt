1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the qualitative study of sums of higher powers, mainly in polynomial rings. In particular, we are interested in the problem of computing the higher Pythagoras numbers of a given ring.

It is known that the theory of quadratic forms is well developed. Literature devoted to that topic is extremely vast. Every quadratic form over a field of characteristic not two is diagonalizable. This is not true for forms of higher degree (cf.
[
24
]
). In particular considering forms of degree at least
3
one has to distinguish the cases of diagonal and general forms.

Let
A
be a commutative ring with identity and
s>1
be a positive integer.

Definition 1.1
.

We define the
s
-th Pythagoras number of
A
,
p_{s}(A)
, to be the least positive integer
\ell
such that any sum of
s
-th powers of elements of
A
can be expressed as a sum of
\ell

s
-th powers of elements of
A
. If such a number does not exist, we put
p_{s}(A)=\infty
.

Cassels Theorem
[
15
]
states that if a quadratic form with coefficients from a field
K
represents a polynomial
f\in K[x]
in a single variable over the field
K(x)
then it already represents
f
over
K[x]
. Taking the above quadratic form to be a sum of squares (sos) we deduce the equality of
2
-Pythagoras numbers
p_{2}(K[x])=p_{2}(K(x))
. Cassels Theorem does not generalize to higher powers
[
39
]
. In particular, a polynomial in a single variable can be a sum of fourth powers of rational functions, but not a sum of fourth powers of polynomials. Currently, this appears to be the main obstruction for computing
p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}[x])
(cf.
Section

5
), however it is known that
p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}(x))
is finite
[
3
]
.

Let us also mention one more difference. Consider the usual
n
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Obviously, it is the zero set of the quadratic polynomial
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2}-1=0
. If one further considers the group of linear isometries of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, then it is known that this action is transitive.
Consider now an algebraic set given by the zero set of
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{2s}-1=0
. There are very few linear isometries of such a set if
s>1
. In particular, the action of linear isometries is not transitive. This follows from the fact that
2s
-norm of
\ell^{2s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is not induced by the inner product for
n>1
(see also
[
19
,
34
]
for the structure of linear isometries of
\ell^{2s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
). Necessarily, any point with some fixed number of zero coordinates cannot be moved to an element with a different number of zeros. Consequently, when dealing with forms of higher degree, one cannot usually simplify things by the use of matrices. One has to consider the general case.

There is a vast literature devoted to the problem of computation of the
2
-Pythagoras number of a ring, see
[

,

,
20
,

,

,

to mention just a few. Similarly there are recent results concerning Waring problems
[

,

,
38
,

]
and rings of

[

as well as henselian local rings
[

. On the other hand, there are very few papers dealing with sums of higher powers in real rings, see
[

for higher Pythagoras numbers of fields and
[

for algebroid curves. Beside those, it is known that

Let
A
be a commutative ring with identity,
s\geq 1
be a positive integer and
a\in A
.

Definition 1.2
.

We define the
s
-length of

as the smallest positive integer
\ell
such that
a
can be written as a sum of
\ell

s
-th powers of elements of
A
.

For any

we consider the cone
\Sigma_{n,sd}^{s}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{sd}
of sums of
s
-th powers of forms of degree
d
. If
s
is even this is a closed, pointed and full-dimensional convex cone. By definition, any form

in
\Sigma_{n,sd}^{s}
has a representation

for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
. The smallest such

is called the
s
-length of

.

Definition 1.3

(homogeneous Pythagoras number)
.

We define the
s
-Pythagoras number
p_{s}(n,sd)
of
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{sd}
as the minimal number
\ell
such that any
f\in\Sigma_{n,sd}^{s}
has such a representation of length at most
\ell
.

The goal of this paper is to study the higher Pythagoras numbers of the polynomial rings. In particular we prove that
p_{2s}(K[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}])=\infty
provided that
K
is a real field and
n>1
. This almost solves an open problem from the paper
[
16
, Problem 8]
. Cases that are still open are precisely
n=1
and
s\geq 2
. From the above, we are able to deduce that for a large class of rings, all of its higher Pythagoras numbers are infinite. The second part of the paper focuses on the study of the 4-Pythagoras number.
It follows from the first part, that similarly as in the quadratic case, the homogenous
2s
-Pythagoras numbers in polynomial rings tends to infinity if we increase the degree as long as we consider forms in at least 3 variables. This led us to study binary forms, especially fourth powers, as this is the first non quadratic case. For any fixed degree
4d
there exists a full-dimensional semi-algebraic subset of
\Sigma_{2,4d}^{4}
such that any form in it has 4-length at most 4. If the degree is at least 10, then this subset consists of elements of precisely 4-length 4. This is also the smallest length that appears in a full-dimensional subset. Writing binary forms as sums of powers of linear forms has already been done by Reznick.

Our main results concern binary octics. We determine the algebraic boundary of the cone
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
by studying its dual cone which can be seen as a linear slice of the cone
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
of ternary quartics that are sums of squares, or equivalently are positive semidefinite (psd). Surprisingly, it turns out that the boundary is irreducible and a generic point of the boundary has a ’special’ length 3 representation as a sum of fourth powers of quadratics. Moreover, this is the unique representation as a sum of fourth powers. This allows us to deduce that the 4-Pythagoras number is either 3 or 4.
We are then able to disprove a conjecture of Reznick
[
42
, Conjecture 7.1]
expecting that sums of fourth powers are “doubly positive” meaning they can be written as sums of squares of psd forms.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
Section

2
contains the study of the higher Pythagoras number of a variety of rings. The main result of this section is the computation of the higher even Pythagoras numbers for the polynomial ring, i.e,

. In
Section

we examine the structure of the cone

of binary octics that are sums of fourth powers of quadratics as well as their

-Pythagoras number. In the next section, we determine the facial structure of the aforementioned cone and disprove Reznick’s Conjecture. We finish the paper with some open problems.

.

In this paper we consider only even powers of homogeneous polynomials. This is because of the following observation. Let

2.
Higher Pythagoras numbers

In this section we discuss higher Pythagoras numbers of rings. We are mainly concerned with the polynomial rings over a real field.
In particular,
p_{2}(n,2d)
corresponds to shortest sums of squares representations of forms. Even these are not well-understood in general (see
[
44
]
).
One of the main difficulties in studying higher Pythagoras numbers is that we are no longer considering quadratic forms which generally behave much nicer than higher tensor powers.

Firstly, let us show a lower bound for
p_{2s}(n,2sd)
by counting dimensions. The dimension of
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2sd}
is equal to
n+2sd-1\choose 2sd
and this is also the dimension of
\Sigma^{2s}_{n,2sd}
as this cone contains a generating set
\{l^{2sd}\colon l\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{1}\}
(see
[
41
, page 2]
) of the real vector space
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2sd}
. The
2s
-th Pythagoras number
p_{2s}(n,2sd)
has to satisfy

p_{2s}(n,2sd){n+d-1\choose d}\geq{n+2sd-1\choose 2sd}.

In other words,

p_{2s}(n,2sd)\geq\frac{{n+2sd-1\choose 2sd}}{{n+d-1\choose d}}.

A simple computation shows that if
d
tends to infinity, then the right hand side tends to a constant value
(2s)^{n-1}
. Upon dehomogenization, this can be translated into the inequality

p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}])\geq(2s)^{n-1}.

In particular
p_{4}({\mathbb{R}}[x])\geq 4
.
It is not a priori clear that the numbers
p_{2s}(n,2sd)
are finite. However, Caratheodory Theorem
[
41
, Proposition 2.3]

gives upper bound of the form

p_{2s}(n,2sd)\leq{n+2sd-1\choose 2sd}.

Our first result is a generalization of
[
16
, Proposition 4.5’]

to higher even powers. Let
s>1
be a positive integer and
K
be a formally real field.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let

be a polynomial of
2s
-length

Proof.

Assume by contrary that this is not the case, i.e.

(1)

G(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}^{2s}.

By setting

we obtain that for each

+(y-x^{r})^{2s}\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}^{2s}.

Since

, both

Consequently, the above expression can be reduced to

We may look at the polynomial

As a result, we get that

and for each

’s into

. As the final step we get

(y-x^{r})^{2s}(g(x,y)-\sum_{i=1}^{l}h_{i}^{2s})=0,

but this implies that the
2s
-length of

is less than

∎

Definition 2.8
.

Let

be a non-constant polynomial such that

We say that

See

]
for examples and further properties of admissible polynomials. In particular, an admissible polynomial has to be positive on an open unbounded set. We may now prove the following.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let

be an admissible polynomial which is not a square. Then

p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}[x,y,\sqrt{f(x,y)}])=\infty.

Proof.

We will carry out a proof for

Clearly, for any invertible matrix

, the rings

and
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y,\sqrt{f(x,y)}]
are isomorphic, hence without loss of generality we may assume that

is a strictly admissible polynomial.

We begin the proof by constructing a sequence of polynomials (cf.
[
8
, Definition 1.10]
).

Consider a sequence of positive integers

such that

•

r_{1}>\deg f
and
r_{i}>4\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}r_{k}

•

f(x,x^{r_{i}})
is a polynomial in
x
, which is either of odd degree, or it has even degree, and the leading coefficient is strictly positive.

We define a sequence of polynomials in the following way:

•

F_{1}=1

•

F_{n}=F_{n-1}\cdot(y-x^{r_{n-1}})^{4}+1

Fix a sequence of polynomial

satisfying the above properties. By the Proposition
2.1

4
-length of

in
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]
is equal to

.

Assume by contrary that the
4
-Pythagoras number of

. Consider the polynomial

. By assumption, we have the following

as the part that is divisible by

Let us now substitute

. We assumed that

is divisible by

. This readily implies that

for some

such that

and additionally

We may now rewrite

After cancelling 1’s and dividing by

.
Again, by dividing by

If the polynomial on the right is nonzero, then necessarily

3.
Binary octics

For the rest of the paper we will work with binary forms.
We do not know if the higher homogenous Pythagoras numbers increase if the degree tends to infinity. This is of course not the case for the 2-Pythagoras number as any positive semidefinite binary form can be written as a sum of at most two squares.
The cone
\Sigma_{2,4}^{4}
of binary quartics that are sums of fourth powers of linear forms is well understood by
[
41
]
. We therefore consider the case of octics and prove that the
4
-Pythagoras number is either 3 or 4 in this case (
3.10
). Moreover, we show
3.12
which states that the algebraic boundary of the cone
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
is irreducible.

Throughout the next two sections length always means
4
-length.
We will often use the sum of
4
-th powers map

\phi_{d}^{k}\colon\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{d}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{4d},\quad(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{4}

and its differential at the point
\underline{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})

\dphi_{d}^{k}(\underline{p})\colon\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{d}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{4d},\quad(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{3}q_{i}.

The image of the differential is given by
\langle p_{1}^{3},\dots,p_{k}^{3}\rangle_{4d}
.

Moreover, for
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
we denote by
\mathcal{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}={\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}({\mathbb{C}})
the zero set of the forms
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
, i.e. the set of all points
\xi\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
such that
f_{i}(\xi)=0
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

The following simple proposition shows that one could hope for a constant 4-Pythagoras number of binary forms and possibly prove
p_{4}(2,4d)=4
. However, this set being full-dimensional is a very small indication. We give a direct, slightly combinatorial argument here.
One may also use powers of linear forms instead of monomials and use known cases of Fröberg’s conjecture (see
[
35
, Lemma 2.2, Theorem 2.3]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n=2
. For every
d\geq 1
the semi-algebraic set of all forms in
\Sigma^{4}_{2,4d}
of length at most 4 is a full-dimensional semi-algebraic set.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the differential
\dphi^{4}_{d}(\underline{p})
is surjective for one specific (and therefore a generic) choice of
\underline{p}\in\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]^{4}_{d}
.
Let
r\in\{0,1,2\}
such that
d\equiv r\mod 3
.
Choose
p_{1}=x^{d},p_{2}=y^{d}
and
p_{3},p_{4}
depending on
r
. In every case we show that every monomial in
\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{4d}
is contained in
I_{4d}=\langle p_{1}^{3},\dots,p_{4}^{3}\rangle_{4d}
which is the component of degree
4d
of the ideal
I=\langle p_{1}^{3},\dots,p_{4}^{3}\rangle
.

In every case, since
x^{3d},y^{3d}\in I
we see that for
k\leq d
and
k\geq 3d
the monomial
x^{k}y^{4d-k}
is contained in
I
.

r=0\,
:
p_{3}=(x^{2}y)^{\frac{d}{3}},\,p_{4}=(xy^{2})^{\frac{d}{3}}

We have
p_{3}^{3}=x^{2d}y^{d},\,p_{4}^{3}=x^{d}y^{2d}
which implies that
x^{k}y^{4d-k}\in I
for
2d\leq k\leq 3d
and
d\leq k\leq 2d
. Hence
I_{4d}=\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{4d}
.

r=2\,
:
p_{3}=(x^{2}y)^{\frac{d-2}{3}}xy,\,p_{4}=(xy^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{3}}xy

We have
p_{3}^{3}=x^{2d-1}y^{d+1},\,p_{4}^{3}=x^{d+1}y^{2d-1}
which shows
x^{k}y^{4d-k}\in I
for
2d-1\leq k\leq 3d-1
and
d+1\leq k\leq 2d+1
. Hence
I_{4d}=\mathbb{R}[\underline{x}]_{4d}
.

r=1\,
: In this case monomials do not suffice. Consider
p_{3}=x^{\frac{d+2}{3}}y^{\frac{2d-2}{3}},\,p_{4}=x^{d}+y^{d}
.

Since
p_{3}^{3}=x^{d+2}y^{2d-2}
, the monomial
x^{k}y^{4d-k}
is in
I
if
d+2\leq k\leq 2d+2
. We are therefore missing
k=d+1
and
2d+3\leq k\leq 3d-1
.
We have

p_{3}^{3}=x^{3d}+y^{3d}+3x^{2d}y^{d}+3x^{d}y^{2d}\equiv x^{2d}y^{d}+x^{d}y^{2d}\mod I_{3d}

and therefore
(x^{2d}y^{d}+x^{d}y^{2d})xy^{d-1}=x^{2d+1}y^{2d-1}+x^{d+1}y^{3d-1}\equiv x^{d+1}y^{3d-1}\mod I_{4d}
. Lastly for every
0\leq s\leq d
we see

(x^{2d}y^{d}+x^{d}y^{2d})x^{s}y^{d-s}=x^{2d+s}y^{2d-s}+x^{d+s}y^{3d-s}\equiv x^{2d+s}y^{2d-s}\mod I_{4d}

since
d+s\in\{d,\dots,2d\}
.
∎

We study the cone of binary octics that are sums of fourth powers of quadratic forms. Via the Veronese map and duality this is equivalent to studying a certain intersection of the psd/sos cone of ternary quartics. It can also be seen as the cone of sums of fourth powers of linear forms in
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]/(b^{2}-ac)
, the coordinate ring of the second Veronese of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

We show that the algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
(i.e. the Zariski closure of the boundary of
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
) is an irreducible hypersurface and consists of binary octics of length at most 3. Using this we deduce that every binary octic that is a sum of fourth powers of quadratic forms can be written as a sum of at most four such forms.

The first goal is to show that any element on the boundary of
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
has 4-length at most 3 (
3.9
).

3.1.
The dual cone

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
consider the
apolarity pairing
on
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and on
{\mathbb{C}}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Given two monomials
\underline{x}^{\alpha},\,\underline{x}^{\beta}
,
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
we define

\langle{\underline{x}^{\alpha}},{\underline{x}^{\beta}}\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{\beta!}{\alpha!}\underline{x}^{\beta-\alpha}\quad&,\text{if }\alpha\leq\beta\\
0\quad&,\text{else}\end{cases}

where
\alpha!=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}!)
.
This is the same as having the first element act on the second via differentiation. For any
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
this gives a scalar product on
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
. Over the complex numbers this is not true, but it is still a perfect pairing.

Consider the dual cone

(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}=\{L\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}\colon L(q^{4})\geq 0\,\forall\,q\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}\}.

Let
q(a,b,c)=ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}
with new variables
a,b,c
and let
L=\sum_{i=0}^{8}a_{i}x^{i}y^{8-i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
. By identifying
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}
and
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
via the apolarity pairing we get

(4)

\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{8}a_{i}\langle{x^{i}y^{8-i}},{x^{i}y^{8-i}}\rangle g_{i}(a,b,c)

for some forms
g_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
. Hence,
L\in(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}\Leftrightarrow\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle\in\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
. It was proven by Hilbert that the cone of positive semidefinite ternary quartics is equal to the sum of squares cone.
This shows that the dual cone
(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
is a linear slice of the sos cone
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
of ternary quartics (i.e. it is an intersection of
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
with a linear subspace). From
Eq.

4
we see that the coefficients of the ternary quartic
\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle
are not linearly independent but that the ternary quartics are contained in a 9-dimensional subspace.

For
F\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
write
F=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}c_{\alpha}\underline{a}^{\alpha}
, where
\underline{a}=(a,b,c)
. We calculate that the subspace
U
of
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
spanned by ternary quartics of the above form is given by the vanishing of the six linear forms

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}c_{220}-c_{301},\,3c_{130}-c_{211},\,\frac{2}{3}c_{022}-c_{103},\,3c_{031}-c_{112},\,12c_{040}-c_{121},\,6c_{040}-c_{202}.

Equivalently, it is the orthogonal complement of
(b^{2}-ac)_{4}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
with respect to apolarity.
Understanding
(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
is the same as studying
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}\cap U
. The facial structure of
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
has been studied by Kunert in
[
33
]
and is rather explicitly known.

Let
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}
and assume
f=\sum_{i=1}^{s}q_{i}^{4}
is on the boundary of
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
. Let
L\in(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
be a linear functional in
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}
vanishing at
f
, then
L(f)=0
and thus
L(q_{i}^{4})=0
(
i=1,\dots,s
) which shows that the points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})={\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2})
corresponding to
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}
are zeros of the psd ternary quartic
F=\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle
.

The
\gl_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
action on
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]
gives rise to an embedding of
\gl_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
into
\gl_{3}({\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1})
which allows some automorphisms on
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
that fix the subspace
U
as well as
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
.

Since we are dealing with real zeros of psd elements understanding the behaviour of derivatives is essential. Let
L\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
and
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Via a direct calculation we see

\partial_{u}\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle=4\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{3}(u_{1}x^{2}+u_{2}xy+u_{3}y^{2})}\rangle

where the partial derivatives are taken with respect to the variables
a,b,c
.

A different way to arrive at the same point is as follows. Let
v_{2}\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be the second Veronese map. With coordinates
a,b,c
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
the image is given as the vanishing set of
b^{2}-ac
. This gives rise to an isomorphism
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2d}\cong{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]/(b^{2}-ac)_{d}
for any
d\geq 1
. Moreover, under this isomorphism the cones
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
and
\Sigma^{4}{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]/(b^{2}-ac)_{1}
are isomorphic. Here the second cone consists of elements that are sums of fourth powers of linear forms. Dualizing the second cone we get

(\Sigma^{4}{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]/(b^{2}-ac)_{4})^{\star}\cong\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}\cap(b^{2}-ac)_{4}^{\perp}.

The fact that the cone of sums of squares
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
and the cone
P_{3,4}
of psd ternary quartics coincide will be essential for certain calculations as testing for non-negativity is very hard.

Let
L\in\partial(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
, then
F:=\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle
is a psd ternary quartic in
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
which therefore is also a sum of squares. Since
L
is on the boundary there exists
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}
such that
L(g^{4})=0
. Thus
F
has a real zero and lies on the boundary of the sos cone
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
.

We show (Proposition
3.3
and Theorem
3.6
) that there are two cases. Either

(i)

|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|\leq 3
or

(ii)

|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|=\infty
,
(L^{\perp})_{3}\neq\{0\}
and
F=l^{4}
for some
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1}
.

We use the characterization of faces of
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
from Kunert
[
33
]
. In this thesis all faces of the sums of squares cone
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
are characterized in terms of the types of singularities of its relative interior points.
In particular, a quartic
F\in\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}
has either infinitely many real points or at most 4.
Our main technical result is to prove that there is no quartic in
\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}\cap U
that has exactly four real zeros.

For the rest of the paper we will slightly abuse notation and will see an element of
(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
as either an element of
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
or a ternary quartic in
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
. In general, we use
f
for binary octics,
F
for ternary quartics and
L
for linear functionals in
({\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8})^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}
. As we have seen the latter two are both dual elements to
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
, however both points of view have their separate advantages.

Remark 3.2
.

Our results imply the following.
There are the following types of ternary quartics
F
on the boundary of the dual cone
(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
.

(i)

F=l^{4}
with
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})=\{(\xi_{1}\colon\xi_{2}\colon\xi_{3})\}
with
\xi_{2}^{2}-4\xi_{1}\xi_{3}>0
, i.e. the associated binary quadratic form is indefinite. And for every such point
\xi
there exists
F
whose only real zero is
\xi
.

(iii)

|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|=2,3
and all points correspond to indefinite binary quadratics. In this case not every pair and triple of points is possible.

3.2.
Main results

For
L\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}
denote by
L^{\perp}
the apolar ideal of
L
, i.e. the ideal generated by all forms
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]
such that
\langle{g},{L}\rangle=0
. Note that
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]/L^{\perp}
is an artinian Gorenstein ring with socle in degree 8. Moreover, since we are working with binary forms the ideal
L^{\perp}
is also a complete intersection with the degrees of the two generators summing up to 10. Considering this Gorenstein ideal already proved to be a main ingredient in studying sum of squares cones, for example in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
L\in(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
and
F=\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle
.
If
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|=\infty
, then
l^{\prime 3}\in(L^{\perp})_{3}
for some
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
and every element in
(L^{\perp})_{6}
is divisible by
l^{\prime 2}
.
Moreover, there exists
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1}
such that
F=l^{4}
.

Proof.

By
[
33
, §2.3.]
either
F=p^{2}
for some indefinite, irreducible
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{2}
or
F=l^{2}u
for some
u\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{2},l\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1}
. Since
F
is singular at any real zero
u=(u_{0}:u_{1}:u_{2})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
we see

\langle{L},{q(u_{0},u_{1},u_{2})^{3}h}\rangle=0\quad\forall h\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}.

If
F=l^{2}u
, there exists a 2-dimensional subspace
V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}
such that for every
q\in V
,
q^{3}\in L^{\perp}
,
V
is given as the orthogonal of
\spn(l)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{1}\cong{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}
. Since
\spn(q^{3}\colon q\in V)=V^{3}
, we see
V^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}\subseteq L^{\perp}
.

If
V
is spanned by a regular sequence, the Hilbert function of the ideal generated by
V
is
(1,2,1)
. Since

V^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}=V^{2}(V{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2})=V^{2}({\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{4})=V(V{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{4})=V{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{6}={\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{8}

and
\langle{\cdot},{L}\rangle
vanishes on it we have a contradiction.
Therefore
V
is spanned by
l^{\prime}l_{1},l^{\prime}l_{2}
for some linear forms
l^{\prime},l_{1},l_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
. Then
V=l^{\prime}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
and
V^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}=l^{\prime 3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{5}
, hence
l^{\prime 3}
is in the apolar ideal of
L
.
Additionally, since the apolar ideal is a complete intersection and there is one generator in degree at most 3, the other generator has degree at least 7 (see
[
26
, Thm 1.44.]
). In particular, every form in degree 6 is divisible by
l^{\prime 2}
. Thus every real zero of
F
is a zero of
l
. Moreover,
l^{\prime 8}\in L^{\perp}
.

Therefore
\langle{L},{l^{\prime 8}}\rangle=0
. We claim that this already implies
F=l^{4}
. After a change of coordinates we can assume
l^{\prime}=x
and therefore
F
vanishes at
(1:0:0)
.
We calculate

U\cap I((1:0:0))_{2}^{2}=\spn(ab^{3}+3a^{2}bc,b^{2}c^{2}+\frac{2}{3}ac^{3},b^{3}c+3abc^{2},b^{4}+12ab^{2}c+6a^{2}c^{2},c^{4},bc^{3}).

The Gram matrices of an element

ab^{3}a_{1}+3a^{2}bca_{1}+b^{2}c^{2}a_{2}+\frac{2}{3}ac^{3}a_{2}+b^{3}ca_{3}+3abc^{2}a_{3}+b^{4}a_{4}+12ab^{2}ca_{4}+6a^{2}c^{2}a_{4}+c^{4}a_{5}+bc^{3}a_{6}

in the intersection have the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrrr}0&9a_{1}&3a_{1}&36a_{4}-\lambda_{1}&9a_{3}-\lambda_{2}\\
9a_{1}&36a_{4}&\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&2a_{2}\\
3a_{1}&\lambda_{1}&6a_{4}&3a_{3}&\lambda_{3}\\
36a_{4}-\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&3a_{3}&6a_{2}-2\lambda_{3}&3a_{6}\\
9a_{3}-\lambda_{2}&2a_{2}&\lambda_{3}&3a_{6}&6a_{5}\end{array}\right)

for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to the basis
ab,ac,b^{2},bc,c^{2}
.
By checking all principle
2\times 2
minors, it is easy to see that the only form in the intersection having a psd Gram matrix is
a_{5}c^{4}
with
a_{5}>0
.

Lastly, assume
F=p^{2}
with
p
indefinite, irreducible. Since
F\in U
the quadratic form
\langle{F},{b^{2}-ac}\rangle\in{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{2}
has to vanish identically. This gives six equations. One then easily checks on a computer that the determinant of the quadratic form
p
is contained in the radical of the ideal generated by the six forms above, i.e.
p
is reducible, a contradiction.
∎

We now prepare to prove
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|\leq 3
if
\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})
is finite. Recall that we already know from
[
33
]
that in this case
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|\leq 4
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2})
be linearly independent with a common factor and
q_{1}^{3},q_{2}^{3}\in L^{\perp}
. Then
l^{3}\in(L^{\perp})_{3}
for some
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
q_{1}=ll_{1},q_{2}=ll_{2}
with
l,l_{1},l_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
. By assumption
(ll_{1})^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2},(ll_{2})^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}\subseteq L^{\perp}
. Since
l_{1},l_{2}
are a regular sequence, the Hilbert function of the ideal
\langle l_{1}^{3},l_{2}^{3}\rangle
is
(1,2,3,2,1)
. Thus

L^{\perp}\supseteq(ll_{1})^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}+(ll_{2})^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}=l^{3}\langle l_{1}^{3},l_{2}^{3}\rangle_{5}=l^{3}{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{5}.

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L\in(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}
and
F=\langle{L},{q(a,b,c)^{4}}\rangle
.
F
does not vanishes at any real point
\xi\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
corresponding to a definite binary quadratic form
q
.

Proof.

Assume otherwise. After a change of coordinates we can assume that
q=x^{2}+y^{2}
.
By
[
41
, Thm 9.5.]

q^{4}
is a sum of five 8th powers of real linear forms.
This shows that
F
vanishes at least at 5 real points, hence vanishes at infinitely many real points. By
3.3
there exists
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
such that
l|q
which is impossible if
q
is positive definite.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
F\in\partial(\Sigma_{2,8}^{4})^{\star}=\partial\Sigma_{3,4}^{2}\cap U
with
\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})
finite. Then
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|\leq 3
.

Proof.

By
[
33
, Lemma 1.38.]
we have
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|\leq 4
since
\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})
is finite.
Assume
|\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})|=4
. Since
\mathcal{V}(F)({\mathbb{R}})
is finite, no three of its real zeros are collinear.
Let
q_{1},\dots,q_{4}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{2}
be the corresponding quadratic forms. If any two of them were not a complete intersection then by
3.4
there exists
l\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
such that
l^{3}\in(L^{\perp})_{3}
and thus
(ll^{\prime})^{4}\in L^{\perp}
for all
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]_{1}
which shows that
F
has infinitely many zeros.

Pick three of these points
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and let
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]
be the ideal of forms vanishing at
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}
. The degree 2 component
I_{2}
is generated by
q_{1}=l_{12}l_{13},q_{2}=l_{13}l_{23},q_{3}=l_{13}l_{23}
where
l_{ij}
is the line through
\xi_{i},\xi_{j}
. Concretely,
l_{ij}
is given as

l_{ij}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{i}^{1}&\xi_{i}^{2}\\
\xi_{j}^{1}&\xi_{j}^{2}\end{pmatrix}a+\det\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{i}^{2}&\xi_{i}^{0}\\
\xi_{j}^{2}&\xi_{j}^{0}\end{pmatrix}b+\det\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{i}^{0}&\xi_{i}^{1}\\
\xi_{j}^{0}&\xi_{j}^{1}\end{pmatrix}c

where
\xi_{i}=(\xi_{i}^{j})_{j}
.
Consider the
15\times 15
-matrix where the first 9 rows correspond to a basis of
U
(with respect to some basis of
{\mathbb{R}}[a,b,c]_{4}
) and the last 6 correspond to generators of
(I_{2}^{2})_{4}
. Since

5.
Open problems

In this last section we propose some open problems which might stimulate further research in this area.

Question 5.1
.

What is the value of
p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}[x])
? In particular, what is the value of
p_{4}({\mathbb{R}}[x])
?

So far the only known lower bounds is the
2s\leq p_{2s}({\mathbb{R}}[x])
as shown at the beginning of the Section 2.

Theorem of Becker
[
3
, Theorem 2.12]
states that for any field
K
we have
p_{2}(K)<\infty
iff
p_{2s}(K)<\infty
for some
s
iff
p_{2s}(K)<\infty
for all
s
. The next conjecture asks for a generalization of the above theorem

Conjecture 5.2
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

p_{2}(R)<\infty

(ii)

p_{2s}(R)<\infty
for some
s

(iii)

p_{2s}(R)<\infty
for all
s
.

If the above conjecture is true, then the problem of finiteness of higher Pythagoras numbers could be reduced to the finiteness of the 2-Pythagoras number. This is important and usually much easier, mostly because the behavior of quadratic forms is well understood compared to forms of higher degrees. It is worth mentioning that the above conjecture does hold for real valuation rings. This follows from
[
4
, Theorem 5.17]
with the simple observation that for valuation rings it is enough to consider units which are sums of
2s
-th powers. Also, this paper provides quite a variety of rings for which the above conjecture trivially holds since all of the quantities are infinite.

Question 5.3
.

We saw in
3.14
that a general element of
\Sigma_{2,8}^{4}
has (up to permutation and roots of unity) 76 complex representations of length 3. Where does this number come from? Can we construct all 76 of them, and possibly even show that some of them are real?

This might be similar to length three sum of squares representations of ternary quartics. These are known to be in one-to-one correspondence (up to orthogonal equivalence) to Steiner complexes associated to the complex curve.

Question 5.4
.

The proof of
3.6
is a computation in the end. It would be nice to understand the reason why such a ternary quartic cannot have 4 real zeros.
