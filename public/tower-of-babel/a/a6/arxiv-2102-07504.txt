Abstract
We extend the 𝙻 ⋆ \mathtt{L}^{\!\star} algorithm to learn bimonoids recognising pomset languages. We then identify a class of pomset automata that accepts precisely the class of pomset languages recognised by bimonoids and show how to convert between bimonoids and automata.



1
Introduction

Automata learning algorithms are useful in automated inference of models, which is needed for verification of hardware and software systems. In
active

learning, the algorithm interacts with a system through tests and observations to produce a model of the system’s behaviour. One of the first active learning algorithms proposed was
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
, due to Dana Angluin
[
2
]
, which infers a minimal deterministic automaton for a target regular language.
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
has been used in a range of verification tasks, including learning error traces in a program
[
5
]
. For more advanced verification tasks, richer automata types are needed and
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
has been extended to e.g. input-output
[
1
]
, register
[
19
]
, and weighted automata
[
15
]
. None of the existing extensions can be used in analysis of concurrent programs.

Partially ordered multisets (pomsets)
[
13
,
12
]
are basic structures used in the modeling and semantics of concurrent programs. Pomsets generalise words, allowing to capture both the sequential and the parallel structure of a trace in a concurrent program. Automata accepting pomset languages are therefore useful to study the operational semantics of concurrent programs—see, for instance, work on concurrent Kleene algebra
[
16
,
25
,
20
,
23
]
.

In this paper, we propose an active learning algorithm for a class of pomset automata. The approach is algebraic: we consider languages of pomsets recognised by bimonoids
[
27
]
(which we shall refer to as pomset recognisers). This can be thought of as a generalisation of the classical approach to language theory of using monoids as word acceptors: bimonoids have an extra operation that models parallel composition in addition to sequential. The two operations give rise to a complex branching structure that makes the learning process non-trivial.

The key observation is that pomset recognisers are tree automata whose algebraic structure satisfies additional equations. We extend tree automata learning algorithms
[
7
,
8
,
30
]
to pomset recognisers. The main challenge is to ensure that intermediate hypotheses in the algorithm are valid pomset recognisers, which is essential in practical scenarios where the learning process might not run to the very end, returning an approximation of the system under learning. This requires equations of bimonoids to be correctly propagated and preserved in the core data structure of the algorithm—the observation table. The proof of termination, in analogy to
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
, relies on the existence of a canonical pomset recogniser of a language, which is based on its syntactic bimonoid. The steps of the algorithm provide hypotheses that get closer in size to the canonical recogniser.

Finally, we bridge the learning algorithm to pomset automata
[
20
,
21
]
by providing two constructions that enable us to seamlessly move between pomset recognisers and pomset automata. Note that although bimonoids provide a useful formalism to denote pomset languages, which is amenable to the design of the learning algorithm, they enforce a redundancy that is not present in pomset automata: whereas a pomset automaton processes a pomset from left to right in sequence, one letter per branch at a time, a bimonoid needs to be able to take the pomset represented as a binary tree in any way and process it bottom-up. This requirement of different decompositions leading to the same result makes bimonoids in general much larger than pomset automata and hence the latter are, in general, a more efficient representation of a pomset language.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. We conclude this introductory section with a review of relevant related work.
Section

2
contains the basic definitions on pomsets and pomset recognisers. The learning algorithm for pomset recognisers appears in
Section

3
, including proofs to ensure termination and invariant preservation.
Section

4
presents constructions to translate between (a class of) pomset automata and pomset recognisers. We conclude with discussion of further work in
Section

5
. Omitted proofs appear in

Appendices

0.A
and
0.B
.

Related Work.

There is a rich literature on adaptations and extensions of
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
from deterministic automata
to various kinds of models, see, e.g.,
[
33
,
17
]
for an overview.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to provide an active learning algorithm
for pomset languages recognised by finite bimonoids.

Our algorithm learns an algebraic recogniser.
Urbat and Schröder
[
32
]
provide a very general learning
approach for languages recognised by algebras for monads
[
4
,
31
]
, based on a reduction to categorical automata, for which they present an
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
-type
algorithm. Their reduction gives rise to an infinite alphabet
in general, so tailored work is needed for deriving algorithms and finite representations.
This can be done for instance for monoids, recognising regular languages,
but it is not clear how this could extend to pomset recognisers. We present a
direct learning algorithm for bimonoids, which does not rely on any encoding.

Our concrete learning algorithm for bimonoids is closely related to
learning approaches for bottom-up tree automata
[
7
,
8
,
30
]
: pomset languages can be viewed as
tree languages satisfying certain equations. Incorporating these equations turned out to be a non-trivial
task, which requires additional checks on the observation table during execution of the algorithm.

Conversion between recognisers and automata for a pomset language was first explored by Lodaya and Weil
[
27
,
26
]
.
Their results relate the expressive power of these formalisms to
sr-expressions
.
As a result, converting between recognisers and automata using their construction uses an sr-expression as an intermediate representation, increasing the resulting state space.
Our construction, however, converts recognisers directly to pomset automata, which keeps the state space relatively small.
Moreover, Lodaya and Weil work focus on pomset languages of
bounded width
, i.e., with an upper bound on the number of parallel events.
In contrast, our conversions work for all recognisable pomset languages (and a suitable class of pomset automata), including those of unbounded width.

Ésik and Németh
[
9
]
considered automata and recognisers for
biposets
, i.e., sp-pomsets without commutativity of parallel composition.
They equate languages recognised by
bisemigroups
(bimonoids without commutativity or units) with those accepted by
parenthesizing automata
.
Our equivalence is similar in structure, but relates a subclass of pomset automata to bimonoids instead.
The results in this paper can easily be adapted to learn representations of biposet languages using bisemigroups, and convert those to parenthesizing automata.

2
Pomset Recognisers

Throughout this paper we fix a finite
alphabet

\Sigma
and assume
\square\not\in\Sigma
.
When defining sets parameterised by a set
X
, say
\mathsf{S}(X)
, we may use
\mathsf{S}
to refer to
\mathsf{S}(\Sigma)
.

We recall pomsets
[
12
,
13
]
, a generalisation of words that model concurrent traces.
A
labelled poset
over
X
is a tuple
\mathbf{u}=\left\langle S_{\mathbf{u}},\leq_{\mathbf{u}},\lambda_{\mathbf{u}}\right\rangle
, where
S_{\mathbf{u}}
is a finite set (the
carrier
of
\mathbf{u}
),
\leq_{\mathbf{u}}

is a partial order on
S_{\mathbf{u}}
(the
order
of
\mathbf{u}
), and
\lambda_{\mathbf{u}}\colon S_{\mathbf{u}}\to X
is a function (the
labelling
of
\mathbf{u}
).
Pomsets are labelled posets up to isomorphism.

Definition 1
(Pomsets)

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
be labelled posets over
X
.
An
embedding
of
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbf{v}
is an injection
h:S_{\mathbf{u}}\to S_{\mathbf{v}}
such that
\lambda_{\mathbf{v}}\circ h=\lambda_{\mathbf{u}}
and
s\leq_{\mathbf{u}}s^{\prime}
if and only if
h(s)\leq_{\mathbf{v}}h(s^{\prime})
.
An
isomorphism
is a bijective embedding whose inverse is also an embedding.
We say
\mathbf{u}
is
isomorphic
to
\mathbf{v}
, denoted
\mathbf{u}\cong\mathbf{v}
, if there exists an isomorphism between
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
.
A
pomset
over
X
is an isomorphism class of labelled posets over
X
, i.e.,
[\mathbf{v}]=\{\mathbf{u}:\mathbf{u}\cong\mathbf{v}\}
.
When
u=[\mathbf{u}]
and
v=[\mathbf{v}]
are pomsets,
u
is a
subpomset
of
v
when there exists an embedding of
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbf{v}
.

When two pomsets are in scope, we tacitly assume that they are represented by labelled posets with disjoint carriers.
We write
1
for the empty pomset.
When
\mathtt{a}\in X
, we write
\mathtt{a}
for the pomset represented by the labelled poset whose sole element is labelled by
\mathtt{a}
.
Pomsets can be composed in sequence and in parallel:

Definition 2
(Pomset composition)

Let
u=[\mathbf{u}]
and
v=[\mathbf{v}]
be pomsets over
X
.
We write
u\parallel v
for the
parallel composition
of
u
and
v
, which is the pomset over
X
represented by the labelled poset

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\parallel\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\left\langle S_{\mathbf{u}}\cup S_{\mathbf{v}},\;\;{\leq_{\mathbf{u}}}\cup{\leq_{\mathbf{v}}},\;\;\lambda_{\mathbf{u}}\cup\lambda_{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle

Similarly, we write
u\cdot v
for the
sequential composition
of
u
and
v
, that is, the pomset represented by the labelled poset

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\left\langle S_{\mathbf{u}}\cup S_{\mathbf{v}},\;\;{\leq_{\mathbf{u}}}\cup{\leq_{\mathbf{v}}}\cup S_{\mathbf{u}}\times S_{\mathbf{v}},\;\;\lambda_{\mathbf{u}}\cup\lambda_{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle

We may elide the dot for sequential composition, for instance writing
\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
for
\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{b}
.

The pomsets we use can be built using sequential and parallel composition.

Definition 3
(Series-parallel pomsets)

The set of
series-parallel pomsets (sp-pomsets)
over
X
, denoted
\mathsf{SP}(X)
, is the smallest set such that
1\in\mathsf{SP}(X)
and
\mathtt{a}\in\mathsf{SP}(X)
for every
\mathtt{a}\in X
, closed under parallel and sequential composition.

Concurrent systems admit executions of operations that are not only ordered in sequence but also allow parallel branches.
An algebraic structure consisting of both a sequential and a parallel composition operation, with a shared unit, is called a
bimonoid
.
Formally, its definition is as follows.

Definition 4
(Bimonoid)

A
bimonoid
is a tuple
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1}\right\rangle
where

•

M
is a set called the
carrier
of the bimonoid,

•

\odot
is a binary associative operation on
M
,

•

\obar
is a binary associative and commutative operation on
M
, and

•

\mathbf{1}\in M
is a unit for both
\odot
(on both sides) and
\obar
.

Bimonoid homomorphisms are defined in the usual way.

Given a set
X
, the
free bimonoid

[
12
]
over
X
is
\left\langle\mathsf{SP}(X),\cdot,\parallel,1\right\rangle
.
The fact that it is free means that for every function
f\colon X\to M
for a given bimonoid
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1}_{M}\right\rangle
there exists a unique bimonoid homomorphism
{f}^{\sharp}\colon\mathsf{SP}(X)\to M
such that the restriction of
{f}^{\sharp}
to
X
is
f
.

Just as monoids can recognise words, bimonoids can recognise pomsets
[
27
]
.
A bimonoid together with the witnesses of recognition is a
pomset recogniser
.

Definition 5
(Pomset recogniser)

A
pomset recogniser
is a tuple
\mathcal{R}=\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1},i,F\right\rangle
where
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1}\right\rangle
is a bimonoid,
i\colon\Sigma\to M
, and
F\subseteq M
.
The
language recognised
by
\mathcal{R}
is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}=\{u\in\mathsf{SP}:{i}^{\sharp}(u)\in F\}\subseteq\mathsf{SP}.

Example 1

Suppose a program consists of a loop, where each iteration runs actions
\mathtt{a}
and
\mathtt{b}
in parallel.
We can describe the behaviour of this program by

\mathcal{L}={\{\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b}\}}^{*}=\{1,\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b},(\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b})\cdot(\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b}),\ldots\}

We can describe this language using a pomset recogniser, as follows.
Let
M=\{q_{\mathtt{a}},q_{\mathtt{b}},q_{1},q_{\bot},\mathbf{1}\}
, and let
\odot
and
\obar
be the operations on
M
given by

q\odot q^{\prime}=\begin{cases}q&q^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}\\
q^{\prime}&q=\mathbf{1}\\
q_{1}&q=q^{\prime}=q_{1}\\
q_{\bot}&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}q\obar q^{\prime}=\begin{cases}q&q^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}\\
q^{\prime}&q=\mathbf{1}\\
q_{1}&\{q,q^{\prime}\}=\{q_{\mathtt{a}},q_{\mathtt{b}}\}\\
q_{\bot}&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

A straightforward proof verifies that
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1}\right\rangle
is a bimonoid.

We set
i(\mathtt{a})=q_{\mathtt{a}}
,
i(\mathtt{b})=q_{\mathtt{b}}
, and
F=\{\mathbf{1},q_{1}\}
.
Now, for
n>0
:

\displaystyle i^{\sharp}(\underbrace{(\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b})\cdots(\mathtt{a}\parallel\mathtt{b})}_{\text{$n$ times}})=\underbrace{(i(\mathtt{a})\parallel i(\mathtt{b}))\odot\cdots\odot(i(\mathtt{a})\parallel i(\mathtt{b}))}_{\text{$n$ times}}=\underbrace{q_{1}\odot\cdots\odot q_{1}}_{\text{$n$ times}}=q_{1}

No other pomsets are mapped to
q_{1}
; hence,
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1},i,F\right\rangle
accepts
\mathcal{L}
.

Example 2

Suppose a program solves a problem recursively, such that the recursive calls are performed in parallel.
In that case, the program would either perform the base action
\mathtt{b}
, or some preprocessing action
\mathtt{a}
followed by running two copies of itself in parallel.
This behaviour can be described by the smallest pomset language
\mathcal{L}
satisfying the following inference rules:

\frac{~}{\mathtt{b}\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{u,v\in\mathcal{L}}{\mathtt{a}\cdot(u\parallel v)\in\mathcal{L}}

This language can be described by a pomset recogniser.
Let our carrier set be
M=\{q_{\mathtt{a}},q_{\mathtt{b}},q_{1},q_{\bot},\mathbf{1}\}
, and let
\odot
and
\obar
be the operations on
M
given by

q\odot q^{\prime}=\begin{cases}q&q^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}\\
q^{\prime}&q=\mathbf{1}\\
q_{\mathtt{b}}&q=q_{\mathtt{a}},q^{\prime}=q_{1}\\
q_{\bot}&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}q\obar q^{\prime}=\begin{cases}q&q^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}\\
q^{\prime}&q=\mathbf{1}\\
q_{1}&q=q^{\prime}=q_{\mathtt{b}}\\
q_{\bot}&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1}\right\rangle
is a bimonoid,
F=\{q_{\mathtt{b}}\}
, and
i\colon\Sigma\to M
is given by setting
i(\mathtt{a})=q_{\mathtt{a}}
and
i(\mathtt{b})=q_{\mathtt{b}}
.
One can then show that
\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1},i,F\right\rangle
accepts
\mathcal{L}
.

Pomset contexts
are used to describe the behaviour of individual elements in a pomset recogniser.
Formally, the set of pomset contexts over a set
X
is given by
\mathsf{PC}(X)=\mathsf{SP}(X\cup\{\square\})
.
Here the element
\square
acts as a placeholder, where a pomset can be plugged in: given a context
c\in\mathsf{PC}(X)
and
t\in\mathsf{SP}(X)
, let
c[t]\in\mathsf{SP}(X)
be obtained by substituting
t
for
\square
in
c
.

3
Learning Pomset Recognisers

In this section we present our algorithm to learn pomset recognisers from an oracle (
the teacher
) that answers
membership
and
equivalence queries
.
A membership query consists of a pomset, to which the teacher replies whether that pomset is in the language; an equivalence query consists of a
hypothesis
pomset recogniser, to which the teacher replies
yes
if it is correct or
no
with a counterexample—a pomset incorrectly classified by the hypothesis—if it is not.

A pomset recogniser is essentially a tree automaton, with the additional constraint that its algebraic structure satisfies the bimonoid axioms.
Our algorithm is therefore relatively close to tree automata learning—in particular Drewes and Högberg
[
7
,
8
]
—but there are several key differences: we optimise the algorithm by taking advantage of the bimonoid axioms, and at the same time need to ensure that the hypotheses generated by the learning process satisfy those axioms.

3.1
Observation Table

We fix a target language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathsf{SP}
throughout this section.
As in the original
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
algorithm, the state of the learner throughout a run of the algorithm is given by a data structure called the
observation table
, which collects information about
\mathcal{L}
.
The table contains rows indexed by pomsets, representing the state reached by the correct pomset recogniser after reading that pomset; and columns indexed by pomset contexts, used to approximately indentify the behaviour of each state.
To represent the additional rows needed to approximate the pomset recogniser structure, we use the following definition.
Given
U\subseteq\mathsf{SP}
, we define

{U}^{+}=\Sigma\cup\{u\cdot v:u,v\in U\}\cup\{u\parallel v:u,v\in U\}\subseteq\mathsf{SP}.

Definition 6
(Observation table)

An
observation table
is a pair
\left\langle S,E\right\rangle
, with
S\subseteq\mathsf{SP}
subpomset-closed and
E\subseteq\mathsf{PC}
such that
1\in S
and
\square\in E
.
These sets induce the function
\mathsf{row}_{\left\langle S,E\right\rangle}\colon S\cup{S}^{+}\to 2^{E}
:

\mathsf{row}_{\left\langle S,E\right\rangle}(s)(e)=1\iff e[s]\in\mathcal{L}.

We often write
\mathsf{row}
instead of
\mathsf{row}_{\left\langle S,E\right\rangle}
when
S
and
E
are clear from the context.

We depict observation tables, or more precisely
\mathsf{row}
, as two separate tables with rows in
S
and
{S}^{+}\setminus S
respectively, see for instance
Example

3
below.

The goal of the learner is to extract a
hypothesis
pomset recogniser from the rows in the table.
More specifically, the carrier of the underlying bimonoid of the hypothesis will be given by the rows indexed by pomsets in
S
.
The structure on the rows is obtained by transferring the structure of the row labels onto the rows (e.g.,
\mathsf{row}(s)\odot\mathsf{row}(t)=\mathsf{row}(s\cdot t)
), but this is not well-defined unless the table satisfies
closedness
,
consistency
, and
associativity
.
Closedness and consistency are standard in
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
, whereas associativity is a new property specific to bimonoid learning.
We discuss each of these properties next, also including
compatibility
, a property that is used to show minimality of
hypotheses.

The first potential issue is a closedness defect: this is the case when a composed row, indexed by an element of
{S}^{+}
, is not indexed by a pomset in
S
.

Definition 8

Conversion to Pomset Automata

Bimonoids are a useful representation of pomset languages because sequential and parallel composition are on an equal footing; in the case of the learning algorithm of the previous section, this helps us treat both operations similarly.
On the other hand, the behaviour of a program is usually thought of as a series of actions, some of which involve launching two or more threads that later combine.
Here, sequential actions form the basic unit of computation, while fork/join patterns of threads are specified separately.

Pomset automata

[
21

encode this more asymmetric model: they can be thought of as non-deterministic finite automata with an additional transition type that brokers forking and joining threads.

In this section, we show how to convert a pomset recogniser to a certain type of pomset automaton, where acceptance of a pomset is guided by its structure; conversely, we show that each of the pomset automata in this class can be represented by a pomset recogniser.
Together with the previous section, this establishes that the languages of pomset automata in this class are learnable.

The language accepted by this PA is the singleton set
\{\mathtt{a}\cdot(\mathtt{b}\parallel\mathtt{c})\cdot\mathtt{a}\}
.

In general, finite pomset automata can accept a very wide range of pomset languages, including all context free (pomset) languages
[

]

It follows that PAs can represent strictly more pomset languages than pomset recognisers.
To tame the expressive power of PAs at least slightly, we propose the following.

We have thus shown that the language of any pomset recogniser can be accepted by a finite and saturated PA.
In turn, this shows that our algorithm can, in principle, be adapted to work with a teacher that takes a (saturated) PA instead of a pomset recogniser as hypothesis, by simply converting the hypothesis pomset recogniser to an equivalent PA before sending it over.

Conversely, we can show that the transition relations of a saturated PA carry the algebraic structure of a bimonoid, and use that to show that a language recognised by a saturated PA is also recognised by a bimonoid.
This shows that our characterisation is “tight”, i.e., languages recognised by saturated PAs are precisely those recognised by bimonoids, and hence learnable.

5
Discussion

To learn DFAs, there are several alternatives to the observation table data structure that reduce the space complexity of the algorithm.
Most notable is the
classification tree

[
24
]
, which distinguishes individual pairs of words (which for us would be pomsets) at every node rather than filling an entire row for each of them.
The TTT algorithm
[
18
]
further builds on this and achieves optimal space complexity.
Given that we developed the first learning algorithm for pomset languages, we opted for the simplicity of the observation table—optimisations such as those analogous to the aforementioned work are left to future research.

We would like to extend our algorithm to learn recognisers based on arbitrary algebraic theories.
One challenge is to ensure that the equations of the theory hold for hypotheses, by generalising our definition of associativity (
Definition

9
).

Our algorithm can also be specialised to learn languages recognised by commutative monoids.
These languages of
multisets
can alternatively be represented as semi-linear sets
[
29
]
or described using Presburger arithmetic
[
11
]
.
While not all languages described this way are recognisable (for instance, the set of multisets over
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}
with as many
\mathtt{a}
’s as
\mathtt{b}
’s
[
27
]
), it would be interesting to be able to learn at least the fragment representable by commutative monoids, and apply that to one of the domains where semi-linear sets are used.

Our algorithm is limited to learning languages of series-parallel pomsets; there exist pomsets which are not series-parallel, each of which must contain an “N-shape”
[
12
,
13
,
34
]
.
Since N-shapes appear in pomsets that describe message passing between threads, we would like to be able to learn such languages as well.
We do not see an obvious way to extend our algorithm to include these pomsets, but perhaps recent techniques from
[
10
]
can provide a solution.

Every hypothesis of our algorithm can be converted to a pomset automaton.
The final pomset recogniser for a bounded-width language is minimal, and hence depth-nilpotent
[
27
]
, which means that it can be converted to a fork-acyclic PA.
In future work, we would like to guarantee that the same holds for intermediate hypotheses when learning a bounded-width language.

Running two threads in parallel may be implemented by running some initial section of those threads in parallel, followed by running the remainder of those threads in parallel.
This interleaving is represented by the
exchange law

[
12
,
13
]
.
One can specialise pomset recognisers to include this interleaving to obtain recognisers of pomset languages closed under subsumption
[
27
]
, i.e., such that if a pomset
u
is recognised, then so are all of the “more sequential” versions of
u
.
We would like to adapt our algorithm to learn these types of recognisers, and exploit the extra structure provided by the exchange law to optimise further.

We have shown that recognisable pomset languages correspond to saturated regular pomset languages (
Lemmas

8
and
9
).
One question that remains is whether there is an algorithm that can learn all or at least a larger class of regular pomset languages.
Given that pomset automata can accept context-free languages (
Figure

1(b)
), we wonder if a suitable notion of context-free grammars for pomset languages could be identified.
Clark
[
6
]
showed that there exists a subclass of context-free languages that can be learned via an adaptation of
\mathtt{L}^{\!\star}
.
Arguably, this adaptation learns recognisers with a monoidal structure and reverses this structure to obtain a grammar.
An extension of this work to pomset languages might lead to a learning algorithm that learns more PAs.

q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{1}$}}_{A}q=q^{\prime}
immediately.
On the other hand, if
u=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma

i(\mathtt{a})\odot q^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q^{\prime}=q
, and hence
q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathtt{a})
.
Therefore,
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{\mathtt{a}}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.

For the inductive step, there are two cases to consider.

•

Suppose that
u=v\cdot w
for
v,w\neq 1
.
Let
q^{\prime\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(w)\odot q^{\prime}
.
We then find by induction that
q^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, note that
{i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot q^{\prime\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot({i}^{\sharp}(w)\odot q^{\prime})=({i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot{i}^{\sharp}(w))\odot q^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q^{\prime}=q
.
Hence, we find by induction that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime\prime}
.
Putting this together, it follows that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.

•

If
u=v\parallel w
for
v,w\neq 1
, then choose
r={i}^{\sharp}(v)
and
r^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(w)
, and note that
{i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot\mathbf{1}=r
and
{i}^{\sharp}(w)\odot\mathbf{1}=r^{\prime}
.
Hence, we find by induction that
r\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
and
r^{\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
.
Since also
(r\obar r^{\prime})\odot q^{\prime}=({i}^{\sharp}(v)\obar{i}^{\sharp}(w))\odot q^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q^{\prime}=q
, it follows that
q^{\prime}\in\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,r^{\prime}|\mskip-4.0mu\})
.
In total,
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
∎

Continuing the proof of
Lemma

8
, to see that
A
is saturated, let
u,v\in\mathsf{SP}
with
u,v\neq 1
.
First, suppose
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
By the above property,
{i}^{\sharp}(u\cdot v)\odot q^{\prime}=q
.
If we now choose
q^{\prime\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot q^{\prime}\in M
, we find that
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q^{\prime\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot{i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot q^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(u\cdot v)\odot q^{\prime}
.
By the same property we find that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.

Next, suppose that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
By the property above, we have
({i}^{\sharp}(u)\obar{i}^{\sharp}(v))\odot q^{\prime}=q
.
If we choose
r={i}^{\sharp}(u)
and
s={i}^{\sharp}(v)
as well as
r^{\prime}=s^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}
, then
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot r^{\prime}=r
and
{i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot s^{\prime}=s
, and thus
r\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}r^{\prime}
and
s\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}s^{\prime}
again by the same property.
Furthermore, since
(r\obar s)\odot q^{\prime}=q
, we have
q^{\prime}\in\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,s|\mskip-4.0mu\})
.

Note that
A
is finite.
To see that
A
accepts
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}
, note that
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}
precisely when
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\in F
, which holds if and only if there exists a
q\in F
with
{i}^{\sharp}(u)=q
, which, by the above, is equivalent to
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}\mathbf{1}
for some
q\in F
, i.e.,
u\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
.
∎

Let
A=\left\langle Q,I,F,\delta,\gamma\right\rangle
be a saturated pomset automaton.
We can construct a pomset recogniser
\mathcal{R}=\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1},i,F^{\prime}\right\rangle
, where

M=\{{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}:u\in\mathsf{SP}\}{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\odot{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\obar{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}}i(\mathtt{a})={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{\mathtt{a}}$}}_{A}}F^{\prime}=\{{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\in M:\exists q\in I,q^{\prime}\in F.\ q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}\}

Now
\odot
and
\obar
are well-defined, and
\mathcal{R}
is a pomset recogniser such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

Proof

Without loss of generality, we can assume that for
u\in\mathsf{SP}
, we have
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{1}$}}_{A}}
if and only if
u=1
: the implication from right to left is obvious, and the converse can be guaranteed (while preserving saturation of
A
as well as its language) by adding a non-initial and non-accepting state without transitions.

Let
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathsf{SP}
such that
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
and
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
.
To prove that the operations are well-defined, we should show
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\cdot v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
and
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\parallel v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
.
For the former equality, we consider two cases.

•

If
u=1
, then
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{1}$}}_{A}}
, and thus
u^{\prime}=1
.
In that case

{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\cdot v^{\prime}}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}

A similar derivation applies if any of the other pomsets are empty.

•

If
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\neq 1
, suppose
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
Because
A
is saturated, we find a
q^{\prime\prime}\in Q
such that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
Since
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
and
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
, it follows that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}q^{\prime\prime}
and
q^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
, and hence
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\cdot v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
This shows that
\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\cdot v}$}}_{A}
is contained in
\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\cdot v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}
; the converse can be shown symmetrically.

Next, we show that
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\parallel v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
; again, we have two cases to consider.

•

If
u=1
, then
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{1}$}}_{A}}
, and thus
u^{\prime}=1
by the above.
Thus,

{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\parallel v^{\prime}}$}}_{A^{\prime}}}

A similar derivation applies if any of the other pomsets are empty.

•

If
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\neq 1
, suppose that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
In that case, since
A
is saturated, we find
r,s\in Q
and
r^{\prime},s^{\prime}\in F
such that
r\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}r^{\prime}
and
s\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}s^{\prime}
, and
q^{\prime}\in\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,s|\mskip-4.0mu\})
.
Since
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
and
{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}_{A}}
, it then follows that
r\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}}$}}_{A}r^{\prime}
and
s\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v^{\prime}}$}}s^{\prime}
, and thus
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\parallel v^{\prime}}$}}q^{\prime}
.
This shows that
\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel v}$}}_{A}
is contained in
\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u^{\prime}\parallel v^{\prime}}$}}
; the converse inclusion follows by a similar argument.

The bimonoid laws can now be proved straightforwardly; for instance,
\obar
is associative because for
u,v,w\in\mathsf{SP}
we have

{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\obar({\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}}\obar{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}})=\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u\parallel(v\parallel w)}$}}_{A}=\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{(u\parallel v)\parallel w}$}}_{A}=({\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\obar{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}})\obar{\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}}

Associativity of
\odot
and the fact that
\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{1}$}}_{A}
is a unit can be shown similarly.
This makes
\mathcal{R}
a proper pomset recogniser.

It should be clear that for
u\in\mathsf{SP}
we have
{i}^{\sharp}(u)={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}
.
From this, it follows that
u\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
if and only if there exist
q\in I
and
q^{\prime}\in F
such that
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
, which holds precisely when
{i}^{\sharp}(u)={\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}}\in F^{\prime}
, and thus
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
∎

Appendix 0.C
Translation to fork-acyclic pomset automata

Bimonoids recognisers can, in general, encode pomset languages of
unbounded width
, i.e., without an upper bound on the antichains in the pomsets, and even pomset languages of
unbounded depth
, with a complex mutual nesting structure between sequential and parallel composition.
By
Lemma

8
, even these can be accepted by a pomset automaton.
Typical programs, however, have a limited number of parallel threads, and tend not to launch threads recursively.
To further restrict PAs so as to exclude this kind of behaviour, we can use the notion of fork-acyclicity
[
27
]
; for pomset automata, this comes down to the following
[
21
]
:

Definition 18
(Fork-acyclicity)

Let
A=\left\langle Q,I,F,\delta,\gamma\right\rangle
be a PA.
We define the
support relation
of
A
, denoted
\preceq_{A}
, as the smallest preorder on
Q
satisfying

\frac{\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\quad q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathtt{a})}{q^{\prime}\preceq_{A}q}\frac{\phi\in\mathbb{M}(Q)\quad q^{\prime}\in\gamma(q,\phi)}{q^{\prime}\preceq_{A}q}\frac{\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,\ldots|\mskip-4.0mu\})}{r\preceq_{A}q}

We say that
A
is
fork-acyclic
if for all
q,r\in Q
and
\phi\in\mathbb{M}(Q)
with
r\in\phi
and
\gamma(q,\phi)\neq\emptyset
it holds that
q\not\preceq_{A}r
.

Example 11

Recall the PAs drawn in
Figure

.
If
A
is the PA in

, then
A
is fork-acyclic; after all, the only fork is given by
q_{2}\in\gamma(q_{1},\{\mskip-4.0mu|q_{3},q_{4}|\mskip-4.0mu\})
, and we have that
q_{3},q_{4}\prec_{A}q_{1}
—in other words, the runs starting at
q_{3}
or
q_{4}
do not depend on
q_{1}
.
On the other hand, if
A
is the PA in
Figure

, then
A
is not fork-acyclic, since
\gamma(q_{1},\{\mskip-4.0mu|q_{3},q_{4}|\mskip-4.0mu\})\neq\emptyset
but
q_{1}\preceq_{A}q_{3}
, since
q_{1}\in\delta(q_{3},\mathtt{a})

Intuitively, if
q
and
q^{\prime}
are states such that
q^{\prime}\preceq_{A}q
, then the pomsets that can be read starting in
q
somehow depend on those that can be read from
q^{\prime}
.
A pomset automaton is fork-acyclic if it can only fork into states whose language does not depend on the point of origin for the fork.
For example, the PA in

\gamma(q_{1},\{\mskip-4.0mu|q_{3},q_{4}|\mskip-4.0mu\})\neq\emptyset
while
q_{1}\preceq_{A}q_{3}
.

The corresponding restriction on pomset recognisers is
depth-nilpotency

[
27
]
, which we adapt for our purposes as follows.

Definition 19
(Depth-nilpotency)

Let
\mathcal{R}=\left\langle M,\odot,\obar,\mathbf{1},i,F\right\rangle
be a pomset recogniser.
We define
\prec_{\mathcal{R}}
as the smallest transitive relation on
M
satisfying the following rule for all
s,u,v,w,t,x,y\in M
:

\frac{s=u\odot(v\obar(w\odot t\odot x))\odot y\quad v\obar(w\odot t\odot x)\neq w\odot t\odot x}{s\prec_{\mathcal{R}}t}

We say that
\mathcal{R}
is
depth-nilpotent
if the following hold:

(i)

there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that every
\prec_{\mathcal{R}}
-chain is of length at most
N
, that is, for all
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\in M
with
q_{1}\prec_{\mathcal{R}}\cdots\prec_{\mathcal{R}}q_{n}
, it holds that
n\leq N
.

(ii)

there exists a
\mathbf{0}\in M\setminus F
such that
s\parallel\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}
for all
s\in M
.

(iii)

if
s,t\in M
and
s\obar t=t
, then either
s=\mathbf{1}
or
t=\mathbf{0}
.

(iv)

for all
u\in\mathsf{SP}
, we have
{i}^{\sharp}(u)=\mathbf{1}
if and only if
u=1
.

When
\mathcal{R}
is depth-nilpotent, we write
D_{\mathcal{R}}(s)
for the length of the longest
\prec_{\mathcal{R}}
-chain starting at
s\in M
, i.e., the maximal
n
such that there exist
s=s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in M
with
s_{1}\prec_{\mathcal{R}}\cdots\prec_{\mathcal{R}}s_{n}
.

Proof
(of
Lemma

10
)

Our proof rests on the following two properties of
A
.

Claim

If
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q^{\prime}=q
and
q\neq\mathbf{0}
, then
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.

Proof

We proceed by induction on
u
.
In the base, we have two cases.
On the one hand, if
u=1
, then
{i}^{\sharp}(u)=\mathbf{1}
, and so the claim holds immediately.
On the other hand, if
u=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathtt{a})
, whence
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
again.

For the inductive step, there are two cases.

•

If
u=v\cdot w
with
v,w\neq 1
, then choose
q^{\prime\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(w)\odot q^{\prime}
.
Now
q^{\prime\prime}\neq\mathbf{0}
, otherwise
q={i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot q^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{0}
.
By induction, we then find that
q^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
, as well as
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}q^{\prime\prime}
.
In total, we have
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.

•

If
u=v\parallel w
with
v,w\neq 1
, then choose
r={i}^{\sharp}(v)
and
s={i}^{\sharp}(w)
.
Now
r,s\neq\mathbf{0}
, otherwise
q={i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot q=({i}^{\sharp}(v)\obar{i}^{\sharp}(w))\odot q^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
.
Furthermore,
r,s\neq\mathbf{1}
, otherwise
v=1
or
w=1
.
Together, this means that
r\obar s\neq r
and
r\obar s\neq s
, by depth-nilpotency of
M
.
Since
{i}^{\sharp}(v)\odot\mathbf{1}=r
and
{i}^{\sharp}(w)\odot\mathbf{1}=s
, we find by induction that
r\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{v}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
and
s\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{w}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
.
Since
q=(r\obar s)\odot q^{\prime}
, it follows that
q\prec_{\mathcal{R}}r,s
, and hence
D_{\mathcal{R}}(r)<D_{\mathcal{R}}(q)
as well as
D_{\mathcal{R}}(s)<D_{\mathcal{R}}(q)
.
We then know that
q^{\prime}\in\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,s|\mskip-4.0mu\})
, and thus
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}q^{\prime}
.
∎

Claim

If
q\preceq_{A}q^{\prime}
, then
D_{\mathcal{R}}(q^{\prime})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}(q)
.

Proof

It suffices to validate the claim for the pairs that generate
\preceq_{A}
.

•

If
q^{\prime}\preceq_{A}q
because
q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathtt{a})
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
{i}^{\sharp}(\mathtt{a})\odot q^{\prime}=q
.
Now suppose that
q^{\prime}\prec_{\mathcal{R}}q^{\prime\prime}
for some
q^{\prime\prime}\in M
; we then obtain
u,v,w,x,y\in M
such that
q^{\prime}=u\odot(v\obar(w\odot q^{\prime\prime}\odot x))\odot y
, and
v\obar(w\odot q^{\prime\prime}\odot x)\neq w\odot q^{\prime\prime}\odot x
.
Clearly,
q={i}^{\sharp}(\mathtt{a})\cdot q^{\prime}={i}^{\sharp}(\mathtt{a})\odot u\odot(v\obar(w\odot q^{\prime\prime}\odot x))\odot y
, and hence
q\prec_{\mathcal{R}}q^{\prime\prime}
.
It follows that every chain starting at
q^{\prime}
can be turned into one starting at
q
, hence
D_{\mathcal{R}}(q^{\prime})\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}(q)
.

If

We return to the proof of
Lemma

10
.
If
u\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, then there exists a
q\in F
with
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
.
By the same argument as in
Lemma

8
,
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\odot\mathbf{1}=q
—after all, the transitions of the new automaton are also transitions of the old automaton.
Hence
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\in F
, meaning
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
Conversely, if
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}
, then
{i}^{\sharp}(u)\neq\mathbf{0}
by depth-nilpotency, and by the first claim above we have
q\mathrel{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\xrightarrow{u}$}}_{A}\mathbf{1}
, meaning
u\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

For fork-acyclicity, let
q,r,s\in M
be such that
\gamma(q,\{\mskip-4.0mu|r,s|\mskip-4.0mu\})\neq\emptyset
.
In that case,
D_{\mathcal{R}}(r)<D_{\mathcal{R}}(q)
by definition; thus
q\preceq_{A}r
cannot hold, because that would imply
D_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}(r)
by the second claim above.
∎

The converse also exists in the literature: the language of a fork-acyclic PA can also be recognised by a depth-nilpotent pomset recogniser, because any fork-acyclic automaton can be converted to an equivalent sr-expression

A direct construction is also possible, if we use the techniques from
