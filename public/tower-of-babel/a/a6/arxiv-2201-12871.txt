Abstract
. We investigate the phase diagram of the complex cubic unitary ensemble of random matrices with the potential V ⁡ ( M ) = − 1 3 ​ M 3 + t ​ M V(M)=-\frac{1}{3}M^{3}+tM where t t is a complex parameter. As proven in our previous paper [ 7 ] , the whole phase space of the model, t ∈ ℂ t\in\mathbb{C} , is partitioned into two phase regions, O 𝗈𝗇𝖾 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{one-cut}} and O 𝗍𝗐𝗈 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{two-cut}} , such that in O 𝗈𝗇𝖾 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{one-cut}} the equilibrium measure is supported by one Jordan arc (cut) and in O 𝗍𝗐𝗈 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{two-cut}} by two cuts. The regions O 𝗈𝗇𝖾 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{one-cut}} and O 𝗍𝗐𝗈 − 𝖼𝗎𝗍 O_{\mathsf{two-cut}} are separated by critical curves, which can be calculated in terms of critical trajectories of an auxiliary quadratic differential. In [ 7 ] the one-cut phase region was investigated in detail. In the present paper we investigate the two-cut region. We prove that in the two-cut region the endpoints of the cuts are analytic functions of the real and imaginary parts of the parameter t t , but not of the parameter t t itself (so that the Cauchy–Riemann equations are violated for the endpoints). We also obtain the semiclassical asymptotics of the orthogonal polynomials associated with the ensemble of random matrices and their recurrence coefficients. The proofs are based on the Riemann–Hilbert approach to semiclassical asymptotics of the orthogonal polynomials and the theory of S S -curves and quadratic differentials.

As shown in

\Xi_{N}(u)=\int_{\Gamma}\ldots\int_{\Gamma}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k})^{2}\,\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{-N\left(\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{j}^{2}-u\zeta_{j}^{3}\right)}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}\zeta_{N}.

We would like to consider all the possible values, including the complex ones, of the parameter

As proven in

The idea of using the orthogonal polynomials in ensembles of random matrices goes back to the classical works of Dyson and Mehta, see the works

\left\{\begin{aligned} \gamma_{n}^{2}&\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2k}}g_{2k}\left(\frac{n}{N},u\right),\\

where the functions

In

\left\{\begin{aligned} \gamma_{n}^{2}&\sim\gamma^{2}_{c}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2k/5}}\,{p}_{2k}(v),\\

where the functions

are expressed in terms of the Boutroux tritronquée solution to Painlevé I equation,

. As shown in

As we have stressed before, the topological expansions from

\int_{\Gamma}z^{k}P_{n}(z;t,N)e^{-NV(z;t)}\mathrm{d}z=0,\quad k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\},

where

(1.11)

V(z;t):=-\dfrac{z^{3}}{3}+tz,\quad t\in\mathbb{C}.

Due to the non-Hermitian character of the above relations, it might happen that polynomial satisfying (
1.10
) is non-unique. In this case we understand by

the monic non-identically zero polynomial of the smallest degree (such a polynomial is always unique). One way of connecting the partition function to orthogonal polynomials is via

. More precisely, let

(1.12)

h_{n}(t,N):=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}P_{n}^{2}(z;t,N)e^{-NV(z;t)}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{D_{n}(t,N)}{D_{n-1}(t,N)},

where
D_{-1}(t,N)\equiv 1
and
D_{n}(t,N):=\mathrm{det}\big[\int_{\Gamma}z^{i+j}e^{-NV(z;t)}\mathrm{d}z\big]_{i,j=0}^{n}
is the Hankel determinant of the moments of the measure of integration in (
1.10

D_{n}(t,N)
is an entire function of

and therefore each
h_{n}(t,N)
is meromorphic in
\mathbb{C}
. Hence, given

, the set of the values

for which there exists

such that
h_{k}(t,N)=0
is countable with no limit points in the finite plane. Outside of this set, the standard argument using orthogonality (
1.10
) shows that

(1.13)

zP_{n}(z;t,N)=P_{n+1}(z;t,N)+\beta_{n}(t,N)P_{n}(z;t,N)+\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)P_{n-1}(z;t,N),

and by analytic continuation (
1.13
) extends to those values of

for which
D_{n-1}(t,N)D_{n}(t,N)\neq 0
(that is,

-th polynomials appearing (
1.13

(1.14)

\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)=h_{n}(t,N)/h_{n-1}(t,N).

Notice that (
1.13
) remains meaningful even if
D_{n-2}(t,N)=0
. In this case
h_{n-2}(t,N)=0
,
h_{n-1}(t,N)=\infty
, and
h_{n}(t,N)
is finite, which means that
P_{n-2}(z;t,N)
is orthogonal to itself and therefore is equal to
P_{n-1}(z;t,N)
but not to
P_{n}(z;t,N)
. Thus,
\gamma_{n}^{2}(t,N)=0
and the last term in (
1.13
) is zero. Notice also that if

(that is,

and

), then we simply have that

by analytic continuation. However, this means that the polynomial
P_{n}(z;t,N)
is orthogonal to itself and therefore
P_{n+1}(z;t,N)=P_{n}(z;t,N)
. Then the analytic continuation argument necessitates that
\beta_{n}(t,N)=\infty
, which also can be seen from the determinantal representation of

as it is the difference of the subleading coefficients of

and

. It is further known that the recurrence coefficients

satisfy the

\frac{\partial^{2}F_{N}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=\gamma_{N}^{2}(t,N),\qquad F_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log Z_{N}(t).

Z_{N}(t)=N!\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}h_{n}(t,N)\big(=N!D_{N-1}(t,N)\big).

2.
Equilibrium Measures and S-Property

It is well understood that the zeros of polynomials satisfying (
1.10
) asymptotically distribute as a certain
weighted equilibrium measure
on an
S-contour
corresponding to the weight function (
1.11
). In this section we discuss these notions in greater detail. Our consideration will use the works of Huybrechs, Kuijlaars, and Lejon
[
25
]
and Kuijlaars and Silva
[
29
]
. Let us start with some
definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
V
be an entire function. The logarithmic energy in the external field
\mathrm{Re}V
of a measure
\nu
in the complex plane
is defined as

E_{V}(\nu)=\iint\log\frac{1}{|s-t|}\mathrm{d}\nu(s)\mathrm{d}\nu(t)+\int\mathrm{Re}V(s)\mathrm{d}\nu(s).

The equilibrium energy of a contour
\Gamma
in the external field
\mathrm{Re}V
is equal to

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}_{V}(\Gamma)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)}E_{V}(\nu),

where
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
denotes the space of Borel probability measures on
\Gamma
.

When
\mathrm{Re}V(s)-\log|s|\to+\infty
as
\Gamma\ni s\to\infty
, there exists a unique minimizing measure for (
2.1
), which is called the
weighted equilibrium measure
of
\Gamma
in the external field
\mathrm{Re}V
, say
\mu_{\Gamma}
, see
[
33
, Theorem I.1.3]
or
[
25
]
. The support of
\mu_{\Gamma}
, say
J_{\Gamma}
, is a compact subset of
\Gamma
. The equilibrium measure
\mu=\mu_{\Gamma}
is characterized by the Euler–Lagrange variational conditions:

(2.2)

2U^{\mu}(z)+\mathrm{Re}V(z)\;\left\{\begin{aligned} &=\ell,\qquad z\in J_{\Gamma},\\
&\geq\ell,\qquad z\in\Gamma\setminus J_{\Gamma},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\ell=\ell_{\Gamma}
is a constant, the Lagrange multiplier, and
U^{\mu}(z):=-\int\log|z-s|\mathrm{d}\mu(s)
is the logarithmic potential of
\mu
, see
[
33
, Theorem I.3.3]
.

Observe that due to the analyticity of the integrand in (
1.1
),
\Gamma
can be varied without changing the value of
Z_{N}(t)
. Henceforth, we suppose that partition function (
1.1
) and orthogonal polynomials (
1.10
) are defined with
\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the following class of contours.

Definition 2.2
.

We shall denote by
\mathcal{T}
the collection of all piecewise smooth contours that extend to infinity in both directions, each admiting a parametrization
z(s)
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, for which there exists
\epsilon\in(0,\pi/6)
and
s_{0}>0
such that

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\mathrm{arg}(z(s))-\pi/3|\leq\epsilon,&s\geq s_{0},\\
|\mathrm{arg}(z(s))-\pi|\leq\epsilon,&s\leq-s_{0},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathrm{arg}(z(s))\in[0,2\pi)
.

Despite the above flexibility, it is well understood in the theory of non-Hermitian orthogonal polynomials, starting with the works of Stahl
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
and Gonchar and Rakhmanov
[
24
]
, that one should use the contour whose equilibrium measure has support
symmetric
(with the
S-property
) in the external field
\mathrm{Re}V
. We make this idea precise in the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

The support
J_{\Gamma}
has the S-property in the external field
\mathrm{Re}V
, if it consists of a finite number of open analytic arcs and their endpoints, and on each arc it holds that

(2.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{+}}\,\big(2U^{\mu_{\Gamma}}+\mathrm{Re}V\big)=\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{-}}\,\big(2U^{\mu_{\Gamma}}+\mathrm{Re}V\big),

where
\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{+}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial n_{-}}
are the normal derivatives from the
(+)
- and
(-)
-side of
\Gamma
. We shall say that a curve
\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}
is an S-curve in the field
\mathrm{Re}V
, if
J_{\Gamma}
has the S-property in this field.

It is also understood that geometrically
J_{\Gamma}
is comprised of
critical trajectories
of a certain quadratic differential. Recall that if
Q
is a meromorphic function, a
trajectory
(resp.
orthogonal trajectory
) of a quadratic differential
-Q(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
is a maximal regular arc on which

-Q(z(s))\big(z^{\prime}(s)\big)^{2}>0\quad\big(\text{resp.}\quad-Q(z(s))\big(z^{\prime}(s)\big)^{2}<0\big)

for any local uniformizing parameter. A trajectory is called
critical
if it is incident with a
finite critical point
(a zero or a simple pole of
-Q(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
) and it is called
short
if it is incident only with finite critical points. We designate the expression
critical (orthogonal) graph of
-Q(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
for the totality of the critical (orthogonal) trajectories
-Q(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
.

The following theorem is a specialization to
V(z;t)
of
[
29
, Theorems 2.3 and 2.4]
.

Theorem 2.1

Let

5.1.
On Quadratic Differentials

To start, let us also recall the following important result, known as Teichmüller’s lemma, see
[
37
, Theorem 14.1]
. Let

be a geodesic polygon of a quadratic differential, that is, a Jordan curve in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
that consists of a finite number of trajectories and orthogonal trajectories of this differential. Then it holds that

(5.1)

\sum_{z\in P}\left(1-\theta(z)\dfrac{2+\mathrm{ord}(z)}{2\pi}\right)=2+\sum_{z\in\mathrm{int}(P)}\mathrm{ord}(z),

where
\mathrm{ord}(z)
is the order of

with respect to the considered differential and
\theta(z)\in[0,2\pi]
,
z\in P
, is the interior angle of
P
at
z
. Both sums in (
5.1
) are finite since only critical points of the differential have a non-zero contribution.

Let us briefly recall the main properties of the differential
\varpi_{t}(z)
. The only critical points of
\varpi_{t}(z)
are the zeros of
Q(z;t)
and the point at infinity. Regular points have order
0
, the order of a zero of

is equal to its multiplicity, and infinity is a critical point of order
-8
. Through each regular point passes exactly one trajectory and one orthogonal trajectory of
\varpi_{t}(z)

[
37
, Theorem 5.5]
. As
Q(z;t)
is a polynomial, no finite union of (orthogonal) trajectories can form a closed Jordan curve, while a trajectory and an orthogonal trajectory can intersect at most once
[
32
, Lemma 8.3]
. Furthermore, (orthogonal) trajectories of
\varpi_{t}(z)
cannot be recurrent (dense in two-dimensional regions)
[
26
, Theorem 3.6]
. From each critical point of order
m>0
there emanate

critical trajectories whose consecutive tangent lines at the critical point form an angle
2\pi/(m+2)
. Furthermore, since infinity is a pole of order 8, the critical trajectories can approach infinity only in six distinguished directions, namely, asymptotically to the lines
L_{-\pi/6}
,
L_{\pi/6}
, and
L_{\pi/2}
, where
L_{\theta}=\{z:~z=re^{\mathrm{i}\theta},~r\in(-\infty,\infty)\}
. In fact, there exists a neighborhood of infinity such that any trajectory entering it necessarily tends to infinity
[
37
, Theorem 7.4]
. This discussion also applies to orthogonal trajectories. In particular, they can approach infinity asymptotically to the lines
L_{0}
,
L_{\pi/3}
, and
L_{2\pi/3}
only.

Denote by
\mathcal{G}
the critical graph of
\varpi_{t}(z)
, that is, the totality of all the critical trajectories of
\varpi_{t}(z)
. Then, see
[

, Theorem 3.5]
, the complement of

can be written as a disjoint union of either half-plane or strip domains. Recall that a

is swept by trajectories unbounded in both directions that approach infinity along consecutive critical directions. Its boundary is connected and consists of a union of two unbounded critical trajectories and a finite number (possibly zero) of short trajectories of
\varpi_{t}(z)

maps end domains conformally onto half planes

is again swept by trajectories unbounded in both directions, but its boundary consists of two disjoint
\varpi_{t}(z)
-paths, each of which is comprised of two unbounded critical trajectories and a finite number (possibly zero) of short trajectories. The map

maps strip domains conformally onto vertical strips
\{w\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ c_{1}<\mathrm{Re}(w)<c_{2}\}

(5.2)

\left|\mathrm{Re}\int_{p}^{q}\sqrt{-\varpi_{t}}\right|

It is also known that

vanishes only at the points of the lattice

. Let

\Theta_{n,k}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\exp\left\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\big(m_{n,k}+\tau n\big)\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})\right\}\frac{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n,k})-\frac{\mathsf{B}+1}{2}\right)}{\theta\left(\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})-\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\big(p^{(k)}\big)-\frac{\mathsf{B}+1}{2}\right)}.

\Theta_{n}(z;t):=\Theta_{n,1}^{(0)}(z),\;\;\vartheta_{n}(z):=\frac{\Theta_{n,1}^{(0)}(z)}{\Theta_{n,1}^{(0)}(\infty)},\;\;\vartheta_{n}^{*}(z):=\frac{\Theta_{n,1}^{(1)}(z)}{\Theta_{n,1}^{(0)}(\infty)},\;\;\text{and}\;\;\widetilde{\vartheta}_{n}(z):=\frac{\Theta_{n,0}^{(0)}(z)}{\Theta_{n,0}^{(1)}(\infty)},

8.
Asymptotic Analysis

8.1.
Initial Riemann-Hilbert Problem

As agreed before, we omit the dependence on
t
whenever it does not cause ambiguity. In what follows, it will be convenient to set

\boldsymbol{I}:=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),\quad\sigma_{1}:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{3}:=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{matrix}\right).

We are seeking solutions of the following sequence of Riemann-Hilbert problems for
2\times 2
matrix functions (RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
):

(a)

\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
and
\lim_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma\ni z\to\infty}\boldsymbol{Y}(z)z^{-n\sigma_{3}}=\boldsymbol{I}
;

(b)

\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
has continuous traces on
\Gamma\setminus\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}\}
that satisfy

\boldsymbol{Y}_{+}(s)=\boldsymbol{Y}_{-}(s)\left(\begin{matrix}1&e^{-N_{n}V(s)}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),

where, as before,
V(z)
is given by (
1.11
) and the sequence
\{N_{n}\}
is such that
|n-N_{n}|\leq N_{*}
for some
N_{*}>0
.

The connection of
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
to orthogonal polynomials was first demonstrated by Fokas, Its, and Kitaev in
[
21
]
and lies in the following. If the solution of
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
exists, then it is necessarily of the form

(8.1)

\boldsymbol{Y}(z)=\left(\begin{matrix}P_{n}(z)&\big(\mathcal{C}P_{n}e^{-N_{n}V}\big)(z)\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{h_{n-1}}P_{n-1}(z)&-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{h_{n-1}}\big(\mathcal{C}P_{n-1}e^{-N_{n}V}\big)(z)\end{matrix}\right),

where
P_{n}(z)=P_{n}(z;t,N_{n})
are the polynomial satisfying orthogonality relations (
1.10
),
h_{n}=h_{n}(t,N_{n})
are the constants defined in (
1.12
), and
\mathcal{C}f(z)
is the Cauchy transform of a function
f
given on
\Gamma
, i.e.,

(\mathcal{C}f)(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{f(s)}{s-z}\mathrm{d}s.

Below, we show the solvability of
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}(t,\varepsilon)
large enough following the framework of the steepest descent analysis introduced by Deift and Zhou
[
15
]
. The latter lies in a series of transformations which reduce
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
to a problem with jumps asymptotically close to identity.

8.2.
Renormalized Riemann-Hilbert Problem

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
is a solution of
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
. Put

(8.2)

\boldsymbol{T}(z):=e^{n\ell_{*}\sigma_{3}/2}\boldsymbol{Y}(z)e^{-n(g(z)+\ell_{*}/2)\sigma_{3}},

where the function
g(z)
is defined by (
6.2
) and
\ell_{*}
was introduced in (
6.3
). Then

\boldsymbol{T}_{+}(s)=\boldsymbol{T}_{-}(s)\left(\begin{matrix}e^{-n(g_{+}(s)-g_{-}(s))}&e^{n(g_{+}(s)+g_{-}(s)-V(s)+\ell_{*})+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&e^{-n(g_{-}(s)-g_{+}(s))}\end{matrix}\right),

s\in\Gamma
, and therefore we deduce from (
4.7
) and (
6.6
)–(
6.8
) that
\boldsymbol{T}(z)
solves RHP-
{\boldsymbol{T}}
:

(a)

\boldsymbol{T}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
and
\lim_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma\ni z\to\infty}\boldsymbol{T}(z)=\boldsymbol{I}
;

(b)

\boldsymbol{T}(z)
has continuous traces on
\Gamma\setminus\{a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2}\}
that satisfy

\boldsymbol{T}_{+}(s)=\boldsymbol{T}_{-}(s)\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\left(\begin{matrix}1&e^{n(2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau+\phi_{a_{1}}(s))+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),&s\in\Gamma(e^{\mathrm{i}\pi}\infty,a_{1}),\\
\left(\begin{matrix}1&e^{n\phi_{b_{2}}(s)+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),&s\in\Gamma(b_{2},e^{\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\infty),\\
\left(\begin{matrix}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\omega n}&e^{n\phi_{a_{2}}(s)+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\omega n}\end{matrix}\right),&s\in\Gamma(b_{1},a_{2}),\end{array}\right.

and

\boldsymbol{T}_{+}(s)=\boldsymbol{T}_{-}(s)\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\left(\begin{matrix}e^{-n\phi_{b_{2}+}(s)}&e^{(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&e^{-n\phi_{b_{2}-}(s)}\end{matrix}\right),&s\in\Gamma(a_{2},b_{2}),\\
\left(\begin{matrix}e^{-n(\phi_{b_{2}+}(s)-2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau)}&e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau n+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
0&e^{-n(\phi_{b_{2}-}(s)-2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau)}\end{matrix}\right),&s\in\Gamma(a_{1},b_{1}).\end{array}\right.

Clearly, if
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{T}}
is solvable and
\boldsymbol{T}(z)
is the solution, then by inverting (
8.2
) one obtains a matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
that solves
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
.

8.3.
Lens Opening

As usual in the steepest descent analysis of matrix Riemann-Hilbert problems for orthogonal polynomials, the next step is based on the identity

\left(\begin{matrix}e^{-n(\phi_{b_{2}+}(s)-C)}&e^{nC+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\

\left(\begin{matrix}0&e^{nC+(n-N_{n})V(s)}\\
-e^{-nC-(n-N_{n})V(s)}&0\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\

that follows from (

), where

when

and

when

8.4.

{\boldsymbol{S}}

{\boldsymbol{S}}
(b). The latter can be easily identified with the help of (

(a)

\boldsymbol{N}(z)
is analytic in

(b)

Let the functions

Indeed,
RHP-
{\boldsymbol{N}}
(a) follows from holomorphy of

and

discussed in Proposition

It was shown in

\boldsymbol{R}(z)=\boldsymbol{I}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}^{(k)}(z)}{n^{k}},\quad n\to\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\cup_{e}\partial U_{e}.

The matrices

(c)

as
