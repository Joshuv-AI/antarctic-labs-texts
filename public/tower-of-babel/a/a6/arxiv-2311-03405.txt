Guangchen Lan

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) is an emerging paradigm for training deep neural networks (DNNs) in distributed manners. Current FL approaches all suffer from high communication overhead and information leakage. In this work, we present a federated learning algorithm based on evolution strategies (FedES), a zeroth-order training method. Instead of transmitting model parameters, FedES only communicates loss values, and thus has very low communication overhead. Moreover, a third party is unable to estimate gradients without knowing the pre-shared seed, which protects data privacy. Experimental results demonstrate FedES can achieve the above benefits while keeping convergence performance the same as that with back propagation methods.



I
Introduction

Federated learning (FL)
[
1
]
offers a promising solution to the challenges posed by data centralization, where users communicate locally trained models rather than raw datasets. Recent work has expanded the scope of FL to federated reinforcement learning
[
2
,
3
]
. The main challenges of FL are the high communication overhead and information leakage during the training process
[
4
]
. In particular, the uplink transmission is usually considered as the bottleneck
[
5
]
, while the downlink transmission can be done through broadcast
[
4
]
. On the other hand, most FL training algorithms rely on back propagation. However, back propagation-based FL algorithms are not applicable when gradient information is not available
[
6
]
.

To reduce communication overhead in FL, FedAvg
[
7
]
sets each client to update models locally with certain steps, and thus requires fewer communication rounds. Using a regularization term, FedProx
[
8
]
extends FedAvg for non-iid data settings with requirements of data similarity.
[
9
]
further uses tensor decomposition methods to compress deep learning models. But these local update methods have to face a client inconsistency problem
[
10
]
, which significantly influences the convergence performance.

To prevent information leakage during communication, a differential privacy method is adopted in
[
11
]
. In
[
12
]
, differential privacy is combined with secure multiparty computation to protect privacy. However, the convergence performance has to suffer the impact of added random noise, especially for high privacy requirements.

To tackle black-box objectives, distributed zeroth-order optimization algorithms are recently proposed in
[
6
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
[
13
]
proposes the ZONE algorithm for non-convex objectives based on primal-dual methods.
[
14
]
then adopts a gradient tracking technique with faster convergence rates.
[
15
]
further achieves a linear speedup convergence rate under Polyak-Łojasiewicz conditions. The local update method in FedAvg is then combined with zeroth-order methods for communication efficiency in
[
6
]
, while it requires high similarity over data sets. However, in previous works, a large number of model parameters or gradients are transmitted in each round and thus it suffers from high communication overhead. The privacy advantage of zeroth-order optimization is not exploited either. On the other hand, natural gradient descent has not been studied in previous zeroth-order optimization methods, which has the optimal performance for neural networks
[
16
]
.

Evolution strategy (ES)
[
17
]
estimates a descent direction (expected as natural gradients) for arbitrary black-box functions, and has been recently used in reinforcement learning
[
18
,
19
]
and computation graphs
[
20
]
. The previous works indicate that ES has a genetic parallel behavior, but it has not been exploited in federated neural network training.

In this paper, we propose a federated learning algorithm based on evolution strategies (FedES) with the following benefits:

•

Communication overhead
. The number of transmitted scalars from each client is equal to the number of mini-batches, which can be extremely smaller than the size of model parameters in conventional FL.

•

Heterogeneity
. FedES keeps the same performance in non-iid and inconsistency settings.

•

Privacy
. Without knowing the common random seed, a third-party attacker gets no information from the communication process.

•

Zeroth-order Optimization
. As no back propagation is required, FedES is suitable for black-box objectives.

In Section III, we propose our federated learning algorithms based on evolution strategies. In Section IV, we analyze the convergence rate of FedES. Section V shows the experimental results and we conclude this paper in Section VI.

II-A

Evolution Strategy

Evolution Strategy (ES) is a family of methods to estimate a natural gradient for black-box functions
[
21
]
. To update a DNN model
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, natural gradients can be estimated based on
n
data samples
\{\xi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as follows

\displaystyle l^{i}

\displaystyle=f(\bm{w}+\bm{\epsilon}^{i};\xi^{i}),

(1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l^{i}\bm{\epsilon}^{i},

(2)

where
f(\cdot)
is a loss function, and
\bm{\epsilon}^{i}
is a perturbation that has the same size as
\bm{w}
with
N
i.i.d. Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
samples. It is seen from (
1
)-(
2
) that the natural gradient
\widetilde{\bm{g}}
is a linear combination of the perturbations to the model
\bm{w}
, with the weights being the losses of the perturbed networks on the training data.

The estimate of
\widetilde{\bm{g}}
in (
1
)-(
2
) has a high variance, and thus antithetic sampling (AS)
[
22
]
can be used for variance reduction. AS perturbs the parameters twice in opposite directions using the same perturbation
\bm{\epsilon}^{i}
, and computes the loss as

\displaystyle l^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(f(\bm{w}+\bm{\epsilon}^{i};\xi^{i})-f(\bm{w}-\bm{\epsilon}^{i};\xi^{i})\big),

(3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l^{i}\bm{\epsilon}^{i}.

(4)

The model parameters are then updated as

\displaystyle\bm{w}\leftarrow\bm{w}-\alpha\widetilde{\bm{g}},

(5)

where
\alpha
is the learning rate parameter.

specifies the relative impact of client

, with one natural setting

, where

is the total number of data samples
[
1
]
.

FedES

The FedES algorithm is given in Algo.

Elite Selection.
We can further reduce the communication overhead of FedES with elite selection. Instead of transmitting all loss values
\{l^{b}_{k}\}_{b=1}^{B_{k}}
to the server in each round, client

\beta B_{k}

only transmits the largest loss value and

. We will show the performances with elite selection in Section V.

VI
Conclusions

We have proposed a federated learning algorithm based on evolution strategies (FedES). Clients and the server only transmit loss values in each round, and thus it costs little in communication. On the other hand, a third party is unable to estimate gradients without knowing the pre-shared seed, which prevents information leakage. With experimental results, we showed that FedES can achieve the above benefits while keeping convergence performance the same as that with back propagation methods.

Appendix

-A

Backgrounds of Natural Gradients

In this subsection, we show the advantages of natural gradients in neural networks from previous work in
[
16
]
. Let
\mathbf{S}=\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\}
be a parameter space where
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
is defined. Proved in
[
16
]
and
[
25
]
, in multi-layer neural networks, the Riemannian structure of
\mathbf{S}
is given by the Fisher information matrix
F(\bm{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. We assume
F(\bm{w})
is invertible. A small increment
\mathrm{d}\bm{w}
is then given by

\displaystyle\lVert\mathrm{d}\bm{w}\lVert^{2}=\sum_{i,j}F_{i,j}(\bm{w})\mathrm{d}w_{i}\mathrm{d}w_{j},

(10)

where
F_{i,j}(\bm{w})
denotes the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix
F(\bm{w})
, and
w_{i}
,
w_{j}
denotes the
i
-th,
j
-th element of
\bm{w}
, respectively. In Euclidean space,
F(\bm{w})
is equal to the identity matrix. The steepest descent direction of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
is defined by

\begin{split}&\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\mathrm{d}\bm{w}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}+\mathrm{d}\bm{w}),\\
&{\rm s.t.}\ \lVert\mathrm{d}\bm{w}\lVert^{2}=\varepsilon^{2},\end{split}

(11)

where
\varepsilon
is a constant that can be arbitrarily small.

Theorem 2
.

The steepest descent direction of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
is given by

\displaystyle-\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})=-F^{-1}(\bm{w})\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}),

(12)

where
\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
denotes the natural gradient.

Proof.
Let
\mathrm{d}\bm{w}=\varepsilon\bm{v}
, where
\lVert\bm{v}\lVert^{2}=\sum_{i,j}F_{i,j}(\bm{w})\mathrm{d}v_{i}\mathrm{d}v_{j}=1
. The goal is transformed to minimize
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}+\mathrm{d}\bm{w})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})+\varepsilon\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})^{T}\cdot\bm{v}
. Through the Lagrangian method, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\big(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})^{T}\cdot\bm{v}-\lambda\bm{v}^{T}F(\bm{w})\bm{v}\big)}{\partial v_{i}}=0,\ i=1,\cdots,N,

(13)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier. Then we have

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}F^{-1}(\bm{w})\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}).

(14)

The natural gradient with the steepest descent direction is then defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})=F^{-1}(\bm{w})\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}).

(15)

-B

Proof of
\bf{Theorem\ \ref{theorem_convergence_rate}}

In Algorithm 1, after receiving loss values from clients, the server has

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{g}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\rho_{k}}{B_{k}}\sum_{b=1}^{B_{k}}\bm{\epsilon}^{b}_{k}l^{b}_{k}.

(16)

Taking
\rho_{k}=\frac{n_{k}}{n}
and
B_{k}=\frac{n_{k}}{n_{B}}
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{g}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}n/n_{B}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{b=1}^{B_{k}}\bm{\epsilon}^{b}_{k}l^{b}_{k}

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}n/n_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{n/n_{B}}\bm{\epsilon}^{i}l^{i},

(18)

which performs overall data samples. Proved in
[
26
,
27
]
,
\widetilde{\bm{g}}
is an unbiased estimator of
\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
, where
\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})=F^{-1}(\bm{w})\cdot\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
is the natural gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})
. The variance of the estimate is not easy to get for neural networks. But we can achieve an arbitrarily small variance as the number of searching directions
\frac{n}{n_{B}}
is large enough. Thus, we consider the situation as
\widetilde{\bm{g}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{w})
with large
\frac{n}{n_{B}}
.

Theorem 3
.

The expected squared error at the
t
-th iteration is given as

\displaystyle V^{t}=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{T}]=\frac{1}{t}F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t^{2}}),

(19)

when
\alpha
in (
5
) is chosen as
\frac{1}{t}
.

Proof.
By Taylor’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})+\nabla\big(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})\big)(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{2}\big).

(20)

At the
(t+1)
-th iteration, we have

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{t+1}=\bm{w}^{t}-\frac{1}{t}\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t}).

(21)

Subtracting
\bm{w}^{*}
and taking the expected square operation on both sides, we have

\displaystyle V^{t+1}=\mathbb{E}[\big(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*}+\frac{1}{t}\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})\big)\big(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*}+\frac{1}{t}\widetilde{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})\big)^{T}].

(22)

Taking
\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})]=\bm{0}
,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla\big(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})\big)]=F(\bm{w}^{*})
, and
F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{t})=F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t})
, we achieve

\displaystyle V^{t+1}=V^{t}-\frac{2}{t}V^{t}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t^{3}}).

(23)

Finally, the variance is given as

\displaystyle V^{t}=\frac{1}{t}F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t^{2}}).

(24)

Now we show the analysis of the convergence rate based on Theorem
3
. By Taylor’s theorem and
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})=0
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})&=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{T}H^{*}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})+\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})^{T}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{3}\big)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{T}H^{*}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})+\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{3}\big),\end{split}

(25)

where
H^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the Hessian matrix at
\bm{w}^{*}
. Taking expectation operation on both sides,
H^{*}=F(\bm{w}^{*})

[
16
]
, and Theorem
3
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{t})]-\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{*})&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{T}H^{*}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{3}\big)]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\Big(H^{*}\ \mathbb{E}[(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{T}(\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})]\Big)+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{3}\big)]\\
&=\frac{1}{2t}{\rm Tr}\Big(H^{*}F^{-1}(\bm{w}^{*})\Big)+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{3}\big)]\\
&=\frac{N}{2t}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t}),\end{split}

(26)

where
{\rm Tr}(\cdot)
denotes the trace operation. As
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}\big((\bm{w}^{t}-\bm{w}^{*})^{2}\big)]=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{t})
, the higher order term in the last second line should not converge slower in bounded regions.
