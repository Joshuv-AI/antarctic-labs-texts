Efficient space-time adaptivity for parabolic evolution equations using wavelets in time and finite elements in space Thanks: Funding: Both authors are supported by the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research (NWO) under contract. no. 613.001.652
Raymond van Venetië and Jan Westerdiep

Abstract
. Considering the space-time adaptive method for parabolic evolution equations introduced in [arXiv:2101.03956 [math.NA]], this work discusses an implementation of the method in which every step is of linear complexity. Exploiting the product structure of the space-time cylinder, the method allows for a family of trial spaces given as the spans of wavelets-in-time tensorized with (locally refined) finite element spaces-in-space. On spaces whose bases are indexed by double-trees , we derive an algorithm that applies the resulting bilinear forms in linear complexity. We provide extensive numerical experiments to demonstrate the linear runtime of the resulting adaptive loop. Supplementary material. Source code is available at [ vVW21 ] .

1.
Introduction

This paper deals with the adaptive numerical solution of parabolic evolution equations using a simultaneous space-time variational formulation. Compared to the more classical time-stepping schemes, these space-time methods are very flexible. Among other things, they are especially well-suited for massively parallel computation (
[
NS19
,
vVW20
]
), and some can guarantee quasi-best approximations from the trial space (
[
And13
,
FK21
,
SZ20
]
).

We are interested in those space-time methods that permit adaptive refinement locally in space
and
time.
Within this class, wavelet-based methods (see
[
SS09
,
GK11
,
KSU15
]
) are attractive, as they can be shown to be
quasi-optimal
:
they produce a sequence of solutions that converges at the best possible rate, at optimal linear computational cost. Moreover, they can overcome the
curse of dimensionality
using a form of
sparse tensor-product approximation
, solving the whole time evolution at a runtime proportional to that of solving the corresponding
stationary
problem.

In
[
SvVW21
]
, we constructed an
r
-linearly converging space-time adaptive solver for
parabolic evolution equations that exploits the
product structure of the space-time cylinder to construct a family of trial spaces given as
the spans of wavelets-in-time
tensorized with (locally refined) finite element spaces-in-space.

The principal difference between this and other wavelet-based methods is that we use wavelets in time
only
, and standard finite elements in space. This eases implementation, and alleviates the need for a suitable spatial wavelet basis, which is generally difficult for general domains (
[
RS18
]
).
Unfortunately, there is no free lunch: a proof of optimal convergence is, for our method, not yet available.

In this work we discuss an implementation of
[
SvVW21
]
in which the different steps
(each iteration of the linear algebraic solver, the error estimation,
Dörfler marking, and refinement of trial- and test spaces)
of the adaptive algorithm are of linear complexity.

Special care has to be taken for matrix-vector products.
For a bilinear form that is ‘local’ and equals
(a sum of) tensor-product(s) of bilinear forms in time and space, and
‘trial’ and ‘test’ spaces spanned by tensor-product multi-level bases with
double-tree
index sets, the resulting
system matrix w.r.t. both bases can be applied in linear complexity, even though this matrix is not sparse. The algorithm that realizes this complexity makes a clever use of multi- to single-scale transformations alternately in time and space.
This
unidirectional principle
was introduced in
[
BZ96
]
for ‘uniform’ sparse grids, so without ‘local refinements’,
and it was later extended to general
downward closed
or
lower sets
, also called
adaptive sparse grids
, in
[
KS14
]
. The definition of a lower set in
[
KS14
]
, there called multi-tree, is more restrictive than our current definition that allows more localized refinements.

To the best of our knowledge, other implementations for the efficient evaluation of tensor-product bilinear forms
(see
[
Pfl10
,
KS14
,
Pab15
,
Rek18
]
)
are based on the concept of hash maps. There, a hash function is used
to map basis functions to array indices.
In an adaptive loop, the final set of basis functions is unknown in advance so it is impossible to construct a hash function that guarantees an upper bound on the number of hash collisions.
Aiming at true linear complexity, we
implement these operations by traversing
trees
and
double-trees
, so without the use of hash maps.

Organization

In §

, we look at the abstract parabolic problem, its stable discretization,

In §

.
In §

2.
Space-time adaptivity for a parabolic model problem

In this section, we summarize the relevant parts of
[
SvVW21
, §2–5]
.

Let
V,H
be separable Hilbert spaces of functions on some “spatial domain” such that
V\hookrightarrow H
with dense and compact embedding.
Identifying
H
with its dual, we obtain the Gelfand triple

V\hookrightarrow H\simeq H^{\prime}\hookrightarrow V^{\prime}
.

For a.e.

t\in I\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(0,T),

let
a(t;\cdot,\cdot)
denote a bilinear form on
V\times V
so that for
any
\eta,\zeta\in V
,
t\mapsto a(t;\eta,\zeta)
is measurable on
I
,
and such that for a.e.
t\in I
,

\displaystyle|a(t;\eta,\zeta)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\eta\|_{V}\|\zeta\|_{V}\quad

\displaystyle(\eta,\zeta\in V)\quad

\displaystyle\text{({boundedness})},

\displaystyle a(t;\eta,\eta)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\eta\|_{V}^{2}\quad

\displaystyle(\eta\in{V})\quad

\displaystyle\text{({coercivity})}.

With
(A(t)\cdot)(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=a(t;\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}({V},V^{\prime})
, given a forcing function
g
and initial value
u_{0}
, we want to solve the
parabolic initial value problem
of

(2.1)

\text{finding $u\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}I\to V$ such that}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{\mathrm{d}u\,}{\mathrm{d}t\,}(t)+A(t)u(t)&=g(t)\quad(t\in I),\\
u(0)&=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

Example 2.1
.

For the model problem of the
heat equation
on
some spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we select
V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
H\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=L_{2}(\Omega)
, and
a(t;\eta,\zeta)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\eta\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\zeta\dif{\mathbf{x}}
.

In our simultaneous space-time variational formulation, the parabolic problem is to find
u
s.t.

(Bu)(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{I}\langle{\textstyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u\,}{\mathrm{d}t\,}}(t),v(t)\rangle_{H}+a(t;u(t),v(t))\mathrm{d}t\,=\int_{I}\langle g(t),v(t)\rangle_{H}=\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g(v)

for all
v
from some suitable space of functions of time and space.
One possibility to enforce the initial condition is by testing against additional test functions.

2.2
[
SS09
]
.

With
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=L_{2}(I;{V})\cap H^{1}(I;V^{\prime})
,
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=L_{2}(I;{V})
, we have

\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}B\\
\gamma_{0}\end{array}\right]\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}(X,Y^{\prime}\times H),

where for
t\in\bar{I}
,
\gamma_{t}\colon u\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
denotes the trace map.
In other words,

(2.2)

\text{finding $u\in X$ s.t.}\quad(Bu,\gamma_{0}u)=(g,u_{0})\quad\text{given}\quad(g,u_{0})\in Y^{\prime}\times H

is a well-posed simultaneous space-time variational formulation of (
2.1
).

We define
A\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}(Y,Y^{\prime})
and
\partial_{t}\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}(X,Y^{\prime})
as

(Au)(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{I}a(t;u(t),v(t))\mathrm{d}t\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=B-A.

Following
[
SW20
]
, we assume that
A
is
self-adjoint
.
Morever, in view of an efficient implementation, we assume that
A
is a finite sum of tensor-product operators. If
A
does not have this structure, one may alternatively consider (low-rank) tensor-product approximations of
A
, see e.g.
[
Hac12
]
for an overview.

We equip
Y
and
X
with
‘energy’-norms

\|\cdot\|_{Y}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(A\cdot)(\cdot),\quad\|\cdot\|_{X}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|\partial_{t}\cdot\|_{Y^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\cdot\|_{Y}^{2}+\|\gamma_{T}\cdot\|_{H}^{2},

which are equivalent to the canonical norms on
Y
and
X
.

The solution
u
of (
2.2
) equals the solution of the following minimization problem

(2.3)

u=\argmin_{w\in X}\|Bw-g\|_{Y^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\gamma_{0}w-u_{0}\|_{H}^{2},

which in turn is the second component of the solution of

(2.4)

\text{finding $(\mu,u)\in Y\times X$ s.t.}\quad\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}A&B\\
B^{\prime}&-\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gamma_{0}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\mu\\
u\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}g\\
-u_{0}\end{array}\right].

Indeed, taking the Schur complement of (
2.4
) w.r.t. the
Y
-block results in the Euler-Lagrange equations of (
2.3
).

2.1.
Discretizations

Take a family
(X^{\delta})_{\delta\in\Delta}
of closed subspaces of
X
, and define

(2.5)

u_{\delta}=\argmin_{w\in X^{\delta}}\|Bw-g\|_{Y^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\gamma_{0}w-u_{0}\|_{H}^{2},

being the best approximation to
u
from
X^{\delta}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{X}
.
Solving this problem, however, is not feasible because of the presence of the dual norm.
Therefore, take
(Y^{\delta})_{\delta\in\Delta}
to be a family of closed subspaces of
Y
such that

(2.6)

X^{\delta}\subseteq Y^{\delta}\quad(\delta\in\Delta),\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{\Delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf_{\delta\in\Delta}\inf_{0\not=w\in X^{\delta}}\sup_{0\not=v\in Y^{\delta}}\frac{(\partial_{t}w)(v)}{\|\partial_{t}w\|_{Y^{\prime}}\|v\|_{Y}}>0.

For
{\underline{\delta}}\in\Delta
with
Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}\supseteq Y^{\delta}
, we replace
Y^{\prime}
by
{Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}
in (
2.5
) yielding the
approximation

u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}=\argmin_{w\in X^{\delta}}\|Bw-g\|_{{Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\gamma_{0}w-u_{0}\|_{H}^{2}.

Notice that
u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}
approximates
u_{\delta}
in that
u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}=u_{\delta}
when
Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}=Y
.

With
E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}\to Y
and
E_{X}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X^{\delta}\to X
denoting the trivial embeddings,
u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}
is the second component of the solution of

\begin{bmatrix}{E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}AE_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}&{E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}BE_{X}^{\delta}\\
{E_{X}^{\delta}}^{\prime}B^{\prime}E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}&-{E_{X}^{\delta}}^{\prime}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gamma_{0}E_{X}^{\delta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{{{\underline{\delta}}}\delta}\\
u^{{{\underline{\delta}}}\delta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}g\\
-{E_{X}^{\delta}}^{\prime}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Taking the Schur complement w.r.t. the
Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}
-block then leads to the equation

(2.7)

\begin{split}{E^{\delta}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}({E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}AE_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}})^{-1}{E^{{\underline{\delta}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}B&+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gamma_{0})E^{\delta}_{X}u^{{{\underline{\delta}}}\delta}\\
&={E^{\delta}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}({E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}AE_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}})^{-1}{E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}g+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}),\end{split}

which has a unique solution (cf.
[
SvVW21
, Lem. 3.3]
) that satisfies
\|u-u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}\|_{X}\leq\gamma_{\Delta}^{-1}\|u-u_{\delta}\|_{X}
whenever
Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}\supseteq Y^{\delta}
; cf.
[
SW20
, Thm. 3.7]
.
For now, we assume the right-hand side of (
2.7
) to be evaluated exactly. Later, in §
4.5
, we will discuss approximation of the right-hand side.

In view of obtaining an efficient solver,
we want to replace the inverses in (
2.7
) while aiming to preserve quasi-optimality of the solution.
To this end, let
K_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}={K_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}({Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime},Y^{{\underline{\delta}}})
be a uniformly
optimal preconditioner for
{E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}AE_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}
that can be applied in linear complexity. Then, for some
\kappa_{\Delta}\geq 1
we have

\frac{((K^{{\underline{\delta}}}_{Y})^{-1}v)(v)}{(Av)(v)}\in[\kappa^{-1}_{\Delta},\kappa_{\Delta}]\quad(\delta\in\Delta,v\in Y^{{\underline{\delta}}}).

Replacing
({E_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}}^{\prime}AE_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}})^{-1}
by
K_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}
, we denote the solution of (
2.7
) again by
u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}
. It is quasi-optimal with
\|u-u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}\|_{X}\leq\tfrac{\kappa_{\Delta}}{\gamma_{\Delta}}\|u-u_{\delta}\|_{X}
; cf.
[
SW20
, Rem. 3.8]
.

2.2.
Adaptive refinement loop

Our adaptive loop, given in Algorithm
1
, takes the familiar
Solve
,
Estimate
,
Mark and refine
steps, and is driven by an efficient and reliable ‘hierarchical basis’ a posteriori error estimator.

The adaptive loop below requires a saturation assumption.
Define a
partial order
on
\Delta
by
\tilde{\delta}\succeq\delta
whenever
X^{\tilde{\delta}}\supseteq X^{\delta}
.
Let
\delta\mapsto{\underline{\delta}}\succeq\delta
be a mapping providing
saturation
in that for some
\zeta<1
,

(2.8)

\|u-u_{{\underline{\delta}}}\|_{X}\leq\zeta\|u-u_{\delta}\|_{X}\quad(\delta\in\Delta).

With this choice of
{\underline{\delta}}
, we are interested in finding
u^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=u^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}\in X^{\delta}
that solves

(2.9)

\underbrace{{E^{\delta}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}K_{Y}^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}{E^{{\underline{\delta}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}B+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gamma_{0})E^{\delta}_{X}}_{S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=}u^{\delta}=\underbrace{{E^{\delta}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}K_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}{E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}g+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}u_{0})}_{f^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=}.

Notice that (
2.9
) is uniquely solvable even with
X^{\underline{\delta}}
as ‘trial space’, and we use this ‘room’ between
X^{\delta}
and
X^{\underline{\delta}}
to our advantage.
Expanding
X^{\delta}
to some intermediate space
X^{\delta}\subset X^{\tilde{\delta}}\subset X^{\underline{\delta}}
yields a
u^{\tilde{\delta}}
that is a better approximation to
u
than
u^{\delta}
; cf.
[
SvVW21
, Prop. 4.2]
. This function will be the successor of
u^{\delta}
in our loop, and we will show that the resulting sequence of functions converges
r
-linearly to

; see Algorithm
1
and Theorem
2.4
.

Solving

Instead of solving the symmetric positive definite system (
2.9
) exactly, we construct an approximate solution
\hat{u}^{\delta}
using Preconditioned Conjugate Gradients (PCG).
To this end, let
K_{X}^{\delta}={K_{X}^{\delta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}\mathrm{is}({X^{\delta}}^{\prime},X^{\delta})
be a uniformly optimal preconditioner for
S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}
.
Then
((K_{X}^{\delta})^{-1}w)(w)\eqsim\|w\|^{2}_{X}\eqsim\|K_{X}^{\delta}S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}w\|^{2}_{X}
for
w\in X^{\delta}
. Writing
w=K_{X}^{\delta}S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}(u^{\delta}-v^{\delta}
) reveals that this induces an algebraic error estimator

(2.10)

\beta^{\delta}(v^{\delta})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt{(f^{\delta}-S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}v^{\delta})(K_{X}^{\delta}(f^{\delta}-S^{{\underline{\delta}}\delta}v^{\delta}))}\eqsim\|u^{\delta}-v^{\delta}\|_{X}\quad(v^{\delta}\in X^{\delta},\delta\in\Delta).

With
\hat{u}^{\delta}_{k}
denoting the approximant at iteration
k
of the PCG loop,
\beta^{\delta}(\hat{u}^{\delta}_{k})
is already available as
\sqrt{\beta_{k}}
, for
\beta_{k}
the variable used in computing the next search direction.

Error estimation

Let

\Theta_{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\theta_{\lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda\in J_{\delta}\}
be some uniformly
X
-stable basis satisfying
X^{\delta}\oplus\spann\Theta_{\delta}=X^{\underline{\delta}}
, in that

(2.11)

\|z+{\bf c}^{\top}\Theta_{\delta}\|_{X}^{2}\eqsim\|z\|_{X}^{2}+\|{\bf c}\|^{2}\quad({\bf c}\in\ell_{2}(J_{\delta}),z\in X^{\delta},\delta\in\Delta).

Define the trivial embedding
P^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X^{\delta}\to X^{\underline{\delta}}
. Akin to (
2.9
), we define
S^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}
and
f^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}
, and with it, the residual-based a posteriori error estimator
{\bf r}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X^{\delta}\to\ell_{2}(J_{\delta})
, as

(2.12)

\displaystyle S^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={E^{\underline{\delta}}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}K_{Y}^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}{E^{{\underline{\delta}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}B+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\gamma_{0})E^{\underline{\delta}}_{X},\quad f^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={E^{\underline{\delta}}_{X}}^{\prime}(B^{\prime}E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}K_{Y}^{{\underline{\delta}}}{E^{{{\underline{\delta}}}}_{Y}}^{\prime}g+\gamma_{0}^{\prime}u_{0}),

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(f^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}-S^{{\underline{\delta}}{\underline{\delta}}}P^{\delta}\hat{u}^{\delta})(\Theta_{\delta}).

For
{\hat{u}}^{\delta}
close to
u^{\delta}
, the error estimator
\|{\bf r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\|
is reliable and efficient:

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume (
2.8
) and (
2.11
),
\tfrac{\kappa_{\Delta}}{\gamma_{\Delta}}<\tfrac{1}{\zeta}
, and fix some
\xi>0
small enough. For
\hat{u}^{\delta}\in X^{\delta}
satisfying
\beta({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\leq\frac{\xi}{1-\xi}\|{\bf r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\|
, we have

\|{\bf r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\|\eqsim\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}\quad\text{and}\quad\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}\lesssim\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}\quad(\delta\in\Delta).

Proof.

For convenience, we write
\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\bf r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})
and
{\bf r}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\bf r}^{\delta}(u^{\delta})
.

By (
2.8
), (
2.11
) and
\tfrac{\kappa_{\Delta}}{\gamma_{\Delta}}<\tfrac{1}{\zeta}
,
[
SvVW21
, Prop. 4.4]
shows that

(2.13)

\|{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\eqsim\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}\quad(\delta\in\Delta).

From (
2.11
) one deduces that
\|{\bf r}^{\delta}-\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\lesssim\|u^{\delta}-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}
; cf.
[
SvVW21
, (4.13)]
.
By assumption, for
\xi<1
, we find
\beta^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\lesssim\xi\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|
.
Combined this reveals

(2.14)

\|{\bf r}^{\delta}-\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:X-stable}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u^{\delta}-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:algerr}}}}{{\eqsim}}\beta^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\lesssim\xi\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|.

Using this, we can show reliability of the estimator by

\displaystyle\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}+\|u^{\delta}-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\makebox[0.0pt][r]{\scriptsize\eqref{eqn:residual-estim},}\eqref{eqn:algerr}}}}{{\eqsim}}\|{\bf r}^{\delta}\|+\beta^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\leq\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|+\|{\bf r}^{\delta}-\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|+\beta^{\delta}({\hat{u}}^{\delta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:res-perturb}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|.

For efficiency of the estimator, we deduce

\displaystyle\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:residual-estim}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}+\|{\bf r}^{\delta}-\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\leq\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}+\|u^{\delta}-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}+\|{\bf r}^{\delta}-\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:res-perturb}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}+\xi\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|,

so taking
\xi
sufficiently small and kicking back
\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|
yields

(2.15)

\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\lesssim\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}.

Similarly, from (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) it follows that

(2.16)

\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\lesssim\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}.

We infer quasi-optimality of
{\hat{u}}^{\delta}
from

\|u-{\hat{u}}^{\delta}\|_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:res-perturb}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}+\xi\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eqn:residual-eff}}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u-u^{\delta}\|_{X}.\qed

In the solve step, we need to iterate PCG until
\beta^{\delta}(\hat{u}^{\delta}_{k})/\|{\bf r}^{\delta}(\hat{u}^{\delta}_{k})\|
is small enough. In the algorithm below, this is ensured by the do-while loop which also avoids the (expensive) recomputation of the residual at every PCG iteration.

Marking and refinement

Denoting the output of the solve step by
\hat{u}^{\delta}
, we drive the adaptive loop by performing Dörfler marking on the residual
\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\bf r}^{\delta}(\hat{u}^{\delta})
, i.e., for some
\theta\in(0,1]
, we mark the smallest set
J\subset J_{\delta}
for which

\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}|_{J}\|\geq\theta\|\hat{\bf r}^{\delta}\|
.
We then construct the smallest
\tilde{\delta}\succeq\delta
such that
X^{\tilde{\delta}}
contains
\spann\Theta_{\delta}|_{J}
.

Data:

\theta\in(0,1]
,
\xi\in(0,1)
,
\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\delta_{\text{init}}\in\Delta
;

t_{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathcal{E}^{\delta}(0)=\sqrt{({E_{Y}^{\underline{\delta}}}^{\prime}g)(K_{Y}^{\underline{\delta}}{E_{Y}^{\underline{\delta}}}^{\prime}g)+\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H}}
;

repeat

Solve:

do

Compute
\hat{u}_{*}^{\delta}\in X^{\delta}
with
\beta^{\delta}(\hat{u}_{*}^{\delta})\leq t_{\delta}/2
;

t_{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta^{\delta}(\hat{u}_{*}^{\delta})
;

e_{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\|{\bf r}^{\delta}({\hat{u}}_{*}^{\delta})\|+t_{\delta}

Adaptive trial- and test spaces

The convergence rate of our adaptive loop is determined by the approximation properties of the family

In §

with a tensor-product of (infinite) bases: a wavelet basis

By imposing a

5.1.
Trees and linear operators in one axis

In §
3

indexed on
\vee_{\Psi}

3

4

In our applications, the support of a wavelet

is a union of simplices of generation
|\lambda|

In time, these simplices are subintervals of

found by dyadic refinement.
In space, they are elements of

Every mother tree

is stored once in memory, and every node
\lambda\in\vee
stores references to its parents, children, and siblings.
We treat the mother tree as infinite by
lazy initialization
, constructing new nodes as they are needed.

5.1.1.
Trees

We store a tree

using the parent-child relation,
and additionally, at each

\vee
.
This allows us to compare different trees subject to the same mother tree.

\Lambda
from its roots in breadth-first, or level-wise, order.

5.2.1.
Memory optimizations

As the memory consumption of a double-tree is significant, at around 280 bytes per node, we want to have as few double-trees in memory as possible.
By storing the nodes of
{\bf\Lambda}
in a persistent container, every node is uniquely identified with its index in the container.
This induces a mapping
\mathbb{R}^{\#{\bf\Lambda}}\leftrightarrow\ell_{2}({\bf\Lambda})
and allows us to overlay multiple vectors on the same underlying double-tree in a memory-friendly way.

The
{\bf\Sigma}
generated by Algorithm
8
for the application of a tensor-product operator can play the role of
{\bf\Theta}
necessary for the application of its transpose operator (and vice versa). This allows tensor-product operators and their transposes to share the double-trees
{\bf\Sigma}
and
{\bf\Theta}
.

With these insights, our implementation of the heat equation has at most 5 different double-trees in memory.

7.
Conclusion

We discussed an implementation of an adaptive solver for a space-time variational formulation of parabolic evolution equations where every step is of linear complexity.

We constructed a family of trial spaces spanned by tensor-products of wavelets in time and hierarchical basis functions in space. The resulting adaptive loop is able to resolve singularities locally in space and time, and we proved its
r
-linear convergence.

After imposing a
double-tree
constraint on the index set of the trial spaces,
we devised an abstract algorithm that is able to apply the system matrices in linear complexity.
We achieve this complexity in practice by a
tree-based
implementation.
The numerical results show high performance of the adaptive loop as a whole.
