On the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality for a wide class of A A -partition functions
Krystian Gajdzica

Abstract
. The A A -partition function p A ​ ( n ) p_{A}(n) enumerates those partitions of n n whose parts belong to a fixed (finite or infinite) set A A of positive integers. On the other hand, the extended A A -partition function p A ​ ( 𝝁 ) p_{A}\left(\bm{\mu}\right) is defined as an multiplicative extension of the A A -partition function to a function on A A -partitions. In this paper, we investigate the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality for a wide class of A A -partition functions. In particular, we examine the property for both the m m -ary partition function b m ​ ( n ) b_{m}(n) and the d d -th power partition function p d ​ ( n ) p_{d}(n) . Moreover, we show that b m ​ ( 𝝁 ) b_{m}(\bm{\mu}) ( p d ​ ( 𝝁 ) p_{d}(\bm{\mu}) ) takes its maximum value at an explicitly described set of m m -ary partitions (power partitions), where 𝝁 \bm{\mu} is an m m -ary partition (a power partition) of n n . Additionally, we exhibit analogous results for the Fibonacci partition function and the ‘factorial’ partition function. It is worth pointing out that an elementary combinatorial reasoning plays a crucial role in our investigation.

1.
Introduction

A partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{j})
of a non-negative integer
n
is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive integers such that

\displaystyle n=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{j}.

Elements
\lambda_{i}
are called parts of the partition
\lambda
. By
P(n)
, we mean the set of all partitions of
n
. Further, the number of all partitions of
n
is denoted by
p(n)
, i.e.
p(n)=\#P(n)
. For instance, we have that
p(4)=5
with

\displaystyle(4),\hskip 5.69046pt(3,1),\hskip 5.69046pt(2,2),\hskip 5.69046pt(2,1,1)\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.69046pt(1,1,1,1).

It is well known that the generating function for
p(n)
takes the form

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-q^{i}}.

That is Euler’s result from 1748. Since then, the partition function has been investigated from various perspectives. Therefore, there is a plethora of literature devoted to its analytic, arithmetic and combinatorial properties. We refer the reader to
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
20
,
32
]
for a comprehensive introduction to the theory of partitions.

Now, let us recall that a sequence
\left(c_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
of real numbers is said to be log-concave if it fulfills
c_{n}^{2}>c_{n-1}c_{n+1}
for every
n\geqslant 1
. In 2015, DeSalvo and Pak
[
13
]
reproved Nicolas’ theorem
[
27
]
concerning the log-concavity of the partition function.

Theorem 1.1

(DeSalvo-Pak, Nicolas)
.

The partition function is log-concave for every
n\geqslant 26
.

Their paper has initiated wide research related to investigating similar phenomena for other variations of the partition function. We encourage the reader to see
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
24
,
29
,
34
]
for more results related to that subject.

However, DeSalvo and Pak’s paper has also motivated to study other inequalities for partition statistics. In 2016, Bessenrodt and Ono
[
8
]
discovered the following result.

Theorem 1.2

(Bessenrodt-Ono)
.

Let
a,b\geqslant 2
be positive integers such that
a+b>9
. Then

p(a)p(b)>p(a+b).

The original proof of the above identity is based on asymptotic estimates due to Lehmer
[
22
]
. There are also two alternative proofs of Theorem
1.2
. The first of them is in combinatorial manner due to Alanazi, Gagola and Munagi
[
2
]
. The second one, on the other hand, is done via induction on
a+b
by Heim and Neuhauser
[
17
]
.

It is worth pointing out that the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality is not only art of art’s sake, but it allows us to determine the maximum value of the form

\max{p(n)}:=\max{\left\{p(\bm{\lambda}):\bm{\lambda}\text{ is a partition of }n\right\}},

where
p(\bm{\lambda})
denotes the extended partition function defined as

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mu}):=\prod_{i=1}^{j}p(\mu_{i})\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for an arbitrary partition }\hskip 5.69046pt\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{j}).

More precisely, the following is true.

Theorem 1.3

(Bessenrodt-Ono)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. For
n\geqslant 4
and
n\not=7
, the maximal value
\max{p(n)}
of the partition function on
P(n)
is attained at the partition

\displaystyle\left(4,4,4,\ldots,4,4\right)\text{ when }n\equiv 0\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),

\displaystyle\left(5,4,4,\ldots,4,4\right)\text{ when }n\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),

\displaystyle\left(6,4,4,\ldots,4,4\right)\text{ when }n\equiv 2\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),

\displaystyle\left(6,5,4,\ldots,4,4\right)\text{ when }n\equiv 3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4).

In particular, if
n\geqslant 8
, then

\displaystyle\max{p(n)}=\begin{cases}5^{\frac{n}{4}},&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),\\
7\cdot 5^{\frac{n-5}{4}},&\text{ if }n\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),\\
11\cdot 5^{\frac{n-6}{4}},&\text{ if }n\equiv 2\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),\\
11\cdot 7\cdot 5^{\frac{n-11}{4}},&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4).\end{cases}

[

In this paper, on the other hand, we investigate the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality for the so-called

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{A}(n)q^{n}=\prod_{a\in A}\frac{1}{1-q^{a}}.

For a various examples of

.

However, the most important result is a property which allows us to examine the validity of the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for a rich class of
A
-partition functions.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
A=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be an arbitrary (finite or infinite) set of positive integers with
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
such that
a_{k}-a_{l}\geqslant a_{3}
for every
k>l\geqslant 3
and
2a_{2}\leqslant a_{3}
. Suppose further that the inequality

\displaystyle p_{A}(w)p_{A}(z)>p_{A}(w+z)

is satisfied for all numbers
w,z\geqslant a_{3}
with
2a_{3}\leqslant w+z\leqslant 2(a_{3}+a_{2})-1
. Then, the inequality

\displaystyle p_{A}(w)p_{A}(z)>p_{A}(w+z)

holds for every
w,z\geqslant a_{3}
.

The usefulness of the above theorem is highlighted in the sequel. In particular, we apply it to deal with the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the power partition function. Finally, it is worth noting that a combinatorial reasoning plays an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

m

1.4
and concluding remarks.

2.
Preliminaries

At first, we fix some notation. By
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant k}
, we mean the set of non-negative integers, the set of positive integers and the set of positive integers greater or equal than
k
, respectively. We do not repeat the definition of the
A
-partition function, because it might be found in Introduction. Throughout the paper, we also use the following conventions. We put
P_{A}(n):=\{\lambda:\lambda\text{ is an }A\text{-partition of }n\}
and define the extended
A
-partition function as

\displaystyle p_{A}(\bm{\lambda}):=\prod_{i=1}^{j}p_{A}(\lambda_{i})\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for an arbitrary $A$-partition }\hskip 5.69046pt\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{j}).

We also set

\displaystyle\max{p_{A}(n)}:=\max{\left\{p_{A}(\bm{\lambda}):\bm{\lambda}\text{ is an }A\text{-partition of }n\right\}}.

Moreover,
p_{A}(n|\text{ condition }X)
and
P_{A}(n|\text{ condition }Y)
denote the number of
A
-partitions of
n
that satisfy the condition
X
and the set of
A
-partitions of
n
that fulfill the condition
Y
, respectively. Further, the Cartesian product of two sets of partitions, let say,
U
and
V
is written as
U\oplus V
, i.e.

\displaystyle U\oplus V=\{\left(\lambda;\mu\right):\lambda\in U,\mu\in V\}.

Finally, it should be pointed out that exponents appearing in a partition refer to multiplicities, e.g.
(5^{3},2^{2},1)=(5,5,5,2,2,1)
.

Now, we are ready to proceed to the main part of the paper.

3.
The proof of Theorem
1.4

Before we delve into the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we need to show an auxiliary result which allows us to reduce the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality problem for

to a finite task.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be an arbitrary (finite or infinite) set of positive integers with
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
such that
a_{k}-a_{l}\geqslant a_{3}

\displaystyle p_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})p_{A}(z)\geqslant p_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})

holds for all positive integers
w\geqslant a_{3}+1
and
z\geqslant 2a_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
a_{2},a_{3},w,z
be as in the statement. Similarly to Alanazi, Gagola and Munagi
[
2
]
, we put

\displaystyle i=i(\lambda):=\max{\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{+}:\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{j+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{t+s}\geqslant z\}},

where
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{t}\geqslant a_{3}
and
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t+s}\right)=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t},1^{s}\right)\in P_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})
is arbitrary. We also assume that
\lambda_{i}=x+y
, where
x+\lambda_{i+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{t+s}=z
and
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i-1}+y=w
. Hence, it follows from the definition of
i
that
x\geqslant 1
and
y\geqslant 0
.

Now, our main aim is to construct an injective map

\displaystyle f:P_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})\to P_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})\oplus P_{A}(z).

In order to do that, let us assume that
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t},1^{s}\right)\in P_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})
. We construct the map in a few steps depending on
x,y
and
s
. For the sake of convenience, we label each of the cases by its general assumptions.

Case 1
(
y=0\text{ and }0\leqslant s\leqslant z
):

In this case, we simply put

\displaystyle f(\lambda)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i-1};\lambda_{i},\ldots,\lambda_{t},1^{s}).

It is clear that if
f(\lambda)=f(\mu)
in this situation, then
\lambda=\mu
.

Case 2
(
y=0\text{ and }z<s\leqslant a_{3}+z
):

Under these assumptions, we set

\displaystyle f(\lambda)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t-1},1^{s-z+\lambda_{t}};1^{z}).

At first, let us notice that
\lambda_{t}
exists as a consequence of the fact that
s-z<a_{3}+1\leqslant w
. Thus, it follows that
\lambda_{t}\geqslant a_{3}
. Now, we suppose that for the partitions
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t+s}\right)=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t},1^{s}\right)
and
\mu=\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{\tilde{t}+\tilde{s}}\right)=\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{\tilde{t}},1^{\tilde{s}}\right)
(which fulfill the conditions

and

, where a tilde indicates corresponding parameters for the partition

\displaystyle f(\lambda)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t-1},1^{s-z+\lambda_{t}};1^{z})=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\tilde{t}-1},1^{\tilde{s}-z+\mu_{\tilde{t}}};1^{z})=f(\mu).

If that is the case, then

and
\lambda_{m}=\mu_{m}
for every
1\leqslant m\leqslant t-1
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle s-z+\lambda_{t}=\tilde{s}-z+\mu_{t}.

Without loss of generality we can assume that

. Thus, we get

\displaystyle a_{k}-a_{l}=\lambda_{t}-\mu_{t}=(\tilde{s}-z)-(s-z)<a_{3}.

Now, it follows from the assumptions in the statement of Theorem
3.1
that

and

It should be also pointed out that Case

appears more than

times as a part in

what is impossible in Case

Remark 3.2
.

For
A=\{a_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
with
a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
, it seems that one needs to demand that
a_{1}=1
in Theorem
3.1
. Nevertheless, let us suppose that
a_{1}>1
,
a_{1}|a_{2}
and
a_{1}|a_{3}
. Dividing
A
by
a_{1}
leads us to
\tilde{A}=\{\tilde{a}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}=\{a_{i}/a_{1}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
, where
\tilde{a}_{1}=1
and
\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{3}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 2}
. Since we only demand that
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
are integers in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we may apply that result to the set
\tilde{A}
if the condition
\tilde{a}_{k}-\tilde{a}_{l}\geqslant\tilde{a}_{3}
holds for every
k>l\geqslant 3
.

At the first glance, it may be not clear how one can apply Theorem
3.1
to obtain some result concerning the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality. However, the idea is uncomplicated, as the succeeding proof exhibits.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
w,z\geqslant a_{3}
. We prove the statement by induction on
w+z
. The correctness of the claim for
2a_{3}\leqslant w+z\leqslant 2(a_{3}+a_{2})-1
follows directly from the assumptions. Thus, let us suppose that it is true for every
2a_{3}\leqslant w+z\leqslant s
for some
s\geqslant 2(a_{3}+a_{2})-1
, and examine the validity of the statement for
w+z=s+1
. Without loss of generality we may require that
w\geqslant z\geqslant a_{3}
. Since
w+z\geqslant 2(a_{3}+a_{2})
and
w\geqslant z
, we have that
w\geqslant a_{3}+a_{2}
. Therefore, it follows that
w-a_{2}\geqslant a_{3}
and

\displaystyle p_{A}(w+z)

\displaystyle=p_{A}(w+z-a_{2})+p_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})

\displaystyle<p_{A}(w-a_{2})p_{A}(z)+p_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{A}(w-a_{2})p_{A}(z)+p_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})p_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=(p_{A}(w-a_{2})+p_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s}))p_{A}(z)=p_{A}(w)p_{A}(z),

where the first inequality is a consequence of the induction hypothesis while the second one is a direct application of Theorem
3.1
and the assumption that
2a_{2}\leqslant a_{3}
. This completes the proof by the law of induction.
∎

In the forthcoming sections, we illustrate that both Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
3.1
might be applied for various
A
-partition functions. However, it is visible and quite unfortunate that we can not use them to solve the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the classical partition function
p(n)
.

4.
The Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the
m
-ary partition function

Throughout this section, we assume that
A=\{m^{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where
m\geqslant 2
is an integer. In other words, we consider the well-known
m
-ary partition function
b_{m}(n)=p_{A}(n)
defined in Introduction. Our first aim is to examine the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality for
b_{m}(n)
of the form

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)>b_{m}(w+z),

where
w
and
z
are arbitrary positive integers. We solve this problem in a few steps depending on these two parameters.

At first, let us require that
w<m
and
z=km+l
for some non-negative integers
k
and
l
with
0\leqslant l\leqslant m-1
. Since
b_{m}(xm+y)=b_{m}(xm)
for every non-negative integer
x
and
0\leqslant y\leqslant m-1
, it follows that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)=b_{m}(w)b_{m}(km)\leqslant b_{m}(km+l+w)=b_{m}(w+z)

with the equality whenever
0\leqslant w+l\leqslant m-1
.

Now, let us suppose that
m\leqslant w\leqslant 2m-1
. In this case, we have the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
m\leqslant w\leqslant 2m-1
and
z=km+l
for some positive integer
k
and
0\leqslant l\leqslant m-1
. If
k\geqslant 5m
, then

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)>b_{m}(w+z).

Proof.

Let us assume that
k,l,w
and
z
are as in the statement. In this proof, we use two well-known identities:

(4.1)

\displaystyle b_{m}(xm+y)=b_{m}(xm)

and

(4.2)

\displaystyle b_{m}(xm)=b_{m}((x-1)m)+b_{m}(x),

which are valid for all positive integers
x
and
y
with
0\leqslant y<m
. Thus, it turns out that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w+z)=b_{m}(km+l+w)\leqslant b_{m}((k+2)m)=b_{m}(k+2)+b_{m}(k+1)+b_{m}(km)

and

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)=2b_{m}(km).

Hence, it is enough to solve the inequality

\displaystyle b_{m}(z)=b_{m}(km)>b_{m}(k+2)+b_{m}(k+1).

Let us assume that
k=dm+j
for some non-negative integers
d
and
j
such that
d\geqslant 5
and
0\leqslant j<m
. Applying both (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we get that

\displaystyle b_{m}(z)=b_{m}(k)+b_{m}(k-1)+b_{m}(k-2)+b_{m}(k-3)+b_{m}(k-4)+b_{m}((k-5)m)

and

\displaystyle b_{m}(k+2)+b_{m}(k+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2b_{m}(k+1)+b_{m}(d+1)\leqslant 2b_{m}(k)+3b_{m}(d+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant b_{m}(k)+b_{m}(k-1)+b_{m}(d)+3b_{m}(d+1).

In consequence, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle b_{m}(k-2)+b_{m}(k-3)+b_{m}(k-4)+b_{m}((k-5)m)>b_{m}(d)+3b_{m}(d+1)

Since
b_{m}((k-5)m)>b_{m}(k-5)
, it is enough to examine the validity of
k-5\geqslant d
, but it is an easy exercise.
∎

Lemma
4.1
allows us to restrict our consideration to the case when
m\leqslant z<5m^{2}
. Since we still have infinitely many possibilities to choose
m
and
z
, an additional auxiliary lemma is needed.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
m\geqslant 4
,
m\leqslant w\leqslant 2m-1
and
z=km+l
for some integers
k
and
l
with
1\leqslant k\leqslant 5m-1
and
0\leqslant l<m
. For
k=1
, we have that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)\geqslant b_{m}(w+z)

with the equality whenever
w+z\geqslant 3m
.

If
k\geqslant 2
and
m\geqslant 6
, then

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)>b_{m}(w+z).

Proof.

Let us assume that all the parameters are as in the claim. One can easily check that the statement is true for
k=1,2
. Therefore, let us demand that
k\geqslant 3
. It follows that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)=2b_{m}(km)

and

\displaystyle b_{m}(w+z)\leqslant b_{m}((k+2)m)=b_{m}(k+2)+b_{m}(k+1)+b_{m}(km).

Since
m\geqslant 6
, we obtain that
k+1<k+2\leqslant 5m+1<m^{2}
. Hence, it is not difficult to observe that

\displaystyle b_{m}(k+2)+b_{m}(k+1)<\frac{k+2}{m}+1+\frac{k+1}{m}+1\hskip 5.69046pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69046ptb_{m}(km)\geqslant k+1.

Thus, it suffices to show that the following

\displaystyle k+1>\frac{k+2}{m}+\frac{k+1}{m}+2

is true, which is an elementary exercise.
∎

Now, we can check all the exceptions in the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for
m\leqslant 5
,
m\leqslant w<2m
and
m\leqslant z<5m^{2}
one by one. However, we omit that task here, because the complete list of the exceptions will be given later (see, Table 1). In the meantime, we proceed to the next case.

At this point, we require that both
w,z\geqslant 2m
. In such a setting the following property is true.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
m\geqslant 4
be fixed. For every
w,z\geqslant 2m
with
w+z\leqslant 2m^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)>b_{m}(w+z).

Proof.

Let us write
w=cm+j
and
z=dm+i
for some positive integers
d\geqslant c\geqslant 2
and
0\leqslant i,j<m
. At first, we consider the case when
c=d=2
. Since
m\geqslant 4
, it follows that

\displaystyle b_{m}(2m+i)b_{m}(2m+j)=9

and

\displaystyle b_{m}(4m+i+j)\leqslant b_{m}(5m)\leqslant b_{4}(20)=8.

Therefore, we can assume that
d\geqslant 3
. Let us deal with the case when
w+z<2m^{2}
. We have that
c\leqslant m-1
, and it is clear that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)=(c+1)b_{m}(dm).

On the other hand, the identities (
4.1
) and (

) assert that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w+z)\leqslant b_{m}((c+d+1)m)=b_{m}(d+c+1)+\cdots+b_{m}(d+1)+b_{m}(dm)

for

. Analogously, we have that

\displaystyle b_{m}(w+z)\leqslant b_{m}((c+d)m)=b_{m}(d+c)+\cdots+b_{m}(d+1)+b_{m}(dm)

for
c+d=2m-1
. Hence, it is transparent that the following inequalities

\displaystyle cb_{m}(dm)\geqslant c(d+1)\geqslant 4c>2(c+1),

hold, as required.

Next, let us suppose that
w+z=2m^{2}
. If
w=z=m^{2}
, then

\displaystyle b_{m}(m^{2})b_{m}(m^{2})=(m+2)^{2}>3m+3=b_{m}(2m^{2}).

Therefore, we consider the case when
w<m^{2}
and
z>m^{2}
. We get that

\displaystyle b_{m}(cm+j)b_{m}(dm+i)=(c+1)(2d-m+2)\geqslant(c+1)(m+2),

where the equality follows from the fact that we can either take
m^{2}
as a part of

or not. Finally, we have

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)\geqslant(c+1)(m+2)\geqslant 3(m+2)>3m+3=b_{m}(2m^{2}).

This completes the proof.

Sketch of the second proof of Theorem

.

Let

and

for some non-negative integers

with

) and (

\displaystyle b_{m}(w)b_{m}(z)=b_{m}(cm)b_{m}(dm)

and

\displaystyle b_{m}(w+z)\leqslant b_{m}((c+d+1)m)

\displaystyle=b_{m}(c+d+1)+\cdots+b_{m}(d+1)+b_{m}(dm)

\displaystyle\leqslant(c+1)b_{m}(2d+1)+b_{m}(dm).

\displaystyle(b_{m}(cm)-1)b_{m}(dm)>(c+1)b_{m}(2d+1).

\displaystyle\begin{cases}b_{m}(cm)-1&>c+1\\

b_{2}(2d)=b_{2}(2d+1)

–

and

.

4.4
to the extended

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
m\geqslant 2
. If
m\geqslant 3
, then the maximal value
\max{b_{m}(n)}
of the

is attained at the partition

\displaystyle\left(m^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor},1^{n-m\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor}\right).

For
m=2
, it is attained at the partitions of the form

\displaystyle\left(4^{i},2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-2i},1^{n-2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil}\right),

where
0\leqslant i\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor
.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\max{b_{m}(n)}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m}\right\rfloor}

for every
m\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

At first, let us notice that
b_{m}(m)=2
,
b_{m}(m^{2})=m+2
and
b_{m}(m^{3})=(m^{2}+4)(m+1)/2-m
. Thus, it is clear that
b_{2}(2)b_{2}(2)=b_{2}(4)
,
b_{2}(4)b_{2}(4)>b_{2}(8)
and
b_{m}^{m}(m^{i-1})>b_{m}(m^{i})
for
m\geqslant 3
and
i=2,3
. Next, using Theorem
4.4
we may write

\displaystyle b_{m}(m^{k})

\displaystyle=b_{m}((m-1)m^{k-1}+m^{k-1})<b_{m}((m-1)m^{k-1})b_{m}(m^{k-1})

\displaystyle=b_{m}((m-2)m^{k-1}+m^{k-1})b_{m}(m^{k-1})<b_{m}((m-2)m^{k-1})b_{m}^{2}(m^{k-1})

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=b_{m}([m-(m-1)]m^{k-1}+m^{k-1})b_{m}^{m-2}(m^{k-1})<b_{m}^{m}(m^{k-1})

for every
k\geqslant 4
. Therefore, it follows from the induction that
b_{m}^{m}(m^{k-1})>b_{m}(m^{k})
for all
m\geqslant 2
and
k\geqslant 3
. Now, the first part of the statement is clear. The second one, on the other hand, is a straightforward consequence of elementary computations.
∎

5.
The Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the power partition function

In this section, we deal with the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the (
d
-th) power partition function
p_{d}(n):=p_{A_{d}}(n)
, where
A_{d}=\{n^{d}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 2}
.

At the beginning, let us observe that
4^{2}-3^{2}<3^{2}
. Thus, we can not simply apply Theorem
1.4
(or Theorem
3.1
) to attack the problem in general. On the other hand, if
d\geqslant 3
and
k>l\geqslant 3
, then
k^{d}-l^{d}>(k-l)dl^{d-1}\geqslant 3^{d}.
Therefore, it follows that Theorem
1.4
might be used when
d\geqslant 3
. For the sake of completeness, we investigate the Bessenrtodt-Ono inequality for
p_{A_{2}}(n)
separately by applying the following analogue of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
A=\{1,a_{2},a_{3},\ldots\}
be an arbitrary (finite or infinite) set of positive integers with
1<a_{2}<a_{3}<\cdots
such that
a_{k}-a_{l}\geqslant a_{3}
for every
k>l\geqslant 4
. Then, the inequality

\displaystyle p_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})p_{A}(z)\geqslant p_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})

holds for all positive integers
w\geqslant a_{3}+1
and
z\geqslant 3a_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
A,w,z
be as in the statement. Similarly to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we construct an injective map

\displaystyle g:P_{A}(w+z|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})\to P_{A}(w|\text{ no }a_{2}\text{'s})\oplus P_{A}(z)

by setting

\displaystyle g(\lambda)=\begin{cases}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{t-1},1^{s-z+\lambda_{t}};a_{2}^{2},1^{z-2a_{2}}),&\text{ if }y=0,z<s\leqslant z+a_{3},\lambda_{t}\geqslant a_{4},\\
f(\lambda),&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where all the notation comes from the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

It is not difficult to show that the map
g(\lambda)
is well-defined and injective.
The details are left to the reader.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
be fixed. For all positive integers
w,z\geqslant 3^{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(w)p_{A_{d}}(z)>p_{A_{d}}(w+z).

If
d=2
, then the above is valid for every
w,z\geqslant 12
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
d\geqslant 3
. At first, we demand that
w,z\geqslant 3^{d}
and
2\cdot 3^{d}\leqslant w+z\leqslant 2(3^{d}+2^{d})-1
. We have that

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(w)p_{A_{d}}(z)\geqslant\left(1+\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right)\left(1+\frac{z}{2^{d}}\right)\geqslant\left(1+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, one can derive that

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(w+z)\leqslant p_{A_{d}}(2(3^{d}+2^{d}))\leqslant 6+\frac{3^{d}+2^{d+1}}{2^{d}}+\frac{3^{d}+2^{d}}{2^{d-1}}=10+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d-1}},

where the last inequality follows from elementary computations and the observation that
4^{d}
might occur as a part of
2(3^{d}+2^{d})
(for
d=3
). Hence, it suffices to check the validity of

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right)^{2}>10+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}+\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d-1}},

or equivalently of

\displaystyle u^{2}-u-9>0,

where
u=(3/2)^{d}
. It is easy to solve the above problem and verify that the required property holds whenever
d\geqslant 4
. For
d=3
, one can check one by one that

\displaystyle p_{A_{3}}(w)p_{A_{3}}(z)>p_{A_{3}}(w+z)

is satisfied for all
2\cdot 3^{3}\leqslant w+z\leqslant 2(3^{3}+2^{3})-1
, as required.
Therefore, Theorem
1.4
completes the proof for every
d\geqslant 3
.

In order to show the property for
d=2
, one can just exhibit a similar reasoning to the above one and apply Theorem
5.1
instead of Theorem
1.4
. The details are left to the reader.
∎

As a consequence of the Bessenrodt-Ono inequality for the power partition function
p_{A_{d}}(n)
, we get an analogue of Theorem
4.5
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
. If
d\geqslant 3
, then the maximal value
\max{p_{A_{d}}(n)}
of the power partition function on
P_{A_{d}}(n)
is attained at the partition

\displaystyle\left((2^{d})^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor},1^{n-2^{d}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}\right).

For
d=2
, it is attained at the partitions of the form

\displaystyle(4^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor},1^{n-4\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor}),\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 0,1,2,3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4),

\displaystyle(9,4^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-9}{4}\right\rfloor},1^{n-4\left\lfloor\frac{n-9}{4}\right\rfloor-9}),\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 1,2,3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)\text{ and }n\geqslant 9,

\displaystyle(9^{2},4^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-18}{4}\right\rfloor},1^{n-4\left\lfloor\frac{n-18}{4}\right\rfloor-18}),\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 2,3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)\text{ and }n\geqslant 18,

\displaystyle(9^{3},4^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-27}{4}\right\rfloor},1^{n-4\left\lfloor\frac{n-27}{4}\right\rfloor-27}),\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)\text{ and }n\geqslant 27.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\max{p_{A_{d}}(n)}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}

for every
d\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

At first, let us notice that
p_{A_{d}}(2^{d})=2
and
p_{A_{d}}(3^{d})=\left\lfloor\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+2
.
Thus, if we consider the extended power partition function and have a partition
\lambda
witha part, let say,
\lambda_{i}=3^{d}
, then we may replace that part by
\left\lfloor\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor
parts equal to
2^{d}
and
3^{d}-2^{d}\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor
ones. It is not difficult to verify that
2^{\left\lfloor\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+2
for every
d\geqslant 2
(with the equality if and only if
d=2
). Therefore, we see that after sucha transformation the value of the extended power partition function grows for
d\geqslant 3
or remains the same if
d=2
.

Now, let us demand that
d\geqslant 3
and
n\geqslant 4
. Theorem
5.2
asserts that

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(n^{d})

\displaystyle=p_{A_{d}}(n^{d}-3^{d}+3^{d})<p_{A_{d}}(n^{d}-3^{d})p_{A_{d}}(3^{d})

\displaystyle=p_{A_{d}}(n^{d}-2\cdot 3^{d}+3^{d})p_{A_{d}}(3^{d})<p_{A_{d}}(n^{d}-2\cdot 3^{d})p_{A_{d}}^{2}(3^{d})

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=p_{A_{d}}\left(n^{d}-3^{d}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-1\right)+3^{d}\right)p_{A_{d}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-2}(3^{d})

\displaystyle<p_{A_{d}}\left(n^{d}-3^{d}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-1\right)\right)p_{A_{d}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-1}(3^{d}).

Next, we can put

\displaystyle w:=n^{d}-3^{d}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-1\right),

and observe that
3^{d}\leqslant w\leqslant 2\cdot 3^{d}-1
. Hence, it turns out that

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(w)=\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{w-3^{d}}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+2<2\left(\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+1\right).

However, it is easy to check that the inequality

\displaystyle 2^{\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}\geqslant 2\left(\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor+1\right)

holds for every
d\geqslant 3
. Hence, we get that
p_{A_{d}}(w)<p_{A_{d}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}(2^{d})
and

\displaystyle p_{A_{d}}(n^{d})<p_{A_{d}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2^{d}}\right\rfloor}(2^{d})p_{A_{d}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n^{d}}{3^{d}}\right\rfloor-1}(3^{d}).

The commentary from the beginning completes the proof for
d\geqslant 3
.

Finally, we deal with the case when
d=2
. The first few sentences of the proof ensure that

p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4,4)})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4,4,1)})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9)})=4.

Furthermore, the similar equality holds if the part
9
occurs two or three times. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{4})})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{4},1^{2})})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9^{2})})=2^{4}

and

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{6})})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{6},1^{3})})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9^{3})})=2^{6}.

However, the part
9
can not occur more than three times. Indeed, if there are four nines, then

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{9})})=2^{9}>p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9^{4})})=2^{8}.

Now, let us suppose that
n=4s
for some positive integer
s\geqslant 3
. It follows that

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{s})})=2^{s}>2^{s-1}=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9,4^{s-3},1^{3})}).

Further, if
n=4s+1
for some
s\geqslant 2
, then

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{s},1)})=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9,4^{s-2})})=2^{s}>2^{s-1}\geqslant p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(9^{2},4^{s-5},1^{3})}).

One can make similar computations for
n\equiv 2,3\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)
to deduce that the partitions from the statement might be the appropriate candidates for the maximal value
\max{p_{A_{2}}(n)}
of the (
2
nd) power partition function on
P_{A_{2}}(n)
.

Next, let us assume that
n
is arbitrary and
\lambda
is a partition of
n
with at least one part equals
16
. Observe that

p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(16)})=8<16=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{4})})
. Hence, if we replace the part
16
by four parts equal
4
, then the value of the extended power partition function (for
d=2
) becomes larger. Similarly, one can easily check that
p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(25)})=19<32=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(16,4^{2},1)})<64=p_{A_{2}}(\bm{(4^{6},1)})
. Thus, let us suppose that
l\geqslant 6
and
l^{2}
occurs as a part of a partition of
n
. Once again, Theorem
5.2
ensures that

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(l^{2})

\displaystyle=p_{A_{2}}(l^{2}-4^{2}+4^{2})<p_{A_{2}}(l^{2}-4^{2})p_{A_{2}}(4^{2})

\displaystyle=p_{A_{2}}(l^{2}-2\cdot 4^{2}+4^{2})p_{A_{2}}(4^{2})<p_{A_{2}}(l^{2}-2\cdot 4^{2})p_{A_{2}}^{2}(4^{2})

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=p_{A_{2}}\left(l^{2}-4^{2}\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{l^{2}}{4^{2}}\right\rfloor-1\right)+4^{2}\right)p_{A_{2}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{l^{2}}{4^{2}}\right\rfloor-2}(4^{2})

\displaystyle<p_{A_{2}}\left(l^{2}-4^{2}\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{l^{2}}{4^{2}}\right\rfloor-1\right)\right)p_{A_{2}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{l^{2}}{4^{2}}\right\rfloor-1}(4^{2}).

Now, one can make some numerical calculations to check the values of
p_{A_{2}}(n)
and to verify the validity of the statement for every
1\leqslant n\leqslant 31
. If we do so, then it follows that

\displaystyle p_{A_{2}}(l^{2})<p_{A_{2}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{l^{2}}{2^{2}}\right\rfloor}(2^{2}),

as required. This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Concluding Remarks

At the end of this paper, we present two additional examples in order to illustrate the usefulness of both Theorem
1.4
(
3.1
) and Proposition
5.1
and encourage the reader to investigate the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequalities for other
A
-partition functions.

The first instance reefers to the well-known Fibonacci numbers. More precisely, let us set
A=F:=\{1,2,3,5,8,13,\ldots\}=\{F_{n}:n\geqslant 2\}
and examine the multiplicative property for the Fibonacci partition function
p_{F}(n)
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
F=\{F_{n}:n\geqslant 2\}
. For every
a,b\geqslant 6
, we have that

p_{F}(a)p_{F}(b)>p_{F}(a+b).

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
a+b
, where
a,b\geqslant 6
. At first, we verify the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality one by one for all the values
12\leqslant a+b\leqslant 15
with
a,b\geqslant 6
. Further, it is convenient to assume that
a\geqslant b
and that the statement is true for every
a+b=12,\ldots,s-1
for some
s\geqslant 16
. For
a+b=s
, we just have

\displaystyle p_{F}(a+b)

\displaystyle=p_{F}(a+b-2)+p_{F}(a+b|\text{ no }2\text{'s})

\displaystyle<p_{F}(a-2)p_{F}(b)+p_{F}(a|\text{ no }2\text{'s})p_{F}(b)=p_{F}(a)p_{F}(b),

where the inequality is a consequence of both Proposition
5.1
and the induction hypothesis.
∎

In consequence, we obtain the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

The maximal value
\max{p_{F}(n)}
of the Fibonacci partition function on
P_{F}(n)
is attained at the partitions of the form

\displaystyle(3^{\frac{n}{3}}),\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 0\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3),

\displaystyle(3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-1},2^{2})\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3),

\displaystyle(5,3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-2},2)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3)\text{ and }n\geqslant 7,

\displaystyle(5^{2},3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-3})\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3)\text{ and }n\geqslant 10,

\displaystyle(3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor},2)\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 2\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3),

\displaystyle(5,3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-1})\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 2\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3)\text{ and }n\geqslant 5.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\max{p_{F}(n)}=\begin{cases}3^{\frac{n}{3}}\hskip 5.69046pt&\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 0\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3),\\
2^{2}\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor-1}\hskip 5.69046pt&\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3),\\
2\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor}\hskip 5.69046pt&\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptn\equiv 2\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3).\end{cases}

Proof.

At first, we make numerical calculations to check the validity of the statement for
n\leqslant 16
. Let us notice that
p_{F}(5)=p_{F}(3)p_{F}(2)=6
.

Next, considering all possible residue class of
n\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu3)
, one can easy verify that the values of the extended Fibonacci partition function agrees on the appropriate partitions in the statement.

Let us assume that
n\geqslant 17
. If we have a partition of
n
with three parts equal to
5
, then we can replace them by five threes:

\displaystyle p_{F}(\bm{(5^{3})})=6^{3}<3^{5}=p_{F}(\bm{(3^{5})}).

The analogous argument shows that
2
also can not appear more than two times as a part of
n
. It is also worth noting that if we consider the maximum value, then neither
8
nor
13
may occur as a part of
n
— that is because of

\displaystyle p_{F}(8)=14<18=\max{p_{F}(8)}=p_{F}(\bm{(5,3)})=p_{F}(\bm{(3^{2},2)})

and

\displaystyle p_{F}(13)=41<108=\max{p_{F}(13)}=p_{F}(\bm{(5^{2},3)})=p_{F}(\bm{(5,3^{2},2)})=p_{F}(\bm{(3^{3},2^{2})}).

Suppose further that
F_{l}
for some
l\geqslant 8
occurs as a part of
n
. By Theorem
6.1
, we get that

\displaystyle p_{F}(F_{l})=p_{F}(F_{l}-8+8)

\displaystyle<p_{F}(F_{l}-8)p_{F}(8)<p_{F}(F_{l}-2\cdot 8)p_{F}^{2}(8)

\displaystyle<\cdots<p_{F}\left((F_{l}-8\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{F_{l}}{8}\right\rfloor-1\right)\right)p_{F}^{\left(\left\lfloor\frac{F_{l}}{8}\right\rfloor-1\right)}(8).

After checking the validity of the statement and calculating the values of
p_{F}(n)
for
n\leqslant 16
, we conclude the required property.
∎

Our last example of the Bessenrodt-Ono type inequality is related to a slightly more exotic
A
-partition function. Actually, we put
A=N:=\{n!:n\geqslant 1\}
and examine the property for, let say, the ‘factorial’ partition function. Since we have already applied Proposition
5.1
to establish Theorem
6.1
, let us now use Theorem
1.4
to prove the following.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
N=\{n!:n\geqslant 1\}
. For every
a,b\geqslant 6
, we have that

p_{N}(a)p_{N}(b)>p_{N}(a+b).

Proof.

It is enough the check the correctness of the claim for all
a,b\geqslant 6
with
12\leqslant a+b\leqslant 15
. If we do so, then Theorem
1.4
completes the proof.
∎

As a direct application of the above property, we obtain the following.

Theorem 6.4
.

The maximal value
\max{p_{N}(n)}
of the factorial partition function on
P_{N}(n)
is attained at the partition

\displaystyle(2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor},1^{n-2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}).

Moreover, we get

\displaystyle\max{p_{N}(n)}=2^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}.

Proof.

We leave the proof as an exercise for the reader.
∎

At the end of this manuscript, let us state a conjecture which arises from many works concerning Bessenrodt-Ono type inequalities, especially from our investigation and Gajdzica’s recent paper
[
14
]
.

Conjecture 6.5
.

Let
A
be an arbitrary set of positive integers with
\gcd A=1
and
\#A\geqslant 2
. For all sufficiently large values of
a
and
b
, we have

\displaystyle p_{A}(a)p_{A}(b)>p_{A}(a+b).
