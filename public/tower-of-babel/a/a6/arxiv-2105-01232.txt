Integration and stochastic integration in Gaussian multiplicative chaos
Isao Sauzedde

Abstract
. We show that for γ < 4 / 3 \gamma<\sqrt{4/3} , it is possible to define the Lévy area of a planar Brownian motion with the Liouville measure of intermittency parameter γ \gamma as the underlying area measure. We also consider the case of smoother curves, and study some properties of the integration map thus defined.



Introduction

In a previous paper
[
13
]
, we showed a Green formula for
\alpha
-Hölder continuous planar curves (with
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
) and for the planar Brownian motion. It allows us to compute the Young or Stratonovich integral of a smooth differential
1
-form
\eta
along the curve
X
as the surface integral of the winding function
\theta_{X}
against the
2
-form
{\rm d}\eta
. In the Brownian case, the surface integral has be interpreted through a principal value method.

In the present paper, we look at the same integrals but with the smooth
2
-form
{\rm d}\eta
replaced with a random measure, the Liouville measure of parameter
\gamma
. For a planar Brownian motion independent of the Liouville measure, we show that, for
\gamma<\sqrt{4/3}
, the principal value is still well defined and lies in
L^{2}
of the product probability space. We also prove continuity, Hölder continuity, and additivity (‘Chen’s relation’) of the integral thus defined. For the case of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous curves, we show similar results, provided
\gamma
is small enough and
\alpha
is large enough.

For a continuous curve
X:[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
\bar{X}
the concatenation of
X
with the straight line segment from
X_{t}
to
X_{s}
. This is an oriented loop, and for all point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Range(\bar{X})
, we denote by
\theta_{X}(z)\in\mathbb{Z}
the winding of this oriented loop
\bar{X}
around the point
z
. Provided
X
is a smooth curve, Green’s theorem is easily seen to implies that for all smooth
1
-form
\eta
,

(1)

\int_{\bar{X}}\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\theta_{X}\;{\rm d}\eta.

In
[
13
]
, we showed that this equality extends to all
\alpha
-Hölder continuous curves
X
with
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. For this, the left-hand side must be interpreted as a Young integral. We further proved that a similar equality also holds for the Brownian motion (in the particular case
\eta=x\;{\rm d}y
, but we can deduce the general case with a few extra work), provided we interpret the left-hand side as a Stratonovich integral and the right-hand side as a kind of principal value. To be more specific, and using superscripts to indicates the coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and denoting the Lebesgue measure by
\lambda
, one has almost surely

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}X^{1}\circ{\rm d}X^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}((1-r)X_{1}+rX_{0})^{1}(X_{0}-X_{1})^{2}\;{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\theta_{X}(z)|\leq K\}}\,\theta_{X}(z)\;{\rm d}\lambda(z)

(2)

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt=\lim_{K\to+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\max(-K,\min(\theta_{X}(z),K))\;{\rm d}\lambda(z).

The goal of this paper is to study the right-hand sides of (
1
) and (
2
), but with
\!\;{\rm d}\eta
(resp.
{\rm d}z
) replaced with a Gaussian multiplicative chaos, that we denote by
\mathsf{M}
.

In this paper,
\mathsf{M}
will be a random measure heuristically described by the formula

(3)

\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\qquad\mathsf{M}(A)=\int_{A}\exp\big(\gamma\Phi_{z}-\tfrac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{z}^{2}]\big)\;{\rm d}\lambda(z),

where
\Phi
is a centered Gaussian field, and
\gamma\in[0,2)
. It is assumed that the covariance kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
of
\Phi
takes the form

K(z,w)=\log_{+}(|z-w|^{-1})+g(z,w),

where
\log_{+}
is the positive part of the logarithm and
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function
with bounded derivatives up to order
2
. The kernel
K
is well defined, as well as the random field
\Phi
, even though the logarithmic divergence of
K
makes
\Phi
a random distribution rather than a random function. However, precisely because
\Phi
is a random distribution, (
3
) does not make sense, and the construction of
\mathsf{M}
cannot rely on this formula. The first construction of a random measure that is a reasonable candidate to be a mathematical incarnation of (
3
) was given using the theory of multiplicative chaos by Kahane
[
9
]
. For an introduction to Gaussian multiplicative chaos, see also
[
11
]
and
[
2
]
. In general, the larger
\gamma
, the more irregular the measure. Most of the time in this paper, we will be working in the case where
\gamma<\sqrt{2}
. We will thus be in the so-called ‘
L^{2}
-phase’, in which it is relatively easy to define and to study
\mathsf{M}
, using martingale methods and Hilbertian techniques.

For the reader more familiar with multiplicative chaos, let us mention that the condition
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
is not necessary to define the measure
\mathsf{M}
, nor to study the integral along Hölder paths, but it is a practical one when we look at the integral along Brownian paths.

We will denote by
(\Omega^{\mathsf{M}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{M}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{M}})
the probability space on which
\mathsf{M}
is defined. We also let
(\Omega^{X},\mathcal{F}^{X},\mathbb{P}^{X})
be a second probability space, on which a planar Brownian motion
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is defined. Finally, we denote by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

the product probability space.

The first part of this paper (Sections 1 to 5) is dedicated to the proof of the following result. Let us emphasize that we stop the Brownian motion at time
1
.

Assume

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\max(-K,\min(\theta_{X}(z),K))\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}(z)

admits a limit

as

.

Moreover, for all

1.
Strategy of the proof of Theorem 1

Let us recall that
X:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a planar Brownian motion defined on
(\Omega^{X},\mathcal{F}^{X},\mathbb{P}^{X})
, and
\theta_{X}
is the winding function associated with
X
. The random measure
\mathsf{M}
is defined on
(\Omega^{\mathsf{M}},\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{M}},\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{M}})
, and is formally given by
\mathsf{M}=e^{\gamma\phi-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\phi^{2}]}\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure and
\phi
is a centered Gaussian field on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with covariance kernel
K:(z,w)\mapsto\log_{+}(|z-w|)+g(z,w)
,
for a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function
g
with bounded derivatives up to order
2
. From now on and until Section 5 included, we will assume that
\gamma<\sqrt{4/3}
.
We are looking at the existence of a limit, as
K\to+\infty
, of the integral with ‘cut-off’

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\max(-K,\min(\theta_{X}(z),K))\;{\rm d}z.

Since
\theta_{X}
takes its values in
\mathbb{Z}
, we can rewrite this integral
as

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}\max(-K,\min(n,K))\mathsf{M}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{X}(z)=n\}),

which is more conveniently written

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\min(n,K)\big(\mathsf{M}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{X}(z)=n\})-\mathsf{M}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{X}(z)=-n\})\big).

Performing a summation by parts, this is finally equal to

\sum_{N=1}^{K}\big(\mathsf{M}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{X}(z)\geq N\})-\mathsf{M}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{X}(z)\leq-N\})\big).

This is the expression we will study. We will show that the general term goes to zero sufficiently fast for the sum to be convergent. Consequently, for
N\geq 1
, we define the following sets:

(7)

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm Range}(\bar{X}):\theta_{X}(z)\geq N\},\ \ \mathcal{D}_{-N}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\rm Range}(\bar{X}):\theta_{X}(z)\leq-N\}.

For a random variable
Z
on
\Omega
, we set

\|Z\|_{p,q}=\|Z\|_{L^{p}(\Omega^{X},L^{q}(\Omega^{\mathsf{M}}))}=\mathbb{E}^{X}[\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[Z^{q}]^{\frac{p}{q}}]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Theorem
1
is then a direct consequence of the following estimation.

Proposition 1.1
.

For all
p\in[2,\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}})
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and
C
such that for all
N
,

\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}\leq CN^{-1-\varepsilon}.

Let us remark that this is only possible thanks to the compensation between
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
and
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})
. Indeed, each of these sets has a Lebesgue measure which is equivalent as
N
tends ton infinity (in
L^{q}
for all
q
, and in the almost sure sense) to
\frac{1}{2\pi N}
, so that even
\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})\|_{p,1}
is two large for the sum to be convergent.
The fact that a compensation occurs can be informally understood as a consequence of the fact that the sets
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and
\mathcal{D}_{-N}
are extremely similar when
N
is large. For a better understanding of the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, we refer to our article
[
14
]
.

The strategy for the proof of Proposition
1.1
is similar to the one we used in
[
13
]
(Sections 3.2 and 3.3), and then in
[
14
]
. In short, we will decompose the quantity
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
into a sum of smaller quantities, corresponding to contributions of pieces of the Brownian trajectory.

To this end, we fix some
t>0
, which should think of as being very small. For a positive integer
N
, we set
T=\lfloor N^{t}\rfloor
.
For
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, we define
X^{i}
to be the restriction of
X
to
[(i-1)T^{-1},iT^{-1}]
,
\bar{X}^{i}
the concatenation of
X^{i}
with the segment between its endpoints,
and
\theta^{i}(z)
the number of times
\bar{X}^{i}
winds around
z
. We set

(8)

\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Range(\bar{X}^{i}):\theta^{i}(z)\geq N\},\ \ \mathcal{D}^{i}_{-N}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Range(\bar{X}^{i}):\theta^{i}(z)\leq-N\}.

Finally, we define

(9)

\mathsf{M}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N}).

This random variable should be thought of as a proxy for
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
, when
N
is large. To control the difference between
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
and
\mathsf{M}_{N}
will be the subject of Section
3
, in which we will prove the following bound.

Proposition

(
3.7
)
.

Let
p\in(1,+\infty)
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then, for
t
small enough, there exists
C
such that for all
N\geq 1
,

\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}_{N}\|_{p,2}\leq CN^{-\frac{3\nu}{4}+2t+\varepsilon}.

In the case where the random measure
\mathsf{M}
has a law invariant by translation, the variables
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N})
are identically distributed. If
\mathsf{M}
also has a nice behaviour under scaling, then these variables are distributed as a scaled version of
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
, and we end up with the informal relation

\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\simeq\sum_{i=1}^{T}(\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{-N}))=\sum_{i=1}^{T}f(T)Z_{i},

where the
Z_{i}
are random variables distributed as
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})
. Let us assume that we can bound nicely the scaling function
f(T)
, and show that the variables
Z_{i}
are not too strongly correlated. Then, inserting a bound on

\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})
on the right-hand side might lead to a
better
bound on
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})
. We refer to this as the

.
The control on the correlation takes the following form.

Notation.
The notation
f(N)\leq N^{a+o(1)}
means that for all
h>0
, there exists
C
such that for all
N\geq 1
,

.

Lemma

(
4.2
)
.

For
t,\varepsilon>0
, and

, let
F_{i,j}=\{|X_{(i+1)T^{-1}}-X_{jT^{-1}}|\geq T^{-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\}
.
For all
p\in\big[2,\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}})
, there exists a constant
C
such that for all
N\geq 1
,

(10)

\mathbb{E}^{X}\big[\big|\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}\big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{i,j}}(\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{-N})(\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{j}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{j}_{-N}))\big]\big|^{\frac{p}{2}}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
\leq C\log(N+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}T^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}N^{-1}\big(N^{-\frac{1}{4}+o(1)}+T^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{p}}\big).

The bootstrap then takes the following form. In the statement, there appears a set
\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{M})
of random measures that will be defined precisely in Section

look like

, in the sense that they are deduced from

by (possibly random) translations and symmetries. In many cases of interest, namely when

.

Lemma

(

)
.

Consider

. Assume that

and all

,

Then, for all

, there exists a constant

such that for all

,

\mathbb{E}^{X}\big[\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}\big[\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq T}\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbbm{1}_{F_{i,j}^{c}}|(\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{i}_{-N}))(\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{j}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}^{j}_{-N}))|\big]^{\frac{p}{2}}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C^{\prime}\log(T+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}T^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{p}+\varepsilon}N^{\zeta}.

2.
Three estimates about the Liouville measures

In this section, we assume that
\gamma<\sqrt{2}
. Recall that
\nu
is the positive real number

\nu=2-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}.

One of the advantages of working in the
L^{2}
-phase (that is, under the assumption
\gamma<\sqrt{2}
) is that one has the following explicit formula.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A,B
be two Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with finite Lebesgue measure. Then,

\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(A)\mathsf{M}(B)]=\int_{A\times B}\exp(\gamma^{2}K(z,w))\;{\rm d}z\;{\rm d}w.

Proof.

We first do an informal computation, which is entirely valid when the kernel
K
is replaced with a continuous kernel
\tilde{K}
(in which case the centered Gaussian field
\phi
with kernel
\tilde{K}
is defined pointwise):

Lemma 3.4

5.
Bootstrapping the bounds

Let us summarize what we did up to here. Our goal is to bound
\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}
. In Section
3
, we showed that, in this estimation,
\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})
can safely be replaced with
\mathsf{M}_{N}
, provided
t
is small and
\gamma<\sqrt{4/3}
. Then, we split
(\mathsf{M}_{N}-\mathsf{M}_{-N})^{2}

into a sum of two terms. Let us call them
\mathsf{M}_{\rm good}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\rm bad}
. The first one is a sum over ‘good terms’, and we have been able in Section

to show that this term is small, provided

.

What remains to be done is thus to control
\mathsf{M}_{\rm bad}
. Here is the place where the bootstrap really starts. In a perfect world, our dearest wish would be to have
\|\mathsf{M}_{\rm bad}\|_{p,2}<CN^{-1-h}
, for some
C
and

. This however is not what we will obtain in the first place. We will first give a bound on
\mathsf{M}_{\rm bad}
which is something like

\|\mathsf{M}_{\rm bad}\|_{p,2}<CN^{\xi}\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}.

It might seem at first that this approach is doomed to fail, because it seems that the problem is now to control
\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}
, which actually was the problem we started with. The crucial point is to obtain a
negative
exponent
\xi
: indeed, in that case, the relation

\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}\leq CN^{-1-\varepsilon}+CN^{\zeta}\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}

does imply

\|\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{N})-\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{D}_{-N})\|_{p,2}\leq C^{\prime}N^{-1-\varepsilon},

for a new constant
C^{\prime}
.

We will show a relation of the form (
26
), with
\xi=t(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{p})
. Let us recall that
\mathcal{T}(\mathsf{M})
is the set of random measures obtained from
\mathsf{M}
by translation by a random variable independent from
\mathsf{M}
, and possibly a symmetry with respect to the horizontal axis.

Let us also recall that the parameter

appears in the definition of the event

,

The work that we have done so far allowed us to define the ‘algebraic Liouville area enclosed by the Brownian curve’ as the

and

\int_{B(0,1)}\frac{|f|^{\rho}(w)}{|z-w|^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}}\;{\rm d}w\leq\|f\|^{\rho}_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\lambda)}\bigg(\int_{B(0,2)}\frac{1}{|w|^{q\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}}\;{\rm d}w\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

The latter integral is finite, and with another identical computation we end up with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|f|^{\rho}(z)}{|z-w|^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|f|^{\rho}(w)}{|z-w|^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}}\;{\rm d}w\bigg)\;{\rm d}z\leq C^{\prime}\|f\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\lambda)}^{2\rho},

hence

which concludes the proof.
∎

Recall that
\nu=2(1-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4})
.

7.
Weak Chen’s relation

We will need the following version of the Chen relation, where the quantifier on
s
,
u
and
t
and the
almost sure
has been exchanged. Let us recall from the introduction that
T_{s,u,t}
is the triangle delimited by
Z_{s}
,
Z_{u}
, and
Z_{t}
(the continuous function
Z:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is assumed to be fixed), and that
\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\in\{\pm 1\}
depends one the cyclic order between these points on the boundary of
T_{s,u,t}
.

Definition 7.1
.

Set
\Delta_{3}=\{(s,t,u):0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq 1\}
. We say that a collection
(\mathbb{A}_{s,t})_{(s,t)\in\Delta}
of random variables satisfies the
weak Chen relation

(
relative to the continuous function
Z
)
if for all
(s,u,t)\in\Delta_{3}
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

\mathbb{A}_{s,t}=\mathbb{A}_{s,u}+\mathbb{A}_{u,t}+\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\mathsf{M}(T_{s,u,t}).

Let us remark that, in contrast with the Chen relation, the weak Chen relation is preserved by the replacement of the family
\mathbb{A}
by one of its modifications (see Definition
8.1
).

Lemma 7.2
.

Under the relaxed assumptions, that is, if
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
and
\gamma<2
, the family of random variables
\mathbb{A}^{Y}
defined by (
29
) satisfies the weak Chen relation relative to
Y
.

Proof.

We give two proofs. The first is much simpler, but understanding the second one will help us to understand the proof of the similar result for the Brownian motion.

First proof.

Let us fix
(s,u,t)\in\Delta_{3}
.
The equality

\theta_{s,t}=\theta_{s,u}+\theta_{u,t}+\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\mathbbm{1}_{T_{s,u,t}}

holds
\lambda
-almost everywhere. Hence,
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{M}}
-almost surely, this equality holds
\mathsf{M}
-almost everywhere. Since all of these functions are
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{M}}
-almost surely
\mathsf{M}
-integrable (Corollary
6.2
),
\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{M}}
-almost surely,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\theta_{s,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\theta_{s,u}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\theta_{u,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\mathbbm{1}_{T_{s,u,t}}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}.

This is exactly the announced equality.

Second proof.

Let us choose
(s,u,t)\in\Delta_{3}
.
We decompose the plane according to the values of the two winding functions
\theta_{s,u}
and
\theta_{u,t}
. Unfortunately, we also have to take the triangle
T_{s,u,t}
into account, which muddles the proof. We invite the reader to write down the simplified version when
Y_{s}=Y_{u}=Y_{t}
.

For three relative integers
j
,
k
, and
n
, we define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{s,u}(z)=j,\ \theta_{u,t}(z)=k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{s,t}(z)=n\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{s,u}(z)=j\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\theta_{u,t}(z)=k\}.

We also define
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}^{\wedge}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\wedge}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1,\wedge}_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2,\wedge}_{n}
the intersection of the triangle
T_{s,u,t}
with (respectively)
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{n}
.

For
z\in\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{j,k}
,
\theta_{s,t}(z)=j+k+\varepsilon_{s,u,t}
.
Hence, for all integer

,

\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{j,k:j+k+\varepsilon_{s,u,t}=n}\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{j,k}.

Besides, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{A}^{1,\wedge}_{j}=\bigsqcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{j,k},

and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{A}^{2,\wedge}_{k}=\bigsqcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{j,k}.

It follows from these relations that

\displaystyle\int_{T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{s,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}(j+k+\varepsilon_{s,u,t})\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{A}^{\wedge}_{j,k})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\mathsf{M}(T_{s,u,t})+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}j\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{A}^{1,\wedge}_{j})+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\mathsf{M}(\mathcal{A}^{2,\wedge}_{k})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{s,u,t}\mathsf{M}(T_{s,u,t})+\int_{T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{s,u}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}+\int_{T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{u,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}.

We then replace the sets
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}^{\wedge}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\wedge}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1,\wedge}_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2,\wedge}_{n}

with the sets
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}^{\vee}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\vee}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1,\vee}_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2,\vee}_{n}
defined as the intersection of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus T_{s,u,t}
with (respectively)
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
,
\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{n}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{n}
.
The same computations hold, except that for
z\in\mathcal{A}^{\vee}_{j,k}
,
\theta_{s,t}(z)
is equal to
j+k
instead of
j+k+\varepsilon_{s,u,t}
.
We end up with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{s,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{s,u}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus T_{s,u,t}}\theta_{u,t}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M},

which allows to conclude the proof.
∎

We now prove the corresponding result for the Brownian motion.

Lemma 7.3
.

If
\gamma<\sqrt{4/3}
, then
\mathbb{A}^{X}
satisfies the weak Chen relation.

We invite the reader to skim through the (rather long) proof a first time, and to convince herself or himself that it is merely a question of interchanging the summation order in a double sum, hence of showing that some residual terms are small.

We fix
(s,u,t)\in\Delta_{3}

\varepsilon(s,u,t)=1
(that is, the triangle has ‘positive orientation’).

For a positive integer

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N}

\displaystyle=\{z:\theta_{X_{|[s,t]}}(z)\geq N\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1}_{N}

\displaystyle=\{z:\theta_{X_{|[s,u]}}(z)\geq N\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}_{N}

\displaystyle=\{z:\theta_{X_{|[u,t]}}(z)\geq N\},

For two positive integers

This is the set of points

and
|\theta_{u,t}(z)|

N
and the other is at least

.
We also use the notations
\mathcal{A}_{N}

\mathcal{A}_{k,j}
,
\mathcal{A}^{1}_{k}
and
\mathcal{A}^{2}_{j}

We will use the following bounds: for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

such that for all

,

(30)

\mathbb{E}^{X}[|\mathcal{D}_{N,M}|]\leq C(NM)^{-1+\varepsilon}.

This is an easy consequence of Lemma 2.4 in
[

.

3.4

For a point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(T_{s,u,t}\cup\Range(X))

\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(A)=\mathsf{M}(A\setminus T_{s,u,t})

For all

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=k}^{+\infty}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}_{l})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=k}^{+\infty}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{l-j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=k-j}^{+\infty}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=1-j}^{+\infty}\min(N,m+j)\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\max(0,\min(N,m+j))\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m}).

This computation remains true if we replace each set
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\ell}

\mathcal{A}^{*}_{-\ell}
and leave everything else unchanged. Doing this susbstitution, subtracting the resulting equality from the one that we just obtained, and using the notation

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{1}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=k}^{+\infty}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=k}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\min(N,j)\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\max(0,\min(N,j))\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m}).

Replacing

(32)

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{1}_{k})-\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{1}_{-k})\big)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}[j]_{N}\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{m}).

The same equation holds after exchanging the superscript

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big(\big(\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}_{k})-\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}_{-k})\big)-\big(\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{1}_{k})-\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{1}_{-k})+\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{2}_{k})-\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{D}^{2}_{-k})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt=\sum_{k,j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\big([k+j]_{N}-[j]_{N}-[k]_{N}\big)\mathsf{M}^{\vee}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{2}_{k}).

\mathbb{N}^{2}

\displaystyle E_{3}=([0,M)\times[N-M,N+M))\cup([N-M,N+M)\times[0,M)),

8.
From weak Chen’s relation to pathwise Chen’s relation

The goal in this section is to show that, up to modification, a map
\mathbb{A}
that satisfies the weak Chen relation does satisfies the Chen relation. For this, we need the
\mathsf{M}
-measure of a triangle to be a continuous function of its vertices, and we are able to do this only under the assumption that
\gamma<2(\sqrt{2}-1)
.

Let us first recall some terminology for functions of two parameters. For functions of three parameters, we use the same definitions with

Our impression is that the terminology of separability, which might have been very commonly used in the past, has gone lost with time. It seems to us that modern introductions to stochastic processes tend to forget about it, to the profit of stronger properties such as continuity or càdlàg property.

The reason why we use separability is that, in order to prove some regularity result, we need to

10.
Uniform estimates in
L^{q}

During the proof of Proposition
9.1
, there is a point that was left aside, about the estimation of
\mathbb{E}[J_{n,n^{\prime}}^{2}]
, where
J_{n,n^{\prime}}
is defined by (
37
). The goal in this section is to prove the estimation (
39
). Our starting point is the following combination of an elementary comparison of a maximum and a sum, and Hölder inequality: for all
q\in[2,\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}})
,

\mathbb{E}[J_{n,n^{\prime}}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[J_{n,n^{\prime}}^{q}]^{\frac{2}{q}}\leq 2^{n+n^{\prime}}\max\big\{\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{M}(R_{i,i+2^{-n},j,j+2^{-n^{\prime}}})^{q}]^{\frac{2}{q}}:i\in{\mathbb{D}}_{n},j\in{\mathbb{D}}_{n^{\prime}}\big\}.

What we need is thus a good uniform bound on the
q
-th moment of the
\mathsf{M}
-measure of a small rectangle. To do this, we study the way in which the
\mathsf{M}
-measure of a rectangle is affected by a smooth transformation of the plane, and prove that a rectangle of given area can be nicely and smoothly sent into a fixed square. Once this is done, we know that the
\mathsf{M}
-measure is not too different from the
\mathsf{M}
-measure of a subset of the fixed square, which gives us what we needed.

In order to understand how the measure
\mathsf{M}
is affected by a smooth transformation of the plane, it is useful to think about its informal definition (
3
). Pushing this expression forward by a diffeomorphism affects it in two ways: it changes the correlation structure of the field in the exponential, and introduces a Jacobian. In order to control the change in the correlation structure of the field, we will use Kahane’s convexity inequalities, which compare the multiplicative Gaussian chaoses associated to two kernels which do not differ too much. The crucial point for us is that our transformation of the plane does not bring too far apart two points that were initially close. We must therefore control something like its Lipschitz norm. On the other hand, we also need the (inverse) Jacobian term that appears not to explode. How to map a possibly long and thin rectangle into a square in a way that satisfies these constraints is explained by Lemma
10.1
.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. There exists a function
\phi:[0,2^{n}]\times[0,2^{-n}]\to[0,10]^{2}

that is injective,
10
-Lipschitz continuous, piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, with Jacobian bounded below by
\frac{1}{10}
.

Proof.

Let us split the rectangle
R=[0,2^{n}]\times[0,2^{-n}]
into
2^{n}
rectangles
R_{1},\dots R_{2^{n}}
, each of which have sides of length

1
and
2^{-n}
. We map each rectangle to a domain shaped like an integral symbol (see Figure
7
below).

Figure 7.
Mapping of one rectangle.

For this, we decompose each
R_{i}
into
L_{i}\sqcup R^{\prime}_{i}\sqcup U_{i}
, where
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
are the two rectangles of width
2^{-n}\frac{\pi}{2}
at the extremities. We parametrize each of them linearly by
[0,2^{-n}\frac{\pi}{2}]\times[0,2^{-n}]
, and
we map them to a quarter of an annulus by
\phi:(2^{-n}x,2^{-n}y)\mapsto(2^{-n}(y+1)\sin(x),2^{-n}(y+1)\cos(x))
. This map (defined on the given rectangle) is
10
-Lipschitz, and its Jacobian determinant is uniformly bounded below by
\frac{1}{10}
.
We finally glue the pieces together, as shown by the following figure (Figure
8
).

gluing the pieces.

This concludes the proof.
∎

As explained at the beginning of the section, we will make use of Kahane’s convexity inequality, which we state in a version adapted to our framework. The proof, which is a notoriously hard one, can be found in
[
9
]
. We assume that
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}
are two multiplicative gaussian chaos with kernels
K
and
K^{\prime}
(and with the same intermittency parameter
\gamma
).

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be some convex function such that

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ |F(x)|\leq M(1+|x|^{\beta}),

for some positive constants
M,\beta
.
Assume that
K(z,w)\leq K^{\prime}(z,w)+C
for some
C\geq 0
. Then, for all compact set
A
,

\mathbb{E}\big[F(\mathsf{M}(A))\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[F(e^{\sqrt{C}-\frac{C}{2}}\mathsf{M}^{\prime}(A))\big].

We can now prove the following lemma.

Lemma 10.3
.

For all
q\in[1,\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}})
, there exists a constant
C
such that for all rectangle
R
with sides of length
l,L\leq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{M}(R)^{q}]<C|R|^{\frac{\xi(q)}{2}},

where
\xi
is the so-called structure exponent
\xi(q)=(2+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2})q-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}q^{2}
.

Proof.

If we restrict ourselves to squares, the result is standard and follows simply from scaling relations (see for example Theorem 2.14 in
[
11
]
). We will reduce the more general case of rectangles to the case of squares thanks to the previous lemma.

First, we fix
m

and
m^{\prime}
such that
l\in[2^{-m-1},2^{-m}],L\in[2^{-m^{\prime}-1},2^{-m^{\prime}}]
where
l
and
L
are the length of the sides of
R
(with
L>l
). Let
\phi
be the map resulting from an application of Lemma
10.1
with
n=m^{\prime}-m
, properly rotated, translated, and conjugated by a homothecy, so that it maps
R
to a square
S
of area
100|R|
.

Let
K_{k}
be an increasing sequence of continuous covariance kernels converging pointwise towards
K
. Let also
(X_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence a continuous centered Gaussian field with covariance kernels
(K_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, and
\mathsf{M}_{k}
the associated measure, given by

\mathsf{M}_{k}=e^{\gamma X_{k}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[X_{k}^{2}]}\;{\rm d}\lambda.

Let also
\phi_{*}(X_{k})
be the centered Gaussian field defined on
\phi(R)
by
\phi_{*}(X_{k})_{u}=(X_{k})_{\phi^{-1}(u)}
. As explained at the beginning of this section, the push-forward of
\mathsf{M}_{k}
by
\phi
and the exponential of the Gaussian field
\phi_{*}(X_{k})
differ by a Jacobian term.

We denote by
J:R\to\mathbb{R}^{*}_{+}
the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant of
\phi
, and by
\mathsf{M}_{k}^{\phi}
the random measure on
\phi(R)
given by

\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}_{k}^{\phi}(u)=\exp\big(\gamma(X_{k})_{\phi^{-1}(u)}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[(X_{k})_{\phi^{-1}(u)}^{2}]\big)\;{\rm d}\lambda(u).

Observe that the push-forward of the measure
\mathsf{M}_{k}
by
\phi
, that we denote by
\phi_{*}(\mathsf{M}_{k})
, is related to
\mathsf{M}_{k}^{\phi}
by the relation

\;{\rm d}\phi_{*}(\mathsf{M}_{k})(u)=J(\phi^{-1}(u))^{-1}\;{\rm d}\mathsf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(u).

Then

\mathsf{M}_{k}(R)=\phi_{*}(M_{k})(\phi(R))\leq 10\mathsf{M}^{\phi}_{k}(\phi(R)),

from which it follows that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}_{k}(R)^{q}]\leq 10^{q}\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}_{k}^{\phi}(\phi(R))^{q}].

Let us now check that we can apply Kahane’s inequality.

For any pair of points
z,w\in R
,
|\phi(z)-\phi(w)|\leq 10|z-w|
. Hence,

\log(|z-w|^{-1})\leq\log(|\phi(z)-\phi(w)|^{-1})+\log(10)
. It follows that there a constant
C
such that for any
z,w\in R
,
K(z,w)\leq K(\phi(z),\phi(w))+C
.
We denote by
\tilde{K}_{k}
the kernel of
\phi_{*}(X_{k})
. For any
u,v\in\phi(R)
,

\tilde{K}_{k}(u,v)=K_{k}(\phi^{-1}(u),\phi^{-1}(v))\leq K(\phi^{-1}(u),\phi^{-1}(v))\leq K(u,v)+C,

for a constant
C
which we allow to vary from line to line. We now apply Kahane convexity inequality, and we deduce that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}_{N}(R)^{q}]\leq 10^{q}\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\phi_{*}(\mathsf{M}_{N})(\phi(R))^{q}]\leq C\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(\phi(R))^{q}]\leq C\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(S)^{q}].

for some constant
C
. The theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos ensures that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}_{N}(R)^{q}]
converges toward
\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(R)^{q}]
, so that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(R)^{q}]\leq C\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{M}}[\mathsf{M}(S)^{q}]\leq C|S|^{\frac{\xi(q)}{2}}\leq C|R|^{\frac{\xi(q)}{2}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎
