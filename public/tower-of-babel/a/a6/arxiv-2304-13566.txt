Geodesics with Unbounded Speed on Fluctuating Surfaces
Andrew Clarke

Abstract
. We construct C ∞ C^{\infty} time-periodic fluctuating surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} such that the corresponding nonautonomous geodesic flow has orbits along which the energy, and thus the speed, goes to infinity.



1.
Introduction

Consider a smooth manifold
M
of dimension
n\geq 2
with a smooth metric
g
. The geodesic flow on
TM
is the flow associated with the Hamiltonian function
H(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}\,g(q)(p,p)
with respect to the symplectic form
dq\wedge dp
, where
q\in M
and
p\in T_{q}M
. Since this is an autonomous Hamiltonian system the energy
H
is an integral of motion, and therefore so too is the speed
\sqrt{2\,H}
. Suppose now we add a small nonautonomous time-periodic perturbation
\varepsilon\,\bar{g}
to the metric to obtain
g_{\varepsilon}(q,t)=g(q)+\varepsilon\,\bar{g}(q,t)
with
\bar{g}(q,t+2\pi)=\bar{g}(q,t)
for all
q\in M
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\varepsilon>0
is small. We say that
g_{\varepsilon}
is a
nonautonomous metric
on
M
if
g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)
defines a Riemannian metric on
M
for each fixed value of time
t
. Nonautonomous metrics of the form
g_{\varepsilon}
give rise to nonautonomous Hamiltonian functions
H_{\varepsilon}(q,p,t)=H(q,p)+\varepsilon\,\bar{H}(q,p,t)
where
H
is as before, and where
\bar{H}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}(q,t)(p,p)
. Since
H_{\varepsilon}
is nonautonomous, its flow
\phi_{\varepsilon,t}
no longer preserves the energy
H_{\varepsilon}
, and so a natural question arises:

Question 1
.

Does the time-periodic nature of
\bar{g}
imply that the changes in energy cannot accumulate, or can we find
(q,p)\in TM
such that
H_{\varepsilon}\left(\phi_{\varepsilon,t}(q,p),t\right)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
?

Orbits of this type, should they exist, travel progressively faster around the manifold, where the speed goes to infinity with time.

A closely related question can be formulated as follows. Suppose now that
M
is a smooth closed hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d\geq 3
, and suppose
M
is equipped with the induced metric
\delta|_{M}
where
\delta
is the standard Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e.
\delta
is the
d\times d
identity matrix). Consider now a
C^{\infty}

2\pi
-time periodic embedding
G_{\varepsilon,t}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
G_{\varepsilon,t}
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to the identity in
C^{\infty}
. Then
M_{\varepsilon,t}=G_{\varepsilon,t}(M)
is a
2\pi
-time periodic
fluctuating hypersurface
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is
O(\varepsilon)
close to
M
in
C^{\infty}
. The geodesic flow on
M_{\varepsilon,t}
is defined by using the coordinates from
M
via the embedding
G_{\varepsilon,t}
and using the (nonautonomous) pullback metric
g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)=\left(G_{\varepsilon,t}\right)^{*}\left(\left.\delta\right|_{M_{\varepsilon,t}}\right)
; then the geodesic flow on the fluctuating hypersurface
M_{\varepsilon,t}
is the flow associated with the Hamiltonian function
H_{\varepsilon}(q,p,t)=\frac{1}{2}\,g_{\varepsilon}(q,t)(p,p)
.

Question 2
.

Does the geodesic flow on the fluctuating hypersurface
M_{\varepsilon,t}
have orbits along which the energy
H_{\varepsilon}
goes to infinity?

Question
1
is closely related to the Mather problem in the case where
M
is a torus
[
8
]
. Questions
1
and
2
are not equivalent, because there exist metrics on
M
that cannot be obtained by pulling back the metric from another hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see for example
[
12
,
39
,
40
]
). These questions, in their full generality, are very difficult to answer, for a number of reasons that are outlined below. The purpose of this paper is to find a class of surfaces
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and embeddings
G_{\varepsilon,t}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
that yield a positive answer to Question
2
(and a fortiori to Question
1
) in the sense of the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
M
be a
C^{\infty}
closed surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the restriction
\left.\delta\right|_{M}
of the Euclidean metric
\delta
such that the corresponding geodesic flow has a hyperbolic periodic orbit and a transverse homoclinic. Then there exist
C^{\infty}

2\pi
-time periodic embeddings
G_{\varepsilon,t}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and points
(q_{0},p_{0}),(q_{1},p_{1})\in TM
such that

(1)

H_{\varepsilon}\left(\phi_{\varepsilon,t}(q_{0},p_{0}),t\right)\to\infty\quad\mathrm{as}\quad t\to\infty

and

(2)

\liminf_{t\to\infty}H_{\varepsilon}\left(\phi_{\varepsilon,t}(q_{1},p_{1}),t\right)=c<\infty,\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}H_{\varepsilon}\left(\phi_{\varepsilon,t}(q_{1},p_{1}),t\right)=\infty

where
\phi_{\varepsilon,t}
is the flow associated with the Hamiltonian function
H_{\varepsilon}(q,p,t)=\frac{1}{2}g_{\varepsilon}(q,t)(p,p)
with
M_{\varepsilon,t}=G_{\varepsilon,t}(M)
,
g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)=\left(G_{\varepsilon,t}\right)^{*}\left(\left.\delta\right|_{M_{\varepsilon,t}}\right)
, and
p\in T_{q}M
. Moreover the initial speed
J_{j}=\|p_{j}\|
is of order 1.

Orbits of the form (
1
) are called
diffusive trajectories
whereas orbits of the form (
2
) are referred to as
oscillatory trajectories
. The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1
is as follows. The assumptions on the geodesic flow on the unperturbed manifold
M
(i.e. existence of a hyperbolic closed geodesic
\gamma
and transverse homoclinic geodesic
\xi
) imply that the geodesic flow has a hyperbolic periodic orbit in every energy level, together with a transverse homoclinic. Indeed, this is because the dynamics of the geodesic flow is the same in every energy level, as a result of the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}
being homogeneous of second degree in the momenta
p
. Therefore we can construct a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
\Lambda
in
TM
consisting of the hyperbolic closed geodesic on an interval of energy levels. The cylinder
\Lambda
is parametrised by an arclength parameter on
\gamma
and the speed of the geodesic flow; since we are interested in the situation where the speed goes to infinity, the cylinder
\Lambda
is noncompact. As a result of the existence of the transverse homoclinic geodesic
\xi
, the stable and unstable manifolds of
\Lambda
have a transverse homoclinic intersection.

Since the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}
is nonautonomous, it is convenient to introduce a new pair of variables
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}
and to define an autonomous Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}^{*}
depending on
q,p,\theta,\Theta
. Here the angle
\theta
represents the
2\pi
-periodic time dependence of
H_{\varepsilon}
, and
\Theta
is a dummy variable that is symplectically conjugate to
\theta
. It is then apparent from the form of the Hamiltonian that the speed of the geodesic flow goes to infinity if and only if
\Theta\to-\infty
. This construction is carried out in Section
2
.

In Section
3
we show how to compute explicitly the first order of the scattering map for a specific class of perturbations. This is done by passing to Fermi coordinates (see for example
[
34
]
) in a neighbourhood of a transverse homoclinic point, and designing a time-periodic nonautonomous perturbation of the metric that is localised in this neighbourhood. We then show that such perturbations of the metric can be realised by a fluctuating surface.

Finally, in Section
4
we choose a specific nonautonomous perturbation of the surface with zero time average, and prove that it produces orbits satisfying (
1
) and (
2
) using shadowing results from
[
27
]
.

This phenomenon is a form of
Arnold diffusion

[
2
,
8
,
17
]
, in the
a priori chaotic
case
[
17
,
26
]
, meaning that the unperturbed system (i.e. the geodesic flow on the static manifold
M
) has a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold, the stable and unstable manifolds of which have a transverse homoclinic intersection. Other forms of Arnold diffusion have been studied: in the
a priori unstable
case
[
18
,
41
]
, the unperturbed system has a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold, but the stable and unstable manifolds coincide; and in the
a priori stable
case the unperturbed system is completely integrable, and no further assumptions are made
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
33
]
. In addition to the geometric approaches to the problem of Arnold diffusion, variational methods have also had success
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
11
,
38
]
.

This idea is closely related to
Fermi acceleration

[
8
,
21
,
25
]
. Fermi acceleration describes the unbounded growth of energy in nonautonomous Hamiltonian systems, typically systems with impacts (i.e. billiards; see
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
). In order to display either Arnold diffusion or Fermi acceleration, a Hamiltonian system must have at least two and a half degrees of freedom, because two-dimensional invariant tori of a Hamiltonian flow divide a four-dimensional phase space into invariant regions. For this reason, Theorem
1
addresses only the case where
M
is a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, as the resulting nonautonomous geodesic flow has two and a half degrees of freedom, but the proof applies equally to the case of hypersurfaces in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces.

The setting of perturbations of geodesic flows in Arnold diffusion is well-studied
[
14
,
17
,
19
,
28
]
. However none of the previous literature applies directly to geodesic flows on fluctuating hypersurfaces and, to the best of the author’s knowledge, the present article is the first in which such models are considered. Moreover the equations of these nonautonomous geodesic flows appear to be immune to an attack of the type contained in those papers. It is therefore unclear a priori that there is not some peculiarity of the nonautonomous geodesic flow equations on fluctuating hypersurfaces that prevents the unbounded drift of energy; the results of this paper prove that there is no such peculiarity.

In this paper we establish the existence of orbits of unbounded energy only for a special class of locally supported nonautonomous perturbations of a special class of surfaces, but Questions
1
and
2
in their full generality would pertain to
generic
nonautonomous perturbations of
generic
surfaces. Broadly, the difficulty in answering these questions from the generic point of view is twofold:

•

The Unperturbed Problem:
To apply the usual methods of (a priori unstable or chaotic) Arnold diffusion, we need the unperturbed system to have a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold, with the stable and unstable manifolds having some (non-transverse or transverse) intersection. This amounts to the existence of a hyperbolic closed geodesic and a homoclinic geodesic for the unperturbed problem; the existence of these objects is an active area of research. It is known that for a fixed manifold
M
there is a
C^{2}
-dense open set of
C^{\infty}
metrics such that these objects exist
[
15
,
16
]
. Moreover it is known that there is a
C^{\infty}
-dense open set of metrics on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that these conditions are satisfied
[
37
]
. It is also known that similar results hold for hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the analytic topology
[
12
]
. However the question of
C^{\infty}
-genericity in the general case remains open.

•

Analysis of Perturbations:
A typical approach that is used in the perturbation theory of geodesic flows is to pass to Fermi coordinates (see
[
34
]
for a description) and perturb the metric in those coordinates (see for example
[
1
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
35
,
39
,
40
]
). The issue with this approach in relation to the current problem is that we must adapt the Fermi coordinates to a homoclinic geodesic (an infinite curve on the compact manifold
M
that is likely to have self-intersections) in order to analyse the scattering map, and Fermi coordinates break down when the curve to which they are adapted has self-intersections. In this paper we consider a neighbourhood of a point on the homoclinic geodesic in which it has no self-intersections, pass to Fermi coordinates in this neighbourhood, and show how to compute the perturbed scattering map when the perturbation of the metric is supported in this neighbourhood. However it is not clear how this method could be adapted to the setting of globally-supported perturbations, nor is it clear that there is another system of coordinates in which the nonautonomous flow can be better understood.

There are a few examples of perturbation theoretic results in geodesic flows in which Fermi coordinates are not used, and the analysis is of a global nature; see for example
[
30
,
31
,
37
]
. In
[
37
]
, the geodesic flow on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is considered; Birkhoff proved that geodesic flows on 2-spheres can be reduced to a Poincaré map on a global surface of section that is diffeomorphic to a cylinder
\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
, and so the analysis in
[
37
]
can use the classical theory of twist maps of the annulus. These techniques however do not apply to manifolds with a different topology to that of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The papers
[
30
,
31
]
are in fact about 3-dimensional Reeb flows, which includes the restriction of geodesic flows on 2-dimensional Riemannian manifolds to the unit tangent bundle, and so they make use of the methods of symplectic topology. It is currently unclear whether such methods could be applied to the problem in consideration.

One setting in which further results of this type could likely be produced is in perturbations of geodesic flows on triaxial ellipsoids in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In a beautiful exposition in
[
36
]
, it is pointed out that such geodesic flows have a pair of hyperbolic periodic orbits (corresponding to the same closed geodesic, traversed in opposite directions) connected by a non-transverse heteroclinic cycle. It is also shown in that paper via Melnikov-type theory that certain perturbations of the metric can split the separatrices. It is possible that such analysis could also be applied to the case of geodesic flows on nonautonomous perturbations of ellipsoids.

In fact, the problem addressed in the current paper was motivated by the following question: does the geodesic flow on a
breathing ellipsoid
(an ellipsoid that is allowed to vary periodically with time through a family of ellipsoids, so that for each fixed time the static manifold is an ellipsoid that is
O(\varepsilon)
close to the original ellipsoid) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
possess orbits along which the energy goes to infinity? This question seems potentially intractable for two reasons: first of all, the Poincaré-Melnikov potential will be some type of elliptic integral, which would likely be quite difficult to interpret analytically; and in any case, it seems to be possible that the scattering map is trivial, in which case the usual techniques of diffusion do not apply. Indeed, this question is similar to the problem addressed in
[
22
]
, in which they considered billiard dynamics in a breathing ellipse in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In that paper, the scattering map turned out to be trivial, and so a secondary perturbation was added in order to guarantee its well-definition.

2.
Analysis of the Unperturbed Problem

The purpose of this section is to establish the existence of a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold with a transverse homoclinic intersection, and to transform our nonautonomous Hamiltonian system with two and a half degrees of freedom to an autonomous Hamiltonian system with three degrees of freedom. In fact, the contents of this section are presented for a hypersurface
M
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
where
n\geq 2
. The analysis in this section is similar to Section 3 of
[
19
]
.

2.1.
The A Priori Chaotic Setting

Denote by
M
a smooth closed manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
, and by
g
a Riemannian metric on
M
. Suppose
M
is a hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and
g
is the restriction to
M
of the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Suppose there is a hyperbolic closed geodesic
\gamma
on
M
; that is, there is a closed geodesic
\gamma
on
M
that gives rise to a hyperbolic periodic orbit of the geodesic flow in each energy level. Suppose moreover there is a transverse homoclinic geodesic
\xi
to
\gamma
; that is to say, a geodesic curve
\xi
on
M
such that the corresponding orbit of the geodesic flow lies in the transverse homoclinic intersection of the stable and unstable manifolds of
\gamma
in each energy level.

Denote by
z=(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})
coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and by
v=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{n})
the corresponding tangent coordinates. Then the restriction to
M
of the 2-form
\omega=dz\wedge dv
defines a symplectic form on
\mathcal{M}=TM
. Reparametrising
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that the length of
\gamma
is
2\pi
. We can thus give the geodesic
\gamma
an arclength parametrisation, which for convenience we denote by
\gamma:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, such that
\|\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\|\equiv 1
. Then
\gamma(\varphi)\in M
and
\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\in T_{\gamma(\varphi)}M
for all
\varphi\in\mathbb{T}
. Fix some
J_{0}>0
. The set

\Lambda=\left\{(z,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2(n+1)}:z=\gamma(\varphi),\,v=J\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\text{ where }\varphi\in\mathbb{T},\,J\geq J_{0}\right\}

defines a 2-dimensional non-compact normally hyperbolic invariant cylinder of the geodesic flow on
M
with respect to the metric
g
. Moreover
\Lambda
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}\times[J_{0},\infty)
, and is equipped with natural coordinates
(\varphi,J)
. In these coordinates, the vector field of the geodesic flow takes the form

(3)

\dot{\varphi}=J,\quad\dot{J}=0.

Lemma 2
.

The restriction to
\Lambda
of the symplectic form
\omega
is

\left.\omega\right|_{\Lambda}=d\varphi\wedge dJ.

Proof.

On
\Lambda
we have
z=\gamma(\varphi)
and
v=J\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)
. Therefore
dz=\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\,d\varphi
and
dv=\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\,dJ+J\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\,d\varphi
. It follows that
dz\wedge dv=d\varphi\wedge dJ
since
\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)=\|\gamma^{\prime}(\varphi)\|^{2}=1
.
∎

Now, we can also give the transverse homoclinic curve
\xi
an arclength parametrisation
\xi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, so
\|\xi^{\prime}(x_{0})\|\equiv 1
for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Fix some
\delta_{0}\in\left(0,\pi\right)
, and define the set

\Gamma=\left\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:z=\xi(x_{0}),\,v=J\xi^{\prime}(x_{0})\text{ where }|x_{0}|\leq\delta_{0},\,J\geq J_{0}\right\}.

Then
\Gamma
is a homoclinic channel for
\Lambda
, because the projections
\left.\pi^{s,u}\right|_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to\Lambda
are diffeomorphisms onto their respective images. We can therefore define the scattering map

s=\pi_{s}\circ\left(\left.\pi_{u}\right|_{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}:\pi_{u}\left(\Gamma\right)\longrightarrow\pi_{s}\left(\Gamma\right).

It can easily be proved that there are
a_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
(independent of
x_{0}
and
J
) such that
\pi_{s}\left(x_{0},J\right)=(x_{0}+a_{+},J)
and
\pi_{u}\left(x_{0},J\right)=(x_{0}+a_{-},J)
. It follows that the scattering map is given by
s(\varphi,J)=(\varphi+\Delta,J)
where
\Delta=a_{+}-a_{-}
.

2.2.
The Extended Phase Space

Now, denote by
q
coordinates on the manifold
M
, by
p
the corresponding tangent coordinates, and by
g
the Riemannian metric. Recall that the geodesic flow is the flow
\phi_{t}
associated with the Hamiltonian function
H(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}g(q)(p,p)
. We now introduce a new variable
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that represents time, and its symplectic conjugate
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}^{*}=\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
the
extended phase space
, and by

(4)

\omega^{*}=\omega+d\theta\wedge d\Theta

the
extended symplectic form
on
\mathcal{M}^{*}
where
\omega
is the symplectic form on
\mathcal{M}=TM
. Define the
extended Hamiltonian

H^{*}:\mathcal{M}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(5)

H^{*}(q,p,\theta,\Theta)=H(q,p)+\Theta.

Denote by
\phi^{*}_{t}
the flow associated with this Hamiltonian. For
(q,p,\theta,\Theta)\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
we have
\phi^{*}_{t}(q,p,\theta,\Theta)=(\phi_{t}(q,p),\theta+t,\Theta)
. It follows that the manifold

\Lambda^{*}=\left\{(q,p,\theta,\Theta)\in\mathcal{M}^{*}:(q,p)\in\Lambda\right\}

defines a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold for
\phi^{*}_{t}
, and is equipped with natural coordinates
(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)
. Moreover, the manifold

\Gamma^{*}=\left\{(q,p,\theta,\Theta)\in\mathcal{M}^{*}:(q,p)\in\Gamma\right\}

is a homoclinic channel, and so we have a scattering map
s^{*}=\pi_{s}^{*}\circ\left(\left.\pi_{u}^{*}\right|_{\Gamma^{*}}\right)^{-1}:\pi_{u}^{*}(\Gamma^{*})\to\pi_{s}^{*}(\Gamma^{*})
where
\pi^{*}_{s,u}:W^{s,u}(\Lambda^{*})\to\Lambda^{*}
are the holonomy maps related to the normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
\Lambda^{*}
. It is not hard to see that
s^{*}
is given by

(6)

s^{*}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=(\varphi+\Delta,J,\theta,\Theta).

It is clear that Theorem
1
is equivalent to the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

There are
C^{\infty}
time-periodic embeddings
G_{\varepsilon,t}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
for which there exists
(q_{0},p_{0},\theta_{0},\Theta_{0})
,
(q_{1},p_{1},\theta_{1},\Theta_{1})\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
such that

\Theta_{0}(t)\to-\infty\quad\mathrm{as}\quad t\to\infty

and

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\Theta_{1}(t)=-\infty,\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\Theta_{1}(t)=c^{*}\in\mathbb{R}

where
\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon,t}(q_{j},p_{j},\theta_{j},\Theta_{j})=(q_{j}(t),p_{j}(t),\theta_{j}(t),\Theta_{j}(t))
with
\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon,t}
denoting the flow of the Hamiltonian function

H_{\varepsilon}^{*}(q,p,\theta,\Theta)=g_{\varepsilon}(q,\theta)(p,p)+\Theta

where
g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)=\left(G_{\varepsilon,t}\right)^{*}\left(\left.\delta\right|_{M_{\varepsilon,t}}\right)
. Moreover the initial speed
J_{j}=\|p_{j}\|
is of order 1.

Remark 1
.

Results of this type typically require initial speed or energy to be very large (see for example
[
19
,
22
]
); however the perturbative technique used in this paper (see Lemma
4
) allows the starting speed to be
O(1)
.

3.
Nonautonomous Perturbations of the Metric

We now assume that
M
is a smooth closed surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(but this construction can easily be extended to higher dimension), with a Riemannian metric
g
defined by the restriction of the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to
M
. Moreover we assume that
M
has a hyperbolic closed geodesic
\gamma
with a transverse homoclinic geodesic
\xi
. We now introduce a
C^{\infty}
nonautonomous perturbation
\bar{g}:M\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
that is
2\pi
-periodic in time, and consider the perturbed metric

g_{\varepsilon}=g+\varepsilon\bar{g}

for small
\varepsilon>0
. Denote by
H_{\varepsilon}=H+\varepsilon\bar{H}
the perturbed Hamiltonian of the geodesic flow, where
\bar{H}(q,p,t)=\bar{g}(q,t)(p,p)
. Lifting this problem to the extended phase space, we obtain the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}^{*}:\mathcal{M}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=H^{*}+\varepsilon\bar{H}^{*}
where
H^{*}
is defined by (
5
), and the Hamiltonian
\bar{H}^{*}
is

(7)

\bar{H}^{*}(q,p,\theta,\Theta)=\frac{1}{2}\bar{g}(q,\theta)(p,p).

Denote by
\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon,t}:\mathcal{M}^{*}\to\mathcal{M}^{*}
the flow of the Hamiltonian
H^{*}_{\varepsilon}
.

3.1.
Computation of the Perturbed Scattering Map

Assume that
\bar{g}
is supported away from the hyperbolic closed geodesic
\gamma
. Then the manifold
\Lambda^{*}
remains a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold for the perturbed flow
\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon,t}
. Since the perturbation is small, the homoclinic channel persists as a manifold
\Gamma^{*}_{\varepsilon}
that is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
\Gamma^{*}
, and so we have a perturbed scattering map
s_{\varepsilon}^{*}
defined on a subset of
\Lambda^{*}
. Define the vector field
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\varepsilon}
on
\pi^{*}_{\epsilon,s}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}^{*})
by the equation
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}s^{*}_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\circ s^{*}_{\varepsilon}
. Denote by
\Omega
the standard
4\times 4
symplectic matrix. It was shown in
[
20
]
(see Theorem 32) that
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{\varepsilon}=\Omega\nabla S^{*}_{\varepsilon}
where the Hamiltonian function
S^{*}_{\varepsilon}
is given by

(8)

S^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\bar{H}^{*}\circ\phi^{*}_{\varepsilon,t}\circ\left(\left.\pi^{*}_{\varepsilon,s}\right|_{\Gamma^{*}_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)\,dt.

It follows that we can write

(9)

s^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=s^{*}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)+\varepsilon\,\left(\Omega\nabla S^{*}\right)\circ s^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)

with

S^{*}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=S^{*}_{0}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\bar{H}^{*}\left(Z(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta,t)\right)\,dt

where

\displaystyle Z(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta,t)=

\displaystyle\phi^{*}_{t}\circ\left(\left.\pi^{*}_{s}\right|_{\Gamma^{*}}\right)^{-1}(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)=\phi^{*}_{t}\left(\xi(\varphi-a_{+}),J\xi^{\prime}(\varphi-a_{+}),\theta,\Theta\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=

Remark 2
.

As Lemma
4
will be used in the proof of Theorem
3
, we will be dealing with situations in which
|\Theta|
,
\|p\|
, and
J
are very large. This can be delicate in perturbation-theoretic settings such as this one, where there are terms of order
\varepsilon
. The typical way of dealing with this is to rescale variables, the Hamiltonian, the symplectic form, and time, and to work with the rescaled system
[
19
]
. In our case, if
J=O(\varepsilon^{-n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
then we can rescale: variables via
\tilde{p}=\varepsilon^{n}p
,
\tilde{J}=\varepsilon^{n}J
,
\tilde{\Theta}=\varepsilon^{n}\Theta
; the Hamiltonian by
\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2n}H_{\varepsilon}
; the symplectic forms by
\tilde{\omega}=\varepsilon^{n}\omega
and
\tilde{\omega}^{*}=\varepsilon^{n}\omega^{*}
; and time by
\tilde{t}=\varepsilon^{n}t
. In this way we obtain the same dynamical system as the case when
J=O(1)
due to the homogeneity of the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}
in momenta. This proves the consistency of the results of Lemma
4
when
\|p\|
is large.

3.2.
Realising the Perturbed Metric as a Fluctuating Surface

In this section we use results from
[
12

13

M=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:Q(z)=0\right\}

(20)

\kappa(z,v)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}Q}{\partial z_{i}\,\partial z_{j}}\left(z\right)\,v_{i}\,v_{j}.

The periodicity of the geodesic
\gamma

\varphi^{*}\in\mathbb{T}

[
12

Now, let
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{R})

Denote by

[

Lemma 5

There is a

for all sufficiently small values of

, where

is the unit normal, defined by (

Denote by

Lemma 6

The Fermi coordinates

\bar{g}_{ij}(x,t)=2\,\psi\left(h(x),t\right)\tilde{C}_{ij}(x)

Therefore if we define

\psi(z,t)=-\kappa\left(\xi\left(h_{0}^{-1}(z)\right),\xi^{\prime}\left(h_{0}^{-1}(z)\right)\right)^{-1}\alpha_{\rho}\left(h_{0}^{-1}(z)\right)\,\beta\left(h_{1}^{-1}(z)\right)\,\chi(t)

4.
Construction of Transition Chains for a Specific Perturbation

The purpose of this section is to construct drifting trajectories for certain perturbations of the surface of the form (
24
), where
\alpha_{\rho}
,
\beta
are as described in Section
3.1
. For concreteness we consider the case when

\chi(t)=\cos t.

Note however that the following argument equally applies to any nonconstant

with zero average. The idea is to apply the shadowing results of
[
27

In order to construct a Poincaré map, we extend the coordinates
(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta)
on
\Lambda^{*}
to

(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta,s,u)
in a neighbourhood of
\Lambda^{*}

[
32

\{\varphi=0\}
is well-defined in a neighbourhood of
\Lambda^{*}
. We now fix an energy level
\{H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=E_{0}\}
of the Hamiltonian function
H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=H^{*}+\varepsilon\bar{H}^{*}
(where
H^{*}
is defined by (
5

\bar{H}^{*}
is the Hamiltonian of the perturbation, defined by (
7

H_{\varepsilon}^{*}
to the section
\{\varphi=0\}\cap\{H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=E_{0}\}
in a neighbourhood of
\Lambda^{*}
. Denote by

F
has a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
\hat{\Lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\cap\{\varphi=0\}\cap\{H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=E_{0}\}
, and we denote by
f=F|_{\hat{\Lambda}}
the restriction to
\hat{\Lambda}
of the map
F
. Let us compute the map
f
. As a result of Lemma
2

\left.\omega^{*}\right|_{\Lambda^{*}}=d\varphi\wedge dJ+d\theta\wedge d\Theta

where the extended symplectic form
\omega^{*}
is defined by (
4

\bar{H}^{*}
of the perturbation is supported away from

\dot{\varphi}=J,\,\dot{J}=0,\dot{\theta}=1,\,\dot{\Theta}=0

\left.\omega^{*}\right|_{\Lambda^{*}}

\left.H_{\varepsilon}^{*}\right|_{\Lambda^{*}}\left(\varphi,J,\theta,\Theta\right)=\frac{J^{2}}{2}+\Theta.

Therefore the energy level
\{H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=E_{0}\}
in
\Lambda^{*}
is defined by the equation
J=\sqrt{2(E_{0}-\Theta)}

define coordinates on the normally hyperbolic cylinder
\hat{\Lambda}=\Lambda^{*}\cap\{\varphi=0\}\cap\{H_{\varepsilon}^{*}=E_{0}\}

Lemma 7
.

The inner map
f:\hat{\Lambda}\to\hat{\Lambda}
is given by

f:\begin{cases}\bar{\theta}=&\theta+\frac{2\,\pi}{\sqrt{2(E_{0}-\Theta)}}\\
\bar{\Theta}=&\Theta\end{cases}

\frac{\partial\bar{\theta}}{\partial\Theta}=O\left(J^{-3}\right)

Proof.

Denote by

\hat{\Lambda}
of the flow of the Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon}^{*}
. For a point

\tau(\theta,\Theta)=\frac{2\,\pi}{J}=\frac{2\,\pi}{\sqrt{2(E_{0}-\Theta)}}.

Since
\dot{\theta}=1
and
\dot{\Theta}=0
it follows that
f
indeed has the form (
26

\frac{\partial\bar{\theta}}{\partial\Theta}=\frac{2\,\pi}{(2(E_{0}-\Theta))^{\frac{3}{2}}}\neq 0

which implies the required twist condition.
∎

Now, there is a foliation of the cylinder
\hat{\Lambda}
by leaves

\mathcal{L}\left(\Theta^{*}\right)=\left\{(\theta,\Theta)\in\hat{\Lambda}:\Theta=\Theta^{*}\right\}

for
\Theta^{*}\leq\Theta_{0}
, each of which is left invariant by the map

as a consequence of Lemma

Due to the contents of Section
3.1
, there is a scattering map
\hat{S}:\hat{\Lambda}\to\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}
where
\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime}
is a locally invariant normally hyperbolic cylinder containing
\hat{\Lambda}
; moreover since
\left.\dot{\Theta}\right|_{\Lambda^{*}}=0
we have
\hat{S}(\theta,\Theta)=(\bar{\theta},\bar{\Theta})
where

(27)

\bar{\Theta}=\Theta+\varepsilon\,J\,\left(\sin\left(\theta+\frac{a_{-}}{J}\right)-R_{\rho}^{0}(0,J,\theta,\Theta)\right)+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}J\right)

and

(28)

\frac{\partial\bar{\Theta}}{\partial\theta}=-\varepsilon\,J\,\left(\cos\left(\theta+\frac{a_{-}}{J}\right)+R^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,J,\theta,\Theta\right)\right)+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}J\right)

with

, where we have set

in (

) and (

), and used (

). By Lemma

\rho>0
sufficiently small so that
\left|R_{\rho}^{j}(0,J,\theta,\Theta)\right|<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}

,
\theta\in T
, and
\Theta\leq\Theta_{0}
. Choose

; by (

\bar{\Theta}^{*}_{+}-\Theta^{*}\geq\varepsilon\,J\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}>0,\quad\bar{\Theta}^{*}_{-}-\Theta^{*}\leq-\varepsilon\,J\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}<0,\quad\left|\frac{\partial\bar{\Theta}^{*}_{\pm}}{\partial\theta}\right|\geq\varepsilon\,J\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}>0.

or
\Theta_{n+1}-\Theta_{n}\leq-\varepsilon\,J\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}

are connected by the scattering map

, in the sense that there is

such that

; moreover,
\hat{S}

in the sense of the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Let

Appendix A
The Scattering Map of a Normally Hyperbolic Invariant Manifold

In this section we denote by
M
a
C^{r}
smooth manifold, and by
\phi^{t}:M\to M
a smooth complete flow with
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\phi^{t}=X
where
X\in C^{r}(M,TM)
. Let
\Lambda\subset M
be a compact
\phi^{t}
-invariant submanifold, possibly with boundary. By
\phi^{t}
-invariant we mean that
X
is tangent to
\Lambda
, but that orbits can escape through the boundary (a concept sometimes referred to as
local
invariance).

We call
\Lambda
a
normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
for
\phi^{t}
if there is
0<\lambda<\mu^{-1}
, a positive constant
C
and an invariant splitting of the tangent bundle

(32)

T_{\Lambda}M=T\Lambda\oplus E^{s}\oplus E^{u}

such that:

(33)

\begin{array}[]{c}\|D\phi^{t}|_{E^{s}}\|\leq C\lambda^{t}\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0,\\
\|D\phi^{t}|_{E^{u}}\|\leq C\lambda^{-t}\mbox{ for all }t\leq 0,\\
\|D\phi^{t}|_{T\Lambda}\|\leq C\mu^{|t|}\mbox{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

Moreover,
\Lambda
is called an
r
-
normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
if it is
C^{r}
smooth, and

(34)

0<\lambda<\mu^{-r}<1

for
r\geq 1
. This is called a
large spectral gap
condition.

This definition guarantees the existence of stable and unstable invariant manifolds
W^{s,u}(\Lambda)\subset M
defined as follows. The local stable manifold
W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Lambda)
is the set of points in a small neighbourhood of
\Lambda
whose forward orbits never leave the neighbourhood, and tend exponentially to
\Lambda
. The local unstable manifold
W^{u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Lambda)
is the set of points in the neighbourhood whose backward orbtis stay in the neighbourhood and tend exponentially to
\Lambda
. We then define

(35)

W^{s}(\Lambda)=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}^{\infty}\phi^{-t}\left(W^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Lambda)\right),\quad W^{u}(\Lambda)=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}^{\infty}\phi^{t}\left(W^{u}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Lambda)\right).

On the stable and unstable manifolds we have the strong stable and strong unstable foliations, the leaves of which we denote by
W^{s,u}(x)
for
x\in\Lambda
. For each
x\in\Lambda
, the leaf
W^{s}(x)
of the strong stable foliation is tangent at
x
to
E^{s}_{x}
, and the leaf
W^{u}(x)
of the strong unstable foliation is tangent at
x
to
E^{u}_{x}
. Moreover the foliations are invariant in the sense that
\phi^{t}\left(W^{s}(x)\right)=W^{s}\left(\phi^{t}(x)\right)
and
\phi^{t}\left(W^{u}(x)\right)=W^{u}\left(\phi^{t}(x)\right)
for each
x\in\Lambda
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We thus define the
holonomy maps

\pi^{s,u}:W^{s,u}(\Lambda)\to\Lambda
to be projections along leaves of the strong stable and strong unstable foliations. That is to say, if
x\in W^{s}(\Lambda)
then there is a unique
x_{+}\in\Lambda
such that
x\in W^{s}(x_{+})
, and so
\pi^{s}(x)=x_{+}
. Similarly, if
x\in W^{u}(\Lambda)
then there is a unique
x_{-}\in\Lambda
such that
x\in W^{u}(x_{-})
, in which case
\pi^{u}(x)=x_{-}
.

Now, suppose that
x\in\left(W^{s}(\Lambda)\pitchfork W^{u}(\Lambda)\right)\setminus\Lambda
is a transverse homoclinic point such that
x\in W^{s}(x_{+})\cap W^{u}(x_{-})
. We say that the homoclinic intersection at
x
is
strongly transverse
if

(36)

\begin{split}T_{x}W^{s}(x_{+})\oplus T_{x}\left(W^{s}(\Lambda)\cap W^{u}(\Lambda)\right)=T_{x}W^{s}(\Lambda),\\
T_{x}W^{u}(x_{-})\oplus T_{x}\left(W^{s}(\Lambda)\cap W^{u}(\Lambda)\right)=T_{x}W^{u}(\Lambda).\end{split}

In this case we can take a sufficiently small neighbourhood
\Gamma
of
x
in
W^{s}(\Lambda)\cap W^{u}(\Lambda)
so that (
36
) holds at each point of
\Gamma
, and the restrictions to
\Gamma
of the holonomy maps are bijections onto their images. We call
\Gamma
a
homoclinic channel
. We can then define the scattering map as follows
[
20
]
.

Let
y_{-}\in\pi^{u}\left(\Gamma\right)
, let
y=\left(\left.\pi^{u}\right|_{\Gamma}\right)^{-1}(y_{-})
, and let
y_{+}=\pi^{s}(y)
. The
scattering map

S:\pi^{u}(\Gamma)\to\pi^{s}(\Gamma)
is defined by

(37)

S=\pi^{s}\circ\left(\pi^{u}\right)^{-1}:y_{-}\longmapsto y_{+}.

Suppose now that the smoothness
r
of
M
and
X
is at least
2
, suppose the normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
\Lambda
is a
C^{r}
submanifold of
M
, and suppose the large spectral gap condition (

) holds. This implies

regularity of the strong stable and strong unstable foliations
[
29
]
, which in turn implies that the scattering map
S
is
C^{r-1}

[
20
]
.
