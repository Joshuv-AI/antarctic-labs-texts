Positive semidefinite interval of matrix pencil and its applications for the generalized trust region subproblems
Thi Ngan Nguyen, Van-Bong Nguyen

Abstract
We are concerned with finding the set I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) I_{\succeq}(A,B) of real values μ \mu such that the matrix pencil A + μ ​ B A+\mu B is positive semidefinite. If A , B A,B are not simultaneously diagonalizable via congruence (SDC), I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) I_{\succeq}(A,B) either is empty or has only one value μ . \mu. When A , B A,B are SDC, I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) , I_{\succeq}(A,B), if not empty, can be a singleton or an interval. Especially, if I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) I_{\succeq}(A,B) is an interval and at least one of the matrices is nonsingular then its interior is the positive definite interval I ≻ ​ ( A , B ) . I_{\succ}(A,B). If A , B A,B are both singular, then even I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) I_{\succeq}(A,B) is an interval, its interior may not be I ≻ ​ ( A , B ) , I_{\succ}(A,B), but A , B A,B are then decomposed to block diagonals of submatrices A 1 , B 1 A_{1},B_{1} with B 1 B_{1} nonsingular such that I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) = I ⪰ ​ ( A 1 , B 1 ) . I_{\succeq}(A,B)=I_{\succeq}(A_{1},B_{1}). Applying I ⪰ ​ ( A , B ) , I_{\succeq}(A,B), the hard-case of the generalized trust-region subproblem (GTRS) can be dealt with by only solving a system of linear equations or reduced to the easy-case of a GTRS of smaller size.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n}
be the space of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices and
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{n}.
In this paper we compute the set

\displaystyle I_{\succeq}(A,B)=\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}:A+\mu B\succeq 0\}

and then apply it for solving the following optimization problem

\hskip 17.07182pt\begin{array}[]{lll}\lambda^{*}=&\min&f(x)=x^{T}Ax+2a^{T}x\\
&{\rm s.t.}&g(x)=x^{T}Bx+2b^{T}x+c\leq 0,\end{array}

(1)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
n-
dimension vectors,
c\in\mathbb{R}.
This problem is referred as the generalized trust region subproblem (GTRS) since it contains the
trust region subproblem (TRS), when
B=I
is the identity matrix,
b=0
and
c=-1,
as a special case.

Our study is inspired
by the following results obtained by Moré
[
10
]
: i) a vector
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a global optimizer of the
GTRS (
1
) if and only if
g(x^{*})\leq 0
and there exists
\mu^{*}\in I_{\succeq}(A,B)\cap[0,\infty)

such that

\displaystyle\nabla f(x^{*})+\mu^{*}\nabla g(x^{*})=0,

(2)

\displaystyle\mu^{*}g(x^{*})=0;

(3)

and ii) if the set defined by
I_{\succ}(A,B)=\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}:A+\mu B\succ 0\}
is nonempty then
it is an open interval and
the function
\varphi(\mu):=g[x(\mu)]
is strictly decreasing on
I_{\succ}(A,B),
unless
x(\mu)

uniquely solved from (
2
) is constant on
I_{\succ}(A,B).

These two results suggest that
\mu^{*}
can be found efficiently whenever
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
is computed.
Unfortunately, in literature, results on
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
are
very limited. The earliest study on computing
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
was, perhaps, by
Caron and Gould
[
3
]
, where the authors suppose that
A
is positive semidefinite and
B
is of rank one or two.
Song
[
11
]
computed
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
under the assumption that
\mathcal{R}(B)\subset\mathcal{R}(A),

where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
denotes the range of a matrix. Moré
[
10
]
, Adachi et al.
[
1
]

computed
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
under the assumption that
I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset.

Out of above conditions, computing
I_{\succeq}(A,B)

is still an open question so far. However, if
I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset,

I_{\succeq}(A,B)
is then the closure of

I_{\succ}(A,B)
and
computing
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
is much easier since the matrices
A,B
are then simultaneously diagonalizable via congruence (SDC), i.e.,
there exists a nonsingular matrix
P
such that
P^{T}AP
and
P^{T}BP
are all diagonal, please see
[
9
]
.
Moré
[
10
]
proposed an algorithm for finding
\mu^{*}
under the assumption that
I_{\succ}(A,B)

is nonempty, while the case
I_{\succ}(A,B)=\emptyset
is referred as the
hard-case
.
The assumption
I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset
is so strictive that it restricts the GTRS (
1
) into a
very special class which always attains a unique optimal solution. Moreover, if

I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset
then
A,B
are SDC
and the GTRS (
1
) is equivalently reformulated as a convex second-order cone programming (SOCP)
[
2
]
.
This result is then
extended that any bounded GTRS can be reformulated as an SOCP even the SDC condition fails
[
5
]
.
The assumption
I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset
also allows
to solve GTRS (
1
) by solving only one eigenpair of a generalized eigenvalue problem of
(2n+1)\times(2n+1)
dimension
[
1
]
.
A more recent result by Jiang and Li can solve GTRS (
1
) even in linear time

but under a stricter condition that

\mu\in(0,1]
and
\lambda_{\rm min}(B)\leq-\xi<0

]
. Hsia et al.

]

solve the GTRS (
1
) when

are SDC, while the case when

are not SDC is still referred as the

.

In this paper, we show that not only when
I_{\succ}(A,B)\neq\emptyset
but also even
I_{\succ}(A,B)=\emptyset

the interval

can be computed by only solving a generalized eigenvalue problem of

dimension.
Specifically, we show that if
A,B
are not SDC then

either is empty or has only one point:

Such a value
\mu

and then checked whether

If
A,B
are SDC
and

is nonsingular, then the matrix pencil

\displaystyle P^{T}(A+\mu B)P=\texttt{diag}((\lambda_{1}+\mu)B_{1},(\lambda_{2}+\mu)B_{2}\ldots,(\lambda_{k}+\mu)B_{k}),

The set

If

is singular and
A
is nonsingular, we can decompose

to the taking the forms

Especially, if

has more than one point, then

and

please see Corollary
1

If
A,B
are SDC and both are singular, then there always exists a nonsingular
matrix
U
such that

A,B
are decomposed to either the following form

\displaystyle U^{T}BU=\texttt{diag}(B_{1},0)\text{ and }U^{T}AU=\texttt{diag}(A_{1},0)

(4)

or

\displaystyle U^{T}BU=\texttt{diag}(B_{1},0)\text{ and }U^{T}AU=\texttt{diag}(A_{1},A_{4}),

(5)

where

is diagonal and, in both cases,

are diagonal and of the same size,

is nonsingular.
If
A,B
are decomposed to (

has even one diagonal negative element then

Otherwise, in both cases,
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=I_{\succeq}(A_{1},B_{1})
with

nonsingular.
To apply
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
for solving GTRS (
1
), we consider the set of candidates of Lagrange multipliers

I=I_{\succeq}(A,B)\cap[0,\infty).

1.

If
I=\emptyset,
(
1
) has no optimal solution since it is unbounded from below;

2.

If
I
is singleton:
I=\{\mu\},
we need only to solve linear equations to check whether
\mu^{*}=\mu;

3.

If

is an interval and

then there is a unique optimal Lagrange multiplier
\mu^{*}\in I.
If

\mu^{*}
is not an endpoint of

a bisection algorithm can find

in the interior of

since the function

is strictly decreasing on

4.

If
I
is an interval and

are converted to the form either (
4
) or (
5
)
and
I_{\succ}(A_{1},B_{1})\neq\emptyset.
In this case, the GTRS (
1
) is either unbounded below or reduced
to a GTRS of
p
variables with matrices

such that
I_{\succ}(A_{1},B_{1})\neq\emptyset.

Computing the positive semidefinite interval
I_{\succeq}(A,B)

In this section, we show computing
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
in two separate cases:
A,B
are SDC and
A,B
are not SDC.
For the former case, we need first the following result.

Lemma 1

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
B
be nonsingular.
Then
A,B
are SDC if and only if there is a nonsingular real matrix
P
such that
P^{-1}B^{-1}AP
is a real diagonal matrix.

Now, if
A,B
are SDC and
B
is nonsingular, by Lemma
1
, there is a nonsingular matrix
P
such that

J:=P^{-1}B^{-1}AP=\texttt{diag}(\lambda_{1}I_{m_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{k}I_{m_{k}}),

is a diagonal matrix, where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}

are the
k
distinct eigenvalues of
B^{-1}A,

I_{m_{t}}
is the identity matrix of size
m_{t}\times m_{t}

and
m_{1}+m_{2}+\ldots+m_{k}=n.

We can suppose without loss of generality that

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k}.

Using

together with the following result we show how to simultaneously decompose
A
and
B
into block diagonals.

Lemma 2

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
K
be a Jordan matrix of form

K=\texttt{diag}(C(\lambda_{1}),C(\lambda_{2}),\cdots,C(\lambda_{k})),

where

are Jordan blocks associated with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
and

K_{i_{j}}(\lambda_{i})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\lambda_{i}&1&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda_{i}&1&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\lambda_{i}&1\\
0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\lambda_{i}\end{array}\right)_{(ij)},\hskip 28.45274ptj=1,2,\cdots,t_{i}.

For a symmetric matrix
S,
if
SK
is symmetric, then
S
is block diagonal and
S=\texttt{diag}(S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{k})
with

{\rm dim}S_{i}={\rm dim}C(\lambda_{i}).

Observe that
P^{T}BP.J=P^{T}AP
and
P^{T}AP
is symmetric. Lemma

indicates that
P^{T}BP
is a block diagonal matrix with the same partition as
J.
That is

\displaystyle P^{T}BP=\texttt{diag}(B_{1},B_{2}\ldots,B_{k}),

(6)

where
B_{t}
is real symmetric matrices of size
m_{t}\times m_{t}
for every

We now have

\displaystyle P^{T}AP=P^{T}BP.J=\texttt{diag}(\lambda_{1}B_{1},\lambda_{2}B_{2}\ldots,\lambda_{k}B_{k}).

(7)

Both (
6
) and (
7
) show that
A,B
are now decomposed into the same block structure and the matrix
pencil
A+\mu B
now becomes

\displaystyle P^{T}(A+\mu B)P=\texttt{diag}((\lambda_{1}+\mu)B_{1},(\lambda_{2}+\mu)B_{2}\ldots,(\lambda_{k}+\mu)B_{k}).

(8)

The requirement
A+\mu B\succeq 0
is then equivalent to

\begin{split}(\lambda_{i}+\mu)B_{i}\succeq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,k.\end{split}

(9)

Using (
9
) we compute
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
are SDC and
B
is nonsingular.

1.

If
B\succ 0
then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=[-\lambda_{k},+\infty);

2.

If
B\prec 0
then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=(-\infty,-\lambda_{1}];

3.

If
B
is indefinite then

(i)

if
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{t}\succ 0
and
B_{t+1},B_{t+2},\ldots,B_{k}\prec 0
for some
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\},
then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=[-\lambda_{t},-\lambda_{t+1}].

(ii)

if
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{t-1}\succ 0,

B_{t}
is indefinite and
B_{t+1},B_{t+2},\ldots,B_{k}\prec 0,
then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=\{-\lambda_{t}\},

(iii)

in other cases, that is either
B_{i},B_{j}
are indefinite for some
i\neq j
or
B_{i}\prec 0,B_{j}\succ 0
for some
i<j
or

B_{i}
is indefinite and
B_{j}\succ 0
for some
i<j,
then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=\emptyset.

Proof.

1.

If
B\succ 0
then
B_{i}\succ 0~\forall i=1,2,\ldots,k.
The inequality (
9
) is then equivalent to

\lambda_{i}+\mu\geq 0~\forall i=1,2,\ldots,k.

Since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k},
we need only
\mu\geq-\lambda_{k}.

This shows
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=[-\lambda_{k},+\infty).

2.

Similarly, if
B\prec 0
then
B_{i}\prec 0~\forall i=1,2,\ldots,k.
The inequality (
9
) is then equivalent to

\lambda_{i}+\mu\leq 0~\forall i=1,2,\ldots,k.
Then
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=(-\infty,-\lambda_{1}].

3.

The case
B
is indefinite:

(i)

if
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{t}\succ 0
and
B_{t+1},B_{t+2},\ldots,B_{k}\prec 0
for some
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\},

the inequality (
9
) then implies

\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}+\mu\geq 0,\forall i=1,2,\ldots,t,\\
\lambda_{i}+\mu\leq 0,\forall i=t+1,\ldots,k.\end{cases}

Since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k},
we have
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=[-\lambda_{t},-\lambda_{t+1}].

(ii)

if
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{t-1}\succ 0,

B_{t}
is indefinite and
B_{t+1},B_{t+2},\ldots,B_{k}\prec 0
for some

t\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}.
The inequality (
9
) then implies

\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}+\mu\geq 0,\forall i=1,2,\ldots,t-1\\
\lambda_{t}+\mu=0\\
\lambda_{i}+\mu\leq 0,\forall i=t+1,\ldots,k.\end{cases}

Since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k},
we have
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=\{-\lambda_{t}\}.

(iii)

if
B_{i},B_{j}
are indefinite, (
9
) implies
\lambda_{i}+\mu=0
and
\lambda_{j}+\mu=0.
This cannot happen since

\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}.
If
B_{i}\prec 0
and
B_{j}\succ 0
for some
i<j,
then

\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}+\mu\leq 0\\
\lambda_{j}+\mu\geq 0\end{cases}

implying
-\lambda_{j}\leq\mu\leq-\lambda_{i}.
This also cannot happen since

\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{j}.
Finally, if
B_{i}
is indefinite and
B_{j}\succ 0
for some
i<j.
Again, by (
9
),

\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}+\mu=0\\
\lambda_{j}+\mu\geq 0\end{cases}

implying
\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{j}.
This also cannot happen.
So
I_{\succeq}(A,B)=\emptyset
in these all three cases.

4
Conclusion and remarks

In this paper, we showed that for a given pair of real symmetric matrices
A,B,
the set
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
of real values
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that the matrix pencil
A+\mu B
is positive semidefinite is always computable by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem
of
n\times n
dimension.
The computation is considered in two separated cases:
If
A,B
are not simultaneously diagonalizable via congruence (SDC),
I_{\succeq}(A,B)

is either empty or singleton, while if
A,B
are SDC,
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
can be empty, singleton or an interval.
In case
I_{\succeq}(A,B)

is an interval, if either
A
or
B
is nonsingular,
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
is the closure of
the positive definite interval
I_{\succ}(A,B).
Otherwise,
A,B
are decomposable to block diagonals
of submatrices
A_{1},B_{1}
with
B_{1}
nonsingular such that

I_{\succeq}(A,B)
is now the closure of
I_{\succ}(A_{1},B_{1}).

With
I_{\succeq}(A,B)
in hand, we are able to solve the generalized trust region subproblem (
1
)
not only in the
easy-case
when
I_{\succ}(A,B)
is nonempty but also in the
hard-case
by only solving the linear
equations.
Our result needs only to solve the generalized eigenvalue problem of
n\times n
dimension compared with
an
(2n+1)\times(2n+1)
generalized eigenvalue problem in
[
1
]
. On the other hand,
we can completely deal with the hard-case of the GTRS (
1
), which was an open problem in

[
10
]
and
[
8
]
.
