On the behavior of multidimensional radially symmetric solutions of the repulsive Euler-Poisson equations
Olga S. Rozanova

Abstract
. It is proved that the radially symmetric solutions of the repulsive Euler-Poisson equations with a non-zero background, corresponding to cold plasma oscillations blow up in many spatial dimensions except for 𝐝 = 4 {\bf d}=4 for almost all initial data. The initial data, for which the solution may not blow up, correspond to simple waves. Moreover, if a solution is globally smooth in time, then it is either affine or tends to affine as t → ∞ t\to\infty .

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study a version of the repulsive Euler-Poisson
equations

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\partial n\over\partial t}+\mbox{div}\,(n{\bf V})=0,\quad\displaystyle{\partial{\bf V}\over\partial t}+\left({\bf V}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf V}=\,k\,\nabla\Phi,\quad\Delta\Phi=n-n_{0},

where the solution components, the scalar functions
n
(density),

\Phi
(force potential), and the vector
{\bf V}
(velocity) depend on
the time
t
and the point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\bf d}
,
{\bf d}\geq 1
,

n_{0}>0
is the density background. Positive or negative value of
the constant
k
corresponds to the repulsive and attractive force,
respectively.

The Euler-Poisson equations arise in many applications, see

[
11
]
for references, but for us they are of interest primarily
in the context of cold plasma oscillations, where
k=1
. At
present, much attention is paid to the study of cold plasma in
connection with the possibility of accelerating electrons in the
wake wave of a powerful laser pulse
[
13
]
; nevertheless,
there are very few theoretical results in this area.

The equations of hydrodynamics of “cold” or electron plasma in the
non-relativistic approximation in dimensionless quantities have the
form (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
8
]
)

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\partial n\over\partial t}+\mbox{div}\,(n{\bf V})=0,\quad\displaystyle{\partial{\bf V}\over\partial t}+\left({\bf V}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf V}=\,-{\bf E}-\left[{\bf V}\times{\bf B}\right],

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf E}}{\partial t}=n{\bf V}+{\rm rot}\,{\bf B},\qquad\frac{\partial{\bf B}}{\partial t}=-{\rm rot}\,{\bf E},\qquad\mbox{div}\,{\bf B}=0,

n
and
{\bf V}=(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3})
are the density and velocity of electrons,

{\bf E}=(E_{1},E_{2},E_{3})
and
{\bf B}=(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})
are vectors of electric and magnetic fields.
All components of solution depends on
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

It is commonly known that the plasma oscillations described by
(
2
), (
3
), tend to break. Mathematically,
the breaking process means a blow-up of the solution, and the appearance
of a delta-shape singularity of the electron density
[
6
]
,
see also
[
5
]
and references therein for numerical
examples illustrating the behavior of solution on the stage of the
blow-up.
Among the main interests of physicists is the study of the possibility of the existence
of a smooth solution for as long as possible.

System (
2
) – (
3
) has an important
class of solutions depending only on the radius-vector of point

r=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}}
, i.e.

(4)

\displaystyle{\bf V}=F(t,r){\bf r},\quad{\bf E}=G(t,r){\bf r},\quad{\bf B}=Q(t,r){\bf r},\quad n=n(t,r),

where
{\bf r}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
.

Since

{\rm div}{\bf B}=3Q(t,r)+r(Q(t,r))_{r},
then the condition
{\rm div}{\bf B}=0
implies
Q(t,r)=\tilde{Q}(t)r^{-3}
, therefore a bounded in the
origin solution exists if and only if
{\bf B}\equiv 0.
In its turn, it implies
{\rm rot}\,{\bf E}=0
.
From the first equations of (
2
) and (
3
) under the assumption
that the solution is sufficiently smooth and that the steady-state density
n_{0}
is equal to 1, we
get

(5)

\displaystyle n=1-\mbox{div}\,{\bf E},

therefore
n
can be removed
from the system. Thus, the resulting system is

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\partial{\bf V}\over\partial t}+\left({\bf V}\cdot\nabla\right){\bf V}=\,-{\bf E},\quad\frac{\partial{\bf E}}{\partial t}+{\bf V}\mbox{div}\,{\bf E}={\bf V}.

If we introduce the potential
\Phi
such that
\nabla\Phi=-{\bf E}
,
we can rewrite system (
5
), (
6
) as the Euler-Poisson
equations (
1
) with
n_{0}=1
.

Note that system (
6
) can be considered in any space dimensions

{\bf d}
(non necessarily
{\bf d}=3
, as in the initial setting). In what
follows, we deal with just this case.

Consider the initial data

(7)

({\bf V},{\bf E})|_{t=0}=(F_{0}(r){\bf r},G_{0}(r){\bf r}),\quad(F_{0}(r),G_{0}(r))\in C^{2}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}),

where
{\bf r}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{\bf d}),\quad r=|{\bf r}|,
with the
physically natural condition
n|_{t=0}>0
.

We call a solution of (
6
), (
7
) smooth for
t\in[0,t_{*})
,
t_{*}\leq\infty
, if the functions
F
and
G
in
(
4
) belong to the class
C^{1}([0,t_{*})\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. The blow-up of solution implies that the
derivatives of solution tends to infinity as
t\to t_{*}<\infty
.

Definition 1
.

Solution
({\bf V},{\bf E})
to system (
6
) is called an affine
solution if it has the form
{\bf V}=\mathfrak{V}(t){\bf r}
,

{\bf E}=\mathfrak{E}(t){\bf r}
, where
\mathfrak{V}
and

\mathfrak{E}
are
({\bf d}\times{\bf d})
matrices.

Definition 2
.

Solution
({\bf V},{\bf E})
to system (
6
) is called a simple wave
if it has the form
{\bf V}=F(t,r){\bf r}
,

{\bf E}=G(t,r){\bf r}
, where
F(t,r)
and

G(t,r)
are functionally dependent.

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1
.

The solution of the Cauchy problem
(
6
), (
7
) for
{\bf d}\geq 2
,
{\bf d}\neq 4
, blows up in a finite time for
all initial data, possibly except for the data, corresponding to simple waves. If the solution is globally smooth in
time, then it is either affine or tends in the
C^{1}
-norm on each
compact subset of the half-axis
[0,\infty)
to an affine solution
as
t\to\infty
.

Theorem
1
can be reformulated in the terms of the Euler-Poisson
equations (
1
).

The results are obtained analytically for small deviations of the trivial steady state and partly thanks
to numerical considerations for arbitrary initial data.

The meaning of Theorem
1
is that in spaces of higher dimensions (except for
{\bf d}=4
)
there cannot be a non-trivial solution of the Euler-Poisson equations, globally smooth in time, for practically all initial data.
In other words, if we want to get a globally smooth solution, we must choose the initial data in a very narrow class of”simple waves,
for which one component the solution depends on another. Such a class of solutions cannot be realized as a perturbation of a stationary state with a compact support (see Sec.
6
), which makes them unpromising both from the point of view of physical applications and from the point of view of a computational experiment.

Note that many results can be obtained in a more convenient way for
the Euler-Poisson equations written as (
6
), this applies, in
particular, to equations with linear damping, cf.
[
18
]

and
[
2
]
.

An important contribution to the study of critical phenomena of
solutions of the repulsive Euler-Poisson equations was made in

[
11
]
, where, in particular, radially symmetric solutions in a
space of many dimensions are studied. In
[
20
]
a critical
threshold in the terms of the initial data was obtained for this
case. However, the background value of the density was taken as zero
in all these studies. In our case, a nonzero background density
naturally arises, which leads to the appearance of oscillations and
radically changes the research technique and the behavior of the
solution. Let us note
[
19
]
, which contains some progress in this case.

Note that the behavior of the solution changes significantly in the
case attractive case
k<0
, we refer to
[
3
]
, where conditions
for the existence of a globally smooth solution in terms of initial
data for zero and non-zero backgrounds are obtained. See also

[
2
]
for recent interesting results concerning critical
thresholds in Euler-Poisson systems in different settings.

2.
Explicit solutions along characteristics

First of all, we notice that (
4
) and (
6
)
imply that
F
and
G
satisfy the following Cauchy problem:

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\partial G\over\partial t}+Fr\displaystyle{\partial G\over\partial r}=F-{\bf d}FG,\quad\displaystyle{\partial F\over\partial t}+Fr\displaystyle{\partial F\over\partial r}=-F^{2}-G,

(9)

(F(0,r),G(0,r))=(F_{0}(r),G_{0}(r)),\quad(F_{0}(r),G_{0}(r))\in C^{2}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}).

Along the characteristic

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{r}=Fr,

starting from the point
r_{0}\in[0,\infty)

system (
8
) takes the form

(11)

\displaystyle\dot{G}=F-{\bf d}FG,\qquad\dot{F}=-F^{2}-G.

On the phase plane
(G,F)
system (
11
) implies one equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle{dF^{2}\over dG}=-\frac{F^{2}+G}{1-{\bf d}G},

which is linear with respect to
F^{2}
and can be explicitly integrated. Indeed,
we have for
{\bf d}=2

(12)

\displaystyle 2F^{2}=(2G-1)\ln|1-2G|+C_{2}(2G-1)-1,

\displaystyle C_{2}=\frac{1+2F^{2}(0,r_{0})}{2G(0,r_{0})-1}-\ln|1-2G(0,r_{0})|,

for
{\bf d}=1
and
{\bf d}\geq 3

(13)

\displaystyle F^{2}=\frac{2G-1}{{\bf d}-2}+C_{\bf d}|1-{\bf d}G|^{\frac{2}{{\bf d}}},\quad C_{\bf d}=\frac{1-2G(0,r_{0})+({\bf d}-2)F^{2}(0,r_{0})}{({\bf d}-2)|1-{\bf d}G(0,r_{0})|^{\frac{2}{{\bf d}}}}.

Lemma 1
.

The condition

n>0
implies
G<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
provided
G(t,r)\in C^{1}([0,t_{*})\times\bar{\mathbb{R}})
,
t_{*}\leq\infty
.

Proof.

From the first equation (
11
) and (
10
) we have

(14)

1-{\bf d}\,G={\rm const}\,r^{-{\bf d}},

therefore the sign of
1-{\bf d}\,G(r(t))
coincides with the sign of

1-{\bf d}G(0,r_{0})
, this means that on the phase plane
(G,F)
of the
system (
11
) the motions on the half-planes
G<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}

and
G>\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
are separated.

Since (
5
) implies
\mbox{div}\,{\bf E}={\bf d}\,G+G_{r}r<1
, then at the point

r=0
the condition
G<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
follows from (
5
)
directly. Further, on the phase plane
(G,F)
there is only one
equilibrium, the origin. Therefore, in the half-plane

G>\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
no bounded trajectory exists. If in some point

r_{0}>0
initially
{\bf d}\,G(0,r_{0})>1
, then
G(r(t))\to+\infty
and

r(t)\to 0
as
t\to t_{*}>0
(see (
14
)), and we obtain a
contradiction with property
n>0
.
\Box

Lemma 2
.

For
{\bf d}\geq 2
the functions
F
and
G
are bounded if and only if

G_{0}(r)<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
.

Proof.

If
G(0,r_{0})<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
,
r_{0}\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, then
the phase trajectories of (
11
) are in the half-plane

G<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}
(Lemma
1
). In this half-plane the leading
term in the right hand side of (
12
) is
(2\,G-1)\ln|1-2\,G|
, and the leading term in the right hand side of
(
13
) is
\frac{2\,G-1}{{\bf d}-2}
. Therefore
F
and
G

are bounded for any
t>0
.
\Box

Note that the situation is different for
{\bf d}=1
, where the
phase trajectory is bounded not for all
G<\frac{1}{{\bf d}}=1
. Indeed,
the leading term as
G\to-\infty
in the right hand side of
(
13
) is
C_{1}(1-G)^{2}
,
F
and
G
can be bounded if only
if
\displaystyle C_{1}=\frac{F^{2}(0)+2G(0)-1}{(1-G(0))^{2}}<0
for every
r_{0}
. The 1D
case is completely analyzed in
[
17
]
.

Our considerations also imply

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\bf d}\geq 2
. Then along every characteristic curve starting from

the functions

and

, solution of (

The following lemma helps us to study the properties of the period

depending on
{\bf d}
.

Lemma 4
.

The period of revolution of the phase curve of equation (

{\bf d}
and the starting point of trajectory, except for

Proof.

The results for
{\bf d}=1

\displaystyle t=s\,(1+\varepsilon w_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}w_{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2}))

\displaystyle\cos s,\quad F_{1}(s)=-\sin s,

\displaystyle G_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-w_{1}\,s\sin s-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}(2+{\bf d})\cos s-\frac{1}{3}(2+{\bf d})\cos 2s,

3.
The behavior of derivatives

We denote
{\mathcal{D}}=\mbox{div}\,{\bf V}
,
\lambda=\mbox{div}\,{\bf E}
,

J_{ij}=\partial_{x_{i}}{V_{i}}\,\partial_{x_{j}}{V_{j}}-\partial_{x_{j}}{V_{i}}\,\partial_{x_{i}}{V_{j}}
,

i\neq j
,

J=\sum\limits_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{\bf d}J_{ij}.
The number of terms in the
sum is
C^{\bf d}_{2}=\frac{({\bf d}-1){\bf d}}{2}
.

It can be readily shown that

(18)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}={\bf d}\,F+F_{r}\,r,\quad\lambda={\bf d}\,G+G_{r}\,r,\quad J=({\bf d}-1)\,F\,F_{r}\,r+\frac{({\bf d}-1){\bf d}}{2}\,F^{2},

therefore

(19)

\displaystyle 2J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,({\bf d}-1)\,{\mathcal{D}}\,F-({\bf d}-1)\,{\bf d}\,F^{2}.

Lemma 5
.

The solution of the Cauchy problem (
8
), (
9
)
with a bounded density belongs to the class
C^{1}([0,t_{*})\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
if and only if
\mathcal{D}
and
\lambda
are
bounded on
[0,t_{*})\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
t_{*}\leq\infty
.

Proof.

If the density is bounded, then, as follows from Lemma
2
,

F
and
G
are bounded. If the solution belongs to the class
C^{1}([0,t_{*})\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, then
F_{r}
and
G_{r}
are bounded for
t\in[0,t_{*})
.
In turn, (
18
) implies that
\mathcal{D}
and
\lambda
are bounded.

On the other hand, if

\mathcal{D}
and
\lambda
are bounded together with the density, then
F
and
G
are bounded by Lemma
2
and

F_{r}
and
G_{r}
are bounded from (
18
).
Since (
8
) is
symmetric hyperbolic system, this implies that the solution is
C^{1}
-
smooth globally in
t
(see
[
10
]
). If
\mathcal{D}
or
\lambda
tends to

\infty
as
t\to t_{*}<\infty
, then a singularity forms at the
point
t_{*}<\infty
.
\Box

System (
6
) implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\partial{\mathcal{D}}\over\partial t}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle({\bf V}\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{D}})=-{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+2J-\lambda,\qquad\displaystyle{\partial\lambda\over\partial t}+({\bf V}\cdot\nabla\lambda)={\mathcal{D}}(1-\lambda).

Taking into account (

),
along the characteristic curve,
starting from the point

, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{D}}=-{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+2\,({\bf d}-1)\,F\,{\mathcal{D}}-\lambda-({\bf d}-1)\,{\bf d}\,F^{2},\qquad\dot{\lambda}={\mathcal{D}}\,(1-\lambda),

a quadratically nonlinear system with the coefficient

found from

Let us introduce new variables:

,

. Evidently, if

and

\dot{u}=-u^{2}-2\,F\,u-v,\quad\dot{v}=-u\,v+(1-{\bf d}\,G)\,u-{\bf d}\,F\,v.

.

Assume
{\bf d}\geq 2
.
If the data (

u_{0}(r_{0})=({\mathcal{D}}-{\bf d}F)|_{t=0,\,r=r_{0}},\quad v_{0}(r_{0})=({\lambda}-{\bf d}G)|_{t=0,\,r=r_{0}},

The lemma is a corollary of Lemmas

,

and

{\mathcal{D}}
and
\lambda
are bounded,
since Lemmas

and

and

-smooth solution to the problem (

), (

) with positive density.

\Box

Corollary 1
.

In the case
{\bf d}\geq 2
the affine solution to (
6

)
is a globally smooth solution with positive density.

Proof.

System (

.

4.
Linearization and the Radon lemma

Next, we need the following version of the Radon lemma (1927)

[

]

Theorem 2

(The Radon lemma)
.

A matrix Riccati equation

(22)

\dot{W}=M_{21}(t)+M_{22}(t)W-WM_{11}(t)-WM_{12}(t)W,

(
