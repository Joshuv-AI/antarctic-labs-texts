Diffusive scaling limit of the Busemann process in Last Passage Percolation
Ofer Busani

Abstract
In exponential last passage percolation, we consider the rescaled Busemann process x ↦ N − 1 / 3 B 0 , [ x ​ N 2 / 3 ] ​ e 1 ρ ( x ∈ ℝ ) x\mapsto N^{-1/3}B^{\rho}_{0,[xN^{2/3}]e_{1}}\,\,(x\in\mathbb{R}) , as a process parametrized by the scaled density ρ = 1 / 2 + μ 4 N − 1 / 3 \rho=1/2+\frac{\mu}{4}N^{-1/3} , and taking values in C ⁡ ( ℝ ) C(\mathbb{R}) . We show that these processes, as N → ∞ N\rightarrow\infty , have a càdlàg scaling limit G = ( G μ ) μ ∈ ℝ G=(G_{\mu})_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}} , parametrized by μ \mu and taking values in C ⁡ ( ℝ ) C(\mathbb{R}) . The limiting process G G , which can be thought of as the Busemann process under the KPZ scaling, can be described as an ensemble of ”sticky” lines of Brownian regularity. We believe G G is the universal scaling limit of Busemann processes in the KPZ universality class. Our proof provides insight into this limiting behaviour by highlighting a connection between the joint distribution of Busemann functions obtained by Fan and Seppäläinen in [ 19 ] , and a sorting algorithm of random walks introduced by O’Connell and Yor in [ 35 ] .

Introduction

In
last passage percolation
(LPP), i.i.d weights are assigned to each vertex of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
y
is above and to the right of
x
, then one defines the last passage time
L(x,y)
as the maximum total weight collected by an up-right path on the lattice, starting at
x
and terminating at
y
. A path on which the total weight is attained is called a
geodesic
from
x
to
y
. Lattice LPP belongs to a large family of LPP models, which can be viewed as random directed metric spaces
[
17
]
, which seem to share the same limiting behaviour of the fluctuations of their observables. Since the seminal work of Rost
[
42
]
where the first order of
L(\bm{0},y)
was established for
y
large in exponential LPP, much progress has been made in the study of these models
[
1
,
39
,
31
,
16
,
29
]
.

One of the aspects of LPPs and random metric-like models that has been studied in the past three decades is that of infinite geodesics. An infinite geodesic is an up-right path on the random environment, such that its restriction to between any two points on it is a geodesic. Questions such as the existence and uniqueness of infinite geodesics as well as other finer results have been studied in
first passage percolation
(FPP) by C. Newman and co-authors
[
27
,
28
,
32
,
30
]
, while results of that flavour for various LPP models can be found in
[
22
,
36
,
7
,
8
,
3
,
29
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
40
]
. One of the main tools of studying infinite geodesics in random (directed) metrics models is the Busemann function - a random stationary real valued function defined on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which satisfies the cocycle property and a so-called Burke’s property. Originating in hyperbolic geometry, the Busemann functions were introduced first to FPP by Newman
[
32
]
and Hoffman
[
26
]
and later to LPP in
[
23
]
. They underlie many of the techniques used to study infinite geodesics
[
3
,
44
]
and have deep connections to the set of exceptional directions at which uniqueness of the geodesics fail
[
29
]
. They are also important in the study of point-to-point geodesics
[
2
]
, their coalescence
[
13
]
and the regularity of the passage time profile around a point
[
36
,
37
,
2
,
13
,
38
]
. Busemann fucntions are usually associated with a direction on the first quadrant of the lattice – for
\rho\in(0,1)
one defines the Busemann function
B^{\rho}
by essentially setting
B^{\rho}(x,y)
to be the difference in passage time between two geodesics starting from
x
and
y
and going in a direction that is parametrised by
\rho\in(0,1)
.
B^{\rho}
turns out to hold much (and in some models all) the information about infinite geodesics emanating from every point of the lattice, in the direction associated with
\rho
. It is possible, to construct Busemann functions of different intensity
\rho
on one probability space to obtain the
Busemann process

\bm{B}=\{B^{\rho}\}_{\rho\in(0,1)}
. The process
\bm{B}
turns out to be very rich, and in the case of Exponential LPP, it was shown to hold all the information on the exceptional directions of non uniqueness of infinite geodesics
[
29
]
. For more on the magic of Busemann functions in the context of LPP see
[
43
,
41
]
.

The random picture of passage times and geodesics has an interesting scaling limit achieved through an incremental progress over the past three decades
[
1
,
39
,
31
]
(to mention a few), culminating in showing that, under the (1:2:3)-KPZ scaling, the microscopic metric-like space of LPP converges to an object dubbed the Directed Landscape(DL)
[
16
]
. The DL is a continuous function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
holding much of the information on the limiting fluctuations of LPP models in the KPZ class. The prelimiting geodesics, in turn, scale to continuous functions on the plane - the geodesics of the DL. While much of the work in recent years have been to understand the scaling limit of the LPP picture on a compact set in the DL, the question of the scaling behavior of infinite geodesics to that of those in the DL is interesting, and first steps were taken very recently in
[
40
]
where the existence of infinite geodesics and Busemann functions was proven in the DL.

In that context, it would be interesting to see what is the Busemann process in the DL
[
15
, Remark 1.3]
i.e. does the limit

\displaystyle B_{DL}^{z}(x,0;y,t)=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{L}(rz,-r;x,0)-\mathcal{L}(rz,-r;y,t)\qquad\forall z,x,y,t\in\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

exist and what are its properties. Note that the index
z
in (
1.1
) plays the role of the direction while
(x,s)
and
(y,t)
are points on the plane. The existence of
B_{DL}^{z}
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
was proven very recently in
[
40
, Section 3.8]
. Following the same progress as in that of the lattice LPP, it would be interesting to see what is the distribution of the Busemann process
z\mapsto B_{DL}^{z}
, its properties, and its connection to infinite geodesics in the DL. From the co-cycle property of the Busemann functions, the study of
B_{DL}
can be broken down into two steps: a) study the Busemann process on one time horizon, i.e. study the process
H_{DL}^{z}
defined by
z\mapsto B_{DL}^{z}(0,0;x,0)
, b) push the process
H_{DL}^{z}
through
\mathcal{L}
to obtain
B_{DL}
on different time horizons i.e.

\displaystyle B_{DL}^{z}(0,0;x,t)=\sup_{y}H^{z}_{DL}(y)\mathcal{L}(y,0;x,t)\qquad z\in\mathbb{R},t>0.

(1.2)

From the stationarity of the Busemann process
\bm{B}
on the lattice and the (1:2:3)-KPZ limit, it is expected that
H_{DL}
should emerge as the diffusive scaling limit of

\displaystyle H^{\rho}_{x}=B^{1/2+\rho}_{(0,0),xe_{1}}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\rho\in(-1/2,1/2),

that is, we expect the following sequence

\displaystyle G^{N}_{\rho}(x):=N^{-1/3}\big[H^{\frac{\rho}{4}N^{-1/3}\rho(z)}_{xN^{2/3}}-2xN^{2/3}\big],

(1.3)

to converge in some sense to an object parameterized by the intensity
\rho
and taking values in
C(\mathbb{R})
. This limit should, perhaps under some reparametrization, be equal in distribution to
H_{DL}
, and so, should be universal in the KPZ class.

The main motivation behind this work is the study of
B_{DL}
and the geodesics associated to it in the DL. This work establishes the limit
G
of the sequence in (
1.3
), which we call the Stationary Horizon (SH), and studies its properties.

Only a few months after the first draft of this paper, Seppäläinen and Sorensen obtained, among other results, the joint distribution of the unscaled Busemann process in Brownian LPP (BLPP), i.e. when the set of directions by which the process is parameterized is macroscopic, as opposed to our work where we scale the directions around a specific macroscopic direction. Interestingly, although not a proven result yet, it seems that the (unscaled) Busemann process in BLPP is essentially the SH. From that perspective, the results in
[
45
,
46
]
would complement the results in this work. One possible explanation for the SH appearance as Busemann process of the BLPP without scaling the direction, is that the BLPP can be thought of as a lattice LPP after a diffusive scaling. It is then not surprising that the Busemann process in BLPP has a scaling invariance property in that direction.

Regarding our proof and techniques, our starting point will be the results of Fan and Seppäläinen in
[
19
]
where the finite dimensional distribution of the Busemann process was obtained. Our main technique is a ”melonization” representation of the map presented in
[
19
]
, which, as it turns out, is closely related to the RSK picture as non stationary queues developed in
[
33
,
10
]
by O’Connell and co-authors. Loosely speaking, melonization of a finite sequence of random walks (RWs) is a sorting process that results in a sequence of ordered RWs and is closely related to the dynamics of queues in tandem. The reason for the term melonization is that by Greene’s Theorem the sum of the height of the
k
top sorted RWs has the same distribution as that of the maximal passage weight collected by
k
non-intersecting paths, which arguably looks like a melon. Even though we do not study a possible connection between our ”stationary RSK” and
k
-paths maximal weight in this paper, we shall still refer to our sorting process as melonization, to point out the relation to previous work in the standard RSK model. Our representation does not seem to be completely new, however, our derivation of it is new, and its interpretation as melonization of random walks is proved important in this work, and appears to be novel in the context of two sided RWs. We discuss this at length in Subsection
2.4
.

This melonization dynamics is behind one of the most salient features of the SH - although
G
is two dimensional as function of space
x
and its drift
\mu
, it can be represented as an ensemble of ”sticky” lines (Figure
1(b)
). Alternatively, we can (and will) think of
G
as a right continuous with left limits (rcll) monotone process which is a jump process when restricted to any compact interval (Figure
1(a)
). This stickiness phenomenon is in line with similar prelimiting and limiting behavior of LPP models studied in the literature, for the Difference Profile
[
6
,
9
,
21
]
, the Busemann process in lattice LPP
[
29
,
19
]
and the Shock Measures
[
15
,
40
,
17
]
. This in turn shows, that the rcll microscopic behaviour of the Busemann function carries over to its diffusive limit. It also implies that infinite geodesics associated with a continuum of directions, emanating from a single point in the DL form a tree (see
1(b)
). We defer this, and the connection between the SH and the infinite geodesics in the DL to a future work.

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}^{(n)}(\overline{I})]_{i}=D^{(i)}(I^{1},...,I^{i})\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

.

.

[

There exists a unique measure

From here to the end of the section we introduce notation and obtain some auxilary results that will be used in the next subsection. For

\displaystyle[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i}=\Bigg[\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}m^{-1}\sum_{k=-m}^{0}I^{i}_{k}\Bigg]^{-1},\qquad\overline{I}\in\mathcal{Z},i\in\mathbb{N}.

\displaystyle(\sigma_{i}\overline{I})_{k}=\begin{cases}\overline{I}_{k}&\text{$k\notin\{i,i+1\}$ or $[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i}\leq[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i+1}$}\\
D(\overline{I}_{i},\overline{I}_{i+1})&\text{$k=i$ and $[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i}>[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i+1}$}\\

\displaystyle[\Gamma(\sigma_{i}\overline{I})]_{k}=\begin{cases}[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{k}&k\notin\{i,i+1\}\\

[\Gamma(\overline{I})]_{i}&k=i+1.\end{cases}

We shall also need the following operator: for

and

\displaystyle[\sigma^{*}_{i}\bm{\rho}]_{l}=\begin{cases}\rho_{l}&\text{$l\notin\{i,i+1\}$ or $\rho_{i}<\rho_{i+1}$}\\

\rho_{i}&\text{$l=i+1$ and $\rho_{i}>\rho_{i+1}$}.\end{cases}

In words, the operator

on the distribution

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose

. Fix

and consider

defined in (

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\overline{I}\sim\nu^{\sigma^{*}_{i}\bm{\rho}}.

From the law of large numbers applied to each element of

, with probability

\Gamma(\overline{I})=\bm{\rho}
. This means there are two cases to check - a)

a.s.- here nothing happens with probability

and so (

) holds. (b)

a.s. - here with probability

, the application of

results in

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\overline{I}=\big(\Pi_{[1,i-1]}(\overline{I}),D(I^{i},I^{i+1}),R(I^{i},I^{i+1}),\Pi_{[i+2,\infty)}(\overline{I})\big).

By hypothesis

, the result now follows from
Lemma

and (

∎

In what follows, if

. Next, define

\displaystyle\sigma_{[i,j]}\overline{I}=\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\cdots\sigma_{j}\overline{I}.

(2.17)

For

\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{X}
denote the constant sequence. i.e.
\mathbf{c}_{i}=c

. We denote
\mathbf{c}^{k}=(\underbrace{\mathbf{c},...,\mathbf{c}}_{\text{k times}})

results in translation of the output.

Lemma 2.4
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(n)}(\overline{I})-\mathbf{c}^{n}=\mathcal{D}^{(n)}(\overline{I}-\mathbf{c}^{n}).

(2.18)

Proof.

From the definition (
2.12
), it is enough to show that

\displaystyle D^{(i)}(I^{1},...,I^{i})-\mathbf{c}=D^{(i)}(I^{1}-\mathbf{c},...,I^{i}-\mathbf{c})\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

(2.19)

Clearly (
2.19
) holds for
i=1
, the proof for
i\geq 2
will follow by induction on
i
. For the base case
i=2
, it is not hard to see that for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle[D(I^{1},I^{2})]_{k}-c=d_{k}-c

\displaystyle=\big(W(I^{1}-\mathbf{c},I^{2}-\mathbf{c})_{k-1}+(I^{1}_{k-1}-c)-(I^{2}_{k}-c)\big)^{-}+I^{1}_{k}-c=[D(I^{1}-\mathbf{c},I^{2}-\mathbf{c})]_{k}.

Assume the hypothesis holds up to

)

\displaystyle D^{(i)}(I^{1},...,I^{i})-\mathbf{c}=D\big(I^{1},D^{(i-1)}(I^{2},...,I^{i})\big)-\mathbf{c}

∎

2.3
The construction
\mathfrak{D}^{(n)}

In this subsection we obtain the main technical tool that underlies the development of much of the main results in this paper. Recall from Theorem
2.2
that in order to obtain the distribution
\mu^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}
, one can simply do the following - start with
\overline{I}\sim\nu^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}
and apply to it the map
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}
. In what comes next, we construct the distribution
\mu^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}
differently. Although the new construction is arguably more convoluted than
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}
, it presents an important feature that facilitates much of the results to come. Let us try to explain here the advantage of the new construction
\mathfrak{D}^{(n)}
, which equals
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}
. The map
\mathfrak{D}^{(n)}
takes as input
\overline{I}\sim\nu^{\bm{\rho}_{n}}
and spits out
(f^{1},...,f^{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. The construction
\mathfrak{D}^{(n)}
is such that for each
2\leq i\leq n
, there exists
u^{i}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
f^{i}=D(f^{i-1},u^{i})
. The upshot, and one of the main results of this section (Proposition
2.11
), is that the r.v.s (recall (
2.5
))
\{S_{oj}(f^{i-1},u^{i})_{m}\}_{2\leq i\leq n}
are mutually independent, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. This property is important since when the difference between
\rho_{i-1}
and
\rho_{i}
is small,
S_{oj}(f^{i-1},u^{i})_{m}
gets large which then implies that
f_{i}=f_{i-1}
on a fixed compact interval with high probability. We will then use the results in this section to show that, on a fixed compact interval, there are only few
i
’s for which
f^{i}\neq f^{i+1}
, which is key to show relative compactness in the Skorohord space. We note here that although the map
\mathfrak{D}
is equivalent to the map constructed in
[
19
, Section 6.2]
, its derivation as a sequential sorting of RWs, is new and plays an important role in the proof of Lemma
3.3
(see the last part of Subsection
2.4
).

Let
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},...)
be a decreasing sequence and let
\overline{I}=(I^{1},I^{2},...)\sim\nu^{\bm{\rho}}
. Set

and for

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{2}

\displaystyle=\overline{I}

\displaystyle f^{2}

\displaystyle=D^{(2)}(\Pi_{[1,2]}\overline{I}^{2})

\displaystyle u^{2}=I^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad v^{2}=I^{1}.

For
i\geq 3

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{[1,i-2]}\overline{I}^{i-1}

\displaystyle f^{i}

\displaystyle=D^{(i)}(\Pi_{[1,i]}\overline{I}^{i})

\displaystyle u^{i}=D^{(i-1)}(\Pi_{[2,i]}\overline{I}^{i})

\displaystyle\qquad v^{i}=\Pi_{1}\overline{I}^{i}.

(2.21)

Note that from (
2.11
) with

it follows that

\displaystyle f^{i}=D(v^{i},u^{i}).

If

and

, we define the map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{(n)}(\overline{I}^{(n)})=(f^{1},...,f^{n}).

Two types of melonization

The connection between queues and LPP goes back to the works of Szczotka and Kelly

4
Convergence to the Stationary Horizon

In this section we prove the main results of this work, i.e. we show that the Busemann process on a horizontal line, converges to a non-trivial limit under diffusive scaling - Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. The sense in which the convergence takes place will be defined rigorously in Subsection
4.1
, here we only try to give a general idea of our intentions. Recall
\{I^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}
from (
3.4
), a family of sequences sampling the value of the Busemann process on the horizontal edges that belong to the
x
-axis. As we have an eye to use
\{I^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}
as initial conditions for our LPP model in future work, a problem to which the relevant scaling is the
1:2:3
-KPZ scaling, the scaling for the initial condition is the diffusive one -
1:2
. For a fixed

we interpolate between the points of

and scale diffusively to obtain a continuous function

on the real line. One should think of

as a process that takes values in the space of continuous functions and is indexed by its drift
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. The goal of this section is to show that

converges in some sense to some non-trivial limit

which we refer to as the

. The processes

are rcll, inherited from Property (
5
) of the Busemann process, hence the appropriate and perhaps natural sense of convergence is that of the Skorohod topology.

This section contains four subsections, intended to establish the two necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence in the Skorohord topology (see Theorem
A.15
). Section
4.1
introduces the prelimiting sequence
\{G^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, the space in which it lives and its associated topology. Here we also prove Condition
2
of Theorem
A.15
. Section
4.2
shows that the finite dimensional distributions of

converge weakly, and so verifying Condition
1
of Theorem
A.15
. Finally, Section
4.3
combines the results of the preceding Sections to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem

.

4.2
Finite dimensional distributions of the stationary horizon

The proof for the finite dimensional convergence to the SH here is different then the one that appeared in earlier versions of the paper and was communicated to the author by Evan Sorensen. The same proof was used in

to show convergence of the TASEP speed process to the the SH in the finite dimensional sense. As the arguments in the following proof are soft, it results in a much shorter and arguably cleaner proof.

For any continuous functions

and

on
\mathbb{R}
such

and

\sup_{-\infty<s\leq 0}[g(s)-f(s)]<\infty,

\displaystyle\Phi(g,f)(t)=f(t)+\sup_{-\infty<s\leq t}[g(s)-f(s)]-\sup_{-\infty<s\leq 0}[g(s)-f(s)].

(4.25)

The map in (

[
34
]
) and it satisfies the following important property;

\displaystyle\text{If $B^{1}$ and $B^{2}$ are Brownian motions with diffusivity $\sigma>0$ and drift $\mu_{1}$ and $\mu_{2}$ respectively,}

(4.26)

\displaystyle\text{then $\Phi(B^{1},B^{2})$ is again a bi-infinite Brownian motion with diffusivity $\sigma$ and drift $\mu_{2}$,}

\displaystyle\text{and that vanishes at the origin.}

In Corollary
A.19
we show that the map
\Phi
can be seen as a continuous version of the map

acting on continuous functions rather than sequences, where the first argument can be thought of as the cumulative service times while the second as cumulative inter-arrival times.

Next we would like to find an analogue of

for some

1.

\Phi^{1}(f_{1})=f_{1}

2.

\Phi^{2}(f_{1},f_{2})=[f_{1},\Phi(f_{1},f_{2})]

3.

\displaystyle\Phi^{k}(f_{1},...,f_{k})=[f_{1},\Phi\big(f_{1},[\Phi^{k-1}(f_{2},...,f_{k})]_{1}\big),...,\Phi\big(f_{1},[\Phi^{k-1}(f_{2},...,f_{k})]_{k-1}\big)]\quad k\geq 3.

(4.27)

It is helpful to compare the map
\Phi^{k}
to the map
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}
form (
2.12
), as well as the construction in (

) with that in (
2.2
). For

we denote the

For a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and integers
k\leq l
, we denote by
x^{k,l}=\sum_{i=k}^{l}x_{i}
. We use the convention
x^{k,l}=0
whenever
l<k
.

Lemma A.18
.

Let
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{s}
be such that
\mathbf{d}=D(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{s})
is well defined. Let
x_{i}=s_{i-1}-a_{i}
. Then

d^{k,l}=\sup_{-\infty<i\leq l}x^{i,l}-w_{k-1}+a^{k,l}+s_{l}-s_{k-1}

(A.46)

Proof.

Denote
x_{i}=s_{i-1}-a_{i}
. From equations (A.8) and (A.11) in
[
4
]

\displaystyle d^{k,l}=

\displaystyle\Big(\inf_{k\leq i\leq l}w_{k-1}+x^{k,i}\Big)^{-}+s^{k,l}

(A.47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sup_{k\leq i\leq l}-w_{k-1}-x^{k,i}\Big)^{+}+s^{k,l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{k-1\leq i\leq l}y^{k-1,i}+s^{k,l}=\sup_{k-1\leq i\leq l}(y^{k-1,l}-y^{i+1,l}+s^{k,l})

where

y_{i}=\begin{cases}0&i=k-1\\
-w_{k-1}-x_{i}&i=k\\
-x_{i}&i>k.\end{cases}

(A.48)

We have

y^{k-1,l}+s^{k,l}=-w_{k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{l}(a_{i}-s_{i-1})+\sum_{i=k}^{l}s_{i}=-w_{k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{l}a_{i}+s_{l}-s_{k-1}

(A.49)

Plugging the last display into (
A.47
)

\displaystyle d^{k,l}=

\displaystyle-w_{k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{l}a_{i}+s_{l}-s_{k-1}+\sup_{k-1\leq i\leq l}(-y^{i+1,l})

(A.50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-w_{k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{l}a_{i}+s_{l}-s_{k-1}+\sup_{k-1\leq i\leq l}(-y^{i+1,l})

Note that

\sup_{k-1\leq i\leq l}(-y^{i+1,l})=\max\big\{w_{k-1}+x^{k,l},\sup_{k+1\leq i\leq l}x^{i,l}\big\}=\sup_{-\infty<i\leq l}x^{i,l}

(A.51)

Plugging the last display into (
A.50
) we obtain the result.
∎

In the next result we use the notation
\hat{\mathbf{s}}:=\{\hat{s}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
where
\hat{s}_{i}=s_{i-1}
.

Corollary A.19
.

Let
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{d}
be as in Lemma
A.18
. Then for every
t\in\mathbb{R}

\Psi[\mathbf{d}](t)=\sup_{-\infty<s\leq t}\Big[\Psi[\hat{\mathbf{s}}](s)-\Psi[\mathbf{a}](s)\Big]-\sup_{-\infty<s\leq 0}\Big[\Psi[\hat{\mathbf{s}}](s)-\Psi[\mathbf{a}](s)\Big]+\Psi[\bm{a}](t)+\mathcal{E}(t),

(A.52)

where for every
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\sup_{|s|\leq M}\big|\mathcal{E}(s)\big|\leq 4\sup_{i\in[-M,M]}|s_{i}|+|a_{i}|.

(A.53)

Proof.

From Lemma
A.18

\displaystyle d^{1,l}=

\displaystyle\sup_{-\infty<i\leq l}x^{i,l}-w_{0}+a^{1,l}+s_{l-1}-s_{0}\qquad l\geq 1

(A.54)

\displaystyle d^{l,0}=

\displaystyle w_{l-1}-\sup_{-\infty<i\leq 0}x^{i,0}-a^{l,0}+s_{l-1}-s_{0}\qquad l\leq-1.

from which it follows that

\Psi[\mathbf{d}](t)=\begin{cases}\sup_{-\infty<i\leq l}x^{i,l}-\sup_{-\infty<s\leq-1}\Psi[\bm{x}](s)+\Psi[\mathbf{a}](t)+s_{0}-s_{t-1}&t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\\
0&t=0\\
\sup_{-\infty<s\leq t}\Psi[\bm{x}](s)-\sup_{-\infty<i\leq 0}x^{i,0}+\Psi[\mathbf{a}](t)+s_{t}-s_{0}&t\in\mathbb{Z}_{-}\end{cases}

(A.55)

The last display translates to

\Psi[\mathbf{d}](t)=\sup_{-\infty<s\leq t}\Psi[\bm{x}](s)-\sup_{-\infty<s\leq 0}\Psi[\bm{x}](s)+\Psi[\bm{a}](t)+\mathcal{E}(t)\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

(A.56)

where
\mathcal{E}
satisfies (
A.53
). The result now follows from the linearity of
\Psi
and the definition of
\bm{x}
.
∎
