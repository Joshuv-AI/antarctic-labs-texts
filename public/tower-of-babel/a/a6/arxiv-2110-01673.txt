Model-completeness and decidability of the additive structure of integers expanded with a function for a Beatty sequence
Mohsen Khani, Ali N. Valizadeh, Afshin Zarei

Abstract
. We introduce a model-complete theory which completely axiomatizes the structure 𝒵 α = ⟨ ℤ , + , 0 , 1 , f ⟩ \mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathbbm{Z},+,0,1,f\rangle where f : x ↦ ⌊ α ​ x ⌋ f:x\mapsto\lfloor\alpha x\rfloor is a unary function with α \alpha a fixed transcendental number. When α \alpha is computable, our theory is recursively enumerable, and hence decidable as a result of completeness. Therefore, this result fits into the more general theme of adding traces of multiplication to integers without losing decidability.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 03B25; Secondary 03C10, 11U09, 11U05

1.
Introduction

The subject of this study is the structure
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathbbm{Z},+,0,1,f\rangle
which contains the integer addition together with a
trace
of multiplication; namely the function
\left\lfloor\alpha x\right\rfloor
whose range is the Beatty sequence with modulo
\alpha
.

Our results lie in the intersection of two active research programs.
On the one hand, it relates to the recent works on
decidability of the expansions of
\langle\mathbbm{Z},+\rangle
as well as their classification either as stable structures, as in

[
C18
]
and
[
CP18
]
, or
unstable structures as in
[
KS17
]
.
In this sense,
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is an instance of an unstable yet decidable expansion of
\langle\mathbb{Z},+\rangle
.

On the other hand,
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is definable in
the structure
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}=\langle\mathbbm{R},<,+,0,\mathbbm{Z},\alpha\mathbbm{Z}\rangle
which lies
in the more general theme of research studying the expansions of
real line with specific discrete additive subgroups.
Most relevant to our work is Hieronymi’s theorem in
[
H16
]
which shows, for the special case
of a quadratic
\alpha
, that the theory of the structure
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
—and as a result

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
—is decidable. Decidability is proved there by showing that
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
is definable in the monadic second-order structure
\langle\mathbbm{N},P(\mathbbm{N}),\in,x\mapsto x+1\rangle
which was already known to be decidable (
[
B62
]
).

The results on
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
have been recently generalized in several directions. In particular, it is shown in
[
HM+21
]
that the common theory of the structures
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is decidable when
\alpha
ranges over irrational numbers less than one. A main feature of the proof appeared there is designing a Büchi automaton which can perform addition over Ostrowski numeration systems.

The current study is along similar lines to
the result by
the first and third authors in
[
KZ22
]

where they applied only simple techniques of model theory
to provide
an alternative proof for the
decidability of
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
in the case that
\alpha
is the golden ratio.
Here, by applying elementary tools in model theory and number theory, we prove that the theory of
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is decidable when
\alpha
is a computable transcendental number. To our knowledge, in the latter case (transcendental
\alpha
) nothing is known about the decidability of the more general structure
\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}
, and we believe that our result forms an important step towards solving that problem as well.

We will discuss the definable sets of
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
later in Subsection
5.2
. But, to have a general picture of the model theory involved in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
, note in particular that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is a model of the theory of
\mathbbm{Z}
-groups, or Presburger arithmetic without order, and hence it defines all congruence classes or arithmetic progressions. The latter are a typical example of definable sets in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
with a somewhat “structured” nature.

On the other hand, there are definable sets in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
with a “random” behaviour, and these sets are typically the subsets of
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
defined using the powers of the function
f
. An aspect of this randomness is reflected in the fact that these sets do not contain an infinite
arithmetic progression (see Subsection
5.2
for more details). However, this random behaviour is actually a consequence of definability of a linear order which turns out to be dense by means of Kronecker’s approximation lemma (Fact
3.1
).

When applied to the simple case of an irrational number
\alpha
, Kronecker’s lemma says that the set of decimal parts of the sequence
\{\alpha n:n\in\mathbbm{N}\}
is dense in the unit interval
(0,1)
.

However, as it will be expanded later, we need the full strength of Kronecker’s lemma. In fact, when
\alpha
is transcendental, all different powers of
f
take an independent part in creating the random behaviour; this is due to independence of all different powers of
\alpha
acting underneath the language. This in fact contrasts the case of a quadratic
\alpha
where this randomness is actually weaker and dependent only on the first power of
\alpha
, mainly because all terms of the language reduce to a linear form; for example,
f^{2}(x)
is equal to
f(x)+x-1
when
\alpha
is the golden ratio (see
[
KZ22
]
).

The following motivating propositions–that won’t directly be used in the paper–reflect
the basic idea on which most of our arguments rely. The idea is
to solve a given system of equations in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
by turning, whenever possible, equations into
inequalities in terms of the decimal parts.
The solution to the system will then be obtained by
an application of
k
-dimensional Kronecker’s lemma, Fact
3.1
, asserting the density of the set
\{([\beta_{1}n],\ldots,[\beta_{k}n])\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

in
(0,1)^{k}
for a

\mathbbm{Q}
-linear independent set of real numbers

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
. Recall that the decimal part of a real number
x
is denoted by
\left[x\right],
namely
\left[x\right]:=x-\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbbm{Z}
:

(1)

a
is in the range of
f
if and only if
\left[\frac{a}{\alpha}\right]
is greater than
1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
. Moreover,
a=f(b)
implies that
a=\left\lfloor\frac{b}{\alpha}\right\rfloor+1
. If
\alpha
is positive and less than
1
, then
f
is a surjection.

(2)

f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)+\ell
for some
\ell\in\{0,1\}
, where
\ell
is equal to
0
if and only if
\left[\alpha a\right]+\left[\alpha b\right]<1
.

Proof.

Part (2) is obvious, for part (1) use the fact that
a=f(b)
implies
\alpha b-1<a<\alpha b.

∎

Fact 1.2

(Kronecker’s approximation lemma, one dimensional)
.

The set of decimal parts of the sequence
\{\alpha n:n\in\mathbbm{N}\}
is dense in the unit interval
(0,1)
.

Proposition 1.3
.

The following system of congruence equations has infinitely many solutions in
\mathbbm{Z}
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m}}{{\equiv}}i\\
f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\equiv}}j.\end{cases}

where
x
is the variable, and
m,n,i,j\in\mathbbm{N}
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
f(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\equiv}}j
if and only if
[\frac{\alpha}{n}x]\in(\frac{j}{n},\frac{j+1}{n})
. Hence, to solve the system above, it suffices to find
y\in\mathbbm{Z}
such that

\left[\frac{(my+i)\alpha}{n}\right]\in(\frac{j}{n},\frac{j+1}{n}).

If
\frac{j+1}{n}>[\frac{i}{n}\alpha]
, by Fact
1.2
we choose
y
such that

\displaystyle\left[\frac{m\alpha}{n}y\right]<1-[\frac{i}{n}\alpha],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\frac{j}{n}-\left[\frac{i}{n}\alpha\right]<\left[\frac{m\alpha}{n}y\right]<\frac{j+1}{n}-\left[\frac{i}{n}\alpha\right].

In the case that
\frac{j+1}{n}<[\frac{i}{n}\alpha]
, again by Fact
1.2
we choose
y
such that

\displaystyle\left[\frac{m\alpha}{n}y\right]>1-\left[\frac{i}{n}\alpha\right],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle 1+\frac{j}{n}-\left[\frac{i}{n}\alpha\right]<\left[\frac{m\alpha}{n}y\right]<1+\frac{j+1}{n}-\left[\frac{i}{n}\alpha\right].

∎

We will gradually present a natural theory
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
for

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
by introducing each axiom or axiom scheme
right after
proving the related properties for
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
.
So, each of the sections below will contain axioms that ensure a partial model-completeness for the final theory
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.
In addition, we will justify how incorporating the further assumption of computability for
\alpha
guarantees the recursive enumerability of our axioms.
We finish by our Main Theorem showing that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is model-complete and, for a computable
\alpha
, this suffices for
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
to be decidable.

We find it helpful to give
a summary of our arguments leading to the proof of model-completeness as follows:

Step 1.

We treat certain relations among the decimal parts as first-order
\mathcal{L}
-formulas (Section
2
).

Step 2.

We divide systems of equations in
\mathcal{L}
into two main categories of non-algebraic (Section
3
) and algebraic formulas (Section
4
).

Step 3.

We use an extended version of Kronecker lemma (Theorem
3.4
) to show that solvability of a system of non-algebraic formulas is equivalent to a quantifier-free
\mathcal{L}
-formula (Theorem
3.10
).

Step 4.

For two models
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
, we show that the solution, in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, of an algebraic system involving only a single variable and with parameters in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, belongs also to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
(Lemma
4.13
). This implicitly shows that a substructure of a model of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is closed under taking inverse of different powers of
f
.

Step 5.

We use a technical trick (Lemma
4.12
) to show that an algebraic system which contains more than two variables reduces to a non-algebraic system of smaller number of variables.

Our last section will contain some additional observations and remarks.

Convention
.

(1)

\alpha
is a fixed computable transcendental number.

(2)

We will be working in the language
\mathcal{L}=\{+,-,0,1,f\}
and unless we state otherwise all formulas are assumed
to be in
\mathcal{L}
. In particular, all axioms and axiom schemes are
\mathcal{L}
-formulas.

(3)

When there is no mention of a model or a theory,
the lemmas and theorems below
concern the structure

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
, and hence
the
variables and parameters will range over the set of integers
\mathbbm{Z}
.

(4)

We occasionally use a finite partial type
\Gamma(x)
as a conjunction of
\mathcal{L}
-formulas as well; that is, we freely use notations like
\exists x\Gamma(x)
instead of writing
\displaystyle{\exists x\bigwedge_{\varphi(x)\in\Gamma(x)}\varphi(x)}
.

A note on overlapping results.

Short before submitting this paper, we learnt of a similar independent work by
Günaydin and Özsahakyan uploaded in arXiv
[
GO22
]
.
The main difference
between the two papers, is that in
[

]

the authors consider the Beatty sequence as a

2.
Describing decimals in
\mathcal{L}

Although our language/theory does not literally contain the decimals, similar observations to Proposition
1.1
show that our theory is expressive enough to describe key properties of decimals by capturing their order and dense distribution in the spirit of Fact
3.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
n\in\mathbbm{N}
with
n\neq 0
.

(1)

There exists
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
such that

f(na)=nf(a)+i.

Indeed
f(na)=nf(a)+i
if and only if
\frac{i}{n}<\left[\alpha a\right]<\frac{i+1}{n}
.

(2)

[\alpha a]+[\alpha b]<1
if and only if
f(a+b)=f(b)+f(b).

(3)

[\alpha a]<[\alpha b]
if and only if
f(b-a)=f(b)-f(a)
.

Proof.

Easy to verify.
∎

Axiom scheme 1

(
for various
n\in\mathbbm{N}
and
0\leq i<n
with
f(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\equiv}}i
)
.

f(\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{n\text{ times}})=\underbrace{f(1)+\cdots+f(1)}_{n\text{ times}}+\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{i\text{ times}}.

Remark 2.2
.

(1)

We can use Axiom scheme
1
together with part (2) of Axiom
2
below to precisely determine in our theory the value of
f(n)
for every
n\in\mathbbm{Z}
. This property is crucial for our theory in order to have
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
as its prime model, and the latter will, in turn, be required for proving the completeness of our theory
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.

(2)

By part (1) of Lemma
2.1
, we see that the rational cut of
\alpha
is included in Axiom scheme
1
. Hence, the computability of
\alpha
is a necessary condition for this axiom scheme to be recursively enumerable.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
m,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
n\in\mathbbm{N}
.

(1)

There exists a quantifier-free
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,y)
such that for all
a,b\in\mathbbm{Z}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models\varphi(a,b)
if and only if

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left[\alpha a\right]<\frac{m}{n}\left[\alpha b\right].

(2)

There exists a quantifier-free
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,y)
such that for all
a,b\in\mathbbm{Z}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models\varphi(a,b)
if and only if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\ell_{1}<n\left[\alpha a\right]+m\left[\alpha b\right]<\ell_{2}.

Proof.

For part (1), first suppose that we have
0<m<n
; other cases can be proved similarly or else they turn into triviality.

Note that there are
i,j\in\mathbbm{N}
with
0\leq i<n
and
0\leq j<m
such that

\left[\alpha a\right]\in(\frac{i}{n},\frac{i+1}{n})
and
\left[\alpha b\right]\in(\frac{j}{m},\frac{j+1}{m})
. The latter conditions are
\mathcal{L}
-expressible using part (1) of Lemma
2.1
. Also, observe that non-trivial cases only happen when
i<m
and
j=i
; that is, both
\left[\alpha a\right]
and
\frac{m}{n}\left[\alpha b\right]
belong to the same interval
(\frac{i}{n},\frac{i+1}{n})
. In this case, using parts (3) and (1) of Lemma
2.1
, we can show that
n\left[\alpha a\right]<m\left[\alpha b\right]
if and only if
f(mb-na)=mf(b)-nf(a)
.

For part (2), note that, depending on whether
m
is negative or positive, non-trivial cases occur only when we have that
-m\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq n
or
0\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq n+m
, respectively. Then, apply an argument similar to the proof of part (1).
∎

The following corollary is a consequence of part (2) of Lemma
2.3
:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbbm{Z}
, and
\bar{m},\bar{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}
. Then, there exists a quantifier-free

-formula
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, depending on
k,\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\bar{m}
and
\bar{n}
, such that
for all
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}
,

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models\varphi(\bar{a},\bar{b})
if and only if

(2.3)

\displaystyle\ell_{1}<\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\left[\alpha b_{i}\right]<\ell_{2}.

Notation 2.5
.

From now on and by injecting suitable variables, we treat various forms of inequalities (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and those appeared in Lemma

as first-order
\mathcal{L}
-formulas. For example, by writing
\ell_{1}<n\left[\alpha x\right]+m\left[\alpha y\right]<\ell_{2}

Using our observations so far and by applying our flexible notation, we are able to encode into our language some of the main features of the decimals involved in the structure
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Note again that, to a decimal like
\left[\alpha a\right]
nothing can be designated in

. In fact,
\mathcal{L}
is capable of describing these decimals merely when they participate in an inequality of the form (

In Section

we deal with axiomatizing the properties of

formulas, that is, formulas containing decimals of the form
[\alpha f^{i}(x)]
(see Definition

). A typical example is the formula

3.
Non-algebraic formulas

As mentioned earlier, some of the essential properties of the function
f
will be described as a consequence of Kronecker’s approximation lemma,
and our aim is to exploit the full extent of
this fact in our axiomatization. A proof of this theorem can be found in
[
HW08
, Theorem 442]
.

Fact 3.1

(Kronecker’s Approximation Lemma)
.

Let
n\in\mathbbm{N}
and let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}
be
such that
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n},1
are
linearly independent over
\mathbbm{Q}
.
Then the set

\big\{([\beta_{1}x],\ldots,[\beta_{n}x]):{x\in\mathbbm{N}}\big\}
is dense in
(0,1)^{n}
.

As a direct result of Kronecker’s lemma, we can readily verify that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
satisfies part (4) of the subsequent axiom. However, it is essential to remember Notation
2.5
, and in particular, that “
\left[\alpha x\right]<\left[\alpha y\right]
” is simply our notation for expressing the formula
f(y-x)=f(y)-f(x)
. Furthermore, it is straightforward to confirm that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
conforms to the remaining three parts of the axiom.

Axiom 2
.

(1)

\forall x\forall y\big(f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)\ \vee\ f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)+1\big)
.

(2)

\forall x\big(f(-x)=-f(x)-1\big)
.

(3)

\forall y\exists x\big(\bigvee_{i=0}^{f(1)}y+i=f(x)\big)
.

(4)

The relation
[\alpha x]<[\alpha y]
is a dense linear order.

Remark 3.2
.

Based on our notation, part (1) of Axiom
2
logically implies that
for all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
we have

\bigvee_{j=0}^{n-1}j<\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\alpha x_{i}]<j+1.

Proof.

With regard to Notation
2.5
, the formula above is simply another way of expressing the following formula:

\bigvee_{j=0}^{n-1}f(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})=f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{n})+j,

which can be demonstrated by
n
-time applying part (1) of Axiom
2
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

An interesting, but not directly relevant, observation
is that each
n\mathbbm{Z}
is dense in
\mathbbm{Z}

with respect to the order relation defined by the decimal parts (Axiom
2
). More generally, given any congruence class

n\mathbbm{Z}+i
and any interval

I=(\left[\alpha a\right],\left[\alpha b\right])\subset(0,1)
, there are infinitely many integers
c\in n\mathbbm{Z}+i
such that
\left[\alpha c\right]\in I
. This can be proved
using the ideas appeared in the proof of Proposition
1.3
.

For given
a,b\in\mathbbm{Z}
, decimals other than
\left[\alpha a\right]
and
\left[\alpha b\right]
also intervene in investigating formulas like
f^{n}(a+b)=f^{n}(a)+f^{n}(b)+\ell
where the exact value of
\ell
is determined by the values of the decimals below:

\left[\alpha a\right],\left[\alpha b\right],\left[\alpha f(a)\right],\left[\alpha f(b)\right],\cdots,\left[\alpha f^{n-1}(a)\right],\left[\alpha f^{n-1}(b)\right].

To engage with such decimals we aim to expand on the idea of the proof of Proposition
1.3
further as follows: Let
f^{0}(x)=x
and notice that since
\alpha
is transcendental, each finite sequence
1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n}
is
\mathbbm{Q}
-linearly independent. This fact provides us with
a more amount of “control” over the decimals of the form
\left[\alpha f^{i}(x)\right]
as in the following theorem and the related axiom scheme.
The following theorem concerns the natural numbers and
the word “dense” there has its
usual meaning in the reals.

Theorem 3.4

(Extended Kronecker’s Lemma)
.

For every
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, the following set of
(n+1)
-tuples is dense in
(0,1)^{n+1}
:

\Big\{\left([\alpha a],[\alpha f(a)],[\alpha f^{2}(a)],\cdots,[\alpha f^{n}(a)]\right):a\in\mathbbm{N}\Big\}.

Proof.

For
a\in\mathbbm{N}
,
note that
\alpha f(a)=\alpha^{2}a-\alpha\left[\alpha a\right]
,
and an easy induction shows that for every
k\geq 2
we have that

\alpha f^{k}(a)=\alpha^{k+1}a-\alpha\left[\alpha^{k}a\right]+\alpha(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k-1}),

where each
j_{i}
is an integer with
|j_{i}|\in\{0,\ldots,\left\lfloor\alpha\right\rfloor\}
determined by the inequality
\frac{j_{i}}{\alpha}<\left[\alpha^{i}a\right]<\frac{j_{i}+1}{\alpha}
.

The remainder of the proof is based on the following straightforward observation: Consider
J_{1},\ldots,J_{n}
as given subintervals of

(0,1)
. Given any subinterval
I_{0}
of
(0,1)
with sufficiently small length, it is possible to find subintervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
of
(0,1)
such that for each
k=1,\ldots,n
, the cumulative sum
I_{0}+I_{1}+\ldots+I_{k}
is contained within
J_{k}
. Here,
I+J
means the set
\{\left[x+y\right]\mid x\in I,y\in J\}
.

Now, suppose that we are to find an
a\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
\left[\alpha f^{i}(a)\right]
belongs to the specified subinterval
J_{i}
of
(0,1)
for each
i
in the set
\{0,\ldots,n\}
. To achieve this, we only need to select a sufficiently small subinterval
I_{0}
of
J_{0}
, determine intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
as described earlier, and find an element
a\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
[\alpha^{i}a]
belongs to
I_{i}
for each
i
. The existence of such
a
is guaranteed by Kronecker’s approximation theorem. ∎

|\bar{y}|=|\bar{m}|=|\bar{n}|=k+1

\vartriangleleft

More generally, define a

with

and
|\bar{y}|=k^{\prime}+1

as a quantifier-free
\mathcal{L}
-formula of the following form

(3.2)

\displaystyle\hskip 35.0pt\sum_{i=0}^{\ell_{1}}n_{1i}\left[\alpha f^{i}(x_{1})\right]+\cdots+\sum_{i=0}^{\ell_{k}}n_{ki}\left[\alpha f^{i}(x_{k})\right]\text{\large$\vartriangleleft$}\sum_{i=0}^{k^{\prime}}m_{i}\left[\alpha y_{i}\right]+\left[\alpha q\right]+\ell

for some
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k},q\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\ell,m_{i},n_{ji}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
\vartriangleleft
as above.

(2)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
\mathcal{L}
-structure,

Hence, a given non-algebraic type

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(x+b)&=f(x)+f(b)+\ell_{1}\\
f^{2}(x+b)&=f(f(x)+f(b)+\ell_{1})=f^{2}(x)+f(f(b)+\ell_{1})+\ell_{2}\\
f^{3}(x+b)&=f(f^{2}(x)+f(f(b)+\ell_{1})+\ell_{2})\\
&=f^{3}(x)+f(f(f(b)+\ell_{1})+\ell_{2})+\ell_{3}\\
&\vdots\end{split}

\displaystyle[\alpha x]+[\alpha b]<1

\displaystyle\quad(>1)

\displaystyle[\alpha f(x)]+[\alpha(f(b)+\ell_{1})]<1

\displaystyle\quad(>1)

\displaystyle[\alpha f^{2}(x)]+[\alpha(f(f(b)+\ell_{1})+\ell_{2})]<1

\displaystyle\quad(>1)

\displaystyle\vdots.

Lemma

below provides a quantifier-free condition for a non-algebraic type to have a solution. To get the idea of its proof via an example,
consider an inequality of the form (

\displaystyle-3<4\left[7\alpha\right]-6\left[9\alpha\right]+\left[8\alpha\right]+5<2.

This is indeed the Fourier-Motzkin elimination algorithm for solving system of linear inequalities in real numbers.

(4.1)

for some

.
Also we call a term of the form (

Definition 4.1

An
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};y)
is called

if it is of the form

where

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
h(x)
is an
f
-polynomial. Then, there exists a minimal
K_{h}\in\mathbbm{N}
, depending on coefficients appearing in (
4.1
), such that:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}\models\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\Big(\bigvee_{j=-K_{h}}^{K_{h}}h(x_{1}+x_{2})=h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+j\Big),\text{ and }

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}\models\forall x\Big(\bigvee_{j=0}^{K_{h}}h(-x)=-h(x)-j\Big).

Proof.

Based on part (1) of Axiom
2
, we can use a simple induction on

to obtain a minimal positive integer
K_{i}
such that for every
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
we have that

\bigvee_{j=0}^{K_{i}}f^{i}(x_{1}+x_{2})=f^{i}(x_{1})+f^{i}(x_{2})+j.

Now, given an
f
-polynomial

of the form (
4.1
), the required minimal

is a natural number–depending on the coefficient appearing in

–less than or equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|m_{i}|K_{i}
. This proves (
4.2
).

A similar argument as above and part (2) of Axiom
2
will work for (
4.3
).

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
h(x)
be an
f
-polynomial.

(1)

For every
n\in\mathbbm{N}
, there exists
K_{n}\in\mathbbm{N}
such that for every

we have

f^{n}(x)=\left\lfloor\alpha^{n}x\right\rfloor-j,

for some
j\in\{0,\ldots,K_{n}\}
.

(2)

There exist
k_{h}\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\beta_{h}\in\mathbbm{R}
such that for every

we have

h(x)=\left\lfloor\beta_{h}x\right\rfloor+j,

for some

satisfying
-k_{h}\leq j\leq k_{h}
. Moreover,
\beta_{h}
is equal to
\sum_{i=0}^{k}m_{i}\alpha^{i}
where the
m_{i}

are as in
(
4.1
).

(3)

There exists
N_{h}\in\mathbbm{N}
such that:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models\forall y\exists x\Big(\bigvee_{j=0}^{N_{h}}h(x)=y+j\Big).

(4)

For every
\ell\in\mathbbm{N}
, there exists an
\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{N}
such that

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models\forall x\forall y\ \Big(\big(\bigvee_{j=-\ell}^{\ell}h(x)-h(y)=j\big)\to\big(\bigvee_{j=-\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}(x-y=j\big)\Big).

Proof.

Part (1) can easily be proved using a simple induction on
n
, and the fact that
f^{n+1}(x)=\left\lfloor\alpha f^{n}(x)\right\rfloor
. Part (2) is also obvious using part (1) and the properties of the floor function.

For part (3), note that the function
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}x\right\rfloor
is monotone and unbounded. So, given

, there exists
x\in\mathbbm{Z}
such that

belongs to the interval with endpoints
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}x\right\rfloor
and
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}(x+1)\right\rfloor
. The length of this interval is at most
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}\right\rfloor+1
. By part (2), there are integers

such that

and
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}(x+1)\right\rfloor=h(x+1)+j_{2}
. Hence, we can take

to be
\left\lfloor\beta_{h}\right\rfloor+2k_{h}+1
.

For part (4), we can use part (2) to conclude that
|\beta_{h}(x-y)|\leq\ell+2k_{h}+1
whenever
|h(x)-h(y)|\leq\ell
. Hence, we can take
\ell^{\prime}
to be equal to
\frac{\ell+2k_{h}+1}{|\beta_{h}|}
.
∎

j^{*}_{i}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
x^{\prime}_{i}
with
|j^{*}_{i}|\leq N_{h^{*}_{i}}
such that

x_{i}=h^{*}_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i})-j^{*}_{i}
. This turns the algebraic part of (
4.7
) into:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\Big(h^{*}_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i})-j^{*}_{i}\Big)=h(w)-j.

Using Lemmas
4.2
and
4.11
, there are
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}
such that the left-hand side of the equation above turns into:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(h(x_{i})-h_{i}(j^{*}_{i})+j_{i}\right).

Definition
3.5
and the proof of Lemma
4.8
show that each
j_{i}
is determined by finitely many non-algebraic formulas, say
\Delta^{*}_{i}(x_{i})
. Additionally, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have
|j_{i}|\leq K^{\prime}
for some
K^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{N}
obtained based on the numbers
K_{h_{i}}
and
K
appearing in Lemmas
4.2
and
4.11
respectively.

All these turn the initial equation into:

h(x^{\prime}_{i}+\cdots+x^{\prime}_{n})-h(w)=J,

for some
J\in\mathbbm{Z}
. The proof of Corollary
4.9
shows that the exact value of
J
is determined by finitely many non-algebraic formulas; we denote it by
\Delta(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n})
.

By Axiom Scheme
6
, for some
J^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{Z}
we have that

x^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+x^{\prime}_{n}-w=J^{\prime}
,
or

x^{\prime}_{n}=w+J^{\prime}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x^{\prime}_{i}.

Thus replacing
x^{\prime}_{n}
and using Corollary
4.9
, the conjunction of formulas in
\Delta(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n})
will be equivalent to a disjunction of finitely many non-algebraic formulas
\Delta^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n-1},w)
. Similarly, the conjunction of formulas in
\Delta^{*}_{n}(x_{n})
introduced above is equivalent to the disjunction of a finite set of non-algebraic formulas which we denote by
\Delta^{*}(x_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n-1},w)
.

Since for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have
x_{i}=h^{*}_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i})-j^{*}_{i}
, we are able to replace each
x_{i}
in
\Gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};\bar{y})
accordingly. Hence, by Corollary
4.9
, each non-algebraic formula in
\Gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};\bar{y})
will be equivalent to a finite disjunction of non-algebraic formulas
\Gamma^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n};\bar{y})
. By replacing
x^{\prime}_{n}
and using Corollary
4.9
once again, each formula in
\Gamma^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n};\bar{y})
will be equivalent to the disjunction of a set of non-algebraic formulas which we denote by
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},w;\bar{y})
.

Finally, the following union is the finite set of non-algebraic formulas required by the lemma:

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\Delta^{*}_{i}(x_{i})\cup\Delta^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n-1},w)\cup\Delta^{*}(x_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n-1},w)\cup\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},w;\bar{y}).

∎

The following lemma considers situations where a model contains the solutions of an algebraic formula with a single variable and with parameters from a smaller model. We see that, the smaller model always contains solutions for another formula which is approximately the same as the original one. This surprisingly leads to finding the exact solutions of the original formula already inside the smaller model.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
be models of

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. Also, suppose that
b\in M_{1}
and
a\in M_{2}
are such that
h(a)=b
for some
f
-polynomial
h(x)
. Then, the element
a
belongs to
M_{1}
.

Proof.

By Axiom scheme
5
, there exist an integer
j\leq N_{h}
and an element
a^{\prime}\in M_{1}
such that we have
h(a^{\prime})=c+j
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Let
\ell=N_{h}
and use Axiom scheme
6
to conclude that
|a-a^{\prime}|\leq\ell^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Now, the axioms of
T_{0}
ensure that
a
is the
\ell^{\prime}
-th successor/predecessor of
a^{\prime}
, and this means that
a
is already a member of
M_{1}
.
∎

We combine all the previous results to prove Lemma
4.14
. As we see in the proof of the Main Theorem, the following lemma is in fact the final main step in showing that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is model-complete.

Lemma 4.14
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2}
are models of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.
Also, suppose that
\bar{b},c\in M_{1}
, that
\Gamma(\bar{x};\bar{b})
with
|\bar{x}|=n
is a finite set of non-algebraic formulas, and that
h_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,h_{n}(x_{n})
are given
f
-polynomials.
Then the following set of formulas is satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
if and only if it is satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
:

(4.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};\bar{b})\cup\Big\{h_{1}(x_{1})+\ldots+h_{n}(x_{n})=c\Big\}.

Proof.

By applying Lemma
4.12
for
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, there are
a\in M_{2}
, an
f
-polynomial
h(x)
, an element
J\in\mathbbm{Z}
with
h(a)=c+J
, and finitely many sets of non-algebraic formulas

\Gamma_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};a,\bar{b}),\ldots,\Gamma_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};a,\bar{b})

such that satisfiability of the set of formulas (
4.8
) in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is equivalent to the truth of a disjunctive formula of the form below:

\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}\left(\bigwedge\Gamma_{i}(\bar{x}^{\prime};\bar{y},w)\right).

Assuming (
4.8
) is satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, at least one of the mentioned sets, say
\Gamma_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};a,\bar{b})
, is satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

Since
c+J
is an element of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, we can use Lemma
4.13
to conclude that the element
a
is also a member of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Hence, by Theorem
3.10
, the set of formulas (
4.8
) is also satisfiable in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
∎

Main Theorem
.

Suppose that
\alpha
is a transcendental real number. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is a complete and model-complete axiomatization for the structure
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
, which has the strict order property. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is decidable whenever
\alpha
is computable.

Proof.

Based on Corollary
4.9
, we can assume that the non-algebraic formulas
\Gamma(\bar{x};\bar{y})
appearing in Lemma
4.14
are in the most general possible form. That is, we do not need to consider sets of non-algebraic formulas of the form
\Gamma\big(h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n});\bar{y}\big)
,
\Gamma\big(h_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,h_{n}(x_{n});\bar{y}\big)
, or the like.

Also, using Lemma
4.2
and similar to the proof of Corollary
4.9
, any term of the language can be replaced by an addition of single-variable terms at the expense of adding finitely many non-algebraic formulas. Hence, the most general form of a quantifier-free
\mathcal{L}
-formula can be considered as the conjunction of a finite set of non-algebraic formulas
\Gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};\bar{b})
with finitely many algebraic formulas of the form
h_{1}(x_{1})+\ldots+h_{n}(x_{n})=z
.

Moreover, Lemma
4.12
shows that the existence of one algebraic equation has two important effects: Firstly, it reduces the number of free variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
required to solve the formula in the smaller model. Secondly, it turns the whole formula into a disjunction of conjunctions of non-algebraic formulas, which of course contain an extra variable
w
. The proof of Lemma
4.14
shows how to handle this extra variable. Therefore, Lemma
4.14
suffices for model-completeness, and there is no need to enrich (
4.8
) with more algebraic formulas.

As mentioned in part (1) of Remark
2.2
, Axiom scheme
1
guarantees that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
is the prime model of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, and this proves the completeness of this theory.

It is obvious that Axiom
2
and Axiom scheme
3
are recursively enumerable even if
\alpha
is not computable. As addressed in Remark
3.8
, the computability of
\alpha
is not required for listing the instances of Axiom scheme
4
either. But, as mentioned in part (2) of Remark
2.2
and also in Remark
4.5
, the computability of
\alpha
is needed and in fact suffices for Axiom schemes
1
,
5
, and
6
to be recursively enumerable.

The strict order property is implied by the fact that the dense linear order relation “
\left[\alpha x\right]<\left[\alpha y\right]
” is definable in
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.
∎

.

By part (1) of Remark
2.2
, a direct consequence of Axiom scheme
1
is that the first-order theory of structures
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
are not elementarily equivalent for different numbers
\alpha
and

. In view of the results appearing in
[
HM+21
]
, part of the following common theory is reflected in our axioms:

\bigcap_{\alpha\in\mathbbm{Q}^{\cMe}}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}.

Remark 4.16
.

Simpler forms of the techniques used in this paper are applied, among others,
in
[
KZ22
]
to eliminate quantifiers for

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
when
\alpha
is the golden ratio. In this case, the specific formula that expresses being in the range of
f
, namely
\exists yf(y)=x
, is equivalent to the quantifier-free formula
x=f(f(x)-x+1)
.
This is mainly due to the fact that for every
x\in\mathbbm{Z}
we have
f^{2}(x)=f(x)+x-1
, which in turn holds because of the algebraic dependence
\alpha^{2}=\alpha+1
.
There does not seem to exist an easy way to eliminate quantifiers even for such a simple formula in the case of a transcendental
\alpha
.

Remark 4.17
.

Our proofs in this paper can be checked to show also an
effective
model-completeness result for the theory of
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
. In fact, based on the proofs appeared in Section
3
, one can use the effective quantifier elimination available in the ordered field of reals to effectively obtain an equivalent quantifier-free formula for any formula of the form
\exists\bar{x}\theta(\bar{x};\bar{y})
where
\theta(\bar{x};\bar{y})
is non-algebraic. Using formula (
4.2
) and Axiom
6
, one can effectively find a universal formula equivalent to
\exists x\big(h(x)=y\big)
for an
f
-polynomial
h(x)
. For an example, when
\alpha
equals Euler’s number
e
the formula
\exists x\big(f(x)=y\big)
is equivalent to

\forall x\Big(y-1\neq f(x)\wedge y+1\neq f(x)\Big).

For systems involving more than one existential variables and containing an algebraic formula, one can apply the proof of Lemma
4.12
to effectively reduce the number of existential variables.

5.
Concluding remarks

5.1.
The case of an algebraic
\alpha

The techniques used in this paper can be applied to obtain the same result for an algebraic
\alpha
. In fact, when
\alpha
satisfies an equation of a minimal degree like

(5.1)

\displaystyle\alpha^{n}+k_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}+\ldots+k_{0}=0,

with integer coefficients, we can use (
5.1
) to calculate the value of a decimal
\left[\alpha f^{m}(x)\right]
, with
m\geq n
, in terms of the decimals

\left[\alpha f(x)\right],\ldots,\left[\alpha f^{n-1}(x)\right].

At the same time, each
f
-polynomial
h(x)
can be assumed to contain powers of
f
strictly less than
n
. Having made the mentioned adjustments, the rest of the argument can easily be carried out.

5.2.
On definable sets

Based on the terminology used in
[
T08
, Section 3.1]
, there appear three fundamental types of sets in various areas of mathematics: The “structured” sets, the “random” sets, and sets of a “hybrid” nature. Below, we make a concise clarification on this phenomena concerning the definable sets in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
.

If a power of
f
does not appear in an existential formula
\varphi(x)
with a single free variable
x
, then the quantifier elimination available in Presburger arithmetic shows that
\varphi(x)
is actually describing a congruence class to which
x
belongs. So in this case,
\varphi(x)
defines an infinite arithmetic progression which is a typical example of a “structured” set by having a completely predictable behaviour.

On the contrary, Connell proved in
[
C60
, Theorem 2]
that no Beatty sequence with an irrational modulo can contain an infinite arithmetic progression. That is the set of solutions of a formula like
\exists y\big(x=f^{n}(y)\big)
, that forms a typical example of an existential formula containing a power of
f
, cannot contain an infinite arithmetic progression. It is not clear to us if the same fact holds for formulas of the form
\exists y\big(x=f(y)+f^{2}(y)\big)
that contain an addition of terms; the latter question might be of interest from the perspective of additive combinatorics.

However in proposition below, and using Theorem
3.1
, we prove that in the range of any term of one variable we can find finite arithmetic progressions of arbitrary large length. This slightly generalizes a similar result by Connell in
[
C60
, Theorem 2]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
h(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}m_{i}f^{i}(x)
. For every
n\in\mathbbm{N}
there exists an arithmetic progression of length
n
in the range of
h
in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

To have an arithmetic progression of length
n
, it suffices to find
x
and
y
such that for every
\ell\leq n
we have that
h(x+\ell y)=h(x)+\ell h(y).
And the latter holds whenever there are
x,y\in\mathbbm{Z}
such that
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
satisfies the following non-algebraic formula for every
1\leq i\leq k

f^{i}(x+ny)=f^{i}(x)+nf^{i}(y),

or equivalently whenever we have the following for every
0\leq i\leq k-1

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\models 0<\left[\alpha f^{i}(x)\right]+n\left[\alpha f^{i}(y)\right]<1.

But, Theorem
3.1
allows us to find
x
and
y
with the desired properties.
∎

A similar argument as in the proof of proposition above shows that for an existential formula
\varphi(x)
of more than one existential variable, the set of solutions
\varphi(\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha})
contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrary finite lengths.

The latter observation shows that the formulas containing a power of
f
behaves more-or-less similar to prime numbers in that they do not contain infinite arithmetic progression whereas they do contain arbitrary long finite arithmetic progressions (
[
GT08
]
). However, Proposition
1.3
shows that such definable sets may differ from the primes in intersecting each congruence class at infinitely many points. But it seems reasonable to consider them as hybrid sets in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
just like as we do for primes in
\mathbbm{Z}
.

To sum up, the structured definable sets in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
are disjoint-by-finite from the hybrid sets, and still another interesting phenomena occurs in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
when we consider two mentioned types of sets from the perspective of the order topology available in
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
by the formula
\left[\alpha x\right]<\left[\alpha y\right]
. In fact, both the structured and hybrid sets find a uniform description in this topology by being simultaneously dense and co-dense there.

5.3.
A connection to o-minimality

We show that the non-algebraic part of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, which we have denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
in Section
3
, gives rise to an o-minimal theory that embodies its main features.

First for a model
\mathcal{M}\models\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
we associate a structure
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
in a language
\mathcal{L}^{*}
that contains a set of predicates meant to capture the non-algebraic content of
\mathcal{M}
.

So let
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\Big\{<,P_{\bar{m},\bar{n},\ell}\Big\}_{\bar{m},\bar{n},\ell\in\mathbbm{Z}}
where each
P_{\bar{m},\bar{n},\ell}
accepts tuples of arity
|\bar{m}|+|\bar{n}|
.
Fix
\mathcal{M}\models\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
and let
A_{\mathcal{M}}
be the subset of (possibly non-standard) reals defined as

A_{\mathcal{M}}:=\Big\{\left[\alpha a\right]\big|a\in M\Big\}.

For
\left[\alpha a_{1}\right],\ldots,\left[\alpha b_{1}\right],\ldots\in A_{\mathcal{M}}
, we let
P_{\bar{m},\bar{n},\ell}(\left[\alpha a_{1}\right],\ldots,\left[\alpha b_{1}\right],\ldots)
hold in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\models\sum m_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]<\sum n_{i}\left[\alpha b_{i}\right]+\ell.

Note in particular that
P_{\bar{1},\bar{1},0}(\left[\alpha a\right],\left[\alpha b\right])
holds in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\models\left[\alpha a\right]<\left[\alpha b\right].

That is,
P_{\bar{1},\bar{1},0}
coincides with the relation
<
in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
. Hence by Axiom 1 this predicate defines a dense linear ordering on
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Towards introducing
T^{*}
, we keep using the notation
\left[\alpha x\right]
for elements of an arbitrary
\mathcal{L}^{*}
-structure
\mathcal{A}
. Also, for simplicity and particularly in axiom schemes (2) and (3) below, we keep thinking of predicates
P_{\bar{m},\bar{n},l}
as if they are reflecting the content of the inequality appeared in (
5.2
), while we carefully have this reservation in mind that an expression like
\sum m_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]
is, by itself, just meaningless in
T^{*}
and does not refer to an actual point.

Let
T^{*}
be the theory that
describes the following:

(1)

The relation
<
is a dense linear order.

(2)

The predicates

P_{\bar{m},\bar{n},\ell}

are consistent
with
the usual addition and ordering of real numbers.
That is
T^{*}
describes how
elements can be moved from
each side of (
5.2
)
to the other.
For example if
P_{\overline{2},\bar{1},0}(a,c)
holds, then we have that
P_{\overline{1},\overline{1,-1},0}(a,c,a)
. This example reflects the content of the fact that
2[\alpha a]<[\alpha c]
implies
[\alpha a]<[\alpha c]-[\alpha a]
in real numbers.

(3)

If
\sum m_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]<\sum n_{i}\left[\alpha b_{i}\right]+\ell<1
then
there is
[\alpha x]
such that

\sum m_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]<[\alpha x]<\sum n_{i}\left[\alpha b_{i}\right]+\ell.

Because of the density enforced on the predicates of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
by the axioms (1) and (3) above, it is easy to verify the following proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

T^{*}
admits quantifier elimination in
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Now, for some model
\mathcal{M}\models\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
it is easy to see that the associated
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
is a model of
T^{*}
. On the other hand,
T^{*}
is
similar to
an o-minimal theory in the sense that
any set
defined by a formula
\varphi(x,\bar{a},\bar{b})

is a finite union of
intervals

of the form below

\left\{x:\sum m_{i}\left[\alpha a_{i}\right]<m\left[\alpha x\right]<\sum n_{i}\left[\alpha b_{i}\right]+\ell\right\}.

But, as mentioned earlier, the endpoints of this
interval
are not actual points in an arbitrary model of
T^{*}
. However, in each of the structures
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
these endpoints turn out to be elements of the form
\left[\alpha a\right]
. Moreover, at the expense of adding/subtracting an integer value to/from
\ell
, we can write
m\left[\alpha x\right]
as
\left[m\alpha x\right]
or equivalently as
\left[\alpha z\right]
for some
z
in
\mathcal{M}
. That is, each
\mathcal{L}^{*}
-formula
\varphi(x,\bar{a},\bar{b})
becomes equivalent to a finite disjunction of formulas of the form below in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
:

\left[\alpha a\right]<\left[\alpha x\right]<\left[\alpha b\right].

Hence for any pair of models
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}\models\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
the two associated structures
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{N}}
are elementary equivalent since we are able to form a back-and-forth system between them. In other words, there exists a completion of
T^{*}
that is o-minimal and is determined by
\mathcal{T}_{\nalg}
.

We finish by posing the following question which is seemingly a natural continuation of the results appeared in this paper:

Question.
Is the structure
\langle\mathbbm{Z},<,+,-,0,1,f\rangle
, which is,
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
augmented by the usual ordering of integers, decidable? Is it model-complete? Or, does it admit quantifier elimination in a naturally expanded language?
