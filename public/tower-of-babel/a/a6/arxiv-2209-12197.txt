First-order Conditions for Optimization in the Wasserstein Space
Nicolas Lanzetti, Saverio Bolognani, Florian Dörfler

Abstract
We study first-order optimality conditions for constrained optimization in the Wasserstein space, whereby one seeks to minimize a real-valued function over the space of probability measures endowed with the Wasserstein distance. Our analysis combines recent insights on the geometry and the differential structure of the Wasserstein space with more classical calculus of variations. We show that simple rationales such as “setting the derivative to zero” and “gradients are aligned at optimality” carry over to the Wasserstein space. We deploy our tools to study and solve optimization problems in the setting of distributionally robust optimization and statistical inference. The generality of our methodology allows us to naturally deal with functionals, such as mean-variance, Kullback-Leibler divergence, and Wasserstein distance, which are traditionally difficult to study in a unified framework.

Introduction

Many problems in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and optimization under uncertainty can be cast as optimization problems over the space of probability measures of the form

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{J(\mu):K(\mu)\leq 0\},

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
J:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
and
K:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
are real-valued functions over the probability space.
Examples of interest include:

Example 1.1

(Distributionally robust optimization)
.

In decision-making problems, one seeks a decision
w\in\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
which minimizes the expected cost
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[f(w,x)\right]
of a function
f:\mathcal{W}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, where the quantity
x
is random with distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since the probability measure
\mu
is rarely known in practice,
distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
aims to minimize the
worst-case
cost over the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\inf_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[f(w,x)\right].

Ambiguity sets are usually designed to ensure (statistical) performance guarantees and computational tractability; see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
and references therein. A popular choice of ambiguity sets (also subject to study in this work, see
Section

4
) are Wasserstein balls, which include all probability measures within a given Wasserstein distance from a prescribed probability measure.

Example 1.2

(Statistical inference)
.

According to Kullback’s principle of minimum cross-entropy
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, the inference of a probability measure
\mu
given a prior estimate
\bar{\mu}
over a class of probability measures
\mathcal{P}
can be formulated as the minimization of the
Kullback-Leibner (KL)
divergence:

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}\kl(\mu|\bar{\mu})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\left(\frac{\rho(x)}{\bar{\rho}(x)}\right)\bar{\rho}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where
\rho
and
\bar{\rho}
are the densities of
\mu
and
\bar{\mu}
. Here,
\mathcal{P}
usually encodes moment constraints, the class of the probability measure, or a ball around a prescribed probability measure.

Example 1.3

(Maximum likelihood deconvolution)
.

Consider the problem of estimating an unknown probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
based on corrupted observations
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, where
Y_{i}=X_{i}+Z_{i}
,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\mu
, and the errors
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are independent copies of
Z\sim\bar{\mu}
(with probability density
\bar{\rho}
) and independent of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. A natural candidate is the maximum-likelihood estimator

\hat{\mu}\coloneqq\argmax_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left((\bar{\rho}\ast\mu)(Y_{i})\right)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{\rho}(Y_{i}-x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right),

(2)

where
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a class of probability measures.
It is well known
[
9
]
that (
2
) can be reformulated as the (unconstrained) minimization the entropic optimal transport distance
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
from the empirical probability measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
.

In general, the optimization problem (
1
) can be formulated (or relaxed) as an infinite-dimensional optimization problem in the (vector) space of signed measures, whereby non-negativity and normalization are introduced as independent constraints. This augmentation permits to access the rich theory of optimization in Banach spaces; e.g., see
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
and references therein.
For instance, when both
J
and
K
are restricted to be the expected value of some real-valued function, (
1
) culminates in the so-called problem of moments, a particular instance of conic linear problems in the space of signed measures for which powerful dual reformulations exist
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
Recent years also witnessed significant efforts in studying (
1
) in the context of
DRO
, whereby one seeks to evaluate the worst-case expected value of a real-valued function over an ambiguity set, oftentimes defined via moments, the
KL
divergence, or the Wasserstein distance
[
1
,
4
,
3
,
2
,
22
,
23
]
. In many cases,
DRO
problems admit dual reformulations, which sometimes even reduce to a tractable finite-dimensional convex optimization problem.
By using the expected value as a local linear approximation of a more general sufficiently well-behaved functional over the probability space,
[
24
]
develops a Frank-Wolfe algorithm in probability spaces for the unconstrained version of (
1
). In most cases, though, dual reformulations only hold when
J
is an expected value, and do not readily generalize to arbitrary constraints.

In this work, we adopt a different approach, and study (
1
) through the lense of optimal transport and its differential properties.
The theory of optimal transport, dating back to the seminal works of Monge
[
25
]
and Kantorovich
[
26
]
, provides us with a way to quantify the distance between probability measures, and thus to define a metric space (the so-called Wasserstein space) of probability measures
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
Notably, this is sufficient to endow the probability space with a differential structure. This enlightening theory, pioneered by
[
30
]
and later formalized by
[
27
]
, culminates in the rigorous formulation of gradient flows in probability spaces; see
[
31
]
for an introduction. While this theory has been widely used to study partial differential equations
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
, applications of Wasserstein gradient flows for optimization purposes remain largely unexplored.

Among the few approaches in this direction,
[
36
]
relies on Wasserstein gradients to devise computational methods to compute the “average” (so-called Wasserstein barycenter) of a collection of probability measures, implicitly defined as an unconstrained optimization problem over the probability space
[
37
,
38
]
.
Other applications in machine learning include the analysis of (over-parametrized) neural network
[
39
,
40
]
, approximate inference
[
41
]
, variational inference
[
42
]
, back-propagation of gradients through discrete random variables
[
43
]
, and policy optimization in reinforcement learning
[
44
]
.
In
[
45
]
, Wasserstein gradient flows are used to design algorithms for the unconstrained version of (
1
).
The closest approach to ours is
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, which studies necessary conditions “à la Pontryagin” for optimality of control problems in the Wasserstein space. While our setting is different, we share some of the fundamentals of this work.

The study of (
1
) in the probability space, and not in the space of signed measures, allows us to formulate simple, intuitive, and interpretable necessary and sufficient conditions for optimality, which oftentimes formally resemble their Euclidean counterparts (e.g., “set the gradient to zero”). In particular, we do not need to repeatedly deal with normalization and non-negativity of the signed measures.

Contributions

More specifically, our contributions are as follows.

1.

We provide a coincise and self-contained review of the Wasserstein space, its geometry, and its differential structure. Our review bases on both existing results as well as on various novel extensions, and it is geared towards solving (
1
).
We do not content ourselves with enunciating the theoretic results, but present many examples of functionals over the Wasserstein space, and characterize their properties such as continuity and differentiability.

2.

We derive novel necessary and sufficient KKT-type conditions for optimality of (
1
) in the case of equality constraints and specific inequality constraints. Our analysis combines recent advances in the geometry and the differential structure of the Wasserstein space with classical methods from calculus of variations.

3.

We show that our methodology can be used to solve various problems (including unsolved ones) in
DRO
and statistical inference with the
KL
divergence, sometimes even in closed form. While not representing the core of this work, the study of these specific problems is of independent interest.

2.1
Background in Measure Theory, Optimal Transport, and Wasserstein Space

We start with basics in measure theory and optimal transport. We refer the reader to
[
29
,
27
,
28
,
49
]
for a comprehensive review.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and by

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty\right\}

the set of probability measures with finite second moment.
We use the notation
\mu\ll\nu
to indicate that
\mu
is
absolutely continuous
with respect to
\nu
. In particular, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of absolutely continuous probability measures (with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{d}
).
The
pushforward
of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through a Borel map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
T_{\#}\mu
, is defined by
(T_{\#}\mu)(A)\coloneqq\mu(T^{-1}(A))
for all Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We recall that for any
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

T_{\#}\mu
-integrable (or non-negative measurable/Borel bounded)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mathrm{d}(T_{\#}\mu)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(T(x))\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Following
[
29
]
, we define two notions of convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

•

narrow convergence
:
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
converges narrowly to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\qquad\forall\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

•

weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
converges weakly in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, denoted by
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
, if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Equivalently
[
29
, Definition 6.8]
,
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
if for all continuous functions
\phi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
|\phi(x)|\leq C(1+\norm{x}^{2})
for
C\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Of course, weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
implies narrow convergence. The converse, though, is not true, as
x\mapsto x^{2}\notin C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Example 2.1

(Narrow convergence
\not\Rightarrow
weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be defined by
\mu_{n}\coloneqq(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}})\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\delta_{n}
. Then,
\mu_{n}
converges narrowly to
\delta_{0}
: for any
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)=\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\phi(0)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\phi(n)\to\phi(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\delta_{0}(x),

where we used that
\phi
is bounded. However,
\mu_{n}\not\rightharpoonup\delta_{0}
as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}n^{2}=1\not\to 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\mathrm{d}\delta_{0}.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we say that
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
plan
(or

) between
\mu
and
\nu
if
(\proj_{1})_{\#}\gamma=\mu
and
(\proj_{2})_{\#}\gamma=\nu
, where
\proj_{1},\proj_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the canonical projections; equivalently for all
\phi,\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
integrable

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(y)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y).

We denote by
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. Since the product measure
\mu\times\nu
has marginals
\mu
and

, respectively,
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty.
We are now ready to define the Wasserstein distance:

Definition 2.2

(Wasserstein distance

,
29
,
28
,

]
)

Let

. We define the (type 2)

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\left(\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3)

We denote by
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
the set of couplings attaining the minimum in (
3
), and we call any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)

optimal transport plan
between
\mu
and
\nu
.

A compactness and lower semi-continuity argument (with respect to the topology induced by

\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty: The minimum in (

\gamma

.
If
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
is of the form
(\Id,T_{\mu}^{\nu})_{\#}\mu

, we say that
\gamma
is induced by an optimal transport

which pushes
\mu
into
\nu
; i.e.,
\nu=(T_{\mu}^{\nu})_{\#}\mu
,

yields the minimum transportation cost, and

is an optimal transport map between
\mu
and
\nu
.
The celebrated Brenier’s theorem sheds light on existence of optimal transport maps:

Theorem 2.3

(Brenier’s theorem
[
29
,
27
,
28
,
49
]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and assume that
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. Then, there exists a unique optimal transport plan
\gamma
, induced by a unique optimal transport map
T_{\mu}^{\nu}
, and
T_{\mu}^{\nu}=\nabla\phi

\mu
-a.e for some convex function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem

2.3
suggests that, whenever
\mu\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
, it is safe to define

\mu
and
\nu
. Its inverse, if it exists, reveals the optimal transport map in “reverse direction”:

(Inverse of optimal transport maps)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assume an optimal transport map
T_{\mu}^{\nu}
exists, and it is invertible

-almost everywhere. Then,
T_{\nu}^{\mu}
exists, and it equals
(T_{\mu}^{\nu})^{-1}

\nu
-a.e.

.

Proposition

does not provide conditions for (

, but it assumes it. A sufficient condition for

. Then, by Brenier’s theorem (

exists and

There is one final important setting where optimal transport maps exist:

Proposition 2.6

(Pushforward via convex functions
[
28
, Theorem 1.48]
)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T=\nabla\phi
for some
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
convex and differentiable
\mu
-almost everywhere. If
\norm{T}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}<+\infty
, then an optimal transport plan between
\mu
and
T_{\#}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by the optimal transport map

.

Proposition

2.6
is non-obvious: There might exist another transport map
\tilde{T}
(or a transport plan) yielding a lower transport cost between
\mu
and
T_{\#}\mu
. We now exploit

2.6
to define a perturbation of measures, which will be a crucial building block for our variational analysis.

Lemma 2.7

(Perturbation of probability measures)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, there exists
\bar{s}>0
such that
\Id+s\nabla\psi
is an optimal transport map from
\mu
to
(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu
for all
s\in(-\bar{s},+\bar{s})
, i.e.,

T_{\mu}^{(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu}=\Id+s\nabla\psi.

Further,
(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu
is

W_{2}(\mu,(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu)=|s|\left\|\nabla\psi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}.

By
Proposition

2.6
, it suffices to establish that
\Id+s\nabla\psi
is the gradient of a convex function. Indeed,
\Id+s\nabla\psi
is smooth, and so differentiable
\mu
-a.e., and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x+s\gradient{}\psi(x)}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\norm{x}+|s|\norm{\gradient{}\psi(x)})^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(4)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x}^{2}+|s|^{2}\norm{\gradient{}\psi(x)}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+|s|^{2}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{\gradient{}\psi(x)}^{2}

\displaystyle<+\infty,

where the maximum of
\norm{\gradient{}\psi(x)}^{2}
exists and is bounded by (i) continuity of the gradient and the norm and (ii) due to compactness of the support of
\psi
. So, by
Proposition

2.6
, if
\Id+s\nabla\psi
is the gradient of a convex function, then it is an optimal transport map between
\mu
and
(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu
.
To prove convexity, by smoothness, it suffices to show that
I+s\nabla^{2}\psi(x)
is positive definite for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Without loss of generality, assume
\nabla^{2}\psi(x)
is non-zero at least for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; else, the statement is trivial.
Let
\bar{s}\coloneqq 1/\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{\nabla^2\psi(x)}>0
, where the maximum exists by compactness of the support of
\psi
and continuity of the norm. Then,
I+s\nabla^{2}\psi(x)
is positive definite for all
s\in(-\bar{s},\bar{s})
, which implies
\Id+s\nabla\psi
is the gradient of a convex function, and thus the statement follows. Finally, (
4
) shows that
(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and, by definition of Wasserstein distance and optimal transport map,

W_{2}(\mu,(\Id+s\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x-(x-s\gradient{}\psi)}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=|s|\norm{\gradient{}\psi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}.

This concludes the proof.

Finally, it is well known that the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(but not on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
), so that
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot))
is a metric space, often called the
Wasserstein space

[
27
,
29
,
28
]
.
We also recall that the Wasserstein distance metrizes weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
29
, Theorem 6.9]
; i.e., for
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)=0
if and only if
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
.

Example 2.8

(
Example

2.1
revisited)
.

In
Example

2.1
,
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\delta_{0})=1
for all
n
, so
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\delta_{0})\not\to 0
, and thus
\mu_{n}
cannot converge weakly in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\delta_{0}
.

2.2
Interpolation and Geodesic Convexity

The theory of optimal transport naturally provides us with a notion of interpolation in the Wasserstein space: Let
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
, and consider the curve

\mu_{t}\coloneqq((1-t)\proj_{1}+t\proj_{2})_{\#}\gamma.

(5)

Then,
\mu_{t}
interpolates between
\mu_{0}
(
t=0
) and
\mu_{1}
(
t=1
). Since multiple optimal transport plans might exist, the interpolation
\mu_{t}
between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
is generally not unique.
Curves of the form (
5
) can be shown to be
constant speed geodesics
in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(i.e.,
W_{2}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=(t-s)W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
), and all constant speed geodesics can be written as (
5
)
[
27
, Theorem 7.2.2]
.
Accordingly, a functional
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
\alpha
-
geodesically convex
(with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
) if for all
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and all
t\in[0,1]
there exists
\mu_{t}
as in (
5
) such that

J(\mu_{t})\leq(1-t)J(\mu_{0})+tJ(\mu_{1})-\frac{\alpha}{2}t(1-t)W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{2}.

(6)

We will sometimes need a stronger notion of geodesic convexity: geodesic convexity
along generalized geodesics
. A generalized geodesic joining
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
with base
\bar{\mu}
is a curve

\mu_{t}\coloneqq((1-t)\proj_{2}+t\proj_{3})_{\#}\gamma,

(7)

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a coupling of
\bar{\mu}
,
\mu_{0}
, and
\mu_{1}
which is optimal between
\bar{\mu}
and
\mu_{0}
(i.e.,
(\proj_{1,2})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\mu_{0})
) and between
\bar{\mu}
and
\mu_{1}
(i.e.,
(\proj_{1,3})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\mu_{2})
), but not necessarily optimal between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. With the choice
\bar{\mu}=\mu_{0}
, we recover the geodesics (
5
). Accordingly,
J
is
\alpha
-
convex along generalized geodesics
if for all
\bar{\mu},\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists
\mu_{t}
as in (
7
) such that

J(\mu_{t})\leq(1-t)J(\mu_{0})+tJ(\mu_{1})-\frac{\alpha}{2}t(1-t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{y-z}^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y,z).

Convexity along generalized geodesics is stronger than geodesic convexity. In particular, if
J
is
\alpha
-convex along generalized geodesics, then it suffices to pick
\bar{\mu}=\mu_{0}
to prove that
J
is
\alpha
-geodesically convex. Finally, if
\alpha=0
we simply say that
J
is geodesically convex (along generalized geodesics).
Finally, if (
6
) holds for any
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
(not necessarily optimal) in (
5
), then
J
is
\alpha
-convex
along any interpolating curve
. Clearly, this implies both geodesic convexity and convexity along generalized geodesics.

2.3
Differential Calculus in the Wasserstein Space

In this section, we equip, perhaps surprisingly, the Wasserstein space with a “differential structure”. Critically,
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot))
does
not
enjoy a linear structure, so classical notions of differentials (e.g., Fréchet) do not apply.
Henceforth, we will consider lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. to weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) functionals of the form
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to(-\infty,+\infty]
. To ease the notation, we define the effective domain of
J
as

D(J)\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):J(\mu)<+\infty\}

and tacitly assume that
J
is proper (i.e.,
D(J)\neq\emptyset
).
Inspired from classical definitions of sub- and super-differentiability in Euclidean settings, sub- and super-differentials are defined as follows:

Definition 2.9

(Wasserstein sub- and super-differential
[
47
]
)
.

Let
\mu\in D(J)
. We say that a map
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
belongs to the
subdifferential

\partial^{-}J(\mu)
of
J
at
\mu
if

J(\nu)-J(\mu)\geq\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\xi(x),y-x\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+o(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))

(8)

for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Similarly,
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
belongs to the
superdifferential

\partial^{+}J(\mu)
of
J
at
\mu
if
(-\xi)\in\partial^{-}(-J)(\mu)
.

By
Definition

2.9
, sub- and super-differential are
functions
in
L^{2}
. Intuitively,
\xi(x)
, with
\xi\in\partial^{-}J(\mu)
, is the “(sub)gradient experienced by the particles of
\mu
located at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
”.
It is now easy to define differentiability:

Definition 2.10

(Differentiable functions
[
47
]
)
.

A functional
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is
Wasserstein differentiable
at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if the intersection of sub- and superdifferential is non-empty; i.e., if
\partial^{-}J(\mu)\cap\partial^{+}J(\mu)\neq\emptyset
.
In this case, we say
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in\partial^{-}J(\mu)\cap\partial^{+}J(\mu)
is a
Wasserstein gradient
of
J
at
\mu
, satisfying

J(\nu)-J(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+o(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))

for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition

2.10
states that Wasserstein gradients are general functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
. As we shall see below, we can impose additional structure, and dictate that gradients belong the “Wasserstein tangent space”, which we define as follows.

Definition 2.11

(Tangent space
[
27
]
)
.

The
tangent space
at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the vector space

\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\overline{\{\nabla\psi:\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}}^{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)},

where the closure is taken with respect to the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
topology.

Remark 2.12
.

Few remarks on
Definitions

2.9
to
2.11
are in order.
First,
Definition

2.11
encapsulates the geometry of
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot))
: Gradients of functions
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
generate optimal transport maps by perturbation of the identity via
(\Id+\varepsilon\nabla\psi)_{\#}\mu
for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small; see
Lemma

2.7
above. Thus, they can be thought as tangent vectors to
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot))
.
Second, an equivalent (more related to optimal transport) definition of tangent space is

\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\overline{\{\lambda(r-\Id):(\Id,r)_{\#}\mu\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,r_{\#}\mu),\lambda>0\}}^{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}.

We refer to
[
27
, Theorem 8.5.1]
for more details.
Third, an alternative definitions of sub- and super-differentiability involves taking the
infimum
instead of the
supremum
in (
8
)
[
27
, Definition 10.3.1]
; yet, this definition is equivalent
[
50
]
.
Fourth, it is straightforward to show linearity; i.e., for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla_{\mu}(\alpha J_{1}+\beta J_{2})(\mu)=\alpha\nabla_{\mu}J_{1}(\mu)+\beta\nabla_{\mu}J_{2}(\mu)
, whenever
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Fifth, we defined (sub)gradients for functions whose domain is
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Yet, our definitions readily extends to any function whose domain is an open subset of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(e.g.,
\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
).

Definition

2.10
does
not
establish uniqueness of Wasserstein gradients. Indeed, the set
\partial^{-}J(\mu)\cap\partial^{+}J(\mu)
generally contains multiple elements. Nonetheless, Wasserstein gradients are, if they exist, unique in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
modulo the equivalence relation induced by
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\perp}
. That is, Wasserstein gradients are unique in
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular, since
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
closed
linear subspace of the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
, the Hilbert decomposition theorem
[
51
, Theorem 4.11]
asserts that any element
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
can be
uniquely
decomposed as
\xi=\xi^{\parallel}+\xi^{\perp}
, with
\xi^{\parallel}\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\xi^{\perp}\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\perp}
. The next proposition shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\xi^{\perp}(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=0

for any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
and any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\xi(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\xi^{\parallel}(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y).

This, together with
Definitions

2.9
and
2.10
, suggests that we can
always
impose that Wasserstein sub- and super-differential belong to the tangent space
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and thus Wasserstein gradients, whenever they exist, are unique and also belong to
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Formally:

Proposition 2.13

(Wasserstein gradients only act on tangent vectors)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
, and
\xi\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\perp}
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\xi(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)=0.

(9)

In particular, we can without loss of generality impose that sub- and super-differential live in
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This way, also the Wasserstein gradient of
J
at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is, if it exists, the unique element
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\partial^{-}J(\mu)\cap\partial^{+}J(\mu)
.

Henceforth, we will therefore regard the Wasserstein gradient of
J
at
\mu
, if it exists, as the unique element
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\partial^{-}J(\mu)\cap\partial^{+}J(\mu)
. While apparently of minor importance, imposing that Wasserstein gradients live in the tangent space
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(instead of in the more general
L^{2}
space) will play a crucial role in later sections.

Next, we show that Wasserstein gradients provide a “linear approximation” even if perturbations are not induced by optimal transport plans. A similar, but less general, result for perturbations induced by transport maps is in
[
52
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Proposition 2.14

(Differentials are “strong”)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
(not necessarily optimal), and let
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu
with Wasserstein gradient
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,

J(\nu)-J(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+o\left(\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{x-y}^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)}\right).

We now adapt three well-known facts on differentiable functions to the Wasserstein space. First, differentiability implies continuity (in the topology induced by the Wasserstein distance):

Proposition 2.15

(Differentiability implies continuity)
.

Let
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,
J
is continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
at
\mu
.

As in the Euclidean case, we can further a characterize Wasserstein gradients of geodesically convex functions (cf.
[
27
, Theorem 10.3.6]
):

Proposition 2.16

(Gradients of geodesically convex functions)
.

Let
J
be
\alpha
-geodesically convex with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose that
J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

J(\nu)-J(\mu)\geq\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+\frac{\alpha}{2}W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}.

(10)

Moreover, Wasserstein gradients are “monotone”: If
J
is differentiable at
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\nu)(y)-\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\geq\alpha W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}

(11)

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
.

Finally, we can prove the chain rule:

Proposition 2.17

(Chain rule)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable,
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be Wasserstein differentiable, and let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,
g\circ J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu
, and

\nabla_{\mu}(g\circ J)(\mu)=g^{\prime}(J(\mu))\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu).

2.4
Examples of Smooth Functionals

After endowing the Wasserstein space with a differential structure, we study the topological and differential properties of five classes of important functionals:

•

optimal transport discrepancies from a reference measure
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\mu\mapsto W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})\coloneqq\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\bar{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,y)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

for some continuously differentiable transport cost
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which also include the (squared) Wasserstein distance.

•

expected value-type functionals:

\mu\mapsto\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[V(x)\right]\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for some twice continuously differentiable
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
;

•

interaction-type functionals:

\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(x-y)\mathrm{d}(\mu\times\mu)(x,y),

where
\mu\times\mu
denotes the product measure, for some continuously differentiable
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, related, among others, to the variance (see
Corollary

2.24
below);

•

internal-energy-type functionals:

\mu\mapsto\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\rho(x))\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
+\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}

for some continuously differentiable
F:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, related, among others, to the entropy;

•

the divergences relative to a reference measure
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\mu\mapsto\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(x)&\text{if }\mu\ll\bar{\mu},\\
+\infty&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}}
is the Radon-Nykodin derivative
[
51
]
; an example is the
KL
divergence.

We start with optimal transport discrepancies:

Proposition 2.18

(Optimal transport discrepancy)
.

Let
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and consider the functional

J(\mu)=W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})\coloneqq\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\bar{\mu})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,y)\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y).

Assume that there exists
C>0
so that
c(y,y)\leq C
for all
y\in\mathrm{supp}(\bar{\mu})
,
c
is twice continuously differentiable in the first argument, and there exists
M>0
so that
\norm{\gradient{x}^2c(x,y)}\leq M
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\in\mathrm{supp}(\bar{\mu})
, and
\norm{\gradient{x}c(x,y)}^{2}\leq M(1+\norm{x}^{2}+\norm{y}^{2})
.
Then,

1.

J
is proper and
J(\mu)\geq 0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

2.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence;

4.

if
-c(\cdot,y)
is
\alpha
-convex (with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
) for all
y\in\mathrm{supp}(\bar{\mu})
, then
-J
is
\alpha
-convex along any interpolating curve;

5.

if
c
is
\alpha
-convex, then
J
is
\alpha
-convex along the curve

\mu_{t}=((1-t)\proj_{2}+t\proj_{3})_{\#}\gamma,

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
(\proj_{12})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\mu)
and
(\proj_{13})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\nu)
(where optimality is intended w.r.t. the transport cost
c
), and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

6.

if
\mu\in D(J)
and
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\bar{\mu})
contains a unique optimal transport plan induced an optimal transport map
T_{\mu}^{\bar{\mu}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
(w.r.t. the cost
c
),
J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu
, and its Wasserstein gradient is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\nabla_{x}c(\cdot,T_{\mu}^{\bar{\mu}}(\cdot)).

Remark 2.19
.

Two remarks are in order.
First, Statement (iv) does
not
imply that
J
is convex along any interpolating curve, but only along the interpolating curves whose “base” is precisely
\bar{\mu}
.
Second, if
c(x,y)={(x-y)}^{\top}A(x-y)
with
A={A}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
positive definite or
c(x,y)=\ell(\norm{x-y})
for
\ell:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
strictly convex which grows faster than linearly, then
\mu
being absolutely continuous is a sufficient condition for existence of a unique optimal transport plan induced by an optimal transport map; see
[
53
,
54
,
29
]
for details and generalizations.

As a corollary, we study the differentiability properties of the squared Wasserstein distance (cf.
[
27
, Chapter 7.3]
for similar results):

Corollary 2.20

(Squared Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and consider the functional

J(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}W_{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})^{2}.

Then,

1.

D(J)=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
J
is proper, and
J(\mu)\geq 0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

2.

\mu\mapsto W_{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})
and
J
are continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

\mu\mapsto W_{2}(\mu,\bar{\mu})
and
J
are lower semi-continuous w.r.t. to narrow convergence;

4.

-J
is
(-1)
-convex (and thus
J
is
(-1)
-concave) along any interpolating curve;

5.

J
is
1
-convex along the curve

\mu_{t}=((1-t)\proj_{2}+t\proj_{3})_{\#}\gamma,

where
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
(\proj_{12})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\mu)
and
(\proj_{13})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\bar{\mu},\nu)
, and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

6.

if
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is absolutely continuous,
J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu
and its Wasserstein gradient is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\Id-T_{\mu}^{\bar{\mu}},

where
\Id:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the identity map and
T_{\mu}^{\bar{\mu}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the (unique) optimal transport map from
\mu
to
\bar{\mu}
.

Unlike in Euclidean (or more generally Hilbertian) settings, the distance function is
not
(geodesically) convex: It is in fact semiconcave. This makes the Wasserstein space a
positively curved metric space
; see
[
27
]
.
This fact, among others, will complicate sufficient conditions for optimality, oftentimes intimately related to convexity. We later circumvent this technical difficulty in
Theorem

3.15
.

Next, we study expected values:

Proposition 2.21

(Expected value)
.

Let
V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with uniformly bounded Hessian (i.e., there exists
M>0
so that
\norm{\gradient{x}^2V(x)}\leq M
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and consider the functional

J(\mu)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[V(x)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Then,

1.

D(J)=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
J
is proper, and
J(\mu)>-\infty
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

2.

J
is continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

if
V^{-}(x)\leq C(1+\norm{x}^{p})
for
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
p<2
, then
J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence;

4.

J
is
\alpha

is
\alpha
-convex;

5.

J
is Wasserstein differentiable and its Wasserstein gradient at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\nabla_{x}V.

We now consider interaction-type functionals:

Proposition 2.22

(Derivative of interaction-type functionals)
.

Let
U\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with uniformly bounded Hessian (i.e., there exists
M>0
so that
\norm{\gradient{z}^2U(z)}\leq M
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and consider the functional

J(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(x-y)\mathrm{d}(\mu\times\mu)(x,y).

Then,

1.

D(J)=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
J
is proper, and

J(\mu)>-\infty
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

2.

J
is continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

if
U^{-}(z)\leq C(1+\norm{z}^{p})
for
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
p<2
, then
J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence;

4.

if
U
is convex, then
J
is convex along any interpolating curve;

5.

J
is Wasserstein differentiable and its Wasserstein gradient at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\nabla U\ast\mu,

provided that
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
, where the convolution is defined as

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x)=(\nabla U\ast\mu)(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla U(x-y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Remark 2.23
.

If
U
is
\alpha
-geodesically convex, then

is not necessarily
\alpha
-geodesically convex; e.g., see

. Nonetheless, if
\alpha\geq 0
, then

is (
0
-)geodesically convex.

We now instantiate
Proposition

2.22
to the study the variance:

Corollary 2.24

(Variance)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
J(\mu)=\var^{\mu}\left[\left\langle a,x\right\rangle\right]
. Then,
J
is continuous w.r.t. to weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, convex along any interpolating curve, and its Wasserstein gradient at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)(x)=2a\left\langle a,x-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x\right]\right\rangle.

Remark 2.25
.

If
d=1
and
a=1
, the Wasserstein gradient reduces to the natural expression
\nabla_{\mu}\var^{\mu}\left[x\right]=2(\Id-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x\right])
. The gradient evaluates to zero when all probability mass is concentrated at the expected value and the variance achieves its global minimum; see
Section

3
.
Moreover,
Corollary

2.24
also follows from the chain rule (
Proposition

2.17
): Since
\var^{\mu}\left[\left\langle a,x\right\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle a,x\right\rangle^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle a,x\right\rangle\right]^{2}
, we conclude

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=2a\left\langle a,\Id\right\rangle-2a\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle a,x\right\rangle\right]=2a\left\langle a,\Id-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x\right]\right\rangle.

We can now recall the properties internal energy-type functionals:

Proposition 2.26

(Derivative of internal energy-type functionals)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex, differentiable, with superlinear growth at infinity, and satisfy
F(0)=0
.
Consider the functional

J(\mu)=\begin{cases}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\rho(x))\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
+\infty&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Then,

1.

D(J)\subset\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
J
is proper;

2.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence.

Moreover, if
s\mapsto s^{d}F(s^{-d})
is convex and non-increasing on
(0,+\infty)
, then

4.

J
is convex along generalized geodesics;

If additionally there exists
C>0
s.t.

for all

, then

5.

J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with Wasserstein gradient

provided that all terms all well defined (i.e.,
\rho F^{\prime}(\rho)+\rho\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
and

As an example, we consider the entropy:

Example 2.27

(Entropy)
.

Consider the entropy
J=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho\log(\rho)\mathrm{d}x
; i.e.,
F(z)=z\log(z)
(with
F(0)=0
). Clearly,

is convex, differentiable, and has superlinear growth.
Moreover,

is convex and non-increasing, and

\displaystyle(z+w)\log(z+w)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+z\log(z)+w\log(w)).

So, if
\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Wasserstein gradient is

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\frac{\nabla(\rho+\rho\log(\rho)-\rho\log(\rho))}{\rho}=\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}.

To conclude, we compute the derivative of divergences:

Proposition 2.28

(Derivative of the divergences)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}

. Let

+\infty&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}}
is the Radon-Nikodyn derivative.
Then,

1.

and
J
is proper;

2.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

3.

J
is lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence.

Moreover, if
s\mapsto s^{d}F(s^{-d})
is convex and non-increasing on
(0,+\infty)
and
\bar{\mu}
is log-concave, then

4.

J
is convex along generalized geodesics.

Additionally, if there exists
C>0
such that
F(z+w)\leq C(1+F(z)+F(w))
for all
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bar{\mu}=e^{-V}\mathcal{L}^{d}

5.

J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with Wasserstein gradient

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)=\frac{\nabla(\rho/e^{-V}F^{\prime}(\rho/e^{-V})-F(\rho/e^{-V}))}{\rho}e^{-V},

provided that all terms are well defined (i.e.,
\rho/e^{-V}F^{\prime}(\rho/e^{-V})+\rho/e^{-V}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
and
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
).

3
Optimality conditions

Consider a lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) functional
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
. For ease of notation, we denote (open) Wasserstein balls of radius
r>0
centered at
\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

B_{r}(\hat{\mu})\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):W_{2}(\mu,\hat{\mu})<r\}.

3.1
Unconstrained Optimization

In the setting of unconstrained optimization, minimizers are defined as usual:

Definition 3.1

(Local, global, and strict minimizers for unconstrained optimization)
.

A probability measure
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
local minimizer
of
J
if there exists
r>0
such that
J(\mu)\geq J(\mu^{\ast})
for all
\mu\in B_{r}(\mu^{\ast})
.
If this holds for any
r>0
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is a
global minimizer
. If
J(\mu)>J(\mu^{\ast})
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mu\neq\mu^{\ast}
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is the
strict minimizer
of
J
.

Similarly to Euclidean settings, local minimality and derivatives are intimately related:

Theorem 3.2

(First-order necessary conditions)
.

Let
\mu^{\ast}
be a local minimizer of
J
and assume
J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
. Then, the Wasserstein gradient of
J
vanishes at
\mu^{\ast}
:

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=0\quad\mu^{\ast}\text{-a.e.},

i.e.,
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})=0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})
.

Surprisingly, the proof of
Theorem

3.2
is not elementary. In particular, we first need a revisited version of fundamental lemma of calculus of variations:

Lemma 3.3

(Fundamental lemma of calculus of variations, revisited)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H\in\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assume that for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle H(x),\nabla\psi(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0.

(12)

Then,
H\equiv 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, by definition of
\mathrm{Tan}_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\left\|H-\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}<\varepsilon.

Consider (
12
) with
\psi=\varphi_{\varepsilon}
, then

\displaystyle 0=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle H,\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|H-\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}-\|H\|^{2}-\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(13)

\displaystyle=\left\|H-\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}-\left\|H\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}-\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2},

and thus

\displaystyle\left\|H\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:proof lemma variations equality 1}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\left\|H-\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}-\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|H-\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon\to 0
to get
H\equiv 0
.
Let
T=\nabla\psi
for some
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
By
Lemma

2.7
, there exists
\bar{s}>0
such that
\Id+\varepsilon T
are the optimal transport maps between
\mu^{\ast}
and
\mu\coloneqq(\Id+\varepsilon T)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
for all
\varepsilon\in(-\bar{s},\bar{s})
. Moreover, observe that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

W_{2}(\mu^{\ast},\mu)=|\varepsilon|\left\|T\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})}.

Thus, by local minimality of
\mu^{\ast}
, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small it holds
J(\mu)-J(\mu^{\ast})\geq 0
. So, for all
\varepsilon>0
, differentiability gives

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{J((\Id+\varepsilon T)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})-J(\mu^{\ast})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x),T(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)+\frac{o(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x),T(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)\geq 0.

Similarly, for
\varepsilon<0
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x),T(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)\leq 0,

and thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x),T(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)=0.

Finally, by
Lemma

3.3
, we conclude
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=0
, for
\mu^{\ast}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Under an additional convexity assumption, we get sufficient conditions:

Theorem 3.4

(First-order sufficient conditions)
.

Assume that
J
is
\alpha
-geodesically convex with
\alpha\geq 0
. Suppose there exists
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
J
is Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
and the Wasserstein gradient of
J
vanishes at
\mu^{\ast}
, i.e.,
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=0

\mu^{\ast}
-a.e.. Then,
\mu^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of
J
. If
\alpha>0
,
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer of
J
.

Proof 3.5
.

Consider a competitor
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\nu\neq\mu
.
Then, by
Proposition

2.16
,

J(\nu)-J(\mu)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}.

By non-negativity of
\alpha
,
J(\nu)\geq J(\mu)
; if additionally
\alpha>0
,
J(\nu)>J(\mu)
. Since
\nu
is arbitrary, we conclude the proof.

Example 3.6
.

Consider
J(\mu)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\right]
. Then, by
Proposition

2.21
,
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})=\Id
. Thus, by
Theorem

3.2
, a necessary condition for optimality is
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=0

\mu^{\ast}
-a.e., which holds if and only if
\mu^{\ast}=\delta_{0}
. By
Proposition

2.21
,
J
is
1
-geodesically convex, and so
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer of
J
, by
Theorem

3.4
.

3.2
Constrained Optimization

Consider now the constrained optimization problem

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{J(\mu):K(\mu)=0\right\},

(14)

for some lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) functional
K:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
. To start, we adapt
Definition

3.1
to the constrained setting:

Definition 3.7

(Local, global, and strict minimizers for constrained optimization)
.

A probability measure
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
local minimizer
of (
14
) if there exists
r>0
such that
J(\mu)\geq J(\mu^{\ast})
for all
\mu\in B_{r}(\mu^{\ast})\cap\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):K(\mu)=0\}
.
If this holds for any
r>0
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is a
global minimizer
. If
J(\mu)>J(\mu^{\ast})
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):K(\mu)=0\},\mu\neq\mu^{\ast}
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is the
strict minimizer
of (
14
).

The definition of (local, global, strict) minimality for inequality-constrained optimization problems is analogous. Then, similarly to Euclidean settings, necessary conditions for optimality take the form of Lagrange multipliers:

Theorem 3.8

(Lagrange multipliers in the Wasserstein space)
.

Let
\mu^{\ast}
be a local minimizer of (
14
) and assume that
J
and
K
are Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
. Assume that
\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})
is non-vanishing at
\mu^{\ast}
, that is, there exists
H=\nabla\varphi
for some
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)\neq 0.

Then, there exists a unique
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
(called multiplier) such that

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x)

for
\mu^{\ast}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To prove the theorem we need the following preliminary lemma, which will later be instrumental in “making arbitrary variations admissible”:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mu^{\ast}
and
H
as in
Theorem

3.8
. Let
T=\nabla\psi
for some
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, there exists
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
and a continuously differentiable function
\sigma_{\bar{\varepsilon}}\in C^{1}:(-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon})\to\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma(0)=0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon})

K((\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu)=0.

Define the function

\displaystyle\chi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\mapsto K((\Id+\varepsilon T+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}).

Trivially,
\chi(0,0)=K(\mu^{\ast})=0
, by feasibility of
\mu^{\ast}
.
We are interested in studying the derivative with respect to
\delta
. To do so, we first need to establish differentability. Let
\delta>0
. Since
H
is the gradient of a smooth compactly supported function, we know, by
Lemma

2.7
, that for
\delta
sufficiently small
\Id+\delta H
is an optimal transport map from
\mu^{\ast}
to
(\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

W_{2}((\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast},\mu^{\ast})=\delta\norm{H}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu^{\ast})}.

Without loss of generality, assume
H
is normalized; i.e.,
\norm{H}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})}=1
.
Then, by Wasserstein differentiability (cf.
Definition

2.10
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{K((\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})-K(\mu^{\ast})}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),y-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)+\frac{o(\delta)}{\delta}

for
\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}(\mu^{\ast},(\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})
. As
\Id+\delta H
is an optimal transport map, we choose
\gamma=(\Id,\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{K((\Id+\delta H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})-K(\mu^{\ast})}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)+\frac{o(\delta)}{\delta},

which proves that the limit
\delta\to 0
exists (and, by definition, equals the derivative). Hence,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}\delta}\right|_{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)+\frac{o(\delta)}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x).

Analogously, we obtain that

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\chi}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\right|_{(0,0)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),T(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x),

which is well defined since
T
has compact support.
Thus, by the implicit function theorem
[
55
, Theorem 1.3.1]
, there exists
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
and a continuously differentiable function
\sigma:(-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon})\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\sigma(0)=0
and

K((\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})=0

for all
\varepsilon\in(-\bar{\varepsilon},+\bar{\varepsilon})
.
The proof goes by variations. As in the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, we will consider variations via gradients of compactly supported smooth functions; we will then deploy
Lemma

3.9
to make sure that variations are admissible (i.e., they satisfy the constraint).
More specifically, let
T=\nabla\psi
for some
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The variation
(\Id+\varepsilon T)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
is
not
admissible, as generally
K((\Id+\varepsilon T)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})\neq 0
.
Yet, we can use
Lemma

3.9
to make the variation admissible. In particular, there exists
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
and
\sigma\in C^{1}((-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon}))
such that the variation
(\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
is admissible for all
\varepsilon\in(-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon})
.
As
\sigma(\varepsilon)=\sigma^{\prime}(0)\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon)
,
Lemma

2.7
gives that, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,
\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H
is an optimal transport map from
\mu^{\ast}
to
(\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast}
.
So,

\displaystyle W_{2}((\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast},\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\left\|\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})}

\displaystyle\leq|\varepsilon|\left\|T\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})}+|\sigma(\varepsilon)|\left\|H\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d};\mu^{\ast})}.

As
\sigma(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, local minimality of
\mu^{\ast}
with
\varepsilon>0
, together with differentiability, gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J((\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H)_{\#}\mu^{\ast})-J(\mu^{\ast})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x),x+\varepsilon T(x)+\sigma(\varepsilon)H(x)-x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)+\frac{o(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T+\frac{\sigma(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}+\frac{o(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}.

Let
\varepsilon\to 0
to conclude

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T+\sigma^{\prime}(0)H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}\geq 0.

If we repeat the argument with
\varepsilon<0
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T+\sigma^{\prime}(0)H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}\leq 0,

and thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T+\sigma^{\prime}(0)H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)

(15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}+\sigma^{\prime}(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}.

Moreover, for all
\varepsilon\in(-\bar{\varepsilon},\bar{\varepsilon})
the function
\varepsilon\mapsto K((\Id+\varepsilon T+\sigma(\varepsilon)H_{\#}\mu)
is zero. Hence, proceeding as above, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}+\sigma^{\prime}(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}=0,

which leads, as by assumption
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}\neq 0
, to

\sigma^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{R}.

So, (
15
) reads

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}=0.

(16)

Define the multiplier as

\lambda\coloneqq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),H\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, (
16
) becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})-\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast}),T\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}=0.

As
T
is arbitrary,
Lemma

3.3
gives

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})-\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})\equiv 0,

or equivalently
\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x)
for
\mu^{\ast}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Finally, to show uniqueness, assume that there exists another
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})(x)=\lambda^{\prime}\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x)

for
\mu^{\ast}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,
\lambda^{\prime}\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})=\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})
, and

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x).

Since, by assumption
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})(x),H(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\ast}(x)\neq 0
, we conclude
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
.

Remark 3.10
.

Few remarks are in order.
First,
Theorems

3.2
and
3.8
provide necessary conditions for optimality. In particular, they do
not
provide conditions for existence of minimizers for
J
or (
14
).
Second,
Theorem

3.8
readily extends to multidimensional constraints of the form
K{}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
, with
l\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, which we deliberately omitted to ease the exposition.
Third,
Theorems

3.2
and
3.8
apply to maximization problems with no changes. One simply considers upper semi-continuous (w.r.t. weak convergence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) functional

with the effective domain
D(J)\coloneqq\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):J(\mu)>-\infty\}\neq\emptyset
.
We will tacitly use this fact throughout the next sections.

Under a convexity assumption, we can study sufficient conditions in the more general setting of inequality-constrained optimization. The first result is an application of
Theorem

3.4
:

Theorem 3.11

(First-order sufficient conditions for constrained optimization)
.

Let
J,K:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be convex along geodesics.
Consider

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{J(\mu):K(\mu)\leq 0\right\}.

(17)

Suppose there exists (finite)
\lambda\geq 0
and
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
J
and
K
are Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})+\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle K(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=0.

Then,
\mu^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of (
17
). Moreover, if
J
is
\alpha_{J}
-convex along geodesics,
K
is
\alpha_{K}
-convex along geodesics, and
\alpha_{J}+\lambda\alpha_{K}>0
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer.

Proof 3.12
.

Without loss of generality,
\lambda>0
; else, the result follows directly from
Theorem

3.4
.
Let
\alpha_{J}\geq 0
be the convexity parameter of
J
and
\alpha_{K}
be the convexity parameter of
K
, then
J+\lambda K
is
(\alpha_{J}+\lambda\alpha_{K})
-geodesically convex.
By
Theorem

3.4
,
\mu^{\ast}
is a (global) minimizer of
\mu\mapsto J(\mu)+\lambda K(\mu)
.
Then, let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\nu\neq\mu^{\ast}
and
K(\nu)\leq 0
:

\displaystyle J(\nu)

\displaystyle\geq J(\nu)+\lambda K(\nu)

\displaystyle\geq J(\mu^{\ast})+\lambda K(\mu^{\ast})+\frac{\alpha_{J}+\lambda\alpha_{K}}{2}W_{2}(\mu^{\ast},\nu)^{2}

\displaystyle=J(\mu^{\ast})+\frac{\alpha_{J}+\lambda\alpha_{K}}{2}W_{2}(\mu^{\ast},\nu)^{2}.

Since
\nu
is arbitrary,
\mu^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of (
17
). If additionally
\alpha_{J}+\lambda\alpha_{K}>0
,
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer.

Let

(18)

Suppose there exists (finite)

and
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
J
and
K
are Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})+\lambda\nabla_{\mu}K(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle K(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=0.

Then,
\mu^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of (
18
). Moreover, if

is
\alpha_{J}
-geodesically convex with

, then
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer.

Proof 3.14
.

The proof leverages
Theorem

3.11
with
\mu\mapsto K(\mu)
and
\mu\mapsto-K(\mu)

Unfortunately, the Wasserstein distance is

convex along geodesics, which prevents us from deploying

Theorem 3.15

(First-order sufficient conditions for constrained optimization, rivisited)
.

Let
J:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be convex along any interpolation curve with convexity parameter
\alpha_{J}\in\mathbb{R}

2.18
. Let
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and suppose that

is
\alpha_{c}
-convex for all

. Consider

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{J(\mu):W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(19)

Suppose there exist finite
\lambda\geq\frac{\alpha_{J}^{-}}{\alpha_{c}}
and
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
J
and
W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})
are Wasserstein differentiable at
\mu^{\ast}
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu)+\lambda\nabla_{\mu}W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle W_{c}(\mu,\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon.

Then,
\mu^{\ast}
is the a global minimizer of (
19
). If additionally
\alpha_{J}>0
or
\lambda>\frac{\alpha_{J}^{-}}{\alpha_{c}}
, then
\mu^{\ast}
is the strict minimizer.

Proof 3.16
.

By
Proposition

2.18
and convexity along interpolation curves of
J
, the function
\mathcal{L}(\mu)=J(\mu)+\lambda W_{c}(\bar{\mu},\mu)
is
(\lambda\alpha_{c}+\alpha_{J})
-convex along the interpolation curve between
\mu^{\ast}
and
\nu

\bar{\mu}

(22)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:first order sufficient conditions constrained wasserstein strong differential 1},\eqref{eq:first order sufficient conditions constrained wasserstein strong differential 2}}{}

is
(\lambda\alpha_{c}+\alpha_{J})
-convex the interpolation curve
\mu_{t}
, for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\begin{aligned} &(1-t)\mathcal{L}(\mu^{\ast})+t\mathcal{L}(\nu)-\frac{\lambda\alpha_{c}+\alpha_{J}}{2}t(1-t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\norm{y-x}^{2}\mathrm{d}((\proj_{2},\proj_{3})_{\#}\gamma)(x,y)\end{aligned}

where the second inequality follows from
\lambda\geq\frac{\alpha_{J}^{-}}{\alpha_{c}}
and suboptimality of the plan
(\proj_{2},\proj_{3})_{\#}\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu^{\ast},\nu)

\mu^{\ast}

4
Application to the Evaluation of Worst-case Risk over Wasserstein Ambiguity Sets

Consider the problem of evaluating the worst-case risk of a real-valued function over an ambiguity set defined in terms of the Wasserstein distance, initimately related to
DRO
(cf.
Example

1.1
).
It is well known that when the risk is the expected value of an (integrable) real-valued function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, then the worst-case risk admits the following dual reformulation
[
4
,
3
]
:

\sup_{\mu\in\bar{B}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu})}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[f\right]=\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}\lambda\varepsilon^{2}-\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda\norm{x-y}^{2}-f(y)\right],

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}}{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}\right)^{2}\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\norm{w}^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}}.

Finally, we multiply (
36
) by
\lambda^{2}(1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2})^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}
and rearrange to obtain the fourth-order polynomial equation
f(\lambda)=0
with

f(\lambda)\coloneqq\lambda^{4}-2\rho\norm{w}^{2}\lambda^{3}+\left(-\frac{\rho^{2}\norm{w}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]-\frac{\norm{w}^{2}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}+\rho^{2}\norm{w}^{4}\right)\lambda^{2}+\frac{\rho\norm{w}^{4}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\lambda-\frac{\rho^{2}\norm{w}^{6}}{4\varepsilon^{2}}.

To ease the notation, let

\Lambda\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,\rho\norm{w}^{2}]:f(\lambda)=0\}.

The set
\Lambda
contains all real roots of
f
such that
1-\rho\norm{w}^{2}/\lambda>0
. Values of
\lambda\in[0,\rho\norm{w}^{2}]
would contradict the optimality of the transport map. Clearly,
|\Lambda|\leq 4
; we will now prove
\Lambda
is non-empty.
First,
f(\lambda)\to+\infty
as
\lambda\to\pm\infty
. Second,
f(\lambda)\to-\rho^{2}\norm{w}^{6}/(4\varepsilon^{2})<0
for
\lambda\uparrow 0
. Third,
f(\lambda)\to-\norm{w}^{6}\rho^{4}\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]/\varepsilon^{2}<0
(since
\hat{\mu}
is absolutely continuous,
\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]>0

\lambda\downarrow\rho\norm{w}^{2}>0

\Lambda
is non-empty and
|\Lambda|\geq 2
: one strictly negative root and one root strictly larger than
\rho\norm{w}^{2}
.
The resulting cost is

\displaystyle J^{\ast}=J(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\var^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro variance opt map}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x-A^{-1}b\right\rangle\right]+\rho\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x-A^{-1}b\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x\right\rangle\right]-\left\langle w,A^{-1}b\right\rangle+\rho\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle w,A^{-1}x-A^{-1}b-A^{-1}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[x\right]+A^{-1}b\right\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro variance A times b}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\norm{w}^{2}}{2\lambda}-\frac{\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle A^{-1}w,x-\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[x\right]\right\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro variance equality Ainv}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\frac{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}{{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\langle w,x-\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[x\right]\right\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\norm{w}^{2}}\right)^{2}\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right].

By inspection, any negative
\lambda\in\Lambda
cannot be optimal, as strictly outperformed by
\lambda>\rho\norm{w}^{2}
. In particular, our candidate for optimality is
\lambda^{\ast}=\max\Lambda
.
To show that
\lambda^{\ast}>\rho\norm{w}^{2}
is indeed optimal, we will use sufficient conditions.
From above, there exists
\mu^{\ast}
absolutely continuous and
\lambda>\rho\norm{w}^{2}
such that

-\nabla_{\mu}J(\mu^{\ast})=\lambda\nabla_{\mu}W_{2}(\mu^{\ast},\hat{\mu})^{2}.

Thanks to
Lemma

4.8
and
Theorem

3.15
,
\mu^{\ast}
is the unique global minimizer. In particular, this also proves that
\Lambda
contains at most one root strictly larger than
\rho\norm{w}^{2}
; else, we would contradict uniqueness.

Remark 4.15
.

When
\rho\to 0
,
\lambda^{\ast}=1/(2\varepsilon)
and we recover the results of
Section

4.1
.
Conversely, when
\rho\to\infty
,
\lambda^{\ast}/\rho=\norm{w}^{2}\pm\norm{w}\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]/\varepsilon
, and

\displaystyle\frac{J^{\ast}}{\rho}

\displaystyle=\left(\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\varepsilon\norm{w}\right)^{2},

(37)

where
\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]\coloneqq\sqrt{\var^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}
is the standard deviation of
\left\langle w,x\right\rangle
. This is again a form of explicit regularization.

Our results are in agreement with
[
61
, Appendix C]
, and additionally fully characterize the worst-case probability measure and prove its uniqueness.
Differently from
[
61
]
, we do not base our analysis on structural ambiguity sets consisting on all semidefinite affine pushforwards of the center
\hat{\mu}

[
61
, Definitions 6]
, but we work in the “full” probability space

4.3
Mean-Standard Deviation

Consider now the mean-standard deviation functional

J(\mu)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\std^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\sqrt{\var^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}

(38)

for some non-zero
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\rho>0
. Also this risk measure found application in portfolio theory
[
63
]
. Due to its nonlinearity, the mean-standard deviation functional has not been studied in the context of
DRO
, with the exception of
[
61
]
which provides an upper bound (so-called Gelbrich risk) on the worst-case cost.
First, we study properties of
J
:

Lemma 4.16

(Properties of
J
)
.

The mean-standard deviation cost functional
-J
is continuous with respect to weak convergence. Moreover, for all
\mu\in\bar{B}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu})

J(\mu)\leq J(\hat{\mu})+\varepsilon\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}.

(39)

Proof 4.17
.

Continuity follows from
Proposition

2.21
and
Corollary

2.24
, together with continuity of
x\mapsto-\sqrt{x}
. To prove the upper bound, let
\mu\in\bar{B}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu})
be arbitrary. Then,

\displaystyle J(\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\std^{\mu}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle w,\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x\right]\right\rangle+\rho\sqrt{{w}^{\top}\var^{\mu}\left[x\right]w}

\displaystyle=\left\langle w,\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[x\right]\right\rangle+\rho\norm{\variance{\mu}{x}^{1/2}w}

\displaystyle\overset{\triangle}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq}\begin{aligned} &\left\langle w,\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[x\right]\right\rangle+\norm{w}\norm{\expectedValue{\mu}{x}-\expectedValue{\refmu}{x}}\\
&+\rho\norm{\variance{\refmu}{x}^{1/2}w}+\rho\norm{w}\norm{\variance{\mu}{x}^{1/2}-\variance{\refmu}{x}^{1/2}}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\overset{\square}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq}J(\hat{\mu})+\norm{w}\left(\norm{\expectedValue{\mu}{x}-\expectedValue{\refmu}{x}}+\rho\norm{\variance{\mu}{x}^{1/2}-\variance{\refmu}{x}^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=J(\hat{\mu})+\norm{w}\left(\norm{\expectedValue{\mu}{x}-\expectedValue{\refmu}{x}}+\rho\norm{\variance{\mu}{x}^{1/2}-\variance{\refmu}{x}^{1/2}}_{\mathrm{Fro}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\heartsuit}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq}J(\hat{\mu})+\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}\sqrt{\norm{\expectedValue{\mu}{x}-\expectedValue{\refmu}{x}}^{2}+\norm{\variance{\mu}{x}^{1/2}-\variance{\refmu}{x}^{1/2}}_{\mathrm{Fro}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\diamondsuit}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\leq}J(\hat{\mu})+\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}W_{2}(\mu,\hat{\mu})

\displaystyle\leq J(\hat{\mu})+\varepsilon\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}},

where

•

in
\triangle
we use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
51
, Theorem 4.2]
;

•

in
\square
we use that the Frobenius norm
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{Fro}}
of a matrix upper bounds its induced norm
\norm{\cdot}

[
64
]
;

•

in
\heartsuit
we use that

\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\geq\frac{a+cb}{\sqrt{1+c^{2}}}
for all non-negative
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

•

in
\diamondsuit
we use Gelbrich bound
[
65
]
:
If
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with mean
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
symmetric and positive semi-definite, then,

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\geq\sqrt{\|m_{1}-m_{2}\|^{2}+\|\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}-\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{Fro}}^{2}},

where
\Sigma^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the unique matrix such that
(\Sigma^{1/2})^{2}=\Sigma
and
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{Fro}}
is the Frobenius norm.

Remark 4.18
.

Again,
-J
is not lower semi-continuous w.r.t. narrow convergence. The proof (by counter-example) is identical the one of the mean-variance functional (
Remark

4.10
).

Second, we show that, if they exist, worst-case probability measures lie at the boundary:

Lemma 4.19

(Existence of a maximizer at the boundary)
.

Assume that there exists a worst-case probability measure
\mu^{\ast}
attaining the supremum (
26
). Then,
\mu^{\ast}
lies on the boundary of the Wasserstein ball; i.e.,
W_{2}(\hat{\mu},\mu^{\ast})=\varepsilon
.

Proof 4.20
.

The proof is identical to the proof of
Lemma

4.11
.

Armed with these results, we can now study optimal solutions:

Proposition 4.21

(Optimal solution of mean-standard deviation
DRO
)
.

Consider problem (
25
) with the mean-standard deviation cost functional (
38
).
Assume that
\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Then, the worst-case probability measure is given by
\mu^{\ast}=T_{\#}\hat{\mu}
, where

T(x)=\left(I+\frac{\rho\varepsilon}{\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}w{w}^{\top}\right)x+\left(1-\frac{\rho\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}{\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}}\frac{w}{\norm{w}},

and the resulting worst-case cost reads

J^{\ast}=\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\varepsilon\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}=J(\hat{\mu})+\varepsilon\norm{w}\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}.

(40)

Proof 4.22
.

Again, we assume sufficient regularity to use necessary conditions for optimality to construct a solution. We will then prove that this solution is indeed optimal.

Let us assume that
\mu^{\ast}
exists and that it is absolutely continuous. By
Theorem

3.8
, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

w+\frac{\rho}{\std^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}w\left\langle w,I-\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]\right\rangle=2\lambda\left(\Id{}-T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\hat{\mu}}\right),

(41)

where we used
Propositions

2.21
,
2.24
and
2.20
, together with the chain rule of
Proposition

2.17
, to compute gradients. By assumption,
\mu^{\ast}
is absolutely continuous, and so
\std^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]>0
. In particular, (
41

T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\hat{\mu}}(x)=\underbrace{\left(\Id{}-\alpha w{w}^{\top}\right)}_{\eqqcolon A}x\underbrace{-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\alpha\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}_{\eqqcolon b}w,

(42)

where
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\rho}{2\lambda\std^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}
, and
A={A}^{\top}
and
\spec(A)=\{1,\ldots,1,1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}\}
.
Since
\mu^{\ast}
is, by assumption, absolutely continuous, the optimal transport map

. Moreover, if

is not invertible, which contradicts

T_{\hat{\mu}}^{\mu^{\ast}}(x)=(T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\hat{\mu}})^{-1}(x)=A^{-1}x-A^{-1}b.

It is readily verified that

well defined since

A^{-1}w=\left(1+\frac{\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}{1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}\right)w=\frac{1}{1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}w

and

Thus, the definition and (

which gives

\alpha=\frac{\rho}{2\lambda\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\norm{w}^{2}}.

(46)

and

1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}=\frac{2\lambda\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}{2\lambda\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\norm{w}^{2}}>0.

Thus,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=I-\frac{\rho}{2\lambda\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\norm{w}^{2}}w{w}^{\top}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\alpha\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]w

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro std opt map}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\alpha\left(\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x\right\rangle\right]-\left\langle w,A^{-1}b\right\rangle)\right)w

\displaystyle=-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\alpha\left(\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle A^{-1}w,x\right\rangle\right]-\left\langle A^{-1}w,b\right\rangle)\right)w

\displaystyle=-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]w-\alpha w{w}^{\top}A^{-1}b

\displaystyle=-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]w+b-A^{-1}b,

and so

A^{-1}b=-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha\norm{w}^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]w\overset{\eqref{eq:dro std alpha}}{=}-\frac{w}{2\lambda}+\frac{\rho}{2\lambda\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]w.

We can now evaluate the constraint

to find an expression for

We can proceed as in (

\displaystyle J^{\ast}=J(\mu^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho\std^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro std opt map}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x-A^{-1}b\right\rangle\right]+\rho\std^{\mu^{\ast}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dro std std star}}{}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,A^{-1}x\right\rangle\right]-\left\langle w,A^{-1}b\right\rangle+\frac{2\lambda^{\ast}\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]+\rho^{2}\norm{w}^{2}}{2\lambda^{\ast}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle=}\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle A^{-1}w,x\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\norm{w}^{2}}{2\lambda^{\ast}}-\frac{\rho\norm{w}^{2}}{2\lambda^{\ast}\std^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]}\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mu}}\left[\left\langle w,x\right\rangle\right]\\

5
Minimum Entropy on Wasserstein Balls

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\kl(\mu|\mu_{\mathrm{p}}):W_{2}(\mu,\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(47)

with prior probability measure
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and reference probability measure
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The optimization problem (
47
) combines the well-known Kullback’s principle of minimum cross-entropy
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
in statistical inference with additional side information, encoded in the probability measure
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}

[
66
]
.
For instance, in
[
66
]
,
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
is chosen to be the empirical probability measure
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
constructed with
N
data points
\{(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, while
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
is a strictly positive probability measure on a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here, we study optimality conditions for (
47
).
We assume that
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}=e^{-V_{\mathrm{p}}}\mathcal{L}^{d}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}=e^{-V_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathcal{L}^{d}
for some
V_{\mathrm{p}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
V_{\mathrm{r}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
convex and continuously differentiable.
To start, we show that a minimizer exists:

Lemma 5.1

(Existence)
.

The optimization problem (
47
) has a solution. Moreover, any minimizer
\mu^{\ast}
is absolutely continuous.

Proof 5.2
.

The proof follows by lower semi-continuity of the
KL
divergence (
Proposition

2.28
) and compactness of Wasserstein balls (
Proposition

4.3
) w.r.t. narrow convergence. Since the
KL
divergence evaluates to infinity for non-absolutely continuous measures, the minimizer is necessarily absolutely continuous.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the minimizer lies on the boundary; else, we trivially have
\mu^{\ast}=\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
. Then,
Theorem

3.2
leads to the following optimality condition, which yields to an (almost) closed-form solution when
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{r}}
are Gaussian:

Proposition 5.3

(Conditions for optimality)
.

Consider problem (
47
), and let
\mu^{\ast}=\rho^{\ast}\mathcal{L}^{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{2,\mathrm{abs}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an optimal solution of (
47

Then, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\nabla V_{\mathrm{p}}-\nabla T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}(\nabla V_{\mathrm{r}}\circ T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}})+\tr\left((\nabla T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}\nabla T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)=2\lambda(\Id-T_{\mu^{\ast}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}),

(48)

provided all terms are well defined almost everywhere.
In particular, if
V_{\mathrm{p}}(x)
and
V_{\mathrm{r}}(x)
are convex quadratic of the form

6
Conclusions

We studied first-order conditions for optimization in the Wasserstein space. We combined the geometric and differential properties of the Wasserstein space with classical calculus of variations to formulate first-order necessary and sufficient conditions for optimality, showing that simple and interpretable rationales (e.g., “set the gradient to zero” and “gradients are aligned at optimality”) carry over to the Wasserstein space.
With our tools we can study and solve, sometimes in closed form, optimization problems in distributionally robust optimization and statistical inference.
We hope our results will pave the way for future research on optimization in probability spaces through the lense of optimal transport.

Appendix A

In this section, we provide some technical background in measure theory.
We start with tightness:

Definition A.1

(Tight)
.

A set of probability measures

A simple criterion for tightness is the following:

Lemma A.2

(Criterion for tightness)

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Suppose there exists
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty.

Then,
\mathcal{K}
is tight.

Proof A.3
.

The statement can be found in
[
27
, Remark 5.1.5]

\varepsilon>0
, and let

C\coloneqq\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty.

\Phi

is compact. Then, for

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\Phi(x)}{C/\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{C}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

Since

Tightness of a set is intimately related to its compactness (under narrow convergence):

Theorem A.4

(Prokhorov
[
27

A set

Next, we focus on the narrow convergence of transport plans

Proposition A.5

(
