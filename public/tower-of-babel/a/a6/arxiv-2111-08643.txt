Abstract
We are concerned with employing Model Order Reduction (MOR) to efficiently solve parameterized multiscale problems using the Localized Orthogonal Decomposition (LOD) multiscale method. Like many multiscale methods, the LOD follows the idea of separating the problem into localized fine-scale subproblems and an effective coarse-scale system derived from the solutions of the local problems. While the Reduced Basis (RB) method has already been used to speed up the solution of the fine-scale problems, the resulting coarse system remained untouched, thus limiting the achievable speed up. In this work we address this issue by applying the RB methodology to a new two-scale formulation of the LOD. By reducing the entire two-scale system, this two-scale Reduced Basis LOD (TSRBLOD) approach, yields reduced order models that are completely independent from the size of the coarse mesh of the multiscale approach, allowing an efficient approximation of the solutions of parameterized multiscale problems even for very large domains. A rigorous and efficient a posteriori estimator bounds the model reduction error, taking into account the approximation error for both the local fine-scale problems and the global coarse-scale system. Keywords : model order reduction, localized orthogonal decomposition, multiscale problems, reduced basis method, a posteriori error estimation, two-scale formulation AMS Mathematics Subject Classification : 35J20, 65N15, 65N30

Introduction

The numerical approximation of partial differential equations that exhibit multiscale structures has many applications in the natural sciences.
Typical examples are, for instance, the simulation of composite materials or of flow in porous media.
Due to the different spatial or temporal scales at which the physics in such problems are modeled,
a direct solution with standard methods such as finite elements is often prohibitively expensive, as very fine meshes over large computational domains are required to resolve all relevant features.

Numerical multiscale methods are designed to overcome this issue by resolving the micro-structures only locally to solve small cell problems that yield the fine-scale information required to build an effective coarse-scale system, which then can be solved with modest computational effort.
Some of these multiscale methods, such as the Heterogeneous Multiscale Method (HMM)
[
40
]
,
are based on ideas from mathematical homogenization and aim at computing effective coefficients for an appropriate coarse-scale equation.
Other approaches, such as the Multiscale Finite Element Method (MsFEM)
[
24
,
15
]
or the Generalized Finite Element Method (GFEM)
[
8
]
, instead construct coarse-scale elements that incorporate the local fine-scale features of the solution and then approximate the solution in the space spanned by these multiscale elements.

The Localized Orthogonal Decomposition (LOD)
[
28
]
, which itself is based on the Variational Multiscale Method (VMM)
[
25
]
, instead makes use of a splitting of the full fine-scale approximation space into a negligible fine-scale component and an orthogonal multiscale space in which the solution is sought.
This multiscale space is then approximated by computing localized approximations of the orthogonal projection into the finescale space (corrector problems).
For an extensive overview of the LOD, we refer to
[
29
]
and the references therein.
In this work we consider a Petrov–Galerkin formulation of the LOD
[
16
]
,
which has lower storage and communication requirements for the computed fine-scale data in comparison to the original Galerkin formulation.

Many problems modeled with partial differential equations include parameters which need to be varied, e.g., to find an optimal choice of parameter values w.r.t. some cost functional.
Due to the large costs of repeatedly solving the problem for different parameters in such workflows, Model Order Reduction (MOR) techniques are often used to replace the model at hand by a quickly evaluable surrogate.
In this work we consider Reduced Basis (RB) methods, which build such a Reduced Order Model (ROM) by projecting the model equations onto a problem adapted subspace spanned by solutions of the Full Order Model (FOM) for appropriately selected
snapshot
parameters.
For a detailed overview and discussion on RB methods, we refer to the monographs
[
22
,
39
]
and the tutorial introduction
[
17
]
.

For large, parameterized multiscale problems, RB methods need to be combined with numerical multiscale methods, as otherwise the solution of the FOM for a single snapshot parameter might already be computationally infeasible.
One possible approach is to speed up the solution of the individual cell problems using RB techniques, as is done in
[
11
]

in the context of numerical homogenization, in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
for the HMM, in
[
14
,
23
,
32
]
for the MsFEM, or in
[
6
]
for the LOD.
This approach is applicable both for problems, where the fine-scale data variation over the domain is parameterized, resulting in a single ROM for all cell problems
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
11
,
23
]
, or for general parameterized problems, where for each individual cell problem a dedicated ROM is built
[
6
,
32
]
.
Some recent works have considered the case where the multiscale data varies only in some cells, e.g., caused by perturbations, by a sequence of modification over time or by other parameterizations, such that individual fine-scale solutions can be reused
[
19
,
21
,
27
]
or a ROM can be built in case of parameterized perturbations
[
31
]
.

All these approaches have in common that, while the constructed surrogates are independent of the resolution of the fine-scale mesh, the effort for their evaluation still scales with the size of the used coarse mesh.
Hence, if the coarse mesh itself is large, the repeated assembly and solution of the coarse system still can become a computational bottleneck.
Further, the influence of the approximation error of the cell problem ROMs on the coarse system is not rigorously controlled.

In this work we introduce a new two-scale reduced basis method for the LOD (TSRBLOD) that takes both the local corrector
problems as well as the coarse-scale problem into account to produce a single small-size ROM that no longer requires explicit solutions of local subproblems in the online phase.
The model is constructed based on a new two-scale formulation of the LOD as a single variational problem, inspired by
the theory of two-scale convergence in mathematical homogenization
[
7
]
.
As the computation of solution snapshots for this model would still be computationally expensive, we combine this
formulation with a preceding reduction of the fine-scale corrector problems similar to the RBLOD approach in
[
6
]
.
Rigorous and efficient a posteriori bounds derived from the two-scale formulation control the error over this entire
two-stage reduction process.

While our two-scale formulation of the LOD is new, an online-efficient RB-ROM for locally periodic homogenization problems was developed for the HMM in
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
based on the two-scale formulation in
[
33
]
.
Further, we mention localized MOR techniques
[
13
]
, which can also be employed to obain online-efficient ROMs for large multiscale problems.
Whereas numerical multiscale methods build on the idea of scale separation and computing effective global systems from
local information, these methods are usually based on the idea of decomposing the computational domain and deriving a globally coupled ROM
from local ROMs associated with the domain decomposition.

2
Problem formulation

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},p\in\mathbb{N}
be a parameter space and
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a bounded Lipschitz domain.
We consider the following prototypical parameterized elliptic partial differential equation: for a fixed parameter
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
,
find
u_{\mu}
such that

(1)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot A_{\mu}(x)\nabla u_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=f(x),\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\;\;\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

We assume that the parameter-dependent coefficient field
A_{\mu}
has a multiscale structure that renders a direct
solution of (
1
) using, e.g., finite elements computationally infeasible due to the
high mesh resolution required to resolve all features of
A_{\mu}
.

Further, we assume
A_{\mu}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
to be symmetric and uniformly elliptic w.r.t.
x
and
\mu
, i.e., there exist
0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
such that

(2)

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}\ \forall x\in\Omega\ \forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \alpha|\xi|^{2}\leq\xi^{T}\cdot A_{\mu}(x)\cdot\xi\leq\beta|\xi|^{2},

and we let
\kappa:=\beta/\alpha
be the maximum contrast of
A_{\mu}
.
Moreover, let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We consider the weak formulation of (
1
):
for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we seek
u_{\mu}\in V:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(3)

a_{\mu}(u_{\mu},v)=F(v)\qquad\forall v\in V,

where

a_{\mu}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\left(A_{\mu}(x)\nabla u(x)\right)\cdot\nabla v(x)\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx\qquad\text{ and }\qquad F(v):=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx.

Due to our assumptions,
a_{\mu}
is a continuous, coercive bilinear form on
V
and
F\in V^{\prime}
, s.t. (
3
)
admits a unique solution by the Lax-Milgram theorem.

As a final assumption, which will be required in
Section

5
to obtain an online-efficient
reduced order model, we assume parameter-separability of
A_{\mu}
, i.e.,
we assume
A_{\mu}
to have a decomposition
A_{\mu}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)A_{q}

with non-parametric
A_{q}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and arbitrary
\theta_{q}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This gives rise to the corresponding decomposition

(4)

\displaystyle a_{\mu}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\,a_{q}(u,v),

of
a_{\mu}
, where
a_{q}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}(A_{q}(x)\nabla u(x))\cdot\nabla v(x)\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx
.
In case
A_{\mu}
does not exhibit such a decomposition, empirical interpolation
[
9
]

can be used to obtain an approximate decomposition of
A_{\mu}
.

Finally, for
v\in V=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
we introduce the following norms:

\lVert v\rVert_{1}:=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v(x)|\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx,\qquad\lVert v\rVert_{a,\mu}:=\int_{\Omega}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}\nabla v(x)|\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx.

Note that
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{1}
is a norm on
V
due to Friedrich’s inequality.

We remark that our approach can be easily generalized to parametric
f
and other
boundary conditions.

3
Localized Orthogonal Decomposition

In this section we review the basic concepts of the LOD. The general idea of the LOD is to decompose the solution space into a subspace
V^{\text{f}}_{h}
of negligible
fine-scale variations and an
a_{\mu}
-orthogonal low-dimensional coarse space of multiscale functions,
in which the solution is approximated.
This multiscale space is constructed by computing suitable fine-scale corrections
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(u_{H})
of functions from a
given coarse finite-element space
V_{H}
.
Due to the dampening of high-frequency oscillations by
a_{\mu}
, these corrections can then be approximated by the
solution of decoupled localized corrector problems.

In recent years, there have been various formulations of the LOD in terms of localization, interpolation, approximation schemes and applications.
Since we are concerned with the case where storage restrictions may prevent to store explicit solutions of the corrector problems, we focus on the Petrov–Galerkin version of the LOD (PG–LOD)
[
16
]
, which is favorable in this respect.
For further background, we refer to
[
29
]
.

3.1
Discretization and patches

Let
V_{h}\subset V
be a conforming finite-element space of dimension
{N_{h}}
, and let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be the corresponding
shape regular mesh over the computational domain
\Omega
.
We assume that the mesh size
h
is chosen such that all features of
A_{\mu}
are resolved, making a global
solution within
V_{h}
infeasible.
Further, we assume to be given a coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
with mesh size
H\gg h
that is aligned with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, and let

V_{H}:=V_{h}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{H}),

where
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{H})
denotes
\mathcal{T}_{H}
-piecewise affine functions that are continuous on
\Omega
.
We denote the dimension of
V_{H}
by
{N_{H}}
.

For an arbitrary set
\omega\subseteq\Omega
, we define coarse grid element patches
U_{k}(\omega)\subset\Omega
of size
0\leq k\in\mathbb{N}
by

U_{0}(\omega):=\omega,\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{k+1}(\omega):=\operatorname{Int}\biggl(\,\overline{\bigcup\left\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}\,\middle|\,\overline{U_{k}(\omega)}\cap\overline{T}\neq\emptyset\right\}}\,\biggr),

where
\operatorname{Int}(X)
is the interior of the set
X
.
For a given patch size
k
, let

C_{k,\text{ovl}}:=\max_{x\in\Omega}\,\#\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}\,|\,x\in U_{k}(T)\}

be the maximum number of element patches overlapping in a single point of
\Omega
.

3.2
Localized multiscale space

In order to define the fine-scale space
V^{\text{f}}_{h}
, we consider a (quasi-)interpolation operator

\mathcal{I}_{H}:V_{h}\to V_{H}
, and let
V^{\text{f}}_{h}:=\ker(\mathcal{I}_{H})
.
Multiple choices for such an interpolation operator are possible (see
[
20
,
38
]
for an overview).
In recent literature, using an operator that is based on local
L^{2}
-projections
[
37
]
has proven advantageous.
However, for the following error analysis we only require that
\mathcal{I}_{H}
is linear,
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{1}
-continuous and idempotent
on
V_{H}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{H}(v_{H})

\displaystyle=v_{H}

\displaystyle\forall v_{H}

\displaystyle\in V_{H},

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{I}_{H}(v_{h})\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}

\displaystyle\in V_{h}.

Next, we define the fine-scale corrections
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(v)\in V^{\text{f}}_{h}
, for a given
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, to be the solution of

a_{\mu}({{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(v_{h}),v^{\text{f}})=a_{\mu}(v_{h},v^{\text{f}})\qquad\qquad\forall\;v^{\text{f}}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h}.

Thus,
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(v_{h})
is the
a_{\mu}
-orthogonal projection of
v_{h}
onto
V^{\text{f}}_{h}
, and
the multiscale space

V_{H,\mu}^{\text{ms}}:=(I-{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}})(V_{H})

is the
a_{\mu}
-orthogonal complement of
V^{\text{f}}_{h}
in
V_{h}
, i.e.,

V_{h}=V_{H,\mu}^{\text{ms}}\oplus_{a_{\mu}}V^{\text{f}}_{h}
.

However, even when
v_{h}
has a small local support,
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(v_{h})
will have global support,
and its computation will require the same effort as a global solution of (
3
) in
V_{h}
.
Thus, we approximate
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mu}}(v_{h})
using localized correctors
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{h})\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
in
the patch-restricted fine-scale spaces
V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}:=V^{\text{f}}_{h}\cap H^{1}_{0}(U_{k}(T))
given by

(5)

\displaystyle a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{h}),v^{\text{f}})=a^{T}_{\mu}(v_{h},v^{\text{f}})\qquad\qquad\forall\;v^{\text{f}}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T},

where
a^{T}_{\mu}
denotes the bilinear form obtained by restricting the integration domain in the definition of
a_{\mu}
to
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
.
We then define the localized corrector by

(6)

{{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\mu}}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu},

and the localized multiscale space
V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
by

V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}:=(I-{{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\mu}})(V_{H}).

Due to the exponential decay of the correctors
[
28
]
, we choose a patch localization parameter of

k\approx|\log H|
to obtain a sufficient approximation.

3.3
Petrov–Galerkin projection

After computing
V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
, we determine an approximation of
u_{\mu}
in this

N_{H}
-dimensional multiscale space via Petrov-Galerkin projection. I.e.,
we let
u_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}\in V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
be the solution of

(7)

a_{\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}},v_{H})=F(v_{H})\qquad\forall\;v_{H}\in V_{H}.

To ensure that (
7
) has a unique solution, inf-sup stability of
a_{\mu}
w.r.t.
V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
and
V_{H}

is required, which has been shown in
[
16
,
21
]
.
Compared to these references, we use a slightly different definition of the inf-sup stability constant for (
7
)
by using
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{1}
instead of
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{a,\mu}
for the test space:

\gamma_{k}:=\inf_{0\neq w_{H}\in V_{H}}\sup_{0\neq v_{H}\in V_{H}}\frac{a_{\mu}(w_{H}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(w_{H}),v_{H})}{\lVert w_{H}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(w_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{1}}.

For
k
large enough, we have that

(8)

\gamma_{k}\approx\alpha^{1/2}C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{-1},

which can be proven with a simple modification of the argument in
[
21
, Section 4]
.
In particular, w.l.o.g. we assume that
\gamma_{k}\leq\alpha^{1/2}
.

Writing the solution of (
7
) as
u_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}=u_{H,k,\mu}-{{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\mu}}(u_{H,k,\mu})
with
u_{H,k,\mu}\in V_{H}
,
we have the following a priori estimate, which was first shown in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(A priori convergence result for the PG-LOD)
.

For a fixed parameter
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, let

u_{h,\mu}\in V_{h}
be the finite-element solution of (
3
) given by

a_{\mu}(u_{h,\mu},v_{h})=F(v_{h})\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Then it holds that

\lVert u_{h,\mu}-u_{H,k,\mu}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u_{h,\mu}-u_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}\rVert_{1}\lesssim(H+\theta^{k}k^{d/2})\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

with
0<\theta<1
independent of
H
and
k
, but dependent on the contrast
\kappa
.

Although our setting is slightly different to the one in
[
16
]
(in terms of localization and interpolation), the proof can still be followed analogously.
For a detailed discussion on the decay variable
\theta
, we refer to
[
20
,
21
,
28
]
.

3.4
Computational aspects

In order to solve (
7
), we need to solve multiple corrector problems (
5
) for every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
.
These correctors are then used to assemble a localized multiscale matrix
\mathbb{K}_{\mu}
given by

(9)

\mathbb{K}_{\mu}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu},\quad\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}\right)_{ji}:=(A_{\mu}(\chi_{T}\nabla-\nabla{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu})\phi_{i}\,,\,\nabla\phi_{j})_{U_{k}(T)},

where
\chi_{T}
denotes the indicator function on
T
and
\phi_{i}
the finite-element basis functions of
V_{H}
.
Solving (
7
) then is equivalent to solving the linear system

(10)

\mathbb{K}_{\mu}\cdot\underline{u}_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}=\mathbb{F},

where
\mathbb{F}_{i}:=F(\phi_{i})
, and
\underline{u}_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{H}}
is the vector of coefficients of
u_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}

w.r.t. the basis of
V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
corresponding to the finite-element basis
\phi_{i}
.

Compared to Galerkin projection onto
V_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
, the system matrix
\mathbb{K}_{\mu}
of the Petrov-Galerkin
formulation has a smaller sparsity pattern, and we need less computational work to assemble the matrix.
Every localized corrector and thus each local contribution to
\mathbb{K}_{\mu}
can be computed in parallel without any communication and can be deleted after the contribution
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}
has been computed.
In particular, note that the local contribution matrices
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}
only have non-zeros in columns
i
for which
T\subseteq\supp\phi_{i}
.

Overall, to compute the LOD solution the following steps are required:

1.

For every
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
: Compute
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(\phi_{i})
by solving (
5
) for each
i
s.t.
T\subseteq\supp\phi_{i}
. Assemble
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}
according to (
9
).

2.

Assemble the localized multiscale stiffness matrix
\mathbb{K}_{\mu}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}
from the local contributions computed in step 1.

3.

Solve equation (
10
) to compute
\underline{u}_{H,k,\mu}^{\text{ms}}
.

In general, neither of the steps 1–3 is computational negligible, and each of these steps has to be repeated to
obtain a solution for a new
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
.
Note that step 1 requires computations on the fine-scale level whereas steps 2 and 3 solely depend on the size of the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
.
In
[
6
]
, RB approximations of the local corrector problems were introduced to obtain a model that is
independent from the size of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
However, for large
\mathcal{T}_{H}
, the costs of solving the reduced corrector problems in step 1 and the further computations
in steps 2 and 3 still can be significant.
The two-scale reduction approach introduced in this work takes all computational steps into account and yields a
reduced order model that is independent from the sizes of both
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\mathcal{T}_{H}
.

4
Two-scale formulation of the PG–LOD

We formulate the PG–LOD method in a two-scale formulation.
In particular, we aim at considering the PG–LOD solution as the solution of one single system where the coarse system (
7
) and all fine scale corrections (
5
) are solved at the same time.
This formulation will be the basis for the Stage 2 ROM constructed in
Section

5.2
.

4.1
The two-scale bilinear form

Let
\mathfrak{V}
denote the two-scale function space given by the direct sum of Hilbert spaces

\mathfrak{V}:=V_{H}\oplus V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}.

In particular, for
\mathfrak{u}=(u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\in\mathfrak{V}
we define the
two-scale
H^{1}
-norm of
\mathfrak{u}
by

\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1}^{2}:=\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{1}^{2}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\left\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}.

On this space, we define the two-scale bilinear form
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}\in\text{Bil}(\mathfrak{V})
given by

\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}\left((u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}),(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}})\right):=\\
a_{\mu}(u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T},v_{H})+\rho^{1/2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}(u^{\text{f}}_{T},v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{\mu}^{T}(u_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T}),

with a stabilization parameter
\rho\geq 1
that will be chosen later.
Further let
\mathfrak{F}\in\mathfrak{V}^{\prime}
be given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}\left((v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}})\right)

\displaystyle:=F(v_{H}),

and define the two-scale solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}\in\mathfrak{V}
of of the PG–LOD by the variational problem

(11)

\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}\left(\mathfrak{u}_{\mu},\mathfrak{v}\right)=\mathfrak{F}(\mathfrak{v})\qquad\forall\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{V}.

We show, that (
11
) is equivalent to the original formulation
(
5
), (
7
):

Proposition 4.1
.

The two-scale solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}\in\mathfrak{V}
of
Eq.

11
is uniquely determined and given by

(12)

\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}=\left[u_{H,k,\mu},\,{\mathcal{Q}}^{T_{1}}_{k,\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu}),\,\ldots,\,{\mathcal{Q}}^{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}_{k,\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu})\right].

Proof.

With
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}
as in (
12
) we have for any

\mathfrak{v}=(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\ldots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u}_{\mu},\mathfrak{v})

\displaystyle=a_{\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu}),v_{H})

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+\rho^{1/2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu}),v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{\mu}^{T}(u_{H,k,\mu},v^{\text{f}}_{T})

where we have used the definition of
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H,k,\mu})
to eliminate the sum over
\mathcal{T}_{H}
,
the definition of
{{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\mu}}

Eq.

6
and the definition of
u_{H,k,\mu}

Eq.

7
.

To show that
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}
is the only solution of (

for all

implies

For that, first note that for

4.2
Analysis of the two-scale bilinear form

We introduce two weighted norms
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
,
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1,\mu}
on
\mathfrak{V}
, w.r.t. which we will show the inf-sup
stability of
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
and derive approximation error bounds.
For arbitrary
\mathfrak{u}=(u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\in\mathfrak{V}

these norms are given by

\displaystyle\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2},

\displaystyle\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1,\mu}^{2}

Proposition 4.2
.

\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
and
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1,\mu}
are norms on
\mathfrak{V}
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{u}\mapsto u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}
and

\mathfrak{u}\mapsto{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}
are linear, the pull-back
norms
\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}
and
\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}

are semi-norms on
\mathfrak{V}
. Hence,
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
is a semi-norm on
\mathfrak{V}

as well.

Further, we have
\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{1}=\lVert\mathcal{I}_{H}(u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\hskip-2.0ptu^{\text{f}}_{T})\rVert_{1}\leq C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\hskip-2.0ptu^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}\leq C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}\alpha^{-1/2}\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\hskip-2.0ptu^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}
, so

\vvvert u\vvvert_{a,\mu}=0
implies
u_{H}=0
. This in turn implies

\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}=\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}=0
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
,
so
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}=0
. Hence,
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
is indeed a norm on
\mathfrak{V}
.
The argument for
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1,\mu}
is similar.
∎

We are going to show that
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
and
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1,\mu}
are equivalent norms, for which we require some technical results.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
be given. Then we have:

\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2}\leq C_{k,\text{ovl}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\leq C_{k,\text{ovl}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}.

Proof.

Using Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla v^{\text{f}}_{T}(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}C_{k,\text{ovl}}\cdot\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla v^{\text{f}}_{T}(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx=C_{k,\text{ovl}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}.

The proof for
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{1}
is the same.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For arbitrary
u_{H}\in V_{H}
we have

\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}.

Proof.

By definition of
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}
we have

\begin{split}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}&\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}),{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}))=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}^{T}(u_{H},{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}))\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\biggl(\int_{T}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla u_{H}(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx\biggr)^{1/2}\cdot\biggl(\int_{T}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx\biggr)^{1/2}\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\leq\biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\int_{T}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla u_{H}(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx\biggr)^{1/2}\cdot\biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\int_{T}|A_{\mu}^{1/2}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})(x)|^{2}\integralend\hskip-1.25ptx\biggr)^{1/2}\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\leq\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\cdot\biggl(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}u_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Dividing by the second factor yields the claim.
∎

Now, we are prepared to show the equivalence of both norms.

Proposition 4.5
.

\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
and
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1,\mu}
are equivalent norms on
\mathfrak{V}

with the following bounds for every
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{V}

C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{-1}\alpha^{1/2}\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1,\mu}\leq\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}\leq\sqrt{3}(1+C_{k,\text{ovl}})^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1,\mu}.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{u}=(u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\ldots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})
. To bound
\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1,\mu}

by
\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}
, note that

(13)

\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{1}^{2}\leq C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{2}\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{2}\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2},

and, using

,

\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{2}\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}.

To bound
\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}
by
\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{1,\mu}
we use
Lemma

4.3
,
Lemma

4.4
and

to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 3\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+3C_{k,\text{ovl}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+3C_{k,\text{ovl}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 3(1+C_{k,\text{ovl}})\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+3C_{k,\text{ovl}}\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 3(1+C_{k,\text{ovl}})\beta\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{1}^{2}+3C_{k,\text{ovl}}\beta\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2}.

Adding

to both sides yields the claim.
∎

Finally, we show that

-
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1}
inf-sup stable with controllable constants.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

\rho:=C_{k,\text{ovl}}\cdot\kappa,

then
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
is
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{a,\mu}
-
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1}
-continuous and inf-sup stable
with the following bounds on the respective constants:

\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{V}}\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathfrak{V}}\frac{\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})}{\vvvert u\vvvert_{a,\mu}\cdot\vvvert v\vvvert_{1}}\leq\beta^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{0\neq\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{V}}\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathfrak{V}}\frac{\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})}{\vvvert u\vvvert_{a,\mu}\cdot\vvvert v\vvvert_{1}}\geq\gamma_{k}/\sqrt{5}.

Proof.

We first bound the continuity constant of
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
. To this end, let

\mathfrak{u}=(u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\ldots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\in\mathfrak{V}
and

\mathfrak{v}=(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\ldots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\in\mathfrak{V}
be arbitrary.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})

\displaystyle=a_{\mu}(u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T},v_{H})-\rho^{1/2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\left(a_{\mu}(u^{\text{f}}_{T},v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{\mu}^{T}(u_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\rho^{1/2}\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\rho\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\cdot\left[\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}\cdot\beta^{1/2}\cdot\vvvert\mathfrak{v}\vvvert_{1}.

\displaystyle\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}\hskip-60.00009pt

\displaystyle\leq\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}+\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T})\rVert_{a,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k}^{-1}\sup_{0\neq v_{H}\in V_{H}}\frac{a_{\mu}(u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}),v_{H})}{\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{1}}+\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T})\rVert_{a,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k}^{-1}\underbrace{\sup_{0\neq v_{H}\in V_{H}}\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},(v_{H},0,\ldots,0))}{\vvvert(v_{H},0,\ldots,0)\vvvert_{1}}}_{A}

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{k}^{-1}\hskip-2.0pt\sup_{0\neq v_{h}\in V_{H}}\hskip-2.0pt\frac{a_{\mu}(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}),v_{H})}{\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{1}}+\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\hskip-2.0pt({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T})\rVert_{a,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k}^{-1}A+\left(\gamma_{k}^{-1}\sup_{0\neq v_{H}\in V_{H}}\frac{\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{a,\mu}}{\lVert v_{H}\rVert_{1}}+1\right)\lVert\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T})\rVert_{a,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k}^{-1}A+(\gamma_{k}^{-1}\beta^{1/2}+1)C_{k,\text{ovl}}^{1/2}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

hence:

\displaystyle\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}+\rho\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{k}^{-2}A^{2}+(2\gamma_{k}^{-2}\beta C_{k,\text{ovl}}+2C_{k,\text{ovl}}+\rho)\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})-u^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}.

Further,

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}(u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}),u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}))}{(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}\frac{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}(u^{\text{f}}_{T}-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(u_{H}),v^{\text{f}}_{T})}{(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{a,\mu}^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{-1/2}\sup_{v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}\frac{\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{\mu}(u^{\text{f}}_{T},v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{\mu}^{T}(u_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T})}{(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1/2}\rho^{-1/2}\underbrace{\sup_{v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}\frac{\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},(0,v^{\text{f}}_{T1},\ldots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}))}{\vvvert(0,v^{\text{f}}_{T1},\ldots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\vvvert_{1}}}_{B}.

Combining both estimates yields

\displaystyle\vvvert\mathfrak{u}\vvvert_{a,\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{k}^{-2}A^{2}+(2\gamma_{k}^{-2}\alpha^{-1}\beta C_{k,\text{ovl}}\rho^{-1}+2\alpha^{-1}C_{k,\text{ovl}}\rho^{-1}+\alpha^{-1})B^{2}

\displaystyle=2\gamma_{k}^{-2}A^{2}+\underbrace{(2\gamma_{k}^{-2}+2\alpha^{-1}\kappa^{-1}+\alpha^{-1})}_{\leq 5\cdot\gamma_{k}^{-2}}B^{2}.

\displaystyle\leq 5\cdot\gamma_{k}^{-2}(A^{2}+B^{2})

\displaystyle=5\cdot\gamma_{k}^{-2}\left(\sup_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathfrak{V}}\frac{\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})}{\vvvert\mathfrak{v}\vvvert_{1}}\right)^{2},

where we have used

and

in the third equality.
In the last equality we have used the fact that the square norm of a linear functional on a direct sum
of Hilbert spaces is the sum of the square norms of the functional restricted to the respective subspaces.
∎

efficiencies of that scale with
\kappa^{1/2}
in the energy norm and with

\kappa
in the 1-norm, which agrees with what is to be expected for
these error bounds in a standard finite element setting.

5
Reduced Basis approach

In this section we describe the construction of the TSRBLOD ROM in detail.
As with all RB methods, the ROM is defined by a projection of the original model
equations, in our case the two-scale formulation
Eq.

11
, onto
a reduced approximation space (
Section

5.2.1
).
Rigorous upper and lower bounds for the MOR error are given by a residual-based
a posteriori error estimator (
Section

5.2.2
).
In order to be able to quickly assemble the ROM for a new parameter
\mu
and to
efficiently evaluate the error estimator, an offline-online decomposition of the
ROM must be performed (
Section

5.2.3
).
As part of the offline phase, the reduced space is constructed
as the linear span of FOM solutions
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu^{*}}
, where the snapshot parameters
\mu^{*}
are selected
via an iterative greedy search over
\mathcal{P}
(
Section

5.2.4
).

The computation of the solution snapshots
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu^{*}}
via
Eq.

12
, however,
requires for each new
\mu^{*}
the recomputation of all corrector problems. For large
problems this may be computationally infeasible.
Thus, we combine our approach (Stage 2) with a preceeding prepartory step similar
to
[
6
]
, where each corrector problem is replaced by an efficient ROM surrogate (Stage 1).
These ROMs are then used in the offline phase of Stage 2 to compute approximate solution snapshots

\mathfrak{u}_{\mu^{*}}
.
Again, Stage 1 is divided into ROM construction (
Section

5.1.1
), error estimation
(
Section

5.1.2
), offline-online decomposition (
Section

5.1.3
) and
construction of the reduced spaces (
Section

5.1.4
).
After a Stage 1 ROM is constructed, all associated fine-mesh data can be deleted.
In particular, all computations in Stage 2 are independent from size of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

We emphasize again that, compared to
[
6
]
, the Stage 2 TSRBLOD ROM is not only
independent from
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, but also from the number of coarse elements. Its size only depends
on the number of selected basis vectors in
Section

5.2.4
.
Further, in contrast to
[
6
]
, the derived error estimator fully takes the effect of the errors of
the reduced corrector problems on the resulting global solution into account and, thus,
rigorously bounds the error of the ROM w.r.t. the LOD solution.

5.1
Stage 1: Computing RB approximations of
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}
and
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}

5.1.1
Definition of the reduced order model

Let
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
be fixed,
and let
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}\subset V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
be an approximation space for the correctors
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
of
v_{H}

for arbitrary
v_{H}\in V_{H}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
.
Then, for given
v_{H}\in V_{H}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, we determine an approximate corrector
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})\in V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
via Galerkin projection onto
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
as the solution of

(20)

a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}),v^{\text{f}}_{T})=a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T})\qquad\forall v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}.

Note that
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
is well-defined since
a_{\mu}
is a coercive bilinear form.
Using these reduced correction operators we can define an approximate localized multiscale matrix

\mathbb{K}_{\mu}^{rb}
given by

\mathbb{K}_{\mu}^{rb}:=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb},\quad\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb}\right)_{ji}:=(A_{\mu}(\chi_{T}\nabla-\nabla{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu})\phi_{i}\,,\,\nabla\phi_{j})_{U_{k}(T)}.

5.1.2
Error estimation

We employ standard RB tools for the a posteriori error estimation of the reduced system (
20
).
In detail, we use the residual-norm based estimate:

(21)

\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu}\leq\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))\leq\kappa^{1/2}\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})\rVert_{a,\mu},

where

\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})):=\alpha^{-1/2}\sup_{v^{\text{f}}_{T}\in V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}\frac{a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}),v^{\text{f}}_{T})}{\lVert v^{\text{f}}_{T}\rVert_{1}}.

The bounds in (
21
) easily follow from the definition
of
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
and the equivalence of
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{a,\mu}
and
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{1}
.

5.1.3
Offline-Online Decomposition

In order to compute
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
and subsequently
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb}
, let
N_{T}:=\dim V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
, and choose a basis

\varphi_{T,n}
,
1\leq n\leq N_{T}
, of
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
.
Expanding
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
w.r.t. this basis as

{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}):=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}c_{n}\cdot\varphi_{T,n},

the coefficient vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{T}}
is given as the solution of the
N_{T}\times N_{T}
-dimensional linear system

(22)

\mathbb{A}^{T}_{\mu}\cdot c=\mathbb{G}^{T}_{\mu}(v_{H}),

where

(\mathbb{A}^{T}_{\mu})_{m,n}:=a_{\mu}(\varphi_{T,n},\varphi_{T,m})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{G}^{T}_{\mu}(v_{H})_{m}:=a^{T}_{\mu}(v_{H},\varphi_{T,m}).

While (
22
) can be solved quickly when
N_{T}
is sufficiently small, we still need to
re-assemble this equation system for each new parameter
\mu
and coarse-scale function
v_{H}
.
This requires time and memory that scales with
\dim V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
.
To avoid these high-dimensional computations we exploit (
4
) and pre-assemble
matrices and vectors

(\mathbb{A}^{T}_{q})_{m,n}:=a_{q}(\varphi_{T,n},\varphi_{T,m})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\mathbb{G}^{T}_{q,j})_{m}:=a^{T}_{q}(\phi_{i_{T,j}},\varphi_{T,m}),

for
1\leq q\leq Q
and
1\leq j\leq J_{T}
, where
J_{T}

is the number of finite-element basis functions
\phi_{i}
of
V_{H}
with support containing
T
and
i_{T,1},\ldots,i_{T,J_{T}}
is an enumeration of these basis functions.
Then,
\mathbb{A}^{T}_{\mu}
and
\mathbb{G}^{T}_{\mu}(v_{H})
can be determined as

\mathbb{A}^{T}_{\mu}:=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\mathbb{A}^{T}_{q}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{G}^{T}_{\mu}(v_{H}):=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{T}}\theta_{q}(\mu)\lambda_{i_{T,j}}(v_{H})\mathbb{G}^{T}_{q,j},

where by
\lambda_{i}\in V_{H}^{\prime}
we denote the dual basis of
\phi_{i}
.

To compute
\mathbb{K}_{\mu}^{rb}
, we further store the matrices

\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{0}\right)_{j,i}:=(A_{q}\chi_{T}\nabla\phi_{i}\,,\,\nabla\phi_{j})_{U_{k}(T)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{rb}\right)_{j,n}:=(A_{q}\nabla\varphi_{T,n}\,,\,\nabla\phi_{j})_{U_{k}(T)}.

Then we have:

\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb}\right)_{j,i}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\left[\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{0}\right)_{j,i}-\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}}c^{i}_{n}(\mu)\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{rb}\right)_{j,n}\right],

where
c^{i}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{T}}
is given as the solution of

\mathbb{A}^{T}_{\mu}\cdot c^{i}(\mu)=\mathbb{G}^{T}_{\mu}(\phi_{i}).

Note that
(\mathbb{K}^{T}_{q,0})_{j,i}
,
(\mathbb{K}^{T}_{q})_{j,n}
and
c^{i}(\mu)
are zero unless the support of
\phi_{i}

is non-disjoint from
T
and the support of
\phi_{j}
is non-disjoint from
U_{k}(T)
.
In particular, only
J_{T}
reduced problems have to be solved in order to determine
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb}
.
The total computational effort for solving these problems is of order
\mathcal{O}(QN_{T}^{2}+N_{T}^{3}+J_{T}N_{T}^{2})
, where
the first term corresponds to the assembly of
\mathbb{A}_{\mu}^{T}
, the second term to its LU decomposition and third term
to the solution of the
J_{T}
linear systems using forward/backward substitution.

Finally, to efficiently evaluate
\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))
, first note that

\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))=\alpha^{-1/2}\lVert\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}(a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},\cdot)-a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}),\cdot))\rVert_{1},

where
\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}:(V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T})^{\prime}\to V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
denotes the Riesz isomorphism.

Following
[
12
]
, let
W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
denote the
M_{T}
-dimensional linear subspace of
V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}
that is
spanned by the vectors

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}(a_{q}(\varphi_{T,n},\cdot))\,\middle|\,1\leq q\leq Q,1\leq n\leq N_{T}\right\}\,\cup\,\hskip-200.0003pt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left\{\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}(a_{q}^{T}(\phi_{i_{T,j}},\cdot))\,\middle|\,1\leq q\leq Q,1\leq j\leq J_{T}\right\}.

Choose an
H^{1}
-orthonormal basis
\psi_{T,m}
of
W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
.
Since
\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}(a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},\cdot)-a_{\mu}(u_{N},\cdot))
lies in
W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
for each
u_{N}\in V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
, we
obtain

\displaystyle\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1/2}\left\lVert\left[\left(\mathcal{R}_{V^{\text{f}}_{h,k,T}}(a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},\cdot)-a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}),\cdot)),\psi_{T,m}\right)_{1}\right]^{M_{T}}_{m=1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1/2}\left\lVert\left[a_{\mu}^{T}(v_{H},\psi_{T,m})-a_{\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}),\psi_{T,m})\right]^{M_{T}}_{m=1}\right\rVert.

Hence, defining the
M_{T}
-dimensional vectors and
M_{T}\times N_{T}
matrices

(23)

(\hat{\mathbb{G}}^{T}_{q,j})_{m}:=a^{T}_{q}(\phi_{i_{T,j}},\psi_{T,m})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\hat{\mathbb{A}}^{T}_{q})_{m,n}:=a_{q}(\varphi_{T,n},\psi_{T,m}),

we obtain

(24)

\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))=\alpha^{-1/2}\left\lVert\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J_{T}}\lambda_{i_{T,j}}(v_{H})\hat{\mathbb{G}}^{T}_{q,j}-\hat{\mathbb{A}}^{T}_{q}\cdot c\right)\right\rVert,

where
c
is again given by (
22
).
We remark that
M_{T}
can be bounded by
Q(N_{T}+J_{T})
.
Hence, the cost of evaluating (
24
) is of order

\mathcal{O}(Q^{2}(N_{T}+J_{T})^{2})
.

5.1.4
Basis generation

To build the reduced spaces
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
, we use a standard weak greedy
[
10
]
approach in order to
minimize the model order reduction error
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})-{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H})
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
and
v_{H}\in V_{H}
.
To this end, we choose a target error tolerance
\varepsilon_{1}
and an appropriate training set of parameters

\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}
, over which we estimate the maximum reduction error.
The initial reduced space
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
is chosen as the zero-dimensional space.
Then, in each iteration, the reduction error is estimated with
\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(v_{H}))
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}
and
\phi_{i_{T,j}}
, and a pair
\mu^{*}
,
\phi_{i_{T,j^{*}}}
maximising the estimate is selected.
Thanks to the offline-online decomposition of
\eta_{T,\mu}
this step does not involve any high-dimensional
computations, so
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}
can be chosen large.
Since (
20
) is linear, it suffices to consider the basis functions
\phi_{i_{T,j}}
as
v_{H}
.
After
\mu^{*}
,
j^{*}
have been found,
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu^{*}}(\phi_{i_{T,j^{*}}})
is computed.

V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
is extended with this solution
snapshot
, and the offline-online decompostion for this extended reduced
space is computed.
The iteration ends, when the maximum estimated error drops below
\varepsilon_{1}
.
A formal definition of the procedure is given in Algorithm
1
.

Data:

T
,
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}
,
\varepsilon_{1}

Result:

V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}

V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}\leftarrow\{0\}
;

1

while

\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}}\max_{1\leq j\leq J_{T}}\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(\phi_{i_{T},j}))>\varepsilon_{1}

do

2

(\mu^{*},j^{*})\leftarrow\argmax_{(\mu^{*},j^{*})\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}\times\{1,\ldots,J_{T}\}}\eta_{T,\mu}({\mathcal{Q}}^{T,rb}_{k,\mu}(\phi_{i_{T},j}))
;

3

V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}\leftarrow\Span(V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}\cup\{{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu^{*}}(\phi_{i_{T,j^{*}}})\})
;

4

end while

5.2
Stage 2: Computing RB approximations of
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}

5.2.1
Definition of the reduced order model

In order to find an approximate solution of
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}
, we assume to be given an appropriate reduced subspace
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
of

\mathfrak{V}
.
As we have proven inf-sup stability of
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
in
Proposition

4.6
, and since the inf-sup stability is
preserved by restricting
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
to a linear subspace, we can define the reduced two-scale solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}^{rb}
as the
unique solution of the residual minimization problem

(25)

\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}^{rb}:=\argmin_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{V}^{rb}}\sup_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathfrak{V}}\frac{\mathfrak{F}(\mathfrak{v})-\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})}{\vvvert\mathfrak{v}\vvvert_{1}}.

As a direct consequence of
Theorem

4.8
,
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}^{rb}
is a quasi best-approximation of
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}
within
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
.

5.2.2
Error estimation

We have already defined a posteriori error estimators for approximations of the two-scale solution
\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}

in
Section

4.3
.
In
Theorem

4.7
we have shown that these estimators yield efficient upper bounds for the approximation errors
in the two-scale energy norm as well as in the Sobolev 1-norm.
Note that even though we will use the Stage 1 approximations
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}^{rb}
of
\mathbb{K}_{T,\mu}
to build the reduced space
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
,
the derived error estimates are with respect to the true LOD solution and take these approximation errors into account.

5.2.3
Offline-Online Decomposition

For the offline-online decomposition of (
25
), we proceed similar to the decomposition of the Stage 1
error estimator
\eta_{T,\mu}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{V}}:\mathfrak{V}^{\prime}\to\mathfrak{V}
the Riesz isomorphism for
\mathfrak{V}
.
Then (
25
) is equivalent to solving

(26)

\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}^{rb}:=\argmin_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{V}^{rb}}\vvvert\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{V}}(\mathfrak{F})-\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{V}}(\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}(\mathfrak{u},\cdot))\vvvert_{1}^{2}.

Let
N:=\dim\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
, and let
\mathfrak{b}_{n}
,
1\leq n\leq N
be a basis of
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
.
We again construct an
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1}
-orthonormal basis
\mathfrak{c}_{m}
for the
M
-dimensional subspace
\mathfrak{W}^{rb}
of
\mathfrak{V}

spanned by the vectors

(27)

\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{V}}(\mathfrak{F})\}\cup\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{V}}(\mathfrak{B}_{q}(\mathfrak{b}_{n},\cdot))\,|\,1\leq n\leq N,1\leq q\leq Q\},

where

\begin{split}\mathfrak{B}_{q}\left((u_{H},u^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,u^{\text{f}}_{T_{{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}),(v_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T_{1}},\dots,v^{\text{f}}_{T_{{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}})\right)&:=\\
a_{q}(u_{H}-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}u^{\text{f}}_{T},v_{H})+&\rho^{1/2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}a_{q}(u^{\text{f}}_{T},v^{\text{f}}_{T})-a_{q}^{T}(u_{H},v^{\text{f}}_{T}),\end{split}

such that
\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}
has the decomposition:

\mathfrak{B}_{\mu}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\mathfrak{B}_{q}.

Using these bases, we define matrices
\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
and the vector

\hat{\mathbb{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
by

(\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q})_{m,n}:=\mathfrak{B}_{q}(\mathfrak{b}_{n},\mathfrak{c}_{m})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\mathbb{F}}_{m}:=\mathfrak{F}(\mathfrak{c}_{m}).

Then, with

\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{\mu}:=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\theta_{q}(\mu)\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q},

solving (
26
) is equivalent to solving the least-squares problem

(28)

c(\mu):=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lVert\hat{\mathbb{F}}-\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{\mu}\cdot c\rVert^{2},

where

\mathfrak{u}_{\mu}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}(\mu)\mathfrak{b}_{n}
.
In the same way we can evaluate the error bounds
\eta_{a,\mu}(\mathfrak{u}_{\mu})
and
\eta_{1,\mu}(\mathfrak{u})
as

\displaystyle\eta_{a,\mu}(\mathfrak{u}_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{5}\gamma_{k}^{-1}\lVert\hat{\mathbb{F}}-\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{\mu}\cdot c(\mu)\rVert,

\displaystyle\eta_{1,\mu}(\mathfrak{u}_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{5}C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}\alpha^{-1/2}\gamma_{k}^{-1}\lVert\hat{\mathbb{F}}-\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{\mu}\cdot c(\mu)\rVert.

Since
M\leq QN+1
, the computational effort for assembling the least-squares system is of order

\mathcal{O}(Q^{2}N^{2})
. Solving the system requires
\mathcal{O}(QN^{3})
operations, and
evaluating the estimators requires
\mathcal{O}(Q^{2}N^{2})
operations.
In particular, the computational effort is completely independent from
h
and
H
.

We still need to show how the matrices
\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q}
and the vector
\hat{\mathbb{F}}
can be computed after Stage 1
without using any data or operations associated with the fine mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
To this end, we assume that
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}\subseteq V_{H}\oplus V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}\subset\mathfrak{V}
.
By construction of the
W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
, we see that for such a
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
,
\mathfrak{W}^{rb}
is a linear subpsace of
V_{H}\oplus W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T_{1}}\oplus\dots W^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}
.
Choose a basis
\mathfrak{b}_{n}
of
\mathfrak{V}^{rb}
with coefficient vectors

\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n}=(\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,H},\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,T_{1}},\dots,\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}})\in\mathbb{R}^{{N_{H}}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{N_{T_{1}}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{R}^{N_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}

w.r.t. the finite element basis
\phi_{i}
of
V_{H}
and the reduced bases
\varphi_{T,n}
of
V^{\text{f},rb}_{k,T}
.
Denote by
\mathbb{S}
the
{N_{H}}\times{N_{H}}
matrix of the Sobolev 1-inner product on
V_{H}
given by

\mathbb{S}_{j,i}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{dx},

and use the
H^{1}
-orthonormal bases
\psi_{T,m}
to isometrically represent the
vectors (
27
) as coefficient vectors in the
direct sum Hilbert space
\underline{\mathfrak{W}}^{rb}:=\mathbb{R}^{{N_{H}}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{M_{T_{1}}}\oplus\dots\mathbb{R}^{M_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}
equipped with the
\mathbb{S}
-inner product in the first and with the Euclidean inner products in the
remaining components.
Checking the definitions of
\mathfrak{B}_{q}
,
\mathfrak{F}
,
\hat{\mathbb{A}}^{T}_{q}
,
\hat{\mathbb{G}}^{T}_{q,j}
,
\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{0}
and
\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{rb}
, we obain the
vectors

(29)

(\mathbb{S}^{-1}\cdot[F(\phi_{i})]_{i},0\dots,0),

and

(30)

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{S}^{-1}\cdot\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}}\left(\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{0}\cdot\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,H}-\mathbb{K}_{T,q}^{rb}\cdot\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,T}\right)\\
\rho^{1/2}\hat{\mathbb{A}}^{T_{1}}_{q}\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,T_{1}}-\rho^{1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{T_{1}}}\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,H,i_{T_{1},j}}\hat{\mathbb{G}}^{T_{1}}_{q,j}\\
\vdots\\
\rho^{1/2}\hat{\mathbb{A}}^{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}_{q}\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}-\rho^{1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}\underline{\mathfrak{b}}_{n,H,i_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|},j}}\hat{\mathbb{G}}^{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}_{q,j}\end{pmatrix},

for
1\leq n\leq N
.
After having computed (
29
) and (
30
), we compute a

\underline{\mathfrak{W}}^{rb}
-orthonormal basis
\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m}
for these vectors with coefficients

\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m}=(\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,H},\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,T_{1}},\dots,\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}),

such that

\mathfrak{c}_{m}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{H}}}\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,H,i}\cdot\phi_{i},\sum_{l=1}^{M_{T_{1}}}\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,T_{1},l}\cdot\psi_{T,l},\dots,\sum_{l=1}^{M_{T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|}}}\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,T_{|\mathcal{T}_{H}|},l}\cdot\psi_{T,l})

is an
\vvvert\>\cdot\>\vvvert_{1}
-orthonormal orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{W}^{rb}
.

Finally, following the definitions again, we see that
\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q}
and
\hat{\mathbb{F}}
can be computed as

\hat{\mathbb{F}}_{m}=\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{c}_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{{N_{H}}}\underline{\mathfrak{c}}_{m,H,i}\cdot F(\phi_{i}),

and

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathbb{A}}_{q})_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{B}_{q}(\mathfrak{b}_{n},\mathfrak{c}_{m})

In particular, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{1}

6
Numerical experiments

In this section we apply the above described method to two test cases and evaluate its efficiency.
For the first, smaller problem we mainly investigate the MOR error and
the performance of the certified error estimator.
The second, large-scale problem will be used to assess the computational speed-up achieved by the TSRBLOD.

In both cases, we use structured 2D-grids
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
\mathcal{T}_{H}
with quadrilateral elements on the domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{2}
.
We specify the number of elements by
n_{h}\times n_{h}
and
n_{H}\times n_{H}
respectively.
We use the interpolation operator from
[
29
, Example 3.1]

and choose the oversampling parameter
k
as the first integer to satisfy
k>|\log(H)|
.
For the evaluation of the error estimator
\eta_{a,\mu}
(
14
) we
approximate
\gamma_{k}\approx\alpha^{1/2}C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}^{-1}
and assume
C_{\mathcal{I}_{H}}\approx 1
. For quadrilateral elements, the overlapping constant can be explicitly computed
by
C_{k,\text{ovl}}=(2k+1)^{2}
.
Moreover, we approximate
\alpha
and the contrast
\kappa
by replacing
\mathcal{P}
in
Eq.

2
by the training set

\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{train}}
.
Thus, while it is not guaranteed that our approximations yield strict upper bounds on the model order reduction errror, the decay
rate and choice of snapshot parameters will not be affected.

We use an
MPI
distributed implementation to benefit from parallelization of the localized corrector problems,
which gives significant speed-ups for all considered methods.
All our computations have been performed on an HPC cluster with 1024 parallel processes.

Petrov–Galerkin variant of the RBLOD method

In order to compare our method to the RBLOD approach introduced in
[
6
]
,
we have implemented a corresponding version of the RBLOD that is applicable to our LOD formulation.
In particular, we use the interpolation operator from
[
29
, Example 3.1]
and Petrov-Galerkin
projection (
7
) in contrast to the Clément interpolation and Galerkin projection used in
[
6
]
.
Further, in
[
6
]
individual ROMs for the local correctors
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T}_{k,\mu}(\phi_{i})
are constructed, where the snapshot parameters are chosen identically among all
{\mathcal{Q}}^{T^{\prime}}_{k,\mu}(\phi_{i})
,
T^{\prime}\subset\operatorname{supp}\psi_{z}
.
In the RBLOD variant implemented by us, we independently train the corrector ROMS for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
as is done in Stage 1 of the TSRBLOD.
We note that for both RBLOD variants, the number of ROMs constructed for each coarse element
T
is equal to the number of coarse-mesh basis functions
\phi_{i}
supported on this element, whereas Stage 1 of the TSRBLOD constructs a single ROM to approximate all local correctors.

Error measures

To quantify the accuracy of the TSRBLOD, we use a validation set
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}\subset\mathcal{P}
of
10
random
parameters and compute the maximum relative approximation errors w.r.t. the PG–LOD
solutions for this set, i.e.,

\displaystyle e^{*,\text{rel}}_{\text{LOD}}:=\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}}\frac{\|u_{H,k,\mu}-\tilde{u}_{\mu}\|_{*}}{\|u_{H,k,\mu}\|_{*}},

where
\tilde{u}_{\mu}
denotes the coarse-scale component of either the
RBLOD or TSRBLOD solution,
u_{H,k,\mu}
denotes the coarse component of the PG–LOD solution (
7
), and
∗
stands for either the
H^{1}
- or
L^{2}
-norm. With the solution of the FEM approximation
u_{h,\mu}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we let

\displaystyle e^{L^{2},\text{rel}}_{\text{FEM}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}}\frac{\|u_{h,\mu}-\tilde{u}_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}}{\|u_{h,\mu}\|_{L^{2}}},\qquad

\displaystyle e^{L^{2},\text{rel}}_{\text{LOD-FEM}}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}}\frac{\|u_{h,\mu}-u_{H,k,\mu}\|_{L^{2}}}{\|u_{h,\mu}\|_{L^{2}}}.

Time measures

For an analysis of the computational wall times, we consider the following quantities:

•

t^{\text{offline}}_{1,\text{av}}(T)
: Average (arithmetic mean) time for creating a ROM for the localized corrector problem(s)
corresponding to a single coarse element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
as discussed in
Section

5.1
.
For the RBLOD , this involves all individual corrector problems for the four basis functions that are supported on

T
.

•

t^{\text{offline}}_{1}
: Total time required to build the corrector ROMs. In case of full parallelization, this
is equal to the maximum of the Stage 1 times over all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
.

•

t^{\text{offline}}_{2}
: Time for creating the Stage 2 TSRBLOD ROM as discussed in
Section

5.2
.

•

t^{\text{offline}}

t^{\text{offline}}_{1}
. For the TSRBLOD this additionally includes
t^{\text{offline}}_{2}
.

•

t^{\text{online}}
: Average time for solving the obtained ROM for a single new
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}
.
In case of the RBLOD, the reduced corrector problems are solved sequentially on a single compute node.

•

t^{\text{LOD}}
: Average time needed to compute the PG–LOD with parallelization of the corrector problems
for a single new
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{val}}
.

7
Concluding remarks and future work

In the work at hand, we have derived a new two-scale reduced scheme for the PG–LOD.
Due to the two-stage reduction process and the independence of the resulting ROMs from the size of both the fine-scale
and the corse-scale meshes, our approach is effective even for large-scale problems.
For ease of presentation, we have assumed non-parametric right-hand sides and did not cosider output functionals.
Incorporating both into our approach is straightforward.
Furthermore, for very large coarse meshes, additional intermediate reduction stages could be added to further
reduce the needed computational effort in Stage 2.
Instead of a fixed a priori choice of the Stage 1 tolerance
\varepsilon_{1}
, the Stage 1 ROMs could be adaptively enriched
during Stage 2 when an insufficient approximation quality of some of the Stage 1 ROMs is detected.
The presented methodology could also be applied to other problem classes and to other multiscale methods.
