Abstract
We study the problem of learning linear temporal logic (LTL) formulas from examples, as a first step towards expressing a property separating positive and negative instances in a way that is comprehensible for humans. In this paper we initiate the study of the computational complexity of the problem. Our main results are hardness results: we show that the LTL learning problem is 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete, both for the full logic and for almost all of its fragments. This motivates the search for efficient heuristics, and highlights the complexity of expressing separating properties in concise natural language.

Introduction

We are interested in the complexity of learning formulas of Linear Temporal Logic (
LTL
) from examples,
in a passive scenario: from a set of positive and negative words, the objective is to construct a formula, as small as possible,
which satisfies the positive words and does not satisfy the negative words.
Passive learning of languages has a long history paved with negative results.
Learning automata is notoriously difficult from a theoretical perspective,
as witnessed by the original
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness result of learning a Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) from examples (?).
This line of hardness results culminates with the inapproximability result of (?) stating that
there is no polynomial time algorithm for learning a DFA from examples even up to a polynomial approximation of their size.

One approach to cope with such hardness results is to change representation, for instance replacing automata by logical formulas;
their syntactic structures make them more amenable to principled search algorithms.
There is a range of potential logical formalisms to choose from depending on the application domain.
Linear Temporal Logic (?), which we abbreviate as
LTL
, is a prominent logic for specifying temporal properties over words,
it has become a de facto standard in many fields such as model checking, program analysis, and motion planning for robotics.
Verification of
LTL
specifications is routinely employed in industrial settings and marks one of the most successful applications of formal methods to real-life problems.
A key property making
LTL
a strong candidate as a concept class is that its syntax does not include variables,
contributing to the fact that
LTL
formulas are typically easy to interpret and therefore useful as explanations.

Over the past five to ten years learning temporal logics (of which
LTL
is the core) has become an active research area.
There are many applications, let us only mention a few: program specification (?) and fault detections (?). We refer to (?) for a longer discussion on the potential and actual applications of learning temporal logics.

Since learning temporal logics is a computationally hard problem, a number of different approaches have been explored.
One of the first and probably most natural is leveraging SAT solvers (?), which can then accommodate noisy data (?).
Another line of work relies on their connections to automata (?), and a third completely different idea approaches it from the lens of Bayesian inference (?). Learning specifically tailored fragments of
LTL
yields the best results in practice (?).
There are a number of temporal logics, and the ideas mentioned above have been extended to more expressive logics such as Property Specification Language (PSL) (?, ?), Computational Tree Logic (CTL) (?), and Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) (?). Other paradigms have been explored to make learning temporal logics useful, such as sketching (?).

Despite this growing interest, very little is known about the computational complexity of the underlying problem;
indeed the works cited above focused on constructing efficient algorithms for practical applications.
The goal of this paper is to initiate the study of the complexity of learning
LTL
formulas from examples.

Our contributions.

We present a set of results for several fragments of
LTL
, showing in almost all cases that the learning problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.
Section
2
gives definitions.

•

Our first
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness result is presented in Section
3
, it states that the learning problem for full
LTL
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard when the alphabet size is part of the input.

•

To obtain membership in
{\mathsf{NP}}
for the learning problem, we show in Section
4
that all fragments of
LTL
have the short formula property. We then study some (degenerate) fragments in Section
5
and show that for these fragments the
LTL
learning problem is in polynomial time.

•

We construct in Section
6
a polynomial-time approximation algorithm for
LTL
with only the next operator and conjunctions, and show that assuming
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{NP}}
, the approximation ratio of this algorithm is optimal.

•

Our most technical results are presented in Section
7
and Section
8
: in the first section we show that almost all fragments with the next operator are hard to approximate, and in the next a similar result for almost all fragments without the next operator.

We conclude in Section
9
.

2
Preliminaries

Let us fix a (finite) alphabet
\Sigma
.
We index words from position
1
(not
0
) and the letter at position
i
in the word
w
is written
w(i)
,
so
w=w(1)\dots w(\ell)
where
\ell
is the length of
w
, written
|w|=\ell
.
We write
w[k\dots]=w(k)\dots w(\ell)
.
To avoid unnecessary technical complications we only consider non-empty words, and let
\Sigma^{+}
denote the set of (non-empty) words.

The syntax of Linear Temporal Logic (
LTL
) includes atomic formulas
c\in\Sigma
and their negations, as well as
\top
and
\bot
, the boolean operators
\wedge
and
\vee
, and the temporal operators
\textbf{X},\textbf{F}
,
G
, and
U
.
Note that as usually done, we work with
LTL
in negation normal form, meaning that negation is only used on atomic formulas.
The semantic of
LTL
over finite words is defined inductively over formulas,
through the notation
w\models\varphi
where
w\in\Sigma^{+}
is a non-empty word,
and
\varphi
is an
LTL
formula.
The definition is given below for the atomic formulas and temporal operators
\textbf{U},\textbf{F},\textbf{G}
, and
X
, with boolean operators interpreted as usual.

•

We have
w\models\top
and
w\not\models\bot
.

•

w\models c
if
w(1)=c
.

•

w\models\textbf{X}\varphi
if
|w|>1
and
w[2\dots]\models\varphi
. It is called the next operator.

•

w\models\textbf{F}\varphi
if
w[i\dots]\models\varphi
for some
i\in[1,|w|]
. It is called the eventually operator.

•

w\models\textbf{G}\varphi
if
w[i\dots]\models\varphi
for all
i\in[1,|w|]
. It is called the globally operator.

•

w\models\varphi\textbf{U}\psi
if there exists
i\in[1,|w|]
such that for
j\in[1,i-1]
we have
w[j\dots]\models\varphi
,
and
w[i\dots]\models\psi
. It is called the until operator.

Note that
\textbf{F}\varphi
is syntactic sugar for
\top\textbf{U}\varphi
.
We say that
w
satisfies
\varphi
when
w\models\varphi
is true.
It is sometimes useful to write
w,i\models\varphi
to mean
w[i\dots]\models\varphi
.
We consider fragments of
LTL
by specifying which boolean connectives and temporal operators are allowed.
For instance
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\wedge)
is the set of all
LTL
formulas using only atomic formulas, conjunctions, and the next operator.
The full logic is
\textbf{LTL}=\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{U},\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land,\lor)
.
More generally, for a set of operators
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{U},\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land,\lor\right\}
, we write
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
for the logic using operators from
Op
.
The size of a formula is the size of its syntactic tree.
We say that two formulas are equivalent if they have the same semantics, and we write
\varphi\equiv\psi
to say that
\varphi
and
\psi
are equivalent.

The
LTL
learning problem.

A sample is a pair
(P,N)
where
P=\left\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\right\}
is a set of positive words and
N=\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\right\}
a set of negative words.
Without loss of generality we can assume that
n=m
(adding duplicate identical words to have an equal number of positive and negative words).
The
LTL
learning decision problem is:

INPUT
:

a sample
(P,N)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

QUESTION
:

does there exist an
LTL
formula
\varphi
of size at most
k

such that for all
u\in P
, we have
u\models\varphi
,

and for all
v\in N
, we have
v\not\models\varphi
?

In that case we say that
\varphi
separates
P
from
N
,
or simply that
\varphi
is a separating formula if the sample is clear from the context.
The
LTL
learning problem is analogously defined for any fragment of
LTL
.

Parameters for complexity analysis.

The three important parameters for the complexity of the
LTL
learning problem are:

n
the number of words,
\ell
the maximum length of the words, and
k
the desired size for the formula.
As we will see, another important parameter is the size of the alphabet. We will consider two settings: either the alphabet
\Sigma
is fixed, or it is part of the input. The size of the alphabet is
|\Sigma|
, the number of letters.

Representation.

The words given as input are represented in a natural way: we work with the RAM model with word size
\log(|\Sigma|+n+\ell)
, which allows us to write a letter in each cell and to manipulate words and positions in a natural way.
We write
|P|
for
\sum_{j\in[1,n]}|u_{j}|
and similarly
N=\sum_{j\in[1,n]}|v_{j}|
. The size of a sample is
|P|+|N|
.

We emphasise a subtlety on the representation of
k
: it can be given in binary (a standard assumption) or in unary.
In the first case, the input size is
O(n\cdot\ell+\log(k))
, so the formula
\varphi
we are looking for may be exponential in the input size!
This means that it is not clear a priori that the
LTL
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
. Opting for a unary encoding, the input size becomes
O(n\cdot\ell+k)
, and in that case an easy argument shows that the
LTL
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
.
We follow the standard representation:
k
is given in binary, and therefore it is not immediate that the
LTL
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
.

A naive algorithm.

Let us start our complexity analysis of the learning
LTL
problem by constructing a naive algorithm for the whole logic.

Theorem 1
.

There exists an algorithm for solving the
LTL
learning problem in time and space
O(|\Sigma|+\text{exp}(k)\cdot n\cdot\ell)
,
where
\text{exp}(k)
is exponential in
k
.

Notice that the dependence of the algorithm presented in Theorem
1
is linear in
n
and
\ell
,
and it is exponential only in
k
, but since
k
is represented in binary this is a doubly-exponential algorithm.

Proof.

For a formula
\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}
, we write
\langle\varphi\rangle:P\cup N\to\left\{0,1\right\}^{\ell}
for the function defined by

\langle\varphi\rangle(w)(i)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }w[i\dots]\models\varphi,\\
0&\text{ if }w[i\dots]\not\models\varphi,\end{cases}

for
w\in P\cup N
.

Note that
\varphi
is separating if and only if
\langle\varphi\rangle(u)(1)=1
and
\langle\varphi\rangle(v)(1)=0
for all
u\in P,v\in N
.
The algorithm simply consists in enumerating all formulas
\varphi
of
LTL
of size at most
k
inductively,
constructing
\langle\varphi\rangle
, and checking whether
\varphi
is separating.
Initially, we construct
\langle a\rangle
for all
a\in\Sigma
, and then once we have computed

\langle\varphi\rangle
and
\langle\psi\rangle
, we can compute
\langle\varphi\textbf{U}\psi\rangle
,
\langle\textbf{F}\varphi\rangle
,
\langle\textbf{G}\varphi\rangle
,
\langle\textbf{X}\varphi\rangle
,
\langle\varphi\wedge\psi\rangle
, and
\langle\varphi\vee\psi\rangle
in time
O(n\cdot\ell)
.
To conclude, we note that the number of formulas of
LTL
of size at most
k
is exponential in
k
.
∎

Subwords

Let
u=u(1)\dots u(\ell)
and
w=w(1)\dots w(\ell^{\prime})
. We say that

•

u
is a
subword
of
w
if there exist
p_{1}<\dots<p_{\ell}
such that
u(i)=w(p_{i})
for all
i\in[1,\ell]
.

•

u
is a
weak subword
of
w
if there exist
p_{1}\leq\dots\leq p_{\ell}
such that
u(i)=w(p_{i})
for all
i\in[1,\ell]
.

For instance,
abba
is a subword of
b\textbf{ab}aaaa\textbf{b}\textbf{a}
,
and
bba
is a weak subword of
a\textbf{ba}a
(using the
b
twice).
We say that a word is non-repeating if every two consecutive letters are different.
If
u
is non-repeating, then
u
is a weak subword of
w
if and only if it is a subword of
w
.

As a warm-up, let us construct simple
LTL
formulas related to subwords.

•

We consider the word
u=u(1)\dots u(\ell)
and
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{\ell}
.
They induce the following
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\land)
formula, called a
pattern
:

\mathcal{P}=\textbf{X}^{p_{1}-1}(u(1)\wedge\textbf{X}^{p_{2}-i_{1}}(\cdots\wedge\textbf{X}^{p_{\ell}-p_{\ell-1}}u(\ell))\cdots).

It is equivalent to the (larger in size) formula
\bigwedge_{i\in[1,\ell]}\textbf{X}^{p_{i}-1}u(i)
, which states that
for each
i\in[1,\ell]
, the letter in position
p_{i}
is
u(i)
.

•

We consider the word
u=u(1)\dots u(\ell)
.
It induces the following
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\land)
formula, called a
fattern
(pattern with an
F
):

\mathcal{F}=\textbf{F}(u(1)\wedge\textbf{F}(\cdots\wedge\textbf{F}u(\ell))\cdots).

A word
w
satisfies
\mathcal{F}
if and only if
u
is a weak subword of
w
.

3
NP-hardness when the alphabet is part of the input

We prove our first hardness result: the learning problem for
LTL
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard for non-constant alphabets for all fragments including
F
and
\lor
, so in particular for full
LTL
.

Theorem 2
.

For all
\left\{\textbf{F},\lor\right\}\subseteq Op
, the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard when the alphabet is part of the input.

Recall that the hitting set problem takes as input
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
subsets of
[1,\ell]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and asks whether there exists
H\subseteq[1,\ell]
of size at most
k
such that for every
j\in[1,n]
we have
H\cap C_{j}\neq\emptyset
.
It is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

We construct a reduction from the hitting set problem.
Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}\subseteq[1,\ell]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
an instance of the hitting set problem.
Let us define the alphabet
\Sigma=\left\{a_{j},b_{j}:j\in[1,n]\right\}
, it has size
2n
.
For
j\in[1,n]
, we define
u_{j}
of length
\ell
by

u_{j}(i)=\begin{cases}a_{j}\text{ if }i\in C_{j},\\
b_{j}\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
v=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}
.
We claim that there exists a hitting set
H
of size at most
k
if and only if there exists a separating formula of
LTL
of size at most
2k
.

Given a hitting set
H
of size
k
, we construct the formula
\textbf{F}(\bigvee_{j\in H}a_{j})
, it is separating by definition of
H
being a hitting set, and it has size
2k
.
Conversely, let us consider a separating formula
\varphi
of size
2k
.
Since all operators have arity one or two, the syntactic tree of
\varphi
contains at most
k
leaves, so the set

H=\left\{j\in[1,\ell]:a_{j}\text{ appears in }\varphi\right\}
has size at most
k
.
Suppose
H
is not a hitting set of
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, there exists
j\in[1,n]
such that
H\cap C_{j}=\emptyset
.
We prove that for all subformulas
\psi
of
\varphi
and for all
i\in[1,\ell]
, if
u_{j}[i\dots]\vDash\psi
then
v[j\dots]\vDash\psi
.
We proceed by induction.

•

If
\psi
is a letter. If
u_{j}[i\dots]\vDash\psi
, this implies that
u_{j}(i)=b_{j}
because
a_{j}
cannot appear in both
\psi
and
u_{j}
since
H\cap C_{j}=\emptyset
. Remark that
v(i)=b_{i}
, so
v[i\dots]\vDash\psi
.

•

The other cases are easily proved by applying directly the induction hypothesis.

In particular, since
u_{j}
satisfies
\varphi
, then so does
v
, a contradiction with
\varphi
being separating.
∎

This could be the end of our study! However the assumption that the alphabet is part of the input is very unusual, and in the rest of the paper we will therefore fix the alphabet size. In the remainder of the paper, we will consider only fragments of
LTL
without the until operator.
In other words, when consider
Op
a set of operators, we assume that
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land,\lor\right\}
.

4
Membership in NP: the short formula property

Definition 1
.

Let
Op
a set of operator, we say that
Op
has the
short formula property
if
there exists a polynomial
P
such that
for all samples
(P,N)
, if there exists an
LTL
(Op) separating formula, then there exists one of size at most

\textbf{P}(|P|+|N|)
.

Note that if
Op
has the short formula property then the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
.

Theorem 3
.

All
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land,\lor\right\}
have the short formula property,
and therefore the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{NP}}
.

This theorem is the consequence of the following propositions, which establish it for various subsets of operators.
We start with some technical lemmas.
The following lemma is easily proved by induction, it shows that
LTL
cannot detect repetitions in the last letter of a word.

Lemma 1
.

Let
u
whose last letter is
a
. For all formulas
\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if
u
satisfies
\varphi
then
ua^{k}
satisfies
\varphi
.

Lemma 2
.

If a formula of the form
\varphi=\textbf{F}(\psi_{1}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{2}\land\left(\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{r}\right)\dots)
is not satisfied by some word
v
then there exist
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
with
k\leq|v|+1
such that
v
does not satisfy
\psi=\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{1}}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{2}}\land\left(\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{i_{k}}\right)\dots)
but all words satisfying
\varphi
also satisfy
\psi
.

Proof.

We set
v_{1}=v
. For all
i\in[2,r]
we set
v_{i}
as the largest suffix of
v_{i-1}
satisfying
\psi_{i}
. If this suffix does not exist, we set
v_{i}=\varepsilon
.
As
v
does not satisfy
\varphi
, we easily obtain that for all
i<r

v_{i}
does not satisfy
\textbf{F}(\psi_{i}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i+1}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{r})\dots)
. Hence
v_{r-1}
does not satisfy
\textbf{F}\psi_{r}
and thus
v_{r}=\varepsilon
. We can now extract a decreasing subsequence of suffixes
v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{k}}
with
i_{1}=1
and for all
j>1
,
i_{j}
is the smallest index larger than
i_{j-1}
such that
v_{i_{j}}\neq v_{i_{j-1}}
if it exists.
Note that as this sequence is decreasing
k
cannot be larger than
|v|+1
. We set
\psi=\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{1}}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{2}}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
.
We have that for all
j>1
,
v_{i_{j}}
is the largest suffix of
v_{i_{j-1}}
satisfying
\psi_{i_{j}}
, and
v_{i_{k}}=\varepsilon
.
It is then easy to infer by reverse induction on
j
that for all
j
,
v_{i_{j}}
does not satisfy
\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{j}}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{j+1}}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
and thus that in particular
v_{i_{1}}=v
does not satisfy
\psi
.

Let
u
be a word satisfying
\varphi
, there is a non-increasing sequence of suffixes
u_{i}
of
u
such that
u_{1}=u
and each
u_{i}
satisfies
\textbf{F}(\psi_{i}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i+1}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{r})\dots)
.
It is then easy to see, by reverse induction on
j
, that for each
j\in[1,k]

u_{i_{j}}
satisfies
\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{j}}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{j+1}}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
.
In particular
u_{1}=u
satisfies
\psi
.
∎

We state and prove a dual version.

Lemma 3
.

If a formula of the form
\varphi=\textbf{G}(\psi_{1}\lor\textbf{G}(\psi_{2}\lor\dots\lor\textbf{G}\psi_{r})\dots)
is satisfied by some word
u
then there exist
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
with
k\leq|u|+1
such that
u
satisfies
\psi=\textbf{G}(\psi_{i_{1}}\lor\textbf{G}(\psi_{i_{2}}\lor\dots\lor\textbf{G}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
and all words satisfying
\psi
also satisfy
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let us momentarily use negation. Note that the proof of Lemma
2
still holds when we allow negations.
The word
u
does not satisfy
\varphi^{\prime}=\textbf{F}(\neg\psi_{1}\land\textbf{F}(\neg\psi_{2}\lor\dots\land\textbf{F}\neg\psi_{r})\dots)
as it is equivalent to the negation of
\varphi
, thus there exist
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
with
k\leq|u|+1
such that
u
does not satisfy
\psi^{\prime}=\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{1}}\land\textbf{F}(\psi_{i_{2}}\land\dots\land\textbf{F}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
but all words satisfying
\varphi^{\prime}
satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.
The formula
\psi=\textbf{G}(\psi_{i_{1}}\lor\textbf{G}(\psi_{i_{2}}\lor\dots\lor\textbf{G}\psi_{i_{k}})\dots)
is equivalent to the negation of
\psi^{\prime}
, thus it is satisfied by
u
, and all words satisfying
\psi
satisfy
\varphi
.
∎

The following fact will be useful for obtaining weak normal forms.

Fact 1
.

For all formulas
\varphi,\psi,\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in\textbf{LTL}
, the following equivalences hold:

1.

\textbf{G}\textbf{G}\varphi\equiv\textbf{G}\varphi
and
\textbf{F}\textbf{F}\varphi\equiv\textbf{F}\varphi
.

2.

\textbf{X}(\varphi\land\psi)\equiv\textbf{X}\varphi\land\textbf{X}\psi
and
\textbf{X}(\varphi\lor\psi)\equiv\textbf{X}\varphi\lor\textbf{X}\psi
.

3.

\textbf{G}\textbf{X}\varphi\equiv\bot
and
\textbf{F}\textbf{X}\varphi\equiv\textbf{X}\textbf{F}\varphi
.

4.

\textbf{G}\textbf{F}\varphi\equiv\textbf{F}\textbf{G}\varphi
(both state that
\psi
is satisfied on the last position)

5.

\textbf{G}(\varphi\land\psi)\equiv\textbf{G}\varphi\land\textbf{G}\psi
and
\textbf{F}(\varphi\lor\psi)\equiv\textbf{F}\varphi\lor\textbf{F}\psi
.

6.

\textbf{F}(\psi\land\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{F}\varphi_{i})\equiv\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{F}(\psi\land\textbf{F}\varphi_{i})
and
\textbf{G}(\psi\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{G}\varphi_{i})\equiv\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{G}(\psi\lor\textbf{G}\varphi_{i})
.

Proposition 1
.

All
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land\right\}
have the short formula property.

Proof.

Let us consider a formula
\varphi
separating
P
from
N
in
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
, we apply a series of transformations to
\varphi
to construct another separating formula of polynomial size.

First of all we push the

5
Degenerate cases: the shortest formula property

Definition 2
.

Let
Op
a set of operators, we say that
Op
has the
shortest formula property
if there is a polynomial time algorithm solving the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem and outputting the minimal separating formula if it exists.

Theorem 4
.

Let
Op
such that either
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X}\right\}
,
Op\subseteq\left\{\lor,\land\right\}
,
Op=\left\{\textbf{G},\land\right\}
.
Then
Op
has the shortest formula property, and therefore the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem is in
{\mathsf{P}}
.
The same holds for
Op=\left\{\textbf{F},\lor\right\}
if the size of the alphabet is fixed.

This theorem is the consequence of the following propositions, which establish it for various subsets
Op
of operators.

Proposition 7
.

All
Op\subseteq\left\{\lor,\land\right\}
have the shortest formula property.

Proof.

Let
(P,N)
a sample.
Let
A\subseteq\Sigma
be the set of first letters of the
u_{i}
. Consider the formula
\psi=\bigvee_{a\in A}a
.
We distinguish two cases.

•

Either
\psi
does not separate the
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
, meaning that some
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
share the same first letter. In that case
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
satisfy the same formulas of
\textbf{LTL}(\lor,\land)
and thus there is no separating formula.

•

Or
\psi
does separate the
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
. In that case it is also of minimal size: say some element
a
of
A
does not appear in a formula
\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}(\lor,\land)
, then as it is satisfied by some
u_{i}
starting with
a
,
\psi
is a tautology and is also satisfied by the
v_{j}
. Hence a separating formula has to contain all letters of

, and thus also at least
|A|-1
boolean operators. As a result,
\psi
is of minimal size.

∎

Proposition 8
.

All
Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X}\right\}
have the shortest formula property.

Proof.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, every formula
\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X})
is equivalent over words of length at most
\ell
to a formula of smaller or equal size in

\left\{\top,\bot,\textbf{X}^{k}a,\textbf{X}^{k}\textbf{F}a,\textbf{X}^{k}\textbf{G}a,\textbf{X}^{k}\textbf{F}\textbf{G}a\mid a\in\Sigma,0\leq k<\ell\right\}.

This can be shown by observing that over those words, for all
\psi
,
\textbf{F}\textbf{X}\psi
and
\textbf{X}\textbf{F}\psi
are equivalent, as well as
\textbf{G}\textbf{X}\psi
and

with

is equivalent to

As a result we can compute a minimal separating formula by enumerating formulas from this set (of polynomial size) and checking which ones separate the positive and negative words.
∎

Proposition 9
.

\left\{\textbf{G},\land\right\}
has the shortest formula property.

Proof.

An easy induction on
\psi
shows that all
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{G},\land)
formulas are equivalent over finite words to a formula of the form
\top
,
\bot
,
a
or
\textbf{G}a
with

, i.e., to a formula of size at most 2.
As those can be enumerated in polynomial time, we can compute a separating formula of minimal size or conclude that it does not exist in polynomial time.
∎

A corollary of our results is that the classification between
\mathsf{P}
and

mostly does not depend upon whether we consider the alphabet as part of the input or not. The only affected subcase is

, which is

Proposition 10
.

\left\{\textbf{F},\lor\right\}
has the shortest formula property when the alphabet is fixed.

Since

commutes with

, every formula can be turned into an equivalent disjunction of formulas of the form

or

An approximation algorithm for
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\land)

An
\alpha

does the following:
the algorithm either determines that there are no separating formulas,
or constructs a separating formula
\varphi
which has size at most
\alpha\cdot m

Theorem 5
.

There exists a
O(n\cdot\ell^{2})

Recall the definition of patterns: a word

induce the following
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\land)

\mathcal{P}=\textbf{X}^{p_{1}-1}(u(1)\wedge\textbf{X}^{p_{2}-i_{1}}(\cdots\wedge\textbf{X}^{p_{\ell}-p_{\ell-1}}u(\ell))\cdots).

It is equivalent to the (larger in size) formula

for each
i\in[1,\ell]

is

.
To determine the size of a pattern

.
The size of

The two parameters of a pattern, last position and width, hint at the key trade-off we will have to face in learning

Atomic formulas are already a special case of patterns.

\textbf{X}^{i_{1}}(c_{1}\wedge\mathcal{P}^{\prime})

\mathcal{P}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{P}_{2}

Let us consider the following easier problem:

The proved inequality implies

.
This quantity is less than

, implying that the algorithm
stops after at most
\log(n)\cdot m
steps.
Consequently, the pattern corresponding to

has size at most

completing the claim on approximation.

A naive complexity analysis yields an implementation of the greedy algorithm running in time

,
leading to an overall complexity of

by running the greedy algorithm on the prefixes of length

of

for each
\ell^{\prime}\in[1,\ell]
.
∎

7
Almost all fragments with the next operator are hard to approximate

Theorem 6
.

The
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\wedge)
learning problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard,
and there are no

Note that Theorem

and Theorem

The hardness result stated in Theorem

follows from a reduction to the set cover problem,
that we define now.
The set cover decision problem is: given

An
\alpha

where
k
is the size of a minimal cover.
The following results form the state of the art for solving exact and approximate variants of the set cover problem.

Theorem 7

((?))
.

The set cover problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete,
and there are no
(1-o(1))\cdot\log(n)
polynomial time approximation algorithms unless
{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{NP}}

Proof.

We construct a reduction from set cover.
Let

subsets of
[1,n]
and

.

Let us consider the word

,

and

We claim that there is a cover of size

from

Thanks to Lemma

We can extend the previous hardness result to all sets of operators

•

We now show that for all

\varphi

separates

\varphi
.

•

If
\varphi=c

•

If

then

.
Since

.

•

If

.

•

The case

Proposition 11
.

For all
\left\{\textbf{X},\land\right\}\subseteq Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\textbf{X},\land,\lor\right\}
, there is a polynomial-time reduction from the
\textbf{LTL}(X,\land)
learning problem for a single positive word to the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
one over the same alphabet.

We recall two (folklore) facts about
LTL

Fact 3

For all
w_{1}\in\Sigma^{*},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{+},N\in\mathbb{N}

\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}

.

.

For all
w_{1}\in\Sigma^{*},N\in\mathbb{N}

with at most

.

We now proceed to the proof of Proposition

Proof.

Let

be words, all of length
\ell
. Let

, we set

\psi_{u}=\bigwedge_{i=0}^{|u|-1}X^{i}u_{i},

, for all

\textbf{u}=(u^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{1}\cdots v^{\prime}_{n})^{M+1}\quad;\quad\textbf{v}_{i}=v^{\prime}_{i}\cdots v^{\prime}_{n}(u^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{1}\cdots v^{\prime}_{n})^{M}.

The formula

separates

from

is equal to

separates

from the

Let

is satisfied by

and

exists and

We first show that

or

(each appearing exactly once in

for some

or
\textbf{G}\psi^{\prime}
.

As the

to the variables in

for all

Note that

satisfies it, since they do not satisfy

Let

•

Since

•

\psi_{p}=\textbf{F}\psi^{\prime}

\textbf{u}[j_{p}\dots]=(u^{\prime}[j_{p}\dots]v^{\prime}_{1}\cdots v^{\prime}_{n})(u^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{1}\cdots v^{\prime}_{n})^{M}

By Lemma

Almost all fragments without the next operator are hard to approximate

A study of
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)

As we will see,
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
over an alphabet of size
2
is very weak.
This degeneracy vanishes when considering alphabets of size at least
3
.
Instead of defining a normal form as we did for
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\wedge)

we characterise the expressive power of
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
and construct for each property expressible in this logic a minimal formula.

Lemma 6
.

For every formula
\varphi\in\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
,
either it is equivalent to false or there exists a finite set of non-repeating words
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
and

We proceed by induction over
\varphi
.

•

For the atomic formula

, the property is satisfied using the empty set of words and

•

If
\varphi=\textbf{F}\varphi^{\prime}
, by induction hypothesis we get
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
and

\varphi^{\prime}
.
We let
w^{\prime}_{i}=cw_{i}

if and only if

is a subword of

If

for

.
In both cases,

Lemma
6
gives a characterisation of the properties expressible in
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
.
It implies that over an alphabet of size
2
the fragment
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
is very weak.
Indeed, there are very few non-repeating words over the alphabet
\Sigma=\left\{a,b\right\}

only prefixes of
abab\dots
and
baba\dots
.
This implies that formulas in
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
over
\Sigma=\left\{a,b\right\}

and
b
(starting from
a
or from
b
)
and check whether the word starts with
a
or
b

In particular, the
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)

Hence we now assume that
\Sigma
has size at least

We move back from semantics to syntax, and show how to construct minimal formulas.
Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
a finite set of non-repeating words and
c\in\Sigma\cup\left\{\varepsilon\right\}
,
we define a formula
\varphi
as follows.

The set of prefixes of

are organised in a forest (set of trees):

w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
,
and its children are the words

which are prefixes of some
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
.
The leaves are labelled by
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
.
We interpret each tree

as a formula
\varphi_{t}
in
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{F},\wedge)
as follows, in an inductive fashion: for

,
if

is labelled

If

and

and if

As an example, consider the set of words

, and the letter

The forest corresponding to

contains two trees:

, and the other one the nodes

The two corresponding formulas are

\textbf{F}(b\wedge\textbf{F}(a\wedge\textbf{F}b))\qquad;\qquad\textbf{F}(a\wedge\textbf{F}b\wedge\textbf{F}c).

And the formula corresponding to the set of words

, and the letter

a\ \wedge\ \textbf{F}(b\wedge\textbf{F}(a\wedge\textbf{F}b))\ \wedge\ \textbf{F}(a\wedge\textbf{F}b\wedge\textbf{F}c).

.

For every non-repeating words
w_{1},\dots,w_{p}
and

\varphi

Adding the initial letter we obtain a grounded fattern

,
in that case the letter

.

Lemma 8

Let

\varphi

and

\text{and $z$ starts with $c$}.\end{cases}

Let

, since

The most technical result in this paper is the following theorem, which strengthens Theorem

Theorem 9
.

For all
\left\{\textbf{F},\land\right\}\subseteq Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\land,\lor,\neg\right\}

Its main difficulty stems from the use of a fixed alphabet, combined with the absence of

To circumvent this problem we will adapt the previous reductions from hitting set: we again use a single positive example

V^{\prime}_{i}
is a suffix of
V_{i}
.
As a result,

is a suffix of
v_{i}
, hence it does not satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.

If
J_{i}=J^{\prime}
and
|U^{\prime}|\leq 2
then
i\notin I^{\prime}
.

If
J_{i}<J^{\prime}
then as

is not a suffix of

we must have

and

is either

or
b(ab)^{M}
.
If

then we set
P^{\prime}_{i}=ab
and
V^{\prime}_{i}=(ab)^{M}
.
As

is a suffix of
v_{i}
, it does not satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.

Similarly if
U^{\prime}=b(ab)^{M}
then we set
P^{\prime}_{i}=ba
and
V^{\prime}_{i}=b(ab)^{M-1}
.
Since
v^{\prime}_{i}=V^{\prime}_{i}cw_{i,J^{\prime}+1}\cdots cw_{i,m}c
is a suffix of
v_{i}
, it does not satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.
We can then apply the induction hypothesis to obtain a separation tree
t
whose root is labelled by
I^{\prime}
,
\psi^{\prime}
and
(J^{\prime}_{i},P^{\prime}_{i},V^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in I^{\prime}}
. We obtain the result by taking a tree whose root is labelled by
I
,
\psi
and
(J_{i},P_{i},V_{i})_{i\in I}
and giving it one child whose subtree is

.

This concludes our induction.
∎

Lemma 11
.

If there exists a separation proof tree for
\varphi
then
||\varphi||\geq 6k(M-1)
.

Proof.

We make some key observations. Let
x
be a node labelled by some letter or its negation. Then:

•

either
x
only has ancestors labelled by formulas of the form
\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
or
\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}
, in which case

and
V_{i}^{x}c
both start with

.

•

or
x
has an ancestor labelled by a formula of the form
\textbf{F}\varphi
or
\textbf{G}\varphi
. Let
y
be its closest such ancestor, and

the only child of
y
. Then
\psi^{y}
is of the form
\textbf{F}\psi^{z}
or
\textbf{G}\psi^{z}
with
\psi^{z}
a boolean combination of formulas including
\psi^{x}
(which is a letter or its negation).

Hence for all
i\in I^{z}
we have that
P^{z}_{i}V^{z}_{i}c
and
V^{z}_{i}c
start with the same letter. Moreover as all nodes from

to

are labelled by formulas of the form
\psi_{1}\lor\psi_{2}
or
\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}
, we have
I^{x}\subseteq I^{z}
and
P^{x}_{i}V^{x}_{i}c=P^{x}_{i}V^{x}_{i}c
and
V^{z}_{i}c=V^{x}_{i}c
for all
i\in I^{x}
. As a result, by definition of a separation tree, we must have
I^{x}=\emptyset
.

Thus for all node
x
, if
x
is a leaf then

. Furthermore, if
x
has two children
y
and

then
I^{x}
is the disjoint union of
I^{y}
and
I^{z}
, and if
x
has a single child

then

. Those facts are direct consequences of the definition of separation tree.

The second key observation is that if
y
is a child of
x
and
J^{x}_{i}=J^{y}_{i}
and
P^{y}_{i}\neq\varepsilon
for some

then
P^{x}_{i}\neq\varepsilon
and
|V_{i}^{y}|\geq|V_{i}^{x}|-1
. If furthermore

then
\psi^{x}
is of the form
\textbf{F}\psi^{y}
with
\psi^{y}
a boolean combination of formulas including at least one letter or its negation.

The third observation is that if
y
is a child of
x
and
J^{x}_{i}<J^{y}_{i}
and
P^{y}_{i}\neq\varepsilon
for some

then
|V_{i}^{y}|\geq 2M-1
and

.

Let
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. In light of the previous observations, there is a branch of the tree
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
(
x_{j+1}
being a child of
x_{j}
for all

) such that
i
is in
I^{x_{j}}
for all

but not in

.
As
i\in I^{x_{k}}
but
i\notin I^{x_{k+1}}
, we have
J_{i}^{x_{k+1}}=J_{i}^{x_{k}}
and
|V_{i}^{x_{k}}|\leq 1
and
P_{i}^{x_{k}}\neq\varepsilon
.
Let
j
be the minimal index such that

. As the sequence of

is non-increasing, all

are equal for

for all

. Therefore

cannot be the root, hence

|V_{i}^{x_{j}}|\geq 2M-1
and

. Furthermore
|V_{i}^{x_{k}}|\leq 1
. Our second remark allows us to conclude that there exist at least
2M-2

\psi^{x_{\ell}}
is of the form
\textbf{F}\psi^{x_{\ell+1}}
with
\psi^{x_{\ell+1}}
a boolean combination of formulas including at least one letter or its negation. For each such

Hence for all

there exists

such that

and
J_{i}^{x}=J_{i}
for at least

, we have

for all

is a solution to the set cover problem. As a result, there are at least

We may now finish the proof of Theorem

.
Recall that we considered an input

,

and

(the encoding is irrelevant). We set

.

Let

By Proposition

, we have

Dual hardness result

We now show hardness for fragments with operators
G
and
\lor
. As we allowed negation in the previous result, we can infer that one almost directly.

Theorem 10
.

For all
\left\{\textbf{G},\lor\right\}\subseteq Op\subseteq\left\{\textbf{F},\textbf{G},\land,\lor,\neg\right\}
, the learning problem for
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even for an alphabet of size
3
.

We take the same instance of the hitting problem, but this time we consider the
\textbf{LTL}(Op)
learning problem with the
v_{i}
as positive words and
u
as the only negative one.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\varphi
be an
\textbf{LTL}(\land,\lor,\neg,F,G)
formula separating the

and
v
, then we have:
k(6M-3)\leq|\varphi|
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be such a formula, and let
\overline{\varphi}
be its negation, with the negations pushed to the bottom.
Formally,
\overline{\varphi}
is defined by induction on
\varphi
:

•

\overline{a}=\neg a

•

\overline{\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}}=\overline{\varphi_{1}}\lor\overline{\varphi_{2}}

•

\overline{\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}}=\overline{\varphi_{1}}\land\overline{\varphi_{2}}

•

\overline{\textbf{F}\varphi_{1}}=\textbf{G}\overline{\varphi_{1}}

•

\overline{\textbf{G}\varphi_{1}}=\textbf{F}\overline{\varphi_{1}}

A clear induction shows that a word satisfies one if and only if it does not satisfy the other (note that this would not be true if we allowed the operator

). Hence
\overline{\varphi}
is satisfied by

but not by the

.
∎

9
Perspectives and open problems

In this paper, we showed
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness of the
LTL
learning problem for all fragments which do not include the until operator.
The same holds adding until for non-constant size alphabets. Hence the main open question is the following:

Open problem 1
.

Is the learning problem
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete for full
LTL
with constant size alphabet?

We consider
LTL
over finite traces; some of the related works we discussed in the introduction actually considered infinite traces as common in the verification research community. All our hardness results easily transfer to the infinite trace case, simply by extending finite traces into infinite ones with a new symbol. In particular, we obtain as corollary that the
LTL
learning problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard for infinite traces when the alphabet is part of the input.

The negative results in this paper suggest looking for approximation algorithms. Beyond
\textbf{LTL}(\textbf{X},\land)
, nothing is known in terms of upper and lower bounds on polynomial time approximation algorithms, leaving an open field of exciting research directions.
In the same vein, one could wonder about the parameterized complexity of the
LTL
learning problem, in particular when fixing the number of words. We leave this question open and hope to inspire further studies!
