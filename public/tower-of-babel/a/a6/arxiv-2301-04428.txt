Abstract
. The Hopf algebra 𝒟 \mathcal{D} which is the subject of this paper can be viewed as a Drinfeld double of the bosonisation of the Jordan plane. Its prime and primitive spectra are completely determined. As a corollary of this analysis it is shown that 𝒟 \mathcal{D} satisfies the Dixmier-Moeglin Equivalence, leading to the formulation of a conjecture on the validity of this equivalence for pointed Noetherian Hopf algebras.

1.2. Hopf algebras in duality.
The full Drinfeld double
\mathcal{D}(H)=H\bowtie H^{\circ}
of an infinite dimensional
Hopf algebra
H
may often be unwieldy due to
H
having “too many” finite dimensional representations and thus leading to an
unmanageably large finite dual
H^{\circ}
. This has generated significant recent interest in constructing doubles

\mathcal{D}(H):=H\bowtie H^{\prime}
where
H^{\prime}
is some suitable Hopf subalgebra of
H^{\circ}
; see for example
[
20
,
8
]

and the papers listed in
\lx@sectionsign
1.1. Much is at present unclear: for example, what is an appropriate definition of a “suitable” Hopf subalgebra
H^{\prime}
; and
does a suitable algebra
H^{\prime}
always exist? The double
\mathcal{D}
of the Jordan plane is a test case for these and other
questions. In particular, some of the desirable properties exhibited by
\mathcal{D}
may form a paradigm for what one might
aim for in defining doubles in more general settings.

1.3. Representation theory.
A striking feature of the representation theory of
\mathcal{D}
is the
fact, proved in
[
1
, Theorem 3.11]
and recalled here in Theorem
2.2
(iii), that
U(\mathfrak{sl}(2,k))
is a quotient
Hopf algebra of
\mathcal{D}
and the finite dimensional irreducible
\mathcal{D}
-modules are precisely the finite dimensional
irreducible
U(\mathfrak{sl}(2,k))
-modules. This immediately suggests a plethora of questions, some of which are addressed
in
[
3
]
, where Verma
\mathcal{D}
-modules are introduced. But many others remain untouched: for instance, what
can be said about the category of locally finite dimensional
\mathcal{D}
-modules, and for each primitive ideal
P
of

\mathcal{D}
can one find a canonical irreducible module (hopefully a factor of a Verma module) whose annihilator equals
P
?
The first step in these questions is to classify the primitive ideals of
\mathcal{D}
, as we do here.

1.4. Dixmier-Moeglin equivalence.
The validity of the Dixmier-Moeglin Equivalence for an algebra
R

yields simultaneous representation-theoretic, algebraic and topological characterisations of the primitive ideals amongst the
prime ideals of
R
. This equivalence is a feature of some but not all
Hopf
k
-algebras; see, for example,
[
6
,
5
]
for discussions of when it holds for a noetherian (Hopf) algebra. As we prove in
Theorem
1.2
,
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the equivalence. See
\lx@sectionsign
5.2
for
the details, where we also give a rather ambitious Conjecture
5.5
, proposing a general result
encompassing all affine Noetherian pointed Hopf
\mathbb{C}
-algebras.

Notation.
Throughout, all vector spaces and all
unadorned tensor products are understood to be over the base field
k
. We denote the comultiplication of a Hopf algebra

H
by
\Delta
and its augmentation ideal by
H^{+}
. The Gelfand-Kirillov, or GK dimension of an object

, while the global (homological) dimension, respectively injective dimension of a ring

is denoted by

\mathrm{gldim}(R)
, respectively

. For precision, we specify that in the
Ore extension

, multiplication
is defined by

(1.1)

It follows that

is a

This follows

2.1.
Definitions and notation

The following definitions and notation from
[
1
]
)
will remain in play throughout the paper. First, the

is

J\;:=\;k\langle x,y:[y,x]=-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}\rangle,

Then the

\displaystyle[u,v]

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}u^{2};\;\;[\zeta,v]=-v;\;\;[\zeta,u]=-u;\;[u,y]=1-g;

\displaystyle[v,x]

\displaystyle=1-g+xu;\;\;[v,y]=yu-g\zeta;\;[v,g]=gu;\;\;[\zeta,y]=y;\;\;[\zeta,x]=x;\;

\displaystyle[x,u]

\displaystyle=[x,g]=[u,g]=[\zeta,g]=0.

by specifying that

is grouplike

and

by setting

and

Observe that

is a Hopf subalgebra of
\mathcal{D}
and in fact, as one can see from the PBW theorem
for
\mathcal{D}
as described in
[

, Proposition 2.3(ii)]

\mathcal{D}=J\otimes_{k}K
as vector spaces. As noted in
[

and

which yields the multiplication relations between these subalgebras as
in
[

]

3.
Ring-theoretic preparations

In this section we assemble some properties needed in the analysis of the primitive spectrum of
\mathcal{D}
. The proofs are most easily approached by viewing
\mathcal{D}
as an iterated Hopf Ore extension starting not from the base field
k
but from the commutative normal Hopf subalgebra
\mathcal{O}(G)=k\langle x,u,g^{\pm 1}\rangle
of Theorem
2.2
(i). More precisely:

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathcal{D}
is an iterated Ore extension

(3.1)

\mathcal{D}\;=\;\mathcal{O}(G)[y;\delta_{1}][\zeta;\delta_{2}][v;\tau,\delta_{3}],

where the derivations
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, the automorphism
\tau
and the
\tau
-derivation
\delta_{3}
can be read off from the defining relations of
\mathcal{D}
given in Subsection
2.1
.

In particular,
\mathcal{D}
is a noetherian domain.

Proof.

Use the proof of
[
2
, Proposition 1.6]
to show that
\mathcal{D}
has basis

\{g^{a}x^{b}u^{c}y^{d}\zeta^{e}v^{f}:a\in\mathbb{Z},b,\dots,f\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Then the form of the relations (
2.1
) combined with

[
12
, Theorem 1, p.438]
show that it is indeed an Ore extension.
∎

Although it will be not needed in this paper, the description (
3.1
) even describes
\mathcal{D}
as an Iterated Hopf Ore Extension (IHOE), in the sense that each extension in that formula is itself a Hopf algebra. It also shows that, by setting

\displaystyle\deg x=\deg u=\deg g=\deg g^{-1}=0;

\displaystyle\deg y=\deg\zeta=\deg v=1,

one obtains a filtration
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathcal{D}
with associated graded algebra

(3.2)

\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{D}\;=\;\mathcal{O}(G)[\overline{y},\overline{\zeta},\overline{v}].

So
\gr_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{D}
is a commutative polynomial algebra in 6 variables with one variable inverted.

3.1.
Homological properties

In this subsection we note that
\mathcal{D}
has certain useful homological properties, and we begin with the relevant definitions.
A ring
A
is called
Auslander Gorenstein
if it has finite injective dimension and satisfies the

Gorenstein condition
: if
p<q

are non-negative integers and
M
is a finitely generated
A
-module, then

\Ext_{A}^{p}(N,\,A)=0
for every submodule
N
of

\Ext_{A}^{q}(M,\,A)
. The ring
A
is
Auslander regular
if it is Auslander Gorenstein of finite global dimension.
Set

j_{A}(M)=\min\{r:\Ext^{r}_{A}(M,A)\not=0\}
for the
homological grade
of
M
.
Then an Auslander Gorenstein ring
A
of finite GK dimension is
called
GK-Cohen-Macaulay
(or just CM), provided
that
j_{A}(M)+\GKdim(M)=\GKdim(A)
holds
for each such
M
. Obviously affine commutative regular rings are both Auslander regular and CM.

Proposition 3.2
.

(i)

\mathcal{D}
is Auslander regular and CM.

(ii)

\mathcal{D}
is AS regular in the sense of, say,
[
19
]
.

(iii)

\GKdim(\mathcal{D})=6=\mathrm{gldim}(\mathcal{D}).

(iv)

GK dimension is an exact function on finitely generated
\mathcal{D}
-modules.

Proof.

(i) By
[
7
, Remark, p.157]
the filtration
\mathcal{F}
is Zariskian and so the result follows from
[
7
, Theorems 3.8, 3.9 and Remark, p. 165]
.

(ii) This is immediate from (i) and
[
10
, Lemma 6.1]
.

(iii) By
[
22
, Corollary 1.4]
,
\GKdim(\mathcal{D})=\GKdim(\gr_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{D}))=6.

By Proposition
3.1
and
[

, Theorem 7.5.3(i)]
, we have

.
By
[

\mathcal{D}
has a finite dimensional module, say

and the CM condition implies that

. Hence

.

(iv) Since

3.2.
Key lemma

The following lemma will be crucial in our analysis of the primitive spectrum of
\mathcal{D}
. In its proof, given an ideal B of a noetherian ring
S
, we denote by
\sqrt{B}
the ideal of
S
such that
\sqrt{B}/B
is the nilradical of
S/B
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated (right or left)
\mathcal{D}
-module such that either
\mathrm{Ann}_{k[x]}(M)\neq 0
or
\mathrm{Ann}_{k[u]}(M)\neq 0
. Then

(i)

there exists
r\geq 1
such that

(\mathfrak{m}_{+}\mathcal{D})^{r}\subseteq(\mathcal{O}(G)^{+}\mathcal{D})^{r}\subseteq\mathrm{Ann}_{\mathcal{D}}(M);

(ii)

\GKdim(M)\leq 3
.

Proof.

(i) Let
I:=\mathrm{Ann}_{\mathcal{D}}(M)
, an ideal of
\mathcal{D}
. Assume that
I\cap k[x]\neq 0
, the proof in the other case being exactly similar, but with
k\langle u,v\rangle
replacing
J
. One easily confirms that every non-zero prime ideal of the Jordan plane
J=k\langle x,y\rangle
contains
x
. Therefore, since
I\cap k[x]\neq 0
, there exists
N\geq 1
such that

x^{N}\in I\cap k[x]\subseteq I\cap\mathcal{O}(G).
Since
\mathcal{O}(G)
is commutative,

(3.3)

x\in\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G)).

Since
I
is an ideal of
\mathcal{D}
,
[v,\,I]\subseteq I
; moreover, from the defining relations of
\mathcal{D}
and the fact that
\mathcal{O}(G)=k\langle x,u,g^{\pm 1}\rangle
,

[v,\,\mathcal{O}(G)]\subseteq\mathcal{O}(G)
. Therefore

(3.4)

[v,\,I\cap\mathcal{O}(G)]\subseteq I\cap\mathcal{O}(G).

Since
k
has characteristic 0 it follows from (
3.4
) and
[
17
, Lemma 3.20]
that

(3.5)

[v,\,\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G))]\subseteq\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G)).

By (
3.3
) and (
3.5
)

[v,\,x]=1-g+xu\in\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G)),

so that
(1-g)\in\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G))
. Then

[v,\,g-1]=[v,g]=gu\in\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G)),

so that
u\in\sqrt{(}I\cap\mathcal{O}(G))
. Since
\mathcal{O}(G)^{+}
is generated by
x
,
u
and
g-1
we deduce that
\mathcal{O}(G)^{+}\mathcal{D}\subseteq\sqrt{I}
, proving (i).

(ii) By (i)
M
is a finitely generated
\mathcal{D}/(\mathcal{O}(G)^{+}\mathcal{D})^{r}
-module for some
r\geq 1
. Since
\mathcal{D}/\mathcal{O}(G)^{+}\mathcal{D}\cong U(\mathfrak{sl}(2,k)
by Theorem
2.2
(ii), and so has GK dimension 3, (ii) follows from this and Proposition
3.2
(iv).
∎

\displaystyle A\;

\displaystyle:=\;\{q^{i}:i\geq 0\}\,\dot{\cup}\,\{x^{j}:j\geq 0\},\;\;\;B\;:=\;\{s^{i}:i\geq 0\}\,\dot{\cup}\,\{x^{j}:j\geq 0\},

\displaystyle:=\;\{q^{i}:i\geq 0\}\,\dot{\cup}\,\{u^{j}:j\geq 0\},\;\;\;D\;:=\;\{s^{i}:i\geq 0\}\,\dot{\cup}\,\{u^{j}:j\geq 0\}.

(1)

(2) Localising at the powers of

p_{C}\,:=\,2q^{-1}u^{-1}v,\;\;q_{C}\,:=\,q,\;\;t_{C}\,:=\,q^{-1}u^{-1},\;\;\eta_{C}\,:=\,-uq\zeta
.

(iv)

In
\mathcal{D}_{(D)}
, set
p_{D}\,:=\,2s^{-1}u^{-1}v,\;\;q_{D}\,:=\,s,\;\;t_{D}\,:=\,s^{-1}u^{-1},\;\;\eta_{D}\,:=\,us\zeta.

We further set
z_{\Omega}:=z
for
\Omega=A
and
\Omega=C
but

when
\Omega=B,D
.

The motivation behind the above definitions becomes clear from the following lemma. For
\Omega=A
, this was obtained in the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 4.8]
. The claims regarding the other elements can be checked by a similar direct calculation.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Omega\in\{A,B,C,D\}.

Then we have the following relations in
Q(\mathcal{D})
:

[p_{\Omega},q_{\Omega}]\,=\,1\,=[\eta_{\Omega},t_{\Omega}],\,\textit{ with all other brackets being zero;}\qed

When
\Omega=A
the following result is given in
[
1
, Theorem 4.8]
, although we give a proof that works for all 4 cases simultaneously.

Theorem 3.8
.

For each
\Omega\in\{A,B,C,D\}
, the localisation
\mathcal{D}_{(\Omega)}
is a localised Weyl algebra over its centre. More precisely:

\mathcal{D}_{(\Omega)}\;=\;A_{2}^{(\Omega)}(k)\otimes S_{(\Omega)},

where
A_{2}^{(\Omega)}(k)
denotes the localisation of the second Weyl algebra over
k
with generators
p_{\Omega},q_{\Omega}^{\pm 1},\eta_{\Omega},t_{\Omega}^{\pm 1}
, while
S_{(\Omega)}
is the commutative ring

S_{(\Omega)}=k[z_{\Omega}^{\pm 1},\omega]
.

Proof.

The generators
z,\,\omega
and
\theta
of
Z(\mathcal{D})
are given in Lemma
2.1
(iii), from which
it follows that the subalgebra
S_{(\Omega)}
of
Q(\mathcal{D})
is contained in the centre
Z(\mathcal{D}_{(\Omega)})
.
Therefore we can consider the subalgebra

(3.6)

\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}\;:=\;S_{(\Omega)}\langle p_{\Omega},q_{\Omega},t_{\Omega},\eta_{\Omega}\rangle\ \subseteq\ \mathcal{D}_{(\Omega)}.

We claim that
the inclusion (
3.6
) is an equality.
In order to prove this, check that given generators of
\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}
are
contained in
\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}
. Thus, for example, when
\Omega=A
, one shows that

\{q^{-1},x^{\pm 1},y,\zeta,g^{\pm 1},u,v\}\subset\mathcal{E}_{(A)},
and similarly in the other cases. Thus,

\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}=\mathcal{D}_{(\Omega)},
as claimed.
As noted in the proof of Lemma
3.5

the localisation of
\mathcal{D}
at
\Omega
involves inverting one central and one ad-nilpotent element of
\mathcal{D}
. Thus, by Proposition
3.2
(iii) and

[
18
, Lemma 4.7]
,
\GKdim(D_{(\Omega})=\GKdim(\mathcal{D})=6
. We conclude that

\GKdim(\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)})=\GKdim(\mathcal{D})=6.

On the other hand, by Lemma
3.7

\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}
is a factor of the ring

which is also a domain of GK-dimension 6. So if
\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}
were a proper factor of

then

would imply that

giving a contradiction.

So the only possibility is that

\mathcal{E}_{(\Omega)}\;\cong\;V_{(\Omega)}=S_{(\Omega)}\otimes_{k}A_{2}^{(\Omega)}(k),

as required. ∎

4.
The primitive spectrum of
\mathcal{D}

In this section we describe the primitive spectrum of
\mathcal{D}
. This splits naturally into several cases:

•

the primitive ideals not containing
\mathfrak{m}_{+}
or
\mathfrak{m}_{0}
; these are the generic ones;

•

the ideal

•

the ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{0}\mathcal{D}
, for which

is a unique prime ideal

;

•

finally,

is also maximal.

The details are given in the next four subsections with the results being combined in Subsection

In this section and in Section

we will without further reference use of the yoga for prime ideals of Noetherian rings under Ore localisation as described in, for example,
[

, Theorems 10.18 and 10.20]
. We use Notation
2.3
to describe the maximal ideals of

and Definition

to define Ore sets in
\mathcal{D}
.

4.1.
The generic minimal primitives

We begin by looking at the generic case.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
be a maximal ideal of
Z(\mathcal{D})
with
\mathfrak{m}\neq\mathfrak{m}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{m}\neq\mathfrak{m}_{0}
. Then the following are true.

(i)

\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
is a completely prime maximal ideal of
\mathcal{D}
.

(ii)

The localisation of
\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
at the powers of (the image of) either
x
or

\Omega\in\{A,B,C,D\}

(iii)

\GKdim(\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D})=4

(iv)

\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}

(v)

\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}

\mathrm{injdim}(\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D})<4.

(i), (ii)
By Notation
2.3

\mathfrak{m}=\langle z-\alpha,\omega-\beta,\theta-\gamma\rangle

\alpha\gamma=\beta^{2}

\mathfrak{m}
, either
(a)

\alpha\neq 0
or
(b)

\gamma\neq 0

Assume

. We prove (ii) for the localisation at the powers of
x

u

C
and
D

A
and
B

\lx@sectionsign
3.3
and applying Theorem

, we see that
\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}_{(A)}
is a maximal ideal of

\mathcal{D}_{(A)}
. Observe that, since

A^{(A)}_{2}(k)
is isomorphic to the localisation of
\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
at the powers of
x
. Define

P_{\mathfrak{m}}\,:=\,\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}_{(A)}\cap\mathcal{D},

so that
P_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a completely prime ideal of
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}\subseteq P_{\mathfrak{m}}
. By definition of
P_{\mathfrak{m}}
,

(4.1)

\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}_{(A)}\;=\;P_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathcal{D}_{(A)}.

We claim that in fact

(4.2)

P_{\mathfrak{m}}\;=\;\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is (left) noetherian there exist
e_{1},\ldots,e_{t}\in P_{\mathfrak{m}}
such that
P_{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}e_{i}
. By (
4.1
), for each

there exist
f_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
and

such that

(4.3)

e_{i}\;=\;f_{i}x^{-s_{i}}.

Define

, and

Thus

is an ideal of
\mathcal{D}
containing
\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
and, by (

),

If

; otherwise we see from Lemma
3.3
that

for some

\mathfrak{m}\subseteq I

, and (

) is proved.

In case
(a)
it remains only to prove that

is a maximal ideal of
\mathcal{D}
. Suppose then that
J
is an ideal of
\mathcal{D}
with

. Then

of
\mathcal{D}_{(A)}

.

Suppose that
(b)
holds rather than
(a)
. Then the element
s
is a unit
\textit{mod}\,\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
, so we use the same argument as for
(a)
, but working with
\mathcal{D}_{(C)}
rather than
\mathcal{D}_{(A)}
.

(iii) By (ii) and
[
18
, Example 3.7 and Theorem 4.9]

at the powers of

has GK dimension 4. Since

acts nilpotently on

, it follows from
[

, Theorem 4.9]
that
\GKdim(\mathcal{D}/\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D})\;=\;4.

(iv) Again we assume
(a)
that

for

, the proof in case

being similar. We can begin a regular central sequence in

with

. Since
\mathcal{D}
is CM of GK-dimension 6 by Proposition

(i,iii)

[

, Theorem 7.2(b)]

is CM of GK-dimension 5. Moreover, by

the CM property ensures that

is GK-homogeneous; that is, it contains no non-zero ideal with GK-dimension strictly less than 5. Since

is a polynomial algebra we can choose

such that
\mathfrak{m}=\langle z-\alpha,y\rangle
. If

is a zero divisor in

we obtain a non-zero ideal of
\mathcal{D}/(z-\alpha)\mathcal{D}
killed by

, contradicting the GK-homogeneity of

in view of (iii). Thus

is a regular central sequence in
\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
.

(v) Since
\mathcal{D}
is CM by Proposition

(i),

is CM with
\GKdim(R)=4
by (iv) and two applications of

, Theorem 7.2(b)]
. The Auslander Gorenstein property is given by (iv) and
[
19
,
\lx@sectionsign
3.4, Remark (3)]
. As

is simple it cannot have a finite dimensional module. Hence

follows from the next lemma.
∎

The following observation is well-known.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R
be a noetherian, Auslander Gorenstein, CM ring and write
\GKdim(R)=m
. Then
\mathrm{injdim}(R)\leq m
. Moreover
\mathrm{injdim}(R)=m\iff

R
has a finite dimensional representation.

Proof.

Let
n=\mathrm{injdim}(R)
and pick a finitely generated
R
-module

such that
\Ext^{n}_{R}(M,R)\not=0
. By
the Auslander condition and the spectral sequence
[

j(E^{nn}(M))=n
for

.
By the CM property
\GKdim(E^{nn}(M))=m-n
and the result follows easily.
∎

4.2.
Non-generic minimal primitives (I) -
\mathfrak{m}_{+}

The next case to consider is
\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{D}
for

\mathfrak{m}^{+}=\mathcal{D}^{+}\cap Z(\mathcal{D})=\langle\omega+16,\theta-16\rangle

We start with a subsidiary result, which works for any field

.

\mathcal{D}
is a Jacobson ring that satisfies the Nullstellensatz, in other words:

(i)

(i)

(i),(ii), let

\GKdim(\mathcal{D}/P_{+})\;=\;4

Non-generic minimal primitives (II) -

In this subsection we begin our study of the ideal

Notation 4.6

Lemma 4.8

On

\displaystyle h^{-1}yh\ =

Set

Lemma 4.10

If there exists a non-zero

Using

17

twice, clearly

is a proper nonzero

\widetilde{T}
. Pick a prime ideal

. Then, by
[
17
, Lemmata 3.18 and 3.21]
, twice,

-invariant prime ideal of

Proposition 4.11
.

There is no proper, non-zero
(\partial_{1},\widetilde{\partial}_{2})
-invariant ideal
I
in
\widetilde{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists such an ideal
I
. By Lemma
4.10
we can and will assume that
I
is a prime ideal.
Suppose, first, that
(xu+\lambda)\in I
, for some
\lambda\in k
. Then

I\ \ni\ \partial_{1}(xu+\lambda)\ =\ (-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}ux-2)x-(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2})u\ =\ -(ux+2)x

and

I\ \ni\ \widetilde{\partial}_{2}(xu+\lambda)\ =\ h\bigl(-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}u^{2}x+({\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}ux+2)u\bigr)\ =\ h\bigl(ux^{2}+2u\bigr)\ =\ h(ux+2)u.

As
(xu+2)^{-1}=h^{2}\in\widetilde{R}
, clearly
\lambda\not=2
and so the two equations imply that
x\in I
, respectively
u\in I
. Thus,
I=x\widetilde{R}+u\widetilde{R}
. But, now
I\ni\partial_{1}(u)=-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}ux-2
and so
I=\widetilde{R}
, a contradiction. We conclude that

(4.19)

I\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset\quad\text{for }\mathcal{C}=\{(xu+\lambda):\lambda\in k^{*}\}

Since
I
is a prime ideal it follows that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(I)
and hence that
I_{\mathcal{C}}
is a proper prime ideal of the localisation
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Next, if
I_{\mathcal{C}}\ni f=f(u)
for some

, then
I_{\mathcal{C}}\ni\partial_{1}(f)=-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(ux+4)\frac{df}{du}
. Hence
\frac{df}{du}\in I_{\mathcal{C}}
. By induction on
\deg f
, this implies that
I_{\mathcal{C}}=\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}}
, a contradiction. Thus
I_{\mathcal{C}}\cap k[u]^{\ast}=\emptyset
and so we can further localise at
\mathcal{S}=k[u]^{\ast}
and conclude that
I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is a proper prime ideal of
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
.
Now consider
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
. We have
\widetilde{R}=k\langle u,x,h,h^{-1}\rangle
and
h^{-2}=(ux+2)
whence

. Thus
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}=\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{C}}
is a localisation of
k(u)[h,h^{-1}]
.

The advantage of working in
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is that we can simplify our derivation
\widetilde{\partial}_{2}
. On
\widetilde{R}
and

\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
write
\partial_{u}=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}
and
\partial_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
. Then, as derivations on either ring,

\partial_{1}=-(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}xu+2)\partial_{u}\ -\ \textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}\partial_{x}\

while

\widetilde{\partial}_{2}\ =\ -\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}hu^{2}\partial_{u}\ +\ h({\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}ux+2)\partial_{x}.

We now set
\mu:=-hu^{2}(ux+4)^{-1}
and take

\widetilde{\partial}_{2}^{\prime}\ :=\ \widetilde{\partial}_{2}+\mu\partial_{1}=\Bigl(-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}hu^{2}+\mu(-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}ux-2)\Bigr)\partial_{u}\ +\ \Bigl(h({\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}ux+2)-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}\mu\Bigr)\partial_{x}.

This element
\mu
has been chosen so that the coefficient of
\partial_{u}
in
\widetilde{\partial}_{2}^{\prime}
is

-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(ux+4)^{-1}\Bigl(hu^{2}(ux+4)-(ux+4)hu^{2}\bigr)\ =\ 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\partial}_{2}^{\prime}\ =

\displaystyle\Bigl(h({\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}ux+2)\ +\ \textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}hx^{2}u^{2}(ux+4)^{-1}\Bigr)\partial_{x}

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle(ux+4)^{-1}h\Bigl(({\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}ux+2)(ux+4)+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}x^{2}u^{2}\Bigr)\partial_{x}

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle(ux+4)^{-1}h\Bigl(2u^{2}x^{2}+8ux+8\Bigr)\partial_{x}

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\beta\partial_{x}\qquad\textit{for}\quad\beta\ :=\ 2(xu+4)^{-1}(ux+2)^{2}h.

Since
I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is invariant under both
\partial_{1}
and
\widetilde{\partial}_{2}
, it is also invariant under
\widetilde{\partial}_{2}^{\prime}
. Since
\beta
is a unit in
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
, it follows that

(4.20)

I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}\textit{ is also invariant under }\beta^{-1}\widetilde{\partial}_{2}^{\prime}=\partial_{x}.

Thus, by (
4.20
) and the expression given above for
\partial_{1}
,
I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is invariant under
(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}ux+2)\partial_{u}
, and therefore under
\partial_{u}
since
\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}ux+2
is a unit. So
I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is invariant under
\partial_{u}
and
\partial_{x}
. Since
\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
is a localisation of

this forces
I_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}=\widetilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{S}}
, giving the required contradiction.
∎

In order to pass between

and

we need:

Lemma 4.12

If

.

Suppose that

The following gives evidence in favour of

Corollary 5.4

Proof.

We check first using the description of the primitive spectrum in
