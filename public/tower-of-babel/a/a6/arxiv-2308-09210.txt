Efficient Algorithms for Attributed Graph Alignment with Vanishing Edge Correlation
Ziao Wang, Weina Wang, and Lele Wang

Abstract
Graph alignment refers to the task of finding the vertex correspondence between two correlated graphs of n n vertices. Extensive study has been done on polynomial-time algorithms for the graph alignment problem under the Erdős–Rényi graph pair model, where the two graphs are Erdős–Rényi graphs with edge probability q u {q_{\mathrm{u}}} , correlated under certain vertex correspondence. To achieve exact recovery of the correspondence, all existing algorithms at least require the edge correlation coefficient ρ u {\rho_{\mathrm{u}}} between the two graphs to be non-vanishing as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty . Moreover, it is conjectured that no polynomial-time algorithm can achieve exact recovery under vanishing edge correlation ρ u < 1 / polylog ⁡ ( n ) {\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}<1/\mathrm{polylog}(n) . In this paper, we show that with a vanishing amount of additional attribute information , exact recovery is polynomial-time feasible under vanishing edge correlation ρ u ≥ n − Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}\geq n^{-\Theta(1)} . We identify a local tree structure, which incorporates one layer of user information and one layer of attribute information, and apply the subgraph counting technique to such structures. A polynomial-time algorithm is proposed that recovers the vertex correspondence for most of the vertices, and then refines the output to achieve exact recovery. The consideration of attribute information is motivated by real-world applications like LinkedIn and Twitter, where user attributes like birthplace and education background can aid alignment.

Introduction

Graph alignment for the Erdős–Rényi graph pair.

The graph alignment problem, also referred to as the graph matching or noisy graph isomorphism problem, is the problem of finding the correspondence between the vertices of two correlated graphs. This problem has garnered significant attention due to its widespread use in various real-world applications
(
Singh et al., 2007
;
Cho and Lee, 2012
;
Haghighi et al., 2005
;
Korula and Lattanzi, 2014
;
Narayanan and Shmatikov, 2009
)
.
The graph alignment problem has been studied under various graph generation models. Among those models, the most well-studied one is the
Erdős–Rényi graph pair model

\mathcal{G}(n,{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}})
by
Pedarsani and Grossglauser, 2011
. In this model, let
\Pi^{*}
denote an underlying permutation on
[n]\triangleq\{1,\ldots,n\}
drawn uniformly at random. We generate two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}^{\prime}
with vertex set
[n]
as follows: For integers
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
, the pair of indicators
(\mathbbm{1}_{\{(i,j)\in G_{1}\}},\mathbbm{1}_{\{(\Pi^{*}(i),\Pi^{*}(j))\in G_{2}^{\prime}\}})
are i.i.d pairs of Bernoulli random variables with mean
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
and correlation coefficient
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}
. Given a pair of graphs
(G_{1},G_{2}^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{G}(n,{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}})
, the goal is to exactly recover the underlying permutation
\Pi^{*}
.

Two main lines of study have emerged in the graph alignment problem. In the first line, researchers assume unlimited computational resources and study the range of graph parameters
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
and
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}
such that recovering the permutation
\Pi^{*}
is feasible or infeasible
(
Pedarsani and Grossglauser, 2011
;
Cullina and Kiyavash, 2016
;
Cullina and Kiyavash, 2017
;
Wu et al., 2022
)
. This is commonly known as the
information-theoretic limit
. The second line of study focuses on the region of graph statistics where exact alignment is feasible with polynomial-time algorithms
(
Dai et al., 2019
;
Fan et al., 2020
;
Ding et al., 2021
;
Mao et al., 2023a
;
Mao et al., 2021
;
Mao et al., 2023b
;
Ding and Li, 2023
)
. This region is commonly known as the polynomial-time feasible region.
Compared to the information-theoretic feasible region, the best-known polynomial-time feasible region requires further assumptions regarding the edge correlation coefficient
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}
. To the best of our knowledge, the best-known polynomial-time feasible region is achieved together by
Mao et al., 2023b
and
Ding and Li, 2023

Attributed graph alignment.

As mentioned above, it is computationally challenging to achieve exact recovery in the low-correlation regime using only the vertex connection information. This motivates us to exploit additional side information about vertices, termed
attributes
, which are often widely available in real-world applications, to facilitate graph alignment.
Examples of publicly available attribute information include (1) LinkedIn user profiles containing age, birthplace, and affiliation and (2) Netflix users’ movie-watching histories and ratings.
The utilization of attribute information has been considered in various of graph inference tasks such as community detection
(
Deshpande et al., 2018
;
Saad and Nosratinia, 2020
;
Lu and Sen, 2023
)
, link prediction
(

;
Nie et al., 2024
)
and so on.
In the context of graph alignment, a natural question is how does the existence of such attribute information facilitate the matching process?

Why can we achieve diminishing correlation coefficient with attributes?

The proposed attributed graph alignment algorithm is based on the technique of
subgraph counting
, which is widely applied to various network analysis problems including cluster identification
(
Mossel et al., 2014
)
, hypothesis testing
(
Mao et al., 2022
)
and graph alignment under the Erdős–Rényi graph pair model
(
Barak et al., 2019
;
Mao et al., 2023b
)
.
In this work, we identify a family of local tree structures involving both users and attributes. The family of trees are indexed by the
\binom{m}{k}
size-
k
attribute subsets, and each of them comprises
k
independent length-
2
paths connecting the root node
i
to the
k
distinct attributes within its index subset, passing through some user vertices. An example of an attributed tree from this family is depicted in Figure
2(a)
.
By counting the occurrence of these simple tree structures rooted at each user
i
, we are able to construct a feature vector of length
\binom{m}{k}
for each
i
. Utilizing the similarity of these feature vectors, we can successfully align almost all users under a vanishing edge correlation
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}=n^{-\Theta(1)}
.

In the context of graph alignment without attributes, a polynomial-time algorithm based on subgraph counting is proposed by
Mao et al., 2023b
. This algorithm relies on counting a family of subgraph structures called the
chandeliers
. Chandeliers are delicately designed trees rooted at

i

. See Figure
2(b)
for an example of a chandelier. By counting the chandeliers, the algorithm proposed by
Mao et al., 2023b
can correctly align almost all the vertices under a constant correlation
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}>\sqrt{\alpha}
.

When comparing chandeliers to the local attributed tree structures counted in this work, we observe a key distinction: while the local attributed trees connect the root
i
to a set of
labelled
attributes, the chandeliers connect the root
i
to a set of
unlabelled
bulbs. As the bulbs are unlabelled trees, it remains uncertain whether a bulb observed in
G_{1}
matches a same bulb observed in
G_{2}^{\prime}
. Consequently, bulbs can be considered as a form of
noisy
signal for detecting vertex correspondence, whereas the attributes in the proposed tree structures provide a
noiseless
signal. This crucial distinction renders the proposed algorithm more robust against edge perturbations between the two graphs, enabling a wider range of edge correlation
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}
in the feasible region.
Another distinction between the attributed trees and the chandeliers lies in the length of the paths. In the attributed trees, the length of each path is fixed to
2
, while in the chandeliers, the path length between the root and a bulb goes to infinity as
n
grows.
In subgraph structures, the vertices on the paths are unlabelled, and thus, a longer wire length would introduce higher noise levels in the subgraph counting algorithm. In chandeliers, the wire length needs to be large enough to counterbalance the noise induced by the overlap in bulb structures. But in our case, as the attributes are individual vertices, we can set the path length to the minimum value of
2
, thereby reducing noise in the alignment process.

Main Results and Proposed Algorithms

In this section, we present characterizations of polynomial-time feasible regions and our proposed polynomial-time algorithms that achieve these feasible regions.
In Section

p
and
q
are the edge probabilities in the base graph for user-user connection and user-attribute connection, and
s_{\mathrm{u}}
and
s_{\mathrm{a}}
are the subsampling probabilities for user-user edges and user-attribute edges. Under this model, condition (
6
) is equivalent to
nps_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}+mqs_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\geq(1+\epsilon)\log n
, which is the information-theoretic limit for exact recovery for a certain range of graph parameters
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
. Condition (
7
) represents the mild condition that the base graph edge probabilities
p
and
q
are bounded away from
1
.

II-B

Proposed subgraph counting algorithm for almost exact recovery

In this section, we introduce the proposed subgraph counting algorithm. For graph
G_{1}
, let
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}\triangleq{A^{\mathrm{u}}}-{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
denote the

normalized matrix with
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{ij}=A^{\mathrm{u}}_{ij}-{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
, and
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}\triangleq{A^{\mathrm{a}}}-{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
denote the

normalized matrix with
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{ij}=A^{\mathrm{a}}_{ij}-{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
.
For graph

, we similarly define the two normalized adjacency matrices
\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}\triangleq B^{\mathrm{u}}-{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
and
\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}\triangleq B^{\mathrm{a}}-{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
. As a result, all the off-diagonal entries of
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}
and
\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}
and all entries of
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}
and
\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}
have zero means.

Suppose we have an attributed graph

with
{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)\subseteq[n]
and
{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)\subseteq{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{a}}}

in graph

as

(8)

Similarly, we define the weight of
S
in graph
G_{2}^{\prime}
as

\omega_{2}(S)\triangleq\prod_{e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\prod_{f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}.

(9)

Note in the definitions above, graph

is not necessarily a subgraph of
G_{1}
or
G_{2}^{\prime}
. Instead,

is a subgraph of the complete attributed graph defined on user set
[n]
and attribute set

. We provide an example of computing the weight

for some

in Figure

.

With the weight of a subgraph defined, we introduce the family of subgraphs we propose to count. Fix a user vertex

and an attribute subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{a}}}
of size

are trees rooted at

. Each tree has
k

2
. The
k
vertices one hop away from the root

are user vertices from
[n]\setminus\{i\}
, and the
k
vertices two hops away from the root

are the

\mathcal{A}
. In other words,
{\mathcal{G}}_{i,\mathcal{A}}
includes all the ways of forming

length-

to the
k
attributes in
\mathcal{A}
. We notice that for any

of size

(10)

This is because in the graph structure we mentioned above, we only have the flexibility to choose the

user vertices that are one hop away from

out of
n-1
users in
[n]\setminus\{i\}
, and the order of choosing those user vertices matters. Given a fixed user vertex

and an attribute subset

of size
k
, we define the weighted count of the collection

in

as

W_{i,\mathcal{A}}(G_{1})\triangleq\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,\mathcal{A}}}\omega_{1}(S).

(11)

Similarly we define the subgraph count in

as

W_{i,\mathcal{A}}(G_{2}^{\prime})\triangleq\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,\mathcal{A}}}\omega_{2}(S).

(12)

In the proposed graph counting algorithm, for each user vertex

, we compute

for each attribute subset
\mathcal{A}
of size

, and use them to form a feature vector of length
\binom{m}{k}
. For each user vertex

Given the feature vectors, for a pair of user vertices

(Complexity of Algorithm

The time complexity of Algorithm

Remark 3

(Choice of parameter

Regarding the choice of

Remark 4

The intuition behind the proposed statistic becomes clearer if we consider the

III
Main Ideas in Achieving Almost Exact Recovery

In this section, we establish Theorem
1
based on three intermediate propositions. We defer the proofs of the intermediate propositions to Appendices
A
and
B
. From this point, we assume without loss of generality that the underlying permutation
\Pi^{*}
is the identity permutation.

III-A

First moment of similarity score

For the similarity score
\Phi_{ij}
to be an appropriate measure of the similarity between two user vertices, we need the expected similarity score between a correct pair
{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]
to be larger than that between a wrong pair
{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]
. The following proposition shows that
{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]>0
, while
{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]=0
.

Proposition 1

(Expectation of similarity score)
.

For a pair of users
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}_{1}}\times{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}_{2}}
, the expectation of the similarity score
\Phi_{ij}
is given by

{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]=\binom{m}{k}({\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2})^{k}({\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2})^{k}\binom{n-1}{k}k!\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=j\}}.

(14)

Moreover, if
k=\Theta(1)
, then

{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]=\Omega(m^{k}n^{k}({\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2})^{k}({\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2})^{k}).

(15)

III-B

Second moment of similarity score

In the proposed algorithm, we separate almost all the correct pairs of matching from the wrong pairs by setting a threshold on the similarity score.
For this algorithm to achieve almost exact recovery, requiring only that the correct pairs have a higher expected similarity score is not enough. We further need that the second moment of the similarity score is small enough, so that the similarity scores concentrate well around their means. In the following two propositions, we provide upper bounds on the similarity score between correct pairs and between wrong pairs, respectively.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose graph parameters
n,m,{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{q_{\mathrm{a}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}
satisfy conditions
n{q_{\mathrm{u}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}=\omega(1),

n\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}=\omega(1),

m{q_{\mathrm{a}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}=\omega\left(\frac{1}{n{q_{\mathrm{u}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}\right),

m{q_{\mathrm{a}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}=\omega\left(\frac{1}{n\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}}\right)
and
m\rho_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}=\omega(1)
. Assume parameter
k
in Algorithm
1
is set to a constant. Then for any
i\in[n]
, we have
\frac{{\mathrm{Var}}(\Phi_{ii})}{{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]^{2}}=o(1).

Proposition 3
.

Suppose graph parameters
n,m,{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{q_{\mathrm{a}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}
satisfy conditions
n\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}=\omega(n^{2/k}),

n^{2}{q_{\mathrm{u}}}=\Omega(1),

m{q_{\mathrm{a}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}=\omega\left(\frac{n^{2/k}}{n\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}}\right)
and
m\rho_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\rho_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}=\omega(n^{2/k}).
Assume parameter
k
in Algorithm
1
is set to a constant.
Then for any
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
with
i\neq j
, we have

\frac{{\mathrm{Var}}(\Phi_{ij})}{{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]^{2}}=o(1/n^{2}).

We comment that the conditions presented in Propositions
2
and
3
are not only sufficient, but also necessary for establishing the variance bounds for the similarity score. We discuss more details about the tightness of these conditions in Appendix
E
.

III-C

Proof of Theorem
1

Given Propositions
1
-
3
, we are ready to prove Theorem
1
. It is easy to check that all conditions in Propositions
2
and
3
are satisfied under conditions (
1
)-(
4
).
To prove Theorem
1
, we define error event
\mathcal{E}_{1}\triangleq\{\exists(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]\colon i\neq j,\Phi_{ij}\geq\tau\}
. Moreover, let
X\triangleq|\{i\in[n]\colon\Phi_{ii}<\tau\}|
. We define error event
\mathcal{E}_{2}\triangleq\{X\geq\lambda n\}
for some
\lambda=o(1)
, which will be specified later.
We claim that it suffices to show
{\mathsf{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2})=o(1)
. To see this, we observe that
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}
represents the event that all wrong pairs have similarity score less than the threshold, which implies that
\hat{\Pi}(i)=\Pi^{*}(i),\forall i\in I
. On event
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c}
implies the event that
|I|\geq n-o(n)
.

Firstly, we show that
{\mathsf{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})=o(1)
. For a wrong pair
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
with
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ij}\geq\tau)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ij}-{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]\geq c{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{{\mathrm{Var}}(\Phi_{ij})}{c^{2}{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}o(1/n^{2}),

where (a) follows because
\tau=c{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]
from Proposition
1
, (b) follows by Chebyshev’s inequality and (c) follows by Proposition
3
.
Applying a union bound over the
n^{2}-n
wrong pairs of users yields that
{\mathsf{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})=o(1)
.

Secondly, let
\lambda=\sqrt{{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ii}<\tau)}
. We show that
{\mathsf{P}}(\mathcal{E}_{2})=o(1)
in the following.
For the similarity score
\Phi_{ii}
for a correct pair, we have

{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ii}<\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ii}-{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]\leq(c-1){\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}\frac{{\mathrm{Var}}(\Phi_{ii})}{(c-1)^{2}{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ii}]^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{=}}o(1),

(16)

where (d) follows by Proposition
1
, (e) follows by Chebyshev’s inequality and (f) follows by Proposition
2
. From (
16
), we have
\lambda=o(1)
. By the linearity of expectation, we know that
{\mathsf{E}}[X]=n{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ii}<\tau)
. By Markov’s inequality, it follows that

{\mathsf{P}}(X\geq\lambda n)\leq\frac{{\mathsf{E}}[X]}{\lambda n}=\sqrt{{\mathsf{P}}(\Phi_{ii}<\tau)}=o(1).

Finally, applying a union bound over
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
completes the proof.

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\left[\prod_{e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\prod_{f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\prod_{g\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(T)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g}\prod_{h\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e^{*}}]{\mathsf{E}}\left[\prod_{e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)\setminus\{e^{*}\}}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\prod_{f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\prod_{g\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(T)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{g}\prod_{h\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T)}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}0,

(18)

where (c) follows because
e^{*}
only appears in
S
, so
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e^{*}}
in independent of the rest of the terms in the product, and (d) follows because each single term in adjacency matrix
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}
has zero mean. Similarly, if
e^{*}\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)
, we still have
{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(T)]=0
. Therefore, the claim holds. The claim further implies that
{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]=0
for any pair
i\neq j
. To see this, we notice that
{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{j,{\mathcal{A}}}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
, and it follows that each term in the summation (
17
) equals to zero.

Now, we can rewrite the summation (
17
) as

\sum_{\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}}:|\mathcal{A}|=k}\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}}\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{G}}_{j,{\mathcal{A}}}}{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(T)]=\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=j\}}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}}:|\mathcal{A}|=k}\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}}{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(S)].

(19)

By symmetry, we notice that for any
|\mathcal{A}|=k
and any
S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}
, the term
\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(S)
has exactly the same distribution. Therefore, we fix a set
{\mathcal{A}}
and a graph
S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}
, and consider the expectation
{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(S)]
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S)\omega_{2}(S)]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\left[\prod_{e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\prod_{f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{=}}\prod_{e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)}{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]\prod_{f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)}{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{=}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}})^{k}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}})^{k},

(20)

where (e) follows by the independence of the terms in the product and (f) follows because
for each
e\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{u}}}(S)
,

{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]={\mathrm{Cov}}(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e},\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})+{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}

and for each
f\in{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S)
,

{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}]={\mathrm{Cov}}(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f},\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f})+{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}]{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{f}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}.

Finally, plugging (
A
) into (
19
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\Phi_{ij}]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=j\}}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{u}}}:|\mathcal{A}|=k}\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}})^{k}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}})^{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(g)}}{{=}}\binom{m}{k}\binom{n-1}{k}k!(\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}})^{k}(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}})^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i=j\}},

(21)

where (g) follows because there are
\binom{m}{k}
attribute subsets of size
k
and for each
|{\mathcal{A}}|=k
,
|{\mathcal{G}}_{i,{\mathcal{A}}}|=\binom{n-1}{k}k!
as given in (
10
).

To see (
15
), we simply need to notice that
\binom{m}{k}=\Omega(m^{k})
and
\binom{n-1}{k}k!=\Omega(n^{k})
when
k=\Theta(1)
.
∎

Now, we focus on one single term

Define the indicator

To see the claim, we observe that if

S_{1}=S_{2}
,

Furthermore, we notice that by the definition of covariance, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Cov}}(\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2}),\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2}))

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})]-{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})]{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})]

Now, we focus on the cross moment

{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})]={\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})]f_{2}(S_{1},S_{2},T_{1},T_{2}).

The reason for the claim is similar to the reason for (

Plugging (

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}(\Phi_{ij})

For the user-attribute edges in

\displaystyle K_{11}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle K_{21}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{1})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{1}))\cap({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{2})\triangle{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{2})),

\displaystyle K_{12}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{1})\triangle{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{1}))\cap({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{2})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{2})),

\displaystyle K_{22}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{1})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{1}))\cap({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{2})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{2})),

\displaystyle K_{02}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{1})\cup{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{1}))^{c}\cap({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{2})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{2})),

\displaystyle K_{20}^{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\triangleq({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{1})\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{1}))\cap({\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(S_{2})\cup{\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{a}}}(T_{2}))^{c}.

For the similar reason as the user-user edges, for an edge

its contribution to the product
\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}[\omega_{1}(S_{1})\omega_{2}(S_{2})\omega_{1}(T_{1})\omega_{2}(T_{2})]

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\left[\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\prod_{e\in K^{\mathrm{u}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}}(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\prod_{e\in K^{\mathrm{a}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}}(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\prod_{e\in K^{\mathrm{u}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\right)\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\prod_{e\in K^{\mathrm{a}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\left({\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\right)^{|K^{\mathrm{u}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}|}\right)\left(\prod_{m_{1},m_{2}}\left({\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\right)^{|K^{\mathrm{a}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}|}\right)

Now, we need to consider the cross moments
{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e},)^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]

{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]

(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e},\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})
is a pair of Bernoulli random variables with correlation coefficient

and marginal probability
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}
, and
(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e},\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})
is a pair of Bernoulli random variables with correlation coefficient
{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}
and marginal probability
{q_{\mathrm{a}}}
.
We bound the cross moments in terms of the graph parameters
{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}},{q_{\mathrm{a}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}
in the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Cross moments upper bound)
.

Suppose
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
{q_{\mathrm{a}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then there exists a constant
c
such that

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-m_{1}-m_{2}}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\leq\begin{cases}{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(1,1),\\
1&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,0)\text{ or }(0,2),\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,1)\text{ or }(1,2),\\
c\max\{1,\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}\}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2),\end{cases}

(31)

and

\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^{-m_{1}-m_{2}}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{1}}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{a}}_{e})^{m_{2}}]\leq\begin{cases}{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(1,1),\\
1&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,0)\text{ or }(0,2),\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{a}}}}}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,1)\text{ or }(1,2),\\
c\max\{1,\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{a}}}}\}&\text{ if }(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2).\end{cases}

(32)

Remark 7
.

The first three inequalities of (
31
) and (
32
) were originally established by
Mao et al., 2022
. In Lemma
1
, we tighten the bounds specifically for the case of
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2)
, ensuring that the bounds are tight up to a constant factor for any
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}=O(1)
and
{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}=O(1)
.

Proof of Lemma
1
..

We firstly show (
31
) by separately consider different pairs of
(m_{1},m_{2})
.

•

Suppose
(m_{1},m_{2})=(0,2)\text{ or }(2,0)
, then we have

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-2}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-2}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}]=1.

•

Suppose
(m_{1},m_{2})=(1,1)
, then we have

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-2}{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-2}{\mathrm{Cov}}(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e},\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})={\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}.

•

Suppose
(m_{1},m_{2})=(1,2)\text{ or }(2,1)
, then we have

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}]=\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-3}{\mathsf{E}}[\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}]=\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}}.

Because
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have
\frac{1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}}
. It follows that

\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}}\leq\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}}.

•

Suppose
(m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2)
, then we have

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-4}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}]=\frac{q_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})^{2}+{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}{q_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})(1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}})^{2}}{q_{\mathrm{u}}^{2}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})^{2}}=1+\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}})^{2}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}.

Because
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have
\frac{(1-2{q_{\mathrm{u}}})^{2}}{1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}\leq 1
, and it follows that

\sigma_{\mathrm{u}}^{-4}{\mathsf{E}}[(\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}(\tilde{B}^{\mathrm{u}}_{e})^{2}]\leq 1+\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}.

To complete the proof of (
31
), we just need to realize that
1+\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}=\Theta(\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}})
when
\frac{{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}\geq 1
, and

Appendix D
Comparison to Existing works for Seeded Graph Alignment

In this section, we compare the feasible region of the proposed algorithms to the best-known feasible region under the context of seeded graph alignment.
In the seeded Erdős–Rényi graph pair model
\mathcal{G}(N,\alpha,p,s)
by
Mossel and Xu, 2020
, the graph generation process is described as follows: First, a base graph
G
is sampled from Erdős–Rényi model
\mathcal{G}(N,p)
. Next, two correlated graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are produced by independently retaining each edge from
G
with a probability
s
. The graph
G_{2}^{\prime}
is then generated by applying a random permutation
\Pi^{*}
to
G_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
denote the sets of vertices in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}^{\prime}
respectively. A subset
\mathcal{V}_{s}\subset\mathcal{V}_{1}
of size
\lfloor N\alpha\rfloor
is then selected uniformly at random, and the vertex pairs
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{(v,\Pi^{*}(v)):v\in\mathcal{V}_{s}\}
are identified as the seed set. The graph pair
(G_{1},G_{2}^{\prime})
and the seed set
\mathcal{I}_{0}
are provided, and the goal is to recover the vertex correspondence for all the remaining vertices.

In the attributed Erdős–Rényi graph pair model
\mathcal{G}(n,{q_{\mathrm{u}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}};m,{q_{\mathrm{a}}},{\rho_{\mathrm{a}}})
, when we set
{q_{\mathrm{u}}}={q_{\mathrm{a}}}
and
{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}={\rho_{\mathrm{a}}}
, the
m
attributes can be viewed as seeds, and graph pair can be viewed as generated from the seeded Erdős–Rényi graph pair model
\mathcal{G}(n+m,\frac{m}{n+m},\frac{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}+{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})},{q_{\mathrm{u}}}+{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}}))
except that the edges between the seeds are removed. Therefore, the feasible region of the proposed algorithms for attributed graph alignment directly implies feasible region for seeded graph alignment.
In the following, we compare the feasible region of the proposed algorithms to that of the algorithms by
Mossel and Xu, 2020
, and demonstrate that the implied feasible region covers certain parameter regimes that are unknown from the literature. For simplicity, we denote
N\triangleq n+m
,
\alpha\triangleq\frac{m}{m+n}
,
p\triangleq\frac{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}}{{q_{\mathrm{u}}}+{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})}
and
s\triangleq{q_{\mathrm{u}}}+{\rho_{\mathrm{u}}}(1-{q_{\mathrm{u}}})
. Firstly, the feasible region by
Mossel and Xu, 2020
requires a constant correlation
s=\Theta(1)
, while our feasible region includes both cases of
s=o(1)
and
s=\Theta(1)
. In the rest of the comparison, we consider constant correlation regime
s=\Theta(1)
. In this regime, we demonstrate that the seed fraction
\alpha
required by the proposed algorithms is strictly smaller in the following two cases:

Case 1:
np=n^{c}
for some constant
1/2<c<1
.
In this case, one can check that conditions (
1
), (
3
) and (
4
) in Theorem
1
, and conditions (
6
) and (
7
) in Theorem
2
are satisfied without any further assumptions. To satisfy condition (
2
), we need to set
m=\omega(n^{\epsilon})
for an arbitrarily small
\epsilon
. This is equivalent as
\alpha=\omega(n^{\epsilon-1})
. In contrast, Theorem 4 in
Mossel and Xu, 2020
requires at least
\alpha=\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n^{c}})
.

Case 2:
np=n^{c}
for some constant
1/3<c<1/2
.
In this case, one can check that conditions (
1
) and (
3
) in Theorem
1
, and conditions (
6
) and (
7
) in Theorem
2
are satisfied without any further assumptions. For conditions (
2
) and (
4
), we need to set
m=\omega(n^{1-2c})
. This is equivalent as
\alpha=\omega(n^{-2c})
. In contrast, Theorem 4 in
Mossel and Xu, 2020
requires at least
\alpha=\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n^{2c}})
.

To provide a fair comparison, we point to that when
np=n^{c}
for some
c=1/2
or
c\leq 1/3
or
c=o(1)
, the algorithms by
Mossel and Xu, 2020
requires strictly less seeds to achieve exact recovery.
