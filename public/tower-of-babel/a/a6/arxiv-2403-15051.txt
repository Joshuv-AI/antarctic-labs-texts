Mesoscopic Lattice Boltzmann modeling of dense gas flows in curvilinear geometries
Sergiu Busuioc

Abstract
We derive the Enskog equation utilizing orthonormal vielbein fields, enabling the utilization of arbitrary coordinate systems to characterize spatial geometry. Additionally, we employ an adapted coordinate system in the momentum space, connected to the physical space through vielbeins. Within this framework, the momentum component perpendicular to a curved boundary can be treated as an independent one, facilitating the application of half-range Gauss-Hermite quadratures. We develop an appropriate finite-difference Lattice Boltzmann model and validate it against a DSMC-like particle-based method for solving the Enskog equation in curvilinear geometries. Our test scenarios include cylindrical Couette flow, cylindrical Fourier flow between coaxial cylinders, and spherical Fourier flow between concentric spheres. Excellent agreement between the two approaches is observed throughout the parameter range and curvature-specific effects are well captured.



I
Introduction

Rarefied gas flows characterized by non-negligible Knudsen numbers (
Kn
), representing the ratio between the mean free path of fluid molecules and the characteristic length of the flow domain, have traditionally been investigated numerically using the Boltzmann equation, treating fluid constituents as point particles. However, when the mean free path becomes comparable to the particle size, the finite molecular size’s influence becomes critical
Ferziger and Kaper 1972
. Practical applications involving this scenario are the single-bubble sonoluminescence
Brenner et al. 2002
, high-pressure shock tubes
Petersen and Hanson 2001
, flows through microfabricated nanomembranes
Holt et al. 2006
and the gas extraction in unconventional reservoirs
Wu et al. 2016
;
Sander et al. 2017
. The Enskog equation
Enskog 1922
provides a means to extend the kinetic theory description beyond the dilute-gas Boltzmann limit. Unlike the Boltzmann approach, the Enskog equation accounts for the finite size of gas molecules, incorporating space correlations between colliding molecules, molecular mutual shielding, and reduction of available volume
Enskog 1922
;
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Ferziger and Kaper 1972
;
Kremer 2010
;
Dorfman et al. 2021
. Numerical solutions of the Enskog equation can be achieved through probabilistic or deterministic methodologies, mirroring the approaches used for the Boltzmann equation. Deterministic techniques, including the Monte Carlo quadrature method
Frezzotti and Sgarra 1993
, the fast spectral method
Wu et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2016
, and the Fokker-Planck approximation
Sadr and Gorji 2017
;
Sadr and Gorji 2019
, have been applied to tackle the Enskog equation. On the other hand, probabilistic methods have emerged, inspired by the success of the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo method (DSMC) pioneered by Alexander et al.
Alexander et al. 1995
, Montanero et al.
Montanero and Santos 1996
, and Frezzotti
Frezzotti 1997
. These methods have been applied to investigate dense gases near solid walls in micro- and nano-channels
Davis 1987
;
Din and Michaelides 1997
;
Frezzotti 1997
;
Frezzotti 1999
;
Nedea et al. 2006
;
Sheng et al. 2020
;
Su et al. 2023
. Its extension to weakly attracting hard-sphere systems has demonstrated efficacy in characterizing various phenomena, including monoatomic
Frezzotti et al. 2005
;
Kon et al. 2014
;
Frezzotti et al. 2019
;
Busuioc et al. 2020
, polyatomic fluids
Bruno and Frezzotti 2019
;
Busuioc and Gibelli 2020
, mixtures
Kobayashi et al. 2017
, the formation and rupture of liquid menisci in nanochannels
Barbante et al. 2015
, as well as the growth/collapse dynamics of spherical nano-droplets/bubbles
Busuioc et al. 2023
.
Despite their reliability, these methods are computationally intensive. To address this, a common approach is to simplify the Enskog collision integral by expanding it into a Taylor series. This simplification, utilized in models like Lattice Boltzmann (LB) models, has been successful in investigating non-ideal gases
Luo 1998
;
Luo 2000
;
Melchionna and Marconi 2007
and multiphase flows
He and Doolen 2002
. More recently, the simplified Enskog collision operator has been successfully implemented in various solvers, including the discrete unified gas kinetic scheme (DUGKS)
Chen et al. 2023
, the discrete velocity method (DVM)
Wang et al. 2020
;
Su et al. 2023
, the discrete Boltzmann method (DBM)
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Gan et al. 2022
, the double-distribution Lattice Boltzmann model (DDLB)
Huang et al. 2021
and the Finite-difference Lattice Boltzmann (FDLB) models
Busuioc 2023
;
Busuioc and Sofonea 2024
. These solvers offer computationally efficient alternatives for investigating micro-scale flow phenomena while maintaining reasonable accuracy.

In this paper, we further extend the recently introduced finite difference Lattice Boltzmann (FDLB) model for bounded dense gas flows
Busuioc and Sofonea 2024
, to tackle flows in curvilinear geometries. In the literature, it’s a common approach to leverage the symmetries inherent in non-Cartesian geometries through the use of curvilinear geometry-fitted coordinates
Guo and Zhao 2003
;
Li and Zhang 2009
;
Lin et al. 2014
;
Watari 2015
;
Hejranfar and Hajihassanpour 2017
;
Velasco et al. 2019
. These coordinates can be selected such that the boundary aligns orthogonally with the unit vector along one of the curvilinear axes. To ensure the implementation of the half-range quadratures
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
along the direction perpendicular to the boundary, an additional step is necessary: the momentum space must be adjusted to align with the new coordinate system, ensuring that the momentum vector components consistently align with the unit vectors corresponding to the curvilinear coordinates. In Ref.
Cardall et al. 2013
, the relativistic Boltzmann equation has been expressed in conservative form with respect to a vielbein (i.e. tetrad) field and a general choice for the parametrization of the momentum space. Later, in Ref.
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
, a formulation of the Boltzmann equation with respect to general coordinates has been presented. In order to keep the momentum space tied to the new coordinate frame, an orthonormal vielbein field (i.e. triad consisting of the non-holonomic unit vectors of the coordinate frame) has been employed, with respect to which the momentum space degrees of freedom are defined. The resulting Boltzmann equation contains inertial forces that ensure that freely streaming particles travel along straight lines in the original Cartesian geometry. Following
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
, we apply the same vielbein framework to the Enskog equation, in order to tackle the flow of dense gases in curvilinear geometries. This framework has been successfully applied to the torus geometry involving binary fluids
Ambruş et al. 2019
.

Furthermore, we demonstrate the applicability of our proposed scheme to dense gas flows enclosed inside curved boundaries by considering the cylindrical Couette flow and cylindrical Fourier flow between coaxial cylinders, as well as the spherical Fourier flow between concentric spheres. In particular, we use the cylindrical/spherical coordinates to parametrize the flow domain, such that the boundaries are orthogonal to the radial directions
R
and
r
, for cylindrical and spherical coordinates systems, respectively. After expressing the momentum space vectors with respect to the unit vectors, we employ the mixed-quadrature lattice Boltzmann (LB) models introduced in Ref.
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
. The mixed quadrature models allow the half-range or full-range Gauss-Hermite quadratures to be independently used on the coordinate axes of the momentum space. The implementation of the inertial forces requires the theory of distributions, as discussed in Ref.
Ambrus et al. 2017
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. For the readers’s convenience, in the first part of Sec.
II
, the simplified Enskog equation is presented along with the Shakhov collision term in Cartesian coordinates. The extension to general coordinates and the vielbein framework is discussed further as well as the cylindrical and spherical cases specific to the problems addressed in this study. The Finite Difference Lattice Boltzmann (FDLB) model with mixed quadratures used to numerically solve the simplified Enskog equation in cylindrically and spherically symmetric setups is briefly introduced in Sec.
III
. This model relies on half-range Gauss-Hermite quadratures in order to account for the boundary-induced discontinuities. The computer simulation results for the cylindrical Couette and the Fourier flows between coaxial cylinders, as well as for the Fourier flow between concentric spheres are reported in Sec.
IV
. We conclude the paper in Sec.
V
.

II
Lattice Boltzmann model in curvilinear geometries

The Enskog equation, proposed in 1922
Enskog 1922
, describes the evolution of a system consisting of rigid spherical molecules. Unlike Boltzmann’s equation, which assumes molecules as point-like particles subjected to local collisions, Enskog’s equation takes into account also the volume of the fluid molecules. This volume restricts the free movement space available to each particle, leading to an increased number of collisions. Additionally, the collisions between particles are non-local, occurring when the centers of the two colliding molecules are separated by one molecular diameter. The Enskog equation can be written as follows
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Kremer 2010
;
Dorfman et al. 2021
:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}}{m}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}}f+\bm{F}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{p}}f=J_{E}=\int\left\{\chi\left({\bm{x}}+\frac{\sigma}{2}{\bm{k}}\right)f({\bm{x}},\bm{p^{*}})f({\bm{x}}+\sigma{\bm{k}},\bm{p_{1}^{*}})\right.\\
-\left.\chi\left({\bm{x}}-\frac{\sigma}{2}{\bm{k}}\right)f({\bm{x}},\bm{p})f({\bm{x}}-\sigma{\bm{k}},\bm{p_{1}})\right\}\sigma^{2}({\bm{p_{r}}}\cdot{\bm{k}})d{\bm{k}}d{\bm{p_{1}}},

(1)

where
m
denotes the particle mass,
\bm{F}=m\bm{a}
represents the external body force,
f\equiv f(\bm{x},\bm{p},t)
is the single-particle distribution function,
\sigma
represents the molecular diameter,
\bm{p_{r}}=\bm{p_{1}}-\bm{p}
is the relative momentum, and
\bm{k}
is the unit vector specifying the relative position of the two colliding particles. The time dependence of the distribution function is omitted for brevity. At time
t
, the distribution function
f
provides the number of particles located within the phase space volume
d\bm{x}d\bm{p}
centered at the point
(\bm{x},\bm{p})
. The right-hand side of the equation above is the Enskog collision operator, denoted
J_{E}
.

The influence of the molecular diameter
\sigma
on the collision frequency is embedded within the contact value of the pair correlation function
\chi
. In the standard Enskog theory (SET),
\chi\equiv\chi_{\text{\tiny SET}}
is assessed at the contact point of two colliding particles within a fluid assumed to be in uniform equilibrium
Kremer 2010
. An approximate yet precise expression for
\chi_{\text{\tiny SET}}
reads:

\chi_{\text{\tiny SET}}[n]=\frac{1}{nb}\left(\frac{P^{hs}}{nk_{B}T}-1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{2-\eta}{(1-\eta)^{3}},

(2)

which is derived from the equation of state (EOS) for the hard-sphere fluid proposed by Carnahan and Starling
Carnahan and Starling 1969
. Here,
n
represents the particle number density,
\eta=b\rho/4
is the reduced particle density (
b=2\pi\sigma^{3}/3m
),
P^{hs}
is the pressure of the hard-sphere fluid,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, and
T
is the temperature. The square brackets in Eq. (
2
) indicate functional dependence.

In the revised Enskog theory (RET), the fluid is assumed to be in a non-uniform equilibrium state
Kremer 2010
;
Dorfman et al. 2021
;
Van Beijeren and Ernst 1973
, leading to a position-dependent particle number density. In this scenario, an effective approximation for the radial distribution function is obtained using the Fischer-Methfessel (FM) prescription
Fischer and Methfessel 1980
. This involves substituting the actual value of the particle number density
n
in Eq. (
2
) with the average particle density
\overline{n}
computed over a spherical volume of radius
\sigma
, centered at
\bm{x}-\frac{\sigma}{2}{\bm{k}}
:

\chi_{\mbox{\tiny RET-FM}}\left[n\Big(\bm{x}-\frac{\sigma}{2}{\bm{k}}\Big)\right]=\chi_{\mbox{\tiny SET}}\left[\overline{n}\Big(\bm{x}-\frac{\sigma}{2}{\bm{k}}\Big)\right].

(3)

The average particle density
\overline{n}
is defined as:

\overline{n}(\bm{x})=\frac{3}{4\pi\sigma^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}n(\bm{x}^{\prime})w(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\,d\bm{x}^{\prime},\quad w(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\qquad\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}\|<\sigma,\\
0,&\qquad\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}\|\geq\sigma.\end{array}\right.

(4)

The Enskog collision operator
J_{E}
can be viewed as an extension of the Boltzmann collision operator which accounts for particles with spatial extent. As the molecular diameter
\sigma
tends towards zero, the contact value of the pair correlation function approaches unity (
\chi\rightarrow 1
), thereby recovering the Boltzmann collision operator
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Kremer 2010
.

Assuming that the pair correlation function
\chi
and the distribution functions appearing in the Enskog collision integral
J_{E}
are smooth around the contact point
\bm{x}
, we can approximate these functions using a Taylor series expansion around
\bm{x}
, leading to the simplified Enskog collision operator
J_{E}\approx J_{0}+J_{1}

Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Kremer 2010
:

\displaystyle J_{0}\equiv J_{0}[f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi\int(f^{*}f_{1}^{*}-ff_{1})\sigma^{2}({\bm{p_{r}}}\cdot{\bm{k}})d{\bm{k}}d{\bm{p_{1}}},

(5)

\displaystyle J_{1}\equiv J_{1}[f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi\sigma\int\bm{k}(f^{*}\bm{\nabla}f_{1}^{*}+f\bm{\nabla}f_{1})\sigma^{2}({\bm{p_{r}}}\cdot{\bm{k}})d{\bm{k}}d{\bm{p_{1}}}

(6)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}\int\bm{k}\bm{\nabla}\chi(f^{*}f_{1}^{*}+ff_{1})\sigma^{2}({\bm{p_{r}}}\cdot{\bm{k}})d{\bm{k}}d{\bm{p_{1}}}.

The term
J_{0}[f]
corresponds to the conventional collision term of the Boltzmann equation multiplied by
\chi
and is treated as such by applying the relaxation time approximation. In this study, we utilize the Shakhov collision term
Shakhov 1968a
;
Shakhov 1968b
:

J_{0}[f]=-\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{S}),

(7)

where
\tau
represents the relaxation time and
f^{S}
is the equilibrium Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution multiplied by a correction factor
Shakhov 1968a
;
Shakhov 1968b
;
Graur and Polikarpov 2009
;
Ambruş et al. 2020
:

f^{S}=f^{\text{\tiny MB}}\left[1+\frac{1-\text{Pr}}{P_{i}k_{B}T}\left(\frac{\bm{\xi}^{2}}{5mk_{B}T}-1\right)\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{q}\right].

(8)

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
f^{\text{\tiny MB}}
is defined as

f^{\text{\tiny MB}}=\frac{n}{(2m\pi k_{B}T)^{3/2}}\exp{\left(-\frac{\bm{\xi}^{2}}{2mk_{B}T}\right)},

(9)

and the heat flux
\bm{q}
is obtained using
\bm{q}=\int d^{3}pf\frac{\bm{\xi}^{2}}{2m}\frac{\bm{\xi}}{m},
where
\bm{\xi}=\bm{p}-m\bm{u}
represents the peculiar momentum,
\text{Pr}=c_{P}\mu/\lambda
denotes the Prandtl number,
c_{P}=5k_{B}/2m
is the specific heat at constant pressure,
\mu
is the shear viscosity,
\lambda
is the thermal conductivity, and
P_{i}=\rho{\cal{G}}T=nk_{B}T
is the ideal gas equation of state, with
\mathcal{G}
being the specific gas constant.
It is important to note that although the Shakhov model does not guarantee non-negativity of the correction factor and the H-theorem has not been proven, the model has been successfully implemented and its accuracy has been tested through comparisons with experimental
Sharipov 2002
;
Sharipov 2003
;
Graur and Polikarpov 2009
or DSMC
Ambruş and Sofonea 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Ambruş et al. 2020
;
Todorova et al. 2020
results.

The term
J_{1}[f]
can be approximated by replacing the distribution functions (
f^{*},f_{1}^{*},f,f_{1}
) with their corresponding equilibrium distribution functions and integrating over
\bm{k}
and
\bm{p_{1}}
to obtain
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Kremer 2010
:

J_{1}[f]\approx J_{1}[f^{\text{\tiny MB}}]=-b\rho\chi f^{\text{\tiny MB}}\left\{\bm{\xi}\cdot\left[\bm{\nabla}\ln(\rho^{2}\chi T)+\frac{3}{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\bm{\nabla}\ln T\right]\right.\\
\left.+\frac{2}{5}\left[2\bm{\zeta}\bm{\zeta}\bm{:\nabla u}+\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\bm{\nabla\cdot u}\right]\right\},

(10)

where
\bm{\zeta}=\bm{\xi}/\sqrt{2RT}
. Incorporating these approximations, the Enskog equation Eq. (
1
) can be expressed as:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{\bm{p}}{m}\bm{\nabla}{\bm{x}}f+\bm{F}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{p}}f=-\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{S})+J_{1}[f^{\text{\tiny MB}}].

(11)

II.1
Enskog equation in curvilinear coordinates

In certain situations, it is convenient to introduce a set of arbitrary coordinates
\{x^{\widetilde{1}},x^{\widetilde{2}},x^{\widetilde{3}}\}
, where
x^{\widetilde{\imath}}\equiv x^{\widetilde{\imath}}(x,y,z)
(in this paper, we restrict our analysis to time-independent coordinate transformations). This coordinate transformation
induces a metric
g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}
, as follows:

ds^{2}=\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}=g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}dx^{\widetilde{\imath}}dx^{\widetilde{\jmath}},

(12)

such that

g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}=\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial x^{i}}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\imath}}}\frac{\partial x^{j}}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\jmath}}}.

(13)

The Enskog equation Eq. (
1
) can be written with respect to these new coordinates as follows:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{p^{\widetilde{\imath}}}{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\imath}}}+\left(F^{\widetilde{\imath}}-\frac{1}{m}\Gamma^{\widetilde{\imath}}{}_{{\widetilde{\jmath}}{\widetilde{k}}}p^{\widetilde{\jmath}}p^{\widetilde{k}}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\widetilde{\imath}}}=J_{E}[f],

(14)

where the components
p^{\widetilde{\imath}}
and
F^{\widetilde{\imath}}
(with respect to the new coordinates) are related to the components
p^{i}
and
F^{i}
(expressed with respect to the old coordinates) through
p^{\widetilde{\imath}}=\frac{\partial x^{{\widetilde{\imath}}}}{\partial x^{i}}p^{i},\quad F^{\widetilde{\imath}}=\frac{\partial x^{{\widetilde{\imath}}}}{\partial x^{i}}F^{i}.
The Christoffel symbols
\Gamma^{\widetilde{\imath}}{}_{{\widetilde{\jmath}}{\widetilde{k}}}
appearing in Eq. (
14
) are defined as:

\Gamma^{\widetilde{\imath}}{}_{{\widetilde{\jmath}}{\widetilde{k}}}=\frac{\partial x^{\widetilde{\imath}}}{\partial x^{\ell}}\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\ell}}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\jmath}}\partial x^{\widetilde{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}g^{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\ell}}}\left(\partial_{\widetilde{k}}g_{{\widetilde{\ell}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}+\partial_{\widetilde{\jmath}}g_{{\widetilde{\ell}}{\widetilde{k}}}-\partial_{\widetilde{\ell}}g_{{\widetilde{\jmath}}{\widetilde{k}}}\right).

The above formalism is sufficient to adapt the coordinate system to a curved boundary. However, the transition to an LB model is not straightforward, since the momentum space has an intrinsic dependence on the coordinates. Indeed, the Maxwellian distribution
f^{\text{\tiny MB}}
(
9
) expressed with respect to the new coordinates, reads:

f^{\text{\tiny MB}}=\frac{n}{(2\pi mT)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}(p^{\widetilde{\imath}}-mu^{\widetilde{\imath}})(p^{\widetilde{\jmath}}-mu^{\widetilde{\jmath}})}{2mT}\right],

(15)

while its moments are calculated as:

M^{{\widetilde{\imath}}_{1},\dots{\widetilde{\imath}}_{n}}_{\text{\tiny MB}}=\sqrt{g}\int d^{3}\widetilde{p}\,f^{\text{\tiny MB}}p^{{\widetilde{\imath}}_{1}}\cdots p^{{\widetilde{\imath}}_{n}},

(16)

where
g
is the determinant of the metric tensor
g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}
.

In order to eliminate the burden of this metric dependence in the expression for the Maxwellian,
it is convenient to introduce a triad (vielbein) with respect to which the metric is diagonal
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
:

g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}dx^{\widetilde{\imath}}dx^{\widetilde{\jmath}}=\delta_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}\omega^{\hat{a}}\omega^{\hat{b}},\quad\omega^{\hat{a}}=\omega^{\hat{a}}_{\widetilde{\jmath}}dx^{\widetilde{\jmath}},

(17)

where
\omega^{\hat{a}}
are the triad one-forms and it follows that:

g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}=\delta_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}\omega^{\hat{a}}_{\widetilde{\imath}}\omega^{\hat{b}}_{\widetilde{\jmath}}.

(18)

The above equation allows three degrees of freedom for the system
\{\omega^{\hat{a}}_{\widetilde{\jmath}}\}
,
corresponding to the invariance of the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) under rotations
with respect to the hatted indices. It is possible to define triad vectors dual to the above
one-forms by introducing the following inner product
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
:

\braket{\omega^{\hat{b}},e_{\hat{a}}}\equiv e_{\hat{a}}^{\widetilde{\imath}}\omega^{\hat{b}}_{\widetilde{\imath}}=\delta^{\hat{b}}{}_{\hat{a}},\quad\text{where}\quad e_{\hat{a}}=e_{\hat{a}}^{\widetilde{\imath}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\imath}}}.

(19)

Using the above triad, the components of vectors can be expressed as
p^{\hat{a}}=\omega^{\hat{a}}_{\widetilde{\imath}}p^{\widetilde{\imath}},
such that

g_{{\widetilde{\imath}}{\widetilde{\jmath}}}p^{\widetilde{\imath}}p^{\widetilde{\jmath}}=\delta_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}p^{\hat{a}}p^{\hat{b}},
resulting in the following Maxwellian:

f^{\text{\tiny MB}}=\frac{n}{(2\pi mT)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{\delta_{{\hat{a}}{\hat{b}}}(p^{\hat{a}}-mu^{\hat{a}})(p^{\hat{b}}-mu^{\hat{b}})}{2mT}\right],

(20)

where the metric dependence disappeared. Its moments can be written as:

M^{{\hat{a}}_{1},\dots{\hat{a}}_{s}}_{\text{\tiny MB}}=\int d^{3}\hat{p}\,f^{\text{\tiny MB}}p^{{\hat{a}}_{1}}\cdots p^{{\hat{a}}_{s}}.

(21)

Also, the moments of the distribution function
f
are given by:

M^{{\hat{a}}_{1},\dots{\hat{a}}_{s}}=\int d^{3}\hat{p}\,f\,p^{{\hat{a}}_{1}}\cdots p^{{\hat{a}}_{s}}.

(22)

In curvilinear coordinates, the Enskog equation reads:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{p^{\hat{a}}}{m}e_{\hat{a}}^{\widetilde{\imath}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\widetilde{\imath}}}+\left(F^{\hat{a}}-\frac{1}{m}\Gamma^{\hat{a}}{}_{{\hat{b}}{\hat{c}}}p^{\hat{b}}p^{\hat{c}}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\hat{a}}}=J_{E}[f],

(23)

Here the Enskog collision is replaced with the simplified Enskog collision operator:

J_{E}[f]=J_{0}[f]+J_{1}[f]=-\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{S})-b\rho\chi f^{\text{\tiny MB}}\left\{\xi^{\hat{a}}\cdot\left[\nabla_{\hat{a}}\ln(\rho^{2}\chi T)+\frac{3}{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\nabla_{\hat{a}}\ln T\right]\right.\\

where

The dilute limit
\sigma\rightarrow 0
corresponds to
\text{Pr}=2/3
.
The Chapman-Enskog expansion of Eq. (
24
) provides relationships between the relaxation time
\tau
and the transport coefficients. In this context, the relaxation time
\tau
is expressed as:

\tau=\frac{\mu}{P_{i}}.

(32)

The quantity
\mu
encompasses both kinetic and potential contributions, which account for the flow of molecules and the collisional effects on the transfer of momentum and energy in the gas
Chapman and Cowling 1970
;
Kremer 2010
. The relaxation time approximation effectively captures the collisional transfer resulting from non-local molecular collisions. It is worth noting that the viscosity of a dense gas with a fixed reduced density
\eta
can be adjusted by varying the molecular diameter
\sigma
and the number density
n
.

The Knudsen number is defined as the ratio of the mean free path and a characteristic length (in our case the distance between cylinders/spheres):

Kn=\frac{\lambda}{L}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}\eta_{0}\chi(\eta_{0})C},

(33)

where
C=L/\sigma
is the confinement ratio
Wu et al. 2015
;
Sheng et al. 2020
;
Su et al. 2023
.

This paper primarily concentrates on benchmarking the cylindrical Couette flow, as well as the cylindrical and spherical Fourier flow.
In these cases, the steady flow either lacks bulk motion or exhibits motion perpendicular to the direction in which the gas density varies. Consequently, the bulk viscosity does not have an impact in these cases.

II.2
Cylindrical coordinates

Let us specialize the formalism of section
II.1
to the case of the Couette and Fourier flow between coaxial cylinders. To describe the geometry of this flow, it is convenient to introduce cylindrical coordinates
\{x^{\widetilde{\imath}}\}=\{R,\varphi,z\}
through
x=R\cos\varphi
and
y=R\sin\varphi
. The line element in cylindrical coordinates and the associated triad are:

ds^{2}=dR^{2}+R^{2}d\varphi^{2}+dz^{2},\qquad e_{\hat{R}}=\partial_{R},\qquad e_{\hat{\varphi}}=R^{-1}\partial_{\varphi},\qquad e_{\hat{z}}=\partial_{z}.

(34)

The Enskog equation when the flow is homogeneous w.r.t.
\varphi
and
z
reads:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{2p^{\hat{R}}}{m}\frac{\partial(fR)}{\partial R^{2}}+\frac{1}{mR}\left[(p^{\hat{\varphi}})^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\hat{R}}}-p^{\hat{R}}\frac{\partial(fp^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial p^{\hat{\varphi}}}\right]=\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{S})-\\
b\rho\chi f^{\text{\tiny MB}}\left\{\xi^{R}\cdot\left[\partial_{R}\ln(\rho^{2}\chi T)+\frac{3}{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\partial_{R}\ln T\right]\right.\\
\left.+\frac{2}{5}\left[2\zeta^{R}\zeta^{R}:\partial_{R}u^{R}+\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\partial_{R}\cdot u^{R}\right]\right\}.

(35)

The advection term is implemented following Ref.
Falle and Komissarov 1996
.

II.3
Spherical coordinates

To describe the spherical Fourier flow we introduce spherical coordinates
\{x^{\widetilde{\imath}}\}=\{r,\theta,\varphi\}
through
x=r\sin\theta\cos\varphi
,
y=r\sin\theta\sin\varphi
and
z=r\cos\theta
. The line element in spherical coordinates and its associated triad are:

ds^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}),\qquad e_{{\hat{r}}}=\partial_{r},\qquad e_{{\hat{\theta}}}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}}{r},\qquad e_{{\hat{\varphi}}}=\frac{r^{-1}}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}.

(36)

Assuming that the flow is homogeneous w.r.t.
\theta
and
\varphi
, the Boltzmann equation
reads:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{3p^{\hat{r}}}{m}\frac{\partial(fr^{2})}{\partial r^{3}}+\frac{1}{mr}\left[\left((p^{\hat{\theta}})^{2}+(p^{\hat{\varphi}})^{2}\right)\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\hat{r}}}-p^{\hat{r}}\left(\frac{\partial(fp^{\hat{\theta}})}{\partial p^{\hat{\theta}}}+\frac{\partial(fp^{\hat{\varphi}})}{\partial p^{\hat{\varphi}}}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\tau}(f-f^{S})-\\
b\rho\chi f^{\text{\tiny MB}}\left\{\xi^{r}\cdot\left[\partial_{r}\ln(\rho^{2}\chi T)+\frac{3}{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\partial_{r}\ln T\right]\right.\\
\left.+\frac{2}{5}\left[2\zeta^{r}\zeta^{r}:\partial_{r}u^{r}+\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)\partial_{r}\cdot u^{r}\right]\right\}.

(37)

The advection term is implemented following Ref.
Downes et al. 2002
.

III
Mixed quadrature LB models

In this paper we employ the mixed quadrature LB models, introduced in Refs.
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
;
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016b
;
Ambrus et al. 2017
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
, to obtain the numerical solution of
the Enskog equation in cylindrically and spherically symmetric flows using the simplified Enskog collision operator.
We briefly describe these models and encourage the reader to refer to the above-mentioned references for more details.
In the case of the cylindrical Couette flow, the dynamics along the
z
direction is straightforward. In this context, it is advantageous to integrate out the trivial degrees of freedom in the momentum space at the level of the model equation. The details can be consulted in Ref.
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
. As discussed in Ref.
Shan et al. 2006
, in our Gauss-Hermite quadrature-based model the moments of the distribution function are recovered
up to a certain expansion order
N
. As such, the resulting evolution of moments up to order
N
of
f
is equivalent to that obtained through a Grad’s expansion procedure. In all Gauss-Hermite quadrature-
based models, the set of distribution functions corresponding to the elements of the discrete velocity set is used instead of the moment integrals, resulting in a purely kinetic description of fluid systems.

The numerical schemes employed in this paper to solve the evolution equations are the third-order total variation diminishing (TVD) Runge-Kutta method for time-stepping
Shu and Osher 1988
, the fifth-order WENO-5 advection scheme
Gan et al. 2011
;
Jiang and Shu 1996
, and the
4
th order central difference scheme used for gradient evaluation
Fornberg 1988
.

Another ingredient needed is the evaluation of the average density
\bar{n}
in Eq. (
4
). In spherical coordinates one has to evaluate intersections of spherical shells in order to compute the integral in Eq. (
4
), for which we have an analytic solution. For cylindrical coordinates, one has to rely on numerically computing the intersection between a cylinder shell and a spherical shell. The procedure is detailed in Appendix
A
.

III.1
Quadrature choice

Depending on the flow regime under consideration, we will consider a mixture of full-range Gauss-Hermite (HLB) and half-range Gauss-Hermite quadratures (HHLB).
Given the choice of the quadrature (full-range or half-range Gauss-Hermite) the total number of quadrature points on the axis
a
is
\mathcal{Q}_{a}=Q_{a}

or
\mathcal{Q}_{a}=2Q_{a}
, respectively, where
Q_{a}
is the quadrature order.
In the non-homogeneous direction (i.e. the radial one) the half-range quadratures are employed and their orders are
Q_{R}
and
Q_{r}
, in the cylindrical and spherical coordinate systems, respectively. In the homogeneous direction, the full-range quadratures are employed and their orders are
Q_{\varphi}
and
(Q_{\theta},Q_{\varphi})
.
We denote such a model using
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
;
Ambrus et al. 2017
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
:

\displaystyle\text{Cylindrical}~:

\displaystyle~{\rm HHLB}(N_{R};Q_{R})\times{\rm HLB(N_{\varphi};Q_{\varphi})},

\displaystyle\text{Spherical}~:

\displaystyle~{\rm HHLB}(N_{r};Q_{r})\times{\rm HLB(N_{\theta};Q_{\theta})}\times{\rm HLB}(N_{\varphi};Q_{\varphi}),

where
N_{a}
represents the order of the expansion of the equilibrium distribution
f^{\text{\tiny MB}}
with
respect to axis
a
, and
Q_{a}
is the quadrature order.

The choice of the quadrature controls the momentum space integration as well as the discretization of the momentum space. In particular, the moments in Eq. (
22
) are evaluated as:

\displaystyle\text{Cylindrical}~:

\displaystyle~M^{{\hat{a}}_{1},\dots{\hat{a}}_{s}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}}f_{ij}\prod_{\ell=1}^{s}p^{{\hat{a}}_{\ell}}_{ij}.

(38)

\displaystyle\text{Spherical}~:

\displaystyle~M^{{\hat{a}}_{1},\dots{\hat{a}}_{s}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{r}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}}f_{ijk}\prod_{\ell=1}^{s}p^{{\hat{a}}_{\ell}}_{ijk}.

(39)

A similar prescription holds for the macroscopic quantities appearing in Eq.
25
.
In the cylindrical geometry, the discrete momenta components of
{\bm{p}}_{ij}=\{p^{{\hat{R}}}_{i},p^{{\hat{\varphi}}}_{j}\}
are
indexed on each direction separately, where
1\leq i\leq\mathcal{Q}_{R}
and
1\leq j\leq\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}
. In the spherical case, the discrete momenta components of
{\bm{p}}_{ijk}=\{p^{{\hat{r}}}_{i},p^{{\hat{\theta}}}_{j},p^{{\hat{\varphi}}}_{k}\}
are
indexed as follows:
1\leq i\leq\mathcal{Q}_{r}
,

1\leq j\leq\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}
and
1\leq k\leq\mathcal{Q}_{\varphi}
. Their components are the roots of the half-range Hermite polynomial
\mathfrak{h}_{Q_{a}}(x)
of order
Q_{a}
or of the full-range Hermite polynomials
H_{Q_{a}}(x)
of order
Q_{a}
. In the case of the half-range quadrature, we use the convention that the indices
i
of the quadrature points lying on the positive semi-axis have the values
1\leq i\leq Q_{a}
, while the corresponding index of the points on the negative semi-axis have the values
Q_{a}+1\leq i\leq 2Q_{a}

Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
.

As an example, for the cylindrical coordinate system, the link between the Boltzmann distribution function
f(p^{\hat{R}},p^{\hat{\theta}},p^{\hat{\varphi}})
and the discrete distributions
f_{ijk}
is given through:

f_{ijk}=\frac{w_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}(Q_{R})w_{j}^{H}(Q_{\theta})w_{k}^{H}(Q_{\varphi})}{\omega(p^{\hat{R}}_{i})\omega(p^{\hat{\theta}}_{k})\omega(p^{\hat{\varphi}}_{j})}f(p^{\hat{R}}_{i},p^{\hat{\theta}}_{j},p^{\hat{\varphi}}_{k}),

(40)

where
\omega(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}
is the weight function for the half-range and full-range Hermite polynomials.
The quadrature weights for the full-range Gauss-Hermite and half-range Gauss-Hermite polynomials, denoted
w_{i}^{H}(Q_{a})
and
w_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}(Q_{a})
, respectively, are given by:

w_{i}^{H}(Q_{a})=\frac{Q_{a}!}{H_{Q_{a}+1}^{2}(p^{\hat{a}}_{i})},\qquad w_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}(Q_{a})=\frac{p^{\hat{a}}_{i}a_{Q_{a}-1}^{2}}{\mathfrak{h}_{Q_{a}-1}^{2}(p^{\hat{a}}_{i})[p^{\hat{a}}_{i}+\mathfrak{h}_{Q_{a}}^{2}(0)/\sqrt{2\pi}]},

(41)

where
a_{\ell}=\frac{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell+1,\ell+1}}{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,\ell}}
is written in terms of the coefficients
\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,s}
of
x^{s}

in the polynomial expansion of
\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}(x)
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
.

III.2
Force terms

The terms involving the derivative of
f
with respect to momentum vector components require an appropriate treatment,
since the discretization of the momentum space removes the functional dependence of
f
on the momentum.
Based on the discussion in Ref.
Ambrus et al. 2017
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
, we write the two types of force terms as:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p^{\hat{a}}}\right)_{ijk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{a}}\mathcal{K}^{a}_{i,i^{\prime}}f_{i^{\prime}jk},

(42)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial(fp^{\hat{a}})}{\partial p^{\hat{a}}}\right)_{ijk}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\mathcal{Q}_{a}}\mathcal{K}^{a}_{i,i^{\prime}}f_{i^{\prime}jk}.

(43)

The matrix
\mathcal{K}^{a}_{i,i^{\prime}}
in Eq.(
42
) has the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{a,H}_{i,i^{\prime}}{}

\displaystyle=-w_{i}^{H}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q_{a}-1}\frac{1}{\ell!}H_{\ell+1}(p^{{\hat{a}}}_{i})H_{\ell}(p^{{\hat{a}}}_{i^{\prime}})

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{a,\mathfrak{h}}_{i,i^{\prime}}{}

\displaystyle=w_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}\sigma_{i}^{\hat{a}}\Bigg\{\frac{1+\sigma_{i}^{\hat{a}}\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q_{a}-2}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}(\left|p_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}}\right|)\times[\frac{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,0}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{s=\ell+1}^{Q_{a}-1}\mathfrak{h}_{s,0}\mathfrak{h}_{s}(\left|p_{i}^{\hat{a}}\right|)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,\ell}}{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell+1,\ell+1}}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell+1}(\left|p_{i}^{\hat{R}}\right|)]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\Phi^{Q_{R}}_{0}(\left|p_{i}^{\hat{R}}\right|)\Phi^{Q_{R}}_{0}(\left|p_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{R}}\right|)\Bigg\}.

(45)

for the full-range and half-range quadrature, respectively. In the above,
\sigma_{i}^{\hat{R}}
and
\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{R}}
, are the signs of
p_{i}^{\hat{R}}
and

p_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{R}}
, respectively.
The function
\Phi^{n}_{s}(x)
is given by
\Phi^{n}_{s}(x)=\sum_{\ell=s}^{n}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,s}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}(x)
, as defined in Ref.
Ambrus et al. 2017
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
.

The matrix
\mathcal{K}^{a}_{i,i^{\prime}}
involved in Eq.(
43
) is written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{a,H}_{i,i^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle-w_{i}^{H}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q_{a}-2}\frac{1}{\ell!}H_{\ell+1}(p_{i}^{\hat{a}})[H_{\ell+1}(p_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}})+\ell H_{\ell-1}(p_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}})],

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{a,\mathfrak{h}}_{i,i^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle-w_{i}^{\mathfrak{h}}\frac{1+\sigma_{i}^{\hat{a}}\sigma_{i^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}}}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{Q_{a}-1}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}(p_{i}^{\hat{a}})\Bigg[\ell\,\mathfrak{h}_{\ell}(p^{\hat{a}}_{i^{\prime}})+\frac{\mathfrak{h}_{\ell,0}^{2}+\mathfrak{h}_{\ell-1,0}^{2}}{a_{\ell-1}\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell-1}(p^{\hat{a}}_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{a_{\ell-1}a_{\ell-2}}\mathfrak{h}_{\ell-2}(p^{\hat{a}}_{i^{\prime}})\Bigg].

for the full-range and half-range quadrature, respectively.

III.3
Equilibrium distribution function

The construction of the equilibrium distribution function (
20
) follows the prescription developed in Refs.
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
;
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016b
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
.
f^{\text{\tiny MB}}
is factorised with respect to the momentum

\displaystyle f^{\text{\tiny MB}}=n\,g_{R}(p^{\hat{R}})g_{\varphi}(p^{\hat{\varphi}})g_{z}(p^{\hat{z}}),

\displaystyle g_{a}(p^{\hat{a}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi mT}}\exp\left[-\frac{(p^{\hat{a}}-mu^{\hat{a}})^{2}}{2mT}\right].

(46)

Following the discretisation of the momentum space,
f^{\text{\tiny MB}}
is replaced by

f^{\text{\tiny MB}}_{ijk}=n\,g_{R,i}g_{\varphi,j}g_{z,k}
.
The function
g_{a,k}
becomes
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016a
;
Ambruş and Sofonea 2016b
;
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019

\displaystyle g_{a,k}^{H}

\displaystyle=w_{k}^{H}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{a}}H_{\ell}(p^{\hat{a}}_{k})\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\frac{(mT-1)^{s}(mu^{\hat{a}})^{\ell-2s}}{2^{s}s!(\ell-2s)!},

(47)

\displaystyle g_{a,k}^{\mathfrak{h}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{k}^{\mathfrak{h}}}{2}\sum_{s=0}^{N_{a}}\left(\frac{mT}{2}\right)^{s/2}\Phi_{s}^{N_{a}}(\left|p^{\hat{a}}_{k}\right|)\times\left[(1+erf\zeta^{\hat{a}})P_{s}^{+}(\zeta^{\hat{a}})+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-\zeta_{{\hat{a}}}^{2}}P_{s}^{*}(\zeta^{\hat{a}})\right],

(48)

for the case of full-range (upperscript
H
) and half-range (upperscript
\mathfrak{h}
) quadrature, respectively.
In the above the expansion order
N_{a}
is a free parameter satisfying
0\leq N_{a}<Q_{a}
.
The exact recovery of the moments (
21
) for polynomials in

of order less than or
equal to

in ensured by an expansion of
g_{a,k}

N_{a}

The expression for
g_{a,k}^{\mathfrak{h}}
holds the following notations:

\zeta^{\hat{a}}=u^{\hat{a}}\sqrt{m/2T}
when
p^{\hat{a}}_{k}>0

\zeta^{\hat{a}}=-u^{\hat{a}}\sqrt{m/2T}
when
p^{\hat{a}}_{k}<0
, while the polynomials
P_{s}^{+}(x)

and
P_{s}^{*}(x)
are defined as

\displaystyle P_{s}^{\pm}(x)=e^{\mp x^{2}}\frac{d^{s}}{dx^{s}}e^{\pm x^{2}},

\displaystyle P_{s}^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\binom{s}{j}P_{j}^{+}(x)P_{s-j-1}^{-}(x).

(49)

V
Conclusions

In this work, a series of dense gas flows bounded by curvilinear walls were considered in order to validate the proposed finite-difference Lattice Boltzmann model employing the simplified Enskog collision integral. For this purpose, the Enskog equation in curvilinear coordinates was written with respect to orthonormal vielbein fields (triads in
3D
), by extending the formalism introduced in Ref.
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
for the Boltzmann equation. The vielbein can be used to align the momentum space along the coordinate directions, while also decoupling the dependence of
({\bm{p}}-m{\bm{u}})^{2}
appearing in the Maxwell-Boltzmann equilibrium distribution on the induced metric tensor. In the simplified Enskog collision model, the Enskog collision integral is approximated using a Taylor expansion and retaining the first-order gradients. Following Ref.
Busuioc and Ambruş 2019
, a half-range Gauss-Hermite quadrature was used on the axis normal to the curved boundaries studied. The model was benchmarked in three setups: cylindrical Couette and Fourier flows between coaxial cylinders, as well as the Fourier flow between concentric spheres.
The simulation parameters range from a low reduced density value (
\eta_{0}=0.01
) to a relatively high value (
\eta_{0}=0.2
), three values of the inner cylinder radius
\mathcal{R}_{L}\in\{1,3,5\}
and three values of the confinement ratio
C\in\{4,7,10\}
.

The FDLB results obtained for the cylindrical Couette flow and the cylindrical and spherical Fourier flow were validated against the corresponding PM results. Reasonable agreement was observed throughout the parameter range. More specifically, our kinetic model adequately captures the effects of denseness, density inhomogeneity, as well as nonequilibrium phenomena within the range of flow parameters investigated. It is important to consider that when a fluid molecule is located at a distance less than a molecular diameter
\sigma
from the wall, a portion of its surface remains protected from collisions since there is not sufficient space available for a second molecule to occupy that part of the spatial domain. As a result, the particle is pushed toward the wall. When dealing with a curved boundary this effect is either diminished or enhanced if a concave or convex boundary is involved. In our case, the more pronounced effect is on the inner cylinder/sphere where the available space is increased due to the shape of the boundary. As such the layering effect is inversely proportional to the curvature. This variation in the layering effect is well captured by the FDLB model proposed.

For the cylindrical Couette flow, we recover with good accuracy the density profile in the channel, while a reasonable accuracy is observed for the azimuthal velocity and temperature profile, with discrepancies between the FDLB and PM results being lower than
5\%
.

For the Fourier flow, we present the density and temperature distribution in the channel for all combinations of the input parameters. Good agreement is observed throughout the entire parameter range. We also include a comparison of the two geometries, pointing to their differences and the results which are in accordance with expectations based on the excluded volume for collision due to the variable curvature/geometry. Additionally, a discussion about the heat flux between concentric cylinders and concentric spheres is presented. Channel constants derived from heat flux show larger values for high curvature and reach asymptotically the planar case results as the radius of the cylinders/spheres is increased.

In conclusion, the presented model demonstrates its capability to handle curved geometries for moderately dense gases.
Moving forward, our goal is to implement the second-order terms in the Taylor expansion of the Enskog collision integral as well as attractive forces between molecules to address multiphase flows.

In order to evaluate the smoothed density
\bar{n}
in Eq. (
4
), in cylindrical coordinates we employ the procedure presented in Ref.
Lamarche and Leroy 1990
. The intersection of the cylinder of radius
R
and the sphere of radius
r
is given by:

V(r,R,b)=\frac{4\pi}{3}r^{3}\Theta(R-b)+\frac{4}{3\sqrt{A-C}}\left\{\Pi(k,-\alpha^{2})\frac{B^{2}s}{C}\right.\\
\left.+K(k)\left[s(A-2B)+(A-B)\frac{3B-C-2A}{3}\right]\right.\\
\left.+E(k)(A-C)\left(-s+\frac{2A+2C-4B}{3}\right)\right\},r<b+R

(52)

where
b
denotes the smallest distance of the axis of the cylinder to the center of the sphere and
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function. We have limited to the description of the intersection volume to the case used in the present study when
r<R
.
In the above, we have introduced the elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kind:

K(k)\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\sqrt{1-k^{2}z^{2}}},\quad E(k)\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dz\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}z^{2}}}

\Pi(k,-\alpha^{2})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dz}{(1-\alpha^{2}z^{2})\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\sqrt{1-k^{2}z^{2}}}

(53)

and the following quantities have been defined:

A=\max\left(r^{2},(b+R)^{2}\right),\quad B=\min\left(r^{2},(b+R)^{2}\right),\quad C=(b-R)^{2}

(54)

k^{2}=\frac{B-C}{A-C},\quad-\alpha^{2}=\frac{B-C}{C},\quad s=(b+R)(b-R)

(55)

These expressions were implemented through the use of the GNU Scientific Library (GSL)
Galassi et al. 2021
, a numerical library for C and C++ programmers.

r_{1}
and
