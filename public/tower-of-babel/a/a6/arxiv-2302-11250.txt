The Complexity of Debt Swapping
Henri Froese, Martin Hoefer, Lisa Wilhelmi

Abstract
A debt swap is an elementary edge swap in a directed, weighted graph, where two edges with the same weight swap their targets. Debt swaps are a natural and appealing operation in financial networks, in which nodes are banks and edges represent debt contracts. They can improve the clearing payments and the stability of these networks. However, their algorithmic properties are not well-understood. We analyze the computational complexity of debt swapping in networks with ranking-based clearing. Our main interest lies in semi-positive swaps, in which no creditor strictly suffers and at least one strictly profits. These swaps lead to a Pareto-improvement in the entire network. We consider network optimization via sequences of v v -improving debt swaps from which a given bank v v strictly profits. We show that every sequence of semi-positive v v -improving swaps has polynomial length. In contrast, for arbitrary v v -improving swaps, the problem of reaching a network configuration that allows no further swaps is PLS -complete. We identify cases in which short sequences of semi-positive swaps exist even without the v v -improving property. In addition, we study reachability problems, i.e., deciding if a sequence of swaps exists between given initial and final networks. We identify a polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary swaps, show NP -hardness for semi-positive swaps and even PSPACE -completeness for v v -improving swaps or swaps that only maintain a lower bound on the assets of a given bank v v . A variety of our results can be extended to arbitrary monotone clearing.

Introduction

Over the last decades, the international financial system has evolved into a complex and highly interconnected network. The close structural connections can give rise to devastating cascading effects, which have been a major issue in the financial crisis of 2008 and its aftermath. Recently, there has been an increasing research interest in the computational complexity of network-based interbank models for financial systems. A fundamental aspect of these models is clearing, a process of debt resolution by which solvency of banks in the system gets determined. In particular, in a financial network (where banks are nodes, and weighted, directed edges correspond to debt contracts) the clearing problem asks for a consistent assignment of payments of debtor banks to their creditors. Based on the clearing, a bank might not receive the full amount of credit it gave to others. As a consequence, it might not have sufficient funds to clear all her own debt. The solution to the clearing problem is of central importance to banks since it directly affects their assets.

Banks have a natural incentive to improve their clearing conditions. Moreover, governmental regulators, central banks, and other financial services have an interest in maintaining and improving the financial system. While payment rules for clearing are often fixed, several other suggestions for improvement exist, affecting the (external) assets of the banks or the network structure
(such as, e.g., bailouts
[
31
]
, portfolio compression
[
42
,
35
]
, and more).

A particularly interesting variant of network improvement is
debt swapping

[
28
]
: There are two debt contracts among two pairs of banks
(u_{1},v_{1})
and
(u_{2},v_{2})
. The contracts have the same value. In a debt swap, the creditor banks
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
change their role as recipients of these contracts. This leaves every bank with the same amounts of debt as before – only the network is affected by the exchange of the edges. A debt swap is a local edge-swap operation and represents a small change in the network structure. Since it involves only a small number of banks, it can be executed in a distributed fashion by the participating banks. Clearly, the banks are likely to agree to this operation only if it does not deteriorate their network position, i.e., it does not decrease their assets in the clearing of the resulting network.

Debt swaps are a very natural and interesting swap operation with many conceptual advantages. For example, they are usually less demanding than the more prominent approach of portfolio compression
[
35
,
42
]
(for a discussion of the relation between these two operations see
[
28
]
). From a computational perspective, debt swaps are a fascinating recent addition to the literature on edge swap operations in graphs. Numerous variants of such swaps have attracted a large interest in computer science over the decades (see related work discussed below). The computational aspects of this swap operation, however, are not well understood.

In this paper, we analyze the computational complexity of debt swaps. We conduct a broad study of network optimization problems surrounding the swap operation. Since a debt swap is a local improvement step in the network, we first focus on local optimization (e.g., from an initial network, can we reach a configuration that allows no more profitable debt swaps?). In addition, we also consider aspects of global optimization (i.e., maximize the assets of a single bank via a sequence of debt swaps) and reachability problems (i.e., decide if a target network structure can be reached using a sequence of debt swaps). We discuss all our results consistently for networks in which banks use natural ranking-based clearing payments
[
10
,
5
,
20
]
. Indeed, many of our results can be extended to arbitrary monotone clearing. Throughout the paper we mention the extension explicitly whenever it applies.

Contribution and Techniques.

After discussing preliminaries in Section
2
, we classify debt swaps into positive (both creditors profit strictly in the clearing after the swap), semi-positive (one creditor profits strictly, the other one remains the same), or arbitrary ones. Our characterization results (discussed in detail in Appendix
B
) show that in every financial network, there can be no positive swap. Semi-positive swaps can exist, and, in fact, they always represent a
Pareto-improvement
: After the swap
no bank in the entire network suffers
, every bank obtains at least as many assets as before. This makes semi-positive swaps very attractive. Similar results were shown before by Papp and Wattenhofer
[
28
]
for proportional payments. We here show similar conditions for ranking-based payments. Indeed, our proofs generalize to
all
monotone payments and substantially generalize their arguments.

Our work concentrates on improvement dynamics with semi-positive debt swaps. Since they Pareto-improve the assets of all banks (and strictly for a creditor bank), every sequence of semi-positive swaps is inherently acyclic and terminates in a local optimum. A natural and challenging question that we study in Section
3
is whether there are short sequences that allow to reach a local optimum in polynomial time. We further refine the characterization of semi-positive swaps into
saturating
, and
extension
swaps, and further into
active
,
semi-
and
non-active
extension swaps. We show that there can be at most a polynomial number of saturating as well as semi- and non-active extension swaps, so the core of the problem lies in the existence of short sequences of active extension swaps.
When a single bank
v
strictly profits from every semi-positive swap, then we prove that every sequence of active extension swaps has indeed a length polynomial in the size of the network (and, thus, so has every sequence of semi-positive swaps). Instead, when we consider arbitrary swaps in which a given bank
v
strictly improves, then there are initial networks from which
every
sequence of such swaps has exponential length. More fundamentally, we prove that it is
PLS
-complete to construct any local optimum, i.e., any network where liabilities of each node are consistent with the initial network and that allows no (arbitrary) debt swap in which the assets of a given bank
v
strictly increase.

The problem without the property of strict improvement for a single bank
v
turns out to be extremely challenging. We also make some progress here – under a condition on the liabilities and the in-degree of partly paid edges of every bank, we can show existence of a short sequence of semi-positive swaps to a local optimum. The general problem remains as an interesting avenue for future work.

In Section
4
we then turn to reachability problems: Given an initial and a target network on the same set of nodes
V
, the goal is to decide whether or not there is a sequence of debt swaps to turn the initial network into the target network. For arbitrary debt swaps, we show that the problem can be solved in polynomial time. In contrast, when we have to maintain a lower bound on the assets of a bank
v
throughout, the problem becomes
PSPACE
-complete. For semi-positive debt swaps, we show that the problem is
NP
-hard.

Moreover, in Appendix
C
, we consider global optimization, i.e., the problem of determining the maximal assets of a given bank that can be obtained by a sequence of debt swaps from a given initial network. We show that this problem is
NP
-hard to approximate, both when considering arbitrary or only semi-positive debt swaps. Similar hardness results apply when determining the maximal
sum of assets of several banks
.

For spatial reasons, we defer some of the proofs to Appendix
A

Financial Networks, Clearing, and Ranking.

We follow previous literature and define financial networks as follows. A financial network
\mathcal{F}=(G,\mathbf{l},\mathbf{a}^{x},\mathbf{f})

with

v

2.1
Characterization

To reason about debt swaps, we first provide a structural analysis of the clearing payments before and after debt swaps. We obtain a characterization of semi-positive and positive swaps. The analysis substantially generalizes techniques from
[
28
]
and yields two major insights. First, there are no positive swaps. Second, every semi-positive debt swap yields a Pareto-improvement in the
entire network
, i.e., no bank in the financial network is harmed while at least one bank strictly profits. Interestingly, the converse also holds: Every Pareto-improving debt swap is semi-positive. For detailed proofs see Appendix
B
.

Theorem 4
.

A financial network with edge-ranking rules allows no positive debt swap.

Theorem 5
.

For a financial network with edge-ranking rules, a debt swap is semi-positive if and only if it is Pareto-improving.

Similar to
[
28
]
, we use an auxiliary network with the same payments as the resulting network after the debt swap. In this auxiliary network, we replace the swap edges with auxiliary sources and sinks for the involved nodes with appropriate assets representing the payments on the swap edges. For a contradiction, we assume that the debt swap is positive. Then, by tracing the payments in the auxiliary network, we show that payments in the original network before the debt swap did not correspond to the maximum clearing state. This yields a contradiction and shows that positive swaps cannot exist. As a rather direct consequence from this characterization, we obtain that semi-positive swaps lead to a Pareto-improvement in the entire network.

The main distinction from the proof for proportional payments in
[
28
]
is that these payments are limited to a particular structure in the auxiliary network and, as such, easier to handle. Instead, our proof for edge-ranking rules turns out to rely only on monotonicity of the allocation rule. By arguing about a broad range of possibilities and dependencies of potential payments, we can show that the result indeed applies to finanical networks with
any monotone payment rules
.

Corollary 6
.

A financial network with monotone allocation rules allows no positive debt swap. In such a network a debt swap is semi-positive if and only if it is Pareto-improving.

3
Debt Swap Dynamics

When two creditors perform a debt swap, the structure of the underlying graph changes. This might enable further swaps which potentially further increase the assets of the involved banks. In this way, the debt swap operation naturally gives rise to
dynamics
where, in each step, a pair of creditors agree to perform a debt swap in the current network. Conceptually, semi-positive swaps are very attractive – they are equivalent to Pareto-improving swaps and improve the situation not only for the creditors but for the entire network.

In this section, we consider natural distributed dynamics of debt swaps. Given an initial financial network with monotone allocation rules, our goal is to find a small number of semi-positive debt swaps to reach a local optimum, i.e., a network structure without semi-positive swaps. We denote this as the
SemiSwap
problem. While all banks (weakly) profit from a semi-positive swap, we are interested in semi-positive swaps that are
v
-improving, i.e.,
strictly increase the assets of a given bank
v
. This is natural, e.g., when
v
is a creditor that profits strictly in every such swap, or
v
is a bank that is “too big to fail” and should strictly profit from network changes. We denote the problem with semi-positive
v
-improving swaps by
\textsc{SemiSwap}(v)
. Given a financial network, can we reach a local optimum using a sequence of polynomial many semi-positive
v
-improving debt swaps?

We consider this question for the broad class of edge-ranking rules. In a given network with
m
edges, there are at most
m\choose 2
edge swaps. Recall that we can compute the clearing state for edge-ranking rules in strongly polynomial time
[
5
]
. Hence, for a given network, we can also enumerate all semi-positive debt swaps in strongly polynomial time.

Periods, Phases, and Active Extension Swaps.

We now discuss constructing short sequences of swaps. Let us first consider saturating swaps.

Observation 10

Given an initial financial network with edge-ranking rules and

We define a

Lemma 11

Given an initial financial network with edge-ranking rules and a sequence of semi-positive debt swaps.
In a single period, there can be at most

Proof.

For each semi-active swap of this kind, one of two possible events occurs: Either (1) an incoming non-active saturated edge of

is changed directly to a non-active one with 0 payments.

as the non-profiting creditor.
∎

3.1.2
Beyond Improvement for a Given Bank

In the remainder of the section, we concentrate on arbitrary active extension swaps (without the property that they are

For the remainder of the section, we focus on instances with two network conditions. First, suppose that throughout every node has at most

Proposition 14
.

Given an initial financial network with edge-ranking rules. Every sequence of semi-positive swaps, during which every node has at most

Proof.

In an active extension swap, the non-profiting creditor

When

Definition 15

(Sufficient Residuals)

In a financial network

Corollary 16
.

For a financial network with edge-ranking rules, the pair

Clearly, if

As a consequence, our goal now is to swap edges with small payments as high as possible in the tree. In particular, consider edges classified by their liability values. For each class, we intend to swap the active edges with lowest payments to the root of the in-tree. Whenever there are active edges from the same class at the root and anywhere deeper in the tree, and the root edge has strictly more payments, then Corollary
16
implies there still exists an active extension swap.

Formally, we construct a sequence of active extension swaps in a single phase “top-down” in each in-tree. In the first
stage
, we only execute active swaps involving a node

, where

Lemma 17
.

Suppose we are given an initial financial network with edge-ranking rules and constant

For every
v\in V_{i}
there is no active extension swap involving
v
in any stage
j>i

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction. Consider stage 1 and

is the root of some in-tree

. Consider a liability value and suppose

For contradiction, consider the first time at which an extension swap involving

that can be swapped with one that is deeper in the tree and has less payment. However, this is impossible: In any previous extension swap the edges that received strictly increased payments lie on the path

still have the payments they had at the end of stage 1. Moreover, all active edges get swapped only in the respective subtrees. As such, no active extension swap with the edges of

For the hypothesis, assume the lemma holds for all stages

and a node

∎

It remains to construct a short sequence of active extension swaps within a single stage

, no ancestor is involved in any extension swap of stage

Lemma 18
.

For any financial network with edge-ranking rules, if every

Proof.

Consider

with

Theorem 19
.

The
SemiSwap

3.2
Sequences of Arbitrary Swaps

In this section, we consider sequences of arbitrary swaps. In contrast to semi-positive ones, these swaps have no natural criterion of progress. We again focus on swaps in which a single bank
v
has to improve strictly. Unfortunately, sequences of such arbitrary
v
-improving swaps can be very long, for every monotone allocation rule (including edge-ranking and proportional rules). Our results show that there are initial networks from which

Indeed, we obtain a more general statement. Consider a maximization problem for the total assets of a given bank
v
defined as follows. In an instance of the
\textsc{MaxAssets}_{v}

Furthermore, an instance of

\mathcal{O}

Observe that any set of edges

v
in the resulting clearing state. The objective function in the instance are the total assets for a given bank
v

Finally, to express edge swaps, we define a neighborhood over the set of financial networks whose edges are consistent with the stubs in the input. For a given financial network
\mathcal{F}

Proposition 20

4
Reachability

A debt swap changes the underlying graph structure of the corresponding financial network. It is a natural reachability question whether, for two given networks, one network can be transformed into the other via a sequence of debt swaps.

More formally, in the
Reach
problem we are given an initial network
\mathcal{F}
and a target network
\mathcal{G}
. Both networks have the same set
V
of nodes and are consistent with the stubs from an instance of
\textsc{MaxAssets}_{v}
. For convenience, we introduce an explicit term for this property. Every node
v\in V
has the same
incidence profile
in both networks (i.e., for each value
l_{i}
the same number of outgoing edges with weight
l_{i}
in both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
and the same number of incoming edges with
l_{i}
in both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
). Moreover,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
have the same monotone allocation rule, i.e.,
v
uses the same edge ranking to allocate assets to outgoing stubs, for each
v\in V
. Overall, we say the networks
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are
consistent with each other
. In Section
3.2
we observed that any debt swap maintains network consistency. Hence, if
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are not consistent with each other, then the answer to every reachability problem considered in this section is trivially negative. For the remainder of the section, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are consistent with each other. To avoid trivialities, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
have different edge sets.

When applying a finite sequence of debt swaps
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots)
to
\mathcal{F}
, we first execute swap
\sigma_{1}
in
\mathcal{F}
and denote the resulting network by
\mathcal{F}^{\sigma_{1}}
. We again assume to have access to an efficient clearing oracle
\mathcal{O}
to compute the clearing state. Then we perform
\sigma_{2}
in
\mathcal{F}^{\sigma_{1}}
, then
\sigma_{3}
in
\mathcal{F}^{\sigma_{2}}
, etc. The process ends when all debt swaps in the sequence were applied. The resulting network is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
. Every financial network created during this sequence is called an
intermediate
network. We say that
\mathcal{G}
is

from

if there exists a sequence of debt swaps
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
such that
\mathcal{F}^{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\mathcal{G}
. The sequence

is called a

In the
Reach
problem, we are interested in reaching sequences of

debt swaps. If we only consider sequences of

\textsc{Reach}(v)

it might be sufficient that

stays solvent. Formally, in the
\textsc{Reach}(v,\ell)
problem we are given consistent networks
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
such that

has the
same

\ell
of total assets in both networks. Decide whether there exists a reaching sequence of debt swaps such that the total assets of

are at least
\ell
in every intermdeiate network.

Our reachability problems can be interpreted as instances of the
s
-
t
-Connectivity (
Stcon
) problem in a transition graph. The nodes of this transition graph are the networks consistent with
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
. There is a directed edge between networks
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and

Lemma 29

(Separability)
.

Suppose
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathcal{F}}
is a pre-network of
\mathcal{F}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{diff}}
the corresponding difference network. The clearing state of
\mathcal{F}
is given by the sum of clearing states in
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{diff}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}={\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}+{\mathbf{p}}^{\text{diff}}
.

Proof.

As mentioned in Section
2
, the feasible asset vectors for a financial network
\mathcal{F}
are solutions of a fixed point problem. We reformulate this property for the feasible payments – they are fixed points of a function
g
with
g_{(u,v)}(\mathbf{c})=f_{(u,v)}(a^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}c_{e})
mapping the convex, compact set
\{\mathbf{c}\mid 0\leq c_{e}\leq l_{e}\}
to itself. The fixed points of
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{g}_{(u,v)}(\mathbf{c})=f_{(u,v)}(\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}c_{e})
are the feasible payments in
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathcal{F}}
, the fixed points of
{g}^{\text{diff}}_{(u,v)}(\mathbf{c})={f}^{\text{diff}}_{(u,v)}(a^{x}_{u}-\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}c_{e})
the feasible payments in
{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{diff}}
.

For the maximal fixed point
{\mathbf{p}}^{\text{diff}}
of
{g}^{\text{diff}}
it holds that

\displaystyle{g}^{\text{diff}}_{(u,v)}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle={f}^{\text{diff}}_{(u,v)}\left(a^{x}_{u}-\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}p_{e}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{(u,v)}\left({\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{a}_{u}+a^{x}_{u}-\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}p_{e}\right)-f_{(u,v)}\left({\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{a}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{(u,v)}\left(\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e}+a^{x}_{u}-\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}p_{e}\right)-f_{(u,v)}\left(\hat{a}^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{(u,v)}\left(a^{x}_{u}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}(p_{e}+{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e})\right)-{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e}\kern 5.0pt.

The second equality follows from the definition of
{f}^{\text{diff}}_{(u,v)}
. The last equality follows since
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}
is the maximal fixed point of
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{g}
.

The clearing state of
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
\mathbf{p}\geq{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}
due to monotonicity of all
f_{e}
. Indeed, for
every
fixed point
\mathbf{c}
of
g
it holds that
\mathbf{c}\geq{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{c}}
, since
a^{x}_{v}>\hat{a}^{x}_{v}
for at least one
v
. As a consequence, every fixed point
\mathbf{c}
can be separated into
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
such that
\mathbf{c}={\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}+\bar{\mathbf{c}}
. Now consider
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
. Since
\mathbf{c}={\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}+\bar{\mathbf{c}}
is a fixed point, we have
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{(u,v)}+\bar{c}_{(u,v)}=f_{(u,v)}(a^{x}_{v}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}({\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e}+\bar{c}_{e}))
for all
(u,v)\in E
. Equivalently,

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{(u,v)}=f_{(u,v)}\left(a^{x}_{v}+\sum_{e\in E^{-}(u)}({\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{p}_{e}+\bar{c}_{e})\right)-\bar{c}_{(u,v)}

and, thus,
\bar{\mathbf{c}}
is a fixed point of
{g}^{\text{diff}}
. Hence, every fixed point of
g
can be separated into
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}
and a fixed point for
{g}^{\text{diff}}
. Since the clearing state
\mathbf{p}
is the
maximal
fixed point of
g
, it must use the
maximal
fixed point of
{g}^{\text{diff}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}={\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}{\mathbf{p}}+{\mathbf{p}}^{\text{diff}}
.
∎

Definition 32

(Linearity)
.

Lemma 33

(Non-Expansivity)
.

For a given financial network, let

be the set of all sinks. If the external assets of a source

By slight abuse of notation, we use

to denote the resulting financial network

s

\Delta

We use

and

to denote the incoming and outgoing assets of

in the clearing state of

and

. Then,

and all other banks, it holds that

\sum_{u\in V\setminus(T\cup\{s\})}p^{-}_{u}+\sum_{t\in T}p^{-}_{t}+p^{-}_{s}=\sum_{u\in V\setminus(T\cup\{s\})}p^{+}_{u}+\sum_{t\in T}p^{+}_{t}+p^{+}_{s}\;.

(1)

and

\sum_{t\in T}p^{-}_{t}=\sum_{u\in V\setminus(T\cup\{s\})}(p^{+}_{u}-p^{-}_{u})+p^{+}_{s}\;.

Every bank

has external assets of 0 and, thus, the outgoing payments of

are upper bounded by the incoming ones, i.e.,

We proceed to show how this result implies hardness results for sequences of swaps. In the instances constructed in the last proof, set

is included in the cover

if and only if bank

has positive incoming assets. Every network with consistent set of edges corresponds to some collection of sets

, and vice versa. Suppose we are given such a network for a collection

from/to the corresponding collection of subsets. Thus, we can reach the network corresponding to the optimal set cover

Corollary 44
.

For a given financial network with monotone allocation rules, an efficient clearing oracle, and a bank
v
, it is
APX
-hard to compute a sequence of arbitrary debt swaps such that (1) the total assets of
v
are maximal or (2) the sum of total assets is maximal.

We strengthen this result as follows. Suppose we start with the collection
[\ell]

[\ell]

[\ell]
, there is a sequence of at most
\ell

v
-improving debt swaps to the network corresponding to
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

Corollary 45
.

For a given financial network with monotone allocation rules, an efficient clearing oracle, and a bank
v
, it is
APX
-hard to compute a sequence of
v
-improving debt swaps such that (1) the total assets of
v
are maximal or (2) the sum of total assets is maximal.
