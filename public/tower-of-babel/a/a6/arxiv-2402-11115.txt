Transverse linear stability of one-dimensional solitary gravity water waves
Frédéric Rousset, Changzhen Sun

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the transverse linear asymptotic stability of one-dimensional small-amplitude solitary waves of the gravity water-waves system. More precisely, we show that the semigroup of the linearized operator about the solitary wave decays exponentially within a spectral subspace supplementary to the space generated by the spectral projection on continuous resonant modes. The key element of the proof is to establish suitable uniform resolvent estimates. To achieve this, we use different arguments depending on the size of the transverse frequencies, for high transverse frequencies, we use reductions based on pseudodifferential calculus, for intermediate ones, we use an energy-based approach relying on the design of various appropriate energy functionals for different regimes of longitudinal frequencies and for low frequencies, we use the KP-II approximation. As a corollary of our main result, we also get the spectral stability in the unweighted energy space. Keywords : solitary waves; asymptotic stability; water waves system; resolvent estimates. 2010 MSC : 35B40, 35C08, 35Q31, 76B15, 76B25.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the transverse linear asymptotic stability of line solitons for the three-dimensional gravity water-waves system in a channel with finite depth. The equations of motions are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}U+U\cdot\nabla U+\nabla P+ge_{3}=0,\\[3.0pt]
\,\mathrm{div}\,U=0,\,\mathop{\rm curl}\nolimits U=0.\end{array}\right.(t,X,z)\in\Omega_{t}\,.

\Omega_{t}=\{-h\leq z\leq\zeta(t,X)\},\quad X=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

\partial_{t}\zeta(t,X)+U_{X}(t,X,\zeta(X))\cdot\nabla_{X}\zeta(t,X)-U_{3}(t,X,\zeta(X))=0\,.

We also impose that the pressure

At the bottom of the fluid, we add the boundary condition:

1.1.
The line solitary waves

As the velocity
U
is divergence and curl free, we can write
U=\nabla\Phi,
where the velocity potential
\Phi
solves the elliptic equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta\Phi=0,\quad(X,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{-h<z<\zeta(t,X)\},\\[3.0pt]
\Phi|_{z=\zeta}=\varphi(t,X),\quad\partial_{z}\Phi|_{z=-h}=0.\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

We then define the Dirichlet-Neumann operator

G[\zeta]\varphi=(\partial_{z}\Phi-\nabla_{X}\zeta\cdot\nabla_{X}\Phi)|_{z=\zeta(t,X)}=\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{X}\zeta|^{2}}\,\nabla\Phi(X,\zeta(t,X))\cdot n(t,X)

where

n(t,X)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{X}\zeta|^{2}}}\big(-\nabla_{X}\zeta,1\big)^{t}

is the unit outward normal vector to the free surface.
The system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) is thus equivalent to the following system on
(\zeta,\varphi)^{t}
evaluated on the surface:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\zeta=G[\zeta]\varphi,\\
\partial_{t}\varphi=-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{X}\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\big(G[\zeta]\varphi+\nabla_{X}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{X}\zeta\big)^{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{X}\zeta|^{2}}}-g\zeta.\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

Since we shall study the solitary waves with speed
c>0,
we first make change of variable
(x,y)\rightarrow(x-ct,y)
to change (
1.5
) as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\zeta=c\partial_{x}\zeta+G[\zeta]\varphi,\\
\partial_{t}\varphi=c\partial_{x}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{X}\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\big(G[\zeta]\varphi+\nabla_{X}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{X}\zeta\big)^{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{X}\zeta|^{2}}}-g\zeta.\end{array}\right.

As is classical in the study of existence of solitary waves, we perform the following change of variable to make
the equation non-dimensional:

\hat{t}={ct}/{h},\quad\hat{X}={X}/{h},\quad\tilde{\zeta}=\zeta/h,\quad\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi/ch.

(1.6)

Then the equation becomes (we omit tilde for the sake of notational convenience)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\zeta=\partial_{x}\zeta+G[\zeta]\varphi\,,\\
\partial_{t}\varphi=\partial_{x}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{X}\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(G[\zeta]\varphi+\nabla_{X}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{X}\zeta)^{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{X}\zeta|^{2}}}-\gamma\zeta\,,\end{array}\right.

(1.7)

where
\gamma=\frac{gh}{c^{2}}.
Note that the above Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation of the water waves system has the following Hamiltonian structure:

\partial_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\zeta\\
\varphi\end{array}\right)=J\,\delta{\mathcal{H}}[\zeta,\varphi],

with

\displaystyle J\,

\displaystyle:=\,\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[\zeta,\varphi]

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{2}\int G[\eta]\varphi\cdot\varphi+\gamma\zeta^{2}-2\,\zeta\,\partial_{x}\varphi\,.

The existence of one dimensional solitary waves, which are stationary solutions to (
1.7
) depending only on the one-dimensional variable
x
, is established in
[
8
,
31
]
:

Theorem 1.1

(Restatement of Beale’s result
[
8
]
)
.

Assuming
\gamma=1-\epsilon^{2},

there exists
\epsilon_{0}
such that for every
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
there is a stationary solution

\zeta_{c}(x),\varphi_{c}(x)
(independent of

), given by

\zeta_{c}(x)=\epsilon^{2}\Theta(\epsilon x,\epsilon),\qquad\varphi_{c}(x)=\epsilon\Phi(\epsilon x,\epsilon),

(1.8)

where
\Theta
and
\Phi
satisfy the following:

(1)
There exists
d>0
, for any integers
k\geq 0,\,\ell\geq 1,
there exists
C_{k},\,C_{\ell}
such that:

|\partial_{\hat{x}}^{k}\Theta(\hat{x},\epsilon)|\leq C_{k}\,e^{-d\,|\hat{x}|},\qquad|\partial_{\hat{x}}^{\ell}\Phi(\hat{x},\epsilon)|\leq C_{\ell}\,e^{-d\,|\hat{x}|}.

(2) It holds that when
\epsilon=0,

\Theta(\hat{x},0)=\partial_{\hat{x}}\Phi(\hat{x},0)=\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}(\hat{x})

(1.9)

where
\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}(\hat{x})=\mathrm{sech}^{2}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\hat{x}\bigg)
.

Note that
\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}
is the steady solution to the KdV equation in the moving frame at unit speed:

-\partial_{x}\Psi+3\Psi\partial_{x}\Psi+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{3}\Psi=0.

Note that the existence is proven in
[
8
]
by using the Levi-Civita formulation but the result can be easily transformed into the Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation stated above.

We now linearize the system (

We can observe that when

.

Let

There exist
\epsilon_{0}>0,\hat{\eta}_{0}=\hat{\eta}_{0}(\hat{a})>0

G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]=\colon e^{ax}\,G[\zeta_{c}]\,e^{-ax}
and study it in the usual Sobolev space:

\displaystyle H_{\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\bigg\{f\,\big|\,\|f\|_{H_{\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\colon\bigg(\int\bigg(\big|\frac{\nabla}{\langle D\rangle^{1/2}}f\big|^{2}+a^{2}|f|^{2}\bigg)(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}<+\infty\bigg\}.

(1.20)

Note that
H_{\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
still depends on
a
but we neglect this dependence in the notation in order to distinguish it with the weighted space
H_{a,\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

An equivalent definition of
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]

is that, for any
f\in H_{*}^{1/2},

G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]f(x)=\sqrt{1+|\nabla\zeta_{c}|^{2}}\big(\partial_{z}\Phi_{a}-\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}\,(\partial_{x}-a)\Phi_{a}\big),

(1.21)

where
\Phi_{a}(x,z)
solves the following equation in
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[-1,\zeta_{c}]:

-\big((\partial_{x}-a)^{2}+\Delta_{y,z}\big)\Phi_{a}=0,\quad\Phi_{a}|_{z=\zeta_{c}}=f,\quad\partial_{z}\Phi_{a}|_{z=-1}=0.

(1.22)

When the surface is flat, that is when
\zeta_{c}=0,
we can find explicitly the solution of the above problem by using the Fourier transform which is now legitimate:

\displaystyle E[0](f)(\cdot,z)=\frac{\cosh\big({\mu_{a}(D)(z+1)}\big)}{\cosh{\mu_{a}(D)}}f\,,\quad\text{ with \, }\mu_{a}(D)=\sqrt{(D_{x}+ia)^{2}+D_{y}^{2}}\,,

(1.23)

where
D
stands for
(D_{x},D_{y})
and
for a complex number
c,

we define
\sqrt{c}
as the limit of the square root with positive real part
of the complex numbers
c+i\delta
when
0<\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}.
Thanks to (
1.23
), we derive that

G_{a}[0]=(\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a})(D)\,.

By using the notation
{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[-1,0]
,
note that for any positive constant
a,
the solution
E[0]f
belongs only to the anisotropic homogeneous space

\dot{H}_{x,z}^{1}(\mathcal{S})=\big\{f\,|\,\partial_{x}f,\partial_{z}f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{S}})\big\}.
However,
its derivative in the transverse variable
y
cannot be bounded in
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}).
Indeed, the symbol
\mu_{a}^{-1}(\xi,\eta)=\big(\sqrt{(\xi+ia)^{2}+\eta^{2}}\big)^{-1}
may exhibit singularity around nonzero points
(\xi,\eta)=(0,\pm a).

This degeneracy is a source of new difficulties
in the study of the transverse stability, in particular, we cannot choose our functional framework as
L_{a}^{2}\times\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}^{-1}L_{a}^{2}
as in
[
48
]
where the asymptotic linear stability is studied for one-dimensional water waves.

To get useful properties of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator for a non-zero surface, as it is classically done, we can use a change of variable to reduce the problem to
the fixed domain
{\mathcal{S}}
and investigate the solvability of the difference between the resulting unknown and
E[0]f.
An important observation is that, although the corresponding linear operator is not elliptic, one can take benefits of
the Poincaré inequality (due to the zero upper boundary condition) when studying the perturbation to control its
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}})
norm by the
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathcal{S}})
norm. Another key point is that
the source term does not contain any
\partial_{y}
derivative of
E[0]f
which does not belong to
L^{2}({\mathcal{S}}).
This provides the solvability and uniqueness of the solution to (
1.22
) in the new variables in the space
H_{0,up}^{1}({\mathcal{S}})+E[0]f.
We refer to Lemma
C.1
for more details.

The rough study of
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
above also motivates us to consider the transformed linear operator

{L}_{a}=\colon e^{ax}L\,e^{-ax}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(\partial_{x}-a)(d_{c}\cdot)&G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]\\[5.0pt]
-w_{c}&d_{c}(\partial_{x}-a)\end{array}\right).\,\,

(1.24)

Since the space
X=\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H_{\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be identified with
e^{ax}X_{a},

we see that the study of
L
on the weighted space
X_{a}
is equivalent to the study of
L_{a}
on the unweighted space
X.
Consequently, Theorem
1.4
is equivalent to the following theorem:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
X=\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H_{\star}^{{1}/{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\,\mathbb{Q}_{a}(\eta_{0})=e^{ax}\,\mathbb{Q}(\eta_{0})\,e^{-ax}.
There exist
\epsilon_{0}>0,\beta_{0}=\beta_{0}(\hat{a})>0,\hat{\eta}_{0}=\hat{\eta}_{0}(\hat{a})>0,C_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0},0<\beta\leq\beta_{0},\,
and any
\lambda\in\Omega_{\beta,\,\epsilon},
the operator

(\lambda-L_{a})
is invertible on the space
\mathbb{Q}_{a}(\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta}_{0})X
and

\|(\lambda-L_{a})^{-1}\|_{B(\mathbb{Q}_{a}(\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta}_{0})X)}\leq C_{0}\,.

(1.25)

1.3.
Outline of the proof and main difficulties

In this subsection, we explain the main issues and ideas for the proof of the existence of the resonant modes in Theorem
1.2
and the resolvent estimate in Theorem
1.7
.

1.3.1.
Resonant modes

The resonant modes

\big(\lambda(\eta),e^{iy\,\eta}U(\cdot,\eta)\big)_{-\eta_{0}\leq\eta\leq\eta_{0}}

are searched by looking for solutions of
the eigenproblems

(\lambda(\eta)-L(\eta))\,U(\cdot,\eta)=0.

As mentioned previously,
L(0)
corresponds to
the linearized operator of one-dimensional water waves about the 1-d soliton
(\zeta_{c},\varphi_{c}),
which has a nontrivial generalized kernel of dimension two.
To find the curves
\lambda(\eta)
bifurcating from
\eta=0
and the eigenfunctions
U(\cdot,\eta),
we use the classical Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction approach. While featuring some algebraic differences, the proof share some similarities with the the arguments presented in
[
44
]
where transverse linear stability of line solitary waves is established for the Benney-Luke equation.

1.3.2.
Uniform resolvent estimate

We now give more details about the proof of the uniform resolvent estimate (
1.25
), which is the core of this paper.
In order to get (
1.25
), the first thing we have to detect is the presence of some damping effects once we work in the weighted space
X_{a}
due to the sign of the group velocity for the constant coefficients part of the original linearized operator
L,
or equivalently the transformed operator
L_{a}
on the unweighted space
X.
This has been used since the pioneer work of Pego and Weinstein
[
50
,
51
]
on the asymptotic stability of KdV equation and Boussinesq equations. To be more precise, consider the constant operator

L_{a}^{0}=\colon\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{x}-a&G_{a}[0]\\[5.0pt]
-\gamma&\partial_{x}-a\end{array}\right),

which is obtained by setting
(\zeta_{c},\varphi_{c})=0
in the definition of
L_{a}
(
1.24
). The two eigenvalues of its symbol are

\lambda_{\pm}^{0}=\colon i(\xi+ia)\pm\sqrt{-\gamma\,\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)}\,,\,\qquad\big(\,\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)=\sqrt{(\xi+ia)^{2}+\eta^{2}}\,\big)\,.

Some algebraic computations show that for any
(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
there exists a positive constant
\beta(\hat{\alpha})={\mathcal{O}}(\hat{\alpha}),
such that

{\rm Re}\,\lambda_{\pm}^{0}\leq-\beta(\hat{\alpha})\,\epsilon^{3}\,.

Consequently, for any
\lambda
such that
{\rm Re}\,\lambda\geq-\beta(\hat{\alpha})\,\epsilon^{3}/2,
it holds that

\big|(\lambda-\lambda_{\pm}^{0})^{-1}\big|\leq\frac{2}{\beta(\hat{\alpha})\epsilon^{3}}\,,

which enables us to conclude that
(\lambda-L_{a}^{0})
is invertible and that the resolvent bounds

\sup_{{\rm Re}\,\lambda\geq-\beta(\hat{\alpha})\,\epsilon^{3}/2}\|(\lambda-L_{a}^{0})^{-1}\|_{B(X)}<+\infty\,

holds. The next step is to obtain the same result (
1.25
) for
L_{a}
in which the terms depending on the solitary waves
(\zeta_{c},\varphi_{c})
are nonzero but are small. The main difficulties in order to obtain this result in the water-waves case are the following.

In the case of the water waves system which is fully nonlinear, the part of the system involving coefficients
depending on the solitary wave cannot be seen as a lower order small perturbation
as in the study of the linearization of semilinear problems,
see for instance
[
44
]
.
More precisely, here the appearance of
v_{c}\partial_{x}
in the diagonal terms of
L_{a}
results in operators that are not bounded and
the difference between
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
and
G_{a}[0]
is not a bounded operator from
H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).
To
address this issue, we cut frequencies and use pseudo-differential calculus
and energy estimates in the high-frequency regions. Further details will be provided later.

Another difficulty lies in the presence of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}].
If
\zeta_{c}
is
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
smooth which is the case in the current study, one can expect to adapt well-known results for the case
a=0
(see
[
5
]

for example) to get that

G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
is a pseudo-differential operator with symbol

\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}(x,\xi,\eta)=\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}(x,\xi,\eta)+\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{0}(x,\xi,\eta)+\cdots\,,

where the principal symbol is

\lambda^{1}_{\zeta_{c}}(x,\xi,\eta)=\sqrt{(\xi+ia)^{2}+\eta^{2}(1+(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c})^{2})}\,.

However, there is no exact formula for the symbol
\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}
.
We thus need to use approximations of
\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}
in conjunction with appropriate perturbation arguments. As will be clear in the proof,
here an approximation
\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{app}
is admissible if
\mathrm{Op}(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}-\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{app})
is a bounded operator from
H^{1/2}_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with operator norm sufficiently small compared to
\epsilon,
for instance

\|\mathrm{Op}(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}-\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{app})\|_{B(H_{*}^{1/2},\,L^{2})}\leq C\epsilon^{\alpha},\quad(\alpha>1).

It turns out that the needed quality of the approximation, that is to say the minimal
\alpha
that we can allow,
is different in different frequency regions.

On the one hand,
when focusing on uniform (in
\epsilon
) high transverse frequencies
\{|\eta|\geq 2\}
, one may expect that the principal symbol
\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}
could be used as an admissible approximation. Nevertheless, although

\mathrm{Op}(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1})
approximates well the operator
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
in terms of regularity, it is far from being a good approximation in terms of the size of the operator norm within
B(H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})).
Indeed, the operator norm of the difference is only
{\mathcal{O}}(1).
This is not enough to prove resolvent bounds by perturbation arguments. Nevertheless, we will show in
Lemma
C.2
that an admissible approximation can be obtained by using a modification
of the principal symbol

\tilde{\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}=\colon\lambda^{1}_{\zeta_{c}}\tanh\lambda^{1}_{\zeta_{c}}.

The operator norm of the difference between
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
and
\mathrm{Op}(\tilde{\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{1})
is now of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2}).
We remark that unsurprisingly, such a modification is needed due to the fact that we are considering water waves in a domain with finite depth.

On the other hand, in the region of lower
transverse frequencies
\{|\eta|\leq 2\},
the damping effect is quite weak, it thus necessitates a more accurate approximation of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator. Here, we shall adapt in the setting of weighted spaces its first order expansion in terms of
\zeta_{c},
which is a pseudo-differential operator with the symbol
[
34
]
:

{\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{app}(x,\xi,\eta)=\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)+\zeta_{c}(x)\mu_{a}^{2}(\tanh^{2}\mu_{a}-1)(\xi,\eta),\quad\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)=\sqrt{(\xi+ia)^{2}+\eta^{2}},

(1.26)

the error belonging to
B(H_{*}^{1/2},\,L^{2})
with norm of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{3}).

We are now in position to outline the
proof of uniform resolvent bounds (
1.25
). We first use frequency cutoffs and different methods to establish the resolvent bounds in different frequency regions. We divide the frequency space
(\xi,\eta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
into three regions, corresponding to (uniform) high, intermediate, and low
transverse
frequencies:

\displaystyle R^{UH}=\big\{(\xi,\eta)\big|\,|\eta|\geq 2\big\},\quad R^{I}=\big\{(\xi,\eta)\big|\,A\epsilon^{2}\leq|\eta|\leq 2\big\},\quad R^{L}=\big\{(\xi,\eta)\big|\,|\eta|\leq A\epsilon^{2}\big\},

(1.27)

where
A
is a sufficiently large constant satisfying
A^{2}\epsilon\leq 1.

We shall call the union of the uniform high frequency and intermediate frequency regions the high frequency region. Note that at this stage we only cut the transverse frequencies and the longitudinal frequency
\xi
is just a fixed parameter. We shall perform further localization in
\xi
within the different regions when needed.

\bullet

Uniform high transverse frequencies.

In the uniform high transverse frequency region
R^{UH},

we define a modified operator
\tilde{L}_{a}
via replacing the Dirichlet Neumann operator in
L_{a}
by
\mathrm{Op}(\tilde{\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}).
We then diagonalize it by using pseudo differential calculus
and thus reduce the existence of the resolvent problem as well as the uniform resolvent bounds to the study of
\lambda-\lambda_{\pm}^{1}(x,\xi,\eta),
where

\lambda_{\pm}^{1}=i\bigg(d_{c}(x)(\xi+ia)\pm\sqrt{w_{c}}(x)\sqrt{\lambda^{1}_{\zeta_{c}}\tanh\lambda^{1}_{\zeta_{c}}}(x,\xi,\eta)\bigg).

It turns out that when
|\eta|\geq 2,
for any
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
any
\lambda\in\Omega_{\beta,\,\epsilon}
with
\beta
small enough, it holds that

{\rm Re}\,(\lambda-\lambda_{\pm}^{1}(x,\xi,\eta))\gtrsim\epsilon.

This, together with Lemma
C.2
, allows us to establish the invertibility of
(\lambda-L_{a})
and the corresponding resolvent bounds in this region by perturbation arguments.

\bullet

Intermediate transverse frequencies.

The study in the intermediate transverse frequency region
R^{I}
is more involved due to a singularity in the previous diagonalization process. Indeed, by using
\mathrm{Op}({\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{app})
to approximate
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}],
we would involve an operator of the form
\mathrm{Op}\big(1/\sqrt{{\lambda}_{\zeta_{c}}^{app}}\big)
which
unfortunately does not belong to
B(L^{2},H_{*}^{1/2}).

This is due to the singularity of the symbol
\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)
in the region

{\mathcal{S}}_{K,\delta}=\bigg\{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\big|\,|\xi|\leq K\epsilon,\,\delta\leq\frac{|\eta|}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+a^{2}}}\leq 2\bigg\},\qquad(\,0<\delta<1\text{ small },\,K\sim\sqrt{A}).

However, since this region is indeed in low frequencies both in longitudinal and transverse variables, we will show that we approximate
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
by simply
-\Delta
and perform directly energy estimates to obtain the desired resolvent bounds.
Since we would have to localize in the longitudinal frequencies and study different sub-regions separately, we found more convenient to adopt in this whole region
R^{I}
an energy-based approach, achieved through the design of appropriate energy functionals.

\bullet

Low transverse frequencies.

In the low transverse frequency region
R^{L},
as in the previous case, in order not to lose derivatives,
it requires a further splitting involving frequencies dual to the
x
variable:

R_{H}^{L}=\{|\xi|\geq K\epsilon,\,|\eta|\leq A\epsilon^{2}\}\qquad R_{L}^{L}=\{|\xi|\leq K\epsilon,\,|\eta|\leq A\epsilon^{2}\}.

In the region
R_{H}^{L},
we perform energy estimates by designing an energy functional consistent with the symbol approximation (
1.26
) of the Dirichlet Neumann operator. In the region

R_{L}^{L},
we take benefits of the KP-II approximation (see Subsection
2.1
) and the resolvent bounds for the linearized operator of KP-II equation around line KdV soliton proved in
[
43
]
. Due to the weak damping effects in this region (which is of order
O(\epsilon^{3})
), instead of using the Fourier projector as in the study of the region
R^{I},
we use smooth cut-offs to split the longitudinal frequencies.
Let us remark finally that the arguments used in this region can provide a proof directly in cartesian coordinates of the linear asymptotic stability of the 1d solitary waves in the one-dimensional water waves system, which has been previously obtained in
[
48
]
in holomorphic coordinates.

Organization of the paper:

We establish the existence of continuous resonant modes, as stated in Theorem
1.2
, in Section 2. This section starts with a formal derivation of the KP-II equation from the water waves in the long-wave regime. Uniform resolvent estimates stated in Theorem
1.7
are then derived in Sections 3 and 4, in the high (uniform high and intermediate) and low transverse frequency regimes, respectively. The proof of spectral stability in the unweighted space stated in Theorem
1.6
is performed in Section 5.
Additionally, various technical results used in the main proofs are presented in the appendix. Specifically, in Appendix
C
, we prove several properties for the transformed Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]
, which are of independent interest.

2.
Spectral study
in the regime of low transverse frequency

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.2
. To be more specific, we will establish that for sufficiently small values of
\eta_{0}
, there exists a spectral curve
\lambda(\eta)\in C([-{\eta_{0}},{\eta_{0}}])
and corresponding eigenmodes
U(\cdot,\eta)\in Y_{a,\eta}=L_{a}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1/2}_{*,a,\eta}(\mathbb{R}),
such that

L(\eta)\,U(\cdot,\eta)=\lambda(\eta)\,U(\cdot,\eta).

(2.1)

Given that
L(\eta)=e^{-iy\eta}\,L\,e^{iy\eta}
, this yields a set of continuous resonant modes

\big(\lambda(\eta),e^{iy\,\eta}U(\cdot,\eta)\big)_{-\eta_{0}\leq\eta\leq\eta_{0}}

for the linearized operator
L
.

2.1.
KP-II approximation

As mentioned in the introduction, it is well known that the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation and the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili II (KP-II) equation are effective approximate models for describing the water wave dynamics in the long-wave region, in one and two dimensions respectively. We thus expect that two dimensional water waves system linearized about the one dimensional solitary water waves
(\zeta_{c},\varphi_{c})
can be approximated by the linearized KP-II equation about the line KdV soliton. This can be rigorously shown by implementing the following scaling:

\displaystyle\hat{t}=\epsilon^{3}t,\quad\,\hat{x}=\epsilon x,\,\quad\hat{y}=\epsilon^{2}y,\qquad(\hat{\zeta},\hat{\phi})(\hat{t},\hat{x})=(\zeta,\phi)(t,x),

(2.2)

Indeed, if
(\zeta,\phi)^{t}
solves the linearized equation (
1.11
), then
(\hat{\zeta},\hat{\phi})
solves:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\epsilon^{3}\partial_{\hat{t}}\hat{\zeta}-\epsilon\partial_{\hat{x}}(\hat{d}_{\epsilon}\hat{\zeta})-\hat{G}[\epsilon^{2}\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon}]\hat{\phi}=0,\\[3.0pt]
\epsilon^{3}\partial_{\hat{t}}\hat{\phi}-\epsilon\hat{d}_{\epsilon}\partial_{\hat{x}}\hat{\phi}+\hat{w}_{\epsilon}\hat{\zeta}=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

where

(\hat{v}_{\epsilon},\hat{Z}_{\epsilon},\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon})(\hat{t},\hat{x})=\epsilon^{-2}(v_{c},Z_{c},\zeta_{c})(t,x),\quad\hat{d}_{\epsilon}=1-\epsilon^{2}\hat{v}_{\epsilon},\,\quad\hat{w}_{\epsilon}=\gamma-\epsilon^{3}\hat{d}_{\epsilon}\partial_{x}\hat{Z}_{\epsilon}.

(2.4)

Moreover, the scaled Dirichlet-Neumann operator is defined as:

\hat{G}[\epsilon^{2}\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon}]f=\partial_{{z}}\hat{\Phi}-\epsilon^{4}\partial_{\hat{x}}\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon}\partial_{\hat{x}}\hat{\Phi}

where
\hat{\Phi}
solves the elliptic problem
in
\Omega=\mathbb{R}\times[-1,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon}]:

\big(\epsilon^{2}\partial_{\hat{x}}^{2}+\epsilon^{4}\partial_{\hat{y}}^{2}+\partial_{{z}}^{2}\big)\hat{\Phi}=0,\quad\hat{\Phi}|_{z=\epsilon^{2}\hat{\zeta}_{\epsilon}}=f,\quad\partial_{z}\hat{\Phi}|_{z=-1}=0.

Let us now skip the subscript
\hat{}
for the sake of notational convenience.
Plugging
\zeta=-w_{\epsilon}^{-1}\big(\epsilon^{3}\partial_{t}-\epsilon d_{\epsilon}\partial_{x}\big)\phi
into the first equation of (
2.3
), we find that
\phi
solves

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{w_{\epsilon}}\partial_{t}^{2}-\frac{2d_{\epsilon}}{w_{\epsilon}}\partial_{t}\partial_{x}-\partial_{x}\big(\frac{d_{\epsilon}}{w_{\epsilon}}\big)\partial_{t}+\epsilon^{-2}\partial_{x}\big(\frac{d_{\epsilon}^{2}}{w_{\epsilon}}\partial_{x}\big)+\epsilon^{-4}\hat{G}[{\epsilon^{2}\zeta_{\epsilon}}]\bigg)\phi=0.

(2.5)

We will do expansions with respect to
\epsilon
in the above equation. The expansion is usually performed by assuming that a high enough Sobolev norm of the solution is bounded
on the appropriate time scale. Here we shall assume that the problem is localized in a bounded frequency region with respect
to both variables, so that we assume that all the differential operators
with positive order are bounded operators in
L_{\hat{a}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).
We shall indeed perform this localization when we
will use this approximation argument.

It follows from the definition that

\frac{d_{\epsilon}}{w_{\epsilon}}=-1+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2}),\qquad\partial_{x}\big(\frac{d_{\epsilon}^{2}}{w_{\epsilon}}\partial_{x}\big)=\partial_{x}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{x}((1-2v_{\epsilon})\partial_{x})+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{3}).

Moreover, on the one hand, when the surface is flat, the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
\hat{G}[0]
has the form

\displaystyle\hat{G}[0]=\sqrt{-\epsilon^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{y}^{2})}\tanh\sqrt{-\epsilon^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{y}^{2})}\,

and thus has the expansion in a bounded frequency region:

\displaystyle\hat{G}[0]

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{y}^{2})-\frac{1}{3}\epsilon^{4}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{y}^{2})^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{6})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\epsilon^{4}(\partial_{y}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{4})+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{6}).

On the other hand, by the expansion of the Dirichlet-Neumann operator in terms of the small amplitude waves
\epsilon^{2}\zeta_{\epsilon}
(refer to Section 3.6.2 of
[
34
]
), we can write,
in a bounded frequency region,

\displaystyle\hat{G}[{\epsilon^{2}\zeta_{\epsilon}}]

\displaystyle=\hat{G}[0]-\hat{G}[0](\epsilon^{2}\zeta_{\epsilon})\hat{G}[0]-\epsilon^{4}\partial_{x}(\zeta_{\epsilon}\partial_{x})+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{6})

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\epsilon^{4}(\partial_{y}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{4})-\epsilon^{4}\partial_{x}(\zeta_{\epsilon}\partial_{x})+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{6}).

Consequently, the leading order of the equation (
2.5
) is:

-2\bigg(\partial_{t}-\partial_{x}\big(1-(2v_{0}+\zeta_{0})\cdot\big)+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{3}+\partial_{y}^{2}\partial_{x}^{-1}\big)\bigg)\partial_{x}\phi=0,

where
v_{0}=\zeta_{0}=\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}=\mathrm{sech}^{2}(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot)
is the soliton profile of the KdV equation:

\partial_{t}\Psi-\partial_{x}\Psi+3\Psi\partial_{x}\Psi+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{3}\Psi=0.

We thus find the linear operator arising in the KP-II equation linearized about the line soliton
\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}:

L_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}=\partial_{x}\big(1-\frac{1}{3}\partial_{x}^{2}-3\Psi_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KdV}}}\cdot\big)-\partial_{y}^{2}\partial_{x}^{-1}.

(2.6)

2.2.
Existence of resonant modes–proof of Theorem
1.2

Denote
U(\cdot,\eta)=(\zeta,\phi)^{t}(\cdot,\eta),\,\,G_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]=e^{-iy\eta}G_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]e^{iy\eta},

then
(
2.1
)
can be written as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda{\zeta}-\partial_{{x}}({d}_{c}{\zeta})-{G}_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]{\phi}=0\,,\\[3.0pt]
\lambda{\phi}-d_{c}\partial_{{x}}{\phi}+{w}_{c}{\zeta}=0\,,\end{array}\right.

or equivalently

\big(\lambda-\partial_{{x}}({d}_{c}\cdot)\big)\big(w_{c}^{-1}\big(\lambda-{d}_{c}\partial_{{x}}\big)\big)\phi+G_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]{\phi}=0\,.

Let us define

\lambda=\epsilon^{3}\Lambda,\,\,\quad\hat{\eta}=\epsilon^{-2}\eta,\,\,\quad\hat{\varphi}(\hat{x},\hat{\eta})=\varphi(x,\eta),\quad\hat{\psi}(\hat{x},\hat{\eta})=(\partial_{\hat{x}}\hat{\phi})(\hat{x},\hat{\eta}).

By the change of variable and skipping the
subscript
\hat{}
for clarity, we find in parallel to (
2.5
)

{\mathcal{T}}(\epsilon,\eta,\Lambda)\psi=\colon\big[L_{0}(\epsilon,\eta)+\Lambda(\eta)L_{1}(\epsilon)+\Lambda^{2}(\eta)L_{2}(\epsilon)\big]\psi=0\,,

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle L_{0}(\epsilon,\eta)=\epsilon^{-2}\partial_{x}\bigg(\frac{d_{\epsilon}^{2}}{w_{\epsilon}}\cdot\bigg)+\epsilon^{-4}\hat{G}_{\epsilon^{2}\eta}[{\epsilon^{2}\zeta_{\epsilon}}]\partial_{x}^{-1},

(2.8)

\displaystyle L_{1}(\epsilon)=-\partial_{x}\bigg(\frac{d_{\epsilon}}{w_{\epsilon}}\partial_{x}^{-1}\bigg)-\frac{d_{\epsilon}}{w_{\epsilon}},\qquad L_{2}(\epsilon)=\epsilon^{2}w_{\epsilon}^{-1}\partial_{x}^{-1}.

We refer to (
2.4

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})+(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})}{2}&\frac{(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})-(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})}{2}\sqrt{-\lambda_{0}/\gamma}\\[5.0pt]
-\gamma&\frac{(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})+(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})}{2}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\colon\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{11},&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{array}\right).

In light of this explicit expression, we have, by remembering

that

\displaystyle\|\big({\rm Id}_{2}-L_{a}^{0}(\xi,\eta)\big)^{-1}\|_{B(X_{A}^{I})}\lesssim\max\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R},|\eta|\leq 2}\bigg\{\frac{1}{|1-\lambda^{0}_{+}|}\,,\frac{1}{|1-\lambda^{0}_{-}|}\,,\frac{(1+\xi^{2})^{1/4}}{|(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})|}\bigg\}<+\infty.

(3.27)

Note that we have used the facts

and
|{\rm Im}\,(1-\lambda^{0}_{\pm})|\thickapprox(1+\xi^{2}+\eta^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
for
|\xi|\gg 1.

To show (
3.25
), it suffices to prove the following estimates

\displaystyle\|A_{11}(\partial_{x}-a)+A_{12}(d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c})\|_{B(L^{2},L^{2})}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\|A_{11}(G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{a}[0])+A_{12}(v_{c}(\partial_{x}-a))\|_{B(H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}},L^{2})}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\|A_{21}(\partial_{x}-a)+A_{22}(d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c})\|_{B(L^{2},H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}})}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\|A_{21}(G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{a}[0])+A_{22}(v_{c}(\partial_{x}-a))\|_{B(H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}},H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}})}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}.

Thanks to the the explicit expressions (
3.26
) and the estimates

\|v_{c}\|_{W_{x}^{1,\infty}}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},\quad\|d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c}\|_{{W_{x}^{1,\infty}}}\lesssim\epsilon^{3},

as well as the estimate

\displaystyle\qquad\|\mathbb{I}_{2}(D_{y})(G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{a}[0])\|_{B(H_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}

whose proof is detailed in Lemma

,
the above four estimates are the consequences of the following algebraic computation:

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R},|\eta|\leq 2}\big(\langle\xi\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}\big)\max\bigg\{\frac{1}{|1-\lambda^{0}_{+}|}\,,\frac{1}{|1-\lambda^{0}_{-}|}\,,\frac{1}{|(1-\lambda^{0}_{+})(1-\lambda^{0}_{-})|}\bigg\}<+\infty.

To summarize, we have shown that
1\in\rho(L_{a};X_{A}^{I})
and thus the proof of Proposition
3.3
is complete.
∎

\displaystyle=\gamma\,{\rm Re}\,\int\,\mathrm{div}_{a}(\zeta_{c}\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)V_{2})\,\,\overline{(Q_{a}(D)-Q_{-a}(D))V_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{4}

\displaystyle=2a\gamma\,{\rm Re}\,\int\,\zeta_{c}(\partial_{x}-a)Q_{a}(D)V_{2}\,\,\overline{Q_{a}(D)V_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\partial_{x}d_{c}\big(\gamma|V_{1}|^{2}-|\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}V_{2}|^{2}\big)-\partial_{x}(d_{c}\,\zeta_{c})|\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)V_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{6}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\,\int\pi_{r}(D)\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}(d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c}V_{1})\overline{\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}V_{2}}+\zeta_{c}\pi_{r}(D)\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)(d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c}V_{1})\overline{\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)V_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{7}

\displaystyle={\rm Re}\,\int\big(W_{\lambda 1}\overline{\gamma V_{1}}+\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}W_{\lambda 2}\,\overline{\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}V_{2}}+\zeta_{c}\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)W_{\lambda 2}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)V_{2}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

We now control
K_{1}-K_{7}
term by term. At first, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality along with Lemma
3.5
and the fact that
\|v_{c}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},
we can find a constant
C>0
such that, for
A
large enough and
\epsilon
sufficiently small,

{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\leq-2\bigg(a\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}(1-v_{c}(x))-\gamma\sup_{|\xi|\leq\delta,\,A\epsilon^{2}\leq|\eta|\leq 2}\big|{\rm Im}\,\sqrt{\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a}}(\xi,\eta)\big|\bigg){\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\leq-CA\epsilon^{3}{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t)).

Next, we apply Lemma
C.7
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to find that

{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\,.

Hereafter, we denote
\lesssim
as
\leq C
for some constant that is independent of
A
and
\epsilon.

Moreover, let us notice that
Q_{a}(D)\in S^{\infty}
and
by (
B.1
),

|Q_{a}(\xi,\eta)-Q_{-a}(\xi,\eta)|\lesssim|{\rm Im}\,\mu_{a}|\lesssim a\lesssim\epsilon,\qquad\forall\,(\xi,\eta)\in{\rm Supp}\,\pi_{r}\,,

which, together with the fact

\|\zeta_{c}\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim\epsilon^{2},\,
yield:

{\mathcal{K}}_{3}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\,.

In addition, thanks to the facts
a=\hat{a}\epsilon
and
\|\partial_{x}(\zeta_{c},d_{c})\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim\epsilon^{3},
one readily gets that:

{\mathcal{K}}_{4}+{\mathcal{K}}_{5}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\,.

Furthermore, as
\|\pi_{r}(D)\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}\|_{B(L^{2})}\lesssim 1,
and
\|\partial_{x}Z_{c}\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim\epsilon^{3},
we find also

{\mathcal{K}}_{6}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\,.

Finally, it stems from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that

\|\pi_{r}(D)\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}W_{\lambda 2}\|_{L^{2}}\approx\|\pi_{r}(D)\nabla_{a}W_{\lambda 2}\|_{L^{2}}\approx\|\pi_{r}(D)W_{\lambda 2}\|_{H_{*}^{1/2}}

that

{\mathcal{K}}_{7}\lesssim\|W_{\lambda}\|_{X}\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))}\,.

Therefore, upon choosing
A
large enough, it holds that:

\partial_{t}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))\leq-\frac{C}{2}A\epsilon^{3}{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))+C_{1}\|W_{\lambda}\|_{X}\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(V(t))}\,.

It then follows from the Grönwall inequality that:

\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}(V(t))\leq e^{-CA\epsilon^{3}t/2}\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{a}}(V(0))+C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-CA\epsilon^{3}(t-s)/2}\|W_{\lambda}(s)\|_{X}\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Plugging
V(t)=e^{\lambda t}U_{r}
into the above estimate and since we had set
W_{\lambda}=\pi_{r}(D)e^{\lambda t}\big(F+\tilde{L}_{a}(U_{h}+U_{s})\big),
we find the inequality

\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}(U_{r})\leq e^{-\beta_{\lambda}t}\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}(U_{r})+C_{1}\big(\|F\|_{X}+\|\pi_{r}(D){L}_{a}^{1}(U_{h}+U_{s})\|_{X}\big)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\beta_{\lambda}(t-s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,

where
\beta_{\lambda}={\rm Re}\,\lambda+CA\epsilon^{3}/2>CA\epsilon^{3}/4

for any
\lambda\in\Omega_{\beta,\epsilon}
provided that
A
is large enough.
As a result, we deduce

\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}(U_{r})\lesssim(A\epsilon^{3})^{-1}\big(\|F\|_{X}+\|\pi_{r}(D){L}_{a}^{1}(U_{h}+U_{s})\|_{X}\big)\,.

The estimate (
3.23
) then follows by applying estimate (
C.13
).

4.
Spectral stability for low transverse frequencies

In this section, we focus on the low transverse frequency region
R^{L}
defined in (
1.27
). Let us set
Z=\mathbb{Q}_{a}(\epsilon^{2}\eta_{0})\mathbb{I}_{A\epsilon^{2}}(D_{y})X,
we want to show that for
\epsilon
small enough,
\Omega_{\beta,\epsilon}\subset\rho(L_{a};Z)
and that there is a constant
C>0,
such that for any
\lambda\in\Omega_{\beta,\epsilon},

\|(\lambda I-L_{a})^{-1}\|_{B(Z)}\leq C.

(4.1)

We begin with a lemma for the resolvent estimate in the high longitudinal frequency region
\{|\xi|\geq K\epsilon\}:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
U\in\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{K\epsilon}(D_{x})X,\,F\in X
which satisfy

\big(\lambda I-L_{a}\big)U=F,
then it holds that, for
K>1
sufficiently large and
\epsilon
small such that
K^{4}\epsilon\leq 1,

\displaystyle\|U\|_{X}\lesssim K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\,\|F\|_{X}.

(4.2)

Proof.

The proof is close to the one of Proposition
3.3
, detailed in Subsection
3.2
. The general idea is to split
U
into two parts
U=U^{h}+U^{m},
where
U^{h},U^{m}
are localized respectively on the (uniform) high and intermediate longitudinal frequencies :

U^{h}=\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})U,\qquad U^{m}=(\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{K\epsilon}{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta})(D_{x})U.

By the definition,
U^{h},U^{m}
solves the equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})\,(\lambda I-L_{a})\,U^{h}=\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})\,(L_{a}^{1}U^{m}+F),\\[4.0pt]
(\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{K\epsilon}{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta})(D_{x})\,(\lambda I-L_{a})\,U^{m}=(\tilde{\mathbb{I}}_{K\epsilon}{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta})(D_{x})\,(L_{a}^{1}U^{h}+F)\,,\end{array}\right.

where
L_{a}^{1}
is defined in (
3.24
).
As in the treatment of
U_{h}
and
U_{r}
in subsections
3.2.1
,
3.2.3
, we introduce the functionals:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(U^{h})=\int|\sqrt{w_{c}}U_{1}^{h}|^{2}+\|\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}U_{2}^{h}\|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{r}(U^{m})=\colon\frac{1}{2}\int\bigg(\gamma\,|U_{1}^{m}|^{2}+|\sqrt{G_{a}[0]}U_{2}^{m}|^{2}+\zeta_{c}\big|\nabla_{a}Q_{a}(D)U_{2}^{m}\big|^{2}\bigg)\,\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,,

and then find by performing energy estimates that:

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}(U^{h})}\lesssim\epsilon^{-1}\big(\|F\|_{X}+\|L_{a}^{1}U^{m}\|_{X}\big),

\displaystyle\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{m}(U^{m})}\lesssim K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\big(\|F\|_{X}+\|{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})L_{a}^{1}U^{h}\|_{X}\big).

Note that we have used the algebraic property of
{\rm Re}\,\sqrt{-\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a}}
in the region
R_{\xi}^{I}
stated in Lemma
3.5
. The estimate (
4.2
) can then be derived
from the fact
\|{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})L_{a}^{1}\|_{B(X)}+\|L_{a}^{1}{\mathbb{I}}_{\delta}(D_{x})\|_{B(X)}\lesssim\epsilon^{2}
which is shown in Lemma
C.10
.
∎

We are now in position to prove the resolvent bound (
4.1
):

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that
\epsilon
and
\eta_{0}
are small enough.
For any
\lambda\in\Omega_{\beta,\epsilon},\,
any
U,F\in Z=\colon\mathbb{Q}_{a}(\epsilon^{2}\eta_{0})\mathbb{I}_{A\epsilon^{2}}(D_{y})X,
such that
(\lambda I-L_{a})U=F,
it holds that

\|U\|_{X}\lesssim\epsilon^{-3}\|F\|_{X}.

(4.3)

Proof.

For the proof, we shall perform different estimates for low longitudinal frequencies (
{|\xi|\leq K\epsilon}
) and high longitudinal frequencies (
{|\xi|\geq K\epsilon}
). In the high-frequency regime, we apply Lemma
4.1
, while in the low-frequency regime, we use the KP-II approximation. Due to the excessively large resolvent bound in the low longitudinal frequency regime, which is of order
\epsilon^{-3}
, we need to use a smooth cutoff function to localize in frequency.

Let
\chi,\chi_{1},\chi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]

be two cut-off functions with the properties:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Supp}\,\chi\subset[-2K\epsilon,2K\epsilon]\,,\quad\chi_{1}\equiv 1\text{ on }[-K\epsilon,K\epsilon]\,,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Supp}\,\chi_{1}\subset[-4K\epsilon,4K\epsilon]\,,\quad\chi\equiv 1\text{ on }[-3K\epsilon,3K\epsilon]\,,

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Supp}\,\chi_{2}\subset[-8K\epsilon,8K\epsilon]\,,\quad\chi_{2}\equiv 1\text{ on }[-4K\epsilon,4K\epsilon]\,,

where
K\geq 1
is large and
\epsilon
is small, such that
K^{4}\epsilon\leq 1.

We define then

U^{H}=(1-{\chi})(D_{x})\,U,\,\qquad U^{L}=\chi_{1}(D_{x})\,U,

which solve the equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\big(\lambda I-L_{a}\big)\,U^{H}=(1-\chi)(D_{x})F-[{\chi}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,U,\\[5.0pt]
\big(\lambda-L_{a}\big)\,{\chi}_{2}(D_{x})\,U^{L}=[{\chi}_{1}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,U+{\chi}(D_{x})F\,.\end{array}\right.

We refer to (
3.24
) for the definition of
L_{a}^{1}.

Applying Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
C.8
, we first have that:

\displaystyle\|U^{H}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\big(\big\|[{\chi}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,U\big\|_{X}+\|F\|_{X}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\big((K\epsilon^{3}+K^{4}\epsilon^{5})\|U\|_{X}+\|F\|_{X}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim(K^{-1}+\epsilon)\,\|U\|_{X}+K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\|F\|_{X}\,.

Note that we have used the assumption
K^{4}\epsilon\leq 1
for the final inequality. It follows from the fact
(1-\chi)(1-\chi_{1})=(1-\chi_{1})
that
\|U\|_{X}\lesssim\|U^{L}\|_{X}+\|U^{H}\|_{X}.
Therefore, upon choosing
K
large enough and
\epsilon
sufficiently small,

\|U^{H}\|_{X}\lesssim(K^{-1}+\epsilon)\,\|U^{L}\|_{X}+K^{-2}\epsilon^{-3}\|F\|_{X}\,.

(4.7)

It now remains to bound
U^{L}.
Since it is localized in low frequencies in both variables, we can diagonalize smoothly the constant matrix

\displaystyle{L}_{a}^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{x}-a&G_{a}[0]\\[5.0pt]
-\gamma&\partial_{x}-a\end{array}\right)=P\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{+}^{0}&0\\[5.0pt]
0&\lambda_{-}^{0}\end{array}\right)P^{-1},\quad P^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]/\gamma}\\[5.0pt]
1&\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]/\gamma}\end{array}\right).

where

\lambda_{\pm}^{0}(\xi,\eta)=i(\xi-ia)\pm\sqrt{-\gamma\mu_{a}\tanh\mu_{a}(\xi,\eta)}\,.

Note that we have,

\|\mathbb{I}_{A\epsilon^{2}}(D_{y})f\|_{X}\approx\|\mathbb{I}_{A\epsilon^{2}}(D_{y})P^{-1}f\|_{\mathcal{X}}\,,\quad(\mathcal{X}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),

(4.14)

and it thus suffices to get an estimate for
\tilde{U}^{L}=\colon P^{-1}U^{L}
in the space
\mathcal{X}.

Let us set

R(x,D)=P^{-1}L_{a}^{1}\,P=\big(R_{ij}\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}\,,\qquad\tilde{F}=P^{-1}\big([{\chi}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,U+{\chi}(D_{x})F\big).

In particular,
R_{11}
takes the form

\displaystyle R_{11}(x,D)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg((\partial_{x}-a)(v_{c}\,\cdot)+\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]}\,v_{c}\,(\partial_{x}-a)\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]}^{-1}

(4.15)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\gamma\,}\big(G_{a}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{a}[0]\big)\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]}^{-1}-\sqrt{\gamma}^{-1}\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]}(d_{c}\,\partial_{x}Z_{c})\bigg).

Moreover, thanks to (
4.14
) and (
C.14
), we have that

\|\,R\,\|_{B(\mathcal{X})}\lesssim(K\epsilon^{3}+K^{4}\epsilon^{5})\lesssim K\epsilon^{3}.

(4.16)

We take
P^{-1}
on both sides of
\eqref{eqULUH}_{2}
and translate it into the following system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\big(\lambda-\lambda_{+}^{0}(D)+R_{11}(x,D)\big)\,{\chi}_{2}(D_{x})\,\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}=-R_{12}\,\tilde{U}_{2}^{L}+\tilde{F}_{1}\,,\\[5.0pt]
\big(\lambda-\lambda_{-}^{0}(D)\big)\,\,\tilde{U}_{2}^{L}=-\big(R_{21}\,\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}+R_{22}\,\tilde{U}_{2}^{L}\big)+\tilde{F}_{2}\,.\end{array}\right.

(4.17)

Thanks to (
3.30
) and (
4.16
), we get by energy estimates that

\|\tilde{U}_{2}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{-1}\big(\|\tilde{F}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+K\epsilon^{3}\|\tilde{U}^{L}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\big).

(4.18)

To estimate
U_{1}^{L},
we need to use the KP-II approximation.
As a preparation, we
introduce the scaling operator

S_{\epsilon}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
S_{\epsilon}f(x,y)=\epsilon^{-3/2}f(\epsilon^{-1}x,\epsilon^{-2}y).

Define then

{\mathcal{P}}=S_{\epsilon}^{-1}\,\mathbb{P}^{\hat{a}}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}(\hat{\eta}_{0})\,S_{\epsilon}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{Q}}=S_{\epsilon}^{-1}\,\mathbb{Q}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}^{\hat{a}}(\hat{\eta}_{0})\,S_{\epsilon}\,.

where
\mathbb{P}^{\hat{a}}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}
is the projection (see (
4.21
) for precise definition) to the resonant space associated to the operator
L_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\hat{a}}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}={\rm Id}-\mathbb{P}^{\hat{a}}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}.
We first claim that it suffices to prove the following two properties:

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{P}}\,\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim(\hat{\eta}_{0}^{2}+\epsilon+K^{3}\epsilon^{2}+K^{-3})\,\|\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{U}^{L}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+\|U^{H}\|_{X}\,,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{Q}}\,\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim K^{-3}\,\|\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}+K\|\tilde{U}^{L}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+\epsilon^{-3}\,\|\tilde{F}_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\,.

(4.20)

Indeed, once these two estimates are proven, we can conclude that for
K
large enough,
\epsilon,\hat{\eta}_{0}
small enough,

\|\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim K\|\tilde{U}^{L}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+\|U^{H}\|_{X}+\epsilon^{-3}\,\|\tilde{F}_{1}\|_{L^{2}}.

This, combined with the estimate (
4.18
), yields, for
K^{4}\epsilon\leq 1
and
\epsilon
small enough,

\displaystyle\|U^{L}\|_{X}\lesssim\|\tilde{U}^{L}\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U^{H}\|_{X}+\epsilon^{-3}\,\|\tilde{F}\|_{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U^{H}\|_{X}+\epsilon^{-3}\,\big(\|F\|_{X}+\|[{\chi}_{1}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,U\|_{X}\big).

As is shown in (
C.15
),

\|[{\chi}_{1}(D_{x}),\,L_{a}^{1}]\,\mathbb{I}_{2}(D_{y})\,U\|_{X}\lesssim\epsilon^{3}\,\big(\|U^{H}\|_{X}+K^{-1}\|U^{L}\|_{L^{2}}\big).

Therefore, choosing
K
large enough, we find that

\|U^{L}\|_{X}\lesssim\|U^{H}\|_{X}+\epsilon^{-3}\,\|F\|_{X},

which, together with (
4.7
), allows us to conclude (
4.3
) upon choosing
K
larger and
\epsilon
smaller if necessary. The proof is thus finished once (
4.19
), (
4.20
) are established. This is done in the following two subsections.
∎

4.1.
Proof of the estimate (
4.19
)

Recall that
Y_{a,\eta}=L_{a}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1/2}_{a,*,\eta}(\mathbb{R}),
where
H^{1/2}_{a,*,\eta}(\mathbb{R})
is defined in (
1.14
).
The projection operator of
L
in the weighted space
X_{a}
is defined as

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{0})f=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{-\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{0}}\big\langle\mathcal{F}_{y}f(\cdot,\eta),\,g_{k}^{*}(\cdot,\eta)\big\rangle_{{Y_{a,\eta}\times Y_{a,\eta}^{*}}}\,g_{k}(\cdot,\eta)\,e^{iy\eta}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,,

where
g_{k}(x,\eta),g_{k}^{*}(x,\eta)
are the basis and dual basis of
L_{a}(\eta)
defined in (
2.29
).
Let

Y_{\eta}=e^{ax}Y_{a,\eta},\qquad g_{k,a}(x,\eta)=e^{ax}g_{k}(x,\eta),\,\qquad g^{*}_{k,a}(x,\eta)=e^{-ax}g_{k}^{*}(x,\eta),

the corresponding projection of the transformed operator
L_{a}=e^{ax}\,L\,e^{-ax}
in the unweighted space
X
has the form

\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})f=e^{-ax}\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})e^{ax}f=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{-\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{0}}\big\langle\mathcal{F}_{y}f(\cdot,\eta),\,g_{k,a}^{*}(\cdot,\eta)\big\rangle_{Y_{\eta}\times Y_{\eta}^{*}}\,g_{k,a}(\cdot,\eta)\,e^{iy\eta}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,.

At this stage, it is useful to
introduce the projection operator on the continuous resonant modes of the linearized operator
L_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}
defined in (
2.6
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}^{\hat{a}}(\hat{\eta}_{0})f=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{-\hat{\eta}_{0}}^{\hat{\eta}_{0}}\big\langle\mathcal{F}_{y}f(\cdot,\hat{\eta}),\,g_{0k,\hat{a}}^{*}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\,g_{0k,\hat{a}}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\,e^{iy\hat{\eta}}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\eta}.

(4.21)

where
g_{0k,\hat{a}}(x,\hat{\eta})=e^{\hat{a}x}g_{0k}(x,\hat{\eta}),\,g^{*}_{0k,\hat{a}}(x,\hat{\eta})=e^{-\hat{a}x}g^{*}_{0k}(x,\hat{\eta})

and
g_{0k,\hat{a}}(x,\hat{\eta}),\,g^{*}_{0k,\hat{a}}(x,\hat{\eta})
are generalized eigenmodes for
L_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}(\hat{\eta})
defined in (
E.2
).

We are now ready to prove (
4.19
). Using the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})U=0,
we write

{\mathcal{P}}\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}={\mathcal{P}}U_{1}^{L}-\big[P^{-1}(D)\,\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})P(D)\tilde{U}^{L}\big]_{1}-\big[P^{-1}(D)\,\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})(1-\chi_{1})(D)U\big]_{1}

where we use
\big[\,\,\big]_{1}
to denote the first element of a vector. The last term in the above identity can be controlled directly, thanks to the equivalence of norms (
4.14
), by
\|(1-\chi_{1})(D)U\|_{X}\lesssim\|U^{H}\|_{X}.
It thus remains to control the first two terms.
By the definition and the change of variables, we have on the one hand

\displaystyle\qquad\big(S_{\epsilon}\,P^{-1}(D)\,\mathbb{P}_{a}(\eta_{0})P(D)\tilde{U}^{L}\big)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\epsilon^{-{3}/{2}}\int_{-\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{0}}e^{iy\,\eta/\epsilon^{2}}\bigg\langle({\mathcal{F}}_{y}\tilde{U}^{L})(\cdot,\eta),\,P^{*}(D,\eta)\,g_{k,a}^{*}(\cdot,\eta)\bigg\rangle P^{-1}(\epsilon D_{x},\eta)\,g_{k,a}(\epsilon^{-1}x,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{-\hat{\eta}_{0}}^{\hat{\eta}_{0}}e^{i{y}\,\hat{\eta}}\bigg\langle{\mathcal{F}}_{y}(S_{\epsilon}\tilde{U}^{L})(\cdot,\hat{\eta}),\,P^{*}(\epsilon D_{{x}},\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})\,g_{k,a}^{*}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})\bigg\rangle\,\epsilon^{-1}P^{-1}(\epsilon D_{{x}},\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})\,g_{k,a}(\epsilon^{-1}{x},\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\eta}\,,

and on the other hand,

\displaystyle\qquad\big(\,\mathbb{P}_{\textnormal{\scalebox{0.8}{KP}}}^{\hat{a}}(\eta_{0})\,S_{\epsilon}\tilde{U}_{1}^{L}\big)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{-\hat{\eta}_{0}}^{\hat{\eta}_{0}}e^{i{y}\,\hat{\eta}}\bigg\langle{\mathcal{F}}_{y}(S_{\epsilon}\tilde{U}_{1}^{L})(\cdot,\hat{\eta}),\,g_{0k,\hat{a}}^{*}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\bigg\rangle\,\,g_{0k,\hat{a}}({x},\hat{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\eta}\,.

Note that the inner product in the above identities is taken in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, since
g_{0k,\hat{a}}
and
g_{k,a}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot)
are smooth and exponentially decaying in
x,
for any
\hat{\eta}\in[-\hat{\eta}_{0},\hat{\eta}_{0}],

it holds that

\displaystyle\|\big(1-\chi_{2}(\epsilon D_{x})\big)(g_{0k,\hat{a}}(\cdot,\hat{\eta}))\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim K^{-3}\|g_{0k,\hat{a}}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\|_{H^{3}}\lesssim K^{-3},

\displaystyle\|\big(1-\chi_{2}(\epsilon D_{x})\big)(g_{k,a}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta}))\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim K^{-3}\|g_{k,a}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta}))\|_{H^{3}}\lesssim K^{-3}.

Consequently, since
S_{\epsilon}
is a diffeomorphism on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),
it suffices to show that for any
\hat{\eta}\in[-\hat{\eta}_{0},\hat{\eta}_{0}],

\displaystyle\bigg\|\chi_{2}(\epsilon D_{x})\bigg(\epsilon^{-1}\big[P^{-1}(D_{x},\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})g_{k,a}\big]_{1}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})-g_{0k,\hat{a}}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\bigg)\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim(\epsilon+\hat{\eta}_{0}^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\chi_{2}(\epsilon D_{x})\bigg(\big[P^{*}(D_{x},\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})g^{*}_{k,a}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\big]_{1}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})-g_{0k,\hat{a}}^{*}\bigg)\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim(\epsilon+\hat{\eta}_{0}^{2})\,.

In view of the definition of
P^{-1}
in (
4
) as well as the fact

\gamma=1+{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2}),\quad-\chi_{2}(D_{x})\mathbb{I}_{A\epsilon^{2}}(\eta)\,e^{iy\eta}\sqrt{-G_{a}[0]}\,e^{-iy\eta}=\epsilon(\partial_{x}-a)+{\mathcal{O}}_{B(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}(K^{3}\epsilon^{3})\,,

it amounts to verify that

\displaystyle\big\|(2\epsilon)^{-1}\big(g_{k,a}^{1}+(\partial_{x}-a)\,g_{k,a}^{2}\big)(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot,\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta})-g_{0k,\hat{a}}(\cdot,\hat{\eta})\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim(\epsilon^{2}+\hat{\eta}_{0}^{2})\,,

5.
Spectral stability in the unweighted space

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.6
, or precisely, that the spectra of the operators
L(\eta)
defined (
1.16
) in the unweighted space

Y_{0}(\eta)=\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H_{0,*,\eta}^{1/2}

are contained in the imaginary axis. Thanks to
[
48
]
, we just need to study the case
\eta\neq 0
.
Note that for
\eta\neq 0
, we have
Y_{0}(\eta)=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1\over 2}(\mathbb{R}).

We first show that the essential spectrum of
L(\eta)
(seen as an unbounded operator on
Y_{0}(\eta)
) is on the imaginary axis.
We write
L(\eta)=M(\eta)+R(\eta),
where

M(\eta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{d_{c}}\,\partial_{x}\,\sqrt{d_{c}}&G_{\eta}[0]\\[5.0pt]
-\gamma&\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}^{-1}\sqrt{d_{c}}\partial_{x}\sqrt{d_{c}}\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}\end{array}\right),\,\,\qquad R(\eta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{11}&R_{12}\\[5.0pt]
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{array}\right)

where

R_{11}=\sqrt{d_{c}}[\sqrt{d_{c}},\partial_{x}],\quad R_{12}=G_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{\eta}[0],\qquad R_{21}=d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c}\,,

R_{22}=\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}^{-1}\bigg[\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]},d_{c}\bigg]\partial_{x}+\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}^{-1}\sqrt{d_{c}}\bigg[\sqrt{d_{c}},\partial_{x}\bigg]\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}\,.

We shall check that
\lambda-M(\eta)
is invertible and then check that
R(\eta)
is a relatively compact perturbation.

Since
\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}:H^{1\over 2}_{0,*,\eta}\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism, the invertibility of the operator
(\lambda-M(\eta))
on
Y_{0}(\eta)
is equivalent to the invertibility
of the operator
(\lambda-M_{1}(\eta))
on
L^{2}\times L^{2}
where

M_{1}(\eta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{d_{c}}\,\partial_{x}\,\sqrt{d_{c}}&(\gamma G_{\eta}[0])^{1\over 2}\\[5.0pt]
-(\gamma G_{\eta}[0])^{1\over 2}&\sqrt{d_{c}}\partial_{x}\sqrt{d_{c}}\end{array}\right).

Next, we also observe that

M_{1}(\eta)=iP^{-1}D(\eta)P,\quad P=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&i\\
1&-i\end{array}\right),\quad D(\eta)=\mbox{diag }(S_{-}(\eta),S_{+}(\eta))

where

S_{\pm}(\eta)=\sqrt{d_{c}}D_{x}\,\sqrt{d_{c}}\pm(\gamma G_{\eta}[0])^{1\over 2},\quad D_{x}={1\over i}\partial_{x}.

Consequently, it suffices to prove that
\lambda-iS_{\pm}(\eta)
are invertible on
L^{2}
for
\lambda\notin i\mathbb{R}
.
Since
S_{\pm}
are clearly self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}
with domain
H^{1}
, their spectrum is real
and thus the spectrum of
iS_{\pm}(\eta)
is included in the imaginary axis.
This show that
\lambda-M(\eta)
is invertible on
Y_{0}(\eta)
for
\lambda\notin i\mathbb{R}
.

Next, we shall prove that

R(\eta)
is a relatively compact perturbation of
\lambda-M(\eta)
on
Y_{0}(\eta)
that is to say that

(\lambda-M(\eta))^{-1}R(\eta)
is a compact operator on
Y_{0}(\eta)
for
\lambda\notin i\mathbb{R}

to get that the essential spectrum of
L(\eta)
is on the imaginary axis.
First, since the domain of
\lambda-M(\eta)
is
\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}Y_{a}(\eta),
we know that
(\lambda-M(\eta))^{-1}
maps
Y_{0}(\eta)
to
\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}Y_{0}(\eta).
Therefore, we just need to show that

\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}R_{11},\quad\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}R_{21},\quad\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}R_{12}\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}^{-1},\quad\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}R_{22}\sqrt{G_{\eta}[0]}^{-1}

are compact operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).
Indeed, we observe that each of the above operators contains the background waves
v_{c},\partial_{x}Z_{c},\zeta_{c}
which have exponential decay and thus belong to the Schwarz class. By using the expansion (
C.9
) for
a=0
as well as the fact that the multiplication by a Schwarz function is compact from
H^{t}(\mathbb{R})
to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
for
t>s
we get the desired property.

It remains to study the presence of eigenvalues for
\lambda\notin i\mathbb{R}
.
By the reversibility of the water waves system,
we already know that the spectrum of
L(\eta)
is symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis, so it suffices to show that there are no eigenvalues
of positive real part.
We proceed by contradiction. Let us assume that for some
\eta_{u}\neq 0
, there exists a nontrivial
U_{u}\in H^{1}\times H^{3\over 2}
and
\sigma_{u}
,
{\rm Re}\,\,\sigma_{u}>0

\sigma_{u}U_{u}=L(\eta_{u})U_{u}.

Since
\sigma_{u}
has to be an isolated eigenvalue, we can define the spectral projection associated to
\sigma_{u}
by

\pi(\eta_{u})={1\over 2i\pi}\int_{\gamma}(\lambda-L(\eta_{u}))^{-1}d\lambda\,,

where
\gamma
is a small circle enclosing
\sigma_{u}
and no other eigenvalue.
Since the dependence of
L(\eta)
in
\eta
is analytic, we can use the analytic perturbation theory in
[
30
]
.
We can assume that
\eta_{u}
is not an exceptional point (otherwise we change it for a nearby non exceptional point), we get that for
\eta\in(\eta_{u}-\delta_{0},\eta_{u}+\delta_{0})

for some small
\delta_{0}>0
, there exists an analytic curve
\sigma(\eta)
of positive real part eigenvalues of
L(\eta)
which are of constant algebraic multiplicity
m
,
such that
\sigma(\eta_{u})=\sigma_{0}
.
Moreover, the eigenprojector

is also analytic. The (possibly trivial) eigennilpotent which is
N(\eta)=(\lambda-L(\eta))\pi(\eta)
is thus also analytic.

As in

[
48
]

of

associated to a positive real part eigenvalue

Y_{a}(\eta).

Let us rewrite
L(\eta)U=\lambda U

(\lambda-L^{0}(\eta))U=L^{1}(\eta)U\,,

where

{L}^{0}(\eta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{x}&G_{\eta}[0]\\[5.0pt]
-\gamma&\partial_{x}\end{array}\right),\,\,\qquad L^{1}(\eta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\partial_{x}(v_{c}\cdot)&G_{\eta}[\zeta_{c}]-G_{\eta}[0]\\[5.0pt]
d_{c}\partial_{x}Z_{c}&-v_{c}\partial_{x}\end{array}\right).

The eigenvalues of the symbol of

\lambda_{\pm,0}^{0}(\xi,\eta)=i\bigg(\xi\mp\sqrt{\gamma\mu_{0}\tanh\mu_{0}}(\xi,\eta)\bigg),\quad\big(\mu_{0}=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}}\big),

for any
\lambda

{\rm Re}\,\lambda>0,

\lambda-L^{0}(\eta)

and the symbol of its inverse

\displaystyle\big(\lambda-L^{0}(\xi,\eta)\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{+,0})(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{-,0})}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{+,0})+(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{-,0})}{2}&\frac{(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{-,0})-(\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{+,0})}{2}\sqrt{-\mu_{0}\tanh\mu_{0}/\gamma}\\[5.0pt]

Moreover, from similar computations as in the proof of (

\lambda-L^{0}(\eta)
is invertible also in the weighted space

and it maps

\langle D_{x}\rangle^{-1}Y_{a}(\eta).

By using the exponential localization of

Next by iterating the argument, we get that for
{\rm Re}\,\,\lambda>0
, any element of the generalized kernel has to be in the weighted
space.

By using this observation we thus get that for

\eta\in(\eta_{0}-\delta,\eta_{0}+\delta)
the image of

\pi(\eta)
is included in the weighted space. We also deduce that all the operators seen as valued into the weighted space
are continuous.

We then define for every
\delta>0
sufficiently small the wave packet

V_{\delta}(t,x,y)=\int_{I_{\delta}}e^{iy\eta\,}e^{L(\eta)t}\,\pi(\eta)U_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,,\quad I_{\delta}=(\eta_{u}-\delta^{m+1},\eta_{u}+\delta^{m+1}).

which solves the evolution problem
\partial_{t}V=LV.

We recall that we have defined
m
as the constant algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalues
\sigma(\eta)
.
Moreover, we notice that from the above argument
V_{\delta}(t)
belongs to the weighted space
and actually to the image of
\mathbb{Q}(\epsilon^{2}\hat{\eta}_{0})X_{a}
.
Indeed, for each
\eta\in I_{\delta}
, even if
\eta\in(-\eta_{0},\eta_{0})
, we have that
\mathcal{F}_{y}V_{\delta}(t,\cdot,\eta)

is orthogonal to the subspace generated by
(g_{k}^{*}(\cdot,\eta)_{k=1,\,2},
which is the subspace generated the eigenvectors
U^{*}(\pm\eta)
of
L(\eta)^{*}
associated to different eigenvalues.

We then observe that we can equivalently write

V_{\delta}(t,x,y)=\int_{I_{\delta}}e^{iy\eta\,}e^{\sigma(\eta)t}e^{N(\eta)t}\,\pi(\eta)U_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,

and that from the Bessel identity, we have

\|V_{\delta}(0)\|_{X_{a}}^{2}=\|e^{ax}V(t)\|_{X}^{2}=C\int_{I_{\delta}}\|\pi(\eta)U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta>0

for
\delta
sufficiently small since
\pi(\eta_{u})U_{u}=U_{u}
.
Moreover, we also obtain that

\|V_{\delta}(\delta^{-1})\|_{X_{a}}^{2}=C\int_{I_{\delta}}e^{2{\rm Re}\,\sigma(\eta)\delta^{-1}}\|e^{\delta^{-1}N(\eta)}\pi(\eta)U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Note that, for
\delta
sufficiently small, we have that

{\rm Re}\,\sigma(\eta)\geq{\rm Re}\,\sigma_{u}/2
and that for
\eta\in I_{\delta}

\|N(\eta)\pi(\eta)U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}\lesssim|\eta-\eta_{u}|\|U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}\lesssim\delta^{m+1}\|U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}

and hence since
N(\eta)^{m}=0
, that

\|e^{\delta^{-1}N(\eta)}U_{u}-U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}\lesssim\delta^{m+1}{1\over\delta^{m}}\lesssim\delta\|U_{u}\|_{Y_{a}(\eta)}.

This yields that for every
\delta
sufficiently small, we have

\|V_{\delta}(\delta^{-1})\|_{X_{a}}^{2}\geq Ce^{{\rm Re}\,\sigma_{u}\over\delta}\|V_{\delta}(0)\|_{X_{a}}^{2}

for some
C>0
independent of
\delta
.
This is in contradiction with the semigroup estimate (
1.19
) for
\delta
sufficiently small
(note that
\epsilon
is fixed for this argument).
This ends the proof.

Given a symbol

to denote the operator associated to

which is defined by, for any

\mathrm{Op}(b)f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int b(x,\xi)\,e^{i\xi(x-x^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\xi\,.

.

(A.2)

Proof.

[

]

\displaystyle A^{-1}=\mathrm{Op}\big(\frac{2}{1+e^{-2\tau\mu_{a}}}\big)+{\mathcal{R}}\,,\qquad\Gamma_{-}(0,-1)\Gamma_{+}(0,-1)=\mathrm{Op}(e^{-2\tau\mu_{a}})+{\mathcal{R}}

and thus

\Gamma_{-}(0,-1)\sigma_{+}(-1)A^{-1}\Gamma_{+}(0,-1)=\mathrm{Op}\big(\frac{2\mu_{a}}{e^{2\mu_{a}}+1}\big)+{\mathcal{R}}\,,

where we denote by
{\mathcal{R}}
a generic bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with operator norm of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{2}),
uniformly in
\eta
such that
|\eta|\geq 2.
Note that we have used that the commutators of the above involved operators contain always some derivatives of
\zeta_{c}
and that
\|(\epsilon^{-2}\zeta_{c},\epsilon^{-3}\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}))\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\lesssim 1.

Consequently, by the identity
\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}-\mu_{a}=\frac{(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c})^{2}\eta^{2}}{\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}+\mu_{a}}

and the fact
{\rm Re}\,\mu_{a}\geq\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}-a^{2}},
we conclude that

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\Phi_{a,\eta}^{app}(f)|_{z=0}

\displaystyle=\tau(x)\bigg(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}\partial_{x}+\mathrm{Op}\big(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}\big(1-\frac{2}{e^{2\mu_{a}}+1}\big)\big)\bigg)f+{\mathcal{R}}f

\displaystyle=\tau(x)\bigg(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}\partial_{x}+\mathrm{Op}\big(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}\tanh\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}\big)\bigg)f+{\mathcal{R}}f,

or equivalently,

\mathrm{Op}\big(\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}\tanh\lambda_{\zeta_{c}}^{1}\big)f=\bigg(-\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}\partial_{x}+\frac{1+(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c})^{2}}{1+\zeta_{c}}\partial_{z}\bigg)\Phi_{a,\eta}^{app}(f)\big|_{z=0}+{\mathcal{R}}f.

Recall that
G_{a,\eta}[\zeta_{c}]
is defined as

\displaystyle G_{a,\eta}[\zeta_{c}]f=\bigg(-\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}\partial_{x}+\frac{1+(\partial_{x}\zeta_{c})^{2}}{1+\zeta_{c}}\partial_{z}\bigg)\Psi_{a,\eta}(f)|_{z=0}\,,

where

solves the problem (
C.1
). Therefore,
to show (
C.4
), it suffices to prove that for any
|\eta|\geq 2,
there is a constant

such that

which is based on for

which are similar to the one performed in the previous lemma and the estimates (
C.5
), (
C.8
).
∎

Proof.

Since

where

\partial_{x}\varphi_{c}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\varphi_{c})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(G[\zeta]\varphi_{c}+\partial_{x}\varphi\cdot\partial_{x}\zeta_{c})^{2}}{\sqrt{1+|\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}|^{2}}}-\gamma\zeta_{c}=0\,.\end{array}\right.

First, by the translational invariance, one gets by differentiating the above profile equations that

\tilde{L}(0)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta_{c}^{\prime}\\
\varphi_{c}^{\prime}\end{array}\right)=0\,.

To find the other element in the generalized kernel, it is convenient to use the change of variable

X=hx,\quad Z=hz,\quad\tilde{\zeta}(X)=h\,\zeta(x),\quad\tilde{\varphi}(X)=ch\,\varphi(x)

and study the obtained system where the wave speed
c
appears explicitly:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}c\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}+G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\tilde{\varphi}_{c}=0\,,\\
c\partial_{x}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\tilde{\varphi}_{c}+\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}\cdot\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c})^{2}}{\sqrt{1+(\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c})^{2}}}-g\tilde{\zeta}_{c}=0\,.\end{array}\right.

Since
\tilde{\zeta}_{c},\,\tilde{\varphi}_{c}
depends smoothly on the wave speed
c,
we can
differentiate the above equation in terms of

to find that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}c\partial_{X}\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}+G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\partial_{c}\tilde{\varphi}-G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\big(\tilde{Z}_{c}\,\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\big)-\partial_{X}\big(\tilde{v}_{c}\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\big)=-\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\,,\\[3.0pt]
c\partial_{X}\partial_{c}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}-\tilde{v}_{c}\partial_{X}\partial_{c}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}+\tilde{Z}_{c}\,G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\big(\partial_{c}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}-\tilde{Z}_{c}\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\big)+\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\partial_{X}\tilde{v}_{c}\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}-g\partial_{c}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}=\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c},\end{array}\right.

(D.2)

where

\tilde{Z}_{c}=\frac{G_{0}[\tilde{\zeta}_{c}]\tilde{\varphi}_{c}+\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}\cdot\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}}{{1+(\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c})^{2}}},\qquad\tilde{v}_{c}=\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}-\tilde{Z}_{c}\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}\,.

Since
\partial_{X}\tilde{\varphi}_{c}(X)=c\,\partial_{x}\varphi_{c}(x),\,\partial_{X}\tilde{\zeta}_{c}(X)=\partial_{x}\zeta_{c}(x),
it holds that

\tilde{Z}_{c}(X)=cZ_{c}(x),\,\tilde{v}_{c}(X)=cv_{c}(x).

Therefore, changing back to the variable
x,
we get from (
D.2
) that

\tilde{L}(0)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c\partial_{c}\zeta_{c}\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{c}(c{\zeta}_{c})\end{array}\right)=-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\zeta}_{c}^{\prime}\\[3.0pt]
{\varphi}_{c}^{\prime}\end{array}\right).

It then stems from the relation (
D.1
) the basis of the generalized kernel of
L(0):

V_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta_{c}^{\prime}\\[3.0pt]
\varphi^{\prime}_{c}-Z_{c}{\zeta}_{c}^{\prime}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta_{c}^{\prime}\\[3.0pt]
v_{c}\end{array}\right),\qquad V_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c\partial_{c}\zeta_{c}\\[3.0pt]
-cZ_{c}\partial_{c}{\zeta}_{c}+\partial_{c}(c\varphi_{c})\end{array}\right),

which satisfy:

Let us remark that the fact that the kernel of

is of algebraic multiplicity

is related to the same property of the linearized operator of KdV equation around KdV soliton, see Appendix C in
[

]
.
At this stage, it is also useful to notice that, by the above identities,
v_{c}
and

solve respectively the equation:

Consequently, it follows from the change of variable (

(D.3)
