Near-Optimal Dynamic Rounding of Fractional Matchings in Bipartite Graphs
Sayan Bhattacharya, Peter Kiss, Aaron Sidford, David Wajc

Abstract
We study dynamic ( 1 − ϵ ) (1-\epsilon) -approximate rounding of fractional matchings—a key ingredient in numerous breakthroughs in the dynamic graph algorithms literature. Our first contribution is a surprisingly simple deterministic rounding algorithm in bipartite graphs with amortized update time O ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ​ log 2 ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ⋅ n ) ) O(\epsilon^{-1}\log^{2}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot n)) , matching an (unconditional) recourse lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) \Omega(\epsilon^{-1}) up to logarithmic factors. Moreover, this algorithm’s update time improves provided the minimum (non-zero) weight in the fractional matching is lower bounded throughout. Combining this algorithm with novel dynamic partial rounding algorithms to increase this minimum weight, we obtain a number of algorithms that improve this dependence on n n . For example, we give a high-probability randomized algorithm with O ~ ​ ( ϵ − 1 ⋅ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) 2 ) \tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\cdot(\log\log n)^{2}) -update time against adaptive adversaries. 1 1 1 Throughout, we use “soft-O” notation, O ~ \tilde{O} , to suppress logarithmic factors in ε \varepsilon , i.e., O ~ ​ ( f ) = O ⁡ ( f ⋅ poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) ) \tilde{O}(f)=O(f\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))) . Further, we provide extensions of our results to general graphs and to maintaining almost-maximal matchings.

Introduction

Dynamic matching is one of the most central and well-studied dynamic algorithm problems.
Here, a graph undergoes edge insertions and deletions, and we wish to quickly update a large matching (vertex-disjoint set of edges) following each such change to the graph.

A cornerstone of numerous dynamic matching results
is the dynamic
relax-and-round
approach: the combination of dynamic
fractional
matching algorithms
[
BHI15
,
BHN16
,
BHN17
,
BK19
,
BCH20
]
with dynamic
rounding
algorithms
[
ACC+18
,
Waj20
,
BK21
,
Kis22
,
BKSW23
]
.
This dynamic fractional matching problem asks to maintain a vector
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0}
such that
x(v):=\sum_{e\ni v}x_{e}
satisfies the fractional degree constraint
x(v)\leq 1
for all vertices
v\in V
and
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert:=\sum_{e}x_{e}
is large compared to
the size of the largest (fractional) matching in the dynamic graph
G=(V,E)
. The goal typically is to solve this problem while
minimizing the amortized or worst-case time per edge update in
G
.
2
2

2

An algorithm has
amortized
update time
f(n)
if every sequence of
t
updates takes at most
t\cdot f(n)
time and has
worst-case
update time
f(n)
if each operation takes at most
f(n)
time.
As we focus on amortized update times, we omit this distinction.

For the rounding problem (the focus of this work), an abstract interface can be defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

A
dynamic rounding algorithm (for fractional matchings)
is a data structure supporting the following operations:

•

\mathtt{init}(G=(V,E),\,{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0},\,\epsilon\in(0,1))
: initializes the data structure for undirected graph
G
with vertices
V
and edges
E
, current fractional matching
{\bf x}
in
G
, and target error
\epsilon
.

•

\mathtt{update}(e\in E,\,\nu\in[0,1])
: sets
x_{e}\leftarrow\nu
under the promise that the resulting
{\bf x}
is a fractional matching in
G
.
3
3

3

Invoking
\mathtt{update}(e,0)
essentially deletes
e
and subsequently invoking
\mathtt{update}(e,\nu)
for
\nu>0
essentially adds
e
back. So,
G
might as well be the complete graph on
V
. However, we find the notation
G=(V,E)
convenient.

The algorithm must maintain a matching
M
in the support of
{\bf x}
,
\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}}):=\{e\in E\mid x_{e}>0\}
, such that
M
is a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation with respect to
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert:=\sum_{e}x_{e}
, i.e.,

M\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})\text{ , }M\text{ is a matching }\text{ , and }|M|\geq(1-\epsilon)\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert\,.

The combination of fast fractional algorithms with fast dynamic rounding algorithms plays a key role in state-of-the-art time/approximation trade-offs for the dynamic matching problem against an adaptive adversary
[
Waj20
,
BK21
,
Kis22
]
, including the recent breakthroughs of
[
BKSW23
,
Beh23
]
.
Here, a randomized algorithm
works against an adaptive adversary
(or
is adaptive
, for short) if its guarantees hold even when future updates depend on the algorithm’s previous output and its internal state. Slightly weaker are
output-adaptive
algorithms, that allow updates to depend only on the algorithms’ output.
Note that deterministic algorithms are automatically adaptive.
A major motivation to study output-adaptive dynamic algorithms is their black-box use as subroutines within other algorithms. (See discussions in, e.g.,
[
NS17
,
BKM+22
,
CK19
]
.)

Despite significant effort and success in designing and applying dynamic rounding algorithms, the update time of current
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate dynamic rounding approaches are slower by large
\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1},\log n)
factors than an unconditional recourse (changes per update) lower bound of
\Omega(\epsilon^{-1})
(
2.3
).
4
4

4

Proving update time lower bounds for approximate dynamic matching is a notoriously challenging open problem. On the other hand,
[
SS21
]
show that recourse can be made
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for any approximate dynamic matching algorithm.

Consequently, rounding is a computational bottleneck for the running time of many
state-of-the-art dynamic matching algorithms

[
Waj20
,
BK21
,
Kis22
,
BKSW23
,
Beh23
,
ABR24
]
and decremental (only allowing deletions) matching algorithms
[

,
JJST22
]
.

The question thus arises,

can one design (output-adaptive) optimal dynamic rounding algorithms for fractional matching?
We answer this question in the affirmative in a strong sense.

Our Contributions

Our main results are deterministic and randomized dynamic fractional matching rounding algorithms for bipartite graphs that match the aforementioned simple recourse lower bound of
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1})

The dynamic bipartite matching rounding problem admits:

\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})

1.2
Our Approach in
a Nutshell

Here we outline our approach, focusing on the key ideas behind
1.2
. To better contrast our techniques with those of prior work, we start by briefly overviewing the latter.

Previous approaches.
Prior dynamic rounding algorithms
[
ACC+18
,
Waj20
,
BK21
,
Kis22
]
all broadly work by partially rounding the fractional matching
{\bf x}
to obtain a matching sparsifier
S
(a sparse subgraph approximately preserving the fractional matching size compared to
{\bf x}
).
Then, they periodically compute a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate matching in this sparsifier
S
using a static
\tilde{O}(|S|\cdot\varepsilon^{-1})
-time algorithm (e.g.,
[
DP14
]
) whenever
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
changes by
\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
, i.e., every
\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert)
updates. This period length guarantees that the matching computed remains a good approximation of the current fractional matching during the period, with as good an approximation ratio as the sparsifier
S
.
Now, for sparsifier
S
to be
O(1)
-approximate, it must have size
|S|=\Omega(\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert)
, and so this approach results in an update time of at least
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
. Known dynamic partial rounding approaches all result in even larger sparsifiers, resulting in large
\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1},\log n)
update times.

Direct to integrality.

Our first rounding algorithm for bipartite graphs breaks from this framework and directly rounds to integrality.
This avoids overhead of periodic recomputation of static near-maximum matching algorithms, necessary to avoid super-linear-in-
\varepsilon^{-1}
update time (or
n^{o(1)}
factors, if we substitute the static approximate algorithms with the breakthrough near-linear-time max-flow algorithm of
[
CKL+22
]
).
The key idea is that, by encoding each edge’s weight in binary, we can round the fractional matching “bit-by-bit”, deciding for each edge whether to round a component of value
2^{-i}
to a component of value
2^{-i+1}
.
This can be done statically in near-linear-time by variants of standard degree splitting algorithms, decreasing the degree of each node in a multigraph by a factor of two (see
Section

2
).
Letting
L:=\log((\min_{e:x_{e}\neq 0}x_{e})^{-1})
, we show that by buffering updates of total value at most
O(\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert/L)
for each power of
2
, we can efficiently dynamize this approach, obtaining a dynamic rounding algorithm with update time
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot L^{2})
.
As we can assume that
\min_{e:x_{e}\neq 0}x_{e}\geq\varepsilon/n^{2}
(
2.2
), this gives our bipartite
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\log^{2}n)
time algorithm.

Faster partial rounding.

The second ingredient needed for
1.2
are a number of algorithms for “partially rounding” fractional matchings, increasing
\min_{e:x_{e}\neq 0}x_{e}
while approximately preserving the value of the fractional matching. (The output is not quite a fractional matching, but in a sense is close to one.
See
4.1
.)
Our partial rounding algorithms draw on a number of techniques, including fast algorithms for partitioning a fractional matching’s support into
multiple
sparsifiers, as opposed to a single such sparsifier in prior work, and a new output-adaptive sampling data structure of possible independent interest (
Appendix

B
).
7
7

7

From this we derive the first output-adaptive matching algorithm that is not also adaptive.
,
8
8

8

Concurrently to our work, another such sampling algorithm was devised
[
YWW23
]
. See discussion in
Appendix

B
.

Combining these partial rounding algorithms with our simple algorithm underlies all our bipartite rounding results of
1.2
, as well as our general graph rounding results of
1.4
(with further work – see
Section

6
).

Preliminaries

Assumptions and Model.
Throughout, we assume that
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert\geq 1
, as otherwise it is trivial to round
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
within a factor of
1-\varepsilon
, by maintaining a pointer to any edge in
\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})
whenever the latter is not empty.
In this paper we work in the word RAM model of computation with words of size
w:=\Theta(\log n)
, allowing us to index any of
2^{O(w)}=\mathrm{poly}(n)
memory addresses, perform arithmetic on
w
-bit words, and draw
w
-bit random variables, all in constant time.
We will perform all above operations on
O(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot n))
-bit words, which is still
O(w)
provided
\varepsilon^{-1}=\mathrm{poly}(n)
. If
\varepsilon
is much smaller, all stated running times trivially increase by a factor of
O(\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

Notation.

For multisets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, we
denote by
S_{1}\uplus S_{2}
the “union” multiset, in which each element has multiplicity that is the sum of its multiplicities in
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.
A vector
{\bf x}
is
\lambda
-uniform
if
x_{e}=\lambda
for all
e\in\mathrm{supp}({\bf{{\bf x}}})
, and is
uniform
if it is
\lambda
-uniform for some
\lambda
. Given fractional matching
{\bf x}
, we call an integral matching
M\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{{\bf x}}})
that is
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate, i.e.,
|M|\geq\lVert{\bf{{\bf x}}}\rVert\cdot(1-\varepsilon)
an
\varepsilon
-approximate rounding
of
{\bf x}
.
Finally, we use the following notion of distance and its monotonicity.

Observation 2.1
.

For vectors
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, define
d^{\epsilon}_{V}({\bf x},{\bf y}):=\sum_{v\in V}(|x(v)-y(v)|-\epsilon)^{+}
, for
(z)^{+}:=\max(0,z)
the positive part of
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, we have

d^{\varepsilon}_{V}({\bf x},{\bf y})\leq d^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{V}({\bf x},{\bf y})
for all
\epsilon\geq\epsilon^{\prime}
. Moreover, by the triangle inequality and the basic fact that
(a+b)^{+}\leq a^{+}+b^{+}
for all real
a,b
, we have
d^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}_{V}({\bf x},{\bf z})\leq d^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{V}({\bf x},{\bf y})+d^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{V}({\bf y},{\bf z})
for all
\varepsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\geq 0
and vectors
{\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
.

Support and Binary encoding.

We denote the binary encoding of each edge
e
’s fractional value by
x_{e}:=\sum_{i}(x_{e})_{i}\cdot 2^{-i}
.
We further let
\mathrm{supp}_{i}({\bf x}):=\{e\in E\mid(x_{e})_{i}=1\}
denote the set of coordinates of
{\bf x}
whose
i
-th bit is a
1
. So,
\mathrm{supp}({\bf x})=\bigcup_{i}\mathrm{supp}_{i}({\bf x})
.
Next, we let
{\bf x}_{\min}:=\min_{e\in\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})}x_{e}
.
The following observation allows us to restrict our attention to a small number of bits when rounding bipartite fractional matchings
{\bf x}
. (In
Section

6
we extend this observation to the structured fractional matchings in general graphs that interest us there.)

Observation 2.2
.

For rounding bipartite fractional matching, by decreasing
\varepsilon
by a constant factor, it is without loss of generality that
{\bf x}_{\min}\geq\varepsilon/n^{2}
and moreover if
\Delta\leq{\bf x}_{\min}
and
L:=1+\lceil\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{-1})\rceil
, we may safely assume that
(x_{e})_{i}=0
for all
i>L
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/3
. Consider the vector
{\bf x}^{\prime}
obtained by zeroing out all entries
e
of
{\bf x}
with
x_{e}<\varepsilon^{\prime}/n^{2}
and setting
(x_{e})_{i}=0
for all edges
e
and indices
i>L
.
Clearly,
\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x^{\prime}}})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})
and
{\bf x}^{\prime}
is a fractional matching, as
{\bf x}^{\prime}\leq{\bf x}
.
The following shows that
\lVert{\bf{x^{\prime}}}\rVert
is not much smaller than
\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert\geq 1
.

\lVert{\bf{x^{\prime}}}\rVert\geq\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert-{n\choose 2}\varepsilon^{\prime}/n^{2}-\sum_{e}\sum_{i>L}2^{-i}\geq\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert-\varepsilon^{\prime}-\sum_{e}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot{\bf x}_{\min}\geq\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert\cdot(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})-\sum_{e}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot x_{e}=(1-2\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert.

Therefore, a matching
M\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x^{\prime}}})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})
that is
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})
-approximate w.r.t.
x^{\prime}
is
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate w.r.t.
{\bf x}
, as
|M|\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\lVert{\bf{x^{\prime}}}\rVert\geq(1-3\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert=(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
.
∎

Recourse Lower Bound.

We note that the number of changes to
M

per update (a.k.a. the rounding algorithm’s
recourse
) is at least
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1})
in the worst case.

Fact 2.3
.

Any
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximate dynamic matching rounding algorithm
\mathcal{A}
must use
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1})
amortized recourse, even in bipartite graphs.

Proof.

Consider a path graph
G
of odd length
4\varepsilon^{-1}+2
with values
1/2
assigned to each edge.
A matching
M\subseteq\mathrm{supp}({\bf{x}})
of size
|M|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
must match all odd-indexed edges of the path.
However, after invoking
\mathtt{update}(\cdot,0)
for the first and last edges in the path, for
|M|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert{\bf{x}}\rVert
(for the new
{\bf x}
),
M
must match all
even
-indexed edges. Therefore, repeatedly invoking
\mathtt{update}(\cdot,0)
and then
\mathtt{update}(\cdot,1/2)
for these two edges sufficiently many times implies that the matching
M
maintained by
\mathcal{A}

{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{x_{<\phi}}}\rVert+\sum_{i\in[L]}|\lVert{\mathbf{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}}\rVert-\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}}\rVert|+|\lVert{\mathbf{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}}\rVert-\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}_{i}}}\rVert|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma+\sum_{i\in[L]}\lVert{\mathbf{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}}\rVert\cdot(\varepsilon+\gamma)+\gamma+\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}_{i}}}\rVert\cdot\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert\cdot 4(\varepsilon+\gamma)+\gamma\cdot 2L

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert\cdot 8(\varepsilon+\gamma)+\gamma\cdot 4\log(n/\gamma)\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}.

Property
(C2)
Vertex Slack
:
d_{V}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat{x}})\leq 2\cdot\gamma+2\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert
as the two matching differ only on the edges of
\mathbf{x_{<\phi}}
or by just a factor of
\varepsilon
.

\displaystyle d_{V}^{\varepsilon+\gamma}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq d_{V}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat{x}})+d_{V}^{\varepsilon+\gamma}(\mathbf{\hat{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\gamma+2\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert+\sum_{i\in[L]}d_{V}^{\varepsilon+\gamma}(\mathbf{\hat{x}_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\gamma+2\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert+\sum_{i\in[L]}2\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{\hat{x}_{i}}}}\rVert\cdot(\gamma+\varepsilon)+\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}\rVert\cdot 4\cdot(\varepsilon+\gamma)+\gamma\cdot\frac{5\cdot\log(n/\gamma)}{\varepsilon}.

Property
(C3)
Edge values
: The property follows as the output is the sum of edge-wise disjoint
(\varepsilon+\gamma,\delta)
-coarsenings.

5.1
Deterministic Static Splitting

In this section we prove

See

Assume

is
\lambda

The algorithm:

Our algorithm inductively constructs sets of vectors

\mathcal{F}_{0}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\}

Running time:

By

O(|\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})|\cdot L)=O(|\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})|\cdot\log(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)

Correctness:

It remains to show that

is a
(4\epsilon,\epsilon)
-split of
{\mathbf{x}}

First, as noted above, each edge in
\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}})

(C0)
of coarsenings, and together they satisfy the covering property of splits. We now show that every vector

{\mathbf{x}}

5.2
Randomized Static Splitting

We now turn to proving
4.8
, restated below for ease of reference.

See
4.8

Proof.

Assume
{\mathbf{x}}
is
\lambda
-uniform. First note that if
\lambda>\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24\cdot\log^{2}n}
then we may return
\{{\mathbf{x}}\}
as the split trivially. Note that if
\lambda\leq\varepsilon/n^{2}
then
\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert\leq\varepsilon
. In this case, it is sufficient to return a split consisting of
|\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})|
coarsenings, where each coarsening is a single edge of
\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})
and has weight
\varepsilon^{4}/\log^{2}n
. The same split can also be returned if
\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert\leq\varepsilon^{2}/\log n

\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24\cdot\log^{2}n}\geq\lambda\geq\varepsilon/n^{2}
and
\lVert{\mathbf{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}}\rVert\geq\varepsilon^{2}/\log n
. We start by defining the following two parameters.

\delta:=\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24\log^{2}n}\text{ and }k:=2^{\lceil\log_{2}\frac{\delta}{\lambda}\rceil}.

The algorithm:
We (implicitly) initialize
k
zero vectors
\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}
.
Next, for each edge
e\in\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})
, we roll a
k
-sided die
i\sim\textrm{Uni}([k])
, and set
x^{(i)}_{e}\leftarrow\delta^{\prime}:=\lambda\cdot k
.

Running time:
The algorithm spends
O(1)
time per edge in
\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}})
, and so it trivially takes
O(|\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}})|)
time.

Correctness:
By construction,

\{\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{x}}^{(i))}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a partition of
\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})
, and so each of these
{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}
satisfy Property
(C0)
of coarsenings.
Moreover, since
\frac{\delta}{\lambda}\leq k\leq 2\cdot\frac{\delta}{\lambda}
, we have that
\delta^{\prime}=k\cdot\lambda\in[\delta,2\delta]
. Hence, each of the vectors
\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(i)}
satisfy Property
(C3)
of an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-coarsening.
It remains to prove the two other properties of a coarsening.

For each edge
e\in\mathrm{supp}({\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}})
and
i\in[k]
, we have
x^{(i)}_{e}\sim\delta^{\prime}\cdot\textrm{Ber}(\frac{1}{k})
,
and so

\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(i)}_{e}\right]=\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{k}=\lambda
.
Thus, by linearity of expectation, we get:
\mathbb{E}[\lVert{{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}}\rVert]=\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert]
.
Consequently, since
\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\log n}
, by standard Chernoff bound and our choices of
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{24\log^{2}n}
and
\delta^{\prime}\leq 2\delta
,
we have that Property
(C1)

holds w.h.p.

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\;\lVert{{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}}\rVert-\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert\;|\geq\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}}}\rVert}{3\delta^{\prime}}\right)\leq 2n^{-4}.

Similarly,
\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(i)}(v)\right]=x(v)
for each vertex
v
, and so again a Chernoff bound implies that:

\displaystyle\Pr[|x^{(i)}(v)-x(v)|\geq\varepsilon]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot 1}{3\delta^{\prime}}\right)\leq 2n^{-4}.

Therefore, by taking a union bound over the
(n+2)
bad events, for each of the
k\leq\frac{2\delta}{\lambda}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon^{4}}{24\log^{2}n}\cdot\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon}\leq 2n^{2}
vectors (i.e.,
O(n^{3})
bad events), we have that
d^{\varepsilon}_{V}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)})=0
.
Therefore Property
(C2)
also holds w.h.p., and so by union bound
\{{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\}
satisfy all the properties of an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-split w.h.p.
∎

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}_{\leq\varepsilon^{3}}}}\rVert-\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}_{\leq\varepsilon^{3}}^{\prime}}}\rVert|\leq 100\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\lVert{\mathbf{{\mathbf{x}}_{\leq\varepsilon^{3}}}}\rVert\right]

(C2)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{1/\varepsilon}\Pr\left[|x_{\leq\varepsilon^{3}}(v)-x_{\leq\varepsilon^{3}}^{\prime}(v)|\geq\varepsilon k\right]\cdot\varepsilon k

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{1/\varepsilon}\frac{10\varepsilon^{2}}{k^{2}}
