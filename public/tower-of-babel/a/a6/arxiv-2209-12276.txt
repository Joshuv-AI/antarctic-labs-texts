Stable determination of a second order perturbation of the polyharmonic operator by boundary measurements
Nesrine Aroua, Mourad Bellassoued

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the inverse boundary value problem for the polyharmonic operator. We prove that the second order perturbations are uniquely determined by the corresponding Dirichlet to Neumann map. More precisely, we show in dimension n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , a logarithmic type stability estimate for the inverse problem under consideration.

35R30, 31B20, 31B30, 35J40.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Introduction

The focus of this paper is the determination of a second order perturbation of the polyharmonic operator from the Dirichlet to Neumann map. We start with some notations. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be a bounded simply connected domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{\infty}
boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
and let
m\geq 2
. Given a symmetric matrix
A=(A_{jk})
, a vector field
B=(B_{j})
and a potential
q
, we consider the perturbed polyharmonic operator formally given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{m}+A(x)D\cdot D+B(x)\cdot D+q(x)

(1.2)

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{m}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}A_{jk}(x)D_{j}D_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{j}(x)D_{j}+q(x),

where
D=i^{-1}\nabla
,
A\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
,
B\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
are the perturbation coefficients. The operator
\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)
equipped with domain

(1.3)

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q})=\{u\in H^{2m}(\Omega)\,:\,\gamma u:=\bigr(u,(-\Delta)u,\ldots,(-\Delta)^{m-1}u\bigr)|_{\Gamma}=0\},

is an unbounded closed operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
with purely discrete spectrum, see
[
14
]
.

For
m\geq 2
and
r>0
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}^{m,r}(\Gamma)
the product space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m,r}(\Gamma)=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}H^{2m-2j-r}(\Gamma),

equipped with norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m,r}(\Gamma)}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\|f_{j}\|_{{H}^{2m-2j-r}(\Gamma)},\quad f=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m-1})\in\mathcal{H}^{m,r}(\Gamma).

Assuming
0
is not an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D):\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
and consider the boundary value problem with Navier boundary conditions

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)u=0&\mathrm{in}\,\Omega,\cr\gamma u=f&\mathrm{on}\,\Gamma,\end{array}\right.

where
f=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m-1})\in\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
. Then (
1.4
) has a unique solution
u:=u_{f}\in H^{2m}(\Omega)
(see Lemma
1.1
below) and the boundary measurements are given by the Dirichlet to Neumann map (D-to-N), defined formally by

(1.5)

\Lambda_{A,B,q}:f\mapsto\widetilde{\gamma}u:=(\partial_{\nu}u,\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)u,\ldots,\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)^{m-1}u)|_{\Gamma},

where
u\in H^{2m}(\Omega)
satisfies the boundary value problem (
1.4
). Here
\nu
denotes the unit outer normal vector to the boundary
\Gamma
at
x
.

Before stating our main result, we recall the following Lemma on the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the problem (
1.4
), the proof is given by Lemma 5.13 in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
A\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
,
B\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
. Suppose that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)
and
f\in\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists a unique solution
u\in H^{2m}(\Omega)
to (
1.4
) satisfying

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{2m}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)}.

Furthermore, we have
\widetilde{\gamma}u:=(\partial_{\nu}u,\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)u,\ldots,\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)^{m-1}u)|_{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)
and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\gamma}u\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)}.

As a corollary of above Lemma, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{A,B,q}
given by (
1.5
)
is bounded from
\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
to

\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)
. We denote by
\|\Lambda_{A,B,q}\|
its norm.

In this paper, we are interested in recovering the coefficients
A
,
B
and
q
from the boundary measurements unclosed in the Dirichlet to Neumann map

(1.6)

\Lambda_{A,B,q}:\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma).

In practice, the polyharmonic operators
(-\Delta)^{m}
,
m\geq 2
, arise in many areas of physics and geometry, including the study of vibration of beams, the Kirchhoff-Love plate equation in the theory elasticity (for
m=2
), the continuum mechanics of buckling problems and the study of the Paneitz-Branson operator in conformal geometry, for more details we refer to
[
12
]
and
[
21
]
.

The uniqueness question of determination the lower order perturbations of polyharmonic operator
(-\Delta)^{m}
,
m\geq 2
, was begun by Krupchyk, Lassas and Uhlmann who showed in
[
19
]
the uniqueness of the zeroth and the first order perturbations from the knowledge of D-to-N map. Later, they proved in
[
18
]
that the unique recovery of a first order perturbation of the biharmonic operator, i.e.,
m=2
, is possible when the D-to-N map given only on a part of the boundary
\Gamma
, where
\Omega
is a bounded domain. In case where
\Omega
is an unbounded domain, we refer the work of Yang
[
28
]
. In
[
13
]
, Ghosh and Krishnan considered the higher order perturbation of polyharmonic operators with partial data on the boundary, where the coefficients attached to perturbed terms are isotropic. Later, Bhattacharyya and Ghosh established in
[
4
]
the uniqueness of a second order perturbations of a polyharmonic operator
(-\Delta)^{m}
,
m\geq 2
, from the D-to-N data. More precisely, they showed that, for
m>2
, the unique determination of a
2-
tensor field
A
, a vector field
B
and a potential
q
provided
A\in W^{3,\infty}(\Omega)
,
B\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
and
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with some restrictions on the support of the coefficients and for
m=2
, they assumed that
A
is an isotropic matrix. In the case of an anisotropic matrix, for
m=2
, they showed in
[
5
]
that the unique determination of second order perturbations of a biharmonic operator is possible from the boundary D-to-N map. Note that in the case of a lower order perturbation up to order
3
of the biharmonic operator, there is an obstruction to uniqueness, as noted in
[
5
]
. Recently, Bhattacharyya, Krishnan and Sahoo established in
[
6
]
the unique recovery of a lower order anisotropic tensor perturbations up to order
m
of a polyharmonic operator from measurements taken on a part of the boundary. All the above mentioned works are stated in the case where all the coefficients of the perturbed polyharmonic operator are sufficiently smooth. In case of lower regularity of the coefficients, we refer to
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
20
]
and to recent work
[
7
]
.

For the stability question, Choudhury and Krishnan proved in
[
10
]
a logarithmic type stability estimate for the zeroth order perturbation of the biharmonic operator,
(-\Delta)^{2}
, for the case when the Neumann data is measured on the whole boundary and a log-log type stability estimate for the case when the Neumann data is measured only on slightly more than half of the boundary. Later, Choudhury and Heck established in
[
9
]
a logarithmic type stability estimate for the zeroth order perturbation of the biharmonic operator with partial data when the inaccessible part of the domain is flat. A natural question to ask is whether higher order perturbations of the polyharmonic operator can be stably recovered from the knowledge of D-to-N map. In this work, we show that if we consider a second order perturbation of the polyharmonic operator
(-\Delta)^{m}
,
m\geq 2
, of the form (
1.1
) then the coefficients
(A,B,q)
depend stably on the bounded map
\Lambda_{A,B,q}
.

1.2.
Main stability results

We here state the main results of this paper concerning conditional logarithmic stability reconstruction of the second order perturbation coefficients
(A,B,q)
from knowledge of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{A,B,q}
given by
(
1.6
)
.

Let us first indicate the required conditions for admissible coefficients
(A,B,q)
. In what follows, we assume that the second order perturbation
A
takes the form

(1.7)

\displaystyle A(x)=a(x)\operatorname{id},\quad\textrm{if}~~m=2.

Let
M>0
and
\sigma_{1}>\frac{n}{2}+3
be given. We define the class of admissible symmetric tensor
(A_{jk})
,
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma_{1}}(M)
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma_{1}}(M)

\displaystyle=\{A\in W^{\operatorname{min}(2m,6),\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}});~~\|A\|_{H^{\sigma_{1}}(\Omega)}\leq M,~\textrm{and satisfying \eqref{cond_A}}\},

Given
M>0
and
\sigma_{2}>\frac{n}{2}+1
, we define the class of admissible vector field
B
and electric potential
q
respectively by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}(M)

\displaystyle=\{B\in W^{4,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n}),~~\|B\|_{H^{\sigma_{2}}(\Omega)}\leq M\},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(M)

\displaystyle=\{q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),~~\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M\}.

Let us define, where
\mu>0
, the function
\Phi_{\mu}
as follows

\Phi_{\mu}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&t=0,\\
|\ln t|^{-\mu}+t^{\frac{1}{2}},&t>0,\end{array}\right.

and we denote by
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)
the space of all compactly supported distributions in
\Omega
.

Our main results state that it is possible to stably determine the symmetric tensor
A
, the vector field
B
and the potential
q
, given the knowledge of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. Precisely, we will prove the following three theorems in sections
4
and
5
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M>0
and
\sigma_{j}>0
,
j=1,2,
as above. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
and
\mu_{1},\kappa_{1}\in(0,1)
, such that for any

,
B^{(j)}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}(M)\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and
q^{(j)}\in\mathcal{Q}(M)
,

, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\|A^{(1)}-A^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\Phi_{\mu_{1}}(\|\Lambda_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}-\Lambda_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}\|)^{\kappa_{1}}.

Here
C
depends only on
\Omega
,
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
,
n
,
m
and
M
, and
\mu_{1}
depends only on
n
and
m
.

.

Let

and
\sigma_{j}>0
,
j=1,2,
as above. Then, there exists a constant

and
\mu_{2},\kappa_{2}\in(0,1)
, such that for any
A^{(j)}\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma_{1}}(M)\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)
,

and

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|B^{(1)}-B^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\Phi_{\mu_{2}}(\|\Lambda_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}-\Lambda_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}\|)^{\kappa_{2}}.

Here

,

, and

,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\|q^{(1)}-q^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\Phi_{\mu_{3}}(\|\Lambda_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}-\Lambda_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}\|).

Here

Let us explain the main difficulties and ideas in the proof of
Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
. We start by
realling the qualitative argument due to Bhattacharyya and Ghosh

[
4
]
. Their starting point
is the following orthogonal identity (see Lemma
3.1
below)

(1.11)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)D\cdot Du+B(x)\cdot Du+q(x)u)\overline{u^{*}}dx=0,

which holds for
u
and
u^{*}
solving
\mathcal{L}_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}u=0
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}u^{*}=0
respectively, wherever
\Lambda_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}=\Lambda_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}
. They, then proceed by constructing of special solutions, called complex geometric optics
solutions (C.G.O- solutions), that are to be used with the integral identity (
1.11
). We mention that the method of C.G.O- solutions that is used
for proving uniqueness question for higher order elliptic operators
goes back to Sylvester and Uhlmann
[
24
]
. The complex geometric optics solutions to the polyharmonic equation take the special form

(1.12)

\displaystyle u(x,h)=e^{\frac{x\cdot\varrho}{h}}(\alpha_{1}(x)+h\alpha_{2}(x)+r(x,h)),

where
\varrho\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
h
is a small parameter,
\alpha_{j}

j=1,2,
are complex amplitudes satisfying some transport equations and
r(\cdot,h)
is remainder term that vanishes when
h
goes to zero.

Now, any symmetric tensor
A\in H^{k}(\Omega)
can be uniquely represented as the sum

(1.13)

\displaystyle A=\widetilde{A}+\nabla_{\text{sym}}V,

where the covector field
V\in H^{k+1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying the boundary condition

(1.14)

\displaystyle V=0,\quad\textrm{on}~~\Gamma,

and the tensor field
\widetilde{A}\in H^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
satisfies the conditions

(1.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\widetilde{A}_{jk}=0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

With the C.G.O-solutions given by (
1.12
) and the integral identity (
1.11
), they deduce at first, in the case when
m>2
, that
A:=A^{(1)}-A^{(2)}
is an isotropic matrix, which they fall into the regime of case when
m=2
. Second, by changing the choice of the amplitudes
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{1}^{*}
, they prove that
B^{(1)}=B^{(2)}
. Hence, the conclusion that
A^{(1)}=A^{(2)}
and
q^{(1)}=q^{(2)}
follows easily.

However, in order to obtain a stability estimate for this inverse boundary value problem, the integral identity (
1.11
) transformed to the following integral inequality

\displaystyle|\int_{\Omega}(AD\cdot Du+B\cdot Du+qu)\overline{u^{*}}dx|\leq\|\Lambda_{A^{(1)},B^{(1)},q^{(1)}}-\Lambda_{A^{(2)},B^{(2)},q^{(2)}}\|\|u\|_{H^{2m}(\Omega)}\|u^{*}\|_{H^{2m}(\Omega)}.

The estimate for the second order perturbation is slightly more complicated. In contrast to the uniqueness result of
[
4
]
, the decomposition (
1.13
) may not be helpful to establish a stability estimate for the 2-tensor field
A
. To remedy this difficulty, we decompose
A
, in the different way, as

(1.16)

\displaystyle A=A^{\prime}+\nabla_{\text{sym}}V+\lambda~id,

where the function
\lambda\in H^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
, the covector field
V\in H^{k+1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying the boundary condition (
1.14
) and the tensor field
A^{\prime}\in H^{k}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
satisfies the conditions (
1.15
) and

(1.17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}(A^{\prime})=0.

In fact, using the condition (
1.17
) we are able to estimate
A^{\prime}:=A^{\prime(1)}-A^{\prime(2)}
. This technique will be discussed later. After this is established, the stability estimates for the vector fields
V
and
B
follow by using the Hodge decomposition. Then by changing the choices of amplitudes , we may estimate the function
\lambda
and therefore, we conclude the stability estimate for
A
. Finally we will combine the estimates we get for
A
and
B
to obtain the stability estimate for the potential
q
. For the case where
m=2
, the stability result would follow similarly except in this case
A=\lambda~id
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we will build complex geometric optics solutions to the perturbed polyharmonic operator. In section
3
, we derive an integral identity involving the perturbations and we give a specific Hodge decomposition of a symmetric tensor
A
and a vector field
B
. Sections
4
and
5
, are concerned to prove the stability estimates respectively in case
m>2
and case
m=2
. In appendix
A
, we develop the proof of Hodge decomposition of a symmetric tensor.

2.
Carleman estimate and C.G.O- solutions

The main strategy of the proof of stability estimates on determining the symmetric tensor
A
, the vector field
B
and the electric potential
q
from the D-to-N map is the use of complex geometrical solutions to estimate the Fourier transform of the difference of two symmetric tensors
A^{(1)}-A^{(2)}
, the difference of two vector fields
B^{(1)}-B^{(2)}
and the difference of two potentials
q^{(1)}-q^{(2)}
. The constructed of C.G.O- solutions is based on the use of Carleman estimates. We therefore first outline some known results about Carleman estimate.

2.1.
Local Carleman estimate

In this section we will first recall the Carleman estimates for semiclassical Lapace operator
(-h^{2}\Delta)
and then we use this Carleman estimate to solve an equation involving a conjugated operator of
\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)
.

We start by collecting several known Lemmas and notations. Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, consider the conjugated operator with respect the weight function
\psi

(2.1)

P_{\psi}(x,hD)=e^{\frac{\psi}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)e^{-\frac{\psi}{h}},\quad h>0.

Following
[
17
]
, we say that
\psi
is a limiting weight function for Laplace operator, if
\nabla\psi\neq 0
in
\Omega
, and if it satisfies the following Poisson bracket condition

\displaystyle\left\{\overline{p}_{\psi},p_{\psi}\right\}(x,\xi)=0\quad\textrm{where}~~p_{\psi}(x,\xi)=0,\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
p_{\psi}
is the semiclassical principal symbol of the opearator
P_{\psi}(x,hD)
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, the limiting weight function
\psi
can be chosen as

(2.2)

\psi(x)=x\cdot\omega,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In what follows we shall equip the standard Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, with the semicalssical norm

(2.3)

\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\langle hD\rangle^{s}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

here
\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, we consider the convexified Carleman weight given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}=\psi+\frac{h}{2\varepsilon}\psi^{2}.

Therefore, for
0<h<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}=O(1).

We begin with the local Carleman estimate for semiclassical Laplace operator
(-h^{2}\Delta)
which is due to
[
22
]
.

In what follows,
{\,\lesssim\,}
holds for
\leq
modulo a multiplicative positive constant independent of
h
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\psi_{\varepsilon}
be the convexified Carleman weight function given by (
2.4
). Then for
0<h\ll\varepsilon
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(2.6)

\frac{h}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\|v\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}\|P_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(x,hD)v\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for any
v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Iterating the Carleman estimate (
2.6
)
m
-times,
m\geq 2
, we get the following local Carleman estimate for the polyharmonic operator
(-h^{2}\Delta)^{m}
, that is

(2.7)

(\frac{h}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})^{m}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}\|e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)^{m}e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for any
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
and
h,\varepsilon>0
small enough.

Armed with inequality (
2.7
) we shall derive a local Carleman estimate for the perturbed polyharmonic operator
\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D)
. Consider the conjugate operator corresponding to the perturbed polyharmonic operator

(2.8)

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\mathcal{L}_{\psi}(x,hD)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\psi}{h}}(h^{2m}\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}(x,D))e^{-\frac{\psi}{h}}

(2.9)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\psi}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)^{m}e^{-\frac{\psi}{h}}+h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi}{h}}+h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\psi}{h}}B\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi}{h}}+h^{2m}q.

In order to estimate the lower order terms in (
2.8
), we use the following result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
t_{1}
,
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
V\in H^{t_{2}}(\Omega)
with
t_{2}>\frac{n}{2}+|t_{1}|
. Then the estimate

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|Vu\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{t_{1}}(\Omega)}{\,\lesssim\,}\|V\|_{H^{t_{2}}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{t_{1}}(\Omega)},

holds for all
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

At first, we extend
V
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we consider
\tilde{V}\in H^{t_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\tilde{V}=V
, in
\Omega
, and

\|\tilde{V}\|_{H^{t_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}\,\|V\|_{H^{t_{2}}(\Omega)}.

Then, we get for
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{V}u)(\xi)=(\widehat{\tilde{V}}\ast\widehat{u})(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\widehat{\tilde{V}}(\xi-\eta)\widehat{u}(\eta)\,d\eta,

which leads directly to

(2.11)

\langle h\xi\rangle^{t_{1}}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{V}u)(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle h\xi\rangle^{t_{1}}\langle h\eta\rangle^{-t_{1}}\,\widehat{\tilde{V}}(\xi-\eta)\langle h\eta\rangle^{t_{1}}\widehat{u}(\eta)\,d\eta.

Now, we apply the Peetre’s inequality (see
[
8
]
page 90)

(2.12)

\langle h\xi\rangle^{t_{1}}\langle h\eta\rangle^{-t_{1}}\leq 2^{|t_{1}|/2}\,\langle\xi-\eta\rangle^{|t_{1}|/2},\quad\forall\,\xi,\,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Further, we define two functions
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
by

w_{1}(\xi)=\langle h\xi\rangle^{t_{1}}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|,\;\;w_{2}(\xi)=\langle\xi\rangle^{|t_{1}|}\,|\widehat{\tilde{V}}(\xi)|,

so that we get using (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) that

\displaystyle|\langle h\xi\rangle^{t_{1}}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{V}u)(\xi)|{\,\lesssim\,}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w_{2}(\xi-\eta)\,w_{1}(\eta)\,d\eta=(w_{1}\ast w_{2})(\xi).

Consequently

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\tilde{V}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{t_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\displaystyle\|w_{1}\ast w_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\,\|w_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|w_{2}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}\,\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{t_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|V\|_{H^{t_{2}}(\Omega)}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{d\xi}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{t_{2}-|t_{1}|}}\Big)^{1/2}.

Since
t_{2}>\frac{n}{2}+|t_{1}|
then the last integral converge. This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma_{1}}(M)
,
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}(M)
and
q\in\mathcal{Q}(M)
and let
\psi
be the limiting Carleman weight function given by (
2.2
) and
-2m\leq s\leq 0
. Then the local Carleman estimate

(2.13)

h^{m}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}\|\mathcal{L}_{\psi}(x,hD)u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\quad m\geq 2,

holds for all
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and all
h>0
small enough.

Proof.

To get the Carleman estimate for
\mathcal{L}_{\psi}(x,hD)
, we add the lower order perturbation to (
2.7
). First we add the zero order perturbation
q
then for
-2m\leq s\leq 0
, we get

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|h^{2m}qu\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2m}\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Next, we consider the first order perturbation
B
, we compute

\displaystyle h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(B\cdot hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u=h^{2m-1}[i(B\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon})u+B\cdot(hD)u].

Moreover, we have
B\cdot(hD)u=hD\cdot(Bu)-h(D\cdot B)u
. Then, by using the fact that
\psi_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
2.5
), we obtain, for
0<h<\varepsilon<1
, that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(B\cdot hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-1}\bigr(\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|Bu\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigr).

Now we estimate the last term in (
2.15
), to do this we distinguish two cases: if
-2m\leq s\leq-1
, we have immediately

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|Bu\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|B\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

and if
-1\leq s\leq 0
, we get from Lemma
2.2
, for
t_{2}:=\sigma_{2}>\frac{n}{2}+1
and
t_{1}:=s+1
,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\|Bu\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|B\|_{H^{\sigma_{2}}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Now plugging (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) into (
2.15
), we conclude for
-2m\leq s\leq 0
,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(B\cdot hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-1}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We end up with the term involving the second order perturbation
A
, by a simple computation we have

\displaystyle h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u

\displaystyle=h^{2m-2}AD\psi_{\varepsilon}\cdot D\psi_{\varepsilon}u-2h^{2m-2}AD\psi_{\varepsilon}\cdot(hD)u

\displaystyle\quad-h^{2m-1}AD\cdot D\psi_{\varepsilon}u+h^{2m-2}A(hD)\cdot(hD)u.

Since

AD\psi_{\varepsilon}\cdot(hD)u=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}hD_{k}(A_{jk}(D_{j}\psi_{\varepsilon})u)-\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}hD_{k}(A_{jk}D_{j}\psi_{\varepsilon})u,

and

\displaystyle A(hD)\cdot(hD)u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}h^{2}D_{j}D_{k}(A_{jk}u)-h^{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(D_{j}D_{k}A_{jk})u-2h\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(D_{j}A_{jk})(hD_{k}u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}h^{2}D_{j}D_{k}(A_{jk}u)+h^{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(D_{j}D_{k}A_{jk})u-2h\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}hD_{k}\bigr(D_{j}(A_{jk})u\bigr).

Then we obtain, for
0<h<\varepsilon<1

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-2}\bigr(\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\bigr(\|A_{jk}(D_{j}\psi_{\varepsilon})u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}}+\|A_{jk}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}}+h\|D_{j}(A_{jk})u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}}\bigr)\bigr).

Again to estimate the last three terms in the above inequality, we distinguish two cases: if
-2\leq s\leq 0
, we obtain from Lemma
2.2

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-2}\Big(\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\bigr(\|A_{jk}D_{j}\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{\sigma_{1}}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|A_{jk}\|_{H^{\sigma_{1}}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|D_{j}A_{jk}\|_{H^{\sigma_{1}-1}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigr)\Big)

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-2}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

with
\sigma_{1}>\frac{n}{2}+3
. Now if
-2m\leq s<-2
, we get immediately

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-2}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Then, we conclude for
-2m\leq s\leq 0

(2.19)

\displaystyle\|h^{2m-2}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}A(hD)\cdot(hD)e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}h^{2m-2}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Combining (
2.14
), (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) we get from (

\displaystyle(\frac{h}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})^{m}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}{\,\lesssim\,}\|h^{2m}e^{\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}\mathcal{L}_{A,B,q}e^{-\frac{\psi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+h^{2m-2}\|u\|_{H_{\mathrm{scl}}^{s+2m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Choosing now
h<\varepsilon<1
small enough, we get our desired estimate.
∎

Solvability of the transport equation

We will now establish the existence of complex amplitude
\alpha_{2}

16

We associate to the operator

\displaystyle p(\xi)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Moreover, we set

Let us first recall the following result due to
[
16
]
, where it is obtained as a consequence of the general theory of
[
15
]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
P(D)\neq 0
, be a differential operator with constant coefficients. Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a linear operator
E\in\mathcal{B}(H^{k}(\Omega))
such that
P(D)(E(f))=f
, for all
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
. Moreover, there exists
C>0
such that

(2.56)

\displaystyle\|E(f)\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\leq C\bigr(\underset{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{sup}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{p}(\xi)}\bigr)\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)},\quad\forall f\in H^{k}(\Omega).

Here
C
depends only on the order of
P(D)
,
n
and
\Omega
.

For
\varrho=\omega+i\widetilde{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}+i\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
as above, we consider the differential operator with constant coefficients

(2.57)

\displaystyle T_{\varrho}(D)=(\varrho\cdot\nabla)^{m},\quad\textrm{for}~~m\geq 2.

As a consequence of the above Lemma we have the following result.

Lemma 2.9
.

The operator
T_{\varrho}(D)
admits a bounded inverse
E_{\varrho}
from
H^{k}(\Omega)
to
H^{k}(\Omega)
, such that, if

and

(2.58)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}.

Here
C>0
is a positive constant depends only on
n
,
m
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.8
, there exists a linear operator
E_{\varrho}\in\mathcal{B}(H^{k}(\Omega))
such that

T_{\varrho}\circ E_{\varrho}f=f,\quad\textrm{for any }f\in H^{k}(\Omega).

Moreover, since the symbol of the differential operator
T_{\varrho}(D)
is given by

p_{\varrho}(\xi)=i^{m}(\varrho\cdot\xi)^{m},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

So we have, by (
2.55
) and Hölder’s inequality

(\widetilde{p}_{\varrho}(\xi))^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|m!\varrho_{j}^{m}|^{2}\geq\frac{(m!)^{2}}{n^{m-1}}\bigr(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\varrho_{j}|^{2}\bigr)^{m}\geq\frac{2^{m}(m!)^{2}}{n^{m-1}}.

Then, from (
2.56

This completes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

3.
Integral estimates and Hodge decomposition

In this section, we will use the properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map to prove an integral estimate, which relates the difference of coefficients to the difference of the corresponding Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. This integral estimate will be our starting point in proving the stability estimates for the inverse problem under consideration. In the second part of this section we give a specific Hodge decomposition of a symmetric tensor
A
and a vector field
B
.

As in the first section we consider a priori constant
M>0
,
\sigma_{1}>\frac{n}{2}+3
and
\sigma_{2}>\frac{n}{2}+1
. Also, we consider for
j=1,2
,
A^{(j)}\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma_{1}}(M)\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)
,
B^{(j)}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}(M)\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and
q^{(j)}\in\mathcal{Q}(M)
pair of admissible coefficients. We denote

(3.1)

A=A^{(2)}-A^{(1)},\qquad B=B^{(2)}-B^{(1)}\quad\text{and }~~~~q=q^{(2)}-q^{(1)}.

We extend
A
,
B
and
q
by zero outside

\Omega
. In the sequel of the next, we denote by
A
,
B
and
q
these extensions and by
\mathcal{L}_{j}(x,D)
, for
j=1,2,
the operator corresponding to the perturbation
(A^{(j)},B^{(j)},q^{(j)})
, that is

(3.2)

\mathcal{L}_{j}(x,D)=\mathcal{L}_{A^{(j)},B^{(j)},q^{(j)}}(x,D),\quad j=1,2.

For notational convenience, the Dirichlet to Neumann map is denoted by

(3.3)

\Lambda^{(j)}=\Lambda_{A^{(j)},B^{(j)},q^{(j)}},\quad j=1,2.

The key idea in the stability result is to use complex geometric optics solutions
u
to
\mathcal{L}_{2}(x,D)u=0
in
\Omega
and

to
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}(x,D)u^{*}=0
in
\Omega
and plug them in an integral identity.

For
A^{(1)}
,
B^{(1)}
and
q^{(1)}
as above. In what follows,
we shall need the generalization of Green’s formula given in
[
1
]
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{1}(x,D)w,v\right)-\left(w,\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}(x,D)v\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\int_{\Gamma}\left[\bigr((-\Delta)^{m-\ell}w\bigr)\bigr(\overline{\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)^{\ell-1}v}\bigr)-\bigr(\partial_{\nu}(-\Delta)^{m-\ell}w\bigr)\bigr(\overline{(-\Delta)^{\ell-1}v}\bigr)\right]ds_{x}

\displaystyle:=\left<\gamma^{t}w,\tilde{\gamma}v\right>-\bigr<\tilde{\gamma}w,\gamma^{t}v\bigr>,

where
\gamma^{t}w=((-\Delta)^{m-1}w,\ldots,(-\Delta)w,w)

\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
denote the

inner product respectively on
\Omega

and
\Gamma
, and

stands for the Euclidean surface measure on
\Gamma
.

We start by the following integral identity.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A
,
B
and
q
given by (
3.1
). For given
f=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m-1}),f^{*}=(f_{0}^{*},\ldots,f_{m-1}^{*})\in\mathcal{H}^{m,\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
, let
u,\,u^{*}\in H^{2m}(\Omega)
solutions respectively, to

(3.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}_{2}(x,D)u=0&\textrm{in}\,\Omega,\cr\gamma u=f&\textrm{on}\,\Gamma,\end{array}\right.;\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{1}(x,D)u^{*}=0&\textrm{in}\,\Omega,\cr\gamma u^{*}=f^{*}&\textrm{on}\,\Gamma.\end{array}\right.

Then the following identity holds true

(3.8)

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)D\cdot Du+B(x)\cdot Du+q(x)u)\overline{u^{*}}dx=\bigr<(\Lambda^{(2)}-\Lambda^{(1)})f,(f^{*})^{t}\bigr>,

where
(f^{*})^{t}=(f_{m-1}^{*},\ldots,f_{0}^{*})

Proof.

Let
u
and
u^{*}
as in the lemma. Let
u_{1}\in H^{2m}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of

(3.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}_{1}(x,D)u_{1}=0&\textrm{in}\,\Omega,\\
\gamma u_{1}=f&\textrm{on}\,\Gamma.\end{array}\right.

Putting

, then

(3.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{L}_{1}(x,D)w=A(x)D\cdot Du+B(x)\cdot Du+q(x)u&\textrm{in}\,\Omega,\cr\gamma w=0&\textrm{on}\,\Gamma.\end{array}\right.

Next, we multiply the first equation in (
3.10

and we then apply the Green’s formula (

) to get

\int_{\Omega}(A(x)D\cdot Du+B(x)\cdot Du+q(x)u)\overline{u^{*}}dx=\int_{\Gamma}(\Lambda^{(2)}-\Lambda^{(1)})f\cdot\overline{(f^{*})^{t}}ds_{x}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2

Let

B
and

\displaystyle{\,\lesssim\,}(e^{C/h}\|\Lambda^{(1)}-\Lambda^{(2)}\|+h+\|A\varrho\cdot\varrho\|_{L^{\infty}})N_{2m,\varrho}(\alpha_{1})N_{2m,\overline{\varrho}}(\alpha_{1}^{*}).

Lemma 4.1
