1.
Introduction

This work introduces a background stochastic Vlasov equation behind stochastic transport and advection equations which gives additional information on the fluctuations and oscillations of solutions. It is based on Young measures; although the use of Young measures in PDEs is classical, the introduction of these particular Vlasov equations seems to be new.
We develop first the theory for the stochastic transport of a passive scalar, where the result is new but just enriches incrementally the existing understanding. More importantly, then we develop the theory for a passive vector field, where also stretching acts besides transport. Here the results based on the background Vlasov equation add completely new statistical information to the stochastic advection equation.

1.1.
Stochastic passive scalar

Consider the stochastic transport equation on the
d
-dimensional torus

\mathbb{T}^{d}

\displaystyle d\theta^{n}\left(t,x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,j}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\cdot\nabla\theta^{n}\left(t,x\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}

\displaystyle\theta^{n}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\overline{\theta}_{0}.

Under suitable assumptions described below we know, since
[
17
]
, that

\theta^{n}
weakly converges to
\overline{\theta}
, solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\theta}\left(t,x\right)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{T}\Delta\overline{\theta}\left(t,x\right)

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\overline{\theta}_{0}

for a suitable constant
\kappa_{T}>0
. It is clear that strong convergence in
L^{2}
cannot hold, since the
L^{2}
-norm is preserved by the stochastic transport equation and not by the limit heat equation (different is the case when a dissipation is already present in the transport equation, see the recent work
[
1
]
). The weak convergence is only weak; here we reinforce this fact by showing that the limit Young measure is not trivial. Indeed we prove that, for
every test functions
\varphi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
\psi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{T}^{d}\right)
, for every
t\in\left[0,T\right]
we
have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi\left(\theta^{n}\left(t,x\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\theta\right)\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx

for a suitable probability measure
\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)
on

\mathbb{R}
, measurably dependent on
\left(t,x\right)
, having the
property

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)=\overline{\theta}\left(t,x\right).

The probability measure
\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)
is the Young measure
associated to the weak convergence of
\theta^{n}
to
\overline{\theta}
, and
solves, in the distributional sense, the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{T}\Delta_{x}\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle\mu\left(0,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(d\theta\right).

As shown by the example of
3.1
below, this Young measure is not trivial, as it should be when strong convergence does not hold.

Locally around each point
x_{0}
(choose, in the limit above, a probability density function
\psi
concentrated around
x_{0}
) the values of
\theta^{n}(x,t)
have large fluctuations/oscillations: the complex Lagrangian dynamics produced by the transport noise mixes the different values of the initial condition
\overline{\theta}_{0}(x)
to such a high degree that everywhere, locally, there are high variations; before taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, these local oscillations around a point
x_{0}
are summarized, at
x_{0}
, by the non-trivial measure
\mu\left(t,x_{0},d\theta\right)
. This is just the general interpretation of Young measures but it may be convenient to recall it for the understanding of the result, in connection with the mixing properties of the flow.

The result above comes from the following fact: in the background of the stochastic transport equation above there is a
stochastic Vlasov equation for measures

\displaystyle d\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{div}_{x}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\left(0,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(d\theta\right)

and
\mu\left(t,x,d\theta\right)
is the weak limit of
\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)
. It is the Vlasov equation of the dynamics

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{x,n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,j}\sigma_{k}^{n}\left(X_{t}^{x,n}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{t}^{x,n}}{dt}

\displaystyle=0

which represents the Lagrangian view of the transport equation, where

\theta_{t}^{x,n}=\theta^{n}\left(t,X_{t}^{x,n}\right).

Let us remark that in homogenization theory there are several results about the diffusion limit, see for instance
[
23
]
. However they always require a dissipation in the original transport equation, hence it is not clear that one can prove for such models a result like the one above for the purely-transport stochastic problem.

1.2.
Stochastic passive vector field

The result described so far is just a minor reformulation of the fundamental
result of
[
17
]
, just emphasizing the local oscillation properties of
\theta^{n}(t,x)
. The real progress comes in the case of vector advected quantities.
Consider the passive vector equation

\displaystyle dB^{n}\left(t,x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,j}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\cdot\nabla B^{n}\left(t,x\right)-B^{n}\left(t,x\right)\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}

\displaystyle B^{n}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{0}.

Also here, very recently
[
11
]
, it has been proved that, in a
suitable weak sense, under suitable assumptions on
\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\right)
,
B^{n}\left(t,x\right)
weakly converges to
\overline{B}\left(t,x\right)
solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{B}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\overline{B}

\displaystyle\overline{B}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\overline{B}_{0}

where
\mathcal{D}
is a linear operator, described below, in some cases just
identically equal to zero. The Lagrangian dynamics behind the vector advection
equation is much richer:

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{x,n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,j}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(X_{t}^{x,N}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}=0

\displaystyle{db_{t}^{x,n}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,j}b_{t}^{x,n}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(X_{t}^{x,n}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}=0

where

b_{t}^{x,n}=B^{n}\left(t,X_{t}^{x,n}\right).

The associated Vlasov equation is now (we denote
\mu_{n}\left(t,x,db\right)
simply by
\mu_{n}
)

\displaystyle d\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{div}_{x}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\mu_{n}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}+\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(b\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\left(x\right)\mu_{n}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k,j}

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\left(0,x,db\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(db\right).

Under suitable assumptions on
\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\right)
we prove that

\mu_{n}\left(t,x,db\right)
weakly converges to
\mu\left(t,x,db\right)
, solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\mu

\displaystyle\mu\left(0,x,db\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(db\right)

for a suitable differential operator
\mathcal{L}
. Opposite to the case of
the passive scalar
\theta
, here
\mathcal{L}
may include second order terms
in
b
which produce a regularization in the
b
-variable of the initial
condition
\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(db\right)
. Again we have
the compatibility relations (now for
\varphi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
,
\psi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{T}^{3}\right)
)

(1.1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\varphi\left(B^{n}\left(t,x\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(b\right)\mu\left(t,x,db\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx

identifying
\mu\left(t,x,db\right)
as the Young measure associated to
the sequence
\left(B^{n}\right)
and

(1.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}b\mu\left(t,x,db\right)=\overline{B}\left(t,x\right).

Up to here, the interpretation of the result would be similar to the scalar case, namely that the reformulation with Young measures gives more insight about the oscillations. However, here the oscillations are not only produced by the mixing mechanism which moves different values of the initial condition close each other. Here the stretching may enhance the oscillations. One could believe that the equation for
\overline{B}
is sufficient to capture the effect of stretching, but it is not so. On the contrary, the background Vlasov equation introduced here does. Let us show this phenomenon.

Consider the case (inspired by
[
16
]
, devoted to the dynamics of polymers in turbulent fluids, which is different from many viewpoints but shares some elements) when suitable assumptions on
\left(\sigma_{k}^{n}\right)
imply

\mathcal{D}\overline{B}=0,

namely

(1.3)

\overline{B}\left(t,x\right)=\overline{B}_{0}\left(x\right).

The equation for
\mu\left(t,x,db\right)
is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(b\right)\nabla_{b}\mu\right)

\displaystyle\mu\left(0,x,db\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}\left(x\right)}\left(db\right)

where the matrix-valued function
\mathcal{L}\left(b\right)
is described below.
Except for
b=0
, this matrix is non-singular and provides solutions with full support in

b
; see
B.6
and its consequence by
B.1
, namely that for every
(x_{0},t)
and
R,r>0
, for large enough
n
,

\mathcal{L}_{3}\left\{x\in B\left(x_{0},r\right):\left|B^{n}\left(t,x\right)\right|\geq R\right\}>0.

Notice also that the stationary equation

\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(b\right)\nabla_{b}f\left(b\right)\right)=0

corresponding to uniform conditions in
x
, is solved by an explicit rotation-invariant
distribution, a probability density function
f(b)
. This density has full support on positive values.

Notice that in the scaling limit regime when
\mathcal{D}\overline{B}=0
holds, transport becomes irrelevant, hence the non-triviality of the Young measure is not due to transport mixing but only to turbulent stretching.

The interpretation of these results then is that the
stochastic vector advection equation, for large
n
, due to the turbulent Lagrangian stretching produced by
\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\right)
, develops wide
variations (and in particular large values) of
B^{n}\left(t,x\right)
at small scale, so that
in an infinitesimal volume around any point
x_{0}
there is a “density” of values
of
B^{n}\left(t,x_{0}\right)
(just a sort of empirical density, for finite

n
) converging in the limit to a measure
\mu\left(t,x_{0},db\right)
with a
true density. For large times the distribution in
x
is uniform and this
“density” of
B
-values is described by
f\left(b\right)
. In spite of the
fact that in the average we have (
1.3
). Therefore the background Vlasov
equation provides new information, not visible at the level of the vector
advection equation. The correspondence of
f\left(b\right)
with
statistical properties observed for magnetic fields in turbulent plasma and
other examples will be discussed elsewhere.

Some Preliminaries on Young Measures

Let us denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We recall here some standard facts on probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
for which we refer to
[
19
, Section 1.1]
,
[
2
, Section 5.1]
.
Let us start denoting by
\{\mathbf{x}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a dense set of
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\{(a-b\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{n}\rvert)\vee c,\quad a,b,c\in\mathbb{Q},\ b\geq 0,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\{f_{1}\vee f_{2}\vee\dots\vee f_{n},\quad f_{i}\in\mathcal{A},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\{g\in\mathbf{A}\cup(-\mathbf{A}):\left\|g\right\|_{C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})}\leq 1\}.

The set
\mathcal{G}
is countable, therefore introducing an enumeration of its elements
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the following distance on the probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}

\displaystyle\rho(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})(\mu-\nu)(d\mathbf{x})\right\rvert}{2^{i}},

it holds

Theorem 2.1
.

The weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})
is induced by the distance
\rho
.

Remark 2.2
.

Due to our definition, the functions
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
are uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, since

\displaystyle g_{i}=\pm(f_{1}\vee\dots\vee f_{n})

for some

and
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. In particular each

is constant outside a certain ball

, from which the required uniform continuity of

follows.

Secondly let us denote by
\mathcal{Y}
the subset of non negative Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
given by the Young measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We refer to
[
3

[
32
]

\mu\in\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle\mu(A\times\mathbb{R}^{k})=\mathcal{L}_{d}(A),

where
\mathcal{L}_{d}
is the Lebesgue’s measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. The latter, by disintegration theorem for measures, implies that there exists a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
parametrized by

,
\mu(x,db)
, such that for each

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x,b)d\mu(x,b)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x,b)\mu(x,db)dx

and the map

is weakly
∗

Given

Since the measure of the

Theorem 2.1
.
We recall now a compactness criteria for Young measures.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that

\displaystyle\forall\delta>0,\ x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\ \exists A_{x}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}\textit{ compact: }\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}^{*}\mu_{n}(x,db)(A_{x}^{c})dx<\delta,

then there exists a subsequence

and a Young measure
\mu
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{n_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{Y}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mu.

Let us denote
B(x,R)\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
the ball of radius
R
centered at
x
. Combining
Theorem 2.1
,
Theorem 2.3
and Ascoli-Arzelà Theorem for functions with values in metric spaces
[
22
]
implies the following

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
\delta>0
, introduce for each
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
some positive quantities
R=R(k,\delta)
and
\theta=\theta(k,\delta,i)
, then the set

\displaystyle K^{\delta}:=\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\{\mu\in C([0,T];\mathcal{Y}):\

\displaystyle\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}^{*}\mu_{t}(x)(B(0,R))dx\leq\frac{1}{k},

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{\lvert t-s\rvert<\theta}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})(\mu_{t}-\mu_{s})(d\mathbf{x})\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{k}\}

is relatively compact in

.

Proof.

Given a sequence
(\mu^{n})\subset K^{\delta},

by Ascoli-Arzelà Theorem, the claim is equivalent to the fact that

(1)

\forall t\in[0,T]

\{\mu^{n}_{t}\}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{Y}
.

(2)

Uniform equicontinuity holds.

The first condition is immediately true due to
Theorem 2.3
, concerning the second one, let us fix
\varepsilon
and find
\overline{k},\ \overline{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\overline{k}>{\frac{2}{\varepsilon}},\quad\sum_{i\geq\overline{i}}\frac{\lvert\mathbb{T}^{d}\rvert}{2^{i-1}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

and
\gamma=\min\{\theta(\overline{k},\delta,i),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,\overline{i}\}\}>0
.
Therefore, for each
n\in\mathbb{N},\ \lvert t-s\rvert\leq\gamma

\displaystyle\rho(\mu^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{s})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{i}-1}\frac{\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x})(\mu^{n}_{t}-\mu^{n}_{s})(d\mathbf{x})\right\rvert}{2^{i}}+2\lvert\mathbb{T}^{d}\rvert\sum_{i\geq\overline{i}}\frac{1}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{i}-1}\frac{1}{2^{i}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon.

∎

2.2.
Main Results

Stochastic Passive Scalar

Let us start describing the coefficients of the stochastic transport term appearing in the transport equation of
subsection 1.1
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{W_{t}^{k,j}\},

{{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0},\ j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}}}
we introduce a sequence of complex-valued Brownian motions adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}

such that

W^{-k,j}_{t}=(W^{k,j}_{t})^{*}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W^{k,j}_{1},{W^{l,m}_{1}}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&\textit{if }k=l,\ m=j\\
0&\textit{otherwise};\end{cases}\quad\left[W^{k,j}_{\cdot},W^{l,m}_{\cdot}\right]_{t}=\begin{cases}2t&\textit{if }k=-l,\ m=j\\
0&\textit{otherwise }.\end{cases}

Secondly, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0},\ j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
we denote by
\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)=\theta_{k,j}^{n}a_{k,j}e^{ik\cdot x}
, where
\{\frac{k}{\lvert k\rvert},a_{k,1},\dots,a_{k,d-1}\}
is an orthonormal system of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
k\in\Gamma_{d,+}
and
a_{k,j}=a_{-k,j}
if
k\in\Gamma_{d,-}
, while

(2.1)

\displaystyle\theta_{k,j}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d\kappa_{T}}{(d-1)c_{n}}}\bm{1}_{\{n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\}}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert^{d/2}},\quad c_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0}\\
n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert^{d}}=O(1),\quad\kappa_{T}>0.

With our choices, we ensure that
W^{n}
takes values in a space of real valued functions.
It is well known that the family
\left\{a_{k,j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}}}e^{ik\cdot x}\right\},\ {{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0},\ j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}}}
is a complete orthogonal systems of
H
made by eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
. Thanks to this choice of the coefficients, the equation for the passive scalar of
subsection 1.1
can be rewritten in Itô form as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\theta^{n}_{t}&=\kappa_{T}\Delta\theta^{n}_{t}dt+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla\theta^{n}_{t}dW^{k,j}_{t},\\
\theta^{n}_{0}&=\overline{\theta}_{0},\end{cases}

see
[
17
, Section 2.3]
for details. We are interested to analytically weak solution of (
2.2
) as described by the following definition.

Definition 2.5
.

A stochastic process
\theta^{n}\in C_{\mathcal{F}}([0,T];{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is a weak solution of equation (
2.2
) if
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.

\displaystyle\langle\theta^{n}_{t},\phi\rangle-\langle\overline{\theta}_{0},\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla\theta^{n}_{s},\nabla\phi\rangle ds+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla\theta^{n}_{s},\phi\rangle dW^{k,j}_{s}

for each
\phi\in{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\ t\in[0,T]
.

The well-posedness of (
2.2
) in the sense of
2.5
is guaranteed by the following standard result, which collect also some a priori estimates crucial for the following arguments.

Theorem 2.6
.

For each
\overline{\theta}_{0}\in{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
there exists a unique weak solution of (
2.2
) in the sense of
2.5
. Moreover
\theta^{n}\in C_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;{H}^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and

\displaystyle d\left\|\theta^{n}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Theorem 2.6
might be well known to the experts. Therefore, we omit its proof which however can be obtained following
verbatim the one provided below in a more complex framework for the magnetic field, see
Theorem 2.9
and
Appendix A
. As discussed in
subsection 1.1
, the results of
[
17
,
14
]
imply, due to the choice of the coefficients (
2.1
), that the family
\theta^{n}
converges
weakly
to
\overline{\theta}
solving

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\overline{\theta}(x,t)&=\kappa_{T}\Delta\overline{\theta}(t,x)\quad x\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\ t\in[0,T]\\
\overline{\theta}(0,x)&=\overline{\theta}_{0}.\end{cases}

Our way to reinterpret this result in our framework is the following: let us introduce the family of Young measures

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)=\delta_{\theta^{n}_{t}(x)}(d\theta),

then the following holds.

Theorem 2.7
.

For every pair
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\psi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and for every
t\in[0,T],\ \overline{\theta}_{0}\in{H}^{1}
, denoting by

\displaystyle{\mu}(t,x,d\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}p(\kappa_{T}t,x-y)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(y)}(d\theta)dy,

then

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx\right\rvert^{2}\right]=0.

In particular
\mu
satisfies

(2.4)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(x)}dx\\
=\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\Delta\psi(x)\mu(s,x,d\theta)dxds

for each
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

and

\overline{\theta}\left(x,t\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta{\mu}\left(t,x,d\theta\right).

The limit PDE satisfied by
\mu
keeps track of the hyperbolic structure of the SPDE satisfied by
\theta^{n}
on the contrary that the one for
\overline{\theta}.
Indeed, choosing formally
\psi\equiv 1,\ \varphi(\theta)=\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx=0.

By Itô formula in
Theorem 2.6
we also have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx=0.

The relation above fails obviously for the the measure
\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{t}(x)}(d\theta)dx
. We will come back on this issue in
3.2
.

Stochastic Passive Vector Field

Similarly to previous subsection, let us start introducing the noise affecting our equation for the magnetic field. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{W_{t}^{k,j}\},

{{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0},\ j\in\{1,2\}}}
we introduce a sequence of complex-valued Brownian motions adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}

such that

W^{-k,j}_{t}=(W^{k,j}_{t})^{*}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W^{k,j}_{1},{W^{l,m}_{1}}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&\textit{if }k=l,\ m=j\\
0&\textit{otherwise};\end{cases}\quad\left[W^{k,j}_{\cdot},W^{l,m}_{\cdot}\right]_{t}=\begin{cases}2t&\textit{if }k=-l,\ m=j\\
0&\textit{otherwise }.\end{cases}

Secondly, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0},\ j\in\{1,2\}
we denote by
\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)=\theta_{k,j}^{n}a_{k,j}e^{ik\cdot x}
, where
\{\frac{k}{\lvert k\rvert},a_{k,1},a_{k,2}\}
is an orthonormal system of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
k\in\Gamma_{3,+}
and
a_{k,j}=a_{-k,j}
if
k\in\Gamma_{3,-}
, while

(2.5)

\displaystyle\theta_{k,j}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\chi}\bm{1}_{\{n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\}}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert^{5/2}},\ {\chi}>0.

With our choices, we ensure that
W^{n}
takes values in a space of real valued functions.
It is well known that the family
\{a_{k,j}\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ik\cdot x}\},\ {{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0},\ j\in\{1,2\}}}
is a complete orthogonal systems of
H
made by eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
. We set

(2.6)

\displaystyle\eta_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert^{5}}=O(n^{-2}),\quad\alpha_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\

Thanks to this choice of the coefficients, the equation for the magnetic field of
subsection 1.2
can be rewritten in Itô form as

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dB^{n}_{t}&=\frac{2}{3}\chi\eta_{n}\Delta B^{n}_{t}dt+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla B^{n}_{t}-B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n})dW^{k,j}_{t},\\
\div B^{n}_{t}&=0,\\
B^{n}_{0}&=\overline{B}_{0},\end{cases}

see
[
11
, Section 2]
for details. We are interested to analytically weak solution of (
2.7
) as described by the following definition.

Definition 2.8
.

A stochastic process
B^{n}\in C_{\mathcal{F}}([0,T];H)\cap L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;V)
is a weak solution of equation (
2.7
) if
\mathbb{P}-
a.s.

\displaystyle\langle B^{n}_{t},\phi\rangle-\langle\overline{B}_{0},\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}\chi\eta_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla B^{n}_{s},\nabla\phi\rangle ds+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla B^{n}_{s}-B^{n}_{s}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n},\phi\rangle dW^{k,j}_{s}

for each
\phi\in V,\ t\in[0,T]
.

The well-posedness of (
2.7
) in the sense of
2.8
is guaranteed by the following standard result, which collect also some a priori estimates crucial for the following arguments.

Theorem 2.9
.

For each
\overline{B}_{0}\in V
there exists a unique weak solution of (
2.7
) in the sense of
2.8
. Moreover
B^{n}\in C_{\mathcal{F}}(0,T;V_{w})
and the following Itô formulas hold

(2.8)

\displaystyle d\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\langle B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n},B^{n}_{t}\rangle dW^{k,j}_{t}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\right\|^{2}dt,

\displaystyle d\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\langle B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n},B^{n}_{t}\rangle dW^{k,j}_{t}+\gamma\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\right\|^{2}dt

(2.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{\gamma-2}{2}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma-4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\lvert\langle B^{n}_{t}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n},B^{n}_{t}\rangle\rvert^{2}dt\quad\gamma\geq 4.

In particular

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T,\chi),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(\overline{B}_{0},T,\chi,\gamma).

Theorem 2.9
might be well known to the experts. Therefore, we postpone its proof which is based on standard arguments, namely compactness via a vanishing viscosity procedure, to
Appendix A
. According to the results of
[
11
]
, due to the choice of the coefficients (
2.6
), the family
B^{n}
converges
weakly
to the constant profile
\overline{B}_{t}\equiv\overline{B}_{0}
. However, looking at the family of Young measures

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(t,x,db)=\delta_{B^{n}_{t}(x)}(db),

more can be seen. Indeed, denoting by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(b):=\frac{8\pi\chi\log 2}{15}\left(2\lvert b\rvert^{2}I-b\otimes b\right)

and by
\mu
the solution of the following PDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu(t,x,db)&=\operatorname{div}(\mathcal{L}(b)\mu(t,x,db))\quad b\in\mathbb{R}^{3},x\in\mathbb{T}^{3},\ t\in[0,T]\\
\mu(0,x,db)&=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db),\end{cases}

defined rigorously in
section 4
, then our main result reads as follows:

Theorem 2.10
.

For each
\overline{B}_{0}\in V
,
\mu_{n}
converges in probability in
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
to
{\mu}
, where
\mu
is the unique weak solution of (
4.23
) in the sense of
4.8
. Moreover

\displaystyle\overline{B}_{0}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}b\mu(t,x,db).

Choosing formally
f(b)=\lvert b\rvert^{2}
as test function for the equation satisfied by
\mu
(this can be done rigorously by approximation as in
4.7
below), we obtain, by simple computations,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\mu(t,x,db)dx

\displaystyle=\left\|\overline{B}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\frac{16\pi\chi\log 2}{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\mu(s,x,db)dxds.

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\mu(t,x,db)dx=e^{\frac{16\pi\chi\log 2}{3}t}\left\|\overline{B}_{0}\right\|^{2}.

On the contrary

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\delta_{\overline{B}_{t}(x)}(db)dx=\left\|\overline{B}_{0}\right\|^{2}.

We see this fact as a consequence that the limit PDE for
\mu
, on the contrary of the one for
\overline{B}_{t}
, keeps track of the capability of the magnetic field induced by the motion of a conducting fluid,

, to self excite. This phonemenon is called in the literature magnetic dynamo,
cf.
[
25
]
.
We postpone to
Appendix B
some more quantitative comments on the consequences of

like properties of the support of the measure
\mu(t,x,db)
and its links with the dynamo theory.

3.
The scalar case

We start by observing that, by density, it is enough to prove the validity of equation
Theorem 2.7
for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Indeed, assume relation (
2.3
) holds for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Let us now consider
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. For each
\varepsilon>0,
by density it is possible to find
\varphi^{N}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi^{N}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi^{N}-\varphi\right\|_{C_{b}}<\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad\left\|\psi^{N}-\psi\right\|_{C_{b}}<\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx\right\rvert^{2}\right]\\
\lesssim\varepsilon+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{N}(\theta)\psi^{N}(x)\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{N}(\theta)\psi^{N}(x)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx\right\rvert^{2}\right].

Considering the limsup in
n
of the expression above we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx\right\rvert^{2}\right]\lesssim\varepsilon

and the claim follows from the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
Secondly, one can prove that the solutions given by
Theorem 2.6
are renormalizable. By
this we mean that, given
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}),\ \psi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\theta^{n}_{t}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)d\delta_{\theta^{n}_{t}(\cdot)}\in C_{\mathcal{F}}([0,T];H^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C_{\mathcal{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi(\theta^{n}_{t}(x))\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi(\overline{\theta}_{0}(x))\psi(x)dx+\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi({\theta}_{s}(x))\Delta\psi(x)dxds

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi({\theta}_{s}(x))\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla\psi(x)dW^{k,j}_{s}\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

In particular, by
Theorem 2.6
, it is the unique solution of (
2.2
) in the sense of
2.5
with initial condition
\varphi\circ\overline{\theta}_{0}.

The proof of this claim can be obtained following verbatim the argument provided below in
subsection 4.1
in a more complex framework for the magnetic field, therefore we avoid to add details in this easier framework.

Recalling the definition of the Young measure

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\theta^{n}_{t}\left(x\right)}\left(d\theta\right),

we can rewrite (
3.1
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\mu_{n}(t,x,db)dx=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\psi(x)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(x)}(db)dx

\displaystyle+\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\Delta\psi(x)\mu_{n}(s,x,db)dxds

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}\\

Notice that we cannot deduce any convergence of

from
the weak convergence of
\theta^{n}
guaranteed by the results of
[
17
]
,
[
14
]
. Thus, the equations for
\theta^{n}
lead
to equations for
\mu_{n}
, but convergence properties do not translate
immediately from
\theta^{n}
to
\mu_{n}
.

The following remark may help to get an intuition about the main result of the section.

Remark 3.1
.

Assume
\overline{\theta}_{0}\left(x\right)=\sum_{j}a_{j}1_{D_{j}}\left(x\right)
over a finite partition
\left(D_{j}\right)
, with different
values
a_{j}
. Then

\theta^{n}\left(x,t\right)=\sum_{j}a_{j}1_{D_{j}^{n}\left(t\right)}\left(x\right)

where
\left\{D_{j}^{n}\left(t\right)\right\}
is again a (random)
partition, and

\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)=\delta_{\theta^{n}_{t}\left(x\right)}\left(d\theta\right)=\sum_{j}\delta_{a_{j}}\left(d\theta\right)1_{D_{j}^{n}\left(t\right)}\left(x\right).

The process
1_{D_{j}^{n}\left(t\right)}\left(x\right)
is the
solution of the stochastic transport equation with initial condition

. Applying for every

the results above we get
that
\mu_{n}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)
weakly converges to

{\mu}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)=\sum_{j}\delta_{a_{j}}\left(d\theta\right)\overline{\theta}_{j}\left(x,t\right)

where
\overline{\theta}_{j}\left(x,t\right)
is the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\overline{\theta}_{j}&=\kappa_{T}\Delta\overline{\theta}_{j}\\
\overline{\theta}_{j}|_{t=0}&=1_{D_{j}}\end{cases}

Now we are ready to provide the proof of

.

Proof of

.

\varphi\in~C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}),

\psi\in~C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
Moreover we know that, denoting by

j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi^{n}_{t}dW^{k,j}_{t}\\
\varphi^{n}_{0}&=\varphi\circ\overline{\theta}_{0}\end{cases}

in the sense of
2.5
. Due to our choice of
\varphi
,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]},\left\|\varphi^{n}_{t}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{C_{b}}\ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

Due to our choice of the coefficients
\sigma_{k,j}^{n}
, see in particular equation (
2.1
), we can apply
[
14
, Theorem 1.7]
. If we denote by
\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}}
the unique weak solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}}&=\kappa_{T}\Delta\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}}\\
\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}}(0)&=\varphi\circ\overline{\theta}_{0},\end{cases}

for each
\psi
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}}(t)-\varphi^{n}(t),\psi\rangle^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{\left\|\psi\right\|^{2}\left\|\kappa_{T}\varphi\right\|_{C_{b}}^{2}}{n^{d}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow+\infty.

Let us rewrite
\langle\overline{\varphi}_{\theta_{0}}(t),\psi\rangle
and
\langle\varphi^{n}(t),\psi\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi^{n}(t),\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\varphi\circ\theta^{n}_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\delta_{\theta^{n}_{t}(x)}(d\theta)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\varphi(\theta)\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx

recalling the definition of
\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)
.
While

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\varphi}_{\overline{\theta}_{0}},\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}p(\kappa_{T}t,x-y)\varphi\circ\overline{\theta}_{0}(y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(\kappa_{T}t,x-y)\varphi(\theta)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(y)}(d\theta)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}p(\kappa_{T}t,x-y)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(y)}(d\theta)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\theta){\mu}(t,x,d\theta)dx,

where

\displaystyle{\mu}(t,x,d\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}p(\kappa_{T}t,x-y)\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{0}(y)}(d\theta)dy.

The remaining claims follow from the definition above of
\mu
and the one of heat kernel. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The measure

\overline{\mu}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)=\delta_{\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)}\left(d\theta\right)

is
not
a solution of the limit PDE. Indeed it satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\varphi(\theta)\overline{\mu}\left(t,x,d\theta\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi\left(x\right)\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)dx

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\varphi(\theta)\overline{\mu}\left(0,x,d\theta\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\psi\left(x\right)\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{0}\left(x\right)\right)dx

\displaystyle\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\Delta\psi(x)\varphi(\theta)\overline{\mu}\left(s,x,d\theta\right)dxds

\displaystyle=\kappa_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta\psi\left(x\right)\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{s}\left(x\right)\right)dxds

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)\partial_{t}\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)\nabla\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right),

but

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)\left|\nabla\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right|^{2}+\varphi^{\prime}\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)\Delta\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\neq\varphi^{\prime}\left(\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right)\right)\Delta\overline{\theta}_{t}\left(x\right).

Moreover, let us observe the following fact concerning the different behaviour of the limit measure
\mu(t,x,d\theta)
and
\overline{\mu}(t,x,d\theta)
. Let us choose as test functions for relation (
2.4
)
\psi\equiv 1
and a family
\varphi^{M}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as
\varphi^{M}(\theta)=M\wedge\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}
. In this way we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{M}(\theta)\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}M\wedge\lvert\overline{\theta}_{0}(x)\rvert^{2}dx.

Therefore, letting
M\rightarrow+\infty,
due to monotone convergence theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}\mu(t,x,d\theta)dx=\left\|\overline{\theta}_{0}\right\|^{2}.

Similarly, the Itô formula of
Theorem 2.6
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}\mu_{n}(t,x,d\theta)dx=\left\|\overline{\theta}_{0}\right\|^{2}.

On the contrary, the dissipative nature of the equation satisfied by
\overline{\theta}
implies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert\theta\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(t,x,d\theta)dx\leq\left\|\overline{\theta}_{0}\right\|^{2}e^{-2\kappa_{T}t}.

4.
The Case of the Magnetic Field

The proof of
Theorem 2.10
relies on a stochastic compactness argument on
\mu_{n}(t,x,db)
. The proof is splitted in three main steps corresponding to
subsection 4.1
,
subsection 4.2
and
subsection 4.3
.
We prove some properties of the Young measure
\mu_{n}(t,x,db)
in
subsection 4.1
. Such properties allow us to show the tightness of the laws of
\mu_{n}(t,x,db)
in the space
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
in
subsection 4.2
. Thanks Skorokhod’s representation theorem, we can pass to an auxiliary probability space where the convergence in law becomes
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
convergence in
subsection 4.3
. This allows us to identify a limit Young measure which satisfies a deterministic PDE and conclude the proof by uniqueness of the solutions of this PDE in our class.

In order to ease the notation, let us fix
\chi=1

Remark 4.1

Let
\varrho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
a non negative, even function such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varrho(x)dx=1,\quad\operatorname{supp}\varrho\subseteq[-1/2,1/2]^{3}.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
\varrho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}\varrho(\cdot/\varepsilon).
If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
it can be extended to a locally integrable periodic function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so that the convolution
\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ast f
makes sense and it is still a periodic function. It is easy to show, see for example
[
11
, Remark 2.15]
that if
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
then

\displaystyle\langle B^{n}_{t},\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle B^{n}_{0},\phi\rangle+\frac{2}{3}\eta_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\langle B^{n}_{s},\Delta\phi\rangle ds-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\sigma_{k,j}^{n}\cdot\nabla\phi,B^{n}_{s}\rangle dW^{k,j}_{s}

In particular, denoting by

and choosing
\phi^{x}(y)=\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-y),\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-y),\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right)^{t}
as a test function we obtain

\displaystyle B^{n,\varepsilon}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=B^{n,\varepsilon}_{0}(x)+\frac{2}{3}\eta_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\Delta B^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}(x)ds

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\cdot\nabla B^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}(x)-B^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}(x)\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\right)dW^{k,j}_{s}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)-\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x-z)\right)\cdot\nabla\varrho_{\varepsilon}(z)B^{n}_{s}(x-z)dzdW^{k,j}_{s}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}B^{n}_{s}(z)\cdot\nabla\left(\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(z)-\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\right)\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-z)dzdW^{k,j}_{s}.

Now on for this subsection we will drop the index
n
in our notation, since we are interested to prove the validity of (
4.2
) for
n
fixed. In order to reach our goal, as it is customary in the framework of hyperbolic equations, we rely on a commutator lemma.
Therefore, let us introduce a notation we will use in the following.
If
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}),\ B\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
are divergence free vector field for some
p\in[1,+\infty)
we denote by

\displaystyle[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(v(x)-v(x-z)\right)\cdot\nabla\varrho_{\varepsilon}(z)B(x-z)dz

and by

\displaystyle[\cdot\nabla v,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}B(z)\cdot\nabla\left(v(z)-v(x)\right)\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-z)dz.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty)
,
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}),\ B\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
divergence free vector fields.
Then

\displaystyle\left\|[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rightarrow 0,\quad\left\|[\cdot\nabla v,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.

Moreover

\displaystyle\left\|[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}},\quad\left\|[\cdot\nabla v,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\varepsilon\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{C^{0}_{b}}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}}.

Proof.

First we observe that by change of variables

\displaystyle[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{v(x)-v(x-\varepsilon y)}{\varepsilon}\nabla\varrho(y)B(x-\varepsilon y)dy.

Secondly, since
v
is smooth, the fundamental theorem of calculus implies

\displaystyle v(x)-v(x-\varepsilon y)=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}y\cdot\nabla v(x-(1-s)y)ds.

Therefore

(4.4)

\displaystyle[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}y\cdot\nabla v(x-(1-s)\varepsilon y)\nabla\varrho(y)B(x-\varepsilon y)dsdy.

Now we are ready to compute the
L^{p}
norm of the commutator. Indeed, by Hölder’s inequality, since the support of
\varrho
is compact

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left\lvert[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B(x)\right\rvert^{p}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}^{p}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}^{p}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\varrho}\lvert B(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}dxdy

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}^{p}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}^{p}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.

Concerning the convergence to
0
we observe that, since

\displaystyle B(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}y\cdot\nabla v(x)\nabla\varrho(y)dy

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}B(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\delta_{i,j}\partial_{j}v^{j}(x)\varrho(y)dy

\displaystyle\equiv 0,

it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\left\|[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B-B(\cdot)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}y\cdot\nabla v(\cdot)\nabla\varrho(y)dy\right\|_{L^{p}}\rightarrow 0.

Thanks to Hölder inequality, relation (
4.4
) and the fact that the support of
\varrho
is included in
[-1/2,1/2]^{3}
we obtain

\left\|[v\cdot\nabla,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B-B(\cdot)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}y\cdot\nabla v(\cdot)\nabla\varrho(y)dy\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\\
\lesssim\int_{[-1/2,1/2]^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-(1-s)\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}dxdsdy\\
=\int_{[-1/2,1/2]^{3}}f^{\varepsilon}(y)dy,

where we denoted by

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}(y)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-(1-s)\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}dxds.

The function
f^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0
a.e. indeed

\displaystyle\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-(1-s)\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle+\lvert B(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}\lvert\nabla v(x-\varepsilon y)-\nabla v(x-(1-s)\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{p}\lvert B(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}^{p}.

Therefore

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}(y)\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert B(x)\nabla v(x)-B(x-\varepsilon y)\nabla v(x-\varepsilon y)\rvert^{p}dxds+\varepsilon^{p}\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}^{p}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\rightarrow 0

since the first term converges to
0
thanks to the continuity of translations in
L^{p}
. Moreover, we can apply dominated convergence theorem since previous computations imply by change of variables

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}(y)\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}^{p}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.

The analysis of the second commutator is easier. Indeed, by change of variables

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}B(z)\cdot\nabla\left(v(z)-v(x)\right)\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-z)dz\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}B(x-\varepsilon z)\cdot\nabla\left(v(x-\varepsilon z)-v(x)\right)\varrho(z)dz\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\left\|v\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}\left\|\varrho\right\|_{C^{0}_{b}}\int_{[-1/2,1/2]^{3}}\lvert B(x-\varepsilon z)\rvert dz.

Therefore by Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\left\|[\cdot\nabla v,\varrho_{\varepsilon}]B\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{p}\left\|v\right\|^{p}_{C^{2}_{b}}\left\|\varrho\right\|^{p}_{C^{0}_{b}}\left\|B\right\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\rightarrow 0.

∎

Next step is to compute, for a smooth function

Proof.

) on

First let us observe that by chain rule and integration by parts

, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}Tr\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}(B^{n}_{r}(x))\nabla^{2}_{b}f(x,B^{n}_{r}(x))\right)dxdr\right\rvert^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert B^{n}_{r}(x)\rvert^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert^{3}}\left\|\nabla^{2}_{b}f\right\|_{C_{b}}dxdr\right\rvert^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha_{n}^{\gamma}|t-s|^{\gamma}\left\|\nabla^{2}_{b}f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{2\gamma}\right]

(4.12)

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{1}(\overline{B}_{0},T,2\gamma)\left\|\nabla^{2}_{b}f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\gamma}\lvert t-s\rvert^{\gamma}.

Lastly
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
can be treated by Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality. Indeed,

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{s}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}f\right)(x,B^{n}_{r}(x))dx\right)^{2}dr\right)^{\gamma/2}\right]

(4.13)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma}\eta_{n}^{\gamma/2}\left\|\nabla_{x}f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\gamma}\lvert t-s\rvert^{\gamma/2}.

Finally

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{s}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}B^{n}_{r}(x)\cdot\nabla\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\nabla_{b}f(x,B^{n}_{r}(x))dx\right)^{2}dr\right)^{\gamma/2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha_{n}^{\gamma/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{\gamma}\right]\left\|\nabla_{b}f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\gamma}\lvert t-s\rvert^{\gamma/2}

(4.14)

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha_{n}^{\gamma/2}C_{1}(\overline{B}_{0},T,\gamma)\left\|\nabla_{b}f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\gamma}\lvert t-s\rvert^{\gamma/2}.

Combining (
4.13
), (
4.14
), (
4.11
), (
4.12
) the result follows.
∎

As an immediate corollary of
4.4
and of the Garsia–Rodemich–Rumsey inequality, see
[
18
]
, we obtain

Corollary 4.5
.

For each
f\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}),\ \phi,\lambda
such that
\phi<\frac{1}{2},\ \phi-\frac{1}{\lambda}>0
then

\displaystyle\int_{T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)\mu_{n}(t,x,db)dx

has a
\phi-\frac{1}{\lambda}
Hölder version. Moreover

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)\mu_{n}(t,x,db)dx\right\|_{C^{0,\phi-\frac{1}{\lambda}}}^{\lambda}\right]\leq C(\phi,\lambda,\overline{B}_{0},T)\left\|f\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}^{\lambda}.

Proof.

Thanks to Garsia–Rodemich–Rumsey inequality we know that for each
t,s

(4.15)

\frac{\left\lvert\int_{T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)\left(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db)\right)dx\right\rvert^{\lambda}}{\lvert t-s\rvert^{\phi\lambda-1}}\\
\leq C(\phi,\lambda)\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\left\lvert\int_{T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)\left(\mu_{n}(r,x,db)-\mu_{n}(v,x,db)\right)dx\right\rvert^{\lambda}}{\lvert r-v\rvert^{\phi\lambda+1}}drdv.

Therefore, combining (
4.15
) and
4.4
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{T^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)\mu_{n}(t,x,db)dx\right\|_{C^{0,\phi-\frac{1}{\lambda}}}^{\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\lambda}\left\|f\right\|_{C_{b}}^{\lambda}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{C_{2}(\overline{B}_{0},T,2\lambda)\left\|f\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\lambda}}{\lvert r-v\rvert^{\lambda(\phi-\frac{1}{2})+1}}drdv

\displaystyle\leq C(\phi,\lambda,\overline{B}_{0},T)\left\|f\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}^{\lambda}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Compactness of the laws

The goal of this section is to show that the laws of
\mu_{n}
are tight in the space of probability measure on
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y}).
We start introducing a family of mollifiers on
\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e. we introduce

\displaystyle h:\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\quad s.t.\ \operatorname{supp}h\subseteq B(\mathbf{0},1/2),\ h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}),\ h\geq 0,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}h(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=1

and set

h_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad h_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{6}}h(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\varepsilon})

We recall that if
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is uniformly continuous then

(4.16)

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}:=h_{\varepsilon}\ast f\rightarrow f\quad\text{in }C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\left\|f^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{C^{1}_{b}}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{C_{b}}\left\|\nabla h\right\|_{W^{1,1}}}{\varepsilon},\quad\left\|f^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{C_{b}}\left\|h\right\|_{W^{2,1}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.6
.

The laws of
\mu_{n}
are tight in the space of probability measure on
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y}).

Proof.

Thanks to
2.4
it is enough to show that for each
\delta>0
there exist a family of positive quantities
R(k,\delta),\ \theta(k,\delta,i),\ i,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}\notin\overline{K^{\delta}})\leq\delta.

By definition of
K^{\delta}
we have thanks to Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}\notin\overline{K^{\delta}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}\notin{K^{\delta}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}^{*}\mu_{n}(t,x,B(0,R(k,\delta)))dx>\frac{1}{k}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\lvert t-s\rvert<\theta(k,\delta,i)}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert>\frac{1}{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}^{*}\mu_{n}(t,x,B(0,R(k,\delta)))dx\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}k\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\lvert t-s\rvert<\theta(k,\delta,i)}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert\right]=H_{1}+H_{2}.

Thanks to the definition of
\mu_{n}
and
Theorem 2.9
we can easily treat
H_{1}
again by Markov’s inequality. Indeed,

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bm{1}_{|B^{n}_{t}(x)|>R(k,\delta)}(x)dx\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\mathbb{T}^{3}\rvert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{k}{R^{2}(k,\delta)}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|B^{n}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right]

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T)\lvert\mathbb{T}^{3}\rvert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{k}{R^{2}(k,\delta)}.

The estimate of
H_{2}
is more involved. Obviously we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert g_{i}(x,b)-g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,b)\rvert\lvert\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\mathbb{T}^{3}\rvert\left\|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{C_{b}}

\displaystyle+\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert.

Due to (
4.16
) for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
we can choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(k,\delta,i)
such that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbb{T}^{3}\rvert\left\|g_{i}-g_{i}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{C_{b}}\leq\frac{3\delta}{2^{i+1}\pi^{2}k^{3}}.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle H_{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}+\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}k\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\lvert t-s\rvert<\theta(k,\delta,i)}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(x,b)(\mu_{n}(t,x,db)-\mu_{n}(s,x,db))\right\rvert\right].

Lastly due to
4.5
, with the choice of
\phi=\frac{3}{8},\ \gamma=8
, and (
4.16
) we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle H_{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}+\frac{C(\overline{B}_{0},T)\left\|h\right\|_{W^{2,1}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}k\left(\theta(k,\delta,i)\right)^{1/4}.

Combining (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}\notin\overline{K^{\delta}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4}+C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T)\lvert\mathbb{T}^{3}\rvert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{k}{R^{2}(k,\delta)}+\frac{C_{3}(\overline{B}_{0},T)\left\|h\right\|_{W^{2,1}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}k\left(\theta(k,\delta,i)\right)^{1/4}.

Choosing

\displaystyle R(k,\delta)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^{3}k^{3}C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T)|\mathbb{T}^{3}|}{3\delta}},\quad\theta(k,\delta,i)=\left(\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{2^{i+1}\pi^{2}k^{3}\left\|h\right\|_{W^{2,1}}C_{3}(\overline{B}_{0},T)}\right)^{4}

the claim follows and the proof is complete.
∎

4.3.
Passage to the Limit

Thanks to
Theorem 4.6
, by Jakubowski version of Skorokhod’s representation’s theorem,
[
21
, Theorem 2]
, up to passing to subsequences, we can find an auxiliary probability space, that for simplicity we continue to call
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and processes

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\ W^{n}:=\{W^{n,k,j}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\right),\quad\left(\overline{\mu},\ W:=\{W^{k,j}\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\right),

such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mu}\quad\text{in }C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

\displaystyle W^{n}\rightarrow W\quad\text{in }C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}})\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

Of course the convergence above between
W^{n}
and
W
can be seen as the uniform convergence of cylindrical Wiener processes
W^{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}e_{k,j}W^{n,k,j},\ W=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}e_{k,j}W^{k,j}
on a suitable Hilbert space
U_{0}
.
Before going on, in order to identify the PDE satisfied by
\overline{\mu}
we provide integrability properties of
\overline{\mu}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The limit measure
\overline{\mu}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)dx\right]\leq C(\overline{B}_{0},T).

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
converges to
\overline{\mu}
in
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
, for each
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
p\geq 1
, by dominated convergence theorem,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(b,x)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(t,x,db)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(b,x)\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)dx\right\rvert^{p}dt\rightarrow 0\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

Choose a smooth test function such that

\displaystyle\phi^{M}(b,x)=\begin{cases}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\quad&\text{if }\lvert b\rvert\leq M\\
0\quad&\text{if }\lvert b\rvert\geq M+1,\end{cases}\quad\left\|\phi^{M}\right\|_{C_{b}}\leq M^{2}.

Obviously
\phi^{M}(b,x)\nearrow\lvert b\rvert^{2}
pointwise. Therefore, due to (
4.19
), Fatou’s Lemma and
Theorem 2.9

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi^{M}(b,x)\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)dx\right\rvert^{p}dt\right)^{1/p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi^{M}(b,x)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(t,x,db)dx\right\rvert^{p}dt\right)^{1/p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\tilde{\mu}_{n}(t,x,db)dx\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T).

Then, by monotone convergence theorem, for each
p\geq 1

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)dx\right\rvert^{p}dt\right)^{1/p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(\overline{B}_{0},T).

This implies that
\left\|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(\cdot,x,db)dx\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}<+\infty\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.
Therefore

(4.21)

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(\cdot,x,db)dx\right\|_{L^{p}(0,T)}\rightarrow\left\|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\overline{\mu}(\cdot,x,db)dx\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}.

Combining (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) we obtain the result by Fatou’s lemma and the continuity of
\overline{\mu}
.
∎

By the properties of Riemann sums we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n}(b)=(b\otimes b):\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
n\leq\lvert k\rvert\leq 2n\end{subarray}}\frac{k\otimes k}{\lvert k\rvert^{5}}\otimes\left(I-\frac{k\otimes k}{\lvert k\rvert^{2}}\right)\right)

(4.22)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(b):=(b\otimes b):\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ 1\leq\lvert\xi\rvert\leq 2}\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{\lvert\xi\rvert^{5}}\otimes\left(I-\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}\right)d\xi=\frac{8\pi\log 2}{15}\left(2\lvert b\rvert^{2}I-b\otimes b\right)

uniformly on compact sets.

Let us introduce our limit pde on the space of Young measures

(4.23)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu(t,x,db)&=\operatorname{div}(\mathcal{L}(b)\mu(t,x,db))\quad b\in\mathbb{R}^{3},x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}\ t\in[0,T]\\
\mu(0,x,db)&=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db)\end{cases}

and its notion of solution

Definition 4.8
.

We say that
\mu\in C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
is a weak solution of equation (
4.23
) if

\displaystyle\mu(0,x,db)=\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db),\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert b\rvert^{2}\mu(t,x,db)dx<+\infty

and for each
f\in C_{c}^{1}((0,T);C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}))
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}f(s,x,b){\mu}(s,x,db)dxds+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}(b)\nabla_{b}f_{s}(x,b)\right){\mu}(s,x,db)dxds=0.

Remark 4.9
.

Arguing as in
[
30
, Remark 2.3]
, if
\mu
is a weak solution of (
4.23
) in the sense of
4.8
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{t_{2}}(x,b)\mu(t_{2},x,db)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{t_{1}}(x,b)\mu(t_{1},x,db)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}f(s,x,b){\mu}(s,x,db)dxds+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}(b)\nabla_{b}f_{s}(x,b)\right)\mu(s,x,db)dxds

for each

0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
and
f\in C^{1}_{b}\left([0,T];C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)
.

The uniqueness of solutions of (
4.23
) in the sense of
4.8
is an easy application of the results of
[
30
]
. Indeed, the following holds.

Proposition 4.10
.

There exists a unique
\mu\in C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
which solves (
4.23
) in the sense of
4.8
.

Proof.

We start observing that for each
b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(b)=\frac{8\pi\log 2\lvert b\rvert^{2}}{15}\frac{b\otimes b}{\lvert b\rvert^{2}}+\frac{16\pi\log 2\lvert b\rvert^{2}}{15}\left(I-\frac{b\otimes b}{\lvert b\rvert^{2}}\right).

Therefore
\mathcal{L}(b)=\mathcal{A}(b)\mathcal{A}(b)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(b)=\frac{\sqrt{8\pi\log 2}\lvert b\rvert}{\sqrt{15}}\frac{b\otimes b}{\lvert b\rvert^{2}}+\frac{4\sqrt{\pi\log 2}\lvert b\rvert}{\sqrt{15}}\left(I-\frac{b\otimes b}{\lvert b\rvert^{2}}\right).

In particular
\mathcal{A}(b)
is a symmetric, non negative matrix for each
b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the function
b\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(b)
is Lipschitz with linear growth.
Since for each
j\in\{1,2,3\},\ \sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i,j}(b)=0
, if
\hat{W}
is a 3D Brownian motion, then the stochastic differential equation

(4.24)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}&=0,\\
db_{t}&=\mathcal{A}(b_{t})d\hat{W}_{t}\\
X_{0}&=x\\
b_{0}&=b\end{cases}

has (
4.23
) as Fokker-Planck equation associated. We have uniqueness in law of weak solutions of the stochastic differential equation (
4.24
) due to the properties of
\mathcal{A}(b)
. The latter implies uniqueness of solutions of (
4.23
) due to
[
30
, Theorem 2.5]
.
∎

Therefore, we are left to show that our limit object
\overline{\mu}
is a weak solution of (
4.23
). This is the content of the following result.

Proposition 4.11
.

\overline{\mu}
is the unique weak solution of (
4.23
) in the sense of
4.8
.

Proof.

We divide the proof in two steps.

Step 1: test function independent of time
. Let us start showing that for each
f\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
t\in[0,T]

(4.25)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)(\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)-\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db))dx\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}(b)\nabla_{b}f(x,b)\right)\overline{\mu}(s,x,db)dxds\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

By standard arguments, see for example
[
15
, Chapter 2]
, also
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
satisfies (
4.2
).
Now we pass to the limit in each term of (
4.2
). Thanks to the a.s. convergence of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
to
\overline{\mu}
in
C([0,T];\mathcal{Y})
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)(\tilde{\mu}_{n}(t,x,db)-\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db))dx\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,b)(\overline{\mu}(t,x,db)-\delta_{\overline{B}_{0}(x)}(db))dx\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

Secondly, as discussed at the beginning of the proof of
4.7
, since
\eta_{n}\rightarrow 0
,
\mathcal{L}^{n}(b)~\rightarrow~\mathcal{L}(b)
uniformly on compact sets and
f
has compact support we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\eta_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Delta_{x}f(x,b)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(s,x,db)dxds\right\rvert\lesssim\eta_{n}\left\|f\right\|_{C^{2}_{b}}~\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}(b)\nabla_{b}f(x,b)\right)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(s,x,db)dxds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}(b)\nabla_{b}f(x,b)\right)\overline{\mu}(s,x,db)dxds\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}(b)-\mathcal{L}(b)\right)\nabla_{b}f(x,b)\right)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(s,x,db)dxds\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}(b)\nabla_{b}f(x,b)\right)\left(\overline{\mu}(s,x,db)-\tilde{\mu}_{n}(s,x,db)\right)dxds\right\rvert\rightarrow 0\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.,

where we used that
\operatorname{div}_{b}\left(\mathcal{L}^{n}(b)-\mathcal{L}(b)\right)=0
to show the convergence of the first summand.
Concerning the stochastic integrals we argue by Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality obtaining

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\
j\in\{1,2\}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sigma_{k,j}^{n}(x)\cdot(\nabla_{x}f)(x,b)\tilde{\mu}_{n}(s,x,db)dxdW^{k,j}_{s}\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{0}\\

Appendix B
Some Remarks on the Occupation Measure

Let
f:\mathbb{T}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a measurable function.
Given
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
and
\varepsilon>0
, call
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}

the probability measure on Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\varphi\left(y\right)\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}\left(dy\right)=\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\varphi\left(f\left(x\right)\right)dx

for every
\varphi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
, where
B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)
is the ball in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
of center
x_{0}
and
radius
\varepsilon
and
\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|
is its Lebesgue measure. In other words,
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}
is the push
forward of the normalized Lebesgue measure
\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\mathcal{L}_{3}|_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}
on
B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)
under the map
f
;
or the image law of the random variable
f
considered on the probability
space
\left(B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right),\mathcal{B}\left(B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right),\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\mathcal{L}_{3}|_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\right)
.
We call
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}
the
local occupation measure around
x_{0}
of size
\varepsilon
.

Clearly, if
f
is continuous, the weak limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
of

\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}
is
\delta_{f\left(x_{0}\right)}
. When
f
is
rapidly oscillating, however,
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}

captures the
local oscillations in a statistical sense
, for finite
\varepsilon
. Here
”statistical” is understood in a spatial sense (opposite to the more common
temporal or probabilistic sense): where (on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
)
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}
gives more mass, more often we observe such values of
f

around
x_{0}
. The notion becomes particularly natural from the view point of
interpretation in the stationary case, notion that we express here only in a
loose way: if the phenomenon at hand is independent of position
x_{0}
and
maybe of a time parameter,
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}
captures the oscillations of

f
in a statistical sense, where now “statistical” may be interpreted in a
wider sense, not only locally spatial.

Rigorously, assume we have a sequence of measurable functions
\left(f_{n}\right)
from
\mathbb{T}^{3}
to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a measurable
function
x\mapsto\nu_{x}
from
\mathbb{T}^{3}
to the space of probability
measures on Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
endowed with the topology of weak
convergence, such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\varphi\left(f_{n}\left(x\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(y\right)\nu_{x}\left(dy\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx

for every
\varphi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)
and
\psi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{T}^{3}\right)
.
The measure-valued function

\left(\nu_{x}\right)
is called the Young measure associated
with the sequence
\left(f_{n}\right)
. The above convergence extends to

\psi\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{T}^{3}\right)
by an easy argument of density,
taking into account that the sequence
\left\|\varphi\left(f_{n}\left(\cdot\right)\right)\right\|_{\infty}
is bounded. Then we can take

\psi=\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\bm{1}_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}
and get

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\varphi\left(f_{n}\left(x\right)\right)dx=\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(y\right)\nu_{x}\left(dy\right)\right)dx.

In other words, if
\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}^{n}
is the local occupation measure
of
f_{n}
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(y\right)\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}^{n}\left(dy\right)=\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(y\right)\nu_{x}\left(dy\right)\right)dx.

More expressively, we may write

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{x_{0},\varepsilon}^{n}=\frac{1}{\left|B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)\right|}\int_{B\left(x_{0},\varepsilon\right)}\nu_{x}dx

in the weak sense of measures. This formula provides an interpretation for the
Young measure
\left(\nu_{x}\right)
of the sequence
\left(f_{n}\right)
:
its local average captures the oscillations of

f_{n}
in a statistical sense, in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In the homogeneous cases, when
\nu_{x}
is independent of
x
, the
interpretation is particularly strong.

B.1.
Large values

In the case of a scalar transport equation, the values of solutions for
positive time cannot exceed the values of the initial conditions.

In the case of a vector-advection equation, the stretching term may increase
the length of the vectors. The physical phenomenon of magnetic dynamo is an
example. The theory developed above may help to recognize the existence of
large values of the length of the vectors. Let us see a quantitative statement.

Assume we have

(B.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\varphi\left(B_{t}^{n}\left(x\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\left(b\right)\mu_{t,x}\left(db\right)\right)\psi\left(x\right)dx

for a sequence of vector fields
\left(B^{n}_{t}\right)
, where
\varphi,\psi,\mu_{t,x}
are as above. Denote by
\mathcal{L}_{3}
the Lebesgue measure
on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

Proposition B.1
.

Assume that there exists a ball
B\left(x_{0},r\right)\subset\mathbb{T}^{3}
and real numbers
R,\lambda>0
such that

\mu_{t,x}\left(B\left(0,R\right)^{c}\right)\geq\lambda\qquad\text{for
all }x\in B\left(x_{0},r\right).

Then, for all
\lambda^{\prime}\in\left(0,\lambda\right)
there exists

n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}

\mathcal{L}_{3}\left\{x\in B\left(x_{0},r\right):\left|B_{t}^{n}\left(x\right)\right|\geq R\right\}\geq\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{3}\left(B\left(x_{0},r\right)\right).

Proof.

For every
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{R}{2}\right)
, let
\varphi_{\delta}
be
a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, equal to 1 on
B\left(0,R-\delta\right)^{c}
, zero on
B\left(0,R-2\delta\right)
, with values in

\left[0,1\right]
in
B\left(0,R-\delta\right)\backslash B\left(0,R-2\delta\right)
; and similarly let
\psi_{\delta}
be a smooth function
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, equal to 1 on
B\left(x_{0},r+\delta\right)
, zero on

B\left(x_{0},r+2\delta\right)
, with values in
\left[0,1\right]
in

B\left(x_{0},r+2\delta\right)\backslash B\left(x_{0},r+\delta\right)
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{\delta}\left(b\right)\mu_{t,x}\left(db\right)\geq\lambda\qquad\text{for all }x\in B\left(x_{0},r\right)

and thus

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{\delta}\left(b\right)\nu_{t,x}\left(db\right)\right)\psi_{\delta}\left(x\right)dx\geq\lambda\mathcal{L}_{3}\left(B_{t}\left(x_{0},r\right)\right).

Therefore, given
\lambda^{\prime}\in\left(0,\lambda\right)
, there exists

n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\varphi_{\delta}\left(B_{t}^{n}\left(x\right)\right)\psi_{\delta}\left(x\right)dx\geq\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{3}\left(B\left(x_{0},r\right)\right).

We may easily deduce, by the arbitrariness of
\delta
, that

\int_{B\left(x_{0},r\right)}\bm{1}_{\left\{\left|B_{t}^{n}\left(x\right)\right|\geq R\right\}}dx\geq\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{3}\left(B\left(x_{0},r\right)\right).

This is the claimed formula.
∎

Due to
Theorem 2.10
, relation (
B.1
) is satisfied
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
up to passing to subsequences. To be able to use
B.1
to say something about large values attained by the stochastic PDE for
B^{n}_{t}
, we prove here also that for every
x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
such that
\overline{B}_{0}(x)\neq 0
, the limit measure
\mu(t,x,db)
has full support on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for every
t>0
. This fact does not follow trivially by a standard application of the Support Theorem because the diffusion matrix
\mathcal{A}(b)
introduced in the proof of
4.10
is not differentiable at
b=0
. To remedy this fact we ‘avoid’ the singularity by means of a localization procedure. We state the two main results we are going to use for the ease of the reader and refer to
[
24
, Theorem 3.5]
,
[
5
, Chapter 9.5]
for details.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be of class
C^{2}
with bounded first and second derivative. For
0\leq t\leq T
, let
X_{t}
be the solution of

dX_{t}=\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

then for every
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
, the support of the measure
P\circ X^{-1}
in
C^{\alpha}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the closure of the set
\left\{S(h),h\in\mathcal{H}\right\}
where
\mathcal{H}
is the Cameron-Martin space of
W
and
S(h)
is given by the solution of

(B.2)

S(h)_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(S(h)_{t})\cdot\dot{h}_{t}dt-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla(\sigma(S(h)_{t}))\sigma(S(h)_{t})dt

Proposition B.3
.

Let
\sigma_{i}(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\ i=1,2
be measurable functions and let
X^{i}
be the solutions of

dX^{i}_{t}=\sigma_{i}(X^{i}_{t})dW_{t}\qquad X^{i}_{0}=x_{0}.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set such that on
D
it holds
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
and

|\sigma_{i}(x)-\sigma_{i}(y)|\leq L|x-y|.

Then, if
\tau_{i}
denotes the exit time of
X_{i}
from
D
, we have

(B.3)

\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\text{ a.s. and }\ \mathbb{P}(X_{i}(t)=X_{2}(t),\text{ for every }0\leq t\leq\tau_{1})=1.

Recalling the notation introduced in the proof of
4.10
, with these tools we prove the following

Proposition B.4
.

Let
b_{t}
be the solution of

(B.4)

db_{t}=\mathcal{A}(b_{t})d\hat{W}_{t}

with
b(0)=b_{0}\neq 0
. Then for every
T>0
,
\varepsilon>0
,
b^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have

\mathbb{P}(|b_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon)>0

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
b^{*}\neq 0
(if it does, choose
|b^{\prime}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, then use
b^{\prime}
as target point). Fix
\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}(|b_{0}|\land|b^{*}|)
,
R\geq 2(|b_{0}|\lor|b^{*}|)
and call
D=B(0,R)\smallsetminus\overline{B(0,\delta)}
the open annulus of radii
\delta
and
R
. Set

\tau=\inf\{t>0:b_{t}\notin D\}
. Now consider a new diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{A}^{r}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{r}(b)=\mathcal{A}(b)
for every
b\in D
and
\mathcal{A}^{r}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Finally consider
b_{t}^{r}
the solution of
db_{t}^{r}=\mathcal{A}^{r}(b_{t})d\hat{W}_{t}
with
b_{0}^{r}=b_{0}
, and
\tau^{r}
its associated exit time from
D
. By
B.3
, we know that
\tau=\tau^{r}
a.s. and
b_{t}=b_{t}^{r}
a.s. for every
t\leq\tau
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|b_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(|b_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon,\ T\leq\tau,\ \sup_{t\leq T}|b_{t}-b_{t}^{r}|=0)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(|b^{r}_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon,\ T\leq\tau^{r}).

Let us now suppose that the line
y_{t}=\frac{t}{T}b^{*}+\frac{T-t}{T}b_{0}
lies inside
D^{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in D:|x|>\delta+\varepsilon\}
. In that case we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|b^{r}_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon,\ T\leq\tau^{r})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(|b^{r}_{T}-b^{*}|\leq\varepsilon,\ T\leq\tau^{r},\ \sup_{t\leq T}|b_{t}^{r}-y_{t}|\leq\varepsilon/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t\leq T}|b_{t}^{r}-y_{t}|\leq\varepsilon/2,\ T\leq\tau^{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t\leq T}|b_{t}^{r}-y_{t}|\leq\varepsilon/2).

Thus, we are left to show that the curve
y_{t}
is in the support of the law of
b^{r}
on
C^{0}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. But now we are in shape to apply
B.2
: we just need to check that there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
y_{t}
solves the control problem
B.2
. This is readily done, setting

(B.5)

h_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}^{-1}(y_{t})\left(\frac{b^{*}-b_{0}}{T}-(\nabla\mathcal{A}(y_{t}))\mathcal{A}(y_{t})\right)dt

Since
y_{t}
has support in
D
,
\mathcal{A}(y_{t})
is invertible with bounded inverse for every
t\leq T
. Also
\mathcal{A}
and
\nabla\mathcal{A}
are bounded, thus the integrand in is
L^{2}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

If instead
y_{t}
does not lie inside of
D
, we can find a middle point
b^{**}
such that the line
\tilde{y}_{t}
connecting
b_{0},b^{**}
and
b^{*}
in time
T
lies inside of
D^{\varepsilon}
and repeat the argument using
\tilde{y}_{t}
in place of
y_{t}
.
∎

To be able to apply
B.1
we need some uniformity of the previous statement with respect to the initial point
x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

Lemma B.5
.

Suppose that
B_{0}\in C(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
then for every
R>0
the map
\mathbb{T}^{3}\ni x\rightarrow\mu_{t,x}(B(0,R))
is continuous.

Proof.

Denote
b_{t}^{\nu}
the same process as above with initial condition
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since the matrix field
\mathcal{A}
is Lipschitz continuous and
B_{0}
is continuous by assumption,
b_{t}^{B_{0}(x_{n})}\rightarrow b_{t}^{B_{0}(x)}
a.s. if
x_{n}\rightarrow x
. The result follows then by dominated convergence recalling that

\mu_{t,x}(B(0,R))=\mathbb{E}[\bm{1}_{B(0,R)}(b_{t}^{B_{0}(x)})].

∎

Corollary B.6
.

If
\overline{B}_{0}\in V\cap C(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and is not identically null, the family of measures
\mu_{t,x}
satisfy the assumption of
B.1
for any value of
R>0
.

Proof.

By
B.4
, for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
such that
\overline{B}_{0}(x_{0})\neq 0
and
R>0
there exist
\lambda>0
such that
\mu_{t,x_{0}}(B(0,R)^{c})>\lambda
, then by continuity of this quantity with respect to
x
ensured by the
B.5
, there exists
r>0
such that
\mu_{t,x}(B(0,R)^{c})\geq\lambda/2
for every
x\in B(x_{0},r)
.
∎
