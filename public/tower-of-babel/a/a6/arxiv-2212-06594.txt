A Hausdorff-measure boundary element method for acoustic scattering by fractal screens
A. M. Caetano, S. N. Chandler-Wilde, A. Gibbs, D. P. Hewett, A. Moiola

Abstract
Sound-soft fractal screens can scatter acoustic waves even when they have zero surface measure. To solve such scattering problems we make what appears to be the first application of the boundary element method (BEM) where each BEM basis function is supported in a fractal set, and the integration involved in the formation of the BEM matrix is with respect to a non-integer order Hausdorff measure rather than the usual (Lebesgue) surface measure. Using recent results on function spaces on fractals, we prove convergence of the Galerkin formulation of this “Hausdorff BEM” for acoustic scattering in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} ( n = 1 , 2 n=1,2 ) when the scatterer, assumed to be a compact subset of ℝ n × { 0 } \mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\} , is a d d -set for some d ∈ ( n − 1 , n ] d\in(n-1,n] , so that, in particular, the scatterer has Hausdorff dimension d d . For a class of fractals that are attractors of iterated function systems, we prove convergence rates for the Hausdorff BEM and superconvergence for smooth antilinear functionals, under certain natural regularity assumptions on the solution of the underlying boundary integral equation. We also propose numerical quadrature routines for the implementation of our Hausdorff BEM, along with a fully discrete convergence analysis, via numerical (Hausdorff measure) integration estimates and inverse estimates on fractals, estimating the discrete condition numbers. Finally, we show numerical experiments that support the sharpness of our theoretical results, and our solution regularity assumptions, including results for scattering in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} by Cantor sets, and in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} by Cantor dusts.

Hausdorff measure and dimension

For

As in

\displaystyle c_{1}r^{d}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}\big(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x)\big)\leq c_{2}r^{d},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\Gamma,\hskip 10.00002pt0<r\leq 1,

Except where indicated explicitly otherwise, results through the rest of the paper hold for all

Corollary 5.5

Under the assumptions of Corollary

\displaystyle\inf_{\psi_{h}\in Y_{h}}\|\psi-\psi_{h}\|_{H^{s_{1}}_{\Gamma}}\leq ch^{s_{2}-s_{1}}\|\psi\|_{H^{s_{2}}_{\Gamma}},\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in H^{s_{2}}_{\Gamma},

\displaystyle|J(\psi_{N})-J^{Q}(\psi_{N})|

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\vec{\psi}})_{j}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}^{-1/2}\bigg(\int_{\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)-\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in L_{h_{Q}}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}}\mu_{\boldsymbol{n}}V(x_{\boldsymbol{n}})\bigg)\Bigg|

\displaystyle h_{Q}^{2}|V|_{2,\mathrm{Hull}(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma))^{1/2}\|{\vec{\psi}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle c_{t}h_{Q}^{2}h^{-t}|V|_{2,\mathrm{Hull}(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma))^{1/2}\|\psi_{N}\|_{H_{\Gamma}^{-t-\frac{n-d}{2}}}.

Remark 5.15

(Value of
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)
)
.

The proposed quadrature formulas require the values of
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}=\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}})
, which are easily computed in terms of
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)
as
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}=\rho_{m_{1}}^{d}\cdots\rho_{m_{\ell}}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)
, for all
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in I_{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
n=1
, the Hausdorff measure of a class of Cantor sets is shown to be
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)=1
in
[
27
, Thm. 1.14–1.15]
; see, e.g.,
[
54
]
for more recent related results.
However, for
n>1
the exact value of the Hausdorff measure of even the simplest IFS attractors is known
only for
d\leq 1
(see, e.g.,
[
53
]
), i.e. for the cases that are not relevant for scattering problems (recall, as discussed above Lemma
4.4
, that
H^{-1/2}_{\Gamma}=\{0\}
and
\phi=0
for
d\leq n-1
).
A simple implementation technique which avoids working with an unknown value for
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)
and produces the correct solution
\phi_{N}
is just to set
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)=1
in all calculations. This is equivalent to
introducing a “normalised Hausdorff measure”
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\star}(\cdot):=\mathcal{H}^{d}(\cdot)/\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)
, so that
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{\star}(\Gamma)=1
, and using it throughout in the BEM in place of
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\cdot)
.

Remark 5.19

(Reduced quadrature)
.

The quadrature rule defined by (
92
) uses the same maximum mesh width
h_{Q}
for all off-diagonal elements
A_{ij}
,
i\neq j
. Since the magnitude of the integrand
\Phi(x,y)
and its derivatives blows up as one approaches the diagonal
x=y
, and decays away from it, it is possible to save computational effort, while maintaining the error bounds (
102
) and (
103
),
and hence the error estimates (
110
) and (
116
).
To achieve this we increase
the quadrature mesh width (and hence potentially decrease the number of quadrature points)
for the computation of
A_{ij}
when the elements
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)}
and
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}
are sufficiently well-separated spatially.
In more detail, from
[
30
, Prop 5.2]
we have that, for
i\neq j
, the error in the quadrature approximation (
92
), with
h_{Q}
replaced by a local quadrature mesh width
h_{Q,i,j}
, satisfies

|A_{ij}-A_{ij}^{Q}|\leq C\mu_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)}\mu_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}h_{Q,i,j}^{2}\Upsilon(R_{ij}),

for some constant
C
independent of
i
and
j
, where

\Upsilon(R):=\frac{1+(kR)^{n/2+1}}{R^{n+1}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptR_{ij}:=\dist(\mathrm{Hull}(\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)}),\mathrm{Hull}(\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)})).

Given
h_{Q}
, one can therefore maintain the bounds (
102
) and (
103
) by replacing
h_{Q}
in (
92
) by

h_{Q,i,j}=h_{Q}\left(\frac{\max_{p\neq q}\Upsilon(R_{pq})}{\Upsilon(R_{ij})}\right)^{1/2}\geq h_{Q}.

If the quantities
R_{ij}
are not known exactly, but satisfy

\displaystyle R^{-}_{ij}\leq R_{ij}\leq R^{+}_{ij},\hskip 20.00003pti\neq j,

(120)

for some known quantities
R^{-}_{ij}\geq 0
and
R^{+}_{ij}<\infty
, then one can maintain (
102
) and (
103
) by replacing
h_{Q}
in (
92
) by

\displaystyle h_{Q,i,j}=h_{Q}\max\left(\left(\frac{\max_{p\neq q}\Upsilon(R^{+}_{pq})}{\Upsilon(R^{-}_{ij})}\right)^{1/2},1\right)\geq h_{Q}.

(121)

Here we are using the fact that
\Upsilon(R)
is a monotonically decreasing function of
R
, and the
\max(\cdot,1)
is needed to ensure that
h_{Q,i,j}\geq h_{Q}
(so that we are not increasing the computational effort unnecessarily) because the quantity inside the square root may be smaller than 1. (This holds in particular if
R^{-}_{ij}=0
, in which case we are interpreting
1/\Upsilon(R^{-}_{ij})
as
1/\Upsilon(0)=1/\infty=0
.)

In our numerical results in §
6
we will compute
A_{ij}^{Q}
as described above, choosing
h_{Q,i,j}
according to (
121
) with

\displaystyle R^{-}_{ij}:=\max(\dist(x_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)},x_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)})-\diam(\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)})-\diam(\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}),0)

(122)

and

\displaystyle R^{+}_{ij}:=\dist(x_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(i)},x_{{\boldsymbol{m}}(j)}),

(123)

which satisfy (
120
).

6
Numerical results

In this section we present numerical results that illustrate our methods and assess the sharpness of our theoretical predictions.
The Hausdorff BEM (
55
) has
been implemented in the Julia language
[
5
]
and the code is available at
https://github.com/AndrewGibbs/IFSintegrals
.
In all examples described in this section, we consider the scattering of plane waves, i.e. the datum
g
is as in (
29
) with
u^{i}(x)=e^{{\mathrm{i}}k\vartheta\cdot x}
and
|\vartheta|=1
.
We validate our implementation against a different method in §
6.4
, but in the rest of our experiments we use as a reference solution a more accurate Hausdorff-BEM solution with a large number of degrees of freedom
N_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Most of our experiments are for homogeneous attractors, in which case
N_{\mathrm{ref}}=M^{\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}}
for some
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathbb{N}
. For Cantor sets
(M=2)
we choose
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}=15
, so that
N_{\mathrm{ref}}=32768
. For Cantor Dusts (
M=4
) we choose
\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}=8
, so that
N_{\mathrm{ref}}=65536
.
Since we do not use any matrix compression, the memory required to store the Galerkin matrices grows like
N^{2}
, with
N=M^{\ell}
when the attractor is homogeneous. Thus, while many of our experiments were run on a standard laptop, some (in particular, the calculation of reference solutions)
required the use of the Myriad High Performance Computing Facility available at University College London, which has computing nodes available with 1.5TB of RAM.

Our implementation uses the quadrature rules described in §
5.4
. Precisely, we approximate the right-hand side in the linear system and linear functionals of the solution (the scattered field and far field) using the quadrature rules (
97
) and (
98
), respectively. We approximate the diagonal matrix elements
A_{ii}
by (
93
)-(
96
). To approximate

Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper we presented and analysed a piecewise-constant Galerkin BEM for acoustic scattering in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
n=1,2
, by a sound-soft planar screen
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
. Our BEM is defined whenever the screen
\Gamma
is a compact
d
-set, for some
n-1<d\leq n
, which includes cases where
\Gamma
is fractal. It is based on an integral equation formulation in which integration is carried out with respect to Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{d}
. For any compact
d
-set we proved that the method converges as the mesh width
h
tends to zero (see Theorem
5.1
).
Regarding the relationship between mesh width and wavelength, in our numerical results presented in §
6
we observe the same behaviour one obtains for a conventional BEM on a smooth scatterer: as
h
decreases towards zero there is a pre-asymptotic phase until
h
reaches the wavelength scale, beyond which one observes the predicted asymptotic behaviour.
In the case where
\Gamma
is the disjoint attractor of an IFS satisfying the OSC, the “elements” in the BEM are self-similar subsets of
\Gamma
, and in §
5.4
we showed how the Galerkin integrals can be evaluated using quadrature rules from
[
30
]
. In this case we also proved fully-discrete convergence rates, under certain regularity assumptions on the integral equation solution (see Theorem
5.6
, Corollaries
5.17
and
5.18
).
Specifically, we showed that the BEM solution converges like
O(h^{s+1/2})
as
h\to 0
, and the near- and far-field solutions like
O(h^{2s+1})
, assuming
\phi\in H^{s}_{\Gamma}
for some
s>-1/2
. We proved the existence of such an
s
in Remark
5.7
, using Proposition
4.9
, but our numerical results in §
6
suggest that, for sufficiently smooth data, it may hold that
\phi\in H^{s}_{\Gamma}
for all
s<-(n-d)/2
, i.e. for all
s
such that the space
H^{s}_{\Gamma}
is non-trivial.
Guided by these observations, we formulated Conjecture
4.8
, which is a statement about the range of Sobolev spaces on which the Hausdorff-measure integral operator
\mathbb{S}
(defined in (
15
)) is invertible.

Proving or disproving Conjecture
4.8
is the main outstanding theoretical question relating to the paper. Other avenues for future research include:

•

The extension of our analysis to non-disjoint fractal screens, such as the Sierpinski triangle screen considered in §
6.5
. Singular quadrature rules for such cases (generalising those described in §
5.4
) have been presented recently in
[
29
]
, but extending our BEM convergence analysis will require a suitable generalisation of the wavelet approximation theory of
[
36
]
, which is yet to be worked out.

•

The extension of our convergence rate analysis to the case
d=n
. This requires somewhat different techniques to the case
d<n
,
and will be presented in a separate article
[
11
]
.

•

The generalisation of our Hausdorff BEM to scattering by non-planar fractal structures. Results in this direction were presented recently in
[
10
]
.

•

The extension to Neumann problems. Theoretical results relating to integral equation formulations of Neumann screen problems were presented in
[
15
]
. These show that the Neumann boundary condition is “weaker” than the Dirichlet condition, in the sense that Neumann screens do not scatter waves unless
H^{1/2}_{\Gamma}\neq\{0\}
, which requires in particular that
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
has positive
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Hence, in the context of scattering by screens that are attractors of IFS satisfying the OSC, only the case
d=n
is relevant. A specific example would be scattering by a sound-hard Koch snowflake screen. However, the development of a BEM for such Neumann problems using a mesh of fractal elements (as we consider in the current paper for the Dirichlet case) is complicated by the fact that non-trivial piecewise polynomials on such meshes cannot be continuous, and hence cannot be
H^{1/2}_{\Gamma}
-conforming. Thus a different approach is required, perhaps involving a discontinuous Galerkin discretization.
We remark that a rigorous convergence analysis for a prefractal BEM approach to the related impedance problem was presented in
[
3
]
.

•

A more detailed investigation into the relative accuracy of our Hausdorff BEM and the alternative prefractal BEM of
[
19
]
, extending the preliminary analysis of §
6.4
. For many problems the Hausdorff BEM appears to be more accurate than the prefractal BEM. However, the comparison between the two is rather subtle: for the examples we considered in §
6.4
, while both methods appear to converge at the same rate, for the same number of degrees of freedom the Hausdorff BEM can be over 1000 times more accurate than the prefractal BEM, or 100 times less accurate, depending on the fractal simulated. As yet we do not understand why.

•

The combination of our Hausdorff BEM with accelerated linear algebra via a technique such as
\mathcal{H}
-matrix compression or the fast multipole method. This would allow the simulation of larger problems with finer mesh widths, permitting the calculation of higher accuracy solutions and/or the study of higher frequency problems.

u_{h}:=\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma}^{*}\psi_{h}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in L_{h}}a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma}^{*}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptv_{h}:=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in L_{h}}a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}.

Then, since the supports of the
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}
are disjoint and again noting (
13
), we have, using (
135
) and (
134
), that

\langle v_{h},u_{h}\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=c_{0}^{1/2}\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in L_{h}}|a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|^{2}\rho_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}^{d/2}\geq c_{0}^{1/2}\,(\rho_{\min}h/h_{0})^{d/2}\,\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

(136)

For
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in L_{h}
, using standard properties of Fourier transforms (e.g.,
[
31
, Prop. 2.2.11]
),

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(1+|\xi|^{2}\right)^{j}\left|\widehat{\sigma}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}\xi)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(1+|\xi/\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}|^{2}\right)^{j}\left|\widehat{\sigma}(\xi)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}^{n-2j}\|\sigma\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

since
\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq 1
,
so, using that
\rho_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\in(\rho_{\min}h/h_{0},h/h_{0}]
by (
134
), and again that the supports of the
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}
are disjoint,

\|v_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in L_{h}}|a_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}|^{2}\|\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\sigma\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(\rho_{\min}h/h_{0})^{n-2j}\,\|a\|_{2}^{2},&\mbox{if }j\geq n/2,\\
\|\sigma\|^{2}_{H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(h/h_{0})^{n-2j}\,\|a\|_{2}^{2},&\mbox{if }j<n/2.\end{array}\right.

In particular this applies for
j=0,L
, where
L:=\lceil\max(J+(n-d)/2,n/2)\rceil
, so that
L\geq\max(s,n/2)
. Hence, and applying Hölder’s inequality to the definition of the
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm and noting that
\|\sigma\|_{H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|\sigma\|_{H^{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
,

\|v_{h}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|v_{h}\|^{1-s/L}_{H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|v_{h}\|^{s/L}_{H^{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|\sigma\|_{H^{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\rho_{\min}^{(n/(2L)-1)s}\,(h/h_{0})^{n/2-s}\,\|a\|_{2}.

(137)

Now, recalling from §
2.4
that
\mathrm{tr}_{\Gamma}^{*}:\mathbb{H}^{-t}(\Gamma)\to H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
has unit norm,

\|\psi_{h}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{-t}(\Gamma)}\geq\|u_{h}\|_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\sup_{v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|\langle v,u_{h}\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|}{\|v\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\geq\frac{|\langle v_{h},u_{h}\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|}{\|v_{h}\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}.

Combining this inequality with (
136
) and (
137
) and recalling (
9
) we obtain that

\|\psi_{h}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{-t}(\Gamma)}\geq\frac{c_{0}^{1/2}}{\|\sigma\|_{H^{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\,\rho^{t+n/2}_{\min}\,(h/h_{0})^{t}\,\|a\|_{2},

and the result follows on recalling that
\|a\|_{2}=\|\psi_{h}\|_{\mathbb{L}_{2}(\Gamma)}
.
∎
