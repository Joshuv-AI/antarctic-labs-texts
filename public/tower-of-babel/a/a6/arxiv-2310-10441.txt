Efficiently matching random inhomogeneous graphs via degree profiles
Jian Ding, Yumou Fei, Yuanzheng Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of recovering the latent vertex correspondence between two correlated random graphs with vastly inhomogeneous and unknown edge probabilities between different pairs of vertices. Inspired by and extending the matching algorithm via degree profiles by Ding, Ma, Wu and Xu (2021), we obtain an efficient matching algorithm as long as the minimal average degree is at least Ω ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log^{2}n) and the minimal correlation is at least 1 − O ⁡ ( log − 2 ⁡ n ) 1-O(\log^{-2}n) .

Introduction

In this paper we study the problem of recovering the latent matching between two correlated random graphs. A key novelty in our work, as illustrated in the following definition, is that we hugely relax the model assumptions in the correlated Erdös–Rényi graph model.

Definition 1.1
.

A pair of random graphs
(G,H)
on

(\alpha,d,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution if the following hold:

•

Consider

and

(G_{ij},H_{ij})

1\leq i\leq j\leq n

•

\PP[G_{ij}=1]=\PP[H_{ij}=1]=p_{ij},\text{ for }1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

•

There are fixed parameters
\delta_{ij}\in[0,\delta]
for
i,j\in[n]

\delta_{ij}=\delta_{ji}

\delta_{ii}=0

\PP[G_{ij}=1,\,H_{ij}=0]=\PP[G_{ij}=0,\,H_{ij}=1]=p_{ij}\delta_{ij},\text{ for }1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

For each

Similar to the random graph matching problem, for many network recovery problems usually the first and the most extensively studied models are of Erdös-Rényi type; prominent examples include the stochastic block model and the planted clique problem for community recovery. That being said, researchers do realize and keep in mind that almost all the realistic networks, either from a scientific domain or from a real-world application, are drastically different from the Erdös-Rényi model. Nevertheless, it is a natural hope that a sound theory developed on the Erdös-Rényi model will eventually be further developed to incorporate more realistic networks. To this end, there are roughly speaking two directions for pursuit: (1) propose and study random graph models that better capture some key features for (at least some) realistic networks; (2) develop

Let us also remark that the extension to inhomogeneous networks (from Erdös-Rényi) is usually considered of practical value since many realistic networks exhibit the feature of inhomogeneity, a well-known example being the observed power law degree distribution for social networks. Choice of balls.
The “balls” used in
[
19
]
are standardized “outdegrees” of neighbors. The authors made this choice to reduce dependency and thus to facilitate the analysis. It is reasonable to speculate that using the usual vertex degrees of neighbors would be sufficient for signatures, as empirically verified by the numerical experiments of
[
19
]
in the Erdös-Rényi case. In contrast to the theoretic result in
[
19
]
but in alignment with its numerical result, our algorithm directly utilizes the degrees of neighbors for the “balls”, with the exception that we employ a different choice of bins (as described later in this subsection).

Furthermore, by eliminating the standardization step, our algorithm no longer requires prior knowledge on the edge probabilities. In the absence of standardization, particularly in the inhomogeneous setting, the degrees of the vertices can vary drastically. Nevertheless, we do know that each vertex’s degree is a nonnegative random variable with a standard deviation proportional to the square root of its mean, due to the assumed upper bound
p_{ij}\leq 1-\alpha
. This leads to the following observation on a “partial” standardization: taking the square root of a vertex’s degree results in a variable with still an arbitrary mean but a standard deviation of constant order. Out of this consideration, we choose the “balls” for the signature of
i
to be the square root of the degrees of neighbors of
i
.

Choice of bins.
In the paper
[
19
]
, the authors defined the bins
I_{1},\dots,I_{L}
as the partition of
[-1/2,1/2]
into equal-length intervals, where
L
is a sufficiently large multiple of
\log n
. This choice aligns with their use of standardized variables with mean 0 and variance 1 as “balls.” In our scenario, the “balls” are also of constant-order variances but may have arbitrary means. Conceivably, the natural choice for bins would be a partition of the entire real line into intervals of length
1/L
. In
Appendix

C
, we will demonstrate that this selection of bins works well for matching Gaussian matrices, which is a simplified model for graph matching. However, due to certain technical limitations of our analysis method (as explained below), we will make an artificial choice (see
Section

2.1
) for the bins in our main theorem on graph matching. Specifically, we define the bins as unions of intervals and consider a continuum family of bins. While we take unions solely for theoretical purposes, having a continuum family of bins does seem to boost the practical performance of the algorithm on specific inhomogeneous models, as suggested by our numerical experiments in
Appendix

D
(see
Figure

3
for a particularly evident demonstration).

The Fubini-Tonelli theorem.
An important part of the analysis is to upper-bound the probability of a correlated pair of “balls” landing in different bins. When a discrete set of bins is employed, this probability estimation poses a technical challenge. In the Gaussian model, as demonstrated in
Lemma

C.8
, the task is manageable. The complexity increases significantly in the Erdös-Rényi model, as indicated by
[
19
, Lemma 9]
. In our setting, which involves inhomogeneity and additional dependence arising from our choice of balls, this task would arguably be even more intricate. It is exactly for the purpose of addressing this challenge that we have chosen a
continuum
family of bins; this then allows us to leverage the Fubini-Tonelli theorem (specifically, in (
4.10
)), which considerably simplifies this part of analysis (even in the original setting of
[
19
]
).

Dealing with dependence.
In line with the approach taken in
[
19
]
, we use
L^{2}
-distances to upper-bound the
L^{1}
-distances of correlated pairs of signatures. This requires the bins to be indexed by a set with bounded measure (see (
4.7
)) and forces us to take unions of intervals as bins. We will show in
Section

4.6
, which we view as one of the main technical contributions of this paper, that the dependence arising from our choice of balls (and our new choice of conditioning, as explained below) can be effectively addressed by transitioning to
L^{2}
-distances.

Conditioning.
In comparison to
[
19
]
, our analysis places a greater emphasis on the analytical tool of conditioning, bringing it to the forefront. As explained in
Remark

3
and
Remark

4
, the conditioning employed in our analysis is carefully designed to address the additional intricacies inherent in our setting. In particular, we want to condition on less information to preserve the symmetry between
G
and
H
. A consequence of this approach is that a greater amount of dependence between the “balls” will remain after the conditioning process, and this requires a somewhat more complicated analysis for concentration, as a price to pay for easier control on the expectation (from the symmetry).

For each

(2.2)

Remark 2

We may identify the range

(2.3)

B_{ij}(t)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{Y_{j}}\in I_{t}\right\}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{H_{ij}=1\}.

(2.4)

Now we can define the distance function

D(i,k)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{kj}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2.5)

Fix an

By definitions in (

For any

,

\PP\left[D(i,i)\geq D(i,k)\right]\leq O(n^{-3}).

As explained at the beginning of
Section

2.4
, we assume that the underlying permutation
\pi=\mathrm{id}
. Note that we have

\PP\left[\mathcal{A}(G,H,c_{3}\log n)\neq\mathrm{id}\right]\leq\PP\left[\exists i,k\in[n]\text{ s.t. }i\neq k\text{ and }D(i,i)\geq D(i,k)\right]\leq O(n^{-1}),

by a union bound over all pairs
i,k
. This finishes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

3
Fake Pairs

This section is devoted to proving
Lemma

2.3
. Our strategy for proving
Lemma

2.3
is to do two rounds of conditioning on the original randomness for
(G,H)
before carrying out the main part of the analysis. We will define the conditionings in
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
and then analyze the remaining randomness in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
.

In the following, let
(G,H)
be a pair of random graphs that follows an
(\alpha,d,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution, and we fix vertices
i\neq k
. Without loss of generality, we assume
d_{i}\geq d_{k}
.

3.1
First Round of Conditioning

Definition 3.1

(Variables)
.

For each
j\in[n]
, define the random variable

R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}=\sum_{\ell\not\in\{i,k\}}p_{j\ell}(1-G_{i\ell}\vee H_{k\ell}).

Also define the random variables

R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}=\sum_{j\neq k}G_{ij}(1-H_{kj})\text{ and }R^{\mathsf{f}}=2+\sum_{j\not\in\{i,k\}}G_{ij}\vee H_{kj}.

Definition 3.2

(Events)
.

Let
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}
be the event

\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\left\{R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}d_{j}\right\}\cap\left\{R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{i}\right\}\cap\left\{R^{\mathsf{f}}\leq 3d_{i}\right\}.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
d\gtrsim\log^{2}n
, then
\PP(\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.

Proof.

Note that the
2(n-2)
random variables
G_{i\ell},H_{k\ell}
for
\ell\not\in\{i,k\}
are mutually independent. So

\E[R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}]\geq\sum_{\ell\not\in\{i,k\}}p_{j\ell}\cdot\alpha^{2}\geq\alpha^{2}(d_{j}-2).

By Hoeffding’s inequality (
Theorem

A.1
) and
p_{j\ell}\leq 1
for all
j,\ell
,

\PP\left[R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}d_{j}\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\left(\alpha^{2}(d_{j}-2)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}d_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{\ell\not\in\{i,k\}}p_{j\ell}^{2}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{4}}{2}(d_{j}-8)\right).

For
R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}
and
R^{\mathsf{f}}
, we have by independence

\E[R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}]=\sum_{j\neq k}p_{ij}(1-p_{kj})\geq\alpha(d_{i}-1)\text{ and }\E[R^{\mathsf{f}}]\leq d_{i}+d_{k}+2\leq 2d_{i}+2.

Then we have by Chernoff bounds (
Corollary

A.3
) that

\PP\left[R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{i}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha(d_{i}/2-2)}{4}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{8}(d_{i}-4)\right),

and that

\PP[R^{\mathsf{f}}\geq 3d_{i}]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{6}(d_{i}-5)\right).

It now follows from a union bound that
\PP(\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.
∎

Definition 3.4

(Conditioning)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the variables
G_{ij}
and
H_{kj}
for all
j\in[n]
.

Clearly,
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}
is
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
-measurable. Throughout the remaining part of
Section

3
, we will always condition on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
and assume that
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}
holds.

3.2
Second Round of Conditioning

Definition 3.5

(Sets)
.

Define the sets

J^{\mathsf{f}}=\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}:G_{ij}\vee H_{kj}=1\}

and

J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}=\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}:G_{ij}=1,H_{kj}=0\}\subset J^{\mathsf{f}}.

Note that under the conditioning of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
, the sets
J^{\mathsf{f}}
and
J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
are deterministic.

Definition 3.6

(Variables)
.

For
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
, define the random variable

S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}=\sum_{\ell\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}}G_{j\ell}.

Definition 3.7

(Events)
.

Let
\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
be the event

\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}:=\bigcap_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}\{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\leq 2d_{j}\}.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
d\gtrsim\log^{2}n
, then
\PP(\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.

Proof.

By definition of
S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}
, we have

\E[S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}]\leq 2+\sum_{\ell\in J^{\mathsf{f}}}p_{j\ell}\leq d_{j}+2.

Since
G_{j\ell}
(for
\ell\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}
) are still independent variables after conditioning on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
, by Chernoff bounds (
Corollary

A.3
) we have

\PP[S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\geq 2d_{j}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{d_{j}-6}{3}\right).

It follows from a union bound that
\PP(\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.
∎

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{f}}
and the variables
G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell}
for
j,\ell\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}
.

Clearly,
\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
is
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
-measurable. Throughout the rest of
Section

3
, we will always condition on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
and assume that
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
holds.

3.3
Concentration of Distance

One important way in which the conditional distribution of
D(i,k)
given
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
behaves nicer than the original distribution of
D(i,k)
is that its concentration is more directly analyzable after the conditioning. In particular, the randomness in
D(i,k)
now naturally decomposes into independent parts, lending itself to the powerful tool of McDiarmid’s inequality.

Lemma 3.10

(Concentration)
.

On
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
, we have

\PP\left[D(i,k)\leq\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]-\frac{100\sqrt{d_{i}\log n}}{L}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\leq O(n^{-3}).

Proof.

After both rounds of conditioning, the distance
D(i,k)
is now a function of the vectors
(X_{j},Y_{j})
, for
j\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}
. In addition, these vectors are now independent of each other, as
(X_{j},Y_{j})
now only depends on
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
for
\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}
.

Now, fix some
j_{0}\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}
and consider the marginal difference the vector
(X_{j_{0}},Y_{j_{0}})
can cause on the value of
D(i,k)
when the values of all other vectors are fixed. As defined in (
2.5
), the value of
D(i,k)
is determined by the
2n
functions
A_{ir}(\cdot)
and
B_{kr}(\cdot)
, for
r\in[n]
. Keeping
(X_{j},Y_{j})
fixed for all
j\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}\setminus\{j_{0}\}
, a change in the vector
(X_{j_{0}},Y_{j_{0}})
will only change two of the
2n
functions:
A_{ij_{0}}(t)
and
B_{kj_{0}}(t)
. From
Proposition

2.1
we know that
A_{ij_{0}}(\cdot)
and
B_{kj_{0}}(\cdot)
are always indicator functions with supports having Lebesgue measure no more than
2/L
. So using the superscript
\mathsf{new}
to denote the values after the change in the vector
(X_{j_{0}},Y_{j_{0}})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|D(i,k)^{\mathsf{new}}-D(i,k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\left|A_{ir}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{ir}(t)\right|+\left|B_{kr}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{kr}(t)\right|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{ij_{0}}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{ij_{0}}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{kj_{0}}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{kj_{0}}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}=\frac{8}{L}.

At any
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}})
, we can now conclude by McDiarmid’s inequality (
Theorem

A.4
) that with conditional probability (on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
) at least
1-O\left(n^{-3}\right)
,

D(i,k)\geq\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3\log n\cdot|J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}|\cdot\left(\frac{8}{L}\right)^{2}}\geq\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]-\frac{100\sqrt{d_{i}\log n}}{L}.

In the last inequality we used
|J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}|\leq R^{\mathsf{f}}\leq 3d_{i}
, as guaranteed by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}
.
∎

3.4
Expectation of Distance

Having
Lemma

3.10
at our disposal, the next logical step towards a proof of
Lemma

2.3
is to give a lower bound on the conditional expectation
\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]
evaluated on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
. This is what we will achieve in this subsection.

Recall that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
, the set
J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
is deterministic. Fix
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
. On the one hand, we get from the definition of
J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
, almost surely
Y_{j}=0
. On the other hand,
X_{j}
can now be written as

X_{j}=S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}}G_{j\ell}.

Conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
, the first part
S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}
is a deterministic quantity, while the second part is a sum of independent Bernoulli variables. We have

\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]=S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}

and

\mathrm{Var}[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]=\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{f}}\cup\{i,k\}}p_{j\ell}(1-p_{j\ell})\in[\alpha R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}},R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}].

In addition, recall that on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
we have
S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\leq 2d_{j}
and
\alpha^{2}d_{j}/2\leq R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}\leq d_{j}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Fix some
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}})
of the underlying probability space.
Fix a vertex
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
. Let
a,b
be positive real numbers such that
b-a=2/L
and

\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}-2\leq a<b\leq\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}.

Let

\gamma_{j}:=\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\alpha e^{-100/\alpha^{2}}}{L}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}\alpha^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{d_{j}}}.

(3.1)

Then we have at
\omega

\PP\left[a\leq\sqrt{X_{j}}\leq b\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\geq\gamma_{j}.

Proof.

Let
X^{\prime}
be a normal random variable with mean
S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}=\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]
and variance
\mathrm{Var}[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]
. Then

\displaystyle\left|a^{2}-(S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|a-\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}\right|\cdot\left|a+\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 2\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{3d_{j}}.

Similarly
|b^{2}-(S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}})|\leq 4\sqrt{3d_{j}}
. So the density function of
X^{\prime}
on the interval
[a^{2},b^{2}]
is no less than

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{Var}[X^{\prime}]}}\exp\left(-\frac{(4\sqrt{3d_{j}})^{2}}{2\mathrm{Var}[X^{\prime}]}\right)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi d_{j}}}e^{-100/\alpha^{3}}.

Since
b^{2}-a^{2}\geq(b-a)\left(2\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}-4\right)\geq 2\alpha\sqrt{d_{j}}/L
, it follows that

\PP[a^{2}\leq X^{\prime}\leq b^{2}]\geq\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\alpha e^{-100/\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{L}.

By Berry-Esseen theorem (
Corollary

A.6
), we have at
\omega

\PP[a^{2}\leq X_{j}\leq b^{2}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]\geq\PP[a^{2}\leq X^{\prime}\leq b^{2}]-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\alpha e^{-100/\alpha^{2}}}{L}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}\alpha^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{d_{j}}}=\gamma_{j}.\qed

Corollary 3.12
.

Assume that
L\geq 5
. Fix a realization
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}})
, and fix a vertex
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
. Let
\gamma_{j}
be as in (
3.1
). Then for each
t\in[0,1)
, we have at
\omega

\gamma_{j}\leq\PP[A_{ij}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]\leq 1-\gamma_{j}.

(3.2)

Proof.

For each
t\in[0,1)
, there is some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}-\frac{3}{2}\leq t+m\leq\sqrt{S_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}+R_{j}^{\mathsf{f}}}-\frac{1}{2}.

So applying
Lemma

3.11
with
a=t+m-1/L
and
b=t+m+1/L
, we have

\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t,m}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\geq\gamma_{j}.

(3.3)

Now, since
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
, it is guaranteed that
G_{ij}=1
, and hence
A_{ij}(t)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t}\right\}
. So

\PP[A_{ij}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]=\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\geq\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t,m}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right],

and the lower bound in (
3.2
) follows by combining the above with (
3.3
).

For the upper bound, let
t^{\prime}\in[0,1)
be such that
|t^{\prime}-t|=\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\frac{1}{L}<\frac{1}{4}
, we have
I_{t}\cap I_{t^{\prime}}=\emptyset
. So the events
\left\{\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t}\right\}
and
\left\{\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t^{\prime}}\right\}
are mutually exclusive, and thus

\PP[A_{ij}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]=\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\leq 1-\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}}\in I_{t^{\prime}}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]=1-\PP[A_{ij}(t^{\prime})=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}].

The upper bound in (
3.2
) then follows by applying the lower bound to
t^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.13

(Expectation)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{3}
such that setting
L=c_{3}\log n
and
d=c_{1}\log^{2}n
, on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
we have

\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]\geq\frac{300\sqrt{d_{i}}}{c_{3}\sqrt{\log n}}.

Proof.

For
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
, let
\gamma_{j}
be as in (
3.1
). Since
L=c_{3}\log n
and
d_{j}\geq d=c_{1}\log^{2}n
for each
j
, by taking a sufficiently large
c_{3}
and then a sufficiently large
c_{1}
, we can ensure that

\min_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}\gamma_{j}\geq\frac{10^{7}}{\alpha c_{3}^{2}\log n}.

In addition, since on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}
we have
|J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}|=R_{0}^{\mathsf{f}}\geq\alpha d_{i}/2\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha c_{1}\log^{2}n
, we can also ensure that
\min_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}\gamma_{j}\geq|J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}|^{-1}
.
Note that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}
, the random vectors
(A_{ij},B_{kj})
(for
j\in[n]
) are mutually independent.
Combining
Corollary

3.12
with
Corollary

B.5
, on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
we have

\E\left[\left|\sum_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}A_{ij}(t)+\sum_{j\not\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}(A_{ij}(t)-B_{kj}(t))\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}\gamma_{j}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha d_{i}}{2}\cdot\frac{10^{7}}{\alpha c_{3}^{2}\log n}}\geq\frac{300\sqrt{d_{i}}}{c_{3}\sqrt{\log n}}.

for each
t\in[0,1)
. By definition of

J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
, for each
j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}
we must have
B_{kj}(t)=0
for all
t
. So on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
we have

\E[D(i,k)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}]=\int_{0}^{1}\E\left[\left|\sum_{j\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}A_{ij}(t)+\sum_{j\not\in J_{1}^{\mathsf{f}}}(A_{ij}(t)-B_{kj}(t))\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{300\sqrt{d_{i}}}{c_{3}\sqrt{\log n}}.\qed

3.5
Proof of
Lemma

2.3

We now combine the concentration results of
Section

3.3
and the expectation results of
Section

3.4
to prove the deviation probability stated in
Lemma

2.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

2.3
.

First pick
c_{1}
and
c_{3}
according to
Lemma

3.13
. Then
Lemmas

3.10
and
3.13
together imply that on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}}
,

\PP\left[\frac{D(i,k)}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\leq\frac{200}{c_{3}\sqrt{\log n}}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{f}}\right]\leq O(n^{-3}).

Finally,
Propositions

3.3
and
3.8
imply that
\PP(\Gamma^{\mathsf{f}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{f}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.
Therefore

\PP\left[\frac{D(i,k)}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\leq\frac{200}{c_{3}\sqrt{\log n}}\right]\leq O(n^{-3})+\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))=O(n^{-3}).\qed

4
True Pairs

This section is devoted to proving
Lemma

2.4
. In order to analyze true pairs, we also need to do two rounds of conditioning, which will be given in
Sections

4.1
and
4.2
. We will then analyze the remaining randomness in
Sections

4.3
,
4.4
,
4.5
and
4.6
.

In the following, let
(G,H)
be a pair of random graphs that follows an
(\alpha,d,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution, where
\alpha
is a fixed constant. We fix a vertex
i\in[n]
.

4.1
First Round of Conditioning

Definition 4.1

(Variables)
.

For
j\in[n]
, define the random variable

R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}=\sum_{\ell\neq i}p_{j\ell}(1-G_{i\ell}\vee H_{i\ell}).

Also define the random variable

R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}=\sum_{j\neq i}G_{ij}\vee H_{ij}.

Definition 4.2

(Events)
.

Let
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
be the event

\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\left\{R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{j}\right\}\cap\left\{\frac{1}{2}d_{i}\leq R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\leq 2d_{i}\right\}.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
d\gtrsim\log^{2}n
and
\delta\lesssim\log^{-2}n
, then
\PP(\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.

Proof.

By definition,

\E[R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}]\geq\sum_{\ell\neq i}p_{j\ell}\cdot(\alpha-(1-\alpha)\delta)\geq(d_{j}-1)(\alpha-\delta).

By Hoeffding’s inequality (
Theorem

A.1
), using
\delta<\alpha/4
and
p_{j\ell}\leq 1
for all
j,\ell
, we have

\PP\left[R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}d_{j}\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\left((\alpha-\delta)(d_{j}-1)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha d_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{\ell\neq i}p_{j\ell}^{2}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}(d_{j}-6)\right).

In addition, we have
d_{i}\leq\E[R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}]\leq d_{i}(1+\delta)
and thus by Chernoff bounds (
Corollary

A.3
)

\PP[R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\geq 2d_{i}]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}(1-3\delta)d_{i}\right),

\PP\left[R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{i}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}d_{i}\right).

It now follows from a union bound that
\PP(\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.
∎

Definition 4.4

(Conditioning)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the variables
G_{ij}\vee H_{ij}
for all
j\in[n]
.

Clearly,
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
is
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
-measurable.

Remark 3
.

Note that we do not condition on the complete information about the neighborhoods of
i
in the two graphs, and this is different from the conditioning used in
Section

3.1
or in the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 1]
. The reason for this lies in the symmetric difference of neighborhoods, i.e.
\{j\in[n]:G_{ij}\neq H_{ij}\}
. In the true pair analysis of
[
19
]
, the contribution to the distance
D(i,i)
from those vertices
j
with
G_{ij}=1,H_{ij}=0
and the contribution from those vertices
j
with
G_{ij}=0,H_{ij}=1
are shown to be
separately
controllable. However, in our context, the contribution from each group of vertices can be much larger than acceptable. So instead of analyzing the two groups separately, we “mix” them together in a single set
J^{\mathsf{t}}
(see (
4.1
)) and seek to show that the contributions from the two groups effectively
cancel
with each other. By not revealing which vertices fall into which groups in
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
, we hope to leave room for a
probabilistic
analysis of the cancellation. See (
4.19
) for a particularly important place where this advantage is harnessed.

4.2
Second Round of Conditioning

Definition 4.5

(Sets)
.

Define the set

J^{\mathsf{t}}=\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}:G_{ij}\vee H_{ij}=1\}.

(4.1)

Note that conditional on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
, it is a deterministic set.

Definition 4.6

(Variables)
.

Define the random variable

S^{\mathsf{t}}=\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}.

Definition 4.7

(Events)
.

Let
\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
be the event

\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}:=\{S^{\mathsf{t}}\leq 10\delta d_{i}\}.

Proposition 4.8
.

If
d\gtrsim\log^{2}n
and
\delta\gtrsim\log^{-2}n
, then on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
we have
\PP(\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}})\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.

Proof.

We have on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}

\E[S^{\mathsf{t}}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}]=\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}2\delta p_{j\ell}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)\leq 2\delta|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\leq 4\delta d_{i}.

Note that the conditional distribution of
S^{\mathsf{t}}
given
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
is a sum of independent variables, all of which have ranges at most
2/d
, so by Chernoff bounds as in
Corollary

A.3
(note that here we need to scale the range by
2/d
),

\PP[S^{\mathsf{t}}\geq 10\delta d_{i}|\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}]\leq\exp\left(-2\cdot\frac{d}{2}\delta d_{i}\right).\qed

Definition 4.9

(Conditioning)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{t}}
and the variables
\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}
and
G_{j\ell}\vee H_{j\ell}
for
j,\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
.

Clearly
\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
is
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
-measurable. For the remaining of
Section

4
, we always condition with respect to
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
and assume that
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
holds.

Remark 4
.

The choice of information contained in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
is the result of a subtle balancing act. In case
G_{j\ell}=H_{j\ell}
, a revelation of the values of
\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}
and
G_{j\ell}\vee H_{j\ell}
also reveals the complete information regarding
G_{j\ell}
and
H_{j\ell}
(this fact will be used in
Proposition

4.16
). On the other hand, if
G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}
, then after conditioning on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
we know that
G_{j\ell}
and
H_{j\ell}
have different values, but nothing more; it remains uniformly random as to which variable takes value 1. For the purpose of bounding the expectation of the distance
D(i,k)
(throughout
Sections

4.5
and
4.6
), it is crucial that we do not lose the symmetry of the random vector
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
.

4.3
Interlude: Truncation

In this subsection, we introduce a truncation operation on the variables
X_{j}
and
Y_{j}
, for
j\in[n]
. While the purpose is again to exclude exceptional events, in order not to ruin the independence among the vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
, we use a truncation rather than a conditioning here.

Take a fixed
j\in[n]
, and define the truncated variable
X_{j}^{\prime}
to be equal to

\begin{cases}\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]-\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]}&\text{if }X_{j}\leq\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]-\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]},\\
X_{j}&\text{if }\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]-\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]}<X_{j}<\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]},\\
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]}&\text{if }\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]}\leq X_{j}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Similarly define variables
Y_{j}^{\prime}
for
j\in[n]
. Define accordingly

A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\right\}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{G_{ij}=1\},

(4.3)

B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\right\}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{H_{ij}=1\},

(4.4)

and finally define

D^{\prime}(i,i)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.5)

Lemma 4.10
.

If
d\gtrsim\log^{2}n
, then on
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
we have

\PP[D^{\prime}(i,i)\neq D(i,i)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]=\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)).

Proof.

We first fix some
j\in[n]
. Since
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
holds, we have
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\geq R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}\geq\alpha d_{i}/2\gtrsim\log^{2}n
. Denoting the value of
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]
by
w
, by Chernoff bounds (
Corollary

A.3
) we have

\PP\left[X_{j}\leq w-\log n\sqrt{w}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log n\sqrt{w}\cdot\frac{\log n\sqrt{w}}{w}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}n\right)

and

\PP\left[X_{j}\geq w+\log n\sqrt{w}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}\log n\sqrt{w}\cdot\min\left\{\frac{\log n\sqrt{w}}{w},1\right\}\right)=\exp\left(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)\right).

So

\PP[X_{j}\neq X_{j}^{\prime}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\leq\PP\left[X_{j}\leq w-\log n\sqrt{w}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]+\PP\left[X_{j}\geq w+\log n\sqrt{w}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]=\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n)).

Since we have

\{D^{\prime}(i,i)\neq D(i,i)\}\subset\bigcup_{j\in[n]}\{X_{j}\neq X_{j}^{\prime}\},

the conclusion follows from a union bound on
j\in[n]
.
∎

In light of
Lemma

4.10
, as far as
Lemma

2.4
is concerned, it is equivalent to analyze the random variable
D^{\prime}(i,i)
instead of
D(i,i)
. This is what we do in the remaining of
Section

4
.

4.4
Concentration of Distance

The analysis here is slightly more complicated than in
Section

3.3
. Here, even after the two rounds of conditioning, the random vectors
(X_{j},Y_{j})
(for
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
) are not mutually independent. This is because randomness might still exist in the edges
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
(for
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}
) due to our choice of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
(see
Remark

4
for more explanation), and such randomness leads to correlation between the vectors
(X_{j},Y_{j})
and
(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})
. So this time, instead of regarding
D^{\prime}(i,i)
as a function of the vectors
(X_{j},Y_{j})
, we regard
D^{\prime}(i,i)
as a function directly of the independent Bernoulli vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
(though some of them will be bundled together and analyzed as a whole). Let us begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Assume that
(G,H)
follows an
(\alpha,4\alpha^{-1}\log^{2}n,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution. Fix
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}})
and a pair
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}
. If we fix all other entries in
G,H
and flip the value of
G_{j\ell}
, then the value of
\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}
(as defined in (
4.2
)) is changed by at most
\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}
.

Proof.

Let
w
be the value of
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]
at the realization
\omega
.
Let
a
be the value of
X_{j}^{\prime}
before flipping and
b
be the value after flipping. Then
|a-b|\leq 1
and
a,b\geq w-\log n\sqrt{w}
. Since
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
holds, we have
w\geq R_{j}^{\mathsf{t}}\geq\alpha d_{j}/2\geq 2\log^{2}n
, using the assumption
d_{j}\geq 4\alpha^{-1}\log^{2}n
. So

\left|\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{w-\log n\sqrt{w}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{w}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}.\qed

Lemma 4.12

(Concentration)
.

Assume that
(G,H)
follows an
(\alpha,4\alpha^{-1}\log^{2}n,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution. On
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
, we have

\PP\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)\geq\E[D^{\prime}(i,i)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\frac{50\sqrt{d_{i}\log n}}{L}+50\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}d_{i}\delta\log n}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\leq O(n^{-3}).

Proof.

In this proof, all randomness is considered under the conditioning of
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
.

Note that the Bernoulli vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
, for
\{j,\ell\}\subset[n]
, are still independent of each other after conditioning on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
. We group the vectors into certain bundles as follows.

•

For
\{j,\ell\}
such that
|\{j,\ell\}\cap J^{\mathsf{t}}|=0
, the vector
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
has no influence on
D^{\prime}(i,i)
.

•

For
\{j,\ell\}
such that
|\{j,\ell\}\cap J^{\mathsf{t}}|=1
, we group the vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
according to the intersection
\{j,\ell\}\cap J^{\mathsf{t}}
. Formally, for
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
,

F_{j}:=\left((G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})\right)_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}.

Note that
(G_{ij},H_{ij})
is also included in the bundle
F_{j}
.

•

For
\{j,\ell\}
such that
|\{j,\ell\}\cap J^{\mathsf{t}}|=2
, we assign
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
individually to a bundle
F_{j\ell}
. Note that by the second round of conditioning,
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
is already fixed unless
\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}
is known to be 1.

Now, we consider
D^{\prime}(i,i)
as a function of these bundles, and upper-bound the marginal difference each bundle can cause on the value of
D^{\prime}(i,i)
when the values of all other bundles are fixed, for the purpose of applying McDiarmid’s inequality. In the remaining part of the proof, we use superscript
\mathsf{new}
to denote the functions and values after a change in the bundle
F_{j}
or
F_{j\ell}
.

Claim 1
.

For
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
, the marginal difference caused by
F_{j}
(viewed as a whole) is no more than
8/L
.

Proof of Claim 1.

As defined in (
4.5
), the value of
D^{\prime}(i,i)
is determined by the
2n
functions
A_{ir}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ir}^{\prime}(\cdot)
, for
r\in[n]
. Keeping all other bundles fixed, a change in the bundle
F_{j}
will only change two of the
2n
functions:
A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)
and
B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)
. If
G_{ij}=0
, then
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
are both zero functions (using the superscript
\mathsf{new}
to denote the values after a change in the bundle
F_{j}
). Otherwise, in the same way as
Proposition

2.1
,
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
are both indicator functions of a cyclic interval of
2/L
. Since similar results also hold for
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|D^{\prime}(i,i)^{\mathsf{new}}-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\left|A_{ir}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{ir}^{\prime}(t)\right|+\left|B_{ir}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{ir}^{\prime}(t)\right|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}+\frac{2}{L}=\frac{8}{L}.\qed

Claim 2
.

For
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}
such that
\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}
is known to be 1, the marginal difference caused by
F_{j\ell}
is no more than

\sqrt{\frac{64}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)}.

Proof of Claim 2.

As defined in (
4.5
), the value of
D^{\prime}(i,i)
is determined by the
2n
functions
A_{ir}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ir}^{\prime}(\cdot)
, for
r\in[n]
. Keeping all other bundles fixed, a change in the bundle
F_{j\ell}
will only change four of the
2n
functions:
A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t),B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)
and
B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)
. If
G_{ij}=0
, then
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
are both zero functions (using the superscript
\mathsf{new}
to denote the values after a change in the bundle
F_{j\ell}
). Otherwise, in the same way as
Proposition

2.1
,
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
are both indicator functions of a cyclic interval of
2/L
, centered at
\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}
and
\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime\,\mathsf{new}}}
respectively. From
Lemma

4.11
we know

\left|\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime\,\mathsf{new}}}-\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}.

So the symmetric difference of the two cyclic intervals, i.e.

\left\{t\in[0,1):A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\neq A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}\right\}

has Lebesgue measure no more than
2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}
. Since similar results also hold for other three pairs of functions, we have

\displaystyle\left|D^{\prime}(i,i)^{\mathsf{new}}-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\left|A_{ir}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{ir}^{\prime}(t)\right|+\left|B_{ir}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{ir}^{\prime}(t)\right|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{ij}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{ij}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\left|A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{i\ell}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\left|B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{i\ell}(t)\right|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{\ell}}}+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{64}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)},

where we used Cauchy-Schwarz in the last inequality.
∎

At any
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}})
, combining the above two claims, we can conclude by McDiarmid’s inequality (
Theorem

A.4
) that with conditional probability (on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
) at least
1-O\left(n^{-3}\right)
,

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(i,i)-\E[D^{\prime}(i,i)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3\log n\cdot\left(\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\left(\frac{8}{L}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}\frac{64}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3\log n\cdot\left(|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\cdot\left(\frac{8}{L}\right)^{2}+\frac{64}{\alpha}S^{\mathsf{t}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{50\sqrt{d_{i}\log n}}{L}+50\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}d_{i}\delta\log n}.

In the last inequality we used
|J^{\mathsf{t}}|=R_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\leq 2d_{i}
and
S^{\mathsf{t}}\leq 10\delta d_{i}
, as guaranteed by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
.
∎

4.5
Expectation of Distance

Now we finally arrive at the crux of the proof: we need to give an upper bound on
\E[D^{\prime}(i,i)|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]
. Let us first define two families of quantities.

Definition 4.13
.

Define for
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}

T_{1}(j):=\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\neq B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

and for
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}

T_{2}(j,\ell)=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\PP\left[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]-\PP\left[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.6)

The following lemma then reduces the task of upper-bounding
\E\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]
to dealing with
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
separately.

Lemma 4.14
.

\E\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\leq\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}T_{1}(j)+4\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}T_{2}(j,\ell)
.

Proof.

We have by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\E\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\leq\int_{0}^{1}\E\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-B_{ij}^{\prime}(t))\right|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.7)

For each
t\in[0,1)
, the integrand on the right hand side of (
4.7
) expands to

\displaystyle\quad\E\left[\left|\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-B_{ij}^{\prime}(t))\right|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\E\left[(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-B_{ij}^{\prime}(t))^{2}\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]+2\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}\E\left[(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-B_{ij}^{\prime}(t))(A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)-B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right].

(4.8)

For each
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}
, the second term on the right hand side of (
4.8
) expands to

\displaystyle\quad\E\left[(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)-B_{ij}^{\prime}(t))(A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)-B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\PP[B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\right)-

\displaystyle\quad\left(\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]+\PP[B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\right)

\displaystyle=2\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]-2\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}].

(4.9)

In the last equality, we used the fact that
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
has the same conditional distribution as
(B_{ij}^{\prime}(t),B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
, and
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
has the same conditional distribution as
(B_{ij}^{\prime}(t),A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
.
The conclusion then follows by combining (
4.7
), (
4.8
) and (
4.9
).
∎

We now give an upper bound on
T_{1}
and will defer the analysis of
T_{2}
to the next subsection. Note that bounding
T_{1}
somewhat corresponds to the true pair analysis in
[
19
]
and the analysis of
T_{2}
is an entirely new ingredient of the proof. It is interesting that due to our continuous version of the balls-into-bins distance, the analysis of
T_{1}
bypasses the rather complicated Lemma 9 of
[
19
]
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Assume that
(G,H)
follows an
(\alpha,4\alpha^{-1}\log^{2}n,\delta)
-edge-correlated distribution. On
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
, we have

\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}T_{1}(j)\leq 50d_{i}\left(\frac{\delta}{L}+\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}\delta}\right).

Proof.

Let us first bound
T_{1}(j)
for a given
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
. Let
\mu(\cdot)
denote the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. By Fubini’s theorem we have

\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\neq B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t=\E\Big[\mu\left(\{t\in[0,1):A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\neq B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\}\right)\Big|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\Big].

(4.10)

We again use analogues of
Proposition

2.1
for
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
. The functions
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
are both indicator functions with supports having Lebesgue measures no more than
2/L
, so the symmetric difference between the supports of
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
, that is,

\left\{t\in[0,1):A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\neq B_{ij}^{\prime}(t)\right\},

has Lebesgue measure no more than
4/L
.

In the case
G_{ij}=H_{ij}
, there is another way to upper-bound the measure of this symmetric difference. In fact, since
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
, the event
\{G_{ij}=H_{ij}\}
implies
G_{ij}=H_{ij}=1
. In this case,
A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)
(resp.
B_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
) is an indicator function of a cyclic interval of length
2/L
with center
\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}
(resp.
\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}
). So the symmetric difference of the two cyclic intervals has Lebesgue measure no more than 2
\left|\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}-\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}\right|
.
Therefore the right hand side of
(
4.10
)
is upper-bounded by

\PP[G_{ij}\neq H_{ij}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\cdot\frac{4}{L}+\PP[G_{ij}=H_{ij}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\cdot\E\left[2\left|\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}-\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}\right|\mid G_{ij}=H_{ij},\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right].

Since
\PP[G_{ij}\neq H_{ij}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]=2\delta_{ij}/(1+\delta_{ij})\leq 2\delta,

it is further upper-bounded by

\frac{8\delta}{L}+\E\left[2\left|\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}-\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}\right|\mid G_{ij}=H_{ij},\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right].

Recall that by
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
and the assumption
d_{j}\geq 4\alpha^{-1}\log^{2}n
, we have
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\geq\alpha d_{j}/2\geq 2\log^{2}n
. So

X_{j}^{\prime}\geq\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]-\log n\sqrt{\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\geq\frac{\alpha d_{j}}{8}.

Similarly,
Y_{j}^{\prime}\geq\alpha d_{j}/8
. It follows that

\displaystyle T_{1}(j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\delta}{L}+\E\left[2\left|\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}}-\sqrt{Y_{j}^{\prime}}\right|\mid G_{ij}=H_{ij},\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\delta}{L}+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}\E\left[|X_{j}-Y_{j}|\middle|G_{ij}=H_{ij},\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\delta}{L}+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]+2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha d_{j}}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}\cup\{i\}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right].

(4.11)

For the second term on the right hand side of (
4.11
), using Cauchy-Schwarz and the bound
|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\leq 2d_{i}
due to
\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}
, then using the conditional independence between vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
(for
\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
) and that the conditional expectation of
G_{j\ell}
and
H_{j\ell}
are the same, and finally using the bound
S^{\mathsf{t}}\leq 10\delta d_{i}
due to
\Pi^{\mathsf{t}}
, we get the following string of three inequalities accordingly:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{j}}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2d_{i}}\left(\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2d_{i}}\left(\sum_{\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}}\left(\frac{1}{d_{j}}+\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}\}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{i}\sqrt{20\delta}.

(4.12)

For the third term on the right hand side of (
4.11
), again using Cauchy-Schwarz and the bound
|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\leq 2d_{i}
, then using the conditional independence between vectors
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
(for
\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}\cup\{i\}
) and that the conditional expectation of
G_{j\ell}
and
H_{j\ell}
are the same, and finally using the bound
|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\leq 2d_{i}
again, we have the following series of transitions:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{j}}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}\cup\{i\}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2d_{i}}\left(\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\E\left[\left|\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}\cup\{i\}}(G_{j\ell}-H_{j\ell})\right|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2d_{i}}\left(\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}\frac{1}{d_{j}}\sum_{\ell\not\in J^{\mathsf{t}}\cup\{i\}}2\delta_{j\ell}p_{j\ell}\right)^{1/2}\leq 2d_{i}\sqrt{2\delta}.

(4.13)

Summing over
j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
on both sides of (
4.11
), then applying (
4.12
), (
4.5
) and the bound
|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\leq 2d_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J^{\mathsf{t}}}T_{1}(j)

\displaystyle\leq|J^{\mathsf{t}}|\cdot\frac{8\delta}{L}+d_{i}\sqrt{160\alpha^{-1}\delta}+8d_{i}\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}\delta}

\displaystyle\leq 50d_{i}\left(\frac{\delta}{L}+\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}\delta}\right).\qed

4.6
Upper bound on
T_{2}

In this subsection, we provide an upper bound for
T_{2}
. As can be seen in the definition (
4.6
), we need to analyze the joint distributions of
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
and of
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
, conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
. A particularly simple case is captured by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.16
.

For
j,\ell\in J^{\mathsf{t}}
, if
G_{j\ell}=H_{j\ell}
then
T_{2}(j,\ell)=0
.

Proof.

If
G_{j\ell}=H_{j\ell}
, the values of
G_{j\ell}
and
H_{j\ell}
are known after conditioning on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
(see
Remark

4
). So after the conditioning, the random function
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
depends only on the randomness in
\left\{G_{jr}\right\}_{r\neq\ell}
and
A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
only depends on the randomness in
\left\{G_{\ell r}\right\}_{r\neq j}
. This means that the random functions
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
are (conditionally) independent. Similarly,
B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
only depends on the randomness in
\left\{H_{\ell r}\right\}_{r\neq j}
, and
A_{ij}^{\prime}(\cdot)
is also independent with
B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
. Since
A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
is identically distributed with
B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(\cdot)
(even after conditioning on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
), we conclude that for any
t\in[0,1)
, the (conditional) joint distribution of
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
is the same as that of
(A_{ij}^{\prime}(t),B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t))
. Consequently by (
4.6
) we must have
T_{2}(j,\ell)=0
.
∎

For the remaining of
Section

4.6
, we fix
\{j,\ell\}\subset J^{\mathsf{t}}
and a realization
\omega\sim(\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}},\Gamma^{\mathsf{t}}\cap\Pi^{\mathsf{t}})
such that
G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}
at
\omega
. Our goal is to give an upper bound on the value of
T_{2}(j,\ell)
at
\omega
.

4.6.1
Preparations: the Alias World

Previously, we have based our analysis exclusively on the randomness existing in the random graphs
G
and
H
. However, as the analysis goes deeper, the random variables arising from
(G,H)
get increasingly intertwined with each other. For the purpose of simplifying notations and reducing distractions, we create some external random variables that simulate parts of the randomness in
(G,H)
.

In this subsection, we will make a series of definitions that form what we call “the alias world”. The random variables here are defined on a different probability space from that of
(G,H)
, but their distributions offer a reflection of the latter. All definitions here will be put into use in
Section

4.6.2
.

Definition 4.17

(Alias variables)
.

Let
Z_{1}
be a random variable that admits the same distribution as the conditional distribution of
1+\sum_{r\not\in\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}
, i.e.

\forall x\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\PP[Z_{1}=x]=\PP\left[1+\sum\nolimits_{r\not\in\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}=x\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right](\omega).

Let
Z_{2}
be a random variable admitting the same distribution as the conditional distribution of
1+\sum_{r\not\in\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{\ell r}
, i.e.

\forall x\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\PP[Z_{2}=x]=\PP\left[1+\sum\nolimits_{r\not\in\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{\ell r}=x\middle|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right](\omega).

Finally, let
Z_{0}
be a Bernoulli random variable with
\PP[Z_{0}=0]=\PP[Z_{0}=1]=\frac{1}{2}
. Furthermore, we assume
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2}
are mutually independent.

Parallel to
Section

4.3
, we accordingly introduce a truncation function for our alias variables.

Definition 4.18
.

Let
w_{1}
be the value
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)
, and let
w_{2}
be the value
\E[X_{\ell}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)
. Define two functions
\mathsf{Trc}_{s}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for
s\in\{1,2\}
, by

\mathsf{Trc}_{s}(x)=\begin{cases}w_{s}-\log n\sqrt{w_{s}}&\text{if }x\leq w_{s}-\log n\sqrt{w_{s}},\\
x&\text{if }w_{s}-\log n\sqrt{w_{s}}<x<w_{s}+\log n\sqrt{w_{s}},\\
w_{s}+\log n\sqrt{w_{s}}&\text{if }w_{s}+\log n\sqrt{w_{s}}\leq x.\end{cases}

Definition 4.19
.

Define a function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,2/L]
by

g(x):=\max\left\{0,\frac{2}{L}-\min_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|x-m\right|\right\}.

The function
g
will be our principal tool for analyzing
T_{2}(j,\ell)
. The reason behind its definition is the following crucial lemma.

Lemma 4.20
.

Recall the definition of
I_{t}
in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), where we assumed
L\geq 5
. Recall that
\mu(\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. For any two real numbers
a,b
, we have the identity

\mu\left(\{t\in[0,1):a,b\in I_{t}\}\right)=g(a-b).

Proof.

For any real numbers
x,y
, define their “cyclic distance” to be

\mathsf{cycd}(x,y):=\min_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-y-m|.

Recall that
\{t\in[0,1):a\in I_{t}\}
is the cyclic interval of length
2/L
centered at
a
, i.e.

\left\{t\in[0,1):\mathsf{cycd}(t,a)\leq\frac{1}{L}\right\}.

So the set
\{t\in[0,1):a,b\in I_{t}\}
is the intersection of two cyclic intervals, and hence

\displaystyle\mu\left(\{t:a,b\in I_{t}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\left\{t:\mathsf{cycd}(t,a)\leq\frac{1}{L}\right\}\cap\left\{t:\mathsf{cycd}(t,b)\leq\frac{1}{L}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\mathsf{cycd}(a,b)\geq\frac{2}{L}\\
\frac{2}{L}-\mathsf{cycd}(a,b),&\text{if }\mathsf{cycd}(a,b)<\frac{2}{L}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\frac{2}{L}-\mathsf{cycd}(a,b)\right\}=g(a-b).

Note that for the second equality above to hold, we need the condition that
\frac{1}{L}<\frac{1}{4}
.
∎

We then define an event on which the variables
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
take typical values.

Definition 4.21

(Events)
.

Let
\Lambda
be the event

\displaystyle\left\{\E[Z_{1}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}\leq Z_{1}\leq\E[Z_{1}]-2+\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\cap

\displaystyle\left\{\E[Z_{2}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{2}]-1}\leq Z_{2}\leq\E[Z_{2}]-2+\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{2}]-1}\right\}

Proposition 4.22
.

If
\E[Z_{1}],\E[Z_{2}]\gtrsim\log^{2}n
, then
\PP(\Lambda)\geq 1-\exp(-\Omega(\log^{2}n))
.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from Chernoff bounds (in a similar way to the proof of
Lemma

4.10
).
∎

Lemma 4.23
.

On the event
\Lambda
, we have
\mathsf{Trc}_{s}(Z_{s})=Z_{s}
and
\mathsf{Trc}_{s}(Z_{s}+1)=Z_{s}+1
, for any
s\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

Observe that we always have

\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}\leq X_{j}\leq 2+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}.

So by
Definition

4.17
, we know that

\E[Z_{1}]-1\leq\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\leq\E[Z_{1}]+1,

(4.14)

and similarly

\E[Z_{2}]-1\leq\E[X_{\ell}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\leq\E[Z_{2}]+1,

Again letting
w_{1}
denote the value
\E[X_{j}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)
and
w_{2}
the value
\E[X_{\ell}|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)
, the condition of
\Lambda
easily implies

w_{s}-\log n\sqrt{w_{s}}\leq Z_{s}<Z_{s}+1\leq w_{s}+\log n\sqrt{w_{s}},

and hence
\mathsf{Trc}_{s}(Z_{s})=Z_{s}
and
\mathsf{Trc}_{s}(Z_{s}+1)=Z_{s}+1
, for
s\in\{1,2\}
.
∎

Finally, we will define a quartet of variables which will be the focus of attention in
Section

4.6.3
.

Definition 4.24

(Variables)
.

Define four random variables

\displaystyle K_{00}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda\}g\left(\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle K_{01}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda\}g\left(\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}+1}\right),

\displaystyle K_{10}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda\}g\left(\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle K_{11}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda\}g\left(\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{2}+1}\right).

4.6.2
Modeling the Real World

We now return from the alias world to the original random graph pair
(G,H)
and show how our alias constructions are able to simulate the actual randomness in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
. The following two lemmas draw an intimate connection between the alias variables and the quantities of interest in (
4.6
).

Lemma 4.25
.

Assume that
L\geq 5
. We have the identity

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\PP[G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\cdot\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+Z_{0})}\right)\right].

Proof.

By the definition in (
4.3
), for any
t\in[0,1)
, we have the inclusion of events
\{A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1\}\subset\{G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1\}
. So we may write

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\PP[G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}},\sqrt{X_{\ell}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\middle|G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1,\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.15)

On the event
\{G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1\}
, we can decompose

(X_{j},X_{\ell})=\left(\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}\right)+G_{j\ell},\;\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{\ell r}\right)+G_{j\ell}\right).

Note that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
, the three variables
\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}\right)
,
\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{\ell r}\right)
and
G_{j\ell}
and the event
\{G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1\}
are mutually independent. Furthermore, since
G_{j\ell}\neq H_{j\ell}
at
\omega
, we have (see
Remark

4
)

\PP[G_{j\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)=\PP[G_{j\ell}=0|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)=\frac{1}{2},

meaning that the conditional distribution of
G_{j\ell}
is the same as the distribution of
Z_{0}
.
Therefore, by
Definition

4.17
, the joint distribution of
(X_{j},X_{\ell})

conditional on both
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
and the event
\{G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1\}
is the same as the distribution of
(Z_{1}+Z_{0},Z_{2}+Z_{0})
. So

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}},\sqrt{X_{\ell}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\middle|G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1,\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})},\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+Z_{0})}\in I_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\E\left[\mu\left(t\in[0,1):\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})},\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+Z_{0})}\in I_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by Fubini's theorem})

\displaystyle=\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+Z_{0})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:rationaleg}}).

(4.16)

The conclusion follows by combining (
4.15
) and (
4.16
).
∎

Lemma 4.26
.

Assume that
L\geq 5
. We have the identity

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\PP[G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\cdot\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1-Z_{0})}\right)\right].

Proof.

By definitions in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), for any

, we have the inclusion of events

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\PP[G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}},\sqrt{Y_{\ell}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\middle|G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1,\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.17)

On the event
\{G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1\}
, we can decompose

(X_{j},Y_{\ell})=\left(\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}\right)+G_{j\ell},\;\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}H_{\ell r}\right)+H_{j\ell}\right).

Note that conditional on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}
, the random variables
\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}G_{jr}\right)
and
\left(1+\sum\nolimits_{r\notin\{i,j,\ell\}}H_{\ell r}\right)
, the random vector
(G_{j\ell},H_{j\ell})
and the event
\{G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1\}
are mutually independent. Moreover, by the symmetry between
G
and

Z_{2}

\omega
, we have (see

\PP[G_{j\ell}=1,H_{j\ell}=0|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)=\PP[G_{j\ell}=0,H_{j\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)=\frac{1}{2},

meaning that the conditional distribution of

is the same as the distribution of
(Z_{0},1-Z_{0})
.
Therefore, by
Definition

, the joint distribution of
(X_{j},Y_{\ell})

conditional on both
\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}

is the same as the distribution of

. So

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{X_{j}^{\prime}},\sqrt{Y_{\ell}^{\prime}}\in I_{t}\middle|G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1,\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}\right](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\PP\left[\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})},\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1-Z_{0})}\in I_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\E\left[\mu\left(t\in[0,1):\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})},\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1-Z_{0})}\in I_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by Fubini's theorem})

\displaystyle=\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1-Z_{0})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:rationaleg}}).

(4.18)

The conclusion follows by combining (
4.17
) and (
4.18
).
∎

Combining the previous two lemmas, we are able to convert

to some quantity completely living in the alias world.

Lemma 4.27
.

Assume that
L\geq 5
. The value of
T_{2}(j,\ell)
at
\omega
is at most

\frac{1}{2}\E\left|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}\right|+\frac{4}{L}\cdot\PP(\Lambda^{c}).

Proof.

By
Lemmas

4.25
and
4.26
and the fact that

\PP[G_{ij}=G_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]=\PP[G_{ij}=H_{i\ell}=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}]\leq 1,

(4.19)

we know that the value of
T_{2}(j,\ell)
at
\omega
is

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=A_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}\PP[A_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=B_{i\ell}^{\prime}(t)=1|\mathcal{G}^{\mathsf{t}}](\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\left|\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+Z_{0})}\right)\right]-\E\left[g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+Z_{0})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1-Z_{0})}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\E\left|g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2})}\right)+g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+1)}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1)}\right)-\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+1)}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2})}\right)-g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+1)}\right)\right|.

(4.20)

From
Lemma

4.23
and
Definition

4.24
we know that for any
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\{0,1\}
, on
\Lambda
we always have

g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+\beta_{1})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+\beta_{2})}\right)=K_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}.

In addition, by definition of the function
g
, on
\Lambda^{c}
we always have

\left|g\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{1}(Z_{1}+\beta_{1})}-\sqrt{\mathsf{Trc}_{2}(Z_{2}+\beta_{2})}\right)\right|\leq\frac{2}{L}.

Plugging the preceding two displays into (
4.20
), it follows that the value of
T_{2}(j,\ell)
at
\omega
is at most

\frac{1}{2}\E|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}|+\frac{4}{L}\cdot\PP(\Lambda^{c}).\qed

4.6.3
Analyzing the Alias

We now return to the alias world to analyze the quantity
K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}
.

Definition 4.28

(Events)
.

Let
Q=\mathbb{Z}\cup(\mathbb{Z}+2/L)\cup(\mathbb{Z}-2/L)
be a discrete set of real numbers. Let
\Lambda_{1}
be the event that the interval

\left[\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}},\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{2}}\right)

has a nonempty intersection with
Q
. Let
\Lambda_{2}
be the event that the interval

\left[\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}+1},\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}}\right)

has a nonempty intersection with

.

Lemma 4.29
.

On
\Lambda_{1}^{c}\cap\Lambda_{2}^{c}
, we have
K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}=0
.

Proof.

On
\Lambda_{1}^{c}\cap\Lambda_{2}^{c}
, the function
g
is linear on the interval
\left[\sqrt{Z_{1}}-\sqrt{Z_{2}+1},\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{2}}\right)
. The conclusion then follows directly from
Definition

4.24
.
∎

Lemma 4.30
.

Assume
\E[Z_{1}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
. Then on
\Lambda
we have

\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1}}.

Proof.

We have

\left(\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{1}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4Z_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{4\left(\E[Z_{1}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{\E[Z_{1}]+1},

where in the second-to-last inequality above we used the condition of
\Lambda
(
Definition

4.21
) and in the last inequality we used the assumption
\E[Z_{1}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
.
∎

Lemma 4.31
.

Assume
\E[Z_{1}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
. Then we have

\PP(\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda)\leq\frac{50\log n}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}.

(4.21)

Proof.

Recall that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are independent. Let us pick an arbitrary integer
y
and condition on the event
\{Z_{2}=y\}
.
On the event
\{Z_{2}=y\}
,
\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda
holds only if the intersection

\left[\sqrt{Z_{1}},\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}\right)\cap(Q+\sqrt{y})\cap\left[\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}},\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1+\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}}\right]

is nonempty. Using the assumption
\E[Z_{1}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
, we get from a straightforward computation that

\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1+\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}}-\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}}\leq 2\log n.

Since each interval of the form
[x,x+1)
contains at most 3 points of the set
(Q+\sqrt{y})
, it follows that the set

(Q+\sqrt{y})\cap\left[\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1-\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}},\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1+\log n\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]-1}}\right]

has cardinality at most
6\log n+3
. So there are at most
6\log n+3
possible values of
Z_{1}
that make
\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda
hold.
Recall from
Definition

4.17
that
Z_{1}
is identically distributed with a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables. By Berry-Esseen theorem (
Corollary

A.6
), for any fixed integer

,

\PP[Z_{1}=x]\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}.

Adding up at most
6\log{n}+3
such inequalities, we have

\PP\left[\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda\middle|Z_{2}=y\right]\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}(6\log n+3)\leq\frac{50\log n}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}.

The conclusion (
4.21
) then follows since
y
is arbitrary.
∎

Finally, we arrive at an upper bound on
\E|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}|
.

Lemma 4.32
.

Assume
\E[Z_{1}],\E[Z_{2}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
. Then we have

\E\left|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}\right|\leq\frac{100\log n}{\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{2}]}}\right).

Proof.

Using the fact that
g
is
1
-Lipschitz and then
Lemma

4.30
, we have

|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}|\leq|K_{00}-K_{10}|+|K_{11}-K_{01}|\leq 2\left|\sqrt{Z_{1}+1}-\sqrt{Z_{1}}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1}}.

Since
\E[Z_{1}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
, we know from
Lemma

4.31
that
\PP(\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda)\leq 50\log n\left(\Var[Z_{1}]\right)^{-1/2}
. By symmetry, since
\E[Z_{2}]\geq 2\log^{2}n-1
we also have
\PP(\Lambda_{2}\cap\Lambda)\leq 50\log n\left(\Var[Z_{2}]\right)^{-1/2}
.
It then follows from
Lemma

4.29
that

\displaystyle\quad\E\left|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\E\Big[\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda_{1}\cap\Lambda\}\left|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}\right|\Big]+\E\Big[\mathbbm{1}\{\Lambda_{2}\cap\Lambda\}\left|K_{00}+K_{11}-K_{10}-K_{01}\right|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{50\log n}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{1}]}}\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1}}+\frac{50\log n}{\sqrt{\Var[Z_{2}]}}\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{\E[Z_{1}]+1}}.\qed

Appendix A
Probabilistic Inequalities

The following concentration inequalities are used many times in this paper.

Theorem A.1

(Hoeffding’s inequality,
[
38
, Theorem D.2]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with
X_{i}
taking values in
[a_{i},b_{i}]
for all
i\in[n]
. Then for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
we have

\PP\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[X_{i}]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

Theorem A.2

(Multiplicative Chernoff bounds,
[
38
, Theorem D.4]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
. Let
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
\mu=\E[S]
. For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
we have

\PP[S\leq(1-\varepsilon)\mu]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\mu\right)

\PP[S\geq(1+\varepsilon)\mu]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2+\varepsilon}\mu\right).

Both
Theorem

A.1
and
Theorem

A.2
can be derived from moment generating functions, but they are tailored to different situations. In particular,
Theorem

A.2
is finer than
Theorem

A.1
when
\mu
is small, while in
Theorem

A.1
the ranges of the random variables are more flexible.

In this paper we often use
Theorem

A.2
in the following form:

Corollary A.3
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in
[0,1]
. Let
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. If
a,b
are real numbers such that
a\leq b\leq\E[S]
, then

\PP[S\leq a]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(b-a)\cdot\frac{b-a}{b}\right).

(A.1)

If
a,b
are real numbers such that
\E[S]\leq b\leq a
, then

\PP[S\geq a]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{3}(a-b)\cdot\min\left\{\frac{a-b}{b},1\right\}\right).

(A.2)

Proof.

For (
A.1
), note that the right hand side of (
A.1
) is monotone decreasing in
b
. So we may assume
b=\E[S]
. Then (
A.1
) becomes a restatement of the first inequality of
Theorem

A.2
.

For (
A.2
), note that the right hand side of (
A.2
) is monotone increasing in
b
. So we may assume
b=\E[S]
. Since
\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon\cdot\min\{\varepsilon,1\}\leq\varepsilon^{2}/(2+\varepsilon)
, (
A.2
) becomes a direct consequence of the second inequality of
Theorem

A.2
.
∎

McDiarmid’s inequality is a generalization of
Theorem

A.1
, and is our principal tool for the concentration analysis in
Sections

3.3
and
4.4
.

Theorem A.4

(McDiarmid’s inequality,
[
38
, Theorem D.8]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking value in a space
\mathcal{X}
. Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. Assume that for any
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,

\sup_{x_{i}^{\mathsf{new}}\in\mathcal{X}}|f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i}^{\mathsf{new}},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n})|\leq c_{i}.

(A.3)

That is, substituting the value of the
i
-th coordinate
x_{i}
changes the value of function
f
by at most
c_{i}
. Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Then for any
\Delta\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-\Delta}
,

\left|f(X)-\E[f(X)]\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}.

The following classical result is useful in
Lemmas

3.11
and
4.31
.

Theorem A.5

(Berry-Esseen theorem,
[
3
,
21
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with
\E[X_{i}]=0
,
\E[X_{i}^{2}]=\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
, and
\E[|X_{i}^{3}|]=\rho_{i}<\infty
. Let

S_{n}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{n}^{2}}}

be the normalized
n
-th partial sum. Denote by
F_{n}
the cumulative distribution function of
S_{n}
and by
\Phi
the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Then we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{n}(x)-\Phi(x)|\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}.

The proof of a sharper version of
Theorem

A.5
can be found in
[
48
]
. In this paper
Theorem

A.5
is used in the following form:

Corollary A.6
.

Let
X
be a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables. Let
X^{\prime}
follow a normal distribution of mean
\E[X]
and variance
\Var[X]
. Then for any real numbers
a
and
b
, we have

\left|\PP[a\leq X\leq b]-\PP[a\leq X^{\prime}\leq b]\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Var[X]}}.

Proof.

Write
X=\E[X]+X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
, where
X_{i}
are independent “centered” Bernoulli variables of the form

\PP[X_{i}=1-p_{i}]=p_{i}\text{ and }\PP[X_{i}=-p_{i}]=1-p_{i}.

Let
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\E[X_{i}^{2}]
and
\rho_{i}=\E[|X_{i}^{3}|]
. Since
|X_{i}|\leq 1
we clearly have
\rho_{i}\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}
.

Let
F_{n}
be the cumulative distribution function of the random variable

S_{n}:=\frac{X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\dots+\sigma_{n}^{2}}}=\frac{X-\E[X]}{\sqrt{\Var[X]}},

and let
\Phi
be the cumulative distribution of the standard normal distribution. We have

\displaystyle\left|\PP[X\leq b]-\PP[X^{\prime}\leq b]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|F_{n}\left(\frac{b-\E[X]}{\Var[X]}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{b-\E[X]}{\Var[X]}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{1}+\dots+\rho_{n}}{(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{n}^{2})^{3/2}}

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{thm:B-E}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{n}^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Var[X]}}

\displaystyle(\text{since }\rho_{i}\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}).

Similarly

\left|\PP[X<a]-\PP[X^{\prime}<a]\right|=\lim_{x\rightarrow a^{-}}\left|\PP[X\leq x]-\PP[X^{\prime}\leq x]\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Var[X]}}.

Therefore

\left|\PP[a\leq X\leq b]-\PP[a\leq X^{\prime}\leq b]\right|\leq\left|\PP[X\leq b]-\PP[X^{\prime}\leq b]\right|+\left|\PP[X<a]-\PP[X^{\prime}<a]\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\Var[X]}}.\qed

Appendix B
Symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
Variables

This appendix serves two purposes. Firstly, it provides the lemma required in the proof of
Lemma

3.13
. Secondly, it lays the groundwork for
Appendix

C
. The focal point of this appendix is the following simple notion that somehow unifies these two objectives.

Definition B.1
.

A random variable
X
is said to be a symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variable if
X
takes value in the set
\{-1,0,1\}
and
\PP[X=-1]=\PP[X=1]
.

The lemma below demonstrates how symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables effectively symmetrize Bernoulli variables.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent Bernoulli variables, and for
i\in[n]
let
p_{i}=\PP[X_{i}=1]
. Suppose
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables with
\PP[Y_{i}=1]=\min\{p_{i},1-p_{i}\}
. Then

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right|.

Proof.

For each
i\in[n]
, let
W_{i}
be a Bernoulli random variable with
\PP[W_{i}=1]=2\min\{p_{i},1-p_{i}\}
, and let
U_{i}
be a random variable taking value in
\{-1,1\}
with
\PP[U_{i}=1]=\frac{1}{2}
. Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{n},U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be mutually independent. Finally, let
u_{i}
be a constant defined by

u_{i}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }p_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2},\\
1&\text{if }p_{i}>\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

It is then easy to observe that for each
i\in[n]
, the random variables
Y_{i}
and
W_{i}U_{i}
are identically distributed. In addition,
2X_{i}-1
and
W_{i}U_{i}+(1-W_{i})u_{i}
are identically distributed. So

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-x\right|=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2X_{i}-1)-x\right|=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(W_{i}U_{i}+(1-W_{i})u_{i}\Big)-x\right|.

(B.1)

It is easy to simplify the right hand side of (
B.1
) if
W_{1},\dots,W_{n}
are fixed. In fact, since each variable
U_{i}
is symmetric around 0, the conditional distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}U_{i}
(given
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
) has median 0. Recall that the
L^{1}
-norm of a translated variable
Z-z
is always minimized when
z
is the median of the variable
Z
. So for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\E\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}U_{i}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-W_{i})u_{i}-x\right)\right|\mid W_{1},\dots,W_{n}\right]\geq\E\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}U_{i}\right|\mid W_{1},\dots,W_{n}\right].

Taking expectation and then infimum over
x\in\mathbb{R}
yields

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(W_{i}U_{i}+(1-W_{i})u_{i}\Big)-x\right|\geq\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}U_{i}\right|=\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right|.

(B.2)

Combining (
B.1
) and (
B.2
) concludes the proof.
∎

We will now delve deeper into the study of sums of independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables. In particular, we examine the
L^{1}
-norm of the sum as a function of the probability parameters associated with each individual symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variable.

Definition B.3
.

Let
n
be any positive integer. We define a function
f_{n}:[0,\frac{1}{2}]^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows. For
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables with
\PP[X_{i}=1]=p_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
. Define

f_{n}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}):=\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|.

Lemma B.4
.

Suppose
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,1/2]
and let
S=p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}
. Then

f_{n}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\geq\frac{2S}{\sqrt{6S+1}}.

Proof.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables with
\PP[X_{i}=1]=p_{i}
and let

X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
We now compute

\E[X^{2}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[X_{i}^{2}]=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=2S.

(B.3)

In addition, we have by independence

\displaystyle\E[X^{4}]=

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{4}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E[X_{i}^{4}]+3\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\E[X_{i}^{2}]\E[X_{j}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}+3\sum_{i=1}^{n}2p_{i}(2S-2p_{i})\leq 2S+12S^{2}.

(B.4)

Furthermore, by Hölder’s inequality we have

\E[X^{4}]\cdot\left(\E|X|\right)^{2}\geq\left(\E[X^{2}]\right)^{3}.

Combined with (
B.3
) and (
B.4
), it yields the desired conclusion.
∎

In
Lemma

3.13
, we used
Lemma

B.4
in the following form.

Corollary B.5
.

Suppose
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent Bernoulli random variables. For each
i\in[n]
suppose
\PP[X_{i}]=p_{i}
such that
\frac{1}{n}\leq p_{i}\leq 1-\frac{1}{n}
. Then for any random variable
Y
independent with
(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, we have

\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}+Y\right|\geq\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{p_{i},1-p_{i}\}}.

Proof.

By independence and then using
Lemma

B.2
, we have

\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}+Y\right|\geq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}f_{n}\Big(\min\{p_{1},1-p_{1}\},\dots,\min\{p_{n},1-p_{n}\}\Big).

(B.5)

Let
S:=\min\{p_{1},1-p_{1}\}+\dots+\min\{p_{n},1-p_{n}\}
. Then
S\geq 1
. By
Lemma

B.4
we then have

f_{n}\Big(\min\{p_{1},1-p_{1}\},\dots,\min\{p_{n},1-p_{n}\}\Big)\geq\frac{2S}{\sqrt{6S+1}}\geq\frac{2S}{\sqrt{7S}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{S}.

(B.6)

The conclusion then follows directly by combining (
B.5
) and (
B.6
).
∎

While the absolute lower bound in
Lemma

B.4
is sufficient for
Lemma

3.13
, the analysis in
Appendix

C
requires a more careful examination on how
f_{n}
changes with its arguments
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
. Our next goal is to establish
Lemma

B.8
, a comparison result in this regard.

Lemma B.6
.

The function
f_{n}
is non-decreasing in each of its arguments.

Proof.

Since
f_{n}
is a symmetric function, it suffices to show monotonicity in the first argument. Assume
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
are independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
random variables, and let
p_{i}=\PP[X_{i}=1]
for
i\in[n]
. For each integer
t
, note that

\E|X_{1}+t|-t=\begin{cases}2p_{1}&\text{if }t=0,\\
0&\text{if }t\neq 0.\end{cases}

So by independence, we have

\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|-\E\left|\sum_{i=2}^{n}X_{i}\right|=2p_{1}\cdot\PP\left[\sum_{i=2}^{n}X_{i}=0\right].

That is,

f_{n}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n})=f_{n}(0,p_{2},\dots,p_{n})+2p_{1}\cdot\PP\left[\sum_{i=2}^{n}X_{i}=0\right],

from which we see that
f_{n}
is non-decreasing in
p_{1}
.
∎

The following lemma is on a basic property of sums of independent symmetric variables, which will be useful in proving
Lemma

B.8
.

Lemma B.7
.

Suppose
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,\frac{1}{4}]
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables with
\PP[X_{i}=1]=p_{i}
. For any constants
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and any integer
x
, we have

\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}=0\right]\geq\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}=x\right].

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be independent Bernoulli variables with

\PP[Y_{i}=1]=\PP[Z_{i}=1]=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4p_{i}}}{2}.

Then the variables
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}-Z_{i}
are identically distributed, for each
i\in[n]
. So for any integer
x
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}=x\right]=\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Z_{i}+x\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i}=y+x\right]\cdot\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Z_{i}=y\right]

\displaystyle(\text{by independence})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i}=y+x\right]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Z_{i}=y\right]^{2}

\displaystyle(\text{by AM-GM inequality})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i}=y\right]\cdot\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Z_{i}=y\right]

\displaystyle\left(Y_{i}\text{ and }Z_{i}\text{ are identically distributed}\right)

\displaystyle=\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(Y_{i}-Z_{i})=0\right]=\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}=0\right].

Lemma B.8
.

Suppose
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,\frac{1}{16}]
. Then

f_{n}(4p_{1},\dots,4p_{n})\geq 2f_{n}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}).

Proof.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be independent symmetric
\{-1,0,1\}
variables with
\PP[X_{i}=1]=4p_{i}
and
\PP[Y_{i}=1]=p_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
. For each
k\in[n]
, observe that for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\E|X_{k}+t|-\E|2Y_{k}+t|=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|t|\geq 2,\\
-2p_{k}&\text{if }|t|=1,\\
4p_{k}&\text{if }t=0.\end{cases}

So by independence between the variables, we have

\displaystyle\quad\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}+X_{k}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}2Y_{i}\right|-\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}+2Y_{k}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}2Y_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=-2p_{k}\cdot\PP\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}2Y_{i}\right|=1\right]+4p_{k}\cdot\PP\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}2Y_{i}=0\right]\geq 0,

(B.7)

using
Lemma

B.7
in the last step.
Summing over all
k\in[n]
on both sides of (
B.7
) yields

\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|\geq\E\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}2Y_{i}\right|,

which is exactly the desired inequality.
∎

Appendix C
Matching Gaussian Matrices

In this appendix, we provide a degree-profile based algorithm for matching correlated Gaussian matrices with arbitrary and inhomogeneous means. While mathematically our
Theorem

1.2
is self-contained from this appendix, we believe that the analysis on the Gaussian case serves as a helpful warm up and illustrates well some of the key intuitions in a simplified context.

Let
(G,H)
be a pair of random symmetric
n\times n
matrices where each pair
(G_{ij},H_{ij})
is sampled from the bivariate normal distribution

\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{ij}\\
\mu_{ij}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho_{ij}\\
\rho_{ij}&1\end{bmatrix}\right)

independently, for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
. Here,
\mu_{ij}
and
\rho_{ij}
are arbitrary and unknown real numbers, with the assumption that
\rho_{ij}
is no less than a given threshold
\rho=1-O(\log^{-2}n)
. We want to find an efficient algorithm which succeeds to recover any unknown permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with high probability when the input is given as
(G_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}
and the permuted matrix
(H_{\pi^{-1}(i),\pi^{-1}(j)})_{i,j\in[n]}
.

We again base our algorithm on a balls-into-bins-type distance function
D:[n]\times[n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For each
i
, we directly use entries in the row
(G_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
(or correspondingly the row
(H_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
) as “balls”, as in
[
19
, Section 2]
; and our “bins” will be the intervals
I_{m}=[\frac{m}{L},\frac{m+1}{L})
, for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Here
L
is a fixed positive integer. For each
i,j\in[n]
we define the Bernoulli variables

A_{ij}(m)=\mathbbm{1}\{G_{ij}\in I_{m}\}\text{ and }B_{ij}(m)=\mathbbm{1}\{H_{ij}\in I_{m}\}.

We define the distance

D(i,k)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(m)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{kj}(m)\right|.

(C.1)

We will show in this appendix that the same algorithm as
Algorithm

1
but based on the distance
D(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
C.1
) suffices for the Gaussian random matrices
(G,H)
. Since our algorithm is permutation-oblivious (see
Proposition

2.2
), we may assume without loss of generality that
\pi=\mathrm{id}
, where
\mathrm{id}
is the identity permutation.

Theorem C.1
.

Suppose
L\geq 10^{5}\log n
and
\rho\geq 1-10^{-8}L^{-2}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the algorithm that outputs
\widehat{\pi}
where
\widehat{\pi}(i)=\argmin_{k\in[n]}D(i,k)
for
i\in[n]
(we use the convention that in the case of multiple minimizers an arbitrary one is picked). Then we have

\PP\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left(G_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in[n]},\;\left(H_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in[n]},\;L\right)=\mathrm{id}\right]\geq 1-O(n^{-1}).

Theorem

C.1
follows immediately from the next lemma and a simple union bound.

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
L\geq 10^{5}\log n
and
\rho\geq 1-10^{-8}L^{-2}
. Let
i,k\in[n]
be a pair of distinct indices. We have

\PP[D(i,k)<D(i,i)]\leq O(n^{-3}).

The remaining of this appendix is devoted to proving
Lemma

C.2
. We fix the indices
i
and
k
throughout. The statement of
Lemma

C.2
concerns only three rows
(G_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
,
(H_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
and
(H_{kj})_{j\in[n]}
of the matrices. The row
(G_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
is entry-wise correlated with the row
(H_{ij})_{j\in[n]}
but is independent with the row
(H_{kj})_{j\in[n]}
, except that there is dependence between
G_{ik}
and
H_{ki}
. As in
[
19
, Section 2]
, we may assume
G_{ik}
and
H_{ki}
are also independent, since by the triangle inequality this changes
D(i,k)-D(i,i)
by at most an additive constant. In the following, we will show

\PP\left[D(i,k)-D(i,i)\leq 2\sqrt{n\log n}\right]\leq O(n^{-3})

(C.2)

under the assumption that
G_{ik}
and
H_{ki}
are also independent, which implies
Lemma

C.2
.

Definition C.3
.

A pair
(j,m)\in[n]\times\mathbb{Z}
is said to be “good” if
I_{m}=\left[\frac{m}{L},\frac{m+1}{L}\right)
satisfies

\mu_{ij}-\log^{2/3}n\leq\frac{m}{L}<\frac{m+1}{L}\leq\mu_{ij}+\log^{2/3}n.

Definition C.4

(Truncation)
.

We define the following approximation for
D(i,i)
:

D^{\prime}(i,i):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{j:(j,m)\text{ good pair}}(A_{ij}(m)-B_{ij}(m))\right|.

(C.3)

Lemma C.5
.

We have

\PP\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)\neq D(i,i)\right]=\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\log^{4/3}n\right)\right).

Proof.

By union bound

\displaystyle\PP\left[D^{\prime}(i,i)\neq D(i,i)\right]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{(j,m)\text{ not good}\}\left(\PP[A_{ij}(m)\neq 0]+\PP[B_{ij}(m)\neq 0]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\PP\left[|G_{ij}-\mu_{ij}|\geq\log^{2/3}n\right]+\PP\left[|H_{ij}-\mu_{ij}|\geq\log^{2/3}n\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\log^{4/3}n\right)\right).\qed

We then study the concentration and expectation of the variable
D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)
separately.

Lemma C.6

(Concentration)
.

We have

\PP\left[D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)\leq\E\left[D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right]-10\sqrt{n\log n}\right]\leq O(n^{-3}).

Proof.

We group the
3n
random variables
G_{i1},\dots,G_{in},H_{i1},\dots,H_{in},H_{k1},\dots,H_{kn}
into
n
bundles, the
j
-th of which consists of
G_{ij},H_{ij}
and
G_{kj}
. Note that these
n
bundles are mutually independent. We now consider
D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)
as a function of the
n
bundles and analyze the marginal difference a bundle (as a whole) can make on the quantity
D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)
. In the following, we use the superscript
\mathsf{new}
to denote the new random variables obtained after changing the
j
-th bundle. Note that
A_{ij}(\cdot)
,
A_{ij}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
,
B_{ij}(\cdot)
and
B_{ij}(\cdot)^{\mathsf{new}}
are all indicator functions of singleton sets. We then have by triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left|D^{\prime}(i,i)^{\mathsf{new}}-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(\left|A_{ir}(m)^{\mathsf{new}}-A_{ir}(m)\right|+\left|B_{ir}(m)^{\mathsf{new}}-B_{ir}(m)\right|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|A_{ij}(m)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|A_{ij}(m)\right|+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|B_{ij}(m)^{\mathsf{new}}\right|+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|B_{ij}(m)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+1+1+1=4.

Similarly
\left|D(i,k)^{\mathsf{new}}-D(i,k)\right|\leq 4
. By the triangle inequality again, for every
j\in[n]
, the marginal difference on the quantity
D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)
caused by the
j
-th bundle is no more than
8
. By McDiarmid’s inequality (
Theorem

A.4
) we conclude that with probability at least
1-O(n^{-3})
,

\displaystyle D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right]-\sqrt{\frac{3\log n}{2}\cdot n\cdot 8^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\E\left[D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right]-10\sqrt{n\log n}.\qed

For the analysis of the expectation of
D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)
, we identify a class of “most significant” pairs among the good pairs.

Definition C.7
.

A pair
(j,m)\in[n]\times\mathbb{Z}
is said to be super-good if
I_{m}=\left[\frac{m}{L},\frac{m+1}{L}\right)
satisfies

\mu_{ij}-1\leq\frac{m}{L}<\frac{m+1}{L}\leq\mu_{ij}+1.

In particular, for any fixed
j\in[n]
there are at least
L
values of
m
such that the pair
(j,m)
is super-good.

The following lemma concerns only one or two Gaussian variables. Its proof is elementary and straightforward, so we provide only a brief sketch here.

Lemma C.8
.

Assume
L\geq 10^{5}\log n
and
\rho\geq 1-10^{-8}L^{-2}
. If
(j,m)
is a good pair, then we have

(1)

\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1]\leq 1/L
.

(2)

100\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1,B_{ij}(m)=0]\leq\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1]
if
n
is sufficiently large.

(3)

\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1]\geq(10L)^{-1}
if
(j,m)
is super-good.

Proof Sketch.

Statements (1) and (3) are trivial by the Gaussian density function and
Definition

C.7
. We then sketch a proof of statement (2). Define a sub-interval

J_{m}=\left[\frac{m}{L}+\frac{1}{800L},\frac{m+1}{L}-\frac{1}{800L}\right]\subset I_{m}.

Write the bivariate normal vector
(G_{ij},H_{ij})
as
\left(X,\rho_{ij}X+\sigma_{ij}Y\right)
, where
X
and
Y
are independent Gaussian variables and
\sigma_{ij}=\sqrt{1-\rho_{ij}^{2}}
. So

\frac{\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1,B_{ij}(m)=0]}{\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1]}\leq\frac{\PP[X\in I_{m}\setminus J_{m}]}{\PP[X\in I_{m}]}+\frac{\PP[X\in J_{m},\,\rho_{ij}X+\sigma_{ij}Y\not\in I_{m}]}{\PP[X\in J_{m}]}.

(C.4)

Then it is straightforward to compute from the Gaussian density function that

\frac{\PP[X\in I_{m}\setminus J_{m}]}{\PP[X\in I_{m}]}\leq\frac{1}{200},

(C.5)

and that when
n
is sufficiently large

\PP\left[\rho_{ij}X+\sigma_{ij}Y\not\in I_{m}\middle|X\in J_{m}\right]\leq\PP\left[|\sigma_{ij}Y|\geq\frac{1}{10^{3}L}\right]\leq\frac{1}{200}.

(C.6)

Combining (
C.4
), (
C.5
) and (
C.6
) then yields statement (2).
∎

Lemma C.9

(Expectation)
.

Assume
L\geq 10^{5}\log n
and
\rho\geq 1-10^{-8}L^{-2}
. If
n
is sufficiently large, we have

\E\left[D(i,k)-D^{\prime}(i,i)\right]\geq 12\sqrt{n\log n}.

For each good pair
(j,m)

p_{jm}=\PP[A_{ij}(m)=1]
and

(2)

100q_{jm}\leq p_{jm}\leq 1/2
. For other pairs

, set

.

Note that for each

, the

) and then

\E[D(i,k)]\geq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\E\left|\sum_{j:(j,m)\text{ good pair}}A_{ij}(m)-x\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}(p_{1m},\dots,p_{nm}),

.

.
Now we have

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}(p_{1m},\cdots,p_{nm})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}\left(\frac{1}{10L}\mathbbm{1}\{1\in E_{m}\},\dots,\frac{1}{10L}\mathbbm{1}\{n\in E_{m}\}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:f_monotone} and \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:CGaussian}\ref{lem:Gaussian3}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{|E_{m}|}{5\sqrt{nL+L^{2}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{j\in E_{m}\}}{5\sqrt{nL+L^{2}}}

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:control_f}})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{L}{5\sqrt{nL+L^{2}}}=\frac{n}{5}\sqrt{\frac{L}{n+L}}\geq 50\sqrt{n\log n}

\displaystyle(\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{def:super_good}}),

Appendix D
Numerical Experiments

In this appendix, we conduct an empirical evaluation of our algorithm. While our theoretical analysis used a specific distance function as in (
2.5
), we include a broader class of distance functions in this empirical study.

We first consider the 1-Wasserstein distance, which is the same distance used in the empirical experiments of
[
19
, Section 5]
. Recall that
X_{j}
(respectively
Y_{j}
) denotes the degree of the vertex
j
in
G
(respectively
H
). Additionally, we introduce
N_{G}(i)
and
N_{H}(i)
as the sets of neighbors of the vertex
i
in
G
and
H
, respectively. We define the distance

D^{\mathsf{ref}}(i,k):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{1}{|N_{G}(i)|}\sum_{j\in N_{G}(i)}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{j}\leq t\}-\frac{1}{|N_{H}(k)|}\sum_{j\in N_{H}(k)}\mathbbm{1}\{Y_{j}\leq t\}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

This is the
L^{1}
-distance between the empirical cumulative distribution functions of the sample sets
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in N_{G}(i)}
and
\{Y_{j}\}_{j\in N_{H}(k)}
. We also define the following analogous distance function where we take square roots of degrees:

D^{\mathsf{cdf}}(i,k):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{1}{|N_{G}(i)|}\sum_{j\in N_{G}(i)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{X_{j}}\leq t\right\}-\frac{1}{|N_{H}(k)|}\sum_{j\in N_{H}(k)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{Y_{j}}\leq t\right\}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t.

1.2

Finally, we consider the Chung-Lu model introduced in
[
6

, a canonical random graph model where we may specify a sequence of expected degrees. To simulate a scale-free network, we specify the degree sequence by independently sampling its elements from a power law distribution.

We now give the precise definition of our model. Let
\gamma\in(2,3)
and
b>0
be two fixed parameters. Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}

\delta

1.1

In our simulations,
\delta

\gamma

b

\gamma=2.5\text{ and }b=10^{4/3},
