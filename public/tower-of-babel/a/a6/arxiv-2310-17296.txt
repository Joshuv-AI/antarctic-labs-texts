1.
Introduction

Cuntz algebras
\mathcal{O}_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, play a predominant role in the
theory of
C^{*}
-algebras and provide important links to other branches
of mathematics such as dynamical systems, representation theory,
wavelet theory, and others, see e.g.
[
8
]
. In
[
16
]

Phillips introduced
L^{p}
-analogues of Cuntz algebras
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
that
are complex Banach algebras represents on
L^{p}
-spaces for

p\in[1,\infty)
. By definition
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
is a Banach algebra which
is universal for
L^{p}
-Cuntz families
, i.e. families of
contractive operators
T_{i},S_{i}
,
i=1,...,n
, acting on an

L^{p}
-space
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
, for some measure space, satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}S_{i}=1,\qquad S_{i}T_{i}=1\quad\text{and}\quad T_{i}S_{i}\text{ is hermitian},\quad\text{for $i=1,...,n$}.

In fact, for any such family, the Banach subalgebra
B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
of
B(L^{p}(\mu))
generated by
T_{i},S_{i}
,
i=1,...n
is naturally isometrically isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
,
and also
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
is simple and purely infinite, see
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
. For
p=2
we necessarily have
S_{i}=T_{i}^{*}
and
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{n}

is the standard Cuntz
C^{*}
-algebra
[
10
]
. For
p\neq 2
, operators
T_{i},S_{i}
are necessarily
spatial partial isometries
by generalized Lamperti’s Theorem, see
[
5
, Theorem 2.26]
.

Cuntz
C^{*}
-algebras, and their generalizations, have natural models as crossed products by transfer operators (often called Exel’s crossed product
[
11
]
), which give link Cuntz algebras with thermodynamical formalism. In this note we define an
L^{p}
-operator algebra crossed product by a transfer operator, and we apply this construction to spectral analysis of weighted composition operators in the context of Cuntz-algebras. This generalizes the motivating example from
[
3
]
, to the case of Banach algebras and operators acting on
L^{p}
-spaces (rather than Hilbert spaces).

More specifically, let
(X,\varphi)
be a
topological Bernoulli shift
on the alphabet with
n
letters, i.e.
X:=\{1,...,n\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a Cantor space with product topology,
and
\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},...):=(x_{2},x_{3}...)
for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...)\in X
. We fix a
transfer operator
for
\varphi
, i.e. a positive operator
L:C(X)\to C(X)
acting on the space of continuous functions on
X
satisfying

L(b\cdot(a\circ\varphi))=L(b)a,\qquad\text{ for }a,b\in C(X).

Then it is necessarily a
Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius operator
of the form

L(a)(y)=\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\varrho(x)a(x),\qquad a\in C(X),

where
\varrho:X\to[0,+\infty)
is a continuous function. One refers to
\varrho
as to the
potential
of the system. We will assume that
\varrho>0
and
L
is unital and so

\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\varrho(x)=1
for all
x\in X
(such operators are algebraically characterized in
[
3
, Proposition 2.13]
). By
the Schauder-Tychonoff fixed point theorem, there is always a probability Borel measure
\mu
on
X
such that
L^{*}(\mu)=\mu
(when
\ln\varrho
is Hölder continuous, such
\mu
is unique and it is a
Gibbs measure
for
\varphi
and
\ln\varrho
). Then
\mu
is
\varphi
-invariant and its support is the whole
X
, see
[
3
, Proposition 3.8]
.
Therefore, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
the composition operator
T_{\varphi}f=f\circ\varphi
is an isometry on
L^{p}_{\mu}(X)

and we may identify elements of
C(X)
with operators of multiplication acting on
L^{p}_{\mu}(X)
.
We write
\pi:C(X)\to B(L^{p}_{\mu}(X))
for the corresponding isometric representation.
We fix
q\in(1,\infty]
with
1/p+1/q=1
(where
1/\infty=0
by convention)
and we put
X_{i}:=\{(i,x_{2},x_{3},...)\in X\}
for
i=1,...,n
.

Proposition 1
.

With the above notation,
S_{\varphi}f(y):=\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\varrho(x)f(x)
defines a contractive operator on
L^{p}_{\mu}(X)
such that

S_{\varphi}\pi(a)T_{\varphi}=\pi(L(a)),\ \ T_{\varphi}\pi(a)=\pi(a\circ\varphi)T_{\varphi},\qquad a\in C(X),

(1)

and operators
T_{i}=\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T_{\varphi}
and
S_{i}=S_{\varphi}\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})
form an
L^{p}
-Cuntz family.
The Banach algebra
B(\pi(C(X)),T_{\varphi},S_{\varphi})
generated by
\pi(C(X))
,
T_{\varphi}
,
S_{\varphi}
is the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
, i.e.
B(\pi(C(X)),T_{\varphi},S_{\varphi})=B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
.

Proof.

Using Jensen’s inequality and
L^{*}(\mu)=\mu
, for any
f\in L^{p}_{\mu}(X)
we get

\|S_{\varphi}f\|^{p}=\int_{X}\Big|\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\varrho(x)f(x)\Big|^{p}\,d\mu\leq\int_{X}\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(y)}\varrho(x)|f(x)|^{p}\,d\mu=\|f\|_{p}^{p}.

Hence
S_{\varphi}
is well defined and contractive. Now the displayed relations (
1
) are easily verified.
The restrictions
\varphi_{i}:=\varphi|_{X_{i}}:X_{i}\to X
,
i=1,...n
, are homeomorphisms and by
[
3
, Proposition 3.5]
we have

\varrho(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(x))=\frac{d\mu\circ\varphi^{-1}_{i}}{d\mu}(x),\qquad x\in X.

We use it to show that
T_{i}=\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T_{\varphi}
are isometries. For any
f\in L^{p}(\mu)
, using also abstract change of variables, we get

\displaystyle\|T_{i}f\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{i}}\varrho(x)^{-1}|f(\varphi_{i}(x))|^{p}d\mu=\int_{X_{i}}\varrho(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(x))^{-1}|f(x)|^{p}d\mu\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}|f(x)|^{p}\frac{d\mu}{d\mu\circ\varphi^{-1}_{i}}(x)d\mu\circ\varphi_{i}^{-1}=\|f\|^{p}.

Let us note that
S_{i}f(x)=\varrho(\varphi^{-1}_{i}(x))^{\frac{1}{p}}f(\varphi^{-1}_{i}(x))
. Therefore we readily get that

S_{i}T_{i}=1
and
T_{i}S_{i}=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})
. These are hermitian projections as mutliplication operators by real functions.
In particular,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}S_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X})=1
,
and
S_{i}
are contractions as for any
f\in L^{p}_{\mu}(X)
, using that
T_{i}
is an isometry, we get

\|S_{i}f\|_{p}=\|T_{i}S_{i}f\|_{p}=\|\mathds{1}_{X_{i}}f\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}.

Hence
T_{i},S_{i}
,
i=1,...,n
, form an
L^{p}
-Cuntz family and so
B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)\cong\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
.
Clearly, we have
B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)\subseteq B(\pi(C(X)),T_{\varphi},S_{\varphi})
, and thus we only need
to prove the reverse inclusion. We first show that
\pi(C(X))\subseteq B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
.
For any
(i_{1},...,i_{N})\in\{1,...,n\}^{N}
we have

T_{i_{1}}...T_{i_{N}}S_{i_{N}}...S_{i_{1}}=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}})\in B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n),

where

X_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}=\{(i_{1},...,i_{N},x_{N+1},...)\in X\}.

(2)

Since sets
X_{i_{1},...,i_{N}}
form a clopen base of
X
, the Stone–Weierstrass theorem implies that
\pi(C(X))\subseteq B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
.
Now we see that operators

T_{\varphi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\varrho^{\frac{1}{p}})T_{i},\qquad S_{\varphi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}\pi(\varrho^{\frac{1}{p}})

are in
B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
.
∎

The above proposition motivates the following definition.

Definition 2
.

A
covariant representation
of the transfer operator
L
on a space
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
for some measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
we mean a triple
(\pi,T,S)
where

\pi:C(X)\to B(L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega))
is a unital contractive homomorphism and
T,S\in B(L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega))
are contractive operators satisfying

S\pi(a)T=\pi(L(a)),\qquad T\pi(a)=\pi(a\circ\varphi)T,\qquad a\in C(X)

(3)

and such that
\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T
,
S\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})
,
i=1,...,n
, are contractive and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})TS\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})=1.

(4)

We denote by
B(\pi(C(X)),T,S)
the Banach subalgebra of
B(L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega))

generated by
\pi(C(X))
,
T
and
S
.

For
p=2
the above definition agrees with the one in
[
11
]
, see
[
13
]
,
[
4
]
and Remark
6
below.
For
p\neq 2
, as we explain in Section
2
, covariant representations are given by multiplication operators and weighted composition operators.
In Section
3
we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

For any (non-zero) covariant representation
(\pi,T,S)
of
L
on an
L^{p}
-space
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
,
p\in[1,\infty)
,
the operators
T_{i}=\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T
and
S_{i}=S_{\varphi}\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})
form an
L^{p}
-Cuntz family, and

B(\pi(C(X)),T,S)=B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)\cong\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}.

As an application we get the following description of spectra of
abstract weighted shift operators on
L^{p}
-spaces associated to the transfer operator
L
.
The proof is given in Section
4
.

Theorem 4
.

For any (non-zero) covariant representation
(\pi,T,S)
of
L
on an
L^{p}
-space
and for any
a\in C(X)
the spectrum of
\pi(a)T
is a disk

\sigma(\pi(a)T)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq r(\pi(a)T)\}

and the spectral radius is given by the variational principle

r(\pi(a)T)=\max_{\mu\in\Erg(X,\varphi)}\exp\left(\int_{X}\ln(|a|\varrho^{\frac{1}{p}})d\mu+\frac{h_{\varphi}(\mu)}{p}\right),

where
\Erg(X,\varphi)
is the set of all
\varphi
-ergodic measures on
X
, and
h_{\varphi}(\mu)
is the Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy of
\varphi
with respect to
\mu
.

Remark 5
.

For
p=1
the expression for
r(\pi(a)T)
is nothing but the topological pressure of
\varphi
with the potential
\ln(|a|\varrho)

For more about the history of such variational formulas and related results, see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
.

2.
Description of covariant represenations for
p\neq 2

Assume that
p\in[1,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
and let
(\pi,T,S)
is a covariant representation on
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
for a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
.
Without changing the Banach space
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
we may assume that the measure space is
localizable
, see
[
12
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
.
Then representation
\pi:C(X)\to B(L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega))
is necessarily given by
multiplication operators
,
see
[
5
, Theorem 2.12]
. That is, a unital
*
-homomorphism
\pi_{0}:C(X)\to L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Omega)
such that

(\pi(a)f)(\xi)=\pi_{0}(a)(\xi)f(\xi),\qquad\xi\in L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega),\,\,\omega\in\Omega.

We now describe the operators
T
and
S
as weighted composition operators.

By the first relation in (
3
) we get
ST=S\pi(1)T=\pi(L(1))=\pi(1)=1
. Since
S
and
T
are contractions, it follows that
T
is an isometry. (In particular,
S
and
T
are
partial isometries in the sense of Mbekhta
[
15
]
.) By the
Banach-Lamperti Theorem
, see
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
,
T
is necessarily a (generalized) weighted composition operator of the form
hT_{\Phi}
.
Namely, the formula
\Phi(A):=\text{supp}(T\mathds{1}_{A})
defines a set monomorphism
\Phi:\Sigma\to\Sigma
, i.e. identifying sets that differ by
\mu
-null sets, we have

1)

A\cap B=\emptyset\Rightarrow\Phi(A)\cap\Phi(B)=\emptyset
,

2)

\Phi\big(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\big)=\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Phi(A_{n})
,

3)

\mu(A)=0\iff\mu(\Phi(A))=0
,

where
A,B,A_{n}\in\Sigma
.
Then
\Phi(\Sigma)
is a
\sigma
-algebra and
\mu\circ\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(A)):=\mu(A)
defines a measure on
\Phi(\Sigma)
equivalent to
\mu|_{\Phi(\Sigma)}
.
Any set monomorphism gives rise to
a linear operator
T_{\Phi}
on the space of measurable functions where

T_{\Phi}\mathds{1}_{A}=\mathds{1}_{\Phi(A)},\qquad A\in\Sigma,

see
[
16
, Proposition 5.6]
. Moreover, there is a measurable function
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
, with
\text{supp}(h)=\Phi(\Omega)
, such that
T=hT_{\Phi}
, i.e.

T\xi(\omega)=h(\omega)T_{\Phi}(\xi)(\omega).\qquad\xi\in L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega),\,\,\omega\in\Omega.

Relation (
4
) implies that
\Phi(\Omega)=\Omega
and
\text{supp}(h)=\Omega
.
The function
h
satisfies

E^{\Phi(\Sigma)}(|h|^{p})=\frac{d\mu\circ\Phi^{-1}}{d\mu},

where
E^{\Phi(\Sigma)}
denotes the conditional expectation with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\Phi(\Sigma)\subseteq\Sigma
.
This condition characterises isometries among weighted composition operators. The Radon-Nikodym derivative exists because the measure space is localizable.

Now we turn to description of
S
. Note that
TS
is a contractive projection onto the range of
T
, that we denote by
M\subseteq L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
.
The
Ando Theorem
that describes contractive projections on
L^{p}
-spaces, see
[
1
, Theorem 2]
which generalizes to arbitrary measures by
[
7
]
. It implies that

TS\xi=\frac{h}{E^{\Phi(\Sigma)}(|h|^{p})}E^{\Phi(\Sigma)}\left(\frac{|h|^{p}}{h}\xi\right)

(for
p=1
we use the fact that
\Phi(\Omega)=\Omega
).
Denoting by
T^{-1}:M\to L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
the inverse of
T:L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)\to M
, we have
S=T^{-1}TS
.
Therefore

S\xi=\frac{d\mu\circ\Phi}{d\mu}T_{\Phi^{-1}}E^{\Phi(\Sigma)}\left(\frac{|h|^{p}}{h}\xi\right).

3.
Proof of Theorem
3

To show that operators
T_{i}=\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T
and
S_{i}=S_{\varphi}\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})
,
i=1,...,n
form a Cuntz family note that the relation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}S_{i}=1
is equivalent to (
3
). Furthermore, since
L(\varrho^{-1}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})=1
, we get

S_{i}T_{i}=S\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{q}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T=S\pi(\varrho^{-1}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T=L(\varrho^{-1}\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})=1.

Using that
\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i}})T_{j}=\delta_{i,j}T_{j}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}S_{i}=1
we obtain that

T_{i}S_{i}=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i}}).

Therefore
T_{i}S_{i}
are hermitian operators by
[
5
, Proposition 2.11]
.
Hence
T_{i},S_{i}
,
i=1,...,n
is an
L^{p}
-Cuntz family and therefore
B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)\cong\mathcal{O}^{p}_{n}
.

It remains to prove that
B(S_{i},T_{i}:i=1,...,n)=B(\pi(C(X)),T,S)
. The inclusion
\subseteq
is obvious.
We prove the reverse inclusion. Recall the notation (
2
).
Using the relation
Ta=(a\circ\varphi)T
,
a\in C(X)
, we get

T_{i_{1}}T_{i_{2}}S_{i_{2}}S_{i_{1}}=T_{i_{1}}\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{2}}})S_{i_{1}}=\pi(\mathds{1}_{\varphi^{-1}(X_{i_{2}})})\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{1}}})=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{1}i_{2}}}).

Proceeding by induction one gets that
T_{i_{1}}...T_{i_{N}}S_{i_{N}}...S_{i_{1}}=\pi(\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{1}...i_{N}}})
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
i_{1},...,i_{N}\in\{1,...,n\}
.
Since functions of the form
\mathds{1}_{X_{i_{1}...i_{N}}}
are linearly dense in
C(X)
, we obtain that
\pi(C(X))\subseteq B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)
.
Thus we also get

T=\pi(\varrho^{\frac{1}{p}})\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i},\,\,S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}\pi(\varrho^{\frac{1}{q}})\in B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n).

Therefore
B(\pi(C(X)),T,S)=B(T_{i},S_{i}:i=1,...,n)\cong\mathcal{O}^{p}_{n}
.

Remark 6
.

If
p=2
, so that
L^{2}_{\mu}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space, then
S=T^{*}
and
S_{i}=T_{i}^{*}
for
i=1,...,n
, by
[
15
, Corollary 3.3]
.
Thus the relations we consider become the standard ones used in the context of
C^{*}
-algebras.

Remark 7
.

Assume
p\neq 2
. It follows from the considerations in this and previous section, it follows that putting
\Omega_{i}=\text{supp}(\pi_{0}(\mathds{1}_{X_{i}}))
,
i=1,...,n
,
we get a decomposition
\Omega:=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}
of
\Omega
and
each
T_{i}:L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)\to L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega_{i})
is an invertible isometry whose inverse is given by

S_{i}
. Moreover, putting

\Phi_{i}(A):=\Phi(A)\cap\Omega_{i},\qquad A\in\Sigma,

\Phi_{i}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
is a set monomorphism with
\Phi_{i}(\Sigma):=\{A\in\Sigma:A\subseteq\Omega_{i}\}
,
and

T_{i}=\pi(\varrho^{-\frac{1}{p}})hT_{\Phi_{i}},\qquad S_{i}=T_{\Phi_{i}^{-1}}\pi(\varrho^{\frac{1}{p}})h^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{i}}

are spatial partial isometries as introduced in
[

]
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
4

Let
(\pi,T,S)
be a covariant representation of
L
on the space
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
for a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
. Let
a\in C(X)
. We show first that the spectrum of
\pi(a)T
is invariant under rotation around zero. As we proved in Theorem
3
, the algebra
B(\pi(C(X)),S,T)
is isomorphic with
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
. Phillips showed in
[
17
, Proposition 5.9]
that
\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p}
has a gauge circle action
\tau:\mathbb{T}\to\text{Aut}(\mathcal{O}_{n}^{p})
, which in therms of
\pi(C(X)),S,T
is determined by the following relations

\tau_{z}|_{\pi(C(X))}=\text{id}|_{\pi(C(X))},\quad\tau_{z}(T)=zT,\quad\tau_{z}(S)=z^{-1}S,\qquad z\in\mathbb{T}.

Now suppose that
\lambda
is in the resolvent set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(\pi(a)T)
. Then for any
z\in\mathbb{T}
the operator
\tau_{z}(\pi(a)T-\lambda\mathds{1})
is invertible and since

\tau_{z}(\pi(a)T-\lambda\mathds{1})=\pi(a)zT-\lambda\mathds{1}=z(\pi(a)T-z^{-1}\lambda\mathds{1})

it follows that
\pi(a)T-z^{-1}\lambda\mathds{1}
is invertible. Therefore
z^{-1}\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(\pi(a)T)
, which means that
\sigma(\pi(a)T)
is invariant under rotation around zero. It implies
\sigma(\pi(a)T)=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}D(r_{i},R_{i})
where
D(r_{i},R_{i})=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:r_{i}\leq|z|\leq R_{i}\}
.
By
[
3
, Proposition 3.3]
(the argument works in
L^{p}
-case) the operator
\pi(a)T
is not invertible and so
0\in\sigma(\pi(a)T)
.
To prove that
\sigma(\pi(a)T)
is a disc it is sufficient to show that
\sigma(\pi(a)T)
is connected.
To do this we use Riesz projections. Assume that on the contrary that
\sigma(\pi(a)T)
contains

D(r,R)
where
0<r\leq R
. By scaling
\pi(a)T
if necessary, we may assume that
1<r
and
\sigma(\pi(a)T)\cap\mathbb{T}=\emptyset
. The
Riesz projection
of
\pi(a)T
corresponding to the unit disk is given by

P=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\pi(a)T-z\mathds{1})^{-1}dz.

By construction
P
is a bounded idempotent, i.e.
P^{2}=P
, such that:

1)

P
commutes with
