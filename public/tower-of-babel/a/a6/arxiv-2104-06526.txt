Permutohedral Complexes and Rational Curves with Cyclic Action
Emily Clader, Chiara Damiolini, Daoji Huang, Shiyue Li, Rohini Ramadas

Abstract
. We define a moduli space of rational curves with finite-order automorphism and weighted orbits, and we prove that the combinatorics of its boundary strata are encoded by a particular polytopal complex that also captures the algebraic structure of a complex reflection group acting on the moduli space. This generalizes the situation for Losev–Manin’s moduli space of curves (whose boundary strata are encoded by the permutohedron and related to the symmetric group) as well as the situation for Batyrev–Blume’s moduli space of curves with involution, and it extends that work beyond the toric context.



1.
Introduction

The moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
of genus-zero stable curves with
n
distinct marked points is a fundamental object in algebraic geometry, in part due to its applicability—to such fields as enumerative geometry, representation theory, and mathematical physics, to name a few—but also because it is an interesting variety in its own right. In particular, while
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is not toric when
n\geq 5
, it shares some of the combinatorial structure that a toric variety would enjoy. The Chow ring of a toric variety, for example, is generated by the toric boundary (the positive-codimension torus-invariant subvarieties) with relations described combinatorially in terms of fan data; analogously, Keel proved in
[
Kee92
]
that the Chow ring of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is generated by the modular boundary (the positive-codimension boundary strata) with relations described combinatorially in terms of dual graphs.

One perspective on the close connection between
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
and toric varieties is that the moduli problem can be tweaked to produce a space that is, in fact, toric. Losev and Manin studied a particularly significant such modification in
[
LM00
]
, constructing a moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
that parameterizes genus-zero curves with marked points
(y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
, where the marked points
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are “heavy”—that is, they cannot coincide with any other marked points—whereas the marked points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are “light” in the sense that they are allowed to coincide with one another. The space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
(which is birational to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n-1}
) is a toric variety, and its associated polytope is the permutohedron
\Pi_{n}
: the convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the
n!
points obtained by permuting the coordinates of
(1,2,\ldots,n)
. Moreover, Losev and Manin proved that the torus-invariant strata of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
are precisely the boundary strata, which implies that there is a dimension-preserving, inclusion-preserving bijection

\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{boundary }\\
\textstyle\text{strata in }\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{faces}\\
\textstyle\text{ of }\Pi_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}.

From a combinatorial
perspective, on the other hand, the faces of
\Pi_{n}
have another interpretation: they encode the generation of the symmetric group
S_{n}
by adjacent transpositions. Namely, the
d
-dimensional faces of
\Pi_{n}
are in inclusion-preserving bijection with the right cosets in
S_{n}
of subgroups of the form

\langle\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d}\rangle\subseteq S_{n},

where
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d}
are adjacent transpositions.

Batyrev and Blume extended the work of Losev and Manin in
[
BB11a
,
BB11b
]
, constructing a moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{2}_{n}
that parameterizes genus-zero curves with an involution
\sigma
, two light fixed points of
\sigma
, one heavy marked orbit of
\sigma
, and
n
light marked orbits. Again, this moduli space is toric, and its torus-invariant strata are precisely the boundary strata, so one obtains a dimension-preserving, inclusion-preserving bijection

\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{boundary }\\
\textstyle\text{strata in }\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{2}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{faces}\\
\textstyle\text{ of }\Delta^{2}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}.

Here,
\Delta^{2}_{n}
is the polytope known as the type-
B
permutohedron, which is the convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the
2^{n}n!
points obtained by permuting the coordinates of
(\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm n)
. Also analogously to the Losev–Manin case, this polytope has a group-theoretic interpretation, this time in terms of the complex reflection group
S(2,n)
of
n\times n
matrices all of whose nonzero entries are
\pm 1
, and with exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column.

The motivation for Batyrev and Blume’s work comes from the theory of root systems. Specifically, they constructed a toric variety associated to any root system and proved
that Losev–Manin space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the toric variety associated to the classical root system
A_{n-1}
, while working instead with the root system
B_{n}
yields their moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{2}_{n}
. From the
perspective of root systems, however, this seems to be the end of the line: Batyrev and Blume proved that the toric varieties in types
C
and
D
do not have equally well-behaved modular interpretations.

In this paper, we propose a generalization of Losev–Manin and Batyrev–Blume’s story in a different direction. Namely, rather than preserve the connection to root systems, we preserve from Batyrev–Blume’s work the existence of an automorphism
\sigma
but allow it to have any finite order
r
. The result is a moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
that parameterizes genus-zero curves with an order-
r
automorphism, two light fixed points, one heavy marked orbit, and
n
light marked orbits.

In one sense, the moduli spaces
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
break the story, because when
r\geq 3
, they are not toric. In particular, then, their boundary strata are not encoded by the faces of a polytope. However, something perhaps more intriguing is true: we prove that there exists a polytopal complex
\Delta^{r}_{n}
whose “
\Delta
-faces” encode the boundary strata of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
. More specifically,
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is defined as the subset of

\Big(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\cdot\mu_{r}\Big)^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}

bounded by certain hyperplanes (where
\mu_{r}
denotes the group of
r
th roots of unity), and its
\Delta
-faces are defined as the intersections of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
with collections of the bounding hyperplanes. When
r=2
, the complex
\Delta^{r}_{n}
specializes to the type-
B
permutohedron
\Delta^{2}_{n}
and
\Delta
-faces are ordinary faces, so Batyrev–Blume’s result is recovered.

Furthermore, we generalize the group-theoretic interpretation of both Losev–Manin and Batyrev–Blume’s moduli spaces. Namely, let
S(r,n)
be the group of
n\times n
matrices all of whose nonzero entries are
r
th roots of unity, and with exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column. Then
S(r,n)
is generated by the set

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{adjacent transpositions}\\
\textstyle\text{ in }S_{n}\subseteq S(r,n)\end{subarray}\right\}\cup\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\zeta&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
&&&\ddots&\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right)\right\},

where
\zeta
is a primitive
r
th root of unity, and we define a
d
-dimensional
\mathcal{T}
-coset
to be a right coset of the form

\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\rangle\cdot A\subseteq S(r,n)

with
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathcal{T}
and
A\in S(r,n)
.

Our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any integers
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
, there are dimension-preserving, inclusion-preserving bijections

\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{boundary }\\
\textstyle\text{strata in }\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\mathcal{T}\text{-cosets}\\
\textstyle\text{ in }S(r,n)\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\Delta\text{-faces}\\
\textstyle\text{ of }\Delta^{r}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}.

Remark 1.2
.

In fact, these bijections preserve quite a bit more of the structure of the above three types of objects. In particular, we show that there are product decompositions of boundary strata,
\mathcal{T}
-cosets, and
\Delta
-faces, as well as
S(r,n)
-actions on each, and the bijections of Theorem
1.1
respect these. See Section
8
for the details.

When
r=2
, Theorem
1.1
specializes to the results of Batyrev and Blume. For larger values of
r
, the theorem indicates that the spaces
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
are unions of toric varieties with independent torus actions, and in this way they occupy a middle ground between toric varieties and the classical moduli spaces of genus-zero curves—a setting in which the applicability of polyhedral methods is an intriguing new avenue for study.

1.1
with two special cases, to give the reader a flavor of the combinatorics involved.

with vertices

, and the combinatorial content of Theorem

is that we can label the faces of

.

Future and related work

In future work, we hope to probe further geometric and combinatorial properties of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
to more fully exploit its proximity to being a toric variety. For example, one could study the tropical manifestation of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{n}_{r}
(along the lines of the work carried out in
[
CGP21
,
Uli15
,
CHMR16
]
for
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
and Hassett spaces), its symmetries (along the lines of
[
Mas14
,
MM17
]
), or the combinatorial structure of its Chow ring (along the lines of
[
Kee92
,
BM13
,
BM14
,
KKL21
]
). The birational geometry of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
would also be very interesting to study. In the case of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
, the parallelism with toric varieties motivated Fulton’s famous F-conjecture as well as the (now disproven) conjecture that the Cox ring of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is finitely-generated
[
CT15
,
GK16
]
. Perhaps the fact that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
is “more toric” than
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
—in that it is combinatorially encoded by a polyhedral object—would make these birational-geometric questions more amenable to study in this setting.

2.
The moduli space

Fix integers
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
. Denote by
\mu_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{*}
the cyclic group of
r
th roots of unity, and denote
\mathbb{Z}_{r}=\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}
.

2.1.
Objects and families

We begin by specifying the underlying curves of the objects we are interested in parameterizing. Throughout, varieties are considered over the field
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
r
-pinwheel curve
is a tree of projective lines meeting at nodes, consisting of a central projective line from which
r
equal-length chains of projective lines (“spokes”) emanate. If each of these spokes has
k
components, we say that the pinwheel curve has
length

k
; in the case where
k=0
, the curve is simply
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

The objects of our moduli space are built from
r
-pinwheel curves as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

An
(r,n)
-curve
consists of the following data:

•

an
r
-pinwheel curve
C
;

•

an order-
r
automorphism
\sigma:C\rightarrow C
;

•

two distinct fixed points
x^{+}
and
x^{-}
of
\sigma
;

•

n
labeled
r
-tuples
(z_{1}^{0},\ldots,z_{1}^{r-1}),\ldots,(z_{n}^{0},\ldots,z_{n}^{r-1})
of points
z_{i}^{j}\in C
satisfying

\sigma(z_{i}^{j})=z_{i}^{j+1\!\!\!\!\mod r}

for each
i
and
j
, where we allow that
z_{i}^{j}=z_{i^{\prime}}^{j^{\prime}}
and that
z_{i}^{j}=x^{\pm}
;

•

an additional labeled
r
-tuple
(y^{0},\ldots,y^{r-1})
satisfying

\sigma(y^{\ell})=y^{\ell+1\!\!\!\!\mod r}

for each
\ell
, whose elements are distinct from one another as well as from
x^{\pm}
and
z_{i}^{j}
.

We refer to an
(r,n)
-curve as
stable
if each irreducible component of
C
contains at least two “heavy” points—where the “heavy” points are the half-nodes and the points
y^{\ell}
—and any irreducible component with exactly two heavy points contains at least one of the “light” points
x^{\pm}
or
z_{i}^{j}
. (This is a special case of the stability condition for Hassett spaces, which will play a major role in the construction of the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
in Section
3
below.)

It is straightforward to see that the stability condition forces
y^{0},\ldots,y^{r-1}
to lie on the
r
leaves of the pinwheel curve, in which case
\sigma
must consist of a rotation taking each spoke of the pinwheel to another, and
x^{\pm}
must both lie on the central component, which we denote
C_{\bullet}
; see Figure
3
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let

Remark 2.4
.

A stable
(r,n)
-curve of type
\zeta
can be viewed as a curve with an action of the cyclic group
\mu_{r}
, in which the generator
\zeta\in\mu_{r}
acts by the automorphism
\sigma
.

Having defined the objects of interest, we now specify the notions of family and morphism of families.

Definition 2.5
.

A
family of stable
(r,n)
-curves over a base scheme
B
is a flat, proper morphism
\pi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow B
equipped with an order-
r
automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
\pi\circ\sigma=\pi
, and sections
x^{\pm},\{z_{i}^{j}\}
, and
\{y^{\ell}\}
of
\pi
such that for any geometric point
b\in B
, the fiber

\bigg(\pi^{-1}(b);\sigma\big|_{\pi^{-1}(b)};x^{\pm}(b),\{y^{\ell}(b)\},\{z_{i}^{j}(b)\}\bigg)

is a stable
(r,n)
-curve. If, furthermore, each fiber has type
\zeta
, we say that the family has type
\zeta
.

Remark 2.6
.

If the base
B
is connected, then every fiber has the same type, so the type of the family can be deduced from considering any single fiber.

Definition 2.7
.

Given families

(\pi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow B;\sigma;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})\,\text{ and }\,(\pi^{\prime}:\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\rightarrow B;\sigma^{\prime};X^{\pm},\{Y^{\ell}\},\{Z_{i}^{j}\})

over the same base
B
, a
morphism
of families is a morphism
s:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
satisfying

•

\pi^{\prime}\circ s=\pi
;

•

\sigma^{\prime}\circ s=s\circ\sigma
;

•

X^{\pm}=s\circ x^{\pm}
,
Z_{i}^{j}=s\circ z_{i}^{j}
, and
Y^{\ell}=s\circ y^{\ell}
.

The goal of Section
3
is to identify a fine moduli space representing the moduli problem specified above, which we denote as follows.

Definition 2.8
.

For any integers
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
, we denote by
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
the moduli space of isomorphism classes of stable
(r,n)
-curves, and we denote by
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{\zeta}\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
the moduli space of all isomorphism classes of stable
(r,n)
-curves.

The reader willing to accept the existence of such a fine moduli space may wish to skip Section
3
entirely and proceed directly to the combinatorics in Section
4
, and they are encouraged to do so. First, however, we must describe the boundary strata in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
, which are critical to the combinatorics that follow.

2.2.
Boundary strata

In any moduli space of curves, a boundary stratum is defined as the closure of the locus of curves of a fixed topological type. More precisely, we have the following definition of boundary strata in our case.

Definition 2.9
.

Any
(C;\sigma;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
has an associated
dual graph
consisting of

•

a vertex
v
for each irreducible component
C_{v}
of
C
;

•

an edge between vertices
v
and
w
if the corresponding irreducible components
C_{v}
and
C_{w}
meet at a node;

•

a half-edge attached to the vertex
v
for each marked point on
C_{v}
, labeled by the name
x^{\pm},y^{\ell}
, or
z_{i}^{j}
of the marked point.

Given such a dual graph
G
, the corresponding
boundary stratum

S_{G}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
is defined as the closure of the set of curves with dual graph
G
.

In particular, passing to the closure means that
S_{G}
includes also degenerations of curves with dual graph
G
; for example, see Figure
4
. It follows that one can detect in terms of dual graphs when there is an inclusion of boundary strata: we have
S_{G}\subseteq S_{H}
if and only if
H
can be obtained from
G
by edge-contraction of some subset of the edges, a procedure that corresponds geometrically to degeneration of a curve in
S_{H}
to a curve in
S_{G}
. For example, if
G
is the top dual graph in Figure
4
and
G_{1}

3.
Construction of the moduli space

We construct the moduli space of stable
(r,n)
-curves as a closed subscheme of a more well-known moduli space constructed by Hassett in
[

, so we begin by recalling the necessary definitions from the theory of Hassett spaces. Throughout, we denote

[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

and

[n]_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}.

3.1.
Hassett spaces and maps between them

For any
g\geq 0
,
n\geq 0
and any weight vector
{\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in(\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1])^{n}}
such that
2g+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}>2
, the associated Hassett space is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,\mathbf{w}}
that is an alternate modular compactification of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
of smooth projective curves of genus
g
with
n
distinct marked points.

Specifically, elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,\mathbf{w}}
are tuples
(C;q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
, where
C
is a projective curve of arithmetic genus
g
and only nodes as singularities, and
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\in C
are marked points with weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
, satisfying the following two conditions:

•

the sum of the weights of any collection of coinciding marked points is at most
1
;

•

for each irreducible component
D
of
C
, if
\{q_{i}\}_{i\in I_{D}}
for
I_{D}\subseteq[n]
are the marked points of
D
and
n_{D}
is the number of half-nodes in
D
, then

2g-2+n_{D}+\sum_{i\in I_{D}}w_{i}>0.

We refer to elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,\mathbf{w}}
as
\mathbf{w}
-stable curves
. Note that the usual moduli space of curves
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
is recovered by taking
w_{i}=1
for all
i\in[n]
.

The special case of this construction that is relevant for the current work is when
g=0
and the weight vector is

(1)

\mathbf{w}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon,\underset{\text{\footnotesize$r$}}{\underbrace{1,\dots,1}},\underset{\text{\footnotesize$nr$}}{\underbrace{\varepsilon,\dots,\varepsilon}}\right)

for any fixed

denote the Hassett space with this weight vector, which is a smooth projective scheme. Let

Lemma 3.2
.

If
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
,
then there exists a unique automorphism
\sigma
of
C
making
(C;\sigma;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})
into a stable
(r,n)
-curve of type
\zeta
, and the elements of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
are precisely the elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
for which such an automorphism exists.

Proof.

Fix an element
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
, and let
C_{\bullet}
be the component of
C
containing
x^{+}
. Then each
y^{\ell}
lies on some (possibly empty) tree of projective lines attached to
C_{\bullet}
at a point
p^{\ell}
, and
x^{-}
lies on some (possibly empty) tree of projective lines attached to
C_{\bullet}
at a point
p^{-}
.

A priori, some of these trees could coincide with one another. However, they cannot all be identical—that is,
x^{-},y^{0},\ldots,y^{r-1}
cannot all lie on a single tree emanating from
C_{\bullet}
—since this would force the only special points on
C_{\bullet}
to be a single node, the marked point
x^{+}
, and possibly some of the
z_{i}^{j}
. With the weights (
1
), such a curve cannot be stable.

Thus, there must be at least one
y^{\ell}
on a different tree than
x^{-}
. In this case, we can choose an automorphism
s
of
C_{\bullet}
such that

s(x^{+})=\infty,\;\;s(p^{-})=0,\;\;s(p^{\ell})=1,

and the condition (
5
) ensures that

s(p^{\ell+1})=\zeta.

(In particular, note that this means that the
y^{\ell+1}
-tree is distinct from both the
y^{\ell}
-tree and the
x^{-}
-tree.) Applying (
5
) again with
\ell
replaced by
\ell+1
shows that

s(p^{\ell+2})=\zeta^{2}.

Continuing in this way proves that
x^{-}
and the
y^{\ell}
’s lie on
r+1
distinct trees emanating from
C_{\bullet}
, attached (under the automorphism
s
) at
0
and roots of unity.

Each such tree must end in one or more leaves. However, the weights (
1
) ensure that a leaf must either contain at least one
y^{\ell}
or both of
x^{+}
and
x^{-}
. Therefore, the
x^{-}
-tree must be empty (that is,
x^{-}\in C_{\bullet}
) and each of the
y^{\ell}
-trees must be a chain of projective lines ending in a single leaf with
y^{\ell}
. What remains to be shown, then, is that each of these

.

Remark 3.4
.

The notion of compatibility satisfies the following properties:

(1)

If
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})
is compatible with
\alpha
, then
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})
is compatible with

for all
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

(2)

If
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z_{i}^{j}\})
is compatible with
\alpha
, then so are all other
(r,n)
-curves with the same dual graph.

(3)

Let
G
and
H
be dual graphs of
(r,n)
-curves such that
H
is obtained via edge-contraction from
G
. If

\alpha
is compatible with

(r,n)
-curves with dual graph

G
, then it is also compatible
with
(r,n)
-curves with dual
graph
H
. In particular, a smooth
(r,n)
-curve is compatible with every
\alpha:[n]_{0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

Remark
3.4
implies that, for fixed
\alpha:[n]_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, the locus of curves not compatible with
\alpha
is a union of boundary strata. Conversely, the locus of curves that are compatible with
\alpha
forms an open set, which we denote by

(6)

in what follows.

3.3.
Proof of fine moduli space

At this point, we have shown via Lemma
3.2

\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
are in bijection with stable
(r,n)
-curves of type
\zeta
. In order to know that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
is a fine moduli space for these objects, though, we must also construct a universal family. This can nearly be bootstrapped from
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}

\iota:\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}

denotes the inclusion, then we can define a universal curve

\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)

\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
by

(7)

\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta):=\iota^{*}\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n},

where
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}
is the universal curve over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
. Furthermore, we can define sections
x^{\pm},y^{\ell}
, and
z^{i}_{j}
of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
by pullback of the corresponding sections of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}
. What remains, however, is to construct a universal automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
. This is the key content of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
be integers, and let
\zeta
be a primitive
r
th root of unity. Then
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
is a fine moduli space for stable
(r,n)
-curves of type
\zeta
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
and its sections
x^{\pm},y^{\ell}
, and
z^{i}_{j}
be defined as in (
7
) and the subsequent paragraph. To prove the theorem, it suffices to construct an automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
such that

(8)

\sigma\circ x^{+}=x^{+},\;\;\sigma\circ x^{-}=x^{-},\;\;\sigma\circ y^{\ell}=y^{\ell+1},\;\;\text{ and }\sigma\circ z_{i}^{j}=z_{i}^{j+1}

for all
i\in[n]
and all
\ell,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. Indeed, if such an automorphism
\sigma
exists, then it makes
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
into a family of stable
(r,n)
-curves, and this family is of type
\zeta
by Lemma
3.2
. It follows that, for any base scheme
B
, one can restrict the bijection

(9)

\{\text{morphisms }B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}\}\leftrightarrow\{\text{families of }\mathbf{w}\text{-stable curves over }B\}/\cong,

which exists by virtue of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
being a fine moduli space for
\mathbf{w}
-stable curves, to yield

(10)

\displaystyle\{\text{morphisms }B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\{\text{families of stable }(r,n)\text{-curves of type }\zeta\text{ over }B\}/\cong

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f^{*}\Big(\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)\Big).

Because it is the restriction of a bijection, the map in (
10
) is certainly injective. It is also surjective, because a family of stable, type-
\zeta

(r,n)
-curves over
B
yields a family of
\mathbf{w}
-stable curves by forgetting
\sigma
, and the latter is the pullback of the universal family on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
under some
f:B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
by the surjectivity of (
9
). The fact that each fiber of the family is in fact a stable
(r,n)
-curve of type
\zeta
implies, by Lemma
3.2
, that
f
lands in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
. Thus, the map in (
10
) is a bijection, which says precisely that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
is the requisite fine moduli space.

To construct the automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
, we patch together automorphisms defined locally on open subsets of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
. Toward defining these open sets, let
\alpha:[n]_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
be any function, and let
U_{\alpha}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
be as in (
6
). For any
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, consider the morphism

\pi_{\alpha+b}\big|_{U_{\alpha}}:U_{\alpha}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}.

Geometrically, this morphism leaves the
y^{\alpha(0)+b}
-spoke unchanged but contracts all other spokes. In particular, since each individual spoke is isomorphic to each other spoke, we see that

\pi_{\alpha+b}\big|_{U_{\alpha}}=\pi_{\alpha+b^{\prime}}\big|_{U_{\alpha}}

for any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
.

Now, let
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
be the restriction of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
to
U_{\alpha}
. Each of the morphisms
\pi_{\alpha+b}\big|_{U_{\alpha}}
can be lifted to a birational morphism

\psi_{\alpha+b}:\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\rightarrow\pi_{\alpha+b}^{*}(\mathcal{C}^{1}_{n})

as in (
2
), which performs the above contraction of all but the
y^{\alpha(0)+b}
spoke on each fiber of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
. Note that since the particular spokes being contracted depend on
b
, we have

\psi_{\alpha+b}\neq\psi_{\alpha+b^{\prime}}

if
b\neq b^{\prime}
.

For any
\alpha:[n]_{0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}
and any
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, denote by

V_{\alpha,b}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}

the largest open subset of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
on which the birational morphism
\psi_{\alpha+b}
is an isomorphism. Geometrically,
V_{\alpha,b}
is obtained from
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
by removing all spokes in each fiber except for the
y^{\alpha(0)+b}
-spoke, including removing the points in the central component of the fiber at which these spokes are attached. In particular, from this geometric description we see that
\{V_{\alpha,b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}}
covers
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
, and therefore varying over all
\alpha
, we obtain an open cover of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
.

Our goal, now, is to construct an isomorphism

\sigma_{\alpha,b}\colon V_{\alpha,b}\to V_{\alpha,b+1}

relative to
U_{\alpha}
, which will serve as the local definition of the universal automorphism
\sigma
. The key observation is that

(11)

\psi_{\alpha+b}(V_{\alpha,b})=\psi_{\alpha+b+1}(V_{\alpha,b+1})

for every
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, from which it follows that
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
can be defined as the composition of the two isomorphisms

(12)

V_{\alpha,b}\xrightarrow{\psi_{\alpha+b}|_{V_{\alpha,b}}}\psi_{\alpha+b}(V_{\alpha,b})=\psi_{\alpha+b+1}(V_{\alpha,b+1})\xrightarrow{(\psi_{\alpha+b+1}|_{V_{\alpha,b+1}})^{-1}}V_{\alpha,b+1}.

To prove (
11
), first observe that, by the definition of the maps
\psi_{\alpha+b}
and
\pi_{\alpha}
, we have

(13)

\psi_{\alpha+b}(V_{\alpha,b})=\psi_{\alpha+b}(\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\text{sm}})\cup\left(\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathcal{C}^{1}_{n})\setminus\bigcup_{\ell\neq 0}\text{im}\left(\psi_{\alpha+b}\circ y^{\alpha(0)+b+\ell}\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\text{sm}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
is the open set whose fibers are smooth curves. For any
\ell\neq 0
, let us describe the morphism

\psi_{\alpha+b}\circ y^{\alpha(0)+b+\ell}:U_{\alpha}\rightarrow\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathcal{C}^{1}_{n})

in geometric terms, by describing its behavior on closed points. Let
C
be a closed point of
U_{\alpha}
, which corresponds to a marked curve
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z^{j}_{i}\})
. The image of
C
under
\pi_{\alpha}
is a point that corresponds to a marked curve
C^{\prime}
, and the image of
C
under
\psi_{\alpha+b}\circ y^{\alpha(0)+b+\ell}
is a closed point of
C^{\prime}
that we will denote
q^{b,\ell}
. By definition,
C^{\prime}
is a chain of projective lines with
x^{\pm}
together on an end component that we denote by
C_{\bullet}
. In this notation, we have
q^{b,\ell}\in C_{\bullet}
, and if we denote the node of
C_{\bullet}
by
p
, then (
5
) implies

(C_{\bullet};x^{+},x^{-},p,q^{b,\ell})\cong(\mathbb{P}^{1};\infty,0,1,\zeta^{\ell}).

Since this result does not depend on
b
, we see that the morphism
\psi_{\alpha+b}\circ y^{\alpha(0)+b+\ell}
is independent of
b
for any
\ell\neq 0
. Given that
\psi_{\alpha+b}(\mathcal{C}^{\text{sm}}_{\alpha})
is also manifestly independent of
b
, it follows from (
13
) that
\psi_{\alpha+b}(V_{\alpha,b})
is independent of
b
, which proves (
11
).

Lastly, we glue the local morphisms
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
defined by (
12
) to give a global morphism

\sigma:\mathcal{C}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)\to\mathcal{C}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)

over the base
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)
. To see that the morphisms
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
indeed agree on the overlaps in their domain, we restrict to fibers of
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)
and describe the maps
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
on closed points. Given any
(C;x^{\pm},\{y^{\ell}\},\{z^{j}_{i}\})\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)
, let us also denote by
C\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}^{r}(\zeta)
the corresponding fiber of the universal curve. By Lemma
3.2
,
C
is an
(r,n)
-curve; we denote its central component by
C_{\bullet}
as usual, and we denote by
C(b)
the sub-curve obtained by taking the union of
C_{\bullet}
and the spoke containing
y^{b}
. Suppose
\alpha:[n]_{0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{r}
is compatible with
C
. Setting

(14)

\displaystyle C^{\circ}(b):=C(b)\cap V_{\alpha,b}=C(b)\setminus\bigcup_{b^{\prime}\neq b}C(b^{\prime}),

we observe that
\sigma_{\alpha,b}|_{C^{\circ}(b)}
defines an isomorphism onto
C^{\circ}(b+1)
that fixes
x^{\pm}
, takes
y^{\alpha(0)}
to
y^{\alpha(0)+1}
, and takes
z_{i}^{\alpha(i)}
to
z_{i}^{\alpha(i)+1}
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, this forces
\sigma_{\alpha,b}|_{C_{\bullet}\cap C^{\circ}(b)}
to be rotation by
\zeta
, once coordinates are chosen on
C_{\bullet}
such that
x_{+}=\infty
,
x_{-}=0
, and the node connecting
C_{\bullet}
to
y^{\alpha(0)}
is
1
. This shows that
\sigma_{\alpha,b}|_{C^{\circ}(b)}
agrees with
\tau|_{C^{\circ}(b)}
, where
\tau
is the unique automorphism of
C
making it into an
(r,n)
-curve of type
\zeta
. In particular, because this description of
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
depends only on the fiber of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
and not on
\alpha
or
b
, the local morphisms
\sigma_{\alpha,b}
indeed glue to give a global automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{C}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
.

Having equipped the universal curve with a universal automorphism
\sigma
, which (by the argument of the previous paragraph) makes it into a family of
(r,n)
-curves of type
\zeta
, the proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

3.4.
Geometric observations

We conclude this section with some geometric observations about the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
. Since these are not needed for the current work, we give only brief indications of the proofs.

Observation 3.6
.

The moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
is smooth, and its boundary (the union of the positive-codimension boundary strata) is a simple normal crossings divisor.

To prove Observation
3.6
, one can leverage the analogous result for the Hassett space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
. In particular, one can show that the morphisms

\pi_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}}:U_{\alpha}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}

are isomorphisms of
U_{\alpha}
onto an open set
U\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
. (Specifically, expressing elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
as
(C;x^{\pm},y,\{z_{i}\})
as in Remark
3.1
, let
C_{\bullet}
be the component containing
x^{\pm}
. Then, after choosing coordinates on
C_{\bullet}
in which
x^{+}=\infty
,
x^{-}=0
, and the half-node of
C_{\bullet}
is
1
, the open set
U\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
consists of curves for which none of the light points
z_{i}
lies at an
r
th root of unity in
C_{\bullet}
.) The local isomorphisms
\pi_{\alpha}
take the boundary stratification of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
to the boundary stratification of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
, so Observation
3.6
follows from the analogous statement for Hassett spaces, which is shown in
[
Has03
]
.

In addition to
\pi_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}}
, there is another natural map from
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
: rather than remembering a single spoke of an
(r,n)
-curve as
\pi_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}}
does, one can identify all
r
spokes with each other. This leads to the following observation.

Observation 3.7
.

There exists a surjective morphism

p:\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}

that sends an
(r,n)
-curve
C
with automorphism
\sigma
to the quotient
C/\sigma
, and
p
realizes
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
as the quotient
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)/(\mathbb{Z}_{r})^{n}
.

This relates
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
with moduli spaces parameterizing coverings of rational curves with marked points: it provides a compactification of the moduli space of coverings with marked orbits that is related to the Harris–Mumford admissible covers spaces
[
HM82
]
in the same way in which Hassett spaces are related to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
. Other works in this direction can be found in
[
Deo14
]
, where a different choice of weights is made that allows ramification points to collide.

Both
\pi_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}}
and
p
can be interpreted from a polytopal perspective, using that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{n}
is a toric variety (and hence has an associated polytope) whereas
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
, as we will see below, has an associated polytopal complex. We return to this in Remark
7.14
.

4.
Decorated nested chains

Having constructed the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
of stable
(r,n)
-curves, we begin the combinatorial heart of the paper: proving that the same combinatorics encodes the boundary strata in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
, the
\mathcal{T}
-cosets in the complex reflection group
S(r,n)
, and the
\Delta
-faces of the polytopal complex
\Delta^{r}_{n}
. The key idea that yields the correspondence between these three types of objects is that all three can be indexed by the discrete data of decorated nested chains, which we now define.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
. A
decorated nested chain of subsets of
[n]
(or simply
chain
, for short) is a tuple

\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a}),

where

\emptyset=I_{0}\subsetneq I_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq I_{k}\subseteq[n]

and

\mathfrak{a}:I_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}.

We refer to
k
as the
length
of the chain. The possibility that
k=0
is allowed, in which case we make the convention that there is a unique length-
0
chain given by
\mathbf{I}=(\emptyset,\mathfrak{a})
for the unique function
\mathfrak{a}:\emptyset\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
. If
n=0
, then the length-
0
chain is the only chain.

In the following three sections, we describe a bijective procedure for producing, from a chain
\mathbf{I}
, either a boundary stratum
S_{\mathbf{I}}
(Proposition
5.4
), a
\mathcal{T}
-coset
C_{\mathbf{I}}
(Proposition
6.6
), or a
\Delta
-face
F_{\mathbf{I}}
of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
(Proposition
7.15
). Furthermore, we interpret both the dimension of a stratum (or
\mathcal{T}
-coset, or
\Delta
-face) and the inclusion relation between strata (or
\mathcal{T}
-cosets, or
\Delta
-faces) in terms of corresponding features of the chain
\mathbf{I}
. In particular, the inclusion relation is described in terms of the following relation on chains.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})
and
\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell},\mathfrak{b})
be chains of length
k
and
\ell
respectively. We say that
\mathbf{I}

refines

\mathbf{J}
if

\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell}\}\subseteq\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}

and

\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a}\big|_{J_{\ell}}.

(Note that if a chain
\mathbf{I}
of length
k
refines a chain
\mathbf{J}
of length
\ell
, then
J_{\ell}\subseteq I_{k}
and so the restriction of
\mathfrak{a}
on
J_{\ell}
is well-defined.)

We will find in what follows that

•

the boundary stratum
S_{\mathbf{I}}
has codimension
k
, where
\mathbf{I}
is a chain of length
k
,

•

for boundary strata
S_{\mathbf{I}}
and
S_{\mathbf{J}}
, we have
S_{\mathbf{I}}\subseteq S_{\mathbf{J}}
if and only if
\mathbf{I}
refines
\mathbf{J}
,

and the exact same statements hold with boundary strata replaced by
\mathcal{T}
-cosets or
\Delta
-faces. Thus, passing through chains provides the dimension-preserving, inclusion-preserving bijection of Theorem
1.1
.

More precisely, we should note that everything that follows depends on the choice of a primitive
r
th root of unity
\zeta
: the boundary stratum
S_{\mathbf{I}}
lies inside a particular component
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
, the definition of the generating set
\mathcal{T}
of
S(r,n)
depends on a choice of
\zeta
, and the
\Delta
-faces of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
are described by intersecting with certain hyperplanes whose definition depends on
\zeta
. Thus, we make the following convention once and for all:

Convention 4.3
.

Throughout what follows,
\zeta
is a fixed choice of primitive
r
th root of unity.

With this set-up in place, we are ready to flesh out the association of chains to each of the requisite objects.

Proposition 5.4

Let

and

(ii)

\mathbf{I}

Proof.

The surjectivity of
\mathbf{I}\mapsto S_{\mathbf{I}}

, then

\mathbf{I}

\mathbf{J}

also encodes one further piece of geometric information: the decomposition of a boundary stratum into a product of smaller-dimensional moduli spaces. More precisely, let
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
denote the Losev–Manin space mentioned in the introduction; in the language of Hassett spaces, this can be described as

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,(1,1,\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon)},

where there are
n
marked points of weight
\epsilon
and
\epsilon\leq 1/n
. In particular, elements of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
are chains of projective lines with two “heavy” marked points (one on each end component of the chain) and
n
“light” marked points. It is worth stressing that, while we used the Losev–Manin space in the introduction to motivate the present work, the spaces
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
are not actually the
r=1
case of the spaces
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{n}_{r}
. See Remark
8.1
.

With this notation, the following proposition gives a product decomposition of the boundary stratum
S_{\mathbf{I}}
, in which the factors can be read off directly from the chain
\mathbf{I}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

For any chain
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})
, there is a natural isomorphism between the boundary stratum
S_{\mathbf{I}}
and a product of smaller-dimensional moduli spaces, as follows:

S_{\mathbf{I}}\cong\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{\left\lvert[n]\setminus I_{k}\right\rvert}\times\prod_{j=1}^{k}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\left\lvert I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}\right\rvert}.

Proof.

This is essentially immediate from the definition of
S_{\mathbf{I}}
: the factor of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{|[n]\setminus I_{k}|}
parameterizes the central component, and the factors
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{|I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}|}
each parameterize a component of one spoke of the pinwheel (which determines all of the other spokes).
∎

6.
Combinatorics of the complex reflection group

We next introduce the group whose structure is combinatorially related to the boundary stratification of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
. This group, denoted
S(r,n)
, consists of all
n\times n
matrices whose only nonzero entries are in the group
\mu_{r}
of
r
th roots of unity, and with exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column. (By convention,
S(r,0)
is a trivial group.) Note that for any
n\geq 0
, there is a natural group isomorphism

S_{n}=S(1,n),

where the permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
is identified with the matrix whose
i
th column is the
\sigma(i)
th standard basis vector.

Remark 6.1
.

The group
S(r,n)
is an example of a complex reflection group (a finite group acting on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
generated by elements whose action fixes a complex hyperplane), and in the classification of complex reflection groups, it is denoted
G(r,1,n)
. It is sometimes also referred to as the “generalized symmetric group” and can be equivalently described as the wreath product
\mu_{r}\wr S_{n}
. For more on complex reflection groups, see
[
LT09
]
.

It is well-known that the symmetric group
S_{n}
is generated by the set of adjacent transpositions. The complex reflection groups
S(r,n)
have an analogous generating set: define

\mathcal{T}=\{s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}\subseteq S(r,n),

where

s_{0}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\zeta&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
&&&\ddots&\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right)

and for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, the element
s_{i}\in S_{n}\subseteq S(r,n)
is the adjacent transposition swapping
i
and
i+1
, or in other words, the matrix obtained by swapping the
i
th and
(i+1)
st columns of the
n\times n
identity matrix. (Note that the definition of
s_{0}
makes use of the choice of primitive
r
th root of unity
\zeta
in Convention
4.3
.)

It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{T}
generates
S(r,n)
: multiplying the identity matrix
I
by adjacent transpositions can bring any column to the first column, and multiplying by powers of
s_{0}
can change the entry in the first column to any power of
\zeta
. More generally, the following remark describes the structure of subgroups of
S(r,n)
generated by elements of
\mathcal{T}
.

Remark 6.2
.

Fix a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and let
H_{\mathcal{S}}
be the subgroup of
S(r,n)
generated by the subset
\mathcal{S}
. Define
\{s_{j_{1}},\dots,s_{j_{k}}\}:=\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{S}
, where
0\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n-1
. Then
H_{\mathcal{S}}
is equal to the group of block-diagonal matrices

\begin{pmatrix}S(r,j_{1})&&&&&\\
&S_{j_{2}-j_{1}}&&&&\\
&&S_{j_{3}-j_{2}}&&&\\
&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&S_{j_{k}-j_{k-1}}&\\
&&&&&S_{n-j_{k}}\end{pmatrix}\subseteq S(r,n).

That is, the upper-left block is an element of the complex reflection group
S(r,j_{1})
, while the remaining blocks are elements of the indicated symmetric groups.

The key objects of interest for this paper are the right cosets in
S(r,n)
of the subgroups described by Remark
6.2
. The following definition establishes the terminology.

Definition 6.3
.

A
\mathcal{T}
-coset
in

is a right coset of the form

\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\rangle\cdot A\subseteq S(r,n)

for some

, where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathcal{T}
and
A\in S(r,n)
. We say that a
\mathcal{T}
-coset as above, where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}
are distinct, has
dimension

d
or
codimension

n-d

In particular, a singleton

is a
0

\mathcal{T}
-coset. The

\mathcal{T}
-cosets are of the form

\langle s_{i}\rangle\cdot A

, and they thus have either two elements or

or

(15)

To make the elements of

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
. The association

\mathbf{I}\mapsto C_{\mathbf{I}}

is a bijection from the set of decorated nested chains of subsets of

to the set of
\mathcal{T}
-cosets in

. Furthermore, this bijection satisfies

(i)

\text{length}(\mathbf{I})=\text{codim}(C_{\mathbf{I}})
,

(ii)

C_{\mathbf{I}}\subseteq C_{\mathbf{J}}
if and only if
\mathbf{I}
refines
\mathbf{J}
.

Proof.

The surjectivity of
\mathbf{I}\mapsto C_{\mathbf{I}}
is clear, since given an arbitrary
\mathcal{T}
-coset

C=\langle s_{\ell_{1}},\ldots,s_{\ell_{n-k}}\rangle\cdot A,

one can define sets
I_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq I_{k}
by (
15
). Condition (ii) of Definition
6.4
then uniquely defines a function
\mathfrak{a}:I_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, and by construction, setting
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})
gives
C=C_{\mathbf{I}}
.

Injectivity of this association will follow from item (ii), while item (i) is immediate from the definition of
\text{codim}(C_{\mathbf{I}})
. Thus, what remains is to prove item (ii).

Let

\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})

and

\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell},\mathfrak{b}),

and suppose that
\mathbf{I}
refines
\mathbf{J}
. Then

\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}\supseteq\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell}\}

and hence

\{|I_{1}|,\ldots,|I_{k}|\}\supseteq\{|J_{1}|,\ldots,|J_{\ell}|\}.

From here, it is straightforward to unpack that

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{I})\subseteq\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{J}),

where
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{I})
is defined by (
16

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{J})
is defined analogously. Furthermore, a matrix
A
satisfying the conditions of Definition
6.4

\mathbf{I}
will satisfy the same conditions for
\mathbf{J}
, so the same matrix can be chosen to represent both cosets. It follows that
C_{\mathbf{I}}\subseteq C_{\mathbf{J}}
.

Conversely, suppose that
C_{\mathbf{I}}\subseteq C_{\mathbf{J}}
. This means that one can choose the same representative for both cosets, so we have

(17)

H_{\mathbf{I}}\cdot A\subseteq H_{\mathbf{J}}\cdot A

for a matrix

. The elements of
C_{\mathbf{I}}
differ from

by permuting the rows within blocks as in Figure

must be unions of the blocks for

. That is, we must have

\{|I_{1}|,\ldots,|I_{k}|\}\supseteq\{|J_{1}|,\ldots,|J_{\ell}|\}.

In particular, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, we must have
|J_{i}|=|I_{j}|
for some

, so the set

indexing the columns with nonzero entries in the last
|J_{i}|

is equal to the set

indexing the columns with nonzero entries in the last

rows of

. That is, we have

\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}\supseteq\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell}\}.

Since
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are determined by the same matrix

\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a}|_{I_{k}}

\mathbf{I}
refines
\mathbf{J}
.

We end this section by noting that, analogously to the way in which Proposition
5.5
gives a product decomposition of a boundary stratum in terms of the combinatorial data of a chain, the
\mathcal{T}
-cosets in

have decompositions as products of groups dictated by their associated chains. To state the decomposition, for any chain
\mathbf{I}
, let

(18)

\Phi_{\mathbf{I}}:S(r,|[n]\setminus I_{k}|)\times S(r,|I_{k}\setminus I_{k-1}|)\times\cdots\times S(r,|I_{2}\setminus I_{1}|)\times S(r,|I_{1}|)\hookrightarrow S(r,n)

be the embedding of the left-hand side as block-diagonal matrices in
S(r,n)
, where the first factor is embedded as the first
|[n]\setminus I_{k}|
rows and the columns indexed by
[n]\setminus I_{k}
, and similarly for the remaining factors.

Proposition 6.7
.

For any chain
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\cdots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})
, let
C_{\mathbf{I}}=H_{\mathbf{I}}\cdot A
be the associated
\mathcal{T}
-coset. Then there is a natural isomorphism between
H_{\mathbf{I}}
and a product of complex reflection groups, as follows:

(19)

H_{\mathbf{I}}\cong S(r,|[n]\setminus I_{k}|)\times\prod_{j=1}^{k}S_{|I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}|}.

More specifically, let
\Phi_{\mathbf{I}}
be as in (
18
) and let
A_{j}\in S(r,|I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}|)
be any matrix whose entry in the
\ell
th column is
\zeta^{-\mathfrak{a}(\ell)}
for each
\ell\in I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}
. Then

(20)

C_{\mathbf{I}}=\Phi_{\mathbf{I}}\left(S(r,|[n]\setminus I_{k}|)\times\prod_{j=1}^{k}S_{|I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}|}\cdot A_{j}\right).

Proof.

Recall from Remark
6.2
that, if
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
H_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the subgroup of
S(r,n)
generated by
\mathcal{S}
, then the block-diagonal decomposition gives an isomorphism

H_{\mathcal{S}}\cong S(r,j_{1})\times S_{j_{2}-j_{1}}\times S_{j_{3}-j_{2}}\times\cdots\times S_{j_{k}-j_{k-1}}\times S_{n-j_{k}},

in which

are defined by

(21)

j\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}\;\Leftrightarrow\;s_{j}\notin\mathcal{S}.

The group

is equal to

for the set

defined by

Combining (

are precisely the matrices with this block-diagonal decomposition and entry

in column

, which proves (

7.
Combinatorics of the permutohedral complex

The third setting that is combinatorially related to the boundary stratification of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
and to the
\mathcal{T}
-coset structure of
S(r,n)
is the permutohedral complex. To define it, we set

\mathsf{Y}:=\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\cdot\mu_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{C}

for any
r\geq 2
, and we set

\delta^{n}_{k}:=n+(n-1)+\cdots+(n-k+1)

for any
n\geq 1
and
k\in[n]
.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 0
. The
n
-dimensional
r
-permutohedral complex
is defined as

\Delta_{n}^{r}:=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathsf{Y}^{n}\;\left|\;\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}|\leq\delta^{n}_{|I|}\text{ for all }I\subseteq[n]\right.\right\}

if
n\geq 1
, or as a single point if
n=0
.

Because in general
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is not a polytope (rather, as we will prove in Corollary
7.12
below, it is a polytopal complex), defining the appropriate notion of “face” requires some care. We carry this out in the following definition.

Definition 7.2
.

A
decorated subset
of
[n]
is
\widetilde{I}=(I,a)
, where
I\subseteq[n]
and
a:I\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
is any function; in other words, it is a length-
1
decorated nested chain. Any decorated subset has an associated hyperplane

H_{\widetilde{I}}:=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\;\left|\;\sum_{i\in I}\zeta^{a(i)}\cdot x_{i}=\delta^{n}_{|I|}\right.\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}.

A
\Delta
-face
of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is defined as any nonempty intersection

\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{k}},

where
\widetilde{I}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{I}_{k}
are a choice of distinct decorated subsets of
[n]
.

When
r=2
, the complex
\Delta^{2}_{n}
is in fact a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(in particular,
\Delta^{2}_{2}
is the octagon illustrated in Figure
1
, and more generally,
\Delta^{2}_{n}
is known as the type-
B
permutohedron) and its
\Delta
-faces are precisely its faces in the usual sense. When
r\geq 3
, on the other hand, the
\Delta
-faces of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
are themselves polytopal complexes. We illustrate this in an example before proving it in general.

Example 7.3
.

Let

and

This example illustrates that
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is a polytopal complex glued from

\Delta

\Delta^{r}_{n}

(n-k

\widetilde{I}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{I}_{k}
must be satisfied in order to ensure that the corresponding
\Delta
-face is nonempty.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\widetilde{I}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{I}_{k}
be
decorated subsets of
[n]
, where
\widetilde{I}_{j}=(I_{j},a_{j})
for each
j
. Then

\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{k}}\neq\emptyset

if and only if, after possibly reordering
\widetilde{I}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{I}_{k}
, the tuple
(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},a_{k})
is a decorated nested chain of subsets of
[n]
.

Proof.

Since
n
is fixed throughout this proof, we write
\delta^{n}_{k}
as simply
\delta_{k}
to avoid cluttering the notation.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the decorated sets

\widetilde{I}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{I}_{k}

are distinct. To prove the forward direction, it suffices to show that

\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}}\cap H_{\widetilde{J}}\neq\emptyset\;\Longrightarrow\;\widetilde{I}\subseteq\widetilde{J}.

Suppose, then, that
x\in\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}}\cap H_{\widetilde{J}}
, and let
\widetilde{I}=(I,a)
and
\widetilde{J}=(J,b)
. The fact that
x\in\Delta^{r}_{n}
means in particular that
x\in\mathsf{Y}^{n}
, so

x=(\lambda_{1}c_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}c_{n})

for some
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
and
c_{i}\in\mu_{r}
. We claim that

c_{i}=\zeta^{-a(i)}

for all
i\in I
.

To see this, note that the fact that
x\in H_{\widetilde{I}}
means that

(23)

\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\cdot\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}=\delta_{|I|},

and the fact that
x\in\Delta^{r}_{n}
means that

\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\leq\delta_{|I|}.

From the triangle inequality we obtain

\delta_{|I|}=\left|\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\cdot\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}\right|\leq\sum_{i\in I}\Big|\lambda_{i}\cdot\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}\Big|=\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\leq\delta_{|I|}.

Thus, the triangle inequality is in fact an equality, which is only possible if the complex numbers
\lambda_{i}\cdot\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}
are all non-negative real scalar multiples of one another. Since their sum is a positive real number by (
23
), they must each individually be non-negative real numbers; that is,

(24)

\lambda_{i}\cdot\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}

for each
i
. Furthermore,
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in I
, since if
\lambda_{j}=0
for some
j\in I
, then

\sum_{i\in I\setminus\{j\}}\lambda_{i}=\delta_{|I|}>\delta_{|I|-1},

violating one of the inequalities in the definition of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
. Thus, we have
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i
, so (
24
) implies
\zeta^{a(i)}c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\cap\mu_{r}
. We conclude that
c(i)=\zeta^{-a(i)}
, as claimed.

This proves that the decorations on
I
are given by the inverses of the coefficients
c(i)
on
x\in\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}}\cap H_{\widetilde{J}}
, and the exact same argument shows that the decorations on
J
are given by the same formula. Thus, what remains to be proved is that either
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
. To see this, notice that the fact that
x\in H_{\widetilde{I}}\cap H_{\widetilde{J}}
can now be expressed as

\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}=\delta_{|I|}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\sum_{i\in J}\lambda_{i}=\delta_{|J|}.

From these equations, we deduce that

\sum_{i\in I\cup J}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{i\in I\setminus(I\cap J)}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i\in J\setminus(I\cap J)}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\cap J}\lambda_{i}=\delta_{|I|}+\delta_{|J|}-\sum_{i\in I\cap J}\lambda_{i}.

By the defining inequalities of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
, we have

\sum_{i\in I\cap J}\lambda_{i}\leq\delta_{|I\cap J|},

so

\sum_{i\in I\setminus(I\cap J)}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i\in J\setminus(I\cap J)}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i\in I\cap J}\lambda_{i}\geq\delta_{|I|}+\delta_{|J|}-\delta_{|I\cap J|},

or in other words,

(25)

\sum_{i\in I\cup J}\lambda_{i}\geq\delta_{|I|}+\delta_{|J|}-\delta_{|I\cap J|}.

But a straightforward calculation shows that if
|I\cap J|
is strictly less than both
|I|
and
|J|
, then

\delta_{|I|}+\delta_{|J|}-\delta_{|I\cap J|}>\delta_{|I|+|J|-|I\cap J|}.

Given that
\delta_{|I|+|J|-|I\cap J|}=\delta_{|I\cup J|}
, we would then obtain from (
25
) that

\sum_{i\in I\cup J}\lambda_{i}>\delta_{|I\cup J|},

contradicting one of the defining inequalities of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
. Thus, we must have either
|I\cap J|=|I|
or
|I\cap J|=|J|
, meaning that either
I\subseteq J
or
J\subseteq I
. This concludes the proof of the forward direction of the lemma.

For the reverse direction, it suffices to show the statement when
k=n
and
\widetilde{I}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{I}_{n}
are distinct, because any chain can be extended to a maximal one. In light of this, let

\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{n},\mathfrak{a})

be a maximal chain, which can equivalently be expressed as

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\{i_{1}\}

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\{i_{1},i_{2}\}

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}\}=[n]

for some
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}
. Setting

(26)

x_{i_{j}}=\zeta^{-\mathfrak{a}(i_{j})}\cdot(n+1-j)

for each
j
, it is straightforward to check that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{n}}
, where
\tilde{I}_{j}:=(I_{j},\mathfrak{a}|_{I_{j}})
. Therefore,
\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap H_{\widetilde{I}_{n}}\neq\emptyset
, so the reverse direction of the lemma is proved.
∎

First, we must recall the definition of the standard permutohedron.

Definition 7.9
.

The
permutohedron

\Pi_{n}
is the set of points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the inequalities

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\delta^{n}_{|I|}

for all
I\subsetneq[n]
and the equality

\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=\delta^{n}_{n}.

(Analogously to the situation for Losev–Manin space, we point out again that while the analogy to the permutohedron motivates the definition of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
, it is not literally the case that setting

recovers
\Pi_{n}

8.1

It is well-known that
\Pi_{n}
is a polytope of dimension
n-1
. More generally, one obtains other polytopes of the same dimension by shifting
\Pi_{n}
, as follows.

Definition 7.10
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. The
\gamma
-shifted permutohedron

\Pi_{n}+\gamma
is the set of points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the inequalities

\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\delta^{n}_{|I|}+|I|\cdot\gamma

for all
I\subsetneq[n]
and the equality

\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=\delta^{n}_{n}+n\gamma.

Given these definitions, we have the following alternative description of a face
F_{\mathbf{I}}
of
\Delta^{r}_{n}

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a})

\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}|\leq\delta_{|I|}^{|[n]\setminus I_{k}|}

(

where the first equality follows from the fact that

are disjoint, the inequality from (

\mathbf{1}^{\prime}

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\mathbf{3}^{\prime}

As a corollary, we find that the
\Delta
-faces of

have a product decomposition, which shows that they are polytopal complexes of the expected dimension.

Corollary 7.12
.

For any chain
\mathbf{I}
, we have

(29)

F_{\mathbf{I}}=\Delta_{\left\lvert[n]\setminus I_{k}\right\rvert}^{r}\times\varphi_{\mathfrak{a}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\Pi_{\left\lvert I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}\right\rvert}+\gamma_{j})\right),

where
\varphi_{\mathfrak{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{|I_{k}|}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{|I_{k}|}
multiplies the
i
th coordinate by
\zeta^{-\mathfrak{a}(i)}
, and
\gamma_{j}:=|[n]\setminus I_{j}|
. In particular,
F_{\mathbf{I}}
is a polytopal complex of dimension
n-k
.

Proof.

The product decomposition (
29
) follows immediately from Proposition
7.11
. For the “in particular,” it suffices to prove that
\Delta^{r}_{n}
itself is a polytopal complex of dimension
n
. If this is the case, then (
29
) implies that
F_{\mathbf{I}}
is a product of polytopal complexes and polytopes, so it is a polytopal complex. Furthermore, using the fact that
\dim(\Pi_{m})=m-1
, we find that the dimension of
F_{\mathbf{I}}
is

|[n]\setminus I_{k}|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(|I_{j}\setminus I_{j-1}|-1\Big)=n-k,

as claimed.

To prove that
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is a polytopal complex of dimension
n
, let
\mathfrak{c}:[n]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, and define

\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}=(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\cdot\zeta^{c(1)})\times\cdots\times(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\cdot\zeta^{c(n)})\subseteq\mathsf{Y}^{n}.

Then
\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}
is naturally identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and under this identification, we have

\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\left|\;\begin{subarray}{c}\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\leq\delta^{n}_{|I|}\;\text{ for all }\;I\subseteq[n],\\
\displaystyle x_{i}\geq 0\;\text{ for all }\;i\in[n]\end{subarray}\right.\right\}.

In particular,
\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}
is a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and it contains the origin as well as the
n
standard basis vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. Since these are
n+1
affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows that
\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}
has dimension
n
. Thus, we have an expression

\Delta^{r}_{n}=\bigcup_{\mathfrak{c}:[n]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{r}}(\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}})

as a union of
n
-dimensional polytopes intersecting only along the faces where some subset of the coordinates is equal to zero. That is,
\Delta^{r}_{n}
is a polytopal complex of dimension
n
.
∎

Remark 7.13
.

The proof of Corollary
7.12
shows that the individual
n

\Delta^{r}_{n}\cap\mathsf{Y}^{n}_{\mathfrak{c}}
that are glued to form

are independent of

. Setting

, we find that
\Delta^{2}_{n}
is, in fact, a polytope (the type-

are the intersection of
\Delta^{2}_{n}
with an octant. When
n=2
, for example,
\Delta^{2}_{2}
is an octagon whose intersection with each quadrant is a pentagon, and these pentagons are visible in Figure

Let

and

is a bijection from the set of decorated nested chains of subsets of

,

(ii)

\mathbf{I}

Proof.

The surjectivity of

Thus, all that remains is to prove item (ii). One direction is clear: if

is a refinement of

are a subset of the hyperplanes intersected to form

Conversely, suppose that

, where

\mathbf{I}=(I_{1},\ldots,I_{k},\mathfrak{a}),

\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell},\mathfrak{b}).

By completing

to a maximal chain (which, in particular, involves extending

for some

. The fact that

where both are defined, and what remains is to prove that

If not, then one possibility is that

. In this case, however, if

Having ruled out this possibility, the failure of (

and from here, we can cite the known face structure of the permutohedron

(see, for example,
[

[

, Section 4.1]

That is, there exists

with

to a function

∎

Proposition 8.5
.

The bijections

\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{boundary }\\
\textstyle\text{strata in }\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\mathcal{T}\text{-cosets}\\
\textstyle\text{ in }S(r,n)\end{subarray}\right\}\longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\Delta\text{-faces}\\
\textstyle\text{ of }\Delta^{r}_{n}\end{subarray}\right\}

\{A\in S(r,n)\;|\;S_{0}\cdot A\in S\}\subseteq S(r,n)

and identify a

both of which are
\mathcal{T}
-cosets.

Proof.

Let

be a boundary stratum, and let

be the
\mathcal{T}
-coset associated to it via the bijection of Theorem

. By Lemma

, the bijection associates each

to the zero-dimensional boundary stratum

. And since it is inclusion-preserving, we have

A\in C_{S}\;\Leftrightarrow\;S_{0}\cdot A\in S.

This proves that

C_{S}=\{A\in S(r,n)\;|\;S_{0}\cdot A\in S\},

so in particular, the latter is indeed a
\mathcal{T}
-coset. The argument for the case of a

is identical.

on the sets of boundary strata,

for a boundary stratum,

indeed acts on each of these sets is a result of the following observations:

preserves the topological type of

•

The action of

on

so it takes

•

The action of

on

on

From here, it is essentially immediate from Proposition

respect these actions.

Corollary 8.7
.

The bijections of Theorem
1.1
are
S(r,n)
-equivariant.

Proof.

Let
S
be a boundary stratum, and let
C_{S}
be the associated
\mathcal{T}
-coset. Under the action of
B\in S(r,n)
, we have

C_{S}\cdot B=\{AB\in S(r,n)\;|\;A\in C_{S}\}=\{A\in S(r,n)\;|\;AB^{-1}\in C_{S}\}.

The element
AB^{-1}\in S(r,n)
corresponds to the boundary stratum
S_{0}\cdot AB^{-1}\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}(\zeta)
, by Lemma
8.4
. Together with the fact that the bijection from
\mathcal{T}
-cosets to boundary strata is inclusion-preserving, this implies that

AB^{-1}\in C_{S}\;\text{ if and only if }\;S_{0}\cdot AB^{-1}\in S.

Thus, we have

C_{S}\cdot B=\{A\in S(r,n)\;|\;S_{0}\cdot AB^{-1}\in S\}=\{A\in S(r,n)\;|\;S_{0}\cdot A\in S\cdot B\},

which, by Proposition
8.5
, is precisely equal to
C_{S\cdot B}
.

This proves that the bijection between the sets of boundary strata and
\mathcal{T}
-cosets is
S(r,n)
-equivariant, and an identical proof shows the same statement for the bijection between
\Delta
-faces of
\Delta^{r}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}
-cosets.
