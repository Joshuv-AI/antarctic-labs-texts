Randomness Requirements and Asymmetries in Nash Equilibria
Edan Orzech, Martin Rinard

Abstract
In general, Nash equilibria in normal-form games may require players to play (probabilistically) mixed strategies. We define a measure of the complexity of finite probability distributions and study the complexity required to play Nash equilibria in finite two player n × n n\times n games with rational payoffs. Our central results show that there exist games in which there is an exponential vs. linear gap in the complexity of the mixed distributions that the two players play in the (unique) Nash equilibrium of these games. This gap induces asymmetries in the amounts of space required by the players to represent and sample from the corresponding distributions using known state-of-the-art sampling algorithms. We also establish exponential upper and lower bounds on the complexity of Nash equilibria in normal-form games. These results highlight (i) the nontriviality of the assumption that players can play any mixed strategy and (ii) the disparity in resources that players may require to play Nash equilibria in normal-form games.

Relationship with entropy

The entropy is often used to bound players’ randomness capabilities
[
HP15
,
NO00
]
(see Section
2
for more details). The entropy of a distribution tells us exactly (up to an additive constant) how many random bits are needed to be sampled, in order to simulate the distribution exactly
[
Knu76
,
Saa+20
]

[
Knu76
,
Saa+20
]

\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
and

have entropies

and
1-\varepsilon
respectively (for some small
\varepsilon>0
), but the latter distribution requires arbitrarily large space as
\delta\to 0
, with respect to
C
, while the former requires only constant space.

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\delta,\frac{1}{2}-\delta\right)

2.3
Imitation Games

Our constructions involve
imitation games
, which are games where one player receives positive payoff iff the player plays the same pure strategy as the other player (for some notion of ‘same’). These games were first defined in
[
MT05
]
, where they were leveraged to derive a simple proof of Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem
[
Kak41
]
. Computing a NE was shown to be as hard in imitation games as in symmetric games, and it is known
[
GKT50
]
that computing a NE in symmetric games is as hard as in general normal-form games. In both reductions the NE profiles remain the same as distributions, up to adding zero-probability events or swapping one of the mixed strategies in the profile with a uniform distribution. In
[
MT10
]
, the authors continue utilizing imitation games to derive additional simple proofs in computational complexity.

We note that the
4\times 4
instance of our constructions first appeared in
[
BDL08
]
. There, the authors established the NP-completeness of finding a NE in which the mixed strategies are uniform on their supports. There, the authors use the
4\times 4
instance to construct a larger game, to reduce the problem they study to 3-SAT. However, their paper is not concerned with the space requirement issues that we address.
Moreover, in the constructions of
[
BDL08
]
, the space requirements are smaller as well as the gaps in the requirements between the two players in the game. In contrast, our constructions exhibit much larger space requirements and asymmetries in the requirements between the players in the NEs, which highlights how large these requirements and asymmetries can get in normal-form games.

We end with two properties of direct relevance to our motivation discussion in the introduction. The first states that if the players do not both have sufficient capability to play a (specific) NE, then the induced game has no NEs.

Theorem 4.2

Let
(A,B)
be a game, and let

(c_{1},c_{2})
-bounded-randomness game has a NE iff there is an

Proof

By the best-response condition (
[
Rou10
]
), for a given strategy profile we only need to consider deterministic, unilateral deviations. These are valid strategies in the

∎

The second property establishes the correlation between

and the Shannon entropy, as was discussed in the introduction. We now establish space complexity bounds on storing the coefficients of a rational distribution.

Theorem 4.3

Let

Proof

Each

Let

. If
\PNE(A,B)\neq\emptyset

C_{i}(A,B)=1

\PNE(A,B)=\emptyset
.

A_{11}>A_{21}
. This implies that
A_{12}<A_{22}
, and
B_{11}<B_{12}
and
B_{21}>B_{22}

p,q\in(0,1)
that satisfy

\displaystyle qA_{11}+(1-q)A_{12}=qA_{21}+(1-q)A_{22},

\displaystyle pB_{11}+(1-p)B_{21}=pB_{12}+(1-p)B_{22}

\displaystyle p=\frac{B_{21}-B_{22}}{B_{12}-B_{11}+B_{21}-B_{22}},~q=\frac{A_{22}-A_{12}}{A_{11}-A_{21}+A_{22}-A_{12}}

after reducing these 2 fractions, using the equality
\gcd(a,a+b)=\gcd(a,b)
, we get

Theorem 0.F.1

If
\PNE(A,B)=\emptyset

\displaystyle C_{1}(A,B)=\frac{B_{12}-B_{11}+B_{21}-B_{22}}{\gcd(B_{12}-B_{11},B_{21}-B_{22})},~C_{2}(A,B)=\frac{A_{11}-A_{21}+A_{22}-A_{12}}{\gcd(A_{11}-A_{21},A_{22}-A_{12})}

0.F.2
Permutation Games

Let
\text{Sym}_{n}
be the symmetric group of size
n
. Given
\pi\in\text{Sym}_{n}
, let
P_{\pi}
be its permutation matrix, so that
P_{\pi}e_{i}=e_{\pi(i)}
. Then
P_{\pi}^{T}=P_{\pi}
. Write
\pi=\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{a}
and
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{b}
the permutations in cycle form. Also write
\pi^{-1}\tau=(\pi^{-1}\tau)_{1}\cdots(\pi^{-1}\tau)_{d}
. Let
|(\pi^{-1}\tau)_{k}|
be the length of the
k
th cycle, and let
J_{k}
be the set of indices in that cycle (e.g.
\left\{1,3,4\right\}
for the first cycle of
(143)(25)
). Let
\pi,\tau\in\text{Sym}_{n}
, and let
A=P_{\pi},B=P_{\tau}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n\times n}
. Consider the game
(A,B)
.
We prove the following:

Theorem 0.F.2

\NE(P_{\pi},P_{\tau})=\left\{(U_{\pi(J)},U_{J})\mid D\subseteq[d],~J=\bigcup_{k\in D}U_{J_{k}}\right\}
, and therefore

C_{1}(A,B)=C_{2}(A,B)=\min_{k\leq d}|(\pi^{-1}\tau)|_{k}

Proof

Take
(x,y)\in\NE(P_{\pi},P_{\tau})
with supports
I,J
. Then
P_{\tau}^{T}x=u{\bf 1}_{J}+s^{1},P_{\pi}y=v{\bf 1}_{I}+s^{2}
for some
u,v>0
, where
0\leq s^{1}\leq{\bf 1}_{[n]\setminus J}\cdot u
and
0\leq s^{2}\leq{\bf 1}_{[n]\setminus I}\cdot v
. Looking more closely,

\displaystyle P_{\tau}^{T}x=P_{\tau^{-1}}x=\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}e_{\tau^{-1}(i)}=u{\bf 1}_{J}+s^{1}

So because
u>0
we get
J\subseteq\tau^{-1}(I)
. Similarly,
I\subseteq\pi(J)
. So
\tau(J)\subseteq I\subseteq\pi(J)
, and these are all equalities because
\pi,\tau
are permutations. In particular
s^{1}=s^{2}={\bf 0}
, so
x=U_{I},y=U_{J}
. Also,
J=\pi^{-1}\tau(J)
, so
J
is a union of cycles of
\pi^{-1}\tau
, so
J=\bigcup_{k\in D}U_{J_{k}}
for some
D\subseteq[d]
. Then,
I=\pi(J)
.
On the other hand, given any such
J
and
I=\pi(J)
, the calculations above verify that
(U_{\pi(J)},U_{J})\in\NE(P_{\pi},P_{\tau})
.
∎

As a corollary, we also obtain
\NE(P_{\pi},P_{\tau})=\NE(I_{n},P_{\pi^{-1}\tau})
.
