An Algebraic Approach to q q -Partial Fractions and Sylvester Denumerants Thanks: Dedicated to Bhagawan Sri Sathya Sai Baba.
N. Uday Kiran

Abstract
In 1857 Sylvester established an elegant theory that certain counting functions (which he termed denumerants) are quasi-polynomials by decomposing them into periodic and non-periodic parts. Each component of the decomposition, called a Sylvester wave, corresponds to a root of unity. Recently several researchers, using either combinatorial arguments or complex analytic techniques, obtained explicit formulas for the waves. In this work, we develop an algebraic approach to the Sylvester’s theory. Our methodology essentially relies on deriving q q -partial fractions of the generating functions of the denumerants, and thereby obtain new explicit formulas for the waves. The formulas we obtain are expressed in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli numbers and a generalization of the Fourier-Dedekind sum. Further, we also prove certain reciprocity theorems of the generalized Fourier-Dedekind sums and a structure result on the top-order terms of the waves. The proofs rely on our evaluation operator and our far-reaching generalization of the Heaviside’s cover-up method for partial fractions. SageMath code for this work is available in the public domain. Restricted partitions of positive integers have been studied extensively starting from the work of Euler on generating functions. So as to deploy binomial expansions, Cayley took the approach of partial fraction decomposition of these generating functions. Subsequently, Sylvester generalized the restricted partitions by formulating the counting problem as the number of non-negative integral solutions to certain linear Diophantine equations. This generalization, which he termed denumerants, permits one to define partitions into specified parts - repeated or not.

Given a
r
-tuple
\textbf{A}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{r})
of positive integers with
\textnormal{gcd}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{r})=1
, the denumerant of
t
with parts from
A
, denoted
d(t;\textbf{A})
, is the cardinality of the set

\left\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}):\sum^{r}_{j=1}x_{j}a_{j}=t\textnormal{ and }\ x_{i}\geq 0,\textnormal{ for }i=1,\cdots,r\right\}.

It can be easily seen that
d(t;\textbf{A})
is given by the generating function

\frac{1}{(1-x^{a_{1}})\cdots(1-x^{a_{r}})}=\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}d(t;\textbf{A})x^{t}.

(1)

The important case of distinct
a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}
corresponds to the restricted partition function associated with the Frobenius problem
[
6
,
1
]
.

In
[
25
]
, Sylvester made a remarkable progress by showing that

d(t;\textbf{A})=\sum W_{j}(t;\textbf{A}),
where the sum is over divisors
j
of the entries in the tuple
A
. The term
W_{j}(t;\textbf{A})
, called a Sylvester wave, is a quasi-polynomial in
t
given by the
coefficient of
z^{-1}
, i.e. the residue at
z=0
of the function

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 0\leq n<j}}{{\textnormal{gcd}(n,j)=1}}}\frac{\xi_{j}^{-nt}e^{zt}}{(1-\xi_{j}^{a_{1}n}e^{-a_{1}z})\cdots(1-\xi_{j}^{a_{r}n}e^{-a_{r}z})},

where
\xi_{j}=e^{2\pi i/j}
. Moreover,
\xi_{1}=1
so that
W_{1}(t;\textbf{A})
is a polynomial in
t
. Glaiser
[
14
]
leveraged these waves to perform extensive hand calculations of restricted partitions (see
[
11
,
2
]
for history).

Building upon Cayley’s work, Munagi
[
20
]
formulated a non-standard approach to partial fractions, the so-called

-partial fractions. The denominators of

-partial fractions are of the form

[
24

obtained explicit expressions for

-partial fractions for partition function of

In this work, we use elementary algebraic methods to obtain explicit

-partial fraction formulas for (
1

Usually, computing a

-partial fraction for (

It is easy to see that

\prod^{5}_{j=1}\frac{1}{1-x^{j}}=\frac{1+x^{2}}{(1-x)(1-x^{3})(1-x^{4})^{2}(1-x^{5})}.

The main results of this paper uncover the beautiful structure of the denumerants associated with the right hand side of (

is the wave corresponding to

1.2
Our Methodology

We first create an algebraic framework by generalizing the Heaviside’s cover-up method. Given distinct numbers
c_{1},\cdots,c_{k}
and
h(x)
a polynomial with
\textnormal{deg}(h)<k
, suppose the expression for the partial fraction is

h(x)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x-c_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{C_{j}}{x-c_{j}},

(11)

then the cover-up method suggests: to obtain
C_{i}
we need to evaluate the left hand side of (
11
) at
x=c_{i}
seemingly ‘covering’ the
(x-c_{i})
factor. Thus, we obtain

C_{i}=h(c_{i})\prod^{k}_{j=1,j\neq i}\frac{1}{c_{i}-c_{j}}.

Heaviside method is given only for the linear case. Man
[
19
]
provided an iterative procedure, based on long division and substitution, to extend the method to the quadratic case. His method does not require factorization into linear factors, differentiation or solving simultaneous equations. This paper gives a rigorous extension to higher degree polynomials the method given in
[
19
]
. We rely on localization of rings, substitution rule associated with the generalized Remainder Theorem and the Bézout’s identity.

The key to rigorously extend this method to higher degree polynomials is our symbolic evaluation operator based on the principles of localization and substitution. Given non-constant polynomials
r(x),s(x),a(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
s(x),a(x)
are relatively prime, we define the evaluation of the fraction
\frac{r(x)}{s(x)}
with respect to
a(x)
by

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)};\ a(x)\right)=(\alpha(x)r(x))\;\textnormal{rem}\;a(x),

where
\alpha(x)s(x)\equiv 1\textnormal{ mod }a(x)
and
rem
is the polynomial remainder operator. Our extension result is stated below and proved in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.2.1

(Extended Cover-Up Method)
.

Let
p_{1}(x),\cdots,p_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be pairwise relatively prime polynomials and let

f(x)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}(x)}=\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{\textnormal{eval}\left(f(x)/g_{j}(x);\ p_{j}(x)\right)}{p_{j}(x)},

where
g_{j}(x)=p_{1}(x)\cdots\widehat{p_{j}(x)}\cdots p_{k}(x)
and
\ \widehat{}
refers to dropping the corresponding factor.

Employing the extended cover-up method we will now demonstrate our

-partial fraction approach. In fact, for our purpose we just need to perform eval on two specific polynomials, namely

As explored in Section

Our Algebraic Approach for Partial Fractions: Extended Cover-Up Method

In this section we provide a rigorous treatment of the eval operator and, subsequently, prove the extended cover-up method given in Theorem
1.2.1
. In order to achieve this we briefly review certain notation and terminology from commutative algebra, and formalize the polynomial remainder operator.

An element
p(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
is a polynomial of the form

p(x)=\alpha_{m}x^{m}+\cdots+\alpha_{0},\ \ \alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q},\textnormal{ for }i=0,\cdots,m.

It is well known that
\mathbb{Q}[x]
is a principal ideal domain. We denote the ideal generated by a polynomial with the same letter but in a italic font, for instance the prime ideal generated by
p(x)
is denoted
\mathfrak{p}
. Given a non-empty multiplicative set
S
(i.e., if
a,b\in S
then
ab\in S
, and
0\notin S
) of
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, the ring of fractions of
\mathbb{Q}[x]
with respect to
S
will be denoted by
S^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]
and is defined by

S^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]=\left\{\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\in\mathbb{Q}(x):f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]\textnormal{ and }g(x)\in S\right\}.

Let
a(x)
factorize as

a(x)=p_{1}(x)^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}(x)^{e_{k}},

(17)

where
p_{1}(x),\cdots,p_{k}(x)
are distinct irreducible polynomials. We localize the ring
\mathbb{Q}[x]
about the multiplicative set

S_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mathbb{Q}[x]-(\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{p}_{k}).

The localization
S^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathbb{Q}[x]
of
\mathbb{Q}[x]
is
the set of all rational functions with denominators not sharing any non-trivial common factor with
a(x)
. It can be easily shown that
S^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathbb{Q}[x]=\cap_{i=1}^{k}S^{-1}_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}\mathbb{Q}[x]
.

For the purpose of defining the eval operator we will express the remainder operator of
f(x)
divided by
p(x)
(denoted
f(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;p(x)
) as a composition of two functions: the function
\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathbb{Q}[x]\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[x]/\mathfrak{p}
defined

\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(f(x))=f(x)+\mathfrak{p},

and
\eta:\mathbb{Q}[x]/\mathfrak{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[x]
defined

\eta(A)=\begin{cases}0&\textnormal{ if }0\in A\\
\textnormal{smallest degree polynomial in }A&\textnormal{ if }0\notin A.\end{cases}

The following lemma can be easily shown.

Lemma 2.0.1
.

The function
\eta\circ\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a homomorphism with polynomial addition and the following equality holds:

(f(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;p(x))=\eta\circ\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}(f(x)).

In this work, for the purpose of derivations, a crucial role is played by the substitution rules. It is interesting (and also unfortunate) to notice that not enough emphasis was given in the literature about the substitution rule of the remainder operator. Indeed, a generalization of substitution rule to higher degree polynomials follows in similar lines to that of the Remainder Theorem
[
18
, Theorem 2.7]
.

Theorem 2.0.2

(Generalized Remainder Theorem
[
18
]
)
.

Let
f(x),g(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
with
g(x)=x^{k}+a_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
and suppose
k<\textnormal{deg}(f)
. Then, the remainder of
f(x)
modulo
g(x)
can be found by substituting the polynomial
s(x)=-a_{k-1}x^{k-1}-\cdots-a_{1}x-a_{0}
for
x^{k}
in
f(x)
, and iterating this substitution in the polynomial thus obtained until its degree becomes less than
k
.

This substitution rule could also be justified by Lemma
2.0.1
in the sense that repeated substitutions retain the resulting element in the coset, given by
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}
, and the substitution process terminates with the smallest degree polynomial, picked by
\eta
.

Equipped with the above substitution rule and using some tricks for manipulation of polynomials one can obtain efficient algorithms to determine the remainder.

Example 2.0.1
.

The remainder of
x^{101}+5x^{31}
by
\Psi_{3}(x)=x^{2}+x+1
can be obtained by first observing that the polynomial
x^{2}+x+1
divides
x^{3}-1
so before substituting
x^{2}
with
-x-1
we can first substitute
x^{3}
with
1
leading us to

\displaystyle(x^{101}+5x^{31})\;\textnormal{rem}\;(x^{2}+x+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x^{101\%3}+5x^{31\%3})\;\textnormal{rem}\;(x^{2}+x+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x^{2}+5x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;(x^{2}+x+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-x-1)+5x=4x-1.

Here we used the fact that the substitution of
x^{3}
by
1
in the factors of
x^{k}
we obtain
x^{k\%3}
, where
\%
is the remainder of integers. This observation leads us to the substitution rule given in Lemma
3.1.1
for the polynomials
\Psi_{m}(x)
.

Let
r(x),s(x)
and
a(x)
be in the ring of polynomials
\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that
s(x)
and
a(x)
are relatively prime. By Bézout’s identity there exist two polynomials
\alpha(x),\beta(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that

\alpha(x)s(x)+\beta(x)a(x)=1.

(18)

Definition 2.0.1
.

Given non-constant polynomials
r(x),s(x),a(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
a(x),s(x)
satisfy (
18
), we define the evaluation of the fraction
\frac{r(x)}{s(x)}
modulo
a(x)
from
S^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathbb{Q}[x]
to
\mathbb{Q}[x]
as

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)};\ a(x)\right)=(\alpha(x)r(x))\;\textnormal{rem}\;a(x),

where
\alpha(x)
is the inverse of
s(x)
modulo
a(x)
, i.e.
\alpha(x)s(x)=1\textnormal{ mod }a(x)
.

The eval operator is well-defined due the following easy to prove isomorphism.

Lemma 2.0.3
.

The rings
S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]/S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathbb{Q}[x]/\mathfrak{a}
are isomorphic.

Suppose
h:S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]/S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[x]/\mathfrak{a}
is the isomorphism and using the functions
\pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\eta
, we can now state the eval function as a sequence of compositions

S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]\xrightarrow{\pi_{S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}}}S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}[x]/S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\xrightarrow{h}\mathbb{Q}[x]/\mathfrak{a}\xrightarrow{\eta}\mathbb{Q}[x].

By (
18
) and observing that

\frac{-\alpha(x)r(x)\beta(x)a(x)}{1-\beta(x)a(x)}\in S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}

we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)};\ a(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\circ h\circ\pi_{S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}}\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)}\right)=\eta\circ h\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)}+S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\circ h\left(\frac{\alpha(x)r(x)}{1-\beta(x)a(x)}+\frac{-\alpha(x)r(x)\beta(x)a(x)}{1-\beta(x)a(x)}+S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\right).

Upon simplification by using the definitions of
h
and
\eta
, and Lemma
2.0.1
we get

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r(x)}{s(x)};\ a(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\circ h\left(\alpha(x)r(x)+S_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\left(\alpha(x)r(x)+\mathfrak{a}\right)=\alpha(x)r(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;a(x).

Therefore,
\textnormal{eval}(\cdot,a(x))
operator can be expressed as the homomorphism
\eta\circ h\circ\pi_{S^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathfrak{a}}
. Hence, eval is a well defined operator which takes in a rational polynomial and gives us a polynomial.

The following properties can also be proved easily from the definitions.

Lemma 2.0.4
.

Given
a(x),r_{i}(x),s_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
\textnormal{gcd}(s_{i}(x),a(x))=1
for
i=0,1
. The following are some properties of
eval
function:

1.

\textnormal{eval}\left(r_{0}(x);\ a(x)\right)=r_{0}(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;a(x)

2.

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r_{0}(x)r_{1}(x)}{s_{0}(x)s_{1}(x)};\ a(x)\right)=\left\{\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r_{0}(x)}{s_{0}(x)};\ a(x)\right)\textnormal{eval}\left(\\
\frac{r_{1}(x)}{s_{1}(x)};\ a(x)\right)\right\}\;\textnormal{rem}\;a(x)

3.

(Substitution Rule)
\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r_{0}(x)-p_{0}(x)a(x)}{s_{0}(x)-q_{0}(x)a(x)};\ a(x)\right)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{r_{0}(x)}{s_{0}(x)};\ a(x)\right)

for some

p_{0}(x),q_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
; or stated in words, one can successively substitute the occurrences of
a(x)
by
0
in both numerator and denominator of the rational polynomial given in the first argument of eval.

2.1
Partial Fraction Decomposition

Given
p_{1}(x),\cdots,p_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
non-trivial polynomials that are pairwise relatively prime i.e.,
\textnormal{gcd}(p_{i}(x),p_{j}(x))=1
for
i\neq j
. Algebraically speaking, a partial fraction decomposition of the rational function of the form

\frac{1}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}=\frac{a_{1}(x)}{p_{1}(x)}+\cdots+\frac{a_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}

(19)

can be viewed as the Bézout’s identity

a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)=1,

where

g_{j}(x)=\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{n}p_{i}(x),\textnormal{ for each }1\leq j\leq n

(20)

which are coprime,
\textnormal{gcd}(g_{1}(x),\cdots,g_{n}(x))=1
.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

Suppose the equation, for
g_{j}(x)
in (
20
),

b_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+b_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)=f(x)

(21)

holds for
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
\textnormal{deg }(f)<\textnormal{deg }(p_{1}\cdots p_{n})
. Then, the following equation also holds

(b_{1}(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;p_{1}(x))g_{1}(x)+\cdots+(b_{n}(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;p_{n}(x))g_{n}(x)=f(x).

(22)

Proof.

To prove (
22
) it suffices to show that the quotients of
b_{i}(x)
when divided by
p_{i}(x)
add up to zero. Suppose we have

b_{i}(x)=p_{i}(x)q_{i}(x)+r_{i}(x)

where
\textnormal{deg}(r_{i})<\textnormal{deg}(p_{i})
, for
i=1,\cdots,n
. Dividing both sides of the equation (
21
) by
p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)
we get

\frac{f(x)}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}=\frac{b_{1}(x)}{p_{1}(x)}+\cdots+\frac{b_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}=\left(q_{1}(x)+\frac{r_{1}(x)}{p_{1}(x)}\right)\cdots+\left(q_{n}(x)+\frac{r_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}\right).

After rearranging the terms we have a difference of two proper rational polynomials equal to a polynomial

\left(\frac{f(x)}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}\right)-\left(\frac{r_{1}(x)}{p_{1}(x)}+\cdots+\frac{r_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}\right)=q_{1}(x)+\cdots+q_{n}(x),

which is possible only if
q_{1}(x)+\cdots+q_{n}(x)=0
. Hence the result is proved.
∎

The advantage of having the above lemma is in simplifying the partial fraction expansion for complicated numerators. We now prove our fundamental lemma which says that eval operator converts certain equations to Bézout’s identities.

Lemma 2.1.2
.

[Fundamental Lemma]
Suppose
g_{j}(x)
satisfy (
20
) and

a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)=f(x)

(23)

holds for
\textnormal{deg }(f)<\textnormal{deg }(p_{1}\cdots p_{n})
and
\textnormal{gcd}(f(x),p_{i}(x))=1
for all
i=1,2,\cdots,n
. Then, the following equation also holds:

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{1}(x)}{f(x)};\ p_{1}(x)\right)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{n}(x)}{f(x)};\ p_{n}(x)\right)g_{n}(x)=1.

(24)

Proof.

As
\textnormal{gcd}(f(x),p_{i}(x))=1
for
1\leq i\leq n
there exist
\alpha_{i}(x),\beta_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that

\alpha_{i}(x)p_{i}(x)+\beta_{i}(x)f(x)=1.

By the definition of eval function it suffices to prove the equation (
24
) with the factors
\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{i}(x)}{f(x)};\ p_{i}(x)\right)
replaced with
a_{i}(x)\beta_{i}(x)\;\textnormal{rem}\;p_{i}(x)
. Dividing both sides of the equation (
23
) by
f(x)
and
\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(x)
we have

\frac{1}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}=\frac{a_{1}(x)}{f(x)p_{1}(x)}+\cdots+\frac{a_{n}(x)}{f(x)p_{n}(x)}.

By multiplying in the numerator with the factor
(\alpha_{i}(x)p_{i}(x)+\beta_{i}(x)f(x))
(which essentially equals
1
) in the
i^{th}
term we have

\frac{1}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{a_{i}(x)(\alpha_{i}(x)p_{i}(x)+\beta_{i}(x)f(x))}{f(x)p_{i}(x)}.

Upon simplifying and rearranging terms we have

\frac{a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)\alpha_{n}(x)}{f(x)}=\frac{1}{p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{n}(x)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}(x)\beta_{i}(x)}{p_{i}(x)},

i.e. left hand side has a denominator
f(x)
and the denominator of the right hand side is coprime to
f(x)
. This is possible only if
a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)\alpha_{n}(x)=0
. Therefore, the right hand side of the above equation is zero. Hence the lemma is established.
∎

We are now ready to prove the extended cover-up method.

Proof of Theorem
1.2.1
.

By Lemma
2.1.1
, without loss of generality, we can assume
f(x)=1
. The polynomials
g_{j}(x)=\prod^{n}_{i=1,i\neq j}p_{i}(x)
for
j=1,\cdots,n
are mutually coprime. By Bézout’s identity there exist
a_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
for
1\leq j\leq n
such that

a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)=1.

It can be easily seen that none of the polynomials
a_{j}(x)
are zero. So, we consider the identity

\left(\frac{a_{1}(x)}{a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)}\right)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+\left(\frac{a_{n}(x)}{a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)}\right)g_{n}(x)=1.

Then by Lemma
2.1.2
we can write the above equation as

\sum^{n}_{i=1}\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{i}(x)}{a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)};\ p_{i}(x)\right)g_{i}(x)=1.

By substitution rule Lemma
2.0.4
(3) we have

\sum^{n}_{i=1}\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{i}(x)\mod p_{i}(x)}{a_{1}(x)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)\mod p_{i}(x)};\ p_{i}(x)\right)g_{i}(x)=1.

As
g_{j}(x)\mod p_{i}(x)=0
if
i\neq j
and
a_{j}(x)
non-zero for all
j=1,\cdots,n

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{g_{1}(x)};\ p_{1}(x)\right)g_{1}(x)+\cdots+\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{g_{n}(x)};\ p_{n}(x)\right)g_{n}(x)=1

Hence the result is proved.
∎

Our method for partial fraction decomposition can be considered as an alternative to the undetermined coefficient method, usually taught at an early stage of higher mathematics. To illustrate the efficacy of our method we provide an example.

Example 2.1.1
.

Using the extended cover-up method we determine the partial fraction of
\frac{1}{(x^{2}+a^{2})(x^{3}+b^{3})}
, for arbitrary but fixed
a,b>0
. Let the partial fraction be given by

\frac{1}{(x^{2}+a^{2})(x^{3}+b^{3})}=\frac{a_{1}(x)}{(x^{2}+a^{2})}+\frac{a_{2}(x)}{(x^{3}+b^{3})}

we now evaluate for
a_{1}(x)
and
a_{2}(x)
.

\displaystyle a_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{x^{3}+b^{3}};\ x^{2}+a^{2}\right)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{x^{3}+b^{3}}\times\frac{x^{3}-b^{3}}{x^{3}-b^{3}};\ x^{2}+a^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{x^{3}-b^{3}}{x^{6}-b^{6}};\ x^{2}+a^{2}\right)=\frac{a^{2}x+b^{3}}{a^{6}+b^{6}},

where we used the substitution rule given in Lemma
2.0.4
(3). Similarly, we have

\displaystyle a_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{x^{2}+a^{2}}\times\frac{x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+a^{4}}{x^{4}-a^{2}x^{2}+a^{4}};\ x^{3}+b^{3}\right)=\frac{-a^{2}x^{2}-b^{3}x-a^{4}}{b^{6}+a^{6}}

Hence, the partial fraction is

\frac{1}{(x^{2}+a^{2})(x^{3}+b^{3})}=\frac{1}{a^{6}+b^{6}}\left(\frac{a^{2}x+b^{3}}{x^{2}+a^{2}}+\frac{-a^{2}x^{2}-b^{3}x-a^{4}}{x^{3}+b^{3}}\right)

One can factorize
(x^{3}+b^{3})
and follow the above method to perform further decomposition. Note that by the undetermined coefficient method this decomposition would have required solving simultaneous linear equations with five unknowns.

2.2
Partial Fraction for Powers

Given a partial fraction decomposition corresponding to

a_{1}(x)p_{1}(x)+a_{2}(x)p_{2}(x)=1,

(25)

Raising both sides of (

1-p_{1}(x)q(x)
we get

\left(\frac{a_{1}(x)^{n}}{1-p_{1}(x)q(x)}\right)p_{1}(x)^{n}+\left(\frac{a_{2}(x)^{n}}{1-p_{1}(x)q(x)}\right)p_{2}(x)^{n}=1.

Thus, in view of the extended cover-up method and the substitution rule Lemma
2.0.4
(3), we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{p_{2}(x)^{n}};p_{1}(x)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{a_{2}(x)^{n}}{1-p_{1}(x)q(x)};p_{1}(x)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(a_{2}(x)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{1}(x)^{k}q(x)^{k};p_{1}(x)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(a_{2}(x)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{1}(x)^{k}q(x)^{k}\right)\textnormal{ rem }p_{1}(x)^{n}.

Theorem 2.2.1
.

Suppose
a_{1}(x)p_{1}(x)+a_{2}(x)p_{2}(x)=1
. Then for a given positive integer
n
we have

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{p_{2}(x)};p_{1}(x)^{n}\right)=\left(p_{2}(x)^{n-1}a_{2}(x)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{1}(x)^{k}q(x)^{k}\right)\textnormal{ rem }p_{1}(x)^{n},

where

q(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
k\end{pmatrix}a_{1}(x)^{k}a_{2}(x)^{n-k}p_{2}(x)^{n-k}p_{1}(x)^{k-1}.

Using Theorem
2.2.1
, Lemma
2.0.4
and the extended cover-up method we can derive a partial fraction formula for the higher power case.

3
The polynomial
\Psi_{m}(x)

The polynomial
\Psi_{m}(x)
plays a key role in the
q
-partial fractions owing to

1-x^{m}=(1-x)\Psi_{m}(x).

Moreover, we observe that
\Psi_{m}(x)
shares a pleasant relationship with the eval operator and also the special classes of numbers, the so-called degenerate Bernoulli numbers.

The (usual) Bernoulli number
B_{k}
may be defined by the generating function

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}B_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}.

Since
(1+\lambda t)^{1/\lambda}\rightarrow e^{t}
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, Carlitz
[
9
]
defined the degenerate Bernoulli number as

\frac{t}{(1+\lambda t)^{1/\lambda}-1}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\beta_{k}(\lambda)\frac{t^{k}}{k!}.

(27)

By setting
\lambda=1/m
and
x=1+\lambda t
in (
27
) we obtain

\frac{m(1-x)}{1-x^{m}}=\frac{m}{\Psi_{m}(x)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)}{k!}(1-x)^{k},

(28)

where
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)=m^{k}\beta_{k}(1/m)
where
\beta_{k}(m)
is the degenerate Bernoulli number. Thus
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)
is the reciprocal polynomial of
\beta_{k}(m)
. See
[
30
]
for some properties of
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)
.

The reciprocal degenerate Bernoulli of order
r
is given by
[
30
,
32
]

\left(\frac{m}{\Psi_{m}(x)}\right)^{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{\tilde{\beta}^{(r)}_{k}(m)}{k!}(1-x)^{k}.

(29)

Raising both sides of (
28
) by a power
r
and expanding we have

\tilde{\beta}_{k}^{(r)}(m)=\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r}=k}\frac{k!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{r}!}\tilde{\beta}_{k_{1}}(m)\cdots\tilde{\beta}_{k_{r}}(m).

Obviously,
\tilde{\beta}^{(1)}_{k}(m)=\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)
. A sum of products formula for
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)
with a fixed
m
is given in
[
32
]
.

3.1
Substitution Rules of
\Psi_{m}(x)

We will now explore the relation between
\Psi_{m}(x)
and the eval function.

Lemma 3.1.1

(Substitution Rule 1)
.

\textnormal{eval}(x^{j};\Psi_{m}(x))=x^{j}\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{m}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\thinspace}l}x^{j\%m}&\textnormal{ if }j\%m\neq m-1\\
-\sum^{m-2}_{i=0}x^{i}&\textnormal{ if }j\%m=m-1.\\
\end{array}\right.

(30)

where
\%
operator is the remainder in the ring of integers.

Proof.

Suppose
j=md+r
, for
0\leq r<m
. Then, the result follows from

x^{md+r}=((x^{m}-1)+1)^{d}x^{r}=q(x)(x^{m}-1)+x^{r}=q(x)(x-1)\Psi_{m}(x)+x^{r}.

∎

The
\textnormal{eval}(\cdot,\Psi_{m}(x)^{r})
function behaves in a satisfying manner under the action of Fourier Transform. This can be seen by another substitution rule.

Lemma 3.1.2

(Substitution Rule 2)
.

Let
f(x),g(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
\textnormal{gcd}(g(x),\Psi_{b}(x))=1
. Suppose

h(x)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)};\ \Psi_{b}(x)^{r}\right)\textnormal{ for some positive integers }r\textnormal{ and }b.

Then, we have
h(\xi_{b})=f(\xi_{b})/g(\xi_{b})
for
\xi_{b}=e^{2\pi i/b}
.

Proof.

As
\textnormal{gcd}(g(x),\Psi_{b}(x))=1
, by Bézout’s identity, there exist
\alpha(x),\beta(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that

\alpha(x)g(x)+\beta(x)\Psi_{b}(x)^{r}=1.

(31)

Substituting
\xi_{b}=e^{2\pi i/b}
both sides of (
31
), we get
\alpha(\xi_{b})g(\xi_{b})=1.

Also,

\displaystyle h(x)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)};\ \Psi_{b}(x)^{r}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)\alpha(x)\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{b}(x)^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x)\alpha(x)-q(x)\Psi_{b}(x)^{r},

for some
q(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Hence, we have
h(\xi_{b})=f(\xi_{b})\alpha(\xi_{b})=f(\xi_{b})/g(\xi_{b}).

∎

3.2
Fourier Series and
\textnormal{eval}(\cdot,\Psi_{b}(x))

The aim of this section is to give a Fourier series for
h(x)/(1-x^{b})^{r}
. It is well known that when
r=1
the Fourier series is a finite Fourier series. For an excellent discussion on finite Fourier series see
[
6
]
.

We first determine the finite Fourier series representation of

\frac{h(x)}{1-x^{b}},\quad\textnormal{ for }\quad h(x)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)};\Psi_{b}(x)\right),

where
f(x),g(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
g(x)
relatively prime to
\Psi_{b}(x)
. Let
a(n)
be a periodic function on
\mathbb{Z}
with a period
b
. Consider the series
H(x)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}a(n)x^{n}
which has the generating function

H(x)=\frac{h(x)}{1-x^{b}}\quad\textnormal{ for }\quad h(x)=\sum^{b-1}_{j=0}a(j)x^{j}.

The finite Fourier series expansion is given by

a(n)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}h(\xi_{b}^{j})\xi_{b}^{-nj},\quad\textnormal{ where }\quad\xi_{b}=e^{2\pi i/b}.

The above coefficients are, essentially, obtained by applying the inverse DFT on
(h(\xi_{b}^{0}),\cdots,h(\xi_{b}^{b-1}))^{T}
.

By
h(x)=\textnormal{eval}\left(f(x)/g(x);\ \Psi_{b}(x)\right)
, Lemma
3.1.2
and the finite Fourier series expansion we deduce

H(x)=\frac{h(x)}{1-x^{b}}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}a(n)x^{n}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}h(\xi_{b}^{j})\xi_{b}^{-nj}\right)x^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum^{b-1}_{j=0}\frac{f(\xi_{b}^{j})}{g(\xi_{b}^{j})}\xi_{b}^{-jn}\right)x^{n}.

(32)

The following example is a connecting link between the
\textnormal{eval}(\cdot;\Psi_{m}(x))
and the Fourier-Dedekind sum given in
[
6
]
.

Example 3.2.1
.

Let
n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}
and
b
be pairwise relatively prime positive integers and suppose
h(x)=(1-x)\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-x^{n_{1}})\cdots(1-x^{n_{k}})};\ \Psi_{b}(x)\right)
. Then the
n^{th}
term in the power series of
h(x)/(1-x^{b})
is

a(n)=\frac{1}{b}\sum^{b-1}_{j=1}\frac{\xi_{b}^{-jn}}{(1-\xi_{b}^{jn_{1}})\cdots(1-\xi_{b}^{jn_{k}})}.

Now, we obtain the Fourier series expansion of

h(x)/(1-x^{b})^{r}

where
r>1
and
\textnormal{deg}(h)<rb
. In this case, the associated Fourier series is no more a finite Fourier series. Nevertheless, one can obtain a simplification by writing

h(x)=\sum^{r-1}_{j=0}h_{j}(x)(1-x^{b})^{j},\textnormal{ where }\textnormal{deg}(h_{j})<b,

(33)

and thus we can express

\frac{h(x)}{(1-x^{b})^{r}}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{(1-x^{b})^{r-j-1}}\frac{h_{j}(x)}{(1-x^{b})}.

Hence, we can obtain the finite Fourier series for each
h_{j}(x)/(1-x^{b})
by applying (
32
). Therefore, we strive to obtain an explicit formula for (
33
) which is a Taylor series like expansion in terms of
(1-x^{b})
. Towards this end we introduce a special differentiation notation

D_{b}(x^{k})=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{b}\right\rfloor x^{k-b}.

For
b=1
,
D_{b}=D
, the standard differentiation operation. Nevertheless, due to a deficiency in Leibniz rule,
D_{b}
cannot be, in general, considered as a differential operator.

Theorem 3.2.1
.

Suppose
h(x)
is a polynomial with
\textnormal{deg}(h)<rb
. Then,

h(x)=\sum^{r-1}_{j=0}\frac{(-1)^{j}h^{(j)}(x)}{j!}(1-x^{b})^{j},

where
h^{(j)}(x)=\textnormal{eval}\left(D_{b}^{j}h(x);1-x^{b}\right)
. In particular,
h^{(0)}(x)
is the remainder of
h(x)
when divided by
1-x^{b}
.

Proof.

Setting
y=x^{b}
, we can write
h(x)=\sum^{r-1}_{i=0}x^{i}\left(\sum^{(r-1)}_{j=0}\alpha_{ij}y^{j}\right)
. The result follows from the Taylor’s theorem about
y=1
on each term
\sum^{(r-1)}_{j=0}\alpha_{ij}y^{j}
.
∎

It is easy to compute
h^{(j)}(x)
in the above Taylor series like expansion by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

For
k\geq 0
and
b>1
,

\textnormal{eval}(x^{k};1-x^{b})=x^{k\%b}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.0.4
, the eval of a polynomial is remainder. Let
k=ab+c
for
0\leq c<b
. Then we can write

x^{k}=x^{c}(1-(1-x^{b}))^{a}=\left(\sum^{a}_{j=1}(-1)^{j}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
j\end{pmatrix}(1-x^{b})^{j-1}\right)(1-x^{b})+x^{c}.

Hence, the remainder when
x^{k}
is divided by
1-x^{b}
equals
x^{c}=x^{k\%b}
.
∎

Furthermore, from the above lemma it is also easy to see that substitution of
x=\xi_{b}
in
h^{(j)}(x)
gives us

h^{(j)}(\xi_{b})=\left.\textnormal{eval}\left(D_{b}^{j}h(x);1-x^{b}\right)\right\rvert_{x=\xi_{b}}=D^{j}_{b}h(\xi_{b}).

In particular, by the substitution rule Lemma
3.1.2
, we have
h^{(0)}(\xi_{b})=h(\xi_{b})
.

Now the Fourier series for the
j^{th}
term corresponding to
h^{(j)}(x)
can be written as

\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\frac{1}{(1-x^{b})^{r-j-1}}\frac{h^{(j)}(x)}{(1-x^{b})}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n}a_{1}(n^{\prime})a_{2}(n-n^{\prime})x^{n},

(34)

where
a_{1}(n)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\begin{pmatrix}\lfloor n/b\rfloor-r-j-2\\
r+j+2\end{pmatrix}
and
a_{2}(n)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}h_{j}(\xi_{b}^{k})\xi_{b}^{-nk}
. In particular, for
j=0
we have

\frac{1}{(1-x^{b})^{r-1}}\frac{h^{(0)}(x)}{(1-x^{b})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}\lfloor n^{\prime}/b\rfloor-r-2\\
r+2\end{pmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}h(\xi_{b}^{k})\xi_{b}^{-(n-n^{\prime})k}\right)x^{n}.

(35)

Finally, the Fourier series for
h(x)/(1-x^{b})^{r}
can be obtained by adding all the terms for
j=0,\cdots,(r-1)
from (
34
).

4
Preparation Lemmas

In this section, we obtain the eval of the functions
(1-x)^{k}
and
\Psi_{m}(x)
through basic partial fractions and prove some preparation lemmas for
q
-partial fractions. We start by performing partial fraction for

\frac{1}{1-x^{m}}=\frac{1}{(1-x)\Psi_{m}(x)}.

Differentiating and multiplying by
x
both sides of the equation

(1-x)\Psi_{m}(x)=1-x^{m}

we get

x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x)(1-x)+(-x)\Psi_{m}(x)=-mx^{m}.

Applying the Fundamental Lemma
2.1.2
we get

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x)}{-mx^{m}};\ \Psi_{m}(x)\right)(1-x)+\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{-x}{-mx^{m}};\ (1-x)\right)\Psi_{m}(x)=1.

Simplifying we get the partial fraction

\frac{1}{(1-x)\Psi_{m}(x)}=\frac{1}{m(1-x)}+\frac{(-\frac{1}{m}x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x))\;\textnormal{rem}\;\Psi_{m}(x)}{\Psi_{m}(x)}.

(36)

Proceeding inductively we can prove the following result.

Lemma 4.0.1
.

For
k\geq 1
the following partial fraction holds:

\frac{1}{(1-x)^{k}\Psi_{m}(x)}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{f_{j}^{(m)}(1)}{(1-x)^{k-j}}+\frac{f_{k}^{(m)}(x)}{\Psi_{m}(x)},

(37)

where
f_{j}^{(m)}(x)=(-\frac{1}{m}x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x))^{j}\;\textnormal{rem}\;\Psi_{m}(x)
and
f_{0}^{(m)}(x)=1
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
. The equation (
36
) is the base case
k=1
. Suppose the result holds for
k
. Multiplying both sides of (
37
) by
\frac{1}{1-x}
we get

\frac{1}{(1-x)^{k+1}\Psi_{m}(x)}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(m)}(1)(1-x)^{j}}{m(1-x)^{k+1}}+\frac{f_{k}^{(m)}(x)}{(1-x)\Psi_{m}(x)}

By performing partial fractions on the second term we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-x)^{k+1}\Psi_{m}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}f_{j}^{(m)}(1)(1-x)^{j}}{m(1-x)^{k+1}}+\frac{f_{k}^{(m)}(1)}{m(1-x)}+\frac{f_{k+1}^{(m)}(x)}{\Psi_{m}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k}f_{j}^{(m)}(1)(1-x)^{j}}{m(1-x)^{k+1}}+\frac{f_{k+1}^{(m)}(x)}{\Psi_{m}(x)}.

Hence the result is proved.
∎

Comparing the terms in equation (
28
) and the terms in equation (
37
) we are motivated to define the reciprocal degenerate Bernoulli polynomial

\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m,x)=f_{k}^{(m)}(x)=\left(-\frac{1}{m}x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x)\right)^{k}\;\textnormal{rem}\;\Psi_{m}(x).

(38)

The reciprocal degenerate Bernoulli number is given by
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)=\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m,1).

Next, we prove another preparation lemma for
q
-partial fractions.

Lemma 4.0.2
.

Let
\textnormal{gcd}(m,n)=1
and let
a,b>0
satisfying
am-bn=1
. Then,

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{\Psi_{m}(x)};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}x^{jm\%n}\;\textnormal{rem}\;\Psi_{n}(x).

(39)

Proof.

Observe that we can write

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{\Psi_{m}(x)};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1-x}{1-x^{m}};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right).

Upon multiplying and dividing by
(x^{(a-1)m}+\cdots+1)
we have

\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1-x}{1-x^{m}};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)=\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{(1-x)(x^{(a-1)m}+\cdots+1)}{1-x^{am}};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right).

(40)

By Lemma
2.0.4
(3), we can replace any one of the equivalent polynomials modulo
\Psi_{n}(x)
at both numerator and the denominator. Moreover, by the Lemma
3.1.1
when we perform modulo
\Psi_{n}(x)
we can first work modulo
(1-x^{n})
and replacing
x^{n}
by
1
. As
am-bn=1
, we have

1-x^{am}=1-x^{am}x^{-bn}=1-x^{am-bn}=1-x\mod(1-x^{n}).

Therefore, from (
40
) we can write

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{\Psi_{m}(x)};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{(1-x)(x^{(a-1)m}+\cdots+1)}{1-x};\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{eval}\left(x^{(a-1)m}+\cdots+1;\ \Psi_{n}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}x^{jm\%n}\;\textnormal{rem}\;\Psi_{n}(x).

∎

The above result can be made precise to provide an exact partial fraction formula. As the proof can be directly obtained with the help of above lemma, we state the result without proof.

Theorem 4.0.3
.

Let
m,n
be two integers that are coprime and, let
m\geq 2
and
n\geq 3
. Consider the smallest possible positive numbers
a,b,\alpha,\beta
for which we have
am-bn=1
and
\alpha n-\beta m=1
. If
a>\beta
then the partial fraction is given by

\frac{1-x}{(1-x^{m})(1-x^{n})}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\alpha-1}x^{jn\%m}}{1-x^{m}}-\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\beta-1}x^{(jm+1)\%n}}{1-x^{n}}

else if
a<\beta
we have the partial fraction

\frac{1-x}{(1-x^{m})(1-x^{n})}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}x^{jm\%n}}{1-x^{n}}-\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}x^{(jn+1)\%m}}{1-x^{m}}.

A similar result but addressing a different case is considered using iterated Laurent series in
[
29
, Proposition 4.3, Lemma 4.5]
.

5
q
-Partial Fractions and Denumerants

Consider the general rational polynomial for
q
-partial fractions

F(x)=\frac{p(x)}{(1-x)^{m}(1-x^{n_{1}})^{r_{1}}\cdots(1-x^{n_{k}})^{r_{k}}},

(41)

where
n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}
are pairwise relatively primes,
s=r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}
,
\textnormal{deg }(p)<m+r_{1}n_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}n_{k}
. We determine the
q
-partial fraction of (
41
) by the factorized expression

\frac{p(x)}{(1-x)^{m+s}\Psi_{n_{1}}(x)^{r_{1}}\cdots\Psi_{n_{k}}(x)^{r_{k}}}=\frac{h_{0}(x)}{(1-x)^{m+s}}+\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{h_{j}(x)}{(1-x^{n_{j}})^{r_{j}}}.

(42)

By the extended cover-up method we have

h_{0}(x)=\left(p(x)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{1}{\Psi_{n_{j}}(x)^{r_{j}}};(1-x)^{m+s}\right)\right)\textnormal{ rem }(1-x)^{m+s}.

By expanding the terms of
h_{0}(x)
using Table
1
we have

h_{0}(x)=\sum^{m+s-1}_{j=0}c_{j}(1-x)^{j},

(43)

where, for
0\leq j<m+s
,

c_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots n_{k}^{r_{k}}}\sum_{i_{0}+i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k}=j}(-1)^{j}\frac{D^{i_{0}}p(1)}{i_{0}!}\frac{\tilde{\beta}^{(r_{1})}_{i_{1}}(n_{1})\cdots\tilde{\beta}^{(r_{k})}_{i_{k}}(n_{k})}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{k}!},

\tilde{\beta}_{k}^{(r)}(n)
is the reciprocal degenerate Bernoulli of order
r
given in (
29
).

Again, by the extended cover-up method we have

h_{j}(x)=(1-x)^{r_{j}}\textnormal{eval}\left(\frac{p(x)}{(1-x)^{m+s}}\prod_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i=1}}{{i\neq j}}}^{k}\frac{1}{\Psi_{n_{i}}(x)^{r_{i}}};\ \Psi_{n_{j}}(x)^{r_{j}}\right),

(44)

where
\textnormal{deg}(h_{j})<n_{j}r_{j}-1
. This polynomial
h_{j}(x)
can be obtained by Table
1
.

In order to obtain a Fourier series expansion (not necessarily finite) we write

\frac{h_{j}(x)}{(1-x^{n_{j}})^{r_{j}}}=\sum^{r_{j}-1}_{i=0}\frac{h_{ij}(x)}{(1-x^{n_{j}})^{r_{j}-i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{r_{j}-1}\frac{1}{(1-x^{n_{j}})^{r_{j}-i-1}}\frac{h_{ij}(x)}{(1-x^{n_{j}})},

(45)

where
\textnormal{deg}(h_{ij})<n_{j}
and use the finite Fourier series expansion of
h_{ij}(x)/(1-x^{n_{j}})
. By Theorem
3.2.1
we can indeed express
h_{j}(x)
in terms of
h_{ij}(x)
, which is the remainder when
D_{r_{j}}^{i}h_{j}(x)
is divided by
1-x^{n_{j}}
. Therefore,
\textnormal{deg}(h_{ij})<n_{j}
. In particular,
h_{0j}(x)
is the remainder when
h_{j}(x)
is divided by
1-x^{n_{j}}
. This establishes Theorem
1.1.2
.

5.1
Computing the Denumerants

We express the denumerants in a concise manner using the piecewise linear function
\lfloor\frac{t}{n}\rfloor
, the greatest integer less than or equal to
\frac{t}{n}
. Suppose
h_{ij}(x)=\sum^{n_{j}-1}_{\alpha=0}c_{\alpha}^{(ij)}x^{\alpha}
. Then, by (
42
) and (
45
), the denumerant is given by

d(t;\textbf{A})=\sum^{m+s-1}_{j=0}c_{j}\begin{pmatrix}t+m+s-j-1\\
t\end{pmatrix}+\sum^{k}_{j=1}\sum^{r_{j}-1}_{i=0}\begin{pmatrix}\lfloor\frac{t}{n_{j}}\rfloor+r_{j}-i-1\\
r_{j}-i-1\end{pmatrix}c_{t\%n_{j}}^{(ij)}.

Assuming the multiplications of the polynomials is done using the FFT algorithm, the computational cost of setting up the equations for computing the denumerant requires
O(n\log n)
steps, where
n=\max\{n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\}
, which is polynomially bound on the numeric values of the input.

Setting
(r_{1},\cdots,r_{k})=(1,\cdots,1)
in the above formula we obtain the corresponding denumerant given in (
16
). For the simplest case
p(x)=1
and
m=0
, we have by Theorem
1.1.2
and Lemma
4.0.1

c_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{1}\cdots n_{k}}\sum_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{k}=j}f^{(n_{1})}_{i_{1}}(1)\cdots f^{(n_{k})}_{i_{k}}(1),\quad\textnormal{ for }0\leq j\leq k-1,

and denoting
h_{j}(x)=\sum^{n_{j}-1}_{i=0}c_{i}^{(j)}x^{i}
the denumerant is given by

d(t;n_{1},\cdots,n_{k})=\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}c_{j}\begin{pmatrix}t+k-j-1\\
t\\
\end{pmatrix}+\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{1}{n_{j}}c^{(j)}_{t\%n_{j}}.

(46)

By this formula one can notice that for the pairwise relatively prime case (and without repetitions) the denumerant is indeed a quasi-polynomial with the periodicity only in the constant term (as observed in
[
5
]
).

Remark 6.0.1

6.1

We provide a purely algebraic proof that the reciprocal polynomial of the degenerate Bernoulli number
\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m)
is a polynomial in
m
, where

\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\tilde{\beta}_{k}(m,x)=\left(-\frac{1}{m}x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x)\right)^{k}\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{m}(x).

Towards this direction, we denote
g_{k}(x)=\left(-x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x)\right)^{k}\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{m}(x)
and show that
g_{k}(1)
is a polynomial in
m
with a factor
m^{k}
. For the purpose of proving this claim we use the well known fact that
m(m+1)
is a factor of the sum of powers
\sigma_{n}(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}i^{n}
.

Lemma 6.1.1
.

The function
\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{k}(x)
, for
n=0,1,2,\cdots,
when evaluated at
x=1
is a polynomial in
m
with zero as a root of multiplicity at least
k
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
. The result holds for
k=1
case. Indeed, by a direct calculation we have

g_{1}(x)=x^{m-2}+\cdots+(m-2)x+(m-1),

which implies
g_{1}(1)=m(m-1)/2
and

\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{1}(x)=(m-2)^{n}\cdot 1x^{m-2}+(m-3)^{n}\cdot 2x^{m-3}+\cdots+1^{n}\cdot(m-2)x

implies

\displaystyle\left.\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{1}(x)\right|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m-2)^{n}\cdot 1+(m-3)^{n}\cdot 2+\cdots+1^{n}\cdot(m-2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m-1)\sigma_{n}(m-1)-\sigma_{n+1}(m-1),

which is also divisible by
m
.

For the induction step, suppose
g_{k}(x)=\alpha_{m-2}x^{m-2}+\cdots+\alpha_{1}x+\alpha_{0}
, where the coefficients
\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{m-2}
are dependent on
m
and
k
, and assume
\left.\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{k}(x)\right|_{x=1}=\sum_{t=0}^{m-2}t^{n}\alpha_{t}
have
m^{k}
as a factor for
n=0,1,\cdots
.

Consider the
(k+1)
case

\displaystyle g_{k+1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(g_{k}(x)(-x\Psi^{\prime}_{m}(x))\right)\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i,j=0,0}^{m-2,m-2}(m-1-j)\alpha_{i}x^{i+j}\right)\textnormal{ rem }\Psi_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i,j=0,0}}{{(i+j)\%m\neq(m-1)}}}^{m-2,m-2}\alpha_{i}(m-1-j)x^{(i+j)\%m}\right)+\left(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i,j=0,0}}{{(i+j)\%m=(m-1)}}}^{m-2,m-2}\alpha_{i}(m-1-j)(x^{m-1}-\Psi_{m}(x))\right),

here we substitute for
x^{m-1}
by
(x^{m-1}-\Psi_{m}(x))
using the Substitution Rule 1 (Lemma
30
). Simplifying we obtain

g_{k+1}(x)=\sum_{i,j=0,0}^{m-2,m-2}\alpha_{i}(m-1-j)x^{(i+j)\%m}-(1+x+\cdots+x^{m-1})\sum_{t=1}^{m-2}t\alpha_{t}.

and substituting
x=1

\displaystyle g_{k+1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0,0}^{m-2,m-2}\alpha_{i}(m-1-j)-m\sum_{t=1}^{m-2}t\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}\frac{m-1}{2}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{t=1}^{m-2}t\alpha_{t}\right)=m^{k+1}\times(\textnormal{polynomial in }m),

where the last statement holds by the induction hypothesis.

Now we show that
\left.\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{k+1}(x)\right|_{x=1}
also has
m^{k+1}
as a factor. Observe that

\left.\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{k}(x)\right|_{x=1}=\sum_{t=0}^{m-2}t^{n}\alpha_{t}

and

\left.\left(xD\right)^{n}g_{k+1}(x)\right|_{x=1}=\sum_{t=0}^{m-2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t-2}i^{n}(t-1-i)+\sum_{i=k}^{m-1}i^{n}(m-1+t-i)\right)\alpha_{t}+\sigma_{n}(m-1)\sum_{t=0}^{m-2}t^{n}\alpha_{t}.

(57)

Fortunately, we don’t need to determine a formula for the summations; instead, we just need to show that
m
is a factor. The inner sum in the right hand side of the first term of the equation (

) can be simplified to

(m-1)t^{n}+\cdots+t(m-1)^{n}=(m-1+t)\left\{\sigma_{n}(m-1)-\sigma_{n+1}(t-1)\right\}-\left\{\sigma_{n+1}(m-1)-\sigma_{n+1}(t-1)\right\}.

Furthermore, setting
m=0
we get

-(t-1)\sigma_{n+1}(t-1)+\sigma_{n+1}(t-1).
Also, the second term is

\displaystyle(t-2)\cdot 1^{n}+\cdots+1\cdot(t-2)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t-1)\sigma_{n}(t-1)-\sigma_{n+1}(t-1)

Thus, the factor

vanishes when
