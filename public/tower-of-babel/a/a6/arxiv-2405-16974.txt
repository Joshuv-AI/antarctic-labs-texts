Inherent quantum resources in stationary spin chains
Marcin Płodzień, Jan Chwedeńczuk, Maciej Lewenstein

Abstract
The standard way to generate many-body quantum correlations is via a dynamical protocol: an initial product state is transformed by interactions that generate non-classical correlations at later times. Here, we show that many-body Bell correlations are inherently present in the eigenstates of a variety of spin-1/2 chains. In particular, we show that the eigenstates and thermal states of the collective Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model possess many-body Bell correlations. We demonstrate that the Bell correlations present in the eigenstates of the Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model can take on quantized values that change discontinuously with variations in the total magnetization. Finally, we show that these many-body Bell correlations persist even in the presence of both diagonal and off-diagonal disorder.

II.1
The Lipkin-Meskhov-Glick model

The LMG model describes a family of 1D chains of
L
spin-
1/2
particles depicted by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i<j=1}^{L}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{x}^{(i)}\hat{\sigma}_{x}^{(j)}+\gamma\hat{\sigma}_{y}^{(i)}\hat{\sigma}_{y}^{(j)}\right)-h\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}.

(1)

Here
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(i)}
is the
n
th Pauli matrix of the
i
th spin (
n=x,y,z
,
i\in\{1\ldots L\}
), while the anisotropy parameter
\gamma
and the magnetic field coupling constant
h
differentiate
the members of this family. The pair-wise interactions are of infinite range, which allows to express the Hamiltonian using the collective-spin operators

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(i)}

(2)

as follows (up to an additive
L
-dependent constant)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\gamma}=-\frac{2}{L}\left(\hat{S}_{x}^{2}+\gamma\hat{S}_{y}^{2}\right)-2h\hat{S}_{z}.

(3)

The spectrum of this Hamiltonian is spanned by
L+1
symmetric Dicke states, namely the eigenstates of the total spin operator
\mathbf{\hat{S}}^{2}=\hat{S}_{x}^{2}+\hat{S}_{y}^{2}+\hat{S}_{z}^{2}

and, for instance, the
\hat{S}_{z}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{S}}^{2}|S,m\rangle

\displaystyle=S(S+1)|S,m\rangle

(4a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{z}|S,m\rangle

\displaystyle=m|S,m\rangle,

(4b)

where
S=L/2
and
m\in\{-S,\ldots,S\}
.

Here we focus on the three emblematic cases of the LMG model, when the anisotropy parameter takes the values
\gamma=-1
,
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
, giving the Hamiltonians
(again, up to an additive constant)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{-1}=-\frac{2}{L}\left(\hat{S}_{x}^{2}-\hat{S}_{y}^{2}\right)-2h\hat{S}_{z},

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=-\frac{2}{L}\hat{S}_{x}^{2}-2h\hat{S}_{z},

(5b)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{+1}=\frac{2}{L}\hat{S}_{z}^{2}-2h\hat{S}_{z}.

(5c)

Particularly interesting is the
\gamma=1
, where the spectrum is spanned by the Dicke states
|S,m\rangle
’s, giving the parabolic dependence of the eigenenergies on
m
, i.e.,

\displaystyle E_{m}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}m^{2}-2hm.

(6)

Its minimal energy is reached when
m
is equal to

\displaystyle m=m_{0}(h)\equiv I\left[\frac{hL}{2}\right],

(7)

i.e., the closest integer (for even
L
) or half-integer (
L
odd) to the minimum of this function.
The ground state is hence

\displaystyle|\psi^{(h)}_{0}\rangle=|S,m_{0}(h)\rangle

(8)

For instance, when
h=0
, the ground state is

\displaystyle|\psi^{(h=0)}_{0}\rangle=|S,0\rangle

(9)

and the double-degenerated excited states lie symmetrically at
m
positive and negative.
On the other hand, when
h
is so large that
m_{0}\geqslant S
the ground-state is

\displaystyle|\psi^{(h)}_{0}\rangle=|S,S\rangle

(10)

and the excited part of the spectrum is spanned by all the
m
’s smaller than
S
—an observation that will be relevant in the Results section, Sec.
III
.

This concludes the preliminary section on the LMG model. We now introduce a correlation function that turns out to be a useful tool that allows us to certify the presence and the strength of many-body
Bell correlations in this system.

II.2
Bell correlations

When assessing the presence and the strength of Bell correlations in an
L
-body system, we must refer to a corresponding model that is consistent with the
postulates of local realism. Here, we assume that each of
L
parties can measure a pair of observables
\sigma^{(k)}_{x}
and
\sigma^{(k)}_{y}
, each yielding binary outcomes, i.e.,
\sigma_{x/y}=\pm 1
,
with
k\in\{1\ldots L\}
. The correlation function (also referred to as the correlator) of these results is here defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}=\left|\langle\prod_{k=1}^{L}\sigma_{+}^{(k)}\rangle\right|^{2},

(11)

with
\sigma_{+}^{(k)}=1/2(\sigma_{x}^{(k)}+i\sigma_{y}^{(k)})
. This correlation is consistent with the local and realistic theory if the average can be expressed in terms of an integral
over the random (“hidden”) variable
\lambda
distributed with a probability density
p(\lambda)
as follows

\displaystyle\langle\prod_{k=1}^{L}\sigma_{+}^{(k)}\rangle=\int\!\!d\lambda\,p(\lambda)\prod_{k=1}^{L}\sigma_{+}^{(k)}(\lambda)

(12)

We substitute this expression into Eq.(
11
) and use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for complex integrals together with the fact that
\left|\sigma_{+}^{(k)}(\lambda)\right|^{2}=1/2
for all
k
,
giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}

\displaystyle=\left|\int\!\!d\lambda\,p(\lambda)\prod_{k=1}^{L}\sigma_{+}^{(k)}(\lambda)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\!\!d\lambda\,p(\lambda)\prod_{k=1}^{L}\left|\sigma_{+}^{(k)}(\lambda)\right|^{2}=2^{-L}.

(13)

We thus conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}\leqslant 2^{-L}

(14)

is the
L
-body Bell inequality, because its violation defies the postulates of local realism expressed in Eq. (
12
). It is valid for systems that yield binary outcomes of local,
single-particle observables. This is the case of the
L
-spin LMG model, hence the quantum-mechanical equivalent of Eq. (
11
), labeled with (
q
) and defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}=\left|\langle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{L}\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}\rangle\right|^{2},

(15)

will be used in the remainder of this work to witness Bell correlations. For convenience, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}=\log_{2}\left(2^{L}\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}\right)

(16)

with which the Bell inequality from Eq. (
14
) takes a more “user-friendly” form

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}\leqslant 0.

(17)

The value of the Bell correlator
{\cal Q}^{(q)}_{L}
provides information about the structure of the many-body quantum state and the strength of many-body Bell correlations. Namely, the maximal value of the correlator that is reachable by quantum mechanical systems is
\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}=L-2
, which is obtained with the Greenberg-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state

\displaystyle|\psi_{\rm ghz}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|1\rangle^{\otimes L}+|0\rangle^{\otimes L}\right).

(18)

Next, all the values of
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}
that lie in the region
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}\in]0,L-2]
signal the presence of Bell correlations of different strength. In particular, when

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}-(n+1)<\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}-n

(19)

with
n\in\mathds{N}
, then {the state is maximally
(n+1)
-local. This means that the state can be expressed in terms of maximally
n+1
subsystems, mutually locally correlated.
For instance, when the state is 2-local, the maximal value of the correlator is reached when
L-1
parties form a quantum-mechanical GHZ state, giving the value of the correlator
\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{4}

while the remaining one is
a particle measurements on which yield binary outcomes, just as duscussed below Eq. (
11
). In this case, the correlator is

\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{2}
giving

\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}=\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}-1
.
Naturally, a 2-local state can have weaker correlations. For instance when the state is a product of two GHZ states of
L/2
particles each, then
\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{4}
giving
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}=\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}-2
.
When the system is 3-local, then again the
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}
is maximal in the scenario, when
L-2
particles form a GHZ state and the remaining two are mutually Bell-uncorrelated, giving
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}=\mathcal{Q}_{L,{\rm max}}^{(q)}-2
.
Now the pattern is established, i.e., a state characterized by maximally
(n+1)
-locality can reproduce Eq. (
19
).
As a last example, we can consider a
L
-spins state being a product of
n
separable parties, each yielding binary outcomes, and not restricted by quantum mechanics, and
k
products of GHZ states, then
\mathcal{E}_{L}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4^{k}}=\frac{1}{2^{2k+n}}
.
The concept of
k
-locality is a close analogy of
k
-separability of quantum states

[
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
,
97
,
98
,
99
]
.
Note that the considered Bell correlator works for
L\geqslant 3
and for
n<L-2
. For
L=2
the GHZ state from Eq. (
18
), though Bell correlated, gives
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}=0
.
When
L
is larger but

(L-1)
-local. Hence maximally a pair can form a Bell-correlated state and according to the above, this is not recognized by
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}

according to Eq. (

II.2.1
Bell correlator for symmetric states

The LMG infinite-range model requires a symmetrized Bell correlator, i.e., such that uses the collective spin operators as defined in Eq. (
2
). The product of
L
rising operators

\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}
that address individual particles, as present in Eq. (
15
), should be replaced as follows

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{k=1}^{L}\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ \hat{S}_{+}^{L}.

(21)

Note however, that when expressing the collective rising operator using Eq. (
2
) we have

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{+}^{L}=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{L}\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}\right]^{L},

(22)

which gives
L!
times more ordered products of
L
distinct operators. This combinatorial factor, stemming from the symmetry of the setup, must be taken into account.
Hence the inequality (
14
) adapted to the symmetric case (denoted by the tilde) reads

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{(q)}=\left|\frac{1}{L!}\langle\hat{S}_{+}^{L}\rangle\right|^{2}\leqslant 2^{-L}

(23)

or in analogy to Eq. (
17
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}^{(q)}=\log_{2}\left(2^{L}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{(q)}\right)\leqslant 0.

(24)

For a detailed discussion of the symmetrized many-body Bell correlators, see
[
111
]
.

II.2.2
Optimized correlator

According to the previous sections, the correlator
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}
takes a product of
L
operators rising the spin projection along the
z
-axis, each in the form of

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{x}^{(k)}+i\hat{\sigma}_{y}^{(k)}\right)

(25)

or, for the symmetrized case, the
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}^{(q)}
is fed with

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{+}=\hat{S}_{x}+i\hat{S}_{y}.

(26)

Naturally, none of the arguments invoked above leading to the formulation of the Bell inequality (
17
), does change upon a choice of different orientations of local axes.
We can always prepare the rotation of the rising operator for the
k
-th spin,

\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}\to\hat{R}_{k}^{\dagger}(\vec{\theta}_{k})\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{(k)}\hat{R}_{k}(\vec{\theta}_{k}),

(27)

where
\hat{R}_{k}(\vec{\theta}_{k})=e^{-i\vec{\theta}_{k}\cdot\vec{\sigma}_{k}}
,
\vec{\theta}_{k}=\{\theta^{(k)}_{x},\theta^{(k)}_{y},\theta^{(k)}_{z}\}
,
\vec{\sigma}_{k}=\{\hat{\sigma}_{x}^{(k)},\hat{\sigma}_{y}^{(k)},\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(k)}\}
,
or for the symmetrized case

\hat{S}_{+}\to\hat{P}^{\dagger}(\vec{\theta})\hat{S}_{+}\hat{P}(\vec{\theta}),

(28)

\hat{P}(\vec{\theta})=e^{-i\vec{\theta}\cdot\vec{S}}
,
\vec{\theta}=\{\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z}\}
,
\vec{S}=\{\hat{S}_{x},\hat{S}_{y},\hat{S}_{z}\}
.
This freedom of choice allows us to optimize the correlator
\mathcal{Q}_{L}^{(q)}
,
i.e., adapt it to the geometry of the many-body state that is under consideration.

In practice the correlator is optimized numerically and its maximal value is found. For the LMG model as considered here, the permutational symmetry of the Dicke states
implies that the optimization procedure is collective. However, in general, when such spin-exchange symmetry is not present, the axes must be
determined individually for each particle.

In the following section, we present the optimized correlator
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}^{(q)}
for the LMG eigenstates.
Before we discuss the results, let us stress again that the many-body Bell correlations are encoded in the GHZ coherence term of the density matrix, see Eq. (
20
), and that
the optimization of the correlator (Heisenberg picture) is equivalent to rotating the density matrix itself (Schrödinger picture).

The action of such a rotation operator on the Dicke state is

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{S}_{y}}|S,m\rangle=\sum_{m^{\prime}=-S}^{S}d_{m;m^{\prime}}^{S}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)|S,m^{\prime}\rangle,

(31)

where

represents an element of the Wigner matrix in the fixed-
S
subspace
3
3

3

See
[
varshalovich1988quantum
]
for the details on the rotation matrices in the SU(2) group
.
As argued below Eq. (

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{(q)}=\left|d_{m;S}^{S}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)d_{m;-S}^{S}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right|^{2}=\left[\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L}{m+\frac{L}{2}}\right]^{2}.

When

For

It is always maximal at

III.2
Thermal states

The next question we answer is about the impact of thermal fluctuations on the Bell correlations in the LMG models.
To investigate this problem, we calculate the thermal density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{T}=\frac{1}{{\cal Z}}\sum_{v}e^{-E_{v}/T}|\psi_{v}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{v}|,

(35)

\gamma

{\cal Z}=\sum_{v}e^{-E_{v}/T}
is the statistical sum and the summation runs over the whole spectrum. Next, we calculate the expectation value of the many-body Bell correlator,

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{(q)}(T)=\big|\frac{1}{L!}{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\varrho}_{T}\hat{P}^{\dagger}(\vec{\theta})\hat{S}_{+}^{(x)L}\hat{P}(\vec{\theta})\right]\big|^{2}

(36)

optimized over
\vec{\theta}
.

L

III.2.2
Bell correlator for
T\geqslant 0
and
\gamma=0
case

Some insight into the structure of the
T-h
graph shown in Fig.
3

can be obtained also for
\gamma=0
. In this case the LMG system can be modelled with an approximate Schrödinger-like equation, as explained below.
We write the stationary Schrodinger equation, acting with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\gamma=0}

on the state decomposed in the basis of eigen-states of
\hat{S}_{x}
operator, namely

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{m=-L/2}^{L/2}\psi_{m}|S,m\rangle

(41)

which, projected onto
|S,m\rangle
, reads

\displaystyle hL\left[\psi_{m+1}f_{+}(z_{m})+\psi_{m-1}f_{-}(z_{m})\right]-\frac{L}{2}\psi_{m}z^{2}_{m}=E\psi_{m}.

(42)

Here, we introduced a normalized variable

\displaystyle z_{m}=2\frac{m}{L}

(43)

that changes between -1 and 1. Also, the coefficients
f_{\pm}(z_{m})
present in Eq. (
42
) are defined as follows

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(z_{m})=\sqrt{\frac{1\mp z_{m}}{2}\left(\frac{1\pm z_{m}}{2}+\frac{1}{L}\right)}.

(44)

For large
L
, the
1/L
terms in these square-roots can be neglected and
z_{m}
becomes quasi-continuous, allowing to approximate
the difference between the state coefficients
\psi_{m\pm 1}
with the second derivative over
z
. This gives a Schrödinger-like equation for the wave-function
\psi_{m}\rightarrow\psi(z)
of a
fictitious unit-mass particle

\displaystyle\left(-\left(\frac{2}{L}\right)^{2}\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+V_{\rm eff}(z)\right)\psi(z)=\tilde{E}\psi(z),

(45)

where the effective potential is

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(z)=-\sqrt{1-z^{2}}+z^{2}\kappa/2

(46)

and
\kappa=\frac{1}{h}
. The details of this derivation can be found in Ref.
[
129
]
.

Crucially, the spectrum of the Eq. (
45
) depends on the value of
\kappa
. When it is large, the second term of Eq. (
46
) dominates giving an effective potential that is close
to harmonic. Interestingly, when
\kappa
surpasses the threshold value
\kappa=-1
, the potential develops two minima located at
\pm z_{0}
, where
z_{0}=(1-1/\kappa^{2})^{1/2}
and becomes

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(z)\simeq\frac{1}{2}\kappa(1-\kappa^{2})(z\pm z_{0})^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(47)

The ground state tends to localize around these minima and form a macroscopic superposition

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm gr}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\psi_{0}(z-z_{0})+\psi_{0}(z+z_{0})\right],

(48)

where
\psi_{0}(z\pm z_{0})
is the Gaussian ground state of each of the the harmonic contributions to Eq. (
47
).
When this superposition forms, the Bell correlator
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}^{(q)}
becomes large, as it is exactly this type of macroscopic superposition that it is sensitive to, see Eq. (
20
).
This is why the central column of Fig.
3
shows non-vansing Bell correlator when
h
is sufficiently small.

However, an important feature of this plot is yet to be explained—namely why the
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{L}^{(q)}
seems more vulnerable to thermal fluctuations when
h
is very small (i.e., the area of non-zero Bell correlations
shrinks with
h\rightarrow 0
).

To explain this behavior, note that as
h
drops, so that
\kappa\rightarrow-\infty
,
the ground state (
48
) becomes (assymptotically) degenerate with the first excited state

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm 1^{st}}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\psi_{0}(z-z_{0})-\psi_{0}(z+z_{0})\right].

(49)

In this regime, any non-zero value of
T
will lead to occupation of both these states. The GHZ coherences of Eqs (
48
) and (
49
) have opposite signs, therefore
the Bell correlations quickly degrade in this thermal mixture. This is a qualitative explanation, for a more quantitative analysis, see
[
130
]
.
When
h\simeq 0
, the ground state is the GHZ state, giving the maximal Bell correlator, but only at
T=0
. This is represented
by a very thin and dark line in the central column of Fig.
3
.

IV
Outlook and Conclusions

This work addressed the problem of generating the strongest possible and scalable many-body quantum correlations in spin-
1/2
chains.
We showed that the creation of quantum states with strong many-body Bell correlations does not necessarily have to be done via dynamical protocols such as one-axis twisting.

We showed that many-body Bell correlations are inherently present in the eigenstates of a broad family of spin-
1/2
models described by the
Lipkin-Meskhov-Glick model with all-to-all and power-law decaying couplings. Next, we showed that inherent many-body Bell correlations are also present in the thermal states of the LMG model, and we estimated the critical temperature above which Bell correlations vanish for the one-axis twisting Hamiltonian. Finally, we showed that the many-body Bell correlations are robust to disorder potential. We hope that this work will shed some light on the properties of the many-body Bell correlations beyond the pure state description
[
128
,
31
]
.

The remaining question is whether there are possibilities of experimental certification of the many-body Bell correlations in the quantum systems. The measurement of the off-diagonal GHZ element of the density matrix in
L=4
qubit system has been prepared experimentally
[
143
]
.
Recently it was shown the many-body Bell correlations can be extracted with the quantum state tomography via the classical shadows technique
[
44
]
up to
L=10
spins.
Another approach is a technique based on multiple quantum coherence
[
144
,
145
]
,
which allows to access to individual elements of the density matrix, including those that encode information about many-body Bell correlations.
