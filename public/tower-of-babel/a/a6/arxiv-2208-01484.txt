Pattern-Avoiding Fishburn Permutations and Ascent Sequences
Eric S. Egge

Abstract
A Fishburn permutation is a permutation which avoids the bivincular pattern ( 231 , { 1 } , { 1 } ) (231,\{1\},\{1\}) , while an ascent sequence is a sequence of nonnegative integers in which each entry is less than or equal to one more than the number of ascents to its left. Fishburn permutations and ascent sequences are linked by a bijection g g of Bousquet-Mélou, Claesson, Dukes, and Kitaev. We write F n ​ ( σ 1 , … , σ k ) F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}) to denote the set of Fishburn permutations of length n n which avoid each of σ 1 , … , σ k \sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k} and we write A n ​ ( α 1 , … , α k ) A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}) to denote the set of ascent sequences which avoid each of α 1 , … , α k \alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k} . We settle a conjecture of Gil and Weiner by showing that g g restricts to a bijection between F n ​ ( 3412 ) F_{n}(3412) and A n ​ ( 201 ) A_{n}(201) . Building on work of Gil and Weiner, we use elementary techniques to enumerate F n ​ ( 123 ) F_{n}(123) with respect to inversion number and number of left-to-right maxima, obtaining expressions in terms of q q -binomial coefficients, and to enumerate F n ​ ( 123 , σ ) F_{n}(123,\sigma) for all σ \sigma . We use generating tree techniques to study the generating functions for F n ​ ( 321 , 1423 ) F_{n}(321,1423) , F n ​ ( 321 , 3124 ) F_{n}(321,3124) , and F n ​ ( 321 , 2143 ) F_{n}(321,2143) with respect to inversion number and number of left-to-right maxima. We use these results to show | F n ​ ( 321 , 1423 ) | = | F n ​ ( 321 , 3124 ) | = F n + 2 − n − 1 |F_{n}(321,1423)|=|F_{n}(321,3124)|=F_{n+2}-n-1 , where F n F_{n} is a Fibonacci number, and | F n ​ ( 321 , 2143 ) | = 2 n − 1 |F_{n}(321,2143)|=2^{n-1} . We conclude with a variety of conjectures and open problems.

Gil and Weiner

3
The Correspondence between
F_{n}(3412)
and
A_{n}(201)

In the last section of
[
23
]
, Gil and Weiner suggest investigating the relationship between pattern-avoid Fishburn permutations and pattern-avoiding ascent sequences.
As a step in this direction, they seem to suggest
A_{n}(120)
might be in bijection with
F_{n}(4123)
for all

.
However, a computer search shows that
|A_{10}(120)|=20754
while
|F_{10}(4123)|=20753
.
On the other hand, they also allude to data in their Table 3 showing that
|F_{n}(3412)|=|A_{n}(201)|
for
0\leq n\leq 8
.
In this section we show that the map
g
of Bousquet-Mélou, Claesson, Dukes, and Kitaev from Fishburn permutations to ascent sequences restricts to a bijection from
F_{n}(3412)
to
A_{n}(201)
.
We note that Cerbai and Claesson
[
8
]
have recently used a more general approach to address this problem.

We begin by describing how inserting an entry into an active site in a Fishburn permutation affects the set of active sites.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
,
\sigma\in S_{n-1}
is a Fishburn permutation, and
\pi
is the Fishburn permutation we obtain by inserting
n
into an active site of
\sigma
.
Then the following hold.

(i)

If a site is not active in
\sigma
then it is not active in
\pi
.

(ii)

If a site is active in
\sigma
and it is not the site into which
n
was inserted to obtain
\pi
, then it is active in
\pi
.

(iii)

The site immediately to the left of
n
in
\pi
is active.

(iv)

The site immediately to the right of
n
in
\pi
is active if and only if
n
is to the right of
n-1
in
\pi
.

Proof.

(i)
If a site is not active in
\sigma
then inserting
n
into that site creates a copy
a,n,a-1
of
f
.
If we insert
n+1
into that site then it will be adjacent to
a
(since we inserted
n
elsewhere to obtain
\pi
) and
a,n+1,a-1
will also be a copy of
f
.
Therefore the site is not active in
\pi
.

(ii)
We prove the contrapositive.
If a site is not active in
\pi
then inserting
n+1
into it will create a copy
a,n+1,a-1
of
f
.
Since the site is not adjacent to
n
in
\pi
, we must have
a<n
.
So if we insert
n
into the site in
\sigma
then
a,n,a-1
will be a copy of
f
, which means the site is not active in
\sigma
.

(iii)
Suppose by way of contradiction that the site immediately to the left of
n
in
\pi
is not active.
Then inserting
n+1
into the site will create a copy
a,n+1,a-1
of
f
.
But
a
is to the left of
n+1
, which is to the left of
n
, so
a,n,a-1
is a copy of
f
in
\pi
, contradicting our assumption that
\pi
avoids
f
.

(iv)
The permutation we obtain from
\pi
by inserting
n+1
immediately to the right of
n
can contain
f
if and only if
n,n+1,n-1
is a copy of
f
.
But this occurs if and only if
n
is to the left of
n-1
in
\pi
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
allows us to connect the relative magnitudes of some entries of
g(\pi)
with the order in which the corresponding entries appear in
\pi
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\pi\in S_{n}
is a Fishburn permutation with
g(\pi)=\alpha
.
If
j<k
and
\alpha_{k}\leq\alpha_{j}
then
k
is to the left of
j
in
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in S_{j}
be the Fishburn permutation we obtain from
\pi
by removing all entries greater than
j
.
Note that
g(\sigma)=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{j}
.
By Lemma
3.1
(iii) we know the site immediately to the left of
j
in
\sigma
is active, and by Lemma
3.1
(i)(ii) we know its label is
\alpha_{j}
.
Furthermore, each time we insert a new entry there will be at least
\alpha_{j}+1
active sites to the left of

3.1
(i)–(iii).
In other words, at every stage the sites with smaller labels (as well as, potentially, some with larger labels) are all to the left of

In particular, if
\alpha_{k}\leq\alpha_{j}
then

is to the left of
j
.
∎

Lemma

also allows us to show that the label on a given active site can only grow as we insert entries in other sites.

Lemma 3.3

Suppose
\sigma\in S_{k}
is a Fishburn permutation,
\pi\in S_{n}
is a Fishburn permutation we obtain from
\sigma
by inserting

If the

, and the label on the site in

\beta_{2}
, then
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider how the label on the

th site can change when we insert a new entry.
When we insert an entry into a site to the right, the label is unchanged by Lemma
3.1
(i)(ii).
When we insert an entry into the

th site the label is also unchanged, by Lemma

Proposition

Our refinement will involve inversion numbers, numbers of left-to-right maxima, and the number of entries in

to the right of

We will connect these statistics to the following statistics on binary sequences.

Definition 4.4
.

Suppose
\alpha
is a binary sequence.

1.

An inversion in

is an ordered pair

such that

,
\alpha(j)=1
, and
\alpha(k)=0
.

to denote the number of inversions in

We write

to denote the number of ones in
\alpha
.

3.

We write

to denote the number of ordered pairs
(j,k)
with

.

4.

We write

to denote the number of ordered pairs

with

and

5.

We write

to denote the rightmost entry of

As we might hope, some of our statistics on permutations correspond with statistics on binary sequences via

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose

.
For any

, the following hold.

(i)

\afterone(\pi)=\ones(g(\pi))
.

(ii)

\ltormax(\pi)=1+\lastentry(g(\pi))=\begin{cases}1&\text{if\ $\pi_{1}=n$;}\\

\inv(\pi)=\inv(g(\pi))+\zerozeros(g(\pi))+\oneones(g(\pi))

Let

be the subset of

with

(i)
Note that
\afterone(\pi)
and
\ones(g(\pi))
are both equal to

.

(ii)
We have

\ltormax(\pi)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if\ $n\in A$};\\
2&\text{if\ $n\not\in A$};\end{cases}

\lastentry(g(\pi))=\begin{cases}0&\text{if\ $\pi_{1}=n$};\\

Since

if and only if

(iii)
Note that

and

such that

We give these results in terms of

Definition 4.6
.

For any nonnegative integers

-binomial coefficient is the generating function

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m+n}{m}_{q}=\sum_{\alpha\in B_{m+n,m}}q^{\inv(\alpha)}.

Here

is the set of binary sequences of length

The reader can check that a generalization of Pascal’s identity holds:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}=q^{m}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{m}_{q}+\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{m-1}_{q}.

The

-binomial coefficients also satisfy a generalization of the binomial theorem:

\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+q^{k}x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\binom{k}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}x^{k}.

We can prove this second result by showing that both sides are equal to

\sum_{\alpha\in B_{n}}q^{\inv(\alpha)+\oneones(\alpha)}x^{\ones(\alpha)},

where
B_{n}
is the set of binary sequences of length
n
.
We obtain the product on the left by prepending entries to build
\alpha

to a binary sequence of length

contributes a factor of

and prepending a

contributes a factor of

.
We obtain the sum on the right by considering the generating function for the binary sequences of length

with exactly

The

-binomial coefficient records the inversions in these sequences while the

records

.

(1)

In particular, we have

We obtain the coefficient of

5
Enumerating

Proposition

reduces the enumeration of Fishburn permutations avoiding at least one of

,

,

, or

and any set of additional patterns to the enumeration of a closely related set of classical pattern-avoiding permutations.
Obtaining enumerations of these sets is well understood; one approach is to use work of Mansour and Vainshtein
[
32
]
.
But again the situation is different for

and
321
.
Our next goal is to enumerate
F_{n}(123,\sigma)
for all
\sigma
, but to accomplish this we first need to enumerate
A_{n}(012,\beta)
for various sequences
\beta
.
With Lemma
4.1
in mind, we start by considering a different type of containment relation on binary sequences.

Definition 5.1
.

We say a binary sequence
\alpha
contains a binary sequence
\beta
whenever
\alpha
contains a subsequence which is identical to
\beta
.
We say
\alpha
avoids
\beta
whenever
\alpha
does not contain
\beta
.
For any binary sequences
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
, we write
B_{n}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
to denote the set of binary sequences of length
n
which avoid each of
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
.

As an example of Definition
5.1
, we note that
1111
does not contain
000
.

As we show next, there is a simple formula for
B_{n}(\beta)
which depends only on the length of
\beta
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose
\beta
is a binary sequence of length
k\geq 1
.
Then the number of binary sequences of length
n\geq 0
which avoid
\beta
is

|B_{n}(\beta)|=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n}{j}.

(3)

The generating function for this sequence is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|B_{n}(\beta)|x^{n}=\frac{(1-x)^{k}-x^{k}}{(1-2x)(1-x)^{k}}.

(4)

Proof.

Taking complements if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that
\beta
begins with
0
.
We argue by induction on
n+k
.

When
n+k=1
we have
k=1
and
n=0
, so
\beta=0
and
B_{0}(\beta)
contains only the empty sequence.
Since the right side of (
3
) is equal to
1
, the result holds in this case.

Now suppose
n+k\geq 2
.
When
k=1
we have
\beta=0
, so
B_{n}(\beta)=\{\underbrace{1\cdots 1}_{n}\}
and
|B_{n}(\beta)|=1
.
The right side of (
3
) is also equal to
1
in this case, so the result holds.
When
k\geq 2
we consider two types of
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
: those
\alpha
beginning with
1
and those beginning with
0
.
We can construct each
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
which begins with
1
uniquely by choosing a binary sequence in
B_{n-1}(\beta)
and placing a
1
at its left end.
By induction, the number of
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
which begin with
1
is

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{j}.

To construct those
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
which begin with
0
, first let
\beta_{0}
be
\beta
with its leftmost zero removed.
Now we can construct each
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
which begins with
0
uniquely by choosing a binary sequence in
B_{n-1}(\beta_{0})
and placing a
0
at its left end.
By induction, the number of
\alpha\in B_{n}(\beta)
which begin with
0
is

\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{j}.

Combining our enumerations, we find

\displaystyle|B_{n}(\beta)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-1}{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{j}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\binom{n-1}{j}+\binom{n-1}{j-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n}{j},

which is what we wanted to prove.

To obtain the generating function for this sequence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n}{j}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}\binom{n}{j}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{x^{j}}{(1-x)^{j+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-x}\left(\frac{1-\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)^{k}}{1-\frac{x}{1-x}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-x)^{k}-x^{k}}{(1-2x)(1-x)^{k}}.

∎

We can now use Proposition

to enumerate

.
We note that if
\beta
contains

then

, so we need only consider the case in which
\beta
avoids

.
In addition, we are particularly interested in those

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose

and
\beta\in A_{k}(012)
contains at least one
1

Then for all

we have

|A_{n}(012,\beta)|=\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{n-1}{j}.

The generating function for this sequence is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|A_{n}(012,\beta)|x^{n}=1+\frac{x(1-x)^{k-1}-x^{k}}{(1-2x)(1-x)^{k-1}}.

Proof.

Since
\beta\in A_{k}(012)
and
\beta
contains at least one

4.1
any copy of
\beta
in

must be identical to
\beta
.
Furthermore, if
\alpha_{0}
and

are
\alpha
and

.
Now the result follows from Proposition

∎

We will find it useful to extend Proposition

to certain binary sequences

Proposition 5.4

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|A_{n}(012,\beta)|x^{n}=1+\frac{x(1-x)^{k}-x^{k+1}}{(1-2x)(1-x)^{k}}.

This is similar to the proof of Proposition

∎

Proposition

.

We start with the generating tree structure for Fishburn permutations which avoid 321 and 1423.

Lemma 7.1
.

Lemma

enables us to classify the permutations in

.

Instead of the Fibonacci numbers, our last enumerative results involve binomial coefficients and powers of two.
In particular, we show that

The above claim would follow from

10
Open Problems and Conjectures

The study of pattern-avoiding Fishburn permutations, pattern-avoiding ascent sequences, and the connections between them still includes numerous open problems, some of which we review in this section.
We focus on enumerative and bijective questions that do not involve statistics on permutations; studying statistics on Fishburn permutations offers a variety of additional avenues for future work.

In addition to Fishburn permutations, pattern-avoiding ascent sequences seem to have connections with other families of permutations.
For example, several authors, beginning with Knuth
[
25
]
, have studied permutations which are sortable using one or more stacks, arranged in various configurations.
Building on work of Atkinson, Murphy, and Ruškuc
[
4
]
and Smith
[
35
]
, Cerbai, Claesson, and Ferrari
[
9
]
have generalized this idea.
Given a permutation
\sigma
, we can construct a sorting machine using two stacks connected in series.
We require that the entries in the first stack always avoid
\sigma
, when read from top to bottom, while the entries in the second stack must always be increasing (in order to obtain the identity permutation).
If
\pi
is sortable using this series of stacks, then we say
\pi
is
\sigma
-machine sortable
.
Cerbai, Claesson, and Ferrari have generated data
[
9
, Section 6]
that support the following conjecture.

Conjecture 10.1

(Cerbai, Claesson, Ferrari)
.

For all
n\geq 0
, the set
A_{n}(201)
is in bijection with permutations in
S_{n}
which are 312-machine sortable.

By comparing the set of
312
-machine sortable permutations in
S_{n}
with
A_{n}(201)
for small
n
, one can confirm that neither
g^{-1}
nor the composition of
g^{-1}
with any of the dihedral symmetries on
S_{n}
restricts to a bijection between these two sets.

In addition to the potential connections between pattern-avoiding Fishburn permutations and certain types of binary sequences and Motzkin paths noted in Open Problems
7.13
,
7.14
,
8.16
,
8.17
, and
8.18
, several equivalences among enumerations of pattern-avoiding Fishburn permutations conjectured by Gil and Weiner
[
23
]
remain open.
These include the following, which we have verified for
n\leq 15
.

Conjecture 10.2

(Gil,Weiner)
.

For all
n\geq 0
we have

|F_{n}(2413)|=|F_{n}(2431)|=|F_{n}(3241)|.

Conjecture 10.3

(Gil,Weiner)

For all

|F_{n}(3214)|=|F_{n}(4132)|=|F_{n}(4213)|.

Gil and Weiner have also shown
[

Several of the sets of Fishburn permutations which avoid two or more of these patterns also appear to be counted by nice sequences.

For some sets
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
of forbidden patterns, all of the permutations in
F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
can be constructed in a simple way from a specific set of permutations.
In that case, finding the number of permutations of each length in this set is equivalent to enumerating
F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
, and in several cases the numbers of these permutations are of interest in their own right.
To make these ideas precise, we first need to introduce a method of combining permutations and some notation.

For any permutations
\sigma
and
\pi
, we write
\sigma\oplus\pi
to denote the permutation we obtain by adding
|\sigma|
to each entry of
\pi
and concatenating the resulting sequences.
For example, if
\sigma=42531
and
\pi=312
then
\sigma\oplus\pi=42531867
.

If a nonempty permutation
\pi
cannot be written in the form
\sigma_{1}\oplus\sigma_{2}
, where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are nonempty, then we say
\pi
is
indecomposable
.
For each
n\geq 0
and any set
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
of permutations, we write
F_{n}^{ind}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
to denote the set of indecomposable permutations in
F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
.
Pattern-avoiding indecomposable permutations have been studied by Gao, Kitaev, and Zhang
[
22
]
, who essentially proved the following result relating
|F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})|
and
|F_{n}^{ind}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})|
.

Lemma 10.15
.

(
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
)

Suppose
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
are indecomposable and
F(x)
and
F^{ind}(x)
are the generating functions

F(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|F_{n}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})|x^{n}

and

F^{ind}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|F_{n}^{ind}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})|x^{n}.

Then we have

F(x)=\frac{1}{1-F^{ind}(x)}

and

F^{ind}(x)=1-\frac{1}{F(x)}.

[
