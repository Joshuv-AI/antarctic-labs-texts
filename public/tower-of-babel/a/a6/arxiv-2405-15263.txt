Point-set games and functions with the hereditary small oscillation property
Marek Balcerzak, Tomasz Natkaniec, Piotr Szuca

Abstract
. Given a metric space X X , we consider certain families of functions f : X → ℝ f\colon X\to\mathbb{R} having the hereditary oscillation property HSOP and the hereditary continuous restriction property HCRP on large sets. When X X is Polish, among them there are families of Baire measurable functions, μ ¯ \overline{\mu} -measurable functions (for a finite nonatomic Borel measure μ \mu on X X ) and Marczewski measurable functions. We obtain their characterizations using a class of equivalent point-set games. In similar aspects, we study cliquish functions, SZ-functions and countably continuous functions.

1.
Introduction

In topology several kinds of games between two players have various important applications. They can characterize certain classes of sets or functions in a transparent way. For some survey articles, see
[
1
,
4
,
8
]
.
In our paper we present characterizations of several natural families of real-valued functions defined on a metric space
X
, via point-set games. We continue ideas initiated in
[
2
]
where point-set games were used to characterize Baire measurable functions on Polish spaces and Lebesgue measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The motivation of
[
2
]
came from the article of Kiss
[
10
]
who obtained a new game characterization of Baire 1 functions. Here our approach is more general. We distinguish classes of functions having the hereditary continuous restriction property HCRP and the hereditary small oscillation property HSOP with respect to various kinds of large sets. Two types of point-set games are useful: one of them is
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
that was considered in
[
2
]
in particular cases, and the other game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
is a slight modification of
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.
These are examples of the so-called
selection games
, which are well-represented in the literature; see e.g.
[
13
]
. In particular, a general versions of
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
and
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
are considered by Clontz in the recent paper
[
6
]
.
We show that these two games are equivalent and, in several settings, determined. However, we leave an open question whether they are determined in general.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 is devoted to the definitions of games
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
and
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
and their relationships. In particular, we show that these games are equivalent. In Section 3 we introduce properties HCSP and HSOP and we prove the basic Theorem
3.1
which describes connections between HCRP and HSOP (for
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to a family
\Sigma
) and the existence of winning strategies of Players I and II in the games
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
(so also in
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
). In Section 4 we show applications of our results to some kinds of measurable functions having the HSOP with respect to the corresponding families
\Sigma
of large sets. Other interrelations with the topic are discussed in Sections 5 and 6. This part of our paper is concerned with cliquish functions, SZ-functions and countably continuous functions.

2.
A scheme of games

Let
X
be a metric space.
Assume that
\Sigma
is a non-empty family of non-empty subsets of
X
and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is fixed.
We will define two point-set games
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
and
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
. Note that
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
was studied in
[
2
]
.

The game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
is defined as follows.
At the initial step of
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
, Player I plays
{P}\in\Sigma
,
then Player II plays a set
{P}_{0}
.
At the
n
th step,
n\geq 1
,
Player I plays a finite sequence
\langle x_{i}\colon k_{n-1}<i\leq k_{n}\rangle

(where
k_{0}=0
and
k_{n}
is a strictly increasing sequence of naturals),
and Player II plays a
{P}_{n}
:

\begin{array}[]{lllllllll}\textrm{Player I}&P&&x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{1}}&&x_{k_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{k_{2}}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{Player II}&&P_{0}&&P_{1}&&P_{2}&&\cdots\end{array}

with the rules that for each integer
n\geq 0
:

•

x_{i}\in P_{n}
for
i=k_{n}+1,\ldots,k_{n+1}
;

•

P_{n}\in\Sigma
and
P_{n}\subseteq{P}
.

Player II wins the game
G_{<\infty}(\Sigma,f)
if

\langle x_{n}\rangle
is convergent and
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=f(\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n})
. Otherwise, Player I wins.

The game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is similar to
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
with one difference: for every
n>0
we assume that
k_{n}=n
, i.e. we have

\begin{array}[]{lllllllll}\textrm{Player I}&P&&x_{1}&&x_{2}&&\cdots\\
\textrm{Player II}&&P_{0}&&P_{1}&&P_{2}&&\cdots\end{array}

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the finite sequence played by Player I in
G_{<\omega}
is non-empty.
This distinguishes this game from the game
G_{<\omega}
defined in
[
6
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

If Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
. If Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
, then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is a non-empty family of non-empty subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
. If Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
, then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proof.

Let
\$_{<\omega}
be a winning strategy of Player I in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
.
We will define a strategy
\$_{1}
for Player I in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)

and we will show that this strategy is winning.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
\$_{<\omega}(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n})=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle
, and we have defined
\$_{1}(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k})
for all
k<n
.
We put
b:=\$_{1}(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n-1})
and let

j_{n}\leq m
be such that

\bar{d}(b,a_{j_{n}})=\max\{\bar{d}(b,a_{j})\colon 1\leq j\leq m\},

where
\bar{d}(x,x^{\prime}):=d(x,x^{\prime})+|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|
.
Then we define

\$_{1}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}):=a_{j_{n}}.

To show that
\$_{1}
is a winning strategy, suppose to the contrary that there exists
a sequence
\langle P_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of sets from
\Sigma

such that, if
x_{n}:=\$_{1}(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=x\textup{\ and\ }\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=f(x).

Since
\$_{<\omega}
is a winning strategy for Player I in
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
,
we can fix
\varepsilon>0
such that, if

\langle x^{\prime}_{k_{n-1}+1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k_{n}}\rangle:=\$_{<\omega}(P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n}),

then for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
n>N
and
j\in\{k_{n-1}+1,\ldots,k_{n}\}
such that

(2)

\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{j},x)>\varepsilon.

By (
1
) we can fix
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3)

\forall_{n\geq N_{0}}\;\bar{d}(x_{n},x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Then, by (
2
) we can find
m>N_{0}
and
j\in\{k_{m-1}+1,\ldots,k_{m}\}
such that

(4)

\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{j},x)>\varepsilon.

Without loss of generality we can assume that

(5)

x_{m}=x^{\prime}_{k_{m-1}+1}

since other cases are analogous.
Then, by the formula defining
\$_{1}
we obtain

(6)

\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{j},x_{m-1})\leq\bar{d}(x_{m},x_{m-1})\textup{ for all\ }j\in\{k_{m-1}+1,\ldots,k_{m}\},

and consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

\displaystyle>\underbrace{\bar{d}(x_{m},x)}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:3})}}=\underbrace{\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{k_{m-1}+1},x)}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:5})}}\geq\underbrace{\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{j},x_{m-1})}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:6})}}-\bar{d}(x,x_{m-1})\geq

\displaystyle\geq\bar{d}(x^{\prime}_{j},x_{m-1})-\underbrace{\varepsilon/3}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:3})}}\geq\bar{d}({x^{\prime}_{j}},x)-\bar{d}(x_{m-1},x)-\varepsilon/3>\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:4})}}-\underbrace{\varepsilon/3}_{\textup{by (\ref{prop:win-I:3})}}-\varepsilon/3=\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

which is a desired contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is a non-empty family of non-empty subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
. If Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
, then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proof.

Let
\$_{1}
be a winning strategy of Player II in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
. We will define the winning strategy
\$_{<\omega}
for Player II in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
.
Let
\$_{<\omega}(P):=\$_{1}(P)
. Assume that
n>0
,
P\in\Sigma
,
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}\}\subseteq X
and

\$_{1}(P,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{m-1}},x_{k_{m-1}+1},\ldots,x_{k_{m}})
is defined for all
m<n
. Let
k_{0}:=1
and
\bar{x}_{0}:=x_{1}
. Then
for every
1\leq i\leq n
pick an integer
t
with
k_{i-1}<t\leq k_{i}
such that

\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{i-1},x_{k_{i-1}+t})=\max\{\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{i-1},x_{j})\colon k_{i-1}<j\leq k_{i}\},

and define
\bar{x}_{i}:=x_{k_{i-1}+t}
. Then let

\$_{<\omega}(P,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}):=\$_{1}(P,\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n}).

We will verify that
\$_{<\omega}
is a winning strategy for Player II in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
.
Suppose to the contrary that there exists a sequence
\langle P,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k_{n}-1},x_{k_{n}}\ldots\rangle
such that Player I wins the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
in which he plays
\langle x_{k_{n-1}+1},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}\rangle
in the
n
th move, and Player II responds with
P_{n}:=\$_{<\omega}(P,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{n}})
.
Since
\$_{1}
is a winning strategy for Player II in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
, there exists

x\in X
such that
\lim_{n}\bar{x}_{n}=x
and
\lim_{n}f(\bar{x}_{n})=f(x)
.
But Player I wins the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
with the moves
\langle x_{k_{n-1}+1},\ldots,x_{k_{n}}\rangle
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so either
the sequence
\langle x_{n}\rangle
does not converge to
x
, or
\langle f(x_{n})\rangle
does not converge to
f(x)
. Therefore, we can fix
\varepsilon>0
such that for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
n>N
such that

\bar{d}(x_{n},x)>\varepsilon.

Since
\lim_{n}\bar{x}_{n}=x
and
\lim_{n}f(\bar{x}_{n})=f(x)
, there is
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{0}
we have

\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{n},x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Fix
n,m>N_{0}
with
\bar{d}(x_{n},x)>\varepsilon
and
k_{m-1}<n\leq k_{m}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\bar{x}_{m}=x_{k_{m-1}+1}
. Then

n=k_{m-1}+j
for some
1<j\leq k_{m}-k_{m-1}
, and
we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon<

\displaystyle\bar{d}(x_{n},x)=\bar{d}(x_{k_{m-1}+j},x)\leq\bar{d}(x_{k_{m-1}+j},\bar{x}_{m-1})+\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{m-1},x)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{m},\bar{x}_{m-1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\leq\bar{d}(\bar{x}_{m},x)+\bar{d}(x,\bar{x}_{m-1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}<\varepsilon,

a contradiction.
∎

We say that two games 1 and 2 are
equivalent
whenever each of players has a winning strategy in
the game 1 if and only if he has a winning strategy in the game 2.
Thus Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
yield the following corollary.

Corollary 2.5
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is a non-empty family of non-empty subsets of a metric space
(X,d)
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
. Then the games
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
and
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
are equivalent.

We can also consider the following modifications of the games
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
and
G_{<\omega}(\sigma,f)
.
Let
\lambda
be an increasing sequence of natural numbers.
The definition of the game
G_{\lambda}(\Sigma,f)
is the same as the definition of
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
with the condition:
k_{n}=\lambda(n)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, if
\lambda(n)=mn
, the game
G_{\lambda}(\Sigma,f)
is denoted by
G_{m}(\Sigma,f)
, i.e.
at the
n
th step of the game
G_{m}(\Sigma,f)
,
where
n\geq 1
,
Player I plays a finite sequence
\langle x_{i}\colon m(n-1)<i\leq mn\rangle
and Player II plays a set
{P}_{n}
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lllllllll}\textrm{Player I}&P&&x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}&&x_{m+1},\ldots,x_{2m}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{Player II}&&P_{0}&&P_{1}&&P_{2}&&\cdots\end{array}

It is easy to observe that:

•

If Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{\lambda}(\Sigma,f)
, for any increasing sequence
\lambda
.

•

If Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
, then he has also a winning strategy in the game
G_{\lambda}(\Sigma,f)
for every
\lambda
.

Therefore, all games
G_{\lambda}(\Sigma,f)
are equivalent to the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
(so also to the game
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f)
).

In the next sections we give several examples where the games
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
(so, also
G_{<\omega}(\Sigma,f))
are determined for functions
f
from special classes of functions with the respectively chosen
\Sigma
.
The following question seems to be interesting in this context.

Problem 2.6
.

Do there exist a family
\Sigma
of non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{R}
and a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is not determined?

3.
Functions with the hereditary small oscillation property on large sets

Let
g\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
with
A\subseteq X
.
Recall the notion of the oscillation
\operatorname{osc}(g,x)
of
g
at a point
x\in A
. Namely, let

\operatorname{osc}(g,x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{diam}(g(A\cap B(x,\varepsilon))

where
B(x,\varepsilon)
denotes the open ball centered at
x
with radius
\varepsilon
.
We know that
g
is continuous at
x\in A
if and only if
\operatorname{osc}(g,x)=0
.

The collection
\Sigma
will play a role of large subsets of
X
. We say that a family
\Sigma

is
dense
if, for each
P\in\Sigma
and every ball
B(x,r)
with
x\in P
there exists
Q\in\Sigma

contained in
P\cap B(x,r)
.

Given a family of non-empty subsets of
X
, we say that a function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
has:

•

the
hereditary continuous restriction property
(
\mathrm{HCRP}
) with respect to
\Sigma
whenever
for each
P\in\Sigma
there exists a subset
Q\subseteq P
in
\Sigma
such that
f\upharpoonright Q
is continuous;

•

the
hereditary small oscillation property
(
\mathrm{HSOP}
) with respect to
\Sigma
whenever
for every
\alpha>0
and each
P\in\Sigma
there exists a subset
Q\subseteq P
in
\Sigma
such that

\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q,x)<\alpha
for all
x\in Q
.

We say that a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{X}
has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
(respectively, the
\mathrm{HSOP}
) with respect to
\Sigma
whenever every function
f
in
\mathcal{F}
has a property with the same name. We use this terminology to simplify the notation in our results. Note that the HCRP was used in
[
3
]
in an implicit way without this special notation.
It is a stronger version of the
continuous restrictions property
(CRP) introduced by Recław in
[
12
]
.

Clearly, the hereditary continuous restriction property for
f
implies its hereditary small oscillation property.
In the next section, we will discuss some examples for which the reverse implication holds.

Now, our aim is to prove the following general result.
We will consider the following statements:

(i)

f
has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\Sigma
;

(ii)

f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\Sigma
;

(iii)

Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
;

(iv)

Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
(X,d)
is a complete metric space and
\Sigma
is a dense family whose members are non-empty closed subsets of
X
.
Then (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) and
\neg
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv).
This means that the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is determined in this case.

Proof.

Fix
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
.

“(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)”.
Assume that
f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\Sigma
.
Let Player I choose
P\in\Sigma
. Let Player II pick a subset
P_{0}\in\Sigma
of
P
such that
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright P_{0},x)<1
for
x\in P_{0}
. Assume that Player I has made his
k
th move. So, points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
have been chosen. Since the family
\Sigma
is dense, there is
Q\in\Sigma
such that

Q\subseteq P_{k-1}\cap B\left(x_{k},\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right).

By the assumption on
f
, there is
P_{k}\subseteq Q
in
\Sigma
with
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|<\frac{1}{k}
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in P_{k}
. Let Player II play
P_{k}
at his
k
th move.
Then Player I will choose
x_{k+1}\in P_{k}
and so on.

Observe that
\langle x_{i}\rangle
is a Cauchy sequence. Indeed, fix positive integers
N
and
k>N
. Then

d(x_{N},x_{k})\leq d(x_{N},x_{N+1})+d(x_{N+1},x_{N+2})+d(x_{N+2},x_{N+3})+

\dots+d(x_{k-1},x_{k}).

Since for
j=N,N+1,\dots,k
we have
x_{j},x_{j+1}\in B(x_{j},\frac{1}{2^{j}})
, so

d(x_{j},x_{j+1})<\frac{2}{2^{j}}

and

d(x_{N},x_{k})\leq\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}<\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}

The remaining details are left to the reader.

Since
X
is complete, there exists
x\in X
such that
\lim_{i}x_{i}=x
. Since the sets
P_{k}
are closed,
x\in\bigcap_{k}P_{k}
, so
|f(x)-f(x_{i})|<\frac{1}{k}
for
i>k
and consequently,
\lim_{i}f(x_{i})=f(x)
.

“
\neg
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)”.
Suppose that
f
has not the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\Sigma
. So, there exist a set
P\in\Sigma
and
\alpha>0
such that, for each
Q\subseteq P
in
\Sigma
, there exists
x\in Q
with
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q,x)\geq\alpha
. Let Player I play as follows.
Firstly, he chooses
P
as above. Next Player II picks
P_{0}\in\Sigma
,
P_{0}\subseteq P
, and Player I chooses any
x_{1}\in P_{0}
. Assume that for some integer
k>0
a point
x_{k-1}
and a set
P_{k}
have been chosen.
Then Player I picks any
x\in P_{k}
and he considers two cases. If
|f(x)-f(x_{k-1})|>\frac{\alpha}{4}
then he picks
x_{k}=x
. Otherwise,
|f(x)-f(x_{k-1})|\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}
and since
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright P_{k},x)\geq\alpha
, there exists
x^{\prime}\in P_{k}
with
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
. Then
|f(x^{\prime})-f(x_{k-1})|\geq\frac{\alpha}{4}
and Player II chooses
x_{k}=x^{\prime}
.
It guarantees that
|f(x_{k-1})-f(x_{k})|\geq\frac{\alpha}{4}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence the sequence
\langle f(x_{n})\rangle
is not convergent and Player I wins the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
. Thus Player I has the winning strategy in this game.

Finally, observe that in both cases, (ii) or
\neg
(ii), one of the players
has a winninig strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
, so the game is determined.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that the assumption that any set in
\Sigma
is closed and
X
is complete is only used in the proof of the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii).

Since the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) holds for any
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
, we have the following fact.

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
(X,d)
is a complete metric space,
\Sigma
is a dense family whose members are non-empty closed subsets of
X
. If
the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) holds for a function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
, then the statements
(i),(ii),(iii),
\neg
(iv) are equivalent.

4.
Applications

Here we discuss some examples of families of functions with the hereditary continuous restriction property on large sets.
Three of them come from the article
[
3
]
where the authors considered characterizations by this property for certain functions that are measurable with respect to some known
\sigma
-algebras of sets.

Now, let
X
be a Polish space without isolated points.
By
\operatorname{Perf}
we denote the family of all nonempty perfect subsets of
X
.
Consider three families of measurable functions.

Baire measurable functions

These are functions
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which are measurable with respect to
\sigma
-algebra of sets with the Baire property. It is well known that if
X
is complete then Baire measurable functions are exactly those functions
f
whose restrictions
f\upharpoonright G
to a residual (comeager) set
G

are continuous (see e.g.
[
9
, Thm 8.38]
). It was proved in
[
3
, Thm 9]
that
f
is Baire measurable if and only if it has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
G_{\delta}
sets
G\subseteq X
having the property: there is a nonempty open set
U
such that
G
is residual in
U
. The family of these
G_{\delta}
sets will be denoted by
\operatorname{G_{Res}}
.

Functions measurable with respect to a measure

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the collection of sets which are measurable with respect to the completion
\overline{\mu}
of a finite nonatomic Borel measure
\mu
on
X
. and let
\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
stand for the family of all perfect subsets of
X
with positive measure. It was proved in
[
3
, Thm 8]
that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{M}
-measurable if and only if it has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
.

Marczewski measurable functions

These are functions
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which are measurable with respect to
\sigma
-algebra of (s)-sets
E\subseteq X
with the property: for each
P\in\operatorname{Perf}
there exists
Q\in\operatorname{Perf}
such that either
Q\subseteq P\cap E
or
Q\subseteq P\setminus E
, cf.
[
14
]
and
[
3
]
. In
[
14
]
the following nice characterization was established (see also
[
3
]
): a function
f
is

(s)
-measurable if and only if it has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\operatorname{Perf}
.

HSOP versus HCRP

We are going to prove that for the above kinds of measurable functions, the
\mathrm{HSOP}
is equivalent to the respective
\mathrm{HCRP}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a Polish space without isolated points and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
. Then:

(a)

if
f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\operatorname{Perf}
, then it is Marczewski measurable;

(b)

given a finite nonatomic Borel measure
\mu
on
X
, if
f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
, then it is measurable with respect to
\overline{\mu}
;

(c)

if
f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\operatorname{G_{Res}}
, then it is Baire measurable.

Consequently, in all these cases, the
\mathrm{HSOP}
is equivalent to the respective
\mathrm{HCRP}
for
f
.

Proof.

In fact, we will use the above-mentioned characterizations of the three kinds of measurability. So, we
will show that the
\mathrm{HSOP}
implies the respective
\mathrm{HCRP}
, as it is stated in the final assertion.

Ad (a). Let
P\in\operatorname{Perf}
. Using the assumption, pick a perfect subset
Q
of
P
such that
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q,x)<1
for each
x\in Q
. Then find two disjoint perfect subsets

Q_{\langle 0\rangle}
,
Q_{\langle 1\rangle}
of
Q
, of diameter less than
1
.
Assume that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, perfect pairwise disjoint sets
Q_{s}
,
s\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, have been chosen where each of them has diameter less than
1/n
, and
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q_{s},x)<1/n
for every
x\in Q_{s}
. For any fixed
Q_{s}
find two disjoint perfect subsets
Q_{s\frown 0}
and
Q_{s\frown 1}
of
Q_{s}
, of diameter less than
1/(n+1)
, with
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q_{s\frown i},x)<1/(n+1)
for all
x\in Q_{s\frown i}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. Finally, define

Q^{\star}:=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{s\in\{0,1\}^{n}}Q_{s}.

Note that
Q^{\star}
is a perfect subset of
P
and
f\upharpoonright Q^{\star}
is continuous since its oscillation at every point of
Q^{\star}
is zero.

Ad (b). We should be more careful in comparison with the previous case. Fix a perfect set
P
of positive measure. In the first step, using the assumption, we consider a maximal disjoint family
\mathcal{J}_{1}
of perfect sets
Q\subseteq P
of positive measure such that
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright Q,x)<1
for all
Q\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
x\in Q
. Note that
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is countable and
\mu(\bigcup{\mathcal{J}_{1}})=\mu(P)
.
Pick a finite family
{\mathcal{J}}_{1}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{1}
such that for
S_{1}:=\bigcup{\mathcal{J}_{1}^{*}}
we have

\mu(P\setminus S_{1})<\mu(P)/2^{2}
. Then
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright S_{1},x)<1
for each
x\in S_{1}
. In the next step, in a similar way, we select finite disjoint family of perfect subsets of
S_{1}
whose union, called
S_{2}
, satisfies the conditions
\mu(S_{1}\setminus S_{2})<\mu(P)/2^{3}
and
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright S_{2},x)<1/2

for each
x\in S_{2}
. In such a way we obtain a decreasing sequence of perfect sets
S_{n}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mu(S_{n}\setminus S_{n+1})<\mu(P)/2^{n+2}\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright S_{n},x)<\frac{1}{n}\;\;\mbox{ for each}\;\;x\in S_{n}.

Then note that the set
S:=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}S_{n}
is closed and
\mu(S)>\mu(P)/2
. Also,
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright S,x)=0
for each
x\in S
. Finally, we can select a perfect set
Q\subseteq S
of positive measure. Then
f\upharpoonright Q
is continuous.

Ad (c). Fix a
G_{\delta}
set
G
that is contained in a nonempty open set
U
where
M:=U\setminus G
is meager (that is, of the first category) of type
F_{\sigma}
. Using the assumption, we can find, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a maximal disjoint family
\mathcal{J}_{k}
of open subsets of
U
such that for every
V\in\mathcal{J}_{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}
there is a meager set
M_{V}\subseteq V
of type
F_{\sigma}
with
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright(V\setminus M_{V}),x)<1/k

for each
x\in V\setminus M_{V}
. Observe that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

the family
\mathcal{J}_{k}
is countable;

•

the set
\bigcup{\mathcal{J}_{k}}
is open and dense in
U
, so
N_{k}:=U\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{J}_{k}
is nowhere dense of type
F_{\sigma}
;

•

the set
W_{k}:=N_{k}\cup\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{J}_{k}}M_{V}
is meager of type
F_{\sigma}
, and

\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright(G\setminus W_{k}),x)<1/k
for each
x\in G\setminus W_{k}
.

Next, we define an increasing sequence of meager sets
T_{n}
(for
n\in\mathbb{N}
) of type
F_{\sigma}
, contained in
U
, such that

\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright(U\setminus T_{n}),x)<1/n
for each
n\in U\setminus T_{k}
. Namely, let
T_{1}:=M\cup W_{1}
, and

T_{n+1}:=T_{n}\cup\bigcup_{i\leq n}W_{i}
. Finally, observe that
T:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}T_{n}
is a meager subset of
U
,
of type
F_{\sigma}
and
H:=U\setminus T
is a set, residual in
U
, of type
G_{\delta}
, contained in
G
, with
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright H,x)=0
for each
x\in H
, as desired.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Statements (b) and (c) in the above proposition can also be deduced from
[
2
, Lemma 10]
. However, for the reader’s convenience, we have decided to include here the direct proofs of these facts.

Observe that, if
X
is a Polish space without isolated points, the families
\operatorname{G_{Res}}
,
\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
and

\operatorname{Perf}
are dense. Additionally,
\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
and
\operatorname{Perf}
consist of closed sets.
So, by Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
3.1
, we can infer the following corollaries.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
X
be a Polish space without isolated points. Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
.
Given a finite nonatomic Borel measure
\mu
on
X
, let
\Sigma:=\operatorname{Perf}^{+}
. Then the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is determined and

•

if
f
is measurable with respect to the completion of
\mu
then Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
,

•

if
f
s not measurable with respect to the completion of
\mu
then Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Note that the
the same result was established in
[
2
]
in a bit different manner.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
X
be a Polish space without isolated points. Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}

and
\Sigma:=\operatorname{Perf}
.
Then the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is determined and

•

if
f
is Marczewski measurable then Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
,

•

if
f
is not Marczewski measurable then Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

We have a small problem with Baire measurable functions since the respective family
\operatorname{G_{Res}}

consists of
G_{\delta}
sets, and the closedness of sets was used in the proof of Theorem
3.1
(cf. Remark
3.2
). We will solve this problem by the following modification.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
X
be a Polish space without isolated points. Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}

and
\Sigma:=\operatorname{G_{Res}}
. Then the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is determined and

•

if
f
is Baire measurable then Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
,

•

if
f
is not Baire measurable then Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proof.

By Remark
3.2
, only the first implication
has to be proved. Assume that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
has the Baire property. Then there is a meager
F_{\sigma}
set
M\subseteq X
such that
f\upharpoonright(X\setminus M)
is continuous. Let
M=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}F_{n}
, where all sets
F_{n}
are closed and
F_{n}\subseteq F_{n+1}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let Player I choose
P\in\operatorname{G_{Res}}
. Assume that
P
is residual in a non-empty open set
U\subseteq X
.
Let Player II pick a subset
P_{0}\in\operatorname{G_{Res}}
of
P\setminus M
such that
\operatorname{cl}(P_{0})\cap F_{1}=\emptyset
. Assume that Player I has made his
k
th move. So, points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
have been chosen. Then Player II plays a set
P_{k}\in\operatorname{G_{Res}}
such that
P_{k}\subseteq P_{k-1}\cap B\left(x_{k},\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right)
,
\operatorname{diam}(P_{k})<\frac{1}{2^{k}}
, and
\operatorname{cl}(P_{k})\cap F_{k}=\emptyset
.
Then Player I will choose
x_{k+1}\in P_{k}
and so on. As in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, one can verify that the sequence
\langle x_{i}\rangle
converges to some
x\in X
. Note that
x_{i}\in\operatorname{cl}(P_{k})
for every
i>k
, hence
x\in\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{cl}(P_{i})
and
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{cl}(P_{i})\cap M=\emptyset
, so
x\in X\setminus M
. Since all points
x_{i}
belong to
P_{0}\subseteq X\setminus M
and
f\upharpoonright(X\setminus M)
is continuous at
x
, so
\lim_{i}f(x_{i})=f(x)
.
∎

Note that in
[
2
]
the Baire measurability of a function was characterized in the language of the winning strategy of Player II in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
where
\Sigma
consists of Baire nonmeager sets.

5.
Cliquish functions

Let
X
be a topological space. Recall that a function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is:

•

cliquish
if for each non-empty open set
W
and
\varepsilon>0
there is a non-empty open set
U\subseteq W
with
\operatorname{diam}(f(U))<\varepsilon
; see
[
15
]
;

•

pointwise discontinuous
if the set
C(f)
of continuity points of
f
is dense in
X
; see
[
11
]
.

It is well-known and easy to show that each pointwise discontinuous function is also cliquish. Moreover, if
X
is a Baire space (i.e.,
X
is non-meager in itself) then those notions are equivalent.
The following fact results directly from the definition.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
X
is a regular (
T_{3}
) space. Then
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is cliquish if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
and for each non-empty open set
W\subseteq X
there is a non-empty open set
U\subseteq W
with
\operatorname{diam}(f(\operatorname{cl}(U))<\varepsilon
.

Let
\mathrm{CLO}(X)
denote the family of all closed subsets of
X
possessing non-empty interior.

Corollary 5.2
.

Assume that
X
is a complete metric space and
\Sigma=\mathrm{CLO}(X)
. Then the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
is determined and

•

if
f
is cliquish then Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
,

•

if
f
is not cliquish then Player I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
,
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is cliquish if and only if it has the HSOP with respect to
\mathrm{CLO}(X)
. Now the assertion holds by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Observe that a function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\mathrm{CLO}(X)
if and only if the set
\operatorname{int}(C(f))
is dense in
X
. Therefore, unlike previous examples, in this case the
\mathrm{HSOP}
and the
\mathrm{HCRP}
are not equivalent.

Example 5.3
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}
. List all rationals in the sequence
\{q_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
f(x)=\frac{1}{n}
for
x=q_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f(x)=0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. Then the set
C(f)=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
, hence
f
is cliquish but
\operatorname{int}(C(f))=\emptyset
, so
f
has not the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\mathrm{CLO}(\mathbb{R})
.

6.
Countably continuous functions and SZ-functions

Assume that
X
is a separable metric space.
A function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is called:

•

\kappa
-continuous
if there exists a partition
\{X_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\kappa\}
such that
f\upharpoonright X_{\alpha}
is continuous for any
\alpha<\kappa
;

•

<\kappa
-continuous
if it is
\lambda
-continuous for any
\lambda<\kappa
;

•

Sierpiński-Zygmund
(in short, SZ) if
f\upharpoonright A
is continuous for no
A\subseteq X
with
\operatorname{card}(A)=\mathfrak{c}
(the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
);

•

somewhere Sierpiński-Zygmund
if there is
X_{0}\subseteq X
such that
\operatorname{card}(X_{0})=\mathfrak{c}
and
f\upharpoonright X_{0}
is SZ on
X_{0}
.

In particular,
\omega
-continuous functions are called
countably continuous
.
Notice that the notions of
\omega
-continuity and
<\mathfrak{c}
-continuity coincide under CH.
The following fact characterizes the
<\mathfrak{c}
-continuous functions
under the assumption that
\mathfrak{c}
is a regular cardinal. This result generalizes a characterization of countable continuity formulated (for
X=\mathbb{R}
, under CH, and without a
proof) by Darji in
[
7
, Theorem 10]
.

Fact 6.1
.

[
5
, Proposition 2.3]

Assume that
\mathfrak{c}
is a regular cardinal and
X
is separable and metric. Then for any
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

f
is
<\mathfrak{c}
-continuous;

(2)

for every
U\subseteq X
with
\operatorname{card}(U)=\mathfrak{c}
there exists a set
V\subseteq U
with
\operatorname{card}(V)=\mathfrak{c}
such that
f\upharpoonright V
is continuous, i.e.
f
has the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to the family
\Sigma=[X]^{\mathfrak{c}}
;

(3)

f
is nowhere SZ, i.e. it is not somewhere SZ.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that
X
is a metric space with
|X|\geq\mathfrak{c}
. Then every function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to the family
\Sigma=[X]^{\mathfrak{c}}
. This means that if
X
is additionally separable, then properties
\mathrm{HCRP}
and
\mathrm{HSOP}
with respect to
\Sigma
are not equivalent.

Proof.

Fix
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
,
\alpha>0
, and
A\in[X]^{\geq\mathfrak{c}}
. We may assume that
|A|=\mathfrak{c}
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\frac{1}{n}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
and, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
let
J_{k,n}=[\frac{k}{n},\frac{k+1}{n})
and
A_{k,n}=f^{-1}[J_{k,n}]
. Then
A=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{k,n}
, so
|A_{k,n}|=\mathfrak{c}
for some
k
. Fix any
B\subseteq A_{k,n}
. Then
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|<\frac{1}{k}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
for any
x,x^{\prime}\in B
, hence
\operatorname{osc}(f\upharpoonright B,x)<\alpha
for each
x\in B
.

The second part of the assertion follows from the fact that if
X
is separable then there exists a SZ-function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
, cf.
[
5
]
, and any such an
f
has the
\mathrm{HSOP}
but it has not the
\mathrm{HCRP}
with respect to
\Sigma
.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{c}
is a regular cardinal,
X
is an uncountable Polish space and
\Sigma=[X]^{\mathfrak{c}}
.
Then for any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
, Player II has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}(\Sigma,f)
.

Proof.

At the beginning of the play, Player I chooses a set
P\in[X]^{\mathfrak{c}}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote a maximal family of pairwise disjoint subsets
A\subset P
such that
|A\cap B(x,\varepsilon)|=\mathfrak{c}
for every
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in A
(i.e.,
A
is
c
-dense-in-itself) and
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|<\frac{1}{n}
for
x,x^{\prime}\in A
.
Observe that
B_{n}:=P\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{A}_{n}
is of size less than
\mathfrak{c}
. Let
B:=\bigcup_{n}B_{n}
.
Since
\mathfrak{c}
is a regular cardinal,

A:=P\setminus B
has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
, so there exists
x_{0}\in A
such that
|A\cap B(x_{0},\varepsilon)|=\mathfrak{c}
for every
\varepsilon>0
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
A_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
with
x_{0}\in A_{k}
. Now, Player II chooses in his
k
th move the set
P_{k}:=A_{k}\cap B(x_{0},\frac{1}{k})
. Then, regardless of the choice of points
x_{k}\in P_{k-1}
by Player I, we have
\lim_{n}x_{n}=x_{0}
and
|f(x)-f(x_{0})|<\frac{1}{k}
, hence
\lim_{n}f(x_{n})=f(x_{0})
.
To conclude, the above is a winning strategy for Player II.
∎
