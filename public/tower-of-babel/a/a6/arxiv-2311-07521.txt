Sticky Brownian motions on star graphs
Stefano Bonaccorsi, Mirko D’Ovidio

Abstract
This paper is concerned with the construction of several stochastic processes in a star graph, that is a non-euclidean structure where some features of the classical modelling fail. We propose a model for trapping phenomena with characterization of the traps in terms of a singular measure. This measure also defines a non-local operator by means of which we introduce a non-local dynamic condition for the parabolic problem on the star graph. We study semi-Markov processes on the rays of the graph in order to obtain a probabilistic representation of the motion on the whole graph. Extensions to general graph structures can be given by applying our results on star graphs.

Introduction

The problem of a complete characterisation and construction of all possible Brownian motions on intervals was posed by Feller

[
18
,
19
,
20
]
and later solved by Itô and McKean
[
25
,
26
]
.
Their solution is based on the theory of the local time for a Brownian motion and the theory of strong Markov processes.
More recently, several papers have addressed the question of what is a Brownian motion on a graph.
In this paper, we continue in this line of research and we address the
sticky
Brownian motion.
We shall work in the special case of a star graph, that is, the union of a finite number of copies of the half-line joined at the origin.
In graph terminology, we say that the star graph has a unique vertex (the origin) and
n
edges of infinite length.
We shall formalise this construction in next subsection.

The processes constructed on this space shares a lot of similarities with their correspondent one dimensional relatives.
For an example we discuss the elliptic problems associated with the infinitesimal generators of the processes (see also
[
23
,
22
]
for related results).
In mathematical physics, the Laplace’s problem
\Delta u=0

and the Poisson’s problem
\Delta u=-g
are related to the analysis of
steady states (for the first equation) and conservative fields (the latter).
Solutions to Laplace’s problem are the
harmonic
functions on a domain with prescribed boundary conditions.
There is an important probabilistic representation for the solutions of both the Laplace’s problem and the Poisson’s problem, which we shall recover in our setting, see Theorems
13
and
16
.

Our choice of working in a star graph is not restrictive and can be justified as follows.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a generic graph with finite numbers of edges and vertices.
In order to define a Brownian motion on
\mathsf{G}
, we can proceed by defining the infinitesimal generator
A
of the process, compare Theorem
2
,
by fixing boundary conditions in every vertex of the graph.
The resulting process is a Brownian motion, possibly with several different kinds of node conditions.

Let us define for each vertex
\mathsf{v}\in\mathsf{G}
the subgraph
\mathsf{G}_{\mathsf{v}}
of all edges starting from
\mathsf{v}
. This is further contained in the star graph with center
\mathsf{v}
having the same number of rays as the edges incident to
\mathsf{v}
.

A second approach to define the process on the whole graph is obtained by considering, first,
a family of Brownian motions on the star graphs
\mathsf{G}_{\mathsf{v}}
, indexed by the set of vertices
\mathsf{v}
;
if the Brownian motion on the graph starts in a certain vertex
\mathsf{v}
, it behaves like the Brownian motion defined on the star graph
\mathsf{G}_{\mathsf{v}}
until the first time it reaches a different vertex.
From this moment, the behaviour of the Brownian motion on the graph coincides with that of the Brownian motion associated to this new vertex, again stopped at the first time of entering the set of neighbor vertices.
Compare for instance with
[
21
]
, where the authors introduce an embedded Markov chain to keep track
of the sequence of vertices in the graph visited by the Brownian motion.

Our results can be considered in many fields such as communications, social sciences, biology and others.
Let us recall the trapping problems (see for example
[
12
]
) and the well-known Bouchaud trap models (
[
3
]
).
There is a vast literature on trapping problems for example in case of regular lattice and fractal structures. They involve a number of traps located in random locations, in these traps we have absorption. The Bouchaud trap models can be considered as reference models for trapping phenomena. They have the same scaling limit as a continuous time random walk leading to the fractional-kinetic equation and also in this case, there exists a consistent literature on the long-time behaviour of this models. In the present paper, we focus on sticky behaviours (including the absorption) realized through non-local dynamic conditions (including the so-called fractional kinetic equation). The non-local effects act independently on the motion determining holding times with infinite mean values, i.e., the process spends on the average an infinite amount of time in the vertex of the star graph.

Let us briefly sketch the structure of this work. In the remaining of this section, we provide all the main definitions and notation needed in the sequel, as well as a summary of our main results.
Section
2
is devoted to present some background material, which extends to our framework known results about the construction of the Brownian motion on a star graph.
In Section
3
we define the sticky Brownian motion and we study its properties, with particular emphasis on its infinitesimal generator.
Section
4
is devoted to the construction and analysis of the sticky Brownian motion with trapping star vertex.
In this case the associated infinitesimal generator is a nonlocal operator described through a fractional dynamic boundary condition.

1.1
The star graph

Let us consider a family of copies of the positive half lines
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathsf{e}_{j}=[0,\infty),\ j=1,\dots,n\}
.
Each point in
\mathcal{E}
is denoted formally by the couple
(j,x)
, where
j
is the relevant ray considered and
x
is the distance from the origin.

According to
[
32
]
, we introduce the equivalence relation on
\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle(j,x)\sim(k,y)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}j=k\ \text{and}\ x=y\\
x=y=0,\ \text{any}\ j,k.\end{cases}

We define the
star graph
as the quotient space
\mathsf{E}=\mathcal{E}/\sim
, i.e., we identify the starting points on all edges and
in
\mathsf{E}
the origin
0\equiv(\cdot,0)
is the unique point that belongs to all the rays.

On every edge, we have an Euclidean structure given by the Euclidean distance, and a measure structure induced by the Lebesgue measure.
These structures are inherited by
the space
\mathsf{E}
: it is a metric space with the distance

\displaystyle d((j,x),(k,y))=|x-y|\mathbbm{1}_{j=k}+(x+y)\mathbbm{1}_{j\not=k}

and a measure space with respect to the direct sum measure induced by the Lebesgue
measure on every edge.

In particular, this metric-measure structure allows us to consider spaces of functions defined on the star graph
\mathsf{E}
based on topological and measure-theoretical notions:
in particular, we introduce the space
C_{0}(\mathsf{E})

of continuous functions
f\colon\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}
that vanish at infinity, equipped with the sup norm; and the Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}(\mathsf{E})
with respect to the
Lebesgue measure.

Let
f\colon\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}
.
As a shortcut we let
f_{j}(x)=f(j,x)
for
j=1,\dots,n
and
x>0
and we define

\displaystyle f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}f(j,r).

(1)

Similarly, we let

\displaystyle f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}f(j,x).

We define
C^{2}_{0}(\mathsf{E})
the space of functions in
C_{0}(\mathsf{E})
that are twice continuously differentiable on each open ray
\mathring{\mathsf{e}}_{j}=(0,\infty)

such that there exists finite the limit

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x),\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

Informally, we shall say that the second derivative
f^{\prime\prime}
can be extended to a function in
C_{0}(\mathsf{E})
.

It shall be evident from the above that any random variable (and, therefore, stochastic process)
\mathcal{X}
with values in
\mathsf{E}\setminus\{0\}
is identified by two components, the
spherical
component
\Theta
which takes values in
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and the
radial
component
X
,
X>0
.
However, if
\mathcal{X}=0
, in order to uniquely define the spherical component, we impose
\Theta=1
and
X=0
.

1.2
Feller’s Brownian motion on a star graph

We adapt the following definition from
[

31
]
to our setting.

Definition 1
.

A Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}=\{\mathcal{Z}_{t},\ t\in[0,\infty)\}
on
\mathsf{E}
is a diffusion process on
\mathsf{E}
, such that the radial component

with absorption at 0 is equivalent to a Brownian motion on the half line

with absorption at the origin.

A diffusion process is a strong Markov process with continuous trajectories on
[0,\zeta)
, where
\zeta
is its lifetime.
In
[
30
]
the following characterization of a Brownian motion is stated.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Z}
is a Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}
as defined in Definition
1
.
Then there exist constants
a,b,\{p_{k}\},c\in[0,1]
, where
k=1,\dots,d
, with

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}=1,\qquad a+b+c=1,\qquad a\not=1,

such that the domain
D(A)
of the generator
A
of
\mathcal{Z}
in
C_{0}(\mathsf{E})
consists exactly of those
f\in C^{2}_{0}(\mathsf{E})
that satisfies

\displaystyle af(0)+\frac{1}{2}cf^{\prime\prime}(0)=b\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}f_{k}^{\prime}(0).

(2)

Moreover, for
f\in D(A)
,

\displaystyle Af({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j,}z)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}f({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j,}z).

Definition 3
.

A
standard
Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}
on
\mathsf{E}
is a Feller’s Brownian motion with domain determined by
a=c=0
,
b=1
.
The values
\{p_{k}\}
represent the probability of finding the Brownian motion on each of the edges
\mathsf{e}_{k}
.

As occurs in the case of a real standard Brownian motion, giving the definition is not sufficient in order to show the existence of such a process.
The construction of this process will be provided in Section

.

1.3
Sticky Brownian motion

According to Theorem
2
, the definition of a
sticky
Brownian motion can be given in terms of the parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
in formula (
2
).
Notice that actually there exists a family of sticky Brownian motions, depending on a parameter
\mu=\frac{b}{c}\in(0,\infty)
.

Definition 4
.

We say that a stochastic process
\mathcal{X}
is a
\mu
-sticky Brownian motion on a graph
\mathsf{E}
if it is a Brownian motion in the sense of Definition
1
and it satisfies
a=0
,
b+c=1
,
b=\mu c
, such that the domain of the infinitesimal generator
A
of the process is given by (
2
).

The construction of a sticky Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}
will be given in Section
3
.
The idea, quite classical in this regards, is to define the process
\mathcal{X}(t)
via a suitable time change of the standard Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}(t)
, time change which employs the local time of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Given a ball
B_{r}(0)=\{\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E}\ :\ |x|<r\}
,
in Section
3.2

we consider the problem of finding the distribution of place and time of exit from the ball.
It is possible to connect this problem with the analysis of harmonic functions on the graph (see Theorem
13
) and the study of functions with prescribed second derivative (see Theorem
16
).

Once we provide the existence of a sticky Brownian motion,
this construction opens the way to define a further family of processes, by taking a further modification of the time change through a subordinator process as given in formula
(
12
) below.
Time changes induce transformations of the speed of the motion of the process, thus leading to more complex and interesting dynamics.
A subordinator
H=\{H_{t},\ t\geq 0\}
is a non-decreasing Lévy process of pure jump type, that is a process with stationary and independent increments, with trajectories that are càdlàg. The Laplace transform of
H_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{-sH_{t}}]=e^{-t\Phi(s)},

where
\Phi(s)
is a Bernstein function, i.e., there exist
\lambda>0
and a Lévy measure
\phi
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(s)=\lambda s+\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-e^{-\kappa s})\,\phi({\rm d}\kappa),

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)}\min\{1,\kappa\}\,\phi({\rm d}\kappa)<\infty.

We will assume that

t\mapsto H_{t}\text{ is strictly increasing, a.s.}

(3)

which requires that
\phi((0,\infty))=\infty
.
In this setting, the first passage time of the subordinator
H_{t}
(i.e., the generalized right-inverse of
H_{t}
) is a process
L_{t}

L_{t}=\inf\{s>0\ :\ H_{s}>t\}.

(4)

Since the sample paths of
H_{t}
are a.s. strictly increasing, the process
L_{t}
has a.s. continuous paths.

2
The construction of the standard Brownian motion

In their book
[
24
]
, N. Ikeda and S. Watanabe provided the construction of a Brownian motion on the real line starting from
the collection of all excursions and then constructing the sample paths of the Brownian motion.
Here, we shall adapt their construction to our goal of defining a Brownian motion on the star graph.

2.1
The space of positive excursions

Define
\mathcal{W}_{+}
the class of all continuous functions
w:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with

•

w(0)=0
, and

•

there exists
\sigma(w)>0
such that

–

w(t)>0
, for
0<t<\sigma(w)
,

–

w(t)=0
for
t\geq\sigma(w)
.

This space is called the
space of positive excursions
. It may be endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-field
\sigma(\mathcal{W}_{+})
generated by the cylindrical sets.

On the space
(\mathcal{W}_{+},\sigma(\mathcal{W}_{+}))
we define a
\sigma
-finite measure
n_{+}
that satisfies

n_{+}(\{w\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\ :\ w(t_{1})\in A_{1},\dots,w(t_{n})\in A_{n}\})\\
=\int_{A_{1}}K(t_{1},x_{1})\,{\rm d}x_{1}\int_{A_{2}}p_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1},x_{1},x_{2})\,{\rm d}x_{2}\dots\int_{A_{n}}p_{0}(t_{n}-t_{n-1},x_{n-1},x_{n})\,{\rm d}x_{n}

where

\displaystyle K(t,x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi t^{3}}}x\exp\left(-x^{2}/2t\right)

is the density (in
t
) of the first passage time of the Brownian motion from level
x
, and

\displaystyle p_{0}(t,x,y)=g_{t}(x-y)-g_{t}(x+y)

is the density (in
y
) of the Brownian motion killed in
0
.

Lemma 5
.

The measure of the set of excursions longer than
t
is finite, and it holds

\displaystyle n_{+}(\{w\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\ :\ \sigma(w)>t\})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\,t}}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to compute

\displaystyle n_{+}(\{w\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\ :\ \sigma(w)>t\})=n_{+}(\{w\in\mathcal{W}_{+}\ :\ w(t)>0\})=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(t,x)\,{\rm d}x=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\,t}}.

∎

2.2
Poisson random measures and Poisson point process

Let
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
be a measurable space.
A
Poisson random measure

\mu
on
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
is a collection of random variables
\{\mu(B),\ B\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})\}

such that

•

\mu(\emptyset)=0
a.s.;

•

for each
B\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})
,
\mu(B)
is Poisson distributed
whenever
\mu(B)<\infty
.

•

if
\{B_{k}\}
are disjoint elements in
\sigma(\mathcal{W})
, then
\mu(\cup B_{k})=\sum\mu(B_{k})
a.s.;

•

if
\{B_{k}\}
are disjoint elements in
\sigma(\mathcal{W})
, then
\{\mu(B_{k})\}
are independent random variables.

Notice that
\mu
induces a
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
on
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
by setting
\lambda(B)=\mathbb{E}[\mu(B)]
.

\lambda
is the
intensity measure
associated with
\mu
.
The converse result also holds: given an intensity measure
\lambda
,
there exists a Poisson random measure with prescribed intensity measure; see
[
24
, Theorem I.9.1]
.

Theorem 6
.

Given a
\sigma
-finite measure
n
on
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
, there exists a
Poisson random measure
\mu
on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that
n(A)=\mathbb{E}[\mu(A)]
for all
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})
.

Next, we add time to the above construction.
Consider the space
S=[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{W}
endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))\otimes\sigma(\mathcal{W})
.
A
Poisson point process

p=(p(t),\ t\in[0,\infty))
is an adapted process taking values in
\mathcal{W}
such that the random measure

\displaystyle N(t,A):=N([0,t)\times A)=\#\{0\leq s<t\ :\ p(s)\in A\},\qquad\text{for $t\geq 0$, $A\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})$,}

is a Poisson random measure on
S
.

We shall denote
D_{p}
the countable support of the point measure
p
(that we can interpret as the times of jump). Notice that
D_{p}
is itself a random set.

The Poisson point process
p
on
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
is
stationary
if
its intensity measure
\mathbb{E}[N({\rm d}t,{\rm d}x)]
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N(t,A)]=tn(A)

for some measure
n
on
(\mathcal{W},\sigma(\mathcal{W}))
.
The
compensated Poisson random measure

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(t,A)=N(t,A)-tn(A),\qquad\text{for $t\geq 0$, $A\in\sigma(\mathcal{W})$}

is a martingale.

2.3
The construction of the standard Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}

Let us begin with the
excursion space
for
\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{Z}=\{1,\dots,n\}\times\mathcal{W}_{+}

(recall the definition of
\mathcal{W}_{+}
in Section
2.1
).
In other words, the excursions of
Z
are simply the excursions of the reflecting Brownian motion paired with the choice of a ray in
\mathsf{E}
.

As we have seen before,
the
excursion point process
is a Poisson point process with intensity measure given by the product of the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
with a unique
\sigma
-finite measure
n
on the excursion space.
In our setting, the excursion measure
n

\mathcal{W}_{Z}
is given by the product measure
\mu\times n_{+}
, where
n_{+}
is the excursion measure on
\mathcal{W}_{+}
and

is a probability measure on

with point masses

.

Then, for any
U\subset\mathcal{W}_{+}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle n(\{j\}\times U)=p_{j}\,n_{+}(U).

As a consequence of Theorem
6

Theorem 7
.

There exists a stationary Poisson point process
p
on
\mathcal{W}_{Z}
with intensity measure

n({\rm d}t,\{j\}\times U)={\rm d}t\,p_{j}\,n_{+}(U)
.

The standard Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}
on
\mathsf{E}
is constructed as follows.
The radial component
Z(t)
is a standard reflected Brownian motion, defined by the stationary Poisson point process

on
\mathcal{W}_{+}
with intensity measure
n_{+}((0,t]\times U)=t\,n_{+}(U)

We introduce the increasing, right-continuous process associated with the reflected Brownian motion

\displaystyle A(s)=\int_{0}^{s^{+}}\int_{\mathcal{W}_{+}}\sigma(x)\,N_{+}({\rm d}u,{\rm d}x)

where we recall that
\sigma(x)=\inf\{t>0:x(t)=0\}
is the length of the excursion

, so that
A(s)
counts the total length of the excursions touched by the Poisson point process
p
on the time interval
[0,s]
.

Denote
\ell(t)=A^{-1}(t)
the (pseudo) inverse of
A(t)
:

\displaystyle\ell(t)=A^{-1}(t)=\inf\{s\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}\ :\ A(s)>t\}

with

\displaystyle A(\ell(t)^{-})=\max\{A(s)\ :\ A(s)\leq t\}

Recall that
s\mapsto A(s)
is strictly increasing, a.s.

We define a standard Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(t)=p(u_{0})(t)\qquad\text{if }0<t<A(u_{0})=\sigma(p(u_{0}))

and in general

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(t)=p(\ell(t))(t-A(\ell(t)^{-})).

(5)

If we denote
p(t)=(\alpha(t),p_{+}(t))\in\{1,\dots,n\}\in\mathcal{W}_{+}
; then we decompose the Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}(t)
into the spherical and radial component as follows

\displaystyle Z(t)=p_{+}(\ell(t))(t-A(\ell(t)^{-})),\qquad\Theta(t)=\alpha(t).

Corollary 8
.

The above construction, in particular, implies that

\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t)=\ell(t)
is the local time at the origin of the standard Brownian motion in
\mathsf{E}
and it coincides with the
local lime
\ell^{+}(t)
of the one dimensional reflected Brownian motion.

Recall that the reflected Brownian motion
Z_{t}
starting from
0
satisfies the following properties:

1.

the zero set
\zeta=\{t>0\ :\ Z_{t}=0\}
has Lebesgue measure
0
;

2.

the zero set
\zeta
is dense in
(0,\varepsilon)
, for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Let us define
\tau_{k}^{t}
the first time the process
\mathcal{Z}
enters the edge

\displaystyle\tau_{k}^{t}=\inf\{s>t\ :\ \Theta_{s}=k\}.

Then, given
\mathcal{Z}(0)=0

for every

(6)

It is sufficient to prove that
\tau^{0}_{k}<\varepsilon

\varepsilon>0
. But this follows because on
(0,\varepsilon)

have an infinite number of returns to 0, hence for each

occurs infinitely often in
(0,\varepsilon)
almost surely.
For the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, we obtain the claim.

For
t>0
, assume that
\Theta(t)\not=k
(otherwise, by the continuity of trajectories, we have

Let us denote
T^{t}_{0}=\inf\{s>t\ :\ Z(s)=0\}
.
Since

has the same distribution of a reflected Brownian motion starting from 0, it follows that

, and we can use the following formula obtained by the reflection principle

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{0}_{0}\in{\rm d}t\mid Z(0)=x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi t^{3}}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}\,{\rm d}t

and strong Markov property to get

or, equivalently, the first entrance time on the edge

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau^{t}_{k}\in{\rm d}s)=\left(\delta_{t}(s)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Theta(t)=t\}}+\frac{1}{\pi s\sqrt{\frac{s-t}{t}}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Theta(t)\not=k\}}\right)\,{\rm d}s.

As a consequence, we see that the restriction of the process
\mathcal{Z}(t)
to the edge

, defined as

\displaystyle Z_{k}(t)=\{0\}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Theta(t)\not=k\}}+Z(t)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Theta(t)=k\}}

is not a strong Markov process, since the waiting time to leave the origin, given

, depends on the past history of the process.

2.4
Infinitesimal generator

In this section, following

\displaystyle Af(\mathsf{z})=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{Z}_{\tau_{\varepsilon}(\mathsf{z})})-f(\mathsf{z})\mid\mathcal{Z}_{0}=\mathsf{z}]}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{z}}[\tau_{\varepsilon}(\mathsf{z})]}.

\displaystyle Af(\mathsf{z})=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}\frac{f(j,r+\varepsilon)-2f(j,r)+f(j,r-\varepsilon)]}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r).

Next we see what happens in the origin. First we state a result about the exit time from the ball that is proved in

.

Lemma 9
.

For
\mathsf{z}=0
it holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}^{0}[\tau_{\varepsilon}(0)]}=\varepsilon^{2}.

Next, let

with

\displaystyle Af(0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{0}[f(Z_{\tau_{\varepsilon}(0)})-f(0)]}{\mathbb{E}^{0}[\tau_{\varepsilon}(0)]}.

As we have seen before, this is equal to

\displaystyle Af(0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}\frac{f(k,\varepsilon)-f(0)}{\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle Af(0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}\frac{\varepsilon f^{\prime}_{k}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}f^{\prime\prime}_{k}(0)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)+\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)

3
Sticky Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}

In the first part of this section, we collect some properties of the sticky Brownian motion
\mathcal{X}
as defined in Definition
4
.
We shall denote
T_{0}
the first passage time from the origin
0\in\mathsf{E}
.
The first property stated in Definition
1
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(i,x)}[f_{1}(\mathcal{X}(t_{1}\wedge T_{0}))\dots f_{k}(\mathcal{X}(t_{k}\wedge T_{0}))]=\mathbb{E}^{x}[f_{1}(i,X(t_{1}\wedge T_{0}))\dots f_{k}(i,X(t_{k}\wedge T_{0}))]

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in C_{0}(\mathsf{E})
and
0\leq t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}<T_{0}
.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is a diffusion process, it is uniquely determined by either its generator
(A_{\mathcal{X}},D(A_{\mathcal{X}}))
,
the transition semigroup

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(\mathsf{x})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}[f(\mathcal{X}(t))],\qquad t\geq 0,\quad\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E},\quad f\in C_{0}(\mathsf{E})

or the associated resolvent

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(\mathsf{x})\,{\rm d}t,\qquad\lambda\geq 0,\quad\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E},\quad f\in C_{0}(\mathsf{E}).

Recall that the infinitesimal generator
(A_{\mathcal{X}},D(A_{\mathcal{X}}))
is, by Definition
4
,

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{X}}f(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x),\qquad\mathsf{x}=(j,x)

\displaystyle D(A_{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in C_{0}^{2}(\mathsf{E})\ :\ \frac{1}{2}cf^{\prime\prime}(0)=b\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}f^{\prime}_{k}(0)\right\}.

Now we characterize the transition semigroup

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(\mathsf{x})=

\displaystyle Q^{D}_{t}f_{i}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Q}_{t-s}f(0)\,\mathbf{P}^{x}(T_{0}\in{\rm d}s)

where

Q^{D}_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X^{D}_{t})]
is the transition semigroup for the Brownian motion killed at the origin (Dirichlet semigroup) and the first passage time law depends only on the radial component
x
of the starting point
\mathsf{x}
, hence it is known to be
[
34
, page 107]

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{x}(T_{0}\in{\rm d}s)=\frac{x}{s}g_{s}(x)
, and substituting in previous formula we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(\mathsf{x})=

\displaystyle Q^{D}_{t}f_{i}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{x}{s}g_{s}(x)\mathcal{Q}_{t-s}f(0)\,{\rm d}s,

(7)

which means that the knowledge of
\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(0)=\mathbb{E}^{0}[f(\mathcal{X}_{t})]
is sufficient to determine the whole semigroup.

Next, for any
t>0
it holds that
\mathcal{Q}_{t}f
belongs to
D(A)
, which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(\mathcal{Q}_{t}f)^{\prime\prime}(0)=b\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{t}f\right)^{\prime}(k,0).

(8)

Notice that in the left-hand side we have used the continuity of the second derivative in order to simplify the notation.

Finally, we consider the resolvent operator.
Let us compute the Laplace transform of (
7
) to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})=U_{\lambda}^{D}f_{i}(x)+\left(\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\frac{x}{t}g_{t}(x)\,{\rm d}t}_{e^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}}\right)\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(0)

(9)

where the resolvent operator of the killed Brownian motion is

\displaystyle U^{D}_{\lambda}\varphi(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}[g_{t}(x-y)-g_{t}(x+y)]\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left[e^{-|x-y|\sqrt{2\lambda}}-e^{-(x+y)\sqrt{2\lambda}}\right]\,\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y.

Taking the Laplace transform in both sides of (
8
), we aim to obtain the analog boundary condition for the resolvent operator
\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle c\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\partial_{t}\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(0)\,{\rm d}t=b\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\partial_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{t}f\right)(k,x)\,{\rm d}t\Big|_{x=0}.

(10)

Next result provides a more tractable form of previous formula.
Notice that in the left had side, we have uses the diffusion equation satisfied pointwise from the transition semigroup in order to compute a first order time derivative instead of the second order space derivative.

Lemma 10
.

The resolvent operator of the sticky Brownian motion is completely determined by the following identities:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})&=U_{\lambda}^{D}f_{i}(x)+{e^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}}\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(0),\qquad\mathsf{x}=(i,x)\\
\left(\lambda+\frac{b}{c}\sqrt{2\lambda}\right)\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(0)&=f(0)+\frac{2b}{c}\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\hat{f_{k}}(\sqrt{2\lambda}).\end{aligned}

(11)

Proof.

We can treat the left hand side of (
10
) without computing the transition semigroup (by an integration by parts):

\displaystyle c\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\partial_{t}\mathcal{Q}_{t}f(0)\,{\rm d}t=c\left(-f(0)+\lambda\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(0)\right).

The right hand side requires some more efforts.
First, recalling (
9
), we compute the space derivative of the resolvent operator of the killed Brownian motion

\displaystyle(U^{D}_{\lambda}\varphi)^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\left[-\int_{(0,x)}e^{-(x-y)\sqrt{2\lambda}}\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y+\int_{(x,\infty)}e^{-(y-x)\sqrt{2\lambda}}\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y+\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-(x+y)\sqrt{2\lambda}}\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y\right]

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}(U^{D}_{\lambda}\varphi)^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}\varphi(y)\,{\rm d}y.

It follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f)^{\prime}(i,x)=2\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}f_{i}(y)\,{\rm d}y-\sqrt{2\lambda}\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f_{i}(0)

if we substitute in (
10
) we obtain the thesis

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f\right)(k,x)\Big|_{x=0}=-\sqrt{2\lambda}\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(0)+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}f_{i}(y)\,{\rm d}y.

∎

3.1
The construction of a sticky Brownian motion

Let us define

\displaystyle V(t)=t+\mu\,\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

(12)

where
\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}
is the local time at

of the standard Brownian motion on
\mathcal{Z}
on
\mathsf{E}
.

In the following result, we prove that the process
\mathcal{X}(t)=\mathcal{Z}(V^{-1}(t))
has the same infinitesimal generator of a sticky Brownian motion on
\mathsf{E}
.
By construction, the inverse
V^{-1}(t)
is a strictly increasing function that remains bounded by
t
, i.e., it slows down the reflecting Brownian motion
Z
at the origin. Thus,

\mathcal{X}
is forced to stop for a random amount of time at the origin.

Theorem 11
.

The process
\mathcal{X}(t\wedge T_{0})
is equivalent in law to a Brownian motion absorbed at the origin for any starting point
\mathsf{x}=(i,x)\not=0
.

Proof.

By construction,
since
\mathcal{X}(t)=(\Theta(t),X(t))
is the polar representation of the process
\mathcal{X}
, with
\Theta(t)
being the selected ray at time
t
and
X(t)
the corresponding radial component, it follows that
T_{0}
is also the first passage time from 0 of the diffusion process

on the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}

In order to get the proof, we consider the resolvent operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}X(t)\mathbbm{1}_{(t<T_{0})}\,{\rm d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}Z(V^{-1}(t))\mathbbm{1}_{(t<T_{0})}\,{\rm d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda V(t)}Z(t)\mathbbm{1}_{(V(t)<T_{0})}\,{\rm d}V(t)\right]

and we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{(t<T_{0})}\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t)=0\right)=1

or equivalently, we can take

in the last integral above, which implies, in particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}X(t)\mathbbm{1}_{(t<T_{0})}\,{\rm d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}Z(t)\mathbbm{1}_{(t<T_{0})}\,{\rm d}t\right]

hence the radial component

(t<T_{0})

∎

Theorem 12

The process

Proof.

We shall provide the thesis by proving that the process

Let us compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(\mathcal{Z}(V^{-1}(t)))\,{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda(t+\mu\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t))}f(\mathcal{Z}(t))\,{\rm d}(t+\mu\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t))\right]=\mathcal{U}^{1}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})+\mathcal{U}^{2}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x}).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda(t+\mu\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t))}f(\mathcal{Z}(t))\,{\rm d}t\right]

Sticky Brownian motion with trapping star vertex

In this section, we modify the time change used in (

time change of the form

\displaystyle V_{H}(t)=t+H\circ\mu\,\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

is the local time at

of the standard Brownian motion on
\mathcal{Z}
on
\mathsf{E}
and

\mathcal{Z}
. We denote by

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{-\lambda y}\right)\phi({\rm d}y),\quad\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=e^{-t\Phi(\lambda)},\quad\lambda>0,\;t\geq 0.

The inverse process

is defined as

.
The process
H
has strictly increasing path and continuous right-inverse
L
. Moreover, the subordinator

may have jumps, so that the inverse

may have plateaux.
We also remark the known result that the stable subordinator is identified with the symbol
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha}
, for

.

The resulting process

can be associated with a sticky Brownian motion and can be regarded as a Brownian motion with trap in the origin
0\in\mathsf{E}
. In particular, we are interested in the (local) Cauchy problem with non-local condition at the origin that we may associate to this process.

Recall that a standard Brownian motion
\mathcal{Z}
on the star graph has a radial component

that is a reflected Brownian motion on the positive half-line.
We shall use consistent notation for the two objects (so we denote the local times
\ell^{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\ell^{Z}
, first passage times from the vertex/origin
\mathcal{T}_{0}
and

).

In the one dimensional case, we know that the inverse to

slows down the reflecting Brownian motion

at the origin.
Thus,

is forced to stop for a random amount of time at the origin.
Since
H
is independent from the couple
(Z,{\ell^{Z}})
, the holding time at the sticky point 0 is independent from
Z
.

Remark 18
.

If we assume that
\tau
is the holding time (at zero) for the sticky Brownian motion

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau>t|X_{\tau})=\exp(-\mu t)

Moreover, the process enjoys the Markov property and the sequence

For the process

of holding times for which (see

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau_{Y}^{i}>t|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(H_{\tau^{i}}>t|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau^{i}>L_{t}|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)

where we used the fact that

is and inverse process. As
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda
the process

becomes the elementary subordinator and

in law.
Since

is independent from

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau^{i}>L_{t}|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau^{i}>L_{t}|X_{\tau^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{E}_{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})],\quad\forall\,i.

The holding times

\displaystyle H_{\tau^{1}}=H_{\tau^{0}+\tau^{1}}-H_{\tau^{0}}\perp H_{\tau^{1}+\tau^{2}}-H_{\tau^{1}}=H_{\tau^{2}}

Theorem 20

The

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}f(\mathsf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(\mathcal{Y}_{t})\,{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{T_{0}}e^{-\lambda t}f(\mathcal{Z}\circ V^{-1}_{H}(t))\,{\rm d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}^{\mathsf{x}}\left[e^{-\lambda T_{0}}\right]\,\mathbb{E}^{0}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda t}f(\mathcal{Z}\circ V^{-1}_{H}(t))\,{\rm d}t\right]=\mathcal{U}_{\lambda}^{1}f(\mathsf{x})+\mathcal{U}^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathsf{x}).

4.2
Non-local operators in time with dynamic conditions

In the literature, several alternative definitions and formulations of fractional derivatives have been proposed,
such as the Riemann-Liouville
[
33
]
and Grünwald-Letnikov
[
13
]
derivatives;
in this paper, we consider a Caputo-Djrbashian type operator associated with the Lévy measure
\phi
of a subordinator
H
through the formula

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,x)\,\overline{\phi}(t-s)\,ds,\quad t>0,\;x\in D

(17)

where
\overline{\phi}(z)=\phi(z,\infty)
is the tail of
\phi
.
The operator
\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}
coincides with the well-known Caputo or Caputo-Djrbashian derivative as
\Phi(z)=z^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
which is the case of stable subordinators. The convolution-type operator known as Caputo-Djrbashian derivative has been introduced by the first author in the works
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
and by the second author who actively investigated this operator starting from the papers
[
16
,
17
]
. The general operator in (
17
) has been considered in
[
28
]
and after in
[
8
,
29
,
35
]
.

It is well-known that the relation between the fractional derivative operator
\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}
and the subordinator
H
(and its inverse
L
)
allows an analysis of PDEs with local boundary conditions and the probabilistic representation of their solutions. The well-known theory can be referred to as non-local initial value problems or non-local Cauchy problems. Here we deal with local problems equipped with non-local boundary conditions, we say non-local boundary value problems. Despite the vast contributes on non-local initial value problems, the literature on non-local boundary value problems seems to be very lacking.

Non-local initial value problems
on the positive half line
involving such a operator have been considered for example in
[
9
,
28
,
35
]
. Their definitions of
\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}
slightly differ as well as the characterization of their results.

A standard condition for (
17
) to be well defined is usually given by requiring that
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
belongs to the set
W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)
of essentially bounded functions with essentially bounded derivatives. This requirement well agrees with the Laplace machinery. Indeed, by considering that (
17
) is defined as a convolution-type operator, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,x)\,dt

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda u(\lambda,x)-u(0,x)\big)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\bar{\phi}(t)dt\right)

where (
[
1
]
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\bar{\phi}(t)dt=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda},\quad\lambda>0

(18)

and
u(\lambda,x)
is the Laplace transform of
u(t,x)
. If
u,u^{\prime}
are bounded, then the Laplace transforms of
u,u^{\prime}
are well-defined. Thus, we consider
u\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)\cap C(D)
for a bounded set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
. We introduce a further characterization by asking for the following condition to be satisfied:

\displaystyle\exists\,M_{D}>0\,:\,\bigg|\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,x)\bigg|\leq M_{D}\,\frac{\kappa(ds)}{ds}

(19)

where

\displaystyle\kappa(ds)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}^{0}(H_{t}\in ds)dt

is the potential measure for the subordinator
H
with symbol
\Phi
. Since
\kappa
and
\bar{\phi}
are associated Sonine kernels for which

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\phi}(t-s)\kappa(ds)=1

and

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,x)|\leq M_{D}\int_{0}^{t}\bar{\phi}(t-s)\kappa(ds),

(20)

then we obtain that
|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,x)|
is uniformly bounded on
(0,\infty)\times D
.

Moving on the star graph, for the operator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\mathsf{x})=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,\mathsf{x})\bar{\phi}(t-s)ds,\quad t>0,\;\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E}

we may consider
u\in W^{1,\infty}(0,\infty)\cap C_{b}(\mathsf{E})
. By following the previous arguments, we consider the following condition:

\displaystyle\exists\,M_{\mathsf{E}}>0\,:\,\bigg|\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,\mathsf{x})\bigg|\leq M_{\mathsf{E}}\,\frac{\kappa(ds)}{ds}.

(21)

Remark 21

For the positive solutions

and

respectively under (

on
\mathsf{E}
and
X
on
[0,\infty)
. Indeed, for the generator
A_{X}
of
X
we have
D(A_{X})\subset C^{2}_{0}([0,\infty))
.

Uniqueness follows from the Laplace techniques: there exists at most one continuous inverse, since our inverse
u
to

is continuous, then
u

∎

Theorem 23
.

For the sequence of holding times
\{\tau^{i}\}_{i}
at
0\in\mathsf{E}
of the process
\mathcal{Y}
on
\mathsf{E}
, it holds that:

i)

\tau^{i}
are i.i.d. random variables whose distribution is given below;

ii)

\mathsf{x}=0\in\mathsf{E}
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{x}}(\tau^{1}>t\,|\,\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{1}}\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{\mathsf{x}\})=\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})],\quad t>0;

iii)

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{1}]<\infty
iff
\mu=\frac{b}{c}<\infty
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}<\infty;

iv)

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{1}]<\infty
and
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
in
W^{1,1}(0,\infty)
imply that
t\mapsto\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\cdot)
is in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
;

v)

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{1}]>0
and
t\mapsto\dot{u}(t,\cdot)
is bounded (
u\in D_{L}
) imply that
t\mapsto\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\cdot)
is bounded (uniformly bounded).

Proof.

The holding time for
\mathcal{Y}
on the vertex
0\in\mathsf{E}
is given by the holding time of
Y
at zero. For the process
Y
we have introduced the sequence
\{\tau_{Y}^{i},\;i\in\mathbb{N}\}
of holding times for which (see Remark
18
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau_{Y}^{i}>t|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau^{i}>L_{t}|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0).

Since
Y
moves along the path of
X
, we have the equivalence
(Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)\equiv(X_{\tau^{i}_{X}}>0)
where, here,
\{\tau^{i}_{X}\}_{i}
is the sequence of holding times for
X
introduced in Remark (
18
). Thus, we get formula (
16
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau^{i}>L_{t}|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})],\quad\forall\,i.

(29)

On each edge
\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\mathcal{E}
, we can therefore write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau^{i}>t\,|\,\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{i}}\in\mathbf{e}_{j}\setminus\{0\})=\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau_{Y}^{i}>t|Y_{\tau_{Y}^{i}}>0)=\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})],\quad j=1,2,\ldots,n,\quad\forall\,i.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{0}(\tau^{i}>t\,|\,\mathcal{Y}_{\tau^{i}}\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{0\})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\,\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})]=\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})],\quad\forall\,i

and we get the claim.

The point
iii)
can be proved by observing that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathbb{E}^{0}[\exp(-\mu L_{t})]\,dt=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\mu+\Phi(\lambda)},\quad\lambda>0.

As
\lambda\to 0
we get
\mathbb{E}[\tau]
. Since
\Phi(0)=0
, we only need to check for the limit of
\Phi(\lambda)/\lambda
as
\lambda\to 0
.

For the point
iv)
we first notice that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\mathsf{x})\,dt=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,\mathsf{x})\,dt\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,\bar{\phi}(t)\,dt\right)

and

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\mathsf{x})|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\bigg|\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(s,\mathsf{x})\bigg|\,\bar{\phi}(s)\,ds,\quad t>0,\,\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E}.

Thus, we get, for
\mathsf{x}\in\mathsf{E}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,\mathsf{x})|dt\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,\mathsf{x})\bigg|dt\right)\left(\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\bar{\phi}(t)dt\right)=\bigg\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\cdot,\mathsf{x})\bigg\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}\left(\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right).

Since
u
solves the heat equation on
\mathsf{E}\setminus\{0\}
and
Au\in C(\mathsf{E})
we write

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}\leq\bigg\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\cdot,0)\bigg\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}\left(\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right).

Assume that
\Phi(\lambda)/\lambda
is finite as
\lambda\to 0
. We conclude that
u(\cdot,0)\in W^{1,1}(0,\infty)
implies
\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(\cdot,0)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Point
v)
basically says that we have no restriction on the symbol
\Phi
. Indeed,
\forall\,\Phi
, that is for
\mathbb{E}[\tau]>0
, Theorem
22
and formula (
20
) hold true. This is the case
u\in D_{L}
. In case
t\mapsto\dot{u}(t,\cdot)
is bounded (for example of exponential order
w>0
,
|\dot{u}|\leq Me^{wt}
) we simply get, at
\mathsf{x}=0
for instance,

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t,0)|\leq M\int_{0}^{t}e^{ws}\bar{\phi}(t-s)ds<\infty.

∎

Remark 24
.

Let us consider
\Phi(\lambda)=a\ln(1+\lambda/b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\frac{a}{b}<\infty.

Thus, the mean holding time is finite in case of Gamma subordinators with
