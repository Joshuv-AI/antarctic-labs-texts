Periodicity and pure periodicity in alternate base systems
Zuzana Masáková, Edita Pelantová

Abstract
We study the Cantor real base numeration system which is a common generalization of two positional systems, namely the Cantor system with a sequence of integer bases and the Rényi system with one real base. We focus on the so-called alternate base 𝓑 \boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}} given by a purely periodic sequence of real numbers greater than 1. We answer an open question of Charlier et al. on the set of numbers with eventually periodic 𝓑 \boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}} -expansions. We also investigate for which bases all sufficiently small rationals have a purely periodic 𝓑 \boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}} -expansion.



1
Introduction

Cantor real base systems were first studied by Caalim and Demglio in
[
5
]
and independently by Charlier and Cisternino in
[
6
]
as a generalisation of Rényi
\beta
-expansions
[
16
]
. While in the Rényi numeration system, one uses for representation of numbers a sum of powers of a single base
\beta>1
, here we
consider a sequence of real bases
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
\beta_{i}>1
. A real number
x\in[0,1)
can be represented by an infinite series

x=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x_{k}}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}},\qquad x_{k}\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that the possibility to represent real numbers in this form was already mentioned in
[
11
]
. Some of the properties of such representations are direct analogies of those proved for
\beta
-expansions, others appear to be much more difficult.

Papers
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
concentrated on characterizing the representations which are produced by the greedy algorithm, the so-called
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions. The characterization is given in terms of a set of lexicographic conditions, which are to be compared to those obtained by Parry
[
15
]
for Rényi
\beta
-expansions.
Charlier and Cisternino
[
6
]
then focused on the sequences of bases that are purely periodic with period of length
p
. They called such a base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
an alternate base, and write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
. They then characterized alternate bases providing sofic systems. Algebraic description of sofic alternate bases is given in
[
8
]
. Note that for
p=1
, one obtains the case of Rényi
\beta
-expansions where soficness was described by Bertrand-Mathis
[
4
]
.

From the arithmetical point of view, one is interested which numbers have
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions with finite, purely periodic or eventually periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions.
The so-called finiteness property (F), i.e. the fact that addition and subtraction of finite
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions yields again a finite
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions, was studied in
[
14
]
, providing some necessary and some sufficient conditions for finiteness, a counterpart of the results of Frougny and Solomyak
[
10
]
and others. A class of bases with (F) property was also given.

The purpose of this article is to study the set
{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
of numbers in the unit interval
[0,1)
with periodic expansions in alternate base numeration systems. For
p=1
, Schmidt
[
17
]
has shown that if
{\rm Per}(\beta)
contains all rational numbers of
[0,1)
, then
\beta
is a Pisot number or a Salem number.
As a partial converse, Schmidt
[
17
]
has also proved that if
\beta
is a Pisot number, then
{\rm Per}(\beta)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta)\cap[0,1)
, where
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
denotes the algebraic extension of rational numbers by
\beta
.

Charlier et al.
[
7
]
have shown the analogy of the above result of Schmidt for alternate base numeration systems.

Theorem 1

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be an alternate base and set

\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
.

(1)

If
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1)\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^{p}{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)})
,
then
\delta
is either a Pisot or a Salem number and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
.

(2)

If
\delta
is a Pisot number and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, then
{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})=\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\cap[0,1)
.

Note that for the implication (1), the authors require that rational numbers have eventually periodic expansion not only in the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
, but also in all its shifts,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)}=(\beta_{i},\beta_{i+1},\dots,\beta_{i+p})
,
i=1,\dots,p
, where the indices are counted modulo
p
. The authors state as a question, whether one can conclude the same requiring only

\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1)\subseteq{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
. In this paper we answer this question in the affirmative. In Section
3
, we prove with the help of several auxiliary statements the following theorem.

Theorem A
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be an alternate base and set

\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
. If
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1)\subseteq{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
,
then
\delta
is either a Pisot or a Salem number and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
.

The second part of our results concerns rational numbers with purely periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions. We will say that an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies pure periodicity property (Property (PP)), if there exists an interval
[0,\gamma)
,
0<\gamma\leq 1
, such that every rational in
[0,\gamma)
has purely periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion. A non-trivial problem is determination of the supremum of all constants
\gamma
exhibiting Property (PP) in base
\mathcal{B}
. Let us denote it by
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
.

Before stating our results, let us recall what is known for the case when
p=1
. For Rényi
\beta
-expansions, Schmidt has shown that quadratic Pisot units with minimal polynomial
x^{2}-mx-1
,
m\geq 1
, satisfy (PP), moreover with
\gamma(\beta)=1
. Later, Hama and Imahashi
[
12
]
derived that if
\beta
is a quadratic Pisot unit not of this type (i.e. with minimal polynomial
x^{2}-mx+1
,
m\geq 3
), then no rational number has purely periodic
\beta
-expansion, thus
\beta
does not possess (PP).

Akiyama
[
2
]
has put Property (PP) into connection with the finiteness property. In particular, he proved the following.

Theorem 2

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\beta>1
satisfies (PP), then
\beta
is a Pisot unit. On the other hand, if
\beta>1
is a Pisot unit with Property (F), then
\beta
satisfies (PP).

The question whether validity of (F) is necessary for (PP) has been decided for quadratic bases
\beta
(as a result of Schmidt
[
17
]
and Hama and Imahashi
[
12
]
) and also for cubic bases
\beta
. This is a result of Adamczewski et al.
[
1
]
who prove that a cubic base
\beta
satisfies (PP) if and only if it is a Pisot unit with (F). Moreover, they show that the constant
\gamma(\beta)
from Property (PP) is irrational for cubic numbers which are not totally real.

In this paper we study Property (PP) of alternate bases. We show a necessary condition.

Theorem B
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be an alternate base with Property (PP). Then
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot or a Salem unit and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for every
i=1,\dots,p
. Moreover, the vector
(\psi(\beta_{1}),\dots,\psi(\beta_{p}))
is not positive for any non-identical embedding
\psi:\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\to\mathbb{C}
.

The proof of Theorem
B
, is provided in Section
4
.
A partial converse of Theorem
B
, a sufficient condition for (PP), is the following.

Theorem C
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be an alternate base with Property (F) such that
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot unit. Then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)}
satisfies (PP) for every
i=1,\dots,p
.

Theorem
C
is shown in Section
5
.
In the last Section
6
we give a class of alternate bases with (PP) for which the constant
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
is equal to 1. We also illustrate the fact that
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)})
may be different from
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
.

2
Preliminaries

Cantor real base is given by a sequence
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}

of real numbers
\beta_{k}>1
. Any
x\in[0,1]
is represented in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
as a series

x=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x_{k}}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}}\qquad\text{with }x_{k}\in\mathbb{N}.

The sequence of integer digits
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\cdots
is called a
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-representation of
x
. The greatest
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-representation of
x
in lexicographic order, called the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
x
, is the one obtain by the greedy algorithm:
Set
r_{0}=x
, and for
k\geq 0
set
a_{k+1}=\lfloor\beta_{k+1}r_{k}\rfloor
,
r_{k+1}=\beta_{k+1}r_{k}-a_{k+1}
. We denote the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
x
by
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
. Note that
0\leq a_{k}<\beta_{k}
.
Setting
r_{0}=1
, one defines the greedy expansion of 1,
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(1)=t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}\cdots
.

For characterisation of integer sequences that are admissible as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions of numbers from the interval
[0,1)
, one needs to define the quasigreedy expansion of 1, denoted
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}^{*}(1)
, as the lexicographically greatest
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-representation of 1 with infinitely may non-zero digits.

Given a Cantor real base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\dots)
, denote for
i\geq 1
the shift of the base,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)}=(\beta_{i},\beta_{i+1},\beta_{i+2},\dots)
. The following statement was proved in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 3

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a Cantor real base. A sequence of integers
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\cdots
is a
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of a number
x\in[0,1)
if and only if for every
i\geq 1
we have

0^{\omega}\preceq x_{i}x_{i+1}x_{i+2}\cdots\prec d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(i)}}^{*}(1).

By
\preceq
we denote the standard lexicographic order;
w^{\omega}
stands for infinite repetition of the string
w
.

If the base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
is a purely periodic sequence with period length
p
, i.e.
\beta_{k+p}=\beta_{k}
for any
k\geq 1
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(k+p)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(k)}
for any
k\geq 1
. In this case we speak about an alternate base and write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
. The special case when
p=1
corresponds to the numeration system with a single base
\beta>1
, as was defined by Rényi and extensively studied by many authors from very diverse points of view.

In
[
14
]
the set
{\rm Fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
of numbers with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions having only finitely many non-zero digits is considered. We call such expansions finite. We denote

{\rm Fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})=\{x\in[0,1):d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)\text{ is finite}\}.

We say that the base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies the finiteness property (F), if
for any
x,y\in{\rm Fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
, we have

x+y\in[0,1)\implies x+y\in\Fin(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})\quad\text{and}\quad x-y\in[0,1)\implies x-y\in\Fin(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}).

(1)

In
[
14
]
, some necessary and some sufficient conditions for an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
with period
p
to satisfy (F) are presented. Among other, it is shown that if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
satisfies the finiteness property, then
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot or a Salem number,
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
and for any non-identical embedding
\psi
of
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
into
\mathbb{C}
, the vector
(\psi(\beta_{1}),\dots,\psi(\beta_{p}))
is not positive.

Recall that a complex number
\delta>1
is a Pisot number, if it is an algebraic integer, i.e. a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients, with all conjugates in the interior of the unit circle. The number
\delta>1
is a Salem number, if it is an algebraic integer with all conjugates in the unit circle and at least one of modulus equal to 1. The algebraic extension of rational numbers by
\delta
is denoted by
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)=\{a_{0}+a_{1}\delta+\cdots a_{n-1}\delta^{n-1}:a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\}
, where
n
is the degree of
\delta
as an algebraic number. Such a field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
has
n
embeddings into
\mathbb{C}
(including the identity), i.e. field monomorphisms
\psi:\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\to\mathbb{C}
, induced by
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\prime}
where
\delta^{\prime}
is a conjugate of
\delta
.

In this paper we are particularly interested in numbers with eventually and purely periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions. According to
[
7
]
, we define

{\rm Per}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})=\{x\in[0,1):d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)\text{ is eventually periodic}\}.

The second part of this paper is focused to rational numbers with purely periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion.

Definition 4
.

An alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p})
has the Pure Periodicity Property (PP), if there exists
\gamma>0
such that
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)
is purely periodic for every
x\in[0,\gamma)\cap\mathbb{Q}
.

In the proof of our result given in Theorem
C
, we will need to extend the definition of a
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion to numbers outside of the unit interval. In
[
9
]
, this is done with full generality for a two-way Cantor real base, here we simplify the task by considering an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
, with
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
.
For a given non-negative number
x
find
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

z=\frac{x}{\delta^{k}}\in[0,1)
. Denote
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(z)=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
. Then set

d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{pk}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}a_{pk+1}a_{pk+2}\cdots.

Having as a convention that two expansions coincide if they are the same up to leading zeros,
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)
is unique not dependent on the choice of
k
.
With this in hand, we can define the set

{\rm fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})=\pm\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\delta^{k}{\rm Fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}).

which gives the set of all real numbers whose absolute value has a finite
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion. Property (F) then translates to saying that
{\rm fin}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})
is closed under addition and subtraction.

We further define the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-integers as numbers having only zeros on the right from the fractional point. We denote

\mathbb{N}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}=\{x\geq 0:d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}0^{\omega}\}.

Proof of Theorem
A

The expansion of a real number
x\in[0,1)
in the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
is of the form

d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\cdots
with integer digits
x_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{k}<\beta_{k}
. We can rewrite

x=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x_{k}}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\delta^{-k}\ \sum_{j=1}^{p}\ x_{(k-1)p+j}\,\Bigl(\!\!\prod_{i=j+1}^{p}\!\!\beta_{i}\Bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}d_{k}\,\delta^{-k}\ .

(2)

The latter can be viewed as a representation of
x
in the base
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
with digits

d_{k}
belonging to the alphabet

\mathcal{D}:=\Bigl\{a_{1}\Bigl(\,\prod_{i=2}^{p}\beta_{i}\Bigr)+a_{2}\Bigl(\,\prod_{i=3}^{p}\beta_{i}\Bigr)+\cdots+a_{p-1}\beta_{p}+a_{p}:a_{k}\in\mathbb{N},a_{k}<\beta_{k}\Bigr\}.

(3)

In order to simplify the notation, denote

\vec{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})^{T}=\!\bigg(\!\prod\limits_{i=2}^{p}\beta_{i},\prod\limits_{i=3}^{p}\beta_{i},\ldots,\prod\limits_{i=p}^{p}\beta_{i},1\bigg)^{T}.

(4)

Then we can express the alphabet
\mathcal{D}
as

\mathcal{D}=\big\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p})\vec{v}:a_{k}\in\mathbb{N},a_{k}<\beta_{k}\big\}.

(5)

Suppose now that the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
x
is eventually periodic. The lengths of the preperiod and the period can always be assumed to be multiples of
p
, say

d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(x)=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{pr}(x_{pr+1}x_{pr+2}\cdots x_{p(r+s)})^{\omega},

which yields an eventually periodic
\delta
-representation of
x
, say

d_{1}\cdots d_{r}(d_{r+1}\cdots d_{r+s})^{\omega}
.
For the value of
x
we can therefore write

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1}}{\delta}+\cdots+\frac{d_{r}}{\delta^{r}}+\big(\frac{d_{r+1}}{\delta^{r+1}}+\cdots+\frac{d_{r+s}}{\delta^{r+s}}\big)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\delta^{is}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta^{r}}(d_{1}\delta^{r-1}+d_{2}\delta^{r-2}+\cdots+d_{r})+

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}-1)}(d_{r+1}\delta^{s-1}+d_{r+2}\delta^{s-2}+\cdots+d_{r+s})\,,

which gives

x\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}-1)=(\delta^{s}-1)\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}\delta^{r-k}+\sum_{k=1}^{s}d_{r+k}\delta^{s-k}\,.

Realizing that the digits
d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
are of the form

d_{k}=\bigl(x_{p(k-1)+1},x_{p(k-1)+2},\ldots,x_{p(k-1)+p-1},x_{pk}\bigr)\vec{v},

we can rewrite the value of the product
x\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}-1)
as

x\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}-1)=\Big((\delta^{s}-1)g_{1}(\delta)+f_{1}(\delta),\dots,(\delta^{s}-1)g_{p}(\delta)+f_{p}(\delta)\Big)\vec{v}\,,

(6)

where
g_{i}
,
f_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,p
, are polynomials with integer non-negative coefficients

g_{i}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}x_{p(k-1)+i}X^{r-k}\,,\qquad f_{i}(X)=\sum_{k=r+1}^{r+s}x_{p(k-1)+i}X^{r+s-k}\,,

of degrees
\deg g_{i}\leq r-1
,
\deg f_{i}\leq s-1
.

In the particular case where the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
x
is purely periodic, we have
r=0
, the polynomials
g_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,p
vanish and we can simplify to

\begin{gathered}x(\delta^{s}-1)=\sum_{k=1}^{s}d_{k}\delta^{s-k}=\big(f_{1}(\delta),\dots,f_{p}(\delta)\big)\vec{v}\,,\\
\quad\text{with}\quad f_{i}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{s}x_{p(k-1)+i}X^{s-k}\,.\end{gathered}

(7)

Now assume that we have
p
rational numbers
\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}
,
j=1,\dots,p
, with eventually periodic
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\frac{p_{j}}{q_{j}})=x_{1}^{(j)}x_{2}^{(j)}\cdots
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that all the expansions have the preperiod
pr
and the period
ps
of the same length, i.e.

d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}\big(\tfrac{p_{j}}{q_{j}}\big)=x_{1}^{(j)}x_{2}^{(j)}\cdots x_{pr}^{(j)}\big(x_{pr+1}^{(j)}x_{pr+2}^{(j)}\cdots x_{p(r+s)}^{(j)}\big)^{\omega}.

(8)

Then we have
p
equalities of the form (
6
) that can be rewritten together into a matrix form,
M\vec{v}=\vec{0}
, where the matrix
M
is given by

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}q_{1}h^{(1)}_{1}(\delta)&q_{1}h^{(1)}_{2}(\delta)&\cdots&q_{1}h^{(1)}_{p-1}(\delta)&q_{1}h^{(1)}_{p}(\delta)-p_{1}\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}\!-\!1)\\[5.69054pt]
q_{2}h^{(2)}_{1}(\delta)&q_{2}h^{(2)}_{2}(\delta)&\cdots&q_{2}h^{(2)}_{p-1}(\delta)&q_{2}h^{(2)}_{p}(\delta)-p_{2}\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}\!-\!1)\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
q_{p}h^{(p)}_{1}(\delta)&q_{p}h^{(p)}_{2}(\delta)&\cdots&q_{p}h^{(p)}_{p-1}(\delta)&q_{p}h^{(p)}_{p}(\delta)-p_{p}\delta^{r}(\delta^{s}\!-\!1)\\
\end{array}\right)\,,

(9)

where for simplicity we have denoted

h_{i}^{(j)}(X)=(X^{s}-1)g_{i}^{(j)}(X)+f_{i}^{(j)}(X)

and

g_{i}^{(j)}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}x_{p(k-1)+i}^{(j)}X^{r-k}\,,\qquad f_{i}^{(j)}(X)=\sum_{k=r+1}^{r+s}x_{p(k-1)+i}^{(j)}X^{r+s-k}\,.

(10)

Lemma 5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be an alternate base and
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
. Suppose there exists a non-singular
p\times p
matrix
M(X)
whose entries are integer polynomials, i.e. belong to
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. Let
M(\delta)\vec{v}=\vec{0}
, where
\vec{v}
is given by (
4
). Then the following hold.

1)

\delta
is an algebraic number.

2)

If the rank of
M(\delta)
is
p-1
, then
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for every
i=1,2,\dots,p
.

Proof.

By assumption, the determinant of
M(X)
is a non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients, say
\det M(X)=F(X)
. For Item 1), it suffices to realize that
\vec{v}
is non-zero, and thus the matrix
M(\delta)
must be singular. We have
\det M(\delta)=0=F(\delta)
, which proves that
\delta
is an algebraic number.

Let us prove Item 2). Since
M(\delta)\vec{v}=\vec{0}
, the vector
\vec{v}
is an eigenvector of
M(\delta)
corresponding to the eigenvalue 0. As rank of
M(\delta)
is
p-1
, the corresponding eigenvector is unique up to multiplication by a constant.
In particular, for any real vector
\vec{u}
satisfying
M(\delta)\vec{u}=\vec{0}
there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\vec{u}=\alpha\vec{v}
.

Since the entries of
M(\delta)
belong to
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, we can choose the eigenvector
\vec{u}
to have entries in
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
. We then have

\beta_{i}=\frac{v_{i-1}}{v_{i}}=\frac{u_{i-1}}{u_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)\,,\ \text{for }i=2,3,\dots,p,\ \text{ and }\ \beta_{1}=\frac{\delta}{\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{p}}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta).

∎

In what follows, we will set the choice of the rational numbers

.

6
A class of bases with (PP)

In this section we present a class of alternate bases in which any rational number in the interval
[0,1)
has purely periodic expansion.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\delta>1
be the positive root of the polynomial
x^{2}-(m+1)x-1
,
m\geq 1
, and let
\beta_{1}=\frac{\delta}{\delta-1}
,
\beta_{2}=\delta-1
.
Then every rational number in the interval
[0,1)
has purely periodic expansion, i.e.
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}})=1
.

It can be easily computed that
\lfloor\beta_{1}\rfloor=1
,
\lfloor\beta_{2}\rfloor=m
and

\begin{aligned} d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(1)&=11,\\
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}}(1)&=m01,\end{aligned}\qquad\text{which implies}\qquad\begin{aligned} d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}^{*}(1)&=(10)^{\omega},\\
d_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}}^{*}(1)&=m0(01)^{\omega}.\end{aligned}

(22)

Note that for
m=2
, we obtain
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\frac{5+\sqrt{13}}{6},\frac{1+\sqrt{13}}{2})
, which is a shift of the example well studied in
[
6
]
.

Proof.

The number
\delta
is a quadratic Pisot unit of the class studied in
[
17
]
for which Schmidt proved that the constant
\gamma
from the (PP) property is equal to 1.
Thus every rational number
r\in(0,1)
has a purely periodic expansion in the Rényi numeration system with base
\delta
, i.e.

d_{\delta}(r)=(d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{n})^{\omega}
where, by the Parry condition
[
15
]
, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

d_{i+1}d_{i+2}d_{i+3}\cdots\prec_{\text{l}ex}((m+1)0)^{\omega}=d_{\delta}^{*}(1).

In particular, the digits
d_{i}
belong to
\{0,1,\dots,m+1\}
, and each digit
d_{i}=(m+1)
is followed by the digit
d_{i+1}=0
.
We thus have

r=\big(\frac{d_{1}}{\delta}+\frac{d_{2}}{\delta^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{d_{n}}{\delta^{n}}\big)\frac{\delta^{n}}{\delta^{n}-1}=\big(\underbrace{{d_{1}}{\delta}^{n-1}+{d_{2}}\delta^{n-2}+\cdots+{d_{n}}}_{=:z}\big)\frac{1}{\delta^{n}-1}.

(23)

We now justify that the last digit in the period is

d_{n}=0
. Indeed,
from (
23
) we derive that

r(\delta^{n}-1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}d_{k}\delta^{n-k}+d_{n}.

Apply the non-identical automorphism
\psi
of the field
\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
, which is induced by
\psi(\delta)=\delta^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{\delta}
. We obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>r((\psi(\delta))^{n}-1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}d_{k}(\psi(\delta))^{n-k}+d_{n}>

\displaystyle>d_{n}-(m+1)\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\delta^{2i+1}}=d_{n}-\frac{m+1}{\delta}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}}=d_{n}-1.

This gives that
d_{n}<1
, whence
d_{n}=0
.

Let us now inspect the expansion of the rational number
r
in base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
. In (
2
), we have shown that a representation of a number
x
in an alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
can be viewed as a representation of
x
in a single base
\delta
with digits in the alphabet
{\mathcal{D}}
. In our case,
{\mathcal{D}}=\{a\beta_{2}+b:a,b\in\mathbb{N},a\leq 1,b\leq m\}
.
Replacing the digits
a\beta_{2}+b\in{\mathcal{D}}
by the pair

\boxed{ab}
, one converts the representation of a number
x
in base
\delta
into a representation of
x
in the alternate base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
. The Rényi expansion (
23
) of
r
is purely periodic with the period being the number
z={d_{1}}{\delta}^{n-1}+{d_{2}}\delta^{n-2}+\cdots+{d_{n}}
. We thus have a
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-representation of
r

r=\underbrace{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 0d_{1}&0d_{2}&\cdots&0d_{n-1}&00\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}_{z}\ \underbrace{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 0d_{1}&0d_{2}&\cdots&0d_{n-1}&00\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}_{z}\ \cdots

(24)

Note that this
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-representation of
r
need not be the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
r
, for: (i) it may contain blocks
\boxed{0(m\!\!+\!\!1)}
which do not represent digits of the alphabet
{\mathcal{D}}
, (ii) the order of blocks
\boxed{ab}
does not respect the condition of admissibility of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions (Theorem
3
) given by (
22
).

We will define an algorithm which rewrites the representation (
24
) into an admissible
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion, by dealing with problems (i) and (ii). We will use
simple relations which hold for
\beta_{2}
and
\delta
and can be derived straightforwardly from their definition, namely

\displaystyle m+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{\delta},

(25)

\displaystyle m+\frac{1}{\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

(26)

\displaystyle m+\frac{\beta_{2}}{\delta}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{2}+\frac{m}{\delta}.

(27)

The algorithm is designed as follows:

(i)

Since
d_{i}=(m\!+\!1)
implies
d_{i+1}=0
, each block
\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr 0(m\!+\!1)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
appears in the sequence (
24
) in the pair
\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 0(m\!+\!1)&00\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
. Rewrite

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 0(m\!+\!1)&00\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\mapsto\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 10&10\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

The two strings represent the same value, thanks to (
25
).
Use this rewriting rule for the string
z=\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 0d_{1}&0d_{2}&\cdots&0d_{n-1}&00\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
of (
24
) until we have a representation of
z
as a string of blocks
\boxed{0b}
,
b\in\{0,\dots,m\}
, and blocks
\boxed{10}\boxed{10}
. Moreover, the string of blocks representing
z
ends either in
\boxed{00}
or in
\boxed{10}\boxed{10}
. We will say that it is reduced.

(ii)

From the condition of admissibility of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansions (Theorem
3
) and the expansions of 1 in the considered base (
22
), we derive that the reduced sequence of blocks obtained in step (i) may contain non-admissible substrings only of the form

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 0m&0b\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
,
1\leq b\leq m
(say Type A) or
\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 0m&10&10\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
(Type B).
For them, we can use the following rewriting rules:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 0m&0b\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\mapsto\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr 10&00(b\!\!-\!\!1)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(28)

and

\boxed{0m}\boxed{10}\boxed{10}\mapsto\boxed{10}\boxed{0m}\boxed{00}.

(29)

One can verify using (
26
) and (
27
) that these rewriting rules preserve the value of the string.

We proceed from left to right by induction on the position of the left-most occurrence of a forbidden substring. In the reduced sequence of blocks we find the left-most occurrence of a forbidden string of Type A or Type B. Using the rewriting rules (
28
) or (
29
), respectively, we obtain again a reduced sequence of blocks whose left-most occurrence of a forbidden string is smaller by two blocks.

After finitely many steps, we obtain a reduced sequence of blocks which does not contain any forbidden strings, i.e. is admissible in base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
.

At last, realize that step (ii) produces a
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-admissible sequence of blocks representing the number
z
which ends with the block
\boxed{00}
, or the block
\boxed{10}
.
In order to obtain the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-expansion of
r
, we need to concatenate the string for
z
infinitely many times. The condition on the ending block of
z
ensures that such a concatenation remains
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
-admissible.

∎

7
Comments

Theorem
A
states that every rational has an eventually periodic expansion in base
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
only if the product
\delta=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}
is a Pisot or a Salem number and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}(\delta)
for every
i
. A partial converse of this statement holds, as shown in
[
7
]
, (cf. Theorem
1
).
It seems to be a difficult question to decide whether an analogue of Item (2) of Theorem
1
can be shown for Salem
\delta
.

Let us mention that the problem of Salem numbers appears already in case that
p=1
, i.e. in case of Rényi
\beta
-expansions. The question of periodicity in non-Pisot bases can be circumvented by considering representations in base
\beta
without requiring that they come out from the greedy algorithm. Such idea was first presented in
[
3
]
, where the authors show that any base
\beta
which is either a rational or an algebraic integer without conjugates on the unit circle admits a finite alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
of digits so that any element in the field
\mathbb{Q}(\beta)
has a periodic
(\beta,{\mathcal{A}})
-representation. Vávra in
[
18
]
then extends this result to all algebraic bases, including Salem numbers.
