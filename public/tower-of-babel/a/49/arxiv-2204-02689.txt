We look for a non-zero ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) -vector in the row space of the adjacency matrix A ⁡ ( Γ ) A(\Gamma) of a graph Γ , \Gamma, provided Γ \Gamma has at least one edge. Akbari, Cameron, and Khosrovshahi conjectured that there exists a non-zero ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) -vector in the row space of A ⁡ ( Γ ) A(\Gamma) (over the real numbers) which does not occur as a row of A ⁡ ( Γ ) . A(\Gamma). This conjecture can be easily verified for graphs having diameter is equal to 1 1 (complete graphs). In this article, we affirmatively prove this conjecture for any graph whose diameter is ≥ 4 . \geq 4. Furthermore, in the remaining two cases that is, for graphs with diameter is equal to 2 2 or 3 , 3, we report some progress in support of the conjecture.

Introduction

Graphs are among the most standard models of both natural and human-made structures. They can be used to model many types of relations and process dynamics in computer science, physical, biological, and social systems. Many problems of practical interest can be represented by graphs. In general, graph theory has a wide range of applications in diverse fields. To investigate the different properties of a graph,
researchers have introduced several matrices associated with it. The most common matrices that have been studied for graphs are defined by associating the vertices with the rows and columns as follows:

Conjecture 1.1

Let

(0,1)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a simple connected graph with a diameter of at least 4. Denote the vertex set of
\Gamma
as
V(\Gamma)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. Given that
\text{diam}(\Gamma)\geq 4
, there exists a shortest path, denoted as
\mathcal{P}=v_{1}\sim v_{2}\sim v_{3}\sim v_{4}\sim v_{5}
, which has a length of 4. Let
R_{i}
represent the
i^{\text{th}}
row of the adjacency matrix
A(\Gamma)
. We assert that the sum
R_{2}+R_{5}
forms a non-zero
(0,1)
-vector within the row space of
A(\Gamma)
(considered over the real numbers) that does not correspond to any row of
A(\Gamma)
. It is evident that

R_{2}+R_{5}=(1,0,1,1,0,\underbrace{*,\ldots,*}_{(n-5)\text{ entries}}).

(7)

It is important to note that the remaining
(n-5)
entries of
R_{2}+R_{5}
can only be either 0 or 1. If not, there would exist a vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)
such that
v_{2}\sim v\sim v_{5}
, leading to a shortest path
v_{1}\sim v_{2}\sim v\sim v_{5}
of length 3. This would contradict the established distance
d(v_{1},v_{5})=4
.

Next, we demonstrate that
R_{2}+R_{5}
cannot be represented as a row of
A(\Gamma)
. Assume, for contradiction, that there exists an index
i\in[n]
such that
R_{i}=R_{2}+R_{5}
. The first and fourth entries of the vector
R_{2}+R_{5}
are both 1, indicating that the vertex
v_{i}
is adjacent to both
v_{1}
and
v_{5}
. Consequently, this would create a path
v_{1}\sim v_{i}\sim v_{4}\sim v_{5}.
This contradicts that
d(v_{1},v_{5})=4
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma

-vector in the row space of

(over the real numbers) which does not occur as a row of
A(\Gamma).

Proof.

Let

Without loss of generality we assume that
v_{1}\sim v_{2}
and
\text{nbd}(v_{1})\cap\text{nbd}(v_{2})=\emptyset.
Suppose that
\text{nbd}(v_{1})=\{v_{11},\cdots,v_{1k}\}

\text{nbd}(v_{2})=\{v_{21},\cdots,v_{2r}\}.
Now, we arrange the vertices of
\Gamma
in the following order:

and

Now, it is easy to see that the first row and the second row of
A(\Gamma)
are

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=(\underbrace{0,1,\cdots,1}_{k+2\text{ entries}},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{r\text{ entries}},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{t\text{ entries}})

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=(\underbrace{1,0,\cdots,0}_{k+2\text{ entries}},\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{r\text{ entries}},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{t\text{ entries}}).

\text{ As a result, }R_{1}+R_{2}=(\underbrace{1,1,\cdots,1}_{k+2\text{ entries}},\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{r\text{ entries}},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{t\text{ entries}}).

Hence the theorem.
