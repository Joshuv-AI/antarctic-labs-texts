Bounds on saddle connections for flat spheres
Kai Fu, Guillaume Tahar

Abstract
. We consider a flat metric with conical singularities on the sphere. Assuming no partial sum of angle defects is equal to 2 ​ π 2\pi , we draw on the geometry of immersed disks to obtain an explicit upper bound on the number of saddle connections with at most k k self-intersections. We also deduce a bound on their lengths for a surface with a normalized area.



1.
Introduction

A
flat sphere
is a topological sphere endowed with flat metric in the complement of finitely many conical singularities. Equivalently, it can be described by a collection of Euclidean triangles glued isometrically along a pairing of edges (see
[
8
]
for details). When conical angles are rational multiples of
\pi
, flat metrics on the sphere are induced by
k
-differentials (see
[
2
]
for an extensive study of these differentials and their moduli spaces).

Definition 1.1
.

A
trajectory
in a flat sphere is a geodesic without singularities in the interior. It is
simple
if there is no self-intersections in the interior. A
saddle connection
is a finite trajectory whose endpoints are conical singularities.
Self-intersections are counted with their multiplicities (see Definition
5.5
).

In this paper, we are interested in the counting of saddle connections on flat spheres. The key ingredients of our bounds are:

(1)

the notion of the
curvature gap
, measuring the obstruction to realize a partition of the set of conical singularities in two sets of equal total curvatures, see Section
2
;

(2)

the geometric properties of
flat annuli
, analogs of flat cylinders of translation surfaces, see Section
3
;

(3)

locally isometric immersions of disks and subsequent
Delaunay triangulations
, see Section
4
.

We focus on the sphere case only in this paper because there is no a reasonable generalisation for our notion of the curvature gap in higher genus cases.

Theorem 1.2
.

In a flat sphere with
n
conical singularities and a curvature gap of
\delta>0
, the number of saddle connections with at most
k
self-intersections is at most
(3n-6)2^{s}
where

s=\frac{20n(n-1)\sqrt{k}+20n}{\delta}
.

In the case of simple saddle connections, the upper bound can be improved to
\frac{1}{(3n-7)!}(\frac{5n}{\delta}+3n-7)^{3n-6}+3n-6
.

Theorem
1.2
is proved in Section
5.2
. It follows essentially from an estimate on combinatorial lengths of simple trajectories in a flat sphere (see Proposition
5.2
) and some topological considerations about self-intersecting arcs.

According to the counting of simple saddle connections in flat annuli (see Section
3.3
), we emphasise that the curvature gap
\delta
cannot be eliminated from the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
.

Using an estimate on lengths of edges for Delaunay triangulations of flat spheres (see Lemma
4.3
), we obtain an upper bound on lengths of these trajectories (including saddle connections). In particular, it does not depend on the systole
1

1

The systole is the length of the shortest simple saddle connection.
of the surface. Delaunay triangulations have already been used to study distributions of saddle connections in flat surfaces, see
[
1
]
for example.

Theorem 1.3
.

In a flat sphere of a unit area with
n
conical singularities and a curvature gap
\delta>0
, the metric length of any trajectory with at most
k
self-intersections is at most

\frac{40n(n-1)\sqrt{k}+40n}{\delta\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{20n(n-1)\sqrt{k}+20n}{\delta^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi}}
.

The bound in the case of a simple trajectory can be slightly improved to
\frac{10n}{\delta\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{5n}{\delta^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi}}
.

Theorem
1.3
is proved in Section
5.3
.

We do not discuss the sharpness of our bounds in Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
in this paper. How to construct sharp bounds will be an interesting question.

The estimate on lengths of Delaunay edges also implies an essentially sharp upper bound on the diameter of flat spheres (see Section
4.2
).

Corollary 1.4
.

In a flat sphere
X
of a unit area with
n
conical singularities and a curvature gap
\delta>0
, the diameter of
X
as a metric space is at most
(n+1)(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}})
.

2.
Discrete curvature

For a conical singularity
M
of an angle
\theta_{M}
in a flat sphere
X
, we define its
discrete curvature

k_{M}=\frac{2\pi-\theta}{2\pi}
. In particular we have
k_{M}\in]-\infty,1[
. The classical Gauss-Bonnet formula writes
\sum\limits_{M\in X}k_{M}=2
.

2.1.
Curvature gap

The obstruction to realize a partition of the set of conical singularities in two sets of equal total curvature is measured by the
curvature gap
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a flat sphere
X
with a finite set
\Lambda
of conical singularities, we define the
curvature gap
of
X
as

\inf\limits_{I\subset\Lambda}|1-\sum\limits_{i\in I}k_{i}|
.

A combinatorial analog of the curvature gap has been introduced in Section 6 of
[
6
]
to characterize strata of quadratic differentials where the corresponding half-translation surfaces can have infinitely many saddle connections.

Indeed, any simple closed geodesic cuts out the sphere into two connected components, each containing a total discrete curvature of
1
by Gauss-Bonnet formula. It follows that a flat sphere satisfying
\delta>0
does not contain any simple closed geodesic (and therefore no flat cylinder).

2.2.
Upper bound on the curvature gap

In this Section, we prove a sharp bound on the curvature gap and characterize completely the equality case.

Lemma 2.2
.

The curvature gap
\delta
of any flat sphere satisfies
\delta\leq\frac{1}{3}
. The equality holds if and only if all of conical angles belong to
2\pi+\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathbb{Z}
.

Equivalently, the equality case for the curvature gap corresponds to flat metrics of finite area induced by meromorphic cubic differentials with singularities of even order.

Proof.

We assume by contradiction that there is a sequence of numbers
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in]-\infty,1[
such that:

•

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}=2
;

•

no partial sum belongs to the interval
]\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}[
;

Without loss of generality, we assume this sequence has a minimal number
n
of elements among sequences with this property (we obviously have
n\geq 3
).

It follows from the hypothesis on partial sums that
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in]-\infty,\frac{2}{3}]
. Now there are two cases depending on the value of
k_{1}+k_{2}
. If
k_{1}+k_{2}<1
, we replace
k_{1},k_{2}
by
k_{1}+k_{2}
in the sequence and get a sequence with a smaller
n
satisfying desired properties. On the opposite, if
k_{1}+k_{2}\geq 1
, we have
k_{1}+k_{2}\in[1,\frac{4}{3}]
and we contradict the hypothesis on partial sums.

If a flat surface has a curvature gap
\delta=\frac{1}{3}
, no partial sum of its discrete curvatures
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
can belong to the open interval
]\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}[
. Positive discrete curvatures are thus contained in the interval
]0,\frac{2}{3}]
. Since their total sum is at least
2
, there is a partial sum at least
\frac{4}{3}
. It forces that two partial sum of positive curvatures are
\frac{2}{3}
and
\frac{4}{3}
respectively. It follows that there is a curvature equal to
\frac{2}{3}
. If there is a positive curvature less than
\frac{2}{3}
, the sum of this curvature with
\frac{2}{3}
is in the open interval
]\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}[
, which is impossible. Thus, every positive number among
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
is equal to
\frac{2}{3}
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
k_{1}=\dots=k_{s}=\frac{2}{3}
and
k_{t}\leq 0
for any
t>s
. Then, for any such
k_{t}
,
k_{t}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}\geq 2
, unless
k_{t}\in\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{Z}
, at least one of the partial sums
k_{t}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}k_{i}
for
l\leq s
belongs to the open interval
]\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}[
. This would contradict
\delta=\frac{1}{3}
. It follow that every discrete curvature
k_{i}
is an integer multiple of
\frac{2}{3}
.

The corresponding flat metrics have conical angles in
2\pi+\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathbb{Z}
. For any developing map
f
of the metric, cubic differential
(df)^{\otimes 3}
is unaffected by the monodromy around conical singularities (formed by translations and rotations of order
3
). Conversely, zeroes and poles of order
k\geq-2
of a cubic differential
\omega
correspond to conical singularities of angle
\frac{(k+3)2\pi}{3}
in the metric induced by differential
|\omega^{1/3}|
(see
[
2
]
for the background on differentials of higher orders).
∎

3.
Flat annuli

Just like the geometry of a translation surface is largely determined by shapes of its flat cylinders,
flat annuli
play an analogous role for flat surfaces.

Definition 3.1
.

For any angle
\theta\in]0,2\pi]
and radii
0\leq R<R^{\prime}
, we define a
flat annulus

\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
by identifying the two radial sides of domain
\{{z\in\mathbb{C}\penalty\ |\penalty\ R\leq|z|\leq R^{\prime}\penalty\ ;\penalty\ 0\leq arg(z)\leq\theta\}}
by a rotation of the angle
\theta
.

We refer to
\theta
as the
apex angle
of the annulus. The definition extends for any
\theta>0
by a covering construction.

The exponential map provides a holomorphic parametrization of the flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
by the flat cylinder obtained from a rectangle
\{{z\in\mathbb{C}\penalty\ |\penalty\ 0\leq Re(z)\leq ln(R^{\prime}/R)\penalty\ ;\penalty\ 0\leq Im(z)\leq\theta\}}
. The
conformal modulus
of the flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
is thus
\frac{ln(R^{\prime}/R)}{\theta}
.

Identifying the center of the flat annulus with
0\in\mathbb{C}
, the polar coordinate of the complex plane provides a pair of orthogonal foliations on
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
:

•

the
radial foliation
, whose leaves are trajectories contained in lines incident to the center of the annulus;

•

the
orthoradial foliation
, whose leaves are circular arcs orthogonal to radial leaves.

In particular, the curvature radius of orthoradial leaves varies between
R
and
R^{\prime}
. We will refer to boundaries of orthoradial leaves of radii
R
and
R^{\prime}
respectively as the
inner boundary arc
and the
outer boundary arc
. If
R=0
, the inner boundary arc is reduced to a conical singularity of the angle
\theta
.

3.1.
Trajectories in a flat annulus

In any annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
, there is an important difference between trajectories starting from the inner boundary arc and trajectories starting from the outer boundary arc. We obtain this description by looking at the lift of the trajectory in the universal cover of the flat annulus.

In any trajectory
t
starting from the inner boundary arc, the radius (determining a leaf in the orthogonal foliation) along the trajectory is strictly increasing from
R
to
R^{\prime}
. It follows that it is simple and leaves the annulus through the outer boundary arc.

For a trajectory
t
starting from the outer boundary arc, we denote by
A
the starting point of
t
and
\alpha
the angle of
t
with the radial leaf incident to
A
. There are two different regimes (see Figure
1
):

Figure 1.
Two trajectories in the universal cover of a flat annulus.

•

if
\alpha\leq\arcsin(R^{\prime}/R)
, the radius is strictly decreasing from
R^{\prime}
to
R
and trajectory
t
eventually leaves the annulus through the inner boundary arc. The trajectory is automatically simple.

•

if
\alpha>\arcsin(R^{\prime}/R)
, the radius is strictly decreasing from
R^{\prime}
to
R^{\prime}\sin(\alpha)
and then strictly increasing from
R^{\prime}\sin(\alpha)
to
R^{\prime}
. The (possibly self-intersecting) trajectory eventually leaves the annulus through the outer boundary.

In the second case, the trajectory is a chord of the outer circle whose central angle is
\pi-2\alpha
. We observe that all the self-intersections of
t
belong to two radial leaves (see Figure
2
):

(1)

the radial leaf
\mathcal{L}
containing the unique point where
t
is tangent to the orthoradial foliation and realizes the minimal radius
R^{\prime}\sin(\alpha)
;

(2)

the radial leaf obtained from
\mathcal{L}
by a rotation of angle
\frac{\theta}{2}
.

A direct counting then proves that the number of self-intersections in trajectory
t
is
\lfloor\frac{\pi-2\alpha}{\theta}\rfloor
.

Figure 2.
The thin and thick red radial leaves are the copies of the leaves where self-intersections of
t
belong to.

3.2.
Monogonal trajectories

In a flat sphere, a
monogonal trajectory
is a simple trajectory with the same endpoint and avoids any conical singularity. We call the endpoint the
turning point
. Notice that the trajectory divides the angle at the turning point into two sub-angles. We call the smaller sub-angle the
interior angle
.

The curvature gap provides constraints on interior angles of monogonal trajectories.

Lemma 3.2
.

In a flat sphere
X
with a curvature gap
\delta
, for any monogonal trajectory
\gamma
, the interior angle
\alpha
of loop
\gamma
satisfies
\alpha\leq\pi-2\pi\delta
.

Proof.

Any closed topological loop
\gamma
decomposes
X
into connected components and the set of conical singularities into two subsets. If
\gamma
is a polygonal path with an interior angle, Gauss-Bonnet formula implies that
\pi-\alpha
equals to
2\pi
times the total curvature encompassed by the loop. By the definition of the curvature gap, our inequality follows.
∎

A monogonal trajectory
\gamma
is characterized by a pair
(\alpha,L)
where
\alpha\in]0,\pi[
is the interior angle while
L
is the length of the segment.

The holonomy of the flat metric along the loop is a rotation of angle
\pi-\alpha
. Each point of
\gamma
has a well-defined radius with respect with the fictive center of this rotation. A direct trigonometric computation proves that the maximal radius
\frac{L}{2\cos(\alpha/2)}
is realized at the turning point while the minimal radius
\frac{L\tan(\alpha/2)}{2}
is realized at the midpoint of the unique segment of
\gamma
.

Each monogonal trajectory
\gamma
can be embedded in a unique way in a
1
-parameter family of disjoint monogonal trajectory
(\gamma_{l})_{L<l<L^{\prime}}
such that:

•

the interior angle of each trajectory of the family are the same;

•

turning points of monogonal trajectories describe a straight line
\mathcal{L}
in the flat surface as
l
changes;

•

\mathcal{L}
is the bisector of the interior angle for each trajectory
\gamma_{l}
.

Provided
\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}>\frac{1}{\sin(\alpha/2)}
, notice that the flat surface which is swept by a family
(\gamma_{l})_{L<l<L^{\prime}}

contains an annulus
\mathcal{A}_{\theta,R,R^{\prime}}
(see Figure
3
) where:

•

\theta=\pi-\alpha
;

•

R=\frac{L}{2\cos(\alpha/2)}
;

•

R^{\prime}=\frac{L^{\prime}\tan(\alpha/2)}{2}
.

Figure 3.
A flat annulus is contained in a
family of monogonal trajectories.

3.3.
Counting saddle connections in a flat annulus

This section is dedicated to explaining why the curvature gap is necessary for our estimates in Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

For
0\leq R<R^{\prime}
and
0<\theta<\pi
, we consider a flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
embedded in a flat sphere such that each boundary arc contains exactly one singularity. We will also assume that these singularities
A,B
belong to the same radial leaf.

Proposition 3.3
.

Flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
contains at least
\arccos{(\frac{R}{R^{\prime}})}\cdot\frac{1}{\theta}
simple saddle connections.

For a flat sphere
X
containing a flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
, Lemma
3.2
proves that apex angle
\theta
satisfies a lower bound
\theta\geq 2\pi\delta
where
\delta
is the curvature gap
\delta
of
X
.
When the apex angle
\theta
goes to zero, the curvature gap of the surface goes to zero and the number and the length of simple saddle connections tend to infinity. This indicates why the curvature gap
\delta
is necessary in the upper bound of Theorem
1.2
.

Taking the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
while bounding the conformal modulus of the flat annulus, we obtain in the limit a flat cylinder containing infinitely many saddle connections with arbitrarily large lengths. In a flat sphere, this can only happen if
\delta=0
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

We develop counterclockwise the flat annulus and denote by
A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
the copies of
A
as in Figure
4
.

We define
I
as the tangency point for a line
IB
incident to
B
and tangent to the circle of radius
r
. Defining
\beta
as
\angle IOB
,
I
belongs to the circular arc between two successive copies
A_{m}
and
A_{m+1}
of
A
, see Figure
4
.

Since
\cos(\beta)=\frac{R}{R^{\prime}}
, we have
m\theta\leq\arccos(\frac{R}{R^{\prime}})\leq(m+1)\theta
. It follows that segments
[BA_{0}],\dots,[BA_{m}]
form a family of
\arccos(\frac{R}{R^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{1}{\theta}
simple saddle connections.

An unfolding of the almost cylinder and the sequence of saddle connections
[BA_{0}],\dots,[BA_{m}]
.

4.
Geometry of immersed disks

4.1.
Locally isometric immersions of disks

In the study of a flat surface, a crucial geometric quantity is the size of the largest open disk we can immerse or embed in the surface without hitting the conical singularities.

Proposition 4.1
.

If there is a locally isometric immersion
f
of an open disk of radius
\lambda>\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
in a flat sphere
X
of a unit area, then
X
contains an embedded flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
where:

•

the apex angle
\theta
satisfies
\theta<\frac{\pi}{4\pi\lambda^{2}-4}
;

•

the conformal modulus
\mu
satisfies
\mu>\sqrt{\pi\lambda^{2}-1}
.

Proof.

We identify the domain of immersion
f
with the centered disk
\mathcal{D}
of radius
\lambda
in the complex plane and set
M=f(0)
. Since
f
is locally injective,
M
is a center of a maximal embedded disk
\mathcal{D}^{o}
in
X
. The area of
\mathcal{D}^{o}
is bounded by the area of
X
so the radius
\rho
of
\mathcal{D}^{o}
satisfies
\rho<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
. In particular, we have
\lambda>\rho
and
\mathcal{D}^{o}
is contained in
\mathcal{D}
.

We deduce from the maximality hypothesis on
\mathcal{D}^{o}
that two points
A,B
of
\partial\mathcal{D}^{o}
have the same image under the immersion. Without loss of generality, we suppose that the mediatrix of the segment
[A,B]
contains the horizontal diameter of disk
\mathcal{D}
. We locate points
A,B
with corresponding complex numbers
\rho e^{i\alpha}
and
\rho e^{i\alpha}
respectively for
\alpha\in]0,\frac{\pi}{2}[
see Figure
5
.

Figure 5.
Delaunay disk
\mathcal{D}
, embedded disk
\mathcal{D}^{o}
, monogonal trajectory
[AB]
and the flat annulus in grey.

A neighborhood of
B
in the tangent line
T_{B}
of
\partial\mathcal{D}^{o}
of
B
has the image under
f
as some segment containing
A
. Since
\mathcal{D}^{o}
is the maximal embedded disk centered on
M
, this segment has also to be contained in the tangent line
T_{A}
to
\partial\mathcal{D}^{o}
. It follows that segment
[AB]
belongs to a family of vertical monogonal trajectories (see Section
3.2
) with turning points located at these identified tangent lines. This family of curves can be continued on
X
until reaching a conical singularity. Since
f(\mathcal{D})
cannot contain any conical singularity, it follows that the intersection point
N
of these two tangent lines is outside
\mathcal{D}
(possibly at infinity), see Figure
5
. Identify point
N
with the real number
x
of the complex plane, we have
x\geq\lambda
.

Each of the two identified points between tangent lines
T_{A},T_{B}
is connected by a circular arc of the center
N
contained in disk
\mathcal{D}
. We denote by
R<R^{\prime}
the radii of these arcs. The images under
f
of these two circular arcs bound a flat annulus
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
where apex angle satisfies
\theta=\pi-2\alpha
.

The area
A=\theta(R^{\prime}+R)(R^{\prime}-R)
of this annulus is bounded by the total area of the surface. Since we have
R^{\prime}-R=2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}
and
\rho^{2}>\frac{1}{\pi}
, we deduce the inequality
A>4\theta(\lambda^{2}-\frac{1}{\pi})
. It follows from
A<1
that
\theta<\frac{\pi}{4\pi\lambda^{2}-4}
.

It remains to give a lower bound on the conformal modulus of
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
. In fact, the modulus is
\frac{ln(R^{\prime}/R)}{\theta}
. We know
\theta=\pi-2\alpha
. Since
R^{\prime}-R=2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}
and
R^{\prime}+R=2\rho\tan{\alpha}
, we have
R^{\prime}=\rho\tan{\alpha}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}
and
R=\rho\tan{\alpha}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}
. Hence, the conformal modulus
\mu
of
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
is given by:

\mu=\frac{\ln\left({\frac{\rho\tan{\alpha}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{\rho\tan{\alpha}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}}\right)}{\pi-2\alpha}.

According to the inequality
\ln(1+x)\geq\frac{x}{1+x}
for
x>-1
, we know that:

\frac{\ln\left({\frac{\rho\tan{\alpha}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{\rho\tan{\alpha}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}}\right)}{\pi-2\alpha}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{\rho\tan{\alpha}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi-2\alpha}.

As
\alpha
increases, the right side of the inequality decreases. In the limit
\alpha\longrightarrow\frac{\pi}{2}
, the flat annulus becomes a flat cylinder and we obtain:

\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{\rho\tan{\alpha}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi-2\alpha}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\rho^{2}}}{\rho}.

It follows then from inequality
\rho<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
that the conformal modulus
\mu
of
\mathcal{A}_{R,R^{\prime},\theta}
is strictly larger than
\sqrt{\pi\lambda^{2}-1}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

An interesting geometric result due to Masur and Smillie (Corollary 5.5 in
[

]
) proves that a translation surface of large diameter contains a long flat cylinder. Proposition
4.1

4.2.
Delaunay triangulations

In any flat sphere
X
(and more generally any flat surface with conical singularities), the
Voronoi cell
of a singularity
M
is the polygonal domain in
X
consisting of points that are closer (with respect to the flat metric) to
M
than to any other conical singularity of
X
. This defines the
Voronoi tessellation
of
X
.

The
Delaunay polygonation
is a construction dual to the Voronoi tessellation. We consider locally isometric immersions of disks into
X
. When at least three points of the boundary circle are mapped to conical singularities of
X
, these disks are called
Delaunay disks
and the immersion of the convex hull of these singularities into
X
is called a
Delaunay polygon
. They form a decomposition of
X
dual to the Voronoi tessellation, see Proposition 3.1 of
[
7
]
for details on these constructions.

If more than three conical singularities of
X
are cocyclic, some Delaunay polygons may be convex polygons with more than three sides. A
Delaunay triangulation
is any subdivision of the Delaunay polygonation into triangles. We refer to edges of such a triangulation as
Delaunay edges
.

The following lemma uses the curvature gap to bound the length of Delaunay edges on any flat sphere.

Lemma 4.3
.

In a flat sphere
X
of a unit area with
n
conical singularities and a curvature gap
\delta>0
, the length
L
of any edge of a Delaunay triangulation of
X
satisfies
L^{2}<\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2\pi\delta}
.

Proof.

We assume by contradiction that the length
\lambda
of some Delaunay edge satisfies
L^{2}\geq\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2\pi\delta}
. It follows that it is contained in the image of a Delaunay disk
\mathcal{D}
of radius
\lambda\geq\frac{L}{2}
by a locally isometric immersion
f
.

Radius
\lambda
is by hypothesis strictly larger than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
. It follows then from Proposition
4.1
that the image of
\mathcal{D}
contains a flat annulus of apex angle
\theta<\frac{\pi}{4\pi\lambda^{2}-4}
. Orthoradial leaves of this flat annulus (see Section
3
) decompose
X
into two connected components whose total curvatures are respectively
\frac{2\pi-\theta}{2\pi}
and
\frac{2\pi+\theta}{2\pi}
. It follows then from the definition of the curvature gap that we have

∎

Locally isometric immersions of disks provide also our main tool to control the complexity of simple trajectories in a flat sphere.

Lemma 4.4
.

In a flat sphere

and its Delaunay disk locally isometric immersion

Proof.

Since

is formed by chords of the disk
\mathcal{D}
. Since

the (possibly infinite) subset of these chords crossing the Delaunay triangle
\mathcal{T}
. Let

\mathcal{C}h

Cutting along chords of

.

We denote by

the union of chords corresponding to edges of this subgraph. The linear graph provides a natural linear order of these chords

. For each chord

.

For any

, we denote by

the domain in

bounded by

and
c_{j+1}
. We are going to estimate the total arc-length

of

We first consider the case where two consecutive chords

and

.

Now we deal with the case where three consecutive chords

4.3.
Essentially sharp upper bound on diameter

We deduce from Lemma

an upper bound on the

of a flat sphere.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

For any pair of points

in the flat sphere, we are going to prove that the distance between

Our upper bound on the diameter is of order

. In fact, this upper bound is essentially sharp which means that there is a surface whose diameter has a lower bound of order

. We will construct such a surface in the following example. It also implies that an upper bound on the diameter has to depend on the number of singularities.

Upper bounds

5.1.
Bounding combinatorial lengths of trajectories

Definition 5.1
.

In a flat sphere endowed with a fixed Delaunay triangulation, for any non-periodic trajectory

in
X

of

as the number of segments of

in the complement of

of the union of Delaunay edges.

In the following proposition, we use the curvature gap to give an upper bound on the combinatorial length of any simple trajectory.

Proposition 5.2
.

We consider a flat sphere
X
with
n
conical singularities, a curvature gap
\delta>0
and a fixed Delaunay triangulation. For any simple trajectory
t
in
X
, unless
t
is contained in a Delaunay edge, the combinatorial length of

is at most
\frac{5n}{\delta}
.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
t
is a saddle connection. Any Delaunay triangle
\mathcal{T}
is contained in the image of some locally isometric immersion
f
of a disk
\mathcal{D}
. Following Lemma
4.4
, the pullback of
t
by this immersion is formed by at most
\frac{5}{2\delta}
chords crossing the triangle.

Since there are
2n-4
triangles in the Delaunay triangulation, the intersection of
t
with the complement of Delaunay edges is formed by at most
(2n-4)\frac{5}{2\delta}
distinct segments. We deduce an upper bound on the number of intersections with Delaunay edges.

If
t
is not a saddle connection, we can mark its endpoints to create conical singularities of angle
2\pi
. This does not change the value of
\delta
but may increase the number of triangles in the Delaunay triangulation by

. The upper bound obtained is
\frac{5n}{\delta}
instead.

Note that the trajectory cannot be periodic because it would cut out the flat sphere into two components and force the curvature gap to be zero.
∎

We next prove an upper bound on the number of simple saddle connections using Proposition

.

Kneser introduced in

, we call a simple path

if it intersects with edges of

transversely except at its endpoints and it enters and leaves a triangle of

via different edges. In particular, if a simple saddle connection is not an edge of a Delaunay triangulation, it is normal with respect to the Delaunay triangulation.

Denote by

a normal simple path. For every edge

of

with the interior of the edge

\gamma(e)

Lemma 5.3
.

Given a triangulation

Proof.

Notice that triangles of

For a triangle of

is even and

for

. As normal coordinates in
[
4
]
, for fixed

For

Corollary 5.4
.

In a flat sphere with
n
conical singularities and a curvature gap of
\delta>0
, the number of simple saddle connections is at most
\frac{(\frac{5n}{\delta}+3n-7)^{3n-6}}{(3n-7)!}+3n-6
.

Proof.

We shall prove an upper bound on the number of saddle connections crossing exactly
k
Delaunay edges. Denote by
\gamma
such a saddle connection. If
\gamma
is an edge of the Delaunay triangulation, there are
3n-6
many choices. If
\gamma
is not an edge of the Delaunay triangulation, it is normal with respect to the Delaunay triangulation. It follows that the normal coordinate of
\gamma

\gamma
crosses exactly
k

, the number of saddle connections crossing exactly

\left(\begin{array}[]{l}k+3(n-2)-1\\

.

Summing all cases up, the total number of saddle connections crossing at most

Since Proposition

\frac{(\frac{5n}{\delta}+3n-7)^{3n-6}}{(3n-7)!}+3n-6

∎

5.2.
Trajectories with self-intersections

In order to bound the combinatorial length of nonsimple trajectories, we combine geometric estimates with purely topological bounds on isotopically disjoint loops. We start by giving a rigorous definition of the number of self-intersections of a trajectory (see
[
3
]
for the notion of self-intersections for geodesics on hyperbolic surfaces).

Definition 5.5

At any self-intersection point

(for the orientation of

of

The following topological lemma follows directly from the pairs-of-pants decomposition.

Lemma 5.6

In a topological sphere with

2n-3
.

A monogonal trajectory (see Section

)

•

•

The following lemma give bounds for a trajectory entirely contained in the inner component relative to a monogonal trajectory. Notice that the result holds as long as the bound on combinatorial lengths of simple trajectories on

Lemma 5.7
.

We consider a flat sphere

with a monogonal trajectory
\gamma

We assume that

be the topological cylinder cut out by

Now we are able to generalize the bound of Proposition

We can decompose trajectory

Since combinatorial lengths of

5.3.
Bounding metric lengths of trajectories

We have an upper bound on combinatorial lengths of trajectories with bounded number of self-intersections (see Proposition
5.8
). Combined with a control on the maximal length of Delaunay edges (see Lemma
4.3
), we get an explicit upper bound on the maximal length of a trajectory with bounded number of self-intersections (including simple trajectories). Remarkably, this bound does not depend on the systole (length of the smallest simple saddle connection) but only on the curvature gap. The bound is uniform on the moduli space of flat sphere with prescribed conical angles.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Following Lemma
4.3
, the length
L
of any Delaunay edge in such a flat sphere
X
satisfies
L^{2}<\frac{4}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2\pi\delta}
and thus
L<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}}
.

For a simple trajectory
t
of
X
that is not contained in a Delaunay edge, we deduce from the proof of Proposition
5.2
that
t
is formed by at most
\frac{5n}{\delta}
segments, each being contained in a unique Delaunay triangle. We obtain immediately that the metric length of
t
is less than
\frac{10n}{\delta\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{5n}{\delta^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi}}
.

For a self-intersecting trajectory
t
with at most
k
self-intersections, Proposition
5.8
implies that the combinatorial length is at most
4l(n-1)\sqrt{|t\cap t|}+4l
and Proposition
5.2
implies that
l\leq\frac{5n}{\delta}
. Combining with
L<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta}}
, the metric length is less than

\frac{40n(n-1)\sqrt{k}+40n}{\delta\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{20n(n-1)\sqrt{k}+20n}{\delta^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi}}.

∎
