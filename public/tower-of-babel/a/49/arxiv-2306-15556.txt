Abstract
. We introduce the Primitive Eulerian polynomial P 𝒜 ​ ( z ) P_{\mathcal{A}}(z) of a central hyperplane arrangement 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . It is a reparametrization of its cocharacteristic polynomial. Previous work of the first author implicitly show that, for simplicial arrangements, P 𝒜 ​ ( z ) P_{\mathcal{A}}(z) has nonnegative coefficients. For reflection arrangements of type A and B, the same work interprets the coefficients of P 𝒜 ​ ( z ) P_{\mathcal{A}}(z) using the (flag)excedance statistic on (signed) permutations. The main result of this article is to provide an interpretation of the coefficients of P 𝒜 ​ ( z ) P_{\mathcal{A}}(z) for all simplicial arrangements only using the geometry and combinatorics of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . This new interpretation sheds more light to the case of reflection arrangements and, for the first time, gives combinatorial meaning to the coefficients of the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of the reflection arrangement of type D. In type B, we find a connection between the Primitive Eulerian polynomial and the 1 / 2 1/2 -Eulerian polynomial of Savage and Viswanathan (2012). We present some real-rootedness results and conjectures for P 𝒜 ​ ( z ) P_{\mathcal{A}}(z) .

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite linear hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{L}
its intersection lattice ordered by inclusion and
\bot\in\mathcal{L}
its minimum element (i.e. the intersection of all the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
).
The aim of this this article is to study the
Primitive Eulerian polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z):=\sum_{\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}}|\mu(\bot,\mathrm{X})|(z-1)^{\codim(\mathrm{X})},

where
\mu
is the Möbius function of
\mathcal{L}
.

The Primitive Eulerian polynomial appeared implicitly in previous work of the first author
[
5
]
, but only in the simplicial case.
Concretely,
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
is the Hilbert-Poincare series of a certain graded subspace of the
polytope algebra of generalized zonotopes of
\mathcal{A}

[
34
,
35
]
,
and therefore has non-negative coefficients.
It can also be obtained as a reparametrization of the cocharacteristic polynomial studied by Novik, Postnikov, and Sturmfels in
[
36
]
which, conjecturally, cannot be obtained as a specialization of the well-known Tutte polynomial.

The main result of this article is to provide an explanation for the non-negativity of these coefficients from the geometry and combinatorics of
\mathcal{A}
, more precisely, via
generic halfspaces
and the
weak order
on the regions of
\mathcal{A}
.
Furthermore, for reflection arrangements of types A, B, and D, we give a combinatorial interpretation of these coefficients in terms of the usual
Coxeter descent
statistic.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{R}
denote the collection of
regions of
\mathcal{A}
: the (closures of) connected components of the complement of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For any two regions
C
and
C^{\prime}
,
{\rm sep}(C,C^{\prime})
is the set of hyperplanes
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
that separate
C
and
C^{\prime}
;
that is, such that
C
and
C^{\prime}
are not contained in the same halfspace bounded by
\mathrm{H}
.
Fix a
base region

B\in\mathcal{R}
.
The
weak order with base region
B

[
33
,
17
]
is the partial order relation
\preceq_{B}
on
\mathcal{R}
defined by

C\preceq_{B}C^{\prime}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad{\rm sep}(B,C)\subseteq{\rm sep}(B,C^{\prime}).

Let
\des_{\preceq_{B}}(C)
denote the number of regions covered by
C
in the partial order
\preceq_{B}
.

A hyperplane
\mathrm{H}
is
generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if it contains
\bot
and does not contain any other flat of
\mathcal{A}
.
A halfspace
\mathsf{h}
bounded by such a hyperplane is also said to be
generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.
A vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
very generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if it is not contained in any hyperplane of
\mathcal{A}
and the halfspace
\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\langle x,v\rangle\leq 0\}
is generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.

A simplicial arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
sharp
if for all regions
C\in\mathcal{R}
, the angle between any two facets of
C
is at most
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. Notably, all
Coxeter
arrangements are sharp.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a sharp arrangement.
Then, for any very generic vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{C\subseteq\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}}z^{\des_{\preceq_{B(v)}}(C)},

where
B(v)\in\mathcal{R}
denotes the unique region of
\mathcal{A}
containing
v
.
The sum is over all regions
C\in\mathcal{R}
contained in
\mathsf{h}^{-}_{v}
.

For a precise statement see
Theorem

3.9
.
The figure on the right illustrates this theorem, as we now explain.
It shows
the intersection with the unit sphere of
a sharp arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The very generic vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
lies in the region antipodal to the region with label
3
, so it is not visible on this picture.
The blue regions
C
are those contained in the halfspace
\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}
, and their labels indicate the values
\des_{\preceq_{B(v)}}(C)
.
It follows that the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of this arrangement is

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=z^{3}+10z^{2}+4z
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is the reflection arrangement of type
{\rm A}_{n-1}
(resp.
{\rm B}_{n}
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the first author provides in
[
5
]

a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
in terms of the excedance (resp. flag-excedance) statistic.
Concretely, let
{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
(resp.
{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
) denote the collection of elements whose action on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
only fixes points in
\bot
, then

P_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(z)=\sum_{w\in{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}z^{\exc(w)}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad P_{\mathfrak{B}_{n}}(z)=\sum_{w\in{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})}z^{\exc_{B}(w)}.

The notation reflects the fact that these are precisely the
cuspidal elements
of the corresponding Coxeter group
[
24
]
.
In
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, they are precisely the cycles of order
n
; and in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, they are the fixed-point-free products of balanced cycles.
See
Sections

5
and
6
for details.
However, up to now, there was no combinatorial interpretation for the reflection arrangement of type D:
using
Theorem

3.9
and building upon the work of Björner and Wachs
[
9
]
, we provide such an interpretation.
More precisely, the Coxeter group of type
{\rm D}_{n}
can be identified with the group of
even signed permutations
: words
w=w_{1}w_{2}\dots w_{n}
with
w_{i}\in[\pm n]=\{-n,\dots,-2,-1,1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
|w_{1}||w_{2}|\dots|w_{n}|\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}

and
w
has an even number of negative letters.
Let
BW^{D}_{n}
be the collection of
w
such that all the right-to-left maxima of
|w_{1}||w_{2}|\dots|w_{n}|\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are negative in
w
and
|w_{1}|\neq n
.
We obtain the following.

Theorem B
.

For all
n\geq 2
,

P_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(z)=\sum_{w\in BW^{D}_{n}}z^{\des(w)}.

For a precise statement see
Theorem

7.1
.
Analogous results for type A and B appear in
Theorems

5.2
and
6.3
.

The article is organized as follows.
In
Section

1
we review some preliminaries on hyperplane arrangements.
We introduce the Primitive Eulerian polynomial in
Section

2
and give a combinatorial interpretation for its coefficients, in the case of simplicial arrangements, in
Section

3
.
We specialize our result to the case of Coxeter arrangements in
Section

4
;
closely examine the types A, B, and D cases in
Sections

5
,
6
and
7
;
and compute the Primitive Eulerian polynomial for an infinite family of simplicial arrangements sitting between the type D and type B arrangements in
Section

8
.
Finally, in
Section

9
we explore some real-rootedness results and conjectures.

1.
Preliminaries and notation

We start by recalling some classical definitions, including restriction, localization and the Tits semigroup (or face semigroup) of a hyperplane arrangement; see for instance
[
3
,
45
]
for more details.
The reader familiar with these concepts can safely proceed to
Section

2
.

In this article we only consider
finite real linear hyperplane arrangements
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., a finite collection of linear hyperplanes
\mathrm{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The subspaces obtained by intersecting some hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
are called
flats
of
\mathcal{A}
.
The collection
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
of flats of
\mathcal{A}
is naturally ordered by inclusion.
It turns out that the poset
(\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}],\leq)
is a graded lattice with maximum element
\top:=\mathbb{R}^{n}

and minimum element
\bot:=\bigcap\mathcal{A}
. The arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
essential
if
\bot=\{0\}
.
Otherwise, we denote by
{\rm ess}(\mathcal{A})
the
essentialization
of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., the intersection of
\mathcal{A}
with the subspace orthogonal to
\bot
.

1.1.
Restriction and localization

Let
\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
be a flat of
\mathcal{A}
.
The
restriction
of
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathrm{X}
is the following hyperplane arrangement inside
\mathrm{X}
:

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{X}}=\{\mathrm{X}\cap\mathrm{H}\,:\,\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A},\,\mathrm{X}\not\subseteq\mathrm{H}\}.

Note that the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{X}}
are precisely the flats
\mathrm{Y}<\mathrm{X}
of dimension
\dim(\mathrm{X})-1
.
On the other hand, the
localization
of
\mathcal{A}
at
\mathrm{X}
is the arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
consisting of those hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathrm{X}
:

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{X}}=\{\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}\,:\,\mathrm{X}\subseteq\mathrm{H}\}.

1.2.
Regions and faces

The complement of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the disjoint union of open sets whose closures are convex polyhedral cones.
These full-dimensional cones are the
regions
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
the collection of regions of
\mathcal{A}
.
A
face
of
\mathcal{A}
is any face of a cone in
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
.
Denote by
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
the collection of faces of
\mathcal{A}
, it forms a complete polyhedral fan.
Its maximal elements are the regions of
\mathcal{A}
, and its unique minimal face, which coincides with the minimal flat
\bot
, is denoted
O\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
.
Finally, the
rank of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
\rank(\mathcal{A})
, is the rank of the poset
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
or, equivalently, the rank of the lattice
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
.

The arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is
simplicial
if every region contains exactly
\rank(\mathcal{A})
faces of rank
1
.
If
\mathcal{A}
is essential, this is equivalent to each cone in
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
being simplicial.

1.3.
The Tits product

The set
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
has the structure of a monoid under the
Tits product
.
Informally, the product
FG
of two faces
F,G\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
is the first face you enter when moving a small positive distance from a point in the relative interior of
F
to a point in the relative interior of
G
, this is illustrated in
Figure

1
.
In order to formalize this product, it will be useful to review the
sign sequence
of a face.

Each hyperplane
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
defines two halfspaces
\mathrm{H}^{+}
and
\mathrm{H}^{-}
.
The choice of signs
+
and
-
for each hyperplane is arbitrary, but fixed.
The sign sequence of a face
F\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
is the vector
\sigma(F)\in\{0,+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}
determined by

\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }F\subseteq\mathrm{H},\\
+&\text{if }F\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{+}\text{ and }F\not\subseteq\mathrm{H},\\
-&\text{if }F\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{-}\text{ and }F\not\subseteq\mathrm{H}.\end{cases}

Given two faces
F,G\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
, the product
FG
is the face with sign sequence

(1)

\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(FG)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F)&\text{if }\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F)\neq 0,\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(G)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This operation first appeared in work of Tits
[
48
]
on Coxeter complexes and of Bland
[
10
]
on oriented matroids.
Brown observed in
[
14
]
that the
monoid
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
is a
left-regular band
, that is, it satisfies
F^{2}=F
and
FGF=FG
for all faces
F,G\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
.
We highlight two other important properties of the Tits product.
Let
O\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
be the central face of
\mathcal{A}
and
F,G\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
be any two faces. Then,

(2)

OF=F=FO\qquad\qquad F\leq FG

That is,
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
is a monoid with unit
O
, and
F
is always a face of the product
FG
.

A hyperplane
\mathrm{H}

separates
two faces
F
and
G
if
\{\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F),\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(G)\}=\{+,-\}
.
That is, if
\mathrm{H}
does not contain
F
nor
G
, and
F
and
G
are contained in opposite halfspaces determined by
\mathrm{H}
.
Let
F\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
, its
opposite face

\overline{F}
is the face with sign sequence
\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(\overline{F})=-\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F)
. Geometrically,
\overline{F}=\{-x\,:\,x\in F\}
.

2.
The Primitive Eulerian polynomial

We introduce the main definition of this article.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a linear arrangement.

Definition 2.1
.

The
Primitive Eulerian polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}
is

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}}|\mu(\bot,\mathrm{X})|(z-1)^{\codim(\mathrm{X})},

where
\mu
denotes the Möbius function of
\mathcal{L}
. The sum is over all the flats of the arrangement.

The Primitive Eulerian polynomial can also be obtained as a reparametrization of
the
cocharacteristic polynomial

\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
:

(3)

\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}}|\mu(\bot,\mathrm{X})|z^{\dim(\mathrm{X})}.

Explicitly,

(4)

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=(z-1)^{n}\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\dfrac{1}{z-1}\big).

Novik, Postnikov, and Sturmfels encountered this polynomial in their study of unimodular toric arrangements and remarked that this polynomial does not seem to be a specialization of the Tutte polynomial of
\mathcal{A}
.
If that is the case, then the Primitive Eulerian polynomial cannot be a specialization of the well-known Tutte polynomial either.

The Primitive Eulerian polynomial is monic: it’s leading coefficient is
|\mu(\bot,\bot)|=1
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
is not trivial (contains at least one hyperplane), then

P_{\mathcal{A}}(0)=\sum_{\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}}|\mu(\bot,\mathrm{X})|(-1)^{\codim(\mathrm{X})}=(-1)^{\rank(\mathcal{A})}\sum_{\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{X})=0.

That is, the constant term of
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
is zero.

Example 2.2

(Rank 1 arrangement)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a hyperplane arrangement of rank
1
: it consists of a single hyperplane
\mathrm{H}
.
Then,
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]=\{\bot<\top\}
, where
\bot=\mathrm{H}
, and

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=(z-1)+1=z.

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
is independent of the dimension of the ambient space.
Indeed, for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, the codimension of a flat
\mathrm{X}\in\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
is precisely the corank of
\mathrm{X}
in the lattice
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
. Thus, the polynomial
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
is completely determined by
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
.
In particular,

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=P_{{\rm ess}(\mathcal{A})}(z).

Example 2.4

(Rank 2 arrangements)
.

For
k\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{I}_{2}(k)
be the linear arrangement of
k
lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Möbius function of
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
is determined by

\mu(\bot,\mathrm{L})=-1\text{ for all lines }\mathrm{L}\in\mathcal{A},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(\bot,\top)=k-1.

We obtain

P_{\mathcal{I}_{2}(k)}(z)=(z-1)^{2}+k(z-1)+(k-1)=z^{2}+(k-2)z.

It directly follows from the definition that the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of a Cartesian product of arrangements is the product of the corresponding Primitive Eulerian polynomials.
That is,

(5)

P_{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(z)=P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)P_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(z).

Previous work of the first author implicitly shows that the polynomial
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
has nonnegative coefficients whenever the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is simplicial.
We provide the details below.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any simplicial arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
are nonnegative.

Proof.

For every flat
\mathrm{Y}\in\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}]
, let
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}}
be a zonotope dual to
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{Y}}
.
That is, such that the normal fan of
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}}
is
\Sigma[\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{Y}}]
.
In particular, the zonotope
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}}
is a simple polytope.
Then,
[
5
, Equation (17)]
shows that the polynomial

\sum_{\mathrm{Y}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{Y})h(\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}},z)
,
where
h(\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}},z)
denotes the
h
polynomial of
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}}
,
has nonnegative coefficients. Manipulating
Definition

2.1
, we obtain

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z+1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y},\mathrm{Z}\\
\mathrm{X}\geq\mathrm{Z}\geq\mathrm{Y}\end{subarray}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{Y})|\mu(\mathrm{Z},\mathrm{X})|z^{\codim(\mathrm{X})}=\sum_{\mathrm{Y}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{Y})f(\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}},z),

where the second equality follows from Zaslavsky’s
[
50

and Las Vergnas’
[

formulas to count the number of regions of an arrangement.
Therefore,

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{\mathrm{Y}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{Y})f(\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}},z-1)=\sum_{\mathrm{Y}}\mu(\bot,\mathrm{Y})h(\mathfrak{z}_{\mathrm{Y}},z)

has nonnegative coefficients.
∎

3.
Generic halfspaces, descents, and a combinatorial interpretation

In this section, we interpret the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
in combinatorial terms for any simplicial arrangement and therefore provide a proof of
Theorem

A
.

3.1.
Generic halfspaces

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nontrivial hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A hyperplane
\mathrm{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(not in the arrangement) is
generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if it contains the minimum flat
\bot
and it does not contain any other flat of
\mathcal{A}
.
The first condition guarantees that there is a canonical correspondence between generic hyperplanes with respect to
\mathcal{A}
and generic hyperplanes with respect to its essentialization
{\rm ess}(\mathcal{A})
.

Figure 3.
Two copies of the spherical representation of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
each with a different hyperplane
\mathrm{H}
not in
\mathcal{A}
(dashed, in red).
The hyperplane on the left is not generic since it contains the marked flat of rank
1
of
\mathcal{A}
.
The hyperplane on the right is generic.

A halfspace
\mathsf{h}
is
generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if its bounding hyperplane is generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\mathsf{h}
be such a halfspace.
A
result of Greene and Zaslavsky
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
shows that the number of regions
C\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
completely contained in
\mathsf{h}
is
|\mu(\bot,\top)|
.
Note that for all flats
\mathrm{X}
, the halfspace
\mathsf{h}\cap\mathrm{X}
is generic with respect to the arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{X}}
.
As a straightforward generalization of Greene and Zaslavsky’s result, and in view of the definition of the cocharacteristic polynomial in
Equation

3
, we obtain:

(6)

\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{F\subseteq\mathsf{h}}z^{\dim(F)},

where the sum is over all the faces
F\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
that are contained in
\mathsf{h}
.

Given a subset
K
of the ambient space, let
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(K)\subseteq\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{A}}(K)\subseteq\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
denote the collection of faces and regions, respectively, of
\mathcal{A}
contained in
K
.
When the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is clear from the context, we drop
\mathcal{A}
from the notation and simply write
\Sigma(K)
and
\mathcal{R}(K)
.
If the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is simplicial, then
\Sigma(K)
can be viewed as a simplicial complex, where the vertices are the rays of
\mathcal{A}
and the central face of
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to the empty face of the simplicial complex.
We obtain the following geometric interpretation for the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
in the simplicial case.

\mathcal{A}
.
Observe that even though
\mathsf{h}

We have that
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=1+7z+12z^{2}+6z^{3}

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=z^{3}+4z^{2}+z

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a simplicial arrangement and
\mathsf{h}
a generic halfspace with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.
Then,

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=z^{n}h(\Sigma(\mathsf{h}),\tfrac{1}{z}).

Proof.

Formula
Equation

6
is equivalent to
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=f(\Sigma(\mathsf{h}),z)
.
Thus,

\displaystyle z^{n}h(\Sigma(\mathsf{h}),\tfrac{1}{z})=z^{n}(1-\tfrac{1}{z})^{n}f\Big(\Sigma(\mathsf{h}),\dfrac{1/z}{1-1/z}\Big)=(z-1)^{n}\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\dfrac{1}{z-1}\big).

The result follows by
Equation

4
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Reiner
[

]
and Stembridge
[

studied

of the form

, where
\mathcal{A}
is a Coxeter arrangement and

(parset) of the arrangement–a convex set obtained as the union of some regions of
\mathcal{A}
.
The complexes obtained form a generic halfspace are in general not convex; see for instance
Figure

.
In that example,
\Sigma(\mathsf{h})
is not convex, and therefore not a cone of the corresponding arrangement (the essentialization of the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
).

3.2.
The weak order

The definitions and results of this section hold for arbitrary linear arrangements, including non-simplicial arrangements.

Recall that a hyperplane
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}

separates
faces
F,G\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
if
\{\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(F),\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}(G)\}=\{-,+\}
.
Given any two regions
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
, let
{\rm sep}(C,C^{\prime})
denote the collection of hyperplanes
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
that separate
C
and
C^{\prime}
.
Fix a region
B\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
which we call a
base region
, and
consider the following relation on
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
:

C\preceq_{B}D\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad{\rm sep}(B,C)\subseteq{\rm sep}(B,D).

Then,
\preceq_{B}
is a partial order with minimum element
B
and maximum element
\overline{B}
, the region opposite to the base region
B
.
This order is called the
weak order of
\mathcal{A}
with base region
B
,
and was independently introduced by Mandel
[
33
]
and Edelman
[
17
]
.
Mandel’s definition was given in the context of oriented matroids, where it was called the
Tope graph
of
\mathcal{A}
.
This order was further studied by Björner, Edelman and Ziegler
[
7
]
, who showed that whenever
\mathcal{A}
is simplicial, the weak order with respect to any base region is a lattice.
When the base region
B
is clear from the context, we write
\preceq
instead of
\preceq_{B}
.

For any face
F\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
, the collection of regions that contain it,

\mathcal{R}_{F}:=\{C\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]\,:\,F\leq C\},

is called the
top-star
of
F
.
See
Figure

5
for an example.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any base region
B
and face
F
, the top-star
\mathcal{R}_{F}
is an interval in the partial order
\preceq_{B}
:
its minimum element is
FB
and its maximum element is
F\overline{B}
.

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence from the next two observations, which follow from the Tits product description in
Equation

1
.
First, if
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
separates
B
and
F
, then it also separates
B
and any region in
\mathcal{R}_{F}
.
Moreover, any hyperplane
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
containing
F
does not separate
B
and
FB
, and does separate
B
and
F\overline{B}
.
∎

Figure 5.
A non-simplicial arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.
The faces
F
(blue) and
G
(red) have rank
1
and
2
, respectively.
The top-stars
\mathcal{R}_{F}
and
\mathcal{R}_{G}
(shaded) contain a minimum and maximum element in the order
\preceq_{B}
.

3.3.
Descents sets

Fix a base region
B\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
.
Given a polyhedral subcomplex
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
, its
descent set
is

\Des_{B}(\Delta):=\{F\in\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]\,:\,F\overline{B}\in\Delta\}.

Observe that, since
F\leq FC
for any faces
F
and
C
(see
Equation

2
), we have that
\Des_{B}(\Delta)\subseteq\Delta
.
However,
\Des_{B}(\Delta)
might fail to be a complex, as illustrated in
Figure

6
.

Figure 6.
A subcomplex
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
in blue, it includes face
F
in red.
Observe, however, that
F\overline{B}\notin\Delta
.
Thus,
F\notin\Des_{B}(\Delta)
,
\Des_{B}(\Delta)\neq\Delta
, and
\Des_{B}(\Delta)
is not a complex.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
be a nonempty polyhedral subcomplex.
Then,

\Des_{B}(\Delta)=\Delta

if and only if
\Delta
is pure and
\mathcal{R}(\Delta):=\Delta\cap\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
is a nonempty upper set of the partial order
\preceq_{B}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Des_{B}(\Delta)=\Delta
.
Since
O\in\Delta
, we have
\overline{B}=O\overline{B}\in\Delta
, so
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
is not empty.
Moreover, every face
F\in\Delta
is contained in an element of
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
, namely
F\overline{B}
, so
\Delta
is pure.
Let
C\in\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
and
D\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
be a region covering
C
in the order
\preceq_{B}
.
Let
F=C\cap D
be the common facet of
C
and
D
.
In particular,
F\leq C
and
F\in\Delta
.
Since the top-star of
F
is simply
\mathcal{R}_{F}=\{C,D\}
and
C\preceq_{B}D
, we have
F\overline{B}=\max_{\preceq_{B}}\mathcal{R}_{F}=D\in\Des_{B}(\Delta)
.
Since this occurs for every
C\in\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
and region
D
covering
C
, we conclude that
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
is an upper set.

Now assume
\Delta
is pure and
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
is a nonempty upper set of
\mathcal{R}
.
Recall that
\Des_{B}(\Delta)\subseteq\Delta
holds in general, we prove the reverse inclusion.
Let
F\in\Delta
and
C\in\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
containing
F
, which exists since
\Delta
is pure.
In particular,
C\in\mathcal{R}_{F}
and
C\preceq_{B}\max_{\preceq_{B}}\mathcal{R}_{F}=F\overline{B}
.
Since
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
is an upper set, we have that
F\overline{B}\in\Delta
, so
F\in\Des_{B}(\Delta)
, as we wanted to show.
∎

3.4.
The simplicial case

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a simplicial arrangement.
Given a base region
B\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
, we let
\des_{\preceq_{B}}(C)
denote the number of regions covered by
C
in the weak order
\preceq_{B}
.
If the base region
B
is clear from context, we simply write
\des(C)
.
The collection of faces
F\leq C
such that
F\overline{B}=C
forms a boolean poset of rank
\des(C)
, see for example
[
3
, Section 7.1.1]
.
Therefore,

(7)

(z+1)^{\des(C)}=\sum_{F\,:\,F\overline{B}=C}z^{\codim(F)}.

The following result is obtained by combining
Equation

7
and
Lemma

3.4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Sigma[\mathcal{A}]
be a pure complex such that
\mathcal{R}(\Delta)
is a nonempty upper set.
Then,

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{R}(\Delta)}(z+1)^{\des(C)}=\sum_{F\in\Delta}z^{\codim(F)}.

Remark 3.6
.

The preceding result can also be deduced using the fact that any linear extension of the weak order gives a shelling of the corresponding complex.
See for example

for a result in the language of oriented matroids,

for a generalization to complete polyhedral fans.

When
\Delta=\Sigma(\mathsf{h})
for a generic halfspace

takes the following form.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathsf{h}
be a generic halfspace with respect to
\mathcal{A}
such that

is an upper set.
Then,

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{C\subseteq\mathsf{h}}z^{\des(C)}.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{H}^{\prime}
be an affine hyperplane contained in

; it is necessarily a parallel translate of the bounding hyperplane of
\mathsf{h}
.
The collection
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}:=\{\mathrm{H}\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}\,:\,\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}\}
forms an
affine hyperplane arrangement
in ambient space
\mathrm{H}^{\prime}
.
The bounded faces of
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}
are precisely those of the form
F\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}
for

.
A result of Zaslavsky
[

shows that the bounded complex of a hyperplane arrangement is pure, and consequently
\Sigma(\mathsf{h})
is pure.

It now follows from

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=(z-1)^{d}\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(\tfrac{1}{z-1})=(z-1)^{d}\sum_{F\subseteq\mathsf{h}}(z-1)^{-\dim(F)}=\sum_{F\subseteq\mathsf{h}}(z-1)^{\codim(F)}=\sum_{C\subseteq\mathsf{h}}z^{\des(C)},

as claimed.
∎

3.5.
Sharp arrangements

In this section we use
d
to denote the dimension of the ambient space, and reserve
n
to denote normal vectors.

Definition 3.8
.

We say that an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
sharp
if it is simplicial, and the angle between any two facets of any region
C\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
is at most
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
.

Notably, all finite reflection arrangements are sharp.
In particular, the coordinate arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sharp for all
d
.

Figure

8
presents a non-sharp arrangement combinatorially isomorphic to the coordinate arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This shows that sharpness is a geometric and not a combinatorial condition.
Moreover, observe that an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is sharp if and only if its essentialization is sharp.

Assume that
\mathcal{A}
is essential and take a region
C\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
.
Let
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{d}
be vectors spanning its rays (faces of dimension
1
).
Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
be the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
dual to
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{d}
; that is
\langle n_{i},r_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}
.
It follows that
n_{k}
is an inward normal to the facet
F_{k}\lessdot C
with rays spanned by
r_{1},\dots,r_{k-1},r_{k+1},\dots,r_{d}
.
Thus, the angle between facets
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
is
\cos^{-1}(-\tfrac{\langle n_{i},n_{j}\rangle}{|\!|n_{i}|\!|\,|\!|n_{j}|\!|})
.
That is, an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is sharp if for all regions
C\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
we have
\langle n_{i},n_{j}\rangle\leq 0
for
i\neq j
.
In this case, since

n_{k}=\langle n_{k},n_{k}\rangle r_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}\langle n_{k},n_{j}\rangle r_{j},

we have that

(8)

r_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\{r_{1},\dots,r_{k-1},n_{k},r_{k+1},\dots,r_{d}\},\quad\text{for }k=1,\dots,d.

A vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if
v
is not contained in any hyperplane of
\mathcal{A}
.
Given a generic
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
B(v)\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}]
be the region of
\mathcal{A}
containing
v
, and let
\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}
be the halfspace
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0\}
.
We say that
v
is
very generic
if in addition
\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}
is generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.

Figure 8.
A rank 2 arrangement that is
not sharp
.
For this particular choice of
v
, the collection
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-})
(consisting of a single region)
is not an upper set with respect to
\preceq_{B(v)}
.
For the region
C
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-})
we have
\des(C)=1
, but
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=z^{2}
.
That is, equation
Equation

9
does not hold for this choice of
v
.

Theorem 3.9

(Theorem A)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a sharp arrangement.
Then, for any very generic vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(9)

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\sum_{C\subseteq\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}}z^{\des_{\preceq_{B(v)}}(C)}.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.7
, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-})
is an upper set of the weak order with base region
B(v)
.
Take regions
C,D
with
C\in\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-})
and
D
covering
C
in the order
\preceq_{B(v)}
,
we claim that
D\in\mathcal{R}(\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-})
.

By intersecting with the space orthogonal to
\bot
if necessary, we assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{A}
is essential.
Let
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{A}
be the hyperplane separating
C
and
D
, and
n_{\mathrm{H}}
be a normal vector of
\mathrm{H}
such that
C\subseteq H^{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\langle n_{\mathrm{H}},x\rangle\geq 0\}
.
Since
C\preceq_{B(v)}D
, we have that
B(v)
is also contained in
H^{+}
.
In particular,
\langle n_{\mathrm{H}},v\rangle>0
.
Let
r_{1},\dots,r_{d-1},r_{d}
be the vectors spanning the rays of
D
numbered so that
r_{1},\dots,r_{d-1}
are the rays of the common facet between
C
and
D
, namely
C\cap D
.
In particular,
r_{1},\dots,r_{d-1}
are rays of
C
and, since
C\subseteq\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}
by hypothesis,
\langle r_{i},v\rangle<0
for all
i\in[d-1]
.
Observe that
n_{\mathrm{H}}=-\lambda n_{d}
for some
\lambda>0
.
It then follows from
Equation

8
with
k=d
that
\langle r_{d},v\rangle<0
and
D\subseteq\mathsf{h}^{-}_{v}
, as we wanted to show.
∎

4.
The Primitive Eulerian polynomial of finite Coxeter arrangements

Let
(W,S)
be a finite Coxeter system and
\mathcal{A}_{W}
be the associated reflection arrangement.
For the combinatorics of Coxeter groups, and realizations of the groups of type A, B, and D as permutation groups, we refer the reader to the book of Björner and Brenti
[
6
]
.

The
Eulerian polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}_{W}
is

E_{W}(z)=\sum_{w\in W}z^{\des(w)},

where
\des
denotes the
descent
statistic on
W
.
That is
\des(w):=\#\{s\in S\,:\,l(ws)<l(w)\}
and
l(w)
denotes the length of
w
with respect to
S
.

Let us now choose a base region
B\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}_{W}]
.
The group
W
acts simply transitively on
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}_{W}]
and
\des(w)=\des_{\preceq_{B}}(wB)
for every
w\in W
.
A real reflection arrangement is always sharp, so
Theorem

3.9
yields the following.

Corollary 4.1
.

For any very generic vector
v
with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{W}
,

(10)

P_{W}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in W\,:\,wB\subseteq\mathsf{h}_{v}^{-}\end{subarray}}z^{\des(w)},

where
B=B(v)\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{A}_{W}]
is the region containing
v
.

Since any reflection arrangement is the Cartesian product of irreducible reflection arrangements, we only concentrate in studying the Primitive Eulerian polynomial for irreducible arrangements.
In
Sections

5
,
6
and
7
, we make an explicit choice of generic
v
for the arrangements of type A, B, and D, respectively, and use formula
Equation

10
to give a combinatorial interpretation for the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of the corresponding type.

The relation between the Eulerian polynomial and the Primitive Eulerian polynomial becomes even more apparent when we compare their generating functions in types A, B, and D.
The generating function for the classical Eulerian polynomials was established by Euler himself
[
18
]
:

(11)

A(z,x):=\sum_{n\geq 0}E_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}(z)\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}=\dfrac{z-1}{z-e^{x(z-1)}}.

We refer the reader to Foata’s survey
[
21
, Section 3]
for a derivation of this formula.
The generating function for the Eulerian polynomials of type B and D are due to Brenti
[
13
, Theorem 3.4 and Corollary 4.9]
.
They can be expressed in terms of
A(z,x)
as follows:

\sum_{n\geq 0}E_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}=e^{x(z-1)}A(z,2x)\qquad\qquad\sum_{n\geq 0}E_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(z)\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}=\big(e^{x(z-1)}-zx\big)A(z,2x).

Compare with the generating functions for the Primitive Eulerian polynomials below.

Theorem 4.2
.

The generating function for the Primitive Eulerian polynomials of type A, B, and D, with
P_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(z):=0
, are:

Type B

Type D

The formulas in type A and B appear in the proofs of Lemma 5.3 and Lemma 6.2 in
[

]
, respectively.
We complete the type D case in
Section

Setting
P_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(z)=0
is not an accident of the proof, it also simplifies the recursive formulas for the Primitive Eulerian polynomials for type D and related arrangements in
Theorems

7.3
and

.

in its one-line notation
w_{1}w_{2}\dots w_{n}
, where
w_{i}=w(i)
, or as a product of disjoint cycles.
For example, both
2\,4\,1\,3\,5
and
(1\,2\,4\,3)(5)
denote the same element of
\mathfrak{S}_{5}
.
The
descent
and
excedance
statistic of a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are:

\displaystyle\des(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,w(i)>w(i+1)\};

\displaystyle\exc(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,w(i)>i\}.

In
[
5
, Corollary 5.5]
, the author obtained an interpretation of the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}(z)
in terms of the excedance statistic on the
cuspidal
elements of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

(12)

P_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}(z)=\sum_{w\in{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}z^{\exc(w)}.

The cuspidal elements of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are precisely the
long cycles
; that is, the permutations whose cycle decomposition consists of exactly one cycle of order
n
.

It is the reflection arrangement corresponding to the
hyperoctahedral group

\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, the Coxeter group of type
{\rm B}_{n}
.

\mathfrak{B}_{n}
is the group of permutations
w
of
[\pm n]:=\{-n,\dots,-1,1,\dots,n\}
satisfying
w(-i)=-w(i)
.
Elements of
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
are called
signed permutations
.
The
window notation
of a signed permutation
w\in\mathfrak{B}_{n}
is the word
w_{1}w_{2}\dots w_{n}
, where
w_{i}=w(i)
.
We often write
\overline{j}
instead of
-j
for
j\in[\pm n]
, so
\overline{\overline{j}}=j
.

In a signed permutation, cycles can be of two forms:

\displaystyle((i_{1}\,i_{2}\dots i_{k})):=

\displaystyle(i_{1}\,i_{2}\dots i_{k})(\overline{i_{1}}\,\overline{i_{2}}\dots\overline{i_{k}}),

\displaystyle[i_{1}\,i_{2}\dots i_{k}]:=

\displaystyle(i_{1}\,i_{2}\dots i_{k}\overline{i_{1}}\,\overline{i_{2}}\dots\overline{i_{k}}),

where
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq[\pm n]
is an
involution-exclusive
subset; see for instance
[
28
,
11
]
.
Recall that a subset
S\subseteq[\pm n]
is involution-exclusive if
S\cap\overline{S}=\emptyset
, where
\overline{S}:=\{\overline{j}\,:\,j\in S\}
.
Cycles of the form
((\dots))
are called
paired cycles
, while those of the form
[\dots]
are called
balanced cycles
.
Every element in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
decomposes uniquely as a product of disjoint paired or balanced cycles, up to reordering and equivalence of cycles. For instance, the following are two expressions in cycle notation for the element
w\in\mathfrak{B}_{7}
whose window notation is

\overline{1}\,2\,\overline{4}\,\overline{3}\,\overline{6}\,\overline{7}\,\overline{5}
:

[1]\,((2))\,((3\,\overline{4}))\,[5\,\overline{6}\,7]=[\overline{1}]\,((2))\,((\overline{3}\,4))\,[\overline{6}\,7\,\overline{5}].

We will make use of the following statistics on signed permutations.
For
w\in\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, define

\displaystyle\des_{A}(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,w(i)>w(i+1)\};

\displaystyle\des_{B}(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[0,n-1]\,:\,w(i)>w(i+1)\},

\displaystyle\text{where }w(0):=0;

\displaystyle\exc_{A}(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,w(i)>i\};

\displaystyle\fneg(w)

\displaystyle=\#\{i\in[n]\,:\,w(i)<0\}.

\des_{B}
corresponds to the Coxeter descent statistic, so we will abbreviate it to
\des
when no confusion arises.

Adin and Roichman
[
2
]
pioneered the study of
flag statistics
on the hyperoctahedral group. Soon after, Adin, Brenti, and Roichman
[
1
]
introduced the
flag-descent
statistic
\fdes
, which in some sense
refines

\des
.
Later, Bagno and Garber
[
4
]
introduced the
flag-excedance
statistic
\fexc
(although with a different name and in the more general context of colored permutations).
We review these definitions below:

\displaystyle\fdes(w)

\displaystyle=\des_{A}(w)+\des_{B}(w);

\displaystyle\fexc(w)

\displaystyle=2\exc_{A}(w)+\fneg(w);

\displaystyle\exc_{B}(w)

\displaystyle=\lfloor\dfrac{{\fexc}(w)+1}{2}\rfloor.

Foata and Han
[
22
]
proved that
\fexc
and
\fdes
have the same distribution.
Since
\des(w)=\des_{B}(w)=\lfloor\dfrac{{\fdes}(w)+1}{2}\rfloor
for all signed permutations
w\in\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, the statistic
\exc_{B}
is also Eulerian (i.e. it has the same distribution as
\des
).

Similar to the type A case,
[
5
, Corollary 5.5]
gives an interpretation of the coefficients of
P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)
in terms of the statistic
\exc_{B}
on the cuspidal elements of
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
:

(15)

P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)=\sum_{w\in{\rm cusp}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})}z^{\exc_{B}(w)},

The cuspidal elements of the hyperoctahedral group
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
are those whose cycle decomposition only involves balanced cycles.

6.1.
Relation to the 1/2-Eulerian polynomials

The coefficients appearing in

We prove this observation in

Let

be the set of

-inversion sequences
defined by

I_{n,2}=\{e\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,:\,0\leq e_{i}\leq 2(i-1)\}.

For

\asc(e)=\#\bigg\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,\dfrac{e_{i}}{2(i-1)+1}<\dfrac{e_{i+1}}{2i+1}\bigg\}.

The
1/2
-Eulerian polynomial

is the polynomial that keeps track of the distribution of
\asc

.
Explicitly,

A_{n}^{(2)}(z):=\sum_{e\in I_{n,2}}z^{\asc(e)}.

Savage and Viswanathan

A more general definition of

exists.
We do not explore these polynomials here.

Proposition 6.5
.

For all
n\geq 0
,
P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)=z^{n}A^{(2)}_{n}(\tfrac{1}{z})
.

Proof.

In view of Savage and Viswanathan’s result, the generating function of the polynomials
z^{n}A^{(2)}_{n}(\tfrac{1}{z})
is

A(\tfrac{1}{z},2xz)^{1/2}=\Big(\dfrac{\tfrac{1}{z}-1}{\tfrac{1}{z}-e^{2zx(\tfrac{1}{z}-1)}}\Big)^{1/2}=\Big(\dfrac{(1-z)e^{2x(z-1)}}{e^{2x(z-1)}-z}\Big)^{1/2}=e^{x(z-1)}A(z,2x)^{1/2}.

This is precisely the generating function of the polynomials
P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)
in
Theorem

4.2
.

6.2.
Some recurrences

Remarkably,
Proposition

2.7
gives a new recurrence for the polynomials
P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)
that does not follow from those known for
A_{n}^{(2)}(z)
.

Theorem 6.9
.

The type B Primitive Eulerian polynomials satisfy the following recursion.
With
P_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}(z)=1
,

P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)=zP_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1}}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}2^{k}P_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1-k}}(z)P_{\mathcal{A}_{k+1}}(z),

for all
n\geq 1
.

This is analogous to the following quadratic recurrence for the type B Eulerian polynomials

E_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)=(1+z)E_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1}}(z)+2z\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}2^{k}E_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1-k}}(z)E_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}(z),

with
E_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}(z)=1
. See for example
[
38
, Theorem 13.2]
.

In order to prove
Theorem

6.9
, we need to review the correspondence between flats of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and type B partitions of
[\pm n]
.

A
type B set partition
of
[\pm n]
is a (weak) set partition
\mathrm{X}=\{S_{0},S_{1},\overline{S_{1}},\dots,S_{k},\overline{S_{k}}\}
of
[\pm n]
such that
S_{0}=\overline{S_{0}}
is the only block allowed to be empty.
The block
S_{0}
is called the
zero block
of
\mathrm{X}
.
Since blocks are pairwise disjoint, the nonzero blocks are involution-exclusive.
We write
\mathrm{X}\vdash^{B}[\pm n]
to denote that
\mathrm{X}
is a type B set partition of
[\pm n]
.
Given a partition
\mathrm{X}\vdash^{B}[\pm n]
, the corresponding flat of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is the intersection of the hyperplanes
x_{i}=x_{j}
for all
i,j
that belong to the same block of
\mathrm{X}
(recall that
x_{-i}=-x_{i}
for
i\in[n]
).
Observe that
\dim(\mathrm{X})
is half the number of nonzero blocks of
\mathrm{X}
as a type B partition.
The partial order relation of
\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{B}_{n}]
becomes the ordering by refinement of set partitions.
If
\mathrm{X}
corresponds to the partition
\{S_{0},S_{1},\overline{S_{1}},\dots,S_{k},\overline{S_{k}}\}
, then

(\mathcal{B}_{n})^{\mathrm{X}}\cong\mathcal{B}_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\mathcal{B}_{n})_{\mathrm{X}}\cong\mathcal{B}_{|S_{0}|/2}\times\mathcal{A}_{|S_{1}|}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}_{|S_{k}|}.

Proof of
Theorem

6.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\mathrm{H}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
be the hyperplane
x_{n}=0
.
Then,
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\mathrm{H}}\cong\mathcal{B}_{n-1}
.
The lines not in
\mathrm{H}
correspond to partitions
\{S_{0},S_{1},\overline{S_{1}}\}
with
n\notin S_{0}
.
If the line
\mathrm{L}
corresponds to such partition, then

(\mathcal{B}_{n})_{\mathrm{L}}\cong\mathcal{B}_{|S_{0}|/2}\times\mathcal{A}_{|S_{1}|}.

It follows from
Equations

5
and
2.7
that

P_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(z)=(z-1)P_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1}}(z)+\sum P_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1-k}}(z)P_{\mathcal{A}_{k+1}}(z),

where the sum is over partitions
\{S_{0},S_{1},\overline{S_{1}}\}
with
n\in S_{1}
and
|S_{1}|=k+1
.
Such a partition is completely determined by the set
S_{1}\setminus\{n\}
, which can be any involution-exclusive
k
subset of
[\pm(n-1)]
.
The result follows since there are exactly
\binom{n-1}{k}2^{k}
such subsets, and

\binom{n-1}{0}2^{0}P_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1-0}}(z)P_{\mathcal{A}_{0+1}}(z)=P_{\mathcal{B}_{n-1}}(z).\qed

In this section, we employ the theory of interlacing polynomials to prove that the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of any arrangement of rank at most

Let

\alpha_{1}\leq\beta_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\dots\leq\beta_{d-1}\leq\alpha_{d},

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d-1}
are the roots of
f
and
g
, respectively. In this case, the polynomial
f(z)+g(z)
is real-rooted. See for example
[
12
, Theorem 8]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of rank

.
Then,

is real-rooted.

Proof.

The cases

follow from the explicit computations in

2.2
and
2.4

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an essential arrangement of
n

\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Fix a hyperplane
\mathrm{H}_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
, and let
k
(resp.
m
) be the number of lines of
\mathcal{A}
contained in
\mathrm{H}_{0}
(resp. not contained in
\mathrm{H}_{0}
).

Proposition

2.7
reads

P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=(z-1)P_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{H}_{0}}}(z)+\sum_{\mathrm{L}}P_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{L}}}(z).

We will show that

interlaces

First,

z={\color[rgb]{0,0.4844,0.9883}{-\big(\tfrac{\sum_{\mathrm{L}}n_{\mathrm{L}}}{m}-2\big)}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4844,0.9883}{0}}.

Claim:
For any
\mathrm{L}
not contained in
\mathrm{H}_{0}
,
n_{\mathrm{L}}\leq k
.

To prove the claim, note that for all pair of distinct hyperplanes
\mathrm{H},\mathrm{H}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathrm{L}
, the lines
\mathrm{H}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\mathrm{H}^{\prime}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}
are distinct. Otherwise, a dimension argument shows that

\mathrm{L}=\mathrm{H}\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}\cap\mathrm{H}^{\prime}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathrm{H}_{0}.

Since
\mathrm{H}_{0}
contains
k
lines, no more than
k
distinct hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
can contain
\mathrm{L}
.

Therefore we have
2\leq n_{\mathrm{L}}\leq k
for all
{\mathrm{L}}
, and
0\leq\tfrac{\sum_{\mathrm{L}}n_{\mathrm{L}}}{m}-2\leq k-2
.
It follows that
\sum_{\mathrm{L}}P_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{L}}}(z)
interlaces
(z-1)P_{\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{H}_{0}}}(z)
:

{\color[rgb]{0.8242,0,0}{-(k-2)}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0.4844,0.9883}{-\big(\tfrac{\sum_{\mathrm{L}}n_{\mathrm{L}}}{m}-2\big)}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0.8242,0,0}{0}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0.4844,0.9883}{0}}\leq{\color[rgb]{0.8242,0,0}{1}},

as we wanted to show.
∎

Remark 9.2
.

Note that this result does not assume the arrangement to be simplicial.
For instance, the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of the graphic arrangement of a
4
-cycle in
Example

2.6
is real-rooted:

z^{3}+3z^{2}-z=z(z-\tfrac{-3+\sqrt{13}}{2})(z-\tfrac{-3-\sqrt{13}}{2}).

Remark 9.3
.

It immediately follows from
Theorem

9.1
that the cocharacteristic polynomial of any arrangement of rank at most
3
is real-rooted. The same is not true for the characteristic polynomial.
For instance, the characteristic polynomial of the graphic arrangement of a
4
-cycle is
z(z-1)(z^{2}-3z+3)
, which has a pair of conjugate complex roots.

9.2.
Real-rootedness fails non-simplicial arrangements in higher rank

If
\mathcal{A}
is a non-simplicial arrangement of rank at least
4
,
P_{\mathcal{A}}(z)
might fail to be real-rooted.

Example 9.4
.

For
n\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the arrangement of
n+1
generic hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It makes sense to say
the
arrangement, because any two such arrangements are combinatorially isomorphic; this is not true if we have more hyperplanes.
The arrangement
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{I}_{2}(3)
, and
\mathcal{G}_{3}
is isomorphic to the graphic arrangement of a
4
-cycle.

We verify using
Proposition

2.7
and induction that

P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(z)=z^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k+1}\binom{n}{k+1}z^{n-k}=z(z-1)^{n}+(n+1)z^{n}-z^{n+1}.

The second expression makes the inductive step cleaner, even though it involves terms of higher degree.
For
n=2
, we have

z(z-1)^{2}+3z^{2}-z^{3}=z^{2}+z=P_{\mathcal{I}_{2}(3)}(z).

Now assume
n\geq 3
and let
\mathrm{H}
be any hyperplane in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
The arrangement
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{H}}
consists of
n
hyperplanes in generic position, so
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{\mathrm{H}}\cong\mathcal{G}_{n-1}
.
Moreover, any line in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is contained in exactly
n-1
hyperplanes; thus there are
\binom{(n+1)-1}{n-1}=n
lines not contained in
\mathrm{H}
, and
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{\mathrm{L}}
is isomorphic to a coordinate arrangement of
n-1
hyperplanes for each line
\mathrm{L}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=(z-1)P_{\mathcal{G}_{n-1}}(z)+nz^{n-1}

\displaystyle=(z-1)(z(z-1)^{n-1}+nz^{n-1}-z^{n})+nz^{n-1}

\displaystyle=z(z-1)^{n}+(n+1)z^{n}-z^{n+1},

as claimed.
Already for
n=4
, we have

P_{\mathcal{G}_{4}}(z)=z^{4}+6z^{3}-4z^{2}+z,

which has two complex roots.

9.3.
Real-rootedness for Coxeter and simplicial arrangements

Frobenius
[
23
]
first proved that the classical (type A) Eulerian polynomials are real-rooted.
In view of identity
Equation

14
, this implies that the type A Primitive Eulerian polynomials are real-rooted.
Later, Savage and Visontai
[
43
]
proved that the polynomials
A^{(2)}_{n}(z)
have only real roots.
It then follows by
Proposition

6.5
that so do the type B Primitive Eulerian polynomials.

With the help of SageMath
[
41
]
, we verified that Primitive Eulerian polynomials of the exceptional type in
Table

1
are all real-rooted.
We have also verified that the type D Primitive Eulerian polynomial
P_{\mathcal{D}_{n}}(z)
is real-rooted for
n\leq 300
.
We conjecture this is true for all values of
n
,
and therefore that the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of any Coxeter arrangement is real-rooted.

Conjecture 9.5
.

The Primitive Eulerian polynomial of any real reflection arrangement is real-rooted.

The corresponding conjecture for the Eulerian polynomial was originally posed by Brenti
[
13
]
and solved two decades later by Savage and Visontai
[
43
]
.

The arrangements
\mathcal{D}_{n,k}
are all simplicial, and not combinatorially isomorphic to reflection arrangements whenever
k\neq 0,n
.
We have computationally verified that
P_{\mathcal{D}_{n,k}}(z)
is real-rooted for all
k\leq n\leq 150
.
We have also verified that the Primitive Eulerian polynomial of the crystallographic simplicial arrangements of Cuntz and Heckenberger
[
16
]
, and the two additional examples in rank
4
by Geis
[
25
]
, are all real-rooted.
We conclude this section with the following conjecture, which would of course imply
9.5
.

Conjecture 9.6
.

The Primitive Eulerian polynomial of any simplicial arrangement is real-rooted.
