Learning to Open Doors with an Aerial Manipulator
Eugenio Cuniato, Ismail Geles, Weixuan Zhang, Olov Andersson, Marco Tognon, Roland Siegwart

Abstract
The field of aerial manipulation has seen rapid advances, transitioning from push-and-slide tasks to interaction with articulated objects. The motion trajectory of these complex actions is usually hand-crafted or a result of online optimization methods like Model Predictive Control or Model Predictive Path Integral control. However, these methods rely on heuristics or model simplifications to efficiently run on onboard hardware, limiting their robustness, and making them sensitive to disturbances and differences between the real environment and its model. In this work, we propose a Reinforcement Learning approach to learn reactive motion behaviors for a manipulation task while producing policies that are robust to disturbances and modeling errors. Specifically, we train a policy to perform a door-opening task with an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle. The policy is trained in a physics simulator and shown in the real world, where it is able to generalize also to door closing tasks never seen in training. We also compare our method against a state-of-the-art Model Predictive Path Integral solution in simulation, showing a considerable increase in robustness and speed.

Introduction

Aerial robotics is a growing area of research developing innovative systems for a wide range of applications
[

,

]
. The field was particularly boosted by the development of novel Micro Aerial Vehicles

This decoupling enabled many new aerial physical interaction tasks otherwise very difficult for standard multirotor systems and reduced the need for complex end-effectors to compensate for the motion of the body.

Such aerial physical interaction tasks are especially relevant in domains such as industrial inspection and maintenance, where many tasks require complex interaction between the robot and the structure of interest.
While most current investigations are limited to pure touching or push-and-slide operations with static surfaces
[
8
,
9
]
, recent works have tackled the problem of interaction with non-static objects, like pushing a cart with possibly unknown properties
[
10
,
11
,
12

. When more complex actions are required, the motion trajectory is usually hand-crafted and dependent on heuristics. Examples of this include the door opening task performed in
[

or the valve turning in
[
14
]
. One way of generating optimized motion trajectories was presented in
[
15
]
, where Model Predictive Control was employed to open a door with an aerial manipulator. However, these methods require an analytical description of the environment, the robot, and most importantly the contact dynamics. The latter are often nonlinear and can easily increase the optimization problem’s complexity if accurate simplifications are not found.

The aerial robot opening a cabinet door during real-world experiments. The robot learns to both open and close the door in real life, even though it was only trained on opening in simulation.

To overcome these complexities, sampling-based approaches like Model Predictive Path Integral
[
16
]
have been receiving attention since they solve optimization problems by recreating the desired task in a dynamic simulator rather than by constructing a full analytical model. This technique was successfully employed in robotic manipulation tasks
[
17
]
, drone racing
[
18
]
, autonomous driving
[
16
]
, and more recently in an autonomous aerial door-opening and valve-turning tasks
[
19
]
. However, while this approach can handle complex contact dynamics while reducing the engineering effort, the sampling can be computationally very expensive since it requires running multiple dynamic simulations online in parallel. This reduces the number of samples, practically reducing the chances of generating feasible and goal-accomplishing trajectories. Moreover, this approach is very sensitive to uncertainties and differences between the simulated and the real world, which limits its applicability in uncertain environments.

In more traditional, non-aerial manipulation literature, Reinforcement Learning has received considerable attention for a variety of complex tasks.Among the various examples, in
[
20
]
a robotic arm learned different policies to reach a target, relocate objects, and open doors, and in
[
21
]
a 27 degree of freedom robotic hand learned complex manipulation skills like hammering a nail or turning and pulling a door’s handle. While Reinforcement Learning has many similarities with Model Predictive Path Integral,
it provides two main advantages:
(i)
the learning process can be performed offline and offboard on more powerful hardware, generating a policy that is fast to infer online and
(ii)
the uncertainties in the robot’s or environment’s model, as well as external disturbances, can be embedded into the training process through
domain randomization
, leading to robust policies. This was also demonstrated in the aerial community for high-speed and acrobatic flying with quadrotors
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. However, despite its potential, this approach is so far rarely adopted in the aerial manipulation field. A few examples include aerial transportation of suspended loads
[
25
]
and, very recently, push-and-slide tasks
[
26
]
, but aerial manipulation of articulated objects has not yet been explored using Reinforcement Learning.

I-A

Methodology and Contributions

In this work, we explore the novel use of Reinforcement Learning for aerial physical interaction with articulated objects, focusing on a door opening task with an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle (depicted in
Fig.

1
). This is a particularly challenging example for two reasons: the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle features complex and slow tilt-arm dynamics which are difficult to simulate (complicating sim-to-real transfer), and the task itself requires centimeter-precise execution in the presence of aerodynamic disturbances from flying close to objects.

We believe that this makes the door opening task a great example to investigate the strengths and weaknesses of Reinforcement Learning-based approaches for complex aerial manipulation tasks.

We train a reactive motion control policy in simulation, which provides pose references to a lower-level pose controller, transfer it to the real world, and compare it to a state-of-the-art model-based control method for the same task.

Specifically, we offer the following contributions:

•

We explore the use of Reinforcement Learning for a complex aerial manipulation task, specifically door-opening.

•

We show that the learned policy outperforms a state-of-the-art Model Predictive Path Integral controller in terms of robustness to errors environment observations and changes in initial conditions as well as task completion speed.

•

We perform sim-to-real transfer on the policy and analyze its behavior on the real robot, including desirable emergent properties like ability to recover from initial failed attempts and generalization to new variants of the task, such as door closing.

II
Model and Control

Our goal is to learn a reactive motion policy for door opening with an Aerial Manipulator composed of an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle and a rigid stick with a hook.
The policy outputs a desired body pose to the pose controller of the robot. In this section we describe the physical model of the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle and the door.

II-A

Aerial robot

Modeling

The aerial robot is an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle composed of a rigid body with six independently tiltable rotor arms and twelve propellers.
Controlling both arm angles and propeller speed, the platform is fully actuated in any orientation, i.e., it can always exert a full 6-degree of freedom wrench.

We describe the position and orientation of the robot’s body frame
\mathcal{F}_{B}
with respect to the world frame
\mathcal{F}_{W}
by the vector
\prescript{}{W}{\bm{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the rotation matrix
\bm{R}_{WB}\in\mathsf{SO}(3)
, respectively.
Then, as already in
[
7
,
27
]
, the robot’s Lagrangian model can be expressed as

\bm{M}\prescript{}{B}{\dot{\bm{v}}}+\bm{c}(\prescript{}{B}{\bm{\omega}})+\bm{g}(\bm{R}_{WB})=\bm{\tau}_{p},

(1)

with inertia matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}
, control wrench
\bm{\tau}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, gravity wrench
\bm{g}(\bm{R}_{WB})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, centrifugal and Coriolis wrench
\bm{c}(\prescript{}{B}{\bm{\omega}})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
and
\prescript{}{B}{\bm{v}}=\left[\prescript{}{B}{\dot{\bm{p}}}^{\top}\;\,\prescript{}{B}{\bm{\omega}}^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
system’s twist all expressed in the body frame.
The control wrench
\bm{\tau}_{c}
is mapped into the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle’s arm tilt angles and rotor speeds as in
[
7
]
. To enable the system to interact with the environment, it is equipped with a rigid stick ending with an hook.
We consider a hook frame
\mathcal{F}_{H}
attached in the geometric middle of the hook cylinder (
Fig.

3
) with the same orientation of the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle’s body
\bm{R}_{WB}
.

Pose controller

We use a pose controller to steer the vehicle’s pose toward a reference position
\prescript{}{W}{\bm{p}}^{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and orientation
\bm{R}_{WB}^{\text{ref}}{}\in\mathsf{SO}(3)
, using the control wrench
\bm{\tau}_{c}
. For more details about the controller, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
.

II-B

Door model

The door is composed of two rigid bodies attached through a 1-degree of freedom rotational joint at the door’s hinge. The door’s handle is rigidly attached on its right side. The robot can fit its hook inside the handle to swing the door open, as shown in
Figure

1
. We define the pose of the door’s handle through the frame
\mathcal{F}_{D}
located in the center of the handle’s rectangle (
Fig.

3
). We also denote its position and orientation in
\mathcal{F}_{W}
by the vector
\prescript{}{W}{\bm{p}}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the rotation matrix
\bm{R}_{WD}\in\mathsf{SO}(3)
, respectively. Finally, we denote the angle of the door w.r.t. its fixed base with
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]$\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}$
, where
\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}
indicates open and
\alpha=0
indicates closed.

III
Policy Description and Training

Fig. 2
:
The robot control scheme composed of the learned door opening policy and the model-based pose controller. The policy knows the door’s opening angle, the relative pose w.r.t. the door and the current robot’s velocity. It then outputs a desired pose for the robot to follow in order to open the door.

Consider an Reinforcement Learning scenario in which a policy receives an observation vector
\bm{o}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{o}}
at each time step
k
, producing an action vector
\bm{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{a}}
. The action affects the simulated environment and gets evaluated with a reward function
r_{k}\left(\bm{o}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{R}
. The agent’s goal is to maximize the discounted sum of rewards
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r_{k}
, with discount factor
\gamma<1
affecting the effective time horizon over which the agent plans. An overview of our policy’s observations and actions is in
Fig.

2
.

III-A

Observation space

The policy’s observations are given by a 19-dimensional vector containing information about both the robot and door state, composed of the following:

III-A
1
Robot velocity

The linear
\prescript{}{B}{\bm{v}}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and angular
\prescript{}{B}{\bm{\omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
velocities of the robot’s body in body frame.

III-A
2
Hook-to-door distance

The distance between the hook frame
\mathcal{F}_{H}
and the door handle frame
\mathcal{F}_{D}
, where the hook should fit to pull the door, defined as
\prescript{}{B}{\bm{p}}_{hd}=\prescript{}{B}{\bm{p}}_{h}-\prescript{}{B}{\bm{p}}_{d}
.

III-A
3
Body-to-door orientation

The flattened rotation matrix
\bm{R}_{BD}
representing the orientation between the body frame
\mathcal{F}_{B}
and the door frame
\mathcal{F}_{D}
.

III-A
4
Door angle

The door’s hinge angle
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
.

III-B

Action space

The policy outputs the desired pose corrections into the door frame
\mathcal{F}_{D}
for the robot, encoded in the action vector
\bm{a}=\left[\prescript{}{D}{\bm{p}}^{\text{ref}}\;\bm{\lambda}_{0}\;\bm{\lambda}_{1}\right]
. The desired position command for the inner pose controller is then obtained as
\prescript{}{W}{\bm{p}}^{\text{ref}}=\prescript{}{W}{\bm{p}}+\bm{R}_{WD}\prescript{}{D}{\bm{p}}^{\text{ref}}
. The two vectors
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
,
\bm{\lambda}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represent two axes of the desired commanded rotation frame.
This allows us to use a continuous representation of the orientation space while using only two 3-dimensional vectors instead of a full 9-dimensional rotation matrix. We then compute the correction rotation matrix
\bm{R}^{\text{ref}}=\left[\vec{e}_{0}\;\;\vec{e}_{1}\;\;\vec{e}_{0}\times\vec{e}_{1}\right]
, applying the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization method to the vectors
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
,
\bm{\lambda}_{1}
as

\vec{e}_{0}=\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{0}}{\lVert\bm{\lambda}_{0}\rVert},\;\;\vec{e}_{1}=\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{1}-\left(\vec{e}_{0}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{1}\right)\vec{e}_{0}}{\lVert\bm{\lambda}_{1}-\left(\vec{e}_{0}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{1}\right)\vec{e}_{0}\rVert},

(2)

where
\vec{e}_{0}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
is the dot product between the two vectors. The desired attitude reference is then computed as
\bm{R}_{WB}^{\text{ref}}=\bm{R}_{WD}\bm{R}^{\text{ref}}
. This way, the policy is commanding pose displacements w.r.t. the current pose - and, implicitly, velocities - to the Aerial Manipulator’s pose controller. The bigger the produced offset, the faster the motion of the Aerial Manipulator in the desired direction.

Hook in door’s handle (
r_{\text{h}}

This reward pushes the hook towards the door handle, defined as

\mathcal{F}_{H}

and the door

\theta_{hd}>0

We assume the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle to be a 6-degree of freedom rigid floating body on which we can directly apply a command wrench

\bm{\tau}_{p}
.

IV
Experimental validation

Here we present two different evaluations of our approach. First, we transfer the policy to the real robot, discussing the difficulties and the lessons we learned in sim-to-real transfer.
Second, we compare the Model Predictive Path Integral and Reinforcement Learning approaches extensively in simulation, analyzing their robustness to perception mismatches
and differences in the task initial conditions (closer or farther from the handle).

IV-A

Real-world experiments

Our experimental set-up consists of an Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle and a cabinet in a motion capture system, where the state of both systems (6 degree of freedom pose of the robot and
\alpha
opening angle of the door and its handle’s position) are given by motion capture at
200\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
, as shown in
Fig.

1
.
The motion capture pose estimate is fused with an on-board IMU for pose control of the robot, with our learned policy providing the commanded pose reference at
200\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
.

We began our experiments with a zero-shot transferred policy (applying the same policy as was trained in simulation), and discuss its limitations on the real system.
Second, we attempt to address the sim-to-real gap by training an improved policy.
The results of both sets of experiments are shown in
Fig.

4
.

IV-A
1
Zero-shot policy transfer

The robot starts in free-flight at around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
from the door handle. It takes around
4\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
to firmly grasp the handle and another
2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
to open the door up to
\alpha=$0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}$
.
We notice that the policy can open the door already with a zero-shot transferred policy in only
6\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
. However, there are two main points to improve: first, the door is not opened all the way to the
0\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}
target; second, there are visible oscillations in the motion of the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle, that become evident once the door is open and reflect into the door’s angle. The oscillations appear especially in the
\bm{x}
,
\bm{y}
linear and
\bm{z}
angular body directions. Interestingly, these are the directions of the wrench that mostly require a rotation of the tilting arms of the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle, which have notably slower dynamics than the propeller motors in the real world.
These differences were not reflected in our simulation, and our hypothesis is this mismatch causes the oscillations and degraded performance.

IV-A
2
Retrained policy

To avoid a big excitation of the tilt-arm actuators, we limit the maximum pose displacement that the policy actions can generate by enforcing a maximum norm on the translation offset and a maximum angle offset from the robot’s current pose, effectively saturating the policy output.
The new results are visible in the right column of
Fig.

4
. The policy is now slower than the previous one due to the new pose command saturations, but it reduces the velocity oscillations, smoothly steering the robot and fully opening the door. The policy still induces oscillatory wrench commands but with a smaller amplitude and, interestingly, a higher frequency than before. These get partially filtered by the real actuator dynamics, leading to a generally smoother behavior of the system.
This confirms our hypothesis that the sim-to-real mismatch was largely caused by the lack of actuator dynamics in the simulation.

Second, the same policy is also able to close the door even if it never experienced this scenario in simulation. By changing the door angle observation from

IV-B

Robustness and comparison to related work

To further evaluate the effect of domain randomization on the robustness of the resulting policy, and to compare to a non-Reinforcement Learning-based approach, we conduct a series of simulation experiments.

Here we compare our learned policy with the recent Model Predictive Path Integral-based door opening method from
[
19
]
and evaluate the robustness of both approaches to errors in the model of the environment and changes in initial conditions.
The robot’s only knowledge of the state of the world consists of the position of the door handle
\prescript{}{B}{\bm{p}}_{hd}
and the door plane
\mathcal{F}_{D}
, both relative to the Omnidirectional Micro Aerial Vehicle, and the door opening angle
\alpha
.

The reason is that Model Predictive Path Integral would often spend most of the time trying to fit the hook into the door’s handle, while the policy would generate more effective hooking trajectories.

V
Conclusions

This work presented an Reinforcement Learning approach to the problem of articulated object manipulation with fully actuated aerial robots. We were able to solve a complex door opening task without the need for an accurate analytical model or computationally heavy online optimizations.
Our policy was able to transfer well from simulation to real world with only minimal modifications, and succeeded not only in opening the door, but also in closing it, which is a task it never witnessed during training.
This suggests that these approaches can be used to generalize to wider varieties of tasks.
We compared our approach to a recent Model Predictive Path Integral solution for the door-opening task, showing an increase in robustness and performance with respect to the state of the art. In particular, the policy succeeded at levels of degraded observations in which the model-based solution would completely fail. Future works will address the problem of simulation to reality transfer of more dynamic motions, giving the policy more freedom in exploring door-opening strategies.
We believe that Reinforcement Learning is capable of finding robust, computationally-efficient, and generalizable solutions to complex aerial manipulation tasks.
