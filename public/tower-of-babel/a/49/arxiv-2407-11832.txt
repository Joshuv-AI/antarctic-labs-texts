1.4
Justification for the Use of the
\gamma
-Approximation Definition

In our approach, we define a
\gamma
-approximation of the number of relevant variables
d(f)
in a parity function
f
such that
\gamma^{-1}(d(f))\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
, instead of using the standard definition
d(f)\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
.

The key reason for this choice is that the latter definition loses its effectiveness when
d(f)
approaches
n
, the number of variables. Specifically, if
d(f)
is close to
n

O(n)
, the condition
d(f)\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
, for
\gamma(n)=\omega(n)
, effectively reduces to
d(f)\leq D\leq n
. In this scenario, the value of
\gamma
becomes less significant because the approximation
D
would naturally fall within the trivial range of
d(f)=O(n)
to
n
.

On the other hand, our chosen definition
\gamma^{-1}(d(f))\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
ensures that the approximation
D
always depends on the function
\gamma
. This definition retains its utility even when
d(f)
is large, as
\gamma^{-1}(d(f))
provides a lower bound that is influenced by
\gamma
, thereby maintaining the approximation’s relevance and precision.

Thus, by using the
\gamma
-approximation definition
\gamma^{-1}(d(f))\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
, we ensure a meaningful and consistent approximation of the number of relevant variables
d(f)
across the entire range of possible values, preserving the value and impact of the function
\gamma
.

t=O((1/(1-\eta_{b})^{k})\log(1/\delta))
iterations, with probability at least
1-\delta/3
, at least one of the outputs is the target. By Lemma
1
, learning the target from a set of
t
linear functions with a success probability of
1-\delta/3
requires
\tilde{O}((1/(1-\eta_{b}-1/q)^{2})(k+\log(1/\delta))
labeled examples.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof.

Let
{\cal A}^{\prime}
be an algorithm that runs in polynomial time, uses labeled examples according to the uniform distribution in the presence of random classification noise, and outputs
\gamma^{-1}(d(f))\leq D\leq\gamma(d(f))
.
Modify the algorithm to output
\min(\gamma(D),n)
. The algorithm now is a
\Delta
-approximation algorithm, where
\Delta(x)=\min(\gamma(\gamma(x)),n)
.
Let
m(n)=\gamma^{-1}(\gamma^{-1}(\pi(n)))
.
Since
\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is strictly increasing and is defined for all
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, we have
\gamma^{-1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, is also strictly increasing, and
m(n)=\omega_{n}(1)
.
Let
\delta^{\prime}=1/(|\mathbb{F}|n^{7})
and
\delta=1/n
. By Lemma
6
, item
1
, there exists
m(n)\leq k(n)\leq\Delta(m(n))=\pi(n)
, and an algorithm that, for every
f\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},k(n))
, with probability at least
1-o_{n}(1)>2/3
, identifies the relevant variables of
f
from random uniform labeled examples in the presence of random classification noise.
Also, this algorithm runs in polynomial time. Since
k(n)\leq\pi(n)
, by Lemma
7
and item 2 in Lemma
6
, there is a
poly(n,1/(1-\eta_{b}-1/|\mathbb{F}|),\min(|\mathbb{F}|,1/(1-\eta_{b})^{\pi(n)}))
time learning algorithm for
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},k)
.
Since
k(n)\geq m(n)=\omega_{n}(1)
, the result follows.
∎

3.2
Approximation Implies Learning
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F})

In this section, we prove.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be any strictly increasing function, where
\gamma(x)>x
for every
x
. Let
\Gamma(x):=\gamma(\gamma(x))
.
Consider any
T(n)
-time algorithm
{\cal A}^{\prime}(n)
that, for any linear function
f\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F})
, uses
Q(n)
random uniform labeled examples of
f
in the presence of random classification noise and, with probability at least
2/3
, returns a
\gamma
-approximation of the number of relevant variables
d(f)
of
f
.
From
{\cal A}^{\prime}(n)
, one can, in polynomial time, construct a
\tilde{O}(|\mathbb{F}|\Gamma(n)^{12})T(O(\Gamma(n)^{2}))\log(1/\delta)
-time algorithm that properly learns
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F})
from
\tilde{O}(\Gamma(n)^{8})Q(O(\Gamma(n)^{2}))\log(|\mathbb{F}|/\delta)
random uniform labeled examples in the presence of random classification noise.

We first show that for every
d
satisfying
12\Gamma(d)^{2}\leq n
, there exists a learning algorithm for
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that for every
1\leq m\leq m^{\prime}:=\Gamma^{-1}(n)
, there exists a
k
such that
m\leq k\leq\Gamma(m)
, and an algorithm that runs in time
T(n)
and, with probability at least
2/3
, properly learns
f\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},k)
under the uniform distribution in the presence of random classification noise, and uses
Q(n)
labeled examples.
Then, for every
d
such that
12\Gamma(d)^{2}\leq n
, there is an algorithm that runs in time
O(T(n)\log(1/\delta))
and properly learns
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
under the uniform distribution in the presence of random classification noise, using
O(Q(n)\allowbreak\log(1/\delta))
labeled examples.

Proof.

Let
d
be an integer such that
12\Gamma(d)^{2}<n
.
Since
\Gamma(d)<n
, we have
d\leq m^{\prime}
. Consequently, there exists
k
such that
d\leq k\leq\Gamma(d)\leq\Gamma(m^{\prime})=n
, along with a proper learning algorithm
{\cal B}(n,k)
for
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},k)
, that runs in time
T(n)
and uses
Q(n)
labeled examples.

We now present an algorithm for learning
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
.
We uniformly at random draw
k-d
variables
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k-d}}
and run the algorithm
{\cal B}(n,k)
.
For each labeled example
(a,b)
of
f
, we feed
{\cal B}
with the labeled example
(a,b+a_{i_{1}}+\cdots+a_{i_{k-d}})
.
This modified labeled example serves as a labeled example for the function
g=f+x_{i_{1}}+\cdots+x_{i_{k-d}}
.
The probability that
g\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},k)
is the probability that none of the variables
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k-d}}
are relevant in
f
. This probability is given by

\prod_{i=d}^{k}\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)\geq 1-\frac{k^{2}}{n}\geq 1-\frac{\Gamma(d)^{2}}{n}\geq\frac{11}{12}.

Therefore, with probability at least
1-(1/3+1/12)>1/2
, algorithm
{\cal B}(n,k)
learns
g
and thus learns
f
.
By Lemma
3
, the result follows.
∎

We now show how to construct a learning algorithm for
{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
for every
d\leq n
.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that for every
n
and every
d
that satisfies
12\Gamma(d)^{2}\leq n
, there is an algorithm
{\cal A}(n)
that runs in time
T(n)
and, with probability at least
2/3
, properly learns
f\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
under the uniform distribution in the presence of random classification noise, using
Q(n)
labeled examples.
Let
N(n)=12\Gamma(n)^{2}
.
Then, for every
n
and every
d\leq n
, there is an algorithm that runs in time
T(N(n))
and, with probability at least
2/3
, properly learns
f\in{\rm Lin}(\mathbb{F},d)
under the uniform distribution in the presence of random classification noise, using
Q(N(n))
labeled examples.

Proof.

Let
N=N(n)
. Then for every
d\leq n
, we have
12\Gamma(d)^{2}\leq 12\Gamma(n)^{2}=N(n)
. We run
{\cal A}(N)
. Whenever the algorithm requests a labeled example, we draw a labeled example
(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}
, append
N-n
random uniform entries to
a
, creating
a^{\prime}
. We then provide
(a^{\prime},b)
to
{\cal A}(N)
. The algorithm is effective for any
d
that satisfies
12\Gamma(d)^{2}\leq N=12\Gamma(n)^{2}
and, thereby, covers all
d\leq n
.
∎

Theorem
4
now follows from Lemma
6
,
8
and
9
.
