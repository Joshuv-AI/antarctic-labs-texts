Breaking the intensity-bias degeneracy in line intensity mapping
Patrick C. Breysse

Abstract
Line intensity mapping is rapidly gaining prominence as a tool to understand galaxy evolution and cosmology at high redshift. However, the standard power spectrum analysis for these surveys suffers from a tight degeneracy between the overall mean line intensity and the bias of the emitting galaxies. We outline a new formalism for the Voxel Intensity Distribution (VID), a one-point statistic which is known to contain information beyond the power spectrum. We use this new calculation to show for the first time that the VID can be used to break this key degeneracy in intensity mapping data.

As with most cosmological observables, intensity maps are most often analyzed in terms of their power spectra, which give the total power present in different Fourier modes of the line fluctuations. The power spectrum
P(k)
at wavenumber
k
of most lines is typically expressed as

P(k)=\overline{T}^{2}\overline{b}^{2}P_{m}(k)+P_{\rm{shot}}.

(1)

P_{m}(k)
is the power spectrum of the underlying dark matter,
\overline{T}
is the mean intensity of the target line,
\overline{b}
is the luminosity-weighted bias of the emitting galaxies, and
P_{\rm{shot}}
is the scale-independent shot noise caused by the discreteness of the emitting sources
Lidz et al. 2011
;
Breysse and Alexandroff 2019
. The form of Eq. (
1
) demonstrates a key limitation of LIM power spectra: that the mean line intensity is degenerate with the clustering bias.
\overline{T}
is a highly desirable quantity to measure, as it maps to the total neutral gas content of the Universe for 21 cm maps, the total molecular gas content for CO, and so on. It is almost always impossible to measure
\overline{T}
by directly averaging a map, as large-scale information is lost during data reduction and foreground cleaning
Liu and Shaw 2020
;
Foss et al. 2021
, and any measurement of
\overline{T}
from
P(k)
is dependent on the modeling of the line bias
Beane and Lidz 2018
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Wolz et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2022
;
Anderson et al. 2022
. Eq. (
1
) only technically gives the monopole of the LIM power spectrum. Higher-order multipoles do include some information which weakens this degeneracy, but in practice,
\overline{T}
and
\overline{b}
remain quite difficult to disentangle even with anisotropies
Breysse et al. 2022b
.

The fluctuations seen in an intensity map are the result of highly nonlinear processes within individual galaxies, and thus are quite non-Gaussian on small scales. The power spectrum therefore does not fully describe the intensity statistics, and we can seek other avenues to access the missing information. Rather than cumbersome higher-order estimators, we will look at the one-point statistics of an intensity field through a quantity termed the Voxel Intensity Distribution (VID). Based on well-established probability-of-deflection or
P(D)
techniques
Scheuer 1957
;
Barcons 1992
;
Windridge and Phillipps 2000
;
Lee et al. 2009
;
Patanchon et al. 2009

Breysse et al. 2016
;
Breysse et al. 2017
;
Ihle et al. 2019
;

;
Libanore et al. 2022

However, expressions of the intensity mapping VID used to date do not explicitly include the bias, and thus cannot self-consistently model the luminosity-clustering dichotomy at the heart of this problem. Here we present a novel theoretical calculation of the VID which accounts for the missing information. We then use a simple example model to demonstrate that a one-point analysis can indeed solve this key problem with the power spectrum.

The expressions in Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) make a key assumption that renders it unsuitable for our purposes: that the luminosity of an object is independent of the density of galaxies in its vicinity. In reality, more massive (and therefore brighter) galaxies are more biased and thus more strongly clustered. In other words, the luminosity function in voxels with high
N
will be skewed towards brighter objects. In order to actually probe the relationship between clustering and the VID, we will need to take into account this crucial effect.

Our new VID calculation is based on a similar formalism applied in Refs.
Thiele et al. 2019
;
Thiele et al. 2020
for the cosmic infrared background and weak lensing fields. Since most lines we consider are sourced within the disks of star-forming galaxies, we model an individual line emitter as a point source residing at the center of a dark matter halo. Satellite galaxies, which should generally be subdominant at high redshift, are left for future work. We further assume that halos are linearly biased tracers of the underlying dark matter field. Consider first the contribution to the VID from sources with a halo mass between
M
and
M+dM
, such that a voxel will contain exactly zero or one such objects. The intensity PDF in a mass bin is

\mathcal{P}(T|M)=\mathcal{P}(N=0)\delta_{D}(T)+\mathcal{P}(N=1)\mathcal{P}_{1}(T|M),

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{1}(T|M)
is the PDF for a single source.

For reasons that will become clear, we will work with the intensity characteristic function

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\widetilde{T}|M)=1+\mathcal{P}(N=1)\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\widetilde{T}|M)-1\right),

(5)

which is obtained by Fourier transforming the original PDF, with
\widetilde{T}\equiv 2\pi/T
as the Fourier conjugate of
T
. We have also used
\mathcal{P}(N=0)=1-\mathcal{P}(N=1)
. Assuming Poisson statistics in our narrow mass bins,

\mathcal{P}(N=1)\approx\overline{N}(M)=\frac{dn}{dM}V_{\rm{vox}}\left(1+b(M)\delta_{m}\right)dM,

(6)

where
\overline{N}(M)\ll 1
is the mean number of halos expected in a voxel with comoving volume
V_{\rm{vox}}
,
dn/dM
is the halo mass function,
b(M)
is the halo bias, and
\delta_{m}
is the matter density contrast at the voxel location. Note the explicit dependence on
b(M)
, which was absent from the older VID derivation. We can then write

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\widetilde{T}|M)=\exp\left[p(\widetilde{T},M)\left(1+b(M)\delta_{m}\right)dM\right],

(7)

where for compactness we have defined

p(\widetilde{T},M)\equiv\frac{dn}{dM}V_{\rm{vox}}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\widetilde{T}|M)-1\right),

(8)

and used the Taylor expansion of the exponential. Higher-order terms of order
(dM)^{2}
are neglected.

The observed voxel intensity will be the sum of the contributions from all of the individual mass bins, so the total PDF will be the convolution of those of each mass bin. By the Fourier convolution theorem, this becomes the product of the characteristic functions such that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\widetilde{T})

\displaystyle=\prod_{M}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(\widetilde{T}|M)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\sum_{M}p(\widetilde{T},M)\left(1+b(M)\delta_{m}\right)dM\right]

(9)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left[\int p(\widetilde{T},M)\left(1+b(M)\delta_{m}\right)dM\right],

where we take
dM
to zero to approximate the integral.

The result of Eq. (
9
) can be separated into two components. The first,

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{un}}(\widetilde{T})=\exp\left[\int p(\widetilde{T},M)dM\right],

(10)

gives the distribution in the absence of clustering. The second component encodes the clustering effect

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{cl}}(\widetilde{T})=\exp\left[\delta_{m}\int p(\widetilde{T},M)b(M)dM\right].

(11)

For our final PDF, we need to average
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{cl}}
over all realizations of
\delta_{m}
. To do so, we use the identity that for Gaussian fields
\langle\exp(x)\rangle=\exp(\langle x^{2}\rangle/2)

Thiele et al. 2019
. This leaves

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{cl}}(\widetilde{T})=\exp\left[\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{2}\left(\int p(\widetilde{T},M)b(M)dM\right)^{2}\right],

(12)

where

\sigma_{m}^{2}=\langle\delta_{m}^{2}\rangle=\int P_{m}(\mathbf{k})\widetilde{W}_{\rm{vox}}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}},

(13)

is the variance of the matter contrast on the scale of a voxel with window function
W_{\rm{vox}}
. Finally, we can add in the effect of instrument noise by convolving in the noise PDF
\mathcal{P}_{\rm{Noise}}(T)
. The final observed VID can then be obtained by inverse Fourier transforming

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{obs}}(\widetilde{T})=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{un}}(\widetilde{T})\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{cl}}(\widetilde{T})\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm{Noise}}(\widetilde{T}).

(14)

We demonstrate our formalism with a simple example forecast based on the CO Mapping Array Project (COMAP,
Cleary et al. 2022
). Note that our goal is not to perform a rigorous experimental forecast, but to explore the differences between bias and luminosity in the VID.

Specifically, we imagine an intensity map targeting the 115 GHz CO(1-0) line using the second-generation COMAP-EoR survey with instrument specifications given in Ref.
Breysse et al. 2022b
. COMAP-EoR will map the CO(1-0) line in redshift bands between
z=2.6
and 8.6, along with some CO(2-1) emission that we neglect here. To model the CO luminosity we apply the scaling relation method from Refs.
Li et al. 2016
;
Keating et al. 2020
, referred to in Ref.
Breysse et al. 2022b
as the “Li16/Keating20” model. In this model, we relate the CO luminosity
L_{\rm{CO}}
of a halo to its infrared luminosity
L_{\rm{IR}}
by

\log L^{\prime}_{\rm{CO}}(M)=\alpha\log L_{\rm{IR}}(M)+\beta,

(15)

where
x^{\prime}\equiv L_{\rm{CO}}/L^{\prime}_{\rm{CO}}=4.9\times 10^{-5}\ L_{\odot}/{(\rm K\ km\ s^{-1}\ pc^{2})}
is the conversion between physical and observer units
Li et al. 2016
. We use the fits from Ref.
Kamenetzky et al. 2016
for
\alpha
and
\beta
. Ref.
Li et al. 2016
provides a model for
L_{\rm{IR}}(M)

Behroozi et al. 2013
. We make the common assumption of a lognormal scatter around the mean relation from Eq. (
15
) with width
\sigma_{\rm{sc}}=0.37
dex.

Under this model, we can write the components of the power spectrum (Eq.
1
) as

\overline{T}=C_{\rm{LT}}\int_{M_{\rm{min}}}^{\infty}L_{\rm{CO}}(M)\frac{dn}{dM}dM,

(16)

\overline{b}=\frac{\int_{M_{\rm{min}}}^{\infty}L_{\rm{CO}}(M)b(M)dn/dMdM}{\int_{M_{\rm{min}}}^{\infty}L_{\rm{CO}}(M)dn/dMdM},

(17)

and

P_{\rm{shot}}=C_{\rm{LT}}^{2}e^{(\sigma_{\rm{sc}}\ln 10)^{2}}\int_{M_{\rm{min}}}^{\infty}L_{\rm{CO}}^{2}(M)\frac{dn}{dM}dM,

(18)

where
M_{\rm{min}}=10^{10}\ M_{\odot}
is the minimum mass for an emitting galaxy.
C_{\rm{LT}}=c^{3}(1+z)/8\pi k_{B}\nu^{3}H(z)
converts between luminosity density and brightness temperature for a line with rest frequency
\nu

c

k_{B}

H(z)

\mathcal{P}_{1}(T|M)=\frac{1}{T\sigma_{\rm{sc}}\sqrt{2\pi}\ln 10}e^{-(\log(T/X_{LT})-\mu)^{2}/2\sigma_{\rm{sc}}^{2}},

(19)

where
X_{LT}=C_{LT}/V_{\rm{vox}}
converts luminosity to brightness temperature in a voxel with volume
V_{\rm{vox}}

Breysse et al. 2017
and

\mu\equiv\log\left(L_{\rm{CO}}(M)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\rm{sc}}^{2}\ln(10),

(20)

sets the mean of the distribution to
L_{\rm{CO}}(M)
.

We parameterize our lack of knowledge of the bias by

b(M)=A_{b}b_{0}(M),

(21)

where we take the Tinker form of
b_{0}(M)

Tinker et al. 2010
and the corresponding mass function
dn/dM

Tinker et al. 2008
. The actual VID estimator we will work with will be the map histogram

(22)

which gives the expected number of voxels with intensity in a bin given by

Our demonstration here, while it illustrates a clear new utility of the VID, is by no means a complete exploration of the relevant model dependencies. The two-parameter
L_{\rm{CO}}(M)
model is likely too simple to capture important realities of the actual signal
Padmanabhan 2018
;
Chung et al. 2022
, and our simple treatment of the bias in Eq. (
21
) belies the fact that
b(M)
in practice depends on the broader cosmological model in ways which are not limited to an overall normalization. We have neglected important effects like the aforementioned covariance between
P(k)
and
B_{i}
, but also the presence of satellite galaxies
Schaan and White 2021
, redshift-space distortions
Kaiser 1987
;
Bernal et al. 2019
, and other higher-order clustering effects
Moradinezhad Dizgah et al. 2022b
. We have also ignored other contaminating emission, including continuum foregrounds
Foss et al. 2021
and the CO(2-1) line
Breysse et al. 2022b
. The importance of these approximations will need to be assessed in future work.

Despite the simplicity of our model though, it is quite clear that the information contained in the VID accomplishes our goal of separating the clustering and luminosity information in the power spectrum. For LIM measurements which mix together information from parsec to gigaparsec scales, having the ability to isolate specific aspects of the emitting population will be key. Our results here serve to firmly demonstrate the value of the VID analysis to understand the high-redshift universe from the rapidly-growing volume of intensity mapping data.
