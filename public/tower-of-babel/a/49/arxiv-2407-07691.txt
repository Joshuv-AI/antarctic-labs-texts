Unimodular Valuations beyond Ehrhart
Ansgar Freyer, Monika Ludwig, Martin Rubey

Abstract
. A complete classification of unimodular valuations on the set of lattice polygons with values in the spaces of polynomials and formal power series, respectively, is established. The valuations are classified in terms of their behaviour with respect to dilation using extensions to unbounded polyhedra and basic invariant theory. 2020 AMS subject classification: 52B20, 52B45



1.
Introduction

For
n\geq 2
, let
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
denote the set of lattice polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is, convex polytopes with vertices in the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. A function
\operatorname{Z}
on
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
with values in a vector space
V
is a
valuation
, if

\operatorname{Z}(P)+\operatorname{Z}(Q)=\operatorname{Z}(P\cup Q)+\operatorname{Z}(P\cap Q)

for all
P,Q\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
such that both
P\cup Q
and
P\cap Q
are also in
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.
The lattice point enumerator
\Lat^{0}\colon{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
, which counts the number of lattice points in
P\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, and
n
-dimensional volume are important valuations on
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.

A fundamental result on valuations on lattice polytopes is the Betke–Kneser Theorem
[
5
]
. It provides a complete classification of real-valued valuations on lattice polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are invariant with respect to the
affine unimodular group
, obtained by the general linear group over the integers
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and translations by vectors from
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. It also provides a characterization of the so-called Ehrhart coefficients
[
10
]
. We state the two-dimensional version.

Theorem 1

(Betke–Kneser)
.

A function
\,\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
is a translation and
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
invariant valuation if and only if there are
\,c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\operatorname{Z}(P)=c_{0}\Lat^{0}_{0}(P)+c_{1}\Lat^{0}_{1}(P)+c_{2}\Lat^{0}_{2}(P)

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

In the planar case, the Ehrhart coefficients have a simple description:
\Lat^{0}_{0}(P)\coloneq 1
for a non-empty lattice polygon
P
, while
\Lat^{0}_{1}(P)
is half the number of lattice points in the boundary of
P
, and
\Lat^{0}_{2}(P)
is the area of
P
.
We refer to
[
2
,
3
,
11
,
12
,
22
]
for general information on valuations on lattice polytopes and to
[
1
,
4
,
6
,
13
,
14
,
19
,
22
]
for some recent results.

We generalize the above theorem and establish a complete classification of polynomial-valued, unimodular valuations on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
Here, we call a valuation
unimodular
if it is translatively polynomial and
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, and we recall both definitions below.

Let
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
denote the vector space of bivariate polynomials and consider the (left) action of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
defined by
\phi f\coloneq f\circ\phi^{\star}
for
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and

\phi\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
\phi^{\star}
is the transpose of
\phi
.
A function
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant
if
\operatorname{Z}(\phi P)=\phi\operatorname{Z}(P)
for every
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

Since
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is the direct sum of the spaces of
r
-homogeneous polynomials
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
for
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, it suffices to classify valuations with values in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
.
This corresponds to a classification of valuations from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
to the space of symmetric
r
-tensors
. In this setting, the question was first considered in
[
19
]
(see also
[
22
, Chapter 21]
), where a complete classification was established for
r\leq 8
. We remark that
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance was considered in
[
19
]
, but there is a mistake in a calculation in the planar case. However, the results are correct for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance in the planar case and for
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance for
n\geq 3
.

All tensor valuations that appear for
r\leq 8
are tensor-valued Ehrhart coefficients (introduced in
[
7
,
8
]
for
r\geq 1
) obtained through a homogeneous decomposition of the
r
th
discrete moment tensor
. This tensor corresponds to the
r
th discrete moment polynomial,

\Lat^{r}(P)\coloneq\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{v\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}v^{r},

where
v^{r}(x,y)=(ax+by)^{r}
for
v=(a,b)
, that is, we consider
(x,y)
as element of the dual of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
v
as an element of its bidual. In particular,
\Lat^{0}
is the classical lattice point enumerator. Similar to Ehrhart’s celebrated result, there is a homogeneous decomposition of
\Lat^{r}
(see
[
7
]
):
for
r\geq 1
and
1\leq i\leq r+2
, there exist
i
-homogeneous valuations
\Lat_{i}^{r}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
such that

(1)

\Lat^{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r+2}\Lat_{i}^{r}.

Here, a function
\operatorname{Z}
is called
i
-homogeneous
if
\operatorname{Z}(mP)=m^{i}\operatorname{Z}(P)
for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

A valuation
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is
translatively polynomial
(of degree at most
r
)
if there exist
associated valuations

\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
for
0\leq j\leq r
such that

(2)

\operatorname{Z}(P+v)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}(P)\frac{v^{j}}{j!}

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The associated valuations are uniquely determined, and by setting
v=0
, we see that
\operatorname{Z}^{r}=\operatorname{Z}
.
Khovanskiĭ and Pukhlikov
[
15
]
showed that every translatively polynomial valuation
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
of degree at most
r
can be written as

\operatorname{Z}=\sum_{i=0}^{r+2}\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r},

where
\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r}
are
i
-homogeneous valuations.

Let
\mathrm{Val}
denote the vector space of translatively polynomial and
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuations
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
. Let
\mathrm{Val}^{r}
be the subspace of valuations
\operatorname{Z}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
. We call
r
the
rank
of the valuation.
It was proved in
[
19
]
that the space

\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}\coloneq\{\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}\colon\operatorname{Z}\text{ is $i$-homogeneous}\}

is spanned by the Ehrhart coefficient
\Lat^{r}_{i}
for
1\leq r\leq 8
and
1\leq i\leq r+2
, and that it is zero-dimensional for
i=0
and
r\geq 1
, as well as for
i>r+2
. Moreover, it was proved that
\dim\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{9}\geq 2
.

Our first main result determines the dimensions of the vector spaces
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
.

Theorem 2
.

For
i,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\dim\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ for }1\leq i<r\text{ and }r-i\text{ odd, and for }r\leq i\leq r+2,\\
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)&\text{ for }i=1<r\text{ and }r-1\text{ even},\\
p_{2,3}\left(\frac{r-i}{2}+1\right)&\text{ for }1<i<r\text{ and }r-i\text{ even},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)\coloneq|\{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}\colon 2k+3\ell=r\}|

is the number of integer partitions of
r
into parts
2
and
3
.

The vector space
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
is spanned by
\Lat_{i}^{r}
if
\dim\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}=1
, and
\Lat_{i}^{r}\neq 0
(that is,
\Lat_{i}^{r}
is not identically zero) for every
1\leq i\leq r+2
. Since
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)=1
for
r\in\{3,5,7\}
,
Theorem

2
gives a basis of
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
for every even
r
and for
r\leq 7
.

The following result provides bases of the vector spaces
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
also for the remaining
r
.
Let
\mathbf{T}
be the standard triangle with vertices at the origin,
e_{1}\coloneq(1,0)
, and
e_{2}\coloneq(0,1)
. Recall that every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
has a (generally non-unique) unimodular triangulation
\mathcal{T}
; that is,
P
can be written as finite union of non-overlapping triangles
\phi\mathbf{T}+v\in\mathcal{T}
with
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Theorem 3
.

For
r>1
odd, a basis of
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
is given by
\Lat_{1}^{2k,3\ell}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
with
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
2k+3\ell=r
, defined for
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
\,\mathcal{T}
any unimodular triangulation of
P
, by

\Lat_{1}^{2k,3\ell}(P)\coloneq\sum\nolimits_{\phi\mathbf{T}+v\in\mathcal{T}}\big(\Lat_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{T})^{k}\Lat_{1}^{3}(\mathbf{T})^{\ell}\big)\circ\phi^{\star}

Here,
\Lat_{1}^{2k,3\ell}(P)
does not depend on the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
P
.

Note that it follows that the Ehrhart coefficient
\Lat_{1}^{7}
is a multiple of
\Lat_{1}^{4,3}
.

Our approach also yields a structure theorem for valuations with values in the space of bivariate formal power series
\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
.
A valuation
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
is called
translatively exponential
if

\operatorname{\overline{Z}}(P+v)=\mathrm{e}^{v}\operatorname{\overline{Z}}(P)

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where we again consider
v
as a linear function on the dual of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}
denote the vector space of translatively exponential,
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuations
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
. Note that the functions

(3)

\overline{\Lat}(P)\coloneq\sum_{v\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{v},\qquad\operatorname{\overline{E}}(P)\coloneq\int_{P}\mathrm{e}^{v}\,\mathrm{d}v

are both in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}
(see
[
2
,
3
]
for more information and applications).

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we say that
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
is
d
-dilative
if

\operatorname{\overline{Z}}(mP)(x,y)=m^{-d}\operatorname{\overline{Z}}(P)(mx,my)

for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
For instance, the exponential integral
\operatorname{\overline{E}}
defined in
Eq.

3
is
(-2)
-dilative.
Set

\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}\coloneq\{\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}\colon\text{ $\operatorname{\overline{Z}}$ is $d$-dilative\lx@text@rbrace}

for
d\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Similar to the translatively polynomial case, dilative valuations decompose the space
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}
, and we determine the dimensions of the vector spaces
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
.

Theorem 4
.

For every
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}
, there are unique
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}_{d}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}=\sum_{d\geq-2}\operatorname{\overline{Z}}_{d}
.
Moreover,

\dim\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ for }-2\leq d\leq 0\text{ and for }d>0\text{ odd},\\
p_{2,3}\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)&\text{ for }d>0\text{ even},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The
r
-summand (that is, the sum of the monomials of degree
r
) of a valuation in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
is in
\mathrm{Val}^{r}_{i}
for
d=r-i
.
We will see that all valuations in
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
for
i>1
occur as
(r-i)
-summand of a translatively exponential valuation in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{r-i}
.
For
d\leq 0
and for
d>0
odd, the space
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
is spanned by the
d
-dilative part
\overline{\Lat}_{d}
of
\overline{\Lat}
(see
Section

7
). For
d>0
even, we will present in
Eq.

25
an explicit representation for all valuations in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
.
Thus, we obtain a triangulation-free description of the valuations in
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
for
i>1
and
r-i
even as summands of valuations in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{r-i}
. In
Section

10
, we describe some consequences of our results.

3.
Preliminaries

We collect results and definitions that are used in our arguments.

3.1.
Associated valuations

For
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
be a translatively polynomial valuation, that is, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we have

\operatorname{Z}(P+v)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}(P)\frac{v^{j}}{j!}

with suitable functions
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r-j}
. By comparing the coefficients of
v^{j}
, we see that each
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}
is uniquely determined and a valuation. Moreover, setting
v=0
, we obtain
\operatorname{Z}^{r}=\operatorname{Z}
. We refer to the valuations
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}
as the
associated valuations of
\operatorname{Z}
. It was shown in
[
19
]
that associated valuations are, in the following way, hereditary.

Lemma 5

(Proposition 6 in
[
19
]
)
.

If
\,\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
is a translatively polynomial valuation with associated valuation
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r-j}
for
0\leq j\leq r
, then each
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}
is translatively polynomial and its associated valuations are
\operatorname{Z}^{r-k}
for
j\leq k\leq r
.

It follows from a result of Khovanskiĭ and Pukhlikov
[
15
]
that
\operatorname{Z}
can be decomposed into
homogeneous components
.

Theorem 6

(Theorem 14 in
[
19
]
)
.

If
\,\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
is a translatively polynomial valuation, then there exist
i
-homogeneous valuations
\operatorname{Z}_{i}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
for
0\leq i\leq r+2
such that
\operatorname{Z}=\sum_{i=0}^{r+2}\operatorname{Z}_{i}
.

The next lemma follows from this decomposition and Propositions 6 and 7 in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
be a translatively polynomial valuation. For
0\leq i\leq r+2
, each homogeneous component
\operatorname{Z}_{i}
is translatively polynomial. The associated functions of
\operatorname{Z}_{i}
are given by the homogeneous decomposition of the associated functions of
\operatorname{Z}
, that is,
(\operatorname{Z}_{i})^{r-j}=(\operatorname{Z}^{r-j})_{i-j}
for
j\leq\min\{i,r\}
and
(\operatorname{Z}_{i})^{r-j}=0
otherwise.

We will use the abbreviation
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}_{i-j}\coloneq(\operatorname{Z}^{r-j})_{i-j}
.

3.2.
Invariant and equivariant valuations.

If the valuation
\operatorname{Z}
in
Lemma

7
is
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
invariant, then its associated valuations inherit the
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
invariance. For a subgroup
G
of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, let
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{G}
be the ring of
G
invariant polynomials. It is easy to see that

(4)

\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\cong\mathbb{R}.

As observed in
[
19
]
, this implies that the constant term in the homogeneous decomposition in
Theorem

6
vanishes if
\operatorname{Z}
is
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
invariant.

Lemma 8
.

For
r\geq 0
and
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}
, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\operatorname{Z}_{0}(P)=c
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. If
r>0
, then this constant is zero.

It was also observed in
[
19
]
that this implies the following result.

Lemma 9
.

If
r\geq 2
and
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
, then
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant.

A valuation
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
is called
odd
if it is equivariant with respect to reflection at the origin, that is, we have
\operatorname{Z}(-P)=-\operatorname{Z}(P)
. Note that since the reflection at the origin is in
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have
\operatorname{Z}(-P)=(-1)^{r}\operatorname{Z}(P)
for any
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}
. So
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}
is odd if and only if
r
is an odd number.

In the following, we will also deal with valuations taking values in the spaces of formal power series or meromorphic functions on
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\star}
. For such valuations, the term
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance will also refer to the action
\phi f=f\circ\phi^{\star}
, where
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
f
is a (formal) function.

3.3.
Planar lattice triangulations

A
decomposition
of a two-dimensional lattice polygon
P
is a set of lattice polygons
\mathcal{T}
such that
the following properties hold:

(1)

P=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}T
.

(2)

For all
S,T\in\mathcal{T}
, the set
S\cap T
is a (possibly empty) face of
S
and
T
.

If all
S\in\mathcal{T}
are triangles, we call
\mathcal{T}
a
triangulation
.

Recall that a triangle
S=\conv\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\}
with
v_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called
unimodular
if it can be expressed as
S=\phi\mathbf{T}+v
, where
\mathbf{T}=\conv\{0,e_{1},e_{2}\}
and
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Here,
\conv
stands for convex hull.
The triangle
S
is unimodular if and only if the edge vectors
v_{i}-v_{0}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
generate
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Note that
v_{0}
might be any of the three vertices of
S
in this condition.
In fact, it follows that
S
is unimodular if and only if
\vol(S)=\tfrac{1}{2}
, where
\vol
stands for (two-dimensional) volume. Note that one has
\vol(S)>\tfrac{1}{2}
for a non-unimodular lattice triangle since its edge vectors generate a proper two-dimensional sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
A triangulation is called unimodular if all of its simplices are unimodular.

Moreover, a lattice triangle is unimodular if and only if it is
empty
; that is, it contains no lattice points other than the vertices. It follows that every lattice polygon
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
admits an unimodular triangulation.

For triangulations
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of a lattice polygon
P
one says that
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are
connected by a flip
, if
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{T}=\{S_{1}^{\prime},S_{2}^{\prime}\}
and, moreover,
S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}=S_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup S_{2}^{\prime}
is a convex quadrilateral. In this case,
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
and
\{S_{1}^{\prime},S_{2}^{\prime}\}
correspond to the two possible triangulations of the quadrilateral.

Theorem 10

(Lawson
[
17
]
)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and let
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be triangulations of
P
. Then there exists a finite sequence of triangulations
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{0},\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{m}=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{i}
are connected by a flip for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Lawson’s theorem holds in more generality for triangulations of arbitrary (non-lattice) point sets.

A valuation
\operatorname{Z}
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
is called
simple
if it vanishes on points and segments.
Using the valuation property inductively, it is easy to see that valuations on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
satisfy the inclusion-exclusion principle. In particular if
\operatorname{Z}
is a simple valuation and
\mathcal{T}
is a triangulation of a polygon
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, we have

(5)

\operatorname{Z}(P)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{Z}(S).

The inclusion-exclusion principle also holds for valuations on lattice polytopes in higher dimensions, but its proof is no longer trivial (see
[
21
]
).

The following corollary of
Theorem

10
helps to construct a valuation “from scratch”. Let
\rho_{i}
denote the reflection on the
i
th coordinate axis and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
the set of unimodular lattice triangles.

Corollary 11
.

If
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant and such that

(6)

\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(v+\mathbf{T})+\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(v+e_{1}+e_{2}-\mathbf{T})=\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(v+e_{1}+\rho_{2}\mathbf{T})+\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(v+e_{2}+\rho_{1}\mathbf{T})

for every
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then there is a unique simple,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
with
\operatorname{Z}(T)=\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)
for every
T\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Conversely, if
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is a simple,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation, then its restriction
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
satisfies
Eq.

6
.

Proof.

Note that

(7)

\mathbf{T}\sqcup(e_{1}+e_{2}-\mathbf{T})=(e_{1}+\rho_{2}\mathbf{T})\sqcup(e_{2}+\rho_{1}\mathbf{T})=[0,1]^{2}.

Let
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}
be as in the statement of the corollary. For
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
two-dimensional, we define
\operatorname{Z}(P;\mathcal{T})\coloneq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)
, where
\mathcal{T}
is a unimodular triangulation of
P
. We claim that
\operatorname{Z}(P;\mathcal{T})
is independent of
\mathcal{T}
. By
Theorem

10
, it is enough to consider a triangulation
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, which is connected to
\mathcal{T}
by a flip. Let
Q
be the convex quadrilateral in which
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
differ. It is easy to see that
Q=v+\phi[0,1]^{2}
for some
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Since there are exactly two lattice triangulations of
[0,1]^{2}
, the ones given in
Eq.

7
, it follows that, say,
\mathcal{T}
contains
v+\phi(e_{1}+e_{2}-\mathbf{T})
and
v+\phi\mathbf{T}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
contains
v+\phi(e_{1}+\rho_{2}\mathbf{T})
and
v+\phi(e_{2}+\rho_{1}\mathbf{T})
. Since
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}
is
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, we obtain
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(v+\phi T)=\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}v+T)
for any unimodular triangle
T
. Thus,
\operatorname{Z}(P;\mathcal{T})=\operatorname{Z}(P;\mathcal{T}^{\prime})
follows from
Eq.

6
, and we can define
\operatorname{Z}(P)\coloneq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)
for
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
two-dimensional. Setting
\operatorname{Z}(P)\coloneq 0
for
\dim P<2
, we obtain a simple,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation with
\operatorname{Z}(T)=\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)
for every
T\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. The uniqueness of
\operatorname{Z}
follows from
Eq.

5
.
The converse statement is immediate since both sides of
Eq.

6
are equal to
\operatorname{Z}(v+[0,1]^{2})
.
∎

The following lemma will be used frequently in the following sections.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}
be simple with associated valuations
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}
for
0\leq j\leq r
.
If
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
is two-dimensional with unimodular triangulation

\mathcal{T}=\{\phi_{i}\mathbf{T}+v_{i}:\phi_{i}\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\,v_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\,1\leq i\leq m\}
,
then

(8)

\operatorname{Z}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\big(\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}(\mathbf{T})\circ\phi_{i}^{\star}\big)\frac{v_{i}^{j}}{j!}.

In particular,
\operatorname{Z}
is uniquely determined by the values
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}(\mathbf{T})
for
1\leq j\leq r
.

Proof.

By using the equivariance properties of
\operatorname{Z}
to
Eq.

5
, we immediately obtain
Eq.

8
. For the last statement, we recall that every two-dimensional lattice polygon has a unimodular triangulation, so
\operatorname{Z}(P)
can always be evaluated via
Eq.

8
.
∎

3.4.
Integer partitions

In Theorem
2
, we defined

(9)

p_{2,3}\left(r\right)\coloneq|\{(k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}\colon 2k+3\ell=r\}|,

the number of integer partitions of
r
into parts
2
and
3
. In explicit terms, one has

(10)

p_{2,3}\left(r\right)=\left\lfloor\frac{r+2}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{r+2}{3}\right\rfloor.

The sequence is
A103221
in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.

4.
One-homogeneous valuations of odd rank

In this section, we prove
Theorem

2
for
i=1
and
r>1
odd.
We require the following result.

Lemma 13
.

Let
r-i>0
be even and
i\geq 1
. If
\,\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
, then
\operatorname{Z}
is simple.

Proof.

First, let
i=1
. It follows from the assumption that
r
is odd and from
Lemma

9
, that
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant. Let
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
be a segment or a point. In either case, a vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
exists, such as
-Q=Q+v
. Since
r
is odd and
\operatorname{Z}
is
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, the valuation
\operatorname{Z}
is also odd.
Hence, by translation invariance,

-\operatorname{Z}(Q)=\operatorname{Z}(-Q)=\operatorname{Z}(Q+v)=\operatorname{Z}(Q)

and, thus,
\operatorname{Z}(Q)=0
.

Now, let
i>1
. By induction and
Lemma

8
, the associated functions
\operatorname{Z}_{i-j}^{r-j}
of
\operatorname{Z}
are simple for
0<j<i
. This implies
\operatorname{Z}(Q+v)=\operatorname{Z}(Q)
for any
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and any lower-dimensional
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

It follows from (
4
) that
\operatorname{Z}(\{0\})=0
. For any
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
p\neq 0
, the translation invariance on lower-dimensional polygons implies that
\operatorname{Z}(\{p\})=\operatorname{Z}(p+\{0\})=0
. So, let
S\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
be a segment. For
m\geq 1
, we can decompose the dilate
mS
into
m
translates
S_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq m
of
S
that intersect at lattice points. We just saw that our valuation vanishes on points. Hence, we obtain from the homogeneity of
\operatorname{Z}
that

m^{i}\operatorname{Z}(S)=\operatorname{Z}(mS)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Z}(S_{i})=m\operatorname{Z}(S).

Since
i>1
, we conclude that
\operatorname{Z}(S)=0
.
∎

Let
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
with
r>1
odd. As we saw earlier,
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant by
Lemma

9
, simple by
Lemma

13
, and odd. The converse statement is also true.

Lemma 14
.

If
r
is odd and
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
is a simple, translation invariant,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation, then
\operatorname{Z}
is one-homogeneous, that is,
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
.

Proof.

Since the valuation
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant, all its associated valuations
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r-j}
vanish except for
\operatorname{Z}^{r}=\operatorname{Z}
. Thus,
Lemma

12
implies that it is enough to check
\operatorname{Z}(m\mathbf{T})=m\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
.

We observe that

m\mathbf{T}=\Big(\bigsqcup_{v\in(m-2)\mathbf{T}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(v+[0,1]^{2})\Big)\sqcup\Big(\bigsqcup_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},~a+b=m-1}(ae_{1}+be_{2}+\mathbf{T})\Big).

Since
\operatorname{Z}
is odd and translation invariant and
[0,1]^{2}
is centrally symmetric, it follows that
\operatorname{Z}(v+[0,1]^{2})=0
for all
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Thus, by
Eq.

5
,

\operatorname{Z}(m\mathbf{T})=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},~a+b=m-1}\operatorname{Z}(ae_{1}+be_{2}+\mathbf{T})=m\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T}),

where we used again that
\operatorname{Z}
is simple and translation invariant.
∎

Let
\mathbf{G}
denote the subgroup of unimodular transformations
\phi\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
for which there exists
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
\mathbf{T}=\phi\mathbf{T}+v
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
r>1
be odd. The evaluation map

\Theta^{r}\colon\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}},\quad\Theta^{r}(\operatorname{Z})\coloneq\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})

is a well-defined linear isomorphism. The valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{f}:=(\Theta^{r})^{-1}(f)
, obtained by the inverse map, is simple for
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
and given by

(11)

\operatorname{Z}_{f}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f\circ\phi_{i}^{\star}.

for any two-dimensional
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})

with unimodular triangulation

\mathcal{T}=\{\phi_{i}\mathbf{T}+v_{i}:\phi_{i}\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\,v_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\,1\leq i\leq m\}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\Theta^{r}(\operatorname{Z})\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
for
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
. If
\phi\in\mathbf{G}
, then there exists
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
\mathbf{T}=\phi\mathbf{T}+v
. Since
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant by
Lemma

9
and
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, it follows that

\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})=\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})\circ\phi^{\star}

for
\phi\in\mathbf{G}
,
which shows that
\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
as required.

The linearity of
\Theta^{r}
is obvious. The injectivity follows directly from
Lemma

12
. To show that
\Theta^{r}
is surjective, we
show that for any
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
the expression
Eq.

11
can serve as a definition of a simple valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{f}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
, which is then necessarily
(\Theta^{r})^{-1}(f)
.

To this end,
consider a unimodular triangle
T\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
represented in two ways as an affine image of the standard triangle
\mathbf{T}
, that is,
T=\phi\mathbf{T}+v=\phi^{\prime}\mathbf{T}+v^{\prime}
for certain
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Rearranging yields

\mathbf{T}=\phi^{-1}\phi^{\prime}\,\mathbf{T}+\phi^{-1}(v^{\prime}-v).

Hence,
\phi^{-1}\phi^{\prime}\in\mathbf{G}
. Since
f
is
\mathbf{G}
invariant, it follows that
f\circ(\phi^{\prime})^{\star}=f\circ\phi^{\star}
, that is,
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)\coloneq f\circ\phi^{\star}
is a well-defined translation invariant,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant function on the set of unimodular triangles.

Next, we note that the triangles
\rho_{1}\mathbf{T}
and
\rho_{2}\mathbf{T}
occurring in
Eq.

6
are opposite in the sense that
\rho_{1}\mathbf{T}=-\rho_{2}\mathbf{T}
. Thus, since the degree of
f
is odd, we have
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(-\mathbf{T})=-\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T})
and
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(\rho_{1}\mathbf{T})=-\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(\rho_{2}\mathbf{T})
. Since
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}
is translation invariant,
Eq.

6
is satisfied. Hence,
Corollary

11
yields a simple
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
with
\operatorname{Z}(T)=\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(T)
for all unimodular triangles
T
. Since
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}
is translation invariant, it follows from
Eq.

5
that
\operatorname{Z}
is translation invariant as well. In particular,
\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r}
and
Lemma

12
shows that
\operatorname{Z}(P)=\operatorname{Z}_{f}(P)
as given by
Eq.

11
.

Finally,
Lemma

14
yields that
\operatorname{Z}_{f}
is 1-homogeneous, that is, we have
\operatorname{Z}_{f}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
as desired.
∎

For
r=1
, the valuations in
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{1}
are not necessarily translation invariant. Thus,
\operatorname{Z}(\mathbf{T})
is not
\,\mathbf{G}
invariant in this case. For
r>1
even, the valuations in
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
are translation invariant by
Lemma

9
, and so the evaluation map
\Theta^{r}
is a well-defined linear map to
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
. However, it is, in general, not an isomorphism.

To compute the dimension of
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
for
r>1
odd, it is now enough to compute the Molien series of the invariant ring
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\mathbf{G}}
. To this end, we
observe that
\mathbf{G}
has order 6. Any unimodular transformation
\phi\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
maps
\mathbf{T}
to a triangle with a vertex at the origin. So if
\phi\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}+v
, we have
v\in\{0,-e_{1},-e_{2}\}
. The two non-zero vertices of
\mathbf{T}
must then be mapped by
\phi
to the non-zero vertices of
\mathbf{T}+v
. Hence, we have

\mathbf{G}=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\
\phantom{-}0&\phantom{-}1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle,

since the group on the right-hand side has order 6 and is contained in
\mathbf{G}
. From this, we see that
\mathbf{G}
is a finite group generated by
pseudo-reflections
, that is, linear transformations that fix a space of codimension 1. The Chevalley–Shephard–Todd Theorem (see, for example,
[
23
, Theorem 7.1.4]
) now implies that the ring of
\mathbf{G}
invariant polynomials,
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\mathbf{G}}
, is finitely generated and that its generators are algebraically independent. Molien’s formula (for example,
[
23
, Theorem 3.1.3]
) yields that the dimension of
\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
is
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)
(defined in
Eq.

9
). In particular,
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\mathbf{G}}
is generated by suitable polynomials of degree
2
and
3
.

Lemma 16
.

The algebraically independent polynomials

\mathbf{p}_{2}(x,y)\coloneq x^{2}-xy+y^{2}
and
\mathbf{p}_{3}(x,y)\coloneq x^{3}-\tfrac{3}{2}(x^{2}y+xy^{2})+y^{3}

generate the invariant ring
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\mathbf{G}}
.

Proof.

It is enough to apply the Reynolds operator
\frac{1}{\lvert\mathbf{G}\rvert}\sum_{\phi\in\mathbf{G}}\phi
, which is a projection onto
\mathbb{R}[x,y]^{\mathbf{G}}
, to the polynomials
x^{2}
and
x^{3}
and thus compute the generators
\mathbf{p}_{2}
and
\mathbf{p}_{3}
.
∎

Combining
Lemma

16
with
Theorem

15
gives the second case of
Theorem

2
.

Corollary 17
.

For
r>1
odd, we have
\dim\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}=p_{2,3}\left(r\right)
.

Note that
r=9
is the first odd number such that
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)>1
.
Since
\Lat^{9}(m\mathbf{T})
is a polynomial of degree 11 in
m
with coefficients
\Lat_{i}^{9}(\mathbf{T})
, we can compute
\Lat_{1}^{9}(\mathbf{T})
via interpolation.
Using
SageMath

[

]
, we obtain
\Lat_{1}^{9}=\operatorname{Z}_{f}
(defined in
Eq.

11
), where

f=\frac{1}{990\cdot 9!}\left(4\mathbf{p}_{3}^{3}-79\mathbf{p}_{2}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{3}\right).

The valuation constructed in
[

, Section 8.2]
is a unimodular valuation that is not a linear combination of Ehrhart tensor coefficients. It corresponds (up to a constant factor) to
\operatorname{Z}_{g}
with
g=\mathbf{p}_{3}^{3}
.

5.
Two-homogeneous valuations of even rank

Throughout the section, we assume that
r>1
is odd and consider a valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
. When does there exist

\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}\in\mathrm{Val}^{r+1}
such that

(12)

\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}(P+v)=\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}(P)+\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(P)v

for every
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
?

To answer this question, we first prove the following lemma on associated valuations, which we will also use in the classification for higher homogeneity degrees.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\operatorname{Y},\operatorname{Z}\in\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
with
r>i>1
and
r-i
even. If the associated valuations satisfy
\,\operatorname{Y}^{r-j}=\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}
for all
0<j<i
, then
\operatorname{Y}=\operatorname{Z}
.

Proof of
Lemma

18
.

Let
\operatorname{X}\coloneq\operatorname{Z}-\operatorname{Y}
. By assumption,
\operatorname{X}
is translation invariant, as well as
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant and
i
-homogeneous, and by
Lemma

13
, it is simple. If
i=2
, then
[
19
, Proposition 23]
yields
\operatorname{X}=0
and thus the claim. If
i>2
, we have
X=0
by
[
20
, Theorem 5]
.
∎

By
Lemma

18
, there is at most one solution
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}
of (
12
). We will now determine for which
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}
a solution
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}
exists. We write
\mathbf{D}
for the group generated by the reflections at the coordinate axes and permutation of the coordinates, that is,

\mathbf{D}=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\,\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle.

Note that
\mathbf{D}
is a finite subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
r>1
be odd and
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{r}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}\in\mathrm{Val}_{2}^{r+1}
such that
Eq.

12
holds for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(2)

There exists
h\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r+1}^{\mathbf{G}}
such that
h+(x+y)\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r+1}^{\mathbf{D}}
.

Moreover, the polynomial
h
is uniquely determined by
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(\mathbf{T})
if the conditions
(1)
and
(2)
are satisfied.

Proof.

First, we observe that condition (2) is by linearity equivalent to

(2’)

There exists
h\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r+1}^{\mathbf{G}}
such that
2h-(\tfrac{x}{3}+\tfrac{y}{3})\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r+1}^{\mathbf{D}}
.

In the proof, we will work with (2’) rather than (2).

We start by showing that (1) implies (2’).
By
Lemma

13
, the valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}
is simple. So, it is determined by

f_{1}\coloneq\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(\mathbf{T})\text{ and }f_{2}\coloneq\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}(\mathbf{T})

by
Lemma

12
. Consider a unimodular transformation
\phi\in\mathbf{G}
such that
\phi\mathbf{T}+v=\mathbf{T}
for some
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Then, by
Eq.

12
and since
f_{1}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}^{\mathbf{G}}
,

(13)

f_{2}=\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{r+1}(\phi\mathbf{T}+v)=f_{2}\circ\phi^{\star}+f_{1}v.

Suppose
\widetilde{f}_{2}
is another solution of (
13
). Then
f_{2}-\widetilde{f}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r+1}^{\mathbf{G}}
. Next, we make the guess that

\operatorname{Z}^{r}
is translatively polynomial with associated valuation
\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}

\operatorname{Z}^{r}_{i}

\operatorname{Z}^{r-j}_{i-j}

In the following, we want to prove that
\Lambda^{d+2}
is a linear isomorphism.
To this end, we construct a valuation on the set of rational polyhedra
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
with values in the vector space

\mathcal{V}\coloneq\spann\{\mathrm{e}^{v}\cdot f:v\in\mathbb{R}^{2},~f\in\mathbb{R}(x,y)\},

a subspace of the space of meromorphic functions. Here,
\mathbb{R}(x,y)
denotes the space of rational functions in
x
and
y
.
The restriction of this valuation to lattice polygons will take values in
\mathbb{R}[[x,y]]
. Here, a polyhedron
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called
rational
if it can be represented as
Q=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon z_{i}(v)\leq b_{i},~1\leq i\leq m\}
for certain
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\in(\mathbb{Q}^{2})^{\star}
and
b_{1}\dots,b_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}
. For
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\pos(X)
denote the positive hull of
X
. We call a rational polyhedron
Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
a
rational cone
if
\pos(Q)=Q
, and we denote the set of rational cones by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
.

We will use the following special case of
[
16
, Theorem 1]
. Let
\operatorname{vert}(Q)
be the set of vertices of
Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
.

Theorem 28

(Lawrence)
.

A valuation
\zeta_{0}\colon\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})\to\mathcal{V}
that vanishes on cones containing a line can be extended to a valuation
\zeta
on
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
by

\zeta(Q)\coloneq\sum\nolimits_{v\in\operatorname{vert}(Q)}\mathrm{e}^{v}\,\zeta_{0}(\fcone(v;Q)),

where
\fcone(v;Q)\coloneq\pos(Q-v)
.

To construct our particular
\zeta_{0}
, we start with a valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}\in\mathrm{Val}_{2}^{d+2}
and consider the rational function

(18)

R(x,y)\coloneq\frac{\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}([0,1]^{2})}{xy}.

Note that
R\in\mathbb{R}(x,y)_{d}
, the space of
d
-homogeneous rational functions in
x
and
y
.

We require some results and notions related to cones (see, for example,
[
9
]
). A pointed two-dimensional cone
C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
is called
unimodular
if
C=\phi_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0})
with
\phi_{C}\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Here
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}\coloneq\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x,y\geq 0\}
. Note that
\phi_{C}
is unique up to a permutation of the coordinates. Since
R
is symmetric in
x
and
y
, the expression
R\circ\phi_{C}^{\star}
is independent of the choice of
\phi_{C}
.
For a general two-dimensional pointed cone
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
, let
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
be the primitive lattice vectors on the boundary of the convex hull of
K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
(labeled in any direction along the boundary), where a lattice vector is
primitive
if its coordinates are co-prime.
For a two-dimensional cone, the
Hilbert decomposition

\can(K)
can be defined as

\can(K)\coloneq\{\pos\{u_{i-1},u_{i}\}\colon 1\leq i\leq m\}.

It is easy to see that the decomposition
\can(K)
is
unimodular
; that is, the cones of
\can(K)
are unimodular.
Moreover,
any unimodular decomposition of
\can(K)
is a refinement of
\can(K)
(cf.
[
24
, Proposition 1.19]
).
We can describe the refinements in a structured way. For a unimodular cone
C=\pos\{u,v\}
, where
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
are primitive vectors, we define the
balanced decomposition
of
C
as
\{\pos\{u,u+v\},\pos\{u+v,v\}\}
.
For two unimodular decompositions
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
of the cone
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
, we say that
\mathcal{T}
is an
elementary refinement
of
\mathcal{S}
if
\mathcal{T}
can be obtained from
\mathcal{S}
by replacing a unimodular cone of
\mathcal{S}
by its balanced decomposition.

Lemma 29
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
be two-dimensional and pointed. For every unimodular decomposition
\mathcal{T}
of
K
, there exist unimodular decompositions
\mathcal{T}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}
(for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
) of
K
such that
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\can(K)
,
\mathcal{T}_{k}=\mathcal{T}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is an elementary refinement of
\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

Since any unimodular triangulation of
K
is a refinement of
\can(K)
, it suffices to prove the lemma for
K
being itself a unimodular cone, that is,
\can(K)=\{K\}
and
K=\pos\{u,v\}
, where
u
and
v
generate
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
We use induction on
m\coloneq|\mathcal{T}|
.
For
m=1
, there is nothing to prove. So, let
m>1
and suppose that the statement of the lemma is true for any unimodular cone
K
and any decomposition
\mathcal{T}
of size less than
m
.

First, we show that the ray
\pos\{u+v\}
is contained in the boundary of two cones of
\mathcal{T}
.
To see this, we apply a unimodular transformation so that
K=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
and, thus,
u+v=(1,1)
.
If
\pos\{(1,1)\}
is not a boundary ray of a cone in
\mathcal{T}
, there exists a unimodular cone
C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
with primitive generators
(a,b),(c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
a>b\geq 0
and
d>c\geq 0
and such that
(1,1)
is contained in the interior
C
. Since
C
is unimodular, there exist
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
(1,1)=k(a,b)+\ell(c,d)
. Using that the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
is in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and solving for
k
and
\ell
gives
k=d-c\geq 1
and
\ell=a-b\geq 1
. This implies
(a,b)=(1,0)
and
(c,d)=(0,1)
. Hence,
C=K
, which contradicts
m>1
. Thus, the ray
\pos\{u+v\}
must be contained in the boundary of a cone of
\mathcal{T}
.

Consequently, we obtain that
\mathcal{T}
is the disjoint union of the two unimodular decompositions

\{T\in\mathcal{T}\colon T\subseteq\pos\{u,u+v\}\}
and

\{T\in\mathcal{T}\colon T\subseteq\pos\{u+v,v\}\}
of the unimodular cones
\pos\{u,u+v\}
and
\pos\{u+v,v\}
, respectively.
Since each of the two decompositions contains fewer cones than
\mathcal{T}
, the induction hypothesis applies, and we see that
\mathcal{T}
is obtained by taking the balanced decomposition of
K
first and then refining the two halves inductively.
∎

For a pointed two-dimensional cone
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
, set

(19)

\zeta_{0}(K)\coloneq\sum_{C\in\can(K)}R\circ\phi_{C}^{\star}.

If
K
is not pointed or if
K
is lower-dimensional, we set
\zeta_{0}(K)\coloneq 0
.

Lemma 30
.

For
d>0
even, the function
\zeta_{0}\colon\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}(x,y)_{d}
, defined in
Eq.

19
, is a simple,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuation that vanishes on cones with lines.

Proof.

We only have to show that
\zeta_{0}
is a valuation.
Let
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
and
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a rational one-dimensional subspace (
L
is generated by a rational vector) that intersects
K
. Let
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
denote the intersections of
K
with the two closed halfspaces determined by
L
. By
[
2
, Lemma 19.5]
, it is sufficient to check that

(20)

\zeta_{0}(K)=\zeta_{0}(K_{+})+\zeta_{0}(K_{-})

for all
K\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})
and rational one-dimensional subspaces
L
.

If
\dim K<2
, this is trivial. So, let
K
be two-dimensional.
First, we assume that
K
is pointed. Let
\can\coloneq\can(K)
and
\can_{\sigma}\coloneq\can(K_{\sigma})
for
\sigma\in\{-,+\}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{T}\coloneq\can_{-}\cup\can_{+}
is a unimodular decomposition of
K
. As such, it is obtained from
\can
by a finite sequence of elementary refinements using
Lemma

29
. Consider a unimodular cone
C\in\mathcal{T}
. By
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance, we may assume that
C=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
. If we show that

\zeta_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0})=\zeta_{0}(\pos\{e_{1},e_{1}+e_{2}\})+\zeta_{0}(\pos\{e_{2}+e_{1},e_{2}\}),

then we can use the
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance and apply
Lemma

29
inductively to obtain
Eq.

20
. Hence, by definition of
\zeta_{0}
, we need to verify that

(21)

R(x,y)=R(x,x+y)+R(x+y,y)

for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Writing
P_{0}\coloneq[0,1]^{2}
,
P_{1}\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}P_{0}
, and
P_{2}\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}P_{0}
, we have

\begin{split}R(x,x+y)=\frac{\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{0})(x,x+y)}{x(x+y)}=\frac{\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{1})}{x(x+y)}.\end{split}

Similarly, we obtain

R(x+y,y)=\frac{\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{2})}{(x+y)y}.

Thus,

R(x,x+y)+R(x+y,y)=\frac{x\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{2})+y\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{1})}{xy(x+y)}.

Let
T\coloneq\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}
. Using the decompositions

P_{0}=T\sqcup(-T+e_{1}+e_{2}),\,P_{1}=T\sqcup(-T+2e_{1}+e_{2}),\,P_{2}=(-T+e_{1}+e_{2})\sqcup(e_{2}+T),

we compute

\displaystyle x(\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{2})-\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{0}))

\displaystyle=x(\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(T+e_{2})-\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(T))

\displaystyle=xy\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}(T)

\displaystyle=y(-x\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}(-T+e_{1}+e_{2}))

\displaystyle=y(\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(-T+e_{1}+e_{2})-\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(-T+2e_{1}+e_{2}))

\displaystyle=y(\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{0})-\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{1})).

We have used that
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}
is simple by
Lemma

13
and that
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}
is odd and translation invariant by
Lemma

9
.
Hence,

x\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{2})+y\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{1})=(x+y)\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(P_{0})

and
Eq.

21
follows. So we obtain
Eq.

20
if
K
is a pointed unimodular cone.

Next, let
K
be a halfspace. We may assume that
L
intersects the interior of
K
. Then,
K_{+}
is unimodular if and only if
K_{-}
is unimodular. First, assume that the cones
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
are unimodular. By
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariance, it suffices to consider the case where
K
is the upper halfplane and
L
is the
y
-axis. In this case,
Eq.

20
is equivalent to the functional equation

(22)

0=R(x,y)+R(-x,y)

for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}([0,1]^{2})
is
\mathbf{D}
invariant,
Eq.

22
is fulfilled.

Next, we assume that
K_{+}
is a non-unimodular cone. We can choose a one-dimensional rational subspace
L^{\prime}
that meets the interior of
K_{+}
such that the cone
K_{+}^{\prime}
bounded by the boundary of
K
and
L^{\prime}
is unimodular. Let
K_{-}^{\prime}
be the closure of
K\setminus K_{+}^{\prime}
, which is a unimodular cone. Thus,

\zeta_{0}(K)=\zeta_{0}(K_{+}^{\prime})+\zeta_{0}(K_{-}^{\prime}).

Moreover,
K_{-}^{\prime}
is decomposed by the subspace
L
into the cones
K_{-}
and
K_{+}^{\prime\prime}
, where
K_{+}^{\prime\prime}
is the closure of
K_{+}\setminus K_{+}^{\prime}
. Since we already proved that
Eq.

20
holds for the pointed cone
K_{-}^{\prime}
we deduce

\zeta_{0}(K)=\zeta_{0}(K_{+}^{\prime})+\zeta_{0}(K_{+}^{\prime\prime})+\zeta_{0}(K_{-})=\zeta_{0}(K_{+})+\zeta_{0}(K_{-}),

which verifies
Eq.

20
for halfspaces. Since the statement is trivial for
K=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the proof is complete.
∎

Using
Theorem

28
, we obtain a simple,
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, and translatively exponential valuation

(23)

\zeta\colon\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Q}^{2})\to\mathcal{V},\quad\zeta(P)\coloneq\sum\nolimits_{v\in\operatorname{vert}(P)}\mathrm{e}^{v}\zeta_{0}(\fcone(v;P)).

Moreover, it follows from
\zeta_{0}(\fcone(v;P))\in\mathbb{R}(x,y)_{d}
that
\zeta
is
d
-dilative. Let us evaluate
\zeta
at the standard triangle
\mathbf{T}
.

Lemma 31
.

For
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}\in\mathrm{Val}_{2}^{d+2}
with
d>0
even, the valuation
\zeta
defined in
Eq.

23
satisfies

\begin{split}\zeta(\mathbf{T})&=2\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(\mathbf{T})\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{y}-1}{y(y-x)}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-1}{x(y-x)}\right)-\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}(\mathbf{T})\cdot\left(\frac{x(\mathrm{e}^{y}-1)}{y(y-x)}-\frac{y(\mathrm{e}^{x}-1)}{x(y-x)}\right)\\
&=2\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(\mathbf{T})\sum_{m>0}\frac{[m]_{x,y}}{(m+1)!}-\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}(\mathbf{T})\sum_{m>0}\frac{[m]_{x,y}-x^{m-1}-y^{m-1}}{m!},\end{split}

where
[m]_{x,y}\coloneq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}x^{j}y^{m-j}
and
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}\in\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{d+1}
is the associated valuation of
\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}
. In particular, the function
\zeta(\mathbf{T})
is analytic.

Proof.

We set
f_{2}:=\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(\mathbf{T})
and
f_{1}\coloneq\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{d+1}(\mathbf{T})
. Since
\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{d+i}
is simple for
i\in\{1,2\}
by
Lemma

13
, considering the triangulation
[0,1]^{2}=\mathbf{T}\sqcup(-\mathbf{T}+e_{1}+e_{2})
, we obtain

(24)

R(x,y)=\frac{2f_{2}(x,y)-(x+y)f_{1}(x,y)}{xy}.

For the standard triangle, we have

\fcone(0;\mathbf{T})=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0},~\fcone(e_{1};\mathbf{T})=\phi_{1}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0},~\text{and}~\fcone(e_{2};\mathbf{T})=\phi_{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0},

where
\phi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\
\phantom{-}1&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}
and

\phi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}0&\phantom{-}1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}
. Note that
\phi_{i}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}-e_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. So, in particular, we have
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathbf{G}
.

Using the representation
Eq.

24
of
R
and the
\mathbf{G}
invariance of
f_{1}
, we compute

\begin{split}R\circ\phi_{2}^{\star}&=\frac{1}{(x-y)(-y)}\left(2f_{2}\circ\phi_{2}^{\star}-(x-y+(-y))f_{1}\circ\phi_{2}^{\star}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{y(y-x)}\left(2\operatorname{Z}_{2}^{d+2}(\mathbf{T}-e_{2})-(x-2y)f_{1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{y(y-x)}\left(2(f_{2}-yf_{1})-(x-2y)f_{1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{y(y-x)}\left(2f_{2}-xf_{1}\right).\end{split}

Following the same lines, we compute

R\circ\phi_{1}^{\star}=\frac{1}{x(x-y)}\left(2f_{2}-yf_{1}\right).

So we obtain

\begin{split}\zeta(\mathbf{T})&=\mathrm{e}^{0}\zeta_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0})+\mathrm{e}^{x}\zeta_{0}(\phi_{1}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0})+\mathrm{e}^{y}\zeta_{0}(\phi_{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0})\\[4.0pt]
&=R+\mathrm{e}^{x}\,R\circ\phi_{1}^{\star}+\mathrm{e}^{y}\,R\circ\phi_{2}^{\star}\\[4.0pt]
&=\frac{2f_{2}-(x+y)f_{1}}{xy}+\mathrm{e}^{x}\,\frac{2f_{2}-yf_{1}}{x(x-y)}+\mathrm{e}^{y}\,\frac{2f_{2}-xf_{1}}{y(y-x)}\\
&=2f_{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{y}-1}{y(y-x)}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-1}{x(y-x)}\right)-f_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{x(\mathrm{e}^{y}-1)}{y(y-x)}-\frac{y(\mathrm{e}^{x}-1)}{x(y-x)}\right).\end{split}

This proves the first identity.

For the second identity, note that
\tfrac{e^{x}-1}{x}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\tfrac{x^{m}}{(m+1)!}
is an analytic function and that
y^{m}-x^{m}=(y-x)[m]_{x,y}
. For the first bracket term, we thus obtain

\frac{\mathrm{e}^{y}-1}{y(y-x)}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-1}{x(y-x)}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{y^{m}-x^{m}}{(m+1)!(y-x)}=\sum_{m>0}\frac{[m]_{x,y}}{(m+1)!}.

In particular, the left-hand side is an analytic function on all
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Similarly, we have for the second bracket term:

\begin{split}\frac{x(\mathrm{e}^{y}-1)}{y(y-x)}-\frac{y(\mathrm{e}^{x}-1)}{x(y-x)}&=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{xy^{m}-yx^{m}}{(m+1)!(y-x)}\\
&=-1+\sum_{m>0}\frac{xy(y^{m-1}-x^{m-1})}{(m+1)!(y-x)}=-1+\sum_{m>0}\frac{xy[m-1]_{x,y}}{(m+1)!}\\
&=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{[m+1]_{x,y}-x^{m}-y^{m}}{(m+1)!}=\sum_{m>0}\frac{[m]_{x,y}-x^{m-1}-y^{m-1}}{m!}.\end{split}

Again, the left-hand side is an analytic function on all
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

Corollary 33

The remaining cases

In this section, we consider the cases in
Theorem

2
and
Theorem

4
where the respective vector spaces are one-dimensional. The arguments used here essentially appear in
[
19
]
already; we recollect them to keep the paper self-contained.

Proposition 34
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,r+2\}
. If
r\leq i\leq r+2
or
r-i
is odd, then
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}=\spann\{\Lat_{i}^{r}\}
. Moreover,
\Lat_{i}^{r}
is not identically zero in these cases and
\Lat_{i}^{r}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0
unless
i=r+2
.

Proof.

We start by showing that
\Lat_{i}^{r}
for
r-i
odd is not identically zero on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{1})
and thus not identically zero on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
Using Faulhaber’s formula (see
[
19
]
for more information), we obtain for
m\geq 1
that

\Lat^{r}(m\,[0,e_{1}])(x,y)=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{k=0}^{m}k^{r}x^{r}=\frac{x^{r}}{r!(r+1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{r+1}{\ell}B_{\ell}\,m^{r+1-\ell},

where
B_{\ell}
denotes the
\ell
th Bernoulli number. Since
B_{\ell}\neq 0
for
\ell
even, this shows that
\Lat_{i}^{r}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0
for
r-i
odd.
Since also
B_{1}\neq 0
, we obtain the claim that
\Lat_{i}^{r}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0
holds for all
i
and
r
as in the proposition with
i\neq r+2
. Moreover,
\Lat_{r+2}^{r}
is not identically zero by
[
19
, Lemma 26]
.

Next, for
r=0
, it is easy to see that
\Lat^{0}_{i}
is not identically zero on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
for
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.
Thus, for
r\leq i\leq r+2
, using
Lemma

7
, we see that
\Lat_{i}^{r}
is not translation invariant since
\Lat_{i-r}^{0}
is among its associated functions. So, in particular,
\Lat_{i}^{r}\neq 0
. So, we have established
\Lat_{i}^{r}\neq 0
for the indices occurring in the proposition.

We note that
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{0}=\spann\{\Lat_{i}^{0}\}
by the Betke–Kneser theorem. For
r\geq 2
even, consider the one-homogeneous valuation
\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}
, which is translation invariant by
Lemma

9
. Define the valuation
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{1})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]_{r}
by
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(S)\coloneq\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}(S\times\{0\})
. By
[
19
, Lemma 5]
, we have
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}(S)\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{r}
. It is elementary to check that there is only one even, one-homogeneous, translatively polynomial valuation from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{1})
to
\mathbb{R}[x]_{r}
. Thus,
\operatorname{Z}^{\prime}=c\Lat_{1}^{r}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that the valuation
\operatorname{Y}\coloneq\operatorname{Z}_{1}^{r}-c\Lat_{1}^{r}
is simple. Lemma 24 from
[
19
]
yields that
\operatorname{Y}([0,1]^{2})=0
. Since we have
[0,1]^{2}=\mathbf{T}\sqcup(-\mathbf{T}+e_{1}+e_{2})
, it follows from the assumption that
r
is even, and the fact that
\operatorname{Y}
is simple, translation invariant, and
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant that
\operatorname{Y}(\mathbf{T})=0
. But this also implies
\operatorname{Y}=0
. So, we have established the statement for one-homogeneous valuations of even rank.

Next, suppose that
i=r=1
. Then,
\Lat_{1}^{1}
is called the
discrete Steiner point
and it follows from Theorem 5 of
[
8
]
that
\mathrm{Val}_{1}^{1}=\spann\{\Lat_{1}^{1}\}
. Hence, we proved the statement for
i=1
.

Finally, let
r>0
and
i>1
satisfy the proposition’s assumptions. Let
\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r}\in\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{r}
. Then,
r-1
and
i-1
also satisfy the assumptions. By induction on
i
, we have
\operatorname{Z}_{i-1}^{r-1}=c\Lat_{i-1}^{r-1}
. Since the associated functions are hereditary by
Lemma

5
, this means that

\operatorname{Z}_{i-j}^{r-j}=c\Lat_{i-j}^{r-j}

for all
0<j\leq\min\{r,i\}
.
Consequently,
\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r}-c\Lat_{i}^{r}
is translation invariant. Using the homogeneous decomposition theorem for translation invariant valuations
[
20
]
, we obtain that the polynomial
m\mapsto(\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r}-c_{i}\Lat_{i}^{r})(mP)
has degree 2. This yields
\operatorname{Z}_{i}^{r}-c\Lat_{i}^{r}=0
as desired, except for
i=2
. But then the claim follows from
[
19
, Proposition 23]
.
∎

For
d\geq-2
, we set
\bLat_{d}\coloneq\sum_{r\geq 0}\Lat_{r-d}^{r}
. By
Lemma

24
and
Lemma

25
, we have
\bLat_{d}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
.
Proposition

34
allows us to determine the space
\mathrm{Val}_{d}
for
d
odd or non-positive.

Corollary 35
.

If
d>0
is odd or
d\in\{-2,-1,0\}
, then
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}=\spann\{\bLat_{d}\}
. Moreover,
\bLat_{d}([0,e_{1}])
is not identically zero in these cases and
\bLat_{d}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0

Proof.

The fact that
\bLat_{d}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0
for

or

odd follows from
\Lat_{1}^{d+1}([0,e_{1}])\neq 0
.
To see that
\bLat_{-2}

, the two-dimensional volume.

Let
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
and consider for

its

-summand
\operatorname{Z}^{r}
. By

25
, we have
\operatorname{Z}^{r}\in\mathrm{Val}_{r-d}^{r}
. By the assumption on

applies, so there exists a constant

\operatorname{Z}^{r}=c_{r}\Lat_{r-d}^{r}

are associated functions of one another. Since also

∎

10.
Consequences

We collect some consequences of our results.

Minkowski additive functions

Theorem

3
can be rewritten as a classification of Minkowski additive functions on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. A function
\operatorname{Z}
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
with values in a vector space is
Minkowski additive
if

\operatorname{Z}(P+Q)=\operatorname{Z}(P)+\operatorname{Z}(Q)

for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, where
P+Q=\{v+w\colon v\in P,\,w\in Q\}
is the Minkowski sum of
P
and
Q
. Since

P+Q=(P\cup Q)+(P\cap Q)

for
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
such that also
P\cap Q,P\cup Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, every Minkowski additive function is a valuation, which is easily seen to be one-homogeneous. We remark that every translation invariant, one-homogeneous valuation is Minkowski additive (cf.
[
20
, Theorem 6]
).

Corollary 36
.

A function
\operatorname{Z}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}[x,y]
is translation invariant,
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant, and Minkowski additive if and only if there are
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and constants
c_{r},c_{k,\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
with
r,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\operatorname{Z}(P)=\sum_{r=0}^{m}c_{2r}\Lat_{1}^{2r}(P)+\sum_{r=0}^{3}c_{2r+1}\Lat_{1}^{2r+1}(P)+\sum_{r=4}^{m}\sum_{\,\,2k+3\ell=2r+1}c_{k,\ell}\Lat_{1}^{2k,3\ell}(P)

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

Here, we have used again that
p_{2,3}\left(r\right)=1
for
r\in\{3,5,7\}
.

Simple, translatively exponential valuations

By
Lemma

13
, each valuation in
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{d+i}
is simple, if
d>0
is even. Hence,
Lemma

24
implies that each valuation in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
is also simple. In fact, for each
i
, the
i
-summand
\operatorname{Z}_{i}
of a valuation
\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
is in
\mathrm{Val}_{i}^{d+i}
and thus simple by
Eq.

17
.

If
d\geq-2
is
not
a positive and even number, then, by
Corollary

35
, we have
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}=\spann\{\bLat_{d}\}
. If
d=-2
, the valuation
\bLat_{(-2)}
is, by definition, the sum of the top-degree components
\Lat_{r+2}^{r}
in
Eq.

1
. Since these correspond to the (continuous) moments by
[
19
, Lemma 26]
, we have

(26)

\bLat_{(-2)}(P)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\Lat_{r+2}^{r}(P)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{1}{r!}\int_{P}v^{r}\mathrm{d}v=\int_{P}\mathrm{e}^{v}\mathrm{d}v.

This valuation is called
the
exponential valuation in
[
2
]
. We introduced it as
\operatorname{\overline{E}}(P)
in the introduction. It is the Laplace transform of the indicator function of
-P
(see
[
18
]
). For our purposes, it is interesting to observe that
Eq.

26
implies that
\bLat_{(-2)}
is simple.

For
d>-2
odd and for
d=0
, the valuations
\bLat_{d}
are not simple by
Corollary

35
. Since also their first non-zero
r
-summand is not simple by
Proposition

34
, it follows that no sum of the valuations
\bLat_{d}
, where
d
is odd or zero, is simple. Hence, we obtain the following characterization of simple, translatively exponential,
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuations from
Theorem

4
.

Corollary 37
.

We have

\{\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\in\overline{\mathrm{Val}}:\operatorname{\overline{Z}}\text{ is simple}\}=\prod_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geq-2,\\
d\text{ even},\,d\neq 0\end{subarray}}}\,\,\,\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}.

Thus, the space of simple, translatively exponential,
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
equivariant valuations consists of the valuations we constructed in
Section

7
together with
\bLat_{(-2)}
. However, one should not regard
\bLat_{(-2)}
as an “exceptional” valuation. In fact, it can be obtained in the same way as the valuations in
\overline{\mathrm{Val}}_{d}
with
d>0
even by choosing
R(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{xy}
in
Eq.

18
and using the previous construction from there on. For this particular case, it is also described in
[
2
, Chapter 8]
how to evaluate
\bLat_{(-2)}(P)
based on an extension to rational polyhedra.
