Functional Time Series Forecasting: Functional Singular Spectrum Analysis Approaches Contributed equally as much as first author
Jordan Trinka

Abstract
In this paper, we propose two nonparametric methods used in the forecasting of functional time-dependent data, namely functional singular spectrum analysis recurrent forecasting and vector forecasting. Both algorithms utilize the results of functional singular spectrum analysis and past observations in order to predict future data points where recurrent forecasting predicts one function at a time and the vector forecasting makes predictions using functional vectors. We compare our forecasting methods to a gold standard algorithm used in the prediction of functional, time-dependent data by way of simulation and real data and we find our techniques do better for periodic stochastic processes.



Keywords: Singular Spectrum Analysis, Functional Time Series, Hilbert Space, Forecasting

1
Introduction

Functional data analysis (FDA) is a growing field of statistics that is showing promising results in analysis due to the fact that functional algorithms act on possibly more informative and smooth data. Often times statistical techniques that act on real-valued scalars or vectors are extended into the functional realm to handle such curved data. One example is principal component analysis (PCA) which was extended into functional PCA (FPCA) and multivariate FPCA so that dimension reduction may be performed on time-independent functional observations and many variants of these methods have been developed, see
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
,
Jeng-Min et al., 2014
, and
Happ and Greven, 2018
for more details. Another example of this concept can be seen in singular spectrum analysis (SSA)
(
Golyandina et al., 2001
)
which is a decomposition technique for time series. The SSA algorithm was extended into functional SSA (FSSA) in
Haghbin et al., 2020a
. They showed that the FSSA algorithm outperforms SSA and FPCA-based approaches in separating out sources of variation for smooth, time-dependent, functional data which is defined as a functional time series (FTS). In addition to SSA being extended to FSSA, the multivariate SSA (MSSA) approaches
(
Golyandina et al., 2015
;
Hassani and Mahmoudvand, 2013
)
have also been extended to the functional realm in
Trinka et al., 2020
where modeling done on a multivariate FTS of intraday temperature curves and images of vegetation in a joint analysis gave more prominent results.

An important problem often confronted by researchers is prediction of stochastic processes.
Golyandina et al., 2001
expanded the results of the SSA and MSSA algorithms to deliver two commonly used nonparametric techniques in forecasting, called (a) SSA recurrent forecasting, and (b) SSA vector forecasting algorithms. Since the aforementioned SSA techniques have seen success in forecasting time series data, one may seek expanding that to the functional world for forecasting FTS.

One of the first approaches to FTS forecasting is given in
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
who found success in predicting mortality rates data. The method of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
has been extended so that more recent FTS observations play a larger role in forecasts
(
Hyndman and Shang, 2009
)
. In addition, extensions have been made so that the method is robust in the presence of outliers
(
Shang, 2019
;
Beyaztas and Shang, 2019
)
. The approach of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
has also inspired a functional extension to the ARMAX model
González et al., 2018
allowing for estimation of moving average terms. In addition, the methodology of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
and its variants have seen success in applications other than just mortality rate data, see
Shang, 2013
and
Wagner-Muns et al., 2018
.

The approach of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
and its extensions consists of two steps. In the first step, they use FPCA, or its variants, to reduce the dimensionality of the functional data and project the curves onto the FPCA basis. In the second step, they perform forecasting of the basis coefficient using various techniques such as ARIMA model. The details of the technique of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
are given in the supplementary material. One may argue the time-dependency is not considered in the first step of their algorithm. In this paper we develop two forecasting algorithms based on FSSA that can incorporates the time-dependency into the decomposition of FTS. Furthermore, the proposed algorithms do not need the stationary assumption.

2
Foundations of FSSA

We begin with some notations leveraged in the FSSA routine
(
Haghbin et al., 2020a
)
. We consider
\mathbf{y}_{N}=\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\right)
as a FTS of length
N
such that each
y_{i}:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
belongs to
\mathbb{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\left[0,1\right]\right)
where
\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\left[0,1\right]\right)
is the space of square integrable real functions defined on the interval
\left[0,1\right]
. For some
x,y
in the Hilbert space,
\mathbb{H}
, we have the that the inner product equipped to
\mathbb{H}
is given by
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\int_{0}^{1}x\left(s\right)y\left(s\right)ds
. For a positive integer
m
, the space,
\mathbb{H}^{m}
, denotes the Cartesian product of
m
copies of
\mathbb{H}
; such that any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}^{m}
has the form
\boldsymbol{x}\left(s\right)=\left(x_{1}\left(s_{1}\right),x_{2}\left(s_{2}\right),\dots,x_{m}\left(s_{m}\right)\right)^{\top}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{H}
,
\mathbf{s}=\left(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{m}\right)
and
s_{i}\in\left[0,1\right]
. For some
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
in the Hilbert space,
\mathbb{H}^{m}
, we define the inner product as
\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle x_{i},y_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. We denote the norms by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}^{m}}
in the spaces
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{m}
respectively. Given
x,y\in\mathbb{H}
, then the tensor (outer) product of these two elements gives us the operator
x\otimes y:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
where for some
h\in\mathbb{H}
, we have
\left(x\otimes y\right)h=\left\langle x,h\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}y
.

For positive integers
L
and
K
, we denote
\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
as the linear space spanned by operators
\mathbfcal{Z}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{L}
, specified by
\left[z_{i,j}\right]_{i=1,\dots,L}^{j=1,\dots,K}
where

\mathbfcal{Z}\boldsymbol{a}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{K}a_{j}z_{1,j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{K}a_{j}z_{L,j}\end{pmatrix},\quad z_{i,j}\in\mathbb{H},\text{ and }\boldsymbol{a}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{K}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

We call an operator
\tilde{\mathbfcal{Z}}=\left[\tilde{z}_{i,j}\right]\in\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
Hankel if
\|\tilde{z}_{i,j}-g_{s}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=0
for some
g_{s}\in\mathbb{H}
, where
s=i+j
. The space of such Hankel operators will be denoted with
\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}
. For two given operators
\mathbfcal{Z}_{1}=\left[z_{i,j}^{\left(1\right)}\right]_{i=1,\dots,L}^{j=1,\dots,K}
and
\mathbfcal{Z}_{2}=\left[z_{i,j}^{\left(2\right)}\right]_{i=1,\dots,L}^{j=1,\dots,K}
in
\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
, define

\left\langle\mathbfcal{Z}_{1},\mathbfcal{Z}_{2}\right\rangle_{\mathbfcal{F}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left\langle z_{i,j}^{\left(1\right)},z_{i,j}^{\left(2\right)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

It follows immediately that
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathbfcal{F}}
, defines an inner product on
\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
. We will call it Frobenius inner product of two operators in
\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
. The associated Frobenius norm is
\|\mathbfcal{Z}\|_{\mathbfcal{F}}=\sqrt{\left\langle\mathbfcal{Z},\mathbfcal{Z}\right\rangle_{\mathbfcal{F}}}
. Now we jump into the FSSA procedure with the goal of extracting time-dependent modes of variation that can be used for forecasting.

2.1
Review of the FSSA Algorithm

For an integer
1<L<N/2
, let
K=N-L+1
and define a set of multivariate functional vectors in
\mathbb{H}^{L}
by

\boldsymbol{x}_{j}=\left(y_{j},y_{j+1},\dots,y_{j+L-1}\right),\quad j=1,\dots,K,

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
’s denote the functional
L
-lagged vectors. We also have that
L
should be chosen to be a multiple of the periodicity present in the data
(
Haghbin et al., 2020a
)
. We now continue with the FSSA methodology.

Step 1. Embedding

In this first step we form the trajectory operator,
\mathbfcal{X}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{L}
, which is defined by

\mathbfcal{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}a_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\quad\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

(1)

We see that the rank of
\mathbfcal{X}
is
0<r\leq K
and that the range of this operator includes all possible linear combinations of the functional
L
-lagged vectors. This step of embedding can also be viewed as applying the invertible operation
\mathbfcal{T}:\mathbb{H}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}
such that
\mathbfcal{T}\left(\mathbf{y}_{N}\right)=\mathbfcal{X}
.

Step 2. Decomposition

Since
\mathbfcal{X}
is of finite rank, then there exists orthonormal elements
\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
from
\mathbb{H}^{L}
and orthonormal vectors
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
from
\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that

\mathbfcal{X}\boldsymbol{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\boldsymbol{a}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i},\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},

where
\lambda_{i}
’s are non-ascending positive scalars. We refer to the previous equation as the functional singular value decomposition (fSVD) of
\mathbfcal{X}
. The
\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
singular value,
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
left singular function, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
right singular vector of the trajectory operator. Therefore we call the collection
\{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}
as the
i^{\text{th}}
eigentriple of
\mathbfcal{X}
. One can write
\mathbfcal{X}
as

\mathbfcal{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}.

Step 3. Grouping

We refer the readers to
Haghbin et al., 2020a
for the general discussion in the grouping step. In this work, we combine rank one operators resulted from fSVD of
\mathbfcal{X}
together for reconstructing the signal. Consider
\mathfrak{S}
to be a subset of indices,
\{1,\dots,r\}
, and define
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
. This allows us to write

\mathbfcal{X}=\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}+\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{n}},

where
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{n}}=\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}^{c}}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
is the residual component, and
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}
represents the signal component.

Step 4. Reconstruction

In this step, we would like to use
\mathbfcal{T}^{-1}:\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{N}
to transform back
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}
to a FTS,
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}\in\mathbb{H}^{N}
, that captures the deterministic nature of
\mathbf{y}_{N}
. To do this, we first perform an orthogonal projection of
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}
onto
\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}
, which is a closed subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}
, with respect to the Frobenius norm. We denote the elements of
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}
and the corresponding orthogonal projection,
\tilde{\mathbfcal{X}}\in\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}
, with
\left[x_{i,j}^{\mathfrak{s}}\right]
and
\left[\tilde{x}_{i,j}\right]
, respectively. Then, using the results of
Haghbin et al., 2020a
we have that

\tilde{x}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{\left(k,l\right):k+l=s}x_{k,l}^{\mathfrak{s}},

where
s=i+j
and
n_{s}
stands for the number of
\left(l,k\right)
pairs such that
l+k=s
. We denote this projection by
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{H}:\mathbb{H}^{L\times K}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{H}^{L\times K}
such that

\tilde{\mathbfcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{H}\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}.

(2)

Similar to (
1
), the operators
\tilde{\mathbfcal{X}}
and
\mathbfcal{X}_{\mathfrak{s}}
can be written as linear combinations of functional
L
-lagged vectors. We denote those lag-vectors as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathfrak{s}}_{j}
respectively. From (
2
), we find that
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}=\mathbfcal{T}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbfcal{X}}=\left(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{N}\right)
.

As an immediate result, we have that the residual elements of the signal,
\mathbf{y}_{N}
, are captured in
\mathbf{y}_{N}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}
and we typically do not include these noisy modes of variation in the SSA-based forecasts, see
Golyandina et al., 2001
;
Golyandina and Zhigljavsky, 2013
;
Golyandina et al., 2015
;
Hassani and Mahmoudvand, 2013
for more information.

3
FSSA Forecasting

The following contains some notation that will be leveraged throughout the rest of this Section and Section
4
. For each functional vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}^{L}
, denote by
\boldsymbol{x}^{\nabla}\in\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta}\in\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
as the functional vectors consisting of the first and the last (respectively)
L-1
components of the vector
\boldsymbol{x}
. Setting
k<r
, let us define
\mathbb{L}=\text{sp}\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
\mathbb{L}^{\nabla}=\text{sp}\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Moreover, let
\pi_{i}\in\mathbb{H}
be the last component of the functional vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
. With this notation, we may continue into the theory of forecasting.

Here, we offer an important theorem and a corollary that will be leveraged in the FSSA recurrent and vector forecasting algorithms. All proofs of presented theory can be found in the supplementary materials.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{E}:=\{\left(\underline{0},\dots,\underline{0},x\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{L}|x\in\mathbb{H}\}
and define the operator
\mathbfcal{V}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
such that
\mathbfcal{V}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi_{i}\otimes\pi_{i}
. If
\mathbb{E}\cap\mathbb{L}=\emptyset
, then we have that

\|\mathbfcal{V}\|=\underset{\|x\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}{\sup}\|\mathbfcal{V}\left(x\right)\|_{\mathbb{H}}<1,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm.

Corollary 3.1
.

Given that the conditions of Theorem
3.1
are met, we have that
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathbfcal{V}^{l}
is a convergent Neumann series and that
\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}
exists. Furthermore, we obtain the equality

\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathbfcal{V}^{l},

where
\mathbfcal{I}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is the identity operator.

The primary assumption seen in Theorem
3.1
is that for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}
, we must have
\boldsymbol{z}\not\in\mathbb{L}
. Now we define
\mathbf{g}_{N+M}=\left(g_{1},\dots,g_{N},g_{N+1},\dots,g_{N+M}\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{N+M}
as a FTS of length
N+M
, where in the following algorithms (recurrent and vector forecasting), the first
N
elements,
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, are close to
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{N}
, and the main goal is to predict the last
M
terms (
g_{N+1}
to
g_{M}
).

3.1
FSSA Recurrent Forecasting Algorithm

The SSA recurrent forecasting approach of
Golyandina et al., 2001
leverages the fact that the next forecasted value of a reconstructed time series can be expressed as a linear combination of the previous
L-1
elements. When we migrate to the functional realm, we move towards linear combinations of functions where the coefficient scalar weights are replaced with operators that perform the weighting. With this in mind, we now give the following theorem which we will employ in the FSSA recurrent and vector forecasting algorithms.

Theorem 3.2
.

Given that the conditions of Theorem
3.1
are met, for any
\boldsymbol{y}=\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{L}\right)\in\mathbb{L}
, the last component,
y_{L}
, can be expressed by a linear combination of the previous
L-1
components:

y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}y_{j},

where
\mathbfcal{A}_{j}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
is an operator given by
\mathbfcal{A}_{j}=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\psi_{j,n}\otimes\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\pi_{n}
, and
\psi_{j,n}\in\mathbb{H}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
component of the
n^{\text{th}}
left singular function.

One may use the result of Theorem
3.2
, and the background of the recurrent SSA algorithm given in
Golyandina et al., 2001
to present the following algorithm.

Algorithm A - FSSA Recurrent Forecasting:

g_{i}=\begin{cases}\tilde{y}_{i}&i=1,\dots,N\\
\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}g_{i+j-L}&i=N+1,\dots,N+M\end{cases}.

For brevity, we call this algorithm
FSSA R-forecasting algorithm
.

3.2
FSSA Vector Forecasting Algorithm

The R-forecasting can be extended into an algorithm known as vector forecasting that allows us to perform prediction using functional
L
-lagged vectors. We first define the operator,
\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
such that for any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\left(\boldsymbol{a}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\nabla}.

It is easy to see the adjoint operator can be obtained as
\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}:\mathbb{H}^{L-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
, such that for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
, we have

\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\end{bmatrix}.

For a given vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{L}
, the goal of vector forecasting is to find a linear operator
\mathbfcal{Q}:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
, such that for some
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbfcal{Q}\boldsymbol{x}
, the distance between the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{\nabla}
is minimal. The following Proposition can be used to obtain such a
\boldsymbol{y}^{\nabla}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given that the conditions of Theorem
3.1
are met, the operator
\mathbf{\Pi}
defined as

\boldsymbol{\Pi}:=\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\Bigl(\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\Bigr)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}

is an orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
onto
\mathbb{L}^{\nabla}
.

As an immediate result of Proposition
3.1
, we have
\boldsymbol{y}^{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta}
. Now, using Theorem
3.2
, one may we obtain the last component of
\boldsymbol{y}
(i.e.,
y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}y^{\nabla}_{j}
).
The following Proposition further simplify this expression.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, we have

y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}{x}^{\Delta}_{j}.

Finally, one may derive the linear operator
\mathbfcal{Q}
as following:

\mathbfcal{Q}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right):=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta}\right)\\
\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{L}.

(4)

Algorithm B - FSSA Vector Forecasting:

1.

Define the functional
L
-lagged vectors

\boldsymbol{w}_{j}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathfrak{s}}_{j}&j=1,\dots,K\\
\mathbfcal{Q}\boldsymbol{w}_{j-1}&j=K+1,\dots,K+M\end{cases}

2.

Form the operator
\mathbfcal{W}\in\mathbb{H}^{L\times\left(K+M\right)}
whose range is linearly spanned by the set
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K+M}
.

3.

Hankelize
\mathbfcal{W}
in order to extract the FTS
\mathbf{g}_{N+M}
.

4.

The functions
g_{N+1},\cdots,g_{N+M}
form the
M
terms of the FSSA vector forecast.

From here, we may refer to this algorithm as
FSSA V-forecasting algorithm
. In the next Section, we present computer implementation of both algorithms.

4
Implementation Strategy

In practice, functional data are being recorded discretely and then converted to functional objects using proper smoothing techniques. We refer to
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
for more details on preprocessing the raw data. Here, we start with some notation that will be leveraged in the implementation of the R-forecasting and V-forecasting algorithms. We assume that
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
is a linearly independent basis for
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, which is a
d
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{H}
. For any
f\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
there exists a unique vector,
\mathbf{c}_{f}=(c_{f,1},\cdots,c_{f,d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where

f=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{c}_{f,i}\nu_{i}.

We refer to
\mathbf{c}_{f}
as the corresponding coefficients of
f
. Let
\mathbf{G}=\left[\left\langle\nu_{i},\nu_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{d}
to be the
d\times d
Gram matrix.

From hereafter, we consider all discussed operators whose domain or range are infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces to operate on or map to the corresponding
d
-dimensional subspace. Since the FSSA R-forecasting and V-forecasting algorithms are both dependent on
\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}
, we explore one particular implementation of this operator in the following lemma that will be leveraged in the recipes for R-forecasting and V-forecasting.

Lemma 4.1
.

Define
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\mathbf{c}_{\pi_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{\pi_{k}}\right]
to be a
d\times k
matrix. The following holds:

\left[\left\langle\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i=1,\dots,d}^{n=1,\dots,k}=\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l}\right).

Remark
: Note that since
\|\mathbfcal{V}\|<1
, the real-valued sequence,
\left(\|\mathbfcal{V}^{l}\|\right)_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
, converges to zero monotonically. As a result, we truncate
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l}
when

As according to (

4.2
FSSA V-Forecasting Algorithm

In order to obtain the recipes for the FSSA V-forecasting algorithm, we first leverage theory developed in
Haghbin et al., 2020a
. For some positive integer,
L
, we define
\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L}
to be the space created from the Cartesian product of
L

\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Now we define the quotient-remainder sequence

j=\left(q_{j}-1\right)L+r_{j},\quad 1\leq q_{j}\leq d,\quad 1\leq r_{j}\leq L,

and from here we define the collection of linearly independent elements
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{Ld}
where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L}
is the zero function in all coordinates except for the
r_{j}^{\text{th}}
which is
\nu_{q_{j}}

\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{Ld}

\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L}

\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L}
can be represented via its corresponding coefficient vector
\mathbf{c}_{\boldsymbol{f}}=(c_{\boldsymbol{f},1},\cdots,c_{\boldsymbol{f},Ld})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Ld}

\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{Ld}{c}_{\boldsymbol{f},i}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}
. We denote the updated Gram matrix with
\mathbf{H}=\left[\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{Ld}

i=\left(q_{i}-1\right)(L-1)+r_{i},\quad 1\leq q_{i}\leq d,\quad 1\leq r_{i}\leq L-1.

This allows us to define
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\nabla}\}_{i=1}^{\left(L-1\right)d}
where
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\nabla}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L-1}

which is
\nu_{q_{i}}

\mathbb{H}_{d}

\boldsymbol{f}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{f}^{\Delta}\in\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L-1}

\{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\nabla}\}_{i=1}^{\left(L-1\right)d}

\mathbf{H}^{\nabla}=\left[\left\langle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{\left(L-1\right)d}

Now we must find the matrix that implements
\boldsymbol{\Pi}:\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{d}^{L-1}

Haghbin et al., 2020a

5
Numerical Studies

In this section, we offer a simulation study and real data examples that showcase the advantage of our novel methods in forecasting periodic FTS. In the simulation study, we compare our approaches with the functional seasonal naive method and we also compare to the popular FTS forecasting technique of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
, which we now call the competing method. We incorporate either seasonality, increasing trend, or both components in various simulation setups. We further illustrate the superior performance in forecasting of a periodic FTS with a real data study where we compare our techniques with the competing method when the optimal parameters are selected for each algorithm applied to highly periodic call center data, moderately periodic remote sensing data, and mortality rate data that has no periodic components. We note that when we state that we are using
k
eigentriples in forecasts, that means we use
k
left singular functions in the prediction.

5.1
Simulation Study

For the simulation study, we use a setup that is similar to that seen in
Haghbin et al., 2020a
and
Trinka et al., 2020

We use a B-spline basis with 15 degrees of freedom to smooth the discrete samplings of functional curves. We have that

We choose

10

20

Golyandina et al., 2001

Haghbin et al., 2020a

Trinka et al., 2020

\kappa

\omega

\omega=0.20

6
Discussion

In this work, we developed efficient nonparametric FTS forecasting techniques that include periodicity into the basis elements. We compared our approaches with the competing method of
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
and found our techniques are superior in forecasting periodic FTS since the FSSA routine captures periodic behavior in the basis elements. As a result researchers can use our methodologies in order to obtain accurate and informative predictions of periodic stochastic processes.

where
\mathbf{I}_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix and
\mathbf{B}=\left[\left\langle\pi_{i},\pi_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}
. To this end,
\left(\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{-1}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{B}\right)^{-1}
. We define
\mathbf{C}=\left[\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}
and now multiply
\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}\right)=\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{B}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}\right)=\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{B}
. Now notice that

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{i},\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\pi_{n},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\left\langle\pi_{i},\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right),\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

Since
\mathbb{E}\cap\mathbb{L}=\emptyset
, we have that
\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathbfcal{V}^{l}
is a linear operator and this allows us to state that

\displaystyle\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\left\langle\pi_{i},\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right),\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{j},\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\pi_{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{V}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)-\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{V}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi_{i},\pi_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbf{B}_{i,j}

and thus, we have
\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
which shows that
\left(\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{-1}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}\right)
. Notice that this also implies that
\mathbf{C}
is symmetric and we will use this fact in the following. Now let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}^{L-1}
and notice that

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\Pi}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right),\boldsymbol{u}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\left(\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\right)^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right),\boldsymbol{u}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{1},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{k},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\end{bmatrix},\boldsymbol{u}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\right)\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{u}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}.

Since
\mathbf{C}
is symmetric we may state that
\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\left\langle\pi_{j},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. As a result, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{i},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{j}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\right)\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{u}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{j},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\right)\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{1},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{k},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\Pi}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}.

Thus
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
is self-adjoint and an orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{L}^{\nabla}
.

∎

Proof of Prop.

3.2

Recall that for some
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{L}
we have
\boldsymbol{y}^{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta}
. Now we wish to show that the last component of
\boldsymbol{y}
, defined as
y_{L}
, has the expression
y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x_{j}^{\Delta}
. Using Theorem
3.2
and the definition of
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
, notice that

\displaystyle y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}y_{j}^{\nabla}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\left(\mathbf{I}_{k}+\mathbf{C}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\end{bmatrix}\right)_{j}=u+v

where

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\psi_{j,n}\right)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}\left(\mathbfcal{P}^{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{1},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\\

Now notice that

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\sum_{n=1}^{k}\psi_{j,n}\otimes\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\psi_{j,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{i,n}-\left\langle\pi_{i},\pi_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L-1}\left\langle x^{\Delta}_{l_{1}},\psi_{l_{1},i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\mathbfcal{V}\left(\pi_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{l_{2}}x^{\Delta}_{l_{2}}-\mathbfcal{V}\left(\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{l_{1}}x^{\Delta}_{l_{1}}\right).

In addition, notice that

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}\left(\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{2}}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{2}}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{1}}^{\nabla}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{3}=1}^{k}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{2}}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{2}}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{n_{1},n_{3}}-\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\pi_{n_{3}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{2}}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{2}}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{1}}\right)-\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\mathbfcal{V}\left(\pi_{n_{1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{2}}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{2}}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)\left(\pi_{n_{1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbfcal{V}\left(\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{k}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\Delta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n_{2}}^{\nabla}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{L-1}}\left(\mathbfcal{I}-\mathbfcal{V}\right)^{-1}\left(\pi_{n_{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbfcal{V}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}\right).

As a result, we find that

y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}y^{\nabla}_{j}=u+v=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x_{j}^{\Delta}-\mathbfcal{V}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}\right)+\mathbfcal{V}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}.

Thus we see that
y_{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}y^{\nabla}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\mathbfcal{A}_{j}x^{\Delta}_{j}
.

Proof of Lemma.

4.1

First, we notice that

\left[\left\langle\mathbfcal{V}^{l}\left(\pi_{n}\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i=1,\dots,d}^{n=1,\dots,k}=\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l}
. For our base case, let

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbfcal{V}\left(\pi_{n}\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{n},\pi_{n_{1}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\left(\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)_{i,n}.

From here, we state our inductive assumption that
\left[\left\langle\mathbfcal{V}^{l}\left(\pi_{n}\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right]_{i=1,\dots,d}^{n=1,\dots,k}=\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l}

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbfcal{V}^{l+1}\left(\pi_{n}\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{k}\cdots\sum_{n_{l+1}=1}^{k}\left\langle\pi_{n},\pi_{n_{l+1}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\pi_{n_{l+1}},\pi_{n_{l}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\cdots\left\langle\pi_{n_{2}},\pi_{n_{1}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\left\langle\pi_{n_{1}},\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l+1}\right)_{i,n}=\left(\mathbf{G}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\left(\mathbf{D}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\right)^{l}\right)_{i,n}=\left\langle\mathbfcal{V}\left(\mathbfcal{V}^{l}\left(\pi_{n}\right)\right),\nu_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

which shows the desired result. As such, we see that

Approach of Hyndman and Ullah to FTS Forecasting

The approach
Hyndman and Ullah, 2007
leverages a weighted FPCA of a FTS to find basis elements that explain variation. They then project their time dependent data onto the basis in order to find scores for each principal component. Finally, they perform forecasting techniques such as ARIMA on the resulting principal component scores in order to predict future observations of the FTS. The following steps outline the process.

1.

Smooth the FTS using a nonparametric smoothing method to estimate
f_{t}\left(x\right)
for
x\in\left[x_{1},x_{p}\right]
from sampling points
\{x_{i},y_{t}\left(x_{i}\right)\}
,
i=1,\dots,p
where
f_{t}\left(x\right)
is a function observed on day
t
and
p
is the number of sampling points

2.

Decompose the fitted observations using a weighted FPCA to obtain the basis expansion of

f_{t}\left(x\right)=\mu\left(x\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{t,k}\phi_{k}\left(x\right)+e_{t}\left(x\right)

where
\mu\left(x\right)
is the mean function,
\{\phi_{k}\left(x\right)\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a set of orthonormal basis functions (notice we truncate at
K
basis elements),
e_{t}\left(x\right)\sim\text{N}\left(0,\nu\left(x\right)\right)
, and
\nu\left(x\right)
is the covariance function

3.

Fit univariate time series models, such as ARIMA, to each of the coefficients
\{\beta_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}

4.

Forecast the coefficients
\{\beta_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
for
t=n+1,\dots,n+h
using the fitted time series models where
n
is the length of the FTS and
h
is the forecast horizon

5.

Multiply the forecasted coefficients by their respective
k^{\text{th}}
basis elements to forecast
\{f_{n+1}\left(x\right),\dots,f_{n+h}\left(x\right)\}.
