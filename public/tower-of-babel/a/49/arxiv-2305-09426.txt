Fluctuations of the energy density and intensity for arbitrary objects in an arbitrary environment
Florian Herz ∗

Abstract
I apply the scattering approach within the framework of macroscopic quantum electrodynamics to derive the variances and mean values of the energy density and intensity for a system of an arbitrary object in an arbitrary environment. To evaluate the temporal bunching character of the energy density and intensity, I determine the ratio of their variances with respect to their mean values. I explicitly evaluate these ratios for the cases of vacuum, a half-space in vacuum, and a sphere in vacuum. Eventually, I extend the applicability of this theory to the case of more than one arbitrary object, independent of the geometrical shapes and materials.



I
Introduction

In most works on near-field thermal radiation, theory and experiment focus on the analysis of coherence properties of the first order, e.g. the heat flux or mean Poynting vector. In contrast, higher order coherence properties for thermal near-field radiation, for instance, the variance of the energy density or the heat flux are only scarcely investigated. From an experimental point of view, the variance or fluctuations around the mean values can only be monitored with improved ultra fast measurement methods. In a theoretical description, this demands the additional assumption of the Gaussian property for thermal radiation in the near-field regime to be able to evaluate the corresponding correlation functions within fluctuational electrodynamics. This property was used to calculate the variance of the Casimir-Lifshitz force
Barton 1991
;
Kogan 2005
;
Messina and Passante 2007
and the vacuum friction
Zurita-Sánchez et al. 2004
in the near-field. In recent years, also the fluctuations of thermal quantities moved into the focus of interest, especially when evaluating their impact on experiments, in which the spectral information is lost while measuring the heat currents
Wise et al. 2022
, for instance. There are also works on the variance of the mean Poynting vector between two planar media
Biehs and Ben-Abdallah 2018
. A very important application of theses higher order correlation functions are Green-Kubo relations which connect the linear transport coefficients of a system out of thermal equilibrium with the equilibrium fluctuations of the corresponding quantity
Golyk et al. 2013
;
Herz and Biehs 2019
.

The first order spatial coherence property of thermal radiation is well studied in the far- and near-field regime. For instance, for half-spaces it was shown that the coherence length strongly depends on the chosen material. If it supports surface waves, the coherence length can be much larger than the well-known
\lambda/2
of black-body radiation but if it does not support them, the coherence lengths can be much shorter
Carminati and Greffet 1999
. This was later validated by discussing the contributions of surface waves, skin-layer currents, and small-scale polarization fluctuations to the cross-spectral density tensor
Henkel et al. 2000
as well as by analyzing the energy density with respect to surface waves
Shchegrov et al. 2000
. For periodically micro-structured SiC and photonic crystals this can be exploited to confine the emission angles to build an infrared antenna
Greffet et al. 2002
;
Laroche et al. 2005
;
Laroche et al. 2006
.

For thermal radiation, the expectation value defined by Glauber
Glauber 1963
has to be evaluated by using the density matrix formalism because it is a mixed state due to the broad range of frequencies involved. Then, these expectation values can be treated by macroscopic quantum electrodynamics (MQED) formalism introduced by Scheel and Buhmann
Scheel and Buhmann 2008
. For some dielectric materials like SiC, the near-field spectrum becomes quasi-monochromatic due to the resonance at the surface phonon polariton (SPhP) frequency. Such a change of the spectrum from broadband in the far-field to quasi-monochromatic in the near-field makes it interesting to investigate the bunching property of the thermal near-field radiation. By employing the scattering approach introduced by Rahi et al.
Rahi et al. 2009
and Krüger et al.
Krüger et al. 2012
, the correlation functions necessary to study second order coherence can even be generalized to basis independent expressions.

In the following, I will derive the mean values and variances of the intensity and the energy density for a system of an arbitrary object in an arbitrary environment. Subsequently, I will compute the degree of coherence which I use to investigate the bunching character of three special systems – vacuum, a substrate in vacuum, and a sphere in vacuum. Eventually, I also extend this theory to a system of more than one arbitrary object.

II
Theoretical framework

In classical electrodynamics the energy density
u
is given by

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\mathbf{E}^{2}(\mathbf{r},t)+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\mathbf{H}^{2}(\mathbf{r},t)

(1)

with the electric field
\mathbf{E}
, the magnetic field
\mathbf{H}
, the vacuum’s permittivity
\varepsilon_{0}
, and vacuum’s permeability
\mu_{0}
which are connected by
\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}=1/c^{2}
. Since fluctuational electrodynamics treat thermal fluctuations as sources of the electromagnetic fields, the fields and the energy density become fluctuational quantities. Therefore, general mean values are evaluated. Here, to write down the mean value of the energy density, the squared expressions on the right hand side will be replaced by the correlation functions of the considered field

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle.

(2)

Note that I replaced the fields by quantum mechanical operators denoted by the
\hat{\cdot}
symbol. Here, I will use the symmetrically ordered operators to obtain the energy density and its fluctuations. In addition, let me introduce the positive and negative frequency field operators
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{\pm}
defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{+}(\mathbf{r},t)+\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{-}(\mathbf{r},t)

(3)

which are simply given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{\pm}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r},\pm\omega)e^{\mp\text{i}\omega t}.

(4)

Herein,
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{+}
describes the annihilation of a photon and
\hat{\mathbf{E}}^{-}
, its Hermitian conjugate, its creation. By using the definition in Eq. (
3
) and taking advantage of the stationarity of the fields, i.e.
\llangle\hat{E}^{+}(\omega)\hat{E}^{-}(\omega^{\prime})\rrangle\propto 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, the energy density becomes

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\Big\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\Bigr[\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{E}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle+\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{E}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\Bigr[\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{H}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle+\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{H}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle\Bigr]\Big\}.

(5)

Note that index
i
indicates Einstein summation over the vector components. In quantum mechanics, however, a measuring process, e.g. in a photon interference experiment, is not described by symmetrically ordered operators. That is because a photon is annihilated at the detector during the measuring process. This corresponds to the intensity
I
defined by normally ordered operators

\displaystyle\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\Big\{\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{E}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{H}_{i}^{+}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\Big\rrangle\Big\}.

(6)

Note that
\Big\llangle I\Big\rrangle
follows from
\Big\llangle u\Big\rrangle
by dropping the first term in each line of Eq. (
5
) and multiplying by
2/\varepsilon_{0}
.

For the fluctuations of the energy density, one has to evaluate the correlation function of the energy density. This results in a correlation function of four operators, namely

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{4}\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{4}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}{4}\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}{4}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle.

(7)

This expression can be rewritten in terms of correlation functions of two operators by exploiting the Gaussian property of thermal radiation yielding

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2}\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}{2}\Bigl[\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle\hat{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{H}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle\hat{H}_{i}(\mathbf{r},t)\hat{E}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle\Bigr].

(8)

Let me now come back to the variance

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}_{u}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime})=\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle-\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle.

(9)

This quantity can now be evaluated by inserting Eq. (
8
) and performing a Fourier transform, giving

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\Bigg\{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\pm ij}(\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)e^{\text{i}\omega\tau}\Bigr]\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\pm ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\Bigr]^{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2}\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\pm ij}(\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)e^{\text{i}\omega\tau}\Bigr]\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\pm ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\Bigr]^{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}{2}\Bigr(\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\pm ij}(\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)e^{\text{i}\omega\tau}\Bigr]\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\pm ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\Bigr]^{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\pm ij}(\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)e^{\text{i}\omega\tau}\Bigr]\Bigl[\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\pm ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}+\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})e^{-\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\Bigr]^{*}\Bigr)\Bigg\}

(10)

with

\displaystyle\mathds{C}_{\text{AB}}^{\pm ij}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\Big\llangle\hat{A}^{+}_{i}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{B}^{-}_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)\Big\rrangle.

(11)

Note that indices
i
and
j
indicate the dependence on the coordinates
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
, respectively. Because of stationarity the variance only depends on the time difference
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
. Note that the relation

\displaystyle\mathds{C}_{\text{AB}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)

\displaystyle={\mathds{C}_{\text{BA}}^{\mp ji}(\omega)}^{*}

(12)

was used as well. Now, let me do the same calculation for the intensity fluctuations

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}

\displaystyle=\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)I(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle-\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\hat{E}_i^{-}(\mathbf{r}, t) \hat{E}_j^{+}(\mathbf{r}', t')}\expectationvalue{\hat{E}_j^{-}(\mathbf{r}', t') \hat{E}_i^{+}(\mathbf{r}, t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}_i^{-}(\mathbf{r}, t) \hat{H}_j^{+}(\mathbf{r}', t')}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}_j^{-}(\mathbf{r}', t') \hat{H}_i^{+}(\mathbf{r}, t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigl[\expectationvalue{\hat{E}_i^{-}(\mathbf{r}, t) \hat{H}_j^{+}(\mathbf{r}', t')}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}_j^{-}(\mathbf{r}', t') \hat{E}_i^{+}(\mathbf{r}, t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\expectationvalue{\hat{H}_i^{-}(\mathbf{r}, t) \hat{E}_j^{+}(\mathbf{r}', t')}\expectationvalue{\hat{E}_j^{-}(\mathbf{r}', t') \hat{H}_i^{+}(\mathbf{r}, t)}\Bigr].

(13)

Keeping in mind that the fields with identical frequency sign are uncorrelated, due to the evaluation of correlation functions with either only creation or annihilation operators, I obtain

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\Bigg\{\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)\mathds{C}_{\text{EE}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)\mathds{C}_{\text{HH}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigr[\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)\mathds{C}_{\text{EH}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})+\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\mp ij}(\omega)\mathds{C}_{\text{HE}}^{\mp ji}(\omega^{\prime})\Bigr]\Bigg\}e^{\text{i}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\tau}.

(14)

Scheme of the considered system. An arbitrary object with temperature
T_{\alpha}
is immersed in an arbitrary background at temperature
T_{\text{b}}
. Here the environment is sketched as a cavity so that the cavity walls bring the environmental field into local equilibrium with temperature
T_{\text{b}}
. The calculated variances contain information about the heat radiation of the object and the background measured at
(t,\mathbf{r})
and
(t^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
(magnifying glasses) independently.

Now, I derive the general expressions of
\Big\llangle u\Big\rrangle
,
\Big\llangle I\Big\rrangle
, and their variances for one arbitrary object labeled by
\alpha
in an arbitrary environment labeled by
b
(see Fig.
1
). For this, I use the scattering approach outlined in Ref.
Herz and Biehs 2019
going back to the formalism introduced in Ref.
Rahi et al. 2009
;
Krüger et al. 2012
. The fields and current densities are written in Dirac notation to obtain basis independent formulas. Therein, the current density
\ket{\mathbf{J}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\ket{\mathbf{J}}

\displaystyle=\ket{\mathbf{J}_\text{fl}}+\frac{1}{\text{i}\mu_{0}\omega}\mathds{T}\ket{\mathbf{E}_\text{b}}

(15)

where
\ket{\mathbf{J}_\text{fl}}
reflects the fluctuational part of the current density and the second contribution shows the induced part. Additionally, the T-operator
\mathds{T}
is used. The T-operator contains the scattering behavior of heat radiation due to the material. The fields
\ket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm E}}=\ket{\mathbf{E}}
and
\ket{\mathbf{F}_{\rm H}}=\ket{\mathbf{H}}
are given by

\displaystyle\ket{\mathbf{F}_k}

\displaystyle=\ket{\mathbf{F}_{k, \text{b}}}+\text{i}\mu_{0}\omega\mathds{G}_{k\text{E}}\ket{\mathbf{J}}

(16)

with
k\in\{\text{E,H}\}
and the Green’s function
\mathds{G}
containing the scattering behavior of heat radiation due to the environment. Then, the correlation function of the fields is

\displaystyle\Big\llangle\ket{\mathbf{F}_k}\otimes\bra{\mathbf{F}_l}\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=2\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left[\left[n_{\text{b}}(\omega)+1\right]\frac{\mathds{G}_{\text{full},kl}-\mathds{G}_{\text{full},lk}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}+\left[n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\mathds{K}_{kl}\right]

(17)

with

\displaystyle\mathds{G}_{\text{full},kl}

\displaystyle=\mathds{G}_{kl}+\mathds{G}_{k\text{E}}\mathds{T}\mathds{G}_{\text{E}l},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathds{K}_{kl}

\displaystyle=\mathds{G}_{k\text{E}}\boldsymbol{\chi}\mathds{G}^{\dagger}_{l\text{E}},

(19)

the Bose-Einstein occupation probability

\displaystyle n_{\gamma}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{\rm B}T_{\gamma}}}-1}

(20)

with the reduced Planck’s constant
\hbar
, the Boltzmann constant
k_{\rm B}
, and the temperature
T_{\gamma}
of object
\gamma
as well as the general susceptibility

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{T}-\mathds{T}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}-\mathds{T}\frac{\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}-\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}\mathds{T}^{\dagger}.

(21)

Keep in mind that
\ket{\mathbf{E}}
is related to
\mathbf{E}^{+}
and
\bra{\mathbf{E}}
is related to
\mathbf{E}^{-}
. That means by interchanging kets and bras, the pre-factor
n_{\text{b}}(\omega)+1
in Eq. (
17
) simply reduces to
n_{\text{b}}(\omega)
. The same is true for the magnetic field. With the correlation function in Eq. (
17
), the intensity becomes

\displaystyle\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{0}}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\text{Tr}\Bigr[\mathds{B}_{kk}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)+\mathds{Q}_{kk}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)\Bigr].

(22)

and its variance is

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\sum_{k,l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\text{Tr}\Bigl(\Bigr[\mathds{B}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)+\mathds{Q}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigr[\mathds{B}_{kl}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})+\mathds{Q}_{kl}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\Bigr]\Bigr)e^{\text{i}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\tau}.

(23)

Note, that the energy density always contains vacuum fluctuations whose frequency integrals do not converge, in general, whereas the mean intensity and its variance do not contain vacuum fluctuations. Since I am interested in the evaluation of the thermal contribution of the energy density fluctuations, I will neglect the vacuum contribution in the following. This yields the mean energy density

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\text{Tr}\Bigr[\text{Re}\left(\mathds{B}_{kk}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)\right)+\mathds{Q}_{kk}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)\Bigr]

(24)

and its variance

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}

\displaystyle=8\sum_{k,l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\text{Tr}\Bigl(\text{Re}\left(\mathds{B}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Re}\left(\mathds{Q}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)e^{-\text{i}\omega\tau}\right)\Bigr)\Bigl[\text{Re}\left(\mathds{B}_{lk}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r},\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Re}\left(\mathds{Q}_{lk}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r},\omega^{\prime})e^{\text{i}\omega^{\prime}\tau}\right)\Bigr]

(25)

using the abbreviations

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=2a_{kl}\hbar k_{0}^{2}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\frac{\mathds{G}_{\text{full},kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)-\mathds{G}_{\text{full},lk}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{r},\omega)}{2\text{i}},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathds{Q}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=2a_{kl}\hbar k_{0}^{2}\left[n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\mathds{K}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega),

(27)

and

\displaystyle a_{kl}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&k=l=\text{E}\\
\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}&k=l=\text{H}\\
\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}}&k\neq l\end{cases}.

(28)

Here I introduced the vacuum wave number
k_{0}=\omega/c
. Note that Tr
(\mathds{Q}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega))
has only real components.

Let me first consider the special case of the variances at
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}
and
\tau=0
for the pure electric case. If one finds a coordinate system in which
\mathds{M}_{\text{EE}}(\mathbf{r})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}[\mathds{B}_{\text{EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)+\mathds{Q}_{\text{EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)]
is diagonal, it is possible to decompose this matrix into
\mathds{M}_{\text{EE}}=\mathds{S}\mathds{D}_{\text{EE}}\mathds{S}^{-1}
. Here, the diagonal matrix
\mathds{D}_{\text{EE}}
contains the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\text{EE}}
of
\mathds{M}_{\text{EE}}
and the matrix
\mathds{S}
has the corresponding eigenvectors of
\mathds{M}_{\text{EE}}
as its columns. Due to the trace operation, one ends up with

\displaystyle\Big\llangle I_{\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{\text{EE},i}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle}{\varepsilon_{0}}

(29)

and

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E},i}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle^{2},

(30)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I,\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big\llangle I_{\text{E},i}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle^{2}.

(31)

When considering isotropic systems like vacuum, all eigenvalues are equal yielding

\displaystyle\Big\llangle I_{\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\varepsilon_{0}}\lambda_{\text{EE}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle}{\varepsilon_{0}}

(32)

and

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I,\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\Big\llangle I_{\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},0)\Big\rrangle^{2}.

(34)

The latter exactly coincides with the result found in
Mandel and Wolf 1995
meaning that intensity fluctuations are on the same order of magnitude as their mean values. Eq. (
33
) now shows that the same is true for the thermal energy density fluctuations but with a different pre-factor.

In general, to investigate the second order coherence properties of thermal radiation, it is reasonable to compare the variance of the considered quantity with its mean value. It allows for classifying the non-classical character of light
Glauber 1963
;
Aspect 2019
as first measured by the Hanbury Brown-Twiss (HBT) experiment
Hanbury Brown and Twiss 1956
. This experiment determined the second order intensity correlation function of a system by using two independent detectors to measure the intensity of a light source for different distances to the source and for time delays between the detectors. The ratio of the second order correlation function and the squared first order correlation function is also called “complex degree of coherence of second order” defined by

\displaystyle g_{u,\text{th}}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle}{\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\tau)}{\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle}

(35)

for the energy density and

\displaystyle g_{I}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\tau)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\tau)}{\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big\rrangle}

(36)

for the intensity. In principle, a setup like the HBT experiment should also be able to measure the second order intensity correlation function of the system considered in this work. Thermal radiation showing bunching belongs to the class of quasi-classical light. For isotropic objects and environments, one can directly read off, due to Eqs. (
33
)-(
34
), that

\displaystyle g_{u_{\text{E}},\text{th}}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{3}=g_{I_{\text{E}}}^{(2)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)+\frac{1}{3}

(37)

when only considering electric contributions. This also defines the maximum value of
g_{\text{E}}^{(2)}
for both, spatial and temporal bunching.

III
Numerical Results

At first, let me validate the main results of Eq. (

)-(
25
) and Eq. (

)-(
36
) by retrieving the well known result for the case of pure vacuum

. Additionally, I want to apply it to two more complex examples of practical interest for experiments, namely a half-space and a sphere, that have not been investigated with respect to the variances of energy density and intensity, yet. In the following, I will use two different types of materials: SiC and gold. SiC as a dielectric material can be modeled by a Lorentz-Oscillator

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{SiC}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\infty}\frac{\omega_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2}-\text{i}\Gamma\omega}{\omega_{t}^{2}-\omega^{2}-\text{i}\Gamma\omega}

with
\varepsilon_{\infty}=6.7
,
\omega_{l}=1.827\times 10^{14}
rad/s,
\omega_{t}=1.495\times 10^{14}
rad/s, and
\Gamma=0.9\times 10^{12}
rad/s. For gold I employ the Drude model
Ordal et al. 1987

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{Au}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{\infty}-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\text{i}\Gamma\omega}

(39)

with
\varepsilon_{\infty}=8.344
,
\omega_{p}=1.372\times 10^{16}

III.1
Energy density and intensity fluctuations of vacuum

In the simplest case of considering black-body radiation in vacuum, one can set
\mathds{T}=0
and, thus, obtain
\mathds{Q}_{kl}=0
because no object is involved. Then, if one is only interested in the temporal coherence, the spatial arguments become identical. The Green’s functions become
\mathds{G}_{\text{full},kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})=\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})
yielding for Eq. (
26
)

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=2a_{kl}\hbar k_{0}^{2}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\times\begin{cases}\frac{\omega}{6\pi c}\mathds{1}&k=l\\
0&k\neq l\end{cases}.

(40)

By using this in Eqs. (
22
)-(
25
), it is straightforward to derive the desired results for the mean values and variances

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle=\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{4}T_{\text{b}}^{4}}{15\hbar^{3}c^{3}}

(41)

and

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{12\hbar^{2}}{\pi^{4}c^{6}\tau_{\text{b}}^{8}}\biggl[\text{Re}\left(\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\right)\biggr]^{2},

(42)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{6\hbar^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\pi^{4}c^{6}\tau_{\text{b}}^{8}}\bigg|\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\bigg|^{2}

(43)

which coincide with the corresponding expression in
Wolf 1954
. Here,

\displaystyle\zeta(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+y)^{x}}

(44)

is the Hurwitz zeta function,
T_{\text{b}}
is the vacuum background temperature, and I defined the vacuum’s coherence time

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{k_{\text{B}}T_{\text{b}}}.

(45)

Comparing the full variances with the mean values, I find

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\Big\llangle u(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2},

(46)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}.

(47)

However, the pure electric or magnetic contributions fulfill the relations of Eqs. (
33
)-(
34
) because both contribute equally to Eqs. (
46
)-(
47
). The factor of
2
is missing in Eqs. (
46
)-(
47
) compared to Eqs. (
33
)-(
34
) when only considering one contribution, e.g. only the electric one. Thereby, I retrieve the results of Ref.
Mandel and Wolf 1995
for vacuum.

The
g^{(2)}
function for the energy density and the intensity are depicted in Fig.
2
. For both quantities one can clearly see the bunching character of thermal vacuum radiation and the loss of coherence at a time delay of
\tau_{\text{b}}=25.5
fs at
T_{\text{b}}=300
K.

g^{(2)}
function of the thermal contribution of the energy density (blue) and of the intensity (red) with respect to the normalized time delay
\tau/\tau_{\text{b}}
for

fs at
T_{\text{b}}=300
K.

III.2
Energy density and intensity fluctuations above a planar substrate

A more sophisticated problem is the calculation of the energy density and intensity fluctuations at distance
d
above a half-space. The half-space is assumed to cover the whole x-y space and the space
z<0
. I only take non-magnetic, homogeneous, and isotropic materials into account for the half-space. The temperature of the half-space is
T_{\alpha}
and the one of the background
T_{\text{b}}
. The Green’s function for the whole system is well known
Sipe 1987
and can be separated into a vacuum part and a scattered contribution due to reflections at the surface of the half-space within the planar wave base as

\displaystyle\mathds{G}_{\text{full,EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\text{d}^{2}k_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{\text{i}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\left[\mathds{G}_{\text{vac,EE}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp},z,z^{\prime})+\mathds{G}_{\text{scat,EE}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp},z,z^{\prime})\right].

(48)

Note that I assume
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
being outside of the half-space. The remaining Green’s functions
\mathds{G}_{\text{EH/HE/HH}}
can be obtained by exploiting the duality relations between the desired Green’s function and
\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}
. The explicit expressions can be found in appendix
A
. Then, it is easy to find the
\mathds{B}
matrices using Eq. (
26
)

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{\text{EE/HH}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\hbar k_{0}^{3}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\sum_{j\in\{\perp,\parallel\}}\left(\frac{1}{6\pi}(1+\delta_{j\perp})+I_{\text{p/s},j}\right)\mathds{A}_{j},

(49)

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{\text{EH/HE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\text{i}\hbar k_{0}^{3}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)I_{\text{mix}}\mathds{A}_{\text{mix}},

(50)

where I introduced the matrices

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{x}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{y}\right),

(51)

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{z}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{z},

(52)

and

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{\text{mix}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{y}-\mathbf{e}_{y}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{x}

(53)

as well as
I_{\text{p/s},j}
and
I_{\text{mix}}
defined in Eqs. (
109
)-(
111
). Note that
\mathds{B}_{\text{HE}}=\mathds{B}_{\text{EH}}^{\dagger}
. The first term in (
49
) corresponds to the vacuum contribution, whereas the second part describes the reflected parts. The mixed Green’s functions possess no vacuum part as in the case of pure vacuum.

To obtain the remaining
\mathds{Q}
matrices defined in Eq. (
27
), I expand the Green’s function and T-operators in
\mathds{K}
as defined in Eq. (
19
) in the plane wave basis as well. These expressions are also well known in literature
Sipe 1987
;
Herz and Biehs 2021
so that I get with Eq. (
27
)

\displaystyle\mathds{Q}_{\text{EE/HH}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\hbar k_{0}^{3}\left[n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\sum_{j\in\{\perp,\parallel\}}K_{\text{p/s},j}\mathds{A}_{j},

(54)

\displaystyle\mathds{Q}_{\text{EH/HE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mp\hbar k_{0}^{3}\left[n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\left[K^{\text{pr}}_{\text{mix}}\pm\text{i}K^{\text{ev}}_{\text{mix}}\right]\mathds{A}_{\text{mix}}

(55)

with
K_{\text{p/s},j}
,
K_{\text{mix}}^{\text{pr}}
, and
K_{\text{mix}}^{\text{ev}}
defined in Eqs. (
120
)-(
115
). Inserting these in Eqs. (
22
)-(
25
), yields the mean values

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{j\in\{\perp,\parallel\}}\left[\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{k,j}(0)+\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{k,j}(0)\right]=\varepsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(56)

and the corresponding variances

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{j\in\{\perp,\parallel\}}(1+\delta_{j\parallel})\text{Re}\left(\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{k,j}(\tau)+\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{k,j}(\tau)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\text{Im}\Bigl(\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{\text{mix}}(\tau)-\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix,ev}}(\tau)\Bigr)^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\text{Re}\Bigl(\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix,pr}}(\tau)\Bigr)^{2},

(57)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{j\in\{\perp,\parallel\}}(1+\delta_{j\parallel})\Big|\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{k,j}(\tau)+\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{k,j}(\tau)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{\text{mix}}(\tau)-\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix,ev}}(\tau)\Big|^{2}+\Big|\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix,pr}}(\tau)\Big|^{2}.

(58)

The
\Gamma
integrals are defined in Eqs. (
116
)-(
119
). These variances fulfill the relations in Eqs. (
30
)-(
31
) when taking into account that there are two directions parallel to the half-space’s surface which equally contribute to the energy density and the intensity, then

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th,E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j\in\{\text{x,y,z}\}}\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E},j}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left(2\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E},x}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}+\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E},z}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}\right),

(59)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I,\text{E}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\{\text{x,y,z}\}}\Big\llangle I_{\text{E},j}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}=2\Big\llangle I_{\text{E},x}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}+\Big\llangle I_{\text{E},z}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle^{2}

(60)

with

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th,E,x/y/z}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\left[\Gamma^{\text{eq}}_{\text{E,x/y/z}}(0)+\Gamma^{\text{leq}}_{\text{E,x/y/z}}(0)\right]=\varepsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I_{\text{E,x/y/z}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(61)

and
\Gamma^{\text{(l)eq}}_{\text{E,x}}=\Gamma^{\text{(l)eq}}_{\text{E,y}}=\Gamma^{\text{(l)eq}}_{\text{E},\perp}/2
.

To validate the results in Eqs. (
56
)-(
58
), let me investigate their asymptotic behavior for some limiting cases. If the position
\mathbf{r}
of the observer is far removed from the half space (
d\rightarrow\infty
), one can safely neglect all evanescent parts of the
I
integrals. Additionally, the scattering terms of the propagating contributions containing the complex exponential functions
e^{2\text{i}k_{z}d}
tend to show decaying features due to the rapid oscillations. The resulting integrals are summarized in Eqs. (
120
)-(
125
). In this limit I obtain the mean values

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{4}}{30c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\left[T_{\alpha}^{4}+T_{\text{b}}^{4}\right]-\frac{\hbar}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega k_{0}^{2}[n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)]\int_{0}^{k_{0}}\text{d}k_{\perp}\frac{k_{\perp}}{k_{z}}\left(|r_{\text{s}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}+|r_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right)=\epsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(62)

and the variances

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\bigg\{\left[\text{Re}\left(\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{E},\perp}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}+2\left[\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{E},z}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}+\left[\text{Re}\left(\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{H},\perp}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left[\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{H},z}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}+2\left[\text{Re}\left(\Gamma_{\text{vac},2}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{mix,pr}}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}\Bigg\}

(63)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big|\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{E},\perp}(\tau)\Big|^{2}+\bigg|\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{E},z}(\tau)\bigg|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Big|\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{H},\perp}(\tau)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\bigg|\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{H},z}(\tau)\bigg|^{2}+\Big|\Gamma_{\text{vac},2}(\tau)-\Gamma^{d\rightarrow\infty}_{\text{mix,pr}}(\tau)\Big|^{2}.

(64)

See Eqs. (
126
)-(
130
) for the abbreviations used in the variance expressions. This already displays that in thermal equilibrium
T_{\alpha}=T_{\beta}
I will obtain the vacuum results because
\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}
will be the only non-vanishing contribution. In the case of a black-body, which means to set
|r_{\text{s/p}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}=0
, additionally
\Gamma_{\text{vac},2}
will contribute to the variances yielding

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{4}}{30c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\left[T_{\alpha}^{4}+T_{\text{b}}^{4}\right]=\epsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(65)

and

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\Big\{3\left[\text{Re}\left(\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}+2\left[\text{Re}\left(\Gamma_{\text{vac},2}(\tau)\right)\right]^{2}\Big\}

(66)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\Big|\Gamma_{\text{vac},1}(\tau)\Big|^{2}+\Big|\Gamma_{\text{vac},2}(\tau)\Big|^{2}.

(67)

Especially Eq. (
65
) shows that both, the black-body half space and the vacuum background, contribute evenly to the energy density. Due to the additional term in both variances, the
g^{(2)}
functions will be larger compared to the pure vacuum case if
T_{\alpha}\neq T_{\beta}
holds. In the opposite case of a perfect conductor, which means setting
|r_{\text{s/p}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}=1
, I obtain

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\text{B}}^{4}T_{\text{b}}^{4}}{15c^{3}\hbar^{3}}=\epsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(68)

and the variances

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=12\left[\text{Re}\left(\frac{k_{\text{B}}^{4}T_{\text{b}}^{4}}{\pi^{2}c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\right)\right]^{2}+2\left[\text{Re}\left(\frac{3k_{\text{B}}^{4}}{4\pi^{2}c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\left[T_{\alpha}^{4}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\alpha}}\right)-T_{\text{b}}^{4}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\right]\right)\right]^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=6\bigg|\frac{k_{\text{B}}^{4}T_{\text{b}}^{4}}{\varepsilon_{0}\pi^{2}c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\bigg|^{2}+\bigg|\frac{3k_{\text{B}}^{4}}{4\pi^{2}\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}\hbar^{3}}\left[T_{\alpha}^{4}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\alpha}}\right)-T_{\text{b}}^{4}\zeta\left(4,1-\text{i}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\text{b}}}\right)\right]\bigg|^{2}.

(70)

The mean values become the vacuum results, again. This is due to Kirchhoff’s law stating that a perfect conductor who reflects all incoming radiation would also not emit any radiation. The variances also show an additional contribution which reflects the presence of the conducting half space. Again, the
g^{(2)}
functions will be larger compared to the pure vacuum case if
T_{\alpha}\neq T_{\beta}
holds.

Let me conclude by considering the quasi-static limit
c\rightarrow\infty
to investigate the case being in the vicinity of the half space. This corresponds to setting
r_{\text{s}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})=0
and
r_{\text{p}}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})=(\varepsilon-1)/(\varepsilon+1)
. The resulting integrals are listed in Eqs. (
131
)-(
137
). Note that I only take into account the leading expressions with respect to
1/d
. This ends up in the mean values

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{8\pi^{2}d^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\left[2n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\frac{\text{Im}(\varepsilon)}{|\varepsilon+1|^{2}}=\varepsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(71)

and variances

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{\hbar}{8\pi^{2}d^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\left[2n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\frac{\text{Im}(\varepsilon)}{|\varepsilon+1|^{2}}\cos(\omega\tau)\right)^{2},

(72)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{8}\bigg|\frac{\hbar}{8\varepsilon_{0}\pi^{2}d^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\left[2n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\frac{\text{Im}(\varepsilon)}{|\varepsilon+1|^{2}}e^{\text{i}\omega\tau}\bigg|^{2}.

(73)

Note that I neglected the vacuum contribution in this case because it becomes negligibly small compared to the quasi-static limit approach of the scattered part and the half space contribution. Also note that the well-known
1/d^{3}
proportionality emerges. In the equilibrium case of
T_{\alpha}=T_{\text{b}}
only the reflection at the half space surface is contributing.

Let me now move on to some numerical evaluations of the half space example. The resulting
g^{(2)}
functions are plotted in Fig.
3
for SiC employing the temperatures
T_{\alpha}=350
K and
T_{\text{b}}=300
K.

Figure 3:
g_{I}^{(2)}
function of the intensity for different distances
d
to the substrate’s surface and for the two limits
c,d\rightarrow\infty
in global equilibrium conditions (
T_{\alpha}=T_{\text{b}}
, dashed lines) as well as in local equilibrium conditions (

, solid lines) with respect to the normalized time delay

. Inset:

function of the thermal contribution of the energy density in local equilibrium for the same distances.

As one would expect, the coherence time
\tau_{c}
, which replaces
\tau_{\text{b}}
for this geometry, now depends on the distance
d
between substrate and observation point and exceeds the vacuum value by at least two orders of magnitude, even for distances like
d=200
nm in agreement with Ref.
Biehs and Ben-Abdallah 2018
. The larger this distance, the shorter becomes the coherence time
\tau_{c}
and gets close to
\tau_{\text{b}}
again in the far-field regime as shown in Eqs. (
62
)-(
64
) or coincides with it in the equilibrium case of
T_{\alpha}=T_{\text{b}}
. The large values
\tau_{c}\gg\tau_{\text{b}}
in the near-field regime exist because SiC possesses two resonance frequencies that become very pronounced in the near field spectrum. These are the SPhP resonance frequency and the transverse optical phonon (TOP) resonance frequency. The SPhP mode is more pronounced in the electric contribution compared to the TOP mode that dominates the spectrum of the magnetic contribution
Dong et al. 2017
;
Herz and Biehs 2022
. Due to the quasi-monochromatic distribution of the spectrum around these two frequencies the correlation time increases. For larger distances
d
the effect of these evanescent waves decreases and the amplitude of the
g^{(2)}
functions decreases as well. This is most pronounced for
d>50
nm since below this distance the curves almost overlap. There is also an increasing drop of amplitude at very short time delays for growing distances
d
for the same reason. This drop happens for time delays on the order of
\tau_{\text{b}}
. Therefore, this drop can be connected to the degrading of the quasi-monochromatic spectrum into a black-body one like in vacuum for growing distances shown here by the
d\rightarrow\infty
approximation taken from Eqs. (
62
)-(
64
). Thus, this
g^{(2)}
function seems to be an overlap of the one corresponding to the vacuum case that I discussed previously, which explains the drop emerging for larger distances, and the one for the quasi-monochromatic spectrum dominated by the SPhP mode, which explains the large
\tau_{c}
values for smaller distances. In the case of
d=5
nm one can observe a good agreement with the quasi-static limit approximation which basically neglects any vacuum contribution so that one is left with the influence of the quasi-monochromatic spectrum instead of an overlap of it with the black-body case. For the chosen distances
d
, the values for the global equilibrium situation (
T_{\alpha}=T_{\text{b}}
) are always smaller than for the local equilibrium case (
T_{\alpha}\neq T_{\text{b}}
) due to the missing
\mathds{K}
matrix contribution. Interestingly, the initial values of the global and local equilibrium conditions for
\tau=0
approach each other for increasing distances
d
. Compared to
g_{I}^{(2)}
,
g_{u,\text{th}}^{(2)}
behaves qualitatively identical regarding the above mentioned points but with a wave-like character.
g_{I}^{(2)}
, however, behaves like the average of
g^{(2)}_{u,\text{th}}
due to the absolute value.

III.3
Energy density and intensity fluctuations in the vicinity of a sphere

Finally, let me apply the here developed theory to a single sphere of radius
R
immersed in vacuum for which I compute the mean values and variances of the energy density and intensity at distance
r
to the center of the sphere. Again, the fully electric Green’s function
\mathds{G}_{\text{full,EE}}
can be decomposed into a vacuum part and a scattering part due to reflections at the sphere’s surface like in Eq. (
48
) but for a different geometrical basis. For that I use the notation
Krüger et al. 2012

\displaystyle\mathds{G}_{\text{vac,EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\text{i}\sum_{P,l,m}\mathbf{E}_{P,l,m}^{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathbf{E}_{P,l,-m}^{\text{reg}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(74)

\displaystyle\mathds{G}_{\text{scat,EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\text{i}\sum_{P,l,m}\mathcal{T}_{l}^{P}\mathbf{E}_{P,l,m}^{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathbf{E}_{P,l,-m}^{\text{reg}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(75)

Here,
P\in\{\text{M,N}\}

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{\text{EE/HH}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\hbar k_{0}^{3}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{6\pi}\mathds{1}+\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\text{Re}\left(\mathcal{B}^{\text{E/H}}_{l,\vartheta\varphi}\left[\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}+\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}\right]+\mathcal{B}^{\text{E/H}}_{l,r}\mathbf{e}_{r}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{r}\right)\right],

(76)

\displaystyle\mathds{B}_{\text{EH}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\text{i}\hbar k_{0}^{3}n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{8\pi}\mathcal{B}^{\text{mix}}_{l,\vartheta\varphi}\left[\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}+\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}\right],

(77)

\displaystyle=\text{i}\hbar k_{0}^{3}\left(n_{\alpha}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right)\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{8\pi}\mathcal{Q}^{\text{mix}}_{l,\vartheta\varphi}\left[\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}+\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\vartheta}\right].

(79)

\mathds{B}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}=\mathds{B}_{\text{EH}}

\mathds{Q}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}=\mathds{Q}_{\text{EH}}

\displaystyle\Big\llangle u_{\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j\in\{r,\vartheta,\varphi\}}\sum_{\gamma\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\Bigr[\Lambda^{\text{eq}}_{j,\gamma}(0)-\Lambda^{\text{leq}}_{j,\gamma}(0)\Bigr]=\varepsilon_{0}\Big\llangle I(\mathbf{r},t)\Big\rrangle

(80)

and the variances

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle=8\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\{r,\vartheta,\varphi\}}\sum_{\gamma\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\Bigl[\text{Re}\Bigl(\Lambda^{\text{eq}}_{j,\gamma}(\tau)-\Lambda^{\text{leq}}_{j,\gamma}(\tau)\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2}+2\Bigl[\text{Re}\Bigl(\Lambda^{\text{eq}}_{\text{mix}}(\tau)-\Lambda^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix}}(\tau)\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\Bigl[\text{Re}\Bigl(\Lambda^{\text{eq}}_{\text{mix}}(-\tau)-\Lambda^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix}}(-\tau)\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2}\bigg\},

(81)

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{I}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r},\tau)

\displaystyle\quad+2\Big|\Lambda^{\text{eq}}_{\text{mix}}(-\tau)+\Lambda^{\text{leq}}_{\text{mix}}(-\tau)\Big|^{2}\bigg\}

(82)

Extension to two objects

To derive the corresponding expressions for
\Big\llangle u\Big\rrangle
,
\Big\llangle I\Big\rrangle
,
\text{Var}_{u,\text{th}}
,
\text{Var}_{I}
,

, and

for two objects, one has to adapt the total fields and currents in Eqs. (
15
)-(
16
) as it is explained in Ref.
Herz and Biehs 2019
. The second object will be labeled by index
\beta
. Thereby, I obtain the fields

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\mathbf{F}}_k}

\displaystyle=\ket{\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{k, \text{b}}}+\text{i}\mu_{0}\omega\mathds{G}_{k\text{E}}\left[\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_\alpha}+\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_\beta}\right]

(91)

and the current density

\displaystyle\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_\alpha}

\displaystyle=\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha, \text{fl}}}+\frac{1}{\text{i}\mu_{0}\omega}\mathds{T}_{\alpha}\ket{\hat{\mathbf{E}}_ \text{b}}+\mathds{T}_{\alpha}\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}\Bigr[\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\beta, \text{fl}}}+\frac{1}{\text{i}\mu_{0}\omega}\mathds{T}_{\beta}\ket{\hat{\mathbf{E}}_\text{b}}+\mathds{T}_{\beta}\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}\ket{\hat{\mathbf{J}}_\alpha}\Bigr].

(92)

Using this, I obtain the new correlation function

\displaystyle\Big\llangle\ket{\hat{\mathbf{F}}_k}\otimes\bra{\hat{\mathbf{F}}_l}\Big\rrangle

\displaystyle=2\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left[\left[n_{\text{b}}(\omega)+1\right]\frac{\mathds{G}_{\text{f},kl}-\mathds{G}_{\text{f},lk}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}+\sum_{\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}}\left[n_{\gamma}(\omega)-n_{\text{b}}(\omega)\right]\mathds{K}_{\gamma}\right]

(93)

with

\displaystyle\mathds{G}_{\text{f},kl}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\mathds{1}+\mathds{O}_{\alpha}\mathds{G}\mathds{T}_{\alpha}+\mathds{O}_{\beta}\mathds{G}\mathds{T}_{\beta}\right)\mathds{G}\right]_{kl},

(94)

\displaystyle\mathds{K}_{\gamma,kl}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathds{O}_{\gamma}\mathds{G}\right]_{k\text{E}}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\gamma}\left[\mathds{G}^{\dagger}\mathds{O}_{\gamma}^{\dagger}\right]_{l\text{E}},

(95)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\gamma,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{T}_{\gamma,k}-\mathds{T}_{\gamma,k}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}-\mathds{T}_{\gamma,k}\frac{\mathds{G}_{kk}-\mathds{G}_{kk}^{\dagger}}{2\text{i}}\mathds{T}_{\gamma,k}^{\dagger}

(96)

as well as

\displaystyle\mathds{O}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathds{1}+\mathds{G}\mathds{T}_{\beta}\right)\left[\mathds{1}-\mathds{G}\mathds{T}_{\alpha}\mathds{G}\mathds{T}_{\beta}\right]^{-1}.

(97)

The above equations, then, have to be inserted into Eqs. (

27

N

V
Conclusion

In this work I employed the methods of mQED and scattering approach to derive expressions for the variances of the energy density and intensity of heat radiation in a systems of an arbitrary object immersed in an arbitrary environment. I compared the general solution with the corresponding mean values and retrieved the expressions found in
Mandel and Wolf 1995
for the ratio of variance and squared mean values for the intensity of isotropic systems. I also extended this to corresponding expressions of the energy density and systems containing three preferred axes like in cartesian, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates. With that formalism I computed the
g^{(2)}
functions of both, the energy density and the intensity, for vacuum, a half-space, and a sphere. Thereby, I retrieved the results of Ref.
Mandel and Wolf 1995
for vacuum, showed the expected distance dependence of the
g^{(2)}
functions above a half-space, and did the same thing for the
g_{I}^{(2)}
of a sphere as well as a multipole order analysis. Interestingly,
g_{I}^{(2)}
can become maximal for distances
r>R
also depending on the material and the chosen temperatures, which seems to be due to higher order multipole moments. The effect behind this feature is unclear for the moment and might be interesting for future investigations. Finally, I showed theoretically how the expressions can be generalized for two arbitrary objects and a many-body system. Perhaps, experimental setups like in the HBT experiment can help to experimentally underpin the findings of this work.
