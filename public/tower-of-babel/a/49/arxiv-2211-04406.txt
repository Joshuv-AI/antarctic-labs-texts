Multiple Packing: Lower and Upper Bounds
Yihan Zhang 1 Shashank Vatedka 2

Abstract
We study the problem of high-dimensional multiple packing in Euclidean space. Multiple packing is a natural generalization of sphere packing and is defined as follows. Let N > 0 N>0 and L ∈ ℤ ≥ 2 L\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2} . A multiple packing is a set 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of points in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} such that any point in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} lies in the intersection of at most L − 1 L-1 balls of radius n ​ N \sqrt{nN} around points in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . We study the multiple packing problem for both bounded point sets whose points have norm at most n ​ P \sqrt{nP} for some constant P > 0 P>0 and unbounded point sets whose points are allowed to be anywhere in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Given a well-known connection with coding theory, multiple packings can be viewed as the Euclidean analog of list-decodable codes, which are well-studied for finite fields. In this paper, we derive various bounds on the largest possible density of a multiple packing in both bounded and unbounded settings. A related notion called average-radius multiple packing is also studied. Some of our lower bounds exactly pin down the asymptotics of certain ensembles of average-radius list-decodable codes, e.g., (expurgated) Gaussian codes and (expurgated) spherical codes. In particular, our lower bound obtained from spherical codes is the best known lower bound on the optimal multiple packing density and is the first lower bound that approaches the known large L L limit under the average-radius notion of multiple packing. To derive these results, we apply tools from high-dimensional geometry and large deviation theory.

III
Our results

We derive upper and lower bounds on the largest multiple packing density in both bounded and unbounded settings.
We compare various bounds that appear in this paper.
Let
C_{L-1}(P,N)
and
\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)
denote the largest density of multiple packings under the standard and the average-radius notions, respectively.
Let

and
\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)
denote the largest density of unbounded multiple packings under the standard and the average-radius notions, respectively.

III-A

Bounded packings

We first focus on the
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packing problem.
Since average-radius multiple packing is stronger than the standard multiple packing,

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)

V
Our techniques

We summarize our techniques below.

For lower bounds, our basic strategy is random coding with expurgation, a standard tool from information theory.
To show the existence of a list-decodable code of rate
R
, we simply randomly sample
e^{nR}
points independently each according to a certain distribution.
There might be bad lists of size
L
that violates the multiple packing condition.
We then throw away (a.k.a. expurgate) one point from each of the bad lists.
By carefully analyzing the error event and choosing a proper rate, we can guarantee that the remaining code has essentially the same rate after the removal process.
We then get a list-decodable code of rate
R
by noting that the remaining code contains no bad lists.

In the above framework, the key ingredient is a good estimate on the probability of the error event, i.e., the probability that the
list-decoding radius
of a size-
L
list is smaller than
\sqrt{nN}
.
Under the standard notion of multiple packing, the list-decoding radius is the
Chebyshev radius
of the list, i.e., the radius of the smallest ball containing the list.
Under the average-radius notion of multiple packing, the list-decoding radius is the
average radius
of the list, i.e., the square root of the average squared distance from each point in the list to the centroid of the list.
Due to the analytic simplicity of the average-radius variant, we manage to obtain the
exact
rate of certain ensembles of average-radius list-decodable codes.
In particular, we get the exact rates of expurgated Gaussian codes and expurgated spherical codes in the bounded setting.
The exact exponents of the probability of the error event are obtained using Cramér’s large deviation principle and Laplace’s method.
Leveraging tools from high-dimensional probability including Khinchin’s inequality, we also have bounds for other ensembles of codes such as (expurgated) ball codes.

The upper bounds in this paper are neither novel nor sharp.
They were sketched in Blinovsky’s works
[
Bli99
,
Bli05b
]
and were improved in followup works
[
BL11
]
.
The purpose of presenting them here is to provide a unified exposition and to clean up the literature.
We close some of the gaps in the analysis of
[
Bli99
,
Bli05b
,
BL11
]
and also present some new techniques.
As an ingredient for the Elias–Bassalygo-type bound, an explicit Plotkin-type bound on the optimal code size above the so-called
Plotkin point
is presented.
Specifically, it is known (and also follows from our bounds) that as
n\to\infty
, the largest
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packing has size
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
as long as
N>\frac{L-1}{L}P
.
The threshold
\frac{N}{P}=\frac{L-1}{P}
is called the Plotkin point.
We present bounds on the optimal code size as a function of
\rho>0
when
N=\frac{L-1}{L}P(1+\rho)
.
Our bound reads as
F(\rho^{-1}+1)
where
F(\cdot)
scales like the Ramsey number.
This bound is far from being tight and indeed it was recently improved by Alon, Bukh and Polyanskiy
[
ABP18
]
.
The exact dependence on
\rho
of the optimal size of codes above the Plotkin point is open.
The suboptimal dependence of our Plotkin-type bound on
\rho
turns out to be sufficient for the proof of the Elias–Bassalygo-type bound.

As another technical contribution, we discover several new representations of the average radius and the Chebyshev radius.
They play crucial roles in facilitating the analyses and the applications of some of these representations go beyond the scope of this paper.
We show that the average squared radius of a list can be written as a quadratic form associated with the list.
This representation is used to analyze Gaussian codes and spherical codes in this paper and infinite constellations in
[
ZV22d
]
.
The average squared radius can also be written as the difference between the average norm of points in the list and the norm of the centroid of the list.
This representation is used to analyze spherical codes and ball codes.
The average squared radius can be further written as the average pairwise distance of the list.
This allows us to give a one-line proof of the Blachman–Few reduction and its strengthened version.
Yet another way of writing the average squared radius using the average norm and the average pairwise correlation turns out to be useful for the proof of the Plotkin-type bound.

Given the intimate connection between packing and error-correcting codes, we will interchangeably use the terms “multiple packing” and “list-decodable code”.

This paper is only concerned with the fundamental limits of multiple packing for asymptotically large dimension

IX-A

Density of bounded and unbounded packings

In general (not only for multiple packing), it is customary to measure the density of a bounded constellation using
Equation

15
while to measure that of an unbounded constellation using
Equation

17
.
Informally, we expect them to be related to each other in the following way.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}
be a constellation in
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\lim\limits_{P\to\infty}{\mathcal{C}}_{P}
.
Then

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|}{\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\left|{\mathcal{C}}_{P}\right|}{\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{P\to\infty}\left(R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})-\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\limsup_{P\to\infty}\left(R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})-\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\sqrt{nP}^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi e}{n}}^{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\limsup_{P\to\infty}\left(R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)\right).

(27)

Suppose that for a certain problem without input constraint, the optimal density
R^{*}({\mathcal{C}}^{*})
is achieved by an unbounded constellation
{\mathcal{C}}^{*}
, while for the same problem with input constraint
P
, the corresponding optimal density
R^{*}({\mathcal{C}}_{P}^{*})
is achieved by a bounded constellation
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}^{*}
.
The above relation between
R^{*}({\mathcal{C}}^{*})
and
R^{*}({\mathcal{C}}_{P}^{*})
given by
Equation

27
is empirically correct for some problems.
However, the reasoning is not exactly rigorous since the bounded constellation
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}^{*}
may not converge to an unbounded constellation as
P
approaches infinity.

Let us look at some examples to justify the above comments.
For the multiple packing problem with asymptotically large
L
, the capacity without input constraint is known to be
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{2\pi eN}

[
ZV22a
]
whereas the capacity with input constraint
P
equals
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}

[
ZVJS22
]
.
These two quantities indeed satisfy the relation given by
Equation

27
.

For the channel coding problem over an AWGN channel, the capacity without input constraint is
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{2\pi eN}
whereas the capacity with input constraint
P
equals
\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{P}{N}\right)
.
It still holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{P\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{P}{N}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)\right)=\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1}{2\pi eN}+\frac{1}{2\pi eP}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{2\pi eN}.

However, from this example, we see that one cannot rearrange both sides of
Equation

and claim that for any

,

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})\asymp R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP).

More representations of the average radius

In this section, we present several different representations of the average radius.

These representations are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 12
.

Let
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
and
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Then the average squared radius of

1.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2};\end{aligned}

2.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle;\end{aligned}

3.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}\right\rangle+\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle.

(29)

At last,

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

We show that

4

4

n+1

.

For any distribution
{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L-1},\quad\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)\geq\frac{\overline{E}(P,N,L)}{L-1},

\displaystyle E(P,N,L)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\ln\mathop{\Pr}_{({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes L}}\left[\rad^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right],

\displaystyle\overline{E}(P,N,L)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\ln\mathop{\Pr}_{({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes L}}\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right].

Let us first consider constructions of
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packings.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
.
We sample

{\mathcal{D}}
and call the point set
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Fix any
(i_{1},\cdots,i_{L})\in\binom{[M]}{L}
.
Suppose we manage to compute the following probability

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\varphi({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i_{1}},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i_{L}})\leq nN\right]\doteq e^{-nE(P,N,L)},

(33)

where
\varphi(\cdot)
denotes either the squared Chebyshev radius
\rad^{2}(\cdot)
or the average squared radius
\overline{\rad}^{2}(\cdot)
.
Then we know that the expected number of
bad
lists (i.e.,
L
-lists
{\mathcal{L}}
for which
\varphi({\mathcal{L}})
is at most
nN
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{{\mathcal{C}}}{L}:\varphi({\mathcal{L}})\leq nN\right\}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\doteq\binom{M}{L}e^{-nE(P,N,L)}\leq M^{L}e^{-nE(P,N,L)}=e^{n(RL-E(P,N,L))}.

We then expurgate one point from each of these bad lists and hope that the total number of removed points is at most, say,
M/2
.
This is satisfied if

\displaystyle e^{n(RL-E(P,N,L)+o(1))}

\displaystyle\leq M/2

\displaystyle\impliedby

\displaystyle RL-E(P,N,L)

\displaystyle\leq R-o(1)

\displaystyle\impliedby

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle\leq\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L-1}-o(1).

Note that the expurgation process destroys all bad
L
-lists, and the remaining subcode
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
of size at least
M/2
is indeed a
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packing.
Therefore, there exists a construction of
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packing with rate approaching
\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L-1}
.
∎

Technically, if the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
is not exactly supported in
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
, e.g.,
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{N}}(\underline{0},PI_{n})
, then we also need to show that the probability

\displaystyle\mathop{\Pr}_{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}>\sqrt{nP}\right]

\displaystyle\doteq e^{-nF(P,N)}

for some exponent
F(P,N)

and argue that the expected number of points with norm larger than
\sqrt{nP}
is negligible, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})^{c}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\doteq e^{n(R-F(P,N))}\ll e^{nR}.

Such a probability is usually very small and the number of points with large norm that will be expurgated is therefore negligible.
As a result, this expurgation step does not put effective constraints on the rate
R
.

The above idea can be adapted to
(N,L-1)
-multiple packings.

Remark 6
.

From
Equation

33
, if we do not proceed with expurgation but instead take a union bound over
(i_{1},\cdots,i_{L})\in\binom{[M]}{L}
, then the bound we eventually get will be
\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L}
which is much less than
\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L-1}
especially for small
L
.
In fact, it can be shown
[
GMR+22
]
that
\frac{E(P,N,L)}{L}
is the
threshold rate
of purely random codes in the sense that below the threshold such codes are list-decodable with high probability and above the threshold such codes are list-decodable with vanishing probability.
Therefore, expurgation is necessary for random coding arguments.

XI
Lower bounds via Gaussian codes

In this section, we analyze average-radius list-decodability of a Gaussian code (with expurgation)
and establish the following theorem.

Theorem 15
.

For any
P,N\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
such that
\frac{N}{P}\leq\frac{L-1}{L}
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
, the
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity is at least

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}+\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}-1\right).

(34)

Remark 7
.

The above bound (
Equation

34
) converges to
\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{P}{N}+\frac{N}{P}-1\right)
as
L\to\infty
.
The limiting value is strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}
which is the list-decoding capacity for asymptotically large
L
(see
Section

IV
).
When
L=2
, the above bound becomes
\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{P}{2N}+\frac{2N}{P}-1\right)
which is lower than the best known
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P^{2}}{4N(P-N)}
(see
Theorem

26
).
Furthermore, the above bound shoots to infinity at
N/P=0
and hits zero at
N/P=\frac{L-1}{L}
.
These two values are tight.
Specifically,
\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)=\infty
at
N/P=0
, obviously.
Also, it turns out that
\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)=0
at the point
N/P=\frac{L-1}{L}
known as the
Plotkin point
(see
Section

XIV
).

Proof.

Construct a codebook by sampling
M=e^{nR}
(for some rate
R>0
to be determined later) Gaussian vectors
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{M}
, each entry i.i.d. from
{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\frac{P}{1+\varepsilon}\right)
where
\varepsilon
is a small number to be determined.
Let
P^{\prime}\coloneqq\frac{P}{1+\varepsilon}
.
The probability that a codeword violates the power constraint is

\displaystyle\mathop{\Pr}_{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\underline{0},P^{\prime}I_{n})}\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}>\sqrt{nP}\right].

(35)

To obtain the exponent of the probability in
Equation

35
, we first recall the Moment Generating Function (MGF) of a chi-square random variable.

Definition 8
.

The
chi-square distribution

\chi^{2}(k)
with
degree of freedom

k
is defined as the distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{2}
where
{\mathbf{g}}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

Fact 16
.

If
{\mathbf{x}}\sim\chi^{2}(k)
, then for
\lambda<1/2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda{\mathbf{x}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-2\lambda}^{-k}.

Plugging the formula in
16
into Cramér’s theorem (
Theorem

8
), we get the first order asymptotics of the tail of a chi-square random variable.

Lemma 17
.

If
{\mathbf{x}}\sim\chi^{2}(k)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\ln\Pr\left[{\mathbf{x}}>(1+\delta)k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-\delta+\ln(1+\delta)),

\displaystyle\text{for $\delta>0$};

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\ln\Pr\left[{\mathbf{x}}<(1-\delta)k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\delta+\ln(1-\delta)),

\displaystyle\text{for $\delta\in(0,1)$}.

Then the probability in
Equation

35
is

\displaystyle\mathop{\Pr}_{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\underline{0},P^{\prime}I_{n})}\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}/\sqrt{P^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}>nP/P^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\Pr}_{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\underline{0},P^{\prime}I_{n})}\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}/\sqrt{P^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}>n(1+\varepsilon)\right]\doteq e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(-\varepsilon+\ln\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\right)},

since
\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}/\sqrt{P^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(n)
.
Therefore the expected number of codewords that violate the power constraint is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{i\in[M]:\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}>\sqrt{nP}\right\}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\doteq Me^{\frac{n}{2}\left(-\varepsilon+\ln\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\right)}=e^{n\left(R+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\varepsilon+\ln\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\right)\right)}.

If
\varepsilon
is set to be sufficiently small, the above number is negligible compared to the size
e^{nR}
of the code.

We now compute the probability that the average squared radius of a list is too small (
Equation

33
with
\varphi(\cdot)=\overline{\rad}^{2}(\cdot)
).

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq nN\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-L\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq LnN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)^{2}-L\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)\right)^{2}\leq LnN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)^{2}-\frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)\right)^{2}\right)\leq LnN\right],

(37)

where
Equation

36
is by
Equation

28

and
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
.

Define

\displaystyle g(x_{1},\cdots,x_{L})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{L}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}x_{i}\right)^{2}.

The above probability (
Equation

37
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}g({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1}(j),\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}(j))\leq LnN\right]

(38)

where
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{N}}(0,P^{\prime})
for each
i\in[L]
and
j\in[n]
.

We note that the function
g(\vec{t})
is a quadratic form of
\vec{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle g(\vec{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\vec{t}(i)^{2}-\frac{1}{L}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\vec{t}(i)\right)^{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{L}\vec{t}(i)^{2}-\frac{2}{L}\sum_{i,j\in[L]:i<j}\vec{t}(i)\vec{t}(j)=\vec{t}^{\top}A\vec{t},

(39)

where

\displaystyle A\coloneqq

\displaystyle I_{L}-\frac{1}{L}J_{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times L}

and
I_{L}
denotes the
L\times L
identity matrix and
J_{L}
denotes the
L\times L
all-one matrix.

Let
\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\coloneqq({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1}(j),\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}(j))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L}
.
By the above manipulation, we have
g(\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j})=\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top}A\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
, where
\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\vec{0},P^{\prime}I_{L})
.
In fact, since
A
is idempotent (i.e.,
A^{2}=A
), such a quadratic form is distributed according to
g(\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j})/P^{\prime}\sim\chi^{2}(\tr(A))=\chi^{2}(L-1)
.
Furthermore, the sum of i.i.d. chi-square random variables is still chi-square distributed and the degrees of freedom are summed.
Therefore,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}g(\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j})/P^{\prime}\sim\chi^{2}((L-1)n)
.

Assume
\frac{N}{P}<\frac{L-1}{L}
.
Let
N=\frac{L-1}{L}P(1-\rho)
for some
\rho>0
.
One can solve
\rho
and get
\rho=1-\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}
.
We now can apply
Lemma

17
to obtain the exact asymptotics of
Equation

38
:

Equation

38

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}g(\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j})/P^{\prime}\leq LnN/P^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\chi^{2}((L-1)n)\leq Ln\cdot\frac{L-1}{L}P(1-\rho)\cdot\frac{1+\varepsilon}{P}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\chi^{2}((L-1)n)\leq n(L-1)(1-\rho)(1+\varepsilon)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\chi^{2}((L-1)n)\leq n(L-1)(1-\delta)\right]

(40)

\displaystyle\doteq\exp\left((L-1)n\frac{1}{2}(\ln(1-\delta)+\delta)\right).

In
Equation

40
, we define
\delta>0
such that
1-\delta=(1-\rho)(1+\varepsilon)
, i.e.,

\delta\coloneqq(1+\varepsilon)\rho-\varepsilon
.
Note that
\delta\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\rho
.

The expected number of
bad
lists is therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}:\overline{\rad}^{2}\left(\left\{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}}\right)\leq nN\right\}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\doteq\binom{M}{L}e^{n\frac{L-1}{2}\left(\ln(1-\delta)+\delta\right)}\leq e^{n\left(LR+\frac{L-1}{2}\left(\ln(1-\delta)+\delta\right)\right)}.

To obtain an
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decodable code, we first throw away all codewords that violate the power constraint.
We then throw away one codeword in each
bad
list whose average squared radius is at most
nN
.
We would like the expected number of codewords we threw away in total not to exceed
M/2
.
This can be satisfied if the rate
R
satisfies

\displaystyle LR+\frac{L-1}{2}\left(\ln(1-\delta)+\delta\right)\leq R-o(1).

That is, (ignoring the
o(1)
factor)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{1}{1-\delta}-\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{1}{(1-\rho)(1+\varepsilon)}-(1+\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN(1+\varepsilon)}-(1+\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}\right)+\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}+\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}-1\right).

We then obtain a
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decodable code of size at least
M/2
.
∎

XII
Lower bounds via spherical codes

In this section, we examine the
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decodability of uniformly random spherical codes (with expurgation).
We first sample
M=e^{nR}
codewords
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{M}
uniformly and independently from
{\mathcal{S}}^{n-1}(\sqrt{nP})
.
We are again interested in the asymptotics of
Equation

33
with
\varphi(\cdot)=\overline{\rad}^{2}(\cdot)
.
In
Sections

XII-A
and
XII-B
respectively, we will obtain a bound on the normalized exponent of
Equation

33
via two different approaches:
normal approximation
and
Khinchin’s inequality
(with sharp constants).
Plugging this bound into the random coding with expurgation framework, we get the following result.

Theorem 18
.

For any
P,N\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
such that
\frac{N}{P}\leq\frac{L-1}{L}
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
, the
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity is at least

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}-\frac{1}{L-1}\ln\frac{L(P-N)}{P}\right).

(41)

Remark 8
.

We make several observations on the behaviour of the bound in
Theorem

18
.

1.

We observe that the bound
Equation

41
for spherical codes is the second largest lower bound for sufficiently large rates.
The largest lower bound turns out to be
Equations

7
and
5
(for all
N,P\geq 0
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
).
It can be proved using error exponents as a proxy
[
ZV22c
]
, or, as we shall see in
Section

XII-D
, using refined analysis for expurgated spherical codes per se.
Indeed, the only difference between these two bounds
Equations

7
and
41
is that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}

\displaystyle\leq\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN},

(42)

where the LHS appears in
Equation

41
and the RHS appears in
Equation

7
.
The above inequality follows from the elementary inequality
\ln x\leq x-1
for any
x>0
.
In fact, the LHS forms the first two terms of the Taylor series of the RHS at
N/P=(L-1)/L
.

2.

However,
Equation

41
gets worse quickly as
N/P
decreases, as plotted in
Figures

1(b)
and
1(c)
.
In particular, for
N/P=0
,
\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)
should be infinity since clearly one can pack arbitrarily many balls of radius zero.
However, the above bound attains a finite value (specifically
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ln L}{L-1}\right)
) at
N/P=0
.
This will be explained in
Remark

10
.

3.

The above bound (
Equation

41
) converges to
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{N}{P}\right)
as
L\to\infty
.
The limiting value is strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}
which is the list-decoding capacity for asymptotically large
L
(see
Section

IV
).

4.

On the other hand, when
L=2
, the above bound becomes
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2N}{P}-\ln\frac{2(P-N)}{P}\right)
which is lower than the best know bound
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P^{2}}{4N(P-N)}
in
Theorem

26
.

Theorem

18
will be proved in
Sections

XII-A
and
XII-B
using two different methods: normal approximation and Khinchin’s inequality.
However, the observations in
Remark

8
suggest that the analysis in the proof of
Theorem

18
is likely suboptimal.
We then prove in
Section

XII-D
the following theorem which shows that (expurgated) spherical codes can in fact achieve a much higher rate.
Indeed, this rate is the exact asymptotics of this code ensemble and matches the best known lower bound
Equation

7
which itself was originally proved using a completely different method (cf.
[
ZV22c
]
).
On the other hand, the advantage of the techniques in
Section

XII-B
is that it can be easily extended to related ensembles such as (expurgated) ball codes (cf.
Section

XII-C
), which is not the case for the techniques to be presented in
Section

XII-D
.

Theorem 19
.

For any
P,N\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
such that
\frac{N}{P}\leq\frac{L-1}{L}
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
, the
(P,N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity is at least

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{L-1}(P,N)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}+\frac{1}{L-1}\ln\frac{P}{L(P-N)}\right].

(43)

Remark 9
.

When
L\to\infty
, the above bound (
Equation

41
) converges to the list-decoding capacity
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}
for
L\to\infty
(see
Section

IV
).
In fact, it can be shown that list-size
L=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+1
is sufficient to achieve
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding rate
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}-{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
for any
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
(which ensures
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+1\geq 2
).
To see this, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}+\frac{1}{L-1}\ln\frac{P}{L(P-N)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\ln L}{L-1}-\frac{c}{L-1}+\ln\frac{L}{L-1}\right),

where we let
c\coloneqq\ln\frac{P}{P-N}>0
.
It then suffices to upper bound the term in parentheses on the RHS assuming
L=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+1\geq 2
.

\displaystyle\frac{\ln L}{L-1}-\frac{c}{L-1}+\ln\frac{L}{L-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln L}{L-1}-\frac{c}{L-1}+\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{L-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln L}{L-1}-\frac{c}{L-1}+\frac{1}{L-1}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+1\right)}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}-\frac{c-1}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}-\frac{c-1}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}-\frac{c-1}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

(45)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\ln\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}+\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}-\frac{(c-1)\varepsilon}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon),

provided that
\varepsilon
is independent of
c
.

Equations

45
and
44
both follow from the elementary inequality
\ln(1+x)\leq x
for any
x>-1
.
This is to be contrasted with the necessary condition
L=\Omega(\varepsilon)
to be derived in
Remark

18
from
Theorem

37
.

For
L=2
,
Equation

41
recovers the best known bound
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P^{2}}{4N(P-N)}
in
[
Bla62
]
.
Furthermore, it is tight at
N/P=0
where the optimal density is
\infty
and
N/P=\frac{L-1}{L}
where the optimal density is
0
.

XII-A

Proof of
Theorem

18
via normal approximation

We need the following lemma for approximating the density of a sum of uniform spherical vectors by that of a Gaussian (with a suitably chosen variance).

Lemma 20

(
[
ML15
]
, Proposition 2)
.

Let
P>0
.
Let
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}
be i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
.
Let
\widetilde{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}
be i.i.d. Gaussian vectors each with distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\underline{0},P\cdot I_{n})
.
Let
{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\widetilde{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
respectively.
Then for any
{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

{P_{{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}}({\underline{y}})}/{P_{\widetilde{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}}({\underline{y}})}\leq\kappa
,
where
\kappa=\kappa(P)>0
is a constant depending only on
P
and
L
but not
n
.

Assume
N=\frac{L-1}{L}P(1-\rho)
for some constant
\rho>0
.
Equivalently,
\rho=1-\frac{LN}{(L-1)P}
.
We are ready to compute
Equation

33
with
\varphi(\cdot)=\overline{\rad}^{2}(\cdot)
.

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[nP-\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq nN\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq n(P-N)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq n\left(\frac{P}{L}+\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)P\rho\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\asymp}}\Pr\left[\frac{-\delta}{P\sqrt{2n}}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)\leq 0\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\asymp}}\frac{\delta}{P\sqrt{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2P\sqrt{\pi n}}.

Equation

converges to

in distribution as

Equation

and height

.
∎

Putting

and applying elementary algebraic manipulation (dropping terms that are subexponential in

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\ln\mathop{\Pr}_{({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\sim Q^{\otimes L}}\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right]

XIII
Lower bounds via reduction to sphere packing

XIII-A

Bounded packings

In
[
BF63

, Blachman and Few proved another lower bound on the
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity.
Their observation is that a sphere packing is going to be
(L-1)
-list-decodable if we suitably dilate the balls so that they have multiplicity of overlap at most
L-1
.
The key lemma they used is the following by Few
[
Few64
, Theorem 2]
.
We reproduce it here with a simpler proof and use it to get lower bounds on the
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity and the
(N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity.

Lemma 25
.

Let
P,N>0
.
If
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a
(P,N,1)
-sphere packing, then it is also a
(P,N^{\prime},L)
-multiple packing for any
0\leq N^{\prime}<\frac{2(L-1)N}{L}
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction.
Suppose the conclusion of the lemma does not hold.
Then we can find an
L
-list
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying
\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\leq nN^{\prime}
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2L^{2}}L(L-1)\left(2\sqrt{nN}\right)^{2}=\frac{2(L-1)}{L}nN.

where the first equality is by
Equation

30
.
This is a contradiction for
N^{\prime}<\frac{2(L-1)}{L}N
and the proof is completed.
∎

We can combine the above lemma with the following lower bound on sphere packing density to get a lower bound on the
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity for any
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.
It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[

]
) that the

-sphere packing density is lower bounded as follows.

]

25
and

Replace

∎

XIV
Upper bounds

In this section, we prove an upper bound on the optimal density of multiple packings on the sphere.
We first show that any
(P,N,L-1)
-multiple packing must have a constant (independent of
n
) size if
N>\frac{L-1}{L}P
.
We then combine this result with an Elias–Bassalygo-type (
[
Bas65
]
) reduction to obtain an upper bound on the optimal density.

XIV-A

Plotkin bound

In this section, we prove a Plotkin-type bound.
It says that any
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decodable code living on a spherical cap of angular radius
\alpha
must have a constant size (in particular,
R({\mathcal{C}})\asymp 0
) if
\frac{N}{P}
is larger than the threshold
\frac{L-1}{L}(\sin\alpha)^{2}
.
When
\alpha=\pi
, it says that the
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity is zero as long as
N>\frac{L-1}{P}
.
Combined with lower bounds given by various constructions in the preceding sections, this gives us a sharp threshold of the largest amount of noise that a positive-rate list-decodable code can correct.

Theorem 33
.

Let
0<\alpha<\pi
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.
Let
P,N>0
be such that
\frac{N}{P}\geq\frac{L-1}{L}(\sin\alpha)^{2}(1+\rho)
for some
\rho>0
.
Then any list-decodable code
{\mathcal{C}}
on a spherical cap of angular radius
\alpha
on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
with list-decoding radius
\sqrt{nN}
and list-size at most
L-1
has size at most
|{\mathcal{C}}|\leq F(\rho^{-1}+1)
for some increasing function
F(\cdot)
.

We need some tools from Ramsey theory for the proof of the above Plotkin-type bound.

Definition 9

(Ramsey number)
.

The
N
-colour Ramsey number

{\mathscr{R}}_{N}(M)
is defined as the smallest integer
K
such that any
N
-colouring of the edges of a size-
K
complete graph must contain a monochromatic complete subgraph of size at least
M
.

It is well-known that
{\mathscr{R}}_{N}(M)
is finite.
There are bounds on
{\mathscr{R}}_{N}(M)
in the literature.
The following simple one suffices for the purposes of this section.

Theorem 34

(
[
WBBJ19
]
)
.

For any
N,M\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
,

{\mathscr{R}}_{N}(M)\leq 2^{N\cdot M^{N-1}}
.

We then present a subcode extraction lemma using the above Ramsey-theoretic tools.

Lemma 35
.

For any code
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
, there exist
\varepsilon\xrightarrow{|{\mathcal{C}}|\to\infty}0
, an increasing function
f(\cdot)
, a constant
0<d<4P
and a subcode
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
of size at least
f(|{\mathcal{C}}|)
such that
\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}\in[\sqrt{n(d-\varepsilon)},\sqrt{n(d+\varepsilon)}]
for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq|{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

The argument is somewhat standard in coding theory and we only give a proof sketch here.
See, e.g.,
[
Bli86
]
or
[
Bli12
, Lemma 4, Chapter 2]
for more detail.
Let us build a complete graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{C}}
on the code
{\mathcal{C}}
.
The vertices of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{C}}
are all codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
and every pair of codewords are connected by an edge.
Clearly, since
{\mathcal{C}}
is in
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
, the distance between any pair of codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
lies in the range
[0,2\sqrt{nP}]
.
Let us uniformly divide the above interval into subintervals each of length
\sqrt{2n\varepsilon}
.
To this end, we need
N\coloneqq\frac{4P}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{2P}{\varepsilon}
many subintervals.
6
6

6

For simplicity, we neglect divisiblility issues and assume that
2P/\varepsilon
is an integer.

Each subinterval is represented by its middle point
\sqrt{nd}
for some
d\in{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\left\{\varepsilon,3\varepsilon,5\varepsilon,\cdots,4P-\varepsilon\right\}
.
If we view each
d\in{\mathcal{S}}
as a colour, then
{\mathcal{S}}
induces an edge colouring of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{C}}
in the following sense.
If
\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}\in[\sqrt{n(d-\varepsilon)},\sqrt{n(d+\varepsilon)}]

({\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j})
gets colour

.
Given such an

-colouring of the edges of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathcal{C}}
, by Ramsey’s theorem (

), as long as the size of
{\mathcal{C}}
exceeds

.
In other words, any code
{\mathcal{C}}
contains a monochromatic subcode
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
of size at least
{\mathscr{R}}^{-1}_{N}(|{\mathcal{C}}|)
.
We have therefore proved the lemma by noting that monochromatic just means that all pairwise distances are
\varepsilon

\sqrt{nd}
for some

.
∎

We discuss here the

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}}\sum_{i\neq j\in{\mathcal{L}}}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\sum_{i\neq j\in[M]}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle\mathds{1}{\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\ni i,{\mathcal{L}}\ni j\right\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\sum_{i\neq j\in[M]}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}}\mathds{1}{\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\ni i,{\mathcal{L}}\ni j\right\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\sum_{i\neq j\in[M]}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle\binom{M-2}{L-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in[M]^{2}}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle-\sum_{i\in[M]}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\binom{M-2}{L-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\left(M^{2}\left\|\overline{{\underline{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-M\cdot nP\right)\binom{M-2}{L-2}.

(77)

In
Equation

77
, we let
\overline{{\underline{x}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{i\in[M]}{\underline{x}}_{i}
.
Note that the norm of
\overline{{\underline{x}}}
can be lower bounded as follows.

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{{\underline{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\in[M]}{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\in[M]}{\underline{x}}_{i}(j)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\in[M]}{\underline{x}}_{i}(1)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{M}\cdot M\cdot\sqrt{nP}\cos\alpha\right)^{2}

(78)

\displaystyle=nP\cdot(\cos\alpha)^{2}.

(79)

Equation

78
is by the assumption
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}
where
{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}
is defined by
Equation

71
.
Putting
Equation

79
back to
Equation

77
, we have

Equation

76

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\frac{1}{\binom{M}{L}}\left(M^{2}nP(\cos\alpha)^{2}-M\cdot nP\right)\binom{M-2}{L-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{L-1}{LM(M-1)}\left(M^{2}nP(\cos\alpha)^{2}-MnP\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{(L-1)M}{L(M-1)}nP(\cos\alpha)^{2}+\frac{L-1}{L(M-1)}nP

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP-\frac{L-1}{L}nP(\cos\alpha)^{2}+\frac{L-1}{L(M-1)}nP-\frac{L-1}{L(M-1)}nP(\cos\alpha)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L}nP(\sin\alpha)^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{M-1}\right).

(80)

Finally,

\displaystyle nN\leq\rad^{2}({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})=\min_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}}\rad^{2}\left(\left\{{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}}\right)\asymp\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{L}}\sim\binom{[M]}{L}}\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}(\left\{{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}})\right]\leq\frac{L-1}{L}nP(\sin\alpha)^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{M-1}\right).

(81)

The asymptotic equality follows from the following two points:
(i)
by

, for any
{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}
,
\rad^{2}(\left\{{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}})\asymp\overline{\rad}^{2}(\left\{{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}})
;
(ii)
by

Assume
\frac{N}{P}=\frac{L-1}{L}(\sin\alpha)^{2}(1+\rho)
for some constant
\rho>0

Rearranging terms on both sides of

\displaystyle\implies

Remark 16
.

In

XIV-B

Elias–Bassalygo bound

In this section, we combine the following covering lemma with
Theorem

33
to get an upper bound on the size of an arbitrary
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decodable code.

Lemma 36

(Folklore)
.

Let
0<\alpha<\pi
.
There exists a collection of
e^{n\left(\ln\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}+o(1)\right)}
spherical caps of angular radius
\alpha
such that they cover the whole sphere.

Remark 17
.

A random covering enjoys the property in
Lemma

36
with probability doubly exponentially close to one.
This lemma is well-known and we omit the proof.
In fact, the converse of
Lemma

36
is also true.
That is, any covering of the sphere by spherical caps of angular radius
\alpha
must have size at least
e^{n\left(\ln\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}-o(1)\right)}
.
We will not use the converse.

We combine
Theorem

33
and
Lemma

36
to prove the following upper bound on the
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity.

Theorem 37
.

Let
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
and
N,P>0
such that
\frac{N}{P}\leq\frac{L-1}{L}
.
The
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity
C_{L-1}(P,N)
is at most
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}
.

Remark 18
.

The above bound approaches
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}
as
L\to\infty
.
The latter quantity is known to be the list-decoding capacity in the large
L
limit (see
Section

IV
).
In fact, it can be shown that to achieve
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decoding rate at least
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}-\varepsilon
, the list-size has to be at least
\Omega(\varepsilon)
.
To see this, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{N}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{L-1}\right).

It then suffices to lower bound the error term:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{L-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4(L-1)},

using
\ln(1+x)\geq x/2
for any
0\leq x\leq 1/2
.
This implies
L\geq\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}+1=\Omega(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
.
A logarithmic gap is noted when contrasting the above necessary condition with the sufficient condition
L={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
derived in
Remark

9
from
Theorem

19
.

For
L=2
, the above bound becomes
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{P}{2N}
which is worse than the linear programing-type bound by Kabatiansky and Levenshtein
[
KL78
]
.
The above bound is tight (specifically, equals
\infty
and
0
, respectively) at
N/P=0
and
N/P=\frac{L-1}{L}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
be an arbitrary
(P,N,L-1)
-list-decodable code.
Let
0<\alpha<\pi
be such that
\frac{N}{P}=\frac{L-1}{L}(\sin\alpha)^{2}(1+\rho)
for some small constant
\rho>0
.
We cover
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
using spherical caps of angular radius
\alpha
.
By
Theorem

33
, on each spherical cap, the number of codewords is at most a constant
C_{\rho}=F(\rho^{-1}+1)
, independent of
n
.
By
Lemma

36
, the number of spherical caps is
e^{n\left(\ln\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}+o(1)\right)}
.
Therefore, the total number of codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
is at most
C_{\rho}\cdot e^{n\left(\ln\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}+o(1)\right)}
, meaning that the rate of
{\mathcal{C}}
is at most

\displaystyle\ln\frac{1}{\sin\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{(\sin\alpha)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(L-1)P(1+\rho)}{LN}.

The theorem is proved once
\rho
is sent to zero.
∎

Remark 19
.

Technically,
Theorem

37
is proved only for
spherical
codes.
However, by
Theorem

13
, this is without loss of generality since any capacity-achieving code can be turned into a spherical code.

The idea in
Theorem

33
can be adapted to the unbounded setting.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an arbitrary
(N,L-1)
-multiple packing of rate
R({\mathcal{C}})
.
The trick is to restrict
{\mathcal{C}}
to a large ball.
Specifically, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}\coloneqq{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
for a sufficiently large
P
such that

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})

\displaystyle\geq R({\mathcal{C}})-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)-\delta,

(82)

for a small
\delta>0
.
We divide
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
into a sequence of shells such that the norm of vectors in each shell is in the range
[\sqrt{n(P^{\prime}-\varepsilon)},\sqrt{n(P^{\prime}+\varepsilon)}]
for some
0\leq P^{\prime}\leq P
and some small
\varepsilon>0
.
By Markov’s inequality (
Lemma

6
), there must exist some
P^{\prime}\in[0,P]
such that the number of codewords in the shell of radius
\sqrt{nP^{\prime}}
is at least
\frac{\left|{\mathcal{C}}_{P}\right|}{P/(2\varepsilon)}
codewords.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{P^{\prime}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{P}
denote the subcode in this shell.
The rate of
{\mathcal{C}}_{P^{\prime}}
is asymptotically the same as that of
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}
.
If
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, it becomes negligible in the proof of
Theorem

33
.
Therefore,
Theorems

33
and
37
continue to hold for
{\mathcal{C}}_{P^{\prime}}
and we have

R({\mathcal{C}}_{P^{\prime}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{LN}{(L-1)P^{\prime}}
.
We do not have control on
P^{\prime}
and should take the extremal value
P^{\prime}=P
.
By
Equation

82
, we conclude

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\leq R({\mathcal{C}}_{P})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)+\delta\asymp R({\mathcal{C}}_{P^{\prime}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)+\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(L-1)P}{LN}-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eP)+\delta=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{L\cdot 2\pi eN}+\delta,

provided that
P
is sufficiently large and consequently
\delta
is sufficiently small.

We summarize our finding in the following theorem.

Theorem 38
.

Let
N>0
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.
The
(N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity
C_{L-1}(N)
is at most
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{L\cdot 2\pi eN}
.

Remark 20
.

The above bound approaches
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{2\pi eN}
as
L\to\infty
.
The latter quantity is known to be the list-decoding capacity in the large
L
limit (see
Section

IV
).

XV
Open questions

We end the paper with several intriguing open questions.

1.

The problem of packing spheres in
\ell_{p}
space was also addressed in the literature
[
Ran55
,
Spe70
,
Bal87
,
Sam13
]
.
Recently, there was an exponential improvement on the optimal packing density in
\ell_{p}
space
[
SSSZ20
]
relying on the Kabatiansky–Levenshtein bound
[
KL78
]
.
It is worth exploring the
\ell_{p}
version of the multiple packing problem.
One obstacle here is that the
\ell_{p}
average radius does not admit a closed form expression unlike the
p=2
case.

2.

We examined the performance of multiple packings obtained from the Gaussian distribution (
Section

XI
), the uniform distribution on a sphere (
Section

XII
) and the uniform distribution in a ball (
Section

XII-C
), respectively.
It turns out that these distributions are special cases of the
n
-dimensional beta distribution
\Beta_{n}(\beta)
when
\beta=\infty,-1,0
, respectively.
If one can prove a Khinchin’s inequality for beta distribution with sharp constants, then it allows us to unify our proofs for three distributions and potentially strengthen our bounds for spherical codes and ball codes.
Also, Khinchin’s inequality for beta distribution is of independent interest for probabilists.
