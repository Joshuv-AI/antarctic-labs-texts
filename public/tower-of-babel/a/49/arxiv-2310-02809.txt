Abstract
. We study the large-time behavior of an ensemble of entities obeying replicator-like stochastic dynamics with mean-field interactions as a model for a primordial ecology. We prove the propagation-of-chaos property and establish conditions for the strong persistence of the N N -replicator system and the existence of invariant distributions for a class of associated McKean-Vlasov dynamics. In particular, our results show that, unlike typical models of neutral ecology, fitness equivalence does not need to be assumed but emerges as a condition for the persistence of the system. Further, neutrality is associated with a unique Dirichlet invariant probability measure. We illustrate our findings with some simple case studies, provide numerical results, and discuss our conclusions in the light of Neutral Theory in ecology. Keywords: Stochastic replicator dynamics, Propagation of Chaos, Stochastic persistence, McKean-Vlasov equation, Invariant distributions, Emergence of ecologies. Mathematics Subject Classification (2010): 60H10 and 92D25.



1.
Introduction

The emergence of life corresponds to the emergence of an ecological system of interacting self-replicating entities. Several characteristics of early life have endured through time and are shared by all forms of life today, from the near universality of the genetic code to the intermediate metabolism
[
32
,
51
]
. It is unlikely that these biological universalities represent only "frozen accidents" linked to a universal common ancestry, but may emerge as a consequence of some fundamental principles associated with information and thermodynamics that affect the robustness and evolvability of biological systems
[
51
,
60
]
. In a similar vein, it is expected that early ecological systems faced important challenges to their persistence in fluctuating environments, where the number of co-existing entities increases forming diverse communities, and that some fundamental principles may also be invoked to understand their persistence. In this work, we are primarily interested in the
principle of neutrality
, which is mathematically expressed in a particular invariant probability distribution associated with the process of abundances of different types of entities (e.g. species) in an ecological system (e.g. community) where birth and death rates are linear, and thus entities can be considered as weakly interacting (e.g., a neutral ecology where species are equivalent in fitness,
[
26
]
) due to the operation of fitness equalizing mechanisms associated to tradeoffs and incompatible optima. These mechanisms render neutrality as a good approximation, even if entities weakly interact, as envisioned in the Red Queen and in the Neutral Theory of Ecology
[
57
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. In particular, using the neutrality approximation in a stochastic framework, we showed in
[
33
]
that a one-dimensional diffusion approximation for the frequency or proportional abundance of species in a community admits a Beta invariant distribution (the Proportional Species Abundance Distribution), the same as found, for instance, for gene frequencies in partially isolated populations
[
63
,
31
]
, which generalizes to a Dirichlet in the multidimensional case.

To model an early ecological system where to shed light on the importance of neutrality in early ecologies, we use a quintessential model for replicating entities, which has been applied to the modeling of polynucleotides in a container, the dynamics of gene frequencies, selection (Price equation), relative densities of interacting populations (Lotka-Volterra model), the frequency of different strategies in a population (game theoretical models), and adaptive dynamics
[
55
,
49
,
25
,
43
]
. One of the most basic and general equations for all these dynamics is the deterministic equation introduced by Taylor and Jonker
[
55
]
and dubbed
replicator equation
by Schuster and Sigmund
[
55
,
49
]
.

Thus, in this article, any given entity in our primordial ecological system will be represented by a replicator, and we will follow its proportional abundance through time. Now, since it can be argued that in the primordial soup, many entities shared a common habitat in this article we will extend the simple replicator dynamics to the setup of a large number of self-reproducing entities (community), constituting in this way an interactive set. In the setting we have chosen, the intraspecific dynamics of each singular replicator will be affected, in the form of a mean-field effect, by the dynamics of the other replicators in the system.

Is neutrality still plausible to emerge in this larger setting? and if so, What is its role?
The sections that follow are devoted to shed light on these questions.

1.1.
Preliminaries

The main motivation of Taylor and Jonker’s replicator model (
[
55
]
) was to unify many game-theoretic models that pursue the study of evolutionary stable strategies (ESS) in the idealized dynamics of animal conflicts as introduced by Maynard Smith and Price
[
52
]
. Recall that Taylor and Jonker’s replicator is a dynamical system
({\bf x}(t):t\geq 0)
taking values on the
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex:

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{d}:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\},

obeying the ordinary differential equation:

(1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d{\bf x}}{dt}={\bf x}\circ(A{\bf x}-\left\langle{\bf x},A{\bf x}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}),

for some initial condition
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Here,
\circ
stands for the Hadamard product (entry-wise),
A
is a suitable
d\times d
real matrix termed the payoff matrix, and
\mathbf{1}
stands for the vector of dimensions
d
with all its entries equal to
1
. Equation (
1
) is intended to model the dynamics of the proportions
(x_{i}:i=1,\ldots,d)
of individuals playing contesting strategies in (the continuous-time approximation of) a multi-round, two-player game: if each player can choose among strategies
1,2,\ldots,d
, the entry
A_{ij}
is interpreted as the payoff from using strategy
i
against an individual using strategy
j
. The term
-\left\langle{\bf x},A{\bf x}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}
modulates the fitness variation in such a way that the fitness of a player of type
i
increases if the payoff is greater than the average payoff of the community (see
[
25
,
20
,
23
]
for a thorough study of these systems in the context of evolutionary game theory).

Many generalizations of the original replicator dynamics can be considered. For example, we can obtain the generalized replicator dynamics:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d{\bf x}}{dt}={\bf x}\circ(F({\bf x})-\left\langle{\bf x},F({\bf x})\right\rangle\mathbf{1}),

for some suitable instantaneous fitness function
F:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this form, replicator dynamics has become a general model for the evolution of populations under frequency-dependent selection, “flexible enough to cover a great deal of evolution models, suggesting a unifying view of replicator selection from the primordial soup up to animal societies” (
[
49
]
). The mathematical analysis of general replicator equations has been extensive (
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
61
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
41
]
,
[
42
]
,
[
10
]
),
[
8
]
, testifying to its fundamental role in the development of evolutionary game theory.

Stochastic versions of the replicator equation have a long tradition. From the initial efforts of Foster and Young (
[
14
]
) and Fudenberg and Harris (
[
17
]
) in exploring the replicator equation in a stochastic differential equation form (see also
[
1
]
,
[
46
]
), the analysis of stochastic replicator equations have become increasingly complex, going beyond the case of only two pure strategies (
[
29
]
) to replicator dynamics with Stratonovich-type perturbations (
[
30
]
), to the analysis of stochastic persistence (
[
3
]
), variations in game dynamics with a potential function (
[
2
]
) to the inclusion of revision protocols such as imitation (
[
47
]
,
[
37
]
).

It must be said that the interaction between the main system and the noise can be interpreted in many different ways. For example, Fudenberg and Harris’s version of the stochastic replicator can be obtained from the following considerations regarding the instantaneous fitness (see
[
17
]
). Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{++}
be the cone of strictly positive real
d
-tuples, let
{\bf Y}_{t}=(Y^{(1)}_{t},Y^{(2)}_{t},\ldots,Y^{(d)}_{t})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{++}
be the vector of abundances at time
t\geq 0
of the
d
types in a focal population, and let:

\displaystyle{\bf X}_{t}=\dfrac{{\bf Y}_{t}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y^{(i)}_{t}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d},

be the relative proportions at time
t
of these types. We prescribe a stochastic evolution of the abundances through the equation.

(2)

\displaystyle d{\bf Y}_{t}={\bf Y}_{t}\circ(A{\bf X}_{t}dt+\Sigma d{\bf W}_{t}),

where
\Sigma:=\text{{diag}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
is a diagonal non-singular matrix and
{\bf W}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{d})
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener process defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and adapted to a right-continuous and augmented filtration
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t}:t\geq 0)
. The rationale behind this evolution is easy to grasp: the instantaneous fitness of type
i
is the payoff associated with the strategy of playing
i
at the density level
{\bf X}
,
plus
a random perturbation. In (
2
) the stochastic term is generally interpreted as environmental fluctuations due, for example, to high-frequency variations of the abiotic or biotic factors that underlie the ecological dynamics. Depending on the time scale of the phenomenon we are interested in, these variations can come from different sources: temperature fluctuations, changes in resource availability, among others.

From equation (
2
), it is easy to show that the dynamics of the proportion obeys the SDE:

(3)

\displaystyle d{\bf X}_{t}={\bf X}_{t}\circ(\tilde{A}{\bf X}_{t}-\left\langle{\bf X}_{t},\tilde{A}{\bf X}_{t}\right\rangle\mathbf{1})dt+\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bf X}_{t}\circ\left(\Sigma e_{i}-\left\langle{\bf X}_{t},\Sigma e_{i}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}\right)dW_{t}^{(i)},

where
\tilde{A}=A-\Sigma\Sigma^{\top}
and
e_{i}
is the
i
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Hofbauer and Imhof
[
22
]
studied equation (
3
), with a focus on the time-averages of the dynamics. Among other results, the authors show that under some conditions on the payoff matrix, the process is positive recurrent and its transition probability function admits an invariant Dirichlet distribution. Interestingly, this holds when the sum of the off-diagonal payoffs and the diagonal obey a simple linear relation (this is their Corollary 3.12).
This symmetry can be regarded as equivalent to a type of neutrality, as described in the first paragraphs of this introduction, and in fact, induces the emergence of a neutral ecology in replicator dynamics, which provides a remarkable connection to the existing neutral theory in community ecology
[
26
,
33
]
. Thus, in this contribution we will use the replicator equation in a stochastic framework as a vehicle to understand the emergence of primordial ecologies and test for the emergence and generality of the fitness equivalence principle outlined in
[
22
,
33
]
, in the context of a community of interacting replicators, as explained next.

1.2.
Our model: interacting replicators

For measurable functions
F:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
, write:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}F({\bf x}):={\bf x}\circ(F({\bf x})-\left\langle{\bf x},F({\bf x})\right\rangle\mathbf{1}),

and extend the definition to matrix-valued functions by tensorisation, namely: if
\mathbf{F}=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{m})
where
F_{i}:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded and measurable, then
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{F}
is the
d\times m
matrix whose
j
-th column is
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}F_{j}({\bf x})
. With this, equation (
3
) can be written as:

(4)

\displaystyle d{\bf X}_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf X}_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}_{t})d{\bf W}_{t},

for a suitable vector field
\Phi
and a suitable matrix-valued function
\Psi
; here
{\bf W}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on a filtered probability space. This is the basic replicator equation we will be working with in this contribution.

Now, to capture the interaction of one replicator with other of the same type, we assume that a smooth function
\Upsilon:\mathbb{S}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given. The notation
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf x},{\bf y})
stands for the action of
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}
on the first variable. Let be given a natural number
N
, and consider the
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
-valued stochastic process defined via

\displaystyle d{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t})dt

(5)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t},{\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t})d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{t},

for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. Here,
({\bf W}^{(i)},i=1,2,\ldots,N)
is a family of independent
\mathbb{F}
-adapted,
d
-dimensional standard Wiener processes carrying the local noises (i.e., noise associated to each species represented by a single replicator).

To fix ideas, consider the case
d=2
,
\Phi
linear and
\Psi
a constant matrix, and assume, for instance, that
\Upsilon
has the form:

\displaystyle\Upsilon({\bf x},{\bf y})=\delta y_{1}\mathbf{E}{\bf x},\qquad\mathbf{E}=\Bigg(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\Bigg)

where
\delta
is a small parameter. We will return to this example in Section
5
, and so it seems just right to use it to illustrate the meaning of the equations above. Indeed, in this case, we can provide an intuitive interpretation of equation (
1.2
): the payoff obtained from using the first strategy against a player using the second one increases proportionally to the overall average frequency of the usage of the first strategy amongst the family of replicators. Thus, at least intuitively, in this system of interacting replicators, the first strategy becomes more resilient, and thus one should expect higher average usage of the first strategy as compared to the situation with no interaction. Of course, the overall effect must take account of the particular form of the intrinsic payoff matrix (the diagonal elements could restrain a net bias to the use of the first strategy) and the noise part (if it is large, randomness may dominate the dynamics), but as we will see later, under appropriate assumptions, the intuition above is not far from the actual long-term picture.

Remark 1
.

In this article, we are considering the case of interacting replicators of just one type, which could be akin to traditional species that are either phylogenetically and functionally close and thus share a similar niche, trophic level, and interactions within communities, as envisioned in the principle of niche conservatism
[
62
]
. As we will see below, this will translate in that, for large
N
, the individual replicators behave as statistically identical entities. The multi-type model (which would amount to mean-field interactions of
N_{1}
replicators of type
1
,
N_{2}
replicators of type
2
, etc.; see for instance models of this type associated to neurosciences
[
13
]
, social sciences
[
7
]
or statistical mechanics
[
6
]
) is technically more involved and represents a reasonable second step in our research program.

Systems analogous to equation (
1.2
) are generically referred to as
mean-field interacting particle systems
, and one of the main problems associated with them is the determination of conditions under which, in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, the assembly of particles (in our case, replicators) behave as if they were independent. This is the
propagation of chaos property
, whose precise content will be given later. For the time being, assume that we are interested in the study of the system (
1.2
) on a finite time horizon and for
large values of

N
. Write
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
for the empirical probability measure induced on the simplex by
\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t}:=({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t},{\bf X}^{(N;2)}_{t},\ldots,{\bf X}^{(N;N)}_{t})^{\top}
, namely:

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{(N)}(A):=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}}(A).

With a similar notation,
\mu_{[0,t]}^{(N)}(A)
stands for the random probability measure on
\mathcal{C}([0,t];\mathbb{S}^{d})
given by:

(6)

\displaystyle\mu^{(N)}(A)=\mu_{[0,t]}^{(N)}(A):=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{[0,t]}}(A).

The following convention will be used in the sequel. For an arbitrary metric space
E
and a measurable function
G:\mathbb{S}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto E
, we will write:

\displaystyle G({\bf x},\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}G({\bf x},{\bf y})\mu(d{\bf y}),

and thus, we can regard
G
as a map
\mathbb{S}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\mapsto E
. With this convention, equation (
1.2
) can be written as:

(7)

\displaystyle d{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t},\mu^{(N)}_{t})dt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(X_{t}^{(N;i)})d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{t},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,N.

Assume that we know that for each
t
in a finite time horizon, the random probability measures
(\mu_{t}^{(N)}:N\geq 1)
converge (almost surely in the weak topology of probability measures) to a certain deterministic probability measure,
\mu_{t}
say. Then, at least intuitively, we should have that for large values of
N
, the dynamics of a typical particle should be governed by:

(8)

\displaystyle d\Xi_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi(\Xi_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon(\Xi_{t},\mu_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi_{t})d{\bf W}_{t},

and, following a Glivenko-Cantelli-like reasoning, it should be apparent that
\mu_{t}
is nothing but the law of
\Xi_{t}
. Equation (
8
) is of the McKean-Vlasov type, and its solutions (whenever they exist) induce a paradigmatic example of a
non-linear Markov process
. Here, the term non-linear has nothing to do with the algebraic degree of the unknown
\Xi
in (
8
) but rather with the fact that the random dynamics depends on the very distribution of the Markov process; in turn, it translates into a non-linearity at the level of the evolution equation associated to the dynamics. There is a rich literature on McKean-Vlasov SDE, with the connection with nonlinear PDE being an important research topic (see the classical
[
35
]
for the motivations underlying this kind of process).

The aim of this article is to study the link between the mean-field system (
1.2
) and the McKean-Vlasov equation (
8
); to study the persistence (in a sense to be defined below) for the mean-field system and to prove existence and uniqueness of non-trivial invariant measures for the McKean-Vlasov equation in some simple cases. All these issues are treated with an eye on the problem posed at the opening of the introduction, namely the emergence and role of neutrality in large interacting sets of biological entities. The organization of the paper reflects closely this sequence of problems. In Section
2
we present all the mathematical preliminaries that allow us to put the models on firm grounds, and we describe our assumptions and main results. Then, we prove the analytical properties of the processes and provide a result of the propagation of chaos linking the
N
-replicator dynamics and the McKean-Vlasov system (Section
3
). Then, for the
N
-replicator systems we investigate conditions that guarantee
the persistence of the whole set of replicators
(Section
4
). For the non-linear replicator, we prove the existence and uniqueness of invariant measures for a particular type of interaction functions and noise (Section
5
). Section
6
shows some simulations and numerical results related to the model treated in Section
5
. Finally, Section
7
discuss our results and points out open questions regarding our research.

2.2.
The stochastic replicator

The structure of the SDEs (and later, McKean-Vlasov type equations) that we are going to consider take their general form from a geometric consideration. Recall that the Shahshahani metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{++}
is given by the metric tensor:

(9)

s_{i,j}({\bf x})=\frac{\norm{\xx}_{1}}{x_{i}}\delta_{i,j},\;({\bf x}\in E,\;i,j=1,\ldots,d).

(see
[
50
]
for further details). The gradient of a
C^{1}
-function
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}\to\mathbb{R}
is transformed by the Shahshahani metric into
S({\bf x})^{-1}\nabla U({\bf x})
, where
S^{-1}({\bf x})
is the inverse of the diagonal matrix
S({\bf x})
whose entries are
s_{i,j}
, that is

S({\bf x})^{-1}\nabla U({\bf x})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{x_{1}}{\norm{\xx}_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}U({\bf x})\\
\vdots\\
\frac{x_{d}}{\norm{\xx}_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}U({\bf x})\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\norm{\xx}_{1}}{\bf x}\circ\nabla U({\bf x}),

where
\circ
denotes Hadamard product. In particular, for
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}
one gets
S({\bf x})^{-1}\nabla U({\bf x})={\bf x}\circ\nabla U({\bf x})
. This gradient projected onto the tangent bundle at
{\bf x}
(hereafter,
\mathcal{T}_{\bf x}=\mathcal{T}
) gives:

(10)

{\bf x}\circ\nabla U({\bf x})-\langle{\bf x}\circ\nabla U({\bf x}),\mathbf{1}\rangle{\bf x}={\bf x}\circ(\nabla U({\bf x})-\langle{\bf x},\nabla U({\bf x})\rangle\mathbf{1}).

Thus, at least if
A
in equation (
1
) is a symmetric payoff matrix, the original deterministic replicator has the form of a gradient-type dynamics on the simplex.

Notice, however, that the right-hand side of equation (
10
) makes sense if we replace
\nabla U
by any
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued function
\bf F
. From now on, write:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}{\bf F}({\bf x}):={\bf x}\circ({\bf F}({\bf x})-\left\langle{\bf F}({\bf x}),{\bf x}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}),

and extend in an obvious way the definition for matrix-valued functions
{\bf G}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Psi:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given. We assume that, at least, both

and

\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

such that

for every

by definition. Note also that by our assumptions, both drift and diffusion components are Lipschitz on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, and thus it is well known that there exists a unique (pathwise) solution to the SDE (
4

\mathbb{S}^{d}
-valued Markov process. Let
(P^{(\Phi,\Psi)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
be the semigroup associated to the Markov process
({\bf X}_{t}:t\geq 0)
associated to the characteristics
(\Phi,\Psi)
. As usual, we consider the semigroup acting from the left on the space of bounded Borel function and acting from the right on Borel measures on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Let

be a Borel set of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. It is a routine fact that

is a Feller semigroup. By compactness, it admits invariant measures

. Recall that a probability measure

is invariant for the semigroup (equivalently, invariant for the process) if
\mu P^{(\Phi,\Psi)}_{t}=\mu
for every

\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{inv}(\Phi,\Psi;B)
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{erg}(\Phi,\Psi;B)
be the subset of
\mathcal{P}_{inv}(\Phi,\Psi;B)
of extremal invariant probability measures supported on

(that is, ergodic probability measures that put no mass on
\mathbb{S}^{d}\setminus B
).

It is well known that for well-behaved Markov processes (for example, for processes whose semigroups enjoy the strong Feller property), different ergodic measures have disjoint supports. Thus, in order to obtain the uniqueness of an ergodic probability measure supported on the interior of the simplex, it seems reasonable to impose conditions on the noise in such a way that no submanifold of dimension less than
d-1
is kept invariant by the dynamics. So, we will impose the following non-degeneracy of the noise.

Assumptions 1
.

(11)

\displaystyle\textbf{span}(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x})_{\cdot,j},j=1,\ldots,m)=\mathcal{T}_{\bf x},

for all
{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
.

Following
[
3
]
, to assess the stochastic persistence of the process
{\bf X}
(and later, the stochastic persistence of interacting replicators), we are going to impose conditions on the natural deterministic process associated with it (namely, the process with no diffusion term). Define the
invasion growth rate
:

(12)

\displaystyle h_{i}({\bf x})=\Phi({\bf x})_{i}-\langle{\bf x},\Phi({\bf x})\rangle,

and set
{\bf h}:=(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{d})^{\top}
. Observe that the
i
-th invasion rate is just the exponent associated to the
i
-th population in the deterministic dynamics. Now, if
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{inv}(\Phi,0;\mathbb{S}^{d})
is an invariant probability measure for the deterministic replicator under which type
i
is absent, then
\mu
can be regarded as an equilibrium distribution for a given strict population with strategies or types indexed by
I\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
with
i\notin I
. Consider the following condition.

For every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{erg}(\Phi,0;\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}\mu h_{i}>0.

The biological interpretation of this condition is just the requirement that any strict population of types in ecological equilibrium (for the deterministic dynamics) allows for the invasion of at least one of the absent types. It turns out that the above condition is equivalent to the following assumption (see e.g.
[
48
]
).

Assumptions 2
.

There exists
{\bf p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{d})^{\top}
a vector of strictly positive components such that:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{erg}(\Phi,0;\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})}\mu\langle{\bf p},{\bf h}\rangle=:\rho>0.

The next theorem is just Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
, which we state here for future comparison.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
\Phi
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then, for every
r>0
, there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(r)>0
such that for every
\Psi
of class
\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
such that
|||{\bf A}_{\Psi}|||<\varepsilon
the following holds:

(1)

\mathcal{P}_{inv}(int(\mathbb{S}^{d}))
is a singleton, i.e., there exists a unique invariant probability measure

supported on the interior of the simplex for the system (

is absolutely-continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure, and the function

is
\mu
-integrable.

(2)

There exist positive constants

such that:

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\delta_{{\bf x}}P_{t},\mu)\leq\dfrac{Ce^{-\alpha t}}{\mathpzc{dist}({\bf x},\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})^{r}},\quad{\bf x}\in int(\mathbb{S}^{d}).

In this article, we extend the previous result to the setup of a family of replicators interacting in a mean-field regime. The next subsection provides the details.

2.3.
Mean-field interacting replicators and McKean-Vlasov evolutionary games

As a first step, we extend the definition of our parameters to account for the interaction among replicators. Let be given a bounded measurable map
\Upsilon:\mathbb{S}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf x},\mu)=\mu\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf x},\cdot)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\mu\Upsilon({\bf x},\cdot).

We assume the following regularity.

Assumptions 3
.

\Upsilon
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}

In what follows, sometimes we will need to refer to the joint effect of both the endogenous and the interaction functions. To that aim, we define:

which is continuous as a map from

is the weak topology of probability measures. With this notation, equation (

\displaystyle d\Xi_{t}={\bf b}(\Xi_{t},\mu_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi_{t})d{\bf W}_{t},

Remark 3
.

Our hypotheses entail that

\displaystyle\|{\bf b}({\bf x},\mu)-{\bf b}({\bf y},\nu)\|_{p}\leq C(p,d)\bigg(\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|_{p}+\textbf{Was}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\bigg).

The following result is proved in the next section.

Proposition 4
.

Under the above assumptions, Equation (

3.1.
Elementary results

We start with a proposition that regards the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the nonlinear McKean-Vlasov equation. Its proof is relatively standard, and consequently, it is deferred to the Apprendix
A.2

Proposition 8
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
3
, equation (
14
) has a unique pathwise (hence strong) solution.

Observe that the process
\Xi
is
not
a Markov process. Indeed, the family of maps
(P_{s}:s\geq 0)
acting on
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
defined via

\displaystyle P_{s}f({\bf x}):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}}(f(\Xi_{s})),

do not determine a semigroup of linear operators, in neat contrast to the Markovian setup. However, the semigroup property still holds in the dual sense. Define the one-parameter family of operators:

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

given by the prescription:
\mathcal{Q}_{t}\mu=\textbf{Law}_{\mu}(\Xi_{t})
, the law of
\Xi_{t}
when started from an initial condition distributed as
\mu
.

Proposition 9
.

The family of operators
(\mathcal{Q}_{t}:t\geq 0)
enjoys the semigroup property:

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{t+s}=\mathcal{Q}_{t}\mathcal{Q}_{s},

for all
s,t\geq 0
.

Remark 10
.

Still, the operators
\mathcal{Q}_{s}
are non-linear: for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

\lambda\in[0,1]
, in general:

\displaystyle(\lambda\mu+(1-\lambda)\nu)\mathcal{Q}_{t}\neq\lambda\mu\mathcal{Q}_{t}+(1-\lambda)\nu\mathcal{Q}_{t}.

Proof.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, and
t,s\geq 0
. Put
\mu_{t}:=\mathcal{Q}_{t}\mu
. Consider random variables
\Xi_{0}
and
\Xi_{1}
distributed as
\mu
and
\mu_{t}
respectively, and the processes
(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{t+s}:s\geq 0)
and
(\Xi^{(\Xi_{1})}_{s}:s\geq 0)
. By (
14
), for

, on the one hand we have:

\displaystyle\Xi_{t+s}^{(\mu)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny Law}}}}{{=}}}\Xi_{t+s}^{(\Xi_{0})}=\Xi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}({\bf b}(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r},\tilde{\Xi}^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r}))dr+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r})d{\bf W}_{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}({\bf b}(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r+t},\tilde{\Xi}^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r+t}))dr+\int_{0}^{s}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{t+r})d{\bf W}_{t+r}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny Law}}}}{{=}}}\Xi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}({\bf b}(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r},\tilde{\Xi}^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r}))dr+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r})d{\bf W}_{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}({\bf b}(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r+t},\tilde{\Xi}^{(\Xi_{0})}_{r+t}))dr+\int_{0}^{s}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(\Xi_{0})}_{t+r})d\tilde{\bf W}_{r}.

where

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny Law}}}}{{=}}}\Xi_{1}+\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}({\bf b}(Y_{r},\tilde{Y}_{r}))dr+\int_{0}^{s}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(Y_{r})d{\bf W}_{r}

\displaystyle=Y_{s}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny Law}}}}{{=}}}\Xi_{s}^{(\mu_{t})},

where the first-to-last line follows from pathwise uniqueness of the solutions of (

({\bf W}^{(1)}_{\cdot},...,{\bf W}^{(N)}_{\cdot})
. Notice that this implies that the law of
({\bf X}^{(N;1)}_{\cdot},...,{\bf X}^{(N;N)}_{\cdot})
is symmetric.

Theorem 14
.

(Propagation of Chaos). Let
(\Xi^{(i)}:i=1,\ldots,N)
be a family of independent copies of the non-linear process solution of (
14
), such that
\Xi^{(i)}_{0}={\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{0}
for every
i=1,\ldots,N
almost surely, and both
{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{\cdot}
and
\Xi^{(i)}_{\cdot}
are driven by indistinguishable Brownian motions. Then, under the above assumptions, for any finite time horizon
[0,S]
there exists a positive constant
K=K(S,d)
such that for every
i=1,\ldots,N
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,S]}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}-\Xi^{(i)}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{K}{N}.

Proof.

Let
{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{\cdot}
and
\Xi^{(i)}_{\cdot}
be the processes specified above, and let
S>0
. Then, for any
t\in[0,S]
we have:

\displaystyle\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}-\Xi^{(i)}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2S\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\bf b}({\bf X}_{s}^{(N;i)},\mu_{s}^{(N)})-{\bf b}(\Xi_{s}^{(i)},\mu_{s})\right\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad+2\left\|\int_{0}^{t}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s})-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(i)}_{s})]d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}.

Now, for the first term of the right-hand side, by the Lipschitz condition of
{\bf b}
, there exists a
K_{1}=K_{1}(S,d)>0
such that:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\bf b}({\bf X}_{s}^{(N;i)},\mu_{s}^{(N)})-{\bf b}(\Xi_{s}^{(i)},\mu_{s})\right\|^{2}ds\leq K_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s}-\Xi^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+K_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{s}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}x\mu_{s}(dx)\right\|^{2}ds.

On the other hand, notice that the term
\int_{0}^{\cdot}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s})-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(i)}_{s})]d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{s}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t}:t\geq 0)
square-integrable martingale, and thus by Doob’s Maximal inequality we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,S]}\left\|\int_{0}^{t}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s})-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(i)}_{s})]d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{S}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s})-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(i)}_{s})]d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}\right).

By Itô’s isometry and the Lipschitz assumption on
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x})
, there exists a constant
K_{2}=K_{2}(S,d)>0
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{S}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s})-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi^{(i)}_{s})]d{\bf W}^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{S}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{s}-\Xi^{(i)}_{s}\right\|^{2}ds\right).

Therefore, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,S]}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}-\Xi^{(i)}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq K_{3}\int_{0}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{u}-\Xi^{(i)}_{u}\right\|^{2}\right)ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+K_{3}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{u}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}x\mu_{u}(dx)\right\|^{2}\right)ds,

for some a constant
K_{3}=K_{3}(S,K_{1},K_{2})
.

Recall that we have assumed that
(\Xi^{(1)}_{\cdot},\ldots,\Xi^{(N)}_{\cdot})
are
N
i.i.d. copies obeying (
14
) such that for all
j=1,...,N
,
\Xi^{(j)}_{0}={\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{0}
almost surely, and
\Xi^{(j)}_{\cdot}
and
{\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{\cdot}
are driven by indistinguishable Brownian motions. Then:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{u}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}x\mu_{u}(dx)\right\|^{2}\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{u}-\Xi^{(j)}_{u})\right\|^{2}\right)ds

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{2}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\Xi^{(j)}_{u}-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}x\mu_{u}(dx))\right\|^{2}\right)ds.

The last term above is
O(N^{-1})
by the Law of Large Numbers and, on the other hand, we have that:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{u}-\Xi^{(j)}_{u})\right\|^{2}\right)ds\leq\int_{0}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{u\leq s}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{u}-\Xi^{(i)}_{u}\right\|^{2}\right)ds,

since, by symmetry, the laws of the
({\bf X}^{(N;j)}_{\cdot}-\Xi^{(j)}_{\cdot})
’s are exchangeable.
Finally, by applying Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,S]}\left\|{\bf X}^{(N;i)}_{t}-\Xi^{(i)}_{t}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq O(1/N)\exp\{2K_{3}S\},

and therefore the theorem follows.

We deduce from the previous result that the sequence of empirical measures
(\mu^{(N)})_{N}
converges to the deterministic limit
\mu:=\textbf{Law}(\Xi_{[0,t]})
, in the weak sense. This fact plus the property that the law of the
N
-replicator
({\bf X}^{(N;1)}_{\cdot},...,{\bf X}^{(N;N)}_{\cdot})
is symmetric (or exchangeable), implies that, for any
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
, ….,
g_{k}
continuous and bounded functions on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, with
k\leq N
, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}^{(N)}_{\mu^{\otimes N}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}g_{k}({\bf X}^{(N;\sigma(i))}_{\cdot})\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\mu,g_{i}\right\rangle,

where
({\bf X}^{(N;\sigma(1))}_{\cdot},...,{\bf X}^{(N;\sigma(N))}_{\cdot})
corresponds to any permutation of the vector
({\bf X}^{(N;1)}_{\cdot},...,{\bf X}^{(N;N)}_{\cdot})
(see Proposition 2.2.i) in
[
54
]
or Proposition 4.2 in
[
36
]
). That is, the
N
-particle system in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
becomes a sequence of independent and identically distributed solutions of the McKean-Vlasov equation.

4.
Strong stochastic persistence for the N-replicator system

In this section we address the problem of persistence of the Markov processes
(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
. For
N\geq 1
, the notation
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
stands for the
N
-fold product of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. The space
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
is endowed with the topology of the topological sum. Since
\mathbb{S}^{d}
is Polish, the same holds for its
N
-fold sum, for the metric:

\displaystyle\mathpzc{dist}_{N}(\vec{\bf x},\vec{\bf y}):=\max_{i}\mathpzc{dist}({\bf x}^{(i)},{\bf y}^{(i)}),

where the notation
\mathpzc{dist}
in the right-hand side stands for the
L^{1}
distance on the simplex. We further set:

\displaystyle\mathpzc{dist}_{N}(\vec{\bf x},\partial(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}):=\min_{j}\mathpzc{dist}({\bf x}^{(j)},\partial\mathbb{S}^{d}).

Let
P^{(N)}
be the semigroup associated with the
N
-replicator system (
7
). The action of the generator associated with the semigroup on smooth functions can be expressed as follows. Given
{\bf x}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf x}^{(N)}
arbitrary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will write
\vec{{\bf x}}
for the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
resulting from the concatenation, in lexicographic order, of the components of the
{\bf x}^{(i)}
’s. For a given smooth function
\varphi:(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
\vec{\bf x}\in(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
we denote by
\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}
the map
\mathbb{S}^{d}\ni{\bf y}\mapsto\varphi({\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf x}^{(N)})
where
{\bf y}
is in the place of the
i
-th block of order
d
. A straightforward computation shows that whenever
\varphi
is a twice-continuously differentiable real-valued function, the action of the generator is given by:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}\varphi(\vec{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf x}^{(i)},\mu_{\vec{\bf x}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr\left(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})^{\top}\nabla\nabla^{\top}\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)})\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right),

where:
\mu_{\vec{{\bf x}}}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bf x}^{(i)}}
, and
\nabla\nabla^{\top}\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}
is the Hessian matrix associated to
\varphi^{(i)}_{\vec{\bf x}}
.

Definition 15
.

(see
[
19
]
,
[
59
]
)
We say that a
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
-valued, right-continuous Markov process
(Y_{t}:t\geq 0)
is
strongly stochastically persistent
(SSP) if there exists a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
such that
\pi(\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))=0
and such that for every
{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
:

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{x}(Y_{t}\in\cdot),\pi(\cdot))\rightarrow 0,

as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Here we will prove that under our running assumptions,
(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
is a SSP processes taking values on
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. In fact, we will prove an extension of Theorem
2
and, in particular, we will obtain geometric rates of convergence for both processes.

To this end, we introduce some notation. We will say that
{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is
\varepsilon
-close to extinction if there exists
i\in I
such that
x_{i}<\varepsilon
. For given
\varepsilon
, define:

\displaystyle\text{Ext}(\varepsilon):=\{{\bf x}\text{ is }\varepsilon-\text{close to extinction}\}.

We write
\text{Ext}_{N}(\varepsilon):=\{\vec{\bf x}\in(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}:{\bf x}^{(i)}\in\text{Ext}(\varepsilon)\text{ for some }i\}
.

To prove convergence to an invariant measure for
(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
, we first observe that it is enough to prove that for every
t>0
there is convergence for the chain
(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{n}:=\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{tn}:n\geq 0)
, i.e. the chain sampled from
\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}
every
t
time units. This claim follows from the Feller property of the semigroup
(P^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
(which can be derived in a rather standard way from the Lipschitz continuity of the parameters) and from the following general fact about Feller processes.

Lemma 16
.

Let
(P_{t}:t\geq 0)
be the Feller semigroup associated with a Markov process
(Y_{t}:t\geq 0)
taking values on a compact metric space
(E,\rho)
. Denote by
(P^{*}_{t}:t\geq 0)
the dual semigroup acting on finite measures. Fix
F\in\mathcal{B}(E)
. Assume that for every
s\in(0,1]
there exists a unique probability measure
\pi_{s}
on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
with
\pi_{s}(F^{C})=0
and such that
P^{*}_{s}\pi_{s}=\pi_{s}
. Then the process
Y
has a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
with
\pi(F^{C})=0
.

Proof.

For every
t\in(0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
, the unique invariant measure for
P^{*}_{t}
is also invariant for
P^{*}_{1}
. Hence,
\pi_{t}=\pi_{1}
for every rational
t
. Then, for general
t
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}

\displaystyle=P^{*}_{\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}}\pi_{1},

Since
P
is Feller and
E
is compact,
P^{*}
is a weakly-continuous semigroup. Hence, sending
n
to
\infty
in the last equation, we obtain
\pi_{1}f=P^{*}_{t}\pi_{1}f
for every
f\in\mathcal{C}(E)
. Since the class of continuous functions is measure-determining on
E
, we get that
\pi_{1}=P^{*}_{t}\pi_{1}
, and by the uniqueness assumption,
\pi_{t}=\pi_{1}
.

Fix
t>0
, and let
P:=P^{(N)}_{t}
. Consider the following statement.

[H]
There exists a continuous function
\mathcal{H}:\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
and constants
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that:

(1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf_{\vec{\bf x}\in\text{Ext}_{N}(\varepsilon)}\mathcal{H}({\bf x})=\infty
.

(2)

P\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})\leq\alpha\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+C
for every
\vec{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
.

Observe that
[H]
entails that for any
{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
, any weak limit point, say
\nu
, of the sequence of probability
(\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P^{n})_{n}
satisfies
\nu(\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))=0
. For suppose this is not the case, and given
\vec{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
, assume that a limit point
\nu
of
(\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P^{n})_{n}
satisfies
\nu(\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))>2\varepsilon
for a given
\varepsilon>0
. Apply
n
times the second condition of
[H]
to get:

\displaystyle P^{n}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})\leq\alpha^{n}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+D,

where
D:=\dfrac{C}{1-\alpha}
. Define
\eta:=\dfrac{\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+D}{\varepsilon}
and consider the set
K_{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{H}^{-1}([0,\eta])
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is continuous,
K_{\varepsilon}
is a closed (indeed, compact) set contained in
\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
(by the first point at
[H]
). Then, for every
n
, Markov inequality yields:

(17)

\displaystyle P^{n}\mathbf{1}_{K_{\varepsilon}^{C}}(\vec{\bf x})\leq\varepsilon.

Consider a continuous function
g:(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}\mapsto[0,1]
with the property
g\equiv 1
in
\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
,
g\equiv 0
on
K_{\varepsilon}
. Since
g\leq\mathbf{1}_{K_{\varepsilon}^{C}}
, by (
17
) we obtain
\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P^{n}g\leq\varepsilon
for every
n
. But
\nu g>2\varepsilon
which contradicts the fact that
\nu
is a weak-limit point.

Remark 17
.

Consider for a while the dynamics given by the McKean-Vlasov replicator
\Xi
discussed in the previous sections, and for every
t\geq 1
consider the operator
\tilde{P}_{t}
acting on bounded functions as
\tilde{P}_{t}f({\bf x}):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}}(f(\Xi_{t}))
. As remarked above, in general, we do not have
\tilde{P}_{nt}=\tilde{P}^{n}_{t}
. So, the previous argument breaks down from the very start in the case of McKean-Vlasov replicators, and thus new tools are needed to face the problem of persistence in this setup.

Thus,
[H]
guarantees that the limit points of the sequence
(\textbf{Law}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{tn}):n\geq 0)
support the whole ensemble of replicators. Also, observe that if
\nu
is an invariant probability measure for the process
\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}
that supports the whole ensemble of replicators, then by definition
\nu P=\nu
. Let
A\subseteq\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})
be a measurable set with
\text{Leb}(A)=0
. Then:

\nu(A)=\nu P(A)=\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P\mathbf{1}_{A}=\int_{\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P\mathbf{1}_{A},

since
\nu
does not charge the boundary of
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. By Proposition
12
, for every
\vec{\bf x}
there exists a density, say
p(\vec{\bf x},\cdot)
, of the probability measure
\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Consequently:

\nu(A)=\int_{\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P\mathbf{1}_{A}\\
=\int_{\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\int_{A}p(\vec{\bf x},\vec{\bf y})d\vec{\bf y}=\int_{A}d\vec{\bf y}\left(\int_{\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})p(\vec{\bf x},\vec{\bf y})\right),

by Fubini’s theorem. Since
\text{Leb}(A)=0
, the last integral vanishes, and we deduce
\nu(A)=0
. In other words, if
\nu
is an invariant measure for the process
\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}
under which the full system persists, then it is continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Finally, assume that we are able to prove that there exists a unique invariant measure, say
\mu
, for the semigroup
P
which is supported at the interior of
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. We claim that
\mathcal{H}
, as in
[H]
, is
\mu
-integrable. Indeed, for
n\geq 0
, define
\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\mathcal{H}\wedge n
. Then, for every
n\geq 1
:

\mu\mathcal{H}_{n}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu P^{k}\mathcal{H}_{n}\leq\mu(\limsup_{k\to\infty}P^{k}\mathcal{H}_{n})\leq D,

where the equality follows since
\mu
is invariant for
P
, and the first inequality is (reverse) Fatou’s Lemma. Apply (direct) Fatou’s Lemma to obtain:

\mu\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}_{n}(\vec{\bf x})\mu(d{\bf x})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})}\mathcal{H}_{n}(\vec{\bf x})\mu(d\vec{\bf x})\leq D,

and we deduce that
\mathcal{H}
is
\mu
-integrable.

Actually, the existence of a function
\mathcal{H}
as in
[H]
implies stronger results (see
[
3
]
, page 186, and the references therein, for the following lemma).

Lemma 18
.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}
exists as in
[H]
. Assume also that
P
possesses a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Then, there exists a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
for the kernel
P
, and moreover:

(1)

\pi\ll\text{Leb}
.

(2)

\mathcal{H}
is
\pi
-integrable.

(3)

There exists
\rho\in(0,1)
and
C^{\prime}>0
such that:

(18)

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P^{n},\pi)\leq C^{\prime}(1+\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x}))\rho^{n},\quad\vec{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}).

Remark 19
.

Observe that (
18
) implies that
\pi
attracts every initial measure
\nu
with
\nu(\partial(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})=0
. Indeed, for any compact set
K\subset\mathpzc{int}{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}
, by the definition of total variation norm and Fubini’s theorem:

\displaystyle\|\nu P^{n}-\pi\|_{TV}

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\textbf{ Borel },|f|\leq 1}\left(\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})P^{n}f(\vec{\bf x})-\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\pi(d\vec{\bf y})f(\vec{\bf y})\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\textbf{ Borel },|f|\leq 1}\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\left(P^{n}(\vec{\bf x},d\vec{\bf y})f(\vec{\bf y})-\pi(d\vec{\bf y})f(\vec{\bf y})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\sup_{f\textbf{ Borel },|f|\leq 1}\int_{(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}}\left(P^{n}(\vec{\bf x},d\vec{\bf y})f(\vec{\bf y})-\pi(d\vec{\bf y})f(\vec{\bf y})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{K}\nu(d\vec{\bf x})\|\delta_{\vec{\bf x}}P^{n}-\pi\|_{TV}+2\nu(\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})\setminus K),

since the total variation norm is bounded by
2
and
\nu
does not charge the boundary. Since
(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
is Polish,
\nu
is regular, and then given
\varepsilon>0
we can choose
K_{\varepsilon}
such that the last term is smaller than
\varepsilon
. The result follows since the term inside the integral converges uniformly to
0
on
K_{\varepsilon}
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 20
.

Assume that
\Phi
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, and Assumptions
1
-
2
-
3
hold. Then for every
r>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
\Upsilon,\Psi
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
such that
\|\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon\|+|||{\bf A}_{\Psi}|||<\delta
, the following hold:

(1)

\mathcal{P}_{inv}^{(N)}(\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))=\{\nu\}
; i.e., there exists a unique invariant probability measure supported on the interior of the
N
-fold topological sum of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
for the system (
13
). Moreover
\nu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure, and the function
\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})\ni\vec{\bf x}\mapsto\mathpzc{dist}_{N}(\vec{\bf x},\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))^{-r/N}
is
\nu
-integrable.

(2)

There exist positive constants
C^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}
such that:

\displaystyle\|\delta_{{\bf x}}P^{(N)}_{t}-\nu\|_{TV}\leq\dfrac{C^{\prime}e^{-\alpha^{\prime}t}}{\mathpzc{dist}_{N}({\bf x},\partial((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}))^{r/N}},\quad{\bf x}\in\mathpzc{int}((\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}).

Proof.
(of Theorem
20
).
We follow closely the steps in the proof Theorem 3.1 of
[
3
]
. Recall the definition of the invasion rates (
12
). There exists
\alpha>0
, a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\partial\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a function
W:\mathbb{S}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
such that:

(19)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bf p},{\bf h}({\bf x})\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla W({\bf x}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x})\right\rangle>\alpha,

for every
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{U}
(see
[
21
]
, Theorems 3.4 and 4,4, and Remark 3.5), and where
\bf p
is taken as in Assumption
2
.
Define the map
V_{N}:(\mathcal{U}\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle V_{N}(\vec{\bf x}):=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V({\bf x}^{(i)}),

where
V({\bf x}):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}\ln(x_j)+W({\bf x})
. Since
{\bf h}({\bf x})=\text{diag}({\bf x})^{-1}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x})
, we have:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle(\nabla V_{N}(\vec{\bf x}))^{(i)},\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla V({\bf x}^{(i)}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right\rangle>\alpha,

on
(\mathcal{U}\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. Apply the generator
L^{(N)}
to
V_{N}
to obtain:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}V_{N}(\vec{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla V^{(i)}_{N,\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\nabla V^{(i)}_{N,\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)}),\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf x}^{(i)},\mu_{\vec{\bf x}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr\left(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})^{\top}\nabla\nabla^{\top}V^{(i)}_{N,\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)})\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})\right),

where the map
V^{(i)}_{N,\vec{\bf x}}
is given by
\mathbb{S}^{d}\ni{\bf y}\mapsto V_{N}({\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf x}^{(N)})
, where
{\bf y}
is in the place of the
i
-th block of order
d
.

Since
W
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, the above expression can be made greater than
\alpha/2
, say, for
\vec{\bf x}\in(\mathcal{U}\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
by choosing
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}>0
small enough and imposing
\|\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon\|+|||{\bf A}_{\Psi}|||<\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}
.

Define
\lambda:=\dfrac{r}{\inf p_{i}}
, and consider the function
\mathcal{H}:(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{++}
given by
\mathcal{H}:=\exp(-\lambda V_N)
, which is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}((\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N})
. Furthermore

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}\inf_{\{\vec{\bf z}:{\bf z}^{(i)}\in\text{Ext}(\eta)\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d}\text{, }\forall i=1,...,N\}}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf z})=\infty.

Since
W
is bounded on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, with
K:=\exp(-\lambda\inf_{\zz\in\simp{d}} W (\zz))
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})

\displaystyle\geq K\exp(-\lambda(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{d} p_j\ln(\xx^{(i)}_j))

\displaystyle\geq K\prod_{i=1}^{N}\exp(-(r/N) \sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(\xx^{(i)}_j))

\displaystyle=K\prod_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{1}{({\bf x}^{(i)}_{1}{\bf x}^{(i)}_{2}\ldots{\bf x}^{(i)}_{d})^{r/N}}

\displaystyle\geq K\prod_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{1}{\mathpzc{dist}({\bf x}^{(i)},\partial(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{r/N}}

(20)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{K}{\mathpzc{dist}_{N}(\vec{\bf x},\partial(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})^{r/N}}.

Let
\Gamma^{(N)}
be the
carré du champ
operator associated to the operator
L^{(N)}
, whose action on smooth functions
\varphi:(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is given by:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(N)}\varphi(\vec{\bf x})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j,k}[\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf x}^{(i)})^{\top}]_{j,k}\partial_{j}\varphi_{\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)})\partial_{k}\varphi_{\vec{\bf x}}({\bf x}^{(i)}).

For smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R},\varphi:(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, we have:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}f(\varphi(\vec{\bf x}))=f^{\prime}(\varphi(\vec{\bf x}))L^{(N)}\varphi(\vec{\bf x})+f^{\prime\prime}(\varphi(\vec{\bf x}))\Gamma^{(N)}\varphi(\vec{\bf x}).

Hence:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})=-\lambda\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})\left(L^{(N)}V_{N}(\vec{\bf x})-\lambda\Gamma^{(N)}V_{N}(\vec{\bf x})\right),

and then we can choose
\tilde{\varepsilon}<\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}
and impose
\|\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon\|+|||{\bf A}_{\Psi}|||<\tilde{\varepsilon}
such that the term inside the parentheses is not smaller than
\alpha/3
, say, on
(\mathcal{U}\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. And then, for this choice:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}H(\vec{\bf x})\leq-\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{3}H(\vec{\bf x}),

on
(\mathcal{U}\setminus\partial\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N}
. Observe that
L^{(N)}\mathcal{H}
is bounded on
(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\setminus\mathcal{U})^{N}
by some absolute constant
C
. Thus, with
\beta:=\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{3}
, we obtain:

(21)

\displaystyle L^{(N)}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})\leq-\beta\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+C,

for all
\vec{\bf x}\in(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
.

Let
\eta_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t})>n\}
, and observe that since
\mathcal{H}
is bounded on compact sets of
(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
, by Lemma
11
, we have
\eta_{n}\to\infty
a.s. For given
t\geq 0
, set
t_{n}:=t\wedge\eta_{n}
. For every
n\geq 1
, the process:

\displaystyle M^{n}_{t}=e^{\beta t_{n}}\mathcal{H}({\bf X}^{(N)}_{t_{n}})-\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t_{0}})-\int_{0}^{t_{n}}e^{\beta s}(\beta\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{s})+L^{(N)}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{s}))ds,\quad t\geq 0,

is a martingale, and thus for every
n\geq 1
, by (
21
):

\mathbb{E}_{\vec{\bf x}}(e^{\beta t_{n}}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t_{n}}))=\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{n}}Ce^{\beta s}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+\dfrac{C}{\beta}(1-e^{\beta t}).

Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{\bf x}}(e^{\beta t}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{t\leq t_{n}})\leq\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+\dfrac{C}{\beta},

and then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{\bf x}}(\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{t\leq t_{n}})\leq e^{-\beta t}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+\dfrac{C}{\beta}.

Since
t_{n}\to t
a.s., by Monotone Convergence, we deduce that for every
t\geq 0
and
\vec{\bf x}\in(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{\bf x}}(\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{t}))\leq e^{-\beta t}\mathcal{H}(\vec{\bf x})+D,

where
D
is a constant. In particular, there exists
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that, at time
1
:

\displaystyle P^{(N)}_{1}\mathcal{H}\leq\gamma\mathcal{H}+D,

on
(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
. By Proposition
12
,
P^{(N)}_{1}
possesses a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Hence, Lemma
18
applies to
P^{(N)}_{1}
and we deduce the existence of a unique invariant probability measure
\mu
for
P^{(N)}_{1}
such that
\mu(\partial(\mathbb{S}^{d})^{N})=0
, and moreover
\mu
attracts every initial condition on
(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))^{N}
at geometric rate. Of course, the same line of reasoning applies to
P^{(N)}_{t}
for every
t\in(0,1]
, and we deduce that for every
t\in(0,1]
there exists a unique invariant measure, say
\pi_{t}
, for the chain
(\vec{\bf X}^{(N)}_{nt}:n\geq 0)
. The uniqueness follows from Lemma
16
.

The remaining parts of the result are exactly those in the reference
[
3
]
.

5.
Invariant distributions for the McKean-Vlasov stochastic replicator

We go back to the initial model of Fudenberg and Harris, as treated in
[
22
]
, and consider a non-linear, McKean-Vlasov-type extension of the model. Assume first that
d=2
, that
A
is given, and that
\Sigma=\sigma I
. Recall that in this case, each replicator solves (
3
). From Theorem 3.6 of
[
22
]
we deduce the following result.

Proposition 21
.

Assume that
a_{12}-a_{22}>0
and
a_{21}-a_{11}>0
, and furthermore:

\displaystyle a_{12}+a_{21}-a_{11}-a_{22}=\sigma^{2}.

Then, the system (
3
) has a unique invariant distribution that does not charge the boundary, namely the absolutely continuous measure with density Beta with parameters
(a_{12}-a_{22})\sigma^{-2}
and
(a_{21}-a_{11})\sigma^{-2}
.

Observe that for the deterministic system associated to (
3
) the unique ergodic probability measures that support strict subsets of the community are delta distribution, namely:
\bf\delta_{1}
with all the mass at the first population, and
\bf\delta_{2}
with all the mass at the second population. It is direct to show that the average positivity of the invasion rates (Assumption
2
) holds in this case.

Assume that we choose
0<\delta<\min\{\sigma^{2},a_{12}-a_{22},a_{21}-a_{11}\}
. For an arbitrary probability measure
\mu
on the simplex, define
\Phi({\bf x})=\tilde{A}{\bf x}
,
\Psi({\bf x})=\Sigma
and

(22)

\displaystyle\Upsilon({\bf x},\mu)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\delta\int y_{1}\mu(dy_{1},dy_{2})\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right)\Bigg){\bf x},

and the associated non-linear replicator:

(23)

\displaystyle d\Xi_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi(\Xi_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon(\Xi_{t},\mu_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(\Xi_{t})d{\bf W}_{t}.

Theorem 22
.

Equation (
23
) admits a unique invariant distribution, namely the
\text{Beta}(s,1-s)
distribution, where
s=\dfrac{a_{12}-a_{22}}{\sigma^{2}-\delta}
.

The proof of the above theorem will be transparent as soon as we prove its extension to higher dimensions (and the computation of the invariant measure will follow after some simple algebraic manipulation).

Before starting the next Lemma, observe that if a payoff matrix
A
satisfies
[C1]
, for every
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\top}\in\mathcal{T}
we have:

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\top}A{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(a_{ij}+a_{ji})x_{i}x_{j}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(a_{ij}+a_{ji}-a_{ii}-a_{jj})x_{i}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}(a_{ij}+a_{ji}-a_{ii}-a_{jj})x_{i}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}(x_{1}+x_{2}+\ldots+x_{i-1}+x_{i+1}+\ldots+x_{d})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}.

In particular, if
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{T}
is not the null vector,
A{\bf x}
cannot be a constant vector. We deduce that the equation
A^{\prime}\alpha=\text{ constant vector }
has at most one solution summing up to
1
.

Lemma 23
.

Assume that
A
satisfies
[C1]
and
[C2]
. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that for any skew-symmetric matrix
E
with
\|E\|<\delta
the matrix
\hat{A}:=A+E
satisfies
[C2]
.

Proof.

First, observe that for
A
and
E
as in the claim, the matrix
A+E
satisfies
[C1]
as well. Next, observe that we can assume that
E
has the block-form:

\displaystyle E=\delta\left(\begin{array}[]{c;{2pt/2pt}c}E_{2}&\bf 0}_{2\times(d-2)\\
\hline\cr[2pt/2pt]{\bf 0}_{(d-2)\times 2}&\bf 0\end{array}\right),\quad\text{ with }\quad E_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},

since, if the result is true for this kind of perturbation, applying repeatedly the lemma we arrive (via permutations of rows and columns) at the desired result.

We aim to find a strictly positive vector
\hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\alpha}_{d})^{\top}
such that
\hat{A}^{\prime}\hat{\alpha}
is a constant vector. Let
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
be the vector and the real number ensured by
[C2]
associated to the matrix
A
. For
i=1,2,\ldots,d-1
, write
\hat{\alpha}_{i}:=\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
, and
\hat{\alpha}_{d}=\alpha_{d}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\varepsilon_{j}
. Then for a certain constant
\hat{c}
, for
i=1,\ldots,d
:

\displaystyle\hat{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\hat{a}_{ij}-\sigma^{2}/2)\hat{\alpha}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{ij}+\delta e_{ij}-\sigma^{2}/2)(\alpha_{j}+\varepsilon_{j})+(a_{id}+\delta e_{id}-\sigma^{2}/2)(\alpha_{d}-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\varepsilon_{k}),

must hold. Then:

\displaystyle\hat{c}

\displaystyle=c+\delta(\alpha_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{1j}-a_{1d})\varepsilon_{j}

\displaystyle=c-\delta(\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon_{1})+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{2j}-a_{2d})\varepsilon_{j}

\displaystyle=c+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{ij}-a_{id})\varepsilon_{j},\quad i=3,4,\ldots,d,

and consequently:

\displaystyle\delta(\alpha_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{1j}-a_{1d})\varepsilon_{j}

\displaystyle=-\delta(\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon_{1})+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{2j}-a_{2d})\varepsilon_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(a_{ij}-a_{id})\varepsilon_{j},

for
i=3,4,\ldots,d
. This reduces to a linear system of
d-1
equations for the
d-1
unknowns
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d-1}
. Let
H^{(\delta)}
be the matrix representing the above system. Then
{\bf\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{d-1})^{\top}
satisfies the system
\bf(S_{\delta})
:

(24)

\displaystyle H^{(\delta)}{\varepsilon^{(\delta)}}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})&\delta\alpha_{2}&\delta\alpha_{2}&\cdots&\delta\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

Since the equation
A^{\prime}\alpha=\text{constant}
is uniquely solvable for
\alpha
strictly positive, summing up to
1
and satisfying
[C1]
, the equation
H^{(0)}\varepsilon^{\prime}=0
has a unique solution, and hence
H^{(0)}
is non singular. On the other side, it is clear that
(H^{(\delta)}:\delta\in[0,1])
is a Lipschitz continuous family of matrices (with respect to a fixed reference norm in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, say the
L^{2}
subordinate matrix norm), and thus there exists
1\geq\overline{\delta}>0
such that for
\delta<\overline{\delta}
there exists a unique solution of
\bf(S_{\delta})
. Let
(\varepsilon^{(\delta)}:\delta\in[0,\overline{\delta}])
be the family of solutions, and let
\ell_{\delta}
be the right hand side of
\bf(S_{\delta})
. Then for
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in[0,\overline{\delta}]
, and some constant
C>0
that can change from line to line:

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon^{(\delta_{1})}-\varepsilon^{(\delta_{2})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}(\ell_{\delta_{1}}-\ell_{\delta_{2}})\|+\|((H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}-(H^{(\delta_{2})})^{-1})\ell_{\delta_{2}}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|(H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}\|\|\ell_{\delta_{1}}-\ell_{\delta_{2}}\|+\|(H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}-(H^{(\delta_{2})})^{-1}\|\|\ell_{\delta_{2}}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|(H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}\||\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}|

\displaystyle\qquad+C\|(H^{(\delta_{1})})^{-1}\|\|(H^{(\delta_{2})})^{-1}\|\|H^{(\delta_{1})}-H^{(\delta_{2})}\|.

As mentioned earlier,
(H^{(\delta)}:\delta\in[0,\overline{\delta}])
is a Lipschitz family; in particular, it has bounded norm and, consequently, the family of inverses is bounded as well (this is so since the coefficients of
H^{(\delta)}
are continuous in
\delta
, and consequently they are bounded on
[0,\overline{\delta}]
; the determinant across the family, being the determinant a continuous functions of the coefficients, is a continuous function of a parameter ranging over a compact interval, and thus is bounded; again, since
[0,\overline{\delta}]
is compact, the determinants are bounded away from zero; Cramer’s rule gives the conclusion). Again, by Lipschitz continuity, the second term is bounded by
C^{\prime}|\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}|
. We deduce that
(\varepsilon^{(\delta)}:\delta\in[0,\overline{\delta}])
is a Lipschitz family. So, we can find
\hat{\delta}<\overline{\delta}
such that for
\delta<\hat{\delta}
the vector
\hat{\alpha}
is still strictly positive. Since, by definition,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hat{\alpha}_{i}=1
, this proves our claim.

As a last ingredient in the proof of the main Theorem of this section, we need an estimate of Wasserstein’s distance between Dirichlet distributions in terms of the differences between their parameters. Hereafter, for a positive vector
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}=1
, the notation
D_{\bf a}
stands for the absolutely continuous measure with
\text{Dirichlet}({\bf a})
density.

Lemma 24
.

For
D_{\bf a}
and
D_{\bf b}
as above:

\displaystyle\textbf{Was}_{1}(D_{\bf a},D_{\bf b})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}2(d-i)|a_{i}-b_{i}|.

We could not find this result in the available literature, so we provide a full proof in the Appendix.

Theorem 25
.

Let
A
be a payoff matrix satisfying
[C1]
and
[C2]
, and
F:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a
\textbf{Was}_{1}
-Lipschitz function with range included in the set of skew-symmetric matrices. Let
\Phi({\bf x})=\tilde{A}{\bf x}
,
\Psi({\bf x})=\Sigma
, and
\Upsilon({\bf x},\mu)=\delta F(\mu){\bf x}
. Then there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for every
\delta<\delta_{0}
the stochastic McKean-Vlasov replicator with the previous parameter admits a unique invariant probability measure putting no mass on the boundary of the simplex.

Proof.

For
A
as above, let
\overline{\delta}
be the tolerance guaranteed by Lemma
23
, and fix
\delta<\overline{\delta}
. For fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathpzc{int}(\mathbb{S}^{d}))
, let
({\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}_{t}:t\geq 0)
be the unique solution of the “frozen” replicator:

(25)

\displaystyle d{\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi({\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon({\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}_{t},\mu)dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi({\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}_{t})d{\bf W}_{t}.

By Theorem 3.6 of
[
22
]
, there exists a vector
\alpha=\alpha(\mu)
of strictly positive entries summing up to
1
such that
D_{\alpha(\mu)}
is invariant for
{\bf X}^{(\mu,\delta)}
. Moreover, by Corollary 3.8 of
[
22
]
, this is the unique invariant distribution that does not charge the boundary. Let
T_{\mu}=D_{\alpha(\mu)}
. Moreover, since
F
is Lipschitz, from the proof of Lemma
23
, it transpires that there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}\|_{1}\leq\delta C\textbf{Was}_{1}(\mu,\nu),

for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
. But then, by Lemma
24
:

\displaystyle\textbf{Was}_{1}(T_{\mu},T_{\nu})\leq 2(d-1)\delta C\textbf{Was}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

We deduce that for
\delta<\min\{\overline{\delta},(2(d-1)C)^{-1}\}:=\delta_{0}
, the map
\mu\mapsto T_{\mu}
is a contraction. By the Contraction Mapping Theorem (see e.g. Theorem 5.7, page 138 of
[
4
]
), there exists a unique
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
such that
\overline{\mu}=T_{\overline{\mu}}
. Clearly,
\overline{\mu}
is invariant for the McKean-Vlasov replicator. Since the range of
T
is a subset of the Dirichlet family, the last claim follows.

6.
Simulations

In Section
5
, for the case of the McKean-Vlasov replicator with linear interaction function and (small) Lipschitz dependence on the instantaneous measure (see Theorem
25
), we have stated the existence of a unique invariant probability measure putting no mass on the boundary. In this section, via numerical simulations, we investigate whether this invariant probability attracts the solutions of the system at large times. To that aim, we use the well-known Euler-Maruyama scheme to simulate the associated
N
-replicator system, with step size
h=0.01
.

Our objective is to study the behaviour of the solutions to (
23
) as
t\rightarrow\infty
, i.e. the long term solutions to

dX^{(N;i)}_{t}=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Phi(X^{(N;i)}_{t})dt+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Upsilon(X^{(N;i)}_{t},X^{(N;j)}_{t})dt+\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}\Psi(X^{(N;i)}_{t})dW_{t},

where
X_{t}^{(N;i)}=(x_{1}^{(N;i)}(t),x_{2}^{(N;i)}(t))
for
N
large. Here
\Phi,\Psi
and
\Upsilon
, correspond to those defined in (
22
). In particular, we simulated the following sets of parameters

(PS1)

A=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&1\\
1&0.5\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma=1,\qquad\delta=0.05,\qquad s=0.5263,

and

(PS2)

A=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.9\\
1&0.4\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma=0.9487,\qquad\delta=0.04,\qquad s=0.5814,

For each of the two sets of parameters, to test the effect of the initial distribution, we perform the simulations for initial conditions uniformly scattered in the unit interval, and also for initial conditions uniformly scattered in the subset
[0.2,0.4]
. We called those distributions
uniform
and
lump
initial conditions (UIC and LIC for short). For each of the four experiments described, we approximate the probability density function from a normalized histogram of the first coordinate of each of the
N
-replicator
\{x_{1}^{(N;i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. This gives us some qualitative understanding of the time convergence of the probability density.

. For any of the numerical experiments performed, the results showed a relatively fast convergence of the empirical mean towards

times the
500
-replicator dynamics with UIC for both sets parameters, saving the trajectory of only one randomly selected replicator each time. We report a goodness-of-fit test regarding the theoretical stationary Beta distribution specified in Theorem
22
at times
t=40
, and
t=50
. In all cases, we will see now that the laws of the trajectories are quite close to the corresponding theoretical stationary Beta distribution (for more details see also appendix Figure
4
).

The implemented test is based on an
L^{2}
-type test statistic proposed in
[
12
, Theorem 1 - Corollary 1]
: based on a random sample
Y_{1},...,Y_{n}
, their test statistic is given by:

T_{n}(\hat{a}_{n},\hat{b}_{n})=n\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left((\hat{a}_{n}+\hat{b}_{n})Y_{j}-\hat{a}_{n}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{j}\geq t\}}-\frac{t^{\hat{a}_{n}}(1-t)^{\hat{b}_{n}}}{B(\hat{a}_{n},\hat{b}_{n})}\right|^{2}dt,

where
B(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Beta function, and
\hat{a}_{n}
and
\hat{b}_{n}
are consistent estimators of the Beta parameters, such as moment estimators or maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs). However, since in our simulations, the stationary distribution is completely specified by the resulting parameterization given in Theorem
22
, we can introduce such values,
a
and
b
, instead of its estimates. Thus, for our null hypothesis,
H_{0}
: “the law of the first coordinate trajectories of the one-dimensional McKean-Vlasov replicator (
23
) follows a
Beta(a,b)
for time
t
”, our test statistic will become:

(26)

T_{n}=T_{n}(a,b)=n\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left((a+b)Y_{j}-a\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{j}\geq t\}}-\frac{t^{a}(1-t)^{b}}{B(a,b)}\right|^{2}dt,

where in our case
a=s
and
b=1-a
. The critical region to reject the
H_{0}
is delimited by
1-\alpha
quantiles of the distribution of the above test statistic under
H_{0}
. Since
T_{n}
is a distance, the larger the observed values of
T_{n}
are, the further we will be from the null hypothesis. To obtain an approximation of the distribution of
T_{n}
under
H_{0}
, we construct the density of
B
values of
T_{n}
, each one obtained by an independent simulated random sample from a
Beta(a,b)
. In our case, we used
B=5000
. We found that the
90\%
quantiles are
0.1056
(PS1) and
0.1119
(PS2). For
t=40
the computed tests statistics are
T_{n}=0.0175
(PS1) and
T_{n}=0.0046
(PS2). For
t=50
the computed tests statistics are
T_{n}=0.0817
(PS1) and
T_{n}=0.0492
(PS2). In all cases, the null hypothesis is not rejected. The corresponding
R
-code can be found at
https://github.com/leonardo-videla/beta_test.git
.

Given these results, it seems reasonable to conjecture that ergodicity must hold, at least in this case. We have no proof yet, and thus this remains an open question.

7.
Discussion and outlook

This paper advances the modeling of biological phenomena, which we have proposed in previous papers using the framework of Open System Dynamics (
[
33
]
,
[
45
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
34
]
,
[
56
]
).
We regard our contribution as a step forward in understanding how the living develops in continuous interaction with the environment, including this time the interaction within communities of living entities in what we envisioned as an ecology of primordial or early life represented by a community of replicators. In particular, we were interested in assessing if neutrality and associated fitness equivalence could emerge in this system and under which conditions. To this end, we generalized the stochastic replicator equation by adding a new component that affects the instantaneous fitness, the idea being that this component accounts for the interaction of one particular replicator population with others, alike ones, within a community in a mean-field regime.

We show that under suitable hypotheses, the asymptotic behavior of interacting replicator dynamics may be approached by independent non-markovian, McKean-Vlasov particles (Section
3
). For the mean-field replicator aggregates, we have shown that the conditions used in
[
3
]
, with some simple modifications, still apply to guarantee the coexistence (in the sense of persistence) of large (but finite) systems of interacting communities (Section
4
). In simple cases (Section
5
), we show the existence of a unique non-trivial invariant probability measure for the McKean-Vlasov replicator. Indeed, in simple setups, we have been able to prove that the Dirichlet family is stable (in the obvious sense that a system with Dirichlet invariant still has a Dirichlet invariant when we introduce the dependence on the measure). This result, which partially extends the work
[
22
]
and
[
29
]
, admits further extensions, and it is a part of our current research efforts. Likewise, regarding the ergodicity of the McKean-Vlasov replicator, we still have no concluding results (although we have shown in Section
6
that numerical experiments give strong empirical support in this direction). This is an open question we continue investigating.

We point out some salient features of this article. The first one refers to the fact that, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to import the McKean-Vlasov machinery into the evolutionary-game-theoretic realm. Also, the study of persistence for interacting, finite aggregates of complex communities seems to be a novel feature. The use of the fixed-point argument to prove existence/uniqueness of invariant measures for McKean-Vlasov systems (see Theorem
7
) also seems to be a technique that has not been previously informed in this literature.

From the point of view of theoretical ecology, we note that the condition
[C1]
can be regarded as the expression of neutrality and fitness equivalence among types, as assumed in the neutral ecological theory (
[
26
]
); in turn, in previous work (
[
33
]
) we have shown that this condition is associated with Beta invariant probability measure. In light of the results of Section
5
, this observation provides theoretical support for the idea that neutrality, or fitness equivalence, is associated with the emergence of ecologies from simple, replicating molecules to complex ecosystems, and is an emergent condition that is related to the persistence of the ecological system. Thus, unlike traditional models in ecology, we did not assume neutrality but neutrality naturally emerged as a condition for persistence.

There are many open directions to continue this work. The first one was alluded to above, namely: a proof for ergodicity of the McKean-Vlasov replicator is still missing. Secondly, we were able to prove persistence for the finite-size particle system under small mean-field and noise terms. The problem of proving the analogous property for the McKean-Vlasov replicator was not faced in this article, but we notice in advance that the techniques used in Section
4
(a modification of the well-known Foster-Lyapunov techniques as treated for example in the classic articles of Meyn and Tweedie
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
) are unlikely to work in the non-linear case since they are strongly grounded on the Markovian nature of the objects they are meant to be applied to. Finally, another aspect that must be investigated is the extension of the model proposed here to the setup of many types of interacting replicators. More specifically, we can think of a myriad of entities of different types, say
N_{i}\geq 1
replicators of type
i
for
i=1,\ldots,M
, where
M
is the fixed number of types. A preliminary assessment of this extended setup shows that the propagation-of-chaos (under suitable conditions) must still hold. The problem of persistence in this multi-type interacting replicator seems to be a challenging issue, and we think it deserves further investigation.
