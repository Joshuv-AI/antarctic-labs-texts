Alan Lew

Abstract

Let Δ k + ​ ( Fl n , q ) \Delta^{+}_{k}(\text{Fl}_{n,q}) be the k k -dimensional weighted upper Laplacian on Fl n , q \text{Fl}_{n,q} . Let

Identifying
\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})

Laplacian operators on simplicial complexes were introduced by Eckmann in

(Garland

Let

\epsilon>0

such that for every prime power

of

For the proof of Theorem

[

]
).

For every

Theorem 1.5
.

Let

be a prime power. Then,

\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
with multiplicity

\binom{n}{n/2}_{q}-\binom{n}{n/2-1}_{q}

Let
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
. Let
\alpha(k)=\min\{k/2,1\}
, and

\mathcal{J}_{k}=\left\{\pm 2\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{n-2k+2}\right):\,1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-2k+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

For every

, define

D_{n,k,\zeta}(q)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\,\left|\lambda-\left(n-2+\zeta\cdot q^{-\frac{k}{2}}\right)\right|\leq C(n)\cdot q^{-(\frac{k}{2}+\alpha(k))}\right\}.

Then, for every prime power

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Weighted Laplacians

A
simplicial complex

X
is a family of subsets of some finite set
V
, such that if
\sigma\in X
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subset\sigma
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in X
. The set
V
is called the
vertex set
of
X
. The elements of
X
are called the
simplices
or
faces
of the complex. The
dimension
of a simplex
\sigma\in X
is defined as
|\sigma|-1
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex on vertex set
V
, and let
k\geq-1
.
An
ordered
k
-simplex

[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]
is a
k
-dimensional simplex
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}\}\in X
together with an order of its vertices. For ordered simplices
\sigma,\tau
such that
\sigma=\tau
as sets, let
(\sigma:\tau)
be the sign of the permutation mapping
\sigma
to
\tau
. Similarly, for ordered simplices
\sigma,\tau
in
X
such that
\tau\subset\sigma
and
\sigma\setminus\tau=\{v\}
for some vertex
v\in\sigma
, let
(\sigma:\tau)
be the sign of the permutation mapping
\sigma
to
v\tau
(where
v\tau
is the ordering of
\tau\cup\{v\}
where
v
is the first vertex, followed by the vertices of
\tau
in their original order).

A
k
-cochain
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued skew-symmetric function on the ordered
k
-simplices of
X
. That is,
\phi
is a
k
-cochain if for any two ordered
k
-simplices
\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}
in
X
that are equal as sets, it satisfies
\phi(\tilde{\sigma})=(\sigma:\tilde{\sigma})\phi(\sigma)
.

Let
C^{k}(X)
denote the space of
k
-cochains on
X
. The
coboundary operator

d_{k}:C^{k}(X)\to C^{k+1}(X)
is defined by

d_{k}\phi([v_{0},\ldots,v_{k+1}])=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{i}\phi([v_{0},\ldots,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{k+1}])

for every
k
-cochain
\phi
and every ordered
(k+1)
-simplex
[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k+1}]
.

Let
X(k)
be the set of all
k
-simplices of
X
, each given a fixed arbitrary order.
Let
w:X\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a weight function on the simplices of
X
. We define an inner product on the vector space
C^{k}(X)
by

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle=\sum_{\sigma\in X(k)}w(\sigma)\phi(\sigma)\psi(\sigma).

Let
d_{k}^{*}
be the operator adjoint to
d_{k}
with respect to this inner product. We define the
weighted upper
k
-Laplacian

\Delta_{k}^{+}(X):C^{k}(X)\to C^{k}(X)
by
\Delta_{k}^{+}(X)=d_{k}^{*}d_{k}.

Note that, by definition,
\Delta_{k}^{+}(X)
is a positive semi-definite operator. In particular, it is diagonalizable, and all its eigenvalues are real and non-negative.

Let
k\geq 0
and
\sigma\in X(k)
. We define the
k
-cochain
1_{\sigma}
by

1_{\sigma}(\tau)=\begin{cases}(\sigma:\tau)&\text{ if $\sigma=\tau$ (as sets)},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The set
\{1_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in X(k)}
forms a basis of the space
C^{k}(X)
, that we will call the standard basis.
We will identify the operator
\Delta_{k}^{+}(X)
with its matrix representation in the standard basis. For
\sigma,\tau\in X(k)
, we will denote by
\Delta_{k}^{+}(X)_{\sigma,\tau}
the matrix element at row indexed by
1_{\sigma}
and column indexed by
1_{\tau}
. We say that
v\in\lk(X,\sigma)
if
v\in V\setminus\sigma
and
\sigma\cup\{v\}\in X
.

Lemma 2.1

(See e.g.
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in X(k)
. Then,

\Delta_{k}^{+}(X)_{\sigma,\tau}=\begin{cases}\sum_{v\in\lk(X,\sigma)}\frac{w(\sigma\cup\{v\})}{w(\sigma)}&\text{ if }\sigma=\tau,\\
-\frac{w(\sigma\cup\tau)}{w(\sigma)}(\sigma:\sigma\cap\tau)(\tau:\sigma\cap\tau)&\text{ if }|\sigma\cap\tau|=k,\,\sigma\cup\tau\in X(k+1),\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

2.2
q
-Binomial coefficients

Let
q
be a prime power, and let
a,b
be integers. The
q
-binomial coefficient

\binom{a}{b}_{q}
is the number of
b
-dimensional subspaces contained in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{a}
. More explicitly, we have

\binom{a}{b}_{q}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{a}(q^{i}-1)}{\prod_{i=1}^{b}(q^{i}-1)\prod_{i=1}^{a-b}(q^{i}-1)}=\frac{\prod_{i=a-b+1}^{a}(q^{i}-1)}{\prod_{i=1}^{b}(q^{i}-1)}

(2.1)

if
a\geq b\geq 0
, and
\binom{a}{b}_{q}=0
otherwise.

We will need the following well known basic result on
q
-binomial coefficients:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0\leq r\leq k\leq n
. Let
U
be an
r
-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Then, the number of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
that contain
U
is
\binom{n-r}{k-r}_{q}
.

Proof.

The number of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
containing
U
is equal to the number of
(k-r)
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}/U\cong{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n-r}
. Hence, there are
\binom{n-r}{k-r}_{q}
such subspaces.
∎

We will need the following simple results about the behavior of the
q
-binomial coefficients as
q
tends to infinity.
We will use the following asymptotic notation: Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that
f(x)=O\left(g(x)\right)
if there exist
x_{0},C>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq Cg(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
0\leq b\leq a
, and let
k\leq b
. Then,

\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}\binom{a}{b}_{q}^{-1}=q^{-k(a-b)}\left(1+O(q^{-1})\right).

Proof.

By (
2.1
), we can write
\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}=f(q)/g(q)
, where
f
is a monic polynomial in
q
of degree
\sum_{i=a-b+1}^{a-k}i=\frac{1}{2}(b-k)(2a-b-k+1)
and
g
is a monic polynomial in
q
of degree
\sum_{i=1}^{b-k}i=\frac{1}{2}(b-k)(b-k+1)
. Similarly, we can write
\binom{a}{b}_{q}=h(q)/s(q)
, where
h
is a monic polynomial in
q
of degree
\frac{1}{2}b(2a-b+1)
and
s
is a monic polynomial in
q
of degree
\frac{1}{2}b(b+1)
.
Therefore, if
k\geq 0
, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{\binom{a}{b}_{q}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{f(q)}{g(q)}\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{\frac{h(q)}{s(q)}}-1=\frac{f(q)\cdot s(q)\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{h(q)\cdot g(q)}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{f(q)\cdot s(q)\cdot q^{k(a-b)}-h(q)\cdot g(q)}{h(q)\cdot g(q)}

Note that

\deg(f(q)\cdot s(q)\cdot q^{k(a-b)})=\frac{1}{2}(2ab+2b-2bk+k^{2}-k)=\deg(h(q)\cdot g(q)).

Since
f(q)\cdot s(q)\cdot q^{k(a-b)}
and
h(q)\cdot g(q)
are both monic polynomials, the degree of the polynomial
f(q)\cdot s(q)\cdot q^{k(a-b)}-h(q)\cdot g(q)
is strictly smaller than that of
h(q)\cdot g(q)
. So, we obtain

\frac{\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{\binom{a}{b}_{q}}-1=O(q^{-1}).

Similarly, if
k<0
, we can write

\frac{\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{\binom{a}{b}_{q}}-1=\frac{\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}}{\binom{a}{b}_{q}\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}}-1=\frac{f(q)\cdot s(q)-h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}}{h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}}.

Note that

\deg(f(q)\cdot s(q))=\deg(h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}).

Since
f(q)\cdot s(q)
and
h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}
are both monic polynomials, the degree of the polynomial
f(q)\cdot s(q)-h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}
is strictly smaller than that of
h(q)\cdot g(q)\cdot q^{-k(a-b)}
. So, we obtain

\frac{\binom{a-k}{b-k}_{q}\cdot q^{k(a-b)}}{\binom{a}{b}_{q}}-1=O(q^{-1}),

as wanted.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we obtain

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
a\geq b\geq 0
be integers. Then,

\binom{a}{b}_{q}=q^{b(a-b)}\left(1+O(q^{-1})\right).

Proof.

We can write

\binom{a}{b}_{q}=\binom{(a-b)-(-b)}{0-(-b)}_{q}\binom{a-b}{0}_{q}^{-1}.

So, by Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

\binom{a}{b}_{q}=q^{b(a-b)}\left(1+O(q^{-1})\right).

∎

We will also use the following inversion formula due to Carlitz:

Lemma 2.5

(Carlitz
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{m}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n=0}^{m}
be two sequences such that

a_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}_{q}b_{k}

for all
0\leq n\leq m
. Then,

b_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}q^{\binom{k+1}{2}+\binom{n}{2}-kn}\binom{n}{k}_{q}a_{k}

for all
0\leq n\leq m
.

3
The weight function

Let
V_{1}\subsetneq V_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
be a flag in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, and let
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}
. Recall that we defined the weight function
w(\sigma)
to be the number of complete flags extending
\sigma
or, equivalently, the number of maximal faces of
\text{Fl}_{n,q}
containing
\sigma
.
In this section we discuss some useful properties of the weight function
w
.

Let
\text{GL}(n,q)
be the group of invertible
n\times n
matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. For
g\in\text{GL}(n,q)
and a subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, let

gV=\{gv:\,v\in V\}.

Note that
gV
is also a subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
and that
\dim(gV)=\dim(V)
.

Let
F
be the flag
V_{1}\subsetneq V_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
. Then, we denote by
gF
the flag

gV_{1}\subsetneq gV_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq gV_{k}.

First, we show that
w
depends only on the dimensions of the subspaces forming the flag:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V_{1}\subsetneq V_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
and
W_{1}\subsetneq W_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq W_{k}
be two flags in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Let
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}
. If
\dim(V_{i})=\dim(W_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, then
w(\sigma)=w(\sigma^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be a basis of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
such that, for all
1\leq i\leq k
, the first
\dim(V_{i})
vectors in the basis form a basis for
V_{i}
. Similarly, let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
be a basis of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
such that, for all
1\leq i\leq k
, the first
\dim(W_{i})=\dim(V_{i})
vectors in the basis form a basis for
W_{i}
.

Let
g\in\text{GL}(n,q)
be the linear isomorphism that maps
v_{i}
to
w_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Then, for all
1\leq i\leq k
, we have
gV_{i}=W_{i}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of complete flags extending
\sigma
, and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the set of complete flags extending
\sigma^{\prime}
. We have a map
\tilde{g}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
defined by
\tilde{g}(F)=gF
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Note that
\tilde{g}
is a bijection: indeed, it is easy to check that
\widetilde{g^{-1}}:\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{F}
is its inverse. Hence,
w(\sigma)=|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|=w(\sigma^{\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k
be an integer. Let
V_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{i-1}\subsetneq V_{i+1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
be a flag in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
, and let
\tau=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{i-1},V_{i+1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
. Let
\dim(V_{i-1})<d<\dim(V_{i+1})
.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the set of
d
-dimensional subspaces
U\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
satisfying
V_{i-1}\subset U\subset V_{i+1}
. For
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
\sigma_{U}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{i-1},U,V_{i+1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
. Then,

w(\tau)=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}w(\sigma_{U}).

Proof.

Notice that, for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
, any complete flag extending
\sigma_{U}
extends also
\tau
. Moreover, each complete flag extending
\tau
extends exactly one of the flags
\sigma_{U}
. Therefore, we have

w(\tau)=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}w(\sigma_{U})

as wanted.
∎

Let
V_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
be a flag in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Let
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
and
\eta=\sigma\setminus\{V_{i}\}
. Recall that we defined
r(\sigma,\eta)=\dim(V_{i+1})-\dim(V_{i-1})
and
t(\sigma,\eta)=\dim(V_{i})-\dim(V_{i-1})
(where
V_{0}=\{0\}
and
V_{k+1}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
V_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_{k}
be a flag in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Let
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
and
\eta=\sigma\setminus\{V_{i}\}
. Then

\frac{w(\sigma)}{w(\eta)}=\binom{r(\sigma,\eta)}{t(\sigma,\eta)}_{q}^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
r=r(\sigma,\eta)
and
t=t(\sigma,\eta)
.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the family of subspaces
U\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
satisfying
V_{i-1}\subset U\subset V_{i+1}
and
\dim(U)=\dim(V_{i})
. By Lemma
2.2
, we have
|\mathcal{U}|=\binom{r}{t}_{q}
.

For every
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
\sigma_{U}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{i-1},U,V_{i+1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
. Then, we have
\sigma=\sigma_{V_{i}}
, and, by Lemma
3.1
,
w(\sigma_{U})=w(\sigma)
for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Therefore, by Lemma
3.2
, we have

w(\eta)=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}w(\sigma_{U})=|\mathcal{U}|\cdot w(\sigma)=\binom{r}{t}_{q}\cdot w(\sigma).

We obtain
\frac{w(\sigma)}{w(\eta)}=\binom{r}{t}_{q}^{-1}
, as wanted.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}(k)
. Then

\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})_{\sigma,\sigma}=n-k-2.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k+1}\}
, and let
d_{i}=\dim(V_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq k+1
.
By Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})_{\sigma,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w(\sigma)}\sum_{U\in\lk(\text{Fl}_{n,q},\sigma)}w(\sigma\cup\{U\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w(\sigma)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[n-1],\\
d\notin\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k+1}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\lk(\text{Fl}_{n,q},\sigma)\\
\dim(U)=d\end{subarray}}w(\sigma\cup\{U\}).

By Lemma
3.2
, we have for all
d\in[n-1]\setminus\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k+1}\}
:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\lk(\text{Fl}_{n,q},\sigma)\\
\dim(U)=d\end{subarray}}w(\sigma\cup\{U\})=w(\sigma).

So, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})_{\sigma,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w(\sigma)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[n-1],\\
d\notin\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k+1}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\lk(\text{Fl}_{n,q},\sigma)\\
\dim(U)=d\end{subarray}}w(\sigma\cup\{U\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w(\sigma)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[n-1],\\
d\notin\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k+1}\}\end{subarray}}w(\sigma)=n-k-2.

∎

An explicit formula for the weight function
w(\sigma)
is given in
[
14
, Lemma 5.4]
, from which it is possible to recover Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
.

Proof of Lemma
1.1
.

For
k\geq 0
, we will consider each flag in
\text{Fl}_{n,q}(k)
as an ordered simplex, ordered by increasing dimension. That is, for
\sigma=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}(k)
, where
V_{1}\subset\cdots\subset V_{k+1}
, we identify
\sigma
with the ordered simplex
[V_{1},\ldots,V_{k+1}]
.
For any
1\leq i\leq k+1
, let
\sigma_{i}\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}(k-1)
be obtained from
\sigma
by removing
V_{i}
. Then
(\sigma:\sigma_{i})
is
(-1)^{i-1}
. So, for
1\leq i<j\leq k+1
, we have
(\sigma:\sigma_{i})(\sigma:\sigma_{j})=(-1)^{i-1}(-1)^{j-1}=(-1)^{j-i}=(-1)^{\epsilon(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j})}
. So, by Lemma
2.1
, Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
, we obtain

\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})_{\sigma,\tau}=\begin{cases}n-k-2&\text{if }\sigma=\tau,\\
(-1)^{\epsilon(\sigma,\tau)+1}\binom{r(\sigma\cup\tau,\sigma)}{t(\sigma\cup\tau,\sigma)}_{q}^{-1}&\text{if }|\sigma\cap\tau|=k,\,\sigma\cup\tau\in\text{Fl}_{n,q},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}(k)
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Let
U,V
be two non-trivial subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
. Assume
U\neq V
. Note that
\{U,V\}\in\text{Fl}_{n,q}
if and only of
U\subsetneq V
or
V\subsetneq U
, and in this case we have
\epsilon(\{U\},\{V\})=0
. Assume
\dim(U)=i
and
\dim(V)=j
. Then, if
U\subsetneq V
, we have
r(\{U,V\},\{U\})=n-i
and
t(\{U,V\},\{U\})=j-i
, and if
V\subsetneq U
we have
r(\{U,V\},\{U\})=i
and
t(\{U,V\},\{U\})=j
. Therefore, by Lemma
1.1
, we obtain

\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})_{U,V}=\begin{cases}n-2&\text{if }U=V,\\
-\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}&\text{if }U\subsetneq V,\\
-\binom{i}{j}_{q}^{-1}&\text{if }V\subsetneq U,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

∎

4
Subspace inclusion matrices

For
0\leq i\leq n
, denote by
S(i)
the collection of all subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of dimension
i
.

Let
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
U\in S(i)
. Define the cochain
1_{U}\in C^{0}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
as

1_{U}(V)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }U=V,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Recall that
\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\{1_{U}:\,U\in S(i)\}
forms a basis for
C^{0}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
.

Let
0\leq i,j\leq n
. Let
A_{ij}
be the
S(i)\times S(j)
matrix

(A_{ij})_{U,V}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }U\subset V\text{ or }V\subset U,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
A_{ij}=A_{ji}^{t}
, and that
A_{ii}
is just the
S(i)\times S(i)
identity matrix
I
. Also, for all
0\leq j\leq n
,
A_{0j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times S(j)}=\mathbb{R}^{S(j)}
is the all-ones vector.
Using the matrices
A_{ij}
, we can give the following description for
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
, which follows immediately from Corollary
1.2
:

Proposition 4.1
.

We can write
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
as an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block matrix

\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c}L_{1,1}&\cdots&L_{1,n-1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
L_{n-1,1}&\cdots&L_{n-1,n-1}\end{array}\right),

where, for
1\leq i,j\leq n-1
,
L_{ij}
is the
S(i)\times S(j)
matrix

L_{ij}=\begin{cases}(n-2)I&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}A_{ij}&\text{ if }i<j,\\
-\binom{i}{j}_{q}^{-1}A_{ij}&\text{ if }i>j.\end{cases}

We will need the following results regarding products of subspace inclusion matrices. First, we will need the following lemma of Kantor (see also
[
7
,
8
,
16
]
for analogous results in the case
q=1
, that is, for “subset inclusion” matrices).

Lemma 4.2

(Kantor
[
11
]
)
.

Let
k\leq j\leq i
. Then

A_{ij}A_{jk}=\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}A_{ik}.

Proof.

Let
U\in S(k)
. Then,

A_{ij}A_{jk}1_{U}=A_{ij}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(j):\\
U\subset V\end{subarray}}1_{V}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(j):\\
U\subset V\end{subarray}}A_{ij}1_{V}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(j):\\
U\subset V\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
V\subset W\end{subarray}}1_{W}.

Let
W\in S(i)
such that
U\subset W
. By Lemma
2.2
, the number of
j
-dimensional subspaces of
W
containing
U
is
\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}
. So, we obtain

A_{ij}A_{jk}1_{U}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
U\subset W\end{subarray}}\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}1_{W}=\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}A_{ik}1_{U}.

Thus,
A_{ij}A_{jk}=\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}A_{ik}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
0\leq k\leq i
, and let
U\in S(k)
. Then, for every sequence
\{\alpha_{r}\}_{r=0}^{k}
, we can write

\sum_{r=0}^{k}\alpha_{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(r):\\
V\subset U\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
V\subset W\end{subarray}}1_{W}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
\dim(U\cap W)=m\end{subarray}}\beta_{m}1_{W},

(4.1)

where

\beta_{m}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{m}{r}_{q}\alpha_{r}

for all
0\leq m\leq k
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq m\leq k
, and let
W\in S(i)
such that
\dim(U\cap W)=m
. The coefficient of
1_{W}
on the right-hand side of Equation (
4.1
) is
\beta_{m}
.

Let
0\leq r\leq k
. The number of
r
-dimensional subspaces of
U\cap W
is
\binom{m}{r}_{q}
if
r\leq m
and
0
otherwise. Therefore, the coefficient of
1_{W}
on the left-hand side of Equation (
4.1
) is

\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{m}{r}_{q}\alpha_{r}.

We obtain

\beta_{m}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{m}{r}_{q}\alpha_{r}

for all
0\leq m\leq k
, as wanted.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
0\leq k\leq i\leq j\leq n
. Then,

A_{ij}A_{jk}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{ijkm}A_{im}A_{mk},

where, for all
0\leq m\leq k
,

c_{ijkm}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{m}{2}-rm}\binom{m}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}.

(4.2)

Proof.

Let
U\in S(k)
. Then, we can write

A_{ij}A_{jk}1_{U}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(j):\\
U\subset V\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
W\subset V\end{subarray}}1_{W}.

Let
0\leq m\leq k
and let
W\in S(i)
such that
\dim(U\cap W)=m
. Then, by Lemma
2.2
, since
\dim(U+W)=\dim(U)+\dim(W)-\dim(U\cap W)=k+i-m
, the number of
j
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
containing both
U
and
W
is

\binom{n-(k+i-m)}{j-(k+i-m)}_{q}=\binom{n-i-k+m}{j-i-k+m}_{q}.

Therefore,

A_{ij}A_{jk}1_{U}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
\dim(U\cap W)=m\end{subarray}}\binom{n-i-k+m}{j-i-k+m}_{q}1_{W}.

Using Lemma
4.3
and the fact that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(m):\\
V\subset U\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
V\subset W\end{subarray}}1_{W}=A_{im}A_{mk}1_{U}
, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{ij}A_{jk}1_{U}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
\dim(U\cap W)=m\end{subarray}}\binom{n-i-k+m}{j-i-k+m}_{q}1_{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{ijkm}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in S(m):\\
V\subset U\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in S(i):\\
V\subset W\end{subarray}}1_{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{ijkm}A_{im}A_{mk}1_{U},

where the coefficients
c_{ijkm}
satisfy the relations

\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom{m}{r}_{q}c_{ijkr}=\binom{n-i-k+m}{j-i-k+m}_{q}

for all
0\leq m\leq k
.
Finally, by Lemma
2.5
, we obtain

c_{ijkm}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{m}{2}-rm}\binom{m}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}.

∎

For convenience, we will denote
c_{ijk}=c_{ijkk}
.
We will need the following Lemma about the asymptotic behavior of the numbers
c_{ijk}
as
q
tends to infinity.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k\leq i<j\leq n-k
. Then,
c_{ij0}=\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}
, and for
k\geq 1
,

c_{ijk}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}=1-q^{-(n-k-j+1)}(1+O(q^{-1}))=1+O(q^{-1}).

(4.3)

Proof.

By (
4.2
), we have

c_{ijk}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}.

For
r=k
, we have

(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}=\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}.

In particular, for
k=0
, we obtain

c_{ij0}=\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}.

Assume
k\geq 1
. For
r<k
, we divide into two cases: if
j-i-k+r<0
, then

(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}=0.

Otherwise, we have by Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.3
,

(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}\\
=(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\cdot q^{r(k-r)}\cdot q^{-(k-r)(n-j)}(1+O(q^{-1}))\\
=(-1)^{k-r}q^{\frac{-r^{2}+r(1+2(n-j))+(k^{2}-k-2k(n-j))}{2}}(1+O(q^{-1})).

The function

f(r)=-r^{2}+r(1+2(n-j))+(k^{2}-k-2k(n-j))

is a parabola with maximum at
r=\frac{1+2(n-j)}{2}=n-j+\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, it is increasing for
r<k\leq n-j<n-j+\frac{1}{2}
. So, for
r=k-1
, we have

f(k-1)=2(k+j-n-1).

and for
r<k-1

f(r)\leq f(k-2)=2(2k+2j-2n-3).

Therefore, for
r=k-1
we obtain (note that
j-i-k+r=j-i-1\geq 0
in this case),

(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}\\
=-q^{-(n-k-j+1)}(1+O(q^{-1})),

and for
r<k-1
we obtain

(-1)^{k-r}q^{\binom{r+1}{2}+\binom{k}{2}-rk}\binom{k}{r}_{q}\binom{n-i-k+r}{j-i-k+r}_{q}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}=O(q^{-(2n-2k-2j+3)}).

So

c_{ijk}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}=1-q^{-(n-k-j+1)}(1+O(q^{-1}))+O(q^{-(2n-2k-2j+3)})\\
=1-q^{-(n-k-j+1)}(1+O(q^{-1})).

Since
n-k-j+1\geq 1
, we obtain
c_{ijk}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}=1+O(q^{-1})
.

∎

For
0\leq i\leq n
, let

E^{i}=\text{span}(\{1_{U}:\,U\in S(i)\}).

In
[
11
]
, Kantor proved the following theorem:

Theorem 4.6

(Kantor
[
11
]
)
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
and
k\leq i\leq n-k
. Then

\text{rank}(A_{ik})=|S(k)|=\binom{n}{k}_{q}.

In particular, the linear map
A_{ik}:E^{k}\to E^{i}

is injective.

Let
\tilde{E}^{0}=E^{0}
, and, for
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, let

\tilde{E}^{k}
be the orthogonal complement in
E^{k}
of the subspace
A_{k,k-1}E^{k-1}
. Note that
\dim(\tilde{E}^{0})=1
and, since by Theorem
4.6

A_{k,k-1}
is injective,
\dim(\tilde{E}^{k})=\dim(E^{k})-\dim(E^{k-1})=\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}
for all
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
0\leq i\leq n
. Then,

E^{i}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\end{subarray}\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i\leq n-k}A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}.

Proof.

Assume first
i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. We argue by induction on
i
. For
i=0
the claim holds trivially.
Let
i>0
. Note that, by Lemma
4.2
, we have for
k\leq i-1
,

A_{i,i-1}A_{i-1,k}\tilde{E}^{k}=A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}.

By the induction hypothesis, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i,i-i}E^{i-1}\bigoplus\tilde{E}^{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i,i-1}\left(\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i-1}A_{i-1,k}\tilde{E}^{k}\right)\bigoplus\tilde{E}^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i-1}A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}\right)\bigoplus\tilde{E}^{i}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i}A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}=\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i\leq n-k}A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}.

Now, let
i>\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. By Lemma
4.2
, we have for
k\leq i\leq n-k
,

A_{i,n-i}A_{n-i,k}\tilde{E}^{k}=A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}.

By Theorem
4.6
, we have
E^{i}=A_{i,n-i}E^{n-i}
. Therefore, since
n-i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{i}

\displaystyle=A_{i,n-i}E^{n-i}=A_{i,n-i}\left(\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i\leq n-k}A_{n-i,k}\tilde{E}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\,k\leq i\leq n-k}A_{ik}\tilde{E}^{k}.

∎

The action of the general linear group
\text{GL}(n,q)
on the
i
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
induces a
\text{GL}(n,q)
-module structure on

E^{i}
. The decomposition of
E^{i}
in Proposition
4.7
is in fact its decomposition into irreducible subrepresentations.
Although we don’t explicitly use this fact here, this was our motivation for the definition of the subspaces
\tilde{E}^{k}
.

The following properties of the subspaces
\tilde{E}^{k}
will be needed later:

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
and let
v\in\tilde{E}^{k}
. Then,
A_{jk}v=0
for all
0\leq j<k
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\tilde{E}^{k}
, we have

v^{t}(A_{k,k-1}u)=0

for all
u\in E^{k-1}
. Hence,
v^{t}A_{k,k-1}=0
. That is,

A_{k-1,k}v=(v^{t}A_{k,k-1})^{t}=0.

Now, let
0\leq j\leq k-1
. By Lemma
4.2
we have

A_{k,k-1}A_{k-1,j}=\binom{k-j}{k-j-1}_{q}A_{kj}.

Transposing the equation, we obtain

A_{jk}v=\binom{k-j}{k-j-1}_{q}^{-1}A_{j,k-1}(A_{k-1,k}v)=0.

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Let
0\leq i<k\leq j\leq n
, and let
v\in\tilde{E}^{k}
. Then,

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=0.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
, we have

A_{kj}A_{ji}=\sum_{m=0}^{i}c_{kjim}A_{km}A_{mi}.

Transposing the equation, we obtain

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=\sum_{m=0}^{i}c_{kjim}A_{im}A_{mk}v.

By Lemma
4.8
, we have
A_{mk}v=0
for all
m\leq i<k
. Therefore, we obtain

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=0,

as wanted.
∎

5
Proof of Theorem
1.7

For
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, let
B_{k}
be a basis for
\tilde{E}^{k}
. Then, by Proposition
4.7
,

B=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\bigcup_{v\in B_{k}}\{A_{ik}v:\,\max\{1,k\}\leq i\leq\min\{n-1,n-k\}\}

is a basis for
C^{0}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
. We can now prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
L=\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
.
Let
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
,
v\in B_{k}
and
\max\{1,k\}\leq j\leq\min\{n-1,n-k\}
. By Proposition
4.1
, we have

LA_{jk}v=-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}A_{ij}A_{jk}v+(n-2)A_{jk}v-\sum_{i=j+1}^{n-1}\binom{i}{j}_{q}^{-1}A_{ij}A_{jk}v.

Let
1\leq i\leq n-1
. If
i<k
, we have, by Lemma
4.9
,

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=0.

If
i>n-k
, then
n-i<k\leq j\leq n-k<i
. So, by Lemma
4.4
, we have (after transposing the equation),

A_{n-i,i}A_{ij}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-i}c_{j,i,n-i,m}A_{n-i,m}A_{mj}.

Therefore, by Lemma
4.9
, we obtain

A_{n-i,i}A_{ij}A_{jk}v=\sum_{m=0}^{n-i}c_{j,i,n-i,m}A_{n-i,m}A_{mj}A_{jk}v=0.

Since
A_{n-i,i}
is invertible (by Theorem
4.6
), we obtain

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=0.

Now, assume
k\leq i\leq n-k
. If
i<j
, we have by Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.8
,

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{ijkm}A_{im}A_{mk}v=c_{ijkk}A_{ik}v=c_{ijk}A_{ik}v.

If
i>j
, we have by Lemma
4.2
,

A_{ij}A_{jk}v=\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}A_{ik}v.

Therefore, we obtain

LA_{jk}v=-\sum_{i=\max\{1,k\}}^{j-1}c_{ijk}\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}^{-1}A_{ik}v+(n-2)A_{jk}v\\
-\sum_{i=j+1}^{\min\{n-1,n-k\}}\binom{i}{j}_{q}^{-1}\binom{i-k}{j-k}_{q}A_{ik}v.

Thus, the subspace spanned by the vectors

\{A_{ik}v:\,\max\{1,k\}\leq i\leq\min\{n-1,n-k\}\}

is invariant under
L
, and the matrix representation of the restriction of
L
on this subspace is exactly

\tilde{L}_{k}
(for the case
k=0
, note that, by (
4.2
), we have
c_{ij0}=\binom{n-i}{j-i}_{q}
). Therefore,
the representation of
L
with respect to the basis
B
is the diagonal block matrix

\begin{pmatrix}L_{0}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&L_{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\end{pmatrix}

where

L_{k}=I_{\dim(\tilde{E}^{k})}\otimes\tilde{L}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{L}_{k}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\tilde{L}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

for all
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.
∎

From Theorem
1.7
we already obtain some interesting immediate consequences. First, we show that the second part of Corollary
1.6
holds:

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then, for every
\epsilon>0
there is an integer
q_{0}(n,\epsilon)
such that, for every prime power
q\geq q_{0}(n,\epsilon)
, every eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0,n-1
of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
satisfies

|\lambda-(n-2)|<\epsilon.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.7
, each eigenvalue of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
is an eigenvalue of the matrix
\tilde{L}_{k}
, for some
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.
It is easy to check that the eigenvalues of
\tilde{L}_{0}
are
0
with multiplicity
1
, and
n-1
with multiplicity
n-2
. Moreover, for all
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, by Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
4.5
, the matrix
\tilde{L}_{k}
tends element-wise to the upper triangular matrix:

\lim_{q\to\infty}(\tilde{L}_{k})_{ij}=\begin{cases}n-2&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-1&\text{ if }i<j,\\
0&\text{ if }i>j.\end{cases}

Therefore, as
q
tends to infinity, all eigenvalues of
\tilde{L}_{k}
tend to
n-2
. ∎

Another immediate consequence of Theorem
1.7
is the following bound on the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
.

Corollary 5.2
.

For every prime power
q\geq 2
, the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
is at most
\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+2
.

Proof.

The number of distinct eigenvalues of
\tilde{L}_{0}
is exactly
2
.
For
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\tilde{L}_{k}
is at most
n-2k+1
(since
\tilde{L}_{k}
is an
(n-2k+1)\times(n-2k+1)
matrix). Therefore, by Theorem
1.7
, the number of distinct eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
is at most

2+\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}(n-2k+1)=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+2.

∎

In order to prove our main result, Theorem
1.5
, we need a more detailed study of the spectra of the matrices
\tilde{L}_{k}
, which is presented in the next section.

6
Spectra of the matrices
\tilde{L}_{k}

For
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{M}_{m,r}(x)
be the set consisting of all matrices
M=M(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
depending on a variable
x
that satisfy

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)&\text{ if }i<j,\\
-x^{-r(i-j)}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)&\text{ if }i>j.\end{cases}

Note that, by Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
4.5
, we have
\tilde{L}_{k}-(n-2)I\in\mathcal{M}_{n-2k+1,k}(q)
for all
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.

First, we estimate the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a matrix in
\mathcal{M}_{m,r}(x)
:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{m,r}(x)
, and let
p(t)=\det(tI-M)=t^{m}+a_{m-1}t^{m-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
be the characteristic polynomial of
M
. Then,
a_{m-1}=0
, and for
0\leq i<m-1
,

a_{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}x^{-r\cdot\frac{m-i}{2}}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)

if
m-i
is even, and

a_{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i-3}{2}}(m-i-1)\binom{(m+i-1)/2}{i}x^{-r\cdot\frac{m-i+1}{2}}(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right))

if
m-i
is odd.

For the proof of Lemma
6.1
, we will need the following auxiliary results.
Let
S_{m}
be the group of permutations on
m
elements.
For a permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
, let

c(\pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)>i\end{subarray}}(\pi(i)-i).

Let

c_{\ell,m}=\min\{c(\pi):\,\pi\in S_{m},\,\pi\text{ has $\ell$ fixed points}\}

and

\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}=\{\pi\in S_{m}:\,\pi\text{ has $\ell$ fixed points and }c(\pi)=c_{\ell,m}\}.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer, and let
\ell\leq m
. Then,

c_{\ell,m}=\left\lceil\frac{m-\ell}{2}\right\rceil.

Moreover, if
m-\ell
is even,
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
consists of all the permutations whose disjoint cycle decomposition is of the form

(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}}+1)

where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}}\in[m]
, and, if
m-\ell
is odd, then
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
consists of all the permutations whose disjoint cycle decomposition is of the form

(j,j+1,j+2)(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}+1),

or

(j+2,j+1,j)(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}+1),

where
j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}\in[m]
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in S_{m}
be a permutation with exactly
\ell
fixed points. Then, since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}i
, we have

0=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\pi(i)-i)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)>i\end{subarray}}(\pi(i)-i)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)<i\end{subarray}}(\pi(i)-i).

So

c(\pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)<i\end{subarray}}(i-\pi(i)).

We obtain

2\cdot c(\pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)>i\end{subarray}}(\pi(i)-i)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)<i\end{subarray}}(i-\pi(i))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[m]:\\
\pi(i)\neq i\end{subarray}}|\pi(i)-i|.

Since
|\pi(i)-i|\geq 1
for all non-fixed points
i
, we obtain

2\cdot c(\pi)\geq m-\ell.

Therefore, since
c(\pi)
, is an integer, we obtain

c(\pi)\geq\left\lceil\frac{m-\ell}{2}\right\rceil.

(6.1)

Moreover, when
m-\ell
is even, equality in (
6.1
) is obtained exactly when
|\pi(i)-i|=1
for all non fixed points
i\in[m]
. Therefore
c_{\ell,m}=\frac{m-\ell}{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
consists of all the permutations whose disjoint cycle decomposition is of the form

(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}}+1)

where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\frac{m-\ell}{2}}\in[m]
.

If
m-\ell
is odd, then equality in (
6.1
)
is obtained exactly when
|\pi(i)-i|=1
for all non fixed points
i\in[m]
except exactly one, which satisfies
|\pi(i)-i|=2
. Therefore
c_{\ell,m}=\frac{m-\ell+1}{2}=\left\lceil\frac{m-\ell}{2}\right\rceil
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
consists of all the permutations whose disjoint cycle decomposition is of the form

(j,j+1,j+2)(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}+1),

or

(j+2,j+1,j)(i_{1},i_{1}+1)(i_{2},i_{2}+1)\cdots(i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}},i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}+1),

where
j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}}\in[m]
.

∎

As a consequence, we obtain:

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer, and let
\ell\leq m
. Then, if
m-\ell
is even,

|\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}|=\binom{(m+\ell)/2}{\ell},

and, if
m-\ell
is odd, then

|\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}|=\binom{(m+\ell-1)/2}{\ell}\cdot(m-\ell-1).

Proof.

We divide into two cases: if
m-\ell
is even, then, by Lemma
6.2
,
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
is the set of all the permutations that can be written as a product of disjoint cycles consisting of
\ell
cycles of length
1
(the
\ell
fixed points) and
\frac{m-\ell}{2}
cycles of length
2
, each having the form
(i,i+1)
for some
i\in[m]
. If we order these cycles in ascending order, we can encode each such permutation as a tiling of an
m\times 1
board by
\ell
tiles of length
1
and
\frac{m-\ell}{2}
tiles of length
2
(where two numbers in
[m]
belong to the same cycle if and only if their corresponding cells in the board are covered by the same tile). We can represent each tiling as a binary word with
\ell
zeroes (the
1
-tiles) and
\frac{m-\ell}{2}
ones (the
2
-tiles). Therefore, we obtain

|\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}|=\binom{\ell+\frac{m-\ell}{2}}{\ell}=\binom{(m+\ell)/2}{\ell}.

Now, assume that
m-\ell
is odd. Then, by Lemma
6.2
,
\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}
is the set of all the permutations that can be written as a product of disjoint cycles consisting of
\ell
cycles of length
1
,
\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}
cycles of length
2
of the form
(i,i+1)
and one cycle of length
3
of the form
(i,i+1,i+2)
or
(i+2,i+1,i)
.
Ordering these cycles by ascending order, we can encode each permutation as a tiling of an
m\times 1
board by
\ell
tiles of length
1
,
\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}
tiles of length
2
, and
1
tile of length
3
. Since such tilings do not encode the orientation of the
3
-cycle,
|\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}|
is exactly twice the number of such tilings.

We can represent each tiling as a ternary word, with
\ell
zeroes (the
1
-tiles),
\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}
ones (the
2
-tiles) and
1
two (the
3
-tile). Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}_{\ell,m}|=2\cdot\binom{\ell+\frac{m-\ell-3}{2}+1}{\ell,\frac{m-\ell-3}{2},1}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\binom{(m+\ell-1)/2}{\ell}\cdot\frac{m-\ell-1}{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{(m+\ell-1)/2}{\ell}\cdot(m-\ell-1).

∎

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Let
B=tI-M
.
We have

p(t)=\det(B)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}\text{sgn}(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{m}B_{\pi(i),i}.

Since the variable
t
appears only in the diagonal entries of
B
, and
B_{ii}=t
for all
1\leq i\leq m
, we can write
p(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}t^{i}
, where

a_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in S_{m}:\\
\pi\text{ has $i$ fixed points}\end{subarray}}\text{sgn}(\pi)\prod_{i\in[m]:\,\pi(i)\neq i}B_{\pi(i),i}.

In particular,
a_{m}=1
and
a_{m-1}=0
(since there are no permutations in
S_{m}
with exactly
m-1
fixed points). By the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{m,r}
, we have

B_{\pi(i),i}=-M_{\pi(i),i}=x^{-r(\pi(i)-i)}(1+O(x^{-1}))

if
\pi(i)>i
, and

B_{\pi(i),i}=-M_{\pi(i),i}=1+O(x^{-1})

if
\pi(i)<i
.
Therefore, for
0\leq i<m-1
, we obtain

a_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in S_{m}:\\
\pi\text{ has $i$ fixed points}\end{subarray}}\text{sgn}(\pi)\cdot x^{-r\cdot c(\pi)}(1+O(x^{-1})).

We divide into two cases. If
m-i
is even, then, by Lemma
6.2
, we have
c(\pi)\geq\frac{m-i}{2}
for all
\pi\in S_{m}
with
i
fixed points. Moreover, all the permutations obtaining this bound satisfy
\text{sgn}(\pi)=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}
. By Corollary
6.3
, there are exactly
\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}
such permutations. Therefore, we obtain

a_{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}x^{-r\cdot\frac{m-i}{2}}(1+O(x^{-1})).

If
m-i
is odd, then, by Lemma
6.2
, we have
c(\pi)\geq\frac{m-i+1}{2}
for all
\pi\in S_{m}
with
i
fixed points. Moreover, all the permutations obtaining this bound satisfy
\text{sgn}(\pi)=(-1)^{\frac{m-i-3}{2}}
. By Corollary
6.3
, there are exactly
(m-i-1)\binom{(m+i-1)/2}{i}
such permutations. Therefore, we obtain

a_{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i-3}{2}}(m-i-1)\binom{(m+i-1)/2}{i}x^{-r\cdot\frac{m-i+1}{2}}(1+O(x^{-1})).

∎

In order to compute the roots of the characteristic polynomial, we introduce the following change of variable:

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
. Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{m,r}(x)
, and let
p(t)
be its characteristic polynomial. Let
\alpha=\min\{r/2,1\}
. Then,

p(s\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}})=x^{-\frac{rm}{2}}\left(F_{m}(s)+h(s,x)\right),

where

F_{m}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-j}{j}s^{m-2j}

and
h(s,x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
m-2
in
s
whose coefficients are in
O\left(x^{-\alpha}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq i\leq m-2
and let
t=s\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}}
. By Lemma
6.1
, if
m-i
is even, we have

a_{i}t^{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}x^{-\frac{rm}{2}}(1+O(x^{-1}))s^{i}\\
=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}x^{-\frac{rm}{2}}s^{i}+O(x^{\frac{-rm}{2}-1})s^{i},

and if
m-i
is odd, then

a_{i}t^{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i-3}{2}}(m-i-1)\binom{(m+i-1)/2}{i}x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}(1+O(x^{-1}))s^{i}\\
=O(x^{-\frac{rm}{2}-\frac{r}{2}})s^{i}.

In addition,
p(t)
has one extra term:
t^{m}=x^{-\frac{rm}{2}}s^{m}
.
Therefore, we can write

p(t)=p(s\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}})=x^{-\frac{r\cdot m}{2}}\left(F_{m}(s)+h(s,x)\right),

where

F_{m}(s)=s^{m}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq m-2,\\
m-i\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}s^{i},

and
h(s,x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
m-2
in
s
, whose coefficients are either in
O(x^{-1})
or
O(x^{-\frac{r}{2}})
, and therefore are in
O(x^{-\alpha})
.

Finally, using the change of summation index
j=(m-i)/2
, we obtain

F_{m}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-j}{j}s^{m-2j}.

∎

The polynomials
F_{m}(s)
are closely related to the sign-alternating Fibonacci polynomials studied by Donnelly et al. in
[
4
]
. Let

G_{m}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-j}{j}s^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor-j}

be the
m
-th sign-alternating Fibonacci polynomial. The roots of
G_{m}(s)
were determined in
[
4
]
:

Theorem 6.5

(Donnelly, Dunkum, Huber, Knupp
[
4
, Corollary 3.2]
)
.

Let
m\geq 2
. Then, the set of roots of
G_{m}(s)
is

\left\{4\cos^{2}\left(\frac{j\pi}{m+1}\right):\,1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

As a consequence, we obtain

Corollary 6.6
.

Let
m\geq 2
, and let

\mathcal{I}_{m}=\left\{\pm 2\cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{m+1}\right):\,1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

Then, the set of roots of
F_{m}(s)
is
\mathcal{I}_{m}
if
m
is even, and
\mathcal{I}_{m}\cup\{0\}
if
m
is odd.

Proof.

Let
m
be even. Then
F_{m}(s)=G_{m}(s^{2})
. Therefore, by Theorem
6.5
, the set of roots of
F_{m}(s)
is
\mathcal{I}_{m}
.

Now, let
m
be odd. Then
F_{m}(s)=s\cdot G_{m}(s^{2})
. So, by Theorem
6.5
, the set of roots of
F_{m}(s)
is
\mathcal{I}_{m}
, plus the additional root
0
.
∎

In the case when
m
is odd, we will also use the following change of variables:

Lemma 6.7
.

Let

be an odd integer, and let

. Let

, and let

be its characteristic polynomial. Then,

p(s\cdot x^{-r})=(-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\cdot x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\cdot s-(m-1)+g(s,x)\right),

where
g(s,x)
is a polynomial of degree
m
in
s
whose coefficients are in
O\left(x^{-1}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq i\leq m-2
and let
t=s\cdot x^{-r}
. By Lemma
6.1
, if
i
is odd, then
m-i
is even, so we have

a_{i}t^{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i}{2}}\binom{(m+i)/2}{i}x^{-\frac{r(m+i)}{2}}(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right))s^{i}.

For
i=1
, we get

a_{1}t=x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\left((-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\cdot\frac{m+1}{2}+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)\cdot s.

For
i\geq 3
, we obtain

a_{i}t^{i}=x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\cdot O(x^{-r})\cdot s^{i}=x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\cdot O(x^{-1})\cdot s^{i}.

If
i
is even, then
m-i
is odd, and we obtain by Lemma

a_{i}t^{i}=(-1)^{\frac{m-i-3}{2}}(m-i-1)\binom{(m+i-1)/2}{i}x^{-\frac{r(m+i+1)}{2}}(1+O(x^{-1}))s^{i}.

For

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\left((-1)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\cdot(m-1)+O(x^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=-x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\left((-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\cdot(m-1)+O(x^{-1})\right).

For

Therefore, we can write

∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\beta>0
. Let

H(z,x)
be a polynomial in
z
with coefficients depending on the real parameter
x
.
Assume that we can write
H(z,x)=\tilde{H}(z)+E(z,x)
, where
\tilde{H}
is a polynomial in
z
, and

E(z,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}(x)z^{j},

where
b_{j}(x)=O(x^{-\beta})
for all
0\leq j\leq m
.
Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
be a simple root of
\tilde{H}
.
Then, there exist
x_{0},C>0
such that, for all
x\geq x_{0}
,
H(z,x)
contains a unique root in the disk

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-\zeta|\leq C\cdot x^{-\beta}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
b_{j}(x)=O(x^{-\beta})
for all
0\leq j\leq m
, there exist
\tilde{x}_{0}>0
and
K>0
such that, for all
x\geq\tilde{x}_{0}
and all
0\leq j\leq m
,

|b_{j}(x)|\leq K\cdot x^{-\beta}.

Let
\zeta
be a simple root of
\tilde{H}(z)
. Since
\zeta
is simple, we have
\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)\neq 0
,
and by Taylor’s theorem,

\tilde{H}(z)=\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)(z-\zeta)+f(z)(z-\zeta),

where
f(z)
is some function satisfying
\lim_{z\to\zeta}f(z)=0
.
Let

C>\frac{2K}{|\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)|}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{m}(|\zeta|+1)^{j},

and assume
|z-\zeta|=Cx^{-\beta}
. Then, we have

|\tilde{H}(z)|\geq Cx^{-\beta}\cdot|\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)|-Cx^{-\beta}\cdot|f(z)|.

Since as
x\to\infty
we have
z\to\zeta
, there is some
\tilde{x}_{1}>0
such that, for
x\geq\tilde{x}_{1}
,
|f(z)|\leq|\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)|/2
, and therefore

|\tilde{H}(z)|\geq Cx^{-\beta}\cdot\frac{|\tilde{H}^{\prime}(\zeta)|}{2}.

On the other hand, for
x\geq\tilde{x}_{0}
,

|E(z,x)|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}|b_{j}(x)|\cdot|z|^{j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}Kx^{-\beta}|z|^{j}.

For
x\geq C^{1/\beta}
we have

|z|\leq|z-\zeta|+|\zeta|=Cx^{-\beta}+|\zeta|\leq|\zeta|+1.

So, for
x\geq\max\{\tilde{x}_{0},C^{1/\beta}\}
,

|E(z,x)|\leq Kx^{-\beta}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(|\zeta|+1)^{j}.

Thus, for
x\geq\max\{\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{1},C^{1/\beta}\}
, we have

|\tilde{H}(z)|>|E(z,x)|

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z-\zeta|=Cx^{-\beta}
.
Hence, by Rouché’s theorem (see e.g.
[
15
]
),
H(z,x)
and
\tilde{H}(z)
have the same number of roots in the disk

D(x)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-\zeta|\leq C\cdot x^{-\beta}\right\}.

Since
\zeta
is a simple root of
\tilde{H}(z)
, then there is some
\tilde{x}_{2}>0
such that, for
x\geq\tilde{x}_{2}
,
\zeta
is the unique root of
\tilde{H}(z)
in
D(x)
. Let
x_{0}=\max\{\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2},C^{1/\beta}\}
. Then, for
x\geq x_{0}
, there is a unique root of
H(z,x)
in the disk
D(x)
.
∎

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
.
Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{m,r}(x)
, and let
\alpha=\min\{r/2,1\}
.
Then, there exist
x_{0}>0
such that the following holds:
for every
\zeta\in\mathcal{I}_{m}
there is some
C_{\zeta}>0
such that, for all
x\geq x_{0}
, there is exactly one eigenvalue of
M
in the disk

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-\zeta\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}}|\leq C_{\zeta}\cdot x^{-(r/2+\alpha)}\right\}.

Moreover, if
m
is odd, there is some
C>0
such that, for all
x\geq x_{0}
, there is exactly one eigenvalue of
M
in the disk

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,\left|z-\frac{2(m-1)}{m+1}\cdot x^{-r}\right|\leq C\cdot x^{-(r+1)}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
p(t)
be the characteristic polynomial of
M
. Let
\alpha=\min\{r/2,1\}
.
By Lemma
6.4
, we have

p(s\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}})=x^{-\frac{rm}{2}}\left(F_{m}(s)+h(s,x)\right),

(6.2)

where

F_{m}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{m-j}{j}s^{m-2j}

and
h(s,x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
m-2
in
s
whose coefficients are in
O(x^{-\alpha})
.
Let
\zeta\in\mathcal{I}_{m}
. By Corollary
6.6
,
\zeta
is a simple root of
F_{m}(s)
. Hence, by Lemma
6.8
, there exist
x_{\zeta}>0
and
C_{\zeta}>0
such that, for
x\geq x_{\zeta}
,
F_{m}(s)+h(s,x)
contains a unique root in the disk

\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\,|s-\zeta|\leq C_{\zeta}\cdot x^{-\alpha}\}.

Therefore, by (
6.2
), for
x\geq x_{\zeta}
,
p(t)
has exactly one root in the disk

D=\{t\in\mathbb{C}:\,|t-\zeta\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}}|\leq C_{\zeta}\cdot x^{-\frac{r}{2}-\alpha}\}.

That is,
M
has exactly one eigenvalue in
D
.

Now, assume that
m
is odd. By Lemma
6.7
, we can write

p(s\cdot x^{-r})=(-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\cdot x^{-\frac{r(m+1)}{2}}\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\cdot s-(m-1)+g(s,x)\right),

(6.3)

where
g(s,x)
is a polynomial of degree
m
in
s
whose coefficients are in
O(x^{-1})
. Since
\frac{2(m-1)}{m+1}
is a simple root of the polynomial

, there exist by Lemma

such that, for

contains a unique root in the disk

\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\,\left|s-\frac{2(m-1)}{m+1}\right|\leq C\cdot x^{-1}\right\}.

Thus, by (

),

contains exactly one root in the disk

D_{2}=\left\{t\in\mathbb{C}:\,\left|t-\frac{2(m-1)}{m+1}\cdot x^{-r}\right|\leq C\cdot x^{-r-1}\right\}.

That is,

has exactly one eigenvalue in

.

Finally, the claim follows by letting

be the maximum of all
x_{\zeta}
(and
\tilde{x}_{0}

∎

We will need also the following simple lemma giving a sufficient condition for certain families of disks to be pairwise disjoint:

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
D_{1}(x),\ldots,D_{m}(x)\subset\mathbb{C}
be a family of disks, depending on a real parameter
x
, defined by

D_{i}(x)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-(a+\zeta_{i}x^{-\alpha_{i}})|\leq C_{i}x^{-\beta_{i}}\right\},

where
a,\zeta_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
C_{i}>0
and
0<\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.
Assume that
\zeta_{i}x^{-\alpha_{i}}\neq\zeta_{j}x^{-\alpha_{j}}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
.
Then, there exists
x_{0}>0
such that, for all
x\geq x_{0}
,
D_{i}(x)\cap D_{j}(x)=\emptyset
for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
.

Proof.

Let
z_{i}=a+\zeta_{i}x^{-\alpha_{i}}
and
R_{i}=C_{i}x^{-\beta_{i}}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.
Let
1\leq i<j\leq m
. Let
\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}\}
and
\beta=\min\{\beta_{i},\beta_{j}\}
. Note that
\alpha<\beta
. Since
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
, there exists
c>0
such that, for large enough
x
,

|z_{i}-z_{j}|\geq\left||\zeta_{i}|x^{-\alpha_{i}}-|\zeta_{j}|x^{-\alpha_{j}}\right|>cx^{-\alpha}.

On the other hand,

R_{i}+R_{j}\leq(C_{i}+C_{j})x^{-\beta}.

Since
\alpha<\beta
, we obtain for large enough
x
,

|z_{i}-z_{j}|>R_{i}+R_{j},

and therefore
D_{i}(x)\cap D_{j}(x)=\emptyset
, as wanted.
∎

Finally, we can prove our main result, Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By Theorem
1.7
, the spectrum of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
is the union of the spectra of the matrices
\tilde{L}_{k}
, each repeated
\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}
times, for
0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. As we already mentioned in the proof of Corollary
5.1
, the eigenvalues of
\tilde{L}_{0}
are
0
with multiplicity
1
, and
n-1
with multiplicity
n-2
. Since
\binom{n}{0}_{q}-\binom{n}{-1}_{q}=1
, we obtain that
0
is an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
with multiplicity
1
, and
n-1
is an eigenvalue with multiplicity
n-2
.

In addition, if
n
is even, then by (
1.2
),
\tilde{L}_{n/2}
is a
1\times 1
matrix with a unique element
n-2
. Therefore,
n-2
is an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
with multiplicity
\binom{n}{n/2}_{q}-\binom{n}{n/2-1}_{q}
.

For
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
, note that, by Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
4.5
, we have
\tilde{L}_{k}-(n-2)I\in\mathcal{M}_{n-2k+1,k}(q)
. Let
\alpha=\min\{k/2,1\}
.
Then, by Proposition
6.9
(and noting that the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
are all real numbers), there exist
\tilde{q}_{k}>0
such that for every
\zeta\in\mathcal{I}_{n-2k+1}=\mathcal{J}_{k}
there is some
C_{\zeta}>0
such that, for
q\geq\tilde{q}_{k}
,
\tilde{L}_{k}
contains exactly one eigenvalue in the interval

\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}:\,|\lambda-(n-2+\zeta\cdot q^{-k/2})|\leq C_{\zeta}\cdot q^{-(k/2+\alpha)}\},

and if
n
is even, there is some
C_{k}>0
such that
\tilde{L}_{k}
contains exactly one eigenvalue in the interval

\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\,\left|\lambda-\left(n-2+\frac{2(n-2k)}{n-2k+2}\cdot q^{-k}\right)\right|\leq C_{k}\cdot q^{-(k+1)}\right\}.

Let
\tilde{q}_{0}(n)=\max\{\tilde{q}_{k}:\,1\leq k\leq\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\}
and let
C(n)
be the maximum of all
C_{\zeta}
and
C_{k}
. Then, for
q\geq\tilde{q}_{0}(n)
,
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
contains at least one eigenvalue, with multiplicity
\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}
, in the interval

D_{n,k,\zeta}(q)=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}:\,|\lambda-(n-2+\zeta\cdot q^{-k/2})|\leq C(n)\cdot q^{-(k/2+\alpha)}\},

for all
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{J}_{k}
. In addition, if
n
is even,
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
contains at least one eigenvalue, of multiplicity
\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}
, in the interval

\tilde{D}_{n,k}(q)=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\,\left|\lambda-\left(n-2+\frac{2(n-2k)}{n-2k+2}\cdot q^{-k}\right)\right|\leq C(n)\cdot q^{-(k+1)}\right\},

for all
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.

If
n
is odd, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{D_{n,k,\zeta}(q):\,\zeta\in\mathcal{J}_{k}\}
. If
n
is even, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{\tilde{D}_{n,k}(q)\}\cup\{D_{n,k,\zeta}(q):\,\zeta\in\mathcal{J}_{k}\}
. Let
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\mathcal{D}_{k}
.
Note that the midpoints of the intervals in
\mathcal{D}
are all distinct. Indeed, this is clear except in the case when
n
is even, where we need to show that for all
1\leq k\leq(n/2-1)/2
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{J}_{k}
,
\zeta\neq 2(n-2k)/(n-2k+2)
. But
\zeta
is (up to sign) twice the cosine of a rational multiple of
\pi
lying strictly between
0
and
\pi/2
; hence, the only rational values that
\zeta
can obtain are
\pm 1
(see
[
12
, Cor. 3.12]
), and it is easy to check that the inequalities
1\neq 2(n-2k)/(n-2k+2)
and
-1\neq 2(n-2k)/(n-2k+2)
always hold for
1\leq k\leq(n/2-1)/2
. Therefore, by Lemma
6.10
, there is some
q_{0}(n)>\tilde{q}_{0}(n)
such that, for
q\geq q_{0}(n)
, the intervals in
\mathcal{D}
are pairwise disjoint.

Finally, let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\cup\{\{0\},\{n-1\}\}
if
n
is odd, and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\cup\{\{0\},\{n-1\},\{n-2\}\}
if
n
is even. Note that
|\mathcal{J}_{k}|=n-2k+1
if
n
is odd, and
|\mathcal{J}_{k}|=n-2k
if
n
is even. So, the total number of eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
(counting multiplicities) in the union of the intervals in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is at least

\displaystyle 1+(n-2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}|\mathcal{J}_{k}|\left(\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}\binom{n}{k}_{q}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}_{q}=|\text{Fl}_{n,q}(0)|

for odd
n
, and at least

1+(n-2)+\left(\binom{n}{n/2}_{q}-\binom{n}{n/2-1}_{q}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1}(|\mathcal{J}_{k}|+1)\left(\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}\right)\\
=\binom{n}{n/2}_{q}+2\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1}\binom{n}{k}_{q}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}_{q}=|\text{Fl}_{n,q}(0)|

for even
n
.
Since
|\text{Fl}_{n,q}(0)|
is the total number of eigenvalues of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
, we must in fact have equality, and therefore, for every
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
, every interval in
\mathcal{D}_{k}
must contain exactly one eigenvalue of
\Delta_{0}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
, with multiplicity
\binom{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{n}{k-1}_{q}
.

∎

Proof of Corollary
1.6
.

The second part of the statement is proved in Corollary

(and also follows immediately from Theorem
1.5
).
We are left to prove the first part. The argument is similar to the one we presented for

.
We divide into two cases. If
n
is odd, then by Theorem

there is some

such that, for

\displaystyle 2+\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}|\mathcal{J}_{k}|

\displaystyle=2+\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}2\left\lfloor\frac{n-2k+1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=2+\sum_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}(n-2k+1)=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+2.

Similarly, if

such that, for

, the number of distinct eigenvalues of

is exactly

\displaystyle 3+\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1}\left(|\mathcal{J}_{k}|+1\right)

\displaystyle=3+\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1}\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{n-2k+1}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle=3+\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1}(n-2k+1)=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor+2.

∎

7
Concluding remarks

It would be interesting to extend the first part of Corollary
1.6
to all values of
q
(for
q
a prime power), or at least to find an explicit bound for the minimal value of
q
for which the claim holds. Similarly, it would be interesting to find explicit bounds for the constant
q_{0}(n)
in Theorem
1.5
. Moreover, based on some numerical evidence, we believe that the constant
C(n)
appearing in Theorem
1.5
may be replaced by some absolute constant
C
(perhaps
C=2
).

The complex
\text{Fl}_{n,q}
is an example of a building (see e.g
[
1
]
). Garland’s Theorem
1.3
holds in fact not only for the complexes
\text{Fl}_{n,q}
but for a much larger family of buildings.
It seems plausible that Papikian’s conjecture may extend to these more general cases (see
[
13
, Conjecture 5.7]
for such a statement in the
0
-dimensional case).
It may be interesting to try to extend our results to other families of buildings.

The higher dimensional cases of Papikian’s conjecture remain open. In ongoing work, by extending some of the ideas presented here to the high dimensional setting, and relying on the theory of unipotent representations of the general linear group
GL(n,q)
(see e.g.
[
10
]
), we developed an algorithm for computing the eigenvalues of
\Delta_{k}^{+}(\text{Fl}_{n,q})
in the limit
q\to\infty
. As a consequence, we were able to verify the second part of Conjecture
1.4
for several small values of
k
and
n
.
