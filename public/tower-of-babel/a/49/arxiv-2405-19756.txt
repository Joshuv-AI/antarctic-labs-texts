1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider a
d
-dimensional measure-valued Markov process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, called super-Brownian motion (henceforth SBM). For convenience of the reader, we give a brief introduction to the SBM and some pertinent results needed in this article.

Let
\ M_{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the finite measure space and
\psi
be a function on
[0,\infty)
of the form:

\displaystyle\psi(u):=-\beta u+\alpha u^{2}+\int^{\infty}_{0}\left(e^{-uy}-1+uy\right)n(dy),~u\geq 0,

(1.1)

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R},~\alpha\geq 0
and
n
is a
\sigma
-finite measure satisfying

\int^{\infty}_{0}(y^{2}\wedge y)n(dy)<\infty.

The function
\psi
is called a branching mechanism.
The SBM with initial value
\mu\in M_{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and branching mechanism
\psi
is an
M_{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-valued process, whose transition probabilities are characterized through their
Laplace transforms. Write
\nu(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\nu(dx)
for a (random) measure
\nu
and positive function
f
. For all
\mu\in\ M_{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and positive bounded function
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[e^{-X_{t}(g)}\right]=e^{-\mu(v(t,x))},

(1.2)

where
v(t,x),~t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unique locally bounded positive solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\Delta v-\psi(v),\cr v(0,x)=g(x).\end{cases}

(1.3)

\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is called a supercritical (critical, subcritical) super-Brownian motion if
\beta>0

(=0,~<0).
We refer the reader to
[
2
,
7
]
for an explicit definition and more elaborate discussions on the super-Brownian motion.
In this paper, we only deal with the supercritical case, that is
\beta>0
. Moreover, to simplify the statement, we always assume
X_{0}=\delta_{0}
in the rest of the article.

In this work, we are interested in the radius of the range of
\{X_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
up to time
t
, which is defined as

R_{t}:=\inf\{r>0:X_{s}(B^{c}(r))=0,~\forall~0\leq s\leq t\},

where
B^{c}(r)
stands for the complementary set of the ball
B(r):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<r\}
. Denote by
S:=\{\forall t\geq 0,X_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})>0\}
the survival set of the SBM. It is well-known that under Grey’s condition,
\mathbb{P}(S)=1-e^{-\lambda^{*}}
(e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 3.8]
), where
\lambda^{*}\in(0,\infty)
is the largest root of the equation
\psi(u)=0
.

In
[
1
, Theorems 1]
, Engländer proved that if
\alpha>0,

n
is a zero measure and
\rho\in(0,\sqrt{2\beta})
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\leq\rho t|S)=-\beta+\sqrt{\beta/2}\rho,

(1.4)

which is the so-called lower deviation probabilities. He also
[
1
]
tried to consider relevant upper deviation probabilities. He found that the upper deviation probabilities of the branching Brownian motion can be deduced from Freidlin
[
3
]
[
4
]
. For the the upper deviation probabilities of the super-Brownian motion, he conjectured that it has the same asymptotics as the branching Brownian motion. But different ideas should be used.

In the following theorem, we confirm this conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that there exists some constant
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{1}y(\log y)^{2+\gamma}n(dy)<\infty.

(1.5)

Assume
\alpha>0
and
\rho\in(\sqrt{2\beta},\infty)
. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\geq\rho t)=-\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}-\beta\right).

(1.6)

Remark 1.1
.

Condition (
1.5
) comes from Ren et al.
[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
. The reason is that, in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(see (
2.15
) below), we in virtue of the maximum of the one-dimensional super-Brownian motion to derive the lower bound. The assumption
\alpha>0
is because in the proof of upper bound, we use the lower deviation probabilities of
R_{t}
. Nevertheless, the assumptions on the branching mechanism is weaker than Pinsky
[
8
]
and Engländer
[
1
]
, who assumed
\psi(u)=-\beta u+\alpha u^{2}
with
\beta,~\alpha>0
.

Remark 1.2
.

In fact, besides using
[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
, one can also obtain the precise lower bound of Theorem
1.1
easily through the comparison lemma; see
[
1
, Proposition 4]
. So, the difficulty lies in the upper bound. To overcome it, we use the the weighted occupation time of the super-Brownian motion to characterize
R_{t}
, and then the precise upper bound is obtained through the Feynman-Kac formula and lower deviation probabilities of
R_{t}
.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we are going to prove Theorem
1.1
. Let’s give a brief discussion about the
strategy of proofs. The proof of the theorem is divided into two parts–lower bound and upper bound. The lower bound is easy by using the maximum to control the radius of the range. For the upper bound, we use partial differential equations to portray probabilities, and establish a Feynman-Kac formula to estimate the upper bound of the solution of the PDE. To be specific, by using a stopping time strategy to the Feynman-Kac formula, we found the solution of the PDE can be bounded above be the by the deviation probabilities of the Brownian motion and lower deviation probabilities of
R_{t}
.

To establish the Feynman-Kac formula, we should first give a formulation of the weighted occupation time
\int^{t}_{0}X_{s}(\phi)ds
, which was first introduced by Iscoe
[
5
]
for the super-stable process. He then used it to study the supporting properties of super-Brownian motions
[
6
]
. Li
[
7
, Section 5.4]
gives a detailed introduction of the weighted occupation time of a general superprocess.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{x}}
(
\mathbb{P}_{x}
) be the probability measure under which the SBM
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
(the
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
) starts from the Dirac measure (the point) at
x
. Moreover, we write
\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{x}}=\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}_{x}=\mathbb{P}
for short. Assume
\phi(x)
and
g(x)
are non-negative, bounded and continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From
[
7
, Corollary 5.17, Theorem 7.12]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{x}}\left[e^{-X_{t}(g)-\int^{t}_{0}X_{s}(\phi)ds}\right]=e^{-v(t,x)},

(2.1)

where
v(t,x),~t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unique locally bounded positive solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\Delta v-\psi(v)+\phi,\cr v(0,x)=g(x).\end{cases}

(2.2)

Now, we are ready to present the Feynman-Kac formula to above partial differential equation. Set

k(v):=\frac{\psi(v)}{v},

where we make the convention that
k(0)=-\beta
if
v=0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Feynman-Kac formula) Let
v(t,x),t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unique solution of (
2.2
). Then, we have

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[g(B_{t})e^{-\int^{t}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}+\int^{t}_{0}\phi(B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}ds\right].

(2.3)

Proof.

We first cast (
2.2
) into the following mild form (see
[
7
, Corollary 5.17]
):

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[g(B_{t})\right]+\int^{t}_{0}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\phi(B_{s})]ds-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int^{t}_{0}\psi(v(t-s,B_{s}))ds\right],

(2.4)

which implies

\displaystyle v(t,x)\leq\|g\|_{\infty}+\|\phi\|_{\infty}t-\left(\min_{u\geq 0}\psi(u)\right)t.

(2.5)

Let
\nabla_{x}
be the gradient operator. By It
\hat{\text{o}}
’s formula,

\displaystyle d\left[v(t-s,B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\right]

(2.6)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}dv(t-s,B_{s})+v(t-s,B_{s})de^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}

(2.7)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\left[-\partial_{t}v(t-s,B_{s})ds+\nabla_{x}v(t-s,B_{s})dB_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta v(t-s,B_{s})ds\right]

(2.8)

\displaystyle~~~-e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\psi(v(t-s,B_{s}))ds

(2.9)

\displaystyle=e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\nabla_{x}v(t-s,B_{s})dB_{s}-e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\phi(B_{s}).

Integrating on both sides gives that

\displaystyle v(t-s,B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}-v(t,x)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\int^{s}_{0}e^{-\int^{s^{\prime}}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\partial_{x}v(t-s^{\prime},B_{s}^{\prime})dB_{s^{\prime}}-\int^{s}_{0}e^{-\int^{s^{\prime}}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\phi(B_{s^{\prime}})ds^{\prime}.

(2.11)

For
s\in[0,t]
, set

\displaystyle M_{s}

\displaystyle:=\int^{s}_{0}e^{-\int^{s^{\prime}}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\partial_{x}v(t-s^{\prime},B_{s}^{\prime})dB_{s^{\prime}}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=v(t-s,B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}-v(t,x)+\int^{s}_{0}e^{-\int^{s^{\prime}}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\phi(B_{s^{\prime}})ds^{\prime}.

(2.13)

This, combined with (
2.5
), implies that
M_{s},~s\in[0,t]
is a bounded martingale. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[g(B_{t})e^{-\int^{t}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}-v(t,x)+\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\int^{s}_{0}k(v(t-r,B_{r}))dr}\phi(B_{s})ds\right]

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}[M_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[M_{0}]=0,

which concludes the lemma.

\Box

At this moment, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lower bound.
Let
H_{t}
be the right-most position of the one-dimensional super-Brownian motion at time
t
. It is simple to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\geq\rho t)\geq\mathbb{P}(H_{t}\geq\rho t).

(2.15)

Then, the desired lower bound follows by using
[
9
, (1.23)]
. In fact,
[
9
, (1.23)]
also needs the assumption that there exists some
\theta\in(0,1]
and
a,~b>0
such that

\psi(u)\geq-au+bu^{1+\theta}~\text{for}~u\geq 0.

But this condition is satisfied, since we have assumed
\alpha>0
.

Upper bound.
Let
N
be a
d
-dimensional standard normal random vector. Then, by the polar coordinates transformation, we have for
z>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|N|\geq z)

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\geq z}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}}dy

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{z}\frac{dw_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}r^{d-1}dr,

(2.17)

where
w_{d}
is the volume of a
d
-dimensional unit ball. Thus, by L’H
\hat{\text{o}}
pital’s rule, we have

\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(|N|\geq z)}{e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}z^{d-2}}=1.

Thus, there exists a positive constant
C_{d}
depending only on
d
such that for any
z>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|N|\geq z)\leq C_{d}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}z^{d-2}.

(2.18)

For
M>0
, let
f(x)
be a smooth and non-negative function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(x)>0\}=B^{c}(M)
. Put
\phi_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda f(x)
for
\lambda>0
. Denote by
v_{\lambda}(t,x)
the solution of (
2.2
) with
\phi=\phi_{\lambda}
,
g=0
. Since
X_{s}(\phi_{\lambda})
is right continuous with respect to
s
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\geq M)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\exists s\in[0,t],X_{s}(B^{c}(M))>0)

(2.19)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\exists s\in[0,t],X_{s}(\phi_{\lambda})>0)

(2.20)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\int^{t}_{0}X_{s}(\phi_{\lambda})ds>0\right)

(2.21)

\displaystyle=1-\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int^{t}_{0}X_{s}(\phi_{\lambda})ds}\right]

(2.22)

\displaystyle=1-\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}e^{-v_{\lambda}(t,0)}

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}v_{\lambda}(t,0).

(2.24)

Let
u_{\lambda}(t,x)
be the unique solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u-(-\beta u+\alpha u^{2})+\phi_{\lambda},\cr u(0,x)=0.\end{cases}

(2.25)

Since

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\frac{1}{2}\Delta u+\psi(u)

\displaystyle=\psi(u)-[-\beta u+\alpha u^{2}]+\phi_{\lambda}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\geq\phi_{\lambda}

(2.27)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{2}\Delta v+\psi(v),

(2.28)

by the maximum priciple (see
[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
), we have for any
t,~\lambda\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

v_{\lambda}(t,x)\leq u_{\lambda}(t,x).

This means, in the following proofs, we can assume
n
is a zero measure in (
1.1
). Namely, without loss of generality, we assume

k(v)=-\beta+\alpha v.

By Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}(t,x)=\int^{t}_{0}e^{\beta s}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}\alpha v_{\lambda}(t-r,B_{r})dr}\right]ds.

(2.29)

In the following proofs, we sometimes write
v(t,x):=v_{\lambda}(t,x)
for short. For
\varepsilon\in(0,1\wedge(\sqrt{\beta}/\rho))
, let
T
be the first time of
\{B_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
to hit the boundary of
B((1-\varepsilon)M)
, i.e.,

T:=\inf\{s\geq 0:|B_{s}|=(1-\varepsilon)M\}.

Let
\{B^{\prime}_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
be a independent Brownian motion of
\{B_{s}\}_{s\geq 0}
. Note that if
s\leq T
, then
\phi_{\lambda}(B_{s})=0
. This, together with (
2.29
), yields that

\displaystyle v(t,0)

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}e^{\beta s}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}\alpha v(t-r,B_{r})dr}\right]ds

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}e^{\beta s}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<s\}}\phi_{\lambda}(B_{s})e^{-\int^{s}_{0}\alpha v(t-r,B_{r})dr}\right]ds

(2.31)

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}e^{\beta s}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<s\}}e^{-\int^{T}_{0}\alpha v(t-r,B_{r})dr}\mathbb{E}_{B_{T}}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B^{\prime}_{s-T})e^{-\int^{s}_{T}\alpha v(t-r,B^{\prime}_{r-T})dr}\right]\right]ds

(2.32)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\int^{t}_{0}e^{\beta s}\mathbf{1}_{\{T<s\}}\mathbb{E}_{B_{T}}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B^{\prime}_{s-T})e^{-\int^{s}_{T}\alpha v(t-r,B^{\prime}_{r-T})dr}\right]ds\right],

(2.33)

where third equality follows from the strong Markov property of Brownian motion. By the Fubini’s theorem, (
2.30
) yields that

\displaystyle v(t,0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\leq t\}}\int^{t}_{T}e^{\beta s}\mathbb{E}_{B_{T}}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B^{\prime}_{s-T})e^{-\int^{s-T}_{0}\alpha v(t-T-r,B^{\prime}_{r})dr}\right]ds\right]

(2.34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\leq t\}}\int^{t-T}_{0}e^{\beta(T+\eta)}\mathbb{E}_{B_{T}}\left[\phi_{\lambda}(B^{\prime}_{\eta})e^{-\int^{\eta}_{0}\alpha v(t-T-r,B^{\prime}_{r})dr}\right]d\eta\right]

(2.35)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\leq t\}}e^{\beta T}v_{\lambda}(t-T,B_{T})\right].

(2.36)

Since
g=0
, from (
2.1
), it is simple to see that
v_{\lambda}(t,x)
is increasing with respect to
\lambda
and
t
. Thus, if we let
M=\rho t
, then there exists a positive constant
C_{\varepsilon,\rho,\beta}
depending only on
\varepsilon,~\rho
and
\beta
such that for
t
large enough,

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}(t-T,B_{T})

\displaystyle\leq v_{\lambda}(t,B_{T})

(2.37)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}v_{\lambda}(t,B_{T})

(2.38)

\displaystyle=-\log\mathbb{P}_{B_{T}}(\forall s\in[0,t],X_{s}(B^{c}(M))=0)

(2.39)

\displaystyle\leq-\log\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{0}}(R_{t}\leq\varepsilon\rho t)

(2.40)

\displaystyle\leq-\log\left[\mathbb{P}_{\delta_{0}}(R_{t}\leq\varepsilon\rho t|S)\mathbb{P}(S)\right]

(2.41)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon,\rho,\beta}t,

(2.42)

where the first equality follows from similar arguments to obtain the last equality of (
2.19
) and the last inequality comes from (
1.4
). For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
B_{s}^{(i)}
be the
i
-th component of the vector
B_{s}
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\max_{s\in[0,t]}|B_{s}|

\displaystyle=\max_{s\in[0,t]}\left[\sum^{d}_{i=1}(B^{(i)}_{s})^{2}\right]^{1/2}

(2.43)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum^{d}_{i=1}(\max_{s\in[0,t]}|B^{(i)}_{s}|)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

(2.44)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left[(\max_{s\in[0,t]}B^{(i)}_{s})\vee(-\min_{s\in[0,t]}B^{(i)}_{s})\right]^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

(2.45)

Since
\max_{s\in[0,t]}B^{(i)}_{s}
and
-\min_{s\in[0,t]}B^{(i)}_{s}
have the same distribution
|B^{i}_{t}|
, it follows as above that if
(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}>1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\leq t)

(2.46)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t]}|B_{s}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)M\right)

(2.47)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\left[\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left(\max_{s\in[0,t]}B^{(i)}_{s}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\geq(1-\varepsilon)M\right)

(2.48)

\displaystyle=2^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\left[\sum^{d}_{i=1}\left(B^{(i)}_{t}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\geq(1-\varepsilon)M\right)

(2.49)

\displaystyle=2^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(|B_{t}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)M\right)

(2.50)

\displaystyle=2^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(|B_{t}|/\sqrt{t}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}\right)

(2.51)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}C_{d}e^{-\frac{[(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}]^{2}}{2}}[(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}]^{d-2},

(2.52)

where the last inequality comes from (
2.18
). Plugging (
2.37
) and (
2.46
) into (
2.34
) yields that for
t
large enough,

\displaystyle v(t,0)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\beta t}\mathbb{P}\left(T\leq t\right)C_{\varepsilon,\rho,\beta}t

(2.53)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\beta t}2^{d}C_{d}e^{-\frac{\left[(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}\right]^{2}}{2}}\left[(1-\varepsilon)\rho\sqrt{t}\right]^{d-2}C_{\varepsilon,\rho,\beta}t.

(2.54)

Plugging (
2.53
) into (
2.19
) and then taking limits yields that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{P}(R_{t}\geq\rho t)\leq-\left(\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}{\rho}^{2}}{2}-\beta\right).

The desired upper bound follows by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
