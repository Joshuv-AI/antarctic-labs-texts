Ridge interpolators in correlated factor regression models – exact risk analysis
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We consider correlated
factor
regression models (FRM) and analyze the performance of classical ridge interpolators. Utilizing powerful
Random Duality Theory
(RDT) mathematical engine, we obtain
precise
closed form characterizations of the underlying optimization problems and all associated optimizing quantities. In particular, we provide
excess prediction risk
characterizations that clearly show the dependence on all key model parameters, covariance matrices, loadings, and dimensions. As a function of the over-parametrization ratio, the generalized least squares (GLS) risk also exhibits the well known
double-descent
(non-monotonic) behavior. Similarly to the classical linear regression models (LRM), we demonstrate that such FRM phenomenon can be smoothened out by the optimally tuned ridge regularization. The theoretical results are supplemented by numerical simulations and an excellent agrement between the two is observed. Moreover, we note that “ridge smootenhing” is often of limited effect already for over-parametrization ratios above
5
and of virtually no effect for those above
10
. This solidifies the notion that one of the recently most popular neural networks paradigms –
zero-training (interpolating) generalizes well
– enjoys wider applicability, including the one within the FRM estimation/prediction context.

Index Terms: Factor regression model; Interpolators. Ridge estimators; Correlations
.

2
Correlated factor regression models — mathematical setup

We consider the following classical (linear) pure factor regression model (FRM)

\displaystyle{\bf y}=\bar{Z}\bar{\beta}+{\bf e},\quad X=\bar{Z}L+\bar{E}

(1)

where
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is a matrix of covariates or feature vectors,
{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a vector of response variables, and vector
{\bf e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and matrix
\bar{E}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
characterize deviations from the idealized noiseless pure factor models (throughout the paper, unless clear from the context or explicitly stated otherwise, all vectors are assumed to be
column vectors
).
\bar{Z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times k}
(
k<m
) represents a low-dimensional space of relevant factors and
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times n}
is the matrix of associated covariates/factors loadings. The model is well known and has been extensively used in a variety of fields, statistics, machine learning, econometrics, finance and so on. As such it needs no extensive introduction besides a few basic pointers.
(i)
First, while it clearly resembles classical linear regression model (LRM), its a particularly distinguishing feature is that both response variables
{\bf y}
and covariates
X
are directly connected to a low-dimensional space (here represented through the matrix of factors,
\bar{Z}
). In fact, if both
{\bf e}
and
\bar{E}
are zero, we have the
idealized
FRM where both response variables and covariates reside exclusively in a common low-dimensional space
Z
. Assuming that
{\bf e}
and
\bar{E}
are significantly smaller (in magnitude) than
L
, model in (
1
) effectively represents a realistic version of the idealized FRM.
(ii)
Second, differently from the classical LRM, the factors
\bar{Z}
are
not
observable (within the LRM context, on the other hand, the factors would be viewed as the covariates
X
and as such would be observable). In other words, the factors
\bar{Z}
are hidden and the model is therefore often called
hidden factor model
as well.
(iii)
While it is clear that the chosen model is a classical FRM, it should also be noted that it is a bit different from similar ones studied throughout the literature. For example, the ones studied in
[
8
,
70
]
(and, in a way, revisited in
[
17
]
) also in their essence rely on a connection to a lower dimensional space. However, a bit extra algebraic (and statistical) work is needed to make the precise connection. A very similar observation applies to the discussion of the latent space model in
[
32
]
mentioned earlier.

2.1
FRM versus LRM estimation

Besides the above key technical differences, there is actually a very relevant conceptual similarity between the FRM and the LRM. Namely, as the LRM, the FRM can be tightly connected to the training/testing data internal relations paradigm. In such a context, one takes
X
and
{\bf y}
as fully observable and views the rows of
X
(
X_{i,:},i=1,\dots,m
) as vectors of features that correspond to the response variables which are the elements of
{\bf y}
(
{\bf y}_{i},i=1,\dots,m
). Given the access to
m
data pairs
(X_{i,:},{\bf y}_{i})
. one would like to determine the common relation that presumably exists within each of them. Given a new data pair
({\bf x}^{(t)},y^{(t)})
and restricting to linearity, the estimation goal can then be summarized as

Ultimate
estimation
goal:

Find

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

such that

\displaystyle\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\hat{\beta}\quad\mbox{is ``close'' to}\quad y^{(t)}.

(2)

One should note right here that, differently from the LRM,
\hat{\beta}
is not an estimate of
\bar{\beta}
. Moreover, their dimensions are even different,
\bar{\beta}
is a
k
-dimensional whereas
\hat{\beta}
is an
n
-dimensional vector. This precisely pinpoints the key difference between the LRM and the FRM estimation. Namely, while in both, LRM and FRM, the estimators aim to provide linear relation between the response variables and covariates, in the FRM, the covariates are not factors themselves (as they are in the LRM) but rather admit a factor type linear decomposition given in (
1
). As is clear from (
1
) and (
2
),
\bar{\beta}
is the linear representation of
{\bf y}
in factors
Z
space, whereas
\hat{\beta}
should be such a representation in covariates
X
space.

One should also note that the estimation goal in (
2
) is somewhat flexibly imprecise. It states that the aimed linear representation should be “close” to the response variable. Various measures of accuracy could be of interest depending on the area of application (for a similar consideration within a sparse regression approach see, e.g.,
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
). We here consider the classical
prediction risk
or, in the machine learning/neural networks terminology, the
testing/generalization error
. Assuming a statistical context with random
X
and
{\bf e}
, the prediction risk of an FRM type of estimator,
\hat{\beta}
, is formally defined as the following

\displaystyle R_{e}(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left(\left(\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\hat{\beta}-y^{(t)}\right)^{2}|X,{\bf y}\right),

(3)

where, the
testing
data pair
({\bf x}^{(t)},y^{(t)})
(testing vector of features
{\bf x}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and testing response variable
y^{(t)}
), are independent of the
training
data pairs
(X_{i,:},{\bf y}_{i})
. One should note that the expectation is over
{\bf x}^{(t)}
(throughout the paper, the subscripts next to
{\mathbb{E}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}
denote the randomness to which they relate (when clear from the context, the subscript(s) are not specified)). This basically means that
R_{e}(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})
, as a function of
X
and
{\bf y}
, is still a random quantity. Throughout the paper, we will find it convenient to work with the closely related, so-called,
excess (prediction) risk
which we will introduce a bit later on. As studying the risk will be the central topic of this paper, two points particularly related to a line of work
[
59
,
57
,
58
]
should be emphasized:
1)
In
[
59
,
57
,
58
]
a (sparse) LRM context is studied where the main goal was the (
in-sample
) estimation of the regression coefficients. As a measure of accuracy of the considered LASSO and SOCP estimators, the residual RMSE (root mean square error) was considered. Here a general (non-sparse) FRM context is studied where the predictive (
out-of-sample
) abilities of the associated estimators are of interest. This effectively dictates a natural replacement of the RMSE by the risk from (
3
).
2)
The definition of the risk given in (
3
) clearly indicates that it depends on both
\bar{\beta}
(the “true”
{\bf y}
linear representation in the factors
Z
space) and
\hat{\beta}
(the estimated linear representations in the covariates
X
space). Besides the natural difference between the meaning of
\bar{\beta}
and
\hat{\beta}
, this also means that studying
\hat{\beta}
falls into the category of, what
[
59
]
terms as, the
problem dependent analyses
. It should be noted that, differently from
[
57
,
58
]
(which considered the usual problem data insensitive worst case context),
[
59
]
is the first problem dependent consideration within the
precise
analyses of regression models. Moreover, within such a context,
[
59
]
uncovered the existence of a non-monotonicity (the U-shape RMSE functional dependence of the SNR) and effectively pioneered studying the appearance of such phenomena in precise analyses. As mentioned earlier, these phenomena would later on become very popular in the context of prediction risk dimensional dependence and associated double-descent (see, e.g.,
[
55
,
9
,
75
,
32
,
21
,
22
,
10
]
).

2.2
Key problem infrastructure features

Before starting the technical analysis, we below first introduce the scenarios that will be considered and the associated analytical infrastructure. Problem dimensions, underlying statistics, and relevant estimators are the three architectural components of particular interest. We discuss all of them separately in the following subsections.

2.2.1
Linearly
related dimensions

We will consider large dimensional scenario with
all
key underlying dimensions going to infinity. Within such a context, of particular interest is mathematically the most challenging, large
n

linear
(or proportional) regime, where

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n},\quad\kappa=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{k}{m},

(4)

and both
\alpha
and
\kappa
clearly remain constant as
n
grows. In a way,
\alpha
can be connected to what is in the classical LRM called the under-parametrization ratio (or the inverse of the over-parametrization ratio). Also, unless otherwise stated, all underlying matrices are assumed to be of full rank and we consider
\alpha\kappa<1
context (the number of factors
k
smaller than the number of features
n
). Even outside such regimes, the results that we will present hold with very minimal (or sometimes even without any) adjustments. However, constantly separating special cases would fill the presentation with a tone of unnecessary details that, besides slightly enhanced generality, would bring no novel conceptual insights. We instead opt to keep the presentation neater and, along the same lines, also often simplify writing by avoiding repeatedly using the
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
notation throughout the derivations. It will not be that difficult to see from the context that all expressions involving
n
are assumed to be taken in the
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}
sense.

2.2.2
Underlying statistics

To further strengthen the overall elegance of the exposition, a general Gaussian statistical scenario will be considered (it should though be kept in mind that the obtained results are universal and hold way more generally). Also,
\bar{\beta}
in (
1
) will be viewed as deterministic and fixed which will render
X
and
{\bf e}
as the only source of randomness. To make everything a bit more concrete, all three
\bar{Z}
,
{\bf e}
, and
\bar{E}
are assumed to have Gaussian entries and, for three full rank (non-necessarily symmetric) matrices
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
\overline{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
, and
\overline{\overline{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
given in the following way

\displaystyle\bar{Z}=ZA,\quad\quad{\bf e}=\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v},\quad\quad\bar{E}=E\overline{\overline{A}},

(5)

where
Z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and
E\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
are independent of each other and have iid standard normal entries and
\sigma
is a deterministic parameter independent of
n
or any other problem dimension. Utilizing (
5
), (
1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\bf y}=\bar{Z}\bar{\beta}+{\bf e}=ZA\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v},\quad X=\bar{Z}L+\bar{E}=ZAL+E\overline{\overline{A}}.

(6)

Clearly,
\bar{Z}_{i:,}
(the rows of factor matrix
\bar{Z}
) are independent of each other but the elements within each row are correlated among themselves. Moreover,the correlation matrix associated with each row is given as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bar{Z}_{i,:}^{T}\bar{Z}_{i,:}=A^{T}A.

(7)

One then analogously has for the rows of matrix
\bar{E}
,
\bar{E}_{i,:}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bar{E}_{i,:}^{T}\bar{E}_{i,:}=\overline{\overline{A}}^{T}\overline{\overline{A}},

(8)

and for the noise correlations

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}{\bf e}{\bf e}^{T}=\overline{A}\overline{A}^{T}.

(9)

It will also be useful to consider the following SVDs of all three
A
,
\overline{A}
, and
\overline{\overline{A}}

\displaystyle A=U\Sigma V^{T},\quad\overline{A}=\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}\overline{V}^{T},\quad\overline{\overline{A}}=\overline{\overline{U}}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}.

(10)

A combination of (
6
) and (
10
) easily gives

\displaystyle{\bf y}=ZA\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v}=ZU\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}\overline{V}^{T}{\bf v},

(11)

and

\displaystyle X=ZAL+E\overline{\overline{A}}=ZU\Sigma V^{T}L+E\overline{\overline{U}}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}.

(12)

Utilizing the rotational symmetry of the standard normal entries of
Z
,
{\bf v}
, and
E
, (
11
) and (
12
) are statistically equivalent to the following

\displaystyle{\bf y}=Z\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v},\quad X=Z\Sigma V^{T}L+E\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}.

(13)

In other words, one can basically view
\bar{Z}
,
{\bf e}
, and
\bar{E}
as

\displaystyle\bar{Z}=Z\Sigma V^{T},\quad{\bf e}=\sigma\overline{U}\overline{\Sigma}{\bf v},\quad\bar{E}=E\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}.

(14)

This also means that the model is statistically fully characterized by three iid standard normal objects
Z
,
{\bf v}
, and
E
, three fixed (deterministic) unitary matrices
V
,
\overline{U}
, and
\overline{\overline{V}}
, and three fixed (deterministic) diagonal matrices
\Sigma
,
\overline{\Sigma}
, and
\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}
. The above assumed statistics allows for the existence of the cross-sectional (or inter-factors and inter-features) correlations. It also allows for intra-sample (or time-series) noise correlations. Taking
\overline{U}
and
\overline{\Sigma}
to be identity matrices easily removes these correlations. We keep them to ensure an easier parallelism with the already existing results.

As is typically the case when considering the predictive abilities of the estimators, unless otherwise stated, we assume that
{\bf x}^{(t)}
(the testing features vector) has the same statistics as each of the rows of the training features matrix
X
and that
y^{(t)}
(the testing response variable) has the same statistics as any of the elements of the vector of the training response variables,
{\bf y}
. In more concrete terms, this means that we take

\displaystyle\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}=\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T}L+\left({\bf e}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T},\quad y^{(t)}=\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T}\bar{\beta}+\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\overline{U}_{ij}\overline{\Sigma}_{jj}\right)^{2}}v,

(15)

where
{\bf z}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
{\bf e}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
v\in{\mathbb{R}}
are comprised of iid standard normals and independent among themselves and of
X
and
{\bf y}
(basically,
X
and
{\bf e}
, or
Z
,
{\bf v}
, and
E
). One should note that, due to intra-sample noise correlations,
y^{(t)}
is a function of
i
. This will however play no role in the risk analysis as we now for any chosen
i
formally introduce the following
excess risk
that will be the main subject of our study

\displaystyle R(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle R_{e}(\bar{\beta},\hat{\beta})-\sigma^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\overline{U}_{ij}\overline{\Sigma}_{jj}\right)^{2}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf x}^{(t)}}\left(\left(\left({\bf x}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\hat{\beta}-y^{(t)}\right)^{2}|X,{\bf y}\right)-\sigma^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\overline{U}_{ij}\overline{\Sigma}_{jj}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf z}^{(t)},{\bf e}^{(t)},v}\left(\left(\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T}\left(L\hat{\beta}-\bar{\beta}\right)+\left({\bf e}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}\hat{\beta}-\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\overline{U}_{ij}\overline{\Sigma}_{jj}\right)^{2}}v\right)^{2}|X,{\bf y}\right)

\displaystyle-\sigma^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\overline{U}_{ij}\overline{\Sigma}_{jj}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\Sigma V^{T}\left(\bar{\beta}-L\hat{\beta}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}\hat{\beta}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where we utilized

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}V\Sigma{\bf z}^{(t)}\left({\bf z}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\Sigma V^{T}=V\Sigma\Sigma V^{T},\quad{\mathbb{E}}\overline{\overline{V}}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}{\bf e}^{(t)}\left({\bf e}^{(t)}\right)^{T}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T}=\overline{\overline{V}}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{\Sigma}}\overline{\overline{V}}^{T},

(17)

and independence of
{\bf z}^{(t)},{\bf e}^{(t)}
, and
v
. Also, as having both
\sigma
and magnitude of
\bar{\beta}
simultaneously vary brings no conceptual advantage over having one of them fixed, we scale one of them to one. For example, unless otherwise stated, we take
\sigma
as potentially varying and
\|\bar{\beta}\|_{2}=1
.

2.2.3
Classical linear estimators

Many estimators
\hat{\beta}
could be of interest. Depending on the main objectives and available resources to achieve them some would be preferable over others. The performance versus computational complexity is usually the key tradeoff that ultimately determines the practically usability of the estimator. We here consider there classical “low complexity” estimators:
1)
Minimum
\ell_{2}
norm interpolator (we will often refer to it as GLS (or the generalized least squares);
2)
Ridge regression estimator (we will often refer to it as ridge estimator); and
3)
Basic least squares (or just LS). These estimators are particularly tailored/useful for certain dimensional regimes. For example, the GLS can be used only in the so-called over-parameterized regime, i.e., when
n>m
(or when
\alpha<1
). Exactly opposite of the GLS, the plain LS makes sense only in the under-parameterized regimes where
\alpha>1
(in a way, one can basically think of the LS as being the under-parameterized adaptation of the GLS). On the other hand, the ridge estimator is a ridge regularized version of the least squares and differently from both non-regularized variants, LS and GLS, it can be used in any dimensional regime. More concrete/technical relations between these estimators are also well known and we will revisit some of them throughout the presentation. To formally introduce the estimators, we parallel their corresponding LRM introduction (see also, e.g.,
[
69
]
).

1) Minimum
\ell_{2}
norm interpolator (over-parameterized regime,
\alpha<1
):

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}

\displaystyle\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle X\beta={\bf y}.

(18)

One also easily observes the following closed form solution

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=X^{T}(XX^{T})^{-1}{\bf y},

(19)

or, alternatively,

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y},

(20)

where
(X^{T}X)^{-1}
is the pseudo-inverse of
X^{T}X
(only the positive eigenvalues are inverted).

2) Ridge estimator (any
\alpha
):
After formally defining the ridge regression estimator (for a given fixed
\lambda>0
) as

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(\lambda)\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}\lambda\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{m}\|{\bf y}-X\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

(21)

one easily obtains its following closed form

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{m}\left(\lambda I+\frac{1}{m}X^{T}X\right)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}=\left(\lambda mI+X^{T}X\right)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}.

(22)

it is well known (and not that difficult to see by simply comparing (
20
) and (
22
)) that the GLS estimator is a special case of the ridge regression one, obtained when
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. In other words, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{gls}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\beta_{rr}(\lambda).

(23)

3) Plain least squares (under-parameterized regime,
\alpha>1
):
Quite likely the most well known (linear or nonlinear) estimator is formally defined as the solution of the least square opptmization

\displaystyle\beta_{ls}\triangleq\mbox{arg}\min_{\beta}\frac{1}{m}\|{\bf y}-X\beta\|_{2}^{2}.

(24)

which trivially admits the following closed form solution

\displaystyle\beta_{ls}=(X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}{\bf y}.

(25)

It is interesting to note that in the under-parameterized regime the above ridge estimator definition can be extended to
\lambda\geq 0

4
An analytical connection between FRM and LRM

As mention earlier, the factor regression considered in this paper is tightly connected to the classical linear regression. We discussed the conceptual differences earlier when the mathematical setup of the FRM was introduced. Despite these differences, the two models can still be analytically connected. We discuss the connection in this section.

To make this connection clearer, it is convenient that we recall on the (statistical) mathematical form of the FRM from (
6
)

\displaystyle{\bf y}=\bar{Z}\bar{\beta}+{\bf e}=ZA\bar{\beta}+\sigma\overline{A}{\bf v},\quad X=\bar{Z}L+\bar{E}=ZAL+E\overline{\overline{A}}.

(159)

We assume
\overline{A}=I
and observe that the rows of
X
are uncorrelated and that the covariance of each row
X_{i,:},1\leq i\leq m,
is

\displaystyle\Sigma_{X}=L^{T}\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}L+\Sigma_{\bar{E}}.

(160)

We now consider the LRM with such an
X
, i.e. we consider

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}=\overline{\overline{X}}\overline{\overline{\beta}}+\overline{\overline{\sigma}}{\bf v},

(161)

where the covariance of each row of
\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}
is

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\overline{\overline{X}}}=L^{T}\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}L+\Sigma_{\bar{E}},

(162)

and

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{X}^{-1}L^{T}\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}\bar{\beta}

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\bar{\beta}^{T}\left(L\Sigma_{\bar{E}}^{-1}L^{T}+\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\bar{\beta}},

(163)

Given the independence over
i
, we gave that for any function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\left(\left({\bf y}_{i},X_{i,:}\right)\Longleftrightarrow\left(\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}_{i},\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(f\left({\bf y}_{i},X_{i,:}\right)\Longleftrightarrow f\left(\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}_{i},\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)\right),

(164)

where the equivalences are in the probabilistic sense. In other words, if we can show that the pairs
\left({\bf y}_{i},X_{i,:}\right)
and
\left(\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}_{i},\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)
are statistically identical, we will then have that

and

By the definitions and (

We then also have for the cross-correlations

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left({\bf y}_{i}X_{i,:}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left(\bar{\beta}^{T}\left(\bar{Z}_{i,:}\right)^{T}X_{i,:}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left(\bar{\beta}^{T}\left(\bar{Z}_{i,:}\right)^{T}\bar{Z}_{i,:}L\right)=\bar{\beta}^{T}\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}L,

(166)

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}_{i}\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{\overline{\beta}}^{T}\left(\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)^{T}\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)=\overline{\overline{\beta}}^{T}\Sigma_{\overline{\overline{X}}}.

We also have for the variance of
{\bf y}_{i}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left({\bf y}_{i}^{2}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left(\bar{\beta}^{T}\left(\bar{Z}_{i,:}\right)^{T}\bar{Z}_{i,:}\bar{\beta}\right)+\sigma^{2}{\mathbb{E}}{\bf v}_{i}^{2}=\bar{\beta}^{T}\Sigma_{\bar{Z}}\bar{\beta}+\sigma^{2},

(169)

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{\overline{{\bf y}}}_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\overline{\overline{\beta}}^{T}\left(\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\right)^{T}\overline{\overline{X}}_{i,:}\overline{\overline{\beta}}\right)+\overline{\overline{\sigma}}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}{\bf v}_{i}^{2}=\overline{\overline{\beta}}^{T}\Sigma_{\overline{\overline{X}}}\overline{\overline{\beta}}+\overline{\overline{\sigma}}^{2}

q=0.5,q_{e}=0.3

The choice of the scaling of loading matrices is done very particularly as well. Namely, one can note that we do not have dimension dependent scaling which we should have if the loadings are unitary. Instead, we selected a small
0.01
but fixed scaling of the noise
E

\mbox{SNR}_{f}

\displaystyle\mbox{SNR}_{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm tr}\,\left(ALL^{T}A^{T}\right)}{{\rm tr}\,\left(\Sigma_{\bar{E}}\right)}=\frac{{\rm tr}\,\left(A^{T}A\right)}{{\rm tr}\,\left(\overline{\overline{A}}^{T}\overline{\overline{A}}\right)}=\frac{{\rm tr}\,\left({\mathcal{A}}(q)\right)}{{\rm tr}\,\left(\sigma_{e}^{2}{\mathcal{A}}(q_{e})\right)}

(182)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm tr}\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\bar{{\mathcal{A}}}(q)\right)\right)}{{\rm tr}\,\left(\sigma_{e}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\bar{{\mathcal{A}}}(q_{e})\right)\right)}=\frac{k}{\sigma_{e}^{2}n}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{e}^{2}}\frac{k}{m}\frac{m}{n}=\frac{\alpha\kappa}{\sigma_{e}^{2}}.

Since
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}=0.01
are fixed, we obtain that the factor SNR behaves as

\displaystyle\mbox{SNR}_{f}=\frac{\alpha\kappa}{\sigma_{e}^{2}}=\frac{50}{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(183)

i.e., it is a decaying function of the over-parametrization ratio
\frac{1}{\alpha}
. For example for
\frac{1}{\alpha}=60
we have

\mbox{SNR}_{f}=\frac{5}{6}
, i.e. a fairly low SNR ratio. Yet the risk still remains well below the null one. It should also be noted, that by a no surprise the risk curve exhibits U-shape lack of monotonicity in the over-parameterized regime. However, what is a bit remarkable is that it takes rather strong over-parameterizations of the order of a few tens to ensure that the risk curve starts turning upward. As the above example shows, before reaching such over-parameterizations it remarkably remains way below the corresponding null-risk one (the excess risk for both the GLS and optimally tuned ridge is about ten times smaller than the null-risk). This is in part due to the fact that the SNR of
{\bf y}
linearity is strong (
\mbox{SNR}_{{\bf y}}=\frac{\|\bar{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=5
) and in part due to a residual factor effect and the fact that the number of factors is smaller than the sample size, i.e.,
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}
. As we will see in the next subsection, when everything is scaled properly the FRM model, in addition to exhibiting the double-descent phenomenon, also allows for basic interpolator and optimally tuned ridge regressor to achieve a monotonic behavior as the over-parametrization increases.

5.2.1
Monotonicity in the over-parameterized regime

Given the above explicit solution for the GLS excess risk, one can precisely determine the type of monotonicity. As the over-parametrization
\frac{1}{\alpha}\rightarrow\infty
, one clearly has
\alpha\rightarrow 0
. We also have for the factor SNR

\displaystyle\mbox{SNR}_{f}=\frac{{\rm tr}\,\left(L^{T}A^{T}AL\right)}{{\rm tr}\,\left(\Sigma_{\bar{E}}\right)}=\frac{c_{L}k}{n}.

\displaystyle c_{L}=\frac{c_{l}n}{k}=\frac{c_{l}}{\alpha\kappa},

c_{l}
is exactly the SNR of factor part of the model,

\displaystyle\mbox{SNR}_{f}=\frac{c_{L}k}{n}=c_{l}.

Keeping in mind that

and

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}

5.2.2
A high SNR regime

It is also interesting to note that the above scenario allows one to immediately sees the effect of the factors
\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}
on the excess risk.
Looking carefully at (
199
), we have that no matter how over-parameterized the system is, for a fixed
\kappa
, no further increase in
\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}
can achieve consistency. To see this, we first from (
201
) observe

\displaystyle\lim_{\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty}z=\lim_{\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\frac{c_{l}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}+2\frac{c_{l}}{\kappa}-2\frac{c_{l}^{2}}{\kappa}+(c_{l}+1)^{2}}+\frac{c_{l}}{\kappa}-c_{l}-1}{2c_{l}}=\frac{1}{\kappa}.

(203)

We then from (
199
) also have

\displaystyle\lim_{\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{Z,{\bf v},E}R(\bar{\beta},\beta_{gls})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1+\kappa z^{2}c_{l}^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{\left(1+c_{l}z\right)^{2}-\kappa z^{2}c_{l}^{2}}

(204)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1+\frac{1}{\kappa}c_{l}^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{\left(1+c_{l}\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}c_{l}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{\kappa}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{1-\kappa}.

This should, however, come as a no surprise. Namely as
\mbox{SNR}_{f}=c_{l}\rightarrow\infty
, for all practical purposes, we have the idealized factor model (i.e. the one without the noise
E
). For such a model one can from
X
immediately get
Z
and the resulting estimation/prediction problem transforms into a classical LRM with the under-parametrization ratio
\kappa
and noise variance
\mbox{SNR}_{{\bf y}}=\bar{\sigma}^{2}
. For such a model the above expression,
\frac{\kappa\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{1-\kappa}
, obtained in (
204
) is exactly the associated excess risk.

5.3
Simulation statistics

In all simulation setups we utilized statistics that exactly match the ones used in the theoretical analyses. However, the analytical results hold way more generally and the Gaussianity of
Z
or
E
is not needed. For the completeness, in addition to the presented simulation results, we also simulated two different statistical regimes, the one with the iid
\pm 1
Bernullis and the other with the iid
\sqrt{12}\mbox{Unif}[-0.5,0.5]
. There was no point to include them as the obtained results were virtually identical to the Gaussian ones even for seemingly relatively small dimensions (
n=600
).

6
Conclusion

We studied classical (linear) factor regression models (FRM) and analyzed the performance of the following three well known estimators associated with them:
(i)
minimum norm interpolators (generalized least squares (GLS));
(ii)
ordinary least squares (LS); and
(iii)
ridge regularized estimators. We considered a statistical Gaussian context with inter-(hidden) factors and noise correlations which by the structure of the model implies inter-features correlations as well. Due to underlying statistics the optimization programs that produce the above three estimators are random. Through a utilization of a powerful mathematical engine, called
Random Duality Theory
(RDT), we analyzed each of these programs and obtained precise closed form characterizations of all associated optimizing quantities. Viewed as a function of the over-parametrization ratio, the
excess prediction risk
(as one of the key such quantities) is shown to exhibit the non-monotonic double-descent behavior. The risk’s dependence of all key model parameters, including the dimensions ratios and covariance matrices can be clearly seen through the provided closed form expressions. Moreover, the expressions turned out to be neatly transparent so that one can easily see the
bias
and
variance
risk’s components and how they depend on all system parameters. For the LS estimator, we obtained analytical results that require no numerical optimization and as such are closed form functions of the models underlying covariance matrices.

We showed that (similarly to the linear regression models (LRM)), the optimally tuned ridge regularization can completely smoothen the FRM double-descent phenomenon. The theoretical analyses are supplemented by the corresponding numerical simulations. Two things are observed:
(i)
There is an excellent agrement between the theoretical and simulated results;
(ii)
The “ridge smoothing” effect appears limited for over-parametrization ratios larger than
5
and as virtually nonexistent for those above
10
. This provides a strong confirmation that simple strategies (such as interpolation) can perform as strongly as the more complex/advanced ones.

A fairly generic character of the presented methodology allows for various extensions and/or generalizations. Those mentioned in
[
69
]
apply here as well. For example, imperfectly observed features, absent features, adversarial responses are only a few among many popular ones. For each of them, different types of estimators can be considered as well. No further conceptual insights are needed to handle all of them through the program presented here. The concrete technical realizations of these extensions are usually problem specific and need to be accordingly tailored. We will present them in separate papers.

As pointed out in the companion paper
[
69
]
(and earlier in
[
67
]
), the standard Gaussianity is not required for the presented RDT considerations (for the completeness we even simulated a couple of different statistical regimes and obtained virtually identical final results). The presentation is, however, much neater if it is used. While the detailed writing gets more cumbersome, no substantial conceptual adjustments are needed to accommodate deviations from the Gaussianity. We view an application of the Lindeberg central limit theorem variant (see, e.g.,
[
47
]
) as the most convenient way to extend the results to different statistics and its realization via the approach of
[
18
]
as particularly elegant.
