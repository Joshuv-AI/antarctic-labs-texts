How many cards should you lay out in a game of EvenQuads : A detailed study of caps in A ​ G ​ ( n , 2 ) AG(n,2)
Julia Crager, Felicia Flores, Timothy E. Goldberg, Lauren L. Rose, Daniel Rose-Levine, Darrion Thornburgh, Raphael Walker

Abstract
. We define a cap in the affine geometry A ​ G ​ ( n , 2 ) AG(n,2) to be a subset in which any collection of 4 points is in general position. In this paper we classify, up to affine equivalence, all caps in A ​ G ​ ( n , 2 ) AG(n,2) of size k ≤ 9 k\leq 9 . As a result, we obtain a complete characterization of caps in dimension n ≤ 6 n\leq 6 , in particular complete and maximal caps. Since the EvenQuads card deck is a model for A ​ G ​ ( 6 , 2 ) AG(6,2) , as a consequence we determine the probability that an arbitrary k k -card layout contains a quad.

This paper serves as both a broad introduction to the mathematics behind
Quads
and a detailed study of collections of cards that don’t contain a quad. These sets are called
2
-caps
in

]
and
Sidon Sets
in
[

Quad-free sets in Quads are analogous to
caps
in the card game SET. A
cap
or a
cap set
in the affine geometry
AG(n,q)
is defined to be a subset in which every collection of
q
points is in general position. A SET deck is a model for the affine geometry
AG(4,3)
, and it can easily be generalized to
AG(n,3)
by adding additional attributes. A SET in the game corresponds to a line in
AG(n,3)
, which consists of three collinear points, and thus a cap is a SET-free collection of cards.

Caps in
AG(n,3)
have been well studied, and there is great interest in determining the maximal size of a cap in
AG(n,3)
. This is known as the
Cap Set
problem. The maximal cap size is currently only known for
n\leq 6
and is otherwise open. However, recently Ellenberg and Gijswijt
[
EG17
]
found a much improved upper bound on maximal cap sizes,
(2.756)^{n}
, making use of a technique called the
polynomial method
in a new way
[
CLP17
]
.
See
[
DM03
]
or
[
McM+17
, Chapter 9]
for a detailed introduction to the
Cap Set
problem. This celebrated open problem both informed and inspired our work.

It turns out that a quads deck is a model for the affine geometry
AG(6,2)
, and it can easily be generalized to
AG(n,2)
. Because the only caps in
AG(n,2)
are the singleton sets, for the purpose of studying quads we redefine a
cap in
AG(n,2)
to be a subset in which every collection of
four
points is in general position. Thus, caps in
AG(n,2)
will correspond to quad-frees set in a quads deck.

Although caps in
AG(n,2)
have not been well studied, there are several important results in the literature. In
[
TW21
, Theorem 5.1]
, Tait and Won found asymptotic bounds for the maximal size
M(n)
of a cap in the affine geometry
AG(n,2)
:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,2^{n/2}\leq M(n)\leq 1+\sqrt{2}\cdot 2^{n/2}.

In
[
RRW22
]
, Redman, Rose, and Walker constructed a small complete cap in
AG(n,2)
for each
n
, providing an upper bound on the size of a minimal complete cap, A
complete cap
, which is a cap such that the addition of any new element will create a quad, need not be of maximal size.

In this paper we classify all caps of size
k
in
AG(n,2)
for
k\leq 9

k
-card layout of cards contains a quad, providing an answer to the question in the title:

How many cards should you lay out
in a game of EvenQuads?

1.1.
Quads Gameplay

Quads is played with a special deck of cards, similar to SET. The deck we use in this paper is pictured in Figure
2
. Each card has a design with three attributes — number, color, and shape — and each attribute can be in exactly one of
four
possible states, given in Table
1
.

Table 1.
The three attributes and their states in Quads.

Attribute

States

Number

1, 2, 3, 4

Color

Red, Green, Yellow, Blue

Shape

Squares, Triangles, Circles, Hearts

A standard Quads deck has 64 cards, one for each combination of states and attributes. The goal of the game is to find sets of 4 cards satisfying the
Q
uad Conditions given below.

Quad Conditions:
A set of 4 cards forms a quad if in each attribute one of the following holds.

(1)

The states are the same on each card.

(2)

The states on each card are all different.

(3)

Two different states occur, each on two cards.

Example 1.1
.

In Figure
3
, each of the first three horizontal sets is a quad. In the first set, the cards have the same number of objects (1), the same color (red), and different shapes. The second has different numbers, the same shape (circles), and two pairs each of two colors. The third has two pairs of shapes, and different numbers and colors. However, the fourth fails the Quad Conditions in the attribute shape, because two cards contain circles and the other two have different shapes.

Figure 3.
Three quads and a non-quad.

The first two Quad Conditions are identical to those for SET, but the third is required in order to make sure that any set of 3 cards can be completed uniquely to a quad. To see this, consider the second quad in Figure
3
. The first three cards make it impossible to have all the same or all different colors. This third condition makes a Quads deck a
Steiner Quadruple System
, analogous to a SET deck being a
Steiner Triple System
.

Quads is played by placing a random layout of cards face up on a table. Players race to identify and pick up quads from these cards. Players collects quads into their own piles, and each time 4 new cards are added to the layout from the deck. This process continues until the deck is empty and no quads remain. If at any point the players all agree there is no quad in the layout, an additional card is added. The player who collects the most cards wins.

1.2.
Card layouts in SET and Quads

A key detail of the rules is how many cards to lay out. The official rules for SET suggest an initial 12 card layout. In order to determine the this number, expected value computations can been used to determine the average number of SETs in a
k
-card layout, and computer trials can be used to approximate the probability of a SET in a random
k
-card layout, as the numbers are too large for brute force computions.
[
McM+17
, Chapter 10]
.

In the case of Quads, we are not only able to compute the exact probability of a quad in a
k
-card layout, but we can also derive these results using algebraic and combinatorial arguments that shed light on the underlying mathematical structures.

The main goal of this paper is to determine the affine equivalence classes of caps of size
k
in
AG(n,2)
. This research was motivated by both the

Cap Set
and the desire to determine how many cards to lay out in a game of Quads. For the latter problem, we begin by classifying caps up to affine equivalence, then count the number in each equivalence class, and use these results to compute the probability that a random
k
-card layout contains a quad. Our findings are summarized in Table

Equivalence classes of maximal caps in

2.
Coordinates for Quads

Just as the cards in a SET deck can be assigned coordinates in the finite vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{4}
in a way compatible with the structure of the game, the same can be done with a Quads deck and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
.

2.1.
Quad cards as binary vectors

As a first step, for each attribute we map the four states to the elements of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, which we denote as binary strings:
\{00,01,10,11\}
. The value assigned to each state is called its
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
-coordinate
. For example, we could use the assignments given in the following table.

Table 3.
An assignment of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
-coordinates to states of attributes of Quads cards.

Attribute

States

(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})
-Coordinates

Number

1,2,3,4

00,01,10,11

Color

Green, Red, Blue, Yellow

00,01,10,11

Shape

Heart, Square, Triangle, Circle

00,01,10,11

In this way, each card in a Quads deck corresponds to an element of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{3}
. For example, the card four-green-triangles corresponds to the element
(11,00,10)
. Using the group isomorphism
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{3}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
, each card corresponds to an element of the vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, called the card’s
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-coordinates
, for example
(11,00,10)\rightarrow(1,1,0,0,1,0)
.
This vector space, and hence the Quads deck, forms a model for the finite affine geometry
AG(6,2)
. For a generalized Quads deck with
n
attributes, the cards can be put in one-to-one correspondence with elements of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
, which is a model for
AG(2n,2)
.

We will refer to the standard deck of
64
cards, with three attributes, as
Quad-64
, and the generalized game with
n
attributes as
Quad-
(4^{n})
. The crucial property of these coordinates is the following result, analogous to the corresponding property of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-coordinates for SET cards as in
[
DM03
, page 3]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
be distinct. Then
\{a,b,c,d\}
is a quad if and only if
a+b+c+d=\vec{0}
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that if
w,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then
w+x+y+z=00
if and only if one of the following is true.

•

The elements
w,x,y,z
are all equal.

•

The elements
w,x,y,z
are all distinct.

•

The list of elements
w,x,y,z
consists of two distinct elements repeated twice.

∎

Recall that since
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
is a vector space over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
we have
a+a=\vec{0}
and
a=-a
. The following corollary assures us that not only can three cards always be completed to a quad, but the fourth card is unique.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
be distinct. Then there is exactly one quad containing these elements, and the fourth element is
d=a+b+c
.

Proof.

Let
d=a+b+c
. First, note that
d
cannot equal any of
a,b,c
, because if
d=a
, for example, then
d=d+b+c
, so
b+c=\vec{0}
, so
b=c
, which contradicts the assumption that
a,b,c
are distinct. Hence
a,b,c,d
are distinct elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.
Now, note that
\{a,b,c,d\}
is a quad by Theorem
2.1
, because

a+b+c+d=a+b+c+(a+b+c)=(a+a)+(b+b)+(c+c)=\vec{0}.

Finally, suppose
d^{\prime}\in D
and
\{a,b,c,d^{\prime}\}
is a quad. Then by Theorem
2.1
we know that
a+b+c+d^{\prime}=\vec{0}
. Adding
d^{\prime}
to both sides yields
a+b+c=d^{\prime}
. Therefore
d
is unique, and exactly one quad contains
a,b,c
.
∎

The next corollary follows immediately from Corollary
2.2
, and provides a useful way for computing the sum of two cards without having to recall the entire coordinate assignment — just which card corresponds to the zero vector. Since the sum of an element with itself is
\vec{0}
and the sum of any element with
\vec{0}
is that element, we need only consider the sum of distinct nonzero elements.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
be distinct nonzero vectors. Then
a+b
is the unique card that makes a quad with
\vec{0}
,
a
, and
b
.

It is clear from the proofs that Theorem
2.1
and Corollaries
2.2
and
2.3
are true in a deck with
n
attributes. In fact, one can also play
Quad-
2^{n}
by using what we call

. For example, if we consider the Quad-64 cards containing red or blue symbols, shown in Figure
4

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{5}
. It’s easy to see that this set is closed under taking quads, i.e. for any three cards, the fourth card that completes a quad is still in the set.

3.
Caps in
AG(n,2)

In this section, we define basic terms and explore properties of caps using
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
as a model for
AG(n,2)
. We also introduce the
Qap Visualizer
, an online tool for viewing, constructing, and analyzing caps.

3.1.
Cap definitions and properties

We now define the notions of quad completion and quad closure.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(1)

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be distinct. The
quad completion
of
a,b,c
is
d=a+b+c
.

(2)

The
exclude set
of
S
, denoted
\exc(S)
, is defined by

\exc(S)=\{a+b+c\mid\text{$a,b,c\in S$ are distinct}\}.

An element of
\exc(S)
is called an
exclude point
of
S
.

(3)

The
quad closure
of
S
is the union of
S
with its exclude set,
\qc(S)=S\cup\exc(S)
.

(4)

An element
p\in\exc(S)
has
multiplicity

m
, and is called an
m
-point
of
S
, if there are exactly
m
distinct (unordered) triples of elements of
S
,

\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}\subseteq S\qquad\text{for $i=1,\ldots,m$},

such that
x_{i}+y_{i}+z_{i}=p
for each
i
.

(5)

The set
S
is a
cap
if
S
does not contain a quad, i.e. for any distinct
a,b,c,d\in S
,
a+b+c+d\neq\vec{0}
.
A cap with
k
elements is called a
k
-cap
.

(6)

A cap
S
is
complete
if
\exc(S)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}-S
.

(7)

A cap is
maximal
if it is of maximal size in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

The concept of a complete or maximal cap generalizes easily to the situation where
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is replaced by any
r
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. This will be made explicit in the next section.

We state some useful properties of caps.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(1)

If
S
has fewer than four elements, it is a cap.

(2)

S
is a cap if and only if
S\cap\exc(S)=\emptyset
.

(3)

S
is a complete cap if and only if
\qc(S)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(4)

Maximal caps are complete.

(5)

If all
k
-caps in dimension
n
are complete, then they are maximal.

(6)

If
S
is a
k
-cap, then

\sum_{p\in\exc(S)}\mult(p)=\binom{k}{3}.

Proof.

Statements 1–4 follow directly from the definitions.

For statement 5, suppose not all
k
-caps are maximal. This means that there exists a maximal
m
-cap
C^{\prime}
, where
m>k
. Now remove points until you have a
k
-cap. This cap will not be complete, since you can add points and still have a cap, contradicting our assumption that all
k
-caps are complete.

For statement 6, suppose
S
is a
k
-cap. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of all
3
-element subsets of
S
. We can map each triple
\{a,b,c\}\in\mathcal{T}
to the element
p=a+b+c\in\exc(S)
, and for each
p\in\exc(S)
the multiplicity of
p
is exactly the number of triples in
\mathcal{T}
that map to
p
. It follows that

\sum_{p\in\exc(S)}\mult(p)=\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert=\binom{k}{3}.

∎

Statement 2 of Proposition
3.2
says that if we want to enlarge a cap
S
, we cannot use any elements from
\exc(S)
. i.e. points in
\exc(S)
must be
excluded
from any expanded cap.

We have a useful fact about
m
-points. Not only must the
m
triples that determine the point be distinct, but they cannot have any overlap.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be a cap, and let
p
be an
m
-point of
C
with
m\geq 2
. Then any two triples
\{a,b,c\},\{d,e,f\}\subseteq C
that sum to
p
must be disjoint.

Proof.

Let
\{a,b,c\},\{d,e,f\}\subseteq C
be distinct triples such that
a+b+c=d+e+f
. If the triples overlap in one point, say
a=d
, then
a+b+c=a+e+f
, so
b+c+d+e=\vec{0}
, so these four points form a quad, contradicting the fact that they come from a cap. If they overlap in two points, say
a=d
and
b=e
, then
a+b+c=a+b+f
, so
c=f
, contradicting the assumption that triples are distinct. Hence the triples must be disjoint.
∎

Proposition
3.3
provides a connection between
m
-points and
k
-caps.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be a
k
-cap. If
\exc(C)
contains an
m
-point, then
k\geq 3m
.

Proof.

Suppose
p\in\exc(C)
is an
m
-point. Then by definition there are exactly
m
distinct triples
\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}\subseteq C
,
i=1,\ldots,m
for which
x_{i}+y_{i}+z_{i}=p
. By Corollary
3.3
, these triples are pairwise disjoint, which means the
3m
elements

x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y_{k},z_{k}\in C

are distinct. Therefore
C
contains at least
3m
elements.
∎

Corollary
3.4
implies that the smallest cap whose exclude set contains a
2
-point is a
6
-cap. In Figure
7
we give an example of a
6
-cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4}
(denoted by green diamonds). Points marked with a
2
are
2
-points, because they can be written in exactly two different ways as the sum of three cap points.

. Points with a red

are

-points.

3.2.
The Qap Visualizer

A very useful tool for analyzing caps is the
Qap Visualizer

[

[

If the user hovers over a number, the triples that exclude this point are highlighted.
The user cannot select an element marked with a number, but choosing any empty square will create a larger cap. The user can also deselect an element by clicking on it again.

4.
Affine Geometry

Although we can study
AG(n,2)
using the axioms of finite geometry, we find it more useful to work in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, which allows us to view affine lines, planes, etc. as translates (cosets) of linear subspaces of a vector space. In particular, we can adapt well known results in linear algebra to study caps and flats in
AG(n,2)
viewed as subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

We first recall some basic definitions and results from affine geometry. A detailed treatment can be found in
[
Aud03
]
, among others. We close the section by highlighting specific properties of the finite affine geometry
AG(n,2)
.

4.1.
Preliminary definitions

We recall basic definitions about affine linear algebra over an arbitrary field
K
. The main takeaways are that (1) any
flat
(translate of a linear subspace) can be partitioned into subflats of a given size, and (2) affine transformations preserve affine combinations.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field
K
, and let
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq V
.

(1)

An
affine combination
of
S
is a linear combination

\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in K
satisfy
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}=1
.

(2)

An
affine dependence
of
S
is a linear combination

\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}=\vec{0},

where
\alpha_{1}+\cdots\alpha_{n}=0
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are not all zero.

(3)

The
affine span
of a subset
S\subseteq V
is the set
\aff(S)
of all affine combinations of finitely many elements of
S
.

(4)

S
is
affinely dependent
if some element of
S
is in the affine span of the other elements. Otherwise
S
is
affinely independent
.

(5)

An
r
-dimensional affine subspace

F
of
V
, called an
r
-flat
, is defined to be the affine span of
r+1
affinely independent elements of
V
, or equivalently, the translate
L+v
of an
r
-dimensional linear subspace

L
of
V
.

(6)

An
affine basis
for an
r
-flat
F\subseteq V
is a set of affinely independent elements of
V
whose affine span is
F
. Equivalently, if
F=L+v
, then an affine basis for
F
is given by
\{x+v\mid x\in B\cup\vec{0}\}
, where
B
is a linear basis for
L
.

(7)

An
affine transformation
between vector spaces
V
and
W
over
K
is a function
A:V\to W
of the form
A(x)=M(x)+y
for all
x\in V
, where
M:V\to W
is a linear transformation and
y\in W
is a fixed vector.

(8)

An affine transformation
A
is an
affine isomorphism
, or
affine equivalence
, if
A
is invertible. In this case, we say that
V
and
W
are
affinely equivalent
, denoted
V\cong W
.

(9)

Subsets
S,T\subseteq V
are
affinely equivalent
, denoted
S\cong T
, if
A(S)=T
for some affine isomorphism
A:V\to V
.

Remark 4.2
.

The following results are standard and follow from the definitions above.

(1)

Any affine basis for an
r
-flat contains
r+1
elements, and every element of a flat can be written uniquely as an affine combination of affine basis elements.

(2)

Two affinely independent sets of the same size are affinely equivalent.

(3)

An affine
r
-flat
F=L+v
is a coset of
L
, and
v
can be any element of
F
. Thus, a partition of
V
into the cosets of
L
is also a partition into
r
-flats, where
L
is the r-flat containing
\vec{0}
.

(4)

When
F
is a field with
q
elements, an
r
-flat will contain
q^{r}
elements.

(5)

An affine transformation
A:V\to W

preserves affine combinations
in the following sense. For any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in V
and affine combination
\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}
, where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in F
sum to
1
, we have

A(\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n})=\alpha_{1}A(x_{1})+\cdots+\alpha_{n}A(x_{n}).

Consequently, affine transformations preserve affine independence and dependence.

Because we will rarely use any kind of span other than an affine one, we will drop the affine description. So if
\aff(S)=F
, we will say that
S
spans
F
.

4.2.
Properties of Flats in AG(n,2)

Affine linear algebra over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
has a particularly nice structure. Although several of the results below are true over any finite field, most make use of the fact that in vector spaces over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the only scalars are 0 and 1.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(1)

An affine combination of
S
is a sum of an odd number of elements of
S
.

(2)

S
is affinely dependent if and only if a sum of an even number of elements of
S
equals
\vec{0}
.

(3)

Affine transformations of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
preserve addition of an odd number of elements.

(4)

An
r
-flat
F
containing a
t
-flat
F^{\prime}
can be partitioned uniquely into
2^{r-t}
pairwise disjoint
t
-flats such that one of them is
F^{\prime}
.

(5)

Let
x
and
y
be points in an
r
-flat
F=L+v
, where
L
is a linear subspace of dimension
r
. Then
x+y\in L
.

Proof.

(1)

Since the coefficients of an affine combination lie in
\{0,1\}
, the sum of the coefficients equals
1
if and only if the number of elements is odd.

(2)

A dependence means that some
x_{i}\in S
is an affine combination of other elements of
S
, hence the sum of an odd number of them. Without loss of generality, assume
x_{1}=x_{2}+\ldots+x_{2t}
. Moving
x_{1}
to the other side, and since
x_{1}=-x_{1}
, we get that
x_{1}+x_{2}+\ldots+x_{2t}=\vec{0}
.

(3)

This follows directly from the fact that affine transformations preserve affine combinations.

(4)

Let
F
be an
r
-flat containing a
t
-flat
F^{\prime}
, and let
v\in F^{\prime}
. Then
F=L+v
for some
r
-dimensional linear subspace
L
, and since
v=-v
we can write
L=F+v
. Then
F^{\prime}+v=L^{\prime}
is a linear subspace of
L
with dimension
t
, and
F^{\prime}=L^{\prime}+v
. Since
\lvert{L}\rvert=2^{r}
and
\lvert{L^{\prime}}\rvert=2^{t}
, we can partition
L
into
2^{r-t}
cosets with respect to
L^{\prime}
, say
C_{1},\ldots C_{r-t}
, one of which is
L^{\prime}
. If we translate each of these cosets by
v
, the resulting sets
C_{1}+v,\ldots C_{r-t}+v
are
t
-flats partitioning
F
, and one of these is
L^{\prime}+v=F^{\prime}
.

(5)

Let
x,y\in F=L+v
. Then
x=a+v
and
y=b+v
for some
a,b\in L
. Hence
x+y=(a+v)+(b+v)=a+b\in L
, since
v+v=\vec{0}
and
L
is closed under addition.

∎

Example 4.4
.

We will find it useful to partition an
r
-flat into
4
-flats, especially because this is how they are displayed in the Qap Visualizer. For example, in Figure

5.
Classifying Caps

In this section, we use tools from affine linear algebra to study quads and caps. We then use this to characterize small caps of at most seven points in terms of their geometric, algebraic, and combinatorial properties. In the next section, we will introduce new techniques to characterize caps of size greater than seven.

Our primary goal is to determine the structure of
k
-caps in dimension
n
. This includes the following:

(1)

Determine the dependence relations among elements in a
k
-cap.

(2)

Determine the smallest dimension of a flat that admits a
k
-cap.

(3)

Determine the number of affine equivalence classes of
k
-caps in each dimension.

(4)

Determine the maximal size of a cap in an
r
-flat.

The following theorem is a compilation of our main findings about caps of sizes up to 9. All of these results are described and proved thoroughly in subsequent sections.

Theorem 5.1

(Summary of Cap Characterizations)
.

(1)

Caps of size
k\leq 4
are affinely independent, and maximal in dimension
k-1
.

(2)

All
5
-caps are affinely independent.

(3)

A
6
-cap is either affinely independent, or has a
6
-point dependence relation. In the latter case it is maximal in dimension
4
.

(4)

A
7
-cap is either affinely independent or has a
6
-point dependence relation. In the latter case it is maximal in dimension
5
.

(5)

An
8
-cap is either affinely independent, contains a
6
-point dependence relation, or contains an
8
-point dependence. In the third case it is complete, but not maximal, in dimension
6
.

(6)

A
9
-cap is either affinely independent, has an
8
-point dependence relation, or has two
6
-point dependence relations. In the last case it is maximal in dimension
6
.

Remark 5.2
.

For each

-caps, so that when

there is only one equivalence class, for

and

In what follows, we also determine the

It is of great general interest to determine

are

•

and

•

r_{11}=r_{12}=7
,

5.1.
Geometric descriptions of quads and caps

We can now describe properties of quads and caps in the context of affine geometry.

Proposition 5.3
.

Quads in
AG(n,2)
are exactly the
2
-flats, or affine planes.

Proof.

Let
\{a,b,c,d\}
be a quad. Then
a+b+c+d=\vec{0}
, so
d=a+b+c
is an affine combination of the three elements
a,b,c
. Since the only affine combinations of
a,b,c
are sums of an odd number of these elements, it follows that
\aff\{a,b,c\}=\{a,b,c,d\}
, so the quad is a flat with
4=2^{2}
elements, hence it is a
2
-flat.

Now let
F
be a
2
-flat. Then
F=\aff\{x,y,z\}
for some affinely independent elements
x,y,z
, and by item 1 of Lemma
4.3
we know
\aff\{x,y,z\}=\{x,y,z,x+y+z\}
. Since
x,y,z
are independent, we know that
x+y+z
is distinct fourth element. Summing these elements yields

x+y+z+(x+y+z)=(x+x)+(y+y)+(z+z)=\vec{0},

so
F=\{x,y,z,x+y+z\}
is a quad.
∎

The following lemma will be used repeatedly in the remainder of this paper.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
C
be a cap, and suppose
y\in C
is also in the span of
C-\{y\}
. Then
y
is the sum of
2m+1
elements of
C^{\prime}=C-\{y\}
where
2m+1\geq 5
.

Proof.

Suppose
y
is an affine combination of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in C^{\prime}
. Recall that affine combinations of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are sums of an odd number of elements. Since
y\notin C^{\prime}
, it cannot be a sum of one element of
C^{\prime}
. If
y
is the sum of three distinct elements of
C^{\prime}
, say
y=x_{i}+x_{j}+x_{k}
, then we have
x_{i}+x_{j}+x_{k}+y=\vec{0}
, so
\{x_{i},x_{j},x_{k},y\}
is a quad, contradicting the fact that
C
is a cap. Hence
y
must be the sum five or more elements of
C^{\prime}
.
∎

Now we show that affine independence is a sufficient condition to be a cap.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
S
be an affinely independent subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. Then
S
is a cap, and every element of
\exc(S)
is a
1
-point.

Proof.

Suppose that
S
is affinely independent but not a cap. Then it contains a quad, so there are distinct elements
a,b,c,d\in S
such that
a+b+c+d=\vec{0}
. Then
d=a+b+c
is an affine combination of elements of
S
, contradicting the assumption that
S
is affinely independent. Therefore
S
must be a cap.

For the rest, let
p\in\exc(S)
be an
m
-point and suppose that
m>1
. Then there are at least two distinct triples
\{x_{1},y_{1},z_{1}\}
and
\{x_{2},y_{2},z_{2}\}
in
S
such that
x_{1}+y_{1}+z_{1}=p
and
x_{2}+y_{2}+z_{2}=p
. Then
x_{1}+y_{1}+z_{1}=x_{2}+y_{2}+z_{2}
, and adding
x_{2}+y_{2}
to both sides yields

x_{1}+y_{1}+z_{1}+x_{2}+y_{2}=z_{2}.

By Corollary
3.3
, the two sets of triples are disjoint, so we have five distinct elements of
S
whose sum is an element of
S
. This contradicts the assumption that
S
is affinely independent, so it must be that
m=1
.
∎

To see that affine independence is not a necessary condition to be a cap, we have the following example.

Example 5.6
.

Consider the
6
-cap
C\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{5}
in Figure

. Note that the exclude set contains
2
-points, so by Proposition
5.5

C
is affinely dependent.

Since every quad completion of an affinely independent cap is a
1
-point, there is a unique triple in the cap that has the point as its sum. Hence, we have the following corollary to Proposition
5.5
.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
C
be an affinely independent
k
-cap. Then
\lvert\exc(C)\rvert=\binom{k}{3}
.

Our next result shows that quad closure of a set
S
(not necessarily a cap) is contained in the affine span of
S
, and by repeatedly applying the quad closure we will eventually get all of
\aff(S)
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
and let
\qc^{i}(S)
denote the result of taking the quad closure of
i
times. Then:

(1)

\qc(S)\subseteq\aff(S)
.

(2)

For some
i
,
\qc^{i}(S)=\aff(S)
.

(3)

S=\qc(S)
if and only if
S=\aff(S)
.

Proof.

(1)

Recall that the exclude set
\exc(S)
consists of all sums of
3
distinct elements of
S
. Since the affine span
\aff(S)
contains of all sums of an odd number of elements of
S
, it certainly contains sums of
1
and
3
elements. Thus,

\qc(S)=S\cup\exc(S)\subseteq\aff(S),

(2)

If
x\in S
, we will say
x
is in the
0
th quad closure of
S
, and we know from above that
\qc^{1}(S)
consists of all sums of
1
and
3
points in
S
. Suppose that all sums of
2m+1
points in
S
lie in
\qc^{i}(S)
, for some fixed
i
, and let
x=s_{1}+\ldots+s_{k}
be the sum of
k=2(m+1)+1=2m+3
points in
S
. Then
k-2=2m+1
, so
r=s_{1}+\ldots+s_{k-2}
is in
\qc^{i}(S)
. This means that
x=r+s_{k-1}+s_{k}
must be in
\qc^{i+1}(S)
. Thus, all sums of
2(m+1)+1
points in
S
lie in
\qc^{i}(S)
. By induction, each point in the affine span of
S
is contained in
\qc^{i}(S)
for some
i
.

(3)

Since
S\subseteq\qc(S)\subseteq
aff(S), then
S=\aff(S)
implies
S=\qc(S)
.

Conversely, if
S=\qc(S)
then applying the quad closure again we obtain
\qc(S)=\qc^{2}(S)
, so
S=\qc^{2}(S)
. Continuing, we see that for some
i
we have
S=\qc^{i}(S)=\aff(S)
.

∎

We now define the dimension of a cap, and introduce some terminology for maximal caps and the minimal dimension of a
k
-cap. (We introduced the definitions of
M(r)
and
r_{k}
at the beginning of this chapter, but recall them here for completion.)

Definition 5.9
.

Let
C
be a cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be a cap.

(1)

The
dimension
of
C
, denoted
\dim(C)
, is the dimension of
\aff(C)
, the smallest flat containing
C
.

(2)

We denote by
r_{k}
the
smallest possible dimension
of a
k
-cap.

(3)

We denote by
M(r)
the
maximal cap size
in an
r
-flat in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(4)

If
C
is
r
-dimensional and complete, we say that
C
is a
complete cap in dimension
r
, and that
C

completes
the
r
-flat
\aff(C)
.

Proposition 5.10
.

(1)

All
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
are affinely independent.

(2)

For any
k
-cap
C
, we have
r_{k}\leq dim(C)\leq k-1
.

(3)

The numbers
r_{k}
are non-decreasing.

(4)

If every
k
-cap of dimension
r
is complete, then
M(r)=k
.

Proof.

(1)

A
k
-cap of dimension
k-1
spans a
(k-1)
-flat. The only way that the affine span of
k
elements can be

dimensional is if they are affinely independent.

(2)

By definition,
r_{k}
is the minimum dimension of a flat that can contain a
k
-cap, hence
r_{k}\leq dim(C)
. Since the affine span of any
k
points in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
has dimension at most
k-1
, it follows that the dimension of a
k
-cap is at most
k-1
.

(3)

Since a subset of a cap is also a cap, it follows that the

are non-decreasing.

(4)

This is a restatement of Proposition
3.2
, which says that if all
k
-caps are complete, then they are maximal.

∎

5.2.
Caps of sizes 1–5

When
k\leq 5
,
k
-caps are easy to characterize, as they are all affinely independent.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
C
be a
k
-cap. Then

(1)

When
k\leq 4
,
C
is a maximal cap in dimension
k-1
.

(2)

When
k\leq 5
,
C
is affinely independent.

(3)

When
k\leq 5
,
r_{k}=k-1
.

(4)

When
0\leq r\leq 3
,
M(r)=r+1
.

Proof.

(1)

Suppose
k\leq 4
. By (2), which we prove below,
C
is affinely independent and the span of
C
consists of all sums of one or three elements (since
k<5
), so
\aff(C)=\qc(C)
, hence
C
is a complete cap. Since all caps of size
k
are complete in dimension
k-1
, by Proposition
5.10

C
must be maximal and hence
M(k-1)=k
.

(2)

Suppose
k\leq 5
. The only affine combinations of elements of
C
are sums of 1, 3, or 5 elements of
C
. Since there are at most five elements in
C
, the only way an element of
x\in C
is a (non-trivial) affine combination of the other elements is if
x
is the sum of 3 of them, which means that
x
is a quad completion, contradicting the fact that
C
is a cap. Therefore
C
is affinely independent.

(3)

All caps of size
k\leq 5
are affinely independent, and hence have dimension
k-1
. Thus they cannot exist in any dimension smaller than
k-1
, so
r_{k}=k-1
for
k\leq 5
.

(4)

In the proof of (1), we showed that
M(k-1)=k
for
1\leq k\leq 4
. Setting
r=k-1
, we have
M(r)=r+1
for
0\leq r\leq 3
.

∎

We can say a bit more about
5
-caps, namely that the quad closure is a
4
-flat minus a single point

. The results below provide a seamless transition to the study of
6
-caps.

Theorem 5.12

(Properties of 5-caps)
.

Let
C=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\}
be a
5
-cap and
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}
be the sum of the elements of
C
.

(1)

The set
\qc(C)\cup\{y\}
is a
4
-flat, and the union is disjoint.

(2)

C
is not complete, but
C\cup\{y\}
is a complete
6
-cap in dimension
4
.

(3)

Every point in
\exc(C\cup\{y\})
has multiplicity
2
.

Proof.

(1)

By Remark
4.3
, every element of
\aff(C)
is the sum of an odd number of elements of
C
. Since
\lvert C\rvert=5
, this means
\aff(C)
consists of sums of one, three, and five distinct elements of
C
. The sums of one or three elements together with
C
form the quad closure
\qc(C)
, and
y
is the unique sum of five elements of
C
, so

\aff(C)=\qc(C)\cup\{y\}.

We now show that
y\notin\qc(C)
, so this union is disjoint. Since
C
is affinely independent, all sums of odd numbers of elements are distinct, so
y
cannot be a sum of one or three elements of
C
. Hence,
y\notin\qc(C)
.

(2)

Since there are
\binom{5}{1}+\binom{5}{3}+\binom{5}{5}=16
distinct sums of odd numbers of elements of
C
,
\aff(C)
must be a
4
-flat. Since
\qc(C)
contains all elements except
y
, the unique sum of five elements, this means that
C
is not a complete cap in dimension
4
. But when we include
y
we have
\qc(C\cup\{y\})=\aff(C)
, so
C\cup\{y\}
is a complete

(3)

Proof.

(1)

. By Theorem

(2)

Suppose

(3)

Suppose

be the

(\Rightarrow)

Proposition 5.19
.

Let
C
be a cap containing a
6
-cap that spans a
4
-flat
F
.
Let

be a partition of

into

-flats such that

. Then each

-flat

with

contains at most one point in

, i.e.,

\lvert F_{i}\cap C\rvert\leq 1\qquad\text{for $i=2,\ldots,2^{n-4}$}.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{6}\in C
be points contained in
F_{1}
, and let
y\in C
be another point. By Corollary
5.16
, a
4

for some

Let

be the linear subspace whose cosets are the flats

for

. Also note that

Now by Corollary

, then

and

must be disjoint, which is impossible since

are distinct points of the

in

6.1.
Caps with at most one dependence relation

We start by classifying equivalence classes of
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
, which are affinely independent, and those of dimension
k-2
, which have exactly one dependence relation. In the first case, there is only one equivalence class. In the second case, there is an equivalence class for each odd number between
5
and
k-1
— note that this can only occur when
k\geq 6
.

Recall that in
AG(n,2)
, if
n<r_{k}
then there cannot be a
k
-cap, so in what follows we assume that
n\geq r_{k}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

(1)

For any
k
, there is exactly one equivalence class of
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
.

(2)

For
k\geq 6
, the
k
-caps of dimension
k-2
can be partitioned into
\left\lfloor\frac{k-2}{2}\right\rfloor-1
non-empty equivalence classes, one for each odd integer
2m+1
such
5\leq 2m+1\leq k-1
. Caps in the equivalence class corresponding to
2m+1
contain a dependence relation of
2m+2
points.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.10
, every
k
-cap of dimension
k-1
consists of
k
affinely independent points, and therefore any two are equivalent. Hence for each
k
there is only one equivalence class of
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
.

Let
C
and
D
be
k
-caps of dimension
k-2
for some
k\geq 6
. Then there exist affinely independent caps
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
and
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
of size
k-1
, and
C-C^{\prime}
and
D-D^{\prime}
contain just one point each, say
x\in C-C^{\prime}
and
y\in D-D^{\prime}
. By Lemma
5.4
,
x
is the sum of an odd number
2\ell+1
of elements of
C^{\prime}
and
y
is the sum of an odd number
2m+1
elements of
D^{\prime}
, where
2\ell+1
and
2m+1
are between
5
and
k-1
.
We will show that
C\cong D
if and only if
\ell=m
.

(\Rightarrow)
:

Suppose
C\cong D
via an affine isomorphism
A:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
with
A(C)=D
. Since
A
preserves affine independence and affine combinations, we know
A(C^{\prime})
is an affinely independent subset of
D
of size
k-1
, and
A(x)
is the sum of
2\ell+1
elements of
A(C^{\prime})
.

Write
x=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{2\ell+1}
for distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2\ell+1}\in C^{\prime}
, and let
x_{2\ell+2},\ldots,x_{k-1}
be the remaining elements of
C^{\prime}
. Then
A(x)=A(x_{1})+\cdots+A(x_{2\ell+1})
, so
A(x)+A(x_{1})+\cdots+A(x_{2\ell+1})=\vec{0}
, which means for
1\leq i\leq 2\ell+1
any
A(x_{i})
is the sum of the other
2\ell+1
elements. Then
y
must be an element of the dependent set
A(C^{\prime})=\{A(x),A(x_{1}),\ldots,A(x_{2\ell+1}),\ldots,A(x_{k-1})\}
, for otherwise the affinely independent set
D^{\prime}=D-\{y\}
will contain the
dependent
set
\{A(x),A(x_{1}),\ldots,A(x_{2\ell+1})\}
. This is a contradiction. Therefore
y=A(x)
or
y=A(x_{i})
for some
1\leq i\leq 2\ell+1
, so
y
is the sum
2\ell+1
elements of
D=A(C)
, as noted above. Note that if
\ell\neq m
, then
y
is also the sum of
2m+1
elements of
D
, and equating the two sums will give an affine dependency in
D^{\prime}
, which is impossible. So it must be that
2\ell+1=2m+1
and hence
\ell=m
.

(\Leftarrow)
:

Suppose
\ell=m
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2m+1}\in C^{\prime}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{2m+1}\in D^{\prime}
be elements such that
x=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{2m+1}
and
y=y_{1}+\cdots+y_{2m+1}
. Since
C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
are affinely independent, we know there is some affine isomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
such that
x_{i}\mapsto y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 2m+1
. Since affine tranformations preserve affine combinations, it follows that the isomorphism also maps
x=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{2m+1}
to
y=y_{1}+\cdots+y_{2m+1}
. Therefore
C\cong D
under this isomorphism.

Now we will show that each equivalence class is nonempty. Let
2m+1
be an odd integer with
6\leq m\leq k-1
. Let
C^{\prime}
be an affinely independent set of
k-1
elements, so
C^{\prime}
is a
(k-1)
-cap of dimension
k-2
, and let
F=\aff(C^{\prime})
. Let
y
be the sum of
2m+1
elements of
C^{\prime}
. Then
y\in F
so
C=C^{\prime}\cup\{y\}\subseteq F
. We will prove that
C
is a
k
-cap of dimension
k-2
. It suffices to show that
y\notin\exc(C^{\prime})
.

Suppose that
y\in\exc(C^{\prime})
. Then
y
is the sum of both three elements of
C^{\prime}
and
2m+1>3
elements of
C^{\prime}
. By the uniqueness of distinct affine combinations of affinely independent elements, this is a contradiction. It follows that
y\notin\exc(C^{\prime})
, so
C=C^{\prime}\cup\{y\}
is a
k
-cap with dimension
k-2
contained in the specified equivalence class.

Finally, note that if
y\in C
is the sum of
2m+1
other points in
C
, then these
2m+2
points sum to
\vec{0}
so form a dependence relation.
∎

The proof above contained the following result.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
C
be a
(k-2)
-dimensional
k
-cap, where
k\geq 6
. Then there is a unique integer odd integer
5\leq 2m+1\leq k-1
such that for any affinely independent subset
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of size

, the singleton in

is a sum of
2m+1
elements of

. Equivalently, the only dependence relation in

contains

points.

The situation for
k
-caps of dimension greater than or equal to

is more complicated in general. We will see that for

, all

-caps have dimension
k-1
or

. It turns out that if we only want to analyze caps in Quad-64, it is sufficient to understand

-caps for

. As

is the only case where there can be a cap of dimension

, we will only develop the theory of

dimensional caps in this context.

6.2.

At this point, the hard work done in the previous sections will pay off. We have everything we need to classify caps of size up to

Theorem 6.3

(Equivalence classes of
k
-caps for

)
.

(1)

For
1\leq k\leq 5
, there is exactly one equivalence class of
k
-caps.

(2)

There are two equivalence classes of
6
-caps, one for each cap dimension.

(3)

There are two equivalence classes of
7
-caps, one for each cap dimension.

(4)

There are three equivalence classes of
8
-caps; one for dimension
7
and two for dimension

.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
, any two
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
are equivalent. Since the all caps of size
k\leq 5
are of dimension
k-1
, there is only one equivalence class for these values of

.

We’ve shown previously that
r_{k}=k-2
for
6\leq k\leq 8
, so caps of these sizes can occur in only two dimensions,
k-1
and
k-2
. In the first case,
k-1
, we have one equivalence class for each
k
.

In the second case, according to
6.1
, there is one non-empty equivalence class of
k
-caps of dimension
k-2
for each odd integer

such that

. When

, we have
5\leq 2m+1\leq 6-1=5
, so there is only one equivalence class, and it corresponds to a dependence relation where one cap point is the sum of the remaining
5
.
When
k=7
, because
5\leq 2m+1\leq 7-1=6
, there is also only one equivalence class in dimension
5
, and it corresponds to a dependence relation where one cap point is the sum of
5
other cap points.
When
k=8
, we have
5\leq 2m+1\leq 8-1=7
, so there are two equivalence classes in dimension
6
. In one case, there is a cap point that is the sum of
5
others, and in the other there is a cap point that is the sum of the remaining
7

Theorem 6.4

(Properties of 8-caps)
.

Let

be an

-cap of dimension

(1)

If

contains a

-point dependence, then there exists a

-point

(2)

If
C
contains an

-point dependence, then

is complete and every

is a

-point.

Proof.

Let
C=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{8}\}
.

Suppose
C
contains a
6
-point dependence, say
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{6}=\vec{0}
. These

has an exclude point of multiplicity at least

,

Suppose

How many cards should you lay out

Definition 7.1
.

Let
Q(n,k)
denote the number of
k
-caps in
AG(n,2)
, and let
Q_{r}(n,k)
denote the number of such
k
-caps of dimension

.

Recall that
M(r)
is the size of a maximal cap in dimension

, and observe that if
M(r)<k
then
Q_{r}(n,k)=0
. Recall also that

is the smallest dimension of a flat that can contain a

-cap, so

is the smallest value of

for which

. Note also that

is at most

.

Lemma 7.2
.

If

, then

Q(n,k)=\sum_{r=r_{k}}^{k-1}Q_{r}(n,k).

Otherwise
Q(n,k)=0
.

Proof.

The maximal dimension of the affine span of a
k
-cap is
k-1
, and
r_{k}
is the minimal dimension for a
k
-cap to exist.
∎

Combined with our work in the previous section classifying equivalence classes of caps, we have the following proposition, which follows immediately from Theorems
6.3
and
6.6
.

Proposition 7.3
.

For
1\leq 9\leq k
, we have

Q(k,n)=\begin{cases}Q_{k-1}(n,k)&\text{if $1\leq k\leq 5$},\\
Q_{k-1}(n,k)+Q_{k-2}(n,k)&\text{if $6\leq k\leq 8$},\\
Q_{k-1}(n,k)+Q_{k-2}(n,k)+Q_{k-3}(n,k)&\text{if $k=9$}.\end{cases}

7.1.
Cap Counting Formulas

We will now derive formulas for the number of
k
-caps of dimensions
k-1
and
k-2
for any
k
, and for the number of
9
-caps of dimension
6
. By Proposition
7.3
, this is all that is required to count the number of
k
-caps with
1\leq k\leq 9
.

Recall that all
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
are affinely independent, and by Proposition
5.5
all affinely independent sets of size
k
are
k
-caps of dimension
k-1
). We now give a general formula for the number of affinely independent
k
-caps. We assume throughout that
n\geq r_{k}
.

Theorem 7.4
.

When
k=1
,
Q_{0}(1,n)=2^{n}
, and for
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle Q_{k-1}(n,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{k!}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(2^{n}-2^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}(2^{n}-1)(2^{n}-2)(2^{n}-4)\cdots(2^{n}-2^{k-2})}{k!}

Proof.

First note that all singleton sets are affinely independent, so
Q_{0}(n,1)=2^{n}
. We prove that the above formula holds for all
k\geq 2
by induction on
k
.

Since all subsets of size
k=2
are affinely independent,

Q_{1}(n,2)=\binom{2^{n}}{2}=\frac{2^{n}(2^{n}-1)}{2!},

which matches the desired formula.

Now suppose
k\geq 2
is an integer and the above formula is true for all affinely independent
k
-caps. Any affinely independent
(k+1)
-cap can be built from an affinely independent
k
-cap
C
, so we just need to count the number of ways we can add a point
x
to
C
so that
C\cup\{x\}
is affinely independent. Recall that
\aff(C)
contains
2^{k-1}
points. The only restriction is that
x\notin\aff(C)
, so
x
can be any of the
2^{n}-2^{k-1}
remaining points in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. For any
(k+1)
-cap there are
k+1
distinct ways to view it as the union of a
k
-cap and an additional point, so this method of constructing
(k+1)
-caps from
k
-caps will produce each distinct
(k+1)
-cap exactly
k+1
times. It follows that

\displaystyle Q_{k}(n,k+1)

\displaystyle=Q_{k-1}(n,k)\cdot\frac{2^{n}-2^{k-1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{2^{n}}{k!}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}(2^{n}-2^{i})\right]\cdot\frac{2^{n}-2^{k-1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{(k+1)!}\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(2^{n}-2^{i}).

Hence by induction, the formula is true for all
k\geq 2
.
∎

Now we count the number of
k
-caps of dimension

.

Theorem 7.5
.

For
k\geq 6
, the number of

-caps of dimension

\displaystyle Q_{k-2}(n,k)

\displaystyle=Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{h}\frac{1}{2i+2}\cdot\binom{k-1}{2i+1}

\displaystyle=Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)\cdot\left[\frac{1}{6}\binom{k-1}{5}+\frac{1}{8}\binom{k-1}{7}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2h+2}\binom{k-1}{2h+1}\right]

.

Proof.

Let

.
By Corollary
6.2
and Theorem
6.1
, the set of
k

k-2
can be partitioned into non-empty equivalence classes

, one for each odd integer

with
2\leq m\leq h
.
Hence

Q_{k-2}(n,k)=\sum_{m=2}^{h}\lvert\mathscr{C}_{m}\rvert,

Let
m
be an integer with
2\leq m\leq h
.
Let
C^{\prime}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}\}
be a
(k-1)
-cap of dimension
k-2
, which means it is affinely independent, let

be any
(2m+1)
-element subset, and let

be the sum of the elements of

.
Note that the

cannot be in
\exc(C^{\prime})
because

.
Therefore the union
C=C^{\prime}\cup\{x_{k}\}
is a

-cap of dimension
k-2
.
Conversely, the proof of Theorem
6.1
demonstrates that every
k
-cap of dimension
k-2
can be constructed in this way.
Since
Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)
is the number of
(k-2)
-dimensional
(k-1)
-caps and
\binom{k-1}{2m+1}
is the number of
(2m+1)
-element subsets, there are

ways to perform this construction, and the results cover every possible
(k-2)
-dimensional
k
-cap at least once.
We will now show that each such cap occurs exactly
2m+2
times by this construction.

Suppose
C=C^{\prime}\cup\{x_{k}\}
is as above. Then

(1)

x_{k}+\sum_{s\in S}s=\vec{0}

is the only affine dependence in
C
. It follows that the only way to obtain the same cap

from our construction is to start with a different independent set of the form
(C^{\prime}-\{s_{0}\})\cup\{x_{k}\}
for some

, and choose

as our

There are
2m+1

, this gives exactly

.

Therefore

\lvert\mathscr{C}_{m}\rvert=\frac{Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)\cdot\binom{k-1}{2m+1}}{2m+2}

and hence

\displaystyle Q_{k-2}(n,k)=\sum_{m=2}^{h}\lvert\mathscr{C}_{m}\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{h}\frac{Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)\cdot\binom{k-1}{2m+1}}{2m+2}

\displaystyle=Q_{k-2}(n,k-1)\cdot\sum_{m=2}^{h}\frac{1}{2m+2}\cdot\binom{k-1}{2m+1}.

∎

Theorem 7.6
.

The number of
9
-caps of dimension
6
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is
Q_{6}(n,9)=\frac{35}{9}\cdot Q_{6}(n,7)
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a
9
-cap of dimension
6
. By the proof of part (d) of Theorem
6.6
we can write
C=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{9}\}
where
C^{\prime}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{7}\}
is affinely independent,
x_{8}
and
x_{9}
are each the sum of 5 elements of
C^{\prime}
, and they have 3 elements of
C^{\prime}
in common. For example

\displaystyle x_{8}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5},

\displaystyle x_{9}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{6}+x_{7}.

is one such solution.

We can construct such a cap as follows. Start with an independent set
C^{\prime}
of size 7. There are
Q_{6}(n,7)
ways to do this. Now choose the 3 elements in common when we write
x_{8}
and
x_{9}
as sums of 5 elements of
C^{\prime}
. There are
\binom{7}{3}=35
ways to do this. Finally,
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\binom{4}{2}=3
is the number of ways to split the remaining four elements of
C^{\prime}
into two pairs, one each to complete the sums for
x_{8}
and
x_{9}
, respectively.

Hence there are
35\cdot 3\cdot Q_{6}(n,7)=105\cdot Q_{6}(n,7)
ways construct a 9-cap, but we have overcounted. We will show that each cap occurs exactly
27
times by this construction. It suffices to count how many different independent sets
C^{\prime}
we could have started with to produce the same cap, with the same dependence relations.

Assume our cap has the form
C=C^{\prime}\cup\{x_{8},x_{9}\}
, where
C^{\prime}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{7}\}
and

\displaystyle x_{8}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5},

\displaystyle x_{9}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{6}+x_{7}.

Then we have a triple point described by

(2)

x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=x_{4}+x_{5}+x_{8}=x_{6}+x_{7}+x_{9}.

This divides the elements of
C
into three
3
-element subsets; call them
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}
. In order to construct an affinely independent subset of size 7, we can take at most 5 points from any pair of sets
Y_{i},Y_{j}
(otherwise we will have a dependence relation), and the remaining 2 will come from the third set
Y_{k}
. There are 3 ways to choose one of
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}
, and
\binom{3}{2}\cdot\binom{3}{2}=9
ways to choose two elements each from the other sets, so there are
3\cdot 9=27
distinct affinely independent
7
-element subsets in
C
.

Thus the number of
6

The official instructions for
EvenQuads
given at
/
https://awm-math.org/publications/playing-cards/EvenQuads/ suggest that players begin the game by laying out 8 cards. However, our calculations show the following:

•

An
8
-card layout will contain a quad about
80\%
of the time.

•

A
9
-card layout will contain a quad about
97\%
of the time.

•

A
10
-card layout will always contain at least one quad.

Thus, to guarantee a quad, you should deal out
10
cards. For a bit more of a challenge, we suggest starting with
9
cards. Finally, for more seasoned players, starting with
8
cards will make it more difficult to find quads, and will require adding another card about one-fifth of the time.

A more detailed description of card layouts in Quad-16, Quad-32, Quad-64, and Quad-128 is forthcoming.
