Small perturbations in the type of boundary conditions for an elliptic operator
E. Bonnetier 1 , C. Dapogny 2 and M.S. Vogelius 3

Abstract
. In this article, we study the impact of a change in the type of boundary conditions of an elliptic boundary value problem. In the context of the conductivity equation we consider a reference problem with mixed homogeneous Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. Two different perturbed versions of this “background” situation are investigated, when (i) The homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is replaced by a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a “small” subset ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} of the Neumann boundary; and when (ii) The homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition is replaced by a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on a “small” subset ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} of the Dirichlet boundary. The relevant quantity that measures the “smallness” of the subset ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} differs in the two cases: while it is the harmonic capacity of ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} in the former case, we introduce a notion of “Neumann capacity” to handle the latter. In the first part of this work we derive representation formulas that catch the structure of the first non trivial term in the asymptotic expansion of the voltage potential, for a general ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} , under the sole assumption that it is “small” in the appropriate sense. In the second part, we explicitly calculate the first non trivial term in the asymptotic expansion of the voltage potential, in the particular geometric situation where the subset ω ε \omega_{\varepsilon} is a vanishing surfacic ball.

1.
General setting of the problem

Understanding the perturbations in physical fields caused by the presence of small inhomogeneities in a known ambient medium is crucial for a variety of purposes.
For instance, it allows one to appraise the robustness of the behavior of a body with respect to alterations of its constituent material,
or to reconstruct “small” inclusions with unknown locations, shapes and properties
inside this body; see
[
6
]
for an overview of such applications.
From the mathematical point of view, this task translates into the asymptotic analysis of the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to a “physical” partial differential equation, whose defining domain or material coefficients are perturbed at a small scale, parametrized by the vanishing parameter
\varepsilon
.
Many instances of this general question have been investigated: beyond the model setting of the conductivity equation, addressed for instance in
[
15
,
10
,
16
]
,
let us mention the studies
[
9
,
13
]
in the context of the linearized elasticity system, or the works
[
11
,
35
]
devoted to the Maxwell equations.

Here we investigate, in the physical context of the conductivity equation, an interesting variant of the aforementioned problems, namely the variant when the type of the boundary condition is changed on small sets.

Throughout this article,
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a smooth, bounded domain (
d=2
or
3
), whose boundary is decomposed as follows

(1.1)

\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma_{D}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{N}},\text{ where }\Gamma_{D},\>\Gamma_{N}\text{ are disjoint, non empty, open Lipschitz subsets of }\partial\Omega~,

where we refer to
Definition

2.1
below for the definition of an open Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
.
The regions
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
correspond to homogeneous Dirichlet, and homogeneous Neumann conditions for the voltage potential, respectively; see
Fig.

1
for an illustration of this setting (in the case
d=3
).
The domain
\Omega
is occupied by a medium with smooth isotropic conductivity
\gamma\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, satisfying the bounds

(1.2)

\forall x\in\Omega,\>\>\alpha\leq\gamma(x)\leq\beta,

for some fixed constants
0<\alpha\leq\beta
.
The “background” voltage potential
u_{0}
, in response to a smooth external source
f\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, is the unique

H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the mixed boundary value problem

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{0})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{0}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}.\end{array}\right.

We notice that, as a consequence of the classical regularity theory for elliptic partial differential equations,

u_{0}
is smooth except at the interface,
\Sigma
, between
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
, where the boundary condition changes type;
see e.g.
[
14
,
34
]
.

In this paper we analyze perturbed versions of
Eq.

1.3
, where the boundary conditions are modified on a “small”, open Lipschitz subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
of the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
More precisely, we are interested in two different situations:

•

The case where the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is replaced by a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
on a “small” open Lipschitz subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, lying strictly inside the region
\Gamma_{N}
. In this situation, the voltage potential
u_{\varepsilon}
is the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the boundary value problem

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\cup\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}.\end{array}\right.

•

The case where the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Gamma_{D}
is replaced by a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on a “small” open Lipschitz
subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, located strictly inside
\Gamma_{D}
. The voltage potential
u_{\varepsilon}
is then the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the boundary value problem

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\cup\omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

In either case, we assume that
\omega_{\varepsilon}
lies “far” from the transition region
\Sigma
, in the sense that

(1.6)

\text{There exists a constant }d_{\text{\rm min}}>0\text{ such that, for all }\varepsilon>0,\>\>\text{\rm dist}(\omega_{\varepsilon},\Sigma)\geq d_{\text{\rm min}}.

Figure 1.
The considered setting when the Neumann region
\Gamma_{N}
is perturbed by a “small” subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
bearing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Such problems show up in multiple physical applications.
The former situation, where homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions are replaced by Dirichlet boundary conditions, is sometimes referred to as the “narrow escape problem” in the literature. Originating from acoustics, it has recently attracted much attention due to its significance in the field of biology.
In this setting indeed,
\Omega
represents a cavity whose boundary is reflecting except on the small absorbing window
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\partial\Omega
.
The particles inside
\Omega
are guided by a Brownian motion; they may only leave through the region
\omega_{\varepsilon}
and
the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to
Eq.

1.4
then represents their mean exit time.
We refer to
[
38
]
and the references therein for an overview of the physical relevance of this problem and for an account of recent developments.
In this context, the asymptotic behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
in the limit where
\varepsilon
vanishes has been analyzed in
[
20
,
57
]

by means of formal matched asymptotic expansions; the rigorous proofs of these results were later provided in
[
19
,
5
]
for “simple” sets
\omega_{\varepsilon}
.
Let us also mention the interesting variant of this “narrow escape problem”, tackled in
[
45
,
46
]
,
where the vanishing exit region
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is connected to a thin, elongated channel, whose presence is modeled through the replacement of homogeneous Neumann conditions by Robin (and not Dirichlet) boundary conditions on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
.

The case
Eq.

1.5
where homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are replaced by homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
on the vanishing region
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\partial\Omega
seems to have been more rarely considered.
Let us however mention the early investigations conducted in
[
33
,
32
]
,
where the asymptotics of the eigen elements of the Laplace operator are examined as the boundary condition passes from Dirichlet to Neumann type
on a small surfacic ball
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\partial\Omega
.
An analogous study is found in
[
58
]
, where, in three space dimensions,
the small subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is shaped as a thin neighborhood of a curve on
\partial\Omega
.
An interesting physical motivation for this problem
was recently provided in the work
[
41
]
, devoted to the construction of metasurfaces capable of affecting such changes in boundary conditions. This mechanism was analyzed from the mathematical point of view, and the corresponding asymptotic behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
was derived in 2d in
[
4
]
, under the technical assumption that the boundary of
\Omega
is completely flat in a neighborhood of the
\omega_{\varepsilon}
;
these results were then used in
[
3
]
so as to determine the optimal placement of such metasurfaces.

The present paper addresses both situations
Eqs.

1.4
and
1.5
: our goal is to understand the asymptotic behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, a limiting regime in which
the small inclusions
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, where boundary conditions are changed, “vanish” in an appropriate sense. As we shall see, the relevant measure of “smallness” for the set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
depends on which one of the above situations we are in.
Our investigations go in two complementary directions. In the first part of this paper, we work from a quite abstract point of view,
making minimal assumptions about the inclusion set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, apart from “smallness”.
We derive the general structure of the lowest order terms in the asymptotic expansion of the perturbation
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
. In the second part of this paper, we consider a more specific situation
as far as the geometry of the inclusion set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is concerned: we assume that
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a surfacic ball of radius
\varepsilon
on
\partial\Omega
.
In the two- and three-dimensional instances of
Eqs.

1.4
and
1.5
,
we precisely calculate the lowest order terms in the asymptotic expansion of the perturbation
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
, thus offering four non trivial examples of our more abstract formulas.
As we shall see, our mathematical treatment of these four cases, based on an integral equation method,
displays some similarities but also important differences. To emphasize both aspects, we shall use the same notation for corresponding quantities, as far as possible.

This paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we recall some background material from functional analysis and potential theory,
which is essential for the rest of our investigation.
In
Section

3
we analyze, from an abstract point of view, the general structure of the (lowest order terms of the) perturbation
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
, when the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is replaced with a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a small subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}\Subset\Gamma_{N}
.
In
Section

4
we investigate the case when the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition is replaced with a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on a small subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}\Subset\Gamma_{D}
.

Sections

5
and
6
are then devoted to the explicit asymptotic expansion of
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
for both scenarios in the particular case where
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a small surfacic ball,
lying in
\Gamma_{N}
or
\Gamma_{D}
, respectively.
In
Section

7
we outline a few natural ideas for future work, suggested by the present study.
At the end this article are four appendices, collecting several useful results from the litterature, as well as some technical calculations in close connection with the topics discussed in the main parts of the text.

2.
Preliminary material

We initiate our study by collecting some essential background material.
In
Section

2.1
, we outline classical results about fractional Sobolev spaces defined on the boundary of a smooth domain
\Omega
, or on a relatively open Lipschitz subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
;
in the latter case we emphasize the difference between the spaces
H^{s}(\Gamma)
and
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
.
In
Section

2.2
we summarize the main properties of layer potential operators, and in
Section

2.3
we make a few remarks about the construction of fundamental solutions to boundary value problems with variable coefficients.
Finally, in
Section

2.4
we introduce and discuss the notion of
H^{1}
capacity,
which turns out to be the relevant measure of smallness for sets supporting Dirichlet boundary conditions.

2.1.
The Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
,
H^{s}(\Gamma)
and
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)

As is customary in the literature, for an arbitrary integer
n\geq 0
,

H^{n}(\partial\Omega)
stands for the Sobolev space of functions
u\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
defined on the boundary of
\Omega
,
whose tangential derivatives up to order
n
also belong to
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, and the space
H^{-n}(\partial\Omega)
is the topological dual of
H^{n}(\partial\Omega)
.

The definition of Sobolev spaces with fractional exponents on the closed hypersurface
\partial\Omega
,
or on an open Lipschitz subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
gives rise to some subtleties,
which we briefly describe in this section, referring to
[
49
]
and
[
36
,
47
]
for more details.

Let us first consider Sobolev spaces of functions attached to the whole boundary
\partial\Omega
. Given a real number
0<s<1
, there are several equivalent ways of defining a norm on the fractional Sobolev Space
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
; we use the following definition

\|v\|^{2}_{H^{s}(\partial\Omega)}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{\partial\Omega}{\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d-1+2s}}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

Note that, in the literature the geodesic distance
d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
is often used in place of the Euclidean one
|x-y|
in the above formula.
However, since
\Omega
is smooth and compact, the resulting norms are equivalent (with a constant depending on
\Omega
); see
Lemma

A.1
.

When
-1<s\leq 0
,
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
is the topological dual of
H^{-s}(\partial\Omega)
.

We next turn to Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Gamma)
, defined on a proper region
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
,
and to this end, we introduce a definition.

Definition 2.1
.

An open, connected subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
is called a Lipschitz subdomain if locally at its boundary,
\Gamma
consists of all points located on one side of the graph of a Lipschitz function. A Lipschitz subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
is then defined to be the reunion of a finite number of Lipschitz subdomains, the closures of which do not intersect.

Let then
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
be a Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
.
For any real number
0<s<1
we introduce the following two classes of Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma
:

•

\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
denotes the space of (restrictions to
\Gamma
of) functions in
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
with compact support inside
\overline{\Gamma}
. This space is equipped with the norm
||\cdot||_{H^{s}(\partial\Omega)}
; it is the closure in
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
of the set of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}
functions on
\partial\Omega
with compact support inside
\Gamma
. Equivalently,
u
belongs to
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
if and only if its extension by
0
to all of
\partial\Omega
,
which we throughout the following still denote by
u
, belongs to
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
.

•

H^{s}(\Gamma)
is the space of the restrictions to
\Gamma
of functions in
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
. This space is equipped with the norm:

(2.1)

\|v\|^{2}_{H^{s}(\Gamma)}=\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+|v|^{2}_{H^{s}(\Gamma)},\text{ where }|v|^{2}_{H^{s}(\Gamma)}:=\int_{\Gamma}{\int_{\Gamma}{\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d-1+2s}}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

which is equivalent to the quotient norm induced by that of
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
, up to constants that may depend on
\Gamma
.

Let us point out a few facts about the relation between both types of spaces:

•

When
0<s<1/2
, the spaces
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
and
H^{s}(\Gamma)
are identical, with equivalent norms.
On the other hand, when
\frac{1}{2}\leq s<1
,
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
is a proper subspace of
H^{s}(\Gamma)
.

•

When
\frac{1}{2}<s<1
, the space
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
coincides with
H^{s}_{0}(\Gamma)
, the closure in
H^{s}(\Gamma)
(for the natural norm
Eq.

2.1
) of the set of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}
functions with compact support
K\Subset\Gamma
.

For any real number
-1<s<0
,
H^{s}(\Gamma)
is still defined as the space of restrictions to
\Gamma
of distributions in
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
(equipped with the quotient norm). This space can be identified with the topological
dual of
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Gamma)
, using as a pairing the natural extension of the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
inner product, that we denote by:

\langle u,v\rangle,\>\>u\in H^{s}(\Gamma),\>\>v\in\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Gamma).

Similarly,
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma)
is the space of distributions in
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)

with compact support inside
\overline{\Gamma}
. It is identified with
the dual space of
H^{-s}(\Gamma)
, using the same pairing (with the same notation).

The case when
s=1/2
is particular:
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
is a proper subspace of
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, with a strictly stronger norm, while the latter space, incidentally, coincides with
H^{1/2}_{0}(\Gamma)
. To better appraise this distinction between
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, we calculate the norm
\|u\|_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)}=\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
of an arbitrary function
u\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\|u\|^{2}_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)}&=&||u||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\int_{\Gamma}{\int_{\Gamma}{\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d}}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+2\int_{\Gamma}{\rho_{\Gamma}(x)|u(x)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\\
&=&\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}+2\int_{\Gamma}{\rho_{\Gamma}(x)|u(x)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}~.\end{array}

The weight
\rho_{\Gamma}
is here defined by

\forall x\in\Gamma,\quad\rho_{\Gamma}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}}\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

The above norm on the space
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)
is stronger than that on
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, and in particular

(2.2)

\left(\int_{\Gamma}{\rho_{\Gamma}(x)|u(x)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|u\|_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma)}~.

The spaces with exponents
\pm\frac{1}{2}
are particularly relevant in the context of variational solutions to boundary value problems like
Eq.

1.3
.
By a variational solution to
Eq.

1.3
we understand a function
u_{0}
in the functional space

H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega),\>\>u=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\right\}

of
H^{1}(\Omega)
functions with vanishing trace on
\Gamma_{D}
(in other words
u_{0}|_{\Gamma_{N}}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{N})
), and for which

\int_{\Omega}\gamma\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla v~\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}fv~\text{\rm d}x~,

for all
v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
(i.e.,
v|_{\Gamma_{N}}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{N})
). Using integration by parts, this identity asserts that:

\gamma\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}=0\text{ as an element in }H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{N}),\text{ and so }\gamma\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{D})~.

2.2.
A short review of layer potentials

In the present section, we denote by
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a smooth bounded domain, and
we briefly recall some background material about layer potential operators associated with
\partial D
;
we refer to
[
7
,
30
,
49
,
61
]
for more details about such operators.

Let
G(x,y)
be the fundamental solution of the operator
-\Delta
in the free space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(2.3)

G(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|&\text{if }d=2~,\\
\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}&\text{if }d=3~.\end{array}\right.

For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the function
y\mapsto G(x,y)
satisfies

-\Delta_{y}G(x,y)=\delta_{y=x}~,

in the sense of distributions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
\delta_{y=x}
is the Dirac distribution at
x
.

For a smooth density function
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\partial D)
, the single layer potential associated with
\phi
is defined by

(2.4)

{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)=\int_{\partial D}{G(x,y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D,

and the corresponding double layer potential is defined by

{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi(x)=\int_{\partial D}{\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D.

The operators
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{D}
extend to bounded operators
from
H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
into
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and from
H^{1/2}(\partial D)
into
H^{1}(D)\cup H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D})
, respectively. In addition, the functions
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi
are both harmonic on
D
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D}
.
Of particular interest are their behavior at the interface
\partial D
.
Let us denote by

(2.5)

g^{\pm}(x):=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}g(x\pm tn(x)),\quad x\in\partial D

the one-sided limits of a function
g
which is smooth enough from either side of
\partial D
and by
[g](x):=g^{+}(x)-g^{-}(x)
the corresponding jump across
\partial D
.
The functions
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi
satisfy the well-known jump relations:

(2.6)

[{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi]=0\text{ and }\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial n}({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi)\right]=-\phi,

and

(2.7)

[{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi]=\phi\text{ and }\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial n}({\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi)\right]=0.

The first and the last of these four jump relations allow to introduce the integral operators
S_{D}
and
R_{D}
, defined for a smooth density function
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\partial D)
by:

S_{D}\phi=({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi)\lvert_{\partial D},\quad S_{D}\phi(x)=\int_{\partial D}{G(x,y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,~~~x\in\partial D,

and

R_{D}\phi=\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left({\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi\right),\quad R_{D}\phi(x)=\underset{\eta\downarrow 0}{\text{\rm f.p.}}\int_{\partial D\setminus B_{\eta}(x)}{\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial n_{x}\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,~~~x\in\partial D,

where f.p. refers to a finite part integral in the sense of Hadamard.
These operators extend as bounded mappings
S_{D}:H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1/2}(\partial D)
and
R_{D}:H^{1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
.

Lastly, we recall the decay properties of the single and double layer potentials at infinity.
For a given density
\phi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
, it follows from the explicit expression
Eq.

2.3
of the fundamental solution
G(x,y)
that, for
d=3

(2.8)

{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right),\text{ and }\lvert\nabla{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)\lvert={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\right),

where we have used the convenient notation
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\right)
to represent a function whose modulus is bounded by
\frac{C}{|x|}
when
|x|
is large enough,
for some constant
C>0
.

The case
d=2
is a little more subtle, and in general one only has

{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)={\mathcal{O}}\left(|\log|x||\right),\text{ and }\lvert\nabla{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)\lvert={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)~,

however, if
\int_{\partial D}{\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=0
, then it holds additionally

(2.9)

{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right),\text{ and }\lvert\nabla{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\phi(x)\lvert={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\right)~.

As far as the double layer potential is concerned, one has for
\phi\in H^{1/2}(\partial D)
and
d=2,3

(2.10)

{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi(x)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{d-1}}\right),\text{ and }\lvert\nabla{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\phi(x)\lvert={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{d}}\right)~.

2.3.
The fundamental solution
N(x,y)
to the background equation
Eq.

1.3

We turn our attention to the case when the reference problem under consideration is not the free-space Laplace equation, but rather the background boundary value problem
Eq.

1.3
.
The fundamental solution
N(x,y)
to the latter is constructed from that
G(x,y)
associated to the operator
-\Delta
in the free space, given by
Eq.

2.3
, in a way which we now briefly describe. We refer, e.g., to
[
31
]
or
[
8
]
for similar results.

For any point
x\in\Omega
, the function
y\mapsto N(x,y)
satisfies the following equation

(2.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y))=\delta_{y=x}&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
N(x,y)=0&\text{for }y\in\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)=0&\text{for }y\in\Gamma_{N}~.\end{array}\right.

This means that, for any function
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\varphi=0
on
\Gamma_{D}
, one has

\varphi(x)=\int_{\Omega}{\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y)\cdot\nabla\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y},\quad x\in\Omega.

By an easy adaptation of the proof of Lemma 2.36 in
[
30
]
, one sees that the function
N(x,y)
is symmetric in its arguments. Furthermore, it is related to the fundamental solution
G(x,y)
to the Laplace equation in free space via the relation

N(x,y)=\frac{1}{\gamma(x)}G(x,y)+R(x,y)~,

where for given
x\in\Omega
,
y\mapsto R(x,y)
is the solution to the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}R(x,y))=\frac{1}{\gamma(x)}\nabla\gamma(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}G(x,y)&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
R(x,y)=-\frac{1}{\gamma(x)}G(x,y)&\text{for }y\in\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma(y)\frac{\partial R}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)=-\frac{\gamma(y)}{\gamma(x)}\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)&\text{for }y\in\Gamma_{N}~.\end{array}\right.

The precise functional characterization of
R(x,y)
follows from standard elliptic regularity theory,
depending on the singularity of
G(x,y)
, see
[
14
,
34
]
.
Without entering into technicalities, let us just mention that, for fixed
x\in\Omega
,
the function
y\mapsto R(x,y)
belongs (at least) to
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, for every open subset
U\Subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus(\Sigma\cup\{x\})
, it is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}
on
\overline{\Omega}\cap U
.

2.4.
The capacity of a subset in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

In one of the two scenarios studied in this article, namely when
\omega_{\varepsilon}
accounts for Dirichlet boundary conditions being imposed inside the Neumann region
\Gamma_{N}
(cf.
Section

3
), the key quantity to measure the “smallness” of the set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
will be the
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
capacity.
For the convenience of the reader, we briefly recall the definition
and two simple results related to this notion, referring to
[
37
]
for further details.

Definition 2.2
.

The capacity
\text{\rm cap}(E)
of an arbitrary subset
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined by:

(2.12)

\text{\rm cap}(E)=\inf\left\{||v||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\>\>v(x)\geq 1\text{ a.e. on an open neighborhood of }E\right\}.

A slightly different formula for
\text{\rm cap}(E)
is that of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For an arbitrary subset
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it holds

(2.13)

\text{\rm cap}(E)=\inf\left\{||v||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\>\>v(x)=1\text{ a.e. on an open neighborhood of }E\right\}.

Proof.

On the one hand, it follows immediately from the definition
Eq.

2.12
that

\text{\rm cap}(E)\leq\inf\left\{||v||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\>\>v(x)=1\text{ a.e. on an open neighborhood of }E\right\}~.

Conversely, if
v_{n}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a sequence of functions such that

v_{n}\geq 1\text{ a.e. on an open neighborhood of }E,\>\>||v_{n}||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\text{\rm cap}(E)~,

then
w_{n}=\min(v_{n},1)
defines a sequence of functions in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
which satisfies

||w_{n}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}\leq||v_{n}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}~;

see for instance
[
37
]
, Proposition 3.1.11. As a result,

\inf\left\{||v||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})},\>\>v(x)=1\text{ a.e. on an open neighborhood of }E\right\}\leq||w_{n}||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq||v_{n}||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\text{\rm cap}(E)~,

which proves
Eq.

2.13
.
∎

We now provide a useful lemma, whereby
the capacity of a subset
\omega
of the boundary
\partial\Omega
of a smooth domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be estimated in terms of the energy norm of a function whose trace equals
1
on
\omega
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\omega
be a Lipschitz open subset of
\partial\Omega
, and let
u
be a function in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
If
u=1
on
\omega
in the sense of traces in
H^{1/2}(\omega)
, then

\text{\rm cap}(\omega)\leq||u||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}.

Proof.

Let
{U}
be an open neighborhood of
\omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and let
\phi
be a
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
function which equals
1
identically on
{U}
.
We decompose the function
u
as:

u=\phi+u-\phi~.

Since the set of functions
v\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with vanishing trace on
\omega
is exactly the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega})
in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
(see
Section

2.1
), and since the trace of
u-\phi
equals
0
on
\omega
, there exists a sequence
v_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega})
such that

\|u-\phi-v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n}~.

We now estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\phi+v_{n}+u-\phi-v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\phi+v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}-\|u-\phi-v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\phi+v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}-\frac{1}{n}~.

The function
\phi+v_{n}
lies in
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
(actually it lies in
C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
) and it equals
1
on an open neighborhood of
\omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. From the definition of
\text{\rm cap}(\omega)
it follows that

\|\phi+v_{n}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\geq\left(\text{\rm cap}(\omega)\right)^{1/2}~,

and so by combination with the previous estimate we get:

\|u\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\geq\left(\text{\rm cap}(\omega)\right)^{1/2}-\frac{1}{n}~.

By passing to the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
we arrive at the desired conclusion.

∎

Remark 2.1
.

From the physical point of view, the capacity of a compact subset
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is the total energy of the electric field in the whole ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, in the equilibrium regime where the potential is constant on
E
(equal to
1
).
Different notions of capacity are found in the literature, depending on the kernel relating the charge distribution (i.e., the source term) to the induced potential.
A very natural notion of capacity is attached to the fundamental solution to the Laplace operator with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a “ground surface”
A
(where the potential is set to
0
); this concept is often associated with the name condenser capacity.
The version proposed in
Definition

2.2
is convenient for our purpose, since it is somehow “universal” (it does not depend on the choice of a fixed grounding subset
A
for the potential), and it involves the Bessel kernel.
It is equivalent to the notion of condenser capacity, up to constants depending on the subset
A
; see for instance
Lemma

3.1
for a result in this direction.
We refer to
[
1
]
for an extensive discussion of the concept of capacity; see also
[
43
,
28
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In
Section

5
we shall be particularly interested in subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of the form

(2.14)

{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\>\>|x|<\varepsilon\right\}~.

{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
is a line segment of length
2\varepsilon
, when
d=2
, and a planar disk with radius
\varepsilon
, when
d=3
.
The following estimates of the capacity of
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
will come in handy

(2.15)

\text{\rm cap}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{C_{2}}{|\log\varepsilon|}\text{ when }d=2,\quad\text{\rm cap}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})\leq C_{3}\varepsilon\text{ when }d=3,

where
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
are universal constants; see for instance Chap. 2 in
[
43
]
.

3.
Replacing Neumann conditions by Dirichlet conditions on a “small set”

In this section, we consider an arbitrary sequence
\omega_{\varepsilon}
of open, Lipschitz subsets of the Neumann region
\Gamma_{N}
,
which are all “well-separated” from the Dirichlet region
\Gamma_{D}
in the sense that the assumption
Eq.

1.6
holds.
The homogeneous Neumann boundary condition
satisfied on
\Gamma_{N}
by the background potential
u_{0}
(see
Eq.

1.3
)
is dropped on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
where it is replaced by a homogeneous Dirichlet condition. The perturbed potential
u_{\varepsilon}
in this situation is the solution to the equation
Eq.

1.4
.

As we shall see, the potential
u_{\varepsilon}
converges to
u_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, when the set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
vanishes in an appropriate sense.
In this general setting, where no additional hypothesis is made about
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
our aim is to establish an abstract representation formula
for the first non-trivial term in the limiting asymptotics of
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
.

3.1.
Some preliminary estimates

We start with some a priori estimates related to modified versions of the perturbed boundary value problem
Eq.

1.4
.
The first of these results
is concerned with the unique solution
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to the problem

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\Delta\chi_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
\chi_{\varepsilon}=1&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
\chi_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~.\end{array}\right.

Let us recall that, by a solution to
Eq.

3.1
we understand a function
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
such that
\chi_{\varepsilon}=1
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
and

(3.2)

\forall v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)\text{ with }v=0\text{ on }\omega_{\varepsilon},\quad\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v\>\text{\rm d}x}=0~.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\omega_{\varepsilon}
be an open Lipschitz subset of the region
\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial\Omega
,
which lies “far” from
\Gamma_{D}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds,
and let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to
Eq.

3.1
.
There exist two constants
0<m\leq M
which depend only on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and the lower bound
d_{\text{\rm min}}
on the distance from
\omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Gamma_{D}
, but are otherwise independent of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, such that

(3.3)

m\>\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\leq||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq M\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})~.

Proof.

We start with the proof of the inequality
m\>\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\leq||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
.

Because of the smoothness of
\Omega
, there exists an extension
\widetilde{\chi_{\varepsilon}}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

such that

(3.4)

||\widetilde{\chi_{\varepsilon}}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq C||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~,

where the constant
C
depends only on
\Omega
(see e.g. Appendix A in
[
49
]
).
Using
Lemma

2.2
we may estimate the capacity of the subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\partial\Omega
, where the trace of
\widetilde{\chi_{\varepsilon}}
equals
1
, by

\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\leq||\widetilde{\chi_{\varepsilon}}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}~.

This inequality, combined with
Eq.

3.4
, yields the desired result.

We now prove that
||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq M\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
. Let us first observe that,
due to a classical variation of the Poincaré inequality, there exists a constant
C>0
which depends only on
\Omega
and
\Gamma_{D}
, such that

(3.5)

||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C||\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}~.

Because of the separation assumption
Eq.

1.6
,
there exists a function
h\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, one has

h(x)=1\text{ for }x\in\omega_{\varepsilon},\>\>h(x)=0\text{ for }x\in\Gamma_{D},\text{ and }\|h\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C~,

where
C
depends on
d_{\text{\rm min}}
, but is otherwise independent of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
.
For any function
\chi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
\chi=1
on an open neighborhood of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, we now have

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}&=&\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\chi h)\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&C||\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}||\chi||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}~.\end{array}

Here we have used the fact that
\chi_{\varepsilon}-\chi h
vanishes on
\Gamma_{D}\cup\omega_{\varepsilon}
, together with the variational formulation
Eq.

3.2
,
to pass from the first line to the second. We immediately conclude that

||\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq C||\chi||^{2}_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}~.

Since this holds for any function
\chi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
which equals identically
1
on an open neighborhood of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
the desired upper bound for
||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
follows by taking the infimum over all such functions
\chi\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

and using the formula
Eq.

2.13
for the capacity, as well as the Poincaré inequality
Eq.

3.5
.
∎

The second result in this section is concerned with solutions to a slight generalization of
Eq.

3.1
, where the prescribed Dirichlet data on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is given by a function
g
(and not constantly equal to
1
) and the conductivity
1
is replaced by
\gamma
. More precisely, we now consider the unique solution
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to the boundary value problem:

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon}=g&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
v_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\end{array}\right.

where
g
is a given function in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
. By a solution to
Eq.

3.6
we understand a function
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)

such that
v_{\varepsilon}=g
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
and

(3.7)

\forall v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)\text{ with }v=0\text{ on }\omega_{\varepsilon},\quad\int_{\Omega}\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v\>\text{\rm d}x=0~.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
d=2
or
d=3
. Let
\omega_{\varepsilon}
be an open Lipschitz subset of the region
\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial\Omega
,
satisfying
Eq.

1.6
, and let
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be the solution to
Eq.

3.6
.
There exists a constant
M
which depends only on
\alpha
,
\beta
, the ellipticity constants of
\gamma
,
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}
,
but is otherwise independent of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, such that

(3.8)

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq M||g||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}~.

In addition,
v_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following improved
L^{2}
estimate

(3.9)

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq M||g||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{4}}~.

Proof.

We first prove
Eq.

3.8
.
Notice that, due to (a modified version of) the Poincaré inequality, it suffices to show that the term
||\nabla v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}
satisfies the desired upper bound. To this end we introduce the solution
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to
Eq.

3.1
.
Since
v_{\varepsilon}-g\chi_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{D}
, the variational formulation
Eq.

3.7
yields

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||\nabla v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}&\leq&C\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&C\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(g\chi_{\varepsilon})\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&C\|g\chi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\[8.61108pt]
&\leq&C\|g\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\|\chi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~.\end{array}

Using the upper bound for
\|\chi_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
supplied by
Lemma

3.1
, and the Poincaré inequality for
v_{\varepsilon}
we conclude that

||\nabla v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq M\|g\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}~,

which is the desired estimate
Eq.

3.8
.

We proceed to prove
Eq.

3.9
.
To this end we rely on a variant of the “classical” Aubin-Nitsche trick
[
12
,
54
,
21
]
.
Let
w_{\varepsilon}
denote the unique solution in
H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
to the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon})=v_{\varepsilon}&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
w_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial w_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~,\end{array}\right.

or rather, in its variational form:

w_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega),\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v\>\text{\rm d}x}=\int_{\Omega}{v_{\varepsilon}v\>\text{\rm d}x}~~\hbox{ for all }v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)~.

Since
Eq.

1.6
holds, there exists a cut-off function
\eta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with the property

\eta=1\text{ on a neighborhood of all the }\omega_{\varepsilon}\text{ and }\eta=0\text{ on an open set }{U}\text{ of }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ with }\Gamma_{D}\Subset{U}~.

The key ingredient of the following derivation is that
w_{\varepsilon}
shows improved regularity with respect to
v_{\varepsilon}
(away from the interface between
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
).
In particular, standard interior elliptic regularity results, discussed e.g. in
[
14
,
34
]
, give

||\eta w_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~.

In addition, since
d=2
or
3
, the classical Sobolev Embedding Theorem ensures that

H^{3}(\Omega)\subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})~~\text{ and for all }v\in H^{3}(\Omega),\>\>\|v\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\|v\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}~,

see e.g.
[
2
]
.
It follows immediately from this and the previous regularity estimate that

(3.10)

\|\eta w_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C||\eta w_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~.

We now calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{\Omega}{v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}&=&\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[7.5pt]
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla(\chi_{\varepsilon}\eta w_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x},\end{array}

where we have introduced the solution
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to
Eq.

3.1
, as well as the fixed cut-off function
\eta
from above. We have also used that
w_{\varepsilon}-\chi_{\varepsilon}\eta w_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{D}
and the variational formulation
Eq.

3.7
.
It now follows that

(3.11)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}&\leq&C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}||\eta w_{\varepsilon}||_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\\
&\leq&C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~,\end{array}

where we have employed
Eq.

3.10
for the last inequality.
Finally, using the estimate

||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq M\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}

from
Lemma

3.1
, together with the already established
H^{1}
estimate
Eq.

3.8
for
v_{\varepsilon}
, it follows from
Eq.

3.11
that

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C||g||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}^{2}\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{2}}~,

as desired.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

We observe that the conclusions of
Lemma

3.2
, and their proofs, extend verbatim
to the case where the scalar conductivity
\gamma
is replaced by a smooth conductivity matrix
A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})^{d\times d}
satisfying the bounds

(3.12)

\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad\alpha|\xi|^{2}\leq A(x)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\beta|\xi|^{2},\quad x\in\overline{\Omega}.

More precisely, the
H^{1}
and
L^{2}
estimates
Eqs.

3.8
and
3.9
still hold true when
v_{\varepsilon}
is the solution to the following anisotropic counterpart of
Eq.

3.6

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon}=g&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
v_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\cdot n=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~.\end{array}\right.

3.2.
The representation formula

The deviation
r_{\varepsilon}:=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
between the perturbed potential and the background potential is the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution
to

(3.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
r_{\varepsilon}=-u_{0}&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
r_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~.\end{array}\right.

Because of our separation assumption
Eq.

1.6
, there exists a smooth compact subset
K\Subset\Gamma_{N}
such that

\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset K
for all
\varepsilon
.
Owing to local elliptic regularity estimates for the background problem
Eq.

1.3
, we have

\|u_{0}\|_{C^{1}(K)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}~,

for a sufficiently large integer
m
(again, see e.g.
[
14
,
34
]
).
Hence, we may construct a
C^{1}
function
g_{0}
on all of
\overline{\Omega}
with the properties that

g_{0}=-u_{0}\text{ on }K~~,\text{ and }\|g_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{C^{1}(K)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}~.

With this notation,
r_{\varepsilon}
is the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
r_{\varepsilon}=g_{0}&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
r_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~.\end{array}\right.

As a straightforward consequence of
Lemma

3.2
, it follows that

(3.14)

||r_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C||f||_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{1/2}~,

and we now search for the next term in the asymptotic expansion of
u_{\varepsilon}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
d=2
or
d=3
, and suppose
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of non-empty, open Lipschitz subsets of
\partial\Omega
, which are all contained in
\Gamma_{N}
and well-separated from
\Gamma_{D}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds. Let
u_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution to
Eq.

1.4
.
Assume that the capacity
\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Then there exists a subsequence, still labeled by
\varepsilon
, and a non-trivial distribution
\mu
in the dual space of
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, such that
for any fixed point
x\in\Omega
, and any
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
with
\eta=1
on
\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})>d_{\text{\rm min}}/2\}
and

\eta=0
on
\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}
, it holds

(3.15)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)-\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\mu_{y}\left[\eta(y)\gamma(y)u_{0}(y)N(x,y)\right]+\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~,~~\hbox{ as }\varepsilon\to 0~.

The term
\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))
goes to zero faster than
\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
uniformly for
x\in K
, where
K
is any compact subset of
\Omega
. The distribution
\mu
depends only on the subsequence
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
\Omega
, and
\Gamma_{N}
.

Proof.

Introducing the fundamental solution
N(x,y)
of the background operator defined in
Section

2.3
, we obtain for any
x\in\Omega

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}r_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&\int_{\Omega}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)(-\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y)))\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\gamma(y)\nabla r_{\varepsilon}(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}y}-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)r_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&=&\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)r_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.\end{array}

Since
y\mapsto\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)
vanishes on
\Gamma_{N}
(i.e. as an element in
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{N})
) and
r_{\varepsilon}
vanishes on
\Gamma_{D}
(i.e.,
r_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{N})
), the second integral in the above right-hand side equals
0
, and so

(3.16)

r_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

To proceed, we now use the same “compensated compactness”, or “clever integration by parts” technique as in
[
15
]
, see also
[
50
]
.
Let
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
be an arbitrary function, which vanishes on the set
\{y\in\partial\Omega~:~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}
. Since
\Omega
is smooth, it is easy to construct a function
\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(3.17)

\psi=\phi\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\text{ and }||\psi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C||\phi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~,

where the constant
C
depends only on
\Omega
.
As before, let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution to
Eq.

3.1
. Since the function
(\phi-\chi_{\varepsilon}\psi)
belongs to
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and vanishes on
\Gamma_{D}\cup\omega_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\psi\>\text{\rm d}s}~.

An integration by parts now yields

(3.18)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}&=&\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\chi_{\varepsilon}\psi)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\psi\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}+\int_{\Omega}{\chi_{\varepsilon}\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi\>\text{\rm d}y}~.\end{array}

Using
Eq.

3.14
and the estimate
Eq.

3.9
applied to
\chi_{\varepsilon}
, we may control the second term in the above right-hand as follows

(3.19)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}{\chi_{\varepsilon}\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi\>\text{\rm d}y}\right\lvert&\leq&C||\chi_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}||r_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}||\psi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\\
&\leq&C\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}}\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.\end{array}

A similar argument makes it possible to rewrite the first term in the right-hand side of
Eq.

3.18
as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\psi\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\nabla(\gamma\psi r_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}-\int_{\Omega}{r_{\varepsilon}\nabla(\gamma\psi)\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\nabla(\gamma\psi r_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}+{\mathcal{O}}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

Inserting these two facts into
Eq.

3.18
we get

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=\int_{\Omega}{\nabla(\gamma\psi r_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}\>+\>{\mathcal{O}}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~,

and so, after another integration by parts

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\gamma\phi r_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}\>+\>{\mathcal{O}}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

Since
\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0
on
\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
,
and since
r_{\varepsilon}=-u_{0}\chi_{\varepsilon}
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{D}
, we may replace
r_{\varepsilon}
with
-u_{0}\chi_{\varepsilon}
in the integral of the above right-hand side, thus obtaining

(3.20)

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}u_{0}\gamma\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}\>+\>{\mathcal{O}}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

Now let
\eta
be a function as introduced in the statement of this theorem:

\eta\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),\>\>\eta=1\text{ on }\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})>d_{\text{\rm min}}/2\}\text{ and }\eta=0\text{ on }\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}.

Then,
N(x,\cdot)\eta(\cdot)
is a
C^{\infty}
function on
\partial\Omega
which vanishes on the set
\{y\in\partial\Omega~:~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{D})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}
and coincides with
N(x,\cdot)
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{D}
.
By a combination of
Eq.

3.16
and
Eq.

3.20
, with
\phi(\cdot)=N(x,\cdot)\eta(\cdot)
, it follows that

(3.21)

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)N(x,y)\eta(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)\chi_{\varepsilon}(y)u_{0}(y)\gamma(y)N(x,y)\eta(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+{\mathcal{O}}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|N(x,\cdot)\eta(\cdot)\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

Finally, the upper bound in
Lemma

3.1
reveals that
\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})>0
(since the
\omega_{\varepsilon}
are non-empty), and that for any function
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)

\left\lvert\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}\right\lvert=\left\lvert\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi)\>\text{\rm d}y}\right\lvert\leq C||\phi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

It follows from the Banach-Alaoglu theorem that, up to extraction of a subsequence, which we still label by
\varepsilon
,
there exists a bounded linear functional
\mu
on
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
such that, for any
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
:

\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu(\phi)~.

The lower bound in
Lemma

3.1
, in combination with Poincaré’s inequality, reveals that
\mu(1)>0
, in other words that
\mu
is non-trivial.
A combination of
Eq.

3.21
and the above convergence result (with
\phi(\cdot)=u_{0}(\cdot)\gamma(\cdot)N(x,\cdot)\eta(\cdot)
) yields the desired representation formula

r_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\mu_{y}\left[\eta(y)u_{0}(y)\gamma(y)N(x,y)\right]+\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~.

The uniformity of the convergence of the remainder term, when the point
x
is confined to a fixed compact subset
K\Subset\Omega
, follows from the fact that the set of functions
\{u_{0}(\cdot)\gamma(\cdot)N(x,\cdot)\eta(\cdot)\}_{x\in K}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
is compact in the
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
topology.
∎

3.3.
Properties of the limiting distribution
\mu

The limiting distribution
\mu
introduced in
Theorem

3.1

is a priori a distribution of order one on
\partial\Omega
, and as such it may depend on first-order derivatives of the argument function
\phi
.
We now show that this is not the case, and that
\mu
is actually a non negative Radon measure on
\partial\Omega
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The distribution
\mu
in
Eq.

3.15
is a non-trivial, non negative Radon measure on
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover, the support of
\mu
is contained in any compact subset
K\subset\partial\Omega

such that
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset K
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough.

Proof.

We recall from the proof of
Theorem

3.1
that the distribution
\mu
is defined by:

\forall\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega),\>\>\mu(\phi)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}~,

where the limit is taken along a subsequence, and
\chi_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is the solution to the equation
Eq.

3.1
. Let
\phi
be an arbitrary function
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
. Since
\partial\Omega
is smooth, it is easy to construct a function
\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

such that

(3.22)

\widetilde{\psi}=\phi\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\text{ and }||\widetilde{\psi}||_{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}=||\phi||_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}~.

Green’s formula then yields

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon})\widetilde{\psi}\>\text{\rm d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\psi})\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}~.

As in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
(see
Eq.

3.19
), the estimates of
Lemma

3.2
show that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\psi})\chi_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}=0~,

and as a consequence

(3.23)

\mu(\phi)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon})\widetilde{\psi}\>\text{\rm d}x}~,

for any function
\phi\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, where
\widetilde{\psi}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
is related to
\phi
by
Eq.

3.22
. On the other hand, using
Lemma

3.1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(3.24)

\forall\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega}),\>\>\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon})\psi\>\text{\rm d}x\right\lvert\leq C||\psi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}.

Hence, using again the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a subsequence of the
\varepsilon
’s and a non negative Radon measure
\nu
on
\overline{\Omega}
such that

\forall\psi\in{\mathcal{C}^{0}}(\overline{\Omega}),\>\>\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi_{\varepsilon})\psi\>\text{\rm d}x}\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}{\psi\>\text{\rm d}\nu}~.

Combining this with
Eq.

3.23
we conclude that:

\mu(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}{\widetilde{\psi}\>\text{\rm d}\nu}

for any
\phi\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, where
\widetilde{\psi}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
is related to
\phi
by
Eq.

3.22
. Moreover,

\left|\int_{\Omega}{\widetilde{\psi}\>\text{\rm d}\nu}\right|\leq C\|\widetilde{\psi}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}=C||\phi||_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)},

and we have thus proved that, for any
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)

|\mu(\phi)|\leq C||\phi||_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}~.

This shows that
\mu
is a Radon measure on
\partial\Omega
, the non negativity of which follows from that of
\nu
.
Moreover, the proof of
Theorem

3.1
has already revealed that
\mu
is non trivial since
\mu(1)>0
.

Finally, let
K\Subset\partial\Omega
be a compact subset of
\partial\Omega
such that
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset K
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. Let

\phi\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
be an arbitrary function with support in the relatively open subset
U:=\partial\Omega\setminus K
.
Then,
\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi
belongs to
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and vanishes on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\Gamma_{D}
, so that

\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=0~.

It follows that

\mu(\phi)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\chi_{\varepsilon}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}=0~.

Since this holds true for any
\phi\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
with support in
U
, the desired result about the support of
\mu
follows.
∎

Proposition

3.1
immediately leads to the following Corollary to
Theorem

3.1
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose
d=2
or
d=3
. Let
\omega_{\varepsilon}
be a sequence of non-empty, open Lipschitz subsets of
\partial\Omega
, which are all contained in
\Gamma_{N}
and are well-separated from
\Gamma_{D}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds. Let
u_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution to
Eq.

1.4
.
Assume furthermore that the capacity
\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Then there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
\varepsilon
, and a non-trivial, non negative Radon measure
\mu
on
\partial\Omega
, such that for any fixed point
x\in\Omega

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)-\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\int_{\partial\Omega}{u_{0}(y)\gamma(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}\mu(y)}+\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})).

The measure
\mu
depends only on the subsequence
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
\Omega
, and
\Gamma_{N}
.
The support of
\mu
lies inside any compact subset
K\subset\partial\Omega
containing the
\omega_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, and the term
\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))
goes to zero faster than
\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
uniformly (in
x
) on compact subsets of
\Omega
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let us comment about the physical meaning of the representation formula of
Corollary

3.1
.

•

The first order term in this expansion arises as the superposition of the potentials
u_{0}(y)\gamma(y)N(x,y)
created at
x
by point sources (monopoles)
which are distributed on the “limiting location” of the vanishing subsets
\omega_{\varepsilon}
. The negative sign in front of this term indicates that these point sources have been replaced by a “ground” (homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition) when passing from the background physical situation to the perturbed one.

•

Assuming for simplicity that
f
has compact support inside
\Omega
, the fact that the term
\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))
(in
Corollary

3.1
) is uniformly small on compact subsets of
\Omega
leads to the following asymptotic expansion for the compliance (or power consumption) of
\Omega
:

\int_{\Omega}f\,u_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\,u_{0}\>\text{\rm d}x-\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\int_{\Omega}f(x)\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)u_{0}(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}\mu(y)}\>\text{\rm d}x+\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~.

Due to the symmetry of the fundamental solution (see
Section

2.3
), we have

u_{0}(y)=\int_{\Omega}{N(x,y)f(x)\>\text{\rm d}x}~,

and this now implies

\int_{\Omega}f\,u_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\,u_{0}\>\text{\rm d}x-\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon})\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(x)u_{0}^{2}(x)\>\text{\rm d}\mu(x)}+\text{\rm o}(\text{\rm cap}(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~.

In particular, the emergence of a small Dirichlet region within the homogeneous Neumann zone
\Gamma_{N}
always decreases the value of the compliance,
which is consistent with physical intuition,
since it amounts to enlarging the region of the boundary
\partial\Omega
where the voltage potential is grounded.

4.
Replacing Dirichlet conditions by Neumann conditions on a “small set”

We presently turn to the opposite situation of that considered in
Section

3
.
The considered sequence
\omega_{\varepsilon}
of “small”, open Lipschitz subsets of
\partial\Omega
is now included in
\Gamma_{D}
,
and it is well-separated from
\Gamma_{N}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds.
The homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition satisfied by the “background” voltage potential
u_{0}
on
\Gamma_{D}
(see
Eq.

1.3
)
is dropped on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, where it is replaced by a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition: the perturbed voltage potential
u_{\varepsilon}

is then the solution to the equation
Eq.

1.5
.
Like in
Section

3
, without any further assumption on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, we aim to derive a representation formula for
u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Let us start by defining the quantity
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
which will measure the “smallness” of a set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
in the present setting.
When
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an arbitrary finite collection of disjoint Lipschitz hypersurfaces, we introduce:

(4.1)

e(\omega)=\max\limits_{\kappa\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\atop\kappa(x)=\pm 1\text{ for }x\in\overline{\omega}}\Bigg\{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}}{(z^{2}+|\nabla z|^{2})\>\text{\rm d}x},\>\>z\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega})\text{ s.t. }\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\Delta z+z=0&\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega},\\
\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}=\kappa&\text{on }\omega\end{array}\right.\Bigg\}~.

In the above formulation,
n
stands for any smooth unit normal vector field on (each connected component of)
\omega
, and the value of
e(\omega)
does not depend on the choice of the particular direction(s) of
n
, due to the presence of the maximum.
More precisely, when
\omega
has only one connected component,
e(\omega)
is the energy of the unique
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega})
solution
z
to the equation

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\Delta z+z=0&\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}~,\\
\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}=1&\text{on }\omega~,\end{array}\right.

and the choice of an orientation for the normal vector
n
to
\omega
only affects the sign of
z
and not the value of the energy
e(\omega)
.
When
\omega
has several connected components, a direction for
n
can be set independently on each connected component of
\omega
;
the possible choices for
\kappa
in
Eq.

4.1
correspond to all possible configurations of the field
n
, and the quantity
e(\omega)
captures the configuration with maximum energy.

In view of the discussion in
Section

2.4
(see notably
Remark

2.1
), it is very tempting to interpret
e(\omega)
as a sort of “capacity” of the set
\omega
,
which, in a Neumann context, measures the energy of the potential in an “equilibrium” situation where the current passing through
\omega
is constant, with amplitude equal to
1
.

Remark 4.1
.

In spite of its intuitive physical interpretation, the quantity
e(\omega)
is not very explicit,
since it involves the solution of a boundary value problem posed on the whole ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
For this reason, we derive in
Appendix

A
several interesting surrogate quantities, depending only on the geometry of
\omega
,
which in some particular cases are equivalent to
e(\omega)
.

In
Section

6
, we shall conduct explicit calculations of the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to
Eq.

1.5
, in the particular case where the inclusion set
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a “surfacic ball” on
\partial\Omega
.
The following estimates for the “smallness” of the planar disk
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
defined in
Eq.

2.14
, which follow straighforwardly from
Appendix

A
(see in particular
Remark

A.1
), will be used repeatedly:

(4.3)

e({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})\leq C_{2}\varepsilon^{2}\text{ if }d=2,\text{ and }e({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})\leq C_{3}\varepsilon^{3}\text{ if }d=3,

for some universal constants
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
.

4.1.
Preliminary estimates

We start with a preliminary result, which is analogous to
Lemma

3.1
,
and is essential for the derivation of our asymptotic representation formula. Let
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
be the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\Delta\zeta_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
\zeta_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\\
\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=1&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~.\end{array}\right.

The following lemma relates the energy of
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
with the quantity
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
defined in
Eq.

4.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\omega_{\varepsilon}
be an open Lipschitz subset of the region
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega
,
which lies “far” from
\Gamma_{N}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds,
and let
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to
Eq.

4.4
.
There exist two constants
0<m\leq M
, which depend only on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and the lower bound
d_{\text{\rm min}}
on the distance from
\omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Gamma_{N}
, but are otherwise independent of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, such that

m\>e(\omega_{\varepsilon})\leq||\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq M\>e(\omega_{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

We start by looking at the right-hand inequality.

The latter is actually quite natural, since
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
can be seen as arising from the solution
z
to (an equation like)
Eq.

4.2
, for a suitable function
\kappa
, by “adding Dirichlet boundary conditions”. An adapted version of the Poincaré inequality for functions with vanishing trace on the set

\left\{x\in\Gamma_{D},\>\>\text{\rm dist}(x,\Gamma_{N})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\right\}

reveals that there exists a constant
C>0
which only depends on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{N}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}
such that

(4.5)

||\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}~.

Let
z_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to
Eq.

4.2
,
where
n
is the unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
pointing outward from
\Omega
(in particular, it is normal to
\omega_{\varepsilon}
)
and
\kappa
constantly equals
1
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
.
An integration by parts, using the boundary conditions satisfied by
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
and
z_{\varepsilon}
, yields

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}&=&\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[7.5pt]
&=&\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}\\[7.5pt]
&=&\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial z_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}\\[7.5pt]
&=&\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial z_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(\eta\zeta_{\varepsilon})\>\text{\rm d}s}~,\\
\end{array}

where
\eta
is a smooth function such that

\eta\equiv 1\text{ on }\left\{x\in\Gamma_{D},\>\>\text{\rm dist}(x,\Gamma_{N})>d_{\text{\rm min}}/2\right\}\text{ and }\eta\equiv 0\text{ on }\left\{x\in\partial\Omega,\>\>\text{\rm dist}(x,\Gamma_{N})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\right\}~.

It follows that

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}&=&\int_{\Omega}{\Big(\nabla z_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(\eta\zeta_{\varepsilon})+z_{\varepsilon}\eta\zeta_{\varepsilon}\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x}\\
&\leq&C||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}||\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq&C||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}_{\varepsilon})}||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}~,\end{array}

where we have used the Poincaré inequality
Eq.

4.5
.
The desired inequality now follows from the definition
Eq.

4.1
of
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
and repeated use of the Poincaré inequality
Eq.

4.5
.

Let us now turn to the left-hand inequality.

To this end, let
z_{\varepsilon}
be the
H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}_{\varepsilon})
solution to (an equation like)
Eq.

4.2
,
where
\kappa
is any
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
function taking values
1
or
-1
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, and
n
again is chosen to be the unit normal to
\partial\Omega
, pointing outward
\Omega
.
The variational formulation associated to (an equation like)
Eq.

4.2
and an integration by parts immediately imply that

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}&=&\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}{\Big(z_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\nabla z_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla z_{\varepsilon}\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x}\\
&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\kappa(z_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}^{-})\>\text{\rm d}s}~.\\
\end{array}

Here we have denoted by
z_{\varepsilon}^{+}
and
z_{\varepsilon}^{-}
the one-sided traces of
z_{\varepsilon}
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
from the exterior and the interior of
\Omega
, respectively (see
Eq.

2.5
).
We obtain

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}&\leq&\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}|z_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}^{-}|\>\text{\rm d}s}\\
&=&\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}|z_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}^{-}|\>\text{\rm d}s}~,\\
\end{array}

where we have used the fact that
z_{\varepsilon}
is continuous across
\partial\Omega
(in the sense of traces) except on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\Omega
is smooth, there exists a bounded linear extension operator
E:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\forall u\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),\>\>||Eu||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C||u||_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\text{ and }Eu=u\text{ on }\partial\Omega~,

for a constant
C
which depends only on
\Omega
.
Based on the previous estimate we calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}&\leq&\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}|Ez_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}^{-}|\>\text{\rm d}s}\\
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla|Ez_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}|\>\text{\rm d}x}\\
&\leq&C||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}||Ez_{\varepsilon}^{+}-z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq&C||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}\left(||z_{\varepsilon}^{+}||_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}+||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)\\
&\leq&C||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega}_{\varepsilon})}~,\end{array}

which finally results in the desired inequality

||z_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}})}\leq C||\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}~.

Since this holds for any choice of the function
\kappa\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
having values
1
or
-1
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
the desired inequality follows by taking the maximum with respect to any such choice.
∎

We now consider the
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution
v_{\varepsilon}
to the boundary value problem

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
v_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=g&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~,\end{array}\right.

where
g
is a given
{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
function.
Our next result provides norm bounds for
v_{\varepsilon}
in terms of the expression
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
d=2
or
d=3
. Let
\omega_{\varepsilon}
be an open Lipschitz subset of the region
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega
, which lies “far” from
\Gamma_{N}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds. There exists a constant
M
, which depends only on
\alpha
,
\beta
, the coercivity constants of
\gamma
,
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{N}
and the lower bound
d_{\text{\rm min}}
on the distance from
\omega_{\varepsilon}
to
\Gamma_{N}
, but is otherwise independent of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, such that
the function
v_{\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

4.6
satisfies the following
H^{1}
estimate

(4.7)

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Me(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}\>||g||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}~.

In addition, the following “improved”
L^{2}
estimate holds

(4.8)

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Me(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{4}}\>||g||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}~.

The quantity
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
is that defined in
Eq.

4.1
.

Proof.

We start by proving
Eq.

4.7
.
Since
\omega_{\varepsilon}
lies inside
\Gamma_{D}
with
\text{\rm dist}(\omega_{\varepsilon},\Gamma_{N})>d_{\text{\rm min}}>0
,
a variant of the Poincaré’s inequality for functions whose trace vanishes on the fixed region
\{x\in\Gamma_{D},\>\>\text{\rm dist}(x,\Gamma_{N})<d_{\text{\rm min}}\}
yields the existence of a constant
C>0
,
depending only on
\partial\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}
, such that

(4.9)

||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C||\nabla v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}~.

We then calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}||\nabla v_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}&\leq&C\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}\\[7.5pt]
&=&C\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}\\[7.5pt]
&=&C\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{gv_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}~.\end{array}

An application of
Eq.

4.9
and introduction of the function
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
– defined in
Eq.

4.4
and estimated in
Lemma

4.1
– now yields

\displaystyle||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C||g||_{{\mathcal{C}^{0}}(\overline{\Omega})}\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|\>\text{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C||g||_{{\mathcal{C}^{0}}(\overline{\Omega})}\int_{\partial\Omega}|v_{\varepsilon}|\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\>\text{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C||g||_{{\mathcal{C}^{0}}(\overline{\Omega})}\int_{\Omega}\nabla|v_{\varepsilon}|\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C||g||_{{\mathcal{C}^{0}}(\overline{\Omega})}\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}~,

and the desired estimate
Eq.

4.7
follows.

Let us now consider the improved
L^{2}
estimate
Eq.

4.8
.

To establish this, we proceed along the lines of the proof of
Lemma

3.2
. As in that proof, let
w_{\varepsilon}
denote the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon})=v_{\varepsilon}&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
w_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial w_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~.\end{array}\right.

Taking advantage of the separation assumption
Eq.

1.6
, we may introduce a cut-off function
\eta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with the property

\eta=1\text{ on a fixed neighborhood of all the }\omega_{\varepsilon}\text{ and }\eta=0\text{ on an open set }{U}\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ with }\Gamma_{N}\Subset{U}~.

The function
w_{\varepsilon}
shows improved regularity with respect to
v_{\varepsilon}
, away from the interface
\Sigma
between the Dirichlet and Neumann regions
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
.
More precisely, arguing as in the proof of
Lemma

3.2
(see in particular
Eq.

3.10
), one obtains that

(4.10)

\|\eta w_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C||\eta w_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~.

We now calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{\Omega}{v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}&=&-\int_{\Omega}\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon})\,v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x\\
&=&\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x}-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\gamma\frac{\partial w_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s\\
&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial n}(\eta w_{\varepsilon})v_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}~.\\
\end{array}

Using the regularity estimate
Eq.

4.10
for
\eta w_{\varepsilon}
and introducing the function
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
,
– defined in
Eq.

4.4
, and estimated in
Lemma

4.1
– we are now led to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{\partial(\eta w_{\varepsilon})}{\partial n}\right\lvert\right\lvert_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}|v_{\varepsilon}|\>\text{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{\partial(\eta w_{\varepsilon})}{\partial n}\right\lvert\right\lvert_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}\int_{\partial\Omega}|v_{\varepsilon}|\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\>\text{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{\partial(\eta w_{\varepsilon})}{\partial n}\right\lvert\right\lvert_{C^{0}(\partial\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}\nabla|v_{\varepsilon}|\cdot\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C||v_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{1}{2}}~.

In combination with the already established estimate
Eq.

4.7
, this yields

\int_{\Omega}{v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}\leq\,C\,e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{3}{2}}||g||^{2}_{{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}~,

exactly as asserted in
Eq.

4.8
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

As in
Section

3
(see
Remark

3.1
) close
inspection of the above proof reveals that both estimates
Eqs.

4.7
and
4.8
still hold true when the function
v_{\varepsilon}
from
Eq.

4.6
is replaced by the solution to the following anisotropic boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}},\\
(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\cdot n=g&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon},\\
(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\cdot n=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
A\in{\mathcal{C}}(\overline{\Omega})^{d\times d}
is a smooth conductivity matrix satisfying the bounds
Eq.

3.12
.

4.2.
The representation formula

One of our main results in this section is the following representation theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
d=2
or
d=3
and that
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of non-empty, open Lipschitz subsets of
\partial\Omega
, which are all contained in
\Gamma_{D}
and well-separated from
\Gamma_{N}
in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds. Let
u_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution to
Eq.

1.5
. Assume that the quantity
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
, given by
Eq.

4.1
, goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Then there exists a subsequence, still labeled by
\varepsilon
, and a non-trivial distribution
\mu
in the dual space of
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
such that
for any fixed point
x\in\Omega
, and any
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
with
\eta=1
on
\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{N})>d_{\text{\rm min}}/2\}
and

\eta=0
on
\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{N})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}

(4.11)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)+e(\omega_{\varepsilon})\>\mu_{y}\left(\eta(y)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\right)+\text{\rm o}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~.

The term
\text{\rm o}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon}))
goes to zero faster than
e(\omega_{\varepsilon})
, uniformly for
x
in any fixed compact subset
K
of
\Omega
. The distribution
\mu
depends only on the subsequence
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
\Omega
, and
\Gamma_{D}
.

Proof.

The proof parallels that of
Theorem

3.1
with appropriate changes. We give a fairly detailed outline of it, except in a few places where we refer back to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.
Let
r_{\varepsilon}
denote the remainder
r_{\varepsilon}:=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}
, which is now the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution to the following problem

(4.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
r_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=-\gamma\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~.\\
\end{array}\right.

Let
x
be a fixed point inside
\Omega
. From the definition
Eq.

2.11
of the fundamental solution
N(x,y)
to the background equation, we obtain after integration by parts

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}r_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&\int_{\Omega}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)(-\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y)))\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{\Omega}{\gamma(y)\nabla r_{\varepsilon}(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~.\\
\end{array}

Another integration by parts of the second term in the above right-hand side reveals that the latter actually vanishes, so in conclusion

(4.13)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}r_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\eta(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.\end{array}

Following the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, we now proceed to calculate, for any given function
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
vanishing on
\{y\in\partial\Omega,~\text{\rm dist}(y,\Gamma_{N})<d_{\text{\rm min}}/3\}
, the limit of the quantity

-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

For this purpose we introduce an extension
\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
of
\phi
satisfying the properties (see
Eq.

3.17
)

\psi=\phi\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\text{ and }||\psi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C||\phi||_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~,

and we consider the unique
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
to the boundary value problem
Eq.

4.4
.
We calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}&=&-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}r_{\varepsilon}\psi\>\text{\rm d}s}\\
&=&-\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla r_{\varepsilon})\psi\>\text{\rm d}y}-\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi)r_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&-\int_{\Omega}{(\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}\cdot\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon})\frac{\psi}{\gamma}\>\text{\rm d}y}\>+\>{\mathcal{O}}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~,\end{array}

where the last identity follows from the improved
L^{2}
estimate (applied to
r_{\varepsilon}
) and the
H^{1}
estimate (applied to
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
) from
Lemma

4.2
;
see the proof of
Theorem

3.1
for details. A repeated use of the same estimates (with the roles of
r_{\varepsilon}
and
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
interchanged) followed by an integration by parts yields

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}&=&-\int_{\Omega}{\gamma\nabla r_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\psi\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\gamma}\right)\>\text{\rm d}x}+{\mathcal{O}}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=&-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{\partial r_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\phi\zeta_{\varepsilon}\>\text{\rm d}s}+{\mathcal{O}}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)};\end{array}

see the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. Using the boundary conditions satisfied by
r_{\varepsilon}
and
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
we finally end up with

(4.14)

-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\left(\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}+{\mathcal{O}}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{5}{4}})\|f\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{C^{1}(\partial\Omega)}~.

From
Lemma

4.1
, we infer that the sequence
\frac{1}{e(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\left(\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\right)

has bounded norm in the dual space of
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
; see more precisely
Eq.

3.24
in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. From the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, it now follows, after extraction of a subsequence (still labeled by
\varepsilon
), that there exists a bounded linear functional
\mu
on
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
such that

(4.15)

\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial\Omega),\>\>\int_{\partial\Omega}{\frac{1}{e(\omega_{\varepsilon})}\left(\frac{\partial\zeta_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi\>\text{\rm d}s}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu(\varphi)~.

Also, due to
Lemma

4.1
, it follows that
\mu(1)>0
, thus revealing that
\mu
is non trivial.
Insertion of
\phi(y)=\eta(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)
into
Eq.

4.14
and application of
Eq.

4.15
with
\varphi(y)=\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(y)\eta(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)
now gives

-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\varepsilon}(y)\eta(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}=e(\omega_{\varepsilon})\mu_{y}\left[\eta(y)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(y)\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)\right]+\text{\rm o}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon}))~,

which in combination with
Eq.

4.13
leads to the desired representation formula
Eq.

4.11
. The uniformity of the convergence of the remainder
\text{\rm o}(e(\omega_{\varepsilon}))
, when
x
is confined to a fixed compact subset of
\Omega
, follows as in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Just as in
Section

3
we may show that the distribution
\mu
is a non negative Radon measure compactly supported “near” the sets
\omega_{\varepsilon}
; in other words, the following analogue of
Proposition

3.1
holds in the present context, whose nearly identical proof is left to the reader.

Proposition 4.1
.

The limiting distribution
\mu
introduced in
Theorem

4.1
is a non negative Radon measure on
\partial\Omega
.
Moreover, the support of
\mu
is contained in any compact subset
K
of
\partial\Omega
such that
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset K
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

This proposition immediately leads to the following corollary to
Theorem

4.1
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Suppose
d=2
or
d=3
and suppose
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of non-empty, open Lipschitz subsets of
\partial\Omega
, which are all contained in
\Gamma_{D}
and are well-separated from
\Gamma_{N}
, in the sense that
Eq.

1.6
holds; let
u_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution to
Eq.

1.5
.
Assume that the quantity

, defined by

.
Then there exists a subsequence, still labeled by

containing the

Remark 4.3

From the physical viewpoint, the second term in the representation formula of

5.1.

This section deals with the case

.

Proof.

We proceed in four steps, relying on several intermediate, technical results, whose proofs are postponed to the end of the section

Step 1.
We establish a representation formula for

Considering a fixed point

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}u_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&-\int_{\Omega}{\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y))u_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\

Using the background problem

Note that

Next, we introduce the mapped potentials

Proof of

Introducing the symmetric, positive definite matrix

M^{-2}=A
, we may write the latter requirement as follows

\forall\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{H}),\>\>\psi(x)=\int_{H}(M^{-1}\nabla_{y}L_{A}(x,y))\cdot(M^{-1}\nabla\psi(y))\>\text{\rm d}y~.

Changing variables and using test functions
\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{H})
of the form
\psi(y)=\widetilde{\psi}(My)
,
\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(M\overline{H})
, we arrive at

\forall x\in MH,\>\>\forall\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(M\overline{H}),\>\>\widetilde{\psi}(x)=\int_{MH}{|\det M^{-1}|\>\nabla_{z}(L_{A}(M^{-1}x,M^{-1}z))\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\psi}(z)\>\text{\rm d}z}~.

Therefore, it suffices that the function
(x,y)\mapsto\frac{1}{|\det M|}L_{A}(M^{-1}x,M^{-1}y)
be a Neumann function for the Laplacian on the rotated half-space
MH
. Such a function can easily be constructed by reflection – more precisely

(5.24)

\frac{1}{|\det M|}L_{A}(M^{-1}x,M^{-1}y)=G(x,y)+G(x,s_{M}(y)),

where

s_{M}(y):=y-2\left(y\cdot\frac{M^{-1}e_{2}}{|M^{-1}e_{2}|}\right)\frac{M^{-1}e_{2}}{|M^{-1}e_{2}|}

is the symmetric image of a point
y\in MH
with respect to the hyperplane
\partial(MH)
(whose unit normal vector equals
\frac{M^{-1}e_{2}}{|M^{-1}e_{2}|}
). The desired expression
Eq.

5.10
for
L_{A}(x,y)
follows immediately.

∎

We next turn to the proof of
Lemma

5.2
concerning the remainder
\eta_{\varepsilon}

Proof of
Lemma

5.2
.

The definition of
\eta_{\varepsilon}
as the right-hand side of
Eq.

5.15
features four terms, which we denote by

We prove that each of these converges to

First, using the smoothness of

(5.25)

Secondly, the term

is an integral over the set
\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus{U}

{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}

and

(

3.1

2.15

For the very same reason, the term

I_{\varepsilon}^{4}(x):=-\int_{\Gamma_{D}}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(\varepsilon x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

5.1

5.1

3.1

Changing variables in the definition of this semi-norm to rescale the above left-hand side, we now get

We conclude from

3.2
(

3.1

We next turn to the proof of the approximation

.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Let
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a smooth bounded domain,
whose boundary
\partial D
is a closed curve containing
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
as a subset.
Since
\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
is the space of distributions in
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
whose extension by
0
to
\partial D

belongs to

is the space of restrictions to

of elements from

(see

2.1
), it is enough to prove that the asymptotic formula in the statement of
Lemma

5.3
holds when all the operators at play are seen as operators from
H^{-1/2}(\partial D)
into
H^{1/2}(\partial D)

To this end, let us first simplify the expression
Eq.

5.10
for the function
L_{A(\varepsilon x)}(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon y)
featured in the definition
Eq.

5.14
of the operator
T_{\varepsilon}

\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\>x\neq y,\quad L_{A(\varepsilon x)}(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon y)=\frac{-1}{\pi\sqrt{|\det A(\varepsilon x)|}}\log\left\lvert M(\varepsilon x)(\varepsilon x-\varepsilon y)\right\lvert~.

The matrix field
A
is given by
Eq.

5.6
, and its definition readily implies that

tends to
\gamma(0)\text{\rm I}
in

of

+\left(\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{|\det A(\varepsilon x)|}}-\frac{1}{\pi\gamma(0)}\right)(|\log\varepsilon|+\alpha)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\varphi\>\text{\rm d}s}+T_{K_{\varepsilon}}\varphi~,

where

is the integral operator with kernel
K_{\varepsilon}(x,x-y)
, and

The first two terms in the right-hand side of

as an operator from

into

.

.

\sup\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k\atop|\beta|\leq k}\sup\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sup\limits_{|z|=1}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K_{\varepsilon}(x,z)\right\lvert\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0~,

\sup\limits_{\varphi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\atop||\varphi||_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}\leq 1}||T_{K_{\varepsilon}}\varphi||_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0~,

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

Proof of (i).
Let
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a smooth bounded domain,
whose boundary
\partial D
is a closed curve containing
{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}
as a subset.
We also introduce another bounded Lipschitz domain
V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that
D\Subset V
, and a smooth cut-off function
\chi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})

such that
\chi\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of
\overline{D}
and
\chi\equiv 0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{V}
.

The proof follows an idea in
[
62
,
63
]
; it relies on the connection between
S_{1}
and the single layer potential

{\mathcal{S}}_{D}:H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\to H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
associated with
D
, as defined in
Eq.

2.4
. More precisely

\forall\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}),\>\>S_{1}\varphi=({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi)\lvert_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}~,

where the density
\varphi
in the right hand side is extended by
0
outside
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
. We first show that
S_{1}:\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})\to H^{1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})

is a Fredholm operator with index
0
by adapting the argument of the proof of Th. 7.6 in
[
49
]
.
The classical mapping properties of the single layer potential
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
imply that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
for any density
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
, the associated potential
u={\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi\in H^{1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
satisfies

||\chi u||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq C||\varphi||_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}~.

Conversely, we infer from the jump relations
Eq.

2.6
of the single layer potential that

(5.31)

||\varphi||_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}=\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{\partial(\chi u)^{+}}{\partial n}-\frac{\partial(\chi u)^{-}}{\partial n}\right\lvert\right\lvert_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}\leq C||\chi u||_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}~.

Now, using again
Eq.

2.6
together with integration by parts, we obtain that, for
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
,

\displaystyle\langle S_{1}\varphi,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\partial D}{{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi\>\varphi\>\text{\rm d}s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}{\chi u\left(\frac{\partial(\chi u)^{+}}{\partial n}-\frac{\partial(\chi u)^{-}}{\partial n}\right)\>\text{\rm d}s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{|\nabla(\chi u)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}}{\Delta(\chi u)\chi u\>\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{|\nabla(\chi u)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}}{\Big(\Delta\chi u+2\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla u\Big)\chi u\>\text{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{|\nabla(\chi u)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}}{\Big(\chi(\Delta\chi)u^{2}+2u\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla(\chi u)-2u^{2}|\nabla\chi|^{2}\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x}~,

which we rewrite as

(5.32)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{|\nabla(\chi u)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}=-\langle S_{1}\varphi,\varphi\rangle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}}{\Big(\chi(\Delta\chi)u^{2}+2u\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla(\chi u)-2u^{2}|\nabla\chi|^{2}\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x}~.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (and rearrangement) now implies the existence of a constant
C
such that

||\nabla(\chi u)||_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})^{2}}^{2}\leq C\Big(||S_{1}\varphi||_{H^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}+||u||_{L^{2}(V)}^{2}\Big)~.

A combination with
Eq.

5.31
, and insertion of
u={\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi
, yields the existence of a constant
C
such that, for arbitrary
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}_{1}})
(extended by
0
outside
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
)

(5.33)

||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}=||\varphi||_{H^{-1/2}(\partial D)}\leq C\Big(||S_{1}\varphi||_{H^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}+||{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi||_{L^{2}(V)}\Big)~.

Since the mapping
H^{-1/2}(\partial D)\ni\varphi\mapsto{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi\in H^{1}(V)
is continuous
and the injection
H^{1}(V)\to L^{2}(V)
is compact, an application of Peetre’s
Lemma

B.1
to
Eq.

5.33
reveals that
S_{1}
has finite dimensional kernel
\text{\rm Ker}(S_{1})\subset\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
,
and closed range
\text{\rm Ran}(S_{1})\subset H^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
. Finally, since
S_{1}
is self-adjoint, it follows that

\text{\rm Ker}(S_{1})=\text{\rm Ran}(S_{1})^{\perp},\text{ and so }\text{\rm Ran}(S_{1})=\text{\rm Ker}(S_{1})^{\perp}~.

In summary
S_{1}
is a Fredholm operator with index
0
.

In order to prove that
S_{1}
is invertible, it thus suffices to prove that it is injective on
\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
.
To this end, let
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
be such that
S_{1}\varphi=0
on
{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}
. We assume first that
\varphi
has mean
0
, that is
\langle\varphi,1\rangle=\int_{\partial D}{\varphi\>\text{\rm d}s}=0
.
Then, the associated single layer potential
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the decay property

(5.34)

|{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi(x)|={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\text{ as }|x|\to\infty;

see
Eq.

2.9
.
From the same integration by parts which led to
Eq.

5.32
(and which can now be carried out without introducing a cut-off function
\chi
because of the decay property
Eq.

5.34
),
we obtain

(5.35)

\langle S_{1}\varphi,\varphi\rangle=0=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{|\nabla({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x},

and so
{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi=0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
As a result,

\varphi=\frac{\partial({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi)^{+}}{\partial n}-\frac{\partial({\mathcal{S}}_{D}\varphi)^{-}}{\partial n}=0\text{ on }\partial D,

as desired. Finally, let us consider the general case where
S_{1}\varphi=0
but
\langle\varphi,1\rangle
does not necessarily vanish.
From
Proposition

C.1
, the function
\varphi_{c}\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
defined by:

\varphi_{c}(x_{1})=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}

\langle\varphi_{c},1\rangle=1,\text{ and }S_{1}\varphi_{c}=\frac{\log 2}{2\pi}\text{ on }{\mathbb{D}}_{1}.

Hence, the element
\varphi_{0}\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})

\varphi_{0}=\varphi-\langle\varphi,1\rangle\varphi_{c},

\langle\varphi_{0},1\rangle=0\text{ and }S_{1}\varphi_{0}=-\langle\varphi,1\rangle S_{1}\varphi_{c}=-\frac{\log 2}{2\pi}\>\langle\varphi,1\rangle,

5.35
reveals that

.
Eventually, since

, we obtain

Proof of (ii).
Both formulas

5.19
and

for a.e.

Proof of

.

As in the proof of

5.2
, we denote the four terms in the right-hand side of

,

The exact same arguments as in the two-dimensional case show that

,

and we focus on the treatment of the last term

From the explicit expression for the function

5.1

Hence, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and a switch to polar coordinates, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}|I^{3}_{\varepsilon}(x)|&\leq&C\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\frac{1}{|\varepsilon x-y|}|\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(y)|\>\text{\rm d}y}\\

Invoking

3.2

3.1

and in particular,

||I^{3}_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\to 0~.

Let us now consider the

To this end, we return to the formula

I_{\varepsilon}^{3}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)=-s_{\varepsilon}(x)-\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus{U}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{\Gamma_{D}}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
+\int_{\widehat{\Gamma_{N}}}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)},\quad x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}~.

This identity, and the mapping properties of the integral operator with kernel

readily imply that

\left\lvert I^{3}_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\lvert_{H^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}\leq C||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\mathcal{O})}~.

After a change of variables in the semi-norm

\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}|I^{3}_{\varepsilon}|_{H^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\leq C||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(\mathcal{O})}~,

and since

∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.6
.

Let
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a smooth bounded domain whose boundary contains
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.
The kernel

of the operator

reads, for

,

see
Eq.

5.10
.
Let us now recall from

5.6
that the matrix field

tends to
\gamma(0)\text{\rm I}
in
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(V)
for any integer

and any open, relatively compact neighborhood

of

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
T_{\varepsilon}
may be decomposed as

T_{\varepsilon}\varphi=\frac{2}{\varepsilon\gamma(0)}S_{1}\varphi+T_{K_{\varepsilon}}\varphi~,

where

According to

Here we have, again, “cut off”

Sketch of proof of
Lemma

5.7
.

The proof is very similar to that of
Lemma

5.4
, and we only point out the differences. Repeating mutatis mutandis the argument presented in the two-dimensional case,
one sees that the operator
S_{1}
is still Fredholm with index
0
, and so, it suffices to prove that it is injective.
To achieve this, let
\phi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
be a density such that

, and let

be the associated potential.
Because of the decay properties at infinity

2.8
of the single layer potential in three space dimensions (which hold even if

), an integration by parts similar to that which led to

5.32
, reveals that

Hence

is constant on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Since
|u(x)|\to 0
as

, it follows that

vanishes identically, and so does
\phi=-\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}^{+}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}^{-}\right)

∎

6.
An explicit asymptotic formula for the case of substituting Neumann conditions

This section exemplifies the general physical setting of
Section

4
:
we consider a smooth, bounded domain
\Omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
whose boundary is made of two disjoint, open Lipschitz subregions
\Gamma_{D}
,
\Gamma_{N}
:
\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma_{D}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{N}}
.
\Sigma
denotes the interface between
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
.
The geometric setting is exactly as in
Section

5
, only with the roles of
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
interchanged. The vanishing subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Gamma_{D}
is of the same nature as in
Eq.

5.1
:
it is the image of the planar disk
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
with radius
\varepsilon
around
0
by the smooth diffeomorphism
T
that maps the domain
{\mathcal{O}}
(whose boundary is flat in a fixed neighborhood
U
of
0
) onto
\Omega
. We also denote
\widehat{\Gamma_{D}}=T^{-1}(\Gamma_{D})
and we assume for convenience that
T
coincides with the identity mapping far from
0
, so that
T^{-1}(\Gamma_{N})=\Gamma_{N}
.
The background potential and the perturbed potential,
u_{0}
and
u_{\varepsilon}
, respectively, are the solutions to the equations

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{0})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{0}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\end{array}\right.\text{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}},\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\cup\omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

Our aim is to derive a precise first order asymptotic expansion of
u_{\varepsilon}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
.
In order to emphasize the similarity of this study with that conducted in
Section

5
, we use the same notation whenever possible. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
d=2
or
3
and let
x\in\overline{\Omega}
,
x\notin\Sigma\cup\left\{0\right\}
. One has the asymptotic expansion

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)+a_{d}\varepsilon^{d}\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,0)+\text{\rm o}(\varepsilon^{d})~,

where the constant
a_{d}
is given by

a_{d}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{\pi}{2}&\text{if }d=2,\\
\frac{1}{3}&\text{if }d=3.\end{array}\right.

Sketch of the proof.

As in the proof of
Eq.

5.3
, we proceed in four steps, introducing the difference
r_{\varepsilon}:=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Step 1.
We construct a representation formula for
u_{\varepsilon}
which only involves the values of
r_{\varepsilon}
inside
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, and the fundamental solution
N(x,y)
to the background equation in
Eq.

6.1
.

To this end, let
x\in\Omega
be arbitrary; using the definition of
N(x,y)
and integrating by parts twice, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}u_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&-\int_{\Omega}{\text{\rm div}_{y}(\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y))u_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&-\int_{\partial\Omega}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)u_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{\Omega}{\gamma(y)\nabla_{y}N(x,y)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)u_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{\Omega}{f(y)N(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~,\end{array}

where the last line follows from the facts that

\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n}(x,y)=\gamma(y)\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}(y)=0\text{ for }y\in\Gamma_{N},\>\>\,N(x,y)=0\text{ for }y\in\Gamma_{D}\text{ and }u_{\varepsilon}(y)=0\text{ for }y\in\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~.

Using that
u_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}=r_{\varepsilon}
on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
in the previous equation, we get for
x\in\Omega

(6.2)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)-\int_{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\gamma(y)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,y)r_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

The above identity also holds for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
,
x\notin\Sigma\cup\left\{0\right\}
provided
\varepsilon
is small enough, since all the quantities involved are smooth in a neighborhood of such points.

Next, we introduce the transformed potentials
v_{0}:=u_{0}\circ T
and
v_{\varepsilon}:=u_{\varepsilon}\circ T
on the domain
{\mathcal{O}}
. A change of variables in the variational formulations for
Eq.

6.1
reveals that
v_{0}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
are the unique
H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
solutions to the equations

(6.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(A\nabla v_{0})=g&\text{in }{\mathcal{O}},\\
v_{0}=0&\text{on }\widehat{\Gamma_{D}},\\
(A\nabla v_{0})\cdot n=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\end{array}\right.\text{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})=g&\text{in }{\mathcal{O}},\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\widehat{\Gamma_{D}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}},\\
(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\cdot n=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\cup{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon},\end{array}\right.

where
g\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
are the smooth function and the matrix field defined by

(6.4)

g=|\det(\nabla T(y))|f(T(y)),\text{ and }A(y)=|\det(\nabla T(y))|\gamma(T(y))\nabla T(y)^{-1}(\nabla T(y)^{t})^{-1}~.

Changing variables in the integral featured in
Eq.

6.2
and rescaling, we arrive at

(6.5)

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)-\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\gamma(T(\varepsilon y))\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,T(\varepsilon y))\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

where we have introduced the function
s_{\varepsilon}:=r_{\varepsilon}\circ T
, and the quantity
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
defined by

(6.6)

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)=\varepsilon^{d-1}s_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)~.

This is the desired representation formula.

Step 2.
We characterize
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
as the solution to an integral equation.
This arises from a representation formula for
u_{\varepsilon}
which differs slightly from
Eq.

6.5
: it is obtained by repeating the derivation of Step 1,
except that a different, explicit fundamental solution
L_{A(x)}(x,y)
is used in place of
N(x,y)
. For any symmetric, positive definite matrix
A
, and any
x\in H
, let
L_{A}(x,y)
be a solution to the following boundary value problem posed on the lower half-space
H

(6.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}_{y}(A\nabla_{y}L_{A}(x,y))=\delta_{y=x}&\text{in }H,\\
L_{A}(x,y)=0&\text{on }\partial H.\end{array}\right.

An explicit formula for one such function is provided by the next lemma, whose proof is completely analogous to that of
Lemma

5.1
and is therefore omitted.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A
be a symmetric, positive definite
d\times d
matrix, and let
M:=A^{-1/2}
.
Let
G(x,y)
be the fundamental solution of the operator
-\Delta
in free space, cf.
Eq.

2.3
.
The function
L_{A}(x,y)
defined by

(6.8)

L_{A}(x,y)=|\det M|\left(G(Mx,My)-G(Mx,My-2y_{d}\frac{M^{-1}e_{d}}{|M^{-1}e_{d}|^{2}}\right),\quad\>\>x\neq y~,

satisfies
Eq.

6.7
.

For a point
x\in{\mathcal{O}}
, we obtain from two successive integrations by parts

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}v_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&-\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\text{\rm div}_{y}(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y))v_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&=&-\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)v_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
&&+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}}{(A\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{g(y)L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~.\end{array}

The same calculation based on the function
v_{0}
, instead of
v_{\varepsilon}
, yields

v_{0}(x)=-\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)v_{0}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla v_{0}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}}{(A\nabla v_{0}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{g(y)L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~.

Forming the difference between these identities, and using the boundary conditions for

and
v_{\varepsilon}
we obtain

(6.9)

s_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\int_{\Gamma_{N}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\\
+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus U}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}-\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}.

We now wish to take the trace of a co-normal derivative of the above identity on
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
. This is possible owing to the next lemma, whose proof is postponed to the end of this section.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let us define the operator
M_{\varepsilon}:\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})\to H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})

M_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

There exists a constant

and the domain

such that, for all

||M_{\varepsilon}\varphi||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}+||\text{\rm div}_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{x}(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi))||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

Using this lemma we obtain the following identity between elements of
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})
:

(6.10)

\displaystyle-A(x)\nabla v_{0}(x)\cdot n(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma_{N}}{A(x)\nabla_{x}\left(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)\right)\cdot n(x)\>s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

\displaystyle\quad+A(x)\nabla\left(\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}\right)\cdot n(x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>A(x)\nabla_{x}(L_{A(x)}(x,y))\cdot n(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt-\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\Big(A(x)\nabla_{x}\left(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)\right)\cdot n(x)\Big)\>s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

We rewrite the latter as

(6.11)

-A(x)\nabla v_{0}(x)\cdot n(x)=R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(x)+A(x)\nabla K_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot n(x)+R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(x)-\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{P(x,y)\>s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

where we have defined the following quantities on
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}

(6.12)

R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(x):=-\int_{\Gamma_{N}}{A(x)\nabla_{x}\left(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)\right)\cdot n(x)\>s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

K_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~,

R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(x):=\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus{U}}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>A(x)\nabla_{x}(L_{A(x)}(x,y))\cdot n(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

and the kernel

(6.13)

P(x,y):=A(x)\nabla_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y))\cdot n(x)~,\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}~.

Rescaling
Eq.

6.11
, we finally arrive at the following integral equation on
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}

(6.14)

T_{\varepsilon}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)+\eta_{\varepsilon},

where the unknown
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
is the quantity introduced in
Eq.

6.6
,
the operator
T_{\varepsilon}:\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})\to H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
is defined by

(6.15)

T_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\varphi(z)\>P(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon z)\>\text{\rm d}s(z)}~,

and the remainder
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
is given by

(6.16)

\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)=\Big((A\nabla v_{0}\cdot n)(\varepsilon x)-\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)\Big)+R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(\varepsilon x)+R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\varepsilon x)+(A\nabla K_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(\varepsilon x)~.

Step 3.
We study the integral equation
Eq.

6.14
to obtain information about the limiting behavior of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
To this end, we estimate the remainder
\eta_{\varepsilon}
and we approximate the operator
T_{\varepsilon}
; this is possible due to the following lemmata, whose proofs are detailed at the end of this section.

Lemma 6.3
.

The remainder term
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
defined in
Eq.

6.16
satisfies

(6.17)

\eta_{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\text{ weakly in }H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})~.

Lemma 6.4
.

The operator
T_{\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

6.15
satisfies the following expansion

(6.18)

\sup\limits_{\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})\atop||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\leq 1}\>\varepsilon^{d}\left\lvert\left\lvert T_{\varepsilon}\varphi-\frac{2\gamma(0)}{\varepsilon^{d}}R_{1}\varphi\right\lvert\right\lvert_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0~,

where the hypersingular operator
R_{1}:\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})\to H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
is defined by

(6.19)

R_{1}\varphi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}c>{\displaystyle}l}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}&\text{if }d=2~,\\
\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}&\text{if }d=3~,\end{array}\right.

and the above integrals are understood as finite parts; see
Section

2.2
.

Inserting the approximation
Eq.

6.18
in the integral equation
Eq.

6.14
,
the function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
satisfies:

(6.20)

R_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}+R_{\varepsilon}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{2}\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)+\varepsilon^{d}\widetilde{\eta_{\varepsilon}}~,

for some sequence
\widetilde{\eta_{\varepsilon}}\in H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
which converges weakly to
0
, and some operators
R_{\varepsilon}:\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})\to H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
, which converge to zero in the operator norm.
This integral equation can now be solved owing to the next lemma, whose proof is also postponed to the end of this section.

Lemma 6.5
.

The operator
R_{1}:\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})\to H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
defined in

6.19
is invertible.

Using this result together with Neumann series to invert the integral equation

C>0
such that

(6.21)

||\varphi_{\varepsilon}||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\leq C\varepsilon^{d},

as well as the following asymptotic expansion

(6.22)

where the explicit expression for the constant

is given by
Proposition

C.1
(ii), (iv).

Step 4.
We pass to the limit in the representation formula

for

.
Arguing as in the proof of

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}u_{\varepsilon}(x)&=&u_{0}(x)-\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)\>\text{\rm d}s(z)}\right)\gamma(0)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,0)+\text{\rm o}(\varepsilon^{d})~,\\
&=&u_{0}(x)-\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{2}\langle R_{1}^{-1}1,1\rangle\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)\frac{\partial N}{\partial n_{y}}(x,0)+\text{\rm o}(\varepsilon^{d})~,\end{array}

where the second line follows from

6.22
.
The explicit expressions for the constant

in 2d and 3d provided in

Proof of
Lemma

6.2
.

The intuition behind the technical argument below is the following: if
M_{\varepsilon}\varphi
was the double layer potential associated with the operator
u\mapsto\text{\rm div}_{y}(A(y)\nabla_{y}u)
(see
Section

2.2
), the quantity
\text{\rm div}(A\nabla(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi))
would vanish exactly on
{\mathcal{O}}
.
Unfortunately, this is not the case since
(x,y)\mapsto L_{A(x)}(x,y)
is not the fundamental solution of this operator. However,
the following calculations show that
M_{\varepsilon}\varphi
is “not too far” from this double layer potential, so that
the terms of highest-order derivatives vanish in the expression of
\text{\rm div}(A\nabla(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi))
,
and the lower-order terms can be controlled.

Before starting, let us introduce some notations. For the sake of clarity, we denote by

L(A,x,y):=L_{A}(x,y)
the function defined in
Lemma

6.1
. The corresponding partial derivatives with respect to the entries
a_{jk}
(
j,k=1,\ldots d
) of the matrix
A
, and with respect to the components
x_{i}
,
y_{i}
of
x
and
y
(
i,=1,\ldots,d
) are denoted by
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{jk}}
,
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}
,
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{i}}
. Throughout the proof,
r(\varphi)\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})
stands for a remainder term, which may vary from one line to the other, but which consistently satisfies the following estimate

||r(\varphi)||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{e})}~.

At first, using the expression for
L(A(x),x,y)
given in
Lemma

6.1
, we calculate

(6.23)

A(x)\nabla_{y}L(A(x),x,y)\cdot n(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{-1}{\pi\sqrt{\det A(x)}}\frac{(y-x)\cdot n(y)}{|M(x)(y-x)|^{2}}&\text{if }d=2,\\
\frac{-1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det A(x)}}\frac{(y-x)\cdot n(y)}{|M(x)(y-x)|^{3}}&\text{if }d=3,\end{array}\right.\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\>\>y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon},\>\>x\neq y~.

Recalling from
Section

2.1
the definition of the space
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})
, and notably the fact that the associated norm is
||u||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}=||u||_{{H}^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})}
,
Theorem

D.2
then implies that
M_{\varepsilon}\varphi\in H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
and that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

||M_{\varepsilon}\varphi||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

We now proceed to prove the estimate

(6.24)

||\text{\rm div}_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{x}(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi))||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

For an arbitrary point
x\in{\mathcal{O}}
, the definition of
M_{\varepsilon}\varphi
boils down to:

M_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{j}}(A(x),x,y)n_{i}(y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

Since
n_{i}(y)=0
on
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
for
i=1,\ldots,d-1
, and since the matrix field
A(x)
is smooth and the function
y\mapsto L(A(x),x,y)
satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial H
,
the above expression actually simplifies into

M_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x)=a_{dd}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

Taking derivatives, we now get, for
x\in{\mathcal{O}}
, and
i=1,\ldots,d
,

(6.25)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi)(x)&=&\frac{\partial a_{dd}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&&\qquad+a_{dd}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)=:V_{i}(\varphi)(x)+W_{i}(\varphi)(x)~,\end{array}

with obvious notations. We infer from this expression that

(6.26)

\text{\rm div}_{x}(A(x)\nabla_{x}(M_{\varepsilon}\varphi))=\text{\rm div}(A(x)V(\varphi))+\text{\rm div}(A(x)W(\varphi)).

Each function
V_{i}(\varphi)
is the multiple of a smooth function with a potential associated to the kernel
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)
,
y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
.
A simple calculation, similar to
Eq.

6.23
, reveals that the latter is (the restriction of) a homogeneous kernel of class
0
in the sense of
Definition

D.1
. It then follows from
Theorem

D.2
that

||V(\varphi)||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}}\leq C||\varphi||_{H^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})}=C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~,

and so

(6.27)

||\text{\rm div}(A(x)V(\varphi))||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

We then focus on the second term
\text{\rm div}(A(x)W(\varphi))
in
Eq.

6.26
. A straightforward calculation yields, for
x\in{\mathcal{O}}
,

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\text{\rm div}(A(x)W(\varphi))(x)&=&\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(a_{ij}a_{dd})(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)}\\
&&+\>\>a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}{a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)}\\
&=&r(\varphi)+a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}{a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)}~,\end{array}

where we have used a similar argument to that used in the treatment of the functions
V_{i}(\varphi)
to pass from the first line to the second.
Using the chain rule to proceed, we obtain

(6.28)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\text{\rm div}(A(x)W(\varphi))&=&a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\Bigg(\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&&+\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{j}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\Bigg)+r(\varphi)\\
&=&a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}a_{kl}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&&+a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)\\
&&+a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{3}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial x_{j}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
&&+a_{dd}(x)\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{3}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+r(\varphi),\\[15.00002pt]
&=:&a_{dd}(x)\Big(Z_{1}(\varphi)+Z_{2}(\varphi)+Z_{3}(\varphi)+Z_{4}(\varphi)\Big)+r(\varphi)~,\end{array}

with obvious notations for
Z_{m}
,
m=1,~\ldots~,4.

We now remark that the function
Z_{1}(\varphi)
is a linear combination of integral operators with smooth coefficients; the kernels of these operators are
\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial y_{d}}
,
y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
, and they are (restrictions of) homogeneous kernels of class
0
in the sense of
Definition

D.1
. Here, we use the fact that taking derivatives with respect to one of the matrix entries
a_{kl}
changes neither the order, the homogeneity, nor the parity of the function involved. It then follows from
Theorem

D.2
that

(6.29)

||Z_{1}(\varphi)||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

By the same token, we obtain

(6.30)

||Z_{2}(\varphi)||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

In order to estimate
Z_{3}(\varphi)
, we rewrite this quantity as

Z_{3}(\varphi)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\right)\\
-\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\frac{\partial a_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}}{\frac{\partial^{3}L}{\partial a_{kl}\partial a_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

and arguing as above, we obtain

(6.31)

||Z_{3}(\varphi)||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon})}~.

This leaves us with the task of estimating
Z_{4}(\varphi)
.
To accomplish this, we rewrite the equation
Eq.

6.7
satisfied by
L(A,x,y)
as

\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(A,x,y)=0,\quad x,y\in\overline{H},\>\>x\neq y~;

note that this holds for an arbitrary, symmetric, positive definite matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, with entries
a_{ij}
. Due to the symmetry property

\forall x,y\in\overline{H},\>x\neq y,\quad L(A,x,y)=L(A,y,x)~,

it follows that

\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(A,x,y)=0,\quad x,y\in\overline{H},\>\>x\neq y~.

Substituting
A(x)=\left\{a_{ij}(x)\right\}
for
A
and taking a derivative with respect to the
y_{d}
variable, we get

\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{3}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial y_{d}}(A(x),x,y)=0~.

It follows that

(6.32)

Z_{4}(\varphi)=0~.

Combining the estimates
Eqs.

6.29
,
6.30
,
6.31
and
6.32
with
Eqs.

6.26
,
6.27
and
6.28
we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

We proceed with the proof of
Lemma

6.3
.

Sketch of the proof of
Lemma

6.3
.

Let us denote by
I_{\varepsilon}^{i}(x)
,
i=1,\ldots,4

6.16

0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

At first, since
v_{0}
is smooth and
(A\nabla v_{0}\cdot n)(0)=\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)

the difference

I_{\varepsilon}^{1}(x)=\Big((A\nabla v_{0}\cdot n)(\varepsilon x)-\gamma(0)\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}(0)\Big)

is easily seen to converge to

strongly in
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})

R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(x)=-\int_{\Gamma_{N}}{A(x)\nabla_{x}\left(A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)\right)\cdot n(x)\>s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}

the convergence properties of
s_{\varepsilon}
expressed in
Lemma

4.2
(
Remark

4.2
) and
Eq.

4.3
imply that
R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(x)
converges to
0
uniformly for

in a fixed neighborhood of the sets
{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
;
in particular,
I_{\varepsilon}^{2}(x)=R_{\varepsilon}^{1}(\varepsilon x)
converges to

strongly in

.
The same argument shows that
I_{\varepsilon}^{3}(x)=R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\varepsilon x)
also converges to

strongly in
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
.

This leaves us with the task of proving that

converges to

weakly in
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
.
Let us introduce a smooth bounded domain

, whose boundary contains
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
. Furthermore, select

so that

is bounded away from
\Gamma_{N}
and
\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus U
.
Recalling the definition of
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
as the space of restrictions to
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
of distributions in

(see
Section

2.1
),
it suffices to show that the vector-valued function

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x):=(A\nabla K_{\varepsilon})(\varepsilon x),\quad x\in D~,

converges to
0
weakly in the Hilbert space

H_{\text{\rm div}}(D):=\left\{\sigma\in L^{2}(D)^{d},\>\>\text{\rm div}\sigma\in L^{2}(D)\right\}~.

We proceed in two steps to achieve this.

Step 1.
We prove that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is a bounded sequence in
H_{\text{\rm div}}(D)
.
To this end, we return to
Eq.

6.9
, which, for
x\in D\subset{\mathcal{O}}
, reads

(6.33)

s_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\int_{\Gamma_{N}}{A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\
+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus U}{(A\nabla s_{\varepsilon}\cdot n)(y)\>L_{A(x)}(x,y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}+K_{\varepsilon}(x)-M_{\varepsilon}s_{\varepsilon}(x),

with

K_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}{(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}~,

and the quantity
M_{\varepsilon}s_{\varepsilon}
is as in
Lemma

6.2
.
It follows from
Lemma

6.2
that
M_{\varepsilon}s_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following estimate

||M_{\varepsilon}s_{\varepsilon}(x)||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}+||\text{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla(M_{\varepsilon}s_{\varepsilon}))||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})}~.

From
Eq.

6.33
, and the fact that
D
is bounded away from
\Gamma_{N}
and
\partial{\mathcal{O}}\setminus U
, we now see that the function
K_{\varepsilon}(x)
satisfies the similar estimate

||K_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}(D)}+||\text{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla K_{\varepsilon})||_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}~.

Rescaling the above inequality (note that
\varepsilon D\subset D
for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small) and using the estimate

||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{2}}~,

which follows readily from
Lemma

4.2
(
Remark

4.2
) and
Eq.

4.3
, we now obtain

\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{2}}||\sigma_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(D)^{d}}+\varepsilon^{\frac{d-2}{2}}||\text{\rm div}\sigma_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{2}}~.

Hence,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is a bounded sequence in
H_{\text{\rm div}}(D)
, and so, up to a subsequence (which we still index by
\varepsilon
) it converges weakly to a limit
\sigma^{*}
in this space.

Step 2.
We prove that the weak limit
\sigma^{*}
is
0
, and this task requires separating the cases

and
d=3
.

When

, we observe that, by definition,

(6.34)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)=A(\varepsilon x)\nabla\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(x),\text{ where }\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}K_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon x)~,

and the same calculation as in the proof of
Lemma

5.2
(see notably

5.29
) reveals that the quantity
K_{\varepsilon}(x)
satisfies

|K_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq C||s_{\varepsilon}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})},\text{ for all }x\in D~.

Hence, we obtain

\int_{D}{|K_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon z)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}z}\leq C\varepsilon^{3},

which proves that

||\widetilde{K_{\varepsilon}}||_{L^{2}(D)}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0~.

It follows from
Eq.

6.34
and the continuity of derivatives in the sense of distributions that
\sigma^{*}=0
.

The case where
d=2
is a little more involved, and we need to estimate the quantity
K_{\varepsilon}
more carefully.
The argument performed for
d=3
in this case only allows us to infer that
\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}
is a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(D)
;
we also know from Step 1 that its gradient is bounded in
L^{2}(D)^{2}
, and so (up to a subsequence)

\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}
converges strongly to a function
K^{*}\in L^{2}(D)
, which we need to analyze further.
For any point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and positive real number
h>0
, we denote by
B(x,h)
the open ball with radius
h
centered at
x
.

We observe that, for
x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
,

|K_{\varepsilon}(x)-K_{\varepsilon}(y)|=\frac{1}{\pi}\left\lvert\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left((A(x)-A(z))\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(x))}}\frac{M^{2}(x)(x-z)}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-(A(y)-A(z))\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(y))}}\frac{M^{2}(y)(y-z)}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)\right)\>\text{\rm d}z\right\lvert~.

Denoting by
h:=|x-y|
we get, since
B(y,h)\subset B(x,2h)\subset B(y,4h)
,

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}|K_{\varepsilon}(x)-K_{\varepsilon}(y)|&\leq&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{B(x,2h)\cap{\mathcal{O}}}\left\lvert(A(x)-A(z))\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(x))}}\frac{M^{2}(x)(x-z)}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)\right\lvert\>\text{\rm d}z\\
&&+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{B(y,4h)\cap{\mathcal{O}}}\left\lvert(A(y)-A(z))\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(y))}}\frac{M^{2}(y)(y-z)}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)\right\lvert\>\text{\rm d}z\\
&&+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\setminus B(x,2h)}\left\lvert\left((A(x)-A(z))\nabla_{z}L_{A(x)}(x,z)-(A(y)-A(z))\nabla_{z}L_{A(y)}(y,z)\right)\cdot\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)\right\lvert\>\text{\rm d}z\\
&=:&J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3},\end{array}

with obvious notations.
Due to the smoothness of the matrix field
A

|J_{1}|\leq C\int_{B(x,2h)\cap{\mathcal{O}}}{|\nabla s_{\varepsilon}(z)|\>\text{\rm d}z}\leq Ch||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}}~,

and a similar estimate holds for
J_{2}
. When it comes to
J_{3}
, we remark that for
z\notin B(x,2h)

2h\leq|x-z|,\quad h\leq|y-z|,\text{ and }\frac{1}{2}|x-z|\leq|y-z|\leq\frac{3}{2}|x-z|~.

We now decompose

\left((A(x)-A(z))\nabla_{z}L_{A(x)}(x,z)-(A(y)-A(z))\nabla_{z}L_{A(y)}(y,z)\right)=b_{1}+b_{2}~,

where

b_{1}=(A(x)-A(y))\nabla_{z}L_{A(x)}(x,z)\text{ and }b_{2}=(A(y)-A(z))\left(\nabla_{z}L_{A(x)}(x,z)-\nabla_{z}L_{A(y)}(y,z)\right).

A simple calculation yields that

|b_{1}|\leq\frac{Ch}{|x-z|}~,

and regarding
b_{2}
, we calculate

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}|b_{2}|&\leq&C|z-y|\left\lvert\nabla_{z}L_{A(x)}(x,z)-\nabla_{z}L_{A(y)}(y,z)\right\lvert\\
&=&C|z-x|\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(x))}}\frac{M^{2}(x)(x-z)}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(y))}}\frac{M^{2}(y)(y-z)}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\right\lvert\\
&\leq&C|z-x|\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(x))}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(A(y))}}\right\lvert\left\lvert\frac{M^{2}(x)(x-z)}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}\right\lvert\\
&&+C|z-x|\left\lvert\frac{M^{2}(x)(x-z)}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}-\frac{M^{2}(y)(y-z)}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\right\lvert\\
&\leq&Ch+C\frac{|z-x|}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}\left\lvert M^{2}(x)(x-z)-M^{2}(y)(y-z)\right\lvert\\
&&+C|z-x||M^{2}(y)(y-z)|\left\lvert\frac{1}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\right\lvert\\
&\leq&Ch+C\frac{|z-x|}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}\left\lvert M^{2}(y)(x-y)\right\lvert+C|z-x|^{2}\left\lvert\frac{1}{|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}}\right\lvert\\
&\leq&Ch+\frac{Ch}{|z-x|}+C\frac{1}{|z-x|^{2}}\left\lvert|M(x)(z-x)|^{2}-|M(y)(z-y)|^{2}\right\lvert\\
&\leq&Ch+\frac{Ch}{|z-x|}+C\frac{1}{|z-x|}\left\lvert|M(x)(z-x)|-|M(y)(z-y)|\right\lvert\quad\leq Ch+\frac{Ch}{|z-x|}~.\end{array}

Summarizing, we now have

|J_{3}|\leq Ch\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\setminus B(x,2h)}{\left(\frac{1}{|z-x|}+1\right)|\nabla s_{\varepsilon}|\>\text{\rm d}z}~,

and so

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}|J_{3}|&\leq&Ch\left(\int_{{\mathcal{O}}\setminus B(x,2h)}{\frac{1}{|z-x|^{2}}\>\text{\rm d}z}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}\\
&\leq&Ch\left(\int_{2h}^{M}{\frac{\text{\rm d}r}{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}\\
&\leq&Ch|\log h|^{\frac{1}{2}}||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}~.\end{array}

With
x
and
y
replaced by
\varepsilon x
and
\varepsilon y
, for
x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we now conclude

|K_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon x)-K_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)|\leq C\varepsilon|x-y|\big|\log|\varepsilon x-\varepsilon y|\big|^{1/2}\,||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}~,

and so

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}|\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(x)-\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}&\leq&C|x-y|^{2}(|\log\varepsilon|+\big|\log|x-y|\big|\,)\,||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}\\
&\leq&C\varepsilon^{2}\left(|x-y|^{2}|\log\varepsilon|+|x-y|^{2}\big|\log|x-y|\big|\right)~,\end{array}

where we have used again
Lemma

4.2
(
Remark

4.2
) and
Eq.

4.3
to estimate
||\nabla s_{\varepsilon}||^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{2}}
.
Integrating the terms in the previous inequality and passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain

\int_{D}\int_{D}{|K^{*}(x)-K^{*}(y)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{D}\int_{D}{|\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(x)-\widetilde{K}_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}y}=0~,

which proves that
K^{*}
is a constant function over
D
. This completes the proof of the fact that
\sigma^{*}=0
, for
d=2
.

∎

Proof of
Lemma

6.4
.

We only provide the proof in the two-dimensional case, the three-dimensional proof being very similar.

Using the definition of the fundamental solution
L_{A}(x,y)
given by
Lemma

6.1
, we get, for arbitrary
y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\varepsilon}
and
x\in\overline{H}
,
x\neq y
,

A(x)\nabla_{y}L_{A(x)}(x,y)\cdot n(y)=-\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\det A(x)}}\frac{y_{2}-x_{2}}{|M(x)(y-x)|^{2}}~.

Hence, a straightforward calculation yields the following expression of the kernel
P(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon y)
of the operator
T_{\varepsilon}
, defined in
Eq.

6.13

P(\varepsilon x,\varepsilon y)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\det A(\varepsilon x)}}\frac{A(\varepsilon x)e_{2}\cdot e_{2}}{|M(\varepsilon x)(\varepsilon y-\varepsilon x)|^{2}}=\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{A(\varepsilon x)e_{2}\cdot e_{2}}{\pi\sqrt{\det A(\varepsilon x)}|M(\varepsilon x)e_{1}\cdot e_{1}|^{2}}\frac{1}{|y-x|^{2}}~,\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\>\>x\neq y~,

and this immediately leads to

T_{\varepsilon}\varphi(x)-\frac{2\gamma(0)}{\varepsilon^{2}}R_{1}\varphi(x)=\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\frac{2A(\varepsilon x)e_{2}\cdot e_{2}}{\sqrt{\det A(\varepsilon x)}|M(\varepsilon x)e_{1}\cdot e_{1}|^{2}}-2\gamma(0)\right)R_{1}\varphi(x)~.

Since the matrix fields
A(x)
and
M(x)
are smooth, with values
A(0)=\gamma(0)\text{\rm I}
and
M(0)=\gamma(0)^{-1/2}I
at
x=0
, we have that

\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{2A(\varepsilon x)e_{2}\cdot e_{2}}{\sqrt{\det A(\varepsilon x)}|M(\varepsilon x)e_{1}\cdot e_{1}|^{2}}-2\gamma(0)\right\lvert\right\lvert_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}})}\leq C\varepsilon~;

in order to verify
Lemma

6.4
it thus suffices to show that the operator

R_{1}\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}\varphi(y)\,ds(y)

(interpreted in terms of finite parts) is a bounded operator from
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
into
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
. For this purpose we can, unfortunately, not directly use the results from
Appendix

D
, since the hypersingular kernel of the above operator does not fit within that framework. To remedy this, we rely on a classical trick for hypersingular operators of the form
R_{1}
, using an alternate representation in terms of a homogeneous kernel operator, and a surface differentiation operator (see e.g.
[
39
]
, §1.2). More precisely, we observe that

\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1}}\left(\frac{y_{1}-x_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\right)\text{ for }x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\>\>x\neq y,

due to the fact that
|x-y|=|x_{1}-y_{1}|
when
x,y\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.
It follows that, for an arbitrary density
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
,

R_{1}\varphi=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\frac{y_{1}-x_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{1}}(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~,

where the right hand side represents a Cauchy principal value. The kernel
\frac{y_{1}-x_{1}}{|x-y|^{2}}
fits within the framework of
Appendix

D
, and it gives rise to an operator of class
0
,
i.e.
, a bounded operator from
\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
into
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
. Since the
operator
\varphi\rightarrow\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{1}}
is bounded from
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
to
\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
, we conclude that
R_{1}
is a bounded operator from
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
into
H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
, as needed.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

6.5
.

As in the proof of
Lemma

5.4
we introduce a smooth bounded domain
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
whose boundary contains the set
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
, and a bounded Lipschitz domain
V
with
D\Subset V
.
We first prove that
R_{1}
is a Fredholm operator with index
0
.
To achieve this, let
\varphi
be an arbitrary element in
\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
(extended by
0
to all of
\partial D
) and set
u={\mathcal{D}}_{D}\varphi\in H^{1}_{\text{\rm loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D)
.
Using the jump relations
Eq.

2.7
, and then integrating by parts on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(which is possible because of the decay properties
Eq.

2.10
) we obtain

(6.35)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\langle R_{1}\varphi,\varphi\rangle&=&\int_{\partial D}{\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}(u^{+}-u^{-})\>\text{\rm d}s}\\
&=&-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D}{|\nabla u|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}~.\end{array}

Since

||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}=||(u^{+}-u^{-})||_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}\leq C\Big(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(V)}\Big)~,

it follows from
Eq.

6.35
that

||\varphi||_{\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}\leq C\left(\|R_{1}\varphi\|_{H^{-1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})}+\|{\mathcal{D}}_{D}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(V)}\right)~.

It now follows as in the proof of
Lemma

5.4
that
R_{1}
is Fredholm with index
0
. Hence, we are left to show that
R_{1}
is injective. But if
R_{1}\varphi=0
for some
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}}_{1})
, the previous calculation with
u={\mathcal{D}}_{D}\varphi
yields

\langle R_{1}\varphi,\varphi\rangle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\partial D}{|\nabla u|^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x}=0,

so that
u
is constant on
D
and on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D}
. Since
u\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, the value of this constant on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D}
must be
0
.
Since
\varphi=u^{+}-u^{-}
vanishes on
\partial D\setminus{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}
, the value of this constant inside
D
is also
0
; hence,

and
\phi=u^{+}-u^{-}=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

7.
Conclusion and future Directions

In this article, we have analyzed the asymptotic behavior of the solution to an elliptic partial differential equation posed on a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

when the accompanying boundary conditions change type on a vanishing subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}
of the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
More precisely, in the model context of the conductivity equation complemented with mixed homogeneous Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions on the respective regions
\Gamma_{D},\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial\Omega
,
we have derived a general representation formula
for the asymptotic structure of the potential
u_{\varepsilon}
when the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is replaced with a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on an arbitrary “small” subset
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Gamma_{N}
(and vice-versa, when the homogeneous Dirichlet condition is replaced with a homogeneous Neumann condition on
\omega_{\varepsilon}\subset\Gamma_{D}
).
Furthermore, in the particular situation where
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a vanishing surfacic ball, we have given precise, explicit asymptotic formulas for
u_{\varepsilon}
. The present findings suggest various directions for further investigations.

•

A natural extension of the present work is to investigate the case where the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Gamma_{D}
,
or the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on
\Gamma_{N}
,
is replaced by yet another type of boundary condition on
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, for instance an
inhomogeneous
Neumann boundary condition, or an inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition. A perhaps even more interesting setting involves a Robin boundary condition, and thus consists in investigating the asymptotic behavior of the solution to the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}+ku_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~,\end{array}\right.

or the solution to the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\gamma\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega~,\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega_{\varepsilon}}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{N}~,\\
\gamma\frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial n}+ku_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\omega_{\varepsilon}~.\end{array}\right.

The understanding of this limiting process, uniformly with respect to the parameter
k
of the Robin condition, would provide a key insight into the nature of the transition between the Dirichlet and Neumann behaviors (established in this paper). In this spirit, see for instance
[
17
,
26
,
53
]
concerning
small volume asymptotic formulas, which are uniform with respect to the properties of the material occupying the vanishing inclusions.

•

Beyond the realm of the conductivity equation, the present study could be extended to other, more challenging physical contexts, e.g., the system of linear elasticity – where homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions account for “clamping” and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions represent absence of traction.

•

Last but not least, it would be interesting to explore the practical applications of these results.
As we have illustrated at in
Remark

3.2
, asymptotic formulas for the solution to “small” perturbations of a “background” boundary value problem
allow to appraise the sensitivity of a quantity of interest (or a performance criterion) with respect to such perturbations. This idea plays into the concepts of topological derivative
[
55
]
and “topological ligaments”
[
51
,
24
]
in optimal design.
In our context it would allow us to appraise the sensitivity of a performance criterion with respect to the introduction of a new, “small” region supporting Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition in the physical boundary value problem. Such a program appears
especially interesting in the context of linear elasticity, where it would significantly complement the study in
[
25
]
; see also
[
59
]
.

Appendix A
A closer look to the quantity
e(\omega)

The purpose of this appendix is to analyze more in depth the quantity
e(\omega)
, defined in
Eq.

4.1
, and
used in
Sections

4
and
6
to assess the “smallness” of a subset
\omega
of
\partial\Omega
, when homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are replaced by homogeneous Neumann conditions. More precisely, we construct explicit quantities which bound
e(\omega)
, and which are not excessively conservative – quantities that do not require the solution of any boundary value problems.

A.1.
Some differential geometry facts

We shall need some basic facts from differential geometry on hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. All of these results are well-known, however,
some are not so easily found in the literature, and for the convenience of the reader we include their proofs in this section. We refer to classical books, such as
[
27
,
44
]
, for further details.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain. We first recall some terminology:

•

The tangent plane to
\partial\Omega
at a point
x\in\partial\Omega
is the hyperplane of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which is orthogonal to the unit normal vector
n(x)
.
The orthogonal projection
P_{x}v\in T_{x}\partial\Omega
of a vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
onto
T_{x}\partial\Omega
is given by

P_{x}v:=v-(v\cdot n(x))n(x)~.

•

The length of a piecewise differentiable curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to\partial\Omega
is defined by

\ell(\gamma):=\int_{a}^{b}{|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\>\text{\rm d}t}~,

where the derivative of
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
is calculated as that of an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued function.
This quantity is obviously independent of the particular parametrization chosen for
\gamma
.

•

A differentiable curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to\partial\Omega
is called a (constant speed) geodesic segment joining the endpoints
\gamma(a)
and
\gamma(b)
if it satisfies:

\forall t\in(a,b),\>\>P_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t))=0~.

•

A geodesic segment
\gamma:[a,b]\to\partial\Omega
is called minimizing if
\ell(\gamma)\leq\ell(\widetilde{\gamma})
for any piecewise differentiable curve
\widetilde{\gamma}(t)

joining
\gamma(a)
to
\gamma(b)
.

•

The geodesic distance between two points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
is defined by:

d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)=\inf\limits_{\gamma:[a,b]\to\partial\Omega\atop\gamma(a)=x,\>\gamma(b)=y}{\ell(\gamma)}~.

•

Likewise, the geodesic distance
\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,K)
from a point
x\in\partial\Omega
to a closed subset
K\subset\partial\Omega
is:

\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,K)=\inf\limits_{y\in K}d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)~.

The distance between two points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
can be measured either in terms of the (extrinsic) Euclidean distance
|x-y|
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

or by means of the (intrinsic) geodesic distance
d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)
.
It turns out that these notions are equivalent in the present context, as stated in the next lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

There exists a constant
c>0
which only depends on
\Omega
such that the following inequalities hold:

\forall x,y\in\partial\Omega~,\>\>c\>d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)\leq|x-y|\leq d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)~.

Proof.

The right inequality is obvious, and we focus on the proof of the left one.
To this end, we introduce a finite open covering
\left\{U_{i}\right\}_{i=1,\ldots,N}
of the smooth, compact hypersurface
\partial\Omega
with the following property: for each
i=1,\ldots,N
, there exist a convex open subset
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and a function
f_{i}:V_{i}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which is smooth on an open neighborhood of
\overline{V_{i}}
,
such that (up to a relabeling of coordinates in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) the mapping

\sigma_{i}:V_{i}\ni(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})\mapsto(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},f_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1}))\in U_{i}

realizes a diffeomorphism from
V_{i}
onto
U_{i}
.
We also denote by
\delta>0
a Lebesgue number associated to this covering, that is

\forall\omega\subset\partial\Omega,\>\>\text{\rm diam}(\omega)\leq\delta\>\>\Rightarrow\>\>\omega\subset U_{i}\text{ for some }i=1,\ldots,N~,

where the diameter
\text{diam}(\omega):=\sup_{x,y\in\omega}{|x-y|}
is understood in the sense of the Euclidean distance.

Now considering two given points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
, we distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
|x-y|>\delta
.
By introducing the quantity
M:=\sup\limits_{p,q\in\partial\Omega}d^{\partial\Omega}(p,q)
, we obtain

d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)\leq M\leq\frac{M}{\delta}|x-y|~.

Case 2:
|x-y|\leq\delta
. Then
x
and
y
belong to a common open subset
U_{i}
, and we let
\widehat{x},\widehat{y}
be the points in
V_{i}
such that
x=\sigma_{i}(\widehat{x})
and
y=\sigma_{i}(\widehat{y})
. We also introduce the differentiable curve
\gamma(t)=\sigma_{i}(\widehat{x}+t(\widehat{y}-\widehat{x}))
connecting
x
to
y
. It follows from the very definition of the geodesic distance
d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)
that

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)&\leq&\int_{0}^{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(t)}\>\text{\rm d}t}\\
&=&\int_{0}^{1}{\sqrt{\nabla\sigma_{i}(\widehat{x}+t(\widehat{y}-\widehat{x}))^{t}\nabla\sigma_{i}(\widehat{x}+t(\widehat{y}-\widehat{x}))(\widehat{y}-\widehat{x})\cdot(\widehat{y}-\widehat{x})}\>\text{\rm d}t}~.\end{array}

For
i=1,\ldots,N
and
\widehat{z}\in V_{i}
, we introduce the eigenvalues
1\leq\lambda^{i}_{1}(\widehat{z})\leq\ldots\leq\lambda^{i}_{d-1}(\widehat{z})
of the
(d-1)\times(d-1)
matrix
\nabla\sigma_{i}(\widehat{z})^{t}\nabla\sigma_{i}(\widehat{z})
, and

M:=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,N}\sup_{\widehat{z}\in V_{i}}\lambda_{d-1}^{i}(\widehat{z})<\infty~.

The bound
M
depends only on the properties of the hypersurface
\partial\Omega
.
We now have

d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)\leq\sqrt{M}|\widehat{x}-\widehat{y}|\leq\sqrt{M}|x-y|~,

as desired.
∎

We recall the definition and the main properties of the
exponential map
,
\text{\rm exp}_{x}
, at a point
x\in\partial\Omega
:

•

The mapping
\text{\rm exp}_{x}:U\to\partial\Omega
is defined on an open neighborhood
U
of
0
in the tangent plane
T_{x}\partial\Omega
by the formula:

\forall v\in U,\>\>\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)=\gamma(1,x,v),

where
t\mapsto\gamma(t,x,v)
is the unique geodesic curve on
\partial\Omega
passing through
x
at
t=0
with velocity
v
:

(A.1)

\gamma(0,x,v)=x,\text{ and }\gamma^{\prime}(0,x,v)=v.

•

For any point
x\in\partial\Omega
, there exists a number
r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x)>0
– the injectivity radius of
x
– such that
\text{\rm exp}_{x}
is a diffeomorphism from the
(d-1)
dimensional ball
B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x))\subset T_{x}\partial\Omega
onto the geodesic ball

B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r_{\text{\rm inj}}(p)):=\{y\in\partial\Omega,\>d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)<r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x)\}

on
\partial\Omega
.
In particular,
\text{\rm exp}_{x}:B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x))\subset T_{x}\partial\Omega\to\partial\Omega
is a local chart for
\partial\Omega
around
x
.

•

At an arbitrary point
x\in\partial\Omega
, the following identity holds:

(A.2)

d^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v))=|v|,\quad v\in B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x))\subset T_{x}\partial\Omega.

•

Since
\partial\Omega
is smooth and compact, there exists a number
r_{\text{\rm inj}}>0
– the injectivity radius of
\partial\Omega
– such that for all
x\in\partial\Omega
,

r_{\text{\rm inj}}<r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x)
.

Let us finally state a useful consequence of the change of variables formula, applied to the exponential mapping.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f\in L^{1}_{\text{\rm loc}}(\partial\Omega)
;
then for any point
x\in\partial\Omega
and
r<r_{\text{\rm inj}}(x)
,

\int_{B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r)}{f(y)\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}=\int_{B(0,r)}{f(\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v))g(v)\>\text{\rm d}v}~,

where
B(0,r)
is the ball with center
0
and radius
r
in
T_{x}\partial\Omega
, and
g
is given by

g(v):=\sqrt{\det(M_{ij}(v))},\>\>M_{ij}(v):=\Big(\text{\rm d}\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)(e_{i})\Big)\cdot\Big(\text{\rm d}\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)(e_{j})\Big),\>\>i,j=1\ldots,d-1~,

is bounded uniformly from below and above by positive constants which depend only on the properties of
\partial\Omega
.
The tangent vectors
\text{\rm d}\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)(e_{i})\in T_{\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)}\partial\Omega
featured in these equations are given by

\text{\rm d}\text{\rm exp}_{x}(v)(e_{i})=\left.\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}s}\gamma(1,x,v+se_{i})\right\lvert_{s=0}~,

where
t\mapsto\gamma(t,x,v)
is the unique geodesic passing through
x
at
t=0
with velocity
v
, see
Eq.

A.1
.

A.2.
Derivation of “geometric” upper bounds for the quantity
e(\omega)

Throughout this section
\omega
is an open Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
.
\omega
lies strictly inside
\Gamma_{D}
, and the setting is as in
Section

4
. We start with the following result.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\omega
be an open Lipschitz subset of
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega
, which is well-separated from
\Gamma_{N}
, i.e.
Eq.

1.6
holds.
There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}
such that

e(\omega)\leq C\int_{\omega}\frac{1}{\rho_{\omega}(x)}~\text{\rm d}s(x)

where
\rho_{\omega}(x)
denotes the weight function defined by

\forall x\in\omega,\quad\rho_{\omega}(x):=\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{\omega}}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}}\>\text{\rm d}s(y)}~.

Proof.

Let us introduce the solution
\zeta\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to
Eq.

4.4
;
it follows from a simple adaptation of
Lemma

4.1
and integration by parts that

(A.3)

e(\omega)\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\text{\rm d}x=C\int_{\omega}\zeta~\text{\rm d}s~,

where the constant
C
depends only on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}
.

A slight generalization of the argument leading to the estimate
Eq.

2.2
in
Section

2.1
,
using that
\zeta
vanishes on
\Gamma_{D}\setminus\overline{\omega}
, gives that for some constant
C
, depending on
\Omega
,
\Gamma_{D}
and
d_{\text{\rm min}}

(A.4)

\left(\int_{\omega}|\zeta(x)|^{2}\rho_{\omega}(x)~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}\leq C\|\zeta\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}~.

A combination of
Eq.

A.3
and
Eq.

A.4
now yields

\displaystyle e(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{\omega}\zeta(x)~\text{\rm d}s(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int_{\omega}\rho_{\omega}(x)^{-1}~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\omega}|\zeta(x)|^{2}\rho_{\omega}(x)~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int_{\omega}\rho_{\omega}(x)^{-1}~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}\|\zeta\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int_{\omega}\rho_{\omega}(x)^{-1}~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}\|\zeta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}~.

Adapting the proof of
Lemma

4.1
, we may prove

\|\zeta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Ce(\omega)^{1/2}~,

and after insertion of this into the last line of the previous estimate (and cancellation), we obtain

e(\omega)^{1/2}\leq C\left(\int_{\omega}\rho_{\omega}(x)^{-1}~\text{\rm d}s(x)\right)^{1/2}~,

which is the desired conclusion.
∎

Let us introduce the notation

D(\omega)=\int_{\omega}\frac{1}{\rho_{\omega}(x)}~\text{\rm d}s(x)~.

It follows from
Lemma

A.3
that
D(\omega)
is an upper bound for
e(\omega)
(up to constants involving the chosen domain
\Omega
and the regions
\Gamma_{D}
,
\Gamma_{N}
of its boundary),
which has the appealing feature that it depends solely on the geometry of
\omega
.
We believe there are many interesting examples where
D(\omega)
is equivalent to
e(\omega)
; actually we have provided such an example in
Section

6
. For this reason we also find it useful to derive an equivalent, but simpler, expression for the measure
D(\omega)
. The remainder of this subsection is devoted to this task, and we start with a lemma.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\omega
be an open Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
. There exists a constant
c>0
, which depends only on
\partial\Omega
, such that

c\int_{\omega}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\leq D(\omega)~.

Proof.

The lemma follows immediately, by integration over
\omega
, if we prove that, for all points
x\in\omega

(A.5)

c\,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\leq\rho_{\omega}(x)^{-1}~.

To achieve this goal, we distinguish between two cases, depending on the size of
\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)
relative to the injectivity radius
r_{\text{\rm inj}}
of
\partial\Omega
.

Case 1:
\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\geq r_{\text{\rm inj}}
.
From the definition of
\rho_{\omega}(x)
and
Lemma

A.1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\rho_{\omega}(x)&\leq&C\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\omega}{\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}}\\
&\leq&\frac{C}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)^{d}}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\text{\rm d}s(y)}\\[5.0pt]
&\leq&\frac{C}{r_{\text{\rm inj}}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}\\
&=&\frac{C}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}~,\end{array}

where the constant
C
is changing from one instance to the next, but depends only on
\partial\Omega
, and not on
\omega
.
Hence,
Eq.

A.5
holds in this case.

Case 2:
\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)<r_{\text{\rm inj}}
.
The exponential mapping
\text{\rm exp}_{x}
induces a diffeomorphism from the ball
B(0,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))\subset T_{x}\partial\Omega
onto the geodesic ball
B^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))
. Since
B^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))
lies inside
\omega
, it follows that

(A.6)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\rho_{\omega}(x)&\leq&C\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus B^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}\\
&=&C\left(\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r_{\text{\rm inj}})}\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}+\int_{B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus B^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}\right)~.\end{array}

As in Case 1, the first integral in the above right-hand side is easily estimated by

(A.7)

\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r_{\text{\rm inj}})}\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}\leq\frac{C}{r_{\text{\rm inj}}^{d}}\leq\frac{C}{r_{\text{\rm inj}}^{d-1}}\frac{1}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}=\frac{C}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}~.

As for the second integral, the exponential change of variables of
Lemma

A.2
, followed by a change to polar coordinates yields

(A.8)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{B^{\partial\Omega}(x,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus B^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}&\leq&C\int_{B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus B(0,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}{\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm exp}_{x}(u))^{d}}}\\
&=&C\int_{B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus B(0,\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}{\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{|u|^{d}}}\\[10.00002pt]
&\leq&C\int_{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}^{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}{\frac{t^{d-2}}{t^{d}}\>\text{\rm d}t}\\
&=&C\left(\frac{1}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega))}-\frac{1}{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}\right).\end{array}

A combination of
Eqs.

A.6
,
A.7
and
A.8
leads to

\rho_{\omega}(x)\leq\frac{C}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}~,

which is exactly
Eq.

A.5
, thus completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

The reverse inequality is more subtle, and it holds only under additional assumptions on the set
\omega\subset\partial\Omega
. Let us introduce a few related definitions.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\omega\subset\partial\Omega
be an open Lipschitz subset.
The set
\omega
is called geodesically convex if for any two points
p,q\in\omega
, there exists a unique
minimizing geodesic segment
\gamma:[0,1]\to\partial\Omega
joining
p
to
q
, with
\gamma([0,1])\subset\omega
.

Definition A.2
.

Let
\omega\subset\partial\Omega
be a geodesically convex, open Lipschitz subset.

•

For any
p\in\partial\omega
, the tangent cone
C_{p}\subset T_{p}\partial\Omega
to
\omega
at
p
is defined by

C_{p}:=\left\{v\in T_{p}\partial\Omega,\>\>\text{\rm exp}_{p}\left(t\frac{v}{|v|}\right)\in\omega\text{ for some }0<t<r_{\text{\rm inj}}(p)\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}~.

•

For any
p\in\partial\omega
, an open half-space
H\subset T_{p}\partial\Omega
is called a supporting half-space for
\omega
at
p
if
C_{p}\subset\overline{H}
.

The following result generalizes well-known properties of convex subsets of the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
in terms of supporting hyperplanes, to the setting of geodesically convex subsets of
\partial\Omega
. It is a summary of the contents of Proposition 1.8 and Lemma 1.7 in
[
18
]
;
see
Fig.

3
for an illustration.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\omega\subset\partial\Omega
be a geodesically convex, open Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
, and let
p\in\partial\omega
.
Then, the tangent cone
C_{p}\subset T_{p}\partial\Omega
to
\omega
at
p
satisfies

C_{p}\setminus\left\{0\right\}=\bigcap{H_{j}}~,

where the intersection is taken over all the supporting half-spaces of
\omega
at
p
.

In addition, if there exists
q\in\omega
and a minimal geodesic segment
\gamma:[0,1]\to\partial\Omega
from
q
to
p
such that
\ell(\gamma)=\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(q,\partial\omega)
,
then
C_{p}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
is exactly the open half-space

(A.9)

H=\left\{v\in T_{p}\partial\Omega,\>\>v\cdot(-\gamma^{\prime}(1))>0\right\}.

Figure 3.
Illustration of the various objects attached to a geodesically convex open subset
\omega\subset\partial\Omega
; (on the right) the tangent cone
C_{p_{1}}
to the point
p_{1}
; (on the left) the point
q
is such that
d^{\partial\Omega}(p_{2},q)=d^{\partial\Omega}(q,\partial\omega)
, and so the half-space
H\subset T_{p_{2}}\partial\Omega
which is normal to the velocity vector
w=\gamma^{\prime}(1)
of the associated minimizing geodesic
\gamma
is exactly the tangent cone to
\omega
at
p_{2}
.

We are now in a position to establish an equivalence between
D(\omega)
and a much simpler integral over
\omega
.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\omega
be a geodesically convex, open Lipschitz subset of
\partial\Omega
.
Then

c\int_{\omega}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}\leq D(\omega)\leq C\int_{\omega}{\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}~,

where the positive constants
c
and
C
depend only on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

The lower bound was already established in
Lemma

A.4
, so it only remains to prove the upper bound.
Let
x\in\omega
be given, and let
p\in\partial\omega
be a point minimizing the geodesic distance from
x
to
\partial\omega
:

\delta:=\text{\rm dist}^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)=d^{\partial\Omega}(x,p)~.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
denote the set

{\mathcal{H}}:=\left\{\text{\rm exp}_{p}\left(t\frac{v}{|v|}\right)~:~v\in H\hbox{ and }0<t<r_{\text{\rm inj}}~\right\}~,

where
H
is the supporting half-space to
\omega
at
p
characterized by
Eq.

A.9
.
Due to
Proposition

A.1
, we also obtain the following estimate

(A.10)

\rho_{\omega}(x)=\int_{\partial\Omega\setminus\omega}{\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{|x-y|^{d}}}\geq\int_{B^{\partial\Omega}(p,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus{\mathcal{H}}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{|x-y|^{d}}}~.

Since the geodesic distance
d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)
between
x
and any point
y\in\partial\Omega
is always larger than the corresponding Euclidean distance
|x-y|
, we conclude that

\rho_{\omega}(x)\geq\int_{B^{\partial\Omega}(p,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\setminus{\mathcal{H}}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}s(y)}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,y)^{d}}}~.

Using the change of variables of
Lemma

A.2
based on the exponential mapping
B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\subset T_{p}\partial\Omega\to B^{\partial\Omega}(p,r_{\text{\rm inj}})\subset\partial\Omega
, we then get

\rho_{\omega}(x)\geq C\int_{\left\{y\in B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}),\>y_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}y}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(y))^{d}}}~,

where
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1})
are the coordinates of the integration variable
y
in an orthonormal frame of
T_{p}\partial\Omega
, with the first coordinate vector being the outer normal to
H
. The triangle inequality for the geodesic distance, and a change of variables yields

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\rho_{\omega}(x)&\geq&C\int_{\left\{y\in B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}),\>y_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}y}{(\delta+d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(y))^{d}}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\frac{C}{\delta^{d}}\int_{\left\{y\in B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}}),\>y_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}y}{\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta}d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(y)\right)^{d}}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{\left\{z\in B(0,\frac{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}{\delta}),\>z_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}z}{\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta}d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(\delta z)\right)^{d}}}~.\end{array}

Let
\ell>0
be the maximum length of a geodesic segment on
\partial\Omega
; obviously
\delta\leq\ell
, and so

(A.11)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\rho_{\omega}(x)&\geq&\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{\left\{z\in B(0,\frac{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}{\ell}),\>z_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}z}{\left(1+\frac{1}{\delta}d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(\delta z)\right)^{d}}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{\left\{z\in B(0,\frac{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}{\ell}),\>z_{1}>0\right\}}{\frac{\text{\rm d}z}{\left(1+|z|\right)^{d}}}~.\end{array}

In the last line we have used that, according to
Eq.

A.2

d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(\delta z))=\delta d^{\partial\Omega}(p,\text{\rm exp}_{p}(z))=\delta|z|,\text{ as long as }\delta z\in B(0,r_{\text{\rm inj}})~,

together with the fact that
|z|<\frac{r_{\text{\rm inj}}}{\ell}
implies
\delta|z|<\frac{\delta}{\ell}r_{\text{\rm inj}}\leq r_{\text{\rm inj}}
.
The estimate
Eq.

A.11
immediately shows that there exists a constant
c>0
which depends only on the properties of
\partial\Omega
(and not on the set
\omega
) such that

\forall x\in\omega,\>\>\rho_{\omega}(x)\geq\frac{c}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)}~.

Finally, this gives

D(\omega)=\int_{\omega}{\frac{1}{\rho_{\omega}(x)}\text{\rm d}s(x)}\leq C\int_{\omega}{d^{\partial\Omega}(x,\partial\omega)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}~,

which is the desired upper bound.
∎

Appendix B
The Peetre lemma

For the convenience of the reader, we recall Peetre’s lemma, which provides a convenient sufficient condition for an operator to be Fredholm;
see for instance
[
56
]
,
[
47
]
(Chap. 2, §5.2), and also
[
64
]
for the precise version below, an interesting proof and useful application examples.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
(E,||\cdot||_{E})
be a Banach space,
(F,||\cdot||_{F})
and
(G,||\cdot||_{G})
be normed vector spaces.
Let
A:E\to F
and
B:E\to G
be bounded operators satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that,

\forall u\in E,\>\>||u||_{E}\leq C\Big(||Au||_{F}+||Bu||_{G}\Big).

(ii)

The operator
B
is compact.

Then,
A
has closed range in
F
and finite-dimensional kernel in
E
.

Appendix C
Equilibrium distributions

In this appendix, we collect some useful results from the literature about the
equilibrium distributions
associated with certain integral operators.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be defined by
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}:=\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\>\>|x|<1\right\}
. Then,

(i)

If
d=2
, the function
\phi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
defined by

\forall(x,0)\in{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1},\>\>\phi(x)=\frac{2}{\log 2\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

satisfies

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}{\log|x-y|\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}=1\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\text{ and }\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}{\phi(x)\>\text{\rm d}x}=\frac{2\pi}{\log 2}~.

(ii)

If
d=2
, the function
\phi\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{D}})
defined by

\forall(x,0)\in{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1},\>\>\phi(x)=-2\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

satisfies

\frac{1}{2\pi}\>\underset{\eta\downarrow 0}{\text{\rm f.p.}}\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}\setminus(x-\eta,x+\eta)}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}=1\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\text{ and }\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}{\phi(x)\>\text{\rm d}x}=-\pi~.

(iii)

If
d=3
, the function
\phi\in\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
defined by

(C.1)

\forall(x,0)\in{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1},\>\>\phi(x)=\frac{4}{\pi\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}

satisfies

(C.2)

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}{\frac{1}{|x-y|}\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}=1\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\text{ and }\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}}{\phi(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}=8~.

(iv)

If
d=3
, the function
\phi\in\widetilde{H}^{1/2}({{\mathbb{D}}}_{1})
defined by

\forall x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\>\>\phi(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}

satisfies

\frac{1}{4\pi}\>\underset{\eta\to 0}{\text{\rm f.p.}}\int_{{{\mathbb{D}}}_{1}\setminus B(x,\eta)}{\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}\phi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}=1\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1},\text{ and }\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\phi(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)}=-\frac{2}{3}~.

The two-dimensional results (i) and (ii) can be proved by means of conformal mapping techniques; see
[
49
]
, Exercises 8.15 and 8.16.
The item (iii) is a fairly well-known result about the capacitance of a flat disk in 3d, and we refer to
[
40
]
Exercise 3.3, or to
[
22
]

for an elegant proof using the connection with Abel’s integral equation.
Finally, for the item (iv), we refer to the articles
[
42
,
48
]
; see also
[
60
]

where these results are used to build a series expansions for the hypersingular operator, for the purpose of operator preconditioning.

Remark C.1
.

Let us comment about the physical significance of the formulas in
Proposition

C.1
.
The points
(i)
and
(iii)
are concerned with the Newtonian potential. In particular,
the equilibrium distribution
\phi
is the charge distribution on
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
which ensures that the induced electrostatic potential is constant (equals
1
) on
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.
The total charge
\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\varphi\>\text{\rm d}s}
associated with this distribution corresponds to the Newtonian version of the capacity of
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.

The points
(ii)
and
(iv)
are perhaps a little more unfamiliar. The function
\phi
is the dipole distribution on
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
which ensures that the induced electric current through
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
is constant (equals
1
). The quantity
\int_{{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\phi\>\text{\rm d}s}
is the associated total dipole charge.

Appendix D
Some useful results about integral operators with homogeneous kernels

In this section, we collect some useful properties of integral operators
whose kernels satisfy specific homogeneity properties. This material is taken from Chap. 4 in
[
52
]
.

Definition D.1
.

Let
m
be a non negative integer; a homogeneous kernel of class
-m
is
a function
K(x,z)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
which satisfies the following properties:

•

For all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
,

\sup\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sup\limits_{|z|=1}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,z)\right\lvert<\infty~.

•

For all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and all index
\beta
with
|\beta|=m
, the function
z\mapsto\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,z)

is odd and homogeneous of degree
-(d-1)
, i.e.,

\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\>z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\},\>\>t>0,\quad\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,-z)=-\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,z),\text{ and }\\
\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,tz)=t^{-(d-1)}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,z)~.

To each homogeneous kernel, it is possible to associate an integral operator
T_{K}
, acting on functions
\varphi:\partial D\to{\mathbb{R}}
, via the formula

(D.1)

T_{K}\varphi(x)=\int_{\partial D}{K(x,x-y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y}.

The following result specifies the mapping properties of this integral operator.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain, and let
K(x,z)
be a homogeneous kernel of class
-m
,
with associated operator
T_{K}
defined in
Eq.

D.1
. Then for each
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the mapping
T_{K}
defines a bounded operator

T_{K}:H^{s}(\partial D)\to H^{s+m}(\partial D)~,

that is, there exists a constant
C_{s,D,K}
such that

\forall\varphi\in H^{s}(\partial D),\>\>||T_{K}\varphi||_{H^{s+m}(\partial D)}\leq C_{s,D,K}||\varphi||_{H^{s}(\partial D)}~.

The constant
C_{s,D,K}
, that is, the operator norm of
T_{K}:H^{s}(\partial D)\to H^{s+m}(\partial D)
,
depends only on
s
,
D
, and the kernel
K
. It can be estimated by

(D.2)

C_{s,D,K}\leq C_{s,D}\sup\limits_{|\alpha|\leq k\atop|\beta|\leq k}\sup\limits_{x\in\partial D}\sup\limits_{|z|=1}\left\lvert\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial z^{\beta}}K(x,z)\right\lvert,

where
k
is a non negative integer which only depends on the space dimension
d
, and
C_{s,D}
is a constant which depends only on
d
and the domain
D
.

Remark D.1
.

The above statement is Th. 4.3.1 in
[
52
]
.
In that reference, the continuity constant of the mappings
T_{K}:H^{s}(\partial D)\to H^{s+m}(\partial D)
is not stated explicitly,
but formula
Eq.

D.2
is obtained by tracking the dependence of this constant with respect to
K
throughout the proof.

We finally state the following result about the potential operator induced by a homogeneous kernel of class
-m
; see
[
23
]
and
[
29
]
(Lemma 21.7).

Theorem D.2
.

Let
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain, and let
K(x,z)
be a homogeneous kernel of class
-m
.
Then for each
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the associated potential operator:

T_{K}\varphi(x)=\int_{\partial D}{K(x,x-y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y},\quad x\notin\partial D,

is a bounded mapping from
H^{s}(\partial D)
into
H^{s+m+\frac{1}{2}}(D)
and
H^{s+m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\overline{D})
.
For any compact subset
L\Subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus D
, there exists a constant
C_{s,D,K,L}
such that,

\forall\varphi\in H^{s}(\partial D),\>\>||T_{K}\varphi||_{H^{s+m+\frac{1}{2}}(D)}+||T_{K}\varphi||_{H^{s+m+\frac{1}{2}}(L)}\leq C_{s,D,K,L}||\varphi||_{H^{s}(\partial D)}.
