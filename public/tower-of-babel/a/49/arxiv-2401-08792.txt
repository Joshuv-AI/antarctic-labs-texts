d-Operators in Banach Lattices
Eduard Emelyanov, Nazife Erkurşun-Özcan, Svetlana Gorokhova 1 1 Sobolev Institute of Mathematics, Novosibirsk, Russia 2 2 Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Science, Hacettepe University, Ankara, Turkey 3 3 Uznyj Matematiceskij Institut VNC RAN, Vladikavkaz, Russia

Abstract
We study operators carrying disjoint bounded subsets of a Banach lattice into compact, weakly compact, and limited subsets of a Banach space. Surprisingly, these operators behave differently with classical compact, weakly compact, and limited operators. We introduce and investigate d-Andrews operators which carry disjoint bounded sets onto Dunford–Pettis sets.



Keywords:
Banach lattice, disjoint bounded set, compact set, limited set, domination problem

2
Definitions and examples

In this section, we introduce
d
-operators for (almost) order bounded,
(weakly) compact, limited, and Dunford–Pettis subsets of the range.
Examples justifying that these operators differ from the corresponding
classical ones are presented.

It is an open question whether each linear operator carrying

d-bdd
sets onto bounded sets is bounded. Since each operator

T:E\to Y
that carries order intervals to bounded sets is bounded (cf.
[

for a positive answer to the question, it sufficies to prove that
each operator carring disjoint order bouned sequences to bounded ones is bounded.
It is worth noting that a linear operator
from a Hilbert space to a normed space is bounded iff it
bounded along orthonormal sequences
[
11

2.1
d-Order bounded operators.

The following two classes of operators rely on order boundedness.

Definition 3
.

An operator
T\in\text{\rm L}(E,F)

d
-order bounded (
\sigma

-order bounded),

2.2
d-Compact operators.

Next, we pass to compactness in Banach spaces.

Definition 4
.

An operator
T\in\text{\rm L}(E,Y)
is
d
-compact
(
\sigma

-compact) if

lies
in a compact subset of

(resp., by
\sigma\text{\rm d-K}(E,Y)

Clearly,

We do not know whether each linear operator carrying

The following example shows that a

-compact operator need not be compact.

Example 1
.

Let

and let
T:L^{\infty}[0,1]\to L^{p}[0,1]
be
the natural embedding. Since
\|T(r_{n}^{+})-T(r_{m}^{+})\|_{p}=2^{-\frac{2}{p}}

for

is the

is not compact.
However,

(f_{n})
is a sequence in a

subset

of

then either some function

occurs infinitely many times in

or
\|f_{n}\|_{p}\to 0

(Tf_{n})=(f_{n})
has

\|\cdot\|_{p}
-convergent subsequence.

As every infinite-dimensional normed lattice contains a normalized disjoint sequence,

\text{\rm d-K}(E)=\text{\rm L}(E)

.

Let

is relatively compact for every disjoint normalized countable

are trivial.
It suffices to prove implications

ii)\Longrightarrow i)
Let

D_{1}=\{x_{+}:x\in D\ \&\ x_{+}\neq 0\}\ \ \ \text{\rm and}\ \ \ D_{2}=\{x_{-}:x\in D\ \&\ x_{-}\neq 0\}.

If one of these sets is empty, say

T(D)\subseteq T(D_{1})\cup\{0\}\subseteq\text{\rm cch}\bigl(\bigl\{T(\|x\|^{-1}x):x\in D_{1}\bigl\}\bigl),

[formula]

where

By the condition

If

[formula]

It follows from (3) that

If

2.3
d-Weakly compact operators.

Now, we consider weak compactness.

Definition 5

An operator

Example 2

Consider an operator

By the Eberlein–Smulian theorem, for

If

(x_{n})

For each

for

and linear otherwise. Since the sequence

Finally, we show that

and linear otherwise. Clearly,

Since the set

2

Let us have a look at limited sets.

Definition 6
.

An operator
T\in\text{\rm L}(E,Y)

d

(
\sigma

is limited for every

Clearly, limited operators and

The following example shows that a

Example 3
.

Let
T:L^{\infty}[0,1]\to L^{1}[0,1]

is

-compact, and hence

The limited sets agree with compact sets in

is not compact.

By the next example, a limited operator
need not be

-compact.

Example 4
.

Let
T:c_{0}\to\ell^{\infty}
be the embedding operator.
By Phillip’s lemma (cf.,
[

),

is a limited subset of

. Therefore,

However,

is not relatively compact in

, and hence

T\notin\text{\rm d-K}(c_{0},\ell^{\infty})
.

In general,

-compact operators need not to be

-limited.

Example 5

Since

T(D)

.

The last kind of operators that is considered in the present paper

Definition 7

An operator

(countable) subset

\text{\rm d-And}(E,Y)

\sigma\text{\rm d-And}(E,Y)

Clearly,
\text{\rm d-K}(E,Y)\subseteq\text{\rm d-Lim}(E,Y)\subseteq\text{\rm d-And}(E,Y)

As mentioned in Remark

The operator

The next example shows that a

Example 6

Let
1<p<\infty

3

Here, we investigate

-(weakly) compact operators.
The following Grothendieck type lemma
(cf.
[

, Theorem 3.44]
and
[

, Proposition 1]
) is useful.

Lemma 1
.

Let, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
A_{\varepsilon}\subseteq X
such that

A\subseteq A_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon B_{X}
.

i)

If each
A_{\varepsilon}
is relatively compact then
A
is relatively compact.

ii)

If each
A_{\varepsilon}
is relatively weakly compact then
A
is relatively weakly compact.

iii)

If each
A_{\varepsilon}
is (almost) limited then
A
is (almost) limited.

iv)

If each
A_{\varepsilon}
is an (almost) Dunford–Pettis set then
A
is an (almost) Dunford–Pettis set.

Proof.

We include only a proof of
iv)
.
Assume, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a Dunford–Pettis set
A_{\varepsilon}

with
A\subseteq A_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon B_{X}
, and let
(f_{n})
be
w
-null
in
X^{\prime}
.
Then
\|f_{n}\|\leq M
for some
M\in\mathbb{R}
and all
n
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, the inequality

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigl(\sup_{x\in A}|f_{n}(x)|\bigl)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigl(\sup_{y\in A_{\varepsilon}}|f_{n}(y)|\bigl)+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigl(\sup_{z\in\varepsilon B_{X}}|f_{n}(z)|\bigl)\leq\varepsilon M

implies
f_{n}\rightrightarrows 0(A)
, and hence

is a Dunford–Pettis set.

For the almost Dunford–Pettis case, letting

to be a Banach lattice,
just replace in the proof above
w
-null
(f_{n})
by
disjoint
w
-null
(f_{n})
.
∎

Suppose

be a disjoint subset of

3.2
When is each bounded operator d-(weakly) compact.

In view of Proposition
2
, each bounded operator

[

Here, we give a condition on

is

-weakly compact.

Theorem 2
.

The following conditions are equivalent.

i)

\text{\rm L}(E,Y)=\text{\rm d-W}(E,Y)
for every

.

ii)

\text{\rm L}(E)=\text{\rm d-W}(E)
.

iii)

E^{\prime}
is a KB-space.

Proof.

i)\Longrightarrow ii)
is trivial.

ii)\Longrightarrow iii)
Let
(x_{n})
be a disjoint bounded sequence in
E
.
By the condition

,
I_{E}\in\text{\rm d-W}(E)
, and hence

is relatively
w
-compact.

w
-null, so
(x_{n})
is
w
-null.
Now,

is a KB-space by
[

iii)\Longrightarrow i)
Let

and

Take a sequence

in

. If

has only finitely many distinct elements

obviously contains even a norm-convergent subsequence.
If
(y_{n})
has infinitely many distinct elements then there is a distinct (and hence disjoint)

(x_{n_{k}})
of elements of

3.3
Domination problem for d-(weakly) compact operators.

It is well known that the (weak) compactness does not pass to dominated operators
in general (cf.,
[
2
, Sections 5.1 and 5.2]
). The following example shows that,
in the classes of
d
-(weakly) compact operators, the situation is not better.

Example 7
.

Consider operators
S,T:\ell^{1}\to c
defined by

Sx=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sum\limits_{i=k}^{\infty}x_{i}\Bigl)e_{k}\ \ \ \&\ \ \ Tx=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\Bigl)e_{k}.

Then
0\leq S\leq T
and
T
is compact (even rank one),
yet
S\notin\text{\rm d-W}(\ell^{1},c)
as the sequence

(Se_{n})=\Bigl(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}\Bigl)
in
c
has no
w
-convergent subsequence.

By the Wickstead theorem, (cf.,
[
2
, Theorem 5.31]
),
if either
E^{\prime}
or
F
has order continuous norm then

0\leq S\leq T\in\text{\rm W}(E,F)
implies
S\in\text{\rm W}(E,F)
.
The following theorem deal with the domination problem
for d-(weakly) compact operators.

Theorem 3
.

Let
0\leq S\leq T\in\text{\rm L}(E,F)
, where
E^{\prime}
is a KB-space.

i)

If
T\in\text{\rm d-K}(E,F)
then
S\in\text{\rm d-K}(E,F)
.

ii)

If
T\in\text{\rm d-W}(E,F)
then
S\in\text{\rm d-W}(E,F)
.

Proof.

i)
Let
D
be a disjoint normalized countable subset of
E_{+}
.
Accordingly to Proposition
2
, we need to show that
S(D)

is relatively compact. So, let
(y_{n})
be a sequence in
S(D)
.
If
y_{n}=0
for infinitely many
n
-th then
(y_{n})
has a norm-null subsequence.
If
y_{n}=0
only for finitely many
n
-th, by removing them, we may suppose
all
y_{n}\neq 0
. For each
n
, pick
x_{n}\in D

with
y_{n}=Sx_{n}
and denote
D_{0}=\bigl\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\bigl\}
.
Then
D_{0}
is a disjoint normalized subset of
E_{+}
.
If
D_{0}
is finite then
S(D_{0})
is finite and hence compact. So, suppose
D_{0}
is infinite.
By the assumption
i)
,
T(D_{0})
is relatively compact. Since
E^{\prime}
is a KB-space,
the disjoint normalized sequence
(x_{n})
is
w
-null, and hence
Tx_{n}\convw 0
.
Suppose
\|Tx_{n}\|\not\to 0
. Then
\|Tx_{n_{k}}\|\geq M>0
for some subsequence

(x_{n_{k}})
of
(x_{n})
. Since
T\in\text{\rm d-K}(E,F)
, there exists a further
subsequence
(x_{n_{k_{j}}})
such that
\|Tx_{n_{k_{j}}}-z\|\to 0
, and hence

Tx_{n_{k_{j}}}\convw z
. Since
Tx_{n}\convw 0
then
z=0
, and hence
\|Tx_{n_{k_{j}}}\|\to 0
violating

\|Tx_{n_{k}}\|\geq M>0
for all
k
. The obtained contradiction proved
\|Tx_{n}\|\to 0
.
Using
0\leq S\leq T
, we obtain
\|y_{n}\|=\|Sx_{n}\|\to 0
. Thus
S\in\text{\rm d-K}(E,F)
.

ii)
It follows from Theorem
2
.
∎

We do not know whether or not the condition on
E^{\prime}
in Theorem
3

is necessary for conclusions in
i)
and/or in
ii)
.
