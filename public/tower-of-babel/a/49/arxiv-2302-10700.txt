Solution formula for the general birth-death chemical diffusion master equation
Alberto Lanconelli, Berk Tan Perçin, Mauricio J. del Razo

Abstract
We propose a solution formula for chemical diffusion master equations of birth and death type. These equations, proposed and formalized in the recent paper [ 5 ] , aim at incorporating the spatial diffusion of molecules into the description provided by the classical chemical master equation. We start from the general approach developed in [ 20 ] and perform a more detailed analysis of the representation found there. This leads to a solution formula for birth-death chemical diffusion master equations which is expressed in terms of the solution to the reaction-diffusion partial differential equation associated with the system under investigation. Such representation also reveals a striking analogy with the solution to the classical birth-death chemical master equations. The solutions of our findings are also illustrated for several examples.



Key words and phrases: chemical diffusion master equation, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, Feynman-Kac formula, spectral methods.

AMS 2000 classification: 60H07; 60H30; 92E20.

Introduction and statement of the main results

The dynamics of biochemical processes in living cells are commonly understood as an interplay between the spatial transport (diffusion) of molecules and their chemical kinetics (reaction), both of which are inherently stochastic at the molecular scale. In the case of systems with small molecule numbers in spatially well-mixed settings, the diffusion is averaged out and the probabilistic dynamics are governed by the well-known chemical master equation (CME)
[
13
,
23
,
24
]
. The CME can be seldom solved analytically
[
16
]
. However, solving a few simple cases analytically can bring valuable insight to the solutions of more complex cases. Alternatively, one can solve it by integrating stochastic trajectories with the Gillespie or tau-leap algorithms
[
1
,
13
]
, by approximation methods
[
8
,
11
,
22
,
25
]
or even by deep learning approaches
[
14
,
17
]
.

In the case of spatially inhomogeneous systems, where diffusion is not averaged out, one would expect to obtain a similar master equation. However, obtaining such an equation is plagued with mathematical difficulties, and although it was hinted in previous work
[
10
]
and formulated for some specific systems
[
26
]
, it was not until recently that this was formalized into the so-called chemical diffusion master equation (CDME)
[
5
,
7
]
. The CDME changes a few paradigms that have not yet been explored thoroughly in stochastic chemical kinetics models. It combines continuous and discrete degrees of freedom, and it models reaction and diffusion as a joint stochastic process. It consists of an infinite sorted family of Fokker-Planck equations, where each level of the sorted family corresponds to a certain number of particles/molecules. The equations at each level describe the spatial diffusion of the corresponding set of particles, and they are coupled to each other via reaction operators, which change the number of particles in the system. The CDME is the theoretical backbone of reaction-diffusion processes, and thus, it is fundamental to model and understand biochemical processes in living cells, as well as to develop multiscale numerical methods
[
6
,
12
,
19
,
27
]
and hybrid algorithms
[
3
,
9
,
4
]
. The stochastic trajectories of the CDME can be often integrated using particle–based reaction–diffusion simulations
[
2
,
15
]
. However, analytic and approximate solutions have not yet been explored in detail. In this work, we work out a method to obtain an analytic solution of the CDME for a simple birth-death reaction system, with the aim to bring insight of the CDME solution of more complex systems.

We consider a system of indistinguishable molecules of a chemical species
S
which undergo

•

diffusion
in the bounded open region
\mathbb{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
;

•

degradation
and
creation
chemical reactions

\mbox{(I)}\quad S\xrightarrow{\lambda_{d}(x)}\varnothing\quad\quad\quad\mbox{(II)}\quad\varnothing\xrightarrow{\lambda_{c}(x)}S,

where
\lambda_{d}(x)
denotes the propensity for reaction (I) to occur for a particle located at position
x\in\mathbb{X}
(i.e., the probability per unit of time for this particle to disappear) while
\lambda_{c}(x)
is the propensity for a new particle to be created at position
x\in\mathbb{X}
by reaction (II).

To describe the evolution in time of such system the authors in
[
5
,
7
]
proposed a set of equations for the number and position of the molecules. Namely, for
t\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}^{n})
they set

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mbox{ number of molecules at time $t$},

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=0)

\displaystyle\int_{A}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathcal{N}(t)=n\}\cap\{(X_{1}(t),...,X_{n}(t))\in A\}\right);

here,
dx_{i}
stands for the three dimensional integration volume
dx^{(1)}_{i}dx^{(2)}_{i}dx^{(3)}_{i}
. Then, according to
[
5
,
7
]
the time evolution of the reaction-diffusion process described above is governed by the following infinite system of equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+(n+1)\int_{\mathbb{X}}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{n+1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n},y)dy\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d}(x_{i})\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\
&+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}(x_{i})\rho_{n-1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})\\
&-\int_{\mathbb{X}}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}),\quad\quad\quad n\geq 0,t>0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{X}^{n};\end{split}\end{cases}

(1.1)

where we agree on assigning value zero to the three sums above when
n=0
. The term

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

in (
1.1
) refers to spatial diffusion of the particles: here,

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}:=\partial^{2}_{x_{i}^{(1)}}+\partial^{2}_{x_{i}^{(2)}}+\partial^{2}_{x_{i}^{(3)}}

stands for the three dimensional Laplace operator. We remark that to ease the notation we choose a driftless isotropic diffusion but the extension to the divergence-form second order partial differential operator

\displaystyle\mathtt{L}_{x_{i}}v:=\sum_{l,m=1}^{3}\partial_{x^{(l)}_{i}}\left(a_{lm}(x_{i})\partial_{x^{(m)}_{i}}v\right)-\sum_{l=1}^{3}\partial_{x^{(l)}_{i}}\left(b_{l}(x_{i})v\right),

which models a general anisotropic diffusion with drift on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is readily obtained. The terms

\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{\mathbb{X}}\lambda_{d}(y)\rho_{n+1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n},y)dy-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d}(x_{i})\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

formalize gain and loss, respectively, due to reaction (I), while

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{c}(x_{i})\rho_{n-1}(t,x_{1},...,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},...,x_{n})-\int_{\mathbb{X}}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

relate to reaction (II). System (
1.1
) is combined with initial and Neumann boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{split}\rho_{0}(0)&=1;\\
\rho_{n}(0,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{X}^{n};\\
\partial_{\nu}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\partial\mathbb{X}^{n}.\end{split}\end{cases}

(1.2)

The initial condition above states that there are no molecules in the system at time zero while the Neumann condition prevents flux through the boundary of
\mathbb{X}
, thus forcing the diffusion of the molecules inside
\mathbb{X}
. The symbol
\partial_{\nu}
in (
1.2
) stands for the directional derivative along the outer normal vector at the boundary of
\mathbb{X}^{n}
.

Aim of this note is to present the following solution formula for (
1.1
)-(
1.2
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
v
be a classical solution of the problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\Delta v(t,x)-\lambda_{d}(x)v(t,x)+\lambda_{c}(x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{X};\\
v(0,x)=0,&x\in\bar{\mathbb{X}};\\
\partial_{\nu}v(t,x)=0,&t\geq 0,x\in\partial\mathbb{X}.\end{cases}

(1.3)

Then, the chemical diffusion master equation (
1.1
) with initial and boundary conditions (
1.2
) has a classical solution given by

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=0)=\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\},\quad t\geq 0,

(1.4)

and for
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}\frac{1}{n!}v(t,x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot v(t,x_{n}),\quad t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{X}^{n}.

(1.5)

To prove the validity of the representations (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) one can trivially differentiate the right hand sides with respect to
t
and verify using (
1.3
) that they indeed solve (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). We will however provide in the next section a constructive derivation of the expressions (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) which is based on the general approach proposed in
[
20
]
; here, an infinite dimensional version of the moment generating function method, which is commonly utilized to solve analytically some chemical master equations (see for details
[
21
]
), is developed. These techniques are also employed in an ongoing work which consider chemical diffusion master equations with higher order reactions.

Remark 1.2
.

It is important to highlight the striking similarities between the representation formulas (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) for the solution of the CDME (

)-(
1.2
) and the solution

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t)=\frac{\left(\frac{\mathtt{c}}{\mathtt{d}}(1-e^{-\mathtt{d}t})\right)^{n}}{n!}e^{-\frac{\mathtt{c}}{\mathtt{d}}(1-e^{-\mathtt{d}t})},\quad t\geq 0,n\geq 0,

(1.7)

with initial condition

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(0)=\delta_{0n},\quad\mbox{for all }n\geq 0.

(1.8)

Equation (
1.7
)-(
1.8
) describes the evolution in time of the probability

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t):=\mathbb{P}(\emph{numberofmoleculesattime$t$}=n)

for the reactions

\displaystyle\emph{(I)}\quad S\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}\varnothing\quad\quad\quad\emph{(II)}\quad\varnothing\xrightarrow{\mathtt{c}}S,

with no molecules at time zero. Here,
\mathtt{d}
and

are the

for degradation and creation reactions, respectively. (To see how (

We note that the function

\displaystyle t\mapsto\frac{\mathtt{c}}{\mathtt{d}}(1-e^{-\mathtt{d}t}),

appearing in (

) solves the deterministic rate equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}v(t)=-\mathtt{d}v(t)+\mathtt{c},&t>0\\
v(0)=0&.\end{cases}

(1.9)

This establishes a perfect agreement between (
1.3
),(
1.4
),(
1.5
), i.e. representation of the solution for (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) and reaction-diffusion PDE, on one side and (

1.8
) and rate equation, on the other side.

Corollary 1.3
.

In the reaction-diffusion model described by the CDME (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), conditioned on the event
\{\mathcal{N}(t)=n\}
the positions of the molecules at time
t
are independent and identically distributed with probability density function

\displaystyle p(t,x):=\frac{v(t,x)}{\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx},\quad x\in\mathbb{X}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=n)=\frac{\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right)^{n}}{n!}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}^{n})
; then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((X_{1}(t),...,X_{n}(t))\in A|\mathcal{N}(t)=n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\{(X_{1}(t),...,X_{n}(t))\in A\}\cap\{\mathcal{N}(t)=n\})}{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{A}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}}{\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{A}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}\frac{1}{n!}v(t,x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot v(t,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}}{\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}\frac{1}{n!}v(t,x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot v(t,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\frac{v(t,x_{1})}{\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx}\cdot\cdot\cdot\frac{v(t,x_{n})}{\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx}dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}.

The second part of the statement is proved as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=n)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}\frac{1}{n!}v(t,x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot v(t,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right)^{n}}{n!}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}.

∎

Constructive proof of Theorem

In this section we propose a constructive method to derive the representation formulas (

For notational purposes we assume
\mathbb{X}=]0,1[

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})\\

&-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy\cdot\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n}),\quad\quad\quad n\geq 0,t>0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in]0,1[^{n},\end{split}\end{cases}

(2.1)

with the usual agreement of assigning value zero to the three sums above when
n=0
, together with initial and Neumann boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{split}\rho_{0}(0)&=1;\\
\rho_{n}(0,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n};\\
\partial_{\nu}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})&=0,\quad n\geq 1,t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\partial[0,1]^{n}.\end{split}\end{cases}

(2.2)

We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=-\partial^{2}_{x}+\lambda_{d}(x),\quad x\in[0,1],

(2.3)

with homogenous Neumann boundary conditions and write
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
for the orthonormal basis of

that diagonalizes the operator
\mathcal{A}
; this means that for all

we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{k}(y)\xi_{j}(y)dy=\delta_{kj},\quad\xi_{k}^{\prime}(0)=\xi_{k}^{\prime}(1)=0,

and there exists a sequence of non negative real numbers
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\xi_{k}=\alpha_{k}\xi_{k},\quad\mbox{ for all $k\geq 1$}.

We observe that
\mathcal{A}
is an unbounded, non negative self-adjoint operator.

Assumption 2.1
.

The sequence of eigenvalues
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is strictly positive.

We now denote by
\Pi_{N}:L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])
the orthogonal projection onto the finite dimensional space spanned by
\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N}\}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}f(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\langle f,\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}\xi_{k}(x),\quad x\in[0,1];

we also set

\displaystyle d_{k}:=\langle\lambda_{d},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])},\quad c_{k}:=\langle\lambda_{c},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])},\quad\gamma:=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(y)dy.

(2.4)

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists
N_{0}\geq 1
such that
\Pi_{N_{0}}\lambda_{d}=\lambda_{d}
; this is equivalent to say
\Pi_{N}\lambda_{d}=\lambda_{d}
for all
N\geq N_{0}
.

In the sequel we set
\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}
to be the orthogonal projection from

to the linear space generated by the functions
\{\xi_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdot\cdot\cdot\otimes\xi_{i_{n}},1\leq i_{1},...,i_{n}\leq N\}

]

Theorem 2.3
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
-
2.2
be in force and denote by
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
a classical solution of equation (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). Then, for any
N\geq N_{0}
and
t\geq 0
we have the representation

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)=\mathbb{E}[u_{N}(t,Z)],

(2.5)

and for any
n\geq 1
and
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
,

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{j_{1},...j_{n}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot\xi_{j_{n}}(x_{n}).

(2.6)

Here,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,Z)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,z)(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}}dz,

(2.7)

while

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u_{N}(t,z)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\partial^{2}_{z_{k}}u_{N}(t,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}z_{k}\right)\partial_{z_{k}}u_{N}(t,z)+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}z_{k}-\gamma\right)u_{N}(t,z)\\
&u_{N}(0,z)=1,\quad t\geq 0,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{split}\end{cases}

(2.8)

We now start working out the details of formulas (
2.5
)-(
2.6
).

Lemma 2.4
.

The solution to the Cauchy problem (
2.8
) can be represented as

\displaystyle u_{N}(t,z)=\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(c_{k}z_{k}g_{k}(t)+c_{k}(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)\right\},

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle g_{k}(t):=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}},\quad t\geq 0,k=1,...,N.

(2.10)

Proof.

The solution to the Cauchy problem (
2.8
) admits the following Feynman-Kac representation (see for instance
[
18
]
)

\displaystyle u_{N}(t,z)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)-\gamma\right)ds\right\}\right],\quad t\geq 0,z=(z_{1},...,z_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(2.11)

Here, for
k\in\{1,...,N\}
, the stochastic process
\{\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{z_{k}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is the unique strong solution of the mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{z_{k}}(t)=\left(d_{k}-c_{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(t),\quad\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{z_{k}}(0)=z_{k},

with

,…,

,….,

) as

\displaystyle u_{N}(t,z)

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)ds\right\}\right]=e^{-\gamma t}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N}\exp\left\{c_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)ds\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\gamma t}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{c_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)ds\right\}\right].

We now want to compute the last expectation explicitly: first of all, we observe that equation (

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=z_{k}e^{-\alpha_{k}t}+\frac{d_{k}-c_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\left(1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}\right)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}\sqrt{2\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(s),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)ds

\displaystyle=z_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}+(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}s}}{\alpha_{k}}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\alpha_{k}(s-u)}\sqrt{2\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(u)ds

\displaystyle=z_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}+(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}s}}{\alpha_{k}}ds+\sqrt{2\alpha_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(s);

in the last equality we employed Fubini theorem for Lebesgue-Wiener integrals. The identity above yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{c_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Z}^{z_{k}}_{k}(s)ds\right\}\right]=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{c_{k}\left(z_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}+(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}s}}{\alpha_{k}}ds\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{c_{k}\sqrt{2\alpha_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(s)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{c_{k}\left(z_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}+(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}s}}{\alpha_{k}}ds\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}\right)^{2}ds\right\},

where in last equality we used the fact that
\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}dW_{k}(s)
is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance
\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}\right)^{2}ds
.
This, together with (
2
), gives

\displaystyle u_{N}(t,z)=

\displaystyle e^{-\gamma t}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\exp\left\{c_{k}\left(z_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}+(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}s}}{\alpha_{k}}ds\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k=1}^{N}\exp\left\{c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}(t-s)}}{\alpha_{k}}\right)^{2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(c_{k}z_{k}g_{k}(t)+c_{k}(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)\right\},

(recall definition (
2.10
)). The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Expectation (
2.5
) can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}-\gamma\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)=\exp\left\{\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}.

(2.14)

Proof.

Let
Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{N})
be an
N
-dimensional vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables; then,

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{N}(t,Z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(c_{k}Z_{i}g_{k}(t)+c_{k}(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(c_{k}(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}Z_{i}g_{k}(t)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{k}^{2}g_{k}(t)^{2}}{2}+c_{k}(d_{k}-c_{k})\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+c_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[c_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{g_{k}(t)^{2}}{2}-\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds\right)+c_{k}d_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\gamma t+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}d_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds\right\}.

The fourth equality follows from the expression of the exponential generating function of a Gaussian vector while the last equality is due to identity

\displaystyle\frac{g_{k}(t)^{2}}{2}-\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds+\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)^{2}ds=0,\quad t\geq 0

which follows from a direct verification (recall definition (
2.10
)). On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}g_{k}(s)ds=\frac{t}{\alpha_{k}}+\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}},

and hence

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}-\gamma\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}.

Moreover, letting
N
to infinity we get

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\exp\left\{t\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}-\gamma\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{t\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}-\gamma\right)+\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}.

Here, we employed the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}=\gamma,

which follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{c_{k}d_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}=

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}^{-1}\lambda_{c},\lambda_{d}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{-1}\lambda_{c},\mathcal{A}\mathtt{1}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}=\langle\lambda_{c},\mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{A}\mathtt{1}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{c},\mathtt{1}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}=\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{c}(x)\mathtt{1}(x)dx=\gamma.

We also denoted
\mathtt{1}(x)=1
,
x\in[0,1]
and exploited the identity
\mathcal{A}\mathtt{1}=\lambda_{d}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Expectation (
2.6
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{1})\right)\cdot\cdot\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{n})\right).

In particular,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{j}t}}{\alpha_{j}}\xi_{j}\right)^{\otimes n}(x_{1},...,x_{n}).

(2.15)

Proof.

We note that according to (
2.9
) we have

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,z)=u_{N}(t,z)c_{j_{1}}g_{j_{1}}(t)\cdot\cdot\cdot c_{j_{n}}g_{j_{n}}(t),

and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,Z)\right]=\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)c_{j_{1}}g_{j_{1}}(t)\cdot\cdot\cdot c_{j_{n}}g_{j_{n}}(t).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{\otimes n}\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{j_{1},...j_{n}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{z_{j_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\partial_{z_{j_{n}}}u_{N}\right)(t,Z)\right]\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot\xi_{j_{n}}(x_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{j_{1},...j_{n}=1}^{N}\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)c_{j_{1}}g_{j_{1}}(t)\cdot\cdot\cdot c_{j_{n}}g_{j_{n}}(t)\xi_{j_{1}}(x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot\xi_{j_{n}}(x_{n})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{(N)}(t)\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{1})\right)\cdot\cdot\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{n})\right).

Moreover, letting
N
to infinity we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{1})\right)\cdot\cdot\cdot\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}g_{j}(t)\xi_{j}(x_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{j}t}}{\alpha_{j}}\xi_{j}\right)^{\otimes n}(x_{1},...,x_{n}).

∎

We are now in a position to show the equivalence between (
2.14
)-(
2.6
) and (
1.4
)-(
1.5
).

We start observing that

\displaystyle d_{k}:=\langle\lambda_{d},\xi_{k}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{A}1,\xi_{k}\rangle=\langle 1,\mathcal{A}\xi_{k}\rangle=\alpha_{k}\langle 1,\xi_{k}\rangle=\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{k}(x)dx.

Therefore, from formula (
2.14
) we can write

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(t)=0)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}d_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{k}(x)dx\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}\xi_{k}(x)\right)dx\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{1}v(t,x)dx\right\},

where we set

\displaystyle v(t,x):=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}\xi_{k}(x).

(2.16)

Note that with this notation we can also write according to (
2.6
) that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x_{1},...,x_{n})=\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(t,x)dx\right\}\frac{1}{n!}v(t,x_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot v(t,x_{n}),\quad t\geq 0,(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{X}^{n}.

If we now prove that the function
v
defined in (
2.16
) solves (
1.3
), then the equivalence between (
2.14
)-(
2.6
) and (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) will be established. Since

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}\xi_{k}(x)\right)=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}e^{-\alpha_{k}t}\xi_{k}(x),

we can conclude that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}v(t,x)-\lambda_{d}(x)v(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{A}v(t,x)=-\mathcal{A}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{k}t}}{\alpha_{k}}\xi_{k}(x)\right)=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}(e^{-\alpha_{k}t}-1)\xi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}e^{-\alpha_{k}t}\xi_{k}(x)-\lambda_{c}(x)=\partial_{t}v(t,x)-\lambda_{c}(x),

proving the desired property (the initial and boundary conditions in (
1.3
