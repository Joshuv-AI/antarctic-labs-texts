Understanding the Experiences of Neurodivergent Undergraduate Physicists Through Critical Disability Physics Identity
Liam G. McDermott, Nazeer A. Mosley

Abstract
As more neurodivergent students enter college, discussion on neurodiversity in higher education is gaining momentum. It is increasingly imperative that we understand how neurodivergent individuals construct their identities within the context of physics programs. Using hte Critical Disability Physics Identity framework, this study offers a nuanced analysis of the lived experiences and identity development of neurodivergent undergraduate physicists through the lens of resource use and political agency. By examining the phenomenon of neurodivergent undergraduate physics identity, we uncover the multifaceted dimensions of their lived experiences, including the marginalization caused by a neurotypical-normative culture, the negotiation of conflicting definitions of being a physicist, and the influence of supportive networks. Our findings shed light on the intersectionality of neurodivergent identity and physics identity, offering valuable insights to educators, researchers, and institutions committed to fostering inclusive learning environments. Ultimately, this research contributes to the ongoing dialogue on diversity, equity, inclusion, and justice within physics and higher education as a whole.

Introduction

In 2021, reaffirming the United States’ commitment to the education of disabled students, U.S. Secretary of Education Miguel Cardona stated "Serving all [disabled students] in our public schools isn’t just written into law - it’s a moral obligation and strong equitable practice. When we recognize and celebrate these differences as strengths, and when we help all [students] make progress toward challenging educational goals, everyone benefits"

It is our hope that this paper will help educators and researchers alike to understand the lived experiences of their neurodivergent undergraduate students and research assistants, and support them in achieving their educational goals.

Language

The language we use, both academically and informally, has an immense power to help or harm historically marginalized communities

;

In this paper, we use Identity-First language (IFL) where possible. This is as opposed to Person-First language (PFL)

The usage of IFL in this paper is reflective of the preferences of the interviewees whose narratives are included in this work, common trends in language use in the broader neurodivergent community
Kapp

It should also be noted that many neurodivergent people do not claim a disability identity

;

;

III
Positionality

Our identities shape how we perceive the world. They give us a particular standpoint through which we understand each other and the way we interact. An important corollary then, is that our identities shape how we analyze data and how we construct knowledge
Mugge
. It is therefore critical that researchers discuss how their positionality affects their analysis and the conclusions they draw from said analysis.

To this effect, Author McDermott is a white, Queer, multiply disabled, neurodivergent PER graduate student. In terms of disability identity, Author McDermott is hard-of-hearing, born with congenital hearing loss. He also has anxiety and depression and experiences chronic pain. In terms of neurodivergent identity, he is autistic, has Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, and has experienced a traumatic brain injury (TBI). Author Mosley is a Black, Queer, non-disabled and non-neurodivergent undergraduate student. It is important that we, as researchers, uplift the voices and narratives of those we research, especially those from historically marginalized communities
Yosso
. Being part of the communities we represent in this paper helps us stay true to this research philosophy.

Our positionality informs our analysis. Author McDermott, being a neurodivergent and otherwise disabled person, is an insider to the neurodivergent and disabled communities. His analysis, therefore, comes from an insider standpoint. This standpoint comes with knowledge of how to parse particulars in pauses- effectively ascertaining the meaning of the negative space in speech. It also allows Author McDermott to navigate semantic interpretation (ie. sarcasm) which may be different from neurotypical forms of communication. However, this does not mean that Author McDermott knows everything about being neurodivergent, and like the rest of the scientific community he is learning more about neurodiversity every day. Therefore, when certain things were unclear, such as references, the impact of experiences on identity, etc., we made sure to ask participants for clarity and we engaged in member checking after analysis.

Despite not being neurodivergent themself, Author Mosley provides their own unique outsider perspective on neurodiversity. For instance, during analysis, Author Mosley is able to challenge McDermott’s perspectives on certain experiences that he perceives as "normal" based on past experiences. This allows for a more in-depth analysis of the data, and a much more nuanced reading of the interviews. Author Mosley also provides insight into experiences being non-white in the United States. Similarly to the above, certain things Author McDermott perceives as out-of-the-ordinary, Mosley is able to correct as being ordinary experiences for non-white individuals. Though we do not have the full picture of the human experience, no one does, together our analysis is stronger and more nuanced than it would be alone.

IV
Critical Disability Physics Identity

Building on Hazari et al.’s Physics Identity
Hazari2010
, Hyater-Adams et al.’s Critical Physics Identity (CPI)
HyaterAdams
, and Putnam’s Political Disability Identity (PDI)
Putnam2005
, we have developed and operationalized the CDPI framework. This framework combines the physics-specific constructs of Physics Identity and CPI, the Racialized Resources of CPI, and the Disability-Specific Domains of PDI to create a model for understanding how identity is constructed by disabled physicists. This framework is specifically useful as it is an assets-based framework, examining what resources and political agency people use to be successful in physics, and how they use them. This allows us to not only focus on what doesn’t work, and thus eliminate harmful practices, but also examine what does work, allowing recommendations for praxis to reinforce good practices.

CDPI combines the Physics-Specific Constructs laid out by Hyater-Adams et al.
HyaterAdams
with new findings by Hazari et al.
Hazari2020
. Namely, it adds the belonging construct to CPI. The CDPI Physics-Specific Constructs are thus recognition-belonging, interest, and performance-competence. Table I shows the operationalized definitions of each CDPI construct. Fig. 1 shows the breakdown of these disability-specific constructs into sub-constructs in a tree diagram. In this research, we take the stance that all people interviewed are capable of doing physics, and doing physics well, given the appropriate resources and their accessibility needs met. We take this stance from work on Neuroqueer Literacies
Kleekamp
;
Smilges
;
McDermott2023a
, which state that failure to perform in normative ways is not indicative of incompetence, but instead evidence of needs not being met and the creation of counter-literacies.

Critical Disability Physics Identity Constructs and Resources

Disability-Specific Construct

Definition

Self-Worth

Belief that oneself holds equal value in society to abled persons.

Discrimination

Belief that disabled people are held at a lower value in society due to ableist norms and prejudices.

Pride

Belief that one belongs to a disability community and that belonging is a good thing.

Common Cause

Aligning oneself with the needs of others. Seeing that what uplifts others, uplifts us all.

Political Acts

Understanding that change can and should be made in society, and engaging in intentional acts to create that change.

Physics-Specific Construct

Definition

Competence-Performance

The alignment or misalignment of one’s performance of physics tasks with their competence in understanding physics.

Interest

Interest in the subject of physics, and in doing and performing physics-related tasks or study.

Recognition-Belonging

Recognition as a physicist by oneself or others, and feeling of belonging in a physics or physics-related community.

Resources

Definition

Material Resource

Physical things which one has or has access to which affect their relationship with physics.

Ideational Resource

Beliefs about physics and one’s relationship to physics which affect their relationship with physics.

Relational Resources

Relationships with people which affect their relationship with physics.

Instead, what matters is whether the political action is taken by oneself or by others on behalf of a person. In addition to these constructs, CDPI makes use of Nasir’s (from Hyater-Adams et al.’s CPI

) racialized resources. These resources are both tangible and intangible things which historically marginalized people can use to strengthen their relationship to their field, namely physics. Instead of looking at what these groups are "lacking," these resources allow us to examine what strengths historically marginalized physicists bring into the classroom and into the laboratory. It is important to note that in our work in operationalizing the CDPI framework

, we found that the Physics- and Disability-Specific Constructs were regularly used as resources themselves. One strength to the CDPI framework is this recognition of how a person from an historically marginalized community can use the foundations of their identity as a form of cultural capital

which can be used as resources to navigate oppressive structures. This, for instance, can involve using one’s understanding of common cause through access to community leaders in minority spaces as relational and ideational resources to navigate legal structures to gain accommodations. We will therefore not only examine general resource use in this paper, but also how these undergraduate physicists bring in strengths from their identity construction to navigate their physics classes and departments. V
Data Collection

We conducted nine semi-structured interviews with neurodivergent undergraduate physicists. These interviews were approximately 1-2 hours in duration, in which subjects were asked questions regarding their experience in physics, their experience as neurodivergent people, and their experience as neurodivergent people in physics (see Appendix I for interview protocol). Author McDermott was the only one to conduct interviews, being a part of the neurodivergent community himself, such that there would be less time required to establish a rapport
Ross
. To ensure the accuracy of the analysis, we also engaged in member checking, sending a copy of the draft paper to each subject with the prompt "please let us know if there is anything you wish to be changed about how you or your views are represented, and what those changes would be"
Creswell2000
. Subjects were given two weeks to respond to this email. This deadline was placed because subjects are neurodivergent, and because of their neurodivergent identity many indicated a strong desire for loose, yet clear deadlines. We have no reason to believe that there was any power imbalance to dissuade subjects from questioning and adjusting the choices we made in their representation.

We report on interviews with nine subjects: Dmitri, Elizabeth, Tom, Wren, Roger, Quincy, Sky, Catalina, and Henry (Sky, Catalina, and Henry are post-baccalaureate non-academic physicists). Subjects were chosen using a convenience sampling method, based on their relation with Author McDermott. During the interview, subjects were prompted to choose a pseudonym to aid in anonymization of their data. Pseudonyms were only rejected if the name was deemed "too distracting" (ie. Elizabeth II, or Yorgomath the Undying). Subjects were prompted at the end of their interview to disclose their demographic information to the extent that they were comfortable. To this effect, five subjects identified as women, two as men, and two as non-binary/agender. Five subjects identified as cisgender, and four subjects identified as transgender/nonbinary. All subjects identified as white. Five subjects identified as bisexual, four subjects identified as straight.
In terms of neurodivergent identity (for the sake of parallelism, we are simply listing identities as nouns), Dmitri identifies with Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD) and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). Elizabeth identifies with autism and OCD. Tom identifies with autism. Quincy identifies with ADHD and autism. Roger identifies with autism, ADHD, bi-polar depression (BPD), and schizoaffective disorder. Wren identifies with BPD, ADHD, and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). Sky identifies with ADHD. Catalina identifies with dyslexia and ADHD. Henry identifies with epilepsy and ADHD. It was and still is very important to us that in conducting this study, we get perspectives from across the whole, incredibly heterogeneous, neurodivergent community. We believe that provide here a diverse enough sample in terms of neurodivergent identity to speak more accurately to the broader neurodivergent community. That being said, there is still much to be done in terms of representation when studying neurodiversity, and this paper is simply a first step.

VI
Methodology

This is a phenomenological study, and we use a phenomenological method to analyze our data. This is because there is so little research about neurodivergent identity construction
Shmulsky
. This is especially true about the intersection between neurodivergent and disciplinary identity, as the authors could find no studies examining this intersection. Because of this dearth of research, we felt that a phenomenological method was best given the need to collect and analyze as much qualitative data as possible in order to ascertain the "big picture" regarding the phenomenon that is neurodivergent people’s identity construction in physics. Additionally, there is already theory which lays the foundation for understanding how people from historically marginalized communities construct their identity in physics
HyaterAdams
. This study builds on this foundation to understand how neurodivergent people construct their identity, and utilize their political agency to find success in their field.

Author McDermott conducted all interviews. Interviews were first transcribed using Zoom’s auto-transcription service. Both authors further transcribed the interviews, both to correct mistakes in auto-transcription (for instance, we found that the auto-transcription had a hard time with the word "neurodivergent"), and to familiarize ourselves with the interviews. Interviews were then coded by both authors using NVivo software. Interviews were deductively coded using an a priori coding scheme based on the CDPI framework. After coding was completed, both authors held meetings to debate codes until consensus was met. There were no instances where consensus could not be reached.

To reinforce the qualitative analysis we conducted, code overlap was analyzed using NVivo software, and statistics were analyzed using Microsoft Excel. We examined only references created by the overlap of Physics-Specific Constructs, Disability-Specific Constructs, and Resources, which yielded a total of 747 references. This analytical choice was made because this study focuses on neurodivergent physics identity, and thus we want to look at instances where neurodivergent identity informs physics identity, and vice versa, and how resources were used to do so. We believe that this code overlap analysis proves useful as it can provide broad, descriptive information of what constructs interact with each other, and similarly what constructs interact with what resources. While this does not tell us how specifically resources/constructs are used for CDPI development, we can use code overlap information to help better deduce which resources/constructs are used most frequently for CDPI development, and for what purpose.

VII
Results

Perhaps most critical to our role as scientists is the communication of our results in both an intelligible and accurate way. This philosophy was cited multiple times by subjects when prompted with the question: "from your perspective, what is the most important part of what you do as a physicist?" Keeping in line with this sentiment, we have broken our results into three subsections. Namely, analyzing code connections between constructs and resources, analyzing code connections between physics-specific and disability-specific constructs, and critically analyzing interviews as a whole. By breaking up our results in this way, we believe we can best elucidate how we draw our conclusions, and best lead practitioners in understanding how learners develop and strengthen their CDPI, and what their role as educators may be in this process. It should be noted that the sum of percentages does not equal 100%. This is because some codes, even some conflicting codes, were coded in the same space.

VII.1
Use of Resources

Tables II, III, and IV display the code connections between constructs and resources as percent of total references, percent of individual construct references, and percent of individual resource references, respectively. The goal of breaking code connections into these three tables is to provide three distinct viewpoints on the same data.

Table II shows which code connections between resources and constructs were referenced most often, with a darker highlight indicating higher frequency of two codes connecting, and lighter highlight indicating lower frequency. While some connections are to be expected, such as positive relational resources connecting highly with feelings of recognition and belonging in physics, or positive ideational resources connecting highly with positive feelings of self-worth, others are more surprising. One connection which stood out as particularly interesting was that positive relational resources connected highly with having performance align with competence. A conjecture for why this may be is that, regularly, subjects cited positive relationships with professors and other educators when they felt supported through non-neurotypical-normative differences in learning. Another notable highly connected code dyad was negative material resources and structural oppressive discrimination. While it is not necessarily a surprise that negative material resources connect highly with structural oppressive discrimination, it is notable that negative material resources connect higher with structural oppressive discrimination than any other resources, and that it connects higher with any form of discrimination than any other resource.

There is also something incredibly important to be gleaned from the negative space as well. Namely, the least connected codes with any resource/construct are both outright oppressive and outright resistant discrimination. Additionally, very few connections are made with negative interest in physics. This pattern of outright discrimination and negative interest being so infrequently connected persists in every subset of our analysis. Furthermore, our dataset consists not only of students who have traditionally succeeded in physics, but also those who proceeded to drop out of physics, or leave the field entirely after graduating. Yet what remains constant across the dataset is the fact that it is not bad and evil ableist professors and peers who negatively impact neurodivergent students, nor is it a lack of interest in physics. Instead, it is how we educate neurodivergent students and the neurotypical-normative system that they are expected to succeed in which negatively impacts their experience and performance in physics.

Breaking the data down into percent of construct references in table III, we see once again that when considering performance and competence, unaligned performance and competence is highly connected with negative material resources. However, with aligned performance and competence, subjects usually reference positive ideational resources. We also see that when considering self-worth, students regularly connected positive self-worth with having positive ideational and relational resources, while negative self-worth connected highly with negative ideational resources. Once again, we see very little outright discrimination, while structural oppressive discrimination connects highly with negative material resources.

When considering specific resources, we again see very little connection with outright discrimination, and very high connection between negative material resources and structural oppressive discrimination. We also see that when subjects reference relational resources, they regularly connect positive relational resources with common cause, positive self worth, performance aligned with competence, and positive recognition and belonging, while very rarely connecting negative relational resources with anything, save for feelings of negative recognition and belonging. We also find that, while not the most highly connected, positive relational resources still regularly connect with all political acts and structural resistant discrimination. When considering material resources, we see positive material resources connect highly with aligned performance and competence, while negative material resources connect highly with structural oppressive discrimination and unaligned performance and competence. Finally, when considering ideational resources, we see positive ideational resources connecting highly with personal political acts, positive self-worth, and performance aligned with competence, while negative ideational resources are very rarely referenced, however when they are, they connect with structural oppressive discrimination and unaligned performance and competence.

Examining Tables II, III, and VI as a whole, we can make a few preliminary claims. First, we see that across students, there is a plethora of positive ideational resources. This concept is further reinforced by the fact that positive ideational resources were referenced more than total relational resources and total material resources. There appears to be a high sense of self-efficacy among neurodivergent undergrads, or a strong positive belief of their relationship with physics or their ability to succeed in physics. However, success in physics in the form of strong performance of physics tasks wavers with negative, perhaps oppressive, material resources. Furthermore, while it seems the structure of physics classes, departments, and culture are severely negatively impacting neurodivergent students, it is the people, find strong, vocal allies, which helps neurodivergent students resist discrimination, find belonging, and succeed at physics-specific tasks.

VII.2
Use of Constructs

Similar to the connections between constructs and resources, we have broken connections between disability-specific and physics-specific constructs into three tables. Tables V, VI, and VII display construct-construct connections as percent of total references, percent of physics-specific constructs, and percent of disability-specific constructs, respectively. Now, tables VI and VII may at a glance seem to be describing the same thing, having identical axes. However, Table VI was constructed by dividing each cell’s raw code connection count by the number of total references of each physics-specific construct, whereas Table VII was constructed by dividing each cell’s raw code connection count by the number of total references of each disability-specific construct. This distinction allows us to gain a better understanding of what physics-specific constructs are potentially being used to bolster what disability-specific constructs, as in Table VI, and what disability-specific constructs are potentially being used to bolster what physics-specific constructs, as in Table VII.

Table V shows us, in terms of overall references, subjects most often connected experiencing structural oppressive discrimination with their performance being unaligned with their competence. On the flip side, we see high connection between structural resistant discrimination, taking personal political action, and having positive self worth, with performance being aligned with competence. We also see positive self-worth and experiencing common cause being highly connected with positive recognition-belonging. Much of this may not be too surprising. After all, feeling like one belongs in a community and is recognized for their identity would sensibly make someone feel positively about themself, and feel like they have goals in common with those around them who share in their disciplinary identity. Similarly, performing in line with one’s competence would likely make one feel positively about themself. But interestingly, with the high connection between personal political acts and structural resistant discrimination with performance aligned with competence and structural oppressive discrimination with performance unaligned with competence, we arrive at three surprising conclusions. Either resisting neurotypical-normative aspects of physics and taking political action to do so helps students perform more in line with their competence and its inverse, having experiences of performance aligning with competence helps students advocate for themselves and resist neurotypical-normative aspects of physics and its inverse, or there could be a constructive feedback loop between performing physics-related tasks in line with one’s competence, self-advocation, and resistance. To gain a better understanding of this, we now examine Tables VI and VII.

In fact, Tables VI and VII indicate that this third option is a remarkably plausible one. Both tables show high connection between personal political acts and structural resistant discrimination with performance aligned with competence regardless of focus on physics- or disability-specific constructs. We do, however, see a much higher percent of connection between personal political acts and performance aligned with competence when considering disability-specific constructs, indicating that perhaps self-advocation, while in a constructive feedback loop with aligned performance, does affect performance more than performance affects self-advocation. A critical reading of the interview transcripts is still needed to make any clear-cut conclusions, however for neurodivergent physicist identity development, it seems very likely that advocating for oneself develops performance more in line with competence, and vice versa.

In further examining Tables VI and VII, we also see that positive interest in physics connects highly with seeing disability as a good thing and positive self-worth. We conjecture that this is because neurodivergent physicists see their neurodivergent identity as largely contributing to their fascination with physics through phenomena such as hyper-focus and special interests
Chellappa
;
Bailey
. We also see that the only instances of negative interest in physics are connected with experiencing structural oppressive discrimination, personal political acts, and structural resistant discrimination. We conjecture that this is because loss of interest in physics may be due to experiencing discrimination, which may make them take the political act to leave physics, or disidentify
Egner
with certain aspects of physics identity. Furthermore, we once again see high connection between positive recognition-belonging with common cause and positive self-worth for both disability- and physics-specific constructs. We also see higher connection between structural resistant discrimination and personal political acts with positive recognition-belonging when considering physics-specific constructs, while only higher connection between personal political acts with positive recognition-belonging when considering disability-specific constructs. This may be because coming to belong in a larger physics community gives neurodivergent physicists resources to resist discrimination and self-advocate, while self-advocating and making the intentional choice to identify as a physicist aids neurodivergent physicists in feeling like they belong in physics and that they are physicists.

In analyzing these code connections, we have hopefully set the stage for effective critical analysis of the collected interviews. We will use the above analysis and discussion of code connections to further assure that our conclusions are, if fact, founded in the reality experienced by neurodivergent physicists, and better flesh out the phenomenon that is neurodivergent identity development in physics.

VII.3
Critical Analysis of Interviews

VII.3.1
Question 1: What does neurodivergent undergraduate physics identity look like under a CDPI lens?

First and foremost, subjects grounded their identity in difference- difference in learning physics, difference in doing physics, and difference in identity development from neurotypical physicists. There was much discussion led by subjects on comparisons made between peers, especially while working together on problem sets or studying for quizzes or exams. Sky describes herself as an "atypical physicist", and provides a metaphor to explain, "just because I’m slightly different than what I would categorize as a typical [physicist] doesn’t mean I’m not one. So just because an apple is if let’s say, an apple is orange colored that doesn’t make it not an apple? It just makes it a really weird apple, like a really weird physicist." She reinforces her identification as a physicist immediately after this statement, saying,

I have some of the characteristics [of a traditional physicist]: if they studied it, which is an important part of that title. So I did study it. And I did succeed at it at the end. I mean, I graduated, and I by the end was able to get A’s and things like that in classes. But yeah. I think that’s why I [say] that I’m atypical because of my struggles with it.

Sky, along with many others spends much of her interview describing the dissonance she feels because of the struggles she has faced in physics. In the same breath she states "I think, to an extent, anybody can be a physicist, if they study it. So, I think- And I know study can mean a lot of different things," and "holding a degree in physics is probably what I would say makes you a physicist… because you have professors who have agreed. ’Yes, you qualified. You’ve passed our exams and things to prove that you know enough to have a certificate in this field.’" Tom initially states, after much discussion regarding the heterogeneity of the field, "a physicist is someone who seeks to answer the larger, deeper questions about the way things work." and describes his feeling like a physicist because, "is kind of the thing I have always done. It’s the thing I have always wanted to do. There was never a period in my life in which I wanted to do anything else." Yet, Tom also states, "I have gotten to do research with regards- in the field of astrophysics, so that, I would say, as a student and as an academic, That is what- that’s my "this is what qualifies me as a physicist", if like i’m actually doing the work I would say," and "Physics is tricky because there are so many qualifiers to get to this point. You have to be good at math. You have to be able to deal with all this verbiage and all these complicated abstract topics. You have to sit through, often, years of lower-level classes to reach this point." There is significant dissonance between you are a physicist if you do physics and you are a physicist if you earn the title.

Tom cites that there are many societal factors which can contribute to this dissonance, stating to be a physicist, "you have to be generally college educated, which means economically stable. I would say, the people most of the time who get to be physicists are people who live fairly unproblematic lives, and somehow end up with an interest in the field." These contributions to developing a physics identity are true, and many other scholars have written on the subject
Cochran
;
HyaterAdams
;
HyaterAdams2019
, and we certainly do not discount this. However from these interviews, there appears to be more to the story- more potentially problematic accidents which contribute to physics identity that higher education systems overlook.

We use the wording "accident" deliberately, as it appears that the aforementioned dissonance can be explained by how many neurodivergent undergrads see their success in physics as accidental. Every single subject indicated that they understand physics, are interested in physics, and do successful real-world research in physics. Therefore, under the first definition, they are physicists. However, when it comes to the second definition, the earning of the title, qualifiers seem arbitrary and random, and successfully passing through these qualifiers seems accidental. To quote Roger about a paper-writing class he took, "I write good papers… But I have a hard time writing fast.[A professor] required a full essay from everything you learned into the class, within an hour-or-so final. I wrote a paragraph and he told me that he basically told me I was pathetic." From Dmitri, "Neurodivergence can either be well aligned or poorly aligned with societal expectations. I think I’m very, very lucky that my neurodivergence is extremely well aligned with societal [expectations]." (Dmitri’s interview is, in fact, where we got the idea to frame competence and performance as "aligned" or "unaligned"). Wren describes the negative aspect of this accidental align/misalignment:

It’s very difficult [to succeed in physics], unless I’m interested in a specific topic. And that’s part of the reason I’m medicated, unless I’m interested in the topic, if my medication isn’t actively working and it’s very obvious when it doesn’t when it doesn’t because I have to be interested in something to get it done…I mean, I plan my homework around when my medication works because I won’t be able to do it. My medication doesn’t work. That’s just the assumption I live under.

To neurodivergent physicists, it seems, success can feel like it’s governed by the phases of the moon, rather than ability to do physics. This appears to have incredibly detrimental effects on neurodivergent students, because, as Wren states, "I feel that’s wrapped up in the idea of, like, succeed or die. And that sort of thing and like this idea of like. I mean, it’s not. Exactly how I feel, but I feel like it’s. Related to the sentiment of a place is earned and not a right… you have to earn the right to exist there."

How one earns this right should be straightforward. As Tom puts it, while "it would be foolish to say there’s not some natural talent that comes along with that," they believe "given enough time and energy, anyone can do what I do. Because if it’s just some mystical thing that lives in a box, because I’m cleverer than you, that’s silly." However, subjects indicated that success is largely weighted towards those who can perform physics neurotypically. About learning in class, Quincy states: "I found that things didn’t really make sense to me… I struggled to focus during my lectures, I struggled to understand what the professors and classmates were saying, and when previous concepts were brought up without review, I could never remember what they were and how to use them." Wren also indicates, from the standpoint of professors looking at her, "in comparison [to my neurotypical peers], I seem slow to understand and grasp the concept and I feel like I have. I have to because of my own efficiency’s sake. I have to study longer to obtain the same results and the same level of understanding." Roger specifies: "So word problems never came easy to me, and physics is just full of fun little word problems. And so that’s probably one of my like- probably from one of the things I have going on with me."

This pressure to perform neurotypically is compounded by peer comparison. A common issue among subjects was being seen as competent in physics by peers, and thus being denied resources to help them perform well. Of this, Quincy states: "I would work on homework with a few during my freshman year, but if I expressed that I didn’t understand something, I was generally met with something like ’what do you mean you don’t understand? You’re doing better in the class than anyone else.’" She adds, "I think this added shame and an even greater fear of failing."

This comparison among peers combined with conceptualizing physics identity as something to be earned also leads to self-denial of identity- belief that, while striving to identify as a physicist, individuals believe they are performing this identity incorrectly, and therefore are undeserving of the title. Sky describes this phenomenon, saying "I think at least for me, it was hard, I’ll be honest, hard not to focus on some of the differences that we had, because I felt like I was atypical." She describes her small undergraduate cohort, leading to her feelings of inadequacy. "We all solved problems very differently. The way we thought about things was very different. so how our brain works. Just- it was pretty evident… I don’t think linearly, I am very chaotic, and very popcorn brained." When prompted to describe how these differences make her feel as a physicist, Sky elaborates that "I really struggled with that, and thought that I was in the wrong." She acknowledges how neurotypical norms solidify this feeling, "because… the way the professors taught, the way everything was very linear… to me what that showed me is that my way of thinking about a problem and going about solving it was not the correct way." Sky goes further, "that was something that definitely made me think my brain wasn’t correct. My brain is bad at doing physics." These detrimental, prevalent comparisons, combined with the high connection between negative relational resources with negative recognition-belonging and negative self-worth, along with the high connection between unaligned performance-competence and negative self-worth and structural oppressive discrimination lead us to conjecture that there is a serious negative effect on neurodivergent physics identity development caused by something akin to a Community of Perception among undergraduates (as opposed to Community of Practice

Specifically, subjects regularly referred to two neurodivergent phenomena with regard to their interest in physics: hyperfocus and special interests. Before getting into the nitty-gritty of subject quotes and storying, we first need to define these phenomena. We use Hupfeld et al.’s definition of hyperfocus, as "state of heightened, focused attention that individuals with ADHD frequently report" (p. 1), acknowledging that this phenomenon is not specific to ADHD, and many neurodivergent people who do not identify with ADHD experience it. We also use Bailey’s
Bailey
definition of special interest as a "ruling passion" reported by neurodivergent individuals. We also acknowledge that the term "special interest" has its grounding in pathologizing frameworks, however, the phrasing has been pretty widely adopted by the broader neurodivergent community (though not everyone uses the term).

Dmitri describes their experience learning about hyperfocus, saying "when I learned about, like, hyperfocusing I was like ’ohh so the thing I’m able to do when working on physics or a video game or arranging music but something I’m not able to do when it comes to like reading or literally anything that doesn’t captivate me?’" They describe physics as something that really held their interest, much longer than many other subjects. Roger concurs, describing his definition of "hyperfixations", saying: "to me, a hyperfixation is something where I’m super excited and focused on it for, like, a period of time. Sometimes that period of time is like 2 weeks and sometimes it’s like 2 days. I kind of just go with the flow." He continues describing what led him to studying physics:

I realize now [they] were more likely hyperfixations, but I was so focused on, like, ’Be an engineer in middle school’ and I’m like, ’I’m going to be an engineer!’ and then like halfway through high school… I was like… ’I’m going to be a chemical engineer.’ And then I took chemistry. And I’m like, ’no, I’m going to be purely chemistry. This is my new- this is my new favorite. I’m going to go with it.’ And then I took physics and I’m like ’no, this is my new favorite. I’m going to go with it. I’m going to run with it’ and I ran with it… Yeah, and that was my new- my new thing and then I kind of like string theory kind of faded off, but physics didn’t. And that’s why I went to college.

Roger concludes, "[physics] was the one that stuck… It comes off and on. But like, it’s stuck for like years at this point now."

This idea of becoming a physicist due to interest verging on obsession was made explicit among almost every subject’s interview, and where it was not explicit, was heavily implied. Elizabeth, when prompted to discuss how/why she pursued study in physics, gave the following reason:

So when I was [a child], we had like a bunch of fridge magnets and stuff, and as a physicist I’m sure you know that the magnet problem is a crazy one, but I was like, you know, ’how do magnets work?’ And my mom, who is not a physicist, was just like ’they just do.’ And I hated that answer and it frustrated me so much and I remember that night like googling a bunch of trying to look up how they worked. And you know, I’m young and I don’t understand that that’s something like that where [the answer is a bit complicated] it’s like I just want to know how it works. And it bothers me that not everyone knows how this thing works.

For Elizabeth, the driving force toward studying physics is this intense need to know. Additionally, like many subjects, Elizabeth is a self-described "space kid." She describes how this intense interest in solving her personal dearth of information regarding magnets and astronomy as a child has since morphed into an intense interest in materials research. She states, "I found new things and materials that are really awesome and now I’m a graphene person and, like, obsessed with that kind of thing. [I’m obsessed with] finding super niche interests that don’t necessarily have answers yet, and wanting to be the one to find those answers."

Combining close examination of these interviews with our code connection analysis, it’s clear to us that neurodivergent physicists correlate their neurodivergent hyperfocus and special interests with their interest in the subject matter. Most importantly, they see this as a quintessentially good thing. Where, as many subjects indicated, hyperfocus may lead to a shirking of "uninteresting" work, like non-special-interest-specific homework, all subjects indicated something akin to a belief that having to deal with uninteresting work is simply a means to an end to do much more important and interesting physics research or physics teaching.

VII.3.2
Question 2: How do neurodivergent undergrads leverage resources in their identity?

Intrinsic to the CDPI framework is the examination of resource use for identity construction. As we have already examined and discussed what neurodivergent undergraduate physics identity looks like under a CDPI lens, it is time now to similarly examine and discuss how this identity comes to be. Upon close reading of these interviews, it’s clear that neurodivergent undergraduates leverage their relational resources and ideational resources to find success in physics, whereas they appear to be most negatively affected by poor material resources.

Regarding the use of relational resources, not many subjects cited relations outside of an academic environment, with the exception of Sky, who attributed much of her success to her support from her mother. Mostly, relational resources could be categorized into either professorial relationships, or peer-to-peer relationships. When discussing the importance of professorial relationships as a resource, Elizabeth has this to offer, "I think [that] mentorship and realizing that these people were once also in your place is very important to feel accepted into this community and to feel like you could make it to where they [professors] are." The assertion that professors were once students, and that doing physics means being a physicist is so incredibly important for neurodivergent undergraduates. About an introductory physics professor who embodies this concept, Catalina states, "I identified with being a physicist because of [Dr. Grayson], for so long that, well, I practice physics. I do physics so; therefore, I am a physicist." About Dr. Grayson’s comments about being a physicist, she says, "that was a huge confidence booster when I was already feeling very nervous about it."

Catalina also highlights the importance of finding strong allies in professors. About taking Quantum Mechanics, she tells us, "I don’t think there was enough done for people like me, because on campus I can come up with coping mechanisms myself." However, "once I got to higher level physics, like in the last year to two years, my dyslexia really got the best of me just because being at home [during lockdown] made it infinitely worse." She details the work her Quantum Mechanics professor put in for her success,

my professor was like ’I think you need those accommodations,’ and because I don’t- [have formal accommodations] because I don’t think dyslexia is something that you could go ’yeah, I need like 30 extra minutes on my exam,’ which is realistically what I needed. She gave them to me out of the good will of her heart, basically, because she could see when I was taking those exams, I would miss one entire question, because I was taking my time to do the math, because we were doing such complex math. So I wouldn’t get to one entire question of the exam, because I didn’t have time.

By finding an ally in this professor who was willing to implement Catalina’s accommodations into the design of her class, Catalina succeeded and passed her class.

Healthy peer-to-peer interactions are critical resources to neurodivergent undergraduate physicists as well. Information sharing about access to resources was a common discussion topic among subjects. Roger tells us a story about how he learned about accommodations for BiPolar Depression:

With all that was going on with me, I more likely required more time on exams and more time for homework. You know, just like, you know, like dropping, like, attendance issues. Attendance issues and all this stuff and I actually- when I first went to college, I spoke with someone who had been diagnosed BiPolar. And he said that he actually couldn’t be dinged for attendance. Because of his diagnosis. And he also had extensions on his homework and all these other things that I could have greatly used.

Roger details that conversations like this are a large part of why he sought out diagnoses and accommodations to help him finish school. Summarizing this idea, Henry states,

"I formed a rather close bond with at least two other neurodivergent physicists who definitely helped me through my classes and challenges I faced as someone who dealt with a disability while earning my degree. I feel that finding those people and forming those bonds is one of the only ways for neurodivergent physicists to make it through a physics education because it can be so discouraging otherwise."

In addition, many other subjects indicated that their success largely came from affinity-based extracurricular organizations, such as a physics club, or minorities in STEM groups.

While ideational resources are highly represented in terms of coding references, they are a bit harder to conceptualize in the way we have done with relational resources. There simply were not clear classifications for ideational resources to be broken down into. However, it appears that the main way that ideational resources appear to be used for neurodivergent undergraduate physicists is through relating their success in physics with their neurodivergent identity. As discussed in the previous section, These undergraduates correlate their interest in physics with hyperfocus and special interests. It appears that the belief that disability is a good thing, one of the disability-specific constructs, is regularly leveraged as an ideational resource. It seems that this intense interest in physics helps solidify neurodivergent undergraduate identity in their belief that they are physicists because they both do physics, and can earn the title.

VIII
Conclusion

Consistent across all interviews, neurodivergent undergraduate physicists are forcefully and unhealthily hammered into a neurotypical-shaped hole. They regularly must reshape how they work so that they can perform neurotypically, at the detriment of their mental health and their learning.Wren describes this perfectly, saying:

I feel like as I’m going about my degree, I am moving faster than I am comfortable if that makes sense in terms of like I have more coursework than I feel I can healthily manage and I’m doing late nights and I’m engaging in unhealthy behaviors to meet these deadlines. And so I don’t know if I ever have time to stop and think too deeply about what is the best strategy to do this.

Catalina furthers this, explaining that neurodivergent students often don’t have the time to be themselves, nor do they have time to begin to take care of their mental health due to expectations that they should behave neurotypically:

there were so many things that just were compounding all the time- that like, I just felt not like me. And as time has passed, and as I’m out of that incredibly stressful situation that college is, I have learned that like I was never really who I am, you know… I never gave myself the time to fully explore, like who I am, and be able to cope with the things that I was feeling rather than just like shoving it down and being like ’you’re good you’re fine’

It is critical that we keep the tax that we, as educators, often impose on neurodivergent students when we don’t provide the resources they need to be successful.

This paper outlines what the phenomenon of neurodivergent undergraduate physics identity looks like. They define their identity based on comparison between their peers, intense interest in physics, and a constant wrestling between belief that physics identity is something anyone who studies physics can claim, and something to be earned. This paper also uncovers worrying aspects of neurodivergent undergraduate physicists’ identity construction. Neurodivergent undergraduates are often subject to well-intentioned Communities of Practice, which quickly turn into Communities of Perception. They are regularly turned away from accessing disability accommodations. Perhaps most worrying, their identity development often comes from belief in random success, as opposed to being competent in physics.

There are steps we can make, as departments, administrators, and as individual actors, to encourage the identity development of our burgeoning neurodivergent physicists. So many subjects indicated that it was individual allies who leverage their own power to alter their course structure, or individually accommodate students, often engaging in direct action contrary to ODS recommendations. Furthermore, engaging and supporting minority spaces in departments appears to help these students find community and share resources to find success in class. Most of all, having and maintaining an open dialogue with our students at all levels is necessary for creating a healthy environment which encourages the growth of all students. To quote Tom’s final remarks in his interview, "[neurodiversity] is a complex and unique situation for each student. Everyone’s a little bit different and neurodivergence really kinda can turn up those differences… but patience is probably the best medicine."
