Cosmic rays from massive star clusters : A close look at Westerlund 1 2020 Cosmic rays from massive star clusters : A close look at Westerlund 1 –
Sourav Bhadra, ABC

Abstract
We study cosmic ray (CR) acceleration in the massive compact star cluster Westerlund 1 in light of its recent detection in γ \gamma -rays. We use 1-D two-fluid hydrodynamical simulations to calculate the CR energy density profiles in the case of CR acceleration in the central region and/or in the wind termination shock, to best match the observed projected γ \gamma -ray luminosity and CR energy density profiles. We find that the inferred CR energy density profiles from observations of γ \gamma -ray luminosity and mass can be much different from the true radial profile. We also revised the error estimates of the inferred CR energy density profile and we find that (a) a 1 / r 1/r profile is not necessarily indicated and (b) CR acceleration at either the central region or the wind termination shock can explain the observations, if the diffusion coefficient is κ C ​ R ∼ 10 27 \kappa_{CR}\sim 10^{27} cm 2 s -1 and a fraction of ≈ 20 % \approx 20\% of the shock power/post-shock pressure is deposited into the CR component. We also study the possibility of discrete supernovae (SN) explosions being responsible for CR acceleration, but find that while γ \gamma -ray observations can allow a SN rate of ∼ 0.03 \sim 0.03 per Myr, the X-ray signatures rule out multiple SN case.



1
Introduction

There is a recent surge of interest in the acceleration of cosmic rays (CRs) in massive star clusters, which are increasingly seen as a supplementary site of CR acceleration in our Galaxy besides isolated supernova remnants.
The
\gamma
-ray observations by
Fermi-LAT, HESS,
and
HAWC
have provided the evidence of hadronic acceleration in handful of massive clusters. For instance, the Cygnus OB association, Westerlund 1, Westerlund 2 in our Galaxy and 30 Doradus in the Large Magellanic Cloud are bright sources of
\gamma
-rays from GeV to several TeV energies and have been interpreted as the powerful CR accelerators (
Abdo et al. 2010
;
Ackermann et al. 2011
;
Abramowski et al. 2015
;
Abeysekara et al. 2021
).
Although star forming regions have been previously discussed as possible sources of CRs (e.g.,
Knödlseder 2013
;
Bykov 2014
;
Aharonian et al 2018
), these
\gamma
-ray observations have strengthened the hypothesis and they allow us to make more detailed theoretical models and thereby to improve our understanding of CR acceleration in these environments.

The idea that massive star clusters are potential acceleration sites of CR has long been discussed in the literature, beginning with energetic arguments
(
Cesarsky & Montmerle 1983
)
.
CR acceleration in these environments can also solve many problems associated with the isolated supernova paradigm including proton acceleration up to
\sim
PeV energies (representing the knee of the Galatic CR spectrum) and the excess of
22
Ne/
20
Ne compared to the standard ISM composition (
Higdon & Lingenfelter 2003
, for a review see
Gabici et al. 2019
). Detailed theoretical investigations support these predictions (e.g.,
Gupta et al. 2020
, Morlino et al 2021).
In this regard,
Gupta et al. 2020
have demonstrated that the problem of the large observed ratio of Neon isotopes (
22
Ne/
20
Ne) can be solved by invoking CR acceleration in stellar winds in star clusters.
Concurrently, it has been suggested from various phenomenological considerations that most of the observed CR grammage in the Galaxy is accumulated in star clusters, and not while propagating through the interstellar medium (ISM) at large (see, e.g.,
Blasi & Serpico 2009
;
Cowsik & Madziwa-Nussinov 2016
;
Eichler 2017
;
Biermann et al. 2018
). Taking this cue,
Nath & Eichler 2020
have shown that the resulting
\gamma
-rays rom star clusters can explain a significant fraction of the observed diffuse Galactic background.
These developments prod us to look deeper at the individual and detailed observations of star clusters.

Recently, using the
\gamma
-ray observations of Cygnus and Westerlund 1 (hereafter referred to as Wd1) and CO/HI observations (REF),
Aharonian et al. 2019
reported that the spatial distribution of CR energy density in these objects follows
1/r
profile. They suggested a steady injection (
\sim
few Myr) of CRs instead of instantaneous injection as normally expected in case of an isolated supernova.Although such profiles can be derived by solving steady state CR transport equation (cf. §
2
), it is worth mentioning that the steady state assumption is questionable when the structure continuously evolves. We subject these observations to scrutiny with two-fluid hydro simulation and check if other interpretations (of the actual CR energy density profile and the mode of CR injection) are ruled out and if the observations can be used to infer the relevant physical parameters for CR acceleration.

In the present work, we have studied different CR injection methods in order to understand the observed
\gamma
-ray luminosity, mass and CR energy density of the Wd1 cluster. We select Wd1 for our study mainly because it is a compact cluster and can be modelled convincingly using 1-D simulations. Although there are a few other clusters which have been detected in
\gamma
-rays (such as Cygnus
(
Bartoli et al. 2014
)
), those objects are distinctly non-spherical in morphology and have substructures, which make them difficult to compare with simulations. We begin with analytical estimates of
\gamma
-ray luminosity in section 2. The numerical simulation set-up is described in section 3. In section 4 we discuss our results followed by further discussions in section 5, and we summarize in section 6.

2
Preliminaries

Aharonian et al. 2019
found that the CR energy density, as estimated from
\gamma
-ray luminosity and mass (inferred by CO emission) in different annuli around the Wd1 cluster, shows a
1/r
profile.
This has been interpreted by them in terms of a steady state between the injection of CRs (with energy
E
) in the central regions and their diffusion, with diffusion coefficient
D(E)
for that energy value.

This reduces the CR transport equation to a partial differential equation in two variables, space and time (instead
of three, including particle energy), given by

{\partial N(E)\over\partial t}=D\nabla^{2}N(E)+Q

(1)

can be re-written as

{\partial e_{\rm cr}\over\partial t}=D\nabla^{2}e_{\rm cr}+{L\over V}

(2)

where
e_{\rm cr}
represents CR energy density and
{L\over V}
the CR luminosity density. Let us assume
that CR particles are injected in a small central region, of radius
r_{0}
, which is much
smaller in extent than the size of the star cluster. Therefore, except for the very central
region, we need to solve the equation

{\partial e_{cr}\over\partial t}=D\nabla^{2}e_{cr}\,.

(3)

In steady state, it reduces to (in spherical symmetry)

{d\over dr}\Bigl(r^{2}{d\over dr}e_{cr}\Bigr)=0\,,\,\Rightarrow e_{cr}\propto\int{dr\over r^{2}}\,,

(4)

which has the solution
e_{cr}={A\over r}+B
, where
B\rightarrow 0
since the CR energy density is zero at infinity,
and where
A
is a constant that depends on the boundary condition at
r_{0}
. This is the
1/r
solution referred to by
Aharonian et al. 2019
.

Note that the above estimate neglects losses due to adiabatic expansion of the gas and CRs, which is relevant for the case of massive
star cluster, because stellar wind produces gradient in thermal and CR pressures. In order to study the effect of
the expanding super-bubble on the CR population, and to calculate the resulting
\gamma
-ray production, we use numerical
simulations for our study. In addition, our approach allows us to consider the wind termination shock as an acceleration site, as
well as to study the effect of time varying CR/mechanical luminosity of the star cluster including discrete SNe.

2.1.1
Hadronic contribution

The major source of
\gamma

\gamma

\gamma

p+p\rightarrow p+p+\pi^{0}\,,\quad\pi^{0}\rightarrow\gamma+\gamma.

(5)

Therefore, observation of
\gamma

To estimate
\gamma

), which yields the luminosity between
(E_{\gamma 1}
and

\displaystyle L_{\gamma}^{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{V}dV\,n_{N}(r)\,e_{cr}(r)\left[\int_{E_{\gamma 1}}^{E_{\gamma 2}}dE_{\gamma}\,E_{\gamma}\,\tilde{q_{\gamma}}\right].

(6)

Here,
q_{\gamma}=dN/(dt\,dV\,dE_{\gamma})
is the number of
\gamma
-ray photons emitted per unit volume per unit time per unit energy, which is proportional to the number density of target nucleon (
n_{N}
) and the CR energy density (
e_{cr}
). The integration is to be carried over the entire volume of the emission region. The isotropic source function
\tilde{q_{\gamma}}
, used in the second equality, is given as
(
Gupta et al. 2018
)
,

\tilde{q_{\gamma}}=\frac{\sigma_{pp}c\left(\frac{E_{\pi}^{0}}{GeV}\right)^{-\alpha_{\gamma}}\left[\left(\frac{2E_{\gamma}}{E_{\pi^{0}}}\right)^{\delta_{\gamma}}+\left(\frac{2E_{\gamma}}{E_{\pi^{0}}}\right)^{-\delta_{\gamma}}\right]^{-\frac{\alpha_{\gamma}}{\delta_{\gamma}}}}{\xi^{\alpha_{\gamma}-2}\left(\frac{3\alpha_{\gamma}}{4}\right)\left(\frac{E_{p}}{(2\alpha_{p}-2){\rm GeV}}\right)\left(\frac{E_{p}}{\rm GeV}\right)^{1-\alpha_{p}}\beta\left(\frac{\alpha_{p}-2}{2},\frac{3-\alpha_{p}}{2}\right)}

(7)

Here,
\xi=2
is the multiplicity factor, which denotes two leading pion jets leaving the interaction site,
E_{p}
and
E_{\pi^{0}}
are the rest mass energy of proton and pions (
\pi^{0}
) respectively. Also, the spectral indices of
the incident CR protons and emitted
\gamma
-ray photons are denoted by

\alpha_{p}
and
\alpha_{\gamma}
respectively,
\delta_{\gamma}=0.14\alpha_{\gamma}^{-1.6}+0.44
is the spectral shape parameter and
\sigma_{pp}=32(0.96+e^{4.4-2.4\alpha_{\gamma}})

mbarn (for details see equations (8), (19)–(21) in
Pfrommer & Enßlin 2004
). We use
\alpha_{\gamma}=\alpha_{p}=2.3
following the spectral fit of
Ackermann et al. 2015

\gamma

6

L_{\gamma}^{H}=1.05\times 10^{-17}\left(\frac{\Delta V}{$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{3}$}\right)\left(\frac{n_{N}}{$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$}\right)\left(\frac{e_{cr}}{$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$}\right)$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$\,.

We use this equation to calculate the

2.1.2
Leptonic contribution

It is also possible to have a leptonic contribution to the total
\gamma
-ray luminosity, from inverse Compton scattering of stellar radiation photons by CR electrons, especially in the inner regions. We estimate the leptonic emission in the following manner. The energy density of CR electrons is assumed to be
0.01
of the total CR energy density (i.e
e_{cr,e}\approx 0.01e_{cr}
). This value has some uncertainty. From observations in the solar system, at CR energy
\sim 10

\mathrm{G}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
, where solar modulation effects are low, the ratio of CR electron to proton energy is known to be
1\%
(
Longair 2011
section 15.1,
Schlickeiser 2002
). Assuming the energy distribution of CR electrons to be
n(\Gamma)=\kappa_{e}\Gamma^{-p}
(in terms of the Lorentz factor
\Gamma
), where
p=2.3
(same as that of protons), the normalization constant
\kappa_{1}
is given by

\kappa_{1}\approx{e_{\rm cr,e}\over m_{e}c^{2}}(p-2)\Bigl[{1\over\Gamma_{L}^{p-2}}-{1\over\Gamma_{U}^{p-2}}\Bigr]^{-1}\,.

(10)

Here, the upper cutoff to the Lorentz factor can be taken as
\Gamma_{U}\rightarrow\infty
and the lower cutoff (
\Gamma_{L}
), as unity. Then, the total IC luminosity (which provides an upper limit to
\gamma
-ray luminosity) is given by (
Rybicki & Lightman 1979
, eqn 7.21),

L_{\gamma}^{IC}=\int_{V}\,dV\,\Bigl[{4\over 3}\sigma_{T}\,c\,e_{\rm ph}\,\kappa_{1}\,{\Gamma_{\rm max}^{3-p}-\Gamma_{\rm min}^{3-p}\over 3-p}\Bigr]\,,

(11)

where,
e_{\rm ph}
is the photon energy density, which at a distance
r
from the central star cluster is given by

e_{\rm ph}=\frac{L_{rad}}{4\pi r^{2}c}.

(12)

Hence one can obtain an upper limit to the leptonic contribution by using equations
10
,
11
&
12
.

Using these equations, one gets a sharply declining profile of
L_{\gamma}
with projected distance, because of the rapid decline of
e_{ph}
with radius. This is in contrast with the observed increasing profile of
L_{\gamma}
with projected distance. The observed profile, therefore, works against the Leptonic interpretation of the origin of
\gamma
-rays. The following reasons may be responsible for the suppression of the Leptonic contribution.

Since IC scattering boosts the seed photon energy by a factor of
\Gamma^{2}
(
\Gamma
being the electron Lorentz factor), a seed (stellar) photon of
\sim 1
eV will require
\Gamma=10^{6}
for it to be scattered into
1
TeV energy. If we take the photons in the waveband
0.01\hbox{--}100
eV (FIR to FUV), then the total radiation luminosity of the cluster is given by
L_{rad}\sim 500L_{w}

(
Leitherer et al. 1999
)
, where
L_{w}
denotes the mechanical luminosity. In the innermost region considered here, within
9
pc, the photon energy density amounts to
\approx 1135
eV/cc.
Therefore, the IC cooling time of such electrons is
\sim m_{e}c^{2}/[\Gamma\sigma_{T}e_{\rm ph}c]\sim 700
years only. Alternatively, electrons that can survive for
4.5
Myr have
\Gamma\leq 120
, which require a seed photon energy of
\geq 69.5
MeV in order to up-scatter into
1
TeV. Therefore we must consider high energy stellar photons (which are rare, being in the Wien tail) that have much smaller energy density and a longer cooling time.

To estimate this, we recall that the stellar radiation field has a black-body spectrum, with the peak corresponding to the most massive OB stars, with surface temperature
\approx 40000
K, and therefore with a peak at
h\nu_{\rm max}\approx 4
eV. The spectrum above this energy in the Wien’s part falls as
\exp(-h\nu/4\,{\rm eV})
. Since we know that the luminosity in Lyman continuum photons (
h\nu>13.6
eV) is
\sim

65

L_{w}

(
Leitherer et al. 1999
)
, we can estimate the luminosity for photons in the Wein’s part accordingly.

By iteration, we find that photons above
\approx 34.0
eV are relevant in the
0\hbox{--}9
pc region, for leptonic contribution above
1
TeV, for electrons with
\Gamma\leq 1.83\times 10^{5}
(which survive for
\geq 4.5
Myr). The corresponding photon energy density is
0.9
eV/cc (still larger than CMB, by the way). Following the same procedure, we find for the
9\hbox{--}18
pc region, the relevant photon energy to be
\geq 28.5
eV, with electron
\Gamma\leq 1.85\times 10^{5}
. We calculated the leptonic
\gamma
-ray contribution in this manner for other annuli, and found that it is much smaller than the hadronic contribution.

3
Numerical set up

We use the publicly available magneto-hydrodynamics code, PLUTO
(
Mignone et al. 2007
)
, which supports CRs as a fluid detailed in
(
Gupta, Sharma, & Mignone 2021
)
. PLUTO is a finite-volume Godunov code based on Riemann solvers, designed to integrate a system of conservation laws of fluid dynamics which adopts a structured mesh. In this work, the code solves the following set of equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{v})=S_{\rho},

(14a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho\vec{v})}{\partial t}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\vec{v}\otimes\vec{v})+\vec{\nabla}(p_{th}+p_{cr})=\rho\vec{g},

(14b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(e_{th}+e_{k})}{\partial t}+\vec{\nabla}[(e_{th}+e_{k})\vec{v}]+\vec{\nabla}[\vec{v}(p_{th}+p_{cr})]

\displaystyle=p_{cr}\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{F}_{tc}+q_{\rm eff}+S_{th}+\rho\vec{v}\cdot\vec{g},

(14c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial e_{cr}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot[e\vec{v}]=-p_{cr}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}-\nabla\cdot\vec{F}_{\rm{crdiff}}+S_{cr},

(14d)

where
\rho
is the mass density,
v
is the fluid velocity,
e_{cr}
is the CR energy density,
S_{\rho}
and
S_{cr}
are the source terms per unit time per unit volume,
\vec{F}_{\rm{crdiff}}
represents CR diffusion flux, and
q_{\rm eff}
accounts for the radiative energy loss of the thermal gas. We have used HLL Riemann solver, piecewise linear reconstruction and RK2 time stepping. In our simulation, we use a CFL number of 0.4 and 1D spherical geometry.

3.3
CR injection

We consider three methods of CR injection in our simulations. In the first case, CRs are injected in the wind driving region i.e within
r_{inj}
. In the second case, CRs are injected in the shocked zones. The last case is a combination of both. These injection regions can be seen as possible CR acceleration sites in this object, where the central injection represents unresolved regions, e.g., colliding winds, which can also accelerate CRs
(
Eichler1993;Bykov2014
)
. We use the following three conditions to identify the shocked zones (
Pfrommer et al. 2017
,
Gupta, Sharma, & Mignone 2021
),

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{v}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0,

(17)

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}p\cdot\frac{\Delta r}{p}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\delta_{threshold},

(18)

\displaystyle\vec{\nabla}{T}\cdot\vec{\nabla}{\rho}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0\,.

(19)

Here,
\vec{v},~p,~\rho,
and
T
are the velocity, pressure, density and temperature of the fluid. The first condition selects compressed zones, the second condition sets the pressure jump at the shock, and the third condition avoids the contact discontinuity. In the third injection method, we use the combined injection of CRs in the wind driving region as well as the shocked region.

For all these injection methods CR injection does not add any additional energy in the computational domain. The injection parameter (
\epsilon_{cr}
or
w_{cr}
) just distributes a fraction of the total mechanical energy in the CRs either in the wind driving region or in the shocked regions.

3.4
Microphysics

3.4.1
CR Diffusion

Our simulations include the effects of CR diffusion. For numerical stability, diffusion typically has a much smaller time step than the CFL time step. We therefore choose STS method
(
Alexiades et al. 2019
)

4
Results

We present our results in this section and then discuss the implications in section 5.

4.1
Structure of star cluster driven bubble

Stars clusters host massive stars as well as supernova explosions, which lead to the formation of a low density bubble around them (
Weaver et al. 1977
;
Gupta et al. 2018
). Although the overall size of these bubbles (a few tens of pc) depends mainly on the total mechanical luminosity deposited by the cluster and the ambient density, the interior structure can qualitatively differ depending on whether the energy deposition is dominated by winds or SN explosions. We discuss these differences below.

The shocked interstellar medium (ISM) and shocked wind regions are separated by a contact discontinuity (CD).

4.2.2
Injection at the shock

Next, we consider the case of CR injection at strong shocks. We mainly consider injection at the wind termination shock (hereafter WTS) as the Mach number of WTS is much larger than the forward shock (hereafter FS); i.e WTS is stronger than FS. \mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

5
Discussion

Our simulated
\gamma
-ray luminosity and mass profiles match the observations (panels (b) and (c) of figure
A4
), for the parameter values mentioned in each case of CR injection . We also note that the inferred CR energy density offers a good match with the observed profiles, in light of the revised error bars that include the uncertainty in mass estimation (panel (d) in each row of figure
A4
). However, we should keep in mind that the simulations are based on some simple assumptions such as spherical symmetry, constant diffusion coefficient etc. More realistic 3-D simulations may yield different results. However, it is reasonable to say that the present simulations offer a good match with the observations, in light of all the uncertainties mentioned earlier.

There are other circumstantial reasons why a flatter CR energy density profile should be considered.
Recently
Abeysekara et al. 2021
have showed (in their figure 2b) that for Cygnus cluster, the CR energy density above
10

\mathrm{T}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
does not strictly follow a
1/r
profile, and their observation does not rule out
e_{cr,inf}
being uniform, which would make it consistent with our simulation results (panel (a) in each row of figure
A4
). At the same time, the CR energy density profile for
100

\mathrm{G}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
does follow
1/r
profile
(
Aharonian et al. 2019
)
.
Abeysekara et al. 2021
interpreted this absence of a
1/r
profile for
\mathrm{T}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
CRs on the basis of larger diffusion rate for higher energy CRs.

The comparison of
L_{\gamma}
and
e_{cr,inf}
from our simulation and observations indicate that the last projected bin is observed to be less luminous than expected from simulation. There can be a variety of reasons for this discrepancy. One possibility is that the outer shell is fragmented and is porous, as in the case of 30 Doradus, for example (which allows the X-ray from shocked wind region to be seen through the holes in the outer shell). Such a fragmented outer shell may make the
\gamma
-ray luminosity in the outer-most bin discrepant from the simulated values.

5.3
Heating due to CRs and CR energy loss

Note that we have not included CR energy loss and
thermal conduction
(can thermal conduction increase the projected gamma ray luminosity by enhancing the interior density? it can be a good point to make; but I fear this can introduce another degeneracy, let’s check it for us and if it seems important then only include.)
in our simulations. CR energy loss, due to Coulomb and hadronic interactions, can indeed be important, as estimated below. Using the expressions for Coulomb and hadronic loss in
Guo & Oh 2008
, the total CR energy loss rate is,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}=7.6\times 10^{-16}\left(\frac{n}{$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$}\right)\,\left(\frac{e_{cr}}{$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$}\right)\,$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}$\,.

(24)

The heating time for the gas is,

\displaystyle t_{H}\approx\frac{1.5nkT}{(1.65\times 10^{-16}\,n\,e_{cr})}\,$\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{c}$\,,

(25)

considering only the Coulomb interaction. Using
e_{cr}\sim
0.45
\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}
,
T=10^{4}
K (corresponds to shell temperature), the heating time scale is
t_{H}\sim 10^{9}

\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
. This heating time is much larger than the dynamical time scale of
4.5
Myr, so the effect of this heating is negligible for the thermal gas.

However, the energy loss time scale for CRs is,

t_{\rm cr,loss}\approx 0.4\,{\rm Myr}\,\Bigl({n\over 50\,m_{H}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}}\Bigr)^{-1}\,.

(26)

We can also estimate the energy loss due to CR streaming heating, for which the heating rate is given by,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm streaming}=-v_{A}\cdot\nabla p_{cr}\,\,$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$\,.

(27)

Here,
p_{cr}
is the CR pressure and
v_{A}
is the Alfven velocity. If we assume equipartition of magnetic and thermal energy density, then
v_{A}\approx 1.3\times 10^{8}

\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
. If we consider the region between
20\hbox{--}50
pc in the density plot (panel (a) of the topmost row of figure
A4
, we find that the gradient of CR pressure (
\Delta p_{cr}
) is
\approx 1.8\times 10^{-11}

\mathrm{d}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{e}\,\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
over a distance (
\Delta r
) of
30
pc. This gives us,
\Gamma_{streaming}\approx 2.6\times 10^{-23}\,$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$
. The energy loss for CR is long, but the heating time scale for the gas is
\sim 0.2

\mathrm{M}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
, (for
n\approx 0.01

\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-3}
). Although this may be important, we have not included streaming heating in our simulations because it will involve making assumptions of magnetic field.

The above discussion, especially regarding the energy loss time scale for CR (equation
26
), shows that CR energy density in the shocked wind and outer shell can significantly decrease over the considered dynamical time scale. This process would decrease the CR energy density in these regions, and consequently decrease
L_{\gamma}
. Therefore,
L_{\gamma}
would be lower than presented here, especially in the outer bins, and make the inferred CR energy density decline with projected distance. This may result in a better match with the observations.

6
Conclusions

We have studied the implication of the recently inferred distribution of CR energy density in massive compact star clusters, taking the particular example of Wd1.
With 1D numerical hydro-dynamical simulation for stellar wind in star clusters, we have studied the projected
\gamma
-ray luminosity, mass and CR energy density for Wd1 cluster and their dependence on diffusion coefficient, injection parameter and ambient density. Our findings are as follows:

•

(1) The most important takeaway from our analysis is that the inferred
1/r
profile of CR energy
density need not reflect its true radial profile. Also, we have shown that even the observed data can accommodate a flatter CR energy density profile, in light of revised error estimates. We have shown that dividing the projected
L_{\gamma}
by the projected mass in different annuli can yield a CR energy density profile that is significantly different from the actual profile. We have also pointed out various uncertainties that would make a straightforward inference difficult,
e.g.
, the lack of morphological symmetry, the uncertainty in mass estimate etc.

•

(2) While a
1/r
profile for the CR energy density allows a simple explanation in terms of a steady state CR luminosity at the centre of the cluster, which makes it appealing, we have studied the cases of more complex scenarios, that of a time-varying CR luminosity, or CR being injected outside the central region (in the wind termination shock, for example), and showed how these scenarios can also be discussed in terms of the observed data. With regard to the site of CR acceleration, we can not rule out any of the CR acceleration process on the basis of these observations because the observed luminosity and mass profile can be explained by all three CR injection methods, as well as discrete supernova case by appropriate choice of diffusion coefficient and injection parameters.

•

(3) The parameters for the best match with observations are not ad-hoc, but are supported by independent arguments. For example, a lower value of diffusion coefficient (
10^{27}
cm
2
s
-1
) can explain the observation for shock injection case, while for central injection a higher value (
3\times 10^{27}
cm
2
s
-1
) is required. These values for the diffusion coefficient is consistent with previous findings. The same goes for the parameter describing the efficiency of CR injection, which is found to be in the range
\epsilon_{cr}\sim 0.1\hbox{--}0.3
, consistent with previous works
(
Gupta et al. 2018
)
.

•

(4) The discrete multiple supernova injection method can also explain the observation with appropriate choice of parameters.
