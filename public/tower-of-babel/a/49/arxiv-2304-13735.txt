Abstract
. Let U ​ ( n , 𝔽 q 2 ) \textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}) denote the subgroup of unitary matrices of the general linear group GL ​ ( n , 𝔽 q 2 ) \textup{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}) which fixes a Hermitian form and M ≥ 2 M\geq 2 an integer. This is a companion paper to the previous works [ 5 ] and [ 9 ] where the elements of the groups GL ​ ( n , 𝔽 q ) \textup{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}) , Sp ​ ( 2 ​ n , 𝔽 q ) \textup{Sp}({2n},\mathbb{F}_{q}) , O ± ​ ( 2 ​ n , 𝔽 q ) \textup{O}^{\pm}({2n},\mathbb{F}_{q}) and O ​ ( 2 ​ n + 1 , 𝔽 q ) \textup{O}({2n+1},\mathbb{F}_{q}) which has an M M -th root in the concerned group, have been described. Here we will describe the M M -th powers in unitary groups for the regular semisimple, semisimple and cyclic elements. Our methods are parallel to those of the Memoir “A generating function approach to the enumeration of matrices in classical groups over finite fields” by Fulman, Neumann and Praeger.

1.
Introduction

After the settlement of Ore’s conjecture,
Waring type problems
in group theory has emerged as an important area of research, posing new difficulties and beautiful results. This subject has a long history, which dates back to the work of Armand Borel, e.g. Borel dominance theorem. Given an element
w\in\mathcal{F}_{l}
(the free group on
l
generators), the map associated with
w
by plugging elements of
G^{l}
in
w
, is called a
word map
. It was proved by A. Borel (in
[
1
]
) (and later by Larsen independently in
[
6
]
) that given a semisimple algebraic group
\mathcal{G}
and a word map
w\colon\mathcal{G}^{l}\longrightarrow\mathcal{G}
, it is a dominant map (that is, image is dense in
\mathcal{G}
with respect to the Zariski topology). The image of
w
will be denoted as
w(\mathcal{G})
hereafter as is the tradition in the subject. The result due to Borel reveals the surprising fact that
w(\mathcal{G})^{2}=\mathcal{G}
. It is therefore natural to ask what happens when
\mathcal{G}
is replaced by an arbitrary group. The general question in this direction is as follows.

Question 1.1
.

Let
G
be a group and
w
be a word. Then

(1)

Is
w
a surjective map on
G
?

(2)

If
w
is not a surjective map is there a constant
k_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w(G)^{k_{w}}=\langle w(G)\rangle
.

Several results have been proved in this direction in the last three decades. For example, it was shown first that for a finite simple group
G
, there exists a constant
c
such that
G=w(G)^{c}
, where
w=x_{1}x_{2}x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
(see
[
14
]
by J. Wilson). Later by collective effort, it was shown that
c
can be chosen to be
1
. This is essentially Ore’s conjecture. Around 1996, Martinez and Zelmanov proved that any element of a sufficiently large
simple group can be written as a product of
f(k)
many
k
-th powers in
[
8
]
. This was also independently proved by Saxl and Wilson in
[
10
]
. This simple-looking result has far-reaching consequences. For example, in the paper
[
10
]
the authors prove that if
G
is a Cartesian product of nonabelian finite simple groups with
G
being a finitely generated profinite group, then every subgroup
H
of finite index in
G
is open.
Over ten years back A. Shalev proved that for a word
w
, there exists positive integer
N(w)
such that every finite simple group
G
having size more than
N(w)
satisfies
w(G)^{3}=G
(see
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
). Later this was improved to the result that
w(G)^{2}=G
for sufficiently large finite simple groups of Lie type
G
, depending upon
w
, by Larsen and Larsen in
[
7
]
. In fact, there are words
w
for which
w(G)\neq G
, and one of the primary examples is a power map.

Fix a positive integer
M\geq 2
. In this article, we choose to focus on the word
x^{M}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
. The corresponding word map will be called
power map
. Our main focus will be to compute probabilities of an element of a particular type to be
M
-th power. We call an element
g\in G
to be
M
-th power if there exists
\alpha\in G
such that
\alpha^{M}=G
. This has been of prime interest for quite some time now. We started looking at this through the approach of statistical group theory. This enables us to answer this question in terms of generating functions. This has been done in
[
5
]
for finite general linear groups, and in
[
9
]
for finite symplectic and orthogonal groups. We will answer this question for unitary groups here. We start with the
description of conjugacy classes and centralizers of an element in
section 2
. This will be used to deduce the problem in the realm of counting conjugacy classes of a particular type, rather than counting elements of a particular type. In
section 3

we define the
\widetilde{M}
-power polynomial which will help us
deduce which conjugacy classes are the
M
-th powers. Later on in
section 4
,
section 5
and
section 6
we deduce the main results.

2.
Conjugacy classes and centralizer in unitary groups

This section briefly recalls the definitions and mentions the results about conjugacy classes and centralizers of elements in unitary groups.
The exposition here is mainly based on the works
[
13
]
by G. E. Wall and
[
12
]
by D. E. Taylor.
For a prime
p
consider
q=p^{l}
for some
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the field
k=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, and the map
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
defined as
\sigma(a):=\overline{a}=a^{q}
which is an order two automorphism of the field. This further induces an automorphism of

\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t]
, the polynomial ring over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. The image of

f\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t]
will be denoted as
\overline{f}
. Consider the Hermitian form given by the matrix

\Lambda_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&1\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
1&0&\ldots&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then we identify the unitary group (corresponding to the above Hermitian form) with the set

\textup{U}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})=\left\{A\in\textup{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})\mid A\Lambda_{n}\overline{A}^{t}=\Lambda_{n}\right\}.

Since all non-degenerate Hermitian forms on a
k^{n}
are unitarily congruent to the standard one that is the Hermitian form given by the above matrix,
we get that all the unitary groups arising are conjugate with each other inside

\textup{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
(see theorem
10.3
of
[
4
]
). From the work of Wall, it is known that two elements
g_{1},g_{2}\in\textup{U}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
are conjugate to each other if and only if they are conjugate in
\textup{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
.
These conjugacy classes are parameterized by special polynomials and partitions.
We call a polynomial
f
of degree
d
to be
\sim
-symmetric
if
\widetilde{f}=f
where
\widetilde{f}(t)=\overline{f(0)}^{-1}t^{d}\bar{f}(t^{-1})
. A polynomial without a proper
\sim
-symmetric factor will be called a
\sim
-irrdeucible
polynomial. Note that a
\sim
-irreducible polynomial can be reducible in the usual sense.
Denote by
\widetilde{\Phi}
to be the set of all
\sim
-irreducible polynomials.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of all partitions of numbers
\leq n
. Then a conjugacy class in
\textup{U}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
is determined by a function
\lambda\colon\widetilde{\Phi}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{n}
which satisfies

(1)

\lambda(f)=\lambda(\widetilde{f})
,

(2)

\sum\limits_{\phi}|\lambda(\phi)|\deg\phi=n
.

We recall the definitions of several kinds of matrices here, for which the existence of
M
-th root will be discussed in this article. A matrix
A
is said to be
separable
if its characteristic polynomial is separable over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
,
semisimple
when the minimal polynomial is separable. Call a matrix

A
to be cyclic if the minimal polynomial is same as the characteristic polynomial
of
A
.

3.
Special polynomials

For our work, we need to deal with certain kinds of polynomials which we describe now.

Definition 3.1
.

For an integer
M\geq 2
, a self-conjugate irreducible monic (SCIM) polynomial
f
of degree
d
will be called a
\widetilde{M}
-power polynomial
if
f(x^{M})
has a SCIM factor
g
of degree
d
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)
denote the number of
\widetilde{M}
-power SCIM polynomials of degree
d
. Then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)=\dfrac{1}{d(M,q^{2d}-1)}\sum\limits_{l|d}\mu(l)\left(M(q^{2d/l}-1),q^{d}+1\right).

Proof.

Let
f
be an
\widetilde{M}
-power SCIM polynomial of degree
d
. Then
f(x^{M})
has a SCIM factor of degree
d
, say
g
.
Now consider
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}[x]
. Then we get that

\displaystyle f(x)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(x-\alpha_{i})\,\text{ and }\,g(x)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(x-\beta_{i}).

Note that we may assume that
\beta_{i}^{M}=\alpha_{i}
, after a permutation of the indices.
Consider the map
\theta_{M}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}
given by
\theta_{M}(\delta)=\delta^{M}
. Then
\alpha_{i}\in\textup{Im}(\theta)
.
Since
f
is an SCIM polynomial we also have that
\alpha_{i}^{q^{d}+1}=1
.
Hence we have that

\displaystyle\beta_{i}^{M(q^{d}+1)=\beta_{i}^{q^{2d}-1}=1},

and that
\beta_{i}^{M}=\alpha_{i}
generates
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

Conversely suppose
\alpha
satisfies
\alpha^{q^{d}+1}=1
and generates
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. If
\varphi
is the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, then
it is of degree
d
. Also all roots
\eta
of
\varphi
satisfy that
\eta^{q^{d}+1}=1
. Thus if
\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)
denotes the number of
\widetilde{M}
-power SCIM polynomials, we will have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)=\dfrac{1}{d}\left|\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}\middle|\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha^{q^{d}+1}=1,\alpha=\theta_{M}(\beta),\\
\beta^{q^{d}+1}=1,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}(\alpha)\end{subarray}\right\}\right|.

Since
\lvert\theta_{M}^{-1}(1)\rvert=(M,q^{2d}-1)
, we get that

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)=\dfrac{1}{d(M,q^{2d}-1)}\left\lvert\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}\middle|\beta^{q^{d}+1}=1,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}(\beta^{M})\right\}\right\rvert.

To ensure that
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}(\beta^{M})
, we must have that
\beta^{M}\not\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{d/l}}
for all
l|d
where
l>1
. To count this, define

\displaystyle E_{l}=\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}\middle|\beta^{q^{d}+1}=1,\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d/l}}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}(\beta^{M})\right\}.

Then we have that
\lvert E_{l}\rvert=(M(q^{2d/l}-1),q^{d+1})
. Hence by the inclusion-exclusion principle, we get that

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)=\dfrac{1}{d(M,q^{2d}-1)}\sum\limits_{l|d}\mu(l)\left(M(q^{2d/l}-1),q^{d}+1\right)

∎

4.
Separable matrices

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f
be an SCIM polynomial of odd degree
d\geq 1
and
\alpha^{M}=C_{f}
. Then
f(x^{M})
has a SCIM factor of degree
d
.

Proof.

Considering
C_{f}
in
\textup{U}({d},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}})
we have that
C_{f}\sim\textup{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
, since it is semisimple. Given that
\alpha^{M}=C_{f}
, we can assume
\alpha
to be semisimple.
Then
\alpha\sim\textup{diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
where
\alpha_{i}
’s are roots of minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. Then
\alpha_{i}^{M}=\lambda_{j(i)}
. We may assume that
j
is the identity function, after permutation.

Hence
f(\alpha_{i}^{M})=0
. Considering
H(x)=f(x^{M})
we get that
H(\alpha_{i})=0
. Hence
\min_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha)|H
. Since
g=\min_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha)|H
is irreducible and
\alpha\in\textup{U}({d},\mathbb{F}_{q^{q}})
, we get that
g
is SCIM and is the required polynomial.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
be a SCIM polynomial of odd degree
d
and
f(x^{M})
has a SCIM factor
g
of degree
d
. Then there exists
\alpha\in\textup{U}({d},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
such that
\alpha^{M}=C_{f}
.

Proof.

We will show that
C_{g}^{M}\sim C_{f}
. In
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2d}}
we have that

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\alpha_{i}),

for the appropriate choice of
\alpha_{i}
s and
\lambda_{i}
s. Since
g(x)|f(x^{M})
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\alpha_{1}^{M}-\lambda_{i})=0.

This implies
\alpha_{1}^{M}=\lambda_{j}
for some
j
, which after permutation can be assumed to be
1
. If
h
is characteristic polynomial of
C_{g}^{M}
, then
h(\lambda_{1})=0
. So
f=h
and this finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
cS_{U}^{M}(n,q)
denote the number of conjugacy classes of separable matrices in
\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
which are
M
-th power. Let
cS_{U}^{M}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}cS_{U}^{M}(n,q)
. Then we have that

\displaystyle cS_{U}^{M}(z)=\prod_{d=\text{odd}}(1+z^{d})^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\prod_{d\geq 1}(1+z^{2d})^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}.

Proof.

Let
A
be a separable matrix and the combinatorial data attached with
A

is given by
\Delta_{A}=\{(f,\lambda_{f})|f\in\Phi\setminus\{0\}\}
.
Since
A
is separable, we must have that
\lambda_{f}\in\{0,1\}
.
Thus in view of
Lemma

4.1
and
Lemma

4.2
we get that
X
has an
M
-th root if and only if

(1)

for
f=\widetilde{f}
, we have
f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}
,

(2)

for
f\neq\widetilde{f}
, we have
f\in\Phi_{M}\setminus\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}
.

Hence the characteristic polynomial of
X
is given by

\displaystyle\ch_{A}(x)=\prod f\prod g\widetilde{g},

for appropriate choice of
f=\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}
and
g\neq\widetilde{g}\in\Phi_{M}\setminus\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}
, depending upon
A
. Thus using
Lemma

3.2
, we conclude that,

\displaystyle cS_{U}^{M}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}}\left(1+z^{\deg{f}}\right)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{g,\widetilde{g}\}\\
g\in\Phi_{M}\setminus\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}\end{subarray}}\left(1+z^{2\deg{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d=\text{odd}}\left(1+z^{d}\right)^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\prod_{d\geq 1}\left(1+z^{2d}\right)^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
S_{U}^{M}(n,q)
denote the number of separable matrices in
\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
which are
M
-th power. Let
S_{U}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{U}^{M}(n,q)
. Then we have that

\displaystyle S_{U}^{M}(z)=\prod_{d=\text{odd}}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{d}}{q^{d}+1}\right)^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\prod_{d\geq 1}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{2d}}{q^{2d}-1}\right)^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}.

Proof.

We will use the work of Wall (see
[
13
]
) for the computation of the corresponding probability.
Given a matrix
A
with
\ch_{A}(x)=\prod_{i,j}f_{i}g_{j}\widetilde{g_{j}}
for appropriate choices of
f_{i},g_{j}
, we have that the centralizer of
A
is of order

\displaystyle C_{\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})}(A)=\prod_{i}C_{\textup{U}({n_{i}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})}(X_{i})\prod_{j}C_{\textup{U}({2m_{j}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})}(Y_{j}),

for
X_{i}
being element of
\textup{U}({n_{i}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
with
\ch_{X_{i}}(x)=f_{i}
and
Y_{i}\in\textup{U}({2m_{i}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
satisfies
\ch_{Y_{j}}(x)=g_{j}\widetilde{g_{j}}
. Since (See
[
3
]
, page 32)

\displaystyle|C_{\textup{U}({n_{i}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})}(X_{i})|=q^{n_{i}}+1\text{ and }|C_{\textup{U}({2m_{j}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})}(Y_{j})|=q^{2m_{j}-1},

we get the desired result.
∎

5.
Cyclic matrices

We will denote the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}C_{f}&I&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&C_{f}&I&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&C_{f}&I\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&C_{f}\end{pmatrix}
by
U(f,n)
where
C_{f}
denotes the standard companion matrix of
f
in
\textup{U}({\deg f},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
. We will start with the following easy but crucial observation.

Lemma 5.1
.

The matrix
U(f,n)^{M}
is conjugate to the matrix
U(g,n)
where
U(f,1)^{M}\sim U(g,1)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
cC_{U}^{M}(n,q)
denote the number of conjugacy classes of cyclic matrices in
\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
which are
M
-th power. Let
cC_{U}^{M}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}cC_{U}^{M}(n,q)z^{n}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle cC_{U}^{M}(z)=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd}}\left(1-z^{d}\right)^{-\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1-z^{2d}\right)^{-\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

Proof.

In the primary decomposition of a given cyclic matrix
A
, there is a unique summand corresponding to each irreducible factor of
\ch_{A}(x)
. If
\phi
, an irreducible factor of
\ch_{A}(x)
satisfies
\widetilde{\phi}=\phi
, then the block corresponding to
\phi
is an
M
-th power
if and only if
\phi
is
\widetilde{M}
-power polynomial.
Again if
\phi\neq\widetilde{\phi}
, then the block corresponding to
\phi\widetilde{\phi}
is an
M
-th power if and only if
\phi
is an
M
-power polynomial.

Hence if the combinatorial data attached with
A

is given by
\Delta_{A}=\{(f,\lambda_{f})|f\in\Phi\setminus\{0\}\}
, then
A
will be an
M
-th power if and only if

(1)

for
f=\widetilde{f}
, we have
f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}
,

(2)

for
f\neq\widetilde{f}
, we have
f\in\Phi_{M}
.

Hence we get that

\displaystyle cC_{U}^{M}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}}\left(1+z^{\deg f}+z^{2\deg f}+\ldots\right)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{g,\widetilde{g}\}\\
g\in\Phi_{M}\setminus\widetilde{\Phi}_{M}\}\end{subarray}}\left(1+z^{2\deg f}+z^{4\deg f}+\ldots\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd}}\left(1-z^{d}\right)^{-\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1-z^{2d}\right)^{-\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
C_{U}^{M}(n,q)
denote the number of cyclic matrices in
\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})
which are
M
-th power. Let
C_{U}^{M}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{U}^{M}(n,q)z^{n}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle C_{U}^{M}(z)=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{odd}}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{d}}{\left(q^{d}+1\right)\left(1-\left(\dfrac{z}{q}\right)^{d}\right)}\right)^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{2d}}{\left(q^{2d}-1\right)\left(1-\left(\dfrac{z}{q}\right)^{2d}\right)}\right)^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

Proof.

As discussed in
Theorem

4.4
, similarly here in this case centralizer of a matrix
A
, is direct product of
with one term for each SCIM polynomial dividing the
\ch_{A}(x)
and each pair
\{g,\widetilde{g}\}
such that

g\widetilde{g}
dividing
\ch_{A}(x)
. Note that if
f=\widetilde{f}
is irreducible of degree
d
and
f(x)^{m}|\ch_{A}(x)
, then the term corresponding to this factor is given by
q^{d(m-1)}(q^{d}+1)
(see
[
3
]
, page 38).
The term corresponding to the pair
\{g,\widetilde{g}\}
is
q^{2d(m-1)}(q^{2d}-1)
where
\left(g(x)\widetilde{g}(x)\right)^{m}|\ch_{A}(x)
and
g
is of degree
d
. Then we have that

\displaystyle C^{M}_{U}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd}}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{d}}{q^{d}+1}+\dfrac{z^{2d}}{q^{d}(q^{d}+1)}+\dfrac{z^{3d}}{q^{2d}(q^{d}+1)}+\ldots\right)^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}

\displaystyle\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1+\dfrac{z^{2d}}{q^{2d}-1}+\dfrac{z^{4d}}{q^{2d}(q^{2d}-1)}+\dfrac{z^{6d}}{q^{4d}(q^{2d}-1)}+\ldots\right)^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

∎

6.
Semisimple matrices

In this section we will concentrate on the case where
M
is a prime satisfying
(M,q)=1
.
This can be generalized with conscious effort in case of prime power (as was done in Section
6
of
[
5
]
)
or general
M
satisfying
(M,q)=1
using indicator functions (see Section
6
of
[
9
]
). Thus to avoid cumbersome expressions, we choose to work with prime
M
. We will need the following result, which dates back to 1955.

Lemma 6.1

(Section
3
,
[
2
]
)
.

Suppose that the polynomial
f(x)
, of degree
n
, is irreducible
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and that
t
is the order of its root. Let
m
be a natural number such that
(m,q)=1
and
m=m_{1}m_{2}
, where
(m_{1},t)=1
and each prime factor of
m_{2}
is a divisor of
t
. Then

(1)

each root of
f(x^{m})
has order of the form
dm_{2}t
, where
d
is a divisor
of
m_{1}
;

(2)

if

Proposition 6.3
.

Let

M
-th powers. Let

\displaystyle cS_{U}^{M}(z)=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd }}\left(1-z^{d}\right)^{-\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1-z^{2d}\right)^{-\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd }}\left(1-z^{dM}\right)^{-\widetilde{S}^{\prime}_{M}(d,q)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1-z^{2dM}\right)^{-S^{\prime}_{M}(d,q)}.

Let
A
be a semisimple matrix and the combinatorial data attached with

is given by
\Delta_{A}=\{(f,\lambda_{f})|f\in\Phi\setminus\{0\}\}
. Here we encounter with four types of polynomials, as described below:

(1)

f

\widetilde{M}
-power SCIM polynomial,

(2)

f

-power polynomial, but satisfies

(3)

f

-power nor
\widetilde{M}
-power,

(4)

anything other than the above three classes.

Then we will have the following constituents coming from each of the factors occurring in the factorization of the characteristic polynomial. Note that

(1)

(2)

\displaystyle cS_{U}^{M}(z)=

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
S_{U}^{M}(n,q)
denote the number of semisimple matrices in
\textup{U}({n},\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}})

M
-th powers. Let

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd }}\left(1+\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\dfrac{z^{dm}}{|\textup{U}(m,q^{2d})|}\right)^{\widetilde{N}_{M}(q,d)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1+\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\dfrac{z^{2dm}}{|\textup{GL}(m,q^{2d})|}\right)^{\widetilde{R}_{M}(q,d)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{d=\text{ odd }}\left(1+\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\dfrac{z^{dmM}}{|\textup{U}(mM,q^{2d})|}\right)^{\widetilde{S}^{\prime}_{M}(d,q)}\times\prod\limits_{d\geq 1}\left(1+\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\dfrac{z^{2dmM}}{|\textup{GL}(mM,q^{2d})|}\right)^{S^{\prime}_{M}(d,q)}.

Note that centralizer order of

|\textup{U}(\lambda_{f},q^{2\deg f})|
