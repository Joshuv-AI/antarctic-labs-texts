Note on general functional flows in equilibrium systems
Kiyoharu Kawana

Abstract
We study the response of generating functionals to a variation of parameters (couplings) in equilibrium systems i.e. in quantum field theory (QFT) and equilibrium statistical mechanics. These parameters can be either physical ones such as coupling constants or artificial ones which are intentionally introduced such as the renormalization scale in field theories. We first derive general functional flow equations for the generating functional (grand-canonical potential) W ⁡ [ J ] W[J] of the connected diagrams. Then, we obtain functional flow equations for the one-particle irreducible ( 1 1 PI) vertex functional (canonical potential) Γ ⁡ [ ϕ ] \Gamma[\phi] by performing the Legendre transformation. By taking the functional derivatives of the flow equations, we can obtain an infinite hierarchical equations for the 1 1 PI vertices. We also point out that a Callan-Symanzik type equation holds among the vertices when partition function is invariant under some changes of the parameters. After discussing general aspects of parameter response, we apply our formalism to several examples and reproduce the well-known functional flow equations. Our response theory provides us a systematic and general way to obtain various functional flow equations in equilibrium systems.

Introduction

For the past half century, Renormalization Group (RG) has played a central role in fundamental physics.
The essence of RG is the coarse-graining of the microscopic degrees of freedom and the construction of effective theory at low-energy scales (long distances).
In particular, one of the great successes is the explanation of universality class of critical phenomena in nature.
For example, in the Wilsonian RG
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, we can discuss the RG flow of the effective action by integrating out the high-energy modes.
Then, critical behaviors of a system are explained by the (linearlized) flow structures around a fixed point.
Not only are they very powerful for describing critical phenomena, the RG is also useful for the (non)perturbative calculations in field theories;
The independence of correlation functions or effective action on the renormalization scale implies nontrivial differential flow equations (Callan-Symanzik equations)
[
4
,
5
]
, and we can systematically improve the perturbative calculations by using these equations
[
6
,
7
,
8
]

Functional Renormalization Group (FRG)

provides us a non-perturbative way to implement the idea of RG in general many-body systems.
Essentially, the FRG combines the functional method in mathmatical physics with the Wilsonian RG by introducing an artificial (IR) cut-off parameter
k
in the action or Hamiltonian, and we see the response of a system to the continuous change of
k
.
By construction, the flow equation of the (average) effective action
\Gamma_{k}[\phi]
is one-loop exact, that is, it is expressed as an one-loop integral with the exact two-point correlation function.
Thus, some reasonable approximations are necessary when one wants to solve the flow equation practically.
See Refs.
[
15
,
16
]
and references therein for various approximation methods and for countless applications of the FRG.

In this paper, we discuss the basic ideas and formulations that underlie these functional flow approaches in general equilibrium systems i.e. in quantum field theory (QFT) and in equilibrium statistical mechanics.
The essence of the flow approach is to see the response of a system to the variation of parameters in the action or Hamiltonian.
The parameters can be either physical ones such as coupling constants
\{g_{k}\}
, temperature
T
, chemical potential
\mu
, or artificial ones that are intentionally added such as the renormalization scale
M

We derive general functional flow equations for the generating functionals in both grand-canonical and canonical formulations.
In QFT, the grand-canonical (canonical) generating functional
W[J]~(\Gamma[\phi])
corresponds to the generating functional of the connected diagrams (one-particle irreducible (
1
PI) diagrams).
In particular, when the partition function is invariant under some changes of parameters,
we can obtain another non-trivial relation for the correlation functions or
1
PI vertices, which can be interpreted as a generalized Callan-Symanzik equation.

After discussing general aspects of parameter response in Section
2

The first (trivial) example is the FRG in QFT, where an IR regulator function
R_{k}(x-y)

The second example is quantum spin systems (quantum Heisenberg models)

The third example is the classical liquid systems

We show that the resultant flow equation is completely the same as the Wetterich’s equation by identifying the cut-off dependent pair potential

Our general theory also enables us to obtain an exact flow equation in the presence of higher-order many-body potentials

The other application in classical liquids is the density renormalization group which was investigated in our previous paper
[

In this case, we consider a scale transformation

In the previous study, we have relied on the field theoretical technique

As a last example, we discuss classical nonequilibrium systems, in particular, the Langevin stochastic systems.
These systems can be treated as equilibrium ones by casting them into the path-integral forms.
We again see that the flow equation of the effective action of these nonequlibirum systems is given by the Wetterich’s equation when a cut-off regulator

In this paper, we mainly focus on theoretical formulations and general aspects of response theory.
More detailed analysis in some concrete models/systems is left for future investigation.

2.1
General flow equations in grand-canonical formulation

We consider a broad class of equilibrium systems whose bare action
S
or Hamiltonian
H
in the
d
dimensional flat spacetime is given by

\displaystyle S~[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]~=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{d}x_{i}\right)\frac{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{n!}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(x_{j})\right)+(i\hbar^{-1})^{-1}\int d^{d}xJ(x)\phi(x)~,

(1)

\displaystyle H[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]~=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{d-1}x_{i}\right)\frac{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{n!}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(x_{j})\right)-\beta^{-1}\int d^{d-1}xJ(x)\phi(x)~,

(2)

where
x_{i}=\overrightarrow{x}_{i}
denotes a position of the
i
-th particle,

J(x)
is an external source,

\{x_{i}\}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{\lambda_{k}\}
represents a set of general parameters (couplings) that can include artificial ones as well.
Here, we simply consider one real scalar model in a continuous spacetime, but generalization to vector models and lattice systems is straightforward.
See Sections
3.2
and
3.4
for examples.
Note also that the microscopic
n
-body potential
v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})
is symmetric under the permutation of positions and can contain the spacetime derivatives
\partial_{\mu}
in general.
For example, an ordinary scalar QFT corresponds to

\displaystyle\quad v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\delta^{(d)}(x_{1}-x_{2})(\partial^{2}-m^{2})~,\quad v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})=-\lambda_{n}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\delta^{(d)}(x_{1}-x_{i})~(\text{for $n\neq 2$})~,

(3)

and the two-body classical liquid system corresponds to
[
18
,
19
]

\displaystyle v_{1}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}v(x,x)~,\quad v_{2}(x,y)=v(x_{1},x_{2})~,\quad v_{n}=0\quad(\text{for $n\geq 3$})~,

(4)

where
v(x,y)
is a microscopic two-body pair potential.
In this case,
\phi(x)=\rho(x)
corresponds to the density of the system and
J(x)=\beta U(x)
corresponds to the external chemical potential.
See Section
3.3
for more details.
To simplify the expressions, we also use the following notations for the first two interactions:

\displaystyle\int d^{\tilde{d}}xv_{1}(x)\phi(x)=\langle v_{1}|\phi\rangle~,\quad\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\int d^{\tilde{d}}y\phi(x)v_{2}(x,y)\phi(y)=\langle\phi|v_{2}|\phi\rangle~.

(5)

where
\tilde{d}=d~(d-1)
for QFT (statistical mechanics).

The partition function is defined by

\displaystyle Z[\{\lambda_{k}\};J]=e^{sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J]}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\int{\cal D}\phi e^{i\hbar^{-1}S[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]}\\
\text{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]}\right)\end{cases}~

(6)

where
s=i\hbar^{-1}~(-\beta)
for
S~(H)
.
Here,
W[\{\lambda_{k}\};J]
corresponds to the generating functional of the connected diagrams (grand-canonical potential) in QFT (statistical mechanics).

We consider the response of the generating functional
sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J]
to a small change of the parameters
\{\delta\lambda_{k}\}
:

\displaystyle\delta(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])

\displaystyle=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\delta v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})\left\langle T\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(x_{i})\right\rangle_{J}~,

(7)

where

\displaystyle\left\langle T\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(x_{i})\right\rangle_{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\begin{cases}\int{\cal D}\phi\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(x_{i})\right)e^{i\hbar^{-1}S[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]}\\
\text{Tr}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi(x_{i})e^{-\beta H[\phi,\{\lambda_{k}\};J]}\right)\end{cases}

(8)

\displaystyle:=G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)~.

(9)

Then, Eq. (
7
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{d\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)\quad(\text{for each $k$})~.

(10)

More generally, for a parametric variation
\lambda_{k}\rightarrow\lambda_{k}(t)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d(sW[\{\lambda_{k}(t)\};J])}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{d\lambda_{k}(t)}{dt}\frac{\partial(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\bigg|_{\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(t)}

(11)

\displaystyle=s\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{d\lambda_{k}(t)}{dt}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial\lambda_{k}}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)\bigg|_{\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}(t)}~.

(12)

Note that
G^{(n)}
contains non-connected diagrams and is related to the connected correlation functions defined by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{n}(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{\delta J(x_{1})\cdots\delta J(x_{n})}

\displaystyle=F^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)~.

(13)

For example,

\displaystyle F^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)

\displaystyle=G^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)~,

(14)

\displaystyle F^{(2)}(x,y;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)

\displaystyle=G^{(2)}(x,y;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)-F^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)F^{(1)}(y;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)~.

(15)

By definition, the connected correlation functions satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)}{\delta J(x_{n+1})}

\displaystyle=F^{(n+1)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J)~.

(16)

For simplicity, we denote the correlation function without the source term as

\displaystyle F^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})=F^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J=0)~.

(17)

Eq. (
10
) is a general functional flow equation in the grand-canonical formulation.
By taking the functional derivatives of Eq. (
10
) with respect to
J(x)
(and putting
J(x)=0
), we can obtain hierarchical equations for the correlation functions.
For example, the first two equations are

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}F^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\})=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\frac{\delta G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{\delta J(x)}~,

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}F^{(2)}(x,y;\{\lambda_{k}\})=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\frac{\delta^{2}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{\delta J(x)\delta J(y)}~.

(19)

The calculation of the right hand sides needs more information of a specific model.
In particular, when we consider parameter changes such that only
G^{(1)}
or
G^{(2)}
appears in the right hand side in Eq. (
10
), Eqs. (
14
)(
15
)(
16
) are sufficient to determine the flow equations for the higher-order correlation functions
F^{(l)}
.
For example, when only
G^{(2)}
appears in Eq. (
10
), the first two equations (
18
)(
19
) become

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}F^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{\tilde{d}}x_{1}\int d^{\tilde{d}}x_{2}\frac{\partial v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\left\{F^{(3)}(x,x_{1},x_{2})+2F^{(2)}(x,x_{1})F^{(1)}(x_{2})\right\}~,

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}F^{(2)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{\tilde{d}}x_{1}\int d^{\tilde{d}}x_{2}\frac{\partial v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\bigg\{F^{(4)}(x,y,x_{1},x_{2})+2F^{(3)}(x,y,x_{1})F^{(1)}(x_{2})+2F^{(2)}(x,x_{1})F^{(2)}(y,x_{2})\bigg\}~,

(21)

where we have omitted
\{\lambda_{k}\}
in the correlation functions for simplicity.
In this case, the flow equations for the higher-order correlation functions
F^{(l)}~(l\geq 3)
can be obtained straightforwardly by taking the functional derivative of Eq. (
19
) with the use of Eq. (
16
).

2.2
General flow equations in canonical formulation

Usually it is more convenient to consider the generating functional of
1
PI vertices (canonical potential)
\Gamma[\phi]
instead of the grand-canonical one
W[J]
.
Here, we derive the flow equation of
\Gamma[\phi]
.

The effective action is defined by the Legendre transformation of
W[\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J]
:

\displaystyle s\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi]

\displaystyle=\underset{J}{{\rm min}}\left(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J]-\langle J|\phi\rangle\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi}]-\langle J_{\phi}|\phi\rangle~,

(23)

where
J_{\phi}
is a solution of

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{\delta J(x)}=F^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\};J)=\phi(x)~.

(24)

This implies that the two-point function is given by

\displaystyle F^{(2)}(x,y;\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})=\frac{\delta(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{\delta J(x)\delta J(y)}\bigg|_{J=J_{\phi}}=\frac{\delta\phi(x)}{\delta J_{\phi}(y)}~

(25)

for a given field
\phi(x)
.
The parameter derivative is calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{d(s\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi])}{d\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}\left(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi}]-\langle J_{\phi}|\phi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{d\lambda_{k}}\bigg|_{J=J_{\phi}}-\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\frac{\partial J_{\phi}}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\frac{\delta}{\delta J_{\phi}(x)}\left(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi}]-\langle J_{\phi}|\phi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(sW[\{\lambda_{k}\};J])}{d\lambda_{k}}\bigg|_{J=J_{\phi}}~,

(26)

where the second term in the second line vanishes by Eq. (
24
).
Thus, by Eq. (
10
), we obtain a general flow equation of
\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi]
:

\displaystyle\frac{d(s\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi])}{d\lambda_{k}}=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})~.

(27)

To derive the flow equations for the higher-oder vertices, we define the
n-
point (
1
PI) vertex by

\displaystyle c^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)=\frac{\delta^{n}(s\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi])}{\delta\phi(x_{1})\cdots\delta\phi(x_{n})}~.

(28)

In particular, the first two vertices can be written as

\displaystyle c^{(1)}(x;\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)=-J_{\phi}(x)~,\quad c^{(2)}(x,y;\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)=-\frac{\delta J_{\phi}(x)}{\delta\phi(y)}~.

(29)

One can check that these vertices and
F^{(2)}
satisfy

\displaystyle\int d^{\tilde{d}}zF^{(2)}(x,z;\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})c^{(2)}(z,y;\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)=-\delta^{(\tilde{d})}(x-y)~,

(30)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F^{(2)}(x_{1},x_{2};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)}{\delta\phi(x_{3})}=\int d^{\tilde{d}}z\int d^{\tilde{d}}z^{\prime}F^{(2)}(x_{1},z;\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)c^{(3)}(x_{3},z,z^{\prime};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)F^{(2)}(x_{2},z^{\prime};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)~,

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta c^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)}{\delta\phi(x_{n+1})}=c^{(n+1)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)\quad(\text{for $n\geq 3$})~.

(32)

To rewrite the flow equation (
27
) in terms of the
1
PI vertices, we have to express the
n-
point correlation function
G^{(n)}
or
F^{(n)}
by
\{c^{(l)}\}
in general.
Then, by taking the functional derivatives of Eq. (
27
) with respect to
\phi(x)
, we can obtain the flow equations for the vertices
\{c^{(l)}\}
.

In particular, as in the grand-canonical case, Eqs. (
30
)(
31
)(
32
) are sufficient to derive the flow equations for the higher-order vertices when only
G^{(2)}
appears in Eq. (
27
).
Here, we summarize the first two equations in this case:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}c^{(1)}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{\tilde{d}}y\int d^{\tilde{d}}y^{\prime}\frac{\partial v_{2}(y,y^{\prime})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\bigg\{\int d^{\tilde{d}}z\int d^{\tilde{d}}z^{\prime}F^{(2)}(y,z)c^{(3)}(x_{1},z,z^{\prime})F^{(2)}(z^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+2\delta^{(\tilde{d})}(x_{1}-y)\phi(y^{\prime})\bigg\}~,

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle c^{(2)}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{\tilde{d}}y\int d^{\tilde{d}}y^{\prime}\frac{\partial v_{2}(y,y^{\prime})}{\partial\lambda_{k}}\bigg\{\int d^{\tilde{d}}z\int d^{\tilde{d}}z^{\prime}F^{(2)}(y,z)c^{(4)}(x_{1},x_{2},z,z^{\prime})F^{(2)}(z^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle+2\int d^{\tilde{d}}z\int d^{\tilde{d}}z^{\prime}\int d^{\tilde{d}}\omega\int d^{\tilde{d}}\omega^{\prime}F^{(2)}(y,z)c^{(3)}(x_{1},z,\omega)F^{(2)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})c^{(3)}(x_{2},\omega^{\prime},z^{\prime})F^{(2)}(z^{\prime},y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+2\delta^{(\tilde{d})}(y-x_{1})\delta^{(\tilde{d})}(y^{\prime}-x_{2})\bigg\}~,

(34)

where we have omitted the parameter dependences and
\phi(x)
in the vertices for simplicity.
The flow equations for higher-order vertices
c^{(l)}~(l\geq 3)
can be straightforwardly obtained by using Eqs. (
31
)(
32
).

2.3
Generalized Callan-Symanzik equations

Let us consider a situation such that the variation of a parameter
\lambda_{0}:=t
can be compensated by the changes of other variables as

\displaystyle\Gamma[\{t-\delta t,\lambda_{k},V\};\phi]=\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k}+\delta\lambda_{k}(\delta t),V+\delta V(\delta t)\};\phi+\delta\phi(\delta t)]~,

(35)

or

\displaystyle\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k},V\};\phi]=\Gamma[\{t+\delta t,\lambda_{k}+\delta\lambda_{k}(\delta t),V+\delta V(\delta t)\};\phi+\delta\phi(\delta t)]~,

(36)

which can be interpreted as a symmetry of the generating functional.
Note that we have also included the volume dependence explicitly.

The above relations mean

\displaystyle-\frac{d\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k},V\};\phi]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k}\frac{\delta\lambda_{k}(\delta t)}{\delta t}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{k}}+\frac{\delta V(\delta t)}{\delta t}\frac{\partial}{\partial V}+\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\frac{\delta\phi(\delta t)}{\delta t}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(x)}\right)\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k},V\};\phi]

(37)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle={\cal D}_{t}\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k},V\};\phi]~.

(38)

By using the general flow equation (
27
) (with
\lambda_{k}\rightarrow t
), we have another relation among the correlation functions:

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{t}(s\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k}\},V;\phi])=-s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{t,\lambda_{k}\})}{\partial t}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{t,\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})~,

(39)

which corresponds to the Callan-Symanzik equation in QFT.
1
1

1

In QFT, the effective action is completely independent of the renormalization scale
t=\log M

and
\Gamma_{t}[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi]
satisfies the Callan-Symanzik equation:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d\Gamma_{t}[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi]}{dt}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{k}}-\int d^{d}x\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\log\phi(x)}\right)\Gamma_{t}[\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi]~.

(40)

In particular, when
t
is an artificial parameter such that
t=1
corresponds to the original system of interest (i.e.
v_{n}(\{t=1,\lambda_{k}\})=v_{n}(\{\lambda_{k}\})
), we have

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{t}(s\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k}\},V;\phi])\bigg|_{t=1}=-s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)\frac{\partial v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{t,\lambda_{k}\})}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=1}G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})~,

(41)

which indicates another nontrivial relation among the correlation functions and vertices in the original system.

Let us see a couple of examples.

GENERAL PRESSURE EQUATION

Here, we represent the generating functional as
W[\{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})\},V;J(x)]
.
Consider a scale transformation of the volume

, where
\epsilon
is an infinitesimal parameter and regarded as

in the above general discussion.
In this case, by the definition of the partition function (
6
),

satisfies

\displaystyle W[\{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})\},(1+\tilde{d}\epsilon)V;J(x)]=W[\{v_{n}(\{(1-\epsilon)x_{i}\})\},V;J((1-\epsilon)x)]~,

(42)

which leads to

\displaystyle({\rm L.H.S})=

\displaystyle\frac{d(sW[\{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})\},(1+\tilde{d}\epsilon)V;J(x)])}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0,J=0}=\tilde{d}\frac{\partial(sW[\{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})\},V])}{\partial\log V}

\displaystyle({\rm R.H.S})=

\displaystyle-s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\})\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}^{(i)}v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})-\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\int d^{\tilde{d}}yy^{\mu}\frac{\delta(\partial_{\mu}^{(y)}J(y))}{\delta J(x)}F^{(1)}(x)

(43)

\displaystyle\therefore~

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(sW[\{v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})\},V;J])}{\partial\log V}=\int d^{\tilde{d}}xF^{(1)}(x)-\frac{s}{\tilde{d}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\})\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}^{(i)}v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})~.

(44)

This is a generalization of the
pressure equation
in the classical liquid systems
[
18
,
19
]
.
In fact, the classical liquid systems correspond to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(-\beta W[V])}{\partial V}\bigg|_{T,\mu}=\beta p~,\quad F^{(1)}(x)=\rho~,

(45)

and Eq. (
44
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{p}{T}=\rho+\frac{1}{T(d-1)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{d-1}x_{i}\right)G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\})\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}^{(i)}v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})~.

(46)

In Section
3.3
, we will see another example of parameter redundancy in the classical simple liquid systems.

SYSTEMS WITH SCALING POTENTIALS

When the microscopic potential
v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{t,\lambda_{k}\})
satisfies the following scaling property

\displaystyle v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{t,\lambda_{k}\})=(t^{\Delta})^{n}v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{\lambda_{k}\})~

(47)

for some (artificial) parameter
t
,
the effective action satisfies

\displaystyle\Gamma[\{t,\lambda_{k}\},V;\phi]=\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k},V\};t^{\Delta}\phi]~,

(48)

which leads to

\displaystyle\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\frac{\delta(s\Gamma[\{\lambda_{k},V\};\phi])}{\delta\log\phi(x)}=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{\lambda_{k}\})G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})

(49)

\displaystyle\therefore~\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\phi(x)c^{(1)}(x,\{\lambda_{k}\};\phi)=s\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}d^{\tilde{d}}x_{i}\right)v_{n}(\{x_{i}\},\{\lambda_{k}\})G^{(n)}(\{x_{i}\};\{\lambda_{k}\};J_{\phi})~.

(50)

For
\phi=
constant, this gives another relation between thermodynamic quantities and correlation functions.

For example, we can consider a two-body system with

\displaystyle v_{2}(x-y)=\frac{v_{0}}{|x-y|^{m}}~,\quad v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})=0\quad(n\neq 2)~.

(51)

which satisfies the scaling property
v_{2}(tx)=t^{-m}v_{2}(x)
.
In this case, Eq. (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\phi(x)c^{(1)}(x)=s\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\int d^{\tilde{d}}yv_{2}(x-y)G^{(2)}(x,y;J_{\phi})~.

(52)

By putting
\phi(x)=\phi=
constant, we then obtain

\displaystyle-J_{\phi}V\phi=s\int d^{\tilde{d}}x\int d^{\tilde{d}}yv_{2}(x-y)G^{(2)}(x,y;J_{\phi})~,

(53)

where we have used Eq. (
29
).
This resembles the sum rules of correlation functions in classical liquid systems
[
18
,
19
]
.
2
2

2

In liquid systems,
\phi=\rho
corresponds to the density and
J_{\rho}
corresponds the chemical potential
\beta\mu
.
Thus,
V\phi=N
is the total particle number in the left hand side.

3
Examples

We apply the general results developed in the previous section to several (non)equilibrium systems and reproduce the well-known functional flow equations discussed in the literatures.

3.1
Functional renormalization group in QFT

In the functional renormalization approach in QFT
[
13
,
15
,
16
]
, we introduce a cut off
k
in the two-point vertex as

\displaystyle v_{2}(x,y;m^{2},k)=\delta^{(d)}(x-y)(\partial^{2}-m^{2})-R_{k}(x-y)~,

(54)

where
R_{k}(x-y)
is a regulator function whose Fourier mode qualitatively satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{k}(p)\sim\begin{cases}0&\text{for $p\gg k$}\\
{\cal O}(k^{2})&\text{for $p\ll k$}\end{cases}~.

(55)

Namely, it suppresses the low-energy modes and takes only the high-energy modes in the partition function.
See Refs.
[
15
,
16
]
and references therein for various proposals of
\tilde{R}_{k}(p)
.

In this case, the general flow equation (
27
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d(s\Gamma[k;\phi])}{dk}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}y\partial_{k}R_{k}(x-y)G_{k}^{(2)}(x,y;J_{\phi})

(56)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}{\rm Tr}\left((\partial_{k}R_{k})G_{k}^{(2)}\right)~,

(57)

with
s=(i\hbar^{-1})^{-1}
.
Or by introducing a new effective action and two-point vertex by

\displaystyle s\Gamma_{k}[\phi]:

\displaystyle=s\Gamma[k;\phi]+\frac{s}{2}\langle\phi|R_{k}|\phi\rangle

(58)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(x)\delta\phi(y)}=s^{-1}c_{k}^{(2)}(x,y)+R_{k}(x,y)~,

(59)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{d(s\Gamma_{k}[\phi])}{dk}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(s\Gamma[k;\phi])}{\partial k}+\frac{s}{2}\langle\phi|\partial_{k}R_{k}|\phi\rangle

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}y(\partial_{k}R_{k})G_{k}^{(2)}(x,y;J_{\phi})+\frac{s}{2}\langle\phi|\partial_{k}R_{k}|\phi\rangle_{J_{\phi}}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}y(\partial_{k}R_{k})F_{k}^{(2)}(x,y;J_{\phi})~.

(62)

\displaystyle\therefore\quad\frac{d(i\hbar^{-1}\Gamma_{k}[\phi])}{dk}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}y(\partial_{k}R_{k})(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}-R_{k})^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[(\partial_{k}R_{k})(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}-R_{k})^{-1}\right]~,

(63)

which is the Wetterich’s equation
[
11
]
in the Minkowski spacetime.
The Euclidean case is obtained by the replacement

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\phi]=i\Gamma_{k}^{E}[\phi]~,\quad\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}=-{\Gamma_{k}^{E}}^{(2)}~.

(64)

Note that the right-hand side is
{\cal O}(s^{0})={\cal O}(\hbar^{0})
, which indicates that this term corresponds to the one-loop diagram.
As generally explained in Section
2.2
, we can obtain the flow equations for higher-order vertices by taking the functional derivative of Eq. (
63
) with respect to
\phi(x)
.

3.2
Quantum spin systems

Quantum spin systems
[
17
]
have played an significant role in statistical mechanics because they capture the essence of quantum many-body systems.
We can also develop the functional flow in these systems
[
27
]
.
In the following, a
(d-1)
dimensional lattice is represented by
\Lambda
, and
i,j,\cdots
denote the sites.
Besides, a collection of bonds, i.e.
(i,j)
such that
i\neq j
, is represented by
\mathscr{B}
, which determines the interactions of sites.

We consider the quantum Heisenberg model:

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}J_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}-\beta^{-1}\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\mathbf{U}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{i}~,

(65)

\displaystyle Z[\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\beta W[\{\mathbf{U}^{i}\}]\right)={\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})~.

(66)

where
J_{ij}
is the (anti)ferromagnetic coupling constant and
\mathbf{H}_{i}=\beta^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{i}=\beta^{-1}(U_{i}^{x},U_{i}^{y},U_{i}^{z})
is an external magnetic field.
The spin operators
\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{i}=(\hat{S}^{x}_{i},\hat{S}^{y}_{i},\hat{S}^{z}_{i})
satisfy the commutation relation

\displaystyle[\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{i},\hat{S}^{\beta}_{j}]=i\delta_{ij}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}\hat{S}^{\gamma}_{i}~,

(67)

where
\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}
is the totally antisymmetric tensor.
The magnitude of the spin is defined by
\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{2}=S(S+1)
.

Now we introduce an artificial parameter
t\in[0,1]
and deform the couplings as
J_{ij}\rightarrow J_{ij}(t)
with the boundary condition
J_{ij}(t=1)=J_{ij}
.
Correspondingly, we represent the generating functionals as

\displaystyle W[t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}]=W_{t}[\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}]~,\quad\Gamma[t;\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}]=\Gamma_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}]~.

(68)

As for the boundary (initial) condition at
t=0
, it is often assumed that
J_{ij}(t=0)
corresponds the coupling of some (exactly) solvable system
[
27
]
.
The correlation functions are defined by

\displaystyle G^{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}(t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z[t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}]}\frac{\delta^{n}Z[t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}]}{\delta U_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\delta U_{i_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}}~,

(69)

\displaystyle F^{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}(t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}):

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{n}(-\beta W_{t}[\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}])}{\delta U_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\delta U_{i_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}}~,

(70)

\displaystyle c^{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}(t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}):

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{n}(-\beta\Gamma_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}])}{\delta S_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\delta S_{i_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}}~.

(71)

Then, by using the general result (
27
), we obtain the flow equation for the canonical potential as

\displaystyle\frac{d(-\beta\Gamma_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}])}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}(\partial_{t}J_{ij}(t))G^{ij}_{\alpha\alpha}(t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}^{S}\})

(72)

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}(\partial_{t}J_{ij}(t))\left[F^{ij}_{\alpha\alpha}(\{\mathbf{U}_{i}^{S}\})+S_{i}^{\alpha}S_{j}^{\alpha}\right]~.

(73)

where
\mathbf{U}_{i}^{S}
is the solution of the Legendre transformation.
Note that we do not have a symmetric factor
\frac{1}{2}
in this case because of the definition of the Hamiltonian (
65
).
As in the FRG in QFT, we can also introduce another canonical functional

\displaystyle-\beta\mathscr{C}_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}]:

\displaystyle=-\beta\Gamma_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}]+\beta\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}{R}_{ij}(t)\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{j}~.

(74)

where
{R}_{ij}(t)=J_{ij}(t)-J_{ij}
.
Now we have

\displaystyle\frac{d(-\beta\mathscr{C}_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}])}{dk}

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}(\partial_{t}J_{ij}(t))F^{ij}_{\alpha\alpha}(t;\{\mathbf{U}_{i}^{S}\})

(75)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathscr{B}}(\partial_{t}R_{ij}(t))\left([\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{t}]^{ij}_{\alpha\alpha}+\partial_{t}R_{ij}(t)\right)^{-1}

(76)

\displaystyle=-{\rm Tr}\left[(\partial_{t}\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{t}^{(2)}+\mathbf{R}_{t})^{-1}\right]~,

(77)

where

\displaystyle[\mathbf{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{t}]^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\delta\mathscr{C}_{t}[\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\}]}{\delta S_{i}^{\alpha}\delta S_{i}^{\beta}}~,\quad[\mathbf{R}_{t}]^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}R_{ij}(t)~.

(78)

Generalization to higher order interactions such as
J_{ijkl}(t)(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j})(\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{l})
is also straightforward by using the general result (
27
).

3.3
Classical liquids

Next example is the classical liquid system:

\displaystyle H_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+V_{N}(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})~,

(79)

where

\displaystyle V_{N}(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})=\sum_{i<j}^{N}v(x_{i},x_{j})+\sum_{i<j<k}^{N}v_{3}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{k})+\cdots

(80)

is a general potential energy of
N
particles.
The grand-canonical partition function
\Xi
and grand potential
W
are defined by

\displaystyle\Xi[T,V;U]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\beta W[T,V;U]\right)

(81)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{N!}\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{1}\int d^{d-1}p_{1}\cdots\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{N}\int d^{d-1}p_{N}\exp\left(-\beta H_{N}+\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}U(x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi mT)^{N(d-1)/2}}{N!}\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{1}\cdots\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{N}\exp\left(-\beta V_{N}+\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}U(x_{i})\right)~,

(82)

where
U(x)
is a position-dependent chemical potential.
The usual thermodynamic equilibrium corresponds to
U(x)=\mu
.
By using the density operator

\displaystyle\rho(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(d-1)}(x-x_{i})~,

(83)

the grand-canonical partition function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Xi[T,\mu,V;U]

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi mT)^{N(d-1)/2}}{N!}\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{1}\cdots\int_{V}d^{d-1}x_{N}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\rho|\beta v|\rho\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{d-1}xv(x,x)\rho(x)+\cdots+\langle\beta U|\rho\rangle\right)~,

(84)

where
\cdots
represents the higher-order potential terms and

\displaystyle\langle\rho|\beta v|\rho\rangle=\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}y\rho(x)\beta v(x,y)\rho(y)~,

(85)

\displaystyle\langle\beta U|\rho\rangle=\beta\int d^{d-1}xU(x)\rho(x)~.

(86)

Comparing Eq. (
84
) with general Hamiltonian (
2
), we can read

\displaystyle v_{1}(x)=-\frac{v(x,x)}{2}~,\quad v_{2}(x,y)=v(x,y)

(87)

in this case.

The Legendre transformation of
-\beta W[T,V;U]
with respect to
U(x)
gives the canonical free energy
-\beta\Gamma[T,V;\rho]
:

\displaystyle-\beta\Gamma[T,V;\rho]

\displaystyle=\underset{U}{\text{Min}}\left(-\beta W[T,V;U]-\langle\beta U|\rho\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta W[T,V;U_{\rho}]-\beta\int d^{d-1}xU_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)~,

(88)

where
\rho(x)
represents a general density field, and
U_{\rho}(x)
is a solution of

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(-\beta W[T,V;U])}{\delta(\beta U(x))}=\rho(x)~.

(89)

HIERARCHICAL REFERENCE THEORY

In the following, we focus on the simple liquids, that is,

\displaystyle v(x,y)=v(|x-y|)~,\quad v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})=0~\text{for $n\geq 3$}~.

(90)

In this case, the Fourier mode

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(q)=\int d^{d-1}xe^{-iq\cdot x}v(x)

(91)

is a function of
|q|
and real
\tilde{v}(q)^{*}=\tilde{v}(-q)=\tilde{v}(q)
.
In the HRT
[
20
,
21
,
24
]
, an artificial IR cut-off
k
is introduced to the Fourier mode of the two-body pair potential
v(x)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{k}(q)\sim\begin{cases}\tilde{v}(q)&\text{for $|q|\gg k$}\\
0&\text{for $|q|\ll k$}\end{cases}~,

(92)

with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{v}_{k}(q)=\tilde{v}_{R}(q)~,\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}\tilde{v}_{k}(q)=\tilde{v}(q)~,

(93)

where
v_{R}(x)
is the pair potential of some reference system.
In the literatures
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, a repulsive potential (such as the hard core potential) is often chosen for
v_{R}(x)
.
3
3

3

Recently, a new approach using the cavity distribution functions was proposed in Ref.
[
24
]
.
The utilization of the cavity distribution functions eliminates possible divergences coming from the strong short-range repulsive potential.

The expression (
92
) is not mathematically complete yet, but we do not need an exact form of
\tilde{v}_{k}(q)
to derive the functional flow equation.
All the quantities calculated by using
v_{k}(x,y)

are represented as

\displaystyle v_{k1}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{v_{k}(x,x)}{2}~,\quad v_{k2}(x,y)=v_{k}(x,y)~,

(94)

\displaystyle W_{k}[T,V;U]~,

\displaystyle\quad\Gamma_{k}[T,V;\rho]~,\quad F^{(l)}_{k}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{l})~,\quad c^{(l)}_{k}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{l})~.

(95)

Now, according to the general result (
27
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d(-\beta\Gamma_{k}[T,V;\rho])}{dk}

\displaystyle=-\beta\bigg[\int d^{d-1}x\partial_{k}v_{k1}(x)F^{(1)}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}y\partial_{k}v_{k2}(x,y)\left(F^{(2)}_{k}(x,y)+F^{(1)}_{k}(x)F^{(1)}_{k}(y)\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}y\partial_{k}v_{k}(x,y)n_{k}^{(2)}(x,y)~,

(96)

where

\displaystyle n_{k}^{(2)}(x,y)=F^{(2)}_{k}(x,y)+F^{(1)}_{k}(x)F_{k}^{(1)}(y)-\delta^{(d-1)}(x-y)F^{(1)}_{k}(y)

(97)

is called the total correlation function in liquid theory.

To derive a more simple expression, we introduce another canonical free energy
{\cal A}_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle-\beta{\cal{A}}_{k}[T,V;\rho]=-\beta\Gamma_{k}[T,V;\rho]

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}y\left\{v_{k}(x,y)-v(x,y)\right\}\{\rho(x)\rho(y)-\delta^{(d)}(x-y)\rho(x)\}~,

(98)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\cal{A}}_{k}[T,V;\rho]=\Gamma_{R}[T,V;\rho]~,\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}{\cal{A}}_{k}[T,V;\rho]=\Gamma[T,V;\rho]~,

(99)

where
\Gamma_{R}[T,V;\rho]
is the canonical potential for a reference system.
By taking the
k
derivative of
-\beta{\cal A}_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d(-\beta{\cal{A}}_{k}[T,V;\rho])}{dk}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}x\partial_{k}v_{k}(x-y)F_{k}^{(2)}(x-y)~.

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{2}{\rm Tr}[(\partial_{k}v_{k})F_{k}^{(2)}]~,

(100)

We represent the two-point vertex defined by
\beta{\cal A}_{k}[T,V;\rho]
as

\displaystyle C_{k}^{(2)}(x-y):=\frac{\delta(\beta{\cal A}_{k}[T,V;\rho])}{\delta\rho(x)\delta\rho(y)}\bigg|_{\rho(x)=n}=-c_{k}^{(2)}(x-y)-\beta v_{k}(x-y)+\beta v(x-y)~,

(101)

which means that the inverse of
F_{k}^{(2)}(x)
is given by
C_{k}^{(2)}(x)+\beta v_{k}(x)-\beta v(x)
.
Thus, Eq. (
100
) can be also written as

\displaystyle\frac{d(\beta{\cal{A}}_{k}[T,V;\rho])}{dk}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[\partial_{k}(\beta v_{k})(C_{k}^{(2)}+\beta v_{k}-\beta v)^{-1}\right]~,

(102)

which is exactly the same form as the Wetterich’s equation (
63
) with the identification
\beta(v_{k}-v)\rightarrow R_{k}
.
This fact indicates that the simple liquid systems essentially belong to the same universality class of the scalar QFT.
See Refs.
[
20
,
21
]
and references therein for the study of critical phenomena based on the above functional flow equation.

DENSITY RENORMALIZATION GROUP

Another application is the density renormalization group which was investigated in our previous paper
[
25
]
.
We are interested in how the classical liquid systems respond to the variation of the density
\rho(x)=n
.
To see this, we consider the scale transformation of the pair potential:

\displaystyle v(x)\quad\rightarrow\quad v(\lambda x)~,\quad\lambda>0~,

(103)

where
\lambda
is regarded as one of the parameters in the general Hamiltonian (
2
).
The corresponding canonical potential is represented as
\Gamma_{\lambda}[T,V;\rho]
.
Then, by repeating the same calculation as Eq. (
96
), we obtain the flow equation

\displaystyle\frac{d(-\beta\Gamma_{\lambda}[T,V;\rho])}{d\lambda}=-\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{d-1}x\int d^{d-1}y~

\displaystyle n_{\lambda}^{(2)}(x,y)(x-y)^{i}\partial_{i}v(\lambda(x-y))~.

(104)

In this case, we can use the idea developed in Section
2.3
, that is, the scale parameter
\lambda
can be compensated by other variables.
In fact, the original grand partition function satisfies

\displaystyle\Xi_{\lambda}[T,V;U(x)]:=\Xi[T,V;U(x)]\bigg|_{v(x)\rightarrow v(\lambda x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi mT)^{N(d-1)/2}}{N!}\int_{V}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}d^{d-1}x_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{i<j}v(\lambda(x_{i}-x_{j}))+\beta\sum_{i}U(x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi mT)^{N(d-1)/2}}{N!}\int_{\lambda^{d-1}V}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}d^{d-1}x_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{i<j}v(x_{i}-x_{j})+\beta\sum_{i}\left\{U(x_{i}/\lambda)-(d-1)T\log\lambda\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi[T,\lambda^{d-1}V;U(x/\lambda)-(d-1)T\log\lambda]~.

(105)

We see that the change of the pair potential under the scale transformation,
v(x)\rightarrow v(\lambda x)
,
can be compensated by the changes of the chemical potential
\mu
, the volume
V
, and the external chemical potential
U(x)
.
For an infinitesimal scale transformation
\lambda=1+\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\Xi_{1+\epsilon}[T,V;U(x)]=\Xi[T,(1+(d-1)\epsilon)V;U(x(1-\epsilon))-(d-1)T\epsilon]~,

(106)

or equivalently the grand potential satisfies

\displaystyle-\beta W_{1+\epsilon}[T,V;U(x)]=-\beta W[T,(1+(d-1)\epsilon)V;U(x(1-\epsilon))-(d-1)T\epsilon]~.

(107)

The Legendre transformation of the left hand side gives
-\beta\Gamma_{1+\epsilon}[T,V;\rho(x)]
by definition.
On the other hand, the Legendre transformation of the right hand side is

\displaystyle\underset{U}{\text{Min}}\left[-\beta W[T,(1+(d-1)\epsilon)V;U(x(1-\epsilon))-(d-1)T\epsilon]-\beta\int_{V}d^{d-1}xU(x)\rho(x)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{U}{\text{Min}}\bigg[-\beta W[T,(1+(d-1)\epsilon)V;U(x(1-\epsilon))-(d-1)T\epsilon]

In the following,

We consider a real scalar field

which obeys the Langevin equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)=-F[\phi(t,x)]+\xi(t,x)~,

(115)

where

\displaystyle P[\xi(t,x)]={\cal N}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi|G^{-1}|\xi\rangle\right)~,

\displaystyle\langle\xi|G^{-1}|\xi\rangle=\int dt\int dt^{\prime}\int d^{d}x\int d^{d}x^{\prime}\xi(t,x)G^{-1}(t,x;t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})~.

(117)

Here
{\cal N}
is a normalization factor.
Correspondingly, the correlation function of
\xi(t,x)
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi(t,x)\xi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=G(t,x;t^{\prime},x^{\prime})~.

(118)

The Langevin system (

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int{\cal D}\phi{\cal O}[\phi(t,x)]\prod_{t,x}\delta\left(\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)-F[\phi(x,t)]-\xi(t,x)\right)\right\rangle

(119)

\displaystyle\propto\left\langle\int{\cal D}\phi\int{\cal D}\phi^{\prime}{\cal O}[\phi(t,x)]\exp\left(i\int dt\int d^{d}x\phi^{\prime}(t,x)\left\{\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+F[\phi(x,t)]-\xi(t,x)\right\}\right)\right\rangle

(120)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\left\langle\exp\left(-i\int dt\int d^{d}x\phi^{\prime}(t,x)\xi(t,x)\right)\right\rangle~,

(121)

where we have introduced another real scalar
\phi^{\prime}(t,x)
and

\displaystyle\langle{\cal O}[\phi(t,x)]\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\cal D}\phi\int{\cal D}\phi^{\prime}{\cal O}[\phi(t,x)]e^{iS[\phi,\phi^{\prime}]}

4
Summary and discussion

In this paper, we have discussed the basic ideas and formulations of functional flow approach in equilibrium systems.
By considering the response of equilibrium systems to a general variation of the parameters, we obtained functional flow equations of the generating functionals.
Conventionally, some IR cut-off parameter is often introduced in the two-body interaction
v_{2}(x,y)
, which leads to the Wetterich type flow equations.
However, this is not the only and unique way to introduce an artificial parameter and one can also consider similar procedures
even for the higher-order potential terms
v_{n}(\{x_{i}\})~(n\geq 3)
in general.
Such a treatment would be useful when a system exhibits long-range correlations, and our response theory provides a straight and systematic way to obtain the functional flow equations for such general cases.
In this paper, we have focused on explaining the theoretical bases of response theory and not studied any concrete systems/models.
We would like to investigate them in future publications.
