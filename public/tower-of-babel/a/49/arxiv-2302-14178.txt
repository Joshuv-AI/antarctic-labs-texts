Hyperbolic Anderson model with Lévy white noise: spatial ergodicity and fluctuation
Raluca M. Balan and Guangqu Zheng

Abstract
. In this paper, we study one-dimensional hyperbolic Anderson models (HAM) driven by space-time pure-jump Lévy white noise in a finite-variance setting. Motivated by recent active research on limit theorems for stochastic partial differential equations driven by Gaussian noises, we present the first study in this Lévy setting. In particular, we first establish the spatial ergodicity of the solution and then a quantitative central limit theorem (CLT) for the spatial averages of the solution to HAM in both Wasserstein distance and Kolmogorov distance, with the same rate of convergence. To achieve the first goal (i.e. spatial ergodicity), we exploit some basic properties of the solution and apply a Poincaré inequality in the Poisson setting, which requires delicate moment estimates on the Malliavin derivatives of the solution. Such moment estimates are obtained in a soft manner by observing a natural connection between the Malliavin derivatives of HAM and a HAM with Dirac delta velocity. To achieve the second goal (i.e. CLT), we need two key ingredients: (i) a univariate second-order Poincaré inequality in the Poisson setting that goes back to Last, Peccati, and Schulte (Probab. Theory Related Fields, 2016) and has been recently improved by Trauthwein (arXiv:2212.03782); (ii) aforementioned moment estimates of Malliavin derivatives up to second order. We also establish a corresponding functional central limit theorem by (a) showing the convergence in finite-dimensional distributions and (b) verifying Kolmogorov’s tightness criterion. Part (a) is made possible by a linearization trick and the univariate second-order Poincaré inequality, while part (b) follows from a standard moment estimate with an application of Rosenthal’s inequality.

1.1.
Stochastic linear wave equation with pure-jump Lévy white noise

Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs)

In the semigroup approach (developed in

In Walsh-Dalang’s approach, the solution is a space-time indexed process

indexed by subsets of the space-time domain (or functions on this domain).
We refer the readers to

59

62

[
21

Regardless of the approach,

and the solution as the output.
One of the fundamental problems for SPDEs
is the well-posedness problem (i.e. existence, uniqueness, and stability
under perturbation

And probabilists have been driven to study/discover
new properties of the SPDE solutions, for example,

, and

intermittency property

Various classes of processes have been proposed as models for the noise
perturbing a partial differential equation,
often derived by an analogy with the noises appearing in the classical SDEs:
Brownian motion, Lévy processes, and fractional Brownian motions.
But the introduction of the infinite dimensional (and spatial) component
changes drastically the problem and leads to new challenges.
The class of SPDEs perturbed by Lévy noise
have been studied extensively in the monograph
[
57

using the semigroup approach,
where they are naturally interpreted as extensions of SDEs
driven by Lévy processes.
One way of which Lévy noise occurs is in the so-called Schrödinger
problem of probabilistic evolution, and several relativistic Hamiltonians
are known to generate Lévy noises; see, for example,
[
34

Remark 1.2
.

In
[
17
]
, the authors studied the moment asymptotics for the solution to
the stochastic heat equation driven by a space-time Lévy white noise
(with a Gaussian component),
whose Lévy measure

for some

If

, this value

the noise may have infinite variance,
and the CLT becomes problematic.
(Even in the classical case of i.i.d. random variables,
the CLT holds if and only if the variable is the domain of attraction of the normal law,
which means that its variance is finite, or its truncated variance is slowly varying.)
When

and
m_{p}<\infty
for some

,
the Lévy noise has finite variance; then
it might be possible to prove a CLT
(similar to the one given by Theorem

)
for the solution to the stochastic heat equation with Lévy noise.
As far as we know, this problem
has not been treated in the literature, even in case of the parabolic Anderson model (PAM).
A key step is to obtain the estimates
(
3.25
) and (
3.26
) for the Malliavin derivatives of the PAM solution.
In the present article, these estimates are derived using
the connection with the solution
v
of the wave equation with Dirac initial velocity,
and the crucial identity (
3.6
), which heavily relies on the fact that the fundamental
wave solution
G
is an indicator function (see (
1.9
)).
Therefore, one needs a different method to study the CLT problem for the heat equation.

Remark 1.3

(

(

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(\widetilde{h}_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n})

\displaystyle V(t,x,z)=Y\mathbf{1}_{(a,b]\times A\times\Gamma}(t,x,z)

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(E,\mu)
be a finite measure space.

(i)
Suppose that
F(\theta)\in\textup{dom}(D)
for every

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{E}\big(|F(\theta)|^{2}+\|DF(\theta)\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}}\big)\mu(d\theta)<\infty.

almost surely for
\mathfrak{m}

\xi\in Z
.

(ii)

(Stochastic Fubini’s theorem)
Suppose that

for each

such that

\textup{dom}(\delta)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{E}\big(|\delta(G(\theta))|^{2}+\|G(\theta)\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\big)\mu(d\theta)<\infty.

Then,

(2.43)

\displaystyle\int_{E}\delta\big(G(\theta)\big)\mu(d\theta)=\delta\bigg(\int_{E}G(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\bigg).

Proof.

(i) Suppose
F(\theta)\in\textup{dom}(D)
admits the chaos expansion

\displaystyle F(\theta)=f_{0}(\theta)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}(\theta)),

where
f_{n}(\theta)\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and for every
\theta\in E
.
Then, the condition (
2.41
) implies that

(2.44)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n!n\int_{E}\|f_{n}(\theta)\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}}\mu(d\theta)<\infty.

Fix any

with

. Then,
we deduce from (

) and Fubini’s theorem
with (

) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[I_{n}(g)I_{n}\Big(\int_{E}f_{n}(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=n!\int_{E}\langle f_{n}(\theta),g\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\mu(d\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\mathbb{E}\big[I_{n}(g)I_{n}(f_{n}(\theta))\big]\mu(d\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[I_{n}(g)\int_{E}I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\theta)\big)\mu(d\theta)\bigg],

which implies that almost surely

\displaystyle I_{n}\Big(\int_{E}f_{n}(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\Big)=\int_{E}I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\theta)\big)\mu(d\theta).

Then it is straightforward to generalize the above argument to

\displaystyle\int_{E}F(\theta)\mu(d\theta)=\int_{E}f_{0}(\theta)\mu(d\theta)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\Big(\int_{E}f_{n}(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\Big),

which, together with (

)-(

) and orthogonality

), implies that

belongs to
\textup{dom}(D)

and

\displaystyle D_{\xi}\int_{E}F(\theta)\mu(d\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nI_{n-1}\Big(\int_{E}f_{n}(\theta,\xi,\bullet)\mu(d\theta)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\int_{E}I_{n-1}\big(f_{n}(\theta,\xi,\bullet)\big)\mu(d\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{E}D_{\xi}I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\theta)\big)\mu(d\theta)=\int_{E}D_{\xi}F(\theta)\mu(d\theta)

almost surely. This proves (i).

Next, we prove (ii). Let
F\in\textup{dom}(D)
.
Then, we deduce from duality relation (

)
and Fubini’s theorem with the condition (

)
that

(2.46)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\bigg[F\delta\Big(\int_{E}G(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\Big)\bigg]&=\mathbb{E}\Big\langle DF,\int_{E}G(\theta)\mu(d\theta)\Big\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\\

Remark 2.7

(i) In this paper, we are mainly concerned with distributional properties.

2.3.
Poincaré inequalities

Recall from the Poincaré inequality (
2.24
) (see also (
2.48
))
that the variance
\textup{Var}(F)
of a Malliavin differentiable random variable
F

is controlled by the first Malliavin derivative
DF
.
That is, if
\|DF\|_{\mathfrak{H}}
is typically small, then
the random variable
F
has small fluctuations.
It was first in a paper by Chatterjee
[
10
]
that
a possible second-order extension of (Gaussian) Poincaré
inequality was investigated.
Suppose
F=g(X_{1},...,X_{m})
is a nice function of i.i.d. standard normal
random variables
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.
If the squared operator norm of
the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}g(X_{1},...,X_{m})
is typically smaller compared
to
\nabla g(X_{1},...,X_{m})
, then
F
is close to a linear combination of
X_{i}
’s
and thus
approximately Gaussian,
with the proximity measured in total-variation distance (
2.50
);
see Theorem 2.2 in
[
10
]

within the development of Stein’s method.
This quantitative bound is
then known as the second-order Gaussian Poincaré inequality.
And it has been generalized by Nourdin, Peccati, and Reinert
[
45
]

to the case where
F
is a general Malliavin differentiable
random variable (with respect to an isonormal Gaussian process)
and may depend on infinitely many coordinates (e.g.,

F=g(\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}})
). See also Vidotto’s improvement in

[
67
]
. In a recent joint work
[
7
]
with Nualart and Quer-Sardanyons,
we implemented this second-order Gaussian Poincaré
inequality to prove the quantitative central limit theorem (CLT)
for stochastic wave equation driven by colored-in-time Gaussian noise.
See also a study for stochastic heat equation in

[
50
]
by Nualart, Xia, and the second author.

Second-order Poincaré inequality on the Poisson space.

In
[
38
]
, Last, Peccati, and Schulte extended the second-order
Gaussian Poincaré inequality to the Poisson setting.
One can apply the results in
[

]
to obtain the quantitative
CLTs under the assumption of finite

which is a more restrictive assumption than (

In a recent work
[

, T. Trauthwein has
improved the second-order Poincaré

With this new ingredient,
we are able to obtain the quantitative CLT (and a corresponding functional CLT)
for the hyperbolic Anderson model (
1.1
)
under the assumption (
1.21
);
see Theorem
1.1
.

Let us first introduce several distances for distributional approximation.
Suppose
F,G
are real random variables with
distribution measures
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.

(i)
d_{\rm FM}
denotes the Fortet-Mourier metric, also known
as the bounded Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm FM}(F,G)&=d_{\rm FM}(\mu,\nu)\\
&=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{E}[h(F)]-\mathbb{E}[h(G)]|:\|h\|_{\infty}+\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1\big\}.\end{aligned}

It is well known that
d_{\rm FM}
characterizes the weak convergence
on
\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)
d_{\rm Wass}
denotes the

1
-Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm Wass}(F,G)&=d_{\rm Wass}(\mu,\nu)\\
&=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{E}[h(F)]-\mathbb{E}[h(G)]|:\textup{Lip}(h)\leq 1\big\}.\end{aligned}

It is trivial that
d_{\rm Wass}(F,G)\geq d_{\rm FM}(F,G)
.

(iii)
d_{\rm Kol}
denotes the Kolmogorov distance:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm Kol}(F,G)&=d_{\rm Kol}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,t]}(F)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,t]}(G)]|:t\in\mathbb{R}\big\}\\
&=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{P}(F\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(G\leq t)|:t\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.\end{aligned}

Kolmogorov distance is a very natural metric in studying the normal approximation,
in view of the fact that for a sequence of real-valued random variables
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

F_{n}
converges in law to a standard normal random variable
Y
(i.e.

d_{\rm FM}(F_{n},Y)\to 0
)
if and only if

d_{\rm Kol}(F_{n},Y)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
; see
[
44
, Proposition C.3.2]
.
It is also well known that

(2.49)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Kol}(F,Y)\leq\sqrt{d_{\rm Wass}(F,Y)},

when
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
;
see, for example,
[
61
, Proposition 1.2]
.

(iv) The aforementioned total-variation distance is defined by

(2.50)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d_{\rm TV}(F,G)&=d_{\rm TV}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\big\{|\mathbb{P}(F\in B)-\mathbb{P}(G\in B)|:B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\big\}.\end{aligned}

It is trivial that
d_{\rm TV}(F,G)\geq d_{\rm Kol}(F,G)
.
The total-variation distance is much stronger than weak convergence.
For example, consider
\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
i.i.d. Poisson random variables
with mean
1
,
F_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(Y_{1}+...+Y_{n}-n)
,
which is an element
of the first Poisson Wiener chaos

,
converges in law to

as

; while due to discrete nature
of
F_{n}
,

for all

. For this reason, we will not consider
total-variation distance for our quantitative CLTs.

Moment inequalities

Recall the definition of

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varphi_{t,R}(r,y)&:=\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t-r}(x-y)dx.\end{aligned}

We record below a few simple facts.

Lemma 2.9
.

(i)
For

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t}(y)dy&=t.\end{aligned}

(ii)
For

and

(2.57)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}}\big[\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)-\varphi_{s,R}(r,y)\big]dy&=2(t-s)R\end{aligned}

for any

(iii)
For

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\,drdy&\leq\frac{4}{3}R(t-s)^{3}\\

As a consequence, we have

(2.59)

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{p}_{t,R}(r,y)\,drdy\leq\begin{cases}2^{\frac{p}{2}}R^{\frac{p}{2}}(t-s)^{2+\frac{p}{2}}\,\,\,&\text{for $p\in[2,4]$}\\
2^{p-1}(t-s)^{p}R^{2}&\text{for $p\in(4,\infty)$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

(i) is trivial. Let us prove (ii) now.

Let
t\geq s\geq 0
. Then,

(2.60)

\displaystyle\varphi_{t,R}(r,y)-\varphi_{s,R}(r,y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-R}^{R}\mathbf{1}_{\{s-r\leq|x-y|<t-r\}}dx,

which implies that

for any

It is also easy to see from (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-R}^{R}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{1}_{\{s-r\leq|x-y|<t-r\}}dy\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=2(t-s)R.

That is, the equality (

To prove the first bound in part (iii),
we write

(2.61)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{s}^{t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}_{t,R}(r,y)\,dy\bigg)dr\\
&\quad=\int_{s}^{t}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t-r}(x_{1}-y)G_{t-r}(x_{2}-y)dx_{1}dx_{2}\bigg)dy\bigg]dr\\
&\quad\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{-R}^{R}\bigg[\int_{-R}^{R}G_{2t-2r}(x_{1}-x_{2})\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x_{2}-y)dy\bigg)dx_{1}\bigg]dx_{2}dr\\
&\quad\leq\int_{s}^{t}2(t-r)^{2}\cdot 2Rdr=\frac{4}{3}R|t-s|^{3},\end{aligned}

where the second step in (

And similarly,

(2.62)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{s}^{t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{4}_{t,R}(r,y)\,dy\bigg)dr&=\int_{s}^{t}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\int_{[-R,R]^{4}}\prod_{j=1}^{4}G_{t-r}(x_{j}-y)d\boldsymbol{x_{4}}\bigg)dy\bigg]dr\\
&\leq\int_{s}^{t}\int_{[-R,R]^{2}}G_{2t-2r}(x_{1}-x_{2})\bigg[\int_{[-R,R]^{2}}G_{2t-2r}(x_{2}-x_{3})\\
&\quad\cdot G_{2t-2r}(x_{3}-x_{4})\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x_{4}-y)dy\bigg)dx_{4}dx_{3}\bigg]dx_{2}dx_{1}dr\\
&\leq\int_{s}^{t}2R^{2}\cdot 4(t-r)^{3}dr=2R^{2}(t-s)^{4}.\end{aligned}

It remains to show the inequality (

The case

follows from the inequalities in (
2.58

by interpolation (i.e. an application of Hölder’s inequality).
For
p\geq 4
an integer, one can repeat the steps in (

to arrive at

and therefore,
the general case follows by interpolation.
This concludes the proof.

4.1.
Spatial ergodicity

We first establish the following strict stationarity.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
t>0
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
. Then,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\big(u(t,x_{1}),\ldots,u(t,x_{k})\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}\big(u(t,x_{1}+y),\ldots,u(t,x_{k}+y)\big).

Proof.

To show (
4.1
), it suffices to prove

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}u(t,x_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}u(t,x_{i}+y)

for any
(c_{1},...,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. By a limiting argument with (
1.13
)-(
1.14
),
we can reduce the verification of (
4.2
)
to showing

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{M}I_{n}(F_{t,x_{i},n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{M}I_{n}(F_{t,x_{i}+y,n})

for any
(c_{1},...,c_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and for any
M\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.

Note that

F_{t,x+y,n}(\boldsymbol{t_{n}},\boldsymbol{x_{n}},\boldsymbol{z_{n}})=F_{t,x,n}(\boldsymbol{t_{n}},\boldsymbol{x_{n}}-y,\boldsymbol{z_{n}})

with
\boldsymbol{x_{n}}-y:=(x_{1}-y,x_{2}-y,...,x_{n}-y)
. This motivates us to
define a Poisson random measure

on

by setting

for every
(A,B,C)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{0})

Then, it follows from the translational invariance of Lebesgue measure that

\displaystyle N_{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}N.

Let

denote the
n

; see Subsection

.
Therefore, we deduce from the definition of multiple integrals with
(
2.12
), (
2.13
), and (

) that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{M}I_{n}(F_{t,x_{i}+y,n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{M}I^{y}_{n}(F_{t,x_{i},n})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{M}I_{n}(F_{t,x_{i},n}),

where the last step is a consequence of (
4.3
).
Hence the proof of Lemma

is completed now.
∎

&\lesssim\frac{1}{R^{1+\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{2}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t}(x-y_{2})G_{t}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx\bigg)\\
&\lesssim R^{-\alpha},\end{aligned}

by performing integration in the order of
dy_{1},dy_{2}
, and
dx
.
That is, we have

(4.32)

\displaystyle\gamma_{5}^{1+\alpha}\lesssim R^{-\alpha}\quad\textup{and equivalently}\quad\gamma_{5}\lesssim R^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

In this step, we need to assume the finiteness of
m_{2+2\alpha}
.

\bullet

Estimation of
\gamma_{6}
.
Note that
\|D_{r_{1},y_{1},z_{1}}F_{R}(t)\|_{2+2\alpha}\lesssim t|z_{1}|
.
Similarly as in (
4.31
), we can write

\displaystyle\gamma_{6}^{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{m_{1+\alpha}m_{2+2\alpha}}{R^{1+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{t}dr_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dr_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{2}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t}(x-y_{2})G_{t}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx\bigg)^{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{-\alpha}.

That is, we have

(4.33)

\displaystyle\gamma_{6}^{1+\alpha}\lesssim R^{-\alpha}\quad\textup{and equivalently}\quad\gamma_{6}\lesssim R^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

In this step, we need to assume the finiteness of
m_{2+2\alpha}
and
m_{1+\alpha}
.

\bullet

Estimation of
\gamma_{7}
.
Similarly as in the estimation of
\gamma_{6}
,
we roughly bound

\|D_{r_{1},y_{1},z_{1}}F_{R}(t)\|_{2+2\alpha}\|D_{r_{2},y_{2},z_{2}}F_{R}(t)\|^{1+2\alpha}_{2+2\alpha}\lesssim t^{2\alpha+2}|z_{1}|\cdot|z_{2}|^{1+2\alpha},

and we can write

\displaystyle\gamma_{7}^{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{m_{2}m_{1+2\alpha}}{R^{1+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{t}dr_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dr_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy_{2}\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}G_{t}(x-y_{2})G_{t}(y_{1}-y_{2})dx\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{-\alpha}.

That is, we have

(4.34)

\displaystyle\gamma_{7}^{1+\alpha}\lesssim R^{-\alpha}\quad\textup{and equivalently}\quad\gamma_{7}\lesssim R^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

In this step, we need to assume the finiteness of
m_{1+2\alpha}

and
m_{2+2\alpha}
, while the finiteness of
m_{1+2\alpha}
is guaranteed
by the finiteness of
m_{1+\alpha}
and
m_{2+2\alpha}
.

Therefore, it follows from (
2.52
) in Proposition
2.8

with (
4.23
), (
4.26
), (
4.30
), (
4.32
), (
4.33
), and (
4.34
)
that

d_{\rm Kol}\Big(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\Big)\lesssim R^{-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}.

Hence the proof of part (iii) in Theorem
1.1
is completed.
∎

\bullet

Part
I I I
: Functional central limit theorems.

In this part, we present the proof of Theorem
1.1
(iv).
The remaining part of the proof consists of two steps: we first show the
convergence in finite-dimensional distributions and then
conclude this section by proving the tightness of the process

\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\{F_{R}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}:R\geq 1\}
.

\bullet

Step 1: Convergence in finite-dimensional distributions.

Fix any
0<t_{1}<...<t_{m}<\infty
with
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.
We need to show that

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(t_{1}),...,\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}(t_{m})\Big)

converges in law to a centered Gaussian vector on
\mathbb{R}^{m}

with covariance matrix
(\Sigma_{t_{i},t_{j}})_{i,j=1,...,m}
,
where
\Sigma
is as in (
1.20
).
Then, it suffices to show that

(4.35)

X_{R}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\frac{F_{R}(t_{j})}{\sqrt{R}}\quad\textup{converges in law to}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\mathcal{G}_{t_{j}},\quad\mbox{as $R\to\infty$}

for any integer
m\geq 1
, for any
b_{1},...,b_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, and for any

t_{1},...,t_{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
where
\mathcal{G}
is a centered continuous Gaussian process with covariance
structure
\Sigma
given as in (
1.20
).
Let

\tau^{2}:=\textup{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\mathcal{G}_{t_{j}}\right)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}b_{j}b_{k}\Sigma_{t_{j},t_{k}}.

Then (
4.35
) is equivalent to

(4.36)

X_{R}\,\,\,\mbox{converges in law to $\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2})$}\,\,\,\mbox{as $R\to\infty$}.

Moreover, by (
4.13
)-(
4.16
),

\tau_{R}^{2}:=\textup{Var}(X_{R})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}b_{j}b_{k}\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t_{j})F_{R}(t_{k})]\to\tau^{2}\,\,\,\mbox{as $R\to\infty$}.

The rest of the proof is trivial if
\tau^{2}=0
. It is also easy to see from the above limit that
if
\tau^{2}>0
, then
\tau_{R}^{2}>0
for large
R
.
Then, without losing any generality, we will assume that both

\tau^{2}_{R}
and
\tau^{2}
are strictly positive for every
R
.

From the Wasserstein bound (
2.51
) in Proposition
2.8
, we deduce that

(4.37)

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\left(\frac{X_{R}}{\tau_{R}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)\leq\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3},

where
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, and
\gamma_{3}
are defined as in (
2.53
) with

F=X_{R}
.
The rest of the arguments are almost identical to those in Part
I I

that we sketch in the following.
First, we write

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{R^{1+\alpha}}\int_{Z}\bigg[\int_{Z}\Big\|D_{\xi_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}F_{R}(t_{j})\Big\|_{2+2\alpha}\Big\|D_{\xi_{1}}D_{\xi_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}b_{k}F_{R}(t_{k})\Big\|_{2+2\alpha}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi_{2})\bigg]^{1+\alpha}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi_{1})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{R^{1+\alpha}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}\int_{Z}\bigg[\int_{Z}\big\|D_{\xi_{2}}F_{R}(t_{j})\big\|_{2+2\alpha}\big\|D_{\xi_{1}}D_{\xi_{2}}F_{R}(t_{k})\big\|_{2+2\alpha}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi_{2})\bigg]^{1+\alpha}\mathfrak{m}(d\xi_{1}).

Note that our estimations in Part
I I
can be carried out in the same way for
t_{j}\neq t_{k}
,
and therefore, we still get
\gamma_{1}^{1+\alpha}\lesssim R^{-\alpha}
. In the same manner,
we can obtain the asymptotical negligibility of
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{3}
, and hence that of the Wasserstein
distance in (
4.37
) under the assumption (
1.21
).

Finally,

\displaystyle d_{\rm Wass}\bigg(\frac{X_{R}}{\tau},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\rm Wass}\bigg(\frac{X_{R}}{\tau},\frac{X_{R}}{\tau_{R}}\bigg)+d_{\rm Wass}\bigg(\frac{X_{R}}{\tau_{R}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{1}{\tau_{R}}\right|\mathbb{E}|X_{R}|+d_{\rm Wass}\bigg(\frac{X_{R}}{\tau_{R}},\mathcal{N}(0,1)\bigg)\to 0\,\,\,\mbox{as $R\to\infty.$}

This implies (
4.36
) and
concludes the proof of the convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions.

\bullet

Step 2: Tightness.
For tightness, we only need to assume the finiteness of
m_{2}
.
We first deduce from Proposition
2.10
(ii) (with
p=2
)
and Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
32
, Theorem 4.23]
)
that for each
R\geq 1
, the process
F_{R}:=\{F_{R}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}

admits a continuous modification that is almost surely
locally
\beta
-Hölder continuous for any
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
Moreover, the bound (
2.65
) in Proposition
2.10
(ii) (with
p=2
),
together with the tightness criterion of Kolmogorov-Chentsov
(see, e.g.,
[
32
, Theorem 23.7]
),
implies that

\big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}F_{R}\big\}_{R\geq 1}

is a tight family of continuous processes;
that is, a tight family of random variables
with values in
C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R})
.

Combining the above two steps, we conclude the desired functional
CLT under the assumption (
1.21
).
Hence, we just finished the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

\square

Appendix A
Proof of the equivalence (
1.6
)

Recall from (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) that

L_{b}(A)=b\cdot\textup{Leb}(A)+M(A)+K(A),

with

M(A):=\int_{A\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)\,\,\,\textup{independent of}\,\,\,K(A):=\int_{A\times\{|z|>1\}}zN(dt,dx,dz).

In what follows, we record a few facts on
M(A)
and
K(A)
:

(i)

the characteristic function of
M(A)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i\lambda M(A)}\big]=\exp\Big(\textup{Leb}(A)\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}(e^{i\lambda z}-1-i\lambda z)\nu(dz)\Big),

and by Lebesgue’s differentiation theorem with the dominance
condition (
1.3
), we deduce that the above characteristic
function is infinitely differentiable and can be extended to an entire
function on
\mathbb{C}
. This implies in particular
that the random variable
M(A)
has finite exponential moments:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{c|M(A)|}\big]<\infty

for every
c>0
; see Lemma 25.7 in
[
63
]
.

(ii)

K(A)
is compound Poisson random variable that can be expressed
as follows:

(A.2)

\displaystyle K(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{Q}Y_{j}

with
\{Y_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
independent random variables
with common distribution
\frac{1}{\nu(\{|z|>1\})}\nu|_{\{|z|>1\}}
,
and
Q
a Poisson random variable with mean
\mathbf{M}:=\textup{Leb}(A)\nu(\{|z|>1\})
that is independent
of
\{Y_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
.

Put
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
. It is easy to see that for any finite
p>0

and for any
finite
measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
,
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle x\rangle^{p}\mu(dx)\sim\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\mu(dx),

from which we deduce that the equivalence (
1.6
) can be rewritten as

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle L_{b}(A)\rangle^{p}\big]<\infty\,\,\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\,\,\int_{\{|z|>1\}}\langle x\rangle^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty.

Proof of (
A.3
) and (
1.6
).

We use the same argument as in the proof of Theorem 25.3 of
[
63
]
.
Fix
p\in(0,\infty)
. Observe first that the function
x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\langle x\rangle^{p}

is sub-multiplicative meaning
that

(A.4)

\displaystyle\langle x+y\rangle^{p}\leq 2^{p}\langle x\rangle^{p}\cdot\langle y\rangle^{p}.

First, assume that
\mathbb{E}\big[\langle L_{b}(A)\rangle^{p}\big]<\infty
, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle b\cdot\textup{Leb}(A)+M(A)+K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]<\infty.

It follows that for
some

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle x_{0}+K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]<\infty.

Then, we can deduce from (
A.4
) with

K(A)=K(A)+x_{0}+(-x_{0})
that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p}\langle-x_{0}\rangle^{p}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle x_{0}+K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]<\infty.

Note that we can get from (
A.2
)
that

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[\langle K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\mathbf{M}}\frac{\mathbf{M}^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Y_{1}+...+Y_{n}\rangle^{p}\big]\\
&\geq e^{-\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}\big],\end{aligned}

which, together with (
A.5
), implies

\int_{\{|z|>1\}}\langle x\rangle^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty.

For the other direction, we can write by using
(
A.4
), (
A.1
), and (
A.6
) with independence
among
Y_{j}
’s
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle L_{b}(A)\rangle^{p}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle K(A)\rangle^{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2+e^{-\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}\big]+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}e^{-\mathbf{M}}\frac{\mathbf{M}^{n}}{n!}\mathbb{E}\big[2^{p(n-1)}\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}\cdots\langle Y_{n}\rangle^{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2+e^{-\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}\big]+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}e^{-\mathbf{M}}\frac{\mathbf{M}^{n}}{n!}2^{p(n-1)}\big(\mathbb{E}[\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}]\big)^{n}<\infty,

provided that
\mathbb{E}[\langle Y_{1}\rangle^{p}]\sim\int_{\{|z|>1\}}\langle x\rangle^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty
. Hence the equivalence (
A.3
) is verified, and so is
the equivalence (
1.6
).
∎
