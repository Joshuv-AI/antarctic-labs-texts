On the modularity of 3 − 3- regular random graphs and random graphs with given degree sequences
Lyuben Lichev, Dieter Mitsche

Abstract
The modularity of a graph is a parameter that measures its community structure; the higher its value (between 0 0 and 1 1 ), the more clustered the graph is. In this paper we show that the modularity of a random 3 − 3- regular graph is at least 0.667026 0.667026 asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.), thereby proving a conjecture of McDiarmid and Skerman. We also improve the a.a.s. upper bound given therein to 0.789998 0.789998 . For a uniformly chosen graph G n G_{n} over a given bounded degree sequence with average degree d ⁡ ( G n ) d(G_{n}) and with | C ​ C ​ ( G n ) | |CC(G_{n})| many connected components, we distinguish two regimes with respect to the existence of a giant component. In the subcritical regime, we compute the second term of the modularity. In the supercritical regime, we prove that there is ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , for which the modularity is a.a.s. at least 2 ​ ( 1 − μ ) d ⁡ ( G n ) + ε , \dfrac{2\left(1-\mu\right)}{d(G_{n})}+\varepsilon, where μ \mu is the asymptotically almost sure limit of | C ​ C ​ ( G n ) | n \dfrac{|CC(G_{n})|}{n} .

Introduction

Recent years have seen a fast increase of network data available and the need for detecting clusters - disjoint groups of nodes with many connections between the elements within a single group and rather few connections between elements of different groups - has become more and more important. Identifying clusters helps to exploit a network more effectively: for example, having detected clusters in social networks allows for targeted advertisements, or having detected clusters in collaboration networks allows for identifying similar papers. Whereas traditional clustering approaches either fix the number of clusters and/or the sizes of the clusters, the concept of
modularity
allows for more flexibility here: whilst rewarding a partition for containing edges within its parts, it penalizes parts incident to too many edges. Introduced by Newman and Girvan in
[
25
]
, it was first studied in physics (see
[
11
,
19
]
) due to its connections to the Potts model in statistical physics presented in
[
29
]
. It was then analyzed in different applications including protein discovery and identifying connections between websites: see
[
10
]
and
[
27
]
for surveys on the use of modularity for community detection in networks. After this successful application in practice, modularity was then also studied from a mathematical point of view. We first give the definition and our results and then refer to related work in the mathematics literature.

For a subset
A
of vertices of
G
, we denote by
e(A)
the number of edges with two endvertices in

and by
\text{vol}(A)
the sum of the degrees of the vertices in

modularity of a partition
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}\}
of the vertices of a graph

q(\mathcal{A})=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k}e(A_{i})-\dfrac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}.

(1)

The first term corresponds to the proportion of edges of
G
that have both endvertices in the same part of
\mathcal{A}
; this term ensures that the edge density within the communities is high. The second term stands for the expected proportion of edges within the given parts in a random graph with the same degree sequence as
G
(or also in a vertex-weighted random graph e.g. given by the Chung-Lu model). Its form suggests that it may be interpreted as a sort of “degree tax”.

For a graph
G=(V,E)

of

where

(for a graph

Related work.

After the introduction of the concept in the already mentioned paper by Newman and Girvan
[
25
]
, due to its success in applications, modularity was analyzed for different graph classes: cycles were analyzed in
[
5
]
, lattices in
[
20
]
and
[
12
]
respectively.
The study of modularity in trees was initiated by Bagrow in
[
1
]
, who showed that
k
-ary trees as well as Galton-Watson-trees have modularity tending to
1
. Later De Montgolfier, Soto and Viennot proved in
[
24
]
that trees with maximum degree
\Delta=o(n^{1/5})
have modularity tending to
1
, which was then extended by McDiarmid and Skerman
[
21
]
to trees with maximum degree
o(n)
(and more generally to graphs that are ’treelike’ in the sense of having low treewidth). More generally, Ostroumova, Prokhorenkova, Prałat, and Raigorodskii showed in
[
28
]
that all connected graphs
G
on
n
vertices with maximal degree
\Delta(n)=o(n)
and average degree
d(n)
satisfy
q^{*}(G)\geq\frac{2}{d(n)}-3\sqrt{\frac{\Delta(n)}{nd(n)}}-\frac{\Delta(n)}{nd(n)}
.
Modularity was also studied for random graphs: the Erdős-Rényi model
G(n,p)
was studied by McDiarmid and Skerman in
[
22
]
: they showed that for
p\leq 1/n
, a.a.s.
q^{*}(G(n,p))=1+o(1)
, whereas for
p\geq 1/n
and
p<1
, a.a.s.
q^{*}(G(n,p))=\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{np}})
. Their results transfer also to the
G(n,M)
model.
For random regular graphs, besides the already mentioned bounds given in
[
21
]
and
[
28
]
of
\frac{2}{3}\leq q^{*}(G)\leq 0.804
for
G\in\mathcal{G}_{3}(n)
, in
[
21
]
McDiarmid and Skerman gave also lower and upper bounds for
d-
regular graphs for other values of
d
, in particular they showed that
0.7631/\sqrt{d}\leq q^{*}(G_{d}(n))
for
d
sufficiently large, and
q^{*}(G_{d}(n))\leq 2/\sqrt{d}
for all
d\geq 3
. For random
2-
regular graphs, they also proved
q^{*}(G_{2}(n))=1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}+o(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n})
. Regarding other models of random graphs, in
[
28
]
it was proved that for a graph
G
chosen according to the preferential attachment model, when adding
m\geq 2
edges at a time,
\max\{\frac{1}{m}-o(1),\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})\}\leq q^{*}(G)<0.94
, where the constant hidden in the asymptotic notation is such that for
m\geq 1000
the second lower bound is better. In the same paper, they also showed that for the spatial preferential attachment model with certain conditions on the parameters the modularity is
1+o(1)
.
From a computational point of view, finding the modularity of a graph was proved to be NP-hard and even approximation of the modularity within a constant multiplicative factor remains NP-hard, see
[
4
]
and
[
7
]
. The concept of modularity was recently extended to hypergraphs, see
[
18
]
.

Graph theoretic preliminaries

First, for a graph
G(V,E)
with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
we define the
relative modularity
q_{r}(A)
of a set

q_{r}(A):=\dfrac{n}{|A|}\left(\dfrac{e(A)}{m}-\dfrac{\text{vol}(A)^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right).

Denoting by
d
the average degree of the graph
G
, one may rewrite this formula as

q_{r}(A):=\dfrac{2e(A)}{d|A|}-\dfrac{\text{vol}(A)^{2}}{d^{2}|A|n}.

(3)

The main motivation of this definition is that one may define the modularity of a partition
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k})
of
V
as a weighted average of the relative modularities of its parts:

q(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\dfrac{|A_{i}|}{n}q_{r}(A_{i}).

In particular, if the modularity of the partition
\mathcal{A}
is at least
q
, then there must exist a part
A_{i}
with relative modularity at least
q
. At the same time, the relative modularity of a part
A_{i}
depends only on
A_{i}
itself and not on the partition that contains it.

One may easily remark that the relative modularity of a set of vertices
A
in a
d-
regular graph can be rewritten as

q_{r}(A)=\dfrac{2e(A)}{d|A|}-\dfrac{|A|}{n}.

Lemma 2.2

We continue with a simple general lower bound on the modularity:

Lemma 2.4
.

The modularity of a graph
G
on
n
vertices, with average degree
d
and maximal degree bounded from above by
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
, is at least

\dfrac{2(n-|CC(G)|)}{dn}-O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

In the proof of Lemma
2.4
we apply Lemma
2.1
to divide the graph into components of order at most
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Let us call
\mathcal{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{r})
the partition of the vertices of
G
into connected components. For every connected component
C_{i}
with more than
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
vertices, apply Lemma
2.1
to an arbitrary spanning tree of
C_{i}
. We deduce that
C_{i}
can be partitioned into connected subgraphs of order between
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
and
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}\}
be the partition obtained from
\mathcal{C}
after dividing the connected components of
G
of order more than
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
.

By convexity of the function
x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto x^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, the sum
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}|A_{i}|^{2}
is maximal when all but at most one of the terms are either equal to
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
or to 0 for any fixed
k
. We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}|A_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\hskip 2.5pt\max_{1\leq i\leq k}|A_{i}|\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}|A_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\hskip 2.5pt\Delta n\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil.

Since in the end we have exactly
k
connected components induced by the vertex sets in
\mathcal{A}
, we obtain

q^{*}(G_{n})\geq q(\mathcal{A})=\dfrac{2}{nd}\sum_{i=1}^{k}e(A_{i})-\dfrac{1}{n^{2}d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}\geq\dfrac{2(n-1-(r-1)-(k-r))}{nd}-\dfrac{\Delta^{3}\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil}{nd^{2}}.

It remains to observe that all parts in
\mathcal{A}
obtained from the division of connected components of
G
of order more than
\Delta\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
have order at least
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
by definition, so
k-r\leq\dfrac{n}{\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor}=O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. This proves the lemma.

Configuration model

The probability space of (multi-)graphs, with which we will be working until the end of this paper, is the
configuration model
introduced by Bender and Canfield in
[
2
]
and further developed by Bollobás in
[
3
]
and by Wormald in
[
32
]
. We describe it first for the case of
d
-regular graphs: we are given
dn
points (also called
half-edges
), with
dn
being even, indexed by
(P_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq d,1\leq j\leq n}
and regrouped into
n
buckets according to their second index. The probability space we work with is the space of perfect matchings of these
dn
points equipped with the uniform probability. We call
configuration
a perfect matching of
(P_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq d,1\leq j\leq n}
.
We now reconstruct the random
d-
regular graph model as follows: we identify the
d-
point buckets with the vertices of our random graph. By abuse of terminology, we use both buckets and vertices in the sequel to refer to the same objects by the above identification. An edge in the random regular graph between two (not necessarily different) vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
corresponds to an edge of the configuration between a point
P
in the bucket
v
and a point
P^{\prime}
in the bucket
v^{\prime}
. It is well known that this model is contiguous to the uniform distribution on random
d-
regular graphs for constant values of
d
, see
[
15
]
. This model can then be easily generalized for graphs with given degree sequences: given a sequence
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
with
d_{i}
denoting the degree of the
i
-th vertex such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=2m
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, identify the
i
-th vertex with a bucket having
d_{i}
points. As before, choose a perfect matching uniformly at random among all pairings and add an edge in the graph between the two vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
for every pair of points
(P_{1},P_{2})
from the buckets
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
that participates in a common edge of the matching.

Differential equation method

The theory of differential equations used to describe the evolution of a discrete random process was introduced by Wormald (see
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
). Given a sequence of discrete random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the basic idea is to consider the expected change between times
t
and
t+1
. Regarding the trajectories
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(properly rescaled) as continuous, one may write the ordinary differential equations suggested by the expected changes. Concentration results from martingale theory are then used to show that, as the size of the input grows large, under relatively mild conditions the trajectory
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is highly concentrated around the value suggested by the solution of the differential equation for a wide range of
t
.

The precise formulation of the theorem given here is taken from
[
31
]
: we say that a function
f
is said to be
L
-Lipschitz on
D\subseteq R^{\ell}
, if
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq L\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}|x_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k}|
holds for all points
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
and
x^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{\ell})
in
D
, where
\max_{1\leq k\leq\ell}|x_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k}|
is the
\ell^{\infty}-
distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
31
]

Given
a,n\geq 1
, a bounded domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{a+1}
, functions
(F_{k})_{1\leq k\leq a}
with

, and

-algebras

, suppose that the random variables

are

. Suppose also that for all

and all

:

1.

\left|\mathbb{E}(Y_{k}(i+1)-Y_{k}(i)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{i})-F_{k}(i/n,Y_{1}(i)/n,\ldots,Y_{a}(i)/n)\right|\leq\delta
for some

Remark 2.7
.

In this paper we only work with differential equations of the type

where

is a Lipschitz function on a domain
D

Let
a,b
be two positive real numbers with
b<a
. Let
(U_{i})_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor an\rfloor}
be urns, each of them with space for at most two balls, and
(B_{j})_{1\leq j\leq\lfloor 2bn\rfloor}
be balls that are, one after the other, thrown uniformly into some urn, where the probability that a ball is thrown into an urn is proportional to the free space in this urn at the moment of throwing.

Lemma 2.8
.

A.a.s. at the end of the process there will be
\dfrac{b(2a-b)}{a}n+o(n)
urns with at least 1 ball.

Proof.

Denote by
N_{0}
the number of urns containing no ball after
2bn
steps. We have that

\mathbb{E}[N_{0}]=\dfrac{\binom{2an-2}{2bn}}{\binom{2an}{2bn}}an=(1+o(1))\dfrac{(a-b)^{2}}{a}n

and

\mathbb{E}[N_{0}(N_{0}-1)]=\dfrac{\binom{2an-4}{2bn}}{\binom{2an}{2bn}}an(an-1)=(1+o(1))\dfrac{(a-b)^{4}}{a^{2}}n^{2}.

One may conclude by the second moment method that the number of urns with at least 1 ball in the end of the process is a.a.s.
(1+o(1))\left(an-\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{a}n\right)=(1+o(1))\frac{b(2b-a)}{a}n
.

∎

\displaystyle=\hskip 3.00003pt\dfrac{1}{|Aut(T)|}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}t_{i}!i!^{t_{i}}\right)\dfrac{(D(n)-1-2(t-1))!!}{(D(n)-1)!!}.

Indeed, one needs to:

•

for every
i\in[\Delta]
, choose the
t_{i}
vertices of degree
i
in
\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}
ways;

•

decide on the position of each vertex of degree
i
in
T
in
t_{i}!
ways;

•

decide on the order of the half-edges, attached to every vertex of degree
i
in
T
, in
i!
ways (since we are counting configurations here, half-edges are labeled);

•

and finally, divide by the probability of constructing all
t-1
edges in
T
.

One also has that:

•

|Aut(T)|\leq t!
,

•

for every
i\in[\Delta]
,

\prod_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\dfrac{d_{i}(n)-j}{d_{i}(n)}=\exp\left(-(1+o(1))\sum_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\dfrac{j}{d_{i}(n)}\right)=1-o(1),

•

\prod_{0\leq j\leq t-2}\dfrac{D(n)-1-2(j-1)}{D(n)}=\exp\left(-(1+o(1))\sum_{0\leq j\leq t-2}\dfrac{1+2(j-1)}{D(n)}\right)=1-o(1).

Therefore, since
t\leq\sqrt{\log n}
, in the regime
n\to+\infty
one may bound the expectation from below by

(1-o(1))\dfrac{1}{t!}D(n)\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}=n^{1-o(1)}.

The variance of
\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}ar(\mathcal{N}_{n}(T))=

\displaystyle\hskip 1.99997pt\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip 1.99997pt\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]+\dfrac{1}{|Aut(T)|^{2}}\mathbb{\prod}_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}\binom{d_{i}(n)-t_{i}}{t_{i}}t_{i}!^{2}i!^{2t_{i}}\dfrac{(D(n)-1-2(2t-2))!!}{(D(n)-1)!!}-

\displaystyle\hskip 1.99997pt\left(\dfrac{1}{|Aut(T)|}\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}(t_{i})!i!^{t_{i}}\dfrac{(D(n)-1-2(t-1))!!}{(D(n)-1)!!}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip 1.99997pt\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]+\dfrac{1}{|Aut(T)|^{2}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}(t_{i})!i!^{t_{i}}\dfrac{(D(n)-1-2(t-1))!!}{(D(n)-1)!!}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 1.99997pt\left(\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\prod_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\dfrac{d_{i}(n)-t_{i}-j}{d_{i}(n)-j}\right)\left(\prod_{0\leq j\leq t-2}\dfrac{D(n)-1-2j}{D(n)-1-2(t-1)-2j}\right)-1\right).

Now, one may use the standard bound

\prod_{1\leq i\leq t}(1+x_{i})\leq\exp\bigg(\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}x_{i}\bigg)

to deduce that

\prod_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\dfrac{d_{i}(n)-t_{i}-j}{d_{i}(n)-j}\leq\exp\left(-t_{i}\sum_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\dfrac{1}{d_{i}(n)-j}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\dfrac{t_{i}^{2}}{d_{i}(n)}\right)

(10)

and

\displaystyle\prod_{0\leq j\leq t-2}\dfrac{D(n)-1-2j}{D(n)-1-2(t-1)-2j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(2(t-1)\sum_{0\leq j\leq t-2}\dfrac{1}{D(n)-1-2(t-1)-2j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(\dfrac{2t^{2}}{D(n)-4t}\right).

We deduce that for every large enough
n

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}ar(\mathcal{N}_{n}(T))

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2}\left(\exp\left(-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\dfrac{t_{i}^{2}}{d_{i}(n)}+\dfrac{2t^{2}}{D(n)-4t}\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]+2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2}\left(\dfrac{2t^{2}}{D(n)-4t}-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\dfrac{t_{i}^{2}}{d_{i}(n)}\right)\leq\hskip 2.5pt\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]+\dfrac{(4+o(1))\log n}{D(n)}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2}.

We conclude by Chebyshev’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\geq\alpha)\leq\dfrac{\mathbb{V}ar(\mathcal{N}_{n}(T))}{\alpha^{2}}.

Choosing for example
\alpha=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2/3}
leads to

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2/3})\leq\dfrac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{1/3}}+\dfrac{(4+o(1))(\log n)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2/3}}{D(n)}\leq\dfrac{1}{n^{1/4}}.

Since
n\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\geq n^{2/3})\leq\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]^{2/3})\leq\dfrac{1}{n^{1/4}}.

The lemma is proved.
∎

Observation 5.6

Under the conditions of point

Lemma 5.7
.

A.a.s.

\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\emph{\text{vol}}(A_{i})^{2}=(1+o(1))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)\end{subarray}}\emph{\text{vol}}(A_{i})^{2}.

Proof.

First, note that the expected number of trees that consist of a single edge is

\dfrac{1}{2}\left(p_{1}n\times\dfrac{p_{1}}{M}\right).

By Lemma
5.5
, the number of isolated edges in
G(n)
is sharply concentrated. Hence, a.a.s.
k=k(n)
is at least
\dfrac{p^{2}_{1}}{4M}n
and

\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}\geq\dfrac{p^{2}_{1}n}{M}.

By Theorem
5.4
, there are at most
R_{2}\log n
cycles in
G(n)
, each containing at most
R_{1}\log n
vertices a.a.s. In total, the components with cycles contribute to
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}
at most

\Delta^{2}R^{2}_{1}R_{2}\log^{3}n=O\left(\log^{3}n\right)=o(n)

a.a.s.

Also, by Observation
5.6
, there is some positive constant
c>0
such that the number of trees of order at least
\sqrt{\log n}
contains at most
\exp(-c\sqrt{\log n})
vertices a.a.s. Thus, the connected components of order at least
\sqrt{\log n}
contribute to
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}
at most

n\exp\left(-c\sqrt{\log n}\right)(R_{1}\log n)^{2}=o(n).

Putting together the three statements above proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

A.a.s.

\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\leq n^{2/3}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
we have that for a particular tree
T
of order at most
\sqrt{\log n}
,

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{n}(T)]|\geq n^{2/3})\leq\dfrac{1}{n^{1/4}}.

On the other hand, it is well-known that there are
t^{t-2}
trees of order
t
(see for example
[
17
]
), and therefore by a union bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}|\mathcal{N}(T)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}(T)]|\geq n^{2/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq\sqrt{\log n}}k^{k-2}\dfrac{1}{n^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\sqrt{\log n}^{\sqrt{\log n}}\dfrac{1}{n^{1/4}}=\hskip 2.5pto(1).

The lemma is proved.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

A.a.s.

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\emph{\text{vol}}(A_{i})^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right]-\dfrac{\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\emph{\text{vol}}(A_{i})^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right|=o\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right).

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right]-\dfrac{\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\dfrac{1}{4m^{2}}|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}(T)]-\mathcal{N}(T)|(2e(T))^{2}.

By Lemma
5.8
, we have that a.a.s. the last expression is at most

\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\dfrac{1}{4m^{2}}n^{2/3}(2e(T))^{2}\leq\dfrac{\left|\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)\right|n^{2/3}\log n}{m^{2}}.

Since the number of trees on
t
vertices is

, one has

\dfrac{\left|\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)\right|n^{2/3}\log n}{m^{2}}\leq\dfrac{\sqrt{\log n}^{\sqrt{\log n}}n^{2/3}\log n}{m^{2}}=o\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right).

The corollary is proved.

Proof of point
1
of Theorem
1.3
.

By combining Lemma
5.7
and Corollary
5.9
it is sufficient to estimate

\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\sum_{G[A_{i}]\in\mathcal{T}\left(\sqrt{\log n}\right)}\text{vol}(A_{i})^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right].

We do this now.

•

First, for every
i\in[\Delta]
, choose the vertices of degree
i
in
T
in
\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}
ways.

•

A well-known result gives the number of trees on
t
vertices with
t_{i}
vertices of degree
i
for every
i\in[\Delta]
, which is equal to (again, see for example
[
17
]
):

\dfrac{(t-2)!}{(\Delta-1)!^{t_{\Delta}}(\Delta-2)!^{t_{\Delta-1}}\dots 2!^{t_{3}}}.

Observe that a sequence
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})
is a
tree sequence
(that is, there is a tree with
t_{i}
vertices of degree
i
for every
i\in[\Delta]
) if and only if satisfies that the number of edges is one less than the number of vertices:

\dfrac{t_{1}+2t_{2}+\dots\Delta t_{\Delta}}{2}=t_{1}+\dots+t_{\Delta}-1,

which is equivalent to

t_{1}=2+\sum_{3\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-2)t_{i}.

(11)

•

Since we count configurations and not simply graphs, for every vertex we multiply by the product of the factorials of the degrees

\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}i!^{t_{i}}

(this gives the number of permutations of the half-edges at each vertex).

•

Finally, multiply by the probability that all
e(T)
edges are present, which is given by

\dfrac{1}{(D(n)-1)(D(n)-3)\dots(D(n)-1-2(e(T)-1))}.

We get that the expected number of trees with
t_{i}
vertices of degree
i
, for every
i\in[\Delta]
, is given by

\displaystyle\dfrac{\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\binom{d_{i}(n)}{t_{i}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}i!^{t_{i}}}{(D(n)-1)(D(n)-3)\dots(D(n)-1-2(t-2))}\dfrac{(t-2)!}{\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-1)!^{t_{i}}}

(12)

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle D(n)\prod_{0\leq i\leq t-2}\dfrac{1}{1-\frac{1+2i}{D(n)}}\prod_{0\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\prod_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{d_{i}(n)}\right)\right)\dfrac{1}{t(t-1)}\binom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}.

Standard analysis shows that

\prod_{0\leq i\leq t-2}\left(1-\dfrac{1+2i}{D(n)}\right)=1-\dfrac{(t-1)^{2}}{D(n)}+o\left(\dfrac{(t-1)^{2}}{D(n)}\right)=1-O\left(\dfrac{\log n}{n}\right),

and by assumption

\prod_{0\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\prod_{0\leq j\leq t_{i}-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{d_{i}(n)}\right)\right)=(1+o(1))\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(1-\dfrac{t_{i}(t_{i}-1)}{2d_{i}(n)}\right)=1-O\left(\dfrac{\log n}{n}\right).

Thus, for every tree sequence
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})
, the above two products do not modify the first order in (
12
). Since for every tree
T
we are interested in the volume of
T
, we multiply each term by
\text{vol}(T)^{2}=(2e(T))^{2}=4(t-1)^{2}
and sum over all tree sequences to deduce the value of
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\dfrac{\text{vol}^{2}(A_{i})}{4m^{2}}\right]
up to a
(1+o(1))-
factor:

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq t\leq\sqrt{\log n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}+t_{2}+\dots+t_{\Delta}=t;\\
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})\\
\text{is a tree sequence}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left(1+O\left(\dfrac{\log n}{n}\right)\right)\dfrac{4(t-1)}{t}\dbinom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}\right)D(n)}{4\left(\dfrac{D(n)}{2}\right)^{2}}.

(13)

Notice that the factor

\binom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}\right)

in (
13
) can be interpreted as the probability that a die with outcomes
1,2,\dots,\Delta
with respective probabilities
\dfrac{d_{1}(n)}{D(n)},\dfrac{2d_{2}(n)}{D(n)},\dots,\dfrac{\Delta d_{\Delta}(n)}{D(n)}
gives
t_{1}
ones,
t_{2}
twos, etc. over
t
independent trials. Under the above interpretation, denote by
X_{i}
the number of trials with outcome
i
. Thus, by a Chernoff bound (Lemma
2.5
) we have for every
\delta\in(0,1)
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|X_{i}-\dfrac{id_{i}(n)t}{D(n)}\right|\geq\dfrac{\delta id_{i}(n)t}{D(n)}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\dfrac{\delta^{2}id_{i}(n)t}{3D(n)}\right).

Recall that under the conditions of point
1
of Theorem
1.3
one has

\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}i(i-2)p_{i}<0.

Let

\delta(n)=-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\dfrac{i(i-2)d_{i}(n)}{D(n)}.

Since for every
i\in[\Delta]
we have that
\dfrac{d_{i}(n)}{n}\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}p_{i}
, for every large enough
n
we have

\delta(n)\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\delta:=-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\dfrac{i(i-2)p_{i}}{M}\in(0,1].

Moreover, for every
n
,
\delta(n)\leq 1
. We deduce that for every large enough
n

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{\Delta})\text{ is a tree sequence }|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t)

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-2)X_{i}=-2\hskip 1.99997pt\bigg|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t\right)

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-2)(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])=-\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-2)\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]-2\hskip 1.99997pt\bigg|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}(i-2)(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\geq\dfrac{\delta(n)t}{2}\hskip 1.99997pt\bigg|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2}\mathbb{P}\left(|(i-2)(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])|\geq\dfrac{|i-2|id_{i}(n)}{2D(n)}t\hskip 1.99997pt\bigg|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t\right)

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.5pt

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2}\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]|\geq\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{2D(n)}t\hskip 1.99997pt\bigg|\hskip 1.99997ptX_{1}+\dots+X_{\Delta}=t\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}}{3}t\right)\leq\hskip 2.5pt\Delta\max_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2}\exp\left(-\dfrac{ip_{i}}{24M}t\right).

Thus, for every large enough
n
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}+t_{2}+\dots+t_{\Delta}=t;\\
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})\\
\text{ is a tree sequence}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{t-1}{t}\binom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\hskip 3.00003pt\Delta\max_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2,p_{i}\neq 0}\exp\left(-\dfrac{ip_{i}}{24M}t\right)

\displaystyle=\hskip 3.00003pt\Delta\exp\left(-\min_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,i\neq 2,p_{i}\neq 0}\left\{\dfrac{ip_{i}}{24M}\right\}t\right).

By using the dominated convergence theorem we conclude that the sum

\sum_{1\leq t\leq\sqrt{\log n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}+t_{2}+\dots+t_{\Delta}=t;\\
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})\\
\text{is a tree sequence}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{4(t-1)}{t}\binom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{id_{i}(n)}{D(n)}\right)^{t_{i}}\right)

converges to a constant
c=c(\boldsymbol{p},\Delta)>0
given by

\sum_{t\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}+t_{2}+\dots+t_{\Delta}=t;\\
(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta})\\
\text{is a tree sequence}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{4(t-1)}{t}\binom{t}{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{ip_{i}}{M}\right)^{t_{i}}\right),

which by (
11
) can be rewritten as

4\sum_{t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{N}}\dfrac{t_{2}+2t_{3}+\dots+(\Delta-1)t_{\Delta}+1}{t_{2}+2t_{3}+\dots+(\Delta-1)t_{\Delta}+2}\binom{t_{2}+2t_{3}\dots+(\Delta-1)t_{\Delta}+2}{\sum(i-2)t_{i}+2,t_{2},\dots,t_{\Delta}}\left(\dfrac{p_{1}}{M}\right)^{2}\prod_{2\leq i\leq\Delta}\left(\dfrac{ip_{i}}{M}\left(\dfrac{p_{1}}{M}\right)^{(i-2)}\right)^{t_{i}}.

This finishes the proof of point
1
of Theorem
1.3
.

In this subsection, we prove point
2
of Theorem
1.3
. First, by Theorem
5.4
a.a.s. all connected components except the largest one have size

. Thus, since

. Thus, it remains to study the giant component. From now on, we condition on the set of vertices of

in the lower bound for random
3-

Lemma 5.10
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that the following holds: for every
C>0
there exists
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
so that a.a.s. there is a set of vertices

consists of at most

connected components.

We prove Lemma
5.10
later on. Admitting Lemma
5.10
, we prove point
2
of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of point
2
of Theorem
1.3
assuming Lemma
5.10
.

Notice that by assumption of Lemma
5.10
,
C_{max}\setminus A_{max}
contains at most

. Thus, by Lemma

, one may divide each such component of

and

(recall that

Lemma 5.30

6
Discussion

On the one hand, we showed that in sparse random graphs coming from a supercritical configuration model the modularity is strictly larger than the trivial lower bound obtained by partitioning into connected components. On the other hand, for random
3-
regular graphs we are more precise by quantifying the gain over this trivial bound (which in this case is equal to
2/3
). Without doubt, by adding more stages (that is, by considering longer chains) in the analysis of the lower bound in the case of random
3-
regular graphs, one could improve the lower bound given by Theorem
1.1
by a little bit. The upper bound still being far, we opted for not pushing this to the limit. Needless to say, it would be interesting to find the exact value of the modularity of random
3-
regular graphs, but our methods are not strong enough to determine this value. Another point for further thought is to find an asymptotic expression for
q^{*}(G(n))
when
Q=0
in Theorem
1.3
- it seems to us that, as it is often the case, this critical regime may be the most complicated to describe quantitatively.
