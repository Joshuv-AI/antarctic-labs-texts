Bayesian Analysis of Generalized Hierarchical Indian Buffet Processes for Within and Across Group Sharing of Latent Features
Lancelot F. James

Abstract
Bayesian nonparametric hierarchical priors are highly effective in providing flexible models for latent data structures exhibiting sharing of information within and across groups. In this work, we focus on latent feature allocation models, where the data structures correspond to multi-sets or unbounded sparse matrices, which we refer to as generalized hierarchical Indian Buffet processes (HIBP). These are based on hierarchical versions of generalized spike and slab Indian Buffet processes (IBP), where the fundamental development in this regard is the Bernoulli-based HIBP, devised by Thibaux and Jordan (2007), as a hierarchical extension of the IBP devised by Griffiths and Ghahramani (2005). With a focus on Bayesian inference, we provide novel explicit descriptions of the joint, marginal, and posterior distributions of the HIBP, significantly advancing our understanding of these processes. Our results allow for exact sampling for the otherwise complex joint marginal distributions. We provide a general characterization of their posterior distributions as well as highlight bottlenecks for practical implementation. Our main focus then shifts to specific tractable results for the remarkable case of Poisson HIBP, which correspond to generalizations of mixed Poisson random count models arising in genetics, imaging, topic modeling, random occupancy, and species sampling models. We show they also have important relations to Bayesian nonparametric latent class models appearing in the literature. Furthermore, we show that all general HIBP may be coupled to Poisson HIBP, allowing for further analysis of such processes.



Keywords:
Bayesian nonparametrics, Bayesian Statistical Machine Learning, Hierarchical Indian Buffet Process, Latent Feature models, Spike and Slab priors

1
Introduction

Bayesian nonparametric hierarchical priors are highly effective in providing flexible models for latent data structures exhibiting sharing of information between and across groups.
Most prominent is the Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP)
[
33
]
, and its subsequent variants, which model latent clustering/class between and across groups. Note in these models individuals share at most one feature within and across groups.

In this work, we focus on tractable Bayesian analysis for hierarchical notions of latent feature allocation models, where the data structures correspond to multi-sets or unbounded sparse matrices. The fundamental development in this regard is the Hierarchical Indian Buffet Process (HIBP), devised in
[
34
]
. The HIBP utilizes a hierarchy of Beta processes to model the sharing of latent features across
J
distinct groups, where each individual group
j
(for
j=1,2,\ldots,J
) consists of
M_{j}
customers. Specifically, each group
j
generates a latent binary random matrix, reflecting the within-group sharing of features, according to a beta-Bernoulli Indian Buffet Process (IBP) prior first devised in
[
12
]
. Similar to the HDP, this information is merged/shared across groups according to another common Beta process which is an example of a Completely Random measure (CRM).

Here we extend this to hierarchical notions of the general spike and slab IBP referred to as generalized HIBP, which include models already appearing in the literature. Most notably our focus will be on Poisson based HIBP of which, single customer, Poisson-Gamma-Gamma or equivalently Negative Binomial-Gamma random count models treated in
[
36
,
38
]
, are notable special cases that otherwise have rich modelling capabilities, and properties. In general, the Poisson HIBP generates complex random count matrices based on multivariate mixed Poisson processes and correspond to generalizations of models arising in genetics, imaging, topic modeling, random occupancy, and species sampling models. We show they also have important relations to Bayesian nonparametric latent class models appearing in the literature. A key feature of our exposition is that these connections allow for distributional recovery of individual latent counts that would otherwise be observables in the non-hierarchical setting. Furthermore, we show that all general HIBP may be coupled to Poisson HIBP, allowing for further analysis of such processes.

The basic Indian Buffet process (IBP) first devised in
[
12
]
, constitutes a class of flexible, and tractable, Bayesian non-parametric priors for a wide range of statistical applications involving latent features. See for example
[
25
]
, for a recent interesting application to problems in genetics. See
[
4
]
for more on the IBP sequential scheme for allocation of features to individual customers, originally described in
[
12
]
, and comparisons with general Chinese restaurant processes for latent class models. The IBP has inspired the development and investigation of generalizations which induce non-binary entries. Notably,
[
35
]
pointed out that images, which play the analogous roles of customers, exhibit multiple occurrences of a feature within a single image, and hence in analogy to the classic gamma-Poisson Bayesian parametric model, proposed the use of Poisson-type IBP with a gamma process prior in that setting. Other possibilities for distributions have been treated in the literature. In particular
[
17
]
provides a unified framework for the posterior analysis of general
spike and slab
IBP type models, and important multivariate extensions, which produce sparse random matrices with arbitrary valued entries based on random variables having mass at zero, but otherwise producing non-zero entries corresponding to any random variable. In addition, see
[
2
]
for potential ideas in regards to matrices with general spike and slab entries. In this regard it is notable that models with general compound Poisson entries, and also complex random count matrices, shall arise naturally within the context of our work. Also the work of
[
6
]
, describe modifications of the Bernoulli IBP utilizing, instead of CRM, scaled subordinators
[
20
]
for better modelling of unseen-features/species models arising for instance in genetics. This modification may be easily applied to our present exposition although we refrain from doing so here. In particular,
[
3
]
discuss this modification for general IBP and hierarchical latent feature models proposed by
[
24
]
and also the HIBP processes we analyze here. Their view of the HIBP is based on less developed properties which we aim to rectify here.

Outline and contributions

As already discussed in for instance the works of
[
24
,
34
,

]
the extension of IBP type processes to processes sharing multiple features over groups has a vast number of possible applications. What remains is a detailed analysis, such as in the case of the Dirichlet process
[

In addition, it is important to establish connections to existing models that may arise in other contexts. Our aim is to do this for the HIBP models we describe here. Specifically in section 2 we begin with more details of the definitions of IBP and HIBP and their building blocks based on completely random measures. We first revisit the key results from
[
17
]
in the general IBP setting, in Proposition
2.1
highlighting a more accessible perspective in terms of a method of decompositions. We then use this along with basic properties of completely random measures to provide initial representations of the HIBP, and then proceed to provide analysis of a key, otherwise hidden, multivariate Poisson process dictating the allocation of features across groups. Notably this process can be seen as a multivariate extension of processes appearing in
[
27
]
. Section 3 then establishes a result to exactly sample from the marginal distribution of any HIBP process. Section 3.1 provides specific details for Poisson HIBP and importantly show any HIBP can be coupled to the Poisson framework. More variables, with interpretations gained in
[
27
]
, are introduced and will form the basis for a method of distributional recovery of key quantities for posterior analysis. Section 4 presents a general description of the posterior distribution for all HIBP, highlighting general stumbling blocks in terms of recovery of latent quantities that would normally be observed in the non-hierarchical setting. Rather than attempting a general discussion of how to address this issue, the remainder of our work focuses on achieving full tractability in the case of general mixed Poisson processes, that is the Poisson HIBP. We also note that the fact that we can couple general HIBP to this framework allows one to obtain further details in those cases. Briefly, focusing on the Poisson HIBP, in section 5 we obtain tractable descriptions of the posterior and predictive descriptions and a description of the otherwise complex marginal distribution which one can see, due to our representations, as sums over Gibbs (product type) structure in relation to permanental processes, see for instance
[
9
]
, appearing in the study of multivariate negative binomial and more general processes, which are otherwise difficult to sample from. We also describe a highy tractable simplification for HIBP which otherwise exhibits a rich structure for modelling with perhaps surprising connections to Pitman-Yor and finite dimensional versions of such processes as arising in recent work of
[
22
,
23
]
, where we provide new ways to exactly sample from the corresponding Pitman-Yor multinomial process, among other properties.

We note further the mixed Poisson framework has firm connections to the species sampling framework as it relates to latent class/clustering models. In particular, Pitman

[

]
, in an unpublished manuscript, shows that the mixed Poisson process has an interpretation, already within the framework of Fisher/McCloskey, in terms of the number of customers/animals sampled/trapped at a given time and the corresponding distribution of the time when this occurs, see
[
13
]
[Section 2.2.1. p. 321] for some more related details. In particular, relevant to our exposition, he points out connections between the work of
[
37
]
on what is equivalently in distribution a single Poisson IBP, that is a mixed Poisson process, to the use of the gamma-trick in
[

]
. In fact the variables, corresponding to the gamma trick, can be directly interpreted as the corresponding arrival times of the mixed Poisson process. This plays an important role leading to one of our key results in Proposition

. Our work, which involves

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{q_{j}^{x_{j,l}}\mathds{1}_{\{c_{j,k,l}>0\}}p_{j}^{c_{j,k,l}}}{1-(1-p_{j})^{\alpha_{j}}}\frac{\alpha_{j}\Gamma(c_{j,k,l}-\alpha_{j})}{c_{j,k,l}!\Gamma(1-\alpha_{j})}.

As a note, recall from the main text, for
0<\alpha_{j}<1,
let
P_{\alpha_{j},0}\sim\mathscr{PY}(\alpha_{j},0,F_{0})
denote the
(\alpha_{j},0)
Pitman-Yor process,
[
14
,
26
]
, constructed from normalizing a
\alpha_{j}
stable-subordinator. The corresponding EPPF describing the distribution of a random partition of
[n_{j,l}]
is then given by:

p_{\alpha_{j},0}(\mathbf{c}_{j,l})=\frac{\alpha^{x_{j,l}-1}_{j}\Gamma(x_{j,l})}{\Gamma(n_{j,l})}\prod_{k=1}^{x_{j,l}}\frac{\Gamma(c_{j,k,l}-\alpha_{j})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha_{j})}.

From this, the probability of the number of blocks
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha_{j},0}^{(n_{j,l})}(k)=\frac{{\alpha^{k-1}_{j}\Gamma(k)}S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},k)}{{\Gamma(n_{j,l})}}
, where
S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},k)
denotes the generalized Stirling number of the second kind, as in
[
29
]
[3.2.3, p. 65-66].

In practice, as we only observe the aggregated counts
N_{j,l}=\sum_{k=1}^{X_{j,l}}\widetilde{C}_{j,k,l}
, we have to work with the marginal distribution involving
(N_{j,l},j\in[J],l\in[r])
.
From Proposition 5.4 and
[
27
]
,

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N}_{j,l}=n_{j,l}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}X_{j,l}=x_{j,l})=\mathds{1}_{\{n_{j,l}\geq x_{j,l}\}}\sum_{*}\prod_{k=1}^{x_{j,l}}\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{C}_{j,k,l}=c_{j,k,l})$\mbox{}

(C.7)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\{n_{j,l}\geq x_{j,l}\}}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{x_{j,l}}p_{j}^{n_{j,l}}}{(1-(1-p_{j})^{\alpha_{j}})^{x_{j,l}}}\sum_{*}\prod_{k=1}^{x_{j,l}}\frac{\Gamma(c_{j,k,l}-\alpha_{j})}{c_{j,k,l}!\Gamma(1-\alpha_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{n_{j,l}\geq x_{j,l}\}}\alpha_{j}^{x_{j,l}}p_{j}^{n_{j,l}}}{(1-(1-p_{j})^{\alpha_{j}})^{x_{j,l}}}\frac{x_{j,l}!S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},x_{j,l})}{n_{j,l}!},

where the summation
\sum_{*}
is over all set of positive integers
(c_{j,1,l},\dots,c_{j,x_{j,l},l})
partitioning
n_{j,l}
. The generalized Stirling number of the second kind
S_{\alpha}(n,x)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(n,x)=\frac{n!}{x!}\sum_{*}\prod_{k=1}^{x}\frac{\Gamma(c_{k}-\alpha)}{c_{k}!\Gamma(1-\alpha)},

(C.8)

and can easily be computed via the following recursive formula,

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(n,1)=\frac{\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)},\quad S_{\alpha}(n,n)=1,

(C.9)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(n+1,x)=(n-\alpha x)S_{\alpha}(n,x)+S_{\alpha}(n,x-1).

Based on this identity, the marginal distribution we will be working with is computed as,

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\varphi=r,(X_{j,l}=x_{j,l},N_{j,l}=n_{j,l},j\in[J],l\in[r]))$\hss}

(C.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Psi_{0}(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})^{r}e^{-\Psi_{0}(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})}}{r!}\prod_{l=1}^{r}\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{x_{l}>0\}}p_{0}^{x_{l}}}{1-(1-p_{0})^{\alpha_{0}}}\frac{\alpha_{0}\Gamma(x_{l}-\alpha_{0})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha_{0})}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{q_{j}^{x_{j,l}}\mathds{1}_{\{n_{j,l}\geq x_{j,l}\}}\alpha_{j}^{x_{j,l}}p_{j}^{n_{j,l}}}{(1-(1-p_{j})^{\alpha_{j}})^{x_{j,l}}}\frac{S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},x_{j,l})}{n_{j,l}!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{0}^{r}e^{-\Psi_{0}(\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})}}{r!(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})^{x_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}-\alpha_{0}r}}\prod_{l=1}^{r}\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{x_{l}>0\}}\Gamma(x_{l}-\alpha_{0})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha_{0})}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{n_{j,l}\geq x_{j,l}\}}\theta_{j}^{x_{j,l}}M_{j}^{n_{j,l}}}{(\zeta_{j}+M_{j})^{n_{j,l}-\alpha_{j}x_{j,l}}}\frac{S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},x_{j,l})}{n_{j,l}!},

where
x_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}:=\sum_{l=1}^{r}x_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{J}x_{j,l}
.

C.II
Detailed description of the sampler

Let
\mathbf{n}_{J}:=(n_{j,l},j\in[J],l\in[r])
be the set of observed word counts. We aim to estimate the parameters
\phi:=\{\alpha_{0},\theta_{0},\{\alpha_{j},\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}\}
, with the parameters
\zeta_{0}
and
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
fixed to
1
. We introduce vague priors on these parameters,

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{logit}\,\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{0}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}0,1)\propto\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}(1-\alpha_{0})}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\Big(\log\frac{\alpha_{0}}{1-\alpha_{0}}\Big)^{2}}{2}\bigg),

(C.11)

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Gamma}(\theta_{0}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}1,1),

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{logit}\,\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{j}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}0,1)\text{ for }j\in[J],

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\theta_{j})

\displaystyle:=\log\,\mathcal{N}(\theta_{j}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}0,1)\propto\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\exp\Big(-(\log\theta_{j})^{2}\Big)\text{ for }j\in[J],

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\phi)

\displaystyle:=\pi_{0}(\alpha_{0})\pi_{0}(\theta_{0})\prod_{j=1}^{J}\pi_{0}(\alpha_{j})\pi_{0}(\theta_{j}).

The target posterior distribution to simulate is then defined as,

\displaystyle\pi(\phi,\mathbf{X}_{J}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\mathbf{n}_{J})\propto\mathbb{P}(\varphi=r,(X_{j,l},\widetilde{N}_{j,l}=n_{j,l},j\in[J],l\in[r]))\pi_{0}(\phi),

(C.12)

where
\mathbf{X}_{J}:=(X_{j,l},j\in[J],l\in[r])
. We proceed by doing Gibbs sampling the latent variables and the parameters
\phi
.

Sampling
\mathbf{X}_{J}
.

The conditional distribution for each entry
X_{j,l}
in
\mathbf{X}_{J}
is simply given as a discrete distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{j,l}=x{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\text{others})\propto\frac{\Gamma(x+x_{l}^{-j}-\alpha_{0})\theta_{j}^{x}(\zeta_{j}+M_{j})^{\alpha_{j}x}S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l},x)}{(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})^{x}},\quad x\in[n_{j,l}],

(C.13)

where
x_{l}^{-j}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}x_{j^{\prime},l}
.

Sampling
\theta_{0}
.

Given the gamma prior, the posterior of
\theta_{0}
is also given as a Gamma distribution.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0}\in d\theta{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\mathrm{others})=\mathrm{Gamma}\bigg(\theta\,\Big|\,r+a,b+\frac{(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}})^{\alpha_{0}}-\zeta_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}}{\alpha_{0}}\bigg).

(C.14)

Sampling the other parameters.

For the parameters other than
\theta_{0}
, unfortunately, the conditional distributions do not admit closed forms. Hence we sample them via random-walk metropolis-Hastings with the following proposal distributions.

\displaystyle q(\alpha_{0}^{\prime}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{logit}\,\mathcal{N}\bigg(\alpha_{0}^{\prime}\,\Big|\,\log\frac{\alpha_{0}}{1-\alpha_{0}},(0.01)^{2}\bigg)\text{ for }j\in\{0\}\cup[J],

(C.15)

\displaystyle q(\theta_{j}^{\prime}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\theta_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{log}\,\mathcal{N}(\theta_{j}^{\prime}{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\log\theta_{j},(0.01)^{2})\text{ for }j\in[J].

C.IV
Computing the predictive probability of a test document

Now assume that we have observed a test document. In order to compute (
C.23
), we need three types of counts
(\widetilde{N}_{j,v}^{(1)},\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)},\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)})
and latent variables
(X_{j,v}^{(1)},X_{j,l}^{(2)},X_{j,l}^{(3)})
, but from the document we can only get the counts

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}^{*}:=(n_{j,v}^{(1)},v\in[r_{j}^{*}]),(n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},l\in[r]),

(C.24)

where
n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2)}+n_{j,l}^{(3)}
. So in order to compute the predictive probability of
\mathbf{n}^{*}
given
\mathbf{Z}_{J}
, we should first recover the latent variables and counts via the Gibbs sampling.

Sampling
X_{j,v}^{(1)}
.

The conditional distribution of
X_{j,v}^{(1)}
given others is a discrete distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{j,v}^{(1)}=x{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\text{others})\propto\frac{\Gamma(x-\alpha_{0})\theta_{j}^{x}(\zeta_{j}+M_{j}+1)^{\alpha_{j}x}S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,v}^{(1)},x)}{(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}+\Delta_{j})^{x}}\text{ for }x\in[n_{j,v}^{(1)}].

(C.25)

Sampling
X_{j,l}^{(2)}
,
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}
and
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}
.

Note that
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}+\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}
. We have three possible cases:

1.

x_{j,l}=0
and
n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}=0
: in this case, the test document do not contain the
l
th word, so
X_{j,l}^{(2)}=\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=0
with probability 1.

2.

x_{j,l}=0
and
n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}>0
: the
l
th word in the test document solely comes from the
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}
, so
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}
and
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=0
with probability 1. Given
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}
, the conditional distribution of
X_{j,l}^{(2)}
is given as a discrete distribution.

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{j,l}^{(2)}=x{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},\text{others})$\mbox{}

(C.26)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(x+x_{l}-\alpha_{0})\theta_{j}^{x}(\zeta_{j}+M_{j}+1)^{\alpha_{j}x}S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},x)}{(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}+\Delta_{j})^{x}}\text{ for }x\in[n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}].

3.

x_{j,l}>0
and
n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}>0
: the
l
th word in the test document either
comes from both
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}
and
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}
or only from one of
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}
and
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}
. The conditional distribution of
X_{j,l}^{(2)}
is a discrete distribution,

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{j,l}^{(2)}=x{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}+\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},\text{others})$\mbox{}

(C.27)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(x+x_{l}-\alpha_{0})\theta_{j}^{x}(\zeta_{j}+M_{j}+1)^{\alpha_{j}x}(S_{\alpha_{j}}(n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},x))^{\mathds{1}_{\{x>0\}}}}{(\zeta_{0}+\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}+\Delta_{j})^{x}}\text{ for }x\in\{0\}\cup[n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}].

If
x_{j,l}^{(2)}
drawn from the above conditional is zero, we have

\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=0
and
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}
with probability 1. Otherwise,
the conditional distribution for
\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}
is given as,

\displaystyle\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}=n{\hskip 0.80002pt|\hskip 0.80002pt}\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(2)}+\widetilde{N}_{j,l}^{(3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)},X_{j,l}^{(2)}=x_{j,l}^{(2)},\text{others})$\mbox{}

(C.28)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{S_{\alpha_{j}}(n,x_{j,l}^{(2)})}{n!}\frac{\Gamma(n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}-n+n_{j,l}-\alpha_{j}x_{j,l})}{(n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}-n)!}\text{ for }n\in[n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}].

Given
n_{j,l}^{(2)}
drawn from the above conditional, we have
n_{j,l}^{(3)}=n_{j,l}^{(2,3)}-n_{j,l}^{(2)}
with probability 1.
