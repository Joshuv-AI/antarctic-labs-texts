Abstract
. In this paper we investigate the problem of finding the minimum of edge density in families of convex mosaics with unit volume cells in 3 3 -dimensional Euclidean space. We solve this problem for two families. In particular, we prove that a translative convex mosaic with unit volume cells has minimal edge density if it is a face-to-face mosaic generated by unit cubes. We also investigate decomposable mosaics, defined as mosaics whose cells are Minkowski sums of cells of two lower dimensional convex mosaics, and show that the edge density of every normal decomposable mosaic with unit volume cells is at least as large as that of a face-to-face mosaic with regular triangle-based right prisms as cells.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to investigate certain properties of mosaics. A
mosaic
or
tiling
of the Euclidean
n
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a countable family
\mathcal{M}
of compact sets with nonempty interiors, called cells, with the property that
\bigcup\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the interiors of any two distinct cells are disjoint. A mosaic is
convex
, if every cell in it is convex; it is known that in this case every cell is a convex polytope
[
32
, Theorem 1]
. If, for a mosaic
\mathcal{M}
, there are universal constants
0<r<R
such that every cell of
\mathcal{M}
contains a ball of radius
r
and is contained in a ball of radius
R
, the mosaic is called
normal
. In this paper we deal only with normal, convex mosaics. A convex mosaic is called
face-to-face
(in the plane also
edge-to-edge
) if every facet of every cell is a facet of exactly one more cell. Furthermore, a convex mosaic is
translative
(resp.
congruent
), if every cell is a translate (resp. a congruent copy) of a given convex polytope. Clearly, every congruent and translative convex mosaic is normal. A cell of a translative convex mosaic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called an
n
-dimensional parallelohedron
.

One of the classic problems of geometry, regarding planar mosaics, is the Honeycomb Conjecture, stating that in a decomposition of the Euclidean plane into cells of equal area, the regular hexagonal grid has minimal edge density. This conjecture first appeared in Roman time in a book of Varro about agriculture
[
33
]
. The problem has been the centre of research throughout the second half of the 20th century
[
10
,
27
]
; it was solved by Fejes Tóth for normal, convex mosaics, while the most general version is due to Hales
[
18
]
, who dropped the condition of convexity.

A famous analogous problem for mosaics in the
3
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was proposed by Lord Kelvin
[
20
]
in 1887; he conjectured that in a tiling of space with cells of unit volume, minimal surface density is attained by a mosaic composed of slightly modified truncated octahedra. Even though this conjecture was disproved by Weaire and Phelan in 1994
[
35
]
, who discovered a tiling of space with two slighly curved ‘polyhedra’ of equal volume and with less surface density than in Lord Kelvin’s mosaic, the original problem of finding the mosaics with equal volume cells and minimal surface density has been extensively studied (see, e.g.
[
21
,
13
,
28
]
).

The problem of determining the minimal surface density of tilings with unit volume cells is also meaningful if we consider only tilings satisfying some additional conditions. The simplest such example is to investigate the family of translative, convex tilings. Regarding this family, an unsolved conjecture of Bezdek
[
4
]
from 2006, restated in
[
22
]
in 2022, states that among translative, convex mosaics with unit volume cells, the ones generated by truncated regular octahedra have minimal surface density. We add that in the authors’ knowledge, there is no subfamily of mosaics for which Kelvin’s problem is solved.

The aim of this paper is to present a more general framework for this problem for convex mosaics, and solve it under some additional conditions.

The combinatorial classes of

Figure 1

The rest of the polyhedra in the picture are obtained by removing some generating segments from the type (5) polyhedron.

Every

By well-known properties of zonotopes (see e.g.

3.
The proof of Theorem

The proof of Theorem

To state this lemma, we denote the family of unit volume

-dimensional parallelohedra by

Lemma 4

Let

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{nF_{i}}{\surf(P)}u_{i}\otimes u_{i}=\Id

To use Lemma

. It is known that the volume of the projection body of any convex body is invariant under volume preserving linear transformations (cf.
[
30

. This follows from the observation that every cell intersecting
C
is contained in a Euclidean disk of radius
3\bar{R}
, and has area that of a Euclidean disk of radius
\bar{r}
.

Remark 2
.

A consequence of Remark
1
is that there is a universal bound
\bar{v}
such that every cell in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
has at most
\bar{v}
(and at least
3
) edges.

Let
k(R)
and
N(R)
denote the numbers of cells and nodes of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C(R)
, respectively. Let the cells of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C_{pr}(R)
be
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{k}
, and
let
v_{i}
be the number of the edges of
F_{i}
. Let
S(R)
denote the sum of all interior angles of all cells of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C(R)\cap H
.

Observe that every cell of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
intersecting
C(R)\cap H
and not contained in it lies in a neighborhood of radius
\bar{R}
, in
H
, of the circle with equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=R^{2}
,
z=0
. Thus, their total area is at most
4R\bar{R}\pi
. Since the area of every cell is between
\bar{r}^{2}\pi
and
\bar{R^{2}}\pi
, the number of these cells is of order
\mathcal{O}(R)
. Using a similar argument and Remark
2
, we obtain the same order of magnitude for the sum of the numbers of edges or vertices of these cells, or their total perimeter. Furthermore, in the same way we can show that
k(R),N(R)
and
S(R)
are all of magnitude
\Theta(R^{2})
. In the next part we prove better estimates for these quantities.

Remark 3
.

Let
v(R)=\frac{1}{k(R)}\sum_{i=1}^{k(R)}v_{i}
. Then
v(R)\leq 6+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{R}\right)
. Indeed, consider the graph
G
the vertices of which are the nodes of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C(R)
, and the edges of which are the edges of the cells of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C(R)\cap H
. In
G
, there might be vertices of degree less than
3
; if
p
is such a vertex, then the degree of
p
is two, and there is at least one edge of a cell of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
intersecting the boundary of
C(R)\cap H
that ends at
p
. By the previous paragraph, the number of such vertices is
\mathcal{O}(R)
, and the numbers of vertices, edges and faces of
G
is
\Theta(R^{2})
. Thus, the observation follows from Euler’s formula for planar graphs.

By the previous paragraph, a ‘typical’ node of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in
C(R)
is surrounded by cells contained in
C(R)
; if this is not true for a node, then the ‘discrepancy’ is due to some cells intersecting the boundary of
C(R)
. Thus,

S(R)=2\pi N(R)+\mathcal{O}(R).

On the other hand, the same quantity can be written as

S(R)=\sum_{i=1}^{k(R)}(v_{i}-2)\pi.

Since
\area(F_{i})=\frac{1}{\ell}
for all
i
,
k(R)=R^{2}\pi\ell+\mathcal{O}(R)
. Combining all these formulas and applying also Remark
3
, we have that

N(R)=\frac{k(R)(v(R)-2)}{2}+\mathcal{O}(R)=\frac{R^{2}\ell\pi}{2}(v(R)-2)+\mathcal{O}(R).

We obtain similarly that the total edge length of
\mathcal{M}
in
C(R)
is

\vol_{1}(\skel_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{1})\cap C(R))=\ell N(R)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\perim(F_{i})+\mathcal{O}(R),

where
\perim(\cdot)
denotes perimeter.

Set
\rho_{1}(\mathcal{M}(R))=\frac{\vol_{1}(\skel_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{1})\cap C(R))}{\vol_{3}(C(R))}
, and
P(R)=\frac{1}{k(R)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\perim(F_{i})
. Note that since
\perim(F_{i})\leq 2\bar{R}\pi
, there is an upper bound on
P(R)
independent of
R
.
Since
\vol_{3}(C(R))=R^{2}\pi\ell
, we have that

\rho_{1}(\mathcal{M}(R))=\frac{1}{2}\ell(v(R)-2)+\frac{1}{2}P(R)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{R}\right).

By the Discrete Isoperimetric Inequality,
\perim(F_{i})\geq 2\sqrt{v_{i}\tan\frac{\pi}{v_{i}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}
; here the right-hand side is the perimeter of the regular
v_{i}
-gon of area
\frac{1}{\ell}
. Thus, by the convexity of the function
x\mapsto\sqrt{x\tan\frac{\pi}{x}}
on the interval
[3,\infty)
, we have

P(R)\geq 2\sqrt{v(R)\tan\frac{\pi}{v(R)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}
. This yields that

(8)

\underline{\rho}_{1}(\mathcal{M})=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\rho_{1}(\mathcal{M}(R))\geq\liminf_{R\to\infty}\left(\frac{\ell}{2}(v(R)-2)+\sqrt{v(R)\tan\frac{\pi}{v(R)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}\right).

Note that by Remark
3
, for any sequence
\{R_{m}\}
, where
R_{m}\to\infty,3\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}v(R_{m})

\leq 6
if the limit exists. Thus, to give a lower bound for
\underline{\rho}_{1}(\mathcal{M})
, it is sufficient to find the minimum of the function

F(x,\ell)=\frac{\ell}{2}(x-2)+\sqrt{x\tan\frac{\pi}{x}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}

under the conditions that
x\in[3,6]
and
\ell\in(0,\infty)
.
From the AM-GM Inequality it follows that for any fixed value
x\in[3,6]
,
F(x,\ell)
is minimal if
\ell=\frac{\sqrt[3]{x\tan\frac{\pi}{x}}}{(x-2)^{2/3}}
,
and its minimum is

F(x,\ell)\geq F\left(x,\frac{\sqrt[3]{x\tan\frac{\pi}{x}}}{(x-2)^{2/3}}\right)=\frac{3}{2}\left(x(x-2)\tan\frac{\pi}{x}\right)^{1/3}

for any
x\in[3,6]
. The expression on the right-hand side of this inequality is strictly increasing on the interval
[3,6]
, and, after substituting
x=3
, we have that

\underline{\rho}_{1}(\mathcal{M})\geq\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}.

This implies the inequality part of Theorem
2
. The equality part can be checked by a simple computation.

5.
Additional remarks and problems

Recall that the
mean width

\mw(K)
of a convex body
K
is the average length of the projection of
K
onto a line passing through the origin
o

[
16
]
. If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a zonotope, i.e. it is defined as
K=\sum_{i=1}^{m}[o,p_{i}]
for some
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then a well-known formula
[
19
,
5
,
22
]
yields that
\mw(K)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|p_{i}|
. Thus, one can readily obtain the minimum of
\mw(P)
over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{tr}
by applying Lemma
3
with the choice
m=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
. This result appeared as the main result, Theorem 1, of
[
22
]
, and thus, Lemma
3
is a generalization of
[
22
, Theorem 1]
.

Our results are also related to a conjecture of Melzak
[
24
]
made in 1965, claiming that among convex polyhedra of unit volume, regular triangle based prisms, with equal-length edges, have minimal total edge length. This conjecture is still very far from settled; for the solution of this problem in smaller families of convex polyhedra and for bounds on this quantity, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
11
,
25
]
.

Corollary
1
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3
, and Corollary
2
can be proved by a much simplified variant of the proof of Theorem
2
. Here, by
L(P)
we denote the total edge length of the convex polyhedron
P
.

Corollary 1
.

For any
3
-dimensional parallelohedron
P
of unit volume, we have

L(P)\geq 12,

with equality if and only if
P
is a unit cube.

Corollary 2
.

For any prism
P
of unit volume, we have

L(P)\geq 2^{2/3}3^{11/6}\approx 11.8962,

with equality if and only if
P
is a regular triangle based right prism, and every edge of
P
is of length
\frac{2^{2/3}}{3^{1/6}}
.

We note that the methods of proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
rely very much on the specific properties of the mosaics under investigation. Due to this, it seems a difficult task to generalize any of the two proofs for larger families of mosaics. Nevertheless, using the tools described in
[
8
]
, it might be possible that some part of the tools in the proof of Theorem
1
can be modified for any zonotopal parallelohedron in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
n\geq 3
. Thus, we ask the following question, where
\mathcal{P}^{n}_{tr}
denotes the family of translative convex mosaics in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with unit volume cells.

Question 1
.

Let
n\geq 4
be fixed. Find the infimum of the edge densities of the elements of
\mathcal{P}^{n}_{tr}
.

We note that unlike in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, even in the first open case
n=4
, there are parallelohedra in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
that are not zonotopes
[
7
]
.

Another possible future direction of research is to consider other families of mosaics in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, like those generated by a fundamental region of one of the crystallographic groups. We note that, like in case of Kelvin’s problem, the answer to our problem may depend on the considered family. For the general case, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

For any normal, convex mosaic
\mathcal{M}
with unit volume cells, we have

\underline{\rho}_{1}(\mathcal{M})\geq\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}.
