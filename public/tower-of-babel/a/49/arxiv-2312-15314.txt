Exact ground state of interacting electrons in magic angle graphene
Simon Becker, Lin Lin, Kevin D. Stubbs

Abstract
. One of the most remarkable theoretical findings in magic angle twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) is the emergence of ferromagnetic Slater determinants as exact ground states for the interacting Hamiltonian at the chiral limit. This discovery provides an explanation for the correlated insulating phase which has been experimentally observed at half filling. This work is the first mathematical study of interacting models in magic angle graphene systems. These include not only TBG but also TBG-like systems featuring four flat bands per valley, and twisted trilayer graphene (TTG) systems with equal twist angles. We identify symmetries of the Bistritzer-MacDonald Hamiltonian that are responsible for characterizing the Hartree-Fock ground states as zero energy many-body ground states. Furthermore, for a general class of Hamiltonian, we establish criteria that the ferromagnetic Slater determinants are the unique ground states within the class of uniformly half-filled, translation invariant Slater determinants. We then demonstrate that these criteria can be explicitly verified for TBG and TBG-like systems at the chiral limit, using properties of Jacobi- θ \theta and Weierstrass- ℘ \wp functions.



1.
Introduction

Over the past few years, moiré structures, notably “magic angle” twisted bilayer graphene (TBG), have attracted significant attention in the condensed matter physics community.
This surge of interest began after numerous experiments
[
14
,
31
,
13
,
22
,
41
]
which demonstrate that TBG has nearly flat energy bands and can host intricate phases, such as the correlated insulator (CI) phases at integer fillings and superconducting (SC) phases at non-integer fillings.
Before these exciting experimental results however, the Bistritzer-MacDonald (BM) model
[
8
]
successfully predicted these nearly flat energy bands near the magic angle of roughly
1.1^{\circ}
.
Yet, the BM model does not directly include electron-electron repulsion (i.e., it is a non-interacting model) and in fact, due to symmetry restrictions, the BM model predicts that twisted bilayer graphene is always in a simple metallic phase.
As a result, the CI and SC phases must arise from electron-electron interactions beyond the non-interacting BM model.

At the chiral limit, we can define a flat-band interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian for TBG that only consists of terms describing electron-electron interactions
[
10
,
15
,
32
,
39
,
38
,
16
,
7
,
20
,
30
,
18
,
29
,
21
,
17
]
.
One of the most remarkable theoretical results on this model is that, at half filling, the FBI Hamiltonian is
frustration-free
, i.e., the Hamiltonian can be written as a sum of terms (in this case, these terms do not commute), and there exists a ground state that minimizes the energy of each term.
For the FBI Hamiltonian, one possible ground state is a single Slater determinant, which is the simplest type of fermionic many-body wavefunctions.
Moreover, it can be shown that for this ground state, in the thermodynamic limit, a strictly positive amount of energy is needed to either add or remove an electron.
This offers
one
possible explanation for the CI phase in TBG at half-filling.

To our knowledge, mathematical studies so far have focussed on understanding the BM model of twisted bilayer and multilayer graphene at the chiral limit
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

, and systematic derivations of the BM Hamiltonian from more complex models such as the atomistic tight binding models
[
36
,

,

.
In this paper, we provide the
first
mathematical study of interacting systems, and specifically the FBI Hamiltonian and its ground states. We note that our analysis applies to the FBI Hamiltonian defined on a single valley of the moiré Brillouin zone.

Our analysis is based on the symmetries of the BM Hamiltonian at the chiral limit. These symmetries are preserved as the number of layers and flat bands varies, which allows us to study TBG as well as TBG-like systems, including twisted multilayer graphene structures.
For example, the standard chiral model for TBG has two layers and two flat bands; a system we refer to as TBG-2.
By changing the interlayer potential, it is possible for two layers of graphene to host four flat bands while still preserving the relevant symmetries
[
5
]
; we refer to this system as TBG-4.
We can also consider a twisted trilayer graphene (TTG) system, where the relative twist between the top and middle and the relative twist between the middle and bottom are the same, and can host
four flat bands
[
28
]
. We refer to this system as the equal angle twisted trilayer graphene (eTTG-4).

Although the FBI Hamiltonian is an idealized and simplified representation of
interacting electrons, it still contains many intricate details.
Our paper does not discuss the derivation of the FBI Hamiltonian from the BM model, or the derivation of the BM model from the finer level atomistic models.
We assume readers are familiar with second quantization and standard treatments of non-interacting periodic systems.

1.1.
Notation

In this paper, operators and matrices acting on the Fock space are represented using the hat notation, such as
\hat{f}^{{\dagger}},\hat{f},\hat{H}_{FBI}
. Operators and matrices that operate in the single particle space (such as
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
for TBG) are indicated without the hat notation, as seen in
H,D
. For a single particle operaotr
H
, the Bloch-Floquet transformed Hamiltonian is denoted by
H_{\mathbf{k}}
, where
\mathbf{k}
is the Brillouin zone index. With some slight abuse of the hat notation, vectors defined in real space are denoted without the hat, for example,
u(\mathbf{r})
. Their corresponding Fourier transforms are indicated with the hat notation, as in
\hat{u}(\mathbf{q})
.
For any given matrix
A
, the operations of entrywise complex conjugation, transpose, and Hermitian conjugation are represented by
\overline{A},A^{\top},
and
A^{{\dagger}}
, respectively.

Due to the symmetries present in twisted graphene systems, the number of flat bands is always an even number which we will denote by
2M
. We denote the set of indices of the flat bands by
\mathcal{N}=\{-M,\cdots,-1,1,\cdots,M\}
.
After a proper discretization of a single valley of the moiré Brillouin zone into a discrete set
\mathcal{K}
with
N_{\mathbf{k}}
points (see the definition of
\mathcal{K}
in (
2.12
)), we can consider a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
consisting of
2MN_{\mathbf{k}}
fermionic modes.
This space is spanned by
2^{2MN_{\mathbf{k}}}
basis vectors of the form

(1.1)

\ket{s_{1},\ldots,s_{2MN_{\mathbf{k}}}}=\prod_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}(\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}})^{s}\ket{\mathrm{vac}},\quad s_{j}\in\{0,1\},

where
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}},\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}
are fermionic creation and annihilation operators satisfying the canonical anticommutation relation (CAR), and the vacuum state
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\ket{0,\ldots,0}
satisfies
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=0
for each
n\in\mathcal{N}
and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.

The FBI Hamiltonian takes the form

(1.2)

\hat{H}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}},

where each
\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
takes the form

(1.3)

\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\alpha_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]_{m,n}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{m\mathbf{k}}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}+[\beta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}]_{m,n}.

Here, the sum over
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is taken over a discrete set which will be specified later;
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
represents a momentum difference and so the terms in
\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
connect states at momentum
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
.
The coefficient matrices
\alpha_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
and
\beta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
are matrices of size
2M\times 2M
(defined in
Eq.

2.22
) depend on the eigenstates of the non-interacting BM Hamiltonian at momenta
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
and inherit a number of symmetries from the BM Hamiltonian.

The total number operator
\hat{N}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}
counts the number of electrons in a state, i.e.,
if
\hat{N}\ket{\psi}=\nu N_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\psi}
, then the number of electrons in
\ket{\psi}
is
\nu N_{\mathbf{k}}
. The number operator

\hat{N}
commutes with
\hat{H}
so we can restrict to functions that are simultaneously eigenfunctions of
\hat{H}
and
\hat{N}
.
The integer filling regime refers to the case when
\nu
is an integer (
0\leq\nu\leq 2M
).
One particular integer filling is
\nu=M(=\frac{1}{2}(2M))
, and the corresponding
\ket{\psi}
is also called a
half-filled
state.
If we further have
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}\ket{\psi}=M\ket{\psi}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\ket{\psi}
is a
uniformly half-filled
state.
The one-body reduced density matrix (1-RDM) is defined as
[P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{nm}=\braket{\psi|\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{m\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}|\psi}
. If
[P(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})]_{nm}=[P(\mathbf{k})]_{nm}\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
, then
\ket{\psi}
is a
translation invariant
state.

This paper considers the uniformly half-filled, translation invariant states, which forms a subspace of
\mathcal{F}
denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{u}
. The ground-state energy in
\mathcal{F}_{u}
is the solution to the following optimization problem

(1.4)

E=\min_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{F}_{u}}\frac{\braket{\psi|\hat{H}|\psi}}{\braket{\psi|\psi}},

and its minimizer (which may not be unique) is called a ground state. For any
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{F}
, by construction
\braket{\psi|\hat{H}|\psi}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\braket{\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\psi|\hat{A}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\psi}\geq 0
. Therefore
\hat{H}
is a positive semidefinite (PSD) Hamiltonian, and
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{F}_{u}
satisfying
\hat{H}\ket{\psi}=0
is a ground state.

We further consider a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{u}

(1.5)

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{M}\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{b}_{i\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\Big|\penalty\ \hat{b}^{{\dagger}}_{i\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}}[\Xi(\mathbf{k})]_{ni},\quad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}[\overline{\Xi(\mathbf{k})}]_{ni}[\Xi(\mathbf{k})]_{nj}=\delta_{ij}\right\}.

Each element of
\mathcal{S}
is called a uniformly half-filled, translation invariant Slater determinant. The
Hartree-Fock
theory solves a much simpler optimization problem

(1.6)

E_{\text{HF}}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\frac{\braket{\psi|\hat{H}|\psi}}{\braket{\psi|\psi}}.

By definition we have
E\leq E_{\text{HF}}
and in general
E<E_{\text{HF}}
.

Main results

In this section, we summarize the main findings of this paper,
emphasizing the algebraic structures, while reserving some of the more
technical specifics for later in the paper.

Our first result is that for the FBI Hamiltonian, there exist two states in

(Informal version of

)
.

The single Slater determinants

for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}

, and

are exact ground states of

1.3.
Discussion and open questions

The states
\ket{\Psi_{\pm}}
are sometimes referred to as “ferromagnetic Slater determinants”. This term is inherited from the physics context where the modes created by
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}
with
n=-M,\ldots,-1
and with
n=1,\ldots,M
are vectors of the sublattice symmetry operator
\mathcal{Z}
with eigenvalues
+1
and
-1
, respectively. These modes can be interpreted as “pseudo” up spins and down spins. With this analogy, we can identify
\ket{\Psi_{+}}
with

\ket{\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\cdots}
and
\ket{\Psi_{-}}
with
\ket{\downarrow\downarrow\downarrow\cdots}
, which are ground states of a ferromagnet.
The states
\ket{\Psi_{\pm}}
also have nonvanishing Chern numbers, and are also referred to as (integer) quantum hall states
[
9
]
.

The mechanism for the uniqueness of the ferromagnetic Slater determinant ground states in twisted graphene is rather different from previous uniqueness results in many-body systems (for example, on the Hubbard model on a planar graph
[
25
,
26
,
24
]
).
Unlike these results, the FBI Hamiltonian involves interactions between all momenta and the ground state is not determined by the connectivity of the underlying graph structure.
Instead, the selection of ferromagnetic Slater determinant as the ground states seems to be related to the joint invariant subspaces of the collection of matrices
\{\alpha_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}},\beta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\}
; any state whose one-body reduced density matrix does not lie in an invariant subspace acquires an energy penalty.

These conditions in
Result

2
are physically relevant. This paper focuses on the spinless, valleyless (or in physical terms, spin and valley polarized) FBI Hamiltonian. When valley degrees of freedom are taken into account, these assumptions become invalid. This leads to additional ground states in
\mathcal{S}
, such as the inter-valley coherent (IVC) states
[
10
,
7
]
. The generalization of our result to FBI Hamiltonians taking into account both spins and valleys will be our future work.

On the other hand, the conditions in
Result

2
are difficult to verify. They can be relaxed and explicitly verified for
M=1
or
2
, as shown in
Result

3
. However, for
M>2
, a generally computationally verifiable method for reformulating these conditions remains unknown.

Even when the conditions are satisfied,
Result

2
only establishes uniqueness among uniformly half-filled, translation-invariant Slater determinants. We conjecture that at half filling, the uniqueness result should extend to all single Slater determinants.
This requires techniques beyond the scope of this initial work and will be our future research.

2.
The Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonian for Twisted Graphene

2.1.
Notational Setup for Twisted Graphene

Before defining the flat-band interacting model for twisted graphene, we introduce a general notation for twisted
N
-layer graphene based on the Bistritzer-MacDonald model.
Twisted bilayer and trilayer graphene correspond to choosing
N=2
and
N=3
respectively.

After a proper choice of units, the lattice vectors for the moiré unit cell in the real space are

(2.1)

\mathbf{v}_{1}:=-\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{2\pi}{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\qquad\mathbf{v}_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}},-\frac{2\pi}{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

These vectors generate the real space moiré lattice
\Gamma
:

(2.2)

\Gamma:=\mathbf{v}_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}+\mathbf{v}_{2}{\mathbb{Z}}=\{a_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+a_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}:a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

The moiré unit cell in the real space is denoted by
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Gamma
and can be identified with

(2.3)

\Omega:=\left\{t_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+t_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2};t_{1},t_{2}\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}.

The dual lattice (or the reciprocal lattice) of
\Gamma
is denoted by
\Gamma^{*}
, and is generated by

(2.4)

\mathbf{g}_{1}:=-\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\qquad\mathbf{g}_{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\frac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Note that
\mathbf{v}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
.
The unit cell in the reciprocal space (or the Brillouin zone) is denoted by
\Omega^{*}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\Gamma^{*}
and can be identified with

(2.5)

\Omega^{*}=\left\{t_{1}\mathbf{g}_{1}+t_{2}\mathbf{g}_{2}:t_{1},t_{2}\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}.

Throughout the paper, a vector in the reciprocal lattice
\Gamma^{*}
is often denoted by
\mathbf{G}
, while a vector in the Brillouin zone
\Omega^{*}
is often denoted by
\mathbf{k}
or
\mathbf{q}
. Note that a generic vector
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
can always be uniquely decomposed as
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}
for some
\mathbf{q}\in\Omega^{*}
and
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
.

In addition to the generating vectors
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
, we also identify three special momentum vectors
\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3}
which are related to each other by a
\frac{2\pi}{3}
-counterclockwise rotation
R_{3}
:

R_{3}:=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}-1&-\sqrt{3}\\
\sqrt{3}&-1\end{bmatrix}.

In particular,

(2.6)

\mathbf{q}_{1}:=[0,1]^{\top}\quad\mathbf{q}_{2}:=R_{3}\mathbf{q}_{1}=[-\sqrt{3}/2,-1/2]^{\top}\quad\mathbf{q}_{3}:=R_{3}\mathbf{q}_{2}=[\sqrt{3}/2,-1/2]^{\top}

Notice that
\mathbf{g}_{1}=\mathbf{q}_{2}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}=\mathbf{q}_{3}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
.

Each moiré unit cell consists of many atoms located on one of the two sublattices, denoted by
\{A=1,B=-1\}
. In the BM model for twisted bilayer and multilayer graphene, all atomistic details in each layer are smeared out except the sublattice information.
The BM Hamiltonian
H
(called a single particle Hamiltonian) acts on a dense subset of the function space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, where
N
is the number of layers.
A function in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
can be written as
\psi(\mathbf{r};\sigma,\ell)
, where
\mathbf{r}=[x_{1},x_{2}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is the real space coordinate,
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}
is the sublattice index, and
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
is the layer index.
The BM Hamiltonian satisfies certain translation symmetry with respect to the moiré lattice
\Gamma
. Therefore an eigenfunction of
H
can be labeled as
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,\ell)
.
Here
n
is called the band index,
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2})\in\Omega^{*}
is the Bloch vector.
For example, a wavefunction for the Hamiltonian of TBG can be written as

(2.7)

\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};A,1)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};A,2)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};B,1)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};B,2)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\\

and for TTG:

(2.8)

\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};A,1)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};A,2)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};A,3)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};B,1)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};B,2)&\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};B,3)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

2.2.
The Flat-Band Interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian

While we adopt first quantization in expressing the BM Hamiltonian (see

The FBI Hamiltonian is defined in terms of the flat-band eigenfunctions of the single particle Hamiltonian

We make the following mild assumptions on

Assumption 1
.

We assume that

\begin{split}H\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})&=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}),\\

\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{-1\mathbf{k}}\leq&\penalty\ 0\leq\varepsilon_{1\mathbf{k}}\leq\cdots\qquad\forall\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}.\end{split}

We furthermore define the Bloch-Floquet transformed Hamiltonian

for all
\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}
and assume that

(1)

The resolvent operator

(2)

If we identify

(3)

There exists a non-empty set of flat bands

\mathcal{N}:=\{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\forall\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*},\,\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}=0\}.

In some systems, such as twisted trilayer graphene (TTG), there can be energy bands which intersect with the flat bands but are not flat themselves.
This can happen in all
N
-layer systems with
N\geq 3
(see Figure
1
for an example).
Let us define such the set of momenta where such crossings occur as follows:

(2.11)

\mathcal{K}_{\rm crossing}=\{\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}:\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}=0,\text{ for some }n\not\in\mathcal{N}\}.

Due to the analyticity assumption on
H_{\mathbf{k}}
, standard results on analytic families of operators
[
19
, VII.5, Theo 1.10]
implies that the set of such crossings must have an empty interior.
Furthermore, Rellich’s theorem implies that the spectral projectors of
H_{\mathbf{k}}
may be chosen analytically throughout the entire Brillioun zone
[
19
, VII.5, Theo 3.9]
.
Therefore, we will assume without loss of generality that the flat-band eigenfunctions
\{\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}:n\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}\}
have been chosen so that the spectral projection onto these eigenfunctions is analytic in
\mathbf{k}
.

In order to write down a many-body wavefunction with a finite number of particles, we discretize the Brillouin zone using a uniform grid
\mathcal{K}
of size
(n_{k_{x}},n_{k_{y}})

(2.12)

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{\frac{i}{n_{k_{x}}}\mathbf{g}_{1}+\frac{j}{n_{k_{y}}}\mathbf{g}_{2}:i\in\{0,1,\cdots,n_{k_{x}}-1\},j\in\{0,1,\cdots,n_{k_{y}}-1\}\right\}\subseteq\Omega^{*}.

Here
\mathbf{g}_{1}
,
\mathbf{g}_{2}
are a pair of generating vectors for the moiré dual lattice
\Gamma^{*}
, see (
2.4
), and we define
N_{\mathbf{k}}:=\#|\mathcal{K}|=n_{k_{x}}n_{k_{y}}
.

The set
\mathcal{K}
is known as a Monkhorst-Pack grid, and the
thermodynamic limit
(TDL) can be reached by taking
n_{k_{x}},n_{k_{y}}\to\infty
.
We also make the following technical assumption on the grid
\mathcal{K}
in relation to the Hamiltonian
H
:

Assumption 2
.

For each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, let
\Pi(\mathbf{k})
be the orthogonal projector onto the flat-band eigenfunctions at
\mathbf{k}
.
We assume that the grid
\mathcal{K}
has been chosen so that for all pairs of momenta
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
there exists a sequence of momenta
\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
so that
\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{k}_{L}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
and
\|\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i+1})-\Pi(\mathbf{k}_{i})\|<1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
.

Remark 1
.

Since
H_{\mathbf{k}}
is assumed to be analytic (
1
), the resolvent mapping
\mathbf{k}\mapsto(H_{\mathbf{k}}-i)^{-1}
is Lipschitz in
\mathbf{k}
.
Therefore, for
2
to be true, it is sufficient for
n_{k_{x}}\gtrsim L
and
n_{k_{y}}\gtrsim L
where
L
is the Lipschitz constant for
(H_{\mathbf{k}}-i)^{-1}
.

Having fixed the grid
\mathcal{K}
and the flat-band eigenfunctions, we may now define the FBI Hamiltonian.
For each flat-band
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
, we first define the band creation and annihilation operators,
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}
, which create or annihilate a particle in state
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
respectively, which satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relation (CAR):

(2.13)

\{\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\quad\{\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=\{\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}},\hat{f}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\}=0.

The definition of the creation and annihilation operators can be periodically extended outside the Brillouin zone as

(2.14)

\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})}=\hat{f}^{{\dagger}}_{n\mathbf{k}},\quad\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})}=\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}},\quad\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}.

Next, we define the flat-band periodic Bloch functions

(2.15)

u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})

which are normalized in the unit cell, i.e.,
\int_{\Omega}|u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=1
.
Let
\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})
denote the Fourier coefficients of
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)

(2.16)

\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j):=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)d\mathbf{r}.

and define the
form factor

(2.17)

[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{mn}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j).

Given
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\Omega^{*},\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
,
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
is a
\#|\mathcal{N}|\times\#|\mathcal{N}|
momentum dependent matrix. Note that
\{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathbf{q}\in\Omega^{*},\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}\}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
; this notation facilitates later discussions.

The following identities which will be useful at various points in our proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

The form factor matrix
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
satisfies the following identities for all
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
and all
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}).

Proof.

This follows from the following simple calculation

(2.20)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\overline{\hat{u}_{m(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\\
&=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}).\end{split}

As for the second identity, one easily checks that
\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{G})=\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})
and so

(2.21)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime};\sigma,j)\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\\
&=\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}).\end{split}

∎

We can now define the flat-band interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian for the twisted
N
-layer graphene
[

,
7
]
.
When we compare the definition of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
with that in
Eqs.

1.2
and
1.3
, we need to show that
\hat{\rho}^{{\dagger}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\hat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
, and that

is a positive semi-definite Hamiltonian. These relations will be verified in
Section

.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}
denote the flat-band creation and annihilation operators, and let
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
be defined as in
Eq.

.
The flat-band interacting (FBI) Hamiltonian is:

(2.22)

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{FBI}&:=\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}+\Gamma^{*}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\\
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})&:=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{mn}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{G}}\right).\end{split}

Here,
\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\hat{V}(|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}|)
is the Fourier transform of a radially symmetric electron-electron potential which satisfies

for all
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

For instance, for TBG, the double gate-screened Coulomb interaction in Fourier space reads (Note that

is well defined)

(2.23)

\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\frac{\tanh(\left\lvert\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right\rvert d/2)}{\left\lvert\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right\rvert}.

Here

3.
A Review of Twisted Bilayer Graphene and equal Twist Angle Trilayer Graphene

To make the assumptions of

[

,
2
,

,
3
,

,
11
]
in the Bistritzer-MacDonald Hamiltonian at the chiral limit.
The single particle Hamiltonian for both chiral TBG and chiral eTTG depend on a parameter
\alpha
, that is inversely proportional to the twisting angle, and take the form of a matrix-valued differential operator

(3.1)

H(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}0&D(\alpha)^{\dagger}\\
D(\alpha)&0\end{bmatrix},

For TBG,

acts on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

(3.2)

D_{\rm TBG}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}&\alpha U_{+}(\mathbf{r})\\
\alpha U_{-}(\mathbf{r})&D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}\end{bmatrix},

and for eTTG,
D(\alpha)
acts on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{3})

(3.3)

D_{\rm eTTG}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}&\alpha U_{+}(\mathbf{r})&0\\
\alpha U_{-}(\mathbf{r})&D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}&\alpha U_{+}(\mathbf{r})\\
0&\alpha U_{-}(\mathbf{r})&D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, single particle Hamiltonians for
N
-layer systems can be defined in terms of operators
D(\alpha)
acting on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
(see, for example,
[
40

).

We remark that the specific form of the single particle Hamiltonian will not be used in our proofs of
Results

1
,
3
and
2
, however we will make use of two important properties which hold for Bistritzer-MacDonald-type Hamiltonians at the chiral limit: (1) the existence of a magic angle with exactly flat bands, and (2) the single particle Hamiltonian commutes with a number of symmetry operations (see
Section

4
).

For small twist angles, due to the lattice structure of graphene, the tunneling potentials
U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})
satisfy the following symmetry properties independent of the number of layers
[

,
34
]
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\overline{\omega}^{\pm(a_{1}+a_{2})}U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle U_{\pm}(R_{3}\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\omega U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle U_{\pm}(x_{1},-x_{2})

\displaystyle=\overline{U_{\pm}(x_{1},x_{2})},

where
\mathbf{r}=[x_{1},x_{2}]^{\top}
,
\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},a_{2}]^{\top}\in\Gamma
,
\omega:=e^{2\pi i/3}
, and
R_{3}
is the
\frac{2\pi}{3}
-counterclockwise rotation.
Potentials
U_{\pm}
satisfying symmetries
Eqs.

3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
are of the general form

(3.7)

U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{nm}e^{\pm i(m\mathbf{g}_{1}-n\mathbf{q}_{2}+\mathbf{q}_{1})\cdot\mathbf{r}}

where we recall the definitions of the dual lattice vectors
\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},
(
Eq.

2.4
) and
\mathbf{q}_{1}
(
Eq.

2.6
).
The coefficients

satisfy, see
[
3
, Prop.2.1]
,

\begin{split}c_{nm}&=\omega c_{(m-n-1)(-n)}=\omega^{2}c_{(-m)(n-m+1)}\text{ and }\\
\overline{c_{nm}}&=c_{(-m)(-n)}=\omega c_{(-n+m-1)m}=\omega^{2}c_{n(n-m+1)}\text{ for all }n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}.\end{split}

It is important to note that due to
Eq.

3.4

\Gamma

&&&1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a}),\qquad\mathcal{R}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\

(3.16)

\begin{split}\times:\ker_{L^{2}_{k,j}}(D_{\text{TBG}}(\alpha_{0}))&\oplus\ker_{L^{2}_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}(D_{\text{TBG}}(\alpha_{0}))\to\ker_{L^{2}_{k+k^{\prime},j+j^{\prime}}}(D_{\text{eTTG}}(\sqrt{2}\alpha_{0}))\\

), we see that we can turn TBG into a
\Gamma
-periodic Hamiltonian by conjugating it by
V(\mathbf{r}):=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}},1,e^{i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}})
to obtain the equivalent periodic Hamiltonian

\mathscr{H}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathscr{D}(\alpha)^{\dagger}\\
\mathscr{D}(\alpha)&0\end{bmatrix}\text{ with }\mathscr{D}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}&\alpha e^{iq_{0}\cdot\mathbf{r}}U_{+}(\mathbf{r})\\
\alpha e^{-iq_{0}\cdot\mathbf{r}}U_{-}(\mathbf{r})&D_{x_{1}}+iD_{x_{2}}+i\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, eTTG can be made periodic by conjugating by

V(\mathbf{r})=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}},e^{-i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}},1,e^{i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}},e^{-i\mathbf{q}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}})

and using the fact that
3\mathbf{q}_{1}=(0,3)^{\top}\in\Gamma^{*}
.

4.
Symmetries and Gauge Fixing

We now discuss the symmetry assumptions required for our main theorem which mirror the symmetries present in TBG and eTTG.
Due to the form of the chiral Hamiltonian
Eq.

3.1
and the symmetries of the
U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})
(
Eqs.

3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
), the Hamiltonians for TBG and eTTG satisfy a number of symmetries in addition to the translation
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{a}}
and rotation
\mathcal{R}
.
In particular, they satisfy a sublattice symmetry
\mathcal{Z}
, a layer symmetry
\mathcal{L}
, and a composite symmetry
\mathcal{Q}
, whose actions are defined as follows:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\psi(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)

\displaystyle=\sigma\psi(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\psi(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\psi(-\mathbf{r};-\sigma,N-j).

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\psi(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)

\displaystyle=\overline{\psi(-\mathbf{r};-\sigma,j)}

In words, the sublattice symmetry multiplies the
A
sublattice by
+1
and states supported on the
B
sublattice by
-1
.
The layer symmetry
\mathcal{L}
, reverses the order of the layers, swaps the
A
and
B
sublattices, multiplies by an alternating minus sign, and maps
\mathbf{r}\mapsto-\mathbf{r}
.
The symmetry
\mathcal{Q}
is a composition of a
C_{2z}
rotation and time reversal
\mathcal{T}
and acts by swapping the
A
and
B
sublattices, taking a complex conjugate, and mapping
\mathbf{r}\mapsto-\mathbf{r}
. The composite symmetry
\mathcal{Q}
is often referred to as the
C_{2z}\mathcal{T}
symmetry.
It is easily checked that
\{\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Q}\}=0
,
[\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{L}]=0
, and
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{L}]=0
.

We will use these symmetries to fix a specific choice of Bloch eigenbasis for the flat bands; a procedure we refer to as “gauge fixing” following the physics terminology.

Assumption 3

(Symmetry Assumptions)
.

We assume that the single particle Hamiltonian
H
commutes with
\mathcal{Q}
and anticommutes with
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{L}
.

Remark 2
.

Since we study the nullspace of
H
, we can consider both symmetries that commute or anticommute with
H
as both types of symmetries fix the nullspace.

If
\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\mathcal{L}
are symmetries of a periodic Hamiltonian, it is easily checked that
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Q}
map
\mathbf{k}
to
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathcal{L}
maps
\mathbf{k}
to
-\mathbf{k}
.

Since the zero energy eigenstates are degenerate within the flat band, once we
fix a basis of orthogonal flat-band eigenfunctions we may perform any
U(2M)

transformation to this basis and get an alternative choice of eigenbasis which
spans the same space. Any such transformation takes the form of the mapping

\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\mapsto\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})[U(\mathbf{k})]_{mn}

where
U(\mathbf{k})
is a unitary matrix. Under this basis transformation, the creation and annihilation operators likewise transform as

\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{n\mathbf{k}}\mapsto\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{m\mathbf{k}}[U(\mathbf{k})]_{mn}\qquad\qquad\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}\mapsto\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}[U(\mathbf{k})^{*}]_{mn}

and similarly the form factor transforms as

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\mapsto U(\mathbf{k})^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})U(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}).

By fixing a specific choice of eigenbasis, which we refer to as “fixing a gauge” following physics terminology, we can force the form factor to satisfy certain properties which will be a key part of our characterization of the Hartree-Fock ground state.
Note that the FBI Hamiltonian only depends on the form factor
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
which in turn only depends on the periodic Bloch functions,
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)
.
As such we will define our gauge fixing in terms of the periodic Bloch functions,
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)
, instead of the Bloch functions.

Remark 3
.

When the Hamiltonian
H
exhibits band crossings at zero energy, the set
\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):n\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}\}
is still closed under the action of symmetry operations for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{\rm crossing}
since the basis at the crossing points is defined through continuity.

Since the zero energy eigenstates
\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
are indexed by
n\in\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{Z}
is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, we may change the gauge and relabel the eigenfunctions so that:

(4.4)

\begin{split}\mathcal{Z}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)&=(+1)u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\qquad n>0,\\
\mathcal{Z}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)&=(-1)u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\qquad n<0.\end{split}

In particular, this implies that
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
is completely supported on the
A
sublattice if
n>0
and completely supported on the
B
sublattice if
n<0
.
We can now properly define the uniformly half-filled, translation invariant, ferromagnetic Slater determinant states:

Definition 4.1

(Ferromagnetic Slater Determinants)
.

Suppose that the Bloch eigenbasis has been chosen so that it satisfies
Eq.

4.4
. We define two uniformly half-filled, translation invariant ferromagnetic Slater determinants, or ferromagnetic Slater determinants for short, to be many-body states of the form:

\ket{\Psi_{+}}=\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\prod_{n>0,n\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\qquad\ket{\Psi_{-}}=\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\prod_{n<0,n\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}.

That is,
\ket{\Psi_{\pm}}
fully fills one of the two eigenspaces of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Since
\mathcal{Q}
is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian and relates the two different sublattices, after fixing the Bloch eigenfunctions to satisfy
Eq.

4.4
, we may fix the span
\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):n>0\}
, and determine
n<0
by the sublattice symmetry
\mathcal{Q}
. That is, for all
n>0
we define

(4.5)

u_{(-n)\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j):=\mathcal{Q}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)=\overline{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r};-\sigma,j)}.

Additionally since
\mathcal{L}
is also a symmetry of the Hamiltonian and relates
\mathbf{k}
and
-\mathbf{k}
, after fixing the periodic Bloch functions to satisfy
Eqs.

4.4
and
4.5
we may fix the space
\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):k_{1}\geq 0\}
and determine
k_{1}<0
by the layer symmetry
\mathcal{L}
. That is,

(4.6)

u_{n(-k_{1},k_{2})}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j):=\mathcal{L}u_{n(k_{1},-k_{2})}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)=(-1)^{j}u_{n(k_{1},-k_{2})}(-\mathbf{r};\sigma,N-j).

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that the gauge has been chosen so that periodic Bloch functions
\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}):n\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{k}\in\Omega^{*}\}
satisfy
Eqs.

4.4
,
4.5
and
4.6
. Then the form factor
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
can be written as

(4.7)

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})=\begin{bmatrix}A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})&\\
&\overline{A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}\end{bmatrix}

where

(4.8)

[A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{mn}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\qquad m,n>0.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be as in
Eq.

2.12
, then
A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
additionally satisfies the following
sum rule
:

(4.9)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\Imag{\tr{(A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}))}}=0,\quad\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}.

Proof.

Recall the definition of the form factor

(4.10)

[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{mn}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j).

Since the definition of the form factor involves a sum over the sublattice
\sigma
and
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
have disjoint sublattice support for
n>0
and
n<0
we immediately see that
[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{mn}=0
if
mn<0
and hence
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})
can be written as a block diagonal matrix.

Due to
\mathcal{Q}
symmetry (
Eq.

4.5
) we have that

(4.11)

\begin{split}\hat{u}_{(-n)\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j)&=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}u_{(-n)\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\overline{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\\
&=\overline{\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r};\sigma,j)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}\\
&=\overline{\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j)}.\end{split}

From this relation we easily see that

(4.12)

[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{(-m)(-n)}=[\overline{\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}]_{mn}

which together with the block diagonal structure implies
Eq.

4.7
. Now we turn to prove the sum rule
Eq.

4.9
.

Due to the layer symmetry (
Eq.

4.6
) we have that

(4.13)

\begin{split}\hat{u}_{n(-\mathbf{k})}(\mathbf{G};\sigma,j)&=\int_{\Omega}e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}(-1)^{j}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r};-\sigma,N-j)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\\
&=(-1)^{j}\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{G};-\sigma,N-j).\end{split}

Recall that
\Gamma^{*}
is closed under the map
\mathbf{G}\mapsto-\mathbf{G}
and therefore

(4.14)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)}\hat{u}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime};\sigma,j)\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}}\sum_{\sigma,j}\overline{\hat{u}_{m(-\mathbf{k})}(-\mathbf{G}^{\prime};-\sigma,N-j)}\hat{u}_{n(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G})}(-\mathbf{G}^{\prime};-\sigma,N-j)\\
&=\Lambda_{-\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G})\end{split}

which for
\mathbf{q}=\bm{0}
reduces to
\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})=\Lambda_{-\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{G})=\Lambda_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}
where the last equality is due to
Eq.

2.18
.
Note that this immediately implies that
\Lambda_{\bm{0}}(\mathbf{G})
is Hermitian.

We recall that elements of
\mathcal{K}
can be written as
\mathbf{k}=\frac{i}{n_{k_{x}}}\mathbf{g}_{1}+\frac{j}{n_{k_{y}}}\mathbf{g}_{2}
for some
i\in[n_{k_{x}}]
and
j\in[n_{k_{y}}]
.
For any
\mathbf{k}
of this form, we can find a
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
so that
-\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, we may partition the momentum grid
\mathcal{K}
into three disjoint sets
\{\bm{0}\}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
.
The point
\bm{0}
is the unique point so that
\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{k}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
are defined so that for each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
there exists a
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}
so that
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
.
Since
A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})=A_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})
for all
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{*}
(
Eq.

2.19
), we have

(4.15)

\begin{split}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})&=A_{\bm{0}}(\mathbf{G})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}}A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\\
&=A_{\bm{0}}(\mathbf{G})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}}A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}}A_{-\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})\\
&=A_{\bm{0}}(\mathbf{G})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}}(A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})+A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}))^{\dagger})\\
\end{split}

and hence
\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\Imag{\tr{(A_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G}))}}=0
. Here we have used
A_{\bm{0}}(\mathbf{G})
is Hermitian.
∎

5.
Properties of the Flat-Band Interacting Hamiltonian

In this section, we start by reviewing the basics on Hartree-Fock theory for FBI Hamiltonians (
Section

5.1
) and then prove some of their important many-body properties.
In particular, we prove that the FBI Hamiltonian (
Eq.

2.22
) is positive semidefinite (
Section

5.2
) and therefore any state which has zero energy must be a ground state.
Using this fact, along with the sum rule (
Eq.

4.9
), we prove that the ferromagnetic Slater determiant state are ground states (
Section

5.3
).
Finally, we show that the ferromagnetic Slater determinants are insulating in the sense of a charge gap, i.e., both adding and removing an electron costs a finite amount of energy (
Section

5.4
).

5.1.
A Review of Hartree-Fock Theory

Recall that Slater determinants defined by the set
\mathcal{S}
in
Eq.

1.5
take the form:

(5.1)

\ket{\Psi_{S}}=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\hat{b}_{i\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}},

where
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
is the vacuum state,
M
is the half the number of bands, and

(5.2)

\hat{b}_{i\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{{\dagger}}\Xi_{ni}(\mathbf{k})\qquad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\Xi_{ni}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\Xi_{nj}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{ij}

defines the creation operator for the Hartree-Fock orbitals for each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}
.

The Hartree-Fock equations can be expressed in terms of the one-body reduced density matrix (1-RDM). The 1-RDM associated with a given Slater determinant
\ket{\Psi_{S}}
can be written as

(5.3)

[P(\mathbf{k})]_{nm}=\braket{\Psi_{S}|\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}|\Psi_{S}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm occ}}\Xi_{ni}(\mathbf{k})\Xi_{mi}^{*}(\mathbf{k}).

Due to the orthogonality relation on
\Xi(\mathbf{k})
given in
Eq.

5.2
, we may verify that for each
\mathbf{k}
the 1-RDM
P(\mathbf{k})
is an orthogonal projection onto an
M
-dimensional vector space.

Remark 4
.

The specific matrix representation of a 1-RDM depends on the choice of basis used to define the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}
.
Using the gauge fixing scheme from
Section

4
, the two ferromagnetic Slater determinant states have 1-RDMs as follows (where
\eta\in\{\pm 1\}
):

(5.4)

\braket{\Psi_{\eta}|\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}|\Psi_{\eta}}=\begin{cases}\delta_{mn}&\eta m,\eta n>0\\
0&\eta m<0\text{ or }\eta n<0\end{cases}

That is, the 1-RDM for
\ket{\Psi_{+}}
is identity for all
\mathbf{k}
and all for
m,n>0
and zero otherwise (and similarly for
\ket{\Psi_{-}}
).

Let us define the manifold of all admissible uniformly-filled 1-RDMs:

\mathcal{M}:=\{P\in{\mathbb{C}}^{(2M)\times(2M)}:P^{2}=P,\penalty\ P^{\dagger}=P,\penalty\ \tr{(P)}=M\}.

Following the standard derivation of Hartree-Fock theory (see e.g.,
[
33
,
23
]
), the Hartree-Fock energy is given by finding the Slater determinant
\ket{\Psi_{S}}
which minimizes the energy of the interacting Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{E}^{\rm(HF)}=\min_{\ket{\Psi_{S}}}\braket{\Psi_{S}|\hat{H}_{FBI}|\Psi_{S}}.

Since
\hat{H}_{FBI}
only involves the electron-electron interaction term, the Hartree-Fock energy can be written as the sum of two functionals acting on the 1-RDM
P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{K}}
. In particular,

(5.5)

\mathcal{E}^{\rm(HF)}=\min_{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{K}}}\Big(J[P]+K[P]\Big)

where
J[\cdot]
and
K[\cdot]
are non-linear functionals in
P
, referred to as the Hartree and Fock energy functionals, respectively.

Due to the specific form of
\hat{H}_{FBI}
, using Wick’s theorem, the Hartree and Fock energies (up to a physically irrelevant constant) can be concisely written in terms the matrix
Q(\mathbf{k}):=2P(\mathbf{k})-I
as follows:

(5.6)

\displaystyle J[P]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}}V(\mathbf{G})\tr{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})Q(\mathbf{k})\Big)}\tr{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\Big)},

(5.7)

\displaystyle K[P]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\Omega|N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\tr{\Big(\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})Q(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}Q(\mathbf{k})\Big)}.

5.2.
Positive Semidefiniteness

In this section, we prove that the many-body Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{FBI}
is positive semidefinite.
We start by proving the following

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the operator
\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
satisfies
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\dagger}=\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

For any fixed
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{G}\in\Gamma^{*}
, we calculate

(5.8)

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{*}]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{mn}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{*}]_{nm}\left(\hat{f}_{m(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{mn}\right).\end{split}

Now we would like to make the change of variables
\mathbf{k}\mapsto\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}
.
Unfortunately, in general
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
does not imply that
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{K}
.
However, due to the definition of
\mathcal{K}
, we may always find a
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}\in\Gamma^{*}
so that

(5.9)

\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}=\widetilde{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{K}.

Under the change of variables
\mathbf{k}\mapsto\widetilde{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}
we have that

(5.10)

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{*}]_{nm}\left(\hat{f}_{m(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}})}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{mn}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})^{*}]_{nm}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{mn}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathcal{N}}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G})]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{mn}\right)=\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G})\end{split}

where in the second line we have used
Eq.

2.14
and
Eq.

2.19
.
∎

Since
\hat{H}_{FBI}
takes the form

(5.11)

\hat{H}_{FBI}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}^{{\dagger}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

and
\hat{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\hat{V}(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})>0
, and immediate corollary is that
any
many-body state
\ket{\Psi}
so that
\hat{H}_{FBI}\ket{\Psi}=0
must be a ground state.

5.3.
Ferromagnetic Slater Determinants are Ground States

We can now check that the ferromagnetic Slater determinant states are ground states by verifying they are zero energy eigenstates.

Proposition 5.2

(Proof of
Result

1
)
.

Suppose that the single particle Hamiltonian
H
satisfies the symmetry assumption (
3
). Then the two ferromagnetic Slater determinant states (
4.1
) are exact many-body ground states of the interacting model
Eq.

2.22
.

Proof.

We show that for all
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}
,
\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\ket{\Psi_{\pm}}=0
where
\ket{\Psi_{\pm}}
is a ferromagnetic Slater determinant.
While we only consider
\ket{\Psi_{+}}
, the calculation for
\ket{\Psi_{-}}
follows similar steps.

(5.12)

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\ket{\Psi_{+}}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\left[\sum_{m,n}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{mn}\delta_{\mathbf{q},\bm{0}}\right)\right]\ket{\Psi_{+}}\\
&=\delta_{\mathbf{q},\bm{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\left[\sum_{m,n}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})]_{mn}\left(\hat{f}_{m\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{f}_{n\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{mn}\right)\right]\ket{\Psi_{+}}\\
&=\delta_{\mathbf{q},\bm{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}}\left[(\sum_{m>0}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})]_{mm})-\frac{1}{2}(\sum_{m}[\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{G})]_{mm})\right]\ket{\Psi_{+}}\\

where in the second line, we have use the fact that

is block diagonal and the ferromagnetic Slater determinant state fully fills the

The last expression vanishes due to the sum rule in

.

5.4.

As we will see, adding or removing an electron to a ferromagnetic Slater determinant always costs a non-zero amount of energy.

Proposition 5.3

Fix

All such excitations can be written in terms of creation and annihilation operators of the following form:

7.1.

Since

After some lengthy computations (

∎

=\frac{1}{2}(|b^{(1)}_{ij}|^{2}+|b^{(2)}_{ij}|^{2})&\bigg(\cos{(\theta_{i}(\mathbf{k})-\theta_{j}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))}+\cos{(\theta_{i}(\mathbf{k})+\theta_{j}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))}\bigg)\\

But since

(A.13)

Combining these calculations together we finally find that

&+|b^{(1)}_{ij}-b^{(2)}_{ij}|^{2}\cos{(\theta_{i}(\mathbf{k})+\theta_{j}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}))}\bigg\}.\end{split}

We start by recalling the definition of the form factor for

\Lambda_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q})=\Phi(\mathbf{k})^{\dagger}\Phi(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}).

Note that
\Phi(\mathbf{k})

\hat{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}

\Phi(\mathbf{k})^{\dagger}\Phi(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}=I

Our goal is to show that there exists a

is full rank or equivalently for all

(B.4)

\begin{split}&\braket{v,\Phi(\mathbf{k})^{\dagger}\Phi(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\Phi(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})^{\dagger}\Phi(\mathbf{k})v}\\

\end{split}

are spectral projector onto the flat bands at momentum
\mathbf{k}
and

Suppose that the system has four flat bands then the following are equivalent for all
\mathbf{k}

\Pi
