1.
Introduction

For a Borel subset
B
of the complex plane
\mathbb{C},
let
M_{0}(B)
denote the set of finite complex-valued Borel measures that are compactly supported in
B
and let
M_{0}^{+}(B)
be the set of positive measures in
M_{0}(B).
The support of
\nu\in M_{0}(\mathbb{C}),

\text{spt}(\nu),
is the smallest closed set that has full
|\nu|
measure. For a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{C},

\nu_{A}
denotes
\nu
restricted to
A.

For a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}
and
\mu\in M_{0}^{+}(K),
the functions in

\mbox{Rat}(K):=\{q:\mbox{$q$ is a rational function
with poles off $K$}\}

are members of
L^{\infty}(\mu)
. We let
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
be the weak-star closure of
\mbox{Rat}(K)
in
L^{\infty}(\mu).

There exists a Borel partition
\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}
of
K
such that

\displaystyle\ R^{\infty}(K,\mu)=L^{\infty}(\mu|_{\Delta_{0}})\oplus R^{\infty}(K,\mu|_{\Delta_{1}})

(1.1)

where
R^{\infty}(K,\mu|_{\Delta_{1}})
contains no non-trivial
L^{\infty}
summands (see
[
3
, Proposition 1.16 on page 281]
).
Call
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
pure if
\Delta_{0}=\emptyset
in (
1.1
). Because of (
1.1
), we shall assume that
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
is pure.
The
envelope

E(K,\mu)
with respect to
K
and
\mu\in M_{0}^{+}(K)
is the set of points
\lambda\in K
such that there exists
\mu_{\lambda}\in M_{0}(K)
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
such that
\mu_{\lambda}(\{\lambda\})=0
and
\int f(z)d\mu_{\lambda}(z)=f(\lambda)
for each
f\in\text{Rat}(K).
From Proposition
2.1
(b),
E(K,\mu)
is a nonempty
{\mathfrak{m}}
measurable set with area density one at each of its points, where
{\mathfrak{m}}
is the area (Lebesgue) measure on
\mathbb{C}
.
For
\lambda\in E(K,\mu)
and
f\in R^{\infty}(K,,\mu),
set
\rho_{K,\mu}(f)(\lambda)=\int fd\mu_{\lambda}.
Clearly
\rho_{K,\mu}(f)(\lambda)
is independent of the particular
\mu_{\lambda}
chosen.
We thus have a map
\rho_{K,\mu}
called Chaumat’s map for
K
and
\mu,
which associates
to each function in
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
a point function on
E(K,\mu).
Chaumat’s Theorem
[
1
]
(also see
[
3
, page 288]
) states the following: The map
\rho_{K,\mu}
is an isometric isomorphism and a weak-star homeomorphism from
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
onto
R^{\infty}(\overline{E(K,\mu)},{\mathfrak{m}}_{E(K,\mu)}),
where
\overline{B}
denotes the closure of the set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
.

For
\mathcal{H}
a complex separable Hilbert space,
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}.
The spectrum of an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is denoted
\sigma(T).
An operator
S\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is called
subnormal
if there exists a complex separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
containing
\mathcal{H}
and a normal operator
N\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
such that
N\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{H}
and
S=N|_{\mathcal{H}}.
Such an
N
is called a
minimal normal extension
(mne) of
S
if the smallest closed subspace of
\mathcal{K}
containing
\mathcal{H}
and reducing
N
is
\mathcal{K}
itself. Any two mnes of
S
are unitarily equivalent in a manner that fixes
S.

The spectrum
\sigma(S)
is the union of
\sigma(N)
and some collection of bounded components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(N).
The book
[
3
]
is a good reference for basic information for subnormal operators.

For a subnormal operator
S\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
with
N=mne(S),
let
\mu
be the scalar-valued spectral measure (svsm) for
N.
Since
\text{spt}(\mu)=\sigma(N)\subset\sigma(S),
we see that
\text{Rat}(\sigma(S))\subset L^{\infty}(\mu).

Thus, for
f\in R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu),
the normal operator
f(N)
is well defined and
f(N)\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{H}.

Therefore, the operator
f(S):=f(N)|_{\mathcal{H}}
defines a functional calculus for
S
and
f\in R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu).

The operator
S
is pure if
S
does not contain a non-trivial normal summand and
S
is
R^{\infty}
pure if
R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu)
is pure. Clearly, if
S
is pure, then
S
is
R^{\infty}
pure.

The following theorem in
[
5
, Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2]
proves the conjecture
\diamond
posed by J. Dudziak in
[
4
, on page 386]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Yang 2023)
Let
S\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be a subnormal operator and let
\mu
be the scalar-valued spectral measure for
N=mne(S).
Assume that
S
is
R^{\infty}
pure. If for
f\in R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu),
there exists
\epsilon_{f}>0
such that

\ |\rho_{\sigma(S),\mu}(f)(z)|\geq\epsilon_{f},~{\mathfrak{m}}_{E(\sigma(S),\mu)}-a.a.,

then
f
is invertible in
R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu)
and
f(S)
is invertible. Consequently,
\sigma(f(S))\subset f(\sigma(S)),
where
f(\sigma(S))
denotes the essential range of
\rho_{\sigma(S),\mu}(f)
in
L^{\infty}({\mathfrak{m}}_{E(\sigma(S),\mu)}).

The second open question posted in
[
4
, page 386]
is that of whether the inclusion
f(\sigma(S))\subset\sigma(f(S))
holds for every
R^{\infty}
pure subnormal operator
S
and every
f\in R^{\infty}(\sigma(S),\mu).
J. Dudziak conjectured that the answer to the question would be no and suggested that an appropriate place to look for an example would be among rationally cyclic
R^{\infty}
pure subnormal operators. For a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}
and
\mu\in M_{0}^{+}(K),
let
R^{2}(K,\mu)
denote the closure of
\text{Rat}(K)
in
L^{2}(\mu)
and let
S_{\mu}
denote the multiplication by
z
on
R^{2}(K,\mu).
Clearly,
R^{2}(K,\mu)=R^{2}(\sigma(S_{\mu}),\mu).
So we will always assume
K=\sigma(S_{\mu}).
It is well known that a rationally cyclic subnormal operator is unitarily equivalent to
S_{\mu}
on
R^{2}(\sigma(S_{\mu}),\mu)
(see
[
2
, III.5.2]
). Our main theorem provides such an example.

Main Theorem
.

There exists
\mu\in M_{0}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
and a function
f\in R^{\infty}(K,\mu),
where
K=\text{spt}\mu,
such that
R^{\infty}(K,\mu)
is pure,

|f|\geq\epsilon,~\mu-a.a.
for some
\epsilon>0,

f
is invertible in
R^{2}(K,\mu)\cap L^{\infty}(\mu),
and
f
is not invertible in
R^{\infty}(K,\mu).

As a result, our corollary below answers the above open question negatively.

Corollary 1.2
.

There exists
\mu\in M_{0}^{+}(\mathbb{C})
and there exists a function
f\in R^{\infty}(\sigma(S_{\mu}),\mu)
such that
S_{\mu}
is
R^{\infty}
pure and
f(\sigma(S_{\mu}))\setminus\sigma(f(S_{\mu}))\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Let
\mu
and
f
be as in Main Theorem. Then, by Main Theorem, there exists
f_{0}\in R^{2}(K,\mu)\cap L^{\infty}(\mu)
such that
ff_{0}=1
and
f
is not invertible in
R^{\infty}(K,\mu).
Since
f_{0}R^{2}(K,\mu)\subset R^{2}(K,\mu),
we see that
f_{0}(S_{\mu})f(S_{\mu})=f(S_{\mu})f_{0}(S_{\mu})=I
which implies
0\notin\sigma(f(S_{\mu})).
Using Theorem
1.1
, we conclude that zero belongs to the essential range of
\rho_{\sigma(S_{\mu}),\mu}(f)
in
L^{\infty}({\mathfrak{m}}_{E(\sigma(S_{\mu}),\mu)}).
Therefore,
0\in f(\sigma(S_{\mu})).

Using the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we have

\ \dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{T}\dfrac{h_{n}(z)}{z-\lambda}dz=h_{n}(\lambda)\rightarrow\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{T}\dfrac{h(z)}{z-\lambda}dz.

Therefore,
\phi_{\lambda}
extends a weak-star continuous homomorphism on
R^{\infty}(K,W{\mathfrak{m}}),
which implies
\lambda\in E(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})
by Proposition
2.1
(a). Thus,
{\mathfrak{m}}(K\setminus E(K,W{\mathfrak{m}}))=0
since
\int_{T}\frac{1}{|z-\lambda|}|dz|<\infty~{\mathfrak{m}}-a.a..

0\in E(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})
follows from (3).

(c): Clearly,

\ R^{2}(A_{n},W{\mathfrak{m}}_{A_{n}})\cap L^{\infty}(W{\mathfrak{m}}_{A_{n}})=R^{\infty}(A_{n},{\mathfrak{m}}_{A_{n}})=H^{\infty}(\text{int}(A_{n})),

where
H^{\infty}(\text{int}(A_{n}))
is the algebra of bounded and analytic functions on
\text{int}(A_{n}).

Since
|f(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2},~z\in\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n},
the function
f|_{A_{n}}
is invertible in
R^{2}(A_{n},W{\mathfrak{m}}_{A_{n}})\cap L^{\infty}(W{\mathfrak{m}}_{A_{n}})
and
\|(f|_{A_{n}})^{-1}\|\leq 2.
Define

h=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(f|_{A_{n}})^{-1}.

Then
\|h\|\leq 2,

h\in R^{2}(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})\cap L^{\infty}(W{\mathfrak{m}}),
and
fh=1,~W{\mathfrak{m}}-a.a..
Therefore,
we conclude that
f
is invertible in
R^{2}(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})\cap L^{\infty}(W{\mathfrak{m}})
and
f
is not invertible in
R^{\infty}(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})
by
0\in E(K,W{\mathfrak{m}})
and (7). This proves (c).
∎

The proof of Main Theorem follows from Lemma
2.2
.
