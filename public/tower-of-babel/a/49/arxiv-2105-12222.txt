Projecting onto rectangular matrices with prescribed row and column sums
Heinz H. Bauschke, Shambhavi Singh, Xianfu Wang

Abstract
In 1990, Romero presented a beautiful formula for the projection onto the set of rectangular matrices with prescribed row and column sums. Variants of Romero’s formula have been rediscovered by Khoury and by Glunt, Hayden, and Reams, for bistochastic (square) matrices in 1998. These results have found various generalizations and applications. In this paper, we provide a formula for the more general problem of finding the projection onto the set of rectangular matrices with prescribed scaled row and column sums. Our approach is based on computing the Moore-Penrose inverse of a certain linear operator associated with the problem. In fact, our analysis holds even for Hilbert-Schmidt operators and we do not have to assume consistency. We also perform numerical experiments featuring the new projection operator.

1
Motivation

A matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called
bistochastic
if all entries of it are nonnegative and all its row and column sums equal
1
.
More generally, a matrix is
generalized bistochastic

if the requirement on nonnegativity is dropped.
The bistochastic matrices form a convex polytope
B
,
commonly called the
Birkhoff polytope
,
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with its extreme points being
the permutation matrices (a seminal result due to
Birkhoff and von Neumann).
A lovely formula provided in 1998 by Khoury
[
8
]

— and also by Glunt et al.
[
5
]
—
gives
the projection of any matrix onto
G
, the affine subspace of
generalized bistochastic matrices
(see
Example

3.8
below).

More generally, nonnegative
rectangular
matrices with prescribed
row and column sums are called
transportation polytopes
.
If the nonnegativity assumption is dropped, then
Romero provided already in 1990 an explicit formula
(see
Remark

3.5
below) which even predates the square case!

On the other hand,
the projection onto the set of nonnegative matrices
N
is simple — just replace every negative entry by
0
.
No explicit formula is known to project a matrix onto the set
of bistochastic matrices; however, because
B=G\cap N
,
one may apply algorithms such as Dykstra’s algorithm to iteratively approximate the projection onto
B
by using
the projection operators
P_{G}
and
P_{N}
(see,
e.g., Takouda’s
[
12
]
for details).

In the case of transportation polytopes, algorithms
which even converge in
finitely many steps

were provided by Calvillo and Romero
[
4
]
.

The goal of this paper is to provide explicit projection
operators in more general settings.
Specifically, we present
a projection formula for finding the
projection onto
the set of rectangular matrices
with prescribed
scaled
row and column sums
.
Such problems arise, e.g., in
discrete tomography

[
13
]
and the study of
transportation polytopes

[
4
]
.
Our approach uses
the Moore-Penrose inverse of a certain linear operator
\mathcal{A}
.
It turns out
that our analysis works even for Hilbert-Schmidt operators
because the range of
\mathcal{A}
can be determined and seen to be
closed. Our main references are
[
3
]
,
[
7
]

(for Hilbert-Schmidt operators),
and
[
6
]
(for the Moore-Penrose inverse).
We also note that consistency is not required.

The paper is organized as follows.
After recording a useful result involving the Moore-Penrose
inverse at the end of this section,
we prove our main results in
Section

2
.
These result are then specialized to rectangular matrices
in
Section

3
.
We then turn to numerical experiments in
Section

4

where we compare the performance of three popular algorithms:
Douglas-Rachford, Method of Alternating Projections, and Dykstra.

We conclude this introductory section with a result
which we believe to be part of the folklore (although
we were not able to pinpoint a crisp reference).

It is formulated using the
Moore-Penrose inverse

of an operator — for the definition of the
Moore-Penrose inverse and its basic properties,
see
[
6
]
(and also
[
3
, pages 57–59]

for a crash course).
The formula presented works even in the case when the problem
is inconsistent and automatically provides a least squares solution.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
A\colon X\to Y
be a continuous linear operator
with closed range between two real Hilbert spaces.
Let
b\in Y
, set
\bar{b}:=P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,A}b
,
and set
C:=A^{-1}\bar{b}
.
Then

(\forall x\in X)\quad P_{C}x=x-A^{\dagger}(Ax-b),

(1)

where
A^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse of
A
.

Proof
. Clearly,
\bar{b}\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,A
;
hence,
C\neq\varnothing
.
Let
x\in X
.
It is well known
(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Example 29.17(ii)]
)
that

P_{C}x=x-A^{\dagger}(Ax-\bar{b}).

(2)

On the other hand,

A^{\dagger}\bar{b}=A^{\dagger}P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,A}b=A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}b=A^{\dagger}b

(3)

using the fact that

AA^{\dagger}=P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,A}

(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Proposition 3.30(ii)]
)
and
A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}

(see, e.g.,
[
6
, Section II.2]
).
Altogether,
P_{C}x=x-A^{\dagger}(Ax-b)
as claimed.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

2
Hilbert-Schmidt operators

From now on, we assume that

\text{
$X$ and $Y$ are
two real Hilbert spaces},

(4)

which in turn give rise to the real Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}:=\big\{{T\colon X\to Y}~\big|~{\text{$T$ is Hilbert-Schmidt}}\big\}.

(5)

Hilbert-Schmidt operators encompass rectangular matrices
— even with infinitely many entries as long as these are
square summable — as well as certain integral operators.
(We refer the reader to

[
7
, Section 2.6]
for basic results
on Hilbert-Schmidt operators

and also recommend
[
10
, Section VI.6]
.)

Moreover,

\mathcal{H}
(is generated by and) contains

rank-one
operators of the form

(v\otimes u)\colon X\to Y\colon x\mapsto\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle v,

(6)

where
(v,u)\in Y\times X
, and with adjoint

(v\otimes u)^{*}\colon Y\to X\colon y\mapsto\left\langle{v},{y}\right\rangle u

(7)

and

(v\otimes u)^{*}=u\otimes v.

(8)

Moreover,

u\otimes u=\|u\|^{2}P_{\,\mathbb{R}u}\quad\text{and}\quad v\otimes v=\|v\|^{2}P_{\,\mathbb{R}v}.

(9)

For the rest of the paper, we fix

e\in X
and
f\in Y
,

(10)

and set

\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{H}\to Y\times X\colon T\mapsto(Te,T^{*}f).

(11)

Proposition 2.1
.

\mathcal{A}
is a continuous linear operator
and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sqrt{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}
.

Proof
. Clearly,
\mathcal{A}
is a linear operator.
Moreover,
(\forall T\in\mathcal{H})

\|\mathcal{A}(T)\|^{2}=\|Te\|^{2}+\|T^{*}f\|^{2}\leq\|T\|_{\textsf{op}}^{2}\|e\|^{2}+\|T^{*}\|_{\textsf{op}}^{2}\|f\|^{2}\leq\|T\|^{2}(\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2})

because the Hilbert-Schmidt norm dominates the operator norm.
It follows that
\mathcal{A}
is continuous and

\|\mathcal{A}\|\leq\sqrt{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}
.
On the other hand,
if
T=f\otimes e
,
then

\|T\|=\|e\|\|f\|
,

\mathcal{A}(T)=(\|e\|^{2}f,\|f\|^{2}e)
and hence

\|\mathcal{A}(T)\|=\|T\|\sqrt{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}
.
Thus
\|\mathcal{A}\|\geq\sqrt{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}
.
Combining these observations, we obtain
altogether that

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sqrt{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

We now prove that
{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
is always closed.

Proposition 2.2
.

(range of
\mathcal{A}
is closed)

The following hold:

(i)

If
e=0
and
f=0
, then

{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}=\{0\}\times\{0\}
.

(ii)

If
e=0
and
f\neq 0
, then

{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}=\{0\}\times X
.

(iii)

If
e\neq 0
and
f=0
, then

{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}=Y\times\{0\}
.

(iv)

If
e\neq 0
and
f\neq 0
, then

{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}=\{(f,-e)\}^{\perp}
.

Consequently,
{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
is always
a closed linear subspace of
Y\times X
.

Proof
.
Item

(i)
: Clear.

Item

(ii)
:
Obviously,
{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{0\}\times X
.
Conversely, let
x\in X
and set

T:=\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes x.

(12)

Then
Te=T0=0
and

T^{*}f=\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}(f\otimes x)^{*}f=\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle x=x

(13)

and thus

(0,x)=(Te,T^{*}f)=\mathcal{A}(T)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
.

Item

(iii)
:
Obviously,
{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}\subseteq Y\times\{0\}
.
Conversely, let
y\in Y
and set

T:=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}y\otimes e.

(14)

Then
T^{*}f=T^{*}0=0
and

Te=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}(y\otimes e)e=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\left\langle{e},{e}\right\rangle y=y

(15)

and thus

(y,0)=(Te,T^{*}f)=\mathcal{A}(T)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
.

Item

(iv)
:
If
(y,x)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
, say

(y,x)=\mathcal{A}(T)=(Te,T^{*}f)
for some
T\in\mathcal{H}
,
then

\displaystyle\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{f},{Te}\right\rangle=\left\langle{T^{*}f},{e}\right\rangle=\left\langle{x},{e}\right\rangle,

(16)

i.e.,
(y,x)\perp(f,-e)
.
It follows that
{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{(f,-e)\}^{\perp}
.

Conversely, let
(y,x)\in\{(f,-e)\}^{\perp}
, i.e.,

\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle
.

Case 1:

\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle\neq 0
.

Set

\zeta:=\frac{1}{\left\langle{x},{e}\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle}\quad\text{and}\quad T:=\zeta(y\otimes x)\in\mathcal{H}.

(17)

Note that

Te=\zeta(y\otimes x)e=\zeta\left\langle{x},{e}\right\rangle y=y

(18)

and

T^{*}f=\zeta(y\otimes x)^{*}f=\zeta\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle x=x;

(19)

therefore,
(y,x)=(Te,T^{*}f)=\mathcal{A}(T)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
.

Case 2:

\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle=0
.

Pick
\xi
and
\eta
in
\mathbb{R}
such that

\xi\|f\|^{2}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\eta\|e\|^{2}=1,

(20)

and set

T:=\xi(f\otimes x)+\eta(y\otimes e)\in\mathcal{H}.

(21)

Then

Te=\xi(f\otimes x)e+\eta(y\otimes e)e=\xi\left\langle{x},{e}\right\rangle f+\eta\left\langle{e},{e}\right\rangle y=0f+\eta\|e\|^{2}y=y

(22)

and

T^{*}f=\xi(f\otimes x)^{*}f+\eta(y\otimes e)^{*}f=\xi\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle x+\eta\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle e=\xi\|f\|^{2}x+0e=x.

(23)

Thus

(y,x)=(Te,T^{*}f)=\mathcal{A}(T)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

We now turn to the adjoint of
\mathcal{A}
:

Proposition 2.3
.

(adjoint of
\mathcal{A}
)

We have

\mathcal{A}^{*}\colon{Y}\times{X}\to\mathcal{H}\colon(y,x)\mapsto y\otimes e+f\otimes x.

(24)

Proof
. Let
T\in\mathcal{H}
and
(y,x)\in Y\times X
.
Let
B
be any orthonormal basis of
X
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{A}(T)},{(y,x)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{(Te,T^{*}f)},{(y,x)}\right\rangle

(25a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{Te},{y}\right\rangle+\left\langle{T^{*}f},{x}\right\rangle

(25b)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{e},{T^{*}y}\right\rangle+\left\langle{T^{*}f},{x}\right\rangle

(25c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}\big(\left\langle{e},{b}\right\rangle\left\langle{b},{T^{*}y}\right\rangle+\left\langle{T^{*}f},{b}\right\rangle\left\langle{b},{x}\right\rangle\big)

(25d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}\left\langle{Tb},{\left\langle{e},{b}\right\rangle y}\right\rangle+\sum_{b\in B}\left\langle{Tb},{\left\langle{x},{b}\right\rangle f}\right\rangle

(25e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}\left\langle{Tb},{(y\otimes e)b}\right\rangle+\sum_{b\in B}\left\langle{Tb},{(f\otimes x)b}\right\rangle

(25f)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{T},{y\otimes e}\right\rangle+\left\langle{T},{f\otimes x}\right\rangle

(25g)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{T},{y\otimes e+f\otimes x}\right\rangle

(25h)

which proves the result.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

We have all the results together to start tackling
the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

(Moore-Penrose inverse of
\mathcal{A}
part 1)

Suppose that
e\neq 0
and
f\neq 0
.
Let
(y,x)\in Y\times X
.
Then

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(y\otimes e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{y}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big)+\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(f\otimes x-\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big).

(26)

Proof
. Set

(v,u):=\bigg(\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(y-\frac{\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\Big),\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(x-\frac{\left\langle{x},{e}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}e\Big)\bigg).

(27)

Then

\displaystyle\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle\Big)

(28a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}\Big)

(28b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}}\cdot\frac{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}-\|f\|^{2}}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}

(28c)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}},

(28d)

and similarly

\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle=\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}.

(29)

Substituting
Eq.

28
and
Eq.

29

in
Eq.

27
yields

(v,u)=\Big(\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\big(y-\left\langle{f},{{v}}\right\rangle f\big),\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\big(x-\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle e\big)\Big).

(30)

Thus

y=\|e\|^{2}v+\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle f\quad\text{and}\quad x=\|f\|^{2}u+\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle e.

(31)

Therefore, using
Eq.

24
,
Eq.

30
,

Eq.

7
, and
Eq.

24
again,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{*}(v,u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}(v\otimes e+f\otimes u)

(32a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}\big((v\otimes e)e+(f\otimes u)e,(v\otimes e)^{*}f+(f\otimes u)^{*}f\big)

(32b)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}\big(\|e\|^{2}v+\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle f,\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle e+\|f\|^{2}u\big)

(32c)

\displaystyle=\big(\|e\|^{2}v+\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle f\big)\otimes e+f\otimes\big(\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle e+\|f\|^{2}u\big)

(32d)

\displaystyle=\|e\|^{2}v\otimes e+\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle f\otimes e+\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle f\otimes e+\|f\|^{2}f\otimes u

(32e)

\displaystyle=\big(\|e\|^{2}v+\left\langle{f},{v}\right\rangle f\big)\otimes e+f\otimes\big(\|f\|^{2}u+\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle e\big)

(32f)

\displaystyle=y\otimes e+f\otimes x

(32g)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}(y,x).

(32h)

To sum up,
we found
\mathcal{A}^{*}(v,u)\in{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}^{*}=(\ker\mathcal{A})^{\perp}

such that

\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{*}(v,u)=\mathcal{A}^{*}(y,x)
.
By
[
3

,

Eq.

30
, and
Eq.

,
we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}(v,u)

(33a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{*}\Big(\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\big(y-\left\langle{f},{{v}}\right\rangle f\big),\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\big(x-\left\langle{e},{u}\right\rangle e\big)\Big)

Theorem 2.5
.

(Moore-Penrose inverse of
\mathcal{A}
part 2)

Let
(y,x)\in Y\times X
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

If
e=0
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)=\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes x
.

(ii)

If
e\neq 0
and

, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}y\otimes e
.

. Let
T\in\mathcal{H}
.

Item

(i)
:
In this case,

\mathcal{A}(T)=(0,T^{*}f)

\mathcal{A}^{*}(y,x)=f\otimes x
.

[

First, using

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\big(\|f\|^{-2}f\otimes x\big)=\|f\|^{-2}\big((f\otimes x)e,(f\otimes x)^{*}f\big)

\displaystyle\big\langle{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)},{(v,u)}\big\rangle=\big\langle{(0,x)},{(v,u)}\big\rangle=\left\langle{x},{u}\right\rangle=\big\langle{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(v,u)},{(y,x)}\big\rangle

P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}^{*}}(T)=\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}(T)=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}(Te)\otimes e+\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes(T^{*}f)-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{Te}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e.

(44)

Furthermore,

P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}}(y,x)=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)=\begin{cases}(0,x),&\text{if $e=0$ and $f\neq 0$;}\\[2.84526pt]
(y,0),&\text{if $e\neq 0$ and $f=0$;}\\[2.84526pt]
(0,0),&\text{if $e=0$ and $f=0$;}\end{cases}

(45)

and

P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}^{*}}(T)=\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}(T)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes(T^{*}f),&\text{if $e=0$ and $f\neq 0$;}\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}(Te)\otimes e,&\text{if $e\neq 0$ and $f=0$;}\\[11.38109pt]
0,&\text{if $e=0$ and $f=0$.}\end{cases}

(46)

Proof
. Using
[

and

Eq.

, we obtain

for

and
f\neq 0

\displaystyle P_{{\operatorname{ran}}\,\mathcal{A}}(y,x)

(47a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}(y,x)

(47b)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\bigg(\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(y\otimes e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{y}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big)+\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(f\otimes x-\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(y\otimes e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{y}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big)e+\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(f\otimes x-\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big)e,

(47d)

(47f)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}\Big(\left\langle{y},{f}\right\rangle e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{y}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle e\Big)+\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle x-\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle e\Big)\bigg)

(47g)

\displaystyle=\bigg(y-\frac{\left\langle{f},{{y}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f+\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle}{\|f\|^{2}}f-\frac{\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle\|e\|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}\big(\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}\big)}f,

(47h)

\displaystyle\quad x-\frac{\left\langle{e},{{x}}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}e+\frac{\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}}e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle\|f\|^{2}}{\|e\|^{2}\big(\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}\big)}e\bigg)

(47i)

\displaystyle=\bigg(y+\frac{-\left\langle{f},{y}\right\rangle\|f\|^{2}+\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle\big(\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}\big)-\left\langle{e},{x}\right\rangle\|e\|^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}\big(\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}\big)}f,

(47j)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}\Big(f\otimes(T^{*}f-r)-\frac{\left\langle{e},{T^{*}f-r}\right\rangle}{\|e\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes e\Big).

Corollary 2.10
.

Suppose that
Y=X
, let
e\in X\smallsetminus\{0\}
,
let
f\in X\smallsetminus\{0\}
,
set

E:=\frac{1}{\|e\|^{2}}e\otimes e=P_{\,\mathbb{R}e}\quad\text{and}\quad F:=\frac{1}{\|f\|^{2}}f\otimes f=P_{\,\mathbb{R}f},

(56)

and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

C:=\big\{{T\in\mathcal{H}}~\big|~{Te=\gamma e\;\text{and}\;T^{*}f=\gamma f}\big\}\neq\varnothing

(57)

and

(\forall T\in\mathcal{H})\quad P_{C}(T)=\gamma\operatorname{Id}+(\operatorname{Id}-F)(T-\gamma\operatorname{Id})(\operatorname{Id}-E).

(58)

Proof
. The projection identities in
Eq.

56

follow from
Eq.

9
.
Note that
\gamma\operatorname{Id}\in C

and hence
C\neq\varnothing
.
We may and do assume without loss of generality
that
\|e\|=1=\|f\|
.

Now let
T\in\mathcal{H}
.
Applying
Eq.

52

with
r:=\gamma f
and
s:=\gamma e
,
we deduce that

\displaystyle P_{C}(T)

\displaystyle=T-\Big((Te-\gamma e)\otimes e-\frac{\left\langle{f},{Te-\gamma e}\right\rangle}{2}f\otimes e\Big)

(59a)

\displaystyle\qquad-\Big(f\otimes(T^{*}f-\gamma f)-\frac{\left\langle{e},{T^{*}f-\gamma f}\right\rangle}{2}f\otimes e\Big)

(59b)

\displaystyle=T-(Te)\otimes e+\gamma e\otimes e+\frac{\left\langle{f},{Te}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle{f},{e}\right\rangle}{2}f\otimes e

(59c)

\displaystyle\qquad-f\otimes(T^{*}f)+\gamma f\otimes f+\frac{\left\langle{Te},{f}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle{e},{f}\right\rangle}{2}f\otimes e

(59d)

\displaystyle=T-(Te)\otimes e-f\otimes(T^{*}f)+\gamma(E+F)+\big(\left\langle{f},{Te}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle{e},{f}\right\rangle\big)f\otimes e

(59e)

\displaystyle=T-TE-FT+\gamma(E+F)+\big(\left\langle{f},{Te}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle{e},{f}\right\rangle\big)f\otimes e

(59f)

\displaystyle=T-TE-FT+\gamma(E+F)+FTE-\gamma FE

(59g)

\displaystyle=\gamma\operatorname{Id}+T-TE-FT+FTE-\gamma\operatorname{Id}+\gamma E+\gamma F-\gamma FE

(59h)

\displaystyle=\gamma\operatorname{Id}+(\operatorname{Id}-F)T(\operatorname{Id}-E)-\gamma(\operatorname{Id}-F)(\operatorname{Id}-E)

(59i)

\displaystyle=\gamma\operatorname{Id}+(\operatorname{Id}-F)(T-\gamma\operatorname{Id})(\operatorname{Id}-E)

(59j)

as claimed.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

C:=\big\{{T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}~\big|~{Te=e=T^{\intercal}e}\big\}\neq\varnothing

(84)

and

(\forall T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\quad P_{C}(T)=E+(\operatorname{Id}-E)T(\operatorname{Id}-E).

(85)

Example 3.8
.

(projection formula for
generalized bistochastic matrices; 1998)

(See

and

Set

(86)

Then

(\forall T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})\quad P_{C}(T)=J+(\operatorname{Id}-J)T(\operatorname{Id}-J).

(87)

Proof

3.7
with
e=u

for which

.

4
Numerical experiments

We consider the problem of finding a rectangular matrix with prescribed row and column sums as well as some
additional constraints on the entries of the matrix.
To be specific and inspired by
[
1
]
,
we seek a real matrix of size
m\times n=4\times 5
such that its
row and column sums are equal to

\bar{s}:=\begin{bmatrix}32,43,33,23\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
and

\bar{r}:=\begin{bmatrix}24,18,37,27,25\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
,
respectively. One solution featuring actually nonnegative integers to
this problem is given by

\begin{array}[]{rrrrr|r}9&4&8&4&7&32\\
7&9&15&7&5&43\\
3&2&9&10&9&33\\
5&3&5&6&4&23\\
\hline\cr 24&18&37&27&25&131\end{array}

Adopting the notation of
Corollary

3.4
, we see that the set

B:=\big\{{T\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 5}}~\big|~{Te=\bar{s}\;\text{and}\;T^{\intercal}f=\bar{r}}\big\}\neq\varnothing

(88)

is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{4\times 5}
and that
an explicit formula for
P_{B}
is available through
Corollary

3.4
.
Next, we define the closed convex “hyper box”

A:=\bigtimes_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,2,3,4\}\\
j\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}\end{subarray}}\big[0,\min\{{\bar{s}}_{i},{\bar{r}}_{j}\}\big].

(89)

For instance, the
(1,3)
-entry of any nonnegative integer solution must lie between
0
and
32=\min\{32,37\}
; thus

A_{1,3}=[0,32]
.
The projection of a real number
\xi
onto the interval

[0,\min({\bar{s}}_{i},{\bar{r}}_{j})]
is given by

\max\{0,\min\{{\bar{s}}_{i},{\bar{r}}_{j},\xi\}\}
.
Because
A
is the Cartesian product of such intervals,
the projection operator
P_{A}
is nothing but the corresponding
product of interval projection operators.

Our problem is thus to

find a matrix
T
in
A\cap B
.

(90)

We shall tackle
Eq.

90
with three well known algorithms:
Douglas-Rachford (DR), Method of Alternating Projections (MAP),
and Dykstra (Dyk). Here is a quick review of how these methods
operate, for a given starting matrix

T_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 5}
and a current matrix

T_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 5}
.

DR updates via

T_{k+1}:=T_{k}-P_{A}(T_{k})+P_{B}(2P_{A}(T_{k})-T_{k}),

(DR)

MAP updates via

T_{k+1}:=P_{B}(P_{A}(T_{k})),

(MAP)

and finally
Dyk initializes also
R_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 5}

and updates via

A_{k+1}:=P_{A}(T_{k}+R_{k}),\;\;R_{k+1}:=T_{k}+R_{k}-A_{k+1},\;\;T_{k+1}:=P_{B}(A_{k+1}).

(Dyk)

For all three algorithms, it is
known that

P_{A}(T_{k})\to\text{some matrix in $A\cap B$;}

(91)

in fact, Dyk satisfies even
P_{A}(T_{k})\to P_{A\cap B}(T_{0})

(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Corollary 28.3, Corollary 5.26, and Theorem 30.7]
).

Consequently,
for each of the three algorithms, we will focus on the sequence

(P_{A}(T_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

(92)

which obviously lies in
A
and which thus
prompts the simple feasibility criterion given by

\delta_{k}:=\|P_{A}(T_{k})-P_{B}(P_{A}(T_{k}))\|.

(93)
