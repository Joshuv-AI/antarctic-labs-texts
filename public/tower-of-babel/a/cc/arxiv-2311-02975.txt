Abstract
. We prove that nonnegative almost minimizers of the horizontal Bernoulli-type functional J ( u , Ω ) := ∫ Ω ( | ∇ 𝔾 u ( x ) | 2 + χ { u > 0 } ( x ) ) d x J(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\Big(|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}+\chi_{\{u>0\}}(x)\Big)\,dx are Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Carnot-Carathéodory distance.

1.
Introduction and main result

In this paper, we study the regularity of almost minimizers of Bernoulli-type energy functionals in Carnot groups.

In the Euclidean setting, the regularity of minimizers for the classical one-phase Bernoulli energy functional

(1.1)

\mathscr{F}(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\Big(|\nabla u(x)|^{2}+\chi_{\{u>0\}}(x)\Big)\,dx

has been deeply studied since the pioneering work of Alt and Caffarelli, see
[
3
]
. We refer the reader to
[
52
]
and the references therein for a comprehensive description of the subject.

More recently, the regularity of almost minimizers associated with Bernoulli-type functionals was investigated as well. We recall that the notion of
almost minimality
is a relaxed notion of minimality that arises from variational problems with constraints, such as problems with prescribed volume or curvature and obstacle problems. Almgren introduced the notion of almost minimizers in geometric measure theory
[
2
]
. In the nonparametric setting, this notion was introduced by Anzellotti in
[
5
]
. Roughly speaking, almost minimizers may be considered as local perturbations of minimizers, which have smaller contribution at small scales. Hence, one may expect that similar regularity results for minimizers hold also for almost minimizers. Nevertheless, the condition satisfied by almost minimizers does not allow to obtain an Euler-Lagrange equation as in the case of minimizers.

Concerning Bernoulli-type functionals, in
[
16
]
, David and Toro proved that almost minimizers of (
1.1
) are locally Lipschitz continuous, which is the optimal regularity for minimizers. Results about the free boundary regularity can be found in
[
15
]
. These works employ variational techniques involving tools coming from potential theory and geometric measure theory.
We refer to
[
48
]
for a comprehensive overview on the topic.
More recently, De Silva and Savin, in
[
18
]
, introduced a more direct approach to obtain Lipschitz regularity of almost minimizers of (
1.1
) based on a dichotomy argument.
Since their techniques involve, mainly, only metric properties, we point out that they may be extended to different more general frameworks. In this direction, see, for instance,
[
17
]
and
[
19
]
, dedicated to the
thin
Bernoulli functional and a one-phase problem driven by the
p
-Laplace operator respectively.

Free boundary problems arising from the minimization of Bernoulli-type functionals may be formulated even in the noncommutative setting of Carnot groups. See, for instance,
[
20
]
for the case of the Heisenberg group. Carnot groups are an intensively-studied noncommutative structure playing a relevant role in many applications. For instance, they represent the tangent model of a general sub-Riemannian manifold and are the natural framework to describe some systems with nonholonomic constraints. On Carnot groups, there exists a comprehensive literature involving geometric measure theory
[
43
,
28
,
29
]
, subelliptic partial differential equations
[
9
,
24
,
25
]
, and differential geometry
[
35
,
36
,
11
]
. Nevertheless, very little is known on Bernoulli free boundary problems in this setting. For the one-phase case, in
[
23
]
, only in the Heisenberg group, the authors proved the existence of minimizers, which turn out to be intrinsic harmonic away from the free boundary and present linear growth close to the free boundary. Besides, they proved some density estimates. Furthermore, we recall that, concerning the two-phase case, classical tools seem to fail in this setting. For instance, as recently shown in
[
21
,
22
]
, an intrinsic Alt-Caffarelli-Friedman monotonicity formula, written as the natural counterpart to the classical Euclidean one, does not hold. In addition, the viscosity approach occurs some difficulties due to the characteristic points. For instance, this is the case of the comparison principle, see
[
20
]
*Section 8. Moreover, we point out that, for these functionals, the regularity results do not follow from the classical theory in the calculus of variations, since the integrand of the functional is discontinuous (in the classical case, the integrand is required to be, for example, Lipschitz continuous, see e.g.,
[
31
]
*condition (8.48) in the Euclidean context or, for example, under stronger regularity assumption in
[
12
,
45
]
, in Carnot groups).

The setting in which we are working on may be summarized as follows. Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of step two. Assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
is a measurable domain. We define the following energy functional

(1.2)

J(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\Big(|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}+\chi_{\{u>0\}}(x)\Big)\,dx,

where
u
belongs to the
horizontal Sobolev space

HW^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
\Omega
,
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u
is the so-called
horizontal gradient
of
u
and
dx
denotes the Haar measure of the group. We refer to Section
2
for the main notation and definitions.

In the present paper, we focus on some regularity properties of almost minimizers for
J
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\kappa\geqslant 0
and
\beta>0
, and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be an open set. We say that
u\in HW^{1,2}(\Omega)
is an almost minimizer of
J
in
\Omega
, with constant
\kappa
and exponent
\beta
, if
u\geqslant 0

\mathcal{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere in
\Omega
and

(1.3)

J(u,B_{\varrho}(x))\leqslant(1+\kappa\varrho^{\beta})J(v,B_{\varrho}(x)),

for every metric ball
B_{\varrho}(x)
, of radius
\varrho\leqslant 0
and center
x\in\mathbb{G}
, such that
\overline{B_{\varrho}(x)}\subset\Omega
and for every
v\in HW^{1,2}(B_{\varrho}(x))
such that
u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{\varrho}(x))
.

Our main result is the following one.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u
be an almost minimizer for
J
in
B_{1}
with constant
\kappa
and exponent
\beta
. Then,

\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leqslant C\Big(\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}+1\Big)

where
C>0
is a constant only depending on the homogeneous dimension
Q
,
\kappa,
and
\beta
.

In addition,
u
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous in a neighborhood of
\left\{u=0\right\}
, namely if
u(0)=0,
then

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right|\leqslant C\quad\mbox{in }B_{r_{0}},

for some
C>0
, only depending on
Q
,
\kappa
and
\beta
, and
r_{0}\in(0,1)
, depending on
Q
,
\kappa
,
\beta
and
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}
.

In particular, the main contribution of our paper concerns the Lipschitz intrinsic regularity of almost minimizers. We remark that our result is also relevant for the case of minimizers (when
\kappa=0
), since it is new also in this direction. Our approach is a generalization of the method introduced in
[
18
]
. Nevertheless, some tools in
[
18
]
, mainly based on comparison arguments with harmonic replacements, cannot be applied on Carnot groups. Indeed, if the step of the group is strictly grater then one, the vector fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
, generating the first layer of the Lie algebra associated to
\mathbb{G}
, have no trivial commutators, i.e.

\left[X_{i},X_{j}\right]\not=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad X_{i}X_{j}\not=X_{j}X_{i},\quad\text{for every }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m_{1}\},\text{ if }i\not=j.

Consequently, denoting by
\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X^{2}_{j}
the positive sub-Laplacian on
\mathbb{G}
, it results

(1.4)

X_{i}\mathcal{L}\not=\mathcal{L}X_{i}

for all
i=1,\ldots,m_{1}
. The lack of commutativity, encoded in (
1.4
), implies that functions
X_{i}u
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{1}\}
, are not
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic even if
u
is. Therefore, the mean value characterization of
|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u|^{2}
on which the argument proposed in
[
18
]
is based, is no longer applicable in our case. To face this aspect, we apply the regularity estimates for subelliptic equations, proved in
[
13
]
, see also
[
44
]
in the case of the Heisenberg group. In addition, following the original idea of Anzellotti in
[
5
]
, we exploit well-known results concerning the Morrey-Campanato-type estimates, developed in
[
40
]
in homogeneous spaces. Moreover, we employ some classical tools coming from geometric analysis in Carnot groups, such as Poincaré and Sobolev-Poincaré inequalities, see, for instance,
[
33
,
26
,
27
,
37
,
38
]
. Finally, it is worth recalling that our Lipschitz intrinsic regularity result only implies Hölder continuous regularity from a Euclidean point of view, since the Carnot-Charathéodory distance is not equivalent to the Euclidean one, see, e.g.,
[
9
]
*Proposition 5.15.1.

The classical motivations to study Bernoulli free boundary problems come from flows with jets and cavities, see, for instance,
[
10
]
*Section 1.1, heat flows
[
1
]
, electrochemical machining
[
34
]
, combustion theory
[
7
]
, electrical impedance tomography
[
6
]
, and phase transitions, see, e.g.,
[
46
,
47
,
50
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall basic notions concerning Carnot groups and some useful tools. Next, in Section
3
, we deal with a dichotomy property, a key step to prove our main result. Finally, Section
4
contains the proof of Theorem
1.2
. We remind, even if it is not explicitly stated, that all the constants appearing in the sequel may change from line to line when they are universal, i.e. when they depend only on the group
\mathbb{G}
.

2.
Notation and preliminary results

2.1.
Carnot Groups

A connected and simply connected Lie group
(\mathbb{G},\circ)
(in general non-abelian) is said to be a
Carnot group of step
k
if its Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
admits a
k
-step stratification
, i.e. there exist linear subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
such that

(2.1)

{\mathfrak{g}}=V_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{k},\quad[V_{1},V_{i}]=V_{i+1},\quad V_{k}\neq\{0\},\quad V_{i}=\{0\}{\textrm{ if }}i>k,

where
[V_{1},V_{i}]
is the subspace of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by the commutators
[X,Y]
with
X\in V_{1}
and
Y\in V_{i}
. The first layer
V_{1}
, the so-called
horizontal layer
, plays a key role in the theory since it generates all the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
by commutation. We point out that a stratified Lie algebra can admit more than one stratification. However, the stratification turns out to be unique up to isomorphisms, thus, the related Carnot group structure is essentially unique (see
[
36
]
*Proposition 1.17). Note that when
k=1
the group
\mathbb{G}
is abelian, so we return to the Euclidean situation.

Setting
m_{i}=\dim(V_{i})
and
h_{i}=m_{1}+\dots+m_{i}
, with
h_{0}=0
, for
i=1,\dots,k
(so that
h_{k}=n=\dim\mathfrak{g}=\dim\mathbb{G}
), we choose a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
adapted to the stratification, that is such that

e_{h_{j-1}+1},\ldots,e_{h_{j}}\;\text{is a basis of}\;V_{j}\;\text{for each}\;j=1,\ldots,k.

Let
X=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be the family of left-invariant vector fields such that
X_{i}(e)=e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, where
e
is the identity of
(\mathbb{G},\circ)
. Thanks to (
2.1
), the subfamily
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m_{1}}\}
generates by commutations all the other vector fields, we will refer to
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
as
generating vector fields
of
(\mathbb{G},\circ)
.

The map
X\mapsto X(e)
, associating a left-invariant vector field
X
to its value in
e
, defines an isomorphism from
\mathfrak{g}
to
T\mathbb{G}_{e}
(in turn identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). We systematically use these identifications. Furthermore, by the assumption that
\mathbb{G}
is simply connected, the exponential map is a global real-analytic diffeomorphism from
\mathfrak{g}
onto
\mathbb{G}
(see, e.g.,
[
51
,
14
]
), so each
x\in\mathbb{G}
can be written in a unique way as
x=\exp(x_{1}X_{1}+\dots+x_{n}X_{n})
. Using these
exponential coordinates
, we identify
x
with the
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{G}
with
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ast)
where the explicit expression of the group operation
\ast
is determined by the Campbell-Hausdorff formula (see, for example,
[
9
,
25
]
). In these coordinates,
e=(0,\dots,0)
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{-1}=(-x_{1},\dots,-x_{n})
, and the adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
of
X_{j}
,
X_{j}^{*}
, for
j=1,\dots,n
, turns out to be
-X_{j}
(see, for instance,
[
29
]
*Proposition 2.2). Moreover, if
x\in\mathbb{G}
and
i=1,\ldots,k
, we set
x^{(i)}:=(x_{h_{i-1}+1},\ldots,x_{h_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
, so that we can also identify
x
with
[x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(k)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Two important families of automorphism of
\mathbb{G}
are the so-called
intrinsic translations
and
dilations
. For any
x\in\mathbb{G}
, the
(left) translation

\tau_{x}:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
is defined as

z\mapsto\tau_{x}z:=x\circ z.

For any
\lambda>0
we call
dilation
, associated with
\lambda
the linear automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
such that

\delta_{\lambda}(v)=\lambda^{i}v,\quad\text{ if }v\in V_{i},\ \text{ for }i=1,\ldots,k.

Through the exponential map, one can transfer the notion of dilation on the group
\mathbb{G}
. We denote again by
\delta_{\lambda}
the map

\exp\circ\;\delta_{\lambda}\circ\exp^{-1}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}.

Using the identification given by exponential coordinates, one can read the map
\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
as

(2.2)

\delta_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(\lambda^{\alpha_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\alpha_{n}}x_{n}),

where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
is called
homogeneity of the variable

x_{i}
in
\mathbb{G}
(see
[
25
]
*Chapter 1) and is defined as

\alpha_{j}=i\quad\text{whenever}\;h_{i-1}+1\leqslant j\leqslant h_{i},

hence
1=\alpha_{1}=\ldots=\alpha_{m}<\alpha_{m+1}=2\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\alpha_{n}=k.

From Definition (
2.2
), one can easily verify the following properties of intrinsic dilations.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
\lambda,\mu>0
, one has:

(1)

\delta_{1}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{G}}
;

(2)

\delta^{-1}_{\lambda}=\delta_{\lambda^{-1}}
;

(3)

\delta_{\lambda}\circ\delta_{\mu}=\delta_{\lambda\mu}
;

(4)

for every
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}
, it holds
\delta_{\lambda}(p)\cdot\delta_{\lambda}(p^{\prime})=\delta_{\lambda}(p\cdot p^{\prime})
.

By left translation, the horizontal layer determines a subbundle of the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{G}
over
\mathbb{G}
. This subbundle, spanned by the family of vector fields
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m_{1}})
, plays a crucial role in the theory and is called the
horizontal bundle

H\mathbb{G}
. The fibers of
H\mathbb{G}
are

H\mathbb{G}_{x}=\mbox{span }\{X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{m_{1}}(x)\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{G}.

A sub-Riemannian structure is induced on
\mathbb{G}
by endowing each fiber of
H\mathbb{G}
with a scalar product
\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle_{x}
, that makes the basis
X_{1}(x),\dots,X_{m_{1}}(x)
orthonormal, and the related norm
|\cdot|_{x}
. These are defined as follows: if
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}v_{i}X_{i}(x)=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m_{1}})
and
w=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}w_{i}X_{i}(x)=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m_{1}})
are in
H\mathbb{G}_{x}
, then
\langle{v},{w}\rangle_{x}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}v_{j}w_{j}
and
|v|_{x}^{2}:=\langle{v},{v}\rangle_{x}
.

The sections of
H\mathbb{G}
are called
horizontal sections
, and for any
x\in\mathbb{G}
, a vector of
H\mathbb{G}_{x}
is a
horizontal vector
, while a vector in
T\mathbb{G}_{x}
that is not horizontal is called a vertical vector. Each horizontal section is identified by its canonical coordinates with respect to this moving frame
X_{1},\dots,X_{m_{1}}
. In this way, a horizontal section
\varphi
is represented with a function
\varphi:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
. To simplify the notation, when dealing with two horizontal sections
\phi
and
\psi
, we drop the index
x
in the scalar product and norm.

We collect some properties of the group operation and canonical vector fields, see
[
9
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The group operation has the form

x\circ y=x+y+\mathcal{Q}(x,y),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{G},

where
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathcal{Q}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Q}_{n}):\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}

and every
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
\alpha_{i}
with respect to the intrinsic dilations of
\mathbb{G}
, that is,

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\delta_{\lambda}(x),\delta_{\lambda}(y))=\lambda^{\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }\lambda>0.

Moreover, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{G}
, we have :

(i)

\mathcal{Q}
is antisymmetric, namely,

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,y)=-\mathcal{Q}_{i}(-y,-x),\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n;

(ii)

\mathcal{Q}_{1}(x,y)=\ldots=\mathcal{Q}_{m_{1}}(x,y)=0,

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,0)=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(0,y)=0\text{ and }\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,x)=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,-x)=0,\quad\text{ for }m_{1}<i\leqslant n,

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,y)=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x_{1},,x_{{h_{j}}-1},y_{1},\ldots,y_{{h_{j}}-1}),\quad\text{ if }1<j\leqslant k\text{ and }i\leqslant h_{j};

(iii)

\mathcal{Q}_{i}(x,y)=\sum_{k,h}{\mathcal{R}_{h,k}^{i}(x,y)(x_{k}y_{h}-x_{h}y_{k})},

where the functions
\mathcal{R}_{h,k}^{i}
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{h}
with respect to the intrinsic dilations and the sum is extended to all
h
and
k
such that
\alpha_{h}+\alpha_{k}\leqslant\alpha_{i}
.

The following result is contained in
[
29
]
*Proposition 2.2.

Proposition 2.3
.

The vector fields
X_{j}
have polynomial coefficients and are of the form

X_{j}(x)=\partial_{j}+\sum_{i>h_{l}}^{n}{q_{i,j}(x)\partial_{i}},\quad\>j=1,\ldots,n\>\>\text{ and }\>\>j\leqslant h_{l},

where
q_{i,j}(x)=\frac{\partial\mathcal{Q}_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(x,y)\rvert_{y=0}
and the
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
’s are those ones defined in Proposition
2.2
for
h_{l-1}<j\leqslant h_{l}
and
1\leqslant l\leqslant k
. So, if
h_{l-1}<j\leqslant h_{l}
, then
q_{i,j}(x)=q_{i,j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{h_{l-1}})
and
q_{i,j}(0)=0
.

2.2.
Intrinsic distance and gauge pseudo-distance

An absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is called
sub-unitary
with respect to
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
if it is a
horizontal curve
, that is, if there exist real measurable functions
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m_{1}}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\dot{\gamma}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}{c_{j}(s)X_{j}(\gamma(s))}\quad\text{for $\mathcal{L}^{1}$-a.e. }s\in[0,T],

with
\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}{c_{j}(s)^{2}}\leqslant 1
for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
s\in[0,T]
.

Definition 2.4

(Carnot-Carathéodory distance)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group. For
p,q\in\mathbb{G}
, we define their
Carnot-Carathéodory distance

d_{c}(p,q)
as

d_{c}(p,q):=\mathrm{inf}\{T>0:\text{ there exists a sub-unitary curve }\gamma\text{ with }\>\gamma(0)=p,\>\gamma(T)=q\}.

We remark that this distance is well defined: the set of sub-unitary curves connecting
p
and
q
is nonempty by Chow’s theorem
[
9
]
*Theorem 19.1.3, since by (
2.1
), the rank of the Lie algebra generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
is
n
. The Carnot-Carathéodory distance
d_{c}
induces on
\mathbb{G}
the same topology as the standard Euclidean one. We shall denote by
B_{r}(p)
the open ball, centered in
p
and of radius
r>0
, associated with
d_{c}
. For the sake of simplicity, if
p=e
, we will use the notation
B_{r}(e)=B_{r}
.

The distance
d_{c}
is equivalent to a more explicit pseudo-distance, called the
gauge pseudo-distance
, defined as follows. Let
||\cdot||
denote the Euclidean distance to the origin in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. For
u=u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}
, with
u_{i}\in V_{i}
, one defines

(2.3)

|u|_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}||u_{i}||^{2k!/i}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k!}}.

The
nonisotropic gauge
in
\mathbb{G}
is

|p|_{\mathbb{G}}:=|\exp^{-1}p|_{\mathfrak{g}},\quad\quad\ \ \ \ \ p\in\mathbb{G},

see
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
. Since the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{G}
is a
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphism (actually, analytic), the map
p\to|p|_{\mathbb{G}}
is
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G}\setminus\{e\})
. Notice that, from (
2.3
) and (
2.2
), we have, for any
\lambda>0
and
p\in\mathbb{G}
,

(2.4)

|\delta_{\lambda}(p)|_{\mathbb{G}}=\lambda|p|_{\mathbb{G}}.

The
gauge pseudo-distance
in
\mathbb{G}
is defined by

(2.5)

d(p,q):=|p^{-1}\circ q|_{\mathbb{G}},\quad\quad\ \ \ \ \ p,q\in\mathbb{G}.

The function
d
has all the properties of a distance, except the triangle inequality, which is satisfied with a universal constant, usually different from one, on the right-hand side, see
[
25
,
9
]
. Since the dilations are group automorphisms, from (
2.5
) and (
2.4
), it follows that
d
is homogeneous of degree one with respect to the group dilations, that is, for any
\lambda>0
and all
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}

d(\delta_{\lambda}(p),\delta_{\lambda}(p^{\prime}))=\lambda d(p,p^{\prime}).

It is well known, see, for instance, Proposition 5.1.4 in
[
9
]
, that there exists a constant
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
, only depending on the group
\mathbb{G}
, such that, for
p\in\mathbb{G}
,

(2.6)

\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}|p|_{\mathbb{G}}\leqslant d_{c}(e,p)\leqslant\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}|p|_{\mathbb{G}}.

The integer

(2.7)

Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{i\text{dim}(V_{i})}

is the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{G}
. It is the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbb{G}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the distance
d_{c}
, see
[
41
]
.

The
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}
is the Haar measure of the group
\mathbb{G}
, i.e., for every
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{G}
and
x\in\mathbb{G}
, it results
\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(x\circ E\right)=\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(E\right)
. Moreover, if
\lambda>0
, then
\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(\delta_{\lambda}\left(E\right)\right)=\lambda^{Q}\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(E\right)
, where
Q
is the homogeneous dimension, see (
2.7
). In particular, for any
r>0
and
p\in\mathbb{G}
, it holds

\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(B_{r}(p)\right)=r^{Q}\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(B_{1}(p)\right)=r^{Q}\mathcal{L}^{n}\left(B_{1}\right).

Let us point out that all the spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{G})
we will deal with are defined with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}
. If
A\subset\mathbb{G}
is
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-measurable, we write
|A|=\mathcal{L}^{n}(A)
. Hereafter, unless differently specified, all considered domains are at least
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-measurable.

A map
L:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\mathbb{G}
-linear
if it is a group homeomorphism from
(\mathbb{G},\circ)
to
(\mathbb{R},+)
and is homogeneous of degree 1 with respect to the intrinsic dilations of
\mathbb{G}
, that is,
L(\delta_{\lambda}(x))=\lambda L(x)
for
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbb{G}
. Similarly, we say that a map
\ell:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathbb{G}
-affine if there exists a
\mathbb{G}
-linear map
L
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\ell(x)=L(x)+c
for every
x\in\mathbb{G}
. Given a basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, all
\mathbb{G}
-linear maps are represented as follows.

Proposition 2.5
.

A map
L:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathbb{G}
-linear if and only if there is
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
such that, if
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{G}
, then

L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}a_{i}x_{i}.

Moreover, if
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{G}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{G}
are given, we set

(2.8)

\pi_{x_{0}}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}x_{j}X_{j}(x_{0}).

2.3.
Folland-Stein and horizontal Sobolev classes

Fixed
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
, the action of vector fields
X_{j}
, with
j=1,\ldots,m_{1}
, on a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is specified by the Lie derivative. We say that a function
f
is differentiable along the direction
X_{j}
at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{G}
, when
j\in\{1,\ldots,m_{1}\}
, if the map
\lambda\mapsto f(\tau_{x_{0}}(\delta_{\lambda}(e_{j})))
is differentiable at
\lambda=0
, where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
. In this case, we will write

X_{j}f(x_{0})=\left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}f(\tau_{x_{0}}(\delta_{\lambda}(e_{j})))\right|_{\lambda=0}.

If, instead,
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
,
X_{j}f
exists in a weak sense if

\int_{\Omega}{fX_{j}\varphi}\>d\mathcal{L}^{n}=-\int_{\Omega}{\varphi X_{j}f}\>d\mathcal{L}^{n}

for each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Let us fix a basis
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
of the horizontal layer. For any function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
for which the partial derivatives
X_{j}f
,
j=1,\ldots,m_{1}
, exist, we define the horizontal gradient of
f
, denoted by
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f
, as the horizontal section

(2.9)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}(X_{i}f)X_{i},

whose coordinates are
(X_{1}f,\ldots,X_{m_{1}}f)
. Moreover, if
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m_{1}})
is a horizontal section such that
X_{j}\phi_{j}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{G})
for
j=1,\dots,m_{1}
, we define
\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}\phi
as the real-valued function

\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}\phi:=-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X_{j}^{*}\phi_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X_{j}\phi_{j}.

The positive
sub-Laplacian
operator on
\mathbb{G}
is the second-order differential operator given by

\mathcal{L}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X^{*}_{j}X_{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}X_{j}^{2}.

It is easy to see that

(2.10)

\mathcal{L}u=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}(\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u).

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is left-invariant with respect to group translations and homogeneous of degree two with respect to group dilations, i.e., for any
x\in\mathbb{G}
and
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathcal{L}(u\circ\tau_{x})=(\mathcal{L}u)\circ\tau_{x},\qquad\mathcal{L}(\delta_{\lambda}(u))=\lambda^{2}\delta_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L}u).

Furthermore, by the assumption (
2.1
), the system
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}\}
satisfies the finite rank condition

\text{rank Lie}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}]=n,

therefore by Hörmander’s theorem
[
32
]
the operator
\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic. However, when the step
k
of
\mathbb{G}
is greater than one, the operator
\mathcal{L}
fails to be elliptic at every point of
\mathbb{G}
.

The Hölder classes
C^{k,\alpha}
had been introduced by Folland and Stein, see
[
24
,
25
]
. The functions in these classes are Hölder continuous with respect to the metric
d_{c}
.

Definition 2.6

(Folland-Stein classes)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be an open set,
0<\alpha\leqslant 1
and
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that

u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
if there exists a constant
M>0
such that

|u(x)-u(y)|\leqslant M\,d_{c}(x,y)^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for every }x,y\in\Omega.

Defining the Hölder seminorm of
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
as

[u]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)}:=\underset{\underset{x\neq y}{x,y\in\Omega}}{\sup}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{d_{c}(x,y)^{\alpha}},

the space
C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
is a Banach space with the norm

\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)}:=\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+[u]_{C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega).}

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
u\in C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)
if
X_{i}u\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(\Omega)
for every
i=1,\ldots,m_{1}
.

Note that
C^{0,1}(\Omega)
coincides with the class of Lipschitz continuous functions with respect to the metric
d_{c}
on
\Omega
. Also, we denote by
C^{k,\lambda}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})
the space of all the horizontal sections
\phi:\Omega\to H\mathbb{G}
,
\phi:=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m_{1}})
, such that
\phi_{j}\in C^{k,\lambda}(\Omega)
for
j=1,\ldots,m_{1}
.

Definition 2.7

(Horizontal Sobolev spaces)
.

Given an open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
,
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
1\leqslant p<\infty
, the horizontal Sobolev spaces are defined as

HW^{1,p}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega):\ X_{j}u\in L^{p}(\Omega)\ \text{for all }\,j=1,\ldots,m_{1}\right\},

which is a Banach space with the norm

\|u\|_{HW^{1,p}(\Omega)}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})}.

The subspace
HW^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
of
HW^{1,p}(\Omega)
is defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{HW^{1,p}(\Omega)}
.

3.
Dichotomy results

The first step for reaching Theorem
1.2
is to prove a
dichotomy
result. Roughly speaking, two situations can occur: either the average of the energy of an almost minimizer decreases in a smaller ball, or the distance between its horizontal gradient and a suitable constant horizontal section becomes as small as we wish (that is,
\mathbb{G}
-linear functions are the ”only ones for which the average does not improve in small balls”). Before passing to the proof of this result, let us state the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

Let be
\ell:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
a
\mathbb{G}
-affine map. By Proposition
2.5
, there exist a suitable
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\ell(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{i}x_{i}+c\qquad\text{for any }x\in\mathbb{G}.

Recalling (
2.9
), by Proposition
2.3
, we directly get

(3.1)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\ell(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{j}X_{j}(x)=:\mathbf{q}(x),\qquad\text{for any }x\in\mathbb{G}.

Thus, the horizontal gradient of a
\mathbb{G}
-affine map
\ell
is a horizontal section
\mathbf{q}
, which has constant components with respect to the moving frame given by generating vector fields. From now on, we call such a horizontal section a
constant
horizontal section. Note, however, that a constant horizontal section still depends on the point
x\in\mathbb{G}
due to the moving frame. We also observe that all
\mathbb{G}
-affine functions
\ell
as before are
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{G}
, in the sense that
\mathcal{L}\ell=0
in
\mathbb{G}
. Indeed, by (
2.10
) and (
3.1
), it immediately holds
\mathcal{L}\ell=0
in
\mathbb{G}
. We refer to
[
9
]
for further characterizations of
\mathbb{G}
-harmonicity within Carnot groups.

Our first result is the following.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
u\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
be such that

(3.2)

J(u,B_{1})\leqslant(1+\sigma)J(v,B_{1})

for all
v\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
such that
u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
.
Denoting by

(3.3)

a:=\left(\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2},

there exists
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
there exist
\eta\in(0,1)
,
M\geqslant 1
, and
\sigma_{0}\in(0,1)
, depending on
\varepsilon
and
Q
,
such that, if
\sigma\in[0,\sigma_{0}]
and
a\geqslant M
, then the following dichotomy holds. Either

(3.4)

\left(\fint_{B_{\eta}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\frac{a}{2},

or

(3.5)

\left(\fint_{B_{\eta}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\varepsilon a,

where
\mathbf{q}:\mathbb{G}\to H\mathbb{G}
is a constant horizontal section, see (
3.1
), with

(3.6)

\frac{a}{4}<|\mathbf{q}|\leqslant C_{0}a,

for some universal constant
C_{0}>0
.

Proof.

We split the proof into several steps.

Step 1: pointwise estimates.

Let
v:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement of
u
in
B_{1}
, that is, the unique solution of Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}v=0\quad\textrm{in\ }B_{1},\\
u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1}),\end{cases}

or, equivalently, the minimizer of Dirichlet energy among all competitors
w\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
such that
u-w\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
, i.e.,

(3.7)

\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx=\min_{u-w\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})}\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w(x)\right|^{2}\,dx.

By Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
, only depending on
Q
, such that

\sup_{B_{1/2}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right|\leqslant C_{0}\left(\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

Then, recalling (
3.3
), by the minimality (
3.7
) of
v
for the Dirichlet energy, we conclude

(3.8)

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right|\leqslant C_{0}a\quad\text{in }B_{1/2}.

Step 2: oscillation estimates.

By Theorem 1.3 in
[
13
]
, we have the following oscillation estimates: for all
\eta\in(0,1/2]
, it holds

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{1}}\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\eta}}X_{i}v\leqslant C\bigg(\frac{\eta}{1/2}\bigg)^{\alpha}\left(\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{1/2},

for some
\alpha=\alpha(Q)\in(0,1]
and universal constant
C>0
.
Therefore, we have

(3.9)

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{1}}|X_{i}v(x)-X_{i}v(0)|\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{1}}\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\eta}}X_{i}v\leqslant C\bigg(\frac{\eta}{1/2}\bigg)^{\alpha}\left(\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{for }x\in B_{\eta}.

Thus, for any
i=1,\dots,m_{1}
, denoting by
q_{i}=X_{i}v(0)
and
\mathbf{q}:\mathbb{G}\to HG
the constant horizontal section defined as in (
3.1
), it results

(3.10)

|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v-\mathbf{q}|\leqslant C_{1}\bigg(\frac{\eta}{1/2}\bigg)^{\alpha}\left(\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{in }B_{\eta},

for some universal constant
C_{1}>0
. This gives that, for all
\eta\in(0,1/2]
,

(3.11)

\begin{split}\fint_{B_{\eta}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\;&\fint_{B_{\eta}}\bigg(C^{2}_{1}\bigg(\frac{\eta}{1/2}\bigg)^{2\alpha}\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)|^{2}\,dy\,\bigg)\,dx\\
=\;&C_{2}\,\eta^{2\alpha}\fint_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)|^{2}dy\\
\leqslant\;&C_{2}\,\eta^{2\alpha}\,a^{2},\end{split}

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C_{2}>0
universal.

Step 3: proximity to the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement.

We know that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant J(u,B_{1})+\int_{B_{1}}(|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v|^{2}-2\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right\rangle)\,dx

\displaystyle=J(u,B_{1})-\int_{B_{1}}(|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v|^{2}+2\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u-v),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right\rangle)\,dx.

On the other hand, since
X^{*}_{j}=-X_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m_{1}
and
u-v\in HW_{0}^{1,2}(B_{1})
, it occurs

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u-v),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\,\int_{B_{1}}X_{j}(u-v)X_{j}v\,dx

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\,\int_{B_{1}}(u-v)X^{2}_{j}v\,dx=\int_{B_{1}}(u-v)\mathcal{L}v\,dx=0,

which together with (
3.12
) gives

(3.14)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}\,dx\leqslant J(u,B_{1})-\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}dx.

Then, using the hypothesis (
3.2
) and the minimality of
v
in (
3.7
), for
\sigma>0
to be made precise later, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}\big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\big)\,dx&\leqslant(1+\sigma)J(v,B_{1})-\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\\
&\leqslant C\left(\sigma\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+1\right)\\
&\leqslant C\left(\sigma\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+1\right)\end{split}

for some suitable positive constant
C
only depending on
Q
. Consequently, taking the average over
B_{1}
, we achieve

\fint_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C(\sigma a^{2}+1).

Using this and (
3.11
), we get

(3.15)

\begin{split}\fint_{B_{\eta}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx&\leqslant 2\fint_{B_{\eta}}\big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\big)\,dx\\
&\leqslant 2\left(\frac{\left|B_{1}\right|}{\left|B_{\eta}\right|}\fint_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+C_{2}\,\eta^{2\alpha}\,a^{2}\right)\\
&\leqslant 2\Big(C\eta^{-Q}(\sigma a^{2}+1)+C_{2}a^{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big),\\
&\leqslant 2C\eta^{-Q}\sigma a^{2}+2C\eta^{-Q}+2C_{2}a^{2}\eta^{2\alpha},\end{split}

and hence

(3.16)

\fint_{B_{\eta}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant 4C\eta^{-Q}\sigma a^{2}+4C\eta^{-Q}+4C_{2}a^{2}\eta^{2\alpha}+2\left|\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}.

Step 4: perturbative estimates.
Now, given
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1/4]
, we claim that
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists
\eta
small enough (depending on
\varepsilon
)
such that if
\sigma
is sufficiently small and
a
sufficiently large (depending on
\eta
,
and thus on
\varepsilon
), then

(3.17)

4C\eta^{-Q}\sigma a^{2}+4C\eta^{-Q}+4C_{2}a^{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\leqslant 2\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}\leqslant\frac{a^{2}}{8}.

Let
\eta>0
sufficiently small such that
\varepsilon^{2}-2C\eta>2C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}
. So, defining

M:=\left(\frac{2C\eta^{-Q}}{\varepsilon^{2}-2C\eta-2C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}}\right)^{1/2},

we can suppose
M\geqslant 1
, by taking
\eta
small enough.
Therefore, for every
a\geqslant M
and
0\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\eta^{Q+1}\allowbreak=:\sigma_{0}
, we have, exploiting the expression of
M
,

\displaystyle 4C\eta^{-Q}\sigma a^{2}+4C\eta^{-Q}+4C_{2}a^{2}\eta^{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle a^{2}\Big(4C\eta+4C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big)+4C\eta^{-Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\Big(4C\eta+4C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big)+2\,M^{2}\Big(\varepsilon^{2}-2C\eta-2C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle a^{2}\Big(4C\eta+4C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big)+2\,a^{2}\Big(\varepsilon^{2}-2C\eta-2C_{2}\eta^{2\alpha}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

which proves (
3.17
), choosing
\varepsilon
small enough. Note that, by Step 2,
\eta\leqslant 1/2
, thus
\sigma_{0}<1
.

Step 5: conclusion of the proof.
In order to complete the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we distinguish two cases. First, we suppose that

\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\leqslant\frac{a}{4}.

Then, by (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{\eta}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\frac{a^{2}}{8}+2\frac{a^{2}}{16}=\frac{a^{2}}{4},

from which we immediately deduce the first alternative (
3.4
).

Otherwise, we assume, recalling (
3.8
),

\frac{a}{4}<\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\leqslant C_{0}\,a,

and therefore, by (
3.15
) and (
3.17
), the second alternative (
3.5
) directly follows.
∎

Now, we show that the alternative (
3.5
) in Proposition
3.2
can be ”improved” when
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
are sufficiently small. This result is the counterpart of Lemma 2.3 in
[
18
]
in the more general setting of Carnot groups of step two.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
be such that
u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
B_{1}
and

(3.18)

J(u,B_{1})\leqslant(1+\sigma)J(v,B_{1})

for all
v\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
such that
u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
.

Let

a:=\left(\fint_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}

and suppose that

(3.19)

a_{1}\geqslant a\geqslant a_{0}>0,

for some suitable
a_{1}\geqslant a_{0}
.
Assume also

(3.20)

\left(\fint_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\varepsilon a,

for some constant horizontal section
\mathbf{q}:\mathbb{G}\to H\mathbb{G}
such that

(3.21)

\frac{a}{8}<|\mathbf{q}|\leqslant 2C_{0}a,

where
C_{0}>0
is the universal constant in Proposition
3.2
.

There exists
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1]
, only depending on
Q
, such that, given
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, there exist
\rho=\rho(\alpha,Q)>0
,
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(Q,\alpha,a_{0},a_{1})>0
, and
c_{0}=c_{0}(Q,\alpha,a_{0},a_{1})>0,
such that if

\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma\leqslant c_{0}\varepsilon^{2},

then

(3.22)

\left(\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\rho^{\alpha}\varepsilon a,

where
\mathbf{\widetilde{q}}:\mathbb{G}\to H\mathbb{G}
is a constant horizontal section, see (
3.1
), for some suitable
\widetilde{q}=(\widetilde{q}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{q}_{m_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
such that

(3.23)

\left|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{\widetilde{q}}\right|\leqslant\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a.

for some universal constant
\widetilde{C}>0
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1: energy estimates for the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement and comparison of energies.
Let us set
\tau:=\frac{1}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}}<1
, where
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}\geqslant 1
is the structural constant given by (
2.6
) only depending on
\mathbb{G}
. Let
\bar{v}
denote the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement of
u
in
B_{\tau}
and
v
be defined as

(3.24)

v:=\begin{cases}\bar{v}&\mbox{in }B_{\tau},\\
u&\mbox{in }B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau}.\end{cases}

We first note that
v\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
and
u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1})
, thus, by hypothesis (
3.18
), we have

J(u,B_{1})\leqslant(1+\sigma)J(v,B_{1}),

which leads to

\displaystyle J(u,B_{\tau})+J(u,B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau})=\;

\displaystyle J(u,B_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\;

\displaystyle(1+\sigma)\,J(v,B_{1})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle J(v,B_{1})+\sigma J(v,B_{1})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle J(v,B_{\tau})+J(v,B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau})+\sigma J(v,B_{1})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle J(v,B_{\tau})+J(u,B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau})+\sigma J(v,B_{1}),

namely,

J(u,B_{\tau})\leqslant J(v,B_{\tau})+\sigma J(v,B_{1}).

By definition of
J
in (
1.2
), this reads as

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+\left|\left\{u>0\right\}\cap B_{\tau}\right|\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+\left|\left\{u>0\right\}\cap B_{\tau}\right|+\sigma J(v,B_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+\left|B_{\tau}\right|+\sigma J(v,B_{1}),

which, since
u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
B_{1}
by assumption, yields

(3.25)

\int_{B_{\tau}}\big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}-\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\big)\,dx\leqslant\left|\left\{u=0\right\}\cap B_{\tau}\right|+\sigma J(v,B_{1}).

Moreover, by definition of
v
in (
3.24
), we have

(3.26)

\begin{split}J(v,B_{1})=\;&\int_{B_{1}}\Big(|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}+\chi_{\{v>0\}}(x)\Big)\,dx\\
\leqslant\;&\int_{B_{\tau}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)|^{2}\,dx+|B_{1}|\\
=\;&\int_{B_{\tau}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\bar{v}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\tau}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}\,dx+|B_{1}|\\
\leqslant\;&\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right|^{2}\,dx+\left|B_{1}\right|\leqslant\;\left|B_{1}\right|(a^{2}+1),\end{split}

where in the second inequality we use the fact that
\bar{v}
is the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement of
u
in
B_{\tau}
and therefore
\bar{v}
minimizes the Dirichlet energy on
B_{\tau}
.

Finally, recalling (
3.13
), by (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) we obtain that

(3.27)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx=\;

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}-2\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right\rangle+\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}-2\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u-v),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\right\rangle-\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\tau}}\left(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}-\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\;

\displaystyle\left|\left\{u=0\right\}\cap B_{\tau}\right|+\sigma J(v,B_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\;

\displaystyle\left|\left\{u=0\right\}\cap B_{\tau}\right|+|B_{1}|\sigma(a^{2}+1).

Step 2: measure estimates for the zero level set.
Now, we claim that

(3.28)

\left|B_{\tau}\cap\left\{u=0\right\}\right|\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta},

for some
C_{1}>0
and
\delta>0
.

Step 2.1: comparison with a
\mathbb{G}
-affine function.
To prove (
3.28
), we consider a function
\ell:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(3.29)

\ell(x):=b+\left\langle\mathbf{q}(x),\pi_{x}(x)\right\rangle=b+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{j}x_{j},\qquad b:=\fint_{B_{1}}u(x)\,dx.

We remark that

\displaystyle(u-\ell)_{B_{1}}:=\fint_{B_{1}}\big(u(x)-\ell(x)\big)\,dx=b-\left(b+\fint_{B_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{j}x_{j}\,dx\right)=0,

where the last equality is a consequence of the symmetry with respect to the identity element of the Carnot-Carathéodory ball
B_{1}
. Then, by the Poincaré inequality (see, e.g.,
[
33
]
) we have

\left\|u-\ell-(u-\ell)_{B_{1}}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}=\left\|u-\ell\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\leqslant C\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(u-\ell)\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})},

for some
C>0
universal.

Since by Proposition
2.3
,
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\ell=\mathbf{q}
, this, together with hypothesis (
3.20
), leads to

(3.30)

\fint_{B_{1}}\left|u(x)-\ell(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}.

Finally, we point out that, since by assumption
u\geqslant 0
, it holds
\ell^{-}\leqslant\left|u-\ell\right|
, so by (
3.30
), we obtain

(3.31)

\fint_{B_{1}}(\ell^{-}(x))^{2}\,dx\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

for some
C>0
universal.

Step 2.2: lower bounds on the
\mathbb{G}
-affine function.
We want to show that if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, then

(3.32)

\ell\geqslant c_{1}a\quad\mbox{in }B_{\tau},

for some
c_{1}>0
. To prove this, we argue by contradiction assuming that

\min_{x\in\overline{B_{\tau}}}\ell(x)<ca

for any
c>0
.
We notice that, for every
x\in B_{\tau}
, recalling
\tau=\frac{1}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}}
and (
2.6
), we get

\left|\ell(x)-b\right|=\left|\left\langle\mathbf{q}(x),\pi_{x}(x)\right\rangle\right|\leqslant\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\left|x^{(1)}\right|\leqslant\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\left|x\right|_{\mathbb{G}}\leqslant\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}\,d_{c}(e,x)\leqslant\frac{\left|\mathbf{q}\right|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}},

where
|x^{(1)}|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
. Consequently,

-\frac{\left|\mathbf{q}\right|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}\leqslant\ell(x)-b\leqslant\frac{\left|\mathbf{q}\right|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}\qquad\text{ for any }x\in B_{\tau},

and therefore

ca>\min_{x\in\overline{B_{\tau}}}\ell(x)\geqslant b-\frac{\left|\mathbf{q}\right|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}},

which gives

(3.33)

b\leqslant ca+\frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}},

for any
c>0
.
Now, taking into account the usual identifications given by exponential coordinates, let us consider

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}:=\Big\{x=\left[x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\right]\in\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\equiv\mathbb{G}:\;x^{(1)}_{j}=-\frac{tq_{j}}{|\mathbf{q}|}+\eta_{j},\;\text{for }j=1,\ldots,m_{1};

\displaystyle x^{(2)}_{i}=\xi_{i},\;\text{for }i=m_{1}+1,\ldots,m_{2},\;{\mbox{
for some }}(t,\eta,\mathbf{\xi})\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

where we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=\Bigg\{(t,\eta,\mathbf{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}:\;

\displaystyle t\in\left[\frac{1}{4\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}},\frac{3}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}\right],

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\eta_{j}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{100\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}}\;\;{\mbox{and}}\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}}\xi_{i}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{100\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}}\Bigg\}.

We observe that if
x\in{\mathcal{B}}
, then

\begin{split}d_{c}(e,x)^{4}&\leqslant\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}|x|_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}=\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\big(-\frac{tq_{j}}{|\mathbf{q}|}+\eta_{j}\big)^{2}\Big)^{2}+\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}}\xi_{i}^{2}\\
&\leqslant 4\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}\Big(t^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\eta_{j}^{2}\Big)^{2}+\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}}\xi_{i}^{2}\leqslant 4\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{4}\Big(\frac{9}{100\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{100\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{100}<1,\end{split}

so we have

(3.34)

{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq B_{1}.

Furthermore, by (
3.33
), we have, for
x\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

\displaystyle\ell(x)=b-t|\mathbf{q}|+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{j}\eta_{j}\leqslant ca+\frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}-\frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{4\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}+\frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{10\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}=ca-\frac{|\mathbf{q}|}{20\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}.

which implies, using hypothesis (
3.21
),

\ell(x)\leqslant ca-\frac{a}{160\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}.

Then, taking
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{320\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}\right)
, we infer that

\ell(x)\leqslant-\frac{a}{320\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}.

Accordingly, exploiting (
3.34
) into (
3.31
), we obtain

\displaystyle C\,|B_{1}|\,\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}\geqslant\int_{B_{1}}(\ell^{-}(x))^{2}\,dx\geqslant\int_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\ell^{-}(x))^{2}\,dx\geqslant\int_{{\mathcal{B}}}\left(\frac{a}{320\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}\right)^{2}\,dx\geqslant\overline{c}a^{2},

for some positive universal constant
\overline{c}
. This establishes the desired contradiction if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, and thus the proof of (
3.32
) is complete.

Step 2.3: conclusion of the proof of (
3.28
).

We can now address the completion of the proof of the measure estimate in (
3.28
).

Recalling the Poincaré-Sobolev inequality (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
38
]
), it holds

\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left|u(x)-\ell(x)\right|^{2^{*}}\,dx\right)^{1/2^{*}}\leqslant C\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\big(u(x)-\ell(x)\big)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}

for some
C>0
universal, where

2^{*}:=\frac{2Q}{Q-2}.

Then, by virtue of (
3.20
), (
3.29
), we get

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left|u(x)-\ell(x)\right|^{2^{*}}\,dx\right)^{1/2^{*}}\leqslant C\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\big(u(x)-\ell(x)\big)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=C\left(\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\varepsilon a.

This and (
3.32
) entail

\displaystyle C\varepsilon a\geqslant\left(\int_{B_{\tau}\cap\left\{u=0\right\}}\left|\ell(x)\right|^{2^{*}}\,dx\right)^{1/2^{*}}\geqslant c_{1}a\left|B_{\tau}\cap\left\{u=0\right\}\right|^{1/2^{*}},

which means, up to renaming constants,

(3.35)

\left|B_{\tau}\cap\left\{u=0\right\}\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2^{*}}.

We notice that

2^{*}=\frac{2Q+4-4}{Q-2}=2+\frac{4}{Q-2}.

Therefore, setting

(3.36)

\delta:=\frac{4}{Q-2}>0,

(
3.28
) immediately follows from (
3.35
).

Step 3: energy comparison.
By (
3.27
) and (
3.28
), we have

(3.37)

\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta}+|B_{1}|\sigma(a^{2}+1).

Consequently, using (
3.20
) and (
3.37
), it holds

(3.38)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\;&2\left(\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)\\
\leqslant\;&C_{2}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}+C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta}+C_{3}\sigma(a^{2}+1),\end{split}

up to renaming constants. Let us suppose
\sigma\leqslant c_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
, with
c_{0}
to be made precise later.
Recalling (
3.38
), we infer

(3.39)

\int_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant C_{2}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}+C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta}+C_{3}c_{0}\varepsilon^{2}(a^{2}+1)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

for some
C>0
universal.

Step 4: conclusion of the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
Since, by Remark
3.1
,
v-\left\langle\mathbf{q},\pi_{\cdot}\right\rangle
is
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic in
B_{\tau}
and

\big(u-\left\langle\mathbf{q},\pi_{\cdot}\right\rangle\big)-\big(v-\left\langle\mathbf{q},\pi_{\cdot}\right\rangle\big)=u-v\in HW^{1,2}_{0}(B_{\tau}),

we have that
v-\left\langle\mathbf{q},\pi_{\cdot}\right\rangle
is
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement of
u-\left\langle\mathbf{q},\pi_{\cdot}\right\rangle
in
B_{\tau}
, being
v
the
\mathbb{G}
-harmonic replacement of
u
in
B_{\tau}
. Thus, by Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
, it exists a constant
C_{0}>0
, depending only on
Q
, such that, for every
x\in B_{\tau}
,

(3.40)

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\leqslant\sup_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v-\mathbf{q}\right|^{2}\leqslant C_{0}\fint_{B_{\tau}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(y)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|^{2}\,dy\leqslant C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

where the last inequality is a consequence of hypothesis (
3.20
). Consequently,

(3.41)

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v-\mathbf{q}\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon a\quad\mbox{in }B_{\tau}

for some constant
C>0
universal.

Denoting by
\bar{q}_{j}:=X_{j}v(0)-q_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m_{1}
, let us define the constant horizontal section

\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\bar{q}_{j}X_{j}(x),\quad\text{for }x\in\mathbb{G}.

By (
3.41
), for all
x\in B_{\tau}
, we have

(3.42)

|\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)|=|\bar{\mathbf{q}}(0)|=|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(0)-\mathbf{q}(0)|\leqslant C\varepsilon a.

Hence, combining (
3.41
) and (
3.42
), we deduce that, for
x\in B_{\tau}
,

(3.43)

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|\leqslant\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)\right|+\left|\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon a,

up to renaming
C>0
universal.

By Theorem 1.3 in
[
13
]
, it exists a constant
C_{0}>0
, depending only on
Q
, such that, for each
\rho\in(0,\tau/2)
, it holds

(3.44)

\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left(C_{0}\left(\frac{\rho}{\tau}\right)^{\mu}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v-\mathbf{q}-\bar{\mathbf{q}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\tau})}\right)^{2}\,dx\leqslant C_{2}\,\rho^{2\mu}\,\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

for some
\mu=\mu(Q)\in(0,1]
and
C_{2}>0
universal, where in the last inequality we used estimate (
3.43
). Putting together (
3.37
) and (
3.44
), we get

(3.45)

\begin{split}&\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\\
\leqslant\;&2\left(\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)\right|^{2}\,dx+\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)\\
\leqslant\;&2C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta}\rho^{-Q}+2{C}\sigma(a^{2}+1)\rho^{-Q}+2C_{2}\rho^{2\mu}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},\end{split}

up to renaming constants.

Now, setting
\alpha_{0}:=\mu
, for every
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0})
, we choose

(3.46)

\rho:=\min\left\{(8C_{2})^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha-\alpha_{0})}},\frac{\tau}{2}\right\},\qquad\varepsilon_{0}:=\left(\frac{\rho^{2\alpha+Q}a_{0}^{2}}{{8}C_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}},\quad{\mbox{and}}\quad c_{0}:=\frac{\rho^{2\alpha+Q}a_{0}^{2}}{8{C}(a_{1}^{2}+1)}.

With these choices, it holds, for every
\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
and
\sigma\leqslant c_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
,

\displaystyle 2C_{2}\rho^{2{\mu}}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha},

\displaystyle 2C_{1}\varepsilon^{2+\delta}\rho^{-Q}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}

and

\displaystyle 2{C}\sigma(a^{2}+1)\rho^{-Q}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}.

Consequently, using (
3.45
), we reach

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)-\bar{\mathbf{q}}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2}\leqslant\rho^{2\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}a^{2},

which gives the desired result in (
3.22
) by calling
\widetilde{q}_{i}:=q_{i}+\bar{q}_{i}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,m_{1}
.

Moreover, from (
3.42
) we have

|\mathbf{q}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}|=|\bar{\mathbf{q}}|\leqslant C\varepsilon a,

which establishes (
3.23
). This completes the proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Let

Suppose that there exist

such that

(3.47)

a\in[a_{0},a_{1}]

and
u

) and (

Then, there exist
\varepsilon_{0}
,
\kappa_{0}
, and

,

Remark 3.5
.

We point out that a consequence of (
3.48
)
in Corollary
3.4

is that, if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small,

(3.50)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\neq\mathbf{0}\quad{\mbox{in }}B_{1/2},

where
\mathbf{0}
is the null horizontal section.
Indeed, by (

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u-\mathbf{q}\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon a\quad\mbox{in }B_{1/2},

which gives

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right|\geqslant\left|\mathbf{q}\right|-C\varepsilon a\quad\mbox{in }B_{1/2}.

Finally, by (

\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right|\geqslant\frac{a}{4}-C\varepsilon a>0\quad\mbox{in }B_{1/2},

as long as

is small enough.

Furthermore, we can conclude that

u>0\quad{\mbox{in }}B_{1/2}.

To check this, we suppose by contradiction that
there exists a point
x_{0}\in B_{1/2}
such that

(by assumption

). Therefore, since

, we know that

3.51
) holds.

In order to prove Corollary
3.4
, we recall the notion of Morrey-Campanato spaces in the setting of Carnot Groups, see
[
40
]
.

Definition 3.6

(Morrey-Campanato spaces)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
. For every
1\leqslant p<+\infty
and
\lambda\in(0,+\infty)
, a horizontal section
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m_{1}})\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})
is said to be in the Morrey-Campanato space
\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,\,p}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})
if

[f]_{\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,\,p}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})}:=\sup_{B\subset\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|B|^{1+p\lambda}}\int_{B}|f(y)-\mathbf{f_{B}}(y)|^{p}\,dy\right)^{1/p}<+\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all metric balls
B\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B}\subset\Omega
and
\mathbf{f_{B}}
is the constant horizontal section given by

\mathbf{f_{B}}(y):=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\left(\fint_{B}f_{i}(z)\,dz\right)X_{i}(y),\quad\text{for }y\in\Omega.

Remark 3.7
.

For every
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m_{1}})\in\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,p}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})
, the quantity
[u]_{\mathcal{E}^{p,\lambda}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})}
is a seminorm in
\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,p}(\Omega,H\mathbb{G})
and

(3.52)

{\mbox{it is equivalent to the quantity }}\quad\sup_{B\subset\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|B|^{1+p\lambda}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\int_{B}\left|u(x)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(x)\right|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p},

where the supremum is taken over all metric balls
B
such that
\overline{B}\subset\Omega
and the infimum is taken over all the constant horizontal sections such that

(3.53)

\boldsymbol{\xi}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\xi_{i}X_{i}(x),\quad\text{for }x\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and some }\xi:=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}.

To prove (
3.52
), we observe that for every ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B}\subset\Omega
and constant horizontal section
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as in (
3.53
), by applying Jensen’s Inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{u_{B}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\left(\fint_{B}|u_{i}(y)-\xi_{i}|\,dy\right)^{2}\right|^{\frac{p}{2}}\leqslant\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\fint_{B}|u_{i}(y)-\xi_{i}|\,dy\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\fint_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(y)\right|\,dy\right)^{p}\leqslant C\fint_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy,

for some constant
C>0
only depending on
p
and
\mathbb{G}
. Consequently, up to renaming
C>0
, it occurs,

\displaystyle\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\mathbf{u_{B}}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p}\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy\,+\,2^{p}|B|\left|\mathbf{u_{B}}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(x)\right|^{p}\,dy

for every constant horizontal section
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as in (
3.53
) and for every ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B}\subset\Omega
, which leads to

\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\mathbf{u_{B}}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy\leqslant C\inf_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy.

On the other hand, for every ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B}\subset\Omega
, it is clear that

\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\mathbf{u_{B}}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy\geqslant\inf_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\int_{B}\left|u(y)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(y)\right|^{p}\,dy.

Now, we recall a result, proved in
[
40
]
, which we will use in the proof of Corollary
3.4
.

Theorem 3.8
.

(
[
40
]
*Theorem 5)

For every metric ball
B\subset\mathbb{G}
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and
p\in[1,+\infty)
, one has

\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,p}(B,H\mathbb{G})=C^{0,\lambda}(B,H\mathbb{G}).

More precisely, it results that a function

if and only if

is equal,

[\phi]_{\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,p}(B,H\mathbb{G})}
and

We also point out the following scaling property
of almost minimizers.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
u
be an almost minimizer for
J
in
B_{1}
with constant
\kappa

\beta

For any

such that
\overline{B_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\subset B_{1/r}
and

Proof.

Given

and

as in the statement of Lemma

and we define

(3.56)

w(x):=rv\left(\delta_{1/r}(x)\right).

Then, with the notation

, for all

Accordingly, we can exploit

\int_{B_{r\varrho}(y_{0})}\Big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(y)\right|^{2}+\chi_{\left\{u>0\right\}}(y)\Big)\,dy\leqslant(1+\kappa r^{\beta}\varrho^{\beta})\int_{B_{r\varrho}(y_{0})}\Big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w(y)\right|^{2}+\chi_{\left\{w>0\right\}}(y)\Big)\,dy.

Furthermore, using, consistently with (

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{r\varrho}(y_{0})}\Big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w(y)\right|^{2}+\chi_{\left\{w>0\right\}}(y)\Big)\,dy={r^{Q}}\int_{B_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\Big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w(\delta_{r}(x))\right|^{2}+\chi_{\left\{w>0\right\}}(\delta_{r}(x))\Big)\,dx\\
&\qquad\qquad=r^{Q}\int_{B_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\Big(\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w_{r}(x)\right|^{2}+\chi_{\left\{w_{r}>0\right\}}(x)\Big)\,dx,\end{split}

and a similar identity holds true replacing

from (
3.56

Proof of Corollary

Thanks to Lemma

We divide the proof into separate steps.

Step 1: iteration of Lemma

\mathbf{q}_{k}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}q_{k,j}X_{j}(x),\qquad\text{for some }\ q_{k}=:(q_{k,1},\ldots,q_{k,m_{1}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}},

such that

\begin{split}&|\mathbf{q}_{k}|\in\left[\frac{a}{4}-\frac{\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a\left(1-\rho^{k\alpha}\right)}{1-\rho^{\alpha}},\,C_{0}a+\frac{\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a\left(1-\rho^{k\alpha}\right)}{1-\rho^{\alpha}}\right]\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{G},\\

with

\mathbf{q}_{k+1}

\begin{split}\left(\fint_{B_{\rho}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u_{r}(x)-\mathbf{q}_{k+1}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\rho^{\alpha}\varepsilon_{k}a\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\left|\mathbf{q}_{k}-{\mathbf{q}}_{k+1}\right|\leqslant\widetilde{C}\varepsilon_{k}a.\end{split}

Since

,

(3.62)

\left(\fint_{B_{\rho^{k+1}}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)-\mathbf{q}_{k+1}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\rho^{\alpha}\rho^{{k\alpha}}\varepsilon a=\rho^{{(k+1)\alpha}}\varepsilon a.

Moreover, using again the inductive assumption and (
3.61

\displaystyle|\mathbf{q}_{k+1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle|\mathbf{q}_{k}|+|\mathbf{q}_{k}-\mathbf{q}_{k+1}|\leqslant C_{0}a+\frac{\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a\left(1-\rho^{k\alpha}\right)}{1-\rho^{\alpha}}+\widetilde{C}\rho^{k\alpha}\varepsilon a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{0}a+\frac{\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a\left(1-\rho^{k\alpha}\right)+\widetilde{C}\rho^{k\alpha}\varepsilon a(1-\rho^{\alpha})}{1-\rho^{\alpha}}=C_{0}a+\frac{\widetilde{C}\varepsilon a\left(1-\rho^{(k+1)\alpha}\right)}{1-\rho^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\left|\left(\fint_{B_{\rho^{k+1}}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}-|\mathbf{q}_{k+1}|\right|

\displaystyle\left|\left(\fint_{B_{\rho^{k+1}}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}-|\mathbf{q}|\right|=\left|\left(\fint_{B_{\rho^{k+1}}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}-|\mathbf{q}_{0}|\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\left|\left(\fint_{B_{\rho^{k+1}}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}-|\mathbf{q}_{k+1}|\right|+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left||\mathbf{q}_{j+1}|-|\mathbf{q}_{j}|\right|

) is also fulfilled (even if with different structural constants). To summarize, all the hypotheses of Corollary
3.4
in this rescaled context are satisfied.

We can thus exploit an adapted version of Corollary
3.4
. This tells us that there exist
\varepsilon_{0}
(possibly different from the one coming from Proposition
3.2
) and
\kappa_{0}
, depending on
Q
,
\beta
,
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
,
such that if

(4.12)

\widetilde{\kappa}\leqslant\kappa_{0}\varepsilon_{0}^{2},

then we have

(4.13)

\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\eta^{k_{0}+1}/2})}\leqslant\bar{C}a(\eta^{k_{0}}),

for some universal constant
\bar{C}>0
. We stress that (
4.12
) holds by choosing
s
in (
4.1
) small enough, i.e., taking
s:=\left(\frac{\kappa_{0}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{2\kappa}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}
.
Notice, in particular, that

(4.14)

\mbox{$s$ depends on $Q$, $\kappa$,
$\beta$, and $\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}$},

by virtue of (
4.11
).

Using (
4.13
), we then get, for all
r\in\Big(0,\frac{\eta^{k_{0}+1}}{2}\Big)
, recalling the definition of
k_{0}
,

(4.15)

\begin{split}&a(r)=\left(\frac{1}{|B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\bar{C}a(\eta^{k_{0}})\leqslant\bar{C}\big(C(\eta)M+2^{-k_{0}}a(1)\big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\bar{C}\big(C(\eta)M+a(1)\big)\leqslant C(M,\eta)(1+a(1)),\end{split}

as long as
C(M,\eta)\geqslant\bar{C}(C(\eta)M+1)
.

Otherwise, if
r\in\left[\frac{\eta^{k_{0}+1}}{2},\eta\right]
, we
take
k_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\eta^{k_{r}+1}<r\leqslant\eta^{k_{r}}
. Note that

\frac{1}{\eta^{k_{r}}}\leqslant\frac{1}{r}\leqslant\frac{2}{\eta^{k_{0}+1}},

whence

(4.16)

k_{r}\leqslant k_{0}+C_{\star},

where
C_{\star}:=1+\frac{\log 2}{\log(1/\eta)}
.

We then distinguish two cases depending on whether
k_{r}\in\{0,\dots,k_{0}\}
. If this holds, then
k_{r}\in{\mathcal{K}}
and therefore

a(\eta^{k_{r}})\leqslant C(\eta)M+2^{-k_{r}}a(1),

from which, recalling (
4.4
), we obtain

(4.17)

\begin{split}&a(r)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{|B_{\eta^{k_{r}+1}}|}\int_{B_{\eta^{k_{r}}}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}=\eta^{-Q/2}a(\eta^{k_{r}})\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\eta^{-Q/2}\big(C(\eta)M+2^{-k_{r}}a(1)\big)\leqslant C(M,\eta)(1+a(1)).\end{split}

If instead
k_{r}>k_{0}
, by (
4.16
), we have

\displaystyle a(r)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{|B_{\eta^{k_{0}+C_{\star}+1}}|}\int_{B_{\eta^{k_{0}}}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}=\eta^{-Q(C_{\star}+1)/2}a(\eta^{k_{0}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\leqslant\eta^{-Q(C_{\star}+1)/2}\big(C(\eta)M+2^{-k_{0}}a(1)\big)\leqslant\eta^{-Q(C_{\star}+1)/2}\big(C(\eta)M+a(1)\big)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\leqslant C(M,\eta)(1+a(1)),

as long as
C(M,\eta)
is chosen sufficiently large. Combining this with (
4.17
) and (
4.15
),
we reach (
4.3
).

Step 2: conclusions.

Up to scaling and translations, we can achieve that (
4.3
) is true in all balls with center
x_{0}
in
B_{1/2}
and sufficiently small radius. In other words, for all
r\in(0,\eta]
, it holds

a(r,x_{0})\leqslant C(M,\eta)(1+a(1)),

where

a(r,x_{0}):=\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}.

Consequently, in view of the Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
49
]
), recalling that
u\in HW^{1,2}(B_{1})
, we obtain, for almost every
x_{0}\in B_{1/2}
,

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(x_{0})\right|=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}a(r,x_{0})\leqslant C(M,\eta)(1+a(1))=C\Big(1+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\Big),

for some
C>0
, and thus

\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leqslant C\Big(1+\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\Big).

We now show that the second claim in Theorem
1.2
is also true. To this end, we can suppose that

(4.18)

\{u=0\}\cap B_{s/100}\neq\varnothing,

and we consider a Lebesgue point
\bar{x}\in B_{s/100}
for
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u
.
Up to a left-translation, we can take that
\bar{x}=0
and modify (
4.18
) into

(4.19)

\{u=0\}\cap B_{s/50}\neq\varnothing.

We claim that, in this case,

(4.20)

{\mathcal{K}}=\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, suppose this does not hold and let
k_{0}
as before (recall (
4.7
)). Hence, according to (
4.10
), we can apply Corollary
3.4
(rescaled as above) and get, from (
3.51
), that
u>0
in
B_{s/2}
, in contradiction with (
4.19
). This establishes (
4.20
).

Therefore, in light of (
4.4
) and (
4.20
), for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

a(\eta^{k})\leqslant C(\eta)M+2^{-k}a(1),

which yields

|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(\bar{x})|=|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u(0)|=\lim_{k\to+\infty}a(\eta^{k})\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\big(C(\eta)M+2^{-k}a(1)\big)=C(\eta)M.

The proof of the second claim in Theorem
1.2
is then complete by also recalling (
4.14
).
