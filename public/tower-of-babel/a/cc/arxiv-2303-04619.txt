Abstract
Instantaneous measurements of the electromagnetic field strength do not reflect the maximum exposure levels possible in a given location. An extrapolation factor needs to be applied to the measurements before comparing them against the local exposure guidelines or recommendations for compliance evaluation. For the fifth generation networks, a standardized approach for extrapolating electromagnetic field values is yet to be defined. This work provides an overview of the state-of-the-art research that focuses on estimating the maximum electromagnetic field exposure caused by radiation from fifth generation base stations. It also considers current efforts by national and international organizations to establish standardized methods for extrapolating the electromagnetic field measurements which is necessary in investigating conformance with the electromagnetic field guidelines and regulations.

This rapid spread of fifth generation technology has sparked public concern on the increasing exposure of humans to the electromagnetic field (EMF) radiated by the emerging technology. Several organizations, such as the World Health Organization, International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP), Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, and International Electrotechnical Commission, work constantly to define and maintain guidelines and recommendations with the goal to protect humans and the environment against any possible harmful effects caused by the exposure to radio-frequency electromagnetic fields
[

The electromagnetic field exposure limits set by the regulatory organizations target the overall maximum possible radiation at a given frequency and location, which is not easily measurable in practice with regular measurement equipment as they only provide the instantaneous and average values of electromagnetic field radiation at a given location. For proper comparison with the regulatory limits, extrapolation of the measured values to the maximum possible electromagnetic field is necessary. This task was relatively straight-forward for previous generations of communication technologies
[
10
]
. However, in fifth generation networks the task is more involved as several aspects and features that distinguish fifth generation from its predecessors need to be considered.

This work investigates current research and standardization attempts for extrapolation of electromagnetic field measurements within the context of fifth generation networks. Our work is structured as follows: in Section
II
, we provide an overview of recent works that consider different extrapolation methods for electromagnetic field measurements relating to fifth generation new radio technology. Section
III
outlines some of the national and international standardization endeavors for the assessment of electromagnetic field exposure in fifth generation. Finally, the conclusions and outlooks for this work are given in Section
IV
.

II
Assessment approaches: state-of-the-art

The general concept for approximating the maximum electromagnetic field exposure caused by fifth generation is similar in principle to that used for previous communication generations. Namely, it is based on measuring the periodic signaling that is always transmitted and independent of a base station’s traffic load
[
10
]
. In the case of fifth generation new radio, the synchronization signal / physical broadcast channel signaling block (or synchronization signal block for short) in the downlink satisfies this requirement.

However, there are some additional considerations that need to be taken into account for the electromagnetic field evaluation in fifth generation networks. For instance, unlike with previous generations, the synchronization signal block is not centered around the carrier frequency, nor does it have a fixed position in the fifth generation new radio frame. Instead, it can occur anywhere across the carrier bandwidth and thus needs to be carefully determined during measurements. Additionally, if beamforming is used, variations between the broadcast and traffic antennas patterns should be taken into consideration which could turn into quite the involved task
[
11
]
. Also, the transmission duty cycle of the time division duplex mode, in terms of downlink to uplink ratio, needs to be reflected when evaluating the extrapolation factor.

In this section, we examine some of the works from both the research and academia communities that studied the assessment of human exposure to electromagnetic field and the problem of extrapolating the measured values to the maximum theoretically possible exposure within the context of fifth generation technology. The extrapolation methods for evaluating the maximum electromagnetic field exposure can be generally categorized as follows:

II-A

Based on frequency-selective methods

Frequency-selective measurements employ a frequency-measuring equipment to evaluate electromagnetic field emissions produced by a given technology. A properly configured spectrum analyzer, for instance, is capable of performing this type of measurement by evaluating the synchronisation signal’s power in zero-span mode. Several works have based their proposed extrapolation techniques of electromagnetic field exposure produced by fifth generation base stations on frequency-selective measurements. One of the earliest works addressing the extrapolation problem of electromagnetic field in fifth generation from a theoretical perspective is
[
12
]
, where Keller proposes two extrapolation methods; the first of which is based on frequency-selective measurements. He also defined the preconditions necessary for the application of this method. Authors in
[
13
]
applied an extrapolation factor for fifth generation base stations operating in non-stand-alone mode that relies on determining the maximum number of subcarriers. The chosen method is an extension of the long term evolution electromagnetic field extrapolation method specified in
[
14
]
, as fifth generation in dynamic spectrum sharing mode shares the spectrum with long term evolution technology and, for instance, uses the same sub-carrier spacing of 15 KHz as long term evolution to guarantee coexistence.
An extrapolation factor that reflects the difference between the bandwidths of the full signal to that of the synchronization signal block and between the maximal gains of the traffic and broadcast beams, is proposed by the authors in
[
15
]
.

In
[
10
]
,
[
11
,
16
,
17
]
, the authors extend the extrapolation method to account for additional factors specific to fifth generation new radio by leveraging the frame structure of fifth generation to extract information on numerology and channel duty cycle, even when such information is not available from the operator. They also verify the proposal of including next generation NodeB forced traffic beams with the help of dedicated user equipments for physical downlink shared channel measurements, and its associated demodulation reference signal, which is vital for estimating the maximum electromagnetic field accurately.

Authors in
[
4
]
design and propose a measurement framework for a stand-alone millimeter-wave base station scenario in a commercial deployment. They also consider what influence does user traffic have over the levels of exposure.

However, this measurement method comes with its own limitations such as difficulty in distinguishing between downlink and uplink transmissions in time division duplex mode, the lack of capacity to discern physical broadcast channel from physical random access channel signals
[
14
]
, and the inability to differentiate between detected emissions from neighbouring beams of new radio cells. Consequently, frequency-selective methods might be appropriate for early stages of fifth generation deployments while bearing in mind their limitations.

II-B

Based on code-selective methods

Code-selective measurements provide a higher level of insight and detail with respect to technology-specific electromagnetic field emissions in comparison to frequency-selective methods; as the measurement equipment is capable of measuring and decoding the individual signaling components from the different mobile communication technologies. For fifth generation networks, it is able to deliver information distinguishing synchronization signal blocks from different cells.

III
Standardization efforts

In this section, we consider existing efforts on the national and international levels that provide guidelines for the extrapolation procedures of fifth generation electromagnetic field measurements to the maximum theoretically possible levels.

On the national level, the Federal Network Agency for Electricity, Gas, Telecommunications, Post and Railway, or Bundesnetzagentur for short, in Germany is concerned with setting the guidelines for the measurement and evaluation of the maximum electromagnetic field exposure. Based on
[
23
]
, the Bundesnetzagentur endorses two methods; both of which extrapolate the electromagnetic field measurements using the frequency-selective method. The first method is based on beam forcing at maximum load to the point where the frequency-selective measurement is carried out. This method is limited to vendor-specific network equipment (due to the tool used for traffic forcing) and requires the support of the network operator. The second and more broadly applicable method is based on frequency-selective measurements of the synchronization signal block, and more specifically, the secondary synchronization signal part within a measurement bandwidth of 2 MHz. Extrapolation to the maximum electromagnetic field takes into account the total bandwidth of the signal, a gain correction factor for base stations operating in beamforming mode which takes into account the difference between the traffic and broadcast beams, and a duty cycle correction factor for the operation in time division duplex mode.

Another example of national efforts is provided by the Federal Institute of Metrology in Switzerland in their technical report
[
24
]
. The Federal Institute of Metrology defines two methods for extrapolation of electromagnetic field for fifth generation new radio base stations based on the previously described measurement approaches in Section
II
. However, it adopts the code-selective method as the reference method whereas the frequency-selective method is seen merely as an approximate one since an overestimation is highly likely when using this method. Both extrapolation methods are based on the measurement of the secondary synchronization signal part of the synchronization signal block. In the code-selective method, the extrapolation is evaluated per new radio cell and the applied extrapolation factor takes into consideration an antenna correction factor that depends on the azimuth and elevation angles, a beam statistic factor that accounts for the use of adaptive antennae, and a duplex factor that depends on the downlink to uplink ratio for time division duplex mode. The total extrapolated electromagnetic field caused by all new radio cells is finally evaluated by applying the root sum square on all new radio cell-specific contributions.
On the other hand, the frequency-selective extrapolation method can be executed by measuring the electromagnetic field over the bandwidth of the secondary synchronization signal using the max-hold function in a spectrum analyzer. It is then necessary to apply a reduction factor to adjust the measurement to reflect the value per resource element and a correction factor that accounts for installations supporting beamforming.

On an international level, the International Electrotechnical Commission published the IEC-62232 standard governing the methods of evaluation for human electromagnetic field exposure using radio frequency measurements in the vicinity of mobile base stations
[
14
]
. However, the latest edition of the standard that was published in 2017 addressed communication technologies up to long term evolution and did not account for the unique features specific to the fifth generation new radio technology, as the first deployment of the latter started only in 2019
[
25
]
. An upgrade of the standard is currently undergoing finalization and is expected to be released and officially adopted by the end of 2022. The latest version of the International Electrotechnical Commission standard will include extensions that are relevant to fifth generation new radio such as evaluation methods that consider base stations supporting massive multiple input multiple output and those operating in dynamic spectrum sharing mode
[
26
]
. It will also reflect modifications in the electromagnetic field exposure limits adopted in the latest guidelines published by the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection in 2020
[
7
]
.

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we provided a short review of current research on the extrapolation of fifth generation new radio electromagnetic field measurements to the maximum electromagnetic field exposure values; a necessary step to ensure the compliance of fifth generation new radio base stations with the electromagnetic field exposure limits and guidelines, set by organizations such as the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection or the responsible national regulatory authorities, that are intended to protect humans and the environment. We classified the research efforts based on the measurement method used in the two main categories of frequency-selective and code-selective methods. We also summarized some of the national and international standardization endeavours that aim to set a reference method for evaluating the human electromagnetic field exposure taking into consideration the recent fifth generation technology.
We plan to use this overview as a foundation for choosing the appropriate measurement methodology and electromagnetic field extrapolation method to assess our recently deployed fifth generation campus network in Kaiserslautern
[
9
]
.
