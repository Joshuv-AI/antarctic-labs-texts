Novel wall-crossing behaviour in rank one 𝒩 = 𝟐 ∗ \bm{{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}} gauge theory
Philipp Rüter, Richard J. Szabo

Abstract
We study the BPS spectrum of four-dimensional 𝒩 = 2 {\mathcal{N}}=2 supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory with gauge group S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) and a massive adjoint hypermultiplet, which has an extremely intricate structure with infinite spectrum in all chambers of its Coulomb moduli space, and is not well understood. We build on previous results by employing the BPS quiver description of the spectrum, and explore the qualitative features in detail using numerical techniques. We find novel and unexpected behaviour in the form of wall-crossings involving interactions between BPS particles with negative electric-magnetic pairings, which we interpret in terms of the reverse orderings of the usual wall-crossing formulas for rank one 𝒩 = 2 {\mathcal{N}}=2 field theories. This identifies new a priori unrelated states in the spectrum.

{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}

[

]

[

,

,

,

,

. The BPS spectrum exhibits very complicated behaviour at generic points of the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{m}

[

]
. Results thus far have therefore been limited to either specific loci in
\mathscr{B}_{m}

{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}

In our study of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}

\gamma\in\Gamma

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon\Gamma\times\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}

Z\colon\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory, every state in the spectrum can be written as a linear combination of three charges
\gamma_{i}

, with pairings
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}\rangle=2

theories. It also hints at an intricate structure of the spectrum which connects

2
Geometry of Rank One
\bm{{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}}
Gauge Theory

The
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory is defined as
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory on
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
coupled to a hypermultiplet of mass
m
in the adjoint representation of the gauge group. In the massless limit
m=0
, the
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetry is enhanced and the field theory becomes the
{\mathcal{N}}=4
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory. On the other hand, in the infinite mass limit
m\to\infty
the hypermultiplet decouples and the field theory becomes the asymptotically free pure
{\mathcal{N}}=2
gauge theory consisting solely of a vector multiplet in the adjoint representation of the gauge group, whose bosonic field content consists of a gauge field
A
, a complex scalar Higgs field
\phi
valued in the complexified gauge algebra, and an auxiliary scalar field
D
. In this paper we consider only the gauge group
SU(2)
of rank one. Like its massless limit, the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory then enjoys manifest
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
S-duality and superconformal symmetry.

The Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{m}
of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory is the base of a fibration whose fibre over
u\in\mathscr{B}_{m}
is the Seiberg-Witten curve
\Sigma=\Sigma_{u;m}
. For gauge group
SU(2)
this is the family of elliptic curves
[
SW94a
]

\Sigma_{u;m}:\quad y^{2}=\big(x-\varepsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})_{u;m}\big)\,\big(x-\varepsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})_{u;m}\big)\,\big(x-\varepsilon_{3}(\tau_{0})_{u;m}\big)\ ,

(2.1)

where

\varepsilon_{i}(\tau_{0})_{u;m}=e_{i}(\tau_{0})\,u+e_{i}(\tau_{0})^{2}\,m^{2}\ .

(2.2)

Here
\tau_{0}
is the complexified (exactly marginal) microscopic gauge coupling, while the Coulomb branch modulus
u\in\mathscr{B}_{m}
is given by

u=u_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\,e_{1}\,m^{2}+m^{2}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,c_{n}\,q_{0}^{n}\ ,

(2.3)

where
u_{0}=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\,\langle{\rm Tr}\,\phi^{2}\rangle
is the Coulomb order parameter, with the trace in the fundamental representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. The series coefficients
c_{n}
correspond to instanton corrections
[
DKM97
]
with
q_{0}={\mathrm{e}}^{\,2\pi\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\tau_{0}}
.
The half-periods
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
form a modular vector of weight two under
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. An explicit closed expression for
u=u(\tau,\tau_{0})
can be found in
[
HKPK13
]
as a function of both the ultraviolet coupling
\tau_{0}
and the complex structure modulus
\tau
of the curve
\Sigma
which is identified as the infrared gauge coupling of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
field theory. This defines a bimodular form of weight
(0,2)
, and consequently
\varepsilon_{i}(\tau,\tau_{0})
form a bimodular vector of weight
(0,4)
.

The Coulomb moduli space
\mathscr{B}_{m}
is topologically the four-punctured sphere
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\infty,u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}}
, where the elliptic curve (
2.1
) degenerates at the singularities
u_{i}=e_{i}(\tau_{0})\,m^{2}
in the
u
-plane. The singularities for
i=1,2,3
are respectively called electric, magnetic and dyonic according to the
U(1)
charges of the massless particles which appear at the singularity; they meet at equal distances from each other because of the cyclic
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
symmetry acting on
\mathscr{B}_{m}
, and hence only one ultraviolet scale
\Lambda_{m}
is generated. These massless particles can be used to give a unique weak coupling description of the gauge theory near each singularity. At the singularity
u=\infty
, the
W^{\pm}
-bosons become infinitely massive and
\tau=\tau_{0}
, so that the low energy effective field theory is an almost superconformal theory.

In this paper we are interested in the microscopic description of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory as a field theory of class
\mathcal{S}

[
Wit97
,
Gai12
]
. For this, we exhibit the Seiberg-Witten curve
\Sigma
as a branched covering of an ultraviolet curve
C
. There is an
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
Möbius transformation sending the four branch points at
x=\infty,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
to the points
-u/m^{2},e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, which maps
[
DW96
]
the elliptic curve (
2.1
) to the double cover
w^{2}=m^{2}\,x+u
of the curve

\displaystyle y^{2}=\big(x-e_{1}(\tau_{0})\big)\,\big(x-e_{2}(\tau_{0})\big)\,(x-e_{3}(\tau_{0})\big)\ .

(2.4)

This is the Weierstrass canonical form of the elliptic curve which describes a torus with complex structure modulus
\tau_{0}
. It can be uniformized by taking
x=\wp(z)
and
y=\frac{1}{2}\,\wp^{\prime}(z)
, where
\wp(z|\tau_{0})
is the Weierstrass
\wp
-function which is a doubly periodic even function of a local holomorphic coordinate
z\in\mathbb{C}
on this torus, with a single double pole
\wp(z|\tau_{0})\simeq\frac{1}{z^{2}}
for
z\to 0
. Then the equation for
\Sigma
becomes

w^{2}=m^{2}\,\wp(z|\tau_{0})+u\ .

(2.5)

This exhibits the Seiberg-Witten curve as a branched two-sheeted covering
\Sigma\to C
of genus two over the torus
C
of modulus
\tau_{0}
with a single regular puncture at
z=0
.

The Seiberg-Witten differential
\lambda=\lambda_{u;m}
on
\Sigma
is the canonical one-form

\lambda=w\,{\rm d}z

(2.6)

on the holomorphic cotangent bundle
T^{*}C\cong C\times\mathbb{C}
, with coordinates
(z,w)
, restricted to the covering
\Sigma\subset T^{*}C
. The meromorphic differential

has two simple poles at the corresponding preimages of

on
\Sigma
, with residues

, and two simple zeroes which correspond to branch points of
\Sigma\rightarrow C
. In this realization, the Seiberg-Witten curve

is equivalently the spectral curve for the moduli space of the
SU(2)
Hitchin system on
C
, interpreted as the space of quantum vacua when the four-dimensional theory is compactified on a circle. This space is biholomorphic (in a certain complex structure) to the moduli space of stable Higgs bundles on
C
, which is a torus fibration over the Coulomb branch whose fiber over
u\in\mathscr{B}_{m}
is an abelian variety of line bundles on
\Sigma_{u;m}

[
Hit87
]
,

2

2

This structure makes the moduli space into an algebraic completely integrable system. In fact,
\Sigma_{u;m}
is the spectral curve of the elliptic Calogero-Moser integrable system

and also (in another complex structure) to the moduli space
\mathscr{M}_{\text{flat}}(C)
which parameterizes flat

It is instructive to consider the two natural mass limits of this class

In the massless limit
m=0

\displaystyle\Sigma_{u_{0};0}:\quad y^{2}=\big(x-e_{1}(\tau_{0})\,u_{0}\big)\,\big(x-e_{2}(\tau_{0})\,u_{0}\big)\,\big(x-e_{3}(\tau_{0})\,u_{0}\big)\ .

(2.7)

This is the Seiberg-Witten curve of
{\mathcal{N}}=4

[

, and the Coulomb moduli space is zero-dimensional,
\mathscr{B}_{0}=\{u_{0}\}
. The Seiberg-Witten curve

coincides with the ultraviolet curve

, and the Seiberg-Witten differential is given by the canonical

-differential as
\lambda_{u_{0};0}=\sqrt{u_{0}}\ \mathrm{d}z

\diamond

In the asymptotically free limit
m\to\infty
, we double scale with the weak coupling limit
\tau_{0}\to{\mathrm{i}}\,\infty
and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{u_{\infty};\infty}:\quad y^{2}=(x-u_{\infty})\,(x^{2}-\Lambda_{\infty}^{4})\ .

This is the Seiberg-Witten curve for the pure

gauge theory with ultraviolet scale
\Lambda_{\infty}

[
SW94
]
. The Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{\infty}
is topologically a three-punctured sphere
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\infty,\Lambda_{\infty}^{2},-\Lambda_{\infty}^{2}}

\varGamma(2)\subset SL(2,\mathbb{Z})

[
SW94
]
. In this limit, the covering equation (

) describes
\Sigma_{u_{\infty};\infty}
as a genus one double cover and becomes
[
IM97
]

\displaystyle w^{2}=-\pi^{2}\,\Lambda_{\infty}^{2}\,\cos(2\pi\,z)+u_{\infty}\ ,

(2.9)

where the ultraviolet curve

is a now a cylinder with irregular punctures at its ends.

3
BPS Spectra and Wall-Crossing

Our object of study in this paper is the BPS spectrum of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory. In this section we shall review some generalities about BPS spectra and wall-crossing in
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theories, focusing on the rank one case and highlighting essential features relevant to the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory. Most of the material of this section is based on
[
GMN13b
]
, where further details may be found.

3.1
BPS States and Walls

A BPS state of a quantum field theory on
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
with rigid
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetry is a one-particle state in the Hilbert space whose mass
M
and central charge
Z
saturate the BPS bound

M\geq|Z|\ .

(3.1)

To each state we assign an electromagnetic charge
\gamma
in the charge lattice
\Gamma
, which for our purposes can be identified geometrically with homology classes of cycles on the Seiberg-Witten curve
\Sigma
in
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
.
3
3

3

More precisely,
\Gamma
should be identified with a sublattice of
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
, since we require charges to be invariant under a combined operation of exchanging the sheets of
\Sigma
and reversing the orientation of
\gamma

[
GMN13b
]
.
The central charge
Z
is a homomorphism of abelian groups

Z\,:\,\Gamma\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\ ,

(3.2)

whose value on
[\gamma]\in H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
is given by the period of the Seiberg-Witten differential
\lambda
:

Z(\gamma)=\oint_{\gamma}\,\lambda\ .

(3.3)

It therefore inherits the dependence on the Coulomb moduli
u\in\mathscr{B}
from
\Sigma_{u}
and
\lambda_{u}
, with
\Gamma
forming a local system of abelian groups over
\mathscr{B}
.

Another important piece of data is the covariantly constant antisymmetric electric-magnetic pairing

\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\,:\,\Gamma\times\Gamma\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\ ,

(3.4)

which can be taken to be the oriented intersection pairing of
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
. Pairs of charges with non-zero electric-magnetic pairing are said to be mutually non-local, otherwise they are mutually local. Charges
\gamma_{\rm f}
in the radical of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
are called flavour charges.
4
4

4

We shall often abuse terminology in the following, referring to an electromagnetic or flavour charge
\gamma
and its central charge
Z(\gamma)
synonymously. We will also frequently refer to a BPS particle by its charge
\gamma
; the corresponding antiparticle in the BPS spectrum has opposite charge
-\gamma
.

The spectrum of BPS states is piecewise constant on the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}
: there are real codimension one walls of marginal stability which separate different regions of
\mathscr{B}
. Such a wall
\mathscr{W}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
occurs when the central charges of two states
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
in the spectrum align:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=\big\{u\in\mathscr{B}\ \big|\ {\rm arg}\big(Z(\gamma;u)\big)={\rm arg}\big(Z(\gamma^{\prime};u)\big)\big\}\ .

(3.5)

The spectrum on the wall is ill-defined since BPS bound states
n\,\gamma+n^{\prime}\,\gamma^{\prime}
(with
n,n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
) can freely form and decay, as

|Z(n\,\gamma+n^{\prime}\,\gamma^{\prime})|=|n\,Z(\gamma)+n^{\prime}\,Z(\gamma^{\prime})|=n\,|Z(\gamma)|+n^{\prime}\,|Z(\gamma^{\prime})|

(3.6)

for electromagnetic charges
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
with parallel central charges
Z(\gamma)
and
Z(\gamma^{\prime})
.
As we vary
u\in\mathscr{B}
, the spectrum changes on crossing such a wall; in particular, a BPS particle with charge
\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}
decays into
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
on crossing
\mathscr{W}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
.

Example 3.7
.

For the pure
SU(2)
gauge theory, there are two regions of the Coulomb moduli space which are separated by a wall which is roughly an ellipse around
0\in\mathscr{B}_{\infty}
passing through the singularities
u=\pm\,\Lambda_{\infty}^{2}
. For small
u
(the strong coupling region), the spectrum consists of two BPS hypermultiplets
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
(the primitive monopole and dyon). On crossing the wall the spectrum changes: it still contains
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, but now additionally a vector state
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
(the
W
-boson), along with two infinite families of hypermultiplets
\gamma_{i}+n\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
with
i=1,2
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
that accumulate towards the
W
-boson as
n\to\pm\,\infty
.

3.2
Ideal Triangulations

One method for computing the spectrum of BPS states at a given point of the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}
is through a family of ideal triangulations
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
of the ultraviolet curve
C
, which depends on an angle
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\,\mathbb{Z}
. These triangulations are constructed from a singular foliation of
C
which is defined as follows
[
GMN13b
,
HN16
]
.

With the Seiberg-Witten differential
\lambda
from (
2.6
), we call a
\vartheta
-trajectory a path
\varrho
on
C
such that

{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\,\vartheta}\,\lambda(v)\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{\times}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\ ,

(3.8)

for any tangent vector
v
to
\varrho
.
These trajectories are the leaves of a singular foliation
\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}
of
C
, where the singular points are the zeroes of
\lambda
, which are also the branch points of the covering
\Sigma\rightarrow C
. The types of curves
\varrho
which are relevant for us are:

\diamond

Generic curves which have both of their endpoints on a puncture.

\diamond

Separating curves which have one endpoint on a puncture and one endpoint on a branch point.

\diamond

Finite curves which have both endpoints on a branch point.

The separating and finite curves are also called Stokes curves in direction
\vartheta
, and they form the Stokes graph in direction
\vartheta
with vertices at the branch points on
C
. The Stokes graph is also called a WKB spectral network, while its oriented edges (the Stokes curves) are also called S-walls and double walls for separating and finite curves, respectively (see
[
HRS21
]
for an up to date review); we will use both nomenclatures interchangeably in this paper.

At generic values of
\vartheta
there are no finite trajectories and the Stokes graph separates
C
into simply connected cells of the foliation
\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}
. Each such cell is bounded by separating curves and contains an infinite family of generic curves.
We can define an ideal triangulation
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
of
C
by taking one representative of the generic curves in each cell to be an edge and the punctures of
C
to be the vertices.
A neighbourhood of each simple branch point has exactly three Stokes curves emanating to or from it, so that each triangle of
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
contains one branch point. A basis of
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
is obtained by fixing a local trivialization of the covering
\Sigma\to C
, via a choice of branch cuts on the base
C
, and from curves which encircle two branch points each. We can use this to pair each edge with a cycle
\gamma
, and denote it by
E_{\gamma}
as shown in Figure
1
.

3.3
Fock-Goncharov Coordinates

The triangulations
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
can also be used to define a system of holomorphic Darboux coordinates
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}^{u,\vartheta}
for the moduli space
\mathscr{M}_{\text{flat}}(C)
of flat
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
connections on
C

[
GMN13b
,
GMN13a
,
HN16
]
. They are indexed by
[\gamma]\in H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
and depend on the parameters
(u,\vartheta)\in\mathscr{B}\times[0,2\pi)
. Defining
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}
requires a choice of some extra discrete data called a framing: given an
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
local system on
C
, this assigns to every puncture
p_{i}\in C
a flat section
s_{i}
of the associated projective line bundle over a neighbourhood of
p_{i}
with monodromy eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
when going around
p_{i}
. For the triangulations defined by the WKB foliations
\mathcal{F}_{\vartheta}
in Section
3.2
, these can be obtained as small flat sections by using WKB analysis
[
GMN13b
]
, and the triangulation
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
with this framing is called a WKB triangulation. In addition to the flip which changes edges of
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
, there is now also an operation that acts on the framing: the pop sends
\mu_{i}
to
\mu_{i}^{-1}
. For an edge
E_{\gamma}
we can then define the coordinate
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}
by assigning single-valued smooth sections
s_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, to the corners of the quadrilateral shown in Figure
1
, ordered clockwise starting from the top right corner.

The Fock-Goncharov coordinate is then given by the cross-ratio

\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}=-\frac{s_{1}\wedge s_{2}}{s_{2}\wedge s_{3}}\,\frac{s_{3}\wedge s_{4}}{s_{4}\wedge s_{1}}\ ,

(3.11)

which is invariant under complex rescaling of the sections
s_{i}
and lies in
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
for generic points in
\mathscr{M}_{\text{flat}}(C)

[
GMN13b
]
.
8
8

8

There are loci in
\mathscr{M}_{\text{flat}}(C)
where coordinates defined like this vanish as two sections align and their exterior product is equal to
0
. The Fock-Goncharov coordinates are therefore defined on a dense open subset of
\mathscr{M}_{\text{flat}}(C)
where this does not happen.
These coordinates span the group ring
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
of the charge lattice
\Gamma
,

\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\,\mathbb{C}\,\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}\,\mathcal{X}_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\mathcal{X}_{\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}\ ,

(3.12)

and they are (exponentiated) Darboux coordinates with respect to the natural holomorphic Poisson structure on the moduli space
\mathscr{M}_{\rm flat}(C)
with Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
given by

\left\{\mathcal{X}_{\gamma},\mathcal{X}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\}=\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\,\mathcal{X}_{\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}\ .

(3.13)

Thus
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}
generate the coordinate ring of the complex algebraic torus with character lattice
\Gamma
, called the complex Poisson torus.

We have seen that as
\vartheta
varies continuously there are critical values
\vartheta_{\rm c}
where the topology of the triangulation undergoes a discontinuous change due to the presence of a BPS state
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
with arg
(Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}))=\vartheta_{\rm c}
. This results in a discontinuity in the coordinates
\big\{\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}^{u,\vartheta}\big\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
given by
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}^{\Omega(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}})}(\mathcal{X}_{\gamma})
, where
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}
is the Kontsevich-Soibelman symplectomorphism acting on the complex Poisson torus as the birational cluster transformation
[
KS08
]

\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}:\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}\longmapsto\mathcal{X}_{\gamma}\,\big(1-\sigma(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}})\,\mathcal{X}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}\big)^{\langle\gamma,\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\rangle}\ ,

(3.14)

and the quadratic refinement
\sigma(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}})
is
-1
for BPS hypermultiplets and
+1
for BPS vector multiplets. This transformation of the coordinates is also based on a relabelling of edges which changes the label
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
as well as any other label
\gamma
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\longmapsto-\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma\longmapsto\gamma+\langle\gamma,\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\rangle_{+}\,\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\ ,

(3.15)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\,\big(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle+|\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle|\big)
is the positive part of the electric-magnetic pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

As we vary
\vartheta
over an angle
\pi
, we get a symplectomorphism
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}
for each BPS state
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
in the spectrum and the resulting ordered product of symplectomorphisms
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}^{\Omega(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}})}
is called the spectrum generator
\mathbb{S}
. The symplectomorphism
\mathbb{S}
is constant on the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}
, but its decomposition in terms of
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}
can change throughout
\mathscr{B}
. The general form of
\mathbb{S}
is the ordered product

\mathbb{S}(\vartheta;u)=\prod^{\curvearrowleft}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}\in\Gamma_{\vartheta}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}^{\Omega(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)}\ ,

(3.16)

where
\Gamma_{\vartheta}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\ |\ \vartheta<\text{arg}(-Z(\gamma))<\vartheta+\pi\}
and the product is taken in increasing order of
\text{arg}(-Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}))
from right to left. The
u
-dependence of
\mathbb{S}(\vartheta;u)
enters through reordering of charges and jumps of the BPS degeneracies
\Omega(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)
when walls of marginal stability are crossed. Recall that in
SU(2)
theories one should only encounter charges lifted from saddle trajectories or closed loops on the ultraviolet curve
C
, which correspond to BPS hypermultiplets with
\Omega(\gamma)=1
and BPS vector multiplets with
\Omega(\gamma)=-2
respectively.

3.4
Wall-Crossing Formulas

As discussed in Section
3.1
, there are real codimension one walls in the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}
at which the spectrum undergoes discontinuous changes. One way to keep track of the changes in the BPS spectrum on crossing these walls of marginal stability is through the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula
[
KS08
]
, which governs permutations of the factors
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}
which make up the spectrum generator
\mathbb{S}
in (
3.16
).

Example 3.17
.

The AD
3
theory is the rank one superconformal field theory at the Argyres-Douglas point in the Coulomb moduli space of
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory with gauge group
SU(3)

[
AD95
]
, whose Seiberg-Witten curve is
w^{2}=z^{3}-3\,\Lambda^{2}\,z+u
with ultraviolet curve
C=\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{\infty\}
. The Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}
in this case has two regions separated by a wall of marginal stability which is roughly an ellipse around
u=0
, passing through the two singular points at
u=\pm\,2\,\Lambda^{3}
. Near
u=0
the spectrum consists of two hypermultiplets
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, with pairing
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=1
and phases arg
(Z(\gamma_{1}))>\text{arg}(Z(\gamma_{2}))
. The spectrum generator then reads

\mathbb{S}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{2}}\ .

(3.18)

At the wall of marginal stability these two states align and in the outside region their ordering is changed to arg
(Z(\gamma_{1}))<\text{arg}(Z(\gamma_{2}))
. We therefore have to permute the symplectomorphisms
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{i}}
in the spectrum generator and for
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=1
this permutation is given by the primitive wall-crossing formula

\mathbb{S}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{2}}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{2}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}}\ .

(3.19)

We therefore find an additional bound state
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
in the region outside of the wall, which is a BPS hypermultiplet.

Example 3.20
.

Recall from Example
3.7
that the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{\infty}
of the pure
SU(2)
gauge theory also has one wall of marginal stability around
u=0
with two hypermultiplets
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in the interior region. However, compared to Example
3.17
, now their pairing is
\langle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\rangle=2
. Crossing the wall of marginal stability then gives the formula

\mathbb{S}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{2}}=\varPi_{2}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}^{-2}\,\varPi_{1}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}\ ,

(3.21)

where

\displaystyle\varPi_{1}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}=\prod_{k\geq 0}^{\curvearrowleft}\,\mathcal{K}_{(k+1)\,\gamma_{1}+k\,\gamma_{2}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\varPi_{2}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}=\prod_{k\geq 0}^{\curvearrowright}\,\mathcal{K}_{k\,\gamma_{1}+(k+1)\,\gamma_{2}}\ .

(3.22)

This shows that, in addition to the state
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
which is a BPS vector multiplet in this case, there are two infinite families given by
(k+1)\,\gamma_{1}+k\,\gamma_{2}
and
k\,\gamma_{1}+(k+1)\,\gamma_{2}
for
k\geq 0
which are hypermultiplets.

For
SU(2)
theories, besides the pairings one and two encountered in Examples
3.17
and
3.20
, the only other electric-magnetic pairing that should occur in wall-crossings is between mutually local charges, i.e.
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0
, in which case no new bound states are formed, and the symplectomorphisms
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
commute. This is particularly relevant for flavour states
\gamma_{\rm f}
, which are mutally local with all other BPS states and cannot be detected by the wall-crossing formula. Pairings larger than two are not expected to occur, as they would lead to “wild” spectra
[
Gal+13
]
.

Negative pairings should only occur in terms of the reverse orderings of wall-crossing formulas such as (
3.19
) and (
3.21
), as they would otherwise violate the no-exotics theorem
[
GMN13
,
Gal+13
]
. In fact, it can be argued from Denef’s multicentre equations that primitive wall-crossings with negative pairings cannot occur
[
Gal+13
]
. These equations describe BPS bound states of D-branes in supergravity and string theory settings
[
Den02
,
DM11
]
, but as shown in
[
Gal+13
]
a four-dimensional field theory limit of them should be applicable to BPS states in
{\mathcal{N}}=2
field theories.

Consider a set of
N
BPS states
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}
placed at points
\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Denef’s equations give a sufficient condition for this collection to again form a BPS configuration, namely

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle j=1}}{{\scriptstyle j\neq i}}}^{N}\,\frac{\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle}{|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|}=2\,\text{Im}\big({\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\,\vartheta}\,Z(\gamma_{i})\big)\ ,

(3.23)

for
i=1,\dots,N
, where
\vartheta=\text{arg}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,Z(\gamma_{i})\big)
.
Now consider two BPS states with pairing
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle<0
, where
\text{arg}(Z(\gamma))>\text{arg}(Z(\gamma^{\prime}))
and no other symplectomorphisms lie between
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
in the spectrum generator. By approaching the wall where the central charges of
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
align, this would lead to a wall-crossing with negative pairing. Then using (
3.23
) it can be shown that
\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}
is a BPS state as well, thereby violating the initial assumption of having no further symplectomorphism factors in between
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
. In fact, from this argument we expect to see exactly the states that would be created by crossing the wall from the other side with pairing
\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\rangle>0
.

In Section
5.5
we will discuss how negative pairing wall-crossings in the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory can be interpreted in terms of the reverse orderings of the pairing two wall-crossing formula, and we identify explicitly some of the bound states that we need for this interpretation.

3.5
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
Spectrum

Let us now summarise some of the known features of the BPS spectrum of the rank one
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory, following
[
SW97
,
Fer97
,
Ali+14
,
Lon15
]
. Recall that the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory can be regarded as an interpolation between the
{\mathcal{N}}=4
and the pure
{\mathcal{N}}=2
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theories, to which it respectively reduces by taking the limit in which the adjoint hypermultiplet mass
m
is sent to zero or decoupled. For
m\to\infty
, the complete spectrum of the pure
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory was described in Example
3.7
. For
m=0
, the BPS spectrum of
{\mathcal{N}}=4
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory consists of any dyon of primitive electromagnetic charge, i.e. all charges

\displaystyle\gamma_{[p,q]}=p\,A+q\,B\ ,

(3.24)

where
(p,q)
are relatively prime integers and
(A,B)
is the canonical symplectic homology basis for the torus of modulus
\tau_{0}
(cf. Section
2
); in particular, the spectrum is not finitely generated. This is a consequence of S-duality: by Bézout’s identity, any such dyon
\gamma_{[p,q]}
is generated by an application of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
to the primitive monopole state with charge
\gamma_{[0,1]}
. These BPS states have mass
M\big(\gamma_{[p,q]}\big)=\big|Z\big(\gamma_{[p,q]}\big)\big|=\sqrt{u_{0}}\,|p+\tau_{0}\,q|
.

One might expect that S-duality of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory could be similarly exploited to analyse the spectrum, but this is not so: whereas in the massless limit there are no walls of marginal stability where BPS bound states can form or decay, there are plenty of walls in the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{m}
for
m\neq 0
and the action of S-duality on the modulus
u
(cf. Section
2
) generally maps between different chambers of
\mathscr{B}_{m}
; later on we shall encounter many explicit instances of such walls and chambers. As one continuously varies the bare mass
m
and coupling
\tau_{0}
, and uses the renormalization group flow, the change in spectrum from
m=\infty
to
m=0
is continuous
[
Fer97
]
. Hence all BPS states of the pure
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory are also in the spectrum of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory, whereas dyons
\gamma_{[p,q]}
are unstable for
m\neq 0
when
(p,q)
have a non-trivial common divisor
[
SW97
]
. The latter feature restricts the number of walls of marginal stability that one must consider to study the eventual decays of BPS states; for example, the primitive monopole cannot decay for
m\neq 0
outside the wall that is inherited from the pure
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory. In
[
SW97
,
Fer97
]
it is shown that BPS states with
any
magnetic (or electric) charges exist in the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
spectrum in the region of the Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{m}
outside the wall of marginal stability of the
W
-boson, which however have mass larger than
m
and so decouple in
\mathscr{B}_{\infty}
. The problem then is to determine the extent to which
{\mathcal{N}}=4
BPS states destabilize and disappear from the spectrum as the mass increases from
m=0
.

The complexity of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
spectrum was exemplified in
[
Ali+14
]
where it was shown that there exists two vector particles, which are mutually non-local, for any choice of central charges. These lead to stable vector states that could form a highly complicated spectrum of bound states, as multiple accumulation rays (one at each vector state) in the region of the central charge plane between the two vector multiplets could be arbitrarily “wild”: the additional wall-crossings of vector multiplets will produce some highly complicated spectrum with infinitely many vector states. We shall encounter precisely this behaviour in our numerical explorations of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
spectrum in Section
5.4.1
, as well as further complexities throughout Section
5
. Geometrically, this is the phenomenon of “accumulation of accumulation rays" (and higher iterations thereof) in the central charge plane (see
[
CLT20
, Section 5.6.1]
), which occurs for ultraviolet curves
C
such as the once-punctured torus or Riemann surfaces without punctures, and is measured by the Cantor-Bendixson rank of the quadratic differential
\lambda^{\otimes 2}

[
Aul18
]
. In Example
4.10
we shall mention an algebraic interpretation of this “wild” behaviour in terms of the representation theory of the associated BPS quiver.

Generally, the spectrum generator for
SU(2)
theories of class
\mathcal{S}
can also be constructed by using just one framed ideal triangulation
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
at fixed generic angle
\vartheta
. This was done by Longhi in
[
Lon15
]
for the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory following the prescription in
[
GMN13b
]
. Choose a symplectic basis of one-cycles
\{\gamma_{i}=(\gamma_{\rm e}^{i},\gamma_{\rm m}^{i})\}_{i=1,2,3}
for the homology group
H_{1}(\Sigma_{u;m},\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
Seiberg-Witten curve. The idea then is to determine the spectrum generator by its action on the basis of Fock-Goncharov coordinates
\mathcal{X}_{\gamma_{i}}
through

\mathcal{X}_{\gamma_{i}}^{\vartheta+\pi}=\mathbb{S}\mathcal{X}_{\gamma_{i}}^{\vartheta}=\mathbb{S}_{i}\,\mathcal{X}_{\gamma_{i}}^{\vartheta}\ .

(3.25)

The spectrum generator can be obtained from
\mathscr{T}_{\vartheta}
by using the fact that the resulting triangulation at
\vartheta+\pi
is the original triangulation but with the entire framing popped. The action of this ‘omnipop’ can then be computed directly.

To find the spectrum, we need to find a decomposition in terms of symplectomorphisms
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
as given in (
3.16
). This can be arbitrarily difficult to do, but sometimes an ansatz for the spectrum can be found and then checked with the spectrum generator. For the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory this is justified by the observation that, even at strong coupling, no new BPS states appear other than the ones expected from perturbation theory around all of its phases
[
SW97
]
. In this case Longhi uses in
[
Lon15
]
a semi-classical ansatz on a very specific subset of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
Coulomb branch
\mathscr{B}_{m}
, namely on the walls of marginal stability defined by

\mathscr{E}_{i}=\Big\{u\in\mathscr{B}_{m}\ \Big|\ \frac{Z(\gamma_{i};u)}{Z(\gamma_{\rm f};u)}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \ ,\ \ \text{arg}\big(Z(\gamma_{i+1};u)\big)<\text{arg}\big(Z(\gamma_{i-1};u)\big)\Big\}\ ,

(3.26)

where the index
i=1,2,3
is read cyclically modulo
3
.
On these walls one of the basis charges aligns with the flavour charge
\gamma_{\rm f}=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}
, which corresponds to the adjoint hypermultiplet of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory with bare mass
m
and constant central charge
Z(\gamma_{\rm f};u)=m
throughout

. This does not lead to any wall-crossing that the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula could detect, since
\gamma_{\rm f}
has zero pairing with any other state. Hence the BPS spectrum is unambiguously defined on the walls
\mathscr{E}_{i}
.

A semi-classical analysis of the field theory yields hypermultiplets
\gamma_{i\pm 1}
, as well as a vector state
\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}
with the usual families of hypermultiplets
\gamma_{i\pm 1}+n\,(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})
in the
m\to\infty
spectrum from Example
3.20
. In addition to these, there are hypermultiplets with electromagnetic charges
\gamma_{\rm f}+\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}
and
\gamma_{\rm f}-(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})=\gamma_{i}
. The corresponding spectrum generator is
[
Lon15
,
Lon18
]

\mathbb{S}=\varPi_{2}^{\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i-1}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\rm f}-(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})}\,\mathcal{K}^{-2}_{\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\rm f}+(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})}\,\varPi_{1}^{\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i-1}}\ ,

(3.27)

where the symplectomorphisms
\varPi_{j}^{\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i-1}}
for
j=1,2
correspond to infinite families of BPS hypermultiplets with charges
\gamma_{j}+k\,(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})
,
\mathcal{K}^{-2}_{\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}}
to a BPS vector multiplet of charge
\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}
, and the remaining
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma_{\rm f}\pm(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})}
to two more hypermultiplets.
9
9

9

The infinite mass limit of the spectrum on the walls of marginal stability
\mathscr{E}_{i}
can be taken as follows. Since
\gamma_{\rm f}
and
\gamma_{i}
align, sending
m
to infinity does not change the phase of
Z(\gamma_{i})

\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}+\gamma_{\rm f}

\gamma_{\rm f}

\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}+\gamma_{\rm f}

\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1}
and the hypermultiplets
\gamma_{i\pm 1}+k\,(\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i+1})

gauge theory. For general regions of the Coulomb branch

are ill-defined in the massless limit to the
{\mathcal{N}}=4

We will compute this spectrum through an algebraic method in Section

4
\bm{{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}}
Spectrum from Representation Theory

In this section we will construct the spectrum of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory on the walls defined in (
3.26
) by using quiver representation theory methods, reproducing in this way the results of
[
Lon15
]
, summarised in Section
3.5
, from an algebraic perspective that is tailored to our later investigations. We will first briefly review the general theory of BPS quivers and the mutation method from
[
Ali+13
,

4.1
BPS Quivers

We can compute the BPS spectrum of the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory from BPS quivers. A quiver is a directed graph, i.e. a set of nodes with directed arrows between them. A quiver can be associated to the BPS spectrum of an
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theory, where the nodes
\{\bullet_{i}\}
correspond to certain charges
\{\gamma_{i}\}
and the arrows to their intersection pairings. A linear representation of a quiver is an assignment of a complex vector space to each node and a linear map between vector spaces for each arrow. Any BPS state will then define a space of quiver representations, characterized uniquely by the dimension vector
\vec{n}=(n_{i})
where
n_{i}
is the dimension of the vector space at node
\bullet_{i}
, together with the moduli of linear maps
{\sf a}^{\vec{n}}_{ji}
represented by
n_{j}{\times}n_{i}
complex matrices for each arrow
\bullet_{i}\longrightarrow\bullet_{j}
.

To assign a quiver to a BPS spectrum, we first fix the cone of particles, i.e. a choice of half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\vartheta}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
centred on the ray
\{z\in\mathbb{C}~|~\text{arg}(z)=\vartheta\}
such that no central charge lies on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}_{\vartheta}={\mathrm{e}}^{\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\vartheta}\,\mathbb{R}
.
10
10

10

This is not always possible. In fact for some
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theories the BPS rays are dense in the circle and any choice of upper half-plane will have charges on its boundary. The spectra of such theories are not finitely generated. An example is
{\mathcal{N}}=4
supersymmetric Yang-Mills theory, and more generally any class
\mathcal{S}
theory whose ultraviolet curve
C
is a Riemann surface without punctures.
Any state
\gamma
whose central charge
Z(\gamma)
lies in this charge plane is considered to be a particle, while
-\gamma
is considered as the corresponding antiparticle. We can then find a positive integral basis
\{\gamma_{i}\}
for the BPS states and write the electromagnetic charge
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
of any BPS particle as

\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}=\sum_{i}\,n_{i}\,\gamma_{i}\qquad\mbox{with}\quad n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\ ,

(4.1)

with corresponding central charge

\displaystyle Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)=\sum_{i}\,n_{i}\,Z(\gamma_{i};u)=\oint_{\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}}\,\lambda

(4.2)

in
\mathbb{H}_{\vartheta}
. This choice of basis is in fact unique.

For a theory with gauge group of rank
r
and with
n_{\rm f}
flavours, the local system
\Gamma
has rank
2r+n_{\rm f}
, and we thus need
2r+n_{\rm f}
basis elements
\gamma_{i}
.
11
11

11

Note that we ask only for a basis for all BPS particles, not a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis that spans the lattice
\Gamma
.
From a physical perspective, we can think of the
\gamma_{i}
as BPS hypermultiplets and of the particles formed by sums of these as composite objects or bound states. This point of view is reinforced by the fact that the basis states
\gamma_{i}
are always BPS hypermultiplets of the theory (see Example
4.7
below).

Having fixed a particle cone and hence obtained the basis
\{\gamma_{i}\}
, we define the BPS quiver as follows:

\diamond

To each
\gamma_{i}
we assign a node
\bullet_{i}
.

\diamond

For each pair
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
we compute the electric-magnetic pairing
\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle
, and we draw
\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle_{+}
arrows from node
\bullet_{i}
to node
\bullet_{j}
.

There is a small subtlety with the second point. Since the direction of arrows is determined by the sign of the electric-magnetic pairing, one does not obtain quivers in this way where arrows between two nodes have opposite directions. This subtlety can be important for certain theories (see
[
Ali+14
]
), but it will be irrelevant for us. Figure

4.2
\Pi
-Stability and Mutations

With the BPS quiver at hand, we can map the problem of computing the BPS spectrum to a problem in representation theory: stable BPS states of the four-dimensional field theory correspond bijectively to
\Pi
-stable representations of the BPS quiver. Any BPS state (
4.1
) defines a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of linear representations of the BPS quiver with dimension vector
\vec{n}=(n_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2r+n_{\rm f}}
given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\vec{n})=\bigoplus_{\bullet_{i}\rightarrow\bullet_{j}}\,{\rm Hom}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}},\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}\big)\ ,

(4.3)

where the direct sum runs through all arrows of the quiver. The complex algebraic gauge group

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\vec{n})=\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{2r+n_{\rm f}}\,GL(n_{i},\mathbb{C})\Big)\,\Big/\,\mathbb{C}^{\times}

(4.4)

acts naturally by basis changes of the vector spaces
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
and by conjugating elements of (
4.3
) as bifundamental fields. The corresponding gauge orbits are precisely the isomorphism classes of representations of the state with charge
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
, which are parameterized by the quotient variety
\mathscr{M}(\vec{n})=\big[\mathscr{R}(\vec{n})/\mathscr{G}(\vec{n})\big]
of complex dimension

\displaystyle d(\vec{n})=1+\sum_{\bullet_{i}\rightarrow\bullet_{j}}\,n_{i}\,n_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{2r+n_{\rm f}}\,n_{i}^{2}\ .

(4.5)

A homomorphism from a quiver representation
\gamma
with dimension vector
\vec{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2r+n_{\rm f}}
to
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
is a set of linear maps
\mathsf{f}_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
for each node
\bullet_{i}
which commute with the linear maps representing the arrows
\bullet_{i}\longrightarrow\bullet_{j}
of the BPS quiver, that is,
\mathsf{f}_{j}\,{\sf a}^{\vec{m}}_{ji}={\sf a}_{ji}^{\vec{n}}\,\mathsf{f}_{i}
. In this way the representations of the BPS quiver form a
\mathbb{C}
-linear abelian category. If the maps
\mathsf{f}_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
are all injective, we say that
\gamma
is a subrepresentation of the quiver representation
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
; concretely this means that
m_{i}\leq n_{i}
for each node
\bullet_{i}
and the linear maps
\mathsf{a}_{ji}^{\vec{m}}:\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{C}^{m_{j}}
are the restrictions of the linear maps
\mathsf{a}^{\vec{n}}_{ji}:\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n_{j}}
.

The quiver representation
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}
is called
\Pi
-stable if all proper subrepresentations
\gamma
have central charges satisfying the strict inequality

\displaystyle{\rm arg}\big(Z(\gamma;u)\big)<{\rm arg}\big(Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)\big)\ .

(4.6)

This condition ensures that the BPS particle with electromagnetic charge
\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}}=\gamma+\cdots
cannot decay into one of its constituent BPS states with charge
\gamma
. The
\Pi
-stable representations are the supersymmetric ground states of the low energy effective
{\mathcal{N}}=4
quantum mechanics of a BPS particle based on the BPS quiver, whose interactions are governed by a
\mathscr{G}(\vec{n})
-invariant holomorphic superpotential
\mathcal{W}
which is a formal sum of traces over oriented cycles of the quiver
[
DFR05
,
Ali+14
]
. The extrema of the superpotential give F-flatness conditions
\partial\mathcal{W}=0
which are realised as relations for the BPS quiver, that is, linear combinations of oriented paths obtained by composing arrows of the quiver.

The stability condition on quiver representations captures wall-crossing phenomena as well: as we vary over the
u
-plane we may encounter walls of marginal stability, where the arguments of
Z(\gamma;u)
and
Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)
agree and a previously
\Pi
-stable representation decays. There can also be wall-crossings of the second kind, across the types of walls discussed in Section
3.2
, which occur when a BPS ray
Z(\gamma_{i};u)
rotates out of the specified half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\vartheta}
. Then the description as a quiver representation breaks down, since there is no longer a positive integral basis. This is fixed by compensating the rotation with a quiver mutation. We will return to this point momentarily.

When the F-term equations are trivially satisfied (for example by setting the vacuum expectation values of some chiral fields to zero), the
\Pi
-stable representations with dimension vector
\vec{n}
form an open subset of the moduli space
\mathscr{M}(\vec{n})
of the expected complex dimension
d(\vec{n})
given by (
4.5
); in general, this dimension is lowered by the number of non-redundant F-flatness constraints. The constraint
d(\vec{n})\geq 0
on the dimension vector already imposes constraints on the allowed combinations (
4.1
), and the resulting BPS state is a particle of spin
\frac{1}{2}\,\big(d(\vec{n})+1\big)

[
Ali+14
]
. For
SU(2)
theories (
r=1
) only hypermultiplets (with
d(\vec{n})=0
) and vector multiplets (with
d(\vec{n})=1
) appear in the BPS spectrum.

After finding the allowed dimension vectors
\vec{n}
, one may then determine which representations correspond to one-particle bound states in the four-dimensional field theory. Their counterparts in quiver theory are Schur representations, that is, representations of the BPS quiver with a one-dimensional space of endomorphisms
[
DFR05a
,
FM00
,
Fio06
]
. In particular, Schur representations are indecomposable, and
\Pi
-stable representations are always Schur representations, but the converses are not necessarily true. By focusing on Schur modules we avoid subtleties associated with “threshold” bound states, as then the set of integers
(n_{i})
is relatively prime; otherwise, if there is a common divisor
d>1
, then
Z(\gamma_{\textrm{\tiny BPS}};u)
aligns with the central charges of the direct sum of
d
copies of a possibly destabilising proper subrepresentation with dimension vector
\frac{1}{d}\,\vec{n}
. Finally, imposing
\Pi
-stability effectively resolves any fixed point singularities and also compactifies the moduli space
\mathscr{M}(\vec{n})
, giving a well-defined
L^{2}
-cohomology which quantizes the moduli space and computes the BPS spectrum.

Example 4.7

The simplest BPS states are always given by the basis charges
\gamma_{i}
themselves, which are trivially
\Pi
-stable everywhere in the Coulomb moduli space

\mathbb{C}
at the node

and all arrow morphisms set to zero. The F-term equations are then always trivially satisfied, the expected dimension (

However, in contrast to the other two quivers shown in Figure

This complexity makes the determination of a complete list of all BPS states in the spectrum of the

theory an extremely arduous task.

In general,
\Pi
-stability of quiver representations is difficult to check even for relatively simple quivers. A simpler and more algorithmic way to compute the BPS spectrum is via the mutation method. This consists of elementary changes of quivers that occur when we vary the choice of half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\vartheta}
. By convention it is rotated clockwise, and therefore at some point the leftmost charge
\gamma
leaves the half-plane and is replaced by its antiparticle
-\gamma
which becomes the new rightmost charge. The leftmost and rightmost charges are always basis elements of the positive integral basis for the respective half-planes, but with this replacement the basis is no longer a positive integral basis. We therefore have to perform a basis change, which is called a mutation on
\gamma
. A mutation generally produces a different but physically equivalent BPS quiver, which acts as a one-dimensional Seiberg duality on the corresponding
{\mathcal{N}}=4
quiver quantum mechanics and preserves the BPS spectrum.

Let
\gamma_{i}
be the BPS particle that rotates out of the plane, and let
\gamma_{j}
be any other charge with
j\neq i
. Then the mutation is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\longmapsto\gamma_{i}^{\prime}=-\gamma_{i}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{j}\longmapsto\gamma_{j}^{\prime}=\gamma_{j}+\langle\gamma_{j},\gamma_{i}\rangle_{+}\,\gamma_{i}\ ,

(4.14)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{+}
is again the positive of part of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as in (
3.15
).
We can continue doing this until we have rotated the half-plane by
\pi
. At this stage every BPS particle has been leftmost and has therefore been mutated on. We have thus found the whole spectrum. It is worth noting that the quiver after a rotation by
\pi
is the same quiver as the original one, but now labelled by the antiparticles
\{-\gamma_{i}\}
. It can often be helpful to start from that quiver and perform inverse mutations, which use the rule

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\longmapsto\gamma_{i}^{\prime}=-\gamma_{i}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{j}\longmapsto\gamma_{j}^{\prime}=\gamma_{j}+\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\rangle_{+}\,\gamma_{i}\ .

(4.15)

In Section
4.3
below we will use these methods to construct the spectrum of the
{\mathcal{N}}{=}2^{*}
theory on the walls given in Section
3.5
. In Section
4.4
we will then have a look at the ideal triangulations involved on the ultraviolet curve
C
to gain an understanding of how the states correspond to cycles on the Seiberg-Witten curve
\Sigma_{u;m}
.

We will construct this spectrum using mutations of BPS quivers, where our starting point is the Markov quiver for the
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
theory constructed in Figure

. It consists of three states
\gamma_{i}
with pairing
\langle\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}\rangle=2
. We note that the Markov quiver is invariant under a cyclic relabelling
\gamma_{i}\mapsto\gamma_{i\pm 1}
, which generates the action of a
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
automorphism of the category of quiver representations on the BPS spectrum. Therefore the analysis on the wall
\mathscr{E}_{3}
easily translates to the other walls
\mathscr{E}_{1}
and
\mathscr{E}_{2}
in (
3.26
).

We start the mutation method by mutating on the state
\gamma
with largest
\arg(Z(\gamma))
, which is
\gamma_{2}
. Applying the mutation rule (
4.14
) we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\longmapsto\gamma_{1}^{\prime}=\gamma_{1}+2\gamma_{2}\ ,\quad\gamma_{2}\longmapsto\gamma_{2}^{\prime}=-\gamma_{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{3}\longmapsto\gamma_{3}^{\prime}=\gamma_{3}\ .

(4.17)

The charge
\gamma
with largest
\arg(Z(\gamma))
is now
\gamma_{1}+2\gamma_{2}
, as expected. We can define a quiver consisting of states
\gamma_{i}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
, which is the Markov quiver after
k
mutations with a renaming to exchange
\gamma_{1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
after each mutation in order to preserve the pairing
\langle\gamma^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}_{i},\gamma^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}_{i+1}\rangle=2
. The resulting quiver is then labelled by

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}:=\gamma_{1}-k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\ ,\quad\gamma_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}:=\gamma_{2}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{3}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}:=\gamma_{3}\ .

(4.18)

With the renaming of charges after each mutation,
\gamma_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
always has largest
\arg(Z(\gamma))
for a given
k
, and since
\langle\gamma_{i}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}},\gamma_{i+1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}\rangle=2
, mutating it gives

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\gamma_{1}-k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+2\,\big(\gamma_{2}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\big)=\gamma_{2}+(k+1)\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\ ,

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto-\gamma_{2}-k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})=\gamma_{1}-(k+1)\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\ ,

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\gamma_{3}\ .

(4.19)

Hence
k
is increased by one and
\gamma_{1}
is exchanged with
\gamma_{2}
; in particular,
\gamma_{1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k+1)$}}=-\gamma_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
. The family (
4.18
) contains states of the form
\gamma_{2}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
, as well as the state
\gamma_{3}
which is not mutated on. These assemble for all
k\geq 0
into the same BPS quiver, reflecting the well-known fact that the mutation equivalence class of the Markov quiver consists of a single element.

To understand the family
\gamma_{1}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
it is helpful to start from the other direction. The starting point is then the initial quiver but labelled by the antiparticles
\tilde{\gamma}{}_{i}=-\gamma_{i}
. We can then mutate again on states, but this time using the inverse mutation rule (
4.15
).
The calculation then carries through as before and we can define a similar family of charges
\tilde{\gamma}{}_{i}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
with
\langle\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}_{i},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}_{i+1}\rangle=2
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}{}_{1}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle:=-\big(\gamma_{1}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\big)\ ,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}{}_{2}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle:=-\big(\gamma_{2}-k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\big)\ ,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}{}_{3}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\displaystyle:=-\gamma_{3}\ ,

(4.20)

where
\tilde{\gamma}{}_{3}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}=\tilde{\gamma}{}_{3}
does not change under the mutations. In this way we find the states
\gamma_{1}+k\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
and mutate on them.

There is one missing piece to the spectrum shown in Figure
7
. From either direction, the quark given by
\gamma_{3}
appeared as a node of the quiver, but we never actually mutated on it. This is because it aligns with the vector multiplet
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
which is the result of the juggle that relates the
k\to\infty
limits of (
4.18
) and (
4.3
). Whereas the particle
\gamma_{3}
is guaranteed to be a stable BPS hypermultiplet as it is always a node of the Markov quiver (cf. Example
4.7
), the stability of
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
does not follow from the present analysis as it would formally require infinitely many mutations. We must therefore resort directly to representation theory on the accumulation ray.

The quiver representation
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
has dimension vector
(1,1,0)
with two non-trivial linear maps
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
representing the arrows
\bullet_{2}\rightrightarrows\bullet_{1}
, which can be characterized by a pair of complex numbers
\vec{\sf a}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
; the F-flatness conditions (
4.10
) are trivially satisfied (cf. Example
4.10
). For generic
\vec{\sf a}
, an easy analysis of commutative diagrams shows that the only proper subrepresentation is
\gamma_{1}
. On the wall
\mathscr{E}_{3}
, the phase of
Z(\gamma_{1})
is smaller than the phase of
Z(\gamma_{2})
, and so
\arg(Z(\gamma_{1}))<\arg(Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}))<\arg(Z(\gamma_{2}))
. Hence
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
is
\Pi
-stable for generic
\vec{\sf a}
. On the other hand,
\gamma_{2}
is a destabilizing proper subrepresentation when
\vec{\sf a}=\vec{0}
, and so the moduli space of
\Pi
-stable representations of
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
is
\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}\big)\big/\,\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
, the spin
1
moduli space of a vector multiplet
[
Ali+14
]
.

Finally, while the mutation method accounts for the totality of BPS states on either side of the accumulation ray in the central charge plane containing
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
, we still need to account for the BPS state
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}=2(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+\gamma_{3}
along the ray in Figure
7
. The stability analysis is a bit lengthy in this case. Since
\Pi
-stable representations are Schur modules, we begin by considering the moduli of representations of the Markov quiver with dimension vector
(2,2,1)
that correspond to Schur modules. Such representations are parameterized by row vectors
\mathsf{a}_{31},\mathsf{b}_{31}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
, column vectors
\mathsf{a}_{23},\mathsf{b}_{23}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and square matrices
\mathsf{a}_{12},\mathsf{b}_{12}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

An endomorphism of a generic representation of this form is given by a complex number
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
acting at the node
\bullet_{3}
, and a pair of matrices
\mathsf{S}_{1},\mathsf{S}_{2}\in GL(2,\mathbb{C})
acting at the nodes
\bullet_{1}
and
\bullet_{2}
respectively. Commutativity of all relevant diagrams results in six equations

\displaystyle\mathsf{a}_{31}\,\mathsf{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\mathsf{a}_{31}\ ,\quad\mathsf{S}_{2}\,\mathsf{a}_{23}=\lambda\,\mathsf{a}_{23}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathsf{S}_{1}\,\mathsf{a}_{12}=\mathsf{a}_{12}\,\mathsf{S}_{2}\ ,

\displaystyle\mathsf{b}_{31}\,\mathsf{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\mathsf{b}_{31}\ ,\quad\mathsf{S}_{2}\,\mathsf{b}_{23}=\lambda\,\mathsf{b}_{23}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathsf{S}_{1}\,\mathsf{b}_{12}=\mathsf{b}_{12}\,\mathsf{S}_{2}\ .

(4.21)

The condition for
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}
to be a Schur representation is that the equations (
4.3
) have the unique solution
\mathsf{S}_{1}=\mathsf{S}_{2}=\lambda\,{\mathbbm{1}}_{2}
. This rules out representations with
\mathsf{a}_{i,i+1}=\mathsf{b}_{i,i+1}=0
for more than one
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

Let us next look at what constraints are imposed by
\Pi
-stability of the resulting Schur representations. For this, note that the only potential destabilizing subrepresentations of
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}
on the wall
\mathscr{E}_{3}
are the constituent BPS states whose central charges lie either on or to the left of the accumulation ray shown in Figure
7
. These are the states with electromagnetic charges
\gamma_{2}
,
\gamma_{3}
,
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{1}+2\gamma_{2}
. An easy analysis of commutative diagrams shows that
\gamma_{2}
can only be a subrepresentation if
\mathsf{a}_{12}=\mathsf{b}_{12}=0
, while
\gamma_{3}
can only be a subrepresentation if
\mathsf{a}_{23}=\mathsf{b}_{23}=0
. These subrepresentations can thus be discarded if we require at least one of the arrows of
\bullet_{2}\rightrightarrows\bullet_{1}
and of
\bullet_{3}\rightrightarrows\bullet_{2}
to be non-zero maps.

(1,1,0)
can be expressed through a commutative diagram

So far we have restricted our analysis of the BPS spectrum of the rank one

5.1
Perturbing the Spectrum

For
u\in\mathscr{E}_{3}
, we keep the central charge
Z_{2}=Z(\gamma_{2};u)
constant and perturb
Z_{1}=Z(\gamma_{1};u)
by

Z_{1}\longmapsto Z_{1}+\delta\,Z_{2}\ ,

(5.1)

where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a small parameter. This perturbs the phase of
Z_{1}
by a small positive amount, and thus closes the wedge between
Z_{2}
and
Z_{1}
as
Z_{1}
rotates anticlockwise. Since
Z_{\rm f}=Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3})=m
is constant on the moduli space, this change of
Z_{1}
while keeping
Z_{2}
constant perturbs
Z_{3}=Z(\gamma_{3};u)
as well by

Z_{3}\longmapsto Z_{3}-\delta\,Z_{2}\ .

(5.2)

Hence
Z_{3}
rotates slightly clockwise. In a similar manner the central charges
Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2};u)
and
Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}\,;u)
are changed by
\delta\,Z_{2}
, and rotate slightly anticlockwise. We should however keep in mind that these two vectors have different magnitudes, so adding a small piece affects them differently. Therefore the central charge
Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2};u)
rotates further than
Z(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}\,;u)
and the two charges become separated. We should also note that the bound states
\gamma_{i}+n\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})
involve multiple copies of
\gamma_{1}
and are affected as well, rotating slightly anticlockwise. This movement of charges is shown in Figure

.

5.4.2
Varying the Mass and Perturbation

In Section
5.4.1
we explored the spectrum at a specific choice of the mass parameter
m
and of the perturbation away from the wall
\mathscr{E}_{3}

\delta

\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

\gamma_{3}
interacting with the same number of states
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
would require a larger choice of
\delta
. On the other hand, even with fewer states
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
between them, the bound states that result from interactions of
\gamma_{3}
now stay closer to
\gamma_{3}
, which leads to overlapping bound state families, and therefore to secondary and higher states as we saw in Section

.

We can also vary
\delta

5.4.1
. Since the states
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

increases the number of states
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
that fit between
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}

\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}

For any choice of

, most of the complicated behaviour seems to occur close to

illuminate this somewhat, as the wall-crossing of
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}

m

at low level cutoffs, since bound states of
\gamma_{3}
and

5.5
Reorganising the Negative Pairing Wall-Crossings

We have encountered wall-crossings of the form
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\cdots\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
with negative pairings
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=-2
. As explained earlier, such wall-crossings should not occur in rank one
{\mathcal{N}}=2
theories as they violate the no-exotics theorem. Furthermore, Denef’s equations (
3.23
) show that there should be further symplectomorphism factors separating
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, which therefore prevent the primitive wall-crossing with negative pairing.

A natural way to make sense of these wall-crossings is as the reverse ordering of the wall-crossing formula for
\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma\rangle=2
, namely

\varPi_{2}^{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma}\,\mathcal{K}^{-2}_{\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}}\,\varPi_{1}^{\gamma^{\prime},\gamma}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\ .

(5.24)

This requires the appropriate bound states of
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
to appear as BPS particles as well. Indeed, following the Denef equations (
3.23
), these states should be BPS particles in the theory when coming close to the wall
\mathscr{W}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
from the side where
\arg(Z(\gamma^{\prime}))>\arg(Z(\gamma))
.

This leads us to propose a variety of states which are in the BPS spectrum, that were missed in our earlier analysis. In this section we show how at least some of these states are certainly in the spectrum and can be identified with other bound states resulting from earlier wall-crossings. The states that we manage to match up are for the most part from the
(k|l)
-families of states with
l>1
, which explains why they were missed in the numerical explorations of Section
5.4
.

We thereby identify a few of the seemingly missing states by explicitly matching up secondary and tertiary bound states. The existence of the remaining states in the spectrum is however highly non-trivial and poses interesting constraints on the BPS spectrum. For example, in Section
5.5.1
we will see that it relates the behaviour of high level secondary states at
k^{\prime}<k
to the spectrum in between
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}
and
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}
near the wall
\mathscr{W}(\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}})\subset\mathscr{B}_{m}
. It would be interesting to gain further insight into this and identify the pattern of required states in more detail. We should emphasize that this behaviour is highly unusual for rank one
{\mathcal{N}}=2
field theories at least: bound states are formed as hypermultiplets accompanying a vector multiplet which at some point on the Coulomb branch leave the spectrum at an entirely different wall-crossing, where they are reorganised as hypermultiplets accompanying different vector bound states of different constituents.

We perform this analysis for the wall-crossings with the pairings (
5.21
) which involve two hypermultiplets, as well as the wall-crossings involving vector multiplets with pairings (
5.23
). We further apply a similar analysis to
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}
which undergoes wall-crossings with
\langle\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}\rangle=-2
, and likewise can be interpreted as a reversion of the primitive pairing two Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula.

5.5.1
Wall-Crossings of Hypermultiplets

We will first discuss the case of a hypermultiplet wall-crossing with negative pairing which we encountered in our Python exploration in (
5.21
). For this first case the pairing in question is

\langle\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}\rangle=-2\ .

(5.25)

The expected bound states from the Denef formula are the vector multiplet
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}
as well as the hypermultiplets
(n+1)\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+n\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}
and
n\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+(n+1)\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}
for
n\geq 0
.

Writing out the vector multiplet more explicitly in terms of our original basis
\gamma_{i}
, it is given by

\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}=3\,\gamma_{1}+(3\,k-4)\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+3\,\gamma_{3}\ .

(5.26)

Because it has the same amount of charges
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{3}
, this state could result for example from a wall-crossing with
\langle\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}+1|l)$}},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime})$}}\rangle=2
which leads to the vector multiplet

\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}+1|l)$}}+\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime})$}}=(l+1)\,\gamma_{1}+(l\,k^{\prime}+l+k^{\prime})\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+(l+1)\,\gamma_{3}\ ,

(5.27)

which is indeed the same as (
5.26
) for
l=2
and
k^{\prime}=k-2
. Note that this state would be missing in the corresponding numerical calculation, since it is built from an
l=2
state, which lies beyond the cutoff of bound states
\textsf{lvlcut}=1
. This suggests that previously created bound states at lower
k
can be reinterpreted as bound states between the states involved in (
5.21
).

We can similarly look for the needed hypermultiplets, starting with

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+n\,\big(\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k|1)$}}+\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-2|-1)$}}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=(3\,n+1)\,\gamma_{1}+(3\,n\,k+k-4\,n)\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+(3\,n+2)\,\gamma_{3}\ .

(5.28)

We again consider bound states arising from
\langle\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}|l)$}},\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}-1)$}}\rangle=2
, but this time allow for arbitrary
l
. The hypermultiplets of the form
(n^{\prime}+1)\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}+1|l)$}}+n^{\prime}\,\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime})$}}
are given by

(n^{\prime}\,l+l+n^{\prime})\,\gamma_{1}+\big((n^{\prime}+1)\,l\,k^{\prime}+(n^{\prime}+1)\,l+n\,k^{\prime}\big)\,(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})+(n^{\prime}+1)\,(l+1)\,\gamma_{3}\ .

(5.29)

Equating (
5.5.1
) and (
5.29

\displaystyle l=\frac{3\,n+1-n^{\prime}}{n^{\prime}+1}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad k-k^{\prime}=\frac{7\,n+1-n^{\prime}}{3\,n+1}\ ,

(5.30)

which need to have simultaneous integer solutions with
k-k^{\prime}\geq 0
and
l\geq 0
for the states to match. In fact, we should really be interested in
l>0
as
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k^{\prime}+1|0)$}}=\gamma_{3}
which leads to trivial solutions due to notational coincidence. Similarly
k-k^{\prime}=1
gives rise to a trivial solution for
n=0
. The non-trivial solutions then correspond to
k-k^{\prime}=2
which is solved either by: (a)
(n^{\prime},l)=(0,4)
giving the

hypermultiplet of (
5.5.1
), not even as a secondary bound state but as
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k-1|4)$}}
; or by (b)
(n^{\prime},l)=(1,3)
giving the
n=2
hypermultiplet of (
5.5.1
), this time as a secondary bound state.

Appendix A
Python Algorithm for Exploring the
\bm{{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}}
Spectrum

To explore the complicated BPS spectrum of the rank one
{\mathcal{N}}=2^{*}
gauge theory away from the wall of marginal stability
\mathscr{E}_{3}
, we use an algorithm implemented in Python
[
R“”ut21
]
that perturbs the spectrum step by step, performs the necessary permutations of symplectomorphisms
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
in the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula, and then adds in any new resulting bound states.

A.1
Implementation of the Algorithm

We start by initialising the spectrum on the wall as given in Section
4.3
, where we specify as input the central charges
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
as well as the mass
m
, which is used to compute the central charge
Z_{3}
via the relation
Z_{1}+Z_{2}+Z_{3}=m
. The spectrum contains infinite families of states so we also take a cutoff
kcut
for the first families of states. Then the spectrum on the wall consists of
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
, the vector multiplet
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}
and the hypermultiplet
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{\rm f}
, as well as
\tilde{\gamma}{}^{\textrm{\tiny$(k)$}}
for
k\leq\textsf{kcut}
. In a similar manner we put a second cutoff
subcut
, which is used for the bound state families that result from the perturbation of the spectrum.

We add small perturbations of the central charges as described in Section
5.1
by

Z_{1}\longmapsto Z_{1}+\delta\,Z_{2}\ ,

(A.1)

where central charges such as
Z_{3}
that include copies of
Z_{1}
are also affected, which we keep track of by a perturbation weight assigned to every state. An electromagnetic charge
\gamma=n_{1}\,\gamma_{1}+n_{2}\,\gamma_{2}+n_{3}\,\gamma_{3}
will have
(n_{1}-n_{3})\,\delta\,Z_{2}
added to its central charge by the perturbation. We perform this perturbation in small steps
\epsilon\ll\delta
and adjust the magnitude of
\epsilon
as we go.

Ideally we would choose
\epsilon
to be so small as to only allow two states
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
to interact, so that we can apply the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula to them and add the bound states to the BPS spectrum. It turns out that this criterion for perturbations is too restrictive and we instead allow for perturbations where only two states form bound states. We will return to this issue in more detail in Appendix
A.2
below.

The full algorithm then consists of the following steps:

\diamond

Perturb the spectrum slightly by
\epsilon
.

\diamond

Check that the perturbation fulfills the criteria outlined in Appendix
A.2
below. If it does not, undo the perturbation and try again with adjusted step size.
19
19

19

Usually this means reducing the step size, but in some cases we also increase it instead.

\diamond

Permute the symplectomorphisms and add resulting bound states to the BPS spectrum.

\diamond

Repeat the procedure until the desired perturbation
\delta
is reached.

The bound states constructed in this way will always be a vector multiplet together with the usual infinite families of hypermultiplets, which are truncated to
subcut
.

A.2
Permutation Criteria

In the algorithm outlined in Appendix
A.1
, we would ideally take small enough steps
\epsilon
, so that we only need to permute two terms of the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula. In practice, however, it turns out that this is too restrictive and leads to infinite loops in the algorithm.

We instead implement permutation criteria which ensure that any occurring permutations can be broken down into simple permutations of neighbouring factors, and that the result does not depend on the order of these simple permutations. First of all, any perturbation that leads to at most one permutation is allowed. For larger permutations we encode them in the form of blocks of integers
[p_{1},p_{2},\dots]
which represent the ways in which the respective factors are moved. For example, in the block

[2,-1,-1]\ ,

(A.2)

the leftmost factor is moved two steps to the right, while the other two factors move one step each to the left.

Such blocks can be decomposed into pairwise permutations as follows. Find the first negative entry, permute the factor one step to the left, adding in any resulting bound states, and then repeat this procedure until completion. Most of the time we encounter the simple case where one state interacts with one or more other states which do not change their respective ordering. The corresponding blocks are of the form

[n,-1,-1,\dots,-1]\qquad\text{or}\qquad[1,1,\dots,1,-n]\ .

(A.3)

These decompose easily and uniquely into pairwise permutations.

Issues arise in particular blocks that contain zeroes. For example, consider the block

[2,0,-2]\ .

(A.4)

One interpretation of this block is that there are three ordered states
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime})
that at some point all align and then afterwards are ordered as
(\gamma^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\gamma)
. Alternatively, if we tried to break it down into single interactions of neighbouring states, there are two routes, starting with either
\gamma
or
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
and exchanging its position with
\gamma^{\prime}
. The decomposition into such permutations is therefore not unique and if bound states result from any such permutations, the resulting BPS spectra will differ as well. We therefore only allow such blocks if at most one of the intersection pairings
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
between the charges involved is non-zero.

This subtle point aside, we can restrict what we call the
permutation length

permlength
of a block, which is defined as

\textsf{permlength}\big([p_{1},p_{2},\dots]\big)=\text{max}(|p_{1}|,|p_{2}|,\dots)\ .

(A.5)

The only
\textsf{permlength}=1
block is
[1,-1]
. For
\textsf{permlength}=2
there are considerably more possibilities including problematic blocks such as (
A.4
). For
\textsf{permlength}\geq 3
the number of possibilities increases drastically. There are even
\textsf{permlength}=3
blocks which do not contain any zeroes, but decompose into blocks which do contain zeroes while following the decomposition procedure, for example

[3,1,-1,-3]\,\longmapsto\,[3,0,0,-3]\ .

(A.6)

We therefore check throughout the permutation process that blocks do not violate the criterion of either having no zeroes or containing at most one non-zero pairing. For almost all of our calculations it is however possible to restrict to
\textsf{permlength}=2
, which is well under control.

Another concern for pertubations is when charges align. It can happen that two or more charges end up with the same phase, either through numerical precision or accidental non-generic angle choice, or through complicated wall-crossings such as the ones anticipated for the blocks (
A.4
). We usually forbid perturbations which lead to alignment of charges and try to increase the step size to push past them. Performing consistency checks on spectra which can be computed with this turned off or on indicates that it is still a fairly safe criterion to drop, but we still try to keep it.

Summarising, the usual criteria we enforce on perturbations entail the following:

1.

No symplectomorphism is moved past more than two other symplectomorphisms.

2.

Permutations are either of the simple form (
A.3
) or involve at most one non-zero pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

3.

No states have coinciding phases.

If these criteria fail the perturbation is undone and a smaller one is performed, unless the failure was the alignment of charges, in which case a larger perturbation is attempted to push past this alignment.

5.4.1
we can usually keep these criteria, but Criterion 1 can be relaxed to
\textsf{permlength}\leq 3
and Criterion 3 can be dropped:

\textsf{subcut}=1
, we can either compute up to
k=13

k=14
if we relax either Criteria

or
3
, obtaining the same spectrum in both cases.
