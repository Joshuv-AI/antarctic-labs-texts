A revisit of the circular law
Zhidong Bai, Jiang Hu

Abstract
Consider a complex random n × n n\times n matrix 𝐗 n = ( x i ​ j ) n × n {\bf X}_{n}=(x_{ij})_{n\times n} , whose entries x i ​ j x_{ij} are independent random variables with zero means and unit variances. It is well-known that Tao and Vu (Ann Probab 38: 2023-2065, 2010) resolved the circular law conjecture, establishing that if the x i ​ j x_{ij} ’s are independent and identically distributed random variables with zero mean and unit variance, the empirical spectral distribution of 1 n ​ 𝐗 n \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n} converges almost surely to the uniform distribution over the unit disk in the complex plane as n → ∞ n\to\infty . This paper demonstrates that the circular law still holds under the more general Lindeberg’s condition: 1 n 2 ∑_i,j=1^n E |x_ij^2|I(|x_ij|> η n )→0, as n → ∞ n\to\infty . This paper is a revisit of the proof procedure of the circular law by Bai in (Ann Probab 25: 494-529, 1997). The key breakthroughs in the paper are establishing a general strong law of large numbers under Lindeberg’s condition and the uniform upper bound for the integral with respect to the smallest eigenvalues of random matrices. These advancements significantly streamline and clarify the proof of the circular law, offering a more direct and simplified approach than other existing methodologies.

1
Introduction

Suppose that
{\bf X}_{n}
is an
n\times n
matrix with entries
x_{kj}
, where
\{x_{kj},\ k,j=1,2,\dots\}
forms an infinite double array of complex
random variables. Using the complex
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2},\,\dots,\,\lambda_{n}
of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}
, we define the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}
by

\mu_{n}(x,y)=\frac{1}{n}\#\left\{i\leq n:\Re(\lambda_{k})\leq x,\ \Im(\lambda_{k})\leq y\right\},

where
\#E
denotes the cardinality of the set
E
.

The main result of our paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that the underlying distribution of
elements of
{\bf X}_{n}
are independent random variables with zero means, unit variances, and satisfying the Lindeberg’s condition: for any fixed
\eta>0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|>\eta\sqrt{n})\to 0.

(1.1)

Then, with probability 1,

\mu_{n}(x,y)
tends to the circular law, i.e., the
uniform distribution over the unit disk in the complex plane.

Since the early 1950s, the conjecture that the distribution
\mu_{n}(x,y)
tends to the circular law has stimulated considerable academic interest. The seminal response was proffered by
Mehta (1967)
, addressing the scenario in which
x_{ij}
are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) standard complex normal variables.
Mehta (1967)
utilized the joint density function of the eigenvalues of the matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}
, a formula originally deduced by
Ginibre (1965)
. Silverstein in
(
Cohen, Kesten and Newman, 1986
)
and Edelman in
(
Edelman, 1997
)
also made important contributions in Gaussian cases. For the non-Gaussian cases,
Girko (1985)
and
Bai (1997)
were the first to formally articulate a proof of the circular law under the conditions that
\{x_{kj},\ k,j=1,2,\dots\}
are i.i.d. and the density of the underlying distribution is bounded. This condition was crucial in the estimation of the smallest singular values of a Hermitian type matrix. Building upon this foundation,
Pan and Zhou (2010)
expanded the methodologies of
Rudelson and Vershynin (2008)
regarding the smallest singular values to include non-centralized matrices, subsequently negating the necessity of the density assumption and affirming the circular conjecture under conditions of
4+\varepsilon
moments. At the same time,
Tao and Vu (2008)
;
Götze and Tikhomirov (2010)
furthered the investigation under a lesser moment condition of
2+\varepsilon
.
Tao and Vu (2010)
subsequently resolved the conjecture by applying the Lindeberg substitution principle in the i.i.d. case. For more information about the circular law, we refer to the survey by
Bordenave and
Chafaï (2012)
and references therein. This survey discussed various results around the circular law including both historical context and contemporary research directions in the field of random matrix theory.

In this paper, unlike
Tao and Vu (2010)
, we introduce a more direct and simpler method to prove the circular law, which is a non-trivial extension of
Bai (1997)
.
Remarkably, the novel proof prevails under a weaker condition than the i.i.d. assumption in
(
Tao and Vu, 2010
)
, specifically under Lindeberg’s condition (
1.1
).

In the sequel, we adopt some of the notation and basic results from
(
Bai, 1997
;
Bai and Silverstein, 2010
)
to make our proof easier to follow.
Denote the ESD of the Hermitian matrix

{\bf H}_{n}=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})^{*}
by

\nu_{n}(x,z)=\frac{1}{n}\#\left\{1\leq i\leq n:\lambda_{i}^{{\bf H}_{n}}(z)\leq x\right\},

where
{\bf I}
is the identity matrix,
\lambda_{k}^{{\bf H}_{n}}(z),1\leq i\leq n
are the real eigenvalues of
{\bf H}_{n}
and
∗
stands for the complex conjugate and transpose of the matrix. The following lemma, proposed by
Girko (1985)
, established a relation between the characteristic function of the ESD
\mu_{n}(x,y)
and an integral involving the ESD of the Hermitian matrix
{\bf H}_{n}
.

Lemma 1.2
.

For any
uv\neq 0
, we have the following formula for the two-dimensional characteristic
function
c_{n}(u,v)
of
\mu_{n}(x,y)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle c_{n}(u,v)=:\int\!\!\!\int e^{iux+ivy}\mu_{n}(dx,dy)

(1.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{4iu\pi}\int\!\!\!\int\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu_{n}(dx,z)\right]e^{ius+ivt}dtds,

where
z=s+it
and
i=\sqrt{-1}
.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Section 12.1.2 of
Bai and Silverstein (2010)
. It is clear that to prove Theorem
1.1
we only need to prove for every pair
(u,v)
such that
uv\neq 0
,
c_{n}(u,v)\to c(u,v)
almost surely, where
c(u,v)
is the characteristic function of the uniform distribution
\mu(x,y)
of
the unit circle in the plane. Moreover, it has been shown in Lemma 3.2 of
Bai (1997)
that for all
uv\neq 0
,

\displaystyle c(u,v)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1}e^{iux+ivy}dxdy=\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{4iu\pi}\int\int g(s,t)e^{ius+ivt}dtds,

(1.3)

where

g(s,t)=\begin{cases}\frac{2s}{s^{2}+t^{2}},&\text{if }s^{2}+t^{2}>1,\\
2s,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, analogous to the proof steps in
Bai (1997)
, we outline the proof of Theorem
1.1
as follows.

Step I:

\nu_{n}(x,z)
converges to
\nu(x,z)
uniformly in every bounded region of
z
,
where

\nu(x,z)
is the limiting spectral distribution (LSD) of the sequence of matrices

{\bf H}_{n}
, and
\nu(x,z)
determines the
circular law, i.e.,

g(s,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu(dx,z).

Step II:

The proof of
the circular law can be reduced to showing that, for every large
A>0

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu_{n}(dx,z)-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu(dx,z)\right]e^{ius+ivt}dsdt=0,

(1.4)

where

T=\{(s,t);\ |s|\leq A,|t|\leq A^{3}\}
.

Step III:

For a suitably defined sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, with probability 1,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\ln x\nu_{n}(dx,z)\right|dsdt=0,

(1.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\ln x\nu(dx,z)\right|dsdt=0,

(1.6)

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\!\!\!\int_{T(\varepsilon)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x(\nu_{n}(dx,z)-\nu(dx,z))dsdt=0,

(1.7)

where

T(\varepsilon)
is a random subset of
T
depending on the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,n
, and will be defined in Section
4
.

Compared with the pioneering work of
Bai (1997)
, the main difficulty of the current study lies in the fact that Lindeberg’s condition
1.1
in Theorem
1.1
can not guarantee enough estimations of the smallest eigenvalues of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I}
and the convergence rate of
\nu_{n}(x,z)
to
\nu(x,z)
,
which cannot be handled in the same way as they did for the i.i.d. and higher moments existing cases. Technically, the key breakthroughs in the paper are twofold. First, we establish a general strong law of large numbers under the Lindeberg’s condition, which ensures the tightness of the eigenvalues of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}
. Second, we provide a uniform upper bound on the integral with respect to the smallest eigenvalues of
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})^{*}
, which helps the proof of the circular law without the estimates of these smallest eigenvalues. It should be noted that some uniform estimate of the smallest eigenvalues of
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}-z{\bf I})^{*}
with respect to
z
is crucial for other existing methods (e.g.,
Bai (1997)
;
Pan and Zhou (2010)
;
Götze and Tikhomirov (2010)
).

In the following three sections, we will present the proofs of Steps I–III, respectively.

2
Step I: Convergence of
\nu_{n}(x,z)

Defined by the Stieltjes transform of
\nu(x,z)

\Delta(\alpha)=\int\frac{1}{x-\alpha}\nu(dx,z),~~~\Im\alpha>0.

From Theorem 2.1 of
Zhou, Bai and Hu (2023)
we have that
under Lindeberg’s condition (
1.1
),
\nu_{n}(x,z)
almost surely tends to a limit
\nu(x,z)
,
whose Stieltjes transform
\Delta=\Delta(\alpha)
is the unique solution on the upper half complex plane to the equation

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{\frac{|z|^{2}}{1+\Delta}-(1+\Delta)\alpha}.

(2.1)

Here we choose
p=n
,
\mathbf{T}_{n}=\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{R}_{n}=-\sqrt{n}z\mathbf{I}
in Theorem 2.1 of
Zhou, Bai and Hu (2023)
. From the proof of Theorem 2.1 of
Zhou, Bai and Hu (2023)
, we can also conclude that the convergence of
\nu_{n}(x,z)
to
\nu(x,z)
is uniformly in every bounded region of
z
. Thus, we omit the details.
Note that (
2.1
) coincides with equation (4.2) of
Bai (1997)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta^{3}+2\Delta^{2}+\left(\frac{\alpha+1-|z|^{2}}{\alpha}\right)\Delta+\frac{1}{\alpha}=0.

(2.2)

It is clear that the solution of the above equation
has three analytic branches when
\alpha\neq 0
and when there is no
multiple root. In fact, there is only one branch (denoted by
\Delta
) that can be the Stieltjes transform of
\nu(x,z)
.
Let
m_{2}(\alpha)
and

m_{3}(\alpha)
be the other two branches. If, say,
m_{2}(\alpha)
is
another Stieltjes transform. For
\alpha
real converging to

-\infty
, from Vieta’s formula we have that
\Delta(\alpha)+m_{2}(\alpha)+m_{3}(\alpha)=-2
, which implies
m_{3}(\alpha)\to-2
. But from Vieta’s formula

\alpha\Delta(\alpha)m_{2}(\alpha)m_{3}(\alpha)=-1
and the fact that

\alpha\Delta(\alpha)
converges to
-1
as
\alpha\to-\infty
, we would have

m_{3}(\alpha)
unbounded. Thus, it is a contradiction. Analogously,
m_{3}(\alpha)
is
not the Stieltjes transform of
\nu(x,z)
.
Moreover,
From Lemma 4.4 in
Bai (1997)
, we know that

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu(dx,z)=g(s,t),

which together with (
1.3
) defines the circular law.
Thus, we complete the proof of Step I.

Step II: Integral range reduction

Before we state the proof of Step II, we give two novel lemmas, which are the key to our proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x_{i},i=1,2,\dots,n
be independent nonnegative random variables with means 1. Then for any positive constant
a
and integer
b>1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}I(x_{i}>a)I(\#\{j;x_{j}>a\}<b)\leq b,

(3.1)

where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

Let
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
be a subset of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
for an integer
k
and denote the event

E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}A_{\{j,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}},

where

A_{\{j,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{x_{j}>a\}&~\text{if }j\in\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\\
\{x_{j}\leq a\}&~\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Without loss of generality, when the indices are listed in a subset, they are listed in their partial order, i.e.,
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}
.
Notice that

the events
E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}
are disjoint for different subscript sets.

(
*
)

Thus, we can obtain that

\displaystyle S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}I(x_{i}>a)I(\#\{j;\ x_{j}>a\}<b)=\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}J_{k},

where

J_{k}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}x_{i_{t}}I(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}})=\max_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}x_{i_{t}}I(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}})~~~~~~~\mbox{(because of $(*)$)}.

Define

J_{ek}=\max_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\Big(\mathbb{E}x_{i_{t}}I(x_{i_{t}}>a)\Big)I\left(\bigcap_{j\neq i_{t}}A_{\{j,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}\right)

and

J_{ok}=\max_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}I\left(\bigcap_{j\neq i_{t}}A_{\{j,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}\right).

It is easy to find that
\mathbb{E}S_{n}=\sum_{k<b}\mathbb{E}J_{k}=\sum_{k<b}\mathbb{E}J_{ek}
and
J_{ek}\leq J_{ok}
since
\mathbb{E}x_{i}I(x_{i}\leq a)\leq 1
.
Then, because
(*)
,

\displaystyle J_{ok}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left(I\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}\big)+I\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\backslash\{i_{t}\}}\big)\right)\right]

(3.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\left[kI\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}\big)+\max_{t\leq k}I\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\backslash\{i_{t}\}}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}kI\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}}\big)+\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}I\big(E_{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}\}}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\{\{\#\{i;x_{i}>a\}=k\}\}+\{\#\{i;x_{i}>a\}=k-1\}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}S_{n}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}\mathbb{E}J_{ek}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}\mathbb{E}J_{ok}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(b-1)P(\#\{i;x_{i}>a\}\leq b-1)+P(\#\{i;x_{i}>a\}\leq b-2)\leq b,

which completes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x_{ij},1\leq i,j\leq n
satisfying the conditions in Theorem
1.1
, then we have the following Strong Law of Large Numbers

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}^{2}|\to 1,a.s..

(3.3)

Define

\displaystyle S_{n1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4}),

\displaystyle S_{n2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|\leq n^{3/4}),

and denote the number of pairs
(ij)
such that
|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4}
by
N
. Then by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}S_{n1}I(N\leq n^{3/4})\leq n^{-5/4},

which together with the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies

S_{n1}I(N\leq n^{3/4})\to 0,~a.s..

Moreover, by Bernstein’s inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n1}I(N>n^{3/4})\neq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})>n^{3/4}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})-\mathbb{E}I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})]>n^{3/4}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})]\Big)\leq 2e^{-bn^{3/4}}

for some constant
b>0
. Here, we have used the facts that

\displaystyle Var(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})\leq\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})\leq\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|>n^{1/2})=o(n^{1/2}).

Thus, we have that

S_{n1}\to 0,~a.s..

For
S_{n2}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq 1-\mathbb{E}S_{n2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|>n^{3/4})\to 0

(3.4)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n2}-\mathbb{E}S_{n2}\right]^{6}\leq O(n^{-3/2}).

(3.5)

From the two estimates above, we have
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}^{2}|I(|x_{ij}|\leq n^{3/4})\to 1,a.s.
. Then the proof is complete.

Next we present the proof of Step II.
Let

g_{n}(s,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,\nu_{n}(dx,z)\mbox{~~and~~}g(s,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,\nu(dx,z).

that

and

If the function

is replaced by

, one can find that the right-hand sides of

) and (

) can be made arbitrarily small by

large enough because of the fact

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}I(|\lambda_{k}|\geq A)\leq\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n^{2}A^{2}}{\rm tr}{\bf X}_{n}{\bf X}_{n}^{*}\leq\frac{1}{A^{2}},a.s..

(3.8)

The same conclusion holds when

is
replaced by
g(s,t)
. Define set

T=\{(s,t):\ |s|\leq A,|t|\leq A^{3}\}.

Therefore, the proof of the circular law

,

\int\!\!\!\int_{T}[g_{n}(s,t)-g(s,t)]e^{ius+ivt}dsdt\to 0,~~\mbox{a.s.
}.

(3.9)

Step III: Completing of the proof Theorem
1.1

In this section, we start with a lemma to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f_{n}(\varepsilon)
be a sequence of functions of
\varepsilon
. If for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
f_{n}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as

, then there exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
such that

.

By the assumption, for every

,

such that for all

,
f_{n}(1/k)<1/k
. When

, define
\varepsilon_{n}=1/k
. Then
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and
f_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})<1/k\to 0
. The proof is complete.
∎Now,
we define the random subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle T(\varepsilon)=T\backslash[\cup_{k=1}^{n}\{(s,t):|t-\lambda_{ki}|\leq\varepsilon/2,|z-\lambda_{k}|\leq\varepsilon\}].

Intuitively,

is

excluding the drum shaped regions

,

.

, we can assume that

.

From Step II, to complete the proof of Theorem

), i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu_{n}(dx,z)-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x\nu(dx,z)\right]e^{ius+ivt}dsdt=0.

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z)\right|dtds\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0,~~\mbox{as $n\to\infty$},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\log x\nu(dx,z)\right|dtds\to 0,~~\mbox{as $n\to\infty$},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T(\varepsilon)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln x(\nu_{n}(dx,z)-\nu(dx,z))dsdt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0.~~\mbox{as $n\to\infty$}.

(4.4)

First, we show (
4.2
). Recall the complex
eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{kr}+i\lambda_{ki}
of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bf X}_{n}
.
Note that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{kr}-s}{|\lambda_{k}-z|^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\log(|\lambda_{k}-z|^{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,\nu_{n}(dx,z).

Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z)\right|dtds=\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2(\lambda_{kr}-s)I_{k}(\varepsilon_{n})}{(\lambda_{kr}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{ki}-t)^{2}}\right|dsdt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\frac{|\lambda_{kr}-s|I_{k}(\varepsilon_{n})}{(\lambda_{kr}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{ki}-t)^{2}}dsdt,

where

I_{k}(\varepsilon_{n})=I((\lambda_{kr}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{ki}-t)^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{2})
.
Note that on
\{(\lambda_{kr}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{ki}-t)^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\}
, we have
|z-\lambda_{k}|\leq\varepsilon_{n}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z)\right|dtds

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{|\lambda_{k}-z|\leq\varepsilon_{n}}\frac{|\lambda_{kr}-s|}{(\lambda_{kr}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{ki}-t)^{2}}dsdt=8\varepsilon_{n}\to 0,

which proves (
4.2
).

Similar to (
4.5
), one can prove that

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\log x\nu(dx,z)\right|dtds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{|\lambda|\leq 1}\int_{|\lambda-z|\leq\varepsilon_{n}}\frac{|\lambda_{r}-s|}{(\lambda_{r}-s)^{2}+(\lambda_{i}-t)^{2}}dsdtd\lambda_{r}d\lambda_{i}=8\varepsilon_{n}\to 0.

This proves (
4.3
).

For (
4.4
), for any
t\in(-A^{3},A^{3})
and
\varepsilon>0
, it is easy to find that the intersection of
T(\varepsilon)
with straight line
t
consists of finite intervals. Write the end points as
s_{1},\dots,s_{2\ell(t)}
and

z_{j}=(s_{j},t)
. It is worth noting that here
\ell(t)
can be 0.
It follows that

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T(\varepsilon)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z)dtds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{-A^{3}}^{A^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}\int_{s_{2j-1}}^{s_{2j}}\frac{2(s-\lambda_{kr})}{(s-\lambda_{kr})^{2}+(t-\lambda_{ki})^{2}}dsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{-A^{3}}^{A^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}(\log(|z_{2j}-\lambda_{k}|^{2})-\log(|z_{2j-1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-A^{3}}^{A^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z_{2j})-\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z_{2j-1})\right)dt.

It is shown by
Zhou, Bai and Hu (2023)
that
\nu_{n}(x,z)
almost surely and uniformly tends to
\nu(x,z)
and
\nu(\cdot,z)
in every bounded region of
z
. Thus, by (
4.1
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int_{T(\varepsilon)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\nu_{n}(dx,z)dtds-\int\!\!\!\int_{T(\varepsilon)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\nu(dx,z)dtds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-A^{3}}^{A^{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\Big(\nu_{n}(dx,z_{2j})-\nu(dx,z_{2j})-\nu_{n}(dx,z_{2j-1})+\nu(dx,z_{2j-1})\Big)dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}

\displaystyle 0.

Then, we obtain (
4.4
).
Consequently, Theorem
1.1
is proved.
