Robust Quantum Control: Analysis & Synthesis via Averaging
Robert L. Kosut, Gaurav Bhole, Herschel Rabitz

Abstract
\textcolor blackAn approach is presented for robustness analysis and quantum (unitary) control synthesis based on the classic method of averaging. The result is a multicriterion optimization competing the nominal (uncertainty-free) fidelity with a well known robustness measure: the size of an interaction (error) Hamiltonian, essentially the first term in the Magnus expansion of an interaction unitary. Combining this with the fact that the topology of the control landscape at high fidelity is determined by the null space of the nominal fidelity Hessian, we arrive at a new two-stage algorithm. Once the nominal fidelity is sufficiently high, we approximate both the nominal fidelity and robustness measure as quadratics in the control increments. An optimal solution is obtained by solving a convex optimization for the control increments at each iteration to keep the nominal fidelity high and reduce the robustness measure. Additionally, by separating fidelity from the robustness measure, more flexibility is available for uncertainty modeling.

Introduction

For a quantum computer that is
ideally

sequence of unitary logic gates, robustness means that at specified
times, despite imperfections or perturbations, each
actual

operation is sufficiently close to its ideal for the intended purpose,

II
Quantum system

The quantum systems addressed are mainly focused on
closed

systems. Our framework for robust control can easily include open
systems which are briefly discussed in §
C.8
. The closed

n
-dimensional quantum system unitary evolution
U(t)
over

t\in[0,T]
is obtained from,

\begin{array}[]{ccl}i\dot{U}(t)&=&\left({\bar{H}}(t)+{\widetilde{H}}(t)\right)U(t),\ U(0)=I_{n}\\
{\bar{H}}(t)&=&H_{0}+H_{c}(t)\end{array}

(1)

The Hamiltonian consists of two terms: the first,
{\bar{H}}(t)
, is the

nominal

drift term,
H_{0}
, and a time-varying control term,
H_{c}(t)
, which
depends on
m
control variables
v_{1}(t),\ldots,v_{m}(t)
. In many cases

H_{c}(t)
is linear in the control variables,
i.e.
,
H_{c}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}(t)H_{j}
. The corresponding nominal (unperturbed) unitary, denoted
by
{\bar{U}}(t)
, evolves from,

i\dot{\bar{U}}(t)={\bar{H}}(t){\bar{U}}(t),\ {\bar{U}}(0)=I_{n}

(2)

The nominal fidelity to achieve a unitary target
W
is,

F_{\rm nom}=|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}(T)/n)|^{2}\in[0,1]

(3)

The second term,
{\widetilde{H}}(t)
, is an uncertain
perturbation
such
that,

\left\{{\widetilde{H}}(t),t\in[0,T]\right\}\in{\cal H}_{\rm unc}

(4)

where the characteristics of

are known,
e.g.
, norm bounds
and/or probability distributions on Hamiltonian parameters or parts

To assess the effect of a specified Hamiltonian perturbation on the
nominal system, it is natural to compare the final-time perturbed
system unitary
U(T)
evolving from (
1
) with the target unitary

W
. One such performance measure is the worst-case fidelity,

F_{\rm wc}=\min_{{\widetilde{H}}\in{\cal H}_{\rm unc}}|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}U(T)/n)|^{2},

(5)

As described in
KosutGB:13
,
F_{\rm wc}
can be approximated by
sampling over the uncertainty set, and for small errors a convex
approximation along with a complementary optimization can provide more
confidence towards having found the worst-case fidelity. Similar
results hold for the average-case fidelity. However, as more error
types and sources are discovered, or others are considered, sampling
or convex approximations can get computationally burdensome,
particularly if multiple error sources have to be simultaneously
evaluated.

IV.2
Multicriterion optimization

To satisfy the two synthesis criteria arising from

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&\Phi(v)=\left\{1-F_{\rm nom}(v),\ J_{\rm rbst}(v)\right\}\\

&v\in{\cal V}_{\rm ctrl}\end{array}

In a later section, where we describe some common types of

which has the generic form shown. Specifically, the matrix

{\cal A}_{\rm unc}(v)

With a single uncertainty, or several with near equal bounds, it is

IV.3
Two-stage algorithm

As alluded to earlier, at the top of the control landscape where

F_{\rm nom}(v)=1
, the gradient
\nabla_{v}F_{\rm nom}(v)=0
, there is potentially a
large null space
RabitzHR:04
;
Ho.PRA.79.013422.2009
;
BeltraniETAL:2011
;
MooreR:2012
;
HockerETAL:2014
;
KosutArenzRabitz:2019
.
This
roaming
freedom at the top of the landscape derives from
the structure of the Hessian of the nominal fidelity,

\nabla_{v}^{2}F_{\rm nom}(v)
, which is negative semidefinite and low-rank,
and this can only have a modest number of eigenvectors with negative
eigenvalues (
i.e.
, these specify the portion of the control that
maximizes the fidelity measure). In turn, the remaining, often very
large null space, with associated zero eigenvalues, can serve as a
space to project the system disturbances. Ergo, one iterative path to
finding a solution to (
11
) is to first maximize only the
nominal fidelity
F_{\rm nom}(v)
. When this fidelity crosses a high
threshold,
f_{0}\approx 1
, then switch to minimizing the robustness
measure
J_{\rm rbst}(v)
while keeping the fidelity above
f_{0}
.
Specifically, we utilize the following two stage algorithm:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{{\bf Stage 1} maximize {\bf only}
$F_{\rm nom}(v)$ {\bf until} $F_{\rm nom}(v)\geq f_{0}$}\\
\mbox{{\bf Stage 2} minimize $J_{\rm rbst}(v)$ {\bf while} $F_{\rm nom}(v)\geq f_{0}$}\end{array}

(12)

The uncertainty-free optimization in Stage 1 can be done by many
different gradient ascent methods. For Stage-2, when
F_{\rm nom}(v)\geq f_{0}
, we show in Appendix §
B

that finding an optimal control increment
\widetilde{v}
to solve
(
12
) can be recast
exactly
as a convex optimization
problem. This approach differs from earlier works which used the
properties of the null space at high fidelity directly
RabitzHR:04
;
Ho.PRA.79.013422.2009
;
BeltraniETAL:2011
;
MooreR:2012
;
HockerETAL:2014
;
KosutArenzRabitz:2019
.

In particular, during Stage-2, let fidelity and robustness at the
current control
v
be denoted by
F_{\rm nom}
and
J_{\rm rbst}
. For a change
in controls from
v
to
v+{\widetilde{v}}
, by approximating both the fidelity
and robustness changes by quadratic functions (gradients and Hessians)
in the control increments
{\widetilde{v}}
, the next control increment can be
found by solving the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&{\widetilde{v}}^{T}{\widetilde{v}}+\alpha(J_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})-J_{\rm rbst})\\
\mbox{subject to}&F_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})\geq f_{0}\end{array}

(13)

where

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})&=F_{\rm nom}+\nabla F_{\rm nom}^{T}{\widetilde{v}}+{\widetilde{v}}^{T}\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}{\widetilde{v}}/2\\
J_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})&=J_{\rm rbst}+\nabla J_{\rm rbst}^{T}{\widetilde{v}}+{\widetilde{v}}^{T}\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}{\widetilde{v}}/2\end{array}

(14)

The objective is a trade-off between a small control increment
{\widetilde{v}}

and a
J_{\rm rbst}
decrease (robustness increase) with robustness weight

\alpha>0
. The constraint keeps the nominal fidelity higher than

f_{0}\approx 1
. Although (
13
) is not always a convex
optimization, using
(
BoydV:04
, App.B)
as shown in Appendix
§
B
, the increment
{\widetilde{v}}
can be found efficiently by
solving for the two variables
\gamma,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
in the associated
(convex) dual optimization problem,

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{maximize}\quad\gamma\\
\mbox{subject to}\quad\lambda\geq 0\\
\begin{bmatrix}I-\lambda\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}&\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom}\\
(\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom})^{T}&-2\lambda(F_{\rm nom}-f_{0})-\gamma\end{bmatrix}\geq 0\end{array}

(15)

From
\lambda
the control increment is given by,

{\widetilde{v}}=-\left(I-\lambda\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}\right)^{-1}(\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom})

(16)

In addition, if the threshold is maintained,
i.e.
,
F_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})>f_{0}
,
then
J_{\rm rbst}
can never
increase. Figures

-
2
show two basic
examples of the two-stage algorithm that exhibit the topological

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\widetilde{H}}_{t}&=&\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}{\widetilde{H}}_{\ell t},\quad{\widetilde{H}}_{\ell t}\in{\cal H}_{\rm unc}^{\ell}\\
G_{\rm avg}^{\ell}&=&\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}{\widetilde{H}}_{\ell t}{\bar{U}}_{t}\end{array}

(18)

For ease of notation we use

as the sample time index if it is

,
T/N
for PWC control
(

V.2
Uncertain constant Hamiltonian parameter

A common form of an uncertain perturbation is one composed of the sum
of products of unknown but bounded
constant
parameters and
known (possibly time-varying) Hamiltonians. The resulting Hamiltonian
uncertainty set is,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\theta_{\ell}{B}_{\ell t}\ \left|\ \begin{array}[]{l}{\vec{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}&\cdots&\theta_{L}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\Theta\\
\left\|{B}_{\ell t}\right\|\leq 1,\ t=1:M\end{array}\right.\right\}

(19)

The corresponding time-averaged Hamiltonian is,

G_{\rm avg}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\theta_{\ell}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}{B}_{\ell t}{\bar{U}}_{t}

(20)

V.3
Bounded parameters

To model the effect of uncertain but bounded Hamiltonian parameters,
assume that the
L\times 1
vector of parameters
\vec{\theta}
belongs to
one of the following three uncertainty sets:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\mbox{peak}&\Theta_{\infty}=\{\|\vec{\theta}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{\infty}\}\\
\mbox{energy}&\Theta_{2}=\{\|\vec{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\delta_{2}\}\\
\mbox{covariance}&\Theta_{\rm prob}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{$\vec{\theta}$ zero-mean}\\
{\rm cov}~\vec{\theta}=C\geq 0\end{array}\right\}\end{array}

(21)

One way to see the effect of the uncertain parameters is to stack

all

n^{2}
elements of
G_{\rm avg}
in (
8
) into the vector

\vec{G}_{\rm avg}
resulting in,

\vec{G}_{\rm avg}={\cal A}~\vec{\theta}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{L}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}\times L}\\
A_{\ell}=(1/M)\sum_{t=1}^{M}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}{B}_{\ell t}{\bar{U}}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\end{array}\right.

(22)

Since
\vec{G}_{\rm avg}
in linear in
\vec{\theta}
, the robustness measures
corresponding to the sets in (
21
) follow directly from the
induced norm properties,
i.e.
,

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\vec{\theta}\in\Theta_{\infty}&\Rightarrow&J_{\rm rbst}=\delta_{\infty}\|{\cal A}\|_{\infty}=\delta_{\infty}{\displaystyle\max_{i=1:n^{2}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}|\vec{A}_{\ell i}|\\
\vec{\theta}\in\Theta_{2}&\Rightarrow&J_{\rm rbst}=\delta_{2}\|{\cal A}\|_{2}\\
\vec{\theta}\in\Theta_{\rm prob}&\Rightarrow&J_{\rm rbst}={\bf tr}({\rm cov}~{G_{\rm avg}})=\|{\cal A}\sqrt{C}\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\end{array}

(23)

These robustness measures reflect individual terms in (
11
),
the
{\cal A}
-matrix being the same for all three cases above. For the
two deterministic sets (
\Theta_{\infty},\Theta_{2}
) the effective

S
-matrix is simply
S=I_{L}
with
\mu=\infty
and
\mu=2
,
respectively. For the probabilistic set (
\Theta_{\rm prob}
),

S=\sqrt{C}
with
\mu={\rm fro}
. Clearly more nuanced uncertainty
configurations are possible,
e.g.
, mixing matrix and vector norms such
as
J_{\rm rbst}=\max_{\theta\in\Theta_{2}}\|G_{\rm avg}\|_{\infty}
, and so on. In
addition, in a sum of measures, each measure could differ, or for
groups of parameters each might have different types of bounds.

V.4
Bias and drift

Although
\vec{\theta}
in the instances described is a bounded uncertain
constant, the specific value within the bound can vary over each run
time interval
t\in[0,T]
. Thus the uncertainty set (
19
) is
describing parameters which can change abruptly from run-to-run. If,
however,
\vec{\theta}_{t}
is slowly drifting in each run, and is memoryless
from run-to-run, then for each of
L
parameters a more suitable model
for
t=1:M
is,

\theta_{\ell,t}=h_{t}^{T}c_{\ell},\quad h_{t}=\frac{1}{M-1}\begin{bmatrix}M-t\\
t-1\end{bmatrix},\quad c_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{\ell}\\
b_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

(24)

In each run
a_{\ell}
is the initial value and
b_{\ell}
is the
final value. Assuming that for some norm each
c_{\ell}
is
independently bounded by
\|c_{\ell}\|\leq\delta_{\ell}
, then
following (
22
)-(
23
), the robustness measure
is,

J_{\rm rbst}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\delta_{\ell}\|{\cal A}_{\ell}\|\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}_{\ell}=\sum_{t=1}^{M}\vec{A}_{\ell,t}h_{t}^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}\times 2}\\
A_{\ell,t}={\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}{B}_{\ell,t}{\bar{U}}_{t}\end{array}\right.

(25)

V.5
Uncertain constant energy-bounded Hamiltonian

An interesting example of a constant Hamiltonian perturbation is the
set of uncertain energy-bounded constant Hamiltonians,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\{{\widetilde{H}}\ \mbox{constant},\ \|{\widetilde{H}}\|_{\rm fro}\leq\delta\}

(26)

Clearly
{\widetilde{H}}
is arbitrary other than subject to an “energy-like” bound.
Consequently, an equivalent uncertainty set is,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}}\theta_{i}\Gamma_{i}\ \left|\begin{array}[]{l}\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\Gamma}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{\Gamma}_{n^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\cal U}(n^{2})\\
\|\vec{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\delta\end{array}\right.\right\}

(27)

where
\Gamma_{i},i=1:n^{2}
is any orthonormal basis set for
n\times n

Hermitian matrices, and where the vector
\vec{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
,
bounded in the two-norm by
\delta
, consists of all the constant
coefficients
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i=1:n^{2}
. The equivalence follows from the
fact that
\|{\widetilde{H}}\|_{\rm fro}=\|{\rm vec}({\widetilde{H}})\|_{2}
along with the induced
norm property of the vector two-norm. Thus for any orthonormal
(Hermitian) basis, the real coefficients are equivalent to all
possible entries in
any constant

{\widetilde{H}}
such that

\|{\widetilde{H}}\|_{\rm fro}\leq\delta
.
It follows that for an energy bounded parameter uncertainty the
appropriate robustness measure is of the same form of
(
23
),
i.e.
,

J_{\rm rbst}=\delta\left\|{\cal A}\right\|_{2}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{n^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\\
A_{i}=(1/M)\sum_{t=1}^{M}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{i}{\bar{U}}_{t}\end{array}\right.

(28)

where the
S
-matrix is simply
I_{n^{2}}
, the identity matrix.

V.6
Uncertain time-varying Hamiltonian parameter

Uncertain parameter time variation can arise in a number of forms
depending on the underlying physics. Here we pose a few generic
uncertainty models which capture the effects of limited-bandwidth and
stationary noise.

V.6.1
Limited bandwidth

Let
\theta_{t},{t=1:M}
denote an uncertain bounded
time-varying

parameter associated with the
single
perturbation

{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\theta_{t}{B}_{t}
where
B_{t}
is known and typically normalized so
that
\left\|B_{t}\right\|\leq 1
. To capture limited-bandwidth, assume that

\theta_{t}
is the output of a “filter” driven by a bounded sequence

w_{t},{t=1:N}
, where the filter is well represented as a
linear-time-varying (LTV) system with causal (convolution) dynamics

Ljung:87
,

\theta_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}K_{t\tau}w_{\tau}

(29)

Since causality implies that
K_{t\tau}=0
for
\tau>t
, the vector of
time-varying parameters can be expressed by
\vec{\theta}={\cal K}\vec{w}
with
{\cal K}
the
M\times M

block-lower-triangular
matrix,

{\cal K}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{11}&&&\\
K_{22}&K_{21}&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
K_{MM}&\cdots&K_{M2}&K_{M1}\end{bmatrix}

(30)

If the filter is linear-time-invariant (LTI) then (
29
) becomes

\theta_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}K_{t-\tau}w_{\tau}
from which is follows that
the matrix
{\cal K}
is lower-triangular Toeplitz,
i.e.
,

{\cal K}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{1}&&&\\
K_{2}&K_{1}&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
K_{M}&\cdots&K_{2}&K_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(31)

For an LTI filter the first column above is the impulse response of
the filter dynamics.

V.6.2
Stationary noise modeling

For either LTV or LTI dynamics, assume further that
\vec{w}
, the
noise filter input, is a zero-mean stationary sequence with unit covariance

{\rm cov}(\vec{w})=I_{M}
. Under these conditions, the Hamiltonian
uncertainty set is,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\theta_{t}{B}_{t}\ \left|\begin{array}[]{l}\vec{\theta}={\cal K}\vec{w},\ \vec{w}\mbox{ zero-mean}\\
{\rm cov}(\vec{w})=I_{M}\end{array}\right.\right\}

(32)

The corresponding
n^{2}\times 1
vector of the time-averaged Hamiltonian is,

\vec{G}_{\rm avg}={\cal A}{\cal K}\vec{w}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\ (n^{2}\times M)\\
A_{t}={\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}{B}_{t}{\bar{U}}_{t}/M\end{array}\right.

(33)

Consequently, for a single error source the output magnitude level is
subsumed in the noise filter
{\cal K}
. (Explicit bound shown in

J_{\rm rbst}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left\|{\cal A}_{\ell}S_{\ell}\right\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{\ell 1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{\ell M}\end{bmatrix}\\
A_{\ell t}={\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}B_{\ell t}{\bar{U}}_{t}\\
S_{\ell}={\cal K}_{\ell}\end{array}\right.

(34)

with
(S_{\ell},{\cal A}_{\ell})
correspondingly defined as previously per
the characteristics of each perturbation and where output power of

{\cal K}_{\ell}

VII
Concluding Remarks & Outlook

The approach to robust quantum control presented here rests on two
theoretical foundations: the classic method of averaging

Hale:80
and the known topological properties of the quantum
control landscape
RabitzHR:04
;
Ho.PRA.79.013422.2009
;
BeltraniETAL:2011
;
MooreR:2012
;
HockerETAL:2014
;
KosutArenzRabitz:2019
. Application of the method of averaging
directly results in a multicriterion optimization problem consisting
of the uncertainty-free fidelity competing with a generic robustness
measure, the latter being a time-domain product of a function (or
functional) of the controls and the character of the uncertainty
(
11
). Such a product form is ubiquitous in the many
applications of the
Small Gain Theorem

Zames:1966
;
DesVid:1975
and generalizations for various
uncertainty models,
e.g.
,
LMI94
;
ZhouDG:96
. These require a
product in a feedback loop to be small: an uncertainty bound and an
uncertainty-free (closed-loop) map. From the averaging theory there
naturally arises a quantitative norm measure of a time-averaged
Hamiltonian (
8
) reflective of the interaction between the
uncertain perturbation and the uncertainty-free unitary
evolution. This is the first term in a Magnus expansion of the
interaction representation between the nominal unitary and the
uncertainty. If this term is sufficiently small, then at most, only
second order error effects can accrue (
9
). In the case of
quantum information sciences for established realizations, the tacit
assumption is that the (possibly known or unknown) perturbations are
small.

The second theoretical foundation, properties of the control landscape
topology, motivated a
two-stage algorithm
(
12
) for
finding the robust control: first, maximize fidelity without regard to
uncertainty, then, at a high fidelity threshold, switch to
maximizing robustness while maintaining the high uncertainty-free
fidelity level.
\textcolor
blackWe view the time-domain robustness
measures developed here together with the two-stage algorithm to
solve the multicrterion problem to be complementary to the
frequency-domain filter function measures and approaches in
GreenETAL:2013
;
Soare2014
;
Kaby:2014
;
Paz-Silva2014
;
QCTRL:2021
;
HaasHamEngr:2019
;
Chalermpusitarak2021
;
Cerfontaine2021
;
Yang:2022
.

In Appendix §
B
we showed that by using a
quadratic approximation of
both
fidelity and robustness in this
region of the control landscape, the task of finding a control
increment at each iteration is a convex optimization problem. The
actual system fidelity robustness of course is subject to the accuracy
of the quadratic approximations.

We derived the specific form of the matrix products in the robustness
measure corresponding to many common uncertainties. The examples
presented, using our two-stage algorithm, clearly illustrate the known
flexibility inherent in the landscape topology at high fidelity.
However, a visual inspection of many of the robust control pulse
sequences does not offer direct insight into why a significant level
of robustness is achieved,
i.e.
, the mechanism involved. In a few cases
there are discernible symmetric pulse patterns. Unfortunately the
basis for understanding provided by Theorem
1
is somewhat
coarse: make the time-averaged Hamiltonian small,
e.g.
, via
(
11
), and yet was shown to operate well. However, there is no
guarantee that any particular degree of robustness can be achieved,
and the best control action at the top of the landscape would most
likely call for a global search, unlike the local procedure utilized
in this paper.

It is important to emphasize that the
actual
effectiveness of
the resulting optimization as predicted by the averaging theory, or by
any other approach, can only be determined by evaluation on the total
system with uncertainties included. For either deterministic or
probabilistic uncertainty sets, this requires simulating the full
system with a sufficient number of samples from the uncertainty
sets. Ultimately an experiment is the final proof.

Looking ahead, we see a significant unanswered question:
How is
robustness precisely achieved for a quantum system?
What is the
underlying mechanism? We mentioned the close connection between the
generic form of the robustness measure (
11
) and the Small Gain
Theorem
Zames:1966
;
DesVid:1975
. For robust linear control the
latter (and subsequent variations
LMI94
;
ZhouDG:96
) have proven
to be extraordinarily effective (and capable of revealing the
robustness mechanism) even against a large class of
uncertainties. Moreover, because the mechanism is understood, and in
many cases, comprehensible, it is possible to describe a very large
class of uncertainty models which encompass what is anticipated
without having to delineate each specific possibility

id4c:92
. Why not for quantum?
For example, is it possible to build a “standard” set of uncertainty
models, each of which captures a large variety of possibilities,
thereby leading to a universal set of robust quantum controls? A
qualitative generic answer can be understood in terms of the landscape
“roaming” feature,
i.e.
, seeking a control where the disturbance has
minimal eigenvector projection along the Hessian eigenvectors of
negative value. Is this what allows a quantitative understanding of
the favorable solutions of the dual problem (
15
),
(
62
) such that maximum robustness is achieved at high
fidelity? Notwithstanding the latter research direction, the
fundamental quantum robustness challenge stated above remains.

Proof of Theorem
1

With no loss in generality, set the bounded Hamiltonian perturbation
in (
4
) to
{\widetilde{H}}(t)=\delta{B}(t)
where for a specified matrix
norm,
\|{B}(t)\|=1
. The interaction unitary
R(t),t\in[0,T]
from
(
6
) then evolves from,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}i\dot{R}(t)&=&\delta A(t)R(t),\ R(0)=I_{n}\\
A(t)&=&{\bar{U}}(t)^{\dagger}{B}(t){\bar{U}}(t)\end{array}

(38)

where
{\bar{U}}(t)
is the nominal (error-free) unitary
(
2
). Define the average interaction unitary,

\bar{R}(T)=e^{-i\delta T\langle A\rangle},\quad\langle A\rangle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}A(t)dt

(39)

To prove Theorem

1
first requires showing that the interaction unitaries
satisfy the following inequalities:

\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\bar{R}(T)-I\right\|\leq e^{\bar{\gamma}(\delta T)}-1\\
\left\|R(T)-\bar{R}(T)\right\|\leq e^{\widetilde{\gamma}(\delta T)^{2}}-1\end{array}

(40)

with
(\bar{\gamma},\widetilde{\gamma})
as defined in (
9
) of Theorem

1
, that is,

\bar{\gamma}=\|\langle A\rangle\|,\quad\widetilde{\gamma}=\max_{t}\|A(t)-\langle A\rangle\|

(41)

Using the form of the standard state transformation for averaging
analysis described in
[
1
, §V.3]
(periodicity, usually
assumed, is not needed here) set,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R(t)&=&(I+\delta K(t))V(t)\\
K(t)&=&-i\int_{0}^{t}\left(A(\tau)-\langle A\rangle\right)d\tau\end{array}

(42)

The matrix
V(t)
, which is is not necessarily a
unitary, is the solution of,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}i\dot{V}&=&\delta\left(\langle A\rangle+D(t)\right)V,\ V(0)=I_{n}\\
D(t)&=&\delta(I+\delta K(t))^{-1}(A(t)K(t)-K(t)\langle A\rangle)\end{array}

(43)

With
\|A(t)\|\leq 1
,
\|\langle A\rangle\|\leq 1
, and

\|A(t)-\langle A\rangle\|\leq\widetilde{\gamma}
, it follows that

\left\|K(t)\right\|\leq\widetilde{\gamma}t
. Additionally,
K(t)+K(t)^{\dagger}=0
ensures
that
\|(I+\delta K(t))^{-1}\|\leq 1
. As a result,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|D(t)\|&\leq&\delta\|A(t)K(t)-K(t)\langle A\rangle\|\\
&\leq&2\delta\|K(t)\|\leq 2\delta\widetilde{\gamma}t\end{array}

(44)

Applying
variation of constants
to (
43
) gives,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}V(t)&=&\bar{R}(t)-i\delta\bar{R}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\bar{R}(\tau)^{\dagger}D(\tau)V(\tau)d\tau\end{array}

(45)

Introducing the error,

E(t)=V(t)-\bar{R}(t)

(46)

Substituting into (
45
) gives,

E(t)=-i\delta\bar{R}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\bar{R}(\tau)^{\dagger}D(\tau)\left(\bar{R}(\tau)+E(\tau)\right)d\tau

(47)

Since
\bar{R}(t)
is a unitary,
\|\bar{R}(t)\|=1
. This together with
the previous bound on
D(t)
gives,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left\|E(t)\right\|&\leq&2\widetilde{\gamma}\delta^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\tau d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\tau\left\|E(\tau)\right\|d\tau\right)\\
&=&\widetilde{\gamma}(\delta t)^{2}+2\widetilde{\gamma}\delta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau\left\|E(\tau)\right\|d\tau\end{array}

(48)

Applying the Bellman-Gronwall Lemma together with the fact that

K(T)=0
implies
R(T)=V(T)
, and hence,
E(T)=R(T)-\bar{R}(T)
, yields
the second inequality in (
40
). The first inequality in
(
40
) is a known result which can also be obtained
by following the same argument which
resulted in the first inequality, now substituting the identity for

R(t)
and then applying Bellman-Gronwell.

To prove the main result (
9
), we need the following
relationship established in
[
22
]
between
fidelity and the Frobenius norm:

\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{E}}=\min_{|\phi|=1}\|U(T)-\phi W\|_{\rm fro}^{2}=2n\left(1-\sqrt{F}\right)\\
F=|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}U(T)/n)|^{2}\\
\phi={\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}U(T)/n)/|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}U(T)/n)|\end{array}

(49)

Likewise for the nominal fidelity,

\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm nom}=\min_{|\phi|=1}\|{\bar{U}}(T)-\phi W\|_{\rm fro}^{2}=2n\left(1-\sqrt{F_{\rm nom}}\right)\\
F_{\rm nom}=|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}(T)/n)|^{2}\\
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\phi}={\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}(T)/n)/|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}(T)/n)|\end{array}

(50)

To simplify notation we drop the final time
T
in all the variables,

e.g.
, let
U=U(T),{\bar{U}}={\bar{U}}(T)
,
etc.
. Applying
(
49
)-(
50
) yields,

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\mathcal{E}}&\leq\|U-\bar{\phi}W\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\\
&=\|{\bar{U}}-\bar{\phi}W+{\bar{U}}(R-I)\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\ \mbox{( using $U={\bar{U}}R$ )}\\
&\leq\left(\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm nom}}+\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}\right)^{2}\ \mbox{( from (\ref{eq:fidnom norm}) )}\end{array}

(51)

From the definitions of
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm nom}
, the last inequality
above is equivalent to,

\begin{array}[]{rl}F&\geq F_{\rm nom}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}/n)\sqrt{F_{\rm nom}}+\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}/2n\right)^{2}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}&=2\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm nom}}\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}+\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\end{array}

(52)

If
F_{\rm nom}\approx 1
, then
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm nom}\approx 0
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\approx\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}^{2}
and,

F\geq 1-\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}^{2}/n+\left(\|R-I\|_{\rm fro}^{2}/2n\right)^{2}

(53)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rl}F&=1-{\cal O}\{\left\|R-I\right\|^{2}\}\\
&=1-{\cal O}\{\left\|\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{R}-I+R-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{R}\right\|^{2}\}\\
&=1-\left({\cal O}\{\left\|\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{R}-I\right\|\}+{\cal O}\{\left\|R-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{R}\right\|\}\right)^{2}\\
&=1-\left({\cal O}\{\bar{\gamma}(\delta T)\}+{\cal O}\{\widetilde{\gamma}(\delta T)^{2}\}\right)^{2}\ \mbox{(from (\ref{eq:interaction}))}\end{array}

(54)

The last line establishes Theorem
1
.

Appendix B
Two-stage algorithm

With
m
PWC controls over
N
uniform intervals, the two-stage
algorithm in §
IV.3
to find the control variable
magnitudes
v\in\mathbb{R}^{mN}
can be summarized as follows:

1.

initialize

v\in{\cal V}_{\rm ctrl}
, set a fidelity threshold

f_{0}\approx 1
and a control increment bound
\beta
.

2.

repeat
for
i=1,2,\ldots
until satisfied

(a)

while

F_{\rm nom}(v^{i})<f_{0}
,
do

v^{i+1}=\arg\min_{v}\left\{1-F_{\rm nom}(v)\ \Bigg|\ \begin{array}[]{l}v=v^{i}+\widetilde{v}\in{\cal V}_{\rm ctrl}\\
\left\|\widetilde{v}\right\|\leq\beta\end{array}\right\}

(55)

(b)

while

F_{\rm nom}(v^{i})\geq f_{0}
,
do

v^{i+1}=\arg\min_{v}\left\{J_{\rm rbst}(v)\ \Bigg|\ \begin{array}[]{l}v=v^{i}+\widetilde{v}\in{\cal V}_{\rm ctrl}\\
\left\|\widetilde{v}\right\|\leq\beta\\
F_{\rm nom}(v)\geq f_{0}\end{array}\right\}

(56)

(c)

stop
when
J_{\rm rbst}(v^{i+1})\approx J_{\rm rbst}(v^{i})

Since the objective switches from minimizing the uncertainty-free
fidelity error,
1-F_{\rm nom}(v)
to minimizing the robustness measure

J_{\rm rbst}(v)
at the fidelity threshold
f_{0}\approx 1
, it is expected
that the accompanying fidelity gradient
\nabla F_{\rm nom}(v)
will be
small. Consequently, the fidelity is best approximated as a quadratic
function of the control increment
\widetilde{v}
in the neighborhood of the
current control
v
. That is, for a sufficiently small
\beta
with

\left\|\widetilde{v}\right\|\leq\beta
the nominal fidelity is approximated as,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{\rm nom}(v+\widetilde{v})&\approx&F_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\\
&=&F_{\rm nom}+\nabla F_{\rm nom}^{T}\widetilde{v}+\widetilde{v}^{T}\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}\widetilde{v}/2\end{array}

(57)

where
F_{\rm nom},\nabla F_{\rm nom},\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}
are evaluated at
v
, the
current control. The Hessian
\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}
may not be negative
definite (or negative semidefinite) as would be the case at a global
fidelity maximum
v^{\star}
where
F_{\rm nom}(v^{\star})=1
and

\nabla F_{\rm nom}(v^{\star})=0
. However, it is known from topological
arguments that at the top, at any global optimum
v^{\star}
, the
Hessian
\nabla F_{\rm nom}(v^{\star})
has
({mN})^{\star}<{mN}
negative
eigenvalues, the rest are zero, and hence, it is negative
semidefinite.

With
F_{\rm nom}\approx 1
, it is to be expected that some number of
eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}
are near zero. This motivates replacing

\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}
with an approximation obtained by setting the small
eigenvalues to
exactly
zero. Any control in the null-space
defined by the corresponding
{mN}-({mN})^{\star}
eigenvectors can be
used for purposes other than keeping
F_{\rm nom}(v)\geq f_{0}
; in
particular, minimizing the robustness measure. However, the solution
method we propose here does not require the Hessian to be positive
semidefinite, so there is no need to make a further approximation of
the fidelity Hessian.

In the neighborhood of the current control
v
, we also approximate
the robustness measure as a quadratic in the control increment

\widetilde{v}
, as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}J_{\rm rbst}(v+\widetilde{v})&\approx&J_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\\
&=&J_{\rm rbst}+\nabla J_{\rm rbst}^{T}\widetilde{v}+\widetilde{v}^{T}\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}\widetilde{v}/2\end{array}

(58)

where
J_{\rm rbst},\nabla J_{\rm rbst},\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}
are evaluated at
v
, the
current control. Even though the fidelity gradient
\nabla F_{\rm nom}
is
small when
F_{\rm nom}\approx 1
, there is no reason to expect that the
robustness gradient,
\nabla J_{\rm rbst}
, is small, nor that its Hessian,

\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}
, is positive semidefinite. Again, we make no further
assumptions or approximations.

In Stage 2, having approximated both fidelity and robustness
objectives as a quadratic in control increment
\widetilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{mN}
via
(
57
)-(
58
), we seek the largest decrease in
robustness, that is, the largest
\Delta>0
such that there exists

\widetilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{mN}
simultaneously satisfying the three constraints:

\widetilde{v}^{T}\widetilde{v}\leq\beta,\quad F_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\geq f_{0},\quad J_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\leq J_{\rm rbst}-\Delta

(59)

Not all these parameters are known: in general would like
\beta
to be
small to satisfy the quadratic approximations and
\Delta
as large as
possible. The threshold fidelity can be set to an
f_{0}
near 1, how
“near” to 1 may vary per problem. The trade-off amongst the
three goals can be expressed by the constrained optimization,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize}&\widetilde{v}^{T}\widetilde{v}/2+\alpha(J_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})-J_{\rm rbst})\\
\mbox{subject to}&F_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\geq f_{0}\end{array}

(60)

The objective simultaneously reflects the first two goals where

\alpha>0
weighs the robustness decrease
(-\Delta)
relative to the
control increment size. Thus the values for
\beta
and
\Delta
are
implicit in the objective; only
f_{0}
and
\alpha
are explicitly
set. Using the quadratic approximations, the optimization
(
60
) becomes,

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mbox{minimize}&\widetilde{v}^{T}(I+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst})\widetilde{v}+2\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}^{T}\widetilde{v}&\\
\mbox{subject to}&\widetilde{v}^{T}\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}\widetilde{v}+2\nabla F_{\rm nom}^{T}\widetilde{v}+2(F_{\rm nom}-f_{0})\geq 0&\end{array}

(61)

At the current control
v
,
(
61
) is not guaranteed to be a convex optimization
problem in the increment
\widetilde{v}
. However, as shown in

[
24
]
, the dual problem in the two variables

\lambda,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
can be obtained from the linear-matrix-inequality
(LMI) constrained convex optimization,

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{maximize}\quad\gamma\\
\mbox{subject to}\quad\lambda\geq 0\\
\begin{bmatrix}I-\lambda\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}&\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom}\\
(\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom})^{T}&-2\lambda(F_{\rm nom}-f_{0})-\gamma\end{bmatrix}\geq 0\end{array}

(62)

Having obtained
\lambda
, the resulting optimal solution for the control
increment is,

\widetilde{v}=-\left(I-\lambda\nabla^{2}F_{\rm nom}+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst}\right)^{-1}(\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}-\lambda\nabla F_{\rm nom})

(63)

From
[
24
]
, if there exists an increment
\widetilde{v}
such that
the constraint is a strict inequality, that is,
F_{\rm quad}({\widetilde{v}})>f_{0}
,
then
strong duality
holds and the two objectives in
(
61
) and (
62
) are equal,
i.e.
,

\widetilde{v}^{T}(I+\alpha\nabla^{2}J_{\rm rbst})\widetilde{v}+2\alpha\nabla J_{\rm rbst}^{T}\widetilde{v}=\gamma

(64)

Since
F_{\rm nom}>f_{0}
and if the LMI in (
62
) is positive definite, it
follows that
\gamma<-2\lambda(F_{\rm nom}-f_{0})
. Thus
\gamma
is (theoretically)
always negative which ensures that the quadratic approximation

J_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})
of
J_{\rm rbst}(v+\widetilde{v})
decreases. As we see in the
numerical examples, the size of the decrease in
J_{\rm rbst}(v+\widetilde{v})

depends on the choice of the weighting parameter
\alpha
in the
objective (
60
). In addition, since we are roaming about
near the top of the landscape where fidelity is very near to 1, the
constraint that
F_{\rm quad}(\widetilde{v})\geq f_{0}
is easily violated by very
small amounts. These look large on the log-scale plots of fidelity
error vs. iteration.

Appendix C
Uncertainty Models

C.1
Linear time invariant noise

Although (
30
) captures
all
LTV filters, the overall
character of the noise will dictate some specific forms. For example,
suppose that it is only known that the noisy parameter has a limited
bandwidth. The least complicated system which captures the bandwidth
constraint only, is a first-order linear-time-invariant (LTI) system
with time constant
\beta
(bandwidth is effectively
1/\beta
) and
whose input, sampled at
T/M
, is the stationary zero-mean white noise
sequence
\vec{w},{\rm cov}(\vec{w})=\delta_{w}^{2}I_{M}
. Then
\theta_{t},{t=1:M}
is the
output of the discrete-time
zero-order-hold
system with
continuous-time transfer function
1/(\beta s+1)
,
i.e.
,

\Theta(z)=(1-a)/(z-a)W(z)
with
a=\exp\{-\frac{T/M}{\beta}\}
.
It follows that the
M\times M
filter matrix
{\cal K}
in (
30
) is

{\cal K}=(1-a)\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\
a&1&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
a^{M-1}&\cdots&a&1\end{bmatrix}

(65)

The discrete-time Fourier transform
\Theta(\omega)
of
\theta_{t},{t=1:M}
is
bounded by,

|\Theta(\omega)|\leq\delta\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{2(1-\cos(\omega T/M))+a^{2}}}

(66)

C.2
PWC noise

A more extreme type of uncertain time-variation is where
\theta_{t}
is
piece-wise-constant. Suppose
\theta_{t}
is constant but bounded
over
L
uniform intervals
{\cal T}_{\ell}
, each of width
T/L
in

[0,T]
. Thus
\theta_{t}
changes abruptly
L
times (jumps) from a given
underlying distribution. Suppose for each of the
L
intervals the
uncertain parameter
\theta_{t},t\in{\cal T}_{\ell}
is independently bounded by

\left\|\theta_{t},t\in{\cal T}_{\ell}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{\infty}
. In other words,
consider the
deterministic noise uncertainty set
,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\theta_{t}{B}_{t}\ \left|\begin{array}[]{l}\theta_{t}=w_{\ell},\ t\in{\cal T}_{\ell},\ \ell=1:L\\
\left\|\theta_{\ell}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{\ell}\end{array}\right.\right\}

(67)

This type of uncertainty has an equivalent frequency domain
spectrum. Since in each of the
L
time intervals the uncertain
parameter varies independently, it can be considered as a
time-sequence over
[0,T]
of uncertain amplitude
\theta_{\ell}
with a
fixed duration
T/L
,
i.e.
, a pulse of known duration and uncertain
magnitude bounded by
\delta
. For a deterministic uncertainty
(
67
), the magnitude of the Fourier transform

\Theta(\omega)
of such a sequence is bounded by the “sinc” function,

i.e.
, for each
\ell=1:L
,

|\Theta_{\ell}(\omega)|\leq\delta\left(\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{L}}\right)\left|{\rm sinc}\left(\frac{\omega\pi}{\omega_{L}}\right)\right|,\ \omega_{L}=\frac{2\pi}{T/L}

(68)

As the number of intervals increase the spectral magnitude decreases
while the bandwidth (
\sim\omega_{L}
) increases, that is, the frequency
spreads which potentially makes robustness more difficult to
attain. The needed temporal behavior of
{\bar{U}}_{t}
to overcome the
frequency spread may be harder to realize under control limitations.
The corresponding vector of the time-averaged Hamiltonian is,

\vec{G}_{\rm avg}={\cal A}S\vec{w}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\cal A}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{\cal A}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\\
S={\rm blk\_diag}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{M/L}&\cdots&\mathbbm{1}_{M/L}\end{bmatrix}\\
{\cal A}_{\ell}=(1/M)\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{\ell}}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}B_{t}{\bar{U}}_{t}\end{array}\right.

(69)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{M/L}
is an
M/L
vector of ones,
{\cal A}
is
n^{2}\times M
,
S
is
M\times L
, and
\left\|\vec{w}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta
. As a
consequence, the robustness measure is the induced matrix norm,

J_{\rm rbst}=\delta\left\|{\cal A}S\right\|_{\infty}

(70)

In effect, each of the piece-wise-constant uncertain parameters is
reflected by a corresponding time-averaged Hamiltonian over each
interval
{\cal T}_{\ell}
resulting in a collection of uncertain constant
bounded parameters each of which is held fixed over some time
intervals. For a probabilistic uncertainty where in each of the
L

intervals
\theta_{t}
is zero-mean with covariance
\delta^{2}I_{L}
, the
corresponding robustness measure becomes,

J_{\rm rbst}=\delta\left\|{\cal A}S\right\|_{\rm fro}

(71)

C.3
Robustness measure as filter function

\textcolor

blackThe robustness measure derived from the averaging approach is
equivalent in at least one instance to a
filter function

measure as described in
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
As an illustration, assume that the interaction Hamiltonian (
6
)
is
G(t)=\theta(t)A(t)
with
A(t)={\bar{U}}(t)^{\dagger}{B}(t){\bar{U}}(t)
, where

B(t)
is known and where
\theta(t)
is a zero-mean wide-sense
stationary random process with autocorrelation function,

s(\tau)={\bf E}\{\theta(t)\theta(t+\tau)\}
.
Clearly
G(t)
and the time-averaged Hamiltonian

\langle G\rangle=(1/T)\int_{0}^{T}G(t)dt
are both zero mean. Using the Frobenius
norm to reflect the size of
\langle G\rangle
, let the robustness measure be
the variance,
i.e.
,
J_{\rm rbst}={\bf E}\|T\langle G\rangle\|_{\rm fro}^{2}
where expectation
is with respect to the noise

. The result is,

\begin{array}[]{rl}J_{\rm rbst}&=\int_{t=0}^{T}\int_{t^{\prime}=0}^{T}s(t-t^{\prime})c(t,t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}dt\\

Replace

J_{\rm rbst}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\cal A}(\omega)S(\omega)d\omega

with,

If in (

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\cal A}(\omega)&=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{T}(T-\tau)e^{i\omega\tau}c(\tau)d\tau\\
c(\tau)&={\bf tr}\left({\bar{U}}(\tau)^{\dagger}{B}{\bar{U}}(\tau){B}\right)\end{array}

(75)

Sometimes

can be measured, and even then, it can be

66

domain actuator dynamics error (

C.4
Additive control noise

Suppose that each of the
m

v_{jt},j=1:m

\{\theta_{jt},{t=1:M}\}

v_{jt}=(\bar{v}_{jt}+\theta_{jt})H_{c}^{j}
where
\bar{v}_{jt},j=1:m
, the
disturbance-free controls, serve as the design variables. As an

j

by the
M

\vec{\theta}_{j}

{\cal K}_{j}
as
in (
29

34
) except here we replace the

with the constant control Hamiltonians

. The additive control noise Hamiltonian uncertainty set is,

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{jt}H_{c}^{j},\ \left|\begin{array}[]{l}\vec{\theta}_{j}={\cal K}\vec{w}_{j},\ \mbox{$\vec{w}_{j}$ zero-mean}\\

(76)

Following the same procedure to arrive at (
34
), the

J_{\rm rbst}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{j}\left\|{\cal A}_{j}S_{j}\right\|_{\rm fro}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{j1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{jM}\end{bmatrix}\\

where each

n^{2}\times M
. Here we assumed that the noise

). Though there are too many variations to
delineate, such as noise drawn independently with the same spectrum,

e.g.
,
\vec{\theta}_{j}={\cal K}\vec{w}_{j}
, or combinations thereof, the procedure

the form of the robustness measure in all these cases will conform to

).

C.5
Multiplicative control noise

Suppose each of the
m
controls
v_{jt},j=1:m
is affected by a
time-varying
multiplicative noise
function
\theta_{jt}
such that

v_{jt}=(1+\theta_{jt})\bar{v}_{jt}H_{c}^{j}
where, as previously described,

\bar{v}_{jt},j=1:m
are the disturbance-free controls. As in the additive

76
) except for
the dependence on the control:

{\cal H}_{\rm unc}=\left\{{\widetilde{H}}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{jt}\bar{v}_{jt}H_{c}^{j},\ \left|\begin{array}[]{l}\vec{\theta}={\cal K}\vec{w},\ \mbox{$\vec{w}$ zero-mean}\\

Following the procedure to obtain (

),

J_{\rm rbst}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{j}\left\|{\cal A}_{j}S_{j}\right\|_{\rm fro}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{j1}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{jM}\end{bmatrix}\\

The only difference between this measure and (

C.6
Uncertain actuator dynamics

Though not strictly noise, uncertain actuator (control generator)
dynamics can appear in the form of a multiplicative control
perturbation. Suppose the actuator dynamics are well modeled as an
LTI system such that each of the
m
control commands
\bar{v}_{tj}
are
effected by similar dynamics,
i.e.
,

v_{tj}=D_{j}(z)\bar{v}_{tj},\ j=1:m,\ t=1:M

(80)

where
D_{j}(z)
is a rational transfer function in the unit delay
operator
z^{-1}
corresponding to the sampling rate
T/M
. Assume
that
D_{j}(z)
is in the uncertainty set,

{\cal D}(\Delta)=\left\{D(z)=(1+\Delta(z)Q(z))\bar{D}(z)\ \left|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\Delta\right\|_{{\mathbf{H}}_{\infty}}\leq\delta\\
\left\|Q\right\|_{{\mathbf{H}}_{\infty}}=1\end{array}\right.\right\}

(81)

The transfer function
\bar{D}(z)
is the perturbation-free LTI model
of the actuator dynamics,
Q(z)
is a normalized weighting factor, and

\Delta(z)
is a completely uncertain transfer function bounded in

{\mathbf{H}}_{\infty}
norm by
\delta
, the maximum magnitude of the frequency
response. This set characterizes a standard transfer function with a
bounded multiplicative model-error
[
29
]
. The equivalent
frequency domain constraint is,

|D(i\omega)/\bar{D}(i\omega)-1|\leq\delta|Q(i\omega)|

(82)

Since
Q(z)
is normalized, the left hand side can never exceed
\delta

over all frequencies.

The nominal and error Hamiltonians associated with this class of
uncertainty are,

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\bar{H}}_{t}&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bar{D}(z)\bar{v}_{tj})H_{j}\\
{\widetilde{H}}_{t}&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{tj}H_{j}\\
\theta_{tj}&=\Delta_{j}(z)Q(z)\bar{D}(z)\bar{v}_{tj}\end{array}

(83)

The time-averaged Hamiltonian (
8
) is then,

\begin{array}[]{rl}{G_{\rm avg}}&=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}\theta_{tj}{\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}H_{j}{\bar{U}}_{t}\end{array}

(84)

For each
j=1:m
, write the
M\times 1
vector with elements

\theta_{tj},t=1:M
as,

{\rm vec}(\theta_{j})={\cal T}(\Delta_{j}(z)){\cal T}(Q(z)\bar{D}(z)){\rm vec}(\bar{v})

(85)

where
{\cal T}(P(z))
is a lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix whose first
column is the impulse response of the LTI system
P(z)
. Since

\left\|\Delta_{j}\right\|_{{\mathbf{H}}_{\infty}}\leq\delta
implies that

\|{\cal T}(\Delta_{j}(z))\|_{2}\leq\delta

[
31
]
, the
robustness measure is similar to the form of (
79
), namely,

\begin{array}[]{rl}J_{\rm rbst}&=\delta\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|{\cal A}_{j}\|_{2}S_{j}\\
{\cal A}_{j}&=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{A}_{1j}&\cdots&\vec{A}_{Mj}\end{bmatrix},\quad A_{tj}={\bar{U}}_{t}^{\dagger}H_{j}{\bar{U}}_{t}\\
S_{j}&=\|{\cal K}{\rm vec}(\bar{v}_{j}))\|_{2},\quad{\cal K}={\cal T}(Q(z)\bar{D}(z))\end{array}

(86)

C.7
Uncertain cross-couplings

An interesting class of closed systems with multiple errors, which has
similarities to an
open
system, is one where the perturbations
are caused by unwanted cross-coupling interactions amongst parallel
channels. To illustrate this, consider two channels operating at the
same time. With respective dimensions
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, the system
Hamiltonian is,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{t}&=&{\bar{H}}_{t}+{\widetilde{H}}_{t}\\
{\bar{H}}_{t}&=&{\bar{H}}_{t}^{(1)}\otimes I_{n_{2}}+I_{n_{1}}\otimes{\bar{H}}_{t}^{(2)}\\
{\widetilde{H}}_{t}&=&{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{(1)}\otimes I_{n_{2}}+I_{n_{1}}\otimes{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{(2)}+{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{\rm int}\end{array}

(87)

with uncertainty sets,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{(i)}&\in&{\cal H}_{\rm unc}^{(i)},\ i=1,2\\
{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{\rm int}&\in&{\cal H}_{\rm unc}^{\rm int}\end{array}

(88)

The total system nominal unitary is the tensor product of each
unperturbed channel:

{\bar{U}}_{t}={\bar{U}}_{t}^{(1)}\otimes{\bar{U}}_{t}^{(2)}
. Consequently, the associated
time-averaged Hamiltonian is,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G_{\rm avg}&=&G_{\rm avg}^{(1)}\otimes I_{n_{2}}+I_{n_{1}}\otimes G_{\rm avg}^{(2)}+G_{\rm avg}^{\rm int}\\
G_{\rm avg}{(i)}&=&\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}({\bar{U}}_{t}^{(i)})^{\dagger}{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{(i)}{{\bar{U}}_{t}^{(i)}},\ i=1,2\\
G_{\rm avg}^{\rm int}&=&\frac{1}{M}\sum_{t=1}^{M}({\bar{U}}_{t}^{(1)}\otimes{\bar{U}}_{t}^{(2)})^{\dagger}{\widetilde{H}}_{t}^{\rm int}({\bar{U}}_{t}^{(1)}\otimes{\bar{U}}_{t}^{(2)})\end{array}

(89)

In general each channel is attempting to make a different unitary. In
this example there are two, denoted by
W_{i},i=1,2
. Thus the nominal
fidelity is the product of each as if they are independent,

F_{\rm nom}=F_{\rm nom}^{(1)}F_{\rm nom}^{(2)}
, or equivalently,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{\rm nom}&=&\left|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}_{N}/n_{1}n_{2})\right|^{2}\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}W=W_{1}\otimes W_{2}\\
{\bar{U}}_{N}={\bar{U}}_{N}^{(1)}\otimes{\bar{U}}_{N}^{(1)}\end{array}\right.\end{array}

(90)

There is some flexibility in the choice of the robustness
measure. Consider, for example, the worst-case over the
time-averaged Hamiltonians:

J_{\rm rbst}=\max\left\{\left\|G_{\rm avg}^{\rm int}\right\|,\left\|G_{\rm avg}^{(1)}\right\|,\left\|G_{\rm avg}^{(2)}\right\|\right\}

(91)

The norms in the regulation term
J_{\rm rbst}
follow from the specific
character of the uncertainty sets.
This format is easily extended to more channels.

C.8
Open Systems

The robustness measures for two represntative models of uncertain open
systems are described: bipartite and Lindblad. These are both
presented in the continuous-time framework.

C.8.1
Bipartite

Consider a bipartite quantum system evolving over the time interval

t\in[0,T]
with “system” dimension
{n_{S}}
and “bath’ dimension

{n_{B}}
. The continuous-time
n={n_{S}}{n_{B}}
dimensional system-bath unitary

U(t)
is obtained from,

\begin{array}[]{rl}i\dot{U}(t)&=H(t)U(t)\\
H(t)&=H_{S}(t)\otimes{I_{n_{B}}}+{I_{n_{S}}}\otimes H_{B}(t)+H_{SB}(t)\end{array}

(92)

where
H_{S}(t)
is uncertainty-free and control dependent, and where

H_{B}(t),H_{SB}(t)
are uncertain with isolated unitaries,

\begin{array}[]{rl}i\dot{U}_{S}(t)&=H_{S}(t)U_{S}(t),\quad i\dot{U}_{B}(t)=H_{B}(t)U_{B}(t)\end{array}

(93)

For a “system”
unitary target
W_{S}
, we use the fidelity as defined in

[
22
]
,

F=\left\|\Gamma/n\right\|_{\rm nuc}^{2}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{S}}}V_{[ii]},\\
V=(W_{S}\otimes{I_{n_{B}}})^{\dagger}~U(T)\end{array}\right.

(94)

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\rm nuc}
is the nuclear-norm (sum of singular values),
and where
\{V_{[ii]},i=1:{n_{S}}\}
are the blosk-diagonal
{n_{B}}\times{n_{B}}

sub-matrices of the unitary
V
. (For a closed-system (no bath), this
fidelity reduces to the standard
F=|{\rm tr}~(W_{S}^{\dagger}U_{S}(T)/{n_{S}})|^{2}
).
In this case it is convenient to define the interaction unitary
as,

R(t)=(U_{S}(t)\otimes U_{B}(t))^{\dagger}U(t)

(95)

Although in many instances the bath self-dynamics weakly interacts
with the system,
e.g.
, the Hamiltonian
H_{B}(t)
is slowly varying over
the operational time, here (
95
) is appropriate with
R(t)

evolving from,

\begin{array}[]{rl}i\dot{R}(t)&=G(t)R(t)\\
G(t)&=(U_{S}(t)\otimes U_{B}(t))^{\dagger}H_{SB}(t)(U_{S}(t)\otimes U_{B}(t))\end{array}

(96)

Suppose the
time-averaged Hamiltonian
\langle G\rangle\approx 0
and the nominal fidelity

F_{\rm nom}=|{\rm tr}~(W_{S}^{\dagger}U_{S}(T)/{n_{S}})|^{2}\approx 1
. Then
R(T)\approx I

and
V\approx W_{S}^{\dagger}U_{S}(T)\otimes U_{B}(T)\approx{I_{n_{S}}}\otimes U_{B}(T)
. Under these conditions the fidelity (
94
) is

F\approx 1
.
It follows from these assumptions that the appropriate robustness
measure is,

J_{\rm rbst}=\max_{H_{B},H_{SB}\in{\cal H}_{\rm unc}}\left\|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}G(t)dt\right\|

(97)

The specific norm depends on the characteristics of
{\cal H}_{\rm unc}
.

C.8.2
Lindblad

A basic Lindblad master equation describes non-unitary evolution of
an
n\times n
density matrix
\rho(t),t\in[0,T]
by,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{\rho}&=-i[{\bar{H}}(t),\rho]+{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\theta),\ \rho(0)=\rho_{0}\\
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\vec{\theta})&=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\left(2L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-(L_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger}\rho+\rho L_{k}L_{k}^{\dagger})\right)\end{array}

(98)

where
{\bar{H}}(t)
is the nominal (uncertainty-free) Hamiltonian as
defined in (
1
). Here the Lindblad term
{\mathcal{L}}(\rho,\vec{\theta})
is
an uncertainty model with known operations
L_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},k=1:K
, where
\vec{\theta}
is a
K\times 1
vector of uncertain
coefficients
\theta_{k}
bounded in norm by
\|\vec{\theta}\|\leq\delta
.

To reconfigure the Lindblad system (
98
) into our standard
multicriterion optimization form (
11
), lift the density to the
vector
x(t)=\vec{\rho}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
. Then (
98
) becomes,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{x}&=-iA(t)x+B(\vec{\theta})x,\ x(0)=\vec{\rho}_{0}\\
A(t)&=I_{n}\otimes{\bar{H}}(t)-{\bar{H}}(t)^{*}\otimes I_{n}\\
B(\vec{\theta})&=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}B_{k}\\
B_{k}&=2(L_{k}^{*}\otimes L_{k})-(I_{n}\otimes L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k}+(L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k})^{T}\otimes I_{n})\end{array}

(99)

Note that the nominal (uncertainty-free) system,

\dot{\bar{x}}=-iA(t)\bar{x},\ \bar{x}(0)=\vec{\rho}_{0}

(100)

has as solution,

\bar{x}(t)=\bar{V}(t)\vec{\rho}_{0}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{V}(t)={\bar{U}}(t)^{*}\otimes{\bar{U}}(t)\\
\dot{\bar{U}}(t)=-i{\bar{H}}(t){\bar{U}}(t),\ {\bar{U}}(0)=I_{n}\end{array}\right.

(101)

Let the lifted state
x(t)=\bar{V}(t)R(t)\vec{\rho}_{0}
where
R(t)
, the
lifted
n^{2}\times n^{2}
interaction unitary, evolves from,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{R}(t)&=G(t,\vec{\theta})R(t),\ R(0)=I_{n^{2}}\\
G(t,\vec{\theta})&=\bar{V}(t)^{\dagger}B(\vec{\theta})\bar{V}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\bar{V}(t)^{\dagger}B_{k}\bar{V}(t)\end{array}

(102)

Here
G(t,\vec{\theta})
is the lifted interaction Hamiltonian. A target
unitary
W
is achieved at the final time
T
if the density matrix

\rho(T)\approx W\rho_{0}W^{\dagger}
for all
\rho_{0}
, or in terms of the
lifted state,
x(T)\approx{\rm vec}(W\rho_{0}W^{\dagger})=(W^{*}\otimes W)\vec{\rho}_{0}
. Following (
49
) together with (
102
)
gives the corresponding distance and fidelity measures,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{E}}&=&\min_{|\phi|=1}\|\bar{V}(T)R(T)-\phi(W^{*}\otimes W)\|_{\rm fro}^{2}\\
&=&2n^{2}(1-{\cal F})\\
{\cal F}&=&\left|{\bf tr}\left((W^{*}\otimes W)^{\dagger}({\bar{U}}(T)^{*}\otimes{\bar{U}}(T))R(T)\right)/n^{2}\right|\end{array}

(103)

Because we have lifted
\rho
to
\vec{\rho}
, thereby inflating the
dimension from
n
to
n^{2}
, the fidelity here,
{\cal F}
, is not squared
as in (
49
). To see why this is appropriate, if
R(T)\approx I_{n^{2}}
, then
{\cal F}\approx F_{\rm nom}=|{\bf tr}(W^{\dagger}{\bar{U}}(T)/n)|^{2}
as
usual.

Since the uncertainty is bounded,
\|\vec{\theta}\|\leq\delta
, it
follows that the robustness measure follows from the corresponding
induced matrix norm of the time-averaged interaction Hamiltonian,

J_{\rm rbst}=\max_{\|\vec{\theta}\|\leq\delta}\|T\vec{G}_{\rm avg}(\vec{\theta})\|=\delta\left\|{\cal A}\right\|\quad

(104)

with

\begin{array}[]{l}{\cal A}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\Gamma}_{1}&\cdots&\vec{\Gamma}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{4}\times K}\\
\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}=\int_{0}^{T}\bar{V}(t)^{\dagger}B_{k}\bar{V}(t)dt\end{array}

(105)

Drift and multiplicative uncertainty

(109)
