Affine Gateaux Differentials and the von Mises Statistical Calculus
Simone Cerreia-Vioglio, Fabio Maccheroni, Massimo Marinacci, Luigi Montrucchio, Lorenzo Stanca

Abstract
This paper presents a general study of one-dimensional differentiability for functionals on convex domains that are not necessarily open. The local approximation is carried out by affine functionals, rather than linear ones as in standard Gateaux differentiability. This affine notion of differentiability naturally arises in many applications and, here and there, it appeared in the literature. Our systematic analysis aims to give a general perspective on it.



Key words
Affine Gateaux differentiability; Gateaux
differentiability; influence functions; robust statistics; envelope theorem;
multi-utility representation.

49J50, 49J52, 26E15, 26B25.

Introduction

To study the asymptotic behavior of statistical functionals, Richard von Mises
elaborated a notion of directional derivability for functionals defined over
spaces of probability distributions.

as

and Fernholz
[

.
As these domains have no
interior points, standard Gateaux differentiability is indeed no longer adequate.

In this paper we build upon von Mises’s idea by studying functionals defined
over abstract convex sets. For a function
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined
on a convex set
C
, the starting point is the directional derivative

Df\left(x;y\right)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{f\left(\left(1-t\right)x+ty\right)-f\left(x\right)}{t}

at a point
x
of
C
along the direction
y
, where
y
is another point of

C
. We use the term weak affine differential when the function
Df(x;\cdot)

is affine on
C
(Definition
6
). For this basic notion it is
already possible to prove a number of results. Yet, we reserve the term
affine differential to the important special case when the affine functional

Df\left(x;\cdot\right)
can be extended to the whole space, yielding a
notion of affine gradient. To exemplify, the von Mises differential is just
our affine differential for functionals whose domain is a space of distributions.

The early notion of differentiability introduced by von Mises has been used in
statistics (see Hampel
[
14
]
, Huber
[
15
, pp. 34-40]
, and
Fernholz
[
23
]
). A similar approach, again on spaces of distributions,
was also used in risk theory (see Chew et al.
[
8
]
). More recently, a
general formulation of Roy’s identity in consumer theory has been established
through affine derivatives by
[
7
]
. Summing up, affine
differentiability naturally pops up in different applications. Our purpose is
to provide a systematic analysis of this notion.

Preliminaries

Throughout
\left\langle X,X^{\ast}\right\rangle
is a dual pair between two
vector spaces
X
and
X^{\ast}
, with generic elements
x
and
x^{\ast}
.
When
X
is normed,
X^{\ast}
is its topological dual, unless otherwise
stated. The pairing map is denoted by
\left\langle x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle
,
with
x\in X
and
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}
. We refer to the
\sigma\left(X,X^{\ast}\right)
-topology as the weak topology of
X
, while we refer to
the
\sigma\left(X^{\ast},X\right)
-topology as the weak* topology of

X^{\ast}
. Let
A
be a subset of
X
. Its
annihilator
, denoted by

A^{\bot}
, is the set of all
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}
that vanish on
A
, i.e.,

\left\langle x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle=0
for all
x\in A
. Clearly,
A^{\bot}
is a weak*-closed vector subspace of
X^{\ast}
.

Throughout
C
denotes a convex subset of
X
. A point
x\in C
is an

algebraic interior point
of
C
if for every
y\in X
there is

\varepsilon>0
such that
x+\varepsilon y\in C
. The
algebraic
interior
of
C
is denoted by
\operatorname*{cor}C
. A point
x\in C
is an
(
algebraic
)
relative

interior point
of
C
if, for every

y\in\operatorname*{aff}C
there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
x+\varepsilon y\in C
. The
relative interior
of
C
is denoted by
\operatorname*{ri}C
. For short, we call
internal
the elements of
\operatorname*{ri}C
.
Clearly,
\operatorname*{cor}C\subseteq\operatorname*{ri}C
.

Let
Y
be a topological space, typically assumed to be metrizable. The set of
all finite signed Borel measures is denoted by
ca\left(Y\right)
. The
subset
\Delta(Y)
of
ca\left(Y\right)
consists of all Borel probability
measures. We denote by
C_{b}\left(Y\right)
the space of all bounded and
continuous functions on
Y
.

A function
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
affine
if
f\left(tx+\left(1-t\right)y\right)=tf\left(x\right)+\left(1-t\right)f\left(y\right)
for all
x,y\in C
and all
t\in\left[0,1\right]
.

Definition 1

An affine function
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
extendable
when it
admits a weakly continuous linear extension to the whole space
X
, that is,
when there exist
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f\left(\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,x^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma
.

As well-known, affine functionals on
C
are not always extendable, even when
weakly continuous (cf. Example
13
). Moreover, the extension of an
affine function may be not unique (when exists).

Proposition 2

Let
f\left(\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,x^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma
on
X
, with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. An affine
functional
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
agrees with
f
on
C
if and only if

g\left(\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,y^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma_{y^{\ast}}
, where
y^{\ast}\in x^{\ast}+\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}

and
\gamma_{y^{\ast}}=f\left(\bar{x}\right)-\left\langle\bar{x},y^{\ast}\right\rangle
, with
\bar{x}\in C
arbitrarily fixed.

Proof
Let
g\left(\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,y^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma_{1}
be an affine functional on
X
such
that, for each
x\in C
,
f\left(x\right)=g\left(x\right)
. This
implies that
\left\langle x,x^{\ast}-y^{\ast}\right\rangle
is constant when

x
runs over
C
. That is,
\left\langle x-y,y^{\ast}-x^{\ast}\right\rangle=0
when
x
and
y
are distinct points of
C
. Hence,
y^{\ast}-x^{\ast}\in\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}
. Equivalently,
y^{\ast}\in x^{\ast}+\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}
. From
f\left(\bar{x}\right)=\left\langle\bar{x},y^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma_{1}
it follows that
\gamma_{1}=f\left(\bar{x}\right)-\left\langle\bar{x},y^{\ast}\right\rangle
.

To prove the converse, let
g\left(\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,y^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma_{y^{\ast}}
be with
y^{\ast}
and

\gamma_{y^{\ast}}
given above. Tedious algebra shows that

g\left(x\right)=\left\langle x-\bar{x}+\bar{x},y^{\ast}-x^{\ast}+x^{\ast}\right\rangle+f\left(\bar{x}\right)-\left\langle\bar{x},y^{\ast}\right\rangle=\left\langle x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma=f\left(x\right)

as desired.
\blacksquare

Thus, the representation of an affine functional on
C
is unique if and only
if
\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}=\left\{0\right\}
. Since

\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}=\left(C-x\right)^{\bot}

(1)

holds for any arbitrary point
x\in C
, we have
\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}=\left\{0\right\}
when
\operatorname*{cor}C\neq\emptyset
. Another
example, which gives a unique extension (if any), is the case of an affine
functional defined on a reproducing convex cone
K
. Clearly
\left(K-K\right)^{\bot}=X^{\bot}=\left\{0\right\}
.

Uniquely extendable affine functionals can be characterized in
finite-dimensional spaces, as the next known result shows.

Proposition 3

An affine functional
f:C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is extendable. The extension is unique if and only if

\operatorname*{int}C\neq\emptyset
.

In normed vector spaces a less general result holds.

Proposition 4

An affine functional
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined on a
convex set
C
of a normed vector space
X
is uniquely extendable if

\operatorname*{int}C\neq\emptyset
. It is weakly continuous when
f
is
locally bounded at some
\operatorname*{int}C
.

3
Affine differentiability

3.1
Differential

We begin with the protagonist of our analysis.

Definition 5

The
affine directional

derivative
of a functional

f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at a point
x\in C
along the direction
y\in C
is
given by

Df\left(x;y\right)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{f\left(\left(1-t\right)x+ty\right)-f\left(x\right)}{t}

(2)

when this limit exists finite.

When this limit exists finite for all
y\in C
, the map
Df\left(x;\cdot\right):C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is well defined. It clearly satisfies
the following properties:

(i)

Df\left(x;x\right)=0
;

(ii)

Df\left(x;\cdot\right)
is homogeneous, i.e.,

Df\left(x;\left(1-\alpha\right)x+\alpha y\right)=\alpha Df\left(x;y\right)

(3)

for all
y\in C
and all
\alpha\in\left[0,1\right]
.

The map
Df\left(x;\cdot\right)
is, in general, not affine (see Example

7
below), a failure that motivates the following taxonomy.

Definition 6

Let
x\in X
. A functional
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{\mathbb{R}}
is:

(i)

weakly

affinely differentiable
at

x
if

Df\left(x;\cdot\right):C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an affine functional;

(ii)

affinely differentiable
at

x
if
Df\left(x;\cdot\right):C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an extendable affine functional.

Weak affine differentiability (for short, wa-differentiability) does not
require any vector topology, which is instead needed for affine
differentiability (for short, a-differentiability). We call the map
Df\left(x;\cdot\right):C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the
wa-differential
of
f
at

x
when it is affine. In Appendix, Proposition
55
provides
sufficient conditions for wa-differentiability.

When
Df\left(x;\cdot\right)
is extendable, we call it the

a-differential
of
f
at
x
; in this case, there is a pair
\left(x^{\ast},\gamma\right)\in X^{\ast}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

Df\left(x;\cdot\right)=\left\langle\cdot,x^{\ast}\right\rangle+\gamma

(4)

This pair is not unique unless
\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}=\left\{0\right\}
. Since
Df\left(x;x\right)=0
, it follows that
\gamma=-\left\langle x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle
and so the affine differential admits
the intrinsic representation

Df\left(x;y\right)=\left\langle y-x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle

(5)

where the inessential scalar
\gamma
has been dropped. In light of
(
1
), equation (
5
) is independent of the element

x^{\ast}
chosen. With this, we call
gradient
of

f
at
x
any
such equivalent
x^{\ast}
. As it is unique up to elements in
\left(C-C\right)^{\bot}
, we have an equivalence class

\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]=x^{\ast}+\left(C-C\right)^{\perp}=x^{\ast}+\left(C-x\right)^{\perp}\in X^{\ast}/\left(C-C\right)^{\perp}

(6)

which is a weak*-closed affine space of
X^{\ast}
. Here
\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)
is a representative element of this equivalent class, so it stands
for any element
x^{\ast}
of this equivalence class. With this, we can write
(
5
) as

Df\left(x;y\right)=\left\langle y-x,\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right\rangle

Example 7

Define

f:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

f\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=x_{1}^{\alpha}x_{2}^{\beta}
,
with
\alpha,\beta>0
.
When
\alpha+\beta<1
,

the
directional derivative at the origin
\mathbf{0}=\left(0,0\right)

does not exist. Instead, it exists when
\alpha+\beta=1
, with

Df\left(\mathbf{0};y_{1},y_{2}\right)=f\left(y_{1},y_{2}\right)
.

But,
f
is not wa-differentiable at

\mathbf{0}

since

Df\left(\mathbf{0};\cdot\right)
is not affine. It is easy to see
that
f
is a-differentiable at
\mathbf{0}
when
\alpha+\beta>1
and the zero functional is

its unique gradient
.
\blacktriangle

It is desirable to have criteria ensuring a-differentiability. The following
conditions are a direct consequence of Propositions
3
and

4
.

Proposition 8

Let
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be wa-differentiable at a point

x\in C
. Then,
f
is a-differentiable at
x_{0}
if either
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
X
is normed,
x\in\operatorname*{int}C
and
f
is locally
Lipschitz at
x
.

Proof
The case
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
follows from Proposition

3
. When
X
is normed, the local Lipschitz condition at

x\in\operatorname*{int}C
implies that
\left|Df\left(x;y\right)\right|\leq L\left\|y-x\right\|
and so that
Df\left(x;\cdot\right)
is locally bounded at
x
. Therefore, Proposition

4
yields the desired result.
\blacksquare

When
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Gateaux differentiable at
x\in C
, the

Gateaux gradient
\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)\in X^{\ast}
is given
by

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{f\left(x+ty\right)-f\left(x\right)}{t}=\left\langle y,\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)\right\rangle\qquad\forall y\in X

The next result shows that affine and Gateaux differentiability are equivalent
only on
\operatorname*{cor}C
, a set that in most relevant cases is empty.

Proposition 9

The function
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
a-differentiable at
x\in\operatorname*{cor}C
if and only if it is Gateaux
differentiable at
x
. In this case,
\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]=\left\{\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)\right\}
.

Here the affine gradient inherits the uniqueness of the Gateaux one.

Proof
Let
f
be Gateaux differentiable at
x\in\operatorname*{cor}C
and let
x^{\ast}=\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)
. For
each
y\in C
,

\left\langle y-x,x^{\ast}\right\rangle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{f\left(x+t\left(y-x\right)\right)-f\left(x\right)}{t}=Df\left(x;y\right)

Hence,
x^{\ast}\in\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]
. As

x\in\operatorname*{cor}C
, the set
\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]
is a singleton and so we can write
x^{\ast}=\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)
. Conversely, let
f
be a-differentiable at
x
, with
x^{\ast}\in\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]
. Take
y\in X
. It follows
that
x+t_{0}y\in C
for some
t_{0}>0
small enough. It holds

t_{0}\left\langle v,x^{\ast}\right\rangle=\left\langle t_{0}v,x^{\ast}\right\rangle=Df\left(x;x+t_{0}v\right)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{f\left(\left(1-t\right)x+t\left(x+t_{0}v\right)\right)}{t}=t_{0}\left\langle v,\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)\right\rangle

Hence,
x^{\ast}=\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)
. This proves that
\left[\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)\right]=\left\{\nabla_{G}f\left(x\right)\right\}
.
\blacksquare

Observe that when
f
has an extension
\tilde{f}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

which is Gateaux differentiable on
C
, it is a-differentiable on
C
and

\nabla_{a}f\left(x\right)=\nabla_{G}\tilde{f}\left(x\right)+\left(C-C\right)^{\perp}
for all
x\in C
.

3.2
Examples

We present a few examples to illustrate the concepts introduced so far.

Example 10

Given an interval
I=\left[a,b\right]
, let

NBV\left(I\right)
be the vector space of the functions

F:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

of bounded variation that are right
continuous on

\left(a,b\right)

and normalized, i.e.,
F\left(a\right)=0
. We consider the standard duality between
NBV\left(I\right)

and the space
C\left(I\right)
of continuous
function
u:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, with pairing defined by
\langle F,u\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}u\mathrm{d}F

where the integral is
Riemann-Stieltjes.

If a functional

T:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

defined on a convex
subset of

NBV\left(I\right)

is a-differentiable at
F
,
there is a function
u_{F}\in C\left(I\right)

such that

DT\left(F;G\right)=\int_{a}^{b}u_{F}\mathrm{d}\left(G-F\right)\qquad\forall G\in C

For instance, consider the convex set
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}\left[a,b\right]

of all probability distributions on the interval (i.e., of
all
\emph{decreasing}

F\in NBV\left(I\right)

\emph{with}\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{d}F=1
). In this case,

\left(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}\right)^{\perp}=\operatorname*{span}\left\{1_{\left[a,b\right]}\right\}

and so the gradient
u_{F}=\nabla_{a}T\left(F\right)

is
unique up to an additive constant.

\blacktriangle

Example 11

Replace
I
with a metric space
Y
and
consider the dual pair

\left\langle ca\left(Y\right),C_{b}\left(Y\right)\right\rangle
. A functional

T:\Delta\left(Y\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is affinely differentiable at
\mu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
if there is
u_{\mu}\in C_{b}\left(Y\right)

such that

DT\left(\mu;\lambda\right)=\int_{X}u_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\left(\lambda-\mu\right)\qquad\forall\mu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)

The gradient
u_{\mu}\in C_{b}\left(Y\right)
is unique up to
an additive constant. Indeed, if
u\in\left(\Delta\left(Y\right)-\Delta\left(Y\right)\right)^{\perp}
,
then
\left\langle u,\lambda-\mu\right\rangle=0
for all
\lambda,\mu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
. By setting
\lambda=\delta_{x}
and
\mu=\delta_{y}
, we then get
u\left(x\right)=u\left(y\right)

for
all
x,y\in Y
. Hence,
u
is constant.

\blacktriangle

Example 12

Let
S
be a metric space and
A
a
convex subset of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Denote by
C
the convex set
of all norm-bounded (i.e.,
\sup_{s\in S}\left\|u(s)\right\|_{n}<\infty
) and continuous maps

u:S\rightarrow A
. Given
\mu\in\Delta\left(S\right)

and
F:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
define
I:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

I(u)=\int F(s,u(s))\mathrm{d}\mu(s)

Assume the following conditions:

(i)

F(\cdot,x)

is Borel measurable for every

x\in A
;

(ii)

F(s,\cdot)

is wa-differentiable for every

s\in S
,
with differential
DF\left(s,x;\cdot\right)
;

(iii)

there exists

\kappa:S\rightarrow\mathbb{\mathbb{R}}
, with

\int\kappa\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
, such that

\left|F\left(\cdot,x\right)-F\left(\cdot,y\right)\right|\leq\kappa\left(\cdot\right)\left\|x-y\right\|_{n}\qquad\forall x,y\in A

Under these conditions,
I
is well defined and
wa-differentiable at each
u\in C
, with

DI(u;g)=\int DF(s,u(s);g\left(s\right))d\mu(s)\qquad\forall g\in C

Indeed, by (iii) we have

\frac{1}{t}|F(s,(1-t)u(s)+tg(s))-F(s,u(s))|\leq\kappa\left(s\right)\left\|g(s)-u(s)\right\|_{n}\qquad\forall s\in S

The desired result thus follows from the Dominated Convergence
Theorem.

\blacktriangle

Inspired by an example in Phelps
[
18
]
, next we present a
wa-differentiable function which is not a-differentiable.

Example 13

Let

C=\left\{\left\{x_{n}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\forall n,\left|x_{n}\right|\leq a_{n}\right\}
,
where
\left\{a_{n}\right\}
is a given
scalar sequence with
0<a_{n}<1
and
\sum_{n}a_{n}<\infty
.
Clearly,
C
is a closed and bounded convex subset of

\ell^{2}
. The convex function

f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

defined by

f\left(x\right)=\left(\sum_{n}x_{n}\right)^{2}

is wa-differentiable at each
x\in C
, with

Df\left(x;y\right)=\left(2\sum_{n}x_{n}\right)\cdot\sum_{n}y_{n}-2f\left(x\right)\qquad\forall y\in C

Clearly,
Df\left(x;\cdot\right)

is affine on
C
.
But,
f
is not a-differentiable at any
x\in C

since there is
no element
u\emph{\ in}

\ell^{2}

such that
\left(u,y\right)=\sum_{n}y_{n}

for all

y\in C.
Indeed, for each
m

consider the point

y_{m}=(0,0,..,a_{m},....)\in C
.
It follows
that
u_{m}a_{m}=\left(u,y_{m}\right)=a_{m}
,
namely,
u_{m}=1
for all
n
. But then

u\notin\ell^{2}
.

The map
y\mapsto\sum_{n}y_{n}
is continuous. Indeed, if the
sequence
\left\{y_{n}^{m}\right\}
converges to
\left\{y_{n}\right\}
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, then it converges
pointwise, i.e.,
y_{n}^{m}\rightarrow y_{n}
for every
n
. By
the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
\sum_{n}y_{n}^{m}\rightarrow\sum_{n}y_{n}
. Hence,
Df\left(x;\cdot\right)

is weakly
continuous.

Finally, observe that
\operatorname*{cor}C
is
empty, something not surprising in light of Proposition

4
.
\blacktriangle

A function
B:C\times C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
biaffine form
when
it is affine in each argument. It is
extendable
when it can be extended
to a bilinear form on
X\times X
. An example of a biaffine form is the map

B:C\times C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

B\left(x,y\right)=\left(\sum_{n}x_{n}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{n}y_{n}\right)

(17)

for all
x,y\in C
.

Proof
Let
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be wa-differentiable.
The function
f
is quasiconvex if and only if, for all
x,y\in C
, its
restrictions
\varphi_{x,y}\left(t\right)=f\left(x_{t}\right)
are
quasiconvex. By Lemma
17
,
f
is hemidifferentiable. Thus,
f
is
quasiconvex if and only if, for all
x,y\in C
,

\varphi_{x,y}\left(t_{2}\right)\leq\varphi_{x,y}\left(t_{1}\right)\Longrightarrow\varphi_{x,y}^{\prime}\left(t_{1}\right)(t_{2}-t_{1})\leq 0

(18)

for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\left[0,1\right]
. By (
10
), it becomes

f\left(x_{t_{2}}\right)\leq f\left(x_{t_{1}}\right)\Longrightarrow\frac{1}{t_{2}-t_{1}}Df(x_{t_{1}};x_{t_{2}})(t_{2}-t_{1})\leq 0

for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in\left[0,1\right]
. By taking
t_{1}=1
and

t_{2}=1
we get the desired result.
\blacksquare

4
Optimization

We begin with a first-order condition for a (global) minimizer.

Proposition 28

Let
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be wa-differentiable. If
\hat{x}\in C
is a minimizer of
f
, then

Df\left(\hat{x};y\right)\geq 0\qquad\forall y\in C

(19)

with equality when
\hat{x}\in\operatorname*{ri}C
.

Clearly the necessary conditions (
19
) are also sufficient when

f
is convex. When
f
is a-differentiable, the first-order condition
(
19
) takes the variational inequality form

\left\langle y-\hat{x},\nabla_{a}f\left(\hat{x}\right)\right\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall y\in C

Proof
Let
\hat{x}
be a minimizer. Then,
f\left(\hat{x}_{t}\right)\geq f\left(\hat{x}\right)
for all
t\in\left[0,1\right]
and
y\in C
. Hence,

Df\left(\hat{x},y\right)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{f\left(\hat{x}_{t}\right)-f\left(\hat{x}\right)}{t}\geq 0

It remains to show that if
\hat{x}\in\operatorname*{ri}C
the wa-differential
vanishes. Fix a point
y\in C
that differs from
\hat{x}
. As
\hat{x}\in\operatorname*{ri}C
, there exist
x\in C
and
\bar{t}\in\left(0,1\right)
such that
\left(1-\bar{t}\right)x+\bar{t}y=\hat{x}
. By
Lemma
17
, the function
\varphi\left(t\right)=f\left(x_{t}\right)
is differentiable at
\bar{t}
and

\varphi^{\prime}\left(\bar{t}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{t}}Df\left(x_{\bar{t}};y\right)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{t}}Df\left(\hat{x};y\right)

On the hand
\varphi
has a minimizer at the interior point
\bar{t}
. Hence,

\varphi^{\prime}\left(\bar{t}\right)=0
and so
Df\left(\hat{x};y\right)=0
for all
y\in C
.
\blacksquare

Example 29

Let

\blacktriangle

Theorem 31

5.1
Influence curve

Let
T:\Delta\left(Y\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be wa-differentiable.
Using the identification
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
, based on Hampel
[
14
]
we
define the
influence function

\mathcal{I}\left(\cdot;T,\mu\right):Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{I}\left(x;T,\mu\right)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{T\left(\left(1-t\right)\mu+t\delta_{x}\right)-T\left(\mu\right)}{t}=DT\left(\mu;\delta_{x}\right)

When
T
is a-differentiable,

\mathcal{I}\left(x;T,\mu\right)=\int u_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\left(\delta_{x}-\mu\right)=u_{\mu}\left(x\right)-\int u_{\mu}d\mu

where
u_{\mu}\in\left[\nabla_{a}T\left(\mu\right)\right]
. Since the
gradient
u_{\mu}
is unique up to an additive constant, under the
normalization
\int u_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu=0
we get

\mathcal{I}\left(\cdot;T,\mu\right)=u_{\mu}\left(\cdot\right)\in C_{b}\left(Y\right)

(23)

The influence function thus completely pins down the a-differential of the
functional
T
as
\mu
varies. Conversely, the influence function agrees with
the gradient under the normalization condition
\int u_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu=0
.

Example 32

The functional

T:\mathcal{D}\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

defined by
T\left(F\right)=F\left(x_{0}\right)
,
with
x_{0}\in\left(0,1\right)
,
is
wa-differentiable. Indeed,

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{T\left(\left(1-t\right)F+tG\right)-T\left(F\right)}{t}=G\left(x_{0}\right)-F\left(x_{0}\right)

and the map

G\mapsto DT\left(F;G\right)=G\left(x_{0}\right)-F\left(x_{0}\right)
is affine. The influence function is given by

\mathcal{I}\left(x;T,F\right)=G_{x}\left(x_{0}\right)-F\left(x_{0}\right)

where
G_{x}
is the distribution function of the Dirac measure

\delta_{x}
. Clearly, it is discontinuous at

x_{0}
. By
(
23
), it should coincide with the continuous function
u_{F}
. Hence,
T

is not a-differentiable at any point

F\in\mathcal{D}\left[0,1\right]
.

\blacktriangle

Thus, the existence of the influence function does not ensure
a-differentiability. Next we show that this is the case even under the
existence of a continuous influence function (see also Example 2.2.3 of

[
23
]
).

Example 33

Define the convex functional
T:\mathcal{D}\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

T\left(F\right)=\sum_{x\in\left[0,1\right]}\left[F\left(x\right)-F\left(x-\right)\right]^{\alpha}

(24)

with
\alpha>1
. This functional measures the jumps of
F
. The series is well defined since there are at most countably many
jumps. Clearly,

\sum_{x\in\left[0,1\right]}\left[F\left(x\right)-F\left(x-\right)\right]^{\alpha}\leq\sum_{x\in\left[0,1\right]}\left[F\left(x\right)-F\left(x-\right)\right]\leq 1

and

the sum is finite.

It is easy to see that
T

is wa-differentiable, with

DT\left(F;G_{x}\right)=-\alpha T\left(F\right)+\alpha\left(F\left(x\right)-F\left(x-\right)\right)^{\alpha-1}

If the distribution
F

is continuous, then
DT\left(F;G_{x}\right)=0
.
We infer that the gradient should vanish everywhere
and, by Theorem
18
,
F
would be constant, a contradiction.
Hence,
T

is not a-differentiable.

\blacktriangle

5.2
Multi-utility representations

Let us now interpret an element
\mu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
as a lottery
with prizes in a metric space
Y
. A decision maker (DM) has a preference
(binary) relation
\succsim
over
\Delta\left(Y\right)
represented by a
utility function
U:\Delta\left(Y\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, namely,

\mu\succsim\lambda\Longleftrightarrow U\left(\mu\right)\geq U\left(\lambda\right)

We introduce the auxiliary subrelation
\succsim^{\ast}
defined by

\mu\succsim^{\ast}\lambda\Longleftrightarrow\alpha\mu+\left(1-\alpha\right)\nu\succsim\alpha\lambda+\left(1-\alpha\right)\nu

\text{for all }\alpha\in\left[0,1\right]\text{ and all }\nu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
. It captures the comparison over which the DM feels
sure (see
[
12
]
and
[
5
]
).

In the next theorem
\nabla_{a}U\left(\mu\right)
is understood to be a
normalized gradient. For instance,
\nabla_{a}U\left(\mu\right)=u_{\mu}

with
\int u_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu=0
, that is, the influence function associated
with the probability measure
\mu
. Moreover, we denote by
\operatorname{Im}\nabla_{a}U\subseteq C_{b}\left(Y\right)
the image
\left\{\nabla_{a}U\left(\lambda\right):\lambda\in\Delta\left(Y\right)\right\}
.

Theorem 34

If
\succsim
is a preference relation with a-differentiable
utility function
U
, then

\mu\succsim^{\ast}\lambda\Longleftrightarrow\int u\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int u\mathrm{d}\lambda\qquad\forall u\in\operatorname{Im}\nabla_{a}U

In the theory of risk, since Machina
[
17
]
the functions
u\in\operatorname{Im}\nabla_{a}U
are known as
local utilities
.

Theorem
34
formalizes the idea that, individually, each local
utility models a local expected utility behavior of
\succsim
, but jointly
all local utilities characterize a global expected utility feature of

\succsim
.

Proof
Define
\hat{\succsim}
by

\mu\,\hat{\succsim}\,\lambda\Longleftrightarrow\int u\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int u\mathrm{d}\lambda\qquad\forall u\in\operatorname{Im}\nabla_{a}U

Assume that
\mu\,\hat{\succsim}\,\lambda
. Let
\nu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
and
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
. By the mean value theorem (Theorem

18
),

d=U\left(\alpha\mu+\left(1-\alpha\right)\nu\right)-U\left(\alpha\lambda+\left(1-\alpha\right)\nu\right)=\alpha\left\langle\nabla_{a}U\left(\zeta\right),\mu-\lambda\right\rangle

where
\zeta=\alpha\left[\left(1-t\right)\mu+t\lambda\right]+\left(1-\alpha\right)\nu
for some
t\in\left(0,1\right)
. Since
\mu\,\hat{\succsim}\,\lambda
, by setting
u_{\zeta}=\nabla_{a}U\left(\zeta\right)
we have

d=\alpha\left[\int u_{\zeta}\mathrm{d}\mu-\int u_{\zeta}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right]\geq 0

that is,
\mu\succsim^{\ast}\lambda
. Conversely, assume
\mu\succsim^{\ast}\lambda
and pick any element
\nu\in\Delta\left(Y\right)
. It follows
that, for each
t\in\left[0,1\right]
,

\left(1-t\right)\nu+t\mu\succsim\left(1-t\right)\nu+t\lambda

Hence, for
t>0
,

\frac{U\left(\left(1-t\right)\nu+t\mu\right)-U\left(\nu\right)}{t}\geq\frac{U\left(\left(1-t\right)\nu+t\lambda\right)-U\left(\nu\right)}{t}

Letting
t\downarrow 0
, we get
\left\langle\nabla_{a}U\left(\nu\right),\mu-\nu\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\nabla_{a}U\left(\nu\right),\lambda-\nu\right\rangle
. Namely,
\left\langle\nabla_{a}U\left(\nu\right),\mu\right\rangle\geq\left\langle\nabla_{a}U\left(\nu\right),\lambda\right\rangle
. That is,
\mu\,\hat{\succsim}\,\lambda
as
desired.
\blacksquare

]
.

5.3
Prospect theory

To each monetary lottery
\mu\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
we
associate its distribution function
F_{\mu}\left(x\right)=\mu\left(\left(-\infty,x\right]\right)
. Fix a nonatomic positive Borel measure

\rho
on the real line and consider the utility function
U:\Delta\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

U\left(\mu\right)=\int_{\left[0,\infty\right]}w_{+}\left(1-F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)-\int_{\left[-\infty,0\right]}w_{-}\left(F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)

(25)

where
w_{+},w_{-}:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]

two strictly increasing and continuously differentiable maps. Instances of
this utility function appear in the Prospect Theory of Tverski and Kahneman

[
24
]
(see Wakker
[
26
]
). The utility function
U
is
wa-differentiable:
6
6

6

Continuous differentiability of
w_{+}

w_{-}

\displaystyle DU\left(\mu;\lambda\right)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{U\left(\mu+t\left(\lambda-\mu\right)\right)-U\left(\mu\right)}{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\left[0,+\infty\right]}w_{+}^{\prime}\left(1-F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\left(F_{\lambda}-F_{\mu}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)-\int_{\left[-\infty,0\right]}w_{-}^{\prime}\left(F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\left(F_{\lambda}-F_{\mu}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)

Hence,

(26)

is bounded and absolutely continuous. By the integration-by-parts formula,

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}\left(F_{\mu}\left(x\right)-F_{\lambda}\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\Phi_{\mu}\left(x\right)=\left[\left(F_{\mu}\left(x\right)-F_{\lambda}\left(x\right)\right)\Phi_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right]_{-R}^{R}-\int_{-R}^{R}\Phi_{\mu}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}\left(F_{\mu}-F_{\lambda}\right)

Letting

, we finally get

since
\left[\left(F_{\lambda}\left(x\right)-F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\Phi_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right]_{-R}^{R}\rightarrow 0
as

In sum,

\nabla_{a}U\left(\mu\right)=\Phi_{\mu}\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\text{ }

. By Fubini’s
Theorem,

\int\Phi_{v}\left(t\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(t\right)=\int\mathrm{d}\mu\left(t\right)\int I_{\left[-\infty,t\right]}\left(x\right)\varphi_{\nu}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)=\int\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)\varphi_{\nu}\left(x\right)\int I_{\left[x,+\infty\right]}\left(t\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(t\right)

where we used the relation
I_{\left[-\infty,t\right]}\left(x\right)=I_{\left[x,+\infty\right]}\left(t\right)
. Hence,

\int\Phi_{v}\left(t\right)\mathrm{d}\mu\left(t\right)=\int\varphi_{\nu}\left(x\right)\left(1-F_{\mu}\left(x-\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)=\int\varphi_{\nu}\left(x\right)\left(1-F_{\mu}\left(x\right)\right)\mathrm{d}\rho\left(x\right)

where the last equality is true because
\rho
is nonatomic. This proves
(
27
).
\blacksquare

5.4
Bayesian robustness

Let
\Theta
be a parameter space and
\mathcal{X}
a sample space. For any
given sample
x\in\mathcal{X}
, a
posterior functional

\rho_{x}:\Delta\left(\Theta\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
maps a prior distribution

\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)
to a scalar representing a posterior statistic of
interest. For example, the posterior mean is given by
\rho_{x}(\mu)=\int\theta\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}(\theta)
, with
\mu_{x}
being the Bayesian
update of
\mu
.

Bayesian robustness (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
) investigate how
posterior outcomes vary under different prior specifications, often by
examining the range of
\rho_{x}
over a set of priors
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Delta\left(\Theta\right)
. When
\mathcal{M}
is convex, our
methods can be used to compute such a range. Indeed, if
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\Delta\left(\Theta\right)
are such that
\rho_{x}(\mathcal{M})=[\rho_{x}(\mu_{1}),\rho_{x}(\mu_{2})]
, then by Proposition
28
it
follows that

\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}D\rho_{x}(\mu_{1};\nu)=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}D\rho_{x}(\mu_{2};\nu)=0

These two necessary conditions can be used to develop numerical algorithms
(see
[
1
]
).

Another statistical functional of interest is the expected posterior loss of a
Bayesian estimator. Formally, denote by
a\in A
the choice of the estimator.
Given a loss function
\ell:A\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a prior

\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)
, the expected loss
L(\mu)
of the Bayesian estimator
is

L(\mu)=\inf_{a\in A}\int\ell(a,\theta)\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)

Given an alternative prior
\nu\in\Delta(\Theta)
, the directional derivative

DL(\mu;\nu)
captures the sensitivity of the estimator to the prior
\mu

(see
[
16
]
and also
[
22
]
for applications in economics). In
the rest of this subsection we show that, thanks to Theorem
31
,
we are able to compute
DL(\mu;\nu)
in important cases of interest, allowing
for an unbounded set of parameters (as common in applications).

So, let

A=\Theta=\mathbb{R}
. Consider

\mu,\nu\in\Delta(\mathbb{R})

with finite first moment. To apply Theorem

31
we study the related optimization problem

L^{\ast}\left(\mu\right)=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}U\left(a,\mu\right)

where

U\left(a,\mu\right)=\int-\left|a-\theta\right|d\mu\left(\theta\right)
.

Proposition 37

The affine directional derivative
DL^{\ast}\left(\mu,\nu\right)
exists.
More specifically,

DL^{\ast}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\sup_{a\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)}DU_{a}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\sup_{a\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)}\int|a-\theta|\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)-\int|a-\theta|\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta)

(28)

Proof
As well-known, the set
\sigma\left(\lambda\right)
of maximizers of the section
U_{a}
for

\lambda\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

is a median value of the distribution
F_{\lambda}
. Specifically, a point
a\in\sigma\left(\lambda\right)
characterized
by the equations

F_{\lambda}\left(a\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad F_{\lambda}\left(a^{-}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}

(29)

The set
{\textstyle\bigcup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}}\sigma\left(\left(1-t\right)\mu+t\nu\right)
is contained into a
compact interval
K
of
\mathbb{R}
(just pick any compact interval that
contains the sets of points
1/2-\eta\leq F_{\mu}(x)\leq 1/2+\eta
and

1/2-\eta\leq F_{\nu}(x)\leq 1/2+\eta
for some
0<\eta<1/2
). By convexity,

\sigma\left(\left(1-t\right)\mu+t\nu\right)\subseteq K
for all

t\in\left[0,1\right]
. Let us show that the two assumptions of Theorem

31
are satisfied. Clearly,
U_{a}
is wa-differentiable and its
gradient is

DU_{a}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=\int|a-\theta|\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)-\int|a-\theta|\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta)

Hence, (i) holds. Take
t_{n}\downarrow 0
and let
\left\{a_{n}\right\}\subseteq K
be any sequence with
a_{n}\in\sigma\left(\left(1-t_{n}\right)\mu+t_{n}\nu\right)
. In view of Theorem
31
-(ii), consider any subsequence
\left\{a_{n_{k}}\right\}
such that the
sequence

\frac{U\left(a_{n_{k}},\left(1-t_{n_{k}}\right)\mu+t_{n_{k}}\nu\right)-U\left(a_{n_{k}},\mu\right)}{t_{n_{k}}}

(30)

converges. As
K
is compact, without loss of generality (passing if needed to
a further subsequence) we can assume that the sequence
\left\{a_{n_{k}}\right\}
converges to a point
\bar{a}
.

Claim
It holds
\bar{a}\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)
.

Proof
For any
\varepsilon>0
we have
a_{n}\leq\bar{a}+\varepsilon
for
n
sufficiently large. By monotonicity,

\frac{1}{2}\leq\left(1-t_{n}\right)F_{\mu}\left(a_{n}\right)+t_{n}F_{\nu}\left(a_{n}\right)\leq\left(1-t_{n}\right)F_{\mu}\left(\bar{a}+\varepsilon\right)+t_{n}F_{\nu}\left(\bar{a}+\varepsilon\right)

As
n\rightarrow\infty
we get
1/2\leq F_{\mu}\left(\bar{a}+\varepsilon\right)
. Since
F_{\mu}
is right-continuos we have
1/2\leq F_{\mu}\left(\bar{a}\right)
which is the first condition of (
29
). As to the
second one, begin now with
a_{n}\geq\bar{a}-\varepsilon
. Hence,
a_{n}-\eta\geq\bar{a}-\varepsilon-\eta
for any
\eta>0
. Thus

\left(1-t_{n}\right)F_{\mu}\left(\bar{a}-\varepsilon-\eta\right)+t_{n}F_{\nu}\left(\bar{a}-\varepsilon-\eta\right)\leq\left(1-t_{n}\right)F_{\mu}\left(a_{n}-\eta\right)+t_{n}F_{\nu}\left(a_{n}-\eta\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}

As
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get
F_{\mu}\left(\bar{a}-\varepsilon-\eta\right)\leq 1/2
for all
\varepsilon+\eta>0
. Hence, condition
(
29
) is true and so
\bar{a}\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)
.
\square

By this Claim, we can write

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{U\left(a_{n_{k}},\left(1-t_{n_{k}}\right)\mu+t_{n_{k}}\nu\right)-U\left(a_{n_{k}},\mu\right)}{t_{n_{k}}}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int|a_{n_{k}}-\theta|\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)-\int|a_{n_{k}}-\theta|\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int|\bar{a}-\theta|\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)-\int|\bar{a}-\theta|\mathrm{d}\nu(\theta)=DU_{\bar{a}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\leq\sup_{a\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)}DU_{a}\left(\mu,\nu\right)

This is the case for every subsequence
\left\{a_{n_{k}}\right\}
for
which (
30
) has a limit. Consequently,

\lim\sup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{U\left(a_{n},\left(1-t_{n}\right)\mu+t_{n}\nu\right)-U\left(a_{n},\mu\right)}{t_{n}}\leq\sup_{a\in\sigma\left(\mu\right)}DU_{a}\left(\mu,\nu\right)

and so assumption (ii) holds. By Theorem
31
we thus have
(
28
).
\blacksquare

6.1
Hadamard and strict differentiability

Endow the convex set
C
with a topology finer than (or equal to) than the
relative weak topology. This finer topology is assumed to be metrizable by a
metric
\rho
.
7
7

7

Usually the metric
\rho

X

\tau

vector structure of the space and
\rho

However, this does not significantly affect our analysis.
This allows us to
restrict ourself to the following sequential formulation of the Hadamard

8
8

8

We omit alternative

Definition 38

The (

)
Hadamard directional

of

at

D_{H}f\left(x;y\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f\left(\left(1-t_{n}\right)x+t_{n}y_{n}\right)-f\left(x\right)}{t_{n}}

when the limit exists finite for every sequence
t_{n}\downarrow 0
and every
sequence
\left\{y_{n}\right\}
in
C
with
\rho\left(y_{n},y\right)\rightarrow 0
.

The function

Analogously,
\nabla_{H}f\left(x\right)\in X^{\ast}

representative of the equivalence class of Hadamard gradients.

Definition 39

A function

for every sequence
t_{n}\downarrow 0
and every sequence
\left\{x_{n}\right\}\subseteq C
with
\rho\left(x_{n},x\right)\rightarrow 0
.

It is worth mentioning a stronger notion of differentiability that combines
the two previous ones.

Definition 40

A function

D_{SH}f\left(x;y\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f\left(\left(1-t_{n}\right)x_{n}+t_{n}y_{n}\right)-f\left(x_{n}\right)}{t_{n}}

Example 41

The quadratic functional
Q:\Delta\left(Y\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Then

\frac{Q\left(\left(1-t_{n}\right)\mu_{n}+t_{n}\lambda_{n}\right)-Q\left(\mu_{n}\right)}{t_{n}}=t_{n}Q\left(\lambda_{n}\right)+\left(t_{n}-2\right)Q\left(\mu_{n}\right)+2\left(1-t_{n}\right)\int\psi\left(x,y\right)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\otimes d\lambda_{n}

As

Hence, the limit of this ratio

.
\blacktriangle

This example can be easily generalized by showing that a quadratic functional

Definition 42

A metric
\rho

C

(i)

\rho\left(x,\alpha y_{1}+(1-\alpha)y_{2}\right)\leq\alpha\rho\left(x,y_{1}\right)+(1-\alpha)\rho\left(x,y_{2}\right)

(ii)

\rho\left(x,x+\alpha\left(y-x\right)\right)=\alpha\rho\left(x,y\right)

\alpha\in\left[0,1\right]
.

For instance, the Prokhorov metric on

]
). With this, next we establish a couple of
differentiability criteria.

Proposition 43

Let
\rho
be convex and
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
wa-differentiable in a
\rho
-neighborhood of a point
\bar{x}\in C
.

(i)

If the map
x\mapsto Df\left(x;y\right)
is
\rho
-continuous
at
\bar{x}
for every

f
is strictly wa-differentiable at

\bar{x}
.

(ii)

If the map
\left(x,y\right)\mapsto Df\left(x;y\right)
is

\rho
-continuous at
\left(\bar{x},y\right)
for every
y\in C
, then
f

is strictly Hadamard wa-differentiable at
\bar{x}
.

Proof
Let
x,y\in C
and
f
be wa-differentiable on

U_{\varepsilon}\left(\bar{x}\right)\cap C
, where
U_{\varepsilon}\left(\bar{x}\right)
is a
\rho
-neighborhood. Since
\rho
is convex,

\rho\left(x_{t},\bar{x}\right)\leq\left(1-t\right)\rho\left(x,\bar{x}\right)+t\rho\left(y,\bar{x}\right)\leq\rho\left(x,\bar{x}\right)+t\rho\left(y,\bar{x}\right)

Consequently, taking the points
x_{n}
such that
\rho\left(x_{n},\bar{x}\right)<\varepsilon/2
and
0<t_{n}<\varepsilon/\left[2\rho\left(y,\bar{x}\right)\right]
, then the points
x_{n}+t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)
and

belong to the neighborhood

. By Theorem
18

f\left(x_{n}+t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)\right)-f\left(x_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\tau_{n}}Df\left(x_{n}+\tau_{n}t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right);x_{n}+t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)\right)

for some
\tau_{n}\in\left(0,1\right)
. On the other hand,

x_{n}+t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)=\left(\frac{1-t_{n}}{1-\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right)\left[x_{n}+\tau_{n}t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)\right]+\left(\frac{\left(1-\tau_{n}\right)t_{n}}{1-\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right)y

Therefore, by (

\frac{f\left(x_{n}+t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right)\right)-f\left(x_{n}\right)}{t_{n}}=\frac{1}{1-\tau_{n}t_{n}}Df\left(x_{n}+\tau_{n}t_{n}\left(y-x_{n}\right);y\right)

\blacksquare

Here is a kind of converse of the previous statement.

Proposition 44

Let
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be strictly Hadamard

\bar{x}\in C

Df\left(x;y\right)

\rho

\bar{x}

is
\rho
-continuous at

\bar{x}

is
\rho
-continuous at
\bar{x}
.

Proof
Fix
y\in C
and consider two sequences
\left\{x_{n}\right\},\left\{y_{n}\right\}\subseteq C
so that
\rho\left(y_{n},y\right)\rightarrow 0
and
\rho\left(x_{n},\bar{x}\right)\rightarrow 0
and such that
\left\{x_{n}\right\}
is contained into the
claimed neighborhood of
\bar{x}

\varepsilon>0
. For every

Proposition 45

Proposition 48

Let
\rho
be convex. A function

is boundedly wa-differentiable at

\left\{y_{n}\right\}

Proof
An implication is obvious as

. Fix a sequence

\rho
-bounded sequence

. From

\rho\left(x,\left(1-\alpha\right)x+\alpha y_{n}\right)\leq\alpha\rho\left(x,y_{n}\right)

it follows that, for

sufficiently small, the sequence

belongs to

for all

, we
can write

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\frac{f\left(x+\tau_{n}\left(z_{n}-x\right)\right)-f\left(x\right)}{\tau_{n}}-A\left(z_{n}\right)\right]=0

We now introduce Frechet wa-differentiability. The convexity of the metric

\rho
is a convenient assumption as it will be readily seen (see Proposition

Definition 49

A function

is
Frechet wa-differentiable
at

if there exists a
\rho
-continuous and affine functional

, with

, such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f\left(x_{n}\right)-f\left(x\right)-A\left(x_{n}\right)}{\rho\left(x_{n},x\right)}=0

for every sequence
\left\{x_{n}\right\}
in
C

\rho\left(x_{n},x\right)\rightarrow 0

We can also formulate a “strict” version in
which we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f\left(y_{n}\right)-f\left(x_{n}\right)-A\left(y_{n}\right)}{\rho\left(x_{n},y_{n}\right)}=0

(35)

\rho\left(x_{n},x\right)\rightarrow 0
and
\rho\left(y_{n},x\right)\rightarrow 0
.

Proposition 50

Let
\rho
be convex. If

Frechet wa-differentiable at
x\in C

at
x
.

Proof
Assume (
34
). Given a sequence

and any bounded sequence

. Thanks to the convexity of

. Therefore,
\rho\left(x,z_{n}\right)\rightarrow 0
. In
light of (
34

f\left(z_{n}\right)-f\left(x\right)-A\left(z_{n}\right)=\rho\left(z_{n},x\right)o\left(1\right)

But
t_{n}^{-1}\rho\left(z_{n},x\right)\leq\rho\left(x,x_{n}\right)
,
which is bounded. Therefore,

f\left(\left(1-t_{n}\right)x+t_{n}x_{n}\right)-f\left(x\right)-t_{n}A\left(x_{n}\right)=o\left(t_{n}\right)

.
\blacksquare

A consequence of this proposition is that the affine function
A
in
(

. Moreover, a Frechet wa-differentiable function

Condition (

distance replaces a norm. In fact, (

\rho\left(y,x\right)\rightarrow 0

When

Next we give a partial converse of the last result.

\tau_{t}\in\left(0,1\right)
. To simplify notation, let us

\overline{\tau}_{t}A=Df\left(H_{t};H_{t}+t\overline{\tau}_{t}\alpha\left(y_{1}-x\right)\right)

\overline{\tau}_{t}=1-\tau_{t}

\beta_{t}=\alpha t\bar{\tau}_{t}
, we get by tedious algebra the

Df\left(H_{t};H_{t}+t\bar{\tau}_{t}\alpha\left(y_{1}-x\right)\right)=Df\left(H_{t};\bar{\beta}_{t}H_{t}+\beta_{t}(y_{1}+K_{t}\right)

y_{1}+K_{t}\in C

3

Hence,

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{A}{t}=\alpha\lim_{t\downarrow 0}Df\left(H_{t};y_{1}+K_{t}\right)

Clearly,
H_{t}\rightarrow x
and
K_{t}\rightarrow 0.
From the joint
hemicontinuity of

, it follows the limit value

\alpha\lim_{t\downarrow 0}Df\left(x;y_{1}\right)
. Therefore we have
proved (
38
). At last, by replacing (

) into
