A C 1 C^{1} -conforming arbitrary-order two-dimensional virtual element method for the fourth-order phase-field equation
Dibyendu Adak, Gianmarco Manzini, Hashem M. Mourad, JeeYeon N. Plohr, Lampros Svolos

Abstract
We present a two-dimensional conforming virtual element method for the fourth-order phase-field equation. Our proposed numerical approach to the solution of this high-order phase-field (HOPF) equation relies on the design of an arbitrary-order accurate, virtual element space with C 1 C^{1} global regularity. Such regularity is guaranteed by taking the values of the virtual element functions and their full gradient at the mesh vertices as degrees of freedom. Attaining high-order accuracy requires also edge polynomial moments of the trace of the virtual element functions and their normal derivatives. In this work, we detail the scheme construction, and prove its convergence by deriving error estimates in different norms. A set of representative test cases allows us to assess the behavior of the method.



1
Introduction

Fracture is a critical failure mode that can cause a rapid loss of
load-carrying capacity and uncontrolled demolition of inhabited
structures.
The prevention of such catastrophic outcomes is the motivation to
develop computationally efficient models of fracture, which are also
capable of accurately capturing material behavior in the post-failure
regime.
The efficient solution to fracture problems remains one of the most
critical research priorities despite the progress made over the past
decades.
To this end, developing efficient numerical techniques with predictive
capabilities requires a multidisciplinary approach.

Over the past decade, phase field (PF) modeling of fracture has gained
significant attention due to its ability to capture complicated crack
patterns (e.g., merging or branching) by utilizing conventional finite
element techniques.
The first PF fracture method is traced back to Bourdin
et al.
[
18
]
, where a numerical implementation
of the variational approach to fracture

[
27
]
was introduced.
Inspired by Griffith’s work, the variational
approach describes fracture as a minimization problem of a total
energy functional, which expresses the competition between bulk
elastic energy and crack surface energy.
By introducing an auxiliary field (denoted by
u
in this paper),
crack surfaces are represented by “diffuse” entities, obviating the
need for injecting discontinuities into the kinematic solution fields.
In the seminal work of Miehe et al.
[
35
]
,
a framework based on continuum mechanics and thermodynamic
arguments was proposed to model brittle fracture.
It was further developed to treat dynamic brittle failure in

[
17
]
.
Moreover, the PF framework was extended to model cohesive fracture in

[
44
]
and
ductile fracture in

[
34
]
.
More recently, multiphysics coupling effects are studied in problems
of brittle fracture
[
46
]
, cohesive fracture

[
38
]
, ductile fracture

[
24
,
41
,
42
]
,
and structural fragmentation
[
36
]
.
The interested reader is refereed to

[
25
,
37
]

for more applications of the PF method and to

[
45
]
for an extensive review.

The numerical solution to PF fracture problems may require a highly
refined mesh to accurately resolve the steep PF gradients that develop
in the vicinity of a crack
[
45
]
, thus being
expensive and possibly non-competitive with respect to other
approaches despite all its modeling capabilities.
Adopting the popular second-order PF fracture model exacerbates this
issue, as it leads to the development of a cusp in the solution field
at the crack surface that negatively impacts the convergence of the
numerical solution when the mesh is refined.
To address this issue, Borden et al.
[
16
]

proposed a high-order phase-field (HOPF) model, where the solution
regularity is increased, and better spatial convergence behavior can
be achieved.
The HOPF model involves a trade-off, as it necessitates using
specialized computational techniques that ensure
C^{1}
-continuity
of the PF unknown to attain convergence.
For example, the Finite Element Method (FEM) with a

C^{0}
-continuous Lagrange polynomial basis is not a good choice.
Its use to treat higher-order PF problems results in discontinuities
in the PF gradient.
Thus, it constitutes a variational crime
[
40
]
, since the
weak form of such problems includes at least second-order spatial
derivatives, e.g., the Laplacian and the Hessian differential
operators.

High regularity of the numerical approximation is of primary
importance when dealing with high-order differential problems.
In addition, global smoothness can be utilized to directly compute
physical quantities (such as fluxes, strains, and stresses) without
resorting to post-processing as in the
C^{0}
-FEM.
From the earliest works in the 1960s,
e.g.,
[
7
,
14
]
,
to the most current attempts,
e.g.,
[
47
,
32
]
, there
are several examples of finite elements with regularity higher than

C^{0}
.
The construction of approximation spaces with such global regularity
has been seen as challenging since they require basis functions with
the same global regularity.
Designing approximations with such enhanced regularity is still an
active research topic.
A non-exhaustive list includes the FEM with Hermite polynomial basis
functions
[
39
]
or B-spline basis functions
(i.e., isogeometric analysis)

[
9
]
;
machine learning techniques (e.g. physics informed neural networks

[
29
]
); fast
Fourier transform (FFT)-based methods

[
33
]
; mixed FEM

[
26
]
;
continuous/discontinuous Galerkin (C/DG) methods

[
43
]
; discontinuous Galerkin
(DG) methods
[
28
]
.
Such approximations have a natural application in the numerical
treatment of problems involving high-order differential operators, as
in the HOPF problem.

The Virtual Element Method (VEM) was initially designed as a
Galerkin-type projection method to extend the FEM from
triangular/tetrahedral and quadrilateral/hexahedral meshes to
polytopal meshes.
The VEM does not need explicit knowledge of the basis functions
spanning the approximation
spaces
[
10
]
.
Instead, local approximation spaces are constructed by solving a
partial differential equation at the element level. These local spaces
are subsequently “glued” together to form a highly regular,
conforming global approximation space.
The basis functions in this case are referred to as “virtual”
because they are not computed in closed form. Only the projection of
the basis onto a subspace of polynomials is known, and is utilized in
the method’s formulation.
The conforming VEM was first developed for second-order elliptic
problems in primal
formulation
[
10
]
,
and then in nonconforming formulation
[
8
]
.
The first works using a
C^{1}
-regular conforming VEM addressed the
classical plate bending
problems
[
20
,
22
]
, second-order
elliptic
problems
[
12
,
13
]
,
and the nonlinear Cahn-Hilliard
equation
[
3
]
.
In
[
6
]
, a highly-regular conforming
VEM is proposed for the two-dimensional polyharmonic problem

(-\Delta)^{p_{1}}u=f
,
p_{1}\geq 1
.
The VEM is based on an approximation space that locally contains
polynomials of degree
r\geq 2p_{1}-1
and has a global
H^{p_{1}}

regularity.
In
[
5
]
, this formulation was
extended to a virtual element space that can have arbitrary regularity

p_{2}\geq p_{1}\geq 1
and contains polynomials of degree

r\geq p_{2}
.
Highly-regular conforming VEM in any dimension has been proposed in

[
21
]
.

HOPF models of dynamic fracture are based on two coupled
governing equations: the momentum conservation equation and the HOPF
evolution equation.
In our recent work

[
4
]
, we developed
a VEM for the momentum equation with linear elastic constitutive
laws.
In the present work, we propose a VEM for the other ingredient of
HOPF fracture models, namely the HOPF evolution equation itself.
The coupling between these two virtual element models is a
non-trivial task, and will be the topic of our future work.
Specifically, we design a conforming VEM for the fourth-order equation
with Laplace and
L^{2}
terms.
The numerical approximation relies on an arbitrary order accurate,
virtual element space with
H^{2}
global regularity.
The degrees of freedom of the lowest-order accurate
C^{1}
-VEM are
the values of the virtual element functions and their gradients at the
mesh vertices.
Attaining high-order accuracy requires additional edge polynomial
moments of the trace of the virtual element functions and their normal
derivatives.
This choice of the degrees of freedom guarantees the global

H^{2}
regularity.
To avoid the computational complexity of the lower order terms, we
introduce an elliptic projection operator that combines the biharmonic
and Laplace operators.

The calculation of the elliptic projection in every cell reduces to a
single matrix calculation instead of two, and our approach requires
less computation than the ones proposed in the existing VEM
literature.
Furthermore, the use of a single elliptic projection simplifies the
fixing of the kernel that reduces to the kernel of the Laplacian
operator.
This technique reduces the computational cost significantly for
higher order VEM spaces and, potentially, for higher dimensions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we briefly discuss the strong and
weak formulations of the HOPF model along with the mathematical
arguments proving their well-posedness.
In Section
3
, we construct the virtual
element approximation of the HOPF equation through the definition of
the virtual element space and the bilinear forms and linear functional
required by the variational formulation.
In Section
4
, we conduct a convergence analysis of the
proposed VEM by deriving error estimates of the discrete scheme.
In Section
5
, we discuss the implementation of
the method and provide details on the discretization.
In Section
6
, we carry out a numerical
investigation about the performance of the proposed method by solving
a manufactured solution problem on a set of representative polygonal
meshes.
In addition, we show that optimal convergence rates are attained for
an example involving a diagonal crack modeled by the phase-field
method.
In Section
7
, we offer our final conclusions and
remarks on possible future work.

2
The high-order phase-field model

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbbm{R}^{2}
be a simply-connected, open, bounded domain
with polygonal boundary
\Gamma
.
The HOPF model is given by the linear, fourth-order, partial
differential equation problem for the real, scalar unknown
u
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}u-\alpha_{1}\Delta u+\alpha_{0}u

\displaystyle=f\phantom{g_{0}g_{1}}\quad\textrm{in~}\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g_{0}\phantom{fg_{1}}\quad\textrm{on~}\Gamma,

(1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u

\displaystyle=g_{1}\phantom{fg_{0}}\quad\textrm{on~}\Gamma,

(1c)

where
\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are strictly-positive,
real constant coefficients;
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is the load term;

g_{0}\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)
and
g_{1}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
are
the univariate functions definining the Dirichlet boundary conditions
on
\Gamma
.

Consider the affine space

V=\big\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega),{v}_{|{\Gamma}}=g_{0},{\partial_{n}v}_{|{\Gamma}}=g_{1}\big\}
,
and its linear subspace
V_{0}=H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
, which we
equivalently define by setting
g_{0}=g_{1}=0
in
V
.
The weak formulation of problem (
1
) reads as:

\displaystyle\mbox{\emph{Find $u\in V_{0}$ such that~}}\quad\mathcal{A}(u,v)=F(v)\quad\forall v\in V_{0},

(2)

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}:V_{0}\times V_{0}\to\mathbbm{R}
and the linear
functional
F:V_{0}\to\mathbbm{R}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{2}(u,v)+\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(u,v)+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}(u,v),

(3)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}(u,v):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\Delta v\,\,d\mathbf{x},\quad\mathcal{A}_{1}(u,v):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\,d\mathbf{x},\quad\mathcal{A}_{0}(u,v):=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\,v\,d\mathbf{x},

(4)

and

\displaystyle F(v):=\int_{\Omega}fv\,d\mathbf{x}.

For the exposition’s convenience, we will also use the additional
bilinear form
\mathcal{B}:V_{0}\times V_{0}\to\mathbbm{R}
given by

\mathcal{B}(u,v):=\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{2}(u,v)+\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(u,v)
,
which is clearly such that
\mathcal{A}(u,v)=\mathcal{B}(u,v)+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}\big(u,v\big)
.
The positive sublinear functional

||\,\cdot\,||_{V_{0}}:V_{0}\to\mathbbm{R}^{+}
given by

\displaystyle||v||_{V_{0}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\big(\left|\Delta v\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}+\left|v\right|^{2}\big)\,d\mathbf{x}

(5)

is a norm on
V_{0}
, and it trivially holds that

||v||_{V_{0}}\leq 2||v||_{2,\Omega}
.
The bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive with respect to the
norm
||\,\cdot\,||_{V_{0}}
.
The coercivity of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
with coercivity constant

\alpha=\min(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
follows on noting that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(v,v)\geq\min(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\int_{\Omega}\big(\left|\Delta v\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}+\left|v\right|^{2}\big)\,d\mathbf{x}=\min(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})||v||_{V_{0}}^{2},

(6)

since
\min(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})>0
.

The symmetric bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is an inner product
on
H^{2}(\Omega)
since from the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)

it follows that
\mathcal{A}(v,v)=0
implies that
v=0
.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies an upper bound for

\mathcal{A}(u,v)
with continuity constant equal to

\max(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(v,v)

\displaystyle\leq\max(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\int_{\Omega}\big(\left|\Delta v\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla v\right|^{2}+\left|v\right|^{2}\big)\,d\mathbf{x}=\max(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})||v||_{V_{0}}^{2}.

(7)

The well-posedness follows from an application of the Lax-Milgram
theorem since
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive and continuous and

F(\cdot)
is continuous
[
19
]
.

4

In this section, we prove the convergence of the VEM by quantifying

To prove such estimates, we need the regularity results associated

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\xi-\alpha_{1}\Delta\xi+\alpha_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=g,\phantom{0}\quad\text{in~}\Omega,

\displaystyle\xi=\partial_{n}\xi

\displaystyle=0,\phantom{g}\quad\text{on~}\Gamma.

Since

1.

the regularity result holds:

(21)

2.

there exists a real number

is bounded from below by

\rho h_{\textrm{E}}
, i.e.,
h_{\textrm{e}}\geq\rho h_{\textrm{E}}
.

A consequence of these properties is that the element
E
admits a
uniformly shape-regular subtriangulation, i.e., the minimum angle of
all the subtriangles partitioning
E
is bounded from below by some
positive constant independent of
h
(and
E
).

4.2
A priori error estimates

The convergence of the virtual element method, which we state in
Theorem
4.5
below, follows from the (standard) error bounds
for the polynomial projection and interpolation operators stated in
two technical lemmas, e.g., Lemma
4.2
and
Lemma
4.3
, which we report below for
completeness without proof (for a proof, see

[
20
,
1
]
) and the
abstract convergence result of Lemma
4.4
.
To ease the notation, in both lemma statements we adopt the convention

|\,\cdot\,|_{0,\textrm{E}}=||\,\cdot\,||_{0,\textrm{E}}
to denote the

L^{2}
-norm over
E
.

Lemma 4.2
(Polynomial Approximation)

Under mesh regularity assumptions
\mathbf{(A1)}
-
\mathbf{(A2)}
,
see Assumption
4.1
, there exists a positive
constant
C
independent of
h
such that for all

v\in H^{\delta}(\textrm{E})
,
\delta
being a real number such that

0\leq\delta\leq k+1
, there exists a polynomial approximation

v_{\pi}\in\mathbbm{P}_{k}(\textrm{E})
, such that

\displaystyle|v-v_{\pi}|_{\ell,\textrm{E}}\leq Ch_{\textrm{E}}^{\delta-\ell}|v|_{\delta,\textrm{E}},\quad\ell=0,\ldots,[\delta],

where
[\delta]
denotes the largest integer equal to or smaller
than
\delta
.
The constant
C
may depend on the mesh regularity parameter

\rho
.

Lemma 4.3
(Virtual element interpolation)

Under mesh regularity assumptions
\mathbf{(A1)}
-
\mathbf{(A2)}
,
see Assumption
4.1
, there exists a positive
constant
C
independent of
h
such that for all

v\in H^{\delta}(\textrm{E})
,
\delta
being a real number such that

2\leq\delta\leq k+1
, there exists a virtual element approximation

v_{\footnotesize{I}}\in V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
such that

\displaystyle|v-v_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{\ell,\textrm{E}}\leq Ch_{\textrm{E}}^{\delta-\ell}|v|_{\delta,\textrm{E}},\quad\ell=0,1,2.

The constant
C
may depend on the mesh regularity parameter

\rho
.

Lemma 4.4
(Abstract result)

Let
u
be the solution of the variational
problem (
2
) and
u_{h}
the solution of the virtual
element method (
8
) under mesh regularity
assumptions
\mathbf{(A1)}
-
\mathbf{(A2)}
.
Then, there exists a positive constant
C>0
, such that the
(piecewise discontinuous) polynomial approximation

u_{\pi}\in\mathbbm{P}_{k}(\Omega_{h})
from Lemma
4.2
, and
the interpolation approximation
u_{\footnotesize{I}}\in V_{k}^{h}
of
u
from
Lemma
4.3
satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle|u-u_{h}|_{2,\Omega}\leq C\Big(|u-u_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{2,\Omega}+|u-u_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}+||f-f_{h}||_{(V_{r}^{h})^{\prime}}\Big),

(23)

where
||\cdot||_{(V_{k}^{h})^{\prime}}
denotes the norm of

(V_{k}^{h})^{\prime}
, the dual space of
V_{k}^{h}
.

Proof.

Let
u_{I}
be the interpolant of
u
that
satisfies Lemma
4.3
, and set

\delta_{h}:=u_{h}-u_{\footnotesize{I}}\in V_{k}^{h}
.
Adding and subtracting
u_{\footnotesize{I}}
and using the Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality yield

\displaystyle|u-u_{h}|_{2,\Omega}\leq|u-u_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{2,\Omega}+|\delta_{h}|_{2,\Omega}.

Lemma
4.3
provides the upper bound for the
first term on the right-hand side, i.e.,

|u-u_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{2,\Omega}
.
Furthermore, by using the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
with
coercivity constant
C_{\alpha}=\min(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,
the definition of
\delta_{h}
, equations (
2
)
and (
8
), and the polynomial consistency of

\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we find that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}|\delta_{h}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{A}_{h}(\delta_{h},\delta_{h})=\mathcal{A}_{h}(u_{h},\delta_{h})-\mathcal{A}_{h}(u_{\footnotesize{I}},\delta_{h})

\displaystyle=f_{h}(\delta_{h})-\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}(u_{\footnotesize{I}}-u_{\pi},\delta_{h})-\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}(u_{\pi},\delta_{h})

\displaystyle=f_{h}(\delta_{h})-f(\delta_{h})-\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\mathcal{A}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}(u_{\footnotesize{I}}-u_{\pi},\delta_{h})+\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{E}}(u-u_{\pi},\delta_{h}).

We conclude the proof of inequality (
23
) by
employing the continuity of
\mathcal{A}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}(\cdot,\cdot)
and

\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{E}}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

The following theorem states the convergence of the virtual element
method and an error estimate for the approximation error measured
using the seminorm
|\,\cdot\,|_{2,\Omega}
(which is a norm on

H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
).

Theorem 4.5
(Convergence in
H^{2}
-seminorm)

Let
u
be the solution of the variational
problem (
2
) with
f\in H^{k-1}(\Omega)
and
u_{h}

the solution of the virtual element method (
8
) under mesh
regularity assumptions
\mathbf{(A1)}
-
\mathbf{(A2)}
.
Then, there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|u-u_{h}|_{2,\Omega}\leq Ch^{k-1}\Big(|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+||f||_{k-1,\Omega}\Big).

The constant
C
is independent of
h
, but depends on the

\alpha
-coefficients associated with the model problem, the mesh
regularity constant
\rho
, and the stability constants of the
bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Proof.

The assertion of the theorem is a straightforward consequence of the
abstract convergence result stated in
Lemma
4.4
, the error bounds from
Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
, and a
standard estimate of the source error term

||f-f_{h}||_{(V_{r}^{h})^{\prime}}
in the right-hand side of
inequality (
23
) along with the regularity
of
f

(cf.
[
10
]
).

Theorem 4.6
(Convergence in
H^{1}
-seminorm)

Let
u
be the solution of the variational
problem (
2
) with
f\in H^{k-1}(\Omega)
and
u_{h}

the solution of the virtual element method (
8
) under mesh
regularity assumptions
\mathbf{(A1)}
-
\mathbf{(A2)}
.
Then, there exists a positive constant
C>0
, such that

\displaystyle|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}\leq Ch^{k}\Big(|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+||f||_{k-1,\Omega}\Big).

(24)

The constant
C
is independent of
h
, but depends on the

\alpha
-coefficients associated with the model problem, the mesh
regularity constant
\rho
, and the stability constants of the
bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Proof.

To prove the assertion of the theorem, we use the duality argument.
Let
\xi
be the solution of the variational formulation of the auxiliary
problem

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\xi-\alpha_{1}\Delta\xi+\alpha_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=-\Delta(u-u_{h})\phantom{0}\quad\textrm{in~}\Omega,

(25a)

\displaystyle\xi=\partial_{n}\xi

\displaystyle=0\phantom{-\Delta(u-u_{h})}\quad\textrm{on~}\Gamma.

(25b)

From suitable hypothesis on
\Omega
, and a standard regularity
argument, see
[
30
]

and (
21
)-(
22
), we know that

\displaystyle||\xi||_{3,\Omega}\leq C||\Delta(u-u_{h})||_{-1}\leq C||u-u_{h}||_{1,\Omega}\leq C|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega},

(26)

where the last step follows from the Poincare inequality, i.e.,

||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}\leq C|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}
, which
holds because
u-u_{h}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
For
\xi
at least in
H^{3}(\Omega)
, the following interpolation
estimate holds:

\displaystyle||\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}||_{0,\Omega}+h_{\textrm{E}}|\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{1,\Omega}+h_{\textrm{E}}^{2}|\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{2,\Omega}\leq Ch_{\textrm{E}}^{3}|\xi|_{3,\Omega}.

(27)

Note that

|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}=-(\Delta(u-u_{h}),u-u_{h})_{0,\Omega}

since the traces of
(u-u_{h})
and
\partial_{n}(u-u_{h})
are zero
on the boundary of
\Omega
.
We multiply both sides of (
25a
) by
u-u_{h}
,
integrate over
\Omega
and integrate by parts the
\Delta^{2}
and

\Delta
terms.
Then, we add and subtract the virtual element interpolant

\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}\in V_{r}^{h}
, cf. Lemma
4.3
, and
we obtain

\displaystyle|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\big(\Delta(u-u_{h}),u-u_{h}\big)_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle=\big(\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\xi-\alpha_{1}\Delta\xi+\alpha_{0}\xi,u-u_{h}\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{2}(u-u_{h},\xi)+\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(u-u_{h},\xi)+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}(u-u_{h},\xi),

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\mathcal{A}(u-u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\Big((f,\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})-(f_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})\Big)+\Big(\mathcal{A}_{h}(u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})-\mathcal{A}(u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mbox{$\mathbf{(T1)}$}+\mbox{$\mathbf{(T2)}$}+\mbox{$\mathbf{(T3)}$}.

(28)

To complete the proof of inequality (
24
), we derive an
upper bound for the three terms
\mathbf{(T1)}
,
\mathbf{(T2)}
,

\mathbf{(T3)}
separately.

We bound the first term as follows below by using the continuity of
the bilinear forms
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and

\mathcal{A}_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and (
27
)
and (
26
):

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mathbf{(T1)}$}=\mathcal{A}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})=\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{2}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\alpha_{2})|u-u_{h}|_{2,\Omega}\,|\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{2,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{1})|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}\,|\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}|_{1,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{0})||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}\,||\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\alpha_{2})h^{k}|u|_{k+1}\,|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{1})h^{2}|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+C(\alpha_{0})h^{3}|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}.

Since we assume that
f\in H^{k-1}(\Omega)
, we bound the second
term by noting that for all
k\geq 2
, the quantity

(f-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k-2}f)
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the constant
functions, e.g.,
\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{0}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}
.
Therefore, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the approximation
properties of the interpolation operator, the
L^{2}
-orthogonal
projection operator, and (
27
)
and (
26
) yield:

\mathbf{(T2)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\int_{\textrm{E}}\big(f-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k-2}f\big)\,\big(\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{0}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}\big)\,d\mathbf{x}\leq||f-\Pi^{0}_{k-2}f||_{0,\Omega}\,||\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\Pi^{0}_{0}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}|f|_{k-1,\Omega}\,\Big(||\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi||_{0,\Omega}+||\xi-\Pi^{0}_{0}\xi||_{0,\Omega}+||\Pi^{0}_{0}(\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})||_{0,\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k}|f|_{k-1,\Omega}\,|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}.

(29)

Finally, we bound the term
\mathbf{(T3)}
by using the (local)
polynomial consistency, which allows us to rewrite the term

\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
as follows

\mbox{$\mathbf{(T3)}$}=\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\Big(\mathcal{A}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}(u_{h}-u_{\pi},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi})+\mathcal{A}^{\textrm{E}}(u_{\pi}-u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi})\Big).

(30)

An application of the continuity property of
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)

and
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the approximation properties of
Lemmas
4.2
, and
4.3
,
and (
27
) yield

\displaystyle\left|\mbox{$\mathbf{(T3)}$}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta^{\ast})\big[\mathcal{A}(u_{h}-u_{\pi},u_{h}-u_{\pi})\big]^{1/\penalty{2}}\,\big[\mathcal{A}(\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi})\big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta^{\ast})\,\Big[\alpha_{2}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{1}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{0}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\Big[\alpha_{2}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{1}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{0}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta^{\ast},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{0})h^{k}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\Big[\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}h^{2}+\alpha_{0}h^{3}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}.

Finally, we substitute the upper bounds of terms
\mathbf{(T1)}
,

\mathbf{(T2)}
, and
\mathbf{(T3)}
into (
28
), and obtain the
assertion of the lemma for
h\to 0
.

Now, we focus to derive the convergence analysis in the
L^{2}
norm.

Theorem 4.7
(Convergence in
L^{2}
-norm)

Let
u
be the solution of the variational
problem (
2
) with
f\in H^{k-1}(\Omega)
and
u_{h}

the solution of the virtual element method (
8
) under mesh
regularity assumptions
4.1
(as in
Theorem
4.5
).
Then, the following estimates holds

\displaystyle||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\Big(|u|_{3,\Omega}+||f||_{1,\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle\text{for}~k=2

(31a)

\displaystyle||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+s}\Big(|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+||f||_{k-1,\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle\text{for}~k\geq 3,

(31b)

where
s
is the regularity index of the function
u
as mentioned
in (
22
).

Proof.

Estimate (
31a
) directly follows from an
application of Theorem
4.6
, Eq. (
24
), and the
Poincaré inequality

||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}\leq C|u-u_{h}|_{1,\Omega}
, which
holds since
u-u_{h}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
To prove inequality (
31b
), we use the duality argument.
Let
\xi\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
be the solution of the variational formulation of the auxiliary
problem

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\xi-\alpha_{1}\Delta\xi+\alpha_{0}\xi

\displaystyle=-\Delta(u-u_{h})\phantom{0}\quad\textrm{in~}\Omega,

(32a)

\displaystyle\xi=\partial_{n}\xi

\displaystyle=0\phantom{-\Delta(u-u_{h})}\quad\textrm{on~}\Gamma.

(32b)

According to
[
31
,
22
]
, we deduce the
following regularity result:

\displaystyle||\xi||_{3+s}\leq C||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}

(33)

for some real number
s\in(1/2,1]
.
We multiply (
32a
) by
u-u_{h}
and integrate
over the domain
\Omega
, and we find that

\displaystyle||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}^{2}=(u-u_{h},u-u_{h})=\Big(\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\xi-\alpha_{1}\Delta\xi+\alpha_{0}\xi,u-u_{h}\Big).

Using the same arguments of the derivation of (
28
), we
obtain

\displaystyle||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\Big((f,\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})-(f_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})\Big)+\Big(\mathcal{A}_{h}(u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})-\mathcal{A}(u_{h},\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\mbox{$\mathbf{(T1)}$}+\mbox{$\mathbf{(T2)}$}+\mbox{$\mathbf{(T3)}$}.

(34)

Upon splitting the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
into the

H^{2}
-,
H^{1}
-, and
L^{2}
-scalar products, using the
continuity of the bilinear forms, and again the interpolation
estimate for
\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}
and (
33
), we obtain that

\mathbf{(T1)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\mathcal{A}_{2}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\alpha_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}(u-u_{h},\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha_{2})h^{k+s}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\,||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{1})h^{k+s+1}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\,||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+C(\alpha_{0})h^{k+s+2}||u||_{k+1,\Omega}\,||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq h^{k+s}\Big(C(\alpha_{2})|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{1})h|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+C(\alpha_{0})h^{2}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\Big)||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}.

(35)

Since we assume that
f\in H^{k-1}(\Omega)
, we bound the second
term by noting that for all
k\geq 3
, the quantity

(f-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k-2}f)
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the linear polynomial
functions, e.g.,
\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{1}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}
.
Therefore, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the approximation
properties of the interpolation operator, the
L^{2}
-orthogonal
projection operator, and (
27
)
and (
26
) yield:

\mathbf{(T2)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}}\int_{\textrm{E}}\big(f-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k-2}f\big)\,\big(\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{1}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}\big)\,d\mathbf{x}\leq||f-\Pi^{0}_{k-2}f||_{0,\Omega}\,||\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\Pi^{0}_{1}\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}||_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}|f|_{k-1,\Omega}\,\Big(||\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi||_{0,\Omega}+||\xi-\Pi^{0}_{1}\xi||_{0,\Omega}+||\Pi^{0}_{1}(\xi-\xi_{\footnotesize{I}})||_{0,\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k}|f|_{k-1,\Omega}\,||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}.

(36)

Finally, we proceed to estimate last term
\mathbf{(T3)}
.
To this end, we employ the polynomial consistency property of the
discrete bilinear forms, the polynomial approximation property of
Lemma
4.2
, and the approximation property of the
interpolation operator of Lemma
4.3
, and we
deduce

\mathbf{(T3)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta^{\ast})\Big[\alpha_{2}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{1}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{0}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\Big[\alpha_{2}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{1}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{0}|\xi_{\footnotesize{I}}-\xi_{\pi}|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta^{\ast})\Big[\alpha_{2}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{2,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{1}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\alpha_{0}|u_{h}-u_{\pi}|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times h^{1+s}\Big[\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}h^{2}+\alpha_{0}h^{4}\Big]^{1/\penalty{2}}||\xi||_{3+s,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\beta^{\ast},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{0}\big)h^{k+s}|u|_{k+1}\,||u-u_{h}||_{0,\Omega}.

(37)

Upon inserting the estimates of (
35
), (
36
), and
(
37
) into (
34
), we obtain the required result
for
h\to 0
.

5
Implementation

In this section, we briefly describe how we implemented the VEM.
The approach follows the general guidelines
of
[
11
]
, here adapted to the
biharmonic problem.

5.1
Vector and matrix notation

We consider the following compact notation.
For all element
\textrm{E}\in\Omega_{h}
, we locally number the degrees of freedom
(
\mathbf{D1}
), (
\mathbf{D2}
), (
\mathbf{D3}
), and (
\mathbf{D4}
) from
1
to
N^{\text{dofs}}
.
Then, we introduce the bounded, linear functionals

\text{dof}_{i}:V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

i=1,\dots,N^{\text{dofs}}
,
such that

\displaystyle\text{dof}_{i}\left(v_{h}\right)~:=\text{~$i$-th~degree~of~freedom~of~}v_{h}

for
v_{h}\in V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
.
Let
\Lambda_{\textrm{E}}=\big\{\text{dof}_{i}\left(\cdot\right)\big\}_{i}
denote the set of
such functionals and collect the degrees of freedom of
v_{h}
in the
vector

\mathbf{v}_{h}=\big(\text{dof}_{1}\left(v_{h}\right),\ldots,\text{dof}_{N^{\text{dofs}}}\left(v_{h}\right)\big)^{T}
.
Since the degrees of freedom (
\mathbf{D1}
), (
\mathbf{D2}
), (
\mathbf{D3}
), and
(
\mathbf{D4}
) are unisolvent in
V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
, the triplet

\big(\textrm{E},V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E}),\Lambda_{\textrm{E}}\big)
is a finite element in the sense
of Ciarlet, cf.
[
23
]
.
This property implies the existence of a Lagrangian basis set

\big\{\varphi_{i}\big\}_{i}
, with
\varphi_{i}\in V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
,

i=1,\dots,N^{\text{dofs}}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\text{dof}_{i}\left(\varphi_{j}\right)=\delta_{ij},\quad i,j=1,2,\dots,N^{\text{dofs}}.

We refer to the basis function set
\big\{\varphi_{i}\big\}_{i}
as the
“canonical” basis of
V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
.
We introduce the compact notation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\varphi_{N^{\text{dofs}}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\text{{T}}},

and write the expansion of a virtual element function
v_{h}
on such a
basis set as

\displaystyle v_{h}(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})^{\text{{T}}}\mathbf{v}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\text{dofs}}}\text{dof}_{i}\left(v_{h}\right)\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\textrm{E}.

We also introduce a compact notation for the basis of the polynomial subspace

\mathbbm{P}_{k}(\textrm{E})\subset V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
, which reads as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}(\mathbf{x})=\left(m_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,m_{n_{k}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\text{{T}}},

where
n_{k}
is the cardinality of
\mathbbm{P}_{k}(\textrm{E})
.
Since the polynomials
m_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
are also virtual element
functions, we can expand them on the canonical basis
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.
We express such expansions as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}(\mathbf{x})^{\text{{T}}}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})^{\text{{T}}}\boldsymbol{D},

where matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
has size
N^{\text{dofs}}\times n_{k}
and collects
all the expansion coefficients

\displaystyle D_{i\ell}=\text{dof}_{i}\left(m_{\ell}\right),

so that

\displaystyle m_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\text{dofs}}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})D_{i\ell}\quad\ell=1,\ldots,n_{k}.

Following this notation, we also express the action of a differential operator
\mathcal{D}
, e.g.,

\mathcal{D}=\Delta
or
\mathcal{D}=\nabla
,
in a entry-wise way, so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\mathcal{D}\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\mathcal{D}\varphi_{N^{\text{dofs}}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\text{{T}}},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{m}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\mathcal{D}m_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\mathcal{D}m_{n_{k}}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{\text{{T}}}.

Similarly, we express the action of the
projectors
\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}
,
\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}
, and
\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}
on the canonical basis functions

\boldsymbol{\varphi}

and their expansion
on the polynomial basis
\boldsymbol{m}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{1},\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{2},\ldots\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{N^{\text{dofs}}}\right]=\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k},

\displaystyle\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{1},\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{2},\ldots\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{N^{\text{dofs}}}\right]=\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k},

\displaystyle\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}

\displaystyle=\left[\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{1},\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{2},\ldots\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{N^{\text{dofs}}}\right]=\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}.

The expansion coefficients for the three projection operators applied
to the basis function
\varphi_{j}
are collected along the
j
-th
column of the projection matrices
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}
,

\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}
.

5.2
Elliptic projector operator

Using this compact notation, we rewrite the variational
problem (
10
)-(
11
)
that
defines the polynomial projection
\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}
on the virtual element
space
V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
as
\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{B}
, where

\boldsymbol{G}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}+\boldsymbol{G}^{0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k}}
,

\boldsymbol{B}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}+\boldsymbol{B}^{0}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{k}\times N^{\text{dofs}}}
,
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{2}+\alpha_{1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}_{1}=\alpha_{2}\int_{\textrm{E}}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{1}\int_{\textrm{E}}\nabla\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}+\alpha_{1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}=\alpha_{2}\int_{\textrm{E}}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{1}\int_{\textrm{E}}\nabla\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x},

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\text{{T}}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}^{\text{{T}}}\end{array}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{B}^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\Pi^{\nabla,\textrm{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\text{{T}}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}^{\text{{T}}}\end{array}\right),\qquad

We can split the linear system providing the projection matrix

\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}
into the summation of the two conditions

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
, which
expresses (
10
), and

\boldsymbol{G}^{0}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{B}^{0}
, which
espresses (
11
).
The latter condition fixes the kernel of the differential operator

\mathcal{L}
.
Therefore, matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
is nonsingular by construction, and we
can formally state that
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}
.

A (double) integration by parts of the right-hand-side of the
definition of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
yields

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2}+\alpha_{1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\int_{\textrm{E}}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{1}\int_{\textrm{E}}\nabla\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\textrm{E}}\Big[\alpha_{2}\Delta^{2}\boldsymbol{m}-\alpha_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\Big]\boldsymbol{\varphi}\,d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\textrm{E}}\Big[\big(\partial_{n}(-\alpha_{2}\boldsymbol{m}+\alpha_{1}\boldsymbol{m})\big)\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}+\alpha_{2}\Delta\boldsymbol{m}\big(\partial_{n}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\big)\Big]\,d\mathbf{x}.

According to what has already been observed about the computability
of the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}(v_{h},q)
, we see that the last
right-hand side above can be easily computable by an evaluation of
the volume and surface integrals through the degrees of freedom of

\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.
We perform the numerical integration of the volume integral through
the degrees of freedom (
\mathbf{D4}
) and the numerical integration of
the edge integrals by evaluating the trace of each basis function

\phi_{i}
and its normal derivative
\partial_{n}\phi_{i}
through the
univariate polynomial interpolation of the degrees of freedom
(
\mathbf{D1}
), (
\mathbf{D2}
), and (
\mathbf{D3}
).

5.3
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector operator

Using this compact notation, we rewrite the variational
problem (
10
)-(
11
) that defines the

L^{2}
-orthogonal polynomial projection
\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}
on the virtual
element space
V_{k}^{h}(\textrm{E})
as
\mathbf{H}\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}=\mathbf{C}
,
where

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\int_{\textrm{E}}\boldsymbol{m}\,\boldsymbol{m}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{C}=\int_{\textrm{E}}\boldsymbol{m}\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\text{{T}}}\,d\mathbf{x}.

We recall that the entries of matrix
\mathbf{C}
that correspond to the
polynomial moments of the Lagrangian basis function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}

against the polynomial of degree up to
k-2
are computable from the
degrees of freedom (
\mathbf{D4}
).
The enhancing condition in the definition of the local virtual element
space (
12
) makes it possible to compute the
polynomial moments of the basis functions
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
against the
polynomials of
\mathbbm{P}_{k}(\textrm{E})\setminus\mathbbm{P}_{k-2}(\textrm{E})
by using the elliptic
projector.
Since matrix
\mathbf{H}
is nonsingular by construction, we can formally
state that
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}=\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{C}
.

5.4
Local matrices

The stiffness matrix is given by the sum of two terms: a
rank-deficient term, which is responsible for the accuracy of the
method, and a stability term, which fixes the correct rank:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}:=\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}^{\text{cons}}+\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}^{\text{stab}},

where

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}^{\text{cons}}\right)_{ij}=\mathcal{B}_{h}^{\textrm{E}}\big(\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{i},\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{j}\big)=\bigg(\big(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\big)^{T}\left[\alpha_{2}\int_{\textrm{E}}\Delta\mathbf{m}\Delta\mathbf{m}^{T}\,d\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{1}\int_{\textrm{E}}\nabla\mathbf{m}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{m}^{T}\,d\mathbf{x}\right]\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\bigg)_{ij}

and

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}^{\text{stab}}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=S^{\textrm{E}}_{h}\left(\left(I-\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\right)\varphi_{i},\left(I-\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\right)\varphi_{j}\right)=\sum_{\ell}\text{dof}_{\ell}\left(\left(I-\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\right)\varphi_{i}\right)\,\text{dof}_{\ell}\left(\left(I-\Pi^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\right)\varphi_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\,\big(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\big)^{T}\,\big(\mathsf{I}-\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\mathcal{L},\textrm{E}}_{k}\big)\,\bigg)_{ij}.

The construction of the local mass matrix uses the
L^{2}
-orthogonal
projection:

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\textrm{E}}\right)_{ij}:=\left(\boldsymbol{M}_{\textrm{E}}^{\text{cons}}\right)_{ij}=\alpha_{0}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\textrm{E}}\big(\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{i},\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{j}\big)=\bigg(\big(\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\big)^{T}\left[\alpha_{0}\int_{\textrm{E}}\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{T}\,d\mathbf{x}\right]\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\bigg)_{ij}.

Note that we do not need to specify a stabilization term in the local
mass matrix.

5.5
Right-hand side approximation

Using again the compact notation, we rewrite the virtual element
approximation of the right-hand side (
20
) as

\displaystyle\langle f_{h},\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\textrm{E}}=\int_{\textrm{E}}f\Pi^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\varphi_{i}\,d\mathbf{x}=\bigg(\left[\int_{\textrm{E}}f\mathbf{m}^{T}\,d\mathbf{x}\right]\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{0,\textrm{E}}_{k}\bigg)_{i}.

(38)

5.6
Formulation of VEM in matrix representation

We assemble the local matrices
\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}
and

\boldsymbol{M}_{\textrm{E}}
, the forcing term

\mathbf{F}_{\textrm{E}}=\langle f_{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\textrm{E}}
and incorporate the boundary
conditions into the global matrices
\boldsymbol{K}
and

\boldsymbol{M}
and the right-hand side vector
\mathbf{F}
in a finite element
way.
The final linear system reads as:

\displaystyle\big(\boldsymbol{K}+\boldsymbol{M}\big)\mathbf{u}_{h}=\mathbf{F},

where
\mathbf{u}_{h}
is the vector collecting the degrees of freedom of the
virtual element solution
u_{h}
.
The final matrix
\boldsymbol{K}+\boldsymbol{M}
is nonsingular by
construction because of the stability property imposed on the local
matrices
\boldsymbol{K}_{\textrm{E}}+\boldsymbol{M}_{\textrm{E}}
and the boundary
conditions.

Test Case 2. Diagonal crack example: Length-scale sensitivity

To showcase the merits of the VEM in PF fracture problems, a
manufactured solution to HOPF equation is assumed as follows

\displaystyle u(x,y)

\displaystyle=\exp(-(x-y)^{2}/\epsilon),\qquad\epsilon\in\big\{1,10^{-1},10^{-2}\big\}.

computational domain
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)

The length-scale parameter
\epsilon

sharp-crack limit.

7
Conclusions and final remark

In this work, we proposed a
C^{1}
-regular virtual element method
for the numerical approximation of a fourth-order phase-field
equation.
The virtual element formulation presented in this work employs a
polyomial projection operator that combines the biharmonic and Laplace
differential operators.
We proved the convergence of the method and derived optimal
convergence rates in different norms.
The theoretical convergence results were confirmed by conducting
numerical experiments on a set of four polygonal meshes.
The good approximation properties of the proposed VEM are also
demonstrated in the context of a problem involving HOPF representation of a crack.

Developing analogous discrete spaces and carrying out the
a
priori
analyses for higher dimension model problems will be the topic
of future research.
Furthermore, coupling the HOPF evolution equation with the
momentum balance equation forms the basis of HOPF models of dynamic
fracture.
With this in mind, in future work, we will be focusing our efforts on
coupling the VEM that we recently developed for the momentum equation
in
[
4
]
with the VEM
presented herein.
