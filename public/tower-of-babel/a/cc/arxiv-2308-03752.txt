Arithmetic groups Lecture notes version 0.6
Vincent Emery

Abstract




Introduction

Arithmetic groups are defined by generalizing the construction of the

modular group

\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, which is obtained by taking matrices
with integral coefficients in the larger group
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. As a first
– obvious – generalization, we can give the example of
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
: we
have just increased the dimension.

The modular group appears in several different areas of mathematics, and
it is not surprising that the same is true for arithmetic groups.
For instance, let us mention:

Number theory

The modular group is used to define the
functional equations satisfied by
modular forms
, which
play a central role in analytic number theory. Using more general
arithmetic groups instead of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, one can extend the notion
of modular forms to the more general setting of
automorphic forms
.

Hyperbolic geometry

The
n
-sphere
S^{n}
is a Riemannian manifold of constant positive sectional
curvature. It is compact. For any dimension
n
, there also
exist compact
n
-manifolds with constant
negative
sectional
curvature. Such a manifold (compact or not) is called hyperbolic.
In low dimensions (up to
5
) there exist geometric constructions of
compact hyperbolic manifolds. But for
n>5
one needs to study
arithmetic groups to construct such
n
-manifolds
M
. Arithmetic groups
will appear as their fundamental groups
\pi_{1}(M)
.

Expander graphs

Expander graphs
are finite graphs that despite relatively
small number of edges have very good connectivity.
These graphs are useful in many areas of mathematics, but their
importance first became clear in computer science, with application
to coding theory notably. One way to obtain expander graphs is to
consider the Cayley graphs of the finite groups
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})
for

n>2
. The idea of this construction – due to Grigori Margulis –
uses deep facts from the representation theory of
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Using
other arithmetic groups (or generalizations of them) and their finite quotients,
one can obtain many more construction of expander graphs.
See for instance the survey by Lubotzky
[
Lub12
]
for more information
about expander graphs.

As for
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, an arithmetic group
\Gamma
is
naturally embedded in a larger group
G
– typically a real Lie group.
What makes arithmetic groups interesting is that in many cases
\Gamma

is a lattice in
G
, which means that the quotient
G/\Gamma
has finite
volume. A lattice
\Gamma\subset G
is called
uniform
if
the quotient
G/\Gamma
is compact. In this course we will mainly study
arithmetic groups that are uniform lattices.

This course is based on many parts of the lecture notes
[
Ben08
]

by Yves Benoist.
We refer to these notes and to
[
Mor14
]
for more comprehensive
texts on arithmetic groups and lattices. Lizhen Ji’s book
[
Ji08
]
is an
excellent survey about the different connections of arithmetic groups
with various subjects in mathematics.

Chapter 1
Euclidean lattices

This chapter serves as an introduction to the more general notion of
lattice that will be presented in Chapter 2; we will also prove Mahler’s
compactness criterion (Theorem
1.21
), which will be
important to us in Chapter 5.

1.1
Definitions and basic facts

We consider the Euclidean
n
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
the standard (positive definite) scalar product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
and the associated norm
|\!|\cdot|\!|
.

Definition 1.1
.

A
(Euclidean) lattice
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a subgroup
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(with respect to vector addition) of the form

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}x_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}x_{n},

(1.1)

where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Equivalently, a Euclidean lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an abelian
subgroup such that

1.

L
spans
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

2.

and
L
has rank
n
.

Example 1.3
.

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a Euclidean lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in (
1.1
), we define its
covolume

– denoted
\covol(L)
–
as the Lebesgue measure
\lambda(\mathcal{F})
of the

fundamental domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;a_{i}\in\left[0,1\right[\right\}.

(1.2)

In other words, the covolume of
L
is defined as the Euclidean volume
of the parallelotope
\mathcal{F}
. As is well known, this volume is given by the
square root of the determinant of the Gram matrix, that is,

\displaystyle\covol(L)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\det(\left<x_{i},x_{j}\right>)}.

(1.3)

Remark 1.4
.

The definition of the covolume does not depends on a choice of the

\mathbb{Z}
-basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
: if
x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}
is another
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
of
L
, then the associated fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has volume

|\det(A)|\cdot\lambda(\mathcal{F})
, where
A
is the matrix representing the
bases change
x_{i}\to x_{i}^{\prime}
. But since
(x_{i})
and
(x_{i}^{\prime})
are both

\mathbb{Z}
-bases of
L
, then
A\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and thus
\det(A)=\pm 1
.

A fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}
has the property that all its translated
sets
x+\mathcal{F}
with
x\in L
are mutually disjoint.
Moreover, the union of all translated sets covers
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a (measurable) set such that all
x+S

are mutually disjoint for
x\in L
. Then
\vol(S)\leq\covol(L)
.

Proof.

Since
\bigcup_{x\in L}x+\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a disjoint cover, we have

\displaystyle\vol(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in L}\vol(S\cap(x+\mathcal{F}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in L}\vol((S-x)\cap\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\vol\left(\bigcup_{-x\in L}(x+S)\cap\mathcal{F}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\vol(\mathcal{F}).

For the last equality (3rd line) we use the fact that the
x+S
are disjoint.
∎

Recall that a topological space
X
is called
discrete
if every
point
x\in X
is open, i.e., all singletons
\left\{x\right\}
are
open (and consequently every subset
A\subset X
is open). Clearly

\mathbb{R}^{n}
is not discrete, whereas its subgroup
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(with respect to the
induced topology) is discrete.

Lemma 1.6
.

Any lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is discrete.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be a basis of
L
, and
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be the
standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The linear map sending
x_{i}
to
e_{i}
(for

i=1,\dots,n
) is an homeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to itself, which
sends
L
to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Thus
L
is discrete, because
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is.
∎

Using the same argument, we see that the set
L\setminus\left\{0\right\}
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It follows that the norm function
|\!|\cdot|\!|

takes a minimum on
L\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.

Definition 1.7

(Systole)
.

For a Euclidean lattice
L
we define its
systole
as the
(positive) real number

\displaystyle\syst(L)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in L\setminus\left\{0\right\}}|\!|x|\!|.

Proposition 1.8
.

For any lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have:

\displaystyle\syst(L)\leq 2\left(\frac{\covol(L)}{\nu_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}},

where
\nu_{n}
is the volume of the
n
-dimensional ball of radius
1
.

Proof.

Let
B_{R}(0)
be an open ball around
0
such all translated
x+B_{R}(0)
are mutually disjoint, and choose
R
to be maximal for this property.
Then by Lemma
1.5
, we have
R^{n}\nu_{n}\leq\covol(L)
.
By maximality of
R
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
x\in L

such that
B_{R+\varepsilon}(0)
and
B_{R+\varepsilon}(x)

intersect. By the triangular inequality, this implies
|\!|x|\!|\leq 2(R+\varepsilon)
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain
x\in L

with
|\!|x|\!|\leq 2R
.
∎

1.2
Topological description

Using topology, one can give an equivalent definition for the notion of
Euclidean lattice. This will motivate the consideration of lattices as
subgroups of a group
G
more general than
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see the next chapter).
First, let recall some basic elements useful to study the
interplay between group theory and topology.

Definition 1.9
.

A
topological group

G
is a group whose underlying set is a
topological space such that the map
f:G\times G\to G
given
by
f(x,y)=xy^{-1}
is continuous.

Remark 1.10
.

From the definition, it follows that the inverse map
x\mapsto x^{-1}
is continuous, and for any element
a\in G
the (left)
multiplication map
\mu_{a}:x\mapsto ax
is continuous (and
similarly for right multiplication).

Remark 1.11
.

A subgroup
H\subset G
of a topological group
G
is again a
topological group, with respect to the induced topology.

Example 1.12
.

The additive group
G=(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
is a topological group with
respect to the real topology on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
: the map
f
is given by

f(x,y)=x-y
and is obviously continuous.

Let
H\subset G
be a subgroup of a topological group
G
. Not only
H

possesses a natural topology determined by
G
, but so does the
quotient set
G/H
as well:

Definition 1.13
.

The
quotient topology
on
G/H
is defined as the finest
topology making the projection map
\pi:G\to G/H
continuous.

Remark 1.14
.

It follows from the definition that a subset
A\subset G/H
is open
if and only the preimage
\pi^{-1}(A)
is open in
G
.

Lemma 1.15
.

The projection map
\pi:G\to G/H
is an open map
(it sends open sets onto open sets)

Proof.

For
U\subset G
open, we have to show that
\pi(U)
is open.
But by Remark
1.14
this is the case exactly when

\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))
is open. The latter is equal to
UH=\bigcup_{h\in H}Uh
,
which is open since each
Uh
is open.
∎

With the definition of the quotient topology we have enough information to state
the topological characterization of Euclidean lattices:

Proposition 1.16
.

A subgroup
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Euclidean lattice if and only if

1.

\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
is compact;

2.

and
L
is discrete.

Proof.

We prove the easy direction of the implication: let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a Euclidean lattice. We have already seen in
Lemma
1.6
that
L
is discrete.
Consider the closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of some fundamental domain for

L
. If
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
denotes the projection map, we can
check directly that
\pi(\overline{\mathcal{F}})=\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
. But
\overline{\mathcal{F}}

is compact, so that
\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
is compact.

For the other direction we refer to
[
ST02
, Theorems 6.1 and
6.6]
(note that the definition of “lattices” from

[
ST02
]
allows
L
to have rank
<n
).
∎

Remark 1.17
.

The second condition in Proposition
1.16

indicates that
L
is relatively small compared to its ambient
space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the other hand, the first condition shows that
L

still “captures” much of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. These two opposite aspects also
appear for the conditions given in
Remark
1.2
.

Remark 1.18
.

Consider the special case
n=1
and
L=\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then the
quotient
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is homeomorphic to the circle
S^{1}
, which is compact.
More generally, for a lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the quotient
\mathbb{R}^{n}/L

is homeomorphic to the
n
-torus
S^{1}\times\cdots\times S^{1}
.
See
[
ST02
, Theorem 6.4]
.

1.3
The space of Euclidean lattices

For some fixed
n
, let
X
denote the set of lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The group
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
acts on
X
on the left: any
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

transforms a lattice
L
into a lattice
gL
.
Moreover, this action is transitive as any lattice
L
can be written
L=gL_{0}
for some
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
L_{0}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The stabilizer of

L_{0}
is the subgroup
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
(matrices with entries in
\mathbb{Z}
and
determinant
\pm 1
). This gives an identification

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}).

(1.4)

Using this identification we can easily put a topology on
X
, namely
the quotient topology, where the topology on
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is the usual
one – induced as a subset of the real vector space
\mathrm{Mat}(n\times n,\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 1.19
.

Both functions
\covol(\cdot)
and
\syst(\cdot)
are continuous on
the space
X
.

Proof.

Denoting by
\pi:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to X
the projection map, we have that the
map
f=\covol\circ\pi
is given by
f(g)=|\det(g)|
. It is obviously
continuous on
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. For
U\subset\mathbb{R}
an open set, the preimage

f^{-1}(U)
is thus open. But
\pi
is open
(Lemma
1.15
),
so that
\pi(f^{-1}(U))=\covol^{-1}(U)
also is open; this proves
the continuity of the covolume on
X
.

The proof for the systole follows the same argument, noting that
f(g)=\syst(gL_{0})

is continuous on
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Our goal is to prove Mahler’s compactness theorem (below), which will
be an important ingredient later during the course. For this we need the following fact
about the geometry of lattices.

Lemma 1.20
.

Let
a>1
. There exists a constant
C(n,a)
such that any lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

with
\syst(L)>1/a
and
\covol(L)<a
has
a basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with
|\!|x_{i}|\!|\leq C(n,a)
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction. If
n=1
, the result is clear for
C(1,a)=a
.
Suppose the result correct for
n-1
, and let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lattice with

\syst(L)>1/a
and
\covol(L)<a
. Let us choose a nonzero
v\in L
of minimal
length, i.e., with
|\!|v|\!|=\syst(L)
. We consider
V=v^{\perp}
, the real subspace
orthogonal to
v
, with the orthogonal projection
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V
. The projection

L^{\prime}=p(L)
is a lattice of
V
with
\syst(L^{\prime})\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\syst(L)
(exercise).
Thus,
\syst(L^{\prime})>\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2a}
.
Moreover, one has
\covol(L)=|\!|v|\!|\covol(L^{\prime})
and so
\covol(L^{\prime})<a^{2}
.

By the induction assumption, there exists a basis
x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n-1}^{\prime}
of
L^{\prime}
such that

|\!|x_{i}^{\prime}|\!|\leq C(n-1,\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}a^{2})
.
By definition of
L^{\prime}
, any
x_{i}^{\prime}
is obtained as the projection
p(x_{i})
for some
x_{i}\in L
.
Moreover, by adding an integral multiple of
v
, we can choose
x_{i}
such that its orthogonal
projection on the line
\mathbb{R}v
has norm less than
|\!|v|\!|
.
But by Proposition
1.8
, the systole
|\!|v|\!|
can be bounded by some constant
D(n,a)
that
depends only on
n
and
a
. By the triangular inequality, we have

|\!|x_{i}|\!|\leq D(n,a)+C(n-1,\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}a^{2})
, and we choose this
upper bound to be the constant
C(n,a)
.

We show that for
x_{n}=v
, the vectors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
form a
basis of
L
. The linear independence is clear, and it remains to
show that these vectors generates
L
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
x\in L
, and
write
p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}
, with
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then for
y=x-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
we have
p(y)=0
, and thus
y
is contained in
L\cap\mathbb{R}x_{n}
. But since

\syst(L)=|\!|x_{n}|\!|
, one has necessarily
y=\lambda_{n}x_{n}
for
some
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This gives
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
,
with integer coefficients.
∎

Finally we state and prove the main theorem of this chapter. Recall that a subset
A\subset X
is
called
relatively compact
if its closure
\overline{A}
is compact.

Theorem 1.21

(Mahler’s compactness criterion)
.

A subset
A\subset X
is relatively compact if and only if both

\covol(\cdot)
and
\syst(\cdot)^{-1}
are bounded on
A
.

Proof.

Let us write
f(L)=\syst(L)^{-1}
. If
A
is relatively compact,
then both
f(\overline{A})
and
\covol(\overline{A})
are compact
subset of
\mathbb{R}
, so that
f(A)
and
\covol(A)
are bounded.

For the other direction, let us suppose
f
and
\covol
bounded on

A
. Then
f
and
\covol
are also bounded on the closure
\overline{A}
.
By Lemma
1.20
, for each
L\in\overline{A}
we can choose
a basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with the norms
|\!|x_{i}|\!|
bounded by some constant
C

(which does not depend on
L
). Denoting
g_{L}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
the matrix with
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

as column vectors, we have
\pi(g_{L})=L
. Let
B=\left\{g_{L}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\,|\,L\in\overline{A}\right\}
,
so that
\pi(B)=\overline{A}
. From Proposition
1.8
and the hypothesis on the systole,
we see that
|\det(g_{L})|=\covol(L)
is bounded away from
0
on
B
. Thus, the closure
\overline{B}

is included in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
B
is bounded,
\overline{B}
is compact. We obtain that
the closed set
\overline{A}
is contained in the compact set
\pi(\overline{B})
,
which implies that
A
is relatively compact.
∎

Remark 1.22
.

For some authors, and in the French terminology, the definition of
“compact” for a space
X
requires that
X
is Hausdorff (as opposed
to the term “quasicompact” for
X
that has the Heine-Borel property
but does not need to be Hausdorff).
We have not worried about this, but we will show in the next chapter
that the quotients we have considered (namely
\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
and

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
) are indeed Hausdorff. See also
Remark
1.18
.

Chapter 2
Lattices in topological groups

In this chapter we define the notion of a lattice in a
topological group
G
. This will generalized the notion of Euclidean
lattice seen in the previous chapter (case
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

2.1
Discrete subgroups and quotients

In this section we will suppose that the topology on the group
G
comes
from a metric. This assumption will simplify the proofs of the results
below (they still hold under weaker assumptions). Note that
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
G=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
are metric space – and all topological groups that
we are going to consider will be metric as well. We denote by
\Gamma\subset G
some discrete subgroup.

Lemma 2.1
.

The subset
\Gamma
is closed in
G
.

Proof.

Since
G
is a metric space, a subset
A\subset G
is closed if any sequence in

A
that converges in
G
has its limit in
A
. Let
(\gamma_{n})
a
sequence in
\Gamma
converging to an element
g\in G
. It follows
that
\mu_{n}=\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n+1}^{-1}
converges to
1\in\Gamma
.
But
\Gamma
is discrete, so that
\mu_{n}
eventually stabilizes to
1
.
Hence
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n+1}
for
n
large enough, and the limit
certainly belongs to
\Gamma
.
∎

The group structure on
\Gamma
is essential in this proof, as the
following counterexample shows.

Example 2.2
.

The subset
\left\{\frac{1}{n}\;|\;n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\right\}
is
discrete, but not closed in
\mathbb{R}
.

Since
G
is a metric space, it is necessarily Hausdorff. The previous
lemma then implies the following.

Corollary 2.3
.

The quotient space
G/\Gamma
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

We use the following general fact: a topological space is Hausdorff
if and only the diagonal
\left\{(x,x)\;|\;x\in X\right\}

is closed in
X\times X
; equivalently
\left\{(x,y)\in X\times X\;|\;x\neq y\right\}
is open.
Consider the product map
\phi=\pi\times\pi:G\times G\to G/\Gamma\times G/\Gamma
; it is continuous with respect to the product
topologies. Moreover, since
\pi
is open, the map
\phi
is open as well.
Since
\Gamma
is closed and
G
is a topological group, the subset

R=\left\{(g,h)\;|\;gh^{-1}\in\Gamma\right\}
is closed in

G\times G
. Then
\phi((G\times G)\setminus R)
is open in
G/\Gamma\times G/\Gamma
. But this subset is exactly the set of pairs

(g\Gamma,h\Gamma)
with
g\Gamma\neq h\Gamma
. Thus
G/\Gamma
is
Hausdorff.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists an open neighbourhood
U\subset G
of
1
such that

U\gamma\cap U=\emptyset
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{1\right\}
.

Proof.

Suppose that no such neighbourhood
U
exists. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
let
U_{n}=B_{1/n}(1)
be the open ball of radius
1/n
around the
identity
1\in G
. Then for each
n
there exist
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma\setminus\left\{1\right\}
such that the intersection

U_{n}\gamma_{n}\cap U_{n}
is nonempty. Let
g_{n}
be an element in this
intersection.
Then
g_{n}
converges to
1
when
n
goes to infinity, and

g_{n}\gamma_{n}^{-1}\to 1
as well.
This is only possible if
\gamma_{n}\to 1
, which contradicts the
fact that
\Gamma
is discrete.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

The projection map
\pi:G\to G/\Gamma
is a local homeomorphism,
that is, each
g\in G
has an open neighbourhood
U
such that

\pi|_{U}:U\to\pi(U)
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

Let
g\in G
and let
U
be an open neighbourhood of
1
as in
Lemma
2.4
. Then
\pi:gU\to\pi(gU)
is
bijective: the surjectivity is obvious and the injectivity follows
from the particular choice of
U
. But since
\pi
is open, this means
that the restriction
\pi|_{gU}
is a homeomorphism.
∎

2.2
Locally compact groups and measure

To define the notion of lattice in complete generality (in the next
section), we need a measure on the topological group
G
that is invariant.
We study the existence of such a measure in this section.

Definition 2.6

(Locally compact group)
.

A
locally compact group

G
is a topological group that is
Hausdorff and locally compact: every
g\in G
has a compact
neighbourhood.

Example 2.7
.

\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
are locally compact. Any discrete group is
locally compact.

Let us consider the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
of
Borel
sets
, that is,
\mathcal{B}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
open subsets of
G
. A measure
\mu
on the measure space
(G,\mathcal{B})
is called
(left) invariant
if for any
g\in G
and
A\in\mathcal{B}
, one has:

\displaystyle\mu(gA)=\mu(A).

(2.1)

A measure
\mu
on
G
will be called
regular
if for any
A\in\mathcal{B}
and any
V\subset G
open, one has:

\displaystyle\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\mu(U)\,|\,A\subset U,U\mathrm{open}\right\};

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu(V)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\mu(K)\,|\,K\subset V,K\mathrm{compact}\right\}.

(2.3)

Theorem 2.8

(Haar’s theorem)
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group. There exists on
(G,\mathcal{B})

a nonzero regular left invariant measure
\mu
that is finite on compact
subset:
\mu(K)<\infty
for any compact
K\in\mathcal{B}
.
Moreover, up to a scalar this measure is unique: if
\mu^{\prime}
is another
such measure, then
\mu^{\prime}=c\mu
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Definition 2.9

(Haar measure)
.

The measure
\mu
given by the preceding theorem is called a

Haar measure
on
G
.

Example 2.10
.

The Lebesgue measure
\lambda
is a Haar measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is
normalized by
\lambda([0,1]^{n})=1
.

Example 2.11
.

The group
\mathbb{R}^{\times}=\GL_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is locally compact. A Haar measure

\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mu(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\frac{1}{|x|}d\lambda(x)\,.

This last example generalizes to the following.

Example 2.12
.

Let
G=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, seen as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and let

\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on the latter. Then a Haar measure

\mu
on
G
is given by:

\displaystyle\mu(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\frac{1}{|\det(g)|^{n}}d\lambda(g)\,.

2.3
Lattices in locally compact groups

Proposition
1.16
, which gives a topological
characterization of Euclidean lattices, suggests to define a lattice in
a topological group
G
as a discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset G
with

G/\Gamma
compact. But another important feature of any Euclidean
lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the existence of a fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of finite volume and such that
L+\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{R}^{n}

(equivalently: the projection
\pi:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/L
is surjective).
It turns out that this viewpoint is more convenient to extend
to notion of lattice to more general groups.

Definition 2.13

(Lattice)
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group, with Haar measure
\mu
.
A discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset G
is called a
lattice

if there exists a measurable set
\mathcal{F}\subset G
such that

\mathcal{F}\Gamma=G
and
\mu(\mathcal{F})<\infty
. In this case we say that

\Gamma
has
finite covolume
.

Remark 2.14
.

By uniqueness of the Haar measure (cf. Theorem
2.8
), this
definition does not depend on the choice of
\mu
.

Remark 2.15
.

Let
\pi:G\to G/\Gamma
be the projection map. The condition
\mathcal{F}\,\Gamma=G
is equivalent to say that
\pi(\mathcal{F})=G/\Gamma
.

Example 2.16
.

A subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a lattice if and only if it is a
Euclidean lattice in the sense of Defintion
1.1
.
The measurable set
\mathcal{F}
can be chosen to be a fundamental domain
determined by the choice of a basis of
\Gamma
.

Remark 2.17
.

We will not use this notion in this course, but a
fundamental
domain
for
\Gamma
in
G
could be defined as a measurable set

\Gamma\subset G
such that
\mathcal{F}\Gamma=G
and which is minimal for this
property. More precisely, the covering
G=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathcal{F}\gamma
should be a disjoint union.

The compactness of the quotient
G/\Gamma
implies a finite covolume for

\Gamma\subset G
, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 2.18
.

If
\Gamma\subset G
is a discrete subgroup such that
G/\Gamma
is
compact, then
\Gamma
is a lattice.

Proof.

For each
y\in G/\Gamma
let us choose
x\in G
with
\pi(x)=y
.
We write
U_{y}\subset G
for an open neighbourhood of
x
, and (since

G
is locally compact) we can choose
U_{y}
to be contained in a compact subset
and thus to have finite measure. Since
\pi
is open, the collection of
subsets
\pi(U_{y})
(when
y
runs over all elements in
G/\Gamma
) in an open
cover of
G
.
By compactness of
G/\Gamma
there exists a finite subcover

\pi(U_{y_{1}}),\dots,\pi(U_{y_{n}})
that suffices to cover all
G
.
Then
\mathcal{F}=U_{y_{1}}\cup\dots\cup U_{y_{n}}
is a measurable set with

\mu(\mathcal{F})<\infty
, and
\pi(\mathcal{F})=G/\Gamma
.
∎

Definition 2.19

(Uniform lattice)
.

A lattice
\Gamma\subset G
is called
uniform
(or

cocompact
) if the quotient
G/\Gamma
is compact.

Example 2.20
.

For
n>1
the discrete group
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is a lattice in
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and
we will see latter in this course that it is
not
uniform.

Chapter 3
Algebraic and arithmetic groups

The examples
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
motivates the
idea of “taking integral points” in a group in order to produce discrete
subgroups. However, for a general topological group
G
there is no such notion
of “integral points”. We need to consider more structure on
G
if we want to
make sense of this construction. To obtain the needed structure we
wil have to study a bit of algebraic geometry.

3.1
Algebraic sets

In this chapter
k
denotes a field of characteristic zero, and
K=\overline{k}
an algebraic closure of
k
, with inclusion

k\subset K
. For any
k
-vector space
\mathbf{V}_{k}
and any field extension

\ell/k
, we write
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\ell\otimes_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}
. Then the map
v\mapsto 1\otimes v
defines a canonical inclusion
\mathbf{V}_{k}\subset\mathbf{V}_{\ell}
.
We will also denote
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{K}
(if the field
k
is clear from the
context).

Polynomial functions

Let
\mathbf{V}_{k}
be a
k
-vector space of finite dimension
n
. A map
f:\mathbf{V}_{k}\to k
is
called
polynomial
if it can be written as a polynomial expression
(with coefficients in
k
) in terms of elements of the dual
\mathbf{V}_{k}^{*}
.
We denote by
k[\mathbf{V}]
the set of polynomial maps on
\mathbf{V}_{k}
. This notation
indicates that we may see these maps as functions on
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{K}
. More
generally, for a field extension
\ell/k
, we write
\ell[\mathbf{V}]=\ell\otimes_{k}k[\mathbf{V}]
and see it as the ring of polynomial functions on
\mathbf{V}_{L}
, where
L=\overline{\ell}
is the algebraic closure.

Remark 3.1
.

This can be made more concrete by
choosing a basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbf{V}_{k}
. If
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\in\mathbf{V}_{k}^{*}

is its dual basis, then
k[\mathbf{V}]
can be identified with the ring of
polynomials
k[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]
, and then
\ell[\mathbf{V}]=\ell[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]
.

Algebraic sets

Any subset
S\subset K[\mathbf{V}]
defines an
algebraic set

\mathbf{V}(S)\subset\mathbf{V}
,
given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(S)=\left\{v\in\mathbf{V}\,|\,f(v)=0\quad\forall\,f\in S\right\}.

(3.1)

In particular,
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}(\{0\})
is an algebraic set.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that taking zeros reverses the inclusion: if
S^{\prime}\subset S
, then

\mathbf{V}(S)\subset\mathbf{V}(S^{\prime})
.

It is not difficult to show that the algebraic sets of
\mathbf{V}
form the
collection of closed subsets of a topology on
\mathbf{V}
, called the

Zariski topology
. This justifies the following definition.

Definition 3.3
.

A subset
X\subset\mathbf{V}
is called
k
-closed
if
X=\mathbf{V}(S)

for some
S\subset k[\mathbf{V}]
.

Remark 3.4
.

The
k
-closed subsets also define a topology on
\mathbf{V}
, called the

k
-Zariski topology
.

Remark 3.5
.

The Zariski topology (and the
k
-Zariski topology) is
not
Hausdorff.

Definition 3.6
.

On any algebraic set
X\subset\mathbf{V}
, we call the
Zariski topology
the
topology induced by the Zariski topology on
\mathbf{V}
.

Rational points

Let
\ell/k
be a field extension such that
\ell\subset K
.
If
X=\mathbf{V}(S)
is a
k
-closed set,
we denote by
X(\ell)
the set
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}\cap X
, and call it the set of

\ell
-rational points
of
X
. In particular,
X=X(K)
. And if

X=\mathbf{V}
, then
X(\ell)=\mathbf{V}_{\ell}
.

Suppose that
k=\mathbb{R}
(so that
K=\mathbb{C}
). Then
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}
(and
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
), as a
real (resp. complex) vector space, has a natural metric topology. We usually refer to
it as the
Hausdorff topology
, in order to distinguish it from the Zariski
topology on
\mathbf{V}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
X=\mathbf{V}(S)
is
\mathbb{R}
-closed, then
X(\mathbb{R})
is closed
in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to the Hausdorff topology. Similarly, for
X
as
a subset of the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the fact that any
f\in S
is polynomial and
thus continuous as a real (or complex) function.
∎

Example 3.8
.

For
\mathbf{V}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
one has
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{V}]\cong\mathbb{C}[x,y]
.
Then for
f=x^{2}+y^{2}-1
and
X=\mathbf{V}(\left\{f\right\})
, we have
that
X(\mathbb{R})
corresponds to the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
A small modification also provides a noncompact example: for
Y=\mathbf{V}(x^{2}-y^{2}-1)
we have that
Y(\mathbb{R})
is noncompact.

Regular functions and ideals

For a
k
-closed subset
X\subset\mathbf{V}
, we denote by
I(X)\subset K[\mathbf{V}]
the
ideal defined by

\displaystyle I(X)=\left\{f\in K[\mathbf{V}]\;|\;f(v)=0\quad\forall\,v\in X\right\}.

(3.2)

Using this ideal, we define the
ring of regular functions
on
X
to be
the
K
-algebra
K[X]=K[\mathbf{V}]/I(X)
. The elements in
K[X]
can indeed be
identified as (polynomials) functions on
X
. We also define:

\displaystyle I_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=k[\mathbf{V}]\cap I(X)\mbox{ and }

(3.3)

\displaystyle k[X]

\displaystyle=k[\mathbf{V}]/I_{k}(X).

(3.4)

Remark 3.9
.

Under our assumption that
\mathrm{char}(k)=0
(or more generally if
k
is

perfect
), for any
k
-closed subset
X\subset\mathbf{V}
the ideal
I_{k}(X)
generates

I(X)
over
K
(one says that
X
is
defined over
k
; for
k
non-perfect
this is a stronger condition than being
k
-closed). Then one has
K\otimes_{k}k[X]\cong K[X]
.

Remark 3.10
.

If
f\in I_{k}(X)
then it certainly vanishes on
X(k)
. But the converse
is not true: in general
I_{k}(X)
is smaller than the set of functions
f\in k[\mathbf{V}]
vanishing on
X(k)
.

Let
X=\mathbf{V}(S)
be an algebraic set. It is easy to check that
\mathbf{V}(S)=\mathbf{V}(\sqrt{(S)})
, where
\sqrt{(S)}
is the
radical
of the ideal
(S)
generated
by
S
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\sqrt{(S)}=\left\{f\in K[\mathbf{V}]\;|\;f^{j}\in(S)\mbox{ for some
}j\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\right\}.

(3.5)

In other words,
\sqrt{(S)}\subset I(X)
. The opposite inclusion holds. For a
proof, see for instance
[
Hum75
, §1.1]
.

Theorem 3.11

(Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz)
.

For
X=\mathbf{V}(S)
, one has
I(X)=\sqrt{(S)}
.

Remark 3.12
.

The fact that
K
is algebraically closed is important here.

Corollary 3.13
.

For
X\subset\mathbf{V}
closed, one has
X=\mathbf{V}(I(X))
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the easy fact (noted above) that
S
and

\sqrt{(S)}
have same sets of zeros:
X=\mathbf{V}(S)=\mathbf{V}(\sqrt{(S)})

∎

It also makes sense to define
I(A)\subset K[\mathbf{V}]
– the set of polynomials
vanishing on
A
– for any subset
A\subset\mathbf{V}
, not necessarily closed.
Then we have the following.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
A
be a subset of
\mathbf{V}
. Then its Zariski closure
\overline{A}
is
equal to
\mathbf{V}(I(A))
.

Proof.

By definition,
\overline{A}
is the smallest algebraic set (with respect to inclusion)
in
\mathbf{V}
that contains
A
. Since
A\subset\mathbf{V}(I(A))
and the latter is
algebraic, we have that
\overline{A}\subset\mathbf{V}(I(A))
. For the other
inclusion, note that
I(\overline{A})\subset I(A)
, so that
\mathbf{V}(I(A))\subset\mathbf{V}(I(\overline{A}))=\overline{A}
(using
Corollary
3.13
for the last equality).
∎

Morphisms

Definition 3.15

(Morphism)
.

A map
\phi:X\to Y
between two algebraic sets
X\subset\mathbf{V}
and
Y\subset\mathbf{W}
is called a
morphism
if for any
f\in K[Y]
one has:

f\circ\phi\in K[X]
. If both
X
and
Y
are defined over
k
, it is
called a
k
-morphism
if
f\in k[Y]
implies
f\circ\phi\in k[X]
.

Remark 3.16
.

This gives also a natural notion of isomorphism. Two
algebraic sets
X
and
Y
are isomorphic if there exists two morphisms

\phi:X\to Y
and
\psi:Y\to X
such
that
\phi\circ\psi=\mathrm{id}|_{X}
and
\psi\circ\phi=\mathrm{id}|_{Y}
.
Similarly one defines the notion of
k
-isomorphism.

Remark 3.17
.

It follows from the definition that morphisms are continuous with respect to the
Zariski topology. However, this property does not suffice to characterize
morphisms between algebraic sets. This indicates that algebraic sets are
more than sets with a particular topology; what is really more important
is the sort of functions that one considers on them (here the
polynomial functions).

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\phi
be a
k
-morphism between the
k
-closed sets
X
and
Y
.
Then
\phi
preserves the
k
-points:
\phi(X(k))\subset Y(k)
.

Proof.

For
Y\subset\mathbf{W}

k
-closed, let
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}
be a basis of
\mathbf{W}_{k}^{*}
.
Then for each
i=1,\dots,m
we have
T_{i}\circ\phi\in k[X]
, and
thus for
x\in X(k)
and
y=\phi(x)
we have:
T_{i}(y)\in k
.
This implies that
y\in\mathbf{W}_{k}
.
∎

Example 3.19
.

Let consider again the
\mathbb{R}
-closed subsets
X
and
Y
from
Example
3.8
. Then there is no
\mathbb{R}
-isomorphism
from
X
and
Y
, as this would contradict the fact that
X(\mathbb{R})
and
Y(\mathbb{R})

are not homeomorphic with respect to the Hausdorff topology.
However, these sets become isomorphic over
\mathbb{C}
: there exists a

\mathbb{C}
-isomorphism
\phi:X\to Y
. It is given by the change of
variable
y\mapsto iy
.

Extension of scalars

Let
\ell/k
be a field extension. We consider an algebraic closure

L=\overline{\ell}
that contains
K=\overline{k}
. Of course, if

\ell/k
is algebraic, then
L=K
. Any
f\in k[\mathbf{V}]
can be seen
as an element in
\ell[\mathbf{V}]=\ell[\mathbf{V}_{L}]
and it follows that any
k
-closed set

X=\mathbf{V}_{K}(S)
determines an
\ell
-closed subset
\mathbf{V}_{L}(S)
. This set is
said to obtain by
extension of scalars
, and we use the notation

X/\ell
for it. Thus for any extension
\ell/k
, the notation
X(\ell)

makes sense.

Remark 3.20
.

It follows from Corollary
3.13
that
X/\ell=\mathbf{V}_{L}(I_{K}(X))
.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
L
be an algebraic closed field containing
K
, and
X\subset\mathbf{V}_{K}

be an algebraic set (where
K
is algebraically closed). Then
X(K)
is Zariski-dense in

X(L)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.14
we have that
\overline{X(K)}=\mathbf{V}_{L}(I_{L}(X(K)))
. We want to show that
\mathbf{V}_{L}(I_{L}(X(K)))=X(L)
, the
inclusion “
\subset
” being obvious. To prove “
\supset
”, we
have to show that any
f\in L[\mathbf{V}]
that vanishes on
X(K)

vanishes on the whole
X(L)
.
Let
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}
be a basis for
L
over
K
.
Then any
f\in L[\mathbf{V}]=L\otimes_{K}K[\mathbf{V}]
can be written as a finite sum

f=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}f_{i}
with
f_{i}\in K[\mathbf{V}]
. If
f
vanishes on

X(K)
, it follows that for any
i
:
f_{i}(v)=0

\forall v\in X(K)
. Thus
f_{i}\in I_{K}(X)
, and by definition of
X/L
we obtain
that every
f_{i}
(and thus
f
) vanishes on
X(L)
.
∎

To put things in perspective: the main situation we have in mind is

k=\mathbb{Q}
and
\ell=\mathbb{R}
. Then, for
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\mathbb{C}
the field
of algebraic numbers we have that
X(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is Zariski-dense in

X(\mathbb{C})
, for any algebraic set
X
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
(or even over

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
).

Note that working over algebraically closed fields is important in
Lemma
3.21
, as the following counterexample shows.

Example 3.22
.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{Q}
. Then for
X=\mathbf{V}(x^{2}+1)
we have
X(\mathbb{Q})=\emptyset
, which is certainly not dense in
X=\left\{\pm i\right\}
. This example also serves
as an illustration of Remark
3.10
, since trivially
every polynomial
f\in k[\mathbf{V}]
vanishes on
X(\mathbb{Q})
.

Principal open subsets

Let
f\in k[\mathbf{V}]
be nonzero. We define the
principal open
subset
(defined over
k
)
D(f)\subset\mathbf{V}
to be the complement

\mathbf{V}\setminus\mathbf{V}(\left\{f\right\})
.
It is indeed open in the Zariski topology. Such a subset of
\mathbf{V}
will be
considered in a way similar as an algebraic set over
k
. Again, the
inclusion in
\mathbf{V}
induces a Zariski topology on
D(f)
.
The ring of regular functions on
D(f)
is defined to be the
K
-algebra

K[\mathbf{V}]
in which we invert
f
:

\displaystyle K[D(f)]

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{g}{f^{s}}\;|\;g\in K[\mathbf{V}],s\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

(3.6)

One defines similarly
k[D(f)]
by inverting
f
in
k[\mathbf{V}]
.
Any closed subset
X\subset D(f)
also comes equipped with a ring of
regular functions, defined as
K[X]=K[D(f)]/I(X)
, where
I(X)
is the
ideal in
D(f)
of functions vanishing on
X
.

Definition 3.23

(Affine variety)
.

We will called a closed subset
X\subset D(f)
in some principal
open subset
D(f)\subset\mathbf{V}
(with
f\in K[\mathbf{V}]
)
an
affine variety
. We say that it is

defined over
k
if it is
k
-closed in
D(f)
and
f\in k[\mathbf{V}]
.

Remark 3.24
.

Note that since
\mathbf{V}=D(c)
for any nonzero constant polynomial
c\in K[\mathbf{V}]
,
we have that algebraic sets are affine varieties in the sense of this definition.

Using the ring of regular functions
K[X]
(and
k[X]
), one can define
similarly to Definition
3.15
the notion of morphism (resp.
k
-morphism) between affine varieties.

Proposition 3.25
.

Every affine variety over
k
is isomorphic to an algebraic set over
k
.

Proof.

The idea is the following.
Let
D(f)
be principal open in
\mathbf{V}
. Let
\mathbf{W}_{k}=\mathbf{V}_{k}\oplus k
, so that
any element in
\mathbf{W}
can be represented as a couple
(x,t)
with
x\in\mathbf{V}
and
t\in K
. We denote by
T
an element in the dual
k^{*}
(thus

T(t)=t
). Let
X\subset\mathbf{W}
be the
k
-closed set
\mathbf{W}(f\cdot T-1)
. Then the map
\phi:D(f)\to X
,
x\mapsto(x,\frac{1}{f(x)})
is a

k
-isomorphism, with inverse
\phi^{-1}:(x,t)\mapsto x
.
It follows that closed subsets in
D(f)
are isomorphic to closed subset
in
X
, which are closed in
\mathbf{W}
.
∎

This proposition shows that we could have defined affine varieties simply as
algebraic sets. However, when dealing with algebraic groups it is natural to
consider principal open subset as well, as we will see in the next section.

Remark 3.26
.

We have given here an ad hoc definition of affine varieties, that fits
well the purpose of studying linear algebraic groups. A first goal when
studying algebraic geometry is to give a more intrinsic notion of
“variety”, which does not limit to affine varieties.
Projective varieties are the first examples of nonaffine varieties that
come to mind.

3.2
Algebraic groups

Let
V_{k}
be a vector space over
k
of dimension
n
, and denote
\mathbf{W}_{k}=\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})
its ring of linear endomorphisms. It is a
k
-vector space
of dimension
n^{2}
, and we can consider
the ring of regular functions
k[\mathbf{W}]
. The determinant
\det:\mathbf{W}_{k}\to k
is a polynomial function, and thus the
general linear
group

\GL(\mathbf{V})
is a principal open subset (defined over
k
) in
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{K}
,
namely corresponding to
D(\det)\subset\mathbf{W}
.
Fixing a basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of
\mathbf{V}_{k}
, we obtain a basis

T_{11},\dots,T_{nn}\in\mathbf{W}_{k}^{*}
. With this identification, we
have

\displaystyle k[\GL(\mathbf{V})]

\displaystyle=k\left[T_{11},\dots,T_{nn},\frac{1}{\det(T_{ij})}\right].

(3.7)

Definition 3.27

(Algebraic group)
.

A subgroup
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
that is
k
-closed (as a subset of
the principal open set
\GL(\mathbf{V})
) is called a
(linear) algebraic group

over
k
, or simply a
k
-group
.

Example 3.28
.

Trivially, the general linear group
\GL(\mathbf{V})
is an algebraic

k
-group. When
\mathbf{V}_{k}=k
has dimension one, we write
\mathbf{G}_{m}
(or

\mathbf{G}_{m}/k
if we want to emphasize the field of definition) for

\GL(\mathbf{V})
, and we call it the
multiplicative group
(over
k
).

Remark 3.29
.

Note that the
k
-points
\GL(\mathbf{V})(k)
are given by
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{k})
.

Example 3.30
.

The
special linear
group
\SL(\mathbf{V})=\mathbf{W}(\det-1)
is a

k
-group.

Example 3.31
.

Let
x\in\GL(\mathbf{V}_{k})
. The subset of automorphisms of
\mathbf{V}
commuting
with
x
:

\displaystyle\left\{g\in\GL(\mathbf{V})\;|\;gx=xg\right\},

is an algebraic
k
-group. To see that it is algebraic, one can choose
a basis
T_{11},\dots,T_{nn}
for
\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})
. The matrix multiplication
by
x
is given coordinate-wise by polynomial function with
coefficients in
k
.

An important feature of any
k
-group
\mathbf{G}
is that for any field extension

\ell/k
, the rational points
\mathbf{G}(\ell)
form a group. This can be
easily check on each of the examples above.
For the proof in general, we first note the following lemma.

Lemma 3.32
.

Let
A\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be a subgroup (not necessarily closed). Then
its Zariski closure
\overline{A}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
is a subgroup (in
particular it is an algebraic group).

Proof.

Trivially,
1\in\overline{A}
. Let
\phi:\GL(\mathbf{V})\to\GL(\mathbf{V})
be the
map given by
\phi(x)=x^{-1}
. It is an isomorphism of algebraic
varieties, and in particular it is a homeomorphism for the Zariski
topology. Then
\phi(\overline{A})=\overline{\phi(A)}=\overline{A}

(since
\phi(A)=A
), which proves that
\overline{A}
is closed under
inversion. One proves similarly that
x\overline{A}=\overline{A}
for
any
x\in A
. Put a bit differently:
Ay\subset\overline{A}
for any

y\in\overline{A}
. But then
\overline{Ay}=\overline{A}y
must be
contained in
\overline{A}
.
∎

Proposition 3.33
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
k
-group, and
\ell/k
be any field extension. Then

\mathbf{G}(\ell)
is a group. In particular, the extension of scalars

\mathbf{G}/\ell
is again an algebraic group.

Proof.

The result is clear when
\ell\subset K=\overline{k}
, since in this
case
\mathbf{G}(\ell)=\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\ell})\cap\mathbf{G}(K)
.
In the general case, it suffices to prove that
\mathbf{G}(L)
is a group for
L=\overline{\ell}
and then to apply the same argument. By
Lemma
3.21
,
\mathbf{G}(K)
is Zariski-dense in
\mathbf{G}(L)

and thus the result follows directly from
Lemma
3.32
.
∎

Corollary 3.34
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group. Then
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is a locally compact group.

Proof.

The proposition shows that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is indeed a group. As a subset of

\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})
it is Hausdorff, and locally compact since it is closed
in
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})\cong\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
(cf. Proposition
3.7
).
∎

Remark 3.35
.

Of course the result remains true for any
\mathbb{R}
-group.

Remark 3.36
.

The same argument shows that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{C})
is also a locally compact group.
And so is
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
denotes the field of
p

numbers (for
p\in\mathbb{N}
a prime).

Definition 3.37

(Morphism of groups)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})

\mathbf{H}\subset\GL(\mathbf{W})
be two algebraic

k

k

Proposition 3.38
.

Let
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
be a
k
-morphism of groups.
Then the kernel

Proof.

Let

The singletons

, and since
\phi

is continuous with respect to the

\ker(\phi)=\phi^{-1}({e})

\mathbf{G}
.
∎

Example 3.39
.

The inclusion
\SL(\mathbf{V})\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
is a
k
-morphism.

Example 3.40
.

The determinant map
\det:\GL(\mathbf{V})\to\mathbf{G}_{m}

A more surprising result is the fact that the image of a morphism is a closed subgroup.

Proposition 3.41
.

Let
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
be a
k
-morphism. Then

is a

k

\mathbf{H}
.

Note that this result has no analogue in the context of topological groups, as

Example 3.42
.

Let
T=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}
be the two-dimensional torus. Then for any irrational

.

We close this section with a remark concerning the generality of our notion of
“algebraic group”.

Remark 3.43
.

We have limited our discussion to
linear
algebraic groups, that is,
closed subgroups of
\GL(\mathbf{V})
. Another point of view – more
general – is to define an algebraic group as a variety together with a
group structure “compatible” with the structure of variety. This has
the advantage to make sense also for variety that are not necessarily
affine. As notable examples of projective algebraic groups we can
mention elliptic curves. However, it turns out that every

affine

algebraic group is isomorphic to a linear algebraic group
(see
[
Hum75
, § 8.6]
).

Example 3.44

(Additive group)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{k}=k
and
\mathbf{V}=K\otimes_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}
. The
addition on
\mathbf{V}
makes it an algebraic group defined over
k
.
It can be identified as a subgroup of
\GL(K^{2})
via the isomorphism

\displaystyle x\mapsto\left(\begin{array}[h]{cc}1&x\\
0&1\end{array}\right).

The group
(K,+)
is referred as to the
additive group
, and
denoted by
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
.

Remark 3.45
.

More generally, one shows that for
\mathbf{V}
of dimension
n
,
the group
(\mathbf{V},+)
is an algebraic group,
isomorphic to the
n
-factors product
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{a}}\times\dots\times\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
.
From the preceding example, the latter can be embedded in

\GL(\mathbf{W}^{\oplus n})
, with
\mathbf{W}\cong K^{2}
.

3.3
Arithmetic groups

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group. We would like to produce
discrete subgroups in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
. To this end we need to fix some
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice in

\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Definition 3.46

(
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of dimension
n
. A
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice

L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a subgroup of rank
n
(as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module) such that

\mathbb{Q}\otimes L=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
(i.e.,
L
spans
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
over
\mathbb{Q}
).

In particular the
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L
is a Euclidean lattice in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}

in the sense of Chapter 1. When the context is clear,
we will often just say “lattice” instead “
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice”.

Remark 3.47
.

In Chapter 2 we have generalized the notion of Euclidean lattice to
extend it to the context of subgroup of more general topological groups
than
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The definition of
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice suggests another type of
generalization – more algebraic.

Lemma 3.48
.

Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be two
\mathbb{Z}
-lattices in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
mL\subset L^{\prime}\subset\frac{1}{m}L
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
L
, and
x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime}
a

\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
L^{\prime}
. Both are
\mathbb{Q}
-bases for
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. In particular, any

x_{i}^{\prime}
can be written as a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}x_{j}.

Let
\alpha
be the product of all denominators of the
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then for every
i=1,\dots,n
we have
\alpha x_{i}^{\prime}\in L
, so that
that
\alpha L^{\prime}\subset L
. Similarly, one proves the existence of

\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\beta L\subset L^{\prime}
, and the result follows for

m=\alpha\beta
.
∎

Note that
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}})
(and thus any of its subgroups) acts naturally on
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Given a lattice
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
and an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
we can define the stabilizer subgroup

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{L}=\left\{g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})\;|\;gL=L\right\}.

(3.10)

It follows that any
g\in\mathbf{G}_{L}
acts as an automorphism of the
free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
L
. In particular,
\det(g)=\pm 1
.

Example 3.49
.

For
\mathbf{G}=\GL(\mathbf{V})
, we have that
\mathbf{G}_{L}
is the full automorphism group

\GL(L)
. In particular, fixing a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
L
we obtain an
identification
\GL(L)=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. It follows that
\GL(L)
is
discrete in
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})\cong\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 3.50
.

The group
\mathbf{G}_{L}
is discrete in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{G}_{L}\subset\GL(L)
, this follows from the discreteness of

\GL(L)
in
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})
.
∎

Definition 3.51

(commensurable groups)
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be two subgroups of a larger group
G
.
We say that
\Gamma_{1}
is
commensurable with

\Gamma_{2}
if

\Delta=\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}
has finite index in both
\Gamma_{1}
and

\Gamma_{2}
.

Remark 3.52
.

“To be commensurable with” is an equivalence relation for the subgroups in

G
.

Example 3.53
.

From Lemma
3.48
one easily deduces that any
two
\mathbb{Z}
-lattices in the same
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
must be
commensurable (note that
mL\subset L
has finite index:
[L:mL]=m^{n}
).

Proposition 3.54
.

Let
L
and
L^{\prime}
be two
\mathbb{Z}
-lattices in the same vector space
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
,
and let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group.
Then
\mathbf{G}_{L}
and
\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}
are commensurable in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Proof.

Note that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
and any
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L
, we
have
\mathbf{G}_{\alpha L}=\mathbf{G}_{L}
. From Lemma
3.48

we have the existence of
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
mL\subset L^{\prime}\subset\frac{1}{m}L
, from which we deduces that
\mathbf{G}_{L}
acts on the set

\displaystyle X=\left\{M\mbox{ $\mathbb{Z}$-lattice in }\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}\;|\;mL\subset M\subset\frac{1}{m}L\right\}.

Under this action, the stabilizer of
L^{\prime}\in X
is exactly
\mathbf{G}_{L}\cap\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}
.
Thus
\mathbf{G}_{L}/(\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{G}_{L})
is in bijection with the orbit of
L^{\prime}
in

X
. But
X
is a finite set (one shows that the number a sublattices of
fixed index in a fixed lattice is finite). Similarly, one proves that
the index
[\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}:\mathbf{G}_{L}\cap\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}]
is finite, so that
\mathbf{G}_{L}
and

\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}
are commensurable.
∎

This motivates that following definition.

Definition 3.55

(Arithmetic subgroup)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group.
Any subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
commensurable with
\mathbf{G}_{L}
for some

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is called an
arithmetic subgroup
of

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
.

The important fact to bear in mind is that as single algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group really
determines a whole commensurability class of subgroups in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Remark 3.56
.

The definition could be extended in
an obvious way to subgroups of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
or
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{C})
.
But then “arithmetic subgroups of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
” could refer to more than
one commensurability class.
To avoid some confusion, we choose here to limit the use of
the term “arithmetic subgroup” for subgroups of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
, and will
extend the notion when speaking of “arithmetic lattices” (next
section).

Example 3.57
.

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is an arithmetic subgroup of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. Similarly for

\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\subset\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Example 3.58
.

For an integer
m\geq 2
, let
\phi_{m}:\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\to\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/m)
be the
homomorphism given on each matrix entry by the reduction mod
m
map
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m
. Its kernel
\Gamma(m)=\ker(\phi_{m})
is called a

principal congruence subgroup
of
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and since

\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/m)
is finite we have that
\Gamma(m)
has finite index in

\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus
\Gamma(m)
is an arithmetic subgroup of
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
.

When choosing a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
L
we obtain an identification
L=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which
in turn identifies
\mathbf{G}_{L}
with a subgroup of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. We have:
\mathbf{G}_{L}=\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})\cap\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and this
justifies the notation
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
for
\mathbf{G}_{L}
. If
L
is clear from the context, or
if the statement is independent of the choice of
L
, we will use this notation

\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Remark 3.59
.

The notation
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
is also commonly used.
Both notation
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are a bit dangerous, since the “
\mathbb{Z}
-points” are
not intrinsically defined in
\mathbf{G}
. If
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphism,
then we can only say that
\phi(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}})
is commensurable with
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. On
the other hand,
\phi
induces an isomorphism
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbf{G}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q})
.

3.4
Arithmetic lattices

Proposition 3.60
.

Let
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be two subgroups of the locally compact
group
G
, and suppose that
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are commensurable.
Then
\Gamma
is a lattice in
G
if and only
\Gamma^{\prime}
is
a lattice in
G
. Moreover, in this case
\Gamma^{\prime}
is uniform if and only if

\Gamma^{\prime}
is uniform.

Proof.

We only have to prove the result assuming
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
with finite index. If
\Gamma
is discrete, then
certainly
\Gamma^{\prime}
is. We write
\Gamma=g_{1}\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\dots\cup g_{n}\Gamma^{\prime}
(finite union), with
g_{i}\in\Gamma
. If

\Gamma^{\prime}
is discrete, then each
g_{i}\Gamma^{\prime}
is discrete. It
easily follows that
\Gamma
is discrete.
Suppose that
\Gamma
is a lattice: there exists
\mathcal{F}
of finite
measure such that
\mathcal{F}\,\Gamma=G
. Then for
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}g_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{F}g_{n}
one has
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\,\Gamma^{\prime}=G
, so that
\Gamma^{\prime}
is also a
lattice. The converse is obvious: if
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a lattice then

\Gamma
is. We leave it as an exercise the proof concerning the
“uniform” property.
∎

Thus taking commensurable subgroups preserves the quality of being a
lattice (resp. being a uniform lattice). It follows that if one
arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
is a lattice in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
, then the same
holds for all arithmetic subgroups of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
. This shows two
important things:

•

The sentence “
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a lattice (resp. uniform lattice)”
in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
makes sense without specifying a particular
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L

such that
\mathbf{G}_{L}=\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

•

We can try to find condition on the
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
that implies
that
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a (uniform) lattice, rather than conditions on

\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
itself.

It is easily shown that any arithmetic subgroup in
\mathbf{G}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}^{*}
is not
a lattice in
\mathbf{G}_{m}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{*}
. This can be use to show that more generally
if the
\mathbb{Q}
-group is such that there exists a nontrivial

\mathbb{Q}
-morphism of group
\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}_{m}
, then
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is
not a lattice.

Definition 3.61

(Character)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an algebraic
k
-group.
A
k
-character
(or
character over
k
) of
\mathbf{G}

is a
k
-morphism of group
\chi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}_{m}/k
.

There is always the trivial character, given by
\chi(g)=1
for all
g\in\mathbf{G}
.

Example 3.62
.

The determinant
\det:\GL(\mathbf{V})\to\mathbf{G}_{m}
is a nontrivial character. Thus,

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is not a lattice in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

The following fundamental theorem asserts that
\mathbf{G}
having a nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character
is the only obstruction for
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
to be a lattice in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
. Its proof
relies on the so-called
reduction theory
, and can be found in

[
Bor69
]
.

Theorem 3.63

(Borel and Harish-Chandra)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group. Then
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
lattice in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial

\mathbb{Q}
-character.

In addition to Proposition
3.60
,
some other elementary modifications preserve lattices. The
following is particularly relevant for us.

Proposition 3.64
.

Let
\phi:G\to H
be a continuous open surjective homomorphism between
locally compact groups, and suppose that
\phi
has compact
kernel. Let
\Gamma\subset G
be a lattice (resp. uniform lattice).
Then
\phi(\Gamma)
is a lattice (resp. uniform lattice) in
H
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
OV00
, Ch.1 Cor. 4.10]

for the general proof, and we prove here only the restricted case
of
\Gamma
uniform, assuming that
G
and
H
are metric.

Let show that
\phi(\Gamma)
is discrete assuming that
G
and

H
are metric. We denote by
K\subset G
the kernel of
\phi
.
Suppose that
\phi(\Gamma)
is not discrete in

H
. Then there exists a sequence
g_{n}\in\Gamma
such that

\phi(g_{n})\to 1
, with all
\phi(g_{n})\neq 1
. This implies
that the distance
d(g_{n}K,K)>0
converges to
0

(if not: there would exist an open
neighbourhood
U\subset G
of
K
such that
U\cap g_{n}K=\emptyset
for infinitely many
g_{n}
in the sequence,
so that
\phi(U)
would be an open neighbourhood of

1
in
H
that does not contain all but finitely of the

\phi(g_{n})
; this contradicts the
convergence of this sequence). Thus there exists a sequence

\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}\in K
such that

g_{n}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}^{-1}\to 1
. Moreover, since
K
is
compact, by considering subsequences we may assume that

\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}^{-1}
converges, say to
h^{-1}\in K
so that

g_{n}\to h
. But since
\Gamma
is discrete we have that

g_{n}=h
for
n
large enough, which implies that

\phi(g_{n})=1
for
n
large enough. This contradicts the
assumption
\phi(g_{n})\neq 1
.

Using a similar argument as in
the proof of Proposition
2.18
, one sees
that
G/\Gamma
is compact if and only if there exists
\mathcal{F}\subset G

compact such that
\mathcal{F}\Gamma=G
. In this case
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\phi(\mathcal{F})
is compact
and such that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\phi(\Gamma)=\phi(G)=H
, proving that
\phi(\Gamma)
is
uniform.
∎

Definition 3.65

(Arithmetic lattice)
.

Let
H
be a locally compact group. A subgroup
\Gamma\subset H
is
called an
arithmetic lattice
if there is an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
such that:

•

\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character;

•

there exists a surjective continuous open homomorphism
\phi:\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\to H
with compact kernel;

•

\Gamma
is commensurable with
\phi(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Remark 3.66
.

It follows from the various results stated above that
\Gamma\subset H

is indeed a lattice.

Remark 3.67
.

As for arithmetic subgroups, a choice of a suitable
(\mathbf{G},\phi)
determines a
single commensurability class of lattices in
H
. But we can now let
\mathbf{G}
and/or

\phi
vary, to get different commensurability classes of lattices in the same

H
. In particular, if
\mathbf{H}
is an algebraic
\mathbb{Q}
-group,
then any
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
without nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character such that

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbf{H}(\mathbb{R})
determines a commensurability classes of arithmetic
lattices, a priori not commensurable with
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Remark 3.68
.

The existence of
(\mathbf{G},\phi)
imposes strong conditions on the locally
compact group
H
. It is typically a real Lie group.

About references

The three standard references on linear algebraic groups – all with the same
title – are the books
[
Hum75
]
by Humphreys,
[
Bor91
]
by Borel,
and
[
Spr98
]
by Springer. All of them start by introducing some of the needed
theory from algebraic geometry. Humphrey’s book is somewhat easier for a
beginner; the drawback is that it does not develop the “rational” theory – over
a non-algebraically closed field (but it surveys this part in a last
chapter).

In most textbooks on algebraic geometry, algebraic sets are defined in the
canonical vector space
K^{n}
, rather than in some
n
-dimensional
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}_{K}
as we did.
The former approach is easier, since it directly identifies
k[\mathbf{V}]
with the
polynomial ring
k[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]
. Here we have chosen to work with
\mathbf{V}

essentially to emphasize the non-intrinsic nature of the “
\mathbb{Z}
-points” in

\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We believe that this facilitates the discussion of arithmetic subgroups.

Arithmetic groups and lattices are discussed for instance in

[
Bor69
]
and
[
OV00
]
.

Chapter 4
More on algebraic groups

In order to prove some results about arithmetic groups, one needs to
better understand the structure theory of algebraic groups, in which
they live. It is the goal of this chapter to survey some useful facts
concerning algebraic groups.

We still assume that the field
k
has characteristic zero, and
K=\overline{k}
.

4.1
Rational representations

As it was defined in Chapter 3, any morphism of group determines a linear
action on a vector space of finite dimension. We emphasize this action in
the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Rational representation)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
k
-group and
\mathbf{V}_{k}
be a finite-dimensional vector space
over
k
. A
k
-rational representation
(or simply
k
-representation
) of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{V}
is a
k
-morphism of group
\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathbf{V})
.

Remark 4.2
.

For any field extension
\ell/k
a
k
-representation furnishes an
action of
\mathbf{G}(\ell)
on
\mathbf{V}_{\ell}
.

It is also useful to consider linear actions of
\mathbf{G}
on some infinite
dimensional vector spaces. This is for instance the case of
K[\mathbf{G}]
, seen as a

K
-vector space. For every
x\in\mathbf{G}
consider the right multiplication
map
\phi_{x}:g\mapsto gx
. It is a morphism of variety from
\mathbf{G}
onto itself,
and thus for any
f\in K[\mathbf{G}]
the
right translation

\rho_{x}f=f\circ\phi_{x}
is again an element of
K[\mathbf{G}]
. Then,
x\mapsto\rho_{x}

defines a representation of
\mathbf{G}
on
the infinite dimensional vector space
K[\mathbf{G}]
. By definition,
(\rho_{x}f)(y)=f(yx)
.
For
x\in\mathbf{G}(k)
we also obtain a restricted map
\rho_{x}:k[\mathbf{G}]\to k[\mathbf{G}]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
be a closed subgroup, and
I=I(\mathbf{H})\subset K[\mathbf{G}]
be the ideal of regular functions vanishing on
\mathbf{H}
.
Then
\mathbf{H}=\left\{x\in\mathbf{G}\;|\;\rho_{x}(I)\subset I\right\}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbf{H}
and
f\in I
. Then
(\rho_{x}f)(y)=f(yx)=0
for any

y\in\mathbf{H}
, so that
\rho_{x}f\in I
. Conversely, suppose that

\rho_{x}(I)\subset I
. In particular for any
f\in I
,
\rho_{x}f

vanishes on the neutral element
e
:
(\rho_{x}f)(e)=f(x)=0
, and
thus
x\in\mathbf{H}
.
∎

The infinite dimensional representation
\rho:\mathbf{G}\to\GL(K[\mathbf{G}])
can be
decomposed into rational subrepresentations: the following result follows for
instance from
[
Spr98
, Prop. 2.3.6]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

There exists a filtration
K^{0}[\mathbf{G}]\subset k^{1}[\mathbf{G}]\subset\dots\subset k[\mathbf{G}]
such that:

•

Each
k^{m}[\mathbf{G}]
is a finite dimensional subspace of

k[\mathbf{G}]
.

•

\sum_{m\geq 0}k^{m}[\mathbf{G}]=k[\mathbf{G}]
.

•

For
K^{m}[\mathbf{G}]=K\otimes_{k}k^{m}[\mathbf{G}]
and
x\in\mathbf{G}
, we
have
\rho_{x}(K^{m}[\mathbf{G}])\subset K^{m}[\mathbf{G}]
, and
\rho:\mathbf{G}\to\GL(K^{m}[\mathbf{G}])
is a
k
-representation.

Theorem 4.5

(Chevalley)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
k
-group
and let
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
be a closed
k
-subgroup.
There exists a
k
-representation of
\mathbf{G}
on a vector space
\mathbf{V}

such that
\mathbf{H}=\left\{g\in\mathbf{G}\;|\;g\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}\right\}
, where

\mathbf{W}_{k}\subset\mathbf{V}_{k}
is some
k
-subspace with
\dim(\mathbf{W}_{k})=1
.

Proof.

Based on the above discussion we explain the idea of the proof,
and refer to
[
Hum75
, §11.2]
for the details.
Let
I_{k}(\mathbf{H})\subset k[\mathbf{G}]
be the ideal of functions
vanishing on
\mathbf{H}
. Then (since
\mathbf{H}
is defined over
k
):
K\otimes_{k}I_{k}=I(\mathbf{H})\subset K[\mathbf{G}]
. Moreover, it is know that
k[\mathbf{G}]
(and
K[\mathbf{G}]
) is a

Noetherian ring
, meaning that all its ideals are finitely generated.
This implies that there exists an integer
m
such that

\displaystyle I^{m}_{k}

\displaystyle=k^{m}[\mathbf{G}]\cap I_{k}(\mathbf{H})

generates the ideal
I_{k}(\mathbf{H})
; in other words,
the subset
\mathbf{H}
corresponds exactly to the set
of points
x\in\mathbf{G}
such that
f(x)=0
for all
f\in I_{k}^{m}
.
Let
I^{m}=K\otimes_{k}I_{k}^{m}=K^{m}[\mathbf{G}]\cap I(\mathbf{H})
.

By Lemma
4.3
and
Proposition
4.4
, an element
x\in\mathbf{G}
belongs
to
\mathbf{H}
if and only if
\rho_{x}(I^{m})\subset I^{m}
.
Let
p=\dim(I^{m})
, and define the
k
-vector space

\mathbf{V}_{k}
to be the exterior algebra
\bigwedge^{p}k^{m}[\mathbf{G}]
.
Let further define
\mathbf{W}_{k}
to be the one-dimensional subspace

\mathbf{W}_{k}=\bigwedge^{p}I_{k}^{m}
. It follows that for this
\mathbf{W}\subset\mathbf{V}
the
rational representation of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{V}
naturally induced by the

k
-representaton
\rho
has the desired properties.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Chevalley’s theorem is also useful for putting
a natural structure of algebraic variety on
\mathbf{G}/\mathbf{H}
,
by identifying this quotient as a orbit in the projective space

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{V})
of
\mathbf{V}
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Suppose moreover that the subgroup
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial
k
-character.
Then there exists
v\in\mathbf{V}_{k}
such that
\mathbf{H}
is the stabilizer of
v
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\left\{g\in\mathbf{G}\;|\;gv=v\right\}.

Proof.

Since
\dim(\mathbf{W})=1
, the
k
-morphism
\mathbf{H}\to\GL(\mathbf{W})
corresponds to
a
k
-character, and thus the action of
\mathbf{H}
on
\mathbf{W}
is trivial. It
suffices then to pick any
v\in\mathbf{W}_{k}
.
∎

4.2
The Lie algebra

We would like to define a notion of tangent space for an algebraic
k
-group

\mathbf{G}
; we will consider such a tangent space at the neutral element
e=1\in\mathbf{G}
.
Let first consider the case
\mathbf{G}=\GL(\mathbf{V})
for some
k
-vector space
\mathbf{V}_{k}\cong k^{n}
. Then
\GL(\mathbf{V})
is open in the vector space
\End(\mathbf{V})\cong K^{n\times n}
, and the “analytical situation”
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

suggests to define the tangent of
\GL(\mathbf{V})
(say at
e=1
) as the whole vector
space
\End(\mathbf{V})
.

Let us choose a basis
(T_{ij})
for
\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})^{*}
, so that any
f\in K[\GL(\mathbf{V})]
is expressible as a polynomial in
T_{ij}
and
\det(T_{ij})^{-1}
.
In particular for any
T_{ij}
the
formal
partial differential

\frac{\partial}{\partial T_{ij}}f
makes sense, and is again an element in

K[\GL(\mathbf{V})]
. Then the total differential is defined at any
x\in\GL(\mathbf{V})
by
df_{x}=\sum\frac{\partial f}{\partial T_{ij}}(x)\cdot T_{ij}\in\End(\mathbf{V})^{*}
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be an algebraic
k
-group. Then we define its tangent
space at
e=1
as the vector subspace

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\End(\mathbf{V})\;|\;df_{e}(X)=0\;\forall f\in I(\mathbf{G})\right\};

(4.1)

and we call it the
Lie algebra
of
\mathbf{G}
. It is customary to denote
the Lie algebra by the corresponding minuscule Fraktur letter:

\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Lie}(G),\mathfrak{h}=\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{H})
, etc.
We further define
\mathfrak{g}_{k}=\mathfrak{g}\cap\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})
, and more generally in the same
way
\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}
for any field extension
\ell/k
.

Example 4.8
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
of
\GL(\mathbf{V})
is the whole
vector space
\End(\mathbf{V})
.

Remark 4.9
.

More precisely, the term “Lie algebra” refer to the vector space
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{G})
together with the anticommutative product
[X,Y]=XY-YX
,
which is called the
Lie bracket
. One can check that this
product (which is defined here on the level of
\End(\mathbf{V})
) is indeed
closed inside
\mathfrak{g}
, that is,
[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
.
In other words:
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
.

Remark 4.10
.

We could use the Lie algebra to define a notion of dimension for
\mathbf{G}
,
by setting
\dim(\mathbf{G})=\dim(\mathfrak{g})
. The usual way to proceed it to define

\dim(\mathbf{G})
using commutative algebra – as the
Krull dimension
of

K[\mathbf{G}]
. One then checks that it coincides with
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
, which
reflects the fact that
\mathbf{G}
is “smooth at
e
” (and thus smooth
everywhere).

Example 4.11
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(V)
of
\SL(\mathbf{V})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{sl}(V)

\displaystyle=\left\{X\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbf{V})\;|\;\mathrm{tr}(X)=0\right\}.

Thus,
\dim(\SL(\mathbf{V}))=\dim(\mathfrak{sl}(\mathbf{V}))=n^{2}-1
, where
n=\dim(\mathbf{V})
.
The fact that the Lie bracket is closed in
\mathfrak{sl}(V)
follows
from the equality:
\mathrm{tr}(XY)=\mathrm{tr}(YX)
.

If
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
is a
k
-morphism of groups, then we obtain a natural
notion of differential at
e
:
d\phi_{e}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{Lie}(H)
. It is the algebraic
version of the usual “analytic” differential that is defined for
k=\mathbb{R}
or

\mathbb{C}
. More precisely, if
k\subset\mathbb{C}
we can always consider the extension of
scalars
\mathbf{G}/\mathbb{C}
, and for
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}\subset\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
the differential can be then
computed by the usual formula:

\displaystyle d\phi_{e}(X)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\phi(e+tX)-\phi(e)}{t}.

(4.2)

Remark 4.12
.

That
d\phi_{e}(X)
indeed lies in
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{H})
can be easily checked by using
the chain rule
d(f\circ\phi)_{e}=df_{\phi(e)}\circ d\phi_{e}
, still
valid in the algebraic setting (here for
f\in K[\mathbf{H}]
).
From our definitions it is more difficult to check – but still true –
that for a
k
-morphism the differential restricts to a
k
-linear map

\mathfrak{g}_{k}\to\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbf{H})_{k}
. Similarly, one has
[\mathfrak{g}_{k},\mathfrak{g}_{k}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{k}
, so that
the Lie bracket restricts to the
k
-points (this justifies the term “Lie
algebra” for
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
as well).

For
k\subset\mathbb{R}
, recall the exponential map
\exp:\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})\to\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})
defined by the (converging) sequence:

\displaystyle\exp(X)=e^{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{X^{j}}{j!}.

(4.3)

Then for any algebraic group
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
over
k\subset\mathbb{R}
,
the exponential restricts to a map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 4.13
.

The group
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is an example of a
Lie group
, and much of its
study is based on the study of the real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Example 4.14
.

In the case of
\SL
, the fact that
\exp(\mathfrak{sl}(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}))\subset\SL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})

follows from the formula
e^{\mathrm{tr}(X)}=\det(e^{X})
.

4.3
The adjoint representation

For the rest of this section we suppose that
k\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
k
-group, and for
g\in\mathbf{G}
we consider the
inner
automorphism

\mathrm{Int}(g):\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}
, defined by
\mathrm{Int}(g)x=gxg^{-1}
.
The differential
d\mathrm{Int}(g)_{e}
determines a linear map from
\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
, and thus an
element of
\GL(\mathfrak{g})
. We denote by
\mathrm{Ad}(g):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
this map.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
, and
g\in\mathbf{G}
. Then for
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, one has

\mathrm{Ad}(g)X=gXg^{-1}
.

Proof.

Since we assume that
k\subset\mathbb{R}
, this can be proved simply be
computing the differentiation formula (
4.2
), where
X\in\End(\mathbf{V})
is seen as an element of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
(for
n=\dim(\mathbf{V})
).
∎

The explicit formula for
\mathrm{Ad}(g)
given in the lemma permits to show the
following important facts:

•

If
g\in\mathbf{G}(k)
, then
\mathrm{Ad}(g)
restricts to a
k
-linear map

\mathfrak{g}_{k}\to\mathfrak{g}_{k}
. Similarly for any field extension
\ell/k
.

•

\mathrm{Ad}(g)
preserves the Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
on
\mathfrak{g}
, and thus
is a automorphism of Lie algebra:
\mathrm{Ad}(g)\in\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
.

•

The map
\mathrm{Ad}:\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathfrak{g})
is a
k
-morphism, and thus a

k
-representation of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathfrak{g}
. It follows from
Proposition
3.41
that the image
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
is
a
k
-subgroup of
\GL(\mathfrak{g})
.

Definition 4.16
.

The representation
\mathrm{Ad}:\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathfrak{g})
is called the
adjoint
representation
of
\mathbf{G}
. The image
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
is called the

adjoint
group of
\mathbf{G}
.

Remark 4.17
.

One can show that the group of Lie algebra automorphisms
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
is
itself a
k
-group (i.e., a
k
-closed subgroup of
\GL(\mathfrak{g})
). Then

\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
is a
k
-subgroup of
\Aut(\mathfrak{g})
.

For a
k
-group
\mathbf{G}
, let us denote by
Z(\mathbf{G})
its center (it is an algebraic
group). If
g\in Z(\mathbf{G})
obviously
\mathrm{Int}(g):\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G}
is the identity, so
that
\mathrm{Ad}(g):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is trivial. Thus
Z(\mathbf{G})\subset\ker(\mathrm{Ad})
. In
characteristic zero, the converse holds as well, assuming that
\mathbf{G}
is connected
(i.e., connected with respect to the Zariski topology). See
[
Hum75
, §13.4]

for the proof.

Proposition 4.18
.

If
\mathbf{G}
is connected, then
\ker(\mathrm{Ad})=Z(\mathbf{G})
.

Remark 4.19
.

In particular, in this situation we see that
Z(\mathbf{G})
is a
k
-group
(assuming that
\mathbf{G}
itself is a
k
-group).

Corollary 4.20
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a connected
k
-group that has trivial center. Then
\mathbf{G}

is
k
-isomorphic to
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
.

Proof.

It is clear that as an abstract group
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{G}
,
and in particular the surjective
k
-morphism
\mathrm{Ad}:\mathbf{G}\to\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
is invertible.
Under our assumption
\mathrm{char}(k)=0
, it turns out that the inverse

\mathrm{Ad}^{-1}
is then necessarily a
k
-morphism as well (cf.

[
Spr98
, Ex. 5.3.5]
), so that
\mathbf{G}
is
k
-isomorphic to

\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
.
∎

Definition 4.21

(Adjoint group)
.

A connected group
\mathbf{G}
is called
adjoint
if its kernel
Z(\mathbf{G})

is trivial.

4.4
Unipotent elements

Let
\mathbf{V}
be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
, of finite dimension
n
.

Definition 4.22

(Nilpotent element)
.

An element
X\in\End(\mathbf{V})
is called
nilpotent

if
X^{p}=0
for some integer
p
.

Proposition 4.23
.

For
X\in\End(\mathbf{V})
, the following are equivalent:

•

X
is nilpotent.

•

All eigenvalues of
X
are
0
.

•

\mathrm{tr}(X^{j})=0
for
j=1,\dots,n
.

Definition 4.24

(Unipotent element)
.

An element
g\in\GL(\mathbf{V})
is called unipotent if
g-1
is nilpotent.
Equivalently:
g
is unipotent if all its eigenvalues (in
\mathbb{C}
) equal
1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be an algebraic
k
-group for some
k\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathfrak{g}\subset\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
for some vector space

\mathbf{V}
, we may speak of unipotent element in
\mathbf{G}
or nilpotent elements in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proposition 4.25
.

If
\mathfrak{g}_{k}
possesses a nonzero nilpotent element, then
\mathbf{G}(k)
has a
nontrivial unipotent element.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
A\in\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})
then
e^{\lambda_{i}}

are the eigenvalues of its exponential
\exp(A)
. Thus for
X\in\mathfrak{g}

nilpotent, we have that
g=\exp(X)\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is unipotent. But the
nilpotence also implies that
\exp(X)
is a finite sum (in

\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})
), so that
g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\cap\End(\mathbf{V}_{k})=\mathbf{G}(k)
.

It remains to prove that
g\neq 1
for
X
nonzero. Let
p\geq 1
be the
least integer such that
X^{p}\neq 0
, and suppose that
\exp(X)=1
.
For any integer
n
we can use the formula
\exp(nX)=\exp(X)^{n}
, so
that we have the following equality:

\displaystyle 1+nX+\frac{n^{2}X^{2}}{2!}+\dots+\frac{n^{p}X^{p}}{p!}

\displaystyle=1.

By dividing by
n^{p}
and letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain
X^{p}=0
.
This contradicts the choice of
p
.
∎

4.5
Semisimple groups

Recall that an abstract group
G
is called simple if it has no proper normal
subgroups (i.e., other than
\left\{1\right\}
and
G
). It is useful to adapt
this definition in the context of algebraic groups.

Definition 4.26

(Simple algebraic group)
.

An algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
is called
simple
if it is not
commutative and has no closed connected normal subgroup apart from
\left\{1\right\}
and
\mathbf{G}
.

Remark 4.27
.

Very often one says “almost simple” in this context, to distinguish
from the notion for abstract groups. Note that if
\mathbf{G}
is simple as an
abstract group, then it is simple in the sense of
definition
4.26
. Of course the converse does not
hold.

Remark 4.28
.

Excluding commutative groups in the definition excludes
\mathbf{G}_{a}
and

\mathbf{G}_{m}
, which both have no proper connected normal subgroups.

Note that in the definition of a simple algebraic group one really exclude the
existence of closed normal subgroup, and not only
k
-closed subgroup (i.e.,

k
-subgroup). The definition can be weakened to the following.

Definition 4.29

(
k
-simple group)
.

A noncommutative
k
-group
\mathbf{G}
is called
k
-simple if it has no normal
connected
k
-subgroup other than
\left\{1\right\}
and
\mathbf{G}
.

Remark 4.30
.

Then “simple” implies “
k
-simple” (but not conversely).

Example 4.31
.

Proposition 4.33

Proof.

We explain the idea.
The center

In characteristic zero, there is a strong relation between closed subgroups of

and Lie subalgebras of

See
[

Let

.

Such a map

Corollary 4.39
.

If

\mathrm{Lie}(\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G}))\cong\mathfrak{g}
.

Proposition 4.40
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a connected
k
-simple algebraic group. Then
\mathbf{G}

k
-character.

Proof.

Again, we limit ourself to the idea of the proof.

A Lie algebra

Example 4.44

If

Chapter 5
Uniform arithmetic lattices

Unless stated otherwise, the algebraic groups considered in this chapter are
connected.

5.1
The compactness criterion

The goal of this chapter is to prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a semisimple adjoint
\mathbb{Q}
-group. If
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
does not contain any nontrivial unipotent
element, then
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact.

Remark 5.2
.

This proves that for such a
\mathbf{G}
, any arithmetic subgroup in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
is
a lattice in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
. This fact readily follows from Theorem
3.63

(which we will not proved during this course).

Several improvements to this theorem are possible. First, it is possible to drop
the assumption that
\mathbf{G}
is adjoint, and work with a more general semisimple

\mathbb{Q}
-group. Moreover, the converse of the theorem also holds: if
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Z})
is a
uniform lattice in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
then
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
cannot contain any unipotent element.

One can show that for a semisimple
\mathbf{G}
the absence of unipotent
elements in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
is equivalent to saying that there is no nontrivial

\mathbb{Q}
-morphism
\mathbf{G}_{m}\to\mathbf{G}
(such a morphism is called a
\mathbb{Q}
-cocharacter).
In this case, one says that
\mathbf{G}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-anisotropic
(in contrast to

\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic). This last condition, equivalent to the absence of rational
unipotent elements in the semismple case, turns out to be the right condition to
state the general result. The following theorem was conjectured by Godement. Its
proof is done by reducing the problem to the statement of
Theorem
5.1
(and its converse). The proof appear for
instance in
[
Bor69
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(Godement’s compactness criterion)
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-group without nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character. Then the
lattice
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is uniform if and only if
\mathbf{G}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-anisotropic.

Together with the theorem of Borel and Harish-Chandra, this result
completely solves the problem of determining when the arithmetic subgroups in
a
\mathbb{Q}
-group
\mathbf{G}
are lattices or uniform lattices in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
.

5.2
Arithmetic groups and representations

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-group and
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space with a

\mathbb{Q}
-representation
\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathbf{V})
. Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
be
an arithmetic subgroup and
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice. Then there
exists a subgroup of finite index
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
that stabilizes

L
: for any
g\in\Gamma_{0}
one has
gL=L
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{W})
. For simplicity we will assume
that
\mathbf{G}
has no
\mathbb{Q}
-character, but the same proof works by realizing

\mathbf{G}
as a closed subgroup of some
\SL(\mathbf{W})
.

By definition, there is a

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L^{\prime}\subset\mathbf{W}_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
\Delta=\mathbf{G}_{L}\cap\Gamma
has
finite index in
\Gamma
. Let us write
\Gamma=g_{1}\Delta\cup\dots\cup g_{r}\Delta
. Then the lattice
L^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}L^{\prime}
is a

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice in
\mathbf{W}_{\mathbb{Q}}
invariant by
\Gamma
. By choosing a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
of
L^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain an embedding
\Gamma\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
(where
n=\dim(\mathbf{W})
and with the identification
\GL(\mathbf{W}_{\mathbb{Q}})=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
).

Let us now consider a
\mathbb{Q}
-representation
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathbf{V})
, and
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice. By choosing a basis of
L
,
we can identify
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}})=\GL_{s}(\mathbb{Q})
.
Then
\phi
can be written by matrix components
\phi=(\phi_{ij})
,
where for
g\in\GL_{n}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, each
\phi_{ij}(g)
is given by a polynomial
in terms of
g_{11},\dots,g_{nn}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}

(note that
\det(g)=1
, since we assume that
\mathbf{G}
has no
\mathbb{Q}
-character).
Let
m
be the least common multiple to
all denominators in the coefficients of all the
\phi_{ij}
’s. Then the
polynomials
f_{ij}=m\phi_{ij}
have coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
be the congruence subgroup

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{g\in\Gamma\;|\;g\equiv 1\mod m\right\},

which has finite index in
\Gamma
. Now, for
g\in\Gamma_{0}
we have
f_{ij}(g)-f_{ij}(e)\in m\mathbb{Z}
for any
(i,j)
, so that

\phi_{ij}(g)\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows that
\phi(\Gamma)\subset\GL_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
,
which means that
\Gamma
stabilizes the
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\to\GL(\mathbf{V})
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-representation, and
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
an arithmetic subgroup.
There exists a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
stabilized by
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
L_{0}\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be any
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice, and (by the preceding
lemma) let
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
a subgroup of finite index
stabilizing
L_{0}
. For
\Gamma=g_{1}\Gamma_{0}\cup\dots\cup g_{n}\Gamma_{0}
, let us define
L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}L_{0}
. Then
L
is a

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice stabilized by
\Gamma
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

To say that
\Gamma
stabilizes a lattice is not the same as saying that

\Gamma
is the full stabilizer of the lattice.

Remark 5.7
.

Note that in Lemma
5.5
,
the lattice
L
stabilized by
\Gamma
can be chosen to contains a given

v\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
(indeed, to contain a given lattice
L_{0}
).

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\phi:\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphism of
\mathbb{Q}
-groups.
If
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
is arithmetic, then
\phi(\Gamma)
is
arithmetic in
\mathbf{H}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Gamma\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})
is arithmetic.
By Lemma
5.5
the image
\phi(\Gamma)

stabilizes a lattice
L
, so that
\phi(\Gamma)\subset\mathbf{H}_{L}
. Applying
the same reasoning with
\phi^{-1}
, there exists
L^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma\subset\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{H}_{L})\subset\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}.

Then the index
[\mathbf{H}_{L}:\phi(\Gamma)]=[\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{H}_{L}):\Gamma]
is
bounded by
[\mathbf{G}_{L^{\prime}}:\Gamma]
, and thus finite. This shows that

\phi(\Gamma)
is arithmetic.
∎

Since
\phi
induces an homeomorphism
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma\cong\mathbf{H}(\mathbb{R})/\phi(\Gamma)
,
we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.9
.

\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is a (uniform) lattice if and only
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbf{H}(\mathbb{R})
is a (uniform) lattice.

In particular, for
\mathbf{G}
semisimple and adjoint we can replace the study of

\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
by the study of
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})_{\mathbb{Z}}
in
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})(\mathbb{R})
.

5.3
A proper embedding

Let
X=\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, that is,
X
can be identified with the space of
of Euclidean lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see Chapter
1
). Using Mahler compactness
criterion, we can deduce in a straightforward way that
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is
not compact: if it were, so would be its image in
X
, which is not the case (the
systole of lattices parametrized by
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is arbitrarily small).

Let
\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-group.
To prove Theorem
5.1
, the idea is also to reduce the problem
to an application of Mahler’s compactness criterion on the embedding

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{L}\hookrightarrow X
, where
X
is identified with

\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}})/\GL(L)
for some
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. But
a priori

compactness of the image in
X
does not implies compactness of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{L}
:
we have to show that the embedding has good properties (namely, that it is

proper
). This is the goal of this section.

Let us fix a basis of
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, so that
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}})
is identified with

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
, and
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\mathbf{G}_{L}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character.
Then the product
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\cdot\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is closed in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Let
g_{n}\cdot\gamma_{n}
that converges to
h\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, with
g_{n}\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
and
\gamma_{n}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. We have to show that
h\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. By the corollary to Chevalley’s theorem
(Corollary
4.7
), there exists a
\mathbb{Q}
-representation of

\GL_{n}
on some
\mathbf{V}
such that
\mathbf{G}
is the stabilizer of some
v\in\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
belongs to
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
gv=v
. It follows that
\gamma_{n}^{-1}g_{n}^{-1}v=\gamma_{n}^{-1}v
converges
to
h^{-1}v
in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Using Lemma
5.5
we can choose a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
containing
v
and stabilized by
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Thus
\gamma_{n}^{-1}v\in L
form a discrete set in
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{R}}
, proving that

\gamma_{n}^{-1}v=h^{-1}v
for
n
large enough. This implies that
h\gamma_{n}^{-1}
stabilizes
v
for
n
large enough, so that
h\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character.
The injection
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to X
is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset of
X
.
In particular,
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact if and only if its image in
X

satisfies to the conditions of Mahler’s compactness criterion.

Proof.

By definition of the quotient topology, the image
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
in
X

is closed if and only if
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is closed in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
which was proved in the preceding lemma.
To see that the injection is a homeomorphism we need to show that
if
S\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is closed in
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
then
S\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is closed in

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
(passing to the quotient this shows the continuity of the
inverse of the injection). But if
S\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is closed, we may write it as

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\setminus U
for some
U\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
open. Then
S\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
=
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\setminus U\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, which is closed.

For the last assertion, denote by
A
the image of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
in
X
.
If
A
satisfies the conditions of Mahler’s criterion, then
A=\overline{A}
is compact, and thus
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is compact (since the
injection is a homeomorphism).
∎

5.4
Proof of the theorem

Let
\mathbf{G}
be semisimple and adjoint, so that
\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial

\mathbb{Q}
-character and
\mathbf{G}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-isomorphic to
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
. Suppose that

\mathbf{G}\subset\GL(\mathbf{V})
, where
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of dimension
N
.
Let
L\subset\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice; fixing a basis of

we have an
identification

, and
\GL(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}})=\GL_{N}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\subset\End(\mathbf{V})
be the Lie algebra of
\mathbf{G}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a

\mathbb{Q}
-subspace of
\End(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{Q}})
, and it follows that the intersection
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\End(L)\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice in
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. By construction,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N\times N}
.
\mathbf{G}
acts on
\mathfrak{g}
via the adjoint representation:
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})\subset\GL(\mathfrak{g})
; but note that since
\mathbf{G}
has no nontrivial
\mathbb{Q}
-character, we
actually have
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})\subset\SL(\mathfrak{g})
. In particular, the adjoint
representation preserves the Euclidean volume on
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
We will denote by
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})_{\mathbb{Z}}
the stabilizer of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}
in
\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})(\mathbb{Q})
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbf{G}_{L}
is not compact. Since
\mathbf{G}\cong\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})
, by
Corollary
5.9
this is the case exactly when

\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})_{\mathbb{Z}}
is not compact. To proves
Theorem

, we need to show that in this case
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{Q})

possesses a nontrivial unipotent element. By

4.25
it suffices to show that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Q}}
has a

Now we apply Proposition
5.11
: if

\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{Ad}(\mathbf{G})_{\mathbb{Z}}
is not compact, then the set of lattices in

given by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathrm{Ad}(g)\cdot\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{Z}}\;|\;g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

does not satisfy the conditions of Mahler’s criterion. But all these lattices
have same covolume, so that the systole gets arbitrarily small in

.
This means that there exists

and

such that

converges to

But this implies that for

converges to

. Since

has coefficients in

we have that for

. By Proposition

, such an

must be

6.2
Number fields and discrete groups

The
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\mathcal{O}
can be seen as a (Euclidean) lattice in a suitable vector

\sigma:k\to\mathbb{C}
we set

if
\sigma

\displaystyle k_{\mathbb{R}}=\prod_{\sigma}k_{\sigma},

where the product runs over each real monomorphism and over one
representative
in each pair of complex monomorphism. Then, as a real vector space

same dimension as the degree
[k:\mathbb{Q}]

Example 6.12
.

•

For

•

For

•

For

In the example

lattice in
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}

For this, we consider the natural injection map

given by

Proposition 6.13

The

In some sense this generalizes the discreteness of

in

We would like to generalize this construction for an arbitrarily

k
-group
\mathbf{G}
, with
k
a number field. To simplify the notation we
will restrict to the case where

It will be convenient to see

Being

\displaystyle\left\{g\in\GL_{N}(\mathbb{C})\;|\;f(g_{11},\dots,g_{nn})=0\mbox{ for all }f\in S\right\},

For

Let

Moreover, if

Suppose that

•

groups but give an alternative description to the construction we already had at

Remark 6.20
.

The assignment

is a functor from the category of the

k
-groups to the category of
\mathbb{Q}
-groups. It is know as

, and denoted by

We refer to
[

, Ch.3 §61]
and
[

for more
information about restriction of scalars.

Lemma 6.26

Let

\mathbb{R}
-group and suppose that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is compact.
Then
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})

Proof.

Suppose that

is unipotent and nontrivial.
By the Jordan normal form
theorem, there exists

such that

\displaystyle hgh^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[h]{ccc}1&1&*\\
0&1&*\\
\vdots&0&\ddots\end{array}\right).

It follows that for
x=hg^{n}h^{-1}
, we have
x_{12}=n
. Thus the
group
h\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})h^{-1}
is not bounded in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Since conjugation
by
h
gives an homeomorphism, we deduces that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is not compact.
∎

\mathbf{G}

\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{O}}
