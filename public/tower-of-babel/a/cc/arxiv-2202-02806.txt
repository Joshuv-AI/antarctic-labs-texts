Multi-component separation, inpainting and denoising with recovery guarantees
Van Tiep Do ∗†

Abstract
. In image processing, problems of separation and reconstruction of missing pixels from incomplete digital images have been far more advanced in past decades. Many empirical results have produced very good results, however, providing a theoretical analysis for the success of algorithms is not an easy task, especially, for inpainting and separating multi-component signals. In this paper, we propose two main algorithms based on l 1 l_{1} constrained and unconstrained minimization for separating N N distinct geometric components and simultaneously filling-in the missing part of the observed image. We then present a theoretical guarantee for these algorithms using compressed sensing technique, which is based on a principle that each component can be sparsely represented by a suitably chosen dictionary. Those sparsifying systems are extended to the case of general frames instead of Parseval frames which have been typically used in the past. We finally prove that the method does indeed succeed in separating point singularities from curvilinear singularities and texture as well as inpainting the missing band contained in curvilinear singularities and texture.



1.
Introduction

In practical applications, many problems in signal or image processing involve the task of separating two or more components from a given image. This problem is of interest in many applications
[
22
,
23
]
, for instance, astronomers may desire to decompose the observed image into pointlike, curvelike, and texture structures. This raises a need to separate various components from the original image in order to effectively analyze them.
Also, the task of inpainting an image which is often composed of two or more components is of interest in many research fields.
However, at the first sight the above problems seem unable to solve from a theoretical point of view because of the ill-poseness of the system that leads to infinitely many solutions. Recently, compressed sensing technique provides a firm foundation to recover a signal from incomplete measurements by exploiting the sparsity of the signal
[
5
]
. It is well known that
l_{1}
minimization performs exceptionally well in locating sparse solutions of underdetermined linear systems.
Based on this observation, a great number of sparsity-based algorithms have been
introduced in
this area. Various empirical works in the past have shown good results
for either inpainting
[
3
]
or geometric separation
[
18
]
.
The
theoretical study
[
10
]
first introduced an asymptotic result for separating points from curves using Parseval frames of wavelets and curvelets.
It was then followed by some other works to either inpaint a single component image
[
7
,
13
,
16
]
or separate two components
[
9
,
10
,
12
]
. Later, our previous work
[
12
]
proposed a framework to deal with simultaneous separation and inpainting for two-component signals.
A critical premise of these methods is that each of the components has a sparse representation in a certain dictionary.

However, all aforementioned papers used Parseval frames and none of them deal with the case of multi-component separation and inpainting. The
problem studied here
is completely different since we have more intersections among components which makes the problem more challenging. Each underlying layer has distinctive features, for instance, pointlike structure (smooth apart from point singularities), cartoon (the piecewise smooth part of the image), and texture (consisting of repeated texels).
There is still a lack of theoretical analysis to explain why simultaneous separation and inpainting is possible for multi-component signals. In an attempt to generalize the theory to multi-component case, the proceeding paper
[
8
]
introduced a theory which also exploited notions of joint concentration and cluster coherence that first appeared in
[
10
]
. However, the theory in
[
8
]
can be problematic to apply for problems of separating more than two components, which will be discussed in Section
3
.

In this paper, motivated by our previous works
[
9
,
12
]
we present a more general framework for simultaneous separation, inpainting, and denoising on degraded multi-component signals. We introduce two algorithms based on
l_{1}
minimization for analyzing these tasks, one deals with noiseless images and the other with additional noise. We then provide a theoretical guarantee for these algorithms by establishing
L_{2}
error of the reconstructions. In addition, our methodology works for non-Parseval frames if each of them possesses a well localized dual frame, see
[
9
]
for the case of separating two components.
We further
apply the results to
the separation of
points from curves and texture while inpaint
ing
the missing content of the image. The key idea to the success of our method lies in the morphological difference among these ground truth components. They are then encoded in three different sparse representation systems, wavelets for representing point singularities, shearlets for representing curvilinear singularities, and Gabor frames for representing texture. Based on that, we prove the success of the proposed algorithms using a multi-scale approach.

2.2.
Problem of image separation

In image processing, the image to be analyzed is often composed of multiple components. Given an image
f

N

(6)

f=\sum_{m}f_{m},

where

For practical purposes, we later assume that these components contain texture and jumps or changes in behavior along point, curvilinear discontinuities.
The prior information on underlying components is important to capture the geometry of the discontinuities by choosing suitable dictionaries, each sparsely represent

2.3.
Problem of image inpainting

Image inpainting

and

denote the orthogonal projection of

onto the missing part and the known part, respectively. Our goal is to reconstruct the original image

3.
Theoretical guarantee for multiple component separation problems

3.1.
Joint concentration analysis

The notion of joint concentration was first introduced in
[
10
]
for separating two geometric components using Paseval frames. There, the joint concentration associated with two Parseval frames
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
and sets of indexes
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
was defined by

(8)

\kappa(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{1}}\Phi_{1}^{\star}x\|_{1}+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{2}}\Phi_{2}^{\star}x\|_{1}}{\|\Phi_{1}^{\star}x\|_{1}+\|\Phi_{2}^{\star}x\|_{1}}.

In our situation, we are now given the known part
P_{K}f
of a signal
f
where
f
is a superposition of
N
geometric constituents.
An approach was proposed in
[
8
]
to deal with separating tasks, i.e.,
P_{K}f=f,
there

(9)

\kappa=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{1}}\Phi_{1}^{*}x\|_{1}+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{2}}\Phi_{2}^{*}x\|_{1}+\dots+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{N}}\Phi_{N}^{*}x\|}{\|\Phi_{1}^{*}x\|_{1}+\|\Phi_{2}^{*}x\|_{1}+\dots+\|\Phi_{N}^{*}x\|_{1}}.

Let us now have a closer look to these definitions.
Let
(f_{1}^{*},f_{2}^{*},\dots,f_{N}^{*})
be the solution of Algorithm
1
with
P_{K}=Id,
and
(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N})
be the truly underlying components.
In case
N=2
, the underlying reason for the definition (
8
) lies in the fact that
f_{1}^{*}-f_{1}=-(f_{2}^{*}-f_{2})
and the absolute value of the error is then encoded into variable
x
in (
8
). It seems plausible that (
9
) is a generalized version of (
8
).
However, the approach can be problematic for more components since from assumption
f_{1}^{\star}+f_{2}^{\star}+f\dots+f_{N}^{\star}=f_{1}+f_{2}+\dots+f_{N},N\geq 3
we can not invoke a similar relation to encode with the same variable
x
as in (
9
).

In our study, we modify the definition of joint concentration and cluster coherence to adapt to the situation of simultaneous separation and inpainting.
Obviously, the separation problem is a trivial case of simultaneous separation and inpainting problem as we can set missing space
\mathcal{H}_{M}=\emptyset
.
Since we just know the known part, i.e.,
P_{K}f=P_{K}(f_{1}^{\star}+f_{2}^{\star}+f\dots+f_{N}^{\star})=P_{K}(f_{1}+f_{2}+\dots+f_{N})
we will encode with different terms corresponding with component errors
f_{i}^{*}-f_{i},1\leq i\leq N.

This will be now made precise.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{N}
be a set of frames with frame bounds
(A_{1},B_{1}),

(A_{2},B_{2}),\dots,(A_{N},B_{N}),

(i)

\kappa_{N}=\sup_{\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}}\frac{\sum_{m}\Big(\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{K}f_{m}\|_{1}+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{M}f_{m}\|_{1}\Big)}{\|\Phi_{1}^{*}f_{1}\|_{1}+\dots+\|\Phi_{N}^{*}f_{N}\|_{1}},

(ii)

the
separation N-joint concentration

\kappa_{N}^{sep}:=\kappa(\Lambda_{1},P_{K}\Phi_{1};\dots;P_{K}\Lambda_{N},\Phi_{N})
by

(11)

\kappa_{N}^{sep}=\sup_{\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}}\frac{\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{1}}\Phi_{1}^{*}P_{K}f_{1}\|_{1}+\dots+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{N}}\Phi_{N}^{*}P_{K}f_{N}\|_{1}}{\|\Phi_{1}^{*}f_{1}\|_{1}+\dots+\|\Phi_{N}^{*}f_{N}\|_{1}},

(iii)

(12)

\kappa_{N}^{inp}=\sup_{(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N})}\frac{\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{1}}\Phi_{1}^{*}P_{M}f_{1}\|_{1}+\dots+\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{N}}\Phi_{N}^{*}P_{M}f_{N}\|_{1}}{\|\Phi_{1}^{*}f_{1}\|_{1}+\dots+\|\Phi_{N}^{*}f_{N}\|_{1}}.

These notions enable us to provide a theoretical for the success of Algorithm
1

In case of without separating, only inpainting, i.e.,

Bounding each term ensures the successful algorithm for the corresponding task.

Proposition 3.3

Let

\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|f_{m}^{\star}-f_{m}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\delta}{1-2\kappa_{N}}.

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|f_{m}^{\star}-f_{m}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

3.2.
Cluster coherence analysis

Although
Proposition
3.3
ensures the success of Algorithm
1
, it is not obvious and may be difficult to verify when the bound is achieved. This raises a need to create a new tool to analyze the bound in practice. For this purpose, the notion of cluster coherence was first introduced in
[
10
]
to replace the notion
of
joint concentration, it was then followed by many authors
[
7
,
12
,
13
,
16
]
. There, the cluster was defined by

\mu_{c}(\Lambda,\Phi_{1};\Phi_{2}):=\max_{j\in J}\sum_{i\in\Lambda}|\langle\phi_{1i},\phi_{2j}\rangle|.

For our analysis, we optimize the definition in
[
10
]
by putting the sum inside the inner product to make the cluster coherence smaller which therefore offers more advantages in proving the success of the proposed algorithm. We also extend it to the case of general frames instead of Parseval frames.

Definition 3.5
.

Given two frames
\Phi_{1}=(\Phi_{1i})_{i\in I}
and
\Phi_{2}=(\Phi_{2j})_{j\in J}
. Then the cluster coherence
\mu_{c}(\Lambda,\Phi_{1};\Phi_{2})
of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
with respect to the index set
\Lambda\subset I
is defined by

(19)

\mu_{c}(\Lambda,\Phi_{1};\Phi_{2}):=\max_{j\in J}|\langle\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\phi_{1i},\phi_{2j}\rangle|

and the cluster coherence associated with missing subspace

(20)

\mu_{c}(\Lambda,P_{M}\Phi_{1};P_{M}\Phi_{2}):=\max_{j\in J}|\langle\sum_{i\in\Lambda}P_{M}\phi_{1i},P_{M}\phi_{2j}\rangle|.

Obviously, by triangle inequality we have

\max_{j\in J}\Big|\langle\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\phi_{1i},\phi_{2j}\rangle\Big|\leq\max_{j\in J}\sum_{i\in\Lambda}|\langle\phi_{1i},\phi_{2j}\rangle|,

and

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider
N
frames
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{N}
associated with index sets
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},

\dots,\Lambda_{N}
, respectively. The followings hold

(i)

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}^{sep}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\inf_{\Phi_{m}^{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{\Phi_{m}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\neq m\\
1\leq n\leq N\end{subarray}}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{n},P_{K}\Phi_{n};\Phi_{m}^{d})\leq\mu_{c,N}^{sep},

where

\mu_{c,N}^{sep}:=\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\inf_{\Phi_{m}^{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{\Phi_{m}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\neq m\\
1\leq n\leq N\end{subarray}}\Big(\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{n},\Phi_{n};\Phi_{m}^{d})+\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{n},P_{M}\Phi_{n};\Phi_{m}^{d})\Big).

(ii)

\kappa_{N}^{inp}\leq\mu_{c,N}^{inp}:=\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\inf_{\Phi_{m}^{d}\in\mathbb{D}_{\Phi_{m}}}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},P_{M}\Phi_{m};\Phi_{m}^{d}).

(iii)

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu_{c,N}:=\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\Big(\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},P_{M}\Phi_{m};\Phi_{m}^{d})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\neq m\\
1\leq n\leq N\end{subarray}}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{n},P_{K}\Phi_{n};\Phi_{m}^{d})\Big)

\displaystyle\mu_{c,N}^{sep}+\mu_{c,N}^{inp}.

Proof.

(i) First, we set
\alpha_{m}:=\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m},
and
\alpha_{m}=(\alpha_{m1},\alpha_{m2},\cdots,)\in l_{2},\forall m\in\{1,2,\dots N\}.
This leads to

Since
\sum_{m=1}^{N}f_{m}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
yields
\sum_{m=1}^{N}P_{K}f_{n}=0.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{K}f_{m}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{n\neq m}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{K}f_{n}\|_{1}=\|\sum_{n\neq m}\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{K}\Phi_{n}^{d}\alpha_{n}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq m}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{m}}\Big(\sum_{j}|\langle P_{K}\Phi_{mi},\Phi_{nj}^{d}\rangle||\alpha_{nj}|\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq m}\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{m}}|\langle P_{K}\Phi_{mi},\Phi_{nj}^{d}\rangle|\Big)|\alpha_{nj}|

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{K}f_{m}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\Big\{\sum_{n\neq m}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},\Phi_{m};\Phi_{n}^{d})\Big\}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m}\|_{1}.

This finishes the proof.

(ii) We set
\alpha_{m}:=\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m}
for each

. By dual frame property, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{M}f_{m}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{M}\Phi_{m}^{d}\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m}\|_{1}=\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}(P_{M}\Phi_{m})^{*}\Phi_{m}^{d}\alpha_{m}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{m}}\Big(\sum_{j}|\langle P_{M}\Phi_{mi},\Phi_{mj}^{d}\rangle||(\alpha_{m})_{j}|\Big)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{m}}|\langle P_{M}\Phi_{mi},\Phi_{mj}^{d}\rangle|\Big)|(\alpha_{m})_{j}|

\displaystyle\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},P_{M}\Phi_{m};\Phi_{m}^{d})\|\alpha_{m}\|_{1}=\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},P_{M}\Phi_{m};\Phi_{m}^{d})\|\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m}\|_{1},

where
\Phi_{m}=\{\Phi_{mj}\}_{j\in I_{m}},\alpha_{n}=(\alpha_{n1},\alpha_{n2},\dots...)\in l_{2}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\mathds{1}_{\Lambda_{m}}\Phi_{m}^{*}P_{M}f_{m}\|_{1}\leq\max_{1\leq m\leq N}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{m},P_{M}\Phi_{m};\Phi_{m}^{d})\cdot\sum_{1\leq m\leq N}\|\Phi_{m}^{*}f_{m}\|_{1}.

This concludes

1\leq n\leq N\end{subarray}}\mu_{c}(\Lambda_{n},P_{K}\Phi_{n};\Phi_{m}^{d})\Big).

5.
Inpainting and denoising of multiple component images in applications

In image processing, the performance of existing algorithms is effective in different scenarios depending on the geometry of underlying components. Natural images may contain more than two geometric structures, for instance, astronomical images are often composed of points, curves, and texture. One interesting question in application is how to inpaint an image which is composed of multiple components the known part of the groundtruth image
f\in\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Mathematically, we assume that we have the following decomposition

(37)

f=\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{T},

where
f
is the signal of our interest,
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C},
and
\mathcal{T}
are three underlying components that are unknown to us. In addition, we assume that
\mathcal{H}
can be decomposed into the known and unknown subspace by
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{K}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{M}
. Our goal is to recover
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{T}
with only the known part
P_{k}f
.

5.1.
Model of components

In this subsection, we introduce models of pointlike, curvelike, and texture structures which we aim to recover. In our analysis, the model of texture is motivated from
[
12
]
with
s=1.
We modified the model of texture for our need as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a window with
\hat{g}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),
and frequency support
\textup{supp }\hat{g}\in[-1,1]^{2}
satisfying the partition of unity condition

(38)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|\hat{g}(\xi+n)|^{2}=1,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Let
I_{T}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a subset of Fourier elements. A texture is defined by

(39)

\mathcal{T}(x)=\sum_{n\in I_{T}}d_{n}g(x)e^{2\pi ix^{\top}n},

where
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
denotes a bounded sequence of complex numbers.

Point-like structure
\mathcal{P}
is modeled as

(40)

\mathcal{P}(x)=\sum_{1}^{K}|x-x_{i}|^{-3/2}.

Since it is certainly impossible to reconstruct point singularities in the missing part, so we assume that
x_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{K},\forall i=1,2,\dots,K.

Curvelike structure is modeled as a line distribution
w\mathcal{L}
acting on
Schwartz functions
by

(41)

\langle w\mathcal{L},f\rangle=\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}w(x_{1})f(x_{1},0)dx_{1},\;f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

5.2.
Sparse representations

In this section, we focus on tools of harmonic analysis for sparsely approximate each component. In particular, we use the following sparsifying systems which might be best adapted to the geometry of its singularities.

•

Gabor tight frames: A tight frame with time-frequency balanced elements.

•

Radial wavelets: Bandlimited wavelets which form a Parseval frame of isotropic generating elements.

•

Shearlets: A highly directional tight frame with increasingly anisotropic elements at fine scales.

We now fix a constant
\epsilon
satisfying

(42)

We consider the following Gabor tight frame

5.3.
Mutiscale approach

In our analysis, the simultaneous separation and inpainting problem is analyzed at each scale
j
. To achieve this goal we use the following class of
frequency filters

F_{j}

(54)

\hat{F}_{j}(\xi):=W_{j}(\xi)=W(\xi/2^{2j}),\quad\forall j\geq 0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

and in low frequency part

\hat{F}_{low}(\xi):=\Theta(\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

By using these filters, we can decompose
f
into small pieces by
f_{(j)}=F_{j}*f,\;j\geq 0
and
f_{low}=F_{low}*f
. Then (
46

f
by

(55)

f=F_{low}*f_{low}+\sum_{j\geq 0}F_{j}*f_{(j)}.

For multi-scale separation and inpainting, we intend to apply Algorithm
1
, Algorithm
2
for each sub-image
f_{(j)}
, then the whole image is reconstructed by
(
55

. Here we assume that
f_{(j)}=\mathcal{P}_{j}+w\mathcal{L}_{j}+\mathcal{T}_{j}
. In the other words, instead of considering the whole signal we now consider the problem of simultaneous separation and inpainting at each scale. For the theoretical analysis, we assume that the model of missing subspace is chosen at each scale
j
to be
\mathcal{H}_{M}=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{h_{j}}}
where
\mathcal{M}_{h_{j}}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2})\mid|x_{1}|\leq h_{j}\}
is a horizontal strip domain.
This model of missing part was first introduced in
[
13
]
and then also adopted in

16
]

(56)

P_{M,j}=\mathds{1}_{\{|x_{1}|\leq h_{j}\}}.

For an illustration, we refer to Fig.
3

We now face to the task of reconstructing the sub-image
f_{(j)}
with the knowledge of
P_{K,j}f_{(j)}
, where

P_{K,j}=1-P_{M,j}.

In addition, we assume that the energy of texture is comparable to point and curvilinear singularities, i.e.,
\|\mathcal{P}_{j}\|_{2}\approx\|w\mathcal{L}_{j}\|_{2}\approx\|\mathcal{T}_{j}\|_{2}.
This is due to the fact that the problem becomes trivial in the case of different levels of energy. Actually, we can derive similar theoretical results by our approach if the energy of each component has a lower bound at each scale. However, for the sake of simplicity, we assume that
\|\mathcal{T}_{j}\|_{2}\approx 2^{2j}
since we have
\|\mathcal{P}_{j}\|_{2}\approx\|w\mathcal{L}_{j}\|_{2}\approx 2^{2j}.
These energy balancing conditions make the problem of geometric separation challenging at every scale.

5.4.
Main results

Our analysis is based on the fact that the geometric property underlying each component is often encoded in the frame coefficients. In addition, important coefficients cluster geometrically in phase space by microlocal analysis. This therefore allows us to separate components based on the machinery in Theorem
3.7
and
4.1
.
Thus, the correct choice of clusters plays a central role in our analysis. We aim to define the optimal set of significant coefficients to derive good cluster sparsity as well as cluster coherence. By Theorem
3.7
and
4.1
, if clusters are chosen large we might have very a good approximation but the cluster coherence will be big which leads to loose control of the
L_{2}
errors.

We now define clusters for wavelet, shearlet, and Gabor systems.
First the cluster of significant wavelet coefficients is defined by

(57)

\Lambda_{1,j}^{\pm}:=\Lambda_{1,j-1}\cup\Lambda_{1,j}\cup\Lambda_{1,j+1},\;\forall j\geq 1,

where
\Lambda_{1,j}=\{p\mid p\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|p|\leq 2^{\epsilon j}\}.

For the line singularity
w\mathcal{L}_{j}
, we define the set of significant coefficients of shearlet system by

(58)

\Lambda_{2,j}^{\pm}:=\Lambda_{2,j-1}\cup\Lambda_{2,j}\cup\Lambda_{2,j+1},\quad\forall j\geq 2,

where

\Lambda_{2,j}:=\Big\{(j,l,k;{\rm v})\;|\;|l|\leq 1,k=(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},|k_{2}-lk_{1}|\leq 2^{\epsilon j}\Big\}.

We define the cluster for texture at scale
j
to be

(59)

\Lambda_{3,j}^{\pm}:=\Big(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap B(0,M_{j})\Big)\times\Big(I_{T}^{\pm}\cap\mathcal{A}_{j}\Big),

where
I_{T}^{\pm}=\{n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid\exists n\in I_{T}:|n^{\prime}-n|\leq 1\},

M_{j}:=2^{\epsilon j/6}
and
B(0,r)
denotes the closed
l_{2}
ball around the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Here we remark that the index set
\Lambda_{1,j}^{\pm},\Lambda_{2,j}^{\pm},\Lambda_{,j}^{\pm}
were chosen instead of
\Lambda_{1j},\Lambda_{2,j},\Lambda_{3,j}
since there have overlap supports between them and their neighbors.

5.5.
Estimate of approximation errors

Lemma 5.6
.

We have
\delta_{1j}=\circ(2^{-Nj}),\quad j\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can restrict the general case to
\mathcal{P}(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{3/2}}.
By definition, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(j^{\prime},m)\notin\Lambda_{1,j}}\langle\psi_{j^{\prime},m},\mathcal{P}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}\geq 0,|m|>2^{\epsilon j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}2^{-2j}W_{j^{\prime}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi^{T}\frac{m}{2^{2j^{\prime}}}}W_{j}(\xi)\frac{1}{|\xi|}d\xi.

We can assume that
j^{\prime}=j
due to the fact that
W_{j^{\prime}}(\xi)W_{j}(\xi)=0
for
|j^{\prime}-j|>1
.
Applying the change of variables
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=2^{-2j}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),
we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{j}\sum_{|m|>2^{\epsilon j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\eta|}W^{2}(\eta)e^{2\pi i\eta^{T}m}d\eta.

We now apply integration by parts with respect to
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
, respectively, for
N=1,2,\dots,

\displaystyle\delta_{1,j}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\neq 0,\\
|m|>2^{\epsilon j}\end{subarray}}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|m_{1}|^{-N}|m_{2}|^{-N}\frac{\partial^{2N}}{\partial\eta_{1}^{N}\partial\eta_{2}^{N}}\Big[\frac{1}{|\eta|}W(\eta)\Big]e^{2\pi i\zeta^{T}m}d\eta\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{N}2^{j}\sum_{|m|>2^{\epsilon j}}|m_{1}|^{-N}|m_{2}|^{-N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{N}2^{j}2^{-(N-1)(\epsilon j-1)},\quad\forall N\in\mathbb{N}.

Here we note that the boundary terms vanish due to the compact support of
W
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

6.
Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we provide a theoretical study of the simultaneous separation, inpainting, and denoising
for multi-component signals. To achieve this, we first modify the notions of joint concentration and cluster coherence to encode the separating and inpainting tasks separately. One guarantees the success of the geometric separation and one for inpainting missing content. This is possible since the missing trip is assumed to be small which does not affect the geometry of the whole signal.
Theoretical results are shown in Theorems
3.7
and
4.1
guarantee the success of our two proposed algorithms, one for constrained
l_{1}
optimization and the other for unconstrained
l_{1}
optimization.

Motivated by the theoretical guarantees, we aim to decompose the corrupted image into pointlike, curvelike, and texture parts as accurately as possible. In our analysis, we use a multi-scale approach that the task of simultaneous separation and inpainting is done separately at each scale
j
by Algorithm
1
and
2
using wavelets, shearlets, and Gabor frames. The whole image is then reconstructed from its sub-images by the reconstruction formula (
55
). The main idea for the success of the proposed algorithms is that the significant coefficients by wavelets, shearlets, and Gabor are clustered geometrically in phase space by microlocal analysis. The powerful tool based on
l_{1}
minimization then enables us to explore the differences among these underlying components. For our main results, Theorem
5.4
and Theorem
5.5
show that at sufficiently fine scales the true components of the corrupted image are almost captured by proposed algorithms.
In applications, our theory can be adapted with the change of components and sparse representation systems as long as they provide negligible cluster sparsity and cluster coherence.
By choosing suitable sparsifying systems our methodology can handle the following tasks for multi-component images:

(1)

Simultaneous separation, inpainting, and denoising: In this case, Algorithm
2
is taken into account.

(2)

Simultaneous separation and inpainting: For this purpose, we can apply Algorithm
1
.

(3)

Inpainting: Once the inpainted components are extracted, one can aggregate them again for inpainting purposes only.

(4)

Image separation: In this situation, we restrict our results to the case of no missing parts in the original image.
