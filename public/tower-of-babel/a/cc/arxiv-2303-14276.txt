Abstract
. Blockchains facilitate decentralization, security, identity, and data management in cyber-physical systems. However, consensus protocols used in blockchains are prone to high message and computational complexity costs and are not suitable to be used in IoT. One way to reduce message complexity is to randomly assign network nodes into committees or shards. Keeping committee sizes small is then desirable in order to achieve lower message complexity, but this comes with a penalty of reduced reliability as there is a higher probability that a large number of faulty nodes will end up in a committee. In this work, we study the problem of estimating a probability of a failure in randomly sharded networks. We provide new results and improve existing bounds on the failure probability. Thus, our framework also paves the way to reduce committee sizes without reducing reliability.



1.
Introduction

Large-scale applications of Cyber-Physical Systems require a significant amount of coordination of a plethora of interconnected devices. Managing thousands of devices pushes the technology to its limits, and creates one of the most important and not yet solved problems of scaling the solution to myriads of connected devices. There are many approaches that try to address this problem efficiently, some of them are focus on centralized solutions, such as cloud
(
Vasconcelos et al. 2019
;
Žarko et al. 2014
;
Khodadadi et al. 2015
;
Cao et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2017
)
, others are introducing novel architectures like mist and fog
(
Vasconcelos et al. 2019
;
Garcia Lopez et al. 2015
;
Kang et al. 2018
;
El-Hasnony et al. 2021
)
or leveraging fully decentralized solutions based on blockchain
(
Michelin et al. 2018
)
. Each of them has its own merits but in this work, we are focusing on blockchains that have been leveraged to provide decentralized, verifiable, trusted, and traceable IoT-based applications
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
.

Similarly, due to the strict requirements of IoT networks, blockchains are suitable to defend against data manipulation attacks by providing immutability and avoiding centralized trusted authority for devices to communicate
(
Salimitari et al. 2020
)
. Therefore, there have been several research works on leveraging blockchains to provide public safety service
(
Xu et al. 2019a
)
, smart surveillance for smart cities
(
Xu et al. 2019b
;
Nikouei et al. 2018
)
, automotive industry
(
Fraga-Lamas and Fernández-Caramés 2019
)
and avionics
(
Blasch et al. 2019
)
. Furthermore, blockchain technology has also been leveraged to provide privacy and security in IoT, identity and data management for IoT and monetization for IoT
(
Ali et al. 2019
)
.

Blockchain uses distributed consensus mechanism to reach an agreement for a value to be added to the chain. Consensus makes sure every honest node will eventually agree on the same block at the same sequence in the chain. Consensus is used to achieve State Machine Replication (SMR).
It makes sure that each participant of the network maintains the same state all the time. Well-constructed blockchains must be fault-resilient in order to survive Byzantine faults
(
Lamport et al. 1982
)
which are one of the most powerful groups of faults in distributed systems.
A Byzantine Fault Tolerant (BFT) system tolerates arbitrary faults (and adversaries). A BFT SMR system provides SMR services in the presence of Byzantine Faults. The upper bound on tolerating Byzantine faults is
f=\frac{n-1}{3}

(
Fischer et al. 1985
)
. This means a system can remain
reliable
as long as the number of Byzantine faults is less than one-third.

BFT-based consensus protocols provide BFT SMR services. These services constitute the core layer of blockchains.
Due to the high interest in blockchain, its scalability in terms of the number of nodes (participants) has been one of the main research problems
(
Luu et al. 2016
;
Jalalzai et al. 2020
;
Jalalzai et al. 5555
;
Jalalzai et al. 2019
;
Yin et al. 2018
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Buterin et al. 2020
)
. One of the limitations of scaling blockchains is the high message complexity and authenticator (cryptographic signatures) complexity. Processing a higher number of messages and/or cryptographic operations in a consensus protocol makes it unsuitable for IoT use cases. This is due to the reason that IoT devices have limited bandwidth and processing capacity. Generally, the number of messages as well as cryptographic operations increase as the number of nodes in the network increases.
To scale blockchains (to reduce message and authenticator complexity) different methods were developed to assign nodes randomly in committees
(
Jalalzai et al. 5555
;
Luu et al. 2016
;
Buterin et al. 2020
;
Baczkowski 2022
)
. The introduction of committees helps to reduce the message and authenticator complexity from
O(N)
to
O(n)
with
n\ll N
, where
n
is the size of the committee and
N
is the total number of nodes.

However, randomly assigning nodes into committees is not a straightforward task. One does not want to form a committee with a majority of Byzantine nodes. Therefore, it is paramount to estimate the probability of such an event. Achieving tighter bounds on a committee size for a desired failure probability will allow a protocol to have smaller committees. Smaller committees mean lower message complexity and cryptographic operations, making a protocol more suitable for IoT use cases.
Therefore, in this work, we present analytical results of calculating the probability of a failure of a network in an event of network random partition. First, we introduce a very general probabilistic framework of random partitions of
N
nodes of different “colours” into
K
committees. This framework allows us to construct joint probability distributions of random partitions. Second, we obtain exact expressions for these probability distributions and use them to study the probability of failure. The latter is the probability of the event that in at least one of the randomly generated committees, the nodes of some colour exceed a fraction
A
of all nodes. We consider the probability of failure and we obtain exact expressions, bounds, and asymptotic estimates. Finally, we apply our analytical framework to the sharding of blockchains, which is a special case of random partitions with two colours
labelling Byzantine and honest nodes
, and improve upon known results in this area. In particular, we achieve tighter bounds on committee sizes for a given probability of failure, than the previous known results. Moreover, the term Byzantine and adversarial has been used interchangeably in this document.

1.1.
Related work

The number of adversaries in a single committee is usually modeled with the binomial
(
Kokoris-Kogias et al. 2018
;
Tennakoon and Gramoli 2022
)
and hypergeometric
(
Zamani et al. 2018
;
Dang et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
probability distributions. The probability of failure is estimated by the union bound which multiplies the probability of failure of a single committee, which uses cumulative distribution function of one of these distributions, by the number of committees
K
. The latter suggests that these univariate distributions are marginals of some joint probability distribution. Recently it was established that in hypergeometric case this joint distribution is the multivariate hypergeometric
(
Hafid et al. 2020a
)
. The latter corresponds to a sampling scenario where nodes for committees are selected, without replacement, from a population of
N
nodes where it is assumed that
M
nodes in this population are adversarial. However, it is not clear what is this joint distribution for the binomial case. We note that the binomial distribution is quite often chosen as an approximation
(
Zamani et al. 2018
)
for the hypergeometric and is expected to be accurate when the committee size
n
is small, the number of nodes
N
is large and the number of adversarial nodes
M
is also large. The probability of failure which uses
full
joint probability distribution, to the best of our knowledge, has not been studied but in one work
(
Hafid et al. 2020a
)
where intuitive derivation of the multivariate hypergeometric distribution was provided and corresponding probability of failure was studied by simulations. The latter showed advantages of using the joint distribution instead of the union bound but left analytic solution to be an open problem
(
Hafid et al. 2020b
)
. The main contribution of this work is to provide a solution to this open problem.

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(N^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1|N_{\mu})\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}}{\sqrt{8N_{\mu}Q(\mu)\left(1-Q(\mu)\right)}}

for
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
,
by the
Lemma
4.7.2 in
(
Ash 1990
)
. Using these bounds in (
26
) gives us the lower and upper bound in (
23
).
∎

We note that a
tighter
upper bound in (

) can be obtained by using the recent result

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(N^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1|N_{\mu})\leq\frac{1}{1-r(\mu)}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}}{\sqrt{2\pi Q(\mu)\left(1-Q(\mu)\right)N_{\mu}}},

where
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, of the

(

)
. Here the term
\frac{1}{1-r(\mu)}
, where
r(\mu)=\frac{\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\left(1-Q(\mu)\right)}{Q(\mu)\left(1-\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}
, is an upper bound on the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{\left(1-Q(\mu)\right)N_{\mu}}r^{k}(\mu)=(1-r^{\left(1-Q(\mu)\right)N_{\mu}+1}(\mu))/(1-r(\mu))
.

3.2.1.
Saddle-point method

For random partitions generated by the probability distribution (
5
), i.e.
exactly

M
nodes out of
N
are of colour
\alpha
, the probability of failure is given by

(31)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathrm{P}\left(\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\}|\{N_{\mu}\},M\right),

where the probability

(32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\}|\{N_{\mu}\};M\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\mathrm{P}\left(N_{1}^{\alpha},\ldots,N_{K}^{\alpha}|N_{1},\ldots,N_{K};M\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathds{1}\left[N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\delta_{M;\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}_{\mu}}\mathds{1}\left[N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\right]}{{N\choose M}}

follows from the result (
14
) for the distribution (
5
).

We would like to obtain a simpler analytic expression for
\delta
but the challenge here is in computing the sums in (
32
) efficiently. It is not clear how to do this for any
N
,
M
and
K
, but in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
with
N_{\mu}<\infty
such that
N=\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}
and
M/N\in(0,1)
we can obtain the following result

(33)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\}|\{N_{\mu}\},M\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\sqrt{\frac{N\,P(1-P)}{\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[\langle(N^{\alpha})^{2}\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}-\langle N^{\alpha}\rangle^{2}_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}\right]}}\mathrm{e}^{N\Psi[Q]}\!+\!O(1/N),

where
P=M/N
and we defined the function

(34)

\displaystyle\Psi[Q]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(P||Q)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\,Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}

and average

(35)

\displaystyle\langle f(N^{\alpha})\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}f(N^{\alpha})}{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}}

for any function
f(N^{\alpha})
. In above
Q\in(0,1)
is the solution of the equation

(36)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\langle N^{\alpha}\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}

for
P\leq A
.

In this work, we will present only a heuristic argument that leads to the equation (
33
), but we envisage that a more rigorous proof of the latter is also possible. Our approach to computing (
32
) is to use the
saddle-point
method of integration, which is quite often used in statistical physics
(
Nishimori 2001
)
and analytic combinatorics
(
Flajolet and Sedgewick 2009
)
to compute moment generating functions, but first in order to apply this method we need to represent the sums in (
32
) as an integral. To this end, we rewrite the numerator in (
32
) as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\delta_{M;\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}_{\mu}}\mathds{1}\left[N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\right]

\displaystyle~~~~~=\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\!\!z^{-M-1+\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}z\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}_{\mu}}\mathds{1}\left[N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\right]

\displaystyle~~~=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{N_{\mu}}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\mathds{1}\left[N^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\right]z^{N^{\alpha}}\right\}z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z

(37)

\displaystyle~~~=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})\right\}\,z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z,

where we used the integral representation
\delta_{n;m}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}z^{-n-1+m}\,\mathrm{d}z
and defined the function

(38)

\displaystyle\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})=\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}z^{N^{\alpha}}.

Second, the denominator

(39)

\displaystyle{N\choose M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\delta_{M;\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{K}=0}^{N_{K}}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\!\!z^{-M-1+\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}^{\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}z\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\phi_{1}(z|N_{\mu})\right\}\,z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z

and hence

(40)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\}|\{N_{\mu}\},M\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})\right\}\,z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z}{\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\phi_{1}(z|N_{\mu})\right\}\,z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z}.

Let us consider the scenario of
N\rightarrow\infty
with
N_{\mu}<\infty
such that
N=\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}
and
0<M/N<1
. Then for the integral

(41)

\displaystyle\oint_{|z|=1}\!\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})\right\}\,z^{-M-1}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{|z|=1}\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{e}^{N\Psi_{A}(z)-\log(z)},

where in above we defined
P=M/N
and

(42)

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-P\log(z)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu}),

we can try to use the saddle-point integration method
(
Fedoryuk 1977
)
. Applying this method allows us to write

(43)

\displaystyle\oint_{|z|=1}\!\!\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{e}^{N\Psi_{A}(z)-\log(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\frac{2\pi}{N\ddot{\Psi}_{A}(z_{0}(A))}}\left[1/z_{0}(A)+O(1/N)\right]\mathrm{e}^{N\Psi_{A}(z_{0}(A))},

where in above we used the definition
\ddot{\Psi}_{A}(z)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\Psi_{A}(z)
and
z_{0}(A)
is the solution of the equation
\dot{\Psi}_{A}(z)=0
, which is given by

(44)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}z^{N^{\alpha}}N^{\alpha}}{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}z^{N^{\alpha}}}.

Thus, for the probability (
32
) we obtain the following expression

(45)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\}|\{N_{\mu}\},M\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1/z_{0}(A)+O(1/N)}{1/z_{0}(1)+O(1/N)}\sqrt{\frac{\ddot{\Psi}_{1}(z_{0}(1))}{\ddot{\Psi}_{A}(z_{0}(A))}}\mathrm{e}^{N[\Psi_{A}(z_{0}(A))-\Psi_{1}(z_{0}(1))]}.

Let us now consider the equation (
44
). In the latter
0<P<1
and hence for

we can set

, where
0<Q<1
, in this equation, giving us

(46)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\langle N^{\alpha}\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}

where we defined the average

(47)

\displaystyle\langle f(N^{\alpha})\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}f(N^{\alpha})}{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}}.

Using
N^{\alpha}\leq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor
in the equation (

) gives us the inequality

(48)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\frac{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\,Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor\leq A

and hence equation (

) has a solution only for

.

Furthermore, for
A=1
the
Q=P
is the solution of the equation (
36
) giving us
z_{0}(1)=P/(1-P)
. The latter can be used to compute the function

(49)

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(z_{0}(1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-P\log(P/(1-P))-\log(1-P)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{N_{\mu}}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}P^{N^{\alpha}}[1-P]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(P),

where
\mathcal{S}(P)=-P\log(P)-(1-P)\log(1-P)

is Shannon’s entropy. For
\Psi_{A}(z_{0}(A))
with

, where
Q
is the solution of the equation (

), we obtain

(50)

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}(z_{0}(A))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-P\log(Q/(1-Q))-\log(1-Q)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\,Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}

and hence the difference

(51)

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}(z_{0}(A))-\Psi_{1}(z_{0}(1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-P\log(Q/(1-Q))-\log(1-Q)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\,Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}-\mathcal{S}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(P||Q)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\log\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}\,Q^{N^{\alpha}}[1-Q]^{N_{\mu}-N^{\alpha}}.

Let us consider now the derivative

(52)

\displaystyle\ddot{\Psi}_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P}{z^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\log\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P}{z^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[\frac{\ddot{\phi}_{A}(z|N_{\mu})}{\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})}-\left\{\frac{\dot{\phi}_{A}(z|N_{\mu})}{\phi_{A}(z|N_{\mu})}\right\}^{2}\right],

where

(53)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{A}(z|N_{\mu})=\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}z^{N^{\alpha}-1}N^{\alpha}.

and

(54)

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}_{A}(z|N_{\mu})=\sum_{N^{\alpha}=0}^{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor}{N_{\mu}\choose N^{\alpha}}z^{N^{\alpha}-2}N^{\alpha}(N^{\alpha}-1).

For

, where

is a solution of the equation (

(55)

\displaystyle\ddot{\Psi}_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-Q}{Q}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[\langle(N^{\alpha})^{2}\rangle_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}-\langle N^{\alpha}\rangle^{2}_{A,Q,N_{\mu};}\right]

and for

it gives us

(56)

\displaystyle\ddot{\Psi}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-P}{P}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[\langle(N^{\alpha})^{2}\rangle_{1,P,N_{\mu};}-\langle N^{\alpha}\rangle^{2}_{1,P,N_{\mu};}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-P}{P}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}N_{\mu}P(1-P)=\frac{\left(1-P\right)^{3}}{P}.

Hence using all of the above results in (

) gives us the equation (

).

3.2.2.
The union bound

The failure event (
21
) is equivalent to the union

\cup_{\mu=1}^{K}\left\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}(\mathcal{A})\rfloor+1\right\}

and hence the probability of failure
\delta=\mathrm{P}\left(\cup_{\mu=1}^{K}\left\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1\right\}\right)
. The latter can be exploited to derive the following

Theorem 3.4.

For random partitions sampled from the probability distribution (
3
) when
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
, the probability of failure

(57)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N\Phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha,\mu),Q(\mu))},

where

(58)

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha,\mu),Q(\mu))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\left(\mathrm{P}(\mu)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}+1-\mathrm{P}(\mu)\right).

Proof.

First, by Boole’s inequality, also known as the union bound, we have that

(59)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(\cup_{\mu=1}^{K}\left\{N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}(\mathcal{A})\rfloor+1\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{P}\left(N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1\right)

and hence the probability of failure

(60)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{P}\left(N_{\mu}^{\alpha}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1\right).

Second, using the result (
6
) for the distribution (
3
) we obtain the probability

(61)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(N^{\alpha}_{\mu}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{\mu}=0}^{N}\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{\mu}=\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}^{N_{\mu}}\mathrm{P}^{\alpha}\!\left(N_{\mu},N^{\alpha}_{\mu}|N\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{\mu}=0}^{N}\mathrm{P}_{\mu}\!\left(N_{\mu}|N\right)\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{\mu}=\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}^{N_{\mu}}\mathrm{P}^{\alpha}_{\mu}\!\left(N^{\alpha}_{\mu}|N_{\mu}\right),

where in above we used that

\mathrm{P}^{\alpha}\!\left(N_{\mu},N^{\alpha}_{\mu}|N\right)
, defined in (
12
), is the marginal of (
6
).

Now the probability

(62)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(N^{\alpha}_{\mu}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1|N_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{\mu}=\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}^{N_{\mu}}\mathrm{P}^{\alpha}_{\mu}\!\left(N^{\alpha}_{\mu}|N_{\mu}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}

by the
Lemma
4.7.2 in
(
Ash 1990
)
for
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
, and hence we obtain

(63)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\left(N^{\alpha}_{\mu}\geq\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{\mu}=0}^{N}\mathrm{P}_{\mu}\!\left(N_{\mu}|N\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{P}(\mu)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}+1-\mathrm{P}(\mu)\right]^{N}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-N\Phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha,\mu),Q(\mu))},

which can be used to bound the sum in (
60
) and hence giving us the inequality (
57
).
∎

We note that a tighter, but slightly more complicated, upper bound on
\delta
can be obtained if we use (
30
) instead of (
28
) in the proof. Furthermore, we obtained a less tight and slightly simpler upper bound in the following

Corollary 3.5.

For random partitions generated by the probability distribution (
3
) when
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
, the probability of failure

(64)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N\Phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha,\mu),Q(\mu))}\leq\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N\mathrm{P}(\mu)\,\phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu),Q(\mu))},

where
\phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu),Q(\mu))=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}
.

Proof.

Using
\log(x)\leq x-1
in the function (
58
) gives us

(65)

\displaystyle-\Phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha,\mu),Q(\mu))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\mu)\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}-1\right]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~=-\mathrm{P}(\mu)\phi(\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu),Q(\mu))

and hence using above in (
57
) gives us the second inequality in (
64
).
∎

Finally, a slight modification of the above proof also gives us the following

Corollary 3.6.

For random partitions generated by the probability distribution (
4
) when
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
, the probability of failure

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}.

Proof.

To show this we exploit that the marginal of the probability distribution (
11
), i.e. the result for (
4
), is the binomial (
8
). Thus if we replace
\mathrm{P}_{\mu}\!\left(N_{\mu}|N\right)
in the proof of

by the
\delta_{N_{\mu};\tilde{N}_{\mu}}
, where
\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\tilde{N}_{\mu}=N
, we obtain (

).
∎

We note that for the hypergeometric distribution (
14
) the probability of failure

(67)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\sum_{N^{\alpha}_{\mu}=\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}^{N_{\mu}}\mathrm{P}\left(N_{\mu}^{\alpha}|N_{\mu};M\right)

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||M/N\right)},

where

is the number of nodes of colour
\alpha
. The first inequality in above is an application of (

) with the marginal (

) and the second

(
Hoeffding 1963
)

(
Chvátal 1979
)
. The union bounds (

), (
66
) and (

) suggest that for

the probability of failure

if we assume that

for all

as

. However, it is easy to construct a partition for each of these bounds such that the bound exceeds unity. Here it is interesting to compare the union bound in (

) with the upper bound in (

) which was derived using a different approach. The latter is bounded above by unity, but the former can be above unity suggesting that (
66
) is a looser bound on

which is confirmed by the following

Lemma 3.7.

For random partitions generated by the probability distribution (
4
) when
\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)<Q(\mu)<1
, where
Q(\mu)=\frac{\lfloor AN_{\mu}\rfloor+1}{N_{\mu}}
, the probability of failure

(69)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}\right]\leq\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-N_{\mu}\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)}.

Proof.

To show this we consider the difference

(70)

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\prod_{\mu=1}^{K}\left[1-W(\mu)\right]-\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}W(\mu),

where we defined the “weight”

. The latter, because of
\mathrm{D}\left(Q(\mu)||\mathrm{P}(\alpha|\mu)\right)\geq 0
, belongs to the interval
(0,1]
for any finite
N_{\mu}
.
We note that
\Delta_{1}=0
and
\Delta_{2}<0
. Let us assume that
\Delta_{K-1}\leq 0
and consider
\Delta_{K}
as follows

(71)

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\left[1-W(K)\right]\prod_{\mu=1}^{K-1}\left[1-W(\mu)\right]-\sum_{\mu=1}^{K-1}W(\mu)-W(K)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{K-1}-W(K)\left\{1-\prod_{\mu=1}^{K-1}\left[1-W(\mu)\right]\right\}.

Now from the
\prod_{\mu=1}^{K-1}\left[1-W(\mu)\right]\leq 1
and
\Delta_{K-1}\leq 0
follows that
\Delta_{K}\leq 0
which, by induction on
K
, completes the proof.
∎

4.
Application

We consider
N
nodes in the blockchain network where a fraction of nodes,
P
, is adversarial. We assume that to improve its scalability, this network is split into
K
shards (or committees). The latter allows the processing of all transactions, encoded in the blockchain, in a distributed way. However, the security of the whole network can be only guaranteed, with
probability

1-\delta
, if
P
is
not
exceeding a certain threshold
A
in
each
committee. The probability of failure
\delta
considered in section
3.2
corresponds to
either the case when the number of adversarial nodes is
N\times P
on
average
, where
\delta
is given by the equation (
26

Second
, we test our analytic results against numerical experiments and we find, up to statistical variability and numerical accuracy, a good agreement as can be seen in Figures
3
and
4
. We also observe that the probability of failure (
31
) is bounded above by the probability (
26

and in the former, this number is fixed. However the difference between the probabilities (
26
) and (

Finally, we note that insights drawn from the above results can be used to design an algorithm which for a given number of nodes
N
finds the
maximum
the number of committees
K
such that the probability of failure is less than (or equal) to a given
\delta
. The latter will reduce the computational complexity in the sharded blockchain, which is proportional to the size of a committee
n
,
without compromising its safety
(
Hafid et al. 2020b
)
. The possibly simplest way to implement such an algorithm is to use the probability of failure (
26
) and
N=nK+r
nodes with
n
and
n+1
nodes assigned, respectively, to the
K-r
and
r
committees. The latter ensures that for any
N
the optimization problem is essentially one-dimensional, where this dimension is
K
, and the former allows the computation of a “current” probability of failure, which is an elementary step of this optimization, to be efficient. We note that here we assume that there are
N\times P
adversarial nodes on average and using this algorithm when there are exactly
N\times P
of adversarial nodes would give us a larger committee size. However, the difference in committee sizes between the latter and the former becomes negligible as
N
is increasing as can be seen in Figure

. The description and pseudocode of one possible variant for such an algorithm are provided in Appendix

5.
Conclusion and Future work

In this work, we have studied random partitions of networks. We introduced a very general probabilistic framework and used this framework to construct probability distributions of random partitions. We showed that these distributions admit exact and explicit expressions which facilitated a study of the probability of failure. For the latter, we obtained exact expressions, bounds, and asymptotic results. For the sharding of blockchains, these results offer significant analytic and algorithmic improvements, but this work still leaves many questions open.

In particular, we have established that the probability of failure (
26
) is an upper bound on the probability (
31
) but this was done numerically and we envisage that to show this in a more rigorous way is also possible. The other question is the relation between the asymptotic result (
33
) for the probability of failure (
31
) and the upper bound (
67
). Here, it is surprising that this bound is very accurate when the number of committees
K
is small but for larger
K
it also approaches the asymptotic result which has quite a different functional form. We note that the bound (
67
) uses univariate hypergeometric distribution which can be difficult to compute numerically and hence for practical applications a simpler bound, but much tighter than (
68
), is more desirable.

Appendix A
Description of the Algorithm

Here we describe the algorithm which given the number of nodes
N
, assumed fraction of adversarial nodes
P
, a fraction of a committee
A
and the probability of failure
\delta
computes the maximum number of committees
K
and corresponding committee sizes. The algorithm computes the RHS of (
26
) for the

N=nK+r
nodes where the
n
and
n+1
nodes are assigned, respectively, to the
K-r
and
r
committees. Initially, all
N
nodes are in one committee, and in subsequent iterations, the number of committees
K
is increased by one until the probability (
26
) is equal to or less than
\delta
. The pseudocode for the latter is provided below.

input :

N
,
\delta
,
A
,
P
;

output :

K
,
n
,
r
,
\mathrm{Prob}
;

K\leftarrow 1
;

n\leftarrow N
;

r\leftarrow 0
;

\mathrm{Prob}\leftarrow 0
;

repeat

/*
Save
K
,
n
,
r
and
\mathrm{Prob}
. */

K_{-1}\leftarrow K
;

n_{-1}\leftarrow n
;

r_{-1}\leftarrow r
;

Prob_{-1}\leftarrow Prob
;

/*
Compute next
K
. */

K\leftarrow K+1
;

/*
Compute remainder, r, and quotient, n when N is divided by K. */

r\leftarrow rem(N,K)
;

n\leftarrow quot(N,K)
;

if

r>0

then

/*
Compute CDF of the Binomial(n,P) and Binomial(n+1,P). */

\mathrm{Prob}_{0}\leftarrow\mathrm{P}(X\leq\lfloor An\rfloor\,|n,P)
;

\mathrm{Prob}_{1}\leftarrow\mathrm{P}(X\leq\lfloor A(n+1)\rfloor\,|n+1,P)
;

/*
Compute the probability of failure. */

Prob\leftarrow 1-\mathrm{Prob}_{0}^{K-r}\mathrm{Prob}_{1}^{r}

else

/*
Compute CDF of the Binomial(n,P). */

\mathrm{Prob}_{0}\leftarrow\mathrm{P}(X\leq\lfloor An\rfloor\,|n,P)
;

/*
Compute the probability of failure. */

Prob\leftarrow 1-\mathrm{Prob}_{0}^{K}

end if

until

\mathrm{Prob}>\delta
;

K\leftarrow K_{-1}
;

n\leftarrow n_{-1}
;

r\leftarrow r_{-1}
;

Prob\leftarrow\mathrm{Prob}_{-1}
;
