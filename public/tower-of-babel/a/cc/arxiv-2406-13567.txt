Galerkin Neural Network-POD for Acoustic and Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Parametric Domains
Philipp Weder †, Mariella Kast †, Fernando Henríquez †, Jan S. Hesthaven †

Abstract
. We investigate reduced-order models for acoustic and electromagnetic wave problems in parametrically defined domains. The parameter-to-solution maps are approximated following the so-called Galerkin POD-NN method, which combines the construction of a reduced basis via proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) with neural networks (NNs). As opposed to the standard reduced basis method, this approach allows for the swift and efficient evaluation of reduced-order solutions for any given parametric input. As is customary in the analysis of problems in random or parametrically defined domains, we start by transporting the formulation to a reference domain. This yields a parameter-dependent variational problem set on parameter-independent functional spaces. In particular, we consider affine-parametric domain transformations characterized by a high-dimensional, possibly countably infinite, parametric input. To keep the number of evaluations of the high-fidelity solutions manageable, we propose using low-discrepancy sequences to sample the parameter space efficiently. Then, we train an NN to learn the coefficients in the reduced representation. This approach completely decouples the offline and online stages of the reduced basis paradigm. Numerical results for the three-dimensional Helmholtz and Maxwell equations confirm the method’s accuracy up to a certain barrier and show significant gains in online speed-up compared to the traditional Galerkin POD method.



1.
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are a ubiquitous approach to physical modeling in engineering and the applied sciences. However, knowledge of the underlying parameters, such as boundary conditions, source terms, or geometry, is often incomplete. Consequentially, parameters are often varied during the search for a specific configuration of the system, e.g., in parameter estimation
[
55
]
, topology optimization
[
6
]
, optimal control
[
54
]
, or uncertainty quantification
[
51
]
.
As these methods require repeated evaluations of the parameterized PDE problem, one speaks of
many-query problems
.

The standard discretization techniques, such as the finite element, finite difference, or finite volume methods, allow precise approximations of the solutions to parameterized PDEs (pPDEs). Yet, such high-fidelity (HF) approximations are intrinsically linked to a prohibitive computational complexity for many-query or real-time applications.
Reduced-order modeling
(ROM) aims at constructing fast surrogate models to accelerate the computation of the approximate solution to a given pPDE problem while retaining an accuracy comparable to that of HF techniques. One common approach to ROM are
reduced basis
(RB) methods. They are based on an
offline-online paradigm
, where first, a reduced basis is constructed from a number of expensive high-fidelity snapshots (offline step). Then, a fast surrogate model is evaluated in the reduced basis (online step).
For a comprehensive review of the RB method for stationary problems with certified error control, we refer to
[
34
,
48
,
47
,
49
]
.

In this work, we focus on the construction of surrogate models from a reduced basis obtained by
the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD). For the Galerkin POD method, the full-order system is projected onto the reduced space, which leads to a reduced system of equations for the RB coefficients. Although effective, this approach does not yield the desired computational speed-up in the general case, as the full-order solution still needs to be assembled in the online stage to evaluate the PDE operators.
Various intrusive techniques aim at alleviating this issue, for example, by exploiting affine
parameter-dependencies
[
34
, Chapter 3.3]
or using hyper-reduction
[
34
, Chapter 5]
to approximate reduced order terms directly.

In contrast, non-intrusive RB methods only rely on HF snapshots and typically construct a surrogate of the map from parameters to RB coefficients. To obtain the response surface, a data-driven regression problem is formulated on the set of RB coefficients of HF snapshots at evaluated parameter location. Then, a query of the surrogate model allows for the solution evaluation of a new, unseen parameter setting. The response surface has to be able to capture the non-linear structure of the underlying parameter-to-solution map of the PDE problem, which makes neural networks
[
33
]
or Gaussian processes
[
23
]
suitable choices. In this work, we explore the construction of projection-based reduced-order models
for acoustic and electromagnetic scattering, which are modeled by the
Helmholtz and time-harmonic Maxwell equations, respectively. For both cases, we consider computational domains with parametrically defined geometries.
Such models are of interest in a variety of applications, e.g. metallic meta-materials
[
4
]
,
and the design of thermovoltaic cells
[
11
]
.

In particular, we are interested in studying affine-parametric
domain deformations with a high-dimensional, possibly countable infinite, parametric input, which leads to a computationally challenging, high-dimensional parameter-to-solution map.
Numerous techniques exist which are tailored to treat efficiently
problems with high-dimensional inputs, such as sparse grid interpolation and quadrature
[
57
,
43
,
24
]
,
higher-order Quasi-Monte Carlo integration (HoQMC)
[
16
,
17
]
, construction of NN
surrogates
[
50
,
32
,
44
,
31
,
1
]
, and model order reduction
[
9
]
.
As pointed out in
[
12
]
, a key property to break the so-called
curse of dimensionality

in the parameter space is the holomorphic dependence of the parameter-to-solution map.
This property has been established for a variety of problems, including for example
subsurface flows
[
8
,
26
,
27
]
, time-harmonic electromagnetic wave
scattering
[
37
,
2
]
, stationary Stokes
and Navier-Stokes equation
[
14
]
, Helmholtz equation

[
35
,
53
,
22
]
, and for boundary
integral operators
[
30
,
28
,
46
,
18
,
15
,
29
]
.

Contributions

We theoretically and computationally study the performance of the POD-NN
applied to the Helmholtz interior impedance problem and the Maxwell
lossy cavity with affine-parametric shape deformations previously used in
[
2
]
.
The parametric holomorphy property, which is crucial for the effectiveness of dimensionality reduction, has previously been established for the Maxwell lossy cavity problem.
For the sake of completeness, we provide the corresponding proof for the Helmholtz impedance problem.

To approximate the complex-valued reduced order coefficients, we modify the POD-NN approach in
[
33
]
and propose
an NN architecture, which models the real and imaginary parts of each reduced coefficient as separate outputs. We show that this does not affect the holomorphy property, indicating that this modified map can also be emulated by NNs. To further facilitate the learning task, we propose a centered POD approach. To justify the effectiveness of the POD-NN approach, we further provide a complete convergence analysis for the reduced basis method for the two model problems.

Finally, we present a comprehensive set of numerical experiments in which
we study the effect of parametric domain transformations with different decay structures, wave numbers of the problems, and hyperparameters of the neural network.

2.
Parametric PDEs: Helmholtz and Maxwell Formulations

In this section, we first state the formulation of pPDEs in a generic fashion, which serves
as a common ground to explain the reduced
basis method applied to both PDE model problems.
Then, we discuss the weak formulations, well-posedness, and discretizations
in more detail for the
Helmholtz impedance and Maxwell lossy cavity problems.

Following previous works on the subject, we introduce the
Galerkin POD for pPDEs, where we put particular emphasis on parametrically defined domains.

2.1.
Parametric PDEs

Throughout, let
\text{U}\coloneqq[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
be the parameter space.
Let
V
be a
complex
Hilbert space
endowed with the scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
and
the induced norm
\norm{\cdot}_{V}=\sqrt{(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}}
.
We denote by
V^{\prime}
the anti-dual space of
V
, i.e.
the set of all anti-linear functionals acting on
V
.
Then, the differential (strong) form of a pPDE can be expressed as follows:
For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
we seek
u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in V
such that

(2.1)

\mathsf{G}\left(u({\boldsymbol{{y}}});{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=0,\quad\text{in }V^{\prime},

where, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
,
\mathsf{G}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):V\to V^{\prime}
is an operator
representing the action of the underlying PDE.
We express (
2.1
) in a variational form.
To this end, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we define the parameter
dependent sesquilinear form
\mathsf{g}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
,
as

(2.2)

\mathsf{g}(u,v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\langle\mathsf{G}\left(u;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right),v\rangle_{V^{\prime}\times V},\quad\forall u,v\in V,

being
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V^{\prime}\times V}
the duality pairing.
Then, the variational formulation of (
2.1
) reads:
Given
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, find
u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in V
such that

(2.3)

\mathsf{g}(u({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})=0,\quad\forall v\in V.

In Sections
2.4
and
2.5
, we consider the particular instance of a linear operator
\mathsf{G}
.

2.2.
The Discrete Full-order Model

Let
V_{h}\subset V
be a finite dimensional subspace of
V

of dimension
N_{h}
with underlying discretization parameter
h>0
.

The Galerkin approximation of the variational problem stated
in (
2.3
) reads as follows:
For a given
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, find
u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in V_{h}
such that

(2.4)

\mathsf{g}(u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),v_{h};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Let
\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N_{h}}\}
be a basis of
V_{h}
.
Each
v_{h}\in V_{h}
admits the following unique
representation

(2.5)

\displaystyle v_{h}=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{h}}c_{m}\left(v_{h}\right)\varphi_{m},

where
c_{m}:V_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
are linear functionals representing the degrees of freedom
of the FE space
V_{h}
.
Therefore, each
v_{h}\in V_{h}
can be uniquely represented by
the sequence
\left\{c_{m}(v_{h})\right\}_{m=1}^{N_{h}}
according to

(2.6)

\displaystyle V_{h}\ni v_{h}\Longleftrightarrow{\bf v}_{h}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\left(v_{h}\right),&\dots,&c_{N_{h}}\left(v_{h}\right)\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}},

Problem
2.4
can be cast in the
following algebraic form: For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
,
find
\mathbf{u}_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}}
such that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}});{\boldsymbol{{y}}})=\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}},

where the residual vector
\mathbf{G}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is defined as

(2.8)

\left(\mathbf{G}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)_{i}\coloneqq\mathsf{g}(u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\varphi_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,N_{h}.

The solution vector
\mathbf{u}_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is obtained by
solving a system of linear equations of size
N_{h}\times N_{h}

for linear problems, while in the non-linear case,
iterative methods must be employed.

2.3.
Parametric Domain Transformations

As previously pointed out, we focus on problem where the parametric input
define shape deformations.
We consider the following family of parametric
domain transformations with respect to a
bounded, Lipschitz domain
\text{D}_{0}
referred to as the
reference domain: For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we define

\boldsymbol{T}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):\text{D}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

(2.9)

\boldsymbol{T}(\widehat{\bf x};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{T}_{0}(\widehat{\bf x})+\sum_{j\geq 1}y_{j}\boldsymbol{T}_{j}(\widehat{\bf x}),\quad\widehat{\bf x}\in\text{D}_{0},\quad{\boldsymbol{{y}}}=\{y_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}\in\text{U},

where
\boldsymbol{T}_{j}:\text{D}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
In principle, we allow for possible countable infinite parametric inputs. However,
in the practical computational implementation we consider a truncation in the parametric dimension.

In the following, we work under the assumptions stated below.

Assumption 2.1
.

For
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
set
b_{j}=\norm{\boldsymbol{T}_j}_{W^{1,\infty}\left({\text{D}}_{0};\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)}
.

(i)

For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
the domain transformation

\boldsymbol{T}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is bijective and bi-Lipschitz, and

(2.10)

\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,{\bf x}=\boldsymbol{T}(\widehat{\bf x};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\;\widehat{\bf x}\in\text{D}_{0}\right\}

defines a bounded, Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(ii)

There exists
p\in(0,1)
such that

\boldsymbol{b}=\{b_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
.

As in
[
2
]
, we consider the following
setting for the numerical experiments to be presented ahead
in Section
5
:
\text{D}_{0}\coloneqq(-1,1)^{3}
,
\boldsymbol{T}_{0}(\widehat{\bf x})=\widehat{\bf x}

together with

(2.11)

\boldsymbol{T}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mu_{j}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
\sin(\pi j \hat{x}_1)\end{array}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{x}=(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2},\widehat{x}_{3})^{\top}\in\text{D}_{0},

i.e. we consider a sinusoidal deformation of the cube in the third coordinate.
In addition, we use the following two choices for
\mu_{j}
.

•

Algebraic Decay.
We assume that
\mu_{j}(r,\theta)=\theta j^{-(r+1)}

for
\theta>0
and
r>1
.

•

Matérn-Like Covariance Decay.

We consider the following sequence mimicking
the decay of the eigenvalues of the Matérn-Like Covariance operator, see
[
56
]
(Chapter 4.2.1) for details, i.e. we set

(2.12)

\mu_{j}(a,\nu,\theta)=\theta\frac{a^{\nu}}{{(a+\pi^{2}j^{2})}^{\nu+0.5}}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+0.5)}{\Gamma(\nu)},\quad a=\frac{2\nu}{l^{2}},

where
l
corresponds to the length scale,

\nu
models the roughness of the perturbation
and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function.
In particular, choosing smaller length scales
l
leads to
reduced importance decay along the first parametric dimensions,
which in turns leads to a richer structure of perturbations
compared to the algebraic decay.
This is visualized ahead in the numerical results section.

Remark 2.2
.

Observe that for the first case, i.e. the algebraic decay, one has

\left\{\mu_{j}(r,\theta)\right\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for
\frac{1}{r}<p<1
,
and in the second case
\left\{\mu_{j}(a,\nu,\theta)\right\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})

for any
\frac{1}{2\nu}<p<1
.

2.4.
The Helmholtz Impedance Problem in Parametric Domains

Herein, we consider the Helmholtz problem equipped with impedance boundary
conditions on a parametrically defined, bounded Lipschitz domain

\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
,
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, with boundary
\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\partial\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
.
Here,
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is as in (
2.10
), i.e. for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}

the image through the affine-parametric domain transformation of the reference domain
\text{D}_{0}
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})),\,g\in L^{2}(\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
and
\kappa>0
be given.
For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we consider the problem of finding
u:\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

such that

(2.13)

\displaystyle-\Delta u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})-\kappa^{2}u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})=f\quad\text{in }\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}}}-\imath\kappa\gamma_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})=g\quad\text{on }\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),

where

(2.14)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}}}:H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\Delta)\rightarrow H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}:H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\rightarrow H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))

denote the Neumann and Dirichlet trace operators.
In (
2.14
), we have that

H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\Delta)\coloneqq\left\{u\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})):\,\Delta u\in L^{2}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\right\},

and
\boldsymbol{\nu}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
signifies the outward-pointing normal vector to
\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
.
The interior impedance Helmholtz problem admits the following variational formulation.

Problem 2.3

(Helmholtz Impedance Problem in
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})),\,g\in L^{2}(\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
.
For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
we seek

u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
such that

(2.15)

\mathsf{a}(u({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})=\ell(v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})),

where the parameter-dependent sesquilinear form

\mathsf{a}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\times H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is defined for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
as

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mathsf{a}\left(u,v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)\coloneqq\int\limits_{\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\overline{v}-\kappa^{2}u\overline{v}\right)\text{d}{\bf x}-\imath\kappa\int\limits_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\gamma_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}u\gamma_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\overline{v}\,\text{ds},\quad\forall u,v\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))

and the parameter-dependent anti-linear form

\ell(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is defined for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
as

(2.17)

\displaystyle\ell(v;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\int\limits_{\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}f\overline{v}\,\text{d}{\bf x}+\int\limits_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}g\gamma_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\overline{v}\,\text{ds}_{\bf x},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})).

By invoking the Banach-Nečas-Babuška theorem, Gårding’s inequality, and the injectivity
of the sesquilinear form
\mathsf{a}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\times H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
one may establish well-posedness of Problem
2.3

(cf.
[
21
, Theorem 35.5]
), pointwise for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
.

2.4.1.
Helmholtz problem in the reference domain

To recast the integrals in (
2.16
)
and (
2.17
) in terms of
\text{D}_{0}
,
we recall the following formulas for domain and boundary transformations,
which can be found in
[
5
]
.
Denote by
d\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
the Jacobian matrix of the transformation

\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
and by
J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
its Jacobian determinant.
For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
we define
\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\rightarrow H^{1}(\text{D}_{0})

as
\left(\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})v\right)(\widehat{\bf x})=v(\boldsymbol{T}(\widehat{\bf x};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
,
\widehat{\bf x}\in\text{D}_{0}
,
which is usually referred to as the
plain pullback
operator.
One has that for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
,
\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is a bounded linear operator with a bounded inverse,
see e.g.
[
14
, Section 3]
and
[
26
, Lemma 1]
.

Furthermore, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
we define the surface Jacobian on
the boundary
\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
as

(2.18)

J_{\text{S}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})=J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\norm{
d\boldsymbol T^{-\top}(\y)
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\y)
}\in L^{\infty}(\text{D}_{0})

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}}){\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{0};\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
For
\widehat{v}\in H^{1}(\text{D}_{0})
we set

v\coloneqq\Phi^{-1}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\widehat{v}
.
Then, we have

(2.19)

\nabla v=d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}\widehat{\nabla}\widehat{v}\circ\boldsymbol{T}^{-1},\quad\int\limits_{\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}v\,\text{d}{\bf x}=\int\limits_{\text{D}_{0}}\widehat{v}J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}\widehat{\bf x},\quad\text{and}\quad\int\limits_{\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}v\text{ds}=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{0}}\widehat{v}J_{\text{S}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}\widehat{\text{s}}_{\widehat{\bf x}},

where the latter identity is also called
Nanson’s formula
,
and
\widehat{\nabla}
denotes the grandient operator in the reference domain.
This allows us to state the variational formulation
for the Helmholtz equation in the reference domain.

Problem 2.4

(Helmholtz Impedance Problem in the Reference Domain)
.

For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we seek
\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0})
such that

(2.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{a}}\left(\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\widehat{v};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=\widehat{\ell}\left(\widehat{v};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right),\quad\forall\widehat{v}\in H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0}),

where for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
the sesquilinear form

\widehat{\mathsf{a}}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0})\times H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is defined as

(2.21)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{a}}\left(\widehat{v},\widehat{w};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\text{D}}_{0}}\left(d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\widehat{\nabla}\widehat{v}\cdot d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\widehat{\nabla}\overline{\widehat{w}}-\kappa^{2}\widehat{v}\overline{\widehat{w}}\right)J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}\widehat{\bf x}

\displaystyle-\imath\kappa\int\limits_{\Gamma_{0}}\widehat{v}\overline{\widehat{w}}J_{\text{S}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}{\text{s}}_{\widehat{\bf x}},\quad\forall\widehat{v},\widehat{w}\in H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0}),

and the anti-linear form
\widehat{\ell}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is defined as

(2.22)

\displaystyle\widehat{\ell}(\widehat{v};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\int\limits_{{\text{D}}_{0}}\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\overline{\widehat{v}}J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}\widehat{\bf x}+\int\limits_{\Gamma_{0}}\widehat{g}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\gamma_{\Gamma_{0}}\overline{\widehat{v}}J_{\text{S}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\text{d}{\text{s}}_{\widehat{\bf x}},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}({\text{D}}_{0}),

where
\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})f\in L^{2}(\text{D}_{0})
and
\widehat{g}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})g\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{0})
.

One can readily see that the unique solvability of Problem
2.3
together
with the properties of the plain pullback operator straightforwardly entail the well-posedness of
Problem
2.4
, and indeed it holds
\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})=\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
for each

{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, where
u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H^{1}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
and
\widehat{u}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H^{1}(\text{D}_{0})
are the unique solutions
to Problems
2.3
and
2.4
, respetively.

2.4.2.
Discrete Full-Order Model for Helmholtz Impedance Problem

\mathsf{g}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})=\widehat{\mathsf{a}}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})-\widehat{\ell}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})

2.21
) and
\widehat{\ell}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
as in (
2.22
).

Regarding the convergence of the full-order model for the Helmholtz impedance
problem with respect to the discretization of the FE space, we have the following result:
For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
there exists
h_{0}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
(i.e. depending on
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}
) such that for

h<h_{0}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
it holds

(2.23)

\norm{
\widehat{u}(\y)
-
\widehat{u}_h(\y)
}_{H^{1}(\text{D}_{0})}\leq C({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\norm{
\widehat{u}(\y)
-
v_h
}_{H^{1}(\text{D}_{0})},

for some
C({\boldsymbol{{y}}})>0
, where
\widehat{u}_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is solution to the following variational problem

(2.24)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{a}}\left(\widehat{u}_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\widehat{v}_{h};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=\widehat{\ell}\left(\widehat{v}_{h};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right),\quad\forall\widehat{v}_{h}\in V_{h}

As it is customary for variational problems satisfying Garding-type inequalities, a minimal level
of resolution of the FE space is required to obtain quasi-optimality, and, therefore, convergence of the Galerkin method. It is important to point out that the minimal level of resolution
h_{0}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
and

C({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
, which in principle depend of the particular instance of
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, can be made independent of
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
by using a finite covering argument and the compactness
of
U
.

2.5.
The Maxwell Lossy Cavity Problem in Parametric Domains

As a second model problem, we consider a time-harmonic electromagnetic
cavity problem with circular frequency
\omega>0
in a parametrically defined
bounded, Lipschitz domain
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
as in (
2.10
).

For simplicity, we consider a constant complex domain conductivity
\sigma

as well as a constant complex dielectric permittivity
\varepsilon
and magnetic
permeability
\mu
.
In addition, we also introduce the quantity

\Lambda\coloneqq\omega^{2}\varepsilon-\imath\omega\sigma
.
As in
[
2
]
, we make the following assumptions:
There exists
\vartheta\in[0,2\pi)
such that

(2.25)

\mu_{b}\coloneqq\real\left\{e^{\imath\vartheta}\mu^{-1}\right\}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda_{b}\coloneqq\real\left\{-e^{\imath\vartheta}\Lambda\right\}>0.

Lastly, we assume a continuous source current density,
i.e. for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
we assume

\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathscr{C}^{0}\left(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}});\mathbb{C}^{3}\right)
.

In this simplified setting, let
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
be the
complex-valued electric and magnetic fields, respectively. Maxwell equations in
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})

read (see, e.g.,
[
42
]
)

(2.26)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\curl\boldsymbol{E}+\imath\omega\mu\boldsymbol{H}&=0\quad\text{in }\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\\
(\imath\omega\varepsilon+\sigma)\boldsymbol{E}-\curl\boldsymbol{H}&=-\imath\omega\boldsymbol{J}\quad\text{ in }\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}).\end{cases}

By defining the quantity
\kappa^{2}\coloneqq\omega^{2}\mu\varepsilon-\imath\omega\mu\sigma

and applying the curl operator to the first equation, we can reduce the system to

(2.27)

\curl(\curl\boldsymbol E)-\kappa^{2}\boldsymbol{E}=-\imath\omega\mu\boldsymbol{J}\quad\text{in }\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})

On the boundary, we assume perfect electrical conductor (PEC) boundary conditions

(2.28)

\gamma_{d}^{\times}\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),

where
\gamma_{d}^{\times}
denotes the flipped tangential trace, i.e.

\gamma_{d}^{\times}\boldsymbol{E}\coloneqq\gamma_{d}\left(\boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right)
. Thus, only the tangential component electric field on the boundary of the domain vanishes.

We define
H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
and
H_{0}(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))
as

(2.29)

\displaystyle H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{2}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))^{3}\mid\curl\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{2}(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))^{3}\},

(2.30)

\displaystyle H_{0}(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{u}\in H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\mid\gamma^{\times}_{D}\boldsymbol{u}=0\},

and we equip them with the norm

\norm{
\boldsymbol u
}_{H\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)}\coloneqq\norm{
\boldsymbol u
}_{L^{2}\left(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)}+\norm{
\curl\boldsymbol u
}_{L^{2}\left(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)}.

The variational formulation of (
2.27
) on the physical domain
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})

reads as follows.

Problem 2.5

(Maxwell Cavity Problem in
\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
)
.

For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we seek

\boldsymbol{E}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)

such that

(2.31)

\mathsf{a}\left(\boldsymbol{E}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\boldsymbol{V};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=\ell\left(\boldsymbol{V};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right),\quad\forall H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)

with the parameter-dependent sesquilinear form

\mathsf{a}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)\times H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
form

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathsf{a}\left(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{W};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)\coloneqq\int\limits_{\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\left(\mu^{-1}(\curl\boldsymbol{V})\cdot(\curl\overline{\boldsymbol{W}})-\Lambda\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{W}}\right)\text{d}{\bf x},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{W}\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)

and the parameter-dependent anti-linear form

\ell(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

(2.33)

\displaystyle\ell\left(\boldsymbol{V};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)\coloneqq-\imath\omega\int\limits_{\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})}\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}\text{d}{\bf x},\quad\boldsymbol{V}\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathscr{C}^{0}\left(\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}});\mathbb{C}^{3}\right)
.

Due to the assumptions in (
2.25
),
we can apply the Lax-Milgram lemma to show the well-posedness of
Problem
2.5
.
Indeed, for a fixed parameter
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
and any

\boldsymbol{V}\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)
it holds

(2.34)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathsf{a}(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right\rvert\geq\min\{\mu_{b},\Lambda_{b}\}\norm{
\bV}^{2}_{H\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)},

with
\mu_{b},\Lambda_{b}
as in (
2.25
).

Consequently, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
the sesquilinear
\mathsf{a}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
is
coercive according to (
2.34
).
Observe that the coercivity constant does not depend on
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
.
In addition, since both
\mathsf{a}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
and
\ell(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}})
are continuous
the Lax-Milgram lemma applies, thus yielding well-posedness of
Problem
2.5
for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
.

2.5.1.
Maxwell Cavity Problem in the Reference Domain

One noteworthy difference tothe Helmholtz problem is
that we have to define the pullback operator
\Phi:V\to V_{0}
differently.
In fact, to preserve
H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D})
-confomity of the fields, we need to use the following
domain transformation, defined for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
as

(2.35)

\displaystyle\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq d\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})^{-\top}(\boldsymbol{v}\circ\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in V({\boldsymbol{{y}}}).

As discussed in
[
19
, Lemma 2.2]
, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
the pullback operator

\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
in (
2.35
) admits
bounded extension
\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in\mathscr{L}_{\text{iso}}\left(H(\mathrm{curl};\Omega(\boldsymbol{\mu})),H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))\right)
.
In addition, for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in H(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}))

it holds that

(2.36)

\curl\left(\Phi({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\det(d\boldsymbol{T}(\y))d\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})((\curl\boldsymbol{u})\circ\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})).

For ease of notation, let us also define the pullback of the current density

\boldsymbol{J}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{J}\circ\boldsymbol{T}_{y}\in\mathscr{C}^{0}\left(\text{D}_{0};\mathbb{C}^{3}\right)
.
With the previous Lemma at hand, we can now state the nominal variational
problem for the Maxwell cavity problem (cf.
[
2
]
).

Problem 2.6

(Maxwell Cavity Problem in the Reference Domain)
.

For each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
, we seek

\widehat{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}_{0}\right)

such that

(2.37)

\widehat{\mathsf{a}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=\widehat{\ell}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right),\quad\forall\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}_{0}\right),

where the sesquilinear form

\widehat{\mathsf{a}}(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}_{0}\right)\times H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}_{0}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is given by

(2.38)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{a}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\text{D}_{0}}J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})^{-1}\mu^{-1}\left(d\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})(\curl\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\right)\cdot\left(d\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})(\curl\overline{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\right)

\displaystyle-\Lambda J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\overline{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}\text{d}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}

and, for each
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
,
the anti-linear form
\widehat{\ell}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}):H_{0}\left(\mathrm{curl};\text{D}_{0}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is given by

(2.39)

\displaystyle\widehat{\ell}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}};{\boldsymbol{{y}}})\coloneqq-\imath\omega\int\limits_{\text{D}_{0}}J({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\boldsymbol{J}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\cdot d\boldsymbol{T}^{-\top}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\overline{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}\text{d}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}.

-conforming

3.
Projection-based Reduced Order Modeling

The Galerkin approximation of the shape-parametric Helmholtz impedance
and Maxwell lossy cavity problems,
as presented in Sections
2.4
and
2.5
,
for each parametric input entails a high computational cost.
For many-query applications or real-time computations, one needs a fast and accurate methodology to evaluate the parameter-to-solution map for each particular instance of the parametric input.

This motivates the use of model order reduction techniques such as
the reduced basis method. Instead of seeking a solution
for each parametric input in a suitable finite-dimensional subspace, we solve the problem
in a reduced space of a much smaller dimension than that of the full-order model.

3.1.
The Reduced Basis Method

Assume that we have access to a reduced basis
V^{(\text{rb})}_{L}
of
dimension
L\ll N_{h}
of the form

V^{(\text{rb})}_{L}=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{L}\}\subset V_{h}
.
We discuss one possible way to construct such a basis in Section
3.2
.
In practical applications one considers only finitely many parametric inputs.
To this end, we define
\mathrm{U}^{(J)}\coloneqq[-1,1]^{J}
, where
J\in\mathbb{N}

corresponds to the parametric dimension.
Then, for a given parametric input
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\mathrm{U}^{(J)}
,
we seek solutions
u^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in V^{(\text{rb})}_{L}
of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle u^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\mathbf{u}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\right)_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}\in V^{\text{(rb)}}_{L},

with
\mathbf{u}^{\text{(rb)}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
being the
reduced coefficients
.

In order to compute the reduced basis solution
u^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})

for a particular
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\mathrm{U}^{(J)}
, we follow a standard Galerkin approach,
see e.g.
[
48
]
.
We project the variational problem (
2.3
)
onto the reduced space
V^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}
, thus yielding the following

reduced basis problem
: Given
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\mathrm{U}^{(J)}
, find
u^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in V^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}
such that

(3.2)

\mathsf{g}\left(u^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}),v^{\text{(rb)}}_{L};{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=0,\quad\forall v^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}\in V^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}.

As in Section
2.2
,
each
\psi_{\ell}\in V^{(\text{rb})}_{L}
can be uniquely represented by
the sequence
\{c_{m}(\psi_{\ell})\}_{m=1}^{N_{h}}
, which we gather in
the vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\ell}
.
Next, we define the
reduced basis matrix
as

(3.3)

\mathbb{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}\coloneqq\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\psi}_{L}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}\times L}.

This matrix encodes the change of basis from the
reduced basis to the FE basis.
Due to (
2.4
), the reduced basis problem
(
3.2
) can be expressed as follows:
Given
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\mathrm{U}^{(J)}
, we seek
\mathbf{u}^{\text{(rb)}}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
such that

(3.4)

\mathbb{V}^{\text{(rb)}\dagger}_{L}\mathbf{G}_{h}\left(\mathbb{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}\mathbf{u}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}({\boldsymbol{{y}}});{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\right)=\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{L},

where
\dagger
denotes the Hermitian conjugate of
\mathbb{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}
.
We will henceforth refer to (
3.4
) as
the Galerkin-POD problem or simply G-POD.

Note that G-POD problem, although a system (either linear or non-linear) of
L
equations, still
requires the assembly of the full-order model as described in
[
34
,
48
]
.
In special cases, e.g.
whenever the dependence of the underlying form
\mathsf{g}
on the parametric input
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\text{U}
is affine,
it is possible that problem (
3.4
) turns out to be independent of
N_{h}

[
48
]
. However, the Helmholtz problem as well as the time-harmonic Maxwell problem presented above do not satisfy this assumption.

3.2.
Reduced Basis Construction: Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

A well-known and straightforward approach to construct a reduced basis is the

proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD) method.
Suppose we have a collection of
N_{s}
evaluations of the full-order model
on a finite parameter set
\Xi_{N_{s}}=\{{\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(N_{s})}\}\subset\mathrm{U}^{(J)}
,
the so-called snapshots
\{u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(1)}),\ldots,u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(N_{s})})\}
.

For what follows, we assume that a sufficiently large number of snapshots
N_{s}

have been computed so that the associated subspace

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Xi}\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\left\{u_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,u_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(N_{s})}\right)\right\}\subset V_{h}

is a good approximation of the continuous
solution manifold

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{h}\coloneqq\{u_{h}({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\mid{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\in\mathrm{U}^{(J)}\}.

Thus, we search for a parameter-independent reduced basis
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{L}\}
for
\mathcal{M}_{\Xi}
, such that
L\ll N_{h}
and such that the reduced basis well approximates
\mathcal{M}_{\Xi}\subset\mathcal{M}_{h}
.

Let
\mathbb{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}\times N_{s}}
denote the
snapshot matrix
,
defined as

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\left(\mathbf{u}_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(N_{s})}\right)\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}\times N_{s}}.

Let
R
be the rank of
\mathbb{S}
.
Then the singular value decomposition yields two unitary matrices

(3.8)

\mathbb{W}=\left(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{N_{s}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}\times N_{h}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{Z}=\left(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{N_{s}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}

and a diagonal matrix

\mathbb{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{R})
, with
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{R}>0
,

R
being the rank of
\mathbb{S}
, such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{W}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{D}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{Z}^{\dagger}.

Algebraically speaking, we want to approximate the columns in
\mathbb{S}
using
L\leq R
orthonormal vectors
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{L}\}
.
The orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{u}_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(n)}\right)
onto
\mathrm{span}\{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{L}\}
is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\mathbf{u}_{h}\left({\boldsymbol{{y}}}^{(n)}\right),\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{l}\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}}}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{l}.

We seek an orthonormal basis
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{L}\}

such that the quantity

(3.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{E}}^{(N_{s})}\left(\mathbb{V}_{L}\right)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N_{s}}\norm{
\mathbf{u}_h\left(\y^{(n)}\right)
-
\sum_{\ell= 1}^L
\left(\mathbf{u}_h\left(\y^{(n)}\right), \widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_\ell\right)_{\C^{N_h}}
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_\ell}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}}},

is minimized. The Schmidt-Eckart-Young theorem
[
48
]

asserts that the minimum is achieved for the basis
\{\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{L}\}

consisting of the first
L
columns of
\mathbb{W}
in the SVD of
\mathbb{S}

in (
3.9
).
Hence, we set
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{l}=\mathbf{w}_{l}
for
l=1,\ldots,L
and thus

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{L}=\left(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{L}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{h}\times L}.

3.3.
Parametric Holomorphy

In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
we have discussed
computational aspects of the reduced basis method for parametric problems.
The reduced basis method, and for that matter any other model
order reduction technique,
can successfully approximate the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{h}

defined in (
3.6
), and its discrete counterpart
\mathcal{M}_{\Xi}
,
provided that there exists an intrinsic low-dimensional dynamics driving the behavior
of solution manifold.

A commonly used concept in nonlinear approximation to
quantify uniform error bounds is the so-called
Kolmogorov’s width.
For a compact subset
\mathcal{K}
of a Banach space

X
it is defined for
L\in\mathbb{N}
as

(3.12)

\displaystyle d_{L}(\mathcal{K},X)\coloneqq\inf_{\operatorname{dim}\left(X_{L}\right)\leq L}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{K}}\min_{w\in X_{L}}\norm{v-w}_{X},

where the outer infimum is taken over all
finite dimensional spaces
X_{L}\subset X
of
dimension smaller than
L
. This quantifies
the suitability of
L
-dimensional subspaces
for the approximation of the solution manifold.
As it has been established, see e.g.
[
34
,
48
]

the convergence analysis of the reduced basis method relies on the existence
of bounds controlling the decay of the Kolmogorov’s width.

A key insight to establish dimension-independent convergence
of Kolmogorov’s width for parametric maps with high-dimensional parametric
inputs corresponds to the analytic or holomorphic dependence of the parameter-to-operator
map upon the parametric variables.

For
s>1
we define
the Bernstein ellipse

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{s}\coloneqq\left\{\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}:\;1\leq\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq s\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

This ellipse has foci at
z=\pm 1
and semi-axes of length

a\coloneqq(s+s^{-1})/2
and
b\coloneqq(s-s^{-1})/2
.
In addition, we define the tensorized poly-ellipse

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j\geq 1}\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{j}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}},

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}\coloneqq\{\rho_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}

is such that
\rho_{j}>1
, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
12
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space equipped with the norm
\norm{\cdot}_{X}
.
For
\varepsilon>0
and
p\in(0,1)
, we say that map

\mathrm{U}\ni{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\mapsto u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in X

is
(\boldsymbol{b},p,\varepsilon)
-holomorphic if and only if:

(i)

The map
\mathrm{U}\ni{{\boldsymbol{{y}}}}\mapsto u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})\in X
is uniformly bounded.

(ii)

There exists a positive sequence
\boldsymbol{b}\coloneqq\{b_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})

and a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any sequence

\boldsymbol{\rho}\coloneqq\{\rho_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}

of numbers strictly larger than one that is

(\boldsymbol{b},\varepsilon)
-admissible, i.e. satisfying

\sum_{j\geq 1}(\rho_{j}-1)b_{j}\leq\varepsilon
,
the map
{\boldsymbol{{y}}}\mapsto u({\boldsymbol{{y}}})
admits a complex
extension
{\boldsymbol{z}}\mapsto u({\boldsymbol{z}})

that is holomorphic with respect to each
variable
z_{j}
on a set of the form

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\coloneqq\displaystyle{\bigotimes_{j\geq 1}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\rho_{j}},

where
\mathcal{O}_{\rho_{j}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\operatorname{dist}(z,[-1,1])<\rho_{j}-1\}
.

(iii)

This extension is bounded on
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

5.1.
Numerical implementation

We use the programming language Julia to conduct our numerical experiments. The high-fidelity FE methods for solving the Helmholtz impedance and Maxwell lossy cavity problem are implemented in
Gridap.jl

[

using Lagrange and Nédélec elements, respectively. After assembly, the linear system is solved by Julia’s native linear solver.
For the boundary variations, the parameter space of the affine transformations is sampled using a Halton or Latin Hypercube sequence generated by the library
QuasiMonteCarlo.jl
. To compute the solution of the Galkerin-POD method, the linear operators are first assembled in
Gridap.jl
, then projected onto the reduced basis, and solved. The neural network architectures and training implementation are based on the library

Performance evaluation

6.
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we present a Galerkin POD-NN method for surrogate modeling
of three-dimensional acoustic and electromagnetic wave problems with
parametric-affine shape deformations. Using readily available results for the Maxwell
cavity problem and our analysis for the Helmholtz impedance problem, we demonstrate the analytic or holomorphic dependency of the
problem’s solution upon such parametric shape deformations.
Based on this analysis, we argue that computational models for this class of problems are amenable to complexity
reduction using a projection-based reduced basis scheme irrespective of the dimensionality
of the parametric domain.
Following the same argument, the
map from the parameters to the coefficients of the RB basis representation is also amenable to approximation, done here by
using NNs. Unlike many commonly computational models for which the Galerkin POD-NN has been applied,
the involved quantities are complex-valued. We propose a formulation in which we separate the reduced coefficients
into their real and imaginary parts. In the training stage, we consider each as a separate trainable, real-valued output, thus allowing us to retain an NN with real-valued features.

Our numerical experiments indicate that the mean field may be a predictor
of reasonable accuracy in some cases, whereas the POD-NN method can
improve this by an order of magnitude or more.
The success of the surrogate model also critically depends on the
hyper-parameter choices of the original problem and interestingly seems
to work well on the Matérn-type decay.
We have further observed that for a given data set, there seems to be an
optimal number of basis functions in terms of efficiency, as no more gains
in accuracy can be achieved by increasing the basis size.
While our theoretical investigations prove convergence rates of the reduced order approximation independent of the parameter dimension, the cost of accurately creating such a reduced order approximation still scales with the parametric dimension, e.g., via the low-discrepancy series or the number of training points that are required for the NN approximation. In principle, this may be addressed by using tailored, high-dimensional quadrature rules as in

[
39
]
.

In the current work, the considerable computational cost of obtaining high fidelity training data for complex 3D problems, limited the size of our training data set, which also shows in the limited success for the most complex problems.
In future work, it would be interesting to investigate whether adding more training data through a larger snapshot set or via an active learning scheme can overcome the error barriers that we observe.
Similarly, including physical knowledge into the NN model, for instance, by adding the residual of the underlying PDE to the loss function, could improve performance when the training data set is of limited size. In addition, the method’s performance could be improved by considering a multi-fidelity setup, where cheaply available lower-fidelity training data with e.g., reduced resolution, provides better coverage of the solution manifold.
