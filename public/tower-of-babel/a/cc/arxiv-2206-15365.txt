Abstract
I develop simple and intuitive bounds for the false discovery rate (FDR) in cross-sectional return predictability publications. The simplest bounds require only summary statistics from previous papers, and show the FDR is at most 25% in eight out of nine previous studies. A more refined bound shows the FDR is at most 9%. These studies include Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016), who “argue that most claimed findings in financial economics are likely false.” I demonstrate how Harvey et al.’s own estimates imply an FDR of 9%, and that their conclusion stems from misinterpreting “insignificant factor” as “false discovery.”

Introduction

Researchers have discovered hundreds of cross-sectional stock return predictors (
Chen & Zimmermann 2022
). This abundance has led to concerns about multiple testing. Intuitively, if researchers run many tests, some will be statistically significant by pure chance.

The false discovery rate (FDR) quantifies this problem. The FDR originates from
Sorić 1989
, who defines a “discovery” as a statistically significant result, and a “false discovery” as a discovery that is (unfortunately) null. Building on this language,
Benjamini & Hochberg 1995
define the false discovery
rate
as the expected share of discoveries that are false. Discoveries that are not false are therefore true.

This paper provides simple and intuitive bounds on the FDR. My “Easy Bound” is derived in just a few lines of math and can be estimated by plugging in summary statistics from previous studies. This bound shows that the FDR is at most 25% based on eight out of the nine studies I examine. My “Visual Bound” is tighter, and is calculated by drawing a picture. The Visual Bound implies that the FDR is at most 9%—in other words, at least 91% of discoveries are true.

These bounds help reconcile the disparate estimates in the literature.
Harvey et al. 2016
and
Chordia et al. 2020
find an FDR exceeding 45%, a result echoed by
Harvey & Liu 2020
. In contrast,
Chen & Zimmermann 2018
and
Jensen et al. 2023
find an FDR close to zero, and
Chen 2024
finds a similarly small FDR. My bounds show that these different conclusions are not due to differences in the underlying data. Data in all of these studies imply an FDR of at most 25%.

Instead, the debate is largely due to differences in interpretation. I explain how
Harvey et al. 2016
’s
Harvey et al. 2016
own methods imply an FDR of just 9%. But the paper misinterprets “insignificant factors” as “false discoveries,” and also conflates “many” with “most,” leading to the claim that most findings are false. Similar misinterpretations occur in
Harvey & Liu 2020
.
Chordia et al. 2020
does not suffer from these misinterpretations, but their high FDR estimates likely stem from the omission of key moments from their calibration.

Replication code is available at
https://github.com/chenandrewy/mostly-true
.

An Easy Bound on the FDR

Signal
i
(a stock-level characteristic) has long-short sample mean return
\bar{r}_{i}
with standard error
\text{SE}_{i}
and t-stat
t_{i}\equiv\bar{r}_{i}/\text{SE}_{i}
. A signal with
E(\bar{r}_{i})=0
is “null,” denoted by
\text{null}_{i}
. The samples are long, so the central limit theorem holds:

\displaystyle t_{i}\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\sim\text{Normal}(0,1).

(1)

Equation (
1
) can also be justified using a bootstrap (
Chen 2021
). These assumptions are standard in cross-sectional asset pricing.

In a sense, FDR methods just add colorful terminology. Following
Sorić 1989
, a “discovery” is a significant result. For the bulk of the paper, I use the classical definition of significance:

\displaystyle i\text{ is a discovery if }|t_{i}|>2.

(2)

A “false discovery” is a significant signal that is (unfortunately) null. Significant signals are also called “predictors” or “findings,” so I also refer to false discoveries as “false predictors” or “false findings.”

With this terminology, a natural definition of the false discovery
rate
is

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

\displaystyle\equiv\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt|t_{i}|>2\right),

(3)

where
i
is randomly selected from some set. In other words,
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
is the probability a significant signal is null. Equation (
3
) is equivalent to the
Benjamini & Hochberg 1995
definition under weak dependence (e.g.
Chen 2024
).

Applying Bayes rule and noting
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})\leq 1.0
leads to an “Easy Bound” on the FDR:

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)}

(4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)}

(5)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{5\%}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)},

(6)

where the last line plugs in Equation (
1
). The Easy Bound is just 5% divided by the probability a signal is significant.

It’s natural to estimate this probability using method of moments: replace
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)
with the share of signals with
|t_{i}|>2
. The resulting bound is intuitive: if the tail under the null (numerator) cannot explain the actual tail (denominator), then the FDR must be small.

However, estimating
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)
runs into the problem of publication bias. Since signals with
|t_{i}|>2
are more likely to be published, the published share of
|t_{i}|>2
overstates
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|>2\right)
. I address this problem by considering worst-case scenarios.

Bounding the FDR with Naive Data Mining

Suppose that data mining a naively-selected set of accounting ratios leads to
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq X
. It follows logically that asset pricing research also produces
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq X
. This is because researchers can use expert judgment, and (weakly) improve on naive data mining. For research to produce
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}>X
would require that experts systematically
under
perform relative to naive data mining, which is implausible.

Critically, the argument is
not
about finding a superset of the published predictors. It does not matter if the set of ratios nests the published predictors, or if they do not. Either way, the argument in the previous paragraph goes through. All that is required is that the ratios are naively selected, and that one believes experts do not systematically underperform naive data mining.

I focus on accounting ratios for three reasons:

1.

It is a priori reasonable to believe accounting ratios are informative about stock prices, and thus could produce an informative bound on the FDR from research. As an extreme counterexample, consider forming portfolios based on a random number generator instead. While this would also bound the FDR from research, the bound would likely be loose.

2.

Accounting numbers are the modal data source for cross-sectional predictors, comprising 43% of the predictors in
Chen & Zimmermann 2022
. Past return predictors are a distant second at 24%. Thus, having a sense of the FDR from naively mining accounting data is an important benchmark.

3.

Several papers study the predictive power of naively-selected accounting ratios, namely
Yan & Zheng 2017
(YZ);
Chordia et al. 2020
(CGS); and
Chen et al. 2025
(CLZ).

As seen in Table
1
, Panel (a), exhibits from YZ, CGS, and CLZ show that at least 20% of naively-selected accounting ratios lead to
|t_{i}|>2
. Plugging 20% into Equation (
6
) implies

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5\%}{0.20}=25\%

(7)

for naively-selected accounting ratios. Then, assuming that the
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
produced by asset pricing experts is not worse than the
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
from naive data mining,
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq 25\%
for the cross-sectional literature. In other words, at least 75% of claimed findings are true.

Studies that focus on naively-selected past return signals are less common. But the existing evidence also implies a small
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

Bounding the FDR with Conservative Extrapolation

A second worst-case scenario comes from conservatively extrapolating the t-stat density into the region affected by publication bias. While extrapolating point estimates is dangerous, we only need to extrapolate an upper bound. That is, we don’t need the true density for
|t_{i}|<2
—we only need a density that exceeds the true density. As long as this condition is satisfied, the
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
implied by the extrapolation bounds the
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
from the research process.

I extrapolate using an exponential distribution for three reasons:

1.

It fits the observed data well. This is seen in Figure
1
, which compares an exponential density with the distribution of t-stats from
Chen & Zimmermann 2022
’s
Chen & Zimmermann 2022
replications of 200 published predictors.

2.

It is conservative. It assumes, in a sense, “exponentially more” file drawer t-stats compared to published t-stats. Conservatism is also seen in how the shape is similar to the data-mined t-stat distribution (dashed line, Figure
1
).

3.

It can be calculated by hand, using summary statistics reported in previous meta-studies.

An exponential extrapolation is also used in Appendix A.1 of
Harvey et al. 2016
.

Figure 1:

To understand the third point, note that the memoryless property of the exponential distribution implies

\displaystyle E\left(|t_{i}|\right)

\displaystyle=E\left(|t_{i}|\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt|t_{i}|>2\right)-2,

(8)

that is,
E(|t_{i}|)
can be estimated by subtracting 2 from the mean
|t_{i}|
conditional on
|t_{i}|
exceeding 2. Since publication generally requires
|t_{i}|>2
, the mean published t-stat is not far from this conditional mean.

This reasoning leads to the following bound:

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5\%}{\exp\left(-2/(\hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}-2)\right)},

(9)

where
\hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}
is the mean published t-stat and the denominator comes from plugging an exponential distribution with mean
\hat{\mu}_{\text{pub}}-2
into Equation (
6
).

Panel (b) of Table
1
applies Equation (
9
) to a variety of studies. For example, Chen and Zimmermann’s
Chen & Zimmermann 2020
Table 2 reports that the mean hand-collected t-stat across 77 published predictors is 4.6. Equation (
9
) then implies
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq 5\%/\exp(-2/(4.6-2))=11\%
. Replications and post-publication data tend to result in lower t-stats, leading to higher FDR bounds using
McLean & Pontiff 2016
and
Jacobs & Müller 2020
. Nevertheless, the results across six independent teams are consistent. All imply that most findings are true.

The entry for
Harvey et al. 2016
(HLZ) is notable. It shows that the Easy Bound, applied to HLZ’s dataset, implies that at least 88% of findings are true. This result suggests the difference in conclusions is not due to differences in data. We will see that different methods are not the issue either, and that the debate is driven by misinterpretations of multiple testing methods (Section
4
).

In summary, the FDR is small because the research process readily generates signals with large t-stats, much more readily than compared to a standard normal distribution. This property can be seen in the frequency of large t-stats in naive data mining experiments or in conservative extrapolations of published t-stats.

More Refined Estimates

One can argue that the Easy Bounds are too conservative. Rather
than bound
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)
based on data, they use the theoretical bound
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)\leq 1.0
. But one can also argue that the previous
bounds are too aggressive. They focus on raw equal-weighted returns rather than factor-adjusted value-weighted returns.

This section addresses both issues. Sections
3.1
-
3.2
provide a visual approach to bounding
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)
with data, following

Storey 2002
. Section
3.4
applies
this method to value-weighted returns, adjusted for various factor
exposures. Along the way, Section
3.3
discusses correlations and standard errors.

A Tighter, Visual Bound

In essence, FDR methods decompose the t-stat histogram into a null component and an alternative component. To understand this interpretation, apply the law of total probability to the event that
|t_{i}|
falls in bin
b
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\right)}_{\left[\text{Data}\right]_{b}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)}_{\equiv\left[\text{Null Component}\right]_{b}}+\underbrace{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{alt}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{alt}_{i}\right)}_{\equiv\left[\text{Alternative Component}\right]_{b}}.

(10)

where
\text{alt}_{i}
is the event that
i
is not null. This decomposition provides a suggestive way to re-write
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
:

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{b\hskip 0.60275pt\subseteq[2,\infty)}\left[\text{Null Component}\right]_{b}}{\sum_{b\hskip 0.60275pt\subseteq[2,\infty)}\left[\text{Data}\right]_{b}},

(11)

where I’ve assumed that the bins have an edge at 2.0. To see why Equation (
11
) holds, plug the definition of the null component into the numerator, and compare to Equation (
4
). Thus, if we can bound the null component, we can bound
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
.

Equation (
10
) provides a bound on the null component. Since the two components are non-negative and sum to the LHS, the null component cannot be larger than
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\right)
. Moreover, we know the null component is proportional to the null distribution. Thus, assuming that the histogram of
|t_{i}|
provides a valid estimate of
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\right)
, we can bound
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
with the following algorithm:

Algorithm 1

Visual Bound

1.

Plot the histogram of absolute t-stats for some data-mined signals.

2.

Draw the largest null histogram that still fits inside the data.

3.

Draw a vertical line at 2.0. To the right of this line, the ratio of the null area to total area is the “Visual Bound” on
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
.

This algorithm is a variation of
Storey 2002
’s
Storey 2002
Algorithm 1, though my argument relies on the validity of histogram estimates, and does not require independence. I return to this point in Section
3.2
.

Importantly, this method requires the use of data-mined signals. Since we use the full distribution of t-stats to compute this decomposition, we need reliable data on null signals, which are likely to be missing among published predictors.

The Implied Upper Bound on
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})

Looking at Panel (a) of Figure
2
, one might have the intuition that the ratio of the red area to the gray area is
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})
. This intuition is almost correct. More precisely, this ratio is an upper bound on
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})
.

To formalize this intuition, sum the definition of null component over the set of all bins
B
:

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in B}\left[\text{Null Component}\right]_{b}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b\in B}\Pr\left(|t_{i}|\in b\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)=\Pr(\text{null}_{i}),

(12)

where I’ve assumed that
B
covers the support of
|t_{i}|
. Panel (a) of Figure
2
shows an upper bound on the LHS. Thus, the ratio of the red area to the gray area is an upper bound on the RHS.

In Panel (a), the ratio of red to gray implies
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})\leq 56\%
. In other words, at least 44% of naively-selected accounting ratios have truly non-zero equal-weighted long-short returns. Though this result may be shocking to some readers, a larger
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})
is implausible, as illustrated in Panel (b).

Panel (b) shows an alternative decomposition, in which
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})>56\%
is possible. The result is an unappealing decomposition. For some bins, the Null Component Bound is much larger than the data. This feature is not only visually unappealing, it also violates basic principles of probability. It implies that, for some bins, either the law of total probability (Equation (
10
)) fails to hold, or that there is a negative number of non-null signals in some bins.

Though this decomposition is unappealing, it is precisely the decomposition used in the Easy Bound (Equation (
6
)). Thus, while the FDR bounds in Section
2
are easy to calculate, they are likely too conservative. They violate the “budget constraint” imposed by the law of total probability.

Yet another way to see that
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})\leq 56\%
is to plug
b=[0,0.5]
into Equation (
10
), and note that the alternative component is non-negative:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\quad+\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{alt}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{alt}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right).

(14)

Solve for
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})
to get

\displaystyle\Pr(\text{null}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\right)}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)}=\frac{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\right)}{0.38},

(15)

where 0.38 is the probability that a standard normal r.v. falls between -0.5 and +0.5. Equation (
15
) provides a simple formula for bounding
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})
. Assuming method of moments can be applied to
\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<0.5\right)
, take the share of
|t_{i}|<0.5
and divide by 0.38. This estimate is equivalent to
Storey 2002
’s
Storey 2002
Equation (6), though the argument here does not require independence.

Figure
2
shows the share of
|t_{i}|<0.5
is approximately
6,200/29,000=21.4\%
for the CLZ equal-weighted strategies. Dividing by 0.38 implies that
\Pr(\text{null}_{i})\leq 21.4\%/0.38=56\%
.

Correlations, Standard Errors, and Factor Structure

The Visual and Easy Bounds do
not
require independence. As seen in Algorithm
1
, FDR methods boil down to histogram estimation, which is valid under weak dependence assumptions described in
Wooldridge 1994
.

Correlations do increase estimation uncertainty. Figure
2
captures this effect with a cluster bootstrap that preserves cross-sectional correlations (following
Fama & French 2010
). The error bars are non-negligible but the histogram is well-estimated overall. As a result, the standard error on the FDR bound is only 0.9 percentage points. In fact, Efron (
Efron 2003
;
Efron 2010
a;
Efron 2010a
b) argues that this cluster bootstrap is too conservative, due to ‘‘bootstrap dilation.’’
1
1

1

Correlations also imply that point estimates of FDR bounds are too conservative, due to Jensen’s inequality effects (see
Schwartzman & Lin 2011
).

Correlations also impact the interpretation. Figure
2
shows roughly 10,000 true discoveries, but they cannot all be independent. If they were, one could combine them to form a portfolio with a Sharpe ratio
\sqrt{10,000}=100
times larger than the individual Sharpe ratios. In reality, combining data-mined predictors leads to annualized out-of-sample Sharpe ratios of about 1.5 (YZ, CLZ,
Chen & Dim 2024
), which, while high, are far short of the
100\times
boost. The observed Sharpe ratios are broadly consistent with CLZ’s principal components analysis, which implies roughly 50 independent discoveries rather than 10,000. Further discussion of out-of-sample performance is in Section
4.5
.

Reconciliation with the Literature

The bounds in this paper provide a partial reconciliation of the literature: Table
1
shows that
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq 25\%
, based on many previous studies. This finding is consistent with YZ;
Chen & Zimmermann 2018
;
Jensen et al. 2023
; and
Chen 2024
; who find either a small FDR or many valid predictors.

This section aims to complete the reconciliation. I re-examine papers that find
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\gg 25\%
, specifically
Harvey et al. 2016
,
Harvey & Liu 2020
, and
Chordia et al. 2020
. I close by discussing out-of-sample tests (
McLean & Pontiff 2016
; YZ;
Linnainmaa & Roberts 2018
; CLZ).

Unlike previous sections where significance is defined as
|t_{i}|>2.0
, this section considers the alternative hurdles used in
Harvey et al. 2016
and related papers. Accordingly, “FDR” in this section refers to notions of a discovery beyond
|t_{i}|>2.0
. “
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}
” still refers to the classical
|t_{i}|>2.0
.

Reconciliation with
Harvey et al. 2016
(HLZ)

HLZ is famous for the abstract, which argues “most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false.” But surprisingly, their own FDR estimates show the opposite.

HLZ find that a t-stat hurdle of around 3.0 implies an FDR
\leq 5\%
(page 26, paragraph 1, and page 34, Table 5). Since most asset pricing t-stats exceed 3.0 (e.g.
Chen 2021
), this implies that for most findings, the true discovery rate exceeds 95%. In other words, most claimed findings are almost certainly true.

Figure
3
takes a closer look. It simulates HLZ’s simulated method of moments (SMM) estimates (Table 5,
\rho=0.2
) and plots the expected returns and t-stats for a sample of 1,378 factors, matching HLZ’s preferred estimate for the total number of factors (both published and unpublished). Panel (a) provides a neat visualization of HLZ’s FDR controls. If we define discoveries using
|t_{i}|>2.95
(dot-dash line), we have about 210 discoveries. Only two of these discoveries are null, leading to an FDR of
2/210\approx 1\%
, consistent with HLZ’s famous 3.0 hurdle and the “FDR(1%)” column of their Table 5. If instead, we define discoveries using
|t_{i}|>2.27
(long-dash line), we have 305 discoveries, 13 nulls, and an FDR of
13/305\approx 5\%
, consistent with the “FDR(5%)” column of their Table 5.

What happens if we use the classical definition of a discovery, and draw a vertical line at 2.0? In this case, we get an FDR of just 9%. In other words, HLZ’s own estimates imply about 91% of findings are true discoveries, using the classical definition.

If HLZ’s estimates imply
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}=9\%
, why do they argue most findings are false? The answer lies in misinterpretations of hypothesis testing, combined with misleading interpretations of numerical results.

Misinterpretations of hypothesis testing are found in HLZ’s section “A multiple testing framework.” This section states: “[i]n a factor testing exercise, the typical null hypothesis is that a factor is not significant. Therefore, a factor being insignificant means the null hypothesis is ‘true’ ” (page 13). Similar statements appear elsewhere on the same page.

This is not the correct way to conduct hypothesis testing, whether in factor testing or otherwise. The typical null hypothesis is not that a factor is not significant—the typical null is that the expected return or alpha is zero (
Bali et al. 2016
, page 7). If a factor is insignificant, the null hypothesis is not necessarily true—insignificance could be a false negative.

These misinterpretations culminate in the conclusion, which refers to
Benjamini & Yekutieli 2001
(BY) Theorem 1.3:

In medical research, the recognition of the multiple testing
problem has led to the disturbing conclusion that “most
claimed research findings are false” (
Ioannidis 2005
).
Our analysis of factor discoveries leads to the same conclusion–many
of the factors discovered in the field of finance are likely false
discoveries: of the 296 published significant factors, 158 would be
considered false discoveries under Bonferonni [sic], 142 under
Holm, 132 under [BY Theorem 1.3] (1%), and 80 under [BY Theorem 1.3] (5%).

Neither the Bonferonni and Holm tests nor BY Theorem 1.3 provide the number of false discoveries. While BY’s Theorem can bound the number of false discoveries, this bound may be very far from the actual number (BY, page 1183).

These methods can, however, provide the number of
insignificant factors
. It thus appears that the quotation misinterprets “insignificant factors” as “false discoveries.” Comparing HLZ’s hurdles to the
Chen 2021
’s
Chen 2021
Table I leads to insignificant factor counts matching the quotation, confirming this misinterpretation.

However, even with this misinterpretation, one still cannot conclude that
most
claimed research findings are false. Only the Bonferroni result of 158/296=53% is close to most. 80/296=27% under BY Theorem 1.3 (5%) is far from most.

Thus, there is a second, and critical, misinterpretation. HLZ not only conflates significance with truth, it also conflates “many” with “most.”

Reconciliation with
Harvey & Liu 2020
(HL)

HL also find evidence in support of a very high FDR. Their section “Revisiting
Yan & Zheng 2017
” (page 2531) begins with:

Applying the preferred methods based on Table III to the 18,000
anomalies, the fraction of true strategies is found to be 0.000%
([BY Theorem 1.3]) and 0.015% (Storey,
\theta
= 0.8) under
a 5% significance level, and 0.006% ([BY Theorem 1.3]) and 0.091%
(Storey,
\theta
= 0.8) under a 10% significance level.

Our results suggest that only about 0.1% (or 18) of the 18,000 anomaly strategies in Yan and Zheng (2017) are classified as “true” to control the FDR at 10%. In contrast, Yan and Zheng (2017) claim that “a large number” of these strategies are true based on the Fama-French (2010) approach.

This quote is at odds with Table
2
, which shows that the fraction of “true” (more precisely, non-null) strategies in the YZ dataset is at least 12.8%, far greater than 0.1%.

This quote is also at odds with BY Theorem 1.3. This theorem provides an upper bound on the FDR, and thus a lower bound on the fraction of true discoveries. So the theorem could show “the fraction of true strategies is found to be
at least
0.000%.” However, showing that the fraction
is
0.000%, using BY Theorem 1.3, is simply not possible.

As in HLZ, this quote can be partially understood as misinterpretations of significance. Indeed, while the quote criticizes YZ for claiming that a large number of strategies are true, YZ do not make such a claim. Instead, YZ make careful claims about significance, like “many fundamental signals are significant” or “a large number of fundamental signals are significant.” This misrepresentation is striking given the strong language in HL. For example, HL claim to “show exactly what went wrong with the inference in Yan and Zheng (2017)” (page 2506).

Panel (b) of Table
2
takes a closer look, by applying Storey with the tuning parameter specified in the HL quote. Using an FDR bound of 5% leads to 0.2% of value-weighted 4-factor alphas being significant, not too far from the 0.1% that HL claim to be “true.” However, none of the other specifications results in a share within an order of magnitude of HL’s 0.1%. Importantly, YZ examine all six of the specifications in Table
2
, and half of the specifications imply at least 24.0% of strategies being significant. Focusing exclusively on 4-factor alphas is consistent with HL’s footnote 18, which states “[w]e focus only on the Fama-French-Carhart four-factor model to save space.” HL do not mention that they focus exclusively on value-weighting, but it seems likely that they do so based on Table
2
.

Thus, there is once again a second critical misinterpretation. HL misinterpret the most stringent of YZ’s six specifications as representative of YZ’s analysis as a whole.

The Problem with Equating Significance and Truth

One might justify equating significance and truth by citing multiple testing problems, which undermine classical tests. This makes it tempting to label published findings that fail multiple testing controls as “false discoveries.”

The problem with this interpretation is illustrated in Panel (b) of Figure
3
. This panel simulates HLZ’s SMM estimates, interpreting factors that fail HLZ’s chosen Bonferroni test (circles) as “false discoveries.”

Factors with expected returns of 75 or even 150 bps per month are now “false discoveries.”
These are not sample mean returns, which may be due to luck, but the fundamental expected returns, cured of sampling error. Thus, equating significance with truth leads to absurd economic conclusions. The idea that a factor with an expected return of 150 bps per month (18% per year) is false is, quite frankly, nonsensical.

Panel (b) illustrates an additional problem. The figure shows many significance hurdles. Which hurdle should define false discoveries? The FDR = 5% hurdle (dashed line) is perhaps the natural one (
Benjamini 2020
), but HLZ’s conclusion emphasizes the extremely conservative Bonferroni 5% test instead.

This approach could lead to "post-truth finance," in which different researchers define different truths. It is unlikely that just any researcher will get to define truth. This power will likely be concentrated among senior scholars, who can in principle declare
any
factor as false (why not a Bonferonni 2% hurdle?).

The dangers should be clear. Scientific revolutions (in Kuhn’s
Kuhn 1997
sense) may become impossible. At the very least, junior scholars would seek agreement with the truth declared by senior scholars. Put differently, equating significance with truth risks reducing statistical power to zero, and allowing false paradigms to become permanent (
Akerlof & Michaillat 2018
).

Reconciliation with
Chordia et al. 2020

CGS report the headline FDR number in their abstract:

We estimate the expected proportion of false rejections that researchers
would produce if they failed to account for multiple hypothesis testing to be about 45%.

This conflicts with Equation (
7
), which shows that this proportion is at most 25%.

Unlike HLZ and HL, CGS use standard statistical language. Throughout their paper, a “rejection” is a discovery, and a false rejection is a discovery that is null (e.g. pages 2135 and 2153). So the reconciliation is not in the interpretation.

Instead, the reconciliation lies in CGS’s calibration procedure. The headline number comes from a model of return signals, in which
\pi
fraction of signals are null (“uninformative,” page 2142). They calibrate
\pi
to their data-mined returns, arriving at
\pi=0.06\%
(page 2154), implying
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)=99.94\%
.

\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)=99.94\%
is difficult to square with the law of total probability and figures reported in CGS. As we saw in Section
3.2
, total probability implies bounds on
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)
of the form:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<c\right)}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<c\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)}

(16)

where
c
is a constant. CGS show in Figure 1 that at least 100,000 of 2.39 million t-stats fall in the bin containing zero. Inspecting the figure closely shows that at most 11 bins fit between zero and 2.0, and thus the width of the bin that contains zero is at least
2/12=0.166
. Plugging these values into Equation (
16
) leads to

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(1\times 10^{5}\right)/\left(2.39\times 10^{6}\right)}{\Pr\left(|t_{i}|<(0.166/2)\hskip 0.86108pt\scalebox{1.1}{|}\hskip 0.86108pt\text{null}_{i}\right)}=63\%,

(17)

not far from the bounds in Table
2
, and far lower than the 99.94% calibrated by CGS. The intuition can be seen by
visually applying Algorithm
1
to Figure 1 of CGS. The largest standard normal distribution that still fits inside this histogram will fail to cover the tails of the histogram.

So how does CGS’s calibration arrive at
\pi=0.06\%
? They calibrate
\pi
to match the share of signals that exceed the
|t_{i}|>2
threshold but fail a more stringent threshold based on
Romano & Wolf 2007
(page 2153). This target moment contains no information about
|t_{i}|\leq 0.5
, which, as seen in Figure
2
, are highly informative about
\Pr\left(\text{null}_{i}\right)
. As shown in Equation (
16
), if CGS had instead targeted the share of small t-stats, they would have arrived at a much higher
\pi
. Since CGS choose the dispersion of informative alphas outside of their calibration (page 2152), this much higher
\pi
would then imply a much lower headline FDR.

Reconciliation with Out-of-Sample (OOS) Tests

At a cursory glance, the literature on OOS tests finds disparate results. The literature finds OOS performance decay ranging from 0% to 60% (e.g.
McLean & Pontiff 2016
;
Linnainmaa & Roberts 2018
;
Jacobs & Müller 2020
).

A closer look reveals that OOS returns are consistently about 30% smaller than the original published returns, if one focuses on OOS tests that are temporally and methodologically close to the originals. Using OOS periods that end before the publication dates, and using portfolio tests that broadly follow the original papers,
Chen & Zimmermann 2020
,
McLean & Pontiff 2016
, and
Jacobs & Müller 2020
find OOS decay of 20%, 26%, and 36%, respectively.

With the large datasets available from data mining, one can examine OOS periods that are even closer to the in-sample periods. CLZ finds OOS decay is as small as 17% among data-mined predictors, if the OOS period ends 12 months after the in-sample period. Like CLZ, YZ and
Chen & Dim 2024
use data mining to produce OOS returns of roughly 50 bps per month. This mild decay and strong OOS performance is quite consistent with the
\text{FDR}_{|t|>2}\leq 25\%
derived in this paper.

Conceptually, OOS tests that are close to the original tests are the most appropriate for validating FDR estimates. OOS samples that deviate significantly from the original tests introduce economic effects that are distinct from multiple testing. For example, changes to information technology can reduce predictability (
Chordia et al. 2014
), while the introduction of retail traders can increase it (
Goetzmann & Huang 2018
). Though these effects are important, they are economic rather than statistical, and should not be measured using FDR methods.

Conclusion and Limitations

This paper provides simple arguments that bound the FDR under publication bias. They handle publication bias by using data-mining experiments or conservative extrapolations as worst-case scenarios. The arguments can be explained in just a few equations and provide clarity to the FDR literature. Using data from many papers, the bounds show that the FDR cannot exceed 25%.

Claims that the FDR exceeds 25% are driven by misinterpreting “insignificant factors” as “false discoveries” and by misleading interpretations of numerical results. Neglecting to fit moments that are highly informative about the FDR also results in overestimates.

As highlighted by the title of this paper, my analysis is limited to statistics. A “true discovery” is a statistically significant predictor that has a non-zero alpha. Many economic issues are buried in this statistical statement, like trading costs (
Novy-Marx & Velikov 2016
), information costs (
Chordia et al. 2014
), and arbitrage effects (
McLean & Pontiff 2016
).
Chen & Velikov 2023
find that, net of these effects, most findings in cross-sectional predictability produce measly profits—even if they are true, statistically speaking.
