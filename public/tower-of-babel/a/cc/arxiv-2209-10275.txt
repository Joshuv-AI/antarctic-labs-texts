Tight Exponential Strong Converse for Source Coding Problem with Encoded Side Information Thanks: Part of this paper was presented in 2023 IEEE International Symposium of Information Theory.
Daisuke Takeuchi , Shun Watanabe

Abstract
The source coding problem with encoded side information is considered. A lower bound on the strong converse exponent has been derived by Oohama, but its tightness has not been clarified. In this paper, we derive a tight strong converse exponent. For the special case such that the side-information does not exists, we demonstrate that our tight exponent of the WAK problem reduces to the known tight expression of that special case while Oohama’s lower bound is strictly loose. The converse part is proved by a judicious use of the change-of-measure argument, which was introduced by Gu-Effros and further developed by Tyagi-Watanabe. Interestingly, the soft Markov constraint, which was introduced by Oohama as a proof technique, is naturally incorporated into the characterization of the exponent. A technical innovation of this paper is recognizing that the soft Markov constraint is a part of the exponent, rather than a penalty term that should be vanished. In fact, via numerical experiment, we provide evidence that the soft Markov constraint is strictly positive. The achievability part is derived by a careful analysis of the type argument; however, unlike the conventional analysis for the achievable rate region, we need to derive the soft Markov constraint in the analysis of the correct probability. Furthermore, we present an application of our derivation of strong converse exponent to the privacy amplification.



I
Introduction

Fig. 1:
WAK network

For source coding problems, the (weak) converse theorems state that, at transmission rates under the theoretical limit, there is no error-free coding scheme as the block length tends to infinity, i.e. the error probability cannot go to
0
.
On the other hand, the strong converse theorems state that, at transmission rates below the theoretical limit, the error probability converges to
1
as the block length tends to infinity.

The source coding problem with encoded side information, also known as the Wyner-Ahlswede-Körner (WAK) problem, is one of fundamental problems in network information theory, and is described in Fig.
1
.
The WAK network consists of two encoders
\varphi^{(n)}_{1},\varphi^{(n)}_{2}
and one decoder
\psi^{(n)}
.
Both encoders are connected to the decoder by each channel, and the decoder aims to reproduce the source observed by the first encoder.
Under the condition of i.i.d. sources, this problem was solved by Wyner
[
30
]
, and Ahlswede and Körner
[
1
]
in the middle of 1970s.
According to their results, the information theoretical limit involves an auxiliary random variable with a Markov chain, which makes this problem difficult.

In this paper, we are interested in the strong converse theorem of WAK network.
As described later, it is known that for the WAK network, the error probability exponentially goes to
1
when a rate pair is outside the achievable rate region.
On the other hand, an explicit form of the exponent function has not been clear.
Oohama
[
21
]
obtained a lower bound of the exponent by using the recursive method.
However, it was unknown whether Oohama’s bound is tight or not.
Thus, we consider the exponent for the WAK network by using another method called the change of measure argument.
Furthermore, we discuss a property of the exponent through a numerical experiment, and we present an application of our derivation of strong converse exponent to one of the important concepts of cryptography: privacy amplification.

I-A

Related Work

Before stating our contribution, we would like to describe the history of exponential strong converse and change of measure argument.
For the WAK network, Ahlswede, Gács and Körner
[
2
]
proved that the correct probability goes to 0 if the rate pair is outside the rate region (
strong converse
).
After that, Gu and Effros
[
9
]
showed that the correct probability decreases exponentially by examining the proof of
[
2
]
(
exponential strong converse
).
Since they just showed the convergence speed, they did not clarify the explicit form of the correct probability exponent in terms of the rate pair
(R_{1},R_{2})
.
Recently, Oohama
[
21
]
derived an explicit lower bound of this exponent function with parametric form.
However, it is unknown whether that bound is tight or not.

In 1973, Arimoto
[
4
]
analyzed the exponential strong converse for the point-to-point channel coding problem.
After that, in 1976, Dueck and Körner
[
8
]
gave the strong converse exponent by using the type method.
Also, exponential strong converse has naturally been extended for multi-terminal networks.
For the Slepian-Wolf problem, Oohama and Han
[
18
]
derived the optimal exponent by using universal coding in 1994.
Furthermore, as partially mentioned above, Oohama
[
19
]
,
[
21
]
derived the exponential strong converses for the WAK problem, the asymmetric broadcast channel problem
[
15
]
and the Wyner-Ziv problem
[
31
]
.
However, due to Markov chains and an auxiliary random variable, the analysis of exponential strong converse for the WAK network is difficult and has not been studied much other than
[
21
]
.

The change of measure argument introduced by Gu and Effros
[
9
]
is a useful technique for discussing the strong converses for various problems.
This enables us to prove strong converses using similar ways as those used for weak converses.
Then, it should be noted that Tyagi and Watanabe
[
25
]
enabled the change of measure argument to be applied to a lot of distributed coding problems including the WAK network (see also an early attempt in
[
27
]
).
After that, various coding problem (for example,
[
24
]
,
[
3
]
) has been analyzed by using the change of measure argument.
More recently, Hamad, Wigger and Sarkiss
[
10
]
further developed the change of measure argument so that the Markov constraint can be proved in a different manner from
[
25
]
.
In this paper, we derive a tight exponent of the correct probability for the WAK network by using the change of measure argument.
Particularly, our characterization clarifies a tight strong converse exponent for the problem such that there are both an auxiliary random variable and the Markov chain in the rate region.

For strong converses, various approaches have been proposed.
Kosut and Kliewer
[
16
]
proposed an association between edge removal and strong converses, and also indicated that the capacity is not changed if an extra edge is removed.
Another related approach was proposed by Jose and Kulkarni
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
.
Their approach based on the linear programming (LP) analyzes and improves the converse bound for coding problems by introducing the so-called LP relaxation.
Furthermore, Liu, Handel and Verdú
[
17
]
developed an method for non-asymptotic converses by using reverse hypercontractivity of Markov semigroups.

I-B

Main Contribution

Looking at the above related works and remaining issues, we are motivated to consider the strong converse exponent for WAK network by judiciously using the change of measure argument.
In this work, we establish a tight characterization of the exponent.
Our main contributions are detailed as follows.

First, we apply the change of measure argument to the exponential strong converse problem for the source coding problem including the Markov chain and an auxiliary random variable.
Originally, the change of measure argument has been used in order to prove strong converses.
We now enables this technique to be applied to the analysis of strong converse exponents by focusing on the correct probability rather than achievable rates.
As mentioned above, a main difficulty to derive tight exponent is handling of the Markov chain constraint that appears in the characterization of the achievable rate region of the WAK problem.
Based on the idea of
[
20
]
,
[
25
]
put the Markov penalty term in terms of the conditional mutual information in the correct probability exponent.
Rather than considering the Markov constraint as a penalty term that should be vanished, we considered regard it as a part of the exponent, which succeeded in deriving a tight exponent.
In fact, our numerical experiment suggests that the soft Markov constraint term is strictly positive.

Second, even though achievability part is proved using manipulations of the standard type method, proving the matching exponent is not trivial.
Particularly, as mentioned in the previous paragraph, our (converse) characterization of exponent may violate the Markov constraint.
However, in the WAK problem, the second encoder’s coding can only depend on
Y^{n}
. Thus, we need to derive the Markov constraint term in the analysis of the correct probability.

Finally, our exponent is strictly tight compared to the previous bound
[
21
]
in a certain situation.
In the channel coding, there are two kinds of expressions for the strong converse exponent: the one by Arimoto
[
4
]
and the other by Dueck and Körner
[
8
]
, which corresponds to the parametric function and the optimization one, respectively.
While the lower bound derived by Oohama
[
21
]
is closer to the expression by Arimoto, our characterization of the exponent is along the lines of the expression by Dueck and Körner.
Therefore, we cannot directly compare our exponent with Oohama’s bound.
By converting our exponent expressed in an optimization problem into a parametric form, it become possible to analytically compare with the bound in
[
21
]
for the special case of single user source coding.

II-B

WAK Network

We consider the network described in Fig.
1
. For a given
source
P_{XY}
on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
this coding system consists of two encoders

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{(n)}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(n)},\ \varphi_{2}^{(n)}:\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(n)},

and one decoder

\displaystyle\psi^{(n)}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(n)}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(n)}\to\mathcal{X}^{n}.

For simplicity, we write encoders and a decoder as
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}

and
\psi
, respectively. Input sequences
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
are
encoded by
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
separately, and the decoder
outputs the estimation
\hat{X}^{n}=\psi(\varphi_{1}(X^{n}),\varphi_{2}(Y^{n}))

of
X^{n}
.

For i.i.d.
(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}
, the error probability of code
\Phi_{n}=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\psi)
is defined as

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{e}}(\Phi_{n}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}):=\Pr(\hat{X}^{n}\neq X^{n}).

A rate pair
(R_{1},R_{2})
is called an achievable rate pair if there exists a sequence of codes such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(n)}|\leq R_{1},\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{M}_{2}^{(n)}|\leq R_{2},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\textnormal{e}}(\Phi_{n}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})=0.

Also, the achievable rate region
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}=\{(R_{1},R_{2}):(R_{1},R_{2})\text{ is achievable}\}.

Wyner
[
30
]
, Ahlswede and Körner
[
1
]
determined
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}
as below.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the region

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X-Y-U\\
|\mathcal{U}|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|+1\end{subarray}}\{(R_{1},R_{2}):

\displaystyle R_{1}\geq H(X|U),R_{2}\geq I(Y\land U)\},

(1)

where the Markov chain
U-Y-X
means

\displaystyle P_{UXY}=P_{X}P_{Y|X}P_{U|Y}.

Then, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}=\mathcal{R}.

(2)

Our main purpose is to find an explicit form of the exponent function for the error probability converging to
1
as
n\to\infty
when
(R_{1},R_{2})
is not achievable. To achieve this aim, we define the correct probability
P_{\textnormal{c}}
and the optimal exponent function
G
:

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c}}(\Phi_{n}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})

\displaystyle=1-P_{\textnormal{e}}(\Phi_{n}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}),

\displaystyle G^{(n)}(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})

\displaystyle=\min_{\Phi_{n}}\left(-\frac{1}{n}\right)\log P_{c}(\Phi_{n}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})

\displaystyle G(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}G^{(n)}(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}).

According to
[
21
]
, the limit in the definition of
G
exists, and the exponent function
G(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY})
is a convex function of
(R_{1},R_{2})
.

Ahlswede, Gács and Körner
[
2
]
proved that the correct probability
P_{\textnormal{c}}
goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
Gu and Effros
[
9
]
showed that the convergence speed of
P_{\mathrm{c}}
is exponential. Also, Oohama
[
21
]
derived a lower bound on
G
.
However, it was not clarified whether the lower bound in
[
21
]
is tight or not.
In the next section, we shall show our result.

III
Main Result

In this section, we show our main result. Before showing it, we set

\displaystyle F(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY})=\min_{P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\biggl\{D(P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{\tilde{U}|\tilde{Y}}P_{XY})+\left|I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{Y})-R_{2}\right|^{+}:R_{1}\geq H(\tilde{X}|\tilde{U}),|\mathcal{U}|\leq|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|+2\biggr\},

(3)

where
|a|^{+}=\max\{a,0\}
.

Now we show our main theorem by using this quantity.

Theorem 2
.

For any
P_{XY}
, we have

\displaystyle G(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY})=F(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY}).

(4)

The proof of this result can be found in Section
V
.
As stated in Section
I
, there are two ways of expressing the exponent: parametric
[
4
]
and optimization
[
8
]
function.
Unlike Oohama’s bound with parametric notations
[
21
]
, we derive the exponent as an optimization function.
Interestingly, although the Markov chain in the achievable rate region cannot be explicitly seen in (
3

plays the role.
In fact, we can break it into the KL-divergence and the conditional mutual information as

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{\tilde{U}|\tilde{Y}}P_{XY})

\displaystyle=D(P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{XY})+D(P_{\tilde{U}|\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{\tilde{U}|\tilde{Y}}|P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}})

\displaystyle=D(P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{X}|\tilde{Y}).

(5)

Then, we get

if the Markov chain is satisfied, which corresponds to replacing the “hard” information constraint with the “soft” cost introduced by Oohama
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
.

Moreover, the exponent

is strictly positive when the rate pair
(R_{1},R_{2})
is outside the achievable rate region
\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}
.
Actually, we can verify this property by considering the contraposition that
(R_{1},R_{2})
is inside the achievable rate region when

that is derived from the Markov constraint
I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{X}|\tilde{Y})=0
and the fact
P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}=P_{XY}
since
D(P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{XY})=0
.

In the following, we investigate our result to observe its properties.

Comparison

In this section, we compare two expressions in Corollary

Proposition 7
.

For the special case described in Fig.

As mentioned in Introduction, two expressions are different in characterization.
Since we cannot compare them as they are, we convert our exponent using

Lemma 8
.

We can rewrite
\hat{F}_{\textnormal{t}}(R_{1}|P_{X})
as

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\textnormal{t}}(R_{1}|P_{X})

\displaystyle=\max_{\theta\leq 0}\frac{-s(\theta)+\theta R_{1}}{1-\theta},

(20)

where the function
s(\theta)
is denoted as

\displaystyle s(\theta)=\log\sum_{x}\{P_{X}(x)\}^{1-\theta}.

Using this lemma, we prove the main result, Proposition
7
.

Proof:

Putting
\theta=-\alpha(1-\mu)\in[-1,0]
, we first see that

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}^{(\mu,\alpha)}(P_{X})=-s(\theta),

(21)

which can be derived from

\displaystyle s(\theta)

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{x}\{P_{X}(x)\}^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\sum_{x}P_{X}(x)\left(\frac{1}{P_{X}(x)}\right)^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\sum_{x}P_{X}(x)\exp\left(\theta\log\frac{1}{P_{X}(x)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\hat{\Omega}^{(\mu,\alpha)}(P_{X}).

(22)

Then, we can rewrite
\hat{F}_{\textnormal{o}}(R_{1}|P_{X})
as

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\textnormal{o}}(R_{1}|P_{X})=\max_{\theta\in[-1,0]}\frac{-s(\theta)+\theta R_{1}}{2-\theta}.

(23)

Now we show that for some
\theta\in[-1,0)

-s(\theta)+\theta R_{1}
is positive, i.e.,
-s(\theta)+\theta R_{1}>0
.
Considering the condition of
R_{1}

R_{1}<H(X)

R_{1}\leq H(X)-\delta
for a small positive number
\delta

Also, from the definition of derivatives, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}s(0)=\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{s(\theta)}{\theta}=H(X).

(24)

Then, when we take
\theta_{0}\in[-1,0)

\delta

\displaystyle\frac{s(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{0}}\geq H(X)-\frac{\delta}{2},

(25)

which derives

\displaystyle R_{1}\leq H(X)-\delta\leq\frac{s(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{0}}-\frac{\delta}{2}<\frac{s(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{0}}.

(26)

Therefore, noting that
\theta_{0}

\displaystyle F_{\textnormal{o}}(R_{1}|P_{X})=\frac{-s(\theta_{1})+\theta_{1}R_{1}}{2-\theta_{1}}.

\displaystyle F_{\textnormal{o}}(R_{1}|P_{X})

\displaystyle<\frac{-s(\theta_{1})+\theta_{1}R_{1}}{1-\theta_{1}}

\displaystyle=F_{\textnormal{t}}(R_{1}|P_{X}),

(28)

V-A

Direct Part

In this section, we prove the direct part. Since we are interested
in a lower bound on the correct probability
P_{\mathrm{c}}
, it
suffices to construct a code such that sequences
(x^{n},y^{n})

in a fixed type class
{\cal T}_{\bar{X}\bar{Y}}
can be decoded
correctly. First, we fix
P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}
that attains
the minimum in (
3
) for
(R_{1}-2\epsilon,R_{2})

where
\epsilon
is a small positive number. Let
P_{\bar{U}\bar{X}\bar{Y}}

be a joint type satisfying

\displaystyle d_{\textnormal{var}}(P_{\bar{U}\bar{X}\bar{Y}},P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}})\leq\frac{|\mathcal{U}||\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}{n}.

(29)

Note that, by the continuity of entropy, we see

\displaystyle H(\bar{X}|\bar{U})

\displaystyle\leq H(\tilde{X}|\tilde{U})+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq R_{1}-\epsilon,

(30)

for sufficiently large
n
. In order to prove the direct part, we
use the following covering lemma.

Lemma 9
.

For a joint type
P_{\bar{U}\bar{Y}}
, there
exists a codebook
{\cal C}=\{u_{1}^{n},\ldots,u_{L}^{n}\}\subseteq{\cal T}_{\bar{U}}

such that

L\leq\exp\{nI(\bar{U}\land\bar{Y})+(|\mathcal{U}||\mathcal{Y}|+4)\log(n+1)\},

(31)

and such that for any
y^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{Y}}
, there exists

u^{n}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying
(u^{n},y^{n})\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{U}\bar{Y}}
.

Proof:

Since the strategy of proving this lemma is almost the same as
[
28
, Lemma 4]
,
we omit it.
∎We describe an overview of code construction. For this coding scheme,
we want to evaluate the correct probability
P_{\textnormal{c}}
. For

\varphi_{1}
, we use the standard random binning with rate
R_{1}
.
For
\varphi_{2}
, we use the quantization using the test channel

P_{\bar{U}|\bar{Y}}
. However,
R_{2}
may be smaller than
I(\bar{U}\land\bar{Y})

that is needed to quantize sequences in
\mathcal{T}_{\bar{Y}}
via
the test channel
P_{\bar{U}|\bar{Y}}
. In that case,
\varphi_{2}

assigns messages to a part of the quantization codebook, and assign
a prescribed constant message to the remaining. The detail of the
coding scheme is described as follows.

Encoding of
\varphi_{1}

Randomly and independently assign an index
m_{1}=F(x^{n})\in\{1,2,\dots,2^{nR_{1}}\}

to each sequence
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. The set of sequences
with the same index
m_{1}
forms a bin
\mathcal{B}(m_{1})
. Observing

x^{n}\in\mathcal{B}(m_{1})
,
\varphi_{1}
sends the bin index

m_{1}
.

Encoding of
\varphi_{2}

Consider a codebook
{\cal C}
given by Lemma
9

and function
f:{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}\to\{1,\ldots,L\}
such that
(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n},y^{n})\in{\cal T}_{\bar{U}\bar{Y}}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
|f^{-1}(1)|\geq|f^{-1}(2)|\geq\cdots\geq|f^{-1}(L)|
.
We define a function
\tilde{f}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(y^{n})=\begin{cases}f(y^{n})&(f(y^{n})\leq 2^{nR_{2}}),\\
1&(f(y^{n})>2^{nR_{2}}).\end{cases}

Then, upon observing
y^{n}\in{\cal Y}^{n}
,
\varphi_{2}
sends

m_{2}=\tilde{f}(y^{n})
if
y^{n}\in{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}
and
m_{2}=1

otherwise.

Decoding.

Observing
(m_{1},m_{2})=(\varphi_{1}(x^{n}),\varphi_{2}(y^{n}))
,

\psi
declares the estimate
\hat{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{m_{2}}^{n})

if it is the unique sequence satisfying
F(\hat{x}^{n})=m_{1}
; otherwise
it declares a prescribed sequence.

Analysis of the correct probability.

We bound the correct probability
P_{\mathrm{c}}
averaged over random
bin assignments. We lower bound the correct probability quite generously,
and estimate the probability such that the following three events
occur consecutively.

1.

The event
E_{1}
such that
y^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{Y}}
and
\tilde{f}(y^{n})=f(y^{n})
:
we denote the probability of this event by
P_{\textnormal{c},1}=\Pr(E_{1})
.

2.

The event
E_{2}
such that
E_{1}
occurs and
x^{n}
is included
in the conditional type class
{\cal T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}\bar{Y}}(u^{n}_{f(y^{n})},y^{n})
:
we denote the conditional probability of this event conditioned that

E_{1}
occurs by
P_{\textnormal{c},2}=\Pr(E_{2}|E_{1})
.

3.

The event
E_{3}
such that
E_{1}\wedge E_{2}
occurs and there
exists no sequence
\hat{x}^{n}\in{\cal T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n})

satisfying
\hat{x}^{n}\neq x^{n}
and
F(\hat{x}^{n})=F(x^{n})
:
we denote the conditional probability of this event conditioned on
the first and second events by
P_{\textnormal{c},3}=\Pr(E_{3}|E_{1}\wedge E_{2})
.

By noting that
{\cal T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}\bar{Y}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n},y^{n})\subseteq{\cal T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n})
,
we can verify that the decoder
\psi
outputs the correct source
sequence
x^{n}
if the three events occur
E_{1},E_{2}
, and
E_{3}

consecutively. Thus, we can lower bound the correct probability as

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c}}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{3}P_{\textnormal{c},i}.

(32)

In the following, we shall calculate each probability
P_{\textnormal{c},i}
.

When
L\leq 2^{nR_{2}}
, the second condition
\tilde{f}(y^{n})=f(y^{n})

of the event
E_{1}
is always satisfied. On the other hand, by noting
that
|f^{-1}(i)|
is sorted in descending order, when
L>2^{nR_{2}}
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{nR_{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{nR_{2}}}|f^{-1}(i)|\geq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}|f^{-1}(i)|=\frac{|{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}|}{L},

(33)

which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2^{nR_{2}}}\frac{|f^{-1}(i)|}{|{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}|}\geq\frac{2^{nR_{2}}}{L}.

(34)

Therefore, by using
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c},1}

\displaystyle=\Pr\{Y^{n}\in{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}\land[f(Y^{n})\leq 2^{nR_{2}}]\}

\displaystyle=P_{Y^{n}}({\cal T}_{\bar{Y}})\sum_{i=1}^{2^{nR_{2}}}\frac{|f^{-1}(i)|}{|{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}|}

\displaystyle\geq P_{Y^{n}}({\cal T}_{\bar{Y}})\min\left\{1,\frac{2^{nR_{2}}}{L}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{Y}|}2^{-n(D(P_{\bar{Y}}||P_{Y})+|(1/n)\log L-R_{2}|^{+})}

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{Y}|}2^{-n(D(P_{\bar{Y}}||P_{Y})+|I(\bar{U}\land\bar{Y})-R_{2}+\delta_{n}|^{+})},

(35)

where
\delta_{n}=(1/n)(|\mathcal{U}||\mathcal{Y}|+4)\log(n+1)
.

By using
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c},2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{n}\in{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}\\
\tilde{f}(y^{n})=f(y^{n})\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{K}P_{X^{n}|Y^{n}}({\cal T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}\bar{Y}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n},y^{n}))

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{U}||\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}2^{-nD(P_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}\bar{Y}}||P_{X|Y}|P_{\bar{U}\bar{Y}})},

(36)

where
K=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{nR_{2}}}|f^{-1}(i)|
is the number of
y^{n}\in{\cal T}_{\bar{Y}}
satisfying

\tilde{f}(y^{n})=f(y^{n})
. Also, since the decoding method searches the unique sequence
\hat{x}^{n}
among
\mathcal{T}_{\tilde{X}|\tilde{U}}(u^{n}_{f(y^{n})})
such that the bin index coincides with

,
P_{\textnormal{c},3}
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c},3}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{(x^{n},y^{n})}\Pr\{(X^{n},Y^{n})=(x^{n},y^{n})|E_{1}\land E_{2}\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{-----.-}\cdot\Pr\{\exists\hat{x}^{n}\neq x^{n}~~\mathrm{s.t.}~~F(\hat{x}^{n})=F(x^{n}),\hat{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n})\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{(x^{n},y^{n})}\Pr\{(X^{n},Y^{n})=(x^{n},y^{n})|E_{1}\land E_{2}\}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n})\\
\hat{x}^{n}\neq x^{n}\end{subarray}}\Pr\{F(\hat{x}^{n})=F(x^{n})\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{(x^{n},y^{n})}\Pr\{(X^{n},Y^{n})=(x^{n},y^{n})|E_{1}\land E_{2}\}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}}(u_{f(y^{n})}^{n})\\
\hat{x}^{n}\neq x^{n}\end{subarray}}2^{-nR_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-2^{-n(R_{1}-H(\bar{X}|\bar{U}))}

\displaystyle\geq 1-2^{-n\epsilon},

(37)

where we use
[
7
, Lemma 2.5]
, and a condition
R_{1}-\epsilon\geq H(\bar{X}|\bar{U})

in the last inequality. Therefore,
P_{\textnormal{c}}
can be evaluated
as

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{c}}

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=1}^{3}P_{\textnormal{c},i}

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{Y}|}2^{-n(D(P_{\bar{Y}}||P_{Y})+|I(\bar{U}\land\bar{Y})-R_{2}+\delta_{n}|^{+})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{U}||\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}2^{-nD(P_{\bar{X}|\bar{U}\bar{Y}}||P_{X|Y}|P_{\bar{U}\bar{Y}})}(1-2^{-n\epsilon})

[

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})+H(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{Y}^{n})\geq n(D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+H(\tilde{X}_{J}|\tilde{Y}_{J})).

\displaystyle H(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{Y}^{n},\tilde{M_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}H(\tilde{X}_{j}|\tilde{Y}^{n},\tilde{M_{2}},\tilde{X}_{j}^{-})

(47)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})\geq n(D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{X}_{J}|\tilde{Y}_{J})).

(48)

Moreover, since
nR_{2}\geq H(\tilde{M_{2}})
, we have

\displaystyle nR_{2}

\displaystyle\geq H(\tilde{M_{2}})

\displaystyle=H(\tilde{M_{2}})-H(\tilde{M_{2}}|\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})

\displaystyle=I(\tilde{M_{2}}\land\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})

\displaystyle=H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})-H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n}|\tilde{M_{2}}).

(49)

Then, we can consider the following inequality

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})

\displaystyle\geq D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})+H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})-H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n}|\tilde{M_{2}})-nR_{2}.

(50)

A very similar argument in
[
25
, Proposition 1]
leads to

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})+H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n})=n(D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+H(\tilde{X}_{J},\tilde{Y}_{J})).

(51)

Also,

\displaystyle H(\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{Y}^{n}|\tilde{M_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}H(\tilde{X}_{j},\tilde{Y}_{j}|\tilde{M_{2}},\tilde{X}_{j}^{-},\tilde{Y}_{j}^{-})

\displaystyle=nH(\tilde{X}_{J},\tilde{Y}_{J}|\tilde{U}_{J},J).

(52)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})

\displaystyle\geq D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+H(\tilde{X}_{J},\tilde{Y}_{J})-H(\tilde{X}_{J},\tilde{Y}_{J}|\tilde{U}_{J},J)-R_{2}

\displaystyle=D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{X}_{J},\tilde{Y}_{J})-R_{2}

\displaystyle=D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{X}_{J}|\tilde{Y}_{J})+I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{Y}_{J})-R_{2}.

(53)

Combining (
42
), (
48
) and (
53
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{P_{\textnormal{c}}}\geq D(P_{\tilde{X}_{J}\tilde{Y}_{J}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{X}_{J}|\tilde{Y}_{J})+\left|I(\tilde{U}_{J},J\land\tilde{Y}_{J})-R_{2}\right|^{+}.

(54)

Noting (
43
) and (
44
), we have a condition that
R_{1}\geq H(\tilde{X}_{J}|\tilde{U}_{J},J)
. As a consequence, we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{\psi,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{P_{\textnormal{c}}}\geq\min_{P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}}\left\{D(P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{X}|\tilde{Y})+\left|I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{Y})-R_{2}\right|^{+}\right\},

(55)

where the minimization of
P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}
is taken under the condition
R_{1}\geq H(\tilde{X}|\tilde{U})
.
Note that we can see

\displaystyle D(P_{\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{XY})+I(\tilde{U}\land\tilde{X}|\tilde{Y})

\displaystyle=D(P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}||P_{\tilde{U}|\tilde{Y}}P_{XY}).

(56)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle G(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY})\geq F(R_{1},R_{2}|P_{XY}),

(57)

except the cardinality bound, which will be discussed in the next section.

Privacy amplification for bounded storage eavesdropper

In this section, we introduce the privacy amplification for bounded storage eavesdropper illustrated in Fig.

(X^{n},Y^{n})

\mathcal{X}^{n}\times\mathcal{Y}^{n}

\mathcal{X}

Suppose that Alice (a sender) and Bob (a receiver) have a random variable

\mathcal{X}^{n}

Y^{n}
on
\mathcal{Y}^{n}

Y^{n}

Y^{n}

In the privacy amplification, a secret key
K_{n}

\displaystyle f_{n}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{K}_{n}=\{0,1\}^{nR_{1}}

S_{n}

K_{n}

\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

Eve has a function value
E_{n}

\displaystyle g_{n}:\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{0,1\}^{nR_{2}}.

In this paper, since we use the total variational distance as a security criterion, we require the quantity

VI-B

Privacy Amplification and WAK Network

In this section, we evaluate the security criterion for the privacy amplification for bounded storage eavesdropper by using our derivation of the tight exponent for the WAK network.
Before we state our argument, we give a well-known lemma regarding the security criterion
[
22
]
(see also
[
26
, Chapter 7]

Lemma 10
.

For a given distribution
P_{XS_{n}}
and a number
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{T}}=\left\{(x^{n},s):\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(x^{n}|s_{n})}\geq\tau\right\}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\log\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}S_{n}}(X^{n}|S_{n})}<\tau\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2^{-\tau+nR_{1}}}.

(58)

One of the goals of privacy amplification is to evaluate a security criterion.
We are interested in the trade-off between the key rate
R_{1}
and Eve’s storage size
R_{2}
under the condition that the key
K_{n}
and Eve’s information
S_{n}
are statistically independent.
Now, by using our derivation of the strong converse exponent, we evaluate the first term on the right-hand side of (
58
) for the privacy amplification illustrated in Fig.
5
.
In the following theorem, we show the evaluation for the security criterion with our exponent.

Theorem 11
.

Setting
\tau=nR_{1}^{\prime}
where
R_{1}^{\prime}=R_{1}+\delta
for a positive number
\sigma
, the security criterion
\Delta
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\Delta\leq 2^{-nF(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}|P_{XY})}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2^{-n\delta}},

(59)

where
F(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}|P_{XY})
is defined by (
3
).
Recall that the function
F(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}|P_{XY})
is positive if and only if
(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2})\notin\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{WAK}}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we evaluate
\Pr(\log(1/P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(X^{n}|S_{n}))<nR_{1}^{\prime})
.
First, let
P^{\prime}_{\textnormal{c}}
be a number such that

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{\textnormal{c}}=\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(X^{n}|S_{n})}<R_{1}^{\prime}\right).

(60)

Also, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{c}

\displaystyle=\left\{(x^{n},y^{n}):\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(x^{n}|g_{n}(y^{n}))}<R_{1}^{\prime}\right\},

(61)

\displaystyle P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\Pr((X^{n},Y^{n})=(x^{n},y^{n})|(X^{n},Y^{n})\in\mathcal{T}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}(x^{n},y^{n})}{P_{X^{n}Y^{n}}(\mathcal{T}^{c})}\mathds{1}[(x^{n},y^{n})\in\mathcal{T}^{c}].

(62)

Then, we can calculate

D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}\tilde{Y}^{n}}||P_{X^{n}Y^{n}})=\log\frac{1}{P^{\prime}_{\textnormal{c}}},

(63)

which corresponds to (
42
) of the converse part in Section
V
.
This means that our converse proof method for the exponent can be also applied to the consideration of the privacy amplification for bounded storage eavesdropper.

Next, defining a random variable
\tilde{S}_{n}=g_{n}(Y^{n})
, we evaluate
R_{1}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle nR_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{1}{P_{\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n}}(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n})}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{P_{\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n}}(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n})}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n})}\right]

\displaystyle=H(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n})+D(P_{\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n}}||P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}|P_{\tilde{S}_{n}})

\displaystyle\geq H(\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n}),

(64)

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation of
P_{\tilde{X}^{n}|\tilde{S}_{n}}
.
Single-letterization in the similar way as in Section
V
yields

\displaystyle R_{1}^{\prime}\geq H(\tilde{X}|\tilde{U}),

(65)

where
\tilde{U}
is a similar random variable to the one that appears in the process of single-letterization in the converse of Section
V
.
Also, in the same manner, we can obtain

\displaystyle R_{2}\geq I(\tilde{Y}\land\tilde{U}).

(66)

According to the proof of the converse for our main theorem, we have

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{\textnormal{c}}\leq 2^{-nF(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}|P_{XY})}.

(67)

Thus, for any
P_{XY}

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{P_{X^{n}|S_{n}}(X^{n}|S_{n})}<R_{1}^{\prime}\right)\leq 2^{-nF(R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}|P_{XY})}.

As a conclusion, by Lemma

(69)

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we derived the tight strong converse exponent for the WAK network.
The rate region of the WAK network has two characteristics: an auxiliary random variable and the Markov chain.
Because of these characteristics, deriving the tight strong converse exponent of the WAK network has been regarded as a challenging problem.
Also, we obtained that the numerical experiment suggests that a better result can be obtained when the Markov constraint does not hold for the arguments of the optimization function
P_{\tilde{U}\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}}
.
We believe that our proof methods can be also applied for other multi-terminal network problems.
