Abstract
The evolution of magnetism in late-type dwarfs remains murky, as we can only weakly predict levels of activity for M dwarfs of a given mass and age. We report results from our spectroscopic survey of M dwarfs in the Southern Continuous Viewing Zone (CVZ) of the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS). As the TESS CVZs overlap with those of the James Webb Space Telescope, our targets constitute a legacy sample for studies of nearby M dwarfs. For 122 stars, we obtained at least one R ≈ 2000 R\approx 2000 optical spectrum with which we measure chromospheric H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha emission, a proxy for magnetic field strength. The fraction of active stars is consistent with what is expected for field M dwarfs; as in previous studies, we find that late-type M dwarfs remain active for longer than their early type counterparts. While the TESS light curves for ≈ \approx 20% of our targets show modulations consistent with rotation, TESS systematics are not well enough understood for confident measurements of rotation periods ( P rot P_{\mathrm{rot}} ) longer than half the length of an observing sector. We report periods for 12 stars for which we measure P rot P_{\mathrm{rot}} ∼ < \mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}} 15 d or find confirmation for the TESS-derived P rot P_{\mathrm{rot}} in the literature. Our sample of 21 P rot P_{\mathrm{rot}} , which includes periods from the literature, is consistent with our targets being spun-down field stars. Finally, we examine the H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha -to-bolometric luminosity distribution for our sample. Two stars are rotating fast enough to be magnetically saturated, but are not, hinting at the possibility that fast rotators may appear inactive in H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha .



I
Introduction

M dwarfs are the most promising targets for searches of Earth-sized transiting exoplanets. Not only are the photometric signals for a given planet size larger than around e.g., a solar-like star, but transits of planets in the stellar habitable zone are more likely and more frequent
(
Gould et al. 2003
)
. Furthermore, based on results from the Kepler mission
(
Borucki et al. 2010
)
, such relatively close-orbiting planets are very common around M dwarfs
(
Dressing & Charbonneau 2015
;
Muirhead et al. 2015
)
.

Striking examples of Earth-sized planets found orbiting late-type dwarfs by recent surveys include the seven orbiting the M8 star TRAPPIST-1
(
Gillon et al. 2017
)
and the 1.32 R
⊕
planet transiting LHS 3844, another M dwarf
(
Vanderspek et al. 2019
)
, found in data from the first month of observations by the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite
(
Ricker et al. 2015
, TESS;)
. To constrain the evolution and habitability of these planets, however, we need to understand the magnetic activity of their parent stars today and in the past.

For example,
Garraffo et al. 2017
and
Vida et al. 2017
found that flaring and winds from TRAPPIST-1, by potentially stripping the atmospheres of its planets, may have made these planets unsuitable for the development of life. By contrast,
Glazier et al. 2020
argued that the flare rate in TRAPPIST-1 is insufficient to catalyze chemical pathways thought to be required for ribonucleic acid synthesis, a key step in the chemical history of Earth. More generally, the flare duty cycle is an example of stellar magnetic activity we need to understand, as it is thought to play a critical roll in the stability and/or depletion of Earth-like atmospheres around old M dwarfs
(
Tilley et al. 2019
;
France et al. 2020
)
.

The activity–rotation relation is a powerful tool for exploring the magnetic behavior of late-type stars. In these stars, the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity (
L_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}
), produced by magnetic heating of the chromosphere, increases as a fraction of its bolometric luminosity (
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
) with faster rotation. But this is true only up to a threshold velocity: stars rotating faster than this velocity show no further increase in activity levels
(
Noyes et al. 1984
)
, and are said to be magnetically saturated. Several theories have been advanced to explain this behavior, including changes in the magnetic dynamo
(
Collier Cameron & Jianke 1994
, e.g.,)
or in the topology of the magnetic field
(
Garraffo et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. Crucially, however, the exact mechanism driving the activity–rotation relation remains unknown, whether in the saturated or in the unsaturated regime.

The activity–rotation relation has been studied with samples encompassing a large range of stellar masses and rotational velocities
(
Delfosse et al. 1998
;
Jackson & Jeffries 2010
;
Douglas et al. 2014
;
Núñez et al. 2015
;
Newton et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. It remains challenging, however, to characterize this relation in the case of slower, less active rotators. Many studies focus on the stars in nearby, generally young (
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}
1 Gyr) open clusters
(
Jackson & Jeffries 2010
;
Douglas et al. 2019
;
Rampalli et al. 2021
, e.g., the
\approx
150-Myr-old NGC 2516 or the
\approx
700-Myr-old Hyades and Praesepe;)
, where the periods are typically
\leq
20-25 d and the later-type stars tend to have strong
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission. The field dwarfs we need to fill in our picture of the activity–rotation relation, in the regime that applies to planet hosts such as TRAPPIST-1, are presumably older and slower, with rotation periods that are more challenging to measure because of the long baseline photometric observations required. TESS data are an unparalleled opportunity to obtain
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
for these older, slow-rotating low-mass stars that are more representative of the overall Galactic population. TESS has a higher sensitivity to the redder spectra of later-type stars than its predecessor Kepler/K2
(
Basri et al. 2005
;
Ricker et al. 2015
)
. In addition, TESS spent its first year (2018 July–2019 July) monitoring the southern ecliptic pole Continuous Viewing Zone (CVZ) for 351 d, and has since then returned to observing the southern hemisphere. While it is currently challenging to confidently measure
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
longer than
\approx
15 d from TESS light curves alone, eventually it should be possible to use the resulting photometry, whether on its own or with that from other surveys
(
Bellm et al. 2019
, e.g., the Zwicky Transient Facility in the north;)
to reliably measure very long periods for TESS targets.

We have conducted a spectroscopic survey of 125 M dwarfs within 80 pc and located in the southern CVZ, obtaining at least one optical spectrum with which to measure the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line strength for 122 of these stars.
Gunning et al. 2014
found that the scatter in
\mathrm{H}\alpha
measurements for a group of field mid-M dwarfs was comparable to the full range of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
measurements exhibited by a single star. This implies that measuring
\mathrm{H}\alpha
only once may not capture the characteristic activity level of a single star, and that it is important to make multiple measurements to understand more fully the uncertainty on any individual measurement. We therefore obtained more than one spectrum for almost two-thirds of our stars, and 50% have at least four spectra. As the TESS CVZs overlap with e.g., the CVZs for the James Webb Space Telescope, our targets constitute a legacy sample for anyone interested in the properties of nearby field M dwarfs and/or of their exoplanets.

In Section
II
, we describe the spectroscopic and photometric data for our sample of M dwarfs. In Section
III
, we describe our
\mathrm{H}\alpha
and rotation-related measurements, from which we derive
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
values and Rossby numbers for our stars. In Section
IV
, we present our main results:
\mathrm{H}\alpha
equivalent-width measurements as a function of spectral type, and
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
as a function of Rossby number, for our targets. In Section
V
, we summarize our results and conclude.

II.1
Assembling our Sample

We used the TESS input catalog Version 8
(
Stassun et al. 2019

to build our volume-limited sample of late-type dwarfs. First, we selected stars with masses
\leq
0.8
{M_{\odot}}

∘

Stassun et al. 2019
in their specially curated Cool Dwarf list. The lowest mass object selected in this manner has a mass of 0.13
{M_{\odot}}
. Next, we searched for Gaia Early Data Release 3
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2021
, EDR3;)
counterparts to stars in this sample. We then adopted the EDR3-based distances of
Bailer-Jones et al. 2021
for the stars and, with these updated distances, recalculated their masses, using
the
Mann et al. 2019
mass–absolute
K
magnitude (
M_{K}
) relation
(
Stassun et al. 2019
, as did)
, which is valid for
M_{K}
between 4 and 10 mag. Finally, we selected all dwarfs from our initial sample with recalculated masses
\leq
0.6
{M_{\odot}}
and within 80 pc.

The resulting catalog contains 125 dwarfs with masses between 0.11 and 0.60
{M_{\odot}}
. Figure
1

Stassun et al. 2019
did not attempt to identify potential binaries in the TICv8, and instead treated all objects as single stars when determining their properties. However, identifying these systems in our sample is essential, as members of binary and higher-order systems can have very different evolutionary histories that result in shorter
P_{\mathrm{rot}}

(
Meibom et al. 2007
;
Douglas et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
and potentially higher levels of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
activity than single stars of the same mass and age.

To help us identify potential binaries and higher-order systems, we used the Gaia EDR3 renormalized unit weight error (RUWE). The RUWE is a goodness-of-fit measure of the single-star model fit to the source’s astrometry. When a star has a RUWE
>1.2
, there is a strong likelihood it has a companion, and the system is usually an unresolved binary (e.g.,
Jorissen 2019
;
Belokurov et al. 2020
). The Gaia spatial resolving limit of
\approx
0
\farcs
7
(
Ziegler et al. 2018
)
corresponds to a semimajor axis
a\approx 6
au for the nearest candidate binary in our sample, and
\approx
54 au for the farthest. Stars in our sample with RUWE
>1.2
are indeed likely to be binaries with small enough separations for their components to have affected each other’s protoplanetary disks in the first 10 Myr of their lifetimes
(
Rebull et al. 2006
;
Meibom et al. 2007
;
Kraus et al. 2016
;
Messina et al. 2017
)
. This underlines the need to consider the observed properties of these stars separately from those of the likely single stars in our sample.

II.3
Optical Light Curves

We extracted TESS photometric data using publicly released full-frame images (FFIs) to create 30-minute cadence light curves for all 125 stars in our sample. First, we downloaded a
21\times 21
pixel cutout image centered on each target via the
TESScut
tool on the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes server
(
Brasseur et al. 2019
)
.
We used a circular aperture of radius 2 pixels around the object to perform the photometric extraction. We estimated the background signal to be subtracted from the light curve through a sigma (
\sigma
) clip over the entire FFI cutout using the
photutils
algorithm
(
Bradley et al. 2016
)
.
The flux for each TESS sector (corresponding to a 27.4 d observing window) was divided by the median to normalize and, consequently, preserve the light curve variability. We performed all these steps using the
tesseract
pipeline
6
6

6

https://github.com/astrofelipe/tesseract
. Finally, we stitched together data for all the available sectors from the first year of TESS operations for each of our targets.

The resulting light curves were carefully processed to remove data affected by systematic effects that clearly impacted their quality. For a fraction of the stars, we identified abrupt flux increases or decreases at the beginning and/or end of some sectors, such as the side the gaps created when the data were being sent to Earth. We attempted to mitigate these effects by discarding photometry offset from the median by more than 5
\sigma
. Even after this 5
\sigma
-clipping, however, there were still obvious systematics present in the data. Because of these issues, we also used the Causal Pixel Modeling approach
(
Hattori et al. 2021
, CPM;)
to re-extract the light curves and confirm the results reported in Section
III.1
.

\approx

III
Rotational Properties and
\mathrm{H}\alpha
measurements of Our Targets

III.1
Measuring Rotation Periods

We used the generalized Lomb–Scargle
(
Zechmeister & Kürster 2009
, GLS;)
periodogram to measure
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
from our TESS light curves. The GLS periodogram takes into account measurement errors when finding the best fit to the data, and generally avoids aliasing in the period determination, and can therefore be considered superior to the standard Lomb-Scargle approach
(
VanderPlas 2018
;
Reinhold & Hekker 2020
)
. We used the false alarm probability (FAP) to assess the significance of the power of the periodogram peaks, considering only
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
for periodograms with a FAP
<
1%. We obtained
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
measurements for 24 out of 125 stars in our intial sample, which represents
\approx
20% of the total.

However, as was highlighted recently by
Claytor et al. 2021
, there are fundamental issues with trying to measure longer
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
with TESS light curves produced by stitching together data from several 27-d sectors. In the resulting light curves, it is difficult to assess whether a signal is caused by true fluctuations in stellar brightness, or whether it is a result of systematics in the data either incorrectly removed or added in the processing of the light curves. It was noticeable, and disconcerting, that we found a number of stars for which the apparent flux modulations (and resulting
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
measurements) coincided with the length of a sector.

As a result, our position is that confidently measuring periods for slow rotators (defined here as having
P_{\mathrm{rot}}

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}
15 d) based only on TESS light curves is challenging, and that reporting these
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
is potentially misleading. We therefore initially limited our TESS-derived sample of
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
to those stars for which
P_{\mathrm{rot}}

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}
15 d. With this restriction, our final sample of reliable
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
from TESS light curves is 12 stars. For each of these objects, we include in the two figure sets in the Appendix the
21\times 21
pixel cutout of the TESS FFI used to extract the light curve, the extracted light curve, the GLS periodogram, and the phase-folded light curve.

Given these limitations in TESS data, we searched the literature for other measurements of
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
for stars in our sample. We found six slow-rotating stars whose periods were inferred by
Astudillo-Defru et al. 2017
by applying an empirical relation between the strength of the Ca II H and K emission lines, another tracer of magnetic activity, and the
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
of M dwarfs. Additionally, we found one rotator in the MEarth Project
(
Berta et al. 2012
)
database
7
7

7

https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/MEarth/DR10/
that is also in our sample, and we used the MEarth
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
to confirm our TESS-derived period of 14.34 d for that star. Finally, we found two stars in our sample with long rotation periods extracted from Evryscope
(
Ratzloff et al. 2019
)
light curves by
Howard et al. 2020
. These two stars are the ones shown in Panel (b) of Figure
4
, and we adopted the Evryscope
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
for them. In this fashion, we increased the size of our sample of rotators to 21 stars.

III.2
Measuring H
\alpha
EWs and Estimating Quiescent EWs

We used the code
PHEW

(
Núñez et al. 2022
)
, which uses
PySpecKit

(
Ginsburg & Mirocha 2011
)
to fit lines with a Voigt profile, to measure automatically the equivalent width (EW) of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line, and to estimate the corresponding EW uncertainty,
for all of our spectra.

We interactively defined continuum regions blueward and redward of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line in each spectrum. These continuum regions varied in length between 5 and 35 Å on each side, but the total length of the continuum considered was never less than 20 Å.

We extracted the noise for each point in our spectra from a Gaussian width equal to the
\sigma
of the flux in the continuum regions defined for the individual spectra.

PHEW
performed 1000 Monte Carlo iterations adding this noise to the spectrum and calculated the mean and
\sigma
of the 1000 resulting EWs, which we assigned as the EW value and 1
\sigma
uncertainty for that spectrum. When a star had multiple spectra, we took the error-weighted mean EW and the weighted mean standard error to be the EW value and 1
\sigma
uncertainty for that star. The overwhelming majority of the stars in our sample with multiple spectra showed little evidence of variability in the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW we measured from their spectra.

We measured an
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW value for 133 of the 551 spectra in our sample. For the other 438, the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line was indistinguishable from zero.
Our EW values range between
-11.5
and 1.1 Å.
The smallest value we could measure for a star with
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission was EW
=-0.5
Å.
For the spectra with
\mathrm{H}\alpha
indistinguishable from zero, we assigned EW = 0 Å and a systematic EW 1
\sigma
uncertainty of 0.5 Å.

The eight stars in our sample with high-resolution HARPS spectra also have low-resolution (GMOS or SOAR) spectra. In six of these stars, the low-resolution spectra exhibited an
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line indistinguishable from zero, whereas the high-resolution spectra showed a measurable
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line in absorption, covering the EW range 0.2-0.5 Å. The lack of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
absorption in the GMOS/SOAR spectra for these six stars reflects the fact that below
\approx
0.5 Å, we reach the smallest resolvable wavelength difference in these spectra. Still, in the other two stars, both low- and high-resolution spectra showed
\mathrm{H}\alpha

III.3
Obtaining
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
is a measure of the fractional contribution of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission relative to the entire energy output of the star, and is therefore a more useful indicator of chromospheric activity than
L_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}
when comparing stars spanning a range of masses.

We obtained
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
for stars with
\mathrm{H}\alpha
in emission by using the relation

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}/L_{\mathrm{bol}}=-W_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\frac{f_{0}}{f_{\mathrm{bol}}},

(1)

where
W_{\mathrm{H}_{\alpha}}
is the relative
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW,
f_{0}
is the continuum flux near the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line, and
f_{\mathrm{bol}}
is the apparent bolometric flux. Empirically, one can calculate
f_{0}/f_{\mathrm{bol}}
, known as
\chi
, as a function of color.
Douglas et al. 2014
derived a relation between
\chi
and color using spectra from the
Phoenix Aces
model spectra
(
Husser et al. 2013
)
with solar metallicity, log(
g
) = 5.0, and 2500
\leq T_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq
5200 K.

We used the log(
\chi
)–(
r^{\prime}-K
) relation in
Douglas et al. 2014
to derive
\chi
values for our stars. We obtained
r^{\prime}
photometry from the AAVSO Photometric All Sky Survey
(
Henden et al. 2016
, APASS;)
, and
K
photometry from the Two Micron All Sky Survey
(
Skrutskie et al. 2006
, 2MASS;)
. For the 20 stars in our sample with no APASS photometry, we used
V
photometry from the TICv8 catalog and the
V
–(
J-K
)–
r^{\prime}
relation from
Dymock & Miles 2009
to estimate
r^{\prime}
. The
K
and
r^{\prime}
photometry we used for each star is included in Table
1
.

We did not calculate
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
for stars with EW uncertainties large enough to cross over into potential
\mathrm{H}\alpha
absorption. This was the case for all stars for which we assigned an EW = 0 Å.

III.4
Calculating Rossby Numbers

The scatter in the activity–rotation relationship is significantly smaller if rotation is parameterized in the form of the Rossby number, defined as
R_{\mathrm{o}}=P_{\mathrm{rot}}/\tau
, where
\tau
is the convective turnover time
(
Noyes et al. 1984
;
Wright et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
. We estimated
\tau
values for our stars using the empirical mass–log(
\tau
) relation of
Wright et al. 2018
, which is based on
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
and X-ray luminosity measurements for almost 850 stars in the mass range 0.08–1.36
{M_{\odot}}
. With those
\tau
values, we calculated
R_{\mathrm{o}}
for the 21 stars in our sample with a measured
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
. Table
1
includes the
\tau
values we calculated for our stars.

IV
Results and Discussion

IV.1
H
\alpha
EW Distribution

The top panel of Figure
7
shows the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW as function of Gaia color and spectral type for the 122 stars in our sample with spectroscopy. We used the
Kiman et al. 2021
definition for the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW boundary as a function of (
G-G_{\mathrm{RP}}
) between active and inactive stars. Stars earlier than M3 appear largely to be inactive, while a larger portion of stars later than that spectral type—corresponding to the transition between Sun-like and fully convective stars
(
Chabrier & Baraffe 1997
)
—are active. This behavior is consistent with the assumption that a sample of field stars should generally be older than a few Gyr. Analyses of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
activity in younger single-aged populations such as Praesepe and the Hyades find that the early M dwarfs are active
(
Douglas et al. 2014
, see, e.g., figure 8 of)
but, by
\approx
1 Gyr, they no longer show evidence for
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission
(
Agüeros et al. 2018
, e.g., the M0-M2 stars in the
\approx
1.3-Gyr-old open cluster NGC 752; see figure 11 of)
.

In Figure
7
, we indicate with orange triangles stars in our sample with RUWE
>
1.2, and thus suspected of being binaries. While there does not seem to be a difference between the overall behavior of potential binaries compared to single stars in this plot, we do note more elevated levels of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in some of the later-type potential binaries. Typically, magnetic activity, and hence
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission, can be increased by the presence of accretion disks
(
Muzerolle et al. 1998
;
Biazzo et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
or by interactions between members of close binaries
(
Morgan et al. 2012
;
Skinner et al. 2017
)
. Although accretion disks have long disappeared at the expected ages of the field stars in our sample
(
Haisch et al. 2001
;
Lee et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, the higher
\mathrm{H}\alpha
levels for stars with high RUWE may be interpreted as evidence of enhanced magnetic activity due to a nearby companion. As mentioned in Section
II.1
, the stars in our sample with high RUWE are likely interacting binaries, potentially resulting in increased activity levels relative to single stars of the same mass. However, a binary identification based on RUWE alone would miss tight (
a~{\lower 3.44444pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}$}}~0.1
au), tidally interacting binaries
(
Belokurov et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. And the enhanced activity in our stars with high RUWE could be driven instead (or additionally) by the strong magnetic interactions in such compact stellar systems. Further observations are required to understand whether stars in our sample with high RUWE have additional, hidden companions.

To provide more context for these data, the bottom panel of Figure
7
shows the active fraction of stars in our sample as a function of the binned Gaia color (black squares). As in
Kiman et al. 2021
, we calculated uncertainties for each bin, based on a binomial distribution, as
\sigma_{f}=(f\times(1-f))/n
, where
f
is the active fraction and
n
is the bin size.
We compared the active fraction of our sample with that of more than
7\times 10^{4}
Sloan Digital Sky Survey
(
York et al. 2000
, SDSS;)
M dwarfs from
West et al. 2011
.
8
8

8

Kiman et al. 2021
did not correct their
\mathrm{H}\alpha
data to account for the basal absorption in inactive stars. We therefore used our measured EW values for this comparison instead of our calculated relative EW values.
The fraction of active stars in our sample is consistent with what is expected for field (i.e.,
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}
1 Gyr) M dwarfs: close to zero for early M dwarfs, and
\approx
0.4 for mid-M dwarfs. As in previous studies of M dwarfs
(
West et al. 2004
;
West et al. 2011
;
Schmidt et al. 2015
;
Kiman et al. 2021
)
, we conclude that later type M dwarfs remain active for longer than their earlier brethren.

IV.2
Activity–Rotation Relation

In Figure
8
, we plot
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
as a function of
R_{\mathrm{o}}
for the stars in our sample with a
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
measurement (see Section
III.3
). For context, we also show the large sample of nearby M dwarfs of
Newton et al. 2017
. These authors’ sample contains more than 400 stars with
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
values, nearly all of which were measured from MEarth light curves, as well as
\mathrm{H}\alpha
relative EW values, i.e., corrected for quiescent
\mathrm{H}\alpha
absorption (see Section
III.2
). The
Newton et al. 2017
sample exhibits a typical saturated/unsaturated behavior
(
Mohanty & Basri 2003
;
Reiners et al. 2012
;
Douglas et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
: stars with
R_{\mathrm{o}}\ {\lower 3.44444pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{>}}$}}\ 0.2
show a power-law dependence of
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
on rotation, while stars with a
R_{\mathrm{o}}
below this threshold are saturated, with
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}

\approx
constant and independent of rotation. Our stars follow the same distribution as the
Newton et al. 2017
sample.

We also include
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
upper limits for stars with
\mathrm{H}\alpha
indistinguishable from zero in our spectra; for these stars, we assumed an EW upper limit of
-0.5
Å to estimate their
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
upper limit. As can be seen in Figure
8
, two of our
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
upper limits fall almost one order of magnitude below the typical value for their
R_{\mathrm{o}}\approx 0.1
. These M dwarfs are rotating fast enough that they should be magnetically saturated, and yet they are not. While
Newton et al. 2017
showed clearly that M dwarfs without detectable
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission are slow rotators, the contrapositive remained unanswered: are all fast rotators
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emitters?

The answer appears to be no, although the number of examples of fast-rotating M dwarfs that do not show
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission is small.
Newton et al. 2017
found one fast-rotating,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-inactive star in their sample. Earlier,
West & Basri 2009
identified three fast rotators in a sample of 14 M6-M7 dwarfs selected because of their unusually low levels of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission in SDSS spectra. There are therefore intriguing suggestions that some fast-rotating M dwarfs may appear inactive in
\mathrm{H}\alpha
. Follow-up X-ray observations of these stars to measure their level of coronal activity, perhaps with the eROSITA instrument aboard the Spectrum-Roentgen-Gamma mission
(
Sunyaev et al. 2021
)
, could help to determine whether these unusual stars are truly magnetically inactive.

Lastly, we see in Figure
8
no distinct behavior for stars in our sample with RUWE
>
1.2. If these stars are indeed unresolved binaries, the fact that their
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
are comparable to the
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
of stars with similar
R_{\mathrm{o}}
in our sample and in the
Newton et al. 2017
sample indicates that their
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
measurements are likely dominated by the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission of only one of the binary components.

Our ability to contribute data to this plot is currently limited by the small number of stars for which we can confidently measure
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
with TESS data. These are primarily fast rotators that appear saturated in this plot. However, there are reasons to be optimistic about our ability to overcome the systematic problems arising from stitching TESS sectors. One promising approach has been to combine ground and space data
(
Andrews et al. 2021
;
Howard et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. With the hoped-for improvements in our confidence in measuring longer
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
from TESS light curves (or by combining TESS light curves with data from other surveys), we aim to eventually add more points to the sample of unsaturated stars shown in Figure
8
.

V
Conclusions

We have conducted a spectroscopic survey of M dwarfs located in the southern TESS CVZ and within 80 pc of the Sun. We used the TICv8
(
Stassun et al. 2019
)
to build our volume-limited sample of 125 stars, and, through a combination of observations with SOAR/Goodman and Gemini-South/GMOS and archival ESO/HARPS data, obtained at least one optical spectrum with which to measure the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line strength for 122 of these stars.

Because measuring
\mathrm{H}\alpha
only once may not capture the characteristic activity level of a single star
(
Gunning et al. 2014
)
, it is important to make multiple measurements to understand more fully the uncertainty on any individual measurement. We therefore obtained more than one spectrum for almost two thirds of our targets. These reduced spectra are publicly available from the Columbia University Academic Commons, an online research repository
9
9

9

Available on the Columbia Academic Commons:
https://doi.org/10.7916/d8-xg58-9x44 (catalog doi:10.7916/d8-xg58-9x44)
.
.

We measured the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
EW for all of the spectra in our sample. For stars with multiple spectra, we took the error-weighted mean EW and the weighted mean standard error as the representative EW and uncertainty. For the majority of our stars, we found an
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line strength indistinguishable from zero, and in the cases where we had more than one spectra, no evidence for variability in the measured line strength.

We calculated quiescent absorption EW values for our stars using the
Newton et al. 2017
model and then determined the relative EW for each star by combining the quiescent and the measured EW values. Stars earlier than M3 in our sample appear largely inactive, while a larger fraction of stars later than that spectral type are active. This behavior is consistent with the assumption that a sample of field stars should be older than a few Gyr. We also compared the active fraction of our sample with that of a large sample of M dwarfs with SDSS spectra
(
West et al. 2011
)
. The fraction of active stars in our sample is consistent with what is expected for field M dwarfs.

We generated photometric light curves for all of our targets using the publicly released TESS FFIs. For each star, the flux for each TESS sector was divided by the median value for that sector, and we then stitched together data for all the available sectors. The resulting, nearly year-long light curves were processed to remove data affected by systematics. This processing, however, is still far from perfect, with TESS systematics not yet well enough understood for confident measurement of
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
longer than roughly half the length of a sector, or
\approx
15 d
(
Claytor et al. 2021
)
.

While about one-fifth of the stars in our sample exhibit modulations consistent with rotation, we report TESS periods only for those stars for which we either measure
P_{\mathrm{rot}}

\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{<}}
15 d with TESS data alone, or for which we find confirmation for the TESS-derived
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
in the literature. There are 12 stars in the former category, and another nine whose periods we confirm or obtain using the literature. Comparing our sample to rotators in the
\approx
700 Myr-old Praesepe cluster underlines the difference in the ages of the two samples: the majority of the likely single stars in our sample have longer
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
than the stars that define the slow rotating sequence for K7 to M3.5 stars in Praesepe. This is consistent with our sample containing older, spun-down field stars.

We estimated
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
and
R_{\mathrm{o}}
for our stars and compared these to the distribution of field M dwarfs assembled by
Newton et al. 2017
. Our ability to contribute data to this distribution is currently hindered by the small number of stars for which we can confidently measure
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
with TESS data. These are a handful of fast and slow rotators that are either magnetically saturated, with
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}

\approx
10
-4
for the stars with
R_{\mathrm{o}}

<
0.2, or that exhibit an activity level consistent with what is expected given their larger
R_{\mathrm{o}}
. Two stars in our sample with
R_{\mathrm{o}}

<
0.2 only have
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
/
L_{\mathrm{bol}}
upper limits. Together with a handful of other similar outliers in the literature, they hint at the possibility that fast rotators may appear inactive in
\mathrm{H}\alpha
. Thus, follow-up observations, e.g., of their coronal X-ray emission, are necessary to determine whether these are truly rapidly rotating, but magnetically inactive, M dwarfs.

The full value of TESS data will be realized when we learn to combine TESS sectors to obtain confident
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
measurements for the slowest rotators. With the hoped-for improvements in our confidence in measuring longer
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
from TESS light curves, we aim eventually to add more points to the sample of unsaturated stars. Our combination of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
and
P_{\mathrm{rot}}
measurements can potentially help constrain the activity-rotation relation for the slowest rotators, as TESS will produce some of its longest-baseline light curves, spread over several years of its observing campaigns, for this sample of M dwarfs.
