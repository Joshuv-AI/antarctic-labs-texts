Decreasing paths of polygons
Isaac Kulp, Charlotte Ochanine, Logan Richard, Leonel Robert, Scott Whitman

Abstract
. We call a continuous path of polygons decreasing if the convex hulls of the polygons form a decreasing family of sets. For an arbitrary polygon of more than three vertices, we characterize the polygons contained in it that can be reached by a decreasing path (attainability problem), and we show that this can be done by a finite application of “pull-in" moves (bang-bang problem). In the case of triangles, this problems was investigated by Goodman, Johansen, Ramsey, and Frydman among others, in connection with the embeddability problem for non-homogeneous Markov processes.

The special case of this problem concerning triangles (
n=3
) has previously been investigated in the context of the embeddability problem for stochastic matrices. The embeddability problem seeks to determine whether a given row stochastic matrix
D
can be obtained as a transition matrix in a (non-homogeneous) Markov process
\{D(t,t^{\prime}):0\leqslant t\leqslant t^{\prime}<\infty\}
(
[
4
]
). The connection between the attainability and embeddability problems goes roughly as follows: To a decreasing path of non-degenerate triangles
[0,t_{0}]\ni t\mapsto(a_{t},b_{t},c_{t})
we can make correspond a continuous-time non-homogeneous Markov process with 3 states whose transition matrices are derived by expressing the vertices of a triangle in the path as convex combinations of the vertices of earlier triangles in the path (see Section
4
). This correspondence extends more generally to decreasing paths of
n
-dimensional simplices and Markov processes with
n
states; see
[
5
]
. In the case of
3\times 3
stochastic matrices, the geometric reformulation of the embeddability problem precisely leads to the attainability problem for triangles. Johansen and Ramsey made use of this reformulation to investigate the embeddability problem for Markov processes of 3 states (
[
6
]
).

We obtain a complete solution of the Attainability Problem for
n\geqslant 4
. The problem remains open for
n\geqslant 3
.

One way to create decreasing paths of polygons is to use pull-in moves. Given a polygon
P=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and two distinct indices
i
and
j
, a pull-in of vertex
p_{i}
toward
p_{j}
results in a polygon
P^{\prime}
whose vertices agree with the vertices of
P
except for vertex
p_{i}^{\prime}
, which is chosen anywhere on the segment
[p_{i},p_{j}]
. Clearly, if
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by a pull-in move, there is a decreasing path going from
P
to
P^{\prime}
. Thus, if
P^{\prime}
can be obtained from
P
by a finite application of pull-in moves, then
P^{\prime}
is attainable from
P
.

Bang-Bang Problem
.

Is every attainable polygon
P^{\prime}
contained in

attainable in finitely many pull-in moves? If so, is there an upper bound on the number of moves needed?

Johansen and Ramsey showed in

that an attainable triangle is also attainable by finitely many pull-in moves, but did not obtain an upper bound on the number of moves needed. An example by Frydman shows that this number is at least

(

). We solve here the Bang-Bang Problem affirmatively for

.

Theorem 1.1

(Bang-Bang Theorem)
.

Let
P
be an
n
-gon. The following are true:

(1)

Any
n
-gon degenerately contained in
P
is attainable from

in fewer than

(2)

For

, any

The number

Equipped with the Poncelet map and the broken line construction (BLC), we obtain an effective way of testing for degenerate containment of
P^{\prime}
in
P
and we formulate a description of the set of polygons attainable from
P
. We describe these results here in general terms.

To test for the degenerate containment of
P^{\prime}
in
P
we ask that the BLC stops before
n
steps when its initial point
x
is chosen from a finite set of test points (Theorem
8.3
).

In order to describe the set of attainable polygons we first need to introduce two of its subsets: the threshold and the vestibule. A polygon
P^{\prime}
contained in
P
is said to be in the
threshold region
if it is attainable from
P
and at least one of its vertices belongs to
\partial P
. In Theorem
9.4
we characterize the polygons in the threshold and non-degenerately contained in
P
in terms of the BLC. We further show that such polygons can be attained from
P
in at most
2n-1
pull-in moves. (For triangles, this result was essentially obtained by Frydman in
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, albeit not stated in this way).
A polygon
P^{\prime}
is said to be in the
vestibule region
if it is an attainable polygon obtained from a polygon in the threshold by one pull-in move, i.e., it is one pull-in move away from a polygon attainable from
P
and with at least one vertex in
\partial P
.

Theorem 1.2

(Attainability Theorem)
.

Let
n\geqslant 4
and let
P
be a convex
n
-gon. The set of polygons attainable from
P
coincides with the set
\mathcal{D}_{P}\cup\mathcal{V}_{P}
of
n
-gons that are either degenerately contained in
P
or in the vestibule region.

This result, combined with effective ways of testing whether a polygon is degenerately contained in
P
and in the vestibule region (Theorems
8.3
and
9.4
), solves the Attainability Problem for
n\geqslant 4
.
In contrast, Frydman’s example of a triangle attainable in 7 moves and no less does not belong to
\mathcal{D}_{P}\cup\mathcal{V}_{P}
. Thus, the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
fails for
n=3
.

2.
Decreasing paths of polygons

Let
n\geqslant 3
. By an
n
-gon, or polygon, we mean an ordered
n
-tuple
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
are called vertices of the polygon.
We denote the set of
n
-gons by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
(thus,
\mathcal{P}_{n}=(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}
). Note that in the sense that we use the term polygon here a vertex may appear multiple times in a polygon.

We will find it convenient to use arithmetic modulo
n
in the index set of an
n
-gon, so that
p_{n+1}=p_{1}
and the list
i,i+1,\ldots,i+n-1
runs through all indices for any
i
.

Given points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
[a,b]
the segment connecting them and including them as endpoints. We follow standard convention for half-open and open segments, e.g.,
[a,b)
denotes
[a,b]\backslash\{b\}
.

Let
P=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an
n
-gon.

•

We call the segments
[p_{1},p_{2}],[p_{2},p_{3}],\ldots,[p_{n},p_{1}]
the edges of
P
. We denote by
\partial P
the union of the edges of
P
.

•

We denote by
\mathrm{co}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
the convex hull of the vertices of
P
.

•

We call
P

set-convex
if none of its vertices is a convex combination of the other ones. Equivalently, if all vertices of
P
are distinct extreme points of
\mathrm{co}(P)
.

•

We call
P
a
simple polygon
if
\partial P
forms a simple closed curve. In this case, we say that
P
is
oriented counterclockwise
, if its vertices

p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}^{\prime}
follow the counterclockwise order of
\partial P
, and it is
oriented clockwise
if its vertices follow the clockwise order
of
\partial P
.

•

We call
P
a
convex polygon
if it is both set-convex and simple.

We define on the set of polygons the following preorder relation:

Definition 2.1
.

Given polygons
P
and
Q
(possibly with different numbers of vertices),
we say that
P
is contained in
Q
, denoted by
P\preccurlyeq Q
, if
\mathrm{co}(P)\subseteq\mathrm{co}(Q)
.

Let
\{P(t):0\leqslant t\leqslant c\}
be a continuous path in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. (The topology in
\mathcal{P}_{n}=(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}
is always assumed to be the standard one.)
We call
P(\cdot)
a
decreasing path
if it is decreasing in the
preorder
\preccurlyeq
, i.e.,
P(t^{\prime})\preccurlyeq P(t)
for all
t^{\prime}\geqslant t
.

Given
n
-gons
P
and
P^{\prime}
, with
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq P
, we say that
P^{\prime}

is
attainable
from
P
if there exists a decreasing path in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
starting at
P
and ending at
P^{\prime}
.

Let
P=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an
n
-gon. Let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
be distinct indices. A
pull-in move
on
P
of vertex
p_{i}
toward vertex
p_{j}
consists in replacing
p_{i}
by a point
p_{i}^{\prime}
in the segment
[p_{i},p_{j}]
, while leaving all other vertices of
P
unchanged. The result of a pull-in move is an
n
-gon
P^{\prime}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,p_{n})
, where
p_{i}^{\prime}=(1-c)p_{i}+cp_{j}
for some
0\leqslant c\leqslant 1
. We call
c
the parameter of the move. Observe that
P^{\prime}
is attainable from
P
by a decreasing path:

[0,c]\ni t\mapsto(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1},(1-t)p_{i}+tp_{j},p_{i+1},\ldots,p_{n}).

Thus, if a polygon is attainable from
P
by the application of finitely many pull-in moves, then it is also attainable from
P
by a decreasing path.

The inverse of a pull-in move is called a push-out move. More concretely, a push-out of vertex
p_{i}
by vertex
p_{j}

results in a polygon
P^{\prime}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,p_{n})
, where
p_{i}\in[p_{j},p_{i}^{\prime}]
. Notice that in the special case when
p_{i}=p_{j}
the new vertex

p_{i}^{\prime}
can be chosen arbitrarily in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Let us regard an
n
-gon as an
n\times 2
matrix, the rows of the matrix simply being the vertices of the polygon.
Let
P^{\prime}
and
P
be
n
-gons such that
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq P
. Then we can write each vertex of
P^{\prime}
as a convex (not unique for
n\geqslant 4)
) combination of vertices of
P
. This can be expressed algebraically as

P^{\prime}=DP,

where
D
is an
n\times n
(row) stochastic matrix and
P^{\prime}
and
P
are regarded as
n\times 2
matrices. The converse is also clearly true: if
P^{\prime}=DP
, with
D
stochastic, then
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq P
.

Suppose that
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by a pull-in move, say, by the move of
p_{i}
towards
p_{j}
with parameter
c
. This can be expressed algebraically as
P^{\prime}=K^{ij}(c)P
, where
K^{ij}(c)
is the matrix differing from the
n\times n
identity matrix only in two entries of the
i
-th row:
k_{ii}=1-c
and
k_{ij}=c
. We call any such matrix an
elementary stochastic matrix
.
It is clear that
P^{\prime}
is attainable from
P
in finitely many pull-in moves if and only if
P^{\prime}=DP
for
D
expressible as a finite product of elementary stochastic matrices.

Lemma 2.2
.

A pull-in move of
p_{i}
toward
p_{j}
followed by a pull-in move of
p_{k}
toward
p_{l}
can always be expressed as
a pull-in move of
p_{k}
toward
p_{l}
followed by a pull-in move of
p_{i}
toward
p_{j}
, excepting the cases when

j=k
and
i\neq l
, or
i=l
and
j\neq k
.

Proof.

Expressing pull-in moves in terms of multiplication by elementary stochastic matrices, this boils down to showing that given
c
and
d
, there exist
c^{\prime}
and
d^{\prime}
such that

K^{kl}(d)K^{ij}(c)=K^{ij}(d^{\prime})K^{kl}(c^{\prime}).

If
i,j,k,l
are pairwise distinct, then
K^{kl}(d)K^{ij}(c)
is the matrix that agrees with the identity matrix except for the
ii
,
ij
,
kk
,
kl
-entries which are
1-c,c,1-d,d,
respectively. So
K^{ij}(c)
and
K^{kl}(d)
commute.

If
i=k
and
j=l
, then
K^{kl}(d)K^{ij}(c)=K^{ij}(c+d-cd).
So we again have commutativity of
K^{ij}(c)
with
K^{kl}(d)
.

Sometimes
c^{\prime},d^{\prime}\neq d,c
, for example when
i=l
and
j=k
. The full case when
i,j,k,l
are not pairwise distinct (i,e.,

p_{i},p_{j},p_{k},p_{l}
are vertices of a triangle) is handled in
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Degenerate polygons

Definition 3.1
.

Given
n
-gons
P
and
P^{\prime}
,
let us say that
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
, or simply degenerate in
P
, if there exists an interpolating
m
-gon
Q
between
P^{\prime}
and
P
, i.e.,

P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
, such that
m<n
.

Clearly, if
P^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq P^{\prime}
, and
P^{\prime}
is degenerate in
P
, then
P^{\prime\prime}
is degenerate in
P
. Observe also that if
P^{\prime}
is not set-convex and contained in
P
, then it is degenerate in
P
, as in this case we can choose
Q
to be the vertices of the convex hull of
P^{\prime}
. In the case that
P
itself is not set-convex, then
P
is degenerate in
P
, and so any
n
-gon
P^{\prime}
contained in
P
is degenerate in
P
as well.

For a fixed
n
-gon
P
we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{P}
the set of all
n
-gons degenerately contained in
P
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The set
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is compact.

Proof.

We can reformulate the existence of an
m
-gon
Q
such that
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
as the existence of row stochastic matrices

D
and
E
, of sizes
n\times m
and
m\times n
respectively, such that
P^{\prime}=DEP
.

Let
(P_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{D}_{P}
. Let us show the existence of a subsequence converging to an element of
\mathcal{D}_{P}
(thus proving compactness). Clearly we can pass to a subsequence, which we relabel as
(P_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, such that for a fixed
m<n
and all
k=1,2\ldots
we have
P_{k}^{\prime}=D_{k}E_{k}P
for row stochastic matrices
D_{k}
and
E_{k}
of sizes
n\times m
and
m\times n
, respectively. Using the compactness of the sets of row stochastic matrices of sizes
n\times m
and
m\times n
, we can assume, after passing again to subsequences and relabeling, that
D_{k}\to D
and
E_{k}\to E
entrywise. Then
P_{k}^{\prime}\to P^{\prime}
, with
P^{\prime}=DEP
, which in turn implies that
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{P}
.
∎

Our goal for the remainder of this section is to show that if
P^{\prime}
is degenerate in
P
, then it is attainable from
P
in fewer than
5n
pull-in moves (Theorem
3.7
). Before doing this, we prove several lemmas.

Let us introduce some terms that will be used below. Let
P
and
P^{\prime}
be polygons.

•

We say that a vertex
p_{i}
occupies a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
if it coincides with it, i.e.,
p_{i}=x
.

•

We call a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
a double point of
P
if there exist
i\neq j
such that
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
both occupy
x
, i.e.,
p_{i}=x=p_{j}
.

•

We say that a vertex
p_{i}^{\prime}
of
P^{\prime}
is stray on
\partial P
if
p_{i}^{\prime}\in\partial P
and
p_{i}^{\prime}
does not occupy a vertex of
P
.

•

We say that
p_{i}^{\prime}
is stranded on
\partial P
if it is stray on
\partial P
and no other vertices of
P^{\prime}

share the same edge with
p_{i}^{\prime}
.

•

We say that
P^{\prime}
is inscribed in
P
if all the vertices of
P^{\prime}
belong to
\partial P
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
P
be a convex
m
-gon. Let
P^{\prime}
be an
n
-gon inscribed in
P
with
n>m
. Then the vertices of
P^{\prime}
can be moved to the vertices of
P

(not necessarily surjectively) in fewer than
\frac{3n}{2}
push-out moves.

Proof.

Assume that not all vertices of
P^{\prime}
occupy vertices of
P
. To move vertices of
P^{\prime}
to vertices of
P
, we can proceed as follows. If a stray vertex
p_{i}^{\prime}
shares some edge
[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
with another vertex
p_{j}^{\prime}
, push
p_{i}^{\prime}
out with
p_{j}^{\prime}
onto either
p_{k}
or
p_{k+1}
.
Continue moving any vertices of
P^{\prime}
that are stray but not stranded to vertices of
P
until perhaps all remaining stray vertices of
P^{\prime}
are stranded. In this situation, we are guaranteed the existence of a vertex of
P
that is a double point of
P^{\prime}
.
Indeed, partitioning
\partial P
in half-open edges
\partial P=\bigsqcup_{l=1}^{m}[p_{l},p_{l+1})
, we see that at least one pair of vertices
p_{i}^{\prime},p_{j}^{\prime}
belong to the same half-open edge of
P
. Since neither of them is stray, they occupy the same vertex of
P
.
We can use this double point to unstrand any stray vertex of
P^{\prime}
. Say such a stray vertex exists and belongs to some edge
[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
. We first push
p_{i}^{\prime}
out onto
p_{k}
with
p_{j}^{\prime}
, then push the stray vertex onto
p_{k+1}
with
p_{i}^{\prime}
. We can continue to move any stranded vertices using double points until perhaps we again have unstranded stray vertices. Alternating between single push-out moves and use of double points, we can move all vertices of
P^{\prime}
to vertices of
P
.

Let’s estimate the number of moves in this algorithm.
Let
M
be the number of times single push-out moves are used to move unstranded stray vertices of
P^{\prime}
, and let
N
be the number of times that a double point is used to move stranded vertices of
P^{\prime}
. The total number of push-out moves in the algorithm is
M+2N
.
Let
s

4.
Decreasing paths and Markov processes

In this section we discuss the connection between decreasing paths of polygons and non-homogeneous Markov processes, and we prove a “chattering principle" type of result (Corollary
4.4
) that will be needed later on.

We start by reviewing the formulation of a continuous-times non-homogeneous Markov processes in
n
states as a parametrized collection of row stochastic matrices. We refer the reader
to
[
5
]
for further details.

By a continuous-time non-homogeneous Markov process of
n
states we understand a continuous map
(s,t)\mapsto D(s,t)
defined for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t<\infty
, taking values in the set of
n\times n
non-singular row stochastic matrices, and such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle D(r,t)

\displaystyle=D(r,s)D(s,t)\hbox{ for all $0\leqslant r\leqslant s\leqslant t$,}

(4.2)

\displaystyle D(t,t)

\displaystyle=I\hbox{ (the identity matrix) for all $t$}.

If the function
D(t)=D(0,t)
is Lipschitz, then it is in particular a.e. differentiable, and

\dot{D}(t)=D(t)Q(t)

where
Q(t)
is a measurable, a.e. bounded
intensity matrix
, i.e., a matrix whose rows add to 0 and whose off-diagonal entries are nonnegative. Conversely, if
t\mapsto Q(t)
is measurable, a.e. bounded on finite intervals, and such that
Q(t)
is an intensity matrix for all
t\geqslant 0
, then the ODE above with
D(0)=I
has a unique solution
D(t)
ranging in the nonsingular stochastic matrices (explicitly given by a Peano-Baker series). Upon setting
D(s,t)=D(s)^{-1}D(t)
for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
we obtain a Markov process.

We call a non-singular row stochastic matrix
D
embeddable if
D=D(s,t)
for some
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
and Markov process
\{D(s,t):0\leqslant s\leqslant t<\infty\}
.
The main result about embeddable stochastic matrices that we will use below is the following:

Theorem 4.1

(
[
7
]
*Theorem 1.9)
.

A nonsigular stochastic matrix
D
is embeddable if and only if it is a limit of products of elementary stochastic matrices.

We remark on two consequences of this theorem that will be used below:

(1)

a product of embeddable stochastic matrices is embeddable,

(2)

the set of embeddable stochastic matrices is closed in the set of nonsingular stochastic matrices.

In order to relate Markov processes to decreasing paths of polygons, we will find it convenient to first introduce a time-reversed form of the Markov process
D(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let
t_{0}>0
. Define

(4.3)

\displaystyle E(t,s)

\displaystyle=D(t_{0}-t,t_{0}-s)\hbox{ for $0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}$},

(4.4)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=E(t,0)\hbox{ for $0\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}$}.

Note that (
4.1
) in terms of
E(\cdot,\cdot)
becomes

E(t,r)=E(t,s)E(s,r),

for
t\geqslant s\geqslant r
.
If the function
t\mapsto E(t)
is Lipschitz, then

\dot{E}(t)=Q(t)E(t)

where
Q(t)
is a measurable, a.e. bounded intensity matrix. Conversely, if
t\mapsto Q(t)
is measurable, a.e. bounded on an interval
[0,t_{0}]
, and such that
Q(t)
is an intensity matrix for all
t
, then the ODE above with
E(0)=I
has a unique solution
t\mapsto E(t)
ranging in the nonsingular stochastic matrices. Upon setting
E(t,s)=E(t)E(s)^{-1}
and
D(s,t)=E(t_{0}-t,t_{0}-s)
we obtain a Markov process defined for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}
.

Now let
P
be an
n
-gon regarded as an
n\times 2
matrix, and define

P(t)=E(t)P\hbox{ for $0\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}$}.

This is a decreasing path of polygons starting at
P
. Indeed,
P(0)=E(0)P=P
and

P(t)=E(t)P=E(t,s)E(s)P=E(t,s)P(s)

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}
. This shows that
P(t)\preccurlyeq P(s)
for
t\geqslant s
.

Remark 4.2
.

In
[
4
]
, Goodman discusses the geometric interpretation of a Markov process as an increasing path of non-degenerate simplices. In the case of
n=3
, these are increasing paths of triangles.

Our goal in the remainder of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

be a convex

-gon oriented counterclockwise, and let

be an

-gon non-degenerately contained in

and attainable from

.

Let

and

be as in the previous theorem. Then

with

attainable from

Proof.

By the previous theorem,

,

, with each

, where

∎

Proof.

Let

.
Since Lipschitz functions are a.e. differentiable,
\dot{p}_{i}(t)
exists for almost all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
and
\dot{p}_{i}(t)
is a bounded measurable function bounded by the Lispchitz constant of

.
For each

such that
\dot{p}_{i}(t)
exists, let

and

be the unique scalars such that

\dot{p}_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}(t)(p_{i+1}(t)-p_{i}(t))+\beta_{i}(t)(p_{i-1}(t)-p_{i}(t)).

The functions

and
t\mapsto\beta_{i}(t)
are measurable and a.e. bounded on
[0,t_{0}]
, since they can be explicitly calculated as

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}\\
\beta_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma_{i}^{-1}\dot{p}_{i},

where
\Gamma_{i}
is the
2\times 2
matrix with column vectors

and
p_{i-1}(t)-p_{i}(t)
.
(Note that
\det(\Gamma_{i}(t))\neq 0
for all

, since

is non-degenerately contained in
P
for all

.)

Let us now argue that
\alpha_{i}(t)
and
\beta_{i}(t)
are nonnegative scalars. Since the path

is decreasing,

for

. Thus,
p_{i}(t^{\prime})-p_{i}(t)
is a linear combination of
p_{i+1}(t)-p_{i}(t)
and
p_{i-1}(t)-p_{i}(t)
with nonnegative coefficients. So for
t^{\prime}>t
, we can write

\frac{1}{t^{\prime}-t}(p_{i}(t^{\prime})-p_{i}(t))=\alpha_{i}(t^{\prime},t)(p_{i+1}(t)-p_{i}(t)+\beta_{i}(t^{\prime},t)(p_{i-1}(t)-p_{i}(t)),

for nonnegative scalars
\alpha_{i}(t^{\prime},t)
and
\beta_{i}(t^{\prime},t)
. Since

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}(t^{\prime},t)\\
\beta_{i}(t^{\prime},t)\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{t^{\prime}-t}\Gamma_{i}^{-1}(p_{i}(t^{\prime})-p_{i}(t)),

by letting
t^{\prime}\to t^{+}
we see that
\alpha_{i}(t^{\prime},t)\to\alpha_{i}(t)
and
\beta_{i}(t^{\prime},t)\to\beta_{i}(t)
. Thus,

\alpha_{i}(t)
and
\beta_{i}(t)
are nonnegative.

Let us rewrite (

) as

\dot{p}_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}(t)p_{i+1}(t)-(\alpha_{i}(t)+\beta_{i}(t))p_{i}(t)+\beta_{i}(t)p_{i-1}(t).

Let
Q(t)
be the
n\times n
intensity matrix

Q(t)=\begin{pmatrix}-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})&\alpha_{1}&\cdots&&\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}&-(\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2})&\alpha_{2}&\cdots&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
\alpha_{n}&\cdots&&\beta_{n}&-(\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n})\end{pmatrix},

defined so that
\dot{P}(t)=Q(t)P(t)
.
Consider now the ODE on the
n\times n
matrices

\dot{E}(t)=Q(t)E(t),

with initial condition
E(0)=I
. This ODE has a unique solution

for
0\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}
(given by a Peano-Baker series) ranging in the nonsingular stochastic matrices. Upon defining

E(t,s)=E(t)E(s)^{-1}
for
t_{0}\geqslant t\geqslant s\geqslant 0
we obtain a reversed Markov process. Moreover, we have
P(t)=E(t)P
for all
t
, as both sides agree at
t=0
and have the same derivative a.e.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Remark 4.8
.

In

Proof of Theorem

.

Let
\{P(t):0\leqslant t\leqslant t_{0}\}
be a decreasing path starting at

and ending at

. Since
P^{\prime}
is non-degenerate in
P
, the distance from any vertex of

to a line formed by any two other vertices of

is bounded below for all

by a positive number
\delta
. Using the uniform continuity of the path

, let us choose

such that

Observe then that

5.
Geometric preliminaries

In this section we lay down some geometric preliminaries that will be needed later on.

5.1.
Directed lines

Let
\ell
be a line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
(a,b)
an ordered pair of distinct points in
\ell
. We call the map
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\ell
defined by

\gamma(t)=(1-t)a+tb
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
the affine parametrization of
\ell
determined by
(a,b)
. This parametrization defines an order
on
\ell
induced by the order on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the choice of an ordered pair of distinct point
(a,b)
in
\ell
induces an order on
\ell
such that
a<b
.
We call
\ell
a
directed line
if it is endowed with either one of the two possible orders induced by its affine parametrizations. We denote the directed line
determined by
(a,b)
by
\ell_{ab}
.

Let
\ell
be a directed line and choose points
a,b\in\ell
such that
a<b
in the order of
\ell
. We say that a point
c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
lies to the left of
\ell
if the triangle
(a,b,c)
is either degenerate or oriented counterclockwise, i.e., if
\Delta(a,b,c)\geqslant 0
where

\Delta(a,b,c)=\mathrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&1\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&1\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&1\end{pmatrix},

and
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
,
b=(b_{1},b_{2})
, and
c=(c_{1},c_{2})
.
If
c
is additionally not on
\ell
, then we say that
c
lies strictly to the left of
\ell
.
Similarly,
c
lies to the right of
\ell
if
(a,b,c)
is oriented clockwise, and strictly to the right of
\ell
if it lies to the right of
\ell
and not on
\ell
. The points on a directed line lie both to the left and to the right of that line.

5.2.
Tangent rays

A ray determines a directed line, so we may speak of the left and right sides of a ray, meaning the left and right sides of the directed line determined by the ray. Given distinct points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
r_{ab}
the ray emanating from
a
and passing through
b
.

Let
S
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Given a point
x
not in the interior of the convex hull of
S
, there exists a unique ray emanating from
x
, henceforth denoted by
r_{x}
, such that

(1)

the set
S
is entirely to the left of
r_{x}
, and

(2)

r_{x}
contains at least one point
p\in S\backslash\{x\}
.

We call
r_{x}
the
right tangent ray
to
S
emanating from
x
.

Perspectivities and projectivities

Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be distinct lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

\ell_{1}

with center

is defined as the map
\alpha\colon\ell_{1}\to\ell_{2}

such that

\alpha(x)=\ell_{ox}\cap\ell_{2}

for all
x\in\ell_{1}
. In the event that
\ell_{ox_{0}}
is parallel to
\ell_{2}
for some (unique)

, we regard
\alpha(x_{0})
as undefined and call

\alpha

\ell_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle o}}{{\wedge}}\ell_{2}
to denote the perspectivity from
\ell_{1}
to
\ell_{2}
with center

.

The composition of two or more perspectivities between multiple lines in the plane is called a
projectivity
, or projective transformation.

Remark 5.1
.

The natural domain for perspectivities and projectivities is the projective plane, as in this setting they are defined everywhere.
More formally then, a projectivity is a composition of perspectivities in the projective plane. We will refrain however from explicitly working in the projective plane, since the concept of a decreasing path of polygons is naturally rooted in the affine plane. Thus, we simply allow projectivities to be undefined at (at most) one point.

Let
\alpha\colon\ell_{1}\to\ell_{2}
be a projectivity between directed lines. Let
\gamma_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\ell_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\ell_{2}
be affine parametrizations of
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}

f=\gamma_{2}^{-1}\circ\alpha\circ\gamma_{1}

is a fractional linear transformation from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., it has the form

f(t)=\frac{at+b}{ct+d}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. From this, the following facts are readily verified:

(1)

If
\alpha
has no pole, then it is an affine transformation.

(2)

If
\alpha
has a pole at

. In this case we call

an

(b)

\alpha

(x_{0},\infty)

\alpha(x)<\alpha(y)
for all

. In this case we call
\alpha
an

(3)

If the segment

maps
[a,b]

bijectively to

.

(4)

If the segment

contains the pole of
\alpha

\alpha

[a,b]
into
\ell_{2}\backslash(\alpha(a),\alpha(b))
, or equivalently,
\ell_{1}\backslash(a,b)
into

[\alpha(a),\alpha(b)]
.

Given a function

between directed lines

\gamma(tx+(1-t)y)\leqslant t\gamma(x)+(1-t)\gamma(y)

6.
The Poncelet map

Let
n\geqslant 3
. If an
n
-gon
P
is not set-convex, then every
n
-gon
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq P
is degenerate in
P
, and so attainable in at most
5n
moves, by Theorem
3.7
.
This solves both the Attainablity and Bang-Bang problems for a non-set-convex
P
. In this section we initiate the investigation of these problems assuming that
P
is set-convex. After a suitable re-indexing of the vertices, a set-convex polygon can be turned into a convex polygon oriented counterclockwise. We thus assume throughout this section that
P=(p_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
is a convex
n
-gon oriented counterclockwise.

We fix an
n
-gon
P^{\prime}=(p_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{n}
contained in
P
. We henceforth assume that not all vertices of
P^{\prime}
are collinear.

Recall that
\partial P
denotes the boundary of
P
, i.e, the union of its edges. Next we define a function
\pi\colon\partial P\to\partial P
. This is the Poncelet map that titles this section.

Definition 6.1

(The Poncelet map)
.

For any
x\in\partial P
, let
r_{x}
be the unique right tangent ray to
P^{\prime}
emanating from
x
. We define
\pi(x)\in\partial P
as the point in
r_{x}\cap\partial P
which is furthest from
x
.

Remark 6.2
.

We can define a a function
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}\colon\partial P\to\partial P
by simply replacing “right tangent ray" with “left tangent ray" in the definition of
\pi
. We call
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}
the clockwise Poncelet map. Our focus will be largely on
\pi
. With the aid of a reflection on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, results on
\pi
are easily transferred to
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}
.

We will now further characterize
\pi
. Let
x\in\partial P
. Since
P^{\prime}
is contained in
P
, and
P
is convex,
x
does not belong to the interior of the convex hull of
P^{\prime}
. Thus, the existence of the right tangent ray
r_{x}
is guaranteed (see
5.2
). There are two cases to consider
in the evaluation of
\pi
:

Interior Case
: The ray
r_{x}
intersects the interior of
P
. In this case,
\pi(x)
is the unique
point distinct from
x
that is both on
r_{x}
and on
\partial P
.

Boundary Case
:

Lemma 6.4
.

Let

be distinct points such that

lies to the left of the directed line

(1)

\mathrm{arc}[b,a)
.

(2)

\pi

\mathrm{arc}[c,\pi(c)]

(3)

\pi(z)\in\mathrm{arc}(z,\pi(c)]

Proof.

(i) Let us show first that

into

. Assume that

, of

in

, which lies to the left of
\ell_{ab}
, is contained in the segment

agrees with the points of

and

A function

such that

,

maps
\mathrm{arc}[x,y]
into
\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)]

Proposition 6.5
.

Any function
f\colon\partial P\mapsto\partial P
that maps
\mathrm{arc}[x,f(x)]
into
\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]
for all nonfixed points
x
of
f
is orientation preserving. In particular, the Poncelet map is orientation preserving.

Proof.

Let
f\colon\partial P\mapsto\partial P
be such that it maps
\mathrm{arc}[x,f(x)]
into
\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]
for all
x
not fixed by
f
.
Observe that the property assumed for
f
is precisely (ii) of the previous lemma. In the previous lemma, (iii) follows from (ii). We thus have that
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[z,f(x)]
for all
x
not fixed by
f
and
z\in\mathrm{arc}(f(x),x]
.

We will make use of another property of
f
regarding fixed points: Let
y\in\partial P
be fixed by
f
.
If
x
is not fixed by
f
, then
\mathrm{arc}(x,f(x))
does not contain any fixed points of
f
(as it is mapped into its complement).
It follows that
y\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]
, i.e.,
f(x)\in\mathrm{arc}[x,y]
. This conclusion holds for all
x\neq y
(fixed or not fixed by
f
).

Let
x\neq y
be such that
f(x)\neq f(y)
, and let
z\in\mathrm{arc}(x,y)
. We wish to show that
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)]
.
We examine several cases.

Case that
y
is fixed by
f
. As remarked above, in this case
f(x)\in\mathrm{arc}[x,y]
and
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[z,y]
. We either have that
z\in\mathrm{arc}(x,f(x))
or that
z\in[f(x),y)
. If
z\in\mathrm{arc}[x,f(x)]
, then

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]\cap\mathrm{arc}[z,y]=\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)].

If
z\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),y]
, then
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[z,y]\subseteq\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)
.

Case that
x
is fixed by
f
. If
f(z)=f(x)
, then
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)]
, as desired.
Assume thus that
f(z)\neq f(x)
. By the previous case, since
y\in\mathrm{arc}(z,x)
and
x
is fixed by
f
, we have that
f(y)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(z),f(x)]
, i.e.,
f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(y),f(x)]
.

In the remaining cases we assume that neither
x
nor
y
is a fixed point of
f
.

Case
x\in\mathrm{arc}[y,f(y)]
. In this case
f(x)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(y),y]
. If
z\in\mathrm{arc}[x,f(x)]
, then

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]\subseteq\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)].

If on the other hand
z\in\mathrm{arc}(f(x),y]\subseteq\mathrm{arc}(f(y),y]
, then

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[z,f(y)]\subseteq\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)].

Case

.
In this case,
f(x)\in[x,f(y)]
. We either have that
f(x)\in(x,y]
or that
f(x)\in(y,f(y))
. We analyze each of these cases next.

Subcase
f(x)\in(x,y]
. If
z\in\mathrm{arc}[x,f(x)]
, then

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]\cap\mathrm{arc}[z,f(y)]=\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)],

and if
z\in\mathrm{arc}(f(x),y]\subseteq(f(y),y]
, then

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[z,f(y)]\subseteq\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)].

Subcase
f(x)\in(y,f(y))

f(z)\in\mathrm{arc}[f(x),x]\cap\mathrm{arc}[z,f(y)]=\mathrm{arc}[f(x),f(y)].\qed

(x,x^{\prime})

P^{\prime}
, e.g.,
p(x)
. (Note that
p(x)\neq x^{\prime}
since
P^{\prime}
is in the interior of
P
.) Thus, the ray
r_{x^{\prime}x}
, emanating from
x^{\prime}
and passing through
x
, is the left tangent ray to
P^{\prime}
. Since this ray passes through the interior of
P
, it intersects
\partial P
at exactly two points:
x
and
x^{\prime}
. It follows that
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}(x^{\prime})=x
. A symmetric argument shows that
\pi
is a left inverse of
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

If some vertices of
P^{\prime}
belong to the boundary of
P
,
then
\pi
is neither continuous, nor injective, nor surjective.

In the following lemma we make use of the following observation whose simple proof we omit: If
f_{1}\colon\partial P\to\partial P
and
f_{2}\colon\partial P\to\partial P
are bijective piecewise projective functions, then
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
is also piecewise projective. Moreover, if
S_{1}\subseteq\partial P
and
S_{2}\subseteq\partial P
are sets of juncture points for
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
respectively, then
S=f_{2}^{-1}(S_{2})\cup S_{1}
is a set of juncture points for
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Suppose that every vertex of
P^{\prime}
belongs to the interior of
P
. Then for each
k\geqslant 1
, the function
\pi^{k}
is piecewise projective with set of juncture points

\Gamma\cup\pi^{-1}(\Gamma)\cup\cdots\pi^{-(k-1)}(\Gamma).

Proof.

By Corollary
6.9
,
\pi
is piecewise projective with set of juncture points
\Gamma
. Repeatedly applying the observation made in the preceding paragraph, we arrive at the set of juncture points for
\pi^{k}
in the statement of the lemma.
∎

7.
The broken line construction

In this section we continue to assume that
P
is a convex
n
-gon oriented counterclockwise, and that
P^{\prime}
is an
n
-gon contained in
P
and such that not all vertices of
P^{\prime}
are collinear. We continue to denote by
\pi\colon\partial P\to\partial P
the Poncelet map relative to
P^{\prime}
defined in the previous section.

Definition 7.1

(Broken Line Construction)
.

We define the broken line construction (BLC) relative to
P^{\prime}
with starting point
x\in\partial P
as follows:
Set
x_{1}=x
and
x_{2}=\pi(x_{1})
. For
k\geqslant 2
, if
\pi(x_{k})
belongs to
\mathrm{arc}(x_{k},x_{1})
, set
x_{k+1}=\pi(x_{k})
. Stop the construction otherwise.

We note that, by Lemma
6.4
(ii),
\pi^{2}(x)\in\mathrm{arc}(\pi(x),x)
for all
x\in\partial P
. This guarantees that
there are always at least three distinct points in the broken line construction.

Remark 7.2
.

We can similarly construct points along
\partial P
from an initial
x\in\partial P
by applying the clockwise Poncelet map
\pi_{\mathrm{cw}}
instead of
\pi
, following a similar iterative process, and using clockwise arcs for the stopping condition. We refer to this construction as the clockwise BLC.

Let
a_{1},...,a_{m}
be distinct points on the boundary
\partial P
, indexed in the counterclockwise order. These points partition
\partial P
into disjoint arcs which we call partition arcs:

\partial P=\mathrm{arc}[a_{1},a_{2})\sqcup\mathrm{arc}[a_{2},a_{3})...\sqcup\mathrm{arc}[a_{m},a_{1}).

Lemma 7.3

(Partition Arcs Lemma)
.

Let
m\geqslant 3
.
Consider distinct points
a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{m}
on
\partial P
, listed in the counterclockwise order, such that
P^{\prime}
lies to the left of
\ell_{a_{i}a_{i+1}}
for all
i
. The broken line construction, started at a point

, has the following property: For each

, if
x_{k}
belongs to
\mathrm{arc}[a_{i},a_{i+1})
, with

, and if

is defined and belongs to
\mathrm{arc}[a_{j},a_{j+1})
, then either

(1)

i<j\leqslant m
, or

(2)

j=1
and
x_{k+1}
is the final point in the construction.

In the second case,
x_{k+1}\in\mathrm{arc}[a_{1},x_{1})
and
\pi(x_{k+1})\in\mathrm{arc}[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

.

Let

, and assume that

. Let

. Then

(1)

and its vertices follow the counterclockwise order along
\partial P
.

(2)

Every half-open edge

, except possibly
[p_{i},p_{i+1})

.

(3)

Q

x_{l}\in[p_{i},p_{i+1})
and
x_{2}=p_{i+1}

(4)

P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
, i.e.,
P^{\prime}
is contained in
Q
.

(5)

For
k=1,\ldots,l-1
, the half-open edge
(x_{k},x_{k+1}]
of

contains the vertex

of
P^{\prime}
. Since the half-open edges of

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality that
i=1
, i.e., that
x\in[p_{1},p_{2})
.

(i) It is clear from the construction of

that its vertices belong to
\partial P
, i.e., that it is inscribed in
P
. It is also clear from construction that since
x_{k+1}\in\mathrm{arc}(x_{k},x_{1})
for all
k
, the vertices of
Q
follow the counterclockwise order along
\partial P
.

(ii) This follows immediately from the partition arcs lemma applied with
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
the vertices of
P
.

(iii) Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant l
. Due to the counterclockwise ordering of the vertices of

,

, which by the convexity of

lies to the left of
\ell_{x_{k}x_{k+1}}
. Let us argue that the only three consecutive vertices of

that can be collinear are
x_{l},x_{1},x_{2}
.

Indeed, for

the vertices

of

belong to

of

If

, and

and

of

, then

, as

and
x_{l}
must belong to different half-open edges. This prevents
x_{1}
from being on this same edge. Thus, the only possibly collinear vertices of

are
x_{l},x_{1},x_{2}
.

Suppose that

are not collinear. Then for each

lie to the left of

and no three consecutive vertices are collinear. This precisely means that

is convex and oriented counterclockwise.

If
x_{l},x_{1},x_{2}
are collinear, then they belong to the same edge

. Then

necessarily contains a vertex on

, namely the pivot

in the evaluation of

at

. So

. In this case we see that
x_{l},x_{2},x_{3}
cannot be collinear, as
x_{3}\notin[p_{1},p_{2}]
. Hence, as before, we conclude that

is convex.

(iv) By construction,

lies to the left of

for

, since
x_{k+1}=\pi(x_{k})
for such

and

for any

P^{\prime}
lies to the left of the line
\ell_{x_{l},\pi(x_{l})}

. Thus,
P^{\prime}
also lies to the left of
\ell_{x_{l}x_{1}}

P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. If
Q
is not convex, we saw that
Q^{\prime}
is convex. Then
P^{\prime}
is left of every edge of
Q^{\prime}
(since in this case
\ell_{x_{l}x_{2}}=\ell_{p_{1}p_{2}}
and
P
is convex). So again we obtain that
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
, as desired.

(v) This is clear from the definition of

x_{k+1}=\pi(x_{k+1})
, and in this case the half-open edge

contains the pivot

.

8.
Degeneracy test

Throughout this section we let
P
be a convex
n
-gon oriented counterclockwise and we let
P^{\prime}
be an
n
-gon contained in
P
. As in the previous section, we assume that not all vertices of
P^{\prime}
are collinear. We denote by
\pi\colon\partial P\to\partial P
the Poncelet map relative to
P^{\prime}
.

In Theorem
8.3
below we identify a finite set of “test" points on
\partial P
from which to start the BLC in order to determine whether
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
. Before proving this theorem we establish a couple of lemmas.

Let us call
x\in\partial P
a
good point
if the BLC (relative to
P^{\prime}
) starting at
x
results in an
l
-gon with
l<n
, and call it bad otherwise.

Lemma 8.1
.

If
x
is good, then
\pi^{k}(x)
is good for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Note that
x
is good if and only if
\pi^{l}(x)\in\mathrm{arc}[x,\pi(x)]
for some
1<l<n
. Let
x
be a good point and set
x^{\prime}=\pi(x)
. Let us show that
x^{\prime}
is good too. We have:

\pi^{l}(x^{\prime})=\pi^{l}(\pi(x))=\pi(\pi^{l}(x)).

Since
\pi
is orientation preserving (Proposition
6.5
) and
\pi^{l}(x)\in\mathrm{arc}[x,\pi(x)]
, it follows that

\pi^{l}(x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{arc}[\pi(x),\pi^{2}(x)]=\mathrm{arc}[x^{\prime},\pi(x^{\prime})].

Thus,
x^{\prime}
is a good point.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
Q
be an
m
-gon inscribed in
P
, with pairwise distinct vertices, and such that
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
. The following statements are true:

(1)

The BLC starting at any point on
\partial P
produces an interpolating polygon with at most
m+1
vertices.

(2)

The BLC starting at any of the vertices of
Q
produces an interpolating polygon with at most
m
vertices.

Proof.

Let
Q=(q_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
, and assume, after re-indexing if necessary, that the vertices of
Q
follow the counterclockwise order along
\partial P
. Let
x\in\partial P
, and let
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{l}
be the polygon resulting from the BLC with initial point
x_{1}=x
.

(i) Since
Q
lies to the left of
\ell_{q_{k}q_{k+1}}
for all
k
,
P^{\prime}
lies also to the left of
\ell_{q_{k}q_{k+1}}
for all
k
. The vertices of
Q
create
m
partition arcs
\mathrm{arc}[q_{k},q_{k+1})
, with
k=1,\ldots,m
, along
\partial P
. By Lemma
7.3
, one of these partition arcs may contain
x_{1}
and
x_{l}
, while each of the others contains at most one point from the BLC. It follows that
l\leqslant m+1
, thus proving (i).

(ii) Suppose now that
x
is a vertex of
Q
, say
x=q_{1}
. In this case
x_{l}
cannot belong to
\mathrm{arc}[q_{1},q_{2})
, since
x_{l}\in\mathrm{arc}(x_{l-1},x_{1})
. Thus each of the
m
partition arcs created by
Q
contains at most one point from the BLC, implying that
l\leqslant m
.
∎

Define a set
T\subseteq\partial P
as

T=P\cup\Gamma_{1},

where
P
stands for its set of vertices and
\Gamma_{1}
is as in Definition
6.7
.
Each point in
\Gamma_{1}
is the push-out onto
\partial P
of some vertex
v
of
\mathrm{co}(P^{\prime})
by its successor
v^{\prime}
(in the counterclockwise order), with
v
and
v^{\prime}
not both belonging to the same edge of
P
.

Theorem 8.3
.

The
n
-gon
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
if and only if there exists
x\in T
such that the BLC starting at
x
results in an
l
-gon with
l<n
.

Proof.

One direction of the theorem is obvious: Any
l
-gon obtained by the BLC is an interpolating polygon between
P^{\prime}
and
P
.
If
l<n
, then this verifies that
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
.

Suppose now that
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
.
Let us further assume that
n\geqslant 4
, since in the case
n=3
degenerate containment is equivalent to collinearity, which we have assumed not to hold for
P^{\prime}
. Our goal is to show that the set
T
contains at least one good point.

We first dispose of several cases in which the theorem can be settled quickly.

Suppose that there exists an interpolating
m
-gon
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
, with
m\leqslant n-2
. By pushing out the vertices of
Q
onto
\partial P
if necessary
(with push-out moves), we may assume that
Q
is inscribed in
P
. Then, by Lemma
8.2
(i), the polygon resulting from the BLC starting at any
x\in\partial P
has at most
m+1\leqslant n-1
vertices. Thus, every point in
\partial P
, including the vertices of
P
, is a good point, proving the theorem in this case.

Suppose that there exists an interpolating
m
-gon
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
, where
m<n
and at least one vertex of
Q
coincides with a vertex of
P
.
By pushing out the remaining vertices of
Q
onto
\partial P
if necessary, we may assume that
Q
is inscribed in
P
.
By Lemma
8.2
(ii), every vertex of
Q
is a good point. In particular, the common vertex of
Q
and
P
, which belongs to the set
T
, is a good point, thus proving the theorem in this case.

Suppose that there exists an interpolating
m
-gon
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q\preccurlyeq P
, where
m<n
and at least two vertices of
Q
belong to the same edge of
P
.
We can push out one of these two vertices with the other one onto a vertex of
P
. We are now in the previously considered case.

Suppose that at least one vertex of
P^{\prime}
belongs to
\partial P
. Say that

.
Choose an interpolating

and with

.
Since
[p_{j},p_{j+1}]

\mathrm{co}(P)
,

is a convex combination of the vertices of

.

and the evaluation

).

does not fall in any of the four cases covered above. We thus assume that the vertices of

in

is an
(n-1)
-gon not sharing a vertex with

and with no two vertices on the same edge of

.

Since the vertices of
P^{\prime}
lie in the interior of
P
, the Poncelet map
\pi
is a piecewise projective function with set of juncture points
\Gamma
(Corollary
6.9
). By Lemma
6.11
,
\pi^{n-1}
is also piecewise projective, and has set of juncture points

T^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\pi^{-1}(\Gamma)\cup\cdots\cup\pi^{-(n-2)}(\Gamma).

If
T^{\prime}
contains a good point, then
\Gamma
or a preimage of
\Gamma
contains a good point. Since the set of good points is closed under evaluation by
\pi
(Lemma

), it follows that
\Gamma
contains a good point. If

is a good point, then either

is a vertex of
P
,

, or
\pi(x)

is a vertex of

. In all cases,

contains a good point. Thus, to prove that

contains a good point it suffices to show that

contains a good point. We will show this next.

We note that the set of good points is non-empty, since the vertices of any

must be good points by Lemma

, and no two vertices of

[p_{2},p_{3}]
, respectively, since
\pi^{(n-1)}(x)
does not change edges for
x\in[c,c^{\prime}]
. This implies that both
c
and
c^{\prime}
are good points, completing the proof.

Let us assume that
\pi^{(n-1)}(x)
belongs to
[p_{n},p_{1}]
for all
x\in[c,c^{\prime}]
.

Denote by
f\colon\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}\to\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}
the projectivity agreeing with
\pi^{(n-1)}
on the interval
[c,c^{\prime}]
.
Note that a point
y\in[c,c^{\prime}]
is good if and only if
f(y)\geqslant y
in the order of
\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}
. In particular, since
x_{1}
is a good point,
f(x_{1})\geqslant x_{1}
. The proof will be complete once we have shown that either
f(c)\geqslant c
or
f(c^{\prime})\geqslant c^{\prime}
.

Write

f=\beta_{n-2}\circ\cdots\circ\beta_{1}\circ\alpha,

where
\alpha=\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}\wedge\ell_{p_{2}p_{3}}
is the first perspectivity used in the BLC starting at
x_{1}
, and

\beta_{k}=\ell_{p_{k+1}p_{k+2}}\wedge\ell_{p_{k+2}p_{k+3}}
for
k=1,\ldots,n-2
are the subsequent perspectivities. Each of these perspectivities is orientation preserving, since in each case the center of perspectivity lies to the left of the domain and codomain lines (Lemma
5.2
). Thus,
f
is an orientation preserving projectivity.

Claim: If
y\in\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}
is such that
\alpha(y)\notin(x_{2},p_{3})
, then
f(y)\in[p_{n},f(x_{1})]
.

Proof of the claim: The center of perspectivity of
\beta_{k}
belongs to the segment
(x_{k+1},x_{k+2})
for all
k
. By Lemma
5.3
(ii), keeping in mind that
\beta_{1}(p_{3})=p_{3}
, the pole of
\beta_{1}
belongs to the segment
(x_{2},p_{3})
. It follows that
\beta_{1}
maps
\ell_{p_{2}p_{3}}\backslash(x_{2},p_{3})
into
[p_{3},x_{3}]
. By a similar argument,
\beta_{k}
maps
[p_{k+1},x_{k+1}]
into
[p_{k+2},x_{k+2}]
for
k=2,\ldots,n-2
. So, if
\alpha(y)
belongs to
\ell_{p_{2}p_{3}}\backslash(x_{2},p_{3})
, then continuing to evaluate on each
\beta_{k}
we obtain that

f(y)=(\beta_{n-2}\circ\cdots\circ\beta_{1}\circ\alpha)(y)\in[p_{n},\beta_{n-2}(x_{n-1})]=[p_{n},f(x_{1})].

This completes the proof of the claim.

If
f
has no pole, then it is an affine function on
\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}
. In this case,
f(c)<c
and
f(x_{1})\geqslant x_{1}
together imply that
f(c^{\prime})\geqslant c^{\prime}
. Thus, either
c
or
c^{\prime}
is a good point, and we are done.

Suppose, then, that
f
has a pole, and let us denote it by
y_{0}
. Since
y_{0}\notin[c,c^{\prime}]
, we have either that
y_{0}<c
or
y_{0}>c^{\prime}
in the order of
\ell_{p_{n}p_{1}}
. Suppose that

. Since
f
is a convex function on

(see Section

), we cannot have that
f(c)<c
,

, and
f(c^{\prime})<c^{\prime}
. Thus, either
f(c)\geqslant c
or
f(c^{\prime})\geqslant c^{\prime}
, i.e., either
c
or
c^{\prime}
is good, and we are done.

Let us outline how our results give an effective way of testing for degenerate containment given a pair of
n
-gons
P
and
P^{\prime}
, with
P^{\prime}\preccurlyeq P
:

(1)

Check whether
P
is set-convex. If it is not, then
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
.

(2)

Check whether the vertices of
P^{\prime}
are all collinear. If they are, then
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
.

(3)

If
P
is set-convex, re-index its vertices so that it is convex and oriented counterclockwise.

(4)

If the vertices of
P^{\prime}
are non-collinear, apply the Broken Line Construction (BLC) starting from points on the set
T
. If, for some such point, the result is an
l
-gon with
l<n
, then
P^{\prime}
is degenerately contained in
P
. Otherwise, it is non-degenerate in
P
.

9.
The Threshold

In this section we begin the investigation of the Attainability and Bang-Bang problems for
n
-gons
P^{\prime}
non-degenerately contained in a convex
n
-gon
P
.

Definition 9.1
.

The threshold set associated to a convex polygon
P
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{P}
, is defined as the set of all polygons
P^{\prime}
attainable from
P
and such that at least one vertex of
P^{\prime}
belongs to
\partial P
.

Throughout this section we let
P
be a convex
n
-gon oriented counterclockwise. In Theorem
9.4
below we characterize the
polygons in
\mathcal{T}_{P}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{P}
(in the threshold and nondegenerate) by an explicit criterion involving the BLC.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
\{P(t):c_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant c_{1}\}
be a continuous path of set-convex polygons. If
P(c_{0})
is convex oriented counterclockwise, then so is
P(c_{1})
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
P(c_{0})
is convex and oriented counterclockwise but
P(c_{1})
is not. Then, for some pairwise distinct indices

i,i+1,j
, we have that
p_{j}(c_{0})
lies strictly to the left of the directed line through
p_{i}(c_{0})
and
p_{i+1}(c_{0})
while
p_{j}(c_{1})
does not lie strictly to the left of the directed line through
p_{i}(c_{1})
and
p_{i+1}(c_{1})
. By continuity, there exists
t\in[c_{0},c_{1}]
such that
p_{j}(t)
,
p_{i}(t)
, and
p_{i+1}(t)
are collinear. This violates that
P(t)
is set-convex.
∎

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\{P(t):0\leqslant t\leqslant c\}
be a decreasing path of polygons such that
P(0)=P
and
P(t)
is non-degenerate in
P
for all
t
. Suppose that
p_{i}(c)\in\partial P
for some
i
. The following statements hold:

(1)

Either
p_{i}(t)\in(p_{i-1},p_{i}]
for all
t\leqslant c
, or
p_{i}(t)\in[p_{i},p_{i+1})
for all
t\leqslant c
.

(2)

Let
t_{0}\in[0,c]
be the least
t
such that
p_{i}(t)
remains fixed thereafter, i.e.,

t_{0}=\min\{t\in[0,c]:p_{i}(t^{\prime})=p_{i}(c)\hbox{ for all }t^{\prime}\in[t,c]\}.

If
p_{i}(c)\in(p_{i-1},p_{i}]
, then
p_{i-1}(t_{0})\in[p_{i-1},p_{i}(t_{0}))
, and if
p_{i}(c)\in[p_{i},p_{i+1})
, then
p_{i+1}(t_{0})\in(p_{i}(t_{0}),p_{i+1}]
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
i=1
.

(i) Suppose
p_{1}(c)
belongs to the edge
[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
of
P
. Let us show that
p_{1}(t)\in[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
for all
t\leqslant c
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists
0\leqslant c^{\prime}<c
such that
p_{1}(c^{\prime})\notin[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
. Let
c_{0}
be the first time after
c^{\prime}
that
p_{1}(t)
reaches the interval
[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
, i.e.,

c_{0}=\min\{t\in[c^{\prime},c]:p_{1}(t)\in[p_{k},p_{k+1}]\}.

Consider the sets
I(t)=\mathrm{co}(P(t))\cap[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
for
0\leqslant t\leqslant c_{0}
. The mapping
t\mapsto I(t)
is obtained by intersecting a decreasing family of convex sets with a segment. It is thus a decreasing family of segments. Since
p_{1}(c_{0})\in I(c_{0})
, these segments are non-empty. Moreover, since
[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
is a face of
\mathrm{co}(P)
, we have

I(t)=\mathrm{co}(P(t))\cap[p_{k},p_{k+1}]=\mathrm{co}(P(t)\cap[p_{k},p_{k+1}]),

and thus the endpoints of
I(t)
are vertices of
P(t)
. Since the vertices of
P(t)
are finitely indexed, there exists an infinite sequence of times
t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots
with
t_{N}\to c_{0}
and two fixed indices
i,j
such that
I(t_{N})=[p_{i}(t_{N}),p_{j}(t_{N})]
for all
N
. Note that
i,j\neq 1
, since
p_{1}(t)\notin[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
for
c^{\prime}\leqslant t<c_{0}
. It follows by continuity that
p_{i}(c_{0}),p_{j}(c_{0})\in I(c_{0})
. If
i=j
, i.e.,
I(t_{N})
is a singleton for all
N
, then
p_{i}(c_{0})=p_{1}(c_{0})
, which contradicts the non-degeneracy of
P(c_{0})
in
P
. If
i\neq j
, then
p_{i}(c_{0}),p_{j}(c_{0}),p_{1}(c_{0})
are collinear vertices of
P(c_{0})
with pairwise distinct indices, which again contradicts the non-degeneracy of
P(c_{0})
in
P
. Hence, we conclude that
p_{1}(t)\in[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
for all
t\leqslant c
.

Since
p_{1}=p_{1}(0)\in[p_{k},p_{k+1}]
, we have either
k=1
or
k=n
. It is not possible to have
p_{1}(t)=p_{2}
for some
t>0
, as this would entail that
p_{1}\in[p_{2},p_{3}]
. Similarly, we have
p_{1}(t)\neq p_{n}
for all
t
. This completes the proof of (i).

(ii) Suppose
p_{1}(c)\in(p_{n},p_{1}]
. Our goal is to show that
p_{n}(t_{0})\in[p_{n},p_{1}(t_{0}))
.
The case
p_{1}(c)\in[p_{1},p_{2})
can be dealt with similarly.

If
t_{0}=0
the results holds trivially, so assume that
t_{0}>0
. Note that, by part (i),
p_{1}(t)\in(p_{n},p_{1}]
for all
t
.
Consider the decreasing intervals
I(t)=\mathrm{co}(P(t))\cap[p_{n},p_{1}]
, for
0\leqslant t\leqslant c
. As argued in part (i), the endpoints of
I(t)
are vertices of
P(t)
. Moreover, by (i), these vertices can only be
p_{n}(t)
and
p_{1}(t)
. Since
p_{1}(t)\in I(t)
for all
t
, we either have that
I(t)=[p_{n}(t),p_{1}(t)]
or
I(t)=\{p_{1}(t)\}
, for each
t
.

By the definition of
t_{0}
, and since
t_{0}>0
, there exist
t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots
such that
t_{N}\to t_{0}
and
p_{1}(t_{N})\neq p_{1}(t_{0})
(
=p_{1}(c)
) for all
N
. Observe that
I(t_{N})
is not a singleton, as it contains both
p_{1}(t_{N})
and
p_{1}(t_{0})
. It follows that
I(t_{N})=[p_{n}(t_{N}),p_{1}(t_{N})]
for all
N
, and by continuity, that
I(t_{0})=[p_{n}(t_{0}),p_{1}(t_{0})]
. This shows that
p_{n}(t_{0})\in[p_{n},p_{1}]
. Since
p_{n}(t)\in[p_{n},p_{1}]
for all
t\leqslant t_{0}
, and
p_{n}(t)\neq p_{1}(t)
for all
t
,
we further deduce that
p_{n}(t_{0})\in[p_{n},p_{1}(t_{0}))
.
∎

Corollary 9.5
.

The threshold set
\mathcal{T}_{P}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

Proof.

A polygon in
\mathcal{T}_{P}
is either degenerate in
P
, in which case it is attainable in fewer than
5n
pull-in moves, or non-degenerate in
P
, in which case it is attainable in at most
2n-1
pull-in moves, by the previous theorem. Thus
\mathcal{T}_{P}
is the intersection of the set of
n
-gons with at least one vertex in
\partial P
, a closed set, and the set of
n
-gons attainable from
P
in at most
5n
pull-in moves, a compact set by Proposition
4.9
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{P}
is compact.
∎

Corollary 9.6
.

Let

be non-degenerate in

, attainable from

, and such that
p_{i}^{\prime}\in(p_{i-1},p_{i}]
. If we change

anywhere along
[p_{i}^{\prime},p_{i}]
, the resulting

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that

. Let
p_{1}^{\prime\prime}\in[p_{1}^{\prime},p_{1}]

. This implies that the BLC relative to

). We wish to show that

, the BLC with starting point

, the BLC relative to

. Thus, we can apply Lemma

to the BLC relative
\tilde{P}
starting at
y_{1}=p_{1}^{\prime\prime}

and the aforementioned partition arcs. This
leaves only two possibilities for the location of
y_{n}
: either
y_{n}\in[x_{1},y_{1})
(the last point in the construction belongs to the same partition arc as the first), or each partition arc contains exactly one point, in which case
y_{n}\in[x_{n},x_{1})
(the last point belongs to the last partition arc). In either case,
y_{n}\in[p_{n},y_{1})
as desired.
∎

10.
The Vestibule

Throughout this section we fix a convex
n
-gon
P
oriented counterclockwise. Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{P}
the set of
n
-gons that are degenerately contained in
P
, and by
\mathcal{T}_{P}
the set of
n
-gons attainable from
P
and with at least one vertex in
\partial P
, i.e., the threshold set associated to
P
.

We call
neighbor pull-in move
a pull-in of a vertex
p_{i}
toward one of its two neighboring vertices
p_{i-1}
or
p_{i+1}
.

Definition 10.1
.

For a fixed convex
n
-gon
P
, we define the vestibule set
\mathcal{V}_{P}
as the set of
n
-gons
P^{\prime}
obtained
by applying one single neighbor pull-in move to an
n
-gon in
\mathcal{T}_{P}
.

In Theorem
10.9
below we show that, for
n\geqslant 4
,
\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P}
is precisely the set of polygons attainable from
P
.
The main step in the proof consists in showing that
\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P}
is closed under pull-in moves (Theorem
10.7
).
Before proving this, we need several preparatory lemmas.

Lemma 10.2
.

The set
\mathcal{V}_{P}
is compact.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{C}_{P}
the set of
n
-gons contained in
P
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
f_{i,i+1}\colon\mathcal{C}_{P}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{P}
be the function implementing a pull-in of
p_{i}^{\prime}
toward
p_{i+1}^{\prime}
:

f_{i,i+1}(P^{\prime},t)=(p_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,(1-t)p_{i}^{\prime}+tp_{i+1}^{\prime},\ldots,p_{n}^{\prime}).

Define similarly
f_{i+1,i}\colon\mathcal{C}_{P}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{P}
for a pull-in of
p_{i+1}^{\prime}
toward
p_{i}^{\prime}
. Then

\mathcal{V}_{P}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}f_{i,i+1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\times[0,1])\cup f_{i+1,i}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\times[0,1]).

It follows that
\mathcal{V}_{P}
is compact, as
f_{i,i+1}
and
f_{i+1,i}
are continuous for all
i
and
\mathcal{T}_{P}
is compact (Corollary
9.5
).
∎

Theorem 10.7
.

For
n\geqslant 4
the set
\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P}
is closed under neighbor pull-in moves.

Proof.

By Lemma
10.5
, it suffices to show that if
P^{\prime}\in\partial(\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P})
, and a neighbor pull-in move is applied to
P^{\prime}
, then for sufficiently
small values of the parameter of the move the polygons on the path belong to
\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P}
. If
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{P}
, then this is indeed the case, as
\mathcal{D}
is obviously closed under pull-in moves. If
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{V}
, a neighbor pull-in move on
P^{\prime}
results in a polygon in
\mathcal{V}
. Let us thus assume that
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{P}\setminus(\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{D})
. That is,
P^{\prime}
is non-degenerate in
P
, all its vertices lie in the interior of
P
, and there exists a neighbor push-out move on
P^{\prime}
which produces a polygon in
\mathcal{T}
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the push-out of vertex
p_{1}^{\prime}
with
p_{n}^{\prime}
onto
\overline{p}_{1}\in\partial P
results in an attainable polygon
\overline{P}
. By Lemma
10.6
, we have that
\overline{p}_{1}\in(p_{n},p_{1})
and that the BLC relative to
P^{\prime}
starting at
\overline{p}_{1}
ends at
p_{n}
.

Let us apply a neighbor pull-in move on
P^{\prime}
. Let
P^{\prime}(t)=(p_{1}^{\prime}(t),\ldots,p_{n}^{\prime}(t))
, for
0\leqslant t\leqslant c
,
denote the path generated by this move, where
P^{\prime}(0)=P^{\prime}
. Let
\overline{P}(t)
denote the polygon resulting from applying on
P^{\prime}(t)
the push-out of
p_{1}^{\prime}(t)
with
p_{n}^{\prime}(t)
onto
\partial P
. Since
p_{1}^{\prime}\in(p_{n},p_{1})
, for small enough
t
we have
\overline{p}_{1}(t)\in(p_{n},p_{1})
. Since our goal is to show that
P^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{V}_{P}\cup\mathcal{D}_{P}
for small enough
t
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\overline{p}_{1}(t)\in(p_{n},p_{1})
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant c
. Further, since
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is a closed set and

P^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{D}_{P}
, we may also assume that
P^{\prime}(t)\notin\mathcal{D}_{P}
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant c
, i.e.,
P^{\prime}(t)
remains non-degenerate along the path, whence also convex and oriented counterclockwise, by Lemma
9.2
.

We consider several cases.

Case 0: pull-in moves not changing the line
\ell_{p_{n}^{\prime}p_{1}^{\prime}}
. More concretely, we mean by this any of the following pull-in moves:

(1)

a pull-in of
p_{i}^{\prime}
toward any vertex of
P^{\prime}
, for
i\neq 1
and
i\neq n
,

(2)

a pull-in of
p_{1}^{\prime}
toward
p_{n}^{\prime}
,

(3)

a pull-in of
p_{n}^{\prime}
toward
p_{1}^{\prime}
.

In all these cases the directed line
\ell_{p_{n}^{\prime}p_{1}^{\prime}}
does not change. Thus, the push-out
\overline{p}_{1}
remains constant, i.e.,
\overline{p}_{1}(t)=\overline{p}_{1}
for all
t
. In these cases the polygon
\overline{P}(t)
is in
\mathcal{T}_{P}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Indeed,
\overline{P}(t)
is attained from
\overline{P}
by the same pull-in move being applied on
P^{\prime}
(see Lemma
2.2
.). It follows that
P^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{V}_{P}
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant c
, as desired.

The only moves not covered by the above case are the pull-ins of
p_{n}^{\prime}
toward
p_{n-1}^{\prime}
and of
p_{1}^{\prime}
toward
p_{2}^{\prime}
.

Case 1: pull-in of
p_{1}^{\prime}
toward
p_{2}^{\prime}
. Observe that

is locally increasing by Lemma

for all

and some

, as desired.
