Euclidean Algebras
Xingde Dai, Wei Huang

Abstract
. We introduce a new example of unital commutative n n -dimensional group algebra ℝ n \mathbb{R}_{n} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . The algebra ℝ n \mathbb{R}_{n} and the complex numbers ℂ \mathbb{C} are astonishingly alike. The zero divisor set of the algebra has Lebesgue μ n \mu_{n} -measure zero. The formula for the Haar measure is established. Also, the analytic function theory in ℝ n , \mathbb{R}_{n}, for n = 2 ​ k n=2k that similar to the classical theory in ℂ \mathbb{C} is introduced. This includes the Cauchy-Riemann equations, mean-value theorem and Louisville theorem.

2.
Multiplication in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},n\geq 2
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space over
\mathbb{R}
and
\{{\vec{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\vec{e}}_{n}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Define a mapping
\mathfrak{g}:\{{\vec{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\vec{e}}_{n}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llc}\mathfrak{g}{\vec{e}}_{1}&=&-{\vec{e}}_{n}\\
\mathfrak{g}{\vec{e}}_{2}&=&{\vec{e}}_{1}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\\
\mathfrak{g}{\vec{e}}_{n}&=&{\vec{e}}_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\right.

Let
I_{n}
denote the identity mapping (matrix) on the real
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It is clear that
\mathfrak{g}^{n}=-I_{n}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{2n}=I_{n}
.
The set
\{\mathfrak{g}^{\ell}\mid\ell=1,2,\cdots,2n\}
is cyclic, hence it is an abelian group of order
2n
.
We write
\mathfrak{g}^{0}=\mathfrak{g}^{2n}=I_{n}
.
For
0<\ell\leq n-1
, the matrix form for the mapping
\mathfrak{g}^{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}O&I_{n-\ell}\\
-I_{\ell}&O^{\prime}\end{array}\right]

where
I_{n-\ell}
is the
(n-\ell)\times(n-\ell)
identity matrix,
I_{\ell}
is the
\ell\times\ell
identity matrix
and
O
a zero matrix.

Since
\mathfrak{g}^{n+k}=-\mathfrak{g}^{k}
.
The linear span of the group
\mathfrak{G}_{n}\equiv\{\mathfrak{g}^{\ell}\mid\ell=1,2,\cdots,2n\}
over
\mathbb{R}
has dimension
n
.
Let
\mathbb{M}_{n}
denote this linear span.
The set
\{\mathfrak{g}^{0},\mathfrak{g}^{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{g}^{n-1}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{M}_{n}
,
a linear subspace of the
n\times n
real matrix space.
It is clear that
\mathbb{M}_{n}
is closed under matrix multiplication.
Since the group
\{\mathfrak{g}^{\ell}\mid\ell=1,2,\cdots,2n\}
is abelian, the space
\mathbb{M}_{n}
with the matrix multiplication is a commutative algebra of dimension
n

with multiplicative identity
I_{n}
. Let
A
be an element in
\mathbb{M}_{n}
with
\det A\neq 0
. The inverse matrix
A^{-1}
exists.
By the Cayley-Hamilton theorem,
A^{-1}

is a linear combination of
\{I_{n},A,A^{2},\cdots,A^{n-1}\}
, hence
A^{-1}
is also an element of
\mathbb{M}_{n}
.

Define the mapping
\varsigma:

\displaystyle\varsigma:{\vec{e}}_{\ell}\mapsto\mathfrak{g}^{\ell-1},\ell=1,2,\cdots,n.

The mapping
\varsigma
is a bijection between bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{M}_{n}
.
The mapping
\varsigma
extends to an isomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\mathbb{M}_{n}
as linear spaces over
\mathbb{R}
.
For an element
{\vec{u}}=[u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{n-1},u_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\varsigma({\vec{u}})=\varsigma\big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}u_{\ell}{\vec{e}}_{\ell}\big)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}u_{\ell}\mathfrak{g}^{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}.

For
{\vec{u}},{\vec{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define
{\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}}
as

\displaystyle{\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}}\equiv\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})\big).

Theorem 2.1
.

The Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 2
equipped with the multiplication
\circledast
is a commutative algebra with multiplicative identity
{\vec{e}}_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
{\vec{u}},{\vec{v}},{\vec{w}}
be elements in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1. Associative Property:

By definition we have
\varsigma({\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}})=\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})
and

\varsigma({\vec{v}}\circledast{\vec{w}})=\varsigma({\vec{v}})\varsigma({\vec{w}})
. Also the multiplication in
\mathbb{M}_{n}
satisfies the
Associative Law. We have

\displaystyle({\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}})\circledast{\vec{w}}

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}})\varsigma({\vec{w}})\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big((\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}}))\varsigma({\vec{w}})\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})(\varsigma({\vec{v}})\varsigma({\vec{w}}))\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}}\circledast{\vec{w}})\big)

\displaystyle={\vec{u}}\circledast({\vec{v}}\circledast{\vec{w}}).

i.e.

\displaystyle({\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}})\circledast{\vec{w}}={\vec{u}}\circledast({\vec{v}}\circledast{\vec{w}}).

2. Commutative Property:

Since the multiplication in
\mathbb{M}_{n}
is commutative,
\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})=\varsigma({\vec{v}})\varsigma({\vec{u}})
, we have

\displaystyle{\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{v}}=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})\big)=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{v}})\varsigma({\vec{u}})\big)={\vec{v}}\circledast{\vec{u}}.

3. Distributive Property:

Based on the facts that
\varsigma
and
\varsigma^{-1}
are isomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{M}_{n}
as linear spaces over
\mathbb{R}
,
also the fact that
\mathbb{M}_{n}
is an algebra satisfying the Distributive Law, we have

\displaystyle{\vec{w}}\circledast({\vec{u}}+{\vec{v}})

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{w}})\varsigma({\vec{u}}+{\vec{v}})\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{w}})\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})+\varsigma({\vec{v}})\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{w}})\varsigma({\vec{u}})+\varsigma({\vec{w}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})\big)

\displaystyle=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{w}})\varsigma({\vec{u}})\big)+\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{w}})\varsigma({\vec{v}})\big)

\displaystyle={\vec{w}}\circledast{\vec{u}}+{\vec{w}}\circledast{\vec{v}}.

i.e.

\displaystyle{\vec{w}}\circledast({\vec{u}}+{\vec{v}})={\vec{w}}\circledast{\vec{u}}+{\vec{w}}\circledast{\vec{v}}.

4. Multiplicative Identity:

The multiplicative identity in
\mathbb{M}_{n}
is
I_{n}=\mathfrak{g}^{0}
and the corresponding element in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\varsigma^{-1}(\mathfrak{g}^{0})={\vec{e}}_{1}=[1,0,\cdots,0]
.
We have

\displaystyle{\vec{u}}\circledast{\vec{e}}_{1}={\vec{e}}_{1}\circledast{\vec{u}}=\varsigma^{-1}\big(I_{n}\varsigma({\vec{u}})\big)=\varsigma^{-1}\big(\varsigma({\vec{u}})\big)={\vec{u}}.

The Theorem
2.1
is proved.

4.
Zero Divisors

An element
{\vec{x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
is a zero divisor if and only if
\varsigma({\vec{x}})
is a zero divisor in
\mathbb{M}_{n}
if and only if

\det(\varsigma({\vec{x}}))
, a homogeneous polynomial in
n
variables
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
has value zero.
Let
\mathfrak{Z}_{{}_{\mathbb{R}_{n}}}
denote the set of zero divisors in
\mathbb{R}_{n}
.
For a polynomial
p
, we will write
\mathfrak{Z}(p)
for
\{{\vec{x}}\mid p({\vec{x}})=0\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p({\vec{x}}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-zero polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mu_{n}(\mathfrak{Z}(p))=0.

Here
\mu_{n}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

We prove the Lemma
4.1
by induction on the dimensions
n
.

For
n=1
. Let
p({\vec{x}})
be a polynomial of degree
m\geq 1
on
\mathbb{R}
. It’s clear
\mathfrak{Z}(p)
is a finite set hence has Lebesgue measure zero.

Assume the statement is true for all
n=1,\cdots,k-1
.
Consider
n=k
.

Let
p({\vec{x}})=p(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
be a non-zero polynomial of degree
m
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and let
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Define

\displaystyle p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}({\vec{x}})\equiv p(t,x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}).

This is a function in
k-1
variables
x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the collection of all points
t
in
\mathbb{R}
with the properties that the function
p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}({\vec{x}})
is not a zero function.
Apply the induction assumption to
p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{k-1}(\mathfrak{Z}(p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}))=0,\forall t\in\mathcal{C}.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{C}
.
Then
p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}({\vec{x}})\equiv 0
. So
(x_{1}-t)
is a factor of
p
. The polynomial has at most
finite factors. Hence the set
\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{C}
is a finite set. So

\displaystyle\mu_{k-1}(\mathfrak{Z}(p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}))=0,\textrm{for $t\in\mathbb{R}$ a.e.}

By Fubini’t theorem

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(\mathfrak{Z}(p))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu_{k-1}(\mathfrak{Z}(p_{{}_{|x_{1}=t}}))dt=0.

The induction completes. The Lemma
4.1
is proved.
∎

Proof.

Let

By definition,

\displaystyle\nu_{n}({\vec{a}}E)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\vec{a}}E}\frac{1}{\left|\det\varsigma({\vec{x}})\right|}d\mu_{n}({\vec{x}})

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\frac{1}{\left|\det\varsigma({\vec{a}}{\vec{y}})\right|}d\mu_{n}({\vec{a}}{\vec{y}}).

By Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle{\vec{a}}{\vec{y}}

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\frac{1}{\left|\det\varsigma({\vec{y}})\right|}d\mu_{n}({\vec{y}}),

Analytic functions in

In this section we will introduce the analytic function theory in

Let

,

\displaystyle f({\vec{x}})=[f_{1}({\vec{x}}),\cdots,f_{n}({\vec{x}})]

By the multiplication table,

We have

\displaystyle f({\vec{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}({\vec{x}}){\vec{w}}^{j-1}.

We say a function

\displaystyle\lim_{\vec{\delta}\rightarrow 0,\vec{\delta}\in\mathbb{G}_{n}}\frac{f({\vec{a}}+\vec{\delta})-f({\vec{a}})}{\vec{\delta}}=f^{\prime}({\vec{a}})\ (\textrm{ or }\frac{df}{d{\vec{x}}}({\vec{a}}))

In this definition, the variable
\vec{\delta}=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}{\vec{w}}^{j-1}
is approaching

\mathbb{G}_{n}

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\vec{a}})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\vec{\delta}\rightarrow 0,\vec{\delta}\in\mathbb{G}_{n}}\frac{f({\vec{a}}+\vec{\delta})-f({\vec{a}})}{\vec{\delta}}=\lim_{x_{j}\rightarrow 0}\frac{f({\vec{a}}+x_{j}{\vec{w}}^{j-1})-f({\vec{a}})}{x_{j}{\vec{w}}^{j-1}},

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\vec{a}})

\displaystyle={\vec{w}}^{-j+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}({\vec{a}}).

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}_{n}
be an open region and

be a function with domain
\Omega
.
We call

(1)

{\vec{x}}\in\Omega

(2)

The second order partial derivatives of

\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}
are continuous in
\Omega
.

It is clear
\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}
by the Clairaut’s Theorem.

To introduce the next results, we set
n=2k
. We have:

(6.1)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\vec{a}})={\vec{w}}^{-j+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}({\vec{a}}),\text{$~j=1,...,2k,$}

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}({\vec{a}})=\left[\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{j}},\cdots,\frac{\partial f_{2k}}{\partial x_{j}}\right].

For any given

, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\vec{a}})={\vec{w}}^{-i+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}({\vec{a}})={\vec{w}}^{-i-k+1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i+k}}({\vec{a}}),

\displaystyle-{\vec{w}}^{k}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}({\vec{a}})=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i+k}}({\vec{a}}),

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}O&-I_{k}\\
I_{k}&O\end{array}\right]\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}({\vec{a}})=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i+k}}({\vec{a}}).

Hence, if

\Omega

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i+k}}\\
\frac{\partial f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i}}=-\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{i+k}}\end{array}\right.\forall m,i,1\leq m,i\leq k.

Theorem 6.1
.

Given a positive even integer

and an open set

\frac{\partial f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i}}=-\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{i+k}}\end{array}\right.\text{$\forall~1\leq m,i\leq k$.}

(2)

For each

,

(6.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i+k}^{2}}=0,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}f_{{m+k}}}{\partial x_{i+k}^{2}}=0.

(3)

Each

is a harmonic function in the sense that

has continuous second order partial derivatives and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}=0.

Proof.

We will prove the Equation (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i+k}}\\
~\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{i+k}}=-\frac{\partial f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i}}\end{array}\right.\text{$\forall~1\leq i\leq k$.}

Hence

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m+k}}{\partial x_{i+k}\partial x_{i}}\\

Therefore
