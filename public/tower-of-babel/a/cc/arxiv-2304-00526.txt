Stable Densities, Fractional Integrals and the Mittag-Leffler Function
Nomvelo Karabo Sibisi sbsnom005@myuct.ac.za

Abstract
This paper combines probability theory and fractional calculus to derive a novel integral representation of the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function or Prabhakar function, where the three parameters are combinations of four base parameters. The fundamental concept is the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of the one-sided stable density, conditioned on a scale factor. Integrating with respect to a gamma distributed scale factor induces a mixture of Riemann-Liouville integrals. A particular combination of four base parameters leads to a representation of the Prabhakar function as a weighted mixture of Riemann-Liouville integrals at different scales. The Prabhakar function constructed in this manner is the Laplace transform of a four-parameter distribution. This general approach gives various known results as special cases (notably, the two-parameter generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution).



Keywords—

stable & gamma distributions; Riemann-Liouville fractional integration; Mittag-Leffler & Prabhakar function; generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution; complete monotonicity.

1
Introduction

There is an intimate relationship between Mittag-Leffler functions and fractional calculus, as comprehensively discussed in Gorenflo
et al.

[
11
]
.
The three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function known as the Prabhakar function has such a prominent role in this context as to inspire the phrase
“Prabhakar fractional calculus” (Giusti
et al.

[
9
]
).
At its core, this involves fractional integrals and derivatives of functions involving the Prabhakar function.

This paper constructs the Prabhakar function itself from fractional integration combined with probability theory. The point of departure is the density of the one-sided stable distribution, conditioned on a scale parameter,
to which we assign a gamma distribution.
Integrating over the scale parameter leads to a novel integral representation
of the Prabhakar function as a weighted mixture of Riemann-Liouville integrals of stable densities.
The formal statement of this construction is contained in Theorem
1
. The three parameters of the Prabhakar function involved in the theorem are, in turn, constructed as combinations of four other parameters
that we refer to as base parameters.

Theorem
1
has an interesting corollary –
the integral representation readily leads to a proof that the Prabhakar function constructed from the four parameters is completely monotone,

i.e.
that the Prabhakar function is the Laplace transform of a distribution parameterised by the four base parameters.
This proof arises from a probabilistic foundation, as opposed to the complex analytic approaches that are normally adopted in the exploration
of the completely monotone character of Mittag-Leffler functions.
Accordingly, we shall devote a good proportion of this paper to a discussion of the corollary on complete monotonicity.

The study of the complete monotonicity of the Mittag-Leffler function
dates back at least to a 1948 paper by Pollard
[
21
]
,
who used a complex analytic method to prove the property for the one-parameter Mittag-Leffler function (Gorenflo
et al.

[
11
]
(3.7.2) reproduces Pollard’s argument).
Górska
et al.

[
12
]
adapted Pollard’s complex analytic method to prove the complete monotonicity of the
three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function.

The Mittag-Leffler function also has an intimate connection with probability theory.
In his proof, Pollard noted that the ultimate result eluded him “without the intervention of
[the Laplace transform of the stable distribution]”.
While he did not use probabilistic language,
Pollard cited personal communication by Feller of a discovery of the result by “methods of probability theory”.

The completely monotone character of the Mittag-Leffler function is much more than an abstract mathematical curiosity.
It plays a fundamental role in models of physical phenomena
such as anomalous dielectric relaxation and viscoelasticity as mentioned, for example, by
de Oliviera
et al.

[
4
]
, Garra and Garrappa
[
8
]
,
Górska
et al.

[
12
]
, Mainardi and Garrappa
[
16
]
.

The conceptual underpinning of this paper, in the form of probability theory and fractional calculus,
bears similarity to the work of Ho
et al.

[
13
]
but there is a difference in context and purpose.
The latter paper is on random partitions of the integers, with a different discussion of the Prabhakar function
from that forming the central theme of this paper.

We start by introducing the primary concepts in Section
2
required for our main contribution in Section
3
.
This is followed by a discussion of various known distributions arising as special cases of Theorem
1
,
notably the generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution.

2
Primary Concepts

2.1
Distributions

2.1.1
Stable Distribution

The one-sided stable distribution
F_{\alpha}(x|t)
(
0<\alpha<1
) on
x\geq 0
,
conditioned on a scale parameter
t>0
, is indirectly defined by its Laplace-Stieltjes transform

(
equivalently, the ordinary Laplace transform of its density
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
)

\displaystyle e^{-ts^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\,dF_{\alpha}(x|t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}f_{\alpha}(x|t)\,dx

(1)

The explicit form of
F_{\alpha}
will not concern us here.
In any case, it is only known in closed form for selected values of
\alpha
, the simplest being for
\alpha=1/2
.
We write
F_{\alpha}(x)\equiv F_{\alpha}(x|t=1)
and
f_{\alpha}(x)\equiv f_{\alpha}(x|t=1)
.
It follows that
F_{\alpha}(x|t)\equiv F_{\alpha}(xt^{-1/\alpha})

and
f_{\alpha}(x|t)\equiv f_{\alpha}(xt^{-1/\alpha})\,t^{-1/\alpha}
.
We may include
\alpha=1
by defining
f_{\alpha=1}(x|t)=\delta(x-t)
(
\delta
is the Dirac delta function)
with Laplace transform
e^{-ts}
.

2.1.2
Gamma Distribution

The gamma distribution
G(x|\mu,\lambda)
(
x>0
), with shape and scale parameters
\mu>0,\lambda\geq 0
respectively,
is given by

\displaystyle dG(x|\mu,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\mu)}\,x^{\mu-1}e^{-\lambda x}\,dx

(2)

We have omitted the usual normalising term
\lambda^{\mu}
in order to accommodate the
\lambda=0
case.

2.2
Mittag-Leffler Function

The (one-parameter) Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(x)
is defined by the infinite series

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}\hskip 9.24994pt\alpha\geq 0

(3)

The Laplace transform of
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})

(x\geq 0,\lambda\geq 0)
is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{\alpha-1}}{\lambda+s^{\alpha}}\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm Re}(s)\geq 0

(4)

There is a three-parameter generalisation of the Mittag-Leffler function, also known as the Prabhakar function, defined by

\displaystyle E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+k)}{k!\,\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}\,x^{k}

(5)

The one-parameter Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(x)
is the special case
\gamma=\beta=1
.
The Laplace transform of
{\cal E}^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(x|\lambda)\equiv x^{\beta-1}E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})
is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\,{\cal E}^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(x|\lambda)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{\alpha\gamma-\beta}}{(\lambda+s^{\alpha})^{\gamma}}

(6)

This is known as Prabhakar’s result in other literature, while
{\cal E}^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(x|\lambda)
is known as the Prabhakar kernel.

2.3
Fractional Integration

The right-sided Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of a function
f(x)
on
x\geq 0
for
\nu>0
is defined by

\displaystyle\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f\}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\{h_{\nu}\star f\}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}f(u)\,du

(7)

where
h_{\nu}(x)=x^{\nu-1}/\Gamma(\nu)
and
h_{\nu}\star f
denotes Laplace convolution.
By the convolution theorem, the Laplace transform of (
7
) is

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f\}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathscr{L}}\{h_{\nu}\star f\}(s)=\widetilde{h}_{\nu}(s)\times\widetilde{f}(s)=s^{-\nu}\widetilde{f}(s)

(8)

where
{\mathscr{L}}\{f\}
and
\widetilde{f}
both denote the Laplace transform of
f
.
Since (
8
) reduces to
\widetilde{f}(s)
for
\nu=0
,
we may define the fractional integral for
\nu=0
as
\{I_{+}^{\,0}\,f\}(x)\equiv\{h_{0}\star f\}(x)=f(x)

where
h_{0}(x)=\delta(x)
.
We shall continue to use the form (
7
) for
\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f\}(x)
(
\nu\geq 0
), with the implicit understanding that, for
\nu=0
,

\{I_{+}^{\,0}\,f\}(x)=f(x)
.

This definition of the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral, equivalent to Laplace convolution, suffices for our purposes.
In a more general definition of (
7
), the integration lower limit need not be zero,
in which case the Laplace convolution equivalence no longer holds.
There is also a left-sided variant
I_{-}^{\nu}\,f
that is not relevant here.

We shall denote the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of a distribution
F(x)
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}dF(u)\hskip 9.24994pt\nu\geq 0

(9)

If
F(x)
has a density
f(x)
, then (
9
) and (
7
) are equivalent.
This holds for the stable distribution
F_{\alpha}(x|t)
, density
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
, whose fractional integral is our fundamental object of study.

The Riemann-Liouville fractional integral satisfies what is often referred to as the semigroup property

I_{+}^{\nu_{1}}I_{+}^{\nu_{2}}=I_{+}^{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}
.
This is equivalent to the convolution statement that
h_{\nu_{1}}\star h_{\nu_{2}}=h_{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}
or the Laplace transform equivalent

s^{-\nu_{1}}s^{-\nu_{2}}=s^{-(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})}
.

2.4
Complete Monotonicity

An infinitely differentiable function
\varphi(x)
on
x>0
is completely monotone if its derivatives
\varphi^{(n)}(x)

satisfy
(-1)^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(x)\geq 0
,
n\geq 0
.
Bernstein’s theorem states that
\varphi(x)
is completely monotone iff it may be expressed as
the Laplace-Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,dF(t)

(10)

for a non-decreasing distribution function
F(t)

(
we shall refer to the Laplace-Stieltjes transform merely as the Laplace transform, except when confusion might arise
)
.
For bounded
F(t)
,
\varphi(x)
is defined on
x\geq 0
.

3
Main Contribution

The fundamental probabilistic construct of this paper, underpinning all else that follows,
is the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of the conditional stable density
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
(
0<\alpha\leq 1
):

\displaystyle\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\{h_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}f_{\alpha}(u|t)\,du

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}dF_{\alpha}(u|t)

(12)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(s)

\displaystyle=s^{-\nu}\,e^{-ts^{\alpha}}

(13)

For completeness,
\{I_{+}^{0}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)=f_{\alpha}(x|t)
and, in keeping with
f_{\alpha=1}(x|t)=\delta(x-t)
,

\displaystyle\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha=1}(\cdot|t)\}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}(x-t)^{\nu-1}&t\leq x\\
0&t>x\end{cases}

(14)

\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
,
\nu\geq 0
, is clearly nonnegative.
The key idea is to assign a distribution
G(t)

t
and
integrate with respect to
G(t)
to generate a mixture density.
Rather than just settling on the single value
t=1
,
this takes into account all values of
t
, each weighted by the corresponding
G(t)
.
Although one might explore various choices of
G(t)
,
our particular interest here is in the gamma distribution
G(t|\mu,\lambda)
of (
2
) above.

Proposition
1
states a property of
\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
that
is key to the theorem that follows.

Proposition 1
.

\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}
and
\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}\}\equiv\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t=1)\}
,
\nu\geq 0
,
are related by the identity

\displaystyle\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)

\displaystyle=t^{(\nu-1)/\alpha}\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}\}(xt^{-1/\alpha})

(15)

Proof of Proposition
1
.

\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
takes the explicit form:

\displaystyle\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}f_{\alpha}(u|t)\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{x}_{0}(x-u)^{\nu-1}f_{\alpha}(ut^{-1/\alpha})\,t^{-1/\alpha}\,du

\displaystyle y=ut^{-1/\alpha}:\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{xt^{-1/\alpha}}_{0}(x-yt^{1/\alpha})^{\nu-1}f_{\alpha}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{(\nu-1)/\alpha}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\int^{xt^{-1/\alpha}}_{0}(xt^{-1/\alpha}-y)^{\nu-1}f_{\alpha}(y)\,dy

The last expression is the explicit form of
t^{(\nu-1)/\alpha}\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}\}(xt^{-1/\alpha})
.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,t^{q}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\,\Gamma(\gamma+q+\theta/\alpha)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\theta/\alpha)}\,E^{\gamma+q+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\alpha q+\theta}(-\lambda)\geq 0

Proof of Corollary

By (

\displaystyle t^{n}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+\theta)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\theta/\alpha)}\,\{I_{+}^{\beta-\alpha\gamma}f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)\,t^{\gamma+(\alpha n+\theta)/\alpha-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\Gamma(\gamma+n+\theta/\alpha)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\theta/\alpha)\Gamma(\beta+\alpha n+\theta)}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\alpha n+\theta}(t)

\displaystyle\implies\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\,\Gamma(\gamma+n+\theta/\alpha)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\theta/\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta+\alpha n+\theta)}

since
P^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\alpha n+\theta}(t)
is normalised.
For the more general case with
\lambda\geq 0
and
q>-\gamma-\theta/\alpha

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,t^{q}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\Gamma(\gamma+q+\theta/\alpha)}{\Gamma(\gamma+\theta/\alpha)\Gamma(\beta+\alpha q+\theta)}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\,dP^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\alpha q+\theta}(t)

since the Laplace transform of

is

\Gamma(\beta+\alpha q+\theta)E^{\gamma+q+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\alpha q+\theta}(-\lambda)

∎

Our argument has solely appealed to probability theory and fractional integration, with the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of the conditional stable density as the basic construct.
To borrow a term from number theory, we might describe the argument as “elementary” in the sense that it has not made
explicit reference to complex analysis.

Complex Analytic:

The starting point in this context is the work by Pollard

There is well-established work (not discussed in this paper) that also appeals to complex analytic methods to prove
the completely monotone character of

,

i.e.
that there exists a distribution whose Laplace transform is

\theta=0

Probabilistic:

In the probability literature,

(one of two distributions bearing the name). The generalisation to two parameters (

We proceed to sketch both complex analytic and probabilistic approaches in the literature.

6
Infinitely Divisible Distributions

There is an intimate relationship between completely monotone functions and the theory of
infinitely divisible distributions on the nonnegative half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)

(Feller
[
6
]
(XIII.4, XIII.7), Steutel and van Harn
[
23
]
(III)).
Sato
[
22
]
considers infinitely divisible distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
but the deliberate restriction to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
makes for simpler discussion
and relates directly to the core concept of complete monotonicity that is of interest here.
There is also an intimate link to the generalised gamma convolutions studied by Bondesson
[
3
]
.

A distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with density
f(x|t)
(
t>0
) is infinitely divisible iff its Laplace transform takes the form

\widetilde{f}(s|t)=e^{-t\psi(s)}
(
s>0
) where
\psi(s)
is a positive function and its derivative
\psi\,^{\prime}(s)
is completely monotone.
Hence there exists a density
\rho(x)
with Laplace transform
\widetilde{\rho}(s)=\psi\,^{\prime}(s)
:

\displaystyle-\frac{\widetilde{f}\,^{\prime}(s|t)}{\widetilde{f}(s|t)}

\displaystyle=t\,\psi\,^{\prime}(s)=t\,\widetilde{\rho}(s)

\displaystyle\text{or}\;-\widetilde{f}\,^{\prime}(s|t)

\displaystyle=t\,\widetilde{\rho}(s)\times\widetilde{f}(s|t)\

(59)

\displaystyle\implies xf(x|t)

\displaystyle=t\,\{\rho\star f(\cdot|t)\}(x)

(60)

These are equivalent ways to characterise infinitely divisible distributions.

For the stable density
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
(
0<\alpha<1
),
\psi_{\alpha}(s)=s^{\alpha}
and

\psi_{\alpha}\,^{\prime}(s)=\rho_{\alpha}(s)=\alpha s^{\alpha-1}

\implies

\rho_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha\,x^{-\alpha}/\Gamma(1-\alpha)
, so that

\{\rho_{\alpha}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)\equiv\alpha\,\{I_{+}^{1-\alpha}f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
.
Hence, for the stable case, (
60
) reproduces (
24
) from the perspective of infinite divisibility.
Put differently, the stable distribution may be looked upon as a bridge between the theory of infinitely divisible distributions and
Riemann-Liouville fractional integration.
(
60
) holds for all infinitely divisible densities, however the convolution
\{\rho\star f(\cdot|t)\}(x)
need not be expressible as
a fractional integral.

7
Discussion

It is worth reviewing the mathematical concepts and theorem(s) that arise in this paper:

1.

F_{\alpha}(x|t)
, the one-sided stable distribution with density
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
.

2.

\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
, the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of
f_{\alpha}(x|t)
.

3.

G(t|\mu,\lambda)
, the gamma distribution (with shape and scale parameters
\mu,\lambda
) assigned to
the scale parameter
t
of the stable distribution.

4.

M^{\nu}_{\alpha,\mu}(x|\lambda)
, the mixture of
\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
with respect to
G(t|\mu,\lambda)
.

5.

Theorem
1
states that

M^{\beta-\alpha\gamma}_{\alpha,\gamma+\theta/\alpha}(x|\lambda)={\cal E}^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(x|\lambda)\equiv x^{\beta+\theta-1}E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})
.

6.

\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\,{\cal E}^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(1|\lambda)\equiv\Gamma(\beta+\theta)\,E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-\lambda)

is the Laplace transform of a probability distribution
P^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)

that we might refer to as the four-parameter Pollard distribution.

This combination of stable distribution, fractional integrals and gamma distribution is a novel probabilistic approach to the study of the
Prabhakar function and its completely monotone character.
Unlike approaches inspired by Pollard’s derivation of
P_{\alpha}(t)
, it does not invoke complex analysis as a starting point.
Equally, the approach of this paper takes a different probabilistic route from that of random experiment metaphors
(Pólya urns, Chinese restaurant process).
It also does not invoke other concepts associated with the construction of the generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution

P_{\alpha,\theta}(t)\equiv P^{1}_{\alpha,1,\theta}(t)
,
such as polynomial tilting of the stable density
f_{\alpha}(x)\to f_{\alpha,\theta}(x)\propto x^{-\theta}f_{\alpha}(x)

(Arbel
et al.

[
1
]
, Devroye
[
5
]
, James
[
14
]
).

We hasten to add that the contrasting of different approaches is not meant to be a comment on their relative merit.
Diversity of approach is commonplace in probability theory. For example, in a context of nonparametric Bayesian analysis,
Ferguson
[
7
]
constructed the Dirichlet process based on
the gamma distribution as the fundamental probabilistic concept,
without invoking the metaphor of a random experiment.
In a nutshell, the Dirichlet distribution arises from normalising gamma-distributed variables over disjoint
“cells” of a partition or tiling of some domain such as time or space
(formally, both the variable and the gamma shape parameter are measures over the domain and thus additive under merging of disjoint sub-domains).
By Condition C in Ferguson
[
7
]
, the Dirichlet process is the set of mutually consistent Dirichlet distributions imposed by
the persistence of gamma distribution behaviour on individual cells under merging or splitting of cells of any partition
(to an arbitrarily fine limit).

Blackwell and MacQueen
[
2
]
observed that the Ferguson approach
“involves a rather deep study of the gamma process” as they proceeded to give an alternate construction based on a generalised Pólya urn scheme.
Such metaphors (Pólya urn draws, stick-breaking, Chinese restaurant table selection) have become the popular motivation for
the Dirichlet process and the Poisson-Dirichlet process
{\rm PD(\alpha,\theta})

used to model discrete systems such as random partitions of the integers.
Given
n
integers, the number
K_{n}
of partition blocks is analogous to the number of tables occupied by the
first
n
customers for the Chinese restaurant process.
The variable
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-\alpha}K_{n}
has the generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution
{\rm ML(\alpha,\theta})
,
also known as the
\alpha
-diversity of
{\rm PD(\alpha,\theta})
.
Pursuing the restaurant metaphor, Möhle
[
17
]
added a cocktail bar in order to construct a
three-parameter Mittag-Leffler distribution
{\rm ML(\alpha,\beta,\gamma})
, referencing Tomovski
et al.

[
24
]
for its explicit functional form
(Górska
et al.

[
12
]
and Tomovski
et al.

[
24
]
followed a complex analytic derivation as discussed in Section
4.2
).

{\rm ML(\alpha,\beta,\gamma})
is equivalent to
P^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
of this paper.

Akin to Ferguson and in keeping with Feller’s proof that
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is the Laplace transform of
P_{\alpha}(t)\equiv 1-F_{\alpha}(1|t)
,
we have chosen to work directly with distributions of continuous variables on
[0,\infty)

to construct
P^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}(t)
, which subsumes the generalised Mittag-Leffler distribution
P_{\alpha,\theta}(t)

as the special case
\beta=\gamma=1
.
It is worth exploring more general choices of (
\beta,\gamma
), which may lead to four-parameter analogues of the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution.

We note that we need not have made specific reference to fractional integrals.
It would have sufficed to refer solely to convolutions of distributions, given that the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral

\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)\equiv\{h_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(x)
where
h_{\nu}(x)=x^{\nu-1}/\Gamma(\nu)

is a particular instance of the Laplace convolution of densities.
While this might have made for an exclusively probabilistic narrative, Mittag-Leffler functions are so intimately associated with fractional calculus
that it seemed logical to import the language of fractional integrals into our probabilistic reasoning.
Finally, it is well worth exploring choices of mixing distribution other than the gamma distribution.
For example, a Poisson distribution would induce a discrete sum over weighted fractional integrals.
This is but one avenue of future exploration.
