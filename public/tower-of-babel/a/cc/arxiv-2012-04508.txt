Equidistribution of primitive lattices in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n}
Tal Horesh, Yakov Karasik

Abstract
We count primitive lattices of rank d d inside ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} as their covolume tends to infinity, with respect to certain parameters of such lattices. These parameters include, for example, the subsapce that a lattice spans, namely its projection to the Grassmannian; its homothety class; and its equivalence class modulo rescaling and rotation, often referred to as a shape . We add to a prior work of Schmidt by allowing sets in the spaces of parameters that are general enough to conclude joint equidistribution of these parameters. In addition to the primitive d d -lattices Λ \Lambda themselves, we also consider their orthogonal complements in ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} , Λ ⟂ \Lambda^{\perp} , and show that the equidistribution occurs jointly for Λ \Lambda and Λ ⟂ \Lambda^{\perp} . Finally, our asymptotic formulas for the number of primitive lattices include an explicit error term.



1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to extend classical counting and equidistribution
results for
primitive vectors
to their higher rank counterparts:

primitive lattices
. A primitive vector is an
n
-tuple of
integers
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
with
\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=1
,
and the the set of primitive vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}_{\operatorname{prim}}^{n}
.
We can associate to each vector
0\neq v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the discrete
subgroup that it spans,
\mathbb{Z}v
; following this logic, a rank
d

(
1\leq d\leq n
) analog for a vector is a
lattice of rank

d
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, namely

\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}v_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}v_{d}

where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are linearly independent. We
will refer to it briefly as a
d
-lattice
. We say that a
d
-lattice

\Lambda
is
integral
if
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and
primitive

if
\Lambda=V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where
V
is a
d
-dimensional rational
subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For example, a primitive
1
-lattice is simply
all the integral points on a rational line, or, equivalently, it is

\mathbb{Z}v
where
v
is a primitive vector.

Questions about counting primitive vectors date back to the days
of Gauss and Dirichlet, e.g. with the
Gauss Class Number problem
.
In the 20th century, questions about equidistribution of integral
vectors began to arise, with the principal example being
Linnik-type
problems

[
Lin68
,
EH99
,
Duk03
,
Duk07
,
ELMV11
,
BO12
,
EMV13
]
.
These questions and others generalize naturally to primitive lattices,
as we now turn to describe.

The Primitive Circle Problem.

The well known Gauss Circle Problem concerns the asymptotic number
of integral vectors up to (euclidean) norm
X>0
. The analogous question
for primitive vectors, namely the asymptotic amount of primitive vectors
up to norm
X
, is often referred to as the
primitive circle
problem

[
Now88
,
ZC99
,
Wu02
]
. In lattices, the role of a norm
is played by the
covolume
: the covolume of
\Lambda
, denoted

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)
, is the volume of a fundamental paralelopiped for

\Lambda
in the linear space

V_{\Lambda}:=\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda).

Thus, the
primitive circle problem for lattices
is
to estimate the asymptotics of

\#\{\text{primitive $d$-lattices in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ of covolume up to $X$}\}

(1.1)

as
X\to\infty
. Note that for
1
-lattices, the notions of norm
and covolume coincide:
\operatorname{covol}(\mathbb{Z}v)=\left\|v\right\|
, hence when
d=1

the above recovers the “original” primitive circle problem. Schmidt

[
Sch68
]
showed that the amount in (
1.1
)
equals

c_{d,n}X^{n}+O(X^{n-\max\{\frac{1}{d},\frac{1}{n-d}\}}),

where

c_{d,n}=\frac{1}{n}{n\choose d}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i=n-d-1}^{n}\operatorname{Leb(B_{i})}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\operatorname{Leb(B_{j})}}\cdot\frac{\prod_{i=2}^{d}\zeta\left(i\right)}{\prod_{j=n-d+1}^{n}\zeta\left(j\right)},

Leb
is the Lebesgue measure, and
B_{i}
is the unit
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{i}
. We remark that the optimal exponent in the error
term of the circle problem (primitive or not) is established only
in dimensions
n\geq 4
. As far as the authors are aware, this (
d=1
,

n\geq 4
) is the only case where an optimal error exponent is known
for the lattices circle problem (primitive or not).

Linnik-type problems.

This is a unifying name for questions on the distribution of the projections
of integral vectors to the unit sphere, i.e. of
v/\left\|v\right\|
when

v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\text{ or $\mathbb{Z}_{\operatorname{prim}}^{n}$}
. Viewing the
unit sphere as the space of oriented lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the analogous
object when considering
d
-lattices would be the
Grassmannian

of oriented
d
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)

(and defined explicitly in Section
2
).
Accordingly, we will view our lattices as carrying an orientation,
which simplifies our discussion on the technical level but has no
effect on the results. In particular, the two-to-one correspondence
between primitive vectors and primitive
1
-lattices (arising from
the fact that
v
and
-v
span the same lattice) becomes a one-to-one
correspondence between primitive vectors and oriented primitive
1
-lattices.
The
average Linnik problem for primitive lattices

is to study the distribution of the (oriented) spaces
V_{\Lambda}

in
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
as
\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)\leq X\to\infty
. Note that
V_{\Lambda}

are exactly the rational subspaces in
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
.

Shapes of orthogonal lattices.

More recently, with the rise of dynamical approaches in number theory,
another type of equidistribution questions for primitive vectors arose.
To a primitive vector
v
we associate the
(n-1)
-lattice
v^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
referred to as as the
orthogonal lattice
of
v
, where
v^{\perp}

is the orthogonal hyperplane to
v
. Several recent papers (by Marklof

[
Mar10
]
, Aka Einsiedler and Shapira
[
AES16b
,
AES16a
]
,
Einsiedler, Mozes, Shah and Shapira
[
EMSS16
]
, Einsiedler R�hr
and Wirth
[
ERW17
]
) studied the equidistribution of
shapes

of the orthogonal lattices to primitive vectors as their norm tends
to infinity, where the shape of a lattice is its similarity class
modulo rotation and homothety. The space of shapes of
d
-lattices
is a double coset space of
\operatorname{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, denoted
\mathcal{X}_{d}

and defined explicitly in Section
2
,
and the aforementioned papers show (among other things) that the shapes
of the orthogonal lattices to
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\operatorname{prim}}^{n}
equidistribute
in
\mathcal{X}_{n-1}
as
\left\|v\right\|\to\infty
w.r.t. a uniform measure
arriving from the Haar measure on
\operatorname{SL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})
. In the works of
Einsiedler et al.  they in fact show that the shapes of
v^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}

equidistribute in
\mathcal{X}_{n-1}

jointly
with the directions

v/\left\|v\right\|
in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Just like for (primitive) vectors, orthogonal lattices can be defined
for (primitive) lattices as well: for a primitive
d
-lattice
\Lambda
,
we let:

\Lambda^{\perp}:=V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n},

where
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
V_{\Lambda}
.
Note that
\Lambda^{\perp}
is primitive by definition, and that it
has rank
n-d
. Also note that
\Lambda\mapsto\Lambda^{\perp}
defines
a bijection between primitive lattices of ranks
d
and
n-d
.
This bijetion extends to a bijection between oriented lattices, with
a natural choice of orientation on the orthogonal lattice (Definition

2.2
).

One could then ask about the equidistribution of shapes of the orthogonal
lattices
\Lambda^{\perp}
to primitive lattices
\Lambda
, where the
one dimensional case
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}v
recovers the question studied in
the aforementioned papers about the equidistribution of shapes of

v^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Equidistribution of a sequence in a finite-volume space can be deduced
from counting in “sufficiently general” subsets of this space.
Indeed, we will count in subsets that have
controlled boundary

(Definition
3.1
).

Theorem 1.1

(A consequence of Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
)
.

Assume that
\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
and

\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{d}\times\mathcal{X}_{n-d}

are BCS’s. Then, the number of primitive
d
-lattices
\Lambda
with

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)\leq X
,
V_{\Lambda}\in\Phi
and
(\operatorname{shape}\left(\Lambda\right),\operatorname{shape}\left(\Lambda^{\perp}\right))\in\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{F}

is

\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{E})\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{F})\operatorname{vol}(\Phi)}{n\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{n}\zeta\left(i\right)}\Upsilon(d)\Upsilon(n-d)\cdot X^{n}+O_{\epsilon}(X^{n-\frac{\tau_{n}n}{\beta}+\epsilon})

for every
\epsilon>0
, where
\tau_{n}=\left\lceil\left(n-1\right)/2\right\rceil/4n^{2}
,

\Upsilon(d):=\frac{d!\prod_{i=2}^{d}\operatorname{Leb(B_{i})}}{\left[\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right):Z\left(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\right)\right]}

(in the denominator is the index of the center of
\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
which is either
1
or
2
), and

\beta=\begin{cases}2\left(n-d-1\right)\left(n^{2}-1\right)+n^{2}&\text{when $\mathcal{E}$ is bounded}\\
2\left(d-1\right)\left(n^{2}-1\right)+n^{2}&\text{when $\mathcal{F}$ is bounded}\\
2\left(\max\left\{d,n-d\right\}-1\right)\left(n^{2}-1\right)+n^{2}&\text{when neither of $\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}$ is bounded}\\
1&\text{when both $\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}$ are bounded}\end{cases}.

In the above theorem,
\operatorname{vol}
stands for a uniform Radon measure (that
is unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar and will be defined
in Section
2
), so it implies the
joint uniform distribution of the directions, shapes, and shapes of
the orthogonal lattices of primitive lattices as their covolume tends
to infinity. In particular, it is quite interesting to observe that
the shapes of
\Lambda
and of
\Lambda^{\perp}
are independent parameters,
meaning that there is no way to know the shape of
\Lambda^{\perp}

given the shape of
\Lambda
, even though the latter lattice determines
the first.

We remark that the equidistribution of shapes of primitive
d
-lattices
was shown in a non-quantitative form by Schmidt in
[
Sch98
]
.

Counting
d
-lattices: Beyond shapes.

There exists a wide body of work on equidistribution problems in the
space

\mathcal{L}_{n}:=\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),

which is the space of unimodular (i.e. with covolume one and positive
orientation) full lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as well as in the space

\mathcal{X}_{n}
of their shapes. The restriction to covolume one
is due to the fact that the space
\mathcal{L}_{n}
(and therefore
\mathcal{X}_{n}
)
has finite volume, while the space of all lattices,
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,
does not. Comparing the spaces
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{X}_{n}
,
the space
\mathcal{L}_{n}
naturally contains “more information”
than
\mathcal{X}_{n}
, which is obtained by modding
\mathcal{L}_{n}

by rotations. This brings up the question of whether one can define
a space of “unimodular
d
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
” so as to consider
the unimodular
d
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
without modding out by
rotations. In Section
2
we introduce
two such spaces, which are homogeneous spaces of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, but
do not carry an
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure. However, they do
support a natural measure which is invariant under a direct product
of two subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. We will prove a stronger statement
than Theorem
1.1
, namely Theorem
3.2
,
where we count primitive
d
-lattices according to their projections
to these more refined spaces, from which we conclude Theorem
1.1
,
of counting primitive
d
-lattices according to their projections
to
\mathcal{X}_{d}
.

Organization of the paper.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2

we define all the spaces and notions related to lattices that appear
throughout this work. In Section
3
we
state Theorem
3.2
, and explain how it implies Theorem

1.1
; then the rest of the paper is devoted to proving
Theorem
3.2
. In Section
4

we define coordinates on
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that are a refinement of the
Iwasawa coordinates, and study their properties. In Section
5

we identify subsets of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that are sets of representatives
for all the quotient spaces that appear in Theorem
3.2
.
In Section
6
,
we associate to each primitive
d
-lattice a unique element in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,
transferring the problem of counting primitive
d
-lattices to the
problem of counting points of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
in a family of increasing
sets in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. These sets are not compact, even though the
number of lattice points in each of the sets is finite. In Section

7
, we reduce to counting in
a family of compact subsets, obtaining a “classical” counting
lattice point problem in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. In Section
8

we state the solution of this counting problem (Proposition
8.1
),
and prove Theorem
3.2
based on it. In Section
9

we prove Proposition
8.1
using
a method developed by Gorodnik and Nevo in
[
GN12
]
, for counting
lattice points in semi-simple Lie groups. In the appendix, we expand
further on the notions introduced in Section
2
,
as some of them are new, and others are not new but possibly unfamiliar
to the reader.

2
Spaces of lattices

We begin by defining explicitly the spaces
\mathcal{X}_{d}
and
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)

appearing in Theorem
1.1
. An oriented subspace on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a subspace with a sign attached, and the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)

is the set of all
d
-dimensional oriented subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It can also be defined as the following quotients

\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)=\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&0_{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right\}=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&\mathbb{R}^{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d,d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

where
P_{d}
is the group of upper triangular matrices of determinant

1
with positive diagonal entries. A coset of
\operatorname{SO}_{n}
(or
\operatorname{SL}_{n}
)
represents an oriented
d
-dimensional subspace
V
if the first

d
columns of the matrices in this coset span
V
with the corresponding
orientation.

Recall that the shape of (an oriented) lattice is its equivalent class
modulo homothety and rotation. The space of shapes of (oriented)
d
-lattices
is

\mathcal{X}_{d}=\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\simeq P_{d}/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right).

Both
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
and
\mathcal{X}_{d}
are equipped with natural
measures that decent from the Haar measure on ambient group, and are
unique up to a positive scalar – for
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
it
is the measure that is invariant under
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
, and
for
\mathcal{X}_{d}
it is the measure that is obtained from the Haar
measure on
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The specific normalizations we choose for
these measures are given in Section
2.3
.

We now proceed to define a space of
d
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that
encodes both their shapes, and their directions (i.e. their projections
to the Grassmannian).

2.1
Spaces of homothety classes of
d
-lattices.

A
unimodular
lattice is an oriented lattice with positive orientation
and covolume one. Recall our notation for the space of rank
n
unimodular
lattices

\mathcal{L}_{n}=\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),

where a matrix in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
lies in the coset that
represents a full unimodular lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if its columns
span this lattice. As the space of shapes
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is obtained
from
\mathcal{L}_{n}
by modding by
\operatorname{SO}_{n}
, the space
\mathcal{L}_{n}

is more refined, containing not only the information about the shape
of a lattice, but also about its position in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To define
the analogous space for
d
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, notice that since

\mathcal{L}_{n}
consists of a unique representative from any equivalence
class of
n
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
modulo homothety, one can identify

\mathcal{L}_{n}
with the space of such equivalence classes. The space
of homothety classes of oriented
d
-lattices inside
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\mathcal{L}_{d,n}:=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{R}^{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\times\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\frac{1}{d}}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\alpha^{-\frac{1}{n-d}}\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:\alpha>0\right\}\right),

where a matrix in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
lies in the coset that
represents an equivalence class of a
d
-lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
and only if its first
d
columns span a positive scalar multiplication
of this lattice, with the corresponding orientation. The need to mod
out by the block-scalar group follows from the fact that the first

d
columns of a matrix in
\operatorname{SL}_{n}
span a lattice that is hardly
ever unimodular, so one needs to mod out by the covolume (hence an
element in this quotient space is an equivalence class of
d
-lattices
up to homothety). However, modding out the block-scalar group comes
with a price, which is that the space
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
does not carry
an
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure. To fix this flaw, we claim (Remark

4.1
) that

\mathcal{L}_{d,n}\simeq\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{d}(\mathbb{R})&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

and observe that, while the quotiented manifold is not a group,
it is diffeomorphic to the group
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\times\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{d}(\mathbb{R})&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
carries a Radon measure that is invariant
under this group, and unique up to a positive scalar. This measure
is the natural one in the sense that, as we shall see below, it projects
to the uniform measures on
\mathcal{L}_{d}
and
\mathcal{X}_{d}
. With
respect to it,
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
has finite volume (which wouldn’t
have been the case if we hadn’t modded out by the covolume, just like
in the case of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
).

2.2
Factor lattices and the space of pairs.

Recall that a lattice
\Lambda
is primitive if it is of the form
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap V_{\Lambda}
;
this is equivalent to the fact that any basis of
\Lambda
can be completed
to a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Theorem
1.1
consists of
a joint equidistribution result for primitive lattices
\Lambda
and
their orthogonal complements
\Lambda^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
.
It is a consequence of the stronger Theorem
3.2

below, in which
\Lambda^{\perp}
is replaced by another
\left(n-d\right)
-lattice
in
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
:

Definition 2.1
.

The
factor lattice

\Lambda^{\pi}
of a primitive
d
-lattice

\Lambda
is the projection of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
.

The factor lattice
\Lambda^{\pi}
is isometric to the quotient
\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda

(Proposition
B.3
), so one should think
of
\Lambda^{\pi}
as a realization of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}/\Lambda
inside
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Notice that, like
\Lambda^{\perp}
,
\Lambda^{\pi}
is a full lattice
inside
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
, and in particular is of rank
n-d
.
The relation between
\Lambda^{\perp}
and
\Lambda^{\pi}
is that
they are
dual
to one another (for the definition of dual lattices,
see
[
Cas71
, I.5]
, or Appendix
A

in the present paper. For the duality of
\Lambda^{\perp}
and
\Lambda^{\pi}
,
see Claim
B.5
). It holds that

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{\pi})=\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)^{-1}=\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{\perp})^{-1}

(
[
Sch67
,
Sch68
]
; see also Proposition
B.4

and Corollary
B.6
in the Appendix). Since
we view
d
-lattices as carrying an orientation, we need to define
an orientation on
\Lambda^{\pi}
and
\Lambda^{\perp}
, which is
done as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
L
be a full lattice
in
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
. A basis
\mathbf{C}
for
L
is positively
oriented if
\det(\mathbf{B}|\mathbf{C})=1
for a positively oriented basis

\mathbf{B}
of
\Lambda
.

The last space we introduce is the space of pairs of oriented lattices

(\Lambda,L)
such that (i)
\Lambda
is a
d
-lattice, (ii)
L

is a full lattice in
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
(hence is of rank
n-d

and its orientation is given in Definition
2.2
),
and (iii)
\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)\operatorname{covol}(L)=1
. In fact, it is the space
of homothety classes of such pairs, where
(\Lambda,L)
is equivalent
to
(\alpha^{\frac{1}{d}}\Lambda,\alpha^{-\frac{1}{n-d}}L)
for every
\alpha>0
.
This space is given as the quotient

\text{$\mathcal{P}_{d,n}$}:=\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{R}^{d,n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\times\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha^{\frac{1}{d}}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\alpha^{-\frac{1}{n-d}}\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:\alpha>0\right\}\right).

Note the similarity to the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
above;
here also, modding out the block-scalar group results in having to

\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure, whih can be fixed by observing (Remark

4.1
) that

\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\simeq\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{d}(\mathbb{R})&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&P_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

and therefore
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
carries a Radon measure that is invariant
under the group
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\times\left[\begin{smallmatrix}P_{d}(\mathbb{R})&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&P_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, and is unique up to a positive scalar.

More details on dual lattices and factor lattices can be found in
the appendix.

Remark 2.4
.

For completeness, we provide an explicit description of the above
maps. The projection
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
is the one
of modding by
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{SL}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right]
\ from the right, and the projection
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}\to\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)

is modding by
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&\operatorname{I}_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
from the right. The projections
\mathcal{L}_{i}\to\mathcal{X}_{i}

for
i=d,n-d
are given by modding from the left by
\operatorname{SO}_{i}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
.
The projections
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{X}_{n-d}

, as mentioned, depend on a choice of coordinates and will be explicated
in Remark
4.2
. The
projection
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{X}_{d}
is modding from the
left by
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{I}_{d}&0_{d\times n-d}\\
0_{n-d\times d}&P_{n-d}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, and similarly for
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}\to\mathcal{X}_{n-d}
.

The projections depicted in Diagram (
2.2
) are critical
to understanding why the measures we introduced on
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}

and
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
are indeed the natural
uniform measures on these spaces; as we shall see below (Lemma
2.5
),
with a consistent choice of normalizations, these measures project
to the uniform measures on
\mathcal{L}_{d}
,
\mathcal{X}_{d}
and

\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
.

Normalizations of the measures.

As explained above, all the spaces considered are endowed with invariant
Radon measures that are unique up to a positive scalar, and have finite
volume with respect to these measures. Hence, by fixing the volume
of the entire space, a unique measure is determined. This is what
we now turn to do, where all the unique measures obtained are denoted
by
\operatorname{vol}
(the space in question should be understood from the context).

In accordance with the fact that
\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SO}_{d-1}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

is diffeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we choose a Haar measure
on the special orthogonal group such that
\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))=\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\cdot\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{d-1}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))
,
namely:

\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))=\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}.

We refer to
[
Gar14
]
for the fact that, with our standard
choice (Section
4.3
) of Haar measure on

\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right))=\prod_{i=2}^{d}\zeta\left(i\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}/\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))
,
where
\zeta
is the Riemann Zeta function. Thus,

\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{L}_{d})=\prod_{i=2}^{d}\zeta\left(i\right).

Similarly, in accordance with the fact that
\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\times\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))

is diffeomorphic to the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
, we choose to normalize
the volume of the Grassmannian as
\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))/(\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right))\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{SO}_{n-d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)))
,
namely

\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{Gr}(d,n))=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}\prod_{i=1}^{n-d-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}}=\frac{\prod_{i=n-d}^{n-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})}}.

Finally, recall from Theorem

Appendix: Dual lattices, factor Lattices

This appendix is completely independent from the paper. It aims to
be a source for facts that are not necessarily unknown or hard to
prove, but can be hard to find in the literature. For example, while
the concept of dual lattices (sometimes referred to as
polar

lattices) is well known
[
Cas71
]
, and we have also found
the notes
[
Reg04
]
very useful), the concept of factor
lattices, a term coined by Schmidt in
[
Sch68
]
, is much
less known, and in fact we have not found any reference for it. Another
aim of this appendix is to add the differential perspective of smooth
maps between spaces of lattices. For example, we will see that the
map sending a
d
-lattice to its dual is an auto-diffeomorphism of
the space of
d
-lattices inside
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In what follows, if the columns of a matrix
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{n\times d}

span a
d
-lattice
\Lambda<\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp. a subspace
V<\mathbb{R}^{n}
),
we say that
\mathbf{B}
is a basis for the lattice
\Lambda
(resp. the subspace
V
). Recall that the covolume of a
d
-lattice
\Lambda
,
which is the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped for
\Lambda

in the linear subspace that it spans, equals
(|\det(\mathbf{B}^{\mbox{t}}\mathbf{B})|)^{1/2}

where
\mathbf{B}
is (any) a basis for
\Lambda
. We will use an underscore
to denote that an object is being spanned by a set, so that
\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}

is the lattice spanned (over
\mathbb{Z}
) by
\mathbf{B}
,

is the
linear space spanned (over
\mathbb{R}
) by
\Lambda
, etc.

Appendix A
Dual lattices

Given a
d
-lattice
\Lambda<\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
dual lattice

of
\Lambda

\Lambda^{*}=\left\{y\in\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda):\forall x\in\Lambda,\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Note that it is contained in
V_{\Lambda}
, and that a priori, it is
unclear that
\Lambda^{*}
is indeed a lattice.

Proposition A.1
.

The matrix
\mathbf{D}_{n\times d}:=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\mbox{t}}\mathbf{B})^{-1}

is basis for
\Lambda^{*}
.

This proposition motivates the notation
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{B}^{*}
for

\mathbf{D}
as above, as well as the name
the dual basis
of
\mathbf{B}
.
The proof will make use of the following lemma:

Lemma A.2
.

\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{D}
span the
same
d
-dimensional linear space in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and their columns
satisfy the orthonormality relation

\left\langle b_{i},d_{j}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}1&i=j\\
0&i\neq j\end{cases}

where
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
are the columns of
\mathbf{B}
and
(d_{i})_{i=1}^{d}

are the columns of
\mathbf{D}
.

Proof.

The matrix
\mathbf{D}
is of the form
\mathbf{B}g
where
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
so
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{D}
span the same linear space in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The orthonormality relation follows from the fact that
\mathbf{B}^{\mbox{t}}\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{I}_{d}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

In order to show
\Lambda_{\mathbf{D}}=\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
, we will prove
two inclusions. For
\Lambda_{\mathbf{D}}\supseteq\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
,
we first note that by Lemma
A.2
,
V_{\mathbf{D}}\supseteq\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
.
As a result, an element
y\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
is of the form

y=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}d_{i}
, where
(d_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
are
the columns of
\mathbf{D}
. Let
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
be the columns
of
\mathbf{B}
. It remains to show that
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
j
,
which is the case since

\alpha_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left\langle b_{i},d_{j}\right\rangle=\langle b_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}d_{j}\rangle=\left\langle b_{i},y\right\rangle\in\mathbb{Z},

where in the second equality we applied Lemma
A.2
,
and in the final inclusion we used
b_{i}\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}
and
y\in\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
.

For the reverse inclusion
\Lambda_{\mathbf{D}}\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
,
it is sufficient to check that
\mathbf{D}\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
,
namely that
\left\langle x,d_{j}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
for every

. Indeed, write

where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then again by Lemma

\left\langle x,d_{j}\right\rangle=\langle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}b_{i},d_{j}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\left\langle b_{i},d_{j}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i,j}=\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}.

The dual lattice

Proof.

Remark A.10
.

Since the spaces
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
are unbounded
but of finite volume, it is desireble to have a criterion for determining
when a set is compact, or alternatively, when does a sequence of elements
in the space diverge to infinity. For the more familiar space of full
unimodular lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
,
which is also non-compact but of finite measure, the answer is provided
by Mahler’s Compactness Criterion
[
BM00
, Chapter V.3]
.
The latter states that a set is compact if and only if there exists

\delta>0
such that all the lattices in this set have shortest vector
of length
>\delta
. Equivalently, a sequence of lattices
\{\Lambda_{m}\}

diverges if and only if the lengths of the shortest vectors in
\{\Lambda_{m}\}

is a squence of positive real numbers that converges to zero. The
purpose of the following is to state an analogous criterion for compactness
in the spaces
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
. For every
equivalence class
\left[\Lambda_{0}\right]\in\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
(resp.
\left[(\Lambda_{0},L_{0})\right]\in\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
),
a
unimodular representative
is the unique representative
\Lambda\in\left[\Lambda_{0}\right]

of covolume one (resp.
(\Lambda,L)\in\left[(\Lambda_{0},L_{0})\right]

such that
\Lambda
and
L
are of covolume one).

Proposition A.11
.

A subset
\Psi
of
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}
is bounded iff there is some

\delta>0
such that for every
\left[\Lambda_{0}\right]\in\Psi
, its unimodular
representative
\Lambda\in\left[\Lambda_{0}\right]
has the property that the
shortest vector of
\Lambda
is of length
\geq\delta
.

A subset
\varXi
of
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
is bounded iff there is
some
\delta>0
such that for every
\left[(\Lambda_{0},L_{0})\right]\in\varXi
,
its unimodular representative
(\Lambda,L)\in\left[(\Lambda_{0},L_{0})\right]

has the property that both shortest vectors of
\Lambda
and

.

Proof.

We will prove the claim for
\mathcal{P}_{d,n}
, since case of
\mathcal{L}_{d,n}

is similar. It is not hard to see (e.g., Remark

Relation between symmetries of a lattice and of its dual

A
shape
of a lattice (sometimes referred to as a
type

of a lattice) is its similarity class modulo rotation and rescaling.
It is a very common parameter to study in the context of lattices,
and appears e.g. in crystallography and the theory of periodic tilings.
If two lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
have the same shape, then in particular
they are invariant under the same symmetries, meaning the same orthogonal
transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The opposite does not hold, e.g. the
lattices
\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\alpha e_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\beta e_{2}

for
1<\alpha<\beta
have different shapes, but both are invariant only
under the orthogonal transformations
\pm\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right],\pm\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\operatorname{O}_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
. Below we observe the surprising fact that even though a lattice
and its dual in general do not have the same shape (e.g.,
\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\alpha e_{2}

and
\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}e_{2}/\alpha
are dual), they do share the same
group of symmetries.

Definition A.12
.

Let
1\leq d\leq n
, and a
d
-lattice
\Lambda
. The finite group

\mbox{Sym}(\Lambda):=\left\{g\in\operatorname{O}(V_{\Lambda}):g\Lambda=\Lambda\right\}

is called the
symmetry group
of
\Lambda
.

Claim A.13
.

The symmetry groups of

\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{*}
are identical.

Proof.

Let
B_{n\times d}
be a matrix for
\Lambda
, so that
D=B\left(B^{\mbox{t}}B\right)^{-1}

is a matrix for
\Lambda^{*}
. Assume
k\in\operatorname{O}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is
in the symmetry group of
\Lambda
, i.e. there exists
\gamma\in\operatorname{GL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

such that
kB=B\gamma
. It follows that
kD=B\gamma^{-\mbox{t}}
, since

kD=kB\left(B^{\mbox{t}}B\right)^{-1}=kB\left(\left(kB\right)^{\mbox{t}}\left(kB\right)\right)^{-1}

and this equals

=B\gamma(\left(B\gamma\right)^{\mbox{t}}\left(B\gamma\right))^{-1}=B\gamma(\gamma^{\mbox{t}}B^{\mbox{t}}B\gamma)^{-1}=B\gamma\cdot\gamma^{-1}(B^{\mbox{t}}B)^{-1}\gamma^{-\mbox{t}}=D\gamma^{-\mbox{t}}.

This means that
k
is also in the symmetry group of
\Lambda^{*}
,
so
\mbox{Sym}\left(\Lambda\right)\subseteq\mbox{Sym}(\Lambda^{*})\subseteq\mbox{Sym}((\Lambda^{*})^{*})=\mbox{Sym}\left(\Lambda\right)
,
resulting in
\mbox{Sym}\left(\Lambda\right)=\mbox{Sym}(\Lambda^{*})
.
∎

on the successive minima of the dual lattice.

Theorem A.14

(

(A.1)

such

[

.

Lemma A.15
.

Let
L

be a
k
-lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
