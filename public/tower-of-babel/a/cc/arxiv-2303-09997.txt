Banach algebras associated to twisted étale groupoids: inverse semigroup disintegration and representations on L p L^{p} -spaces
Krzysztof Bardadyn, Bartosz Kwaśniewski, Andrew McKee

Abstract
. We introduce Banach algebras associated to twisted étale groupoids ( 𝒢 , ℒ ) (\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}) and to twisted inverse semigroup actions. This provides a unifying framework for numerous recent papers on L p L^{p} -operator algebras and the theory of groupoid C ∗ C^{*} -algebras. We prove disintegrations theorems that allow to study Banach algebras associated to ( 𝒢 , ℒ ) (\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}) as universal Banach algebras generated by C 0 ​ ( X ) C_{0}(X) and a twisted inverse semigroup S S of partial isometries subject to some relations. They work best when the target of a representation is a dual Banach algebra. For representations on dual Banach spaces, they allow to extend representations to twisted Borel convolution algebras, which is crucial when the groupoid is non-Hausdorff. We establish fundamental norm estimates and hierarchy for full and reduced L p L^{p} -operator algebras for ( 𝒢 , ℒ ) (\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}) and p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] , whose special cases have been studied recently by Gardella–Lupini, Choi–Gardella–Thiel and Hetland–Ortega. We show that in the constructions of L p L^{p} -analogues of Cuntz or graph algebras, by Phillips and Cortiñas–Rodríguez, the use of spatial partial isometries is not an assumption, in fact it is forced by the relations. We also introduce tight inverse semigroup Banach algebras that cover ample groupoid Banach algebras, and discuss Banach algebras associated to directed graphs. Our results cover non-Hausdorff étale groupoids and both real and complex algebras. Some of the results are new already for complex C ∗ C^{*} -algebras.

Since the category of (real or complex)

Thus by a

A

For an inverse semigroup

into contractive elements of

Then the operators

in the sense of Mbekhta

(which are the usual partial isometries when

-algebra).
For a
twisted action

(\alpha,u)
, à la Buss–Exel
[
17
]

S
on a Banach algebra
A
we define the crossed product
A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u)}S
as a Banach algebra universal for
covariant representations

(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
,
where
\pi
is a representation of
A
in
B

v
is a twisted representation of
S
in
B

[
68
]
,
[
17
]
.
To get uniqueness of
disintegration
of representations of

In different situations this could mean different things.
When the domains of the action are not unital this requires weak limits and so normalization works well for instance when
(B,B_{*})
is a dual Banach algebra in the sense of Runde, see
[
65

,
[
29

. The latter case covers covariant representations on reflexive Banach spaces
E

We discuss these issues in detail in Section

For a twisted étale groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})

*

\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
of quasi-continuous compactly supported sections has plenty of natural Banach algebra completions.
We decided to work only with norms that coincide with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
for sections with supports in open bisections (apparently all norms considered so far in the literature have this property).
For any unital inverse semigroup
S
of open bisections covering

we define the

F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
If

is wide and consists of bisections where the bundle
\mathcal{L}
is trivial (which can be always arranged), we have an inverse semigroup action
(\alpha,u)
of

(this is implicit in
[
17
]

Our first main result (Theorem

F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\cong C_{0}(X)\rtimes_{(\alpha,u)}S

that establishes a bijective correspondence between representations of
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and normalized covariant representations of
(\alpha,u)
.
In our second disintegration theorem we use that the action
(\alpha,u)
of
S
on
C_{0}(X)
extends naturally to an action
(\widetilde{\alpha},u)
of
S
on the algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)

\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
of Borel sections of
\mathcal{L}

so that we have

F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}}\cong\mathcal{B}(X)\rtimes_{(\widetilde{\alpha},u)}S.

Moreover, every representation of
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
on a dual Banach space

extends to a representation of
\mathcal{B}(X)\rtimes_{(\widetilde{\alpha},u)}S
,
and so it disintegrates to a covariant representation of
(\widetilde{\alpha},u)
on
E

This can be viewed (see Corollary
4.33
) as a far reaching generalization of similar recent results in
[
20
]

[
16

, which were recently used in the study of amenability of the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra

when
\mathcal{G}
is non-Hausdorff. We believe this tool will be useful in other contexts.

These results can be applied to Banach algebras
F_{\mathcal{R}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
which are universal for representations of
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
from a fixed class
\mathcal{R}
of representations.
For

universal twisted groupoid

-operator algebra

as

whose restriction to

is positive in Banach lattice sense. Here the main structural result (Theorem

-operator algebras.
In fact, our disintegration theorem allows us to prove (see Theorem

)
that denoting by

\|f\|_{L^{1}}:=\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{d(\gamma)=x}|f(\gamma)|,\qquad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}:=\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{r(\gamma)=x}|f(\gamma)|

These estimates are already interesting when

-operator algebra crossed products agree with

-operator algebras of the corresponding transformation groupoids – the problem formulated below

-operator algebra

-projection

Our third main result (Theorem

,

is universal for

, i.e. covariant representations

).

and

2.
Representations of inverse semigroups and
C_{0}(X)

Most of the facts presented here are probably known to experts, so we included only sketches of, or coordinates leading to, the arguments when we were not able to find a reference in appropriate generality.
We discuss representations of
C_{0}(X)
on
L^{p}
-spaces with
p\in[1,\infty]
, and the key result here is Theorem
2.13
. For an introduction to
L^{p}
-operator algebras we refer to
[
37
]
. We generalize spatial partial isometries of Phillips and Gardella by defining
L^{p}
-partial isometries on an arbitrary Banach space
E
. The link between the two is provided by a version of the Banach–Lamperti theorem (Theorem
2.28
).
Also we recall the fundamental relationship between actions of inverse semigroups on topological spaces and étale groupoids.

2.1.
Spatial isometries on
L^{p}
-spaces

Throughout we fix
p\in[1,\infty]
and the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
of real or complex numbers.
If
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is a measure space we write
L^{p}(\mu)
for the corresponding Lebesgue space viewed as a Banach space over
\mathbb{F}
.
Occasionally, to emphasize which field is used, we will write
L^{p}(\mu,\mathbb{F})
.
Recall that the Banach space
L^{p}(\mu)
can be recovered from
\mu:\Sigma\to[0,\infty]
, or in fact from the measure
[\mu]:[\Sigma]\to[0,\infty]
defined on the Boolean algebra
[\Sigma]
obtained from
\Sigma
by identifying sets of measure zero (the presentation in
[
37
]
,
[
41
]
uses this ‘point-free’ approach to
L^{p}
-spaces, but we stay with the old-fashioned approach).
Also if
p<\infty
, then
L^{p}(\mu)\cong L^{p}(\mu_{f})
where
\mu_{f}(A)=\sup\{\mu(B):A\supseteq B\in\Sigma,\mu(B)<\infty\}
is the
semi-finite
part of
\mu
, and the isomorphism is identity on integrable simple functions.
Recall that a measure
\mu
is called
localizable
if it is semi-finite and
[\Sigma]
is Dedekind complete.
In general, if
\mu
is semi-finite, then
[\mu]
extends uniquely to the Dedekind completion of
[\Sigma]
giving a localizable measure on the completion
[
36
]
.
Using the Loomis–Sikorski Theorem we may represent this abstract measure by a concrete localizable measure space
(\overline{\Omega},\overline{\Sigma},\overline{\mu})
(see
[
36
]
,
[
12
]
) and again
L^{p}(\mu)\cong L^{p}(\overline{\mu})
by the isometric isomorphism sending characteristic functions to characteristic functions.
Thus when considering
L^{p}
-spaces with
p<\infty
we may always assume the measure is localizable, or even decomposable (strictly localizable), see
[
53
, Corollary on page 136]
or
[
12
, Theorem 7.17]
).
For
p=\infty
the assumption that
\mu
is localizable is also natural. For instance,
\mu
is localizable if and only if
L^{\infty}(\mu)
is canonically isomorphic to the dual of
L^{1}(\mu)
if and only if
L^{\infty}(\mu)
is canonically a von Neumann algebra acting on
L^{2}(\mu)
(and every abelian
W^{*}
-algebra is of this form), see
[
12
]
and references therein.

Definition 2.1
.

A
set isomorphism
from a measure space
(\Omega_{\mu},\Sigma_{\mu},\mu)
onto a measure space
(\Omega_{\nu},\Sigma_{\nu},\nu)
is a map
\Phi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\mu}
that descends to an isomorphism of Boolean algebras
[\Phi]:[\Sigma_{\nu}]\to[\Sigma_{\mu}]
.
Thus, since the Boolean structure is determined by its preorder, it suffices to assume

(1)

\nu(C\setminus D)=0
if and only if
\mu(\Phi(C)\setminus\Phi(D))=0
for all
C,D\in\Sigma_{\nu}
;

(2)

for each
C\in\Sigma_{\mu}
there is
\Phi^{*}(C)\in\Sigma_{\nu}
such that
\Phi(\Phi^{*}(C))=C
up to a
\mu
-null set.

Condition
(2)
gives a map
\Phi^{*}:\Sigma_{\mu}\to\Sigma_{\nu}
, which is a set isomorphism that descends to an isomorphism
[\Phi^{*}]:[\Sigma_{\mu}]\to[\Sigma_{\nu}]
inverse to
[\Phi]:[\Sigma_{\nu}]\to[\Sigma_{\mu}]
.

We denote by
L_{0}(\mu)
the algebra of
\mathbb{F}
-valued measurable functions identified up to equality
\mu
-almost everywhere.
A set isomorphism
\Phi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\mu}
uniquely defines a linear operator
T_{\Phi}:L_{0}(\nu)\to L_{0}(\mu)
such that

T_{\Phi}1_{C}=1_{\Phi(C)},\qquad C\in\Sigma_{\nu},

and
T_{\Phi}
preserves (monotone) limits, cf.
[
59
, Proposition 5.6]
.
This is an algebra isomorphism
L_{0}(\nu)\cong L_{0}(\mu)
whose inverse is
T_{\Phi^{*}}
.
We call
T_{\Phi}
a (generalized)
composition operator
.
It preserves the range of functions and in particular it restricts to an isometric isomorphism

[

, Proposition 7.16]
.
However, unless

preserves measures, it does not preserve the

.
To force this, we will consider

aT_{\Phi}:L_{0}(\nu)\to L_{0}(\mu)

.
Note that

is a measure equivalent to
\nu

\nu
is localizable then
the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{\mu\circ\Phi}{\nu}
exists, and is strictly positive
\nu
-almost everywhere, see
[

, Theorem 2.7]
,
[
12
, Theorem 4.4]
.
Similar comments apply to the measures
\nu\circ\Phi^{*}
and
\mu
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider localizable measures
\mu
and
\nu
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.
For any set isomorphism
\Phi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\mu}
the weighted composition operator

U_{\Phi}:=\left(\frac{d\nu\circ\Phi^{*}}{d\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}T_{\Phi}

is an invertible isometry
U_{\Phi}:L^{p}(\nu)\to L^{p}(\mu)
.
Moreover, the group
UL^{\infty}(\mu)
of measurable modulus one functions
\omega\in L_{0}(\mu)
embeds into the group of invertible isometries of

as multiplication operators.
Thus for any
\omega\in UL^{\infty}(\mu)
the weighted composition operator
\omega U_{\Phi}:L^{p}(\nu)\to L^{p}(\mu)
is an invertible isometry.

Proof.

This is straightforward and known, see, for instance,
[
41
, Lemmas 3.2, 3.3]
.
∎

Definition 2.3

(
[
59
]
)
.

We call the invertible isometries
\omega U_{\Phi}
described in Lemma

spatial
.

Proposition 2.4
.

We have
(\omega U_{\Phi})^{-1}=T_{\Phi^{*}}(\overline{\omega})U_{\Phi^{*}}
and
\omega U_{\Phi}\circ\upsilon T_{\Psi}=\omega T_{\Phi}(\upsilon)U_{\Phi\circ\Psi}
for all localizable measures
\mu
,
\nu
,
\eta
, all

, set isomorphisms
\Phi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\mu}
,
\Psi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\eta}
and functions
\omega\in UL^{\infty}(\mu)
,
\upsilon\in UL^{\infty}(\nu)
.

In particular, invertible spatial isometries on

\mu

UL^{\infty}(\mu)\rtimes\Aut([\Sigma_{\mu}])

where

Proof.

The first part follows from (the calculations in the proof of)
[

[

∎

Proposition 2.5

(Banach–Lamperti theorem)
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]\setminus\{2\}
and
\mu,\nu
be localizable measures.
Then every invertible isometry from
L^{p}(\mu)
to
L^{p}(\nu)
is spatial.

Proof.

For
p\in(1,\infty)\setminus\{2\}
,
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, and
\mu=\nu
this is proved in
[
41
, Theorem 3.7]
, see also
[
37
, Theorem 2.12]
, but the proof works for two localizable measures
\mu
and
\nu
, and also for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
p=1
.
In fact since
L^{\infty}(\mu)\cong L^{1}(\mu)^{\prime}
the case
p=1
can be deduced from the case
p=\infty
.
The assertion for
p=\infty
follows from the Banach–Stone Theorem combined with the Gelfand Theorem. Indeed, we have
L^{\infty}(\mu)\cong C(X)
and
L^{\infty}(\nu)\cong C(Y)
where
X
,
Y
are compact Hausdorff (hyperstonean) spaces, and every invertible isometry
C(Y)\to C(X)
is of the form
aT
where
a\in C(X)
,
|a|=1
, and
T:C(Y)\to C(X)
is an isometric algebra isomorphism.
The Boolean algebras of idempotent elements in
C(Y)
and
C(X)
are canonically isomorphic to
[\Sigma_{\nu}]
and
[\Sigma_{\mu}]
, respectively.
Thus
T
induces a set isomorphism
\Phi:\Sigma_{\nu}\to\Sigma_{\mu}
such that the action of

T_{\Phi}:L^{\infty}(\nu)\to L^{\infty}(\mu)
on idempotents in
L^{\infty}(\nu)
agrees with the action of
T
on the corresponding idempotents in
C(Y)
.
As the idempotents are linearly dense in the considered spaces we conclude that the isometry
L^{\infty}(\nu)\to L^{\infty}(\mu)
corresponding to
aT
is
\omega T_{\Phi}
where

\omega\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is the function corresponding to
a\in C(X)\cong L^{\infty}(\mu)
. ∎

(Kaplansky–Bonsall)
.

We now slightly extend
[
23
, Proposition 2.7]
, see also
[
14
, Proposition 2.12]
and references therein, which describes hermitian operators acting on complex

-spaces.
This forces the representations of the complex algebra

on
L^{p}
and
C_{0}
-spaces to act by multiplication operators.

Proposition 2.11
.

Assume
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and let
E=L^{p}(\mu)
for a localizable
\mu
and
p\in[1,\infty]\setminus\{2\}
, or
E=C_{0}(\Omega)
for a locally compact Hausdorff space
\Omega
.
An element
a\in B(E)
is hermitian (i.e.
\|e^{ita}\|=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
) if and only if
a
is a multiplication operator by a bounded real-valued function (from
L^{\infty}(\mu,\mathbb{R})
if
E=L^{p}(\mu)
and from
C_{b}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
if
E=C_{0}(\Omega)
).

Proof.

When
E=L^{p}(\mu)
and
p<\infty
this is proved in
[
23
, Proposition 2.7]
.
The proof relies on the Banach–Lamperti Theorem and it also works for
p=\infty
, with the Banach–Lamperti theorem extended as in Proposition
2.5
.
We show it also works for
E=C_{0}(\Omega)
with the Banach–Lamperti theorem replaced by the Banach–Stone theorem. We recall the argument for the ‘only if’ part (the ‘if’ part is straightforward).
Let
a\in B(C_{0}(\Omega))
be hermitian. We may assume that
\|a\|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
The elements
u_{t}:=e^{ita}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, form a continuous group of invertible isometries.
By the Banach–Stone theorem for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
there is
\omega_{t}\in C_{b}(\Omega)
with
|\omega_{t}|=1

and a homeomorphism
\varphi_{t}:\Omega\to\Omega
such that
[u_{t}\xi](x)=\omega_{t}(x)\xi(\varphi_{t}(x))
.
Using this, a simple calculation, for
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, gives

\|u_{t}-u_{s}\|=\max\big\{\|\omega_{t}-\omega_{s}\|_{\infty},2(1-\delta_{\varphi_{t},\varphi_{s}})\big\}.

Since
t\mapsto u_{t}
is norm continuous and
u_{0}=1
is the identity operator, we get that
\varphi_{t}=\mathrm{id}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence each
u_{t}
is the operator of multiplication by
\omega_{t}
.
Since
\|a\|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
the spectrum of
u_{1}=e^{ia}
is contained in the half circle
\{e^{it}:t\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\}
where the holomorphic inverse
\log
of
\exp
is defined.
Applying analytic functional calculus to
u_{1}
we get
ia=\log(u_{1})
.
Since
m:C_{b}(\Omega)\to B(C_{0}(\Omega)));\ m(h)\xi(x):=h(x)\xi(x)
is a unital homomorphism, we obtain
a=-i\log(u_{1})=-i\log(m(\omega_{1}))=m(-i\log(\omega_{1}))
.
Thus
a
is a multiplication operator by
-i\log(\omega_{1})
, and since
a
is bounded and hermitian we must have
-i\log(\omega_{1})\in C_{b}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Recall that
L^{p}
-spaces and
C_{0}
-spaces are naturally Banach lattices with the lattice structure given by positive (non-negative) functions.
Operators preserving positive functions (and therefore the lattice structure) are called
positive
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
E
be a real Banach space of the form
L^{p}(\mu,\mathbb{R})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
or
C_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, and let
E_{\mathbb{C}}
be its Banach lattice complexification, that is either
L^{p}(\mu,\mathbb{C})
or
C_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
.
A representation
\pi:C_{0}(X,\mathbb{R})\to B(E)
which is positive in the sense that it sends positive functions to positive operators, complexifies via the formula

\big(\pi_{\mathbb{C}}(a+ib)\big)(\xi+i\eta):=\pi(a)\xi-\pi(b)\eta+i\big(\pi(a)\eta+\pi(b)\xi\big),

\xi,\eta\in L^{p}(\mu,\mathbb{R})
,
a,b\in C_{0}(X,\mathbb{R})
,
to a representation
\pi_{\mathbb{C}}:C_{0}(X,\mathbb{C})\to B(E_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that
\pi_{\mathbb{C}}
is a complex algebra homomorphism.
Since
\pi
is positive, both
\pi
and
\pi_{\mathbb{C}}
take values in the lattice of regular operators, see
[
1
, Section 3.2]
.
If
E=L^{p}(\mu,\mathbb{R})
, then it is Dedekind complete and so by the Riesz–Kantorovich formula
[
1
, Corollary 3.25]
,
|\pi_{\mathbb{C}}(a+ib)\xi|\leq\pi_{\mathbb{C}}(|a+ib|)|\xi|
for all
\xi\in E_{\mathbb{C}}
.
This implies that
\|\pi_{\mathbb{C}}(a+ib)\|\leq\|\pi_{\mathbb{C}}(|a+ib|)\|=\|\pi(|a+ib|)\|\leq\||a+ib|\|_{\infty}=\|a+ib\|_{\infty}
.
Hence
\pi_{\mathbb{C}}
is contractive.
When
E=C_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
we may pass to duals to reduce to the
L^{1}
-case.
Namely,
E^{\prime}\cong L^{1}(\mu)
for a measure
\mu
and the formula
\pi^{\prime}(a):=\pi(a)^{\prime}
defines a positive representation
\pi^{\prime}:C_{0}(X,\mathbb{R})\to B(L^{1}(\mu))
.
∎

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
\pi:C_{0}(X)\to B(E)
be a non-degenerate representation where

for a localizable
\mu
and

, or

if

Proof.

By Lemma
2.12

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
.
If

is not compact we may extend

∎

Example 2.14

(Representations from involutive isometries)

Corollary 2.15
.

The complex Banach algebra

admits an isometric non-degenerate representation on

Lemma 2.17
.

For any representation

The algebra

2.3.
Inverse semigroups and étale groupoids

Let
S
be a semigroup.
An element
t\in S
is called
partially invertible
if there is an element
t^{*}\in S
such that
t=tt^{*}t
and
t^{*}=t^{*}tt^{*}
.
Then
t^{*}t
and
tt^{*}
are idempotents, and we call
t^{*}
a
generalized inverse
for
t
.
The semigroup
S
is called an
inverse semigroup
if every element in
S
has a unique generalized inverse.
Assume this. Then the map
t\mapsto t^{*}
is an anti-multiplicative involution, and any semigroup homomorphism between two inverse semigroups is automatically
*
-preserving.
The set of idempotents

E(S):=\{e\in S:e^{2}=e\}=\{tt^{*}:t\in S\}=\{t^{*}t:t\in S\}

is an abelian semigroup (see for instance
[
58
, Proposition 2.1.1]
).
A partial order on
S
is defined by
s\leq t
if and only if
s=ts^{*}s
, if and only if there is
e\in E(S)
with
s=te
,
s,t\in S
, see
[
58
]
.
If
S
has a unit
1
then
e\in E(S)
if and only if
e\leq 1
.

A
partial bijection
on a set
X
is a bijection
h:U\to h(U)
between subsets
U
,
h(U)
of
X
. The set
\PBij(X)
of all partial bijections on
X
form an inverse semigroup with composition of
h:U\to h(U)
and
f:V\to f(V)
defined as the bijection
h\circ f:f^{-1}(U\cap f(V))\to h(U\cap f(V))
.
Then
h^{*}=h^{-1}:h(U)\to U
,
h\in E(\PBij(X))
if and only if
h=\mathrm{id}|_{U}
for some
U\subseteq X
, and
h\leq f
if and only if
f
is an extension of
h
.
Every inverse semigroup
S
can be viewed as a subsemigroup of
\PBij(S)
, where
h_{t}:S_{t^{*}}\to S_{t}
is given by
h_{t}(s):=ts
and
S_{t}:=\{s\in S:ss^{*}\leq t^{*}t\}
for all
t\in S
, see
[
58
, Proposition 2.1.3]
.
A
partial homeomorphism
on a topological space
X
is a homeomorphism
h:U\to h(U)
between two open subsets
U
and
h(U)
of
X
.
Partial homeomorphisms form an inverse subsemigroup of
\PBij(X)
that we denote by
\PHomeo(X)
.

Definition 2.18
.

An
action of an inverse semigroup
S
on a topological space
X
is a non-degenerate semigroup homomorphism
h:S\to\PHomeo(X)
: a family of partial homeomorphisms
h_{t}:X_{t^{*}}\to X_{t}
such that
h_{t}\circ h_{s}=h_{ts}
for all
s,t\in S
, and
\bigcup_{t\in S}X_{t}=X
.

A groupoid is a small category
\mathcal{G}
in which every arrow is invertible.
If
\mathcal{G}
(identified with the set of arrows) is equipped with a topology that makes the range, domain, composition, and inverse of arrows continuous, we call
\mathcal{G}
a
topological groupoid
.
We denote by
X\subseteq\mathcal{G}
the
unit space
of
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be an
étale groupoid
with unit space
X
, i.e.
\mathcal{G}
is a topological groupoid where the range and domain maps
r,d:\mathcal{G}\to X\subseteq\mathcal{G}
are local homeomorphisms.
In particular,
X
is an open subset of
\mathcal{G}
.
We will assume that
X
is a locally compact Hausdorff space, in which case
\mathcal{G}
is necessarily locally compact and locally Hausdorff, but
\mathcal{G}
is Hausdorff if and only if
X
is closed in
\mathcal{G}
, see for instance
[
69
, Lemma 2.3.2]
.
A
bisection
of a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is an open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that the restrictions
r|_{U}
and
d|_{U}
are homeomorphisms onto open sets of
\mathcal{G}
.
So a topological groupoid is étale if and only if it can be covered by bisections.

Example 2.19

(Inverse semigroup actions from étale groupoids)
.

The set of bisections
\Bis(\mathcal{G})
of an étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
forms a unital inverse semigroup with multiplication and involution given by

U\cdot V:=\{\gamma\eta:\text{$\gamma\in U,\ \eta\in V$ are composable}\},\qquad U^{*}=U^{-1}:=\{\gamma^{-1}:\gamma\in U\}.

Then
X
is a unit of
\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.
For each
U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})
we have a homeomorphism

h_{U}:=r\circ d|_{U}^{-1}:d(U)\to r(U),

and these homeomorphisms define a
canonical action

h:\Bis(\mathcal{G})\to\PHomeo(X)
on the unit space
X

[
34
, Proposition 5.3]
.
In fact we may view this action as a restriction of a partial action
\tilde{h}:\Bis(\mathcal{G})\to\PHomeo(\mathcal{G})
on the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, where
\tilde{h}_{U}:r^{-1}(d(U))\to r^{-1}(r(U))
is given by
\tilde{h}_{U}(\gamma)=d|_{U}^{-1}(r(\gamma))\gamma
.
In other words,
\tilde{h}_{U}(\gamma)=\eta\gamma
, where
\eta
is the unique element in
U
that can be composed with
\gamma
.
Using this interpretation one sees that
\tilde{h}
is indeed an inverse semigroup action. It restricts to the canonical action
h
in the sense that
h=r\circ\tilde{h}|_{X}
.

Example 2.20

(Étale groupoids from inverse semigroup actions)
.

For any inverse semigroup action
h:S\to\PHomeo(X)
the
transformation groupoid

S\ltimes_{h}X
is defined as follows, see
[
58
, p. 140]
or
[
52
, 2.1]
. In
[
34
, Section 4]
it is called the groupoid of germs, but we believe this term should be reserved for the different construction that is used for instance in
[
63
]
.
The arrows of
S\ltimes_{h}X
are equivalence classes of pairs
(t,x)
for
x\in X_{t^{*}}\subseteq X
; two pairs
(t,x)
and
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
are equivalent if
x=x^{\prime}
and there is
v\in S
with
v\leq t,t^{\prime}
and
x\in X_{v^{*}}
.
The range and domain maps
r,d:S\ltimes_{h}X\rightrightarrows X
and the multiplication are defined by
r([t,x]):=h_{t}(x)
,
d([t,x]):=x
, and
[s,h_{t}(x)]\cdot[t,x]=[s\cdot t,x]
for
x\in X_{(st)^{*}}
.
We give
S\ltimes_{h}X
the unique topology such that the sets
U_{t}:=\{[t,x]:x\in X_{t^{*}}\}
are open bisections of
S\ltimes_{h}X
.
Then
S\ltimes_{h}X
is an étale groupoid and the map
S\ni t\mapsto U_{t}\in\Bis(S\ltimes_{h}X)
is a semigroup homomorphism.
Composing it with the canonical homomorphism
\Bis(S\ltimes_{h}X)\to\PHomeo(X)
we recover the action
h
.
Composing it with the canonical homomorphism
\Bis(S\ltimes_{h}X)\to\PHomeo(S\ltimes_{h}X)
we
get an extension
\tilde{h}
of
h
where

\tilde{h}_{t}:\{[s,x]:x\in h_{s}^{-1}(X_{t^{*}})\}\to\{[s,x]:x\in h_{s}^{-1}(X_{t})\}
is given by
\tilde{h}_{t}([s,x]):=[ts,x].

In this way every inverse semigroup action can be viewed as coming from an étale groupoid, and then it can be extended to an action on this groupoid.
Conversely, for any étale groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and any inverse subsemigroup
S\subseteq\text{Bis}(\mathcal{G})
, acting canonically on
X
, we have a continuous open groupoid homomorphism
S\ltimes_{h}X\ni[U,x]\mapsto(d|_{U})^{-1}(x)\in\mathcal{G}
.
This is an isomorphism
S\ltimes_{h}X\cong\mathcal{G}
if and only if
S
is
wide
in the sense that
S
covers
G
and
U\cap V
is a union of bisections in
S
for all
U,V\in S
, see
[
34
, Proposition 5.4]
,
[
52
, Proposition 2.2]
. In particular, every étale groupoid is a transformation groupoid.

2.4.
L^{p}
-partial isometries on Banach spaces and spatial partial isometries

Partial isometries acting on arbitrary Banach spaces were defined by Mbekhta
[
56
, Definition 4.1]
, see also
[
23
, 8.1]
, and in Banach algebras the definition can be phrased as follows.

Definition 2.21
.

A
partial isometry in a Banach algebra

B
is a partially invertible element
t
in the semigroup
B_{1}
of contractive elements, so
t\in B_{1}
and there exists
t^{*}\in B_{1}
such that
t=tt^{*}t
and
t^{*}=t^{*}tt^{*}
.
If
B
is unital and complex we say that
t\in B
is an
MP
-partial isometry
if
t
is contractive and admits a contractive Moore–Penrose generalized inverse
t^{*}\in B
, i.e.
t^{*}
is a generalised inverse of
t
and the idempotents
tt^{*}
and
t^{*}t
are hermitian.

If
B
is a
C^{*}
-algebra (real or complex) then a contractive generalized inverse to a partial isometry
t\in B
is necessarily the adjoint element
t^{*}\in B
, see
[
56
, 3.1, 3.3]
and Remark
2.9
, so in this case both notions in Definition
2.21
coincide with the usual notion of partial isometry in a
C^{*}
-algebra.
However, for many Banach spaces
E
, including
L^{1}(\mu)
,
c_{0}
,
\ell^{\infty}
, a partial isometry
t\in B(E)
may have many contractive generalized inverses.
On the other hand, if the Moore–Penrose generalized inverse exists it is necessarily unique.
So the
MP
-partial isometries have uniquely determined generalized inverse
MP
-partial isometries.
Partial isometries usually do not form a semigroup (the product of two partial isometries
s,t\in B
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
is a partial isometry if and only if the projections
s^{*}s
and
tt^{*}
commute, see
[
43
]
).
For
p\in[1,\infty]\setminus\{2\}
and any Banach space
E
we introduce a natural inverse semigroup of partial isometries in
B(E)
.
Recall, see
[
9
]
,
[
3
]
, that an
L^{p}
-projection on
E
is an idempotent
P\in B(E)
such that for all
\xi\in E
we have

\|\xi\|^{p}=\|P\xi\|^{p}+\|(1-P)\xi\|^{p}

when
p<\infty
and
\|\xi\|=\max\{\|P\xi\|,\|(1-P)\xi\|\}
when
p=\infty
.
The
L^{2}
-projections on a Hilbert space are simply the orthogonal projections.
If
p\neq 2
, then all
L^{p}
-projections on
E
commute and they form a Boolean algebra
\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)\subseteq B(E)
.
The Banach algebra
C_{p}(E):=\clsp\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
generated by
\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
is called the
p
-Cunningham algebra
of
E
, and we have
C_{p}(E)\cong C_{0}(X)
where
X
is a totally disconnected space (the Stone dual to
\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
), see
[
9
]
for details.
We extend the notion of
L^{p}
-projection to partial isometries as follows:

Definition 2.22
.

An
L^{p}
-partial isometry
on a Banach space
E
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, is a contraction
T\in B(E)
that has a contractive generalized inverse
T^{*}\in B(E)
such that
TT^{*}
and
T^{*}T
are
L^{p}
-projections.
We denote by
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
the set of all
L^{p}
-partial isometries on
E
.

Proposition 2.23
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]\setminus\{2\}
the set
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
is an inverse semigroup whose idempotents are
\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
.
Passing to duals yields an injective antimultiplicative map
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)\ni P\mapsto P^{\prime}\in L^{q}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E^{\prime})
where
1/p+1/q=1
.

Proof.

Let
S,T\in L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
and choose the corresponding generalized inverses
S^{*},T^{*}\in L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
, so that the corresponding idempotents
TT^{*}
,
T^{*}T
,
SS^{*}
,
SS^{*}
are in
\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
.
By commutativity of these idempotents,
S^{*}T^{*}
is a generalized inverse of
TS
. Assume
p<\infty
.
Using the
L^{p}
-projection property of
SS^{*}
,
T^{*}T
,
TT^{*}
and that
T^{*}
is an isometry on the range of
T
(and vice versa), for any
x\in E
, we get

\begin{split}\|TSS^{*}T^{*}x\|^{p}&=\|T^{*}TSS^{*}T^{*}x\|^{p}=\|SS^{*}(T^{*}T)T^{*}x\|^{p}=\|SS^{*}T^{*}x\|^{p}\\
&=\|T^{*}x\|^{p}-\|(1-SS^{*})T^{*}x\|^{p}=\|TT^{*}x\|^{p}-\|T^{*}x-SS^{*}(T^{*}T)T^{*}x\|^{p}\\
&=\|x\|^{p}-\|(1-TT^{*})x\|^{p}-\|TT^{*}x-TSS^{*}T^{*}x\|^{p}\\
&=\|x\|^{p}-\|(1-TSS^{*}T^{*})x\|^{p}.\end{split}

Hence
TSS^{*}T^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
.
By symmetry we also get
S^{*}T^{*}TS\in\mathbb{P}_{p}(E)
.
Hence
TS\in L^{p}\textup{-PIso}(E)
, and so
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
is a semigroup.
Assume in addition that
T
is the generalized inverse of
S
.
Using that
S^{*}S
is an
L^{p}
-projection and
STS=S
we get

\|TSx\|^{p}=\|S^{*}STSx\|^{p}+\|(1-S^{*}S)TSx\|^{p}=\|TSx\|^{p}+\|TSx-S^{*}Sx\|^{p}.

Hence
\|TSx-S^{*}Sx\|^{p}=0
and so
TS=S^{*}S
.
Similarly one shows that
ST=SS^{*}
.
This implies that
T=S^{*}
, (cf.
[
50
, Propositon 2.4]
), and proves the uniqueness of the generalized inverse in
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
. Thus
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
is an inverse semigroup. For
p=\infty
one readily adapts the above proof to see that
L^{\infty}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
is a semigroup.
As we noted above every element in
L^{\infty}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
has a generalized inverse, and it is unique as we claim that we may embed
L^{\infty}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)
into the inverse semigroup opposite to

L^{1}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E^{\prime})
. Indeed, by
[
9
, Lemma 1.4]
an idempotent
P\in B(E)
is
L^{p}
-projections if and only if

P^{\prime}\in B(E^{\prime})
is an
L^{q}
-projection, where
1/p+1/q=1
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
. This implies that
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E)\ni P\mapsto P^{\prime}\in L^{q}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(E^{\prime})
is a well-defined injective anithomorphism of semigroups, where
1/p+1/q=1
,
p,q\in[1,\infty]\setminus 2
.
∎

If
E=L^{p}(\mu)
for a localizable measure
\mu
and
p\in[1,\infty]\setminus\{2\}

P\in B(L^{p}(\mu))
is an
L^{p}

is an operator of multiplication by a characteristic function, see
[

In particular, we have an isomorphism of Boolean algebras
\mathbb{P}_{p}(L^{p}(\mu))\cong[\Sigma_{\mu}]
.
We now discuss the relationship between
L^{p}\mathchar 45\relax\PIso(L^{p}(\mu))
and spatial partial isometries, originally introduced (for
\sigma

[
59
]
,
[

Definition 2.24
.

A
subspace
of a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma_{\mu},\mu)
is
(D,\Sigma_{D},\mu|_{D})
where
D\in\Sigma
,
\Sigma_{D}:=\{C\cap D:C\in\Sigma\}
and
\mu|_{D}:=\mu|_{\Sigma_{D}}
.
A
partial set automorphism
of
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is a set isomorphism between two subspaces of
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
.
So a partial set automorphism is a map
\Phi:\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}\to\Sigma_{D_{\Phi}}
(
D_{\Phi},D_{\Phi^{*}}\in\Sigma
) that descends to a Boolean isomorphism
[\Phi]:[\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}]\to[\Sigma_{D_{\Phi}}]
.
We define
\PAut([\Sigma_{\mu}]):=\{[\Phi]:\text{$\Phi$ is a partial set automorphism}\}
for the set of partial automorphisms of the Boolean algebra
[\Sigma_{\mu}]
.

By definition
\PAut([\Sigma_{\mu}])
is the set of isomorphisms between ideals in the Boolean algebra
[\Sigma_{\mu}]
. It forms an inverse semigroup that can be identified with an inverse semigroup of operators on
L_{0}(\mu)
.
Indeed, a partial automorphism
\Phi
defines the composition operator isomorphism
T_{\Phi}:L_{0}(\mu|_{D_{\Phi^{*}}})\to L_{0}(\mu|_{D_{\Phi}})
whose inverse is given by
T_{\Phi^{*}}
.
Identifying
L_{0}(\mu|_{D_{\Phi^{*}}})
and
L_{0}(\mu|_{D_{\Phi}})
with subspaces of
L_{0}(\mu)
we see that
\Phi
determines uniquely a linear operator
T_{\Phi}:L_{0}(\mu)\to L_{0}(\mu)
such that
T_{\Phi}
preserves (monotone) limits and

T_{\Phi}1_{C}=1_{\Phi(C\cap D_{\Phi^{*}})},\qquad C\in\Sigma.

We still call
T_{\Phi}:L_{0}(\mu)\to L_{0}(\mu)
a (generalized)
composition operator
.
For any two partial automorphisms
\Phi:\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}\to\Sigma_{D_{\Phi}}
and

\Psi:\Sigma_{D_{\Psi^{*}}}\to\Sigma_{D_{\Psi}}
we have
T_{\Phi}\circ T_{\Psi}=T_{\Phi\circ\Psi}
,
where
\Phi\circ\Psi:\Sigma_{\Psi^{*}(D_{\Phi^{*}}\cap D_{\Psi})}\to\Sigma_{\Phi(D_{\Phi^{*}}\cap D_{\Psi})}
is a well defined partial automorphism of
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
.
Also
T_{\Phi^{*}}
is the unique generalized inverse for
T_{\Phi}
among the composition operators.
In particular, composition operators on
L_{0}(\mu)
form an inverse semigroup naturally isomorphic to
\PAut([\Sigma_{\mu}])
.
By the very definition of the partial automorphism
\Phi:\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}\to\Sigma_{D_{\Phi}}
, the map
\mu\circ\Phi:\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}\to[0,\infty]
is a measure on
D_{\Phi^{*}}
equivalent to
\mu|_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}
.
Thus we may consider the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{d\mu\circ\Phi}{d\mu|_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}}
as a function on
\Omega
which is zero outside
D_{\Phi^{*}}
.
Similar remarks apply to the inverse partial automorphism
\Phi^{*}:\Sigma_{D_{\Phi}}\to\Sigma_{D_{\Phi^{*}}}
.

3.
Twisted inverse semigroup actions and their crossed products

We introduce Banach algebra crossed products by twisted inverse semigroup actions, that generalize
C^{*}
-algebraic constructions introduced by Buss and Exel in
[
17
]
.

3.1.
The crossed product

By an
ideal
in a Banach algebra we always mean a closed two-sided ideal.
A
partial automorphism
on a Banach algebra
A
is an isometric isomorphism
\alpha:I\to J
between two ideals
I,J
of
A
.
The partial automorphisms on
A
form an inverse subsemigroup of
\PBij(A)
that we denote by
\PAut(A)
.

Definition 3.1
.

An
action of a unital inverse semigroup
S
on a Banach algebra
A
is a unital semigroup homomorphism
\alpha:S\to\PAut(A)
.
Thus an action of
S
on
A
consists of (closed, two-sided) ideals
I_{t}
of
A
and isometric isomorphisms
\alpha_{t}:I_{t^{*}}\to I_{t}
for all
t\in S
, such that
\alpha_{1}=\mathrm{id}_{A}
and
\alpha_{s}\circ\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{st}
for all
s,t\in S
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
A
is a
C^{*}
-algebra then all ideals
I_{t}
are closed under involution, and all isometric isomorphisms
\alpha_{t}:I_{t^{*}}\to I_{t}
are necessarily
*
-preserving.
Hence Definition
3.1
is consistent with the well established
C^{*}
-algebraic version
[
67
]
.

Example 3.3
.

If
A=C_{0}(X)
for a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
then
\PAut(A)\cong\PHomeo(X)
.
Indeed, every ideal
I
in
A
is of the form
I=C_{0}(U)
for an open set
U\subseteq X
(we treat elements in
C_{0}(U)
as functions in
C_{0}(X)
that vanish outside
U
), and any partial automorphism
\alpha:I\to J
is given by the composition
\alpha(a)=a\circ h^{-1}
with a partial homeomorphism
h:U\to V
, where
I=C_{0}(U)
and
J=C_{0}(V)
.
Thus inverse semigroup actions on the Banach algebra
C_{0}(X)
are equivalent to inverse semigroup actions on the topological space
X
.

Twisted inverse semigroup actions on
C^{*}
-algebras were first introduced by Sieben
[
68
]
, but a more general definition given by Buss and Exel in
[
17
]
is needed for a number of problems.
To generalize this to Banach algebras it seems reasonable to assume that all ideals involved in an action have
(contractive, two-sided) approximate units
.
This in particular guarantees that the corresponding multiplier algebras have good properties and various approaches to multipliers coincide.
We refer to
[
28
]
, see also
[
29
]
and references therein, for more details.
Following
[
29
, Section 2]
, for a Banach algebra
A
we define the
multiplier algebra
as

\mathcal{M}(A):=\big\{(L,R):\text{$L,R:A\to A$ satisfy $aL(b)=R(a)b$ for all $a,b\in A$}\big\}.

Then
\mathcal{M}(A)
is automatically a unital closed subalgebra of the
\ell^{\infty}
-direct sum
B(A)\oplus B(A)^{\op}
, see [24, Proposition 2.5].
In particular, the maps in the pair
(L,R)\in\mathcal{M}(A)
are automatically bounded linear operators, and
\mathcal{M}(A)
is a Banach algebra with the norm
\|(L,R)\|=\max\{\|L\|,\|R\|\}
.
Also
\mathcal{M}(A)
is complete in the strict topology, which is defined by the seminorms
(L,R)\mapsto\|L(a)\|+\|R(a)\|
,
a\in A
.
Every
a\in A
yields a multiplier
(L_{a},R_{a})
where
L_{a}(b):=ab
,
R_{a}(b):=ba
, and if
A
has a contractive approximate unit the resulting map is isometric and allows us to treat
A
as an ideal of
\mathcal{M}(A)
.
Then any isometric automorphism
\alpha:A\to A
extends uniquely to an isometric automorphism
\overline{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathcal{M}(A)
.
We denote by

\mathcal{U}M(A):=\{u\in\mathcal{M}(A):\text{$u$ is invertible and $\|u\|=\|u^{-1}\|=1$}\},

the group of invertible isometries in
\mathcal{M}(A)
.

Definition 3.4

(cf.
[
17
, Definition 4.1]
)
.

A
twisted action
of an inverse semigroup
S
on a Banach algebra
A
is a pair
(\alpha,u)
, where
\alpha=\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in S}
is a family of partial automorphisms
\alpha_{t}:I_{t^{*}}\to I_{t}
of
A
, such that each ideal
I_{t}
contains an approximate unit and their union is linearly dense in
A
,
and
u=\{u(s,t)\}_{s,t\in S}
is a family of unitary multipliers
u(s,t)\in\mathcal{U}M(I_{st})
,
t,s\in S
,
such that the following holds for all
r,s,t\in S
and
e,f\in E(S)
:

(A1)

\alpha_{s}\circ\alpha_{t}=\textup{Ad}_{u(s,t)}\alpha_{st}
;

(A2)

\alpha_{r}\big(au(s,t)\big)u(r,st)=\alpha_{r}(a)u(r,s)u(rs,t)
for
a\in I_{r^{*}}\cap I_{st}
;

(A3)

u(e,f)=1_{ef}
and
u(t,t^{*}t)=u(tt^{*},t)=1_{t}
, where
1_{t}
is the unit of
\mathcal{M}(I_{t})
;

(A4)

u(t^{*},e)u(t^{*}e,t)a=u(t^{*},t)a
for all

.

Remark 3.5
.

All properties in
[
17
, Lemma 4.6]
hold for the maps and ideals involved in a twisted action on a Banach algebra as defined above.
In particular, for all
s,t\in S
and
e\in E(S)
we have

I_{t}=I_{tt^{*}},\quad\overline{\alpha}_{t}\big(u(t^{*},t)\big)=u(t,t^{*}),\quad\alpha_{e}=\mathrm{id}|_{I_{e}},\quad\alpha_{s}(I_{s^{*}}\cap I_{t})=I_{st},

and
I_{s}\subseteq I_{t}
whenever
s\leq t
.
We will use these relations, often without warning.

The authors of
[
67
]
,
[
68
]
,
[
17
]
consider covariant representations on Hilbert spaces.
We propose a more general definition of representations in Banach algebras.
Our definition works best when the target algebra has a predual, which we explain in detail in Subsection
3.2
below.
Therefore in general we will use the
double dual

B^{\prime\prime}
of a Banach algebra
B
(which has a predual
B^{\prime}
by construction).
Recall that
B^{\prime\prime}
is again naturally a Banach algebra with either of the Arens products, in which
B
sits as a
B^{\prime}
-weakly dense Banach algebra.
We will consider
B^{\prime\prime}
equipped with the
second Arens product
, which for
a,b\in B^{\prime\prime}
is determined by the formula
a\cdot b:=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{\beta}B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}b_{\beta}

where
\{a_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
,
\{b_{\beta}\}_{\beta}\subseteq B
are nets that are convergent to
a
and
b
respectively, in the weak
∗
topology on
B^{\prime\prime}
induced by
B^{\prime}
.
The first Arens product is given by
a\square b:=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{\alpha}B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{\beta}a_{\alpha}b_{\beta}
, and
B
is called
Arens regular
if the two products coincide,
see
[
28
]
for more details.
Our decision to use the second Arens product is arbitrary, and in any case we will be primarily interested in products
av\in B
where
a\in B
and
v\in B^{\prime\prime}
,
in which case the first and second Arens products always agree.
Also recall that every
C^{*}
-algebra (complex or real) is Arens regular.

For any representation
\pi:A\to B
the double adjoint
\pi^{\prime\prime}:A^{\prime\prime}\to B^{\prime\prime}
is a
B^{\prime}
-weakly continuous representation that extends
\pi
, and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
is isometric whenever
\pi
is.
In particular, for every Banach subalgebra

we may identify
A^{\prime\prime}
with a subalgebra of
B^{\prime\prime}
.
Also any contractive approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}\}
in
A
converges
A^{\prime}
-weakly to a left identity

in
A^{\prime\prime}
(and a right identity for the first Arens product in
A^{\prime\prime}
) and the map
(L,R)\mapsto L^{\prime\prime}(1_{A})
is an isometric embedding allowing us to assume that
\mathcal{M}(A)\subseteq\{b\in A^{\prime\prime}:\text{$ba,ab\in A$ for all $a\in A$}\}\subseteq A^{\prime\prime}
, see
[
28
, Proposition 2.9.16 and Theorem 2.9.49]
.
In particular, for each ideal

in
A
, which has an approximate unit, we will adopt the identifications
I\subseteq\mathcal{M}(I)\subseteq I^{\prime\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime\prime}
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
(\alpha,u)
be a twisted action of
S
on
A
.
A
covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
in a Banach algebra
B
is a pair
(\pi,v)
, where
\pi:A\to B
is a representation and
v:S\to(B^{\prime\prime})_{1}
is a map into contractive elements in
B^{\prime\prime}
, such that:

(CR1)

v_{t}\pi(a)=\pi(\alpha_{t}(a))v_{t}\in B
for all
a\in I_{t^{*}}
;

(CR2)

\pi(a)v_{s}v_{t}=\pi(au(s,t))v_{st}
for all
a\in I_{st}
,
s,t\in S
;

(CR3)

\pi(a)v_{e}=\pi(a)
for all
a\in I_{e}
,

.

We call
B(\pi,v):=\clsp\{\pi(a_{t})v_{t}:a_{t}\in I_{t},\,t\in S\}
the

of
(\pi,v)
.
We say that
(\pi,v)
is injective, isometric, etc. if
\pi
has that property.
We also say that
(\pi,v)
is

-normalized
if

(CR4)

v_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}(\pi(\mu_{i}^{t})v_{t})
, for all

, where
\{\mu^{t}_{i}\}_{i}
is an approximate unit in

.

Remark 3.7
.

Condition

\pi(a)v_{t}=v_{t}\pi(\alpha_{t}^{-1}(a))\in B
for all
a\in I_{t}
,

.

can be viewed as a “partial mutliplier” of

, and

.

and

imply that
v_{e}\pi(a)=\pi(a)
for all

,

.
Employing also

we get

(3.3)

v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t^{*}}=\pi\big(\alpha_{t}(a)u(t,t^{*})\big)

for all

,

.
Also,
(CR3)
and
(CR4)
imply that
v_{e}=\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{e})
for every

.

Notice that the elements

of Definition

are not required to be partial isometries. The definition of the generalised inverse

of

in the proof below involves both

below look different.

Proposition 3.8
.

A pair
(\pi,v)
is a

-normalized covariant representation of

if and only if

is a representation and

,

, admits a contractive generalised inverse

,

for all

,

;

(2)

v_{s}v_{t}=\pi^{\prime\prime}(u(s,t))v_{st}

(3)

v_{t}v_{t}^{*}=\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t})

Moreover, for any covariant representation

of
(\alpha,u)
in

-normalized covariant representation

such that

and

, namely
\tilde{v}_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}(\pi(\mu_{i}^{t})v_{t})=v_{t}\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t^{*}})
,

.

Assume
(\pi,v)
is a pair with the properties described in the assertion.
For every

and

, using
(3)
and
(1)
we have

which is

Condition
(2)
immediately gives

.
By

we have
v_{t}=v_{t}v_{t}^{*}v_{t}=\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t})v_{t}=v_{t}\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t^{*}})
.
This implies

and that for

we get

Therefore

,
and using this one gets

.
Hence
(\pi,v)
is a
B^{\prime}
-normalized covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
.

For the converse let
(\pi,v)
be any
B^{\prime}
-normalized covariant representation.
For each

choose an approximate unit
\{\mu^{t}_{i}\}_{i}
in
I_{t}=I_{tt^{*}}
.
Using
(CR1)
and
B^{\prime}
-continuity of multiplication in
B^{\prime\prime}
in the second variable we get

B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\big(\pi(\mu^{t}_{i})v_{t}\big)=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\big(v_{t}\pi(\alpha_{t}^{-1}(\mu^{t}_{i}))\big)=v_{t}B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\pi\big(\alpha_{t}^{-1}(\mu^{t}_{i})\big)=v_{t}\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t^{*}}),

because

is an approximate unit in
I_{t^{*}}
which is
B^{\prime}
-convergent to

.
Thus
v_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}(\pi(\mu^{t}_{i})v_{t})=v_{t}\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{t^{*}})
.
Also
(CR3)
and
(CR4)
imply that
v_{e}=\pi^{\prime\prime}(1_{e})
for all
e\in E(S)
.
We put
v_{t}^{*}:=v_{t^{*}}\pi^{\prime\prime}(u(t,t^{*})^{-1})
. Using
(CR1)
,
(A1)
and
(CR2)
, and being careful
to use only
B^{\prime}
-continuity of multiplication in
B^{\prime\prime}
in the second variable, we get

\begin{split}v_{t}v_{t}^{*}&=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}v_{t}v_{t^{*}}\pi\big(\mu^{t}_{i}u(t,t^{*})^{-1}\big)=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\pi\big(\alpha_{t}\circ\alpha_{t^{*}}\big(\mu^{t}_{i}u(t,t^{*})^{-1}\big)\big)v_{t}v_{t^{*}}\\

&=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\pi\big(u(t,t^{*})\mu^{t}_{i}u(t,t^{*})^{-1}u(t,t^{*})^{-1}u(t,t^{*})\big)v_{tt^{*}}\\

\begin{split}v_{t}^{*}v_{t}&=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}v_{t^{*}}\pi\big(u(t,t^{*})^{-1}\mu^{t}_{i}\big)v_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\pi\big(u(t^{*},t)^{-1}\alpha_{t^{*}}(\mu^{t}_{i})\big)v_{t^{*}}v_{t}\\

This finishes the proof of

and

are mutual generalized inverses.
For

\begin{split}v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t}^{*}&=v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t^{*}}\pi^{\prime\prime}\big(u(t,t^{*})^{-1}\big)=\pi\big(\alpha_{t}(a)\big)v_{t}v_{t^{*}}\pi^{\prime\prime}\big(u(t,t^{*})^{-1}\big)\\

(1)

\alpha_{s}^{\prime\prime}(1_{s^{*}}1_{t})=1_{st}

and therefore

which proves

.

satisfies all properties in the assertion.

Now let
(\pi,v)
be any covariant representation of

in

.

is a Banach
*
-algebra with involution
given by
f^{*}(t):=\alpha_{t}^{-1}(f(t^{*})^{*})u(t^{*},t)^{-1}
,
t\in S
.
Lemma
3.10
(see also
[
68
, Proposition 3.7]
) implies that every covariant representation
(\pi,v)
of
\alpha
in a Banach algebra
B
‘integrates’ to a representation
\pi\times v:\ell^{1}(\alpha,u)\to B
given by

\pi\times v(f):=\sum_{t\in S}\pi\big(f(t)\big)v_{t},\qquad f\in\ell^{1}(\alpha,u),

and
\overline{\pi\times v(\ell^{1}(\alpha,u))}=B(\pi,v)
is a Banach algebra.

Definition 3.11
.

For a fixed class
\mathcal{R}
of covariant representations of
(\alpha,u)
we define the
\mathcal{R}
-crossed product
for
(\alpha,u)
, denoted
A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u),\mathcal{R}}S
, as the Hausdorff completion of
\ell^{1}(\alpha,u)
in the seminorm

\|f\|_{\mathcal{R}}:=\sup\{\|\pi\times v(f)\|:(\pi,v)\in\mathcal{R}\}.

The
universal Banach algebra crossed product

A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u)}S
is the crossed product associated to the class of all covariant representations of
(\alpha,u)
.

Remark 3.12
.

If
S=G
is a group then we necessarily have
I_{t}=A
for all

, so
\alpha:G\to\Aut(A)
, and

is
\ell^{1}(G,A)
as a Banach space.
When

such crossed products are studied in
[
7
]
, see also
[
8
]
.
For general
A
, but when the twist

is trivial,
(A,G,\alpha)
is a Banach algebra dynamical system in the sense of
[
32
]
, and the algebras
A\rtimes_{\alpha,\mathcal{R}}G
are examples of crossed products considered in
[
32
]
.

3.2.
Covariant representations in dual Banach algebras and on Banach spaces

We fix a twisted action
(\alpha,u)
of an inverse semigroup
S
on
A
.
We will now discuss situations, beyond the one described in Remark
3.9
, when in the study of algebras generated by covariant representations we do not need to pass through the bidual algebra
B^{\prime\prime}
.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
(\pi,v)
be a covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
in a Banach algebra
B
.
If
B
has a predual Banach space
B_{*}
, we say that
(\pi,v)
is
B_{*}
-normalized
if, for every
t\in S
, we have
v_{t}=B_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}(\pi(\mu_{i}^{t})v_{t})
, for an approximate unit
\{\mu^{t}_{i}\}_{i}
in
I_{t}=I_{tt^{*}}
, where
B_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim
denotes the limit in the weak
∗
topology on
B
induced by
B_{*}
.
In particular, then
v:S\to B
takes values in
B
(rather than in
B^{\prime\prime}
).

A
dual Banach algebra
is a pair
(B,B_{*})
where
B
is a Banach algebra,
B_{*}
is a predual of
B
, and in addition multiplication in
B
is separately
B_{*}
-continuous, see
[
65
]
,
[
29
]
. Examples of dual Banach algebras include all
W^{*}
-algebras (complex or real) with their unique preduals, and all algebras
B(E)
of all bounded operators on a reflexive Banach space
E
, equipped with the canonical predual Banach space
E^{\prime}\widehat{\otimes}E
.
By
[
46
, Theorem 5.6]
, every representation
\pi:A\to B
into a dual Banach algebra
(B,B_{*})
has a unique extension to a representation
\overline{\pi}:\mathcal{M}(A)\to B
which is strictly-
B_{*}
-continuous; it is given by
\overline{\pi}(m):=B_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\pi(m\mu_{i})
for an approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i}
in
A
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Assume
(B,B_{*})
is a dual Banach algebra.
For any covariant representation
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B
there is a unique
B_{*}
-normalized covariant representation
(\pi,\tilde{v})
such that
\pi(a)v_{t}=\pi(a)\tilde{v}_{t}
for all
a\in I_{t}
and
t\in S
.
A pair
(\pi,v)
is a
B_{*}
-normalized covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
in
B
if and only if
\pi:A\to B
is a representation and
v:S\to B_{1}
is a map such that every
v_{t}
,
t\in S
, admits a contractive generalised inverse
v_{t}^{*}
, satisfying

v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t}^{*}=\pi\big(\alpha_{t}(a)\big),\quad v_{s}v_{t}=\pi_{st}\big(u(s,t)\big)v_{st},\quad\text{$v_{t}v_{t}^{*}=\pi_{t}(1_{t})$ and $v_{t}^{*}v_{t}=\pi_{t^{*}}(1_{t^{*}})$},

where
a\in I_{t^{*}}
,
s,t\in S
and
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{M}(I_{t})\to B
is the unique strictly-
B_{*}
-continuous extension of
\pi|_{I_{t}}
.

Proof.

Follow the proof of Proposition
3.8
(some arguments can be simplified as the multiplication in
B
is
B_{*}
-continuous in both variables).
∎

Remark 3.15
.

For any Banach algebra
B
the pair
(B^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime})
is a dual Banach algebra if and only if
B
is Arens regular. Thus Proposition
3.8
cannot be deduced from Proposition
3.14
.
We were not able to find a nice common generalization of these two statements.

Remark 3.16
.

Proposition
3.14
implies that a
B_{*}
-normalized representation of
an untwisted action
\alpha
(an action with a trivial twist) in a dual Banach algebra
B
is a pair
(\pi,v)
, where
\pi:A\to B
is a representation and
v:S\to B_{1}
is a semigroup homomorphism, such that
v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t^{*}}=\pi(\alpha_{t}(a))
and
v_{e}=\pi_{e}(1_{e})
for
a\in I_{t^{*}}
,
s,t\in S
,
e\in E(S)
.

We now relate the above discussion to representations on Banach spaces.
Note that if
\pi:A\to B(E)
is a representation then, for each
t\in S
,
\pi
yields a non-degenerate representation
I_{t}\to B(\overline{\pi(I_{t})E})
which thus uniquely extends to a representation

\overline{\pi}_{t}:\mathcal{M}(I_{t})\to B(\overline{\pi(I_{t})E});\ \overline{\pi}_{t}(m)\big(\pi(a)x\big):=\pi(ma)x,\qquad m\in\mathcal{M}(I_{t}),\ a\in I_{t},\ x\in E,

see for instance
[
40
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Definition 3.17
.

A
covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
on a Banach space
E
is a pair
(\pi,v)
where
\pi:A\to B(E)
is a representation and
v:S\to B(E)_{1}
has the property that every
v_{t}
,
t\in S
, admits a contractive generalised inverse
v_{t}^{*}
, satisfying:

(SCR1)

v_{t}\pi(a)v_{t}^{*}=\pi(\alpha_{t}(a))
for all
a\in I_{t^{*}}
,
t\in S
;

(SCR2)

the ranges of
v_{t}
and
v_{t}^{*}
are
\overline{\pi(I_{t})E}
and
\overline{\pi(I_{t^{*}})E}
respectively, for all
t\in S
;

(SCR3)

v_{s}v_{t}=\overline{\pi}_{st}(u(s,t))v_{st}
for all
s,t\in S
.

Equality in
(SCR3)
holds pointwise and it makes sense due to
(SCR2)
.
We say that
(\pi,v)
is non-degenerate, injective, etc. if
\pi
has that property.

Remark 3.18
.

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
,
E=H
is a Hilbert space and
A
is a
C^{*}
-algebra then Definition
3.17
is equivalent to
[
68
, Definition 3.2]
and
[
17
, Definition 6.2]
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
(\pi,v)
be a covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
on a Banach space
E
.
Then
(\pi,v)
is a covariant representation in the Banach algebra
B(E)
such that each
v_{e}\in B(E)
,
e\in E(S)
, is a strong limit of
\{\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})\}_{i}
, where
\{\mu_{i}^{e}\}_{i}
is a contractive approximate unit in
I_{e}
.

Proof.

The arguments in the beginning of the proof of Proposition
3.8
show that
(\pi,v)
is a covariant representation in the Banach algebra
B(E)
.
Then each
v_{e}\in B(E)
,
e\in E(S)
, is a projection onto
\overline{\pi(I_{e})E}
which commutes with elements of
\pi(I_{e})
, and thus
v_{e}
is a strong limit of
\{\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})\}_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 3.20
.

If
A
is a complex
C^{*}
-algebra and
(\pi,v)
is a non-degenerate covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
on a complex Banach space
E
then each
v_{t}
,
t\in S
, is an
MP
-partial isometry on
E
.

Proof.

Since
\pi
is non-degenerate we may extend it to the unitization of
A
and so we may assume that
A
and
\pi
are in fact unital.
Since
A
is a
C^{*}
-algebra, for each
e\in E(S)
we may choose an approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}^{e}\}_{i}
in
I_{e}
consisting of hermitian operators.
Then
\{\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})\}_{i}
are hermitian operators by
[
23
, Lemma 2.4]
.
As
v_{e}
is a strong limit of
\{\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})\}_{i}
, this implies that
v_{e}
is also hermitian.
Indeed, recall that
a\in B(E)
is hermitian if and only if its numerical range
V(a)=\{f(ax):x\in E,\ f\in E^{\prime},\ \|f\|=\|x\|=1=f(x)\}
is real.
Hence for any
x\in E,\ f\in E^{\prime}
with
\|f\|=\|x\|=1=f(x)
we get
f(v_{e}x)=f(\lim_{i}\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})x)\in\mathbb{R}
, and so
v_{e}
is hermitian.
As
v_{t}v_{t}^{*}=v_{tt^{*}}
and
v_{t}^{*}v_{t}=v_{t^{*}t}
, where
tt^{*},t^{*}t\in E(S)
, for all
t\in S
, we conclude that
\{v_{t}\}_{t\in S}\subseteq B(E)_{1}
are
MP
-partial isometries.
∎

Lemma 3.21
.

Assume that
E
is a reflexive Banach space, and recall that the projective tensor product Banach space
B_{*}:=E^{\prime}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}E
is naturally a predual of
B:=B(E)
making it a dual Banach algebra.
A covariant representation
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B(E)
is
B_{*}
-normalized if and only if
(\pi,v)
is a covariant representation on the Banach space
E
.

Proof.

We identify
f\otimes\xi\in E^{\prime}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}E=B_{*}
with the functional map
B\ni b\mapsto f\otimes\xi(b):=f(b\xi)\in\mathbb{F}
.
Assume
(\pi,v)
is
B_{*}
-normalized, and so
\{v_{t}\}_{t\in S}\subseteq B(E)
are partial isometries with the associated generalized inverses
\{v_{t}^{*}\}_{t\in S}\subseteq B(E)
, satisfying the relations described in Proposition
3.14
, which include
(SCR1)
.
For
e\in E(S)
we have
v_{e}=\pi_{e}(1_{e})=B_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})
, where
\{\mu_{i}^{e}\}_{i}
is an approximate unit in
I_{e}
.
Hence
f(v_{e}\xi)=\lim_{i}f(\pi(\mu_{i}^{e})\xi)
for all
(f,\xi)\in E^{\prime}\times E
, which implies that for any
\xi\in E
we have
v_{e}\xi\in\overline{\pi(I_{e})E}^{E^{\prime}}
.
As the weak and norm closures of convex sets coincide this means that the range of
v_{e}
is
\overline{\pi(I_{e})E}
.
Thus, for each
t\in S
, we have
v_{tt^{*}}=\pi_{t}(1_{t})
is a projection onto the space
\overline{\pi(I_{t})E}=\overline{\pi(I_{tt^{*}})E}
.
This implies
(SCR2)
because
v_{t}=\pi_{t}(1_{t})v_{t}
and
v_{t}^{*}=\pi_{t}(1_{t^{*}})v_{t}^{*}
.
We also have
\pi_{t}(m)=\overline{\pi}_{t}(m)\pi_{t}(1_{t})
for
m\in\mathcal{M}(I_{t})
, which immediately gives
(SCR3)
from
v_{s}v_{t}=\pi_{st}(u(s,t))v_{st}
.

Conversely, assume
(\pi,v)
is a covariant representation in the sense of Definition
3.17
.
By Lemma
3.19
each
v_{tt^{*}}
is a strong limit of
\{\pi(\mu_{i}^{t})\}_{i}
, where
\{\mu_{i}^{t}\}_{i}
is an approximate unit in
I_{t}=I_{tt^{*}}
.
Using this, one sees that
v_{tt^{*}}=\pi_{t}(1_{t})
and
\pi_{t}(m)=\overline{\pi}_{t}(m)\pi_{t}(1_{t})
for
m\in\mathcal{M}(I_{t})
.
Now one readily gets the relations in Proposition
3.14
.
∎

Corollary 3.22
.

For any covariant representation
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B(E)
, where
E
is a reflexive Banach space, there is a unique covariant representation
(\pi,\tilde{v})
on
E
such that
\pi(a)v_{t}=\pi(a)\tilde{v}_{t}
for all
a\in I_{t}
and
t\in S
(in fact
(\pi,\tilde{v})
is
E^{\prime}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}E
-normalized).

Proof.

Combine Lemma
3.21
and Proposition
3.14
.
∎

Corollary 3.23
.

Let
(\alpha,u)
be a twisted inverse semigroup action on a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{C^{*}}
be the class of all covariant representations of
(\alpha,u)
in some
C^{*}
-algebra, and let
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{Hil}}
be the class of all covariant representations of
(\alpha,u)
on some Hilbert space.
Then

A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u),\mathcal{R}_{C^{*}}}S=A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u),\mathcal{R}_{\textup{Hil}}}S,

and this coincides with the crossed product
A\rtimes_{(\alpha,u)}S
introduced in
[
17
]
,
[
68
]
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{Hil}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{C^{*}}
we get
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}_{\textup{Hil}}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}_{C^{*}}}
.
This is equality, because for any covariant representation
(\pi,v)\in\mathcal{R}_{C^{*}}
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
, we may assume that the enveloping
W^{*}
-algebra
B^{\prime\prime}
is faithfully represented on a Hilbert space
H
.
Then, by Corollary
3.22
, the renormalized covariant representation
(\pi,\tilde{v})
is a covariant representation of
\alpha
on

, so
(\pi,\tilde{v})\in\mathcal{R}_{\textup{Hil}}
.
The claim follows because Corollary
3.22
implies
\pi\times v(f)=\pi\times\tilde{v}(f)
for all

.
∎

4.
Groupoid Banach algebras and inverse semigroup disintegration

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an étale groupoid with locally compact Hausdorff unit space
X
.
By a twist over
\mathcal{G}
we mean a Fell line bundle
[
49
]
.
This is equivalent to twists introduced by Kumjian in
[
48
]
, see
[
49
, 2.5.iv]
.
More specifically, let
\mathcal{L}
be a line bundle over
\mathcal{G}
.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{L}=\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}L_{\gamma}
is a topological space such that the canonical projection
\mathcal{L}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{G}
is continuous and open, each fiber
L_{\gamma}\cong\mathbb{F}
is a one dimensional Banach space with a structure consistent with the topology of
\mathcal{L}
, in the sense that the maps

\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}L_{\gamma}\times L_{\gamma}\ni(z,w)\mapsto z+w\in\mathcal{L},\quad\mathbb{F}\times\mathcal{L}\ni(\lambda,z)\mapsto\lambda z\in\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{L}\ni z\mapsto|z|\in\mathbb{R}

are continuous.
Then
\mathcal{L}
is necessarily locally trivial.
For any open
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
we write
C(U,\mathcal{L})
for the linear space of continuous sections in the restricted bundle

\mathcal{L}|_{U}:=\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in U}L_{\gamma}
(i.e. continuous maps
f:U\to\mathcal{L}
with
f(\gamma)\in L_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in U
).
Similarly we denote by
C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
C_{0}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
C_{b}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
C_{u}(U,\mathcal{L})
the spaces of continuous sections with compact support, vanishing at infinity, bounded and unitary, respectively.
Then
C_{0}(U,\mathcal{L})
is a Banach space with the norm
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\max_{\gamma\in U}|f(\gamma)|
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is locally trivial, every point in
\mathcal{G}
has an open neighbourhood
U
and unitary section
f\in C(U,\mathcal{L})
, i.e.
|f(\gamma)|=1
, for all
\gamma\in U
.
This section induces an isometric isomorphism
C_{0}(U,\mathcal{L})\cong C_{0}(U)
, as for every
g\in C_{0}(U,\mathcal{L})
there is a unique element
g/f\in C_{0}(U)
such that
g(\gamma)=(g/f)(\gamma)f(\gamma)
,
\gamma\in U
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
twist over the groupoid

\mathcal{G}
is a line bundle
\mathcal{L}=\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}L_{\gamma}
over
\mathcal{G}
together with a continuous
multiplication

{\cdot}:\bigsqcup_{d(\gamma)=r(\eta)\in\mathcal{G}}L_{\gamma}\times L_{\eta}\to\mathcal{L}
, which is associative (whenever the product makes sense), makes the modulus multiplicative, and induces bilinear maps
L_{\gamma}\times L_{\eta}\longrightarrow L_{\gamma\eta}
;
and a continuous
involution

{}^{*}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
that restricts to conjugate linear maps
L_{\gamma}\to L_{\gamma^{-1}}
, preserves the modulus (norm), is anti-multiplicative and
z\cdot z^{*}=|z|^{2}\in L_{r(\gamma)}\cong\mathbb{F}
for every
z\in L_{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.
See
[
49
]
for more details.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be a twisted groupoid.
The set
S(\mathcal{L})
of bisections
U
for which the restricted line bundle
\mathcal{L}|_{U}
is trivial forms a unital wide inverse subsemigroup
S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

The restricted bundle
\mathcal{L}|_{U}
is trivial if and only if there is a continuous unitary section
c_{U}\in C_{u}(U,\mathcal{L})
.
If
\mathcal{L}|_{U}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{V}
are trivial, and
c_{U}\in C_{u}(U,\mathcal{L}),\ c_{V}\in C_{u}(V,\mathcal{L})
, then
\mathcal{L}|_{UV}
and
\mathcal{L}|_{U^{-1}}
are trivial because the convolution product
c_{U}*c_{V}\in C_{u}(UV,\mathcal{L})
and
c_{U}^{*}\in C_{u}(U^{-1},\mathcal{L})
(in a Fell line bundle the norm
|\cdot|
is multiplicative, rather than just submultiplicative).
Thus
S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
is an inverse semigroup.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is locally trivial,
S(\mathcal{L})
is wide.
Moreover,
\mathcal{L}
is trivial over
X
as each fiber
L_{x}\cong\mathbb{F}
is a one-dimensional algebra with unit
1_{x}
, and the unit section defined by
1(x):=1_{x}
is unitary and continuous.
∎

According to Lemma
4.2
we may and we shall always assume that

\mathcal{L}|_{X}=X\times\mathbb{F}
is trivial
.

Example 4.3

(Twist by a cocycle)
.

The trivial bundle
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{G}\times\mathbb{F}
with pointwise operations is a
trivial twist
.
More generally, if
\sigma
is a
continuous normalised
2
-cocycle
on
\mathcal{G}
, that is a continuous map
\sigma:\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\to\{z\in\mathbb{F}:|z|=1\}
satisfying

\sigma\big(r(\gamma),\gamma\big)=1=\sigma\big(\gamma,d(\gamma)\big),\qquad\sigma(\alpha,\beta)\sigma(\alpha\beta,\gamma)=\sigma(\beta,\gamma)\sigma(\alpha,\beta\gamma)

for every composable triple
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
,
then we may consider a twist
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
given by the trivial bundle
\mathcal{G}\times\mathbb{F}
with operations given by

(\alpha,w)\cdot(\beta,z):=\big(\alpha\beta,\sigma(\alpha,\beta)wz\big),\qquad(\alpha,w)^{*}:=\big(\alpha^{-1},\overline{\sigma(\alpha^{-1},\alpha)w}\big).

Every twist based on a trivial line bundle is of this form.

4.1.
Algebras of quasi-continuous sections

Let us fix a twisted groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
Recall our convention that
\mathcal{G}
is locally compact and only locally Hausdorff.
Thus the associated
*
-algebra will be defined on the set of quasi-continuous compactly supported sections:

\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\spane\{f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L}):U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})\},

where we treat sections of
\mathcal{L}|_{U}
as sections of
\mathcal{L}
that vanish outside
U
.
Note that
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})=\spane\{f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L}):U\in\mathcal{C}\}
for any cover
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
, cf.
[
34
, Proposition 3.10]
.
In particular, we may always take
\mathcal{C}=S(\mathcal{L})
. If the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is Hausdorff, then
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})=C_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
is the usual space of continuous compactly supported sections.
We define a
*
-algebra structure on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
by

(4.4)

(f*g)(\gamma):=\sum_{r(\eta)=r(\gamma)}f(\eta)\cdot g(\eta^{-1}\gamma),\qquad(f^{*})(\gamma):=f(\gamma^{-1})^{*},

where
f,g\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, cf.
[
34
, Proposition 3.11]
.
Since we assume that
\mathcal{L}|_{X}
is trivial, we get that
C_{c}(X)=C_{c}(X,\mathcal{L})
is a
*
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.

The domain and range maps
d,r:\mathcal{G}\to X
induce the following three submultiplicative norms on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
:

(4.5)

\|f\|_{d_{*}}:=\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{d(\gamma)=x}|f(\gamma)|,\qquad\|f\|_{r_{*}}:=\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{r(\gamma)=x}|f(\gamma)|,

(4.6)

\|f\|_{I}:=\max\{\|f\|_{d_{*}},\|f\|_{r_{*}}\},

see
[
58
, the proof of Theorem 2.2.1]
or
[
62
, II, 1.4]
.
The norm
\|\cdot\|_{I}
is called the
I
-norm
and was introduced by Hahn
[
42
]
.
It has the advantage that it is preserved by the involution
*
, while for the remaining norms we only have
\|f^{*}\|_{d_{*}}=\|f\|_{r_{*}}
.
If
\|\cdot\|
is a
C^{*}
-norm on the
*
-algebra
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
then minimality of the
C^{*}
-norm on a
C^{*}
-algebra implies that
\|f\|=\|f\|_{\infty}
for
f\in C_{c}(X)
(as we have this for every
f\in C_{0}(U)\subseteq C_{c}(X)
where
U
is precompact open), and then the
C^{*}
-equality gives that
\|f\|=\|f\|_{\infty}
for every
f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
and
U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.
Using this and the triangle inequality one infers that there is a largest
C^{*}
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{C^{*}}
on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
We prove below (see Corollary
5.8
) that
\|\cdot\|_{C^{*}}
does not exceed
\|\cdot\|_{I}
.
This is well known when
\mathcal{G}
is Hausdorff (
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and there is no twist), see
[
69
, Lemma 3.2.3]
, cf.
[
19
, Lemma 4.3]
.
In the non-Hausdorff case this is much more delicate, see
[
26
, Corollary 6.6]
.

Definition 4.4
.

We write
F_{I}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{I}}
,
F_{d_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{d_{*}}}
,
F_{r_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{r_{*}}}
, and
C^{*}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{C^{*}}}

for the completions in the corresponding norms. When twist is trivial we omit writing it.

The important feature of the above norms is that when restricted to any subspace
C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, where
U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})
, they coincide with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
Hence each space
C_{0}(U,\mathcal{L})
embeds naturally into the completions of
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
in each of these norms.
We introduce yet another norm which is maximal with respect to this property.
In fact we will associate norms to subsemigroups of
\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
be a semigroup covering
\mathcal{G}
.
There is a submultiplicative norm on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
given by

(4.7)

\|f\|_{\max}^{S}:=\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|f_{k}\|_{\infty}:f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k},\ f_{k}\in C_{c}(U_{k},\mathcal{L}),\ U_{k}\in S\right\}.

This is the largest submultiplicative norm on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
which agrees with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on each
C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, for
U\in S
.
If
S
is an inverse semigroup, then the involution is isometric in
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
there are
f_{k}\in C_{c}(U_{k},\mathcal{L})
and
g_{l}\in C_{c}(V_{k},\mathcal{L})
, where
U_{k},V_{l}\in S
, such that
f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}
,
g=\sum_{l=1}^{m}g_{l}
and
\|f\|_{\max}^{S}+\varepsilon>\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|f_{k}\|_{\infty}
and
\|g\|_{\max}^{S}+\varepsilon>\sum_{l=1}^{m}\|g_{l}\|_{\infty}
.
Then
f*g=\sum_{k,l}f_{k}*g_{l}
, and
f_{k}*g_{l}\in C_{c}(U_{k}V_{l},\mathcal{L})
,
U_{k}V_{l}\in S
. Therefore

\|f*g\|_{\max}^{S}\leq\sum_{k,l}\|f_{k}*g_{l}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{k,l}\|f_{k}\|_{\infty}\|g_{l}\|_{\infty}<\big(\|f\|_{\max}^{S}+\varepsilon\big)\big(\|g\|_{\max}^{S}+\varepsilon\big).

This shows that
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
is submultiplicative.
The proof of the triangle inequality is even simpler.
If in addition
f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
for some
U\in S
, then there is
\gamma_{0}\in U
where
|f|
attains its maximum, and then (retaining the above choice of
f_{k}
’s)

\|f\|_{\infty}=|f(\gamma_{0})|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}|f_{k}(\gamma_{0})|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|f_{k}\|_{\infty}<\|f\|_{\max}^{S}+\varepsilon,

which shows that
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|^{S}_{\max}
.
The converse inequality
\|f\|_{\max}^{S}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
holds by (
4.7
).
Hence
\|\cdot\|^{S}_{\max}
coincides with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on
C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
for
U\in S
.
If
\|\cdot\|
is any norm on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
that agrees with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on
C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, for
U\in S
, then for any
f=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}
,
f_{k}\in C_{c}(U_{k},\mathcal{L})
,
U_{k}\in S
, we have
\|f\|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|f_{k}\|=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|f_{k}\|_{\infty}
, which implies
\|f\|\leq\|f\|_{\max}^{S}
.
If
S
is an inverse semigroup then
f^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}^{*}
,
f_{k}^{*}\in C_{c}(U_{k}^{*},\mathcal{L})
, where
U_{k}^{*}\in S
.
Thus
\|f^{*}\|_{\max}^{S}=\|f\|_{\max}^{S}
by (
4.7
).
∎

Definition 4.6
.

For any unital inverse subsemigroup
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
covering
\mathcal{G}
, we denote by
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}}
the associated Banach
*
-algebra.
We also denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\max}
the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{\Bis(\mathcal{G})}
and put
F(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=F^{\Bis(\mathcal{G})}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.

Remark 4.7
.

The assumption that
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
is a unital means that
S
contains
X
, which is equivalent to that

C_{0}(X)
isometrically embeds into
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
We have

\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\leq\|\cdot\|_{d_{*}},\,\|\cdot\|_{C^{*}},\,\|\cdot\|_{r_{*}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{I}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}

on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
. In particular,
\|\cdot\|_{\max}
is minimal
amongst all norms
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
(
S\subseteq T\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{T}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
).
In general there is no maximal amongst the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
.
In our analysis we may restrict only to wide inverse semigroups as the ‘saturation’
\widetilde{S}:=\{U:U\subseteq V\in S\}
of
S
is a wide inverse subsemigroup of
\Bis(\mathcal{G})
and

\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}=\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{\widetilde{S}}
.
Moreover
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
is majorized by a norm coming from an inverse subsemigroup of
S(\mathcal{L})
from Lemma
4.2
.
Namely,
\widetilde{S}\cap S(\mathcal{L})
is a wide inverse semigroup and

(4.8)

\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{\widetilde{S}\cap S(\mathcal{L})}.

Example 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=G\times X
be the transformation groupoid for a (discrete) group action
h:G\to\text{Homeo}(X)
. The natural

G
-grading of
\mathcal{G}
is given by

S:=\{\{g\}\times X\}_{g\in G}\cong G
.
For every

we have

\|f\|_{d_{*}}=\max_{x\in X}\sum_{g\in G}|f(g,x)|,\,\,\|f\|_{r_{*}}=\max_{x\in X}\sum_{g\in G}|f(g,\varphi_{g}(x))|\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\|f\|_{\max}^{S}=\sum_{g\in G}\max_{x\in X}|f(g,x)|.

Monod
[
57
]
denotes the algebra
F_{d_{*}}(\mathcal{G}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G})}^{\|\cdot\|_{d_{*}}}
by
C(X,\ell^{1}G)
and calls it the Banach algebra crossed product. It plays crucial role in the study of amenability in
[
57
]
.
However, it is much more common to reserve the name Banach algebra crossed product to the
*
-algebra
\ell^{1}(G,C_{0}(X))\cong F^{S}(\mathcal{G})
, see
[

]
,
[
31
]
, and references therein. Note that the Hahn norm
\|\cdot\|_{I}
is in general strictly smaller than

.
Obviously, when

is singleton, so that
\mathcal{G}=G
is a group, then

and all the norms

coincide with the

-norm in

.

We fix a unital inverse subsemigroup
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
covering
\mathcal{G}
.
By (
4.8
), in the following definition we may always assume that
S
is a wide inverse subsemigroup of
S(\mathcal{L})
.

Definition 4.9
.

For a class
\mathcal{R}
of representations of
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
we denote by
F_{\mathcal{R}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}}

\|f\|_{\mathcal{R}}:=\sup\{\|\psi(f)\|:\psi\in\mathcal{R}\}.

If
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}
, then we will write
F_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
for

.

Remark 4.10
.

If
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\max}
, then the algebra
F_{\mathcal{R}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
does not depend on the choice of
S
, as it
can be viewed as a Hausdorff completion of
F(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
In this paper we will be mainly interested in the case when
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{I}
(and hence all the more

).

All the aforementioned Banach algebras have approximate units.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
be a unital inverse semigroup covering
\mathcal{G}
. An approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i}
in
C_{0}(X)

is also an approximate unit in
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, and for any representation
\psi:F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
the net
\{\psi(\mu_{i})\}_{i}
is an approximate unit in
\overline{\psi(F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}))}
.
In particular, if
X
is compact then
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and
\overline{\psi(F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}))}
are unital.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\{\mu_{i}*f\}_{i}
converges to
f
in
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
for
f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
U\in S
.
But then
\mu_{i}*f-f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
and therefore
\|\mu_{i}*f-f\|_{\max}^{S}=\|\mu_{i}*f-f\|_{\infty}=\max_{\gamma\in U}|\mu_{i}(r(\gamma))f(\gamma)-f(\gamma)|
, which obviously tends to zero.
∎

We write
A\cong B
to indicate that two Banach algebras
A
and
B
are isometrically isomorphic.
We write
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{anti}}}{{\cong}}B
if there is an isometry from
A
onto
B
which is anti-linear and anti-multiplicative.
We write
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dense}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}B
if there is a representation of
A
in
B
with dense range.
By Remark
4.7
the identity on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})

extends to canonical representations

F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dense}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F_{I}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dense}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F_{d_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}),F_{r_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}).

4.2.
Opposite algebras and twists

We denote by
A^{\op}
the
opposite algebra
to an algebra
A
, meaning that the linear structure of
A^{\op}
is the same as that of
A
but the multiplication is performed in the reverse order.
When
A
is a Banach algebra then
A^{\op}
is a Banach algebra with the same norm.
Fix a twisted groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and an inverse semigroup
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
covering
\mathcal{G}
.
An
opposite twist

\mathcal{L}^{\op}
on
\mathcal{G}
is defined as follows (see
[
21
]
).
The fibers are
L_{\gamma}^{\op}:=L_{\gamma^{-1}}
,
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
, and the topology is induced by the sections

(4.9)

(\widecheck{f})(\gamma):=f(\gamma^{-1}),

where
f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.
The involution on
\mathcal{L}^{\op}
is given by the involution maps
L_{\gamma^{-1}}\to L_{\gamma}
from
\mathcal{L}
,
and the
multiplication maps
L_{\gamma}^{\op}\times L_{\eta}^{\op}\longrightarrow L_{\gamma\eta}^{\op}
are given by
(z,w)\mapsto w\cdot z
where
z\in L_{\gamma}^{\op}=\mathcal{L}_{\gamma^{-1}}
,
w\in L_{\eta}^{\op}=\mathcal{L}_{\eta^{-1}}
and
d(\gamma)=r(\eta)
.
The map
f\mapsto\widecheck{f}
yields a
*
-isomorphism

\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op}).

In particular, the untwisted algebra
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G})^{\op}\cong\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G})
is self-opposite.
It follows from the formulas for the norms
\|\cdot\|_{d_{*}},\|\cdot\|_{r_{*}},\|\cdot\|_{I},\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
that the above isomorphism extends to isometric isomorphisms

F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op}),\qquad F_{I}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F_{I}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op}),\qquad F_{d_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F_{r_{*}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op}).

Thus the algebras
F^{S}(\mathcal{G})
and
F_{I}(\mathcal{G})
are self-opposite, while for the remaining two algebras we have only
F_{d_{*}}(\mathcal{G})^{\op}\cong F_{r_{*}}(\mathcal{G})
.
Let us consider a slightly more general situation.

Definition 4.12
.

For a class
\mathcal{E}
of Banach spaces we write
F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=F_{\mathcal{R}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{E}}:=\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{R}}

for
\mathcal{R}
consisting of all representations of
F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
in
B(E)
for
E\in\mathcal{E}
.

If
\mathcal{E}
is a class of Banach spaces we denote by
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
the class of dual spaces to spaces in
\mathcal{E}
.
For any other class
\mathcal{F}
of Banach spaces we write
\mathcal{E}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}
if for every
E\in\mathcal{E}
there is
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
E\cong F
.

Proposition 4.13
.

If
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}
then the map
f\mapsto\widecheck{f}
induces a contractive homomorphism
F_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dense}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
.
If
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{E}
then this homomorphism is an isometric isomorphism
F_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
.

Proof.

For any representation
\pi:F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\to B(E)
the formula
\pi^{\prime}(f):=\pi(\widecheck{f})^{\prime}
,
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
,
defines a representation
\pi^{\prime}:F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})\to B(E^{\prime})
.
If
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}
and
E\in\mathcal{E}
, then we may replace
E^{\prime}
by
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
E^{\prime}\cong F
, so that we get a representation
\pi^{\prime}:F^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})\to B(F)
.
This implies that
\|\widecheck{f}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}
for
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
.
Thus the isomorphism
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
extends to a representation
F_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{dense}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{E}
implies
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}
.
Hence
F_{\mathcal{F}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
if
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Corollary 4.14
.

If
\mathcal{E}
consists of reflexive Banach spaces, then
F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
.

Proof.

Take
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
in Proposition
4.13
.
∎

Corollary 4.15
.

We have
C^{*}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
and
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})^{\op}\cong C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
4.14
to the case where
\mathcal{E}
consists of Hilbert spaces.
∎

The untwisted version of Corollary
4.14
is known (see
[
39
]
,
[
38
]
) and Corollary
4.15
is a special case of the main result in
[
21
]
.
We can apply Proposition
4.13
beyond reflexive spaces.

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the class of
L^{1}
-spaces,
\mathcal{F}_{1}
the class of
L^{\infty}
-spaces,
\mathcal{F}_{2}
the class of
C_{0}
-spaces and
\mathcal{F}_{3}
the class of Lindenstrauss spaces, cf. Remark

.
Then

F_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\cong F_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\cong F_{\mathcal{F}_{3}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\cong F_{\mathcal{E}}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})^{\op}.

Proof.

We clearly have
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{F}_{2}
and
\mathcal{F}_{3}^{\prime}\subseteq_{\iso}\mathcal{E}
.
We have
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{3}
as

-spaces are abstract

-spaces and so their duals are (abstract)

-spaces, see for instance
[

.
Thus

and the assertion follows from the second part of Proposition

.
∎

4.3.
From twisted groupoids to twisted actions and back

We describe the correspondence between twisted groupoids and twisted actions of inverse semigroups on
C_{0}(X)
, which implicitly can be found in
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
.
Let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be a twisted groupoid.
Take any wide inverse subsemigroup
S\subseteq S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
and let
h:S\to\PHomeo(X)
be the canonical action.
Then we have the inverse semigroup action
\alpha:S\to\PAut(C_{0}(X))
given by
\alpha_{U}(a):=a\circ h_{U^{*}}
for
a\in I_{d(U)}:=C_{0}(d(U))
,
U\in S
.
For each
U\in S(\mathcal{L})
take any continuous unitary section
c_{U}\in C_{u}(U,\mathcal{L})
, but if
U\subseteq X
let
c_{U}(x):=(x,1)\subseteq X\times\mathbb{F}
,
x\in U
.
For any
U,V\in S(\mathcal{L})
there is a unique unitary function
u(U,V)\in C_{u}(r(UV))
satisfying
u(U,V)\big(r(\gamma)\big)c_{UV}(\gamma\eta)=c_{U}(\gamma)\cdot c_{V}(\eta)
,
\gamma\in U,\ \eta\in V,\ d(\gamma)=r(\eta)
.
In fact we have

(4.10)

\alpha_{U}(a)=c_{U}*a*c_{U}^{*},\qquad u(U,V)=c_{U}*c_{V}*c_{UV}^{*},\qquad a\in C_{0}(d(U)),\ U,V\in S,

with operations given by (
4.4
).

Lemma 4.17
.

For any twisted groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
the pair
(\alpha,u)
defined above is a twisted action of
S
on
C_{0}(X)
.

Proof.

Since
C_{0}(X)
is commutative
(A1)
is equivalent to
\alpha
being an action.
Axioms
(A2)
,
(A3)
,
(A4)
can be checked exactly as in the proof of
[
17
, Proposition 3.6]
using (
4.10
).
∎

Example 4.18
.

If the twist
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
comes from a
2
-cocycle
\sigma
then
S(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma})=\Bis(\mathcal{G})
and for any
\eta\in U
,
\gamma\in V
, we have
u(U,V)(\eta\gamma)=\sigma(\eta,\gamma)\overline{\sigma((\eta\gamma)^{-1},\eta\gamma)}
.

Now let
(\alpha,u)
be any twisted action of
S
on
C_{0}(X)
.
Since
C_{0}(X)
is commutative
\alpha:S\to\PAut(C_{0}(X))
is a semigroup homomorphism, and hence there is a semigroup homomorphism

\alpha_{t}(a)=a\circ h_{t^{*}}
for

,

.

over

, and two triples

\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{G};\ [a,t,x]\mapsto[t,x],

\displaystyle[a,s,h_{t}(x)]\cdot[b,t,x]

Definition 4.20

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be a twisted étale groupoid.
Let

be any wide inverse semigroup and
(\alpha,u)
a twisted action of

4.4.
Integration and disintegration in Banach algebras

We fix a twisted action
(\alpha,u)
of an inverse semigroup
S
on
C_{0}(X)
and let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be the associated twisted groupoid as described in the previous subsection.
By Lemma
4.21
every twisted étale groupoid arises in this way.
The sets
U_{t}=\{[t,x]:x\in X_{t}\}
,
t\in S
, together with
X
form a unital wide inverse subsemigroup of
S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
. We denote it by
\overline{S}
. For each
t\in S
we have a linear isometric isomorphism

(4.11)

C_{c}(X_{t})\ni a_{t}\mapsto a_{t}\delta_{t}\in C_{c}(U_{t},\mathcal{L}),\qquad a_{t}\delta_{t}[t,x]:=[a_{t},t,x],\qquad x\in X_{t},

and we may view
(C_{c}(U_{t},\mathcal{L}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
as a subspace of
(\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}),\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S})
.

Lemma 4.22
.

\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
is spanned by elements
a_{t}\delta_{t}
,
a_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t})
,
t\in S
, and

(1)

a_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot a_{t}\delta_{t}=a_{s}(a_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\delta_{st}
and
(a_{t}\delta_{t})^{*}=\overline{a_{t}\circ h_{t}}\cdot\overline{u(t,t^{*})}\delta_{t^{*}}
;

(2)

s\leq t
implies
X_{s}\subseteq X_{t}
and
a\delta_{s}=a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\delta_{t}
for any
a\in C_{c}(X_{s})
.

Proof.

We only explain
(2)
as the rest is straightforward.
Let
s\leq t
.
Then
X_{s}\subseteq X_{t}
by the composition law (Remark
3.5
) and so also
U_{s}\subseteq U_{t}
.
For
a\in C_{c}(X_{s})
we have
a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\in C_{c}(X_{s})\subseteq C_{c}(X_{t})
and so
a\delta_{s}\in C_{c}(U_{s},\mathcal{L})
and
a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\delta_{t}\in C_{c}(U_{t},\mathcal{L})
.
Take
x\in X_{t^{*}}
.
If
x\in X_{s^{*}}
then
[t,x]=[s,x]
and

a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\delta_{t}[t,x]=[a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)},t,x]=[a,s,x]=a\delta_{s}[t,x]

by the equivalence relations defining
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{L}
.
If
x\notin X_{s^{*}}
then
a\delta_{s}[t,x]=0
by convention, and
a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\delta_{t}[t,x]=[a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)},t,x]=[0,t,x]=0
because
a(h_{t}(x))=0
.
Hence
a\delta_{s}=a\overline{u(ss^{*},t)}\delta_{t}
.
∎

Proposition 4.23
.

Every covariant representation
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in a Banach algebra
B
integrates to
a representation
\pi\rtimes v:F^{\overline{S}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
such that

\pi\rtimes v(a_{t}\delta_{t})=\pi(a_{t})v_{t},\qquad a_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t}),\ t\in S.

If
B
is a
C^{*}
-algebra then
\pi\rtimes v
is
*
-preserving.
If
(\pi,\tilde{v})
is a
B^{\prime}
-normalization (or
B_{*}
-normalization) of
(\pi,v)
as in Proposition
3.8
(or Proposition
3.14
) then
\pi\rtimes v=\pi\rtimes\tilde{v}
.

Proof.

We claim that the formula
\pi\rtimes v(\sum_{t\in F}a_{t}\delta_{t})=\sum_{t\in F}\pi(a_{t})v_{t}
, where
F\subseteq S
is finite, yields a well-defined map
\pi\rtimes v:\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
, or equivalently that
\sum_{t\in F}a_{t}\delta_{t}=0
implies
\sum_{t\in F}\pi(a_{t})v_{t}=0
.
This is proved in
[
34
, Lemma 8.4]
in the untwisted case, under the assumption that
\mathcal{G}
is second countable, using measure theoretical methods.
We prove it in general using topological tools and induction on the cardinality of
F
.
Suppose that
\sum_{t\in F}a_{t}\delta_{t}=0
.
If
|F|=1
then
\sum_{t\in F}\pi(a_{t})v_{t}=0
(because
a_{t}\delta_{t}=0
if and only if
a_{t}=0
).
Now assume the claim is true for all sets with cardinality smaller than that of
F
.
Pick any
t_{0}\in F
and put
F_{0}=F\setminus\{t_{0}\}
.
Then
\sum_{t\in F_{0}}a_{t}\delta_{t}=-a_{t_{0}}\delta_{t_{0}}
, so the closed support of
a_{t_{0}}\delta_{t_{0}}
, which we denote by
K
, is a compact set covered by
\{U_{t}\cap U_{t_{0}}\}_{t\in F_{0}}
.
Since
\overline{S}
is wide,
U_{t}\cap U_{t_{0}}=\bigcup_{s\leq t,t_{0}}U_{s}
.
So for each
t\in F_{0}
we may find
s_{1,t},...,s_{n_{t},t}\leq t,t_{0}
such that
K\subseteq\bigcup_{t\in F}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{t}}U_{s_{i,t}}
.
Let
\{\varrho_{s_{i,t}}\}_{i=1...,n_{t},t\in F_{0}}
be a partition of unity on
K
subordinate to this open cover (which exists as everything happens in the Hausdorff space
U_{t_{0}}
).
Write
\widetilde{\varrho}_{s_{i,t}}:=\varrho_{s_{i,t}}\circ r|_{U_{s_{i,t}}}\in C_{c}(X_{s_{i,t}})\subseteq C_{c}(X_{t}\cap X_{t_{0}})
for each
i
and
t
.
Then by Lemma
4.22
(2)
and Lemma
3.10
(2)
(applied to each
t
,
2n_{t}
-times) we get

a_{t_{0}}\delta_{t_{0}}=\sum_{t\in F_{0}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\widetilde{\varrho}_{s_{i,t}}a_{t_{0}}\frac{u(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{*},t_{0})}{u(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{*},t)}\right)\delta_{t},\qquad\pi(a_{t_{0}})v_{t_{0}}=\sum_{t\in F_{0}}\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\widetilde{\varrho}_{s_{i,t}}a_{t_{0}}\frac{u(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{*},t_{0})}{u(s_{i,t}s_{i,t}^{*},t)}\right)v_{t}.

Thus
\sum_{t\in F}a_{t}\delta_{t}=\sum_{t\in F_{0}}b_{t}\delta_{t}
and
\sum_{t\in F}\pi(a_{t})v_{t}=\sum_{t\in F_{0}}\pi(b_{t})v_{t}
for some
b_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t})
,
t\in F_{0}
.
Hence the claim follows by the inductive hypothesis.
Now Lemma
3.10
(1)
and Lemma
4.22
(1)
readily imply that
\pi\rtimes v:\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
is an algebra homomorphism, which is
*
-preserving if
B
is a
C^{*}
-algebra.
It is
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
contractive because
\|\pi\rtimes v(a_{t}\delta_{t})\|=\|\pi(a_{t})v_{t}\|\leq\|a_{t}\|_{\infty}=\|a_{t}\delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
for every
a_{t}\delta_{t}\in C_{c}(U_{t},\mathcal{L})
,
t\in S
.
Hence it extends to a representation
\pi\rtimes v:F^{\overline{S}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
, which is
*
-preserving if
B
is a
C^{*}
-algebra.
The last statement is clear.
∎

The following lemma will allow us to show the converse to Proposition
4.23
.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
\psi:A\to E
be a contractive linear map from a
C^{*}
-algebra into a Banach space
E
which has a predual Banach space
E_{*}
.
There is a contractive element
\psi(1_{A})\in E
such that for every approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq A
we have
\psi(1_{A})=E_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(\mu_{i})\in E
.

Proof.

For simplicity we assume
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
(the real case can be reduced to the complex case by complexification).
We identify
E_{*}
with a subspace of
E^{\prime}
.
Let
f\in E_{*}
.
Then
\psi^{\prime}(f)=f\circ\psi:A\to\mathbb{C}
is a bounded functional.
Hence it decomposes to
\psi^{\prime}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\tau_{k}
where
\tau_{k}:A\to\mathbb{C}
,
k=0,...,3
, are positive functionals.
Applying the GNS-construction to each
\tau_{k}
, we get representations
\pi_{k}:A\to B(H_{k})
and cyclic vectors
\omega_{k}\in H_{k}
such that
\psi^{\prime}(f)(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\langle\pi_{k}(a)\omega_{k},\omega_{k}\rangle
.
Since
\{\pi_{k}(\mu_{i})\}_{i}
is strongly convergent to the identity on
H_{k}
we conclude that
\{f(\psi(\mu_{i}))\}_{i}
is convergent in
\mathbb{C}
to the number
c_{f,\psi}:=\sum_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\|\omega_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\|\tau_{k}\|
that depends only on
\psi
and
f
(it does not depend on
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i}
).
Now, since the net
\{\psi(\mu_{i})\}_{i}
is bounded in
E
, the Banach–Alaoglu Theorem says that there is a subnet
\{\psi(\mu_{i_{j}})\}_{j}
with an
E_{*}
-limit
\psi(1_{A}):=E_{*}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{j}\psi(\mu_{i_{j}})\in E
.
So in particular
f(\psi(1_{A}))=\lim_{j}f(\psi(\mu_{i_{j}}))=c_{f,\psi}
for every
f\in E_{*}
.
This implies that every net
\{\psi(\mu_{i})\}_{i}
, where
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i}
is an approximate unit, is
E_{*}
-convergent to
\psi(1_{A})
.
∎

Theorem 4.25
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be a twisted étale groupoid. Let
(\alpha,u)
be any twisted inverse semigroup action of
S
that models
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, and denote by
\overline{S}
be the unitization of the image of

in
S(\mathcal{L})
(one can always take a wide unital inverse subsemigroup

).
Then
\psi=\pi\rtimes v
yields a bijective correspondence between representations
\psi:F^{\overline{S}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
in a Banach algebra
B
and

(1)

B^{\prime}
-normalized covariant representations
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B
;

(2)

B_{*}
-normalized covariant representations
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B
, if

is a dual Banach algebra;

(3)

covariant representations
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
on
E

and
E
is a reflexive Banach space.

If each
X_{t}
,

, is compact then the pairs
(\pi,v)
in
(1)
–
(3)
coincide with covariant representations
(\pi,v)
of
(\alpha,u)
in
B
such that
v_{t}=\pi(1_{X_{t}})v_{t}\in B
for all
t\in S
.

Proof.

Let
\psi:F^{\overline{S}}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B
be a representation.
Then
\pi:=\psi|_{C_{0}(X)}
is a representation of
C_{0}(X)
.
For every
t\in S
, the map
C_{c}(X_{t})\ni a_{t}\mapsto\psi(a_{t}\delta_{t})\in B
extends to a linear contraction
C_{0}(X_{t})\to B\subseteq B^{\prime\prime}
.
Let
v_{t}\in B^{\prime\prime}
be the element associated to this map in Lemma
4.24
, where
E=B^{\prime\prime}
and
E_{*}=B^{\prime}
.
Namely, for any approximate unit
\{\mu_{i}^{t}\}_{i}\subseteq C_{0}(X_{t})
we have
v_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})
.
Recall that we consider multiplication in
B^{\prime\prime}
which is
B^{\prime}
-continuous in the second variable.
For
a\in C_{c}(X_{t})=C_{c}(X_{tt^{*}})
we have
(a\delta_{tt^{*}})*(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})\to a\delta_{t}
in
C_{0}(U_{t},\mathcal{L})

Thus

\pi(a)v_{t}=\psi(a\delta_{tt^{*}})B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(a\delta_{tt^{*}}*\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})=\psi(a\delta_{t}).

For

(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})*(a\delta_{t^{*}t})=(\alpha_{t}(a)\mu_{i}^{t})\delta_{t}\to\alpha_{t}(a)\delta_{t}
in

Multiplication in

is
B^{\prime}
-continuous in the first variable if the second variable is in

, so

v_{t}\pi(a)=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t}*a\delta_{t^{*}t})=\psi\big(\alpha_{t}(a)\delta_{t}\big)=\pi(\alpha_{t}(a))v_{t}.

If in addition
t=e\in E(S)
then
v_{e}\pi(a)=\psi(\alpha_{e}(a)\delta_{e})=\psi(a\delta_{e})=\pi(a)
.
If
a\in C_{c}(X_{st})
for
s,t\in S
then
\alpha_{s}^{-1}(a)=a\circ h_{s}\in C_{c}(X_{s^{*}}\cap X_{t})
and therefore
(a\delta_{s})*(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})=a(\mu_{i}^{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\delta_{st}=\alpha_{s}(\alpha_{s}^{-1}(a)\mu_{i}^{t})u(s,t)\delta_{st}\to au(s,t)\delta_{t}
in

Thus

\pi(a)v_{s}v_{t}=B^{\prime}\mathchar 45\relax\lim_{i}\psi(a\delta)\psi(\mu_{i}^{t}\delta_{t})=\psi\big(au(s,t)\delta_{t}\big)=\pi\big(au(s,t)\big)v_{t}.

By construction

.
Hence we see that

is a
B^{\prime}
-normalized covariant representation of
(\alpha,u)
with
\psi=\pi\rtimes v
.

In view of Proposition
4.23
, this gives
(1)
as a

-normalized a covariant representation

of

with

(2)
and
(3)

3.14

The last part of the assertion follows from Remark

4.5.
Borel extension-disintegration

We obtain yet another disintegration theorem by extending representations to a certain algebra of Borel sections, using weak integrals and the extension of functionals via the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem.
This works for representations in the algebra
B(E)
where
E
is a Banach space with a predual (but is not necessarily reflexive).

Let us fix a twisted étale groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and a unital inverse subsemigroup
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
covering
\mathcal{G}
.
For any open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
we denote by

\mathcal{B}(U,\mathcal{L})
the linear space of bounded Borel sections of
\mathcal{L}|_{U}
. We view it as a subspace of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
in an obvious way. Then the space

\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\spane\{f\in\mathcal{B}(U,\mathcal{L}):U\in S\}

is a
*
-algebra with the convolution and involution given by standard formulas (
4.4
).
In particular, we have
\mathcal{B}(U,\mathcal{L})\cdot\mathcal{B}(V,\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{B}(UV,\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{B}(U,\mathcal{L})^{*}=\mathcal{B}(U^{*},\mathcal{L})
for all
U,V\in S
.
For any open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
denote by
\mathcal{B}_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
the set of
f\in\mathcal{B}(U,\mathcal{L})
whose strict support
\supp(f):=\{\gamma\in U:f(\gamma)\neq 0\}
is contained in a compact subset of
U
.

Lemma 4.28
.

\mathcal{B}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})=\spane\{f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}(U,\mathcal{L}):U\in S\}
for any
S\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
covering
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and pick a compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{G}
containing
\supp(f)
.
By considering a family of bisections that are compactly contained in elements of
S
, we may cover
K
by
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in S
which has a refinement
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})

such that for each
i
the closure of
V_{i}
in
U_{i}
is compact, for all
i
.
Then the closure
K_{i}
of each
K\cap V_{i}
in
U_{i}
is compact. Thus
f_{i}:=f|_{V_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{l=1}^{i-1}V_{i}}\in\mathcal{B}_{c}(U_{i},\mathcal{L})
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
and
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}
.
∎

The above lemma implies that
\mathcal{B}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
is a
*
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
In the untwisted (usually second countable) case the algebra
\mathcal{B}_{c}(\mathcal{G})
appears in a number of sources, see for instance in

[
58
, page 42]
,
[
38
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
16
]
. Here we find
\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
more useful, as it contains the indicator functions
1_{U}
,
U\in S
.

From now on we assume that
S\subseteq S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
. Without changing
\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})

we may also assume that
S
is closed under inclusions, and so it is wide.
Let us fix unitary sections
c_{U}\in C_{u}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
U\in S
, and denote by
(\alpha,u)

the associated twisted action of
S
on

Proposition 4.30
.

Let
E
be a Banach space with a predual
E_{*}
.
Let
S\subseteq S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
be a unital inverse semigroup that covers
\mathcal{G}
.
Every
\|\cdot\|_{\max}^{S}
-contractive homomorphism
\psi:\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B(E)
extends to a homomorphism
\widetilde{\psi}:\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B(E)
, which is determined by
the formula

(4.13)

\langle\xi,\widetilde{\psi}(b\delta_{U})\eta\rangle=\int_{r(U)}b\,d\mu^{U}_{\xi,\eta},\qquad b\in\mathcal{B}(r(U)),\ U\in S,\ \xi\in E_{*},\ \eta\in E,

where
\mu^{U}_{\xi,\eta}
is the unique (complex or signed) Radon measure corresponding to the functional

C_{c}(r(U))\ni a\mapsto\langle\xi,\psi(a\delta_{U})\rangle
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
S
is closed under taking open subsets and so it is a wide inverse semigroup.
We denote by
(\alpha,u)
the associated twisted inverse semigroup action of
S
.
For every
\xi\in E^{\prime}
,
\eta\in E
,
t\in S
, we denote by
\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{t}
the unique Radon measure such that
\int_{X_{t}}a\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{t}=\langle\xi,\psi(a\delta_{t})\eta\rangle
for all
a\in C_{c}(X_{t})
(which is given by the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem).
Thus we have a bounded linear functional
\psi^{t}_{\xi,\eta}:\mathcal{B}(U_{t},\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{F}
where
\psi^{t}_{\xi,\eta}(b\delta_{t}):=\int_{X_{t}}b\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{t}
,
for
b\in\mathcal{B}(X_{t})
, and
\|\psi^{t}_{\xi,\eta}\|\leq\|\xi\|\cdot\|\eta\|
.
In fact for a fixed
t\in S
and
b\in\mathcal{B}(X_{t})
, the map
E^{\prime}\times E\ni(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\psi^{t}_{\xi,\eta}(b\delta_{t})\in\mathbb{F}
is a bounded bilinear form (for every Borel
U\subseteq X_{t}
the map
E^{\prime}\times E\ni(\xi,\eta)\mapsto\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{t}(U)
is bilinear) with norm not exceeding
\|b\|_{\infty}
.
Restricting this form to
E_{*}\times E\subseteq E^{\prime}\times E
we conclude that for each
b\in\mathcal{B}(X_{t})
there is an operator
\psi_{t}(b\delta_{t})\in B(E)=B(E_{*}^{\prime})
with
\|\psi_{t}(b\delta_{t})\|\leq\|b\|_{\infty}
given by

\langle\xi,\psi_{t}(b\delta_{t})\eta\rangle=\psi^{t}_{\xi,\eta}(b\delta_{t})=\int_{X_{t}}b\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{t},\qquad t\in S,\ \xi\in E_{*},\ \eta\in E.

This is essentially (
4.13
), written in different notation, and clearly

\psi_{t}(b\delta_{t})=\psi(b\delta_{t})
when
b\in C_{c}(X_{t})
.
Now take
s,t\in S
.
We claim that
\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})\psi_{t}(b_{t}\delta_{t})=\psi_{st}(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})
for all
b_{s}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{s})
,
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{t})
.
Recall that
b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t}=b_{s}(b_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\delta_{st}
.
Assume first that
b_{s}\in C_{c}(X_{s})
,
b_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t})
. Then, for every
(\xi,\eta)\in E^{\prime}\times E
,

\int_{X_{s}}b_{s}\,d\mu_{\xi,\psi(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta}^{s}=\langle\xi,\psi(b_{s}\delta_{s})\psi(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle=\langle\xi,\psi(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle=\int_{X_{st}}b_{s}(b_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}.

By uniqueness of the Radon measure corresponding to a linear functional, this implies that
d\mu_{\xi,\psi(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta}^{s}=(b_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}
as Borel measures supported on
X_{st}\subseteq X_{s}
.
Thus fixing
b_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t})
we conclude that
\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{t})(b_{t}\delta_{t})=\psi_{st}(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})
for every
b_{s}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{s})
.
Hence for any
b_{s}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{s})
and
b_{t}\in C_{c}(X_{t})
we get

\begin{split}\int_{X_{t}}b_{t}\,d\mu_{\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})^{\prime}\xi,\eta}^{t}&=\langle\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})^{\prime}\xi,\psi(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle=\langle\xi,\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})\psi(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle=\langle\xi,\psi_{st}(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle\\
&=\int_{X_{st}}b_{s}(b_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}=\int_{X_{t}}b_{t}(b_{s}u(s,t))\circ h_{s}\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}\circ h_{s}.\end{split}

Therefore, similarly to above,
d\mu_{\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})^{\prime}\xi,\eta}^{t}=(b_{s}u(s,t))\circ h_{s}\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}\circ h_{s}
.
Using this for for all
b_{s}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{s})
,
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{t})
we get

\begin{split}\langle\xi,\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})\psi_{t}(b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle&=\int_{X_{t}}b_{t}\,d\mu_{\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})^{\prime}\xi,\eta}^{t}=\int_{X_{t}}b_{t}(b_{s}u(s,t))\circ h_{s}\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}\circ h_{s}\\
&=\int_{X_{st}}b_{s}(b_{t}\circ h_{s^{*}})u(s,t)\,d\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{st}=\langle\xi,\psi_{st}(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})\eta\rangle.\end{split}

Thus
\psi_{s}(b_{s}\delta_{s})\psi_{t}(b_{t}\delta_{t})=\psi_{st}(b_{s}\delta_{s}\cdot b_{t}\delta_{t})
as claimed.

The above identity now implies that the pair
(\widetilde{\pi},v)
, where
\widetilde{\pi}:=\psi_{e}
and
v_{t}:=\psi_{t}(1_{X_{t}})
, for
t\in S
, is a covariant representation of the associated action
(\widetilde{\alpha},u)
of
S
on
\mathcal{B}(X)
.
By Proposition
4.29
,
(\widetilde{\pi},v)
yields a homomorphism
\widetilde{\psi}:\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B(E)
, which is the desired extension. ∎

Theorem 4.31
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
be a twisted étale groupoid.
Let
S\subseteq S(\mathcal{L})\subseteq\Bis(\mathcal{G})
be a unital wide inverse semigroup, and let
(\alpha,u)
and
(\widetilde{\alpha},u)
be the associated twisted actions of
S

\mathcal{B}^{S}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
which agrees with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
on each slice

,

(it agrees with the norm of

for some covariant representation
(\widetilde{\pi},v)
of
(\widetilde{\alpha},u)
on

.

Proof.

We have

4.26
.

,

implies the natural isometric isomorphism

Clearly,

-isometric homorphism

∎

5.
Representations on
L^{p}
-spaces

Fix
p\in[1,\infty]
and let
q\in[1,\infty]
be the number satisfying
1/p+1/q=1
, with the convention that
1/\infty=0
.
Take a twisted étale groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
with a locally compact Hausdorff unit space
X
.
Regular representations of
\mathcal{G}
on
\ell^{p}
-spaces (with
p<\infty
) were considered in
[
23
]
, and of
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
(but using a different picture of the twist) in
[
45
]
.
We spell out some details using our notation. We denote by
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
the Banach space of all sections of
\mathcal{L}
for which the norm
\|f\|_{p}=(\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}|f(\gamma)|^{p})^{1/p}
when
p<\infty
and

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}|f(\gamma)|
when
p=\infty
, is finite.
We have an isometric isomorphism
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\cong\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For each
p\in[1,\infty]
the formula

\Lambda_{p}(f)\xi(\gamma):=(f*\xi)(\gamma)=\sum_{r(\eta)=r(\gamma)}f(\eta)\cdot\xi(\eta^{-1}\gamma),\qquad f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}),\ \xi\in\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}),

defines a representation
\Lambda_{p}:F(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B(\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}))
, which is injective on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, and

(1)

for
p\in(1,\infty)
we have
\|\Lambda_{p}(f)\|\leq\|f\|_{*d}^{1/p}\cdot\|f\|_{*r}^{1/q}\leq\|f\|_{I}
, while
\|\Lambda_{1}(f)\|=\|f\|_{*d}
and
\|\Lambda_{\infty}(f)\|=\|f\|_{*r}
, for all
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
;

(2)

putting
F^{p}_{\red}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}):=\overline{\Lambda_{p}(\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}))}
, we have
F^{p}_{\red}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\op}\cong F^{q}_{\red}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
, with the isometric isomorphism induced by the map
f\mapsto\widecheck{f}
.
In particular,
F^{p}_{\red}(\mathcal{G})^{\op}\cong F^{q}_{\red}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

It is routine to check that the formula in the assertion gives a well defined homomorphism
\Lambda_{p}:\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})\to B(\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}))
, and
\|\Lambda_{p}(f)\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
for any
f\in C_{c}(U,\mathcal{L})
,
U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G})
.
Hence
\Lambda_{p}
is
\|\cdot\|_{\max}
-contractive and thus it uniquely extends to a representation of
F(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
, see also Remark
5.2
below.
For each
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
choose a norm one element
1_{\gamma}\in L_{\gamma}
and treat it as a section of
\mathcal{L}
which is zero at
\eta\neq\gamma
.
Then
\{1_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}
is a Schauder basis for
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
and for any
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G})
we have

\|\Lambda_{p}(f)1_{\gamma}\|_{p}=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{d(\eta)=d(\gamma)}|f(\eta\gamma^{-1})|^{p}\right)^{1/p},&\text{ if $p<\infty$}\\
\sup_{d(\eta)=d(\gamma)}|f(\eta\gamma^{-1})|,&\text{ if $p=\infty$}.\end{cases}

This implies that
\Lambda_{p}
is injective on
\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
.
Also using this one sees that
\|\Lambda_{1}(f)\|=\|f\|_{*d}
and
\|\Lambda_{\infty}(f)\|=\|f\|_{*r}
.
Then the inequalities
\|\Lambda_{p}(f)\|\leq\|f\|_{*d}^{1/p}\cdot\|f\|_{*r}^{1/q}\leq\|f\|_{I}
follow from the Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem.
This proves
(1)
.
To see
(2)
it suffices to note that under the standard isomorphism
\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})^{\prime}\cong\ell^{q}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
given by the pairing
\langle\xi,\eta\rangle:=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\xi(\gamma)\eta(\gamma)
, for
\xi\in\ell^{p}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L})
,
\eta\in\ell^{q}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{L}^{\op})
, we have
\Lambda^{p}(f)^{\prime}=\Lambda^{q}(\widecheck{f})
for all
f\in\mathfrak{C}_{c}(\mathcal{G})

(note that
\xi(\gamma)\eta(\gamma)\in L_{r(\gamma)}=\mathbb{F}
because the bundle
\mathcal{L}
is trivial over
X
).
∎

6.
Banach algebras associated to inverse semigroups

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup.
The algebra
\mathbb{F}S
of formal finite
\mathbb{F}
-linear combinations of elements in
S
and multiplication induced from
S
is the universal algebra for semigroup homomorphisms of
S
.
Since
S
is more than just a semigroup, we will consider here completions of
\mathbb{F}S
such that the commutative subalgebra
\mathbb{F}E
, generated by the idempotents
E:=E(S)
, is a uniform Banach algebra.
This boils down to an assumption of
joint contractiveness
that we introduce in Definition
6.4
below.
When applied to tight representations this will give us a useful description of Banach algebras associated to ample groupoids.
Some of our results here are already new and might be interesting for
C^{*}
-algebraists.

We start by recalling the groupoid models for
\mathbb{F}S
and
\mathbb{F}E
from
[
72
]
.
Recall that the semigroup
E
is a semilattice: the ‘meet’ and ‘preorder’ are defined by
e\wedge f:=ef
and
e\leq f
if and only if
ef=e
.
We write
e<f
if
e\leq f
and
e\neq f
.
If
E
has a zero, we say
e
and
f
are orthogonal if
ef=0
.
The set
\widehat{E}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{E}
of all non-zero homomorphisms
\phi:E\to\{0,1\}
is a totally disconnected locally compact Hausdorff space in the product topology of the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{E}
(which is the topology of pointwise convergence).
In fact, putting
Z(e):=\{\phi\in\widehat{E}:\phi(e)=1\}
, the sets
Z(e)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}Z(f)
, where
e\in E
and
F\subseteq eE
is finite, form a basis of compact-open sets for
\widehat{E}
.
We call
\widehat{E}
the
spectrum
of
E
.
A map
\phi:E\to\{0,1\}
is in
\widehat{E}
if and only if its support
\supp(\phi):=\{e\in E:\phi(e)=1\}
is a
filter
of
E
, i.e. a non-empty, upward-closed and downward directed and subset of
E
.
There is a natural action
h:S\to\PHomeo(\widehat{E})
given by

h_{t}:Z(t^{*}t)\to Z(tt^{*});\ h_{t}(\phi)(e):=\phi(t^{*}et),\qquad t\in S,\ e\in E,\ \phi\in\widehat{E}

(see
[
34
, Proposition 10.3]
).
The corresponding transformation groupoid
\mathcal{G}(S):=S\ltimes_{h}\widehat{E}
is ample.
It is covered by compact open bisections
U_{t}=\{[t,\phi]:\phi\in Z(t^{*}t)\}
,
t\in S
.
The associated Steinberg algebra is

A(\mathcal{G}(S)):=\spane\{1_{U}:\text{$U\in\Bis(\mathcal{G}(S))$ compact open}\}=\spane\{1_{U_{t}}:t\in S\}\subseteq C_{c}(\mathcal{G}(S)).

By a homomorphism from the semigroup
S
to an algebra
B
we mean a semigroup homomorphism
v:S\to B
into the multiplicative semigroup of
B
.

Proposition 6.1

(
[
72
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

The map
t\mapsto 1_{U_{t}}
determines an isomorphism
\mathbb{F}S\cong A(\mathcal{G}(S))
.
Thus for any
\mathbb{F}
-algebra
B
, the relation
\psi(1_{U_{t}})=v_{t}
,
t\in S
, establishes a bijective correspondence between algebra homomorphisms
\psi:A(\mathcal{G}(S))\to B
and homomorphisms
v:S\to B
.

We have the following commutative subalgebra of
A(\mathcal{G}(S))
:

A(\widehat{E}):=\spane\{1_{U}:\text{$U\subseteq\widehat{E}$ compact open}\}=\spane\{1_{Z(e)}:e\in E\}\subseteq C_{c}(\widehat{E})\subseteq C_{c}(\mathcal{G}(S)).

Proposition
6.1
applied to
E
gives a bijective correspondence between algebra homomorphisms
\pi:A(\widehat{E})\to B
and homomorphisms
v:E\to B
.
We now aim to characterise injectivity of
\pi
in terms of
v
.
For finite
F\subseteq E
we write
\bigwedge{F}:=\bigwedge_{e\in F}e=\prod_{e\in F}e
.

Lemma 6.2

(Inclusion-exclusion principle)
.

Let
v:E\to B
be a homomorphism into an algebra
B
.
For any finite
F\subseteq E
and
F_{0}\subseteq F
put

(6.17)

P_{F_{0}}^{F}:=\prod_{f\in F\setminus F_{0}}(v_{\bigwedge{F_{0}}}-v_{\bigwedge{F_{0}}\wedge f})=\sum_{F_{0}\subseteq G\subseteq F}(-1)^{|G\setminus F_{0}|}v_{\bigwedge{G}},

with the convention
P_{F}^{F}=v_{\bigwedge{F}}
and
P_{\emptyset}^{F}=0
.
Then
\{P_{F_{0}}^{F}\}_{F_{0}\subseteq F}
are mutually orthogonal idempotents such that

v_{e}=\sum_{e\in F_{0}\subseteq F}P_{F_{0}}^{F}
,
e\in F
.

Proof.

This can be proved by induction on
|F|
.
If
|F|=1
the assertion is trivial.
Suppose the assertion holds whenever
|F|<n
, and fix
F
with
|F|=n
.
Fix also
e_{0}\in F
and let
H=F\setminus\{e_{0}\}
.
For any
F_{0}\subseteq F
we then have

P_{F_{0}}^{F}=\begin{cases}P_{F_{0}}^{H}-P_{F_{0}}^{H}v_{e_{0}},&e_{0}\not\in F_{0},\\
P_{F_{0}\setminus\{e_{0}\}}^{H}v_{e_{0}},&e_{0}\in F_{0},\ F_{0}\neq\{e_{0}\},\\
v_{e_{0}}-\sum_{H_{0}\subseteq H}P_{H_{0}}^{H},&F_{0}\neq\{e_{0}\}.\end{cases}

By the inductive hypothesis
\{P_{H_{0}}^{H}\}_{H_{0}\subseteq H}
are mutually orthogonal and
v_{e}=\sum_{e\in H_{0}\subseteq H}P_{H_{0}}^{H}
,
e\in H
.
Using this and the above relations it is readily checked that
\{P_{F_{0}}^{F}\}_{F_{0}\subseteq F}
are also mutually orthogonal, and
v_{e}=\sum_{e\in F_{0}\subseteq F}P_{F_{0}}^{F}
,
e\in F
.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\pi:A(\widehat{E})\to B
be an algebra homomorphism and let
v:E\to B
be the corresponding semigroup homomorphism.
Then
\pi
is injective if and only if
\prod_{f\in F}(v_{e}-v_{f})\neq 0
for every
e\in E\setminus\{0\}
and finite
F\subseteq eE\setminus\{e\}
.

Proof.

Idempotents (
6.17
) corresponding to idempotents
\{1_{Z(f)}\}_{f\in F}\subseteq A(E)
are characteristic functions of basic cylinder sets
Z\big(\bigwedge F_{0}\big)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F\setminus F_{0}}Z\big(\bigwedge F_{0}\wedge f\big)
.
Hence, by Lemma
6.2
, every element in
A(E)
is a finite linear combination of mutually orthogonal idempotents of the form
1_{Z(e)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}Z(f)}
, where
e\in E\setminus\{0\}
and
F\subseteq eE\setminus\{e\}
, which are mapped by
\pi
to mutually orthogonal idempotents of the form
\prod_{f\in F}(v_{e}-v_{f})
.
Moreover,
1_{Z(e)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}Z(f)}\neq 0
because
\supp\phi:=\{f\in E:f\geq e\}
is a filter that defines
\phi
that is in
Z(e)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}Z(f)
.
Hence
\pi
is injective if and only if it is non-zero on elements of the form
1_{Z(e)\setminus\bigcup_{f\in F}Z(f)}
.
∎

For representations in Banach algebras we need an analytic condition.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
B
be a Banach algebra over
\mathbb{F}
and let
M\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be a set.
We say that a collection of mutually orthogonal idempotents
\{P_{i}\}_{i\in F}\subseteq B
is
jointly
M
-contractive
if

\|\sum_{i\in F}a_{i}{P_{i}}\|\leq\max_{i\in F}|a_{i}|,\qquad a_{i}\in M,\ i\in F.

Remark 6.5
.

The above concept embraces a number of other notions existing in the literature.
For instance, if
B
is a unital Banach algebra then
\{P,1-P\}
are jointly
\{0,1\}
-contractive if and only if
\{P,1-P\}\subseteq B_{1}
, that is
P
is bicontractive
[
22
]
,
[
10
]
. The projection in Example
2.14
is bicontractive but not an
L^{p}
-projection.
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, then
\{P,1-P\}
are jointly
\mathbb{T}
-contractive if and only if
zP+w(1-P)
are isometries (contractive invertible elements with contractive inverses), for all
z,w\in\mathbb{T}
, which by definition means that
P
is
bicircular
, see
[
71
]
.
Every bicircular projection is bicontractive but not conversely, see
[
71
]
.
In fact, a projection
P
is bicircular if and only if it is hermitian
[
47
]
, and so bicontractive projections in Example
2.14
are not bicircular by Theorem
2.28
.
For any
p\in[1,\infty]
and any Banach space
E
, mutually orthogonal
L^{p}
-projections
\{P_{i}\}_{i\in F}\subseteq B(E)
are jointly
\mathbb{F}
-contractive (the Banach algebra they generate embeds isometrically into
c_{0}(F)
).

Definition 6.6
.

A
representation of the inverse semigroup

in a Banach algebra

is a semigroup homomorphism

such that for every finite

the idempotents

-contractive.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let

be a semigroup homomorphism into the contractive elements in a Banach algebra

(1)

(2)

the map

(1)

.
By Proposition

, the map

, for finite

and

,

Let

be the orthogonal idempotents associated to

The corresponding idempotents associated to

Theorem 6.9
.

Let

be the associated universal groupoid for an inverse semigroup

We have an isometric isomorphism

.
Namely, the relations

establish a bijective correspondence between representations

and
v:S\to B_{1}
in a Banach algebra

.
Moreover,

is isometric if and only if for every

and every finite

we have

.

Proof.

By the last part of Theorem
4.25
every representation of

in

is of the form
\pi\rtimes v
where

is a representation and

is a semigroup homomorphism such that

,

,
and

for all

,

.
In particular, Lemma
6.7
implies that

is a representation of the inverse semigroup
S
.
Conversely, if

is a representation of the inverse semigroup
S
, then by Proposition

there is a unique algebra homomorphism

such that

,

.
Putting

.

Remark 6.11

When

We now pass to representations of

Here by a

Definition 6.12

Remark 6.13

If

.

By

, the set

.

When

By

6.1.
Banach algebras associated to directed graphs

Let
Q=(Q^{0},Q^{1},r_{Q},s_{Q})

(sometimes also called a
quiver
).
So
Q^{0}
is the set of vertices,
Q^{1}
is the set of edges and
r_{Q},s_{Q}:Q^{1}\to Q^{0}
are the range and source maps.
The vertices in

regular
and the remaining
Q_{\textup{sing}}:=Q^{0}\setminus Q_{\textup{reg}}
are called
singular
.
We denote by
Q^{n}
,
n>0
, the set of finite paths
\mu=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{n}
, where
s_{Q}(\mu_{i})=r_{Q}(\mu_{i+1})
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.
Then
|\mu|=n
stands for the length of
\mu
and
Q^{*}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}Q^{n}
is the set of all finite paths (vertices are treated as paths of length zero).
We denote by
Q^{\infty}
the set of infinite paths and put
Q^{\leq\infty}:=Q^{*}\cup Q^{\infty}
.
The maps
r_{Q},s_{Q}
extend naturally to
Q^{*}
and
r_{Q}
extends to
Q^{\infty}
.
We recall the defintion of the Leavitt path algebra associated to
Q
, see
[
2
]
.

Definition 6.19
.

A
Q
-family in an algebra
B
(over

(P,T)

(CK1)

\{T_{e}T_{e}^{*}\}_{e\in E^{1}}
are mutually orthogonal and

for all
e\in Q^{1}

(CK3)

P_{v}=\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(v)}T_{e}T_{e}^{*}
for all

Let
(P,T)
be a
Q
-family in

For

By definition the

The elements

F\subseteq Q^{*}
the elements

P^{F}_{\mu}:=\prod_{\mu\mu^{\prime}\in F\setminus\{\mu\}}T_{\mu}T_{\mu}^{*}-T_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}T_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}^{*},\qquad\mu\in F,

(with the convention
P^{F}_{\mu}:=T_{\mu}T_{\mu}^{*}
if the product is over the empty set)
are mutually orthogonal idempotents such that
T_{\mu}T_{\mu}^{*}=\sum_{\mu\mu^{\prime}\in F\setminus\{\mu\}}P^{F}_{\mu}
, so they span the algebra
\spane\{T_{\mu}T_{\mu}^{*}:\mu\in F\}
.
If
B
is a Banach algebra, then the map
(A(\partial Q),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\rightarrow\spane\{T_{\mu}T_{\mu}^{*}:\mu\in Q^{*}\}\subseteq B
;
1_{Z(\mu)}\mapsto t_{\mu}t_{\mu}^{*}
is contractive if and only if for every finite
F\subseteq E^{*}
the idempotents
\{P^{F}_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in F}
are jointly
\mathbb{F}
-contractive (Definition
6.4
).

Proof.

The first part follows from
[
70
, Lemma 3.1]
, see also
[
76
, Lemma 3.4]
.
This corresponds to the fact every element
f\in A(\partial Q)
can be written in the form
f=\sum_{\mu\in F}a_{\mu}1_{Z(\mu,F)}
where
Z(\mu,F):=Z(\mu)\setminus\bigcup_{\mu^{\prime}\in F\setminus\{\mu\}}Z(\mu\mu^{\prime})
are pairwise disjoint compact open sets for all
\mu\in F
and a fixed finite set
F\subseteq Q^{*}
.
Then
\|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{\mu\in F}|a_{\mu}|
, and the map in the second part of the assertion sends
f
to
\sum_{\mu\in F}a_{\mu}{P^{F}_{\mu}}
, so it is contractive if
\sum_{\mu\in F}a_{\mu}{P^{F}_{\mu}}\leq\max_{\mu\in F}|a_{\mu}|
for all choices of
F
and
a_{\mu}
.
∎

Theorem 6.24
.

For any directed graph

we have natural isometric isomorphisms

(6.20)

More precisely,

(6.21)

\psi(p_{v})=P_{v}=V_{(v,v)},\qquad\psi(t_{e})=T_{e}=V_{(e,s(e))},\qquad\psi(t_{e}^{*})=T_{e}^{*}=V_{(s(e),e)},

, Banach

Remark 6.27

(Banach–Cuntz algebras)
.

For any
n>1
consider the graph

with a single vertex

and
n
edges

.
Then our

is a Banach algebra analogue of the Cuntz

-algebra

Daws and Horváth
[
30
]
proposed a different construction: the

is by definition the quotient of the Banach

-algebra

]

It seems unlikely that this homomorphism is surjective.
To prove this one could try to show that there are no bounded traces on

, cf.
[
30
