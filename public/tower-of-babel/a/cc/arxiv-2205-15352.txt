Canonical representations of surface groups
Aaron Landesman, Daniel Litt

Abstract
. Let Σ g , n \Sigma_{g,n} be an orientable surface of genus g g with n n punctures. We study actions of the mapping class group Mod g , n \operatorname{Mod}_{g,n} of Σ g , n \Sigma_{g,n} via Hodge-theoretic and arithmetic techniques. We show that if ρ : π 1 ​ ( Σ g , n ) → GL r ⁡ ( ℂ ) \rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C}) is a representation whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under Mod g , n \operatorname{Mod}_{g,n} , and r < g + 1 r<\sqrt{g+1} , then ρ \rho has finite image. This answers questions of Junho Peter Whang and Mark Kisin. We give applications of our methods to the Putman-Wieland conjecture, the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture, and a question of Esnault-Kerz. The proofs rely on non-abelian Hodge theory, our earlier work on semistability of isomonodromic deformations, and recent work of Esnault-Groechenig and Klevdal-Patrikis on Simpson’s integrality conjecture for cohomologically rigid local systems.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Let
\Sigma_{g,n}
be an orientable topological surface of genus
g
with
n
punctures, and let
x\in\Sigma_{g,n}
be a basepoint.
We denote the mapping class group of
\Sigma_{g,n}
by
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
.
There is a natural outer action of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
on
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n},x)
, induced by the action of
\operatorname{Homeo}^{+}(\Sigma_{g,n})
on
\Sigma_{g,n}
.
Hence,
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
acts on the set of conjugacy classes of
representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n},x)
into
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
. Our goal is to study the finite orbits of this action
via Hodge-theoretic and arithmetic techniques.

Definition 1.1.1
.

For
g,n,r\geq 0
,
a representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n},x)\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

is
MCG-finite
if the conjugacy class of
\rho
has finite orbit under the action of

\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
.

Note here that we are studying
conjugacy classes
of representations. For
example, semisimple MCG-finite representations correspond to finite

\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
-orbits in the character variety of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
, not
its representation variety. Indeed,
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
does not naturally act on
the representation variety of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
.

Any representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
constructed without making choices
(e.g. without naming a specific curve in
\Sigma_{g,n}
),
or by making choices with only a finite amount of indeterminacy, is MCG-finite.
So we view MCG-finite representations
as the
canonical
representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
.

The study of MCG-finite
representations has a long history.
It has a close
connection to isomonodromy and the Painlevé VI equation,
originally introduced in 1902 by Painlevé

[
Pai02
]

and Gambier

[
Gam10
]
.
For each
g,n,r
, conjugacy classes of semisimple MCG-finite representations

\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

correspond to algebraic solutions
to a certain
isomonodromy
differential equation, see
[
CH19
, Theorem A]
.
Algebraic solutions to the Painlevé VI equation in an appropriate choice of coordinates
correspond to MCG-finite representations with trivial determinant in
the particular case
g=0,n=4,r=2
(see

[
Mah99
, §1-§3]

and
[
Dor01
, §2]
).

There is substantial literature on the
search for algebraic solutions
to the Painlevé VI equation,

[
AK02
,
Boa05
,
Boa06
,
Boa07b
,
Boa07a
,
CL09
,
Dub96
,
DM00
,
Hit95
,
Hit03
,
Kit05
,
Kit06
]
,
culminating in the classification given in
[
LT14
]
. Boalch
[
Boa10
]
pitches the problem of classifying algebraic solutions to Painlevé VI as a natural generalization of Schwarz’s list of hypergeometric equations with finite monodromy
[
Sch73
]
.

We will later see that the classification of MCG-finite representations is
closely connected to several major open questions in low-dimensional topology.
In particular, it has connections to the Putman-Wieland conjecture

[
PW13
, Conjecture 1.2]
and Ivanov’s conjecture that mapping class groups do not virtually surject onto
\mathbb{Z}

[
Iva06
, §7]
.
These conjectures in turn have applications to algebraic geometry, as they would
determine the first rational homology of finite covers of
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
.

Many interesting classes of
representations are MCG-finite.
Examples include
the rigid local systems studied by Katz
[
Kat96
]
,
representations constructed via TQFT techniques (e.g.
[
Kup11
,
KS16
,
KS18a
,
KS18b
]
),
and representations of algebro-geometric interest, such as those constructed via the Kodaira-Parshin trick (e.g.
[
LL19
, Example 3.3.1]
).
We will see later that MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
are closely related to representations of finite-index subgroups of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n+1}
.

1.2.
Results for surface groups

Given the difficulty of classifying MCG-finite representations in the
g=0,n=4,r=2
case (encompassing Painlevé VI), it may be surprising that
we are able to obtain a complete and simple characterization of MCG-finite representations when

g\gg r
.

Theorem 1.2.1
.

For
g,n,r\geq 0
, let

\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

be a MCG-finite
representation. If
r<\sqrt{g+1}
, then
\rho
has finite image.

This answers a question of Junho Peter Whang
[
LL19
, Question
1.5.3]
. We will prove

Theorem 1.2.1
in

§ 8.7
.

While the statement appears to be purely topological or even group-theoretic in nature, the proof relies on (non-abelian and mixed) Hodge theory, and also takes input from the Langlands program, through the work of Esnault-Groechenig
[
EG18
]

and Klevdal-Patrikis
[
KP20b
]

on integrality of cohomologically rigid local systems.

Remark 1.2.2
.

Some bound on the rank as in
Theorem 1.2.1
is
necessary. Indeed, there are a number of interesting MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
of rank
r\gg g
with infinite image, as explained in
§ 10.2
.
We have no reason to think that the precise bound of

\sqrt{g+1}
is optimal in general.
That said the bound cannot be improved too much. It is sharp when
g=1
,
by
10.2.1
, but not for
g=2,3
, by
10.2.2
.
For general
g
there are MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
of rank

2g+1
with infinite image (
10.1.6
(
3
)), so the bound cannot be improved to be better than linear in
g
.
We summarize the situation in
Figure 1
.

Figure 1.
The geography of known MCG-finite representations.

Theorem 1.2.1
shows that the region
r<\sqrt{g+1}
contains
only MCG-finite representations with finite image. As explained in

§ 10.2
, there are examples of MCG-finite rank
r
representations with infinite image for
r\gg g
. For
r
polynomial in
g
, all known examples have virtually solvable image. For
r
exponential in
g
, there are interesting MCG-finite irreps with infinite image.

As explained in
[
CH19
, Theorem A]
,

Theorem 1.2.1
yields a classification result for algebraic solutions to isomonodromy differential equations:

Corollary 1.2.3
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
, and let
D\subset C

be a reduced effective divisor. Let
({E},\nabla)
be a semisimple flat
vector bundle on
C
with regular singularities along
D
, with
\rk{E}<\sqrt{g+1}
. Suppose that the eigenvalues of the residue matrices of

({E},\nabla)
have real parts in
[0,1)
. Then
({E},\nabla)
has an algebraic universal isomonodromic deformation if and only if
({E},\nabla)
has finite monodromy.

Note that the condition on residue matrices can always be achieved after a birational gauge transformation, by replacing
({E},\nabla)
with the Deligne canonical extension of
({E},\nabla)|_{C\setminus D}
.

This algebro-geometric reformulation of our main result is not just window
dressing. It is the perspective we will take in the proof of
Theorem 1.2.1
.

1.3.
An application to low rank local systems on
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}

We next record a consequence of our main result for local systems of low rank on families of curves.
As in
1.10.1
, we say a family
\pi:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{M}
of smooth proper genus

g
curves with geometrically connected fibers, equipped with
n
disjoint sections
s_{i}:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{C}
, is
versal
if the corresponding map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}

is dominant. We call

\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}:=\mathscr{C}\setminus\bigcup_{i}s_{i}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathscr{M}
a
punctured versal family
of genus
g
curves.

The following is immediate from
Theorem 1.2.1
, by
2.1.3
below.

Corollary 1.3.1
.

Suppose
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}

is a punctured versal family of genus
g
curves,
and
\mathbb{V}
is a local system on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
of rank
<\sqrt{g+1}
.
For any fiber
C^{\circ}
of
\pi^{\circ}
, the local system
\mathbb{V}|_{C^{\circ}}
has finite monodromy.

1.4.
The Putman-Wieland conjecture

Among the main ingredients in the proof of
Theorem 1.2.1
are some new results toward the Putman-Wieland conjecture, sketched below and
described in detail in
§ 7
.

We now set some notation to state the Putman-Wieland conjecture.
Let
\Sigma_{g,n}
be a surface and let
\phi:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n},x)\twoheadrightarrow H
be a surjection onto a finite group, corresponding to
some finite
H
-cover
\Sigma_{g^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{g}
branched over
n
points. Let
\Gamma
be the stabilizer of
\phi
up to conjugacy in the pure mapping class group of
\Sigma_{g,n+1}
. Then
\Gamma
acts naturally acts on
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})
.

We say that
the Putman-Wieland conjecture for
(g,n,H)
holds
if
for every such
H
-cover
\Sigma_{g^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{g}
,
all nonzero vectors in
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})
have infinite orbit under
\Gamma
.
The original Putman-Wieland conjecture

[
PW13
, Conjecture 1.2]

asserts that for any fixed
g\geq 2,n\geq 0
,
the Putman-Wieland conjecture for
(g,n,H)

holds for every
H
.
We prove
Putman-Wieland conjecture for
(g,n,H)
for any fixed
H
once
g

is sufficiently large.
In this way, our results are an “asymptotic” version of the Putman-Wieland conjecture.

Theorem 1.4.1
.

For
g\geq 2,n\geq 0
, and any finite group
H
with

\#H<g^{2}
,
the Putman-Wieland conjecture for
(g,n,H)
holds.

Theorem 1.4.1
is proven below as a case of
7.2.4
.
More generally, for arbitrary
H
we prove that the Putman-Wieland conjecture holds for the subspace of
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})
spanned by irreducible representations of
H
of small rank.

Theorem 1.4.2
.

Let
\rho
be an irreducible representation of
H
with
\dim\rho<g
, and let
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})^{\rho}
be the
\rho
-isotypic component of
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})
. Then no nonzero vector in
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}})^{\rho}
has finite orbit under
\Gamma
.

Theorem 1.4.2
is proven below as a special case of

Theorem 7.2.1
.

As we shall see in the course of the proof of
Theorem 1.2.1
,
especially
8.6.1
,
the Putman-Wieland conjecture is connected to the classification of MCG-finite representations with virtually solvable image.

1.5.
Arithmetic applications

In
§ 9
, we give a number of applications
to questions in arithmetic geometry. For example, we show in

Theorem 9.1.2
that low rank representations of the
arithmetic étale fundamental group of a generic curve of genus
g
have finite
image when restricted to the geometric fundamental group. This verifies a
prediction of the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture, see

9.1.5
.
These results also answer a question of Esnault-Kerz, see
9.1.3
.

As a consequence, we construct many residual
representations of the geometric fundamental group of a generic curve of genus

g
which have no lifts to representations of geometric origin. These examples are related to a conjecture of de Jong
[
dJ01
]
(proven by Gaitsgory
[
Gai07
]
) and a question of Flach, see
9.2.5
and
9.2.6
.

1.6.
A consequence for free groups

From
Theorem 1.2.1
, we deduce an analogous result for free groups.

Corollary 1.6.1
.

Let
F_{N}
be a free group on
N
generators, with
N=2g
or
N=2g+1
.
Let

\rho:F_{N}\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

be a representation whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
. If
r<\sqrt{g+1}
, then
\rho
has finite image.

Proof.

If
N
is even, choose an isomorphism
F_{N}\simeq\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,1})

and if
N
is odd choose
F_{N}\simeq\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,2})
. Any
representation of
F_{N}
with finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
yields a
representation of a genus
g
surface group with finite orbit under the
mapping class group. Such a representation has finite image by
Theorem 1.2.1
.
∎

Remark 1.6.2
.

It is natural to ask if the bound on
r
in
1.6.1
is sharp.
There exist (non-semisimple) representations of
F_{N}
of fairly low rank (
r=N+1
) with infinite image and finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
, see

10.2.7
, so the bound cannot be improved too much.
However, in contrast with the case of MCG-finite representations, we do not know any examples of
semisimple
representations of
F_{N}
with infinite image and finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
as soon as
N\geq 3
.
Indeed, a conjecture of Grunewald and Lubotzky
[
GL09
, Conjecture in
§9.2]
, combined with the main result of

[
FH17
]
, implies that no such examples exist when
N\geq 3
. In the interests of provocation, we conjecture
(
10.2.8
)
that such examples
do
exist.
As evidence, we offer several examples of semisimple MCG-finite representations of surface groups with infinite image, see
§ 10.2
. Note that there are interesting semisimple representations of
F_{2}
with finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{2})
, see
10.2.7
.

Remark 1.6.3
.

One appeal of
1.6.1
lies in the fact that it admits a completely elementary reformulation, using Nielsen’s description of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{N})
,
see
[
Nie24
]

and also
[
MKS04
, Theorem 3.2, p. 131]
.
Let
(A_{1},\cdots,A_{N})
be an
N
-tuple of invertible
r\times r
complex matrices, with
N=2g
or
N=2g+1
. Consider the following operations on
N
-tuples of invertible matrices:

(1)

The cyclic permutation

c:(A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})\mapsto(A_{2},A_{3},\cdots,A_{N},A_{1})

(2)

The transposition

\tau:(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\cdots,A_{N})\mapsto(A_{2},A_{1},A_{3},\cdots,A_{N})

(3)

The inversion map

\epsilon:(A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})\mapsto(A_{1}^{-1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})

(4)

The Dehn twist

d:(A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})\mapsto(A_{1}A_{2},A_{2},\cdots,A_{N})

We say
(A_{1},\cdots,A_{N})
and
(A_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,A_{N}^{\prime})
are
conjugate
if there exists some
B
such that
A_{i}=BA_{i}^{\prime}B^{-1}
for all
i
.

Now suppose that the set of
N
-tuples obtained from
(A_{1},\cdots,A_{N})
by repeatedly applying
c,\tau,\epsilon
, and
d
only intersect finitely many conjugacy classes of
N
-tuples

(A_{1},\cdots,A_{N})
.
If
r<\sqrt{g+1}
,

1.6.1
implies that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}
generate a finite subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
.

Remark 1.6.4
.

Similarly to
1.6.3
, one could make

Theorem 1.2.1
explicit, using any one of the known
generating sets of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
, but the formulas are a bit more
involved, as in
[
CH19
, §6]
. It would in our view be of great interest to find a
proof
of
Theorem 1.2.1
or
1.6.1
of a similarly explicit nature.

Remark 1.6.5
.

A result analogous to
1.6.1
follows immediately for
characteristic quotients of free or surface groups. For example, let
G
be a
characteristic quotient of a free group
F_{N}
on
N=2g
or
N=2g+1
generators,
and
\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
a representation whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under

. If

.

1.7.
Cohomological results

One of the new technical inputs we use to achieve
Theorem 1.2.1
is
an analysis of the cohomology of unitary local systems on families of curves.
Our main result, which follows from an analysis of the derivative of the period
map associated to the mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of unitary local
systems, is the following.
We adapt notation described later in
1.10.1
.

Theorem 1.7.1
.

Let
\pi:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{M}
be a smooth proper family of
n
-pointed
curves of genus
g
with geometrically connected fibers, so that the associated
map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
is dominant étale. Let
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
be the associated family of punctured curves.
Let
\mathbb{V}
be a unitary local system on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
. Then any
nonzero sub-local system of
R^{1}\pi_{*}^{\circ}\mathbb{V}
has rank at least
2g-2\rk\mathbb{V}
.

We prove this in
§ 6.1
, and give a related cohomological vanishing theorem with milder unitarity hypotheses in
Theorem 6.2.1
.
Note that
Theorem 1.7.1
shows that
H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi_{*}^{\circ}\mathbb{V})=0
if

g>\operatorname{rk}\mathbb{V}
.
This result is used several times in the proof of

Theorem 1.2.1
, as described in

§ 1.9
.
It is especially interesting when
\mathbb{V}
has finite monodromy, where it is closely related to the Putman-Wieland conjecture, as described
above in
§ 1.4
and in more detail in
§ 7
.

1.8.
Prior work

1.8.1.
MCG-finite representations and Painlevé VI

As mentioned in
§ 1.1
, there has been a huge amount of effort put forth towards classifying algebraic solutions to the Painlevé VI equation, arguably beginning with work of Riemann
[
Rie57
]
and Schwarz
[
Sch73
]

and culminating in the classification
[
LT14
]
.
Calligaris-Mazzocco
[
CM18
]
classified algebraic
solutions satisfying several conditions in the case
g=0,n=5,r=2
, but the complete classification in
this case remains open. See also
[
Mic06
]
for a related result in the genus zero case, when the local monodromy matrices are given by reflections; this appears to be the first result showing that MCG-finite representations (satisfying certain conditions) have finite image.

For all the work invested in the algebraic solutions to Painlevé VI, this only
addresses the case of classifying MCG-finite representations with
g=0,n=4,r=2
, and trivial determinant, as explained in
[
LT14
]
.
In higher genus, all work that we know of has been on the case of

2
-dimensional representations.
The beautiful paper
[
BGMW17
]
handles the
2
-dimensional case (
r=2,g\geq 1,n\geq 0
) with trivial
determinant.
The non-semisimple case
r=2,g\geq 1,n\geq 0
with arbitrary determinant
is explained in
[
CH19
, Theorem B]
.
These results appear to depend crucially on the assumption
r=2
.

Remark 1.8.2
.

The above-mentioned results in rank
2
led Junho Peter Whang to ask whether, for
g\gg r
, all MCG-finite rank
r
representations of
\Sigma_{g,n}

have finite image
[
LL19
, Question 1.5.3]
.
More generally, motivated by the
p
-curvature conjecture, Mark Kisin asked whether MCG-finite representations
necessarily have finite image,
see
[
BGMW17
, p. 3]
and
[
Sin10
, p. 1]
. (Note that
counterexamples are known in general, see
[
BKMS18
, Theorem
5.1]
for counterexamples coming from TQFT techniques or

[
LL19
, Example 3.3.1]
for counterexamples coming
from the Kodaira-Parshin trick, as well as

§ 10.2
of this paper.)
In this way,
Theorem 1.2.1
answers Whang’s question
affirmatively, and provides a positive answer to Kisin’s question in the regime

g>r^{2}-1
.

There has also been much work put towards finding interesting
examples
of MCG-finite representations, notably
[
Dor01
,
Dia13
,
Gir16b
,
DL15
,
Gir16a
]
.

In a more arithmetic direction,
[
BGS16
]

announced striking results on strong approximation for Markoff triples, obtained by analyzing the
arithmetic properties of subvarieties of the character variety parametrizing
2
-dimensional
representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{1,1})
.
The first step in their analysis is to determine the finite orbits of the
mapping class group action on this character variety
[
BGS16
, last paragraph of p.
132]
.
In this way, our
Theorem 1.2.1
can be viewed as a necessary
first step
toward attempting to generalize their approach to
higher rank, higher genus character
varieties.

1.8.3.
MCG-finite representations and geometric topology

In the world of low-dimensional topology a number of authors have studied the
dynamics of mapping class group actions on character varieties; finite orbits
are the same as semisimple MCG-finite representations. Kasahara

[
Kas15
]
relates fixed points of this action
corresponding to faithful representations to the well-known question of
linearity of the mapping class group. Goldman and many other authors have
studied ergodicity of these actions, see
[
Gol06
]
and the
references therein. Previte-Xia
[
PX00
,
PX02
]
study the relationship between density of the image of
an
SU(2)
-representation and density of its mapping class group orbit. In some
sense our main result is a partial answer to
[
Gol06
, Question
2.7]
, which asks for necessary and sufficient conditions for a
representation to have dense orbit under the mapping class group. We
characterize the most extreme possible failure of density, namely the case of
finite orbit.

There are also a number of related results on representations of the mapping class group.
Farb, Lubotzky, and Minsky
[
FLM01
, Theorem 1.6]
show there are no faithful linear
representations of finite index subgroups of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,0}
of dimension
<2\sqrt{g-1}
. See
10.1.7
for a comparison of our results to theirs.
See also

[
FH13
,
Kor02
,
Fun11
,
KP20a
]

for bounds on the dimension of representations of

\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
, although these results only address representations of the full mapping
class group, as opposed to representations of finite index subgroups.

1.8.4.
The Putman-Wieland conjecture

The other main contribution of

this paper, towards the Putman-Wieland conjecture, has a number of precursors,
notably
[
Loo97
,
GLLM15
,
Loo21
]
.
Our Hodge-theoretic approach is related to an approach suggested by Looijenga
[
Loo15
]
.
The strongest previous result towards the Putman-Wieland conjecture is perhaps
the main result of
[
GLLM15
]
, which says that for certain quotients of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
,
called
\phi

1.9.1.
Step 1. The unitary case.

Every unitary representation is a direct sum of irreducible unitary representations.
Therefore, we suppose

on

Applying

1.9.2.
Step 2. The semisimple case.

Now suppose

of

\mathbb{V}_{0}
to a fiber

1.9.3.
Step 3. The general case

The crucial input for dealing with

extensions split.
For simplicity, let’s suppose for the purpose of this sketch that
\rho
is an extension of two irreducible MCG-finite representations

and

By the previous step,

The splitting of this extension of

corresponds to the vanishing of a certain

1.9.4.
The proof of our cohomological vanishing results

Note that we used
Theorem 1.7.1
, or its immediate consequence,

Theorem 6.2.1
,
in every step above.
Before explaining the idea of the proof of these results, let us recall the
setting. We begin with a scheme
\mathscr{M}
with a dominant étale map

\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
. We denote the associated family of punctured curves by
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
. We are given a local system
\mathbb{V}
on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
.

Theorem 1.7.1
says that if
\mathbb{V}
is unitary,
there are no sub-local systems of
R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{V}
of low rank (that is, rank less
than
2g-2\rk\mathbb{V}
).
Let
C^{\circ}
be a fiber of
\pi^{\circ}
.
We deduce
Theorem 6.2.1
, which says that when
\mathbb{V}|_{C^{\circ}}

is unitary and has low rank (less than
g
), then
R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{V}
has no global sections.
The benefit of
Theorem 6.2.1
is that we only need check
unitarity on fibers, but the cost is that we are only able to rule out global
sections, i.e. trivial sub-local systems, instead of arbitrary low rank
sub-local systems.

First, we deduce
Theorem 6.2.1
from

Theorem 1.7.1
.
The idea is to use the assumption that

\mathbb{V}
is unitary on fibers to
reduce via
2.4.2
to the case that
\mathbb{V}
is a
tensor product
\mathbb{U}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}
, with
\mathbb{U}
unitary.
A nonzero global section of

yields a nonzero map
\mathbb{W}^{\vee}\to R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{U}
, and hence a

, contradicting

Theorem 1.7.1
.

The proof of

Theorem 1.7.1
boils down to an analysis of the derivative of the period map associated to the complex variation of mixed Hodge structure on

, for
\mathbb{V}
unitary.
We identify this derivative with a natural multiplication map

H^{0}(E\otimes\omega_{C}(D))\otimes H^{0}(E^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{C})\to H^{0}(\omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}(D))

in

Theorem 5.1.6
, where

is the smooth projective compactification of

with boundary

, and

is the vector bundle on

associated to

by the Mehta-Seshadri correspondence
[

]
. By the theorem of the fixed part, the existence of a low rank sub-local system of

places restrictions on this map, which we rule out
using vector bundle methods,
as developed in
[
LL22

ultimately relying on Clifford’s theorem for vector bundles.

3.
Deformation-theoretic preliminaries

In this section we introduce some deformation theory of group representations. In
§ 8
, we will use the theory developed here in conjunction with the vanishing results proven in
§ 6
to show that MCG-finite representations have certain rigidity properties.

3.1.
Deformations of representations

We begin by recalling a standard description of deformations of representations in terms of cohomological data.

Recall

1.10.2
:
for
R
a ring and a representation
\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(R)
, we use
\operatorname{ad}(\rho)

to denote the adjoint
G
-representation obtained by composing
\rho
with

\operatorname{GL}_{r}(R)\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{pgl}_{r}(R)).

Fix a group
G
and a representation
\rho_{0}:G\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
\operatorname{Art}
be the category of local Artin
\mathbb{C}
-algebras with residue field
\mathbb{C}
. If
A\in\operatorname{Art}
, with
maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{A}
, we say that a representation
\rho_{A}:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(A)
is a deformation of
\rho_{0}
if
\rho_{A}=\rho_{0}\bmod\mathfrak{m}_{A}
. The
constant
deformation is the deformation obtained via the composition

G\overset{\rho_{0}}{\to}GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})\to GL_{r}(A).

We say a representation
\rho:G\to GL_{r}(A)
is
conjugate to a constant representation
if there exists a matrix
M\in GL_{r}(A)
such that
M\rho M^{-1}
factors through
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
.
Say a deformation
\rho_{A}
of
\rho_{0}
has
constant determinant
if
\det\rho_{A}=\det\rho_{0}
, regarded as a map
G\to A^{\times}
via the natural inclusion
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\hookrightarrow A^{\times}
.

We now define deformations of representations over square-zero extensions in

\operatorname{Art}
.
Given a square-zero extension

0\to I\to B\to A\to 0

with
A,B\in\operatorname{Art}
, and a representation
\rho_{A}:G\to GL_{r}(A)
, a deformation of
\rho_{A}
is a representation
\rho_{B}:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(B)
with
\rho_{B}=\rho_{A}\bmod I
. Two such deformations
\rho_{B},\rho_{B}^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if there exists a matrix
M\in\operatorname{GL}_{r}(B)
with
\rho_{B}=M\rho_{B}^{\prime}M^{-1}
, such that
M=\operatorname{id}\bmod I
.

Lemma 3.1.1
.

Fix a group
G
and a representation
\rho_{0}:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
. Consider

0\to\epsilon A\to A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}\to A\to 0

with
A\in\operatorname{Art}
, and let
\rho_{A}:G\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(A)
be a deformation of
\rho_{0}
over
A
with constant determinant.
The set of equivalence classes of deformations of
\rho_{A}
to an
A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}
-representation with constant determinant is naturally in bijection with
H^{1}(G,\operatorname{ad}(\rho_{A}))
.
Moreover, this description is functorial in
G
.

Proof.

See
[
Maz97
, Chapter V, §21, Proposition 1]
.
∎

Lemma 3.1.2
.

Let

1\to N\to G\to Q\to 1

be a short exact sequence of groups (i.e.
N
is normal in
G
and
Q=G/N
). Let
A\in\operatorname{Art}
and

\rho:G\to GL_{r}(A)

be a representation, and suppose that
H^{0}(Q,H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\rho)|_{N}))=0.
If
\rho_{\epsilon}
is any deformation of
\rho
to
A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}
with constant determinant, then
\rho_{\epsilon}|_{N}
is equivalent to
\rho\otimes_{A}A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}|_{N}
.

Proof.

By
3.1.1
, the set of equivalence classes of deformations of
\rho
to
A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}
is naturally in bijection with
H^{1}(G,\operatorname{ad}(\rho))
, and the set of equivalence classes of deformations of
\rho|_{N}
is naturally in bijection with
H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\rho)|_{N})
. By functoriality, the restriction map is given by the natural map

H^{1}(G,\operatorname{ad}(\rho))\to H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\rho)|_{N}),

which, by the
5
-term exact sequence from the Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence, factors through
H^{0}(Q,H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\rho)|_{N}))=0.
Hence
\rho_{\epsilon}|_{N}
is equivalent to
\rho\otimes_{A}A[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}|_{N}
as desired.
∎

3.2.
A criterion for constancy

The next proposition gives a criterion for a deformation of a group representation to induce the trivial deformation on a normal subgroup.
This will be used in the proof of the semisimple version of our main theorem,

Theorem 8.5.3
.

We define

B_{n}=\mathbb{C}[t]/t^{n+1},

R_{n}=B_{n-1}[\epsilon]/\epsilon^{2}=\mathbb{C}[t,\epsilon]/(t^{n},\epsilon^{2}).

Let
d_{n}:B_{n}\to R_{n}
be the map given by

d_{n}:f(t)\mapsto f(t)+\epsilon f^{\prime}(t),

where
f^{\prime}(t)
is the derivative of
f(t)
. Note that
d_{n}
is injective.

Below we say that an element of
GL_{r}(B_{n})
is constant if it lies in
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})\subset GL_{r}(B_{n})
. We say that an element of
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
is constant mod
t^{n+1}
if its image in
GL_{r}(B_{n})
is constant. Likewise, a representation
\rho
into
GL_{r}(B_{n})
is constant if it factors through
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
; it is conjugate to a constant representation if there exists
M\in GL_{r}(B_{n})
such that
M\rho M^{-1}
is constant. Similarly, a representation into
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
is constant mod
t^{n+1}
if its image in
GL_{r}(B_{n})
is constant.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

Let

1\to N\to G\to Q\to 1

be a short exact sequence of groups. Let

\rho_{0}:G\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})

be a representation, and suppose that for all
n
and all deformations
\gamma:G\to GL_{r}(B_{n})
of
\rho_{0}
with
\gamma|_{N}
constant, we have
H^{0}(Q,H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\gamma)|_{N}))=0.
If

\rho_{\infty}:G\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])

is any representation with
\rho_{\infty}=\rho_{0}\bmod t
and constant determinant, then
\rho_{\infty}|_{N}
is conjugate to a constant representation. That is, there exists
M_{\infty}\in GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
such that
M_{\infty}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}M_{\infty}^{-1}
factors through
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

We wish to construct
M_{\infty}\in GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
such that
M_{\infty}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}M_{\infty}^{-1}
is constant, i.e. factors through
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
. We do so by successive approximation. For all
m\geq 0
, set
\rho_{m}:G\to GL_{r}(B_{m})
to be
\rho_{\infty}\bmod t^{m+1}
.

Set
S_{1}=\operatorname{id}
. Suppose we have found
S_{n}\in GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
such
that
S_{n}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}S_{n}^{-1}
is constant modulo
t^{n}
. We claim it
suffices to construct an element
M_{n}\in GL_{r}(B_{n}),M_{n}=\operatorname{id}\bmod t^{n}
, such
that
M_{n}S_{n}\rho_{n}|_{N}S_{n}^{-1}M_{n}^{-1}
is constant. Indeed, let

M_{1}=\id
and for
n\geq 1
, let

\widetilde{M_{n}}
be an arbitrary lift of
M_{n}
to
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
.
Then, the representation
\widetilde{M_{n}}S_{n}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}S_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{M_{n}}^{-1}
is
constant mod
t^{n+1}
. Set
S_{n+1}=\widetilde{M_{n}}S_{n}
, so that
S_{n+1}=\widetilde{M_{n}}\cdot\widetilde{M_{n-1}}\cdots\widetilde{M_{1}}
by induction.
Then, setting

M_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{M_{n}}\cdot\widetilde{M_{n-1}}\cdots\widetilde{M_{1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n},

we claim that the limit converges as

\widetilde{M_{n}}\to\operatorname{id}
, and
M_{\infty}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}M_{\infty}^{-1}
is
constant. This claim holds because
M_{\infty}=S_{n}\bmod t^{n}
for all
n
, so we
have

M_{\infty}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}M_{\infty}^{-1}\bmod t^{n}=S_{n}\rho_{\infty}|_{N}S_{n}^{-1}\bmod t^{n},

which is constant for all
n
by construction.

We now construct the desired matrix
M_{n}\in GL_{r}(B_{n})
. Replacing
\rho_{\infty}
by
S_{n}\rho_{\infty}S_{n}^{-1}
, we may assume
\rho_{\infty}|_{N}
is constant modulo
t^{n}
, i.e.
\rho_{n-1}|_{N}
is constant. We wish to find
M_{n}\in GL_{r}(B_{n})
such that

(1)

M_{n}=\operatorname{id}\bmod t^{n}
, and

(2)

M_{n}\rho_{n}|_{N}M_{n}^{-1}
is constant.

Given
g\in N
, we let
\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{r\times r}(B_{n-1})
be the matrix obtained by differentiating the entries of
\rho_{n}(g)
. The representation
d_{n}\circ\rho_{n}|_{N}
is given by

g\mapsto\rho_{0}(g)+\epsilon\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime},

as
\rho_{n}|_{N}
is constant mod
t^{n}
.

Note that
d_{n}\circ\rho_{n}|_{N}:N\to GL_{r}(R_{n})
is constant mod
\epsilon
(as it is equal to
\rho_{n-1}|_{N}
mod
\epsilon
). By hypothesis,

H^{0}(Q,H^{1}(N,\operatorname{ad}(\rho_{n-1})|_{N}))=0,

and so by
3.1.2
,
d_{n}\circ\rho_{n}|_{N}
is conjugate to the constant representation

\rho_{n-1}|_{N}\otimes_{B_{n-1}}R_{n}=\rho_{0}|_{N}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}R_{n}

by some matrix

\operatorname{id}+\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}t^{i}\in GL_{r}(R_{n}),

where
the
C_{i}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{r\times r}(\mathbb{C})
. The idea of the remainder of the proof is to view the matrix above as a vector field which we may flow along to make
\rho_{n}|_{N}
constant; we find the desired conjugating matrix
M_{n}
by “integrating” this vector field.

We compute that for
g\in N
,

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}R_{n})(g)

\displaystyle=(\rho_{n-1}\otimes_{B_{n-1}}R_{n})(g)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{id}+\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}t^{i})(d_{n}\circ\rho_{n}(g))(\operatorname{id}-\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}t^{i})

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{id}+\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}t^{i})(\rho_{0}(g)+\epsilon\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime})(\operatorname{id}-\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}t^{i})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(g)+\epsilon\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime}+\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}[C_{i},\rho_{0}(g)]t^{i}.

As
\rho(g)
is constant mod
t^{n}
,
\rho(g)^{\prime}
is
0
mod
t^{n-1}
. Hence equating coefficients, we find

[C_{i},\rho_{0}(g)]=0\text{ for $i<n-1$}

and

\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime}=[\rho_{0}(g),C_{n-1}]t^{n-1}.

Now set

M_{n}=\operatorname{id}+\frac{C_{n-1}}{n}t^{n}\in GL_{r}(B_{n}).

We claim
M_{n}\rho_{n}|_{N}M_{n}^{-1}
is constant. Indeed,

\displaystyle M_{n}\rho_{n}(g)M_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{id}+\frac{C_{n-1}}{n}t^{n})\rho_{n}(g)(\operatorname{id}-\frac{C_{n-1}}{n}t^{n})

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}(g)+\frac{t}{n}[t^{n-1}C_{n-1},\rho_{n}(g)]

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}(g)+\frac{t}{n}[t^{n-1}C_{n-1},\rho_{0}(g)]

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}(g)-\frac{t}{n}\rho_{n}(g)^{\prime}.

But the only non-vanishing coefficient of
\rho(g)^{\prime}
is the coefficient of
t^{n-1}
, so the above expression is constant as desired.
∎

First, Griffiths transversality holds vacuously, as the Hodge filtration only has two steps.

4.2.
Variations on the theorem of the fixed part

Our main goal here is to prove

4.2.2
, which is a variant of the theorem of the fixed part.

Theorem 4.2.1

(Theorem of the fixed part,
[
SZ85
, 4.19]
,
[
PS08
, 14.52]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a smooth quasiprojective complex variety, and let
\mathbb{W}
be an admissible graded-polarizable real variation of mixed Hodge structure on
\mathscr{X}
. Then there is a natural real Hodge structure on
H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathbb{W})
such that the natural inclusion

H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\mathbb{W})\otimes\underline{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{W}

is a morphism of real variations of mixed Hodge structure.

For our main results, will need a variant of the theorem of the fixed part for irreducible representations appearing
the cohomology of a unitary local system on a family of curves.
Given a complex local system
\mathbb{L}
on a variety, we define the local system

\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}:=\begin{cases}\mathbb{L}&\text{ if $\mathbb{L}$ admits a real structure}\\
\mathbb{L}\oplus\overline{\mathbb{L}}&\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Note that
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
admits a natural real structure. Note that if
\mathbb{L}
is an irreducible local system underlying a complex variation of Hodge structure (necessarily unique up to reindexing), and if
\mathbb{L}
admits a (necessarily unique) real structure, then
\mathbb{L}
in fact underlies a real variation of Hodge structure. Indeed, the complex variation carried by
\mathbb{L}
must be preserved (up to reindexing) by complex conjugation, as otherwise
\mathbb{L}
would carry two distinct complex variations.

Proposition 4.2.2
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a smooth quasiprojective complex variety, and let
\mathbb{W}
be an admissible graded-polarizable real variation of mixed Hodge structure on
\mathscr{X}
. Let
\mathbb{L}
be an irreducible complex local system on
\mathscr{X}
such that
\Hom(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{W}_{\mathbb{C}})\not=0.

Then,
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
underlies a polarizable real variation of Hodge structure, and there exists a nonzero
constant
real mixed Hodge structure
Q
and a nonzero map of variations of mixed Hodge structure

Q\otimes\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{W}.

Proof.

First, let
i
be an integer for which there exists a nonzero map
\mathbb{L}\to\operatorname{gr}^{i}_{W}\mathbb{W}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then

\operatorname{gr}^{i}_{W}\mathbb{W}_{\mathbb{C}}=\oplus_{j}\mathbb{V}_{j}\otimes W_{j},

where the

\mathbb{V}_{j}
are distinct irreducible local systems carrying
polarizable complex variations of Hodge structure, and the
W_{j}
are
constant complex variations
[
LL22
, Proposition 4.1.4(2)]
. By assumption
\mathbb{L}

occurs among the
\mathbb{V}_{j}
, and hence carries a complex PVHS.
Hence

\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}
carries a real PVHS.

Let
Q:=\Hom(\widetilde{\mathbb{L}},\mathbb{W})=H^{0}(\mathscr{X},\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\vee}\otimes\mathbb{W})
. The real variation

\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\vee}\otimes\mathbb{W}
is a tensor
product of a pure (hence admissible) variation with an
admissible variation, hence is admissible itself. By the theorem
of the fixed part,
Theorem 4.2.1
,
Q
carries a canonical real mixed Hodge structure such that the evaluation map

Q\otimes\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{W}

is a map of variations, as desired. The map is nonzero by construction.
∎

4.3.
Consequences of non-abelian Hodge theory

The main result from non-abelian Hodge theory that we will use is the following result of Mochizuki, generalizing earlier work of Simpson in the projective case:

Theorem 4.3.1

(
[
Moc06
, Theorem 10.5]
)
.

Let
\overline{X}
be a smooth projective variety and
D\subset\overline{X}
a strict normal crossings divisor. Let

X=\overline{X}\setminus D
.
Any representation

\rho:\pi_{1}(X)\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

with finite determinant admits a
deformation with constant determinant to a representation underlying a complex PVHS.

Proof.

Aside from the statement about determinants, this is precisely
[
Moc06
, Theorem 10.5]
; the statement about determinants follows by examining the proof of that theorem.
∎

We also use the related result that cohomologically rigid representations underlie complex PVHS.

Lemma 4.3.2
.

Let
X,D,\rho
be as in
Theorem 4.3.1
. Suppose in
addition that
\rho
is semisimple with finite determinant and
H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)=0
. Then
\rho
underlies a complex PVHS.

Proof.

Let
G
denote the Zariski-closure of the image of
\rho
.
Note
G
is reductive by semisimplicity of
\rho
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
the Lie algebra of
G
, viewed as a
\pi_{1}(X)
-representation. Note
that
\mathfrak{g}
is a subalgebra of
\operatorname{ad}\rho
as
\rho
has
finite determinant, and is moreover a summand of
\operatorname{ad}\rho
as

\operatorname{ad}\rho
is semisimple. Hence
\rho
is cohomologically rigid as a

G
-representation, as
H^{1}(X,\mathfrak{g})\subset H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)=0
.

We may apply
[
Moc06
, Lemma 10.13]
,
which yields that

\rho
admits a deformation as a
G(\mathbb{C})
-representation to

\rho_{0}:\pi_{1}(X)\to G(\mathbb{C})
, which
underlies a PVHS.
But

\rho
is rigid and hence admits no
non-trivial deformations. This implies
\rho=\rho_{0}
, so
\rho
underlies a PVHS.
∎

5.
The period map associated to a unitary representation

In this section, we give an explicit description of the derivative of the period map
on the cohomology of a unitary vector bundle over a punctured curve.
In
§ 5.1
,
we set up notation to describe the derivative of the period map,
and state our description in

In

,
we study a natural bilinear pairing on vector bundles,

5.1.
The period map

We now describe the period map associated to the
first cohomology of a unitary local system over a punctured curve.
We aim to state
Theorem 5.1.6
, which is a
generalization of the classical statement that, for
C
a curve, the multiplication map

H^{0}(C,\omega)\otimes H^{0}(C,\omega)\to H^{0}(C,\omega^{\otimes 2})

can be
identified via Serre duality with the derivative of the period map associated to the Hodge structure on

[
Voi07
, Lemma 10.22]

Notation 5.1.1
.

Suppose
\pi:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{B}
is a relative smooth proper curve over a smooth
contractible complex-analytic base
\mathscr{B}
. Suppose
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}:\mathscr{B}\to\mathscr{C}
are sections to
\pi
with disjoint
images
\mathscr{D}_{1},\cdots,\mathscr{D}_{n}
, and let
\mathscr{D}
be
the union of the
\mathscr{D}_{i}

\mathscr{C}^{\circ}=\mathscr{C}\setminus\mathscr{D}
,

\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{B}
be the composition
\pi^{\circ}=\pi\circ j
.
Let
\mathbb{V}
be a unitary

-local system on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
and
let

\mathbb{W}:=R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{V}

\mathscr{B}

Let

denote the Deligne canonical extension

[

]

5.1.2.
Constructing the period map

We next construct the relevant period map in (
5.2
).
Set
\mathscr{H}:=\mathbb{W}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{B}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{1}\pi_{*}(\mathscr{E}\overset{\nabla}{\to}\mathscr{E}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}/\mathscr{B}}(\log\mathscr{D}))

right hand side being

[
Del70

As the vector bundle

arises from a local system, it carries a natural Gauss-Manin connection

Because we are assuming

[

\displaystyle F^{1}\mathscr{H}

\displaystyle:=\pi_{*}(\mathscr{E}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\mathscr{C}/\mathscr{B}}(\log\mathscr{D}))

\displaystyle\simeq R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathscr{E},

Since

We therefore obtain a period map

\displaystyle P:\mathscr{B}\to\operatorname{Gr}(\rk F^{1}\mathscr{H},\rk\mathscr{H}),

for

the Grassmannian of

Remark 5.1.3
.

In the above

We will also often identify

The derivative of the period map

Having set up notation for the period map, we now describe its derivative.

-linear map

5.2.
A bilinear pairing

Let
C
be a smooth proper connected curve of genus
g

D\subset C

.
In this section, we study the natural perfect pairing

(5.5)

\displaystyle B_{E}:(E\otimes\omega_{C}(D))\times(E^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{C})\to\omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}(D)

where
\operatorname{tr}
denotes the trace pairing
{E}\otimes{E}^{\vee}\to\mathscr{O}_{C}.

Our motivation for considering this pairing is the close relationship between
(
5.5
) and

computes the derivative of the period map.

The main result of this section is

which gives a sufficient criterion for the rank of

to be large in terms of
this bilinear pairing. It is this lower bound on the

that ultimately leads to the bound of

in our main result,

Theorem 1.2.1
.
In
§ 6
and
§ 7
we use

to analyze mapping class group representations appearing in the
cohomology of unitary local systems.

We next review background on parabolic bundles, in order to state

5.2.3
.
For a more detailed and leisurely introduction to parabolic bundles, we suggest
the reader consult

[
LL22
, §2]

and references therein.

5.2.1.
A lightning review of parabolic bundles

Fix a smooth proper curve
C
and let
D=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
be a reduced divisor on

C
.
Recall that a
parabolic bundle
on
(C,D)
is a vector bundle
E
on
C
, a
decreasing filtration
E_{x_{j}}=E_{j}^{1}\supsetneq E_{j}^{2}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq E_{j}^{n_{j}+1}=0
for each
1\leq j\leq n
, and an increasing sequence of real numbers
0\leq\alpha_{j}^{1}<\alpha_{j}^{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{j}^{n_{j}}<1
for each
1\leq j\leq n
.
referred to as
weights
.
We use
E_{\star}=(E,\{E^{i}_{j}\},\{\alpha^{i}_{j}\})
to denote the data of a parabolic bundle.

Given a parabolic bundle

E_{\star}=(E,\{E^{i}_{j}\},\{\alpha^{i}_{j}\})
,
let
J\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
denote the set of
integers
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
for which
\alpha^{1}_{j}=0
, and define

\displaystyle\widehat{E}_{0}:=\ker(E\to\oplus_{j\in J}E_{x_{j}}/E_{j}^{2}).

(This is a special case of more general notation used for coparabolic bundles
as in
[
LL22
, 2.2.8]
or the equivalent

[
BY96
, Definition 2.3]
, but is all we will need for this
paper.)
In particular,
\widehat{E}_{0}\subset E
is a subbundle.

Parabolic
bundles admit a notion of
parabolic stability
, analogous to the usual
notion of stability for vector bundles, which we next recall.
First, the
parabolic degree
of a parabolic bundle
E_{\star}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{par-deg}(E_{\star}):=\deg(E)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\alpha^{i}_{j}\dim(E_{j}^{i}/E_{j}^{i+1}).

Then, the
parabolic slope
is defined by
\mu_{\star}(E_{\star}):=\operatorname{par-deg}(E_{\star})/\rk(E_{\star})
.
Any subbundle
F\subset E
has an induced parabolic structure
F_{\star}\subset E_{\star}
defined as
follows:
We take the filtration
over
x_{j}
on
F
is obtained
from the filtration

F_{x_{j}}=E^{1}_{j}\cap F_{x_{j}}\supset E^{2}_{j}\cap F_{x_{j}}\supset\cdots\supset E^{n_{j}+1}_{j}\cap F_{x_{j}}=0

by removing redundancies. For the weight associated to
F^{i}_{j}\subset F_{x_{j}}
one takes

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,1\leq k\leq n_{j}\end{subarray}}\{\alpha^{k}_{j}:F^{i}_{j}=E^{k}_{j}\cap F_{x_{j}}\}.

A parabolic bundle
E_{\star}
is
parabolically semi-stable
if for every
nonzero subbundle
F\subset E
with
induced parabolic structure
F_{\star}
, we have
\mu_{\star}(F_{\star})\leq\mu_{\star}(E_{\star})
.

Mehta and Seshadri

[
MS80
, Theorem 4.1, Remark 4.3]
,
give a correspondence between

parabolically stable parabolic bundles of parabolic degree zero
on

(C,D)
and irreducible unitary local systems on
C\setminus D
.
This bijection sends a local system
\mathbb{V}
to the Deligne canonical extension of
(\mathbb{V}\otimes\mathscr{O},\operatorname{id}\otimes d)
with the parabolic structure induced by the connection (as in
[
LL22
, Definition 3.3.1]
).

Remark 5.2.2
.

It is not entirely trivial to extract from
[
MS80
]
that the parabolic vector bundle they construct from a unitary representation is in fact the parabolic vector bundle associated to the Deligne canonical extension of the associated flat vector bundle. What we will use is the fact that the parabolic vector bundle associated to the Deligne canonical extension is parabolically semistable, which follows from
[
Sim90
, Theorem 5]
, for example. A generalization of this fact about unitary local systems (for complex PVHS) is nicely explained in
[
AHL19
, §5]
.

The main technical result we will use regarding
B_{E}
is:

Proposition 5.2.3
.

Let
E_{\star}
be a semistable parabolic bundle on
(C,D)
of parabolic
degree zero, with underlying vector bundle
E
. Let
v\in H^{0}(C,E\otimes\omega_{C}(D))
be a nonzero global section, and suppose the map

\displaystyle H^{0}(C,B_{E}(v,-)):H^{0}(C,E^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{C})

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{0}(C,\omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}(D))

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto B_{E}(v,u)

has rank
r
. Then
\operatorname{rk}(E)\geq g-r.

Before proceeding with the proof, we
recall
[
LL22
, Proposition 6.3.6]
, which the
reader may take as a black box.
For a less technical variant of this statement, see

[
LL22
, Proposition 6.3.1]
.

Proposition 5.2.4

(
[
LL22
, Proposition 6.3.6]
)
.

Suppose
C
is a smooth proper connected genus
g
curve and

E_{\star}=(E,\{E^{i}_{j}\},\{\alpha^{i}_{j}\})
is a nonzero parabolic bundle
C

with respect to
D=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
.
Suppose
E_{\star}
is parabolically semistable.
Let
U\subset\widehat{E}_{0}
be a (non-parabolic) subbundle with

c:=\rk E-\rk U
and

\delta:=h^{0}(C,\widehat{E}_{0})-h^{0}(C,U)
.

(I)

If
\mu_{\star}({E}_{\star})>2g-2+n
,
then
\rk E>gc-\delta
.

(II)

If
\mu_{\star}({E}_{\star})=2g-2+n
, then
\rk E\geq gc-\delta
.

Remark 5.2.5
.

The statement of
5.2.4
is equivalent to
[
LL22
, Proposition
6.3.6]
, but differs slightly in that we write
“
E_{\star}
is parabolically stable” in place of “
\widehat{E}_{\star}
is
coparabolically stable” and
\mu_{\star}(E_{\star})
in place of

\mu_{\star}(\widehat{E}_{\star})
.
However, by definition

is parabolically stable if and only if
\widehat{E}_{\star}
is
coparabolically stable and
\mu_{\star}(E_{\star})=\mu_{\star}(\widehat{E}_{\star})

[

.

Proof of
5.2.3
.

The idea is to apply

5.2.4
(II) with
c=1
and

\delta=r
, and we now set up notation to do so.
Set
n=\deg D
. Let
f_{v}:E^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{C}\to\omega_{C}^{\otimes 2}(D)
be the map of vector bundles induced by

, and set
U=\ker(f_{v})
.
As

is a perfect pairing and

has corank one in

.

We conclude by applying

(II)
to the parabolic bundle

.
This is parabolically stable by definition and
has slope
\mu((E_{\star})^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{C}(D))=2g-2+n
,
since

has parabolic slope

.

6.
The main cohomological results

In

unitary systems on families of curves contain no low rank local systems.
From this we derive a related vanishing result in

Throughout this section, we continue to use

6.1.
A rank bound

We now prove our main result on the cohomology of unitary local systems on versal families of curves,

6.2.
A vanishing result

We apply

Theorem 6.2.1
.

With notation as in
1.10.1
,
let
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
be a punctured versal

g

be a point and set

-algebra with residue field

, and let
\mathbb{V}
be a locally constant sheaf of free

\mathscr{C}^{\circ}

such that

(1)

\mathbb{V}|_{C^{\circ}}
is a constant deformation of a unitary local system, i.e. there exists a unitary complex local system

on

and an isomorphism

, and

.

Then

.

Proof.

Given a dominant map
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}\to\mathscr{M}
,
we have an injection

H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{V})\to H^{0}(\mathscr{M}^{\prime},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{V}|_{\mathscr{M}^{\prime}}).

Hence, it is enough to prove the claim after replacing
\mathscr{M}
by
some
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}
with a dominant map to
\mathscr{M}
.
Combining this with
2.4.2
, we may assume
\mathbb{V}\simeq\oplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{U}_{i}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}_{i}
, where
\mathbb{W}_{i}
are locally constant sheaves of free
A
-modules on
\mathscr{M}
and

\mathbb{U}_{i}
are unitary complex local systems on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
.
It is enough to show that

H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}(\mathbb{U}_{i}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}_{i}))=H^{0}(\mathscr{M},(R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{U}_{i})\otimes\mathbb{W}_{i})=0

.
Letting
\mathfrak{m}_{A}
be the maximal ideal of

, the

.

.

is a unitary complex local system on

\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
and
\mathbb{W}
is an arbitrary complex local system on
\mathscr{M}

with

\displaystyle H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}(\mathbb{U}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}))=H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{U}\otimes\mathbb{W})\neq 0,

7.1.

We next discuss some applications of our methods to the Putman-Wieland

for

g\gg 0

Notation 7.1.1
.

Let

Fix a basepoint

\operatorname{PMod}_{g,b,p+1}

.
Let

be a finite index normal subgroup
corresponding to a finite Galois covering space
\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{g,b,p}
,
or equivalently a surjection

\phi:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,b,p},v_{0})\twoheadrightarrow H
, with
H=\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,b,p},v_{0})/K
a finite group.
Let
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{PMod}_{g,b,p+1}
denote the finite index subgroup preserving

\phi
up to conjugacy.
Viewing

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})
as an
H
-representation,
if
\rho
is an irreducible
H
-representation we let

\displaystyle H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}:=\rho\otimes\Hom_{H}(\rho,H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C}))

denote the
\rho
-isotypic component.
We obtain an action of
\Gamma
on
K^{\text{ab}}\otimes\mathbb{C}=H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})

and hence on the characteristic subrepresentation

H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}
.
By filling in the punctures and deleted discs, we also obtain
an action of
\Gamma
on
V_{K}:=H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})
, referred to in

[
PW13
, p. 80-81]
as a
higher Prym
representation
.

Definition 7.1.2
.

Fix a finite group
H
and nonnegative integers
g,b,
and
p
.
Let
\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
be the statement that for any
surjection
\phi:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,b,p},v_{0})\twoheadrightarrow H
, taking
K:=\ker\phi
and
v\in V_{K}
any nonzero vector,

v
has infinite orbit under
\Gamma
.

Conjecture 7.1.3

(Putman-Wieland,
[
PW13
, Conjecture
1.2]
)
.

\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
holds for every group
H
with
g\geq 2,b\geq 0,p\geq 0
.

Remark 7.1.4
.

We note that

[
PW13
, Conjecture 1.2]

is stated without a group
H
and with
\mathbb{Q}
coefficients instead of

\mathbb{C}
coefficients.
We use this equivalent statement to more easily state our results,
which imply that

\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
holds whenever

is small compared to the genus

.

Remark 7.1.5
.

There is a counterexample to the Putman-Wieland conjecture when

[
Mar
, Theorem 1.3]
.

7.2.
Our results

We are able to verify
\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
in many new cases.
Moreover, we prove the following more general statement, ruling out
not only invariant vectors in
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})
, but even ruling out low-dimensional invariant subspaces
of isotypic components.
The proof is given below in
§ 7.3
.

Theorem 7.2.1
.

With notation as in
7.1.1
, let
\rho
be an irreducible
complex
H
-representation and
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be a finite-index subgroup. Then

H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}

has no nonzero
\Gamma^{\prime}
-invariant subrepresentations
of dimension strictly less than
2g-2\dim\rho
.
The same holds for

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}
in place of

H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}
.

Remark 7.2.2
.

In
Theorem 7.2.1
, it is important that we restrict our attention to subrepresentations, as opposed to arbitrary subquotients, as the
\Gamma
-representation
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})
is not in general semisimple for
b^{\prime}+p^{\prime}>0
.

A perhaps more concrete corollary is the following.

Corollary 7.2.3
.

\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
holds for every group
H

such that every irreducible representation
\rho
of
H
has dimension

\dim\rho<g
.

Proof.

Fix an irreducible representation
\rho
of
H
. By assumption,
\dim\rho<g
. Then

2g-2\dim\rho>1
, and so by

Theorem 7.2.1
,

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}
has no nonzero
\Gamma^{\prime}
-invariant
1
-dimensional subrepresentations for any
\rho
and any finite index
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
.
In particular,
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})
has no nonzero invariant
vectors under any finite index
\Gamma^{\prime}
. But if there was a nonzero vector
v\in V_{K}
with finite orbit, its stabilizer would yield a finite index
\Gamma^{\prime}
with a fixed vector.
∎

Even more concretely, we can give the following bound on
\#H
independent of its
representation theory.

Corollary 7.2.4
.

For fixed
g,b,p\geq 0
,

\PW_{g,b,p}^{H}
holds for every group
H
with
\#H<g^{2}
.

Proof.

If
H
is a finite group of order
\#H<g^{2}
, then because

is the sum of the squares of the dimensions of the irreducible representations of

H
by
[
FH91
, (2.19)]
,
no irreducible representations of
H
have
dimension
\geq g
. The result follows
from
7.2.3
.
∎

In order to prove
Theorem 7.2.1
,
we first note that it suffices to consider the case of surfaces without boundary.

Lemma 7.2.5
.

With notation as in
7.1.1
,
for any
g\geq 0,b,p\geq 0
,
and
\rho
an irreducible
H
-representation, let
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be a finite index subgroup. Then there exists a finite index

\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\subset\operatorname{PMod}_{g,0,b+p+1}
and
an isomorphism
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}\to H_{1}(\Sigma_{g,0,b^{\prime}+p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}

inducing a bijection from
\Gamma^{\prime}
-invariant subspaces to

\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
-invariant subspaces.

Proof.

Define the natural inclusion
\iota:\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}}\hookrightarrow\Sigma_{g,0,b^{\prime}+p^{\prime}}
,
shrinking boundary components to punctures.
There is a deformation retraction from
\Sigma_{g,0,b^{\prime}+p^{\prime}}
onto the subspace

\iota(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}})

and so
\iota
induces an isomorphism

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},b^{\prime},p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})\to H_{1}(\Sigma_{g,0,b^{\prime}+p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C}).

The kernel of the induced surjection
\operatorname{PMod}_{g,b,p}\to\operatorname{PMod}_{g,0,b+p}

is generated by
\mathbb{Z}^{b}
acting on
S_{g,b,p}
by rotating the

[

and hence this

Let
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
be the image of

under this natural map
\operatorname{PMod}_{g,b,p+1}\to\operatorname{PMod}_{g,0,b+p+1}

The action of
\Gamma^{\prime}
on

∎

7.3.

We now prove

Theorem 7.2.1
, which
is more or less an immediate consequence of

applied to the irreducible representations of

,
once one sets up the appropriate local systems.
For the proof, we will need the existence of a versal family of

-covers,
for

which we now define.

Definition 7.3.1
.

As in
7.1.1
, specify a surjection

\phi:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,0,n},v_{0})\twoheadrightarrow H
.
A
versal family of
\phi
-covers
is the data of

(1)

a dominant étale morphism
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
, with
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
the associated family of punctured curves,

(2)

a point
c\in\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
,
m=\pi^{\circ}(c)
,

, and an identification
i:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,0,n},v_{0})\simeq\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)
, and

(3)

a finite étale Galois

-cover
f:\mathscr{X}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
inducing a map

\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)\to\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)/\pi_{1}(\mathscr{X}^{\circ},x)\simeq H

agreeing with the surjection
\phi
under the
identification of

.

Proof of
Theorem 7.2.1
.

Using
7.2.5
,
it is enough to prove

Theorem 7.2.1

when

.

By
[
Wew98
, Theorem 4]
, there exists a versal family of

\phi
-covers
\mathscr{X}^{\circ}\xrightarrow{f}\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\xrightarrow{\pi^{\circ}}\mathscr{M}
.
Technically,

[
Wew98
, Theorem 4]
constructs
\mathscr{M}
as a Deligne-Mumford stack,
where points have isotropy groups isomorphic to the center of
H
, but we may replace this stack by
a scheme which has a dominant étale map to this stack.
Further, after replacing
\mathscr{M}
with another scheme that has a dominant étale map
to
\mathscr{M}
, corresponding generically to an étale multisection of
\pi^{\circ}
, we may assume
\pi^{\circ}
admits a section, and hence that the map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
lifts to a map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}
.

As

is a Galois finite étale
H
-cover, for each irreducible
representation
\rho
of
H
, we obtain a local system
\mathbb{U}_{\rho}

on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
, with monodromy representation given by
\rho
,
and with
\mathbb{U}_{\rho}|_{\mathscr{X}^{\circ}}
trivial. After replacing
\mathscr{M}
with a dominant étale cover, we may write

f_{*}\mathbb{C}=\bigoplus_{\rho}\mathbb{U}_{\rho}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}_{\rho},

where

\mathbb{W}_{\rho}:=\pi^{\circ}_{*}\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{U}_{\rho},f_{*}\mathbb{C})
,
by
2.4.2
(where we take
A=\mathbb{C}
).
As

\mathbb{U}_{\rho}
has finite monodromy by

(1), and

has finite monodromy by definition, the same is true for

\mathbb{W}_{\rho}
. Thus after replacing
\mathscr{M}
with a finite étale cover we may assume each

\mathbb{W}_{\rho}
is a trivial local system.

We next verify the first part of

Theorem 7.2.1
, about
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},0,p^{\prime}},\mathbb{Q})^{\rho}
.
Observe that for
m\in\mathscr{M}
, the action of
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M},m)
on

R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}(\mathbb{U}_{\rho}\otimes(\pi^{\circ})^{*}\mathbb{W}_{\rho})_{m}=(R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{U}_{\rho}\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\rho})_{m}

is identified with its action on

H^{1}(\mathscr{X}^{\circ}_{m},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}=H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},0,p^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}
.
Note also that the action of

\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M})
factors through
\Gamma
,
under our given map
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M})\to\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1})=\operatorname{PMod}_{g,n+1}
.
To prove the first part of

Theorem 7.2.1
, about
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},0,p^{\prime}},\mathbb{Q})^{\rho}
, it thus suffices to show that, after replacing

with an arbitrary finite étale cover,
R^{1}(\pi^{\circ}_{*}\mathbb{U}_{\rho})\otimes\mathbb{W}_{\rho}
contains no non-trivial

As

is a trivial local system, this holds by applying

to

We have thus far proven a statement for the cohomology of

conclude we deduce a corresponding statement for the homology of

Using the inclusion

H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}\subset H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}},\mathbb{C})^{\rho}

-invariant subrepresentations

Hence,

∎

8.
Proof of the main theorem on MCG-finite representations

We have finally developed the tools to verify our main result,
Theorem 1.2.1
: that MCG-finite representations of rank
r<\sqrt{g+1}
have finite image.
The proof is fairly involved, so the reader may find it useful to refer to the sketch in
§ 1.9
. Briefly, we
start by reviewing the notion of cohomological rigidity in

§ 8.1

and prove the necessary rigidity results in

§ 8.2
, using our main vanishing result,

Theorem 6.2.1
.
We next deduce the integrality of MCG-finite representations of low rank in

§ 8.3

using a result of
Klevdal-Patrikis
[
KP20b
]
, which builds on work of Esnault-Groechenig
[
EG18
]
and ultimately relies on input from the Langlands program and known cases of the companion conjectures.
We then combine the above ingredients with input from non-abelian Hodge theory to deduce that low rank unitary MCG-finite representations
have finite image in
§ 8.4
.
We generalize this to the semisimple but non-unitary case in

§ 8.5
, again using non-abelian Hodge theory.
Finally, in
§ 8.6
we bootstrap these results to the general, non-semisimple case,
using
Theorem 7.2.1
on the Putman-Wieland conjecture.

8.1.
Background on Cohomological rigidity

We now define the notion of cohomological rigidity, which detects whether a
representation has any first order deformations.

Definition 8.1.1

(Cohomological rigidity)
.

Let
\overline{X}
be a smooth projective variety,
Z\subset\overline{X}
a
strict normal crossings divisor, and
X=\overline{X}\setminus Z
. Let
G
be
a reductive group over
\mathbb{C}
and

\rho:\pi_{1}(X)\to G(\mathbb{C})

a homomorphism such that the monodromy at infinity is quasi-unipotent.
The representation
\rho
is said to be
cohomologically rigid
if

H^{1}(\overline{X},j_{!*}\operatorname{ad}\rho)=0,

where
j:X\hookrightarrow\overline{X}
is the natural inclusion,
j_{!*}
is the intermediate
extension, and
\operatorname{ad}\rho
is defined as in
1.10.2
.

If
\mathbb{V}
is the local system on
X
associated to
\rho
, we call
\mathbb{V}
cohomologically rigid if
\rho
is.

Remark 8.1.2
.

In the case
\rho
is irreducible, see
[
KP20b
, Proposition 4.7, Remark
4.8]
for a moduli-theoretic
interpretation of cohomological rigidity.
This says that
\rho
is a smooth isolated point of the appropriate character variety, parametrizing representations with fixed local monodromy at infinity. Note that if
\overline{X}
is a curve,
j_{!*}=j_{*}.

We will typically prove cohomological rigidity by computing

H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)
, via the next lemma.

Lemma 8.1.3
.

In the setting of
8.1.1
, assume
H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)=0
. Then
\rho
is
cohomologically rigid.

Proof.

This follows from the identification

H^{1}(\overline{X},j_{!*}\operatorname{ad}\rho)\simeq H^{1}(U,a_{*}\operatorname{ad}\rho)
for

U\subset\overline{X}
a certain dense open subscheme containing
X

with
a:X\to U
the inclusion
[
EG18
, Remark
2.4]
(see also
[
KP20b
, Remark
4.8]
).
The Leray spectral sequence gives an injection

H^{1}(U,a_{*}\operatorname{ad}\rho)\hookrightarrow H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)
, implying
the claim.
∎

Definition 8.1.4
.

If
H^{1}(X,\operatorname{ad}\rho)=0
, we say that
\rho
is
strongly cohomologically rigid
.

8.2.
Cohomological rigidity and unitary MCG-finite representations

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
be a unitary MCG-finite
representation of rank
<\sqrt{g+1}
. We will study
\rho
by associating to
\rho
a certain unitary local system on a well-chosen versal family of curves. Crucially, this unitary local system will be cohomologically rigid.
This will follow from our main vanishing result

Theorem 6.2.1
.

Throughout this section, we use notation as in

1.10.1
. In particular,
\pi:\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{M}
is a versal family of
n
-pointed curves of genus
g
,

\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
is the associated family of
punctured curves,
m\in\mathscr{M}
is a basepoint, and
C^{\circ}=\mathscr{C}_{m}^{\circ}
.

Proposition 8.2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a
\operatorname{GL}_{r}
(respectively,
\operatorname{PGL}_{r}
)-local system on

\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
. Suppose that for
m\in\mathscr{M}
,

\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
is (respectively, is the projectivization
of) an irreducible,
unitary local system. Then
\mathbb{V}
is strongly cohomologically rigid. In
particular,
\mathbb{V}
is cohomologically rigid.

Proof.

By
8.1.3
, it is enough to show

\mathbb{V}
is strongly cohomologically rigid.
We let
\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V}
denote the adjoint local system as in

1.10.2
.
We will check

H^{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V})=0
.
Using the Leray spectral sequence associated to the map
\pi^{\circ}
, it suffices to show that

H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V})=H^{1}(\mathscr{M},\pi^{\circ}_{*}\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V})=0.

We have

H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi^{\circ}_{*}\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V})=0

by
Theorem 6.2.1
(taking
A=\mathbb{C}
), as
\rk\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V}=r^{2}-1<g
and
\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
is unitary by assumption.

To conclude, it is enough to show
\pi^{\circ}_{*}\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V}=0
.
This is follows from Schur’s lemma because

\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
is (the projectivization of) an
irreducible local system, and so
\operatorname{ad}\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
has no
\pi_{1}({C}^{\circ})
-invariants.
∎

8.3.
Verifying integrality

The next step of the proof is to use the cohomological rigidity provided by

8.2.1
to deduce integrality of MCG-finite unitary representations.

Definition 8.3.1
.

Let
G
be a group scheme over
\mathbb{Z}
. We say a representation
\pi_{1}(X,x)\to G(\mathbb{C})
is
integral

if it is conjugate to a representation which factors through
G(\mathscr{O}_{K})
for some number field
K
.
A local system on a connected space
X
is integral if the corresponding monodromy representation is.

We will primarily be concerned with integrality with respect to the group
schemes
G=\operatorname{PGL}_{r}
and
G=\operatorname{GL}_{r}
.
The basic idea to show our local system is integral is to apply the main result of

[
KP20b
]
, which builds on
[
EG18
]
and ultimately relies on Lafforgue’s work on the Langlands program for function fields and consequent work on Deligne’s companion conjectures.
To apply this result, we need to know the monodromy of our cohomologically rigid
local system
\mathbb{V}
around boundary components of a strict normal crossings compactification of
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}

is quasi-unipotent, which we will verify using the following result.

Proposition 8.3.2

(
[
AS16
, Proposition 2.4]
)
.

Suppose that
g\geq 3
,
\gamma\subset\Sigma_{g,n}
is a simple closed curve,
and
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
is a
finite index subgroup. Let
\delta_{\gamma}\in\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
be the Dehn twist about
\gamma
. Let
\rho:\Gamma\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
be a representation. Then for
m
such that
\delta_{\gamma}^{m}\in\Gamma
,
\rho(\delta_{\gamma}^{m})
is quasi-unipotent.

We now verify that the projectivization of a unitary MCG-finite representation is integral.

Lemma 8.3.3
.

Let
g\geq 3
be an integer, and let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducible, unitary, MCG-finite representation, with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
. Then the composition

\mathbb{P}\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\overset{\rho}{\to}\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{PGL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

is integral.

Proof.

By
2.2.3
, there exists a scheme
\mathscr{M}
with a finite étale map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
, with associated family of punctured curves
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
, and a representation

\widetilde{\rho}:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ})\to\operatorname{PGL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

agreeing with
\mathbb{P}\rho
on restriction to a fiber of
\pi^{\circ}
. It suffices to show that
\widetilde{\rho}
is integral.
By
8.2.1
,

\widetilde{\rho}
is cohomologically rigid.
Because the abelianization of
\operatorname{PGL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
is
trivial, it follows from

[
KP20b
, Theorem 1.2]
that
\widetilde{\rho}

is integral once we verify that
\widetilde{\rho}
has quasi-unipotent local monodromy around the boundary components of
a good (i.e. strict normal crossing) compactification of
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
.

One may construct a strict normal crossing compactification as follows.

\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
is a finite étale cover of
\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}
by
construction; let
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}}^{\prime}
be a strict normal crossing
compactification of
\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}
obtained by blowing up boundary strata
of the Deligne-Mumford compactification
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}}
(which
is only a normal crossing compactification, and is not in general strict). Let

\overline{\mathscr{C}}
be the normalization of
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}}^{\prime}
in the function field of
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
. By

8.3.2
, it suffices to check that the local
monodromy about the boundary components of
\overline{\mathscr{C}}
correspond
to products of commuting Dehn twists about simple closed curves, under the
identification of
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ})
with a subgroup of

\operatorname{PMod}_{g,n+1}=\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1})
.
This is because a product of
commuting quasi-unipotent matrices is quasi-unipotent. To check the claim about
local monodromy about boundary components, it suffices to check the
corresponding claim for
\overline{\mathscr{M}_{g,n+1}}^{\prime}
. But this is

[
LLSS20
, Lemma 2.1.1]
.
∎

The next result shows we can lift integrality from the projectivization of a unitary irreducible MCG-finite representation to the original representation.

Lemma 8.3.4
.

Let
g\geq 3
be an integer, and let
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
be an irreducible, unitary, MCG-finite representation, with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
. Then
\rho
is integral.

Proof.

By
2.3.1
,
\det\rho
has finite image, and
hence
\rho
factors through
G(\mathbb{C})
, where
G\subset\operatorname{GL}_{r}
is a
flat affine
\mathbb{Z}
-group scheme containing
\operatorname{SL}_{r}
with finite index (for
example, we can take
G=\det^{-1}(\mu_{m})
, where
\det\rho
factors through

\mu_{m}
). Note that the natural morphism
G\to\operatorname{PGL}_{r}
is finite. After
conjugating
\rho
by some matrix we may assume
\mathbb{P}\rho
factors through

\operatorname{PGL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{K})
for some number field
K
, by
8.3.3
.

As
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
is finitely generated, it suffices to show that for a generator
\alpha\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
, there exists some finite extension
K^{\prime}/K
such that the matrix
\rho(\alpha)
lies in
G(\mathscr{O}_{K^{\prime}})
.
The obstruction to lifting any given
\mathscr{O}_{K}
point of
\operatorname{PGL}_{r}
to

G
is a
\ker(G\to\operatorname{PGL}_{r})
torsor, which becomes trivial on some finite
flat extension, and hence over
\mathscr{O}_{K^{\prime}}
for some finite
K^{\prime}/K
.
∎

8.4.
Verifying finiteness

We now show that a unitary MCG-finite representation
\rho
of low rank has finite image. We have already shown that, in the irreducible setting, such representations are defined over
\mathscr{O}_{K}
. We will use the techniques of
[
LL22
]
to deduce that they have finite monodromy. To do so, we will argue that for each embedding
\tau:\mathscr{O}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
\rho\otimes_{\mathscr{O}_{K},\tau}\mathbb{C}
has unitary monodromy. We know this for one such
\tau
but not for the rest.
To prove unitarity for all such
\tau
, we will use
[
LL22
, Theorem
1.2.12]
, and to verify its hypotheses we will need
to use non-abelian Hodge theory to construct certain complex PVHS.

Proposition 8.4.1
.

Let

\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

be a unitary MCG-finite representation with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
. Then
\rho
has finite image.

Proof.

As
\rho
is unitary, it is semisimple. As an irreducible sub-representation of an MCG-finite representation is MCG-finite by
2.1.1
, we may without loss of generality assume
\rho
is irreducible.

If
g<3
,
r\leq 1
, and so we may conclude by
2.3.1
. Thus we may assume
g\geq 3
. By
8.3.4
, we may assume
\rho
factors through
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{K})
for some number field
K
and some embedding
\iota:\mathscr{O}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
We now adjust notation so that
\rho
denotes this
\mathscr{O}_{K}
-representation and
\rho_{\iota}=\rho\otimes_{\mathscr{O}_{K},\iota}\mathbb{C}

denotes our original MCG-finite representation. By
2.3.5
, there exists a punctured versal family
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}

and a representation
\rho^{\prime}_{\iota}:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
with finite determinant, restricting to
\rho_{\iota}
on
the fundamental group of a fiber of
\pi^{\circ}
, i.e. for
C^{\circ}
a fiber of

\pi^{\circ}
,
\rho^{\prime}_{\iota}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ})}=\rho_{\iota}
. Note that
\rho^{\prime}_{\iota}
is irreducible (as
\rho_{\iota}

is irreducible), and strongly cohomologically rigid, by
8.2.1
.

Choose any embedding
\tau:\mathscr{O}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and let

\rho_{\tau}:=\rho\otimes_{\mathscr{O}_{K},\tau}\mathbb{C}
. Note that
\rho_{\tau}
is MCG-finite, as the same is true for
\rho_{\iota}
.
We aim to show
\rho_{\tau}
is unitary.

Choose
\sigma:\mathbb{C}\overset{\sim}{\to}\mathbb{C}
so that
\sigma\circ\iota=\tau
, and set
\rho_{\tau}^{\prime}=\rho_{\iota}^{\prime}\otimes_{\mathbb{C},\sigma}\mathbb{C}
. Note that
\rho_{\tau}^{\prime}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ})}=\rho_{\tau}
.
Now observe that
\rho_{\tau}^{\prime}
is strongly cohomologically rigid, as the same is true for
\rho_{\iota}^{\prime}
, and strong cohomological rigidity (being a cohomological condition) is preserved by automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover
\rho_{\tau}^{\prime}
is irreducible with finite determinant, as the same is true for
\rho_{\iota}^{\prime}
.

We next show
\rho_{\tau}
is unitary.
By
[
LL22
, Theorem 1.2.12]
,
in order to show
\rho_{\tau}
is unitary, it is enough to show
\rho^{\prime}_{\tau}
underlies
a complex PVHS on
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}_{\tau}
is strongly cohomologically rigid and irreducible with finite determinant,
this follows from
4.3.2
.

We are thus in the following situation:
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{K})
is a representation such that for each
\tau:\mathscr{O}_{K}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
\rho\otimes_{\mathscr{O}_{K},\tau}\mathbb{C}
is unitary. Such a representation has finite image by
[
LL22
, Lemma 7.2.1]
.
∎

8.5.
Reduction from the semisimple case to the unitary case

In this section we will prove
Theorem 1.2.1
for semisimple
representations.
The idea is to first deform our representation to a complex PVHS via
non-abelian Hodge theory. Then we will argue that this complex PVHS is in fact unitary, using the results of
[
LL22
]
, so that we
can apply
8.4.1
. Finally we will use the rigidity properties of unitary MCG-finite representations to argue
that our deformation was in fact trivial—the deformed representation was in fact the one we started with.

Lemma 8.5.1
.

Let
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
be a punctured versal family
of curves of genus
g
. Let
c\in\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
be a point, set

m=\pi^{\circ}(c)
, and let
C^{\circ}=\mathscr{C}^{\circ}_{m}
. Let

\rho_{\infty}:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])

be a representation with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
and constant determinant, and let
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\infty}\otimes\mathbb{C}
. Suppose
\rho_{0}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is unitary. Then
\rho_{\infty}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is conjugate to a constant representation.

Proof.

We apply
3.2.1
to the short exact sequence

1\to\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)\to\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)\to\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M},m)\to 1.

We must verify that for any
n
and any deformation
\gamma:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[t]/t^{n+1})
of
\rho_{0}
with
\gamma|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
constant, we have

H^{0}(\mathscr{M},R^{1}\pi_{*}\operatorname{ad}(\gamma))=0.

But this follows from
Theorem 6.2.1
. Indeed,

\rk_{\mathbb{C}[t]/t^{n+1}}\operatorname{ad}(\gamma)=r^{2}-1<g

as
r<\sqrt{g+1}
by assumption.
∎

Lemma 8.5.2
.

Let
\pi^{\circ}:\mathscr{C}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}
be a punctured versal family
of curves of genus
g
. Let
c\in\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
be a point, set

m=\pi^{\circ}(c)
, and let
C^{\circ}=\mathscr{C}^{\circ}_{m}
. Let
X
be a
finite-type connected
\mathbb{C}
-scheme, and let

\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{X}(X))

be a representation
with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
and constant determinant. For a closed point
x\in X
with residue field

\kappa(x)=\mathbb{C}
, let
\rho_{x}:=\rho\otimes_{\mathscr{O}_{X}(X)}\kappa(x)
.
Suppose there exists a closed point
y\in X
such that
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is unitary. Then for each
closed point
x\in X
,
\rho_{x}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is conjugate to
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality
X
is a reduced (not necessarily irreducible) connected curve, as any closed point
x\in X
is connected to
y
by such a curve. We claim it suffices to prove the statement if the parameter space
X
is smooth and irreducible.

To reduce to this case, suppose
X
is neither smooth nor irreducible, and let
\tilde{Y}
be the normalization of the component
Y
of
X
containing
y
, and
\iota:\tilde{Y}\to X
the natural map. By the case of smooth irreducible curves,
\iota^{*}\rho|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}\otimes\kappa(y^{\prime})
is independent of
y^{\prime}\in\tilde{Y}(\mathbb{C})
, and thus
\rho|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}\otimes\kappa(y^{\prime\prime})
is independent of
y^{\prime\prime}\in Y
as well, and in particular unitary for all
y^{\prime\prime}
. Let
Z
be a connected component of the closure of
X-\operatorname{im}(\iota)
. As
X
is connected,
Z
intersects
Y
non-trivially, say at some point
z\in Y
. But as
z
is in
Y
,
\rho_{z}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is unitary, and hence we are done by induction on the number of components of
X
.

So we now assume
X
is a smooth connected curve. Let
\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c),\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C}))
be the representation variety parametrizing
r
-dimensional complex representations of
\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)
. There is a natural
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
action on
\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c),\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C}))
induced by conjugation. Let

f:X\to\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c),\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C}))

be the map sending a point
x
to
\rho_{x}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
. We wish to show that the image of
f
lies in a single
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit, namely that of
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
.

Choose a local parameter
t
of
X
at
y
, so that
\widehat{\mathscr{O}_{X,y}}\simeq\mathbb{C}[[t]]
. Let

\rho_{\infty}:\pi_{1}(\mathscr{C}^{\circ},c)\to GL_{r}(\mathbb{C}[[t]])

be the corresponding representation. By
8.5.1
,
\rho_{\infty}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is conjugate to a constant representation. It follows that there exists a dense open
subset
U\subset X
containing
y
such that
f(U)
is contained in the

\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit of
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
.

As
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
is unitary, hence semisimple, its
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit in
\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c),\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C}))
is closed
[
Sik12
, Theorem 30]
. Hence the closure
\overline{U}
of
U
maps into the
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
-orbit of
\rho_{y}|_{\pi_{1}(C^{\circ},c)}
. But as
X
is irreducible,
\overline{U}=X
, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem 8.5.3
.

Let

\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

be a semisimple MCG-finite representation, and suppose
r<\sqrt{g+1}
. Then
\rho
has finite image.

Proof.

Observe that if
\rho
is a sum of irreducible representations, each of
which have finite monodromy, then
\rho
has finite monodromy as well.
As a summand of an MCG-finite representation is MCG-finite by

2.1.1
, it suffices to treat the case that
\rho
is irreducible.

By
2.3.5
, there exists a dominant
étale map
\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
, and a local system

\mathbb{V}
with finite determinant on the total space
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
of the associated
family of
n
-times punctured curves of genus
g
, such that for

C^{\circ}
a fiber of
\pi^{\circ}
,
\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
has monodromy representation given by
\rho
.
By
Theorem 4.3.1
,
\mathbb{V}
admits a deformation with constant determinant to a
local system
\mathbb{V}_{0}
underlying a complex PVHS.
Since
r<2\sqrt{g+1}
, it follows from
[
LL22
, Theorem
1.2.12]
that
the local system
\mathbb{V}_{0}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
is unitary. Moreover,
\mathbb{V}_{0}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
is MCG-finite by
2.1.3
.

It follows from
8.4.1

that
\mathbb{V}_{0}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
has finite monodromy.
To conclude, it’s enough to show that
\mathbb{V}|_{{C}^{\circ}}=\mathbb{V}_{0}|_{{C}^{\circ}}
.
But this is immediate from
8.5.2
.
∎

8.6.
Completion of the non-semisimple case

We come to the final step, where we verify the non-semisimple case of
Theorem 1.2.1
.
The idea is to show that if we do have a non-semisimple MCG-finite representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
of low rank, we
can produce a certain
finite cover
\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
of
\Sigma_{g,n}
, which violates
our results toward the Putman-Wieland conjecture.

If
\rho
is a
G
-representation and
\rho^{\prime}\subset\rho
is a subrepresentation, we say that
\rho^{\prime}
is
characteristic
if it is stable under all
G
-automorphisms of
\rho
(for example, the socle of
\rho
, or an isotypic component thereof).

Lemma 8.6.1
.

Let
\rho
be an MCG-finite representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
, and let
\rho_{1}\subset\rho
be a semisimple, characteristic subrepresentation of
\rho
. Let
\rho_{2}=\rho/\rho_{1}
, and suppose
\rho_{2}
is semisimple as well. If
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
have finite image and
\rk\rho<\sqrt{g+1}
, then
\rho
has finite image as well, i.e. the extension of
\rho_{2}
by
\rho_{1}
splits.

Proof.

If
g=0
,
\rho
is the zero representation, so without loss of generality we may assume
g
is positive.

Decompose
\rho_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\rho_{1}
as
\rho_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\rho_{1}\simeq\oplus_{i}\sigma_{i}^{\oplus n_{i}}
, with the
\sigma_{i}
irreducible and pairwise non-isomorphic, and the
n_{i}
positive integers.
Since
\dim\rho_{1}+\dim\rho_{2}=\dim\rho<\sqrt{g+1}
,
we obtain from the AM-GM inequality that
\dim\rho_{1}\cdot\dim\rho_{2}<(\sqrt{g+1}/2)^{2}=(g+1)/4
. Therefore, for every
i
,

n_{i}\cdot\dim\sigma_{i}<(g+1)/4

and hence
n_{i},\dim\sigma_{i}<(g+1)/4
.
To put ourselves in the setting of

Theorem 7.2.1
, we choose an additional
basepoint
v\in\Sigma_{g,n}
and let
\Sigma_{g,n+1}:=\Sigma_{g,n}\setminus\{v\}
. Let
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n+1}
be a finite index subgroup stabilizing the conjugacy class of each
\sigma_{i}
.

Let
\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}\to\Sigma_{g,n}
be a finite Galois cover, with Galois group
H
, upon which the local
systems corresponding to both

\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
trivialize (for example, the cover defined by
\ker(\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2})
).
It suffices to show that
\rho|_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})}
is trivial. As

\rho|_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})}
has abelian image, it factors through an

H
-equivariant map

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})\to\rho_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\rho_{1}.

Equivalently, this is the extension class corresponding to

\rho
in

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})}(\rho_{2},\rho_{1})=H^{1}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}),\Hom(\rho_{2},\rho_{1}))=\Hom(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}),\Hom(\rho_{2},\rho_{1})).

Projecting onto the
\sigma_{i}
-isotypic piece of
\rho_{2}^{\vee}\otimes\rho_{1}
yields a
\Gamma
-stable quotient of

H_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})^{\sigma_{i}}
, or equivalently a
\Gamma
-stable
subspace of
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})^{\sigma_{i}}
, of rank at most
(g+1)/4
.
Call this subspace
Q_{i}
.
But by

Theorem 7.2.1
, we have that any nonzero

\Gamma
-stable subspace of
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})^{\sigma_{i}}
has rank at
least
2g-2\dim\sigma_{i}
, which satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle 2g-2\dim\sigma_{i}>2g-(g+1)/2=\frac{3g}{2}-\frac{1}{2}>(g+1)/4>n_{i}\dim\sigma_{i}.

Hence
Q_{i}
equals zero. As this holds for all
i
,
\rho|_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}})}
is trivial as desired.
∎

8.7.

We finally complete the proof of our main theorem, that
MCG-finite representations

\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

with
r<\sqrt{g+1}
have finite image.

Proof of
Theorem 1.2.1
.

The proof is by induction on the length of the socle filtration. The base case is
Theorem 8.5.3
. Now let
\rho_{1}
be the socle of
\rho
. Because the socle of a representation is characteristic both
\rho_{1}

and
\rho/\rho_{1}
are
MCG-finite.
Therefore, both
\rho_{1}
and
\rho/\rho_{1}
have finite image by induction.
We conclude by
8.6.1
.
∎

9.
Consequences for arithmetic representations

The main arithmetic consequence of
Theorem 1.2.1
is
Theorem 9.1.2
below, which verifies a prediction of the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture, as we now explain.
Throughout this section, we will no longer be working over
\mathbb{C}
.

9.1.
Application to relative Fontaine-Mazur

Definition 9.1.1
.

Let
X
be a variety over a finitely-generated field
K
with algebraic closure
\overline{K}
, and

\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{K}})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})

a
continuous representation. We
say that
\rho
is
arithmetic
if its conjugacy class has finite orbit
under the action of
\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
on the set of isomorphism classes of

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{K}})
-representations. Here the action is
induced by the natural outer action of
\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
on

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{K}})
.

The relative form of the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture predicts
that all semisimple arithmetic representations are of geometric origin

[
Pet20
, Conjecture 1 bis]
. Our main arithmetic result, a
straightforward application of
Theorem 1.2.1
, is that this is true for representations of low rank on the
generic

n
-pointed curve of genus
g
.
In fact, we show such representations are not only of geometric origin:
they even have finite image.

Theorem 9.1.2
.

Let
K
be a finitely-generated field of characteristic zero and
(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
a smooth
n
-pointed geometrically connected curve of genus
g

over
K
,
such that the corresponding map
\operatorname{Spec}(K)\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n,\mathbb{Q}}
factors through the generic point.
If
r<\sqrt{g+1}
, any continuous arithmetic representation

\displaystyle\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})

has finite image.

We prove this at the end of
§ 9.1

after spelling out
some consequences.

Remark 9.1.3
.

Let
K,(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be as in the theorem, and choose any
embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Let
C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
be the associated Riemann surface. By
[
LL22
, Lemma 7.5.1]
,

Theorem 9.1.2
implies that arithmetic
representations are not Zariski-dense in the

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
-points of the character variety
parametrizing semisimple representations of

\pi_{1}(C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})
of rank
r
, for

1<r<\sqrt{g+1}
. This answers negatively a question of Esnault and Kerz

[
EK20
, Question 9.1(1)]
.

Recall that a local system
\mathbb{V}
on a smooth complex variety
X
is of geometric origin if there is
some Zariski open
U\subset X
and a smooth proper morphism
f:Y\to U
so that

\mathbb{V}|_{U}
is a subquotient of
R^{i}f_{*}\mathbb{C}
for some
i
.

Corollary 9.1.4
.

Let
K
and
(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be as in

Theorem 9.1.2
,
and let

\rho
be a continuous representation

\displaystyle\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})

with
\dim\rho<\sqrt{g+1}
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\rho
has finite image.

(2)

\rho
is arithmetic.

(3)

For any embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and any isomorphism
\mathbb{C}\simeq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
, the local system corresponding to
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}|_{C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}}
on
C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
is of geometric
origin.

(4)

For any embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and any isomorphism
\mathbb{C}\simeq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}
, the local system corresponding to
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}|_{C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}}
on
C^{\operatorname{an}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
underlies an
integral PVHS.

Moreover,
(1),(3)
, and
(4)
are equivalent whenever
\dim\rho<2\sqrt{g+1}
.

Proof.

The equivalence of
(1)
and
(2)
when
\dim\rho<\sqrt{g+1}
holds by

Theorem 9.1.2
, and
(1)

certainly implies
(3)
and
(4)
for any value of
\dim\rho
.
Next,
(3)
implies
(4)
for any value of
\dim\rho
since any local system of geometric origin
underlies an integral PVHS. This is well known, but see, for example, the proof of

[
LL22
, Corollary 1.2.7]
.

To conclude, it remains to show that
(4)
implies
(1)
when
\dim\rho<2\sqrt{g+1}
.
This follows from
[
LL22
, Corollary 1.2.7]
,
by choosing an embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
corresponding to an analytically very general complex point of
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
.
By
[
LL22
, Corollary 1.2.7]
,
a local system on an analytically very general curve underlying an integral PVHS has finite monodromy whenever its dimension is

<2\sqrt{g+1}
.
Therefore,
\rho
also has finite monodromy.
∎

Remark 9.1.5
.

The relative Fontaine-Mazur conjecture predicts that all semisimple arithmetic local systems
are of geometric origin. Hence on a generic curve, all semisimple arithmetic local systems
\rho
with
\dim\rho<2\sqrt{g+1}
should have finite monodromy by

9.1.4
.
In particular, this suggests one should be able to improve the bound of
\sqrt{g+1}
in

Theorem 9.1.2
to
2\sqrt{g+1}
if one
restricts to semisimple representations.

Note that arithmeticity of
\rho
on
C
over
K
is an a stronger condition than having finite orbit under
the action of the mapping class group after base change to
\mathbb{C}
, and so
this does not mean the bound of
Theorem 8.5.3
can be improved
to
2\sqrt{g+1}
. Indeed, it cannot be improved, even in the semisimple case, when
g=1
, see

10.2.1
.

Proof of
Theorem 9.1.2
.

Let
C^{\circ}_{\overline{K}}:=C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
. For
R
a ring, let
\mathscr{M}_{g,n,R}
denote the moduli stack of genus
g
,
n
-pointed curves over

\operatorname{Spec}R

and let
\pi^{\circ}_{R}:\mathscr{C}_{R}^{\circ}\to\mathscr{M}_{g,n,R}
denote the universal
n
-punctured genus

g
curve over
R
.

9.2.
Application to lifting residual representations

Let
K,(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be as in

Theorem 9.1.2
. We next explain why there are residual
representations of
\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})
which do not lift to arithmetic representations in characteristic zero.
In particular, in
9.2.3

we construct residual representations of geometric
fundamental groups which are not “of geometric origin,” as we now define. To our knowledge these are the first such examples.

Definition 9.2.1
.

Let
K
be an algebraically closed field, and let
X/K
be a variety. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field of characteristic different from that of
K
. Let
L
be the fraction field of the Witt vectors
W(\mathbb{F})
. We say that a continuous representation

\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{K}})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F})

is
of geometric origin
if there exists a continuous representation

\xi:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X_{\overline{K}})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\overline{L})

such that:

(1)

There exists a
\xi
-stable

\mathscr{O}_{\overline{L}}
-lattice
W\subset\overline{L}^{r}
with

W\otimes\overline{\mathbb{F}}\simeq\rho\otimes\overline{\mathbb{F}}
.

(2)

There exists a dense open subscheme
U\subset X
and a smooth proper morphism
\pi:Y\to U
, such that
\xi|_{\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(U_{\overline{K}})}
arises as a subquotient of the monodromy representation of
R^{i}\pi_{*}\overline{L}
for some
i\geq 0.

In other words, we say a residual representation is of geometric origin if it
arises as the reduction of a characteristic zero representation of geometric
origin.

Corollary 9.2.2
.

Let
K,(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be as in
Theorem 9.1.2
. Then for
1<r<\sqrt{g+1}
and
p\gg_{r}0
, no surjective representation

\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

admits an arithmetic lift to characteristic zero. In particular no such representation is of geometric origin.

Proof.

By definition, a mod
p
representation of geometric origin lifts to a
representation of geometric origin over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. As representations of geometric origin are arithmetic, the second statement follows from the first.

Thus it suffices to show that for
p\gg 0
and
\rho
as in the theorem,
\rho
does not admit an arithmetic lift. Suppose to the contrary that it did admit an arithmetic lift
\xi
; by
Theorem 9.1.2
,
\xi
would have finite image. Thus it suffices to show that for
Q
a finite, totally ramified extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, there do not exist any finite subgroups of
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{Q})
surjecting onto
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
if
p\gg_{r}0
.

This follows from Jordan’s theorem on finite subgroups of
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(Q)
,
where
Q
is any field of characteristic zero
(
[
Jor78
, p. 91]
or
[
CR66
, Theorem
36.13]
). Recall that Jordan’s theorem says
that there exists some constant
n(r)
such that if
G\subset\operatorname{GL}_{r}(Q)
is a finite subgroup,
G
contains an abelian normal subgroup of index dividing
n(r)
. In particular, for any
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, we have

[g_{1}^{n(r)},g_{2}^{n(r)}]=I_{r},

where
I_{r}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix.

Now define
\overline{g_{1}},\overline{g_{2}}\in\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
by

\overline{g_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\oplus I_{r-2}

\overline{g_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\oplus I_{r-2}.

Direct computation shows that
[\overline{g_{1}}^{n(r)},\overline{g_{2}}^{n(r)}]\neq I_{r}
as long as
p
does not divide
n(r)
.
But if
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
was the image of a finite subgroup of

\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathscr{O}_{Q})
, this equality would hold by Jordan’s theorem, giving the claim. Indeed, we have shown the result for all
p
not dividing the constant
n(r)
from Jordan’s theorem.
∎

Using
9.2.2
, we now construct
examples of residual representations with no arithmetic lifts.

Example 9.2.3
.

Fix non-negative integers
g,n
so that
n\geq 1
if
g=1
and
n\geq 3
if
g=0
.
Fix
r
with
1<r<\sqrt{g+1}
. We claim
that for any
n
-pointed genus
g
curve
(C,x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, and any
p
,
there exist surjective representations

\rho:\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

as in
9.2.2
.
In particular these representations do not admit arithmetic lifts to
characteristic
0
.

If
n>0
, maintaining the assumption
n\geq 3
when
g=0
, we have
that

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})
is
profinite free on at least
2
generators.
The claim in this case follows from the fact that
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is generated by two elements

[
Wat89
]
.

We now deal with the case
n=0
and
g\geq 2
.
Indeed, this follows from the fact that

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{\overline{K}})
surjects onto a free profinite group on

g
generators by writing it as the profinite completion of
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{1},\ldots,b_{g}\rangle/\prod_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]
and considering the map
to the free profinite group generated by
c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}
sending
a_{i}\mapsto c_{i},b_{i}\mapsto\operatorname{id}
.

We conclude the section with several remarks describing consequences of the
above non-liftable residual representations.

Remark 9.2.4
.

9.2.2
provides a counterexample to a particularly optimistic extension of de Jong’s conjecture
[
dJ01
, Conjecture 2.3, Theorem 3.5]
. De Jong’s conjecture, proven by Gaitsgory
[
Gai07
]
, implies that an absolutely irreducible residual
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-representations of geometric fundamental groups of smooth curves over finite fields of characteristic not dividing
q
always lift to arithmetic representations over a field of characteristic zero. One might naturally ask if the same is true for curves over arbitrary finitely-generated fields;
9.2.2
shows that this is not the case.

Remark 9.2.5
.

The solution to de Jong’s conjecture, as described in

9.2.4
, implies that deformation rings of
absolutely irreducible
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-representations of arithmetic fundamental
groups of curves over finite fields (of characteristic different from
p
) are
always complete intersections over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Our results show that the
analogous statement is not true for the arithmetic fundamental group

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(C_{K}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\})
as in

Theorem 9.1.2
because they are not flat over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by
9.2.3
.

Remark 9.2.6
.

Flach asks
[
CO05
, p. 7]
if deformation rings of
absolutely irreducible residual representations of profinite groups are
always complete intersections. By now it is well-known that the answer
to this question is in general “no” (see e.g.
[
EM16
]

for a more or less complete answer to this question, and the references
therein). Our result
9.2.2
shows that
this question has a negative answer even for arithmetic fundamental groups of
generic smooth curves, as explained in
9.2.5
.

10.
Questions and examples

10.1.
Rigidity questions for mapping class groups

One of the key ingredients of our arguments is the statement that certain representations of (finite index subgroups of)
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
are rigid.

Question 10.1.1
.

Let
g\gg 0
. Is every irreducible complex representation of every finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
rigid?

The answer is, perhaps, plausibly “yes” if one accepts the well-known analogy
between
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
and lattices in simple Lie groups of rank
>1
, as
all complex representations of such lattices are rigid.
For
g
small, there
are examples of irreducible non-rigid representations of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
(for
example,
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{M}_{0,4})
is free on two generators).

Remark 10.1.2
.

A positive answer to
10.1.1
would imply Ivanov’s
conjecture that
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
does not virtually surject onto
\mathbb{Z}

as stated in
[
Iva06
, §7]
and
[
Kir97
, Problem
2.11.A]
. Indeed, suppose some finite index subgroup

\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
admitted a surjection
f:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. For
t\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the representation

\displaystyle\rho_{t}:\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mapsto t^{n}

is a non-trivial
family of one-dimensional representations of
\mathbb{Z}
. Then the
representation
\rho_{t}\circ f
is a non-constant family of irreducible
representations of
\Gamma
.

A positive answer to
10.1.1
would also imply a positive answer to the following question:

Question 10.1.3
.

Let
g\gg 0
. Let
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
be a finite index subgroup, and
let
X_{r}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n}))
be the character variety parametrizing

r
-dimensional semisimple representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
. Is the fixed locus
X_{r}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n}))^{\Gamma}
finite?

Remark 10.1.4
.

One reason to believe
10.1.3
has a positive answer is that the profinite analogue does. Namely, let
X
be any normal connected algebraic variety over
\mathbb{C}
, and let
\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X)
be its profinite fundamental group. Let
\Gamma
be a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{Out}(\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X))
. Then
\Gamma
acts on the set of conjugacy classes of continuous representations

\pi_{1}^{\text{\'{e}t}}(X)\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}).

It follows from
[
Lit21
, Remark 1.1.4]
that the
\Gamma
-fixed points are
discrete
in the
\ell
-adic topology. (The result there is stated for curves, but one may reduce to this case by the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem.)

If one accepts in addition Simpson’s motivicity conjecture for rigid local systems
[
Sim92
, Conjecture on p. 9]
, a positive answer to
10.1.1
would imply that every semisimple local system on
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
is of geometric origin for
g\gg 0
. This would be of particular interest for those local systems not obviously of geometric origin, e.g. the local systems arising from TQFT constructions.
Thus one might ask:

Question 10.1.5
.

For
g\gg 0
, is every semisimple local system on
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
of geometric origin? Let
C
be a Riemann surface of genus
g\gg 0
. Is every semisimple MCG-finite representation of
\pi_{1}(C)
of geometric origin?

Example 10.1.6
.

The analogy between the representation theory of mapping class groups and the representation theory of lattices in simple Lie groups of rank greater than one only goes so far. Indeed, mapping class groups typically admit non-rigid reducible representations, as we now explain.

(1)

Let
H=H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})
. Morita
[
Mor93
]
produces a nonzero class

\sigma\in H^{1}(\operatorname{Mod}_{g},(\wedge^{3}H)/H),

closely related to the Johnson homomorphism, yielding a non-split extension of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g}
-representations

1\to\wedge^{3}H/H\to W\to\mathbb{Q}\to 1.

The representation
W
is evidently a non-trivial deformation of
\mathbb{Q}\oplus\wedge^{3}H/H
, and hence this latter representation is non-rigid.

(2)

An essentially identical but perhaps slightly simpler argument gives examples for punctured surfaces. For
n>0
the group
\operatorname{PMod}_{g,n}
acts on
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n-1},x)
, and hence on the group algebra
\mathbb{Q}[\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n-1},x)]
. Let
\mathscr{I}\subset\mathbb{Q}[\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n-1},x)]
be the augmentation ideal. Then direct computation shows that the short exact sequence of
\operatorname{PMod}_{g,n}
-representations

0\to\mathscr{I}^{2}/\mathscr{I}^{3}\to\mathscr{I}/\mathscr{I}^{3}\to\mathscr{I}/\mathscr{I}^{2}\to 0

does not split, and hence the representation
\mathscr{I}/\mathscr{I}^{2}\oplus\mathscr{I}^{2}/\mathscr{I}^{3}
is not rigid.

(3)

For another example, one may consider the non-torsion class in

H^{1}(\operatorname{Mod}_{g,1},H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Z}))

constructed in
[
Mor89
, Proposition 6.4]
, which yields a non-split extension of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,1}
-representations

1\to H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})\to W\to\mathbb{Q}\to 1.

One may make the restriction to the point-pushing subgroup
W|_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})}
explicit as follows. Let

a:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})\to\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})^{\operatorname{ab}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}\simeq H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})

be the map induced by the Hurewicz isomorphism
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Z})=\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})^{\operatorname{ab}}
. Choose a splitting
W=H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})\oplus\mathbb{Q}e
, where we view
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})
as isomorphic to
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})
via the universal coefficient theorem and Poincaré duality. Then
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
acts trivially on
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Q})
, and an element
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
acts on
e
via the map

e\mapsto e+a(\gamma).

Remark 10.1.7
.

For a related discussion of ways mapping class groups
do not
behave like lattices in simple Lie groups of rank greater than one, see
[
FLM01
]
. Interestingly,
[
FLM01
, Theorem 1.6]
shows there are no faithful linear
representations of finite index subgroups of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,0}
of dimension
<2\sqrt{g-1}
. A related result follows from our work here, as we now explain.

Let
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{PMod}_{g,n+1}
be a finite-index subgroup containing the point-pushing subgroup.
It follows from
Theorem 1.2.1
,

2.4.1
(1), and

2.1.2

that if
\rho
is a representation of
\Gamma
of rank
<\sqrt{g+1}

(1)

whose restriction to the point-pushing subgroup is irreducible, and

(2)

with finite determinant

then
\rho
has finite image.
Although the two results are not comparable,
it is interesting that the bound in

[
FLM01
]
is also asymptotic to
\sqrt{g}
.

10.2.
Bounds and examples

It is natural to ask how sharp the bound of
\sqrt{g+1}
in

Theorem 1.2.1
is. We have no reason to believe it is sharp.
That said, one cannot expect a bound that is much stronger, as the extension

W
constructed in
10.1.6
(
3
) yields a
non-trivial unipotent representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
of rank
2g+1
, fixed
by the action of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g}
, namely
W|_{\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})}
.

Remark 10.2.1
.

The bounds of many of our main results are sharp when
g=1
.
In
g=1
, there are semisimple
2
-dimensional “special dihedral” MCG-finite representations
of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{1,n})
for
n>0

[
BGMW17
, Theorem B]
,
with image contained in the infinite dihedral group.
This shows sharpness of
Theorem 1.2.1
and also the semisimple
case,
Theorem 8.5.3
.
Moreover, there exist
2
-dimensional non-semisimple MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{1,n})
for
n>0
, see
[
CH19
, Theorem B]
.
This implies sharpness of the bound in
8.6.1
.

Note that the “special dihedral” MCG-finite representations of
[
BGMW17
, Theorem B]
are not in general arithmetic in the sense of
9.1.1
, as in general their local monodromy at infinity is not quasi-unipotent.

Remark 10.2.2
.

On the other hand, the bound
r<\sqrt{g+1}
in
Theorem 1.2.1
is not sharp when
g=2
and
g=3
.
Indeed, to verify this, we only need show there are no
2
-dimensional
MCG-finite representations with infinite image. There are no non-semisimple such
representations by
[
CH19
, Theorem B]
, and so it only
remains to show there are no irreducible
2
-dimensional
representations.
Any such representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
has finite determinant by
2.3.2
.
Replacing
\rho
by
\rho\otimes\det\rho^{-1/2}
, we would obtain another
MCG-finite
2
-dimensional representation with infinite image factoring
through
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
No such representations exist by
[
BGMW17
, Theorem A]
.

Question 10.2.3
.

Let
g,n\geq 0
. What is the minimal rank of an MCG-finite representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
with infinite image?

It is plausible that much stronger bounds
than
\sqrt{g+1}
hold in
Theorem 1.2.1

if one assumes the representation is semisimple (see
Figure 1
).

Example 10.2.4
.

We know of examples of MCG-finite
semisimple representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
with infinite image, of rank exponential in
g
. For example, one may via TQFT techniques construct representations of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}

of rank exponential in
g

[
KS16
, Corollary 4.3]
,
[
BKMS18
, Theorem 5.1]
;
these representations are non-trivial when restricted to the point-pushing subgroup, and hence by
2.1.2
yield MCG-finite representations.
Similarly, representations of
\operatorname{Mod}_{g,n}
constructed via variants of
the Kodaira-Parshin trick

[
LL19
, Example 3.3.1]

(see also
[
LL22
, Proposition 5.1.1 and Remark 5.1.2]
)
are semisimple of rank exponential in
g
and restrict to
MCG-finite, semisimple representations of the point-pushing subgroup, with infinite image.

Example 10.2.5
.

In genus zero, there is a huge collection of MCG-finite representations, namely the
rigid local systems
studied by Katz
[
Kat96
]
. The use of rigidity in our proof of
Theorem 1.2.1
, and

, suggest that the study of MCG-finite representations is a natural generalization of the study of rigid local systems to the higher genus setting.

Question 10.2.6
.

Let
g,n\geq 0
. What is the minimal rank of a semisimple MCG-finite representation of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
with infinite image?

In our view it would be extremely interesting to give an improved bound for semisimple MCG-finite representations, and to produce fundamentally new examples of semisimple MCG-finite representations.

Remark 10.2.7

(Bounds for free groups)
.

A variant of the construction in
10.1.6
(
3
)
gives an example of a representation of the free group
F_{N}
on
N
generators
of rank
N+1
, whose conjugacy class is fixed by the action of
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
,
see
[
PR00
, p. 1444]
. For example, when

, this representation is given by the matrices

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus again the bound of
\sqrt{g+1}
in

cannot be improved too much further if one
allows non-semisimple representations. However it is in principle possible that
there is
no
semisimple representation of
F_{N}
with infinite image whose
conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
when
N\geq 3
. Indeed, this would
follow from a conjecture of Grunewald and Lubotzky, see
[
GL09
, §9.2]
, using the main result of
[
FH17
]
.

There are interesting semisimple representations of
F_{2}
whose conjugacy class
has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{2})
. This follows, for example, from the linearity of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{2})

[
Kra00
]
, e.g. by choosing a faithful representation of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{2})
and restricting to the inner automorphisms, and then semisimplifying. Alternately, one may construct examples by applying the Kodaira-Parshin trick to construct representations of
\operatorname{Mod}_{1,2}
, which is commensurable with
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{2})
.

As remarked in the introduction, we conjecture the opposite of Grunewald and Lubotzky:

Conjecture 10.2.8
.

For all
N>0
, there exist semisimple representations of
F_{N}
with infinite image, whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
.

Our main impetus for this conjecture is the existence of many interesting
MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
, see
10.2.4
. In our view this conjecture is of great importance, both because of the intrinsic interest of “canonical” representations of
F_{N}
(i.e. those with finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
), and because of its relationship to the representation theory of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{N})
.

Remark 10.2.9
.

We briefly spell out the the relationship between representations of
F_{N}
whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
, and the representation theory of

\operatorname{Aut}(F_{N})
.
If
\Gamma\subset\operatorname{Aut}(F_{N})
is a finite index subgroup containing the inner automorphisms, and

\rho:\Gamma\to\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})

is a representation, then the conjugacy
class of
\rho|_{F_{N}}
has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
, via a proof
analogous to that of

2.1.2
. Conversely, as in to the proof of
2.2.2
, the projectivization of any irreducible
F_{N}
-representation whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
arises from a projective representation of a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{N})
. See
[
BKP19
, Theorem 7.12]
for a related result about low-rank projective representations of certain finite index subgroups of
\operatorname{Aut}(F_{n})
.

10.3.
Classification

The following is evidently quite difficult, but in our view is of great interest:

Question 10.3.1
.

Can one classify MCG-finite representations of
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})
of rank
r
? Equivalently, can one classify algebraic solutions to the rank
r
isomonodromy differential equations over
\mathscr{M}_{g,n}
? Can one classify representations of the free group
F_{N}
on
N
generators whose conjugacy class has finite orbit under
\operatorname{Out}(F_{N})
?

Remark 10.3.2
.

It would also be extremely interesting to learn of any new sources of

examples
of MCG-finite representations. For example, are there MCG-finite representations
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n})\to\operatorname{SL}_{r}(\mathbb{C})
with (Zariski-)dense image for
r
arbitrarily large?

Lemma A.1.4

Let

Proof.

Voi07
