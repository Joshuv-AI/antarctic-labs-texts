Abstract
Carbon Dioxide is an important tracer of the chemistry and physics in the terrestrial planet forming zone. Using a thermo-chemical model that has been tested against the mid-infrared water emission we re-interpret the CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} emission as observed with Spitzer . We find that both water UV-shielding and extra chemical heating significantly reduce the total CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} column in the emitting layer. Water UV-shielding is the more efficient effect, reducing the CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} column by ∼ \sim 2 orders of magnitude. These lower CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} abundances lead to CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} -to- H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} flux ratios that are closer to the observed values, but CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} emission is still too bright, especially in relative terms. Invoking the depletion of elemental oxygen outside of the water mid-plane iceline more strongly impacts the CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} emission than it does the H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} emission, bringing the CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} -to- H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} emission in line with the observed values. We conclude that the CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} emission observed with Spitzer -IRS is coming from a thin layer in the photo-sphere of the disk, similar to the strong water lines. Below this layer, we expect CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} not to be present except when replenished by a physical process. I
Introduction

Carbon Dioxide,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, is an important molecule in the interstellar inventory.
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is a carrier of significant volatile carbon and oxygen in both interstellar
(
Boogert et al. 2015
)
and cometary
(
Mumma & Charnley 2011
)
ices. However, the lack of a permanent dipole and its abundance in the Earth’s atmosphere requires infrared space missions for astronomical observations.
The chemistry of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is closely linked to
CO
, a common precursor to
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, with which
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
shares the
OH
radical as a precursor in both gas and grain-surface chemistry
(
Smith et al. 2004
;
Arasa et al. 2013
, e.g.)
. Such close chemical links to the main reservoirs of carbon and oxygen make
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
an important tracer of the carbon and oxygen elemental abundances, especially the overall C/O ratio. As the C/O ratio is proposed to link planet composition and formation history
(
Öberg et al. 2011
)
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
likely has an important role to play in the unraveling of this complex problem.

Towards proto-planetary disks,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
has most commonly been observed in emission using
Spitzer
-IRS
(
Carr & Najita 2008
;
Pontoppidan et al. 2010
, e.g.)
. CO
2
emission has been inferred to originate from the warm (
>
300 K) surface layers confined to within the inner few au
(
Salyk et al. 2011
;
Bosman et al. 2017
)
. In these strongly irradiated disk surface layers the abundance of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is sensitive to both the C/O ratio, through the abundance of its precursor
OH

(
Woitke et al. 2018
;
Anderson et al. 2021
, e.g.)
, as well as the elemental abundance of C
(
Bosman et al. 2018a
, See Fig. 1 in )
.

The interplay between dust growth, dynamics, and chemistry in the outer (tens of au) disk might also significantly impact the abundance of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in the inner few au where excitation conditions allow for detectable rovibrational emission.
The low
CO
abundances in the outer disk are possibly pointing to a sequestration of carbon into
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
ices
(
Eistrup et al. 2016
;
Bosman et al. 2018b
)
. This is seen in detailed models that link chemistry to dust evolution
(
Krijt et al. 2020
)
. As these ices drift to inside the CO
2
sublimation front (or ice line), they could greatly impact the inner disk
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance
(
Bosman et al. 2017
;
Booth et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. Thus CO
2
might be a sensitive tracer of dust drift.

Gas dynamics in the inner few au could also impact the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance structure. Vertical mixing of water, followed by sequestration on large grains dubbed the “vertical cold finger effect” has been invoked to explain the limited extent of the water surface reservoir
(
Meijerink et al. 2009
;
Blevins et al. 2016
;
Bosman & Bergin 2021
)
and dynamical simulations have shown it to be effective
(
Krijt et al. 2016
)
. Such sequestration would similarly affect
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outside of the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
iceline. Furthermore between the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
icelines, the sequestration of water can impact the elemental abundances of Carbon and Oxygen, creating an environment in which
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is inefficiently formed, and the excess oxygen lost to water ice in the mid-plane.

The abundance of CO
2
is a crucial component in understanding the overall C/O ratio of gas and ices, but it also can be a probe of the overall disk chemical evolution, which is linked to the disk gas and dust physics. To fully understand these processes it requires the emission be analyzed in detail.
Unfortunately, recent modeling efforts have difficulty in simultaneously predicting the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
fluxes in the mid-infrared
(
Woitke et al. 2018
;
Anderson et al. 2021
)
. In this paper, we used an updated set of thermo-chemical models that has been tested against the infrared water observations
(
Bosman et al. 2022
)
to study the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance structure and emission from the inner disk regions. Crucially, our model includes the effects of water UV-shielding. This must be present given the large water columns
(
Bethell & Bergin 2009
)
leading to UV absorption at higher surface layers than would be set by the dust optical surface to UV photons; this can strongly alter the chemical structure of which
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is a key component.

(
Rothman et al. 2013
)

)

feature around 15
\mu
m we denote this as the
01^{1}0-00^{0}0

Q
-branch, but it is implied that this includes a smaller contribution of the more highly excited
02^{2}0
–
01^{1}0
,
03^{3}0
–
02^{2}0
, etc.
Q
-branches.

For comparison with slab excitation models we use the model from
Banzatti et al. 2012
, using representative slab model parameters from
Salyk et al. 2011
. For water we use a column of
3\times 10^{18}
cm
-2
and a excitation temperature of 500 K, as done in

)

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
spectra line up nicely with the slab model spectra that are representative for the
Spitzer
-IRS observations.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column

The thermo-chemical model with water UV-shielding and chemical heating consistently predict a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column in the surface layers that is around
10^{16}
cm
-2
. This is orders of magnitude lower than the
10^{18}
cm
-2
columns that follow from the models that do not include water UV-shielding. However, it is on the high end of the observed data where columns span the
3\times 10^{14}
–
3\times 10^{16}
cm
-2
, with additional non-detection implying even lower
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
columns. As such our models are not capturing the full variation in the observed population. They are however representative of a significant part of the full population.

The gas temperatures in our model at the outer edge of the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission is colder (
<
500 K) than the inferred temperatures from the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Spitzer
-IRS observations
(
Salyk et al. 2011
,
\sim
700 K;)
. This could imply that the radial temperature profile in the disk is impacting the shape of the
01^{1}0
–
00^{0}0

Q
-branch forcing the slab-model fit to a higher temperature, but lower column solution. This could also explain why the slab models consistently find a higher gas-temperature for the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
than for the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}

(
Salyk et al. 2011
)
, whereas in the models they both emit from the same gas. With individual
P
and
R
-branch lines of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and better isolated lines of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
in the JWST-MIRI spectra, it should be possible to get a better handle on the gas-temperature and column densities of both these species.

There are chemical pathways that could lead to lower
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundances. Increasing the gas-temperature changes the balance between
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation, which both rely on OH; as a result a higher temperature lowers the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance. A temperature increase requires either a larger total UV flux or mechanical heating, as currently most of the energy in the UV photons is already converted into heat in the models
(
Bosman et al. 2022
)
.

Another possible way to lower the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance (and decrease emission) is to increase its destruction rate. Two and three-body gas-phase reactions that destroy
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are few and inefficient
(
Bosman et al. 2018a
, see)
, whereas reactions due to an ionization source (cosmic-rays and X-rays) are already included. This leaves the dissociation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
due to UV, which already is the main destruction pathway in the surface layers where
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission is being produced. The current stellar spectrum contains a significant amount of Ly-
\alpha
. The photodissociation cross-section of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is modest near Ly-
\alpha
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is not efficiently dissociated in our model
(
Huestis & Berkowitz 2010
;
Archer et al. 2013
;
Heays et al. 2017
)
. However, these cross-sections are from measurements at room temperatures. At elevated temperatures, the UV cross section at Ly-
\alpha
increases by up to two orders of magnitude
(
Venot et al. 2018
)
. Implementing these high temperature cross sections, however, had little effect on the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
distribution or spectra. In these models the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance decreased by 10–20% in the disk surface layers. As such, we deem that our chemical model is robust in its treatment of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
destruction pathways.

IV.3
Radial extent of the emitting layer

In the chemical models the radial extent of the emitting layer is naturally restricted by the presence of
OH
. Formation of
OH
is only efficient at high enough temperatures that the reaction barrier of the
O
+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
reaction can be over come. This effectively sets the maximal
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
emitting area. As chemically
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is linked to
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
through the presence of
OH
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is thus bound to a similar region from which emission can arise as the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
emitting region.

\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, however, due to the lower upper level energies of the main transitions around 15
\mu
m, emits more strongly from colder gas a large radii when compared to water transitions accessible to JWST. This is compounded by the higher
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance in colder gas. Radially restricting the emission to warmer gas impacts the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission more strongly than the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
emission. This can be seen in Fig.
3
. As the radially restricted emission reproduces the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-to-
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
flux ratios from the observations. This implies that a the emitting areas are actually restricted more tightly than the chemistry implies and that a physical process like the “cold finger effect” is active
(
Meijerink et al. 2009
;
Bosman & Bergin 2021
, e.g.)
. This process impacts the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
directly by mixing it downward and locking up the water in the midplane ices
(
Krijt et al. 2016
, see, e.g.)
. This, in turn, impacts the chemical equilibrium of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
due to the lower availability of oxygen, critical for the formation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. As
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is constantly destroyed in the surface layers by UV, any available gaseous
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
between the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
mid-plane icelines will constantly be reprocessed into
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
which can then be trapped in the mid-plane ice. We note that trapping
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
ice would still create a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
rich atmosphere outside of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
iceline due to back diffusion of the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
gas
(
Bosman et al. 2018a
)
.

This could be relatively easily tested by comparing velocity resolved line profiles of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, unfortunately
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is hard to do from the ground
(
Bosman et al. 2017
, e.g.)
and currently no planned space mission that will cover this wavelength range with a medium or high spectral resolving power (
\lambda/\Delta\lambda>10000
). As such, the emitting area from
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
will have to be extracted from analysis of the velocity unresolved spectra. Comparison with the 4.3
\mu
m band which can be observed with JWST-NIRSpec could be helpful here as these lines originate from a radially more constrained region and are thus less affected by the abundance of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
outside of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
midplane iceline (see Appendix
C
). The feature strength ratio between the 4.3
\mu
m band and the 15
\mu
m band could thus be a measure for the radial extent of the total
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emitting region.

V
Conclusions

We study the impact of water UV-shielding and chemical heating on the predicted
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission arising from within the terrestrial planet-forming zones of gas-rich disks around 15
\mu
m using the thermo-chemical model DALI. Our base model finds a similar result as found in previously published models
(
Woitke et al. 2018
;
Anderson et al. 2021
)
in that the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is too bright relative to the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
emission.

We find that the water UV-shielding has a strong impact on the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance structure. With UV-shielding included, disk photospheric
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is constrained to a thin layer close to the H/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
transition. This yields a
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column of a few times
10^{16}
cm
-2
, two orders less than the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column prediction without water UV-shielding,
\sim
10^{18}
cm
-2
. The lower
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column is in line with the observationally derived column. This suggests that water-UV shielding is present in disk systems, which is consistent with simple conclusions based on the inferred water vapor column
(
Bethell & Bergin 2009
)
.
This large change in column has a strong effect on the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission, both in absolute terms, but particularly in the flux ratio between the main isotopologue
01^{1}0
–
00^{0}0

Q
-branch on the one hand and the two
10^{0}0-01^{1}0
Q-branch features at 13.90 and 16.20
\mu
m or the main
\hphantom{{}^{\text{13}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Q
-branch on the other hand.

We find the best agreement between thermo-chemical model spectra prediction and observations, if we invoke a radial restriction of the emitting area of both
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
on top of the inclusion of water UV-shielding and extra chemical heating. The radial restriction increases the average temperature of the emitting gas, which appears to be required by observations. This has an impact on the chemistry, lowering the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-to-
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance, as well as favoring the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
lines relative to the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
lines as the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
lines have higher upper level energies than the main
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
lines in the spectra.

We propose that this is evidence of the “cold finger effect”, that is the sequestration of oxygen in the form of water on the mid-plane ice outside of the mid-plane water iceline. This process would lower the oxygen abundance until the C/O ratio reaches unity, locking all oxygen in CO and preventing the formation of both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, which rely on the presence of O or OH, outside of the water mid-plane iceline, even in the disk atmosphere.

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}

>10
