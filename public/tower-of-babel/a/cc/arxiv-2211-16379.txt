Elfs, trees and quantum walks
Simon Apers, Stephen Piddock

Abstract
We study an elementary Markov process on graphs based on electric flow sampling (elfs). The elfs process repeatedly samples from an electric flow on a graph. While the sinks of the flow are fixed, the source is updated using the electric flow sample, and the process ends when it hits a sink vertex. We argue that this process naturally connects to many key quantities of interest. E.g., we describe a random walk coupling which implies that the elfs process has the same arrival distribution as a random walk. We also analyze the electric hitting time, which is the expected time before the process hits a sink vertex. As our main technical contribution, we show that the electric hitting time on trees is logarithmic in the graph size and weights. The initial motivation behind the elfs process is that quantum walks can sample from electric flows, and they can hence implement this process very naturally. This yields a quantum walk algorithm for sampling from the random walk arrival distribution, which has widespread applications. It complements the existing line of quantum walk search algorithms which only return an element from the sink, but yield no insight in the distribution of the returned element. By our bound on the electric hitting time on trees, the quantum walk algorithm on trees requires quadratically fewer steps than the random walk hitting time, up to polylog factors.

Introduction and summary

It has long been known that there is an elegant and fundamental connection between electric networks and random walks on graph, justified through the theory of harmonic functions
[
DS84
]
.
This connection gives precise characterizations of random walk and Markov chain properties in terms of the associated electric network properties
[
CRR+96
]
, and it has been instrumental in the study of recurrence and percolation properties
[
LP17
]
, and the design of randomized and dynamic algorithms
[
Bro89
,
GLP22
]
.
In this work we consider a new process called the
electric flow sampling process
or
elfs process
, which repeatedly samples from an electric flow on a graph.
While the sinks of the flow are fixed, the source is updated using the electric flow sample, and the process ends when a sample hits a sink vertex.
We analyze this process, uncovering many connections with the usual random walks and electric networks framework.
One highlight is a coupling of the elfs process to the random walk from the initial source to the sinks.
This shows that the process has the same arrival distribution as the random walk.
The coupling also reveals a close connection to a new quantity called the random walk “escape time”.
We conjecture, and provide evidence, that a random walk can sample from an electric flow in a number of steps given by the escape time.
Then we analyze the “electric hitting time”, which is the expected number of electric flow samples before the process hits a sink vertex (put more mundanely, how long until the elfs hit the sink?).
As the main technical contribution, we show that on trees the electric hitting time is logarithmic in the graph size and edge weights, and we reveal a connection to the entropy of the arrival distribution.

The elfs process is alternatively (and originally) motivated by a more recent but similarly profound connection between electric networks and
quantum
walks.
Quantum walks are the quantum analogue of random walks on graphs, and they are instrumental in the design of quantum algorithms and the study of quantum computing and quantum physics more broadly.
The connection between quantum walks and electric networks seems to first have been described by Belovs
[
Bel13
,
BCJ+13
]
, who used it in a quantum walk search algorithm for 3-distinctness.
It was later used and refined with applications to backtracking algorithms
[
Mon18
,
JW18
]
, the traveling-salesman problem
[
MLM17
]
, 2-player games
[
AK17
]
and branch-and-bound algorithms
[
Mon20
]
.
The direct impulse for this work came from work by Piddock
[
Pid19
]
, in which it was shown that quantum walks can be used very naturally to sample from electric flows.
We show that this requires a number of quantum walk steps bounded by the
square root
of the random walk escape time, providing a quadratic speedup over the conjectured random walk complexity.
By repeatedly using a quantum walk for electric flow sampling, we obtain a quantum walk algorithm that approximately samples from the random walk arrival distribution.
This opens up new avenues for finding quantum speedups over random walk algorithms.
Using our bound on the electric hitting time on trees, we show that the resulting quantum walk algorithm samples from the arrival distribution on trees in quadratically fewer steps than the random walk hitting time, up to polylogarithmic factors.

1.1
Elfs

Here we describe in more detail our results concerning the elfs process and its connection to random walks.
Consider a graph
G=(V,E,w)

w_{e}\geq 0

The unit electric flow between a source vertex

The resulting energy of the electric flow is called the effective resistance

If we interpret the graph as an electric network, by replacing every edge

M

Alternatively, it is the unique unit potential flow from

The process ends when step 2. picks a vertex

We define the limit or

The

Arrival distribution and electric hitting time

With some effort, we can find a closed and algebraic form for the transition matrix of the elfs process, expressed in terms of the graph Laplacian.
This leads to some (a priori) mysterious conclusions.

Consider the elfs process with initial source vertex
s
and sink
M

f
denote the unit electric flow from
s

M

From the algebraic expression, we find that the arrival distribution equals

I.e., the process ends at vertex

From the connection between random walks and electric networks, we know that this equals the arrival distribution of a

The algebraic expression also gives some insight into the electric hitting time.

Using that

Random walk coupling

We obtain a more intelligible explanation for these observations (and terminologies) by proving that there exists a coupling between a random walk from
s
to
M

Consider the respective Markov chains

\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\rho}\in M\}.

We show that there exist random stopping times
0<\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}<\dots<\nu_{\rho}=\tau
such that

\left(\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\},\,\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1}=X_{\nu_{1}},\dots,Y_{\rho}=X_{\nu_{\rho}}\in M\}\right)

describes a coupling between the two processes.
In other words, we can think of the elfs process as a subsampling of the random walk from
s
to
M
.
A direct consequence of this coupling is that both processes have the same arrival distribution (i.e.,
\mathbb{P}[Y_{\rho}=y]=\mathbb{P}[X_{\tau}=y]

We proceed by giving a random walk interpretation of the escape time
\mathrm{ET}_{s}=\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v^{2}_{x}d_{x}
.
Letting
\mathrm{esc}_{s}=1+\max\{t\mid X_{t}=s\}
denote the time at which the random walk has left

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{esc}_{s}]=\mathrm{ET}_{s}.

While this seems like an elementary random walk quantity, it seems to not have been studied before.
Apart from its connection to electric flow sampling, we also demonstrate it as a useful quantity for designing random walk algorithms.
Specifically, we design a random walk algorithm for approximating the random walk escape probability or the effective resistance
R_{s}

\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{ET}_{s}

This contrasts with a simpler algorithm that requires
O(\mathrm{HT}_{s}/\varepsilon^{2})

While trivially

1.2
Trees

We defined the electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
as the expected number of electric flow samples before the elfs process from source
s
hits the sink
M
.
As a trivial but important example, consider a path of length
n
with the source on one end and the sink on the other end.
After a single sample, the source will jump approximately uniformly at random between the source and the sink.
In expectation, this repeatedly halves the distance between the source and the sink, yielding an electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in\Theta(\log n)
.

Figure 2
:
n
-vertex path graph with source
s
and sink
M
. The electric hitxting time from
s
to
M
is
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in\Theta(\log n)
.

Surprisingly, if both endpoints of the line are marked (or equivalently, we consider an
n
-cycle) then bounding the electric hitting time is much more fiddly, yet a similar
\Theta(\log n)
bound holds.
In fact, and this is our main technical contribution, we show that a similar bound holds for any tree graph.
This contrasts with general graphs, as we show that for instance a complete graph or an expander graph can have electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in\Omega(\poly(n))
.

To state our bound, consider a tree
T
, a source vertex
s
and a sink
M
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
s
is the root of the tree and
M
is the set of leaves.
If the tree is unweighted, then we show that

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2+\log(R_{s})+2H(\mu),

with
H(\mu)
the entropy of the arrival distribution
\mu
on
M
.
Since
R_{s}\leq n
and
H(\mu)\leq\log(|M|)
, this implies that
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in O(\log n)
.
If the tree is weighted, then

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2+\log(R_{s}d_{M})+H(\mu)-D(\mu||\sigma_{M}),

with
d_{M}
the total weight of the edges incident on
M
, and
D(\mu||\sigma_{M})\geq 0
the relative entropy between the arrival distribution
\mu
and the stationary distribution
\sigma_{M}
restricted to
M
.
With
w_{\min}
and
w_{\max}
the minimum and maximum edge weight, respectively, we can use that
R_{s}\leq n/w_{\min}
to get
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in O(\log(nw_{\max}/w_{\min}))
.

As hinted by the path with both endpoints in the sink, the proof for general trees is rather involved.
On a high level, we apply a divide-and-conquer argument.
We use the notion of a Schur complement to show how to contract part of the tree, without affecting the elfs process in the uncontracted part.
This allows us to bound the electric hitting time “from the bottom up”, i.e., we prove a recurrence relation on the electric hitting time in a tree, based on the electric hitting time in its subtrees.

Schur complement

On a general graph
G
, the Schur complement on a vertex subset
S
describes a modified graph
G^{\prime}
with vertex set
S
such that a random walk on
G^{\prime}
behaves the same as a random walk on
G
, when considering only the vertices in
S
.
Similarly, the potentials of an electric flow between any pair of vertices in
S
are the same in
G
and
G^{\prime}
.
As a consequence, it seems natural that also the elfs process on
G^{\prime}
can be related to that on
G
.
We show that this is the case when there is a single vertex connecting
S
to its complement
S^{c}
.
We call such a vertex a “cut vertex”.
We exploit how a cut vertex implies that, for any source vertex
s\in S
, the electric flow on
S^{c}
is always the same (up to a global rescaling).
Since any vertex in a tree graph is a cut vertex, this implies the Schur complement result on trees.

:
Schur complement
G^{\prime}
of a tree graph
G
. We relate the number of steps of the elfs process on
G
with source
s
and sink
M
(dotted circles) to the elfs process on

.

Induction on tree depth

We then bound the electric hitting time with an inductive argument.
Using the Schur complement, we find that the number of steps that the elfs process spends in a subtree only depends on the structure of that subtree, and the electric flow going from the source
s
into that subtree.
In particular, there is an easy expression for the number of steps on an edge going directly into the sink.
This is the base case for our inductive argument.
The inductive step then relates the number of steps of the elfs process in a subtree to the number of steps in smaller subtrees of that subtree.
This allows us to bound the electric hitting time by working from the sink, at the bottom of the tree, up to the source, at the root of the tree.

1.3
Quantum walks

We showed that electric flow sampling is closely tied to random walks.
However, the original motivation came from the study of
quantum
walks on graphs.
While there are varying definitions, the canonical definition by Szegedy
[
Sze04
]
describes quantum walks in terms of reflection operators and invariant subspaces.
It was recognized, most notably by Belovs
[
Bel13
]
, that this definition naturally connects with the linear algebraic formulation of electric networks and electric flows.
This was further exploited by Piddock
[
Pid19
]
, who showed that quantum walks can be used very naturally to prepare the quantum flow state

|f\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{s}}}\sum_{e}\frac{f_{e}}{\sqrt{w_{e}}}|e\rangle.

Measuring this state returns an edge
e
with probability
f_{e}^{2}/(R_{s}w_{e})
, which corresponds to an electric flow sample.
We show that an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
|f\rangle
can be prepared using
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}/\varepsilon)
steps of a quantum walk, given an upper bound
\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{ET}_{s}
.
This quadratically improves (and further motivates) the conjectured number of random walk steps required for electric flow sampling.

Quantum elfs process

We can simulate the elfs process by repeatedly applying the quantum walk algorithm for electric flow sampling.
Ignoring errors, the total number of quantum walks steps required would be

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\rho-1}\sqrt{\mathrm{ET}_{Y_{j}}}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{HT}_{s}\mathrm{EHT}_{s}}\leq 2\mathrm{HT}_{s},

where
\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\rho}\in M\}
is the original elfs process.
The first inequality follows from combining
Eq.

2
with Jensen’s inequality, and the second inequality uses
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2\mathrm{HT}_{s}
.
This gives an almost quadratic speedup when the electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
is small.
Taking the errors into account, we conjecture that it is possible to
\varepsilon
-approximately simulate the elfs process in running time
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}\hat{\mathrm{EHT}}_{s}}/\poly(\varepsilon))
, where
\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{HT}_{s}
and
\hat{\mathrm{EHT}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
are given bounds.
Falling short of proving this, we do prove a weaker bound of

\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}}\hat{\mathrm{EHT}}_{s}^{3/2}/\varepsilon^{2}\right).

(3)

For the particular case of trees we can invoke our earlier bound
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in O(\log(nw_{\max}/w_{\min}))
.
Combined with
Eq.

3
this yields a quantum walk algorithm with complexity scaling as
\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}}/\varepsilon^{2}
(up to polylogarithmic factors) for sampling from a distribution
\varepsilon
-close to the elfs process.

Quantum walk search

The resulting quantum walk algorithm connects most naturally to the long line of research on quantum walk search, which studies the use of quantum walks for finding distinguished or “marked” vertices on a graph.
Building on a long line of work
[
Sze04
,
MNRS11
,
KMOR16
,
AS19
]
, it was recently proven that quantum walks can find such a marked element quadratically faster than the random walk hitting time
[
AGJK20
]
, provided that we start from the stationary distribution.
This is a nontrivial restriction.
If instead we start from a fixed initial vertex, then a quadratic speedup is only known over the random walk
commute time

[
Bel13
,
AGJ21
,
Pid19
]
, which can be much larger than the hitting time.
Our quantum elfs process naturally fits in this quantum walk search framework.
Conceptually, we make two new contributions.

On the one hand, the quantum elfs process is a quantum walk algorithm that finds a marked element that is drawn from the random walk arrival distribution.
This contrasts with most earlier quantum walk algorithms that yield no insight into the quantum walk arrival distribution.
Moreover, it opens up the use of quantum walks to speed up the many classical algorithms that rely on the random walk arrival distribution, including algorithms for random spanning trees
[
Bro89
]
, semi-supervised learning
[
ZGL03
]
and linear system solving
[
CS15
]
.

On the other hand, the runtime of our quantum walk algorithm is closely related to the random walk hitting time.
E.g., on trees our quantum walk algorithm has complexity
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{HT}_{s}})
.
More generally, the conjectured complexity of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{HT}_{s}\mathrm{EHT}_{s}})
is always bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\mathrm{HT}_{s})
.
This contrasts with the aforementioned quantum walk algorithms that start from a single vertex and have a runtime
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{CT}_{s}})

[
Bel13
,
AGJ21
,
Pid19
]
.
Here
\mathrm{CT}_{s}
is the random walk
commute time
from
s
to
M
,
and this is mostly incomparable to the random walk hitting time.

Finally, we also describe a quantum walk algorithm for estimating the escape probability or effective resistance.
This is a quantum counterpart to our random walk algorithm based on the escape time, and has a quadratically better runtime of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}/\varepsilon^{3/2})
, for some given upper bound
\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{ET}_{s}
.
The quantum algorithm refines existing quantum walk algorithms based on the commute time, whose runtime is
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{CT}_{s}}/\varepsilon^{3/2})

[
IJ19
,
Pid19
]
.

For convenience, we extend

We can associate an electric network to

.
If

then it has conductance
w_{xy}=0
.
For any

and

f^{s,M}=f
from
s
to
M
.

3

3

We drop the superscripts on

This is the unique unit flow

from

to

The resulting energy of the electric flow is called the effective resistance

In other words, there exists a

2.2
Markov chains and random walks

Consider a Markov chain
\{X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots\}
with states
X_{t}\in V
.
Its dynamics are determined by transition probabilities
P_{xy}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=y\mid X_{t}=x)
.
As a consequence, if the distribution of
X_{t}
is described by row vector
p_{t}
, then
X_{t+1}
is distributed according to the distribution
p_{t+1}=p_{t}P
.
A
random walk
on a graph
G
is described by a Markov chain with transition matrix
P=D^{-1}A=I-D^{-1}L
.
It turns out that any
reversible
Markov chain can be written as a random walk on an appropriately weighted graph.

A Markov chain that is
absorbing
in a sink
M\subseteq V
has a transition matrix of the form

P=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{UU}&P_{UM}\\
0&I_{MM}\\
\end{array}\right),

where we have split the state space into sink vertices (
M
) and remaining vertices (
U=V\backslash M
).

Hitting time.

For a general Markov chain, the hitting time
\tau_{M}
of sink
M
is the expected time at which the chain hits a vertex in
M
.
Starting from a vertex
s
, we denote its expectation value
\mathbb{E}_{s}(\tau)
by
\mathrm{HT}_{s}
.
We have that (see e.g.
[
KS83
]
)

\mathrm{HT}_{s}=\sum_{x}(I_{UU}-P_{UU})^{-1}_{sx}.

For a random walk from
s
this becomes

\mathrm{HT}_{s}=\sum_{x}(I_{UU}-P_{UU})^{-1}_{sx}=\sum_{x}(L_{UU}^{-1}D)_{sx}=\sum_{x}v_{x}d_{x},

where we recall that
v_{x}
is the voltage at
x
in the unit electric flow from
s
to
M
.

Arrival distribution.

We will also be interested in the distribution with which the random walk hits
M
.
We call this the
arrival distribution

\mu
.
For a Markov chain that starts from
s
and that is absorbing in
M
, this is given by the infinite time distribution
\mu_{y}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(P^{n})_{sy}
.
Assuming that the hitting time is finite from any initial vertex (and so
\left\lVert P_{UU}\right\rVert<1
), we can rewrite

\lim_{t\to\infty}P^{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{UU}^{t}&\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}P_{UU}^{t}P_{UM}\\
0&I_{MM}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&(I_{UU}-P_{UU})^{-1}P_{UM}\\
0&I_{MM}\\
\end{array}\right),

and so

\mu_{y}=\big((I_{UU}-P_{UU})^{-1}P_{UM}\big)_{sy}.

(4)

For a random walk this is

\mu_{y}=\big((I_{UU}-P_{UU})^{-1}P_{UM}\big)_{sy}=\big((L_{UU}^{-1}D_{UU})(-D_{UU}^{-1}L_{UM})\big)_{sy}=-(L_{UU}^{-1}L_{UM})_{sy}.

(5)

Recalling that
(L_{UU}^{-1})_{sx}=v_{x}
, this becomes

\mu_{y}=\sum_{x}v_{x}w_{xy}=\sum_{x}f_{xy}.

I.e., the probability of arriving in sink vertex
y
equals the electric flow going into
y
.

Other useful identities.

We summarize some additional identities that relate properties of random walks and electric flows.
Proofs of these identities can be found in e.g.
[
LP17
]
.
For a random walk from
s
that is absorbing in
M
, let
S_{xy}
denote the expected number of traversals from
x
to
y
.
Then

\mathbb{E}_{s}[S_{xy}]=v_{x}w_{xy}.

(6)

With
T_{x}
the expected number of visits to
x
, this implies that

\mathbb{E}_{s}[T_{x}]=v_{x}d_{x}.

(7)

This generalizes the expression for the random walk arrival distribution.

Now consider a non-absorbing random walk starting from
s
.
We denote by
\tau^{+}_{s}
the expected
return time
to
s
.
By Kac’s lemma this is equal to

\mathbb{E}_{s}[\tau^{+}_{s}]=\frac{1}{\pi(s)}=\frac{d_{s}}{\sum_{x}d_{x}}.

(8)

Finally, we also have that

\mathbb{P}_{s}[\tau_{s}^{+}>\tau_{M}]=\frac{1}{R_{s}d_{s}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tau_{s}<\tau_{M}]=\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}.

(9)

3
Electric flow sampling process

We begin our analysis of the elfs process, which we recall below.

Electric flow sampling (elfs) process

From an initial source vertex
s
and fixed sink
M\subseteq V
, repeat the following:

1.

Let
f
denote the unit electric flow from source
s
to sink
M
.

Sample an edge
e
with probability proportional to
f_{e}^{2}/w_{e}
.

2.

Pick a random endpoint
x
from
e
and set the source
s=x
.

The process ends when step 2. picks a vertex
x\in M
.

In the following, we give a closed form algebraic expression for the transition matrix of the elfs process.
From this expression, we find expressions related to the arrival distribution and electric hitting time.

3.1
Algebraic form and arrival distribution

For a fixed sink
M
, the elfs process describes a Markov chain on the vertex set
V
.
We let
Q
denote its transition matrix.
Starting from a source
s
, the transition probability
Q_{sx}
denotes the probability of picking
x
in step 2.
In the following lemma we give a closed and useful expression for
Q
.
We use shorthand
A*B
for the entrywise product of two matrices, and introduce voltage matrix
V
and diagonal resistance matrix
R
defined by

(V)_{xy}=v^{x}_{y}\quad\text{ and }\quad(R)_{xx}=R_{x}.

Lemma 1
.

The transition matrix of the elfs process with sink
M
is

Q=\left(\begin{matrix}I_{UU}-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}(V*V)L_{UU}&-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}(V*V)L_{UM}\\
0&I_{MM}\end{matrix}\right).

In particular, with
v
the voltages of the unit electric flow from
s
to
M
,

Q_{sx}=\frac{1}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y}(v_{x}-v_{y})^{2}w_{xy}.

(10)

Proof.

The probability of picking an edge
(x,y)
when sampling from the electric flow from
s
to
M
is

Q_{s,(x,y)}=\frac{1}{R_{s}}\frac{(f_{xy})^{2}}{w_{xy}}=\frac{1}{R_{s}}(v_{x}-v_{y})^{2}w_{xy}.

The probability of picking a vertex
x
is hence

\displaystyle Q_{sx}=\sum_{y}\frac{Q_{s,(x,y)}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\frac{(v_{x}-v_{y})^{2}w_{xy}}{2R_{s}}

\displaystyle=2v_{x}\sum_{y}\frac{(v_{x}-v_{y})w_{xy}}{2R_{s}}-\sum_{y}\frac{(v_{x}^{2}-v_{y}^{2})w_{xy}}{2R_{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}\delta_{sx}-\sum_{y}\frac{(v_{x}^{2}-v_{y}^{2})w_{xy}}{2R_{s}}=\delta_{sx}-\frac{1}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y}v_{y}^{2}L_{yx}.

In the last equality we used that
v_{s}=R_{s}
.
In the third equality we used flow conservation and the fact that
v_{x}=0
for
x\in M
to rewrite

v_{x}\left(\sum_{y}(v_{x}-v_{y})w_{xy}\right)=v_{x}\left(\sum_{y}f_{xy}\right)=v_{x}\left(\delta_{sx}-\sum_{z\in M}q_{z}\delta_{xz}\right)=v_{x}\delta_{sx},

with
q_{z}
the flow going into vertex
z\in M
.
The final expression for
Q_{sx}
exactly equals the claimed expression.
∎

From this expression we can easily prove that the elfs arrival distribution equals the random walk arrival distribution.

Corollary 1
.

The arrival distribution of the elfs process from initial source
s
to sink
M
is equal to the arrival distribution of a random walk from
s
to
M
.

Proof.

Plugging the expression for the elfs transition matrix
Q
into expression
Eq.

4
for the Markov chain arrival distribution, we get that the elfs arrival distribution equals
q_{y}=((I_{UU}-Q_{UU})^{-1}Q_{UM})_{sy}
.
By
Lemma

1
we can rewrite

\displaystyle(I_{UU}-Q_{UU})^{-1}Q_{UM}

\displaystyle=\left(2L_{UU}^{-1}(V*V)^{-1}R\right)\left(-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}(V*V)L_{UM}\right)=-L_{UU}^{-1}L_{UM},

and so
q_{y}=(-L_{UU}^{-1}L_{UM})_{sy}
.
This matches the standard random walk arrival distribution in
Eq.

5
.
∎

In fact, even the edge from which the elfs process arrives in the sink is distributed equally to the edge with which a random walk arrives in the sink (whose probability equals the flow along that edge).
This is easily seen by considering a modified graph
\hat{G}
obtained by replacing any sink vertex
y
by a separate vertex
u_{xy}
for every incoming edge
(x,y)
.
The probability of arriving in
u_{xy}
(and hence of taking the edge
(x,y)
) is then
f_{xy}
for both the random walk and the elfs process.
Given that both processes behave exactly the same on
G
and
\hat{G}
, this must be the case on the original graph as well.

3.2
Electric hitting time

Next we consider the
electric hitting time
.
From an initial source vertex
s
and a fixed sink
M
, the electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
equals the expected number of samples before the elfs process hits the sink.
We discuss the electric hitting time on illustrative examples such as the path graph, the binary tree and the complete graph.
Then we analyze the electric hitting time using the algebraic expression from
Lemma

1
.
We show that a single electric flow sample reduces in expectation the (random walk) hitting time by a certain amount.
This proves for instance that
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2\mathrm{HT}_{s}
.
In the later
Part

II
we prove that the electric hitting on trees is logarithmic in the graph size and weights.

3.2.1
Examples

In the following we analyze the electric hitting time on some canonical (unweigthed) graph examples.

Path graph.

A simple yet key example is that of the elfs process on a path graph with one sink vertex (see
Fig.

2
in the intro).
The unit electric flow from a source vertex
s
to the sink
M
takes value
f_{xy}=1
on each of the edges between
s
and
M
.

A single electric flow sample from
s
will return an approximately uniform vertex between
s
and
M
.
This will repeatedly half the distance from
s
to
M
, and for a path of length
n
this yields an electric hitting time
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in\Theta(\log n)
.

As trivial as this example may seem, the situation becomes surprisingly more complicated when the sink includes
both
ends of the path.
4
4

4

Equivalently, consider a single sink vertex on a
cycle
graph.

It follows from our later bound for the electric hitting time on trees in
Theorem

1
that also this example has electric hitting time
O(\log n)
.

Complete graph.

Another insightful exercise is to analyze the electric hitting time on the complete graph.
Consider an initial source vertex
s
and a sink
M\subseteq V
.
By explicit calculation we find the probability
p
of hitting a sink vertex after a single electric flow sample from
s
.
Thanks to the symmetry of the complete graph, this probability is independent of
s
.
Hence, the elfs process effectively corresponds to a repeated Bernoulli experiment with success probability
p
.
The electric hitting time is then simply the number of trials until success, which is
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}=1/p
.

Figure 4
:
n
-vertex complete graph with source
s
and sink
M
. The electric hitting time from
s
to
M
is
\Theta(|M|(1-|M|/n))
.

It remains to calculate the probability
p
that an electric flow sample from
s
returns a sink vertex.
To this end, one can check that the unit electric flow from
s
to
M
is determined by the voltages
v_{s}=R_{s}=(|M|+1)/(n|M|)
and
v_{x}=1/(n|M|)
for
x\notin M
.
After some straightforward calculation this yields a success probability
p
that is

p=\frac{1}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y\in M}\sum_{x\notin M}f_{xy}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|M|+1}+\frac{|M|}{n}\right).

The electric hitting time is hence
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}=1/p\in\Theta(\min\{|M|,n/|M|\})
.
In contrast to the random walk hitting time, this shows that adding more vertices to the sink can actually
increase
the electric hitting time.

Expander graph.

Using a more qualitative argument, we see that a similar bound holds for an expander graph.
We use that for a
d
-regular expander it holds that
R_{x,M}\in\Theta(1/d)
,
\mathrm{HT}_{x}\in\Theta(n/|M|)
and
\mathrm{CT}_{x}\in\Theta(n)
.

It is now easy to see that
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in O(\min\{|M|,n/|M|\})
. The
O(n/|M|)
bound follows from the fact that
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\in O(\mathrm{HT}_{s})\in O(n/|M|)
.
The
O(|M|)
bound follows from the fact that the flow going into
M
can only spread over
O(d|M|)
edges, and that the effective resistance is always
\Theta(1/d)
.

3.2.2
What happens to the classical hitting time?

We can use the classical hitting time as a measure of “distance” from the sink. After one step of electric flow sampling, the expected hitting time of the classical random walk is (using
|\mathbf{1}\rangle
to denote the all ones vector)

\displaystyle\langle s|Q_{UU}VD|\mathbf{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle s|VD|\mathbf{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle s|R^{-1}(V*V)L_{UU}VD|\mathbf{1}\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle=\langle s|VD|\mathbf{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle s|R^{-1}(V*V)D|\mathbf{1}\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HT}_{s}-\frac{\mathrm{ET}_{s}}{2},

(13)

where we introduced the shorthand

\mathrm{ET}_{s}=\langle s|R^{-1}(V*V)D|\mathbf{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}.

(14)

We will call this quantity the “random walk escape time” from
s
, a name that we will justify in the next section.
So, in expectation, a single elf sample knocks off
\mathrm{ET}_{s}/2
from the expected hitting time.
The following bounds will be useful:

1\leq R_{s}d_{s}\leq\mathrm{ET}_{s}\leq\mathrm{HT}_{s}

(15)

Proof.

For the right hand side, we use that
v_{x}\leq R_{s}
for all
x
to show that

\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}\leq\sum_{x}v_{x}d_{x}=\mathrm{HT}_{s}
.
For the left hand side we have

\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}\geq\frac{1}{R_{s}}v_{s}^{2}d_{s}=R_{s}d_{s}\geq 1.\qed

With
\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\rho}\in M\}
the elfs process, it follows (and we can check directly) that:

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}=\mathbb{E}[\rho]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\rho-1}\mathrm{ET}_{Y_{j}}\right]=2\mathrm{HT}_{s},

(16)

so that the electric hitting time is at most twice the random walk hitting time.

3.2.3
What happens to the electric potential?

Alternatively, for any
s\notin M
, we can use the electric potential
\langle s|V
to measure our progress.
Take an arbitrary initial vertex
x\notin M
and let
y
be the sampled vertex, then we have that

\mathbb{E}_{x}[V_{sy}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\langle y|V|s\rangle]=\langle x|Q_{UU}V|s\rangle.

Using the expression
Q_{UU}=I_{UU}-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}(V\ast V)L_{UU}
this becomes

\mathbb{E}_{x}[v^{s}_{y}]=\langle x|V|s\rangle-\frac{\langle x|V|s\rangle^{2}}{2R_{x}}=v^{s}_{x}\left(1-\frac{v^{s}_{x}}{2R_{x}}\right).

For
x=s
this shows that after a single sample the potential halves in expectation.
Using that
v^{s}_{x}=v^{x}_{s}
and
v^{s}_{x}=R_{x}\mathbb{P}_{s}[\tau_{x}<\tau_{M}]
we can cleanly rewrite the expression as

\mathbb{E}_{x}[v^{s}_{y}]=v^{s}_{x}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{s}[\tau_{x}<\tau_{M}]\right).

4
Random walks

In this section we show that there exists a coupling between the elfs process and a random walk from source
s
to sink
M
.
This gives more insightful proofs for the facts that (i) the elfs process has the same arrival distribution as a random walk, and (ii) a single electric flow sample reduces the hitting time by
\mathrm{ET}_{s}
in expectation.
Then, we give an interpretation of this same quantity
\mathrm{ET}_{s}
as the random walk
escape time
, and use it to design random walk algorithms for estimating effective resistances.

4.1
Random walk coupling

Here we construct a coupling between a random walk from
s
to
M
and the elfs process, described by respective Markov chains

\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\rho}\in M\}.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\tau]=\mathrm{HT}_{s}
and
\mathbb{E}[\rho]=\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
.
We also proved (and will reprove) that both walks have the same arrival distribution, and so
\mathbb{P}[Y_{\rho}=y]=\mathbb{P}[X_{\tau}=y]
for all
y
.

The coupling is based on a simple random walk stopping rule
\nu
for which
X_{\nu}=Y_{1}
.
For a random walk starting from
s
, the rule
\nu
is the following: when visiting a vertex
x
, stop with probability

p_{x}=\frac{w_{xx^{\prime}}}{w_{xx^{\prime}}+d_{x}},\quad\text{ with }w_{xx^{\prime}}=\sum_{y}\left(\frac{v_{x}-v_{y}}{v_{x}}\right)^{2}w_{xy},

(17)

and otherwise continue.
This is equivalent to appending to every vertex
x\in U
a new sink vertex
x^{\prime}
through an edge with weight
w_{xx^{\prime}}
.
The main result of this section is the following lemma, whose proof we postpone to the end of the section.

Lemma 2

(Coupling lemma)
.

The probability that a random walk from
s
with stopping rule
\nu
stops at vertex
x
is

\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{\nu}=x]=\mathbb{P}_{s}[Y_{1}=x]=Q_{s,x}.

Moreover, the expected length of the stopping rule is
\mathbb{E}_{s}[\nu]=\mathrm{ET}_{s}/2
.

By repeatedly applying this stopping rule (until the random walk stops at a vertex in
M
), we obtain stopping times
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{\rho}=\tau
such that

\left(\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\},\,\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1}=X_{\nu_{1}},\dots,Y_{\rho}=X_{\nu_{\rho}}\in M\}\right)

describes a coupling between the random walk and the elfs process.
We illustrate this coupling in
Fig.

5
.

Figure 5
:
(identical to Figure
1
) Coupling between a random walk
\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\}
and an elfs process
\{Y_{0}=s,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\rho}\in M\}
through a stopping rule
\nu
.
From
s
this ensures that
X_{\nu}=Y_{1}
.

The coupling implies directly that the elfs process has the same arrival distribution as a random walk, reproving
Corollary

1
.
The coupling also gives an alternative proof for
Eq.

16
, which effectively states that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\rho}(\nu_{j}-\nu_{j-1})\right]=\mathbb{E}[\nu_{\rho}]=\mathbb{E}[\tau]=\mathrm{HT}_{s}.

We now give the proof of the coupling lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

2
(coupling lemma).

We consider the equivalent formulation of the stopping rule using a random walk on a modified graph
G^{\prime}
, which is obtained by adding to each non-sink vertex
x\in U
an additional sink vertex
x^{\prime}
with an edge
(x,x^{\prime})
of weight
w_{xx^{\prime}}
(as in
Eq.

17
).
Let
U^{\prime}
denote this new set of vertices.
If we run a random walk on
G^{\prime}
until it hits a vertex in the new sink
U^{\prime}\cup M
, then it will stop at a vertex
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
with arrival probability
\mathbb{P}[X_{\nu}=x]
(and it will stop at a vertex
y\in M
with probability
\mathbb{P}[X_{\nu}=y]
).

Figure 6
:
Modified graph
G^{\prime}
. The stopping rule
\nu
is equivalent to a random walk from
s
hitting a sink vertex in
U^{\prime}\cup M
.

Let
f^{\prime}
denote the unit electric flow on
G^{\prime}
from
s
to
U^{\prime}\cup M
.
We argue that this flow is determined by the voltages

v^{\prime}_{x}=v_{x}^{2}/(2R_{s})

for
x\in U
and
v^{\prime}_{x}=0
for
x\in U^{\prime}\cup M
.
Recalling that the voltages are the
unique
potentials that induce a unit flow from
s
to
U^{\prime}\cup M
, it suffices to prove that these voltages induce such a unit flow.
This follows from direct computation: for any
x\in U
we have that the outgoing flow is

\displaystyle\sum_{y}f^{\prime}_{xy}+f^{\prime}_{xx^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}(v^{\prime}_{x}-v^{\prime}_{y})w_{xy}+v^{\prime}_{x}w_{xx^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\frac{v_{x}^{2}-v_{y}^{2}}{2R_{s}}w_{xy}+\frac{v_{x}^{2}}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y}\left(\frac{v_{x}-v_{y}}{v_{x}}\right)^{2}w_{xy}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}\sum_{y}(v_{x}-v_{y})w_{xy}=\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}\delta_{sx}=\delta_{sx}.

For
y\in M
the incoming flow is

\sum_{x}f^{\prime}_{xy}=\sum_{x\in U}v^{\prime}_{x}w_{xy}=\sum_{x\in U}\frac{v_{x}^{2}}{2R_{s}}w_{xy}.

By
Eq.

10
this equals
Q_{sy}
.
As the incoming flow equals the arrival probability
\mathbb{P}[X_{\nu}=y]
, we have that
\mathbb{P}[X_{\nu}=y]=Q_{sy}
.
For
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
it is

f^{\prime}_{xx^{\prime}}=v^{\prime}_{x}w^{\prime}_{xx^{\prime}}=\frac{v_{x}^{2}}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y}\left(\frac{v_{x}-v_{y}}{v_{x}}\right)^{2}w_{xy}=\frac{1}{2R_{s}}\sum_{y}(v_{x}-v_{y})^{2}w_{xy}.

By
Eq.

10
this equals
Q_{sx}
, and hence
\mathbb{P}[X_{\nu}=x]=Q_{sx}
.
This proves the first statement of the lemma.

To prove the second statement, note that the expected length of the stopping rule
\nu
equals the expected number of edges traversed by the random walk between the “original” vertices
U\cup M
(i.e., excluding edges of the form
(x,x^{\prime})
), up until the random walk hits
U^{\prime}\cup M
.
By
Eq.

6
, the expected number of times that the random walk crosses such an edge
(x,y)
is
v^{\prime}_{x}w_{xy}
.
Summing this over all edges in the original graph we get

\sum_{x,y\in U\cup M}v^{\prime}_{x}w_{xy}=\sum_{x\in U}v^{\prime}_{x}d_{x}=\frac{1}{2R_{s}}\sum_{x\in U}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}=\frac{\mathrm{ET}_{s}}{2}.\qed

There exists a similar stopping rule for sampling
edges
.
A

\{\tilde{X}_{0}=s,\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{\tau}\in M\}
from

\tilde{X}_{i}=x

Z_{i}=e=(x,y)
with probability

, and let
\tilde{X}_{i+1}
be a random endpoint of

.
Now consider the edge stopping rule
\mu

p_{xy}=\frac{(v_{x}-v_{y})^{2}}{v_{x}^{2}+v_{y}^{2}}.

We have the following edge coupling lemma, the proof of which we defer to

.

Lemma 3

(Edge coupling lemma)
.

The probability that a random walk from

with stopping rule

stops at edge

is

\mathbb{P}_{s}[Z_{\mu}=e]=f_{e}^{2}/(R_{s}w_{e}).

4.2
Random walk escape time

For a random walk
\{X_{0}=s,X_{1},\dots,X_{\tau}\in M\}
from
s
to
M
, consider the quantity
\mathrm{esc}=1+\max\{t\mid X_{t}=s\}
, which denotes the time at which the random walk has left
s
for the final time.
In this section we prove the following lemma, with
v
the voltage in the unit electric flow from
s
to
M
.

Lemma 4

(escape time identity)
.

\mathbb{E}_{s}(\mathrm{esc})=\mathrm{ET}_{s}=\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}
.

Proof.

We prove this identity by combining two existing identities.
First, on a random walk from
s
to
M
, the expected number of visits to a vertex
x
is
v_{x}d_{x}
(
Eq.

7
).
Second, the probability that a walk starting at
x
hits
s
before
M
is
\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tau_{s}<\tau_{M}]=v_{x}/R_{s}
(
Eq.

9
).
Hence, a fraction
v_{x}/R_{s}
of the visits to
x
will return to
s
before
M
, and this gives a total of
v_{x}^{2}d_{x}/R_{s}
visits to
x
before the walk hits
s
for the final time.
Taking the sum over all
x
then gives the expected escape time
\mathbb{E}_{s}[\mathrm{esc}]=\frac{1}{R_{s}}\sum_{x}v_{x}^{2}d_{x}
.

More rigorously, we expand

\mathbb{E}_{s}[\mathrm{esc}]=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\ind(t<\mathrm{esc})\right]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{s}[t<\mathrm{esc}].

By rewriting

\mathbb{P}_{s}[t<\mathrm{esc}]=\sum_{x}\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{t}=x]\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tau_{s}<\tau_{M}]=\sum_{x}\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{t}=x]\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}},

this becomes

\mathbb{E}_{s}[\mathrm{esc}]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x}\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{t}=x]\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}=\sum_{x}\frac{v_{x}}{R_{s}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{t}=x].

By
Eq.

7
we have that
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{s}[X_{t}=x]=\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\ind[X_{t}=x]\right]=v_{x}d_{x}
, and this gives the claimed expression.
∎

For the interested reader, we give in
Appendix

A
an alternative proof of the escape time identity using an elegant tool called the
Doob transform
.

The escape time identity justifies the name
random walk escape time
for the quantity
\mathrm{ET}_{s}
, which we encountered previously.
Note that the escape time from
s
clearly satisfies the inequalities

1\leq|\{t\>|\>X_{t}=s\}|\leq\mathrm{esc}_{s}\leq\tau,

with
\tau
the hitting time from
s
to
M
.
Taking expectations, and using that
\mathbb{E}_{s}[|\{t\>|\>X_{t}=s\}|]=R_{s}d_{s}
(by
Eq.

9
), we find an alternative proof of the bounds in
Eq.

15
which state that

1\leq R_{s}d_{s}\leq\mathrm{ET}_{s}\leq\mathrm{HT}_{s}.

4.3
Approximating effective resistances

In this section we illustrate the use of the escape time in designing random walk algorithms.
We describe a classical algorithm that approximates the escape probability or effective resistance

using

We contrast this with a simpler algorithm that requires

The use of random walks to estimate graph parameters is an interesting area on itself, and we refer the interested reader to for instance
[

,

,

.
In the second half of the paper, we will discuss how these random walk algorithms parallel existing algorithms based on

At the end of this section, we formulate a conjecture stating that it is possible to produce an electric flow sample (approximately) from

4.3.1
Approximating the effective resistance

Here we describe two algorithms for estimating the random walk escape probability
p_{s}
from a source
s
and sink
M
, which is defined as

p_{s}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}[\tau_{M}<\tau^{+}_{s}],

with
\tau_{M}
the hitting time from
s
to
M
and
\tau^{+}_{s}
the return time from
s
back to
s
.
Recalling
Eq.

9
, this relates to the effective resistance
R_{s}
through the relation

p_{s}=\frac{1}{d_{s}R_{s}},

so that equivalently the algorithms estimate the effective resistance (when given
d_{s}
).
Both algorithms are based on random walks, and can be implemented space efficiently.

Hitting time algorithm.

The following algorithm has expected runtime
O(\mathrm{HT}_{s}/\varepsilon^{2})
and it returns an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
R_{s}d_{s}
(and hence the escape probability) with constant probability.

Hitting time algorithm for estimating
R_{s}d_{s}

Repeat for
i=1,2,\dots,k\in O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
:

1.

From source
s
, run a random walk until you hit
M
.
Let
S_{i}
be the number of visits to
s
during the walk.

Return
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}
.

We now argue that, with constant probability, the algorithm returns an approximation of
R_{s}d_{s}
with multiplicative error
\varepsilon
.
Using that
\mathbb{P}[\tau_{M}<\tau_{s}^{+}]=1/(R_{s}d_{s})=p_{s}
, we have that the random variable
S_{i}
corresponds to the number of trials until success of a Bernoulli variable with success probability
p_{s}
.
For any
i
we then have that
\mathbb{E}_{s}(S_{i})=1/p_{s}=R_{s}d_{s}
and
\mathbb{E}_{s}(S_{i}^{2})=O(1/p_{s}^{2})=O(R_{s}^{2}d_{s}^{2})
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, we have that the returned estimate
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}=(1\pm\varepsilon)R_{s}d_{s}
with constant probability.

Escape time algorithm

In Step 1. of the commute time algorithm, we run a random walk from
s
until it either returns to
s
or it hits
M
.
The statistic that we are interested in is the number of returns to
s
before hitting
M
.
However, this is equivalent to the number of returns to
s
before
escaping
from
s
.
Hence, we can actually interrupt the walk after
O(\mathrm{ET}_{s})
steps instead of
O(\mathrm{HT}_{s})
steps, and this can lead to a faster algorithm.

Given an upper bound
\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{ET}_{s}
, the following algorithm requires
O(\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}/\varepsilon^{3})
steps of a random walk.

Escape time algorithm for estimating
R_{s}d_{s}

Let
\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}\geq\mathrm{ET}_{s}
be an upper bound on the escape time.

Repeat for
i=1,2,\dots,k\in O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
:

1.

From source
s
, run
\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}/\varepsilon
steps of a random walk (absorbing in
M
).
Let
\hat{S}_{i}
be the number of visits to
s
during the walk.

Return
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{S}_{i}
.

To check correctness, first we check that
\hat{S}_{i}
is a good estimate for the actual number of returns
S_{i}
before hitting
M
.
To this end, note that
\hat{S}_{i}\leq S_{i}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

{}_{s}(S_{i}-\hat{S}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{esc}_{s}<\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\;\mathbb{E}\bigg(S_{i}-\hat{S}_{i}\;\Big|\;\mathrm{esc}_{s}<\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)+\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{esc}_{s}\geq\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\;\mathbb{E}\bigg(S_{i}-\hat{S}_{i}\;\Big|\;\mathrm{esc}_{s}\geq\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle=0+\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathrm{esc}_{s}\geq\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\bigg(S_{i}-\hat{S}_{i}\;\Big|\;\mathrm{esc}_{s}\geq\frac{\hat{\mathrm{ET}}_{s}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}(S_{i}).

Hence, we have
(1-\varepsilon)R_{s}d_{s}\leq\mathbb{E}(\hat{S}_{i})\leq R_{s}d_{s}
.
Furthermore
\hat{S}_{i}\leq S_{i}
implies that
\mathbb{E}(\hat{S}_{i}^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(S_{i}^{2})\in O(R_{s}^{2}d_{s}^{2})
.
By again applying Chebyshev’s inequality, we get that the mean over
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
samples from
\hat{S}_{i}
gives with constant probability an
\varepsilon
-multiplicative approximation to
R_{s}d_{s}
, and hence
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{S}_{i}=(1\pm\varepsilon)R_{s}d_{s}
.

4.3.2
Electric flow sampling with random walks

A different, potential application of the escape time is related to sampling from the electric flow from
s
to
M
, and thus generating a single step of the elfs process.
From the random walk coupling in
Section

4.1
it is clear that such an electric flow sample can be obtained from a random walk with a stopping rule whose expected length equals the escape time
\mathrm{ET}_{s}/2
.
However, the stopping rule in

requires exact knowledge of the electric vertex potentials in the electric
s
-
M

This seems hard to ensure in general, and hence the stopping rule as stated seems infeasible to actually implement.
Nevertheless, we conjecture that there does exist a random walk algorithm with a similar runtime, at least for
approximately
sampling from the elfs process.

Conjecture 1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to sample
\varepsilon
-approximately from the electric flow from
s
to
M
using
O(\mathrm{ET}_{s}\poly(1/\varepsilon))
random walk steps.

We anticipate that a resolution of this conjecture would have various applications.
One example is an open question from
[
AKP19
]
about sublinear algorithms for sampling according to the
\ell_{2}
-weights of the solution of a linear system (similar to quantum algorithms for linear system solving
[
HHL09
]
).
Since the electric flow can be interpreted as the solution of a linear system in the edge-vertex incidence matrix, a resolution of
1
implies a sublinear algorithm for sampling from the

-weights of this linear system.

Part II
Trees

In this part we study the electric hitting time on trees.
In
Section

3.2.1
we already discussed the
n
-vertex line with a sink on one end, whose electric hitting time is easily shown to be
O(\log n)
.
Extending this to a line with sinks on both ends is significantly more involved.
In this section, we show that in fact a similar, logarithmic bound on the electric hitting time holds for any trees.
This is captured by the following theorem, which is the main technical contribution of our work.

Theorem 1

(Electric hitting time on trees)
.

Let
T
be a tree,
s
an initial source vertex and
M\subseteq V
a sink.
For each
m\in M
, let
w_{m}
be the weight of the edge incident to
m
, and let
f_{m}
be the flow on this edge in the unit electric flow from
s
to
M
.
Then it holds that

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2+\sum_{m\in M}f_{m}\log\left(\frac{R_{s}w_{m}}{f_{m}^{2}}\right).

We mention some instructive ways of rewriting this bound.
If the tree is unweighted (and so
w_{m}=1
for all
m\in M
), then

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq 2+\log R_{s}+2\sum_{m\in M}f_{m}\log\left(\frac{1}{f_{m}}\right)=2+\log R_{s}+2H(\mu),

where
H(\mu)
is the entropy of the arrival distribution
\mu
at the sink vertices.
Since
R_{s}\leq n
and
H(\mu)\leq\log|M|
, we have
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}=O(\log n)
.

In the case of a weighted tree, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{EHT}_{s}

\displaystyle\leq 2+\log(R_{s}d_{M})+\sum_{m\in M}f_{m}\log\left(\frac{1}{f_{m}}\right)+\sum_{m\in M}f_{m}\log\left(\frac{w_{m}}{d_{M}f_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=2+\log(R_{s}d_{M})+H(\mu)-D(\mu||\sigma_{M})

where
d_{M}=\sum_{m\in M}w_{m}
and
D(\mu||\sigma_{M})\geq 0
is the relative entropy between the arrival distribution
\mu
and
\sigma_{M}
, the stationary distribution restricted to
M
, defined by
\sigma_{M}(m)=w_{m}/d_{M}
.
Using the bound
R_{s}<n/w_{\min}
, this is
O(\log(nw_{\max}/w_{\min}))
, with
w_{\max}
and
w_{\min}
the maximum and minimum edge weights, respectively.

We prove Theorem
1
using an inductive argument.
First, in
Section

5
, we introduce the notion of Schur complement for graphs and random walks, and we show that it also applies to the elfs process on trees.
This allows us to contract a part of the tree without affecting the elfs process on the uncontracted part.
Then, in
Section

6
, we use this to prove an inductive step that relates the number of steps of the elfs process in a subtree to that in smaller subtrees of that subtree.
The base case of the inductive argument corresponds to edges directly incident on the sink, which we can treat explicitly.

Quantum walks and electric networks

We can define quantum walks using much of the terminology introduced earlier.
While a random walk is defined over the vertex space, a quantum walk takes place over the edge space.
I.e., the state of a quantum walk is described by a quantum state
|\psi\rangle\in\ell(E)
.
It can be helpful to think about the basis state
|\psi\rangle=|x,y\rangle
as a quantum walk positioned on vertex
x
, but keeping a memory of the previous position
y
.

To describe the dynamics, we first define the unitary operators

D_{x}=2|\phi_{x}\rangle\langle\phi_{x}|-I\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigoplus_{x}D_{x}.

These describe reflections around the star state
|\phi_{x}\rangle
and the star subspace
\ell^{*}(E)
, respectively.
We also define the
\swap
operator by
\swap|xy\rangle=|yx\rangle
.
This operator describes a reflection around the symmetric subspace
\ell^{+}(E)
.
Following
[
Sze04
,
MNRS11
]
, the quantum walk operator is then defined as the product of reflections
\swap\cdot\bigoplus_{x}D_{x}
.
This structure is close to the structure of a random walk on a graph, where the reflection
\bigoplus_{x}D_{x}
mixes up the second (memory) register of the walk, while the
\swap
operator corresponds to an actual move along the resulting edge.

In this work, we use a slight variation called the
absorbing
quantum walk, as in
[
Bel13
,
Pid19
]
.
For a source vertex
s
and sink
M
, it is defined as

U=\swap\cdot\bigoplus_{x\notin\{s,M\}}D_{x}.

This corresponds to a product of reflections around
\ell_{s,M}^{*}(E)\coloneqq\linspan\{|\phi_{x}\rangle\mid x\notin\{s,M\}\}
and
\ell^{+}(E)
, based on which we can easily characterize the invariant subspace of

.

8.1
Quantum elfs

From last section it is clear that if we can project the initial state

The algorithm and its analysis largely follow that of Piddock

Algorithm 1

1.

.

2.

Measure the phase estimation register.
If the estimate returns “0”, output the resulting state.
Otherwise, go back to step 1.

Step 1. can be implemented with

8.2
Quantum elfs process

We can implement the elfs process by repeatedly using the quantum walk sampling algorithm (and, in fact, this was the initial inspiration).

Lemma 11
.

\mathrm{EHT}_{s}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\rho-1}\sqrt{\mathrm{ET}_{Y_{j}}}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{HT}_{s}\,\mathrm{EHT}_{s}}\leq 2\,\mathrm{HT}_{s}.

The first inequality follows trivially from the fact that
\mathrm{ET}_{x}\geq 1

\mathbb{E}[\rho]=\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
.
The second inequality is an application of Jensen’s inequality (Lemma
14

16

The third inequality follows from the fact that

∎

The expected number of quantum walk steps is hence upper bounded by twice the random walk hitting time.
If the electric hitting time is small (as is the case for trees), then it is almost quadratically smaller than the hitting time.

Taking into account the errors, we conjecture that there exists a quantum walk algorithm that samples from a distribution
\varepsilon
-close to the elfs process with a runtime of the form

A particular appeal of this is that it is always upper bounded by the hitting time (see also next section).
Falling short of proving this, we do prove a slightly weaker bound.

Theorem 3

Given upper bounds

9
Applications

In this final section we discuss some direct applications of the quantum elfs process.

9.1
Quantum walk search

There is a vast literature on what is called
quantum walk search
, which is the use of quantum walks on graphs to find distinguished or “marked” vertices
[
AGJ21
]
.
The key idea is that in different scenarios quantum walks can have a quadratically improved hitting time as compared to random walks.
This can be interpreted as a local or graph-theoretic analogue of the quadratic speedup in Grover’s quantum search algorithm.
Despite the long line of progress, many key questions remain unanswered.
What can we say about the arrival distribution of a quantum walk?
Can quantum walks always achieve a speedup over random walks?

Our work sheds new light on these questions.
On the one hand, we describe a quantum walk search algorithm that retains the same arrival distribution as a random walk.
This contrasts with former works, where typically nothing could be said about the quantum walk arrival distribution.
Moreover, the random walk arrival distribution plays a critical role in numerous algorithms, from sampling random spanning trees
[
Bro89
]
and semi-supervised learning
[
ZGL03
]
to linear system solving
[
CS15
]
.
Our work opens an avenue towards quantum walk speedups for all of these problems.

On the other hand, we make progress on the question whether quantum walks can always achieve a quantum speedup over the random walk hitting time.
If we have upper bounds
\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}
and
\hat{\mathrm{EHT}}_{s}
on the hitting time and electric hitting time, respectively, then
Theorem

3
shows how to find a marked element
\varepsilon
-close to the arrival distribution in
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}}\mathrm{EHT}_{s}^{3/2}/\varepsilon^{2})
quantum walk steps.
We also conjectured that there exists a quantum walk algorithm with runtime
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\hat{\mathrm{HT}}_{s}\mathrm{EHT}_{s}}/\poly(\varepsilon))
.
This is always upper bounded by the hitting time (up to polylog-factors and
\varepsilon
-dependency), and is close to a quadratic speedup when the electric hitting time is small.

A differentiating factor with former quantum walk search algorithms is that our quantum elfs process requires upper bounds on the hitting time and electric hitting time.
This seems unavoidable if we want to sample a marked element from the right arrival distribution.
If, however, we do not care about the arrival distribution, then we do not need upper bounds on
\mathrm{HT}_{s}
and
\mathrm{EHT}_{s}
as we show in the following theorem.

Edge coupling lemma

Here we prove the edge coupling lemma, which we recall below.

Proof.

Similar to the (vertex) coupling rule (

\mu
