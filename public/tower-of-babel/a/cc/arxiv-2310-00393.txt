New SDP Roundings and Certifiable Approximation for Cubic Optimization
Jun-Ting Hsieh, Pravesh K. Kothari, Lucas Pesenti, Luca Trevisan

Abstract
We give new rounding schemes for SDP relaxations for the problems of maximizing cubic polynomials over the unit sphere and the n n -dimensional hypercube. In both cases, the resulting algorithms yield a O ⁡ ( n / k ) O(\sqrt{n/k}) multiplicative approximation in 2 O ⁡ ( k ) ​ poly ⁡ ( n ) 2^{O(k)}\operatorname{poly}(n) time. In particular, we obtain a O ⁡ ( n / log ⁡ n ) O(\sqrt{n/\log n}) approximation in polynomial time. For the unit sphere, this improves on the rounding algorithms of [ BGG+17 ] that need quasi-polynomial time to obtain a similar approximation guarantee. Over the n n -dimensional hypercube, our results match the guarantee of a search algorithm of Khot and Naor [ KN08 ] that obtains a similar approximation ratio via techniques from convex geometry. Unlike their method, our algorithm obtains an upper bound on the integrality gap of SDP relaxations for the problem and as a result, also yields a certificate on the optimum value of the input instance. Our results naturally generalize to homogeneous polynomials of higher degree and imply improved algorithms for approximating satisfiable instances of Max-3SAT. Our main motivation is the stark lack of rounding techniques for SDP relaxations of higher degree polynomial optimization in sharp contrast to a rich theory of SDP roundings for the quadratic case. Our rounding algorithms introduce two new ideas: 1) a new polynomial reweighting based method to round sum-of-squares relaxations of higher degree polynomial maximization problems, and 2) a general technique to compress such relaxations down to substantially smaller SDPs by relying on an explicit construction of certain hitting sets. We hope that our work will inspire improved rounding algorithms for polynomial optimization and related problems.

1.1
Our results

In this paper, we design new rounding algorithms for SDP relaxations of cubic polynomial optimization and as a consequence, obtain improved guarantees for polynomial optimization over both the unit sphere and the hypercube. Our first result gives a rounding algorithm for the
canonical
sum-of-squares relaxation for homogeneous cubic optimization.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal, see
Theorems

3.3
and
5.1
)
.

For every
k\geqslant 6,n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an
n^{O(k)}
-time rounding algorithm for the canonical degree-
k
sum-of-squares relaxation of homogeneous cubic maximization problem (over both the unit sphere and the hypercube) that achieves an
O(\sqrt{n/k})
-multiplicative approximation.

This relaxation was first analyzed in
[
BGG+17
]
to obtain similar guarantees to
Theorem

1.1
but only for the case of the unit sphere. Their rounding algorithm is based on significantly different ideas relying on “weak decoupling” inequalities and involve reasoning about eigenvectors of the SDP solution. In particular, such techniques seem to have no natural analogs over the Boolean hypercube. As a result, prior to our work, there were no known bounds on the integrality gap of the sum-of-squares relaxations of the foundational problem of homogeneous polynomial optimization over the hypercube.

Our techniques in fact extend naturally to homogeneous polynomial optimization for all
odd
-degree polynomials (see
Appendix

B
). Handling
even
degree polynomials is related to the inherent (and well-known) issue of non-existence of decoupling inequalities and is an outstanding open question.
Informally speaking, decoupling inequalities (
Lemmas

2.9
and
B.1
) allow us to assume that the underlying objective function is a
tripartite
polynomial (i.e., the variables can be partitioned into three classes such that each non-zero monomial uses one variable from each class) with only a constant factor difference in the optimum. Such a fact is simply not true for homogeneous even-degree polynomials (see
Example

B.2
). Indeed, this is the key reason why
[
BGG+17
]
considers the problem of maximizing the
absolute
value of a polynomial instead, in which case decoupling inequalities are known for all degrees
[
HLZ10
]
.
We note (see
Appendix

B
) that for maximizing the absolute value of a polynomial, our techniques naturally extend to homogeneous polynomials of all degrees.

Compressing SDPs.

Our second main result shows that the sum-of-squares relaxation can be

from

Theorem 1.2

(Informal, see
Theorems

4.1
and
5.2
)
.

For every
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
-time algorithm that takes input a homogeneous multilinear cubic polynomial

in
n

O(\sqrt{n/k})
-approximation to the optimum of
f
over the hypercube or the unit sphere.
Moreover, our algorithm is obtained by rounding an SDP relaxation of the cubic optimization problem.

Theorem

1.2
improves the approximation guarantees of
[
BGG+17
]
. In particular, we obtain an
O(\sqrt{n/\log n})
approximation algorithm in polynomial time, as opposed to quasi-polynomial time in
[
BGG+17
]
. In contrast to the prior works on pruning SoS relaxations, the proof of

1.2

Over the
n
-dimensional hypercube, an
O(\sqrt{n/\log n})
-approximation algorithm for homogeneous cubic maximization was given by Khot and Naor
[
KN08
]
. Their algorithm relies on ideas from convex geometry and involves a randomized reduction to the classical bipartite quadratic optimization problem. Our algorithm for

[

]
.

Finally, we note that while prior works
[

,
BRS11
]
obtain smaller SDPs by

that showed a degree reduction result for some applications in robust statistics where one can take a high degree SoS relaxation and get a new SDP with additional variables but a lower degree with a similar performance. However, the result of
[

utilizes problem-specific structure and does not appear to be directly relevant to our applications in this work.

Certification algorithms and application to 3SAT.

As a key consequence of our new rounding algorithms, we obtain
certificates
of upper bounds on the true maximum of a homogeneous cubic objective. This is because rounding algorithms immediately imply upper bounds on the integrality gaps of the underlying SDP relaxations. It is often desirable (both from a practical, e.g. SAT solving, and theoretical standpoint, e.g. refutation problems in average-case complexity) to ask for algorithms that produce certificates on the optimum of an optimization problem.
We note that the problem of finding certifiable approximation algorithms for cubic maximization was formulated by Trevisan in his blog post
[
Tre
]
. We refer the reader to the post for additional discussion.

In
Section

6
, we demonstrate a concrete advantage of our new certification algorithm by using it to obtain an improved rounding algorithm for
satisfiable
3SAT instances:

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a polynomial-time randomized algorithm that, given a satisfiable 3SAT formula with
n
variables, finds with high probability an assignment satisfying a
(\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{3}{4}}))
-fraction of the clauses.

For comparison, the best known result of Håstad and Venkatesh
[
HV04
]
gives
\frac{7}{8}+\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right)
(via random assignments), which is in general polynomially worse as
m
can be
\Omega(n^{3})
. On the lower bound side, achieving approximation
\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
is NP-hard
[
Hås01
]
.

Note that the fraction of satisfied constraints of a 3SAT formula can be written as
\frac{7}{8}+f(x)
, where
f(x)
is a
non-homogeneous
degree-3 polynomial (the advantage over random assignment).
Thus, due to the degree-2 part of
f
, the naive approach of decoupling and optimizing the degree-3 part of
f
(e.g., using
Theorem

1.1
) does not work.
Our main idea is that even though
f
has components of degree 1 and 2, they behave nicely when the instance is satisfiable (see
6.2
).
This allows us to add extra constraints to the sum-of-squares relaxation that enforce the niceness of the degree-2 part (see
Algorithm

1
).
We remark that this is an example of the benefit of designing
certifiable
approximation algorithms using SDPs, as it allows us to add additional constraints for specific applications.

We leave as future work the problem of achieving value
\frac{7}{8}+\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
for 3SAT, which would match our result for the degree-3 homogeneous case.

1.2
Technical overview

Our rounding algorithm.

Our first main idea is a new rounding scheme based on
polynomial reweightings
(introduced in
[
BKS17
]
) that at once replicates the guarantees of
[
BGG+17
]
on the unit sphere while naturally extending (in fact, with essentially no change!) to the hypercube.
Let us elaborate on this new scheme of roundings next.

Our rounding works with
decoupled
polynomials — cubic polynomials of three separate classes of variables
x,y,z
such that the input polynomial can be written as
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}q_{i}(y,z)
for some quadratic polynomial
q_{i}\coloneqq q_{i}(y,z)=y^{\top}T_{i}z
. Via classical decoupling inequalities (
Lemma

2.9
), working with such polynomials is without loss of generality (at the cost of losing only an absolute constant factor in the approximation ratio).

Informally speaking, an SDP solution is described by a linear operator
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
that maps low-degree polynomials into real numbers so that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[p^{2}]\geqslant 0
for every
p
that is low-degree (see
Section

2.1
). In other words, the linear operator “pretends” like an expectation of a probability distribution over the unit sphere/hypercube for the purpose of taking expectations of low-degree polynomials.

Consider the value that an SDP solution assigns to the input polynomial
f
. In particular, if it so happens that
\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[q_{i}]|
is at least an
O(\sqrt{k/n})
-fraction of the SDP objective, then by using the Grothendieck inequality (
2.5
) to round
y
and
z
into integral
\overline{y},\overline{z}
and setting
x_{i}
to be
\sign(q_{i}(\overline{y},\overline{z}))
, we obtain an
O(\sqrt{k/n})
-approximation to the optimum. Now, this reasoning relies on the SDP solution
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
giving a large value to
\sum_{i}|q_{i}|
, that is,
\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[q_{i}]|
being large.

Of course, there is no reason for such a property to hold for the actual SDP solution we compute.
Our key idea is to show that there is an efficiently computable transformation from the initial
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
into a new
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}
with the property that 1)
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}
continues to have an objective value as large as the one we began with, and 2)
\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}[q_{i}]|
is at least an
O(\sqrt{k/n})
-fraction of the SDP objective.
This transformation is obtained by “reweighting” (a generalization of “conditioning” procedure first used in
[
BKS17
]
for rounding high-degree sum-of-squares SDP relaxations) the original solution
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
by an appropriate sum-of-squares polynomial
r(x)
. In fact,
r(x)
is chosen to be an appropriate random polynomial in our analysis. Informally speaking, if one thinks of the SDP solution as an actual probability distribution over
x,y,z
, then our reweighting can be thought of as shifting mass around a randomly chosen direction in
x
to ensure that the
\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}[q_{i}]|
becomes large enough.

Compression via hitting sets for halfspaces.

Constructing the compressed SDP for
Theorem

1.2
involves adding some constraints that enforce a certain appropriate variant of
anti-concentration
inequalities to hold for all projections of the SDP solution. Such inequalities are implied by the constraints in a degree-
k
sum-of-squares relaxation, but would cost an
n^{O(k)}
-bound in the size of the resulting SDP. Our main observation is that we can obtain the relevant anti-concentration inequalities by explicitly forcing constraints on an appropriate “hitting set” for halfspaces. To implement this, we need an efficient construction of a set of
2^{k}n^{O(1)}
points from
\{-1,1\}^{n}
(respectively,
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
elements of the unit sphere) such that for every vector
w
there is at least one element
x
of the hitting set such that
\langle x,w\rangle\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{k/n})\|w\|_{1}

(respectively, at least one element
x
such that

\langle x,w\rangle\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{k/n})\|w\|_{2}
). The desired condition occurs with probability
2^{-\Theta(k)}
for a Rademacher (respectively, Gaussian) choice of
x
, so the required hitting set property is satisfied by the support of any pseudorandom generator that
\varepsilon
-fools all linear threshold functions for
\varepsilon=2^{-\Theta(k)}
. To obtain the compressed SDPs we desire, we need
\varepsilon
-PRGs (or even
\varepsilon
-Hitting Set Generators) for all linear threshold functions with optimal seed length
O(\log n+\log(1/\varepsilon))
. However, the best known seed length for an efficient, explicit construction is
O(\log n+\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon))

[
MZ10
]
, which amounts to a hitting set of size
n^{O(\log n)}
in our setting. Instead, we give an elementary construction of a set as above with nearly optimal size that yields us our compressed SDP.

2.1
Sum-of-Squares SDPs

We refer the reader to the monograph
[
FKP19
]
and the lecture notes
[
BS16
]
for a detailed exposition of the sum-of-squares method and its usage in algorithm design.

A
degree-
\ell
pseudo-distribution

\mu
over variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
corresponds to a linear operator
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}
that maps polynomials of degree
\leqslant\ell
to real numbers and satisfies
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,1=1
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p^{2}]\geqslant 0
for every polynomial
p(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
of degree
\leqslant\ell/2
. We say that such a pseudo-distribution satisfies the
hypercube
constraints if
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[px_{i}^{2}]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p]
for every polynomial
p
of degree
\leqslant\ell-2
and every
i\in[n]
. We say that such a pseudo-distribution satisfies the
unit sphere
constraints if
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}p]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p]
for every
p
of degree
\leqslant\ell-2
.

Given a polynomial
p
(with the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the coefficients being
\lVert p\rVert_{1}
) over
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
, a pseudo-distribution of degree
\ell
over the unit sphere or the hypercube that maximizes
p
within an additive
\varepsilon\lVert p\rVert_{1}
error can be found in time
n^{O(\ell)}\operatorname{polylog}(n/\varepsilon)
via the ellipsoid method.

Reweighting.

Given a pseudo-distribution
\mu
over the unit sphere or the hypercube, a
reweighting
of
\mu
by a sum-of-squares polynomial

satisfying
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[q]>0
is a pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
that maps any polynomial

to
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}[p]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[pq]/\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[q]
. For any
\mu
of degree
\ell
and

of degree

,
\mu^{\prime}
is a pseudo-distribution of degree at least
\ell-r
. Furthermore, if
\mu
satisfies the unit sphere (or the hypercube) constraints then so does
\mu^{\prime}
as long as
r\leqslant\ell-2
.

A

The

p_{S}\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}

\mu
, we have
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f^{2t-2}\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f^{2t}\right]^{\frac{t-1}{t}}
.

2.2
Basic rounding algorithms

We state here three standard rounding algorithms for the quadratic case.

Fact 2.5

(Grothendieck rounding
[
AN06
]
)
.

There is a rounding algorithm that, given any degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu
over
x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, outputs
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\overline{x}^{\top}M\overline{y}\geqslant\frac{1}{K_{G}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[x^{\top}My\right]
,
where
K_{G}<1.783
is the Grothendieck constant.

Lemma 2.6

(Lossless rounding on the unit sphere)
.

Given any degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu
over
x\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
, there is a rounding algorithm that outputs
\overline{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
such that for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\overline{x}^{\top}M\overline{x}\geqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[x^{\top}Mx\right]
.

Proof.

Let
X=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[xx^{\top}\right]
and let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}
be its eigen-decomposition with
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
and
v_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Since
\mu
is over the unit sphere,
\operatorname{tr}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
, hence
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
defines a valid probability distribution.
Now if we sample
\overline{x}=v_{i}
with probability
\lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, we get

\mathbb{E}[\overline{x}^{\top}M\overline{x}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}v_{i}^{\top}Mv_{i}=\langle X,M\rangle=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[x^{\top}Mx\right]
.
In particular one of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
must have quadratic form at least
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[x^{\top}Mx\right]
.
∎

Theorem 2.7

(Charikar-Wirth rounding
[
CW04
]
)
.

Given any degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu
over
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, for any
T>0
, there is a polynomial-time sampleable distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with zero diagonal entries,

\mathbb{E}_{\overline{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\overline{x}^{\top}M\overline{x}\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[x^{\top}Mx\right]-8e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}|M_{ij}|\,.

In particular, if
\mu
is an optimal pseudo-distribution for the degree-2 SoS relaxation of
\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}x^{\top}Mx
, then by picking
T=\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\overline{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\overline{x}^{\top}M\overline{x}\right]\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}x^{\top}Mx\,.

2.3
Anti-concentration

We will need the following anti-concentration result that can be deduced from standard hypercontractivity (see e.g.
[
AGK04
, Lemma 3.2]
).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the following: there exists a constant
B>0
such that
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[p(x)^{4}]\leqslant B^{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[p(x)^{2}\right]^{2}
for every degree-
d
polynomial
p
.
Then, for any degree-
d
polynomial
p
,

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[p(x)>\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\,p\right]\geqslant 2^{-\frac{4}{3}}B^{-d}\,.

Relevant special cases for what follows are when
\mathcal{D}
is the uniform distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
or the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
, which both satisfy the assumption with
B=9
(see e.g. for reference
[
O’D14
, Theorem 9.21]
and
[
Bog98
, Theorem 1.6.2]
).

2.4
Decoupling

A standard technique for polynomial optimization problems is
decoupling
, which relates the optimum of
\langle T,x^{\otimes 3}\rangle
(the “coupled” polynomial) to the optimum of
\langle T,x\otimes y\otimes z\rangle
(the “decoupled” polynomial). For polynomial optimization over the
n
-dimensional hypercube or the unit sphere, these two quantities are within a constant factor from each other when the polynomial has
degree 3
.
Thus, in the rest of the paper, we will assume that the given polynomial is already decoupled.

Lemma 2.9

(Decoupling
[
KN08
,
HLZ10
]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be either
\{-1,1\}^{n}
or
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
.
Consider a multilinear homogeneous degree-3 polynomial in
n
variables
f(x)=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}T_{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}
(where
T
is a symmetric
3
-tensor). Consider also the decoupled version of
f
:
\widetilde{f}(x,y,z)=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
.
Then,

\max_{x\in\Omega}f(x)\geqslant\frac{2}{9}\cdot\max_{x,y,z\in\Omega}\widetilde{f}(x,y,z)\,.

In
Appendix

B
, we prove a generalization of
Lemma

2.9
to all
odd-degree
polynomials.

2.5
The algorithm of Khot and Naor

In this section, we describe the algorithm of Khot and Naor
[
KN08
]
yielding an approximation of
O(\sqrt{n/\log n})
to the maximum of
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
over
x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
The original algorithm was done via a reduction to estimating the
L_{1}
-diameter of some convex body.
We reformulate the algorithm in a more direct way, which also serves as inspiration for our algorithms in
Section

3
:

1.

Sample
\overline{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Let
f_{\overline{x}}(y,z)\coloneqq\langle T,\overline{x}\otimes y\otimes z\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{x}_{i}(y^{\top}T_{i}z)
be the (decoupled) degree-2 polynomial in variables
y
and
z
. Here
T_{i}
is a slice of the tensor
T
, that is, an
n\times n
matrix.

2.

Using Grothendieck rounding (
2.5
), find
\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
which achieve a constant factor approximation for
\max_{y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f_{\overline{x}}(y,z)
.

3.

Finally, repeat steps 1 and 2
\operatorname{poly}(n)
times and output the best solution
(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})
obtained.

The key lemma to analyze this algorithm is the following anti-concentration inequality.

Lemma 2.10

(Lemma 3.2 of
[
KN08
]
)
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, there is a constant
c(\delta)>0
such that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
are i.i.d.
\{\pm 1\}
random variables, then

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\geqslant\sqrt{\frac{\delta\log n}{n}}\cdot\|a\|_{1}\right]\geqslant\frac{c(\delta)}{n^{\delta}}\,.

The proof of Khot and Naor’s result easily follows from
Lemma

2.10
.
Indeed, let
x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be an optimal solution.
First observe that it is always optimal to set
x^{*}_{i}\coloneqq\textnormal{sgn}(\langle T_{i},y^{*}\otimes z^{*}\rangle)
, so that
\mathsf{OPT}\coloneqq f(x^{*},y^{*},z^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle T_{i},y^{*}\otimes z^{*}\rangle|
. Then, for any
\overline{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, the algorithm outputs
(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})
such that

\displaystyle f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\max_{y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(\overline{x},y,z)\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot f(\overline{x},y^{*},z^{*})\,.

However, by
Lemma

2.10
, with at least inverse polynomial probability, a random
\overline{x}
satisfies

f(\overline{x},y^{*},z^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{x}_{i}\langle T_{i},y^{*}\otimes z^{*}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle T_{i},y^{*}\otimes z^{*}\rangle|=\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}\,.

Thus, by repeating
\operatorname{poly}(n)
times, with high probability the algorithm outputs an assignment that has value
\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
.

3
Rounding SoS relaxations for cubic optimization

In this section, we present our rounding algorithms for the canonical degree-
k
SoS relaxation of cubic optimization over the hypercube.

•

In
Section

3.1
, we show how to achieve approximation
O(\sqrt{n})
by rounding the canonical degree-
6
SoS relaxation via polynomial reweightings.

•

In
Section

3.2
, we extend the previous argument to achieve approximation
O(\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}})
by rounding the canonical degree-(
6k
) SoS relaxation. The additional key idea is a sum-of-squares proof of a certain anti-concentration argument.

3.1
An
O(\sqrt{n})
-factor approximation

We start by giving a simple polynomial-time certification and rounding algorithm via constant-degree SoS that achieves approximation
O(\sqrt{n})
for cubic optimization over the hypercube. Our key technical ingredient is a new use of polynomial reweightings of pseudo-distributions (see
Lemma

3.2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

For any decoupled homogeneous degree-3 polynomial
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
, the degree-6 SoS relaxation of
\max_{x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x,y,z)

has integrality gap at most
O(\sqrt{n})
.

Furthermore, there is a polynomial-time rounding algorithm that, given a degree-6 pseudo-distribution
\mu
with
\mathsf{SOS}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}f(x,y,z)>0
, outputs a solution
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with value
f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{\mathsf{SOS}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
.

We describe another algorithm outputting a certificate with similar guarantees in
Appendix

A
. In comparison, the proof in this section will come together with a rounding and will allow us to build up towards a more general tradeoff between time and approximation in
Section

3.2
.

Recall from
Section

2.5
that the strategy of
[
KN08
]
is to first sample
\overline{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and then solve for
\overline{y}
and
\overline{z}
using Grothendieck rounding.
One might expect that a similar strategy works to round an optimal SoS solution.
However, given an optimal pseudo-distribution
\mu
, it is not clear how
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{x}_{i}(y^{\top}T_{i}z)
relates to the SoS value
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(y^{\top}T_{i}z)
.
In fact, the former can be much smaller than the latter or even zero.

Denote
q_{i}(y,z)\coloneqq y^{\top}T_{i}z
for convenience.
Our key idea is that even though
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle
may be small, we can reweight the pseudo-distribution
\mu
and get another pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
such that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle\gtrsim(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle^{2})^{1/2}
. Furthermore, the quantity on the right-hand side can be related to the SoS value (for a typical
\overline{x}
).
One may view this procedure as raising the (pseudo-) expectation of a random variable to be close to its (pseudo-) standard deviation, which is reminiscent of the scalar fixing lemma of
[
BKS17
]
.

We capture this idea in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a degree-
t
polynomial and let
\mu
be a degree-
3t
pseudo-distribution over
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
There is reweighting of
\mu
by a degree-
2t
polynomial such that the resulting degree-
t
pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
satisfies

\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}[p]\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\cdot\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]}\,.

Proof.

Let
m\coloneqq\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]}>0
. We can assume that
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p]\right|<\frac{m}{3}
, otherwise we are done without any reweighting.

First, suppose that
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{3}\right]\right|\geqslant\frac{m^{3}}{3}
. Reweight
\mu
by the degree-
2t
SoS polynomial
p^{2}
and let
\mu^{\prime}
be the resulting pseudo-distribution. Then we have
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}[p]\right|=\left|\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{3}\right]}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]}\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\cdot\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]}
.

Now suppose that
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{3}\right]\right|<\frac{m^{3}}{3}
. Reweight
\mu
by the degree-
2t
SoS polynomial
(p+m)^{2}
and let
\mu^{\prime}
be the resulting pseudo-distribution. Note that:

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[(p+m)^{2}\right]=2m^{2}+2m\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p\right]\in\left[\frac{4m^{2}}{3},\frac{8m^{2}}{3}\right]\,,

where we used
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p]\right|<\frac{m}{3}
. In particular, we are reweighting by a polynomial with non-zero pseudo-expectation, so this is a well-defined operation. Moreover,

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[(p+m)^{2}p\right]\geqslant 2m\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]-\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{3}\right]\right\rvert-m^{2}\cdot\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[p]\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{4m^{3}}{3}\,.

Putting everything together, we obtain
\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}[p]\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2}\right]}
.

Thus, we get the desired result in both cases.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
q_{i}(y,z)\coloneqq y^{\top}T_{i}z
for each
i\in[n]
.
For simplicity of notation, we will drop the dependence on
y,z
and denote
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
.
Then, we have

\mathsf{SOS}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[x_{i}q_{i}]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[q_{i}^{2}]}\leqslant\sqrt{n\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[q_{i}^{2}]}=\sqrt{n\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|q\|_{2}^{2}}\,,

by Cauchy-Schwarz and its pseudo-expectation version (
2.3
).
Next, since
\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\langle v,h\rangle^{2}=\|v\|_{2}^{2}
is a polynomial identity,

\mathsf{SOS}^{2}\leqslant n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,\langle q,h\rangle^{2}\,,

(1)

where we recall that
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
are degree-2 polynomials in
y,z
.
We now describe the rounding algorithm.

1.

Sample
h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, and set
\overline{x}\coloneqq h
.

2.

Reweight the pseudo-distribution
\mu
via
Lemma

3.2
to get a degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
such that
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2}}
.

3.

Use Grothendieck rounding (
2.5
) on
\mu^{\prime}
to obtain solutions
\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
satisfying
\overline{y}^{\top}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}T_{i})\overline{z}\geqslant\frac{1}{K_{G}}\cdot\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|
(we can get the guarantees with the absolute value by flipping the sign of
h
).

First, note that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2}
is a degree-2 polynomial in
h
, so by Paley-Zygmund inequality,

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left[\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2}\geqslant\frac{\mathsf{SOS}^{2}}{n}\right]\geqslant\Omega(1)\,,

meaning that we get a “good”
h
with constant probability. For a good
h
, it also holds that

f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\frac{1}{K_{G}}\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{3K_{G}}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2}}\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{\mathsf{SOS}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\,.

Thus, repeating the above
\operatorname{poly}(n)
times, we can obtain a solution with value
\Omega\left(\frac{\mathsf{SOS}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
with high probability.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Going beyond
O(\sqrt{n})
-approximation via higher-degree SoS

We now switch to a general time/approximation tradeoff for the problem by rounding higher levels of the SoS hierarchy.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
k,n
be integers such that
2\leqslant k\leqslant n
.
For any decoupled homogeneous degree-3 polynomial
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
, the canonical degree-
(6k)
SoS relaxation of
\max_{x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x,y,z)
has integrality gap at most
O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\Big)
.

Furthermore, there is an
n^{O(k)}
-time rounding algorithm that, given a degree-
(6k)
pseudo-distribution with
\mathsf{SOS}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}f>0
, outputs a solution
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with value
f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
.

Recall that the
\sqrt{n}
approximation factor in the previous section was coming from relating the SoS value
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle x,q\rangle
to a quantity of the form
\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle h,q\rangle^{2}
. To make use of higher levels of the SoS hierarchy, we will now connect the SoS value to higher moments of the form
\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle h,q\rangle^{2k}
. The proof of
Theorem

3.3
will then follow from a high-degree version of the polynomial reweighting from
Lemma

3.2
.

One can interpret the inequality from the previous section
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle x,q\rangle^{2}\leqslant n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle h,q\rangle^{2}
as the SoS analog of the inequality
\|q\|_{1}^{2}\leqslant n\cdot\|q\|_{2}^{2}=n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\langle h,q\rangle^{2}
that holds for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by Cauchy-Schwarz and an explicit variance equality. Our higher-level proof also has a classical analog, namely:

\|q\|_{1}\leqslant O(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\langle h,q\rangle^{2k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}\,.

(2)

Such an inequality holds for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
Mon90
]
. To see that, decompose
q
and
h
into
k
(arbitrary) blocks
q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}
and
h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(t)}
of size roughly
\frac{n}{k}
. By Paley-Zygmund inequality, we get that
\left\lvert\langle q^{(i)},h^{(i)}\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\left\|q^{(i)}\right\|_{2}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\left\|q^{(i)}\right\|_{1}
holds with at least constant probability for any fixed
i\in[k]
. So with probability at least
2^{-O(k)}
we have
\left\lvert\langle q,h\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|q\|_{1}
, which in turn implies
Equation

2
.

Although this proof is streamlined, the part using Paley-Zygmund and independence across the
k
blocks does not directly translate into a sum-of-squares proof. We now give a different and degree-
O(k)
sum-of-squares proof of the inequality.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
k<n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be indeterminates and
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
be such that each
v_{i}
is a polynomial of degree
\leqslant t
. Then,

\left\{x_{i}^{2}=1,\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}\ \mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.80215pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2(t+1)k}$}}}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,v}$}}}$}}}\ \mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left[\langle h,v\rangle^{2k}\right]\geqslant\left(\frac{k}{4n}\right)^{k}\langle x,v\rangle^{2k}\,.

Proof.

We divide
[n]
into
k
blocks, each of size at most
\lceil\frac{n}{k}\rceil
.
For
t\in[k]
, let
x^{(t)},v^{(t)}
be the vectors
x,v
restricted to the
t
-th block. Then,

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.80215pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2(t+1)k}$}}}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,v}$}}}$}}}\ \langle x,v\rangle^{2k}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle\right)^{2k}\leqslant 2^{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle\right)^{2k}\,,

(3)

since
(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle)^{2k}
is a square for each
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
.
Expanding the above and using the fact that all odd moments of
\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{k}
vanish, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle\right)^{2k}=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:|\gamma|=k}c_{\gamma}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2\gamma_{t}}\,,

(4)

where
c_{\gamma}\coloneqq\frac{(2k)!}{\prod_{t=1}^{k}(2\gamma_{t})!}
.
Here
|\gamma|=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma
represents a multiset of
[k]
of size
|\gamma|
.
Next, by SoS Cauchy-Schwarz (
2.2
), we have that

\left\{x_{i}^{2}=1,\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}\ \mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{27.38199pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-27.38199pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[27.38199pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2(t+1)}$}}}\hskip-27.38199pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[27.38199pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,v}$}}}$}}}\ \langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\|x^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|v^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\left(\frac{2n}{k}\right)\cdot\|v^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\,,

since
x^{(t)}
has dimension at most
\lceil\frac{n}{k}\rceil\leqslant\frac{2n}{k}
.
Next, using the identity
\|v^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{h^{(t)}}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2}
where
h^{(t)}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{\dim(x^{(t)})}
,

\displaystyle\left\{x_{i}^{2}=1,\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}\ \mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.80215pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2(t+1)k}$}}}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,v}$}}}$}}}\ \prod_{t=1}^{k}\langle x^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2\gamma_{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{t=1}^{k}\left(\frac{2n}{k}\right)^{\gamma_{t}}\|v^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2\gamma_{t}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2n}{k}\right)^{k}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\left(\mathbb{E}_{h^{(t)}}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2}\right)^{\gamma_{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{2n}{k}\right)^{k}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{h^{(t)}}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2\gamma_{t}}\,,

where the last inequality uses
2.2
.
Combining the above with
Equations

3
and
4
, we have

\left\{x_{i}^{2}=1,\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}\ \mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.80215pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2(t+1)k}$}}}\hskip-31.80215pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.80215pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,v}$}}}$}}}\ \langle x,v\rangle^{2k}\leqslant\left(\frac{4n}{k}\right)^{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:|\gamma|=k}c_{\gamma}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2\gamma_{t}}\,.

Finally, since
h^{(1)},\dots,h^{(k)}
are uniformly random Boolean vectors, multiplying
h^{(t)}
by
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\{\pm 1\}
does not change the distribution.
Thus, applying
Equation

4
again, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{h}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:|\gamma|=k}c_{\gamma}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle^{2\gamma_{t}}=\mathbb{E}_{h}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle\right)^{2k}=\mathbb{E}_{h}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k}\langle h^{(t)},v^{(t)}\rangle\right)^{2k}=\mathbb{E}_{h}\langle h,v\rangle^{2k}\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Our second key ingredient is the analog of
Lemma

3.2
for high moments.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
p
be a degree-
t
polynomial in variables
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\mu
be a degree-
(2k+2)t
pseudo-distribution.
There is a degree-
2kt
reweighting of
\mu
such that the resulting pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
satisfies

\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}p\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}\,.

Proof.

Let
m\coloneqq\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}>0
.

First, consider reweighting
\mu
by the degree-
2kt
sum-of-squares polynomial
p^{2k}
and denote by
\mu_{1}
the resulting pseudo-distribution. Then, we have
\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{1}}\left[p\right]\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k+1}\right]\right\rvert}{m^{2k}}
. We are done if this is larger than
\frac{m}{3}
, hence it remains to handle the case
\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k+1}\right]\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{m^{2k+1}}{3}
.

Now, reweight
\mu
by
p^{2k-2}
and denote by
\mu_{2}
the resulting pseudo-distribution. Note that by the pseudo-distribution version of Cauchy-Schwarz (
2.3
), as long as
\mu
is a degree-
(2k+2)t
pseudo-distribution,

0<\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k}\right]^{2}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k+2}\right]\,,

so that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]>0
and the reweighting is well-defined. Furthermore, we have
\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{2}}\left[p\right]\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-1}\right]\right\rvert}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]}
. Once again, we are done if this is larger than
\frac{m}{3}
, so we assume from now on that
\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-1}\right]\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{m}{3}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]
.

Finally, we consider the reweighting of
\mu
by the SoS polynomial
(p+m)^{2}p^{2k-2}
and call
\mu_{3}
the resulting pseudo-distribution. We have:

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[(p+m)^{2}p^{2k-2}\right]=m^{2k}+2m\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-1}\right]+m^{2}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]\in\left(0,\frac{8m^{2k}}{3}\right]\,,

where we also use
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-2}\right]\leqslant m^{2k-2}
(which follows from
2.4
). In particular, the reweighting for
\mu_{3}
is well-defined. Similarly, we have

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[(p+m)^{2}p^{2k-1}\right]\geqslant 2m^{2k+1}-\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k+1}\right]\right\rvert-m^{2}\cdot\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[p^{2k-1}\right]\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{4m^{2k+1}}{3}\,.

Thus,
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{3}}\left[p\right]\geqslant\frac{m}{2}
holds in this case, which concludes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove

3.3
.

.

Similarly to the proof of

q_{i}=q_{i}(y,z)=y^{\top}T_{i}z

By

∎

4
Polynomial-size SDPs via compressed SoS relaxations

This section is dedicated to the proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k,n
be integers such that
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. There is a
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
-time certification algorithm that, given a decoupled homogeneous degree-3 polynomial
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{i}z_{k}
achieves
O(\sqrt{n/k})
-approximation to
\mathsf{OPT}\coloneqq\max_{x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x,y,z)
.
Moreover, there is a corresponding rounding algorithm running in
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
time that outputs a solution
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with value
f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
.

Roughly, we will proceed by “compressing” the SDP relaxations analyzed in
Section

3
. We will use some explicit hitting set of size
2^{k}n^{O(1)}
and use it to define some constant-degree SoS relaxations with
2^{k}n^{O(1)}
variables and one additional axiom.

4.1
The blockwise construction of the hitting set

Before explaining how to write down the relaxations, we describe the construction of our hitting set over a small sample space that “fools” high moments in every direction. We will mimic the anti-concentration proof from
Section

3.2
by decomposing the
n
-dimensional vectors into
k
blocks.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k
divides
n
.
Define the distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\widehat{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
as follows.

1.

Sample
\widehat{b}
from a 4-wise independent distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{\frac{n}{k}}
, that is, let
\widehat{b}=f(s)
, where
f:\{\pm 1\}^{r}\to\{\pm 1\}^{\frac{n}{k}}
is a 4-wise independent pseudorandom generator with seed
s\sim\{\pm 1\}^{r}
and
r=O(\log n)
.

2.

Sample
\widehat{c}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{k}
independently of
\widehat{b}
.

3.

Let
\widehat{x}\coloneqq\widehat{c}\otimes\widehat{b}
. In other words, decompose
\widehat{x}
into
k
blocks
\widehat{x}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{x}^{(k)}
of size
\frac{n}{k}
and set
\widehat{x}^{(i)}\coloneqq\widehat{c}_{i}\cdot\widehat{b}
for all
i\in[k]
.

The following observation can be deduced for example from the classical construction of
k
-wise independent sets of random variables
[
Jof74
]
.

Claim 4.3
.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
can be obtained as the uniform distribution over a sample space of size
2^{k}n^{O(1)}
. In particular, for any
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\widehat{x}=x\right]\geqslant 2^{-k}n^{-O(1)}
.

We will also need the following result, which is a direct consequence of the Paley-Zygmund inequality and the 4-wise independence of
\widehat{b}
.

Claim 4.4
.

For all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n}{k}}
,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\widehat{b}}\left[\left\lvert\langle\widehat{b},w\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{2}\right]\geqslant\Omega(1)
.

Finally, the following lower bound on the moments of
\widehat{x}
will be the key ingredient to prove that our relaxation provides a correct certificate to the optimum.

Lemma 4.5

(Large moments in every direction)
.

For all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle^{2k}\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}n^{-O(1)}\|w\|_{1}^{2k}\,.

Proof.

We first decompose
w
into
k
blocks
w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(k)}
of size
\frac{n}{k}
, in such a way that
\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{c}_{i}\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle
. Now for any fixed block
i\in[k]
, we know from
4.4
that with at least constant probability over
\widehat{b}
, it holds that

|\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\|w^{(i)}\|_{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|w^{(i)}\|_{1}
,
where the last inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz. In turn, by linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{b}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle\right|\right]\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|w\|_{1}\,.

In particular, there exists some
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
satisfying
|\langle x,w\rangle|\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|w\|_{1}
. By
4.3
, this
x
must be drawn with probability at least
2^{-k}n^{-O(1)}
from
\mathcal{D}
. Finally, we apply Markov’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle^{2k}\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}\|w\|_{1}^{2k}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[|\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle|\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|w\|_{1}\right]\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}n^{-O(1)}\|w\|_{1}^{2k}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

4.2
Proof of
Theorem

4.1

We are now ready to state and analyze the SDP relaxation. The high-level intuition is the following: write
q_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j,k}T_{ijk}y_{j}z_{k}
for all
i\in[n]
, so that our goal is now to maximize
\langle x,q\rangle
, which by symmetry is equivalent to maximizing
\langle x,q\rangle^{2}
. Instead of maximizing over
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
we essentially pick a random
\widehat{x}
from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
that has large
2k
-th moments in every direction. Then we replace the objective function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\max_{w}\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2}
by the following proxy:

\max_{\mu\text{ pseudo-distribution on $w$}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2(k+1)}}{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2k}}\,.

As
k
grows, this yields a sequence of increasingly better approximations leveraging higher moments of the variables. Since expanding the
2k
-th powers would require solving an SDP of size
n^{\Omega(k)}
, we introduce auxiliary variables
\{M_{\widehat{x}}\}
corresponding to
\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{k}
in combinatorial solutions.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Assume without of loss of generality that
k
divides
n
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the pseudorandom distribution from
Definition

4.2
. Furthermore, we fix a guess
\alpha\geqslant 0
for the value of the optimum of the cubic optimization problem (the final certification and rounding algorithms will be obtained by binary searching for the best possible value of
\alpha
).

The relaxation.

We solve for
feasibility
the degree-12 SoS program over the following variables:

•

variables
y_{j}
and
z_{k}
for all
j,k\in[n]
. To lighten notations we let
q_{i}\coloneqq q_{i}(y,z)=\sum_{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}y_{j}z_{k}
for all
i\in[n]
(each
q_{i}
is a degree-2 polynomial) and write
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
.

•

variables
M_{x}
for each
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
.

and under the following additional polynomial constraints:

\displaystyle y_{j}^{2}=1\quad\text{for all $j\in[n]$}\,,

\displaystyle z_{k}^{2}=1\quad\text{for all $k\in[n]$}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[M_{\widehat{x}}^{2}\left(\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)\right]\geqslant 0\,.

(5)

By construction of
\mathcal{D}
, the relaxation has
2^{k}n^{O(1)}
variables and constraints.

The rounding algorithm.

First, we check that any feasible solution to the SoS program can be rounded into an integral solution
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
achieving value
\Omega(\alpha)
. Suppose that there exists some degree-12 pseudo-distribution
\mu
(over
y
,
z
and
M_{x}
) satisfying all the constraints. Then by
Equation

5
, there exists
\overline{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
satisfying

\alpha^{2}\leqslant\frac{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}M_{\overline{x}}^{2}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle^{2}}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}M_{\overline{x}}^{2}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle^{2}\,,

where
\mu^{\prime}
is the degree-6 pseudo-distribution obtained by reweighting
\mu
by the SoS polynomial
M_{\overline{x}}^{2}
. We now use
Lemma

3.2
to construct from
\mu^{\prime}
a degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime\prime}
that satisfies

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle^{2}\leqslant 9\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle\right)^{2}
.

Finally we use Grothendieck rounding (
2.5
) on
\mu^{\prime\prime}
to find
\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\frac{1}{K_{G}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}f(\overline{x},y,z)=\frac{1}{K_{G}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}\langle\overline{x},q\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\alpha\,.

Approximation factor.

Our final algorithm consists of a binary search to get the largest value of
\alpha\geqslant 0
that makes the SoS program above feasible. Then, some explicit multiple of
\alpha
coming from the analysis of our rounding provides a correct upper bound certificate on
\mathsf{OPT}
.

We now check that this achieves approximation
O(\sqrt{n/k})
. Fix any triplet
x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and let
q_{i}^{*}=\sum_{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}y_{j}^{*}z_{k}^{*}
for all
i\in[n]
. Suppose that
(x^{*},y^{*},z^{*})
achieves the optimum of the original problem, so that
\mathsf{OPT}=\langle x^{*},q^{*}\rangle=\|q^{*}\|_{1}
. We set
(y,z)=(y^{*},z^{*})
and
M_{x}=\langle x,q^{*}\rangle^{k}
for all
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
, and we prove that this defines a feasible solution. By Hölder’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k+2}\geqslant(\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k})^{\frac{2k+2}{2k}}
, and
Lemma

4.5
then yields

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}M_{\widehat{x}}^{2}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}M_{\hat{x}}^{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k+2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k}}\geqslant\left(\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k}\right)^{1/k}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{k}{n}\cdot n^{-O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)}\mathsf{OPT}^{2}\,.

Assume without loss of generality that
k=\Omega(\log n)
(since otherwise, one can always increase
k
to
\Theta(\log n)
without affecting the target runtime). Then, as long as
\alpha\leqslant O(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
,
Equation

5
is satisfied. This completes the proof.
∎

5
Optimization over the unit sphere

In this section, we prove the approximation results for cubic optimization over the unit sphere matching our results over the hypercube:

•

In
Section

5.1
, we show that the canonical degree-
6k
sum-of-squares relaxation has integrality gap
O(\sqrt{n/k})
by describing an appropriate rounding algorithm.

•

In
Section

5.2
, we prove that a pruned SDP can achieve approximation
O(\sqrt{n/k})
in time
2^{O(k)}\text{poly}(n)
.

5.1
Analysis of the canonical degree-
k
SoS relaxation

We prove that the canonical degree-
k
SoS relaxation for optimizing over the unit sphere has integrality gap at most
O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\Big)
. The proof mirrors the hypercube case (
Theorem

3.3
), although the analysis is much simpler here since we can directly relate the SoS value to the moments of the Gaussian distribution.

Theorem 5.1
.

Fix
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
.
Given any decoupled homogeneous degree-3 polynomial
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
, the canonical degree-
6k
SoS relaxation of
\max_{x,y,z\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}f(x,y,z)
has integrality gap
O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}\Big)
.
Furthermore, given any degree-
6k
pseudo-distribution
\mu
over
\left(\mathcal{S}^{n-1}\right)^{3}
such that
\mathsf{SOS}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}f>0
,
there is an
n^{O(k)}
-time randomized rounding algorithm that outputs with high probability
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
such that
f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, we start by defining
q_{i}=q_{i}(y,z)=y^{\top}T_{i}z
and consider

\mathsf{SOS}^{2k}=\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle x,q\rangle\right)^{2k}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle x,q\rangle^{2k}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[\|x\|_{2}^{2k}\cdot\|q\|_{2}^{2k}\right]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|q\|_{2}^{2k}\,,

using Cauchy-Schwarz and the pseudo-expectation version of Hölder’s inequality (
2.4
).

Then, let
h\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
. We know from standard estimates on the moments of the Gaussian distribution that for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{E}_{h}\langle h,v\rangle^{2k}=c_{k}\|v\|_{2}^{2k}
, where
c_{k}=(2k-1)!!\geqslant(k/2)^{k}
.
Thus, we have

\mathsf{SOS}^{2k}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|q\|_{2}^{2k}\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{k}\right)^{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2k}\,.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2k}
is a degree-
(2k)
polynomial in
h
, by
Lemma

2.8
,
h
satisfies
\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{k})\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
with probability at least
2^{-O(k)}
.
Moreover, with probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
we have
\|h\|_{2}\leqslant O(\sqrt{n})
.

Hence, our rounding algorithm goes as follows,

1.

Sample
h\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and let
\overline{x}=\frac{h}{\|h\|_{2}}
.

2.

Reweight the pseudo-distribution
\mu
via
Lemma

3.5
to obtain a degree-2 pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
such that
\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\Big(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle q,h\rangle^{2k}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2k}}
.

3.

Use the lossless rounding for quadratic forms over the sphere (
Lemma

2.6
) on
\mu^{\prime}
to obtain solutions
\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
such that
\overline{y}^{\top}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}T_{i})\overline{z}\geqslant\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|
(we can flip the sign of
h
to get the guarantee with the absolute value).

Putting everything together, it holds with probability at least
2^{-O(k)}
that

f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})=\frac{1}{\|h\|_{2}}\overline{y}^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}T_{i}\right)\overline{z}\geqslant\frac{1}{\|h\|_{2}}\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle q,h\rangle\right|\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\mathsf{SOS}\,.

Repeating this
\operatorname{poly}(n,2^{k})
times, we obtain a rounding algorithm that satisfies the desired guarantees with high probablity.
∎

5.2
Analysis of compressed SoS relaxations over the unit sphere

In this section, we prove the analogous statement of
Theorem

4.1
over the sphere.

Theorem 5.2
.

Fix
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. There is a
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
-time certification algorithm that given a decoupled homogeneous degree-3 polynomial
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{i}z_{k}
achieves
O(\sqrt{n/k})
-approximation to

\mathsf{OPT}\coloneqq\max_{x,y,z\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}f(x,y,z)\,.

Moreover, there is a corresponding rounding algorithm running in
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
time that outputs a solution
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
with value
f(\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z})\geqslant\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\Big)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
.

Our proof relies on a hitting set construction analogous to
Lemma

4.5
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
with
k=\Omega(\log n)
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
supported on at most
2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}
vectors such that for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle^{2k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2k}}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\|w\|_{2}\,.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
k
divides
n
. We define the distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
as follows.

1.

Sample
\widehat{b}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\frac{n}{k}}
from a 4-wise independent distribution. Similarly to
4.3
, this can be achieved by taking the uniform distribution over a subset of
\{\pm 1\}^{\frac{n}{k}}
of size
n^{O(1)}
.

2.

Sample independently
\widehat{c}
uniformly over an
\varepsilon
-net of
\mathcal{S}^{k-1}
of size
O(1/\varepsilon)^{k}
for
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{10}
.

3.

Output
\widehat{x}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\widehat{c}\otimes\widehat{b}
. Note in particular that
\widehat{x}
is a unit vector.

Decompose
w
in
k
blocks
w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(k)}
of size
\frac{n}{k}
such that
\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\sum_{i}\widehat{c}_{i}\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle
.

4.4
states that for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant\frac{n}{k}
,

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\widehat{b}}\left[\left\lvert\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\|w^{(i)}\|_{2}\right]\geqslant\Omega(1)\,.

This implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\widehat{b},w^{(i)}\rangle^{2}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\|w\|_{2}^{2}
. On the other hand, for any
b\in\{\pm 1\}^{\frac{n}{k}}
, we can find
c=c(b)
in the
\varepsilon
-net such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\langle b,w^{(i)}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle b,w^{(i)}\rangle^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Therefore, there must exist
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
such that
\langle x,w\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\|w\|_{2}
, and this
x
is drawn with probability at least
2^{-O(k)}n^{-O(1)}
. Finally, by Markov inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle^{2k}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left(|\langle\widehat{x},w\rangle|\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\|w\|_{2}\right)\cdot\Omega\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}\|w\|_{2}^{2k}

\displaystyle=2^{-O(k)}n^{-O(1)}\Omega\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}\|w\|_{2}^{2k}\,.

This completes the proof assuming
k=\Omega(\log n)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

5.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

The proof is identical to the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, with the following exceptions:

•

the boolean constraints
y_{j}^{2}=1
and

for all

become spherical constraints:
\|y\|_{2}^{2}=1
and

.

•

instead of Grothendieck’s rounding we use the (lossless) rounding for quadratic forms over the sphere from

2.6
.

•

if

achieve the optimum for the cubic maximization problem, then
\mathsf{OPT}=\|q^{*}\|_{2}
, where

for all

.

•

the last sequence of inequalities in the proof of

4.1
becomes as follows after using

:

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[M_{\widehat{x}}^{2}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[M_{\hat{x}}^{2}\right]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k+2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k}}\geqslant\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q^{*}\rangle^{2k}\right)^{1/k}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{k}{n}\cdot n^{-O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)}\mathsf{OPT}^{2}\,.

6
Rounding algorithm for 3SAT

In this section, we consider 3SAT formulas where each 3-tuple of variables appears at most once, i.e. there are no two clauses with the same set of variables.
Håstad and Venkatesh
[
HV04
]
used an anti-concentration result of
[
AGK04
]
to prove that any 3SAT formula with
m
clauses has value at least
\frac{7}{8}+\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
(which is achieved by a random assignment with probability
\Omega(\frac{1}{m})
).

We prove the following improvement over this result:

Theorem 6.1

(Restatement of
Theorem

1.3
)
.

There is a polynomial-time randomized algorithm that, given a satisfiable 3SAT formula with
n
variables, finds with high probability an assignment satisfying a
(\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{3}{4}}))
-fraction of the clauses.

Notations.

A 3SAT clause
C
with variables
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
and negation pattern
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}
can be written as

\psi_{C}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\frac{7}{8}-\frac{1}{8}(\sigma_{1}x_{1}+\sigma_{2}x_{2}+\sigma_{3}x_{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}x_{1}x_{2}+\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}x_{2}x_{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}x_{1}x_{3}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})\,.

Here we adopt the convention that
-1
is
\mathsf{True}
and
+1
is
\mathsf{False}
, and we can see that
\psi_{C}(x)=0
if
\sigma_{1}x_{1}=\sigma_{2}x_{2}=\sigma_{3}x_{3}=+1
, and
\psi_{C}(x)=1
otherwise.
Thus, a 3SAT formula can be represented as a function
\psi:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to[0,1]
,

\psi(x)=\frac{7}{8}+f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)+f_{3}(x)\,,

where
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
are homogeneous polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

Observe that
\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}|f_{1}(x)|,\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}|f_{2}(x)|\leqslant 3/8
, and
\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}|f_{3}(x)|\leqslant 1/8
. In particular, this last statement implies the following crucial observation:

Observation 6.2
.

If
x^{*}
is a satisfying assignment, then
f_{1}(x^{*})+f_{2}(x^{*})\geqslant 0
.

Before proceeding to describing the algorithm, we show the following version of degree-3 decoupling that augments the guarantees of
Lemma

2.9
by controlling also the degree-1 and degree-2 parts:

Lemma 6.3

(Recoupling)
.

Given
x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, there exists a polynomial-time sampleable distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

1.

For any degree-3 homogeneous multilinear polynomial
f(x)=\sum_{i,j,k\in[n]}T_{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}
(where
T
is a symmetric
3
-tensor) and the corresponding decoupled polynomial
\widetilde{f}(x,y,z)=\sum_{i,j,k\in[n]}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
,

\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(x^{\prime})]=\frac{2}{9}\cdot\widetilde{f}(x,y,z)\,.

2.

For any degree-2 homogeneous multilinear polynomial
g(x)=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}M_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}
,

\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}[g(x^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{9}\cdot(g(x)+g(y)+g(z))\,.

3.

\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x^{\prime}\right]=0
.

Proof.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
can be sampled as follows:

•

Sample
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
independently and uniformly in
\pm 1
and let
b_{3}=b_{1}b_{2}
. Then
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
has a pairwise independent distribution and
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}=1
.

•

Independently for each
i\in[n]
, sample
x_{i}^{\prime}
uniformly in the multiset
\{b_{1}x_{i},b_{2}y_{i},b_{3}z_{i}\}
.

Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(x^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{b_{1}x_{i}+b_{2}y_{i}+b_{3}z_{i}}{3}\cdot\frac{b_{1}x_{j}+b_{2}y_{j}+b_{3}z_{j}}{3}\cdot\frac{b_{1}x_{k}+b_{2}y_{k}+b_{3}z_{k}}{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{27}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}\cdot(x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}+x_{i}z_{j}y_{k}+x_{j}y_{i}z_{k}+x_{j}y_{k}z_{i}+x_{k}y_{j}z_{i}+x_{k}y_{i}z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{9}\cdot\widetilde{f}(x,y,z)\,.

Similarly, for degree-2 homogeneous multilinear polynomials,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[g(x^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}M_{ij}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{b_{1}x_{i}+b_{2}y_{i}+b_{3}z_{i}}{3}\cdot\frac{b_{1}x_{j}+b_{2}y_{j}+b_{3}z_{j}}{3}\right]=\frac{1}{9}\cdot(g(x)+g(y)+g(z))\,.

The third part follows similarly.
∎

The algorithm.

We now describe the algorithm that achieves the guarantees of
Theorem

6.1
. We define
\delta\coloneqq\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}\log n}
(for some small constant
c>0
to be picked at the end of
Lemma

6.7
).

Algorithm 1

(3SAT)
.

Input:

A 3SAT instance in
n
variables:
\psi(x)=\frac{7}{8}+f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)+f_{3}(x)
where
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
are homogeneous polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

Output:

An assignment
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with value
\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{3}{4}})
with high probability.

Operation:

1.

Solve for feasibility the following two degree-2 SoS programs over variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
:

(a)

with axioms
x_{i}^{2}=1
for all
i\in[n]
,
f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\geqslant 0
, and
f_{1}(x)>\delta
;

(b)

with axioms
x_{i}^{2}=1
for all
i\in[n]
,
f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\geqslant 0
, and
f_{1}(x)<-\delta
.

If none of these programs is feasible, move to the next case.

Otherwise, let
\mu
be either a feasible degree-2 pseudo-distribution for (a), or the negation of a feasible degree-2 pseudo-distribution for (b).

•

Sample
g\sim\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}(x-\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x)(x-\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x)^{\top})
.

•

For all
i\in[n]
, set
\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{g_{i}}{T}
if
|g_{i}|\leqslant T
and
\overline{x}_{i}=\text{sign}(g_{i})
otherwise (for some parameter
T=T(n)>0
to be chosen in
Lemma

6.4
).

•

Sample
x^{(1)}_{i}=1
with probability
\frac{1+p\overline{x}_{i}}{2}
and
x^{(1)}_{i}=-1
with probability
\frac{1-p\overline{x}_{i}}{2}
, independently for all
i\in[n]
(for some parameter
p=p(n)>0
to be chosen in
Lemma

6.4
).

2.

Get
x^{\prime}
by optimizing
f_{2}
using the second part of
Theorem

2.7
.
Then, set
x^{(2)}=x^{\prime}
or
x^{(2)}=-x^{\prime}
depending on which of the two has higher value.

3.

Let
\widetilde{f_{3}}(x,y,z)
be the decoupled polynomial of
f_{3}(x)
.

•

Run the degree-6 SoS relaxation of cubic optimization of
\widetilde{f}_{3}
in variables
x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
with the additional axioms
f_{2}(y)\geqslant-\delta
and
f_{2}(z)\geqslant-\delta
.

•

Sample
\overline{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and reweight the pseudo-distribution
\mu
as in the algorithm of
Theorem

3.1
.

•

Apply Charikar-Wirth rounding (
Theorem

2.7
) on the reweighted
\mu^{\prime}
to get
\overline{y},\overline{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

•

Finally, obtain
x^{(3)}
by recoupling
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}
via
Lemma

6.3
.

4.

Pick the best assignment among
x^{(1)},x^{(2)},x^{(3)}
.

5.

Repeat the steps 1 to 4
\text{poly}(n)
times and output the best assignment obtained.

The first three steps in the algorithm correspond to each of the degree-1, 2, or 3 part being large. On a high level, our strategy is quite natural. If the degree-2 part is large, we use the classical roundings for degree-2 polynomials. If the degree-3 part is large, then we use our rounding for decoupled degree-3 polynomials from
Section

3
. In those two cases, we get an assignment with value
\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. If the degree-1 part is large, we introduce a different algorithm based on a degree-2 SoS relaxation with additional axioms. Our rounding in this case is inspired by the proof of
Theorem

2.7
and uses an additional idea to make the degree-3 part negligible. In this last case, we get an assignment with value
\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{3}{4}})
.

We analyze separately these three cases now.

Lemma 6.4

(Large degree-1 part)
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a degree-2 pseudo-distribution such that:

\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]\right|\geqslant\delta\,,\quad\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\right]\geqslant 0\,.

Then
x^{(1)}
has value
\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{3}{4}})
.

Proof.

First, up to negating the pseudo-distribution (which does not affect the degree-2 part), we can assume without loss of generality that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]\geqslant\delta
.

The rounding proceeds in two steps. We introduce some parameters
p=p(n)\in[0,1]
and
T=T(n)>0
to be fixed later.

1.

Sample a Gaussian with mean and covariance given by the degree-2 pseudo-distribution:
g\sim\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}(x-\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x)(x-\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x)^{\top})
. Then, let
\overline{x}\in[-1,1]^{n}
be defined as follows: for each
i\in[n]
, let
\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{g_{i}}{T}
if
|g_{i}|\leqslant T
and
\overline{x}_{i}=\text{sign}(g_{i})
otherwise.

2.

Use
\overline{x}
as a bias for sampling
x^{(1)}
: sample for each
i\in[n]
independently:
x_{i}^{(1)}=1
with probability
\frac{1+p\overline{x}_{i}}{2}
and
x^{(1)}_{i}=-1
with probability
\frac{1-p\overline{x}_{i}}{2}
.

We now analyze this rounding. Define

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\overline{x}_{i}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}g_{i},\quad\Delta_{ij}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{x}_{i}\overline{x}_{j}\right]-\frac{1}{T^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}g_{j}\right]\,.

Then, at the end of the first step, we have (here,
\|f_{1}\|_{1}
and
\|f_{2}\|_{1}
denote the sum of the absolute value of the coefficients of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(\overline{x})\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{T}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]-\|f_{1}\|_{1}\cdot\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\Delta_{i}|\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{2}(\overline{x})\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{2}(x)\right]-\|f_{2}\|_{1}\cdot\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}|\Delta_{ij}|\,.

(7)

Claim 6.5
.

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\Delta_{i}|
and
\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}|\Delta_{ij}|
are both at most
O(1)\cdot e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{8}}
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[n]
. First,

\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|\leqslant T}-1)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\text{sign}(g_{i})\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T}\right]\,.

Since
\mu
is a degree-2 pseudo-distribution over the hypercube,
g_{i}
is a Gaussian with mean
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}x_{i}\in[-1,1]
and variance 1. Define
p_{i}\coloneqq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|g_{i}|>T)\leqslant e^{-T^{2}/4}
(this holds provided
T
is a large enough constant). By the triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

|\Delta_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T}\right]\right|+p_{i}\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\sqrt{2p_{i}}+p_{i}\leqslant O(1)\cdot e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{8}}\,.

Fix now
i,j\in[n]
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}g_{j}\left(\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|\leqslant T,|g_{j}|\leqslant T}-1\right)\right]+\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\text{sign}(g_{i})g_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T,|g_{j}|\leqslant T}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\text{sign}(g_{j})\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|\leqslant T,|g_{j}|>T}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\text{sign}(g_{i})\text{sign}(g_{j})\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T,|g_{j}|>T}\right]\,.

Note that
(g_{i},g_{j})
is a 2-dimensional Gaussian with marginal means bounded by 1 in absolute value and
|\mathbb{E}[g_{i}g_{j}]|=|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[x_{i}x_{j}]|\leqslant 1
by
2.3
. Hence, using once again the triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle|\Delta_{ij}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{i}g_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T\text{ or }|g_{j}|>T}]\right|+\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{j}|\mathbf{1}_{|g_{i}|>T}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|g_{i}|\mathbf{1}_{|g_{j}|>T}\right]\right)+p_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}g_{j}^{2}\right]\left(p_{i}+p_{j}\right)}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{T}\left(\sqrt{p_{i}}+\sqrt{p_{j}}\right)+p_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant O(1)\cdot e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{8}}\,.\qed

Since
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
come from a 3SAT instance with
m\leqslant n^{3}
clauses, we have
\|f_{1}\|_{1},\|f_{2}\|_{1}\leqslant O(n^{3})
. Thus, if we pick
T=\sqrt{48\log n}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(\overline{x})+f_{2}(\overline{x})\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{48\log n}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{48\log n}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{2}(x)\right]-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\geqslant-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\,,

where we used our assumption
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\right]\geqslant 0
(together with the fact that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{1}(x)\right]\geqslant 0)
.
Next, at the end of second step, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(x^{(1)})\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(\overline{x})\right],\quad\mathbb{E}\left[f_{2}(x^{(1)})\right]=p^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{2}(\overline{x})\right]\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{3}(x^{(1)})\right]

\displaystyle=p^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{3}(\overline{x})\right]\geqslant-p^{3}\,.

(9)

Hence, by
Equation

8
and
Equation

6
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(x^{(1)})+f_{2}(x^{(1)})\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(p-p^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(\overline{x})\right]+p^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(\overline{x})+f_{2}(\overline{x})\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{\delta(p-p^{2})}{\sqrt{\log n}}-O\left(\frac{p^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)-O\left(\frac{p}{n^{2}}\right)\,.

Setting

, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[f_{1}(x^{(1)})+f_{2}(x^{(1)})\right]\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{n^{-\frac{3}{4}}}{\log^{2.5}n}\,,

and we conclude by combining with
Equation

9
.
∎

\frac{7}{8}+\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})

Lemma 6.7

(Large degree-3 part)

with variables

Appendix A
A simple
O(\sqrt{n})
-certifiable upper bound

In this section, we provide a simple alternative certification algorithm achieving the same approximation ratio as
Theorem

3.1
.
Let
f(x,y,z)=\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
where
(T_{i,j,k})_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}
is a symmetric 3-tensor. We want to approximate

\max_{x,y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x,y,z)\,.

(11)

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a pairwise independent distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. We can use a construction in which
|\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})|=O(n)
. We will assume without loss of generality that if
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
then also
-\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
.

We consider the following approximation algorithm: for each
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
, find a constant factor approximation of

\max_{y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(\widehat{x},y,z)=\max_{y,z\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j,k\leqslant n}T_{i,j,k}\widehat{x}_{i}y_{j}z_{k}\,,

(12)

using (the proof of) Grothendieck’s inequality. Output the best solution over all choices of
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
.

Call
r
the maximum of the Grothendieck relaxation of
Equation

12
over all
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
. The algorithm outputs a solution of value
\Omega(r)
. We want to prove that the standard degree-4 SoS relaxation of
Equation

11
has optimum at most
r\cdot\sqrt{n}
, which establishes an
O(\sqrt{n})
integrality gap.

Let
\mu
be the optimal pseudo-distribution for the degree-4 SoS relaxation of
Equation

11
and
\mathsf{SOS}
be its value.
Let
q_{i}(y,z)=y^{\top}T_{i}z=\sum_{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n}T_{i,j,k}y_{j}z_{k}
, and write
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
such that
f(x,y,z)=\langle x,q\rangle
.
We have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{SOS}^{2}=\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle x,q\rangle\right)^{2}\leqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[\langle x,q\rangle^{2}]\leqslant n\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|q\|_{2}^{2}

by Cauchy-Schwarz (
2.3
).

On the other hand, by definition of
r
and our assumption that
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
is symmetric, for every
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
there is a degree-2 SoS proof (over variables
y
,
z
) that

f(\widehat{x},y,z)\leqslant r\text{ and }f(\widehat{x},y,z)\geqslant-r\,.

Hence, for every
\widehat{x}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})
there is a degree-4 SoS proof that

f(\widehat{x},y,z)^{2}=\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2}\leqslant r^{2}\,,

which means that

r^{2}\geqslant\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},q\rangle^{2}=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|q\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathsf{SOS}^{2}\,,

where we use the fact that the pairwise independence of
\mathcal{D}
implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\langle\widehat{x},v\rangle^{2}=\|v\|_{2}^{2}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Putting things together we get
\mathsf{SOS}^{2}\leqslant n\cdot r^{2}
, which completes the argument.

Appendix B
Optimizing higher-degree polynomials

B.1
Decoupling inequalities

We first prove a decoupling lemma for all odd-degree polynomials.

Lemma B.1

(High-degree version of
Lemma

2.9
)
.

Let
d
be an odd integer.
Let
f(x)=\langle T,x^{\otimes d}\rangle
be a multilinear homogeneous degree-
d
polynomial in
n
variables (where
T
is a symmetric
d
-tensor),
and let
\widetilde{f}(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)})=\langle T,x^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x^{(d)}\rangle
be the decoupled polynomial of
f
.
Then, given any
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, there exists a sampleable distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(y)\right]=\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)\,.

As a consequence,

\max_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(y)\geqslant\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\max_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)\,.

Proof.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
can be sampled as follows,

•

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d-1}
be i.i.d. uniform
\pm 1
random variables and let
b_{d}\coloneqq b_{1}\ldots b_{d-1}
. Note that the distribution of
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
is
(d-1)
-wise independent and
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{d}=1
.

•

Independently for each
i\in[n]
, sample
y_{i}
uniformly from
\{b_{j}x_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j\in[d]}
.

Since each
y_{i}
is sampled independently conditioned on
b
, we have that for any pairwise distinct indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]
,

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{d}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{b}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}x_{i_{k}}^{(j)}\right)\right]=d^{-d}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}\in[d]}\mathbb{E}_{b}\left[b_{j_{1}}\cdots b_{j_{d}}\right]\cdot x_{i_{1}}^{(j_{1})}\cdots x_{i_{d}}^{(j_{d})}\,.

Since
b
follows a
(d-1)
-wise independent distribution,
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{d}=1
and
d
is odd,
\mathbb{E}_{b}\left[b_{j_{1}}\cdots b_{j_{d}}\right]
does not vanish (and equals 1) if and only if
j_{1},\dots,j_{d}
are distinct, i.e.,
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}\}=[d]
.
Thus, the summation above is simply summing over permutations of
[d]
:

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[y_{i_{1}}\cdots y_{i_{d}}\right]=\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{S}_{d}}\left[x_{i_{1}}^{(\pi(1))}\cdots x_{i_{d}}^{(\pi(d))}\right]=\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathbb{S}_{d}}\left[x_{i_{\pi(1)}}^{(1)}\cdots x_{i_{\pi(d)}}^{(d)}\right]\,,

where
\pi\sim\mathbb{S}_{d}
denotes a random permutation of
[d]
. Finally, as
T
is a symmetric tensor, we deduce

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f(y)\right]=\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)\,.

This proves the first statement of the lemma.
The second statement follows immediately.
∎

For even-degree polynomials,
Lemma

B.1
simply cannot hold.
This is easy to appreciate by discussing the simple setting of quadratics:

Example B.2

(Impossibility of decoupling for quadratics)
.

Consider the matrix
Q=I-\vec{1}\vec{1}^{\top}
, and define the multilinear quadratic polynomial
f(x)=x^{\top}Qx
and the decoupled polynomial
\widetilde{f}(x,y)=x^{\top}Qy
.
Then, it is easy to verify that
\max_{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x)=n
but
\max_{x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{f}(x,y)=n^{2}-n
, which is a
\operatorname{poly}(n)
gap.

On the other hand, if we only consider
\max_{y}|f(y)|
like in the setting of
[
BGG+17
]
(as opposed to
\max_{y}f(y)
), then decoupling inequalities with the same guarantees as
Lemma

B.1
hold for
any
degree:

Lemma B.3

(Decoupling for absolute values, any degree)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
f(x)=\langle T,x^{\otimes d}\rangle
be a multilinear homogeneous degree-
d
polynomial in
n
variables (where
T
is a symmetric
d
-tensor),
and let
\widetilde{f}(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)})=\langle T,x^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x^{(d)}\rangle
be the decoupled polynomial of
f
.
Then,

\max_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}|f(y)|\geqslant\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\max_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)\,.

Proof.

We use the trick that
\max_{y\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}|f(y)|=\max_{y\in[-1,1]^{n}}|f(y)|
.
Thus, given assignments
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, it suffices to round to a
y\in[-1,1]^{n}
.

We next state the well-known polarization identity for degree-
d
homogeneous polynomials:

\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\cdots\varepsilon_{d}}{d!}f\left(\varepsilon_{1}x^{(1)}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{d}x^{(d)}\right)\right]\,.

Define
y_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{1}{d}(\varepsilon_{1}x^{(1)}+\cdots\varepsilon_{d}x^{(d)})\in[-1,1]^{n}
.
Then, rewriting the above and using the triangle inequality,

\widetilde{f}\left(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\right)=\frac{d^{d}}{d!}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left[\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\cdots\varepsilon_{d}\cdot f(y_{\varepsilon})\right]\leqslant\frac{d^{d}}{d!}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\left[|f(y_{\varepsilon})|\right]\,.

Thus, there exists a
y_{\varepsilon}\in[-1,1]^{n}
such that
|f(y_{\varepsilon})|\geqslant\frac{d!}{d^{d}}\cdot\widetilde{f}(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)})
.
∎

B.2
Rounding SoS relaxations for high-degree polynomials

We now give a simple polynomial-time certification and rounding algorithm using the canonical sum-of-squares relaxation that achieves approximation
O(n^{\frac{d}{2}-1})
for optimizing
decoupled
homogeneous degree-
d
polynomials over the hypercube.
The proof is essentially identical to the cubic case (
Theorem

3.1
).

Theorem B.4
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
n\geqslant 1
be integers.
Given any decoupled homogeneous degree-
d
polynomial
f(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)})=\langle T,x^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x^{(d)}\rangle
, the degree-
2d
SoS relaxation of

\max_{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}f(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(d)})

has integrality gap at most
O(n^{\frac{d}{2}-1})
.
Furthermore, given a degree-
2d
pseudo-distribution
\mu
such that
\mathsf{SOS}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}f>0
,
there is a randomized
n^{O(d)}
-time rounding algorithm that outputs
\overline{x}^{(1)},\dots,\overline{x}^{(d)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that with high probability
f(\overline{x}^{(1)},\dots,\overline{x}^{(d)})\geqslant\Omega(n^{-\frac{d}{2}+1})\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
.

Proof.

For
i_{3},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]
, we let
q_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\coloneqq\langle T_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}},x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}\rangle
, a degree-2 polynomial in
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(2)}
, where
T_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}}
is an
n\times n
matrix corresponding to a slice of the tensor
T
.
For simplicity of notation, we will drop the dependence on
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(2)}
and write
Q\coloneqq Q(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})=(q_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}})_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]}
as an order-
(d-2)
tensor whose entries are degree-2 polynomials in
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(2)}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{SOS}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[q_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}}x_{i_{3}}^{(3)}\cdots x_{i_{d}}^{(d)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[q_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{n^{d-2}\sum_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}\in[n]}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[q_{i_{3},\dots,i_{d}}^{2}\right]}=n^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,\|Q\|_{F}^{2}}\,,

using Cauchy-Schwarz and its pseudo-expectation version.
Here,
\|Q\|_{F}^{2}
is the sum of the squared coefficients of the tensor
Q
.
However, taking
h^{(3)},\dots,h^{(d)}
to be i.i.d. uniform samples from
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{h^{(3)},\dots,h^{(d)}}\langle Q,h^{(3)}\otimes\cdots\otimes h^{(d)}\rangle^{2}=\|Q\|_{F}^{2}\,.

For simplicity, we denote
H\coloneqq h^{(3)}\otimes\cdots\otimes h^{(d)}
. Then,

\mathsf{SOS}^{2}\leqslant n^{d-2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{H}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,\left\langle Q,H\right\rangle^{2}\,,

where we recall that the coefficients of
Q
are degree-2 polynomials in
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(2)}
.
We now describe the rounding algorithm.

1.

Sample
h^{(3)},\dots,h^{(d)}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, and set
\overline{x}^{(j)}\coloneqq h^{(j)}
for
j\geqslant 3
.
Denote
H\coloneqq h^{(3)}\otimes\cdots\otimes h^{(d)}
.

2.

Apply
Lemma

3.2
to obtain from
\mu
a degree-
2
pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
satisfying

\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle Q,H\rangle\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle Q,H\rangle^{2}}\,.

3.

Use Grothendieck rounding (
2.5
) on
\mu^{\prime}
to obtain solutions
\overline{x}^{(1)},\overline{x}^{(2)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

\langle Q(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),H\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{K_{G}}\cdot\left|\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{\prime}}\langle Q,H\rangle\right|
(we can always flip the sign of
h^{(3)}
to get the guarantee with the absolute value).

First, note that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle Q,H\rangle^{2}
is a degree-
2(d-2)
polynomial in
h^{(3)},\ldots,h^{(d)}
, thus by
Lemma

2.8
and hypercontractivity of polynomials over the hypercube, we know that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{H}\left[\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,\langle Q,H\rangle^{2}\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{H}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\,\langle Q,H\rangle^{2}\right]\geqslant 2^{-O(d)}\,.

Hence, with probability at least
2^{-O(d)}
, we get a “good”
H
such that
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\langle Q,H\rangle^{2}\geqslant n^{-(d-2)}\cdot\mathsf{SOS}^{2}
. Putting everything together, with probability at least
2^{-O(d)}
we obtain
\overline{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\overline{x}^{(d)}
such that
f(\overline{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\overline{x}^{(d)})\geqslant\Omega(n^{-\frac{d}{2}+1})\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
.
Repeating the above
\operatorname{poly}(n,2^{d})
times, we can obtain a solution with value
\Omega(n^{-\frac{d}{2}+1})\cdot\mathsf{SOS}
with high probability.
This completes the proof.
∎
