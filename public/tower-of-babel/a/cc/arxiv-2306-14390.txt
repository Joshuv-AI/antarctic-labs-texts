Analysis of the Decoder Width for Parametric Partial Differential Equations Thanks: Bin Dong is the corresponding author.
Zhanhong Ye, Hongsheng Liu, Zidong Wang, Bin Dong

Abstract
. Recently, Meta-Auto-Decoder (MAD) was proposed as a novel reduced order model (ROM) for solving parametric partial differential equations (PDEs), and the best possible performance of this method can be quantified by the decoder width. This paper aims to provide a theoretical analysis related to the decoder width. The solution sets of several parametric PDEs are examined, and the upper bounds of the corresponding decoder widths are estimated. In addition to the elliptic and the parabolic equations on a fixed domain, we investigate the advection equations that present challenges for classical linear ROMs, as well as the elliptic equations with the computational domain shape as a variable PDE parameter. The resulting fast decay rates of the decoder widths indicate the promising potential of MAD in addressing these problems.

1.
Introduction

Many important phenomena in science and engineering can be modeled by the so-called parametric partial differential equations (PDEs), which can be formulated as

(1.1)

\mathcal{L}_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\gamma_{1}}u=0,\ {\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\gamma_{2}}u=0,\ {\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\partial\Omega.

Here,
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma_{1}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\gamma_{2}}
are differential operators parametrized by
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, respectively, and
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}
denotes the independent variable in spatiotemporal-dependent PDEs.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the space of parameters.
For a given set of parameters of the PDEs
\eta=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\Omega)\in\mathcal{A}
, we assume (
1.1
) to admit a unique solution
u^{\eta}\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
where
\mathcal{U}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
is a Banach space of functions defined on
\Omega
.
In the simpler case when the computational domain
\Omega
is fixed, we shall denote for simplicity
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
.
Taking
S:\eta\mapsto u^{\eta}
to be the solution mapping, the set of solutions is then given by
S(\mathcal{A})=\{u^{\eta}\mid\eta\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

Solving the parametric PDEs (
1.1
) quickly for a series of
\eta
’s
is crucial in many application domains, such as inverse problems
[
15
]
, inverse design
[
27
]
, optimal control
[
6
]
and uncertainty quantification
[
3
,
23
]
.
In order to obtain an efficient solver for the parametric PDEs, the reduced order modeling technique aims to construct a suitable low-dimensional trial manifold containing (at least approximately) the solution set
S(\mathcal{A})
.
By restricting the original system (
1.1
) or its discretized version to this trial manifold, a reduced order model (ROM) is produced, whose low-dimensional nature would enable faster solving.
However, taking into account the intrinsic complexity of the solution set, the solution provided by the ROM may not achieve the desired accuracy, especially when the dimensionality of the trial manifold is insufficient.
The concept of
widths
is then introduced to quantify the best possible accuracy using an
n
-dimensional trial manifold.
Different types of trial manifolds are considered in different ROMs, and the definitions of widths would vary accordingly.

A widespread family of ROMs, such as reduced basis methods (RBMs)
[
32
]
, utilize only those trial manifolds that are linear subspaces,
and represent the approximate solution as a linear combination of basis functions

\hat{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}u_{i}.

The Kolmogorov width (
2.1
) serves as a theoretical tool for analyzing such ROMs.
As the solution set of advection-dominated parametric PDEs typically exhibits a slow decaying Kolmogorov width
[
30
,
19
]
,
these linear ROMs would struggle to balance between efficiency and accuracy when solving these equations, and this has become a long-standing challenge in the field of parametric PDEs.

Making use of the nonlinear representation of the neural networks (NNs),
many novel ROMs are proposed
[
26
,
17
,
18
,
21
,
5
,
35
]
.
Specifically, the recently proposed ROM named Meta-Auto-Decoder (MAD)
[
22
,
35
]
seeks to construct a Lipschitz continuous mapping
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
, where
Z=\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the latent space.
Denoting
Z_{B}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\mid\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
to be the closed unit ball of
Z
, MAD takes
D(Z_{B})
as the trial manifold, and solves (
1.1
) by searching on this trial manifold (MAD-L) or possibly in a neighborhood of it (MAD-LM).
The corresponding width associated with this novel ROM is termed the
decoder width
, which will be further introduced in Section
3
.
In contrast to the Kolmogorov width, the decoder width exhibits a much faster decay for certain advection equations (Section
4.4
),
indicating the superiority of MAD over classical linear ROMs.

Moreover, MAD is mesh-free since it
represents the decoder mapping
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
as
z\mapsto u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)
, where
u_{\theta}
can be modeled (in the simplest case) as a neural network that takes the concatenation of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
as input.
On the contrary, typical ROMs are based on classical numerical methods such as finite difference method (FDM) and finite element method (FEM), in which the computational domain
\Omega
is often discretized,
and we cannot evaluate the solution
u^{\eta}
to (
1.1
) at arbitrary spatiotemporal points
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
without interpolation.
In the case of
parametric PDEs on a variable domain, i.e. those including
\Omega
as part of the variable parameter
\eta
, further convenience would be brought by the mesh-free nature.
Typical discretization-based ROMs need to assume the existence of a
reference domain

\Omega^{\text{ref}}
, and associate to each possible
\Omega
with an explicit domain transformation
T_{\Omega}:\Omega^{\text{ref}}\to\Omega
,
which could be hard to design by hand for complex-shaped domains, and has the additional prerequisite that no topology changes in
\Omega
should occur.
These are no longer necessary for the MAD method, as any
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be taken as the input of a neural network. The corresponding notion of decoder width can be introduced by using a
master domain

\Omega^{\text{m}}
, as will be further explained in Section
3.2
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first notion of width that can deal with domain deformation without using a reference domain.

The current manuscript primarily focuses on analyzing the decoder width,
which quantifies the best possible performance of the MAD method.
In comparison to the stable manifold width (
2.3
), the decoder width does not exceed its value (Proposition
3.2
), and the difference is sometimes real (Example
3.4
).
We also estimate the decoder widths of the solution sets of several common parametric PDEs.
For the elliptic and the parabolic equations, including those on variable domains, the decoder widths would become zero for finite values of
n
if
\mathcal{A}
is finite-dimensional (Example
4.3
,
4.6
,
4.12
and
4.14
).
Even when
\mathcal{A}
is infinite-dimensional, the decoder width can still exhibit a fast decay rate, depending on the internal complexity of
\mathcal{A}
(Example
4.7
and
4.15
).
In the case of the advection equations, an exponential decay rate is guaranteed when using the
L^{2}
-norm (Example
4.23
),
and the decoder width may become zero if the
L^{1}
-norm is employed instead (Example
4.21
).
We also compare the decay rates of the decoder width with that of the Kolmogorov width and the manifold width in Table
1
.
These results demonstrate the promising potential of the MAD method in solving these parametric PDEs,
and motivate the choice of the latent dimension
n
in its implementation.

The rest of the paper is orgnized as follows.
In Section
2
we provide a brief review of the related work, including the definitions of existing widths.
In Section
3
, we present the definition of the decoder width along with its fundamental properties, covering both the fixed domain case and the variable domain case.
In Section
4
, we examine the solution sets of several parametric PDEs, and estimate the upper bounds of the corresponding decoder widths.
We shall only state the conclusions in Section
3
and
4
, and defer all the proofs till Section
5
.
Our conclusions as well as further discussions will be given in Section
6
.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Widths and Parametric PDEs

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a Banach space, and
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{U}
be a compact subset.
If we aim at approximating
\mathcal{K}
using a linear trial manifold as in classical ROMs,
then the best possible accuracy can be measured by the well-known Kolmogorov width
[
25
]
.
To be more specific, given a linear subspace
U_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}
of dimension
n
, its performance in approximating the elements of
\mathcal{K}
is evaluated by the worst case error

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}d_{\mathcal{U}}(u,U_{n})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{v\in U_{n}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

The
Kolmogorov width
is then defined by taking infimum over all such linear subspaces

(2.1)

d_{n}(\mathcal{K})_{\mathcal{U}}=\inf_{U_{n}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{v\in U_{n}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

We may write
d_{n}(\mathcal{K})=d_{n}(\mathcal{K})_{\mathcal{U}}
for simplicity when
\mathcal{U}
is clear from the context, and this applies to all notions of widths to be introduced below.
In the context of parametric PDEs, the case of particular interest is taking
\mathcal{K}=S(\mathcal{A})
to be the solution set.
For the elliptic equations, the corresponding Kolmogorov width of the solution set exhibits rapid decay
[
8
,
34
]
, indicating that conventional linear ROMs can achieve a satisfactory accuracy with a reasonable latent dimension
n
.
However, the case is quite different for advection-dominant problems
[
30
,
19
]
, posing significant performance challenges for linear ROMs.

In light of the limitations of the linear ansatz, nonlinear alternatives to the Kolmogorov width are considered, and the manifold width proposed in
[
12
]
is a noticeable candidate.
Let
Z=\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a fixed
n
-dimensional latent space, and
E:\mathcal{K}\to Z
,
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
be two continuous mappings, we consider the worst case reconstruction error given by

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|u-D(E(u))\|_{\mathcal{U}}=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|u-D(E(u))\|_{\mathcal{U}},

and the
manifold width
is defined by taking infimum over all possible mapping pairs
(E,D)
:

(2.2)

d_{n}^{\mathrm{Mani}}(\mathcal{K})=\inf_{E,D}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|u-D(E(u))\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

For the solution set of certain elliptic equations, this width is known to become zero even for finite
n

[
16
]
.
If we further require these two mappings to be
l
-Lipschitz continuous, a variant named the
stable manifold width

[
7
]
is then obtained as
1
1

1

The original definition given in
[
7
]
actually takes infimum over all possible norms
\|\cdot\|_{Z}
on
Z
along with
E,D
.
Here we choose a fixed norm
\|\cdot\|_{Z}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
as in the decoder width (
3.2
), which won’t make a difference for our purpose, and (
2.5
) is still valid.

(2.3)

d_{n,l}^{\mathrm{SMani}}(\mathcal{K})=\inf_{E,D\ l\text{-Lip}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|u-D(E(u))\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

We note that some recently proposed ROMs
[
26
,
17
,
18
,
21
,
5
]
find the trial manifold by training a convolutional autoencoder, and these notions of widths can serve as a quantified criterion for their best possible performance.

Another notion termed the entropy number, though not named as a “width”, is also noteworthy due to its close relationship to these concepts.
Given an integer
n>0
, the
entropy number
is defined to be the infimum of all
\epsilon>0
such that
2^{n}
balls of radius
\epsilon
cover
\mathcal{K}

[
4
]
, or equivalently

(2.4)

\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K})=\inf_{V_{n}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{v\in V_{n}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{U}},

where the first infimum is taken over all subsets
V_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}
containing no more than
2^{n}
points.
If the additional restriction
V_{n}\subset\mathcal{K}
is imposed, we obtain the so-called
inner entropy number

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\mathcal{K})
,
and
\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K})\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\mathcal{K})\leq 2\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K})
holds.
When
\mathcal{U}
is a Hilbert space,
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
asserts the inequality

(2.5)

d^{\text{SMani}}_{26n,2}(\mathcal{K})\leq 3\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K}).

More variants of widths exist in the realm of metric geometry, including the Alexandroff width
[
1
]
and the Urysohn width
[
20
]
.
The interested readers may refer to
[
11
]
for a further discussion on this topic.

2.2.
PDEs on a Variable Domain

Some parametric PDEs may take the shape of the computational domain as the variable parameter, i.e.
\eta=\Omega
, which have attracted attention due to both theoretical and engineering interests.
Solving shape optimization problems typically requires to apply sensitivity analysis to the solutions to (
1.1
).
To be more specific, we consider a class of domains
\Omega_{\epsilon}
parametrized by a small parameter
|\epsilon|\ll 1
, and the derivative of
u^{\Omega_{\epsilon}}
(after transformed to a reference domain) with respect to
\epsilon
is analyzed
[
24
,
33
,
2
]
.
Another bunch of work investigates scenarios where the domain is perturbed singularly.
Given a series of domains
\Omega_{n}
approaching
\Omega
, the convergence of the corresponding solutions
u^{\Omega_{n}}
is studied
[
9
,
10
,
13
]
, and the solutions on different domains are compared via direct restrictions to subdomains.
However, these works primarily focus on the limiting behavior of a varying domain.
In contrast, the concept of widths is related to the whole solution set
S(\mathcal{A})=S(\{\Omega\})
, which takes into account the entire distribution of all possible domains.
The main challenge in analyzing widths in this context arises from the fact that the solutions involved are defined on different domains and, consequently, belong to different function spaces.
Transforming all these solutions to a fixed reference domain appears to be one resolution to this issue
[
32
]
, but lacks flexibility as we have discussed in Section
1
.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to introduce a version of width for variable-domain PDEs without involving a reference domain (Section
3.2
), and provide a theoretical estimation of it for several special cases (Section
4.3
).

3.
Definition and Basic Properties of the Decoder Width

For the rest of this paper, we denote
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
to be the
L^{p}
-norm of a function, and
\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{1}}
similarly.
The
\ell^{p}
-norm of a vector or matrix is denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, making

\ell^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
to be a finite-dimensional Banach space, as well as
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})=(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
endowed with the matrix norm.
If
\mathcal{V}
is a Banach space,

(3.1)

\bar{B}_{R}(\mathcal{V})=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\mid\|v\|_{\mathcal{V}}\leq R\}

represents the closed ball of radius
R>0
in
\mathcal{V}
.

3.1.
The Decoder Width on a Fixed Domain

We recall the notion of the decoder width initially introduced in the context of the MAD method
[
35
]
.
Given a fixed domain
\Omega
,
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
is a fixed Banach space, and we are interested in the approximation of a compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{U}
.
MAD constructs the trial manifold by using a decoder mapping
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
similar to the autoencoders, but does not involve the encoder mapping
E:\mathcal{K}\to Z
.
The corresponding
decoder width
would thereby be different from the stable manifold width (
2.3
), and is defined as

(3.2)

\begin{split}d_{n,l}^{\mathrm{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})&=\inf_{D\ l\text{-Lip}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}d_{\mathcal{U}}(u,\{D(\boldsymbol{z})\mid\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\leq 1\})\\
&=\inf_{D\ l\text{-Lip}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in Z_{B}}\|u-D(\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathcal{U}},\end{split}

where the first infimum is taken over all mappings
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
that are
l
-Lipschitz continuous.
The constraint
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\leq 1
and the Lipschitz continuity condition are required to avoid highly irregular mappings resembling the space-filling curves, which will be further discussed in Section
6
.

For the case of a fixed domain, the decoder width is very similar to the Lipschitz width proposed in
[
31
]
.
In the definition of the Lipschitz width, the latent space
Z=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is assumed to be a general finite-dimensional Banach space, and an additional infimum over all possible norms
\|\cdot\|_{Z}
on
Z
is taken before the first infimum in (
3.2
),
that is,

d_{n,l}^{\mathrm{Lip}}(\mathcal{K})=\inf_{\|\cdot\|_{Z}}\inf_{D\ l\text{-Lip}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in Z_{B}}\|u-D(\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

However, the decoder width sticks to the
\ell^{2}
-norm on
Z
.
As the
\ell^{2}
-norm is typically more convenient for numerical optimization, we take the decoder width as the more suitable notion of width related to the MAD method.
Indeed, it is worth noting that
the Lipschitz width is not greater than the decoder width, and any upper bound derived for the decoder width automatically serves as an upper bound for the Lipschitz width as well.

In order to give an analysis of the decoder width, we find it useful to introduce the concept of the
restricted decoder width

d_{n,l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\mathcal{K})
, which shares the same expression as (
3.2
) but with the first infimum taken over all
l
-Lipschitz mappings
D:Z\to\mathcal{K}
.
In other words, it imposes the additional requirement that the image of
D
should lie inside
\mathcal{K}
, and we have the obvious inequality
d_{n,l}^{\text{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})\leq d_{n,l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\mathcal{K})
.

The following properties will be useful for the analysis of the decoder width:

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{F(K_{V})}
, where

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathcal{V}
is a compact subset,

\mathcal{V}
is another Banach space,
and
\mathcal{F:K_{V}\to U}
is an
L_{\mathcal{F}}
-Lipschitz mapping.
Then we have

d_{n,L_{\mathcal{F}}l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\mathcal{K})\leq L_{\mathcal{F}}d_{n,l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{V}}).

The obvious corollary is

d_{n,L_{\mathcal{F}}l}^{\text{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})\leq L_{\mathcal{F}}d_{n,l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{V}}).

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume the compact subset
\mathcal{K}
has diameter
\delta_{\mathcal{K}}=\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{K})=\sup_{u,v\in\mathcal{K}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{U}}<+\infty
. Then

d_{n,\delta_{\mathcal{K}}l^{2}}^{\mathrm{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})\leq d_{n,l}^{\mathrm{SMani}}(\mathcal{K}).

Corollary 3.3
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is a Hilbert space, and
\mathcal{K}
has diameter
\delta_{\mathcal{K}}=\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{K})<+\infty
, then we shall have

d^{\text{Deco}}_{26n,4\delta_{\mathcal{K}}}(\mathcal{K})\leq 3\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K}).

Compared with the stable manifold width (
2.3
), the decoder width would not take a greater value according to Proposition
3.2
.
Here a strict inequality can appear, as is shown in the following simple example:

Example 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the Euclidean plane, and
\mathcal{K}=\{u\in\mathcal{U}\mid\|u\|_{2}=1\}=S^{1}
be the unit circle.
Then we have
d_{1,l}^{\text{SMani}}(\mathcal{K})\geq 1
for any
l>0
, but
d_{1,\pi}^{\text{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})=0
.
See Figure
1
for an illustration.

Figure 1
.
An illustration of Example
3.4
. With an appropriately chosen decoder mapping
D_{1}:Z\to\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{K}
can be fitted perfectly by the trial manifold
D_{1}(Z_{B})
, and the decoder width is therefore zero.

Remark 3.5
.

The Lipschitz width exhibits similar properties to the decoder width.
The inequality presented in Proposition
3.2
also appears in
[
31
, Theorem 5.1]
,
and
[
31
, Theorem 5.3]
generalizes Example
3.4
to the case of all
n\geq 1
.
However, for the Lipschitz width, an inequality stronger than Corollary
3.3
can be given (see
[
31
, Theorem 3.3]
), and the proof relies on choosing a norm different than
\ell^{2}
(more specifically,
\ell^{\infty}
) on
Z
.

Corollary
3.3
can be derived directly by combining Proposition
3.2
with (
2.5
).
The proof of the other assertions above will be given in Section
5
.

3.2.
The Decoder Width on a Variable Domain

Another more complicated case of interest would involve a variable domain, rather than a fixed one.
Now let
\mathcal{K}
be a set of functions, and each element
u\in\mathcal{K}
is associated with a domain
\Omega_{u}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
u\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{u};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
2
2

2

Here
d_{u}>0
is a fixed integer independent of
u
.
.
This scenario arises when the shape of the computational domain
\Omega
is part of the variable parameter in the parametric PDEs.
In this case, the unique solution to (
1.1
) for any PDE parameter
\eta=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\Omega)\in\mathcal{A}
is denoted as
u^{\eta}
, and we have
\Omega_{u^{\eta}}=\Omega
.
This case poses a challenge, as different elements of
\mathcal{K}
may lie in different function spaces, and the distance between them is not readily defined.
The natural resolution to this issue is to modify all these functions to fit into the same function space, which can be achived by using either a reference domain or a master domain.

In the first case, we assume there is a
reference domain

\Omega^{\text{ref}}
, and every function
u\in\mathcal{K}
is associated with a diffeomorphism
T_{u}:\Omega^{\text{ref}}\to\Omega_{u}
, which satisfies certain regularity conditions (depending on
\mathcal{U}
).
Making use of this diffeomorphism, we may obtain another function on the reference domain

\bar{u}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}})=u(T_{u}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega^{\text{ref}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}).

The modified set of functions
\bar{\mathcal{K}}=\{\bar{u}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\mid u\in\mathcal{K}\}
is now a subset of the function space
\mathcal{U}(\Omega^{\text{ref}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
and all previous definitions of widths (including the decoder width) can be applied.
This reformulation is convenient for classical mesh-based ROMs
[
32
]
, but may lack flexibility as discussed in Section
1
.

In the second case, we assume instead there is a
master domain

\Omega^{\text{m}}
containing all possible
\Omega
’s as its subdomain, i.e.,
\Omega_{u}\subseteq\Omega^{\text{m}}
for any
u\in\mathcal{K}
.
Using restrictions instead of domain transformations,
the decoder width is now defined as

(3.3)

d_{n,l}^{\mathrm{Deco}}(\mathcal{K})=\inf_{D\ l\text{-Lip}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in Z_{B}}\Bigl\|u-D(\boldsymbol{z})|_{\Omega_{u}}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{u};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})},

where the first infimum is taken over all mappings
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}(\Omega^{\text{m}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
that are
l
-Lipschitz continuous.
Conceptually, it aims to approximate the target function
u
by restricting the decoded function
D(\boldsymbol{z})
to the subdomain
\Omega_{u}\subseteq\Omega^{\text{m}}
on which
u
is defined.
This alternative reformulation is prefered in the context of the MAD method, since we take neural networks as the mesh-free ansatz of PDE solutions, which can be evaluated for all
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In order to estimate this type of decoder width of the solution set
S(\mathcal{A})
, we may seek to construct an extended solution mapping
\bar{S}:\mathcal{A\to U}(\Omega^{\text{m}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}),\eta\mapsto\bar{u}^{\eta}

which satisfies
\bar{u}^{\eta}|_{\Omega}=u^{\eta}
for any

Let

it is then easy to see that

holds, since we have

In this way, we have essentially converted the variable domain case to the simpler fixed domain case.

4.
Decoder Width Estimation for Several Parametric PDEs

In this section, we shall examine several common parametric PDEs, and estimate the decoder widths of the corresponding solution sets.
The proofs of these assertions can be found in Section
5
.

Before diving into the rigorous statements and proofs, we shall first give a brief informal description of the intuition behind.
For the elliptic and the parabolic equations on a fixed domain, the solution mappings are Lipschitz continuous, i.e.
\|u-\bar{u}\|\leq C\|\eta-\bar{\eta}\|
holds.
By Proposition
3.1
, this would imply
d_{n,Cl}^{\text{Deco}}(\{u^{\eta}\})\leq Cd_{n,l}^{\text{rDeco}}(\{\eta\})
.
When the specific form of
\mathcal{A}=\{\eta\}
is given (in the examples), the specific upper bounds for
d_{n,Cl}^{\text{Deco}}(\{u^{\eta}\})
can be derived accordingly.

In terms of the scenario involving a variable domain, we only present the results for the elliptic equations, and the case of the parabolic equations could be tackled in a similar manner.
The overall strategy for deriving upper bounds remains consistent with the fixed-domain case,
but additional efforts are required to establish the Lipschitz continuity of the solution mapping, since the variable PDE parameter
\eta
now incorporates the shape of the computational domain
\Omega
.
To address this, we first examine how the solution
u
is affected by a small perturbation of
\Omega
(Section
5.4.1
).
A master domain
\Omega^{\text{m}}
is predefined, and the perturbed domain
\Omega_{2}=\beta(\Omega)
is given by an automorphism
\beta:\Omega^{\text{m}}\to\Omega^{\text{m}}
that is close to the identity.
Subsequently, we compare solutions on two arbitrary domains by introducing a sequence of intermediate domains, and conclude that the solution mapping is indeed Lipschitz continuous (Proposition
4.10
).

The case appears to be slightly different for the advection equations on a fixed domain, as the solution mapping is Lipschitz only when the function space
\mathcal{U}
is endowed with the
L^{1}
-norm.
If we switch to the
L^{2}
-norm that is more convenient for numerical computation,
the solution mapping becomes only
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous, i.e.
\|u-\bar{u}\|\leq C\sqrt{\|\eta-\bar{\eta}\|}
.
We would then recourse to the entropy number as the alternative tool for analysis, and show
\epsilon_{n}(\{u^{\eta}\})\leq C\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\{\eta\})}
(Lemma
5.16
).
Since
d^{\text{Deco}}_{26n,4\delta}(\{u^{\eta}\})\leq 3\epsilon_{n}(\{u^{\eta}\})
holds according to Corollary
3.3
, the specific form of the upper bounds can be established.

Proof of Proposition

.

Let

l

We define a new mapping

For given

5.3.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all

\frac{r}{2}\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq A[u,u;t]+C_{B}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

Proof.

Omitting the time

\begin{split}r\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}&=\int_{\Omega}r\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{a}(\nabla u)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=A[u,u]-\int_{\Omega}(u\boldsymbol{b}^{\mathrm{T}}(\nabla u)+cu^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.\end{split}

The second term in the last equation above can be estimated as

\begin{split}-\int_{\Omega}u\boldsymbol{b}^{\mathrm{T}}(\nabla u)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&\leq\int_{\Omega}\|b\|_{2}\cdot\|\nabla u\|_{2}\cdot|u|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\leq R\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u\|_{2}\cdot|u|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\leq\frac{r}{2}\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{R^{2}}{2r}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=\frac{r}{2}\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{R^{2}}{2r}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\end{split}

and the third term as

\begin{split}-\int_{\Omega}cu^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&\leq\|c\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\leq R\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.\end{split}

Combining the inequalities above, the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a matrix. Then
\det(I+Q)\leq(1+\alpha)^{d}
, where
\alpha=\|Q\|_{2}
.
If
\alpha<1
, we further have
\det(I+Q)\geq(1-\alpha)^{d}
.

Proof.

If
Q
is symmetric, we may let
Q=P^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda P
, where
P
is orthogonal and
\Lambda
is diagonal.
Then
\alpha=\|P^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda P\|_{2}=\|\Lambda\|_{2}
. By the definition of
\ell^{2}
-norm, we have
\|\Lambda\|_{2}=\max_{i}|\Lambda_{ii}|
since it is diagonal, and

\begin{split}\det(I+Q)&=\det(I+P^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda P)=\det(P^{\mathrm{T}}(I+\Lambda)P)\\
&=\det(I+\Lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+\Lambda_{ii})\\
&\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}|1+\Lambda_{ii}|\leq(\max_{i}|1+\Lambda_{ii}|)^{d}\\
&\leq(1+\max_{i}|\Lambda_{ii}|)^{d}=(1+\|\Lambda\|_{2})^{d}\\
&=(1+\alpha)^{d}.\end{split}

For non-symmetric
Q
,

\det(I+Q)^{2}=\det(I+Q)\det(I+Q^{\mathrm{T}})=\det(I+Q+Q^{\mathrm{T}}+QQ^{\mathrm{T}}).

The matrix
Q_{1}=Q+Q^{\mathrm{T}}+QQ^{\mathrm{T}}
is symmetric, and

\|Q_{1}\|_{2}\leq\|Q\|_{2}+\|Q^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}+\|QQ^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}\leq 2\|Q\|_{2}+\|Q\|_{2}^{2}=2\alpha+\alpha^{2}.

Then
\det(I+Q_{1})\leq(1+2\alpha+\alpha^{2})^{d}=(1+\alpha)^{2d}
by what we have proved.
This shows
\det(I+Q)\leq(1+\alpha)^{d}
.

When
\alpha<1
, it is easy to verify that the sum

I-Q+Q^{2}-Q^{3}+\cdots=(I+Q)^{-1}

is convergent. We let
Q_{2}=(I+Q)^{-1}-I
, and

\begin{split}\|Q_{2}\|_{2}&=\left\lVert-Q+Q^{2}-Q^{3}+\cdots\right\rVert_{2}\\
&\leq\|Q\|_{2}+\|Q^{2}\|_{2}+\|Q^{3}\|_{2}+\cdots\\
&\leq\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{3}+\cdots=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},\end{split}

\det(I+Q_{2})\leq(1+\|Q_{2}\|_{2})^{d}\leq\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{d}=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{d}}.

The conclusion follows from
\det(I+Q)=1/\det(I+Q_{2})
.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
J(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\partial\beta(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}
be the Jacobian matrix of
\beta
at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
.
When
\delta_{\beta}\leq\delta_{0}

(1-\delta_{\beta})^{d}\leq\det J(\boldsymbol{x})\leq(1+\delta_{\beta})^{d}.

Note that
\|J(\boldsymbol{x})-I_{d}\|_{2}=\left\|\frac{\partial\beta(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}-I_{d}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta_{\beta}
.
By Lemma
5.5
, we have

\det J(\boldsymbol{x})=\det(I_{d}+(J(\boldsymbol{x})-I_{d}))\leq(1+\|J(\boldsymbol{x})-I_{d}\|_{2})^{d}\leq(1+\delta_{\beta})^{d}.

Since
\delta_{\beta}\leq\delta_{0}<1
, the other side of the inequality can be derived from a similar argument.
∎

Proposition 5.7

\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}\leq C_{\text{P}}R/r

\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}+t(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x})
for

.
Then
\beta_{0}=\mathrm{id}
,
\beta_{1}=\beta
,
\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
by the convexity of
\Omega^{\text{m}}
, and

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))&=\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\cdot(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}),\\
\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\rvert&\leq|\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))|\cdot\|\beta-\mathrm{id}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\text{m}};\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\leq\delta_{\beta}|\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))|,\\
(v(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))-v(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}&=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\cdot\int_{0}^{1}1^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\delta_{\beta}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

As
\|\frac{\partial\beta(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}-I_{d}\|_{2}\leq\delta_{\beta}
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
, the mapping
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\beta(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}
is
\delta_{\beta}
-Lipschitz continuous, and
\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
is an injection.
In fact, if
\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
for some
t\in(0,1)
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=t(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-t(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})-\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
, and
|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\leq t\delta_{\beta}|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|
, thereby
|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|=0
since
t\delta_{\beta}<\delta_{\beta}\leq 1
.

Now for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
,
\|\frac{\partial\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}-I_{d}\|_{2}=\|t(\frac{\partial\beta(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}-I_{d})\|_{2}\leq t\delta_{\beta}
, and Lemma
5.5
would imply
\det\frac{\partial\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\geq(1-t\delta_{\beta})^{d}\geq(1-\delta_{\beta})^{d}
.
We have

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}|\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&=\int_{\beta_{t}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}|\nabla v(\boldsymbol{y})|^{2}\left(\det\frac{\partial\beta_{t}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\biggm|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\beta_{t}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}|\nabla v(\boldsymbol{y})|^{2}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\\
&=(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d}\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}^{2},\\
\|v\circ\beta-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}^{2}&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}(v(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))-v(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}\delta_{\beta}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla v(\beta_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\leq\delta_{\beta}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d}\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d}\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega^{\text{m}})}^{2}.\end{split}

We use
(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}\leq 9/8<2
to conclude the proof.

Lemma 5.10
.

\tilde{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\text{m}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{2})
is the weak solution to
\tilde{L}\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}
in
\Omega^{\text{m}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{2}

\begin{split}\tilde{L}u(\boldsymbol{y})&=-\nabla_{y}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\tilde{a}}(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla_{y}u(\boldsymbol{y})),\\
\boldsymbol{\tilde{a}}(\boldsymbol{y})&=\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}(\beta^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})),\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\frac{J(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})J(\boldsymbol{x})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})},\\
\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})&=\frac{f(\beta^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}))}{\det J(\beta^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}))}\end{split}

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
,
\boldsymbol{x}=\beta^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y})

Proof.

For any
\tilde{v}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\text{m}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{2})

v(\boldsymbol{x})=\tilde{v}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))

\nabla_{x}v(\boldsymbol{x})=J(\boldsymbol{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{y}\tilde{v}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})).

Since
\beta(\boldsymbol{x})
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism from
\bar{\Omega}^{\text{m}}
to itself,
J(\boldsymbol{x})
is continuous on
\bar{\Omega}^{\text{m}}
, and then
\|J(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2}
must be bounded.
This implies
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\text{m}}\setminus\partial\Omega)
.
We have

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}f(\boldsymbol{x})v(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}(\nabla_{x}u(\boldsymbol{x}))^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{x}v(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}(J(\boldsymbol{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{y}\tilde{u}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})))^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{a}(\boldsymbol{x})J(\boldsymbol{x})^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{y}\tilde{v}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}(\nabla_{y}\tilde{u}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x})))^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{y}\tilde{v}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))\det J(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\

Furthermore,

(5.14)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}f(\boldsymbol{x})v(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}\tilde{f}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))\tilde{v}(\beta(\boldsymbol{x}))\det J(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega^{\text{m}}}\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{y})\tilde{v}(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}.\end{split}

Since these equalities hold for any
\tilde{v}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\text{m}}\setminus\partial\Omega_{2})
, the conclusion follows from the definition of a weak solution.

Proposition 5.11
.

If

Proposition 5.12
.

Assume
\delta_{\beta}<1
.
For all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega^{\text{m}}
, we have

\left\lVert\frac{J(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}-I\right\rVert_{2}\leq\delta_{\beta}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}+(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}-1.

Furthermore, if
\delta_{\beta}\leq\delta_{0}
, then

\left\lVert\frac{J(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}-I\right\rVert_{2}\leq C_{J}\delta_{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{4},

where
C_{J}=\bigl(9+\delta_{0}^{-1}\bigr)/8
.

Proof.

Making use of Corollary
5.6

and the fact that the function
\psi(s)=|\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}-1|
achieves its maximum at endpoints,
we have

\begin{split}\left\lVert\frac{J(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}-I\right\rVert_{2}&\leq\left\lVert\frac{J(\boldsymbol{x})-I}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}-1\biggr)I\right\rVert_{2}\\
&=\frac{\|J(\boldsymbol{x})-I\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}+\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det J(\boldsymbol{x})}}-1\right|\\
&\leq\delta_{\beta}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}+\max((1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}-1,1-(1+\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2})\\
&=\delta_{\beta}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}+(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}-1.\end{split}

When
\delta_{\beta}\leq\delta_{0}
,
the condition
\delta_{\beta}\leq 1-(8/9)^{2/d}
implies
(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}\leq 9/8
.
The convexity of the function
\phi(t)=(1-t)^{-d/2}
on
0\leq t<1
ensures
\frac{\phi(\delta_{\beta})-\phi(0)}{\delta_{\beta}}\leq\frac{\phi(\delta_{0})-\phi(0)}{\delta_{0}}
, hence

(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}-1\leq((1-\delta_{0})^{-d/2}-1)\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{\delta_{0}}\leq\left(\frac{9}{8}-1\right)\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{\delta_{0}}=\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{8\delta_{0}}.

This gives

\delta_{\beta}(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}+(1-\delta_{\beta})^{-d/2}-1\leq\frac{9}{8}\delta_{\beta}+\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{8\delta_{0}}=C_{J}\delta_{\beta}.

Since
\delta_{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{9}
holds as well, we further have

\frac{9}{8}\delta_{\beta}+\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{\delta_{0}}\leq\frac{9}{8}\cdot\frac{1}{9}+\frac{1}{8}\cdot 1=\frac{1}{4}.

∎

Proposition 5.14

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\mathcal{V,U}
be two Banach spaces,
\mathcal{K_{V}}\subset\mathcal{V}

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{K_{V}}\to\mathcal{U}

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\mathcal{K})\leq w(\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\mathcal{K_{V}})).

\epsilon_{n}(\mathcal{K})\leq w(\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(\mathcal{K_{V}})).

Proof.

\begin{split}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{K_{V}})}\inf_{\bar{u}\in\mathcal{F}(V_{n})}\|u-\bar{u}\|_{\mathcal{U}}&=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{K_{V}}}\inf_{\bar{v}\in V_{n}}\|\mathcal{F}(v)-\mathcal{F}(\bar{v})\|_{\mathcal{U}}\\

w(\cdot)

and

6.
Discussions and Conclusions

Figure 4
.
If we do not impose the
l
-Lipschitz continuity constraint on the decoder mapping
D
, the resulting trial manifold
D(Z_{B})
may resemble a space-filling curve.

In the definition of the decoder width, we impose the
l
-Lipschitz continuity condition as well as the restriction
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
Both of them are required to give a reasonable notion of width.
If either of the conditions is dropped, the width could become identically zero even for latent dimension
n=1
, since
D(Z_{B})
or
D(Z)
may resemble the space-filling curve as illustrated in Figure
4
.
In such cases, the corresponding ROM would indeed have a strong representational power, and be capable of approximating any desired solutions to the parametric PDEs.
However, it leaves all the challenges to the online process of finding the latent vector
\boldsymbol{z}
, as the corresponding optimization problem has become highly non-convex.
This makes the ROM unuseful in the realistic settings.

We also note that, although these two conditions are theoretically essential, they might not be introduced explicitly in the numerical implementation of the MAD method.
As we have explained in
[
35
]
, the hard constraint
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}\leq 1
can be relaxed to a soft constraint, i.e. an additional penalty term in the objective function during the offline pre-training and the online fine-tuning stages.
Furthermore, we represent the decoder mapping
D:Z\to\mathcal{U}
as a neural network trained via stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which is typically known to exhibit the implicit regularization phenomenon
[
29
,
28
]
.
Even if the explicit Lipschitz continuity constraint is not imposed on the decoder mapping during the offline pre-training stage,
we would expect to obtain a
D
without the ill-posedness that is theoretically possible.
Further numerical verification may be helpful in this respect, and is left for future work.

The current work admits many possible extensions,
especially when we focus on the parametric PDEs on a variable domain.
While the analysis in this paper is limited to the elliptic case, it may be extended to a broader range of PDEs,
including the parabolic equations and the wave equations.
Furthermore, our Assumption
4.9
requires a shared topological structure among all possible domains
\Omega
.
For scenarios where the domain topology changes,
the MAD method (as a numerical solver) is still applicable due to its flexibility,
but a theoretical estimation of the corresponding decoder width remains to be developed.
As a reference domain is no longer available in this setting, new methods of analysis have to be introduced.

In sum, this paper analyzes the basic properties of the decoder width, which serves as a quantified criterion for the best possible performance of the MAD method.
Theoretical estimations of the decoder width for several parametric PDEs are provided, including the advection equations (Section
4.4
) where classical linear ROMs appear to be inefficient, and the elliptic equations on a variable domain (Section
4.3
) which could bring inconvenience for other deep autoencoder-based ROMs
[
26
,
17
,
18
,
21
]
.
These results indicate the great potential of the MAD method for solving these parametric PDEs.
