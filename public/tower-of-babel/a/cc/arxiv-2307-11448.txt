On the convergence order of the Euler scheme for scalar SDEs with Hölder-type diffusion coefficients
Annalena Mickel, Andreas Neuenkirch

Abstract
We study the Euler scheme for scalar non-autonomous stochastic differential equations, whose diffusion coefficient is not globally Lipschitz but a fractional power of a globally Lipschitz function. We analyse the strong error and establish a criterion, which relates the convergence order of the Euler scheme to an inverse moment condition for the diffusion coefficient. Our result in particular applies to Cox-Ingersoll-Ross-, Chan-Karolyi-Longstaff-Sanders- or Wright-Fisher-type stochastic differential equations and thus provides a unifying framework. Key words: scalar SDEs, Euler scheme, non-standard assumptions, fractional power, L 1 L^{1} -error, inverse moment condition 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: 65C30; 60H35

Introduction and Main Result

Consider the scalar non-autonomous stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dX_{t}=a(t,X_{t})dt+c(t,X_{t})dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
W=(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a Brownian motion and the drift and diffusion coefficients
a,c:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfy suitable assumptions. Explicit solutions of such SDEs are rarely known and a simple and popular numerical method for this equation is the Euler scheme.
For an equidistant discretization with
N\in\mathbb{N}
time steps, i.e.,

t_{k}=k\frac{T}{N},\quad k=0,\ldots,N,

the equidistant Euler scheme for (
1
) is given by
x^{(N)}_{k}=x_{k}
with

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+a(t_{k},x_{k})(t_{k+1}-t_{k})+c(t_{k},x_{k})(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1.

(2)

It is well known that the Euler scheme (
2
) for SDE (
1
) has strong convergence order
1/2
, i.e., one has

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\,N^{1/2}\,\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}\mathbb{E}\left|X_{t_{k}}-x_{k}^{(N)}\right|<\infty,

if the drift and diffusion coefficient satisfy the following standard assumptions (HLG) and (L).

Assumption 1.1
.

We will use the following assumptions on functions
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which are measurable.

(HLG)

There exists a constant
K>0
such that

|f(t,x)-f(s,x)|\leq K(1+|x|)|t-s|^{1/2}

for all
s,t\in[0,T]
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(L)

There exists a constant
K>0
such that

|f(t,x)-f(t,y)|\leq K|x-y|

for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

(P)

We have

f(t,x)\geq 0

for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

In recent years, the analysis of numerical methods for SDEs under non-standard assumptions has become a very active research field, see Subsection
1.1
for an overview.
In this article, we consider SDEs where the drift coefficient satisfies the standard assumptions, but the diffusion coefficient is a fractional power of a standard diffusion coefficient. More precisely, we will study the SDE

dX_{t}=a(t,X_{t})dt+\sigma(t,X_{t})^{\mathfrak{g}}dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

where

a,\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfy (HLG) and (L),
\sigma
satisfies additionally (P)
and we have
\mathfrak{g}\in[1/2,1)
.
Since
a
and
\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}}
are continuous and of linear growth, the existence of a weak solution to Equation (
3
) follows from the works of Skorokhod (see
[
40
,
41
]
and also
[
23
]
). Since moreover

|\sigma(t,x)^{\mathfrak{g}}-\sigma(t,y)^{\mathfrak{g}}|\leq K^{\mathfrak{g}}|x-y|^{\mathfrak{g}}

for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

and

\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}x^{-2\mathfrak{g}}dx=\infty

for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have strong uniqueness and consequently existence of a unique strong solution to Equation (
3
) by the results of Yamada and Watanabe (see, e.g., Proposition 2.13 and Corollary 3.23 in Chapter V of
[
21
]
).

Typical examples for SDE (
3
) are the non-autonomous Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
)

dX_{t}=\kappa(t)\left(\lambda(t)-X_{t}\right)dt+\theta(t)\sqrt{X_{t}^{+}}dW_{t},

the non-autonomous Chan-Karolyi-Longstaff-Sanders (CKLS) process (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
)

dX_{t}=\kappa(t)\left(\lambda(t)-X_{t}\right)dt+\theta(t)\left(X_{t}^{+}\right)^{\mathfrak{g}}dW_{t},

with
\mathfrak{g}\in(1/2,1)

and the non-autonomous Wright-Fisher (WF) process (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
)

dX_{t}=\kappa(t)\left(\lambda(t)-X_{t}\right)dt+\theta(t)\sqrt{(X_{t}(1-X_{t}))^{+}}dW_{t},

with
\kappa,\lambda,\theta:[0,T]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
. These SDEs have applications in various fields, e.g., in biology and finance, to mention a few.

To formulate our main result, we will need the following assumption:

Assumption 1.2
.

Assume that
a,\sigma
satisfy (HLG) and (L) and that
\sigma
additionally satisfies (P).
Moreover let
\mathfrak{g}\in[1/2,1)
and assume that there exists
\mathfrak{s}\in[0,1-\mathfrak{g}]
such that

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(s,X_{s})^{2(\mathfrak{g}+\mathfrak{s}-1)}\right]ds<\infty.

(4)

Our main result reads then as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\in[1/2,1)
be given and assume that Assumption
1.2
holds for a fixed
\mathfrak{s}\in[0,1-\mathfrak{g}]
. Moreover, let
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the solution of (
3
) and let
(x_{k})_{k=0,\ldots,N}
be given by the Euler scheme (
2
) with diffusion coefficient
c=\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}}
. Then we have

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\,N^{\lambda}\,\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}\mathbb{E}\left|X_{t_{k}}-x_{k}^{(N)}\right|=0

for all
\lambda<1/2-\mathfrak{s}
.

How to interpret condition (
4
)? For this note first that the irregularity of the diffusion coefficient
\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}}
arises from the set

\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}=\{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}:\,\sigma(t,x)=0\}.

For
(t_{*},x_{*})\in\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
and a neighbourhood
U_{*}
of
(t_{*},x_{*})
the map
U_{*}\ni(t,x)\mapsto\sigma(t,x)^{\mathfrak{g}}\in[0,\infty)
is only Hölder continuous of order
\mathfrak{g}
and not Lipschitz continuous.
Moreover, since
\mathfrak{g}+\mathfrak{s}\leq 1
the exponent in condition (
4
) is zero or negative. Thus, this condition is a weighted measure, how much the solution
X
takes values in or close to the set
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
. In the case
\mathfrak{s}=0
, that is

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(s,X_{s})^{2\mathfrak{g}-2}\right]ds<\infty,

the process
X
is sufficiently apart from the set
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
and we have directly convergence order
1/2-\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon>0
. If on the other hand

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(s,X_{s})^{2\mathfrak{g}-2}\right]ds=\infty,

the process
X
is too close to the set
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
and we need a compensation factor
\mathfrak{s}
and obtain convergence order
1/2-\mathfrak{s}-\varepsilon
.

Finally, let us comment on three limiting cases:

(i)

For
\mathfrak{g}=1
condition (
4
) would be trivially satisfied for
\mathfrak{s}=0
and we would (formally) obtain convergence order
1/2-\varepsilon
in accordance with the classic result for the Euler scheme.

(ii)

If
\sigma
is uniformly elliptic, i.e.,

\inf_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\sigma(t,x)>0,

then condition (
4
) is trivially satisfied for
\mathfrak{s}=0
and we obtain convergence order
1/2-\varepsilon
. This is again in accordance with the classic result for the Euler scheme, since
\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}}
satisfies (HLG) and (L), if
\sigma
is uniformly elliptic.

(iii)

If
\mathfrak{g}>1/2
we can choose
\mathfrak{s}=1-\mathfrak{g}
. Then condition (
4
) is satisfied, since
\mathfrak{g}+\mathfrak{s}-1=0
, and we obtain convergence order
\mathfrak{g}-1/2-\varepsilon
. This recovers in our setup the results of
[
42
]
and
[
14
]
for autonomous SDEs.

How to verify condition (
4
)? For this we present two approaches in Subsection
2.1
and Subsection
2.2
.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows:
In the next two subsections, we give an overview of previous results in the literature and outline our notation. Section
2
applies our main result to several SDEs, in particular to the above mentioned CIR, CKLS and WF processes. Section
3
is devoted to the proof of our main result, while in the Appendix we collect some general auxiliary results.

\displaystyle|b(t,x)-b(s,y)|\leq K|x-y|,\quad|c(t,x)-c(s,y)|\leq K|x-y|^{\mathfrak{g}},\quad|f(t,x)-f(s,y)|\leq K|x-y|^{\beta_{3}}

1.2
Notation

As already mentioned, we will work with an equidistant discretization

t_{k}=k\Delta t,\quad k=0,\ldots,N,

with
\Delta t=T/N
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, we define
n(t):=\max\{k\in\{0,...,N\}:t_{k}\leq t\}
and
\eta(t):=t_{n(t)}
. Constants whose values depend only on

,

,
\mathfrak{g}
will be denoted in the following by
C
, regardless of their value. Other dependencies will be denoted by subscripts, i.e.,

. Moreover, the value of all these constants can change from line to line. We will work on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},{P})
where the filtration satisfies the usual conditions, and (in-)equalities between random variables or random processes are understood
{P}
-a.s. unless mentioned otherwise.

Finally, for
p\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{p}(M;\mathbb{R})
the set of
p

M
to
\mathbb{R}
and by
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{b}(M;\mathbb{R})
the set of continuously differentiable functions from
M
to
\mathbb{R}
, whose derivatives are bounded. Moreover, for
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(M;\mathbb{R})
the set of
\gamma

2
Examples

Condition (
4
) from Assumption
1.2
gives structural insight in the convergence behaviour of the Euler scheme, but how to verify it? Here we present two approaches. The first one is based on the Feller test and Itō’s lemma.
The second one is based on a time change and the comparison lemma for SDEs.

2.1
Autonomous SDEs on domains

Here we consider the autonomous equation

dX_{t}=a(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})^{\mathfrak{g}}dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\qquad X_{0}=x_{0},

(6)

and we assume that
a\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and
\sigma\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R};[0,\infty))
. To apply Itō’s lemma to derive an explicit criterion for condition (
4
) we need that
\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}}
is differentiable on the support of
X
. To this end, we assume that there exists

I=(l,r)
with
-\infty\leq l\leq r\leq\infty

such that

\displaystyle\forall x\in I:\quad\sigma(x)>0,

as well as

P(X_{t}\in I,\,t\in[0,T])=1.

The latter condition can be checked, e.g., by the Feller test, see Theorem
4.1
in the Appendix.

Under these assumptions it is well known that the Euler scheme has convergence order
1/2
in law, that is

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\,N^{1/2}\,\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}\big|X_{t_{k}}-x_{k}^{(N)}\big|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\max_{t\in[0,T]}|U_{t}|,

where the limiting process
U
is the solution of the SDE

\displaystyle dU_{t}

\displaystyle=a^{\prime}(X_{t})U_{t}dt+\mathfrak{g}\sigma^{\mathfrak{g}-1}(X_{t})\sigma^{\prime}(X_{t})U_{t}dW_{t}+\frac{\mathfrak{g}}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma(X_{t})^{2\mathfrak{g}-1}\sigma^{\prime}(X_{t})dB_{t},\qquad U_{0}=0,

(7)

where
B=(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is another Brownian motion which is independent of
W
.
See
[
35
]
and
[
39
]
. Thus it is reasonable to expect that in this case the Euler scheme has also strong convergence order
1/2
. The limiting SDE (
7
) also illustrates that the bottleneck is the error propagation and not the local error, since we have the exponent
\mathfrak{g}-1
in the error propagation term and the less restrictive exponent
2\mathfrak{g}-1
in the local error term.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\in[1,2,1)
,
a\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and
\sigma\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R};[0,\infty))
.
Moreover let
I=(l,r),-\infty\leq l\leq r\leq\infty
, and assume that the unique solution of the SDE

dX_{t}=a(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})^{\mathfrak{g}}dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}\in I,

satisfies

P(X_{t}\in I,\,t\in[0,T])=1

as well as

\displaystyle\forall x\in I:\quad\sigma(x)>0.

Finally, assume that

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in I}\left[\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(x)a(x)}{\sigma(x)}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma^{\prime\prime}(x)\sigma(x)^{2\mathfrak{g}}}{\sigma(x)}+\left(\mathfrak{g}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\frac{(\sigma^{\prime}(x))^{2}\sigma(x)^{2\mathfrak{g}}}{\sigma(x)^{2}}\right]>-\infty.

(8)

Then we have

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\,N^{\lambda}\,\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}\mathbb{E}\left|X_{t_{k}}-x_{k}^{(N)}\right|=0

for all
\lambda<1/2
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k>\sigma(x_{0})^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}
and set

\tau_{k}=\inf\{t\in[0,T]:\,\sigma(X_{t})^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}\geq k\}

with the convention
\inf\emptyset=T
. An application of Itō’s lemma and the local martingale property of the Itō integral give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sigma(X_{t\wedge\tau_{k}})^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma(x_{0})^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}+\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{k}}h_{a,\sigma,\mathfrak{g}}(X_{s})ds\right)

with

\displaystyle h_{a,\sigma,\mathfrak{g}}(x)

\displaystyle=(2\mathfrak{g}-2)\sigma(x)^{2\mathfrak{g}-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\sigma^{\prime}(x)\sigma^{-1}(x)a(x)+\left(\mathfrak{g}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\sigma(x)^{2\mathfrak{g}-2}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma(x)^{2\mathfrak{g}-1}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(x)\right).

Assumption (
8
) now yields the existence of constant
C>0
, which is in particular independent of
k
, such that

h_{a,\sigma,\mathfrak{g}}(x)\leq C\sigma(x)^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}

for all
x\in I
.
Gronwall’s lemma gives then the existence of another constant
C>0
, which is in particular independent of
k
, such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left(\sigma(X_{t\wedge\tau_{k}})^{2(\mathfrak{g}-1)}\right)\leq C.

Assumption (
4
) with
\mathfrak{s}=0
now follows by taking
k\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

2.2
Prototype SDEs

In this subsection we consider the SDEs

dX_{t}=a(t,X_{t})dt+c_{i}(t,X_{t})dW_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T],

(9)

for
i=1,2,3
, where

c_{i}(t,x)=\theta(t)h_{i}(x)

with
\theta\in C^{1/2}([0,T];(0,\infty))
and

h_{1}(x)=\sqrt{x^{+}},\qquad h_{2}(x)=\sqrt{(x(1-x))^{+}},\qquad h_{3}(x)=(x^{+})^{\mathfrak{g}},

where
\mathfrak{g}\in(1/2,1)
.

Theses SDEs are extensions of the classical CIR, CKLS and WF processes. For verifying Assumption
1.2
, the following time change procedure will be helpful. So, let

\Theta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad\Theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{2}(s)ds.

Then
\Theta
is strictly increasing and has an inverse
A:=\Theta^{-1}
. Now consider the time-changed process

\widetilde{X}_{t}=X_{A(t)},\qquad t\in[0,A^{-1}(T)].

Since
X
satisfies

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}a(s,X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\theta(s)h_{i}(X_{s})dW_{s},\qquad t\in[0,T],

we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{A(t)}a(s,X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{A(t)}\theta(s)h_{i}(X_{s})dW_{s},\qquad t\in[0,T].

Now set

\widetilde{W}_{t}=\int_{0}^{A(t)}\theta(s)dW_{s},\qquad t\in[0,A^{-1}(T)].

Then
\widetilde{W}
is a Brownian motion on
[0,A^{-1}(T)]
and the rules for the time change for Riemann and Itō integrals, see Proposition
4.2
in the Appendix, yield that

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(A(s),\widetilde{X}_{s})}{\theta(A(s))^{2}}ds+\int_{0}^{t}h_{i}(\widetilde{X}_{s})d\widetilde{W}_{s},\qquad t\in[0,A^{-1}(T)].

This enables us to exploit the classical bounds for the inverse moments of the CIR, CKLS and WF processes. See Subsection
4.3
in the Appendix.

For
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
we obtain the following result:

Proposition 2.2
.

(i) Let
x_{0}>0
and
\theta\in\mathcal{C}^{1/2}([0,T];(0,\infty))
. Moreover, assume that the drift coefficient

a
satisfies (HLG), (L)
and assume furthermore that

\mu_{0}:=\min_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{a(t,0)}{\theta(t)^{2}}>0.

Then the unique strong solution of

dX_{t}=a(t,X_{t})dt+\theta(t)\sqrt{X_{t}^{+}}dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\qquad X_{0}=x_{0},

satisfies
P(X_{t}\geq 0,t\in[0,T])=1
and we have

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\,\,N^{\lambda}\,\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}\mathbb{E}\left|X_{t_{k}}-x_{k}^{(N)}\right|=0

for all

\lambda<\min\{1/2,\mu_{0}\}.

(ii) Let
x_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\theta\in\mathcal{C}^{1/2}([0,T];(0,\infty))
. Moreover, assume that the drift coefficient

a
satisfies (HLG), (L)
and assume furthermore that

\mu_{0}:=\min_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{a(t,0)}{\theta(t)^{2}}>0\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mu_{1}:=-\max_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{a(t,1)}{\theta(t)^{2}}>0.

For the diffusion coefficient in (i) note that

\|\theta\|_{\infty}:=\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\theta(t)|<\infty

\displaystyle|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(s,x)|

Using this and the time change described above

Here, we denote

Note that (HLG) and (L) imply that

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p\geq 1

1.1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}|^{p}\right]+\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|X_{t}-X_{s}|^{p}}{|t-s|^{p/2}}\right]\leq C_{p}

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\bar{x}_{t}^{(N)}|^{p}\right]+\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|\bar{x}_{t}^{(N)}-\bar{x}_{s}^{(N)}|^{p}}{|t-s|^{p/2}}\right]\leq C_{p}.

The next lemma gives us a bound for the expected local time in zero of a semimartingale. It is taken from

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any real-valued, continuous semimartingale

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L^{0}_{t}(Y)\right]\leq 4\delta

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{s}\in(0,\delta)\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{s}\geq\delta\}}e^{1-\frac{Y_{s}}{\delta}}\right)dY_{s}\right]

Here
(L_{t}^{0}(Y))_{t\in[0,T]}
is the local time of

in

. For almost all

the map

is continuous and non-decreasing with

.
For more background on local times see, e.g., Chapter III.7 in
[

. For our purposes, it suffices to mention the Tanaka-Meyer formula for continuous semimartingales of the form

Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}y^{A}_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}^{M}dW_{s},\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
(y_{t}^{A})_{t\in[0,T]}
and
(y_{t}^{M})_{t\in[0,T]}

(12)

Finally, the following inequalities will be very useful in our computations.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right)
and
\beta\in(0,1]
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|x^{\mathfrak{g}}-y^{\mathfrak{g}}\right|\leq 2x^{-(1-\mathfrak{g})}\left|x-y\right|\qquad\textrm{for all }\quad x>0,\,y\geq 0,

and

\displaystyle\left|x^{\mathfrak{g}}-y^{\mathfrak{g}}\right|\leq 2x^{-(1-\mathfrak{g})\beta}\left|x-y\right|^{\beta+\mathfrak{g}(1-\beta)}\qquad\textrm{for all }\quad x>0,\,y\geq 0.

(14)

(i) We first prove the assertion

We start with

\displaystyle(x^{\mathfrak{g}}-y^{\mathfrak{g}})\left(x^{1-\mathfrak{g}}+y^{1-\mathfrak{g}}\right)

\displaystyle=y^{\mathfrak{g}}\left(\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\mathfrak{g}}-1\right)y^{1-\mathfrak{g}}\left(\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{1-\mathfrak{g}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=y\left(\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\mathfrak{g}}-1\right)\left(\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{1-\mathfrak{g}}+1\right).

Now set
z:=\frac{x}{y}
. Notice that

. Therefore, it remains to show that

\displaystyle(z^{\mathfrak{g}}-1)\left(z^{1-\mathfrak{g}}+1\right)\leq 2(z-1)

\displaystyle f(z):=2(z-1)-(z^{\mathfrak{g}}-1)\left(z^{1-\mathfrak{g}}+1\right).

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z)=1-\mathfrak{g}z^{\mathfrak{g}-1}+(1-\mathfrak{g})z^{-\mathfrak{g}}

(ii) For the second assertion note that

Consider the autonomous SDE

4.2
A deterministic time change

The following auxiliary result deals with deterministic time changes for Riemann and Itō integrals.
For this, let
\theta\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];(0,\infty))

\Theta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad\Theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{2}(s)ds.

\widetilde{W}_{t}=\int_{0}^{A(t)}\theta(s)dW_{s},\qquad t\in[0,A^{-1}(T)],

[

Proposition 4.2
.
