The Cyclic Flats of an ℒ \mathcal{L} -Polymatroid
Eimear Byrne, Andrew Fulcher

Abstract
We consider properties of the class of ℒ \mathcal{L} -polymatroids, especially those that are defined on a finite modular complemented lattice ℒ \mathcal{L} . We give a set of cover-weight axioms and hence give a cryptomorphism between these axioms and the rank axioms of an ℒ \mathcal{L} -polymatroid. We introduce the notion of a cyclic flat of an ℒ \mathcal{L} -polymatroid and study properties of its lattice of cyclic flats. We show that the weighted lattice of cyclic flats of an ℒ \mathcal{L} -polymatroid along with its atomic weights are sufficient to define its rank function on ℒ \mathcal{L} . In our main result, we characterise those lattices 𝒵 ⊆ ℒ \mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{L} such that 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} is the collection of cyclic flats of an ℒ \mathcal{L} -polymatroid.



Keywords.
cyclic flat, polymatroid, modular lattice, cryptomorphism, submodular function

1
Introduction

The lattice of cyclic flats of a matroid plays an important role in matroid theory.
Every finite lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of cyclic flats of some matroid
[
7
,
30
]
and transversal matroids can be characterised in terms of their cyclic flats
[
9
,
10
,
23
,
27
]
.
There have also been some interesting studies of invariants of matroids and polymatroids in relation to their cyclic flats. For example, in
[
17
]
an expression for the Tutte polynomial of a matroid is given in terms of the ranks of its cyclic flats. The
{\cal{G}}
-invariant of a matroid and its connection with the cyclic flats of a matroid was considered in
[
6
,
8
]
. More precisely, it was shown that these invariants can be computed from the
configuration
of a matroid, which carries strictly less information than its weighted lattice of cyclic flats.

Any matroid is uniquely characterised by its lattice of flats. Similarly, knowledge of the lattice of cyclic flats, along with the ranks of its elements, is sufficient to reconstruct the rank function of the corresponding matroid. However the lattice of cyclic flats of a matroid has much fewer elements than its lattice of flats and hence gives a much more compact representation of the matroid.
See
[
19
]
for an explicit algorithm to reconstruct the lattice of flats of a matroid from its lattice of cyclic flats. The results of
[
7
,
30
]
have also been extended to more general settings: in
[
15
]
, it is shown that a polymatroid is completely determined by its weighted lattice of cyclic flats, along with the ranks of its atoms, while in
[
1
]
a cryptomorphism for the cyclic flats is given for
q
-matroids.

In this paper we consider the theory of polymatroids in the context of more general lattices, with the main focus on complemented, modular lattices. We call a non-negative, bounded, increasing, submodular function defined on a lattice
\mathcal{L}
an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. We introduce the concept of a cyclic flat of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. Our main contribution is to extend the results of
[
1
,
7
,
15
]
to the more general setting of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. That is, we derive a cryptomorphism for
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroids in terms of a weighted lattice of cyclic flats
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, along with the ranks of the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
. To achieve this, we propose a list of cyclic flat axioms that a weighted lattice
\mathcal{Z}
must satisfy in order to be embedded into the weighted lattice
\mathcal{L}
of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid as its lattice of cyclic flats.

We remark that generalizations of matroids and polymatroids have been in the literature for some time. In
[
18
]
, matroid theory was extended from Boolean lattices to partially ordered sets. There have been several papers on
supermatroids
, in which matroid theory on various classes of lattices are considered, such as distributive and modular lattices
[
16
,
26
,
33
,
32
]
. More recently,
q
-analogues of matroids and polymatroids have been studied
[
25
,
12
,
11
,
20
,
21
,
29
]
. However, with the exception of
[
2
]
, as far as the authors are aware, generalizations of
polymatroids
to arbitrary finite modular complemented lattices have not been given much consideration.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we establish notation and give some fundamentals. In Section
3
we introduce the cover-weight axioms and hence give a cryptomorphism between these axioms and the rank axioms of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, extending results of
[
4
,
13
]
. In Section
4
we introduce the notion of a cyclic element of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid and describe its lattice of cyclic flats. We show that the rank function of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid is completely determined by its weighted lattice of cyclic flats, along with the ranks of the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
. We introduce the concept of a
quasi-modular function
, which is crucial for capturing the behaviour of the rank function of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid in terms of its weighted lattice of cyclic flats. Section
5
contains the main contribution of this paper. We define a set of six cyclic flat axioms and hence demonstrate a cryptomorphism for
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroids. In Section
6
we close the paper with some commentary on the cyclic flat axioms given in Section
5
and their relations to cyclic flat axioms of matroids, polymatroids and
q
-matroids.

2
Preliminaries

The primary difference between polymatroids and their
q
-analogues is determined by the underlying lattices upon which they are defined; the former is defined on the power set of a finite set, and the latter is defined on the subspace lattice of a finite dimensional vector space over a finite field. Both of these lattices are
complemented modular
lattices, but in the case of the Boolean lattice, complements are unique. In terms of the underlying lattice properties, many of the results we present in this paper only rely on the lattice being complemented and modular. For background reading on lattice theory, see
[
3
,
22
,
28
]
. We include the following fundamental definitions and notation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice with meet
\wedge
and join
\vee
. We write
{\mathbf{1}}:=1_{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathbf{0}}:=0_{\mathcal{L}}
.
Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
A\leq B
.

1.

If
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
we write
A\dot{\vee}B=A\vee B
.

2.

An
interval

[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is the set of all
X\in\mathcal{L}
such that
A\leq X\leq B
. It defines the
interval sublattice

([A,B],\leq,\vee,\wedge)
. We define
(A,B):=\{C\in[A,B]:A<C<B\}
. We write
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{L})
to denote the collection of intervals of
\mathcal{L}
.

3.

Let
C\in[A,B]
. We say that
D
is a
complement
of
C
in
[A,B]
if
C\wedge D=A
and
C\vee D=B
. We define
\textbf{C}(A):=\{C\in\mathcal{L}:C\wedge A={\mathbf{0}},C\vee A={\mathbf{1}}\}
. If
X\in\textbf{C}(A)
, we write
X\dot{\vee}A={\mathbf{1}}
.

4.

\mathcal{L}
is called
relatively complemented
if every
C\in[A,B]
has a complement in
[A,B]
and is called
complemented
if every
C\in\mathcal{L}
has a complement in
\mathcal{L}
.

5.

If
[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is such that for any
X\in\mathcal{L}
,
X\in[A,B]
implies that
X=A
or
X=B
, then
B
is called a
cover
of
A
and we write
A\lessdot B
.

6.

An
atom
of
\mathcal{L}
is any element that is a cover of
0
. A
coatom
of
\mathcal{L}
is any element that is covered by
1
.
We define
\mathcal{A}([A,B]):=\{X\in[A,B]:A\lessdot X\}
and

\mathcal{H}([A,B]):=\{X\in[A,B]:X\lessdot B\}
. We also define

\mathcal{A}(B):=\mathcal{A}([{\mathbf{0}},B])
and
\mathcal{H}(B):=\mathcal{H}([{\mathbf{0}},B])
.

7.

A
chain
from
A
to
B
is a totally ordered subset of
[A,B]
with respect to
\leq
. A chain from
A
to
B
is called a
maximal
chain in
[A,B]
if it is not properly contained in any other chain from
A
to
B
.
A finite
chain
from
A
to
B
is a sequence of the form

A=X_{1}<\cdots<X_{k+1}=B
with
X_{j}\in\mathcal{L}
for
j\in[k]
, in which case we say that the chain has length
k
. If a chain in
\mathcal{L}
is not finite then it is called an infinite chain.

8.

If
\mathcal{L}
has no infinite chains, we define the
height
of
B
, which we denote by
\textup{h}(B)
, to be the maximum length of all maximal chains from
0
to
B
. The
length
of
[A,B]
is defined to be
\textup{len}([A,B]):=\textup{h}(B)-\textup{h}(A)
.

9.

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{L})
and let
A
be the join of the elements of
S
. We say that
S
is
independent
if

\textup{h}(A)=|S|
.

10.

\mathcal{L}
is called
modular
if for all
A,B,C\in\mathcal{L}
, we have that

A\geq C
implies
(A\wedge B)\vee C=A\wedge(B\vee C).

For the remainder,
\mathcal{L}
will denote a lattice with meet
\wedge
, join
\vee
minimal element
{\mathbf{0}}
and maximal element
{\mathbf{1}}
.
If the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is modular, then all its maximal chains have the same length.
Every modular complemented lattice is relatively complemented.
In a complemented lattice, a given element need not have a unique complement (for example, in a subspace lattice, elements have multiple complements).

Definition 2.2
.

An
(\mathcal{L},t)
-
polymatroid
is a pair
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},r)
such that
r:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a map that satisfies the following properties for any
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
.

(R1)

0\leq r(A)\leq t\cdot\textup{h}(A)
,

(R2)

if
A\leq B
then
r(A)\leq r(B)
, i.e.
r
is increasing on
\mathcal{L}
,

(R3)

r(A)-r(A\wedge B)\geq r(A\vee B)-r(B)
, i.e.
r
is submodular on
\mathcal{L}
.

In the case that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
, we say that
\mathcal{P}
is a
(q,t)
-polymatroid (or simply a
q
-polymatroid). If, furthermore,
t=1
,
and
r
is integer-valued, then we say that
\mathcal{P}
is a
q
-matroid.

In the case that
\mathcal{L}
is the Boolean lattice, then an
\mathcal{P}
is a polymatroid, and if furthermore,
t=1
, and
r
is integer-valued, then
\mathcal{P}
is a matroid.
Definition
2.2
is a direct extension of the definition of a
q
-polymatroid found in
[
29
]
. We will often refer to an
(\mathcal{L},t)
-polymatroid as an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. We will make use of the following notation; see
[
22
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
[A,B],[C,D]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
be intervals. We write
[A,B]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny up}}}}{{\sim}}}[C,D]
if
A=B\wedge C
and
D=B\vee C
. Dually, we write
[A,B]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny dn}}}}{{\sim}}}[C,D]
if
C=A\wedge D
and
B=A\vee D
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\vee_{1},\wedge_{1})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{2},\vee_{2},\wedge_{2})
be a pair of lattices. A
lattice isomorphism
is a bijective map
\varphi
between the elements of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
such that
\varphi(A\vee B)=\varphi(A)\vee\varphi(B)
and
\varphi(A\wedge B)=\varphi(A)\wedge\varphi(B)
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. If such a map exists, we say that
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are
isomorphic
and we write
\mathcal{L}_{1}\cong\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

It is well-known that
[A,B]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny up}}}}{{\sim}}}[C,D]
does not imply that
[A,B]\cong[C,D]
in an arbitrary lattice, while this implication holds in modular lattices, as outlined in the following theorem, (see e.g.
[
22
]
).

Theorem 2.5

(Isomorphism Theorem for Modular Lattices)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular lattice.
Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
. Then the intervals
[A,A\vee B]
and
[A\wedge B,B]
are isomorphic.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
2.5
is that if
\mathcal{L}
is modular, for any
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
we have that
\textup{h}(A)+\textup{h}(B)=\textup{h}(A\vee B)+\textup{h}(A\wedge B)
. We will use this property frequently in later proofs.

The following proposition gives us a useful case of submodularity of the rank function. It follows immediately from Definition
2.3
and
(R3)
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
[A,B],[C,D]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
be intervals. The following hold.

1.

If
[A,B]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny dn}}}}{{\sim}}}[C,D]
, then
r(B)-r(A)\leq r(D)-r(C)
.

2.

If
[A,B]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny up}}}}{{\sim}}}[C,D]
, then
r(B)-r(A)\geq r(D)-r(C)
.

The following lemma and corollary will be useful for proofs later in this paper.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular lattice.
Let
A,B,C\in\mathcal{L}
and suppose that
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
and
(A\dot{\vee}B)\wedge C={\mathbf{0}}
. We then have that
A\wedge(B\dot{\vee}C)={\mathbf{0}}
.

Proof.

Since
A\vee B\vee C=(A\dot{\vee}B)\dot{\vee}C
by assumption, we have
\textup{h}(A\vee B\vee C)=\textup{h}((A\dot{\vee}B)\dot{\vee}C)=\textup{h}(A)+\textup{h}(B)+\textup{h}(C)=\textup{h}(A)+\textup{h}(B\dot{\vee}C)
. Since
\textup{h}(A\vee(B\vee C))=\textup{h}(A)+\textup{h}(B\vee C)-\textup{h}(A\wedge(B\vee C))
, it follows that
\textup{h}(A\wedge(B\vee C))=0
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a complemented, modular lattice.
Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
and
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
. There exists
A^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(A)
with
B\leq A^{c}
.

Proof.

Let
C\in\textup{{C}}(A\dot{\vee}B)
. By Lemma
2.7
we have that
B\dot{\vee}C\in\textup{{C}}(A)
. Clearly,
B\leq B\dot{\vee}C
, and so the result follows.
∎

By Corollary
2.8
, if
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
and
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
then
B
is a relative complement of
A
in
[{\mathbf{0}},A\vee B]
and can be
extended
to a complement of
A
in
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
and let
B^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(B)
.
If
A=(A\wedge B)\dot{\vee}(A\wedge B^{c})
, we say that
B^{c}

decomposes

A
; that is, if
A\wedge B^{c}
is a relative complement of
B\wedge A
in the interval
[{\mathbf{0}},A]
.
We denote the set of elements of
\textup{{C}}(A)
that decompose
B
by
\textup{{C}}(A;B):=\{A^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(A):(A^{c}\wedge B)\dot{\vee}(A\wedge B)=B\}
.

Note that the property in Definition
2.9
is not in general satisfied by all complements of a given element. However, in subsequent sections we will make frequent use of elements with this property.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular complemented lattice.
Let
A\leq B\in\mathcal{L}
. We have that
A^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(A;B)
for any
A^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(A)
.

Proof.

We have that
{\mathbf{1}}=A\dot{\vee}A^{c}
and thus
B=(A\dot{\vee}A^{c})\wedge B=A\dot{\vee}(A^{c}\wedge B)
, where the second equality follows from the modular law on
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular complemented lattice and
let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
. Then
\textup{\bf{C}}(B;A)
is non-empty.

Proof.

Consider the interval
[{\mathbf{0}},A]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and the space
A\wedge B\leq A
. There exists a relative complement
C
of
A\wedge B
in
[{\mathbf{0}},A]
. Clearly,
B\wedge C={\mathbf{0}}
.
By Corollary
2.8
,
C
can be extended to a complement
C^{\prime}
of
B
and since
C\vee(A\wedge B)\leq(C^{\prime}\wedge A)\dot{\vee}(B\wedge A)\leq A=C\dot{\vee}(A\wedge B)
, we have
C^{\prime}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(B;A)
.
∎

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular complemented lattice.
Let
A,B,C\in\mathcal{L}
such that
A\leq B
. Then
\textup{{C}}(C;A)\cap\textup{{C}}(C;B)
is non-empty.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.11
, choose a relative complement of
C\wedge A
in
[{\mathbf{0}},A]
, which by Corollary
2.8
can be extended it to a relative complement of
C\wedge B
in
[{\mathbf{0}},B]
and then to a complement of
C
in
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

3
The Cover-Weighted Lattice of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

Throughout this section we will let
(\mathcal{L},r)
denote an arbitrary, fixed
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

The notion of a
weighted lattice
is long established and can be found in
[
31
]
, for instance. We consider a similar object, which we call a
cover-weighted lattice
.
It was shown in
[
4
]
that the rank function of a
q
-matroid is cryptomorphic to a bicolouring of the support lattice satisfying a set of axioms on its intervals of length two. This characterisation of
q
-matroids is intuitive and useful, as can be seen in
[
4
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
14
]
. In this section, we will
provide a characterisation of
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroids in terms of
interval-weight
axioms and show that in the case that
\mathcal{L}
is modular, there exist cover-weight axioms on the intervals of length two yielding a cryptomorphism with the rank function of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

Definition 3.1
.

A
cover-weighted lattice

(\mathcal{L},w)
is a lattice
\mathcal{L}
with a non-negative function
w
defined on its intervals of length one (covers). Explicitly, if
[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is a cover, then
w([A,B])\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We call
w
a
cover weighting
of
\mathcal{L}
.

We now introduce axioms for a cover-weighted lattice, which we will show in
Theorem
3.3
yields a cryptomorphism for
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroids as defined in Definition
2.2
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},w)
be a cover-weighted lattice. We define the
interval-weight axioms
as follows.

(IW1)

For every interval
[A,B]
of
\mathcal{L}
and every maximal chain
A=X_{0}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{m}=B
,
\displaystyle w([A,B]):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w([X_{i-1},X_{i}])
is a constant uniquely determined by
[A,B]
.

(IW2)

The axiom
(IW1)
holds, and for all
X\in\mathcal{L}
,
w([A,X\vee A])\leq w([A\wedge X,X])
.

If
(\mathcal{L},w)
is a cover-weighted lattice satisfying
(IW1)
then the cover weight
w:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{L})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
can be extended to a map on
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{L})
, by which we denote the collection of intervals of
\mathcal{L}
. As we will see in the next theorem, if
(\mathcal{L},w)
satisfies the interval-weight axioms then the weight function on the intervals of the form
[{\mathbf{0}},A]
of
\mathcal{L}
corresponds to the rank function of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.
More precisely, we have the following cryptomorphism between the rank axioms and the interval-weight axioms.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},w)
be a cover-weighted lattice, and let

r:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

1.

If
(\mathcal{L},r)
satisfies the rank axioms, then there exists a cover-weighting
w_{r}
such that
(\mathcal{L},w_{r})
satisfies the interval-weight axioms.

2.

If
(\mathcal{L},w)
satisfies the interval-weight axioms, then there exists a rank function
r_{w}
of
\mathcal{L}
such that
(\mathcal{L},r_{w})
satisfies the rank axioms.

3.

Furthermore, if either of the above hypotheses hold, then
w_{r_{w}}=w
and
r_{w_{r}}=r.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{L},r)
satisfies (R1)-(R3).
For each interval
[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, define

w_{r}([A,B]):=r(B)-r(A).

By
(R2)
, we have that
w_{r}([A,B])\geq 0
and so
(\mathcal{L},w_{r})
is a cover-weighted lattice.
Now let
[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, and let
A=X_{0}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{m}=B
be a maximal chain from
A
to
B
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{r}([X_{i-1},X_{i}])=r(B)-r(X_{m-1})+r(X_{m-1})-r(X_{m-2})+\cdots+r(X_{1})-r(A)=w_{r}([A,B]),

which is clearly a constant independent of the choice of maximal chain from
A
to
B
. It follows that
(IW1)
holds for
(\mathcal{L},w_{r})
.
The property
(IW2)
now follows from
(IW1)
and as a direct consequence of

(R3)
. This proves 1.

Conversely, suppose that
(\mathcal{L},w)
satisfies
(IW1)
and
(IW2)
. For any
A\in\mathcal{L}
,
and any maximal chain
{\mathbf{0}}=X_{0}\lessdot X_{1}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{m}=A
, we have that

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}w([X_{i},X_{i+1}])
depends only on
A
and so the assignment

r_{w}(A):=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}w([X_{i},X_{i+1}])

is a function
r_{w}:\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
It is clear that
r_{w}({\mathbf{0}})=0
and that
r_{w}
is increasing. The submodularity property
(R3)
follows directly from
(IW2)
.
To see that 3 holds, let
[A,B]\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and let

{\mathbf{0}}\lessdot X_{1}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot X_{s}=A\lessdot\cdots\lessdot X_{s+1}\lessdot X_{m}=B
be a maximal chain from
{\mathbf{0}}
to
B
that contains
A
.
Then
w_{r_{w}}([A,B])=r_{w}(B)-r_{w}(A)=\sum_{j=s+2}^{m-1}w([X_{j},X_{j+1}])=w([A,B])
.
Similarly,
r_{w_{r}}(B)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}w_{r}([X_{j},X_{j+1}])=r(B)
.
∎

We may now define the following maps.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
r:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfy the axioms
(R1)
-
(R3)
and let

(\mathcal{L},w)
be a cover-weighted lattice that satisfies the axioms
(IW1)
and
(IW2)
.
We define the following functions:

w_{r}:\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{L})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
w_{r}([A,B]):=r(B)-r(A)\>\forall\>A,B\in\mathcal{L}
;

r_{w}:\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\displaystyle r_{w}(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w([X_{i-1},X_{i}])\>\forall\>A\in\mathcal{L}
and
{\mathbf{0}}=X_{0}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{m}=A
.

In the instance that
\mathcal{L}
is a modular lattice, we may replace the axioms
(IW1)
and
(IW2)
with axioms that refer only to the intervals of length
2
in
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular lattice equipped with a cover-weighting
w
.
Let
[A,B]
be an arbitrary interval of length two in
\mathcal{L}
and let
X,Y\in(A,B)
. We define the following
cover-weight
axioms.

(CW1)

w([A,X])+w([X,B])=w([A,Y])+w([Y,B])
,

(CW2)

w([A,X])\geq w([Y,B])
whenever
X\neq Y
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modular lattice equipped with a cover-weighting
w
. Suppose that
(CW1)
and
(CW2)
hold for every interval of
\mathcal{L}
of length 2 in
\mathcal{L}
.
Then
(\mathcal{L},w)
satisfies the interval-weight axioms
(IW1)
and
(IW2)
. In particular,

(\mathcal{L},r_{w})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

Proof.

Let
[A,B]
be an interval of
\mathcal{L}
.
The statement clearly holds if
\textup{len}([A,B])\leq 2
, by
(CW1)
. We proceed now by induction on the length of
[A,B]
.
Let
A=X_{0}\lessdot X_{1}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{m}=B
and
A=Y_{0}\lessdot Y_{1}\lessdot\dots\lessdot Y_{m}=B
be a pair of maximal chains in
[A,B]
.
Let
Z=X_{1}\vee Y_{1}
and let
Z=Z_{0}\lessdot Z_{1}\lessdot\dots\lessdot Z_{m-2}=B
be a maximal chain from
Z
to
B
. By the induction hypothesis, we have that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}w([X_{i},X_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=w([X_{1},Z])+\sum_{i=0}^{m-3}w([Z_{i},Z_{i+1}]),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}w([Y_{i},Y_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=w([Y_{1},Z])+\sum_{i=0}^{m-3}w([Z_{i},Z_{i+1}]).

Furthermore, we have that
w([A,X_{1}])+w([X_{1},Z])=w([A,Y_{1}])+w([Y_{1},Z])
, which gives us

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}w([X_{i},X_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=w([A,X_{1}])+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}w([X_{i},X_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=w([A,X_{1}])+w([X_{1},Z])+\sum_{i=0}^{m-3}w([Z_{i},Z_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=w([A,Y_{1}])+w([Y_{1},Z])+\sum_{i=0}^{m-3}w([Z_{i},Z_{i+1}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}w([Y_{i},Y_{i+1}]).

Now let
X\in\mathcal{L}
and consider the interval
[A\wedge X,A]
.
Let
A\lessdot A_{1}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot A_{m}=A\vee X
be a maximal
chain from
A
to
A\vee X
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is modular, we have that

[A\wedge X,A]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny up}}}}{{\sim}}}[A_{i}\wedge X,A_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq m
.
By
(CW2)
, we have that
w([A\wedge X,A])\geq w([A_{1}\wedge X,A_{1}])
and more generally,
w([A_{i}\wedge X,A_{i}])\geq w([A_{i+1}\wedge X,A_{i+1}])
for
1\leq i\leq m
. It now follows that
(IW2)
holds.
∎

We now have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
r:\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
(R1)
-
(R3)
hold for every interval of length 2 in
\mathcal{L}
. Then
(\mathcal{L},r)
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

A benefit of defining an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid via the intervals of length two of its cover-weighted lattice can be found in a proof of a new cryptomorphism, which we establish in Section
5
; we will use the characterisation given by Corollary
3.7
in the proof of Theorem
5.10
.

4
Cyclic Flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid

For the remainder of this paper, unless explicitly stated otherwise,
\mathcal{L}
will denote an arbitrary modular, complemented lattice and
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},r)
will denote an arbitrary, fixed
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.
In this section, we will introduce the notion of a
cyclic flat
of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, which extends the definition for matroids, polymatroids and
q
-matroids; see
[
1
,
5
,
15
,
24
]
. Furthermore, we will show that the cyclic flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid (under this definition), along with the ranks of the atoms of the
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, determine the rank function of the
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid.

First, we define a
flat
of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, whose definition is extended in the obvious way from the same notion for matroids.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. We say that
X
is a
flat
of
\mathcal{P}
if
r(X)<r(X\vee x)
for all
x\nleq X
.

Now, we introduce the notion of a
cyclic
element.

Definition 4.2
.

We call
X\in\mathcal{L}

cyclic
in
\mathcal{P}
if for all
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
one of the following is satisfied:

1.

r(X)=r(H)
or

2.

0<r(X)-r(H)
and there exists
a\in\mathcal{A}(X)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H)
such that
r(X)-r(H)<r(a)
.

We also refer to a cyclic element of
\mathcal{P}
as a
cycle
of
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition
4.2
generalises the definitions of cyclic elements for polymatroids and
q
-matroids given in
[
15
]
and
[
1
]
respectively.
If
X\in\mathcal{L}
is both cyclic and a flat, we call it a
cyclic flat
. In the case of
q
-matroids, the lattice of cyclic flats, along with the rank of each cyclic flat, determines the rank function of the
q
-matroid
[
1
]
. For polymatroids, the lattice of cyclic flats, along with their ranks and the ranks of the atoms of the ambient lattice, determines the rank function of the polymatroid
[
15
]
. We will show that an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid is determined by its cyclic flats and their ranks along with the ranks of the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
.

We first establish some preliminary results, which we will use later.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{L}
be cyclic in
\mathcal{P}
and let
X\in\mathcal{L}
such that
C\leq X
. Let
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
. If
C\nleq H
, then
r(X)-r(H)<r(a)
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}(X)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H)
.

Proof.

If
C\nleq H
, then
[H,X]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny dn}}}}{{\sim}}}[C\wedge H,C]
. By Proposition
2.6
we have that
r(X)-r(H)\leq r(C)-r(C\wedge H)
, while
r(C)-r(C\wedge H)<r(a)
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}(C)\backslash\mathcal{A}(C\wedge H)
by the cyclicity of
C
. Since
e\in\mathcal{A}(X)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H)
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

The join of two cycles of
\mathcal{P}
is a cycle of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{L}
be cycles and let
C=C_{1}\vee C_{2}
. Let
H\in\mathcal{H}(C)
. At least one of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
is not in
[{\mathbf{0}},H]
. By Lemma
4.3
, we have that either
r(H)=r(C)
or
r(C)-r(H)<r(a)
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}(C)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H)
and so
C
is cyclic.
∎

Definition 4.5
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. The
cyclic operator
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined by

\textup{cyc}(X):=\bigvee\{Y\leq X:Y\textup{ is cyclic in }\mathcal{P}\}\>\forall X\>\in\mathcal{L}.

By Lemma
4.4
,
\cyc(X)
is the unique maximal cycle contained in
X
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. If
X
is a flat of
\mathcal{P}
, then
\cyc(X)
is a flat of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
be a flat of
\mathcal{P}
and let
C:=\cyc(X)
. If
C=X
then the statement holds trivially, so suppose otherwise. Since
X
is not cyclic, there exists
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
,
0<r(X)-r(H)=r(a)
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}(X)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H)
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.3
, we have that
C\leq H
for such
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
. Furthermore, by Proposition
2.6
, such an element
H
is a flat since
r(H)<r(X)
and
[X,X\vee a^{\prime}]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny dn}}}}{{\sim}}}[H,H\vee a^{\prime}]
for any atom
a^{\prime}\nleq X
.
If
C=H
, then we have found the required flat of
\mathcal{P}
. Otherwise we continue:
C
is the maximal cyclic element of
H
, being maximal in
X
. Since
H
is not cyclic, there exists a flat
H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(H)
that contains the maximal cyclic element
C
. Repeating this process, we eventually arrive at
C
, which is thus a (cyclic) flat.
∎

Definition 4.7
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. The
closure
of
X
in
\mathcal{P}
is defined to be

\cl(X):=\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{L}:r(X\vee x)=r(X)\}.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. It is easy to see that the closure of
X
in
\mathcal{P}
is a flat of
\mathcal{P}
, and that if
X
is a flat of
\mathcal{P}
, then
X=\cl(X)
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{L}
. If
C
is cyclic in
\mathcal{P}
then
\cl(C)
is cyclic.

Proof.

Let
C\in\mathcal{L}
be cyclic and
Z:=\textup{cl}(C)
. Let
H\in\mathcal{H}(Z)
. If
C\leq H
then
r(H)=r(Z)
. For
C\nleq H
, the result follows by Lemma
4.3
.
∎

It is also clear that the closure of a cycle of
\mathcal{P}
is the minimal flat that contains that cycle. Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.8
therefore give us the following proposition.

Proposition 4.9
.

The poset of cyclic flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid is a lattice
(\mathcal{Z},\wedge_{\mathcal{Z}},\vee_{\mathcal{Z}})
such that for all
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
:

•

Z_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{Z}}Z_{2}:=\cyc(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2})
,

•

Z_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}Z_{2}:=\cl(Z_{1}\vee Z_{2})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
we have that
\textup{cyc}(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}
. By Lemma
4.8
we have that
\textup{cl}(Z_{1}\vee Z_{2})\in\mathcal{Z}
.
∎

Definition 4.10
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. We define:

\mathcal{B}(X):=\{\beta\subseteq\mathcal{A}(X):|\beta|=\textup{h}(X),\vee\beta=X\}.

As is the case for polymatroids
[
15
]
, knowledge of the lattice of cyclic flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, as well as their respective ranks, and the ranks of the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
is sufficient uniquely determine the
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. We will prove this shortly.
A key part of this approach will be to use use a convolution of the restriction of the rank function of
\mathcal{P}
to its lattice of cyclic flats with a function
\mu_{r}
. We’ll see that the resulting convolution coincides with the rank function of
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
f:\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{L})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be an arbitrary map. We define the function
\mu_{f}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\mu_{f}(A):=\textup{min}\left\{\sum_{x\in\beta}f(x):\beta\in\mathcal{B}(A)\right\},\>\forall\>A\in\mathcal{L}\backslash\{{\mathbf{0}}\},

and
\mu_{f}({\mathbf{0}}):=0
.

The function
\mu_{f}
from Definition
4.11
is a generalisation of the function with similar notation found in
[
15
]
, wherein
\mathcal{L}
is the Boolean lattice and hence
\mu_{f}(A)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(A)}f(a)
. In this case it is easy to see that
\mu_{f}
is a modular function on
\mathcal{L}
.
For the arbitrary
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},r)
, we have
\mu_{r}(A)=\textup{min}\left\{\sum_{x\in\beta}r(x):\beta\in\mathcal{B}(A)\right\}
for any
A\in\mathcal{L}
; observe that by
(R3)
,
r(A)\leq\mu_{r}(A)
.

Remark 4.12
.

For the arbitrary
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},r)
, the function
\mu_{r}
is not necessarily increasing on
\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, if
\mu_{r}
is not increasing, then it is also not a modular function of
\mathcal{L}
:
for if
\mu_{r}
is modular, then for any
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
satisfying
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
we have

\mu_{r}(A\dot{\vee}B)=\mu_{r}(A\vee B)+\mu_{r}(A\wedge B)=\mu_{r}(A)+\mu_{r}(B)\geq\mu_{r}(A)
. We illustrate these remarks in the following example.

Example 4.13
.

Consider the
(2,2)
-polymatroid of rank two shown in Figure
1
.
The atoms
\langle e_{1}\rangle,\langle e_{2}\rangle,\langle e_{1}+e_{1}\rangle
, which are
1
-dimensional subspaces of
E=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{3}
, all have rank
2
while the remaining atoms have rank
1
.
All other non-zero subspaces of
E
have rank
2
.
It is straightforward to check that
\mu_{r}(\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle)=4
, while

\mu_{r}(E)=3
.
Clearly, if
\mu_{r}
is a modular function then it is increasing, so
\mu_{r}
is thus not modular either.

Figure 1:
A
(2,2)
-polymatroid  (
2
,
1
,
0
)

We may make use of a special case of submodularity with respect to
\mu_{f}
, as shown in the following.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}
and
A\wedge B={\mathbf{0}}
. We have that
\mu_{f}(A\dot{\vee}B)\leq\mu_{f}(A)+\mu_{f}(B)
.

Proof.

For any
\beta_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(A)
and
\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(B)
we have that
\beta_{1}\dot{\cup}\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(A\dot{\vee}B)
. Therefore, the choice of
\beta_{1}\dot{\cup}\beta_{2}
that achieves the minimal value for
\mu_{f}(A)+\mu_{f}(B)
can achieve that same value for
\mu_{f}(A\dot{\vee}B)
. The result follows.
∎

We include the following definition to aid in later statements.

Definition 4.15
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
and let
{\mathbf{0}}=H_{m}\lessdot\dots\lessdot H_{0}=X
be a maximal chain from
{\mathbf{0}}
to
X
. For
k\in[m]
, we define

L_{k}:=\{a\in\mathcal{A}(X):a\leq H_{k-1},a\nleq H_{k}\}
, which we call a
layer
of the chain. We call the list of sets
(L_{1},\dots,L_{m})
, the
associated layering
of this chain.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
and let
{\mathbf{0}}=H_{m}\lessdot\dots\lessdot H_{0}=X
be a maximal chain with associated layering
(L_{1},\dots,L_{m})
. For any
Y\leq X
, we have that
\mathcal{A}(Y)
intersects non-trivially with exactly
\textup{h}(Y)
layers.

Proof.

We will prove this by induction. Let
\textup{h}(Y)=2
and let
k
be the greatest integer in
[m]
such that
Y\wedge H_{k}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
.
We have that
\textup{h}(Y\wedge H_{k})=1
, since otherwise
Y\wedge H_{k+1}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
. Let
j\in[m]
be the greatest integer less than
k
such that
Y\wedge H_{j}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
. Since
H_{k}<H_{j}
, we must have that
Y\leq H_{j}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}(Y)
meets both
L_{k+1}
and
L_{j+1}
non-trivially.
Assume now that the statement holds for all elements of
[{\mathbf{0}},X]
of height at most
k
.
Let
Y\leq X
such that
\textup{h}(Y)=h
. By hypothesis,
\mathcal{A}(Y)
intersects non-trivially with exactly
h
layers of the associated layering
(L_{1},\dots,L_{m})
.
Let
a\in\mathcal{A}(X)\backslash\mathcal{A}(Y)
be such that
a\in L_{k}
for some
k\in[m]
.
Let
x\in\mathcal{A}(Y)\cap L_{j}
for some
j\in[m]
.
If
j\neq k
, then clearly

\mathcal{A}(x\vee a)
meets both
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
non-trivially.
If
j=k
, then there is a single atom
y_{1}\in\mathcal{A}(x\vee a)
that is contained in
H_{k}
, while
y_{1}\notin\mathcal{A}(Y)
, necessarily.
We have that
y_{1}\in L_{k_{1}}
for some
k_{1}>k
.
If there exists
a_{1}\in\mathcal{A}(Y)\cap L_{k_{1}}
then there exists a unique

y_{2}\in\mathcal{A}(y_{1}\vee a_{1})
in
H_{k_{1}}
. Continuing in this manner, we must arrive at a
j
such that
y_{j}\in L_{k_{j}}
and
\mathcal{A}(Y)\cap L_{k_{j}}=\varnothing
. Indeed, otherwise we have
y_{j}\in L_{m}
, and since
|L_{m}|=1
, this gives
y_{j}\in\mathcal{A}(Y)
, which yields a contradiction. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}(Y\dot{\vee}a)
intersects non-trivially with at least
h+1=\textup{h}(Y\dot{\vee}a)
layers
L_{i}
. The result now follows.
∎

The following lemma may be viewed as a generalisation of
[
15
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
and let

A\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
\textup{cyc}(X)\leq A\leq X
. We have that

r(A)=r(\textup{cyc}(X))+\mu_{r}(\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\wedge A)

for any
\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(\textup{cyc}(X))
.

Proof.

If
A
is cyclic then
A=\textup{cyc}(F)
and the statement holds trivially, so assume then that
A
is not cyclic. Therefore, there exists
H_{1}\in\mathcal{H}(A)
such that
0<r(A)-r(H_{1})=r(a)
for any
a\in\mathcal{A}(A)\backslash\mathcal{A}(H_{1})
. By Lemma
4.3
we must have that
\textup{cyc}(X)\leq H_{1}
. If
H_{1}
is cyclic, then
H_{1}=\textup{cyc}(X)
, while if
H_{1}
is not cyclic we may repeat the argument and hence produce a maximal chain:

\textup{cyc}(X)=H_{k}\lessdot\dots\lessdot H_{1}\lessdot H_{0}=A,

such that
0<r(H_{i-1})-r(H_{i})=r(a_{i})
for any
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(H_{i-1})\backslash\mathcal{A}(H_{i})
for each
i\in[k]
.
It is clear that
a_{i}\in L_{i}
where
(L_{1},\dots,L_{k})
is the associated layering of the chain
H_{k}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot H_{0}
. It follows that we have:

\displaystyle r(A)-r(\cyc(X))=r(H_{0})-r(H_{1})+r(H_{1})-r(H_{2})+\cdots+r(H_{k-1})-r(H_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(a_{i}).

By construction, we have
\cyc(X)\dot{\vee}\left(\dot{\bigvee}_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\right)=A
for
arbitrary
a_{i}\in L_{i}
. Moreover, every member of
\textup{\bf{C}}(\cyc(X))

decomposes
A
by Proposition
2.10
.
From Lemma
4.16
,
it follows that
\dot{\bigvee}_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\wedge A
for some choice of
a_{i}\in L_{i}
. By the definition of
\mu_{r}
and applying
(R3)
, we now have that:

\mu_{r}(\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\wedge A)\leq\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(a_{i})=r(A)-r(\textup{cyc}(X))\leq r(\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\wedge A)\leq\mu_{r}(\textup{cyc}(X)^{c}\wedge A),

from which the result follows.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this section, which says that the rank function of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid is determined by its cyclic flats with their ranks and the ranks of the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be the lattice of cyclic flats of
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},r)
. For any
X\in\mathcal{L}
, we have that

r(X)=\textup{min}\{r(Z)+\mu_{r}(Z^{c}\wedge X):Z\in\mathcal{Z},Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)\}.

Moreover, if
Z=\textup{cyc}(\textup{cl}(X))
, then for any
Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)
, we have

r(X)=r(Z)+\mu_{r}(X\wedge Z^{c}).

Proof.

By
(R3)
, we have

r(X)=r((Z\wedge X)\dot{\vee}(Z^{c}\wedge X))\leq r(Z)+r(Z^{c}\wedge X)\leq r(Z)+\mu_{r}(Z^{c}\wedge X)
for any
Z\in\mathcal{L}
and
Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)
.
In particular,
r(X)\leq\min\{r(Z)+\mu_{r}(Z^{c}\wedge X):Z\in\mathcal{Z},Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)\}
.
We will show that this minimum is attained for
Z=\cyc(\cl(X))
.
Let

We can compute

5
Characterising the cyclic flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid

In this section we provide a set of axioms that characterise when a lattice
\mathcal{Z}
is the lattice of cyclic flats of an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. The sets of axioms given in
[
1
]
and
[
15
]
can be retrieved respectively from the axioms of Definition
5.4
by letting
f
be a function taking values in
\{0,1\}
, and by letting
\mathcal{L}
be a subspace and a Boolean lattice, respectively.

Definition 5.1
.

A
weighted
lattice
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda)
is a lattice
\mathcal{Z}
with a non-negative function
\lambda:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Notation 5.2
.

For the remainder of this section we let
(\mathcal{Z},\vee_{\mathcal{Z}},\wedge_{\mathcal{Z}})
denote an arbitrary lattice such that
\mathcal{Z}
is a subset of
\mathcal{L}
; we say that
\mathcal{Z}
is
supported
on
\mathcal{L}
. Note that the meet and join operations of
\mathcal{Z}
may differ from those of
\mathcal{L}
. Complements are taken in the lattice
\mathcal{L}
(indeed
\mathcal{Z}
may not be complemented).
We let
f:\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{L})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\lambda:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denote arbitrary but fixed non-negative functions. We write

(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
to denote a lattice
\mathcal{Z}
endowed with a pair of such functions
\lambda
and
f
.

Definition 5.3
.

For any
X\in\mathcal{L}
,
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and
Z^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(Z)
we define:

1.

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X;Z^{c},Z):=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})
,

2.

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X):=\textup{min}\{\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X;Z^{c},Z):Z\in\mathcal{Z},Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)\},

3.

\mathcal{Z}(X):=\{Z\in\mathcal{Z}:\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X;Z^{c},Z)=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X),\text{ for some }Z^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)\}
.

The following axioms apply to an arbitrary weighted lattice
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda)
where
\mathcal{Z}
is a subset of
\mathcal{L}
and also where the atoms of
\mathcal{L}
are given arbitrary weights.
We will see that these axioms characterise precisely when
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid with cyclic flats
\mathcal{Z}
.

Definition 5.4
.

We call the following axioms the
cyclic flat
axioms.
Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
.

(Z1)

There exists
Z\in\mathcal{Z}(X)
such that:

(i)

for any
Z^{c},Z^{\tilde{c}}\in\textup{{C}}(Z;X)
, we have
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X,Z^{c},Z)=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X)=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X,Z^{\tilde{c}},Z)
and

\mu_{f}((X\dot{\vee}a_{1})\wedge Z_{1}^{c})+\mu_{f}(X\wedge(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}^{c^{\prime}}))+\mu_{f}((X\dot{\vee}a_{2})\wedge(Z_{1}\vee Z_{2})^{c})

since

\tilde{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}

\lambda(Z)\leq\lambda(Z\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{Z})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})
for any
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z};Z)
by Lemma
5.12
. Taking the minimum over
\tilde{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}
we get
\lambda(Z)\leq\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)
.
∎

Lemma 5.14
.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
satisfies the axiom
(Z1)
. Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
and
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
be such that
(Z1)
is satisfied and let
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X)=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})
for any
Z^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(Z;X)
. If
Z<X
then there exists
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
such that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X)-\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\mu_{f}(X\wedge H^{c})
for any
H^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(H)
.

Proof.

Let
Z^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(Z;X)
. By
(Z1)
(ii)
,
\mu_{f}
is quasi-modular on
[{\mathbf{0}},X\wedge Z^{c}]
. Applying Lemma
4.28
and Corollary
4.29
we can choose a maximal chain
{\mathbf{0}}=H_{m}\lessdot\dots\lessdot H_{0}=X\wedge Z^{c}
with associated layering
L_{1},\dots,L_{m}
such that the
f
is constant on the set of elements of
L_{k}
for each
k\in[m]
, and hence
\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(a_{i})
for arbitrary
a_{i}\in L_{i}
. By the minimality of
\mu_{f}(H_{1})
, we have that
\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})=\mu_{f}(H_{1})+\mu_{f}(a)
for any
a\in L_{1}
.
Let
H\in\mathcal{H}(X)
be such that
H\wedge Z^{c}:=H_{1}
.
By
(Z1)
(i)
, there exists

\tilde{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})
for any
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z};H)
.
By the definition of
\rho
, for any
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z};H)
we have:

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X)=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})

and

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})\leq\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c}).

Summing these inequalities together, yields:

\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})\leq\mu_{f}(X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c}),

for any
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z};H)
.
Let
a\in L_{1}
, so
a\nleq H_{1}
but
a\leq X\wedge Z^{c}
. If
a\nleq\tilde{Z}
, then there exists
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z};H)
such that
a\leq\tilde{Z}^{c}
and hence
X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c}=(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})\dot{\vee}a
. Therefore, by our choice of
H
and the quasi-modularity of
\mu_{f}
on
[{\mathbf{0}},X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c}]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c})+\mu_{f}(a)+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{f}(X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{f}(H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c})+\mu_{f}(a)+\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c}),

and so equality holds in (
5
).

If
a\leq\tilde{Z}
, then we have that
X\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c}=(H\dot{\vee}a)\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c}=H\wedge\tilde{Z}^{c}
since
\tilde{Z}^{c}
decomposes
H
, contradicting (
5
). Therefore, we have that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c})
. It follows that

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(X)-\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})-\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c}).

Since we chose
H\wedge Z^{c}<X\wedge Z^{c}
, we must have that
\mu_{f}(X\wedge Z^{c})-\mu_{f}(H\wedge Z^{c})=\mu_{f}(a)=\mu_{f}(X\wedge H^{c})
for some
H^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(H)
. Since
a\in L_{1}
was arbitrary,
H^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(H)
is arbitrary.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
satisfies the axioms
(Z1)
and
(Z2)
and
(Z5)
. Then
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid, and for every flat
F
of
\mathcal{P}
, we have that
Z\leq F
for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}(F)
.

Proof.

By
(Z1)
and
(Z2)
and
(Z5)
we have that
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. Let
F\in\mathcal{L}
be a flat and let
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(F)=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(F\wedge Z^{c})
for some
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Since our choice of
Z^{c}
decomposes
F
, we have that
F\wedge Z^{c}=(F\vee Z)\wedge Z^{c}
. Therefore, we have that

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(F\vee Z)\geq\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(F)=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}(F\wedge Z^{c})=\lambda(Z)+\mu_{f}((F\vee Z)\wedge Z^{c})\geq\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(F\vee Z),

which means that
Z\leq F
because
F
is a flat.
∎

Corollary 5.16
.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
satisfies the axioms
(Z1)
and
(Z2)
and
(Z5)
. Every cyclic flat of
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

By
(Z1)
and
(Z2)
and
(Z5)
we have that
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid. By Lemma
5.15
, we have that
Z\leq F
. If
F\notin\mathcal{Z}
, then
Z<F
. By Lemma
5.14
we have that
F
is not cyclic.
∎

Lemma 5.17
.

If
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
satisfies the axioms
(Z1)
-
(Z6)
, then every element of
(\mathcal{Z},\lambda,f)
is a cyclic flat of
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
. First we show that
Z
is a flat. Let
a\in\mathcal{L}
be an atom not contained in
Z
. We know that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)=\lambda(Z)
. Let
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z\vee a)=\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}((Z\vee a)\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})
for some
\tilde{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}(Z)
and
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(\tilde{Z};Z\vee a)
.
If
Z\leq\tilde{Z}
then by
(Z6)
it follows that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)<\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z\vee a)
and hence we may assume that
Z\nleq\tilde{Z}
. By Lemma
5.12
, we have
\lambda(Z\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{Z})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})
.
Therefore,

\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)=\lambda(Z)\leq\lambda(Z\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{Z})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}((Z\vee a)\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z\vee a),

where the last inequality holds since by Corollary
4.30
,
\mu_{f}
is increasing on
[{\mathbf{0}},(Z\vee a)\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c}]
.
If
\tilde{Z}\nleq Z
, then the first inequality is strict by
(Z4)
.
If
\tilde{Z}\leq Z
, then
a\notin\tilde{Z}
, which means
Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c}<(Z\vee a)\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c}
, and thus the third inequality is strict by
(Z1)
(ii)
and
(Z6)
.
It follows that
Z
is a flat of
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
.

To show that
Z
is cyclic we let
H\in\mathcal{H}(Z)
and let
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)=\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})
for some
\tilde{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}(H)
and
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{\bf{C}}(\tilde{Z};H)
. If
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)
then there is nothing to show, so assume that
\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)>\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)
. Since
(\mathcal{L},\rho_{(\lambda,f)})
is an
\mathcal{L}
-polymatroid and
Z
is a flat, we can clearly deduce that
H
is also a flat since
[Z,Z\vee a]\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny dn}}}}{{\sim}}}[H,H\vee a]
for any
a\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{L})\backslash\mathcal{A}(Z)
. Therefore,
0<\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z\vee a)-\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)\leq\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H\vee a)-\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.15
, we have that
\tilde{Z}\leq H
. Then by Proposition
2.10
we have that
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z},H)
for all
\tilde{Z}^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(\tilde{Z})
. By Lemma
5.12
we have the following sequence of inequalities.

\displaystyle\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)=\lambda(Z)\leq\lambda(Z\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{Z})\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(\tilde{Z})+\mu_{f}(H\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})+\mu_{f}(H^{c}\wedge Z)

\displaystyle=\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)+\mu_{f}(H^{c}\wedge Z)

for any
H^{c}\in\textup{{C}}(H)
such that
Z\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c}=(H\wedge\penalty\ \tilde{Z}^{c})\dot{\vee}(H^{c}\wedge Z)
. Since
\tilde{Z}^{c}
was chosen arbitrarily, we have that
H^{c}
is arbitrary. First we note that the above gives us
0<\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(Z)-\rho_{(\lambda,f)}(H)\leq\mu_{f}(H^{c}\wedge Z)
, namely that
\mu_{f}(H^{c}\wedge Z)
is positive. Therefore, if
\tilde{Z}=Z
, then the third inequality is strict. If
\tilde{Z}\nleq Z
, then by
(Z4)
the first inequality is strict. If
\tilde{Z}<Z
, then by
(Z4)
we have that either the second or third inequality is strict for some choice of
H^{c}
.
∎

Final Remarks

In this final section, we comment on the cyclic flat axioms given
in Definition
5.4
and compare existing cyclic flat axioms with them.

Remark 6.1
.

We comment on the similarities and differences between the axioms
(Z1)
-
(Z6)
provided here, and those provided in
[
15
, Section 3.1]
, which apply to polymatroids.

•

Axiom
(Z1)
is vacuously satisfied in the case that
\mathcal{L}
is the Boolean lattice, since in that case complements are unique. This immediately gives that

(Z1)
(i)
is satisfied, while
(Z1)
(ii)
also holds in this case since
\mu_{f}
is modular on
\mathcal{L}
.

•

In the case that
\mathcal{L}
is the Boolean lattice, we have that
\mu_{f}
is increasing
on
\mathcal{L}
and hence in that case
(Z2)
is sufficiently replaced by the axiom:

\lambda(Z_{1})+\lambda(Z_{2})\geq\lambda(Z_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{Z}}Z_{2})+\lambda(Z_{1}\vee_{\mathcal{Z}}Z_{2})+\mu_{f}(Z_{1}\wedge Z_{2}\wedge(Z_{1}\wedge_{\mathcal{Z}}Z_{2})^{c}),

for any
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
.

•

(Z4)
(ii)
is never satisfied in the Boolean case because of the modularity of
\mu_{f}
, which therefore causes
(Z4)
to coincide with one of the cyclic flat axioms found in
[
15
]
.

Remark 6.2
.

Now we will comment on some similarities and differences between the axioms
(Z1)
-
(Z6)
, and those provided in
[
1
, Definition 3.1]
, which apply to
q
-matroids. Recall from Remark
4.19
that if

•

Let

(i)

(Z1)
(ii)
is satisfied.

Since

is an increasing function, we have that
(Z2)
becomes equivalent to

Since

(ii)

coincides with
[

Since

[
