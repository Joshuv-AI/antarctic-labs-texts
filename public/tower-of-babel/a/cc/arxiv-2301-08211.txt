Berndt-Type Integrals and Series Associated with Ramanujan and Jacobi Elliptic Functions
Ce Xu and Jianqiang Zhao[1mm] a. School of Mathematics and Statistics, Anhui Normal University,Wuhu 241002, P.R. Chinab. Department of Mathematics, The Bishop’s School, La Jolla, CA 92037, USA

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we evaluate in closed forms two families of infinite integrals containing hyperbolic and trigonometric functions in their integrands. We call them Berndt-type integrals since he initiated the study of similar integrals. We first establish explicit evaluations of four classes of hyperbolic sums by special values of the Gamma function, by two completely different approaches, which extend those sums considered by Ramanujan and Zucker previously. We discover the first by refining two results of Ramanujan concerning some
q
-series. For the second we compare both the Fourier series expansions and the Maclaurin series expansions of a few Jacobi elliptic functions. Next, by contour integrations we convert two families of Berndt-type integrals to the above hyperbolic sums, all of which can be evaluated in closed forms. We then discover explicit formulas for one of the two families. Throughout the paper we present many examples which enable us to formulate a conjectural explicit formula for the other family of the Berndt-type integrals at the end.

Keywords
: Berndt-type integral,
q
-series, hyperbolic and trigonometric functions, contour integration, Jacobi elliptic functions.

Brief review of complete elliptic integral of the first kind

To fix notation we now give a very brief review of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind in this section. As usual,
(a)_{n}
denotes the ascending Pochhammer symbol defined by

\displaystyle(a)_{n}:=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)\quad{\rm and}\quad(a)_{0}:=1,

is a nonnegative integer. Let
\Gamma\left(z\right)
be
the Gamma function as usual. If

\Gamma(z):=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,dt.

The Gaussian or ordinary hypergeometric function

|x|<1

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\displaystyle\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\quad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}).

(2.11)

The complete elliptic integral of the first kind is defined by (see
[
16
]
)

\displaystyle K:=K(x):=K(k^{2}):=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\displaystyle\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\displaystyle\frac{1}{2};1;k^{2}\right).

(2.12)

Here
x=k^{2}
and
k\ (0<k<1)
is the modulus of
K
. The complementary modulus
k^{\prime}
is defined by
k^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-k^{2}}
.
Furthermore, we set as usual

\displaystyle K^{\prime}:=K(k^{\prime 2})=\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\displaystyle\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1-k^{\prime 2}\sin^{2}\varphi}}=\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},\displaystyle\frac{1}{2};1;1-k^{2}\right).

(2.13)

In order to better state our main results, we shall henceforth adopt the notations of Ramanujan (see Berndt’s book
[
2
]
). Let

\displaystyle x:=k^{2},\quad y:=y(x):=\pi\displaystyle\frac{K^{\prime}}{K},\quad q:=q(x):=e^{-y},\quad z:=z(x):=\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}K,\quad z^{\prime}:=\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dx}.

(2.14)

Using the identity

, it is easily shown that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\frac{ab}{c}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a+1,b+1;c+1;x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)=\displaystyle\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(a+n,b+n;c+n;x)\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}).

Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}z}{dx^{n}}=\displaystyle\frac{\left(1/2\right)^{2}_{n}}{n!}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+n,\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+n;1+n;x\right).

Applying the identity (see

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(a,b;\displaystyle\frac{a+b+1}{2};\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\right)=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{a+b+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{a+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\displaystyle\frac{b+1}{2}\right)},

7
Some preliminary results of Jacobi elliptic functions

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
and the second part of Theorem
1.3
we need to switch gears and
turn to Jacobi elliptic functions. This large family of functions have a very long history and
many books have been devoted to its study. We will only need some of them in this paper.

The Jacobi elliptic function
{\rm sn}(u)
is defined via the inversion of the elliptic integral

\displaystyle u=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varphi}\displaystyle\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}t}}\quad(0<k^{2}<1),

(7.65)

namely,
{\rm sn}(u):=\sin\varphi
, where
k=\mod u
is referred to as the elliptic modulus, and
\varphi={\rm am}(u,k)={\rm am}(u)
is referred to as the Jacobi amplitude. The Jacobi elliptic functions
{\rm cn}u
and
{\rm dn}u
may be defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm cn}(u):=\sqrt{1-{\rm sn}^{2}(u)},\quad{\rm dn}(u):=\sqrt{1-k^{2}{\rm sn}^{2}(u)}.

Using the Jacobi elliptic functions
{\rm sn}(u)
,
{\rm cn}(u)
and
{\rm dn}(u)
, we give the definitions of Jacobi elliptic functions
{\rm nc}(u)
,
{\rm dc}(u)
and
{\rm ds}(u)
, as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm nc}(u):=\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm cn}(u)},\quad{\rm dc}(u):=\displaystyle\frac{{\rm dn}(u)}{{\rm cn}(u)},\quad{\rm ds}(u):=\displaystyle\frac{{\rm dn}(u)}{{\rm sn}(u)}.

We will need to use the Maclaurin (or Laurent) series of these functions. First, we consider their higher derivatives.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any integer
m\geq 0
there is a polynomial
f_{m}(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,Z]
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d^{m}}{du^{m}}{\rm nc}(u)=f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2},&\hbox{if $m$ is odd;}\\
1,&\hbox{if $m$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m
. By definition

{\rm nc}(u)=(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-1/2}

so we get
f_{0}(X,Y,Z)=1
. By the chain rule

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}{\rm nc}(u)=\sin{\varphi}\cos{\varphi}(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-3/2}\displaystyle\frac{d{\varphi}}{du}=\sin{\varphi}\cos{\varphi}(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-3/2}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2}.

Thus
f_{1}(X,Y,Z)=XY
. Suppose the lemma holds for
m
, namely,
f_{m}(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,Z]
for some

m\geq 1
. If
m+1>1
is even then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d^{m+1}}{du^{m+1}}{\rm nc}(u)=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{d}{d{\varphi}}\Big(f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2}\Big)\cdot(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{d}{d{\varphi}}\Big(f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})\Big)(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})

\displaystyle\,+(2m+1)f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})\sin{\varphi}\cos{\varphi}(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-3/2}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})

\displaystyle\,-f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}(k^{2}\sin{\varphi}\cos{\varphi}).

Hence

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(X,Y,Z)=

\displaystyle\,\left(Y\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}f_{m}(X,Y,Z)-X\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial Y}f_{m}(X,Y,Z)\right)(1-X^{2})(1-X^{2}Z)

\displaystyle\,+XY\Big((2m+1)(1-X^{2}Z)-Z(1-X^{2})\Big)f_{m}(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,Z]

by induction assumption. If
m+1>1
is odd then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d^{m+1}}{du^{m+1}}{\rm nc}(u)=

\displaystyle\,\displaystyle\frac{d}{d{\varphi}}\Big(f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}\Big)\cdot(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left\{\displaystyle\frac{d}{d{\varphi}}\Big(f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})\Big)(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-1/2}\right.

\displaystyle\,\left.+(2m+1)f_{m}(\sin{\varphi},\cos{\varphi},k^{2})\sin{\varphi}\cos{\varphi}(1-\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{-m-3/2}\right\}(1-k^{2}\sin^{2}{\varphi})^{1/2}

Hence

\displaystyle f_{m+1}(X,Y,Z)=

\displaystyle\,\left(Y\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}f_{m}(X,Y,Z)-X\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial Y}f_{m}(X,Y,Z)\right)(1-X^{2})

\displaystyle\,+(2m+1)XYf_{m}(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,Z]

by induction assumption. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎
