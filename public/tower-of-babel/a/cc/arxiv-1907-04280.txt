Abstract
. The Gauss–Borel or L ​ U LU factorization of Gram matrices of bilinear forms is the pivotal element in the discussion of the theory of biorthogonal polynomials. The construction of biorthogonal families of polynomials and its second kind functions, of the spectral matrices modeling the multiplication by the independent variable x x , the Christoffel–Darboux kernel and its projection properties, are discussed from this point of view. Then, the Hankel case is presented and different properties, specific of this case, as the three terms relations, Heine formulas, Gauss quadrature and the Christoffel-Darboux formula are given. The classical orthogonal polynomial of Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi type are discussed and characterized within this scheme. Finally, it is shown who this approach is instrumental in the derivation of Christoffel formulas for general Christoffel and Geronimus perturbations of the bilinear forms.

2.
LU
factorization and Gram matrix

Given a square complex matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, an
LU
factorization refers to the factorization of
A
into a lower triangular matrix
L
and an upper triangular matrix
U

2.1.
LDU
factorization

An
LDU
decomposition is a decomposition of the form

\displaystyle A=LDU,

where
D
is a diagonal matrix, and
L
and
U
are unitriangular matrices, meaning that all the entries on the diagonals of
L
and
U
are one.

Existence and uniqueness

•

Any
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
admits an
LU
(or
LDU
) factorization if and only if all its leading principal minors are nonzero.

•

If
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a singular matrix of rank
r
, then it admits an
LU
factorization if the first
r
leading principal minors are nonzero, although the converse is not true.

•

If
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
has an
LDU
factorization, then the factorization is unique.

Cholesky factorization

•

If
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a symmetric (
A=A^{\top}
) matrix, we can choose
U
as the transpose of
L

\displaystyle A=LDL^{\top}.

•

When
A
is Hermitian,
A=A^{\dagger}
, we can similarly find

\displaystyle A=LDL^{\dagger}.

These decomposition are called Cholesky factorizations. The Cholesky decomposition always exists and is unique — provided the matrix is positive definite.

2.2.
Schur complements

Given a block matrix
M

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}[c|c]A&B\\
\hline\cr C&D\end{pmatrix}

with
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
,
C\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times p}
and
D\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
, we define its Schur complement with respect to
A

as

\displaystyle M\backslash A:=D-CA^{-1}B\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}.

Proposition 1

(Schur complements and
LU
factorization)
.

If
A
is nonsingular we have for the block matrix
M
the following factorization

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&0_{p\times q}\\
CA^{-1}&I_{q}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0_{p\times q}\\
0_{q\times p}&M\backslash A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&A^{-1}B\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{pmatrix}.

For a detailed account of Schur complements see
[
40
]
.

Given a matrix
M=(M_{i,j})_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
a
\ell+1
,
\ell<N
, truncation is given by

\displaystyle M^{[\ell+1]}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}M_{0,0}&M_{0,1}&\dots&M_{0,\ell}\\
M_{1,0}&M_{1,1}&\dots&M_{1,\ell}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{\ell,0}&M_{\ell,1}&\dots&M_{\ell,\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 2

(Nonzero minors and
LU
factorization)
.

Any nonsingular matrix
M\in\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
with all its leading principal minors different from zero, i. e.,
\det M^{[\ell+1]}\neq 0
,
\ell\in\{0,1,,\dots,N-1\}
, has a
LDU
factorization.

Proof.

As
\det M^{[N-1]}\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}I_{N-1}&0_{(N-1)\times 1}\\
v_{N}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}M^{[N-1]}&0_{(N-1)\times 1}\\
0_{1\times(N-1)}&M\backslash M^{[N-1]}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{N-1}&w_{N}\\
0_{1\times(N-1)}&1\end{pmatrix}

where
v_{N}
and
w_{N}
are row and column vectors, respectively, with
N-1
components.

Now, from
\det M^{[N-2]}\neq 0
we deduce

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07182ptM^{[N-1]}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{N-2}&0_{(N-2)\times 1}\\
v_{N-1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}M^{[N-2]}&0_{(N-2)\times 1}\\
0_{1\times(N-2)}&M^{[N-1]}\backslash M^{[N-2]}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{N-2}&w_{N-1}\\
0_{1\times(N-2)}&1\end{pmatrix}

which inserted in the previous result yields

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}[c|cc]I_{N-2}&\lx@intercol\hfil 0_{(N-2)\times 2}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr\hbox{\multirowsetup*}&1&0\\
&*&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}[c|cc]M^{[N-2]}&\lx@intercol\hfil 0_{(N-2)\times 2}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr\hbox{\multirowsetup$0_{2\times(N-2)}$}&M^{[N-1]}\backslash M^{[N-2]}&0\\
&0&M\backslash M^{[N-1]}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}[c|cc]I_{N-2}&\lx@intercol\hfil*\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\hline\cr\hbox{\multirowsetup$0_{2\times(N-2)}$}&1&*\\
&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

We finally get an
LDU
factorization with

\displaystyle D=\operatorname{diag}\big(M^{[1]}\backslash M^{[0]},M^{[2]}\backslash M^{[1]},\dots,M\backslash M^{[N-1]}\big).

∎

2.3.
Bilinear forms, Gram matrices and
LU
factorizations

A bilinear form
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle
on the ring of complex valued polynomials in the variable
x
,
\mathbb{C}[x]
, is a continuous map

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cccc}\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle:&\mathbb{C}[x]\times\mathbb{C}[x]&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{C}\\
&(P(x),Q(x))&\mapsto&\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle\end{array}

such that for any triple
P(x),Q(x),R(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
the following properties are fulfilled

i)

\left\langle{AP(x)+BQ(x),R(y)}\right\rangle=A\left\langle{P(x),R(y)}\right\rangle+B\left\langle{Q(x),R(y)}\right\rangle
,
\forall A,B\in\mathbb{C}
,

ii)

\left\langle{P(x),AQ(y)+BR(y)}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle A+\left\langle{P(x),R(y)}\right\rangle B
,
\forall A,B\in\mathbb{C}
.

Observe that we have not chosen the conjugate in one of the variables.

For
P(x)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\deg P}p_{k}x^{k}
and
Q(x)=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\deg Q}q_{l}x^{l}
the bilinear form is

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\dots,\deg P\\
l=1,\dots,\deg Q\end{subarray}}p_{k}G_{k,l}q_{l},

\displaystyle G_{k,l}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{x^{k},y^{l}}\right\rangle.

The corresponding
semi-infinite
matrix

\displaystyle G=\begin{pmatrix}G_{0,0}&G_{0,1}&\dots\\
G_{1,0}&G_{1,1}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix},

is the so called
Gram matrix
of the sesquilinear form.

Examples:

•

Borel measures.

A first example is given by a complex (or real) Borel measure
\operatorname{d}\mu
with support
\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{d}\mu)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Given any pair of matrix polynomials
P(x),Q(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
we introduce the following bilinear form

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(x)}\right\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(x)\operatorname{d}\mu(x)Q(x).

•

Example: Linear functionals

We consider the space of polynomials
\mathbb{C}[x]
, with an appropriate topology, as the space of fundamental functions and take the space of generalized functions as the corresponding continuous linear functionals. Take a linear functional
u\in(\mathbb{C}[x])^{\prime}
and consider

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(x)}\right\rangle_{u}=u(P(x)Q(x)).

In both examples the Gram matrix is a Hankel matrix
G_{i+1,j}=G_{i,j+1}

In these cases, the Gram matrix is also known as moment matrix as we have

\displaystyle G_{i,j}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{i+j}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=m_{i+j},

where
m_{j}
is known as the
j
-moment,
for the measure case, while for the linear functional scenario we have

\displaystyle G_{i,j}=u(x^{i+j}).

Schwartz generalized kernels

There are bilinear forms which do not have this particular Hankel type symmetry. Let
u_{x,y}\in(\mathbb{C}[x,y])^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{C}[\![x,y]\!]
so that

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle=\langle u_{x,y},P(x)\otimes Q(y)\rangle.

The Gram matrix of this bilinear form has entries

\displaystyle G_{k,l}=\langle u_{x,y},x^{k}\otimes y^{l}\rangle.

This gives a continuous linear map
\mathcal{L}_{u}:\mathbb{C}[y]\to(\mathbb{C}[x])^{\prime}
such that
\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{u}(Q(y)),P(x)\rangle
.
See
[
37
]
for an introduction to bivariate generalized kernels.

Integrals kernels.

A kernel
u(x,y)
is a complex valued locally integrable function,
that defines an integral operator
f(x)\mapsto g(x)=\int u(x,y)f(y)\operatorname{d}y
and

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}P(x)u(x,y)Q(y)\operatorname{d}x\operatorname{d}y

There is an obvious way of ordering the monomials
\{x^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in a semi-infinite vector

\displaystyle\chi(x)=(1,x,x^{2},\dots)^{\top}

Also, we consider

\displaystyle\chi^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=(x^{-1},x^{-2},x^{-3},\dots)^{\top},

with

\displaystyle(\chi(x))^{\top}\chi^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x-y},

\displaystyle|y|<|x|.

The semi-infinite Gram matrix can be written as follows

G=\left\langle{\chi,\chi^{\top}}\right\rangle
.
For a Borel measure it reads

G=\int\chi(x)\big(\chi(x)\big)^{\top}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)

and for a linear functional

G=\left\langle{u(x),\chi(x)\big(\chi(x)\big)^{\top}}\right\rangle
.
When dealing with an integral kernel we have

G=\int\chi(x)\big(\chi(y)\big)^{\top}u(x,y)\operatorname{d}x

and for a Schwartz kernel

G=\left\langle{u(x,y),\chi(x)\big(\chi(y)\big)^{\top}}\right\rangle
.

3.
Orthogonal polynomials

Definition 1

(Quasi-definite bilinear forms)
.

We say that a bilinear form
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle
is quasi-definite whenever its Gram matrix
has all its leading principal minors different from zero.

Proposition 3

(Quasi-definiteness and
LDU
factorization)
.

The Gram matrix of a quasi-definite bilinear form admits a unique
LDU
factorization.

Given a quasi-definite bilinear form in the space of polynomials we consider the
LDU
factorization of its Gram matrix in the form

\displaystyle G=(S_{1})^{-1}H(S_{2})^{-\top}

where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are lower unitriangular matrices and
H
is a diagonal matrix.

When the quasi-definite bilinear form comes from a Borel measure or a linear functional the corresponding Gram matrix, now known as moment matrix, is symmetric:
G=G^{\top}
. Thus, the
LDU
factorization becomes a Cholesky factorization. Whenever the Borel measure is positive (sign defined will equally do) the moment matrix is a positive definite matrix, i.e., all the principal minors of the moment matrix are strictly positive.
Given either a Borel measure or a linear functional, we consider its
LDU
factorization

\displaystyle G=S^{-1}HS^{-\top},

where
S
is a lower unitriangular matrix and
H
is a diagonal matrix. For a Borel positive measure, the diagonal coefficients
H_{k}
are positive,
H_{k}>0
.

Definition 2

(Constructing the polynomials)
.

Given a Gram matrix and its Gauss–Borel factorization we construct the following two families of polynomials

\displaystyle P_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=S_{1}\chi(x),

\displaystyle P_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=S_{2}\chi(x).

Here
P_{i}=(P_{i,0},P_{i,1},\dots,)
with
P_{i,k}(x)=x^{k}+\cdots
, monic polynomials of degree
k
.

Proposition 4

(Orthogonality relations)
.

The above families of polynomials satisfy the following orthogonality relations

\displaystyle\left\langle{P_{1,k}(x),y^{l}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{l,k}H_{k},

\displaystyle\left\langle{x^{l},P_{2,k}(y)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{l,k}H_{k},

\displaystyle 0\leq l<k.

Proof.

We have that
\left\langle{P_{1}(x),\big(\chi(y)\big)^{\top}}\right\rangle=S_{1}\left\langle{\chi(x),(\chi(y))^{\top}}\right\rangle=S_{1}G=H(S_{2})^{-\top}
is an upper triangular matrix, and the result follows.
∎

Proposition 5

(Biorthogonality relations)
.

The above families of polynomials are biorthogonal

\displaystyle\left\langle{P_{1,k}(x),P_{2,l}(x)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{l,k}H_{k}.

Proof.

We have that
\left\langle{P_{1}(x),\big(P_{2}(y)\big)^{\top}}\right\rangle=S_{1}\left\langle{\chi(x),(\chi(y))^{\top}}\right\rangle(S_{2})^{\top}=S_{1}G=H
.
∎

3.1.
Quasi-determinants

We include this brief section here because, despite the fact that for the standard orthogonality the quotient of determinants is enough to describe adequately the results, in more general situations quasi-determinants are needed. Also, even in this situation they give more compact expressions. As we will see we can understand them as an extension of determinants to noncommutative situations and also as Schur complements.

Some history

In the late 1920 Archibald Richardson, one of the two responsible of Littlewood–Richardson rule, and the famous logician Arend Heyting, founder of intuitionist logic, studied possible extensions of the determinant notion to division rings. Heyting defined the
designant
of a matrix with noncommutative entries, which for
2\times 2
matrices was the Schur complement, and generalized to larger dimensions by induction.

The situation nowadays

1990 till today, was given by Gel’fand, Rektah and collaborators, see
[
26
]
.
Quasi-determinants were defined over free division rings and was early noticed that
is not an analog of the commutative determinant but rather of a ratio of determinants
. A cornerstone for quasi-determinants is the
heredity principle
,
quasi-determinants of quasi-determinants are quasi-determinants
; there is no analog of such a principle for determinants.

We start with
k=2

Proposition 6

(Heredity Principle)
.

Quasi-determinants of quasi-determinants are quasi-determinants.

Given any set
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
the heredity principle allows us to define the quasi-determinant

\displaystyle\Theta_{I}(A)=\Theta_{i_{1}}(\Theta_{i_{2}}(\cdots\Theta_{i_{m}}(A)\cdots)).

Proposition 7

(Quasi-determinantal expressions)
.

The sequence of biorthogonal polynomials an its squared norms are quasi-determinants

\displaystyle P_{1,k}(x)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{*}\begin{bmatrix}[ccccc|c]&&G^{[k]}&&&\begin{matrix}1\\
x\\
\vdots\\
x^{k-1}\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr G_{k,0}&G_{k,1}&\dots&&G_{k,k-1}&x^{k}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle H_{k}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{*}(G^{[k+1]}),

\displaystyle P_{2,k}(x)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{*}\begin{bmatrix}[ccccc|c]&&G^{[k]}&&&\begin{matrix}G_{0,k}\\
G_{1,k}\\
\vdots\\
G_{k,k-1}\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr 1&x&\dots&&x^{k-1}&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

From
LU
factorization,
recalling that
S_{k,k}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}S_{k,0}&S_{k,1}&\dots&S_{k,k-1}\end{pmatrix}G^{[k]}=-\begin{pmatrix}G_{k,0}&G_{k,1}&\dots&G_{k,k-1}\end{pmatrix}.

As
G^{[k]}
is a non singular matrix

On the other hand,

\displaystyle H_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{k,0}&S_{k,1}&\dots&S_{k,k-1}&S_{k,k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G_{0,k}\\
G_{1,k}\\
\vdots\\
G_{k-1,k}\\
G_{k,k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{k,0}&S_{k,1}&\dots&S_{k,k-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}G_{0,k}\\
G_{1,k}\\
\vdots\\
G_{k-1,k}\end{pmatrix}+G_{k,k}.

∎

(

.

For

Whenever

3.3.
Spectral matrices

Let us introduce the shift or spectral matrix

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&1&0&\dots\\
0&0&0&1&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

The following spectral properties hold

\displaystyle\Lambda\chi(x)

\displaystyle=x\chi(x),

\displaystyle\Lambda\chi^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\chi^{*}(x).

If
(E_{i,j})_{s,t}:=\delta_{s,i}\delta_{t,j}
we have

•

In the one hand
\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}=\mathbb{I}
; and, in the other hand,
\Lambda^{\top}\Lambda=\mathbb{I}-E_{0,0}
.

•

\Lambda^{\top}\chi(x)=\frac{1}{x}(\mathbb{I}-E_{0,0})\chi(x)
.

We introduce the semi-infinite matrices

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle:=S_{1}\Lambda(S_{1})^{-1},

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle:=S_{2}\Lambda(S_{2})^{-1}.

Proposition 9

(Spectral matrices are Hessenberg)
.

The spectral matrices are lower uni-Hessenberg matrices, i.e., of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}*&1&0&0&\dots\\
*&*&1&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 10

(Spectrality)
.

The spectral matrices satisfy the eigenvalue property

\displaystyle J_{1}P_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=zP_{1}(z),

\displaystyle J_{2}P_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=zP_{2}(z).

Proposition 11

(Eigenvalues of the truncation and roots of the polynomials)
.

The roots of
P_{i,k}(z)
and the eigenvalues of the truncation
J_{i}^{[k]}
coincide.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle J_{i}^{[k]}\begin{pmatrix}P_{i,0}(x)\\
P_{i,1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k-1}(x)\end{pmatrix}=x\begin{pmatrix}P_{i,0}(x)\\
P_{i,1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k-1}(x)\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

For a root
\alpha
, i.e.,
P_{i,k}(\alpha)=0
we arrive to

\displaystyle\left(J^{[k]}\right)\begin{pmatrix}P_{i,0}(\alpha)\\
P_{1}(\alpha)\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k-1}(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}P_{i,0}(\alpha)\\
P_{i,1}(\alpha)\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k-1}(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}

and, therefore, we have the eigenvector
\begin{pmatrix}P_{i,0}(\alpha)\\
P_{i,1}(\alpha)\\
\vdots\\
P_{i,k-1}(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}
with eigenvalue

.
∎

3.4.
Christoffel–Darboux kernels

Definition 4

(Christoffel–Darboux kernels)
.

Given two sequences of matrix biorthogonal polynomials

\big\{P_{1,k}(x)\big\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and
\big\{P_{2,k}(y)\big\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
,

with respect to the sesquilinear form
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle_{u}
, we define the
n
-th Christoffel–Darboux kernel matrix polynomial

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{2,k}(y)(H_{k})^{-1}P_{1,k}(x),

and the mixed Christoffel–Darboux kernel

\displaystyle K^{\operatorname{mix}}_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{2,k}(y)(H_{k})^{-1}C_{1,k}(x).

Proposition 12

(Projection properties)
.

i)

For a quasidefinite generalized kernel
u_{x,y}
,
the corresponding Christoffel–Darboux kernel gives the projection operator

\displaystyle\left\langle{K_{n}(x,z),\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{j}P_{2,j}(y)}\right\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{j}P_{2,j}(z),

\displaystyle\left\langle{\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{j}P_{1,j}(x),K_{n}(z,y)}\right\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}P_{1,j}(z).

for any
m\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

ii)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{K_{n}(x,z),y^{l}}\right\rangle_{u}

\displaystyle=z^{l},

\displaystyle l\in

\displaystyle\{0,1,\dots,n\}.

Proposition 13

(ABC Theorem (Aitken, Berg and Collar)
[
36
]
)
.

We have the following relation

\displaystyle K^{[l]}(x,y)=\left(\chi^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left(G^{[l]}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(x).

Proof.

Is a consequence of the following

\displaystyle\left(\chi^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left(G^{[l]}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\chi^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left((S_{1}^{[l]})^{-1}H^{[l]}(S_{2}^{[l]})^{-\top}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(x)=\left(S_{2}^{[l]}\chi^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left(H^{[l]}\right)^{-1}S_{1}^{[l]}\chi^{[l]}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(P_{2}^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left(H^{[l]}\right)^{-1}P_{1}^{[l]}(x).

∎

Proposition 14

(Reproducing property)
.

As we are dealing with a projection we find

\displaystyle\langle K^{[l]}(x,z_{2}),K^{[l]}(z_{1},y)\rangle_{u}=K^{[l]}(z_{1},z_{2}).

Proof.

As an exercise, let us use the ABC theorem

\langle K^{[l]}(x,z_{2}),K^{[l]}(z_{1},y)\rangle_{u}\begin{aligned} &=\left\langle\left(\chi^{[l]}(z_{2})\right)^{\top}\left(G^{[l]}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(x),\left(\chi^{[l]}(y)\right)^{\top}\left(G^{[l]}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(z_{1})\right\rangle_{u}\\
&=\left(\chi^{[l]}(z_{2})\right)^{\top}\left(G^{[l]}\right)^{-1}\chi^{[l]}(z_{1}).\end{aligned}

∎

4.
Standard orthogonality: Hankel reduction

Recall that for bilinear forms associated to a Borel measure or a linear functional the Gram matrix is a Hankel matrix,
G_{i,j+1}=G_{i+1,j}
. We will consider in this section some properties that appear in this situation and not in the general scheme.

4.1.
Recursion relations

This property is just a reflection of the self-adjointness of the multiplication operator by
x
with respect to inner product is reflected in the Hankel structure of the moment matrix

\displaystyle\langle xf,h\rangle_{\mu}=\langle f,xh\rangle_{\mu}\Rightarrow\Lambda G=G\Lambda^{\top}\Rightarrow G_{i,j}=G_{i+j}.

That leads to the tridiagonal form of the spectral matrices, now named after Jacobi.

Proposition 15
.

The spectral matrices are linked by

J_{1}H=H(J_{2})^{\top}
.

Proof.

From the
LU
factorization (Cholesky)
G=S^{-1}HS^{-\top}
and the symmetry
\Lambda G=G\Lambda^{\top}
we find

\displaystyle S\Lambda S^{-1}=H(S\Lambda S^{-1})^{\top}H^{-1}.

∎

This tridiagonal Jacobi matrix
J:=J_{1}
can be written as follows

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}-S_{10}&1&0&0&0&0&\dots\\
H_{1}H_{0}^{-1}&S_{10}-S_{21}&1&0&0&0&\dots\\
0&H_{2}H_{1}^{-1}&S_{21}-S_{32}&1&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&H_{3}H_{2}^{-1}&S_{32}-S_{43}&1&0&\dots\\
0&0&0&H_{4}H_{3}^{-1}&S_{43}-S_{54}&1&\dots\\
0&0&0&0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the spectral properties lead to the well known recursion relations.

Proposition 16

(3-term relations)
.

The orthogonal polynomials and the corresponding second kind functions fulfill

\displaystyle JP(x)

\displaystyle=xP(x),

\displaystyle\frac{H_{k}}{H_{k-1}}P_{k-1}+(S_{k,k-1}-S_{k+1,k})P_{k}+P_{k+1}=xP_{k},

\displaystyle JC(x)

\displaystyle=xC(x)-H_{0}\boldsymbol{e}_{0},

\displaystyle\frac{H_{k}}{H_{k-1}}C_{k-1}+(S_{k,k-1}-S_{k+1,k})C_{k}+C_{k+1}=xC_{k},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{0}:=\left(1,0,0,\dots,0,\dots\right)^{\top}

4.2.
Heine formulas

As the Gram matrix now is a moment matrix we find the well known Heine formulas:

Proposition 17

(Heine integral representation)
.

The orthogonal polynomials can be written as follows

\displaystyle P_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!\det\left[G^{[k]}\right]}\int\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-x_{j})\prod_{1\leq j<n\leq k}(x_{n}-x_{j})^{2}\operatorname{d}\mu(x_{1})\operatorname{d}\mu(x_{2})\dots\operatorname{d}\mu(x_{k})

Proof.

From the quasi-determinantal expression we get

\displaystyle P_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\left[G^{[k]}\right]}\det\begin{bmatrix}[ccccc|c]&&G^{[k]}&&&\begin{matrix}1\\
x\\
\vdots\\
x^{k-1}\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr G_{k,0}&G_{k,1}&\dots&&G_{k,k-1}&x^{k}\end{bmatrix},

and using the Vandermonde formula we get the result.
∎

4.3.
Gauss quadrature formula

Proposition 18

(Eigenvalues of the truncation of the spectral matrix)
.

Assume that the measure
\mu
does not change sign in its support
(a,b)
. Then, all eigenvalues of the truncations of the spectral matrices
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
belong to
(a,b)
and are simple.

Proof.

Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset(a,b)
be the points where the polynomial
P_{n}
changes sign in
(a,b)
. Then, as
P_{n}(z)
has
n
roots,
m\leq n
. Therefore,
(x-a_{1})\dots(x-a_{m})P_{n}(x)
does not change sign in
(a,b)
, but from the orthogonality relations we know that
P_{n}
is orthogonal to any polynomial of degree less than
n
and, consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}(x-a_{1})\dots(x-a_{m})P_{n}(x)\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=0,

for
m<n
, which is, as
\mu
does not change sign in its support, contradictory. Hence, the only possibility is to have
m=n
.
∎

Proposition 19

(Powers of truncations of the spectral matrices)
.

\displaystyle\left(J^{[k]}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{k,1}^{j}&&&\\
&\alpha_{k,2}^{j}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\alpha_{k,k}^{j}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
P_{1}(\alpha_{k,1})&P_{1}(\alpha_{k,2})&\dots&P_{1}(\alpha_{k,k})\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
P_{k-1}(\alpha_{k,1})&P_{k-1}(\alpha_{k,2})&\dots&P_{k-1}(\alpha_{k,k})\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 20
.

The following identity holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x^{j}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=(J^{j})_{0,0}H_{0}.

Proof.

It follows from 3-term relation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x^{j}P(x)&=J^{j}P(x)&&\Longrightarrow&x^{j}&=(J^{j})_{0,0}+(J^{j})_{0,1}P_{1}(x)+\dots+(J^{j})_{0,j}P_{j}(x),\end{aligned}

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x^{j}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=(J^{j})_{0,0}\langle 1,1\rangle+(J^{j})_{0,1}\langle P_{1},1\rangle+\dots+(J^{j})_{0,j}\langle P_{j},1\rangle=(J^{j})_{0,0}H_{0}.

∎

Proposition 21

(Gaussian cuadrature)
.

The roots
\{\alpha_{k,l}\}_{l=1}^{k}
of the orthogonal polynomials
P_{k}(x)
are the
k
points for the quadrature of
\mu
with
precision
2k-1
. Namely,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x^{j}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\lambda_{j,l}\alpha_{k,l}^{j},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq j\leq 2k-1,

for some coefficients
\lambda_{j,l}
.

Proof.

From the
(2+1)
-diagonal structure of the Jacobi matrix
J
it follows that

\left(J^{j}\right)_{0,0}=\left(\left[J^{[k]}\right]^{j}\right)_{0,0}
whenever
0\leq j\leq 2k-1
.

Now, using the diagonal form of the powers of
J
and denoting by

\mathbb{P}^{-1}\rfloor_{1}
the first column of
\mathbb{P}^{-1}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x^{j}\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=\left(\left[J^{[k]}\right]^{j}\right)_{0,0}H_{0}=H_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{k,1}^{j}&\alpha_{k,2}^{j}&\dots&\alpha_{k,k}^{j}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}^{-1}\rfloor_{1}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\lambda_{j,l}\alpha_{k,l}^{j},

for
0\leq j\leq 2k-1
.

∎

4.4.
Christoffel–Darboux formula

Proposition 22

(Christoffel–Darboux formula)
.

The Christoffel–Darboux kernel satisfies

\displaystyle(x-y)K_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=P_{n}(y)(H_{n})^{-1}P_{n+1}(x)-P_{n+1}(y)(H_{n})^{-1}P_{n}(x),

with confluent version given by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\frac{(P_{k}(x))^{2}}{H_{k}}=\frac{1}{H_{l-1}}\Big(P^{\prime}_{l}(x)P_{l-1}(x)-P^{\prime}_{l-1}(x)P_{l}(x)\Big)

Proof.

From the eigenvalue property we get

\displaystyle\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[l]}P(y)^{[l]}+\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[l,\geq l]}P(y)^{[\geq l]}

\displaystyle=y\left(H^{-1}\right)^{[l]}P(y)^{[l]},

\displaystyle[P(x)^{\top}]^{[l]}\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[l]}+[P(x)^{\top}]^{[\geq l]}\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[\geq l,l]}

\displaystyle=x[P(x)^{\top}]^{[l]}\left(H^{-1}\right)^{[l]}.

Left multiply the first eq. by
[P(x)^{\top}]^{[l]}
and right multiply the second by
P(y)^{[l]}
, and subtract both results to get

\displaystyle[P(x)^{\top}]^{[l]}\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[l,\geq l]}P(y)^{[\geq l]}-[P(x)^{\top}]^{[\geq l]}\left(H^{-1}J\right)^{[\geq l,l]}P(y)^{[l]}=(y-x)[P(x)^{\top}]^{[l]}\cdot\left(H^{-1}\right)^{[l]}P(y)^{[l]}

and the result follows. To prove the confluent case just take the limit
y\rightarrow x
in the previous case.
∎

Proposition 23

(Christoffel–Darboux formula for mixed kernels)
.

The mixed Christoffel-Darboux kernel fulfills

\displaystyle(x-{y})K^{\operatorname{(mix})}_{n}(x,y)=P_{n}(y)(H_{n})^{-1}C_{n+1}(x)-P_{n+1}(y)(H_{n})^{-1}C_{n}(x)+1

Proof.

As in the no mixed case consider the expression
P(x)^{\top}H^{-1}JC(y)
and the two possible ways of computing them
either as

P(x)^{\top}\left[H^{-1}JC(y)\right]
or as
\left[P(x)^{\top}H^{-1}J\right]C(y)
.

∎

5.
Very classical orthogonal polynomials: Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials

Here we study the definite positive case. Neither Bessel polynomials nor Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials with right parameters leading to quasi definite linear functionals are considered.
The very classical orthogonal polynomials, Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi can be understood
à la Bochner
as the eigenfunctions of second order differential operators, associated with corresponding definite positive Borel measures. They also can be characterized by a Rodrigues formula or if you want as those that when derivated preserve the orthogonal character.

Definition 5

(Pearson equation)
.

The weight
u_{\gamma}
is said very classical whenever we have polynomials

p_{2}(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
and
p_{1,\gamma}(x)=(A_{\gamma}-2a)x+(B_{\gamma}-b)
with
A_{\gamma}\neq 0
such that
u_{\gamma}

satisfies

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}x}u_{\gamma}=p_{1,\gamma}(x)u_{\gamma}

The well known very classical weights are:

•

Hermite
u(x)=\operatorname{e}^{-x^{2}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
p_{1}=-2x
,
p_{2}=1

•

Laguerre
u_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha}\operatorname{e}^{-x}
,
\alpha>-1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with
p_{1,\alpha}=(\alpha-x)
,
p_{2}=x

•

Jacobi
u_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
,
\alpha,\beta>-1
,
x\in(-1,1)
, with
p_{1,\alpha,\beta}=-[(\alpha-\beta)+(\alpha+\beta)x]
,
p_{2}=1-x^{2}

They depend upon: zero (
\gamma=\{\varnothing\}
), one (
\gamma=\{\alpha\}
) and two parameters (
\gamma=\{\alpha,\beta\}
) respectively.

•

We denote
u_{\gamma+1}(x)
the action of increasing by one all the parameters in a classical weight
u_{\gamma}(x)
. For example, Hermite do not change (no parameter present), in Laguerre we have
\alpha\mapsto\alpha+1
while in Jacobi the shift is
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\alpha+1,\beta+1)

•

p_{2}(x)u_{\gamma}(x)=u_{\gamma+1}(x)

Proposition 24

(Properties)
.

For the very classical weights we have

\displaystyle p_{2}u_{\gamma}\big\lvert_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=u_{\gamma+1}\big\lvert_{\partial\Omega}=0,

\displaystyle\langle p_{2}f^{\prime},h\rangle_{u_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=-\langle f,(p_{2}^{\prime}+p_{1})h\rangle_{u_{\gamma}}-\langle f,p_{2}h^{\prime}\rangle_{u_{\gamma}}.

We can matrix represent the action of derivation with the semi-infinite matrix

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\dots\\
1&0&0&0&\dots\\
0&2&0&0&\dots\\
0&0&3&0&\dots\\
0&0&0&4&\ddots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle D\chi(x)=\chi(x)^{\prime}.

Using
f(x)=\left(f_{0},f_{1},\dots\right)\chi(x)=\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\chi(x)

we can write

\displaystyle xf(x)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\Lambda\chi(x),

\displaystyle f(x)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}D\chi(x).

Proposition 25

(Symmetry of the moment matrix)
.

The Gram matrices of the classical weights are linked by

\displaystyle DG_{\gamma+1}=-G_{\gamma}\Big(p_{2}^{\prime}(\Lambda)+p_{1,\gamma}(\Lambda)+Dp_{2}(\Lambda)\Big)^{\top}.

Proposition 26

(

factorization and classical weights)
.

The

\displaystyle S_{\gamma}DS_{\gamma+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-H_{\gamma}\Big(S_{\gamma+1}\big(p_{2}^{\prime}(\Lambda)+p_{1,\gamma}(\Lambda)+Dp_{2}(\Lambda)\big)S_{\gamma}^{-1}\Big)^{\top}H_{\gamma+1}^{-1}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle(S_{\gamma})_{n+1,n}

\displaystyle=(n+1)\frac{B+nb}{A+2na},

\displaystyle(H_{\gamma})_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{-n}{A_{\gamma}+(n-1)a}(H_{\gamma+1})_{n-1}.

Proposition 27

(Self-adjoint differential operator)
.

For the classical weights we have a self-adjoint second order differential operator, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\langle\left[p_{2}\frac{\operatorname{d}^{2}}{\operatorname{d}x^{2}}+(p_{2}^{\prime}+p_{1,_{\gamma}})\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}x}\right]f,h\right\rangle_{u_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle f,\left[p_{2}\frac{\operatorname{d}^{2}}{\operatorname{d}x^{2}}+(p_{2}^{\prime}+p_{1,_{\gamma}})\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}x}\right]h\right\rangle_{u_{\gamma}}.

Proposition 28

(Semi-infinite matrix version)
.

The matrices of classical moments enjoy the following additional symmetry given by the matrix representation of
a linear second-order differential operator with polynomial coefficients

\displaystyle[D^{2}(a\Lambda^{2}+b\Lambda+c)+D(A_{\gamma}\Lambda+B_{\gamma})]G_{\gamma}=G_{\gamma}\left[D^{2}(a\Lambda^{2}+b\Lambda+c)+D(A_{\gamma}\Lambda+B_{\gamma})\right]^{\top}.

Observe that calling

\displaystyle[M^{2}(aJ^{2}+bJ+c)+M(A_{\gamma}J+B_{\gamma})]=\left[M^{2}(aJ^{2}+bJ+c)+M(A_{\gamma}J+B_{\gamma})\right]^{\top}.

(Diagonalizing the self-adjoint differential operator)

\displaystyle N_{\gamma}:=S_{\gamma}\left[D^{2}(a\Lambda^{2}+b\Lambda+c)+D(A_{\gamma}\Lambda+B_{\gamma})\right]S_{\gamma}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&0&\dots\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

The diagonal coefficients,
(N_{\gamma})_{n}=n(A_{\gamma}+(n-1)a)
, are the eigenvalues of the sequence of classical orthogonal polynomials, being these one the corresponding eigenfunctions

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}

\displaystyle:=p_{2}\frac{\operatorname{d}^{2}}{\operatorname{d}x^{2}}+(p_{2}^{\prime}+p_{1,_{\gamma}})\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}x}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}\left[P_{\gamma}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=N_{\gamma}P_{\gamma}(x).

In particular, for the three classical families, we have

.
\dfrac{\operatorname{d}u}{\operatorname{d}x}=-2xu

,

,

,

,

\displaystyle=0,

6.
Christoffel and Geronimus transformations

6.1.
Some history

Three perturbations have attracted the interest of the researchers.

Christoffel perturbations, that appear when you consider a new functional
\hat{u}=p(x)u
, where
p(x)
is a polynomial,
were
studied in 1858 by the German mathematician E. B. Christoffel
in
[
23
]

in the framework of Gaussian quadrature rules.

Christoffel found explicit formulas relating the corresponding sequences of orthogonal polynomials with respect to two measures, the Lebesgue measure
\operatorname{d}\mu
supported in the interval
(-1,1)
and
d\hat{\mu}(x)=p(x)d\mu(x)
, with
p(x)=(x-q_{1})\cdots(x-q_{N})
a signed polynomial in the support of
\operatorname{d}\mu
, as well as the distribution of their zeros as nodes in such quadrature rules. Nowadays, these are called
Christoffel formulas
, and can be considered a classical result in the theory of orthogonal polynomials which can be found in a number of textbooks, see for example
Chihara, Szegő or Gautschi.

In the theory of orthogonal polynomials,
connection formulas
between two families of orthogonal polynomials allow to express any polynomial of a given degree
n
as a linear combination of all polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n
in the second family. A noteworthy fact regarding the Christoffel finding is that in this case the number of terms does not grow with the degree
n
but remarkably, and on the contrary, remain constant, equal to the degree of the perturbing polynomial.

Geronimus transformation appears when you are dealing with perturbed functionals
v
defined by
p(x)v=u,
where
p(x)
is a polynomial. Such a kind of transformations were used by the
Russian mathematician J. L. Geronimus
in

[
27
]

in order to have a nice proof of a result by W. Hahn concerning the characterization of classical orthogonal polynomials (Hermite, Laguerre, Jacobi, and Bessel) as those orthogonal polynomials whose first derivatives are also orthogonal polynomials, for an English account of Geronimus’ paper see
[
28
]
.

Again, as happened for the Christoffel transformation, within the Geronimus transformation one can find Christoffel type formulas, now in terms of the second kind functions, relating the corresponding sequences of orthogonal polynomials, for example the work of P. Maroni studied this situation for a perturbation of the type
p(x)=x-a
.

Despite that in the paper by Geronimus no Christoffel type formula was derived, in order to distinguish these Christoffel type formulas from those for Christoffel transformations, we refer to them as Christoffel–Geronimus. formulas.

The more general problem related to linear functionals
u
and
v
satisfying
p(x)u=q(x)v,
where
p(x),q(x)
are polynomials has been analyzed the Russian mathematician V. B. Uvarov back in 1969
[
39
]
see also
[
41
]
where the term linear spectral was given.

Uvarov found Christoffel type formulas, see
[
39
]
,that allow for any pair of perturbing polynomials
p(x)
and
q(x)
, to find the new orthogonal polynomials in terms of determinantal expressions of the original unperturbed second kind functions and orthogonal polynomials. On the other hand, the addition of a finite number of Dirac masses to a linear functional appears in the framework of the spectral analysis of fourth order linear differential operators with polynomial coefficients and with orthogonal polynomials as eigenfunctions. Therein you have the so called Laguerre-type, Legendre-type and Jacobi-type orthogonal polynomials introduced by H. L. Krall.

A more general analysis from the point of view of the algebraic properties of the sequences of orthogonal polynomials associated to the linear functionals
u
and
w=u+\sum_{n=0}^{N}M_{n}\delta(x-a_{n})
, the so-called general Uvarov transformation by Zhedanov, see
[
41
]
, has been done for the positive definite case by Uvarov.

Definition 6

(Christoffel transformations and the Schwartz kernel)
.

Given a polynomial

of degree

, and a generalized kernel

Proposition 30

(Christoffel transformation and the bilinear form and Gram matrix)
.

The perturbed Gram matrix
\hat{G}:=\langle\chi(x),\chi(y)\rangle_{\hat{u}}
and the original one
G
satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{G}=W_{C}(\Lambda)G.

The sequilinear forms are related by

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)W_{G}(y)}\right\rangle_{\hat{u}}=\left\langle{P(x)W_{C}(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle_{u}.

Definition 7

(Geronimus transformations and the Schwartz kernel)
.

Given a generalized kernel
u_{x,y}\in\mathcal{O}_{c}^{\prime}
with a given support
\operatorname{supp}u_{x,y}
, and a polynomial

\check{u}_{x,y}

\displaystyle\check{u}_{x,y}W(y)=u_{x,y}.

Proposition 31

(Geronimus transformation and the bilinear form and Gram matrix)
.

In terms of bilinear forms a Geronimus transformation fulfills

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)W(y)}\right\rangle_{\check{u}}=\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle_{u},

while, in terms of the corresponding Gram matrices, satisfies

\displaystyle\check{G}W(\Lambda^{\top})=G.

Definition 8

(Linear spectral transformations and the Schwartz kernel)
.

Given two polynomials

of degrees

, and a generalized kernel

\hat{u}_{x,y}
of
u_{x,y}
is a matrix of generalized kernels such that

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{x,y}W_{G}(y)=W_{C}(x)u_{x,y}.

Proposition 32

(Linear spectral transformation and the bilinear form and Gram matrix)
.

The perturbed Gram matrix
\hat{G}:=\langle\chi(x),\chi(y)\rangle_{\hat{u}}
and the original one
G
satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{G}W_{G}(\Lambda^{\top})=W_{C}(\Lambda)G.

The sequilinear forms are related by

\displaystyle\left\langle{P(x),Q(y)W_{G}(y)}\right\rangle_{\hat{u}}=\left\langle{P(x)W_{C}(x),Q(y)}\right\rangle_{u}.

Proposition 33

(Hankel case: Christoffel and Geronimus)
.

Given monic polynomials

\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}=N

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle:=W_{C}(x)\mu(x),

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\Omega.

\displaystyle\check{\mu}(x)

Notice that the Geronimus transformation of a measure does not need to be a measure.

Proposition 34

(Hankel case: linear spectral)

Whenever the polynomials

and
W_{G}(x)
are coprime the composition of the above transformations
is the linear spectral transformation

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle:=\widehat{(\check{\mu})}(x)=\frac{W_{C}(x)\mu(x)}{W_{G}(x)}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{i}-1}(-1)^{l}\frac{\xi_{i,l}}{l!}W_{G}(x)\delta^{(l)}(x-q_{i}),

Why linear spectral? Because the behavior of the Markov function

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(x-r)\operatorname{d}\mu}{y-x}=(y-r)\int\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu}{y-x}-\int\operatorname{d}\mu(x)=(y-r)C_{0}(y)-h_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(y-r)C_{0}(y)-(q-r)C_{0}(q)+(q-r)\xi}{(y-q)}.

It also holds that

\displaystyle\check{P}_{2,n}(y)=H_{n-1}\frac{(y-a)(K_{n-1}^{(\text{mix})}(a,y)-\left\langle{\xi_{x},K_{n-1}(x,y)}\right\rangle)+1}{C_{1,n-1}(a)-\left\langle{\xi_{x},P_{1,n-1}(x)}\right\rangle}.

Proof.

The mixed kernel
\check{K}_{n-1}^{(\text{mix})}(x,y)
will have singularity problems at

. This issue can be handled as before with the aid of the CD kernel and we get

\displaystyle(y-a)(K_{n-1}^{(\text{mix})}(a,y)-\left\langle{\xi_{x},K_{n-1}(x,y)}\right\rangle)+1

\displaystyle=\check{P}_{2,n}(y)(\check{H}_{n})^{-1}\omega_{n,n-1}\big(C_{1,n-1}(a)-\left\langle{\xi_{x},P_{1,n-1}(x)}\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\check{P}_{2,n}(y)(H_{n-1})^{-1}\big(C_{1,n-1}(a)-\left\langle{\xi_{x},P_{1,n-1}(x)}\right\rangle\big).

∎

Proposition 46
.
