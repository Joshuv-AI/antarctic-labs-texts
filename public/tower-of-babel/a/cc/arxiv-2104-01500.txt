1.1
State of Art

Let
t>0
and
\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For real-valued functions
\varphi
resp.
\psi
with membership in the Schwarz space
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
resp. the
space of tempered distributions

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, let

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}\varphi)(\bm{\xi})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})e^{-i\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle}d\bm{x},

\displaystyle~\varphi\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})~

(1)

be the Fourier transform over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\psi)(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\psi(\bm{\xi})e^{i\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle}d\bm{\xi},

\displaystyle~\psi\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})~

(2)

its inverse.

The kernel function

defined through the Fourier inversion formula
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}e^{-\tau|\bm{\xi}|^{\alpha}})(\bm{x})

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\tau|\bm{\xi}|^{\alpha}}e^{i\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle}d\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle(~\Re(\tau)\geq 0~)

(3)

arises as the fundamental solution of the fractional heat equation of order
\alpha

(\alpha>0)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t)=-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t).

(4)

Indeed, for
\tau=t
the kernel function
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},t)
solves eq. (
4
) and satisfies the initial condition

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},0):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},t)=\delta(\bm{x}).

An effective way to reduce the right-hand side of (
3
) to a one-dimensional integral is provided in
[
1

by the following Fourier inversion formula

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\phi(|\bm{\xi}|)e^{i\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle}d\bm{\xi}=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}{|\bm{x}|^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(\rho)\rho^{\frac{n}{2}}J_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(\rho|\bm{x}|)d\rho

(5)

underlying to radial functions
\psi(\bm{\xi})=\phi(|\bm{\xi}|)
(see also
[
2

). Moreover, a simple calculation based on the change of variable
\rho\rightarrow\frac{\rho}{|\bm{x}|}
on the right-hand side of (
5
) shows that eq. (
3
) can be cast in the form

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau\left(\frac{\rho}{|\bm{x}|}\right)^{\alpha}}~\rho^{\frac{n}{2}}J_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(\rho)d\rho~~~(\Re(\tau)\geq 0),

(6)

where

denotes the
\nu-
th Bessel function (see
[
3
, Chapter 14]
).

We point out here that
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)
corresponds to higher-dimensional generalization of the so-called Lévy distribution of order
\alpha
(cf.
[
4
]
). In the one-dimensional case, i.e.
\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}J_{-\frac{1}{2}}(\rho)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cos(\rho)
when

(see
[
3
, subsection 14.7]
), the integral representation (

) has been tackled by renowned mathematicians such as F. Bernstein
[
5
]
, P. Lévy
[
6
]
, G. Polya
[
7
]
, W.R. Burwell
[
8
]
and D.V. Widder
[
9
]
.

In higher dimensions, the interest in studying the fundamental solution for fractional diffusion problems of type (
4
) for values of
\alpha
in the range
0<\alpha\leq 2
has a vast literature (see e.g.
[
10
]
and the references therein), especially when we are dealing with
symmetric stable processes
of index
\alpha
(cf.
[
11
,
12
]
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
through the
positivity condition

\displaystyle\varphi\geq 0\Longrightarrow\exp(-t\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})\varphi\geq 0
of the diffusive semigroup
\displaystyle\left\{\exp(-t\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})\right\}_{t\geq 0}
of Markovian type.

In stark contrast, the cases where
\alpha>2
– corresponding to a fractional interpolation of higher-order heat equations (case of
\frac{\alpha}{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{1\}
) – are less understood (cf.
[
13
]
). In fact, the oscillator behavior of the Bessel functions appearing on the right-hand side of (
6
) shows in turn that the
positivity condition
is no longer preserved for the semigroup
\left\{\exp(-t\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})\right\}_{t\geq 0}
in case of
\alpha\geq 4
and whence, the classical methods adopted for the second order heat equation do cannot be applied in this context. We refer to
[
14
]
for a wide overview of the problem and to
[
15
]
for further advances beyond the biharmonic case (i.e. when
\frac{\alpha}{2}=2
) treated on the paper
[
16
]
.

Accordingly to the literature, the optimal environment to deal with the biharmonic heat equation and fractional heat equation (
4
) of order
\alpha>4
can be provided by an exploitation of the notion of pseudo-Markov processes or even as composition of Brownian motions
or stable processes with Brownian motions. Those approaches has been popularized in the stochastic community since fundamental the papers of Hochberg (cf.
[
17
]
) and Funaki (cf.
[
18
]
), but we will not pursue these topics in the present paper.

On the other hand, we notice that the model problem involving the space-fractional heat equation (
4
) only provides faithful generalizations for higher-order heat equations of even order (i.e.
\alpha=2m
, with
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
).
Also, on the multidimensional generalization of the Lévy-Feller approach the lack of a closed form for the underlying generator semigroup is one of the major difficulties (cf.
[
19
]
).

To the best of our knowledge, to derive faithful analogues of Airy like functions in higher dimensions, carrying higher-order heat operators of odd type (cf.
[
20
]
), one may consider the pseudodifferential reformulation of Dirac theory in the spirit of the seminal paper
[
21
]
of C. Li, A. McIntosh & T. Qian (see also
[
22
]
for a broad overview) in a way that the Dirac operator
\bm{D}
is recasted in terms of the identity
\bm{D}=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{H}
. Here and elsewhere,
\mathcal{H}
stands for the Riesz-Hilbert transform.

The aim of this paper is to amalgate the study of the fundamental solutions of the polyharmonic heat-type
\partial_{t}+(-\Delta)^{m}
(
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
) and the higher-order Dirac-type operators
\partial_{t}\pm i\bm{D}^{2m+1}
(
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
) as well. To this end, we will investigate the fundamental solutions
\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t;\theta)

of the modified version of the space-fractional heat equation (
4
) carrying the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
of order
\alpha
, with
2m\leq\alpha<2m+2
(
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{t}\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t;\theta)=-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t;\theta)&,~\mbox{for}&(\bm{x},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,\infty)\\
\\
\Phi_{\alpha}(\bm{x},0;\theta)=\delta(\bm{x})&,~\mbox{for}&\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\end{array}\right.

The evolution equation (
1.1
) is build upon the Cauchy problem of Lévy-Feller type considered by Gorenflo et al in
[
23
]
.
Here, the exponentiation operator

\exp\left(i\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)
carrying the
skewness parameter

\theta
in the range

mimics the fractional Hilbert transform in optics introduced by Lohmann et al in
[
24
]
(see also
[
25
, section 4]
and
[
26
, section 4]
).

The main gain of our model problem formulation agaisnt the multidimensional approaches available on the literature relies on the replacement of the parametrized measure on the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
– hard to compute, in general (cf.
[
27
]
)– by a rotation action in the plane parametrized by
\theta\mapsto\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)
– easy to describe through the Fourier multiplier reformulation of
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\frac{-i\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|}\mathcal{F}
(cf.
[
25
, section 4]
).

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Clifford algebras and Dirac operators

Let
{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfying the graded anti-commuting relations

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{j}{\bf e}_{k}+{\bf e}_{k}{\bf e}_{j}=-2\delta_{jk}

for any

\displaystyle j,k=1,2,\ldots,n,

(15)

and
C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
the universal Clifford algebra with
signature
(0,n)
.

The Clifford algebra
C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
is an associative algebra with identity
{\bf e}_{\emptyset}:=1
, containing
{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as subspaces.
In particular, vectors
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2}\ldots,x_{n})
and
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are represented in terms of the linear combinations

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}{\bf e}_{j}

resp.

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j},

whereas the Euclidean inner product
\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle
between
\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\xi_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}

recasted in terms of anti-commutator
\displaystyle\langle\bm{x},\bm{\xi}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}\bm{\xi}+\bm{\xi}\bm{x})
, belongs to the center of
C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
. By the preceding relation it follows that the square
\bm{\xi}^{2}
is a real number satisfying
\bm{\xi}^{2}=-|\bm{\xi}|^{2}
, where
|\bm{\xi}|:={\langle\bm{\xi},\bm{\xi}\rangle}^{\frac{1}{2}}
stands for the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Here we would like to stress that the linear space isomorphism given by the mapping

{\bf e}_{j_{1}}{\bf e}_{j_{2}}\ldots{\bf e}_{j_{r}}\mapsto dx_{j_{1}}dx_{j_{2}}\ldots dx_{j_{r}}
, with
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{r}\leq n
, enable us to establish an isomorphim between
C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
and the exterior algebra

\displaystyle\bigwedge^{*}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n}\bigwedge^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(cf.
[
30
, Chapter 4]
) so that
C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
has dimension
2^{n}
.

In the sequel we always consider multivector functions
(\bm{x},t)\mapsto\varPsi(\bm{x},t)
with values on the complexified Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
, written as
linear combinations in terms of the
r
-multivector basis

{\bf e}_{j_{1}}{\bf e}_{j_{2}}\ldots{\bf e}_{j_{r}}
labeled by the subsets

J=\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{r}\}
of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
i.e.

\displaystyle\varPsi(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{|J|=r}\psi_{J}(\bm{x},t){\bf e}_{J},

\displaystyle\mbox{with}~{\bf e}_{J}={\bf e}_{j_{1}}{\bf e}_{j_{2}}\ldots{\bf e}_{j_{r}}

\displaystyle\mbox{and}~\psi_{J}(\bm{x},t)\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(16)

It is well know that the Dirac operator
\bm{D}
, defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bm{D}\varPsi(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf e}_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\varPsi(\bm{x},t),

(17)

factorizes the Euclidean Laplacian
\displaystyle\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{j}}^{2}
, that is
\bm{D}^{2}=-\Delta
.
Further, induction over
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
shows us that

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{2m}=(-\Delta)^{m}

and

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{2m+1}=(-\Delta)^{m}\bm{D}.

(18)

2.2
Background on function spaces, distributions and Fourier transform

To extend the action of the Fourier transform (
1
) and its inverse (
2
) by component wise application to Clifford-valued functions (
16
) we need to consider the
{\dagger}-
conjugation
operation
\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\mapsto\varPsi(\bm{x},t)^{\dagger}
on the
complexified Clifford algebra

{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
defined as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}(\Phi(\bm{x},t)\varPsi(\bm{x},t))^{\dagger}=\varPsi(\bm{x},t)^{\dagger}\Phi(\bm{x},t)^{\dagger}\\
(\psi_{J}(\bm{x},t){\bf e}_{J})^{\dagger}=\overline{\psi_{J}(\bm{x},t)}~{\bf e}_{j_{r}}^{\dagger}\ldots{\bf e}_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}{\bf e}_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}~~~(1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{r}\leq n)\\
{\bf e}_{j}^{\dagger}=-{\bf e}_{j}~~~(1\leq j\leq n)\end{array}.

Henceforth, the norm
\|\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\|
of the Clifford-valued-function (
16
) induced by the identity
\|\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\|^{2}=\varPsi(\bm{x},t)^{\dagger}\varPsi(\bm{x},t)
– which is positive definite in the view of (
2.2
) and of the the anti-commuting relations (

) – allows us to define

\displaystyle\|\varPsi(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{p}}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\|^{p}d\bm{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&\mbox{if}&1\leq p<\infty\\
\\
\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\|\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\|,&\mbox{if}&p=\infty\end{array}\right.

as the underlying norm of the
Bochner type spaces

L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
(cf.
[
31
, subsection 1.2.b]
).

We then consider the multi-index abbreviations

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(\mu)}:=\left(x_{1}\right)^{\mu_{1}}\left(x_{2}\right)^{\mu_{2}}\ldots\left(x_{n}\right)^{\mu_{n}},

for

\displaystyle\mu:=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in({\mathbb{N}}_{0})^{n}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\bm{x}}\right)^{\nu}:=\left(\partial_{x_{1}}\right)^{\nu_{1}}\ldots\left(\partial_{x_{n}}\right)^{\nu_{n}},

for

\displaystyle\nu:=(\nu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n})\in({\mathbb{N}}_{0})^{n}

\displaystyle|\mu|=\mu_{1}+\ldots+\mu_{n},

for

\displaystyle\mu:=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n})\in({\mathbb{N}}_{0})^{n}

\displaystyle|\nu|=\nu_{1}+\ldots+\nu_{n},

for

\displaystyle\nu:=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n})\in({\mathbb{N}}_{0})^{n}.

to exploit the definition of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and its dual
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
to Clifford-valued distributions in the spirit of
[
31
, subsections 2.4.c & 2.4.d]
.

The space of
rapidly decaying functions

\varPsi(\cdot,t)
with values on
{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
, is the Fréchet space defined by the family of semi-norm conditions underlying to the positive constant
M>0
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}~;~|\mu|+|\nu|<M}~\left\|\bm{x}^{(\mu)}\left(\partial_{\bm{x}}\right)^{\nu}\varPsi(\bm{x},t)\right\|<\infty

whereas the space of
{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}-
valued
tempered distributions

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
consists of
continuous linear functionals

induced by the mapping
\varPsi(\cdot,t)\mapsto\langle\varPsi(\cdot,t),\Phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}}
, whereby

\displaystyle\langle\Psi(\cdot,t),\Phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\varPsi(\bm{x},t)^{\dagger}\Phi(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}

(27)

stands for the sesquilinear form (possibly Clifford-valued).

Notice that
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
is a dense subset in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
(cf.
[
31
, Proposition 2.4.23.]
) for any
1\leq p<\infty
(i.e. for values of
p\neq\infty
). On the other hand, the sesquilinear form (
27
) permits us not only to exploit the main features of the Fourier transform such as the mapping property
\mathcal{F}:L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})\rightarrow L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
(cf.
[
31
, Definition 2.4.1]
) and the Plancherel theorem (cf.
[
31
, Theorem 2.4.9]
) but also to extend the action of the Fourier transform (
1
) to an isomorphism in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}\varPsi(\cdot,t),\mathcal{F}\Phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\varPsi(\cdot,t),\Phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}},~~\mbox{for all}~~

\displaystyle\varPsi(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})~\&~

\displaystyle\Phi(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n}).

In particular, that allows us to show that for every
\varPsi(\cdot,t)
with membership in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
, the family of
continuous linear functionals

\varPsi(\cdot,t)\mapsto\langle\varPsi(\cdot,t),\Phi(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}}
always define an element of
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
(see
[
31
, Example 2.4.26.]
for further details).

Remark 2.1
.

Since
e^{-\tau|\bm{\xi}|^{\alpha}}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
for values of
\Re(\tau)\geq 0
(see Remark
1.2
) one can thus infer from density arguments, depicted as above, that the kernel function
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)
defined viz eq. (
3
) belongs to
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
, whenever
1\leq p<\infty
.

2.3
Background on Riesz-Hilbert-Type transforms

Next we review the construction of the Hilbert transform– the so-called fractional Riesz-Hilbert transform. Following the framework considered in the series of papers
[
25
,
26
]
we derive the pseudo-differential representation of the so-called Riesz-Hilbert transform through the pseudo-differential identity

\displaystyle\bm{D}=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{H},

(28)

where
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}|\bm{\xi}|^{\alpha}\mathcal{F}
denotes the fractional Riesz operator of order
\alpha
(cf.
[
1
, p. 483]
).

First, we note that in the view of the spectral identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(D\Psi)(\bm{\xi},t)=-i\bm{\xi}\mathcal{F}\Psi(\bm{\xi},t),

with

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j}.

and of the spherical decomposition

\displaystyle-i\bm{\xi}=|\bm{\xi}|\frac{-i\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|},

with

\displaystyle|\bm{\xi}|=(-\bm{\xi}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\neq 0

it is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{H}
admits the pseudo-differential representation (cf.
[
25
, section 4.]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\frac{-i\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|}\mathcal{F}

(29)

Next, we are going to derive the a close formula for the fractional Hilbert transform. To do so, we shall make use of the formal series expansion

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{k}\pi^{k}\theta^{k}}{2^{k}k!}\mathcal{H}^{k}

(30)

The next lemma corresponds to an abridged version of
[
26
, Definition 4.1]
(see eq. (4.2)) and
[
25
, Theorem 4.2.]
(see also
[
26
, Theorem 4.2.]
):

Lemma 2.2
.

The fractional Riesz-Hilbert transform
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)
admits the polar representation

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)=\cos\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\right)I+i\sin\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\right)\mathcal{H}.

(31)

Moreover,

\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right):L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})\rightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})

is continuous and bounded for values of
1<p<\infty
.

Proof 2.3
.

From the formal series expansion (
30
) of
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)

one can infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right)\Psi(\cdot,t)\right](\bm{\xi})=\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}h(\bm{\xi})\right)\mathcal{F}\Psi(\bm{\xi},t),

(32)

where
h(\bm{\xi}):=\frac{-i\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|}
denotes the Fourier multiplier of
\mathcal{H}
.

Here, we recall that
h(\bm{\xi})
arising on the exponentiation representation
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}h(\bm{\xi})\right)
is a unitary Clifford vector, i.e.
\left(~h(\bm{\xi})~\right)^{2}=1
. By expanding now
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}h(\bm{\xi})\right)
as a formal series expansion, the set of recursive relations

\displaystyle\left(~h(\bm{\xi})~\right)^{2j}=1

and

\displaystyle\left(~h(\bm{\xi})~\right)^{2j+1}=h(\bm{\xi})

yielding from induction over
j
, leads to the splitting formula

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}h(\bm{\xi})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\pi)^{2j}\theta^{2j}}{2^{2j}(2j)!}\left(~h(\bm{\xi})~\right)^{2j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\pi)^{2j+1}\theta^{2j+1}}{2^{2j+1}(2j+1)!}\left(~h(\bm{\xi})~\right)^{2j+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j}\pi^{2j}\theta^{2j}}{2^{2j}(2j)!}+ih(\bm{\xi})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{j}\pi^{2j+1}\theta^{2j+1}}{2^{2j+1}(2j+1)!}

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\frac{\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\right)+\frac{\bm{\xi}}{|\bm{\xi}|}\sin\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{2}\right).

Finally, by taking the inverse of the Fourier transform (
2
) on both sides of (
32
) the one gets from (
29
) the eq. (
31
), as expected.

Moreover, the proof of continuity and boundedness of
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\theta}{2}\mathcal{H}\right):L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})\rightarrow L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};{\mathbb{C}}\otimes C\kern-1.00006pt\ell_{0,n})
for values of
1<p<\infty
is, up to a linearity argument, similar to the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 4.2.]
.

3
Mellin-Barnes and Wright series representations of the Fundamental solution

3.1
Mellin-Barnes representation

Following the framework considered in
[
29
,
20
]
, we start to to reformulate
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)
represented through eq. (
6
) as a Mellin convolution at the point
r=|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
(cf.
[
32
, Section 4.]
) namely:

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\rho}\right)g(\rho)\frac{d\rho}{\rho},

with

\displaystyle f(\rho)=e^{-\rho^{-\alpha}}

and

\displaystyle g(\rho)=\rho^{\frac{n}{2}+1}J_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(\rho).

Furthermore, by exploiting the Mellin convolution theorem (see eq.
[
32
, Theorem 3.]
), we also have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)\}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\mathcal{M}\left\{f\left(|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)\right\}(s)~.~\mathcal{M}\left\{g\left(|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)\right\}(s),

(33)

whereby

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{\phi(\rho)\}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(\rho)\rho^{s-1}d\rho,

with

\displaystyle s\in{\mathbb{C}},

(34)

stands for the Mellin transform (cf.
[
32
, eq.(1.2) of p. 326]
).

The next step thus consists in computing the Mellin transforms
\mathcal{M}\{f(\rho)\}(s)
and
\mathcal{M}\{g(\rho)\}(s)
and then inverting the product
\mathcal{M}\{f(\rho)\}(s).\mathcal{M}\{g(\rho)\}(s)
at
\rho=|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
by means of the Mellin inversion formula (cf.
[
32
, eq. (1.3) of p.326]
)

\displaystyle\phi(\rho)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\mathcal{M}\{\phi(\rho)\}(s)~\rho^{-s}~ds,

with

\displaystyle\rho>0~~~\&~~c=\Re(s).

(35)

Thereby, from a straightforwardly application of property

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{\rho^{\beta}\phi(\kappa\rho^{\gamma})\}(s)=\frac{1}{|\gamma|}\kappa^{-\frac{s+\beta}{\gamma}}\mathcal{M}\{\phi(\rho)\}\left(\frac{s+\beta}{\gamma}\right)

carrying the parameters
\beta\in{\mathbb{C}},~\gamma\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
and
\kappa>0
(cf.
[
32
, Proposition 1.]
), there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{f(\rho)\}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|-\alpha|}\mathcal{M}\{e^{-\rho}\}\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{g(\rho)\}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{J_{\frac{n}{2}-1}\left({\rho}\right)\right\}\left(s+\frac{n}{2}+1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2^{\frac{n}{2}+s}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)}

so that (
33
) equals

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\{K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)\}(s)=\frac{2^{s}}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)}.

(36)

On computations above we have used the Eulerian representation of the Gamma function (see
[
3
, Chapter 13]
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho}\rho^{s-1}d\rho=\Gamma(s)

(37)

and the Weber integral representation (cf.
[
1
, p. 490, eq. (25.27)]
) carrying the parameters
\beta=\frac{n}{2}+s
and
\nu=\frac{n}{2}-1
(see also
[
33
, p. 57, eq. (2.46)]
):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{\beta}J_{\nu}(\rho)d\rho=2^{\beta}~\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+\beta+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-\beta+1}{2}\right)},

for

\displaystyle-\Re(\nu)-1<\Re(\beta)<\frac{1}{2}

(38)

As a consequence of (
33
) and of the Mellin inversion formula (
35
) we conclude that for
\rho=|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\mathcal{M}\{K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)\}(s)~\left(|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)^{-s}ds\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}~\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)}~\left({\frac{|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{2}}\right)^{-s}ds,\end{array}

or equivalently,

K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)=\frac{1}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}H^{1,1}_{1,2}\left[~{\frac{|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{2}}~\begin{array}[]{|ll}(1,\frac{1}{\alpha})&\\
\\
(\frac{n}{2},\frac{1}{2})&(1,\frac{1}{2})\end{array}~\right]

using the H-function notation (
[
33
, p. 2]
&
[
28
, eq. (5.1)]
).

By applying general existence conditions for the H-function we conclude that, in case of
\alpha>1
, the integral representation (
3.1
) yields a uniformly convergent series expansion – conditions
\mu>0
and
q\geq 1
carrying the parameters
\mu=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}
and

(cf.
[
33
, Case 1: of Theorem 1.1.]
).

3.2
Series Expansion of Wright type

To represent explicitly
K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)
as a convergent series expansion of Wright type, one transform the closed path joining the endpoints
c-i\infty
and
c+i\infty
associated to the straight line
\Re(s)=c
(
-n<c<\frac{1-n}{2}
) as a loop beginning and ending at
-\infty
and enriching all the simple poles
s=-n-2k
(
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
).

Using the fact that for values of
\alpha>1
the intersection of the simple poles of
\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}-s\right)
and
\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)
yields an empty set (
\alpha k\neq-n-2k
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
), there holds by a straightforward aplication of the standard residue theorem, we can evaluate the contour integral appearing on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) as an infinite sum of the residues at
s=-n-2k
(
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
).

Namely, we have

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lim_{s\rightarrow-n-2k}(s+n+2k)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)}~\left({\frac{|\bm{x}|\tau^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{2}}\right)^{-s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lim_{s\rightarrow-n-2k}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}+k\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\right)~\frac{2\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)}~\left({\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}\tau^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}{4}}\right)^{-\frac{s}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\bm{x}|^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}~~\frac{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{\alpha}+\frac{2}{\alpha}k\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+k\right)}~\left({\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}\tau^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}{4}}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}+k}.

After a straightforward simplification we recognize that, for values of
\alpha>1
, the kernel function

reduces to a Wright series expansion of the type (
11
). Namely, one gets the closed representation

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},\tau)=\frac{2^{1-n}}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\tau^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}}~{~}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left[~\begin{array}[]{ll|}(\frac{n}{\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha})&\\
(\frac{n}{2},1)&\end{array}~-{\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}\tau^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}{4}}\right].

Remark 3.1
.

From the substitution

on (

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{\alpha,n}(\bm{x},te^{i\frac{\pi\theta}{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-n}}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}}~e^{-\frac{i\pi\theta}{\alpha}\frac{n}{2}}~{~}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left[~\begin{array}[]{ll|}(\frac{n}{\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha})&\\
(\frac{n}{2},1)&\end{array}~-{\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}\tau^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}{4}}e^{-\frac{i\pi\theta}{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-n}}{\alpha\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}t^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}~~\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{\alpha}+\frac{2}{\alpha}k\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+k\right)}~\frac{\left(-{\frac{|\bm{x}|^{2}\tau^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}{4}}\right)^{k}e^{-i\frac{\pi\theta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{n}{2}+k\right)}}{k!}.

Remark 3.2

For
