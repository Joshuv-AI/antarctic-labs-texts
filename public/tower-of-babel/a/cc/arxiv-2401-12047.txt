Fast degree-preserving rewiring of complex networks
Shane Mannion, Pádraig MacCarron, Akrati Saxena, Frank W. Takes

Abstract
In this paper we introduce a new, fast, degree-preserving rewiring algorithm for altering the assortativity of complex networks, which we call Fast total link (FTL) rewiring algorithm. Commonly used existing algorithms require a large number of iterations, in particular in the case of large dense networks. This can especially be problematic when we wish to study ensembles of networks. In this work we aim to overcome aforementioned scalability problems by performing a rewiring of all edges at once to achieve a very high assortativity value before rewiring samples of edges at once to reduce this high assortativity value to the target value. The proposed method performs better than existing methods by several orders of magnitude for a range of structurally diverse complex networks, both in terms of the number of iterations taken, and time taken to reach a given assortativity value. Here we test our proposed algorithm on networks with up to 100,000 100,000 nodes and around 750,000 750,000 edges and find that the relative improvements in speed remain, showing that the algorithm is both efficient and scalable.



I
Introduction

The rewiring of networks is an important task that is of broad interest to the network science community. Rewiring takes place on a network to change one property of the network either with no regard for other properties (random rewiring) or while holding other properties fixed, for example the degree sequence. We refer to the latter as degree-preserving rewiring. Rewiring algorithms are often used to create networks with desirable properties for the study of dynamical processes on the networks. Examples of such rewiring algorithms include rewiring to increase network robustness
Chan and Akoglu 2016
, clustering
Alstott et al. 2019
;
Kashyap and Ambika 2018
, synchronisability
Bertotti and Modanese 2020
and assortativity
Kashyap and Ambika 2018
;
Van Mieghem et al. 2010
;
Winterbach et al. 2012
. The focus of this paper is on degree-preserving rewiring for tuning assortativity values. Such algorithms take a graph and a target assortativity value as inputs, and return a graph where the node degrees are unchanged, but the assortativity value is now at or very close to the target value.

Broadly speaking, rewiring algorithms are either Monte-Carlo-based, where edges are randomly selected repeatedly to be rewired until a goal is achieved, or simulated annealing-based
Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1993
. Simulated annealing defines a “system temperature”. This temperature starts at a large value and decreases by some chosen factor at each iteration of the algorithm. The higher the temperature, the larger the probability that a backward step (i.e., a step that moves away from the goal value) is allowed
de Arruda et al. 2018
, which helps to avoid local optima. Both approaches can be used to the same end; for example in
Chan and Akoglu 2016
and
Buesser et al. 2011
, the aim is to increase the robustness of networks, with the former employing Monte-Carlo-based algorithms, and the latter using simulated annealing. In this paper, we focus on Monte-Carlo-based degree-preserving rewiring algorithms.

Both Monte-Carlo and simulated-annealing based algorithms are typically not designed with computational efficiency in mind.
The majority of existing algorithms appear to rewire just two edges at one time
Kashyap and Ambika 2018
;
Van Mieghem et al. 2010
;
Winterbach et al. 2012
. As the contribution of one edge
to the overall assortativity is very small, it is no surprise that many iterations are required for algorithms that rewire just two edges at once. The approach proposed in this work is to rewire a larger number of edges at once, reaching the target assortativity values much faster.

In our algorithm, the edges to be formed are created strategically instead of randomly. It is for this reason that we use a Monte-Carlo based approach. A backwards step, as utilised in simulated annealing is (in the vast majority of cases) not possible, due to the fact that new edges are constructed in such a way that they will increase the assortativity, whereas in a simulated annealing approach, edges are created randomly, and the new edges may either increase or decrease the assortativity.
We demonstrate the efficacy of the approach by studying both random graphs with varying degree distributions and a number of real-world networks.

The remainder of this paper is laid out as follows: In Section
II
we discuss the widely used existing algorithm and its limitations. In Section
III
we discuss how our algorithm works for both assortative and disassortative rewiring. Following discussion of our algorithm, we compare its performance to that of the original algorithm using simulated networks and real-world networks in Section
IV
.Finally, we discuss conclusions and potential future work in Section
V
.

II
Background & Original rewiring algorithm

A graph or network is an object comprised of a set of
N
nodes and a set of
L
links (or edges) that connect these nodes. The number of edges that are connected to a node is called the
degree
of a node and is denoted
k
. The
degree distribution

p_{k}
of a graph (i.e., the distribution of the probability that a randomly chosen node has degree
k
), is the subject of much research
Albert and Barabási 2002
;
Amaral et al. 2000
;
Newman et al. 2001
. In addition, the
assortativity
of a graph, which is the correlation between the degrees of nodes that are connected by edges, has important practical consequence, such as impacting the rate at which a disease will spread across a network
Newman 2002
. We can vary the latter of these properties while keeping the former fixed through the use of a degree-preserving rewiring algorithm.

Often attributed to
Van Mieghem et al. 2010
, the degree preserving rewiring algorithm works as follows. Two edges (
a\sim b
) and (
c\sim d
) are selected. The ways in which to configure two edges among these four nodes, i.e., (
a\sim c
) and (
b\sim d
) or (
a\sim d
) and (
b\sim c
) are considered. If the goal is to increase the assortativity, then a pair of edges that results in an increase in the assortativity is selected. If disassortativity is desired, then a pair of edges that reduces the assortativity is selected. Figure
1

demonstrates the two ways in which a selected pair of edges can be rewired. The choice of situation (a) or (b) is based on the formula for the degree assortativity, denoted by
r
described in
Van Mieghem et al. 2010
,

r=1-\frac{\sum_{i\sim j}(k_{i}-k_{j})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{3}-\frac{1}{2L}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}}.

(1)

In this equation,
k_{i}
is the degree of node
i
. The authors note that a degree-preserving rewiring will only change the numerator of the fraction in Equation (
1
). When two edges are rewired, there are three ways in which the edges can be formed (including the original two edges) which are shown in Figure
1
. In the scenario in which
k_{n_{1}}\geq k_{n_{2}}\geq k_{n_{3}}\geq k_{n_{4}}
where
k_{n_{i}}
is the degree of node
i
in Figure
1
, we would have
r_{b}\geq r\geq r_{a}
, where
r
is the assortativity of the graph before the rewiring step and
r_{a}
and
r_{b}
are the assortativity values after rewiring to configuration
a
and
b
respectively. As such, if we want to increase the assortativity, we would choose configuration
b
, and if we wish to decrease the assortativity we would choose configuration
a
.

III
Proposed Method: Fast Rewiring Algorithm

In this section we first discuss a naive improvement we made to the existing algorithm. The preliminary results obtained ultimately inspired the workings of the proposed algorithm outlined in the second part of this section.

III.1
Naive simultaneous rewiring

We begin by introducing an additional parameter to the existing algorithm,
m
. Existing algorithms take a graph
G
, and a target assortativity
r_{t}
as inputs. We define the additional parameter
m
as the number of edges to be rewired in a given iteration of the algorithm.

The logic of how we rewire is the same as what is done for the algorithm discussed in Section
II
;
however, the formulation is based on a different derivation. As shown in previous work
MacCarron et al. 2023
, the assortativity can be written as

r=\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{i,j}\frac{A_{ij}(k_{i}-\text{E}[\kappa])(k_{j}-\text{E}[\kappa])}{\text{E}[\kappa^{2}]-\text{E}[\kappa]^{2}},

(2)

where
A
is the adjacency matrix and E
[\kappa]
is the average degree at the end of an edge (not to be confused with the mean degree of the network). It follows from Equation (
2
) that if two nodes are such that one has a degree greater than the average degree at the end of an edge and the other has a degree less than the average degree at the end of an edge, this edge will contribute negatively to the assortativity. If both nodes have degree greater than or both have degree less than the average degree at the end of an edge, this edge will contribute positively to the assortativity. The change we make exploits this fact.

The improved algorithm works as follows:
m
edges are chosen, resulting in
2m
nodes being selected which
need not
be unique. The nodes are ranked by their degree from
0
to
2m-1
. If an increase in assortativity is desired then node
0
is connected to node
1
, node
2
to node
3
, and so on, until node
2m-2
is connected to node
2m-1
. This way, every node is losing one edge and gaining one edge. These edges are then added to the graph, while the
m
edges that are chosen at each iteration are removed. If a decrease in assortativity is desired, the new edges are formed between nodes
0
and
2m-1
, nodes
1
and
2m-2
, and so on.

Preliminary experiments showed that a rewiring algorithm based on the procedure explained above showed some, although inconsistent success. In some cases, it reduced the amount of time taken to rewire a graph by up to 95%, in other cases it made no improvement whatsoever. Figure
2
shows the time taken to rewire Erdős-Rényi graphs of varying sizes, all with an average degree of 5, from their original assortativity values (
r\approx 0
) to an assortativity value of 0.25. As we can see, even though increasing the number of edges rewired at each step reduces the time taken (before it increases again, which will be discussed later in this section), the amount of time taken to rewire the graphs is still quite long.

Figure 2:
Time taken to rewire Erdős-Rényi graphs of varying sizes to an assortativity value of 0.25 for different numbers of chosen edges
m
per algorithm iteration.

One obvious problem with this algorithm is that some of the edges we attempt to form may be self edges (since the nodes are ranked by degree, the same node appearing twice may be connected to itself). However, testing finds that such edges are exceedingly rare in the rewiring process.

The less obvious problem, which is the reason for the lackluster performance of this method comes from the fact that any of the edges we are attempting to add may already exist in the network, and if even one of the potential edges we are attempting to add does exist, the sample of edges must be discarded and redrawn. It is because of this then that increasing the number of edges rewired in a given iteration does not necessarily improve the algorithm. To illustrate why, we look at the adjacency matrix.

Consider a network with
N
nodes and
L
edges. Allowing no self edges, the adjacency matrix of this network would have
2L
non-zero elements. If we remove
m
edges from the graph in our rewiring problem, then the proportion of non-zero elements in the adjacency matrix is

\frac{2L-2m}{(N-1)^{2}}.

(3)

We then must add
m
edges back into the graph, i.e., add
2m
ones to the adjacency matrix. Since we are preserving the degree, these ones must be added to the same portion of the adjacency matrix that they were removed from, i.e., the square submatrix made up of the rows and columns corresponding to the (up to)
2m
nodes affected by the removal of our
2m
edges. We then make the following two assumptions. First, we assume that the ones in the adjacency matrix are randomly distributed (which is only the case for an Erdős-Rényi random graph, however, this simple case is sufficient to illustrate our point). As such, the proportion of ones in this submatrix is the same as that for the adjacency matrix. Secondly, we assume that we are adding edges randomly and not based on the degrees of the nodes. Essentially, we must randomly select
m
pairs of elements of this submatrix (one for each node of the edge being formed), and all of the elements we select must be zeroes, otherwise the sample will be discarded.

This process can be viewed a Bernoulli trial, and our probability of success for a sample size of
m
edges is

p=\left(1-\frac{2L-2m}{(N-1)^{2}}\right)^{m}.

(4)

In testing, we found that small increases to
m
improves the algorithm, as such changes still resulted in a relatively high probability of success. Once
m
becomes larger, this probability of success becomes very small. This is observed in Figure
2
, where increasing the number of edges selected per iteration helps only to a certain extent.

III.2
Fast total link rewiring

It is the inspection of Equation (
4
) that leads us to the solution of the problem discussed in the previous section.
In this equation, the probability of success for an individual edge being added also depends on the number of edges being sampled at once. As
m
approaches
L
, the probability of failure will decrease and in the case where
m=L
, the probability of an edge that we are attempting to form existing in the graph is zero, as we have first removed all edges from the graph. Figure
3
shows this probability for all possible sample sizes for an Erdős-Rényi graph of size
N=1000
and average degree
\langle k\rangle=5
. With this in mind, we can describe fully our new degree-preserving rewiring algorithm.

Fast degree-assortative rewiring

Our new algorithm, which we call
fast total link rewiring
(or
FTL
rewiring), has two main steps, and one additional step present
only in the case of disassortative rewiring
. First, we increase (decrease) the assortativity to a very high (low) value by rewiring all edges at once. We refer to this step as
total rewiring
. From this extreme value, we rewire
m
edges at once as described in Section
III.1
to reach the target value.

To increase the assortativity of a graph, we begin with a total rewiring. In this total rewiring step, all edges are removed, and the nodes are ranked by their original degree (in this ranking, each node appears only once). The highest degree node (with say degree
k
) is connected to the next
k
highest degree nodes in the graph. We then perform the same procedure for the second-highest degree node in the graph, and continue like so, ensuring that we are at no point exceeding the degree of any node. Figure
4
shows a diagram of this process. The resulting graph is then of relatively high assortativity, perhaps even the largest assortativity possible for the given degree sequence, although we have not yet proven this. Figure
5
(a) shows how edges are rewired in this step of the algorithm.

Figure 4:
The total rewiring step of the FTL rewiring algorithm described in section
III
. Magenta coloured nodes represent those nodes that have been connected to a number of other nodes equal to their degree in the original graph.

Following our total rewiring, we want to reduce the assortativity from this large value to whatever the desired value is. To do this we select two (or
m
) edges from our rewired graph, and again order the nodes (which now need not be unique) by their degree. The node with the largest degree is connected to the node with the smallest degree, and the middle two nodes are connected. This is repeated until the desired assortativity value is reached. This is the same process that is described in our discussion of the simultaneous rewiring in Section
III.1
. Note also that these new edges are checked to ensure no loops, duplicate edges, or edges that are already present in the graph are being added. If any edge fails this check, then the sample of
m
edges is discarded and a new sample is drawn.

Earlier in this section, we discussed how increasing the number of edges being rewired at once does not necessarily improve the speed of the rewiring. This is no longer the case here. As we have already mentioned, if an edge connects two nodes whose degrees are both greater (or less) than than the average degree at the end of an edge for the graph, such an edge will increase the assortativity. After our total rewiring, the vast majority of the edges in the graph will contribute positively to the assortativity, and since we are now trying to form disassortative edges, we can rewire many more edges at each iteration without running into the problem of these edges already being present in the graph. This allows us to rewire a larger sample of edges at once, further improving the speed of the FTL rewiring algorithm.

Fast degree-disassortative rewiring

To decrease the assortativity of a graph we again perform a total rewiring. The bottom of Figure
4
shows the total rewiring for disassortative rewiring. As we can see from the diagram, we connect the highest degree node whose degree we again call
k
, to the
k
lowest degree nodes in the graph. This results in a graph configuration with a very low assortativity value, which we can then increase to a desired value.

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, there is an additional step in the algorithm when it is used to decrease the assortativity. We find that when we perform a total rewiring to reduce the assortativity, some nodes in the resulting graph have a degree that is less than their original degree. The presence of the additional step is due to the need to rectify this.

Once the total rewiring is completed, we calculate the difference in the current total degree of the graph and the original total degree. We call this number
\Delta k
. We then randomly select
\frac{\Delta k}{2}
edges from the network and remove these edges. We rank the nodes with incorrect degrees by their original degree, with each node appearing
\delta_{i}
times, where
\delta_{i}
is the difference between the original degree of node
i
and its current degree. We rank the nodes belonging to the randomly sampled edges by their degree. We then connect the highest ranking node here to the
\delta_{i}
lowest degree nodes in the randomly selected nodes, in a similar way to what is shown in Figure
5
(b). Due to this step however, it is unlikely that the resulting configuration is the minimum possible assortativity for the network, as the edges use to correct the differences in node degrees are chosen randomly.

Once we have our graph with a very low assortativity value, we rewire samples of
m
edges to increase the assortativity to our desired value. The edges are selected, their nodes ranked, and connected in a way such that each node is connected to the node next to it in the rankings, as shown in Figure
5
(a). Again, we are now able to rewire many edges at a time, as very few positively assortative edges exist in the graph after the total rewiring.

Having described our FTL rewiring algorithm, we next move on to showing how it performs when compared to the original rewiring algorithm.

Results

In this section we look at the results of our testing of the FTL rewiring algorithm. In particular we wish to look at four aspects of the algorithms performance. Most importantly, after outlining the experimental setup in Section

IV.1
Experimental Setup

To test the FTL rewiring algorithm, we employ it on both real-world network datasets, and randomly generated graphs. In both cases, we select several target assortativity values to rewire each network to. The reason we pick several is that due to the first step (i.e., the total rewiring step) of the FTL rewiring algorithm which we described in the previous section, rewiring the graph to assortativity value close to its maximum or minimum will be, somewhat counterintuitively, faster than rewiring it to a value close to its starting value. This is not the case for the original algorithm. As such, it may be the case that for small changes in assortativity value, the original algorithm performs better and we will explore this is the coming sections.

In addition, we test several sample sizes (
m=2,5,10,20,
and
50
). In Figures
6
,
8

9
and
10
, we show only the best performing sample size for a given rewiring to avoid visual clutter.

We test the FTL rewiring algorithm’s performance on three empirical networks. Namely we look at the US airport network used by Van Meighem
et al.
in
Van Mieghem et al. 2010
, the US power-grid network analysed by Watts and Strogatz
Watts and Strogatz 1998
, and a subset of the Facebook user network studied by McAuley and Leskovec
Leskovec and Mcauley 2012
. We chose these networks as they each represent different types of network (transport, infrastructure, and social, respectively) with large variation in their densities and degree distributions.

In addition to the real world networks described above, we study the performance of the FTL rewiring algorithm on randomly generated networks with degree sequences drawn from the following distributions; Poisson (Erdős-Rényi graphs), exponential, lognormal, Weibull, and power-law (Barabási-Albert graphs). The Poisson and power-law graphs are generated using the Networkx Python package’s built in methods
Hagberg et al. 2008

Randomly generated graphs

One of the results we find in our study of randomly generated graphs is that the maximum and minimum assortativity values that one can obtain for a given network differ depending on the distribution from which the node degrees are drawn. As a consequence of this, we use the total rewiring step of the FTL rewiring algorithm to find maximum and minimum assortativity values for a handful of graphs from each distribution, and then use these to choose reasonable target values. This is by no means a hard upper bound on the possible assortativity that can be achieved, but provides a target that is likely possible while still being far enough away from the initial assortativity to be able to compare the performance of the two algorithms.

4
), the probability of success depends on
\frac{2M-2m}{(N-1)^{2}}
. Evaluating the full equation for the power-grid network with
m=50
gives a probability of success of 0.97. Comparatively, this same probability for the airport network is just 0.51. This difference is due to the ratio between
L
and
M
for each graph. As such, we can use a much larger sample size with the power-grid network.

Indeed, we could further increase the sample size here without reducing the probability of success too much, though there is not much to be gained in practical terms by doing this as the absolute time taken to successfully rewire this graph is quite low. That being said, one could calculate from Equation (
4
) an upper bound on the sample size that should be used for a given network, though this also depends on the distance between the starting assortativity value and the target assortativity, as discussed earlier.

To note, all of the experiments described in this section are repeated for the case of rewiring the network to be disassortative. For the sake of brevity, only the case of positive rewiring is discussed here, as the results in the negative rewiring case are almost identical, with the only differences being what choice of
m
leads to the fastest rewiring for each network. Plots of these results corresponding to Figures
6
,
8
,

and
10
can be found in the
Appendix
.

We note also a possible limitation to this work. Given that we impose only that the degrees of nodes remains unchanged, it is likely that drastic changes in other network properties occur during the rewiring process, some of which may be undesirable. One can easily imagine that the clustering coefficient of a network is dramatically affected by rewiring. As we will see in the next section, holding other network properties fixed during the rewiring process is one of many potential avenues for further research.

V
conclusions

In this paper we have introduced the FTL rewiring algorithm for tuning the assortativity of a network via edge rewiring. We demonstrate that this new version reduces computation time by several orders of magnitude, performing even better in the most complex case of rewiring large, densely connected graphs to assortativity values that are far from the starting value.

We touched on the maximum and minimum assortativity values that can be realised by a graph with a given degree sequence in Section
IV
, and we believe further exploration of this to be an interesting avenue for future work. Additionally we aim to formulate a way to obtain an optimum for the number of edges,
m
, for a graph based on its number of nodes, edges, and perhaps its degree distribution. Furthermore, we believe that the concepts used to develop this algorithm could be applied to other objectives, such as changing the level of clustering in a graph, as well as more intricate measurements of complex networks, such as fault-tolerance or robustness. This would also address the limitations to this study mentioned in Section
IV.4
.

We have provided a Python package implementing this algorithm that can be used by anyone. This package can be found on GitHub at the link provided in the
Appendix
.

As rewiring is an important part of many models used in network science across a wide range of areas of study, we expect that the approach presented in this paper will prove useful for the network science community.
