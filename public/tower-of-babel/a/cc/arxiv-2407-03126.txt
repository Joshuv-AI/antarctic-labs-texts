Game-Theoretic Protection Adoption Against Networked SIS Epidemics
Abhisek Satapathi, Ashish R. Hota

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate game-theoretic strategies for containing spreading processes on large-scale networks. Specifically, we consider the class of networked susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) epidemics where a large population of agents strategically choose whether to adopt partially effective protection. We define the utilities of the agents which depends on the degree of the agent, its individual infection status and action, as well as the the overall prevalence of the epidemic and strategy profile of the entire population. We further present the coupled dynamics of epidemic evolution as well as strategy update which is assumed to follow the replicator dynamics. By relying on timescale separation arguments, we first derive the optimal strategy of protection adoption by the agents for a given epidemic state, and then present the reduced epidemic dynamics. The existence and uniqueness of endemic equilibrium is rigorously characterized and forms the main result of this paper. Finally, we present extensive numerical results to highlight the impacts of heterogeneous node degrees, infection rates, cost of protection adoption, and effectiveness of protection on the epidemic prevalence at the equilibrium.

Effective containment of spreading processes, such as infectious diseases spreading in society

While mathematical modeling and analysis of epidemics has a long history
[
2
,
1
,
8
]
, the recent COVID-19 pandemic has led to renewed interest in this topic. In particular, a substantial body of recent work has examined the impacts of decentralized containment strategies against epidemics in the framework of game theory; see
[

]
for a recent comprehensive review. Two broad classes of containment strategies have been examined using game theory. In the first line of work, the decision-makers or agents adopt vaccination against the disease
[

,

,

]
by evaluating the trade-off between the cost of vaccine and probability of being infected in the steady-state of the SIS epidemic dynamics. While earlier works (such as
[

) considered a homogeneous population of agents, later works (such as
[
10
,
12
,
13
]
) considered agents with heterogeneous degrees.

In the second line of work, agents adopt protection measures, such as wearing masks, social distancing, etc., against the epidemic. While vaccination is typically a one-time irreversible decision, protection adoption is often reversible, and the agents can revise their action or strategy dynamically as the epidemic builds up or wanes. As a result, the evolution of the epidemic and the actions of the agents often evolve in a comparable time-scale. Accordingly, several recent papers, including
[
14
,

,

,

]
, have analyzed the dynamics of coupled evolution of the SIS epidemic and protection adoption behavior, its equilibria and stability for a large population of homogeneous agents, while
[

,

,

have studied the above phenomenon for agents on a network with heterogeneous node degrees. A few related works
[

have also examined the coupled evolution of opinion and epidemic as well as opinion and action
[
23
]
. Finally, similar investigations
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
have also been carried out for the class of Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) epidemics and its variants where recovery grants permanent immunity from future infections.

In this paper, we generalize the settings examined in prior works
[
27
,
17
]
to include networked interaction among agents, and further generalize the setting in
[
19
]
to account for partially effective protection. Specifically, each agent is either susceptible or infected at a given point of time, and chooses whether to adopt protection or remain unprotected. For a susceptible individual, adopting protection gives partial immunity from the disease, while an infected protected individual causes new infection with a smaller probability compared to an infected individual who does not adopt protection. We assume that the agents are divided into different sub-populations depending on their degree, and that the epidemic evolution is governed by the degree-based mean-field (DBMF) approximation of the SIS epidemic
[
8
]
.

For the proposed setting, we define the utility of each sub-population which depends on their individual infection status, chosen action and the disease state and strategies adopted by the entire population. We formulate the coupled disease-behavior dynamics, and leverage time-scale separation arguments to derive the Nash equilibrium strategies for a given epidemic state, i.e., we assume that the evolution of protection adoption is faster than the evolution of the disease. Thus, our work is complementary to the closely related setting
[
20
]
which assumed the epidemic dynamics to be the faster dynamics and the behavior adoption to be the slower dynamics. We then derive the (slower) dynamics of epidemic evolution when all agents adopt their equilibrium strategies; this dynamics takes the form of a switched or hybrid system. We rigorously prove the existence and uniqueness of its equilibrium by leveraging several structural properties of the endemic equilibrium. Numerical simulations show that the coupled dynamics converges to the equilibrium. In addition, we numerically illustrate the impacts of heterogeneous node degrees, degree-dependent infection rates, and cost of protection adoption on the expected fraction of infected nodes at the equilibrium.

II
Networked SIS Epidemic under Partially Effective Protection

II-A

Degree-Based Mean-Field Approximation

We consider a large population of agents, where each agent has a specific degree (number of neighbors) from the set
\mathcal{D}\in\{1,2,\ldots,d^{\max}\}
. Let
y^{d}(t)\in[0,1]
be the proportion of agents with degree
d\in\mathcal{D}
that is infected at time
t
, with
1-y^{d}(t)
be the proportion that is susceptible. Let
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)
and
z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t)
denote the proportion of susceptible and infected agents with degree
d
that remain unprotected at time
t
, respectively. Let
\mathbf{z}:=\{z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}},z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
the strategy profile of the entire population, and let
\mathbf{x}:=\{y^{d},z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}},z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
denote the (time-varying) social state.

An infected individual with degree
d
transmits the infection with probability
\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}\in(0,1)
when it adopts protection, and with probability
\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}\in(0,1)
when it is unprotected. A susceptible individual that adopts protection is
\alpha\in(0,1)
times (less) likely to become infected compared to a susceptible unprotected individual. Finally, an infected individual recovers with probability
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Following the DBMF approximation of the SIS epidemic model, the infected proportion
y^{d}(t)
evolves in continuous-time as

\dot{y}^{d}(t)=-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)))d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t)),

(1)

where
\Theta(\mathbf{x})
is the probability with which a randomly chosen neighbor of a node with degree
d
transmits infection to it. The quantity
(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)))
captures the fact that among the susceptible proportion,
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)
fraction does not adopt protection and encounters an infection probability given by
d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t))
, while
1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)
fraction adopts protection and encounters a smaller infection probability
\alpha d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t))
. We define

\Theta(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))y^{d}\Big],

(2)

where
m_{d}
is the proportion of nodes with degree
d
in the entire population and
d^{\mathtt{avg}}=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}dm_{d}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
m_{d}>0
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
. The first term specifies the probability of a randomly chosen neighbor having degree
d
in accordance with the configuration model
[
6
]
. The second term denotes the probability of becoming infected if it comes in contact with an infected neighbor of degree
d
which depends on the strategy adopted by infected agents having degree
d
. The third term denotes the probability of the neighbor with degree
d
being infected in the first place.

We now analyze the steady-state of the epidemic dynamics (
1
) for a given strategy profile
\mathbf{z}
of the population. By setting
\dot{y}^{d}(t)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma y^{d}=(1-y^{d})(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z}))

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z}))y^{d}

\displaystyle\qquad=(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})

(3)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle y^{d}=\frac{(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})}{\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})}

(4)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{z})=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})}{\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})}\Big]

(5)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{z})\Big[1-\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d}{\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{z})}\Big]\Big]=0.

(6)

Note that (
5
) is obtained by substituting the expression of
y^{d}
obtained in (
4
) in the definition of
\Theta(\mathbf{z})
given in (
2
). It now follows that
\Theta(\mathbf{z})=0
is always a solution of (
6
) which corresponds to the disease-free equilibrium. In addition, there may be nonzero solution(s)
\Theta(\mathbf{z})
of (
6
) depending on the strategy profile
\mathbf{z}
and other parameters as formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Equation (
6
) admits a nonzero solution
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})
if and only if

1<\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{d^{2}m_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))\Big].

Furthermore,
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})=1
is not a solution of (
6
).

Proof.

Note that for
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})>0
to be a solution of (
6
), we must have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d}{\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})}\Big].

Note that the R.H.S. is monotonically decreasing in
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})
.
At
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))\frac{(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d}{\gamma+(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d}\Big]

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))\Big]

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}=1.

Therefore,
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})=1
is not a solution of (
6
).
Therefore, a nonzero solution exists if and only if the R.H.S. exceeds
1
at
\Theta^{\star}(\mathbf{z})=0
. In other words,

1<\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{d^{2}m_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}(\beta_{\mathtt{U}}^{d}z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}+\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}))(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))\Big].

This concludes the proof.
∎

In the following subsection, we analyze the equilibrium behavior when agents adopt protection in a game-theoretic manner.

II-B

Game-Theoretic Protection Adoption

We now define the payoff vector of the agents. A susceptible agent aims to balance the trade-off between the cost of adopting protection, denoted by the parameter
c_{\mathtt{P}}>0
, and the expected loss upon becoming infected. The expected loss is computed as the product of loss upon infection, captured by a parameter
L>0
, and the instantaneous probability of becoming infected. The latter quantity depends on the current social state
\mathbf{x}
, degree of the agent, and the action chosen by the agent. Formally, for a susceptible agent with degree
d
, we define its payoffs to be

F^{d}_{\mathtt{SU}}(\mathbf{x})=-Ld\Theta(\mathbf{x}),\qquad F^{d}_{\mathtt{SP}}(\mathbf{x})=-c_{\mathtt{P}}-L\alpha d\Theta(\mathbf{x}),

(7)

if the agent remains unprotected and adopts protection, respectively. In particular, when the agent adopts protection, it encounters an additional cost
c_{\mathtt{P}}
though its probability of becoming infected reduces due to the multiplying factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

In contrast, an infected agent is already infected, and there is no immediate risk of becoming infected. Consequently, we define

F^{d}_{\mathtt{IU}}(\mathbf{x})=-c_{\mathtt{IU}},\qquad F^{d}_{\mathtt{IP}}(\mathbf{x})=-c_{\mathtt{IP}},

(8)

to be the payoff of an infected agent if it remains unprotected and adopts protection, respectively. The parameter
c_{\mathtt{IU}}>0
captures the penalty imposed on an infected agent if it does not adopt protection (or adhere to quarantine norms), while
c_{\mathtt{IP}}>0
captures the inconvenience caused due to adopting protection while being sick. Note that the payoffs do not depend on the degree
d
or social state
\mathbf{x}
. We further assume
c_{\mathtt{IU}}>c_{\mathtt{IP}}
, which indicates that infected agents prefer to adopt protection.

We assume that agents revise their protection adoption strategies following the replicator dynamics
[
28
]
. We further assume that agents only replicate the strategies of other agents who have the same degree and infection status, i.e., susceptible individuals with degree
d
only replicate the strategies of other susceptible individuals of the same degree
d
(likewise for infected individuals). Consequently, the proportion of unprotected susceptible nodes of degree
d
evolves as

\displaystyle\dot{z}^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)

\displaystyle=z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t))\big[F^{d}_{\mathtt{SU}}(\mathbf{x}(t))-F^{d}_{\mathtt{SP}}(\mathbf{x}(t))\big]

\displaystyle=z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t))\big[c_{\mathtt{P}}-L(1-\alpha)d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t))\big].

(9)

Similarly, for infected individuals, we have

\displaystyle\dot{z}^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t)

\displaystyle=z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t)(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t))(c_{\mathtt{IP}}-c_{\mathtt{IU}}).

(10)

Thus, equations (
1
), (
9
) and (
10
) characterize the coupled evolution of the epidemic and population states at the same time-scale. The above set of dynamics has a large number of equilibrium points due to the structure of the replicator dynamics which induces stationary points at both
0
and
1
. In order to obtain further insights into the behavior of the above system, and characterize the equilibrium points, we analyze the coupled dynamics under timescale separation. Motivated by the past work
[
17
]
, we focus on the case in which the replicator dynamics evolves faster than the epidemic dynamics. This is a reasonable assumption as agents are likely to adjust their behavior faster than the spread of the epidemic due to increased awareness derived from conventional as well as social media. The coupled dynamics can now be written as a slow-fast system given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{y}^{d}(t)&=-\!\gamma y^{d}(t)\!+\!(1\!-y^{d}(t))(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)\!+\!\alpha(1\!-\!z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)))d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t)),\\
\epsilon\dot{z}^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)&=z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t)(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}(t))\big[c_{\mathtt{P}}-L(1-\alpha)d\Theta(\mathbf{x}(t))\big],\\
\epsilon\dot{z}^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t)&=z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t)(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}(t))(c_{\mathtt{IP}}-c_{\mathtt{IU}}),\end{split}

(11)

for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
, and where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
is a timescale separation variable
[
29
]
.

We first characterize the behavior of the agents (captured by (
9
) and (
10
)) for a given infection state
\mathbf{y}=\{y^{d}\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
. It follows from (
8
) and our assumption
c_{\mathtt{IU}}>c_{\mathtt{IP}}
that infected agents strictly prefer to adopt protection irrespective of the social state, and as a result,
z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}=0
is the unique stable equilibrium point of (
10
).

From the R.H.S. of (
2
), it follows that when
z^{d}_{\mathtt{I}}=0
,
\Theta(\mathbf{x})
does not depend on
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}
when
\mathbf{y}
is specified. Therefore, with a slight abuse of notation, we define

\Theta(\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}y^{d}\Big].

(12)

Now, for a susceptible agent, adopting protection is strictly preferred if and only if

\displaystyle F^{d}_{\mathtt{SU}}(\mathbf{x})<F^{d}_{\mathtt{SP}}(\mathbf{x})\iff

\displaystyle-Ld\Theta(\mathbf{y})<-c_{\mathtt{P}}-L\alpha d\Theta(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle c_{\mathtt{P}}<L(1-\alpha)d\Theta(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{y})>\frac{c_{\mathtt{P}}}{L(1-\alpha)d}=:\Theta^{d}_{th}.

(13)

In other words, the optimal strategy of a susceptible agent with degree
d
depends on whether
\Theta(\mathbf{y})
exceeds the degree-specific threshold
\Theta^{d}_{th}
defined in (
13
). If
\Theta(\mathbf{y})>\Theta^{d}_{th}
, then
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}=0
is the stable equilibrium of (
9
), while if
\Theta(\mathbf{y})<\Theta^{d}_{th}
, then the
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}=1
is the stable equilibrium of (
9
). If
\Theta(\mathbf{y})=\Theta^{d}_{th}
, then any
z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
could emerge as the equilibrium of (
9
). Thus, at a given
\mathbf{y}
, the replicator dynamics (which is the fast system since
\epsilon<1
) associated with both infected and susceptible agents has a unique stable equilibrium point except at a point with measure zero.

We now state the reduced dynamics for the epidemic (which is the slow system) as

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))<\Theta^{d}_{th}:

\displaystyle\quad\dot{y}^{d}(t)=-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))d\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t)),

(14a)

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))=\Theta^{d}_{th}:

\displaystyle\quad\dot{y}^{d}(t)\in\{-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))\times

\displaystyle\quad(z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}+\alpha(1-z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}))d\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))\ \rvert\ z^{d}_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]\},

(14b)

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))>\Theta^{d}_{th}:

\displaystyle\quad\dot{y}^{d}(t)=-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))\alpha d\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t)).

(14c)

The above dynamics approximates the coupled dynamics (
11
) as
\epsilon\to 0
, i.e., when individuals adopt protection in a strategic manner to maximize their payoffs as a function of the current epidemic state. Note that (
14
) admits a Filippov solution
[
30
, Proposition 3]
solution because the R.H.S. of (
14b
) is measurable and bounded.

Remark 1
.

The dynamical system (
14
) can be viewed as the dynamics of epidemic evolution when central authorities restrict interaction of nodes of a certain degree
d
whenever
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))
exceeds the threshold
\Theta^{d}_{th}
.

III
Analysis of Equilibrium

In this section, we characterize the existence and uniqueness of equilibrium of the dynamics (
14
). First observe that the thresholds
\Theta^{d}_{th}
, defined in (
13
), are monotonically decreasing in the degree
d
, i.e., agents with a larger degree switch to adopting protection for a smaller value of
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))
. Let
d_{\min}
be the smallest degree for which
\Theta^{d_{\min}}_{th}<1
. Then, we divide the region
[0,1]
into
(d^{\max}-d_{\min}+2)
number of intervals, denoted
\{\mathcal{I}_{d^{\max}+1},\mathcal{I}_{d^{\max}},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d_{\min}}\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{d^{\max}+1}:=[0,\Theta^{d^{\max}}_{th}),

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{d_{\min}}:=(\Theta^{d_{\min}}_{th},1],

(15b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{d}:=(\Theta^{d}_{th},\Theta^{d-1}_{th}),

(15c)

for
d\in\{d^{\max},d^{\max}-1,\ldots,d_{\min}+1\}
.

We will now closely examine the dynamics (
14
) when
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))
belongs to one of the intervals as stated above. If
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))\in\mathcal{I}_{d^{\star}}
, then
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))>\Theta^{d}_{th}
for all degree
d\geq d^{\star}
, and
\Theta(\mathbf{y}(t))<\Theta^{d}_{th}
for all degree
d<d^{\star}
. For this particular regime, the dynamics (
14
) can be stated as:

\displaystyle d<d^{\star}:

\displaystyle\quad\dot{y}^{d}(t)=-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad d\sum_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{d^{\prime}m_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d^{\prime}}y^{d^{\prime}}(t)\Big],

(16a)

\displaystyle d\geq d^{\star}:

\displaystyle\quad\dot{y}^{d}(t)=-\gamma y^{d}(t)+(1-y^{d}(t))\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\alpha d\sum_{d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\frac{d^{\prime}m_{d^{\prime}}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d^{\prime}}y^{d^{\prime}}(t)\Big].

(16b)

Before analyzing the equilibria of (
14
), we prove the following result on the equilibria of (
16
). We first define the following quantity:

\mathcal{R}(d^{\star}):=\sum^{d^{\star}-1}_{d=1}\frac{d^{2}m_{d}\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}\gamma}+\sum^{d^{\max}}_{d=d^{\star}}\frac{\alpha d^{2}m_{d}\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}\gamma}.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the dynamics (
16
) for a specified
d^{\star}\in\{d_{\min},\ldots,d^{\max}+1\}
. We have the following characterization of its equilibria.

1.

If
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})\leq 1
, then the disease-free equilibrium is the only equilibrium of (
16
).

2.

If
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})>1
, then in addition to the disease-free equilibrium, there exists a unique nonzero endemic equilibrium of (
16
).

The proof is presented in Appendix
A-B
, and leverages connection between the dynamics in (
16
), and the N-Intertwined mean-field approximation (NIMFA) of the networked SIS epidemic dynamics
[
31
,
32
]
.

When
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})>1
, we denote the endemic equilibrium with
\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star})
. Following (
6
), the quantity
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))
at the endemic equilibrium is the unique value satisfying

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum^{d^{\star}-1}_{d=1}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}\frac{d\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}}{\gamma+d\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum^{d^{\max}}_{d=d^{\star}}\Big[\frac{dm_{d}}{d^{\mathtt{avg}}}\frac{\alpha d\beta_{\mathtt{P}}^{d}}{\gamma+\alpha d\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))}\Big].

(17)

If
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})\leq 1
, we define
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))=0
. The following lemma establishes monotonicity of
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})
and
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))
.

Lemma 2
.

The quantities
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})
and
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))
are monotonically increasing in
d^{\star}
.

Proof.

Note that as
d^{\star}
increases, some entries move from the second summation to the first summation in the definition of
\mathcal{R}(d^{\star})
, and the terms in the first summation are larger because
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Similarly, if
d^{\star}
increases and
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}})
remains unchanged, then the R.H.S. of (
17
) increases. In order to achieve R.H.S. equal to
1
,
\Theta(\mathbf{y}_{\mathtt{EE}}(d^{\star}))
needs to increase.
∎

We are now ready to establish the existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium of the dynamics (
14
) by leveraging the results established above.

Theorem 1
.

For the dynamics (
14
), we have the following characterization of its equilibria.

1.

If
\mathcal{R}(d^{\max}+1)\leq 1
, then the disease-free equilibrium is the only equilibrium of (
14
).

2.

Now suppose
\mathcal{R}(d^{\max}+1)>1
. Let
\Theta^{d^{\max}+1}_{th}:=0
for convenience. Let

V
Conclusion

We analyzed the problem of strategic adoption of partially effective protection in large-scale networks in the population game framework. We derived the coupled epidemic-behavioral dynamics and relied on time-scale separation to derive the epidemic dynamics under optimal protection adoption strategies of the agents which depends on their degree. We then rigorously established the existence and uniqueness of stationary equilibrium of the above dynamics. We numerically illustrated the convergence of the dynamics to the equilibrium as well as the impacts of heterogeneous node degrees, infection rates and cost of protection adoption on the epidemic prevalence at the equilibrium. We aim to leverage the insights derived from this work to design intervention schemes which incentivizes protection adoption among users and reduce the prevalence of epidemics in follow up works. In addition, analyzing the protection adoption behavior of non-myopic or forward-looking agents for networked SIS as well as other classes of epidemic models remain as promising directions for future research.

Consider a directed graph or network, denoted

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{i}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma p_{i}(t)+(1-p_{i}(t))\sum^{n}_{j=1}a_{ij}p_{j}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)

\displaystyle=(A-D)p(t)-P(t)Ap(t),

(19)

where
D=\mathtt{diag}(\gamma,\gamma,\ldots,\gamma)
is the diagonal matrix of all recovery rates, and

. We now reproduce the following theorem from past works regarding the existence and stability of the equilibrium points of (
19

Theorem 2

(
[

,
32
]
)

Suppose the graph

is strongly connected. Then,

1.

the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) with

\rho(D^{-1}A)\leq 1
, and

2.

a unique endemic equilibrium (EE) with

exists if and only if
