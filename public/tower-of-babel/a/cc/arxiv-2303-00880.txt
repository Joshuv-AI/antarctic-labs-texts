A complex-valued non-Gaussianity measure for quantum states of light
Andrew J. Pizzimenti ∗, Prajit Dhara ∗, Zacharie Van Herstraeten ∗, Sijie Cheng, Christos N. Gagatsos ∗

Abstract
We consider a quantity that is the differential relative entropy between a generic Wigner function and a Gaussian one. We prove that said quantity is minimized with respect to its Gaussian argument, if both Wigner functions in the argument of the Wigner differential entropy have the same first and second moments, i.e., if the Gaussian argument is the Gaussian associate of the other, generic Wigner function. Therefore, we introduce the differential relative entropy between any Wigner function and its Gaussian associate and we examine its potential as a non-Gaussianity measure. We prove that said quantity is faithful, invariant under Gaussian unitary operations, and find a sufficient condition on its monotonic behavior under Gaussian channels. We provide numerical results supporting aforesaid condition. The proposed, phase-space based non-Gaussianity measure is complex-valued, with its imaginary part possessing the physical meaning of the Wigner function’s negative volume. At the same time, the real part of this measure provides an extra layer of information, rendering the complex-valued quantity a measure of non-Gaussianity, instead of a quantity pertaining only to the negativity of the Wigner function. We examine the measure’s usefulness to non-Gaussian quantum state engineering with partial measurements.

I
Introduction

Non-Gaussian states of light have attracted the interest of the broad quantum information community as they possess properties which unlock quantum enhancement in a series of protocols. Particularly, quantum systems that do not exhibit negativity in their Wigner function descriptions, can be simulated efficiently by a classical computer
Mari and Eisert 2012
and it has been demonstrated that such states do not enable a quantum computational advantage
Veitch et al. 2013
. In the same context, non-Gaussian states are of fundamental interest for quantum computers’ architectures, based on all-photonic cluster states
Pant et al. 2019
. Further, non-Gaussian states play a central role in quantum communications
Niset et al. 2009
, while they are useful for all-optical quantum repeaters
Pant et al. 2017
;
Azuma et al. 2015
;
He et al. 2020
.

Past, recent, and contemporary works
Pizzimenti et al. 2021
;
Gagatsos and Guha 2021
;
Fiurášek et al. 2005
;
Ourjoumtsev et al. 2009
;
Ra et al. 2017
;
Takahashi et al. 2008
;
Averchenko et al. 2016
;
Tualle-Brouri et al. 2009
;
Marek et al. 2008
;
Barnett et al. 2018
;
Walschaers et al. 2018
;
Arzani et al. 2019
;
Walschaers et al. 2017
;
Chabaud et al. 2017
;
Su et al. 2019
;
Gagatsos and Guha 2019
have studied the production of non-Gaussian states utilizing conditional partial measurements. In such schemes a subset of the modes of a multi-mode Gaussian state are projected on non-Gaussian states (for e.g. Fock states, which describe photon counting). This is turn projects the undetected modes into a non-Gaussian state (referred to as the conditional state), unless the conditional state is a result of partial projection on vacuum states. Typically, the task is to optimize the parameters of the Gaussian resource state and to identify the photon counting pattern(s), such that the generated non-Gaussian state is a good approximation, as quantified by some figure of merit, of a non-Gaussian target state.

Therefore, quantifying the non-Gaussian character of a state is essential. This task can be separated into two distinct tasks: To work within the context of a resource theory and establish a non-Gaussianity monotone, or to work within the context of a non-Gaussianity measure. Monotones and measures, are quantities that map a state
\rho
to a number, i.e., the action of a non-Gaussianity monotone or measure
\mu[.]
on the state
\rho
is
\mu[\rho]=c\in\mathbb{C}
. Monotones and measures are thus functionals of the state (ergo our notation using square brackets), i.e., quantities that map the state (written on some basis) or a quasi-probability of the state to a complex number
c
. This number should be informative on the non-Gaussian character of the state.

To explain the difference between monotones and measures, let us take a few steps back. A general resource theory requires the definition of three things: the
resource
, the operations that cannot increase the defined resource (typically called
free operations
), and the states that do not possess the resource (typically referred to as
free states
). In the context of a non-Gaussianity resource theory, one can define the resource to be the negativity of the Wigner function, i.e., Wigner-positive states, even if they are non-Gaussian, are considered free states. The other option is to define the overall non-Gaussianity of the state as a resource, where free states are all Gaussian states. For both approaches, the free operations are all Gaussian protocols: Gaussian unitary operations, partial trace, partial Gaussian measurements, tensor product with a Gaussian state. A proper monotone evaluated for any given state from both of the aforementioned approaches, should then remain invariant under Gaussian unitary operations, should not increase on average under partial trace, be non-increasing on average when part of the state is measured by projections on Gaussian states, and remain invariant when the state under question is composed (by a tensor-product) with a Gaussian state. Further properties of a monotone may include
faithfulness
, i.e., the monotone to be equal to zero if and only if the state for which is evaluated for is a free state. Important works considering the negativity of the Wigner function as a resource include Refs.
Albarelli et al. 2018
;
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
. A resource theory, considering all Gaussian states as free states is however lacking. Another approach, is to define a
measure
of non-Gaussianity. Non-Gaussianity measures are mathematically more relaxed than their monotone counterparts. However, they are equally important in informing on the non-Gaussian character of the state under examination. Non-Gaussianity measures are generally expected to be faithful, invariant under Gaussian unitary operations, and non-increasing under Gaussian channels. The line of research yielded interesting results
Genoni et al. 2007
;
Genoni et al. 2008
;
Genoni and Paris 2010
;
Park et al. 2021
;
Park et al. 2021
and in this work, we follow the same path.

The phase-space description of quantum states of light, and particularly the Wigner function, has enabled the detailed study of quantum light. It is well known that the Wigner function constitutes a complete description of a state. Furthermore, the Wigner function can reveal intrinsic properties of a quantum system pertaining to its non-Gaussian character, i.e., exhibiting negative volume, shape of positive and negative regions. Of course, examining the shape of Wigner function assumes that we study single-mode states, which correspond to three-dimensional plots of the corresponding Wigner function. This last remark, motivates further our pursue of a non-Gaussianity state for generic, multi-dimensional state. Then, the question we pose is if we can use the Wigner function of a state to build a faithful non-Gaussianity measure. It is true that some of the measures already existing in the literature, can be worked out in phase-space. For example, the measure based on the Hilbert-Schmidt distance
Genoni et al. 2007
;
Genoni and Paris 2010
, essentially requires to calculate the trace of the product of two density operators; something that can be done efficiently in phase-space by integrating together the two corresponding Wigner functions (and taking into account the proper constant in front of said integral, see
Ferraro et al. 2005
). Also, recent work has established phase-space based non-Gaussianity measures
Park et al. 2021
. In this work, we exploit the recently introduced Wigner entropy
Van Herstraeten and Cerf 2021
;
Hertz 2018
and we introduce a complex-valued non-Gaussianity measure whose imaginary part is the total negative volume of the Wigner function corresponding to the state under examination. The real part of the measure provides further information on the non-Gaussian character of the state, and both imaginary and real parts form a faithful non-Gaussianity measure. Therefore, our measure incorporates the virtues of a genuine
quantum
non-Gaussianity measure into a single measure that incorporates the non-Gaussian character of positive Gaussian states as well.

This paper is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
we define the Wigner relative entropy, the Gaussian associate state, and our measure. In Sec.
III
we prove that our measure is faithful, i.e., it is equal to zero if and only if the state under question is Gaussian. In Sec.
IV
, we prove that our measure remains invariant under Gaussian unitary operations. In Sec.
V
we give a sufficient condition on the expected behavior of the measure under Gaussian channels. We also provide numerical evidence that our measure is monotonic under Gaussian channels. In the subsequent sections, we examine further properties. Namely, in Sec.
VI
we employ functional methods to minimize our measure. Section
VII
examines a few non-Gaussian states of interest and the potential of the utility of our measure for quantum state engineering based on probabilistic, heralded schemes. Finally, in Sec.
VIII
we outline the main findings of this work and we discuss potential further research paths.

II
Definition of the measure

Gaussian states are uniquely defined by their displacement vector
\mathbf{d}
and their covariance matrix
V

(see for example
Ferraro et al. 2005
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
). Of course, any non-Gaussian
N-
mode state has a
2N-
dimensional displacement vector and
2N\times 2N-
dimensional covariance matrix, whose definitions are identical to their Gaussian counterparts. That is, for every state
\rho
, the elements
d_{i}
and
V_{ij}
of the displacement vector and covariance matrix respectively, are defined as,

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{r}_{i}\rho)

(1)

\displaystyle V_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\{\hat{r}_{i}-d_{i},\hat{r}_{j}-d_{j}\}\rho\right),

(2)

where
i,j=1,\ldots,N
.

We define the Gaussian associate of
\rho
, denoted as
\rho_{G}
, the state that has the same displacement vector and covariance matrix as
\rho
. State
\rho
corresponds to a Wigner function
W(\mathbf{r})
, while state
\rho_{G}
corresponds to the Gaussian Wigner function,

\displaystyle W_{G}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{V}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d})^{T}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d})}

(3)

where
N
is the number of modes,
\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})
are the coordinates’ vector, such that
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{N})
is the vector of canonical position and
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})
is the vector of canonical momentum. We work in the
qqpp
representation of the state and we set
\hbar=1
.

Typically, a non-Gaussianity measure (henceforth denoted as nGM) for a state
\rho
, is a distance-like measure between the state under examination and its
closest
Gaussian state, intuitively expected to be the state
\rho_{G}
. For example, the quantum relative entropy and a measure based on the Hilbert-Schmidt distance between
\rho
and
\rho_{G}
have been studied in Ref.
Genoni et al. 2007
;
Genoni et al. 2008
;
Genoni and Paris 2010
.

Using the quantum Wigner entropy, which is
Van Herstraeten and Cerf 2021
;
Hertz 2018
defined as,

\displaystyle h[W]=-\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\ln W(\mathbf{r}),

(4)

we define the Wigner relative entropy (WRE) between a Wigner function
W(\mathbf{r})
and some Gaussian Wigner function of a quantum state
\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})
as,

\displaystyle D[W||\tilde{W}_{G}]=-h[W]-\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\ln\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})

(5)

The Gaussian Wigner function
\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})
is arbitrary, i.e. ,

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}\sqrt{\det\tilde{\mathbf{V}}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}})}

(6)

is not assumed to be equal to
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
. Instead, we define the quantity,

\displaystyle\mu[W]=\underset{\tilde{W}_{G}\in\mathcal{G}}{\text{min}}D[W||\tilde{W}_{G}],

(7)

where the functional minimization with respect to
\tilde{W}_{G}
is over the set
\mathcal{G}
of all Gaussian Wigner functions. Using Eqs. (
3
) and (
6
), Eq. (
5
) gives,

\displaystyle h[W||\tilde{W}_{G}]=-h[W]+N\ln(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\tilde{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{d}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}(\mathbf{d}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}}).

(8)

In Eq. (
8
), the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}
is positive definite (as it is the inverse of a quantum state’s covariance matrix). Therefore, we have the inequality,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{d}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}(\mathbf{d}-\tilde{\mathbf{d}})\geq 0

(9)

with equality to zero if and only if
\mathbf{d}=\tilde{\mathbf{d}}
, which means that
\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})
and
W(\mathbf{r})
must have identical displacement vectors. Therefore, from Eq. (
8
), we now have to minimize the quantity,

\displaystyle h[W||\tilde{W}_{G}]=

\displaystyle-h[W]+N\ln(2\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\tilde{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}).

(10)

We proceed to show that Eq. (
10
) is minimized if and only if
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{V}
. Using the right-hand side of Eq. (
10
) it suffices to prove the following inequality,

\displaystyle\ln\det\tilde{\mathbf{V}}+\text{Tr}(\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})\geq\ln\det\mathbf{V}+\text{Tr}(\mathbf{VV}^{-1})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})\geq\ln\det(\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})+2N

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\mathbf{A}\geq\ln\det\mathbf{A}+2N,

(11)

where we have defined
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}
. The matrix
A
is positive definite as is the product of positive definite matrices. Let the eigenvalues of
A
be
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2N}\}
with
\lambda_{i}>0,\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,2N
. For any positive
x
, it is always true that
x-1\geq\ln x
. Therefore, for the eigenvalues of
A
, we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\left(\lambda_{i}-1\right)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\ln\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\lambda_{i}-2N\geq\ln\prod_{i=1}^{2N}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\mathbf{A}\geq\ln\det\mathbf{A}+2N,

(12)

which is the desired inequality (
11
). Therefore,
D[W||\tilde{W}_{G}]
is minimized if
\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})
is the Gaussian associate of
W(\mathbf{r})
, i.e.,
\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})=W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
.

Hence, with the proof above Eq. (
7
) gives,

\displaystyle\mu[W]=D[W||W_{G}].

(13)

We note that there is only one argument in
\mu[.]
as
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
has a functional dependence on
W(\mathbf{r})
and is uniquely defined. Using Eq. (
5
), we write,

\displaystyle\mu[W]

\displaystyle=-h[W]-\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\ln W_{G}(\mathbf{r})

(14a)

\displaystyle=-h[W]-\int d^{2N}rW_{G}(\mathbf{r})\ln W_{G}(\mathbf{r})

(14b)

\displaystyle=-h[W]+\ln\left((2\pi e)^{N}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{V}}\right),

(14c)

where we have used the fact that,

\displaystyle\ln W_{G}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\ln\left((2\pi)^{N}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{V}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d})^{T}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d}),

(15)

rendering the integrals in Eqs. (
14a
) and (
14b
) equal, i.e., only the first and second moments will play role in said integrals. Equation (
14b
) is equal to Eq. (
14c
) as straightforward evaluation gives,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d^{2N}rW_{G}(\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d})^{T}\mathbf{V}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{d})=-N.

It is of central importance to discuss how to deal with Wigner functions that posses negative volume. We can write any Wigner function as,

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{r})=|W(\mathbf{r})|e^{i(2k+1)\pi\phi(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})},

(16)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and,

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&W(\mathbf{r})<0\\
0,&W(\mathbf{r})\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

is the marker function which distinguishes between the positive and negative functional values in the domain. Therefore,
\ln W(\mathbf{r})=\ln|W(\mathbf{r})|+i(2k+1)\pi\phi(\mathbf{r})
where
k
defines the branch of the complex logarithm. We choose to work with
k=-1
and we rewrite Eq. (
14a
) as,

\displaystyle\mu[W]=\text{Re}\mu[W]+i\text{Im}\mu[W],

(19)

where,

\displaystyle\text{Re}\mu[W]

\displaystyle=-\text{Re}h[W]+h[W_{G}]

(20)

\displaystyle\text{Im}\mu[W]

\displaystyle=-\pi\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\phi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\pi|\mathcal{\mathcal{V}}_{-}|

(21)

and

\displaystyle\text{Re}h[W]=-\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\ln|W(\mathbf{r})|,

(22)

\displaystyle h[W_{G}]

\displaystyle=-\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})\ln W_{G}(\mathbf{r})

(23a)

\displaystyle=-\int d^{2N}rW_{G}(\mathbf{r})\ln W_{G}(\mathbf{r})

(23b)

\displaystyle=\ln\left((2\pi e)^{N}\sqrt{\det\mathbf{V}}\right),

(23c)

where
|\mathcal{\mathcal{V}}_{-}|
is the total negative volume of the Wigner function
W(\mathbf{r})
.

III
Faithfulness

By
faithfulness
we mean that
W(\mathbf{r})
is Gaussian, if and only if
\mu[W]=0
.
We clarify that the meaning of
\mu[W]=0
is that both its real and imaginary parts are simultaneously equal to zero.

Proof of direct statement:
Let
W(\mathbf{r})=\tilde{W}_{G}(\mathbf{r})
, i.e., the Wigner function under consideration is Gaussian, but not necessarily equal to its Gaussian associate
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
. The Gaussian associate of
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
is equal to itself. Since a Gaussian Wigner function is positive over its entire domain, i.e.,
|W_{G}(\mathbf{r})|=W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
, we get,

\displaystyle\text{Im}\mu[\tilde{W}_{G}]=\pi|\mathcal{V}_{-}|=0.

(24)

From said fact and Eqs. (
4
) and (
22
), we get
\text{Re}h[\tilde{W}_{G}]=h[\tilde{W}_{G}]
. Hence using Eq. (
20
) we have,

\displaystyle\text{Re}\mu[\tilde{W}_{G}]

\displaystyle=-\text{Re}h[\tilde{W}_{G}]+h[\tilde{W}_{G}]=0.

(25)

Proof of converse statement:
For some Wigner function
W(\mathbf{r})
, let
\mu[W]=0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{Re}\mu[W]

\displaystyle=0

(26a)

\displaystyle\text{Im}\mu[W]

\displaystyle=0.

(26b)

Equation (
26b
) imposes that
W(\mathbf{r})
is positive over its entire domain,
which implies that
W(\mathbf{r})
is well-defined probability distribution function i.e.
W(\mathbf{r})\equiv W_{>0}(\mathbf{r})
. Consequently,
W(\mathbf{r})
can be analyzed using classical probability theory, even if the state corresponding to
W(\mathbf{r})
, is a quantum state with no classical description. Therefore,
\text{Re}\,\mu[W_{>0}]
represents a classical relative entropy quantity being equal to zero,

\displaystyle D[W_{>0}||\tilde{W}_{G}]=0

(27)

which necessarily implies that
W_{>0}(\mathbf{r})=\tilde{W}_{G}(\textbf{r})
. It is worthwhile to note that given the natural emergence of
\mathrm{Im}\,\mu[W]
as the total negative volume of the
W(\mathbf{r})
ensures the faithfulness property of the complex-valued
\mu[W]
.

IV
Invariance under Gaussian unitary operators

\text{Re}h[W]
(and consequently
h[W_{G}]
) are proven to be invariant under symplectic transformations in Ref.
Hertz 2018
. Equivalently, Gaussian unitary operations acting on the state or to the corresponding Wigner function
W(\mathbf{r})
leave
\text{Re}h[W]
unchanged. Symplectic transformations also conserve the negative volume of a Wigner function, i.e.,
\text{Im}\mu[W]
remains invariant when the corresponding state undergoes a Gaussian unitary transformation. Therefore
\mu[W]
is invariant under Gaussian unitary operations.

V
Behavior under Gaussian channels

V.1
Sufficient Condition

Figure 1:
Plot of the trace of the Fisher information matrix
\text{Tr}(\mathbf{J})
(blue) and
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{V}^{-1})
(orange) for the Fock states from
n=0
up to
n=40
.

Intuitively, we expect the value of a non-Gaussianity measure to decrease under the action of a Gaussian channel. Namely, we expect such channels to yield a
more Gaussian
output compared to its input, since it has been mixed with a Gaussian environment through some Gaussian unitary.
In our concern, we study a complex-valued non-Gaussianity measure, and should then check separately the behaviour of the real and imaginary parts.
Fortunately for us, the imaginary part of
\mu[W]
is proportional to the negative volume of
W
, which has previously been studied and shown to be non-increasing under the action of a Gaussian channel
Albarelli et al. 2018
;
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
.
This section will thus be devoted to the evolution of
\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
when
W
evolves in a Gaussian channel.

In Appendix
A
, we present an equivalent condition for the real part of the measure to decrease under any Gaussian channel.
The condition reads as follows,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}\geq\mathbf{J}^{-1}\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{V}^{-1}\leq\mathbf{J}

(28)

where
\mathbf{V}
is the covariance matrix of
W(\mathbf{r})
and
\mathbf{J}
is the Fisher information matrix corresponding to
W(\mathbf{r})
(which can be extended to Wigner functions with negative regions, see Appendix
A
).
The equivalent condition from which Eq. (
28
) stems from is (see Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{V}^{-1}-\mathbf{J})]\leq 0,

(29)

for all quantum covariance matrices
\mathbf{G}
. For single-mode channels, if
\mathbf{G}\propto\mathbf{I}
(with a positive constant), the channel can be a Gaussian phase-invariant channel, i.e., the pure loss channel or the quantum-limited amplifier. For said case, Eq. (
29
) becomes,

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(\mathbf{V}^{-1})\leq\text{Tr}(\mathbf{J}),

(30)

which is what we plot in Fig.
1
, for the special case of Fock states
|n\rangle
, known to possess negative regions in their Wigner representations for
n\geq 1
. The approach to compute
\mathbf{J}
for Fock states is given in Appendix
B
and is based on computing the principal value of the involved integrals when is necessary.

Interestingly, Eq. (
28
) corresponds precisely to the Cramér-Rao bound
Cramér 1999
;
Rao 1992
, and is thus always satisfied for non-negative Wigner functions, as they correspond to genuine probability distributions. For Wigner functions that take negative values, we were not able conclude that Eq. (
28
) always holds, nor we have a reason to believe that it does for all states. Nevertheless, our numerical study is partially presented in Fig.
1
illustrating that Eq. (
29
) is satisfied for Fock states under the action of certain Gaussian channels, i.e., when
\mathbf{G}\propto\mathbf{I}
. Furthermore, in Section
V.2
we provide more numerical evidence on the decreasing behavior of
\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
under Gaussian channels.

V.2
Numerical Evidence

In this section we show that
\mu[W]
, for a set of random qudits, is non-increasing under a thermal loss channel. The action of a thermal loss channel is to combine the input state,
\rho_{0}
, and a thermal state with mean photon number
\overline{n}_{B}
, represented by
\rho_{\text{th}}(\overline{n}_{B})
, on a beam-splitter of transmissivity
\tau
. Tracing out the environmental mode leaves us with the channel output,
\rho_{f}
. Letting
\overline{n}_{B}
go to zero reduces the thermal loss channel to the pure loss channel and letting
\tau
go to
1
corresponds to applying identity to
\rho_{0}
. We choose to work with the Kraus operators representation instead of checking the vallidity of Eq. (
28
). The thermal loss channel may be decomposed into a pure loss channel of transmissivity
\eta=\tau/G
and quantum-limited amplifier with gain
G=1+(1-\tau)\overline{n}_{B}

Ivan et al. 2011
. The thermal loss channel output is then given by
\rho_{f}=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\hat{B}_{k}\hat{A}_{l}\rho_{0}\hat{A}_{l}^{\dagger}\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}
where the Kraus operators of the pure loss channel,
\hat{A}_{l}
, and quantum-limited amplifier,
\hat{B}_{k}
, are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(1-\eta)^{l}}{l!}}\eta^{\hat{n}/2}\hat{a}^{l}

(31a)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k!}\frac{1}{G}\Big(\frac{G-1}{G}\Big)^{k}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{k}G^{-\hat{n}/2}.

(31b)

We prepare
100
random qudits for
d\in\{2,3,4\}
, i.e.
300
qudits in total, by generating random diagonal states in the logical basis
\{\ket{\overline{0}}\!\!\bra{\overline{0}},\ket{\overline{1}}\!\!\bra{\overline{1}},\ldots,\ket{\overline{d-1}}\!\!\bra{\overline{d-1}}\}
and then applying a Haar-random unitary to them
Zyczkowski and Kus 1994
;
Życzkowski et al. 1998
. By setting the logical states to different Fock states we can systematically explore the behavior of our measure under the thermal loss channel for states of varying complexity. In Fig.
2

VI
Minimum value of the measure

In this paper we define a nGM which is complex and therefore, proving that its real and imaginary parts are strictly positive, is unnecessary; Indeed, one can redefine the nGM as
|\mu[W]|^{2}
which is by definition always non-negative and all properties in previous sections and numerical simulations in previous and next sections hold without any qualitative difference. However, since we explore a new quantity, i.e., essentially a relative entropy between Wigner functions, we will undertake the task to examine the minimum value of the real and imaginary parts of
\mu[W]
.

VI.1
Real part

We employ functional methods to find (local) minima of the functional
\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
, and show that they coincide with Gaussian states. We consider the functional,

\displaystyle R[W]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Re}\mu[W]+\lambda_{1}G^{(1)}[W]

(34)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}G_{i}^{(2)}[W]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\Lambda_{ij}G_{ij}^{(3)}[W],

where
\text{Re}\mu[W]
is given in Eq. (
20
),
\{\lambda_{1},\mu_{i},\Lambda_{ij}\},\ i,j=1,\ldots,N
are the Lagrange multipliers to impose constraints in a functional form. The first constraint requires that the
W(\textbf{r})
must be normalized to
1
,

\displaystyle G^{(1)}[W]=\int d^{2N}r\,W(\mathbf{r})=1.

(35)

The second set of constraints demand that
W(\mathbf{r})
and
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
have the same displacement vector with components
\{d_{i}\},\ i=1,\ldots,N
,

\displaystyle G_{i}^{(2)}[W]=\int d^{2N}r\,W(\textbf{r})r_{i}=d_{i}.

(36)

The last set of constraints are functionals ensuring that
W(\mathbf{r})
and
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
have the same covariance matrices with elements
V_{ij},\ i,j=1,\ldots,N
,

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{(3)}[W]=\int d^{2N}r\,W(\textbf{r})r_{i}r_{j}=V_{ij}+d_{i}d_{j}.

(37)

We impose a fourth constraint,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{r}\rightarrow\mathbf{r}_{0}}W(\textbf{r})=W(\textbf{r}_{0}),\forall\ \mathbf{r}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(38)

i.e.,
W(\mathbf{r})
must be a continuous function, as a necessary criterion for
W(\textbf{r})
to correspond to a physical state
Mandilara et al. 2009
. This last constraint is not imposed in the form of a Lagrange multiplier, instead, it will be used to filter out non-physical critical points.

Equations (
36
) and (
37
) ensure that
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
is indeed the Gaussian associate of
W(\textbf{r})
. Setting up said relation between
W(\textbf{r})
and
W_{G}(\textbf{r})
through the constraints allows one to treat
W_{G}(\textbf{r})
in Eq. (
34
) as being any Gaussian Wigner function, i.e., not necessarily the Gaussian associate of
W(\textbf{r})
; the constraints will impose the desired relation between
W(\textbf{r})
and
W_{G}(\textbf{r})
. Therefore, when performing the functional derivative on Eq. (
34
), all terms containing only
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
, will disappear. Therefore, we can rewrite Eq. (
34
) as,

\displaystyle R[W]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Re}h[W]+\lambda_{1}G^{(1)}[W]

(39)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}G_{i}^{(2)}[W]+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\Lambda_{ij}G_{ij}^{(3)}[W].

In Appendix
C
we perform the first functional derivative and we find all critical points, (i.e., functions
W(\mathbf{\mathbf{r}})
) that respect the constraints of Eqs. (
35
)–(
37
). These critical points are the Gaussian associate of
W(\mathbf{r})
, i.e.,
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
, and
partially flipped
Gaussians, i.e., functions that satisfy
|W_{fG}(\mathbf{r})|=W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
. The partially flipped Gaussian Wigner function can be thought of as follows: take a Gaussian Wigner function and flip part(s) of it (i.e. negate the function value) with respect to any (or all) of the
\mathbf{r}
axes. We note that such functions are non-continuous, except if one flips the whole Gaussian function, however, such a case is rejected as it would violate the normalization condition of Eq. (
35
). Moreover, all partially flipped Gaussians violate constraint (
38
). Therefore, the only
critical point that corresponds to physical state is
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
.

Subsequently, we calculate the second functional derivative of Eq. (
39
), and we find that
W_{G}(\mathbf{r})
corresponds to a minimum for Eq. (
39
). When the functional of Eq. (
39
) is evaluated for
W_{G}(\textbf{r})
, gives
\text{Re}\mu[W_{G}]=0
. The partially flipped Gaussian Wigner functions do not correspond to neither minima nor maxima.

From these observations, we conclude that the Gaussian state with first and second order moments defined by (
36
)-(
37
) is the only local minimum of the functional
\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
.
If we were to consider only Wigner-positive states, we could at this point establish that it is also a global minimum, from the concavity of
\mathrm{Re}h
over Wigner-positive states (which follows from the concavity of
\varphi(x)=-x\ln x
over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
). However, we cannot use that argument anymore when Wigner-negative states come into the picture, as the functional
\mathrm{Re}h
then becomes neither concave nor convex (since
\varphi(x)=-x\ln|x|
is neither concave nor convex over
\mathbb{R}
). Having a single physical minimum, does not exclude the possibility of
\text{Re}\mu[W]
being unbounded from below. However, we expect (from numerics of Sections
VII
and
V.2
, where all
\text{Re}\mu[W]
are non-negative) that some physicality condition, which is non-tractable to include in the analytic functional optimization, could render
\text{Re}{\mu}[W]
bounded from below by zero.

VI.2
Imaginary part

From Eq. (
21
), the imaginary part is always non-negative, i.e.,
\text{Im }\mu[W]\geq 0
, as it is proportional (with a positive constant) to modulo the negative volume of the Wigner function. It is worthwhile to note that this is a direct consequence of choosing a negative
k
as the branch of the complex logarithm in Sec.
II
.

VIII
Conclusions

In this work we ventured to give an application-flavored interpretation of the recently introduced quantum Wigner entropy
Van Herstraeten and Cerf 2021
. To this end, we introduced the WRE and defined
\mu[W]
such that under the condition of Eq. (
28
) behaves as a proper non-Gaussianity measure. Our measure can serve as a figure of merit of the quality of non-Gaussian states, generated by partial non-Gaussian measurements on Gaussian states. Indeed, the nGM introduced in this work holds information on the negative volume of the Wigner function in a naturally emerging way. At the same time,
\text{Re}\mu[W]
provides an extra layer of information on the non-Gaussian character of the underlying state. We anticipate that our work will spark interest in non-Gaussian state engineering in the following way: Optimizing the Gaussian state under partial photon detection and the (partial) photon number pattern
Su et al. 2019
, to generate a state whose
\mu[W]
is close to
\mu[W_{\text{GKP}}]
, where
W_{\text{GKP}}
is the Wigner function of the physical (i.e., finite squeezing) GKP state. Examination and comparison of the error-correcting properties of the measure-optimized states (in contrast to fidelity-optimized states) is an open question for forthcoming work.

Moreover, for future theoretical endeavors, complete understanding of the properties and physical meaning of
\text{Re}\mu[W]
is of potential interest. It is worthwhile to examine the potential use of
\mu[W]
within the context of a non-Gaussianity resource theory (i.e. from the point of view of monotones). Lastly, we believe that the conditions of Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
) are interesting in their own right; non-negative Wigner functions are known to satisfy them, however it is interesting that a plethora of partialy-negative Wigner functions (e.g. cat states and GKP states under thermal loss) also seem to numerically satisfy them, as the strong evidence presented in this work suggests. Therefore, a complete understanding of which states under the action of which Gaussian channel, respect said conditions is another possible future research path.

Appendix A
Evolution of the non-Gaussianity measure in Gaussian channels

In this Appendix, we derive a sufficient condition for the non-Gaussianity measure to decrease under the action of any Gaussian channel.
The condition is then shown to be always satisfied for states with non-negative Wigner functions.
Note that it is proven in
Albarelli et al. 2018
;
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
that the negative volume can only decrease under the action of a Gaussian channel, so that we only condiser the real part of the non-Gaussianity measure here,
i.e.

\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
.

A.1
Multimode bosonic Gaussian channels

The mathematical description of multimode Gaussian channels is provided in Ref.
Caruso et al. 2008
.
In Wigner space, multimode Gaussian channels act as the succession of a rescaling operation and a convolution with a Gaussian distribution (for completeness we should also include a displacement, but we omit it here since it has no effect on our measure).
We define the multimode rescaling operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}
and the convolution as follows:

\displaystyle\left(A\ast B\right)(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\iint A(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})B(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W\right](\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det\mathbf{S}|}W\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{r}\right)

(45)

Note that when the rescaling matrix is proportional to the identity (so that
\mathbf{S}=s\mathbf{I}
), we will simply note the rescaling operator as
\mathcal{L}_{s}
(in place of
\mathcal{L}_{s\mathbf{I}}
).
Using these two operations, we can then express any Gaussian channel
\mathcal{M}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left[W\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W\ast G\right],

(46)

where
\mathbf{S}
is the rescaling matrix and
G
is some multimode Gaussian distribution.
Note that in order to ensure the physicality of the channel
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathbf{S}
and
G
should satisfy some conditions (see
Caruso et al. 2008
).
As an example, a noisy lossy channel (with transmittance
\eta
and thermal environment of
\bar{n}
photons) corresponds to choosing
\mathbf{S}=\sqrt{\eta}\mathbf{I}_{2}
and
{G=\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{(1-\eta)/\eta}}[W_{\bar{n}}]}
where
W_{\bar{n}}=\exp(-(x^{2}+p^{2})/(2\bar{n}+1))/(\pi(2\bar{n}+1))
is the Wigner function of a thermal state.
Similarly, a noisy amplifier corresponds to
\mathbf{S}=\sqrt{g}\mathbf{I}_{2}
and
{G=\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{(g-1)/g}}[W_{\bar{n}}]}
.

It is a simple derivation to show that the rescaling operator adds a constant to the differential entropy of
W
:

\displaystyle h\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W\right]\right]=h[W]+\ln(|\det\mathbf{S}|).

(47)

Since the non-Gaussianity is a difference of two entropies (and since the rescaling acts identically on
W
and its Gaussian associate), it directly follows that the non-Gaussianity measure is invariant under rescaling:

\displaystyle\mu[\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W\right]]=h[\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W\right]]-h[\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{S}}\left[W_{G}\right]]=h[W]-h[W_{G}]=\mu[W].

(48)

As a consequence, the evolution of the non-Gaussianity measure under a Gaussian channel is solely affected by the convolution with the Gaussian distribution.

A.2
Infinitesimal Gaussian convolution

We are now going to show that it is sufficient to prove that the measure is decreasing under an infinitesimal Gaussian convolution.
Indeed, let us observe that any Gaussian distribution
G
(with covariance matrix
\mathbf{G}
) can be decomposed into an arbitrary number of convolutions (assuming
1/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}
):

\displaystyle G=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\ast\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]}_{1/\varepsilon\text{ times}}.

(49)

This is a consequence of the fact that covariance matrices add up under convolution, and that the rescaling operator
\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
applies a Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\mathbf{G}
onto another Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\varepsilon\mathbf{G}
.
As it appears, when
\varepsilon
tends towards zero, the Gaussian distribution
G
can be decomposed into (an infinite number of) convolutions of arbitrarily narrow Gaussian distributions (with covariance matrices
\varepsilon\mathbf{G}
).
Proving that the non-Gaussianity measure increases in the limiting case
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
would then imply that it inceases for any multiple of
\mathbf{\varepsilon}\mathbf{G}
, and in particular for
G
(having covariance matrix
\mathbf{G}
).
The limiting case
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
corresponds to taking the derivative of the measure,
i.e.
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathrm{Re}\mu\left[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\right]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}\leq 0.

(50)

We are going to focus on the latter condition in the following of this Appendix.

In the following, we use the fact the the convolution of two Gaussians is a Gaussian and that covariance matrices add up under convolution.
We use the notation
h_{G}[W]
to denote the entropy of the Gaussian associate of
W
.

\displaystyle\mu[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]

\displaystyle=h_{G}[W_{\ast}\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]-h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]

(51)

\displaystyle=h[W_{G}\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]-h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]

(52)

\displaystyle=N\ln(2\pi e)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\Big(\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\Big)-h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]

(53)

We now take the derivative with respect to
\varepsilon
on both sides.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mu[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ln\Big(\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\Big)-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\right)^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\right)^{-1}\det(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\Tr\left[(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G})\right]-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\left(\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon\mathbf{G}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}\right]-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]

where we have used Jacobi’s formula for the derivative of the determinant of a matrix.
Finally, we need to evaluate that expression in the limit case
\varepsilon=0
, which yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mu[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\right]-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}h[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}[G]]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}.

(54)

A.3
De Bruijn’s identity for Wigner functions

The second term in the RHS of Eq. (
54
) corresponds to the increase of entropy of
W
when it undergoes an infinitesimal Gaussian convolution.
If
W
was a genuine probability distribution, that quantity would be related to the Fisher information through the so-called De Bruijn identity
Stam 1959
.
In this section, we show that De Bruijn’s identity can be extended to Wigner functions, even when they take negative values.
We provide here a concise proof, which closely follows the the usual derivation of De Bruijn’s identity
Cover and Thomas 1999
;
Johnson 2004a
.

Let us first observe that solutions of the heat equation
\partial W/\partial\varepsilon=(1/2)\Delta W
(where
\Delta=\sum_{i}(\partial/\partial r_{i})^{2}
) have the form:

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}=W_{0}\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[N\right],

(55)

where
N
is a standard Gaussian disitribution of covariance matrix
\mathbf{I}
.
Then, we can compute the derivative of
\mathrm{Re}h(W_{\varepsilon})
with respect to
\varepsilon
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathrm{Re}h[W_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\Big(W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\ln|W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})|\Big)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=-\int\Big(\ln|W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})|+1\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int\Big(\ln|W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})|+1\Big)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}^{2}}W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\Big(\ln|W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})|+1\Big)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\right)\right]_{r_{i}=-\infty}^{+\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}\ln|W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})|\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int\left(\frac{1}{W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}W(\mathbf{r})\right)^{2}W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

(56)

Here, we have used integration by parts.
Then, from a similar argument as presented in
Cover and Thomas 1999
, we concluded that the term in square brackets is zero.
The next step of the proof is then to generalize to Gaussian distribution which are non-standard (going from
N
to any Gaussian
G
).
To that purpose, we introduce the Fisher information matrix
\mathbf{J}
, which is defined from
W
as follows:

\displaystyle J_{ij}\equiv\left(\mathbf{J}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{1}{W(\mathbf{r})}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}W(\mathbf{r})\right)\left(\frac{1}{W(\mathbf{r})}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}W(\mathbf{r})\right)W(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(57)

Then, following a similar argument as the one presented in
Johnson 2004a
, we extend the proof to the case of non-standard Gaussian distributions, which gives us:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathrm{Re}h\left[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\right]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{J}\right].

(58)

where
\mathbf{J}
is the Fisher information matrix of
W
and
\mathbf{G}
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian
G
(note that if
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I}
we find Eq. (
56
)).
Eq. (
58
) is precisely the multivariate De Bruijn identity
Costa and Cover 1984
;
Johnson 2004b
, and we have shown here that it holds even for distributions that take negative values (under the condition that we extend usual entropy to the real part of the complex-valued entropy).
Using this relation, we can finally rewrite Eq. (
54
) as:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathrm{Re}\mu\left[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\right]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{V}^{-1}-\mathbf{J})\right].

(59)

If we want the latter expression to be negative for any Gaussian channel (and thus for any covariance matrix
\mathbf{G}
), we need the matrix
(\mathbf{V}^{-1}-\mathbf{J})
to be negative semi-definite.

A.4
A proof for non-negative Wigner functions with the Cramér-Rao bound

In the particular case of Wigner-positive states (having non-negative Wigner functions), we can prove that Eq. (
50
) always holds.
First, we will use the fact that the Fisher information matrix is always positive semi-definite (for genuine probability distributions).
Then, we will use the multivariate Cramér-Rao bound
Bobrovsky et al. 1987
, which states the following:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}\geq\mathbf{J}^{-1},

(60)

and should be understood as
\left(\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{J}^{-1}\right)
being a positive semi-definite matrix.
Then, starting from (
59
) we find:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathrm{Re}\mu\left[W\ast\mathcal{L}_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[G\right]\right]\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{G}\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}-\mathbf{J}\right)\right]

(61)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{J}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{J}\right]

(62)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

(63)

where we have used the fact that the product of positive semi-definite matrices is positive semi-definite, and that the trace of a positive semi-definite matrix is non-negative.

In conclusion, we have proven that if a state has a non-negative Wigner function, then its non-Gaussianity measure
\mathrm{Re}\mu[W]
can only decrease when the state evolves in a Gaussian channel.
However, if the state has a Wigner function that takes negative values, we couldn’t use the Cramér-Rao bound to conclude our proof.

Appendix C
Critical points of
\mu[W]

Let us revise some basic elements of functional derivatives (for a concise overview see also
(
Fredrickson 2005
, Appendix C therein)
). The functional we will consider is of the following integral form,

\displaystyle F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}rg(W(\mathbf{r}))+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}G^{(i)}[W(\mathbf{r})],

(92)

where
g(W(\mathbf{r}))
is not a functional, but rather a form that involves
W(\mathbf{r})
, e.g.,
g(W(\mathbf{r}))=-W(\mathbf{r})\ln|W(\mathbf{r})|
and
G^{(i)}[W(\mathbf{r})]
are functionals representing
K
constraints, i.e., to find the critical points of
F[W(\mathbf{r})]
over normalized Wigner functions the constraint
G^{(1)}[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}rW(\mathbf{r})=1
should be included.

Let us assume that the functionals
G_{i}[W(\mathbf{r})]
are in an integral form and therefore Eq. (
92
) can be written as,

\displaystyle F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}rf(W(\mathbf{r})).

(93)

The functional derivative is defined through the (usual) derivative,

\displaystyle\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}r\frac{df(W(\mathbf{r})+\varepsilon h(\mathbf{r}))}{d\varepsilon}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0},

(94)

where
h(\mathbf{r})
is
any
function. After performing the derivative with respect to
\varepsilon
and taking the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, if Eq. (
94
) assumes the form,

\displaystyle\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}r\frac{df(W(\mathbf{r})+\varepsilon h(\mathbf{r}))}{d\varepsilon}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}=\int d^{2N}rh(\mathbf{r})\frac{\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})},

(95)

i.e., if the function
h(\mathbf{r})
factors out, then the term multiplying
h(\mathbf{r})
, denoted as
\frac{\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})}
, is called the functional derivative of the functional
F[W(\mathbf{r})]
with respect to the function
W(\mathbf{r})
. Otherwise, the functional derivative cannot be defined.

Setting Eq. (
95
) equal to zero, and solving for
W(\mathbf{r})
will give the Wigner functions that serve as critical points of the functional
F[W(\mathbf{r})]
. We have,

\displaystyle\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{2N}r_{A}d^{2M}r_{B}h(\mathbf{r})\frac{\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})}=0\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(96)

where the last step is valid since
h(\mathbf{r})
is any function.
The critical points will depend on
\lambda_{i}
. To identify the
\lambda_{i}
, one takes the solutions of Eq. (
96
), and plugs them into the constraints which are typically given by taking the (usual) derivatives of
F[W(\mathbf{r})]
with respect to each
\lambda_{i}
.

Similarly, we can define the second functional derivative by starting with,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}r\frac{d^{2}f(W(\mathbf{r})+\varepsilon h(\mathbf{r}))}{d\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}.

(97)

If Eq. (
97
) can be written as,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}F[W(\mathbf{r})]=\int d^{2N}r\frac{d^{2}f(W(\mathbf{r})+\varepsilon h(\mathbf{r}))}{d\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}=\int d^{2N}rh^{2}(\mathbf{r})\frac{\delta^{2}F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})^{2}},

(98)

then the second functional derivative exist and is equal to
\frac{\delta^{2}F[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})^{2}}
.

Applying this recipe to Eq. (
34
) (or Eq. (
39

\displaystyle\frac{\delta R[W(\mathbf{r})]}{\delta W(\mathbf{r})}=1+\lambda_{1}+\ln|W(\textbf{r})|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}r_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\Lambda_{ij}r_{i}r_{j}

(99)

Setting Eq. (
99
) equal to zero and using vector-vector and vector-matrix multiplication (for compactness), we get,

\displaystyle|W^{\text{CP}}(\textbf{r})|=\frac{1}{|k|}\exp\left[-\left(\mathbf{r}^{T}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{r}+\bm{\delta}^{T}\mathbf{r}\right)\right],

(100)

where
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is a matrix with elements
\Lambda_{ij}
,
\bm{\delta}
is a vector with elements
\delta_{i}
, and

Equation (

W(\textbf{r})
to the right hand side, or we equate
W(\textbf{r})
to a
partially flipped
version of the exponential, i.e., an

(101)

Using the constraints of Eqs. (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{V}^{-1},

(102)

(103)

\displaystyle|k|

The second functional derivative of Eq. (

Appendix D
Wigner Function of Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) States

The finitely squeezed GKP qubit states
Terhal and Weigand 2016
;
Seshadreesan et al. 2022
are defined in terms of a summation of displaced squeezed vacuum states as follows,

\displaystyle\ket{\bar{L}}=\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-\pi{\Delta}^{2}(2t+\bar{L})^{2}/2)\hat{D}((2t+\bar{L})\sqrt{\pi})\hat{S}(\xi)\ket{\mathrm{0}}

(108)

where
\bar{L}=\{0,1\}
for the two logical qubit states. Let us use
\rho_{\bar{L}}=\ket{\bar{L}}\!\!\bra{\bar{L}}
to represent the density operators for the qubit states. We first calculate the symmetrically ordered characteristic function for
\rho_{\bar{L}}
defined as

\displaystyle\chi_{W}(\eta,\eta^{*})=\Tr\left(\rho_{\bar{L}}e^{-\eta^{*}\hat{a}+\eta\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\right)=\left\langle\hat{D}(\eta)\right\rangle_{\rho_{\bar{L}}}.

(109)

Before we proceed, let us make the following abbreviations

\displaystyle\ket{\bar{L}}\!\!\bra{\bar{L}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\pi{\Delta}^{2}}{2}[(2t+\bar{L})^{2}+(2t^{\prime}+\bar{L})^{2}]}\hat{D}((2t+\bar{L})\sqrt{{\pi}/{2}})\hat{S}(\xi)\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}^{\dagger}((2t^{\prime}+\bar{L})\sqrt{{\pi}/{2}})

(110)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{S}(\xi)\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta)

(111)

with
f(t)=\exp(-\pi{\Delta}^{2}(2t+\bar{L})^{2}/2),\alpha=(2t+\bar{L})\sqrt{\pi/2}\text{ and }\beta=(2t^{\prime}+\bar{L})\sqrt{\pi/2}
. Simplifying
\left\langle\hat{D}(\eta)\right\rangle_{\rho_{\bar{L}}}
yields,

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{D}(\eta)\right\rangle_{\rho_{\bar{L}}}=\Tr\left(\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{S}(\xi)\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta)\hat{D}(\eta)\right)

(112a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{S}(\xi)\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta)\hat{D}(\eta)\right)

(112b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta)\hat{D}(\eta)\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{S}(\xi)\right)

(112c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}(\tau)\hat{S}(\xi)\right)g(\eta,\alpha,\beta)

(112d)

where
\tau\equiv\eta+\alpha-\beta
and since
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
g(\eta,\alpha,\beta)=\exp\left({(\eta-\eta^{*})(\alpha+\beta)}/{2}\right)
. We may further simplify as,

\displaystyle\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{D}(\tau)\hat{S}(\xi)\right)g(\eta,\alpha,\beta)

(113a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{S}^{\dagger}(\xi)\hat{S}(\xi)\hat{D}(\tau^{\prime})\right)g(\eta,\alpha,\beta)

(113b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\;\Tr\left(\ket{\mathrm{0}}\!\!\bra{\mathrm{0}}\hat{D}(\tau^{\prime})\right)g(\eta,\alpha,\beta)

(113c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\exp\left({-(\eta-\eta^{*})(\alpha+\beta)}/{2}\right)\exp\left(-{|\tau^{\prime}|^{2}}/{2}\right)

(113d)

where we made the substitution,
\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cosh|\xi|+\tau^{*}e^{i\theta}\sinh|\xi|
, as a result of reordering
\hat{S}(\xi)
with
\hat{D}(\tau)
. Let us assume that
\xi
is real i.e.
|\xi|=\xi;\theta=0
which gives

\displaystyle|\tau^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\tau|^{2}\left(\cosh^{2}\xi+\sinh^{2}\xi\right)+\sinh\xi\cosh\xi\,(\tau^{2}+\tau^{*2})

(114)

We see the following simplifications

\displaystyle\tau^{2}=\eta^{2}+(\alpha-\beta)^{2}+2\eta(\alpha-\beta),

(115a)

\displaystyle|\tau|^{2}=|\eta|^{2}+(\alpha-\beta)^{2}+(\eta+\eta^{*})(\alpha-\beta).

(115b)

This gives us the final relation,

\displaystyle\chi_{W}(\eta)=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)f(t^{\prime})\exp\biggl(

\displaystyle\frac{(\eta-\eta^{*})(\alpha+\beta)}{2}-\frac{|\eta|^{2}(\cosh^{2}\xi+\sinh^{2}\xi)}{2}-\frac{(\eta^{2}+\eta^{*2})(\cosh\xi\sinh\xi)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(\eta+\eta^{*})(\alpha-\beta)(\cosh\xi+\sinh\xi)^{2}}{2}-\frac{(\alpha-\beta)^{2}(\cosh\xi+\sinh\xi)^{2}}{2}\biggr).

(116)

Subsequently one may use the Fourier transform relation between

and

as follows

\displaystyle W(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int d^{2}\eta\;\chi_{W}(\eta)e^{-\eta\gamma^{*}+\eta^{*}\gamma},

(117)

This Fourier transform kernel simplifies to

and

\displaystyle W_{\ket{\bar{L}}\bra{\bar{L}}}(q,p)|_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left(-p^{2}e^{-2\xi}+i\sqrt{2}p(\alpha-\beta)-e^{2\xi}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\alpha+\beta)+q\biggr)^{2}\right).

(118)

Hence the complete Wigner function may now be expressed as,

\displaystyle W_{\ket{\bar{L}}\bra{\bar{L}}}(q,p)=

\displaystyle\sum_{t,s=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-\frac{\pi{e}^{-2\xi}}{2}[(2s+\bar{L})^{2}+(2t+\bar{L})^{2}]}\exp\left(-p^{2}e^{-2\xi}+i2\sqrt{2}p(t-s)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}-e^{2\xi}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(s+t+2\bar{L})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}+q\biggr)^{2}\right)

(119)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t,s=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-\frac{\pi{\Delta}^{2}}{2}[(2t+\bar{L})^{2}+(2t^{\prime}+\bar{L})^{2}]}\exp\left(-p^{2}\Delta^{2}+i2\sqrt{2}p(t-s)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}-\Delta^{-2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(s+t+2\bar{L})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}+q\biggr)^{2}\right).

(120)

where
\xi=-\ln{\Delta}
. Note that the Wigner function is not properly normalized and must be done so ad-hoc.
