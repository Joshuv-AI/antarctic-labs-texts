Franco Rota

Abstract
. We study the stability manifold of local models of orbifold quotients of elliptic curves. In particular, we show that a region of the stability manifold is a covering space of the regular set of the Tits cone of the associated elliptic root system. The construction requires an explicit description of the McKay correspondence [ 10 ] for A N A_{N} surface singularities and a study of wall-crossing phenomena.

1.
Introduction

The space of stability conditions on a triangulated category
\mathcal{D}
was introduced by Bridgeland in
[
6
]
, following work of Douglas on
\Pi
-stability in string theory
[
11
]
. Bridgeland shows that the set of these stability conditions is a complex manifold
\Stab(\mathcal{D})

[
6
]
, equipped with a local isomorphism

\pi\colon\Stab(\mathcal{D})\to\Hom(K(\mathcal{D}),\mathbb{C}).

The stability manifold
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
is fully understood in the case when
\mathcal{D}
is the derived category of coherent sheaves on a smooth projective curve (see
[
6
]
for the elliptic curve,
[
24
]
for curves of positive genus, and
[
3
]
,
[
28
]
for the projective line). In the case of an elliptic curve, the stability manifold acquires a mirror-symmetric significance, in fact, it can be expressed as a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-bundle over the modular curve
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we show that a similar interpretation is possible for quotients of elliptic curves by a group of automorphisms. We work on surfaces and describe the stability manifold for
\mathcal{D}
a certain triangulated category on a local model of an elliptic quotient. The main result of this paper is Theorem
1.2
, which expresses
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
as a covering space of a subset of
\Hom(K(\mathcal{D}),\mathbb{C})
determined by the data of the quotient. Theorem
1.2
represents an extension of previous results in two directions: on the one hand, it is an analog of the work of Bridgeland and Thomas on Kleinian singularities
[
9
]
,
[
33
]
in the context of simple elliptic singularities. At the same time, it extends Ikeda’s result
[
18
]
on arbitrary root systems of symmetric Kac-Moody Lie algebras to the case of elliptic root systems.

Summary of the results

Let
X
be the orbifold quotient of an elliptic curve
E
by a group of group automorphisms of
E
. The orbifold
X
is a weighted projective line of genus
0
in the sense of Geigle and Lenzing
[
12
]
. We consider its local model; in other words, we embed
X
as the zero section in the total space of its cotangent bundle
Y\coloneqq\Tot(\omega_{X})
, and let
\mathcal{D}
be the triangulated subcategory of
D^{b}(\Coh(Y))
generated by sheaves supported on
X
.

Studying
\mathcal{D}
, rather than
D^{b}(X)
, has two main advantages: the elliptic root system associated with
X
is more evident, and one can use the McKay correspondence to compare the local orbifold to a smooth surface. From this point of view, local orbifold elliptic quotients represent an analog of Kleinian singularities.

The space
\Hom(K(\mathcal{D}),\mathbb{C})
can be given a representation-theoretic interpretation as follows. The bilinear Euler form
\chi\colon K(\mathcal{D})\times K(\mathcal{D})\to\mathbb{Z}
defined as

\chi(E,F)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Hom_{\mathcal{D}}(E,F[i])

is symmetric since
\mathcal{D}
is a K3-category, and
K(\mathcal{D})
is identified with the root lattice of an elliptic root system
R
,
whose bilinear form matches the Euler form. This premise is similar to Bridgeland’s in
[
9
]
, with the difference that
\chi
is only negative semindefinite here. We denote by
a\coloneqq-[\mathcal{O}_{x}]
and
b\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{2}[\omega_{X}^{\otimes i}]
the two classes generating its radical.

The Weyl group
W
on
\Hom(K(\mathcal{D}),\mathbb{C})
acts on the region

\mathbb{E}\coloneqq\left\{Z\in\Hom(K(\mathcal{D}),\mathbb{C})\;|\;Z(a)=1,\im Z(b)>0\right\},

which coincides with the Tits cone of the affine root system
R_{a}=R/\mathbb{Z}a
(Lemma
3.9
).
Let
D
be a fundamental domain for the action of
W
on
\mathbb{E}
. We exhibit a region
U
in the stability manifold which is homeomorphic to
D
(Prop.
4.18
) and lift the action of
W
using a group
\Br(\mathcal{D})
of autoequivalences of
\mathcal{D}
, generated by spherical twists (as defined by Seidel and Thomas
[
31
]
).

A key step in the construction of
U
is the McKay correspondence
[
10
]
: it gives an equivalence of categories between
D^{b}(Y)
and the minimal resolution
Y^{\prime}
of the coarse space of
Y
. In turn, this induces an equivalence between
\mathcal{D}
and the triangulated category
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
generated by sheaves supported on the pull-back of the zero section to
Y^{\prime}
. We define a heart of a bounded t-structure
\mathcal{A}_{R}\subset\mathcal{D}
as the inverse image of
\Coh(Y^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
Then, we use the relation between coherent sheaves on
Y
and perverse sheaves on
Y^{\prime}
(see
[
5
,
37
]
) to explicitly describe
\mathcal{A}_{R}
, classify its objects (Prop.
4.17
), and finally define
U
as the region of
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
containing conditions
(Z,\mathcal{A}_{R})
with
Z\in D
.
Denote by
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
) the connected component of
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
containing
U
.

We will often restrict our attention to the locus of
normalized
stability conditions

\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\{\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})\in\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})\;|\;Z(a)=1\}

rather than the full
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
, and we let
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
be the connected component of
\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})
containing
U
.
Normalization is a natural approach, effective in the study of threefold singularities (see for example
[
34
]
) and fitting with the representation-theoretic definition of
\mathbb{E}
. Moreover, every stability condition in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
is obtained from
\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})
using the natural
\mathbb{C}
-action (see Remark
4.21
).

We show in Prop.
4.20
that the condition

(*)\colon\quad\im\frac{Z(b)}{Z(a)}>0

is automatic for all stability conditions in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
(and hence in
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
), and therefore
\pi
maps
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
to
\mathbb{E}
.
The proof requires to understand wall-crossing for some specific classes in
K(\mathcal{D})
, which we do in Section
5
. Our wall-crossing result can be viewed as a local analog of the classification of indecomposable sheaves on
X
by Lenzing and Meltzer
[
23
, Theor. 4.6]
:

Theorem 1.1

( =
5.4
)
.

Let
\alpha
be a root in the elliptic root lattice
K(\mathcal{D})
, and let
\sigma\in\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
be generic with respect to
\alpha
. Then, there exists a
\sigma
-stable object

of class

is rigid if

The image of

This leads to the main result of this paper (analogous to

( =

There is a covering map

and the group

acts as group of deck transformations.

Let

There is an isomorphism

Where

Remarks and further problems

Remark 1.4

(Representation theory)
.

From the point of view of representation theory, the categories
\mathcal{D}
discussed here are equivalent to the CY-2 completions of Ringel’s canonical algebras (see
[
32
]
).

Remark 1.5

(Mirror symmetry)
.

Theorem
1.2
can be interpreted as an istance of the same principle outlined in
[
8
]
for elliptic curves.

The general automorphism group of an elliptic curve
E
is
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, generated by the involution
\iota
.
Over the field of complex numbers, there are only two possibilities for special automorphism groups, namely
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
. These give rise to three possible quotients:
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{3,3,3}
,
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{4,4,2}
and
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{6,3,2}
, whose mirror partners are the
simple elliptic singularities

E_{6}^{(1,1)},E_{7}^{(1,1)}
, and
E_{8}^{(1,1)}

[
22
]
,
[
27
]
. To these singularities, Saito associates a
universal unfolding space
and an elliptic root system
[
29
]
. If
X
is one of these quotients, a hyperbolic extension of
\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}/W
is the universal unfolding of the mirror elliptic singularity. Thus, Theorem
1.2
details the relation between the unfolding spaces and the stability manifold and gives a partial answer to Conjecture 1.3 in
[
32
]
.

The automorphism group of a general elliptic curve
E
is generated by its involution
\iota
. Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
hold for
X=[E/\iota]
, however, a mirror-symmetric interpretation seems less clear in this case.

As in
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
, we expect the following properties:

Conjecture 1.6
.

(i)

The space
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
is connected, so that
\Stab(\mathcal{D})=\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
;

(ii)

the space
\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})
is simply connected. This would also show that the Artin group
G_{W}\simeq\pi_{1}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}/W)
(see Proposition
3.14
) is isomorphic to
\Br(\mathcal{D})
.

See
[
18
]
and references therein for progress on Conjecture
1.6
in related frameworks.

2.
Stability conditions

Stability conditions on triangulated categories were first introduced by Bridgeland and were inspired by work of Douglas on string theory (see
[
6
]
and references therein). We recall here the definition and basic properties of stability conditions and the stability manifold. We refer the interested reader to the seminal work of Bridgeland
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and to the surveys
[
16
]
,
[
25
]
.

In what follows,
\mathbb{T}
is a triangulated category, with Grothendieck group
K(\mathbb{T})
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
slicing
of
\mathbb{T}
is a collection
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{P}(\phi)\}_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}
of full additive subcategories of
\mathbb{T}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

\Hom(\mathcal{P}(\phi_{1}),\mathcal{P}(\phi_{2}))=0
for
\phi_{1}<\phi_{2}
;

(ii)

for all
E\in\mathbb{T}
there are real numbers
\phi_{1}>...>\phi_{m}
, objects
E_{i}\in\mathbb{T}
and a collection of triangles

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0=E_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces...}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{m-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{m}=E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{m}}

where
A_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\phi_{i})
;

(iii)

\mathcal{P}(\phi)[1]=\mathcal{P}(\phi+1)
.

The extremes
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{m}
are denoted
\phi^{+}(E)
and
\phi^{-}(E)
respectively. Given a slicing
\mathcal{P}
, for
\alpha\leq\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we denote by
\mathcal{P}((\alpha,\beta))
the extension closure of the subcategories
\{\mathcal{P}(\phi)\,\colon\,\phi\in(\alpha,\beta)\}
(similar definitions work for other intervals in
\mathbb{R}
).

Definition 2.2
.

A
stability condition
on
\mathbb{T}
is a pair
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})
where:

(i)

\mathcal{P}
is a slicing of
\mathbb{T}
;

(ii)

Z\colon K(\mathbb{T})\to\mathbb{C}
is an additive homomorphism called the
central charge
;

and they satisfy the following properties:

(1)

For any non-zero
E\in\mathcal{P}(\phi)
,

Z([E])\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cdot e^{i\pi\phi};

(2)

(Support property) Fix any norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
on
K(\mathbb{T})
. Then we require

\inf\left\{\dfrac{\lvert Z([E])\rvert}{\lVert[E]\rVert}\,\colon\,0\neq E\in\mathcal{P}(\phi),\,\phi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}>0.

Given a stability condition
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})
, we’ll refer to
\mathcal{P}((0,1])
as to the
heart
associated to
\sigma
. In fact,
\mathcal{P}((\alpha,\alpha+1])
is always the heart of a bounded
t
-structure for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and it’s an abelian category.

If
E\in\mathcal{P}((\alpha,\alpha+1])
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then we say that
E
has
phase

\phi
if
Z([E])\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cdot e^{i\pi\phi}
, for
\phi\in(\alpha,\alpha+1]
. The nonzero objects of
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
are said to be
\sigma
-semistable
of phase
\phi
, and the simple objects of
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
are said to be
\sigma
-stable
.

For the general theory about bounded
t
-structures, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
, here we only recall the following lemma, which will be useful in what follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{T}
be hearts of bounded t-structures on a triangulated category
\mathbb{T}
. If
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}
,
then
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

This is
[
25
, Ex. 5.6]
.

Remark 2.4

(
[
6
, Prop. 5.3]
)
.

To construct stability conditions it is often convenient to use an alternative definition. In fact, sometimes we will write a stability condition as a pair
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{A})
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the heart of a bounded
t
-structure and
Z
is a
stability function
satisfying Harder-Narasimhan and support property. A stability function is a linear map
Z\colon K(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{C}
such that any non-zero
E\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies
Z([E])\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cdot e^{i\pi\phi}
with
\phi\in(0,1]
. Then one defines
\phi
to be the phase of
E
, and declares
E
to be
\sigma
-(semi)stable if for all non-zero subobjects
F\in\mathcal{A}
of
E
,
\phi(F)<(\leq)\phi(E)
. We say that
Z
satisfies the HN property if for every
E\in\mathcal{A}
there is a unique filtration

0=E_{0}\subset E_{1}\subset...\subset E_{n-1}\subset E_{n}=E

such that the quotients
E_{i}/E_{i-1}
are
\sigma
-semistable of phases
\phi_{i}=\phi(E_{i}/E_{i-1})
,
\phi_{1}>\phi_{2}>...>\phi_{n}
. The support property is the same as in Definition
2.2
.
To recover a slicing as in Definition
2.2
, set
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
to be the category of
\sigma
-semistable objects of phase
\phi
for
\phi\in(0,1]
, and declare
\mathcal{P}(\phi)=\mathcal{P}(\phi+n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The following proposition is a useful tool to check the Harder-Narasimhan property:

Proposition 2.5

(
[
25
, Prop. 4.10]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is an abelian category, and
Z\colon K(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{C}
is a stability function. If

(i)

the category
\mathcal{A}
is noetherian, and

(ii)

the image of
\im Z
is discrete in
\mathbb{R}
,

then
Z
has the Harder-Narasimhan property.

2.1.
The Stability manifold

Let
\Stab(\mathbb{T})
denote the set of stability conditions on
\mathbb{T}
. In
[
6
, Sec. 6]
, Bridgeland shows that the function

(1)

f(\sigma,\tau)=\sup_{0\neq E\in\mathbb{T}}\{\lvert\phi^{+}_{\sigma}(E)-\phi^{+}_{\tau}(E)\rvert,\lvert\phi^{-}_{\sigma}(E)-\phi^{-}_{\tau}(E)\rvert\}

determines a generalized metric on
\Stab(\mathbb{T})
which makes it into a topological space. Moreover, the central charge map
\pi\colon\Stab(\mathbb{T})\to\Hom(K(\mathbb{T}),\mathbb{C})
given by
(Z,\mathcal{P})\mapsto Z
is a local homeomorphism, and it makes
\Stab(\mathbb{T})
into a complex manifold of dimension
\rk(K(\mathbb{T}))
[
6
, Thm. 1.2]
.
The following lemma which will be useful later:

Lemma 2.6

(
[
6
, Lemma 6.4]
)
.

Let
\sigma
,
\tau\in\Stab(\mathbb{T})
be stability conditions with
\pi(\sigma)=\pi(\tau)
. If
f(\sigma,\tau)<1
, then
\sigma=\tau
.

Next, we recall two group actions on
\Stab(\mathbb{T})
. The additive group
\mathbb{C}
acts as follows: for
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
, define
z\cdot(Z,\mathcal{P})=(Z^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
, with

(2)

Z^{\prime}(-)=e^{-z}Z(-),\qquad\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\phi)=\mathcal{P}\left(\phi+\frac{y}{\pi}\right).

The group of autoequivalences
\Aut(\mathbb{T})
also acts on
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
: for
\Phi\in\Aut(\mathcal{D})
and
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})\in\Stab(\mathcal{D})
, define
\Phi\cdot(Z,\mathcal{P})=(Z^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
as the stability condition with

(3)

Z^{\prime}(E)\coloneqq Z(\Phi^{-1}(E))\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(\phi)\coloneqq\Phi\mathcal{P}(\phi).

2.2.
Torsion pairs and tilts of abelian categories

Next, we recall the definition of a
tilt
of an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
, which is a technique to produce new abelian subcategories of
D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
. Indeed, the tilt of a heart of a bounded t-structure is a new heart in
D^{b}(\mathcal{A})

[
13
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category. A
torsion pair
for
\mathcal{A}
is a pair of full subcategories
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
such that:

(i)

\text{Hom}\left(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F}\right)=0
;

(ii)

for any
E\in\mathcal{A}
there exists a short exact sequence

0\to T\to E\to F\to 0

where
T\in\mathcal{T}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

Given a torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
on an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
, we define
\mathcal{A}^{\sharp}=\langle\mathcal{F}[1],\mathcal{T}\rangle
to be the
extension closure
of
\mathcal{F}[1]
and
\mathcal{T}
, i.e. smallest full subcategory of
D^{b}(\mathcal{A})
containing
\mathcal{F}[1]
and
\mathcal{T}
closed under extensions.
\mathcal{A}^{\sharp}
is called the
tilt
of
\mathcal{A}
along the torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
. Sometimes we will also refer to
\mathcal{A}^{\sharp}[-1]=\langle\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T}[-1]\rangle
as to the tilt, but no confusion should arise.

3.
Elliptic root systems

In this section we introduce elliptic root systems and recall some of their properties. Elliptic root systems were introduced by Saito
[
29
,
30
]
, in our exposition we draw also from
[
32
]
and
[
19
]
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
29
, Def. 1]
)
.

Let
F
be a real vector space of rank
l+2
, equipped with a positive semidefinite symmetric bilinear form
I\colon F\times F\to F
, whose radical
\rad I
has rank 2. An
elliptic root system
associated to
(F,I)
is a subset
R\subset F
of non-isotropic elements such that:

(1)

the additive group generated by
R
, denoted
Q(R)
, is a full sublattice of
F
. That is, the embedding
Q(R)\subset F
induces an isomorphism
Q(R)_{\mathbb{R}}\simeq F
;

(2)

the form
I
takes integer values on
R\times R
;

(3)

For all
\alpha
in
R
, the reflection

w_{\alpha}(x)=x-I(x,\alpha)\alpha\,\text{ for }\,x\in F

satisfies
w_{\alpha}(R)=R
;

(4)

if
R=R_{1}\cup R_{2}
with
R_{1}\perp R_{2}
, then either
R_{1}
or
R_{2}
is empty.

The subgroup
W
of
\Aut(F,I)
generated by the
w_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in R
is called the
Weyl group
of the root system
R
. The lattice
\rad I\cap Q(R)
is full in the two-dimensional vector space
\rad I
. A
marking
of
R
is the choice of a 1-dimensional subspace
G\subset\rad I
, and
(R,G)
is called a
marked elliptic root system
.

To a marked elliptic root system we can associate an affine root system
R_{a}
and a finite root system
R_{f}
of rank
l
by considering the quotients

\displaystyle F_{a}\coloneqq F/G

\displaystyle R_{a}\coloneqq R/R\cap G

\displaystyle F_{f}\coloneqq F/\rad I

\displaystyle R_{f}\coloneqq R/R\cap\rad I

and the bilinear forms induced on
F_{f}
and
F_{a}
by
I
.

Now fix a marked root system
(R,G)
, with generators
a,b
for
\rad I\cap Q(R)
and
G=\mathbb{R}a
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
19
, Cor. 2.3]
)
.

The root system

(ii)

\Phi_{S}(S)\simeq S[-1]

for any spherical object

Lemma 4.12

Lemma 4.13

(see
[
36
, Lemma 3.1]
)

If there is a series of inclusions in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, say

...\subset M_{k}\subset M_{k-1}\subset...\subset M_{0}

whose quotients lie in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, then the sequence must eventually stabilize.

Proof.

We follow an argument similar to
[
36
, Lemma 3.1]
.
First, we may assume that all the quotients
F_{k}\coloneqq M_{0}/M_{k}
are supported on one curve
C\coloneqq C_{i}
. Moreover:

Claim
.

We may assume that for all
k
, the quotients

are torsion free sheaves

, such that

has connected support
D_{k}\subset C
.

Indeed, by definition of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
every

admits a surjection to some

. By restricting
L_{k}
to one of the connected components

of its support, we may assume that
L_{k}
has connected support.
So we have quotients

F_{k}\twoheadrightarrow L_{k}

which define exact sequences

0\to M_{k}^{(1)}\to M_{k}\to L_{k}\to 0.

The quotient
F_{k}^{(1)}
of
M_{k+1}\to M_{k}^{(1)}
fits into an exact sequence

where
\textch_{{1}}{(F_{k}^{(1)})}=\textch_{{1}}{(F_{k})}-\textch_{{1}}{(L_{k})}
is a positive linear combination
\sum a_{j}[C_{i,j}]
with coefficients strictly smaller than those of
\textch_{{1}}{(F_{k})}
. We can then repeat this process for the map

until we get a finite chain of inclusions

satisfying the statement of the claim.

We proceed to show that the sequence of inclusions must terminate with an induction on the length
l
of the chain of rational curves

In order to see this, apply the functor
\Hom(-,\mathcal{O}_{C}(C))
to the short exact sequence

(11)

For

, one computes

\Hom(M_{i},\mathcal{O}_{C}(C))>\Hom(M_{i+1},\mathcal{O}_{C}(C))

If

, one has

(12)

\begin{split}\hom(M_{k},\mathcal{O}_{C}(C))-\hom(M_{k+1},\mathcal{O}_{C}(C))=\\

For the second statement, fix a point

away from the intersections, and consider the cokernel

(\epsilon)\colon\quad\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-2)\to L_{D}\to R_{D}.

From the sequence

\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-2)\to\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-1)\to\mathcal{O}_{t}

one sees that
\Ext^{1}(R_{D},\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-2))\simeq\Ext^{1}(R_{D},\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-1))
because
t\notin\text{Supp}R_{D}
. Pushing forward the extension class
(\epsilon)
to
\Ext^{1}(R_{D},\mathcal{O}_{C_{d_{1}}}(-1))
produces an object
L_{D}^{\prime}
as in the statement.
∎

Lemma 4.16
.

Suppose an object
T\in\mathcal{A}_{R}
is supported on an orbifold point
p_{i}
. Then
T
is obtained by repeated extensions of the following objects:

(i)

t_{i}^{j}
with
j\neq 0
;

(ii)

clusters supported at
p_{i}
;

(iii)

N[-1]
where
N
is the proper quotient of a cluster.

Proof.

This is equivalent to classifying sheaves of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
supported on
C\coloneqq C_{i}
. First, we consider sheaves in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. A sheaf in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is an extension of subsheaves
L\subset\mathcal{O}_{C}(C)
with connected support. Any such inclusion must factor thorugh an inclusion
L\subseteq L_{D}
, where
L_{D}
is as in Lemma
4.15
and the cokernel
L_{D}/L
is torsion. We have that
\Psi(L)[1]
and
\Psi(L_{D})[1]
are sheaves on
X
, so applying the McKay functor to

L\to L_{D}\to L_{D}/L

we obtain a short exact sequence of sheaves in
\mathcal{B}
:

M\to\Psi(L)[1]\to\Psi(L_{D})[1],

where
M
is obtained by repeated extensions of clusters. Now we claim that
\Psi(L_{D})[1]
is a proper quotient of a cluster. In fact, apply
\Psi
to the exact sequence (
15
) of Lemma
4.15
:
\Psi(\mathcal{O}_{t})
is a cluster, and
\Psi(L^{\prime}_{D})
is a sheaf obtained by repeated extensions of
t_{i}^{j}
,
j\neq 0
. This yields a short exact sequence in
\mathcal{B}

0\to\Psi(L^{\prime}_{D})\to\Psi(\mathcal{O}_{t})\to\Psi(L_{D})[1]\to 0

which exhibits
\Psi(L_{D})[1]
as the quotient of a cluster. This exhausts part (
iii
).

Now, consider a sheaf
B\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. The torsion part
B_{tor}
of
B
is obtained by repeated extensions of points, so
\Psi(B_{tor})
is as in part (
ii
). We may then assume that
B
is torsion free with connected support. If
B
is supported on a single irreducible component
C_{i}
, then
B
is a sum of line bundles of the form
\mathcal{O}_{C_{i}}(k)
. Since
\Hom(B,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})=0
, we must have
k>-2
. Then
\Psi(B)
is obtained as an extension of
t_{i}^{j}
by clusters.
If
B
is supported on more than one irreducible component, suppose that
C_{j}
is a terminal component of the support of
B
and consider the restriction of
B
to
C_{j}
. Then there is an exact sequence

B^{\prime}\to B\to B_{|C_{j}}

where
B^{\prime}
is supported on a shorter chain.
B_{|C_{j}}
is supported on one irreducible curve, so it is as above. If
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, we repeat this procedure. Otherwise,
B^{\prime}
fits in a short exact sequence of sheaves

B^{\prime\prime}\to B^{\prime}\to F

with
B^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Sheaves in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are classified above, so we can assume that
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and conclude by induction on the length of the supporting chain.
∎

As a consequence of the results in this section, we obtain the following description of objects in
\mathcal{A}_{R}
:

Proposition 4.17
.

Objects in
\mathcal{A}_{R}
are obtained by repeated extensions from:

(i)

line bundles on
X
;

(ii)

skyscraper sheaves

for

;

(iii)

objects supported on the

’s, classified in Lemma

.

4.5.
The fundamental region and normalization

Recall the notation introduced in Section

. In this section, we use the heart

to construct a region

.

For every point

Proof.

Pick

shows that

∎

We observe right away the following Lemma:

Lemma 4.19
.

Let

. Then, all

,

∎

5.
Wall-crossing in
\mathcal{D}

In this section, we apply the wall-crossing methods of
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
to the
K3
-category
\mathcal{D}
. First, we produce stable objects for a certain stability condition in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
. We then analyze wall crossing for spherical and radical classes, obtaining Theorem
5.4
. From it, we obtain a proof of Proposition
4.20
and of the claims of Remark

. The results of this section hold if one works with normalized stability conditions with the same arguments, so we do not repeat them. The notation is as above.

5.1.
Stability conditions on
\Coh(X)
and
\mathcal{B}

Geigle and Lenzing define slope-stability on a weighted projective line in
[
12
, Sec. 5]
. Define a stability condition
\tau_{0}^{\prime}\coloneqq(Z_{0},\Coh(X))\in\Stab(X)
with

Z_{0}=-\deg+i\rk,

where
\deg(\mathcal{O}_{p_{i}}\otimes\chi^{j})
is defined to be
\frac{1}{a_{i}}
for all orbifold points
p_{i}
and all
j=0,...,a_{i}-1
. Then, slope-stability is equivalent to
\tau_{0}^{\prime}
-stability on
X
. We say that a root
\alpha\in R\cup\Delta_{im}
is
positive
if
Z_{0}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}_{<0}
. Results about
\tau_{0}^{\prime}
-stability are summarized in
[
23
]
:

Theorem 5.1

(
[
23
, Theor. 4.6]
)
.

Let
X
be as above,
\alpha\in R\cup\Delta_{im}
. Then:

(i)

there exists an indecomposable sheaf
F
of class
\alpha
if and only if
\alpha
is a positive root;

(ii)

the sheaf
F
is unique up to isomorphism if
\alpha
is a real root, and varies in a one-parameter family if
\alpha
is imaginary;

(iii)

an indecomposable sheaf is
\tau_{0}^{\prime}
-semistable, and it is
\tau_{0}^{\prime}
-stable if and only if
\alpha
is primitive.

By Lemma
4.2
, we can regard
Z_{0}
as a map defined on
K(\mathcal{D})
, and define a stability condition
\tau_{0}\in\Stab(\mathcal{D})
as
(Z_{0},\mathcal{B})
. By construction,
\tau_{0}
lies in the boundary of a fundamental chamber in
\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
(for example because
\im Z_{0}(t_{i}^{j})=0
for all
i,j
).

We say that an object
E\in\mathcal{D}
is
semi-rigid
if
\ext^{1}(E,E)=2
. Then we have:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in R\cup\Delta_{im}
be a positive root. If
\alpha
is a real root, there exist a
\tau_{0}
-semistable spherical sheaf in
\mathcal{B}
of class
\alpha
. If
\alpha
is imaginary, there is a one-parameter family of semi-rigid
\tau_{0}
-semistable sheaves in
\mathcal{B}
of class
\alpha
. If
\alpha
is primitive, the same statement holds with stability instead of semistability.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, there exists a
\tau_{0}^{\prime}
-semistable sheaf
E^{\prime}
on
X
of class
\alpha
. Let
E\coloneqq\iota_{*}(E^{\prime})
be the indecomposable sheaf in
\mathcal{B}
obtained by pushing forward
E^{\prime}
. The sheaf
E
is
\tau_{0}
-semistable: since
E
is supported on
X
then so must be every subsheaf
S\subset E
. This implies that
S=\iota_{*}S^{\prime}
for some
S^{\prime}\in\Coh(X)
. Then,
S
destabilizes
E
if and only if
S^{\prime}
destabilizes
E^{\prime}
.

Next, we show that
E
is spherical if
\alpha
is a real root. Deformations of
E^{\prime}
are governed by the group
\Ext^{1}_{X}(E^{\prime},E^{\prime})
, so Theorem
5.1
implies that
\Ext^{1}_{X}(E^{\prime},E^{\prime})=0
, hence
\Ext^{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(E,E)=0
by Lemma
4.1
. On the other hand, since
\alpha
is real one must have
\chi(\alpha,\alpha)=2
, so
E
is spherical. Similarly, one argues that
E
is semi-rigid if
\alpha
is imaginary. The claim about stability follows again from Theorem
5.1
.
∎

5.2.
Wall-crossing in
\Stab(\mathcal{D})

The lattice
K(\mathcal{D})
can be equipped with the Mukai pairing

(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\coloneqq-\chi(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}).

The pairing has a rank 2 radical
\rad\chi
generated by
a
and
b
, and it induces a negative definite pairing on
K(\mathcal{D})/\rad\chi
, since the Euler form on
K(\mathcal{D})/\rad\chi
coincides with the Cartan matrix of the root system
R_{f}
, which is positive definite.

Since
K(\mathcal{D})
is negative semidefinite, the class
\mathbf{v}
of a stable object can only satisfy
\mathbf{v}^{2}=0
or
\mathbf{v}^{2}=-2
. In the first case,
\mathbf{v}
belongs to
\rad\chi
, and we call it a
radical class
. Classes with
\mathbf{v}^{2}=-2
are called
spherical classes
.

First, notice that since
K(\mathcal{D})
is a discrete lattice, we have a finiteness result for walls:

Proposition 5.3

(
[
1
, Prop. 3.3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a triangulated category such that
K(\mathcal{D})
is a lattice of finite rank. Let
\Stab^{*}(\mathcal{D})\subset\Stab(\mathcal{D})
be a connected component of its space of stability conditions. Fix a primitive class
\mathbf{v}\in K(\mathcal{D})
, and an arbitrary set
S\subset D
of objects of class
\mathbf{v}
. Then there exists a collection of walls
W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
with
\mathbf{w}\in K(\mathcal{D})
, with the following properties:

(a)

Every wall
W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
is a closed submanifold with boundary of real codimension one;

(b)

The collection
W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
is locally finite (i.e., every compact subset
K\subset\Stab^{*}(\mathcal{D})
intersects only a finite number of walls);

(c)

For every stability condition
(Z,\mathcal{P})\in W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
, there exists a phase
\phi
and an inclusion
F_{\mathbf{w}}\to E_{\mathbf{v}}
in
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
with
[F_{\mathbf{w}}]=\mathbf{w}
and some
E_{\mathbf{v}}\in S
;

(d)

If
\mathcal{C}\subset\Stab^{*}(\mathcal{D})
is a connected component of the complement of
\cup_{\mathbf{w}\in K(\mathcal{D})}W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
, and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
, then an object
E_{\mathbf{v}}\in S
is
\sigma_{1}
-stable
if and only if it is
\sigma_{2}
-stable.

Recall that
\sigma\in\Stab(\mathcal{D})
is said to be
generic
with respect to
\mathbf{v}\in K(\mathcal{D})
if
\sigma
does not lie on any of the walls of the wall-and-chamber decomposition associated to
\mathbf{v}
. The goal of this section is to prove the following Theorem:

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\alpha\in R\subset K(\mathcal{D})
be a positive root. Let
\sigma\in\Stab^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
be generic with respect to
\alpha
. Then, there exists a
\sigma
-stable object
E
of class
\alpha
. The object
E
is rigid if
\alpha
is a real root, and it varies in a family if
\alpha
is imaginary.

We will make use of the following well-known property of K3-categories.

Lemma 5.5

(
[
17
, Prop. 2.9]
)
.

Let
\sigma\in\Stab(\mathcal{D})
.

(i)

If
E\in\mathcal{D}
is spherical, then all of its
\sigma
-stable factors are spherical;

(ii)

if
E\in\mathcal{D}
is semi-rigid, then all of its
\sigma
-stable factors are spherical, except for possibly one semi-rigid factor.

Before moving forward, we recall a construction from
[
2
]
.
Fix a primitive class
\mathbf{v}\in K(\mathcal{D})
, let
S
be the set of objects of
\mathcal{D}
of class
\mathbf{v}
, and let
W=W^{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
be a wall of the wall-and-chamber decomposition of
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
associated to
\mathbf{v}
. Then we can associate to
W
the rank 2 lattice
H_{W}\subset K(\mathcal{D})
:

(18)

H_{W}=\left\{\mathbf{w}\in K(\mathcal{D})\mid\im\frac{Z(\mathbf{v})}{Z(\mathbf{w})}=0\mbox{ for all }\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})\in W\right\}.

The rank of
H_{W}
is at least 2 because it contains at least
\mathbf{v}
and the linearly independent class
\mathbf{w}
destabilizing at
W
. If it had rank bigger than 2, the definition (
18
) would imply that
W
has codimension higher than 1.

For any
\sigma=(Z,\mathcal{P})\in W
, let
C_{\sigma}\subset H_{W}\otimes\mathbb{R}
be the cone spanned by classes
\mathbf{c}
satisfying

\mathbf{c}^{2}\geq-2\quad\mbox{and}\quad\im\frac{Z(\mathbf{c})}{Z(\mathbf{v})}>0.

We will refer to
C_{\sigma}
as to the
cone of
\sigma
-effective classes
in
H_{W}
.

5.2.1.
Wall-crossing for spherical classes

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a primitive spherical class in
K(\mathcal{D})
, and
W
be a wall for
\mathbf{v}
. Then
H_{W}
is a primitive lattice of rank two generated by
\mathbf{v}
and a spherical class
\mathbf{w}
. It is negative definite (with respect to the restriction of the Mukai pairing). Moreover, there are only three possibilities for the intersection form, and:

(i)

if
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=0
, then
H_{W}
contains no spherical classes except for
\pm\mathbf{v}
and
\pm\mathbf{w}
;

(ii)

if
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=-1
, the only spherical classes in
H_{W}
are
\pm\mathbf{v}
,
\pm\mathbf{w}
, and
\pm(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})
;

(iii)

if
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=1
, the only spherical classes in
H_{W}
are
\pm\mathbf{v}
,
\pm\mathbf{w}
, and
\pm(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})
.

Proof.

We have that
\mathbf{v}\in H_{W}
has
\mathbf{v}^{2}<0
and
\mathbf{w}
must be a spherical class by Lemma.
5.5
. So both
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
project to non-zero vectors in
K(\mathcal{D})/\rad\chi
. The intersection matrix of
H_{W}
can be computed on
K(\mathcal{D})/\rad\chi
, where the Mukai pairing coincides with the opposite of the Cartan intersection matrix, so it is negative definite.

The signature of the form implies that the determinant of the intersection form be positive, which rules out all values of
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})
except for
0
and
\pm 1
. The spherical classes are the integer solutions of

-2=(x\mathbf{v}+y\mathbf{w})^{2}=-2x^{2}-2y^{2}+2(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})xy

in these three cases.
∎

Let
W
be a wall for
\mathbf{v}
. Then, we denote by
\sigma_{0}
a stability condition which only lies on the wall
W
, and consider a path in
\Stab(\mathcal{D})
passing through
\sigma_{0}
and connecting
\sigma^{+}
and
\sigma^{-}
, two stability conditions lying in adjacent chambers.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
W
as above, suppose that there exists an indecomposable
\sigma_{0}
-semistable spherical object
E
of class
\mathbf{v}
. Then there is a
\sigma^{+}
-stable spherical object
E^{+}
of class
\mathbf{v}
. Likewise, there exist a
\sigma^{-}
-stable object
E^{-}
of class
\mathbf{v}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
, the Jordan-Hölder factors of
E
are spherical objects. In other words,
\mathbf{v}
can be written as a sum of spherical classes in
C_{\sigma_{0}}
. If
E
is
\sigma_{0}
-stable, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, Lemma
5.6
shows that, up to the sign of
\mathbf{w}
,
E
has a Jordan-Hölder filtration

B\to E\to A

where
B
,
A
have class
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}
, respectively.
Observe that
\Ext^{1}(A,B)=\Ext^{1}(B,A)\neq 0
since
E
is indecomposable, and denote by
E^{\prime}
the non-trivial extension

A\to E^{\prime}\to B.

If
\phi_{\sigma^{+}}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})>\phi_{\sigma^{+}}(\mathbf{w})
set
E^{+}=E
. If
\phi_{\sigma^{+}}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})<\phi_{\sigma^{+}}(\mathbf{w})
, set
E^{+}=E^{\prime}
.
In any case,
E^{+}
satisfies the assumptions of
[
2
, Lemma 9.3]
, and hence is
\sigma^{+}
-stable.
∎

5.2.2.
Wall-crossing for radical classes

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a primitive radical class in
K(\mathcal{D})
, and
W
be a wall for
\mathbf{v}
. Then
H_{W}
contains a spherical class
\mathbf{w}
and the intersection matrix of
H_{W}
is

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-2\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Another generator of
H_{W}
,
\mathbf{w}
, is either radical or semi-rigid by Lemma
5.5
. If it is semirigid,
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w})=0
, so the intersection form is zero on
H_{W}
and
H_{W}
contains no spherical classes.
Then every
\sigma_{0}
-semistable object
E
of class
\mathbf{v}
must be stable on
W
, because it can only have one Jordan-Hölder factor, so
W
is not a wall.
The only other possibility is that
\bf w
is spherical and the intersection form is as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

For
W
as above, suppose that there exists an indecomposable
\sigma_{0}
-semistable semi-rigid object
E
of class
\mathbf{v}
. Then there is a
\sigma^{+}
-stable semi-rigid object
E^{+}
of class
\mathbf{v}
. Likewise, there exist a
\sigma^{-}
-stable semi-rigid object
E^{-}
of class
\mathbf{v}
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of Lemma
5.7
. If
E
is
\sigma_{0}
-stable there is nothing to prove, otherwise it must have at least a spherical stable factor. Then one can write
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}
with
\mathbf{a}\in C_{\sigma_{0}}
spherical, and
\mathbf{b}\in C_{\sigma_{0}}
. By Lemma
5.8
, the only spherical classes in
H
are of the form
\pm\mathbf{w}+n\mathbf{v}
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
; then
\mathbf{b}
has to be spherical as well, and there is only one integer
N
such that

fixes
\mathcal{O}_{C_{i,1}}(-1)=\Psi^{-1}t_{i}^{1}
. Let
M_{p}
denote the cluster corresponding to
p
. Then
\Phi
fixes
M_{p}
because
\Phi^{\prime}
fixes
\mathcal{O}_{p}
. Moreover,
M_{p}
has a unique composition series by the
t_{i}^{j}
, which are all fixed by
\Phi
except possibly
t_{i}^{0}
. Then
\Phi
must also fix
t_{i}^{0}
for
i=1,...,r
.

Then, since every cluster has a composition series with factors the simple sheaves
t_{i}^{j}
and
\Phi
fixes the
t_{i}^{j}
for all
j=0,...,a_{i}-1
, it must also send any cluster to a cluster. In other words,
\Phi^{\prime}
sends skyscraper sheaves of points on any exceptional curve
C_{i}
to skyscraper sheaves.

Once can then apply
[
15
, Cor. 5.23]
, which implies that there exists an automorphism
\phi
of
Y^{\prime}
such that
\Phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{O}_{t})\simeq\mathcal{O}_{\phi(t)}
and
\Phi^{\prime}\simeq(-\otimes\mathcal{L})\circ\phi_{*}
for some line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
Y^{\prime}
. The automorphism
\phi
is the identity, because it is the identity on the dense open complement of
X^{\prime}
. The Picard group of
\Tot(\omega_{X})
is isomorphic to
\Pic(X)\bigoplus(\oplus\mathbb{Z}\{C_{i,j}\})
hence the only line bundle fixing the
\Psi^{-1}(S)
with
S\in\Pi
is the trivial one. Then,
\Phi^{\prime}\simeq\id
as we wished to prove.
∎

6.2.
Proof of main results

Denote by
\bar{\pi}
the composition of the maps
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}\to\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}/W
. Then we have:

Theorem 6.10
.

The map

\bar{\pi}\colon\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})\to\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}/W

is a covering map, and the group
\Br(\mathcal{D})
acts as group of deck transformations.

Proof.

We only need to show that the quotient of
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
by
\Br(\mathcal{D})
coincides with
\mathsf{X}_{\mathrm{reg}}/W
. Equivalently, for every pair of stability conditions
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
satisfying
\bar{\pi}(\sigma_{1})=\bar{\pi}(\sigma_{2})
, we need to exhibit an element
\Phi\in\Br(\mathcal{D})
such that
\sigma_{1}=\Phi\cdot\sigma_{2}
.

By Corollary
6.5
, it is enough to show this when
\sigma_{1}\in U
. Moreover, there exists
\Phi\in\Br(\mathcal{D})
such that
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\sigma_{2}
lies in
U
. Then we have

\pi(\sigma_{2}^{\prime})=[\Phi]\cdot\pi(\sigma_{2})=[\Phi]\cdot\pi(\sigma_{1})

in
D
. Since
U
and
D
are homeomorphic, this implies that
[\Phi]=\id
and
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\sigma_{1}
.
∎

Let
\Aut^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})\subset\Aut(\mathcal{D})
be the subgroup of autoequivalences which preserve the component
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
. Write
\Aut^{\dagger}_{*}(\mathcal{D})
for the quotient of
\Aut^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
by the subgroup of autoequivalences which act trivially on
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
.

Corollary 6.11
.

There is an isomorphism

\Aut^{\dagger}_{*}(\mathcal{D})\simeq\Br(\mathcal{D})\rtimes\Aut(\Gamma),

Where
Aut(\Gamma)
acts on
\Br(\mathcal{D})
by permuting the generators.

Proof.

The argument is the same as
[
9
, Cor. 1.4]
. Observe that unlike in
[
9
]
, the shift autoequivalence does not belong to
\Aut^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
, since it maps
\Stab_{n}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{D})
to a different connected component in
\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})
if it’s an even shift or outside
\Stab_{n}(\mathcal{D})
if it’s odd.
∎
