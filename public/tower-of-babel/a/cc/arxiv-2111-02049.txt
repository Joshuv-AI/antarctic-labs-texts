Noise inference for ergodic Lévy driven SDE This work was partly supported by JST CREST Grant Number JPMJCR14D7, Japan.
Hiroki Masuda, Lorenzo Mercuri, Yuma Uehara

Abstract
. We study inference for the driving Lévy noise of an ergodic stochastic differential equation (SDE) model, when the process is observed at high-frequency and long time and when the drift and scale coefficients contain finite-dimensional unknown parameters. By making use of the Gaussian quasi-likelihood function for the coefficients, we derive a stochastic expansion for functionals of the unit-time residuals, which clarifies some quantitative effect of plugging in the estimators of the coefficients, thereby enabling us to take several inference procedures for the driving-noise characteristics into account. We also present new classes and methods available in YUIMA for the simulation and the estimation of a Lévy SDE model. We highlight the flexibility of these new advances in YUIMA using simulated and real data.



1.
Introduction

We consider the following univariate Markovian stochastic differential equation (SDE):

(1)

dX_{t}=a(X_{t},\alpha)dt+c(X_{t-},\gamma)dJ^{\eta}_{t},

where:

•

The coefficients
a
and
c
are smooth enough with
c
being non-degenerate, and known except for an unknown parameter

\theta:=(\gamma,\alpha)\in\Theta_{\gamma}\times\Theta_{\alpha}=:\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}

for bounded convex domains
\Theta_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{\alpha}}
and
\Theta_{\gamma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{\gamma}}
(
p=p_{\alpha}+p_{\gamma}
);

•

The driving noise
J^{\eta}
is a standardized non-Gaussian Lévy process with finite moments, whose distribution depends on an unknown parameter
\eta\in\Theta_{\eta}
, a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{p_{\eta}}
, and where
J^{\eta}
is independent of
X_{0}
.

We write
J=J^{\eta}
in the sequel. We suppose that there are true values
\theta_{0}=(\gamma_{0},\alpha_{0})\in\Theta_{\gamma}\times\Theta_{\alpha}
and
\eta_{0}\in\Theta_{\eta}
which induce the true image measure
P
of
(X,J)
, and that we observe a discrete-time sample
\bm{X}_{n}:=(X_{t_{j}})_{j=1}^{n}
, where
t_{j}=t_{j}^{n}:=jh_{n}
, with the sampling stepsize
h=h_{n}\to 0
satisfying that

(2)

T_{n}:=nh\to\infty\ \text{ and }nh^{2}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,

the so-called rapidly increasing experimental design.

Our objective is to estimate the value
\xi_{0}:=(\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
under the ergodicity.
We remark the parameter may not completely characterize the distribution
\mathcal{L}(J)
, so that the problem is not necessarily parametric; for example,
\eta
is just a skewness or kurtosis, which may or may not completely determine
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
.

For the estimation of the coefficient parameter
\theta
, from the statistical point of view it is important what kind of distributional is used to approximate the conditional distribution of
X_{t_{j}}
given
X_{t_{j-1}}
.
Previously,
[
12
]
and
[
18
]
considered estimation of
\theta
based on the Gaussian quasi-likelihood (GQL), and proved the asymptotic normality and the tail probability estimates of the Gaussian quasi-maximum likelihood estimator (GQMLE).
At the expense of efficiency, the GQL based method has the robustness against misspecification of the driving-noise distribution, which may be crucial in the context of time-series models, see
[
17
, Section 6.2]
.
Further, concerned with estimation of a Lévy-measure functional of the form
\int\varphi(z)\nu(dz)
, with
\nu
denoting the Lévy measure of
J
, the previous study
[
13
]
proposed a moment-matching based estimator and proved its asymptotic normality at rate
\sqrt{T_{n}}
.
However, the procedure imposed some stringent conditions on the behavior of
\varphi
around the origin
[
13
, Assumption 2.7]
, and was not quite suitable if we want to estimate
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
directly; in general, estimation of the distribution and that of the corresponding Lévy measure can be of technically rather different matters.

In this paper, we will propose yet another strategy for estimating
\eta
based on the unit-time approximation, which goes as follows:

(1)

First we construct the GQMLE
\hat{\theta}_{n}:=(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{\alpha}_{n})
and the residual

(3)

\widehat{\Delta_{j}J}=\widehat{\Delta_{j}J}^{n}:=\frac{X_{t_{j}}-X_{t_{j-1}}-ha(X_{t_{j-1}},\hat{\alpha}_{n})}{c(X_{t_{j-1}},\hat{\gamma}_{n})}.

(2)

We then estimate the
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
-i.i.d. sequence (
i=1,\dots,n
)

\epsilon_{i}:=J_{i}-J_{i-1}

by adding up the finer increments over the
i
th unit-time interval
[i-1,i]
:

(4)

\hat{\epsilon}_{i}:=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\widehat{\Delta_{j}J},

where, for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor\}
,

\displaystyle A_{i}:=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\,i-1<t_{j}\leq i\right\}=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\,\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor+1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor\right\},

and then measure the discrepancy between a functional of
\{\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\}
and that of
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
through a stochastic expansion.

(3)

For an appropriate function
m
, we estimate
\eta
by

\hat{\eta}_{n}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\eta\in\overline{\Theta_{\eta}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}m\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\eta\right).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
We briefly summarize some prerequisites in Section
2
, and then presents theoretical results in Section
3
.
Section
4
introduces new classes and methods in YUIMA
R
package (
[
2
]
and
[
7
]
) for the estimation procedure proposed in the previous sections.
Some numerical examples based on simulated and real data are given in Section
5
.

2.2.
Basic assumptions

Denote by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},P)

Assumption 2.1
.

The Lévy process

E[J_{1}]=0
,
E[J_{1}^{2}]=1
, and
E[|J_{1}|^{q}]<\infty
, for any
q>0
.

Assumption 2.2
.

(1)

The drift coefficient
a(\cdot,\alpha_{0})
and scale coefficient

are Lipschitz continuous, and
c(x,\gamma)\neq 0
for every
(x,\gamma)
.

(2)

For each
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
and
k\in\left\{0,\dots,4\right\}
, the following conditions hold:

•

The coefficients
a
and
c
admit the partial derivatives
\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{\alpha}^{k}a
and
\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{\gamma}^{k}c
for
i\geq 0
and
k\geq 0
, all of which have continuous extensions as elements in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Theta})
.

•

There exists nonnegative constant
C_{i,k}
satisfying

(5)

\sup_{(x,\alpha,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Theta}_{\alpha}\times\overline{\Theta}_{\gamma}}\frac{1}{1+|x|^{C_{i,k}}}\left\{|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{\alpha}^{k}a(x,\alpha)|+|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{\gamma}^{k}c(x,\gamma)|+|c^{-1}(x,\gamma)|\right\}<\infty.

Then

Assumption 2.3
.

(1)

There exists a probability measure
\pi_{0}
such that for every
q>0
, we can find constants
a>0
and
C_{q}>0
for which

(6)

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\exp(at)||P_{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi_{0}(\cdot)||_{h_{q}}\leq C_{q}h_{q}(x),

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
where
h_{q}(x):=1+|x|^{q}
.

(2)

For any
q>0
, we have

(7)

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}E[|X_{t}|^{q}]<\infty.

See
[
12
, Proposition 5.4]
for easy-to-verify conditions for Assumption
2.3
.

We introduce a block-diagonal
p\times p
-matrix

\Gamma=\mathop{\rm diag}(\Gamma_{\gamma},\Gamma_{\alpha}),

whose components are defined by:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma}:=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\partial_{\gamma}c(x,\gamma_{0}))^{\otimes 2}}{c^{2}(x,\gamma_{0})}\pi_{0}(dx),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\partial_{\alpha}a(x,\alpha_{0}))^{\otimes 2}}{c^{2}(x,\gamma_{0})}\pi_{0}(dx).

Assumption 2.4
.

\Gamma
is positive definite.

We define real valued functions
\mathbb{H}_{1}(\gamma)
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}(\alpha)
on
\Theta_{\gamma}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}
by

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{1}(\gamma)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\log c^{2}(x,\gamma)+\frac{c^{2}(x,\gamma_{0})}{c^{2}(x,\gamma)}\right)\pi_{0}(dx),

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{2}(\alpha)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}c(x,\gamma_{0})^{-2}(a(x,\alpha_{0})-a(x,\alpha))^{2}\pi_{0}(dx).

We assume the following identifiability condition for
\mathbb{H}_{1}(\gamma)
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}(\alpha)
:

Assumption 2.5
.

\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
,
and there exist positive constants
\chi_{\gamma}
and
\chi_{\alpha}
such that for all
(\gamma,\alpha)\in\Theta
,

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{1}(\gamma):=\mathbb{H}_{1}(\gamma)-\mathbb{H}_{1}(\gamma_{0})\leq-\chi_{\gamma}|\gamma-\gamma_{0}|^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{2}(\alpha):=\mathbb{H}_{2}(\alpha)-\mathbb{H}_{2}(\alpha_{0})\leq-\chi_{\alpha}|\alpha-\alpha_{0}|^{2}.

2.3.
Examples

Although Assumption
2.1
imposes a strong restriction on the mean and variance structure of
Z
, there is still room for statistical modeling of
Z
with respect to, for example, its skewness and jump activity.
In this section, with the parameter constraints for Assumption
2.1
, we give concrete Lévy processes induced from subordinators (i.e. non-decreasing Lévy processes) by the following two procedures:
For given two independent subordinators
\tau^{1}
and
\tau^{2}
, one can easily construct a possibly skewed Lévy process of finite variation by taking its bilateral version:
\tau^{\prime}:=\tau^{1}-\tau^{2}
.
Another way to deliver a new Lévy process is to take a normal mean variance mixture of a subordinator: for
\mu,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, a subordinator
\tau
, and a standard normal random variable
\eta
being independent of
\tau
, the normal mean variance mixture of
\tau
at time
t
is given by

Z_{t}=\mu t+\tau_{t}\beta+\sqrt{\tau_{t}}\eta.

By their construction,
E[|\tau^{\prime}|^{q}]<\infty
and
E[|Z|^{q}]<\infty
hold as long as the
q
-th moment of
\tau^{1},\tau^{2}
and
\tau
exists.
It is worth noting that having a generator of
\tau^{1},\tau^{2}
and
\tau
in hand, we can directly obtain that of
\tau^{\prime}
and
Z
.
All of the following induced Lévy processes can be generated by the functions
setLaw
and
simulate
in YUIMA package.

Example 2.6
.

(Bilateral gamma
[
9
]
)
For
\delta_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{2},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the bilateral gamma process
\tau^{\prime}
is defined by the difference of two independent gamma subordinators
\tau^{1}
and
\tau^{2}
whose Lévy densities are expressed as: for each
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle f_{\tau^{i}}(z)=\frac{\delta_{i}}{z}e^{-\gamma_{i}z},\quad z>0.

We write the law of
\tau^{\prime}_{1}
as
bgamma(\delta_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{2},\gamma_{2})
and it is straightforward from the form of
f_{\tau^{i}}
that
\tau^{\prime}_{t}\sim bgamma(\delta_{1}t,\gamma_{1},\delta_{2}t,\gamma_{2})
.
Since the density function of
bgamma(\delta_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{2},\gamma_{2})
is the convolution of two gamma density, it satisfies the symmetry relation

p(x;\delta_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{2},\gamma_{2})=p(-x;\delta_{2},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\gamma_{1}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},

and on the positive real line, its form is given by

p(x;\delta_{1},\gamma_{1},\delta_{2},\gamma_{2})=\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\delta_{1}}\gamma_{2}^{\delta_{2}}}{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}\Gamma(\delta_{1})}x^{\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})-1}e^{-\frac{x}{2}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})}W_{\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}),\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}-1)}(x(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})),

where
W_{\lambda,\mu}(z)
denotes the Whittaker function.
By using the independence between
\tau^{1}
and
\tau^{2}
, the parameter constraints for Assumption
2.1
are written as follows:

E[Z_{1}]=\frac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma_{1}}-\frac{\delta_{2}}{\gamma_{2}}=0,\quad V[Z_{1}]=\frac{\delta_{1}}{\gamma^{2}_{1}}+\frac{\delta_{2}}{\gamma^{2}_{2}}=1.

Example 2.7
.

(Normal (exponentially) tempered stable) The normal (exponentially) tempered stable law
NTS(\alpha,a,b,\beta,\mu)
is defined by the law of the normal mean variance mixture of the positive exponentially tempered stable random variable whose Lévy density is given by

f(z)=az^{-1-\alpha}e^{-bz},\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\ a>0,\ b>0,

and its law is denoted by
TS(\alpha,a,b)
; especially inverse Gaussian law corresponds to
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
.
From
[
15
, Theorem 30.1]
, the Lévy density of
NTS(\alpha,a,b,\beta,\mu)
is explicitly expressed as:

g(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}ae^{\beta z}\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2b+\beta^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}K_{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\left(z\sqrt{(2b+\beta^{2})}\right),

where
K_{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}
stands for the modified Bessel function of the third kind with index
\alpha+\frac{1}{2}
.
From the expression of the Lévy density, the associated positive exponentially tempered stable subordinator
\tau
satisfies
\tau_{t}\sim TS(\alpha,at,b)
and thus the corresponding normal (exponentially) tempered stable process
Z
also does
Z_{t}\sim NTS(\alpha,at,b,\beta,\mu t)
.
Since
z^{\lambda}K_{\lambda}(z)\lesssim 1
as
z\downarrow 0
for
\lambda>0
, the Blumenthal-Getoor index of
Z
is
2\alpha
.
The parameter constraints for Assumption
2.1
are written as follows:

E[Z_{1}]=\mu-a\alpha\Gamma(-\alpha)b^{\alpha-1}\beta=0,\quad V[Z_{1}]=a\alpha\Gamma(-\alpha)b^{\alpha-1}\left[\frac{(\alpha-1)\beta^{2}}{b}-1\right]=1.

Especially in the simple case where
\mu=\beta=0
(that is,
Z
is a time-changed Brownian motion), the above constraints are reduced to

-a\alpha b^{\alpha-1}\Gamma(-\alpha)=1.

2.4.
Stepwise Gaussian quasi-likelihood estimation

Here and in what follows, for any process
Y
we will denote by
\Delta_{j}Y
the
j
-th increment

\Delta_{j}Y:=Y_{t_{j}}-Y_{t_{j-1}},

and
f_{j-1}(\theta):=f(X_{t_{j-1}},\theta)
for a measurable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Theta}
.
Building on the discrete time formal Gaussian approximation, we define the stepwise GQL functions
\mathbb{H}_{1,n}(\gamma)
and
\mathbb{H}_{2,n}(\alpha)
as follows
[
18
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{1,n}(\gamma):=-\frac{1}{2T_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(h\log c^{2}_{j-1}(\gamma)+\frac{(\Delta_{j}X)^{2}}{c^{2}_{j-1}(\gamma)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{2,n}(\alpha):=-\frac{1}{2T_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(\Delta_{j}X-ha_{j-1}(\alpha))^{2}}{hc^{2}_{j-1}(\hat{\gamma}_{n})},

where
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
is any maximizer of
\mathbb{H}_{1,n}
over
\overline{\Theta}_{\gamma}
.
We then define the associated stepwise GQMLE
\hat{\theta}_{n}:=(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{\alpha}_{n})
where
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
is any maximizer of
\mathbb{H}_{2,n}
over
\overline{\Theta}_{\alpha}
.
Formally, the first-stage
\mathbb{H}_{1,n}(\gamma)
corresponds to the quasi log-likelihoods associated with the (fake) approximation
N(x,\,c^{2}(x,\gamma)h)
for
\mathcal{L}(X_{t_{j}}|X_{t_{j-1}}=x)
, and also the second-stage
\mathbb{H}_{2,n}(\alpha)
does to the one associated with
N(x+a(x,\alpha)h,\,c^{2}(x,\hat{\gamma}_{n})h)
.

Let
\nu_{0}(dz)
denote the (true) Lévy measure of
J
, and

\nu_{k}:=\int z^{k}\,\nu_{0}(dz),\qquad k\geq 2.

Under the aforementioned Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
, both
\nu_{3}
and
\nu_{4}
exist and are finite (and
E[J_{1}^{2}]=\nu_{2}+\sigma^{2}=1
, where
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
denotes the Gaussian variance of
J
, possibly
\sigma^{2}=0
).
We can deduce the asymptotic normality and the uniform tail-probability estimate:

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
holds.

(1)

The GQMLE
\hat{\theta}_{n}=(\hat{\gamma}_{n},\hat{\alpha}_{n})
satisfies

(12)

\sqrt{T_{n}}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}N_{p_{\gamma}+p_{\alpha}}\left(0,\Gamma^{-1}\Sigma_{\theta}(\Gamma^{-1})^{\top}\right),

where the
p\times p
-matrix

\Sigma_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{\gamma}&\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}\\
\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}^{\top}&\Sigma_{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},

is defined by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\gamma}=\nu_{4}\,\int\left(\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c(x,\gamma_{0})}{c(x,\gamma_{0})}\right)^{\otimes 2}\pi_{0}(dx),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}=\nu_{3}\,\int\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c(x,\gamma_{0})\partial_{\alpha}a(x,\alpha_{0})}{c^{2}(x,\gamma_{0})}\pi_{0}(dx),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha}=\int\left(\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a(x,\alpha_{0})}{c(x,\gamma_{0})}\right)^{\otimes 2}\pi_{0}(dx).

(2)

For any
L>0
, there exists a constant
C_{L}>0
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P\left(\left|\sqrt{T_{n}}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})\right|>r\right)\leq\frac{C_{L}}{r^{L}},\qquad r>0.

We note that
\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}
,
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{n}
are asymptotically independent if
\nu_{3}=0
, hence if in particular
\nu
is symmetric.
We refer to
[
12
]
and
[
18
]
for technical details of the proof of Theorem
2.8
;
although the two cited papers used a
Z
-estimator type identifiability condition, which is seemingly different from Assumption
2.5
(
M
-estimator type), it is trivial that we can follow the same line without any essential change.

To appreciate the difficulty of relaxing the standing condition
nh^{2}\to 0
in (
2
), let us first mention the case of diffusions:
let
(w_{t})
be a standard Wiener process, and consider the following one-dimensional diffusion process

dY_{t}=a(Y_{t})dt+b(Y_{t})dw_{t}

defined on the stochastic bases
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}},P)
.
Write the infinitesimal generator of
Y
as
\mathcal{A}
.
By repeatedly applying Itô’s formula, it follows that for
0\leq s<t
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sufficiently smooth function
f
, we have

\displaystyle f(Y_{t})

\displaystyle=f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\partial f(Y_{u})dY_{u}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\partial^{2}f(Y_{u})b^{2}(Y_{u})du

\displaystyle=f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\left(\partial f(Y_{u})a(Y_{u})+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}f(Y_{u})b^{2}(Y_{u})\right)du+\int_{s}^{t}\partial f(Y_{u})b(Y_{u})dw_{u}

\displaystyle=f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{A}f(Y_{u})du+\int_{s}^{t}\partial f(Y_{u})b(Y_{u})dw_{u}

\displaystyle=f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\left(\mathcal{A}f(Y_{s})+\int_{u}^{s}\mathcal{A}^{2}f(Y_{v})dv+\int_{u}^{s}\partial\mathcal{A}f(Y_{v})b(Y_{v})dw_{v}\right)du+\int_{s}^{t}\partial f(Y_{u})b(Y_{u})dw_{u}

\displaystyle=f(Y_{s})+(t-s)\mathcal{A}f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\left(\int_{u}^{s}\mathcal{A}^{2}f(Y_{v})dv+\int_{u}^{s}\partial\mathcal{A}f(Y_{v})b(Y_{v})dw_{v}\right)du+\int_{s}^{t}\partial f(Y_{u})b(Y_{u})dw_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\frac{(t-s)^{i}}{i!}\mathcal{A}^{i}f(Y_{s})+\int_{s}^{t}\int\dots\int\mathcal{A}^{q}f(Y_{v_{q}})dv_{1}\dots dv_{q}+\text{(martingale term)}.

Thus, under suitable integrability conditions, we obtain the expansion of
E[f(Y_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
:

(13)

E[f(Y_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\frac{(t-s)^{i}}{i!}\mathcal{A}^{i}f(Y_{s})+O_{p}((t-s)^{q}).

In particular, the first-order approximation of the conditional expectation and conditional variance are given by

\displaystyle E[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle\approx X_{s}+(t-s)a(X_{s},\alpha_{0}),

\displaystyle V[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle\approx E[(X_{t}-X_{s}-(t-s)a(X_{s},\alpha_{0}))^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\approx(t-s)c(X_{s},\gamma_{0}),

respectively, which are used for constructing the GQL for diffusions.
Relaxation of the condition
nh^{2}\to 0
to

for
k>2
is then possible by taking

large enough according to the value of
k
:
the associated GQMLE has the consistency and asymptotic normality under

.
We refer to
[
8
]
for details.
At this point, we should remark that
the GQL based on the first term on the right-hand side of (
13
) (with
f(y)=y
and
f(y)=y^{2}
) is fully explicit whatever

On the other hand, although similar Itô-Taylor expansions to
E[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
and
V[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]
can be easily derived in our Lévy driven case (
1
), the corresponding infinitesimal generator contains not only the differential operator but also the integral operator with respect to the Lévy measure
\nu_{\eta}
of the driving Lévy noise.
Specifically, its infinitesimal generator
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
is given by

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}f(x)=a(x,\alpha)\partial f(x)+\int(f(x+c(x,\gamma)z)-f(x)-\partial f(x)c(x,\gamma)z)\nu_{\eta}(dz),

for a suitable function
f
.
Consequently, the modified GQL based on the higher-order Itô-Taylor expansion (
13
) contains the unknown parameter
\eta
in addition to the drift and scale parameters.
It is not clear that the simultaneous estimation of
\alpha,\gamma,
and
\eta
by the modified GQL has a nice theoretical property.
Even if it does, the entailed numerical optimization involved would be quite heavy and unstable since, for each
\eta
, we need to repeatedly compute several integrals with respect to
\nu_{\eta}
inside of the modified GQL.
For this reason, it is difficult to remove the condition
nh^{2}\to 0
in the present general non-linear-SDE setting, as long as we use the GQL based on the stochastic expansion (
13
).

In practice, the sampling points
t_{1},\dots t_{n}
may not be equally spaced.
In such a case, supposing (
2
), we remark that the same statement as in Theorem
2.8
remains in place under the additional “sampling-balance” condition:

\frac{\min_{1\leq j\leq n}(t_{j}-t_{j-1})}{\max_{1\leq j\leq n}(t_{j}-t_{j-1})}\to 1.

For more technical details, see the discussion in
[
12
, p. 1604–1605]
.

3.
Theoretical results

3.1.
Stochastic expansion of residual functional

Having the GQMLE in hand, we now turn to approximating
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
, the the unit-time distribution of
J
, based on the residuals

\hat{\epsilon}_{i}=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\widehat{\Delta_{j}J},

where

\widehat{\Delta_{j}J}=\frac{\Delta_{j}X-ha_{j-1}(\hat{\alpha}_{n})}{c_{j-1}(\hat{\gamma}_{n})}.

Write
\hat{u}_{\alpha,n}=\sqrt{T_{n}}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha_{0})
and
\hat{u}_{\gamma,n}=\sqrt{T_{n}}(\hat{\gamma}_{n}-\gamma_{0})
, and let

\hat{u}_{\theta,n}:=(\hat{u}_{\gamma,n},\hat{u}_{\alpha,n}).

From now on we will mostly omit “
(\theta_{0})
” from notation. In particular, for a measurable function
f(x,\theta)
we will abbreviate
f_{j-1}(\theta_{0})
as
f_{j-1}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
hold. Let
\rho:\,\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function such that

\max_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}\left|\partial^{i}_{\epsilon}\rho(\epsilon)\right|\lesssim 1+|\epsilon|^{C}.

Then, we have

(14)

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\rho\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)b_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]+o_{p}\left(\sqrt{T_{n}}\right),

where the random sequence
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is given by

b_{i}=b_{i}(\theta_{0}):=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\gamma}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\Delta_{j}X-ha_{j-1})\\
-hc^{-1}_{j-1}\partial_{\alpha}a_{j-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem
3.1
reveals the quantitative effect of plugging in
\hat{\theta}_{n}
.
The second term on the right-hand side of (
14
) is not
o_{p}(\sqrt{T_{n}})
but
O_{p}(\sqrt{T_{n}})
, which implies that the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\eta}_{n}
is indeed subject to influence of the proposed unit-time approximation; this is natural and expected, for we are using the
\sqrt{T_{n}}
-consistent (generally sub-optimal) estimator of
\theta
.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we begin with a preliminary estimate.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
, for each
r\geq 2
we have

(15)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}b_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim h\vee T_{n}^{-r}.

Proof.

We will abbreviate
a(X_{s},\alpha_{0})
as
a_{s}
and so on; with a slight abuse of notation, we will write
a_{j-1}
for
a_{t_{j-1}}
.
Write
\widehat{\Delta_{j}J}=\delta_{j}(\hat{\theta}_{n})
; then,

\delta_{j}(\hat{\theta}_{n})=\delta_{j}+(\partial_{\theta}\delta_{j})[\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}]+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\delta_{j}(\theta_{0}+s_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}))\right)[(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})^{\otimes 2}]

for a suitable random point
s_{n}\in[0,1]
.
Decompose
\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}(\widehat{\Delta_{j}J}-\Delta_{j}J)+\left(J_{(\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\rfloor+1)h}-J_{i-1}\right)+\left(J_{i}-J_{\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\rfloor h}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}b_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]+\zeta_{1,i}+\zeta_{2,i}+\zeta_{3,i},

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{1,i}:=\frac{1}{2T_{n}}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\delta_{j}(\theta_{0}+s_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}))\right)[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}^{\otimes 2}],

\displaystyle\zeta_{2,i}:=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\delta_{j}-\Delta_{j}J\right)=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}c_{j-1}^{-1}\left(\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}(a_{s}-a_{j-1})ds+\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}(c_{s-}-c_{j-1})dJ_{s}\right),

\displaystyle\zeta_{3,i}:=\left(J_{(\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\rfloor+1)h}-J_{i-1}\right)+\left(J_{i}-J_{\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\rfloor h}\right).

Before proceeding, let us note that by Theorem
2.8
the sequence
(\hat{u}_{\theta,n})
is
L^{r}
-bounded for each
r\geq 2
:

(16)

E\left(|\hat{u}_{\theta,n}|^{r}\right)\lesssim 1,

where we implicitly assume that
r\geq 1
when using this notation.

First we will deduce

(17)

\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left(\left|\zeta_{1,i}\right|^{r}\right)\lesssim T_{n}^{-r}.

Since the parameter space
\Theta=\Theta_{\alpha}\times\Theta_{\gamma}
is supposed to be bounded and convex, the Sobolev inequality is in force (see
[
1
]
for details): for a random field
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Theta)
and
q>p
, we have

E\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|u(\theta)|^{q}\right]\lesssim\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{E[|u(\theta)|^{q}]+E[|\partial_{\theta}u(\theta)|^{q}]\right\}.

Noting the identities

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma}^{k}\partial_{\alpha}^{l}\delta_{j}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-h\left\{\partial_{\gamma}^{k}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)\right\}\partial_{\alpha}^{l}a_{j-1}(\alpha),

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma}^{k}\delta_{j}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\{\partial_{\gamma}^{k}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)\right\}(\Delta_{j}X-ha_{j-1}(\alpha)),

valid for each
k\geq 0
and
l\geq 1
,
we can apply Sobolev’s and Jensen’s inequalities to conclude that, for
r>p_{\gamma}
,

\displaystyle E\left[\left|\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\partial_{\theta}^{2}\delta_{j}(\theta_{0}+s_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0}))\right)\right|^{r}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim E\left[\sup_{\gamma\in\Theta_{\gamma}}\left|\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\partial_{\gamma}^{2}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}c_{s-}dJ_{s}\right|^{r}\right]+E\left[h\sum_{j\in A_{i}}(1+|X_{t_{j-1}}|^{C})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{k\in\{2,3\}}\sup_{\gamma\in\Theta_{\gamma}}E\left[\left|\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\partial_{\gamma}^{k}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}c_{s-}dJ_{s}\right|^{r}\right]+1.

Let
\chi_{j}(s)
denote the indicator function of the interval
[t_{j-1},t_{j})
.

To proceed, we recall Burkholder’s inequality for stochastic integrals with respect to a centered Lévy process: under the moment conditions on
J_{\eta}
, for any predictable process
H
and
q\geq 2
we have

E\left[\left|\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}H_{s}dJ_{s}\right|^{q}\right]\leq K_{q}(\nu_{\eta,2}^{q/2}+\nu_{\eta,q})\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}E\left[|H_{s}|^{q}\right]ds\lesssim\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}E\left[|H_{s}|^{q}\right]ds,

where
K_{q}
is a positive constant depending only on
q
, and
\nu_{\eta,k}:=\int z^{k}\,\nu_{\eta}(dz)
for
k\geq 2
(see
[
14
, Theorem IV 48]
).

Then, we see that the last expectation equals

(18)

\displaystyle E\left[\left|\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\chi_{j}(s)\partial_{\gamma}^{k}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)c_{s-}dJ_{s}\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}E\left[\left|\partial_{\gamma}^{k}(c^{-1})_{j-1}(\gamma)\,c_{s}\right|^{r}\right]ds\lesssim 1.

This together with (
16
) concludes (
17
).

Turning to
\zeta_{2,i}
, we note the standard moment estimate: for any real
r\geq 2
,

\max_{j\leq n}\sup_{s\in(t_{j-1},t_{j}]}E\left[|X_{s}-X_{t_{j-1}}|^{r}\right]\lesssim h.

With this and the Lipschitz property of
x\mapsto(a(x,\alpha_{0}),c(x,\gamma_{0}))
, analogous arguments as in handling
\zeta_{1,i}
yield that for each
r\geq 2

(19)

\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\zeta_{2,i}\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim h.

As for the remaining
\zeta_{3,i}
, it follows from Assumption
2.1
and the stationarity of increments that for each
r\geq 2
,

(20)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\zeta_{3,i}\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim E\left[\left(J_{j-1}-J_{\lfloor\frac{j-1}{h}\rfloor h+h}\right)^{r}\right]+E\left[\left(J_{\lfloor\frac{j}{h}\rfloor h}-J_{j}\right)^{r}\right]\lesssim h.

Piecing together (
17
), (
19
), and (
20
) concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Mimicking the estimates for (
17
), for each
r\geq 2
we obtain

\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}b_{i}\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim T_{n}^{-r/2}.

Combined with (
15
) and (
16
), it follows from Hölder’s inequality that for each
r\geq 2
,

(21)

\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}\right|^{r}\right]\lesssim h\vee T_{n}^{-\frac{r}{2}},

and hence
\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E[|\hat{\epsilon}_{i}|^{r}]\lesssim 1
as well.
We use the expression

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\rho\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}^{2}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}+u\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}\right)\right)(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i})^{2}

for a (random)
u\in[0,1]
.
By means of Schwarz’s inequality and (
15
),

\displaystyle E\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)b_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]\right|\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}\right)\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}b_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]\right)\right|\right]\lesssim T_{n}\sqrt{h\vee T_{n}^{-2}}=\sqrt{T_{n}}\sqrt{nh^{2}\vee T_{n}^{-1}}=o\left(\sqrt{T_{n}}\right).

By the moment estimates in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, Hölder’s inequality, and (
21
), and also recalling (
2
), we see that for
\delta\in(1,2]
,

\displaystyle E\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\epsilon}^{2}\rho\left(\epsilon_{i}+u\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}\right)\right)(\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i})^{2}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim T_{n}\max_{1\leq i\leq\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}E\left[\left|\hat{\epsilon}_{i}-\epsilon_{i}\right|^{2\delta}\right]^{1/\delta}\lesssim\sqrt{T_{n}}\left\{(nh^{1+2/\delta})^{\delta/2}\vee T_{n}^{-\delta/2}\right\}^{1/\delta}=o\left(\sqrt{T_{n}}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
M
-estimation of noise parameter

We keep Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
in force.
Having Theorem
3.1
in hand, we proceed with estimation of
\eta
based on the unit-time residual sequence
(\hat{\epsilon}_{i})
. Let

\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta):=\frac{1}{T_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}m(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\eta),

and consider an
M
-estimator

(22)

\hat{\eta}_{n}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\eta\in\overline{\Theta_{\eta}}}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta).

Among others, this includes the (quasi) maximum-likelihood for
m(\epsilon,\eta)=:\log f(\epsilon;\eta)
, where
\{f(\epsilon;\eta):\,\eta\in\Theta_{\eta}\}
is a model for the unit-time noise distribution
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
.
We need to impose several conditions on the function
m
, all of which are standard in the general theory of
M
-estimation.

Assumption 3.3
.

(1)

m\in\mathcal{C}^{2,3}(\mathbb{R}\times\Theta_{\eta})
and
\displaystyle{\max_{i\in\{0,1,2\}\atop k\in\{0,1,2,3\}}\sup_{\eta\in\overline{\Theta_{\eta}}}\left|\partial^{i}_{\epsilon}\partial^{k}_{\eta}m(\epsilon,\eta)\right|\lesssim 1+|\epsilon|^{C}}
for some
C\geq 0
.

(2)

E\left[\partial_{\eta}m(J_{1},\eta_{0})\right]=0
, the
p_{\eta}\times p_{\eta}
-matrix

E\left[\left(\partial_{\eta}m(J_{1},\eta_{0})\right)^{\otimes 2}\right]

is positive definite, and

\{\eta_{0}\}=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\eta}E\left[m(J_{1},\eta)\right].

The consistency of
\hat{\eta}_{n}
can be easily seen from Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
:
we have the continuous random function

\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\eta):=\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta)-\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta_{0})\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{Y}(\eta):=E\left[m(J_{1},\eta)\right]-E\left[m(J_{1},\eta_{0})\right],

where
\mathbb{Y}(\eta)\leq 0
by Jensen’s inequality with
\mathbb{Y}(\eta)=0
if and only if
\eta=\eta_{0}
, and moreover, the convergence is uniform in
\eta\in\overline{\Theta_{\eta}}
since
\sup_{n}E[\sup_{\eta}|\partial_{\eta}\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\eta)|]<\infty
.
Hence the consistency
\hat{\eta}_{n}\xrightarrow{p}\eta_{0}
follows.

We turn to the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\eta}_{n}
.
Let
\hat{u}_{\eta,n}:=\sqrt{T_{n}}(\hat{\eta}_{n}-\eta_{0})
.
In the sequel, for any measurable function
f(x,\theta)
we will simply write
\hat{f}_{j-1}
for
f_{j-1}(\hat{\theta}_{n})
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
to
2.5
, and Assumption
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\hat{\eta}_{n})\right)[\hat{u}_{\eta,n}]-\frac{1}{T_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\left(\partial_{\eta}\partial_{\epsilon}m\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\hat{\eta}_{n}\right)\left(\hat{b}_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]\right)\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}N_{p_{\eta}}\left(0,E\left[\left(\partial_{\eta}m(J_{1},\eta_{0})\right)^{\otimes 2}\right]\right),

where

\hat{b}_{i}:=\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\partial_{\gamma}(c^{-1})}_{j-1}(\Delta_{j}X-h\hat{a}_{j-1})\\
-h\widehat{c^{-1}}_{j-1}\widehat{\partial_{\alpha}a}_{j-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

By the consistency
\hat{\eta}_{n}\xrightarrow{p}\eta_{0}
we may and do focus on the event
\{\partial_{\eta}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\hat{\eta}_{n})=0\}
, on which

\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta_{0}+s(\hat{\eta}_{n}-\eta_{0}))ds\right)[\hat{u}_{\eta,n}]=\sqrt{T_{n}}\partial_{\eta}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}.

Then, by Theorem

we have

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\hat{\eta}_{n})+o_{p}(1)\right)[\hat{u}_{\eta,n}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\eta}m\left(\epsilon_{i},\eta_{0}\right)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\frac{1}{T_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\left(\partial_{\eta}\partial_{\epsilon}m\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\hat{\eta}_{n}\right)\left(\hat{b}_{i}[\hat{u}_{\theta,n}]\right)\right)+o_{p}(1).

Hence, letting

we have

(23)

\hat{H}_{n}[\hat{u}_{n}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\eta}m\left(\epsilon_{i},\eta_{0}\right)+o_{p}(1),

where

p_{\eta}\times(p+p_{\eta})

\hat{H}_{n}=\left(-\frac{1}{T_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor T_{n}\right\rfloor}\partial_{\eta}\partial_{\epsilon}m\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{i},\hat{\eta}_{n}\right)\hat{b}_{i},~-\partial_{\eta}^{2}\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\hat{\eta}_{n})\right),

Since the random variables

are i.i.d., the desired result follows on applying the central limit theorem to (

∎

Theorem 3.7

Under Assumptions

, and Assumption
3.5
, we have

\hat{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{n}[\hat{u}_{n}]\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}N_{p+p_{\eta}}\left(0,I_{p+p_{\eta}}\right).

By Theorem
3.7
, we have

T_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0},\hat{\eta}_{n}-\eta_{0})\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{n}^{\top}\hat{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0},\hat{\eta}_{n}-\eta_{0})^{\top}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\chi^{2}(p+p_{\eta}),

based on which we can construct an approximate confidence set for
(\theta_{0},\eta_{0})
, and also perform a Wald-type test.
Also trivially, we can recover the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\theta}_{n}
:

T_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})\,\hat{\Gamma}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\Sigma}_{\gamma,n}&\hat{\Sigma}_{\gamma,\alpha,n}\\
\hat{\Sigma}_{\gamma,\alpha,n}^{\top}&\hat{\Sigma}_{\alpha,n}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\hat{\Gamma}_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta_{0})^{\top}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\chi^{2}(p).

It is difficult to obtain
\Sigma_{\eta,\gamma}
and
\Sigma_{\eta,\alpha}
in explicit easy-to-handle forms even if the coefficients and
m
are simple.
However, by an application of Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the estimates in the proof of Theorem 3.8,
we can observe that at least, the left-hand-sides in (
24
) and (
25
) are tight.
Moreover, we can formally write their limit by means of the representation theorem (
[
10
, Proposition 3]
): there exists a predictable process
s\mapsto\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)
such that

(26)

\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}\partial_{\eta}m(\epsilon_{i},\eta_{0})=\int_{0}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}\int\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)\tilde{N}(ds,dz).

From Itô’s formula and some calculations, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}\Delta_{j}J

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\chi_{j}(s)\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}\right)dJ_{s}=\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\int\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\chi_{j}(s)\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}\right)z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\int\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{s-}}{c_{s-}}z\tilde{N}(ds,dz),

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}\left[(\Delta_{j}J)^{2}-h\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\chi_{j}(s)\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}(J_{s-}-J_{t_{j-1}})\right)dJ_{s}+\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\int\sum_{j\in A_{i}}\left(\chi_{j}(s)\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{j-1}}{c_{j-1}}\right)z^{2}\tilde{N}(ds,dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{h}\right\rfloor h}^{\left\lfloor\frac{i}{h}\right\rfloor h}\int\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{s-}}{c_{s-}}z^{2}\tilde{N}(ds,dz)+O_{p}(h_{n}).

To sum up, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{n}[\hat{u}_{n}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{n}}}\int_{0}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}\int\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{s-}}{c_{s-}}z^{2}\\
\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{s-}}{c_{s-}}z\\
\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)\end{pmatrix}\tilde{N}(ds,dz)+o_{p}(1).

By applying the central limit theorem for the stochastic integral with respect to a Poisson random measure (cf.
[
19
, Lemma A.2]
), the isometry property of the stochastic integral yield that under suitable moment and regularity conditions,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{n}[\hat{u}_{n}]\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\rightarrow}N_{p+p_{\eta}}\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{\gamma}&\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}&\Sigma_{\eta,\gamma}\\
\Sigma_{\gamma,\alpha}^{\top}&\Sigma_{\alpha}&\Sigma_{\eta,\alpha}\\
\Sigma_{\eta,\gamma}^{\top}&\Sigma_{\eta,\alpha}^{\top}&E\left[\left(\partial_{\eta}m(J_{1},\eta_{0})\right)^{\otimes 2}\right]\end{pmatrix}\right),

where
\Sigma_{\eta,\gamma}
and
\Sigma_{\eta,\alpha}
are given by the limits in probability:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}\int E\left[\frac{\partial_{\gamma}c_{s-}}{c_{s-}}\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)\right]z^{2}\nu_{0}(dz)ds\xrightarrow{p}\Sigma_{\eta,\gamma},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{n}}\int_{0}^{\lfloor T_{n}\rfloor}\int E\left[\frac{\partial_{\alpha}a_{s-}}{c_{s-}}\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)\right]z\nu_{0}(dz)ds\xrightarrow{p}\Sigma_{\eta,\alpha},

and the other ingredients are the same as our previous works (cf. Theorem
2.8
).
However, since the explicit form of
\tilde{\xi}_{\eta,n}(s,z)
cannot be obtained in general, it is difficult to check the above convergence.

Finally, we would like to add that Theorem
3.7
and the resulting Wald-type test are valid without Assumption
3.5
if the minimum eigenvalue of
\hat{\Sigma}
is positive uniformly in
n
.
Such a condition for eigenvalues is often assumed in the context of (non-)linear regression.

3.3.

3.3.1.
Dimension of the processes

For the asymptotics of the GQMLE, we could consider multivariate
X
without any essential change
[
18
]
;
we will conduct related simulations in Section
5.2
.
Moreover, the estimator of
(\alpha,\gamma)
may not be necessarily the GQMLE and could be any measurable mappings
\hat{\theta}_{n}=\hat{\theta}_{n}(\bm{X}_{n})
for which we have an asymptotically linear representation as in (
28
).

3.3.2.
Model selection for
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})

Residual based on information criterion (IC) formulation after estimation should be possible (both AIC and BIC types).
We can infer the structure of
J
as in the i.i.d case, yet should be careful in making possibly necessary corrections stemming from the stochastic expansion (
14
).
For example, for the AIC statistics to be theoretically in effect, among other conditions it is required that the random sequence
(\hat{u}_{\eta,n})_{n}
is
L^{2+\delta}(P)
-bounded for some
\delta>0
. It could be verified by means of the uniform tail-probability estimate for
(\hat{u}_{\eta,n})_{n}
through the random function
\mathbb{H}_{3,n}(\eta)
; indeed, we could make use of the same machinery to deduce Theorem
2.8
(2).

3.3.3.
Setting of noise inference

Although we have set a finite-dimensional
\eta
above, we could consider infinite-dimensional
\eta
, most generally
\mathcal{L}(J_{1})
itself: once
\{\hat{\epsilon}_{j}\}
has been constructed, it is also possible to take into account conventional nonparametric procedures, such as the kernel density estimation, and also goodness-of-fit tests; see Section
5.3
for an illustration.

5.1.
Univariate Lévy SDE model

In this section, we show how to use YUIMA in the simulation and estimation of an univariate SDE driven by a pure jump Lévy process defined by the user through an object of
yuima.law
class. The model, that we consider, is defined by the following SDE:

(34)

dX_{t}=\alpha_{1}\left(\alpha_{2}-X_{t}\right)dt+\gamma\mbox{d}J_{t}

where
\alpha_{2}
is a real parameter while
\alpha_{1}
and
\gamma
are positive parameters, and where
\left\{J_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a symmetric Variance Gamma process with parameter
\eta>0
.

In this example we use an object of
yuima.law
class to construct a link between YUIMA and the
VarianceGamma
package
[
16
]
available in CRAN. We use two functions available in the package
VaranceGamma
respectively
rvg
for the random number generation and
dvg
to construct the density function. The parametrization in
VarianceGamma
package was introduced in
[
11
]
where the symmetric Variance Gamma
J
random variable is defined as a normal variance mean mixture with a gamma subordinator.
Specifically, we set

\phi_{J_{1}}\left(u\right)=\left(1+\sigma^{2}\nu\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\nu}}

for the characteristic function of
J_{1}

5.2.
Multivariate Lévy SDE model

In this section, we simulate and estimate a bivariate SDE model driven by two independent symmetric Variance Gamma processes. As done in the previous section we construct the random number generator and the joint density function of the underlying bivariate Lévy process using the function developed in the
VarianceGamma
package. We report below the code for simulating and estimating the process
X_{t}:=\left[X_{1,t},X_{2,t}\right]^{\top}
that satisfies the following system of SDEs:

(35)

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}dX_{1,t}=\alpha_{1,1}\left(\alpha_{1,2}-X_{1,t}-0.2X_{2,t}\right)dt+\gamma_{1}dJ_{1,t}\\
dX_{2,t}=\alpha_{2,1}\left(\alpha_{2,2}-X_{2,t}\right)dt+\gamma_{2}dJ_{2,t}\\
\end{array}\right.

where
J_{t}=\left[J_{t,1},J_{t,2}\right]^{\top}
is a bivariate Lévy process where the components are two independent symmetric Variance Gamma processes.

The first step is to construct an object of
yuima.law
class that contains a random number generator and the joint density of the bivariate Lévy process
J_{t}
. As done for the model in (
34
) we use the functions available in the
R
package
VarianceGamma
. The random number generator of the increments can be defined using the following command lines:

#### Construction of a bivariate rng function ####
R> myrng2 <- function(n, eta1, eta2, t){
+ res0 <- rvg(n, vgC = 0, sigma = sqrt(t), theta = 0, nu = 1 / (eta1 * t))
+ cbind(res0, rvg(n, vgC = 0, sigma = sqrt(t), theta = 0, nu = 1 / (eta2 * t)))
+ }
Compared with the random number generator used in the univariate case, the result of the function
rng
is a two-column matrix where each column contains increments generated from a symmetric Variance Gamma random variable. Exploiting the independence assumption we construct the joint density of the process
J_{t}
as a product of two univariate symmetric Variance Gamma densities using the following
R
function:

#### Construction of the joint density ####
R> mydens2 <- function(x, eta1, eta2, t){
+ dvg(x[,1], vgC = 0, sigma = sqrt(t), theta = 0, nu = 1/(eta1 * t)) *
+ dvg(x[,2], vgC = 0, sigma = sqrt(t), theta = 0, nu = 1/(eta2 * t))
+ }
Using the constructor
setLaw
, we build an object of
yuima.law
that contains information for simulating the noise
J_{t}
and for estimating the parameters in (
35
