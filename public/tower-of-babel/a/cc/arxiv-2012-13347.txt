Bra-Ket Representation of the Inertia Tensor Preprint: KPOP E {E} -2020-04
U-Rae Kim, Dohyun Kim, Jungil Lee

Abstract
We employ Dirac’s bra-ket notation to define the inertia tensor operator that is independent of the choice of bases or coordinate system. The principal axes and the corresponding principal values for the elliptic plate are determined only based on the geometry. By making use of a general symmetric tensor operator, we develop a method of diagonalization that is convenient and intuitive in determining the eigenvector. We demonstrate that the bra-ket approach greatly simplifies the computation of the inertia tensor with an example of an N N -dimensional ellipsoid. The exploitation of the bra-ket notation to compute the inertia tensor in classical mechanics should provide undergraduate students with a strong background necessary to deal with abstract quantum mechanical problems.

I
Introduction

The inertia tensor is one of the essential ingredients in classical mechanics
with which one can investigate the rotational properties of rigid-body
motion
CM
. The symmetric nature of the rank-2 Cartesian tensor
guarantees that it is described by three fundamental parameters
called the principal moments of inertia
I_{i}
, each of which is the moment of inertia
along a principal axis. The principal axes are orthogonal so that
they construct a natural choice of the Cartesian coordinate system. Additional
three parameters of the symmetric tensor can be allocated to the
rotation from the natural coordinate system of the principal axes to
an arbitrary coordinate system. Thus, the tensor is one of the best
tools with which students can train on how to deal with the eigenvalue
problem of a Hermitian matrix. The underlying mathematical structure
of this problem is closely related to the normal modes. It can
further be extended to the Hilbert space in the continuum limit
QM
.
Hence, we believe that teaching inertia tensors is of great importance
in giving students a strong background for their
future study of quantum mechanics.

Many pedagogical materials involving the general and the inertia tensors are available
Dixon-1951
;
Rogers-2004
;
Rogers-2005
;
Sivardiere-2005
;
Battaglia-2013
;
Abdulghany-2017
,
including undergraduate level textbooks
CM
;
QM
.
Most of them deal with
the inertia tensor in explicit matrix representations.
In this paper, we employ Dirac’s bra-ket
notation
Dirac-1923
;
Dirac-1939
;
Dirac-1958
to define the
inertia tensor operator, from which one can project out the
corresponding inertia tensor elements by applying a bra and a ket
for the unit basis vectors
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
along the principal axes.
While the completeness relation
reflects the homogeneousness and isotropy of the Euclidean space

\mathbf{1}=|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|
,
the inertia tensor operator acquires the anisotropy of the mass
distribution so that each principal axis is distinct in general:

\mathbf{I}=I_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|
.
The coordinate dependence of the inertia tensor of a system with the unit basis vector
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
can be read off
directly from the operator by finding the projections:

\mathbbm{I}=(I_{ij})=(\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle)
. The operator method is independent
of the coordinate system. Thus, the computation of the
matrix element is minimal because one needs to compute only
three fundamental scalar integrals
I_{i}
that are the principal moments.
An extension to the covariant approach as in
Ee-2017
is straightforward
because of the coordinate independence of this formulation.

This paper is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
, we list the definitions
of the quantities
involving the bra-ket notation and the inertia tensor operator. A summary of
the relationship between the principal axes and the coordinate dependence
of the inertia tensor is given in Sec.
III
. As applications of
the approach developed in the preceding sections, we compute
the inertia tensor for an ellipsoid in the three- and
N
-dimensional
Euclidean spaces in Sec.
IV
and
we conclude in Sec.
V
. Appendices are attached
to list the basis operators for the rank-2 Cartesian tensors
in two and three dimensions.

II
Definitions

II.1
Bra-Ket Notation

By employing Dirac’s bra-ket notation
Dirac-1923
;
Dirac-1939
;
Dirac-1958
,
one can express
the scalar product of any two Euclidean
vectors
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
in
N
dimensions as

\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{V}=\langle\mathbf{U}|\mathbf{V}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{V}|\mathbf{U}\rangle.

(1)

Let us consider unit basis vectors
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
in the Cartesian coordinate system
that satisfy the orthonormal condition and the completeness relation

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{jk},

(2)

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1},

(3)

where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker delta and
i
is summed over
1
,
\cdots
,
N
. In the remainder of this paper,
any repeated vector indices are assumed to be summed over unless specified.
Any vector can be expanded in terms of the bases as

|\mathbf{V}\rangle=V_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle,

(4)

where the Cartesian component
V_{i}

of
\mathbf{V}
can be read off as

V_{i}=\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{V}\rangle.

(5)

The identity operator
\mathbf{1}
is invariant under rotation.
Thus, the corresponding matrix representation
\mathbbm{1}
is
independent of the coordinate system:

\mathbbm{1}=(\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{1}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle)=(\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle)=(\delta_{ij}).

(6)

II.2
Inertia Tensor Operator

The
ij
element of the inertia tensor of a rigid body is a symmetric rank-2 Cartesian tensor:

I_{ij}=\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\rho(\mathbf{x})[\delta_{ij}\mathbf{x}^{2}-x_{i}x_{j}],

(7)

where
\rho(\mathbf{x})
is the mass density at point
\mathbf{x}
and the integration
is over the space. The rotational properties of the inertia tensor
is completely determined by the scalar field
\rho(\textbf{x})
.
By employing Dirac’s bra-ket notation, we can express the
inertia tensor operator
as

\mathbf{I}=\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}\rho(\textbf{x})\big[\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle\mathbf{1}-|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\,\big].

(8)

The matrix representation of the inertia tensor
\mathbbm{I}=(I_{ij})
in the coordinate system with the basis vectors
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
’s
can be expressed as

I_{ij}=\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle.

(9)

Note that the quantities like
V_{i}
in Eq. (
4
)
or
I_{ij}
in Eq. (
7
) depend on the choice of basis vectors or
coordinate system. However, the operator representation as in
Eq. (
8
) is independent of the coordinate system unless
it is rotationally invariant as in Eq. (
3
).

III
Geometry and Principal Axes

We consider an elliptic plate that has the simplest geometry without having
rotational symmetry in two dimensions.
The principal axes and the corresponding principal values for the rigid body
are first considered,
not based on the inertia tensor
but based on the geometry only, through the parametric equation.
The operator for the geometry of the rigid body is extended to
the inertia tensor operator.

III.1
Principal Axes

The parametric curve of an ellipse on the
xy
plane is

\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}=1,

(10)

where
a
and
b
are the semi-major and semi-minor axes
that are along the
x
and
y
axes, respectively.
An important feature of this expression is that the left side
is the sum of squared numbers. We call the coordinate axes
of the system the

principal axes

with which a physical observable can be expressed as the sum of
squared numbers. In this case, those principal axes consist
of the major and the minor axes. Thus, any expression for an
observable acquires the most compact form. This is a reflection
of the symmetries of a physical observable.

We define
x_{1}\equiv x
,
x_{2}\equiv y
, and the orthonormal basis vectors

\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
’s along the principal axes to find that

|\mathbf{x}\rangle=x_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle.

(11)

The parametric equation (
10
) can be represented by

\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}|\mathbf{x}\rangle=1,

(12)

where
\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}
is the tensor operator defined by

\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}=\alpha_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|+\beta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|.

(13)

The operator
\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}
is a special case of
\gamma=0

in a general symmetric tensor operator in Eq. (
69
).
The subscript P indicates that the operator is written
in the frame whose coordinate axes are principal axes.
Here, the coefficients

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}},

(14a)

\displaystyle\beta_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{2}},

(14b)

are the
principal values

for the operator
\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}

corresponding to principal axes
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}
, respectively.

The operator for the inertia tensor for a two-dimensional rigid body must be of the form

\mathbf{I}=I_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|+I_{2}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|.

(15)

This is true for any two-dimensional rigid body
as well as the elliptic plate that we consider in this section.
The reason is that we can always construct an equivalent elliptic plate that has the
same principal axes with the same principal values.

III.2
Non-principal Axes

Figure 1:

An elliptic plate with semi-major axis
a
and semi-minor axis
b
.
The principal axes
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
’s of the plate
are related to
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
’s under rotation by an angle
\theta
.

We shall find that the operator
\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}
in Eq. (
13
)
acquires additional contributions in a frame of reference
whose axes are not principal.
We consider another set of orthonormal basis vectors
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
’s
that are related to the principal axes under rotation as shown in Fig.
1
:

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phantom{-}\cos\theta|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle+\sin\theta|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle,

(16)

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sin\theta|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle+\cos\theta|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle.

(17)

The corresponding directional cosines are given by

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle=\phantom{+}\cos\theta,&&\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle=\sin\theta,\\
\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle=-\sin\theta,&&\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle=\cos\theta.\end{matrix}

By multiplying the identity operators in Eq. (
3
) to both sides of Eq. (
13
),
we can express the operator
\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}

in terms of the basis kets
|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle
’s as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\,\mathbf{S}_{\textrm{P}}\mathbf{1}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle+\beta_{\textrm{P}}\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle\Big)|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}+\beta_{\textrm{P}})\mathbf{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}})(\mathbf{S}_{1}\cos 2\theta+\mathbf{S}_{2}\sin 2\theta)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle s_{0}\mathbf{1}+s_{1}\mathbf{S}_{1}+s_{2}\mathbf{S}_{2},

where
the operators
\mathbf{S}_{i}
’s are defined in Eqs. (
73
), (
74
),
and (
75
)
and

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}+\beta_{\textrm{P}}),

(19a)

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}})\cos 2\theta,

(19b)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}})\sin 2\theta.

(19c)

The off-diagonal contribution emerges
through the contribution proportional to
\mathbf{S}_{2}
.
Note that the parametrization in Eq. (
18
) is applicable to
any real symmetric rank-2 tensor operator.

Additionally,
s_{0}
and
s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}
are invariant separately:

\displaystyle\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}}|=\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon(\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}}),

(20)

\displaystyle s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha^{2}_{\textrm{P}}+\beta^{2}_{\textrm{P}}),

(21)

where
\epsilon
is the sign of
\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}}
:

\epsilon=\textrm{sgn}[\alpha_{\textrm{P}}-\beta_{\textrm{P}}].

(22)

The angle
\theta
can be determined as

\displaystyle\cos 2\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon s_{1}}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}},

(23)

\displaystyle\sin 2\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon s_{2}}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}},

(24)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}.

(25)

The principal values can be expressed as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}+\epsilon\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}},

(26)

\displaystyle\beta_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}-\epsilon\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}},

(27)

which correspond, respectively, to the principal axes

\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}
.

III.3
Diagonalization

We determine the angle to diagonalize
a real symmetric rank-2 tensor operator
\mathbf{S}
in Eq. (
69
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=s_{0}\mathbf{1}+s_{1}\mathbf{S}_{1}+s_{2}\mathbf{S}_{2},

(28)

to reach the form in Eq. (
13
). In most textbooks, the readers are advised to solve the eigenvalue problem
to find the principal axes.
Although such an approach is mathematically straightforward, it does not tend to provide
students with physical intuitions. Thus, one must
investigate the solution further to interpret the physical meaning even after obtaining the result.
Here, we rotate the orthonormal basis vectors from

\{\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\}

to

\{\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\}
as shown in Fig.
1
as

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\theta|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle-\sin\theta|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle,

(29)

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin\theta|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle+\cos\theta|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle.

(30)

Substituting these into Eq. (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{0}+s_{1}\cos 2\theta+s_{2}\sin 2\theta)|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|

(31)

\displaystyle+(s_{0}-s_{1}\cos 2\theta-s_{2}\sin 2\theta)|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|

\displaystyle+(-s_{1}\sin 2\theta+s_{2}\cos 2\theta)(|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|+|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|).\phantom{X}

The operator
\mathbf{S}
reaches the principal form in which the crossing terms

|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|
and

|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|
vanish:

\mathbf{S}=\alpha_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}|+\beta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}|,

(32)

where
\alpha_{\textrm{P}}
and
\beta_{\textrm{P}}
are principal values in Eq. (
14
).
The vanishing condition is satisfied when

s_{1}\sin 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}=s_{2}\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}.

(33)

The angle
\theta_{\textrm{P}}
can
always be chosen as

\theta_{\textrm{P}}=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}},\quad 0\leq\theta_{\textrm{P}}\leq\pi/2.

(34)

A convenient choice of the solution set is

(\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}},\sin 2\theta_{\textrm{P}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}}(s_{1},s_{2}).

(35)

The principal values are determined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}+s_{1}\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}+s_{2}\sin 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}+\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}},

\displaystyle\beta_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}-s_{1}\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}-s_{2}\sin 2\theta_{\textrm{P}}

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{0}-\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}},

where we have made use of the solution set in Eq. (
35
).

By making use of the trigonometric identity
\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta=\cos 2\theta

and applying the choice in Eq. (
34
),
we can always determine
\cos\theta_{\text{P}}
and
\sin\theta_{\text{P}}
uniquely as

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}})}

(38a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\frac{s_{1}}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}}\right]},

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{\textrm{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\cos 2\theta_{\textrm{P}})}

(38b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{s_{1}}{\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}}\right]}.

The corresponding principal axes are

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phantom{-}\cos\theta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle+\sin\theta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle,

(39)

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sin\theta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle+\cos\theta_{\textrm{P}}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle.

(40)

Our determination of the rotation matrix that diagonalizes
the symmetric tensor operator is manifest. This approach is
particularly convenient and intuitive in determining the eigenvector.

IV
Inertia Tensor of an Ellipsoid

In Sec.
III
, we found that the parametric equation
for the ellipse can be expressed minimally by choosing
the principal axes as the coordinate axes. The same principal axes
can be used to express the inertia tensor that shares the principal axes
because it is a fundamental property of symmetric tensor operators.
In this section, we compute the inertia tensor operators for uniform ellipsoids in the three- and
N
-dimensional Euclidean spaces.
Because the inertia tensor operator for the elliptic plate
can be obtained from the three-dimensional counterpart,
we omit that calculation.

IV.1
Ellipsoid in Three Dimensions

As an application, we compute the inertia tensor for a uniform ellipsoid of mass
M
in three-dimensional Euclidean space.
The parametric equation (
10
) can be generalized to
\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}/a_{i}^{2}=1
. Then the mass distribution is

\rho(\textbf{x},\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3})=\frac{3M}{4\pi a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\Theta[1-\langle\textbf{x}|\mathbf{E}|\textbf{x}\rangle],

(41)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function and
\mathbf{E}
is a symmetric tensor operator

\mathbf{E}=\frac{1}{a_{i}^{2}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|.

(42)

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
are the orthonormal basis vectors along the principal axes of the ellipsoid.
Because a real symmetric rank-2 tensor operator in three dimensions can be expressed as
Eq. (
82
), the inertia tensor operator must be expanded as

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\rho(\textbf{x},\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3})\big[\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle\mathbf{1}-|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\,\big]

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|+\sum_{i<j}\gamma_{ij}\mathbf{S}_{ij},

where

\mathbf{S}_{ij}=|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}|+|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|
.
The scalar factors in Eq. (
43
) can be read off as

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle,

(44)

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle.

(45)

Therefore, the matrix element

I_{ij}=\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle

of the inertia tensor

\mathbbm{I}

can be determined
as

I_{ij}=\alpha_{k}q_{ki}q_{kj}+\sum_{k<\ell}\gamma_{k\ell}\left(q_{ki}q_{\ell j}+q_{\ell i}q_{kj}\right),

(46)

where
q_{ki}\equiv\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle=\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
.

Let us consider the scalar integrals for
\alpha_{i}
and
\gamma_{ij}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3M}{4\pi a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}\,(\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle-\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle^{2}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3M}{4\pi a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle,

(47)

where

\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}\equiv\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}\,\Theta[1-\langle\textbf{x}|\mathbf{E}|\textbf{x}\rangle].

(48)

Here, we denote
V
by the region inside the ellipsoid. If we define

\mathbf{y}=\frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\mathbf{x}\rangle}{a_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i},

(49)

then the region
V
is
parametrized by

\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{y}\rangle\leq 1.

(50)

In addition, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{y},

(51a)

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\textbf{x}\rangle=a_{k}^{2}\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\rangle^{2},

(51b)

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{i}^{2}\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle^{2},

(51c)

where
i
in the last line is not summed over.

Making a change of variable in Eq. (
47
) by using the identities in Eq. (
51
)
and carrying out the integration, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3M}{4\pi a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}\,(\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle-\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3M}{4\pi}a_{k}^{2}(1-\delta_{ki})\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\rangle^{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3M}{4\pi a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{x}\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

where

\gamma_{ij}=0
because the integrand is an odd function of both

\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle
and

\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle
while the region
V
is even in those variables.
Note that the factor
(1-\delta_{ki})
in
\alpha_{i}
is the projection operator for the direction perpendicular to
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
.
The isotropy of the Euclidean space guarantees that

\displaystyle\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{V}d^{3}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\mathbf{y}\rangle

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{15}.

As a result, the principal moments are determined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}M(a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}),

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}M(a_{3}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5}M(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}).

(54)

IV.2
Ellipsoid in
N
Dimensions

As a pedagogical example, we evaluate the inertia tensor for a uniform ellipsoid of mass
M
in
N
-dimensional Euclidean space.
The parametric equation of the ellipsoid in
N
dimensions is

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}^{2}}=1.

(55)

The volume of the ellipsoid is given by

V_{N}=\int d^{N}\mathbf{x}\,\Theta[1-\langle\textbf{x}|\mathbf{E}_{N}|\textbf{x}\rangle],

(56)

where
\mathbf{E}_{N}
is a symmetric tensor operator

\mathbf{E}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{2}}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|.

(57)

Note that
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
are the orthonormal basis vectors along the principal axes of the ellipsoid.
Introducing the rescaled vector
\mathbf{y}
which is the
N
-dimensional
generalization of Eq. (
49
), we find that

\displaystyle V_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\right)\int d^{N}\mathbf{y}\,\Theta[1-\mathbf{y}^{2}]

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\right)\Omega_{N}\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{N-1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{N}}{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i},

where
\Omega_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional solid angle
Ee-2017

\displaystyle\Omega_{N}=\frac{2\pi^{\tfrac{1}{2}N}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}N)}.

(59)

The mass distribution is

\rho(\textbf{x},\mathbf{q}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{q}_{N})=\frac{NM}{\Omega_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}}\Theta[1-\langle\textbf{x}|\mathbf{E}_{N}|\textbf{x}\rangle].

(60)

Because the inertia tensor operator is symmetric, it must be expanded as

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{N}\mathbf{x}\,\rho(\textbf{x},\mathbf{q}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{q}_{N})\big[\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle\mathbf{1}-|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\,\big]

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|+\sum_{i<j}\gamma_{ij}\mathbf{S}_{ij},

where

\mathbf{S}_{ij}=|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}|+|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|.

The scalar factors in Eq. (
61
) can be determined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle,

(62)

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle.

(63)

Therefore, the matrix element

I_{ij}=\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle

of the inertia tensor

\mathbbm{I}
can be computed
as

I_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}q_{ki}q_{kj}+\sum_{k<\ell}\gamma_{k\ell}\left(q_{ki}q_{\ell j}q_{\ell i}q_{kj}\right),

(64)

where
q_{ki}\equiv\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle=\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
.

We evaluate the scalar integrals for
\alpha_{i}
and
\gamma_{ij}
as

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{NM}{\Omega_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}}\int_{V}d^{N}\mathbf{x}\,(\langle\textbf{x}|\textbf{x}\rangle-\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle^{2})

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{NM}{\Omega_{N}}a_{k}^{2}(1-\delta_{ki})\int_{V}d^{N}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\rangle^{2},

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{NM}{\Omega_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}}\int_{V}d^{N}\mathbf{x}\langle\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}|\textbf{x}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

where
\gamma_{ij}=0
because both

\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}\rangle
and

\langle\textbf{x}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\rangle
in
the integrand are odd while the region
V
is even in those variables.
We notice that
(1-\delta_{ki})
is the projection operator for the direction perpendicular to
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
.
By making use of the isotropy of the Euclidean space,
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{V}d^{N}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{V}d^{N}\mathbf{y}\,\langle\textbf{y}|\mathbf{y}\rangle

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{N}}{N}\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{N+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{N}}{N(N+2)}.

Therefore,

\alpha_{i}=\frac{M}{N+2}\sum_{k\neq i}a_{k}^{2},

(68)

which reproduces the result in Eq. (
54
) when
N=3
.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have employed Dirac’s bra-ket
notation
Dirac-1923
;
Dirac-1939
;
Dirac-1958
to define
the inertia tensor operator
\mathbf{I}
in Eq. (
8
).
This operator is independent of the choice of bases or coordinate system.
The matrix elements
I_{ij}
of the inertia tensor can be read off by applying the
bra and the ket for the unit basis vectors
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
as

I_{ij}=\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{I}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle
.
The computation of the
matrix element is minimal when the principal axes are taken to be the bases
because one needs only to compute the
three fundamental scalar integrals
I_{i}
that are the principal moments.

We have considered an elliptic plate, which has the simplest geometry without having
rotational symmetry in two dimensions.
The principal axes and the corresponding principal values for the elliptic plate
have been determined only based on the geometry through
the introduction of the symmetric operator
that satisfies the parametric equation of the ellipse.
It is worthwhile to mention that the inertia tensor operator of any planar lamina can always be represented by
the symmetric tensor of the elliptic plate that has the same principal values.
By making use of a general symmetric tensor operator in Eq. (
69
),
we have constructed the method of diagonalization without solving an eigenvalue problem.
This approach is particularly convenient and intuitive in determining the eigenvector.

As applications, we have determined the inertia tensor operators
for uniform ellipsoids in three and
N
dimensions.
The bra-ket approach allows one to express the tensor operator in a compact form,
and one has to evaluate only the minimal number of the scalar integrals,
the principal moments of inertia.
We have demonstrated that
the bra-ket notation interplayed with various rescaling of the state kets
greatly simplifies the evaluation of the scalar integrals corresponding to
the principal moments of the
N
-dimensional ellipsoid.

The bra-ket approach, which is independent of the coordinate choice is naturally extended to the covariant approach that is given, for example, in
Ee-2017
. The exploitation of the bra-ket notation to compute the inertia tensor in classical mechanics
should provide undergraduate students with
a strong background
that may result in a smooth transition to attack abstract quantum mechanical problems.

Appendix A
Basis Tensor Operator in Two Dimensions

In two dimensions, at most two linearly independent vectors are available.
By making use of the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization procedure,
we can always express the operator in terms of two orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
.
We set
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}
to be the orthonormal basis vectors along these
directions.
The real symmetric tensor operator must be of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|+\beta|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|+\gamma(|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|+|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|)

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i},

where
s_{i}
is a scalar

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta),

(70)

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta),

(71)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma.

(72)

Also, the operators
\mathbf{S}_{i}
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|+|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|=\mathbf{1},

(73)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|-|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|,

(74)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|+|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|.

(75)

From these definitions, we find that

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{1}),

(76)

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{S}_{1}).

(77)

These basis tensor operators
\mathbf{S}_{i}
’s have a common feature:

\mathbf{S}_{i}^{2}=\mathbf{1},

(78)

where
i=0
, 1, 2 and the vector index
i
is not summed over.

Their matrix representations

\mathbbm{S}_{k}\equiv(\langle\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|\mathbf{S}_{k}|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle)
are

\displaystyle\mathbbm{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{+}0\\
0&\phantom{+}1\end{pmatrix},

(79)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{+}0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(80)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&\phantom{+}1\\
1&\phantom{+}0\end{pmatrix}.

(81)

Note that only
\mathbbm{S}_{0}
is traceful, and
that the remaining matrices
\mathbbm{S}_{1}
and
\mathbbm{S}_{2}
are both symmetric and traceless.
The last two matrices are identical to the Pauli sigma matrices:

\mathbbm{S}_{1}=\bbsigma_{3}
and
\mathbbm{S}_{2}=\bbsigma_{1}
.
