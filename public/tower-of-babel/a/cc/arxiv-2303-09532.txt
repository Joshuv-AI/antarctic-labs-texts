Variational Principles for Mirror Descent and Mirror Langevin Dynamics
Belinda Tzen, Anant Raj, Maxim Raginsky, Francis Bach

Abstract
Mirror descent, introduced by Nemirovski and Yudin in the 1970s, is a primal-dual convex optimization method that can be tailored to the geometry of the optimization problem at hand through the choice of a strongly convex potential function. It arises as a basic primitive in a variety of applications, including large-scale optimization, machine learning, and control. This paper proposes a variational formulation of mirror descent and of its stochastic variant, mirror Langevin dynamics. The main idea, inspired by the classic work of Brezis and Ekeland on variational principles for gradient flows, is to show that mirror descent emerges as a closed-loop solution for a certain optimal control problem, and the Bellman value function is given by the Bregman divergence between the initial condition and the global minimizer of the objective function.



I
Introduction

The continuous-time gradient flow

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla f(x(t)),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}

(1)

for a
C^{1}
objective function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a basic primitive in continuous-time optimization and control. Under additional assumptions on the objective
f
, the trajectory of (
1
) converges to a minimizer of
f
, which justifies thinking of the gradient flow as a method for asymptotically solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize }f(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(2)

However, apart from the local characterization of
-\nabla f(x)
as the “direction of steepest descent,” there appears to be little discussion of the sense, if any, in which (
1
) is “optimal” among all dynamical systems that asymptotically solve (
2
).

One of the few exceptions is the variational principle of Brezis and Ekeland
[
1
,
2
]
: Fix an arbitrary time horizon
T>0
. Then, among all absolutely continuous curves
x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
x(0)=x_{0}
, the trajectory of (
1
) on
[0,T]
minimizes the action functional

\displaystyle S(x(\cdot))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int^{T}_{0}\{f(x(t))+f^{*}(-\dot{x}(t))\}\dif t+\frac{1}{2}|x(T)|^{2},

(3)

where

\displaystyle f^{*}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\{\langle v,x\rangle-f(x)\}

is the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate of
f
and
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The minimum value of
S
over all such
x(\cdot)
is equal to
\frac{1}{2}|x_{0}|^{2}
. The underlying idea is simple and boils down to a careful analysis of the equality cases of the Fenchel–Young inequality

\displaystyle f(x)+f^{*}(v)\geq\langle v,x\rangle.

However, the Brezis–Ekeland variational principle does not say anything about the asymptotic behavior of the extremal trajectories or about the curious fact that a finite-horizon problem of minimizing the action (
3
) has a solution given by the flow of a time-invariant dynamical system.

In this paper, we revisit this problem from a control-theoretic point of view and provide a new variational interpretation of the gradient flow as an
infinite-horizon stabilizing optimal control

[
3
, §8.5]
. Moreover, we consider a more general method of
mirror descent
. This method, introduced in the 1970s by Nemirovski and Yudin
[
4
, Ch. 3]
, can be tailored to the geometry of the optimization problem at hand through the choice of a strongly convex
potential function
. In continuous time, mirror descent is implemented by a time-invariant dynamical system whose state
x(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and output
y(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
evolve according to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}(t)&=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t))),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}\\
y(t)&=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t))\end{split}

(4)

where
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
the potential function and
\varphi^{*}
is its Legendre–Fenchel conjugate. It is common to refer to
x(t)
and
y(t)
, respectively, as the
dual-space
and the
primal-space
trajectories. (The Euclidean gradient flow (
1
) is a special case of (
4
) with
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
.)

I-A

Brief summary of contributions

We show the following: Suppose that the objective
f
is strictly convex. For the controlled system
\dot{x}(t)=u(t),y(t)=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t))
, we consider the class of all
stabilizing controls

[
3
, §8.5]
, i.e., appropriately well-behaved functions
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that the output
y(t)
converges, as
t\to\infty
, to the unique minimizer of
f
. This class contains, among others, sufficiently smooth state feedback controls of the form
u(t)=k(x(t))
. We then identify an instantaneous cost function
q(x,u)
, closely related to the Lagrangian in (
3
), such that the state feedback law
k(x)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))
[or, equivalently, the output feedback law
\tilde{k}(y)=-\nabla f(y)
] gives a control that minimizes the infinite-horizon cost

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{0}q(x(t),u(t))\dif t

(5)

over all stabilizing controls. Moreover, the value function
V(x_{0})
, i.e., the minimum value of this cost as a function of the initial state
x_{0}
, is given by a certain “distance,” induced by the potential
\varphi
, between the initial output
y_{0}=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x_{0})
and the unique minimizer of
f
. At the same time,
V(x)
is the Lyapunov function for the closed-loop system
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
.

We also consider a stochastic variant of mirror descent, the so-called
mirror Langevin dynamics

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, which is implemented by an Itô stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\!\!\!\dif X_{t}&=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))\dif t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))^{-1}}\dif W_{t}\\
\!\!\!Y_{t}&=\nabla\varphi^{*}(X_{t})\end{split}

(6)

driven by a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, where
\varepsilon>0
is a small “temperature” parameter. In this setting, we consider a finite-horizon optimal control problem of minimizing the expected cost

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{0}q(X_{t},u_{t})\dif t+r(X_{T})\Bigg|X_{0}=x_{0}\Bigg],

over all admissible control processes
(u_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
entering into the controlled SDE

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}=u_{t}\dif t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))^{-1}}\dif W_{t}.

Here,
q
is the same cost as in (
5
) and
r
is an appropriately chosen terminal cost. As in the deterministic case, the mirror Langevin dynamics (
6
) emerges as the closed-loop system corresponding to the optimal control, although the value function is now time-dependent.

II
The deterministic problem

II-A

Some preliminaries

We assume that the objective function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{1}
and strictly convex and that the potential function
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex. We let
\bar{y}
denote the unique global minimizer of
f
. Both
f
and
\varphi
are assumed to be of
Legendre type
, i.e.,
|\nabla f(x)|,|\nabla\varphi(x)|\to+\infty
as
|x|\to+\infty
. As a consequence (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Thm. 26.5]
), the gradient map
\nabla\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is bijective, with
(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}=\nabla\varphi^{*}
. The potential
\varphi
and its conjugate
\varphi^{*}
induce the so-called
Bregman divergences

\displaystyle\begin{split}D_{\varphi}(y,y^{\prime})&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi(y)-\varphi(y^{\prime})-\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{\prime}),y-y^{\prime}\rangle\\
D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,x^{\prime})&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi^{*}(x)-\varphi^{*}(x^{\prime})-\langle\nabla\varphi^{*}(x^{\prime}),x-x^{\prime}\rangle\end{split}

(7)

and the following relation holds for any pair of points
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and their “mirror images”
x=\nabla\varphi(y),x^{\prime}=\nabla\varphi(y^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}(y,y^{\prime})=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x^{\prime},x)

(cf.
[
9
, §11.2]
for details). We also require that, for each fixed
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the map
x\mapsto D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,x^{\prime})
is
radially unbounded
, i.e.,
D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,x^{\prime})\to+\infty
as
|x|\to+\infty
. This will be satisfied, for example, if
\varphi
is strongly convex, i.e., there exists some
\alpha>0
, such that

\displaystyle\varphi(y^{\prime})\geq\varphi(y)+\langle\nabla\varphi(y),y^{\prime}-y\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}|y^{\prime}-y|^{2}

(8)

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Radial unboundedness is needed for the invocation of the Lyapunov criterion for global asymptotic stability
[
3
, Sec. 5.7]
.

Remark 1
.

We assume that
\varphi
is finite on all of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
mainly to keep the exposition simple. It is not hard to adapt the analysis to the case when the potential function
\varphi
is defined on a closed convex set
\mathsf{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with nonempty interior, and
|\nabla\varphi(x)|\to+\infty
as
x
approaches any point on the boundary of
\mathsf{X}
. This corresponds to the problem of minimizing
f(x)
subject to the constraint
x\in\mathsf{X}
.

II-B

Infinite-horizon optimal stabilizing controls

Consider the time-invariant controlled dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=u(t).

(9)

For any
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
denote the class of all
stabilizing controls
at
x_{0}
, i.e., all locally essentially bounded maps
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, such that the trajectory
x(t)
of (
9
) with
x(0)=x_{0}
is defined for all
t\geq 0
and
x(t)\to\bar{x}
as
t\to\infty
, where
\bar{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\nabla\varphi(\bar{y})
. We would like to minimize the cost

\displaystyle J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int^{\infty}_{0}q(x(t),u(t))\dif t,

over all
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
, where

\displaystyle q(x,u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))+f^{*}(-u)+\langle u,\bar{y}\rangle.

(10)

The class
\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
is evidently nonempty since, for example, the control
u(t)=(\bar{x}-x_{0}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq t\leq 1\}}
is stabilizing at
x_{0}
. We denote by
V(x_{0})
the
value function
, i.e., infimum of
J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))
over all
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
.

II-C

The main result

Let
V(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,\bar{x})
. Theorem
1
, stated and proved below, states that
V
is the value function for the above infinite-horizon optimal control problem, and that the mirror descent dynamics (
4
) is the closed-loop system corresponding to an optimal stabilizing control. Moreover, the value function
V
is also a global Lyapunov function for (
4
), and the point
\bar{x}
is its global asymptotically stable equilibrium. The proof makes essential use of the following lemma, which we will also need at several points in the sequel:

Lemma 1
.

The function
V
has the following properties:

1.

It is
C^{2}
and strictly convex.

2.

V(\bar{x})=0
, and
V(x)>0
for
x\neq\bar{x}
.

3.

V(x)\to+\infty
as
|x|\to+\infty
.

Moreover, the following inequality holds for
\dot{V}(x,u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\nabla V(x),u\rangle
:

\displaystyle\dot{V}(x,u)+q(x,u)\geq 0,\qquad x,u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

(11)

and equality is attained iff
u=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))
.

Proof.

Items 1)–3) are immediate consequences of our assumptions on
\varphi
. Moreover, a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\dot{V}(x,u)+q(x,u)=f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))+f^{*}(-u)+\langle u,\nabla\varphi^{*}(x)\rangle,

which is nonnegative by the Fenchel–Young inequality. The equality condition in (
11
) follows by
[
8
, Thm. 23.5]
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

We have the following:

1.

For any stabilizing control
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
and for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}q(x(t),u(t))\dif t\geq V(x_{0})-V(x(t)).

(12)

In particular,
J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))\geq V(x_{0})
.

2.

For each
x_{0}
, the closed-loop system
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
gives rise to an optimal stabilizing control
u(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
, such that

\displaystyle J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))=V(x_{0})=D_{\varphi}(x_{0},\bar{x}).

Moreover,
V(x)
is a global Lyapunov function for the closed-loop system.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be given and consider an arbitrary stabilizing control
u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}_{x_{0}}
. Then, for
\dot{x}(t)=u(t)
with
x(0)=x_{0}
we have

\displaystyle V(x(t))-V(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\dif}{\dif s}V(x(s))\dif s

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}\dot{V}(x(s),u(s))\dif s

\displaystyle\geq-\int^{t}_{0}q(x(s),u(s))\dif s,

where the last step follows from (
11
). Rearranging gives (
12
). Moreover, taking the limit as
t\to\infty
and using the fact that
V(x(t))\to 0
as
t\to\infty
since
u(\cdot)
is stabilizing, we get the inequality
J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))\geq V(x)
.

Next, consider the closed-loop system
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
,
x(0)=x_{0}
, that generates the mirror descent flow. Then
\bar{x}
is evidently an equilibrium point since
\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(\bar{x}))=\nabla f(\bar{y})=0
. Letting
y(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t))
and using (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif t}V(x(t))

\displaystyle=\dot{V}(x(t),-\nabla f(y(t)))

\displaystyle=-q(x(t),-\nabla f(y(t)))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(y(t)),\bar{y}\rangle-f^{*}(\nabla f(y(t)))-f(y(t))

\displaystyle\leq f(\bar{y})-f(y(t)),

which is strictly negative whenever
y(t)\neq\bar{y}
by the strict convexity of
f
, or, equivalently, whenever
x(t)\neq\bar{x}
since
\nabla\varphi^{*}
is a bijection. Together with Lemma
1
, this shows that
V
is a global Lyapunov function
[
3
, Def. 5.7.1]
for the above closed-loop system, so
\bar{x}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
[
3
, Thm. 17]
. Thus, the control
u(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
is stabilizing at
x_{0}
, and
J_{\infty}(x_{0},u(\cdot))=V(x_{0})
from the equality condition in (
11
).
∎

II-D

Quantitative estimates

Theorem
1
allows us to obtain quantitative estimates on the approach of the trajectory of (
4
) to equilibrium. While similar estimates have been given in some earlier works
[
10
,
11
]
, the appeal of our optimal control perspective is that it allows to obtain such guarantees in a unified manner. It will be useful to introduce the following definition
[
12
]
: We say that the objective function
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex
(
\mu\geq 0
) w.r.t. the potential function
\varphi
if

\displaystyle f(y^{\prime})\geq f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),y^{\prime}-y\rangle+\mu D_{\varphi}(y^{\prime},y),\,\,y,y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(Obviously, if
\mu=0
, this is simply convexity; when
\mu>0
, the function
f
has some nonzero “curvature” in some neighborhood of each point
x
, where the “geometry” is determined by the potential
\varphi
.)

Theorem 2
.

Let
(x(t),y(t))
,
t\geq 0
, be the state and the output trajectories of the mirror descent dynamics (
4
) starting from
x(0)=x_{0}
and
y(0)=y_{0}=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x_{0})
. Then the following holds for every
t>0
:

1.

If
f
is convex, then

\displaystyle f(y(t))-f(\bar{y})\leq\frac{1}{t}D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0}).

(13)

2.

If
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t.
\varphi
then

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y(t))\leq D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})e^{-\mu t},

(14)

and in that case the system (
4
) is exponentially stable.

Proof.

Let
u(t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x(t)))
be the state feedback law that achieves
V(x_{0})
. Then

\displaystyle q(x(t),u(t))

\displaystyle=-\dot{V}(x(t),u(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(y(t)),y(t)-\bar{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=f(y(t))-f(\bar{y})+D_{f}(\bar{y},y(t)).

where the first equality is by Lemma
1
and the last equality follows by rearranging and using the definition
D_{f}(y,y^{\prime})=f(y)-f(y^{\prime})-\langle f(y^{\prime}),y-y^{\prime}\rangle
. Therefore, using Theorem
1
and the fact that
D_{f}(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle V(x_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\int^{t}_{0}q(x(s),u(s))\dif s

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}\{f(y(s))-f(\bar{y})\}\dif s

\displaystyle\geq t\big(f(y(t))-f(\bar{y})\big),

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the value of the objective
f
decreases along the output trajectory
y(t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif t}f(y(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(y(t)),\dot{y}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(y(t)),\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x(t))\dot{x}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla f(y(t)),\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x(t))\nabla f(y(t))\rangle\leq 0

— since
\varphi^{*}
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, its Hessian
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)
is positive definite for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Dividing by
t
and using the fact that
V(x_{0})=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x_{0},\bar{x})=D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})
, we get (
13
).

When
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif t}V(x(t))

\displaystyle=\dot{V}(x(t),u(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(y(t)),\bar{y}-y(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq f(\bar{y})-f(y(t))-\mu D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y(t))

\displaystyle=f(\bar{y})-f(y(t))-\mu D_{\varphi^{*}}(x(t),\bar{x})

\displaystyle=f(\bar{y})-f(y(t))-\mu V(x(t)).

Integrating gives the estimate

\displaystyle V(x(t))\leq e^{-\mu t}V(x_{0})+\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\mu(t-s)}\{f(\bar{y})-f(y(s))\}\dif s

which yields (
14
) since
f(\bar{y})\leq f(y)
for all
y
.
∎

II-E

A simple example

As a simple illustration, consider the quadratic objective
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}|Ax-b|^{2}
with
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and the quadratic potential
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
. Assume that
A^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}A
is nonsingular. Then the instantaneous cost
q(x,u)
in (
10
) takes the form

\displaystyle q(x,u)=\frac{1}{2}|Ax-b|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle u,(A^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}A)^{-1}u\rangle-\frac{1}{2}|A\bar{y}-b|^{2},

where
\bar{y}=(A^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}A)^{-1}A^{\hbox{\it\tiny T}}b
is the unique minimizer of
f
. Thus, the control-theoretic interpretation of the gradient flow for this problem naturally leads to infinite-horizon optimal stabilization of a linear system with a quadratic cost.

III
The stochastic problem

We now consider a stochastic version of continuous-time mirror descent, dubbed
mirror Langevin dynamics
, or MLD
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. The MLD generates a pair of random trajectories
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
according to (
6
). The words “Langevin dynamics” allude to the fact that, with the quadratic potential
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
, (
6
) reduces to the usual Langevin dynamics

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})\dif t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\dif W_{t}.

The use of MLD is mainly in the context of sampling, where one makes use of the fact that the steady-state probability density of
Y_{t}
is proportional to
e^{-f/\varepsilon}
. Moreover, since this limiting density concentrates on the set of global minimizers of
f
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the sampling problem is intimately related to the problem of minimizing
f
.

III-A

Some preliminaries

In addition to the conditions imposed on
f
and
\varphi
in Sec.
II-A
, we also assume the following:

•

the objective function
f
has a Lipschitz-continuous gradient;

•

the potential function
\varphi
is
C^{2}
, strongly convex, cf. (
8
), and has the
modified self-concordance property

[
7
]
, i.e., there exists some constant
c>0
, such that

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)}-\sqrt{\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{\prime})}\|_{2}\leq c|x-x^{\prime}|

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the
2
-Schatten (or Hilbert–Schmidt) norm.

In particular, the above assumption on
\varphi
implies that
\varphi^{*}
has a Lipschitz-continuous gradient
[
13
, Thm. 4.2.1]
and that the map
x\mapsto\sqrt{(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x))^{-1}}
is Lipschitz-continuous
[
7
]
.

III-B

A finite-horizon optimal control problem

We work in the usual setting of controlled diffusion processes
[
14
, §VI.3-4]
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbf{P}})
be a probability space with a complete and right-continuous filtration, and let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a standard
n
-dimensional
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-Brownian motion. Let a finite horizon
0<T<\infty
be given. An
admissible control
(of state feedback type) is any measurable function
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, such that the Itô SDE

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}=u(X_{t},t)\dif t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))^{-1}}\dif W_{t}

(15)

has a unique strong solution for all
t\in[0,T]
and for any deterministic initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}
(cf.
[
15
, §5.2]
for details). For each
t\in[0,T]
define the
expected cost-to-go

\displaystyle J(x,t;u(\cdot))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{t}q(X_{s},u_{s})\dif s+D_{\varphi^{*}}(X_{T},\bar{x})\Bigg|X_{t}=x\Bigg],

(16)

with the instantaneous cost
q
the same as in (
10
), where
u_{t}
is shorthand for
u(X_{t},t)
, and let

\displaystyle V(x,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{u(\cdot)\text{ admissible}}J(x,t;u(\cdot))

(17)

be the value function. We say that an admissible control
u(\cdot)
is optimal if
J(x,t;u(\cdot))=V(x,t)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and all
t\in[0,T]
. Observe that, in contrast with the deterministic infinite-horizon problem posed in Sec.
II-B
, here we are dealing with a finite-horizon stochastic problem, and there is, in addition to the instantaneous cost
q
, also a terminal cost
D_{\varphi^{*}}(\cdot,\bar{x})
. The form of the performance criterion in (
III-B
) is reminiscent of the Brezis–Ekeland action functional (
3
).

III-C

The main result

Theorem 3
.

The value function in (
17
) is equal to

\displaystyle V(x,t)=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,\bar{x})+\varepsilon n(T-t),

(18)

and the feedback control
u(x,t)=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi(x))
is optimal.

Proof.

We use the verification theorem from the theory of controlled diffusions
[
14
, §VI.4]
. We associate to the controlled diffusion process (
15
) a family of infinitesimal generators
(\mathcal{L}^{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where
\mathcal{L}^{u}
is the second-order linear differential operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}u_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\varepsilon\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\big(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)\big)^{-1}_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

Then it is readily verified that the function
V
defined in (
18
) is a solution of the
Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V(x,t)+\min_{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\{\mathcal{L}^{u}V(x,t)+q(x,u)\big\}=0

(19)

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[0,T]
with the terminal condition
V(x,T)=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,\bar{x})
. Indeed, since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon n,

\displaystyle\nabla V(x,t)

\displaystyle=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x)-\nabla\varphi^{*}(\bar{x}),

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}V(x,t)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)

we can follow the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
1
to show that, for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V(x,t)+\mathcal{L}^{u}V(x,t)+q(x,u)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon n+\langle u,\nabla V(x,t)\rangle+\varepsilon\,{\rm tr}\big\{(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x))^{-1}\nabla^{2}V(x,t)\big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))+f^{*}(-u)+\langle u,\nabla\varphi^{*}(\bar{x})\rangle

\displaystyle=f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))+f^{*}(-u)+\langle u,\nabla\varphi^{*}(x)\rangle\geq 0,

with equality iff
u=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))
. Thus,
V(x,t)
is a solution of the HJB equation (
19
), and evidently
V(x,T)=D_{\varphi^{*}}(x,\bar{x})
. Then, by Theorem 4.1 in
[
14
, §VI.4]
,
V(x,t)
is the value function in (
17
), and the control given by

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\big\{\mathcal{L}^{u}V(x,t)+q(x,u)\big\}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\big\{f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))+f^{*}(-u)+\langle u,\nabla\varphi^{*}(x)\big\}

\displaystyle=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))

is optimal (note that it is also time-invariant). This control is admissible since, by our assumptions on
f
and
\varphi
, the maps
b(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(x))
and
\sigma(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{2\varepsilon(\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x))^{-1}}
are Lipschitz-continuous and have at most linear growth, i.e., there exists a constant
K>0
, such that

\displaystyle|b(x)-b(x^{\prime})|+\|\sigma(x)-\sigma(x^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq K|x-x^{\prime}|,

\displaystyle|b(x)|^{2}+\|\sigma(x)\|^{2}_{2}\leq K(1+|x|^{2})

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Consequently, with the choice of
u(x,t)=b(x)
, the SDE (
15
) has a unique strong solution
[
15
, §5.2, Thm. 2.5]
, so
u(\cdot)
is indeed admissible.
∎

III-D

Quantitative estimates

Theorem 4
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t})
,
t\geq 0
, be the random state and output trajectories of the mirror Langevin dynamics (
6
) with deterministic initial condition
X_{0}=x_{0}
and
Y_{0}=y_{0}=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x_{0})
. Then the following holds for every
T>0
:

1.

If
f
is convex, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{0}\{f(Y_{t})-f(\bar{y})\}\dif t\Bigg|Y_{0}=y_{0}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{T}D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})+\varepsilon n.

(20)

2.

If
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t.
\varphi
, for
\mu>0
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},Y_{t})|Y_{0}=y_{0}]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})e^{-\mu t}+\frac{\varepsilon n}{\mu}(1-e^{-\mu T}).

(21)

Proof.

Let
u_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\nabla f(\nabla\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))
. Then, proceeding just like in the proof of Theorem
2
, we can write

\displaystyle q(X_{t},u_{t})

\displaystyle=f(Y_{t})+f^{*}(-u_{t})+\langle u_{t},\bar{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=f(Y_{t})-f(\bar{y})+D_{f}(\bar{y},Y_{t})

\displaystyle\geq f(Y_{t})-f(\bar{y}).

Using this together with Theorem
3
gives

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})+\varepsilon nT=D_{\varphi}(x_{0},\bar{x})+\varepsilon nT

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{0}q(X_{t},u_{t})\dif t+D_{\varphi}(X_{T},\bar{x})\Bigg|X_{0}=x_{0}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{0}\{f(Y_{t})-f(\bar{y})\}\dif t\Bigg|X_{0}=x_{0}\Bigg].

Dividing both sides by
T>0
and using the fact the
\sigma
-algebras
\sigma(X_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in[0,T])
and
\sigma(Y_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in[0,T])
coincide since
\nabla\varphi^{*}
is a bijection, we obtain (
1
).

When
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex, we have

\displaystyle f(\bar{y})-f(Y_{t})\geq\langle\nabla f(Y_{t}),\bar{y}-Y_{t}\rangle+\mu D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},Y_{t}).

(22)

On the other hand, by Itô’s lemma and by (
19
),

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!V(X_{t},t)

\displaystyle=V(X_{0},0)+\int^{t}_{0}\langle\nabla f(Y_{s}),\bar{y}-Y_{s}\rangle\dif s+M_{t},

(23)

where
M_{t}
is a zero-mean
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-martingale. Since

\displaystyle V(X_{s},s)

\displaystyle=D_{\varphi*}(X_{s},\bar{x})+\varepsilon n(T-s)

\displaystyle=D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},Y_{s})+\varepsilon n(T-s),

combining (
22
) and (
23
) and then taking expectations given
Y_{0}=y_{0}
yields

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},Y_{t})|Y_{0}=y_{0}]

\displaystyle\leq D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})+\varepsilon nt-\mu\int^{t}_{0}{\mathbf{E}}[D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},Y_{s})|Y_{0}=y_{0}]\dif s

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Grönwall’s inequality gives (
2
).
∎

Note that, in contrast with the deterministic setting (cf. Theorem
2
), when the objective function
f
is not strongly convex, we only have guarantees on the expected average objective
{\mathbf{E}}[\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}f(Y_{t})\dif t|Y_{0}=y_{0}]
, which, owing to the convexity of
f
, translates into an optimization error estimate for the time average of the trajectory,
\tilde{Y}_{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}Y_{t}\dif t
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f(\tilde{Y}_{T})-f(\bar{y})|Y_{0}=y_{0}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\int^{T}_{0}\{f(Y_{t})-f(\bar{y})\}\dif y\Bigg|Y_{0}=y_{0}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y_{0})+\varepsilon n.

However, as the following result shows, in the low-noise regime (i.e., for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
), with high probability, the MLD output trajectory
(Y_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
closely tracks the deterministic mirror-descent output trajectory
(y(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
with the same initial condition
Y_{0}=y(0)=y_{0}
:

Theorem 5
.

There exist positive time-independent constants
C_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, such that, for every
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{C_{1}T^{3}}e^{-C_{2}T}
, the following estimate holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|f(Y_{t})-f(y(t))|\leq\frac{C_{3}}{T}\sqrt{n\log\frac{n}{\delta}}.

(24)

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|Y_{t}-y(t)|
. The following estimate holds by the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
:

\displaystyle f(Y_{t})-f(y(t))\leq\langle\nabla f(y(t)),Y_{t}-y(t)\rangle+\frac{L_{f}}{2}|Y_{t}-y(t)|^{2},

where
L_{f}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
. Moreover, the gradient norms
|\nabla f(y(t))|
are uniformly bounded since

\displaystyle|\nabla f(y(t))|

\displaystyle\leq|\nabla f(y(t))-\nabla f(y(0))|+|\nabla f(y(0))|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}|y(t)-y(0)|+|\nabla f(y(0))|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}|y(t)-\bar{y}|+L_{f}|y(0)-\bar{y}|+|\nabla f(y(0))|

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{f}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}D_{\varphi}(\bar{y},y(0))}+|\nabla f(y(0))|=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K_{0},

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|f(y(t))-f(y(0))|\leq K_{0}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Delta_{t}+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Delta^{2}_{t}.

(25)

Define the matrix-valued process
(\xi_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
by
\xi_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(X_{t}))^{-1}}
. For each
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\displaystyle|X_{t}-x(t)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}\int^{t}_{0}|Y_{s}-y(s)|\dif s+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Bigg|\int^{t}_{0}\xi_{s}\dif W_{s}\Bigg|.

By our assumptions on
\varphi
, there exist positive constants
\kappa_{2}\geq\kappa_{1}>0
, such that the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)
lie in the interval
[\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}]
. Hence, the process
\xi_{t}
is uniformly bounded, so the quadratic variations of the matrix entries
[\xi^{ij}]_{t}
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
, are uniformly bounded by a positive multiple of
t
. Hence, by the time-change theorem for martingales
[
15
, §3.4, Thm. 4.6]
, there exist a constant
\kappa>0
and a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Bigg|\int^{t}_{0}\xi_{s}\dif W_{s}\Bigg|\leq\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|.

Since
\nabla\varphi^{*}
is Lipschitz-continuous, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\varphi^{*}}|X_{t}-x(t)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\varphi^{*}}L_{f}\int^{t}_{0}\Delta_{s}\dif s+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}L_{\varphi^{*}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|.

Grönwall’s inequality therefore gives

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Delta_{t}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}L_{\varphi^{*}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|e^{L_{\varphi^{*}}L_{f}T}

(26)

If
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\varphi^{*}}T^{3}}e^{-2L_{\varphi^{*}}L_{f}T}
, then, using (
26
) in (
25
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|f(Y_{t})-f(y(t))|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}K_{0}}{T^{3/2}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|+\frac{L_{f}}{T^{3}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|^{2}.

By the reflection principle for the Brownian motion
[
15
, p. 96]
, for every
r>0

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}\left\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq\kappa T}|B_{t}|\geq r\right\}\leq 2{\mathbf{P}}\left\{|B_{\kappa T}|\geq r\right\}\leq 4ne^{-r^{2}/2n\kappa T},

and therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|f(Y_{t})-f(y(t))|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{T}\sqrt{n\log\frac{n}{\delta}}

with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\tilde{C}
is a constant that depends on
K_{0},L_{f},\kappa
.
∎

IV
Conclusion and future directions

In this paper, we have presented an interpretation of deterministic and stochastic continuous-time mirror descent methods in the framework of “inverse optimal control”
[
16
]
—that is, given an autonomous (i.e., control-free) dynamical system, identify a controlled dynamical system and a cost criterion, such that the autonomous dynamics can be viewed as the closed-loop system corresponding to an optimal control. An intriguing direction for future research is to interpret other optimization methods, such as the heavy-ball method
[
17
]
, through the inverse optimal control lens.
