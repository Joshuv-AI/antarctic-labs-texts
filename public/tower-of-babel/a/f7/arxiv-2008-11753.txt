Abstract
. We answer an open complexity question by Hofman, Lasota, Mayr, Totzke (LMCS 2016) for simulation preorder on the class of succinct one-counter nets ( i.e. , one-counter automata with no zero tests where counter increments and decrements are integers written in binary); the problem was known to be PSPACE-hard and in EXPSPACE. We show that all relations between bisimulation equivalence and simulation preorder are EXPSPACE-hard for these nets; simulation preorder is thus EXPSPACE-complete. The result is proven by a reduction from reachability games whose EXPSPACE-completeness in the case of succinct one-counter nets was shown by Hunter (RP 2015), by using other results. We also provide a direct self-contained EXPSPACE-completeness proof for a special case of such reachability games, namely for a modification of countdown games that were shown EXPTIME-complete by Jurdzinski, Sproston, Laroussinie (LMCS 2008); in our modification the initial counter value is not given but is freely chosen by the first player. We also present an alternative proof for the upper bound by Hofman et al. In particular, we give a new simplified proof of the belt theorem that yields a simple graphic presentation of simulation preorder on (non-succinct) one-counter nets and leads to a polynomial-space algorithm (which is trivially extended to an exponential-space algorithm for succinct one-counter nets).

Introduction

One-counter automata (OCA),
i.e.
, finite automata
equipped with a nonnegative counter,
are studied as one of the simplest models
of infinite-state systems. They can be viewed as a special case of
Minsky counter machines, or as a special case of pushdown automata.
In general, OCA can test the value of
the counter for zero,
i.e.
, some transitions could be enabled only if the value
of the counter is zero. One-counter nets (OCN) are a “monotonic” subclass of OCA where every
transition enabled for zero is also enabled for nonzero values. As
usual, we can consider
deterministic, nondeterministic, and/or alternating versions of OCA
and/or OCN.
The basic versions are
unary

counter can be incremented and decremented by one in one step,
while in the
succinct
versions

(but fixed for a given transition);
as usual, the changes are assumed to be
written in binary in a description of a given automaton.
(Remark. In some papers the term “unary” is used differently,
for machines with a single-letter input alphabet.)

Problems that have been studied on OCA and OCN include reachability,
equivalence, model checking, and also different kinds of
games played on these automata.
One of the earliest results

(t,t^{\prime})\in R
;
if, moreover, for every
(s,s^{\prime})\in R
and every

s^{\prime}\xrightarrow{a}t^{\prime}
there is
s\xrightarrow{a}t
such that
(t,t^{\prime})\in R
, then
R

is a
bisimulation
.

The union of all simulations (on
S
) is the maximal
simulation, denoted
\mathop{\preceq}
; it is a preorder, called

simulation preorder
.
The union of all bisimulations is the maximal
bisimulation, denoted
\mathop{\sim}
; it is an equivalence, called

bisimulation equivalence
(or
bisimilarity
).
We obviously have

\mathop{\sim}\subseteq\mathop{\preceq}
.

We can write

s_{1}\mathop{\preceq}s_{2}
or
s_{1}\mathop{\sim}s_{2}
also for states
s_{1}
,

s_{2}
from different LTSs
\mathcal{L}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, in which case
the LTS arising by the disjoint union of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is
(implicitly) referred to.

It is useful to think in terms of two-player turn-based games,
played
by Attacker and Defender (or Spoiler and Duplicator).
A round
of the
simulation game
from a (current) pair
(s,s^{\prime})

proceeds as follows:
Attacker chooses
a transition
s\xrightarrow{a}t
, and Defender responds with some

s^{\prime}\xrightarrow{a}t^{\prime}
(for the action
a
chosen by Attacker); the play then
continues with another round, now from the current pair
(t,t^{\prime})
.
If a player has no legal move in a round, then the other player wins;
infinite plays are deemed to be Defender’s wins.
It is standard that
s\mathop{\not\preceq}s^{\prime}
iff Attacker has a winning
strategy from
(s,s^{\prime})
.

The
bisimulation game
is analogous, but
in any round starting from
(s,s^{\prime})

Attacker can choose to
play
s\xrightarrow{a}t
or
s^{\prime}\xrightarrow{a}t^{\prime}
, and Defender has to respond
with some
s^{\prime}\xrightarrow{a}t^{\prime}
or
s\xrightarrow{a}t
, respectively.
Here we have
s\mathop{\not\sim}s^{\prime}
iff Attacker has a winning
strategy from
(s,s^{\prime})
.

Stratified simulation, and ranks of pairs of states

Given

\mathcal{L}=(S,Act,(\xrightarrow{a})_{a\in Act})
,
we use (transfinite) induction to define the relations
\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda}

where
\lambda
ranges over the class
Ord
of ordinals.
We put
\mathop{\preceq}_{0}=S\times S
. For
\lambda>0
we have

s\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda}s^{\prime}
if for each transition
s\xrightarrow{a}t

and each
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
there is a
transition
s^{\prime}\xrightarrow{a}t^{\prime}
where
t\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda^{\prime}}t^{\prime}
.

We note that
\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\supseteq\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda}
when

\lambda^{\prime}\leq\lambda
, and that
\mathop{\preceq}=\bigcap_{\lambda\in Ord}\mathop{\preceq}_{\lambda}
. For each pair
(s,s^{\prime})\not\in\mathop{\preceq}
, we define
its
rank

\textsc{rank}(s,s^{\prime})
as the least ordinal
\lambda

such that
s\mathop{\not\preceq}_{\lambda}s^{\prime}
.
We note in particular that
\textsc{rank}(s,s^{\prime})=1
iff
s
enables an action
a
(i.e.,

s\xrightarrow{a}
) that is not enabled in
s^{\prime}
(i.e.,
s^{\prime}\not\xrightarrow{a}
).

Remark 1
.

We use such a general definition for the purpose of the general
reduction presented in Section
4
.
Otherwise we
consider just (special cases of) LTSs that
are
image-finite
(i.e., in which the sets

\{t\mid s\xrightarrow{a}t\}
are finite for all
s\in S
,
a\in Act
); in such
systems
we have
\mathop{\preceq}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\preceq}_{i}
and

\textsc{rank}(s,s^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}
for each
(s,s^{\prime})\not\in\mathop{\preceq}
.

We could define the analogous concepts for bisimulation equivalence as
well.

One-counter nets (OCNs and SOCNs), and their
associated LTSs

A
labelled one-counter net
,
or just a
one-counter net
or even
just an
OCN
for short,
is a triple

\mathcal{N}=(Q,Act,\delta),

where
Q
is the finite set of
control states
,
Act
the finite
set of
actions
,
and
\delta\subseteq Q\times Act\times\{{-}1,0,{+}1\}\times Q
is
the
set of (
labelled transition
)
rules
.
By allowing
\delta
to be
a finite subset of
Q\times Act\times\mathbb{Z}\times Q
,
and presenting
z\in\mathbb{Z}
in the rules
(q,a,z,q^{\prime})

in binary, we
get a
succinct one-counter net
, or a
SOCN
for short.
A rule
(q,a,z,q^{\prime})
is usually presented as

q\xrightarrow{a,z}q^{\prime}
.

Each OCN or SOCN
\mathcal{N}=(Q,Act,\delta)
has the
associated LTS

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}}=(Q\times\mathbb{N},Act,(\xrightarrow{a})_{a\in Act})

(1)

where
(q,m)\xrightarrow{a}(q^{\prime},n)
iff
q\xrightarrow{a,n-m}q^{\prime}
is a rule in
\delta
.
We often write a state
(q,m)
, which is also called a
configuration
,
in the form
q(m)
, and we view
m
as a value of a nonnegative counter.
A rule
q\xrightarrow{a,z}q^{\prime}
thus induces transitions
q(m)\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}(m{+}z)
for
all
m\geq\max\{0,{-}z\}
.

We remark that
one-counter automata
extend one-counter nets
by the ability to test zero, i.e., by transitions that are enabled only if the counter value is zero.

Reachability games (r-games), winning areas, ranks of states

We are interested in reachability games played in LTSs
associated with (succinct) one-counter nets, but we first define the
respective notions generally.

By a
reachability game
, or an
r-game
for short, we mean
a tuple

\mathcal{G}=(V,V_{\exists},\xrightarrow{},\mathcal{T}),

where
V
is the set of
states
(or
vertices
),

V_{\exists}\subseteq V
is the set of
Eve’s states
,

\mathop{\xrightarrow{}}\subseteq{V\times V}
is the
transition relation

(or the set of
transitions
), and
\mathcal{T}\subseteq V
is the set
of
target states
.
By
Adam’s states
we mean the elements of

V_{\forall}=V\smallsetminus V_{\exists}
.

Eve’s winning area

is
\mathit{Win}_{\exists}=\bigcup_{\lambda\in Ord}W_{\lambda}
, for
Ord
being
the class of ordinals, where the sets
W_{\lambda}\subseteq V
are defined
inductively as follows.

We put
W_{0}=\mathcal{T}
; for
\lambda>0
we put

W_{<\lambda}=\bigcup_{\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda}W_{\lambda^{\prime}}
, and we
stipulate:

(1)

if
s\not\in W_{<\lambda}
,
s\in V_{\exists}
, and

s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}
for some
\bar{s}\in W_{<\lambda}
, then
s\in W_{\lambda}
;

(2)

if
s\not\in W_{<\lambda}
,
s\in V_{\forall}
,
and we have
\emptyset\neq\{\bar{s}\mid s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}\}\subseteq W_{<\lambda}
,
then
s\in W_{\lambda}
.

(If (a) applies, then
\lambda
is surely a successor ordinal.)

For each
s\in\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
, by
\textsc{rank}(s)
we denote (the unique)

\lambda
such that

s\in W_{\lambda}
. A transition
s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}
is

rank-reducing
if
\textsc{rank}({s})>\textsc{rank}(\bar{s})
.
We note that for any
s\in\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
with
\textsc{rank}(s)>0
we have:
if
s\in V_{\exists}
, then there is at least one rank-reducing
transition
s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}
(in fact,
\textsc{rank}(s)=\textsc{rank}(\bar{s}){+}1
in
this case); if
s\in V_{\forall}
, then there is at least one
transition
s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}
and all such transitions are rank-reducing.
This entails that
\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
is the set of states from which Eve has a
strategy that guarantees reaching (some state in)
\mathcal{T}
when Eve
is choosing a next transition in Eve’s states
and Adam is choosing a next transition in Adam’s states.

Remark 2
.

We are primarily interested in the games that have (at most)
countably many states and are finitely branching (the sets

\{\bar{s}\mid s\xrightarrow{}\bar{s}\}
are finite for all
s
). In such cases
we have
\textsc{rank}(s)\in\mathbb{N}
for each
s\in\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
.
We have again introduced the general definition for the purpose of the
reduction in Section
4
.

Reachability games on succinct one-counter nets

We now define specific r-games,
presented by SOCNs
with
partitioned
control-state sets; these succinct one-counter nets are

unlabelled

a singleton.

By a
succinct one-counter net reachability game

socn-r-game

mean a tuple

\mathcal{N}=(Q,Q_{\exists},\delta,p_{win})

Q
is the finite set of
(control) states
,

Q_{\exists}\subseteq Q

,

We often present a rule

By

mean the elements of

(2)

We often write

(to decide succinct
one-counter net r-games) as follows:

:

Socn-Rg

Instance
:

(with integers

.

Question

in the game

.

We have defined the target states (in

.
There are other natural variants

In Section

Countdown games

We define a
countdown game

as a socn-r-game
\mathcal{N}=(Q,Q_{\exists},\delta,p_{win})
, where in every
rule
q\xrightarrow{z}q^{\prime}
in
\delta
we have
z<0
.
The problem
Cg
is defined as follows:

Name
:

Cg
(countdown games)

Instance
:

a countdown game
\mathcal{N}
(with integers in
rules written in binary),

and an initial configuration

p_{0}(n_{0})
where
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
(
n_{0}
in binary).

Question
:

is
p_{0}(n_{0})\in\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
?

The problem
Cg
(in an equivalent form) was shown
EXPTIME
-complete
in
[
DBLP:journals/lmcs/JurdzinskiSL08
]
.
Here we define an existential version,
i.e.
the problem
Ecg
:

Name
:

Ecg
(existential countdown games)

Instance
:

a countdown game
\mathcal{N}
and a control state
p_{0}
.

Question
:

is there some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{0}(n)\in\mathit{Win}_{\exists}
?

We note that
Ecg
can be viewed as a subproblem of

:
given an instance of

, it suffices to add a fresh
Eve’s state
p^{\prime}_{0}
and rules
p^{\prime}_{0}\xrightarrow{1}p^{\prime}_{0}
,
p^{\prime}_{0}\xrightarrow{0}p_{0}
;
the question then is if

.

3.
EXPSPACE-Completeness
of Existential Countdown Games

In this section we prove the following new theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Ecg
(existential countdown game) is
EXPSPACE
-complete.

Our
EXPSPACE
-hardness proof of ECG
is, in fact, a particular instance of a simple general method.
A slight modification also yields a proof of

EXPTIME
-hardness of countdown games that is an alternative to the
proof in
[
DBLP:journals/lmcs/JurdzinskiSL08

and in
[
DBLP:journals/ipl/Kiefer13

Theorem 5

[
DBLP:journals/lmcs/JurdzinskiSL08
]

EXPTIME

In the rest of this section we present

We start with the upper bounds since these are obvious.

3.1.
ECG is in EXPSPACE (and CG in

Let us consider an

\mathcal{N}=(Q,Q_{\exists},\delta,p_{win})

By

We can stepwise construct

We have

Therefore

belongs to
EXPSPACE
.

It is also clear that

(in space

no winning strategy if

is not accepted by
\mathcal{M}
.

In the formal construction presented below
we proceed by introducing an intermediate auxiliary problem (in
two versions) that
allows us to avoid some technicalities in the construction

\mathcal{M}

the consistency with the (empty) initial configuration is then

5.
Structure of simulation preorder
on one-counter nets

In this section we give a new self-contained proof clarifying
the structure of simulation preorder
\mathop{\preceq}
on the LTS
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}}

associated with a given one-counter net
\mathcal{N}
;
this will also
yield a polynomial-space algorithm generating a description
of
\mathop{\preceq}
on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{N}}
that can be used to decide if
p(m)\mathop{\preceq}q(n)
.

We first show
a natural graphic presentation
of the relation
\mathop{\preceq}
, and in Section
5.1
we
show
its linear-belt form; the result
is captured by the
belt theorem
.
The proof is inspired
by
[
DBLP:conf/sofsem/JancarMS99
]
but is substantially
different.
A main new ingredient is the notion of so called down-black and up-white
lines, and their limits in which we do not a priori exclude lines with
irrational slopes; this allows us to avoid many technicalities used in
previous proofs, while the presented “geometric” ideas should be
straightforward, and transparent due to the respective figures.

In Section
5.2
we prove that
the slopes and the widths of belts are presented/bounded by small
integers.
Though this
quantitative belt theorem
is in principle equivalent to the respective
theorem proved in
[
DBLP:journals/corr/HofmanLMT16
]
, our proof is
again conceptually different;
using the novel notions, the quantitative characteristics
are derived
from the (qualitative) belt theorem easily.

For completeness,
in Section
we briefly recall the idea
from the previous papers that shows how the achieved structural
results yield a polynomial-space algorithm generating a description
of
\mathop{\preceq}
for a given one-counter net.

In the sequel we assume a fixed OCN
\mathcal{N}=(Q,Act,\delta)
if not said otherwise.
By

\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
we denote the sets of
reals, of nonnegative reals, and of positive reals, respectively.
We also use
\infty
for an infinite amount (in particular for the
slope of a vertical line); hence
\alpha<\infty
for all

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. By
\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle
, where

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
,
we denote the set
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}\mid\gamma\leq\alpha\leq\gamma^{\prime}\}
; in particular,

\langle 0,\infty\rangle=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Monotonic black-white presentation of the simulation
preorder
\mathop{\preceq}

For each pair
(p,q)\in Q\times Q

we define the (black-white)

colouring

C_{\langle p,q\rangle}

of the integer points in the first quadrant of the plane

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
; we put

C_{\langle p,q\rangle}:{\rm Nature}\times{\rm Nature}\to\left\{\mathrm{black},\mathrm{white}\right\}
where

\displaystyle C_{\langle p,q\rangle}(m,n)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{black}&\mbox{if }p(m)\mathop{\preceq}q(n),\\
\mathrm{white}&\mbox{if }p(m)\mathop{\not\preceq}q(n).\end{cases}

We recall the definition of
\textsc{rank}(s,s^{\prime})
in
the paragraph “Stratified simulation, and ranks of pairs of states”
in Section
2
; this yields
the definition of
\textsc{rank}(p(m),q(n))
for our fixed OCN

\mathcal{N}=(Q,Act,\delta)
. Hence
for each
(p,q)\in Q\times Q
we have
that each white point
(m,n)
in

has an associated finite rank, namely

\textsc{rank}(p(m),q(n))\in\mathbb{N}
.

The next proposition captures the trivial fact
that “
black is upwards- and leftwards-closed
” and
“
white is downwards- and rightwards-closed
”
(as is also depicted in Figure
3
in Introduction, which also
shows the “linear-belt form” of the colourings).

Proposition 12

(Black and white monotonicity)
.

If
C_{\langle p,q\rangle}(m,n)=\mathrm{black}
, then

C_{\langle p,q\rangle}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=\mathrm{black}
for all
m^{\prime}\leq m

and
n^{\prime}\geq n
.
Hence if
C_{\langle p,q\rangle}(m,n)=\mathrm{white}
,
then
C_{\langle p,q\rangle}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=\mathrm{white}
for all

m^{\prime}\geq m
and
n^{\prime}\leq n
.

Proof 5.1
.

Since OCNs are monotonic in the sense that
p(m)\xrightarrow{a}q(n)
implies

p(m{+}i)\xrightarrow{a}q(n{+}i)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we will easily verify
that the relation

R=\big\{\big(p(m^{\prime}),q(n^{\prime})\big)\mid p(m)\mathop{\preceq}q(n)\textnormal{ for some }m\geq m^{\prime}\textnormal{ and }n\leq n^{\prime}\big\}

is a simulation relation.
Since

\mathop{\preceq}\subseteq R

, and the proof
will be finished.
To verify that

we consider

and

.
If
p(m^{\prime})\xrightarrow{a}p^{\prime}(m^{\prime}{+}i)
(for

), then
p(m)\xrightarrow{a}p^{\prime}(m{+}i)
. Since

, we have

where

and

p^{\prime}(m{+}i)\mathop{\preceq}q^{\prime}(n{+}j)
. Hence
q(n^{\prime})\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}(n^{\prime}{+}j)
,
and
\big(p^{\prime}(m^{\prime}{+}i),q^{\prime}(n^{\prime}{+}j)\big)\in R
.
