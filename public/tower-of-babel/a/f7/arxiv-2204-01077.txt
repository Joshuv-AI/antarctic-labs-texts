Brillouin Zones of Integer Lattices and Their Perturbations Thanks: Submitted to the editors on April 17, 2022.
Herbert Edelsbrunner, Alexey Garber, Mohadese Ghafari, Teresa Heiss, Morteza Saghafian, Mathijs Wintraecken

Abstract
For a locally finite set, A ⊆ ℝ d A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d} , the k k -th Brillouin zone of a ∈ A a\in A is the region of points x ∈ ℝ d x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d} for which ‖ x − a ‖ {\|{x}-{a}\|} is the k k -th smallest among the Euclidean distances between x x and the points in A A . If A A is a lattice, the k k -th Brillouin zones of the points in A A are translates of each other, which tile space. Depending on the value of k k , they express medium- or long-range order in the set. We study fundamental geometric and combinatorial properties of Brillouin zones, focusing on the integer lattice and its perturbations. Our results include the stability of a Brillouin zone under perturbations, a linear upper bound on the number of chambers in a zone for lattices in ℝ 2 {{\mathbb{R}}}^{2} , and the convergence of the maximum volume of a chamber to zero for the integer lattice.



keywords

Brillouin zones, Voronoi tessellations, plane arrangements, Gauss circle problem, asymptotic analysis

Geometric Background

In this section, we introduce the necessary background on Brillouin zones, the related bisector arrangements, and Voronoi tessellations.

2.1
Types of Sets

The results in this paper apply to a small number of different types of point sets in Euclidean space.
The primary concern is that their Voronoi tessellations are well defined and that the dual Delaunay mosaic covers the entire space.

A set
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is
Delone

1

Delaunay mosaics
and

if there are constants

, and every closed ball of radius

Assuming

2.2
Brillouin Zones

In some areas of mathematics, “Brillouin zone” is a synonym for “Voronoi domain”.
We define them so the zones depend on a positive integer parameter.

Definition 1
(Brillouin Zones).

Let
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
be a locally finite point set with a distinguished point,
0\in A
, and let
k\geq 1
be an integer.
The
k
-th Brillouin zone
of
0
is the set
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{A})}}
of points
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
such that

\|{x}-{a}\|

\displaystyle<{\|{x}-{0}\|}\mbox{\rm~for at most~}k-1\mbox{\rm~points~}a\in A\setminus\{0\},

(1)

\|{x}-{b}\|

\displaystyle\leq{\|{x}-{0}\|}\mbox{\rm~for at least~}k-1\mbox{\rm~points~}b\in A\setminus\{0\}.

(2)

In the same way we can define
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({a},{A})}}
for every point
a\in A
.
Note that
{{\rm Zone}_{1}{({0},{A})}}
is the Voronoi domain of
0
: all points
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
for which no point in
A
is closer to
x
than
0
.
To show that Brillouin zones are closed, observe the following. When fixing a set of at least
k-1
points for (
1
) and its subset of at most
k-1
points for (
2
), the sets defined by (
1
) and (
2
) are closed and so is their intersection.
The

th Brillouin zone of

is the (possibly infinite) union of these closed sets over all suitable choices of sets of at least
k-1
points and the corresponding subsets. However, its intersection with every compact neighborhood is determined by a finite number of points of
A
and therefore it is closed as the finite union of closed sets.
Because this holds for any compact neighborhood the
k
th Brillouin zone is closed.
Denoting the open ball with center
x
and radius
{\|{x}\|}={\|{x}-{0}\|}
by
B(x,{\|{x}\|})

1
) and (
2

at least
k-1

in
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
and at most
k-1
in the closure of
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
.
To construct the Brillouin zones, we draw the bisectors defined by

and all points

We can therefore label each

For

, every Brillouin zone is the difference between the (closed) star-convex set defined by (

It is therefore not difficult to prove the following fundamental result on the shape of the zones:

Lemma 2
(Thickened Sphere).

Let
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
be locally finite and coarsely dense, and assume
0\in A
.
Then for every finite integer

, the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
has the homotopy type of
{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Every ray
L
emanating from the origin
0
has non-empty intersection with the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
.
To show this, consider a point
x
moving along the ray
L
together with the (moving) ball
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
centered at
x
passing through the origin.
Since
A
is coarsely dense, the half space orthogonal to
L
(and passing through
0
) contains infinitely many points of
A
. Thus, there is a point
x
on the ray
L
such that the open ball
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
contains at least
k
points in

, and thus condition (

is not in the Brillouin zone.
However, when moving
x

towards the origin, the first time that the open ball

contains at most

k

A\setminus\{0\}
. Hence, we found a point

satisfying both (
1
) and (
2

Next, we use star-convexity to construct a deformation retraction: Every point

k

gets moved by a straight line to

The image under this retraction of the points

for all the rays

2.4
Voronoi Tessellations

For fixed
k\geq 1
, the
k
-th Brillouin zones of the points in
A
form the
order-
k
Brillouin tessellation
, which is also known as the
degree-
k
Voronoi tessellation

[
16
]
.
It relates to the better known order-
k
Voronoi tessellation, see e.g.
[
24
]
, which we introduce first.

Given a finite set
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and a non-empty subset
Q\subseteq A
, we define the
region
of
Q
as the points
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
that satisfy
{\|{x}-{q}\|}\leq{\|{x}-{a}\|}
for all
q\in Q
and
a\in A\setminus Q
.
If non-empty, this region is a convex polyhedron, and if
A
is in general position, then this polyhedron is
d
-dimensional.
The
order-
k
Voronoi tessellation
is the polyhedral complex consisting of the regions of subsets
Q\subseteq A
of size
{{\#}{Q}}=k
and the faces shared by these polyhedra.

For finite sets
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
, the number of cells in the order-
k
Voronoi tessellation is well understood.
Part of the reason is that in the
2
-dimensional generic case, every vertex is shared by exactly two tessellations of consecutive order.
We thus distinguish between the
old
and
new
vertices of the order-
k
Voronoi tessellation, which it shares with the order-
(k-1)
and order-
(k+1)
Voronoi tessellations, respectively.
Using induction, Lee proved that there are fewer than
[4k-2]n
vertices,
[6k-3]n
edges, and
[2k-1]n
regions
[
24
]
.
We will need more precise estimates, so we follow
[
6
]
and view the order-
k
Voronoi tessellation in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
as the projection of cells in an arrangement of planes in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}
, and then overcount by moving to the
3
-sphere.
The latter amounts to mapping the planes to
2
-dimensional great-spheres in
{{\mathbb{S}}}^{3}
, which effectively combines the order-
k
with the order-
(n-k)
Voronoi tessellation.
We call the result of this view the
spherical order-
k
Voronoi tessellation.

The benefit of this approach is that we get equalities for the number of cells, rather than inequalities.
Indeed, applying the
3
-dimensional methods in
[
6
]
to
2
dimensions, things simplify considerably and it is not difficult to count the faces assuming general position:

Proposition 4
(Spherical Order-
k
Voronoi Tessellation).

Let
A
be
n\geq 4
points in general position in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
Then for
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the spherical order-

A
has
u_{k}=2(k-1)(n-k)
old vertices,
w_{k}=2k(n-k)-2k
new vertices,
e_{k}=(6k-3)(n-k)-3k
edges, and
r_{k}=(2k-1)(n-k)-(k-2)
regions.

In dimensions three and higher, counting the cells in the order-
k
Voronoi tessellations is significantly more difficult
[
6
]
and only rough upper and lower bounds are known.
The
order-
k
Brillouin tessellation is the overlay of the order-
(k-1)
and order-
k

1

It decomposes
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
into convex regions such that any two points in the same region have the same
k
-th nearest point in
A
.
Each such region is a chamber of the
k
-th Brillouin zone of this

-th nearest point in
A
.
In the
2
-dimensional case, it is not difficult to get good bounds on the number of cells from Proposition
4

Corollary 5
(Spherical Order-
k
Brillouin Tessellation).

Let
A
be
n\geq 4
points in general position in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
Then for

, the spherical order-

has
(6k-6)(n-k)-4

(12k-12)(n-k)-6
edges, and
(6k-6)(n-k)

Proof.

For the vertices, we add the numbers of the order-
(k-1)
and order-

Voronoi tessellations and remove duplicates:

For the edges, we add the numbers:

To count the regions, we use the Euler formula for the

(e_{k-1}+e_{k})-(u_{k-1}+u_{k}+w_{k})+2=(6k-6)(n-k)

2.5
Perturbations of the Integer Lattice

We call an injective map,
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, a
perturbation
of the integer lattice, and the supremum of the
{\|{a}-{\varphi(a)}\|}
over all
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
its
magnitude
.
The perturbation is
bounded
if its magnitude is finite.
We call the image of the map,
P=\varphi({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})
, a
perturbed integer lattice
.
Without loss of generality, we assume throughout this paper that
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
is a fixed point; that is:
\varphi(0)=0
.
We generate perturbations randomly, by picking
\varphi(a)
uniformly at random in
a+[-\tau,\tau]^{d}
for each
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, in which
\tau
is the
strength
of the perturbation.
Standardizing to three strengths, we call the generated perturbation
weak
,
medium
,
strong
if
\tau=0.02,0.10,0.50
, respectively.

While magnitude and strength are different concepts, we discuss them in disjoint contexts and thus use the same letter,
\tau
, to denote either.
The strength of a perturbation is relevant in many of our computational experiments, and in Appendix
A
, which supports the experiments by analyzing how many Brillouin zones in a finite arrangement of bisectors are reliable.

Its

-dimensional volume and—by a classic result
[
5
]
—the volume of any other Brillouin zone is
{{\rm vol}{({{{\rm Zone}_{1}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}})}}=1
.
Let
\varrho_{k}=\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}
and note that
{{\rm vol}{({B(0,\varrho_{k})})}}=k
.
The first
k-1
Brillouin zones of
0
have a total volume of
k-1
, but since their union is not a perfect geometric ball, they do not cover all of
B(0,\varrho_{k-1})
.
Hence,
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
contains points in the interior of
B(0,\varrho_{k-1})
, which implies
r_{k}<\varrho_{k-1}
.
A symmetric argument implies
R_{k}>\varrho_{k}
.
This implies that the width satisfies

\displaystyle W_{k}

\displaystyle=R_{k}-r_{k}>\varrho_{k}-\varrho_{k-1}=\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}-\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{(k-1)}}{{\nu_{d}}}}.

(8)

To prove the lower bound for
r_{k}
and the upper bounds for
R_{k}
and
W_{k}
, we use a straightforward solution to the generalization of the Gauss circle problem to
d
dimensions; see
[
21
]
and references therein for the problem and relevant progress.
Letting
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, not necessarily in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
,
the number of integer points in
B(x,\rho)
satisfies

\displaystyle\nu_{d}[\rho-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}]^{d}

\displaystyle<{{\#}{\mbox{\rm points}}}<\nu_{d}[\rho+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}]^{d}.

(9)

To see this, we note that the unit cubes of the points inside
B(x,\rho)
cover
B(x,\rho-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}})
, which implies the first inequality in (
9
) by a volume argument.
Symmetrically, these unit cubes are contained in
B(x,\rho+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}})
, which implies the second inequality, again by a volume argument.
Setting
\rho={\|{x}\|}
and
{{\#}{\mbox{\rm points}}}=k
, we rearrange the two inequalities and get bounds on the minimum and maximum distances from
0
:

\displaystyle\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}

\displaystyle<r_{k}\leq{\|{x}\|}\leq R_{k}<\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}.

(10)

This implies that the width is
W_{k}=R_{k}-r_{k}<\sqrt{d}
, as claimed.

We remark that the lower bound on the width in Theorem
6
tends to
0
when
k
goes to infinity, while the upper bound is a constant independent of
k
.
Using earlier work by van der Corput
[
10
]
, Kwakkel improves upon our upper bound in two dimensions, showing that the width of the
k
-th Brillouin zone goes to zero as
k
goes to infinity
[
23
, Theorem 3.2]
.
With appropriate changes of the constants, all bounds in Theorem
6
extend to Delone sets in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
.
Indeed, we can use volume arguments to adjust (
9
) to the more general case of Delone sets, while leaving the rest of the argument as is.

We generalize the lower bound for

and the upper bound for

in Theorem

{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}

Theorem 7
(Width for Perturbed Integer Lattices).

Let
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
be a bounded perturbation with magnitude

, and let

Then the distances and the width of the

\displaystyle<\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}+\tau,

(11)

\displaystyle W_{k}(0)

\displaystyle<\sqrt{d}+2\tau.

(12)

Proof.

Write
\varrho_{k}=\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}

and the claimed upper bound for

.
We get (

.

4
Stability

While Theorem
7
bounds the width under perturbations, it falls short of showing that the Brillouin zones are stable, which we prove in this section.
A related result is the stability of Voronoi regions, which was proven by Reem
[
25
]
.

4.1
Two Technical Lemmas

We begin with an observation about the function
\min_{k}\colon{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
that maps a vector of
n
numbers to the
k
-th smallest among them.
We write
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
for the components of
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
and
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n}
for the components of
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
.

Lemma 8
(Stability of Rank).

For any
1\leq k\leq n
, the map
\min_{k}\colon{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is
1
-Lipschitz under the
L_{\infty}
-norm; that is: if
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq{\varepsilon}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, then
|\min_{k}(x)-\min_{k}(y)|\leq{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
X_{0}
contain the
k
smallest components of
x
, and let
X_{1}
contain the
n-k+1
largest components of
x
, in which ties are broken arbitrarily.
We note that
\max(X_{0})=\min_{k}(x)=\min(X_{1})
.
Let
y_{i}\in Y_{0}
iff
x_{i}\in X_{0}
, and similarly for
Y_{1}
and
X_{1}
.
Since corresponding components differ by at most
{\varepsilon}
, we have
\max(Y_{0})-{\varepsilon}\leq\min_{k}(x)\leq\min(Y_{1})+{\varepsilon}
, and because
{{\#}{Y_{0}}}=k
and
{{\#}{Y_{1}}}=n-k+1
, we have
\min(Y_{1})\leq\min_{k}(y)\leq\max(Y_{0})
.
Hence,
\min_{k}(y)-{\varepsilon}\leq\min_{k}(x)\leq\min_{k}(y)+{\varepsilon}
, which is equivalent to the claimed inequality.

The next technical lemma asserts the stability of the intersection of a half-line with the bisector of two points.
In the setting we consider, the half-line emanates from one of the two points, which we assume is
0\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
.
Let
a\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
be the second point, let
u\in{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
be the direction of the half-line, and assume
{\langle u,a\rangle}>0
so that the intersection between the half-line and the bisector of
0,a
exists.
Writing
\lambda u
for the points of the half-line, we solve
\lambda={\|{\lambda u}-{a}\|}
to get
\lambda(a)=\frac{1}{2}{\|{a}\|}^{2}/{\langle u,a\rangle}
as the parameter value of the intersection point.

Lemma 9
(Stability of Crossing).

Let
u\in{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
be a direction and
a\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
a point with
{\langle u,a\rangle}>0
.
Then for every
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists
\tau>0
such that
{\|{p}-{a}\|}<\tau
implies that
\lambda(p)
is well defined and satisfies
\lambda(p)<\lambda(a)+{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

For
\tau<{\langle u,a\rangle}
, we have
{\langle u,p\rangle}\geq{\langle u,a-\tau u\rangle}={\langle u,a\rangle}-\tau>0
, so the bisector of
0,p
has a unique intersection point with the half-line of points
\lambda u
, and this intersection point is given by
\lambda(p)=\frac{1}{2}{\|{p}\|}^{2}/{\langle u,p\rangle}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(p)}{\lambda(a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\|{p}\|}^{2}{\langle u,a\rangle}}{{\|{a}\|}^{2}{\langle u,p\rangle}}<\frac{{\|{a+\frac{\tau a}{{\|{a}\|}}}\|}^{2}{\langle u,a\rangle}}{{\|{a}\|}^{2}{\langle u,a-\tau u\rangle}}=\frac{{\|{a}\|}^{2}{\langle u,a\rangle}}{{\|{a}\|}^{2}{\langle u,a\rangle}}\frac{(1+\frac{\tau}{{\|{a}\|}})^{2}}{(1-\frac{\tau}{{\langle u,a\rangle}})},

(13)

in which the first ratio of the right-hand side in (
13
) cancels.
Since
{\|{a}\|}
,
{\|{a}\|}^{2}
,
{\langle u,a\rangle}
, and
\lambda(a)
are all fixed and positive, it is easy to find a sufficiently small
\tau>0
so that the remaining ratio is at most
1+{\varepsilon}/\lambda(a)
.
This implies
\lambda(p)<\lambda(a)+{\varepsilon}
, as required.

4.2
The Stability of Brillouin Zones

Write
{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
for the set of points
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
for which fewer than
k
points in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
have distance less than
{\|{x}\|}
from
x
, and note that
{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
is the union of the first
k
Brillouin zones of
0
.
Since the Brillouin zones have disjoint interiors, this implies that
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
is the closure of
{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}\setminus{{\rm dom}_{k-1}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
.
For the same reason,

\displaystyle\partial{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}

\displaystyle=\partial{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}\cup\partial{{\rm dom}_{k-1}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}

(14)

for the boundary of the
k
-th Brillouin zone.
For any direction
u\in{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
, consider the half-line of points
\lambda u
, with
\lambda\geq 0
, and write
\alpha_{k}(u)
for the unique
\lambda
such that
\lambda u\in\partial{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
.
Similarly, write
\beta_{k}(u)
for the unique
\lambda
such that
\lambda u\in\partial{{\rm dom}_{k}{({0},{P})}}
, in which

\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a perturbation of the integer lattice and
P=\varphi({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})
is the perturbed set.

Theorem 10
(Stability of Brillouin Zones).

For every integer
k\geq 1
and real
{\varepsilon}>0
, there is a sufficiently small
\tau>0
such that for every perturbation
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
with
\varphi(0)=0
and
\sup\{{\|{a}-{\varphi(a)}\|}\}<\tau
, we have
|\beta_{k}(u)-\alpha_{k}(u)|<{\varepsilon}
for every direction
u\in{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Assume a sufficiently small
\tau>0
, and let
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
be a perturbation of the integer grid such that
\varphi(0)=0
and
\sup\{{\|{a}-{\varphi(a)}\|}\}<\tau
.
Fixing a direction
u\in{{\mathbb{S}}}^{d-1}
, we begin by constructing a set
A\subseteq{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
that satisfies the conditions needed to apply Lemmas
8
and
9
; that is:

1.

{\langle u,a\rangle}>0
so
\lambda(a)=\frac{1}{2}{\|{a}\|}^{2}/{\langle u,a\rangle}
is well defined for every
a\in A
,

2.

\alpha_{k}(u)
is the
k
-th smallest of the
\lambda(a)
,
a\in A
,

3.

{\langle u,p\rangle}>0
so
\lambda(p)=\frac{1}{2}{\|{p}\|}^{2}/{\langle u,p\rangle}
is well defined for every
p\in\varphi(A)
,

4.

\beta_{k}(u)
is the
k
-th smallest of the
\lambda(p)
,
p\in\varphi(A)
.

In short, to satisfy Conditions 1 and 3, we pick points in the open half-space defined by
{\langle x,u\rangle}>0
, and to satisfy Conditions 2 and 4, we include sufficiently many points whose bisectors intersect the half-line defined by
u
near
0
.
To be specific, we set
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt[d]{{k}/{\nu_{d}}}+{\sqrt{d}}/{2}+1
,
and let
B_{0}=B(\lambda_{0}u,\lambda_{0})
be the open ball passing through
0
.
By Theorem
6
,
B_{0}
contains at least
k
integer points, including the
k
points whose bisectors with
0
intersect the half-line at the first
k
crossings.
Assuming
\tau\leq 1
, by Theorem
7
,
B_{0}
contains at least
k
points of
\varphi({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})
, including the
k
points whose bisectors with
0
intersect the half-line at the first
k
crossings.
The set

consists of all points

such that

or
\varphi(a)\in B_{0}
. Observe that for this choice of

, Conditions 2 and 4 are satisfied.

To establish the remaining two properties,
note that

trivially satisfies
{\langle u,a\rangle}>0
.
Assuming
\tau
is smaller than

satisfies

.

Similarly, points

For the last step, let

Let

5
Number of Chambers

We get upper bounds on the number of chambers of individual Brillouin zones from the relation of the zones to

5.1
Integer Lattice in the Plane

As mentioned in Section
2
, there is a connection between
k
-th Brillouin zones and order-
k
Voronoi tessellations, which we exploit to get a linear upper bound on the number of chambers in the
2
-dimensional lattice case.
We also prove a lower bound, for which we need an extension of a well known number theoretic fact.
We begin with this extension.

Lemma 11
(#Integer Points on Circle).

For every
{\varepsilon}>0
, every circle of radius
R
passes through at most
O(R^{\varepsilon})
integer points.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be a circle with radius
R
and assume first that it is centered at the origin in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
Let
n(R)
be the number of integer points on
\Gamma
, which is the number of ways we can write
R^{2}
as a sum of two squares.
It is known that
n(R)
is at most some constant times the number of divisors of
R

[
9
, Lemma 2]
, which implies
n(R)=O(R^{\varepsilon})
for every
{\varepsilon}>0
; see
[
20
]
.

Next consider the more general case, in which the center of
\Gamma
is not an integer point.
We can assume that
\Gamma
passes through at least three integer points, else there is nothing to prove.
We may also assume that one of these three points is
0=(0,0)
, and we write
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
and
b=(b_{1},b_{2})
for the other two integer points.
The center of
\Gamma
is at the intersection of the two bisectors defined by
0,a
and by
0,b
.
Equivalently, its coordinates are the solutions to the linear system

\displaystyle 2a_{1}x_{1}+2a_{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2},

(15)

\displaystyle 2b_{1}x_{1}+2b_{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}.

(16)

Using Cramer’s Rule, we get the coordinates of the center as ratios of integer determinants, and the two ratios share the denominator, which is
\Delta=2a_{1}b_{2}-2a_{2}b_{1}
.
By assumption, the radius of
\Gamma
is
R
, which implies that the coordinates are at most
2R
, and thus
|\Delta|\leq 16R^{2}
.
Scaling
\Gamma
by
\Delta
, we get a new circle, with radius at most
16R^{3}
, whose center is an integer point.
The integer points on
\Gamma
map to integer points on the new circle, but there can be only
n(\Delta R)
such integer points, which is
O(R^{\varepsilon})
, for every
{\varepsilon}>0
.

Theorem 12
(#Chambers for 2D Integer Lattice).

For every
k\geq 2
and
{\varepsilon}>0
, the number of chambers in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
is at least
\Omega(k^{1-{\varepsilon}})
and at most
6k-6
.

Proof.

We first prove the lower bound.
Let
x
be an interior point of a chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
, and write
A(x)
for the
k-1
integer points in the interior of
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
.
The point
-x
is interior to the diametrically opposite chamber, and
A(-x)
is another set of
k-1
integer points.
Since
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
and
B(-x,{\|{x}\|})
have disjoint interiors,
A(x)
and
A(-x)
are disjoint.

For
k\geq 2
, the
k
-th Brillouin zone consists of a cyclic ring of chambers, each sharing a vertex with its predecessor and another vertex with its successor along the cyclic order.
For each chamber, we get a set of
k-1
integer points, and we consider the sets of two consecutive chambers that share a vertex
y
.
If a point
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
belongs to one set but not the other, then the bisector of
0
and
a
passes through
y
.
The integer points whose bisectors with
0
pass through
y
all lie on the circle centered at
y
and passing through
0
.
By Theorem
6
, the radius of this circle is at most some constant times
\sqrt{k}
, so Lemma
11
implies that there are at most
O(k^{\varepsilon})
integer points on this circle, for any
{\varepsilon}>0
.
The claimed lower bound follows because we need at least
\Omega(k^{1-{\varepsilon}})
steps between adjacent chambers of the
k
-th Brillouin zone to exchange all points, which is necessary to travel from
A(x)
to
A(-x)
.

We second prove the upper bound.
Let
\rho
be the radius, which we will specify later, and consider the integer lattice within the ball
B(0,\rho)
.
Set
A=B(0,\rho)\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
and
n={{\#}{A}}
, and note that (
9
) implies
\pi[\rho-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}]^{2}<n<\pi[\rho+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}]^{2}
.
Write
{{\rm Bri}_{k}{({A})}}
for the order-
k
Brillouin tessellation of
A
defined in Section
2
.
For
k=1
, this is the ordinary Voronoi tessellation with one (
2
-dimensional) region per point, and for
k\geq 2
, Corollary
5
asserts that the number of regions is less than
[6k-6]n
.

For
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
, we call a closed ball
a
-anchored
if
a
belongs to its boundary, and we call a point,
b\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
,
k
-near
to
a
if there is an
a
-anchored ball,
B
, with
b\in{\rm int\,}{B}
and
{{\#}{(B\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2})}}\leq k-1
.
Now recall that
{{\rm Bri}_{k}{({A})}}
is obtained by drawing the
k
-th Brillouin zones of all points in
A
next to each other.
The
k
-th Brillouin zone of
a
in
A
is the same as in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
if all
k
-near points of
a
in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
also belong to
A
.
By (
6
), the maximum distance of
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
from a point in its
k
-th Brillouin zone is
R_{k}<\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\pi}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}
.
It follows that the disk
B(a,2R_{k})
contains all
k
-near points of
a
.
Hence, for all points
a\in B(0,\rho-2R_{k})\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
, the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
a
in
A
is the same as in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
.
Writing
n_{0}
for the number of such points, we use again a volume argument to see that
n_{0}>\pi[\rho-2R_{k}-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}]^{2}
.
The number of remaining points in
A
is of lower order.
To do the final counting, let
\chi_{k}
be the number of chambers in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of a point in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
.
The total number of chambers we get for the
n_{0}
points is less than the number of regions in
{{\rm Bri}_{k}{({A})}}
.
Assuming
k\geq 2
, this implies

\displaystyle\chi_{k}

\displaystyle<[6k-6]\frac{n}{n_{0}}<[6k-6]\frac{\pi[\rho+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}]^{2}}{\pi[\rho-2R_{k}-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}]^{2}}.

(17)

We can make the ratio as close to
1
as we like by choosing
\rho
as large as we like.
This finally implies
\chi_{k}\leq 6k-6
.

The upper bound on the number of chambers extends without adjustment of constants to general lattices in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
For a lattice in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
that contains no four points on a common circle, the lower bound argument in the above proof implies a matching linear lower bound.
Contrary to lattices, a locally finite point set can have a Brillouin zone with infinitely many chambers; see
[
30
, Abbildung 3.4]
for an example.

5.2
Integer Lattices Beyond Two Dimensions

The proof of the upper bound in Theorem
12
uses Corollary
5
, which only holds in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
To generalize to higher dimension we need a different strategy.
Using inversion, we transform the problem of counting chambers of the
k
-th Brillouin zone to counting
k
-sets of a finite set.
We begin by proving that sets of
O(k)
points suffice for the transformation.
Recall that a point
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
is
k
-near
to
0
if there exists a
0
-anchored ball,
B
, such that
a\in{\rm int\,}{B}
and
{{\#}{({\rm int\,}{B}\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})}}\leq k-1
.
Theorem
6
implies an upper bound on the number of
k
-near points.

Figure 6:
The image of the integer lattice under inversion.
The dotted lines and circles are the images of nine vertical and nine horizontal integer lines.
The
blue
unit circle is preserved by the inversion.
Since all integer points other than
0
lie on or outside the unit circle, their images under the inversion all lie on or inside the unit circle.

Lemma 13
(#Near Points).

For every integer
k\geq 1
, the number of
k
-near points of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
is
O(k)
, assuming
d
is constant.

Proof.

The center of any
0
-anchored closed ball with fewer than
k
integer points in the interior belongs to one of the first
k
Brillouin zones of
0
.
By (
6
), the distance of such a point from
0
is at most
R_{k}<\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}
.
It follows that all
k
-near points are contained in
B(0,2R_{k})
.
By a straightforward volume argument, the number of such points is bounded from above by the volume of
B(0,2R_{k}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}})
, which is

\displaystyle\nu_{d}\left[2R_{k}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}\right]^{d}

\displaystyle<\nu_{d}\left[2\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}+\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{d}\right]^{d}.

(18)

Since
d
is a constant, this volume is
O(k)
, so the number of
k
-near points is
O(k)
.

For the next step, we
invert

{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
in the unit sphere using the map
\iota\colon{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
defined by
\iota(x)=x/{\|{x}\|}^{2}
; see Figure
6
.
It preserves the unit sphere and exchanges points inside with points outside this sphere.
Importantly, it maps every
0
-anchored ball to a closed half-space that does not contain
0
.
In particular, it maps a
0
-anchored ball,
B
, with
{{\#}{({\rm int\,}{B}\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})}}=k-1
to a half-space,
\iota(B)
, that contains
k-1
points of
A=\iota({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
in its interior.
We call these points a
(k-1)
-set
of
A
.
By Lemma
13
, there is a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
of size
{{\#}{A^{\prime}}}=O(k)
such that every
(k-1)
-set of
A
is also a
(k-1)
-set of
A^{\prime}
.
This is interesting because counting
k
-sets is a much studied while poorly understood problem in discrete geometry.
Nevertheless, non-trivial bounds on the maximum number of
k
-sets are known in all finite dimensions, and the inversion together with Lemma
13
implies similar bounds for the number of chambers of Brillouin zones.
Let
{f_{k}^{(d)}{(n)}}
be the maximum number of
k
-sets any set of
n
points in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
can have.

Theorem 14
(#Chambers for Integer Lattices).

For every
k\geq 1
, there exists a constant,
C
, depending on
d
, such that
{f_{k-1}^{(d)}{(Ck)}}
is an upper bound on the number of chambers of the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
.

At the time of writing this paper, the best known upper bounds for
{f_{k}^{(d)}{(n)}}
in which
n=O(k)
are
{f_{k}^{(2)}{(n)}}=O(n^{4/3})
in
[
11
]
,
{f_{k}^{(3)}{(n)}}=O(n^{5/2})
in
[
27
]
, and
{f_{k}^{(d)}{(n)}}=O(n^{d-c_{d}})
with

small and tending to

as

grows in
[
2
]
.
The bounds in Theorem
14
extend to Delone sets, so it is humbling that we did not get better bounds even for the integer lattices beyond two dimensions.

6
Size of a Chamber

In this section, we prove bounds on the maximum diameter of a chamber in a
k
-th Brillouin zone.
Bounds on the volume and other measures follow.

6.1
Integer Lattices

The integer lattice forces the chambers of the
k
-th Brillouin zone to shrink when
k
increases.
To prove a quantified version of this claim, we begin with an exercise in elementary geometry.
While the application of the result from this exercise is in
d
dimensions, it is convenient to temporarily add another dimension and embed
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d+1}
.

Let
c
be a positive constant,
R\geq c
a radius, and
\Sigma_{0}
a
d
-sphere with radius
\rho_{0}=R
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d+1}
.
We construct a
(d-1)
-sphere
\Sigma_{1}
by slicing
\Sigma_{0}
with a
d
-plane at distance
\rho_{0}-c
from the center of
\Sigma_{0}
.
It is easy to check that the radius of
\Sigma_{1}
is
\rho_{1}=\sqrt{2c\rho_{0}-c^{2}}
, which is necessarily at least
c
.
We iterate and thus get a sequence of spheres
\Sigma_{i}
, for
0\leq i\leq d
, in which the dimension of
\Sigma_{i}
is
d-i
, and the radius of every subsequent sphere is
\rho_{i}=\sqrt{2c\rho_{i-1}-c^{2}}\geq c
.
Writing the radii in terms of
R
, we get

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2cR-c^{2}}~~~~~~~~~~~~<(2c)^{1/2}R^{1/2},

(19)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2c\sqrt{2cR-c^{2}}-c^{2}}<(2c)^{3/4}R^{1/4},

(20)

and more generally
\rho_{i}\leq(2c)^{1-1/2^{i}}R^{1/2^{i}}
for
0\leq i\leq d
.
Write
z_{i}
for the center of
\Sigma_{i}
and observe that its distance to the closest point on
\Sigma_{0}
is

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=R-{\|{z_{i}}-{z_{0}}\|}=R-\sqrt{R^{2}-\rho_{i}^{2}}=R-\sqrt{\left(R-\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{2R}\right)^{2}-\frac{\rho_{i}^{4}}{4R^{2}}}

(21)

\displaystyle\leq R-\left(R-\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{2R}\right)+\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{2R}\leq\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{R}\leq\frac{4c^{2}}{R}\left(\frac{R}{2c}\right)^{1/2^{i-1}}=2c\left(\frac{2c}{R}\right)^{1-1/2^{i-1}}.

(22)

Observe that all
d+1
centers lie in a
d
-plane that intersects
\Sigma_{0}
in a
(d-1)
-sphere of radius
R
, which we denote
\Sigma_{0}^{\prime}
.
It follows that
\delta_{i}
is also the distance of
z_{i}
to the closest point of
\Sigma_{0}^{\prime}
, for
0\leq i\leq d
.
Of particular interest is the last center,
z_{d}
, and its distance to
\Sigma_{0}^{\prime}
, which is
\delta_{d}
.
The
d
-plane that contains
\Sigma_{0}^{\prime}
is where we apply the insights from the exercise.

We claim that in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, there are necessarily many integer points at distance at most
\delta_{d}
from
\Sigma_{0}
.
To formulate this claim, we call a point on a
(d-1)
-sphere in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
a
pole
if there is an index
1\leq i\leq d
such that the point has either minimum or maximum
i
-th coordinate among all points of the sphere.
A
(d-1)
-sphere has
2d
poles.
For the next definition, we let
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
be the
d
-plane spanned by the first
d
coordinate vectors of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d+1}
.
An
integer
(i+1)
-plane
is an axis-parallel
(i+1)
-dimensional plane in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d+1}
normal to
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
passing through at least one integer point.
It is normal to
d-i
of the first
d
coordinate axes and determined by the corresponding
d-i
values, which are integers.

The largest open ball in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
that contains no point in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
has radius
\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}
.
This implies that for a
(d-1)
-sphere with radius
R\geq\sqrt{d}
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, and for any coordinate axis, there exists an integer
d
-plane orthogonal to that axis at distance at least
R-\sqrt{d}
from the center that contains at least one point of
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
on or inside the sphere.
This motivates us to set
c=\sqrt{d}
.

Lemma 15
(Nearby Integer Points).

For every
(d-1)
-sphere with radius
R\geq\sqrt{d}
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, and every one of its poles, there is an integer point at distance at most
R
to the center, at most
\sqrt{2\sqrt{d}R}
to the pole, and at most
2\sqrt{d}(2\sqrt{d}/R)^{1-1/2^{d-1}}
to the closest point on the
(d-1)
-sphere.

Proof.

Let
S_{0}
be a
d
-sphere with center in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and radius
R\geq\sqrt{d}
in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d+1}
, and write
B_{0}
for the closed
(d+1)
-ball whose boundary is
S_{0}
.
Let
S_{0}^{\prime}=S_{0}\cap{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, which is a
(d-1)
-sphere of radius
R
and thus encloses or passes through at least one point in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
.
Because of symmetry, it is sufficient to construct a point
p\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
that satisfies the conditions of the lemma for one given pole of
S_{0}^{\prime}
.
Let this pole be the point with maximum first coordinate among all points of
S_{0}^{\prime}
.
Let
h_{1}
be the integer
d
-plane orthogonal to the first coordinate axis, with maximum first coordinate such that
B_{0}\cap h_{1}\cap{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\neq\emptyset
.
Let
S_{1}=S_{0}\cap h_{1}
be the corresponding
(d-1)
-sphere, and note that its radius is
r_{1}\leq\rho_{1}
, in which
\rho_{1}
is defined with
c=\sqrt{d}
in (
19
).
Write
B_{1}=B_{0}\cap h_{1}
and iterate to get a sequence of
d+1
spheres, as before.
By construction, the radii of these spheres satisfy
r_{i}\leq\rho_{i}
, and their centers have distance
d_{i}\leq\delta_{i}
to their closest points on
S_{0}^{\prime}
.
Also by construction, each
B_{i}
contains at least one point in
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
, and since its center maximizes the distance to
S_{0}^{\prime}
, the distance of this integer point to the closest point on
S_{0}^{\prime}
is at most
d_{i}\leq\delta_{i}
.
The case
i=d
proves the claimed bounds on the distance to
S_{0}^{\prime}
and to the center of
S_{0}^{\prime}
.

To also bound the distance to the pole, observe that
p\in B_{1}
, which is a
d
-ball with radius
r_{1}\leq\rho_{1}=\sqrt{2\sqrt{d}R-d}
.
The distance of the pole to
z_{1}
, the center of the
d
-ball, is at most
\sqrt{d}
, so the distance to
p
is at most
\sqrt{r_{1}^{2}+d}\leq\sqrt{2\sqrt{d}R}
.

Let now
S(x,r)
and
S(y,R)
be two
(d-1)
-spheres with centers
x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and radii
r\leq R
.
Assuming

, an entire hemisphere of

lies outside the other sphere, namely the hemisphere of points

that satisfy
{\langle a-y,y-x\rangle}\geq 0
.
We prove that this hemisphere contains a large cap within which all points have distance at least some constant times
{\|{x}-{y}\|}
to the closest point of

.
Specifically, if

is a point in this hemisphere and

is the angle between

and the central point,

, of the hemisphere, as in Figure
8
, then

\displaystyle{\mbox{$\|{a}-{x}\|$}}^{2}

\displaystyle=R^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+(R\cos\theta+{\|{x}-{y}\|})^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle=R^{2}+2R{\|{x}-{y}\|}\cos\theta+{\|{x}-{y}\|}^{2}\geq(R+{\|{x}-{y}\|}\cos\theta)^{2}.

(24)

The distance of
a
to the closest point of

is therefore

{\|{a}-{x}\|}-r\geq{\|{a}-{x}\|}-R\geq{\|{x}-{y}\|}\cos\theta
.
In words, for every constant angle

, the distance between

and the closest point of

is at least some constant fraction of the distance between the centers.

By assumption,

.
The half-circle of points

The cone with angle

In the following, we will consider cones, for which we introduce the following nomenclature.
By a

with

y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
,
direction

, and

\theta_{0}<\frac{\pi}{2}
, we mean the set of points

such that the angle between

and

is at most
\theta_{0}
; see Figure

.
We will need
\theta_{0}
such that at least one of the integer points that satisfy the conditions of Lemma

For every direction

, there is a pole

of

with

,
and this is best possible because

for all poles if

Thus any cone with angle at least

contains at least one pole.
We choose the threshold a bit larger so that any such cone also contains the nearby integer point.

Lemma 16
(Poles and Cones).

Let
\Sigma
be a sphere with radius
R\geq(18d-\frac{1}{2})\sqrt{d}
.
Then every cone with apex at the center of
\Sigma
and angle
\theta_{0}=\arccos\nicefrac{{1}}{{(3\sqrt{d})}}
contains at least one pole of
\Sigma
together with the nearby integer point as constructed in the proof of Lemma

.

Proof.

Write
y
for the center of
\Sigma
and let
z
be an arbitrary point on the sphere. Before showing the claim for the cone with apex
y
, direction
z-y
and angle
\theta_{0}
(below denoted by
\theta_{0}
-cone), we consider a cone with the same apex and direction but a smaller angle
\varphi
(below denoted by
\varphi
-cone):
As mentioned above, every cone with angle
\varphi=\arccos\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
contains at least one pole,
b
.
Let
\Sigma_{2}
be the
(d-2)
-sphere obtained by slicing
\Sigma
with the
(d-1)
-plane of points
a
that satisfy
{\langle a-y,b-y\rangle}=R(R-\sqrt{d})
.
The radius of
\Sigma_{2}
is
\rho_{2}=\sqrt{2\sqrt{d}R-d}
.
We need to show that
\theta_{0}-\varphi
is large enough for the

-cone to not only contain

, but also
\Sigma_{2}
, and thus also the integer point nearby.
Using the triangle inequality on the unit sphere, we observe that it suffices to show that the angle of the cone defined by

is less than
\theta_{0}-\varphi
.
We begin by computing the squared (Hausdorff) distance between the

\displaystyle\!\!R^{2}(\cos\varphi\!-\!\cos\theta_{0})^{2}\!+\!R^{2}(\sin\theta_{0}\!-\!\sin\varphi)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq R^{2}(\cos\varphi\!-\!\cos\theta_{0})^{2}\geq\left(\tfrac{R}{\sqrt{d}}\!-\!\tfrac{R}{3\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2}=\tfrac{4R^{2}}{9d}.

(25)

With the assumed lower bound on

, this is at least

.

.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 17
(Size for Integer Lattices).

For every
d\geq 2
, every chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
has diameter at most
O(k^{(-1+1/2^{d-1})/d})
.
Specifically, for
k\geq\nu_{d}(18d\sqrt{d})^{d}
, the diameter is bounded from above by
(18d\sqrt{d})\cdot{\nu_{d}}^{1/d}\cdot k^{(-1+1/2^{d-1})/d}
.

Proof.

Let
x
and
y
be points in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
, and let
S(x,r)
and
S(y,R)
be the spheres with centers
x,y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
and radii
r={\|{x}\|}
and
R={\|{y}\|}
.
Assuming the distance between
x
and
y
exceeds the claimed upper bound on the diameter, we prove that
x
and
y
belong to different chambers by showing that
S(x,r)
and
S(y,R)
enclose different sets of integer points.
We get
r,R>\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}-\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}
from Theorem
6
.
By the assumed lower bound on
k
, we have
\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}\geq 18d\sqrt{d}
and therefore
R\geq 18d\sqrt{d}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}
, so we can apply Lemma
16
.
Furthermore,
R>\frac{2}{3}\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}
, although the constant could be improved.

Assuming
r\leq R
, the hemisphere of points
a\in S(y,R)
with
{\langle a-y,y-x\rangle}\geq 0
lies outside
S(x,r)
.
By Lemma
15
, there are integer points enclosed by
S(y,R)
whose distance from the sphere is at most

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{d}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{R}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}}

\displaystyle<2\sqrt{d}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{d}\sqrt[d]{\nu_{d}}}{\sqrt[d]{k}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}}<\frac{D_{k}}{3\sqrt{d}},

(26)

in which
D_{k}=(18d\sqrt{d})\cdot{\nu_{d}}^{1/d}\cdot k^{(-1+1/2^{d-1})/d}
.
Note that
{\|{x}-{y}\|}>D_{k}
, by assumption.
The extra factor is
\nicefrac{{1}}{{(3\sqrt{d})}}=\cos\theta_{0}
, so there exists an integer point on or enclosed by
S(y,R)
that lies outside
S(x,r)
.
Hence
x
and
y
lie in different chambers, which implies the claimed upper bound on the diameter.

A chamber with diameter
D
can be enclosed in a cube with edges of length
D
.
This implies that the volume of the chamber is bounded by
D^{d}
.
Theorem
17
thus implies that the volume of a chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
is at most
O(k^{-1+1/2^{d-1}})
.

We can also get lower bounds for the maximum diameter and volume.
For example,
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}})}}
has unit area and by Theorem
12
consists of
O(k)
chambers.
It follows that the average and therefore also the maximum area of a chamber in this zone is
\Omega(k^{-1})
.
By Lemma
2
and Theorem
6
, the
k
-th Brillouin zone surrounds
0
at a distance about
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\pi}}}
from
0
.
The sum of diameters is therefore at least
\Omega(k^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
, which implies that the average and therefore the maximum diameter of a chamber is
\Omega(k^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}})
.
Using Theorem
14
, similar but weaker lower bounds can be obtained for integer lattices in
d\geq 3
dimensions.

The authors of this paper believe that the bound in Theorem
17
extends to lattices, but it does not extend to Delone sets.
Indeed we will see shortly that the bounds break down even for perturbations of the integer lattice.

6.2
Perturbed Integer Lattices

As proved above, the size of the largest chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin tessellation of the integer lattice approaches zero as
k
goes to infinity.
This property is not necessarily shared by arbitrarily small perturbations of
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
.

Theorem 18
(Size for Perturbed Integer Lattices).

For every
\tau<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists a perturbation
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
with magnitude at most
\tau
such that for every
k\geq 1
, there exists a point
b_{k}\in\varphi({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})
such that its
k
-th Brillouin zone contains a chamber with diameter at least
\tau
and volume at least
\nu_{d}[\frac{\tau}{2}]^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\tau<\frac{1}{2}
.
We first define a local perturbation,
\varphi_{k}
, to create a large chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of a specific point,
\varphi_{k}(0)=0
, and for a specific integer,
k
.
For
k=1
no perturbation is needed because the
1
-st Brilloun zone (the Voronoi domain) of the integer lattice contains a ball of radius
\frac{1}{2}
.

For
k\geq 2
, we construct
P=\varphi_{k}({{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d})
with
\varphi_{k}(0)=0
and
{\|{a}-{\varphi_{k}(a)}\|}\leq\tau
for every
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
x
be an interior point of
{{\rm Zone}_{k}{({0},{{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}})}}
, which implies that
k-1\geq 1
integer points lie in the interior of
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
,
0
lies on its boundary, and all other integer points lie outside the closed ball.
We have
{\|{x}\|}>\frac{1}{2}
, else the ball could not contain an integer point in its interior.
We construct
P
by moving the points
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
other than the origin
orthogonally away from the sphere bounding
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
if their distance to the closest point on the sphere is less than
\tau
.
Letting
\delta=|{\|{x}-{a}\|}-{\|{x}\|}|
be this distance, we define

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(a)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}a-(\tau-\delta)\cdot\tfrac{x-a}{{\|{x}-{a}\|}}&\mbox{\rm if~~~~~~\,}{\|{x}\|}<{\|{x}-{a}\|}<{\|{x}\|}+\tau,\\
a+(\tau-\delta)\cdot\tfrac{x-a}{{\|{x}-{a}\|}}&\mbox{\rm if~}{\|{x}\|}-\tau<{\|{x}-{a}\|}<{\|{x}\|},\\
a&\mbox{\rm otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The integer points with distance at least
\tau
from the sphere remain where they are, and the others are moved to a location at distance
\tau
from the sphere.
Since
\tau<\frac{1}{2}
, the images of the integer points are distinct.
Let
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
have distance less than
\frac{\tau}{2}
from
x
.
By construction, every point of
P
in the interior of
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
is in the closure of
B(x,{\|{x}\|}-\tau)
which is included in the interior of
B(y,{\|{y}\|})
because the distance between the centers of these balls is smaller than the difference of the radii.
Similarly, every point of
P
outside the closure of
B(x,{\|{x}\|})
is outside the interior of
B(x,{\|{x}\|}+\tau)
and thus also outside the closure of
B(y,{\|{y}\|})
.
Hence,
x
and
y
belong to the same chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in P
.
By construction, this chamber contains a ball with radius
\frac{\tau}{2}
and therefore has the claimed diameter and volume.

Note that the above construction can be performed for each
k
with a different point
b_{k}\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
instead of
0
.
By Theorem
7
, additional perturbations of the integer points outside the ball
B(b_{k},\sqrt[d]{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\nu_{d}}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}+\tau)

cannot destroy the large chamber created by the perturbation.
Hence, we can choose the points
b_{k}
with increasing distance from each other so that the respective balls do not intersect and thus the modifications do not interfere, yielding one perturbation
\varphi
that creates large chambers for every
k
.

7
Discussion

Brillouin zones originate with Léon Brillouin’s work on the propagation of electron waves in crystal lattices
[
8
]
.
This paper addresses fundamental geometric and combinatorial questions about these zones.
The study is interesting already for the integer lattices, which together with their perturbations are the focus of this paper.
We expect that most of our findings extend to general lattices in Euclidean space, if not verbatim then in spirit.
Some of our results also extend to broader categories, such as periodic sets and Delone sets; see the comments following the proofs of the theorems throughout this paper.
The reported work opens a number of questions for future inquiry:

1

Are the bounds proved throughout this paper tight?
In particular:

a

Can the
O(k)
bound on the number of chambers of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
proved in Theorem
12
be complemented with an
\Omega(k)
bound for the same quantity?

b

Can the upper bound on the number of chambers in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
given in Theorem
14
be improved to
O(k^{d-1})
?
Equivalently, does the inversion of
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
through the unit sphere in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
have only
O(k^{d-1})

k
-sets?

c

Is the bound on the maximum diameter of a chamber in the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{d}
proved in Theorem
17
asymptotically tight?
Figure
9
suggests it is in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.

2

Recall the notion of distortion of the boundary of the
k
-th Brillouin zone introduced in Section
4.3
(see Figure
5
).
Prove or disprove that the distortion converges to
\nicefrac{{4}}{{\pi}}
, which we note is the universal constant for the distortion of Voronoi paths in the plane
[
4
,
15
]
.

Appendix A
Computational Background

We support our theoretical findings with data that counts and measures Brillouin zones and their chambers in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
.
To generate this data for the integer lattice, we use a cut-off,

m>0
, and construct the arrangement defined by the bisectors of
0
and all integer points in
[-m,m]^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, denoted
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}
.
There are about
4m^{2}
lines forming an arrangement of
O(m^{4})
vertices, edges, and chambers.
Using a classic incremental algorithm,
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}
can be computed in time
O(m^{4})

[
12
, Chapter 7]
.
We use
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}
to collect the data, and for this we need to know how many Brillouin zones in
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}
are also in
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\infty}
.

Similarly, we collect the data for perturbations of
{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
by constructing the corresponding arrangements of perturbed bisectors.
As explained in Section
2.5
, we require that
0\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
is not perturbed, and we call the perturbation
weak
,
medium
, and
strong
if every integer point,
a\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}
, is mapped uniformly at random to
\varphi(a)\in a+[-\tau,\tau]^{2}
, in which
\tau=0.02
,
0.10
, and
0.50
, respectively.
Write
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\varphi)
for the arrangement of bisectors defined by
0
and all
\varphi(a)
with
a\in[-m,m]^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
Again we need to know how many Brillouin zones of
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\varphi)
are also in
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\infty}(\varphi)
.

Lemma A.1
(#Correct Brillouin Zones).

Let
\varphi\colon{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
be a perturbation of strength
\tau
.
For every
k<\frac{\pi}{4}[m+1-\sqrt{2}-(2\sqrt{2}+1)\tau]^{2}
, the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
in
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\varphi)
is also the
k
-th Brillouin zone of
0
in
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\infty}(\varphi)
.

Proof.

Let
b\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{2}\setminus[-m,m]^{2}
and note that the distance of
0
from the bisector defined by
0
and
\varphi(b)
is at least
\frac{1}{2}[m+1-\tau]
.
The restriction of
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\varphi)
to the disk with center
0
and radius
\frac{1}{2}[m+1-\tau]
is therefore the same as the restriction of
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\infty}(\varphi)
to the disk.
By Theorem
7
, the first
k
Brillouin zones of
0
for a perturbation with magnitude
\sqrt{2}\tau
are contained in the disk of radius
R_{k}(0)<\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\pi}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}+\sqrt{2}\tau
.
Combining these bounds gives
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{k}}{{\pi}}}+\nicefrac{{\sqrt{2}}}{{2}}+\sqrt{2}\tau<\frac{1}{2}[m+1-\tau]
, and therefore
k<\tfrac{\pi}{4}[m+1-\sqrt{2}-(2\sqrt{2}+1)\tau]^{2}
is a sufficient condition for
R_{k}(0)<\frac{1}{2}[m+1-\tau]
.

For example, setting
m=9
and
\tau=0.02
,
0.1
, and
0.5
, we get
k<56.8
,
52.8
, and
34.9
, respectively.
In words, the data we compute for weak, medium, and strong perturbations are correct up to
k=56
,
52
, and
34
, respectively, and possibly contaminated by missing bisectors beyond these values of
k
;
see the captions of Figures
2
,
4
,
5
,
7
, and
9
.

Two or more lines in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}
are
concurrent
if they all share a common point.
A non-trivial numerical aspect of the computations is the recognition of concurrent bisectors as such.
This is especially important when we count chambers in the highly degenerate bisector arrangement defined by the integer lattice.
We use rational arithmetic to recognize concurrency, as we now describe.
Let
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
and
b=(b_{1},b_{2})
be two linearly independent integer points.
The bisectors of
0,a
and
0,b
intersect in the center of the unique circle that passes through
0,a,b
.
To compute this point, we solve the linear system

\displaystyle 2a_{1}x_{1}+2a_{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2},

(30)

\displaystyle 2b_{1}x_{1}+2b_{2}x_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}.

(31)

Using Cramer’s rule, we get
x_{1}={\Delta_{1}}/{\Delta}
and
x_{2}={\Delta_{2}}/{\Delta}
, in which
\Delta=2a_{1}b_{2}-2b_{1}a_{2}
,
\Delta_{1}=(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2})b_{2}-(b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2})a_{1}
, and
\Delta_{2}=a_{1}(b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2})-b_{1}(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2})
.
Assuming the coordinates are integers of absolute size at most
m
, we have
|\Delta|,|\Delta_{1}|,|\Delta_{2}|\leq 4m^{3}
.
In this paper, we use
m\leq 9
, so each of these integers can be represented by
1+\log_{2}(4m^{3})\leq 13
bits.

To implement a perturbation, we scale up the integer lattice by a factor
p=10000
and randomly pick
\varphi(a)
from the integer points
pa+[-q,q]^{2}
, in which
q=200,1000,5000
depending on whether we desire a
weak
,
medium
, or
strong
perturbation.
This way the coordinates of the perturbed points are still integers so that concurrent lines can be recognized with rational arithmetic as described above.
By construction, the coordinates of the perturbed point have absolute size at most
pm+q
.
Accordingly, the relevant determinants satisfy
|\Delta|,|\Delta_{1}|,|\Delta_{2}|\leq 4[pm+q]^{3}
.
For the above choices, we have
pm+q\leq 95000<2^{17}
, so each of them can be represented by
1+\log_{2}(4[pm+q]^{3})\leq 54
bits.
This is still well within the limit of a
64
-bit computer word.
