On ADMM in Heterogeneous Federated Learning: Personalization, Robustness, and Fairness
Shengkun Zhu, Jinshan Zeng, Sheng Wang, Yuan Sun, Xiaodong Li, Yuan Yao, Zhiyong Peng

Abstract
Statistical heterogeneity is a root cause of tension among accuracy, fairness, and robustness of federated learning (FL), and is key in paving a path forward. Personalized federated learning (PFL) is an approach that aims to reduce the impact of statistical heterogeneity by developing personalized models for individual users, while also inherently providing benefits in terms of fairness and robustness. However, existing PFL frameworks focus on improving the performance of personalized models while neglecting the global model. This results in PFL suffering from lower solution accuracy when clients have different kinds of heterogeneous data. Moreover, these frameworks typically achieve sublinear convergence rates and rely on strong assumptions. In this paper, we employ the Moreau envelope as a regularized loss function and propose FLAME , an optimization framework by utilizing the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) to train personalized and global models. Due to the gradient-free nature of ADMM, FLAME alleviates the need for tuning the learning rate during training of the global model. We demonstrate that FLAME can generalize to the existing PFL and FL frameworks. Moreover, we propose a model selection strategy to improve performance in situations where clients have different types of heterogeneous data. Our theoretical analysis establishes the global convergence and two kinds of convergence rates for FLAME under mild assumptions. Specifically, under the assumption of gradient Lipschitz continuity, we obtain a sublinear convergence rate. Further assuming the loss function is lower semicontinuous, coercive, and either real analytic or semialgebraic, we can obtain constant, linear, and sublinear convergence rates under different conditions. We also theoretically demonstrate that FLAME is more robust and fair than the state-of-the-art methods on a class of linear problems. We thoroughly conduct experiments by utilizing six schemes to partition non-i.i.d. data, confirming the performance comparison among state-of-the-art methods. Our experimental findings show that FLAME outperforms state-of-the-art methods in convergence and accuracy, and it achieves higher test accuracy under various attacks and performs more uniformly across clients in terms of robustness and fairness.

1) Current PFL methods struggle to perform well when different clients have various types of heterogeneous data;
2) The convergence of existing PFL frameworks still relies on strong assumptions (gradient Lipschitz continuity, bounded variance, and bounded diversity);

III-C

Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers

ADMM is an optimization method that belongs to the class of augmented Lagrangian methods and is particularly well-suited for solving the following general problem
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}f(\boldsymbol{w})+g(\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\text{s.t. }A\boldsymbol{w}+B\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0},

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We directly give the augmented Lagrangian function of the problem as follows,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\pi})&:=f(\boldsymbol{w})+g(\boldsymbol{v})\\
&+\langle\boldsymbol{\pi},A\boldsymbol{w}+B\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{b}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}||A\boldsymbol{w}+B\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{b}||^{2},\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the dual variable, and
\rho>0
is the penalty parameter. After initializing the variables with
(\boldsymbol{w}^{0},\boldsymbol{v}^{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{0})
, ADMM iteratively performs the following steps:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}&=\operatorname{argmin}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v}^{t},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}),\\
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}&=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}),\\
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t+1}&=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}+\rho(A\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}+B\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{b}).\end{split}

ADMM exhibits distributed and parallel computing capabilities, effectively addresses equality-constrained problems, and provides global convergence guarantees
[
93
]
, making it particularly well-suited for tackling large-scale optimization problems. It finds widespread applications in distributed computing, machine learning, and related fields.

The Moreau envelope is an essential concept in the fields of mathematics and optimization

72
]

Definition 3

(Moreau envelope

Consider a function

F(\boldsymbol{w}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{\theta}||^{2},

(2)

where

\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{w}):=\text{prox}_{f/\lambda}(w)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{w}||^{2}\right\}\!.\!

(3)

The Moreau envelope provides a smooth approximation of the original function

. This approximation is helpful when dealing with optimization algorithms that require smooth functions. As
\lambda
becomes smaller, the Moreau envelope approaches the original function, making it useful for approximating and optimizing non-smooth functions.

To construct the objective function for PFL, we employ the approach outlined in
[
85
]
, where
f_{i}
is substituted by the Moreau envelope of
f_{i}
in the optimization Problem (
1
).
The specific formulation of the problem is presented as follows:

\begin{split}&\min_{\boldsymbol{w}}\!\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}F_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}),\\
\text{where }F_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}):=&\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}||^{2},\quad i\in[m].\end{split}

(5)

Note that
F_{i}
is the Moreau envelope of
f_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
is the personalized model of client
i
. The hyperparameter
\lambda
controls the influence of the global model
\boldsymbol{w}
on the personalized model
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
.
A higher value of
\lambda
provides an advantage to clients with unreliable data by harnessing extensive data aggregation, whereas a lower
\lambda
places greater emphasis on personalization for clients with a substantial amount of useful data. Note that
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
is used to prevent extreme cases where
\lambda=0
(no FL) or
\lambda=\infty
(no PFL).
The overall concept is to enable clients to develop their personalized models in different directions while remaining close to the global model
\boldsymbol{w}
contributed by every client.
Note that Problem (
5
) is a bi-level optimization problem. The conventional approach to solving bi-level problems typically involves initially using a first-order gradient method to solve
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
in the lower-level problem, obtaining an approximate solution. This approximate solution is then incorporated into the upper-level problem, followed by another round of the first-order gradient method to solve
\boldsymbol{w}
in the upper-level problem. Iterating through this process multiple times yields the final solutions. Even though the first-order gradient method is simple, it suffers from low solution accuracy and is cumbersome to fine-tune parameters like the learning rate. To address these issues, we propose a relaxed form of Problem (
5
) as follows,

\min_{\boldsymbol{w},\Theta}\Bigl\{f(\Theta,\boldsymbol{w}):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\Bigl(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}||^{2}\Bigr)\Bigr\},

(6)

where
\Theta:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is the set of personalized models. Note that Problem (
6
) is a multi-block optimization problem with respect to
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\Theta
. It is obvious that Problem (
6
) serves as a lower bound for Problem (
5
).
We aim to learn an optimal personalized model
\Theta^{*}
and an optimal global model
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
that minimizes
f(\Theta,\boldsymbol{w})
. That is

\displaystyle\Theta^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*}:=\argmin_{\Theta,\boldsymbol{w}}f(\Theta,\boldsymbol{w}),

(7)

and the corresponding optimal function value is given as

\displaystyle f^{*}:=f(\Theta^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*}).

(8)

Given that ADMM, as a primal-dual method, is often regarded as more iteration-stable and converges faster compared to gradient-based approaches, we consider employing ADMM to solve Problem (
6
).
Firstly, we consider introducing the auxiliary variable
W:=\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
to transform Problem (
6
) into a separable form (with respect to a partition or splitting of the variable into multi-block variables). That is

\begin{split}&\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}}\Bigr\{\tilde{f}(\Theta,W)\!:=\!\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\Bigl(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}||^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl\},\!\!\\
&\,\,\,\text{ s.t. }\,\boldsymbol{w}_{i}=\boldsymbol{w},\quad i\in[m],\end{split}

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
can be regarded as the
local model
of client
i
. Note that Problem (
9
) is equivalent to Problem (
6
) in the sense that the optimal solutions coincide.
We consider using the exact penalty method, ADMM, to solve Problem (
9
), as it involves linear constraints and multiple block variables, making ADMM well-suited for this optimization problem.
Moreover, in Section
IV-E
, we will analyze in detail how other PFL frameworks can be seen as solving Problem (
9
) by using an inexact penalty method.
To implement ADMM for Problem (
9
), we establish the corresponding augmented Lagrangian function as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\Theta,W,\Pi,\boldsymbol{w})&:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}),\\
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i},\boldsymbol{w})&:=\alpha_{i}(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\!-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}||^{2})\\
&+\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}||\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}||^{2},\end{split}

(10)

where
\Pi:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
is the set of dual variables, and
\rho>0
is the penalty parameter. The ADMM framework for solving Problem (
9
) can be summarized as follows: after initializing the variables with
(\Theta^{0},W^{0},\Pi^{0},\boldsymbol{w}^{0})
, the following update steps are executed iteratively for each
t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}^{t}),

(11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}(\lambda\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}+\rho\boldsymbol{w}^{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t}),

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t}+\rho(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{t}),

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\mathcal{L}(\Theta^{t+1},W^{t+1},\boldsymbol{w},\Pi^{t+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{i}^{t+1}).

(14)

Note that we do not provide a closed-form solution for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
due to the possibly non-convex nature of
f_{i}
. We will discuss this issue in detail in Section
IV-C
.

IV-B

Stationary Points

We present the optimal conditions of Problems (
6
) and (
9
).

Definition 4

(Stationary point)
.

A point
(\Theta^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*})
is a stationary point of Problem (
6
) if it satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*})+\lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})&=0,&i\in[m],\\
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*}&=0.&\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

A point
(\Theta^{*},W^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*},\Pi^{*})
is a stationary point of Problem (
9
) if it satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*})+\lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{*})&=0,&i\in[m],\\
\alpha_{i}\lambda(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{*})+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{*}&=0,&i\in[m],\\
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{*}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}&=0,&i\in[m],\\
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{*}&=0.&\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

Definition
4
indicates that a locally optimal solution of Problem (
6
) (resp. (
9
)) must satisfy (
15
) (resp. (
16
)). When
f_{i}
is convex for any
i\in[m]
, then a point is the globally optimal solution of Problem (
6
) (resp. (
9
)) if and only if it satisfies (
15
) (resp. (
16
)). Moreover, a stationary point
(\Theta^{*},W^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*},\Pi^{*})
of Problem (
9
) can imply (
15
), which indicates that
(\Theta^{*},\boldsymbol{w}^{*})
is also a stationary point of Problem (
6
).

(18)

which is useful in the convergence analysis.

and

IV-D

Model Selection

Since different clients often have various types of heterogeneous data, we refer to this situation as a
hybrid skew
. As shown in Fig.
2
, client 1 has label skew data, while client 2 has quantity skew data.
Personalized models often perform well under label skew but generally lag behind global models in other types of heterogeneous data, as validated in our numerical experiments.
Therefore, in the context of PFL, we propose a model selection strategy: after training models for each client, we choose the model with better performance between personalized and global models for deployment, which we refer to as a
hybrid model
.
However, existing PFL frameworks usually do not focus on the performance of global models
[
85
,
53
]
. They even make the global model unusable by mapping it to a low-dimensional space through low-dimension projection
[
59
]
. Due to the poor performance of the global model, these methods typically do not perform well on heterogeneous data with hybrid skew. In contrast,
FLAME
uses ADMM as an optimization method, which can improve the accuracy of both personalized and global models, thereby adapting to various types of heterogeneous data.

Fig. 2:
An example of
FLAME
. Various clients may have different types of non-i.i.d. data (label and quantity skew). Selected clients download the global model from the server, and update their personalized models and local models, while unselected clients keep their model parameters unchanged. Client selection is performed on the server, and update messages
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
uploaded by clients are used to update the global model.

Connections with Existing Work

In this section, we demonstrate the connections and comparison between

and other FL frameworks:
pFedMe

[

]
,

[

, and

[

. Note that

[

\displaystyle\min_{\Theta,\boldsymbol{w}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),\,\,\text{s.t. }\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}=\boldsymbol{w},\,i\in[m].

V
Theoretical Analysis

V-A

Main Assumptions

We begin by providing the definitions of
graph
,
semicontinuous
,
real analytic
, and
semialgebraic
functions that are used in our assumptions.

Definition 5

(Graph)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an extended real-valued function, its graph is defined by

\displaystyle\text{Graph}(f):=\{(\boldsymbol{x},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}:y=f(\boldsymbol{x})\},

and its domain is defined by
\text{dom}(f):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:f(\boldsymbol{x})<+\infty\}
. If
f
is a proper function, i.e.,
\text{dom}(f)\neq\emptyset
, then the set of its global minimizers is defined by

\displaystyle\argmin f:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:f(\boldsymbol{x})=\inf f\}

Definition 6

(Semicontinuous)
.

A function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is called lower semicontinuous if for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\inf f(x)\geq f(x_{0}).

Definition 7

(Real analytic function
[
41
]
)
.

A function
f
is real analytic on an open set
\mathcal{X}
in the real line if for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
f(x)
can be represented as

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}a_{i}(x-x_{0})^{i},

where the coefficients
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{+\infty}
are real numbers and the series is convergent to
f(x)
for
x
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

Definition 8

(Semialgebraic set and function
[
11
]
)
.

a)

A set
\mathcal{X}
is called semialgebraic if it can be represented by

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\cup_{i=1}^{r}\cap_{j=1}^{s}\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:P_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})=0,Q_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})>0\},

where
P_{ij}
and
Q_{ij}
are real polynomial functions for
1\leq i\leq r
and
1\leq j\leq s
.

b)

A function
f
is called semialgebraic if its graph
Graph(f)
is semialgebraic.

According to
[
63
,
11
,
80
]
, the class of semialgebraic sets is stable under operations such as finite union, finite intersection, Cartesian product, and complementation. Common examples include polynomial functions, the indicator function of a semialgebraic set, and the Euclidean norm.
Next, we present the assumptions used in our convergence analysis.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose that

a)

the expected loss function
f_{i},\,i\in[m]
is a proper lower semicontinuous and nonnegative function,

b)

the expected loss function
f_{i},\,i\in[m]
is either real analytic or semialgebraic.

Assumption 2

(Gradient Lipschitz continuity)
.

The expected loss function
f_{i}
is
L
-smooth (gradient Lipschitz continue), i.e., for
\forall\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
, the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})-\nabla

\displaystyle f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})\|\leq L\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|.

(20)

Assumption 3
.

The expected loss function
f_{i}
is coercive. That is,
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\to+\infty
when
\theta_{i}\to+\infty
.

According to
[
41
,
63
,
11
,
80
]
, most of the commonly used loss functions, such as squared, logistic, hinge, or cross-entropy losses, can be verified to satisfy Assumption
1
.
Assumption
2
is a standard assumption in the convergence analysis of FL, as evidenced by
[
85
,
114
,
115
,
58
]
.
Assumption
3
is widely used in establishing the convergence properties of optimization algorithms to bound the generated sequence, as evidenced by
[
107
,
115
,
108
]
.
Our assumptions for convergence are weaker than the assumptions of other PFL frameworks
[
85
,
53
]
, which include gradient Lipschitz continuity, bounded variance, and bounded diversity.

V-B

Global Convergence

Our global convergence analysis is based on the analytical framework delineated in
[
4
]
.
Let
\mathcal{P}^{t}:=(\Theta^{t},W^{t},\Pi^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})
, then by defining the Lyapunov function
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})
as

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\iota_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{t},\end{split}

(21)

where
\iota_{i}=\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\bigl((\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}-1)\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{L\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}\bigr)(1-\upsilon_{i})}

the analytical framework identifies four crucial components: ensuring
sufficient descent
and
relative error
conditions, verifying the
continuity
condition, and confirming the
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KŁ) property
of sequences
\{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})\}
.
Next, we
present two key lemmas of sufficient decent and relative error, while leaving other details in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 1

(Sufficient descent)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
holds. Let
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
denote the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
, let each client
i
choose
\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\rho)}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}<0
,
\rho\geq\lambda\alpha_{i}
, and
\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{L\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}>0
, then for all
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})\geq\mathcal{D}_{1}\Delta\Gamma^{t+1},\,\text{where}

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma^{t+1}\!:=\!\sum_{i=1}^{m}(

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\|^{2}\!+\!\|\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}_{i}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t}\|^{2}\!+\!\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}\!-\!\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t}\|^{2}),

and
\mathcal{D}_{1}:=\min_{i}\{\frac{\rho}{2},\frac{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\rho)}{\rho^{2}}\}
.

Lemma 2

(Relative error)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
holds. Let
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
denote the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
, and let
\varepsilon^{t+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\epsilon_{i}^{t+1}
, then for all

, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\partial\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})\|^{2}\leq D_{2}(\Delta\Gamma^{t+1}+\varepsilon^{t+1}),\,\text{where}

\displaystyle\partial\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t}):=(\{\partial_{\Theta}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\},\{\partial_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\},\{\partial_{\Pi}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\},\{\partial_{\boldsymbol{w}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\})(\mathcal{P}^{t}),

and
D_{2}:=\max_{i}\{4\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}})+2\alpha_{i}^{2}(L^{2}+2\lambda^{2})+2\rho^{2}+2\}
.

Note that in the relative error, there exists an error term
\varepsilon^{t+1}
on the right-hand side of the inequality. The current theoretical framework cannot accommodate the presence of this error term
[
93
]
. Next, we provide an analysis of global convergence in the presence of this error term.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
2

,

a)

Sequence
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
is bounded.

b)

Sequences
\{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})\}
,
\{f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})\}
, and
\{\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t})\}
converge to the same value, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t}).

(22)

c)

\nabla_{\Theta}f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})
,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})
,
\nabla_{\Theta}\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t})
, and
\nabla_{W}\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t})
eventually vanish, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\nabla_{\Theta}f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\nabla_{\Theta}\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t})=0,

(23)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}f(\Theta^{t},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\nabla_{W}\tilde{f}(\Theta^{t},W^{t})=0.

(24)

Theorem
1
establishes the convergence property of the objective function values. We next consider the convergence property of the sequences
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2
and
3
hold, let each client
i
choose
\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\rho)}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}<0
,
\rho\geq\lambda\alpha_{i}
, and
\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{L\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}>0
, then the following results hold.

a)

The accumulating point
\mathcal{P}^{\infty}
of sequences
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
is a stationary point of Problem (
9
), and
(\Theta^{\infty},W^{\infty})
is a stationary point of Problem (
6
).

b)

Under Assumption
1
, the sequence
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
converges to
\mathcal{P}^{\infty}
.

Note that the establishment of Theorem
2
does not rely on the convexity of the loss functions
f_{i}
. Consequently, the sequence is ensured to reach a stationary point for Problems (
6
) and (
9
). Furthermore, if we make an additional assumption of convexity for
f_{i}
, then the sequence will converge to the optimal solution of Problems (
6
) and (
9
).

V-C

Convergence Rate

We have demonstrated the convergence of Algorithm
1
. Next, we aim to examine the rate of this convergence. Specifically, we would like to explore two types of convergence rates under different assumptions.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds. Let each client
i
choose
\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\rho)}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}<0
,
\rho\geq\lambda\alpha_{i}
, and
\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{L\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}>0
, then for all
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}||\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})||^{2}\leq\frac{D_{2}}{D_{1}T}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{0})-f^{*}\Bigr)+D_{2}\varepsilon^{1},

(25)

According to Theorem
3
, we obtain the summation of
||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})||^{2}
vanishes with a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{T})
, which is considered sublinear. It is worth noting that this convergence rate is established solely under Assumption
2
, which pertains to gradient Lipschitz continuity, a condition that is not hard to satisfy. In contrast, the results in
[
85
,
53
,
59
]
are obtained under more assumptions, including gradient Lipschitz continuity, bounded variance, and bounded diversity.
If we further suppose that Assumption
1
holds, then we can achieve an improved convergence rate as follows.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\{\mathcal{P}^{t}\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
, and
\mathcal{P}^{\infty}
be its limit, let
\psi(x)=\frac{\sqrt{c}}{1-\tau}x^{1-\tau}
be a desingularizing function (see Definition
9
), where
c>0
and
\tau\in[0,1)
, then under Assumptions
1
and
2
, let each client
i
choose
\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}(1+\rho)}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}<0
,
\rho\geq\lambda\alpha_{i}
, and
\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\frac{L\alpha_{i}+\rho}{2}>0
, the following results hold.

a)

If
\tau=0
, then there exists a
t_{1}
such that the sequence
\{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})\}
,
t\geq t_{1}
converges in a finite number of iterations.

b)

If
\tau\in(0,1/2]
, then there exists a
t_{2}
such that for any
t\geq t_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})\!-\!\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\!\leq\!(\frac{cD_{2}}{D_{1}\!+\!cD_{2}})^{t-t_{2}}(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t_{2}})\!-\!f^{*}\!+\!D_{1}\varepsilon^{t+1}).

c)

If
\tau\in(1/2,1)
, then there exists a
t_{3}
such that for any
t\geq t_{3}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\leq\Bigl(\frac{cD_{2}}{(2\tau-1)\mu D_{1}(t-t_{3})}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2\tau-1}},

where
\mu>0
is a constant.

Theorem
4
shows that when
\tau=0
, the convergence rate reaches a constant. For
\tau\in(0,1/2]
, the convergence rate is linear, and for
\tau\in(1/2,1)
, the convergence rate is sublinear.
Note that although Theorem
4
requires Assumptions
1
and
2
to hold, they are generally satisfied for most loss functions and remains weaker than those in
[
85
,
53
,
59
]
.

V-D

Robustness and Fairness

Inspired by
[
59
]
, we explore the robustness and fairness benefits of
FLAME
on a class of linear problems and compare
FLAME
with
pFedMe

[
85
]
and
Ditto

[
53
]
. We focus on a simplified setting as in
[
59
]
: infinite local update steps, a single communication round, and all clients participating.
Suppose the truly personalized model on client
i
is
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
, each client possesses
N
samples, and the covariate on client
i
is
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is fixed. The observations are generated by
y_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}+z_{i,j}
, where
z_{i,j}
denotes an i.i.d. Gaussian noise with distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
Then the loss on client
i
is
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(y_{i,j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
. For simplicity, we assume
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{\top}=Nb\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
, and there are
m_{a}
malicious clients and
m_{b}
benign clients, with
m_{a}+m_{b}=m
.
We examine the robustness of
FLAME
under three types of Byzantine attacks:

•

Same-value attacks

[
59
]
: The message sent by a Byzantine client
i
is set as
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{(ma)}=p\boldsymbol{1}_{d}
, where
p\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2})
;

•

Sign-flipping attacks

[
5
]
: The message sent by a Byzantine client
i
is set as
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{(ma)}=-|p|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
, where
p\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2})
;

•

Gaussian attacks

[
98
]
: The message sent by a Byzantine client
i
is set as
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{(ma)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\gamma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
.

Proposition 2
.

Let the average testing losses on benign clients for the personalized and global models of
FLAME
,
pFedMe
, and
Ditto
be
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{FLAME-PM}}
,
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{pFedMe-PM}}
,
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{Ditto-PM}}
,
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{FLAME-GM}}
,
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{pFedMe-GM}}
,
\text{Loss}^{\texttt{Ditto-GM}}
, let
q:=\frac{2\lambda\alpha_{i}}{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}\frac{b}{b+\lambda}<1
, then under the three types of Byzantine attacks, the following results hold.

\displaystyle\text{Loss}^{\texttt{FLAME-GM}}\leq\text{Loss}^{\texttt{pFedMe-GM}}=\text{Loss}^{\texttt{Ditto-GM}},

when
q\geq\frac{mNb\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{b}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b}}{d\sigma^{2}+m_{b}Nb}
under same-value and Gaussian attacks, and
q\geq\frac{\frac{1}{m_{b}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}}{\frac{d}{m^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{m_{b}\sigma^{2}}{bm}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\frac{\trace(\boldsymbol{V}_{i})}{d}\Bigr)+\frac{m_{a}}{m_{b}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}}
under sign-flipping attacks, and

\displaystyle\text{Loss}^{\texttt{FLAME-PM}}\leq\text{Loss}^{\texttt{pFedMe-PM}}=\text{Loss}^{\texttt{Ditto-PM}},

when
q\geq\frac{mb(m_{b}N\lambda\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{b}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b}-d\sigma^{2})}{d\sigma^{2}m_{b}\lambda+m_{b}^{2}Nb\lambda\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{b}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b}}
under same-value and Gaussian attacks, ​and
q\geq\frac{\frac{b\lambda}{m_{b}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}-\frac{bd\sigma^{2}}{mN}}{\frac{d\sigma^{2}m_{b}\lambda}{m^{2}N}+\frac{b\lambda d}{m^{2}}\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\!\!\!\!\frac{\trace(\boldsymbol{V}_{i})}{d}+\frac{b\lambda m_{a}}{m_{b}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}}
under sign-flipping attacks, where

\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b}\!=\!\frac{1}{m_{b}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^{\prime}}
,
\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}\!=\!\frac{1}{m}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{b}}\!\!\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\!-\!\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\!\!\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\gamma\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^{\prime}}\Bigr)
, and
\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\!=\!\frac{\pi-2}{\pi}\gamma^{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\top}+\gamma^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{bm}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
.

The proof of Proposition
2
can be obtained in Appendix
B-C
. Proposition
2
implies
FLAME
outperforms both
pFedMe
and
Ditto
in terms of robustness under regular conditions. From the constraints on
q
, we can see that when the dataset has many features, and each client has a small number of data points,
m_{b}N\lambda\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{b}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{b}-d\sigma^{2}
and
\frac{b\lambda^{2}}{m_{b}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}-\frac{bd\sigma^{2}\lambda}{mN}
may be smaller than 0. In this case, for any
q\in(0,1)
, the robustness of
FLAME
is better than that of
pFedMe
and
Ditto
. Therefore,
FLAME
is a potentially better PFL framework for scenarios with many features and limited dataset sizes in each client.
Next, we turn to the fairness analysis of
FLAME
.

Proposition 3
.

Let the variance of test losses on different clients for the personalized and global models of
FLAME
,
pFedMe
, and
Ditto
be

\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{FLAME-PM}})\}
,
\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{pFedMe-PM}})\}
,
\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{Ditto-PM}})\}
,
\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{FLAME-GM}})\}
,
\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{pFedMe-GM}})\}
,
\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{Ditto-GM}})\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{FLAME-PM}})\}<\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{pFedMe-PM}})\}=\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{Ditto-PM}})\},

\displaystyle\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{FLAME-GM}})\}<\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{pFedMe-GM}})\}=\var\{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{\texttt{Ditto-GM}})\}.

The proof of Proposition
3
is given in Appendix
B-D
.
Proposition
3
demonstrates that
FLAME
consistently results in more uniform test losses, which implies
FLAME
is more fair than
pFedMe
and
Ditto
.

Partitions.

To accommodate data heterogeneity, we adopt the same data partitioning strategies as described in
[
50
]
and
[
104
]
, which respectively are a good experimental study on FL with non-i.i.d. data silos and a good review of heterogeneous FL.
These partitions encompass label skew, feature skew, quality skew, and quantity skew. Regarding label skew, quality skew, and quantity skew, we conduct experiments on
MNIST
,
FMNIST
,
MNIST
, and
CIFAR10
datasets. Additionally, we address feature skew specifically on the
FEMNIST

For label skew, we consider two scenarios.
The first involves
quantity-based label imbalance
, where we organize the training data by their labels and distribute them into shards, with each client being assigned 2-6 shards randomly.
The second scenario, referred to as
distribution-based label imbalance
, involves allocating a portion of samples from each label to every client based on the Dirichlet distribution. Specifically, we sample from
p_{k}\sim Dir(\beta)
to assign a
p_{ki}
proportion of samples of class
k
to client
i
. Here,
Dir(\cdot)
denotes the Dirichlet distribution with a concentration parameter
\beta>0
.
For feature skew, we propose partitioning the
FEMNIST
dataset into different clients based on individual writers. Given that character features such as stroke width and slant often vary among writers, a discernible feature skew naturally arises across these clients.
For quality skew, we consider utilizing
noise-based quality imbalance
, which involves introducing varying levels of Gaussian noise to each client’s local dataset to achieve different quality distributions.
Specifically, given a noise level
\sigma
, we add
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\sim Gau(\sigma\cdot i/m)
for client
i
, where
Gau(\sigma\cdot i/m)
is a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance
\sigma\cdot i/m
.
For quantity skew, we employ the Dirichlet distribution to distribute varying amounts of data samples among each party.
Specifically, we sample from
p\sim Dir(\beta)
and assign a
p_{i}
proportion of samples to client
i
.
Moreover, to illustrate that different clients may possess various types of heterogeneous data, we designate half of the clients with quantity-based label imbalance data and the other half with quantity skew data, which we refer to as
hybrid skew
.

VI-D

Summary of Lessons Learned

We have compared the performance of
FLAME
with state-of-the-art methods and validated the impact of algorithmic hyperparameters on the performance of
FLAME
.
Our experimental findings have led to the following definitive conclusions:

•

Under label skew, personalized models typically achieve better accuracy. In contrast, global models generally attain higher accuracy under feature skew, quantity skew, and quality skew. When dealing with hybrid skew, our model selection strategy can effectively improve accuracy.

•

FLAME
demonstrates superior accuracy and convergence compared to state-of-the-art methods. This is particularly evident in its personalized and global models, which achieve higher accuracy and faster convergence.

•

The robustness and fairness of
FLAME
outperform those of state-of-the-art methods. This is primarily evidenced by
FLAME
exhibiting smaller variances in testing losses and achieving lower testing losses under Byzantine attacks.

•

Due to the fact that
FLAME
does not require the adjustment of learning rate when training the global model, it significantly reduces the burden of hyperparameter tuning in comparison to
pFedMe
and
Ditto
.

•

Choosing appropriate parameters can significantly enhance the performance of
FLAME
. The regularization parameter
\lambda
can adjust the gap between personalized and global models, with a larger
\lambda
narrowing the gap between them. Increasing local iterations can improve model accuracy, thus expediting convergence. Reducing the penalty parameter can loosen the constraints, thereby accelerating convergence. When communication capacity permits, it is advisable to engage with a greater number of clients whenever possible, as this can expedite convergence.

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we proposed a PFL framework,
FLAME
, to address the impact of various types of heterogeneous data across different clients.
We formulated the optimization problem for PFL based on the Moreau envelope and solved it using the ADMM. We proposed a model selection strategy that chooses the model with higher accuracy from either the personalized or global models.
We established global convergence for
FLAME
and proposed two kinds of convergence rates under mild conditions.
We theoretically demonstrated that
FLAME
has improved robustness and fairness compared to
pFedMe
and
Ditto
on a class of linear problems.
Our experimental results demonstrated the superior performance of
FLAME
in terms of accuracy, convergence, robustness, and fairness on various kinds of heterogeneous data compared to state-of-the-art methods. Furthermore,
FLAME
, when applied to solving the global model, eliminates the need for learning rate adjustments, thereby alleviating the burden of hyperparameter tuning in contrast to
pFedMe
and
Ditto
.

In the future, we will focus on addressing privacy issues in FL, with an emphasis on using techniques such as encryption and differential privacy to mitigate privacy leakages.

Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz Property of Lagrangian Function
\mathcal{L}

To prove Theorem 1, we need to demonstrate that the KŁ property holds for the considered Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}
.
We begin by presenting the definition of KŁ property and elucidating some properties of real analytic and semialgebraic functions.

Definition 9

(Desingularizing Function)
.

A function
\psi:[0,\kappa)\to(0,+\infty)
satisfying the following conditions is a desingularizing function:

a)

\psi
is concave and continuously differentiable on
(0,\kappa)
;

b)

\psi
is continuous at 0 and
\psi(0)=0
;

c)

For any
x\in(0,\kappa),

\psi^{\prime}(x)>0
.

Definition 10

(Fréchet Subdifferential
[
76
]
and Limiting Subdifferential
[
71
]
)
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\text{dom}(h)
, the Fréchet subdifferential of
h
at
\boldsymbol{x}
, represented by
\hat{\partial}h(\boldsymbol{x})
, is the set of vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{x},}\inf_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{h(\boldsymbol{y})-h(\boldsymbol{x})-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}\geq 0.

When
\boldsymbol{x}\notin\text{dom}(h)
, we set
\hat{\partial}h(\boldsymbol{x})=\emptyset
. The limiting subdifferential (or simply subdifferential) of
h
, represented by
\partial h(\boldsymbol{x})
at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\text{dom}(h)
is defined by

\displaystyle\partial h(\boldsymbol{x}):=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\exists\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\to\boldsymbol{x},h(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\to h(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{z}^{t}\in\hat{\partial}h(\boldsymbol{x})\to\boldsymbol{z}\}.

Definition 11

(Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz Property
[
12
]
)
.

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is said to have Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KŁ) Property at
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\text{dom}(\partial h)
if there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, a constant
\kappa
, and a desingularizing function
\psi
, such that for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in U\cap\text{dom}(\partial h)
and
h(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})<h(\boldsymbol{x})<h(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})+\kappa
, it holds that,

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(h(\boldsymbol{x})-h(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}))\text{dist}(0,\partial h(\boldsymbol{x}))\geq 1,

(40)

where
\text{dist}(0,\partial h(\boldsymbol{x})):=\inf\{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|:\boldsymbol{z}\in\partial h(\boldsymbol{x})\}
represents the distance between zero to the set
\partial h(\boldsymbol{x})
.
If
h
satisfies the KŁ property, then
f
is called a KŁ function.

The definition of the KŁ property means that the function under consideration is sharp up to a reparametrization
[
3
]
. Particularly, when
h
is smooth, finite-valued, and
h(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=0
, then (
40
) can be represented as

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\psi\circ h)(\boldsymbol{x})\|\geq 1.

(41)

(
41
) can be interpreted as follows:
by reparametrizing the values of
h
through
\psi
, we obtain a well-defined function. The function
\psi
serves the purpose of transforming a singular region, where gradients are arbitrarily small, into a regular region, characterized by gradients bounded away from zero.
The class of KŁ functions encompasses a diverse range of function types, comprising real analytic functions (as defined in Definition
7
), semialgebraic functions (as delineated in Definition
8
), as well as tame functions defined within certain o-minimal structures
[
45
]
. Additionally, it encompasses continuous subanalytic functions
[
12
]
and locally strongly convex functions
[
99
]
.

Lemma 5

(Properties of real analytic functions
[
41
]
)
.

The sums, products, and compositions of real analytic functions are real analytic functions.

Lemma 6

(Properties of semialgebraic functions
[
11
]
)
.

a)

The finite union, finite intersection, and complement of semialgebraic sets are semialgebraic. The closure and the interior of a semialgebraic set are semialgebraic.

b)

The composition

of semialgebraic mappings

and

is semialgebraic.

c)

The sum of two semialgebraic functions is semialgebraic.

Lemma 7

(Subanalytic functions
[

]
)
.

a)

b)

and
f_{2}

is a subanalytic function if at least one of them map a bounded set to a bounded set or if both of them are nonnegative.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}-\frac{cD_{2}}{R}\frac{\varepsilon^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}.

(116)

a)

If
\varepsilon^{t+1}
is a lower-order infinitesimal of
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{2\tau}
, then we can obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}=\infty.

(117)

Based on (
117
), we can derive the convergence rate of
\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}\to 0
is faster than
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{t+1}\to 0
, which implies

\displaystyle\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}<\min_{i}\nu_{i}\times\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}<\frac{1}{R}\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau},

(118)

Note that (
118
) is conflict with
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{-2\tau}\geq\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{-2\tau}/R
, then
\varepsilon^{t+1}
cannot be a lower-order infinitesimal of
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{2\tau}
.

b)

If
\varepsilon^{t+1}
is a higher-order infinitesimal of
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{2\tau}
, then we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}=0.

(119)

If
\varepsilon^{t+1}
and
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{2\tau}
are same-order infinitesimals, then we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}=\alpha\neq 0.

(120)

Then there exists
Q>0
such that
\frac{\varepsilon^{t+1}}{\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\bigr)^{2\tau}}\leq Q
. Therefore, we obtain that

\displaystyle\phi\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)-\phi\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{R}-\frac{cD_{2}Q}{R}.

(121)

Consider another case, if
\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{-2\tau}<\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{-2\tau}/R
, then we can infer

\displaystyle R^{\frac{1}{2\tau}}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)<\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty}),

(122)

which implies

\displaystyle\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}

\displaystyle>\overline{R}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau},

(123)

\displaystyle\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}-\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}

\displaystyle>(\overline{R}-1)\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau},

(124)

where
\overline{R}=R^{\frac{2\tau-1}{\tau}}>1
. Since
\overline{R}-1>0
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\to 0^{+}
, then there exists
\overline{\mu}>0
such that

for all
t\geq t_{3}
.
Therefore, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}-\Bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}\geq\overline{\mu}>0

(125)

for all
t\geq t_{3}
. Then we bound

\displaystyle\phi\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)-\phi\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)=

\displaystyle\frac{cD_{2}}{(1-2\tau)D_{1}}\Biggl(\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}-\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)^{1-2\tau}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{c\overline{\mu}D_{2}}{(2\tau-1)D_{1}}.

(126)

If we define

, one can combine (
111
) and (
126
) to obtain that

\displaystyle\phi\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)-\phi\Bigl(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\Bigr)\geq\mu

(127)

for all
t\geq t_{3}
. By summing (
127
) from
t_{3}
to some

greater than
t_{3}
, we obtain

(128)

which implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{\infty})\leq\Bigl(\frac{(2\tau-1)\mu D_{1}}{cD_{2}}(t-t_{3})\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{1-2\tau}},

(129)

which completes the proof.

p\geq 1
and
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{sub}\times d}
is a random matrix that is generated initially and will not vary anymore.

d_{sub}
is the dimension of the shared-and-fixed random subspace.
Without loss of generality, we consider the case where
p=2
, referring to
Lp-Proj-2
.
Next, we consider converting (
182
) into the following optimization problem using the same approach as applied to
FLAME
,

\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}}\Bigr\{\tilde{f}(\Theta,W):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\Bigl(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}||^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl\},\,\,\text{ s.t. }\,\boldsymbol{w}_{i}=\boldsymbol{w},\,\,i\in[m].\end{split}

(183)

To implement ADMM for Problem (
183
), we establish the corresponding augmented Lagrangian function as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\Theta,W,\Pi,\boldsymbol{w})&:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}),\\
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i},\boldsymbol{w})&:=\alpha_{i}(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}||^{2})+\langle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}||\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}||^{2},\end{split}

(184)

The ADMM framework for solving Problem (
183
) can be summarized as follows: after initializing the variables with
(\Theta^{0},W^{0},\Pi^{0},\boldsymbol{w}^{0})
, the following update steps are executed iteratively for each
t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}^{t},),

(185)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1},\boldsymbol{w}_{i},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{t}_{i},\boldsymbol{w}^{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda\alpha_{i}+\rho}(\lambda\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}+\rho\boldsymbol{w}^{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t}),

(186)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{t}+\rho(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{t}),

(187)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\mathcal{L}(\Theta^{t+1},W^{t+1},\boldsymbol{w},\Pi^{t+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{t+1}+\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{i}^{t+1}).

(188)

In Algorithm
2
, we introduce the ADMM-based
Lp-Proj-2
. Except for the updates of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
, all other aspects are identical to
FLAME
.
After being mapped by the projection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
, the global model is compressed into a lower-dimensional matrix, rendering it no longer useful.
