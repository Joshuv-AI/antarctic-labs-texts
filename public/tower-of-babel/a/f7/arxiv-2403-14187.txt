Abstract
. In this paper, we revisit asymptotic stability for the two-dimensional incompressible porous media equation and the Stokes transport system in a periodic channel. It is well-known that a stratified density, which strictly decreases in the vertical direction, is asymptotically stable under sufficiently small and smooth perturbations. We provide improvements in the regularity assumptions on the perturbation and in the convergence rate. Unlike the standard approach for stability analysis relying on linearized equations, we directly address the nonlinear problem by exploiting the energy structure of each system. While it is widely known that the potential energy is a Lyapunov functional in both systems, our key observation is that the second derivative of the potential energy reveals a (degenerate) coercive structure, which arises from the fact that the solution converges to the minimizer of the energy.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate asymptotic stability in the incompressible porous media equation (IPM) and the Stokes transport system in a periodic channel
\Omega:=\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1)
. To introduce the models, let us consider a continuity equation with a velocity field
u(t,x)
,

\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(u\rho)=0,\text{ in $\Omega$ and $\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x)$,}

for some nonnegative scalar-valued function
\rho_{0}
, which will be referred to as density throughout the paper. Neglecting physical constants, the velocity
u
in each model is determined by
\rho
via

\begin{cases}u=-\nabla p-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix},\text{ with }\nabla\cdot u=0\text{ in $\Omega$ and }u\cdot\vec{n}=0\text{ on $\partial\Omega$,}&\text{(IPM) }\\
\Delta u=-\nabla p-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix},\text{ with }\nabla\cdot u\text{ in $\Omega$ and }u=0\text{ on $\partial\Omega$},&\text{(Stokes) }\end{cases}

where
\vec{n}
denotes the unit normal vector on
\partial\Omega
. We note that, given
\rho
, the above equations (referred to as Darcy’s law and the steady Stokes equation for the IPM and the Stokes transport system, respectively) uniquely determine the velocity
u
and the pressure
p
(up to a constant) (See e.g.
[
3
, Chapter 1]
for the IPM and
[
7
, Chapter IV]
for the Stokes).

Both the IPM equation and the Stokes transport system describe the evolution of transported density driven by an incompressible fluid subject to gravity. Depending on the physical context, these equations can be interpreted in various ways. However, as our primary focus is on their mathematical analysis, interested readers are directed to
[
11
,
10
,
18
]
for a more detailed exploration of the physical motivations.

In both equations, it is well-known that any stratified density,
\rho_{s}(x)=\rho_{s}(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho_{s}(x_{2})
, which is independent of the horizontal variable
x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
, is a steady state. Indeed, the vector field
(0,\rho_{s}(x_{2}))^{T}
can be easily represented as a gradient field. Therefore the velocity determined by either Darcy’s law or the steady Stokes equation corresponds to a trivial vector field
u\equiv 0
. In the paper, our stability analysis will be focused on the stratified densities satisfying the following additional condition:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(x_{2})<0,\text{ for all $x\in\Omega$}.

In the remainder of this section, we will provide a brief overview of relevant background information and present our main results separately for each equation.

1.1.
The IPM equation

We recall that the IPM equation: For
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{(IPM)}=\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\\
u=-\nabla p-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix},\quad\nabla\cdot u=0,\end{cases}

with boundary condition
u\cdot\vec{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x)
.
Thanks to the incompressibility, the velocity can be recovered in terms of the stream function
\Psi
:

(1.3)

\displaystyle u=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\text{ where $\Psi$ is a solution to }\begin{cases}-\Delta\Psi=\partial_{1}\rho&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

We first review well-posedness theory. For strong solutions, the local well-posedness with smooth initial data for the IPM equation (
1.1
) can be derived by a standard energy estimate in the case where the spatial domain
\Omega
does not have a boundary. More precisely, when
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, any initial data in
H^{k}
for
k>2
possesses a unique local-in-time solution
\rho\in C([0,T];H^{k}(\Omega))
(see
[
4
,
15
]
). However, when the boundary is present, the energy estimate becomes more involved. Especially it was proved in
[
2
]
that the IPM equation in a periodic channel is locally wellposed in a subspace of
H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq 3
with an additional conditions on the boundary behavior of the solution (See Theorem
3.3
for a more precise statement). Unlike the local well-posedness, the problem of global existence v.s. finite time blow-up for smooth initial data still remains open, while several blow up criteria have been established in
[
4
]
. For weak solutions, the global existence of the weak solutions with
L^{p}
initial data is currently not available to the best of our knowledge. The nonuniqueness in the class
L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{2})
by means of the convex integration
[
19
]
. We also note that the global existence of patch-type solutions (the so-called Muskat problem) with the presence of a surface tension have been established in
[
13
]
using the optimal transport theory.

While the global well-posedness for general initial data (sufficiently smooth) is still out of reach, there are several stability results near a particular steady state
\rho_{s}(x_{2}):=1-x_{2}
, which, as a byproduct, ensures the global existence. Considering the IPM equation in the spatial domain
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it was proved in
[
6
]
that if
\rVert\rho_{0}-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\epsilon
with
k\geq 20
, the solution converges eventually to
\rho_{s}
, satisfying
\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{(1+t)^{1/4}}
. In the same paper, the author also established asymptotic stability in
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}
in the class
H^{k}
for
k\geq 20
and proved that such perturbed solutions eventually converge to a stratified density, which might not be the same as
\rho_{s}
. Stability in a periodic channel
\Omega=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
was investigated in
[
2
]
, proving that in the class
H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq 10
a perturbed solution eventually converges to a stratified density, which again might not be the same as
\rho_{s}
. While these results requires sufficiently large regularity on the initial perturbation, the authors in
[
1
]
recently reduced such regularity assumptions, establishing asymptotic stability in the class
H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap H^{1-s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
k>3
and
0<s<1
. We also mention the work
[
14
]
which proves asymptotic stability in a periodic channel for a perturbation in
H^{k}
for
3<k\in\mathbb{N}
, assuming that the vertical derivative of the steady state
\rho_{s}
is sufficiently large, depending on the size of the perturbation.

These results concerning asymptotic stability reveal the difficulty to specify a permanent description of the long time limit of the solution. The main challenge stems from the fact that the equilibria of the IPM are not isolated; given two stratified densities
\rho_{s}(x_{2})

f(x_{2})
, any function of the form
\rho_{s}(x_{2})+\epsilon f(x_{2})
for any
\epsilon>0
is another equilibrium. However, as noted in
[
5
]
the IPM equation is a transport equation with an incompressible flow, therefore as long as such a limit is achieved as a strong limit in
C^{1}
, each super-level set of the solution
\rho(t)
must preserve its topological properties and the area. Given an initial data, a stratified density preserving such properties can be uniquely determined as the so-called vertical (decreasing) rearrangement
\rho^{*}
,

\rho(x)\mapsto\rho^{*}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{\left\{0\leq x_{2}\leq|\left\{\rho>s\right\}|\right\}}ds.

Note that such vertical rearrangement is invariant under any measure preserving continuous diffeomorphism, especially,
\rho(t)^{*}=\rho_{0}^{*}
. From this property, one can easily deduce that the long-time limit that was not specified in the above earlier works must be indeed
\rho_{0}^{*}
, which can be precisely specified from the initial data.

A somewhat trivial but crucial observation is that
\rho^{*}
is a local minimizer of the potential energy defined as

E_{P}(\rho):=\int_{\Omega}\rho(x)x_{2}dx.

More precisely, one can easily deduce that for any measure preserving diffeomorphism
h:\Omega\mapsto\Omega
, it holds that

E_{P}(\rho^{*}\circ h)\geq E_{P}(\rho^{*}),

and the equality is obtained if and only if
h
is the identity map. Moreover, the potential energy is a Lyapunov functional to the IPM equation in the sense that given a sufficiently smooth solution
\rho(t)
, namely,

\frac{d}{dt}E_{P}(\rho(t))=\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx=\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}dx=-\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}dx,

where the last equality is due to Darcy’s law and the incompressibility of the flow. In this view, the solution to the IPM equation can be thought of as a minimizing curve associated to the potential energy whose long-time limit is the ground state of the energy. Hence the asymptotic stability can be obtained by achieving a sufficiently fast decay of
E_{P}(\rho(t))
towards
E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*})
. Our precise statement of the main theorem for the IPM equation is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
k\geq 3
and
\rho_{s}(x_{2}):=1-x_{2}
. There exist
\epsilon=\epsilon(k)>0
and
C=C(k)>0
such that if
\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\in H^{k}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\rVert\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\leq\epsilon
, then there exists a unique solution
\rho\in C([0,\infty);H^{k}(\Omega))
to the IPM equation (
1.2
) and it satisfies

\rVert\rho(t)-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\leq C\epsilon,\text{ for all $t>0$},

Furthermore, the potential energy decays as

E_{P}(\rho(t))-E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*})\leq C\epsilon^{2}t^{-k}.

Consequently, the solution
\rho(t)
converges to the vertical rearrangement of the initial data:

\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{t^{k/2}},\text{ for all $t>0$.}

A few remarks are in order:

Remark 1.2
.

As mentioned earlier, several results concerning asymptotic stability for the IPM equation are available in the literature (e.g.,
[
1
,
6
,
2
]
). Compared to these results, our theorem requires slightly weaker regularity on the initial perturbation. Furthermore, our poof in this paper is essentially different from all of these results in that we do not rely on the linearized system but exploit the decay of the potential energy. The key observation of this paper is that the potential energy is not only a Lyapunov functional but also reveals another (degenerate) coercive structure in its second derivative, i.e.,
\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E_{P}(\rho(t))\geq\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. See Subsection
1.3
for more detailed explanation how such property can be used in the proof.

Remark 1.3
.

The author expects that the regularity assumption
H^{k}
for
k\geq 3
can be relaxed even further, and a similar strategy used this paper would work out for initial perturbation
\rho_{0}-\rho_{s}
sufficiently small
H^{k}
for
k>1+\sqrt{3}
. More concrete evidence of such an expectation will be explained in Subsection
1.3
. The main reasons why we do not included such a stronger statement are because we do not want to introduce extra complications of the proof by involving fractional Sobolev spaces, and the local well-posedness of the equation is currently not directly available for lower regularity spaces (see Theorem
3.3
). We emphasize that the perturbation regularity cannot be relaxed too much, considering the long time instability result in
H^{2-\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0

[
15
, Theorem 1.5]
. Noticing that
H^{2}
barely fails to embed into
C^{1}
, it seems to be an interesting question whether an asymptotic stability can be established for a small perturbation in
H^{2+\epsilon}
.

Remark 1.4
.

From the stream function formulation (
1.3
), it is straightforward to see the velocity and the density formally stay in the same regularity class, especially,
\rVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\rho-\overline{\rho}\rVert_{L^{2}}
, where
\overline{\rho}
is the spatial average of
\rho
. However, compared to the decay rate of the density stated in the above theorem, our argument reveals slightly better decay rates of the kinetic energy and the anisotropic kinetic energy in a time-average sense, that is,

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq C_{k}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k+1}},\text{ and }\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq C_{k}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k+2}},\text{ for all $t>0$, $k\geq 3$.}

Remark 1.5
.

Theorem
1.1
only concerns perturbations near a specific steady state
\rho_{s}(x_{2})=1-x_{2}
. However, the author expects that our result can be generalized to perturbations near any sufficiently regular stratified density
\rho_{s}
such that
\inf_{x\in\Omega}\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(x_{2})>0
. Again the reason why we do not pursue such a more general statement is due to a lack of an exact statement in the literature about a local well-posedness theorem near general steady states. Instead, we will establish asymptotic stability near general stratified steady states for the Stokes transport system (see Theorem
1.6
), which could provide more evident ideas for the IPM equation as well.

1.2.
Stokes transport system

The Stokes transport system is another active scalar equation and it shares several interesting properties with the IPM equation. We first recall the system:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\text{(Stokes)}=\begin{cases}\rho_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\rho=0,&\text{for $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega$}\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
\Delta u=-\nabla p-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\rho\end{pmatrix},\quad u=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

As in the IPM, the incompressibility condition allows for a stream function formulation for the velocity field:

(1.5)

\displaystyle u=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\text{ where $\Psi$ solves }\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=\partial_{1}\rho&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

From the stream function formulation, one can easily notice that the velocity in the Stokes transport is much more regular than in the IPM equation. Indeed, such regular structure enables a global well-posedness theorem in a standard manner; if
\rho_{0}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq 3
, then there exists a unique solution
\rho\in C([0,\infty),H^{k}(\Omega))
(see,
[
5
, Theorem A.1]
or
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
). We also mention that the regular of the velocity can yield quite robust structures for weak solutions, for instance,
L^{3}
initial data
\rho_{0}
yields a unique regular Lagrangian solution for three-dimensional model
[
12
, Theorem 2.2, Theorem 2.4]
.

Various long time behaviors of the system (
1.4
) have been investigated in
[
5
]
, where the authors studied asymptotic stability and boundary layer formation for initial data near
\rho_{s}(x_{2})=1-x_{2}
. We also mention that the authors in
[
8
,
9
]
studied the interface problem, where
\rho
is given as a characteristic function representing two different fluid densities, establishing global existence and asymptotic stability/instability of the interface depending on the Reyleigh-Taylor stability criterion.

In regard to the Stokes transport system, our main result in this paper is a slight extension of the asymptotic stability obtained in
[
5
]
, especially concerning the regularity assumption on the initial perturbation. This result will be established by adapting a similar strategy that we exploit for the IPM equation based on the energy structure. More precise statement is as follows:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\rho_{s}(x_{2})
be a stratified steady state such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\gamma:=\inf_{x\in\Omega}\left(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(x_{2})\right)>0,\quad\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Then there exist
\epsilon=\epsilon(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)})
and
C=C(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})
such that
if
\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{4}(\Omega)
and
\rVert\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}\leq\epsilon
, then the unique solution
\rho\in C([0,\infty);H^{4}(\Omega))
to the Stokes transport system (
1.4
) satisfies

\rVert\rho(t)-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C\epsilon,\text{ for all $t>0$}.

Furthermore, the potential energy decays as

E_{P}(\rho(t))-E_{P}(\rho_{0}^{*})\leq C\epsilon^{2}t^{-2}.

Consequently, the solution
\rho(t)
converges to its vertical rearrangement:

\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\frac{\epsilon}{t},\text{ for all $t>0$.}

Remark 1.7
.

A similar asymptotic stability result was already provided in
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
, where the authors assumed that the initial perturbation is small in
H^{6}(\Omega)
near
\rho_{s}(x_{2})=1-x_{2}
. The authors also provided clear evidence that such a result can be obtained near more general stratified steady state which is sufficiently regular. As noted earlier, the proof presented in this paper is different in that our analysis does not use the linearized equation and it is an energy functional based method. This approach requires a slightly weaker regularity assumption for the initial perturbation. However, as in the IPM equation, there is a threshold of the regularity for the stability. Indeed,
[
17
, Theorem 3.7.2]
provides an example of long time instability for small initial perturbation in
H^{2-\epsilon}
near any steady state.

Remark 1.8
.

As in Remark
1.4
, our proof reveals a slight faster decay estimate for the velocity compared to that of the density. More precisely, we obtain

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq C\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{t^{3}},\text{ and }\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq C\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{t^{4}},\text{ for all $t>0$.}

1.3.
A sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.1

Let us describe the structure of the proof for the IPM equation. A similar strategy will be adapted to prove the stability for the Stokes transport system.

We consider initial data
\rho_{0}
such that
\rVert\rho_{0}-\overline{\rho}\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq\epsilon
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
and we will assume
k\geq 3
. For convenience, we denote

\theta(t):=\rho(t)-{\rho}_{s},\quad\rho_{0}^{*}:=\text{ the vertical (decreasing) rearrangement of $\rho_{0}$,}

and

E(t):=\int_{\Omega}(\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*})(x)x_{2}dx,\quad K(t):=\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thanks to the weight,
x_{2}
, in the integral expression for
E
, it is evident that
\rho_{0}^{*}
is the unique minimizer of
\rho\mapsto E_{P}(\rho)
among all the functions which can be obtained by a pushforward of
\rho_{0}
under a measure preserving map. Also if
\rho_{0}^{*}
is a non-degenerate minimizer, then
\rho\mapsto E_{P}(\rho)
is expected to satisfy a quadratic lower bound in a suitable space. In this paper, we will look for such a lower bound in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and establish in Proposition
2.5
that

(1.7)

\displaystyle E(t)\sim\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Furthermore, as long as the solution
\rho(t)
stays close to a stratified density in the space
H^{3}(\Omega)
, the fact that
H^{3}(\Omega)
continuously embeds into
C^{1}(\Omega)
suggests that each level set of
\rho(t)
is also a graph of the horizontal variable
x_{1}
, from which one can infer that

(1.8)

\displaystyle\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand, the time derivative of
E(t)
can be computed as

(1.9)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho(t,x)x_{2}dx=\int u_{2}\rho dx=\int\partial_{1}\Psi\rho dx=-\int\Psi\partial_{1}\rho dx=\int\Psi\Delta\Psi dx=-K(t).

The Biot-Savart law in (
1.3
) and the Gargliado-Nirenberg inequality tell us that

\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}=\rVert\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{k}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{1/k}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-1)/k}.

Combining this with (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), we get

K(t)=\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2k/(k-1)}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{-2/k}\geq_{C}E(t)^{k/(k-1)}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{-2/(k-1)}.

Substituting this into (
1.9
), we obtain

(1.10)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq_{C}-E(t)^{k/(k-1)}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{-2/(k-1)}.

This inequality is the main source of the asymptotic stability. Let us use the following notation which is slightly different from the usual convention: For
\alpha>0
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle f(t)=O(t^{-\alpha}),\text{ if \ $\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq C(1+t)^{-\alpha}$ for some $C>0$}.

Clearly
f(t)=O(t^{-\alpha})
means that
f
decays like
t^{-\alpha}
in average. Let us make an ansatz:

(1.12)

\displaystyle\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\epsilon,\quad\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}=O(t^{-\alpha}),\text{ for all $t>0$, for some $\alpha>0$}

Under this ansatz, one can immediately deduce from the inequality (
1.10
) that

(1.13)

\displaystyle E(t)=O({t^{-(k+2\alpha-1)}}).

With this energy decay rate, the energy variation in time (
1.9
) suggests that
K(t)
decays faster than
E(t)
by a factor of
t^{-1}
. Indeed, this elementary heuristic can be made rigorous by measuring the decay rates as an average (Lemma
2.2
). Hence we can deduce

K(t)=O(t^{-(k+2\alpha)}).

Having such a decay rate for
K(t)
, we will proceed to look at a higher derivative of the potential energy in time. A key observation is that the second derivative of the energy
E(t)
also exhibits a coercive structure, namely,

\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}K(t)\geq C\rVert u_{2}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

which is the result of Proposition
3.7
. We emphasize that such coercive structure should not come as a surprise, because the solution is expected to converge to a non-degenerate minimizer of the potential energy. Again, our notation (
1.11
) allows us to postulate that
\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
will decay faster than
K(t)
by a factor
t^{-1}
, that is,

(1.14)

\displaystyle\rVert u_{2}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=O(t^{-(k+2\alpha+1)}).

So far, the decay rates of the energies have been derived under the ansatz (
1.12
), therefore it must be justified in order to close the argument. To this end, in Proposition
3.6
, we will derive the following estimate (an informal estimate is presented at this point for simplicity):

(1.15)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}.

Using again the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality and Young’s inequality, we deduce

\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-2/k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/k}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-2/k}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/k}\leq\eta\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+C_{\eta}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-2)/(k-1)},

for any
\eta\ll 1
. Noting that
\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\epsilon\ll 1
as long as the ansatz (
1.12
) is valid, we substitute this estimate into (
1.15
), yielding that

(1.16)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-2)/({k-1})}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}=\epsilon^{2}O(t^{-(k+2\alpha+1)(k-2)/(2k-2)}),

where the last equality is due to (
1.14
). In this differential inequality, a sufficient condition for the ansatz (
1.12
) to persist is that the right-hand side should decay faster than
O(t^{-(1)})
, that is,

(1.17)

\displaystyle\frac{(k+2\alpha+1)(k-2)}{2k-2}>1.

Indeed, if this condition is satisfied, integrating the both sides of (
1.16
) in time yields that

\sup_{t>0}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt\leq C_{k,\alpha,\theta_{0}}\epsilon^{2}.

In this case,
t\mapsto\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}
is integrable in time, which indicates that our ansatz (
1.12
) should hold at least for some
\alpha\geq 1/2
;

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla\Psi(s)\rVert_{H^{k}}ds\leq\left(\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert\nabla\Psi(s)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}ds\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{k,\alpha,\theta_{0}}\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{2}{t}}\text{ for all $t>0$.}

For
\alpha\geq 1/2
, the minimum value of
k
for the sufficient condition (
1.17
) to hold can be directly computed:

\frac{(k+2\alpha+1)(k-2)}{2k-2}>1\Longleftarrow\frac{(k+2)(k-2)}{2k-2}>1\Longleftarrow k>1+\sqrt{3}.

The range of
k
stated in Theorem
1.1
is strong enough to satisfy the sufficient condition for the above scheme. Especially, (
1.13
) with
\alpha\geq 1/2
directly gives the decay rate of the potential energy stated in the Theorem
1.1
, resulting in the desired asymptotic stability.

2.
Preparation: Time-average decay and vertical rearrangement

2.1.
Time-average decay rates in differential inequalities

In the proof of asymptotic stability, we will measure the decay rates of the energy quantities in a time-average manner. To prepare for this analysis, we will gather useful lemmas concerning simple differential inequalities. In what follows
[0,T]
will denote an arbitrary time interval for some
T>0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha>0,\ 1<n
. Let
a(t)
and
f(t)
be nonnegative functions on
[0,T]
such that

\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq-a(t)^{-\alpha}f(t)^{n},\quad f(0)=f_{0}.

Then,
f
satisfies

f(t)\leq_{\alpha,n}\frac{A^{\alpha/(n-1)}}{t^{(\alpha+1)/(n-1)}},\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$, where $A:=\int_{0}^{t}a(s)ds.$}

Proof.

Dividing the differential inequality by
f(t)^{n}
and integrating it in time, we find that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f^{n-1}(t)}-\frac{1}{f_{0}^{n-1}}\geq_{n}\int_{0}^{t}a(s)^{-\alpha}ds.

Since
\alpha>0
, Jensen’s inequality yields that
\int_{0}^{t}a(s)^{-\alpha}\frac{ds}{t}\geq\left(\int_{0}^{t}a(s)ds\right)^{-\alpha}t^{\alpha}=A^{-\alpha}t^{\alpha},

which implies

\int_{0}^{t}a(s)^{-\alpha}ds\geq_{n}A^{-\alpha}t^{\alpha+1}.

Plugging this into (
2.1
), we get
\frac{1}{f^{n-1}(t)}\geq_{n}A^{-\alpha}t^{\alpha+1}
, which immediately gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n>0
and
f(t),g(t),h(t)
be nonnegative functions on
[0,T]
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq-g(t),\ \frac{d}{dt}g(t)\leq-h(t),\text{ and }f(t)\leq\frac{C}{t^{n}},

for some
C>0
.
Then, it holds that

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds\leq_{n}\frac{C}{t^{n+1}},\text{ and }\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}h(s)ds\leq_{n}\frac{C}{t^{n+2}},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

Proof.

Let us choose
s,t\in[0,T]
arbitrary so that
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
. Integrating
f^{\prime}(t)\leq-g(t)
over
[s,t]
for
s\in(0,t)
, we get
f(t)-f(s)+\int_{s}^{t}g(u)du\leq 0
. Hence, the upper bound of
f(t)
tells us that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}g(u)du\leq f(s)\leq\frac{C}{s^{n}},\text{ for $0<s<t<T$.}

Plugging in
s=t/2
, we see that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}g(u)du\leq\frac{C}{t^{n+1}},

and this is the desired estimate for
g
.

Similarly, we integrate
g^{\prime}\leq-h
and observe that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}h(u)du\leq g(s),\text{ for $0<s<t<T$}.

Integrating one more time in
s
over
[t/4,t]
, we find that the left-hand side must satisfy

\int_{t/4}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}h(u)duds=\int_{t/4}^{t}h(u)\int_{t/4}^{u}dsdu=\int_{t/4}^{t}h(u)(u-t/4)du\geq\int_{t/2}^{t}h(u)(u-t/4)du\geq\frac{t}{4}\int_{t/2}^{t}h(u)du.

On the other hand, integrating the right-hand side in (
2.5
) over
[t/4.t]
yields

\int_{t/4}^{t}g(s)ds=\int_{t/4}^{t/2}g(s)ds+\int_{t/2}^{t}g(s)ds\leq_{n}\frac{C}{(t/2)^{n}}+\frac{C}{t^{n}}\leq_{n}\frac{C}{t^{n}},

where we used (
2.4
). Putting them together, we obtain

t\int_{t/2}^{t}h(u)du\leq_{n}\frac{C}{t^{n}},

Dividing the both sides by
t^{2}
, we derive the desired estimate for
h
, finishing the proof.
∎

It is an elementary fact that if a bounded function
f
exhibits a decay rate
O(t^{-(1+\epsilon)})
, it is integrable over all time, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)dt<C_{\epsilon}<\infty
. In the next lemma, we demonstrate that if
f(t)
decays like
O(t^{1+\epsilon})
in a time-average sense, the same conclusion holds.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
T>2
and
n>1
. Let
f(t)
be a nonnegative function on
[0,T]
such that

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}f(s)ds\leq\frac{E}{t^{n}},\text{ for some $E>0$, for all $t\in[2,T]$}.

Then, for
\alpha\in(1/n,1]
, we have

\int_{1}^{T}f(t)^{\alpha}ds\leq C_{\alpha,n}E^{\alpha},

where
C_{\alpha,n}>0
does not depend on
T
.

Proof.

We pick
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{T}{2^{N+1}}\leq 1\leq\frac{T}{2^{N}}\leq 2,

and define
T_{i}:={2^{-i}}T
, for
i=0,\ldots,N
. We decompose

\int_{1}^{T}f(t)^{\alpha}ds=\int_{1}^{T_{N}}f(t)^{\alpha}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i-1}}f(t)^{\alpha}dt.

Since
T_{N}\leq 2
and
\alpha\leq 1
, applying Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{T_{N}}f(t)^{\alpha}dt\leq\int_{1}^{2}f(t)^{\alpha}dt\leq\left(\int_{1}^{2}f(t)dt\right)^{\alpha}\leq E^{\alpha}.

For
1\leq i\leq N
, again Jensen’s inequality gives us that

\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i-1}}f(t)^{\alpha}dt\leq\left(\frac{1}{T_{i-1}-T_{i}}\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i-1}}f(t)dt\right)^{\alpha}|T_{i-1}-T_{i}|=\left(\frac{1}{T_{i}}\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i-1}}f(t)dt\right)^{\alpha}T_{i}\leq_{\alpha}E^{\alpha}T_{i}^{1-\alpha n},

where the last inequality follows from the decay hypothesis for
f
. Summing over
i=1,\ldots,N
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{T_{i}}^{T_{i-1}}f(t)^{\alpha}dt\leq_{\alpha}E^{\alpha}T^{1-\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left({2^{\alpha n-1}}\right)^{i}\leq_{\alpha,n}E^{\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{2^{N}}\right)^{1-\alpha n}\leq C_{\alpha,n}E^{\alpha},

where the last inequality follows from (
2.6
). Combining this with (
2.7
), we finish the proof.∎

2.2.
Vertical rearrangement

Given a Borel measurable function
f
on
\Omega
, we define its vertical (decreasing) rearrangement as

(2.8)

\displaystyle f^{*}(x_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{\left\{0\leq x_{2}\leq|\left\{f>s\right\}|\right\}}ds.

By its definition, it is clear that
x_{2}\mapsto f^{*}(x_{2})
is monotone decreasing. In the rest of the section, we consider a stratified density
\rho_{s}(x)=\rho_{s}(x_{2})
, a function
f
that is close to
\rho_{s}
in a Sobolev space and its vertical rearrangement.

Before presenting the lemmas, let us collect some basic properties for
\rho_{s}
. We will always assume that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\gamma:=\inf_{\Omega}(-\partial_{2}\rho(x_{2}))>0,\quad\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}<\infty.

By the monotonicity of
\rho_{s}
, we can describe the image of
\rho_{s}
as

(2.10)

\displaystyle I:=\rho_{s}(\Omega)=[\rho_{s}(1),\rho_{s}(0)].

The inverse function theorem, combined with the assumption that
\gamma>0
, guarantees the existence of the inverse of
\rho_{s}
, that is,
\phi_{0}:=\rho_{s}^{-1}:I\mapsto[0,1]
is well-defined. Moreover, since
\rho_{s}
depends on the single variable
x_{2}
, the regularity assumption in (
2.9
), combined with the usual Sobolev embedding theorem, ensures that
\rho_{s}\in C^{3}(\Omega)
, and
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{C^{3}}\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}.
With such information, one can straightforwardly deduce the following estimates:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{s}\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{ss}\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{22}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{222}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}).

Noting that
H^{3}(\Omega)
continuously embeds into
C^{1}(\Omega)
, one can infer that if a function
f
is sufficiently close to
\rho_{s}
in
H^{3}
, similar properties of the level sets and the inverse function of
f
can be quantitatively estimated. This is will be the main implication of the next lemma. In the rest of this section, the implicit constant
C
, that appears in the proofs, may depend on
\gamma
and
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}
but we will omit its dependence in the notation for simplicity.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
\rho_{s}
satisfies (
2.9
). There exists
\delta=\delta(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})>0
, such that if

f={\rho}_{s}\text{ on $\partial\Omega$ and }\rVert f-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq\delta,

then there exist
\phi_{1}:I\mapsto[0,1]
and
h:\mathbb{T}\times I\to[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(x_{1},s)dx_{1}=0\text{ and }f(x_{1},\phi_{1}(s)+h(x_{1},s))=s=f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s)),\text{ for $(x_{1},s)\in\mathbb{T}\times I$.}

Furthermore, the following estimates hold:

(2.12)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{ss}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{s}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{s}h\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\rVert f-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}.

where
C>0
is a constant which depends on
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}
and
\gamma
.

Figure 1.
Illustration of the level sets of
\rho_{s}
and
f
. For each
s\in I
,
\phi_{1}
is uniquely determined so that the deviation
x_{1}\mapsto h(x_{1},s)
has zero average in
x_{1}
.

Proof.

An illustration for the proof of the lemma is presented in Figure
1
.

We notice that the image of
f
is exactly equal to the interval
I
, which is the image of
{\rho}_{s}
. Indeed, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{2}f(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}{\rho}_{s}(x_{2})+(\partial_{2}f(x_{2})-\partial_{2}{\rho}_{s}(x_{2}))\leq-\gamma+C\rVert f-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}\leq-\gamma+C\delta<0,

for sufficiently small
\delta>0
.
Hence,
x_{2}\mapsto f(x_{1},x_{2})
is strictly decreasing. Since
f={\rho}_{s}
on
\partial\Omega
, it follows that
f(\overline{\Omega})=I
.

Thanks to the monotonicity, the implicit function theorem tells us that there is a parametrization of the level curves,
\left\{f=s\right\}
, which we will denote by
\phi(\cdot,s)
, that is,

(2.14)

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))=s\text{ for each $(x_{1},s)\in\mathbb{T}\times I$.}

Let us rewrite it as

(2.15)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},s)=\phi_{0}(s)+g(x_{1},s),\text{ where }g(x_{1},s):=\phi(x_{1},s)-\phi_{0}(s).

Now, we will aim to derive necessary estimates for
g
. Writing

\displaystyle 0=\rho_{s}(\phi_{0}(s))-f(x_{1},\phi(s))=\rho_{s}(\phi_{0}(s))-\rho_{s}(\phi(x_{1},s))+\left(\rho_{s}(\phi(x_{1},s))-f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))\right),

we notice that

(2.16)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{g(x_{1},s)}\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(y+\phi_{0}(s))dy=\rho_{s}(\phi(x_{1},s))-f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s)).

Since
\partial_{2}\rho_{s}<-\gamma<0
and
\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}
, we find that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\rVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}.

Differentiating (
2.16
) with respect to
s
, we obtain

(2.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}g(x_{1},s)\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(\phi(x_{1},s))+\int_{0}^{g(x_{1},s)}\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(y+\phi_{0}(s))\partial_{s}\phi_{0}(s)dy=(\partial_{2}\rho_{s}-\partial_{2}f)(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))\partial_{s}\phi(x_{1},s).

Again, using
\partial_{2}\rho_{s}<-\gamma<0
, we have
|\partial_{s}g\partial_{2}\rho_{s}|\geq C|\partial_{s}g|
. On the other hand, using (
2.11
) and (
2.17
), we can estimate the integral on the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{g(x_{1},s)}\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(y+\phi_{0}(s))\partial_{s}\phi_{0}(s)dy\right|\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}.

For the right-hand side, at each point
(x_{1},s)\in\mathbb{T}\times I
, we have

|(\partial_{2}\rho_{s}-\partial_{2}f)\partial_{s}\phi|\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{H^{3}}|\partial_{s}\phi|\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{H^{3}}|\partial_{s}\phi_{0}|+\delta|\partial_{s}g|\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{H^{3}}+\delta|\partial_{s}g|.

Assuming
\delta
is sufficiently small depending on
\gamma
, these estimates and (
2.18
) give us

(2.19)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{s}g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}.

Next let us estimate
\partial_{ss}g
.
Once again, differentiating (
2.18
) in
s
and using the chain rule, we get (at each point
(x_{1},s)
)

(2.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{ss}g\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(\phi)

\displaystyle=A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}+A_{4}+A_{5}+A_{6},

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=-\partial_{s}g\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(\phi)\partial_{s}\phi,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=-\partial_{s}g\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(\phi)\partial_{s}\phi_{0}

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\int_{0}^{g}\partial_{222}\rho_{s}(y+\phi_{0})(\partial_{s}\phi_{0})^{2}dy

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle:=-\int_{0}^{g}\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(y+\phi_{0})\partial_{ss}\phi_{0}(s)dy,

\displaystyle A_{5}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi)(\partial_{s}\phi)^{2}

\displaystyle A_{6}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{2}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi)\partial_{ss}\phi.

Again,
\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\leq-\gamma<0
gives us that the left-hand side of (
2.20
) can be estimated as

|\partial_{ss}g\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(\phi)|\geq C|\partial_{ss}g|.

Using (
2.11
), (
2.17
) and (
2.19
), it is straightforward that

\displaystyle|A_{1}|,|A_{2}|,|A_{3}|,|A_{4}|\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}},\quad|A_{5}|\leq_{C}|\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)|,\quad|A_{6}|\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\delta|\partial_{ss}g|.

Plugging them into (
2.20
), we obtain a pointwise estimate:

(2.21)

\displaystyle|\partial_{ss}g(x_{1},s)|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert f-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}}+|\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(\phi(x_{1},s))-\partial_{22}f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))|.

Then for each fixed
x_{1}
, we treat the functions above as a function of
s
. Applying the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,1}(I)\mapsto L^{\infty}(I)
, we have that for each
x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in I}

\displaystyle|\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(\phi(s))-\partial_{22}f(\phi(s))|\leq_{C}\int_{I}|\partial_{s}(\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi(s)))|ds+\int_{I}|\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi(s))|ds

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\int_{I}|\partial_{222}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi(s))\partial_{s}\phi(s)|ds+\int_{I}|\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)(\phi(s))|ds

(2.22)

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{222}(\rho_{s}-f)(x_{1},x_{2})|dx_{2}+\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{22}(\rho_{s}-f)(x_{1},x_{2})|dx_{2}\left(\sup_{s}|\partial_{s}\phi(s)|^{-1}\right),

where we used the change of variables
\phi(s)\to x_{2}
in the last inequality. Note that

|\partial_{s}\phi(s)|\geq|\partial_{s}\phi_{0}(s)|-|\partial_{s}g(s)|\geq\gamma-\delta>C,

where the second inequality is due to (
2.19
). Hence, integrating (
2.2
) in
x_{1}
over
\mathbb{T}
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sup_{s\in I}|\partial_{22}\rho_{s}(\phi(s))-\partial_{22}f(\phi(s))|dx_{1}\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{W^{3,1}(\Omega)}+\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{W^{2,1}(\Omega)}\leq_{C}\rVert\rho_{s}-f\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}.

Combining this with (
2.21
), we arrive at

(2.23)

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in I}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\partial_{ss}g(x,s)|dx\leq\rVert f-\overline{\rho}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}.

Towards the proof, we define
\phi_{1}
and
h
as follows:

\phi_{1}:=\phi_{0}(s)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}g(x,s)dx,\quad h(x,s):=g(x,s)-\int_{\mathbb{T}}g(x,s)dx.

Let us check if
\phi_{1}
and
h
satisfy the desired properties. By its definition, we have
\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(x,s)dx=0
for each
s\in I
. Also, (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) tells us that

f(x,\phi_{1}(s)+h(x,s))=f(x,\phi_{0}(s)+g(x,s))=f(x,\phi(x,s))=s.

Collecting the estimates for
g
obtained in (
2.17
), (
2.19
) and (
2.23
), we see that

(2.24)

\displaystyle\rVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}},\rVert\partial_{s}h\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times I)},\ \rVert\partial_{s}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times I)},\ \rVert\partial_{ss}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}\times I)}\leq C\rVert f-\overline{\rho}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)},

which proves (
2.12
).
To finish the proof, we have to prove
f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))=s.

To this end, observe that for each
s\in I
, it holds that

\left|\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega:\phi_{1}^{-1}>s\right\}\right|=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}1_{\left\{\phi_{1}^{-1}(x_{2})\geq s\right\}}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\phi_{1}(s)}dx=2\pi\phi_{1}(s).

We also have

\displaystyle\left|\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Omega:{f}>s\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}1_{\left\{f(x_{1},x_{2})>s\right\}}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}1_{\left\{x_{2}\leq\phi(x_{1},s)\right\}}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi(x_{1},s)dx_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(\phi_{0}(s)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}g(x_{1},s)dx_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi\phi_{1}(s).

This implies that the areas of every super-level set of
\phi_{1}^{-1}
and
f
are equal. Recalling the definition of the rearrangement in (
2.8
), we arrive at

f^{*}(x_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{0\leq x_{2}\leq|\left\{f>s\right\}|}ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{0\leq x_{2}\leq|\left\{\phi_{1}^{-1}>s\right\}|}ds=\phi^{-1}(x_{2}).

This proves
f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))=s.

∎

Recall that for
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, its potential energy is defined as

(2.25)

\displaystyle E_{P}(f):=\int_{\Omega}f(x)x_{2}dx.

It is well-known that for any stratified function
\rho_{s}
is a critical point of
E_{P}
under any divergence-free perturbation. In the case where
f
is sufficiently close to
\rho_{s}
, the next proposition will quantitatively demonstrate that
f^{*}
is the unique minimizer of
E_{P}
among all functions with the same area of super-level sets.

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
\rho_{s}
satisfies (
2.9
). There exists
\delta=\delta(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})>0
such that if
f={\rho}_{s}
on
\partial\Omega
and
\rVert f-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq\delta
, then

(2.26)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq E_{P}(f)-E_{P}(f^{*})\leq C\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Moreover, we have

(2.27)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq C\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The constant
C
depends only on
\gamma
and
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}
.

Proof.

Let us prove (
2.26
) first. For sufficiently small
\delta>0
, Lemma
2.4
ensures the existence of
\phi_{1},h
such that

(2.28)

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbb{T}}h(x,s)dx

\displaystyle=0,\quad f(x,\phi_{1}(s)+h(x,s))=s=f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s)),

and

(2.29)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{ss}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{s}\phi_{1}-\partial_{s}\phi_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert\partial_{s}h\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\delta,

where
\phi_{0}
is the inverse of
\rho_{s}
, which trivially implies
\partial_{s}\phi_{0}(s)=\frac{1}{\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(\phi_{0}(s))}
. From (
2.9
), it follows that

(2.30)

\displaystyle 0<\frac{1}{C}\leq-\partial_{s}\phi_{0}<C.

Since
f^{*}
is the inverse of
\phi_{1}
, the inverse function theorem and the estimates for
\phi_{1}
in (
2.29
) tell us that

(2.31)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{22}f^{*}-\partial_{22}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\rVert\partial_{2}f^{*}-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\delta.

Denoting
\phi(x,s):=\phi_{1}(s)+h(x_{1},s)
and using the change of variables,
x_{2}\to\phi(x_{1},s)
, we have

(2.32)

\displaystyle\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}|f(x,\phi(x,s))-f^{*}(\phi(x,s))|^{2}|\partial_{s}\phi(x,s)|dxds.

Thanks to (
2.28
), we have

\displaystyle f(x,\phi(x,s))

\displaystyle-f^{*}(\phi(x,s))

\displaystyle=s-f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))+\left(f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))-f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s)+h(x,s))\right)

\displaystyle=f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))-f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s)+h(x,s))

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))h(x,s)+O\left(|\partial_{22}f^{*}||h(x_{1},s)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}\rho_{s}h(x,s)+O\left(|\partial_{2}(\rho_{s}-f^{*})||h(x_{1},s)|\right)+O\left(|\partial_{22}f^{*}||h(x,s)|^{2}\right)

(2.33)

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}\rho_{s}h(x,s)+O(\delta|h(x,s)|),

where the last equality follows from (
2.31
). Note that (
2.29
) also implies
|\partial_{s}\phi-\partial_{s}\phi_{0}|\leq_{C}\delta
, thus (
2.30
) gives

(2.34)

\displaystyle 0<\frac{1}{C}\leq-\partial_{s}\phi\leq C.

Plugging this and (
2.2
) into (
2.32
), we obtain, for sufficiently small
\delta>0
,

(2.35)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert h\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1])}\leq\rVert f-f^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\rVert h\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,1])}.

On the other hand,
E_{P}(f)
can be written as

\displaystyle E_{P}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}f(x_{1},x_{2})x_{2}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))\phi(x_{1},s)\partial_{s}\phi(x_{1},s)dsdx_{1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}s\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}\left(\phi(x_{1},s)\right)^{2}dsdx_{1}.

Since
f=f^{*}=\rho_{s}
on
\partial\Omega
and
\rho_{s}
is strictly decreasing, we have

\phi(x_{1},\rho_{s}(0))=\phi_{1}(\rho_{s}(0))=0,\quad\phi(x_{1},\rho_{s}(1))=\phi_{1}(\rho_{s}(1))=1.

Using this, we can continue the computation above as

\displaystyle E_{P}(f)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi(x_{1},\rho_{s}(0))^{2}-\phi(x_{1},\rho_{s}(1))^{2}dx_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}(\phi_{1}(s)+h(x_{1},s))^{2}dsdx_{1}

\displaystyle=\pi+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{1}(s)^{2}+h(x_{1},s)^{2}dsdx_{1},

where the last equality follows from that
h
has zero average in
x
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle E_{P}(f^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{1}f^{*}(x_{2})dx=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}f^{*}(\phi_{1}(s))\phi_{1}(s)\partial_{s}\phi_{1}(s)dsdx_{1}=\pi+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}\phi_{1}(s)^{2}ds.

Consequently, we arrive at
E(f)-E(f^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{I}|h(x_{1},s)|^{2}dsdx_{1}.

Combining this with (
2.35
), the estimates in (
2.26
) is verified.

Now, let us prove (
2.27
). Differentiating (
2.28
) in
x_{1}
, we see that

0=\partial_{1}(f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s)))=\partial_{1}f(x_{1},\phi(x,s))+\partial_{2}f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))\partial_{1}h(x_{1},s).

Similarly, diffierentiating (
2.28
) in
s
yields
1=\partial_{2}f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))\partial_{s}\phi(s)
, thus

\partial_{1}f(x_{1},\phi(x_{1},s))=-\frac{\partial_{1}h(x_{1},s)}{\partial_{s}\phi(x_{1},s)}.

Then, using the change of variables
x_{2}\to\phi(x_{1},s)
and also using (
2.34
), we obtain

\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq C\rVert\partial_{1}h\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times I)}\geq C\rVert h\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times I)},

where the last inequality follows from the zero-average in
x
of
h
in (
2.28
) and the Poincaré inequality. Therefore, combining this with (
2.35
), we conclude that (
2.27
) holds.
∎

3.
Stability in the IPM equation

In this section, we aim to prove the asymptotic stability for the incompressible porous media equation (
1.2
). Throughout the section, we will fix

(3.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(x_{2}):=1-x_{2}.

3.1.
Preliminaries for the IPM

Let us review important previous results concerning the local existence of the IPM equation (
1.2
) in the domain
\Omega=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
. For further details, we refer readers to the paper by Castro–Córdoba–Lear
[
2
]
.

We recall the following spaces from
[
2
, Section 1]
: For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle X^{k}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in H^{k}(\Omega):\partial_{2}^{n}f|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\text{ for }n=0,2,4,.\ldots k^{\star}\right\},\text{ where }k^{\star}:=\begin{cases}k-2,&\text{ if $k$ is even},\\
k-1&\text{ if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

That is,
X^{k}(\Omega)
is the closure of
\left\{f\in C^{\infty}({\Omega}):\partial_{2}^{n}f|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\text{ for }n=0,2,4,.\ldots k^{\star}\right\}
in the norm
H^{k}
. It is worth noting that the usual trace theorem,
H^{k}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{k-1}(\partial\Omega)
, ensures that the vanishing normal derivatives in the above definition is well-defined. For convenience, we denote

X^{\infty}(\Omega):=\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}X^{k}(\Omega).

By definition, it is clear from (
1.18
) that

(3.3)

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\subset X^{\infty}(\Omega).

If
f\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)
, it holds that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{1}^{n_{1}}\partial_{2}^{2n_{2}}f\in X^{\infty}(\Omega),\text{ for any $n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}$, }

where
\partial_{n}
denotes the normal derivative of
f
on
\partial_{\Omega}
. Especially, we can use the integration by parts in the vertical variable without a boundary integral:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}^{k+1}f(x)\partial_{2}^{k+1}g(x)dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}^{k}f(x)\partial_{2}^{k+2}g(x)dx,\text{ for any $k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}$ and $f,g\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)$.}

In a usual domain without boundary, for instance
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is trivial that the Sobolev norms can be bounded by looking at only each single directional derivatives, that is,

\rVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right).

In a bounded domain, this property may depend on the boundary condition, since a mixed derivative might not be well controlled. While the next lemma seems intuitively obvious, we will give a proof for the sake of completeness, although the proof will be postponed to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)
. For any
n,k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}^{n}\partial_{2}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{n,k}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{n+k}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{n+k}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Consequently, we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\rVert f\rVert_{{H}^{k}}\leq C_{k}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\text{ for all $k\geq 0$}.

Let us consider a solution
\rho(t)
to the IPM equation. We denote

(3.8)

\displaystyle\theta(t):=\rho(t)-\rho_{s}.

Substituting
\rho=\theta+{\rho}_{s}
in (
1.2
), one can easily see that
\theta(t)
solves

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\theta_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\theta=u_{2},\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi,\end{cases}\text{ with }\begin{cases}-\Delta\Psi=\partial_{1}\theta,&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
\Psi=0,&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

The next lemma tells us that if the solution
\theta(t)\in X^{k}
, then the stream function
\Psi(t)
behaves in a similar manner.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
2
, Lemma 3.1]

Let
f\in X^{k}(\Omega)
and let
\Psi
be a solution to

\begin{cases}\Delta\Psi=-\partial_{1}f&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

Then
\Psi\in X^{k+1}(\Omega)
and it satisfies
\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{k+1}}\leq C_{k}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}
.

The local well-posedness to the equation (
3.9
) was established in
[
2
]
:

Theorem 3.3
.

[
2
, Theorem 4.1]

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 3
. For any
\theta_{0}\in X^{k}(\Omega)
, there exists a time
T=T(\rVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H^{3}})>0
and a unique solution
\theta\in C(0,T;X^{k}(\Omega))
for the equation (
3.9
).

Thanks to the local well-posedness theorem, we will assume that the initial data is smooth, that is,
\theta_{0}\in X^{\infty}
. In view of the statement of Theorem
1.1
, the general case where
\theta_{0}\in H^{k}_{0}(\Omega)
will be managed by usual compactness argument in the end of the section.

3.2.
Energy estimates

In this section, we aim to derive an a priori energy estimate. The main result will be given in Proposition
3.6
.

Let us recall the basic tame estimates concerning the Sobolev spaces. In the next lemma, we use the following notations: For
\alpha\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\})^{2}
,

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\quad|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\quad\partial^{\alpha}:=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
2
, Lemma 4.2]

Let
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, for
\alpha,\beta\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\})^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\rVert\partial^{\alpha}f\partial^{\beta}g\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{\alpha,\beta}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}\rVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert g\rVert_{H^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}\rVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\rVert\partial^{\alpha}(fg)-f\partial^{\alpha}g\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{\alpha,\beta}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{|\alpha|}}\rVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert f\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert g\rVert_{H^{|\alpha|-1}}.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\overline{f}(x_{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}
, it holds that

\rVert f-\overline{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{1}}.

Proof.

We notice the following pointwise estimate:

(3.10)

\displaystyle|(f-\overline{f})(x)|^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x_{1},x_{2})-f(z,x_{2})dz\right)^{2}\leq C\int_{\mathbb{T}}(f(x_{1},x_{2})-f(z,x_{2}))^{2}dz.

The integrand in the right-hand side can be written as
f(x_{1},x_{2})-f(z,x_{2})=\int_{x_{1}}^{z}\partial_{1}f(a,x_{2})da.

For each fixed
x_{1}
, we apply the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}([0,1])\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}([0,1])
to the map
x_{2}\mapsto\int_{x_{1}}^{z}\partial_{1}f(a,x_{2})da
, yielding that

\sup_{x_{2}\in[0,1]}\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{z}\partial_{1}f(a,x_{2})da\right|^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x_{1}}^{z}|\partial_{12}f(a,y)|^{2}+|\partial_{1}f(a,y)|^{2}dady\leq\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Therefore, taking the supremum over
x\in\Omega
in (
3.10
), the desired result follows.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\theta(t)\in C(0,T;X^{\infty}(\Omega))
be the unique smooth solution to (
3.9
) for some
T>0
. For
k\geq 3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-C_{k}(1-C_{k}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}})\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{{H}^{k}}^{2}+C_{k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}.

Proof.

In the proof, the implicit constant
C
may depend on
k
, but its dependence will be omitted for simplicity.
In what follows
\partial_{i}
will denote either
\partial_{1}
or
\partial_{2}
.
Using (
3.9
), we compute

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\int\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx+\int\partial_{i}^{k}u_{2}\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx

We simplify the linear term first. Recalling from (
3.9
) that
u_{2}=\partial_{1}\Psi
and
\partial_{1}\theta=-\Delta\Psi
, we have

\displaystyle\int\partial_{i}^{k}u_{2}\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\partial_{1}\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx=-\int\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi\partial_{i}^{k}\partial_{1}\theta dx=\int\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi\partial_{i}^{k}\Delta\Psi dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi\nabla(\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi)\cdot\vec{n}(x)d\sigma(x)-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi|^{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi|^{2}dx,

where the last equality follows from (
3.5
), which ensures that the integral over
\partial\Omega
vanishes. Since
\theta\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)
, it follows from Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.1
that

\sum_{i=1,2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\Psi|^{2}dx\geq_{C}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}
. Since
\Psi=0
on
\partial\Omega
, the Poincaré inequality gives us
\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}\geq_{C}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}
, consequently,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,2}\int\partial_{i}^{k}u_{2}\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx\leq_{C}-\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{{H}^{k}}^{2}.

Now, we move on to the nonlinear term. We write

\displaystyle\int\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\left(\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx+\int u\cdot\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\left(\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx+\int u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}^{k}\theta)^{2}\right)dx

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx,

where the last equality follows from the integration by parts and
u\cdot\vec{n}=0
on the boundary. We claim that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left|\int\left(\partial_{i}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{i}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{i}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{{H}^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}\right),

either
i=1
or
i=2
. Once we have the above claim, combining it with (
3.12
) yields the desired energy estimate. Thus, in the rest of the proof, we will aim to prove the claim (
3.14
). We consider two cases,
i=1
and
i=2
, separately.

Case
i=1
.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives us

(3.15)

\displaystyle\left|\int\left(\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{1}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

while Lemma
3.4
tells us that

\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\left(\rVert u\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\nabla\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right).

Since
k\geq 3
, the Sobolev inequality gives us

(3.16)

\displaystyle\rVert u\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}=\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}},\text{ and }\rVert\nabla\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Hence we have
\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}
. Plugging this into (
3.15
), we obtain

\left|\int\left(\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{1}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Furthermore, from the Poisson equation in (
3.9
), we find that

\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}=\rVert\partial_{1}^{k-1}\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}},

therefore, we conclude

(3.17)

\displaystyle\left|\int\left(\partial_{1}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{1}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}},

which verifies (
3.14
).

Case
i=2
.

Splitting
u\cdot\nabla=u_{1}\partial_{1}+u_{2}\partial_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{1}\partial_{1}\theta)-u_{1}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{1}\theta\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx+\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{2}\partial_{2}\theta)-u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{2}\theta\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx

(3.18)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

The first integral
I_{1}
can be estimated as before; applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

(3.19)

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq C\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{1}\partial_{1}\theta)-u_{1}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

while Lemma
3.4
gives us

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{1}\partial_{1}\theta)-u_{1}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{k-1}}+\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{k-1}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right),

where we used the estimate for
u
in (
3.16
) to get the second inequality. Again, using the Sobolev inequality and the Poisson equation in (
3.9
), we estimate

\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{k-1}}=\rVert\Delta\Psi\rVert_{H^{k-1}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}},

which gives us
\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{1}\partial_{1}\theta)-u_{1}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}
.
Plugging this into (
3.19
), we conclude

(3.20)

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Now, let us estimate
I_{2}
in (
3.2
). We denote

(3.21)

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}(x_{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\theta(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}.

We split
I_{2}
as

(3.22)

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{2}\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta}))-u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx+\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u_{2}\partial_{2}\overline{\theta})-u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k}\partial_{2}\overline{\theta}\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx=:I_{21}+I_{22}

We estimate
I_{21}
first. In a similar manner as above, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Sobolev inequalities yield

\displaystyle|I_{21}|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{H^{k-1}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{H^{k}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}

(3.23)

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Moreover, Lemma
3.5
implies

\rVert\partial_{2}(\theta-\overline{\theta})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{12}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{2}\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}},

where the last inequality follows from
k\geq 3
. Plugging this and
\rVert u\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}
into (
3.2
), we obtain

(3.24)

\displaystyle|I_{21}|\leq C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}\right).

Next, we estimate
I_{22}
in (
3.22
). By expanding
I_{22}
using the product rule, we have

(3.25)

\displaystyle I_{22}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}C_{k,j}\int\partial_{2}^{j}u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx.

When
j=1
, we have

(3.26)

\displaystyle\int\partial_{2}u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k}\overline{\theta}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{2}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\overline{\theta}\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{2}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}.

For
j\geq 2
, noting that
u_{2}=\partial_{1}\Psi
, we can apply the integration by parts in each integral as

\displaystyle\int\partial_{2}^{j}u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=-\int\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}dx\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta d\sigma(x)+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}\left(\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\right)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}\left(\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\right)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta dx.

To see the last inequality, note that by Lemma
3.2
, we have that
\Psi,\overline{\theta},\partial_{1}\theta
are all in
X^{\infty}
. Since at least one of
j,k-j+1,k-1
must be even, the definition of the space
X^{k}(\Omega)
in (
3.2
) tells us that the boundary integral must vanish.

To continue, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get

\displaystyle\left|\int\partial_{2}^{j}u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}\partial_{2}\left(\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\right)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\rVert\partial_{2}^{j+1}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\rVert\partial_{2}^{j+1}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}},\text{ for $j\geq 2$},

where we used
\rVert\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k-1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}=\rVert\partial_{2}^{k-1}\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}
to get the last inequality.

Let us estimate
\rVert\partial_{2}^{j+1}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}
in (
3.2
). Since
2\leq j\leq k
, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{2}^{j+1}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}\rVert\overline{\theta}\rVert_{H^{k-j+2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}},

where the second last inequality follows from the Sobolev inequality, noticing that
\overline{\theta}
depends only on the variable
x_{2}
.

Let us estimate
\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}
in (
3.2
). When
j=k
, we have

\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq_{C}\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\partial_{22}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq_{C}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

When
j\leq k-1
, we have

\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

where in the the last inequality, we used the Sobolev inequality and
j\geq 2
. Thus, we obtain

\rVert\partial_{2}^{j}\Psi\partial_{2}^{k-j+2}\overline{\theta}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}},\text{ for all $j\geq 2$.}

Plugging this and (
3.28
) into (
3.2
), we get

\left|\int\partial_{2}^{j}u_{2}\partial_{2}^{k-j+1}\overline{\theta}\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}\text{ for $j\geq 2$.}

Combining this with (
3.26
) and plugging them into (
3.25
), we see that

|I_{22}|\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{2}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Plugging this and (
3.24
) into (
3.22
), we get

|I_{2}|\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Plugging this and (
3.20
) into (
3.2
), we conclude

\left|\int\left(\partial_{2}^{k}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)-u\nabla\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\right)\partial_{2}^{k}\theta dx\right|\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Combining this with (
3.17
), we obtain (
3.14
).
∎

3.3.
Analysis of the energy structure

The main objective in this subsection is to derive sufficient convergence rate of
\rho(t)
to the equilibrium
\rho_{0}^{*}
, while
\rho
stays close to
{\rho}_{s}
. We emphasize that
\theta(t)=\rho(t)-\rho_{s}
will stay small but not necessarily decay. Thus it is important to distinguish the roles of
\theta(t)
and
\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}
. We will consider the potential energy and the kinetic energy defined as

(3.29)

\displaystyle E(t):=\int_{\Omega}(\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*})x_{2}dx,\quad K(t):=\rVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since we are interested in a solution that is close to
\rho_{s}
, we will assume, throughout this subsection, that

(3.30)

\displaystyle\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0},\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$ for some $T>0$ and $\delta_{0}\ll 1$.}

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
k\geq 3
. There exists
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(k)>0
such that if
\rho(t)
satisfies (
3.30
), then

(3.31)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-K(t),\quad\frac{d}{dt}K(t)\leq-C_{k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

Differentiating
E(t)
, we see that

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{t}x_{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla\rho x_{2}dx=\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx.

Using (
1.3
), we can further simplify the last expression as

\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\Psi\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\Psi\partial_{1}\rho dx=\int_{\Omega}\Psi\Delta\Psi dx=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}dx,

therefore, we get

(3.33)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}dx=-K(t).

In order to estimate
\frac{d}{dt}K(t)
, we differentiate (
3.32
) and obtain

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho_{t}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u_{2}\rho dx.

Using
u_{2}=\partial_{1}\Psi
and
\partial_{1}\rho=\Delta\Psi
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u_{2}\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Psi\partial_{1}\rho dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Psi\Delta\Psi dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Delta\Psi\Psi dx=-\int_{\Omega}{\partial_{t}}\partial_{1}\rho\Psi dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\rho u_{2}dx

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho_{t}=-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}u\cdot\nabla\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho dx

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx-\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Using the assumption on
\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{3}}
in (
3.30
) and (
3.1
), we have

-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx=-\int u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho_{s}dx-\int u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\theta dx\geq\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta_{0}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Using (
3.33
), we see that the above inequality implies

(3.34)

\displaystyle C\frac{d}{dt}K(t)+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Let us estimate
\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Using (
1.3
), we rewrite

(3.35)

\displaystyle\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\rVert\partial_{2}\Psi\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{4}}\rVert\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{4}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation theorem. Moreover, we notice from (
1.3
) that
g(x_{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}
satisfies

\partial_{22}g(x_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{22}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Delta\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})-\partial_{11}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{1}(-\rho+\partial_{1}\Psi)dx=0,

with the boundary condition,
g(0)=g(1)=0
. Therefore,
g=0
for all
x_{2}\in[0,1]
. In other words, the map
x_{1}\to\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})
has zero average for each fixed
x_{2}
. Then, the Poincaré inequality tells us

\rVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\partial_{1}\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}=\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Plugging this into (
3.35
), we get

\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\delta_{0}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the second inequality follows from Lemma
3.2
and the last inequality follows from (
3.30
). Plugging this into (
3.34
), we obtain that for sufficiently small
\delta_{0}>0
,

\frac{d}{dt}K(t)\leq-C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Combining this with (
3.33
), we finish the proof of the proposition.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
k\geq 3
. There exists
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(k)>0
such that if (
3.30
) holds, then,

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{k}},

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}K(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{k+1}},

\displaystyle\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{k+2}},

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
2.5
, it holds that

(3.36)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq E(t)\leq C\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Now, using the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation theorem, we observe that

\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\rVert\Delta\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{1/k}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-1)/k}.

On the other hand, applying (
2.27
), we get
\rVert\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}\geq C\rVert\rho-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}
. Combining this with the above estimate, we find

\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C\left(\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{-1/k}\rVert\rho-\rho_{0}^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)^{2k/(k-1)}\geq C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{-2/(k-1)}E(t)^{k/(k-1)}.

where the last inequality follows from (
3.36
). Hence, the variation of the potential energy in (
3.31
) must satisfy

(3.37)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq-C\left(\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\right)^{-1/(k-1)}E(t)^{k/(k-1)}.

Applying Lemma
2.1
with
\alpha=1/(k-1)>0
and
n=k/(k-1)
and
a(t)=\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}
, we get

(3.38)

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{k}}.

Applying Lemma
2.2
to (
3.31
) with
f(t)=E(t),\ g(t)=K(t),\ h(t)=C\rVert u_{2}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we get

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}K(s)ds\leq\frac{C}{t^{k+1}},\text{ and }\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\frac{C\delta}{t^{k+2}}.

Together with (
3.38
), we obtain the desired estimates.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
k\geq 3
be fixed. Let
\delta_{0}
be be fixed as in Proposition
3.7
. We claim that there exists
\epsilon_{0}(k)>0
such that if
\theta_{0}:=\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
and

\displaystyle\rVert\rho_{0}-{\rho}_{s}\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0},

then for all
t>0
, it holds that

(3.39)

\displaystyle\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt<C\epsilon^{2},\text{ for some $C=C(k)>0$.}

Let us suppose for the moment that the claim is true. From Theorem
3.3
, we know that the maximal existence time depends only on
\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{3}}
, thus, the solution
\rho
exists globally in time. Also, if necessary, we can further assume that
\epsilon
is small enough to ensure Corollary
3.8
is applicable, that is, the solution
\rho(t)
satisfies

(3.40)

\displaystyle E(t)\leq\frac{C\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k}},\quad\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}K(s)ds\leq\frac{C\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k+1}},\quad\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\frac{C\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k+2}},\text{ for all $t>0$}.

In order to prove the theorem for
\rho_{0}\in H^{k}_{0}
without assuming the smoothness, we can simply find an approximation
\rho_{0,n}
such that
\rho_{0,n}-\overline{\rho}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
\rho_{0,n}\to\rho_{0}
in
H^{k}
. Then (
3.40
) and (
3.39
) are satisfied by the global solutions
\rho_{n}(t)
, with initial data
\rho_{0,n}
. Since all estimates are uniform in
n
, the unique limit
\rho(t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}(t)
is the global in time solution satisfying the same estimates, that is,

\rVert\rho(t)-\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}\quad\int_{\Omega}(\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*})(x)x_{2}dx\leq C\epsilon^{2}t^{-k},\text{ for all $t>0$}.

Together with (
2.26
), we obtain all the desired estimates to establish Theorem
1.1
.

In the rest of the proof, we aim to prove (
3.39
). Towards a contradiction, let
T^{*}>0
be the first time that (
3.39
) breaks down, that is,

(3.41)

\displaystyle\rVert\theta(T^{*})\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\rVert\nabla\Psi(T^{*})\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}dt=M\epsilon^{2}\ll 1,

for some
M>0
, which will be chosen later. Towards a contradiction, let us denote

f(t):=\rVert\partial_{1}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\theta_{0}=\rho_{0}-\overline{\rho}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have
\theta_{0}\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Therefore it follows from Theorem
3.3
that
\theta(t)\in X^{\infty}(\Omega)
for
t\in[0,T^{*}]
. Since
\theta(t)
vanishes on the boundary, Lemma
3.1
tells us that

(3.42)

\displaystyle C^{-1}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq f(t)\leq C\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq CM\epsilon^{2},\quad f(0)\leq C\epsilon^{2}.

Using the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq 3
, we have

(3.43)

\displaystyle\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}\rVert\leq\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq f(t)^{1/2},

where the second last inequality follows from (
3.2
).
Using this and Proposition
3.6
, we observe that
f(t)
satisfies

(3.44)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq-C\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+Cf(t)^{3/2},\text{ for $t\in[0,T^{*}]$.}

From this we see that
f
satisfies
\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\leq Cf^{3/2}
. Since
f(0)\leq C\epsilon^{2}
, one can easily find that

(3.45)

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\frac{C}{(\epsilon^{-1}-t)^{2}},\text{ for $t\in[0,T^{*}]$.}

This implies that for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, it holds that

(3.46)

\displaystyle f(t)\leq C\epsilon^{2},\text{ for $t\in[0,2]$.}

In other words, for (
3.41
) to happen, we must have

(3.47)

\displaystyle T^{*}>2.

Now we analyze the energy inequality in a more careful manner. Using the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation theorem, we see that

\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-2/k}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-2/k}.

Hence, using Young’s inequality, we derive

\displaystyle\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2/k}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1-2/k}f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\eta\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+C_{\eta}f(t)^{k/(k-1)}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-2)/(k-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+C_{\eta}(M\epsilon^{2})^{k/(k-1)}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-2)/(k-1)},\text{ for any $\eta>0$}.

Substituting this into (
3.44
) with sufficiently small
\eta
depending only on the implicit constant
C
, we arrive at

f^{\prime}(t)\leq-C\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+C(M\epsilon^{2})^{k/(k-1)}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{(k-2)/(k-1)},

Integrating the above in
t
over
[1,T^{*}]
, we obtain

(3.48)

\displaystyle f(T^{*})-f(1)+C_{k}\int_{1}^{T^{*}}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt\leq C_{k}(M\epsilon^{2})^{k/(k-1)}\int_{1}^{T^{*}}\left(\rVert u_{2}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{k-2}{2(k-1)}}dt.

Thanks to
M\epsilon^{2}\ll 1
in (
3.41
), we apply Corollary
3.8
and obtain

\frac{2}{t}\int_{t/2}^{t}\rVert u_{2}(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}ds\leq\frac{CM\epsilon^{2}}{t^{k+2}},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T^{*}]$.}

Since
k\geq 3
, we have
n:=k+2>1
and
\alpha:=\frac{k-2}{2(k-1)}\in(1/n,1)
. Thus applying Lemma
2.3
with
f=\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
and
E=CM\epsilon^{2}
, we get

\int_{1}^{T^{*}}\left(\rVert u_{2}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{k-2}{2(k-1)}}dt\leq C(M\epsilon^{2})^{\frac{k-2}{2(k-1)}}.

Plugging this and (
3.46
) into (
3.48
), we obtain

f(T^{*})+C\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{1}\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt+\epsilon^{2}+(M\epsilon^{2})^{\frac{3k-2}{2(k-1)}}\right)\leq C\epsilon^{2}+C(M\epsilon^{2})^{\frac{3k-2}{2(k-1)}},

where we used (
3.43
) to justify the last inequality. Finally using
f(T^{*})\geq C\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}
, which is due to the last inequality in (
3.43
), we notice that for (
3.41
) to hold, we must have

M\epsilon^{2}=\rVert\theta(T^{*})\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\rVert\nabla\Psi(T^{*})\rVert_{H^{k}(\Omega)}^{2}dt\leq_{C}f(T^{*})+\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\rVert\nabla\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}dt\leq C\epsilon^{2}+C(M\epsilon^{2})^{\frac{3k-2}{2(k-1)}}.

Since
k\geq 3
, we have
\frac{3k-2}{2(k-1)}>1
. Therefore our assumption
M\epsilon^{2}\ll 1
in (
3.41
) and the above estimate give us

M\epsilon^{2}\leq C\epsilon^{2}.

This leads to a contradiction with (
3.41
), if
M
is chosen strictly larger than some implicit constant
C
, which depends only on
k
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4.
Stability in the Stokes transport system

In this section, our goal is to prove the asymptotic stability for the Stokes transport system (
1.4
). Throughout the section, we will assume that
\rho_{s}=\rho_{s}(x_{2})
is a function that is independent of
x_{1}
, and satisfies

(4.1)

\displaystyle\gamma:=\inf_{x\in\Omega}\left(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}(x_{2})\right)>0,\quad\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}<\infty.

In the rest of the paper, we allow the implicit constant
C
to depend on
\gamma
and
\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}
, but we omit its dependence in the notation for simplicity. While the proof will exhibit a structure quite similar to that of the previous section, we will furnish enough details for the sake of completeness of the paper.

4.1.
Preliminaries for the Stokes transport system

Let us briefly review relevant results concerning the Stokes transport system in the periodic channel For more details, we refer readers to the paper by Dalibard–Guillod–Leblond
[
5
]
and additional references therein.

Let us consider a solution
\rho(t)
to the Stokes transport system. As in the previous section we denote

(4.2)

\displaystyle\theta(t):=\rho(t)-\rho_{s}.

Substituting
\rho=\theta+{\rho}_{s}
in (
1.4
), it immediately follows that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\theta_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\theta=-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2},\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
\end{cases}with\ \begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=\partial_{1}\theta,&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0,&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$}.\end{cases}

Thanks to the Bilaplacian operator in the equation for the stream function, the velocity in the Stokes transport system is much regular compared to that of the IPM equation. More quantitative estimate can be found in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
5
, Lemma B.1]

Let
f\in H^{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq-2
and
\Psi
be a solution to

\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\Psi=f&\text{ in $\Omega$},\\
\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

Then
\Psi\in H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{k+4}(\Omega)
and
\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{k+4}}\leq C_{k}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}
.

Thanks to more regular nature of the velocity, the global in time existence of the solution follows in a standard way. More precise statement can be formulated as below.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
5
, Lemma 2.1, Theorem A.1]
Let
\theta_{0}\in H^{k}(\Omega)
with
k\geq 3
. Then there exists a unique global solution
\rho(t)
to (
1.4
) such that
\rho\in C(\mathbb{R}^{+};H^{m}(\Omega))
. Furthermore, denoting
\theta:=\rho-{\rho}_{s}
and
\overline{\theta}(x_{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\theta(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}
, it holds that if
\theta_{0}=\partial_{n}\theta=\partial_{n}^{2}\overline{\theta}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then

\theta(t)=\partial_{2}\theta(t)=\partial_{2}^{2}\overline{\theta}(t)=0,\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}

In view of Theorem
1.6
, we will consider initial data
\theta_{0}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{4}(\Omega)
. The usual trace theorem ensures that
\theta,\partial_{2}\theta
and
\partial_{22}\overline{\theta}
are indeed well-defined on the boundary
\partial\Omega
and vanish identically as stated in the above theorem.

4.2.
Energy estimates

In this subsection, our objective is to derive an a priori energy estimate. The main result is presented in Proposition
4.4
. As one can notice from the proposition, we will focus on estimating the evolution of
\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}
, rather than directly examining
\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{4}}
. The reason is to mitigate potential complications in the finer analysis of the anisotropic nature of the nonlinear term in the equation. As expected, the norm
\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}
is equivalent to
\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{4}}
for solutions vanishing on the boundary. Although the immediate equivalence between these two norms may not be apparent through elementary integration by parts, Lemma
4.1
provides a rigorous justification

Lemma 4.3
.

If
f\in H^{4}(\Omega)
satisfies
f=\partial_{2}f=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\int_{\Omega}f(x)dx=0
, then

\rVert f\rVert_{{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\rVert\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The only problematic part is to ensure that the mixed derivatives can be estimated by the Laplacian/Bilaplacian operator. The first inequality is trivial since
\rVert\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\rVert\partial_{11}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\partial_{22}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\rVert\partial_{12}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
, which is strong enough to control all the second derivatives of
f
. For the second inequality, we simply apply Lemma
4.1
with
k=0
, yielding the desired result.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{4}(\Omega)
and
\theta(t)
be the unique smooth solution to (
4.3
). It holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq_{C}-C(1-C\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}})\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\ +\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

Using (
4.3
), we compute

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx+\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx

We simplify the linear term first. Let us write

\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx=\int\left(\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})-(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s})\Delta^{2}u_{2}\right)\Delta^{2}\theta dx+\int(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s})\Delta^{2}u_{2}\Delta^{2}\theta dx=:A_{1}+A_{2}.

For
A_{1}
, the usual tame estimate yields

\displaystyle|A_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})-(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s})\Delta^{2}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

For
A_{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\int-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\Delta^{2}\Psi\Delta^{2}\theta dx=\int\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta\partial_{1}\Delta^{2}\theta dx=\int\Delta(\partial_{2}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta)\partial_{1}\Delta\theta dx

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\int\partial_{2}\rho_{s}|\partial_{1}\Delta\theta|^{2}dx+\int(\partial_{222}\rho_{s}\partial_{1}\theta+2\partial_{22}\rho_{s}\partial_{12}\theta)\partial_{1}\Delta\theta dx=:A_{21}+A_{22},

while
A_{22}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle|A_{22}|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{222}\rho_{s}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{2}}^{3/2}\leq\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{3/2}\leq\frac{\inf(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s})}{4}A_{21}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

where the last inequality follows from Young’s inequality. Thus, using (
4.1
), we observe that (
4.2
) can be written as

A_{2}\leq-\frac{3}{4}\inf(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s})\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Combining this with (
4.2
), we get

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{2}(-\partial_{2}\rho_{s}u_{2})\Delta^{2}\theta dx

\displaystyle\leq-C\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

where the last equality follows from Lemma
4.2
, which ensures that the integral over
\partial\Omega
appearing in the integration by parts vanishes.

Now we estimate the integral coming from the nonlinear term in (
4.4
). We derive a necessary estimate in a separate lemma. Indeed, Lemma
4.5
gives us that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left|\int\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx\right|\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\theta\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Using Lemma
4.1
,

\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq_{C}\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{5}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\theta\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq_{C}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta\theta\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.3
. Plugging this into (
4.8
), and combining it with (
4.7
), we conclude the proposition.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
u
be a smooth divergence free vector field such that
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
f
be a smooth scalar-valued function such that
f=\partial_{2}f=\partial_{2}^{2}\overline{f}=0
on
\partial\Omega
where
\overline{f}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)\Delta^{2}fdx\right|\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

Proof.

We split the integral
\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)\Delta^{2}fdx
as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)-u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{2}f\right)\Delta^{2}\theta+\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}(\Delta^{2}f)^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)-u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{2}\theta\right)\Delta^{2}fdx,

where the last integral vanishes due to the incompressibility of
u
and the boundary condition
u=0
on
\Omega
. Next, we further decompose the last integral as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla f)-u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{2}f\right)\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u_{1}\partial_{1}f)-u_{1}\Delta^{2}\partial_{1}f\right)\Delta^{2}fdx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u_{2}\partial_{2}f)-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}f\right)\Delta^{2}fdx

(4.9)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

Estimate for
I_{1}
.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we estimate

|I_{1}|\leq\rVert\Delta^{2}(u_{1}\partial_{1}f)-u_{1}\Delta^{2}\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Using Lemma
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\rVert\Delta^{2}(u_{1}\partial_{1}f)-u_{1}\Delta^{2}\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{2}}+\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{3}}.

where the second inequality is due to the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, using Lemma
4.3
, we can replace
\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{2}}
and
\rVert\partial_{1}f\rVert_{H^{3}}
in the above estimate by
\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}
and
\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}
, respectively.
Hence, we obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle|I_{1}|\leq\left(\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u_{1}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Before we start estimating
I_{2}
, we consider the structure of the bi-Laplacian acting on a product of two functions. Expanding the bi-Laplacian using the formula
\Delta(fg)=\Delta gh+2\nabla g\cdot\nabla h+g\Delta h
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}(gh)-f\Delta^{2}h

\displaystyle=\Delta(\Delta gh+2\nabla g\cdot\nabla h+g\Delta h)-g\Delta^{2}h

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}gh+2\nabla\Delta g\cdot\nabla h+\Delta g\Delta h

\displaystyle\ +2\left(\nabla\Delta g\cdot\nabla h+\nabla\partial_{1}g\cdot\nabla\partial_{1}h+\nabla\partial_{2}g\cdot\nabla\partial_{2}h+\nabla g\cdot\nabla\Delta h\right)

\displaystyle\ +\Delta g\Delta h+\nabla g\cdot\nabla\Delta h

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}gh+4\nabla\Delta g\cdot\nabla h+2{\left(\nabla\partial_{1}g\cdot\nabla\partial_{1}h+\nabla\partial_{2}g\cdot\nabla\partial_{2}h+\Delta g\cdot\Delta h\right)}+\nabla g\cdot\nabla\Delta h.

Estimate for
I_{2}
.

Next, we move on to estimate
I_{2}
in (
4.2
). Recalling our notation that
\overline{f}(x_{2}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{2},

we split
I_{2}
as

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u_{2}\partial_{2}f)-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}f\right)\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u_{2}\partial_{2}(f-\overline{f}))-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}(f-\overline{f})\right)\Delta^{2}fdx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta^{2}(u_{2}\partial_{2}\overline{f})-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}\overline{f}\right)\Delta^{2}{f}dx

(4.12)

\displaystyle=:I_{21}+I_{22}.

Again, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives us

|I_{21}|\leq\rVert\Delta^{2}(u_{2}(f-\overline{f}))-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}(f-\overline{f})\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}},

while Lemma
3.4
yields

\displaystyle\rVert\Delta^{2}(u_{2}(f-\overline{f}))-u_{2}\Delta^{2}\partial_{2}(f-\overline{f})\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{2}(f-\overline{f})\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert f-\overline{f}\rVert_{H^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{12}f\rVert_{{H}^{1}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

where the second inequality is due to
\rVert\overline{f}\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}\leq\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}(\Omega)}
and Lemma
3.5
, and the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.3
. Hence, we get

(4.13)

\displaystyle|I_{21}|\leq\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Next, we estimate
I_{22}
. Recalling
I_{22}
from (
4.2
) and applying (
4.2
) with
g=u_{2},\ h=\partial_{2}\overline{f}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}u_{2}\partial_{2}\overline{f}\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}(4\nabla\Delta u_{2}\cdot\nabla\partial_{2}\overline{f}+2\left(\nabla\partial_{1}u_{2}\cdot\nabla\partial_{12}\overline{f}+\nabla\partial_{2}u_{2}\cdot\nabla\partial_{22}\overline{f}+\Delta u_{2}\Delta\partial_{2}\overline{f}+\nabla u_{2}\cdot\nabla\Delta\partial_{2}\overline{f}\right))\Delta^{2}fdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}u_{2}\partial_{2}\overline{f}\Delta^{2}fdx+\int_{\Omega}(4\partial_{2}\Delta u_{2}\cdot\partial_{22}\overline{f}+2\left((\partial_{22}u_{2}+\Delta u_{2})\cdot\partial_{222}\overline{f}+\partial_{2}u_{2}\cdot\partial_{2222}\overline{f}\right))\Delta^{2}fdx

(4.14)

\displaystyle=:I_{221}+I_{222},

where the second last equality is due to the fact that
\overline{f}
does not depend on the variable
x_{1}
.
For
I_{222}
, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we just need a crude estimate,

\displaystyle|I_{222}|

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\rVert\partial_{22}\overline{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert f\rVert_{H^{4}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\leq_{C}\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we used the Sobolev embedding
L^{\infty}([0,1])\hookrightarrow H^{1}([0,1])
to have
\rVert\partial_{22}\overline{f}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\rVert{f}\rVert_{H^{4}}
in the the second inequality, and we used Lemma
4.3
in the last inequality.

Lastly, we estimate
I_{221}
. Let
\Psi
be the stream function of
u
such that

\int_{\Omega}\Psi dx=0,\quad u=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi\text{ with }\Psi=\nabla\Psi=0\text{ on $\partial\Omega$}.

Indeed, such a stream function exists since
u
is divergence free and, especially,
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Using the integration by parts, we get

\displaystyle I_{221}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\Delta^{2}\Psi\partial_{2}\overline{f}\Delta^{2}fdx=-\int\left(\Delta^{2}\Psi\partial_{2}\overline{f}\right)\Delta^{2}\partial_{1}fdx=-\int\Delta\left(\Delta^{2}\Psi\partial_{2}\overline{f}\right)\Delta\partial_{1}fdx,

where the integration by parts in the last equality is justified by the assumption that
\partial_{2}\theta=\partial_{22}\overline{f}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, expanding
\Delta(\Delta^{2}\Psi\partial_{2}\overline{f})
, we get

\displaystyle I_{221}

\displaystyle=-\int(\Delta^{3}\Psi\partial_{2}\overline{f}+2\partial_{2}\Delta^{2}\Psi\cdot\partial_{22}\overline{f}+\Delta^{2}\Psi\partial_{222}\overline{f})\partial_{1}\Delta\theta dx.

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle|I_{221}|

\displaystyle\leq\rVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{6}}\rVert\overline{f}\rVert_{W^{3,\infty}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\overline{f}\rVert_{H^{4}}\leq\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}},

where we used Lemma
4.3
in the last inequality. Combining this with (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), we get

|I_{22}|\leq\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Combining this with (
4.13
) and (
4.2
), we have

|I_{2}|\leq\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Combining this with (
4.10
) and (
4.2
), we get

\left|\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{2}(u\cdot\nabla\theta)\Delta^{2}\theta dx\right|\leq\left(\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{H^{3}}+\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{W^{2,\infty}}\right)\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert u\rVert_{H^{5}}\rVert\partial_{1}\Delta f\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert\Delta^{2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Analysis of the energy structure

As in the previous section for the IPM equation, we define

(4.16)

\displaystyle E(t):=\int_{\Omega}(\rho(t)-\rho_{0}^{*})x_{2}dx,\quad K(t):=\rVert\Delta\Psi(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where
\rho_{0}^{*}
is the vertical rearrangement of the initial density
\rho_{0}
. Throughout this subsection, we will assume that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Psi(t)\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2}dt\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}\ll 1,\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$ for some $T>0$ and $\delta_{0}>0$}.

Proposition 4.6
.

There exists
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(\gamma,\rVert\rho_{s}\rVert_{H^{4}})>0
such that if (
4.17
) holds, then

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-K(t),\quad\frac{d}{dt}K(t)\leq-C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

Differentiating the energy difference
E(t)
in time, we see that

(4.19)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{t}x_{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla\rho x_{2}dx=\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx.

Using (
1.5
), we can further simplify the last expression as

\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\Psi\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\Psi\partial_{1}\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\Psi\Delta^{2}\Psi dx=-\int_{\Omega}|\Delta\Psi|^{2}dx,

therefore, we get

(4.20)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-\int_{\Omega}|\Delta\Psi|^{2}dx=-K(t).

In order to estimate
\frac{d}{dt}K(t)
, taking a derivative in (
4.19
), we compute

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho_{t}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u_{2}\rho dx.

Using
u_{2}=\partial_{1}\Psi
and
\partial_{1}\rho=\Delta^{2}\Psi
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u_{2}\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Psi\partial_{1}\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Psi\Delta^{2}\Psi dx=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\Delta^{2}\Psi\Psi dx=-\int_{\Omega}{\partial_{t}}\partial_{1}\rho\Psi dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\rho u_{2}dx

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}E(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u_{2}\rho_{t}=-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}u\cdot\nabla\rho dx=-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho dx

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx-\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Using the assumption on
\rVert\theta\rVert_{H^{3}}
in (
4.17
), we have

-\int_{\Omega}u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho dx=-\int u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}\rho_{s}dx-\int u_{2}^{2}\partial_{2}(\rho-\rho_{s})dx\geq\gamma\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\sqrt{\delta_{0}}\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

where we used the strict positivity of
\gamma
which is assumed in (
4.1
).
Using (
4.20
), we see that the above inequality implies

(4.21)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}K(t)+C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\rVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Let us estimate
\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Using (
1.5
), we rewrite

(4.22)

\displaystyle\rVert u_{1}\partial_{1}\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\rVert\partial_{2}\Psi\Delta^{2}\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\rVert\nabla\Psi\rVert_{L^{4}}\rVert\Delta^{2}\Psi\rVert_{L^{4}}\leq\rVert\Delta^{2}\Psi\rVert_{H^{2}}\rVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}},

where the last inequality is due to the Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation theorem. Moreover, we notice from (
1.5
) that
g(x_{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{2222}g(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{2222}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Delta^{2}\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})-(\partial_{1111}+2\partial_{1122})\Psi(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{1}\rho dx=0,

4.3

\displaystyle C^{-1}g(t)\leq{C}^{-1}\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{4}}^{2}\leq f(t)\leq C\rVert\theta(t)\rVert_{H^{4}}^{2}\leq CM\epsilon^{2},\text{ for all $t\in[0,T^{*}]$},\quad f(0)\leq C\epsilon^{2},

Substituting this into (

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma

We argue by induction on

, that is, we will prove that for every

,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}^{n}\partial_{2}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{n,k}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{n+k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{n+k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\right),\text{ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}$ and $f\in X^{\infty}$.}

When

.
We split the cases

and

Case:

.
It is trivial that

(A.2)

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{2}^{k+1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C_{k}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{k+1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k+1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\right).

n=1
.
We have

\displaystyle\rVert\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k+1}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k+1}f(x)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}^{k+1}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{11}\partial_{2}^{k+1}f(x)\partial_{2}^{k+1}f(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{11}\partial_{2}^{k}f(x)\partial_{2}^{k+2}f(x)dx

where the integration by parts to get the second equality is justified by (
3.5
). Using the induction hypothesis (
A.1
) for

\rVert\partial_{11}\partial_{2}^{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{k}\left(\rVert\partial_{1}^{k+2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\rVert\partial_{2}^{k+2}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)
