Remarkable structures in integrable probability, I: max-independence structures
Yacine Barhoumi-Andréani

Abstract
. We analyse in a systematic way the occurrences of a remarkable structure in the theory of integrable probability that we call a “max-independence structure”, when random variables are constructed as a maximum of a sequence of independent random variables. The list of treated examples contains : the G ​ U ​ E GU\!E and G ​ O ​ E GO\!E Tracy-Widom distributions, the extreme eigenvalues/eigenangles of random hermitian/unitary matrices (and in particular the historical example of the G ​ U ​ E GU\!E extreme eigenvalues), the Hopf-Cole solution to the KPZ equation with Dirac initial condition (continuum random polymer) and the symmetric Schur measure. In this last case, the largest part of the underlying random partition is the maximum of an i.i.d randomisation of the deterministic sequence of negative integers. In the case of the G ​ U ​ E GU\!E Tracy-Widom distribution, the random variables use a rescaling of the prolate hyperspheroidal wave functions that were introduced by Heurtley and Slepian in the context of circular optical mirrors. To illustrate the utility of such a structure, we rescale the largest eigenvalue of the G ​ U ​ E GU\!E written as a maximum of N N independent random variables with the classical Poisson approximation for sums of indicators. We use for this the Okamoto-Noumi-Yamada theory of the sigma-form of the Painlevé equation applied to random matrix theory by Forrester-Witte. By doing so, we find a new expression for the cumulative distribution function of the G ​ U ​ E GU\!E Tracy-Widom distribution which is shown to be equivalent to the classical one using manipulations à la Forrester-Witte.

1.2.
Max-independence structures

1.2.1.
Detropicalised motivation : independence for traces and total parts

Two of the most classical models in integrable probability are given by the
{GU\!E}_{N}
and the Schur measure, defined respectively in (
43
) and (
76
). The eigenvalues
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k,N})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}
of
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\!N}\sim{GU\!E}_{N}
satisfies the identity in law (which is in fact a deterministic identity given by the invariance by conjugacy of the trace)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\!N}[k,k]

The sum in the RHS is composed of independent (identically distributed) Gaussians. An identity of the same type is given for the parts of the random partition
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\operatorname{Schur}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})}:=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}^{\operatorname{Schur}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})})_{k\geqslant 1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Schur}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})}
where
{\boldsymbol{p}}:=(p_{k})_{k}
,
{\boldsymbol{q}}:=(q_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
and
p_{i},q_{j}\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}^{\operatorname{Schur}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\sum_{i,j\geqslant 1}\operatorname{Geom}(p_{i}q_{j})

Here,
\operatorname{Geom}(q)
designates a random variable geometrically distributed with the convention
\mathbb{P}{\left(\operatorname{Geom}(q)=k\right)}=(1-q)q^{k}\mathds{1}_{\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}
; see (
117
) for a proof. In the same vein as for the
{GU\!E}
, the random variables in the RHS of the previous identity are independent.

One could call such identities in law between sums of dependent random variables and sums of independent random variables a
sum-independence structure
. In this paper, we will be concerned with a
tropical
version (see
[
80
]
) of these two identities :

\displaystyle\max_{k\geqslant 1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\max_{k\geqslant 1}W_{k}

where the random variables
(W_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
in the RHS are independent. We call such an identity a
max-independence structure
3
3

3

It also includes the case of a minimum, so we could also call it a
tropical independence structure
, but in the same vein convexity relates to concavity, we will stick to this terminology.

3.

3.1.

{GU\!E}

The Gaussian Unitary Ensemble of size

4.
Max-independence structure in the symmetric Schur measure

The relevant notations for this section are given in Annex

.

4.1.
Motivations

The Schur measure was introduced by Okounkov
[
99
,
101
]
following a study of the
z
-measure
[
100
]
introduced by Borodin and Olshanski
[
32
,
33
]
to generalise the Plancherel measure ; this last measure saw a regain of activity in relation with the Ulam problem on the longuest increasing subsequence of a random uniform permutation by Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
6
]
. Such generalisations were designed to highlight the general mechanism explaining the apparition of a determinantal structure in the Poisson-Plancherel measure and we refer to the surveys
[
75
,
76
,
104
]
for a zoo of models that fit into this framework. The general explanation is as follows: the Schur measure of parameters
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a measure on partitions
\mathbb{Y}_{\infty}:=\cup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathbb{Y}_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\!\!\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(\lambda):=H{\left[-\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\right]}s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{A}\right]}s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{B}\right]}

(76)

The specialisation
s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{A}\right]}
is written
\varphi(s_{\lambda})
in
[
104
]
and should be understood as an “abstract” algebra morphism of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
where the generators
(h_{k})_{k}
,
(e_{k})_{k}
or
(p_{k})_{k}
have been attributed a particular value, the value of the Schur function resulting then from an algebraic formula expressing it with the generators, for instance the Jacobi-Trudi formula (
113
). This measure is a probability measure if the numbers such defined are real, for instance if
\mathcal{A}=\overline{\mathcal{B}}\subset\mathbb{C}
, or if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are positive specialisations of the Schur functions (see Annex
A
).

The main result of interest is the determinantal character (with explicit kernel) of the shifted point process
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-k+a)_{k\geqslant 1}
(with
a\in\left\{0,1,\frac{1}{2}\right\}
depending on the conventions),
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
being the random partition distributed according to
\mathbb{P}_{\!\!\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
. So far, seven proofs of this determinantal nature have been given and we refer to them for this fact: fermionic Gaussian “integral”
[
99
]
, Riemann-Hilbert problems
[
22
]
, Jacobi-Trudi formula
[
107
]
, ratio of alternants formula
[
75
]
, Giambelli formula and
L
-ensembles
[
29
]
, coupling on Gelfand-Tsetlin graph
[
55
]
and Macdonald operator
[
1
]
. See also
[
30
]
for a discussion on the kernel.

The success of this generalisation prefigures the numerous extensions of the framework in many different directions, for instance the dynamical version of “Schur processes”
[
102
,
103
]
and, in fine, the creation of the theory of integrable probability
[
26
,
34
]
.

Remark 4.1
.

We adopt here the convention of e.g. Johansson
[
75
]
that writes this measure on partitions over
\mathbb{Z}
, as opposed to the Okounkov convention
[
99
,
101
]
(the half-infinite wedge space/fermionic Fock space has a basis indexed by Maya diagrams written on
\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
by convention). Such conventions are of course equivalent, but the formuli differ by some square roots ; formuli are given here for the process
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-k)_{k\geqslant 1}
and not
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-k+\frac{1}{2})_{k\geqslant 1}
.

4.2.
The Poisson-Plancherel measure

The Plancherel measure on
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
is defined by
[
31
,
74
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{d_{\lambda}^{2}}{n!}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\lambda\vdash n\right\}}

(77)

where
d_{\lambda}
is the dimension of the irreducible module of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
indexed by
\lambda
(see e.g.
[
83
, I-6]
). This measure can be understood as the conditioning of a general measure
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
on
\mathbb{Y}_{\infty}
by the event
\left\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\vdash n\right\}
(where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
), and the law of
{\left|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right|}
gives then the choice of the relevant randomisation for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\sim\mathbb{P}_{n}
to obtain a determinantal structure. Since
d_{\lambda}=n!\,s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}
by (
112
), setting
{\left|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right|}\sim\operatorname{Po}(\xi)
gives the Schur measure of parameters
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\sqrt{\xi}\mathcal{E}
. We thus define the Poisson-Plancherel measure on
\mathbb{Y}_{\infty}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}(\lambda):=e^{-\xi}\xi^{{\left|\lambda\right|}}{\left(\frac{d_{\lambda}}{{\left|\lambda\right|}!}\right)}^{2}

(78)

One then has

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:=(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-k)_{k\geqslant 1}\sim\operatorname{DPP}(\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)})

implying in particular

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{(\xi)}<s\right)}=\det(I-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)})_{\ell^{2}(s+\mathbb{N})}=\det(I-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi),s})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}

where
\mathcal{K}_{\!\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi),s}(x,y)=\mathcal{K}_{\!\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}(x+s,y+s)
.

The corresponding specialisation of the Schur kernel
K_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
in this case is the
discrete Bessel kernel

[
31
,
74
]
that has several forms :

•

the
Hankel convolutive form
is
[
74
, (3.31)]

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}(x,y):=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}B_{k}^{(\xi)}(x)B_{k}^{(\xi)}(y),\qquad B_{k}^{(\xi)}(x):=J_{x+k}(2\sqrt{\xi})

(79)

where
J_{k}
is the
k
-th Bessel function of the first kind defined by :

\displaystyle J_{k}(x):={\left[z^{k}\right]}e^{x(z-z^{-1})/2}

(80)

•

the
Christoffel-Darboux form
is (
[
101
, (23)]
,
[
74
, (1.10)]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}(x,y)=\sqrt{\xi}\frac{B_{1}^{(\xi)}(x)B_{0}^{(\xi)}(y)-B_{0}^{(\xi)}(x)B_{1}^{(\xi)}(y)}{x-y}\mathds{1}_{\left\{x\neq y\right\}}+\sqrt{\xi}\dot{J}_{x}(2\sqrt{\xi})\mathds{1}_{\left\{x=y\right\}}

(81)

with
\dot{J}_{x}(t):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}J_{\alpha}(t)\big|_{\alpha=x}
(see e.g.
[
105
, 10.2.4]
)

•

and the
integral form
is
[
101
, (22)]

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}(x,y)&=\oint_{\mathbb{U}\times\rho\mathbb{U}}\frac{z^{-x}\omega^{-y}}{1-z\omega}e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z-z^{-1}+\omega-\omega^{-1})}\frac{d^{*}z}{z}\frac{d^{*}\omega}{\omega},\qquad\rho<1\\
&=\oint_{\mathbb{U}\times R\mathbb{U}}\frac{z^{-x}\omega^{+y}}{1-z\omega^{-1}}e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z-z^{-1}-(\omega-\omega^{-1}))}\frac{d^{*}z}{z}\frac{d^{*}\omega}{\omega},\qquad R>1\end{aligned}

(82)

Using the expansion
\frac{1}{1-z\omega}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(z\omega)^{k}
for
z\in\mathbb{U}
and
\omega\in\rho\mathbb{U}
with
\rho<1
, one obtains (
79
) from (
82
) and (
80
).

The Hankel convolutive form (
79
) is in direct analogy with the Hankel convolutive form (
2
) of the Airy kernel :
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi),s}
is the square of the (discrete) Hankel convolution operator

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(B_{s}^{(\xi)}):f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\mapsto\sum_{k\geqslant 0}B_{s}^{(\xi)}(\cdot+k)f(k)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

namely

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\!\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi),s}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(B_{s}^{(\xi)})^{2}

(83)

which is analogous to (
16
) on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

One could hope to extend the proof of theorem
1.2
using a discrete analogue of
\det(I-{\boldsymbol{K}}_{t})=\exp{\left(-\int_{t}^{+\infty}\operatorname{tr}((I-{\boldsymbol{K}}_{s})^{-1}\dot{{\boldsymbol{K}}}_{s})ds\right)}
. Such a formula reads

\displaystyle\det(I-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{N})_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}=\exp{\left(-\sum_{\ell\geqslant N}\operatorname{tr}{\left(\log(I-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell+1})-\log(I-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell})\right)}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\right)}

(84)

To simplify it, one needs the theory of Orthogonal Polynomials on the Unit Circle (OPUC).

4.3.
OPUCs

The main reference for this § is
[
115
]
from which we will only present the properties that are required in the sequel.

A family
(\Phi_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
of monic polynomials (i.e.
\Phi_{n}(z)=z^{n}+\cdots
) is orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu)
for a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{U}
if

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi_{k},\Phi_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\!L^{2}(\mu)}:=\oint{U}\Phi_{k}\overline{\Phi_{\ell}}\,d\mu=\left|\!\left|\Phi_{k}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\delta_{k,\ell}

Defining the anti-
L^{2}(\mu)
-unitary map
∗,n
by
P^{*,n}(z):=z^{n}\overline{P(\overline{z}^{-1})}
and dropping the
n
by convention when the polynomial is of degree
n
, one can show that
[
115
, (2.17), (2.18)]

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(z)=z\Phi_{n}(z)-\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z),\qquad\alpha_{n}:=-\overline{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}

The coefficient
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
are called the
Verblunsky coefficients
and the previous recursion is called the
Szegö recursion
. One can easily prove that
[
115
, (2.19)]

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n+1}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}={\left(1-{\left|\alpha_{n}\right|}^{2}\right)}\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}{\left(1-{\left|\alpha_{k}\right|}^{2}\right)}

(85)

and that for a non trivial measure
\mu
(i.e. not supported on a finite set of points)
{\left|\alpha_{k}\right|}<1

[
115
, thm. 2.1]
. As a result, one has

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n+1}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leqslant\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

(86)

For a probability measure
\mu
, one defines the Toeplitz determinant
D_{n}(\mu)
as

\displaystyle D_{n}(\mu):=\det{\left(\oint{U}z^{i-j}d\mu(z)\right)}_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}

Hence, using linear combinations of lines and columns one gets

\displaystyle D_{n}(\mu):=\det{\left(\oint{U}\Phi_{i}(z)\overline{\Phi_{j}(z)}d\mu(z)\right)}_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n}\left|\!\left|\Phi_{j}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-{\left|\alpha_{j}\right|}^{2})^{n-j}

This implies the Szegö theorem
[
115
, (8.2)]

\displaystyle F(\mu):=\prod_{j\geqslant 0}(1-{\left|\alpha_{j}\right|}^{2})=\lim_{n\to+\infty}D_{n}(\mu)^{1/n}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{D_{n+1}(\mu)}{D_{n}(\mu)}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left|\!\left|\Phi_{n}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

(87)

Note that
F(\mu)
can be equal to 0, in which case
(\alpha_{k})_{k}\notin\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
(see
[
115
, thm. 2.2]
for a consequence).

4.4.
Independence structure in the Poisson-Plancherel measure

The Cauchy formula for the Cauchy kernel (
108
) in abstract alphabets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
and the (restricted) orthogonality of the Schur functions (
115
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}H{\left[\widehat{\omega}(\mathcal{A}U+\mathcal{B}U^{-1})\right]}dU

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}\sum_{\lambda,\mu}s_{\lambda}{\left[\widehat{\omega}\mathcal{A}\right]}s_{\lambda}(U)s_{\mu}{\left[\widehat{\omega}\mathcal{B}\right]}s_{\mu}(U^{-1})dU

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell(\lambda)\leqslant N}s_{\lambda}{\left[\widehat{\omega}\mathcal{A}\right]}s_{\lambda}{\left[\widehat{\omega}\mathcal{B}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\leqslant N}s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{A}\right]}s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{B}\right]}

As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}=H{\left[-\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\right]}\int_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}H{\left[\widehat{\omega}(\mathcal{A}U+\mathcal{B}U^{-1})\right]}dU

(88)

Using Weyl integration formula (
53
) and the Andréieff-Heine-Szegö formula (
44
), the random unitary matrix integral in the RHS can be transformed into the Toeplitz determinant of symbol
z\mapsto H{\left[\widehat{\omega}(z\mathcal{A}+z^{-1}\mathcal{B})\right]}
(see remark
3.6
). The determinantal character of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}
allows moreover to write the LHS as a Fredholm determinant on
\ell^{2}(N+\mathbb{N}^{*})
. The formula thus obtained is the celebrated Borodin-Okounkov formula
[
30
]
that was also discovered by Geronimo and Case the context of the inverse scattering method
[
39
]
and that meanwhile had several other proofs and generalisations
[
7
,
10
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

Let us only consider the Plancherel case
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\sqrt{\xi}\mathcal{E}
. One has
H{\left[-\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\right]}=H{\left[-\xi\mathcal{E}\right]}=e^{-\xi}
, and
H{\left[\widehat{\omega}\mathcal{A}U\right]}=e^{p_{1}{\left[\sqrt{\xi}U\right]}}=e^{\sqrt{\xi}\operatorname{tr}(U)}
, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}=e^{-\xi}\int_{\mathcal{U}_{N}}e^{\sqrt{\xi}\operatorname{tr}(U+U^{-1})}dU=e^{-\xi}\det\mathbb{T}_{N}(\mathcal{I}_{\xi}),\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\xi}(z):=e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z+z^{-1})}

Define the probability measure on
\mathbb{U}

\displaystyle\mu_{\xi}(d\theta):=\frac{e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z+z^{-1})}}{I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})}\frac{d^{*}z}{z},\qquad I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi}):={\left[z^{0}\right]}e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z+z^{-1})}

where the normalisation
I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})
is the
0
-th modified Bessel function defined by

\displaystyle I_{k}(s):={\left[z^{k}\right]}e^{s(z+z^{-1})}

We also define :

•

{\left(\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}\right)}_{k\geqslant 0}
as the OPUCs for
L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})
,

•

(\alpha_{k}(\xi))_{k\geqslant 0}
as their Verblunsky coefficients.

Then, writing the Toeplitz determinant as a product of norms of OPUCs gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\oint{U}{\left|\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}(z)\right|}^{2}e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z+z^{-1})}\frac{d^{*}z}{z}

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})\left|\!\left|\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})}^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi}\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-{\left|\alpha_{j}(\xi)\right|}^{2})

Set

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \pi_{k}(\xi)&:=I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})\left|\!\left|\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})}^{2}=\oint{U}{\left|\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}(z)\right|}^{2}e^{\sqrt{\xi}(z+z^{-1})}\frac{d^{*}z}{z}\\
q_{k}(\xi)&:=\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(\xi)}\end{aligned}

(89)

Letting
N\to+\infty
gives the the Szegö theorem (
87
)

\displaystyle 1=e^{-\xi}\prod_{k\geqslant 0}\pi_{k}(\xi)

Note that when the measure
\mu
is not a probability measure, one needs to take into account the normalisation factor and replace the norms by the
\pi_{k}
s. In this setting, the Szegö theorem/the absolute convergence of the product gives

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(\xi)\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}1\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\left|\!\left|\Phi_{k}^{(\xi)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})}^{2}\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})^{-1}=\prod_{j\geqslant 0}(1-{\left|\alpha_{j}(\xi)\right|}^{2})

As a consequence of the previous computations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}=\prod_{k\geqslant N}\pi_{k}(\xi)^{-1}=:\prod_{k\geqslant N}q_{k}(\xi)=\prod_{\ell\geqslant 0}q_{\ell+N}(\xi)

We are now ready to state the

Theorem 4.2

(
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}
is a maximum of a sequence of independent random variables)
.

There exist i.i.d. non negative random variables
(Z_{k}^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)})_{k\geqslant 0}
such that

\displaystyle\boxed{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\max_{k\geqslant 0}\!\left\{Z_{k}^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}-k\right\}}

(90)

Moreover

\displaystyle Z_{1}^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\inf\!\left\{j\geqslant 0\,/\,\forall\,m\geqslant j,\ B_{j}(\xi)=0\right\}

(91)

where the
(B_{j}(\xi))_{j\geqslant 0}
are independent
\left\{0,1\right\}
-Bernoulli random variables of expectation
{\left|\alpha_{j}(\xi)\right|}^{2}
.

Proof.

One thus has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}=\prod_{\ell\geqslant 0}q_{\ell+N}(\xi)

with

\displaystyle 0\leqslant q_{\ell}(\xi)=\frac{1}{I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})}\left|\!\left|\Phi_{\ell}^{(\xi)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})}^{-2}\underset{\ell\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}1

Moreover, since norms of OPUCs are decreasing by (
86
),
(q_{\ell}(\xi))_{\ell}
is decreasing. This can also be seen using (
85
), noticing that
1-{\left|\alpha_{k}(\xi)\right|}^{2}<1
and writing

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I_{0}(\sqrt{\xi})}\left|\!\left|\Phi_{\ell}^{(\xi)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{U},\mu_{\xi})}^{-2}=\prod_{k\geqslant 0}(1-{\left|\alpha_{k}(\xi)\right|}^{2})\times\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{1-{\left|\alpha_{j}(\xi)\right|}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\geqslant\ell}(1-{\left|\alpha_{k}(\xi)\right|}^{2})

As a result, there exists a random variable
Z^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(\xi)=\mathbb{P}{\left(Z^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}\leqslant\ell\right)}

and in particular, with an i.i.d. sequence
(Z^{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}_{\ell})_{\ell}
of such random variables

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{PoPl}(\xi)}{\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\leqslant N\right)}

Remark on the discrete Fuchs lemma

In the vein of §

Lemma 4.4

(Discrete unnormalised and varying interval Fuchs lemma)
.

\bullet
For
I\subset\mathbb{N}
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}:\ell^{2}(I)\to\ell^{2}(I)
be self-adjoint. Let
(g_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell})
be such that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell}g_{\ell}
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Define
\Delta u_{k}:=u_{k+1}-u_{k}
and
\nabla u_{k}:=u_{k}-u_{k-1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda_{\ell}=\frac{\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\!\ell^{2}(I)}}{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\!\ell^{2}(I)}}

\bullet
For
I_{\ell}:=\ell+\mathbb{N}:=\{\ell,\ell+1,\dots,\}
, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}:\ell^{2}(I_{\ell})\to\ell^{2}(I_{\ell})
be self-adjoint with a kernel independent of
\ell
. Let
(g_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell})
be such that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell}g_{\ell}
(with an index to denote the action on
\ell^{2}(I_{\ell})
). Then,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell+1}=\lambda_{\ell}\frac{\left\langle g_{\ell},g_{\ell+1}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}(I_{\ell+1})}}{\left\langle g_{\ell},g_{\ell+1}\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}(I_{\ell})}}

Proof.

A discrete differentiation of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell}g_{\ell}
yields to

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell+1}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}\Delta g_{\ell}=\Delta\lambda_{\ell}g_{\ell+1}+\lambda_{\ell}\Delta g_{\ell}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)\Delta g_{\ell}=-\Delta(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)g_{\ell+1}

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}
and
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)\Delta g_{\ell}\in{\operatorname{Im}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)
, one has
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)\Delta g_{\ell}\perp\ker(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}I)=\mathbb{R}g_{\ell}
. In the first case, one takes the scalar product with
g_{\ell}
to obtain the result.

In the second case, one has

\displaystyle(\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell})g_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geqslant\ell+1}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,r)g_{\ell+1}(r)-\sum_{r\geqslant\ell}\mathcal{K}(\cdot,r)g_{\ell+1}(r)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{K}(\cdot,\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell)

Hence, with
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\ell}:=\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\ell^{2}(I_{\ell})}

\displaystyle\left\langle(\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell})g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\mathcal{K}(\cdot,\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell),g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\mathcal{K}(\ell,\cdot),g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}g_{\ell+1}(\ell)

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell}(\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell)

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{\ell}g_{\ell}(\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell)

Finally

\displaystyle\Delta\lambda_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}}{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{\ell}\frac{g_{\ell}(\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell)}{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}}

namely

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell}{\left(1-\frac{g_{\ell}(\ell)g_{\ell+1}(\ell)}{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell}\frac{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell+1\color[rgb]{0,0,0}}}{\left\langle g_{\ell+1},g_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\ell}}

which gives the result.
∎

4.7.
A characterisation of the KPZ universality class with max-independence, II

Similarly to §
3.4.6
, one can formalise when a convergence towards the Tracy-Widom or the Gumbel distribution occurs in the case of a max-independence structure obtained as a randomisation of the sequence of integers :

Theorem 4.5

(Convergence of Schur max-independence structures)
.

Let
(Z_{k}(\xi))_{k\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with a diverging parameter
\xi\to+\infty
. Define

with

. Suppose moreover that one has :

(1)

either the local CLT

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}\mathbb{P}{\left(Z(\xi)={\left[m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}X\right]}\right)}\underset{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\phi(x)

(94)

(2)

or (equivalently) the right LDP

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}\mathbb{P}{\left(Z(\xi)\geqslant m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}X\right)}\underset{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\Phi(X):=\int_{X}^{+\infty}\phi

(95)

Suppose moreover that the previous convergences occur, as functions of
X
, in

for all

and that

Proof.

The Poisson approximation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{\left(\max_{k\geqslant 0}\left\{Z_{k}(\xi)-k\right\}\leqslant x\right)}=\exp{\left(-\sum_{k\geqslant 0}p_{k}\right)}+O{\left(\sum_{k\geqslant 0}p_{k}^{2}\right)}

One has on the one hand

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant 0}p_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geqslant x}\overline{F}_{\!\xi}(\ell)=\int_{x}^{+\infty}\overline{F}_{\!\xi}(t)dt

as one defines

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\!\xi}(\ell):=\mathbb{P}{\left(Z(\xi)>\ell\right)}=\mathbb{P}{\left(Z(\xi)>{\left[\ell\right]}\right)}=\overline{F}_{\!\xi}({\left[\ell\right]})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\sum_{\ell\geqslant k+x}p_{\xi}(\ell),\qquad p_{\xi}(\ell):=\mathbb{P}{\left(Z(\xi)=\ell\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(\ell-x)_{+}p_{\xi}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\int{R}(y-x)_{+}p_{\xi}({\left[y\right]})dy

which shows that with this particular max-independence structure, the Poisson approximation already gives the final form.

Changing variable
t=m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}T
and
y=m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}Y
then gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geqslant 0}p_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}^{+\infty}\sigma_{\xi}\overline{F}_{\!\xi}(m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}T)dT\hskip 39.83368pt\underset{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\int_{X}^{+\infty}\Phi(T)dT

\displaystyle=\int{R}(Y-X)_{+}\sigma_{\xi}p_{\xi}({\left[m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}Y\right]})dY\underset{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\int{R}(Y-X)_{+}\phi(Y)dY

using respectively (
95
) and (
94
). Last, the same convergence but in
L^{2}
allows to control the speed of convergence in the Poisson approximation into the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}p_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int{R}(Y-X)_{+}{\left(\sigma_{\xi}p_{\xi}({\left[m_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}Y\right]})\right)}^{2}dY

\displaystyle\underset{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\int{R}(Y-X)_{+}\phi(Y)^{2}dY

i.e.

. This concludes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle X^{N}:=x_{1}^{N}x_{2}^{N}\dots x_{k}^{N},\qquad X^{-1}:=x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}\dots x_{k}^{-1}

A.1.3.
Partitions

The partitions of an integer

such that
{\left|\lambda\right|}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=n

\lambda\vdash n

{\left|\lambda\right|}=n

\ell(\lambda)
. The transpose of a partition will be denoted by
\lambda^{\prime}
. We refer to
[

A.1.5.
Plethystic sum and product

Recall that
p_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots):=\sum_{\ell\geqslant 1}x_{\ell}^{k}
. Given the sets of variables or “alphabets”
X:=\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k\geqslant 1}
and
Y:=\left\{y_{k}\right\}_{k\geqslant 1}
, one defines

\displaystyle p_{k}{\left[X+Y\right]}

\displaystyle=p_{k}{\left[X\right]}+p_{k}{\left[Y\right]}

\displaystyle p_{k}{\left[X\cdot Y\right]}

\displaystyle=p_{k}{\left[X\right]}p_{k}{\left[Y\right]}

Note the difference of convention between
p_{k}(X+Y)=\sum_{\ell}(x_{\ell}+y_{\ell})^{k}
and
p_{k}[X+Y]
.

With these definitions,
X+Y
is the alphabet obtained by concatenating
X
and
Y
and
X\cdot Y=\left\{x_{k}y_{\ell}\right\}_{k,\ell\geqslant 1}
is a “tensor product” alphabet. It is clear that
+
and
\cdot
are associative operations on the alphabets. From now on, we will forget the dot when considering a tensor alphabet and we will write
XY
for it ; we will also forget the
\left\{\cdot\right\}
for alphabets. For instance, we write
tX
for
\left\{t\right\}\cdot X
.

It clear that

The link betwen the

A.1.7.
Plethystic difference

The difference of two alphabets
X-Y
is defined as the formal alphabet such that
[

, 1-3, ex. 23]

\displaystyle H{\left[X-Y\right]}=\frac{H{\left[X\right]}}{H{\left[Y\right]}}

This last definition is equivalent to

\displaystyle p_{k}{\left[X-Y\right]}=p_{k}{\left[X\right]}-p_{k}{\left[Y\right]}

Note that

. To differentiate between these two operations, we define the “minus sign” alphabet

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=\left\{-1\right\}

(106)

which is such that

A.1.8.
Involution
\omega

The fundamental involution

A.1.9.

The Cauchy identity is

\displaystyle H{\left[XY\right]}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}{\left[X\right]}s_{\lambda}{\left[Y\right]}

Acting plethystically on

A.1.10.
Exponential alphabet

The alphabet
\mathcal{E}
is defined by setting

\displaystyle p_{k}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{k=1\right\}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad H{\left[t\mathcal{E}\right]}=e^{t}

(111)

Schur-Weyl duality gives
[
83
, I-7 (7.7) p. 114]

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\vdash{\left|\lambda\right|}}\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}\frac{p_{\mu}}{z_{\mu}}

For
\lambda\vdash n
, one has
p_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}=\prod_{k}p_{\lambda_{k}}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{\lambda=1^{n}\right\}}
, hence

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}=\sum_{\mu\vdash n}\chi^{\lambda}_{\mu}\frac{p_{\mu}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}}{z_{\mu}}=\frac{\chi^{\lambda}_{1^{n}}}{z_{1^{n}}}=\frac{\chi^{\lambda}({\operatorname{id}}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})}{n!}

Since
\chi^{\lambda}=\operatorname{tr}{\left(\rho^{\lambda}\right)}
with
\rho^{\lambda}:{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\rightarrow\operatorname{Mat}_{d_{\lambda}}(\mathbb{C})
(or
\textrm{End}(V^{\lambda})
with
V^{\lambda}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d_{\lambda}}
), the dimension
d_{\lambda}
of the irreducible module
V^{\lambda}
is given by
\chi^{\lambda}({\operatorname{id}}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})=\operatorname{tr}{\left(I_{d_{\lambda}}\right)}
, hence

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}={\left|\lambda\right|}!\,s_{\lambda}{\left[\mathcal{E}\right]}

(112)

A.1.11.
The Schur functions

The Schur functions have several equivalent definitions. One is for instance the Jacobi-Trudi identity

, I-3 (3.4)]

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\det{\left(h_{\lambda_{i+\ell-j}}\right)}_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant\ell},\quad\forall\,\ell\geqslant\ell(\lambda)

which is equivalent, with the simple manipulations of e.g.

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}{\left[X\right]}={\left[U^{\lambda}\right]}H{\left[XU-U^{\varepsilon R}\right]}

These functions satisfy the property of “restricted” orthogonality
[

]

[

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}s_{\lambda}(U)s_{\mu}(U^{-1})dU=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\ell(\lambda)\leqslant n\right\}}

(115)

where

is the normalised Haar measure of the unitary group

, whose diagonal part is given by the

measure

; see
[

The Schur functions are orthogonal for the Hall scalar product

\displaystyle\left\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}\right\rangle=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}

(116)

A.2.
The Schur measure and the Schur kernel

The Schur measure is defined in (
76
). The fact that it is a probability measure for a positive specialisation comes from the Cauchy identity (
108
). The law of the total parts of the measure is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}{\left(t^{{\left|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right|}}\right)}=H{\left[(t-1)\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\right]}

(117)

One has used the scaling property
s_{\lambda}{\left[tX\right]}=t^{\left|\lambda\right|}s_{\lambda}{\left[X\right]}
. In particular, for
\mathcal{A}=(p_{k})_{k}
and
\mathcal{B}=(q_{k})_{k}
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}{\left(t^{{\left|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right|}}\right)}=\prod_{i,j}H{\left[(t-1)p_{i}q_{j}\right]}=\prod_{i,j}\frac{1-p_{i}q_{j}}{1-tp_{i}q_{j}}=\prod_{i,j}\mathbb{E}{{\left(t^{\operatorname{Geom}(p_{i}q_{j})}\right)}}

hence the equality in law
{\left|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}\right|}=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Geom}(p_{i}q_{j})
with independent random variables in the RHS. Of course, since the only ingredients needed for such an equality in law are the scaling of the symmetric functions and the Cauchy identity, it is also valid for e.g. the Macdonald measure
[
23
]
, but with random variables different from the geometric ones.

The Schur kernel operator is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(a_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\widetilde{a}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}^{-1})

(118)

where the fundamental function
a\equiv a_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
is given by

\displaystyle a_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(z):=H_{\varepsilon}{\left[z\mathcal{A}-z^{-1}\mathcal{B}\right]}

(119)

with
H_{\mathcal{A}}{\left[X\right]}:=H{\left[\mathcal{A}X\right]}
,
\widetilde{f}(z):=f(z^{-1})
and
f^{-1}:=1/f
here.

Supposing that
a(z)\equiv a_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{a}_{k}z^{k}
, the Schur kernel can take the following forms :

•

Hankel convolutive form
[
10
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}[x,y]=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\widehat{a}_{k}(x)\widehat{a}_{k}(y),\qquad\widehat{a}_{k}(x):=\widehat{a}_{k+x}

(120)

•

Integral form
[
30
, (2.1), (2.2)]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\operatorname{Schur}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}[x,y]={\left[z^{x}w^{y}\right]}\frac{a(z)/a(w)}{z/w-1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\left|z/w\right|}>1\right\}}

(121)

•

Christoffel-Darboux form : see
[
30
, rk. 2]
for the expression.

B.1.
Definitions

We define the following
prolate hyperspheroidal wave function
operator

\displaystyle\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c}:=\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}+c^{2}(1-x^{2})-\frac{\rho^{2}}{x^{2}},\qquad\rho^{2}\equiv\rho_{N}^{2}:=N^{2}-\frac{1}{4}

(122)

This operator commutes in
L^{2}([0,1])
with the operator
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\!\!N,c}:L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])
with kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\!N,c}(x,y):=\sqrt{cxy}\,J_{N}(cxy),\qquad J_{m}(x):={\left[z^{m}\right]}e^{x(z-z^{-1})/2}

(123)

whose origin in optics comes, amongst others, from
[
69
,
70
,
118
]

“the modes in a maser interferometer with confocal spherical mirrors of circular cross section”.

The common eigenvectors of
\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c}
and
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\!\!N,c}
are called
prolate hyperspheroidal wave functions
and are denoted by
\psi_{k}^{(N,c)}
.

The operator
\operatorname{PHO}_{1/2,c}
is the more classical
prolate spheroidal wave function
operator (up to an additive constant) that commutes with the
sine kernel operator

{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\!{\operatorname{sinc}}}
with kernel
(x,y)\mapsto{\operatorname{sinc}}(\pi(x-y))
in
L^{2}([-1,1])

[
117
, (23), (26)]
; see Mehta
[
86
, ch. 6 & 18]
for a use of its eigenvectors in Random Matrix Theory and
[
105
, ch. 30 p. 697]
for properties of these functions. Note that the Bessel function satisfies
J_{1/2}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\sin(x)
, hence
\operatorname{PHO}_{1/2,c}
also commutes with the operator of kernel
(x,y)\mapsto\sin(cxy)
on
L^{2}([0,1])
which is equal, up to a symmetrisation that does not change the (real) eigenvectors and eigenvalues, to the square root of
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\!{\operatorname{sinc}}}
that has kernel
e^{icxy}
, see e.g.
[
86
, (6.3.17)]
.

The operator
\operatorname{PHO}_{N,0}
is a particular specialisation of an intertwinning of the Jacobi differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}+(\beta-\alpha-x(\alpha+\beta))\frac{d}{dx}
whose eigenfunctions are the Jacobi polynomials. More precisely, if
P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
are the Jacobi polynomials
[
120
, ch. IV]
, defining the odd Zernike polynomials by
[
114
, (16)]

\displaystyle T_{k}^{(N)}(x):=\tau_{k}^{(N)}x^{N+\frac{1}{2}}P_{k}^{(N,0)}(1-2x^{2}),\qquad\tau_{k}^{(N)}:=\sqrt{2(2k+N+1)}

(124)

one has

\displaystyle\operatorname{PHO}_{N,0}T_{k}^{(N)}=-\kappa_{k}^{(N)}T_{k}^{(N)},\qquad\kappa_{k}^{(N)}:=(N+2k+\tfrac{1}{2})(N+2k+\tfrac{3}{2})

The tri-band relation of orthogonal polynomials implies that the (semi-infinite) matrix of the endomorphism of multiplication by
X^{2}
in the basis of the
T_{k}^{(N)}
is tri-diagonal. Since the matrix of
\operatorname{PHO}_{N,0}
in this basis is diagonal and
\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c}=\operatorname{PHO}_{N,0}+c^{2}(1-X^{2})
, one concludes that the matrix of
\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c}
is tri-diagonal in the
(T_{k}^{(N)})_{k\geqslant 0}
basis (with explicit coefficients coming from the Jacobi polynomials). As a result, expanding the eigenvectors
(\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
in this basis according to

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}d_{\ell,k}^{\,(N,c)}T_{k}^{(N)},\qquad\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c}\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}=\lambda_{\ell}^{(N,c)}\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}

one obtains the semi-infinite matrix equation
[
114
, (30)]

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{M}}^{(N,c)}d_{\ell,\cdot}^{\,(N,c)}=\lambda_{\ell}^{(N,c)}d_{\ell,\cdot}^{\,(N,c)},\qquad{\boldsymbol{M}}^{(N,c)}:=\mathrm{Mat}_{(T_{k}^{(N)})_{k}}(\operatorname{PHO}_{N,c})

In the same vein as for the prolate spheroidal wave functions, one can expand
\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}
and
\lambda_{\ell}^{(N,c)}
into powers of
c
; see e.g.
[

]
.

B.2.
Rescaling

Lemma B.1

(Limiting hyperspheroidal operators)
.

Set

\displaystyle x:=1-\frac{X}{2}N^{-2/3},\qquad y:=1-\frac{Y}{2}N^{-2/3},\qquad c:=2N-sN^{1/3}

Then, locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle\frac{N^{1/6}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathcal{J}_{2N,2N-sN^{1/3}}(1-XN^{1/3},1-YN^{1/3})\underset{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}{\operatorname{Ai}}(X+Y+s)

and, on test functions that are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with exponential decay at infinity (say)

\displaystyle\frac{N^{-2/3}}{4}\operatorname{PHO}_{N,N-sN^{1/3}/2}\underset{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\mathcal{L}_{TW,s}:=\frac{d}{dX}X\frac{d}{dX}-X(X+s)

As a result, the eigenvalues
\Lambda_{k}^{(N,N-sN^{1/3}/2)}
of
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\!N,N-sN^{1/3}/2}
satisfy

\displaystyle N^{-1/6}\Lambda_{k}^{(N,N-sN^{1/3}/2)}\underset{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\lambda_{k}(s)

where
\lambda_{k}(s)
is the
k
-th eigenvalue of
{\boldsymbol{H}}({\operatorname{Ai}}_{s})
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Remark B.2
.

If one defines

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{N,c}(X,Y)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{J}_{N,c}(e^{-X},e^{-Y})e^{-(X+Y)/2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{N,c}(X)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{c}\,e^{-X}J_{N}(c\,e^{-X}),

\displaystyle\Psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}(X)

\displaystyle:=\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}(e^{-X})e^{-X/2}

and the associated operator
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{\!N,c}
acting on

with kernel

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{\!N,c}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{H}}(\widetilde{J}_{N,c})\curvearrowright L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{\!N,c}\Psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\ell}^{(N,c)}\Psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}

and lemma
B.1
can be rephrased into the weak convergence of operators of the Hankel type in

given by the convergence of their symbol. This convergence can be strengthen into a strong convergence depending on the functional space in which belongs the symbol, see e.g.
[

[

The operator

is thus, in a certain sense, more natural than the original Heurtley-Slepian operator

(since one remains in the framework of Hankel operators on

). The eigenvectors of this operator are the
\psi_{\ell}^{(N,c)}(e^{-x})e^{-x/2}
, which is somewhat similar to the exponential change of variable of Whittaker functions
[
23
, § 4]
/
[
62
,
63
]
. The commuting differential operator to
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}_{\!N,c}
on

is easily proven to be

\displaystyle\widetilde{\operatorname{PHO}}_{N,c}:=\frac{d}{dx}{\left(e^{2x}-1\right)}\frac{d}{dx}+2{\left(\rho^{2}-1\right)}e^{2x}+c^{2}e^{-2x},\qquad\rho^{2}:=N^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}

Proof.

Using the limit

76
, (4.39)]
/
[

\displaystyle\nu^{1/3}J_{2\nu}(2\nu-X\nu^{1/3})\underset{\nu\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}{\operatorname{Ai}}(X)

The second claim comes from the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{N^{-2/3}}{4}\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}
