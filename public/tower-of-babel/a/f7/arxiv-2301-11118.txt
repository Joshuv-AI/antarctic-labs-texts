Abstract
. OWL ontologies, whose formal semantics are rooted in Description Logic (DL), have been widely used for knowledge representation. Similar to Knowledge Graphs (KGs), ontologies are often incomplete, and maintaining and constructing them has proved challenging. While classical deductive reasoning algorithms use the precise formal semantics of an ontology to predict missing facts, recent years have witnessed growing interest in inductive reasoning techniques that can derive probable facts from an ontology. Similar to KGs, a promising approach is to learn ontology embeddings in a latent vector space, while additionally ensuring they adhere to the semantics of the underlying DL. While a variety of approaches have been proposed, current ontology embedding methods suffer from several shortcomings, especially that they all fail to faithfully model one-to-many, many-to-one, and many-to-many relations and role inclusion axioms. To address this problem and improve ontology completion performance, we propose a novel ontology embedding method named Box 2 {}^{\textsf{2}} EL for the DL ℰ ​ ℒ + ⁣ + \mathcal{EL^{++}} , which represents both concepts and roles as boxes (i.e., axis-aligned hyperrectangles), and models inter-concept relationships using a bumping mechanism. We theoretically prove the soundness of Box 2 {}^{\textsf{2}} EL and conduct an extensive experimental evaluation, achieving state-of-the-art results across a variety of datasets on the tasks of subsumption prediction, role assertion prediction, and approximating deductive reasoning. 1 1 1 Code and data are available at https://github.com/KRR-Oxford/BoxSquaredEL .



1.
Introduction

Ontologies are a widely used formalism to represent general knowledge about a domain
(
Staab and Studer 2010
)
. They are usually specified in the Web Ontology Language (OWL)
(
Grau et al. 2008
)
standard developed by W3C
2
2

2

https://www.w3.org/OWL/
, and have been widely adopted in many domains such as the Semantic Web
(
Héder 2014
)
, healthcare
(
Schulz et al. 2009
)
, bioinformatics
(
Hoehndorf et al. 2011
)
, and geoinformatics
(
Zhai et al. 2010
)
. OWL allows for the expression of a variety of statements, ranging from simple relational facts to specifying concept hierarchies and complex logical relationships, and is underpinned by Description Logic (DL)
(
Baader et al. 2005c
)
to define its formal semantics.

Although many real-world OWL ontologies have been developed and used with great success, such as the Gene Ontology GO
(
Ashburner et al. 2000
)
and the food ontology FoodOn
(
Dooley et al. 2018
)
, both maintaining these existing OWL ontologies and creating new ontologies has proved challenging and relies mostly on manual labor carried out by experts. Common ontology curation tasks include completing missing subsumptions between concepts (or membership relations between individuals and concepts) and identifying missing logical restrictions between concepts.
Symbolic logical reasoning algorithms such as HermiT
(
Glimm et al. 2014
)
and ELK
(
Kazakov et al. 2014
)
help address this problem by deductively inferring implicit knowledge from the precise semantics of an ontology, but this classical reasoning is often too rigid for real-world OWL ontologies —  especially in the presence of incomplete or noisy data —  and cannot derive knowledge that is only
probable
from the given ontology.

At the same time, there has been growing interest in representation learning-based methods for completing Knowledge Graphs (KGs)
(
Hogan et al. 2021
)
, i.e. relational facts in the form of RDF
3
3

3

Resource Description Framework.
https://www.w3.org/RDF/
.
triples <Subject, Predicate, Object>. Most of these approaches first learn structure-preserving
embeddings
of the entities and relations (predicates) of a KG in a latent vector space and then use them to score the likelihood of novel facts
(
Wang et al. 2017
)
.
For example, the classic method TransE
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
maps entities and relations to vectors such that translating the subject embedding by the relation embedding approximately yields the object embedding.

Similar embedding-based techniques as for KGs have been developed for inductive reasoning in ontologies, which promises to complement classical deductive reasoning for ontology curation tasks.
Some approaches such as OPA2Vec
(
Smaili et al. 2019
)
and OWL2Vec*
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
rely on exploiting textual meta information (e.g., concept labels and comments) to model similarities between entities, but do not retain the semantics defined by the underlying DL. Other approaches aim to directly embed the logical information of an OWL ontology in the latent space
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
;
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Peng et al. 2022
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
, mostly targeting the OWL 2 EL profile
(
Krötzsch 2012
)
, whose semantics are defined according to the DL
\mathcal{EL^{++}}

(
Baader et al. 2005a
)
. Prominent examples include
ELEm
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
)
and its extension EmEL
++
(
Mondal et al. 2021
)
, which model concepts as high-dimensional balls, but fail to faithfully capture concept conjunction, since the intersection of two balls is no longer a ball. This led to the development of the state-of-the-art methods BoxEL
(
Xiong et al. 2022
)
and ELBE
(
Peng et al. 2022
)
, which instead represent concepts as boxes (i.e., axis-aligned hyperrectangles).
However, all of these approaches still rely on TransE
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
to model roles (i.e., binary relations) as simple translations, which is unable to capture one-to-many, many-to-one, or many-to-many relationships
(
Wang et al. 2014
;
Lin et al. 2015
)
, and is limited in its ability to faithfully represent inclusion relationships between roles.
Furthermore, these current works focus on the basic task of predicting subsumptions between named concepts, without considering complex concepts defined with logical operators or complex logical relationships in evaluation.

In this paper, we propose
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
, a novel OWL ontology embedding method targeting the semantics of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
, which has been widely adopted in many real-life large-scale ontologies
(
Smith et al. 2007
;
Hoehndorf et al. 2011
)
.
4
4

4

The logical constructors provided by
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
are very common. While some OWL ontologies use more complicated features not supported by
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
,
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
can still be used in that case to model the subset of axioms that fall into
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
.
To address the aforementioned limitations of existing approaches, we instead draw inspiration from BoxE
(
Abboud et al. 2020
)
and represent both relations and concepts as boxes, while modeling interactions between concepts via a bumping mechanism.
We not only demonstrate how
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
overcomes the shortcomings of previous methods, but also prove that it is
sound
, i.e., faithfully captures the semantics of the underlying DL, which shows its theoretical correctness and supports interpretable inference for ontology completion. We evaluate our method in the two different inductive reasoning settings of concept subsumption prediction —  involving both named and complex concepts —  and role assertion (link) prediction, and on approximating deductive reasoning. Our results demonstrate that the theoretical advantages of our approach manifest themselves in practice and lead to state-of-the-art performance across a variety of datasets.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Description Logic Ontologies

A DL ontology
\mathcal{O}
describes some domain of interest in terms of individuals, concepts and roles, where individuals correspond to objects in the domain, concepts represent sets of objects, and roles are binary relations between objects. We limit our discussion to the DL
\mathcal{EL^{++}}

(
Baader et al. 2005a
)
, which underpins the OWL 2 EL profile
(
Krötzsch 2012
)
. It is widely adopted since it contains many useful and important knowledge representation features, while allowing for reasoning in polynomial time.
Given sets
\mathcal{N}_{I}
,
\mathcal{N}_{C}
and
\mathcal{N}_{R}
of, respectively, individual, concept, and role names,
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
concepts are recursively defined as

\top\;|\;\bot\;|\;A\;|\;C\sqcap D\;|\;\exists r.C\;|\;\{a\}

where
\top
is the top concept,
\bot
is the bottom concept,
A\in\mathcal{N}_{C}
is an atomic (or
named
) concept,
r\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
is an atomic role,
a\in\mathcal{N}_{I}
is an individual, and
C
and
D
are themselves (possibly complex)
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
concepts. We say a concept is
complex
when it is constructed with a logical operator such as
\sqcap
or
\exists
.
An
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
ontology
\mathcal{O}
consists of a TBox
\mathcal{T}
and an ABox
\mathcal{A}
. The TBox consists of logical background knowledge in the form of concept subsumption axioms
C\sqsubseteq D
and role inclusion axioms
r_{1}\circ\cdots\circ r_{k}\sqsubseteq r
, while the ABox contains concrete data in the form of concept and role assertion axioms
C(a)
and
r(a,b)
.
Note that a relational fact from a KG in the form of an RDF triple
(a,r,b)
is equivalent to a role assertion axiom in the form of
r(a,b)
, and thus ontologies can be seen as extending KGs with more complex conceptual and logical information.

Example 0.

The following ontology models a simple family domain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\{

\displaystyle\mathsf{Father}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{Parent}\sqcap\mathsf{Male},\;\mathsf{Mother}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{Parent}\sqcap\mathsf{Female},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Child}\sqsubseteq\exists\mathsf{hasParent}.\mathsf{Father},\;\mathsf{Child}\sqsubseteq\exists\mathsf{hasParent}.\mathsf{Mother},

\displaystyle\mathsf{hasParent}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{relatedTo}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{

\displaystyle\mathsf{Father}(\mathsf{Alex}),\mathsf{Child}(\mathsf{Bob}),\mathsf{hasParent}(\mathsf{Bob},\mathsf{Alex})\}

The TBox specifies that a father is a male parent, a mother is a female parent, every child has a father and a mother, and having a parent implies being related to that parent; the ABox states that Alex is a father, Bob is a child, and Alex is a parent of Bob.

Similarly to first order logic, the semantics of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
are defined in terms of
interpretations
that map individuals to elements, concept names to subsets, and role names to binary relations over some set called the
interpretation domain
. An interpretation
\mathcal{I}
that satisfies the semantics of every axiom in
\mathcal{O}
is called a
model
of
\mathcal{O}
, denoted as
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{O}
. See Appendix A for a formal discussion of the semantics.

Remark.

\mathcal{EL^{++}}
also allows for so-called
concrete domains
(a.k.a. datatypes and values), which we do not consider in this paper. Technically, we work on the
\mathcal{ELHO}(\circ)^{\bot}
subset of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
.

2.2.
Subsumption Inference

A central problem in DLs is to infer concept subsumptions from an ontology
\mathcal{O}
. In general, these subsumptions can involve both named and complex concepts.
Classical reasoning algorithms leverage the logical information in
\mathcal{O}
to derive subsumptions that logically follow from the semantics; for example, we can infer from the ontology in
Example

1
that
\mathsf{Child}\sqsubseteq\exists\mathsf{relatedTo}.\mathsf{Father}
. In contrast,
inductive
reasoning (also called
prediction
) aims to infer
probable
subsumptions from
\mathcal{O}
. Note that when we limit ourselves to predicting subsumptions of the form
\{a\}\sqsubseteq\exists r.\{b\}
, which are equivalent to role assertion axioms
r(a,b)
, this is identical to the problem of
link prediction
in KGs (i.e., KG completion).

The majority of the existing prediction methods focus only on subsumptions between named concepts. Some approaches embed the formal semantics defined by the DL, while others focus on utilizing textual information such as concept labels and comments.
For the former, please see
Section

2.4
.
For the latter, OPA2Vec
(
Smaili et al. 2019
)
and OWL2Vec*
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
use a Word2Vec model trained with local graph structure augmented corpora to embed the text for predicting subsumptions, while the recent method BERTSubs
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
fine-tunes a BERT model together with an attached classifier for predicting subsumptions involving both named concepts and complex concepts.
Although these works consider a small part of the formal semantics such as the concept hierarchy as the context of a concept for augmenting prediction, they do not model the (complete) logical relationships of the ontology in the vector space.
They are complementary to semantic ontology embedding methods including
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
, but jointly embedding DL semantics and textual information is out of the scope of this paper.

2.3.
Knowledge Graph Embeddings

KG embedding models such as TransE
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
, DistMult
(
Yang et al. 2015
)
, ComplEx
(
Trouillon et al. 2016
)
and BoxE
(
Abboud et al. 2020
)
aim to solve the problem of completing KGs composed of purely relational facts, and can be thought of as modeling only the role assertion part of the ABox of an OWL ontology
(
Wang et al. 2017
)
.
In particular, BoxE
(
Abboud et al. 2020
)
also represents relations as boxes and adopts bump vectors, but it models relational facts alone, whereas we aim at much more complex DL ontologies with logical relationships involving concepts and roles.

Some KG embedding methods take background knowledge into account and are therefore related to ontology embedding techniques. However, these methods still focus only on modeling relational facts in a KG, using the background knowledge as constraints, and most of them only support logical rules concerning relations
(
Rocktäschel et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Guo et al. 2016
;
Nayyeri et al. 2020
;
Nayyeri et al. 2021
)
or schemas in simple languages like RDF Schema
(
Hao et al. 2019
;
Xiang et al. 2021
)
.
In contrast, ontology embedding methods including
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
focus on OWL ontologies, which contain a large quantity of conceptual knowledge in the form of subsumptions and logical relationships. Furthermore, these methods can only be applied in the setting of link prediction, whereas ontology embeddings also allow predicting novel conceptual information or logical background knowledge itself.

2.4.
Semantic Ontology Embeddings

Several ontology embedding methods for DL semantics have been proposed by learning geometric models.
ELEm
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
)
is among the first to embed
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
, and EmEL
++
(
Mondal et al. 2021
)
extends ELEm by considering role inclusion axioms.
However, both methods represent concepts as high-dimensional balls, which have the disadvantage of not being closed under intersection.
Our concept representation based on boxes has previously been used in the two recent methods BoxEL
(
Xiong et al. 2022
)
and ELBE
(
Peng et al. 2022
)
.
Mondal et al. 2021
is the only other technique we are aware of that also models role inclusion axioms; the other methods consider only a smaller subset of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
. All previous methods simply model roles (binary relations) by a single vector-based translation as in TransE
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
, which fails to faithfully capture one-to-many, many-to-one, and many-to-many relations
(
Wang et al. 2014
;
Lin et al. 2015
)
.
We use box-based modeling in combination with bump vectors to address the above problem, achieving better performance in ontology completion.

Going beyond
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
,
Özçep et al. 2020
introduce a cone-based model for the more expressive DL
\mathcal{ALC}
; however, their contribution is mainly theoretical since they provide neither an implementation nor an evaluation. Embed2Reason
(
Garg et al. 2019
)
is another embedding approach for
\mathcal{ALC}
ontologies based on quantum logic
(
Birkhoff and Von Neumann 1936
)
. In contrast to our work, its focus is on ABox instead of subsumption reasoning.
Finally, it is worth mentioning that in comparison with all these DL embedding works, we conduct a more thorough evaluation by considering predicting subsumptions between not only named concepts, but also named concepts and complex concepts involving logical operators.

3.
Method

In order to perform inductive reasoning over an
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
ontology
\mathcal{O}
with signature
\Sigma=(\mathcal{N}_{C},\mathcal{N}_{R},\mathcal{N}_{I})
, we follow the general framework of
Kulmanov et al. 2019
and learn embeddings that correspond to
geometric models
of
\mathcal{O}
; that is, (logical) models with interpretation domain
\Delta=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We now specify how
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
maps concepts, individuals, and roles to the embedding space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and describe the loss functions that encode the axioms of
\mathcal{O}
.

3.1.
Geometric Representation

Concepts and individuals.

We follow recent work
(
Peng et al. 2022
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
and represent concepts as
n
-dimensional
boxes
, i.e.,
axis-aligned hyperrectangles
. This representation has several advantages over the alternative based on
n
-dimensional
balls
, such as closure under concept intersection, and has been shown to work well in practice. Formally, we associate with every concept
C\in\mathcal{N}_{C}
two vectors
\bm{l}_{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\bm{u}_{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\bm{l}_{C}\leq\bm{u}_{C}
, where
\leq
is applied element-wise. These vectors form the
lower
and
upper
corner of the box of
C
, i.e.,
\textnormal{Box}(C)=\{\,\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\bm{l}_{C}\leq\bm{x}\leq\bm{u}_{C}\,\}
. The
center
of
\textnormal{Box}(C)
is given by
(\bm{l}_{C}+\bm{u}_{C})/2
, and its
offset
is
(\bm{u}_{C}-\bm{l}_{C})/2
.

We represent individuals
a\in\mathcal{N}_{I}
as
points

\bm{e}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the embedding space. For notational convenience, nominals (e.g.,
\{a\}
) are then formally mapped to boxes with volume 0, i.e.,
\bm{l}_{\{a\}}=\bm{u}_{\{a\}}=\bm{e}_{a}
.

Roles.

While most existing
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
embedding models represent roles (binary relations) via simple translations in the form of TransE
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
, we instead follow the idea of the BoxE KG embedding model
(
Abboud et al. 2020
)
.
That is, we associate every role
r\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
with a
head box

\textnormal{Head}(r)
and a
tail box

\textnormal{Tail}(r)
. Intuitively, every point in the head box is related via
r
to every point in the tail box. This representation is made more expressive by introducing translational bump vectors
\textnormal{Bump}(C)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for every atomic concept
C
, which model interactions between related concepts by dynamically translating their embeddings. An axiom of the form
C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D
is then considered to hold if

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textnormal{Box}(C)\oplus\textnormal{Bump}(D)&\subseteq\textnormal{Head}(r)\quad\text{and}\\
\textnormal{Box}(D)\oplus\textnormal{Bump}(C)&\subseteq\textnormal{Tail}(r),\end{split}

where
\oplus
denotes the operation of translating a box along a bump vector, i.e.,
\textnormal{Box}(C)\oplus\textnormal{Bump}(D)=\{\,\bm{x}+\textnormal{Bump}(D)\mid\bm{x}\in\textnormal{Box}(C)\,\}
.

1
illustrates 2-dimensional
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
embeddings that form a logical model of the TBox in
Example

1
, since, e.g.,
\textnormal{Box}(\mathsf{Father})\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(\mathsf{Parent})\cap\textnormal{Box}(\mathsf{Male})
and
Equation

1
holds for all relevant axioms. We will formalise the logical geometric model associated with a set of
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
embeddings in
Theorem

2
. Note also that the embeddings furthermore imply the subsumption
\mathsf{Child}\sqsubseteq\exists\mathsf{relatedTo}.\mathsf{Father}
(again by
Equation

1
), illustrating their utility for approximate reasoning.

Expressiveness.

The previous example illustrates the expressive power of
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
. Representing roles as head and tail boxes allows modeling one-to-many relationships such as
hasParent
faithfully, in contrast to previous approaches that employ a TransE-based role representation. Moreover, role inclusion axioms such as
\mathsf{hasParent}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{relatedTo}
can be represented naturally via inclusion constraints on the relevant head and tail boxes. In contrast, previous methods either do not consider role inclusion axioms at all or only rudimentarily approximate them by forcing the embeddings of the involved roles to be similar
(
Mondal et al. 2021
)
.

Model complexity.

Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
requires
2n|\mathcal{N}_{C}|+n|\mathcal{N}_{I}|
parameters to store the lower and upper corners of the box embeddings of concepts and points associated with individuals. In order to represent the head and tail boxes for every relation and a bump vector per concept and individual, the model needs
4n|\mathcal{N}_{R}|+n(|\mathcal{N}_{C}|+|\mathcal{N}_{I}|)
additional parameters. Therefore, the total space complexity of
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
is in
O\Bigl(n\bigl(3|\mathcal{N}_{C}|+2|\mathcal{N}_{I}|+4|\mathcal{N}_{R}|\bigr)\Bigr)
.

3.2.
Training Procedure

In order to learn embeddings for
\mathcal{O}
, we first convert its ABox axioms into equivalent TBox axioms using the transformation rules

\displaystyle C(a)\;

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow\;\{a\}\sqsubseteq C

\displaystyle r(a,b)\;

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow\;\{a\}\sqsubseteq\exists r.\{b\}

and then normalize the axioms using the standard procedure in
(
Baader et al. 2005a
;
Baader et al. 2005b
)
. This results in a normalized ontology in which all axioms are in one of the normal forms described below. Crucially, this procedure is semantics-preserving: every model of the normalized ontology is also a model of the original ontology
(
Baader et al. 2005a
)
. For more details on normalization, see Appendix B. We introduce separate loss functions for axioms in each normal form, which intuitively ensure that the learned embeddings adhere to the semantics of
\mathcal{O}
. Finally, we minimize the sum of all loss terms
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})
via mini-batch gradient descent.

Our loss functions are based on the distance-based loss formulation in
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
;
Peng et al. 2022
)

and aim to minimize the element-wise distance between the embeddings of related concepts. Given two arbitrary boxes
A
and
B
, this element-wise distance is computed as

\bm{d}(A,B)=\left\lvert\bm{c}(A)-\bm{c}(B)\right\rvert-\bm{o}(A)-\bm{o}(B),

where
\bm{c}(\cdot)
and
\bm{o}(\cdot)
denote the center and offset of a box, respectively. Note that for nominals
\{a\}
we have that
\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(\{a\}))=\bm{e}_{a}
and
\bm{o}(\textnormal{Box}(\{a\}))=\bm{0}
.

Generic inclusion loss.

Before defining loss functions for the different
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
normal forms, we first introduce a generic inclusion loss
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)
. It encourages box
A
to be contained in box
B
and is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=\begin{cases}\left\lVert\max\{\bm{0},\,\bm{d}(A,B)+2\bm{o}(A)-\gamma\}\right\rVert&\text{if }B\neq\emptyset\\
\max\{0,\bm{o}(A)_{1}+1\}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is a margin hyperparameter. If
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=0
, either
A
lies within
\gamma
-distance of
B
in each dimension, or both
A
and
B
are empty.

We next introduce each normal form and the corresponding loss function.
Note that all concepts in the normal forms below are atomic concepts or nominals (and not complex).

First normal form (NF1).

For an NF1 axiom of the form
C\sqsubseteq D
, the learned embeddings need to satisfy
\textnormal{Box}(C)\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(D)
, corresponding to the semantics of concept inclusion. Therefore, we define the loss for NF1 as simply the inclusion loss:

\mathcal{L}_{1}(C,D)=\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(C),\textnormal{Box}(D)).

Second normal form (NF2).

For an NF2 axiom of the form
C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq E
, we similarly require that the intersection of the boxes of
C
and
D
is within the box of
E
. The intersection of
\textnormal{Box}(C)
and
\textnormal{Box}(D)
is itself a box with lower corner
\max\{\bm{l}_{C},\bm{l}_{D}\}
and upper corner
\min\{\bm{u}_{C},\bm{u}_{D}\}
, where max and min are applied element-wise. We thus have

\mathcal{L}_{2}(C,D,E)=\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}\Bigl(\textnormal{Box}(C)\cap\textnormal{Box}(D),\textnormal{Box}(E)\Bigr).

However, this formulation is problematic since it can be easily minimized to 0 by setting
\textnormal{Box}(C)
and
\textnormal{Box}(D)
to be disjoint. While disjoint embeddings for
C
and
D
would technically not violate the semantics, usually an axiom of the form
C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq\bot
would have been used directly if it had been the intention that
C
and
D
should be disjoint. Therefore, we add the additional term

\left\lVert\max\left\{\bm{0},\,\max\{\bm{l}_{C},\bm{l}_{D}\}-\min\{\bm{u}_{C},\bm{u}_{D}\}\right\}\right\rVert

to the loss, which intuitively encourages
\textnormal{Box}(C)\cap\textnormal{Box}(D)
to be non-empty by making all elements of its offset vector positive.

Third normal form (NF3).

For an NF3 axiom of the form
C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D
, the embeddings should satisfy
\textnormal{Box}(C)+\textnormal{Bump}(D)\subseteq\textnormal{Head}(r)
and
\textnormal{Box}(D)+\textnormal{Bump}(C)\subseteq\textnormal{Tail}(r)
. This is captured by the following loss function:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{3}(C,r,D)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(C)+\textnormal{Bump}(D),\textnormal{Head}(r))\\
+\,\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(D)+\textnormal{Bump}(C),\textnormal{Tail}(r))\Bigr).\end{split}

is empty, we furthermore add the term
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(C),\emptyset)
to also make
\textnormal{Box}(C)

Fourth normal form (NF4).

For an NF4 axiom of the form
\exists r.C\sqsubseteq D
, we need to ensure that all points in the embedding space that are connected to
C
via role
r
are contained in
\textnormal{Box}(D)
. It can be seen from our geometric representation that the set of these points is contained in the set
\textnormal{Head}(r)-\textnormal{Bump}(C)
. We therefore define the loss for the fourth normal form as

\mathcal{L}_{4}(r,C,D)=\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Head}(r)-\textnormal{Bump}(C),\textnormal{Box}(D)).

Fifth normal form (NF5).

Axioms of the fifth normal form
C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq\bot
state that concepts
C
and
D
are disjoint. Our corresponding loss function penalizes embeddings for which the element-wise distance is not greater than 0 (within a margin of
\gamma
) and is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{5}(C,D)=\left\lVert\max\{\bm{0},\,-(\bm{d}(\textnormal{Box}(C),\textnormal{Box}(D))+\gamma)\}\right\rVert.

Role inclusion axioms.

After normalization, role inclusion axioms are either of the form
r\sqsubseteq s
or
r_{1}\circ r_{2}\sqsubseteq s
. For the first case, we define the loss function

\mathcal{L}_{6}(r,s)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Head}(r),\textnormal{Head}(s))\\
+\,\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Tail}(r),\textnormal{Tail}(s))\Bigr),

which intuitively makes any embeddings related via role
r
also related via
s
. Similarly, in the second case we have the loss

\mathcal{L}_{7}(r_{1},r_{2},s)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Head}(r_{1}),\textnormal{Head}(s))\\
+\,\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Tail}(r_{2}),\textnormal{Tail}(s))\Bigr).

Regularization.

In order to prevent our expressive role representation from overfitting, we furthermore
L^{2}
-regularize the bump vectors by adding the regularization term

\lambda\sum_{C\in\mathcal{N}_{C}\cup\mathcal{N}_{I}}\left\lVert\textnormal{Bump}(C)\right\rVert,

where
\lambda
is a hyperparameter.

Negative sampling.

In addition to the above loss functions, we also employ
negative sampling
to further improve the quality of the learned embeddings. We follow previous work
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
;
Peng et al. 2022
)
and generate negative samples for axioms of the form
C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D
by replacing either
C
or
D
with a randomly selected different concept, similar to negative sampling in KGs
(
Bordes et al. 2013
)
. For every NF3 axiom we generate a new set of
\omega\geq 1
negative samples
C\not\sqsubseteq\exists r.D
in every epoch, for which we optimize the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\not\sqsubseteq}(C,r,D)={}

\displaystyle\left(\delta-\mu(\textnormal{Box}(C)+\textnormal{Bump}(D),\,\textnormal{Head}(r))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle{}+{}

\displaystyle\left(\delta-\mu(\textnormal{Box}(D)+\textnormal{Bump}(C),\,\textnormal{Tail}(r))\right)^{2},

where
\mu(A,B)=\left\lVert\max\{\bm{0},\,\bm{d}(A,B)+\gamma\}\right\rVert
is the minimal distance between any two points in
A
and
B
and
\delta>0
is a hyperparameter that controls how unlikely the negative samples are made by the model.

As in the case of KGs, the above procedure may occasionally generate false negative samples, i.e., negative axioms that actually do follow from
\mathcal{O}
. However, on average it will predominantly produce true negatives, which we find to empirically improve the learned embeddings.

3.3.
Soundness

We now show that the embeddings learned by
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
indeed correspond to a logical geometric model of the given ontology
\mathcal{O}
.

Theorem 2 (Soundness).

Let
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
be an
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
ontology. If
\gamma\leq 0
and there exist
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
embeddings in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})=0
, then these embeddings are a model of
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof sketch.

The proof relies on the correctness of our loss functions, i.e., if
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=0
then
A\subseteq B
. We first construct a geometric interpretation
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{O}
from the embeddings by interpreting every individual as its associated point, every concept as the set of points in its associated box, and every role as
the Cartesian product of its head box and its tail box. Since the loss for every axiom is 0, the embeddings satisfy the corresponding semantics. We thus have that
\mathcal{I}
is a model of
\mathcal{O}
.
∎

A formal proof of
Theorem

2
is given in Appendix C. While our optimization procedure might not achieve a loss of 0 in practice, the importance of
Theorem

2
is that it demonstrates that the learned embeddings converge to a semantically meaningful representation of the ontology in which all of its axioms are satisfied, i.e., a model of
\mathcal{O}
. The embeddings therefore indeed encode the axioms in
\mathcal{O}
and are thus useful to perform inductive or approximate reasoning.

4.2.
General Subsumption Prediction

We next evaluate
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
on general subsumption prediction for inductive reasoning. In contrast to previous work
(
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
, we not only consider subsumptions between atomic (named) concepts, but also the more challenging task of predicting subsumptions between atomic concepts and complex concepts.

Benchmark.

We introduce a modification of the benchmark based on three biomedical ontologies GALEN
(
Rector et al. 1996
)
, Gene Ontology (GO)
(
Ashburner et al. 2000
)
and Anatomy (a.k.a. Uberon)
(
Mungall et al. 2012
)
, that has been considered in previous DL embedding works
(
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
. For each ontology, our benchmark consists of axioms split into training (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) sets for each normal form. This enables us to evaluate ontology embedding models on subsumption prediction between atomic concepts (NF1), atomic concepts and conjunctions (NF2), and atomic concepts and existential restrictions (NF3 and NF4). We report statistics on the sizes of these ontologies, including the numbers of axioms of different forms, in Table 7 in the appendix.

Baselines.

We compare
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
with the state-of-the-art ontology embedding methods ELEm
(
Kulmanov et al. 2019
)
, EmEL
++

(
Mondal et al. 2021
)
, BoxEL
(
Xiong et al. 2022
)
, and ELBE
(
Peng et al. 2022
)
. We do not consider any traditional KG embedding methods in our experiments, since they have been shown to be considerably outperformed by ontology embedding methods
(
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
and are not applicable in the setting of complex concepts.

Evaluation protocol.

To evaluate the subsumption prediction performance, we follow the literature
(
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
and report a variety of ranking-based metrics on the testing set. Given a test axiom in some normal form, we generate a set of candidate predictions by replacing the atomic side of the subsumption with all the atomic concepts in
\mathcal{N}_{C}
. We then rank all candidate predictions by a score based on the distance between the embeddings of the concepts of the subsumption (for details see Appendix F) and record the rank of the true axiom. We report the standard metrics Hits@
k
(H@
k
), where
k\in\{1,10,100\}
, the median rank (Med), the mean reciprocal rank (MRR), the mean rank (MR), and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). These metrics are computed for the axioms in each normal form individually, as well as combined across normal forms. See Appendix G for definitions of the metrics.

We finally investigate how well our model can approximate

We again consider the GALEN, GO, and Anatomy ontologies. Instead of splitting the axioms into separate training, validation, and testing sets, we now train the models on the entire ontology using all of its asserted axioms. For evaluation, we use an

Deductive vs inductive reasoning.

Comparing the results in
Tables

To see why, note that the learned embeddings are used to make purely statistical predictions about missing axioms. The soundness of our method guarantees that these predictions align with the semantics of the ontology. However, we do not explicitly perform logical inference steps in the embedding space, as would be required to derive logical inferences similar to a deductive reasoning algorithm. We illustrate this difference with a concrete example in Appendix I. While embedding methods can thus be useful to approximate deductive reasoning, the two approaches are best used in conjunction in order to combine formal derivations with inductive and probable knowledge.

Conclusion

We developed
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
, a novel OWL ontology embedding method that adopts box-based representations for both concepts and roles. This representation is able to model complex logical constructs from
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
and overcomes the limitations of previous approaches in representing roles and role inclusion axioms.
We formally proved that our method is sound, i.e., correctly represents the semantics of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
, and performed an extensive empirical evaluation, achieving state-of-the-art results in concept subsumption prediction, role assertion prediction, and approximating deductive reasoning.

Appendix A
Semantics of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}

The semantics of
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
are defined in terms of interpretations. An
interpretation
is a tuple
\mathcal{I}=(\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},\cdot^{\mathcal{I}})
of a non-empty set
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
, the
interpretation domain
, and a mapping function
\cdot^{\mathcal{I}}\colon N_{C}\cup N_{I}\cup N_{R}\to\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
, that maps

•

individuals
a\in\mathcal{N}_{I}
to objects
a^{\mathcal{I}}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
;

•

concept names
C\in\mathcal{N}_{C}
to subsets
C^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
; and

•

role names
r\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
to binary relations
r^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\times\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
.

The mapping
\cdot^{\mathcal{I}}
is extended to arbitrary concepts as follows:

\displaystyle\top^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle\bot^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\{a\}^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=\{a^{\mathcal{I}}\},

\displaystyle(C\sqcap D)^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=C^{\mathcal{I}}\cap D^{\mathcal{I}},

\displaystyle(\exists r.C)^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=\{\,x\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\mid\exists y\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}.\,(x,y)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}\land y\in C^{\mathcal{I}}\,\}.

An interpretation thus establishes a precise correspondence between syntactical
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
concepts and sets of objects in the interpretation domain. An interpretation
\mathcal{I}

satisfies
a concept subsumption axiom
C\sqsubseteq D
if
C^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq D^{\mathcal{I}}
and a role inclusion axiom
r_{1}\circ\dots\circ r_{k}\sqsubseteq r
if
r_{1}^{\mathcal{I}};\,\dots;\,r_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}\sqsubseteq r^{\mathcal{I}}
, where
;
denotes relation composition. Furthermore,
\mathcal{I}
satisfies a concept assertion
C(a)
if
a^{\mathcal{I}}\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
and a role assertion
r(a,b)
if
(a^{\mathcal{I}},b^{\mathcal{I}})\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
.
An interpretation that satisfies each axiom and assertion in an ontology
\mathcal{O}
is called a
model
of
\mathcal{O}
, denoted as
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{O}
.

Appendix B
Normalization Procedure

We give a brief overview of the normalization procedure for
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
ontologies introduced in
(
Baader et al. 2005a
)
.
Given an
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
TBox
\mathcal{T}
, let
\mathsf{BC}_{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{N}_{C}\cup\{\top\}\cup\{\{a\}\mid a\in\mathcal{N}_{I}\}
be the set of
basic concept descriptions
.
\mathcal{T}
is in
normal form
if (1) all concept subsumption axioms are of one of the forms

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq D\

\displaystyle C_{1}\sqcap C_{2}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq D

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\exists r.C_{2}

\displaystyle\exists r.C_{1}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq D,

where
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathsf{BC}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
D\in\mathsf{BC}_{\mathcal{T}}\cup\{\bot\}
, and (2) all role inclusion axioms are of the form
r\sqsubseteq s
or
r_{1}\circ r_{2}\sqsubseteq s
. Any
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
TBox
\mathcal{T}
can be converted into a normalized TBox
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
whose size is linear in the size of
\mathcal{T}
by exhaustively applying a number of translation rules that introduce new concept and role names. The details of these translation rules are given in
(
Baader et al. 2005b
)
. The resulting ontology is a
conservative extension
of
\mathcal{T}
; that is, every model of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is also a model of
\mathcal{T}
. Since the ABox of an ontology can be readily converted into equivalent TBox axioms (see
Section

3.2
), this procedure equally applies to normalizing entire ontologies.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem 1 (Soundness)

In order to prove
Theorem

2
, we first show the correctness of our loss functions. Recall that the inclusion loss
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)
of two boxes
A
and
B
is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=\begin{cases}\left\lVert\max\{\bm{0},\,\bm{d}(A,B)+2\bm{o}(A)-\gamma\}\right\rVert&\text{if }B\neq\emptyset\\
\max\{0,\bm{o}(A)_{1}+1\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 0.

Let
A
and
B
be boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
\gamma\leq 0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=0
, then
A\subseteq B
.

Proof.

First, assume that
B=\emptyset
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=0
, we have
\bm{o}(A)_{1}=\bm{u}_{A,1}-\bm{l}_{A,1}\leq-1
and thus
\bm{l}_{A,1}>\bm{u}_{A,1}
. Therefore
A=\emptyset
.

If
B\neq\emptyset
, we show that
\bm{l}_{B}\leq\bm{l}_{A}
and
\bm{u}_{A}\leq\bm{u}_{B}
. Assume
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(A,B)=0
. We have that

\displaystyle\bm{d}(A,B)+2\bm{o}(A)-\gamma

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{c}(A)-\bm{c}(B)\right\rvert+\bm{o}(A)-\bm{o}(B)-\gamma

\displaystyle\leq 0

and thus

\left\lvert\bm{c}(A)-\bm{c}(B)\right\rvert+\bm{o}(A)-\bm{o}(B)\leq\gamma\leq 0.

Now fix an arbitrary dimension
k
such that
1\leq k\leq n
. We distinguish two cases:

Case 1:
\bm{c}(A)_{k}\geq\bm{c}(B)_{k}.

We eliminate the absolute value function and use
\bm{u}_{\beta}=\bm{c}(\beta)+\bm{o}(\beta)
for an arbitrary box
\beta
to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{A,\,k}-\bm{u}_{B,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{A,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{u}_{B,\,k}.

Since
\bm{c}(A)_{k}\geq\bm{c}(B)_{k}
, we furthermore have by the definition of
\bm{c}(\cdot)
that

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{l}_{A,\,k}+\bm{u}_{A,\,k}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\bm{l}_{B,\,k}+\bm{u}_{B,\,k}}{2}

\displaystyle\bm{l}_{A,\,k}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{l}_{B,\,k}+\underbrace{\bm{u}_{B,\,k}-\bm{u}_{A,\,k}}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\bm{l}_{A,\,k}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{l}_{B,\,k}.

Case 2:
\bm{c}(A)_{k}\leq\bm{c}(B)_{k}.

Similarly to the first case, we eliminate the absolute value function and use
\bm{l}_{\beta}=\bm{c}(\beta)-\bm{o}(\beta)
to obtain

\displaystyle-\bm{l}_{A,\,k}+\bm{l}_{B,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\bm{l}_{B,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{l}_{A,\,k}.

Because
\bm{c}(A)_{k}\leq\bm{c}(B)_{k}
, we furthermore have

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{l}_{A,\,k}+\bm{u}_{A,\,k}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bm{l}_{B,\,k}+\bm{u}_{B,\,k}}{2}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\bm{l}_{A,\,k}-\bm{l}_{B,\,k}}_{\geq 0}+\,\bm{u}_{A,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{u}_{B,\,k}

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{A,\,k}

\displaystyle\leq\bm{u}_{B,\,k}.

Now, consider an arbitrary point
\bm{a}\in A
, i.e.,
\bm{l}_{A}\leq\bm{a}\leq\bm{u}_{A}
. But then

\bm{l}_{B}\leq\bm{l}_{A}\leq\bm{a}\leq\bm{u}_{A}\leq\bm{u}_{B}

and thus
\bm{a}\in B
.
∎

Theorem 1 (Soundness).

Let
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
be an
\mathcal{EL^{++}}
ontology. If
\gamma\leq 0
and there exist
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
embeddings in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})=0
, then these embeddings are a model of
\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

We first perform the standard steps of transforming the ABox and normalizing the axioms in
\mathcal{O}
. Let
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
denote the resulting ontology. The
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
embeddings induce the geometric interpretation
\mathcal{I}=(\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},\cdot^{\mathcal{I}})
defined as follows:

(1)

\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(2)

for every concept
C\in\mathcal{N}_{C}
, let
C^{\mathcal{I}}=\textnormal{Box}(C)
,

(3)

for every individual
a\in\mathcal{N}_{I}
, let
a^{\mathcal{I}}=\bm{e}_{a}
,

(4)

for every role
r\in\mathcal{N}_{R}
, let
r^{\mathcal{I}}=\textnormal{Head}(r)\times\textnormal{Tail}(r)
.

We show that
\mathcal{I}
is a model of
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
. First, note that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})=0
implies that the regularization loss is 0, and thus
\textnormal{Bump}(C)=\bm{0}
for any
C\in\mathcal{N}_{C}\cup\mathcal{N}_{I}
. We now show that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies every axiom
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
, distinguishing between the different normal forms. Implicitly, we make frequent use of
Lemma

1
, which we do not state explicitly for the sake of brevity.

Case 1:

\alpha=C\sqsubseteq D.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{1}(C,D)=0
and therefore
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(C),\textnormal{Box}(D))=0
, we have that
\textnormal{Box}(C)\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(D)
. But then it immediately follows from the definition of
\mathcal{I}
that
C^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq D^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case 2:
\alpha=C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq E.

We have that
\mathcal{L}_{2}(C,D,E)=0
and therefore it follows that
\textnormal{Box}(C)\cap\textnormal{Box}(D)\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(E)
. Hence, we have
(C\sqcap D)^{\mathcal{I}}=C^{\mathcal{I}}\cap D^{\mathcal{I}}=\textnormal{Box}(C)\cap\textnormal{Box}(D)\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(E)=E^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case 3:
\alpha=C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D.

Assume
D^{\mathcal{I}}\neq\emptyset
.
Let
x\in C^{\mathcal{I}}=\textnormal{Box}(C)
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{3}(C,r,D)=0
and all bump vectors are
\bm{0}
, we have
\textnormal{Box}(C)\subseteq\textnormal{Head}(r)
and therefore
x\in\textnormal{Head}(r)
. Similarly, for any
y\in D^{\mathcal{I}}
we have
y\in\textnormal{Tail}(r)
. But then
(x,y)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
and therefore
x\in(\exists r.D)^{\mathcal{I}}
.

If on the other hand
D^{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
, we also have
(\exists r.D)^{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
. Since
\textnormal{Box}(D)
is empty, we furthermore have
\mathcal{L}_{\subseteq}(\textnormal{Box}(C),\emptyset)=0
and therefore
\textnormal{Box}(C)=\emptyset
, i.e.,
C^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq(\exists r.D)^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case 4:
\alpha=\exists r.C\sqsubseteq D.

Assume
D^{\mathcal{I}}\neq\emptyset
.
Let
x\in(\exists r.C)^{\mathcal{I}}
. Hence, there exist a
y\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
(x,y)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
. By the definition of
r^{\mathcal{I}}
, we must therefore have
x\in\textnormal{Head}(r)
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{4}(r,C,D)=0
, furthermore
\textnormal{Head}(r)\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(D)
and therefore
x\in D^{\mathcal{I}}
.

If on the other hand
D^{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
, we have
\textnormal{Head}(r)\subseteq\emptyset
and thus
r=\emptyset
, so
(\exists r.C)^{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
.

Case 5:
\alpha=C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq\bot.

We have
\mathcal{L}_{5}(C,D)=0
, so by
Lemma

2
we have that
(C\sqcap D)^{\mathcal{I}}=\textnormal{Box}(C)\cap\textnormal{Box}(D)=\emptyset\subseteq\bot^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case 6:
\alpha=r\sqsubseteq s.
Let
(a,b)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
. By the definition of
r^{\mathcal{I}}
,
a\in\textnormal{Head}(r)
and
b\in\textnormal{Tail}(r)
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{6}(r,s)=0
, we furthermore have
\textnormal{Head}(r)\subseteq\textnormal{Head}(s)
and
\textnormal{Tail}(r)\subseteq\textnormal{Tail}(s)
and hence
(a,b)\in s^{\mathcal{I}}
.

Case 7:
\alpha=r_{1}\circ r_{2}\sqsubseteq s.
Let
(a,b)\in r_{1}^{\mathcal{I}}\circ r_{2}^{\mathcal{I}}
. By definition, we have
a\in\textnormal{Head}(r_{1})
and
b\in\textnormal{Tail}(r_{2})
. Because
\mathcal{L}_{7}(r_{1},r_{2},s)=0
,
\textnormal{Head}(r_{1})\subseteq\textnormal{Head}(s)
and
\textnormal{Tail}(r_{2})\subseteq\textnormal{Tail}(s)
, so
(a,b)\in s^{\mathcal{I}}
.

We have shown that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies every axiom in
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
, and is therefore a model of
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
. But since
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
is a conservative extension of
\mathcal{O}

(
Baader et al. 2005a
)
, it follows that
\mathcal{I}
is also a model of
\mathcal{O}
.

Appendix F
Scoring functions

Scoring functions are used to compute the likelihood of candidate axioms based on the learned embeddings of their concepts. We define a scoring function
s(\cdot)
for candidate axioms in all four normal forms NF1–4.

First and second normal form.

For an NF1 axiom
C\sqsubseteq D
, the score is based simply on the distance between the embeddings of
C
and
D
, i.e., for
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
we have

s(C\sqsubseteq D)=-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(C))-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(D))\right\rVert.

The same formulation can be used for the baseline methods. Similarly, for NF2 axioms
C\sqcap D\sqsubseteq E
, the score is defined as the negative distance of the embedding of
E
to the intersection of
C
and
D
in the embedding space.

Third normal form.

For axioms in the third and fourth normal form the scoring function differs between
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
and the baseline methods, because of the different role representation. For
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
, the score for a subsumption
C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D
is naturally defined as

\displaystyle s(C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D)=

\displaystyle-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(C))+\textnormal{Bump}(D)-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Head}(r))\right\rVert

\displaystyle-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(D))+\textnormal{Bump}(C)-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Tail}(r))\right\rVert.

In the baseline methods, the score is computed similarly to TransE:

s(C\sqsubseteq\exists r.D)=-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(C))+\bm{v}(r)-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(D))\right\rVert,

where
\bm{v}(r)
is the embedding of role
r
.

Fourth normal form.

Finally, for an NF4 axiom
\exists r.C\sqsubseteq D
, the score assigned by
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
is given by

s(\exists r.C\sqsubseteq D)=-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Head}(r))-\textnormal{Bump}(C)-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(D))\right\rVert,

and for the baseline methods we have

s(\exists r.C\sqsubseteq D)=-\left\lVert\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(C))-v(r)-\bm{c}(\textnormal{Box}(D))\right\rVert.

Volume-based scoring functions.

An alternative, which is employed by BoxEL
(
Xiong et al. 2022
)
, is to define scoring functions based on volumes instead of distances. We empirically find distance-based scoring functions to perform much better, which is why we adopt this approach in
Box
{}^{\textsf{2}}
EL
.

Appendix G
Evaluation metrics

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
denote the testing set and
\rk(\alpha)
be the rank of the true test axiom
\alpha
with respect to all candidate predictions. Hits@
k
measures the fraction of test axioms with rank
\leq k
, i.e.,

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}}\mathbb{1}[\rk(\alpha)\leq k].

The mean rank is simply the average of the ranks of all axioms in the testing set.
Often the median rank is used instead, since this metric is more robust to outliers. An alternative is the mean reciprocal rank, defined as

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}}\frac{1}{\rk(\alpha)},

which takes on values between 0 and 1 only. Finally, to compute the area under the ROC curve we regard the model as a binary classifier parameterized by a threshold
k
, that assigns
true
to a candidate prediction if its rank is less than or equal to
k
and
false
otherwise. The ROC curve is then obtained by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate for varying thresholds
k
.

Appendix I
Comparison of deductive and inductive reasoning

We investigate the comparatively lower performance of the embedding models on deductive reasoning compared to the inductive subsumption prediction task with a concrete example. In the GALEN ontology, we find the following subsumption axiom to predict in the testing set:

\mathsf{SodiumLactate}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{NAMEDComplexChemical}.

This axiom cannot be logically inferred from the training data. However, the following similar subsumptions are part of the training data:

\displaystyle\mathsf{SodiumLactate}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\exists\textsf{isMadeOf}.\mathsf{Sodium}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SodiumBicarbonate}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\exists\textsf{isMadeOf}.\mathsf{Sodium}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SodiumCitrate}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\exists\textsf{isMadeOf}.\mathsf{Sodium}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SodiumBicarbonate}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\mathsf{NAMEDComplexChemical}

\displaystyle\mathsf{SodiumCitrate}

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\mathsf{NAMEDComplexChemical}.

It seems quite likely that our model will be able to exploit the statistical information contained in these subsumptions to learn embeddings where
SodiumLactate
is close to
NAMEDComplexChemical
, and thus achieve a high score for the desired true axiom.

In contrast, in the deductive reasoning setting, all testing axioms are logical inferences of the training data. One such testing axiom is

\mathsf{SodiumLactate}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{SodiumCompound}.

This logical inference has been reached by a reasoning algorithm such as ELK
(
Kazakov et al. 2014
)
by performing the sequence of derivations listed in
Figure

4
.
Embedding methods, on the other hand, do not explicitly perform these derivations. Instead, they learn embeddings that align with the semantics of the ontology, i.e., in convergence we will have that
\textnormal{Box}(\mathsf{SodiumLactate})\subseteq\textnormal{Box}(\mathsf{SodiumCompound})
. However, our model has to learn highly accurate embeddings for a number of different concepts and roles involved in the derivation sequence for this to actually hold in practice. Furthermore, the model then makes statistical predictions based on the scoring functions instead of only predicting axioms that completely align with the semantics. Hence there may be many other plausible axioms that are ranked higher by the model, decreasing the score of the desired logical inference.
