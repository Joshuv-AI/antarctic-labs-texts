Optimization in quantum metrology and entanglement theory using semidefinite programming
Árpád Lukács, Róbert Trényi, Tamás Vértesi, Géza Tóth

Abstract
We discuss efficient methods to optimize the metrological performance over local Hamiltonians in a bipartite quantum system. For a given quantum state, our methods find the best local Hamiltonian for which the state outperforms separable states the most from the point of view of quantum metrology. We show that this problem can be reduced to maximize the quantum Fisher information over a certain set of Hamiltonians. We present the quantum Fisher information in a bilinear form and maximize it by an iterative see-saw (ISS) method, in which each step is based on semidefinite programming. We also solve the problem with the method of moments that works very well for smaller systems. We consider a number of other problems in quantum information theory that can be solved in a similar manner. For instance, we determine the bound entangled quantum states that maximally violate the Computable Cross Norm-Realignment (CCNR) criterion. Our approach is one of the efficient methods that can be applied for an optimization of the unitary dynamics in quantum metrology, the other methods being, for example, machine learning, variational quantum circuits, or neural networks. The advantage of our method is the fast and robust convergence due to the simple mathematical structure of the approach.



I
Introduction

Quantum entanglement is at the heart of quantum physics and several quantum
information processing applications

Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Friis et al. 2019
;
Horodecki 2021
.
It also plays a central role in quantum metrology. It is known that
interparticle entanglement is needed to overcome the shot-noise limit of the
precision in parameter estimation in a linear interferometer

Pezzé and Smerzi 2009
. It has also been shown that the larger the
achieved precision in parameter estimation with a multiparticle system, the
larger the depth of entanglement the state must possess

Hyllus et al. 2012
;
Tóth 2012
. However, not all entangled states
are useful for metrology
Hyllus et al. 2010
.

At this point, several important questions arise. How can we find the best
quantum state for metrology for a given setup? Perhaps, historically one of the
first methods was the iterative see-saw (ISS) method applied in the quantum
Fisher information picture

Macieszczak 2013
;
Tóth and Vértesi 2018
;
Chabuda et al. 2020
;
Pál et al. 2021
,
as well as in the Bayesian one

Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2011
;
Macieszczak et al. 2014a
;
Jarzyna and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2015
.
There are also methods based on an optimization over the purifications

Escher et al. 2011
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański and Maccone 2014
.
It is also possible to use variational techniques for efficiently searching in
the high-dimensional space of quantum states, which was also
applied for a similar task
Koczor et al. 2020
;
Beckey et al. 2022
.
The variational search methods can even be tailored for
particular physical systems, such as tweezer arrays as programmable quantum
sensors
Kaubruegger et al. 2019
.

Another relevant question is how we could find the optimal Hamiltonian for a
given quantum state. In the qubit case, it has been studied how to optimize the
metrological performance for local Hamiltonians that are all Pauli spin matrices
rotated by some unitary
Hyllus et al. 2010
. In this case, the best
metrological performance achievable by separable states is a constant
independent of the Hamiltonian. Any state that has a better metrological
performance than that must be entangled.

The problem of the metrological performance of a bipartite state of qudits with
a dimension larger than two is more complicated. In this case, the local
Hamiltonians cannot be transformed into each other by local unitaries, and the
best metrological performance achievable by separable states depends on the
Hamiltonian. Thus, we introduce the metrological gain as a
general way to characterize the quantum advantage of a system with arbitrary
dimensions. It is defined as the ratio of the quantum Fisher information and
the maximum of the quantum Fisher information over separable states maximized
over local Hamiltonians
Tóth et al. 2020
. The metrological gain is
convex in the state, and it cannot decrease if additional copies or ancilla
systems are added
Tóth et al. 2020
. The optimization over the local
Hamiltonian can also be carried out via an ISS method

Tóth et al. 2020
;
Pál et al. 2021
;
Trényi et al. 2024
. Thus, our method
uses an ISS algorithm for optimizing the unitary dynamics rather than for
optimizing the state. Note that instead of using the metrological gain, one can
also consider the difference of the quantum Fisher information and its maximum
for separable states
Tan et al. 2021
, or even consider the difference with
respect to a bound that depends on the state

Yadin et al. 2018
;
Morris et al. 2020
;
Gessner et al. 2016
;
Gessner et al. 2017
.

In this paper, we examine the optimization problem above in a great depth. We
elaborate the point that local Hamiltonians with meaningful constraints form a
convex set. Thus, we have to maximize the metrological gain over a convex set of
local Hamiltonians. We find several algorithms to maximize the metrological
gain. The first method is based on maximizing a quadratic expression using a
see-saw over a bilinear expression, and its computational costs are minimal. We
present other algorithms that need quadratic programming with quadratic
constraints and relaxations. Such methods work for small systems. We test the
methods and compare them to each other. We list a number of similar problems
that can be solved in an analogous way, such as maximization of norms over a
convex set.

Our work is also related to recent efforts to solve various problems of
numerical optimization in quantum metrology, including the combination of the tasks mentioned above,
such as using an ISS for optimizing the quantum state and the unitary dynamics
Tóth et al. 2020
.
An ISS-based method has been used to optimize over a general dynamics using its Choi-Jamiolkowski
representation

.
An ISS-based method has also been used to optimize over adaptive strategies, when
coherently probing several independent quantum channels
Kurdzialek et al. 2024
.
An optimization over the probe state and the dynamics has been realized experimentally in a programmable quantum sensor realized in trapped cold ions
Marciniak et al. 2022
. For the identification of the optimal quantum metrological protocol in large systems,
matrix product operators have been used
Chabuda et al. 2020
.
Optimization methods, including ISS, has been considered in estimating spectral density of a stochastic signal field

Gardner et al. 2024
.
Finally,
numerical optimization in quantum metrology in various complex scenarios has been considered including multiparameter estimation

Albarelli et al. 2019
;
Gessner et al. 2020
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
;

;

;

, and there are even methods that are based on

Our work has a relation to resource theories, in which the quantum Fisher
information can be used to identify quantum resources

Chitambar and Gour 2019
. Indeed, quantum Fisher information
can be ideal to characterize quantum systems realized in Noisy

;

;

The bound for quantum Fisher information for separable states has already been

;

II
Background

In this section, we review entanglement theory and quantum metrology.

II.1
Entanglement theory

In this section, we summarize basic notions of entanglement theory

Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
. A bipartite quantum state is
called separable if it can be written as mixture of product states as

Werner 1989

\varrho_{\rm sep}=\sum_{k}p_{k}\varrho^{(k)}_{1}\otimes\varrho^{(k)}_{2},

(1)

where
p_{k}
are probabilities. If a quantum state cannot be decomposed as in
Eq. (
1
), then the state is entangled.

Deciding whether a quantum state is entangled is a hard task in general.
However, there are some necessary conditions for separability, that are easy to
test. If these conditions are violated then the state is entangled.

One of the most important conditions of this type is the condition based on the
positivity of the partial transpose (PPT). For a bipartite density matrix given
as

\varrho=\sum_{kl,mn}\varrho_{kl,mn}|k\rangle\langle l|\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|

(2)

the partial transpose according to first subsystem is defined by exchanging
subscripts
k
and
l
as

\varrho^{T1}=\sum_{kl,mn}\varrho_{lk,mn}|k\rangle\langle l|\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|.

(3)

It has been shown that for separable quantum states

Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996

\varrho^{T1}\geq 0

(4)

holds. Thus, if
\varrho^{T1}
has a negative eigenvalue then the quantum state
is entangled. For
2\times 2
and
2\times 3
systems, the PPT condition detects
all entangled states
Horodecki et al. 1996
. For systems of size

3\times 3
and larger, there are PPT entangled states

Horodecki 1997
;
Horodecki et al. 1998
. They are also quantum
states that are called bound entangled since their entanglement cannot be
distilled to singlet states with local operations and classical communication
(LOCC). Some of the bound entangled states are detected as entangled by the CCNR
criterion
Rudolph 2005
;
Chen and Wu 2003
.

II.2
Quantum metrology

In this section, we summarize basic notions of quantum metrology such as the quantum Fisher information and the Cramér-Rao bound. We will also discuss the multi-parameter case.

The fundamental task of quantum metrology is the following. We have a probe state or an initial state
\varrho,
that evolves according to the dynamics

\varrho_{\theta}=U_{\theta}\varrho U_{\theta}^{\dagger},

(5)

where the unitary is given as

U_{\theta}=\exp(-iH\theta).

(6)

Here
H
is the Hamiltonian of the evolution and
\theta
is a parameter. Then, the metrological precision in estimating
\theta

is bounded by the Cramér-Rao bound

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 1982
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Petz 2008
;
Braunstein et al. 1996
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
;
Pezze and Smerzi 2014
;
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
;
Pezzè et al. 2018
;
Paris 2009

(\Delta\theta)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\nu\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]},

(7)

where
\nu
is the number of independent repetitions, and
\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]
is the quantum Fisher information, defined as

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 1982
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Petz 2008
;
Braunstein et al. 1996

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]=\sum_{k,l}Q_{kl}^{2}|H_{kl}|^{2}.

(8)

Here, we consider a density matrix with the following eigendecomposition

\varrho=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|,

(9)

the constant coefficients
Q_{kl}
depend only on the eigenvalues of the probe state and are given as

Q_{kl}:=\sqrt{2\frac{(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{l})^{2}}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}},

(10)

whenever the denominator is not zero, and the matrix element of the Hamiltonian in the basis of the eigenvectors of
the density matrix is given as

H_{kl}=\langle k|H|l\rangle.

(11)

The quantum Fisher information in Eq. (
8
) can be written in a different form that will be useful in our calculations as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{kl}Q_{kl}^{2}\left[(H_{kl}^{\rm{r}})^{2}+(H_{kl}^{\rm{i}})^{2}\right],

(12)

where the superscripts "r" and "i" denote the real and imaginary parts,
respectively.

After defining the quantum Fisher information,
it is important to stress its basic properties,
namely, that it is convex in the state, and it is bounded from above by the variance as

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]\leq 4(\Delta H)^{2},

(13)

where for pure states there is an equality in Eq. (
13
).

Moreover, there is an important inequality for the quantum Fisher information with the error propagation formula

Hotta and Ozawa 2004
;
Escher 2012
;
Fröwis et al. 2015

(\Delta\theta)^{2}_{M}=\frac{(\Delta M)^{2}}{\langle{i[M,H]}\rangle^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]},

(14)

which gives us, essentially, the precision of the
\theta

parameter estimation, provided that we measure in the eigenbasis of the
observable
M.
It can be shown that the uncertainty

(\Delta\theta)^{2}_{M}
is the smallest, and thus the bound in Eq. (
14
) is
the best if we choose
M
to be the so-called symmetric logarithmic derivative

M_{\rm opt}=2i\sum_{k,l}\frac{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{l}}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}|k\rangle\langle l|\langle k|H|l\rangle,

(15)

which fulfills

i[\varrho,H]=\frac{1}{2}\{\varrho,M_{\rm opt}\},

(16)

where
\{X,Y\}=XY+YX
is the anticommutator.
In this case, the inequality in Eq. (
14
) is saturated.

So far we discussed the case of estimating a single parameter. Let us consider
now the estimation of several parameters when the unitary dynamics
is given by

\exp\left(-i\sum_{n}H^{(n)}\theta^{(n)}\right),

(17)

where
H^{(n)}
is the Hamiltonian corresponding to the parameter

\theta^{(n)}.
The Cramér-Rao bound can be generalized to this case as

C-\nu^{-1}F^{-1}\geq 0,

(18)

where the inequality in Eq. (
18
) means that the left-hand side is a positive
semidefinite matrix,
C
is now the covariance matrix with elements

C_{mn}=\langle{\theta^{(m)}\theta^{(n)}}\rangle-\langle{\theta^{(m)}}\rangle\langle{\theta^{(n)}}\rangle,

(19)

and
F
is the Fisher matrix, and its elements are given as

\displaystyle F_{mn}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H^{(m)},H^{(n)}].

(20)

Here, the quantum Fisher information with two operators is given as

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,A,B]=\sum_{k,l}Q_{kl}^{2}A_{kl}B_{kl}^{*}\equiv{\rm Tr}[(Q\circ Q\circ A)B],

(21)

where
\circ
denotes elementwise or Hadamard product. After straightforward algebra,
the following equivalent formulation can be obtained

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,A,B]=\sum_{kl}Q_{kl}^{2}\bigg[A_{kl}^{\rm{r}}B_{kl}^{\rm{r}}+A_{kl}^{\rm{i}}B_{kl}^{\rm{i}}\bigg].

(22)

If
\varrho
has low rank, then, based on Eq. (
10
), the
Q

matrix is sparse, which can greatly simplify the calculation of Eq. (
22
).
In contrast to the single parameter case, the generalized
Cramér-Rao bound in Eq. (
18
) cannot be always saturated even in the limit of many repetitions.

III
Basic notions for optimizing the metrological performance

When maximizing the quantum metrological performance over a Hamiltonian, we have
to consider two important issues.

First, not all Hamiltonians are easy to realize. In particular, local
Hamiltonians are much easier to realize than Hamiltonians with an interaction
term, and such Hamiltonians appear most typically in quantum metrology. Thus, we
will restrict the optimization for such Hamiltonians given as

{\mathcal{H}}=H_{1}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes H_{2},

(23)

where
H_{n}
are single-subsystem operators. In the rest of the paper,

{\mathcal{H}}
will denote such local Hamiltonians. Moreover, we will use

\mathcal{L}
to denote the set of local Hamiltonians. With
this, the considered metrological process is shown schematically in
Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
The considered metrological process starts by
preparing a given bipartite probe state
\varrho
that undergoes the unitary
dynamics
U_{\theta}=e^{-i\mathcal{H}\theta}
. This results in the
parametrized output state
\varrho_{\theta}=U_{\theta}\varrho U_{\theta}^{\dagger}
, where
\theta
is the parameter that we try to estimate.
It is assumed that the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
that generates the unitary
dynamics is local (
i.e.,

{\mathcal{H}}\in{\mathcal{L}}
). A
Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}
on a bipartite system is said to be local if it can
be written as
{\mathcal{H}}=H_{1}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes H_{2},

where
H_{1}
(
H_{2}
) is a Hamiltonian on the first (second) subsystem. In other
words, local Hamiltonians do not contain interaction terms between the two
parties. In this setting, for a given probe state
\varrho
, we look for the
optimal local Hamiltonian such that the output state has the capability to
provide the best possible metrological performance
precision in estimating the parameter
\theta.

We will discuss in the text, how to quantify the metrological
performance of a quantum state with a given local Hamiltonian.

Second, we have to note that for the quantum Fisher information

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,cH]=|c|^{2}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,H]

(24)

holds, where
c
is some constant. Thus, it is easy to improve the metrological
performance of any Hamiltonian by multiplying it by a constant factor. In order
to obtain a meaningful optimization problem, we have to consider a formulation
of such a problem where such a "trick" cannot be used to increase the quantum
Fisher information to infinity. One option could be to divide the quantum Fisher
information with the square of some norm or seminorm of the Hamiltonian, for
which

||cH||=|c|\cdot||H||.

(25)

In principle, several norms for the normalization, for instance, the Hilbert-Schmidt norm

||X||_{\rm HS}=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}(XX^{\dagger})}.

(26)

In this paper, we argue that it is advantageous to compare the
metrological performance of a state to the best performance of separable states.
Therefore, we will divide the quantum Fisher information of the state by the maximum quantum
Fisher information achievable by separable states given as for some
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}
as
Ciampini et al. 2016
;
Tóth and Vértesi 2018

\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})=\sum_{n=1,2}[\sigma_{\max}(H_{n})-\sigma_{\min}(H_{n})]^{2},

(27)

where
\sigma_{\max}(X)
and
\sigma_{\min}(X)
denotes the maximal and minimal
eigenvalues, respectively.

III.1
Seminorm for local Hamiltonians

Let us now present the details of our arguments. We will examine some properties
of the maximum of the quantum Fisher information for separable states given in Eq. (
27
). Let us consider first the quantity

\delta(X)=\sigma_{\max}(X)-\sigma_{\min}(X),

(28)

where
\sigma_{\min}(X)
and
\sigma_{\max}(X)
denote the smallest and largest
eigenvalues of
X,
respectively. Simple algebra shows that

Boixo et al. 2007
;
*[InequalitiessimilartoEq.\eqref{eq:fqdelta}
;
alsoinvolvingthepurityofthestateandthevariance
;
canbefoundat][]Toth2017Lower

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,X]\leq\delta^{2}(X).

(29)

With Eq. (
28
), the maximum of the quantum Fisher information for
separable states,
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})},
can be expressed as

\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})\equiv\delta^{2}(H_{1})+\delta^{2}(H_{2}).

(30)

Next, let us prove some important properties of
\delta(X).

Observation 1.
We know that
\delta(X)
is a seminorm. While this is shown
in Ref.
Boixo et al. 2007
, for completeness and since the steps of the proof
will be needed later, we present a brief argument to prove it.

Proof.
We need the well-known fact that for Hermitian matrices
A
and
B

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm max}(A+B)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm max}(A)+\sigma_{\rm max}(B),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm min}(A+B)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm min}(A)+\sigma_{\rm min}(B),

(31)

hold, following from Weyl’s inequality
Bhatia 1997
. Based on these, we will now prove that
\delta(X)
has all the properties of a seminorm
Reed and Simon 1990
;
Rudin 1991
, examining the three properties one by one.

(i) Subadditivity or the triangle
inequality holds since

\delta(A+B)\leq\delta(A)+\delta(B)

(32)

for all
A,B
based on Eq. (
31
).

(ii) Absolute homogeneity holds
since

\delta(cX)=|c|\delta(X)

(33)

for all
c
and
X.

(iii) Non-negativity
holds since
\delta(X)\geq 0
holds for all
X.
If
X
is a zero matrix then

\delta(X)=0.
However,
\delta(X)=0
does not imply that
X
is a zero matrix,
thus
\delta(X)
is not a norm, only a seminorm
Reed and Simon 1990
;
Rudin 1991
.

\hfill\blacksquare

Next, let us prove some important properties of the maximal quantum Fisher
information for separable states.

Observation 2.
The expression with the square root of the maximum for separable states

\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})}

(34)

is a seminorm for
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}.

Proof.
We will now follows steps similar to those in the proof of Observation 1, showing that the three properties of seminorms are satisfied.

(i) Let us consider a local Hamiltonian that is the sum of two
local Hamiltonians

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}+\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime},

(35)

where the two local Hamiltonians are given as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\prime}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes H_{2}^{\prime},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes H_{2}^{\prime\prime}.

(36)

Then, the following series of inequalities prove the subadditivity property

\displaystyle\sqrt{\delta^{2}(H_{1}^{\prime}+H_{1}^{\prime\prime})+\delta^{2}(H_{2}^{\prime}+H_{2}^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\sqrt{[\delta(H_{1}^{\prime})+\delta(H_{1}^{\prime\prime})]^{2}+[\delta(H_{2}^{\prime})+\delta(H_{2}^{\prime\prime})]^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\sqrt{\delta^{2}(H_{1}^{\prime})+\delta^{2}(H_{2}^{\prime})}+\sqrt{\delta^{2}(H_{1}^{\prime\prime})+\delta^{2}(H_{2}^{\prime\prime})}.

(37)

The first inequality in Eq. (
37
) is due to the subadditivity of

\delta(X)
and the monotonicity of
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
in
x
and
y.
The second
inequality is based on the relation

\sqrt{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}+(y_{1}+y_{2})^{2}}\leq\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}}+\sqrt{x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}},

(38)

which can be proved by straightforward algebra.

(ii) Absolute homogeneity,

\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(c\mathcal{H})}=|c|\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})}

(39)

for any real
c
follows directly from the expression for
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})
with
\delta(H_{n})
in Eq. (
30
), using the absolute homogeneity property of
\delta
given in
Eq (
33
)

(iii) Non-negativity can be proven using the expression for
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})
with
\delta(H_{n})
in Eq. (
30
) and the non-negativity of
\delta
.
\hfill\blacksquare

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})=0
holds if and only if
\mathcal{H}=c\openone,
where
c
is a real constant. Thus, in a certain sense,

\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}-\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime})
plays a role of distance between
local Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}
such that the distance is
zero if
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}-\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}
is the identity times some constant. In this
case, the two matrices are not identical, but from the point of view of quantum
metrology they act the same way.

Let us see some consequences of the observation. As any seminorm,

\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})}
is convex in the local Hamiltonian. Due
to that,
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})
is also convex.

III.2
Metrological gain

Based on these ideas, we define the metrological gain for a given local
Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}
and for a given quantum state
\varrho
as

Tóth et al. 2020

g_{\mathcal{H}}(\varrho)=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]}{\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})}.

(40)

In Eq. (
40
), we normalize the quantum Fisher information with a square of a
seminorm of local Hamiltonians. Then, we would like to obtain the gain based on an optimization over all local Hamiltonians as

g(\varrho)=\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}}g_{\mathcal{H}}(\varrho).

(41)

Maximizing
g_{\mathcal{H}}
over
\mathcal{H}
is difficult since both the
numerator and denominator depend on
\mathcal{H}.
Let us consider a subset of
Hamiltonians of the type Eq. (
23
) such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(H_{n})=-c_{n},\quad\sigma_{\max}(H_{n})=+c_{n}

(42)

for
n=1,2.
Then, the maximum of the quantum Fisher information for separable states is

\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{({\rm sep})}(\mathcal{H})=4(c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}).

(43)

We can define the metrological gain for Hamiltonians that fulfill Eq. (
42
) as

g_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\varrho)=\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]}{4(c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2})},

(44)

where we call
\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
the set of local Hamiltonians satisfying
Eq. (
42
). Finally, the maximal gain over all possible local Hamiltonians is given as

g(\varrho)=\max_{c_{1},c_{2}}g_{c_{1},c_{2}}.

(45)

IV
Method for maximizing the quantum Fisher information over local Hamiltonians

In the previous section, we have shown that maximizing the metrological gain is
essentially reduced to maximizing the quantum Fisher information with some
constraints on the local Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
46
), that is,
computing

\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}],

(49)

where we remember that
\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
is the set of local Hamiltonians
fulfilling Eq. (
42

maximize the quantum Fisher information with such constraints.

IV.1
See-saw optimization based on a bilinear form

In Ref.
Tóth et al. 2020
, a method was presented to maximize the quantum
Fisher information over local Hamiltonians for a given quantum state. It is an
iterative method, that needs to compute the symmetric logarithmic derivative
Eq. (
15
) at each step and is based on quantum metrological considerations. In
this section, we will present a simpler formulation based on general ideas in
optimization theory. Later, this will also make it possible to find
provably the global maximum of the quantum Fisher information.

The quantum Fisher information given in Eq. (
12
) is a weighted sum of
squares of the real and imaginary parts of the elements of the Hamiltonian. It can further be written as

\vec{v}^{T}R\vec{v},

(50)

where
R
contains the
Q_{kl}
’s and
\vec{v}
contains the
H_{kl}^{\rm{r}}
’s
and the
H_{kl}^{\rm{i}}
’s. The matrix
R
is a positive semidefinite diagonal
matrix. Minimizing such an expression over
\vec{v}
is a simple problem.
On the other hand, maximizing it is difficult. A usual way of maximizing such an expression is as follows

Konno 1976a
;
Konno 1976b
;
Floudas and Visweswaran 1995
. We replace
the optimization over
\vec{v}
by a simultaneous optimization of the vectors

\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
of a bilinear form as

\max_{\vec{v}}\vec{v}^{T}R\vec{v}=\max_{\vec{v},\vec{w}}\vec{v}^{T}R\vec{w}.

(51)

This can be a basis for a see-saw-type optimization.

Based on this, we can write the following.

Observation 3.
The quantum Fisher information can be maximized with
an iterative see-saw (ISS) method. We
need to use the formula maximizing over local Hamiltonians

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\max_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\sum_{kl}Q_{kl}^{2}\bigg[\mathcal{H}_{kl}^{\rm{r}}\mathcal{K}_{kl}^{\rm{r}}+\mathcal{H}_{kl}^{\rm{i}}\mathcal{K}_{kl}^{\rm{i}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\equiv\max_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}{\rm Tr}[(Q\circ Q\circ\mathcal{H})\mathcal{K}],

(52)

Based on Eq. (
52
), we can carry out the following algorithm. Initially, we
choose
\mathcal{H}
randomly. Then, we maximize over the
matrix
\mathcal{K}
while keeping
\mathcal{H}
fixed. Such an optimization can
be carried out, for instance, by semidefinite programming

Vandenberghe and Boyd 1996
or by simple matrix algebraic calculations,
see Appendix
B
. Then, we maximize over
\mathcal{H}
while keeping
\mathcal{K}
fixed. Then, we optimize again over
\mathcal{K}
, etc.
This process is illustrated in Fig.

. Note that we do not
have to verify that the random initial Hamiltonian fulfills the constraints
Eq. (
46
), since a random initial Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and
c\mathcal{H}
for any
c>0
as initial Hamiltonian will lead to the same optimal

\mathcal{K}.
(An alternative formulation of the optimization problem is in
Appendix
C
. With similar methods, we also analyze the optimization over a
quantum state rather than over the Hamiltonian Appendix
D
.)

\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=\max_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}].

IV.2
Quadratic programming with quadratic constraints

In the previous section, we considered methods that aim to maximize the quantum
Fisher information over the Hamiltonian numerically. In practice, they are very
efficient. Still, it is not certain that they find the global optimum and they
might find a smaller value. In this section, we present methods that always find
the global optimum. However, they work only for small systems. We also present
simple approximations of such methods called, relaxation. They find either the
maximum or a value larger than that. The method given in Sec.
IV.1

looking for the maximum with the see-saw and the methods discussed in this
section can be used together. The gap between the two results give information
about the location of the maximum. If the two methods find the same value, then
it is the global maximum.

The basic idea of the method of moments can be understood as follows. According
to the Shor relaxation
Burer and Ye 2020
;
*[][.OriginallypublishedinSov.J.Comput.Syst.Sci.25
;
1-11(1987).]Shor1987Quadratic

maximization of Eq. (

{\rm Tr}(RX),

(55)

where the Hermitian

X\leq\vec{v}\vec{v}^{T}.

(56)

Note that Eq. (
56
) can easily be coded in semidefinite programming. We can
also add further and further conditions on
X
that lead to lower and lower
values for the maximum. Similar relaxation techniques have been used in
entanglement theory to detect entanglement,
non-locality
Navascués et al. 2008
, to obtain the maximum of
an operator for separable states
Eisert et al. 2004
or
even to determine the ground state energy in spin one-half Heisenberg
models
Wang et al. 2023
.

For our problem, we used the YALMIP package
Löfberg 2004
and the
method of moments
Lasserre 2001

Other problems in which sums of quadratic expressions must be maximized over a convex set

In this section, we consider optimization problems in quantum information
science that can be formulated similarly to the maximization of the quantum
Fisher information.

V.1
Optimization over various subsystems alternatingly

In quantum information, see-saw methods have already been used

Werner and Wolf 2001a
;
Werner and Wolf 2001b
;
Liang and Doherty 2007
;
Pál and Vértesi 2010
;
Navascués et al. 2020
.
Problems related to entanglement in multipartite systems seem to be ideal for
such methods. For instance, a typical problem is maximizing an operator
expectation value for a product state

\varrho=\varrho_{1}\otimes\varrho_{2}.

(61)

Then, we can maximize alternatingly over
\varrho_{1}
and over
\varrho_{2}
.

V.2
Maximization of the Wigner-Yanase skew information

The Wigner-Yanase skew information is defined as
Wigner and Yanase 1963

I_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})={\rm Tr}(\mathcal{H}^{2}\varrho)-{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{H}\sqrt{\varrho}\mathcal{H}\sqrt{\varrho}).

(62)

Here, we optimize over
\mathcal{H}.
It is clear how to maximize Eq. (
62
)
with a see-saw method.

\max_{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}I_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[(\mathcal{H}\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{K}\mathcal{H})\varrho]-{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{H}\sqrt{\varrho}\mathcal{K}\sqrt{\varrho}).

(63)

Maximizing the Wigner-Yanase skew information can be used
to lower bound the quantum Fisher information based on

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]\geq 4I_{\varrho}(\mathcal{H}).

(64)

. From a comparison with
the results for the quantum Fisher information, we can see that for the last
three of the five examples, there is an equality in Eq. (

). This has
been known for the last two examples
Tóth and Vértesi 2018
;
Pál et al. 2021

V.3
Searching for the eigenstate with maximal/minimal
eigenvalue

In this section, we discuss how to look for the largest eigenvalues of a matrix
with a see-saw method similar to the one discussed in Sec.
IV.1
.
Interestingly, the see-saw is guaranteed to converge in this case.

We need the relation that converts the maximization over a quadratic form into a
maximization over a bilinear expression as

\max_{|\Psi\rangle}\langle{\Psi|A|\Psi}\rangle=\max_{|\Psi\rangle,|\Phi\rangle}\langle{\Psi|A|\Phi}\rangle.

(65)

where
A
is a Hermitian matrix and the right-hand side of Eq. (
65
) can
immediately be used to define a see-saw algorithm. We shall show that such an
algorithm is equivalent to the von Mises method (power iteration) for finding
the maximal eigenvalue of a positive self-adjoint operator.

Before presenting the see-saw, let us consider the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\langle{\Psi|A\Phi}\rangle\leq\sqrt{\langle{A\Phi|A\Phi}\rangle\langle{\Psi|\Psi}\rangle}=\sqrt{\langle{A\Phi|A\Phi}\rangle},

(66)

where we took into account that
\langle{\Psi|\Psi}\rangle=1
. The inequality is
saturated for

|\Psi\rangle=|A\Phi\rangle/\sqrt{\langle{A\Phi|A\Phi}\rangle}.

(67)

Now, the see-saw procedure is as follows:

(i) Choose some random
|\Phi\rangle
unit
vector.

(ii) Maximize over
|\Psi\rangle
and
|\Phi\rangle
alternatingly. First,
|\Psi\rangle
is chosen based on Eq. (
67
).
Then, in the alternate step, we use

|\Phi\rangle=|A\Psi\rangle/\sqrt{\langle{A\Psi|A\Psi}\rangle}.

(68)

We can now recognize the steps of the power iteration, which is known to
converge as long as the starting vector (
|\Phi\rangle
in our case) overlaps with
the eigenvector corresponding to the maximal eigenvalue of
A
.

V.4
Maximization of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm

In this section, we look at the problem of maximizing a matrix norm with a method similar to the
one discussed in Sec.
IV.1
. Let us consider the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
given in Eq. (
26
), for which the maximization can be rewritten

\max_{X\in S}||X||_{\rm HS}^{2}=\max_{X,Y\in S}{\rm Tr}\left(XY^{\dagger}\right),

(69)

where
S
is some set of matrices. A straightforward see-saw optimization can be
formulated based on Eq. (
69
). If
S
is the set of density matrices then the
maximization of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm is just the maximization of the purity

{\rm Tr}(\varrho^{2})
or the minimization of the linear entropy
S_{\rm lin}(\varrho)=1-{\rm Tr}(\varrho^{2}).

For instance, we can consider obtaining

\displaystyle\text{$\max_{{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{l}\varrho:\;{\rm Tr}(O^{(k)}\varrho)=e^{(k)},\\
k=1,2,...,M\end{array}}}$}\;{\rm Tr}(\varrho^{2}),

where
\varrho
is a density matrix,
O^{(k)}
are operators, and
e^{(k)}
are
constants. The constraints require that the expectation values of the
O^{(k)}
operators equal the cosntants
e^{(k)}.

The optimization problem will lead to pure states if there are pure
states satisfying the condition. It can be solved with the method of moments as
well as with a see-saw iteration. The see-saw method shows a rapid convergence.
In fact, it converges typically in a single step. After examining this simple
case, we can have more complicated constraints on the quantum state. For
instance, we can consider PPT states or states with a given negativity

Tóth and Vértesi 2018
.

We add that in entanglement theory, the optimization over pure product states of bipartite and
multipartite systems is an important task. This can be carried out if we require
that the reduced density matrices are pure
Eisert et al. 2004
. In this
case, the purity of the reduced states
\varrho_{n}
appears among the
constraints as

{\rm Tr}(\varrho_{n}^{2})=1.

(74)

We add that optimization over pure states and rank constrained optimization is
possible with other methods using semidefinite programming

Gross et al. 2010b
;
Yu et al. 2022

V.5
Maximization of the trace norm

Another important norm in quantum physics, the trace norm is defined as

||X||_{\rm tr}={\rm Tr}\left(\sqrt{XX^{\dagger}}\right).

(75)

Observation 5.
The trace norm of a Hermitian matrix given in
Eq. (
75
) can be maximized with a see-saw method.

Proof.
In order to obtain an optimization over a bilinear form, we use the
dual relation between the trace norm and the spectral (operator) norm

Hiai and Petz 2014
;
Bhatia 1997
, based on which

||X||_{\rm tr}=\max_{Y\in\mathbbm{R}^{m\times m}:YY^{T}\leq\openone}{\rm Tr}(X^{T}Y).

(76)

The maximization of
||X||_{\rm tr}
over a set
S
can then
be written as

\max_{X\in S}||X||_{\rm tr}=\max_{X\in S}\max_{Y\in\mathbbm{R}^{m\times m}:YY^{T}\leq\openone}{\rm Tr}(X^{T}Y),

(77)

which can be calculated by a see-saw similar to the ones discussed before. We
have to maximize
{\rm Tr}(X^{T}Y)
alternatingly by
X
and
Y.

\hfill\blacksquare

In entanglement theory, the trace norm is used to define the CCNR criterion for
entanglement detection
Rudolph 2005
;
Chen and Wu 2003

for every bipartite separable state
\varrho,
i.e., states of the form given in
Eq. (
1

||R(\varrho)||_{\rm tr}\leq 1,

(78)

where
R(\varrho)
is the realigned matrix obtained by a certain permutation of
the elements of
\varrho.
If Eq. (
78
) is violated then the state is
entangled. Clearly, the larger the left-hand side of Eq. (
78
), the larger the
violation of Eq. (
78
).

VI
Conclusions

We discussed how to maximize the metrological gain over local Hamiltonians for a bipartite quantum system. We presented methods that exploit the fact that the the quantum Fisher information can be written as a bilinear expression of the elements of the Hamiltonian, if the Hamiltonian is given in the basis of the density matrix eigenstates. We showed an iterative see-saw (ISS) method that can maximize the quantum Fisher information for large systems. We showed another method based on quadratic programming. We tested the two approaches on concrete examples. We also considered several similar problems that can be solved based on such ideas, for instance, obtaining the maximum violation of the Computable Cross Norm-Realignment (CCNR) criterion for states with a positive partial transpose. Our work can straightforwardly be generalized to multiparticle systems.

Appendix A
Special set of separable states similar to the set of local Hamiltonians

In this Appendix, we consider a set of separable states that are analogous to
local Hamiltonians, namely states of the form

\varrho=p\left(\varrho_{1}\otimes\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{2}}\right)+(1-p)\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{1}}\otimes\varrho_{2}\right)\,,

(79)

where
0\leq p\leq 1,

d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are the dimensions of the two Hilbert
spaces.

First, for completeness, we explicitly show that the set of states of the form
given in Eq. (
79
) is convex. Let

\varrho^{(k)}=p_{k}\left(\varrho_{1}^{(k)}\otimes\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{2}}\right)+(1-p_{k})\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{1}}\otimes\varrho_{2}^{(k)}\right)

(80)

for
k={\rm I,II}
, be two such states. Let us consider a density matrix being the mixture of
\varrho^{({\rm I})}
and
\varrho^{({\rm II})}

\varrho=r\varrho^{({\rm I})}+(1-r)\varrho^{({\rm II})},

(81)

where
0\leq r\leq 1.
We shall show, that there exist
\varrho_{1}
,

\varrho_{2}
and
p
such that
\varrho
can be written in the form given in Eq. (
79
). Let us
consider the unnormalized density matrices

\displaystyle\varrho_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle rp_{\rm I}\varrho_{1}^{({\rm I})}+(1-r)p_{\rm II}\varrho_{1}^{({\rm II})},

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(1-p_{\rm I})\varrho_{2}^{({\rm I})}+(1-r)(1-p_{\rm II})\varrho_{2}^{({\rm II})},

(82)

Let us also define their traces as

t_{1}={\rm Tr}(\varrho_{1}^{\prime}),\quad t_{2}={\rm Tr}(\varrho_{2}^{\prime}),

(83)

Then, with
\varrho_{1}=\varrho_{1}^{\prime}/t_{1},

\varrho_{2}=\varrho_{2}^{\prime}/t_{2},

p=t_{1}
,

\varrho
can be formally written as in Eq. (
79
). We add that
both
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
are density matrices: combinations of positive
self-adjoint matrices with positive coefficients, thus positive, and of unit
trace. What remains is to check that
t_{1}+t_{2}=1
, which holds.

Let us now consider the recovery of
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}
from a state of
the form (
79
). As a first step, the reduced density matrices
are

\displaystyle\varrho^{\rm red}_{1}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{2}\varrho=p\varrho_{1}+(1-p)\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{1}},

(84)

\displaystyle\varrho^{\rm red}_{2}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{1}\varrho=(1-p)\varrho_{2}+p\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d_{2}}.

It is possible to solve Eqs. (
84
) for
\varrho_{1}
and
\varrho_{2}

as

\displaystyle\varrho_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\varrho^{\rm red}_{1}-\frac{1-p}{d_{1}}\mathbbm{1}\right),

(85)

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-p}\left(\varrho^{\rm red}_{2}-\frac{p}{d_{2}}\mathbbm{1}\right).

However, if
p
is not known, the recovery is not unique. Eqs. (
85
) yield a solution for any
p
, for which
\varrho_{1}\geq 0

and
\varrho_{2}\geq 0
holds, constraining
p
as

1-d_{1}\sigma_{\rm min}(\varrho^{\rm red}_{1})\leq p\leq d_{2}\sigma_{\rm min}(\varrho^{\rm red}_{2}).

(86)

Note, that the bounds (
86
) also ensure that
0\leq p\leq 1
.
Based on Eq. (
86
), a necessary condition for the existence of some
allowed
p
is

1-d_{1}\sigma_{\rm min}(\varrho^{\rm red}_{1})\leq d_{2}\sigma_{\rm min}(\varrho^{\rm red}_{2}),

(87)

otherwise the state cannot be written in the form given in Eq. (
79
).
It can also happen that Eq. (
86
) allows a single
p
value. For
instance, if one of the reduced density matrices
\varrho^{\rm red}_{1}
or

\varrho^{\rm red}_{2}
is not of full rank, only
p=1
or
p=0
, respectively, is
possible.

Based on these, if a density matrix
\varrho
is given only, and one needs to
know if it is of the form (
79
), one needs to choose a parameter

p
fulfilling the bounds (
86
), calculate
\varrho_{1}
and

\varrho_{2}
, and afterwards verify, if formula (
79
) reproduces
the density matrix given. If it works for one allowed
p
value, it will work
for the others as well, thus we need to try it only once. Thus, we can always decide
whether a state is of the form Eq. (
79
), while it is not possible to
decide efficiently whether a quantum state is separable or not
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Friis et al. 2019
;
Horodecki 2021
.

Straightforward algebra shows that for states of the form Eq. (
80
) and
for traceless
A
and
B
Hermitian operators the relation

\langle{A\otimes B}\rangle=0

(88)

holds. Note that Eq. (
88
) does not mean that there are no correlations, since

\langle{A\otimes B}\rangle-\langle{A\otimes\openone}\rangle\langle{\openone\otimes B}\rangle

(89)

is not necessarily zero. Let us now consider a full basis of traceless
Hermitian matrices in the two subsystem

\{G_{1}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{s_{1}},\;\{G_{2}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{s_{2}},

(90)

with the number of elements given as

s_{n}=d_{n}^{2}-1

(91)

for
n=1,2.

We assume that the basis matrices are pairwise orthogonal to each other

{\rm Tr}(G_{n}^{(k)}G_{n}^{(l)})=2\delta_{kl},

(92)

where
n=1,2
and
\delta_{kl}
is the Kronecker symbol. Matrices
G_{1}^{(k)}
and

G_{2}^{(l)}
are the
SU(d)
generators for
d=d_{1},
and
d=d_{2},
respectively.
Then, any density matrix
\varrho
can be expressed as a linear combination as

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}\openone\otimes\openone+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\lambda_{1}^{(k)}G_{1}^{(k)}\otimes\openone

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\lambda_{2}^{(k)}\openone\otimes G_{2}^{(k)}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l}K_{k,l}G_{1}^{(k)}\otimes G_{2}^{(l)},

for
1\leq k\leq s_{1}
and
1\leq l\leq s_{2}.
Here,
\lambda_{1}^{(k)},

\lambda_{2}^{(l)},
and
K_{k,l}
are real coefficients that form the elements of
the coherence vector of the system that describes the state of the system
equivalently to the density matrix
Mahler and Weberruß 1998
. Due to Eq. (
88
),
most of the
d_{1}^{2}d_{2}^{2}-1
elements of the coherence vector are zero since

K_{k,l}=\langle{G_{1}^{(k)}\otimes G_{2}^{(l)}}\rangle=0

(94)

holds for all
k,l.
Thus, the number of nonzero elements is at most
s_{1}+s_{2}.

Appendix B
See-saw algorithm with and without semidefinite programming

In this Appendix, we discuss how to implement a single step of the ISS
method given in Sec.
IV.1
.

Based on Observation 3, we need to obtain

\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}{\rm Tr}[(Q\circ Q\circ\mathcal{H}_{\rm old})\mathcal{H}].

(95)

Here
\mathcal{H}_{\rm old}
is the previous guess or the initial random guess.
\mathcal{H}
is of the form
Eq. (
23
), where
H_{n}
fulfill Eq. (
46
) for
n=1,2.
This can
be solved by semidefinite programming.

Equation (
95
) can also be solved without semidefinite programming. The
quantity to be maximized in Eq. (
95
) can be written as

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]={\rm Tr}(W_{1}H_{1})+{\rm Tr}(W_{2}H_{2})

(96)

where
W_{n}
is given as

W_{n}={\rm Tr}_{\{1,2\}\backslash n}[(Q\circ Q\circ\mathcal{H}_{\rm old})\mathcal{H}].

(97)

Let us write down the eigendecomposition of
W_{n}
as

W_{n}=U_{n}D_{n}U_{n}^{\dagger},

(98)

where
U_{n}
is unitary and
D_{n}
is diagonal. We define the diagonal matrix

(\tilde{D}_{n})_{k,k}=c_{k}s[(D_{n})_{k,k}],

(99)

where
s(x)=1
if
x\geq 0,
and
-1
otherwise. With these the optimal
Hamiltonians are given by

H_{n}^{({\rm opt})}=U_{n}\tilde{D}_{n}U_{n}^{\dagger}

(100)

for
n=1,2.
From these, we can obtain the new
\mathcal{H}_{\rm old}
and go to the next iteration step.
Note that a similar scheme was presented in another context in
Ref.
Tóth et al. 2020
.

Appendix C
Alternative method to optimize quadratic functions

In this Appendix, we present an alternative of the method given in
Sec.
IV.1
. We need the optimization over a constrained and an
unconstrained quantity, while in the method of Sec.
IV.1
, we need to
optimize over two constrained quantities.

Let us try to maximize the convex function

f(x)=q^{2}x^{2}

(101)

on a convex set. We could check the extreme point of the set, however, we look
for a method that can easily be generalized for larger number of variables.

We introduce another function with an auxiliary variable
y
that is easier to
maximize. We can maximize
f(x)
given in Eq. (
101
) as

\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y}g(x,y),

(102)

where we define a two-variable function as

g(x,y)=4y(qx-y),

(103)

where
\mathcal{X}
is a convex set of real numbers, and
y
is real.
g(x,y)
is plotted in Fig.
4
for
q=1.

Figure 4:
Plot of
g(x,y)
as defined in Eq. (
103
) for
q=1.
In the insets, curves for
x_{0}=0
and
y_{0}=0.5
are shown on the sides, while the middle inset is for
y=(x-1)/2.
Red lines in the main figure correspond to the three insets. By inspection we can see that
g(x,y)
is not concave.

Then the maximization can be carried out as follows.

(i) Choose a random
x
.

(ii) Maximize
g(x,y)
over
y
for a fixed
x.
We have to use the condition
for maximum

\frac{\partial g(x,y)}{\partial y}=4qx-8y=0.

(104)

Hence, we have to set the auxiliary variable
y
to

y=\frac{1}{2}qx.

(105)

(iii) Maximize
g(x,y)
over
x
for a fixed
y.
The maximum will be taken at
an extreme point of
\mathcal{X}.
After completing step (iii), go to step (ii).

The function
g(x,y)
is linear in
x,
concave in
y.
The
Hessian matrix of the second order derivatives is

D=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\phantom{-}4q\\
4q&-8\end{array}\right).

(106)

The eigenvalues of the Hessian are

\lambda_{1/2}=\pm 4\sqrt{q^{2}+1}-4.

(107)

One of the eigenvalues is positive for any
q>0.
Thus,
g(x,y)
is not concave in

(x,y)
since the Hessian has positive eigenvalues. Thus, it is not guaranteed
that we will find a unique maximum of a concave function.

Let us now generalize these ideas to several variables. Let us assume that

\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
and
\varrho
is a density matrix. Then,
we can write that

\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=\max_{\mathcal{H}\in\mathcal{L}_{c_{1},c_{2}}}\max_{Y}G(\mathcal{H},Y),

(108)

where
G(\mathcal{H},Y)
is defined as

\displaystyle G(\mathcal{H},Y)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=4\sum_{kl}\left[Y^{\rm r}_{kl}(Q_{kl}H_{kl}^{\rm r}-Y_{kl}^{\rm r})+Y^{\rm i}_{kl}(Q_{kl}H_{kl}^{\rm i}-Y_{kl}^{\rm i})\right],

(109)

Note the difference compared to the bilinear form in Eq. (
52
): The
optimization over
Y_{kl}
is unconstrained.

Note that instead of Eq. (
103
), for instance, the following form is also
possible

g(x,y)=4w(y)[qx-w(y)],

(110)

where
c
is a constant and
w(y)
is a function. Then,

\frac{\partial g(x,y)}{\partial y}=4\frac{dw}{dy}\left(qx-2w\right)=0,

(111)

hence
w=\frac{1}{2}qx
and we arrive at
g=q^{2}x^{2}
analogously to Eq. (
101
).

Appendix D
Optimization over the state rather than the Hamiltonian

In this Appendix,
we will analyze the method optimizing the quantum Fisher information over the quantum state,
rather than over the Hamiltonian, described in
Ref.
Macieszczak 2013
;
Macieszczak et al. 2014b
;
Chabuda et al. 2020
.
We would like to examine whether it finds the global optimum.
It turns out that the see-saw used for optimization is similar to that of Appendix
C
.
Thus, we will apply an analysis similar to the one presented in Appendix
C
.

Let us review the method briefly.
We need to maximize the error propagation formula

\displaystyle\max_{{\varrho}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{{\varrho}}\max_{M}1/{(\Delta\theta)^{2}_{M}}

(112)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\varrho}\max_{M}{\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle^{2}}/{(\Delta M)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\varrho}\max_{M}{\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle^{2}}/{\langle{M^{2}}\rangle},

where the last equality stands, since
M
operators for which
\langle{M}\rangle_{\varrho}=0

maximize both
(\Delta M)^{2}
and
\langle{M^{2}}\rangle,
and for such operators
(\Delta M)^{2}=\langle{M^{2}}\rangle.

Then, maximizing a fraction over
M
such that
M
appears both in the numerator and the denominator is a difficult task. Thus, a variational approach has been used to obtain the optimum as
Macieszczak 2013

\displaystyle\max_{{\varrho}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\varrho}\max_{M}\max_{\alpha}\{-\alpha^{2}\langle{M^{2}}\rangle+2\alpha\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle\}.

For the maximum
Macieszczak 2013

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\left(-\alpha^{2}\langle{M^{2}}\rangle+2\alpha\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=-2\alpha\langle{M^{2}}\rangle+2\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle=0

(114)

holds. Then, for the optimum
\alpha=\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M]}\rangle/\langle{M^{2}}\rangle
and substituting this into Eq. (
), we obtain the right-hand side of Eq. (
112
). If we carry out the optimization over
\alpha
and
M
then we find that the optimum is taken when
M
equals the symmetric logarithmic derivative and
\alpha=1.

In order to decrease the number of parameters we need to optimize, the optimization in Eq. (
) can be rewritten as

\max_{{\varrho}}\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=\max_{\varrho}\max_{M^{\prime}}\{-\langle{(M^{\prime})^{2}}\rangle+2\langle{i[{\mathcal{H}},M^{\prime}]}\rangle\},

(115)

where
M^{\prime}
takes the role of
\alpha M.
Note that the function in Eq. (
115
) is concave in
M^{\prime}

and linear in
\varrho.

The expression in Eq. (
115
) can be used to define a
two-step see-saw algorithm that will find better and better quantum states.
First we choose a random state. Then, maximize the expression over
M^{\prime}.
One can show that the maximum is taken by setting
M^{\prime}
to be the symmetric logarithmic derivative given in Eq. (
15
)
Macieszczak 2013
. Then, we optimize over
\varrho.
Then, we go back to the step maximizing over
M^{\prime},
and so on. The
optimization seems to work very well, converging almost always in practice.

After reviewing the powerful method in Refs.
Macieszczak 2013
;
Macieszczak et al. 2014b
;
Chabuda et al. 2020
, the
question arises: do we always find the global maximum? For that we need that the expression maximized in Eq. (
115
)
is strictly concave in
(\varrho,M^{\prime}).

Let us see a concrete example. Let us consider a single qubit with a density
matrix

\varrho=\frac{1}{2}(\openone+\sigma_{x}x),

(116)

where the allowed values for the real parameter
x
are given by

x_{\min}\leq x\leq x_{\max},

(117)

and
[x_{\min},x_{\max}]
is a subset of the
[-1,1]
interval.
Clearly,

\langle{\sigma_{x}}\rangle_{\varrho}=x.

(118)

We will carry out an optimization of the quantum Fisher information over the convex set of states defined above. Let us now consider the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}=\sigma_{z}.

(119)

Then, the symmetric logarithmic derivative, defined in Eq. (
15
), multiplied by
\alpha
is of the form

M^{\prime}=\sigma_{y}y,

(120)

where
y
is the auxiliary, real parameter. The expression maximized in Eq. (
115
) equals

h(x,y)=-y^{2}+4xy,

(121)

which is maximized for a given
x
if
y=2x.

Then, we can eliminate
y
and obtain

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=4x^{2}.

(122)

The Hessian matrix of the second order derivatives of the expression in Eq. (
121
) is

D=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\phantom{-}4\\
4&-2\end{array}\right).

(123)

Since one of the two eigenvalues of the Hessian is negative, the other is
positive, the expression in Eq. (
121
) is not concave in
x
and
y.

In fact, Eq. (
121
) is concave in
y
and linear in
x,
but not concave in
(x,y).

The function
h(x,y)
given in Eq. (
121
) is plotted in Fig.
5
, which
makes it possible to understand the convexity properties of Eq. (
121
).
Thus, the expression maximized in Eq. (
115
) is in general not concave in
(\varrho,M^{\prime}).

Figure 5:
Plot of
h(x,y)
as defined in Eq. (
121
). In the insets, curves for
x_{0}=0
and
y_{0}=0.5
are shown on the sides, while the middle inset is for
y=x.
Red lines in the main figure correspond to the three insets. By inspection, we can see that
h(x,y)
is not concave.

Let us consider a maximization of Eq. (
121
) without further constraints such that
x_{\min}=-1
and
x_{\max}=+1.
We start from a random
\varrho,
which in our case means to start with a random
x.
Let us consider the case that
x>0.
Then, we will have
y=2x>0.
Then, when optimizing over
x
in the next step, we get
x=+1.
Let us now consider the case that
x<0.
Then, we will have
y=2x<0.
Then, when optimizing over
x,
we get
x=-1.
Thus, we will find the correct maximum, independently from the initial state. The reason is that the Fisher information is invariant under the transformation
x\rightarrow-x.

Let us now consider a single qubit in a general state

\varrho=\frac{1}{2}(\openone+r_{x}\sigma_{x}+r_{y}\sigma_{y}+r_{z}\sigma_{z}),

(124)

and a general single-qubit
M^{\prime}
as

M^{\prime}=m_{x}\sigma_{x}+m_{y}\sigma_{y}+m_{z}\sigma_{z}.

(125)

With these, the quantum Fisher information can be given as

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=\max_{m_{x},m_{y},m_{z}}4(m_{y}r_{x}-m_{x}r_{y})-m_{x}^{2}-m_{y}^{2}-m_{z}^{2}.

(126)

The maximum is taken at
m_{x}=-2r_{y},m_{y}=2r_{x},
and
m_{z}=0.

After eliminating
m_{k}
we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=4(r_{x}^{2}+r_{y}^{2}),

(127)

which takes the global maximum for all points for which
r_{x}^{2}+r_{y}^{2}=1
and
r_{z}=0.
Thus, starting from any random initial state, we reach the global maximum.

In the more general case of larger systems, the quantum Fisher information is not the quadratic function of the density matrix elements anymore. However, even in this case, if
\varrho_{\rm opt}
is maximizing the quantum Fisher information
\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}],
then
\varrho_{\rm opt}^{\prime}=\exp(-iK)\varrho_{\rm opt}\exp(+iK)
also maximizes it, where
[\mathcal{H},K]=0.
Thus, even though the final state of the iteration depends on the initial state, the algorithm finds the global maximum. We tested the optimization problem for physical quantum states of various sizes (
2\times 2
,
3\times 3
, …,
8\times 8
) and for simplicity, we used semidefinite programming to look for the maximum over
\varrho.
We found that very typically the algorithm finds the global maximum. In principle, it is possible to find an initial state for which
M^{\prime},
calculated by Eq. (
15
), equals the zero matrix, however small random added noise can easily help, and the iteration will lead to the global maximum. The optimization works well even if we consider the convex set of states passing through a physical map
Macieszczak 2013
.

Let us consider a concrete iteration on a subset of physical quantum states of the type given in Eq. (
116
), and assume
-1\leq x_{\min}<0<x_{\max}\leq 1
, which can be incorporated in the optimization as the condition

x_{\min}\leq\langle{\sigma_{x}}\rangle_{\varrho}\leq x_{\max}.

(128)

We start from a random
\varrho,
that is, we start with a random
x.
Let us consider the case that
x>0.
Then, we will have
y=2x>0.
Then, when optimizing over
x
in the next step, we get
x=x_{\max}.
Let us now consider the case that
x<0.
Then, we will have
y=2x<0.
Then, when optimizing over
x,
we get
x=x_{\min}.
Thus, depending on the sign of the random initial
x,
we will arrive at
x=x_{\min}
or at
x=x_{\max},
which is not necessarily the global optimum.

Considering a single qubit in a general state, conditions such as

\displaystyle x_{\min}\leq\langle{\sigma_{x}}\rangle_{\varrho}\leq x_{\max},

\displaystyle y_{\min}\leq\langle{\sigma_{y}}\rangle_{\varrho}\leq y_{\max},

(129)

where
y_{\min}
and
y_{\max}
are real numbers giving the bounds of the allowed region for
\langle{\sigma_{y}}\rangle_{\varrho}
, can result in reaching different local optima starting from different initial states, even if small random perturbations are added. The proof is similar to the proof of the one-dimensional case discussed before. For example if
x_{\min}=y_{\min}=-1,

x_{\max}=y_{\max}=0.15,
and the initial state is

\varrho^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\openone+0.05\sigma_{x}+0.05\sigma_{y}-0.2\sigma_{z}),

(130)

then the optimization leads to
r_{x}=x_{\max},

r_{y}=y_{\max},
and
\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho,\mathcal{H}]=0.72,
and does not reach the maximal value
4.00.

More practical, relevant subsets of physical quantum states are PPT states in a bipartite system. Metrology with such states have been studied using semidefinite programming
Tóth and Vértesi 2018
. In Ref.
Tóth and Vértesi 2018
, a family of bipartite PPT entangled states have been found numerically maximizing the quantum Fisher information. Later, PPT states having the same quantum Fisher information have been found analytically, which is a strong indication that the method found the global maximum
Pál et al. 2021
.

In multiparticle systems, one can consider states that are PPT with respect to all bipartitions. Such four-qubit states have been studied
Tóth and Vértesi 2018
. Such states form a very complicated set, and thus they are good candidates to test the method with a difficult task. We find that the maximum found in such systems might depend on the initial state. However, after starting from a couple of random initial values the iteration finds the global maximum.

In summary, in most relevant multi-qudit problems, the method seems to converge extremely well, which is very likely due to the fact that the global maximum is taken by many states. It is also important that several auxiliary variables are added and the function to be maximized is concave according to these auxiliary variables.

\displaystyle|\Phi^{\pm}\rangle
