A probabilistic computation of a Mehta integral
Liviu I. Nicolaescu

Abstract
. We use the Kac-Rice formula to compute the Mehta integral describing the normalization constant arising in the statistics of the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble.



1.
Introduction

We denote by
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
the space of real symmetric
m\times m
matrices. This is a Euclidean space with respect to the inner product
(A,B):=\tr(AB)
. This inner product is invariant with respect to the action of the orthogonal group
O(m)
on
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.

We define

\ell_{ij},\omega_{ij}:\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\to{\mathbb{R}},\;\;\ell_{ij}(A)=a_{ij},\;\;\omega_{ij}(A):=\begin{cases}a_{ij},&i=j,\\
\sqrt{2}a_{ij},&i<j.\end{cases}

(1.1)

The collection
(\omega_{ij})_{i\leq j}
defines linear coordinates on
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
that are orthonormal with respect to the above inner product on
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
. The volume density induced by this metric is

\vol\big[\,dA\,\big]:=\prod_{i\leq j}d\omega_{ij}=2^{\frac{1}{2}\binom{m}{2}}\prod_{i\leq j}d\ell_{ij}.

For any real numbers
u,v
such that

v>0,mu+2v>0,

(1.2)

we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{m}^{u,v}
the space
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
equipped with the centered Gaussian measure
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{u,v}\big[\,dA\,\big]
uniquely determined by the covariance equalities

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\ell_{ij}(A)\ell_{k\ell}(A)\,\big]=u\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell}+v(\delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell}+\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}),\;\;\forall 1\leq i,j,\;k,\ell\leq m.

(1.3)

In particular we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\ell_{ii}^{2}\,\big]=u+2v,\;\;{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\ell_{ii}\ell_{jj}\,\big]=u,\;\;\;{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\ell_{ij}^{2}\,\big]=v,\;\;\forall 1\leq i\neq j\leq m,

(1.4)

while all other covariances are trivial. The ensemble
\mathcal{S}^{0,v}
is a rescaled version of the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) and we will refer to it as
\GOE_{m}^{v}
. The inequalities (
1.2
) guarantee that the covariance form defined by (
1.3
) is positive definite so that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{u,v}
is nondegenerate.

For
u>0
the ensemble
\mathcal{S}_{m}^{u,v}
can be given an alternate description. More precisely a random
A\in\mathcal{S}_{m}^{u,v}
can be described as a sum

A=B+\ X{\mathbbm{1}}_{m},\;\;B\in\GOE_{m}^{v},\;\;X\in\boldsymbol{N}(0,u),\;\;\mbox{ $B$ and $X$ independent}.

We write this

\mathcal{S}_{m}^{u,v}=\GOE_{m}^{v}\hat{+}N(0,u){\mathbbm{1}}_{m},

(1.5)

where
\hat{+}
indicates a sum of
independent
variables.

In the special case
\GOE_{m}^{v}
we have
u=0
and

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0,v}\big[\,dA\,\big]=\frac{1}{(4\pi v)^{\frac{m(m+1)}{4}}}e^{-\frac{1}{4v}\tr A^{2}}\vol\big[\,dA\,\big].

(1.6)

Note that
\GOE_{m}^{1/2}
corresponds to the Gaussian measure on
\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
canonically associated to the inner product
(A,B)=\tr(AB)
.

We have a
Weyl integration formula

[
2
]
which states that if
f:\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a measurable function which is invariant under conjugation, then the value
f(A)
at
A\in\Sym({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
depends only on the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(A)
of
A
and we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\GOE_{m}^{v}}\big[\,f(X)\,\big]=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m})\underbrace{\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{4v}}}_{=:Q_{m,v}(\lambda)}\;|d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}|,

(1.7)

where the normalization constant
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)
is defined by

{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\prod_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{4v}}|d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}|

=(2v)^{\frac{m(m+1)}{4}}\times\underbrace{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\prod_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{2}}|d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}|}_{=:\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}}.

The integral
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
is usually referred to as
Mehta’s integral
. Its value was first determined in 1960 by M. L. Mehta,
[
8
]
. Later Mehta observed that this integral was known earlier to N. G. de Brujin
[
4
]
. It was subsequently observed that Mehta’s integral is a limit of the
Selberg integrals
,
[
2
, Eq. (2.5.11)]
,
[
6
, Sec. 4.7.1]
. More precisely, we have

\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}=(2\pi)^{\frac{m}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j+3}{2})}{\Gamma(3/2)}=2^{\frac{3m}{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\Gamma\Big(\,\frac{j+3}{2}\,\Big).

(1.8)

The goal of this note is to provide a probabilistic proof of (
1.8
).

We will determine
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
inductively by computing explicitly the ratios
\frac{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}}
,
\forall m\geq 1
and observing by immediate direct computation that

\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt=(2\pi)^{1/2}.

Here is the strategy. Any symmetric
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrix
A
determines a function on the unit sphere
S^{m}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}

\Phi_{A}:S^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}},\;\;\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\,A{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\big),

where
(-,-)
is the canonical inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}
. When
A\in\GOE_{m+1}^{v}
, then with probability
1
the matrix
A
is simple and the Gaussian random function
\Phi_{A}
is Morse.

To the random matrix
A\in\GOE_{m+1}^{v}
we can associate two random measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
. The first is the
spectral measure

{\sigma}_{A}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Spec(A)}\delta_{\lambda},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
concentrated at
x
. The second one is the
discriminant measure

\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=\sum_{\nabla\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0}\delta_{2\Phi_{A}(x)}.

The critical values of
2\Phi_{A}
are precisely the eigenvalues of
A
and the critical points are the unit eigenvectors of
A
. The function is Morse iff
A
is simple, i.e., its eigenvalues are distinct. In this case to each critical value of
A
there corresponds exactly two critical points. With probability
1
we have

\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=2\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}.

Then for any Borel subset
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{D}_{A}\big[\,C\,\big]\,\big]=2{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}\big[\,C\,\big]\,\big].

(1.9)

In particular

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{D}_{A}\big[\,{\mathbb{R}}\,\big]\,\big]=2{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}\big[\,{\mathbb{R}}\,\big]\,\big]=2(m+1).

(1.10)

Using the Kac-Rice formula we will be able to express
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{D}_{A}\big[\,{\mathbb{R}}\,\big]\,\big]
as an
explicit
multiple of the ratio
\frac{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}}
.

Here is the structure of the paper. Section
2
contains several probabilistic digressions. The first one concerns the expectation of the absolute value of characteristic polynomial of a random matrix
A\in\GOE
. The second one describes a version of the Kac-Rice formula needed in the proof. The last digression of this section is a well known classical result commonly referred to as the Gaussian regression formula. We give a coordinate free description of this result not readily available in traditional probabilistic sources, but very convenient to use in geometric applications. Then last section provides the details of the strategy outlined above.

2.
Probabilistic digressions

For any positive integer
n
we define the
normalized

1
-point correlation function
\rho_{n,v}(x)
of
\GOE_{n}^{v}
to be

\rho_{n,v}(x)=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{n}(v)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}Q_{n,v}(x,\lambda_{2},\dotsc,\lambda_{n})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{n}.

For any Borel measurable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
we have
[
5
, §4.4]

\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{\GOE_{n}^{v}}\big[\,\tr f(X)\,\big]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(\lambda)\rho_{n,v}(\lambda)d\lambda.

(2.1)

The equality (
2.1
) characterizes
\rho_{n,v}
. We want to draw attention to a confusing situation in the existing literature on the subject. Some authors, such as M. L. Mehta
[
9
]
, define the
1
-point correlation function
R_{n}(x)
by the equality

{\mathbb{E}}_{\GOE_{n}^{1/2}}\big[\,\tr f(X)\,\big]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(\lambda)R_{n}(\lambda)d\lambda.

The expected value of the absolute value of the determinant of of a random
A\in\GOE_{m}^{v}
can be expressed neatly in terms of the correlation function
\rho_{m+1,v}
. More precisely, we have the following result first observed by Y.V. Fyodorov
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
v>0
. Then for any
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\GOE_{m}^{v}}\big[\,|\det(A-c{\mathbbm{1}}_{m})|\,\big]=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}(v)}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\rho_{m+1,v}(c)=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}(2v)^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}}\rho_{m+1,v}(c).

(2.2)

Proof.

Using Weyl’s integration formula we deduce

{\mathbb{E}}_{\GOE_{m}^{v}}\big[\,|\det(A-c{\mathbbm{1}}_{m})|\,\big]=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{4v}}|c-\lambda_{i}|\prod_{i\leq j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}

=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{-\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{4v}}|c-\lambda_{i}|\prod_{i\leq j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}

=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}(v)}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}(v)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}Q_{m+1,v}(c,\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{m}

=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}(v)}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}(v)}\rho_{m+1,v}(c)=\frac{e^{\frac{c^{2}}{4v}}(2v)^{\frac{m+1}{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m+1}}{\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}}\rho_{m+1,v}(c).

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

We will need a special version of the Kac-Rice formula. Let
(M,g)
be a compact Riemann manifold. Denote by
\vol_{g}[-]
the volume element on
M
determined by
g
and
\nabla^{g}
the Levi-Civita connection of
g
. If
F\in C^{2}(M)
, then we define the Hessian of
F
at
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in M
to be the linear operator

\Hess_{F}({\boldsymbol{p}}):T_{\boldsymbol{p}}M\to T_{\boldsymbol{p}}M,\;\;\Hess_{F}({\boldsymbol{p}})X=\nabla^{g}_{X}\nabla F,

where
\nabla^{g}F
is the metric gradient of
F
.

Suppose that
F:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Morse function. For any subset
S\subset M
we denote by
Z(S,dF)
the number of critical points of
F
inside
S
and
B
is an open subset We denote by
\mathcal{D}(F)
the
discriminant set
of
F
, i.e., the set of critical values of
F
. The
discriminant measure
of
F
is the pushforward

\boldsymbol{D}_{F}=\sum_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}Z(F^{-1}(t),dF)\delta_{t}.

The discriminant measure is concentrated on
\mathcal{D}(F)
. For
{\varphi}\in C^{0}_{\mathrm{cpt}}({\mathbb{R}})
we set

\boldsymbol{D}_{F}\big[\,{\varphi}\,\big]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\varphi}d\boldsymbol{D}_{F}.

When
F
is random,
\boldsymbol{D}_{F}\big[\,{\varphi}\,\big]
is a random variable. We have the following result
[
1
, Thm. 12.4.1]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that
F:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
C^{2}
Gaussian random function function satisfying the ampleness condition

for any
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in M
the Gaussian vector
F({\boldsymbol{p}})\oplus dF({\boldsymbol{p}})\in T^{*}_{\boldsymbol{p}}M
is nondegenerate.

We denote by
{{\mathbb{P}}}_{F(p)}
the probability distribution of the random variable
F({\boldsymbol{p}})
and by
p_{dF(p)}
the probability
density
of the Gaussian vector
dF({\boldsymbol{p}})
.

Then
F
is
\mathrm{a.s.}
Morse and, for any function
{\varphi}\in C^{0}_{\mathrm{cpt}}({\mathbb{R}})
we have

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{D}_{F}[{\varphi}]\,\big]\hskip 341.43306pt\\
=\int_{M}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\big|\,\det\Hess_{F}({\boldsymbol{p}})\,\big|\,\|\,dF({\boldsymbol{p}})=0,\,F({\boldsymbol{p}})=t\,\big]{\varphi}(t){{\mathbb{P}}}_{F({\boldsymbol{p}})}\big[\,dt\,\big]\right)p_{dF({\boldsymbol{p}})}(0)\vol_{g}\big[\,d{\boldsymbol{p}}\,\big]\\
=\int_{M}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\big|\,\det\Hess_{F}({\boldsymbol{p}})\,\big|{\varphi}\big(\,F({\boldsymbol{p}})\,\big)\,\|\,dF({\boldsymbol{p}})=0\,\big].\hskip 142.26378pt\end{split}

(2.3)

Above,
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,-\,\|\,-\,\big]
denotes appropriate conditional expectations.

When applying the Kac-Rice formula we need to evaluate certain conditional expectations. In the Gaussian case this is readily achieved using the classical Gaussian regression formula. In the remainder of this section we describe this Gaussian regression in a form convenient in geometric applications.

Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are finite dimensional vector spaces. Consider two random vectors

X:(\Omega,\mathcal{S},{{\mathbb{P}}})\to{\boldsymbol{X}},\;\;Y:(\Omega,\mathcal{S},{{\mathbb{P}}})\to{\boldsymbol{Y}},

where
(\Omega,\mathcal{S},{{\mathbb{P}}}))
is a probability space. The mean or expectation of
X
is the vector

m(X)={\mathbb{E}}\big[\,X\,\big]=\int_{\Omega}X(\omega){{\mathbb{P}}}\big[\,d\omega\,\big]\in{\boldsymbol{X}},

whenever the integral is well defined. The random vector
X
is called centered if
m(X)=0
.

The
covariance form
of
Y
and
X
is the bilinear form

\cov\big[\,Y,X\,\big]:{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{*}\times{\boldsymbol{X}}^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}

given by

\cov\big[\,Y,X\,\big](\eta,\xi)=\cov\big[\,\langle\eta,Y\rangle,\langle\xi,X\rangle\,\big],\;\;\forall\eta\in{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{*},\;\xi\in{\boldsymbol{X}}^{*}.

If
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are equipped with inner products
(-,-)_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and respectively
(-,-)_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, then we can identify
\cov\big[\,Y,X\,\big]
with a linear operator
C_{Y,X}:{\boldsymbol{X}}\to{\boldsymbol{Y}}
. Concretely, if
({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(\boldsymbol{f}_{j})_{j\in J}
are
orthonormal
bases of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and respectively
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, and we set
X_{i}:=({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i},X)_{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
Y_{j}:=(\boldsymbol{f}_{j},Y)_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, then in these bases the operator
C_{Y,X}
is described by matrix
(c_{ji})_{(j,i)\in J\times I}
, where
c_{ji}:=\cov\big[\,Y_{j},X_{i}\,\big]
. Hence

C_{Y,X}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}=\sum_{j}c_{ji}\boldsymbol{f}_{j}.

We will refer to
C_{Y,X}
as the
correlator
of
Y
with
X
. Some times, for typographical reasons, we will use the alternate notation
\cor\big[\,Y,X\,\big]:=C_{Y,X}
.

The variance operator of
X
is
\var\big[\,X\,\big]:=C_{X,X}
. We say that
X
is nondegenerate if its variance operator is invertible. Observe that
C_{X,Y}:{\boldsymbol{Y}}\to{\boldsymbol{X}}
is the adjoint of
C_{Y,X}
,
C_{X,Y}=C_{Y,X}^{*}
.

If
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are equipped with inner products, then
{\boldsymbol{X}}\oplus{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is equipped with the direct sum of these inner products and in this case
\var\big[\,X\oplus Y\,\big]:{\boldsymbol{X}}\oplus{\boldsymbol{Y}}\to{\boldsymbol{X}}\oplus{\boldsymbol{Y}}
admits the block decomposition

\Var\big[\,X\oplus Y\,\big]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\var\big[\,X\,\big]&C_{X,Y}\\
C_{Y,X}&\var\big[\,Y\,\big]\end{array}\right].

The random vectors
X,Y
are said to be
jointly Gaussian
if the random vector
X\oplus Y
is Gaussian.

Proposition 2.3

(Gaussian regression formula)
.

Suppose that
X,Y
are Gaussian vectors valued in the Euclidean spaces
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and respectively
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
. Assume additionally that

(i)

the random vectors
X,Y
are jointly Gaussian and,

(ii)

X
is nondegenerate.

Define the
regression operator

R_{Y,X}:{\boldsymbol{X}}\to{\boldsymbol{Y}},\;\;R_{Y,X}:=C_{Y,X}\var[X]^{-1}

(2.4)

Then the following hold.

(a) The conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,Y\,\|\,X\,\big]
is the Gaussian vector described by the linear regression formula

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,Y\,\|\,X\,\big]=m(Y)-R_{Y,X}m(X)+R_{Y,X}X.

(2.5)

(b) For any
x\in X

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,Y\big|\,X=x\,\big]=m(Y)-R_{Y,X}m(X)+R_{Y,X}x.

(c) The random vector vector
Z=Y-{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,Y\,\|\,X\,\big]
is Gaussian and independent of
X
. It has mean
0
and variance operator

\Delta_{Y,X}=\Var\big[\,Y\,\big]-D_{Y,X}:{\boldsymbol{Y}}\to{\boldsymbol{Y}},\;\;D_{Y,X}=C_{Y,X}\Var[X]^{-1}C_{X,Y}.

(2.6)

Moreover, for any bounded measurable function
f:{\boldsymbol{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and any
x\in{\boldsymbol{X}}
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,f(Y)\big|\,X=x\,\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[\,f\big(\,Z+m(Y)-R_{Y,X}m(X)+R_{Y,X}x\,\big)\,\big].

(2.7)

In particular, if
X
and
Y
are centered we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,f(Y)\big|\,X=x\,\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[\,f\big(\,Z+R_{Y,X}x\,\big)\,\big].

(2.8)

For a proof we refer to
[
3
, Prop. 2.1]
.

3.
The computation of the Mehta integral

As explained in the introduction, when
A
runs in the Gaussian ensemble
\GOE_{m+1}^{v}
we obtain a Gaussian function

\Phi=\Phi_{A}:S^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}.

This function is invariant under the natural
O(m+1)
-action on
S^{m}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The Gaussian function
\Phi_{A}
is
\mathrm{a.s.}
Morse.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the Gaussian section
\nabla\Phi_{A}
of
TS^{m}
i satisfies the ampleness condition (
2.2
). Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in S^{m}
. If
\Proj_{\boldsymbol{x}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}
the orthogonal projection onto
T_{\boldsymbol{x}}S^{m}
, then

\nabla\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\Proj_{\boldsymbol{x}}A{\boldsymbol{x}}=A{\boldsymbol{x}}-(A{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{x}}.

The map

\Sym_{m+1}({\mathbb{R}})\ni A\mapsto A{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}

is onto and thus the map

\Sym_{m+1}({\mathbb{R}})\ni A\mapsto\Proj_{\boldsymbol{x}}A{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{\boldsymbol{x}}S^{m}

is also onto, thus proving that the gradient
\nabla\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is nondegenerate since the Gaussian ensemble
\GOE_{m+1,v}
is nondegenerate.

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

The spectral measure of
A
is

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}:=\sum_{\lambda\in\Spec(A)}\mult(\lambda)\delta_{\lambda}.

The discriminant measure of
\Phi_{A}
is

\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=\sum_{\nabla\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0}\delta_{2\Phi_{A}(x)}.

With probability
1
we have
\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=2\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}
. Then for any Borel subset
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\frac{1}{(m+1)}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{D}_{A}\big[\,C\,\big]\,\big]=\frac{2}{m+1}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{A}\big[\,C\,\big]\,\big]=2\int_{C}\rho_{m+1,v}(\lambda)d\lambda.

(3.1)

Using the Kac-Rice formula (
2.3
) we will give an alternate description of the left-hand-side of the above equality. We will need to describe explictly the integrand in this formula.

For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in S^{m}
we denote by
\Hess_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})
the Hessian of
\Phi_{A}
at
{\boldsymbol{x}}
viewed as a symmetric operator
T_{\boldsymbol{x}}S^{m}\to T_{\boldsymbol{x}}S^{m}
.

Denote by
(x^{0},x^{1},\dotsc,x^{m})
the canonical Euclidean coordinates on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}
. Since
\Phi_{A}
is
O(m+1)
invariant, the distribution of
\Hess_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is independent of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
so it suffices to determine it at any point of our choosing. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the north pole

{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{n}=(1,0,\dotsc,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m+1}

Then
T_{\boldsymbol{n}}S^{m}=\{x^{0}=0\}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}:=\big(\,x^{1},\dotsc,x^{m})
are orthonormal coordinates on
T_{\boldsymbol{n}}S^{m}
. The coordinates
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}
also define local coordinates on
S^{m}
. More precisely, the upper hemisphere

S^{m}_{+}:=\big\{\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\in S^{m};\;\;x^{0}>0\,\big\}

admits the parametrization

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{x}}\big(\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\,\big)=\big(\,x^{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}),{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\,\big)\in S^{m},\;\;x^{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{*})=\sqrt{1-\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\|^{2}}.

The round metric on
S^{m}
satisfies

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O\big(\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\|^{2}\,\big)\;\;\mbox{near $\boldsymbol{n}$}.

(3.2)

On the upper hemisphere we will view
\Phi_{A}
as a function of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}
.

If
A=(a_{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq m}
, then in the coordinates
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}
we have

\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{2}a_{00}\big(\,1-\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\|^{2}\,\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{jj}(x^{j})^{2}+\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq m}a_{jk}x^{j}x^{k},

=\frac{1}{2}a_{00}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(\,a_{jj}-a_{00}\,\big)(x^{j})^{2}+\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq m}a_{jk}x^{j}x^{k},

\nabla\Phi_{A}(\boldsymbol{n})=d\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{*})|_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}=0}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{0j}dx^{j}.

Since
A\in\GOE_{m+1}^{v}
, covariance kernel of
\Phi_{A}
is

\mathcal{K}_{A}(\boldsymbol{n},{\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathbb{E}}\big[\,\Phi_{A}(\boldsymbol{n})\Phi_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,\big]=\frac{1}{4}\big(\,1-\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\|^{2}\,\big){\mathbb{E}}\big[\,a_{00}^{2}\,\big]=\frac{v}{2}\big(\,1-\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{*}\|^{2}\,\big).

Denote by
A_{*}
the
