Numerical analysis of a FE/SAV scheme for a Caginalp phase field model with mechanical effects in stereolithography
Xingguang Jin, Kei Fong Lam, Changqing Ye

Abstract
In this work we propose a phase field model based on a Caginalp system with mechanical effects to study the underlying physical and chemical processes behind stereolithography, which is an additive manufacturing (3D printing) technique that builds objects in a layer-by-layer fashion by using an ultraviolet laser to solidify liquid polymer resins. Existence of weak solutions is established by demonstrating the convergence of a numerical scheme based on a first order scalar auxiliary variable temporal discretization and a finite element spatial discretization. We further establish uniqueness and regularity of solutions, as well as optimal error estimates for the Caginalp system that are supported by numerical simulations. We also present some qualitative two-dimensional simulations of the stereolithography processes captured by the model.



Keywords:
Stereolithography, 3D printing, scalar auxiliary variable, Caginalp phase field system, finite element discretization, convergence analysis, error analysis

35K35, 35K58, 65M12, 65M15, 74B05

Introduction

In this work we study the following system of equations posed on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
and for a fixed terminal time
T>0
:

\displaystyle\alpha\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle=\lambda\varepsilon\Delta\varphi-\lambda\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(\varphi)-\gamma(\theta-\theta_{c})p(\varphi)

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\delta\partial_{t}\theta

\displaystyle=\gamma p(\varphi)\partial_{t}\varphi+\Delta\theta

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\,(\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)+\beta(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathbb{I}))

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega_{T},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi=\partial_{\bm{n}}\theta=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\varphi(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(\cdot),\hskip 10.00002pt\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

(1.1e)

In the above the primary variables of the model are
\varphi
(the phase field variable),
\theta
(the temperature) and
\bm{u}
(the elastic displacement). With
\bm{n}
as the outer unit normal of
\partial\Omega
we denote by
\partial_{\bm{n}}
the outward normal derivative, i.e.,
\partial_{\bm{n}}f=\nabla f\cdot\bm{n}
. The parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\delta
,
\varepsilon
,
\lambda
and
\theta_{c}
are fixed positive constants, and the model (
1.1
) consists of a phase field system for
(\varphi,\theta)
that resembles the Caginalp model
[
10
]
, coupling with a quasi-static linearized elasticity system. In (
1.1a
),
W^{\prime}
is the derivative of a double well potential
W
, while
p=P^{\prime}
is the derivative of a non-negative and bounded function
P
. In the setting where
\gamma=0
,
\alpha=\varepsilon
and
\lambda=1
this reduces to the familiar Allen–Cahn equation:

\varepsilon\partial_{t}\varphi=\varepsilon\Delta\varphi-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(\varphi),

while in the setting where
p(\varphi)=1
,
\theta_{c}=\alpha=\delta=0
and
\gamma=\lambda=1
, we obtain the Cahn–Hilliard equation from (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) with
-\theta
playing the role of the associated chemical potential for
\varphi
:

\partial_{t}\varphi=-\Delta\theta,\hskip 10.00002pt-\theta=-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(\varphi).

The classical example for
W
is the quartic potential
W(s)=\frac{1}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2}
, so that
W^{\prime}(s)=s^{3}-s
. In (
1.1c
), the quantity
\mathcal{E}(\bm{u}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}+(\nabla\bm{u})^{\top})
is the symmetric strain tensor,
\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)
is an eigenstrain,
\mathbb{I}
is the second order identity tensor, and
\mathbb{C}(\varphi)
is a symmetric and positive definite fourth order elasticity tensor depending on
\varphi
. We furnish (
1.1a
)-(
1.1c
) with the boundary conditions (
1.1d
) (homogeneous Neumann for
\varphi
and
\theta
, and homogeneous Dirichlet for
\bm{u}
) and initial conditions (
1.1e
).

We propose the system (
1.1
) as a phenomenological description for the physical processes behind stereolithography, which is an additive manufacturing (also colloquially known as 3D printing) technique that utilizes ultraviolet lasers to cure/solidify photosensitive liquid polymer resin in order to build objects and products in a layer-by-layer fashion. Despite being one of the earliest forms of additive manufacturing, stereolithography still remains a popular choice among modern practitioners to fabricate complex geometric shapes in an inexpensive, rapid and scalable fashion. However, much of the technological expertise and operating procedures are based on empirical experiments and work experience, while the understanding of the underlying physical and chemical changes behind the curing process remained incomplete. These mechanisms are instrumental in improving the product quality and printing precision in order to address some of the challenges preventing additive manufacturing as a whole into integrating with existing manufacturing infrastructures, see e.g. the review article
[
1
]
for more details.

In the literature there have been several contributions on the development of mathematical models for stereolithography, all of which have the common feature that decompose the physical and chemical processes involved into multiple submodels that are consecutively coupled. We provide a derivation of our model (
1.1
) in Section
2
and a comparison with several previous approaches. Our proposal to use a phase field model (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) to encode the evolution of the curing process, similarly done in
[
47
]
, is motivated from viewing the photopolymerization
of the liquid polymer akin to that of solidification in material sciences. Indeed, the liquid polymers polymerized into a solid state only when a critical temperature is exceeded, and mathematically this can be captured with models such as the classical Stefan problem employing a sharp interface free boundary description, or by the Caginalp model (and by close association also the Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard equations) employing a diffuse interface description. Included in (
1.1a
) is a phenomenological term
\gamma(\theta-\theta_{c})p(\varphi)
that allows for a mechanism to determine the energetically favorable phase based on the value of the temperature. We then coupled (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) to a quasi-static linear elastic system (
1.1c
) to model the build up of mechanical properties of the cured polymers.

The main contribution of this work is the proposal and analysis of a fully discrete numerical scheme for (
1.1
) based on finite element (FE) spatial discretization and the recently popularized scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) time discretization approach
[
36
,
37
]
. In order to achieve unconditional numerical stability in the presence of the nonlinear term
W^{\prime}(\varphi)
in (
1.1a
), various approaches have been proposed by many authors, among which we mention the convex-concave splitting approach
[
15
,
16
]
(resulting in nonlinear discrete systems), the stabilized linearly implicit approach
[
38
]
(requiring large stabilization parameters and modified potential function), the Lagrange multiplier approach
[
22
]
, the invariant energy quadratization approach
[
12
]
and the scalar auxiliary variable approach
[
36
,
37
]
. These aforementioned methods are applicable to a large class of equations arising as gradient flows of appropriate energy functionals, and we choose the SAV approach primarily due to its implementational advantage where advancing to the next time iteration for (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
) requires only solving linear systems rather than employing complicated Newton iterations.

Our numerical analysis demonstrates the convergence of discrete solutions to a weak solution of (
1.1
) as the discretization parameters tend to zero. We additionally establish uniqueness and regularity of the continuous solution, thereby providing a well-posedness result for our proposed model. This is in contrast to previous works for stereolithography where the well-posedness of the models were not addressed. As a consequence, for the Caginalp submodel we can derive optimal error estimates
for our numerical scheme without assuming additional smoothness of the exact solutions. For related works on the numerical analysis of the SAV approach applied to Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard type systems we point to
[
26
,
31
,
35
]
for convergence of discrete solutions to weak solutions, and to
[
11
,
23
,
29
,
35
,
46
,
45
]
for error estimates under additional smoothness of the exact solutions.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in Section

we provide a derivation of (
1.1
) and give a comparison with existing models in the literature proposed for stereolithography. The fully discrete numerical scheme is introduced in Section
3

.

2
Model derivation

Consider a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing a partially constructed object positioned on a platform surrounded by a viscous liquid resin. The object is made of the same type of material as the resin, but is fully cured (solidified). An ultraviolet laser positioned outside the domain
\Omega
serves to trace out the design of the printed object layer-by-layer. The energy from the laser induces a polymerization reaction that causes the exposed liquid resin to transition to a cured phase. After a layer has been created on top of the existing object, the platform which it rests on is then lowered, and a re-coating blade moves across the surface to level the viscous resin covering the newly cured layer. The laser then traces out the next layer and the process repeats until the final layer is built. The schematics is summarized in Figure
1
and more details can be found in
[
5
]
.

Figure 1
:
Section view of the printing process involved in stereolithography. An object is printed layer by layer on an adjustable platform submerged in a vat of liquid resin. The resin hardens when struck by an ultraviolet laser positioned outside the vat, and the platform lowers in order to harden the next layer of resin directly on top of the previous one.

The multiphysical nature of stereolithography has led to a modeling approach coupling individual physical submodels for each of the following four processes: (i) the irradiation and absorption of the laser energy; (ii) the conversion of liquid monomers to solid polymers via polymerization; (iii) the propagation of heat from polymerization; and (iv) the build up of mechanical properties during the curing process.

2.1
Laser irradiation

As the laser is positioned outside the domain
\Omega
, it is sufficient to focus on the attenuation (gradual reduction of intensity) inside the domain. Many models have been proposed, see e.g.
[
5
,
14
,
20
,
25
,
33
,
34
,
43
]
and the references cited therein. While this part is not the main focus of the present work, for convenience let us provide the simplest description. We use the notation that the top layer of the three-dimensional domain where the laser hits the resin is
\{z=0\}
. Assuming a Gaussian beam profile and using the Beer–Lambert law for the laser attenuation, the laser intensity
I
can be modeled as

I(\bm{x},t)=I_{0}(t)\exp\Big(\frac{-(x^{2}+y^{2})}{w_{0}^{2}}\Big)\exp\Big(\frac{z}{D_{p}}\Big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\bm{x}=(x,y,z)\in\Omega,\,t\geq 0,

with incident laser intensity
I_{0}(t)
as a function of time, beam radius
w_{0}
and penetration depth
D_{p}>0
. Refraction effects can be neglected as the distance between the resin surface
\{z=0\}
and the submerged object is typically small. Further extensions can be found in
[
5
, Chap. 8.2]
for refraction effects, in
[
44
]
for scattering and diffraction effects, and in
[
9
]
for diffusion and absorption effects. In this work we assume the laser intensity
I
is a prescribed function and enters into the next submodel via a heat source.

2.2
Phase field model for polymerization and heat propagation

In many works, a full reaction kinetic description employing systems of coupled ordinary differential equations or reaction-diffusion equations has been used for the photopolymerization of monomers in Part (ii), see e.g.
[
5
,
20
,
27
,
43
,
44
]
, in order to account for the evolution of the concentrations of different monomers and radicals (molecules with at least one unpaired valence electron) during the polymerization process. The key variable that quantifies the amount of monomer conversion to polymer is the
degree of conversion

[
43
]
, defined as the relative difference between the initial and current concentrations of monomers. However, the complexity of these types of model increases with the number of distinct monomer and radical species, and from a measurement viewpoint, accurately quantifying individual monomer and radical concentrations can be difficult in experimental settings.

Hence, we follow
[
14
,
47
]
and propose a phenomenological model that encodes the degree of conversion without accessing individual species concentrations. The degree of conversion and the status of polymerization can be implicitly summarized by the location of the interfaces between the liquid resin (sol phase) and the cured resin (gel phase), whose evolution can be tracked and measured in more accessible ways
[
34
]
.

In Part (iii), heat propagation during the polymerization process is often modeled using a heat equation with source term depending on the time evolution of the degree of conversion
[
14
,
20
,
44
]
. We take a slightly different approach by combining Parts (ii) and (iii) into one coupled model, where the primary variables are the phase field variable
\varphi
representing the degree of conversion and a temperature variable
\theta
. We propose an energy functional of the form

\mathbb{E}(\varphi,\theta):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}W(\varphi)-\frac{\delta}{2}|\theta|^{2}+\gamma P(\varphi)(\theta-\theta_{c})\,\mathrm{dx}

where the first two terms constitute the Ginzburg–Landau functional involving a non-negative potential
W
with two equal minima at
\pm 1
and a constant surface tension coefficient
\lambda>0
. We make use of the well-known behavior that for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, minimizers of the Ginzburg–Landau functional attain values close to the stable constant minima
\pm 1
of
W
and transition smoothly from one value to the another in thin layers whose thickness scales with
\varepsilon
. Thus, it is expected that the model develops large regions in
\Omega
where
\varphi
is close to
\pm 1
, which allows us to associate the sol phase as the region where
\{\varphi\sim-1\}
, and the gel phase as the region where
\{\varphi\sim 1\}
. In particular,
\varphi
can be interpreted as the difference in the volume fractions of the gel phase and of the sol phase.

In the third term of
E
the constant
\delta
corresponds to the specific heat coefficient, and we proposed the fourth term in order to capture the following behavior
[
47
]
: The gel phase is energetically preferable when the temperature
\theta
exceeds a constant critical temperature
\theta_{c}
, while the sol phase is preferred when
\theta<\theta_{c}
. Together with a function
P:[-1,1]\to[0,\infty)
that has a maximum at
s=-1
and a minimum at
s=1
, we see that this behavior is captured when we try to minimize the fourth term in
E
. Two examples satisfying the requirements are
P(s)=\frac{1}{2}(1-s)
and
P(s)=\frac{1}{4}(1-s)^{2}(2+s)
for
s\in[-1,1]
with constant extensions
P(s)=1
for
s<-1
and
P(s)=0
for
s>1
. Furthermore, the constant
\gamma
corresponds to the latent heat coefficient.

Based on the energy
E
, the evolution of
\varphi
can be obtained with a non-conserving gradient flow:

\alpha\partial_{t}\varphi=-\frac{\delta E}{\delta\varphi}=\lambda\varepsilon\Delta\varphi-\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon}W^{\prime}(\varphi)-\gamma(\theta-\theta_{c})p(\varphi),

where
\alpha>0
and
p(s)=P^{\prime}(s)
. For the temperature, we assume an isobaric (constant pressure) process, and the change in the enthalpy
H:=-\frac{\delta E}{\delta\theta}=\delta\theta-\gamma P(\varphi)
is captured by the balance law

\partial_{t}H=\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}+f

with thermal flux
\bm{q}
and heat source
f
. We take Fourier’s law
\bm{q}=-\nabla\theta
which leads to

\delta\partial_{t}\theta-\gamma p(\varphi)\partial_{t}\varphi=\Delta\theta+f.

The term
p(\varphi)\partial_{t}\varphi
can be interpreted as the curing rate
[
14
]
. This leads to the submodel (
1.1a
)-(
1.1b
), which is also known in the phase field literature as the Caginalp model
[
10
]
when
p(s)=1
. Note that we can incorporate the laser intensity from Part (i) into the heat source
f
as a prescribed function, see e.g.
[
44
]
. For the subsequent mathematical analysis we set
f=0
for simplicity.

2.3
Mechanical effects

For Part (iv), it is assumed that mechanical properties only develop for the gel phase, and mechanical stresses do not influence the polymerization process in Parts (ii) and (iii), see also Remark
2.1
below. However, in order to obtain a non-degenerate system of equations amenable to further analysis, we make use of the ersatz material approach in phase field-based structural topology optimization, see e.g.
[
7
,
8
,
40
,
42
]
, which treats the sol phase as a very soft elastic material, so that we can define a displacement vector
\bm{u}
over the entirety of
\Omega
.

Similar to
[
14
,
44
,
47
]
we decompose the total strain tensor
\bm{e}
into a sum of an infinitesimal linear elastic strain
\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{u}+(\nabla\bm{u})^{\top})
, a thermal strain
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}:=\beta(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathbb{I}
with identity tensor
\mathbb{I}
, initial temperature
\theta_{0}
and thermal expansion coefficient
\beta>0
, and an induced chemical strain
\mathcal{E}_{c}
arising from the increase in density due to polymerization that results in shrinkage of the cured resin counteracting the thermal expansion
[
5
,
14
,
44
,
47
]
. This induced strain is assumed to be isotropic and only occurs in the gel phase
[
44
]
. One example we can take is
\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)=\zeta(1-P(\varphi))\mathbb{I}=:m(\varphi)\mathbb{I}
, where
\zeta>0
is a scalar corresponding to the maximum shrinkage strain, so that
\mathcal{E}_{c}(-1)=\bm{0}
.

As both the sol and gel phase are modeled elastic materials, let
\mathbb{C}^{(0)}
and
\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
denote their corresponding constant elasticity tensors that are positive definite on symmetric matrices and fulfil the usual symmetric conditions of linear elasticity (see (

A6

) below), respectively. We introduce a interpolation fourth order elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}(\varphi)=(1-k(\varphi))\mathbb{C}^{(0)}+k(\varphi)\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
for some function
k:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
satisfying
k(1)=1
and
k(-1)=0
. Then, the balance of linear momentum in the absence of external body forces yields

\mathrm{div}\,(\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)+\beta(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathbb{I}))=\bm{0}.

Combining the three derived equations for
(\varphi,\theta,\bm{u})
and furnishing initial-boundary conditions leads to our phase field model (
1.1
).

Let us provide two examples of
\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
for applications relevant to additive manufacturing. Assuming gel phases is an isotropic linearly elastic material, then
\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{(1)}_{ijmn}

\displaystyle=\lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}+\mu(\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm})

\displaystyle=\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}+\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}(\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm})

with gel phase Lamè constants
\lambda
and
\mu
, Young’s modulus
E
and Poisson ratio
\nu
that are related via the relations

\lambda=\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)},\hskip 10.00002pt\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\hskip 10.00002ptE=\frac{\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)}{\lambda+\mu},\hskip 10.00002pt\nu=\frac{\lambda}{2(\lambda+\mu)}.

For the sol phase that is treated as an ersatz material, we fix
0<\kappa\ll 1
and consider the sol phase Lamè constants to be
\kappa\lambda
and
\kappa\mu
, i.e.,
\mathbb{C}^{(0)}=\kappa\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
, and this leads to an interpolation elasticity tensor of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(\varphi)_{ijmn}=(\kappa\lambda+k(\varphi)(1-\kappa)\lambda)\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}+(\kappa\mu+k(\varphi)(1-\kappa)\mu)(\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm}).

(2.1)

Assuming as in
[
14
]
that the Poisson ratio
\nu
is constant throughout polymerization, i.e., the Poisson ratios of both sol and gel phases are equal, while setting the Young’s modulus of the sol phase as
\kappa E
, then an alternative interpolation elasticity tensor to (
2.1
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(\varphi)_{ijmn}=\frac{\nu(\kappa E+k(\varphi)(1-\kappa)E)}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}+\frac{\kappa E+k(\varphi)(1-\kappa)E}{2(1+\nu)}(\delta_{im}\delta_{jn}+\delta_{in}\delta_{jm}).

(2.2)

On the other hand, it is well known that objects built by additive manufacturing techniques exhibit anisotropic material properties due to the layer by layer printing process, which can be described by modeling both sol and gel phases as orthotropic materials
[
6
,
24
]
. In three spatial dimensions, the elasticity tensor of such materials has 9 independent components due to having three mutually orthogonal planes of reflection symmetry.

For orthotropic materials, it is more common to express the compliance tensor (inverse of elasticity tensor) in terms of materials parameters. Let
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
denote the Young’s moduli in the three principal directions,
\{\nu_{ij}\}_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{3}
denote the Poisson’s ratio characterizing the transverse strain in the
j
th direction when the material is stressed in the
i
th direction, and
\{G_{ij}\}_{i,j=1,\,i<j}^{3}
denote the shear modulus characterizing the ratio between the shear stress in the
i
th direction and the shear strain in the
j
th direction. Then, the fourth order compliance tensor
[\mathbb{C}^{(1)}]^{-1}
of an orthotropic material expressed in Voigt notation reads as

[\mathbb{C}^{(1)}]^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{E_{1}}&-\frac{\nu_{21}}{E_{2}}&-\frac{\nu_{31}}{E_{3}}&0&0&0\\
-\frac{\nu_{12}}{E_{1}}&\frac{1}{E_{2}}&-\frac{\nu_{32}}{E_{3}}&0&0&0\\
-\frac{\nu_{13}}{E_{1}}&-\frac{\nu_{23}}{E_{2}}&\frac{1}{E_{3}}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{1}{2G_{23}}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\frac{1}{2G_{13}}&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&\frac{1}{2G_{12}}\end{pmatrix},

where by symmetry requirements, it holds that

\frac{\nu_{21}}{E_{2}}=\frac{\nu_{12}}{E_{1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\nu_{31}}{E_{3}}=\frac{\nu_{13}}{E_{1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\nu_{32}}{E_{3}}=\frac{\nu_{23}}{E_{2}}.

Taking
\mathbb{C}^{(0)}=\kappa\mathbb{C}^{(1)}
with
0<\kappa\ll 1
for the sol phase, we can consider an interpolation elasticity tensor for orthotropic materials of the form

\mathbb{C}(\varphi)=[\kappa+k(\varphi)(1-\kappa)]\mathbb{C}^{(1)}.

2.4
Boundary conditions

From the schematics in Figure
1
, we can assume that in a typical set-up the printed object (gel phase) is completely submerged in the liquid resin (sol phase). Hence, appropriate boundary conditions for
\varphi
can be the homogeneous Neumann condition
\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi=0
or the Dirichlet boundary condition
\varphi=-1
. For the temperature we can prescribe homogeneous Neumann condition
\partial_{\bm{n}}\theta=0
to describe thermal isolation of the construction environment
[
44
]
. Alternatively, a Robin boundary condition
\partial_{\bm{n}}\theta=\theta_{\infty}-\theta
with ambient temperature
\theta_{\infty}
is suitable, see
[
14
,
47
]
. From the set-up in Figure
1
and on account of the ersatz material approximation of the sol phase, an appropriate boundary condition for the displacement is a homogeneous Dirichlet condition
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
. Another option is to consider a mixed boundary condition where the boundary
\partial\Omega
is decomposed into a disjoint union of relatively open subsets
\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}
where traction-free conditions
\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)+\beta(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathbb{I})\bm{n}=\bm{0}
are imposed on
\Gamma_{1}
, while
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
is imposed on
\Gamma_{0}
.

2.5
Comparison with similar phenomenological models

In this section we provide a comparison between (
1.1
) and similar phenomenological models with mechanical effects. Let us comment that in choosing
\varphi
to be the difference in volume fractions of the gel and sol phases means that
\varphi
should belong to the physically relevant interval
[-1,1]
. Hence, in our model we emphasize the values of the interpolating functions
P(\varphi)
and
k(\varphi)
at
\pm 1
in order to capture the relevant physical effects in the sol phase
\{\varphi\sim-1\}
and in the gel phase
\{\varphi\sim 1\}
. However, it is also possible to choose
\varphi
as a different physical variable, such as the relative difference between initial and current monomer concentrations, leading to a different physically relevant interval
[0,1]
and with the sol phase now defined as
\{\varphi\sim 0\}
and the gel phase remaining as
\{\varphi\sim 1\}
. Mathematically, this only involves a composition of the potential
W
with a suitable affine linear function so that the new potential has its minima at
0
and
1
. One example of such a potential is the function
W(s)=s^{2}(s-1)^{2}
.

Our closest counterpart is the model of
[
47
]
, which also employs a phase field description where
\varphi\in[0,1]
is the volume fraction of the molecules that have undergone the sol-gel transition. The equation for
\varphi
is of a similar Allen–Cahn type as (
1.1a
), which can be obtained by setting
\lambda=\varepsilon
, and replacing
W(\varphi)
with
\frac{k_{B}}{v_{a}}\theta[(1-\varphi)\ln(1-\varphi)+\chi\varphi(1-\varphi)]
with the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
, an interaction parameter
\chi
, and the volume of a single monomer/solvent molecule
v_{a}
, as well as replacing
\gamma(\theta-\theta_{c})p(\varphi)
with
6\varphi(1-\varphi)(f_{\mathrm{gel}}-f_{\mathrm{sol}})
with bulk free energy density at the gel phase
f_{\mathrm{gel}}
and the sol phase
f_{\mathrm{sol}}
, respectively:

\partial_{t}\varphi=\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\varphi+\tfrac{k_{B}}{v_{a}}\theta(1+\ln(1-\varphi)+\chi(2\varphi-1))-6\varphi(1-\varphi)(f_{\mathrm{gel}}-f_{\mathrm{sol}}).

The main difference with (
1.1a
) is that the temperature appears in the potential term as a prefactor, while the temperature equation reads as

\rho\delta\partial_{t}\theta=k\Delta\theta

with heat conductivity coefficient
k
and mass density
\rho=\frac{\rho_{0}}{1+\mathrm{div}\,\bm{u}}
where
\rho_{0}
represents the initial mass density. Notice that there is no explicit terms in
\varphi
appearing in the temperature equation, but the coupling with
\varphi
enters via the displacement
\bm{u}
. In addition to the decomposition of the total strain into a sum of elastic, thermal and chemical shrinkage strains, the mechanical behavior considered in
[
47
]
is described by a rheological model also includes viscoelastic effects, which we have neglected in this work.

In
[
44
]
(see also
[
14
]
for the one-dimensional analogue),
\varphi\in[0,1]
is chosen as the relative difference between initial and current monomer concentrations, and a phenomenological model is proposed taking the form of the following differential equation

\partial_{t}\varphi=rf(\varphi)I^{b},

with rate constant
r
that can depend on temperature
\theta
, reaction kinetics model function
f
(linear in
[
44
]
and
m
th order polynomial in
[
14
]
), and laser intensity
I
with exponent
b\in[0.5,1]
, see
[
5
, Chap. 9.3]
. Notice that a similar type of equation can be obtained from (
1.1a
) by setting
\lambda=0
and choosing
p(s)
appropriately. The temperature equation adapted from
[
20
]
reads as

\rho\delta\partial_{t}\theta=\mathrm{div}\,(k\nabla\theta)-(\Delta H)\partial_{t}\varphi

with constant mass density
\rho
, thermal conductivity
k
and heat of polymerization
\Delta H
, which we note the resemblance to (
1.1b
). For the mechanical response, a linear elastic behavior is assumed employing a similar decomposition of the total strain into a sum of elastic, thermal and chemical components. A
\varphi
-dependent Young’s modulus
E(\varphi)
is prescribed:

E(\varphi)=\begin{cases}e_{0}E_{\mathrm{pol}}&\text{ for }\varphi<\varphi_{\mathrm{gel}},\\
\Big(\frac{1-e_{0}}{1-\varphi_{\mathrm{gel}}}(\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{gel}})\Big)E_{\mathrm{pol}}&\text{ for }\varphi\geq\varphi_{\mathrm{gel}},\end{cases}

for constants
0<e_{0}\ll 1
, Young modulus of the gel phase
E_{\mathrm{pol}}
, and gel-point
\varphi_{\mathrm{gel}}
for conversion defined as the point where the liquid resin transforms to a solid polymer. Then, with a constant Poisson’s ratio
\nu
, the phase dependent Lamè constants

\lambda(\varphi)=\frac{E(\varphi)}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)},\hskip 10.00002pt\mu(\varphi)=\frac{E(\varphi)}{2(1+\nu)},

enter into an elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}(\varphi)
of the form (
2.1
) and the resulting momentum balance equation resembles (
1.1c
) and (
2.2
).

Remark 2.1
.

We note that (
1.1
) somewhat resembles the Cahn–Larché model
[
28
,
32
]
(where we use the notation
\mu
to denote the associated chemical potential)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle=\Delta\mu,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\varepsilon^{-1}W^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)):\mathbb{C}^{\prime}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi))-\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)):\mathcal{E}_{c}^{\prime}(\varphi),

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\,(\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi))),

with the associated energy functional

\mathbb{E}(\varphi,\bm{u}):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi)):\mathbb{C}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{E}_{c}(\varphi))\,dx.

The most significant difference is the inclusion of an elastic contribution in
\mathbb{E}
, leading to an additional term appear in the equation for
\mu
and thus allowing elastic stress to influence the evolution of
\varphi
.
This elastic influence is not present in (
1.1
) due to how we build the model by connecting submodels in a sequence. To the best of our knowledge, it is not entirely clear if this mechanical feedback is detected in the physical and chemical processes of stereolithography, and further investigations would warrant a comprehensive study in model calibration and validation with experimental data. We mention that a numerical analysis of a finite element scheme for the Cahn–Larché system can be found in
[
19
]
.

3.3
Fully discrete finite element approximation

Dividing the time interval

into a uniform partition of subintervials

for

with
\tau=t^{n}-t^{n-1}
as the time step and
N_{\tau}\tau=T
. Let
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
denote a regular family of conformal quasiuniform triangulations that partition

\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\text{diam}(K)\leq h

\mathcal{S}_{h}

\mathcal{S}_{h}:=\{f_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\,:\,f_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(K)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega),

. For the approximation of the elasticity system, we introduce the function space:

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}=\{\bm{f}_{h}\in(\mathcal{S}_{h})^{d}\,:\,\bm{f}_{h}=\bm{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

We recall the nodal interpolation operator

defined as
\mathrm{I}_{h}(\eta)(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}\eta(\bm{x}_{k})\chi_{k}^{h}(\bm{x})

\mathrm{I}_{h}(\eta)(\bm{x}_{k})=\eta(\bm{x}_{k})
for all nodes
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k=1,\dots,Z_{h}}
of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

Let us recall some well-known results concerning the nodal interpolation operator: there exist positive constants

and

, such that

\displaystyle c\|f_{h}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\big(\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{I}_{h}(|f_{h}|^{p})\,\mathrm{dx}\big)^{1/p}\leq C\|f_{h}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall f_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h},\,p\in[1,\infty),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|f-\mathrm{I}_{h}(f)\|+h\|\nabla(f-\mathrm{I}_{h}(f))\|\leq Ch^{2}\|f\|_{H^{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall f\in H^{2}(\Omega),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|f-\mathrm{I}_{h}(f)\|_{L^{q}}+h\|\nabla(f-\mathrm{I}_{h}(f))\|_{L^{q}}\leq Ch\|f\|_{W^{1,q}}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall f\in W^{1,q}(\Omega),\,q\in(2,\infty]

(3.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\|f-\mathrm{I}_{h}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}}=0\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\forall f\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}).

(3.7)

We also note that for any

such that

with constants

and

, by the definition of the nodal interpolation operator, for any

,

\displaystyle k_{0}=k_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}\chi_{k}^{h}(\bm{x})\leq\mathrm{I}_{h}(\eta)(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}\eta(\bm{x}_{k})\chi_{k}^{h}(\bm{x})\leq k_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}\chi_{k}^{h}(\bm{x})=k_{1}.

(3.8)

Let us recall the discrete Neumann-Laplacian
\Delta_{h}:\mathcal{S}_{h}\to\mathcal{S}_{h}
for a function
q_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
as

\displaystyle(\Delta_{h}q_{h},\zeta_{h}):=-(\nabla q_{h},\nabla\zeta_{h})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\zeta_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}.

(3.9)

Under (

A1

[
3

that for any

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{h}\|_{L^{r}}\leq C\|\Delta_{h}f_{h}\|\text{ where }\begin{cases}r<\infty&\text{ if }d=2,\\
r=6&\text{ if }d=3.\end{cases}

(3.10)

For the error analysis in Section
5
we will make use of the Ritz projection operator

defined as

(\nabla f-\nabla(R_{h}f),\nabla\psi_{h})=0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\psi_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}

satisfying

. Then, the following properties hold (see e.g.
[
41
]

\displaystyle\|f-R_{h}f\|+h\|\nabla(f-R_{h}f)\|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{s}\|f\|_{H^{s}},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|f-R_{h}f\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{s}\ell_{h}\|f\|_{W^{s,\infty}},

and

Then, our proposed numerical scheme for (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\tau}(\varphi^{n}_{h}-\varphi^{n-1}_{h},\psi_{h})+\frac{\lambda q^{n}_{h}}{\varepsilon Q_{h}^{n-1}}(W^{\prime}(\varphi^{n-1}_{h}),\psi_{h})+\gamma((\theta^{n}_{h}-\theta_{c})p(\varphi^{n-1}_{h}),\psi_{h})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\lambda\varepsilon(\nabla\varphi^{n}_{h},\nabla\psi_{h}),

3.4
Unique solvability

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
n=1,\dots,N_{\tau}
, given

Proof.

Suppose we have two quadruple of solutions
\{(\varphi^{n}_{h,i},\theta^{n}_{h,i},q^{n}_{h,i},\bm{u}^{n}_{h,i})\}_{i=1,2}

)-(

). Denoted their differences by
(\varphi,\theta,q,\bm{u})\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathcal{S}_{h}\times\mathbb{R}\times\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{h,0}

(\varphi,\theta,q)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\tau}(\varphi,\psi_{h})+\frac{\lambda q}{Q^{n-1}_{h}}(W^{\prime}(\varphi^{n-1}_{h}),\psi_{h})+\gamma(p(\varphi_{h}^{n-1})\theta,\psi_{h})+\lambda\varepsilon(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\psi_{h}),

(3.16a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\delta(\theta,\psi_{h})-\gamma(p(\varphi^{n-1}_{h})\varphi,\psi_{h})+\tau(\nabla\theta,\nabla\psi_{h}),

(3.16b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=q-\frac{(W^{\prime}(\varphi^{n-1}_{h}),\varphi)}{2\varepsilon Q^{n-1}_{h}}.

(3.16c)

\psi_{h}=\varphi
in (

),
\psi_{h}=\theta
in (

), and multiplying (

\varphi^{n}_{h,1}=\varphi^{n}_{h,2}

yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(1-\mathrm{I}_{h}(k(\varphi^{n}_{h})))\mathbb{C}^{(0)}\mathcal{E}(\bm{u}):\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})+\mathrm{I}_{h}(k(\varphi^{n}_{h}))\mathbb{C}^{(1)}\mathcal{E}(\bm{u}):\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})\,\mathrm{dx}

With the dual basis of nodes, we introduce

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{M}^{-1}[(\varphi_{h}^{n},\chi_{k}^{h})]_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}=(\varphi_{h}^{n}(\bm{x}_{1}),\dots,\varphi_{h}^{n}(\bm{x}_{Z_{h}}))^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{M}^{-1}[(\theta_{h}^{n},\chi_{k}^{h})]_{k=1}^{Z_{h}}=(\theta_{h}^{n}(\bm{x}_{1}),\dots,\theta_{h}^{n}(\bm{x}_{Z_{h}}))^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{n-1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\lambda W^{\prime}(\bm{\varphi}_{h}^{n-1})}{\varepsilon Q_{h}^{n-1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{c}^{n-1}:=\gamma\theta_{c}\mathbb{M}^{-1}[(p(\varphi_{h}^{n-1}),\chi_{k}^{h})]_{k=1}^{Z_{h}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{i,j}=(p(\varphi_{h}^{n-1})\chi_{i}^{h},\chi_{j}^{h}),

where the nonlinearities are applied component-wise. Then, choosing

for

Remark 3.2
.

The computational cost for each update step in (

) amounts to solving three linear systems:

in

,

(5.2)

(5.3)

f(t^{n})-f(t^{n-1})-\tau\partial_{t}f(t^{n})=\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}(t^{n}-s)\partial_{tt}f(s)\,ds,

we see that for
T_{f}^{n-1}:=f(t^{n})-f(t^{n-1})-\tau\partial_{t}f(t^{n})
, where
f\in\{\varphi,\theta,q\}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|T_{\varphi}^{n-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{3}\|\partial_{tt}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\|T_{\theta}^{n-1}\|^{2}\leq C\tau^{3}\|\partial_{tt}\theta\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}^{2},

(5.4)

\displaystyle|T_{q}^{n-1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{3}\|q^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n})}^{2}.

A short calculation shows

\displaystyle\Psi^{k-1}-\Psi^{k}=\frac{W^{\prime}(\varphi^{k-1})-W^{\prime}(\varphi^{k})}{Q(\varphi^{k-1})}+\frac{W^{\prime}(\varphi^{k})[Q^{2}(\varphi^{k})-Q^{2}(\varphi^{k-1})]}{(Q(\varphi^{k})+Q(\varphi^{k-1}))Q(\varphi^{k})Q(\varphi^{k-1})}.

(5.5)

From the definition of
Q
and using (
3.36
) we see

\displaystyle|Q^{2}(\varphi^{k})-Q^{2}(\varphi^{k-1})|

\displaystyle\leq C\|W(\varphi^{k})-W(\varphi^{k-1})\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\varphi^{k}\|_{L^{6}}^{3}+\|\varphi^{k-1}\|_{L^{6}}^{3})\|\varphi^{k}-\varphi^{k-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi^{k}-\varphi^{k-1}\|\leq C\tau^{1/2}\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{k-1},t^{k};L^{2})}

while invoking the Gagliardo–Nirenburg inequality and (
3.37
) we have

\displaystyle\|W^{\prime}(\varphi^{k})-W^{\prime}(\varphi^{k-1})\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\varphi^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\varphi^{k-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2})\|\varphi^{k}-\varphi^{k-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi^{k}-\varphi^{k-1}\|\leq C\tau^{1/2}\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{k-1},t^{k};L^{2})}.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Psi^{k}-\Psi^{k-1}\|\leq C\tau^{1/2}\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{k-1},t^{k};L^{2})}.

(5.6)

Likewise, using the Lipschitz continuity of
p=P^{\prime}
, we also have for
r\in[2,\infty)

\displaystyle\|p^{k}-p^{k-1}\|_{L^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi^{k}-\varphi^{k-1}\|_{L^{r}}\leq C\tau^{\frac{r-1}{r}}\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{r}(t^{k-1},t^{k};L^{r})}.

(5.7)

Then, from (
5.1a
)-(
5.1c
) we infer that

\displaystyle\|X_{\varphi}^{n-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{\varphi}^{n-1}\|+\tau\|\theta^{n}-\theta_{c}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|p^{n}-p^{n-1}\|+\tau|q^{n}|\|\Psi^{n}-\Psi^{n-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{3/2}\big(\|\partial_{tt}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}+\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}\big),

\displaystyle\|X_{\theta}^{n-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{\theta}^{n-1}\|+\|p^{n-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|T_{\varphi}^{n-1}\|+\tau\|p^{n-1}-p^{n}\|_{L^{4}}\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{n}\|_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{3/2}\big(\|\partial_{tt}\theta\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}+\|\partial_{tt}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}\big)+C\tau^{7/4}\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{4}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{4})},

\displaystyle|X_{q}^{n-1}|

\displaystyle\leq|T_{q}^{n-1}|+C\|W^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})\|\|T_{\varphi}^{n-1}\|+C\tau\|\Psi^{n-1}-\Psi^{n}\|\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{3/2}\big(\|q^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n})}+\|\partial_{tt}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n-1},t^{n};L^{2})}\big).

To derive the estimate (
5.2
) it suffices to square both sides of the above inequalities, divide by
\tau
and sum from
n=1
to
n=N_{\tau}
. For the term involving
\tau^{7/4}
we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to see that

∎

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{h}^{M}-\varphi^{M}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\kappa_{\varphi}^{M}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\varphi^{M}-R_{h}\varphi^{M}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C(h^{3/2}+\tau^{1/2})+Ch\ell_{h}\leq 1.

This establishes (
5.10
) for
k=M
. Hence, the estimates (
5.20
)-(
5.22
) hold for
k=M
for all
h<h_{1}
and
\tau<\tau_{1}
. On the other hand, if
h>h_{1}
or
\tau>\tau_{1}
, then from the stability estimate (
3.19
) of Lemma
3.1
it holds that

\displaystyle\|\kappa_{\varphi}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\kappa_{\theta}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+|e^{k}|^{2}\leq C+C\big(\|R_{h}\varphi^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|R_{h}\theta^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+|q^{k}|^{2}\big)\leq C

with constant
C
independent of
k
. In particular, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\kappa_{\varphi}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\kappa_{\theta}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+|e^{k}|^{2}\leq C\leq C(\tau_{1}^{-2}+h_{1}^{-4})(\tau^{2}+h^{4})

(5.23)

if
\tau>\tau_{1}
or
h>h_{1}
. Combining (
5.20
)-(
5.20
) and (
5.23
) we have that for any
\tau
and
h
,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{\tau}}\big(\|\kappa_{\varphi}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\kappa_{\theta}^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+|e^{k}|^{2}\big)\leq C(h^{4}+\tau^{2}).

Then, by (
3.11
) and the triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{\tau}}\big(\|\varphi_{h}^{k}-\varphi^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\theta_{h}^{k}-\theta^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C(h^{4}+\tau^{2})+\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{\tau}}\big(\|\varphi^{k}-R_{h}\varphi^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|\theta^{k}-R_{h}\theta^{k}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\big)\leq C(h^{2}+\tau^{2}),

as well as

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{\tau}}\big(\|\varphi_{h}^{k}-\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\|\theta_{h}^{k}-\theta^{k}\|^{2}+|q_{h}^{k}-q^{k}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C(h^{4}+\tau^{2})+\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{\tau}}\big(\|\varphi^{k}-R_{h}\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\|\theta^{k}-R_{h}\theta^{k}\|^{2}\big)\leq C(h^{4}+\tau^{2}).

∎

Remark 5.1
.

We are not able to derive estimates for the error
\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{h}
due to the lack of
H^{2}
-spatial regularity for the displacement
\bm{u}
. This is despite (
1.1c
) being a linear elasticity system with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions, as the spatially varying elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}(\varphi)
complicates the regularity arguments for elliptic systems.

7
Conclusion

In this contribution, we proposed a new mathematical model for the physical processes occurring in stereolithography based on a Caginalp phase field system with mechanical effects. A fully discrete unconditionally stable numerical scheme based on the finite element method for spatial discretization and the scalar auxiliary variable approach for temporal discretization is proposed and analyzed. We established existence, uniqueness and convergence of fully discrete solutions, as well as error estimates against the exact solution to the Caginalp submodel. The implementation of the discrete system is efficient as advancing to the next time iteration requires only solving linear systems, and the numerical simulations presented support our theoretical findings, as well as reproducing the expected behaviour during the polymerization processes in stereolithography.
