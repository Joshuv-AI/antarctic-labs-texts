An Internal Language for Categories Enriched over Generalised Metric Spaces
Fredrik Dahlqvist, Renato Neves

Abstract
Programs with a continuous state space or that interact with physical processes often require notions of equivalence going beyond the standard binary setting in which equivalence either holds or does not hold. In this paper we explore the idea of equivalence taking values in a quantale 𝒱 \mathcal{V} , which covers the cases of (in)equations and (ultra)metric equations among others. Our main result is the introduction of a 𝒱 \mathcal{V} -equational deductive system for linear λ \lambda -calculus together with a proof that it is sound and complete (in fact, an internal language) for a class of enriched autonomous categories. In the case of inequations, we get an internal language for autonomous categories enriched over partial orders. In the case of (ultra)metric equations, we get an internal language for autonomous categories enriched over (ultra)metric spaces. We use our results to obtain examples of inequational and metric equational systems for higher-order programs that contain real-time and probabilistic behaviour.

Introduction

Programs frequently act over a
continuous
state space or
interact with physical processes like time progression or the movement
of a vehicle. Such features naturally call for notions of
approximation and refinement integrated in different aspects of
program equivalence. Our paper falls in this line of research.
Specifically, our aim is to integrate notions of approximation and
refinement into the
equational system
of linear

\lambda
-calculus
[
4
,
27
,
28
]
.

The core idea that we explore in this paper is to have equations

t=_{q}s
labelled by elements
q
of a quantale
\mathcal{V}
. This
covers a wide range of situations, among which the cases of
(in)equations
[
22
,
2
]
and metric
equations
[
29
,
30
]
. The latter case is perhaps less
known: it consists of equations
t=_{\epsilon}s
labelled by a
non-negative rational number
\epsilon
which represents the ‘maximum
distance’ that the two terms
t
and
s
can be from each other. In
order to illustrate metric equations, consider a programming language
with a (ground) type
X
and a signature of operations

\Sigma=\{\mathtt{wait_{n}}:X\to X\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
that model
time progression over computations of type
X
. Specifically,

\mathtt{wait_{n}}(x)
reads as “add a latency of
n
seconds to the
computation
x
”. In this context, the following axioms involving
metric equations arise naturally:

\displaystyle\mathtt{wait_{0}}(x)=_{0}x\hskip 28.45274pt\mathtt{wait_{n}}(\mathtt{wait_{m}}(x))=_{0}\mathtt{wait_{n+m}}(x)\hskip 28.45274pt\mathtt{wait_{n}}(x)=_{\epsilon}\mathtt{wait_{m}}(x)\epsilon=|m-n|

(1)

An equation
t=_{0}s
states that the terms
t
and

are exactly the
same and equations

state that
t
and
s
differ by

at most

\epsilon
seconds in their execution time.

Contributions.
In this paper we introduce an equational
deductive system for linear
\lambda
-calculus in which equations are
labelled by elements of a quantale
\mathcal{V}
. By using key
features of a quantale’s structure, we show that this deductive system
is
sound and complete
for a class of enriched symmetric
monoidal closed categories (
i.e.
enriched autonomous categories). In
particular, if we fix
\mathcal{V}
to be the Boolean quantale this
class of categories consists of autonomous categories enriched over
partial orders. If we fix
\mathcal{V}
to be the (ultra)metric
quantale, this class of categories consists of autonomous categories
enriched over (ultra)metric spaces. The aforementioned example of wait
calls fits in the setting in which
\mathcal{V}
is the metric
quantale. Our result provides this example with a sound and complete
metric equational system, where the models are all those autonomous
categories enriched over metric spaces that can soundly interpret the
axioms of wait calls (
1
).

The next contribution of our paper falls in one of the major topics of
categorical logic: to establish
logical descriptions
of certain
classes of categories. A famous result of this kind is the
correspondence between
\lambda
-calculus and Cartesian closed
categories which states that the former is the
internal
language
of the latter
[
23
]
– such a correspondence
allows to study Cartesian closed categories by means of logical
tools. An analogous result is presented in
[
27
,
28
]

for linear
\lambda
-calculus and symmetric monoidal closed (
i.e.
autonomous) categories. We show that linear
\lambda
-calculus
equipped with a
\mathcal{V}
-equational system is the internal
language of autonomous categories enriched over ‘generalised metric
spaces’.

Related work.
Several approaches to incorporating
quantitative information to programming languages have been explored
in the literature. Closest to this work are various approaches
targeted at
\lambda
-calculi.
In
[
7
,
8
]
a notion of distance
called
context distance
is developed, first for an affine, then
for a more general
\lambda
-calculus, with probabilistic programs as
the main motivation.
[
13
]
considers a notion of
quantale-valued
applicative (bi)similarity
, an operational

coinductive
technique used for showing contextual equivalence
between two programs. Recently,
[
33
]
presented several
Cartesian closed categories of generalised metric spaces that provide
a quantitative semantics to simply-typed
\lambda
-calculus based on a
generalisation of logical relations. None of these examples reason
about distances in a quantitative equational system, and in this
respect our work is closer to the metric universal algebra developed
in
[
29
,
30
]
.

A different approach consists in encoding quantitative information via
a type system. In particular, graded (modal) types

[
15
,
12
,
31
]

have found applications in
e.g.
differential privacy
[
34
]
and
information flow
[
1
]
. This approach is to some
extent orthogonal to ours as it mainly aims to model coeffects, whilst
we aim to reason about the intrinsic quantitative nature of

\lambda
-terms acting
e.g.
on continuous or ordered spaces.

Quantum programs provide an interesting example of intrinsically
quantitative programs, by which we mean that the metric structure on
quantum states does not arise from (co)effects. Recently,

[
18
]
showed how the issue of noise in a quantum
while-language can be handled by developing a
deductive system

to determine how similar a quantum program is from its idealised,
noise-free version; an approach very much in the spirit of this work.

An internal language for autonomous categories

In this section we briefly recall linear
\lambda
-calculus, which can
be regarded as a term assignment system for the exponential free,
multiplicative fragment of
intuitionistic linear logic
. Then we
recall that it is sound and complete w.r.t. autonomous categories, and
also that it is an internal language for such categories. We
mention only what is needed to present
our results, the interested reader will find a more detailed
exposition in
[
27
,
4
,
28
]
. Let
us start by fixing a
class

G

types for linear
\lambda

\mathbb{A}::=X\in G\mid\mathbb{I}\mid\mathbb{A}\otimes\mathbb{A}\mid\mathbb{A}\multimap\mathbb{A}

We also fix a class
\Sigma

f:\mathbb{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{A}
with
n\geq 1
. As usual,
we use Greek letters
\Gamma,\Delta,E,\dots
to denote
typing
contexts
,
i.e.
lists

of typed

occurs at most once in

We will use the notion of a shuffle for building a linear typing
system such that the

is admissible and each

(details about these below) has a

unique derivation
– this will allow us to refer to a

By shuffle we mean a permutation of typed variables in a context
sequence
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
such that for all

the
relative order of the variables in
\Gamma_{i}
is
preserved
[
37
]

\Gamma_{1}=x:\mathbb{A},y:\mathbb{B}
and
\Gamma_{2}=z:\mathbb{C}
then

z:\mathbb{C},x:\mathbb{A},y:\mathbb{B}
is a shuffle but

y:\mathbb{B},x:\mathbb{A},z:\mathbb{C}
is
not
, because we changed
the order in which
x
and
y
appear in
\Gamma_{1}
. As explained
in
[
37
]
(and also in the proof of
Lemma
1

crucial for judgements having unique derivations. We denote by

\mathrm{Sf}(\Gamma_{1};\dots;\Gamma_{n})
the set of shuffles on

\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
.

All judgements

For every judgement

Theorem 3
.

The equations presented in Fig.

judgement interpretation. Specifically if

\Gamma\rhd v=w:\mathbb{A}

Definition 4

(Linear
\lambda
-theories)
.

Consider a tuple
(G,\Sigma)
consisting of a class
G
of ground
types and a class
\Sigma
of sorted operation symbols. A linear

\lambda
-theory
((G,\Sigma),Ax)
is a triple such that
Ax
is a
class of equations-in-context over linear
\lambda
-terms built from

(G,\Sigma)
.

The elements of
Ax
are called axioms (of the theory). Let

be the smallest congruence that contains
Ax

Fig.
6
, and that is closed under the exchange and
substitution rules. We call the elements of

Definition 5

(Models of linear
\lambda
-theories)
.

Consider a

\lambda
-theory
((G,\Sigma),Ax)
and an autonomous category

\mathsf{C}
. Suppose that for each

we have an interpretation

\llbracket X\rrbracket
that is a
\mathsf{C}
-object and analogously for the operation
symbols. This interpretation structure is a
model
of the
theory if all axioms are satisfied by the interpretation.

\lambda
-calculus and autonomous categories, established via

Theorem 6

(Soundness & Completeness)
.

Consider a linear
\lambda
-theory
T
. An equation

\Gamma\rhd v=w:\mathbb{A}
is a theorem of
T
iff it
is satisfied by all models of the theory.

Proof sketch.

Soundness follows by induction over the rules that define
Th(Ax)

(Definition
4
) and by
Theorem
3
. Completeness is based on the idea of a
Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra: it follows from building the

syntactic category

of

(also known as term

also that equality

\llbracket\Gamma\rhd v:\mathbb{A}\rrbracket=\llbracket\Gamma\rhd w:\mathbb{A}\rrbracket
in
the syntactic category is equivalent to provability

\Gamma\rhd v=w:\mathbb{A}
in the theory.

The syntactic category of
T
has as objects the
types of
T
and as morphisms
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
the equivalence
classes (w.r.t. provability) of terms

for which we can derive

.

. The model induces a functor

Proof sketch.

Consider a model of

. Then for any

, the induced functor

into

∎

An autonomous category
\mathsf{C}
induces a linear
\lambda
-theory

\mathrm{Lang}(\mathsf{C})
whose ground types

are the objects of

and whose signature

\mathsf{C}
plus certain isomorphisms that we describe

from the

\mathrm{Lang}(\mathsf{C})

\hat{\mathbb{I}}
, and the exponential by

\widehat{\multimap}

\displaystyle i(\mathbb{I})=\hat{\mathbb{I}}\qquad i(X)=X\qquad i(\mathbb{A}\otimes\mathbb{B})=i(\mathbb{A})\ \hat{\otimes}\ i(\mathbb{B})\qquad i(\mathbb{A}\multimap\mathbb{B})=i(\mathbb{A})\ \widehat{\multimap}\ i(\mathbb{B})

(2)

For each type
\mathbb{A}

Theorem 8

(Internal language)
.

For every autonomous category
\mathsf{C}
there exists an equivalence of

Proof sketch.

By construction, we have an interpretation of
\mathrm{Lang}(\mathsf{C})

3.2
Semantics of
\mathcal{V}
-equations

In this subsection we set the necessary background for presenting a
sound and complete class of models for (symmetric) linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theories. We start by recalling basics concepts
of
\mathcal{V}
-categories, which are central in a field initiated by
Lawvere in
[
24
]
and can be intuitively seen as generalised

[
38
,
17
,
39

\mathcal{V}
-categories provide structure to suitably interpret

\mathcal{V}
-equations.

Definition 14
.

A (small)
\mathcal{V}
-category is a pair
(X,a)
where
X
is a
class (set) and

is a function that
satisfies:

\displaystyle k\leq a(x,x)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad a(x,y)\otimes a(y,z)\leq a(x,z)\hskip 56.9055pt(x,y,z\in X)

For two
\mathcal{V}
-categories
(X,a)
and
(Y,b)

\mathcal{V}
-functor

is a function

that satisfies the inequality

for all

.

Small
\mathcal{V}
-categories and
\mathcal{V}
-functors form a
category which we denote by
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
. A
\mathcal{V}
-category
(X,a)

is called
symmetric
if
a(x,y)=a(y,x)
for all
x,y\in X
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym}}
the full subcategory of
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
whose
objects are symmetric. Every
\mathcal{V}
-category carries a natural
order defined by
x\leq y
whenever
k\leq a(x,y)
. A

\mathcal{V}
-category is called

if its natural order
is anti-symmetric. We denote by
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}
the full subcategory of

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
whose objects are separated.

Example 15
.

For
\mathcal{V}
the Boolean quantale,

is the category

\mathsf{Pos}
of partially ordered sets and monotone maps;

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym,sep}}
is simply the category
\mathsf{Set}
of sets and functions. For

\mathcal{V}
the metric quantale,
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym,sep}}
is the category
\mathsf{Met}

of extended metric spaces and non-expansive maps. In what follows we
omit the qualifier ‘extended’ in ‘extended (ultra)metric
spaces’. For
\mathcal{V}
the ultrametric quantale,
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym,sep}}
is
the category of ultrametric spaces and non-expansive maps.

The inclusion functor

has a left
adjoint
[
17
]

equivalence relation

whenever

and

(for

the natural order introduced earlier). Then this relation
induces the separated
\mathcal{V}
-category

is defined as

for every

\mathcal{V}
-category

(X/_{\sim},\tilde{a})

symmetry, and therefore we automatically obtain the following result.

Theorem 16
.

The inclusion functor
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym,sep}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sym}}
has a left
adjoint.

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-functor, and
the fact that

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-adjunction. For example, for the sixth rule we reason as

\displaystyle\,a(\llbracket f(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\rrbracket,\llbracket f(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\rrbracket)

\displaystyle\geq q_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes q_{n}

and that

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-functor. The

\displaystyle\,a(\llbracket v[v^{\prime}/x]\rrbracket,\llbracket w[w^{\prime}/x]\rrbracket)

\displaystyle\geq a(\id\otimes\llbracket v^{\prime}\rrbracket,\id\otimes\llbracket w^{\prime}\rrbracket)\otimes a(\llbracket v\rrbracket,\llbracket w\rrbracket)

\displaystyle=a(\llbracket v^{\prime}\rrbracket,\llbracket w^{\prime}\rrbracket)\otimes a(\llbracket v\rrbracket,\llbracket w\rrbracket)

\displaystyle\geq q\otimes r

The proof for the rule concerning

3.3
Soundness, completeness, and internal language

In this subsection we establish a formal correspondence between linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theories and
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-enriched autonomous
categories, via soundness, completeness, and internal language
theorems. A key construct in this correspondence is the quotienting of
a
\mathcal{V}
-category into a
separated

\mathcal{V}
-category: we will use it to identify linear

\lambda
-terms when generating a syntactic category (from a linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory) that satisfies the axioms of
autonomous categories. This naturally leads to the following notion of
a model for linear
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theories.

Definition 22

(Models of linear
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theories)
.

Consider a linear
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory
((G,\Sigma),Ax)
and
a
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}
-enriched autonomous category
\mathsf{C}
. Suppose that for
each
X\in G
we have an interpretation
\llbracket X\rrbracket
as a

\mathsf{C}
-object and analogously for the operation symbols. This
interpretation structure is a model of the theory if all axioms in

Ax
are satisfied by the interpretation.

Another thing that we need to take into account is the size of
categories. In Section
2
we did not assume that
autonomous categories should be locally small. In particular linear

\lambda
-theories are able to generate non-(locally small)
categories. Now we need to be stricter because
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}
-enriched
autonomous categories are always locally small (recall the definition
of
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}
). Thus for two types
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
of a

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory
T
, consider the class

\mathsf{Values}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
of values
v
such that

x:\mathbb{A}\rhd v:\mathbb{B}
. We equip
\mathsf{Values}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})

with the function

a:\mathsf{Values}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})\times\mathsf{Values}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})\to\mathcal{V}
defined by,

\displaystyle a(v,w)=\bigvee\{q\mid v=_{q}w\text{ is a theorem of }T\}

It is easy to see that
(\mathsf{Values}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}),a)
is a (possibly
large)
\mathcal{V}
-category. We then quotient this

\mathcal{V}
-category into a
separated

\mathcal{V}
-category
which we suggestively denote by
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
(as detailed in
the proof of the next theorem,
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
will serve as a
hom-object of a syntactic category
\mathsf{C}
generated from a linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory). Following the nomenclature
of
[
25
]
, we call
T

varietal
if

\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is a
small

\mathcal{V}
-category. In
the rest of the paper we will only work with varietal theories and
locally small categories.

Theorem 23

(Soundness & Completeness)
.

Consider a varietal
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory. A

\mathcal{V}
-equation-in-context
\Gamma\rhd v=_{q}w:\mathbb{A}

is a theorem iff it holds in all models of the theory.

Proof sketch.

Soundness follows by induction over the rules that define the class

Th(Ax)
(Definition
12
) and by
Theorem
21
. For completeness, we use a strategy
similar to the proof of
Theorem
6
, and take advantage of the
quotienting of a
\mathcal{V}
-category into a separated

\mathcal{V}
-category. Recall that we assume that the theory is

varietal
and therefore can safely take
\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})

to be a small
\mathcal{V}
-category. Note that the quotienting
process identifies all terms
x:\mathbb{A}\rhd v:\mathbb{B}
and

x:\mathbb{A}\rhd w:\mathbb{B}
such that
v=_{\top}w
and

w=_{\top}v
. Such a relation contains the equations-in-context from
Fig.
6
and moreover it is straighforward to show that
it is compatible with the term formation rules of linear

\lambda
-calculus (Fig.
1
). So, analogously to
Theorem
6
we obtain an autonomous category

\mathsf{C}
whose objects are the types of the language and whose
hom-sets are the underlying sets of the
\mathcal{V}
-categories

\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
.

Our next step is to show that the category
\mathsf{C}
has a

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}
-enriched autonomous structure. We start by showing that the
composition map

\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})\otimes\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C})\to\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
is a
\mathcal{V}
-functor:

\displaystyle a(([v^{\prime}],[v]),([w^{\prime}],[w]))

\displaystyle=a([v],[w])\otimes a([v^{\prime}],[w^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=a(v,w)\otimes a(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{q\mid v=_{q}w\}\otimes\bigvee\{r\mid v^{\prime}=_{r}w^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{q\otimes r\mid v=_{q}w,v^{\prime}=_{r}w^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee\{q\mid v[v^{\prime}/x]=_{q}w[w^{\prime}/x]\}

\displaystyle(A\subseteq B\Rightarrow\bigvee A\leq\bigvee B)

\displaystyle=a(v[v^{\prime}/x],w[w^{\prime}/x])

\displaystyle=a([v[v^{\prime}/x]],[w[w^{\prime}/x]])

\displaystyle=a([v]\cdot[v^{\prime}],[w]\cdot[w^{\prime}])

The fact that
\otimes:\mathsf{C}\times\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{C}
is a

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-functor follows by an analogous reasoning. Next, we need to
show that

is a

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-adjunction. It is straightforward to show that both
functors are
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
-functors, and from a similar reasoning it follows
that the isomorphism

\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{A}\multimap\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathsf{C}(\mathbb{B}\otimes\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
is a
\mathcal{V}
-isomorphism.

The final step is to show that if an equation

\Gamma\rhd v=_{q}w:\mathbb{A}
with

is satisfied by

\mathsf{C}
then it is a theorem of the linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory. By assumption

a([v],[w])=a(v,w)=\bigvee\{r\mid v=_{r}w\}\geq q
. It follows
from the definition of the way-below relation that for all
x\in B

with
x\ll q
there exists a
finite

such that
x\leq\bigvee A
. Then
by an application of rule

in Fig.
7

in

Fig.

∎

Next we establish results that will be key in the proof of the

be syntactic category of a

\mathcal{V}\lambda

Theorem 24
.

Consider a linear
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory

and a model of

on a
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}

Proof.

Consider a model of

\mathsf{C}

\mathcal{V}
-categories) in
\mathrm{Syn}(T)

\mathsf{C}

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}

.

The linear
\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theory

Theorem 26

(Internal language)
.

For every
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}

\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}_{\mathsf{sep}}

Proof.

Let
a
denote the underlying function of the

\mathcal{V}
-categories) in
\mathrm{Syn}(\mathrm{Lang}(\mathsf{C}))
and
b
the

\mathcal{V}
-categories) in

\mathsf{C}

Example 27

(Wait calls)
.

We now return to the example of wait calls and the corresponding
metric axioms (
1

a model over
\mathsf{Met}
for this theory: fix a metric space
A
,
interpret the ground type
X
as
\mathbb{N}\otimes A
and the operation
symbol
\mathtt{wait_{n}}:X\to X
as the non-expansive map,
\llbracket\mathtt{wait_{n}}\rrbracket:\mathbb{N}\otimes A\to\mathbb{N}\otimes A

(i,a)\mapsto(i+n,a)

Since we already know that
\mathsf{Met}
is enriched over itself (recall
Definition
19
and Example
20
) we only
need to show that the axioms in (
1

proposed interpretation. This can be shown via a few routine
calculations.

Now, it may be the case that is unnecessary to know the

distance
between the execution time of two programs –
instead it suffices to know whether a program finishes its execution

before
another one. This leads us to linear

\mathcal{V}\lambda
-theories where
\mathcal{V}
is the Boolean
quantale. We call such theories
linear ordered

\lambda
-theories
. Recall the language from the Introduction
with a single ground type
X
and the signature of wait calls

\Sigma=\{\mathtt{wait_{n}}:X\to X\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then we
adapt the metric axioms (
1
) to the case of the Boolean
quantale by considering instead:

\displaystyle\mathtt{wait_{0}}(x)=x\hskip 28.45274pt\mathtt{wait_{n}}(\mathtt{wait_{m}}(x))=\mathtt{wait_{n+m}}(x)\hskip 28.45274pt\mathtt{wait_{n}}(x)\leq\mathtt{wait_{m}}(x)n\leq m

where a classical equation
v=w
is shorthand for
v\leq w
(i.e.
v=_{1}w
) and

(i.e.

). In the resulting theory

\lambda
-term that defines the composition of two functions

\displaystyle v\ (\lambda x.\ \mathtt{wait_{1}}(x))\leq v\ (\lambda x.\ \mathtt{wait_{1}}(\mathtt{wait_{1}}(x)))

A

into

Example 28

(Probabilistic programs)
.

We consider ground types
\real,\real^{+},\mathtt{unit}
and a signature

\mathtt{normal}(x,y)

\displaystyle\mathtt{bernoulli}(x_{1},x_{2},p(\ast))=_{\left\lvert p-q\right\rvert}\mathtt{bernoulli}(x_{1},x_{2},q(\ast))p,q\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}

(3)

We interpret the resulting linear metric

20

Theorem 29
.

The category
\mathsf{Ban}
is a
\mathsf{Met}

In particular,
\mathsf{Ban}

\displaystyle\mathtt{walk1}\triangleq\lambda x:\real.\mathtt{bernoulli}(0,x+\mathtt{normal}(0,1),p)

\displaystyle\mathtt{walk2}\triangleq\lambda x:\real.\mathtt{bernoulli}(0,x+\mathtt{normal}(0,1),q),\qquad p,q\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}.

As the names suggest, these two terms of type

probabilistic programming language. Furthermore, the
tensor in the
\lambda
-calculus allows us to easily scale up this reasoning
to random walks in higher dimensions such as

\mathtt{walk1}\otimes\mathtt{walk2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

Functorial connection to previous work.
As a concluding
note, let us introduce a simple yet instructive functorial connection
between (1) the categorical semantics of linear
\lambda
-calculus
with the
\mathcal{V}
-equational system, (2) the categorical
semantics of linear
\lambda
-calculus with the equational system of
Section
2
, and (3) the algebraic semantics of the
exponential free, multiplicative fragment of linear logic. First we
need to recall some well-known facts. As detailed before, typical
categorical models of linear
\lambda
-calculus and its equational
system are locally small autonomous categories. The latter form a
quasicategory
\mathsf{Aut}
whose morphisms are autonomous functors. The
usual algebraic models of the exponential free, multiplicative
fragment of linear logic are the so-called

lineales

[
10
]
. In a nutshell, a lineale is a poset

(X,\leq)
paired with a commutative, monoid operation

\otimes:X\times X\to X
that satisfies certain
conditions. Lineales are almost quantales: the only difference is that
they do not require
X
to be cocomplete. The key idea in algebraic
semantics is that the order
\leq
in the lineale encodes the logic’s
entailment relation. A functorial connection between autonomous
categories and lineales (
i.e.
between (2) and (3)) is stated
in
[
10
]
and is based on the following two
observations. First, (possibly large) lineales can be
seen as
thin
autonomous categories,
i.e.
as elements of the
enriched quasicategory
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{Aut}
. Second, the inclusion

\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{Aut}

\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Aut}
has a left adjoint which

collapses all morphisms
of a given autonomous category
\mathsf{C}

(intuitively, it eliminates the ability of
\mathsf{C}
to differentiate
different terms between two types). This provides an adjoint situation
between (2) and (3). We can now expand this connection to our
categorical semantics of linear
\lambda
-calculus and corresponding

\mathcal{V}
-equational system (
i.e.
(1)) in the following way. The
forgetful functor
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}\to\mathsf{Set}
has a left adjoint

D:\mathsf{Set}\to\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat}
which sends a set
X
to
DX=(X,d)
,

\displaystyle d(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}k&\text{ if }x_{1}=x_{2}\\
\bot&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

This left adjoint is strong monoidal, specifically we have

D(X_{1}\times X_{2})=DX_{1}\otimes DX_{2}
and

\mathbb{I}=(1,(\ast,\ast)\mapsto k)=D1
. This gives rise to the
functors,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathcal{V}\text{-}\mathsf{Cat})\text{-}\mathsf{Aut}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Aut}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{0,1\}\text{-}\mathsf{Aut}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{D}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}

where

equips the hom-sets of an autonomous category with the
corresponding discrete
\mathcal{V}
-category and

morphisms of an autonomous category as described earlier. The right

\lambda
-calculus with the
\mathcal{V}

i.e.

\lambda

\displaystyle d(ST,S^{\prime}T^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq d((T,S),(T^{\prime},S^{\prime}))

\mathsf{Met}

\displaystyle d(S\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}T,S^{\prime}\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}T^{\prime})
