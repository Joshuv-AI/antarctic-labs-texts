Fixation of leadership in non-Markovian growth processes
T. Iyer

Abstract
Consider a model where N N equal agents possess ‘values’, belonging to ℕ 0 \mathbb{N}_{0} , that are subject to incremental growth over time. More precisely, the values of the agents are represented by N N independent, increasing ℕ 0 \mathbb{N}_{0} valued processes with random, independent waiting times between jumps. We show that the event that a single agent possesses the maximum value for all sufficiently large values of time (called ‘leadership’) occurs with probability zero or one, and provide necessary and sufficient conditions for this to occur. Under mild conditions we also provide criteria for a single agent to become the unique agent of maximum value for all sufficiently large times, and also conditions for the emergence of a unique agent having value that tends to infinity before ‘explosion’ occurs (i.e. conditions for ‘strict leadership’ or ‘monopoly’ to occur almost surely). The novelty of this model lies in allowing non-exponentially distributed waiting times between jumps in value. In the particular case when waiting times are mixtures of exponential distributions, we improve a well-established result on the ‘balls in bins’ model with feedback, removing the requirement that the feedback function be bounded from below and also allowing random feedback functions. As part of the proofs we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for the distribution of a convergent series of independent random variables to have an atom on the real line, a result which we believe may be of interest in its own right.



Keywords:
Growth processes, birth processes, balls in bins processes with feedback, generalised Pólya urns, non-linear urns, atoms of random series, convergence of random series, reinforced processes.

Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

In various circumstances, it is of interest to model the dynamics of values of various agents subject to growth over time. As the values of agents grow it may be the case that they can leverage their value to grow at a faster rate, leading to reinforced growth and ‘rich-gets-richer’ effects. In such a situation a natural question is whether or not a ‘leader’ forms: a single agent that eventually obtains and retains maximal value for all sufficiently large times.

Such a model is ubiquitous. One of the first, well-known, applications was to economics, where one might consider agents to be companies, with ‘values’ representing wealth
[
1
,
2
]
. In this case, ‘leadership’ may indicate the formation of a company with dominant market share, or even the formation of ‘monopolies’
[
11
]
. In other applications, one may consider agents representing political policies, with ‘leadership’ representing the formation of a most popular policy (called institutional ‘stickiness’ in
[
36
]
). In modelling neuron development, agents might also represent neurites, before the formation of a ‘leader’, that is, the specification of an axon
[
23
]
.

In previous applications, these dynamics have been modelled by ‘balls-in-bins’ processes
[
11
]
(also known as non-linear urns
[
15
]
or, originally, as generalised urn processes
[
18
,
33
]
). In these processes, agents are represented by a fixed number of
urns
, and the values of agents, encoded by the number of
balls
the associated urn contains. The rate of ‘growth’ is measured by a feedback function
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. Then starting from a given initial condition, at each discrete time-step, one selects an urn containing
j
balls (say) with probability proportional to
f(j)
, and adds a ball to it.

This model generalises the classical Pólya-Eggenberger urn scheme
[
13
]
– the case
f(j)=j
– and thus was first introduced under the name ‘generalised urns’ in
[
18
]
. It was later re-introduced under the name ‘balls-in-bins’ in
[
11
]
, however, we use the latter terminology to avoid confusion with other generalisations of the Pólya-Eggenberger urn scheme (e.g.
[
21
]
).

In these processes,
strict leadership
(known as ‘eventual leadership’ in
[
28
]
) occurs if there is a unique urn containing the maximum number of balls for all but finitely many time-steps. On the other hand
monopoly
(a term coined in
[
11
]
), occurs if for all but finitely many time-steps, a single urn is chosen for new additions of balls. A well-known result, as stated in
[
28
]
, is the following:

Theorem

(From
[
11
,
23
,
26
,
31
,
27
,
28
]
)
.

In the balls-in-bins process, with feedback function
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, regardless of the initial conditions of the process:

•

Monopoly occurs with probability zero or one. Moreover, monopoly occurs with probability one if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}1/f(j)<\infty
.

•

If the feedback function
f
is bounded from below, strict leadership occurs with probability zero or one. Moreover, strict leadership occurs with probability one if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}1/f(j)^{2}<\infty
.

The first result concerning monopoly comes from Rubin’s argument (presented in
[
9
]
). On the other hand, the result regarding strict leadership was first proved in the case

in
[
23
]
, and generalised by subsequent works of Oliveira and Spencer
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. Thus, if

, for

- corresponding to instance of the model studied in, for example,
[
7
,
11
,
23
]
- the model exhibits
phase-transitions
at the values
p=1
and

respectively, corresponding to whether or not monopoly, or strict leadership occur.

It is important to note that the conditions on summability of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)
and
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)^{2}
appear more widely in other models of reinforcement, where
f
, informally, represents the degree of reinforcement. The condition of summability of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)
, appears as a condition for ‘explosion’ in first-passage percolation in trees
[
35
]
, in ‘fixation’ of edge-reinforced random walks
[
9
,
38
,
34
]
, and in ‘connectivity transitions’ of growing trees of the ‘generalised preferential attachment’ type
[
30
,
19
,
20
]
. On the other hand, the condition of summability of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)^{2}
also arise in the context of growing generalised preferential attachment graphs; in criteria for the emergence of the emergence of a ‘persistent hub’. In these latter models, a persistent hub represents a node in the graph whose degree is the largest for all but finitely many time-steps in the evolution of the network
[
10
,
14
,
5
]
.

A common approach to analyse the balls-in-bins model, and other similar models is via a continuous time embedding. This approach dates back to Athreya and Karlin in a related model
[
3
]
, but was first exploited in the context of the balls-in-bins model by Rubin
[
9
]
. One considers the collection of urns as a continuous time Markov process, with the number of balls in a particular urn increasing from
j
to
j+1
independently at rate
f(j)
. The embedded Markov chain recovers the original balls-in-bins model. The continuous time representation immediately gives meaning to condition on the summability of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)
, as a necessary and sufficient condition for explosion of the pure-birth process associated with the number of balls in a particular urn.

There is a large literature related to the balls-in-bins model, concerning properties such as the limiting proportion of balls in certain urn
[
18
,
33
,
2
,
15
,
17
]
, the emergence of ‘weak monopoly’, that is, an urn whose limiting ‘market share’ tends to one
[
15
]
, and the number of balls of ‘losing type’ when monopoly occurs
[
16
]
. Other results are concerned with properties such as the probability of monopoly, or leadership by an urn when one varies the initial conditions of the urns
[
26
,
29
,
28
,
27
]
. A number of generalisations of the model have also been studied, including, models with varied feedback functions across different urns
[
15
,
25
]
, models where the number of balls replaced is asymmetric
[
17
]
, time-dependent models, with random numbers of balls added at each time-step
[
32
,
39
]
, and interacting urn models
[
8
,
24
,
37
]
. We also remark that there is a large literature on urns that generalise the Pólya-Eggenberger urn scheme in other ways, for example
[
3
,
21
,
4
,
22
]
.

However, one limitation of the balls-in-bins model, and its extensions in the literature is that they are Markov processes. Moreover, in many contexts, one might regard the continuous time embedding of this model as more realistic in modelling the growth of values of agents: if values represent the wealth of companies, the times when ‘growth’ take place often occurs in continuous time, rather than in discrete time-steps. However, in these applications it is not desirable to require exponentially distributed waiting times between jumps, as this may not always be realistic. This motivates the present study: the analysis of more general non-Markovian processes which generalise the balls-in-bins model, where the waiting times in the underlying continuous time process are independent but not necessarily exponentially distributed.

1.2
Contributions of this paper and overview

The contributions of this paper are the following:

•

In Theorem
1.4
we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for leadership to occur with probability zero or one in these more general competing growth processes, in other words, conditions for a single agent to have maximum value for all but finitely many time-steps. Moreover, in Theorem
1.4
and Corollary
1.8
we provide sufficient criteria for monopoly and strict leadership to occur with probability zero or with probability one. Unlike leadership, the latter two properties are not zero-one events in general, as shown in Remark
1.6
.

•

In Theorem
1.13
we prove an important auxiliary result which may be of independent interest: necessary and sufficient criteria for the distribution of a convergent sum of independent random variables to have no atom on the real-line. Another auxiliary result, Proposition
1.16
, shows that when a series of independent random variables fails to converge, and the summands are symmetric, the partial sums associated with the series cross the origin infinitely often.

•

The results from Theorem
1.4
shed new light on the summability of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)^{2}
in regards to the balls-in-bins process scheme, showing that this condition arises when analysing the convergence of certain random series in Theorem
1.4
. In particular, applying Theorem
1.4
leads to a new result regarding strict leadership in balls-in-bins schemes in Theorem
1.10
,
allowing for the feedback function to be random, and showing that it need not be bounded from below.

Section
1.3
contains the formal description of the model and the main results related to the model: Theorem
1.4
, Corollary
1.8
and Proposition
2.1
. It also contains auxiliary results on random series that may be of independent interest: Theorem
1.13
and Proposition
1.16
. Section
1.4
deals with applications to the ‘balls-in-bins’ scheme, providing a formal overview of that model, and the main result in Theorem
1.10
. Section
2
includes the proofs of the results appearing in this article, with sub-sections that can generally (aside from a few global definitions) be read independently of each other.

1.3
Model description and main results

We consider a finite family of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
valued growth processes with independent waiting times between jumps. Suppose we have
A\geq 2

agents
labelled by the elements of
[A]:=\left\{1,\ldots,A\right\}
. To each
a\in[A]
, we associate an identically distributed sequence of mutually independent random variables
(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, taking values in
[0,\infty)
. Each agent
a\in[A]
has a
value

v_{a}\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that, the value of
a
at time
t
,
v_{a}(t)
, increases over time. The quantity
X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{k}
is the time taken for the value of
a
to increase from
k-1
to
k
, and the agent
a
begins with a value of
v_{a}(0)
. Thus, given the
initial value

v_{a}(0)
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

v_{a}(t)=v_{a}(0)+k\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a}(0)+k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}\leq t<\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a}(0)+k+1}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}.

We are interested in the vector of values of agents as time evolves, until, possibly, an agent reaches infinite value. To do so, we set
\tau_{0}:=0
. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\tau_{n}:=\inf{\left\{t\geq 0\colon\sum_{a=1}^{A}v_{a}(t)\geq n\right\}}.

(1)

We are generally interested in the discrete process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which represents the evolution of values in the system as the total sum of values of agents increases. We call this a
competing growth process
.

Remark 1.1
.

The main reason for analysing the process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and not the continuous time process
(v_{a}(t))_{a\in[A],t\in[0,\infty)}
, is that we may have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}<\infty
, i.e., explosion may occur. In many applications, there may be a total cap on the total possible sum of ‘values’. For example, if the agents represent companies, there may be a cap on the total possible wealth that may exist. Thus, more relevant is the behaviour of the model before ‘resources’ run out – i.e., the behaviour before explosion.

Remark 1.2
.

As far as we are aware, the general process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
has not been studied in the literature before. However, this process generalises the balls-in-bin process in a similar manner to the way trees associated with
Crump-Mode-Jagers
branching processes generalise ‘preferential attachment trees’ - see, for example,
[
19
]
. There is a large amount of literature concerning Crump-Mode-Jagers branching processes.

Definition 1.3
.

With regards to the process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
:

•

We say
leadership
occurs if for some
a\in[A]
, we have
\max_{a^{\prime}\in[A]}v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})=v_{a}(\tau_{n})
for all but finitely many
n
.

•

We say
strict leadership
occurs, if for some
unique

a\in A
,
\max_{a^{\prime}\in[A]}v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})=v_{a}(\tau_{n})
for all but finitely many
n
.

•

Finally, we say
monopoly
occurs if there is a unique
a\in A
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a}(\tau_{n})=\infty
.

Clearly the occurence of monopoly implies strict leadership, which in turn implies leadership.

The results in this paper involve criteria related to convergence of random series. Recall that, given a sequence of mutually independent random variables
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, by the Kolmogorov
0
-
1
law, the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
either converges almost surely or diverges almost surely. Necessary and sufficient conditions for this convergence are provided by the classical Kolmogorov three series theorem - see Section
1.5
. Note also, that divergence of a series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}
of non-negative terms is equivalent to
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}=\infty
.

The first main result is a general classification of leadership in the process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Suppose that
(X_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},(X^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
denote generic, independent sequences with
(X_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sim(X^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sim(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

In the process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we have the following:

1.

Leadership occurs with probability zero or one. Moreover, leadership occurs with probability one if and only if the random series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})
converges almost surely.

2.

If
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}
diverges almost surely, then monopoly occurs with probability zero. On the other hand, if
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}
converges almost surely, and either

(a)

For any
a\neq a^{\prime}\in[A]
,
\exists j>v_{a}(0)\wedge v_{a^{\prime}}(0)
such that
X_{j}
has no atom on
[0,\infty)
, or,

(b)

For any sequence
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}\neq c_{j}\right)=\infty
,

then monopoly occurs with probability one.

3.

If the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})
converges almost surely, and, for any
a\neq a^{\prime}\in[A]

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(0\leq\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}(X^{\prime}_{j}-X_{j})-\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a^{\prime}}(0)}X_{j}<X_{k+1}\right)<\infty,

(2)

strict leadership occurs with probability one.

Remark 1.5
.

Convergence of random series have been used in proving sufficiency of
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/f(j)^{2}
for leadership in the balls-in-bins model (see, for example
[
23
,
28
]
). The main technicality of this article lies in extending these criteria to the general setting, using Theorem
1.13
– the proof is comparatively much simpler when we can assume that distribution of one of the
X_{j}
has no atom on
[0,\infty)
. Meanwhile, in the proof of necessity, we use Proposition
1.16
– the techniques used for this direction in the balls-in-bins model (or related preferential attachment models), involving normal approximation or approximation by Brownian motion
[
23
,
28
,
10
,
5
]
, do not extend to the general setting where variances of waiting times may not exist.

Remark 1.6
.

Item
1
of Theorem
1.4
shows that leadership occurs with probability
0
or
1
in general. This does not seem to be immediately clear from applying, for example, standard zero-one laws. Moreover, it is actually the case that monopoly and strict leadership are
not
zero-one events in general, this is the content of Proposition
2.1
, stated and proved in Section
2.2
. Intuitively, the added requirement of having a ‘unique’ leader removes the zero-one property. In addition, note that leadership is a property that does not depend on the initial condition, whereas this is not the case for monopoly or strict leadership.

Remark 1.7
.

In the definition of
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
the sequences
(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are identically distributed across
a\in[A]
. We believe it may be possible to extend the results, to some extent, to the case where the sequences
(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent, but not identically distributed, random variables, but do not endeavour to do this here. Such processes would generalise balls-in-bins-models with asymmetric feedback (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
).

The following corollary provides criteria for strict leadership which may be easier to verify:

Corollary 1.8
.

In the process
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, if the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})
converges almost surely,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j}\neq 0\right)=\infty
,
and, for any
\varepsilon>0

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}>\varepsilon\right)<\infty,

(3)

then strict leadership occurs with probability one.

1.4
Applications to balls-in-bins models

As previously mentioned, a particular instance of the model
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the
balls-in-bins
model with, possibly random feedback function (see also Proposition
2.5
for explicit proof). Suppose one begins at time zero with
A
urns,
(u_{a}(0))_{a\in[A]}
, with each
u_{a}(0)\in\mathbb{N}
, representing the number of
balls
that urn contains. To each urn
a\in[A]
is a mutually independent sequence of random variables
(F_{a}(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where, for each
a,a^{\prime}\in[A]
, we have
(F_{a}(j)_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sim(F_{a^{\prime}}(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Definition 1.9
.

Given an initial condition
(u_{a}(0))_{a\in[A]}
, and random variables
(F_{a}(j))_{a\in[A],j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, the balls-in-bins process with feedback
(u_{a}(n))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is the discrete time Markov process defined recursively as follows.

1.

At time
0
, sample the random variables
(F_{a}(u_{a}(0)))_{a\in[A]}
.

2.

At time
n+1
, given the collection
(u_{a}(n))_{a\in[A]}
, and
(F_{a}(u_{a}(n)))_{a\in[A]}
, sample an urn
a
with probability

\frac{F_{a}(u_{a}(n))}{\sum_{a=1}^{A}F_{a}(u_{a}(n))}.

Set
u_{a}(n+1)=u_{a}(n)+1
, and for
a^{\prime}\neq a
, set
u_{a^{\prime}}(n+1)=u_{a^{\prime}}(n)
. Sample
F_{a}(u_{a}(n+1))
.

Suppose
(F(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
(F^{\prime}(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
denote generic, independent sequences of independent random variables with
(F(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sim(F^{\prime}(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sim(F_{a}(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, for
a\in[A]
.
Suppose that the events of leadership, strict leadership and monopoly from Definition
1.3
are defined analogously for the balls-in-bins model with feedback
(u_{a}(n))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Theorem 1.10
.

In the balls-in-bins model with feedback
(u_{a}(n))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, with feedback function given by
(F(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we have the following statements:

1.

Monopoly occurs with probability zero or one. Moreover, monopoly occurs with probability one if and only if
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/F(j)<\infty
almost surely.

2.

Strict leadership occurs with probability zero or one. Moreover, strict leadership occurs with probability one if and only if
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/F(j)^{2}<\infty
almost surely.

Remark 1.11
.

Whilst Item
1
of Theorem
1.10
has not been stated in the context with random feedback functions before, the proof of Item

uses essentially the same argument as that from Rubin
[
9
]
. However, we include its proof for completeness. Moreover, as mentioned in Remark
1.5
one of the directions of Item

uses similar ideas to

, but the general result requires novel techniques.

1.5
Auxiliary results on the convergence of random series

As the results of this paper rely heavily on criteria for convergence of random series of independent random variables, we recall here the well-known Kolmogorov three series theorem:

Theorem

(Kolmogorov three series theorem, e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 2.5.8., page 73]
)
.

For a sequence of mutually independent random variables
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, let
C>0
be given. Then the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
converges almost surely if and only if

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|S_{j}\right|>C\right)<\infty,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{\left|S_{j}\right|\leq C}\right]<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits\left(S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{\left|S_{j}\right|\leq C}\right)<\infty

(4)

Remark 1.12
.

Recall that, if
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\right]<\infty
and
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits\left(S_{j}\right)<\infty
then
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
converges almost surely - this is the Kolmogorov two series theorem.
Note also that, if each
S_{j}\geq 0
almost surely, then one only needs to check convergence of the first two series in (
4
) - see Proposition
2.4
.

The proofs of Theorem
1.4
and Corollary
1.8
use Theorem
1.13
whilst the proof of Theorem
1.4
also uses Proposition
1.16
. We believe that Theorem
1.13
may be of interest in its own right.

Theorem 1.13
.

Suppose that
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of mutually independent random variables such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
converges almost surely. Then the distribution of
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
contains an atom on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if, for some collection
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}

\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j}\right)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq c_{j}\right)<\infty.

(5)

Remark 1.14
.

Equation (
5
) in Theorem
1.13
, together with the Borel–Cantelli lemma shows that a convergent series of random variables
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
contains an atom, if and only if, the random variables
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
coincide with deterministic values
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for all but finitely many
j
.

Remark 1.15
.

Note that, if the random variables
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are symmetric, Equation (
55
) can only be satisfied if

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq 0\right)<\infty.

(6)

Indeed, Equation (
55
) implies that, for
j
sufficiently large,
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=-c_{j}\right)>1/2
, which is impossible if infinitely many values of
c_{j}
are non-zero.

Finally, we have the following useful proposition:

Proposition 1.16

(Divergence of series of symmetric random variables)
.

Suppose that
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of mutually independent, symmetric random variables. If
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
diverges almost surely, then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=-\infty\quad\text{almost surely.}

(7)

2.1
Notation

In what follows, it will be helpful to have explicit notation for the events of leadership, strict leadership and monopoly.
For an agent
a\in[A]
, we define the events of
leadership
of
a
,
\lead_{a}
,
monopoly
of
a
,
\mon_{a}
, and
strict leadership
of
a
,
\slead_{a}
respectively by

\lead_{a}:=\left\{\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\colon\;\forall n\geq N\;\forall a^{\prime}\in[A]\;a^{\prime}\neq a\implies v_{a}(\tau_{n})\geq v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})\right\}.

(8)

\mon_{a}:=\left\{\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\colon\;\forall n\geq N\;v_{a}(\tau_{n})=v_{a}(\tau_{N})\right\},

(9)

and

\slead_{a}:=\left\{\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\colon\;\forall n\geq N\;\forall a^{\prime}\in[A]\;a^{\prime}\neq a\implies v_{a}(\tau_{n})>v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})\right\}.

(10)

Thus, leadership coincides with the event
\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\lead_{a}
, monopoly with
\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\mon_{a}
and strict leadership with
\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\slead_{a}
.

We assume throughout that ‘empty’ sums (for example
\sum_{j=1}^{0}X_{j}
) are zero. Moreover, given a collection of events
(\mathcal{E}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in a probability space, we use the abbreviation “
\mathcal{E}_{j}\text{ i. o.}
” to denote the event that infinitely many
\mathcal{E}_{j}
occur, i.e.,
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j=n}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{j}
occurs.

2.2
Proofs of Theorem
1.4
, Corollary
1.8
and Proposition
2.1

We start with the proof of Theorem
1.4
, which is self-contained, except for usage of Theorem
1.13
and Proposition
1.16
. The proofs of Corollary
1.8
and Proposition

use ideas, and sometimes notation, from the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Item
1
of Theorem
1.4
.

For Item
1
, suppose, first, that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})
converges almost surely. We show that given any two agents, eventually one of them has a value that does not exceed the other, i.e. one is a ‘winner’. Thus, by iteratively ordering agents, we can find a leader.

In this regard, suppose
a,a^{\prime}\in[A]
are agents.
Then we define

\win(a,a^{\prime}):=\left\{\exists N\in\mathbb{N}\colon\;\forall n\geq N\;v_{a}(\tau_{n})\geq v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})\right\}.

Note that
\win(\cdot,\cdot)
leads to a transitive relation, in the sense that, for
a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in[A]

\win(a,a^{\prime})\cap\win(a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\win(a,a^{\prime\prime}).

Our goal is to show that the relation is complete, that is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{a\neq a^{\prime}}\left(\win(a,a^{\prime})\cup\win(a^{\prime},a)\right)\right)=1,

(11)

so that, by iteratively ordering elements, the event
\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\bigcap_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\win(a,a^{\prime})=\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\lead_{a}
occurs.
For (
11
), suppose that
a,a^{\prime}\in[A]
with
a\neq a^{\prime}
, and assume, without loss of generality, that
v_{a}(0)\leq v_{a^{\prime}}(0)
. Now, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a}(\tau_{n})<\infty
or
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})<\infty
, note that either
\win(a,a^{\prime})
occurs or
\win(a^{\prime},a)
occurs, depending on whether or not
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a}(\tau_{n})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})
. Thus, we may assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a}(\tau_{n})=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{a^{\prime}}(\tau_{n})=\infty
. There are now two cases.
First, suppose that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}\neq X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\right)=\infty
. Then, noting that the random variables
X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j}
are symmetric, by Theorem
1.13
and Remark
1.15
, the almost surely convergent sum

\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a^{\prime}}(0)}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}+\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{\infty}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}-X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\right),

(12)

contains no atom at
0
, and thus is almost surely strictly positive or negative. Therefore, with probability one, for all
k
sufficiently large,

\text{either}\quad\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}<\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\quad\text{or}\quad\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}<\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}.

(13)

The partial sum
\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}
represents the time taken for the the value of
a
to reach
k
, and similarly for
a^{\prime}
. Thus (
13
) implies that one of
a
or
a^{\prime}
is a winner, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\left(\win(a,a^{\prime})\cup\win(a^{\prime},a)\right)=1
. As the finite intersection of almost sure events, (
11
) follows.
If, however,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}\neq X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\right)<\infty
, then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}=X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}
for all but finitely many
j
. This implies that eventually, the partial sums converging to (
12
) are constant: the convergent series has all but finitely many terms equal to zero. Thus, for all
k
sufficiently large, either (
13
) holds, or

\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}=\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}.

(14)

As
\win(a,a^{\prime})\cup\win(a^{\prime},a)
also allows for the case that
both

a
and
a^{\prime}
have the same values for
n
sufficiently large, again, this implies
\mathbb{P}\left(\win(a,a^{\prime})\cup\win(a^{\prime},a)\right)=1
, and thus implies (
11
).

For the converse direction of Item
1
, suppose that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})
diverges almost surely, and again, assume without loss of generality that
v_{a}(0)\leq v_{a^{\prime}}(0)
. Then by Proposition
1.16
, with probability one we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a^{\prime}}(0)}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}+\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}-X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\right)=\infty\quad\text{and}

(15)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\liminf_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{v_{a^{\prime}}(0)}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}+\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}-X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\right)=-\infty.

(16)

Therefore, we have both

\displaystyle\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}<\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\quad\text{for infinitely many $k$, and }

(17)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\sum_{j=v_{a}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}>\sum_{j=v_{a^{\prime}}(0)+1}^{k}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a^{\prime}}})}}_{j}\quad\text{for infinitely many $k$.}

(18)

This implies that

, and, since

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\lead_{a}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{a\in[A]}\bigcap_{a^{\prime}\neq a}\win(a,a^{\prime})\right)=0.

∎

Suppose that, for each

is given by the ‘minimum time’ taken for an agent to increase in value, after the total sum of values is
n
. The fourth equality follows from the memoryless property of the exponential distribution: for each
a\in[A]
, the distribution of
X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{v_{a}(\tau_{n})+1}
is invariant under conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
. Indeed, either
\tau_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{v_{a}(\tau_{n})}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}
, in which case
X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{v_{a}(\tau_{n})+1}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
, or,
\tau_{n}>\sum_{j=1}^{v_{a}(\tau_{n})}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}
, hence, by the memoryless property, for any
c\in[0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{v_{a}(\tau_{n})+1}>\bigg(\tau_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{v_{a}(\tau_{n})}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}\bigg)+c\,\bigg|\,X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{v_{a}(\tau_{n})+1}>\tau_{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{v_{a}(\tau_{n})}X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{j}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({a}})}}_{v_{a}(\tau_{n})+1}>c\right),

from which the claim follows. Now, by the properties concerning minima of independent exponential random variables, the right side of (
28
) is equal to

\frac{F_{a}(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))}{\sum_{a=1}^{A}F_{a}(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))}.

Thus, given
(F_{a}(j))_{a\in[A],j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
(v_{a}(\tau_{n}))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
follows the same transition probabilities as the balls-in-bins process
(u_{a}(n))_{a\in[A],n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
as defined in Definition
1.9
. The result follows.
∎

For Item

is a strengthening of the Kolmogorov three series theorem when the summands are non-negative.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of mutually independent random variables taking values in
[0,\infty)
. Let
C>0
be given. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}<\infty
if and only if

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}>C\right)<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right]<\infty.

(30)

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Suppose that, for some
C>0
,

either
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}>C\right)=\infty
or
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right]=\infty
.

Then we already know from the Kolmogorov three series theorem that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}=\infty
almost surely. For the other direction, we use the following theorem from
[
6
]
.

Claim 2.4.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
Y
be a random variable taking values in
[m,M]
. Then

\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits\left(Y\right)\leq(M-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right])(\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]-m).

The random variable
S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}
takes values in
[0,C]
, and clearly
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}}\right]\geq 0
. Therefore, by Claim
2.4.1
,

\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits\left(S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right)\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right],

thus,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right]<\infty
implies that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits\left(S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}\leq C}\right)<\infty
. The result, therefore, follows from the Kolmogorov three series theorem.

Finally, to prove Proposition

, we use the following elementary lemma:

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are exponentially distributed, with parameters
r,r^{\prime}
respectively. Then, for any
C>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z|>C\right)=\frac{r^{\prime}}{r+r^{\prime}}e^{-rc}+\frac{r}{r+r^{\prime}}e^{-r^{\prime}c},

(33)

and

\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|Z|\leq C}\right]=\frac{2}{r+r^{\prime}}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}q(Cr)}{r^{2}}+\frac{rq(Cr^{\prime})}{(r^{\prime})^{2}}\right)\quad\text{ where }\quad q(x)=1-e^{-x}(1+x+x^{2}/2).

(34)

In particular,

\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\right]=\frac{2}{r+r^{\prime}}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r^{2}}+\frac{r}{(r^{\prime})^{2}}\right).

(35)

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

Note that by Fubini’s theorem, for a non-negative random variable
Y
, and
s>0

\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{s}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{Y}sy^{s-1}\mathrm{d}y\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}sy^{s-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y\geq y}\mathrm{d}y\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}sy^{s-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq y\right)\mathrm{d}y.

(36)

Moreover, if
Z:=Y-Y^{\prime}
, then for all
y>0

\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq y\,\bigg|\,Z\geq 0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq Y^{\prime}+y\,\bigg|\,Y\geq Y^{\prime}\right)=e^{-ry},

and since
\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq 0\right)=\frac{r^{\prime}}{r+r^{\prime}}
, for each
y\geq 0
we have
\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq y\right)=\frac{r^{\prime}}{r+r^{\prime}}e^{-ry}
. Writing
Z=Z\mathbf{1}_{Z\geq 0}+Z\mathbf{1}_{Z<0}
, this already yields (
33
) by symmetry. Finally, by applying (
36
) to the random variable
Y:=Z\mathbf{1}_{0\leq Z\leq C}
, with
s=2
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\mathbf{1}_{0\leq Z\leq C}\right]=\frac{r^{\prime}}{r+r^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{C}2y\left(e^{-ry}-e^{-rC}\right)\mathrm{d}y=\frac{2r^{\prime}}{r+r^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1-e^{-Cr}(1+cr+(cr)^{2}/2)}{r^{2}}\right),

leading again, by symmetry to (
34
). Equation (
35
) follows from sending
C\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

.

We first show that

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j}\right)\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F(j)\leq\eta\right\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F^{\prime}(j)\leq\eta\right\}}\right)\quad\text{ converges, almost surely.}

(37)

On the other hand, the series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F(j),F^{\prime}(j)>\eta\right\}}

35
) in Lemma
2.6

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_{j+1}-X^{\prime}_{j+1}\right)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F(j),F^{\prime}(j)>\eta\right\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{F(j)+F^{\prime}(j)}\left(\frac{F^{\prime}(j)}{F(j)^{2}}+\frac{F(j)}{F^{\prime}(j)^{2}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F(j),F^{\prime}(j)>\eta\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{F(j)^{3}+F^{\prime}(j)^{3}}{\left(F(j)+F^{\prime}(j)\right)F(j)^{2}F^{\prime}(j)^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F(j),F^{\prime}(j)>\eta\right\}}\right]

(40)

where the second to last equality uses the factorisation

.

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(X_{j}-X^{\prime}_{j})

2.4
Proof of Theorem

The heart of Theorem

lies in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of mutually independent, symmetric random variables, with
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}<\infty
almost surely. Suppose also that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}\neq 0\right)=\infty

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right)=0.

We first prove Theorem
1.13
, then prove Proposition
2.7
over the rest of the section. For Theorem
1.13
, we also require the following lemmata

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose that
Y,Y^{\prime}
are i.i.d. random variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}
. Then the distribution of
Y
contains an atom in
\mathbb{R}
if and only if
\mathbb{P}\left(Y=Y^{\prime}\right)>0
.

Proof of Lemma
2.8
.

If for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\mathbb{P}\left(Y=c\right)>0
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(Y=Y^{\prime}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y-Y^{\prime}=0\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Y=c,Y^{\prime}=c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y=c\right)^{2}.

(53)

On the other hand,

\mathbb{P}\left(Y-Y^{\prime}=0\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[Y=Y^{\prime}\,\bigg|\,Y^{\prime}\right]\right].

(54)

If for all
c>0
,
\mathbb{P}\left(Y=c\right)=0
, then the term inside the expectation in (
54
) is zero almost surely, hence
\mathbb{P}\left(Y-Y^{\prime}=0\right)=0
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent sequences of mutually independent random variables, with
Y_{j}\sim Y^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

For some collection
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
,

\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j}\right)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq c_{j}\right)<\infty.

2.

\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\infty.

Proof of Lemma
2.9
.

To show that the Statement
1
implies Statement
2
, note that, if such a sequence
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is given, then
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j},Y^{\prime}_{j}=c_{j}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j}\right)^{2}>0
. Moreover, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{Y_{j}\neq c_{j}\text{ i.o.}\right\}\cup\left\{Y^{\prime}_{j}\neq c_{j}\text{ i.o.}\right\}\right)=0.

Hence, almost surely, for all but finitely many
j
,
Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}=c_{j}
. Again, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it must be the case that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\infty
, hence Statement
2
is satisfied.

On the other hand, suppose Statement
2
is satisfied. Then, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\text{ i.o.}\right)=0.

Now, if we first sample the sequence
(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, it must be the case that

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\text{ i.o.}\,\bigg|\,(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\right)=0\quad\text{almost surely.}

But, by independence of
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, this implies that there must be a sequence
(c^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, coinciding with the realisation of
(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq c^{\prime}_{j}\text{ i.o.}\right)=0
. By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, this implies that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq c^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\infty
. As a result,
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c^{\prime}_{j}\right)>0
for all
j\geq j_{0}
, say. To form
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, we set
c_{j}=c^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j\geq j_{0}
. For
j<j_{0}
, by applying Lemma
2.8
we may choose values
c_{j}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=c_{j}\right)>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

First note that, if
(Y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent sequences of mutually independent random variables, and
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
converges almost surely, then by Lemma
2.8
applied to the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
, the distribution of
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
has an atom if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j})
has an atom at
0
. Now, by Lemma
2.9
, it suffices to show that the distribution of
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Y_{j}
contains an atom on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if

\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\infty.

(55)

If Equation (
55
) is satisfied, then we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j})=0\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\{Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j}=0\right\}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)\right)>0,

since
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}\neq Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)<\infty
, and each
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)>0
.

If Equation (
55
) is not satisfied because, for some
i
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}\neq Y_{i}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}-Y^{\prime}_{i}\neq 0\right)=1
, by Lemma
2.8
,
Y_{i}
cannot have an atom on
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j})=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}=Y^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}(Y^{\prime}_{j}-Y_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}=Y^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}(Y^{\prime}_{j}-Y_{j})\,\bigg|\,Y^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}(Y^{\prime}_{j}-Y_{j})\right)\right]=0,

(56)

where the final equality in (
56
) follows from the fact that, since
Y_{i}
has no atom, the term inside the expectation in (
56
) is zero almost surely. Finally, if Equation (
55
) is not satisfied because
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{j}=Y^{\prime}_{j}\right)=\infty
, we can apply Proposition
2.7
to the sequence
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}:=(Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
to again show that
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(Y_{j}-Y^{\prime}_{j})=0\right)=0
.
∎

2.4.1
Proof of Proposition
2.7

We first prove the following:

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of mutually independent, symmetric random variables, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
converges almost surely. Suppose that for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=0\right)=0
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}=0\right)=0.

Given Lemma
2.10
, we are ready to prove Proposition
2.7

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

We construct a random variable with the same distribution as series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}
in two stages. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
I_{k}
be a Bernoulli random variable such that
\mathbb{P}\left(I_{k}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}\neq 0\right)
, and set
\mathcal{I}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\colon I_{k}=1\right\}
. By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, and the assumption that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}\neq 0\right)=\infty
, we have
|\mathcal{I}|=\infty
almost surely. Now, we define random variables
\tilde{S_{i}},i\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{S}_{i}\in A\right)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}\in A\right)/\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}\neq 0\right)&\text{if $\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}\neq 0\right)>0$, }\\
0&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}.

By construction, one can readily verify that we have
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}\sim\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{S}_{i}
. In addition, since each
S_{i}
is symmetric, one can verify that
\tilde{S}_{i}
is symmetric. Finally, after conditioning on the random, almost surely infinite set
\mathcal{I}
,
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{S}_{i}
is a sum of symmetric, mutually independent, almost surely non-zero random variables. Thus, by Lemma
2.10
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{S}_{i}=0\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{S}_{i}=0\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{I}\right)\right]=0.

∎

The proof of Lemma
2.10
is a bit more technical. The idea is that, since each
S_{k}
is symmetric, if
S_{k}
contains an atom at
c\neq 0
,
S_{k}
also contains an atom at
-c
. By ‘flipping’ the value of
S_{k}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}S_{j}=0,S_{k}=c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}=-2c,S_{k}=-c\right)
. One can then leverage this idea to show that the distribution of
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}
has ‘too many atoms’ to be a probability distribution.

In order to prove Lemma
2.10
, we introduce some notation with regards to the measures describing random variables. For a random variable
Y
with values in
\mathbb{R}
, we denote the measure associated with
Y
by
\mu^{Y}
. Given a measurable set
U
, we denote by
\mu^{Y}|_{U}
the restriction of the measure
\mu^{Y}
to the set
U
. Then given
Y
, we define

\mathcal{A}_{Y}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mu^{Y}(\{x\})>0\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
is at most countable, hence measurable. We can therefore define the quantities
\mu^{Y}_{\text{disc.}}
,
\mu^{Y}_{\text{cont.}}
such that

\mu^{Y}_{\text{disc.}}:=\mu^{Y}|_{\mathcal{A}_{Y}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{S}_{\text{cont.}}:=\mu^{Y}|_{\mathcal{A}_{Y}^{c}}\quad\text{so that}\quad\mu^{Y}=\mu^{Y}_{\text{disc.}}+\mu^{Y}_{\text{cont.}}.

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we also use the notation
cA:=\{cx\colon x\in A\}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.10
.

We first have the following claim

Claim 2.10.1
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{S_{k}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,|S_{k}|\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{k}|}\right).

(57)

Claim
2.10.1
readily implies that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|S_{k}|\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{k}|}\right)=\mu_{\disc}^{S_{k}}(\mathbb{R}),

hence implies Lemma
2.10
if
\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{\disc}^{S_{k}}(\mathbb{R})=0
. Therefore, assume, in order to obtain a contradiction, that
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right)>0
, and, for simplicity of notation, set

p_{1}:=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\right),\quad p_{2}:=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{\disc}^{S_{k}}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{ so that}\quad 0<p_{1}\leq p_{2}.

Claim 2.10.2
.

Suppose
p_{1}>0
. Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,p_{1})
, there exists sequences
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
,
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
with

c_{1}>c_{2}>\cdots>0,\quad n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\cdots,\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}n_{k}=\infty;

such that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mu_{\disc}^{|S_{k}|}((c_{k+1},c_{k}])>\mu_{\disc}^{|S_{k}|}([0,\infty))-p_{1}+\varepsilon.

(58)

For
\varepsilon>0
and sequences
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
,
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying the conditions of Claim
2.10.2
, we first observe that, for each
n_{k}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{{n_{k}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{{n_{k}}}|}\cap(c_{k+1},c_{k}]\right)\geq\varepsilon.

(59)

Indeed, if not, note that (
58
) implies that
\mu_{\disc}^{|S_{{n_{k}}}|}((c_{k+1},c_{k}]^{c})<p_{1}-\varepsilon
. Therefore, by Claim
2.10.1
,

p_{1}=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{{n_{k}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{{n_{k}}}|}\cap(c_{k+1},c_{k}]\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{{n_{k}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{{n_{k}}}|}\cap(c_{k+1},c_{k}]^{c}\right)<\varepsilon+p_{1}-\varepsilon,

a contradiction. Therefore, assume (
59
). For simplicity of notation, set
P_{k}:=\mathcal{A}_{|S_{{n_{k}}}|}\cap(c_{k+1},c_{k}]
. Note now that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, exploiting the symmetry of the random variables
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,|S_{n_{k}}|\in P_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in P_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,|S_{n_{k}}|=x\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x\in P_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\neq n_{k}}S_{i}=-x,S_{n_{k}}=x\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x\in P_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\neq n_{k}}S_{i}=-x,S_{n_{k}}=-x\right)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}\in\left(-2P_{k}\cup 2P_{k}\right)\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon=\infty,

Proof of Claim

.

For a given

, suppose, in order to obtain a contradiction, that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,|S_{k}|\in\mathcal{A}_{|S_{k}|}^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{S_{k}}^{c}\right)>0.

Then by conditioning on the value of

(using the associated regular conditional probability measure), and exploiting the mutual independence of the random variables

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0,S_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{S_{k}}^{c}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}=0\,\bigg|\,S_{k}\right)\mathbf{1}_{S_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{S_{k}}^{c}}\right]=\int_{B}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\neq k}S_{i}=-x\right)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\cont}^{S_{k}}(x)>0.

The latter equation implies that

\mu_{\cont}^{S_{k}}\left(\left\{x\in B\colon\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\neq k}S_{i}=-x\right)>0\right\}\right)>0.

Since, for each
y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

, this implies that the set
\left\{x\in B\colon\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\neq k}S_{i}=-x\right)>0\right\}

Proof of Claim

\mu_{\disc}^{|S_{1}|}((0,c_{1}])>\mu_{\disc}^{|S_{1}|}([0,\infty))-\frac{\delta}{2},

and similarly, since

2.5
Proof of Proposition
1.16

For Proposition
1.16
, we use the following generalisation of the martingale convergence theorem, for martingales with bounded increments.

Theorem 2.11

(See, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 4.3.1, page 194]
)
.

Let
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a martingale sequence, such that, for some
C>0
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\left|Y_{k+1}-Y_{k}\right|\leq C\quad\text{almost surely.}

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}\text{ exists and is finite}\right\}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{2}:=\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}=\infty\right\}\cap\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}Y_{n}=-\infty\right\}
. Then
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}\right)=1
.

Proof of Proposition
1.16
.

First note that the event
\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\right\}
is a tail event with respect to the sequence of independent random variables
(S_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, hence by the Kolmogorov
0
-
1
law occurs with probability
0
or
1
. Note also that

\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\right\}=\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}-S_{j}=-\infty\right\},

and hence, if the left side occurs with probability
1
, so does the right. Since
\sum_{j=1}^{n}-S_{j}\sim\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}
, we deduce that the events

\displaystyle\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\right\}\cap\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=-\infty\right\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\right\}\cup\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=-\infty\right\}

(63)

coincide up to null sets. We now consider the various cases under which the Kolmogorov three series theorem is not satisfied. Suppose first, that Equation (
4
) is not satisfied by having, for each
C>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|S_{j}|>C\right)=\infty.

Then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, and taking a countable intersection of almost sure events, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{C\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{|S_{j}|>C\,\text{ i.o.}\right\}\right)=1.

(64)

As a result, for each fixed
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}S_{j}\leq a,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\geq b\right)=0.

Indeed, if, with positive probability,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\leq a
and
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\geq b
, then with positive probability, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n\geq N
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\geq b-1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\leq a+1
. But, by (
64
),
|S_{j}|>|a|+|b|+2
for infinitely many
j
almost surely, so this cannot be the case. Therefore, by a union bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}<\infty,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}>-\infty\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\leq a,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\geq b\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\leq a,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\geq b\right)=0.

Therefore
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=\infty\right\}\cup\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}=-\infty\right\}\right)=1
, and Equation (
7
) follows from (
63
).

The other case where Equation (
4
) is not satisfied is when there exists
C>0
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|S_{j}|>C\right)<\infty,\text{ but }\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}\right]=\infty.

Now, by Borel-Cantelli, there are only finitely many terms
S_{j}
such that
|S_{j}|>C
, and since
|S_{j}|<\infty
almost surely, these terms make only a finite contribution to
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}
, and likewise,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}
. Therefore, it suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}=\infty,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}=-\infty\right)=1.

But now, if we set
M_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}
,
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale sequence, with
|M_{n}-M_{n-1}|\leq C
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.11

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}\text{ exists and is finite }\right\}\cup\left\{\limsup_{n\to\infty}M_{n}=\infty,\liminf_{n\to\infty}M_{n}=-\infty\right\}\right)=1.

Since by (
63
), both events in the probability occur with probability
0
or
1
, we need only show that the series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}S_{j}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}\quad\text{ does not converge almost surely.}

But now, since
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{j}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{|S_{j}|\leq C}\right]=\infty
this follows from the converse direction of the Kolmogorov three series theorem.
∎
