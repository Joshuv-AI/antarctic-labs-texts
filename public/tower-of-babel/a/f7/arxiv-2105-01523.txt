Free energy surface of two-step nucleation
Dean Eaton, Ivan Saika-Voivod, Richard K. Bowles, Peter H. Poole

Abstract
We test the theoretical free energy surface (FES) for two-step nucleation (TSN) proposed by Iwamatsu [J. Chem. Phys. 134 , 164508 (2011)] by comparing the predictions of the theory to numerical results for the FES recently reported from Monte Carlo simulations of TSN in a simple lattice system [James, et al., J. Chem. Phys. 150 , 074501 (2019)]. No adjustable parameters are used to make this comparison. That is, all the parameters of the theory are evaluated directly for the model system, yielding a predicted FES which we then compare to the FES obtained from simulations. We find that the theoretical FES successfully predicts the numerically-evaluated FES over a range of thermodynamic conditions that spans distinct regimes of behavior associated with TSN. All the qualitative features of the FES are captured by the theory and the quantitative comparison is also very good. Our results demonstrate that Iwamatsu’s extension of classical nucleation theory provides an excellent framework for understanding the thermodynamics of TSN.



I
Introduction

In a simple nucleation process, the nucleus of a new stable phase forms and grows from within a homogeneous metastable phase,
e.g. when a liquid droplet appears in a supersaturated vapour.
Classical nucleation theory (CNT) has long been a valuable tool for conceptualizing the nature of simple nucleation processes and for providing a starting point for quantitative estimates of nucleation barriers and rates
Debenedetti 1996
;
Kashchiev 2000
;
Kelton and Greer 2010
. The accuracy and limitations of CNT have been tested by comparisons with experiments and computer simulations. In the case of simulations, tests of CNT are often facilitated by the direct evaluation of the physical parameters that appear in the theory, such as chemical potentials, surface tensions, and nucleus growth rates.
Such tests have guided the refinement of CNT-based approaches to improve quantitative predictions for real systems; see e.g. Refs.
Ryu and Cai 2010
;
Espinosa et al. 2014
;
Richard and Speck 2018
.

Complex non-classical nucleation processes that deviate significantly from the predictions of CNT are receiving increased attention in recent years
Sosso et al. 2016
;
Karthika, Radhakrishnan, and Kalaichelvi 2016
;
Jehannin, Rao, and Cölfen 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2019
.
In particular, “two-step nucleation" (TSN) has become a focus of interest
due to its role in important phenomena such as biomineralization and protein crystallization
Vekilov 2004
;
van Meel et al. 2008
;
Vekilov 2010
;
Iwamatsu 2011
;
Sear 2012
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Lutsko 2019
;
Kashchiev 2020
;
L’vov and Umantsev 2020
;
Shao, Yang, and Tang 2020
.
The TSN process is shown schematically in the upper panels of Fig.
1
.
In the first step of TSN, a cluster of an intermediate phase appears within the homogeneous metastable phase.
In the second step, the stable phase appears and grows from within the finite cluster of the intermediate phase.
TSN has been identified and studied in a growing range of systems, both in experiments
Vekilov 2004
;
Vekilov 2010
;
Peng et al. 2014
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Ishizuka et al. 2016
;
Zhang 2017
;
Yamazaki et al. 2017
;
Gebauer, Völkel, and Cölfen 2008
;
Pouget et al. 2009
;
Ou et al. 2019
;
Fang, Hagan, and Rogers 2020

and simulations
Duff and Peters 2009
;
Vatamanu and Kusalik 2010
;
Whitelam 2010
;
Wallace et al. 2013
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Lifanov, Vorselaars, and Quigley 2016
;
Santra, Singh, and Bagchi 2018
;
Kumar and Molinero 2018
;
James et al. 2019
;
Schmid and Nielaba 2019
;
Shi and Tanaka 2019
;
Jiang, Debenedetti, and Panagiotopoulos 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Arjun, Berendsen, and Bolhuis 2019
.

To develop a theoretical framework for TSN it is natural to use CNT as a starting point
Iwamatsu 2011
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Banerjee and Bagchi 2018
;
Kashchiev 2020
;
Shao, Yang, and Tang 2020
.
The thermodynamics of simple (i.e. one-step) nucleation in CNT is described by the model for the free energy of formation of a nucleus of size
n
, which in three dimensions is given by,

G=n\,\Delta\mu+\phi\,n^{2/3}\sigma.

(1)

Here
\Delta\mu
is the difference in chemical potential between the metastable and stable phases,
\sigma
is the surface tension between the two phases, and
\phi
is a shape factor. This model for
G
captures the competition between the decrease of the free energy as
n
monomers coalesce to form a cluster of the stable phase, and the increase in the free energy due to the cost of the interface, having surface area
\phi\,n^{2/3}
, separating the two phases. In the case of TSN, the reaction coordinate
n
must be replaced by at least two coordinates, e.g. one to quantify the overall size of the nucleus, and another to specify the proportion of the intermediate and stable phases occurring within the nucleus. The model for
G
in TSN will therefore be a free energy surface (FES), rather than a single-variable function such as that given in Eq.
1
.

An early proposal for the FES of TSN was presented by Iwamatsu in 2011
Iwamatsu 2011
. As described in detail below, this model FES is formulated as a sum of two CNT-like contributions, one for the formation of the intermediate phase from the metastable phase, and the other for the formation of the stable phase within the intermediate phase droplet. An additional term is included to account for the interaction between the interfaces within the multiphase nucleus. Variations of this model have been studied in subsequent work, which have shown it to be successful in predicting qualitative phenonmena characteristic of TSN
Iwamatsu 2011
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Kashchiev 2020
;
Shao, Yang, and Tang 2020
.
We note that expressions similar to that in Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011
had previously been used to describe the related phenomena of deliquescence and efflorescence, which require consideration of the free energy of a multiphase droplet surrounded by a metastable vapor phase
Djikaev et al. 2001
;
Shchekin, Shabaev, and Hellmuth 2013
.

Despite the interest in using a CNT-inspired approach to model TSN, there have been comparatively few studies which quantitatively test the predictions of a proposed FES against results obtained from experiments or simulations. Ref.
Qi et al. 2015
shows that an expression for the FES similar to that proposed in Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011

compares well to the FES found from simulations of TSN in a quasi-2D colloidal system. The success of this comparison suggests that a more extensive test is warranted.
In a recent study of TSN in a 2D Ising-like lattice model, Ref.
James et al. 2019
presents high-resolution results for the FES obtained from simulations over a wide range of thermodynamic conditions. These results are well-suited for comparison with an analytic theory. Accordingly, the goal of the present work is to use the simulation results of Ref.
James et al. 2019
to test the theory for the FES of TSN proposed in Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011
.

As shown below, we conduct this test by first evaluating all the required parameters of the theory for the FES from the model system itself.
Ref.
James et al. 2019

already provides the required data for the chemical potentials and surface tensions of the bulk phases involved in the observed TSN process. In the present work, we separately calculate the parameters required to model the interaction between the two interfaces occurring in the multiphase nucleus.
As a result, we are able to present a comparison of the FES as predicted by theory and as obtained directly from simulations that does not depend on any adjustable parameters.

This paper is organized as follows: Section II describes Iwamatsu’s model for the FES of TSN
Iwamatsu 2011

and re-expresses it in a form appropriate for comparison with the results of Ref.
James et al. 2019
. Section III describes the lattice model studied in Ref.
James et al. 2019
and Section IV summarizes the simulation results for the FES and other thermodynamic properties calculated in Ref.
James et al. 2019
. Section V presents new simulations to determine the interaction parameters required to model the interaction of two nearby interfaces. A comparison of the predicted and simulated FES is given in Section VI, followed by a discussion in Section VII. The Supplemental Materials (SM) provide details on the order parameters used to characterize the FES and additional information on the simulations methods used to calculate the system free energy.

II
Theory for the free energy surface

The upper panels of Fig.
1
show an idealized TSN process in which the end product is
a nucleus having a core-shell structure. Surrounded by the bulk metastable phase
{\cal A}
(green), this nucleus has an outer shell of the intermediate
{\cal B}
phase (blue) and a core region of the stable
{\cal C}
phase (red).
Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011
expresses the free energy to create a nucleus having a core-shell structure as the sum of three contributions,

G=G_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}(R)+G_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}(r)+G_{\rm int}.

(2)

Here,
G_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}(R)

is the free energy to create a region of phase
{\cal B}
of radius
R
within phase
{\cal A}
.

G_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}(r)

is the free energy to create a core region of phase
{\cal C}
of radius
r
within the
{\cal B}
region.
G_{\rm int}
models the contribution to the free energy resulting from the interaction of the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces.

Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011
uses CNT expressions for
G_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}(R)
and
G_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}(r)
of the form of Eq.
1
to express
G
for a three dimensional core-shell nucleus as,

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\,\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}+4\pi R^{2}\,\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}

(3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle n_{\rm core}\,\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}+4\pi r^{2}\,\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}

(4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4\pi R^{2}S\exp\big[-(R-r)/\xi\big].

(5)

In Eq.
5
, the total number of particles in the nucleus (core and shell) is
n
, and the core contains
n_{\rm core}
particles.
\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}=\mu_{\cal B}-\mu_{\cal A}
is the difference in the chemical potential between the bulk phases
{\cal B}
and
{\cal A}
, and
\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
is the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
surface tension.
\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
and
\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
are similarly defined.
The last term in Eq.
5
models
G_{\rm int}
and is related to the disjoining pressure associated with the double interface
Djikaev et al. 2001
;
Iwamatsu 2011
.
The spreading parameter
S
is defined by,

S=\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal C}}-\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}-\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}},

(6)

where
\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal C}}

is the
{\cal A}{\cal C}
surface tension.
Note that
\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal C}}
is the surface tension of an interface where the
{\cal A}
and
{\cal C}
phases are in direct contact, without a wetting layer of
{\cal B}
between them.
The length scale
\xi
characterizes the range of the interaction between the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces.
A nucleus morphology that conforms to the core-shell structure depicted in Fig.
1
requires that
S\geq 0
, which corresponds to a repulsive interaction between the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces, and complete wetting of the
{\cal C}
phase by the
{\cal B}
phase.
Eq.
5
reduces to the conventional CNT expression for direct (i.e. one-step) nucleation from
{\cal A}
to
{\cal C}
when
r=R
, in which case the
{\cal B}
phase never appears
as an intermediate phase or as a
wetting layer during the nucleation process.

To generalize our analysis to both two and three dimensions, we write
the surface area
A
of a cluster of
n
particles as
A=\phi\,n^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha=(D-1)/D
depends on the dimension of space
D
. We further write the radius
R
of a cluster of size
n
as
R=\psi\,n^{\gamma}
, where
\gamma=1/D
.
For a circular cluster in
D=2
, we have
\alpha=\gamma=1/2
,
\phi=(4\pi v)^{1/2}
and
\psi=(v/\pi)^{1/2}
,
where
v
is the area per molecule.
For a spherical cluster in
D=3
, we have
\alpha=2/3
,
\gamma=1/3
,
\phi=(36\pi v^{2})^{1/3}

and
\psi=(3v/4\pi)^{1/3}
,
where
v
is the volume per molecule.

We define the composition of the nucleus as,

x=n_{\rm core}/n.

(7)

With the above definitions, we can rewrite the FES described by Eq.
5
solely in terms of
n
and
x
as,

\displaystyle G(n,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\,\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}+\phi\,n^{\alpha}\,\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}

(8)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle xn\,\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}+\phi\,(xn)^{\alpha}\,\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}

(9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\phi\,n^{\alpha}S\exp\big[-\psi\,n^{\gamma}(1-x^{\gamma})/\xi\big].

(10)

Eq.
10
expresses the model of Ref.
Iwamatsu 2011
for the FES for TSN in a form that can be directly compared with simulation results for the FES obtained in terms of
n
and
x
. These order parameters have been chosen in previous studies of the FES for TSN
Duff and Peters 2009
;
Schmid and Nielaba 2019
including Ref.
James et al. 2019
.

III
Lattice model and cluster properties

We test Eq.
10
using the results obtained for the lattice model described in detail in Ref.
James et al. 2019
. This system is a
D=2
model of a metamagnet in which Ising spins
s_{i}=\pm 1
interact via antiferromagnetic nearest-neighbor (nn) and ferromagnetic next-nearest-neighbour (nnn) interactions on a square lattice of
N=L^{2}
sites with periodic boundary conditions
Landau 1972
;
Landau and Swendsen 1981
;
Rikvold et al. 1983
;
Herrmann 1984
.
The energy
E
of a microstate is,

\frac{E}{J}=\sum_{\langle\rm nn\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\langle\rm nnn\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}-H\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}-H_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}s_{i},

(11)

where
J
is the magnitude of the nn interaction energy.

H

H_{s}
is the staggered field, and
\sigma_{i}=(-1)^{x_{i}+y_{i}}

where
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are respectively the integer horizontal and vertical coordinates of site
i

Ref.
James et al. 2019

having a ground state at

having a ground state at

with all

As shown in Fig.

and

Ref.

uses Monte Carlo simulations to study the TSN process
in which the metastable
{\cal A}
phase transforms to the stable
{\cal C}
phase,
during which the
{\cal B}
phase plays an intermediate role in the nucleation process.
As described in Ref.
James et al. 2019
, it is straightforward to identify local clusters of sites belonging to the

{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}

phases in any given configuration of the lattice model. An example is shown in the lower panels of Fig.

, in which regions of
{\cal A}
,

To study the nucleation process that begins in the metastable

phase, we must identify clusters of sites that deviate from the

phase. To quantify the cluster properties that correspond to
n
and

in Eq.
10
, we first define a cluster as a contiguous group of
{\cal B}
(blue) or
{\cal C}
(red) sites, plus any
{\cal A}
(green) sites that are completely enclosed by this group of
{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}
sites. The cluster size

is the total number of these
{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}
sites, respectively denoted

,

and

, so that,

n=n^{\cal A}+n^{\cal B}+n^{\cal C}.

(12)

We define the core of the cluster as the largest contiguous group of
{\cal C}
(red) sites that belong to the cluster. The size of the core
n_{\rm core}
is the number of these
{\cal C}
sites, plus any
{\cal A}
or
{\cal B}
sites that are completely enclosed by the
{\cal C}
sites of the core. The cluster composition

is then evaluated using Eq.

.

Ref.

uses approximate definitions for the cluster size and composition, denoted here as
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
, and given by,

\displaystyle\tilde{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\cal B}+n^{\cal C}

(13)

\displaystyle\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\cal C}/\tilde{n}.

(14)

These approximations were chosen in Ref.
James et al. 2019
for computational efficiency, since both
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
are computed from
n^{\cal B}
and
n^{\cal C}
alone, without requiring the identification of the largest
{\cal C}
region in the cluster, or the relatively rare
{\cal A}
sites that occur within the cluster. (Note that in Ref.
James et al. 2019
,
\tilde{n}
is denoted as “
n
" and
\tilde{x}
is denoted as “

".)

In order to maintain consistency with the simulation data of Ref.
James et al. 2019
,
the new simulations presented here are also conducted using
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
to quantify cluster properties. Nonetheless, as described in SM Section S1, we derive an approximate transformation that allows us to convert given values of
(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})

to corresponding values of
(n,x)
. This transformation allows us to use simulation results obtained in terms of
(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
to test predictions expressed in terms of
(n,x)
. As described in SM, we find that carrying out this transformation is particularly important for accurate estimation of the parameters

IV
Simulation results for the free energy surface

In Ref.
James et al. 2019
,
umbrella sampling Monte Carlo (MC) simulations were used to generate detailed numerical estimates for the FES describing the TSN process in which the metastable
{\cal A}
phase converts to the stable
{\cal C}
phase
Kumar et al. 1992
;
Tuckerman 2010
;
Grossfield 2018
.
This FES
is defined as the free energy of a system of size
N
in which the
largest
cluster occurring in the
{\cal A}
phase is of size
\tilde{n}
and composition
\tilde{x}
.
The transformation given in SM allows us to express this FES in terms of
n
and
x
.
This transformed FES may be directly compared to
G(n,x)
as defined in Eq.
10

for values of
\tilde{n}
such that the largest cluster in the system is much larger than all other clusters in the system, and
for values of
\tilde{x}
such that the largest
{\cal C}
-phase region within the largest cluster is much larger than all other
{\cal C}
-phase regions in this cluster. We find that these conditions are met when
\tilde{n}>500
and
\tilde{x}>0.05
. In addition, the FES evaluated in Ref.
James et al. 2019
differs from
G(n,x)
in Eq.
10
by a constant which was not determined in Ref.
James et al. 2019
. As described in SM Section
S2
, we have conducted new simulations to determine this constant
ten Wolde, Ruiz-Montero, and Frenkel 1996
;
Auer and Frenkel 2004
;
Lundrigan and Saika-Voivod 2009
.
After applying these adjustments to the data in Ref.
James et al. 2019
, we present here in
Fig.
3
(a-c) estimates of
G(n,x)

for three characteristic cases of TSN
as evaluated directly from the MC simulations described in Ref.
James et al. 2019
.

We also consider the one dimensional (1D) free energy as a function of
n
alone, which is evaluated from
G(n,x)
using,

\beta{G}_{1}(n)=-\log\int_{0}^{1}\exp[-\beta{G}(n,x)]\,dx.

(15)

We define the size
n^{*}
of the critical nucleus as the value of
n
at which
G_{1}
is a maximum.
Following Ref.
James et al. 2019

we define the average of
x
at fixed
n
as,

\langle x\rangle=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}x\,\exp[-\beta{G}(n,x)]\,dx}{\int_{0}^{1}\exp[-\beta{G}(n,x)]\,dx}.

(16)

The fluctuations in
x
, quantified by
\chi=\langle x^{2}\rangle-\langle x\rangle^{2}
, are a maximum at
n=n_{c}
.
As explained in Ref.
James et al. 2019
,
the significance of
n_{c}
is that for
n<n_{c}
the most probable state of the nucleus is dominated by the
{\cal B}
phase, while for
n>n_{c}
the most probable nucleus will have a core region of
{\cal C}
surrounded by
{\cal B}
. That is, when the nucleus grows to a size greater than
n_{c}
, a discontinuous phase transition from a pure-
{\cal B}
nucleus to a core-shell
{\cal C}
-
{\cal B}
structure becomes possible.
Expressions analogous to Eqs.
15
and
16
, using
(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
instead of
(n,x)
, are used to define
\tilde{n}^{*}
and
\tilde{n}_{c}
.
We define the height of the nucleation barrier
G^{*}
as the maximum value of
G_{1}(n)
or
G_{1}(\tilde{n})
, as appropriate.

G_{1}(\tilde{n})
is shown in Fig.
4
(a) for several values of
H
as found from MC simulations, and
Fig.
5
shows the simulation results for
\tilde{n}^{*}
,
\tilde{n}_{c}
and
G^{*}

Our goal is to test the degree to which
G(n,x)
as defined in Eq.
10
can predict the results found from MC simulations. To do so, we require values for the six parameters that occur in Eq.
10
:

\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
,
\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
,

V
Interaction of two interfaces

We conduct new simulations of the metamagnet model to estimate the parameters
S
and
\xi
that characterize

, the free energy of interaction of the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}

The simplest geometry in which to study the interaction of these two interfaces is the “planar” case where two flat, parallel interfaces separate semi-infinite regions of the bulk
{\cal A}
and
{\cal C}
phases, and where a layer of the
{\cal B}
phase of width
\Delta r
lies between the
{\cal A}
and
{\cal C}

{\cal A}{\cal B}
-
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces separated by stripes of the
{\cal A}
and
{\cal C}
phases.

The same umbrella sampling procedure used to study circular clusters in Ref.
James et al. 2019

is used here to study the planar interface case.
In the planar case, the largest “cluster” in the system is now a rectangular region spanning the periodic boundaries in the vertical direction and consisting of a thick stripe of the
{\cal C}
(red) phase with thinner wetting layers of the
{\cal B}
(blue) phase on either side.
In the planar geometry, the width of each
{\cal B}
layer, and thus the distance between the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces, is on average,

\Delta r=\frac{n(1-x)}{2L}.

(17)

Umbrella sampling simulations that control both
n
and
x
can therefore be used to control
\Delta r
.

VII
discussion

As noted above, there are no adjustable parameters when we compare the FES as obtained from MC simulations and from Eq.
10
. The simulated FES is determined entirely from the microstates of the metamagnet, generated via umbrella sampling runs that explore heterogeneous states in which a localized cluster of size
n
and composition
x
occurs in the
{\cal A}
phase. The predicted FES obtained from Eq.
10
is based on measured
properties of pure homogeneous bulk phases (
\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
and
\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
) or properties obtained from systems in which a flat interface separates homogeneous bulk phases (
\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
,
\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
,
S
and
\xi
). Our results thus demonstrate that, similar to CNT for simple (one-step) nucleation, a satisfactory CNT-based theory for the FES of TSN can be constructed using only information on pure bulk phases and flat macroscopic interfaces between bulk phases.

Ref.
James et al. 2019
emphasized the significance of the ridge-crossing process, also observed here, by which the nucleus switches from the
{\cal B}
channel to the
{\cal C}
channel.
This process is a discontinuous phase transition that occurs in the finite-sized nucleus as it grows. This is termed a “fluctuation phase transition" (FPT) in Ref.
James et al. 2019

because it is a phase transition that occurs in a transient and spatially localized fluctuation, which in the present case is the nucleus.
The FPT is probable only when
n>n_{c}
. If
n_{c}<n^{*}
then the FPT occurs as a restructuring of the pre-critical nucleus before it reaches the transition state that represents the exit from the basin of the bulk metastable
{\cal A}
phase; see Fig.
10
(a). This case may provide a way to understand non-classical effects observed in pre-critical nuclei that otherwise seem to pass through a transition state typical of simple, one-step nucleation
Zhou et al. 2019
.
Alternatively, when
n_{c}>n^{*}
, the nucleus has already passed through the transition state and exited the metastable phase before undergoing the FPT that converts it to a nucleus that contains the stable
{\cal C}
phase; see Fig.
10
(c). It is this case that is normally associated with TSN.
In sum, our results show that a FPT is a feature of the FES given by Eq.
10
under all conditions studied here and so
may provide a unified explanation of a wide range of non-classical behavior associated with both pre-critical and post-critical nuclei.

We show in Fig.
11

contour plots of
G
as defined in Eq.
10
plotted in terms of
(n,n_{\rm core})
rather than in terms of
(n,x)
, for the same state points as in Fig.
10
. Several previous works have represented the FES of TSN in terms of
(n,n_{\rm core})
, or equivalent variables
Shchekin, Shabaev, and Hellmuth 2013
;
Qi et al. 2015
;
Shao, Yang, and Tang 2020
;
Kashchiev 2020
, and we provide these plots here to facilitate comparison with these studies. When comparing the representations of the FES given in Figs.
10
and
11
, we note that it is easier to resolve the ridge (and the associated FPT) that separates the

{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}
channels when the FES is plotted in terms of
(n,x)
, especially when
n_{c}<n^{*}
.
Our results show that only the
{\cal B}
channel of the FES connects to the metastable
{\cal A}
phase at
n=0
. That is, the most probable small fluctuations in the metastable phase are those with the lowest surface tension, which here are
{\cal B}
-phase clusters. Only when the cluster has grown to sizes larger than
n_{c}
can the stable
{\cal C}
phase appear in the nucleus, via the FPT. This behavior is difficult to resolve when the FES is plotted as in Fig.
11
, especially when
n_{c}<n^{*}
.
Previous studies of TSN have discussed the possibility that two thermodynamically defined pathways originate from the metastable state at
n=0
on the FES
Iwamatsu 2011
;
Kashchiev 2020
.
The model of the FES given by Eq.
10
is not consistent with this picture.

The main characteristics of TSN as described by Eq.
10
when
n_{c}>n^{*}
are summarized in Fig.
12
. The FES in Fig.
12

is the same as that shown as a contour plot in Fig.
10
(c). As stated above, a single exit pathway (the blue
{\cal B}
channel) leads out of the metastable state. The nucleus passes through the transition state (white circle) but remains in the intermediate
{\cal B}
phase. The pathway leading to the stable phase (the red
{\cal C}
channel) can only be reached when
n>n_{c}
and via a FPT that carries the nucleus over the ridge in the FES. Notably, since the transition from the
{\cal B}
channel to the
{\cal C}
channel does not pass through a saddle point, knowledge of the FES alone is not sufficient for predicting the size of the nucleus at the FPT. Rather, the growth dynamics of the nucleus and the relative rates of relaxation of
n
and
x
will be controlling factors.
The FES in Fig.
12
thus illustrates how long-lived intermediate-phase nuclei can persist and grow to large size before the stable phase finally appears, a common feature of TSN
Vekilov 2004
;
Vekilov 2010
;
Sear 2012
;
L’vov and Umantsev 2020
.
The green line is an example of such a nucleation trajectory in which the nucleus lingers in the
{\cal B}
channel well beyond
n_{c}
before converting to the
{\cal C}
channel that leads to the stable phase.

Our results also confirm that the inclusion of the interaction
G_{\rm int}
between the
{\cal A}{\cal B}
and
{\cal B}{\cal C}
interfaces plays an important role in controlling the shape of the FES for TSN. A recent study examined the FES formed without including
G_{\rm int}
and the topography of the surface is distinctly different
Kashchiev 2020
. In particular, a significant local maximum occurs in the FES that is almost always absent in the FES generated by Eq.
10
when
G_{\rm int}
is included, at least for our system. It will be interesting for future work to explore the range of FES topographies that result from models of the form of Eq.
10
when applied to different systems.

We also note that our study does not address a number of factors that may significantly influence the shape of the FES. For example, Eq.
10
assumes complete wetting of the
{\cal C}
phase by the
{\cal B}
phase. Incomplete wetting changes the geometry of the two-phase nucleus and would require modifications to the form of Eq.
10
. Also, in our system, the volume per monomer does not vary from one phase to another, but this will clearly have an impact on systems in which density is an order parameter, such as crystal formation from a liquid. We have also not explicitly examined temperature-dependent effects since we have used the thermodynamic fields
H
and
H_{s}
to vary the relative chemical potentials of the three phases involved in our simulation model. More broadly, the present work does not address the key question of the implications of the FES presented here for the estimation of nucleation rates
van Meel et al. 2008
;
Chen et al. 2008
. These are all important avenues for future work.

In summary, our results demonstrate that Iwamatsu’s model
Iwamatsu 2011
for the FES of TSN works well for the 2D lattice system studied here. Eq.
10
provides a useful qualitative picture of the thermodynamics of TSN, and also yields quantitative predictions that are a satisfactory starting point for estimating the behaviour of a real system. More generally, our results confirm that significant insights into non-classical nucleation processes can be achieved by an extension of the concepts of traditional CNT to more complex systems.

Order parameter transformation

The order parameters
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
introduced in Ref.
James et al. 2019

are related to, but not identical with, the order parameters
n
and
x
that appear here in Eq.
10
. There are three reasons for this: (i)
\tilde{n}
ignores
{\cal A}
sites that naturally occur in the
{\cal B}
-dominated shell of the cluster; (ii)
\tilde{x}
ignores the
{\cal B}
sites that naturally occur in the
{\cal C}
-dominated core; and (iii)
\tilde{x}
erroneously includes the
{\cal C}
sites that naturally occur in the shell when estimating the fractional size of the core. In an accurate definition of
n
and
x
, all of the small equilibrium fluctuations that occur in both the core and the shell should be counted as part of each region. Here we derive the relations that we use to convert values of
(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
to the corresponding values of
(n,x)
.

Consider our system as a set of sites which are each assigned to the

{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}
or
{\cal C}
phase. See for example the system configuration in Fig.

, where

S2
Comparing free energy surfaces from MC simulations and theory

Both in Ref.
James et al. 2019

and in the present work, the FES evaluated from MC simulations is obtained in terms of global (i.e. system-level) order parameters that correspond to the size (
\tilde{n}
) and composition (
\tilde{x}
) of the
largest
cluster in the system.
We denote the FES that we compute directly from MC simulations as
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
.
That is, to quantify the thermodynamic properties of the clusters that occur in the
{\cal A}
phase, we evaluate
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
,
the FES of a system of size
N
of the bulk
{\cal A}
phase in which the largest cluster in the system has size
\tilde{n}
and composition
\tilde{x}

Duff and Peters 2009
.

We obtain
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
from umbrella sampling MC simulations at fixed
(N,H_{s},H,T)

Tuckerman 2010
.

{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
is computed using,

\beta{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})=-\log[P(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})]+C_{o},

(S22)

where
P(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})

is proportional to the probability to observe a system microstate in which the largest cluster is of size
\tilde{n}
and composition
\tilde{x}
.
The value of the constant
C_{o}

is chosen so that
{\cal G}=0
at the local minimum of
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
that occurs close to the origin at
(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})=(0,0)
.

We estimate

P(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
from 2D umbrella sampling simulations using a biasing potential that depends on both
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
,

U_{B}=\kappa_{n}(\tilde{n}-\tilde{n}^{\prime})^{2}+\kappa_{x}(\tilde{x}-\tilde{x}^{\prime})^{2},

(S23)

where
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{x}^{\prime}
are target values of
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}
to be sampled in a given umbrella sampling simulation, and

\kappa_{n}
and
\kappa_{x}
control the range of sampling around
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{x}^{\prime}
.
Results from multiple umbrella sampling runs conducted at fixed
(N,H_{s},H,T)

are combined using the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) to estimate the full
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
FES at a given state point
Kumar et al. 1992
;
Tuckerman 2010
;
Grossfield 2018
.

S3
umbrella sampling simulations of a planar interface

To estimate the parameters
S
and
\xi
that appear in Eq.
10
, we conduct 2D umbrella sampling simulations of the kind described in Section
S2
to find
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
for a system in which the
{\cal A}
and
{\cal C}
phases are separated by a planar interface containing a thin wetting layer of the
{\cal B}
phase.

We choose
L=400
,
\kappa_{n}=0.0005J
and

\kappa_{x}=500J
.
For each choice of
(N,H_{s},H,T)

we conduct 60 simulations for

\tilde{n}^{\prime}\in\{79900,80000,80100\}
, and for

\tilde{x}^{\prime}=i/20
where the integer
i\in\{0,1,2,\dots,19\}
. To study a system with planar interfaces, each run is initiated from a perfect
{\cal A}
configuration, into which a thick vertical stripe of the perfect
{\cal C}
phase has been inserted. A thinner vertical stripe of the perfect
{\cal B}
phase is then inserted at the two
{\cal A}{\cal C}
interfaces. In any given run, the number of
{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}
sites inserted is chosen so that
\tilde{n}
is closest to
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
and
so that the proportion of
{\cal B}
and
{\cal C}
sites gives a value of
\tilde{x}
closest to
\tilde{x}^{\prime}
. Our choice of values for
L
and
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
generate system configurations in which the
{\cal B}
-phase wetting layers are separated by approximately
L/2
. The thickness of each wetting layer is controlled by the choice
\tilde{x}^{\prime}
.

This system is equilibrated for
5\times 10^{4}
MCS (Monte Carlo steps), and then the time series of
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}

is recorded every 100 MCS for
10^{6}
MCS.
We sample configurations using Metropolis single-spin-flip MC dynamics
Binder and Landau 2009
.
Trial configurations are accepted or rejected using the umbrella potential every 1 MCS.
One MCS corresponds to
L^{2}
attempts to flip the spin of a randomly chosen lattice site.
Our time series for
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{x}

are analyzed using WHAM to evaluate
P(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
and
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
.
We estimate that the error in
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
is not more than
1kT
.
We exclude from the WHAM analysis any run for which the acceptance rate for the umbrella sampling is less than
0.1
, which occurs in a few cases when the local variation of

{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
is very steep.

We calculate the
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
surface only for large values of
\tilde{n}
in the vicinity of
\tilde{n}=80000
. Also, as we will see below, the range of
\tilde{x}
from which we extract estimates for
S
and
\xi
occurs at
\tilde{x}>0.7
. Therefore, for the same reasons that justify Eq.
S24
,

{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
as calculated here (i.e. for a system with a planar interface) estimates a section of the
G_{\parallel}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
surface, up to an undetermined constant
C_{\parallel}
, where
G_{\parallel}
is defined by Eq.
20
.
We extract the cut through the
{\cal G}(\tilde{n},\tilde{x})
surface at fixed
\tilde{n}_{o}=80000
, which is the one-dimensional function
{\cal G}(\tilde{n}_{o},\tilde{x})
.
Using the transformation given in Eqs.
S18
and
S19
, we then convert
{\cal G}(\tilde{n}_{o},\tilde{x})
to the 1D cut through the FES for
{\cal G}(n,x)
along which
n
and
x
vary such that
\tilde{n}=\tilde{n}_{o}
remains constant, which we denote
G_{\parallel}^{o}
.

We fit Eq.
20
(plus the constant
C_{\parallel}
) to our data for
G_{\parallel}^{o}
,
where
S
,
\xi
and
C_{\parallel}
are the fit parameters, and where the values of
\Delta\mu_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
,
\Delta\mu_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
,
\sigma_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
,
\sigma_{{\cal B}{\cal C}}
are fixed to those reported in Ref.
James et al. 2019
.
We restrict the fit to data lying near
x_{\rm min}
, the value of
x
at which the minimum of

G_{\parallel}^{o}

occurs, since this is the range of
x
in which the stripe geometry is most stable and
is thus where Eq.
20
is the appropriate model of the system free energy. Specifically, we fit using data points for which
\beta G_{\parallel}^{o}<20
for
x<x_{\rm min}
, and for which

\beta G_{\parallel}^{o}<8
for

. For the range of
H
studied here, we find that

varies from 0.88 to 0.94.
