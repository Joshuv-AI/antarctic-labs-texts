1.
Introduction

1.1.
Random graph processes

Graphs are among the most universal structures, and they have therefore served as mathematical models in many settings. It is often the case that these settings evolve in time according to a stochastic rule. A variety of random graph processes have been introduced and studied extensively to this end. Most of these processes are defined so that they start with a specific initial graph on vertex set
[n]
(for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
), and this vertex set remains unchanged. Further, it is usually asymptotic properties as
n
tends to infinity which are investigated. Let us give some examples. The simplest such process is the
Erdős–Rényi graph process
, introduced in the seminal paper
[
8
]
of Erdős and Rényi in 1960. The process starts with the edgeless graph
G_{0}
on
[n]
. In each step
t=1,\ldots,\binom{n}{2}
, the graph
G_{t}
is obtained by taking
G_{t-1}
and turning a uniformly random non-edge into an edge. Obviously, the graph
G_{\binom{n}{2}}
is complete. Another process introduced by Bollobás and Erdős at a “conference in Fairbanks, Alaska, in 1990, while admiring the playful bears”
[
6
]
, is the
triangle removal process
. The process starts with the complete graph
G_{0}
on
[n]
. In each step
t=1,2,\ldots
, the graph
G_{t}
is obtained by taking
G_{t-1}
, choosing a randomly selected triangle (uniformly among the set of all triangles in
G_{t-1}
), and erasing its three edges. The process stops at the (random) time when
G_{t}
is triangle free. A major question raised already by Bollobás and Erdős in 1990 was what the number of edges of the final graph
G_{t}
is. This has been a subject of a long line of research, culminating in
[
3
]
which establishes that
e(G_{t})=n^{3/2+o(1)}
. General
H
-removal processes were studied much less, mostly motivated by extending the setting of the Erdős–Hanani conjecture resolved in
[
25
]
, see e.g.
[
22
,
26
]
. A process which looks like a dual process in many aspects is the following one, called the
H
-free process
. Let
H
be a fixed graph. We start with the edgeless graph
G_{0}
. In each step
t
we select, uniformly at random, a non-edge whose addition to
G_{t-1}
does not create a copy of
H
, and turn it into an edge. Hence, when the process stops,
G_{t}
is a maximal
H
-free graph. The case
H=K_{3}
is now well understood due to analyses by Bohman and Keevash
[
5
]
and Fiz Pontiveros, Griffiths, and Morris
[
10
]
. In particular, these results imply the currently best known lower bound for the off-diagonal Ramsey number
R(3,k)
. Among the numerous further papers studying the triangle-free process and other
H
-free processes, let us highlight
[
21
,
4
,
28
]
. The last class of processes we mention before turning to the main subject of this paper are the
Achlioptas processes
, introduced in
[
1
]
. We start with the edgeless graph on
[n]
. In each step we select
k=2
random non-edges (natural generalizations for larger
k
exist)
f_{1}=x_{1}y_{1}
and
f_{2}=x_{2}y_{2}
. Then, we choose one of them to add into the graph according to a predetermined rule. For example, the so-called ‘product rule’ selects the non-edge
f_{i}
which minimizes the product of the sizes of the connected components to which
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
belong. Indeed, the motivation behind many Achlioptas rules was to alter the time of the emergence of the giant component. See, e.g.
[
23
,
24
]
, for more on Achlioptas processes.

Let us note that an obvious generalization of the processes above would be to start them from graphs other than the complete or the edgeless one. It seems that the reason such generalizations were not studied was that it was not clear what the observed features should be.

1.2.
Flip processes, basics

In
[
12
]
, the following general class of random graph processes was introduced. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be given and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the set of all graphs on the vertex set
[k]
. Let
\mathcal{R}\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{H}_{k}\times\mathcal{H}_{k}}
be a matrix which is stochastic, that is, for every
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=1
. We call
k
the
order
and
\mathcal{R}
the
rule
of the flip process. Given
\mathcal{R}
, an integer
n\geq k
and an
initial graph

G_{0}
with vertex set
[n]
, the corresponding
flip process
proceeds as follows. For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we sample uniformly at random an ordered tuple
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{k})
of distinct vertices in
[n]
. Let
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the
drawn graph
, that is, the graph on
[k]
such that
ij\in E(F)
if and only if
v_{i}v_{j}\in G_{t-1}
. Next, we sample a graph
H
, which we term the
replacement graph
, according to the probability distribution
(\mathcal{R}_{F,H})_{H}
and replace
G_{t-1}[\mathbf{v}]
by
H
in the graph
G_{t-1}
to obtain
G_{t}
. To be precise, we place a copy of
H
on
\mathbf{v}
so that
ij\in E(H)
if and only if
v_{i}v_{j}\in G_{t}
. A flip process is a discrete time Markov process. It is (unlike all the previously mentioned processes) defined for all times.

We observe that the Erdős–Rényi process and the triangle removal process have flip process variants. The
Erdős–Rényi flip process
starts with an arbitrary graph on
n\geq 2
vertices. In each step, a random pair of vertices is chosen and turned into an edge. If the pair is already an edge then the step is idle. The
triangle removal flip process
starts with an arbitrary graph on
n\geq 3
vertices. In each step, a random triple of vertices is chosen. If the triple forms a triangle then its three edges are removed. Otherwise, the step is idle. Note that the difference between the original processes and their flip process variants is the idle steps in the latter. While
H
-removal processes can be viewed as flip processes, other classes mentioned in Section
1.1
, like the
H
-free process and Achlioptas processes, cannot.

In
[
12
]
, flip processes were studied from the perspective of dense graph limits. Recall that the main objects of this theory, introduced in
[
19
,
7
]
, are two-variable symmetric measurable functions defined on the square of a probability space
(\Omega,\pi)
(which we assume to have a separable sigma-algebra and atomless measure) with values in
[0,1]
. These functions are called
graphons
. We denote the set of all graphons by
\mathcal{W}_{0}
. Recall that one of the most important metrics on
\mathcal{W}_{0}
is the
cut norm distance
, which is given by

(1)

d_{\square}(U,W)=\left\lVert U-W\right\rVert_{\square}=\sup_{S,T\subseteq\Omega}\left|\int_{S\times T}(U-W)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}\right|\;.

Recall also that each finite graph
G
can be represented as a graphon. To this end, take an arbitrary partition
(\Omega_{v}:v\in V(G))
of
\Omega
into sets of equal measure. Let the graphon
W_{G}
be defined to be
1
on each rectangle
\Omega_{u}\times\Omega_{v}
for which
uv\in E(G)
, and 0 elsewhere. Note that this graphon representation depends on the choice of the partition
(\Omega_{v}:v\in V(G))
. However, we will assume that graphs on the same vertex set are represented using the same partition of
\Omega
.
Since each step of a flip process of order
k
flips at most
\binom{k}{2}
pairs of vertices (from a non-edge to an edge or vice versa), it follows that we need to perform
\Omega(n^{2})
steps to see a change in the evolution of an
n
-vertex graph through the lens of dense graph limits. Indeed, one of the main results of
[
12
]
is the following correspondence.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal, with loose quantification; see Theorem
in
[
12
]
)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for every rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k
, there exists an
evolution function

\Phi:\mathcal{W}_{0}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{0}
so that, writing
\Phi^{t}W=\Phi(W,t)
and treating
\mathcal{W}_{0}
as the metric space with metric
d_{\square}
, we have the following.

(i)

For any fixed graphon
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
, we have
\Phi^{0}W=W
(
W
is the ‘initial graphon’) and the map
t\mapsto\Phi^{t}W:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{0}
, which we call the
trajectory starting at
W
, is continuous.

(ii)

The trajectory depends continuously on the initial conditions, i.e., for any fixed time
t\geq 0
, the map
\Phi^{t}(\cdot):\mathcal{W}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{0}
is continuous. The variable
t
is ‘time’.

(iii)

Let
G_{0},G_{1},\dots
be the flip process starting with an
n
-vertex graph
G_{0}
. Denote by
W_{\ell}
,
\ell=0,1,\dots
, the (random) graphon that represents the graph
G_{\ell}
. For every
\varepsilon>0
and
T\geq 0
, with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(n^{2}))
for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cap[0,Tn^{2}]
we have
d_{\square}(W_{\ell},\Phi^{\ell/n^{2}}W_{0})<\varepsilon
.

The evolution function
\Phi:\mathcal{W}_{0}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{0}
is constructed in a way which is only partly explicit. More specifically, given a rule
\mathcal{R}
, a
velocity field

\mathfrak{V}_{\>}:\mathcal{W}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
is explicitly constructed (here,
\mathcal{W}
is the space of 2-variable symmetric functions in
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
),
and the trajectory starting at any initial graphon
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
is then defined as the unique solution of the (Banach-space-valued) differential equation

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\Phi^{t}W=\mathfrak{V}_{\>}\left(\Phi^{t}W\right)\;\mbox{with initial condition}\;\Phi^{0}W=W\;,

which can be equivalently formulated as the integral equation

(2)

\Phi^{t}W=W+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{V}_{\>}\left(\Phi^{\tau}W\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\;.

As we shall see in this paper, obtaining explicit trajectories is a difficult task even for fairly simple rules.

In the rest of this introduction we motivate further key notions in the theory of flip processes.

1.3.
Flip processes, going back in time

Theorem
1.1
(iii)
says that for any time
t\geq 0
the graphon
\Phi^{t}W
approximately describes the structure of a typical graph after
tn^{2}
steps of the flip process started at an
n
-vertex graph
G_{0}
which is close to
W
. We may ask a complementary question: does there exist a graphon
U
such that, starting at an
n
-vertex graph
H_{0}
that is close to
U

tn^{2}
steps we typically arrive to a graph which is similar to
W

\Phi^{-t}W:=U
, and the above property can be written as
\Phi^{t}(\Phi^{-t}W)=W
. Note that
\Phi^{-t}W
need not always exist. To give a specific example, in the Erdős–Rényi flip process, there is no way we could arrive at an edgeless graph after performing any number of steps starting from any graph. This means that
\Phi^{-t}0
does not exist for any
t>0
. As a generalization of this example, for any constant graphon
C\in(0,1)
, the backwards trajectory
\Phi^{-t}C
is a constant graphon which decreases as
t
increases until it reaches 0 (at time
t=-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-C)

[
12

)]
), and for larger
t
,
\Phi^{-t}C
is not defined. Switching back to the general setting, we call the supremum of times
t
for which
\Phi^{-t}W
exists the
age of a graphon
W
. Furthermore, if
\mathrm{age}(W)<\infty
then it can be shown that
\mathrm{orig}(W):=\Phi^{-\mathrm{age}(W)}W
is well-defined (see Theorem
in
[
12
]
). We call
\mathrm{orig}(W)
the
origin
of
W
. To return to the example of the Erdős–Rényi flip process, the origin of every constant graphon
C\in[0,1)
is the constant-
0
. The constant-
1
does not have an origin, but it has an infinite age and
\Phi^{t}1=1
for every time
t
, positive or negative.

1.4.
Flip processes, destinations

For a general rule and a fixed initial graphon
W
, there are several qualitatively different possible evolutions of the trajectory
(\Phi^{t}W)_{t\geq 0}
. The simplest one is when
W
is a
fixed point
, that is,
\Phi^{t}W=W
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The next simplest is when
\Phi^{t}W
converges as
t\to\infty
. Here we choose the weakest sensible notion of convergence, that is, cut norm distance convergence.
1
1

1

However, in
[
12
]
it is conjectured that this setting actually implies convergence in the
L^{1}
-norm and possibly even in the
L^{\infty}
-norm.
In such a setting we denote the limit by
\mathrm{dest}(W)
and call it the
destination of

W
. There are more complicated types of trajectories. For example, in
[
12
]
it was shown that a periodic trajectory exists for a certain rule. Further, when a starting graphon is taken in an proximity of such a periodic trajectory, its trajectory spirals towards the periodic one. However, at this moment we do not see a classification of trajectories beyond convergent/nonconvergent (where the former also contains fixed points).

For a convergent trajectory, it is of interest to investigate the speed of convergence. Bounds which are independent of the starting graphon are particularly interesting. More formally, suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is a rule for which every trajectory converges. We can define for each
\delta>0
the
lower convergence time

\tau^{-}(\delta)=\sup\{\inf\{T:d_{\square}(\Phi^{T}W,\mathrm{dest}(W))<\delta\}:W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\}

and the
upper convergence time

\tau^{+}(\delta)=\sup\{\sup\{T:d_{\square}(\Phi^{T}W,\mathrm{dest}(W))\geq\delta\}:W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\}\;.

1.5.1.
Ignorant flip processes (Section
4
)

Ignorant flip processes are flip processes where the distribution for the replacement graph does not depend on the drawn graph. That is, the rule
\mathcal{R}
satisfies that for any
F,F^{\prime},H
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=\mathcal{R}_{F^{\prime},H}
. Ignorant flip processes are one of the few examples where we can describe the trajectories explicitly; this gives a complete description of their behaviour. In particular, all the trajectories of an ignorant flip process converge to the same constant graphon whose value is the expected edge density
2

2

The

{e(H)}/{\binom{v(H)}{2}}

according to the output distribution

(here
F
is arbitrary). The Erdős–Renyi flip process is a prominent example of an ignorant flip process.

1.5.2.
Stirring flip processes (Section
5
)

While the output distribution in an ignorant flip process does not depend on the drawn graph at all, in a stirring flip process it depends only on a very basic graph parameter — its number of edges. In Section
5
we investigate balanced stirring flip processes, in which the expected number of edges in the replacement graph is equal to the number of edges in the drawn graph. We explicitly describe the trajectories of any balanced stirring flip process. Not surprisingly, the edge density is preserved along each trajectory. The description does not depend on the actual rule but only on its order. That is, any balanced stirring flip process of a given order yields the same trajectories.

1.5.3.
Complementing flip processes (Section
6
)

The complementing flip process is a flip process where we replace the drawn graph by its graph complement. Informally, we thus sparsify the host graph at places where its density lies above
\frac{1}{2}
and densify it at places where its density lies below
\frac{1}{2}
. It follows that each trajectory should converge to the constant-
\frac{1}{2}
graphon. We work out the trajectories explicitly, confirming this intuition.

1.5.4.
Extremist flip processes (Section
7
)

The extremist flip process is defined as follows. If the drawn graph has edge density less than
1/2
, we replace it by the edgeless graph. If the edge density is greater than
1/2
, we replace it by the clique. If the density is exactly
1/2
, then there is some freedom in defining the replacement graph (our default choice is do nothing). Intuitively, dense graph(on)s converge to the complete graph (constant-
1
graphon) while sparse graph(on)s converge to the edgeless graph (constant-
0
graphon). The main focus of our treatment of extremist flip processes is in making this precise. Further, we study the limit behaviour as
k
grows.

1.5.5.
Monotone flip processes (Section
8
)

We introduce several variants, but, roughly speaking, a monotone flip process is a flip process where in each step we add edges, or at least add edges in expectation. In such a situation we expect the trajectories to be increasing in time and in particular to converge. Indeed, most of our treatment of monotone flip processes focuses on these features (including one negative example).

1.5.6.
Component completion flip processes (Section
9
)

In a component completion flip process, we replace the drawn graph (which may have several connected components) by its so-called component completion graph, that is, we turn each connected component into a clique. Hence, the component structure of the graph does not change during the evolution. Note also that this procedure is void for orders
k=1,2
. For
k\geq 3
we prove that the trajectories converge to a graphon with the same component structure, where each component is constant-
1
.

1.5.7.
Removal flip processes (Section
10
)

Removal flip processes generalize the triangle removal flip process. We fix a graph
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. In each step of the process, we delete a copy of
F
, should the drawn graph contain it. More formally, for
H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
which contains
F
as a subgraph (preserving the labels) we have
\mathcal{R}_{H,H-F}=1
while for
H
which does not contain
F
as a labelled subgraph we have
\mathcal{R}_{H,H}=1
. The theory of removal flip processes is particularly elegant. There are two results which we could obtain only with the help of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma: that convergence times for removal processes are finite and uniformly bounded and that the map which maps each graphon to its destination is continuous in the cut norm distance.

1.5.8.
Quadratically many steps and beyond

Most of the results we obtain concern trajectories. Due to the correspondence in Theorem
1.1
, this tells us about the typical evolution of a finite
n
-vertex graph in a flip process for
\Theta(n^{2})
steps. However, for the extremist and removal flip processes we found interesting questions which go beyond this time horizon.

2.1.1.
Grönwall’s inequality

Here we state two variants of Grönwall’s inequality. The following lemma is a variant of Grönwall’s inequality in integral form.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
27
, Theorem 1.10]
)
.

Let
f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a continuous non-negative function.
Suppose that for some constants
A\geq 0
,
C>0
we have

(3)

f(t)\leq A+C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]

(4)

f(t)\leq Ae^{C(t-t_{0})}\quad\text{ for all }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\;.

The following lemma is a variant of Grönwall’s inequality for differentiable functions where the differential inequality holds subject to a condition on the value of the function.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
D,K>0
and
f\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a differentiable function satisfying
f(0)\leq D
and
f^{\prime}(t)\leq-Kf(t)
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
such that
f(t)\leq D
. Then for all
t\in[0,\infty)
we have
f(t)\leq f(0)e^{-Kt}
.

Proof.

First we show that we have
f(t)\leq D
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Indeed, suppose that we have
f(t^{\prime})>D
for some
t^{\prime}>0
. Let
T:=\inf\{t\geq 0:f(t)>D\}
. By the continuity of
f
we have

, so we have
f^{\prime}(T)\leq-Kf(T)=-KD<0
. By the definition of

, there exists

such that for all

f(T+\varepsilon)\leq f(T)+\varepsilon(KD/2+f^{\prime}(T))\leq f(T)-KD\varepsilon/2<D.

This contradicts the definition of

, so we have
f(t)\leq D
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
.

The function

is differentiable with

, so for every

2.1.2.
On notation of integrals

For the sake of brevity and clarity we sometimes use simplified notation to denote an integral, namely we often write
\int_{x=a}^{b}f(x)
(or
\int_{x=a}^{b}f
) instead of
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
for functions of a single real argument (that is, the underlying measure is the Lebesgue measure) and
\int_{x\in A}f(x)
(or
\int_{x\in A}f

2.1.3.
Differential equations in Banach spaces

We need to recall concepts of the derivative and the integral in Banach spaces. However we do not need almost any results from the abstract theory of differential equations in Banach spaces since we only need to recall results obtained already in
[
12
]
. Let
\mathcal{E}
be a Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|

f:I\to\mathcal{E}
is defined for an open set

then we say that

at

(5)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}f(t)=\mathfrak{O}(f(t))\;\mbox{with initial condition}\;f(0)=z\;,

where
z\in\mathcal{E}
,

takes values in
\mathcal{E}
, and
\mathfrak{O}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
is (locally) Lipschitz. Such a differential equation can be rewritten as the integral equation

(6)

f(t)=z+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{O}(f(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\;,

where the integral is the so-called Cauchy–Bochner integral in the Banach space
\mathcal{E}
(it is easy to show that this integral is well-defined, since
s\mapsto\mathfrak{O}(f(s))
is continuous). The integral has all usual properties of the Riemann integral of continuous real-valued functions, with the norm playing the role of the absolute value (see Section
in
[
12
]
).

In this paper, we specifically consider differential equations in the Banach space
(\mathcal{W},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})
, which is a closed subspace of
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
(where
\Omega
is the probability space on which our graphons are defined). Recall that the elements of
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
are equivalence classes (where equivalent means equal almost everywhere) of measurable functions, which means that it is not formally meaningful to evaluate an element
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
at a given point
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
. However, it is much more intuitive to pretend we can do this and, for example (i) evaluate
f(t)
(see (
2
) for a prominent example) at point
(x,y)
by integrating a real-valued curve
s\mapsto\mathfrak{V}_{\>}\left(f(s)\right)(x,y)
; (ii) deduce that
f(t)\leq g(t)
for every
t\in[a,b]
implies
\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(t)
(where the inequalities are understood to hold almost everywhere). In
[
12
]
it was shown that this apparently non-rigorous approach can be perfectly justified. We believe that this approach of dealing with trajectories “pointwise” has an advantage on the intuitive level, and we stick to it, referring a meticulous reader to the required technical tools for justification in Appendix
in
[
12
]
.

What follows is the only abstract fact we need that is not available in
[
12
]
. It says that a solution of a differential equation of the type in (
5
) changes only little if we perturb the velocity field. This statement is very likely standard, but we found it easier to give a four-line proof than to find a source in the literature.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider a Banach space
\mathcal{E}
with norm
\|\cdot\|
. Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{E}
be a set, and
A,K>0
be constants. Let
X,Y:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
be functions. Suppose that
X
is
K
-Lipschitz, that is, we have

(7)

\left\|X(x)-X(y)\right\|\leq K\left\|x-y\right\|\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in\mathcal{U}\;.

Suppose further that for each
x\in\mathcal{U}
we have

(8)

\|X(x)-Y(x)\|\leq A\;.

Given
x_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
, let
\alpha,\beta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
satisfy the integral equations

\alpha(t)=x_{0}+\int_{s=0}^{t}X(\alpha(s))\quad\beta(t)=x_{0}+\int_{s=0}^{t}Y(\beta(s))\quad\mbox{for each $t\in[0,T]$}

Then for each
t\in[0,T]
we have
\|\alpha(t)-\beta(t)\|\leq AT\exp(Kt)
.

Proof.

Set
f(t):=\|\alpha(t)-\beta(t)\|
. Then

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s=0}^{t}\left\|X(\alpha(s))-Y(\beta(s))\right\|\overset{\mbox{\tiny{\eqref{eq:BMo}}}}{\leq}\int_{s=0}^{t}\big(A+\left\|X(\alpha(s))-X(\beta(s))\right\|\big)

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{\tiny{\eqref{eq:AMo}}}}{\leq}\int_{s=0}^{t}\big(A+Kf(s)\big)\leq AT+K\int_{s=0}^{t}f(t)\;.

Hence, the claim follows from Lemma
2.1
.
∎

2.2.
Graphons

Our notation regarding graphons follows
[
18
]
. Recall that
(\Omega,\pi)
is an atomless probability space with an implicit separable sigma-algebra. We write
\mathcal{W}\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
for the set of bounded symmetric measurable functions; these are called

.
Graphons
, which we denote by
\mathcal{W}_{0}
, are then those kernels whose range is a subset of
[0,1]
.

2.2.1.
Densities and degrees

The notion of homomorphism densities of a finite graph is one of the cornerstones of the theory of graph limits.

For a function
f:\Omega^{V}\to\mathbb{R}
and a subset
S\subset V
we write

\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{S}=\int_{\Omega^{S}}f(x_{i}:i\in V)\prod_{i\in S}\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}).

Definition 2.4
.

Given a kernel
W
and a graph
F=(V,E)
, we define the
density of
F
in
W
as

t(F,W)\coloneqq\int\prod_{ij\in E}W(x_{i},x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{V}\;.

and the
induced density of
F
in
W
as

{t_{\mathrm{ind}}}(F,W)\coloneqq\int\prod_{ij\in E}W(x_{i},x_{j})\prod_{ij\notin E}\left(1-W(x_{i},x_{j})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{V}\;.

In particular, the
edge density of
W
is the number
t(K_{2},W)=\int W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}
.

A
rooted graph
is a triple
(R,V,E)
, where
(V,E)
is a graph and
R
is a subset of
V
; we call the elements of
R
the
roots
.

Definition 2.5

(Rooted densities)
.

Given a graphon
W
, a rooted graph
F=(R,V,E)
, and
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{i}:i\in R)\in\Omega^{R}
we define the functions

\displaystyle t^{\mathbf{x}}(F,W)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\prod_{ij\in E}W(x_{i},x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{V\setminus R}\;\textrm{ and}

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{\mathbf{x}}(F,W)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\prod_{ij\in E}W(x_{i},x_{j})\prod_{ij\notin E}\left(1-W(x_{i},x_{j})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{V\setminus R}\;.

Further, if
\mathbf{A}=(A_{i})_{i\in R}
is a collection of sets
A_{i}\subset\Omega
then we define the quantities

t^{\mathbf{A}}(F,W)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{i}A_{i}}t^{\mathbf{x}}(F,W)\quad\text{and}\quad t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{\mathbf{A}}(F,W)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{i}A_{i}}t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{\mathbf{x}}(F,W)\;.

One of the key properties of densities is that they, as functions of a graphon, are continuous with respect to the cut norm distance.

Lemma 2.6

(Counting lemma,
[
18
, Lemma 10.23]
)
.

Suppose that we have a graph
F
of order
k
and two graphons
U
and
W
. Then we have

|t(F,U)-t(F,W)|\leq\textstyle\binom{k}{2}d_{\square}(U,W).

Lastly, we introduce the notions of degree and degree-regularity.

Definition 2.7
.

The

of a kernel

is the function
\deg_{W}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
\deg_{W}(x)=\int_{y\in\Omega}W(x,y)
. We say that a kernel

of edge density

is

if for
\pi
-almost every

.

2.2.2.
Sampling

A graphon
W
defines, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a probability distribution on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
as follows. Sample independent

-random elements

,

\delta_{\square}(G,W)=\inf\{d_{\square}(U,W)\}

Lemma 2.8

(Sampling lemma,
[

)

For

\mathbb{P}\left(\delta_{\square}(\mathbb{G}(k,W),W)\leq 22/\sqrt{\log_{2}k}\right)\geq 1-\exp(-k/(2\log_{2}k))\;.

2.2.3.

In Section

While for many purposes in the area of graph limits the Frieze–Kannan regularity lemma is sufficient, here we need Szemerédi’s regularity lemma. The notions of density and regularity are standard, but typically appear in the setting of finite graphs. The graphon setting is used for example in

Definition 2.9
.

Suppose that
W
is a graphon and

\left|\pi(C^{\prime})\pi(D^{\prime})d(C,D)-\int_{C^{\prime}\times D^{\prime}}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}\right|\leq\alpha\pi(C)\pi(D)\;.

.

Suppose that

is a graphon. For

Theorem 2.11

For each

there exists an

-regular partition of

with at most

sets.

In the setting of regular pairs, homomorphism densities can be controlled. This is the subject of the counting lemma, which we state in a version tailored to our purposes.

Let

Proof.

2.2.4.
Inequality between clique densities

Here we shall prove an inequality which relates clique densities in graphons. This is a consequence of the Kruskal–Katona theorem
[
16
,
15
]
. In fact, our proof uses a slightly weaker version due to Lovász
[

, Exercise 13.31(b)]
. While we need this inequality only for the case

(which can be derived from
[
9
]
), we shall state and prove the general case for possible future use. For

and a real number
x\geq m
we write
\binom{x}{m}:=\frac{x(x-1)\ldots(x-m+1)}{m!}
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
be a graphon and
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
r<k
. Then we have

t(K_{r},W)\geq t(K_{k},W)^{r/k}\;.

In particular, for all
k\geq 3
we have
\int W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}=t(K_{2},W)\geq t(K_{k},W)^{2/k}
.

Proof.

Define
\alpha:=t(K_{k},W)^{1/k}
. Pick a sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of graphs with
v(G_{n})=n
which converges to the graphon
W
. Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers so that the number of
k
-cliques in
G_{n}
is
\binom{u_{n}}{k}
, that is,
t(K_{k},G_{n})={\binom{u_{n}}{k}}/{\binom{n}{k}}
; this implies
u_{n}\sim\alpha n
.
Exercise 13.31 in Lovász
[
17
]
(for
H
consisting of the vertex sets of
k
-cliques) implies that the number of

-cliques in
G_{n}
is at least
\binom{u_{n}}{r}
, whence

t(K_{r},W)=\lim_{n\to\infty}t(K_{r},G_{n})\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\binom{u_{n}}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}=\alpha^{r}=t(K_{k},W)^{r/k}\;,

as required.

2.3.
Probability

The classical Berry–Esseen theorem establishes quantitative bounds on the accuracy of the approximation of the probability distribution of a sum of independent and identically distributed random variables by a normal distribution with the same mean and variance. We will apply this to prove Theorem

Theorem 2.14

(Berry–Esseen theorem)
.

There is a positive constant
C
such that the following holds. Suppose that
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
are i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=0
,
\var X_{i}=\sigma^{2}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{3}=\beta<\infty
for all

. Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq x\sigma\sqrt{k}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z\leq x\right)\right|\leq\frac{C\beta}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{k}},

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian random variable.

\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)=\sum_{a\neq b}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)-W(x,y)\right].

It follows that

(16)

\displaystyle\int\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{H})-e(\mathbb{F})\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})-(k)_{2}t(K_{2},W).

3.2.
Trajectories

In this subsection, we recap the notion of trajectories which we mentioned informally in Theorem
1.1
. We make several adjustments, following the way trajectories were constructed in
[
12
]
. Firstly, we define trajectories not only for graphons but for general kernels. Secondly, we define not only forward evolution in time but also backward evolution. It turns out that in order to allow for these generalizations, we cannot prescribe the time domain for a trajectory in advance.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a rule
\mathcal{R}
and a kernel
W\in\mathcal{W}
, a
trajectory starting at
W
is a differentiable function
\Phi^{\cdot}W:I\to(\mathcal{W},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})
defined on an open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing zero, that satisfies the autonomous differential equation

(18)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\Phi^{t}W=\mathfrak{V}_{\>}\Phi^{t}W\;

with the initial condition

(19)

\Phi^{0}W=W\;.\\

The following theorem implies that if a trajectory starts a graphon (rather than a general kernel), it is unique (provided we extended it maximally) and is defined for all positive times. Such a trajectory is always defined for some negative times (as
\mathcal{D}_{W}
is open), but it might leave the graphon space immediately (i.e., we might have
I_{W}=[0,\infty)
).

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem
in
[
12
]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is a rule and
W\in\mathcal{W}
is a kernel. Then the following hold.

(i)

There is an open interval
\mathcal{D}_{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing
0
and a trajectory
\Phi^{\cdot}W:\mathcal{D}_{W}\to\mathcal{W}
starting at
W
such that any other trajectory starting at
W
is a restriction of
\Phi^{\cdot}W
to a subinterval of
\mathcal{D}_{W}
.

(ii)

For any
u\in\mathcal{D}_{W}
we have
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi^{u}W}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t+u\in\mathcal{D}_{W}\}
and for every
t\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi^{u}W}
we have

\Phi^{t}\Phi^{u}W=\Phi^{t+u}W.

(iii)

If
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
is a graphon, the set
I_{W}:=\{t\in\mathcal{D}_{W}:\Phi^{t}W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\}
is a closed interval containing
[0,\infty)
.

Definition 3.3
.

For
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
we write
\mathrm{age}(W):=-\inf I_{W}\in[0,\infty]
.

Remark 3.4

(Remark
in
[
12
]
)
.

A trajectory
\Phi^{\cdot}W:I\to\mathcal{W}
satisfies

\Phi^{t}W=W+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{\tau}W\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad t\in I,

where the integral is defined with respect to the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

The last preliminary result we shall need about trajectories says the following. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule and
\mathcal{A}
be a set of graphons which have a certain profile on a fixed rectangle
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
. Suppose that for each graphon in
\mathcal{A}
, the velocity restricted to the rectangle
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
is zero. Then each trajectory which starts in
\mathcal{A}
stays confined to
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 3.5

(Proposition
in
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule and
\alpha:S\rightarrow[0,1]
be a symmetric function where
S:=\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\cup\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\{W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}:W{\restriction_{S}}=\alpha\}
. Suppose that for each
W\in\mathcal{A}
we have
(\mathfrak{V}_{\>}W){\restriction_{S}}=0
. Then for each
U\in\mathcal{A}
and each
t\geq 0
we have
\Phi^{t}U\in\mathcal{A}
.

In this subsection we assume that

For every

3.4.
Destinations and speed of convergence

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule. We say that the trajectory of a graphon
U
is
convergent
if there exists a
destination
of
U
, that is, a graphon denoted by
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(U)
such that
\Phi^{t}U\xrightarrow{\square}\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(U)
as
t\to\infty
. We say that the flip process with rule
\mathcal{R}
is
convergent
if all its trajectories converge.

Proposition 3.9

(
[
12
, Proposition
]
)
.

If
W=\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(U)
for some
U\in\mathcal{W}_{0}

\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W~=~0
.

For a rule
\mathcal{R}
, and
\delta>0
we define the
lower convergence time

\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)
and the
upper convergence time

\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)
by

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\sup\{\inf\{t:d_{\square}(\Phi^{t}W,\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W))<\delta\}:W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\}\;,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\sup\{\sup\{t:d_{\square}(\Phi^{t}W,\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W))\geq\delta\}:W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\}\;.

4.
Ignorant flip processes

The first and in a sense the most trivial class of flip processes we introduce are the ignorant flip processes. These are flip processes in which the replacement graph is selected independently of the drawn graph.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k
is
ignorant
if for any
F,F^{\prime},H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have
\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=\mathcal{R}_{F^{\prime},H}
. The
ignorant output density
is
d:=\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{F,H}e(H)
(here
F
is arbitrary).

Observe that the Erdős–Rényi flip process is an ignorant flip process of order 2 with output distribution
\{\mathcal{R}_{F,H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{K_{2}}
for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

Our main result about ignorant rules is Proposition
4.2
, which explicitly describes their trajectories, destinations, speed of convergence and age.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is an ignorant rule of order
k
with ignorant output density
d
. Then for each graphon
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
t\in I_{W}
we have

(22)

\Phi^{t}W=d+(W-d)\cdot e^{-(k)_{2}t}.

Hence, we have
\mathrm{dest}(W)\equiv d
and

(23)

\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)=\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)=\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\max\{d,1-d\}}{\delta}\right)

for each
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
. Furthermore, writing
i_{W}:=\essinf(W)
and
s_{W}:=\esssup(W)
, we have

(24)

\mathrm{age}(W)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\min\left\{\ln\frac{d}{d-i_{W}}\;,\;\ln\frac{1-d}{s_{W}-d}\right\}\quad&\text{if }i_{W}<d<s_{W}\;,\\
\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{d}{d-i_{W}}\quad&\text{if }i_{W}<s_{W}\leq d\;,\\
\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{1-d}{s_{W}-d}\quad&\text{if }d\leq i_{W}<s_{W}\;,\\
\infty&\text{ if }W\equiv d.\end{cases}

In particular,
\mathrm{age}(W)
is finite unless
W\equiv d
.

Proof.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
. We first compute the velocity
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}
using the notation preceding equation (
15
). Since the distribution of
\mathbb{H}
is independent of
(U_{v},v\in[k])
, by (
15
) we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\neq b}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)-W(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})-(k)_{2}W(x,y)=(k)_{2}\left(d-W(x,y)\right).

It is clear that the initial condition
\Phi^{t}W=W
is satisfied. By the uniqueness of trajectories, it is enough to verify that the function on the right hand side of (
22
) satisfies the differential equation (
18
). For
x,y\in\Omega
we set
f(t)=d+(W(x,y)-d)\cdot e^{-(k)_{2}t}
. Observe that for each
t\geq 0
we have

\frac{f(t+\varepsilon)-f(t)}{\varepsilon}\to f^{\prime}(t)=(k)_{2}\left(d-W(x,y)\right)e^{-(k)_{2}t}=(k)_{2}\left(d-f(t)\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where the convergence is uniform over all choices of
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
by Taylor’s theorem because
W
is bounded. This implies that (
22
) indeed describes the trajectory starting at
W
under the ignorant rule
\mathcal{R}
.

Clearly, all trajectories converge to the same destination, the constant-
d
graphon. By (
22
) we have
d_{\square}(\Phi^{t}W,d)\leq\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W-d\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\max\{d,1-d\}e^{-(k)_{2}t}
, with both equalities attained at the constant-
0
graphon if
d\geq 1/2
and at the constant-
1
graphon if
d\leq 1/2
. Hence, we have
\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)=\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)=\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\max\{d,1-d\}}{\delta}\right)
for each
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Now observe that (
22
) tells us that for a point
(x,y)
where
W(x,y)<d
, the value of
\Phi^{-t}W
dips below
0
at
t=\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{d}{d-W(x,y)}
, and for a point
(x,y)
where
W(x,y)>d
, the value of
\Phi^{-t}W
exceeds
1
at
t=\frac{1}{(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{1-d}{W(x,y)-d}
. This implies (
24
).
∎

5.
Stirring flip processes

All trajectories of ignorant flip processes converge to a constant given by the ignorant output density. Being a bit less ignorant this time, we introduce
stirring flip processes
, which takes into account the edge count (but nothing else) of the drawn graph. In this section we focus on
balanced
stirring flip processes, in which the expected number of edges in the replacement graph is equal to the number of edges in the drawn graph. In Section
7
we will study
extremist flip processes
, which are “very unbalanced” stirring flip processes.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that a rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k
is
stirring
if for each
F
and
F^{\prime}
with
e(F)=e(F^{\prime})
and each
H
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=\mathcal{R}_{F^{\prime},H}
. We say that the rule
\mathcal{R}
is
balanced
if for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have
e(F)=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{F,H}e(H)
.

Two natural examples of a balanced stirring rule are the rule where a drawn graph with
\ell
edges is replaced by the Erdős–Rényi uniform random graph with
\ell
edges and the rule where a drawn graph with
\ell
edges is replaced by the Erdős–Rényi binomial random graph with edge probability
\ell/\binom{k}{2}
.

Our main result describes the trajectories of a stirring flip process in the most important balanced case.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is a balanced stirring rule of order
k\geq 3
and let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
. Then for every
t\in I_{W}
we have

(25)

\begin{split}\Phi^{t}W(x,y)&=\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}+(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)-2\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1})\cdot e^{-t(k-1)_{2}}\\
&\quad+(W(x,y)-\deg_{W}(x)-\deg_{W}(y)+\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1})\cdot e^{-t((k)_{2}-2)}\;.\end{split}

In particular, the edge density stays constant along the trajectory and
\mathrm{dest}(W)=\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and set
d:=\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}
. The following is our key claim.

Claim 5.3
.

Given a graphon
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
, for all
x,y\in\Omega
we have

(26)

\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)=(2k-4)(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y))+(k-2)_{2}d-\left((k)_{2}-2\right)W(x,y).

Before we prove this claim, let us first demonstrate how it implies our theorem. By the uniqueness of trajectories, it is enough to verify that (
25
) satisfies the differential equation (
18
). Write
f_{(x,y)}(t)
for the right-hand side of (
25
), set
U_{t}
to be the graphon given by
U_{t}(x,y):=f_{(x,y)}(t)
and set
g_{x}(t):=\deg_{U_{t}}(x)=d+(\deg_{W}(x)-d)e^{-t(k-1)_{2}}
. Observe that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{(x,y)}(t)

\displaystyle=-(k-1)_{2}(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)-2d)e^{-t(k-1)_{2}}

\displaystyle\;\quad-((k)_{2}-2)(W(x,y)-\deg_{W}(x)-\deg_{W}(y)+d)e^{-t((k)_{2}-2)}

\displaystyle=\left((k)_{2}-2\right)(d-f_{(x,y)}(t))+2(k-2)(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)-2d)e^{-t(k-1)_{2}}

\displaystyle=(2k-4)(g_{x}(t)+g_{y}(t))+(k-2)_{2}d-\left((k)_{2}-2\right)f_{(x,y)}(t)\;.

Furthermore, for each
t\geq 0
the convergence in
(f_{(x,y)}(t+\varepsilon)-f_{(x,y)}(t))/\varepsilon\to f^{\prime}_{(x,y)}(t)
is uniform over all choices of
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
by Taylor’s theorem because
W
is bounded. This implies that
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}U_{t}=\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}U_{t}
in the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
. Moreover, we have that
U_{0}=W
. Hence, by uniqueness (
25
) indeed describes the trajectory starting at
W
under a balanced stirring rule.

It remains to prove Claim
5.3
.

Proof of Claim
5.3
.

Let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
x,y\in\Omega
. Using the notation preceding equation (
15
), observe that

(27)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\,|\,U_{1}=x,U_{2}=y\right)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\neq b}\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{F}\,|\,U_{1}=x,U_{2}=y\right)\\
&=W(x,y)+(k-2)\left(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)\right)+\binom{k-2}{2}d.\end{split}

Setting
q_{m}(ab):=\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,e(\mathbb{F})=m\right)
, we have

(28)

\sum_{a\neq b}q_{m}(ab)=2\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{H})\,|\,e(\mathbb{F})=m\right)=2m.

Since the distribution of
\mathbb{F}
is invariant under permutation of vertices, for any pair of distinct vertices
a
and
b
the quantity
\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})=m\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)
is the same; we shall denote it by
p_{x,y}(m)
. We have

(29)

\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\textstyle\binom{k}{2}}p_{x,y}(m)q_{m}(ab).

By summing (
29
) over all pairs
a,b
of distinct vertices, interchanging the order of summation, and applying (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a\neq b}\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\textstyle\binom{k}{2}}p_{x,y}(m)\sum_{a\neq b}q_{m}(ab)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\textstyle\binom{k}{2}}\left[p_{x,y}(m)\cdot 2m\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\,|\,U_{1}=x,U_{2}=y\right).

Now by recalling (
15
) and applying (
27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\neq b}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)-W(x,y)\right],

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\,|\,U_{1}=x,U_{2}=y\right)-(k)_{2}W(x,y)

\displaystyle=(2k-4)(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y))+(k-2)_{2}d-W(x,y)\left((k)_{2}-2\right)

as required.

∎

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.1.
Age of a balanced stirring process

Recall that the age of a graphon
W
is the infimum of times
t\geq 0
for which the range of
\Phi^{-t}W
is not wholly contained in
[0,1]
. That is, given a balanced stirring rule as in Proposition
5.2
, we want to know the infimum of
t\geq 0
where we have for some
x,y\in\Omega
that

(30)

\begin{split}\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}&+(\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)-2\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1})\cdot e^{t(k-1)_{2}}+\\
&+(W(x,y)-\deg_{W}(x)-\deg_{W}(y)+\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1})\cdot e^{t((k)_{2}-2)}\;\end{split}

is more than 1 or less than 0. While there is no simple formula to deduce the age from (
30
) in general because we have two exponential terms with different coefficients and exponents, in the next proposition we give a formula for the age in two special cases: degree-regular graphons and graphons with a separable form. These correspond to the cases where only one exponential term has a positive coefficient. Further, we show that the age is finite except when the graphon is constant.

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is a balanced stirring rule of order
k\geq 3
. Then the following hold for all
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
. Write
d:=\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}
. If
W=d
, then
\mathrm{age}(W)=\infty
. Otherwise, we have
\mathrm{age}(W)<\infty
and in particular:

(i)

if
W
is degree-regular, then

\mathrm{age}(W)=\frac{1}{(k)_{2}-2}\cdot\min\left\{\ln\frac{d}{d-\essinf(W)}\;,\;\ln\frac{1-d}{\esssup(W)-d}\right\}\;;

(ii)

if
W
is of the form
W(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)
, then

\mathrm{age}(W)=\frac{1}{(k-1)_{1}}\cdot\min\left\{\ln\frac{d}{d-\essinf(W)}\;,\;\ln\frac{1-d}{\esssup(W)-d}\right\}\;.

Proof.

W=d
is clearly a fixed point, so we have
\mathrm{age}(W)=\infty
. We now show that
\mathrm{age}(W)=\infty
only if
W=d
. Suppose that
\mathrm{age}(W)=\infty
.
Since
(k-1)_{2}\neq(k)_{2}-2
, the expression in (
30
) can stay in
[0,1]
for all
x,y\in\Omega
only if the coefficients of both exponential terms are zero.
The first one implies
\deg_{W}\equiv d
and then the second one implies
W\equiv d
.

Hence, we further assume that
W
is not constant, which implies
\essinf W<d<\esssup W
. In the case
(i)
, the equation (
30
) simplifies to

d+(W(x,y)-d)\cdot e^{t((k)_{2}-2)}\;,

and then the formula for age indeed expresses the time when the values of
\Phi^{-t}W
first cross one of the two thresholds
0
and
1
.

The case
(ii)
is equivalent to
W(x,y)=\deg_{W}(x)+\deg_{W}(y)-d
. Hence (
30
) simplifies to

d+(W(x,y)-d)\cdot e^{t(k-1)_{1}}\;,

which implies the age in the same way as in the case
(i)
.
∎

6.
Complementing flip processes

The
complementing rule of order
k
is the rule of order
k
where the replacement graph is the complement of the drawn graph. Our main result about complementing rules is Proposition
6.1
, which explicitly describes their trajectories, destinations, speed of convergence and age.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is the complementing rule of order
k\geq 2
. Then for each graphon
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
t\in I_{W}
we have

(31)

\Phi^{t}W(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}+\left(W(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot e^{-2(k)_{2}t}\;.

Hence, we have
\mathrm{dest}(W)\equiv 1/2
and

(32)

\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)=\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)=\frac{-\ln(2\delta)}{2(k)_{2}}

for each
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
. Furthermore, writing
i_{W}:=\essinf(W)
and
s_{W}:=\esssup(W)
, we have

(33)

\mathrm{age}(W)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2(k)_{2}}\min\left\{\ln\frac{1}{1-2i_{W}}\;,\;\ln\frac{1}{2s_{W}-1}\right\}\quad&\text{if }i_{W}<1/2<s_{W}\;,\\
\frac{1}{2(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{1}{1-2i_{W}}\quad&\text{if }i_{W}<s_{W}\leq 1/2\;,\\
\frac{1}{2(k)_{2}}\ln\frac{1}{2s_{W}}\quad&\text{if }1/2\leq i_{W}<s_{W}\;,\\
\infty&\text{ if }W\equiv 1/2.\end{cases}

Proof.

We calculate the velocity using the notation preceding the formula (
15
). Note that
ab\in\mathbb{H}
if and only if
ab\notin\mathbb{F}
, so

\mathbb{P}\left(ab\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(ab\notin\mathbb{F}\,|\,U_{a}=x,U_{b}=y\right)=1-W(x,y)\;.

Hence, by (
15
) we have

\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)=\sum_{1\leq a\neq b\leq k}(1-W(x,y)-W(x,y))=(k)_{2}(1-2W(x,y))\;.

Write
f_{(x,y)}(t)
for the right-hand side of (
31
). Observe that for each
t\geq 0
we have

\frac{f_{(x,y)}(t+\varepsilon)-f_{(x,y)}(t)}{\varepsilon}\to f^{\prime}_{(x,y)}(t)=(k)_{2}(1-2W(x,y))e^{-2(k)_{2}t}=(k)_{2}\left(1-2f_{(x,y)}(t)\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where the convergence is uniform over all choices of
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
by Taylor’s theorem because
W
is bounded. Since the trajectory
\Phi^{\cdot}W
is unique by Theorem
3.2
, we have
\Phi^{t}W(x,y)=f_{(x,y)}(t)
as desired.

Clearly, the unique destination is the constant-
\frac{1}{2}
graphon. By (
22
) we have
d_{\square}(\Phi^{t}W,\frac{1}{2})\leq\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W-\frac{1}{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-2(k)_{2}t}
, with both equalities attained at the constant-
0
and constant-
1
graphons. Hence, we have
\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)=\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\delta)=\frac{-\ln(2\delta)}{2(k)_{2}}
for each
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Note that (
31
) tells us that for a point
(x,y)
where
W(x,y)<\frac{1}{2}
, the value of
\Phi^{-t}W
dips below
0
at
t=-\frac{1}{2(k)_{2}}\ln(1-2W(x,y))
, and for a point
(x,y)
where
W(x,y)>\frac{1}{2}
, the value of
\Phi^{-t}W
exceeds
1
at
t=-\frac{1}{2(k)_{2}}\ln(2W(x,y)-1)
. This implies (
33
).
∎

We conjecture that the complementing flip process exhibits the fastest convergence among flip processes whose trajectories converge to a single destination. Observe that the convergence time of the complementing flip process (see (
32
)) is half that of an ignorant process with output density
1/2
(cf. (
23
)).

Conjecture 6.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is a convergent flip process of order
k
with a single destination. Then for each
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
we have
\tau_{\mathcal{R}}^{-}(\delta)\geq\frac{-\ln(2\delta)}{2(k)_{2}}
.

7.
Extremist flip processes

In this section we define the extremist rules and prove results about their behaviour.

Definition 7.1

(Extremist rule)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The
extremist
rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k
is defined by

(34)

\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if }e(F)<\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\textrm{ and }H=\overline{K_{k}},\\
1&\textrm{ if }e(F)>\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\textrm{ and }H=K_{k},\\
1&\textrm{ if }e(F)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\textrm{ and }H=F,\\
0&\textrm{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

A possible variation of the extremist rule is to permit the replacement of graphs with
\binom{k}{2}/2
edges by a clique or empty graph with equal probabilities. In fact, most proofs from this section (except for Theorem
7.6
) work for this variation, but for the sake of simplicity we shall not consider it.

7.1.
Behaviour on constants

We begin by considering the behaviour of trajectories under extremist rules on constant graphons. Recall that a trajectory that starts with a constant graphon takes values only among constant graphons and the velocity is a constant function (see
[
12
, Corollary
]
). Therefore, we shall identify the constant-
p
kernel with the number
p
. We prove the following proposition, which implies that the trajectory of an extremist rule which starts from the constant-
p
graphon converges to
0
if
p<1/2
, to
1
if
p>1/2
and is fixed for
p=1/2
.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
\mathcal{R}
is the extremist rule of order
k\geq 3
, then we have
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)=0
for
p\in\left\{0,1/2,1\right\}
,
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)<0
for
p\in(0,1/2)
and
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)>0
for
p\in(1/2,1)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)
is a constant kernel, we have
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)\equiv\int_{\Omega^{2}}\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)
. Therefore, by (
17
) and denoting by
\mathbb{G}
the random graph
\mathbb{G}(k,p)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)

\displaystyle=2\left[\textstyle\binom{k}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{G})>\textstyle\binom{k}{2}/2\right)+\textstyle\binom{k}{2}/2\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{G})=\textstyle\binom{k}{2}/2\right)\right]-(k)_{2}p.

Writing
n=\binom{k}{2}
and
B_{n,p}
for a binomial random variable with parameters
n
and
p
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)

\displaystyle=2n\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}>n/2)+n\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}=n/2)-2np

\displaystyle=n\big(\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}>n/2)+\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}\geq n/2)-2p\big).

It is clear that
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)=0
for
p\in\left\{0,1/2,1\right\}
. To prove the proposition, it is enough to show that for the function

f_{n}(p):=\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}>n/2)+\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}\geq n/2)

its derivative
f_{n}^{\prime}
is strictly increasing on
(0,1/2)
and strictly decreasing on
(1/2,1)
. Indeed, this implies that
p\mapsto f_{n}(p)
is strictly convex on
(0,1/2)
and strictly concave on
(1/2,1)
. Then, these properties are inherited by
p\mapsto\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)
, thereby implying that
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}(p)
is negative on
(0,1/2)
and positive on
(1/2,1)
. Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}p}\left(\mathbb{P}(B_{n,p}=k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}p}\left(\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}\left(kp^{k-1}(1-p)^{n-k}-(n-k)p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=n\left[\binom{n-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{n-k}-\binom{n-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=n\left[\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k-1)-\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k)\right].

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle f_{n}^{\prime}(p)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{k>n/2}\left[\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k-1)-\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+n\sum_{k\geq n/2}\left[\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k-1)-\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=k)\right]

\displaystyle=n\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(B_{n-1,p}=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(B_{n-1,p}=\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rceil\right)\right)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\begin{cases}2\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor),&\quad n\text{ odd}\\
\mathbb{P}(B_{n-1,p}=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor)\left(1+\frac{p}{1-p}\right)&\quad n\text{ even}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\mathbb{I}_{n\text{ odd}}\right)n\cdot\binom{n-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor}(p(1-p))^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor},

Now
k\geq 3
implies that
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor\geq 1
, so the polynomial
f^{\prime}_{n}(p)
inherits from the parabola
p(1-p)
the property of being strictly increasing on
(0,1/2)
and strictly decreasing on
(1/2,1)
. This completes our proof.
∎

7.2.
Density thresholds

In the previous subsection we saw that for constant graphons we have
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=0
as soon as the density of
W
is below
1/2
. In this subsection we consider when we start with an arbitrary initial graphon
W
and investigate what density guarantees
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=0
. We define the
density threshold
for the extremist rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k\geq 3
to be

\theta(k):=\sup\{p\in[0,1]:\forall W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}\ \left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}\leq p\text{ implies }\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=0\}.

In the following proposition, we collect a few useful properties related to the density threshold.

Proposition 7.3
.

The following holds for the extremist rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k\geq 3
.

(i)

For every
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and every
t\in I_{W}
we have
\Phi^{t}(1-W)=1-\Phi^{t}W
. In particular, if
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=0
, then
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(1-W)=1
.

(ii)

For each
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
satisfying
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}>1-\theta(k)
we have
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=1
.

(iii)

\theta(k)\leq 1/2
.

Proof.

First we show
(i)
.
From formula (
15
) it is easy to see that for any graphon
W
we have
\mathfrak{V}_{\>}(1-W)=-\mathfrak{V}_{\>}W
, so
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}(1-\Phi^{t}W)=-\mathfrak{V}_{\>}\Phi^{t}W=\mathfrak{V}_{\>}(1-\Phi^{t}(W))
, that is,
t\mapsto 1-\Phi^{t}W
is a trajectory. Since it starts at the same graphon as the trajectory
t\mapsto\Phi^{t}(1-W)
, by uniqueness (see Theorem
3.2
) it follows that
\Phi^{t}(1-W)=1-\Phi^{t}W
for every
t\in I_{W}
. The second conclusion is obvious.

To prove
(ii)
, let
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
satisfy
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}>1-\theta(k)
. Since
\left\lVert 1-W\right\rVert_{1}=1-\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}<\theta(k)
, we have
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(1-W)=0
and therefore
(i)
implies
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=1
.

Part
(iii)
follows from the fact that the constant-
1/2
graphon
W
satisfies
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=1/2
.
∎

We prove the following lower bound on the density threshold. In particular, we show that the density threshold is strictly positive for
k\geq 5
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
k\geq 5
be an integer and define
\sigma(k):=\max\{2^{-4-24/(k-5)},1/2-\sqrt{48/k}\}
for
k\geq 6
and
\sigma(5)=1/30
. For the extremist rule
\mathcal{R}
of order
k
we have that

\theta(k)\geq\sigma(k)>0.

Proof.

The following is our key claim.

Claim 7.5
.

Given a graphon
U\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
satisfying
\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sigma(k)
, we have

(35)

\int\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}U\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}\leq-\binom{k}{2}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{1}.

Before we prove this claim, let us first demonstrate how it implies our theorem. Fix
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
satisfying
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sigma(k)
. Consider the function
f(t):=\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W\right\rVert_{1}
defined for
t\in[0,\infty)
. Aiming to apply Lemma
2.2
with
D=\sigma(k)
, we note that
f(0)\leq\sigma(k)
and
f(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
. We argue that
f^{\prime}(t)=\int\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{t}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}
. Indeed, we have that

f^{\prime}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left\lVert\Phi^{t+\varepsilon}W\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W\right\rVert_{1}}{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\frac{\Phi^{t+\varepsilon}W-\Phi^{t}W}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}.

Now by Lemma
3.7
the integrand is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{t}W
in the
L^{\infty}
-norm (and thus in the
L^{1}
-norm), so in the limit we get
f^{\prime}(t)=\int\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}\Phi^{t}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}
. Together with (
35
) we get that
f^{\prime}(t)\leq-\textstyle\binom{k}{2}f(t)
whenever
f(t)\leq\sigma(k)
, so by Lemma
2.2
we have
f(t)\leq f(0)e^{-\binom{k}{2}t}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Therefore, it follows that
\mathrm{dest}_{\mathcal{R}}(W)=0
and we conclude that
\theta(k)\geq\sigma(k)>0
.

It remains to prove Claim
7.5
.

Proof of Claim
7.5
.

Set
p:=\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{1}
. Sample
\mathbb{F}\sim\mathbb{G}(k,U)
and sample
\mathbb{H}
according to the distribution
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{H}=H\,|\,\mathbb{F}=F\right)=\mathcal{R}_{F,H}
. We first obtain an upper bound for
\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})
. Since
\mathbb{E}\left(e(\mathbb{H})\,|\,\mathbb{F}=F\right)=0
for any
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
with fewer than
\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}
edges, we have

(36)

\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})\leq\binom{k}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right).

Now suppose that we have

(37)

\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)\leq\frac{p}{2}.

Then by (
17
) and (
36
) we conclude that

\int\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}U\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}=2\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})-(k)_{2}\cdot p\leq p\left(\binom{k}{2}-(k)_{2}\right)=-p\binom{k}{2},

which yields (
35
). Hence, it remains to prove (
37
).

We begin with
k=5
and
p\leq 1/30
. Note that if
\mathbb{F}
has at least
\frac{1}{2}\textstyle\binom{k}{2}=5
edges then it has a matching with
2
edges.
There are
\binom{5}{2}\binom{3}{2}/2=15
such matchings in
K_{5}
and each of them appears in
\mathbb{F}
with probability
p^{2}
(as each edge occurs independently with probability
p
), so we have

\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{F}\text{ contains a $2$-matching}\right)\leq 15p^{2}\leq\frac{p}{2}.

Next, we consider
k\geq 6
and
p\leq 2^{-4-24/(k-5)}
. We partition
K_{k}
into disjoint maximum matchings
M_{1},\dots,M_{r}
, with
r=\binom{k}{2}/\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor\in\{k-1,k\}
. If
e(\mathbb{F})\geq\binom{k}{2}/2
then there exists
i\in[r]
such that

e(\mathbb{F}\cap M_{i})\geq\left\lceil\frac{\textstyle\binom{k}{2}}{2r}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}{2}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{4}\right\rfloor=:k^{\prime}\;.

Therefore, using
r\leq k\leq 2^{k/2}
and
\binom{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{k^{\prime}}\leq 2^{k/2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in[r]}\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F}\cap M_{i})\geq k^{\prime}\right)\leq r\binom{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{k^{\prime}}p^{k^{\prime}}\leq 2^{k}p^{(k-1)/4}\leq\frac{p}{2}.

Finally, we consider
2^{-4-24/(k-5)}\leq p\leq 1/2-\sqrt{48/k}
; here we may assume that
k\geq 192
. We consider the function
f(G):=e(G)/(v(G)-1)
. Since
f
varies by at most
1
under the editing of edges incident to any fixed vertex, by
[
18
, Theorem 10.3]
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(f(\mathbb{F})\geq\mathbb{E}f(\mathbb{F})+(\tfrac{1}{2}-p)k/2\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1/2-p)^{2}k}{8}\right).

Now we have
e>2
and
\frac{(1/2-p)^{2}k}{8}\geq 6\geq 5+\frac{24}{k-5}
, so we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})\geq\textstyle\binom{k}{2}/2\right)\leq 2^{-5-24/(k-5)}\leq\frac{p}{2}

as required.

∎

This completes our proof.
∎

Now we show that the results of Theorem
7.4
cannot be extended to
k\in\{3,4\}
.

Theorem 7.6
.

\theta(3)=\theta(4)=0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be the extremist rule of order
k\in\{3,4\}
. Define

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(F,a,b):F\in\mathcal{H}_{k},e(F)<\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2},ab\in E(F)\right\}\ \textrm{ and }

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(F,a,b):F\in\mathcal{H}_{k},e(F)>\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2},ab\notin E(F)\right\}.

By (
14
) and (
34
), for each
W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and each
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
we have

(38)

\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W(x,y)=\sum_{(F,a,b)\in B_{2}}t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{(x,y)}(F^{a,b},W)-\sum_{(F,a,b)\in B_{1}}t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{(x,y)}(F^{a,b},W).

We shall construct a sequence
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of graphons so that
\left\lVert W_{n}\right\rVert_{1}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W_{n}\right\rVert_{\square}\nrightarrow 0
as
t\to\infty
. Fix an arbitrary sequence
(\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of subsets of
\Omega
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\pi(\Omega_{n})=1/n
and
\Omega_{n+1}\subseteq\Omega_{n}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
set
S_{n}:=((\Omega\setminus\Omega_{n})\times\Omega_{n})\cup(\Omega_{n}\times(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{n}))
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{W\in\mathcal{W}_{0}:W{\restriction_{S_{n}}}=1\}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the graphon
W_{n}:=\mathbb{I}_{S_{n}}
. Note that
\left\lVert W_{n}\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{2}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
W_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
. By Theorem
3.2
there is a unique trajectory
\Phi^{t}W_{n}
on
[0,\infty)
. Let
U\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
. For any
(F,a,b)\in B_{1}\cup B_{2}
and
(x,y)\in S_{n}
we have
t_{\mathrm{ind}}^{(x,y)}(F^{a,b},U)=0
, so by (
38
) we have
(\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}U){\restriction_{S_{n}}}=0
. Now by applying Proposition
3.5
with
\mathcal{A}_{n}
we have
\Phi^{t}W_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. We have
\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W_{n}\right\rVert_{\square}\geq\frac{2}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
; in particular,
\left\lVert\Phi^{t}W_{n}\right\rVert_{\square}\nrightarrow 0
as
t\to\infty
. Hence, we have
\theta(3)=\theta(4)=0
.
∎

We also prove an upper bound on the density threshold.

Theorem 7.7
.

There exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geq K
, setting
C_{k}:=(k)_{2}^{2}2^{\binom{k}{2}-1}
, we have
\theta(k)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{(k)_{2}}{2\cdot 10^{5}C_{k}}
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the constant returned by Theorem
2.14
and set
K:=400C^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the extremist rule of order
k\geq K
and set
f(k):=\frac{(k)_{2}}{2\cdot 10^{5}C_{k}}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\theta(k)>1/2-f(k)
.

Set
\delta:=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-2\theta(k)+2f(k)}
and
\gamma:=1/2-\delta
. Fix a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
such that
\pi(\Omega^{\prime})=\gamma
. Set
S:=((\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})\times\Omega^{\prime})\cup(\Omega^{\prime}\times(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}))
. Let
W:=\mathbb{I}_{S}
. Note that
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}=\theta(k)-f(k)<\theta(k)
. Sample
\mathbb{F}\sim\mathbb{G}(k,W)
and sample
\mathbb{H}
according to the distribution
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{H}=H\,|\,\mathbb{F}=F\right)=\mathcal{R}_{F,H}
. Note that

(39)

\mathbb{E}e(\mathbb{H})\geq\binom{k}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(e(\mathbb{F})>\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right).

Set
Y:=e(\mathbb{F})
. By the structure of
W
we have
B\sim\operatorname{Bin}(k,\gamma)
such that
Y=B(k-B)
. Write
B=\sum_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}
as the sum of
k
independent random variables
B_{i}\sim\ber(\gamma)
. Set
X_{i}:=B_{i}-\gamma
for
i\in[k]
and
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y>\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|B-\frac{k}{2}\right|<\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|X|<\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2}-k\delta\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|X|}{\sqrt{\var(X)}}<\frac{1-\sqrt{4kf(k)}}{\sqrt{1-4f(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|X|}{\sqrt{\var(X)}}<0.99\right).

Now by Theorem
2.14
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|X|}{\sqrt{\var(X)}}<0.99\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Z<0.99\right)-\frac{1}{9}\geq 0.55,

where
Z\sim N(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian random variable. Then by (
17
) and (
39
) we have

(40)

\int\mathfrak{V}_{\>}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}\geq(k)_{2}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(Y>\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\right)-\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}\right)\geq\frac{(k)_{2}}{100}.

Set
T:=\frac{1}{200C_{k}}
. By (
21
) in Lemma
3.7
we have

\int\left(\Phi^{T}W-W-T\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}\leq\left\lVert\Phi^{T}W-W-T\mathfrak{V}_{\mathcal{R}}W\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{k}(k)_{2}T^{2},

so by (
40
) we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Phi^{T}W\right\rVert_{1}=\int\Phi^{T}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}+T\int\mathfrak{V}_{\>}W\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{2}-C_{k}(k)_{2}T^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{(k)_{2}}{2\cdot 10^{4}C_{k}}-\frac{(k)_{2}}{4\cdot 10^{4}C_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(k)_{2}}{8\cdot 10^{4}C_{k}}>1-\theta(k).

Hence, by Proposition
7.3
(ii)
we have
\mathrm{dest}(W)=\mathrm{dest}(\Phi^{T}W)=1
. On the other hand,
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{1}<\theta(k)
implies that
\mathrm{dest}(W)=0
. This is a contradiction, so we must have
\theta(k)\leq 1/2-f(k)
.
∎

7.3.
Trajectory rescaling for large
k

8.
Monotone flip processes

Broadly speaking, monotone flip processes are those where the number of edges does not decrease in any step. Formally, we define a few different notions of monotonicity.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule of order
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
\mathcal{R}
is

•

non-decreasing
if for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have
\mathcal{R}_{F,H}=0
whenever
H
is not a (labelled) supergraph of
F
.

•

increasing
if it is non-decreasing and in addition for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\setminus\{K_{k}\}
there exists a proper supergraph
H
of
F
with
\mathcal{R}_{F,H}>0
.

•

density-non-decreasing
if for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have
\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{R}_{F,H}e(H)\geq e(F)
.

•

density-increasing
if it is density-non-decreasing and further for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\setminus\{K_{k}\}
the above inequality is strict.

In particular, a non-decreasing rule is density-non-decreasing and an increasing rule is density-increasing.

The following proposition asserts that non-decreasing and density-increasing flip processes are convergent, but this need not be true for density-non-decreasing flip processes.

Proposition 8.2
.

(i)

Each trajectory of a non-decreasing rule
\mathcal{R}
has a destination.

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a density-increasing rule. The destination of each trajectory is the constant-
1
graphon. The age of each graphon except the constant-
1
graphon is finite.

(iii)

There is a density-non-decreasing rule with a non-convergent trajectory.

Removal flip processes

In a removal flip process, we have a graph

which we remove if it is contained in the drawn graph while drawing an

Definition 10.1

are

Remark 10.3

Consider Definition

will that path actually be removed; if a triangle is sampled, then two of its three edges are removed. While this may seem to be at odds with the spirit of

It is not entirely apparent how to define removal rules which remove a family of graphs. For example, how should we define a removal rule of order

10.4.
Stabilization in a removal flip process

In Section
7.4
we argued that extremist flip processes may take very long to stabilize. On the other hand, removal flip processes stabilize substantially faster. Indeed, an

-removal flip process started on an

-many distinct

Question 10.13

For each graph

we cannot connect the concept of destinations to the structure of a
final graph
, that is, the graph after stabilization occurs. Our next theorem shows that, in the case of removal flip processes, destinations serve as good approximations for the final graphs.

Theorem 10.14
.

Given a graph
F
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Suppose that
G^{*}
is the final graph in the
F
-removal flip process started at an
n
-vertex graph
G
. Let
W_{G}
and
W_{G^{*}}
be the graphon representations of
G
and
G^{*}
, respectively. Then with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
we have
d_{\square}(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),W_{G^{*}})<\varepsilon
.

The proof uses several tools, including Theorem
10.9
and the graph removal lemma, the latter of which we now recall.

Proposition 10.15

(Graph removal lemma)
.

Given a graph
F
, for every
\alpha>0
there exists
\beta>0
with the following property. Suppose that
G
is an
n
-vertex graph whose density of
F
is less than
\beta
. Then we can remove at most
\alpha n^{2}
edges from
G
and make it
F
-free.

Proof of Theorem
10.14
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
F
be a graph. Observe that the statement is trivial when
e(F)=0
, so we shall assume that
e(F)>0
and
v(F)\geq 2
. Proposition
10.15
returns
\beta>0
for input
\alpha:=\frac{\varepsilon}{4e(F)}
. Set
\gamma:=\min\{\alpha,\beta/k^{2}\}
. Theorem
10.9
returns
T\geq 0
for error parameter
\gamma
. Lastly, let
n_{0}
be sufficiently large so that we have
1-\exp(-\Omega(n_{0}^{2}))\geq 1-\varepsilon
in Theorem
1.1
with error parameter
\gamma
and input
T\geq 0
.

Suppose now
G
is an
n
-vertex graph for
n\geq n_{0}
. We run the
F
-removal flip process on
G
and obtain a sequence of graphs
G_{0}:=G,G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{\kappa(G)}=:G^{*}
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the event that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cap[0,Tn^{2}]
we have
d_{\square}(W_{G_{\ell}},\Phi^{\ell/n^{2}}W_{G_{0}})<\gamma
. Theorem
1.1
tells us that the probability of this event is at least
1-\varepsilon
. Assume that
\mathcal{B}
holds. We shall prove that
d_{\square}(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),W_{G^{*}})<\varepsilon
.

We have
d_{\square}\left(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),\Phi^{T}W_{G}\right)\leq\gamma
and
d_{\square}\left(\Phi^{T}W_{G},W_{G_{Tn^{2}}}\right)\leq\gamma
by Theorem
10.9
and Theorem
1.1
respectively. Putting them together, we obtain

(74)

d_{\square}\left(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),W_{G_{Tn^{2}}}\right)\leq d_{\square}\left(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),\Phi^{T}W_{G}\right)+d_{\square}\left(\Phi^{T}W_{G},W_{G_{Tn^{2}}}\right)\leq 2\gamma.

Since
\mathrm{dest}(W_{G})
is
F
-free by Fact
10.5
, it follows from (
74
) and Lemma
2.6
that
t(F,W_{G_{Tn^{2}}})<\beta
. Then, Proposition
10.15
tells us that we can find a set
R\subset E(G_{Tn^{2}})
of at most
\frac{\varepsilon n^{2}}{4e(F)}
edges whose removal would destroy all the copies of
F
in
G_{Tn^{2}}
. In particular, every copy of
F
in
G_{Tn^{2}}
has an edge in
R
. Let
X\subseteq[Tn^{2},\ldots,\kappa(G)]
be the indices of the non-idle steps. In particular,
X\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\kappa(G)>Tn^{2}
. Observe that in each non-idle step indexed in
X
a copy of
F
in
G_{Tn^{2}}
is removed, so at least one edge from
R
is removed. Hence, we have

e(G_{Tn^{2}})-e(G^{*})=e(F)\cdot|X|\leq e(F)\cdot|R|\leq\frac{\varepsilon n^{2}}{4}\;,

which implies
d_{\square}\left(W_{G_{Tn^{2}}},W_{G^{*}}\right)\leq\varepsilon/4
. Combining this with (
74
), we obtain

d_{\square}\left(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),W_{G^{*}}\right)\leq d_{\square}\left(\mathrm{dest}(W_{G}),W_{G_{Tn^{2}}}\right)+d_{\square}\left(W_{G_{Tn^{2}}},W_{G^{*}}\right)<\varepsilon,

as was needed.
∎
