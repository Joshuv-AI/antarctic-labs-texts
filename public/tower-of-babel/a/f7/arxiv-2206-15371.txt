Discrete integrable systems and random Lax matrices
T. Grava, M. Gisonni, G. Gubbiotti, G. Mazzuca

Abstract
We study properties of Hamiltonian integrable systems with random initial data by considering their Lax representation. Specifically, we investigate the spectral behaviour of the corresponding Lax matrices when the number N N of degrees of freedom of the system goes to infinity and the initial data is sampled according to a properly chosen Gibbs measure. We give an exact description of the limit density of states for the exponential Toda lattice and the Volterra lattice in terms of the Laguerre and antisymmetric Gaussian β \beta -ensemble in the high temperature regime. For generalizations of the Volterra lattice to short range interactions, called INB additive and multiplicative lattices, the focusing Ablowitz–Ladik lattice and the focusing Schur flow, we derive numerically the density of states. For all these systems, we obtain explicitly the density of states in the ground states.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some standard tools to study Hamiltonian integrable systems that we need throughout the manuscript . For further details, we refer to various textbooks and
monographs
[
10
,
9
,
11
,
54
]
.

A
Poisson manifold
is a pair
(\mathcal{P},\,\left\{.\,,.\right\})

where
\mathcal{P}
is a
n
-dimensional differentiable manifold and

\left\{.\,,.\right\}
is an antisymmetric bilinear operation on the space

{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})

of smooth functions over
\mathcal{P}
,

(8)

\begin{split}{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})\times&{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})\\
&(f,g)\longrightarrow{}\{f,g\}\end{split}

such that for all functions
f,g,h\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})
,
it satisfies:

1.

the
Jacobi identity

(9)

\{\{f,g\},h\}+\{\{h,f\},g\}+\{\{g,h\},f\}=0,

2.

the
Leibniz rule

(10)

\{hf,g\}=h\{f,g\}+\{h,g\}f.

The operator
\left\{.\,,.\right\}
is called a
Poisson bracket
.
When there is no risk of confusion, we simply denote a Poisson
manifold by
\mathcal{P}
, where the Poisson bracket is assumed to be fixed
and given.

In local coordinates
\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)

the Poisson bracket is specified by an antisymmetric
(2,0)

tensor
\pi^{ij}(\boldsymbol{a})
, the
Poisson tensor
,
acting on the coordinates as

(11)

\{a_{i},a_{j}\}=\pi^{ij}(\boldsymbol{a}),\qquad i,j=1,\dots n.

The Jacobi identity on the coordinates is equivalent to the relation

(12)

\frac{\partial\pi^{ij}(\boldsymbol{a})}{\partial a_{s}}\pi^{sk}(\boldsymbol{a})+\frac{\partial\pi^{ki}(\boldsymbol{a})}{\partial a_{s}}\pi^{sj}(\boldsymbol{a})+\frac{\partial\pi^{jk}(\boldsymbol{a})}{\partial a_{s}}\pi^{si}(\boldsymbol{a})=0,\quad 1\leq i<j<k\leq n,

where we are summing over repeated indices.
In an open subset of
\mathcal{P}
the Poisson tensor has a fixed even rank
2r\leq n
.
By antisymmetry, it follows that the Poisson tensor can be non-degenerate,
meaning that
\det\pi(\boldsymbol{a})\neq 0
,
only if the dimension
n
of the base space is even, namely
n=2N
.

Given a function
H(\mathbf{a})\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})
, it generates
a set of so-called
Hamilton’s equations
through the relation

(13)

\dot{a}_{j}=\{a_{j},H\}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi^{ij}(\mathbf{a})\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial a_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,n.

The function
H
itself is called a
Hamiltonian
.
The previous set of equations defines a continuum time flow from an initial condition

\mathbf{a}(0)\in\mathcal{P}
to its time evolution
t>0
, namely

\Phi_{t}\colon\mathbf{a}(0)\to\mathbf{a}(t)
.
A function
K=K(\boldsymbol{a})
is constant under evolution

\Phi_{t}
if and only if

(14)

\dot{K}=\{K,H\}=0.

In this case the quantity
K
is called a
first integral
or
a
constant of motion
.
The notion of Liouville integrability is strictly
related to the number of first integrals and the rank of the associated
Poisson tensor.

Definition 2.1

(Liouville integrability)
.

A Hamiltonian system (
13
) on a Poisson manifold
\mathcal{P}
of
rank
2r\leq n
is Liouville integrable if there are
k=n-r

first integrals
H_{1}
, …,
H_{k}
in involution

(15)

\{H_{i},H_{j}\}=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,k\,,

and functionally independent, namely

(16)

\rank\left(\frac{\partial H_{i}}{\partial a_{j}}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\dots,k\\
j=1,\dots,n\end{subarray}}=k\,,

in a dense subset of
\mathcal{P}
.

As discussed in the Introduction, random initial data are obtained from an invariant measure
\mathrm{d}\mu
of the form (
3
).
More precisely, this means that the measure of every subset
S\subset\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\mathrm{d}\mu

is preserved under the time-evolution
\Phi_{t}
,

(17)

\int_{\Phi_{t}(S)}\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{S}\mathrm{d}\mu.

Interpreting the evolution as a coordinate transformation, we have

(18)

\int_{\Phi_{t}(S)}\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{S}\Phi_{t}^{*}(\mathrm{d}\mu),

where
\Phi_{t}^{*}(\mathrm{d}\mu)
is the pull-back of
\mathrm{d}\mu
through
\Phi_{t}
.
This shows that the condition (
17
) is satisfied
if
\Phi_{t}^{*}(\mathrm{d}\mu)=\mathrm{d}\mu
.
In coordinates, for a measure written in the form (
3
), namely
\mathrm{d}\mu=m\left(\mathbf{a}\right)\mathrm{d}a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}a_{n},
the invariance of the measure with respect to the Hamiltonian flow
is expressed by the condition

(19)

\Div\left(m(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{f}_{H}(\boldsymbol{a})\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}}(m(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{f}_{H}(\mathbf{a}))_{i})=0,

where
\Div
is the usual euclidean divergence, see e.g.
[
36
, Chapter 1]
.
The vector field
\mathbf{f}_{H}
is specified by the Hamiltonian
H
via the relation

(\mathbf{f}_{H})_{i}=\{a_{i},H\}
.
The condition (
19
) can be written in the form

(20)

\{m,H\}+m\Div\left(\mathbf{f}_{H}\right)=0.

From formula (
20
) we immediately have two important
consequences.

•

If the Hamiltonian vector field is divergence free, like in the
case of a canonical Poisson bracket, it follows that the Euclidean measure

(21)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}=\mathrm{d}a_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}a_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}a_{n}

is an invariant measure.

•

If
K
is a first integral and
m
is the density of an invariant measure, then
from the Leibniz rule (
10
) it follows that

(22)

\widetilde{m}:=f(K)m

is the density of another invariant measure for every scalar
function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})
.

In all the examples of this manuscript, the Hamiltonian vector fields
are divergence free, so we will be allowed to consider the generalized
Gibbs ensemble described in the Introduction where
\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}
the Euclidean measure in (
21
).

3
Laguerre
\beta
-ensemble and the exponential Toda lattice

In this section, we introduce an integrable model that we call
exponential Toda lattice
, since it resembles the well-know Toda lattice. We construct the Lax pair for this system, and we define its generalized Gibbs measure. Finally, we compute the mean density of states of the Lax matrix.

The exponential Toda lattice is the Hamiltonian system on
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{R}^{2N}
with canonical Poisson bracket described by the Hamiltonian

(23)

H_{E}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{q}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-p_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}\,,\quad p_{j},q_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

We consider periodic boundary conditions

(24)

q_{j+N}=q_{j}+\Delta,\quad p_{j+N}=p_{j},\qquad\forall\,j\in\mathbb{Z},

and
\Delta\geq 0
is an arbitrary constant. The equations of motion are given in Hamiltonian form as

(25)

\begin{split}&\dot{q}_{j}=\frac{\partial H_{E}}{\partial p_{j}}=-e^{-p_{j}}\,,\\
&\dot{p}_{j}=-\frac{\partial H_{E}}{\partial q_{j}}=e^{q_{j-1}-q_{j}}-e^{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}\,.\end{split}

The system possesses two trivial constants of motion,

(26)

H_{0}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{q}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(q_{j}-q_{j+1}),\qquad H_{1}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{q}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j},

the first one due to periodicity, the second one due to the translational invariance of the Hamiltonian (
23
).
In order to obtain a Lax pair for this system we introduce, in the spirit of Flaschka and Manakov
[
27
,
26
,
46
]
, the variables

(27)

x_{j}=e^{-\frac{p_{j}}{2}},\qquad y_{j}=e^{\frac{q_{j}-q_{j+1}}{2}}=e^{-\frac{r_{j}}{2}},\;\;r_{j}=q_{j+1}-q_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,N,

where we notice that
\prod_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}=e^{-\frac{\Delta}{2}}
.
In these variables, the Hamiltonian (
23
) and the constants of motion (
26
) transform into

(28)

H_{E}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(x_{j}^{2}+y_{j}^{2}),\qquad H_{0}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log y_{j},\qquad H_{1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=-2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log x_{j}.

The Hamilton’s equations (
25
) become

(29)

\dot{x_{j}}=\frac{x_{j}}{2}\left(y_{j}^{2}-y_{j-1}^{2}\right),\qquad\dot{y_{j}}=\frac{y_{j}}{2}\left(x_{j+1}^{2}-x_{j}^{2}\right),\quad j=1,\dots,N,

where
x_{N+1}=x_{1},\,y_{0}=y_{N}
.

One can explicitly construct a Lax pair for this system. Let us introduce the matrix
E_{r,s}
, defined as
\left(E_{r,s}\right)_{ij}=\delta^{i}_{r}\delta^{j}_{s}
. Set

(30)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(x_{j}^{2}+y_{j-1}^{2})E_{j,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}y_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j}),

(31)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{x_{j}y_{j}}{2}(E_{j,j+1}-E_{j+1,j}),

where, accounting for periodic boundary conditions, indices are taken modulo
N
, so that
E_{i,j+N}=E_{i+N,j}=E_{i,j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. For example, the matrix
L
in (
30
) has the explicit form

(32)

L=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{2}+y_{N}^{2}&x_{1}y_{1}&&&x_{N}y_{N}\\
x_{1}y_{1}&x_{2}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}&x_{2}y_{2}&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&x_{N-1}y_{N-1}\\
x_{N}y_{N}&&&x_{N-1}y_{N-1}&x_{N}^{2}+y_{N-1}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The system of equations (
29
) then admits the Lax representation

(33)

\dot{L}=[A,L].

Hence, the quantities
H_{m}=\textrm{Tr}\left(L^{m-1}\right)
,
m=2,\ldots,N+1
are constants of motion as well as the eigenvalues of
L
.
For the exponential Toda lattice, we define the generalized Gibbs ensemble as

(34)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{ET}=\frac{1}{Z^{E}_{N}(\beta,\eta,\theta)}\exp\left(-\beta H_{E}+\theta H_{0}-\eta H_{1}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\,,

where
\beta,\eta,\theta>0
, the Hamiltonians
H_{E}
,
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are defined in (

) and (

is the normalization constant,
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=\mathrm{d}r_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}r_{N}
and analogously for

. We notice that according to this measure, all the variables are independent, moreover all

. After introducing the variables

\mathrm{d}\mu_{ET}=\frac{1}{Z^{H_{E}}_{N}(\beta,\eta,\theta)}\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2\eta-1}e^{-\beta x_{j}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{j}\prod_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}^{2\theta-1}e^{-\beta y_{j}^{2}}\mathrm{d}y_{j}\,.

Let

be the

f_{{2\alpha}}(r)=\frac{r^{2\alpha-1}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}}{2^{\alpha-1}\Gamma(\alpha)},\qquad r\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

and

The Lax matrix

in (

-ensemble

is the matrix transpose.
On the other hand, the Laguerre

-ensemble is given by the set of matrices

(38)

L_{\alpha,\gamma}=B_{\alpha,\gamma}(B_{\alpha,\gamma})^{\intercal},\quad B_{\alpha,\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
y_{1}&x_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&y_{N-1}&x_{N}\end{matrix}&\mbox{\Large 0}_{{\small N\times(M-N)}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\chi_{\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma}}\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\chi_{2\alpha}\quad n=1,\ldots,N-1.

Thus, the following entry wise measure on the matrices

\mathrm{d}\mu_{B_{\alpha,\gamma}}=\frac{1}{\left(2^{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}-1}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma})\right)^{N}\left(2^{\alpha-1}\Gamma(\alpha)\right)^{N-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{\frac{2\alpha}{\gamma}-1}e^{-\frac{x_{j}^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{j}\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}y_{j}^{2\alpha-1}e^{-\frac{y_{j}^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{d}y_{j}\,.

We are interested in studying the density of states

in (

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{j}}\xrightharpoonup{N\to\infty}\nu_{ET}\,,

where

In order to study the density of states of the exponential Toda lattice, we need the following result proved in

Theorem 3.1

(cf.
[
48
]
, Theorem 1.1)

Consider the matrix

and here

of the Lax matrix
L=BB^{\intercal}
takes the form

(45)

\nu_{ET}=\beta\partial_{\alpha}(\alpha\mu_{\alpha,\gamma}(\beta x))_{|_{\alpha=\theta,\gamma=\frac{\theta}{\eta}}}\mathrm{d}x,\quad x\geq 0\,,

where the density
\mu_{\alpha,\gamma}
is defined in (
44
).

Proof.

First, we notice that by virtue of general theory, see
[
12
, Theorem A.43]
, we can restrict to the case
y_{N}=0
in (
35
). As observed above, performing the change of variables
(x_{j},y_{j})\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta}}(x_{j},y_{j})
, which amounts to rescale
B\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta}}B
, one has that the matrix entries of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta}}B
are distributed as the matrix entries of
B_{\theta,\frac{\theta}{\eta}}
.
Applying Theorem
3.1
we obtain the claim.
∎

3.1
Parameter Limit

In this section, we examine the low-temperature limit of the Hamiltonian system (
23
). Namely, we want to compute the eigenvalues of the Lax matrix
L
in (
30
) in the limit
\beta,\theta,\eta\to\infty
, in such a way that

\eta=\widetilde{\eta}\beta,\quad\theta=\widetilde{\theta}\beta,

where
\widetilde{\eta}
and
\widetilde{\theta}
are in compact sets of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Since all
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
are independent random variables, we just have to consider the weak limit of the rescaled chi-distributions, respectively

f_{2\tilde{\eta}\beta}(\sqrt{2\beta}x)\sqrt{2\beta}\mathrm{d}x,\quad f_{2\tilde{\theta}\beta}(\sqrt{2\beta}y)\sqrt{2\beta}\mathrm{d}y\,.

We explicitly work out one of the cases above.

We consider a continuous and bounded function
h\;:\;\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
and evaluate the limit

(46)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)f_{2\eta\beta}(\sqrt{2\beta}x)\sqrt{2\beta}\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)e^{\beta(2\widetilde{\eta}\log x-x^{2})}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2\widetilde{\eta}\beta}e^{-\beta x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x}=h\left(\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\right)\,.

The last identity has been obtained by applying the Laplace method (see
[
50
]
) and observing that the minimizer of the term
2\widetilde{\eta}\log(x)-x^{2}
in the exponent
of the integral is
x_{0}=\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}
.

As a consequence, we conclude that
x_{j}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}
and
y_{j}\rightharpoonup\sqrt{\widetilde{\theta}}
,
j=1,\ldots,N
as
\beta\to\infty
,
where with
\rightharpoonup
we denote the weak convergence. The previous limit implies that the measure
\nu_{ET}
in (
45
) weakly converges, in the low temperature limit, to the density of states of the matrix
L_{\infty}

(47)

L_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\eta}+\widetilde{\theta}&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}&&&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}\\
\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}&\widetilde{\eta}+\widetilde{\theta}&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}\\
\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}&&&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}&\widetilde{\eta}+\widetilde{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Indeed, the fact that
L
is tridiagonal with iid entries along the diagonals, implies its
k
-th moment depends on a multiple of
k
number of variables only; specifically looking back at the Lax matrix
L
in (
32
)

(48)

\left\langle\textrm{Tr}\left(L^{k}\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(L^{k}\right)_{jj}\right\rangle=N\cdot\left\langle\left(L^{k}\right)_{11}\right\rangle=:N\cdot\left\langle f\left(x_{N-k},\dots,x_{N+k};y_{N-k},\dots,y_{N+k}\right)\right\rangle,

for some function
f(\cdot)
of its entries. Then, passing to the density of states (
42
) and renaming the iid variables, the scaling factor
N
identically cancels out and moments converge,

(49)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\textrm{Tr}\left(L^{k}\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle f\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k};y_{1},\dots,y_{2k}\right)\right\rangle.

The eigenvalues being functions of moments, density of states converges as well. In particular, this also shows that the two limit commute in taking the density of states at low temperature,

(50)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{j}}=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{j}}=\nu_{\infty}\,,

since the limits can be passed directly to the variables
x_{i},y_{i}
.

The matrix
L_{\infty}
is a circulant matrix, so its eigenvalues can be computed explicitly
[
33
]
as

(51)

\lambda_{j}=\widetilde{\eta}+\widetilde{\theta}+2\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}\widetilde{\theta}}\cos\left(2\pi\frac{j}{N}\right)\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,N\,.

From this explicit expression, it follows that the density of states of
L_{\infty}
is

(52)

\nu_{L_{\infty}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(c_{+}-x)(x-c_{-})}}\mathbbm{1}_{(c_{-},c_{+})},\quad c_{\pm}=(\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\pm\sqrt{\widetilde{\theta}})^{2}.

here
\mathbbm{1}_{(c_{-},c_{+})}
is the indicator function of the set
(c_{-},c_{+})
. In particular, this measure belongs to the class of Arcsin distributions.
Thus, we proved the following result.

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider the random Lax matrix
L
in (
30
) sampled from the Gibbs ensemble
\mathrm{d}\mu_{ET}
(
35
) of the exponential Toda lattice (
29
). The density of states of the matrix
L
in the low-temperature limit, i.e. when
\beta,\theta,\eta\to\infty
in such a way that
\eta=\widetilde{\eta}\beta,\,\theta=\widetilde{\theta}\beta
, with
\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\theta}
in compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, is the Arcsin distribution given by (
52
).

4
Volterra lattice

The
Volterra lattice
, also known as the
discrete KdV equation
, describes the motion of
N
particles on the line with equations

(53)

\dot{a_{j}}=a_{j}\left(a_{j+1}-a_{j-1}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

It was originally introduced by Volterra to study
population evolution in a hierarchical system of competing species. It was first
solved by Kac and van Moerbeke in
[
41
]
using a discrete version of inverse scattering due to Flaschka
[
26
]
. Equations (
53
) can be considered as a finite-dimensional approximation of the Korteweg–de Vries equation.

The phase space is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and we consider periodic boundary conditions
a_{j}=a_{j+N}
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. The Volterra lattice is a reduction of the
second flow of the Toda lattice

[
41
]
. Indeed, the latter is described by the dynamical system

(54)

\displaystyle\dot{a_{j}}

\displaystyle=a_{j}\left(b_{j+1}^{2}-b_{j}^{2}+a_{j+1}-a_{j-1}\right),

\displaystyle\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

(55)

\displaystyle\dot{b_{j}}

\displaystyle=a_{j}(b_{j+1}+b_{j})-a_{j-1}(b_{j}+b_{j-1}),

\displaystyle\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

and equations (
53
) are recovered just by setting
b_{j}\equiv 0
. The Hamiltonian structure of the equations follows from the one of the Toda lattice. On the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
we introduce the Poisson bracket

(56)

\{a_{j},a_{i}\}_{\textrm{Volt}}=a_{j}a_{i}(\delta_{i,j+1}-\delta_{i,j-1})\,

and the Hamiltonian
H_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\,
so that the equations of motion (
53
) can be written in the Hamiltonian form

(57)

\dot{a}_{j}=\{a_{j},H_{1}\}_{\textrm{Volt}}\,.

An elementary constant of motion for the system is
H_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
that is independent of
H_{1}
.

The Volterra lattice is a completely integrable system, and it admits several equivalent
Lax representations
, see e.g.
[
41
,
51
]
. The classical one reads

(58)

\dot{L}_{1}=\left[A_{1},L_{1}\right],

where

(59)

\begin{split}L_{1}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j+1}E_{j+1,j}+E_{j,j+1},\\
A_{1}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(a_{j}+a_{j+1})E_{j,j}+E_{j,j+2}\,,\end{split}

where we recall that the matrix
E_{r,s}
is defined as
\left(E_{r,s}\right)_{ij}=\delta^{i}_{r}\delta^{j}_{s}
and
E_{j+N,i}=E_{j,i+N}=E_{j,i}
.
There exists also a
symmetric
formulation due to Moser
[
51
]
,

(60)

\begin{split}\dot{L}_{2}&=\left[A_{2},L_{2}\right]\\
L_{2}&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{a}_{j}(E_{j,j+1}+E_{j+1,j})\,,\\
A_{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}(E_{j,j+2}-E_{j+2,j})\,,\end{split}

which assumes that all
a_{j}>0
.

Furthermore, we point out that there exists also an
antisymmetric
formulation for this Lax pair, indeed a straightforward computation yields

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
a_{j}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
. Then, the dynamical system (
53
) admits an antisymmetric Lax matrix
L_{3}
with companion matrix
A_{3}
, namely the equations of motion are equivalent to
\dot{L_{3}}=\left[A_{3},L_{3}\right]
with

(61)

\displaystyle L_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{a_{j}}(E_{j,j+1}-E_{j+1,j}),

(62)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{a_{j}a_{j+1}}(E_{j+2,j}-E_{j,j+2}).

4.1
Gibbs Ensemble

We introduce a Gibbs ensemble for the Volterra lattice (
53
) by observing
that its vector field
f_{j}=a_{j}\left(a_{j+1}-a_{j-1}\right)
is divergence free, due to the periodic boundary conditions. Therefore, an invariant measure can be obtained from
(
22
). We use
H_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}a_{j},
and
H_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
as constants of motion to construct the invariant measure

(63)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}(\mathbf{a})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,\eta)}e^{-\beta H_{1}+(\eta-1)\log H_{0}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a},\quad\beta,\eta>0,

where

(64)

Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,\eta)=\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\eta\right)}{\beta^{\eta}}\right)^{N}<\infty,

and
\Gamma\left(\eta\right)
is the Euler gamma function
[
19
, §5]
. We notice that according to this measure, all the variables are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).

Next we want to characterize the density of states of the antisymmetric Lax
L_{3}
of the Volterra lattice given in Proposition
4.1
.
Among the three Lax matrices of the Volterra lattice, the matrix
L_{3}
is particularly useful since it allows us to connect the Volterra lattice with a specific
\alpha
-ensemble, namely the antisymmetric Gaussian
\alpha
-ensemble.
The antisymmetric Gaussian
\alpha
-ensemble, see
[
28
]
, is the family of random
antisymmetric tridiagonal matrices

(65)

L_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}0&y_{1}\\
-y_{1}&0&y_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-y_{N-2}&0&y_{N-1}\\
&&&-y_{N-1}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
y_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables with density

(66)

f_{2\alpha}(y)=\frac{y^{2\alpha-1}e^{-y^{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,,

which is just a rescaled chi-distribution. Even though we use a different expression of the chi-distribution with respect to section
3
, we keep the same notation
f_{2\alpha}(y)
for the density that will be used only in this section.
This distribution induces a measure on the entries of the matrix
L_{\alpha}
, namely

(67)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{L_{\alpha}}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}y_{i}^{2\alpha-1}e^{-y_{i}^{2}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(y_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^{N-1}}.

In
[
28
]
the authors studied this matrix ensemble in connection with the antisymmetric Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble introduced by Dumitriu and Forrester
[
22
]

in the high temperature regime, and computed explicitly its density of states
\nu_{L_{\alpha}}(x)
, defined as

(68)

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\Im(\lambda_{j})}\xrightharpoonup{N\to\infty}\nu_{L_{\alpha}}(x)\,,

where
\lambda_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
L_{\alpha}
. Since the matrix
L_{\alpha}
is antisymmetric with real entries, its eigenvalues are purely imaginary numbers.

Theorem 4.2

(cf.
[
28
]
)
.

The density of states of the random matrix
L_{\alpha}
in (
65
), is explicitly given by

(69)

\nu_{L_{\alpha}}(x)=\partial_{\alpha}(\alpha\theta_{\alpha}(x))\mathrm{d}x\,,

where

(70)

\theta_{\alpha}(x)=\left|\Gamma(\alpha)W_{-\alpha+1/2,0}(-x)\right|^{-2}\,,

here
\Gamma(x)
is the gamma function and
W_{k,\mu}(z)
is the Whittaker function (see Appendix
B
) .

Remark 4.3
.

The proof of Theorem
4.2
has been obtained in
[
28
]
by comparing the antisymmetric Gaussian
\alpha
-ensemble (
65
) with the antisymmetric Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble at high temperature, which was considered in the same paper.

We notice that performing the change of coordinates
a_{j}=x_{j}^{2}
, the Gibbs ensemble (
63
) reads:

(71)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2\eta-1}e^{-\beta\sum_{j=1}^{N}x^{2}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x_{j})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{Z_{N}^{\textrm{Volt}}(\beta,\eta)}\,,

which, up to a rescaling
x_{j}\to x_{j}/\sqrt{\beta}
and for the extra term
x_{N}
in the probability distribution, is exactly the distribution (
67
) of the matrix
L_{\alpha}
. Furthermore, the matrix
L_{3}
is a 2 rank perturbation of the matrix
L_{\alpha}
. Therefore, by a corollary of
[
12
, Theorem A.41]
and Theorem
4.2
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 4.4
.

Consider the matrix
L_{3}
in (
62
) endowed with the Gibbs measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textrm{Volt}}
(
63
). Then, the density of states of the matrix
L_{3}
is explicitly given by

(72)

\nu_{\textrm{Volt}}(x)=\sqrt{\beta}\partial_{\eta}\left(\eta\theta_{\eta}(\sqrt{\beta}x)\right)\mathrm{d}x\,,

where
\theta_{\alpha}(x)
is given in (
70
).

4.2
Parameter Limit

As for the case of exponential Toda (
29
), in this section we consider the low-temperature regime of the Volterra lattice, namely the limit
\eta,\beta\to\infty
, in such a way that
\eta=\beta\widetilde{\eta}
, with
\widetilde{\eta}
in a compact set of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and we compute the density of states of the matrix
L_{3}
(
61
) in this regime.

Applying the same techniques of Section
3.1
, we conclude that the density of states of the matrix
L_{3}
in the low-temperature limit coincides with the one of the matrix
L_{\infty}
, where

(73)

L_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}&&&-\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\\
-\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}&0&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}&&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\\
\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}&&&-\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

Since the matrix
L_{\infty}
is circulant, we can readily compute its eigenvalues as

(74)

\lambda_{j}=2i\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}}\sin\left(2\pi\frac{j}{N}\right)\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,N\,.

From this explicit formula, it follows that the density of states of the matrix
L_{\infty}
reads

(75)

\nu_{L_{\infty}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\widetilde{\eta}-x^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{(-2\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}},2\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}})}(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

Such measure coincides with the measure
\nu_{L^{\alpha}}
in the low-temperature limit, and it belongs to the class of Arcsin distributions. Thus, we just proved the following.

Proposition 4.5
.

Consider the Gibbs ensemble
\mathrm{d}\mu_{Volt}
of the Volterra lattice (
63
), in the low-temperature limit, i.e.
\beta,\eta\to\infty
, in such a way that
\eta=\widetilde{\eta}\beta
, where

\widetilde{\eta}
is in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then, the density of states
\nu_{\textrm{Volt}}
of the Lax matrix
L_{3}
in (
61
) converges, in this regime, to

(76)

\nu_{\textrm{Volt}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\widetilde{\eta}-x^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{(-2\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}},2\sqrt{\widetilde{\eta}})}(x)\mathrm{d}x\,.

5
Generalization of the Volterra lattice: the INB
k
-lattices

The Volterra lattice (
53
) can be generalized in a variety of ways. The most natural ones are two families of lattices described in

[
17
]
(see also
[
16
,
40
,
52
]
) which include short range interactions.
The first family is called
additive
Itoh–Narita–Bogoyavleskii (INB)
k
-lattice
and is defined by the equations

(77)

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{i+j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{i-j}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,\;N\geq k\in N.

The second family is called the

multiplicative

Itoh–Narita–Bogoyavleskii (INB)
k
-lattice
and is defined by the equations

(78)

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}a_{i+j}-\prod_{j=1}^{k}a_{i-j}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,\;N\geq k\in N.

In both cases we consider the periodicity condition

a_{j+N}=a_{j}
holds.

Setting
k=1
, we recover from both lattices the Volterra one
(
53
). Further generalizations of the INB lattice were recently considered in

[
25
]
.

A crucial difference in the two models is that in the additive lattice (
77
)
the interaction is on arbitrary number of points, but the non-linearity is still
quadratic like the original Volterra lattice (
53
); on the other hand
, the multiplicative lattice (
78
)
admits non-linearity of
arbitrary order
.
Moreover, both families admit the KdV equation as continuum limits,
see
[
17
]
.

As mentioned earlier, the additive INB
k
-lattice is an integrable system for
all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, since they all admit a Lax pair formulation (
2
).
For the additive INB lattice (
77
), it reads

(79)

\displaystyle L^{(+,k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(a_{i+k}E_{i+k,i}+E_{i,i+1}\right),

(80)

\displaystyle A^{(+,k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{i+j}\right)E_{i,i}+E_{i,i+k+1}\,,

we recall that we are always considering periodic boundary conditions,
so for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{j+N}=a_{j}
and
E_{i,j+N}=E_{i+N,j}=E_{i,j}
.
The constants of motion obtained through this Lax pair are in involution with respect
to the Poisson bracket

(81)

\left\{a_{j},a_{i}\right\}_{(+,k)}=a_{j}a_{i}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{k}\delta_{j+s,i}-\sum_{s=1}^{k}\delta_{j-s,i}\right).

Then, the additive INB
k
-lattice (
77
) can be written as

(82)

\dot{a}_{i}=\left\{a_{i},H_{1}\right\}_{(+,k)},

where the Hamiltonian function
H_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
is the same
as in equation (
57
). In the same way, it is possible to
prove that the function
H_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
is a first
integral for the additive INB
k
-lattice (
77
) as well.

Similarly, the multiplicative INB
k
-lattices can be endowed with a Lax Pair for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, therefore it is another example of integrable systems.
Specifically, for the periodic case we presented in equation (
78
), the Lax pair
reads

(83)

\displaystyle L^{(\times,k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(a_{i}E_{i,i+1}+E_{i+k,i}\right),

(84)

\displaystyle A^{(\times,k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k}a_{i+j}\right)E_{i,i+k+1}\,.

We notice that both
H_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
, and
H_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}
are constants of motion for these systems, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 5.1
.

For fixed
k
, there exists a transformation that maps the multiplicative INB
k
-lattice to the additive one. Namely, consider the system (
78
) and define the new set of variables

(85)

b_{i}:=a_{i}\cdot\dots\cdot a_{i+k-1},\quad i=1,\dots,N,

where the indices are taken modulo
N
. Then, it is immediate to see that

(86)

\dot{a}_{i}=a_{i}\left(b_{i+1}-b_{i+k-1}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,

which in turn, due to telescopic summations, implies

\dot{b}_{i}=b_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{i+j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{i-j}\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,

which is (
77
). The transformation (
85
) is invertible only when
k
and
N
are co-prime, for a more detailed discussion see
[
17
]
.

Gibbs Ensemble

We want to introduce an invariant measure for the INB lattices
(
77
) and (
78
). Since

and

Unlike the Lax matrix of the Volterra lattice, the Lax matrices of these generalizations lack of a known random matrix model to compare with. For this reason, we present numerical investigations of the density of states for these random Lax matrices for several values of the parameters

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for
N
large enough

(88)

\textrm{Tr}\left((L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}\right)=\textrm{Tr}\left((L^{(\times,k)})^{\ell}\right)=0\,,

if
\ell
is not an integer multiple of
k+1
.

Proof.

We prove the statement for the additive case, the proof in the multiplicative one is analogous.

The main idea is to relate each addendum appearing in
\textrm{Tr}\left((L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}\right)
to a specific path in the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
plane, and prove that such a path exists if and only if
\ell=m(k+1)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, we can focus on the first element of the diagonal of
(L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}
, write
(L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}(1,1)
, since all the other ones can be recovered shifting the indices. First, we write
(L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}(1,1)
as

(89)

(L^{(+,k)})^{\ell}(1,1)=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}=1}^{N}L^{(+,k)}(1,i_{1})L^{(+,k)}(i_{1},i_{2})\cdots L^{(+,k)}(i_{\ell-1},1)\,.

We notice that, due to the structure of
L^{(+,k)}
, if
L^{(+,k)}(1,i_{1})\cdots L^{(+,k)}(i_{\ell-1},1)
is not zero, then either
i_{s+1}=i_{s}+1
or
i_{s+1}=i_{s}-k
modulo
N
. Now, consider paths in the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
plane from the point
(0,0)
to
(\ell,0)
, such that the only permitted steps are the up step
(1,1)
and the down step
(1,-k)
. Since these paths resemble the classical Dyck paths, we call them
(1,k)
-Dyck paths of length
\ell
. Given a non-zero element of the product in (
89
), we can construct the corresponding path in the following way.
We start at
(0,0)
, then if
|i_{1}-1|=1
we make an up step of height
1
, otherwise we make a down step of height
k
, and so on.

For each path, let
n
be the number of up steps and
m
the number of down steps, then

(90)

m+n=\ell\,,\quad n-mk=0\,,

since there is a total of
\ell
step, and the path has to go back to height
0
. Thus, we deduce that

(91)

m(k+1)=\ell\,,

and the claim is proven.
∎

5.2
Parameter limit

As in the previous cases, although we are not able to give an explicit formula for the density of states of the INB lattices for general
\beta,\eta
, we can characterize this measure in the low-temperature limit.
Specifically, we consider the limit as
\beta,\eta\to\infty
in such a way that
\eta=\widetilde{\eta}\beta
, with
\widetilde{\eta}
in a compact set of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and we compute the density of states of the matrices
L^{(+,k)},L^{(\times,k)}
, endowed with the probability inherited from
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textrm{INB}}
(
87
), in this limit.

The procedure is the same as in the case of Volterra (see Section
4.2
). Indeed, following the same line, we can conclude that the densities of states
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB},+,k}
and
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB},\times,k}
coincide with the densities of
\mathfrak{L}^{(+)}
and
\mathfrak{L}^{(\times)}
respectively, where

(94)

\mathfrak{L}^{(+,k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\widetilde{\eta}E_{i+k,i}+E_{i,i+1}\right),\quad\mathfrak{L}^{(\times,k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(E_{i+k,i}+\widetilde{\eta}E_{i,i+1}\right)\,.

We notice that both matrices are circulant, thus we can compute their eigenvalues explicitly as

(95)

\lambda_{j}^{(+,k)}=e^{-2\pi i\frac{j}{N}}+\widetilde{\eta}e^{2\pi i\frac{jk}{N}}\,,\quad\lambda_{j}^{(\times,k)}=\widetilde{\eta}e^{-2\pi i\frac{j}{N}}+e^{2\pi i\frac{jk}{N}}\,,

here
j=1,\ldots,N
. Thus, in the large
N
limit, we deduce that the support of the measures
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}+,k}
and
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}\times,k}
are the hypotrochoids

(96)

\gamma_{+,k}(t,\widetilde{\eta},1)=e^{-it}+\widetilde{\eta}e^{ikt}\,,\quad\gamma_{\times,k}(t,\widetilde{\eta},1)=\widetilde{\eta}e^{-it}+e^{ikt}\,,\quad t\in[0;2\pi)\,,

and the limiting eigenvalue densities are

(97)

\begin{split}&\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}+,k}=\dfrac{|dz|}{2\pi\sqrt{1+\tilde{\eta}^{2}k^{2}-k(|z|^{2}-1-\tilde{\eta}^{2})}},\quad z\in\gamma_{+,k}\,,\\
&\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}\times,k}=\dfrac{|dz|}{2\pi\sqrt{\tilde{\eta}^{2}+k^{2}-k(|z|^{2}-1-\tilde{\eta}^{2})}},\quad z\in\gamma_{\times,k}.\end{split}

We summarize these results in the following Proposition.

Proposition 5.4
.

The densities of states of the Lax matrices
L^{(+,k)}
and
L^{(\times,k)}
in (
79
) and (
83
) endowed with the Gibbs measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\textrm{INB}}
in (
87
), in the low temperature limit, i.e. when
\eta,\,\beta\to\infty
, in such a way that
\eta=\widetilde{\eta}\beta
, with
\widetilde{\eta}
in a compact set of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, are given respectively by
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}+,k}
and
\nu^{\infty}_{\textrm{INB}\times,k}
in (
97
).

When the parameters satisfy the relation

6
The focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice

The
focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice
is the following system of spatial discrete differential equations

(98)

i\dot{a}_{j}+a_{j+1}+a_{j-1}-2a_{j}+|a_{j}|^{2}(a_{j-1}+a_{j+1})=0\,,

where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{C},\,j=1,\ldots,N
,
N\geq 3
, and we consider periodic boundary conditions
a_{j+N}=a_{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This equation was introduced by Ablowitz and Ladik
[
1
,
2
]
, by searching integrable spatial discretization of the cubic non-linear Schrödinger Equation (NLS) for the complex function

\psi(x,t)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

(99)

i\partial_{t}\psi(x,t)+\partial^{2}_{x}\psi(x,t)+2\lvert\psi(x,t)\rvert^{2}\psi(x,t)=0\,,

In contrast with what happens in the defocusing case, the particles
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
are free to explore the whole
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, which is the phase space of the system.

On the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
we consider the Poisson bracket

[
24
,
29
]

(100)

\{f,g\}=i\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{j}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{a}_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial a_{j}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial a_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\overline{a}_{j}}\right)\,,\quad f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{N}).

We notice that the phase shift
a_{j}(t)\to e^{-2it}a_{j}(t)
transforms the AL lattice (
98
) into the equation

(101)

\dot{a}_{j}=i\,\rho_{j}^{2}(a_{j+1}+a_{j-1}),\quad\rho_{j}=\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}},

which we call the
reduced AL equation
.
We remark that the quantity
H_{0}=2\ln\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{j}^{2}\right)
is the generator of the shift
a_{j}(t)\to e^{-2it}a_{j}(t)
, while
H_{1}=-K^{(1)}-\overline{K^{(1)}}
with

(102)

K^{(1)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\overline{a}_{j+1},

generates the flow (
101
). Therefore, we can rewrite the AL equation as

(103)

\dot{a}_{j}=\{a_{j},H_{AL}\}\,,\quad H_{AL}=H_{0}+H_{1}\,.

Moreover, it is straightforward to verify that
\{H_{0},H_{1}\}=0
.
The Poisson bracket induces the symplectic form

(104)

\omega=i\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\rho_{j}^{2}}\mathrm{d}a_{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{a}_{j}\,,\quad\rho_{j}=\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}}\,,

that is invariant under the evolution generated by the Hamiltonians
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.
Therefore, the volume form

\omega^{N}=\omega\wedge\dots\wedge\omega,

is also invariant.
In view of these properties, we can define the Gibbs ensemble for the focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice on the phase space
\mathbb{C}^{N}
as

(105)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{AL}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{AL}(\beta)}e^{\frac{\beta}{2}H_{0}}\omega^{N}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{AL}(\beta)}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+|a_{j}|^{2}\right)^{-\beta-1}\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{a}\,,\quad\beta>0\,,

where
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
,
\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{a}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(i\mathrm{d}a_{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{a}_{j})
and
Z_{N}^{AL}(\beta)
is the normalization constant of the system. We notice that according to this measure, all the variables are i.i.d.

Remark 6.1
.

The measure with density
\exp(-\beta H_{AL})
and
\beta>0
is not bounded nor normalizable on the whole phase space. For this reason, we have defined the Gibbs ensemble as in (
105
).
Furthermore, we observe that the measure (
105
) has a finite number of moments, which implies that the corresponding density of states of the Lax matrix (see (
108
) below), if it exists, would have a finite number of moments.

The focusing AL lattice is a complete integrable system. Indeed it admits a Lax representation, first obtained by Ablowitz and Ladik from the discretization of the Zakharov-Shabat Lax pair for the focusing non-linear Schrödinger equation
[
63
]
.
Gesztesy, Holden, Michor, and Teschl
[
30
]
found a different Lax pair for the infinite case of focusing AL lattice, and for its general hierarchy.
To adapt their construction, we double the size of the lattice according to the periodic boundary conditions, thus we consider a chain of
2N
particles
a_{1},\ldots,a_{2N}
such that
a_{j}=a_{j+N}
for
j=1,\ldots,N
.
Define the
2\times 2
matrix
\Xi_{j}

(106)

\Xi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}-a_{j}&\rho_{j}\\
\rho_{j}&-\overline{a_{j}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad j=1,\dots,2N\,,

and the
2N\times 2N
matrices

(107)

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{a_{2N}}&&&&&\rho_{2N}\\
&\Xi_{2}\\
&&\Xi_{4}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&\Xi_{2N-2}\\
\rho_{2N}&&&&&-a_{2N}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\Xi_{1}\\
&\Xi_{3}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&\Xi_{2N-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Now let us define the Lax matrix

(108)

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{L}\mathcal{M}\,,

that has the structure of a
5
-band diagonal matrix

\begin{pmatrix}*&*&*&&&&&&&*\\
*&*&*&&&&&&&*\\
&*&*&*&*&&&&&\\
&*&*&*&*&&&&&\\
&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
&&&&&&*&*&*&*\\
&&&&&&*&*&*&*\\
*&&&&&&&*&*&*\\
*&&&&&&&*&*&*\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

The
N
-periodic equation (
101
) is equivalent to the following Lax equation for the matrix
\mathcal{E}
,

(109)

\dot{\mathcal{E}}=\left[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E}\right]\,,

where

(110)

\mathcal{A}=\frac{i}{2}(\mathcal{E}_{+}-\mathcal{E}_{-}-\mathcal{E}_{+}^{-1}+\mathcal{E}^{-1}_{-})\,,

where the two projections
M_{+},\,M_{-}
are defined for a
2N\times 2N
matrix as

(111)

M_{+}=\begin{cases}M_{\ell,j}\,,\quad\ell<j\leq\ell+N\\
M_{\ell,j}\,,\quad\ell>j+N\\
0\quad\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}\,,\quad M_{-}=\begin{cases}M_{\ell,j}\,,\quad j<\ell\leq j+N\\
M_{\ell,j}\,,\quad j>\ell+N\\
0\quad\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

We notice that the Lax matrix
\mathcal{E}
has a similar structure to the one of the defocusing AL lattice obtained by Nenciu, and Simon
[
53
,
55
]
. The crucial difference is that while for the defocusing AL lattice the blocks
\Xi_{j}
are unitary matrices, for the focusing lattice this is not the case since

\Xi_{j}\Xi_{j}^{\dagger}\neq I_{2}
where
I_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and
†
stands for hermitian conjugate.

The measure
\mathrm{d}\mu_{AL}
induces a probability distribution on the entries of the matrix
\mathcal{E}
, thus it becomes a random matrix. As in the previous cases, one would like to connect the density of states for this random matrix to the density of states of some
\beta
-ensemble in the high temperature regime, but, as in the case of the INB lattices, we lack of a matrix representation of some
\beta
-ensemble with eigenvalues supported on the plane.

We make the following observations. The matrix
\Xi_{j}
(
106
) is complex symmetric, and it can be factorized in the form

(112)

\Xi_{j}=U_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\overline{a_{j}}}{|a_{j}|\left(|a_{j}|+\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}}\right)}&0\\
0&-\frac{a_{j}}{|a_{j}|}(|a_{j}|+\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}})\end{pmatrix}U_{j}

where the matrices
U_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a_{j}}{\sqrt{2}|a_{j}|}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&-\frac{\overline{a_{j}}}{\sqrt{2}|a_{j}|}\end{pmatrix}
are unitary,
U_{j}^{-1}=U_{j}^{\dagger}
. Thus defining the matrices

(113)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\overline{a_{2N}}}{\sqrt{2}|a_{2N}|}&&&&&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
&U_{2}\\
&&U_{4}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&U_{2N-2}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&&&&&\frac{a_{2N}}{\sqrt{2}|a_{2N}|}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}\\
&U_{3}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&U_{2N-1}\end{pmatrix}\,,

we can rewrite the Lax matrix
\mathcal{E}
(
108
) as

(114)

\mathcal{E}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\Lambda_{\text{odd}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\Lambda_{\text{even}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\,,

where, defining
c_{j}:=\frac{\overline{a_{j}}}{|a_{j}|\left(|a_{j}|+\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}}\right)}
, the matrices
\Lambda_{\text{odd}}
and
\Lambda_{\text{even}}
are given by

(115)

\begin{split}&\Lambda_{\text{odd}}=\diag\left(c_{1},-\frac{1}{c_{1}},c_{3},-\frac{1}{c_{3}},\dots,c_{2N-1},-\frac{1}{c_{2N-1}}\right)\\
&\Lambda_{\text{even}}=\diag\left(-\frac{1}{c_{2N}},c_{2},-\frac{1}{c_{2}},\ldots,c_{2N}\right)\,.\end{split}

Since we are interested in the distribution of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{E}
, it follows from the factorization (
114
) that we can also consider the matrix
\Lambda_{\text{odd}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\Lambda_{\text{even}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\intercal}
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. The eigenvalues of
\Lambda_{\text{even}},\Lambda_{\text{odd}}
come in pairs, such that if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue, then also
-\lambda^{-1}
is an eigenvalue. The matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a periodic CMV matrix
[
18
]
, thus its eigenvalues are on the unit circle.

Thus, we are in a similar setting considered in
[
59
,
60
,
34
]
. Indeed in
[
59
]
the authors derived the eigenvalues distribution of
U\sqrt{D}
where
U
is a Haar distributed unitary matrix and
D
is a fixed diagonal matrix with positive eigenvalues. They show that the density of states is rotational invariant and it is supported on a single ring whose radii
r_{1}<r_{2}
satisfy the constraint
d_{min}<r_{1}<r_{2}<d_{max}
, where
d_{min}
and
d_{max}
are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
D
. In
[
34
]
, the authors considered a similar problem, namely the characterization of the density of states for a matrix of the form
UTV
, where
U,V
are independent unitary matrices Haar distributed, and
T
is a real diagonal matrix independent of
U,V
. They proved, under some mild conditions, that the density of states of the matrix
UTV
is radially symmetric and it is supported on a ring.

It is therefore reasonable to expect that the density of states of the random Lax matrix of the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice is rotational invariant, but with unbounded support, indeed the eigenvalues of
\Lambda_{\text{even}},\Lambda_{\text{odd}}
could grow to infinity.

. For this reason, we investigate the behaviour of the modulus of the eigenvalues, see Fig
6

6.1
, we expect that the tails should decay just polynomially fast.

6.1
Parameter Limit

Despite not being able to explicitly compute the density of states for general values of
\beta
, we can perform such an analysis in the low-temperature limit, namely when
\beta\to\infty
.

We notice that, according to (
105
), all the
a_{j}
are independent. Hence, in order to obtain the density of states in the low-temperature limit, we have to compute the weak limit of the density

(116)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\beta}=\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{-\beta-1}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{\mathbb{D}}(1+|z|^{2})^{-\beta-1}\mathrm{d}z}\,.

Proceeding as in the previous cases, it follows that the following limit holds for all bounded and continuous
f\;:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{R}
:

(117)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{D}}f(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\beta}=f(0)

The previous limit implies that the density of states of the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice in the low temperature limit is equal to the one of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{L}},\,\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
are
2N\times 2N
matrices

(118)

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&&&1\\
&\widetilde{\Xi}\\
&&\widetilde{\Xi}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&\widetilde{\Xi}\\
1&&&&&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\Xi}\\
&\widetilde{\Xi}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&\widetilde{\Xi}\end{pmatrix}\,,

and
\widehat{\Xi}
is defined as the unitary matrix

(119)

\widehat{\Xi}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

To compute the density of states for the matrix
\mathcal{E}
, we notice that both
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
are permutation matrices. Specifically, we identify them with the following permutations

(120)

\begin{split}&\widehat{\mathcal{M}}\longleftrightarrow(2N,1)(2,3)(4,5)\ldots(2N-2,2N-1)\,,\\
&\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\longleftrightarrow(1,2)(3,4)(5,6)\ldots(2N-1,2N)\,.\end{split}

As a consequence, the matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
itself corresponds to the permutation

(121)

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\longleftrightarrow(1,3,5,\ldots,2N-1)(2,4,6,\ldots,2N)\,.

This implies that the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
are all double, and given by

\lambda_{j}=e^{2\pi i\frac{j}{N}}\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,2N\,.

From this explicit expression of the eigenvalues, it is straightforward to prove that

(122)

\nu_{AL}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\,,\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi)\,.

Thus, we have proved the following

Proposition 6.2
.

Consider the Gibbs ensemble
\mathrm{d}\mu_{AL}
(
105
) of the focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice in the low-temperature limit, i.e.
\beta\to\infty
. Then, the density of states
\nu_{AL}
of the Lax matrix
\mathcal{E}
(
108
) is given by

(123)

\nu_{AL}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}\,,\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi)\,.

7
Schur flow

The
focusing Schur flow
, also known as
discrete mKdV
, is an integrable system deeply related to the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice. Its equations of motion are

(124)

\dot{a}_{j}=\rho_{j}^{2}(a_{j+1}-a_{j-1})\,,\quad\rho_{j}=\sqrt{1+|a_{j}|^{2}}\,,

where here we consider periodic boundary conditions,
a_{j}=a_{j+N}
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Notice that if
a_{j}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
, then
a_{j}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
for all times, implying that
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an invariant subspace for the dynamics.

Recalling the definition (
102
) for
K^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\overline{a}_{j+1}
and introducing the Poisson bracket

(125)

\{f,g\}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{j}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{a}_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial a_{j}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial a_{j}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\overline{a}_{j}}\right)\,,\quad f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{N}),

we can rewrite the equations of motion (
124
) as

(126)

\dot{a}_{j}=\{a_{j},H_{S}\}\,,\quad H_{S}=K^{(1)}-\overline{K^{(1)}}\,.

Notice that the quantity
H_{0}=-2\ln\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{j}^{2}\right)
is a global first integral for the system. Moreover, one can deduce immediately from the equations of motion that
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is invariant for the dynamics. Thus, in view of the Hamiltonian representation and this invariance, we define the Gibbs measure for the Schur flow as

(127)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{S}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{S}(\beta)}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+a_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\beta-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a}\,,\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\,,

where
Z_{N}^{S}(\beta)
is the normalization constant of the system,

(128)

Z_{N}^{S}(\beta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1+a_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\beta-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a}\,.

Remark 7.1
.

Similarly to the focusing AL case, the classical Gibbs ensemble is not well-defined on the whole phase space. Indeed, the measure with density
e^{-\beta H_{S}}
,
\beta>0
cannot be normalized on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

The Schur flow is a completely integrable system since it admits a Lax formulation. Namely, define the
2\times 2
matrix
\Xi_{j}

(129)

\Xi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}-a_{j}&\rho_{j}\\
\rho_{j}&-\overline{a_{j}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad j=1,\dots,2N\,,

and, similarly to the Ablowitz-Ladik case, the
2N\times 2N
matrices

(130)

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{a_{2N}}&&&&&\rho_{2N}\\
&\Xi_{2}\\
&&\Xi_{4}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&\Xi_{2N-2}\\
\rho_{2N}&&&&&-a_{2N}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\Xi_{1}\\
&\Xi_{3}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&\Xi_{2N-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Then, the
N
-periodic equation (
124
) is equivalent to the following Lax equation for the matrix
\mathcal{E}
:

(131)

\dot{\mathcal{E}}=\left[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E}\right]\,,

where

(132)

\mathcal{A}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{+}+\mathcal{E}_{+}^{-1}-\mathcal{E}_{-}-\mathcal{E}_{-}^{-1}\right)\,,

where the two projection
+,-
are defined in (
111
).

Carrying on with the approach of this article, we study the density of states
\nu_{S}
for the matrix
\mathcal{E}
when the entries are distributed according to the measure (
127
). First, we notice that Remark
6.1
is valid also in the case of the focusing Schur flow. Moreover, since the variables
a_{j}
are real, we can factorize the matrices
\Xi_{j}
in the following way:

(133)

\Xi_{j}=U_{0}\diag\left(c_{j},-\frac{1}{c_{j}}\right)U_{0},\,\quad c_{j}=\sqrt{1+a_{j}^{2}}-a_{j},\,\quad U_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

where we note that
U_{0}^{-1}=U_{0}
,
so that the above is a similarity transformation.
Thus, defining

(134)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&&&&&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
&U_{0}\\
&&U_{0}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&U_{0}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&&&&&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}U_{0}\\
&U_{0}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&U_{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,

we can rewrite the Lax matrix of the Schur flow
\mathcal{E}
as

(135)

\mathcal{E}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\Lambda_{\text{odd}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\Lambda_{\text{even}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\,,

where

(136)

\begin{split}&\Lambda_{\text{odd}}=\diag\left(c_{1},-\frac{1}{c_{1}},c_{3},\ldots\right)\,,\\
&\Lambda_{\text{even}}=\diag\left(-\frac{1}{c_{2N}},c_{2},\ldots,c_{2N}\right)\,.\end{split}

As in the case of the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice, since we are interested in just the distribution of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{E}
, we can consider the matrix
\Lambda_{\text{odd}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\Lambda_{\text{even}}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\intercal}
, where

(137)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}.

As in the AL case, the eigenvalues of
\Lambda_{\text{even}},\Lambda_{\text{odd}}
come in pairs, such that if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue, then also
-\lambda^{-1}
is an eigenvalue. The main difference with the case of the focusing AL lattice is that in this case the matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is
deterministic
. Thus, one can be led to think that the eigenvalue distribution of the Schur flow would be similar to the one of the AL lattice, but it is not the case.
Indeed, we perform several numerical investigations, reported in Fig
7
, which shows that the behaviour of the eigenvalues is different in the two situations.

We notice that a consistent part of the eigenvalues tend to stay close to the real axis, see Fig

[
23
]
. The main reason is that the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
are not evenly spaced on the unit circle, but they are constrained to the left semicircle, and are more dense nearby
\pm i
(see Fig
8
). Indeed we can give an accurate description of the spectrum of this matrix.

More precisely, we have the following.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
be the
2N\times 2N
matrix defined in (
137
).
Its eigenvalues are the solutions of the quadratic equations

(138)

\lambda+\frac{1}{\lambda}+1=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right),\quad\quad j=0,1,\dots,N-1,

counting the multiplicity.

Proof.

.

Remark 7.3
.

From equation (

) we can infer the limiting distributions of the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
. We already know all of its eigenvalues lie in the unit circle, hence we can write
\lambda=e^{i\varphi}
for some
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
. Equation (

) thus becomes

(139)

e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}+1=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)\quad\iff\quad\varphi=\arccos\left(\frac{1}{2}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Passing to the limit
N\to\infty
, by standard methods, we can compute the limiting density of the argument
\varphi
of the eigenvalues as

(140)

\mu(\varphi)\mathrm{d}\varphi=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi]}(\varphi)-\mathbbm{1}_{[-\pi,-\frac{\pi}{2}]}(\varphi)\right)\frac{\sin{\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\pi\sqrt{1-\left(1+2\cos{\varphi}\right)^{2}}}

This behaviour is shown in Fig

Furthermore, as in the case of the AL lattice, for large
\beta

\mathcal{E}
in (

in (

\beta\to\infty

8
Conclusions and outlook

In this manuscript, we underlined the deep relation between integrable systems and random matrix theory. Specifically, we showed that the exponential Toda lattice, and the Volterra one are related to the Laguerre
\beta
-ensemble at high temperature and the antisymmetric Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble at high temperature respectively. As we already mentioned in the introduction, these are not isolated and lucky relations, indeed the Toda lattice is related to the Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble at high temperature, and the Ablowitz-Ladik one to the Circular
\beta
ensemble. Thus, in view of the new relations that we obtained, we conclude that each classical
\beta
-ensemble in the high temperature regime is related to some integrable model, see Table
1
.

Furthermore, we numerically investigate the additive and multiplicative INB lattices, which are a generalization of the Volterra one. Since the former lattice is related to the antisymmetric Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble, it may be possible that the INB lattice could be related to some generalization of the antisymmetric Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble themselves.

Finally, we considered the focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice and the focusing Schur flow. In view of our numerical investigations, we were able to formulate a precise conjecture regarding the eigenvalues distribution of the focusing Ablowitz-Ladik lattice. Considering the focusing Schur flow, we were not able to formulate a precise conjecture for its eigenvalues distribution. The expectation is that it would be related to the a possible Ginibre
\beta
-ensemble.

It would be fascinating to consider the generalized Gibbs ensemble for the exponential Toda lattice and the Volterra one, and prove that both their empirical measures satisfy a Large Deviation principle in the spirit of
[
35
,
49
]
. Moreover, it would be interesting to apply the theory of Generalized Hydrodynamic
[
13
]
to have some insight regarding the behaviour of the correlation functions for these systems.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
7.2

Recall that the matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is defined as
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
are as in (
134
). It is a block circulant matrix, indeed we can write

(141)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}E_{0}&E_{1}&&&E_{-1}\\
E_{-1}&E_{0}&E_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&E_{1}\\
E_{1}&&&E_{-1}&E_{0}\end{pmatrix},

with

(142)

E_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

One can immediately check that
\lambda=\pm i
are eigenvalues for
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
with eigenvectors

(143)

v_{\pm i}=\left(\mp i,1,\mp i,1,\dots,\mp i,1\right)^{\intercal}.

We now claim that, for fixed
N
, the remaining eigenvalues have multiplicity 2 and are the
(N-1)
solutions to
\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{N}=I_{2}
, where we defined

(144)

\Omega\left(\lambda\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&-\lambda-1\\
-\lambda-1&\frac{\lambda^{2}+2\lambda+2}{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}.

Such solutions are obtained by solving the equation

(145)

\lambda+\frac{1}{\lambda}+1=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)\qquad for\;\ j=0,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor\,.

For
j=0
the solutions to (
145
) are
\pm i
which we already treated separately. Indeed
\Omega\left(\pm i\right)
is not diagonalizable and
\Omega\left(\pm i\right)^{N}\neq I_{2}
for every
N
greater than 0. For any other
j\in\{1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor\}
, the solutions to (
145
) are

(146)

\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)-1}{2}\pm i\frac{\sqrt{3+2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)-\cos^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)}}{2}.

Since both the real and imaginary part are monotone functions of
j
, different
j^{\prime}s
will correspond to different solutions. Hence, if
N
is odd, we will have a total of
N-1
solutions coming from (
146
); if
N
is even one has
N-2
distinct solutions
coming from the equation in (
145
) plus the double solution
\lambda=-1
obtained for
j=N/2
.

For a given eigenvalue
\lambda
, the corresponding independent eigenvectors are

(147)

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\left((1,0)\Omega\left(\lambda\right),\dots,\,(1,0)\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{N-1},1,0\right)^{\intercal},

(148)

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=\left((0,1)\Omega\left(\lambda\right),\dots,\,(0,1)\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{N-1},0,1\right)^{\intercal}\,.

Let us check the correctness of the claim. Write
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}v_{1}:=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})^{\intercal}
, where
w_{j}
are two-dimensional row vectors,
then using the fact that
\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{N}=I_{2}
, one can compute for any
k=1,\dots,N
,

(149)

\displaystyle w_{k}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{-1}\,\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{-1}+E_{0}+E_{1}\,\Omega\left(\lambda\right)\right)\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(150)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\lambda+1&\lambda\\
\lambda+1&\lambda\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
-1&-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&-\lambda-1\\
-\lambda&\lambda+1\end{pmatrix}\right)\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(151)

\displaystyle=\lambda\cdot\Omega\left(\lambda\right)^{k}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

which shows that
v_{1}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
. The same proof clearly applies to the other eigenvector
v_{2}
.

Appendix B
Confluent Hypergeometric Functions

B.1
Tricomi Confluent Hypergeometric function

The Tricomi Confluent Hypergeometric function
\psi(a,b;z)
, that we introduced in Theorem
3.1
, is defined as the solution of the Kummer’s equation

(152)

z\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+(b-z)\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}z}-a\psi=0\,,

such that

(153)

\psi(a,b;z)\sim z^{-a}\,,\quad z\to\infty\,,|\arg(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\delta\,,

here
\delta
is a small positive constant. The origin is a regular singular point for this equation with indexes
0
and
1-b
, moreover, this equation has an irregular singularity at infinity of rank one. Usually,
\psi(a,b;z)
has a branch point at
0
, whose principal branch is the same as
z^{-a}
, thus it has a cut on the
z
plane along the interval
(-\infty,0]
.

B.2
Whittaker function

The Whittaker function
W_{\mu,\kappa}(z)
, that we introduced in Theorem
4.2
, is defined as the solution to the Whittaker’s equation

(154)

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}W}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}+\left(-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\kappa}{z}+\frac{1-4\mu^{2}}{4z^{2}}\right)W=0\,,

such that

(155)

W_{\mu,\kappa}(z)\sim e^{-\frac{z}{2}}z^{\kappa}\,,\quad z\to\infty\,,|\arg(z)|\leq\frac{3}{2}\pi-\delta\,,

here
\delta
is a small positive constant. The Whittaker’s equation is obtained from Kummer’s equation (
152
) via the substitution
W=e^{-\frac{z}{2}}z^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\psi(a,b;z)
, with
\kappa=\frac{1}{2}(b-a)
, and
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)
. It has a regular singularity at
0
with index
\frac{1}{2}\pm\mu
, and an irregular singularity at infinity of rank
1
. Moreover, the following equality holds

(156)

W_{\mu,\kappa}(z)=e^{-\frac{z}{2}}z^{\frac{1}{2}+\mu}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mu-\kappa,1+2\mu;z\right)\,.

For a more general overview on Confluent Hypergeometric functions, we refer to
[
19
, §13]
, and
[
4
, Chapter 13]
.
