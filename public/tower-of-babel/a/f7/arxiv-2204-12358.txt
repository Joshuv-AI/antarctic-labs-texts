Polylogarithmic Sketches for Clustering
Moses Charikar, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
Given n n points in ℓ p d \ell_{p}^{d} , we consider the problem of partitioning points into k k clusters with associated centers. The cost of a clustering is the sum of p th p^{\text{th}} powers of distances of points to their cluster centers. For p ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] p\in[1,2] , we design sketches of size poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ​ d ) , k , 1 / ϵ ) \mathrm{poly}(\log(nd),k,1/\epsilon) such that the cost of the optimal clustering can be estimated to within factor 1 + ϵ 1+\epsilon , despite the fact that the compressed representation does not contain enough information to recover the cluster centers or the partition into clusters. This leads to a streaming algorithm for estimating the clustering cost with space poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ​ d ) , k , 1 / ϵ ) \mathrm{poly}(\log(nd),k,1/\epsilon) . We also obtain a distributed memory algorithm, where the n n points are arbitrarily partitioned amongst m m machines, each of which sends information to a central party who then computes an approximation of the clustering cost. Prior to this work, no such streaming or distributed-memory algorithm was known with sublinear dependence on d d for p ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) p\in[1,2) .

Introduction

Given a large number of high-dimensional points, is it possible to compress the raw representation into a very compact sketch so that we can understand how clusterable the data is from just this highly compressed representation?
Given
n
points in
d

k
clusters from a compressed representation whose size is polylogarithmic in both
n
and
d
.

For
n,d\in\mathbbm{N}
, let
P=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be any set of points with polynomially bounded entries (i.e., all coordinates may be represented with
O(\log(nd))
bits). The
(k,z)
-clustering problem in
\ell_{p}^{d}

k

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\min_{c_{\ell}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\sum_{x\in C_{\ell}}\|x-c_{\ell}\|_{p}^{z}.

(1)

The problem is a generalization of the

(p=2)

We note that the raw representation of the dataset uses

, despite the fact that we do not have enough information to compute the clusters nor the cluster centers which achieve such cost.

In addition to constructing coresets, an algorithm may seek to optimize the dependence on

1.2
Technical Overview

We give an overview of Theorem
3
. Once that is established, combining the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median sketch with coresets, thereby establishing Theorems
1
and
2
is (relatively) straight-forward (for more details, see Section
3
). Recall that for
p\in[1,2]
, we will process
n
points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
, and aim to return an approximation to the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median cost:

\displaystyle\min_{c\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-c\|_{p}^{p}.

(2)

It will be useful to assume that the points are centered, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=0\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
(we can enforce this because our sketches will be linear). The approach will come from the fact that the above optimization problem decomposes into a sum of
d
independent optimizations, one for each coordinate, and (
2
) seeks to evaluate the sum. Specifically, we may write

\displaystyle\min_{c\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-c\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\min_{c_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{ij}-c_{j}|^{p}.

Furthermore, for any fixed
j\in[d]
, estimating

\displaystyle\min_{c_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{ij}-c_{j}|^{p}

(3)

is much more amenable to
\ell_{p}
-sketching. Specifically, we let
x_{\cdot,j}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
be the vector containing the
j
-th coordinates of all
n
points, and
{\bf 1}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
be the all-1’s vector. Then, the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{ij}-c_{j}|^{p}=\|x_{\cdot,j}-c_{j}{\bf 1}\|_{p}^{p}
, and since the
\ell_{p}
-sketches are linear, an algorithm may maintain
\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,j})\in\mathbbm{R}^{t}
(for
t=\mathrm{poly}(\log(nd))
) and after processing, could iterate through various values of
c_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}
to evaluate

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{ij}-c_{j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\|x_{\cdot,j}-c_{j}{\bf 1}\|_{p}\approx_{1\pm\epsilon}\text{ estimate of }(\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,j}),\mathbf{sk}(c_{j}{\bf 1})),

and output the smallest value of
c_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}
found. In order to guarantee an
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximation of (
3
), only
\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon)
values of
c_{j}
need to be tried (since first evaluating
c_{j}=0
will specify the range where the optimal
c_{j}
may lie). A simple union bound implies that for any fixed
j\in[d]
, we can prepare a small sketch
\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,j})
from which we can approximate (
3
).

In summary, we want to estimate the sum of
d
minimization problems. Even though each of the
d
problems could be solved independently with a linear sketch, we do not want to process
d
linear sketches (as this increases space). In addition, we do not know which of the
d
minimizations will significantly affect the sum; hence, if we only (uniformly) sampled few
\boldsymbol{j}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{j}_{t}\sim[d]
and only processed
t\ll d
sketches along the sampled dimensions, the variance of the estimator may be too large, making it completely useless. The technique we will use was recently developed in
[
CJLW22
]
, building on
[
AKO10
,
JW18
]
, under the name “
\ell_{p}
-sampling with meta-data.” In this paper, we further develop the ideas, and apply them to sketches for clustering in a simple and modular way. We refer the reader to Remark
4
(following this technical overview), where we expand on the comparison to
[
CJLW22
]
.

The goal is to approximate the sum of the
d
minimization problems by
importance sampling
(see Chapter 9 of
[
Owe13
]
). While importance sampling is a well-known technique, it’s use in (one-pass) linear sketching algorithms is counter-intuitive, and we are not aware of any linear sketches which use importance sampling in the literature, expect for this and the recent work of
[
CJLW22
,
CJK+22
]
. Importance sampling will aim to estimate (
2
) by sampling with respect to an alternate distribution
\mathcal{D}
. In particular, (
2
) may be re-written as

\displaystyle d\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{j}\sim[d]}\left[\min_{c_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i\boldsymbol{j}}-c_{\boldsymbol{j}}|^{p}\right]=d\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\right]\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbf{Y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\min_{c_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i\boldsymbol{j}}-c_{\boldsymbol{j}}|^{p}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[\boldsymbol{j}]},

(4)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution chosen so the variance of the random variable
\mathbf{Y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
for
\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}
is bounded. Once the variance of the random variable is bounded, only a few samples are needed to estimate its expectation in (
4
). In general, the alternate distribution
\mathcal{D}
depends on the data in order to decrease the variance; for instance, coordinates
j\in[d]
whose value of (
3
) is higher should be sampled more often. Hence, importance sampling inherently interacts with the data in a two-stage process: 1) first, it samples
\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}
(where the distribution is data-dependent), and 2) second, it evaluates
\mathbf{Y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
by using (
3
) and
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{D}}[\boldsymbol{j}]
for the value
\boldsymbol{j}\in[d]
specified in the first step.

In a two-pass algorithm, the two steps may be implemented sequentially. A
sampling

[
JW18
]
, is used to sample
\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}
in the first pass. In the second pass, the algorithm knows the value of the sampled
\boldsymbol{j}
, so it maintains a sketch
\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,\boldsymbol{j}})
of size
t

\mathbf{sk}^{\prime}(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathcal{D}}[\boldsymbol{j}])

(to estimate
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[\boldsymbol{j}]
) from which it can evaluate the random variable
\mathbf{Y}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
. The counter-intuitive aspect is that, in this case, we will perform both steps in one-pass:

•

We will use an
\ell_{p}
-sampling sketch of
[
JW18
]
to sample from an importance sampling distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and

•

Concurrently, we prepare

d

\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,j})
to evaluate (
3
), one for each
j\in[d]
, and
d

\mathbf{sk}^{\prime}(\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[j])
to evaluate
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[j]
, one for each
j\in[d]
. The non-trivial part is to
sketch the sketches
. by compressing the

O(\mathrm{polylog}(nd))
-bit Count-Min data structure
[
CM05
]
.

The guarantee will be that the
\ell_{p}
-sampling sketch of
[
JW18
]
generates a sample

\widehat{\mathbf{sk}_{1}}\approx\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,\boldsymbol{j}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{sk}_{2}}\approx\mathbf{sk}^{\prime}(\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[\boldsymbol{j}]).

Furthermore, the sketch evaluation algorithm, which executes on the approximation

\widehat{\mathbf{sk}_{2}}

While the above plan provides a general recipe for importance sampling, the idea of “sketching the sketches” may not be applied in a black-box manner. First, the alternate distribution
\mathcal{D}
should admit a sampling sketch. Second, the sketch evaluation algorithm for
\mathbf{sk}(x_{\cdot,j})
and
\mathbf{sk}^{\prime}(\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathcal{D}}[j])
should be robust to the errors introduced by the Count-Min compression. Bounding the errors introduced by the Count-Min data structure, and ensuring that the approximate sketches
\widehat{\mathbf{sk}_{1}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{sk}_{2}}
constitutes the bulk of the technical work. Specifically for us, the plan is executed as follows: when
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=0\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
, every
j
satisfies (see Appendix
A

Statement of Main Lemma

Theorem 5

Fix

, as well as

with probability at least

We work with the following representation of a linear sketch. The processed set of
n

\mathbbm{R}^{d}
are stacked to form a vector

. A linear sketch using space

\mathcal{M}
supported on
s\times(nd)

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
, with probability
1-\delta
over the draw of
\mathbf{S}\sim\mathcal{M}
, an algorithm with access to the vector
\mathbf{S}x\in\mathbbm{R}^{s}
and
\mathbf{S}
can output

Linear sketches of the above form imply efficient streaming algorithms, albeit with some subtleties. It is useful to first assume that the streaming algorithm can store its randomness for free (we will address this in Subsection

) so that it knows the matrix
\mathbf{S}
. In particular, since
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbbm{R}^{s\times nd}
acts on the vector

which vertically stacks

, the columns of

may be broken up into

\mathbf{S}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{S}_{1}&\mathbf{S}_{2}&\dots&\mathbf{S}_{n}\end{array}\right],\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{S}x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{i}x_{i}.

In the

model, an algorithm would process the points one-at-a-time, and at time-step
j

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathbf{S}_{i}x_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}^{s}
. In the

model of streaming, there is a subtlety in the implementation; namely, as the algorithm receives insertions and deletions of points in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
, it must know which index
i\in[n]
it is considering. The reason is that the algorithm should know which of the sub-matrix
\mathbf{S}_{i}
to update the point with.

For our application of the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median sketch to
(k,p)
-clustering in
\ell_{p}
, we consider a weighted
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median. Namely, for points

and weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1

\ell_{p}^{p}
-median cost with respect to weights

\min_{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\|y-x_{i}\|_{p}^{p}.

It is useful to first consider of
\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{n}=1/n
. For general weights, the sketch will receive as input
\mathbf{S}=[\mathbf{S}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{S}_{n}]\in\mathbbm{R}^{s\times(nd)}
, the vector
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{1/p}\mathbf{S}_{i}x_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}^{s}
, and the weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
.

Centering Points

There is a straight-forward way to process the points so as to assume they are centered. Specifically, the average point may be subtracted from every point by applying a linear map, and since our sketch is linear, subtracting the average point may be incorporated into the sketch. For weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in[0,1]
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
, we consider the linear map

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mathop{\mapsto}\left(x_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i},\dots,x_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\right)\in\mathbbm{R}^{nd}.

Hence, we assume, without loss of generality, that the points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}=0\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}.

(8)

The centering is useful for deriving the following set of inequalities, which will be useful for our sketching procedures. Suppose we denote
y\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
as the point which minimizes
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\|y-x_{i}\|_{p}^{p}
. Then, for every
j\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|y_{j}-x_{ij}|^{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|x_{ij}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|y_{j}-x_{ij}|^{p}.

Importantly for us, every
j\in[d]
satisfies

\displaystyle 2^{-p}\leq\dfrac{\min_{y_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|y_{j}-x_{ij}|^{p}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|x_{ij}|^{p}}\leq 1.

(9)

We let
\mathcal{D}
be the distribution supported on
[d]
given by setting, for each
j\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\boldsymbol{j}=j\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|x_{ij}|^{p}\qquad\text{where}\qquad Z=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|x_{ij^{\prime}}|^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\|x_{i}\|_{p}^{p}.

Then, the quantity we want to estimate may be equivalently re-written as:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\min_{y_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|y_{j}-x_{ij}|^{p}

\displaystyle=Z\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{\min_{y_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbbm{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|y_{\boldsymbol{j}}-x_{i\boldsymbol{j}}|^{p}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}|x_{i\boldsymbol{j}}|^{p}}\right],

(10)

where the value within the expectation is bounded between
2^{-p}
and
1
. Furthermore, the quantity
Z
will be sketched with an
\ell_{p}
-sketch, and a sample
\boldsymbol{j}\sim\mathcal{D}
will be drawn with an
\ell_{p}
-sampling sketch. Hence, the plan is to produce

samples of
\boldsymbol{j}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{j}_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}
, and produce a sketch to evaluate the numerator inside the expectation, and the denominator inside the expectation. Taking an empirical average of the samples to estimate the expectation, and multiplying it by the estimate of

2.5
Storing the randomness

The previous section described the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median sketch assuming infinite precision on the random variables, as well as complete access to the randomness used. Here, we remark on how to: 1) utilize bounded precision on the entries of
\mathbf{S}
(to avoid storing infinite-precision real numbers), and 2) utilize Nisan’s pseudorandom generator in order to maintain
\mathbf{S}
implicitly. This technique, first used in
[
Ind06
]
for the
\ell_{p}
-sketch, is by now standard in the streaming literature.

Bounded Precision

We assume that the input vectors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
and the weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
have polynomially bounded entries (i.e., every number lies
[-\mathrm{poly}(nd),\mathrm{poly}(nd)]
and is specified by
O(\log(nd))
bits). The only infinite-precision numbers are the
p
-stable random variables and the exponential random variables, so we will discretize them so they require only
O(\log(nd))
random bits to generate them. Similarly to Claim 3 of
[
Ind06
]
our algorithm’s output, as a function of the
p
-stable and exponential random variables, always considers polynomially bounded entries and has bounded derivatives in all but a
1/\mathrm{poly}(nd)
-fraction of the probability space. With probability
1/\mathrm{poly}(nd)
, the
p
-stable random variables may become too large or exponentials too small. Furthermore, for any fixed setting of the
p
-stable random variables, a discontinuity in the algorithm’s output occurs when the exponential random variables cause
\boldsymbol{k}
may suddenly switch, when the maximum of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
in (
18
) is not unique. When we discretize the exponential distribution, the probability this occurs is at most
1/\mathrm{poly}(nd)
.

Using Nisan’s Pseudorandom Generator

Instead of utilizing
snd\cdot O(\log(nd))
random bits to generate the matrix
\mathbf{S}
, we show that we may utilize Nisan’s pseudorandom generator. This technique is standard when the sketch is linear: we may re-order the elements of the stream so as to update the algorithm with a finite-state machine which reads the random bits in
O(\log(nd))
-sized chunks. The minor subtlety is that algorithm of Lemma
2.1
requires access to
\mathbf{S}
in order to produce its output
(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, and hence refers back to previous random bits.

There is a simple fix: for each fixed setting of the input
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in(\{0,1\}^{O(\log(nd))})^{nd}
and weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in(\{0,1\}^{O(\log(nd))})^{n}
, and
j^{\prime}\in[d]
and
y\in\mathbbm{R}
specified by
O(\log(nd))
bits, there exists a low-space finite state machine which reads the randomness in
O(\log(nd))
chunks and checks whether
\boldsymbol{j}=j^{\prime}
, and if so, checks that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
satisfies the guarantees of Lemma
2.1
, and accurately estimates
\boldsymbol{\beta}
if
z=y
. The finite state machine receives its input in the order of the coordinates; this way, it can temporarily store
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
to compute
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
and find (
18
). Furthermore, while processing coordinate
j^{\prime}
, it checks the cost of using
y
as a center. Since Nisan’s pseudorandom generator fools every such finite state machine, and we try at most
O(d)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log d,1/\epsilon)
possible coordinates
j^{\prime}
and centers
y
, a union bound will show that the output of the algorithm under true random bits and under Nisan’s pseudorandom generator differs by at most
1/\mathrm{poly}(nd/\epsilon)
in total variation distance.

Here, we show that, for

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\left|x_{ij}-\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}x_{i^{\prime}j}\right|^{p}\quad\text{($x_{1j},\dots,x_{nj}$ are centered)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}|x_{ij}-x_{i^{\prime}j}|^{p},\quad\text{(Jensen's Inequality)}

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}|x_{ij}-x_{i^{\prime}j}|^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}|x_{ij}-y_{j}+y_{j}-x_{i^{\prime}j}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{i^{\prime}}\left(|y_{j}-x_{ij}|+|y_{j}-x_{i^{\prime}j}|\right)^{p}\quad\text{(Triangle Inequality)}

Appendix B
Sketching Medoid Costs

Another notion of centrality of a set of points is the medoid: for a metric space
(X,d_{X})
and a set of
n
points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
, the medoid of the set of points is

\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{y\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{X}(x_{i},y).

Analogously, we may define the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-medoid cost of a set of points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}

\min_{y\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-y\|_{p}^{p}.

Similarly to the case of
\ell_{p}^{p}
-medians, the hope is to leverage sketching algorithms for
\ell_{p}
-norms in order to sketch the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-medoid cost. However, as we now show, the
\ell_{p}^{p}
-median and
\ell_{p}^{p}
-medoid are very different from the sketching perspective.

Theorem 10

Proof:
[Proof of Theorem

We devise a protocol which utilizes a streaming algorithm for approximating the medoid in order to solve the indexing problem. Specifically, let
\alpha=\epsilon/10

, where
\alpha n\in\mathbbm{N}

P_{A}(y)=\{e_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}:y_{i}=1\}.

•
