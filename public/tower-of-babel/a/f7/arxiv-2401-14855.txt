Double parton distributions with flavor interference from lattice QCD Preprint: DESY-24-014
Daniel Reitinger, Christian Zimmermann, Markus Diehl, and Andreas Schäfer

Abstract
We study double parton distributions with flavor interference in the nucleon and compare them with previous results for the flavor diagonal case. We investigate both unpolarized and polarized partons. We compare our lattice results with those obtained from the simple description of the proton in terms of an SU(6) symmetric three-quark wave function and find that this description fails for both flavor and polarization dependence. We also derive and test a factorization ansatz for the unpolarized flavor interference distribution in terms of single-parton distributions and find that this ansatz fails to a large extent.

Introduction

The Standard Model of particle physics has been extremely successful in describing experimental data at high energies. As part of this, the fundamental QCD Lagrangian has been established beyond any reasonable doubt. However, many aspects of the resulting hadron properties are still only poorly understood, e.g. the entanglement of two partons in the wave functions of a proton. Work on this front is not only motivated by the goal to better understand the strong interaction, but also by the fact that QCD effects often limit the sensitivity of searches for physics beyond the Standard Model.

An intriguing phenomenon in hadron collisions is double parton scattering (DPS), a mechanism in which two partons in each hadron take part in a hard scattering subprocess. Building on pioneering work from the 1970s and 1980s

;

A crucial input for computing DPS are double parton distributions (DPDs), which describe the joint distribution of two partons inside a hadron. They quantify several types of two-particle correlations in the proton wave function and are not well known. It is natural to explore to which extent lattice QCD calculations can provide guidance in this context. In two previous publications
Bali:2020mij
;
Bali:2021gel

Diehl:2011yj

Lin:2017snn

Ji:2013dva
;
Ji:2020ect

One should bear in mind that neither of these lattice approaches is sensitive to parton momentum fractions smaller than, say,
10^{-2}
, which are responsible for much of the phase space where DPS is observed. (The same holds for quark models, which have been used extensively to compute DPDs

;

;
Broniowski:2020jwk
.) However, partons with larger momentum fractions at low resolution scales are the “seeds” of evolution and thus have an imprint on partons with smaller momentum fractions at high scales. At high scales, large parton momentum fractions are probed in the production of heavy particles and thus of interest in searches for new physics. Moreover, larger parton momentum fractions become relevant when the products of the two hard scatters in DPS have a large rapidity separation. This is an interesting kinematic region, where DPS is often appreciable compared to single hard scattering. Finally, DPDs at larger momentum fractions are of interest in their own right from the point of view of exploring hadron structure.

With this in mind, we complement in the present paper our previous study
Bali:2021gel
of DPDs in the nucleon. Specifically, we investigate flavor interference DPDs, which are characterized by different flavors for the quark (or antiquark) initiating a specific hard scattering in the amplitude and in its complex conjugate. Such distributions contribute for instance to the double Drell-Yan process. They were introduced in
Diehl:2011yj

Mellin moments) there is however no argument that flavor interference should be suppressed. We will investigate whether this is the case by comparing the corresponding two-current matrix elements computed on the lattice. We will furthermore compare our lattice results (both for the flavor diagonal and the interference case) with the predictions obtained from an
SU(6)
symmetric three-quark wave function of the proton.

Theory background

2.1
Definitions and properties

In the following, we review some basic definitions and properties of double parton distributions in the context of our lattice simulation. For more details the reader is referred to
Bali:2020mij
;
Bali:2021gel
. DPDs describe the joint probability of finding two quarks with given polarization in a hadron. In this work we focus on the proton. We average over its polarization
\lambda
, which is indicated by the notation
\sum^{\prime}_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda}
. The definition of DPDs is given by

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})=2p^{+}\int\mathrm{d}y^{-}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{1}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{2}}{2\pi}\,

\displaystyle e^{i\mskip 1.5mu(x_{1}z_{1}^{-}+x_{2}z_{2}^{-})\mskip 1.5mup^{+}}

\displaystyle\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\,\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\,,

(1)

where we use light-cone coordinates
v^{\pm}:=(v^{0}\pm v^{3})/\sqrt{2}
and
\boldsymbol{v}:=(v^{1},v^{2})
for a given four-vector
v^{\mu}
. The light-cone operators are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{a}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}\bigl(y-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\,\Gamma_{a}\,q\bigl(y+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\Big|_{z^{+}=y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}}\,,

(2)

where
a
specifies the quark flavor and polarization, which is determined by the spin projections

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\gamma^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Delta q}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\gamma^{+}\gamma_{5}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta q}^{j}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}i\sigma^{j+}\gamma_{5}\quad(j=1,2)\,.

(3)

q
refers to an unpolarized quark,
\Delta q
to a longitudinally polarized quark and
\delta q
to a transversely polarized quark. The DPDs (
2.1
) can be decomposed in terms of rotationally invariant functions:

\displaystyle F_{q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=f_{q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=f_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}^{j_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{j_{1}k}\boldsymbol{y}^{k}\,mf_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{j_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{j_{2}k}\boldsymbol{y}^{k}\,mf_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{j_{1}j_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\delta^{j_{1}j_{2}}f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(2\boldsymbol{y}^{j_{1}}\boldsymbol{y}^{j_{2}}-\delta^{j_{1}j_{2}}\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\bigr)\mskip 1.5mum^{2}f^{\mskip 1.5mut}_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},y^{2})\,,

(4)

where
m
denotes the proton mass and
\epsilon^{jk}
is the antisymmetric tensor in two dimensions (
\epsilon^{12}\penalty\ =\penalty\ 1
). The definitions given above can be extended for the case of flavor-changing operators, i.e. (
2
) is modified so that the quark field has a different quark flavor than the conjugate quark field,
q\neq q^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{a}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}\bigl(y-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\,\Gamma_{a}\,q^{\prime}\bigl(y+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\Big|_{z^{+}=y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}}\,,

(5)

with
a=(qq^{\prime}),\Delta(qq^{\prime}),\delta(qq^{\prime})
. The corresponding functions
F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})
given by inserting the operators (
5
) in (
2.1
) are called flavor interference distributions or flavor interference DPDs. For instance, an interference contribution to the unpolarized channel is given by
F_{(ud)(du)}
, which corresponds to the operator combination
\mathcal{O}_{(ud)}\mathcal{O}_{(du)}
. Notice that in contrast to flavor diagonal DPDs, these interference DPDs cannot be interpreted as parton density distributions. Hence, there is no positivity constraint for them. Flavor interference DPDs are relevant in the description of double parton scattering, where they represent flavor interference contributions in the cross section. An example is shown in figure
1
.

In the context of lattice calculations, it is useful to introduce so-called skewed DPDs, which are a generalization of ordinary DPDs in the sense that one introduces a difference between the momentum fractions of the quark in the wave function and in its complex conjugate. This difference is quantified by the skewness parameter
\zeta
:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=2p^{+}\int\mathrm{d}y^{-}e^{-i\zeta y^{-}p^{+}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{1}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{2}}{2\pi}\,e^{i\mskip 1.5mu(x_{1}z_{1}^{-}+x_{2}z_{2}^{-})\mskip 1.5mup^{+}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\,\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\,.

(6)

This skewed DPD has already been used in
Bali:2020mij
;
Bali:2021gel
, where also the region of support w.r.t. the parameters
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
and
\zeta
has been discussed in detail. Let us recall that this support region is given by:

\displaystyle|x_{i}\pm{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta|\leq 1\,,\qquad|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|\leq 1\,,\qquad|\zeta|\leq 1\,.

(7)

In figure
2
we give a graphical representation of skewed DPDs, where we indicate the longitudinal momentum fraction of the quarks in the wave function and its complex conjugate.

Skewed DPDs and their parton momentum fractions for the flavor
diagonal case (left) and for flavor interference (right).

Moreover, we define Mellin moments w.r.t.
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
:

\displaystyle I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\;f_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,.

(8)

The symmetry properties of flavor diagonal DPDs are discussed in
Bali:2021gel
. In the following we briefly show how these properties generalize to the flavor interference case.

For unpolarized distributions, one finds

\displaystyle f_{(ud)(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{(du)(ud)}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\bigl[f_{(ud)(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\bigr]^{*}

\displaystyle=f_{(du)(ud)}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle f_{(ud)(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{(du)(ud)}(x_{2},x_{1},-\zeta,y^{2})\,,

(9)

where the first relation follows from
PT
invariance, the second one from taking the hermitian conjugate of the definition (
2.1
), and the third one from interchanging the two operators. From the first two relations, it follows that
f_{(ud)(du)}
is real valued. Equations analogous to (
2.1
) relate
f_{\Delta(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\Delta(du)}
with
f_{\Delta(du)\mskip 1.5mu\Delta(ud)}
,
f_{\delta(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(du)}
with
f_{\delta(du)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(ud)}
, and
f^{t}_{\delta(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(du)}
with
f^{t}_{\delta(du)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(ud)}
. For a single transverse polarization, we have instead

\displaystyle f_{(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{(du)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(ud)}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\bigl[f_{(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\bigr]^{*}

\displaystyle=f_{(du)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(ud)}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle f_{(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\delta(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle={}-f_{\delta(du)(ud)}(x_{2},x_{1},-\zeta,y^{2})\,.

(10)

Also in this case, we find that the distributions are real valued. Parity invariance implies that
F_{(ud)\mskip 1.5mu\Delta(du)}
,
F_{(du)\mskip 1.5mu\Delta(ud)}
,
F_{\Delta(ud)(du)}
, and
F_{\Delta(du)(ud)}
are zero.

2.2
Euclidean matrix elements

Information about DPDs can be obtained from first principles on the lattice through Euclidean two-current matrix elements. This has been worked out in detail for the pion
Bali:2020mij
and the nucleon
Bali:2021gel
. In the following, we recall the important definitions and relations to DPDs and extend them to the case of flavor interference. The Euclidean two-current matrix element of the nucleon is defined as:

\displaystyle M^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},i_{1}i_{2}}(p,y)

\displaystyle:=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}J^{\mu_{1}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2},i_{1}}(y)\,J^{\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{3}q_{4},i_{2}}(0)\ket{p,\lambda}\,,

(11)

where we take the average of the proton spin. The currents
J^{\mu\dots}_{qq^{\prime},i}
are local quark bilinear operators. In this work we focus on three types of currents, which are defined as:

\displaystyle J_{qq^{\prime},V}^{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\mu}\mskip 1.5muq^{\prime}(y)\,,

\displaystyle J_{qq^{\prime},A}^{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\,q^{\prime}(y)\,,

\displaystyle J_{qq^{\prime},T}^{\mu\nu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\sigma^{\mu\nu}\mskip 1.5muq^{\prime}(y)\,.

(12)

In order to make contact with the DPDs, we decompose the matrix elements in terms of Lorentz invariant functions:

\displaystyle M^{\{\mu\nu\}}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},VV}-\tfrac{1}{4}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\alpha\beta}M^{\alpha\beta}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},VV}

\displaystyle=u_{VV,A}^{\mu\nu}\,A_{(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}+u_{VV,B}^{\mu\nu}\,m^{2}\mskip 1.5muB_{(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}

\displaystyle\quad+u_{VV,C}^{\mu\nu}\,m^{4}\mskip 1.5muC_{(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}\,,

\displaystyle M^{\mu\nu\rho}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},TV}+\tfrac{2}{3}g_{\vphantom{q_{1}T}}^{\rho[\mu}M^{\nu]\alpha\beta}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},TV}g_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=u_{TV,A}^{\mu\nu\rho}\,m\mskip 1.5muA_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}+u_{TV,B}^{\mu\nu\rho}\,m^{3}\mskip 1.5muB_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}\,,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl[M^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},TT}+M^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},TT}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}_{TT,A}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,A_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}+\tilde{u}_{TT,B}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,m^{2}\mskip 1.5muB_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{u}_{TT,C}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,m^{2}\mskip 1.5muC_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}+\tilde{u}_{TT,D}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,m^{4}\mskip 1.5muD_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}

\displaystyle\quad+u_{TT,E}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,m^{2}\mskip 1.5mu\widetilde{E}_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}\,,

(13)

where the basis tensors
u
and
\tilde{u}
, which depend on the Lorentz vectors
y
and
p
, have been defined in
Bali:2021gel
, equation (2.26). The quantities
A_{(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}
, etc. are Lorentz scalar functions depending only on
y^{2}
and
py
. At leading twist, we only need to consider the functions
A_{(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}
,
A_{\Delta(q_{1}q_{2})\Delta(q_{3}q_{4})}
,
A_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})(q_{3}q_{4})}
,
A_{(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}
,
A_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}
, and
B_{\delta(q_{1}q_{2})\delta(q_{3}q_{4})}
. These so-called twist-two functions are directly related to the Mellin moments (
8
):

\displaystyle I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(py)\,e^{-i\zeta py}\,A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py,y^{2})\,,

(14)

\displaystyle I^{t}_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(py)\,e^{-i\zeta py}\,B_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py,y^{2})\,,

(15)

where (
15
) is only defined for transverse polarization of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. In this work, we shall restrict ourselves to
\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu=\vec{\mskip 0.5mu0}\mskip 1.5mu
and, therefore,
py=0
. In this case, the twist-two functions correspond to the first moment in
\zeta
of the DPD:

\displaystyle A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py=0,y^{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})\,.

(16)

In

\displaystyle\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}\left[\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}+\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}-2\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}}+\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}}+\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}-2\ket{u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}+\right.

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=4a\,,

\displaystyle\quad\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=a\,,

\displaystyle=a\,,

\displaystyle\quad\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{d}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=a\,,

\displaystyle\quad\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{d}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=-2a\,,

\displaystyle\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{d}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}\gamma^{+}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=-2a\,,

\displaystyle\quad\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}(\bar{d}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}u^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})(\bar{u}^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow}\gamma^{+}d^{\mskip 1.5mu\downarrow})\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(19)

where it is understood that the four field operators are taken at different positions as specified by (
2.1
) and (
2
). Here the factor
a
depends on the orbital part of the proton wave function, which we leave unspecified. The DPDs are expressed in terms of proton matrix elements of the operators (
18
), i.e.
f_{a_{1}a_{2}}\propto\bra{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}\ket{p^{\mskip 1.5mu\uparrow}}
. Using the results given in (
19
), we find:

\displaystyle f_{ud}

\displaystyle=+6\tilde{a}\,,

\displaystyle\quad f_{uu}

\displaystyle=+6\tilde{a}\,,

\displaystyle\quad f_{(du)(ud)}

\displaystyle=-3\tilde{a}\,,

\displaystyle f_{\Delta u\Delta d}

\displaystyle=-4\tilde{a}\,,

\displaystyle\quad f_{\Delta u\Delta u}

\displaystyle=+2\tilde{a}\,,

\displaystyle\quad f_{\Delta(du)\Delta(ud)}

\displaystyle=+5\tilde{a}\,.

(20)

with an overall factor
\tilde{a}
that depends again on the orbital part of the wave function. Since this orbital part is isotropic, there is no difference regarding the direction of the quark polarization. Hence, the values obtained for
f_{\Delta u\Delta d}
are the same for
f_{\delta u\delta d}
, and likewise for all other flavor combinations. Predictions that are independent of the factor
\tilde{a}
are obtained for
ratios
of DPDs, such as:

\displaystyle\frac{f_{(du)(ud)}}{f_{ud}}=-\frac{1}{2}\,,\qquad\quad\frac{f_{(du)(ud)}}{f_{uu}}=-\frac{1}{2}\,,\qquad\quad\frac{f_{ud}}{f_{uu}}=+1\,,

(21)

and

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\Delta(du)\Delta(ud)}}{f_{ud}}=+\frac{5}{6}\,,\qquad\quad\frac{f_{\Delta u\Delta d}}{f_{ud}}=-\frac{2}{3}\,,\qquad\quad\frac{f_{\Delta u\Delta u}}{f_{uu}}=+\frac{1}{3}\,.

(22)

Analogous predictions hold for the ratios of the twist-two functions
A_{a_{1}a_{2}}
and can be directly checked against lattice results.

3
Lattice calculation

In the following, we give an overview of the calculation of two-current matrix elements on the lattice and briefly review the techniques that are used. A detailed explanation is given in
Bali:2021gel
. This shall now be extended to flavor-changing operators.

Let us first recall that the two-current matrix element (
11
) of the nucleon at
y^{0}=0
is related to the nucleon four-point function
C_{4\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)
by the following formula:

\displaystyle\left.M_{ij}(p,y)\vphantom{\sum}\right|_{y^{0}=0}=C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu):=2V\sqrt{m^{2}+\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu^{2}}\left.\frac{C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)}{C^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{2pt}}(t)}\right|_{0\ll\tau\ll t}\,,

(23)

where
V
is the spatial lattice volume and the four-point function
C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)
is given by:

\displaystyle C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle:=a^{6}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu}e^{-i\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}-\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu)}\ \left\langle\operatorname{tr}\left\{P_{+}\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},t)\ J_{i}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ J_{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mu0}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ \overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu,0)\right\}\right\rangle\,.

(24)

Here
P_{+}=\left(\mathbb{1}+\gamma_{4}\right)/2
projects onto positive parity, and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)
and
\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)
are the nucleon interpolators, for which we choose:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)

\displaystyle:=\left.\epsilon_{abc}\ \left[\bar{u}_{a}(x)\ C\gamma_{5}\ \bar{d}_{b}^{\,T}(x)\right]\bar{u}_{c}(x)\vphantom{\sum}\right|_{x^{4}=t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)

\displaystyle:=\left.\epsilon_{abc}\ u_{a}(x)\left[u_{b}^{T}(x)\ C\gamma_{5}\ d_{c}(x)\right]\vphantom{\sum}\right|_{x^{4}=t}\,,

(25)

where
C
is the charge conjugation matrix in Dirac space. The two-point function
C^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{2pt}}(t)
appearing in (
23
) is defined as:

\displaystyle C^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{2pt}}(t)

\displaystyle:=a^{6}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu}e^{-i\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}-\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu)}\ \left\langle\operatorname{tr}\left\{P_{+}\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},t)\ \overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu,0)\right\}\right\rangle\,.

(26)

Wick contractions:

The four-point function (
24
) decomposes into a definite set of Wick contractions w.r.t. the fermion fields. There are five types of contractions, which we call
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
and
D
. These contractions are represented by the graphs in figure
3
. Notice that, depending on the quark flavor of the operators, there are several contributions for each contraction type. For
C_{1}
-type graphs, we denote this by the flavor indices of the operator insertions
J_{q_{1}q_{2},i}
and
J_{q_{3}q_{4},j}
, i.e.
C_{1,q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4}}
. For the proton, only the contractions
C_{1,uudd}
,
C_{1,uuuu}
and
C_{1,duud}
contribute (together with
C_{1,dduu}
and
C_{1,uddu}
, which are obtained by exchanging the two currents). In our calculation, we consider only proton matrix elements in the iso-symmetric limit, where the
u
-quark and the
d
-quark have the same mass. In that case, there are two independent contributions with
C_{2}
- or
S_{1}
-topology, namely
C_{2,u}
,
C_{2,d}
,
S_{1,u}
and
S_{1,d}
. The indicated flavor refers to the quark line that connects one of the currents with the proton source.

the explicit contraction depends on all involved flavors. Provided that the quark masses are taken to be equal,

\displaystyle=C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,uudd}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+S^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,u}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+S^{ji,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,d}(-\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+D^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)\,,

\displaystyle\left.M_{uuuu,ij}(p,y)\right|_{y^{0}=0}

\displaystyle=C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,uuuu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2,u}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+C^{ji,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2,u}(-\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+S^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,u}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+S^{ji,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{1,u}(-\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)

\displaystyle\left.M_{dddd,ij}(p,y)\right|_{y^{0}=0}

\displaystyle\quad+S_{2}^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)+D^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)\,,

Renormalization:

The lattice operators are renormalized multiplicatively and converted to the
\overline{\mathrm{MS}}
-scheme using the factors
Z_{i}
:

\displaystyle J_{i}^{\overline{\mathrm{MS}}}(y)=Z_{i}J_{i}^{\mathrm{latt}}(y)\,.

(29)

For
\beta=3.4
, the corresponding values of
Z_{i}
are
RQCD:2020kuu
:

\displaystyle Z_{V}=0.7128\,,\qquad Z_{A}=0.7525\,,\qquad Z_{T}=0.8335\,,

(30)

for the choice

\displaystyle\mu=2\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}\,.

(31)

The renormalization of the two-current matrix elements is given by:

\displaystyle M^{\overline{\mathrm{MS}}}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},i_{1}i_{2}}=Z_{i_{1}}Z_{i_{2}}M^{\mathrm{latt}}_{q_{1}q_{2}q_{3}q_{4},i_{1}i_{2}}\,.

(32)

A sketch summarizing the techniques used to evaluate the Wick contractions is given in figure

and
C_{2}
additionally involve stochastic wall sources for a given timeslice
t
, for which we use
Z_{2}\times Z_{2}
sources. We denote the corresponding propagated stochastic source (”stochastic propagator”) by
\psi_{t}^{(\ell)}
, where
\ell
indicates the stochastic source. In the case of
C_{2}
, the stochastic source timeslice and the timeslice where the stochastic propagator is evaluated are identical. Therefore, the propagator is improved by removing terms in the corresponding hopping parameter expansion that are trivially zero in the exact case but contribute to the stochastic noise. A similar improvement is performed for the loop
L_{1}
appearing in
S_{1}
and
D
, which is also evaluated using stochastic propagators. Notice that for the
D
contraction we have two versions, one using stochastic sources for both loops, and one where we use point sources only for one of the two loops. More details on the techniques are given in
Bali:2021gel
. This reference gives also explicit expressions for the quantities that are evaluated on the lattice, except for the contraction
C_{1,duud}
, which contributes only in the context of flavor interference. We find:

\displaystyle C_{1,duud}^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{3}}{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\left.\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}\sum_{\ell}^{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\left\langle\left[q_{2,t,j}^{T,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}(x)\ q_{1,t,i}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}(x+y)\right]\right\rangle\right|_{x^{4}=\tau,y^{4}=0}\,

(33)

with

\displaystyle\left(q_{1,t,i}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}\right)^{a}_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle:=\left(\overline{S}_{321,t,i}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}\right)^{a}_{\alpha}(y)-\left(\overline{S}_{123,t,i}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}\right)^{a}_{\alpha}(y)\,,

\displaystyle\left(q_{2,t,j}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu,(\ell)}\right)^{a}_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle:=\left[\psi_{t}^{\dagger,(\ell)}(y)\ \gamma_{5}\Gamma_{j}\ M^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{z}(y)\right]^{a}_{\alpha}\,,

(34)

where

4.1
Data quality

Before we discuss our results in a physical context, we first investigate the quality of our data. In particular, we focus on the potential presence of excited states and of lattice artifacts, such as anisotropy effects. We only treat the contraction
C_{1,duud}

Excited states:

In the following we consider the

\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu

\tau

Anisotropy:

Since we compute on a lattice with periodic boundary conditions, the effect of so-called “mirror charges” becomes important if the distance between the two current approaches half the lattice size
Burkardt:1994pw
;
Alexandrou:2008ru
. As a consequence, there are anisotropy effects whose size depends on the angle
\theta(\vec{y}\mskip 1.5mu)
between
\vec{y}
and the closest diagonal of the lattice. Moreover, the lattice propagator itself exhibits anisotropy effects, which become large at small distances
Bali:2018spj
;
Cichy:2012is
. This is the case for contractions where both operators are connected directly via a quark propagator, i.e. the
C_{2}
and
S_{2}
contributions. In order to reduce the violation of Lorentz invariance, only regions with
|\vec{y}\mskip 1.5mu|=y\geq 4a
are considered. As discussed in section 4.1 of
Bali:2021gel
, we limit ourselves to distances
y\leq 16a
and take into account only data points close to the lattice diagonal, in order to reduce discretization artifacts and finite volumes effects. Specifically, only data points which fulfill

\displaystyle\cos(\theta(\vec{y}\mskip 1.5mu))>0.9

(35)

are considered. For the contraction
C_{1,duud}
the most important source of anisotropy is given by mirror charges, which leads to the saw tooth pattern already observed in other contractions with
C_{1}
topology. This is illustrated in figure
6
, where the data points fulfilling the constraint (
35
) are plotted in red.

5
Factorization tests

A common strategy for modeling DPDs is to express them in terms of single-parton distributions, assuming that correlations between the two partons can be neglected. A corresponding ansatz can also be formulated at the level of twist-two functions
Bali:2020mij
;
Bali:2021gel
. We now extend this formulation to the flavor interference case, which we will find to be special in this context. We limit ourselves to unpolarized quarks in the following

Technically, the factorization ansatz is obtained by inserting a complete set of eigenstates between the two operators in the DPD matrix element in (
2.1
) and neglecting all intermediate states except for the ground state:

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\ \mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}

\displaystyle\quad\approx\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{\prime+}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}{2p^{\prime+}(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{-iy(p^{\prime}-p)}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(0,z_{1})\ket{p^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\bra{p^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\,.

(36)

In the flavor diagonal case, the matrix elements on the r.h.s. can be directly identified with GPD matrix elements
f_{a}^{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(\bar{x},\xi,\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p})
, which leads to the factorization formula for DPDs
Diehl:2011yj
:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2(1-\zeta)}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{r}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{-i\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{y}}\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}f_{a_{1}}^{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(\bar{x}_{1},-\xi,\boldsymbol{0},-\boldsymbol{r})\ f_{a_{2}}^{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(\bar{x}_{2},\xi,-\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{0})\,,

(37)

where
f
is defined in equation (5.5) of
Bali:2021gel
. The notation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}
indicates that (
37
) is an assumption, which we investigate in the following.

and
\xi
are functions of
\zeta
and/or
x_{i}
:

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}(x_{i},\zeta):=\frac{2x_{i}}{2-\zeta}\,,\qquad\xi(\zeta):=\frac{\zeta}{2-\zeta}\,.

(38)

For flavor-changing operators, the matrix elements in the second line of (

\displaystyle\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{(ud)}\ket{n}=\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{u}\ket{p}-\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{d}\ket{p}=\bra{n}\mathcal{O}_{(du)}\ket{p}\,,

(39)

which leads to the following decomposition of the flavor interference matrix element in terms of flavor conserving matrix elements:

\displaystyle\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{(ud)}\ket{n}\bra{n}\mathcal{O}_{(du)}\ket{p}\ =\Bigl[\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{u}\ket{p}-\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{d}\ket{p}\Bigr]^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{u}\ket{p}\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{u}\ket{p}-2\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{u}\ket{p}\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{d}\ket{p}+\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{d}\ket{p}\bra{p}\mathcal{O}_{d}\ket{p}\,.

(40)

Inserting this into the factorization hypothesis (
36

\displaystyle F_{(ud)(du)}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})=F_{uu}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})-2F_{ud}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})+F_{dd}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})\,.

(41)

Moreover, taking Mellin moments and performing a Fourier transform w.r.t.
\zeta

\displaystyle A_{(ud)(du)}(py,y^{2})=A_{uu}(py,y^{2})-2A_{ud}(py,y^{2})+A_{dd}(py,y^{2})\,.

(42)

6
Conclusions

We extended our work on nucleon DPDs
Bali:2021gel
by considering flavor interference distributions. We calculated the corresponding Wick contractions and extracted the twist-two functions for the flavor combination
(ud)(du)
for all relevant quark polarizations. This we did for zero proton momentum, which implies
py=0
. We find that the resulting signal for flavor interference is non-negligible and of the same order of magnitude as the
dd
contributions. This suggests that at large parton momentum fractions, flavor interference DPDs may not be negligible.

Several relations between the different flavor and polarization combinations can be derived in a simple
SU(6)
quark model, which can then be compared to the corresponding lattice quantities. We observe good agreement between the lattice data and the
SU(6)
results for unpolarized quarks if we only take into account the contribution of the
C_{1}
contraction, which is most consistent with the picture of a three-quark wave function. If one considers the full connected contribution (
C_{1}+C_{2}
) there is a significant mismatch, which increases for smaller quark distances. The
SU(6)
model completely fails in the case of two-parton matrix elements for polarized quarks. Finally, we considered the factorization of flavor interference DPDs in terms of GPDs. We found that, depending on the considered quark distance, the factorization ansatz is either not reliable or it does not work in the region where the signal is clearly non-zero.
