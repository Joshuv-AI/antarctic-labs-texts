Witnessing entanglement of remote particles with incomplete teleportation
Vahid Jannessary, Fatemeh Rezazadeh, Sadegh Raeisi, Vahid Karimipour

Abstract
Having common reference frames or aligned coordinate systems, is one of the presumptions in witnessing entanglement in a two-party state possessed by two remote parties. This assumption may fail for many reasons. With an unlimited supply of singlet states, the two parties can first align their coordinate systems and then measure any entanglement witness. In this article, we propose an alternative method which uses the same resource for incomplete teleportation of states between the two parties, enabeling them to witness the entanglement of any shared state by local measurements without the need of prior alignment of the coordinate systems. The method works for any kind of witness and in any dimension. Beyond the context of Entanglement Witnesses, our method works also for remote measurements of observables of particles (entangled or not) in laboratories which may have deficiency in their resources.

The basic idea of the paper is to note the utility of an incomplete teleportation which is based on a specific two-outcome measurement by Alice. We remind that the standard or
complete teleportation
protocol of qubits requires a Bell measurement performed by Alice who will send the results by sending two classical bits to Bob. Bob will use this information to fully recover the state hold by Alice. While the explicit calculations are done for pure states, by linearity any mixed state can also be teleported from Alice to Bob. Hence this procedure is capable to send the share of Alice in any entangled state to Bob, a process which is similar to entanglement swapping

However, as we will show in the sequel, the crucial point is that to determine whether or not a shared state between Alice and Bob is entangled, a complete teleportation and hence a common coordinate system is not needed. As we will show a specific two-outcome measurement by Alice followed by a one-bit classical communication, can teleport the state in a way which although is incomplete, nevertheless can fully witness the entanglement of the shared state. The nature of this measurement is that no common reference frame is necessary. In fact, we will show that no matter what kind of Entanglement Witness
W

The only resource that they consume is a singlet state (in the particular dimension of states) and a single bit of classical information. Needless to say, in any type of measurement, including those of Entanglement Witnesses, to reach a reliable statistics of results, a multitude of states is needed which accordingly requires the same number of shared singlet states. In passing we should note that besides witnessing entanglement, the method presented here, can be used to make any kind of measurement on a shared state between the two parties. At the end, we argue that besides removing the need for common coordinate systems, our method has other applications which may go beyond the simple determination of whether a shared state is entangled or not.

Witnessing entanglement of qubit states

II.2
An example

In

S=\{\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}\},

(i.e. in contrast to operators of the form
\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}\ \ i\neq j)
.
These witnesses are of the form

\tilde{W}=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|^{\Gamma},

(16)

where
\Gamma
denotes partial transpose and
|\phi\rangle
has the following general form

|\phi\rangle=a|\phi_{1}\rangle+b|\phi_{2}\rangle,

(17)

in which
|\phi_{1}\rangle
and
|\phi_{2}\rangle
, depending on the witness, are two of the Bell states. An example of this type of Entanglement Witness, when written in terms of Pauli operators takes the following form

\tilde{W}=\frac{1}{4}\big[I\otimes I+\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}+(a^{2}-b^{2})\big(\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})+2ab\big(\sigma_{z}\otimes I+I\otimes\sigma_{z}\big)\big],

(18)

from which we find
\tilde{W}_{2}=Tr_{1}\tilde{W}=\frac{1}{2}(I+2ab\sigma_{z})
and hence

\begin{cases}\tilde{W}^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}=\tilde{W}\\
\tilde{W}^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}=I\otimes I+2abI\otimes\sigma_{z}-3\tilde{W}.\end{cases}

(19)

Note that these two different forms for witnesses, do not require different experimental setups. In fact, as noted in

and

are measured, but the data are handled differently depending on the classical bit that Alice sends.

II.3
Witnessing entanglement of higher dimensional states

We now turn our attention to witnesses for higher dimensional states. The well known protocol for teleporting a
d-
dimensional state, is based on sharing a Bell state of the form
|\Phi_{00}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k,k\rangle
and making a measurement in the Bell basis

|\Phi_{m,n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\omega^{km}|k,k+n\rangle,

where
\omega^{d}=1
. However, the incomplete teleportation which we have described and is possible in the absence of shared reference frames, depends critically on a specific type of singlet state (a state with zero total angular momentum), being shared between the two parties.
Such a state which we denote by

|\Psi_{ab}\rangle
is invariant under a representation
R\otimes R\in SO(3)\times SO(3)
and is different from the state
|\phi_{00}\rangle
which is invarint under
U\otimes U^{*}\in SU(d)\times SU(d)
. We assume that the states are
d=2j+1
dimensional where
j
denotes the total spin of each particle.
To proceed we first need the general form of a singlet state of two spin
j
particles. It is easily seen that this unique singlet state is given by

|\Psi_{ab}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2j+1}}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}(-1)^{m}|m_{a},-m_{b}\rangle,

(20)

where in this and later formulas, we have used the abbreviated angular momentum notation
|m\rangle
instead of
|j,m\rangle
.

Remark 3
.

The term singlet is normally used when we add the angular momentum of two spin
1/2
particles, where there is only one state with zero total angular momentum. In higher dimensions, there are more than one state with zero total angular momentum. Here we mean a specific type of such state whose explicit form is given in eq. (
20

One can verify that this state is a singlet state by simply checking that

J^{tot}_{z}|\Psi\rangle=J^{tot}_{\pm}|\Psi\rangle=0,

(21)

where
J^{tot}_{z}
and
J^{tot}_{\pm}
are the total spin-

J_{\mu}^{tot}=J_{\mu}^{(a)}\otimes I^{(b)}+I^{(a)}\otimes J_{\mu}^{(b)}
and

stands for

. Using abbreviated notation (
|m\rangle\equiv|j,m\rangle
), the action of angular momentum operators on each part (

or

J_{z}|m\rangle=m|m\rangle,\ \ \ \ \ J_{\pm}|m\rangle=\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm 1)}|m\pm 1\rangle.

(22)

The singlet state, having zero total angular momentum, is rotationally invariant and has the same expression in every coordinate system.

Remark 4
.

Although we use the language of spins, the scheme works for the case where information is encoded in other degrees of freedom, such as polarization of photons, ground and excited states of a two-level atom etc. In this general setting, a unitary transformation

which up to now we have called rotation, is any unitary operation which transforms the un-primed states into primed states. A singlet state in this context is any state which is invariant under the operation

\pi_{0}
retains its meaning. In fact for any

This requires that the two players should be able to use conventional gates for qudit systems and perform the above tasks. We will show this in the appendix.

Similar to the qubit case, here again, Alice makes a measurement by two projectors
\{\pi_{0},\pi_{1}\}
, but now

is the projection on the spin

is the projection on any other spin different from zero.
Thus, this measurement simply determines whether the total spin of two particles is zero or not and does not determine the total spin of the two particles and this is all that we need for incomplete teleportation. For now, consider the incomplete teleportation of a single particle in the state
\rho_{a^{\prime}}

X^{(0)}_{b}=\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\sum_{k,l}\rho_{kl}|k_{b}\rangle\langle l_{b}|=\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\rho,

(33)

which shows that the original state of Alice (
\rho_{a^{\prime}}=\rho
) has been teleported to Bob with a probability
p_{0}=\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}
. To find the output state when the result is
1
, we note that

\displaystyle X^{(1)}_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tr_{aa^{\prime}}\big[(\rho_{a^{\prime}}\otimes\Psi_{ab})(\pi_{1})_{aa^{\prime}}\big]

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tr_{aa^{\prime}}\big[(\rho_{a^{\prime}}\otimes\Psi_{ab})(I-\pi_{0})_{aa^{\prime}}\big]

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tr_{aa^{\prime}}(\rho_{a^{\prime}}\otimes\Psi_{ab})-\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\rho

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tr_{a^{\prime}}\rho_{a^{\prime}}Tr_{a}\Psi_{ab}-\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\rho

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2j+1}I-\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\rho,

(38)

where in the last line, we have used
Tr(\rho_{a^{\prime}})=1
and
Tr_{a}\Psi_{ab}=\frac{1}{2j+1}I
and have used our conventions for subscripts denoting the states.
We also find
Tr(X^{(1)}_{b})=1-\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}=\frac{4j(j+1)}{(2j+1)^{2}}
. The original state of Alice,
\rho_{a^{\prime}}
, being a mixed state, allows us to extend these results to teleportation of entangled states or equivalently to entanglement swapping from the pair
(a^{\prime}b^{\prime})
to the pair
(bb^{\prime})
as shown in figure (

) yield the final results of entanglement swapping or teleported states:

\rho^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}=\rho\ \ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \ \rho^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}=\frac{(2j+1)(I\otimes\rho_{2})-\rho}{4j(j+1)},

(39)

which are produced with respective probabilities

p_{0}=\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}\ \ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \ p_{1}=1-\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}.

(40)

Here
\rho
is the original entangled states between Alice and Bob, i.e.
\rho_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}=\rho
.
In exact continuation of the analysis for the qubit case, we now find that the two witnesses are

\begin{cases}W^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}=W\\
W^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}=(2j+1)I\otimes W_{2}-4j(j+1)W.\end{cases}

(41)

It is in order to provide the explicit calculation for this general case, that is to show that
Tr(W^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}\rho^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}})=Tr(W\rho)
. The proof is as follows: from (
41
) and (
39

Tr(W_{bb^{\prime}}^{(1)}\rho^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{4j(j+1)}Tr\Big[\Big((2j+1)I\otimes W_{2}-4j(j+1)W\Big)\Big({(2j+1)(I\otimes\rho_{2})-\rho}\Big)\Big].

(42)

Using the identities

Tr((I\otimes W_{2})(I\otimes\rho_{2}))=(2j+1)Tr_{2}(W_{2}\rho_{2})\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ Tr((I\otimes W_{2})\rho)=Tr(W(I\otimes\rho_{2}))=Tr_{2}(W_{2}\rho_{2})

(43)

we find that many of the terms in the right hand side of (

) vanish and we are left with
Tr(W\rho)
.

In the special case of qubits (
j=\frac{1}{2}
), we recover the results for qubit case (

III
Remote measurement

Although our main motivation has been to witness the entanglement of states where the two parties need only check the inequality
Tr(W\rho)
remotely, our method can be used to remotely measure non-local observables. Here and in the sequel by
measurement
, we mean determining
the expectation value of an observable
. This is specially useful when one of the parties does not have sufficient experimental resources to make any type of measurement. In this case, the method presented for entangelement witnesses, can be slightly modified for adapttion to this new situation. We assume that Alice and Bob want to make a measurement of a non-local observable
G
on a shared state
\rho_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}
. Following the same method as explained in previous sections, we find that
\rho^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}=\rho
and
\rho^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}=\frac{(2j+1)(I\otimes\rho_{2})-\rho}{4j(j+1)}
with the same probabilities as before and Bob makes local measurements
G^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}
and
G^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}
which have the same form as in (
41
) with
W
replaced with
G
everywhere:

\begin{cases}G^{(0)}_{bb^{\prime}}=G\\
G^{(1)}_{bb^{\prime}}=(2j+1)I\otimes G_{2}-4j(j+1)G.\end{cases}

(44)

In this way, Alice and Bob, without having a common reference frame, and with different resources for measurements, are able to measure any non-local observable
G
on their shared state.

IV
Conclusion

We have presented a method for witnessing entanglement of a two-particle state which has been shared between two remote parties, even when the two parties do not have any common coordinate system. The method works for any dimension and for any kind of Entanglement Witness
W
. Like the case where the two particles are in one single laboratory, measuring an Entanglement Witness requires many copies of the state and hence, it is required that the two parties share a sufficient number of singlet states, possibly sent to them by a third party, to ascertain a reliable statistics of their measurements. Besides the lack of shared reference frame, there is one more important motivation for our method. The need may sometimes arise when one of the two parties has the necessary complex resources for measuring an arbitrary observable
G
(not necessarily an Entanglement Witness
W
), on a state which is situated in a remote laboratory (i.e. a satellite) with limited resources for measurement. In such a case, our method allows the state to be incompletely teleported to the laboratory with sufficient resources for measurement. As mentioned in the introduction, the works reported in

Their criteria for detecting non-classical correlation is violation of the Bell inequality. Detecting this violation is obviously more demanding than measuring a single suitable observable, i.e. an entanglement witness. Moreover, an entanglement witness has the advantage of being sutiable also for detecting entanglement in mixed states. Therefore it would be interesting to see if the method of random measurements developed in

Appendix: Generating singlet states by
d-
level quantum circuits

In this appendix our scheme is not specific to spin
-j
systems and works for any
d-
level system, i.e. any qudit. We need to show that with the standard gate set of qudit systems, namely the generalized Pauli operators, the Hadamard and the CNOT gate, one can prepare a singlet state and can make a measurement of
\{\pi_{0},\pi_{1}\}
, as described in the text. Needless to say, here we are concerned with theoretical and not experimental aspect of this task. To do this, we first map the angular momentum states of a spin
j-
particle to the conventional states of a qudit. Remebering the abbreviated notation for spin states (
|j,m\rangle\equiv|m\rangle
), this mapping is as follows:

\{|-j\rangle,\ |-j+1\rangle,\ \cdots|j-1\rangle,|j\rangle\}\ \ \ \longrightarrow\{\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{0}\right\rangle\kern-1.93335pt\right\rangle},\ \mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{1}\right\rangle\kern-1.93335pt\right\rangle},\ \cdots\ \mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{d-2}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle},\ \mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{d-1}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}\}

where
d=2j+1
and we have used the
\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{.}\right\rangle\kern-0.31667pt\right\rangle}
notation for the qudit states to distinguish them from the spin states. Therefore the singlet state is identified with the following qudit state of two particles,

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{m}\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{m,d-1-m}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}.

(45)

We will now show that this state can be prepared by a qudit circuit, comprised of generalized Pauli operators and the Hadamard operator. These operators are defined as follows:

X\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}=\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k+1}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle},\hskip 28.45274ptZ\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}=\omega^{k}\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle},\hskip 28.45274pt\omega^{d}=1,

(46)

and

H\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}\omega^{kl}\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{l}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle},\hskip 28.45274ptCNOT\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k,l}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}=\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k,k+l\ mod\ d}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}.

(47)

We will later need the curious property of the Hadamard operator, namely

H^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{-k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}\mathinner{\left\langle\kern-2.08334pt\left\langle{k}\right|\mkern-2.0mu\right|}.

(48)

The cyclic property of all these operators imply that the singlet state which we need to prepare is of the form

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{k}\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{k,-1-k}\right\rangle\kern-2.08334pt\right\rangle}.

(49)

A simple examination shows that the singlet state can be prepared from the product state
\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{0,0}\right\rangle\kern-1.93335pt\right\rangle}
by a simple quantum circuit, namely that

|\Psi\rangle=(Z^{\frac{d}{2}}\otimes X^{-1}H^{2})CNOT(H\otimes I)\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{0,0}\right\rangle\kern-1.93335pt\right\rangle},

(50)

the verification of which is straightforward. Conversely, as in the qubit case, if the reverse circuit
(H\otimes I)CNOT(Z^{\frac{d}{2}}\otimes X^{-1}H^{2})
, projects any arbitrary state
\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{\Phi}\right\rangle\kern-2.05pt\right\rangle}
onto
\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{0,0}\right\rangle\kern-1.93335pt\right\rangle}
, it means that the total spin of the state
\mathinner{\left|\mkern-2.0mu\left|{\Phi}\right\rangle\kern-2.05pt\right\rangle}
is zero. Any other outcome, indicates a total spin different from zero. This stems from the orthogonality of the spin states and the unitarity of the circuit.
