The weakness of finding descending sequences in ill-founded linear orders
Jun Le Goh, Arno Pauly, Manlio Valenti

Abstract
We explore the Weihrauch degree of the problems “find a bad sequence in a non-well quasi order” ( 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} ) and “find a descending sequence in an ill-founded linear order” ( 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} ). We prove that 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} is strictly Weihrauch reducible to 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} , correcting our mistaken claim in [ 15 ] . This is done by separating their respective first-order parts. On the other hand, we show that 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} and 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} have the same finitary and deterministic parts, confirming that 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} and 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} have very similar uniform computational strength. We prove that König’s lemma 𝖪𝖫 \mathsf{KL} and the problem 𝗐𝖫𝗂𝗌𝗍 2 ℕ , ≤ ω \mathsf{wList}_{{2^{\mathbb{N}}},\leq\omega} of enumerating a given non-empty countable closed subset of 2 ℕ {2^{\mathbb{N}}} are not Weihrauch reducible to 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} or 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} , resolving two main open questions raised in [ 15 ] . We also answer the question, raised in [ 12 ] , on the existence of a “parallel quotient” operator, and study the behavior of 𝖡𝖲 \mathsf{BS} and 𝖣𝖲 \mathsf{DS} under the quotient with some known problems.

Introduction

Linear and quasi-orders are ubiquitous structures that play an important role in all areas of mathematics. Computability theory has been successfully applied to highlight the difference between classical and effective properties of orders. A well-known example is that the simple fact that every infinite linear order contains an infinite ascending sequence or an infinite descending sequence is not computably true. A more extreme example is the existence of computable ill-founded linear orders with no hyperarithmetic descending sequence (see e.g.
[
26
, Lemma III.2.1]
). These orders have been extensively used and studied in reverse mathematics (under the name of pseudo-well-orders)
[
27
]
. We refer the reader to
[
11
]
for a more comprehensive presentation of computability-theoretical results on linear and partial orders.

The natural generalization of well-orders in the context of quasi-orders is the notion of
well quasi-orders
: formally, a quasi-order
(Q,\preceq)
is called well quasi-order (abbreviated wqo) if, for every infinite sequence
(q_{n})_{{n\in\mathbb{N}}}
of elements of
Q
, there are
i,j
with
i<j
such that
q_{i}\preceq_{Q}q_{j}
. This can be restated by saying that a quasi-order is a wqo if it contains no infinite bad sequences, where a (possibly finite) sequence
(q_{n})_{{n}}
is called bad if
q_{i}\not\preceq_{Q}q_{j}
for every
i<j
. Equivalently, wqo’s can be defined as quasi-orders that contain no infinite descending sequence and no infinite antichain. There is an extensive literature on the theory of wqo’s. For an overview, we refer the reader to
[
22
]
.

In this paper, we continue our investigation (started in
[
15
]
) of the uniform computational strength of the problems:

•

given a countable ill-founded linear order, find an infinite Descending Sequence in it (
\mathsf{DS}

•

given a countable non-well quasi-order, find a Bad Sequence in it (
\mathsf{BS}

A suitable framework for this is given by Weihrauch reducibility (see

for a self-contained introduction). Several results on the Weihrauch degree of

were proved in our previous work, where we showed e.g. that, despite the fact that

is not a hyperarithmetic problem, it is rather weak from the uniform point of view, as it only computes the limit computable functions. In the language of Weihrauch reducibility, this can be stated as

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}

2
Background

We now briefly introduce the relevant notions in Weihrauch complexity. For a more thorough presentation, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
. We end this section with the observation that
\mathsf{BS}
is equivalent to its restriction to partial orders (
).

A
represented space

\mathbf{X}=(X,\delta_{\mathbf{X}})
consists of a set
X
and a (possibly partial) surjection
\delta_{\mathbf{X}}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to X
.
Many spaces of interest can be represented in standard ways, such as:

\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
,

\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
,
initial segments of
\mathbb{N}
,
the set of binary relations on
\mathbb{N}
,
the set of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
-definable subsets of
\mathbb{N}
where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is a pointclass in the arithmetic or projective hierarchy,
countable Cartesian products and countable disjoint unions of represented spaces. For the formal definitions of the representation maps of these spaces, we refer the reader to
[
3
]
, or to our previous paper
[
15
]
.

A
problem

f
is a (possibly partial) multivalued function between represented spaces
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, denoted
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
.
For each
x\in X
,
f(x)
denotes the set of possible outputs (i.e.,
f
-solutions
) corresponding to the input
x
.
The
domain

\operatorname{dom}(f)
is the set of all
x\in X
such that
f(x)
is non-empty.
Such an
x
is called an
f
-instance
.
If
f(x)
is a singleton for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
,
we say
f
is
single-valued
and write
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
.
In this case, if
y
is the
f
-solution to
x
,
we write
f(x)=y
instead of (the formally correct)
f(x)=\{y\}
. A function
F:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a
realizer
for a problem
f
if whenever
p
is a name for some
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
, then
F(p)
is a name for an
f
-solution to
x
. We say a problem is
computable
(resp.
continuous
) if it has a computable (resp. continuous) realizer.

A problem
f
is
Weihrauch reducible
to a problem
g
,
written
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
,
if there are computable maps
\Phi,\Psi:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that if
p
is a name for some
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
, then

(1)

\Phi(p)
is a name for some
y\in\operatorname{dom}(g)
, and

(2)

if
q
is a name for some
g
-solution of
y
,
then
\Psi(p,q)
is a name for some
f
-solution of
x
.

If
\Phi
and
\Psi
satisfy the above, we say that
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g

via
\Phi
,
\Psi
. We sometimes refer to
\Phi
and
\Psi
as the
forward
and
backward
functionals respectively. We say that
f
is
strongly Weihrauch reducible
to
g
, written
f\leq_{\textrm{sW}}g
, if the above holds but with
\Psi(p,q)
replaced by
\Psi(q)
in (2). For us, strong Weihrauch reducibility is only of occasional technical interest.

Weihrauch reducibility forms a preorder on problems.
We say
f
and
g
are
Weihrauch equivalent
, written
f\equiv_{\textrm{W}}g
,
if
f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
and
g\leq_{\textrm{W}}f
.
The
\equiv_{\textrm{W}}
-equivalence classes (
Weihrauch degrees
) are partially ordered by
\leq_{\textrm{W}}
.
Among the numerous algebraic operations in the Weihrauch degrees, we consider:

•

for problems
f_{i}:\subseteq\mathbf{X}_{i}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}_{i}
, the
parallel product

f_{0}\times f_{1}:\subseteq\mathbf{X}_{0}\times\mathbf{X}_{1}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}_{0}\times\mathbf{Y}_{1}\text{ defined by }(x_{0},x_{1})\mapsto f_{0}(x_{0})\times f_{1}(x_{1}),

i.e., given an
f_{0}
-instance and an
f_{1}
-instance, solve both;

•

for a problem
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
, the
(infinite) parallelization

\widehat{f}:\subseteq\mathbf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\text{ defined by }(x_{i})_{i}\mapsto\prod_{i}\nolimits f(x_{i}),

i.e., given a countable sequence of
f
-instances, solve all of them.

These operations are defined on problems,
but they all lift to the Weihrauch degrees.
Parallelization even forms a closure operator, i.e.,

f\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{f}
,

f\leq_{\textrm{W}}g
implies
\widehat{f}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{g}
,
and
\widehat{\widehat{f}}\equiv_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{f}
. We also briefly mention two further operations on Weihrauch degrees, respectively called
compositional product
and
implication
, characterized by
f*g=\max_{\leq_{\textrm{W}}}\{F\circ G{}\,:\,{}F\leq_{\textrm{W}}f\wedge G\leq_{\textrm{W}}g\}
and
f\rightarrow g=\min_{\leq_{\textrm{W}}}\{h{}\,:\,{}g\leq_{\textrm{W}}f*h\}
. Both are total operations. The compositional product can be equivalently described as follows: let
(\Phi_{w})_{{w\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}}
be an effective enumeration of all partial continuous functionals
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
G_{\delta}
domain. For every
f:\subseteq\mathbf{X}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{Y}
,
g:\subseteq\mathbf{U}\rightrightarrows\mathbf{V}
, the domain of
f*g
is the set

\{(w,u)\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\operatorname{dom}(g){}\,:\,{}(\forall v\in g(u))(\forall p_{v}\in\delta_{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}(v))(\delta_{\mathbf{X}}\Phi_{w}(p_{v})\in\operatorname{dom}(f))\}.

Given as input a pair
(w,u)
,
f*g
produces a pair
(y,v)
with
v\in g(u)
and
y\in f\delta_{\mathbf{X}}\Phi_{w}(p_{v})
for some
p_{v}\in\delta_{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}(v)
. If
f,g
are problems on the Baire space,
f*g
is simply the problem that maps
(w,u)
to a pair
(y,v)
with
v\in g(u)
and
y\in f(v)
. For details, we refer to
[
4
,
29
]
.

The Weihrauch degrees also support a number of interior operators,
which have been used to separate degrees of interest (see e.g.
[
28
, §3.1]
).
For any problem
f
and any represented space
\mathbf{X}
, the problem

\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{X}}(f):=\max_{\leq_{\textrm{W}}}\nolimits\{g\leq_{\textrm{W}}f{}\,:\,{}g\text{ has codomain }\mathbf{X}\text{ and is single-valued}\}

exists
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
.
We call
\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}}(f)
the
deterministic part of
f
and denote it by
\operatorname{Det}\!\left(f\right)
for short. We say that a problem is
deterministic
if
f\equiv_{\textrm{W}}\operatorname{Det}\!\left(f\right)
, i.e. if
f
is Weihrauch equivalent to a single-valued function on Baire space. This terminology is motivated by the observation that the treatment of multi-valued functions in computable analysis inherently corresponds to a non-deterministic computation point of view.

Observe that
[
15
, Proposition 3.6]
can be generalized slightly:

Proposition 2.1
.

\operatorname{Det}\!\left(f\right)\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{2}}(f)}
. (
\mathbf{2}
is the two-point space with the discrete topology.)

Proof.

Suppose
g
is single-valued, has codomain
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and
g\leq_{\textrm{W}}f
.
Define a single-valued problem
h
with codomain
2
as follows:
Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and a
g
-instance
x
,
produce
1
if
g(x)(n)\geq m
, otherwise produce
0
.
It is easy to see that
g\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{h}
and
h\leq_{\textrm{W}}f
.
The latter implies
h\leq_{\textrm{W}}\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{2}}(f)
and so
g\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{h}\leq_{\textrm{W}}\widehat{\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{2}}(f)}
.
∎

For any problem
f
and
\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbf{k}
,
it is also known that

\max_{\leq_{\textrm{W}}}\nolimits\{g\leq_{\textrm{W}}f{}\,:\,{}g\text{ has codomain }\mathbf{X}\}

exists. For
\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{N}
we call it the
first-order part of
f

[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
, denoted by
{}^{1}f
,
while for
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{k}
we call it the
\mathbf{k}
-finitary part of
f

[
10
, Proposition 2.9]
,
denoted by
\operatorname{Fin}_{k}\!\left(f\right)
. We say that a problem is
first-order
if
f\equiv_{\textrm{W}}{}^{1}f
.
It is immediate from the definitions that
\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{\mathbb{N}}}(f)\leq_{\textrm{W}}{}^{1}f

\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbf{k}}(f)\leq_{\textrm{W}}\operatorname{Fin}_{k}\!\left(f\right)\leq_{\textrm{W}}{}^{1}f

\sigma
from an initial segment of
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}
, with length denoted by

. The set of finite (resp. infinite) strings of natural numbers is denoted by

(resp.

and

and

The constant sequence with value

is denoted by

. For

, this is the empty string
\varepsilon
. We use

for the constant infinite string with value
n
. The concatenation of strings
\sigma

\sigma\smash{\raisebox{3.87495pt}{$\smallfrown$}}\tau

For each
