C-R-T Fractionalization, Fermions, and Mod 8 Periodicity
Zheyan Wan, Juven Wang, Shing-Tung Yau, Yi-Zhuang You

Abstract
Charge conjugation (C), mirror reflection (R), time reversal (T), and fermion parity ( − 1 ) F (-1)^{\rm F} are basic discrete spacetime and internal symmetries of the Dirac fermions. In this article, we determine the group, called the C-R-T fractionalization, which is a group extension of ℤ 2 C × ℤ 2 R × ℤ 2 T \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm C}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm T} by the fermion parity ℤ 2 F \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm F} , and its extension class in all spacetime dimensions d d , for a single-particle fermion theory. For Dirac fermions, with the canonical CRT symmetry ℤ 2 CRT \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm CRT} , the C-R-T fractionalization has two possibilities that only depend on spacetime dimensions d d modulo 8, which are order-16 nonabelian groups, including the famous Pauli group. For Majorana fermions, we determine the R-T fractionalization in all spacetime dimensions d = 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 mod 8 d=0,1,2,3,4\mod 8 , an order-8 abelian or nonabelian group. For Weyl fermions, we determine the C or T fractionalization in all even spacetime dimensions d d , which is an order-4 abelian group. We only have an order-2 ℤ 2 F \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\rm F} group for Majorana-Weyl fermions. We determine the maximal number of linearly independent Dirac and Majorana mass terms and construct them explicitly. We also discuss how the conventional Dirac and Majorana mass terms break the symmetries C, R, or T. We study the domain wall dimensional reduction of the fermions and their C-R-T fractionalization: from d d -dim Dirac to ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -dim Dirac or Weyl and from d d -dim Majorana to ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -dim Majorana or Majorana-Weyl.

Introduction and summary

The study of symmetries and their breaking patterns is a cornerstone of modern theoretical physics, providing deep insights into the laws that govern the behavior of fundamental particles and fields.
Among the symmetries of particular interest are charge conjugation (C), mirror reflection (R), and time reversal (T), each representing a fundamental operation that can be applied to physical systems

Charge conjugation inverts the charge of particles, mirror reflection flips one of the spatial coordinates, and time reversal switches the direction of time’s flow. These discrete symmetries, and their conservation or violation, have profound implications for our understanding of the universe at its most fundamental level.

The time-reversal T and mirror reflection R are discrete spacetime symmetry transformations that are not part of the proper orthochronous continuous Lorentz symmetry group

II
Clifford algebras and representations

A fermion is a particle that follows Fermi–Dirac statistics. A fermion in a Lorentz invariant field theory is a Grassmann-valued Lorentz covariant spinor. Namely, the fermion is both a spinor and a Grassmann number satisfying the anti-commutation condition. Upon the field quantization, a fermion becomes a field operator, which satisfies the anti-commutation condition of the fermion field operator.
There are many varieties of fermions, with the four most common types being:

•

Dirac fermions,

•

Weyl fermions,

•

Majorana fermions, and

•

Majorana-Weyl fermions.

II.1
Clifford algebras

Mathematically, a vector space over a field (
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
) is a set with two operations: addition and scalar multiplication (the scalar is in the field) which satisfy certain conditions; a ring is a set with two operations: addition and multiplication which satisfy certain conditions; an algebra over a field (

Let

\displaystyle v^{2}=-Q(v)1,\;\;\;\forall v\in V.

(7)

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle vw+wv=-2f_{Q}(v,w)1,\;\;\;\forall v,w\in V

(8)

where

\displaystyle f_{Q}(v,w)=\frac{1}{2}(Q(v+w)-Q(v)-Q(w))

(9)

is the associated bilinear form of

is nondegenerate, namely

The algebra generated by

of

If

1.

Charge conjugation

The Dirac equation interacting with an external electromagnetic field is

\displaystyle(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}-m)\psi=0

(113)

and hence the charge-conjugated field equation is

\displaystyle(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+e\Gamma^{\mu}A_{\mu}-m)\psi^{{\rm C}}=0.

(114)

We find that if
\psi
satisfies (
113
), then
\psi^{{\rm C}}=M_{{\rm C}}\psi^{*}
satisfies (
114
) provided that

\displaystyle M_{{\rm C}}\Gamma^{\mu*}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}=-\Gamma^{\mu}.

(115)

First, we consider
d=2k+2
.
Note that only
\Gamma^{2},\Gamma^{4},\dots,\Gamma^{2k}
are imaginary, this implies that
M_{{\rm C}}
commutes with
\Gamma^{2},\Gamma^{4},\dots,\Gamma^{2k}
, anticommutes with
\Gamma^{0},\Gamma^{1},\Gamma^{3},\dots,\Gamma^{2k+1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle M_{{\rm C}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Gamma^{2}\Gamma^{4}\cdots\Gamma^{2k}&k\text{ odd,}\\
\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\Gamma^{3}\cdots\Gamma^{2k+1}&k\text{ even}\end{array}\right.

is a solution to (
115
).

Next, we consider
d=2k+3
.
Recall that
\Gamma^{2k+2}=\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt^{k+1}\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\cdots\Gamma^{2k+1}
. So if
M_{{\rm C}}\Gamma^{\mu*}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}=-\Gamma^{\mu}
for
\mu=0,1,\dots,2k+1
, then
M_{{\rm C}}\Gamma^{2k+2*}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}=(-1)^{k+1}\Gamma^{2k+2}.

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\cdots\Gamma^{2k+1}
.

For even
k
, we can choose
M_{{\rm C}}
as we did for
d=2k+2
such that (
115
) is satisfied. So
\psi^{{\rm C}}=M_{{\rm C}}\psi^{*}=\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\Gamma^{3}\cdots\Gamma^{2k+1}\psi^{*}
.

For odd
k
, if we choose
M_{{\rm C}}
as we did for
d=2k+2
, then (
115
) can not be satisfied, while
(\Gamma M_{{\rm C}})\Gamma^{\mu*}(\Gamma M_{{\rm C}})^{-1}=\Gamma^{\mu}
for
\mu=0,1,\dots,2k+2
. Therefore, the mass term breaks the C symmetry in this case. For the massless case,
\psi^{{\rm C}}=\Gamma M_{{\rm C}}\psi^{*}=\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\Gamma^{3}\cdots\Gamma^{2k+1}\psi^{*}
.

2.

Mirror reflection

The mirror reflected Dirac equation is

\displaystyle(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{0}\partial_{0}+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{1}\partial_{1}+\cdots+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j-1}\partial_{j-1}-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j}\partial_{j}+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j+1}\partial_{j+1}+\cdots+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{d-1}\partial_{d-1}-m)\cdot{}

\displaystyle\psi^{{\rm R}_{j}}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},-x_{j},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{d-1})=0.

\displaystyle M_{{\rm R}_{j}}(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{0}\partial_{0}+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{1}\partial_{1}+\cdots+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{d-1}\partial_{d-1}-m)M_{{\rm R}_{j}}^{-1}{}

\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{0}\partial_{0}+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{1}\partial_{1}+\cdots+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j-1}\partial_{j-1}-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j}\partial_{j}+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{j+1}\partial_{j+1}+\cdots+\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt\Gamma^{d-1}\partial_{d-1}-m.

\displaystyle-\Gamma^{j},{}

\displaystyle M_{{\rm R}_{j}}\Gamma^{i}M_{{\rm R}_{j}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{i},\;\;\;i=1,\dots,j-1,j+1,\dots,d-1.

For

V
Mass term

In this section, we determine the maximal number of linearly independent Dirac and Majorana mass terms and discuss how the Dirac and Majorana mass terms break the symmetries C, R, or T.

V.1
Dirac mass term

In this subsection, we will determine the maximal number of linearly independent Dirac mass terms. We will use Definition
II.4
for Dirac fermions, namely a Dirac fermion is a map from the Minkowski spacetime
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1,1}
to the complex spin representation
S
of the spin group
{\rm Spin}_{d-1,1}
. Since the complex representations of
{\rm Spin}_{d-1,1}
are the same as the complex representations of the spin group
{\rm Spin}(d)
, we will regard
S
as the complex spin representation of
{\rm Spin}(d)
. The main reference for this subsection is
Bröcker and tom
Dieck 1985
.

Proposition V.1
.

(
(
Bröcker and tom
Dieck 1985
, Proposition VI.6.1)
)
The group
{\rm Spin}(2n)
has as fundamental system of weights

\displaystyle\lambda_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{1}+\cdots+e_{\nu},\;\;\;1\leq\nu\leq n-2,{}

\displaystyle\partial_{n}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n-1}\pm e_{n}),

(200)

where
e_{\nu}
is the
\nu
-th standard unit vector of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
The group
{\rm Spin}(2n+1)
has as fundamental system of weights

\displaystyle\lambda_{\nu},1\leq\nu\leq n-1,{}

\displaystyle\partial_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{n}).

(201)

These fundamental weights are dominant for fundamental representations

\displaystyle\wedge^{1},\wedge^{2},\dots,\wedge^{n-2},S_{+}^{n},S_{-}^{n}\;\;\;\text{of }{\rm Spin}(2n),\;\;\;\text{and}{}

\displaystyle\wedge^{1},\wedge^{2},\dots,\wedge^{n-1},S^{n}\;\;\;\text{of }{\rm Spin}(2n+1).

(202)

The representations
\wedge^{\nu}
arise from the representations of
{\rm SO}(d)
with identical notation via the projection
\rho:{\rm Spin}(d)\to{\rm SO}(d)
. In other words,
\wedge^{\nu}{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is the
\nu
-th exterior power of the
d

{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, we also shorthand the notation
\wedge^{\nu}{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
as
\wedge^{\nu}
. The representations
S
,
S_{+}
, and
S_{-}
are the half-spin representations of
{\rm Spin}(d)
.

The irreducible representations of
{\rm Spin}(2n+1)
with dominant weights
2\partial
is denoted by
\wedge^{n}
.
The irreducible representations of
{\rm Spin}(2n)
with dominant weights
2\partial^{+}
,

\partial^{+}+\partial^{-}

\wedge_{+}^{n}
,
\wedge_{-}^{n}

\wedge^{n-1}

The Dirac mass term can be defined as a homomorphism of representations from
S\otimes S
to
1^{{\mathbb{C}}}
(the trivial complex representation) where
S
is the complex spin representation of
{\rm Spin}(d-1,1)
and
d
is the spacetime dimension. If

is odd,
\operatorname{Hom}(S\otimes S,1^{{\mathbb{C}}})={\mathbb{C}}

d
is even,
\operatorname{Hom}(S\otimes S,1^{{\mathbb{C}}})={\mathbb{C}}^{2}
because
S\otimes S
contains two trivial summands, so there are two linearly independent Dirac mass terms.

The following results are consequences of
(
Bröcker and tom
Dieck 1985
, Theorem VI.6.2)
.
If
S
is a complex spin representation of
{\rm Spin}(d)
and

\displaystyle S\otimes S=1\oplus\wedge^{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\wedge^{n-1}\oplus\wedge^{n}.

(203)

Since
S\otimes S
contains only one trivial summand, there is only one linearly independent Dirac mass term.

If
S
is a complex spin representation of
{\rm Spin}(d)
and
d=2n
is even, then
S=S_{+}\oplus S_{-}
and

\displaystyle S_{+}\otimes S_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\wedge^{n}_{+}\oplus\wedge^{n-2}\oplus\wedge^{n-4}\oplus\cdots,{}

\displaystyle S_{+}\otimes S_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\wedge^{n-1}\oplus\wedge^{n-3}\oplus\wedge^{n-5}\oplus\cdots,{}

\displaystyle S_{-}\otimes S_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\wedge^{n}_{-}\oplus\wedge^{n-2}\oplus\wedge^{n-4}\oplus\cdots.

(204)

The sums end in
\wedge^{4}\oplus\wedge^{2}\oplus 1
or
\wedge^{3}\oplus\wedge^{1}
.
If
n

and

n

S_{+}\otimes S_{-}
and

We can explicitly construct the Dirac mass terms.

V.2
Majorana mass term

In this subsection, we determine the maximal number of linearly independent Majorana mass terms.

The Majorana mass term can be defined as a homomorphism of representations from
S_{{\rm C}}\otimes S_{{\rm C}}
to
1^{{\mathbb{R}}}
(the trivial real representation) where

S

{\rm Spin}(d-1,1)

By Definition
II.8

\psi

\psi=M_{{\rm C}}\psi^{*}

If
M_{{\rm C}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\eta M_{{\rm C}}

\displaystyle(\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma^{0}\psi)^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma^{0}\psi^{*}{}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\psi^{\dagger}M_{{\rm C}}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma^{0}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}\psi{}

\displaystyle\eta\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma^{0}\psi.

(\psi\psi^{\prime})^{*}=-\psi^{*}\psi^{\prime*}

\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}

In the second equality, we have used the Majorana condition
\psi=M_{{\rm C}}\psi^{*}

In the third equality, we have used
M_{{\rm C}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\eta M_{{\rm C}}
and
M_{{\rm C}}\Gamma^{0}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}=-\Gamma^{0}

\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma^{0}\psi

\displaystyle-\psi^{\mathsf{T}}(-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Gamma^{0*}\Gamma^{1*}\cdot\Gamma^{d-1*}\psi^{*}{}

\displaystyle-\psi^{\dagger}M_{{\rm C}}^{\mathsf{T}}(-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Gamma^{0*}\Gamma^{1*}\cdot\Gamma^{d-1*}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}\psi{}

\displaystyle-\psi^{\dagger}\eta M_{{\rm C}}(-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Gamma^{0*}\Gamma^{1*}\cdot\Gamma^{d-1*}M_{{\rm C}}^{-1}\psi{}

\displaystyle-(-\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{i}\hskip 1.0pt)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\eta\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma^{0}\Gamma^{1}\cdots\Gamma^{d-1}\psi{}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\frac{d}{2}}\eta\psi^{\dagger}{\rm diag}(-I,I)\psi

In the second equality, we have used the fact that

has to be massless while the fermion in the bulk has to be massive.
The two possibilities of the C-R-T fractionalization with the canonical CRT for Dirac fermions in even spacetime dimensions
d
are reduced to the two possibilities of the C-R-T fractionalization with the canonical CRT for Dirac fermions in odd spacetime dimensions
d-1
such that the C-R-T fractionalization with the canonical CRT for Dirac fermions in even spacetime dimensions
d
and its domain wall dimensional reduction are the same.
The R-T fractionalization for Majorana fermions in spacetime dimensions
d=4\mod 8
is reduced to the R-T fractionalization for Majorana fermions in spacetime dimensions
d-1=3\mod 8
.
The first choice of the R-T fractionalization for Majorana fermions in spacetime dimensions
d=2\mod 8
is reduced to the R-T fractionalization for Majorana fermions in spacetime dimensions
d-1=1\mod 8
. Only the first choice works because the first choice is for the massive case and the fermion in the bulk has to be massive.

We discuss possible further domain wall dimensional reduction of fermions in Fig.
5
. We may combine the left and right Majorana-Weyl or Weyl fermions into a Majorana or Dirac fermion. We may also identify the Majorana and Weyl fermions in spacetime dimensions
d=4\mod 8
.
For
d=0\mod 4
, the matrix of the charge conjugation is block-off-diagonal in the Weyl representation (see Table
1
and App.
A
), namely
M_{{\rm C}}=\begin{pmatrix}&M\\
-M&\end{pmatrix}
for some matrix
M
.
So a Majorana fermion is of the form
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}

where
\psi_{R}=-M\psi_{L}^{*}
and
\psi_{L}=M\psi_{R}^{*}
.
Therefore, a Majorana fermion can be identified with a Weyl fermion for
d=0\mod 4
if and only if
MM^{*}=-1
. In fact, for
d=2k+2
(
k
odd),
M=\eta_{{\rm C}}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{4}\cdots\gamma^{2k}
(see Table
1
and App.
A
), so

\displaystyle MM^{*}=\gamma^{2}\gamma^{4}\cdots\gamma^{2k}(\gamma^{2}\gamma^{4}\cdots\gamma^{2k})^{*}=(-1)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}.

(248)

Therefore,
MM^{*}=-1
if and only if
d=4\mod 8
. That is, a Majorana fermion can be identified with a Weyl fermion for
d=4\mod 8
.
Therefore, the domain wall dimensional reduction of fermions may be performed continuously.
