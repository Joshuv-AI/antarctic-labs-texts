Abstract
We propose and rigorously analyse semi- and fully discrete discontinuous Galerkin methods for an initial and boundary value problem describing inertial viscoelasticity in terms of elastic and viscoelastic stress components, and with mixed boundary conditions. The arbitrary-order spatial discretisation imposes strongly the symmetry of the stress tensor, and it is combined with a Newmark trapezoidal rule as time-advancing scheme. We establish stability and convergence properties, and the theoretical findings are further confirmed via illustrative numerical simulations in 2D and 3D.

1
Introduction

Scope and related work.

Viscoelastic models can be used to describe a large class of conventional and unconventional materials with time-dependent mechanical behaviour, including polymers and elastomers, metals at high temperature, and, notably, some types of biological tissues (comprising extracellular matrix, cells, cell clusters, and so on)
[
20
]
. The key constituents of collagen and elastin inherently exhibit viscoelastic and elastic characteristics (a viscoelastic material will eventually return to its original shape upon the removal of any deforming force). The combination of these two properties is observed in many other materials under mechanical loads
[
31
]
, and they are utilised in a wide range of applications to damp mechanical shocks, to attenuate resonant vibrations, and to control noise propagation. Viscoelastic material laws are defined fitting tests of important phenomena such as creep compliance, rate-dependency of stress, hysteresis, and stress-relaxation. One of the simplest models involving strain history in the constitutive equations is Zener’s (or
standard linear
) model in viscoelasticity
[
36
]
, which is able to replicate creep-recovery and stress-relaxation
[
31
]
. It consists of a spring and a Maxwell component in parallel, where, in turn, the Maxwell component is an assemblage of one spring and one viscous element (dashpot) in serial. As in, e.g.,
[
10
,
15
]
, based on the Boltzmann superposition principle, it is possible to use the Volterra integral form of the typical constitutive law for Zener’s model to eliminate the stress and formulate the viscoelastic system as an integro-differential problem written only in terms of displacement. Pure displacement formulations have been also studied from the numerical analysis viewpoint, and a number of contributions are available regarding continuous and discontinuous Galerkin methods including, for instance,
[
11
,
19
,
26
,
27
,
32
]
. Even if the acceleration term endows the displacement with additional time regularity, the analysis is still far from trivial.

The analysis of viscoelasticity based only on a differential representation is also feasible. It suffices to consider a dual-mixed framework where the dual variable, the stress, enters the system together with the primal unknown (displacement). To the authors’ knowledge, this has been first proposed in
[
6
]
, introducing a stress splitting into elastic and viscoelastic contributions and focusing on first-order approximation of stress and using special grids. The approach was later extended in
[
22
,
28
,
29
,
35
]
to high-order and to more general finite element discretisations. The analysis of these formulations is based on a dynamical system approach since the systems are of first-order type involving stress and velocity. In contrast, here we follow the methods advanced in
[
12
,
23
]
, where one re-formulates the problem as a second-order hyperbolic PDE. This is achieved by using the momentum balance to remove the acceleration, leading to a second-order in time of grad-div type written solely in terms of the Cauchy stress, which is separated into elastic and viscoelastic parts.

An important issue in the construction of stable mixed methods for elasticity is the preservation of symmetry for the Cauchy stress. Starting from the foundational work
[
5
]
, there has been an abundant body of developments in the design and analysis of conforming mixed finite elements on simplicial and rectangular meshes for both 2D and 3D; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
18
]
. However, even if the simultaneous imposition of H(div)-conformity and strong symmetry of stress is possible, it typically entails a very large number of degrees of freedom and schemes that are not trivial to implement using standard finite element libraries. The usual ways to overcome this difficulty consist in either, (a) maintaining H(div)-conformity and relaxing the symmetry constraint, which comes at the expense of adding the rotation tensor as additional field variable playing the role of a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the angular momentum conservation constraint, see for example
[
5
,
7
,
16
]
and the references therein; or (b) to renounce H(div)-conformity and use non-conforming or DG approximations as in, e.g.,
[
4
,
17
,
34
]
.

Motivated by the ability of DG methods to handle efficiently
hp
-adaptive strategies and to facilitate the implementation of high order methods, we opt herein for an H(div)-based interior penalty method to solve the standard linear solid model of viscoelasticity. The space discretisation strategy amounts to approximate each stress component by symmetric tensors with piecewise polynomial entries of arbitrary degree
k\geq 1
, in 2D and 3D. We point out that our continuous formulation only asks the Cauchy stress (the sum of the elastic and viscoelastic stress components) to be H(div)-conforming. Consequently, the present approach only penalises the jumps of the normal total stress on the internal facets, therefore requiring fewer coupling conditions between the local degrees of freedom than penalising each stress component separately. We show that the resulting mixed DG semi-discrete scheme is robust and accurate for general domains and boundary conditions, and for heterogeneous media. Additionally, we prove that the fully discrete scheme relying on the classical second-order implicit Newmark method is stable and convergent. Finally, under piecewise regularity assumptions on the exact solution of the problem, we derive optimal asymptotic error estimates in a suitable H(div)-DG norm.

Outline.

The contents of this paper have been organized in the following manner. The remainder of this section contains preliminary notational conventions and definition of useful functional spaces. Section
2
presents the precise definition of Zener’s model problem along with the derivation of its weak formulation in mixed form and recalling its unique solvability. Preliminary definitions and auxiliary tools needed for the analysis in discontinuous finite dimensional spaces are collected in Section
3
. The precise definition and the analysis of convergence for a semi-discrete mixed method are detailed in Section
4
, and the fully discrete case is treated in Section
5
. Several numerical results are presented in Section
6
, confirming the expected rates of convergence for different parameter sets including the nearly incompressible regime, and illustrating the use of the method in relatively simple problems of applicative relevance.

Recurrent notation and Sobolev spaces.

We denote the space of real matrices of order
d\times d
by
\mathbb{M}
, and let
\mathbb{S}:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{M};\ \boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathtt{t}}\}
be the subspace of symmetric matrices, where
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathtt{t}}:=(\tau_{ji})
stands for the transpose of
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{ij})
. The component-wise inner product of two matrices
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{M}
is defined by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\tau}:=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_{ij}\tau_{ij}
.

Let
D
be a polyhedral Lipschitz bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=2,3)
, with boundary
\partial D
. Along this paper we convene to apply all differential operators row-wise. Hence, given a tensorial function
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:D\to\mathbb{M}
and a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set the divergence
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma}:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}:D\to\mathbb{M}
, and the linearised strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\Omega\to\mathbb{S}
as

(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma})_{i}:=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}\,,\quad(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})_{ij}:=\partial_{j}u_{i}\,,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathtt{t}}\right].

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{s}(D,E)
stands for the usual Hilbertian Sobolev space of functions with domain
D
and values in E, where
E
is either
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{M}
. In the case
E=\mathbb{R}
we simply write
H^{s}(D)
. The norm of
H^{s}(D,E)
is denoted
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{s,D}
and the corresponding semi-norm
|\cdot|_{s,D}
, indistinctly for
E=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{M}
. We use the convention
H^{0}(D,E):=L^{2}(D,E)
and let
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
be the inner product in
L^{2}(D,E)
, for
E=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{M}
, namely,

(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{D}:=\int_{D}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\ \forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})_{D}:=\int_{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\tau},\ \forall\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(D,\mathbb{M}).

(1.1)

The space of tensors in
L^{2}(D,\mathbb{S})
with divergence in
L^{2}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is denoted
H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,D,\mathbb{S})
. We denote the corresponding norm
\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\rVert^{2}_{H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,D)}:=\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\rVert_{0,D}^{2}+\lVert\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau}\rVert^{2}_{0,D}
. Let
\boldsymbol{n}
be the outward unit normal vector to
\partial D
. The Green formula

(\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}))_{D}+(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{v})_{D}=\int_{\partial D}\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d}),

can be used to extend the normal trace operator
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{D},\mathbb{S})\ni\boldsymbol{\tau}\to(\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{\partial\Omega})\boldsymbol{n}
to a linear continuous mapping
(\cdot|_{\partial\Omega})\boldsymbol{n}:\,H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,D,\mathbb{S})\to H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the dual of
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Sobolev spaces for time dependent problems.
Since we will deal with a space-time domain problem, besides the Sobolev spaces defined above, we need to introduce spaces of functions acting on a bounded time interval
(0,T)
and with values in a separable Hilbert space
V
, whose norm is denoted here by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{V}
. In particular, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
L^{p}(V)
is the space of classes of functions
f:\ (0,T)\to V
that are Böchner-measurable and such that
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(V)}<\infty
, with

\lVert f\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}(V)}:=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert f(t)\rVert_{V}^{p}\,\text{d}t\quad\hbox{for $1\leq p<\infty$},\quad\hbox{and}\quad\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)}:=\esssup_{[0,T]}\lVert f(t)\rVert_{V}.

We use the notation
\mathcal{C}^{0}(V)
for the Banach space consisting of all continuous functions
f:\ [0,T]\to V
. More generally, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{C}^{k}(V)
denotes the subspace of
\mathcal{C}^{0}(V)
of all functions
f
with (strong) derivatives
\frac{\text{d}^{j}f}{\text{d}t^{j}}
in
\mathcal{C}^{0}(V)
for all
1\leq j\leq k
. In what follows, we will use indistinctly the notations
\dot{f}:=\frac{\text{d}f}{\text{d}t}
and
\ddot{f}:=\frac{\text{d}^{2}f}{\text{d}t^{2}}
to express the first and second derivatives with respect to
t
. Furthermore, we consider the Sobolev space

W^{1,\infty}(V):=\left\{f:\ \exists g\in L^{\infty}(V)\ \text{and}\ \exists f_{0}\in V\ \text{such that}\ f(t)=f_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\,\text{d}s\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\right\},

and define the space
W^{k,\infty}(V)
recursively for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Throughout this paper, we shall use the letter
C
to denote a generic positive constant independent of the mesh size
h
and the time discretisation parameter
\Delta t
, that may stand for different values at its different occurrences. Moreover, given any positive expressions
X
and
Y
depending on
h
and
\Delta t
, the notation
X\,\lesssim\,Y
means that
X\,\leq\,C\,Y
.

2
A mixed variational formulation of the Zener model

We aim to study the dynamical equation of motion

\rho\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$},

for a viscoelastic body represented by a polyhedral Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
). Here,
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the displacement field,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{S}
is the Cauchy stress tensor and
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the body force. We assume that the linearised strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})
is related to the stress tensor through Zener’s constitutive law for viscoelasticity (see
[
31
]
):

\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\omega\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\omega\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}})\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$}.

(2.1)

The symmetric and positive definite fourth-order tensors
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are such that
\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C}
is also positive definite in order to guarantee that the system is dissipative. We assume that the relaxation time
\omega\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is positive and bounded away from zero:
\omega(x)\geq\omega_{0}>0

a.e.
in
\Omega
. Moreover, we assume that there exists a polygonal/polyhedral disjoint partition
\big\{\Omega_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,J\big\}
of
\bar{\Omega}
such that
\rho|_{\Omega_{j}}:=\rho_{j}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,J
and let
\rho^{+}:=\max_{j}\rho_{j}
and
\rho^{-}:=\min_{j}\rho_{j}
.

We assume mixed loading boundary conditions: the structure is clamped (
\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{0}
) on
\Gamma_{D}\times(0,T]
where the boundary subset
\Gamma_{D}\subset\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
is of positive surface measure, and free of stress (
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\boldsymbol{n}=\mathbf{0}
) on
\Gamma_{N}\times(0,T]
, where
\Gamma_{N}:=\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{D}
. By
\boldsymbol{n}
we denote the exterior unit normal vector on
\Gamma
. Finally, we assume the initial conditions:

\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\quad\text{in $\Omega$},\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\quad\text{in $\Omega$},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}\quad\text{in $\Omega$}.

(2.2)

With the purpose of having the (total) stress tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as a primary unknown, we additively decompose this variable into a purely elastic component
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})
and a viscoelastic component
\boldsymbol{\zeta}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, which allows us to deduce from the constitutive law (
2.1
) that

\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\tfrac{1}{\omega}\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}).

Hence, adopting the notations
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{C}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{V}:=(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{C})^{-1}
, the model problem can be recast in terms of
\boldsymbol{u}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})&=\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$},\\[1.07639pt]
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})&=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{\mathtt{t}}\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$},\\[1.07639pt]
\mathcal{A}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}&=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}})\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$},\\[1.07639pt]
\mathcal{V}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}&=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}})\quad\text{in $\Omega\times(0,T]$},\\[1.07639pt]
\boldsymbol{u}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{D}\times(0,T]$},\\[1.07639pt]
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{n}&=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma_{N}\times(0,T]$}.\end{split}

(2.3)

One readily notes that the material law splits now into two parts, one for each component of the total stress. The main unknown consists in a pair of tensors
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M})
such that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega,\mathbb{S})
. The traction boundary condition on
\Gamma_{N}
has to be included in an essential manner, for which we require the following closed subspace of
H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega,\mathbb{S})

H_{N}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega,\mathbb{S}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega,\mathbb{S});\quad\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\quad\text{$\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})$,\, $\boldsymbol{v}|_{\Gamma_{D}}=\mathbf{0}$}\right\},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma}
holds for the duality pairing between
H^{1/2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We then consider the energy space

\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}:=\Big\{(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}):\ \boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H_{N}(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits,\Omega,\mathbb{S})\Big\},

endowed with the Hilbertian norm

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}}:=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}.

In what follows, when
D=\Omega
in (
1.1
), we simply denote the
L^{2}
-inner product by
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
. We consider an arbitrary
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}
, test the third and fourth rows of (
2.3
) with
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and add the resulting equations to get

(\mathcal{A}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\eta})+\Big(\mathcal{V}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\tfrac{1}{\omega}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),\boldsymbol{\tau}\Big)=\Big(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\Big)=\Big(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\Big),

(2.4)

where the last identity follows from the symmetry of
\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
Next, we integrate by parts in the right-hand side of (
2.4
) and take into account the boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{N}\times(0,T]
to obtain

(\mathcal{A}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\eta})+\Big(\mathcal{V}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\tfrac{1}{\omega}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),\boldsymbol{\tau}\Big)=-\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big).

(2.5)

Substituting back
\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}=\rho^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol{f}+\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\big)
into (
2.5
) yields

A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\tau})+\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)=-\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big).

for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}
,
where

A\Big((\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big):=(\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\eta})+(\mathcal{V}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\tau}),\quad(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\,(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}).

It is important to notice that, as a consequence of our hypotheses on
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, the bilinear form
A
is symmetric, bounded and coercive, i.e., there exist positive constants
M
and
\alpha
, depending only on
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, such that

\displaystyle\Big|A\Big((\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq M\|(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|_{0,\Omega}\|(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}),

(2.6)

\displaystyle A\Big((\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\|(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\qquad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}).

(2.7)

We let

\mathfrak{L}_{\text{sym}}^{2}:=\left\{(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M});\ \boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{S})\right\},

and consider the following variational formulation of (
2.3
): Given
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we look for
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}})\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathfrak{L}_{\text{sym}}^{2})
satisfying, for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\Big\{A\Big((\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big\}+\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)

\displaystyle=-\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big),

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(0),\boldsymbol{\zeta}(0))=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}),\qquad(\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0),\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(0))

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}),

(2.8)

where
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}
and
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1})\in\mathfrak{L}_{\text{sym}}^{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}),\quad\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}:=\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}),\quad\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}:=\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}-\tfrac{1}{\omega}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}.

Classical techniques for second order evolution problems with energy methods
[
9
,
25
]
have been successfully applied in
[
12
, Theorem 5.2, Lemma 5.1]
to prove the well-posedness of (
2
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{f}\in H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then, problem (
2
) admits a unique solution. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that,

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})(t)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}}+\max_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert(\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})(t)\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}\leq C\Big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))}+\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}}+\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1})\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}\Big).

3
Finite element spaces and auxiliary results

We consider a sequence
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h}
of shape regular meshes that subdivide the domain
\bar{\Omega}
into triangles/tetrahedra
K
of diameter
h_{K}
. The parameter
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\{h_{K}\}
represents the mesh size of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We assume that
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is aligned with the partition
\bar{\Omega}=\cup_{j=1}^{J}\bar{\Omega}_{j}
and that
\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j}):=\left\{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h};\ K\subset\Omega_{j}\right\}
is a shape regular mesh of
\bar{\Omega}_{j}
for all
j=1,\cdots,J
and all
h
.
For all
s\geq 0
, we consider the broken Sobolev space

H^{s}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j}):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega);\ v|_{\Omega_{j}}\in H^{s}(\Omega_{j}),\ \forall j=1,\ldots,J\right\}

corresponding to the partition
\bar{\Omega}=\cup_{j=1}^{J}\bar{\Omega}_{j}
.
Its vectorial and tensorial versions are denoted
H^{s}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{s}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j},\mathbb{M})
, respectively. Similarly, the broken Sobolev space with respect to the subdivision of
\bar{\Omega}
into
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is

H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega,E):\quad\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in H^{s}(K,E)\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},\quad\text{for $E\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{M}\}$}.

For each
\boldsymbol{v}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{K}\}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}:=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K}\}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{M})
the components
\boldsymbol{v}_{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K}
represent the restrictions
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{K}
. When no confusion arises, the subscripts will be dropped.

Hereafter, given an integer
m\geq 0
and a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{P}_{m}(D)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
m
on
D
. We introduce the space

\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\ v|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(K),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}

of piecewise polynomial functions relatively to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We also consider the space
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h},E)
of functions with values in
E
and entries in
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, where
E
is either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{M}
or
\mathbb{S}
.

Let us introduce now notations related to DG approximations of
H(\text{div})
-type spaces. We say that a closed subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is an interior edge/face if
F
has a positive
(d-1)
-dimensional measure and if there are distinct elements
K
and
K^{\prime}
such that
F=\bar{K}\cap\bar{K}^{\prime}
. A closed subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is a boundary edge/face if there exists
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
such that
F
is an edge/face of
K
and
F=\bar{K}\cap\partial\Omega
. We consider the set
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
of interior edges/faces, the set
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
of boundary edges/faces.
We assume that
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
is compatible with the partition
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
in the sense that, if

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}:\,F\subset\Gamma_{D}\right\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}:\,F\subset\Gamma_{N}\right\},

then
\Gamma_{D}=\cup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}}F
and
\Gamma_{N}=\cup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}}F
. We denote

\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N},

and we introduce the set
\mathcal{F}(K):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h};\,F\subset\partial K\}
of edges/faces composing the boundary of
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We will need the space (given on the skeletons of the triangulations
\mathcal{T}_{h}
)
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}):=\bigoplus_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}L^{2}(F)
. Its vector valued version is denoted
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Here again, the components
\boldsymbol{v}_{F}
of
\boldsymbol{v}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{F}\}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
coincide with the restrictions
\boldsymbol{v}|_{F}
. We endow
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the inner product

(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{u}_{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{F}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})

and denote the corresponding norm
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}:=(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}
. From now on,
h_{\mathcal{F}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
is the piecewise constant function defined by
h_{\mathcal{F}}|_{F}:=h_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}
with
h_{F}
denoting the diameter of edge/face
F
.

Given
\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{M})
, with
s>1/2
, we define averages
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and jumps
{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\rrbracket}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}_{F}:=(\boldsymbol{v}_{K}+\boldsymbol{v}_{K^{\prime}})/2\quad\text{and}\quad{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\rrbracket}_{F}:=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K}\boldsymbol{n}_{K}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{n}_{K^{\prime}}\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(K)\cap\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime}),

with the conventions

\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}_{F}:=\boldsymbol{v}_{K}\quad\text{and}\quad{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\rrbracket}_{F}:=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K}\boldsymbol{n}_{K}\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(K),\,\,F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N},

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{K}
is the outward unit normal vector to
\partial K
.

For any
k\geq 1
, we consider
\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h}:=\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{S})
and let
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}(h):=\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}+\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h}
. Given
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}(h)
, we define
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})|_{K}:=\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{K}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K})
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and endow
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}(h)
with the norm

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}(h)}:=\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}}\rrbracket}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}.

(3.1)

We end this section by recalling technical results that will be needed in what follows. We begin with the well-known trace inequality, see for example
[
24
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
such that

h_{K}^{1/2}\lVert v\rVert_{0,\partial K}\leq C\big(\lVert v\rVert_{0,K}+h_{K}\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{0,K}\big),

(3.2)

for all
v\in H^{1}(K)
and all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

It is easy to deduce from (
3.2
) the following discrete trace inequality (see also
[
24
, Proposition 4.1]
)

\left\lVert h^{1/2}_{\mathcal{F}}\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{v\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right\rVert_{0,\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\leq C_{\textup{tr}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}).

(3.3)

The Scott–Zhang like quasi-interpolation operator
\Pi_{h}:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
, obtained in
[
8
]
by applying an
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
followed by an averaging procedure with continuous and piecewise
\mathcal{P}_{k}
range, will be especially useful in the forthcoming analysis. We recall in the next lemma the local approximation properties provided in
[
8
, Theorem 5.2]
. Let us first introduce some notations. For any
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we introduce the subset of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
defined by
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}:=\left\{K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:\,K\cap K^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right\}
and let
D_{K}=\text{interior}\left(\cup_{K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}}K^{\prime}\right)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The quasi-interpolation operator
\Pi_{h}
is invariant in the space
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
and there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
such that

|v-\Pi_{h}v|_{m,K}\leq Ch_{K}^{r-m}|v|_{r,D_{K}},

(3.4)

for all real numbers
0\leq r\leq k+1
, all natural numbers
0\leq m\leq[r]
, all
v\in H^{r}(D_{K})
, and all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Here
[r]
stands for the the largest integer less than or equal to
r
.

We point out that, as a consequence of (
3.4
) and the triangle inequality, it holds

|\Pi_{h}v|_{m,K}\lesssim|v|_{m,D_{K}},

(3.5)

for all natural numbers
0\leq m\leq k+1
, all
v\in H^{m}(D_{K})
, and all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Moreover, it is straightforward to deduce from (
3.4
) and the multiplicative trace inequality (
3.2
) that

h_{K}^{1/2}\lVert v-\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{0,\partial K}+h_{K}^{3/2}\lVert\nabla(v-\Pi_{h}v)\rVert_{0,\partial K}\lesssim h_{K}^{r}|v|_{r,D_{K}},

(3.6)

for all
2\leq r\leq k+1
(
k\geq 1
), all
v\in H^{r}(D_{K})
and all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

We infer from (
3.5
) a global stability property for
\Pi_{h}
on
H^{m}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j})
,
0\leq m\leq k+1
, by taking advantage of the fact that the cardinal
\#(\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K})
of
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}
is uniformly bounded for all
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and all
h
, as a consequence of the shape-regularity of the mesh sequence
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
. Indeed, given
v\in H^{m}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j})
, we let
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}(\Omega_{j}):=\left\{K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j}):\,K\cap K^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\right\}
be the subset of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}
that are contained in
\bar{\Omega}_{j}
and denote
D_{K}^{j}:=\text{interior}\left(\cup_{K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}(\Omega_{j})}K^{\prime}\right)
. It follows from (
3.5
) that

\lVert\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{m,K}\lesssim\lVert v\rVert_{m,D_{K}^{j}},\quad\forall v\in H^{m}(D^{j}_{K}),\,\,0\leq m\leq k+1,\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}(\Omega_{j}).

Summing over
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}(\Omega_{j})
and using that
\#(\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K}(\Omega_{j}))\leq\#(\mathcal{T}_{h}^{K})\leq c
for all
j\leq J
and all
h
, we deduce that

\lVert\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{m,\Omega_{j}}\lesssim\lVert v\rVert_{m,\Omega_{j}},\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,J.

(3.7)

Finally, it follows from a successive application of the discrete trace inequality (
3.3
) and the stability estimate (
3.7
) for
m=1
that

\left\lVert h^{1/2}_{\mathcal{F}}\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\nabla\Pi_{h}v\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right\rVert_{0,\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\leq C_{\textup{tr}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}|\Pi_{h}v|_{1,\Omega_{j}}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{1,\Omega_{j}}\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j}).

(3.8)

In the sequel, we use the same notation for the tensorial version
\Pi_{h}:\,L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{S})\to\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{S})\cap H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{S})
of the quasi-interpolation operator, which is obtained by applying the scalar operator componentwise. It is important to notice that such an operator preserves tensor symmetry. As a consequence of (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) we have the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
such that

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}+\left\lVert\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\big((\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\big)\right\rVert_{0,\Omega}\\
+\left\lVert h_{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\big((\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\big)\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right\rVert_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\leq Ch^{\min\{r,k\}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Big(\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert_{r,\Omega_{j}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert_{r+1,\Omega_{j}}\Big),

(3.9)

for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\text{sym}}
such that
(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\in H^{r}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j},\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M})
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H^{r+1}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
r\geq 1
.

Proof.

Given
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j})
,
1\leq j\leq J
, we obtain from (
3.4
) that

\left\lVert(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert^{2}_{0,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{2\min\{r,k+1\}}(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\rVert^{2}_{r,D^{j}_{K}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert^{2}_{r,D^{j}_{K}}),

(3.10)

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\big((\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{0,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big((\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{0,K}\lesssim h_{K}^{2\min\{r,k\}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert^{2}_{r+1,D^{j}_{K}}.

(3.11)

For the last term in the left-hand side of (
3.9
), we notice that

\left\lVert h_{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits\big((\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\big)\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j})}h_{K}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Big((\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\Big)\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial K},

and (
3.6
) yields

h_{K}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Big((\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})-\Pi_{h}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\Big)\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial K}\lesssim h_{K}^{2\min\{r,k\}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\rVert^{2}_{r+1,D_{K}^{j}},\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j}).

(3.12)

Summing (
3.10
), (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) over
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(\Omega_{j})
and then over
j=1,\ldots,J
and invoking the shape-regularity of the mesh sequence give the result.
∎

4
The semi-discrete DG problem and its convergence analysis

Our DG scheme requires the external force
\boldsymbol{f}
to have traces at the interelement boundaries of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. In order to make hypotheses on the data that are realistic from the practical point of view, we will only assume that
\boldsymbol{f}
is piecewise smooth relatively to the partition
\{\Omega_{j},\,j=1,\ldots,J\}
of
\bar{\Omega}
.

Assumption 4.1
.

The body force satisfies
\boldsymbol{f}\in W^{1,\infty}(H^{1}(\cup_{j}\Omega_{j},\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

We are now in a position to introduce the following discontinuous Galerkin semi-discretisation of (
2
): Find
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h})\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h})
solution of

\displaystyle A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{h},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{h}),(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}),\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Big(\texttt{a}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}=-\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}},\quad

(4.1)

for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\in\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h}
, where
\texttt{a}>0
is a given, sufficiently large parameter.

We assume that the solution
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}(t),\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}(t))
of problem (
4
) is started up with the initial conditions

(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h}(0),\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}(0))=(\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{0}),\quad(\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{h}(0),\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{h}(0))=(\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}).

(4.2)

In this way, the projected error
(\boldsymbol{e}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(t),\boldsymbol{e}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(t)):=(\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{h},\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h})(t)
satisfies, by construction, the vanishing initial conditions:

(\boldsymbol{e}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{e}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})(0)=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\quad\text{and}\quad(\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}^{h}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})(0)=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}).

The convergence analysis of the semi-discrete problem (
4
) requires the following time regularity assumptions on the solution
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})
of (
2
) that are not guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
.

Assumption 4.2
.

The solution
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})
of (
2
) satisfies

i)

(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})\in W^{3,\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega),\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S})
,

ii)

and
\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\in W^{1,\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega),\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We begin by verifying that the DG scheme (
4
) is consistent with problem (
2
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
(i), the solution
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\zeta})
of (
2
) satisfies the identity

\displaystyle A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(\texttt{a}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}=-\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}},

(4.3)

for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\in\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h}
.

Proof.

Let us first notice that the acceleration field
\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}=\tfrac{1}{\rho}\big(\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})+\boldsymbol{f}\big)\in L^{\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
satisfies, by virtue of Assumption
4.2
(i),
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}})=\mathcal{A}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in L^{\infty}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{S}))
. It follows from the boundary condition on
\Gamma_{D}
and Korn’s inequality that
\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\in L^{\infty}(H^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Hence, using that
{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\rrbracket}=\mathbf{0}
yields

\displaystyle A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}-\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(\texttt{a}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}=A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\qquad\quad-\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}-\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)+\Big(\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\tfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}},

(4.4)

for all
(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\in\mathcal{H}^{DG}_{\text{sym},h}
. Now, taking into account that (
\boldsymbol{u}|_{\Gamma_{D}}=\mathbf{0}
),

\displaystyle\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{\partial K}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\boldsymbol{n}_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big(\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\right)_{K}+\left(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\right)_{K}\Big),

and keeping in mind (
2.4
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},\mathop{\mathbf{div}}\nolimits_{h}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)-\Big(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}},{\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}}\rrbracket}\Big)_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}),\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\Big)=-A\Big((\ddot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ddot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\Big)-(\tfrac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{V}\dot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}).

Substituting back the last identity in (
4
) gives the sought consistency result.
∎

The fully discrete scheme and its convergence analysis

Given

[0,T]
with step size

\Delta t:=T/L

\phi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
and for each

we denote
\phi^{k}:=\phi(t_{k})

\phi^{k+\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\phi^{k+1}+\phi^{k}}{2}

\phi^{k-\frac{1}{2}}:=\frac{\phi^{k}+\phi^{k-1}}{2}
, and
\widehat{\phi^{k}}:=\frac{\phi^{k+\frac{1}{2}}+\phi^{k-\frac{1}{2}}}{2}=\frac{\phi^{k-1}+2\phi^{k}+\phi^{k+1}}{4}
. We adopt the same notation for
vector/tensor valued functions. We also introduce the discrete time derivatives

\partial_{t}\phi^{k}:=\frac{\phi^{k+1}-\phi^{k}}{\Delta t},\quad\bar{\partial}_{t}\phi^{k}:=\frac{\phi^{k}-\phi^{k-1}}{\Delta t}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial^{0}_{t}\phi^{k}:=\frac{\phi^{k+1}-\phi^{k-1}}{2\Delta t}.

In what follows we utilise the Newmark trapezoidal rule for the time discretisation of
(
4
)-(
4.2
). Namely, for
k=1,\ldots,L-1
,
we seek

Example 2: Plane stress viscoelasticity in perforated plates.

Our next example simulates the transient behaviour of perforated plates in viscoelastic vs purely elastic cases, focusing on plane stress conditions. We adapt the configuration proposed in
[
14
]
to Zener’s rheological model and take Hookean constitutive laws for the elastic spring and viscous dashpot stresses selecting the density
\rho=1
Kg/m
3
, characteristic time
\omega=0.15
s, stabilisation parameter with
\texttt{a}^{*}=10
, and Young moduli and Poisson ratios:

E_{\mathcal{C}}=30\,\text{KPa},\quad\nu_{\mathcal{C}}=0.3,\quad E_{\mathcal{D}}=40\,\text{KPa},\quad\nu_{\mathcal{D}}=0.49.

The domain is a square plate with a circular hole of radius 0.25 m:
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}\setminus B_{0.25}(0.5,0.5)\,\text{m}^{2}
and the domain boundaries are split into the bottom segment
\Gamma_{D}

on which we impose zero displacements, and the remainder of the boundary
\Gamma_{N}
where we prescribe normal stresses. On the top segment we set a time-dependent traction

\boldsymbol{g}_{N}=\bigl(0,-\frac{1}{2}H(t\leq 1)\sin(\pi t/5)\bigr)^{\tt t}
, where
H
is the Heaviside function (meaning that a sinusoidal load is applied on the top edge until
t=1
s and then it is suddenly released), and on the vertical and circle sub-boundaries we set a traction free condition
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
We use the numerical method in (
5.1
) with polynomial degree
k=2
(yielding an overall quadratic order of convergence) and consider a final time of
T=15
s, with a time step of
\Delta t=0.1
s. The unstructured mesh contains 23’275 triangular elements. We plot in the left panels of Figure
6.2
three snapshots (at times 0.5 s, 1 s, 1.5 s) of the elastic and viscous stress magnitudes portrayed on the deformed domain (where the displacement is magnified by a factor of 10 to assist a better visualisation). For comparison we also plot (in the third row) solution snapshots for the purely elastic case (and where, after removing the load, the body immediately goes back to the undeformed configuration). Moreover, we show in the rightmost three panels, the evolution (only until
t=7
s) of axial stress in the vertical direction (in KPa) and vertical displacements (in m) at a point located between the circular hole and the top sub-boundary, as well as the dynamic behaviour of the
L^{2}-
norms of stresses and displacement. The results illustrate the expected dissipation property.

Example 4: Localisation of viscous stresses in a multilayered cross-section of spinal cord.
To conclude this section we consider a simple viscoelastic model for a segment of cervical spinal cord (consisting of white and grey matter), surrounded by the pia mater (represented as a thin layer of elastic material). The problem setup mimics indentation tests as in, e.g.,
[
30
,
37
]
, which in turn replicate problems arising due to degenerative factors.
We only take a transversal cross-section of approximately 13 mm in maximal diameter, and in this case the indentation region is simply a curved subset of the anterior part of the pia mater, having length 4 mm. The geometry and unstructured mesh have been generated from the images in
[
33
]
using the mesh manipulator GMSH
[
13
]
. In this region we will impose, as in the previous tests, a traction
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{n}=(0,-P)^{-\tt t}
, with
P
a given time-dependent pressure profile with maximal amplitude 650 Pa. The posterior part of the pia mater (a sub-boundary of length 4 mm) will be considered as a rigid posterior support and therefore zero displacement boundary conditions will be prescribed. The remainder of the boundary (of the pia mater) is taken as stress-free (see the sketch in Figure
6.4
, top left).

An advantage of the DG-based formulation advanced herein is that it permits us to readily consider discontinuous material parameters.
For the three different layers of the domain we use the following values for Young modulus and Poisson ratio (values from
[
21
,
33
,
37
]
, see also
[
20
,
30
]
),

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{pia}}=2300\,\text{Pa},\quad\nu^{\mathrm{pia}}=0.3,\quad E^{\mathrm{white}}_{\mathcal{C}}=840\,\text{Pa},\quad\nu^{\mathrm{white}}_{\mathcal{C}}=0.479,\quad E^{\mathrm{white}}_{\mathcal{D}}=2030\,\text{Pa},\quad\nu^{\mathrm{white}}_{\mathcal{D}}=0.49,

\displaystyle\quad E^{\mathrm{grey}}_{\mathcal{C}}=1600\,\text{Pa},\quad\nu^{\mathrm{grey}}_{\mathcal{C}}=0.49,\quad E^{\mathrm{grey}}_{\mathcal{D}}=2030\,\text{Pa},\quad\nu^{\mathrm{grey}}_{\mathcal{D}}=0.49.

Note that in
[
37
]
the pia matter is considered elastic and the white and grey matter subdomains are considered hyperelastic, in
[
30
]
there is only pia mater and homogeneous spinal cord (all visco-hyperelastic), in
[
21
]
the spinal cord is homogeneous and linear viscoelastic, whereas in
[
33
]
a poroelastic model has been used for all layers. Here the elastic behaviour of the pia mater is modelled with a much smaller value than in the rest of the domain,
\omega^{\mathrm{pia}}=1/1000\,\text{s}<\omega^{\mathrm{white,grey}}=1/6.7
s). A fixed time step
\Delta t=0.01
s is used and we run the simulation until
T=1
s. The top-centre and top-right panels of Figure
6.4
shows a sample of deformed configuration and distribution of total stress and elastic stress magnitude at time
t=0.35
s.
For this problem we investigate numerically the decay of the elastic energy
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})
, which, using the definition of viscous and elastic stress contributions, can be written as

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{elast}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}):(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}).

In addition, we also track the value of the energy norm defined in (
3.1
). These quantities are plotted in the middle and bottom rows of Figure
6.4
together with transients of the principal stresses and displacements at three different locations in the domain layers. The deformation vs load, as well as the stress distribution on the white and grey matter regions is qualitatively consistent with the behaviour reported in
[
37
]
. In the pia mater, as expected, only the elastic stress is visible.
