Multiplicative arithmetic functions and the generalized Ewens measure
Dor Elboim, Ofir Gorodetsky

Abstract
Random integers, sampled uniformly from [ 1 , x ] [1,x] , share similarities with random permutations, sampled uniformly from S n S_{n} . These similarities include the Erdős–Kac theorem on the distribution of the number of prime factors of a random integer, and Billingsley’s theorem on the largest prime factors of a random integer. In this paper we extend this analogy to non-uniform distributions. Given a multiplicative function α : ℕ → ℝ ≥ 0 \alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} , one may associate with it a measure on the integers in [ 1 , x ] [1,x] , where n n is sampled with probability proportional to the value α ⁡ ( n ) \alpha(n) . Analogously, given a sequence { θ i } i ≥ 1 \{\theta_{i}\}_{i\geq 1} of non-negative reals, one may associate with it a measure on S n S_{n} that assigns to a permutation a probability proportional to a product of weights over the cycles of the permutation. This measure is known as the generalized Ewens measure. We study the case where the mean value of α \alpha over primes tends to some positive θ \theta , as well as the weights α ⁡ ( p ) ≈ ( log ⁡ p ) γ \alpha(p)\approx(\log p)^{\gamma} . In both cases, we obtain results in the integer setting which are in agreement with those in the permutation setting.

Introduction

The analogy between permutations and integers is a well-established one, see the surveys
[
6
, Ch. 1]
and
[
28
]
. The analogy leads to advancements both in permutations and in integers, see e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
17
,
18
]
. This analogy always involves comparing a uniformly drawn integer in
[1,x]
and a uniformly drawn permutation from
S_{n}
, where
n\approx\log x
. Our results suggest that the analogy persists even when the chosen measures are not uniform.

We begin with setup and notation.
Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group on
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Given
\pi\in S_{n}
, we denote by
\ell_{1}(\pi)\geq\ell_{2}(\pi)\geq\ldots
the lengths of the disjoint cycles of
\pi
, arranged in non-increasing order. They satisfy

\ell_{1}(\pi)+\ell_{2}(\pi)+\ldots=n.

(1.1)

We let
C_{i}(\pi)
be the number of cycles of
\pi
of length
i
and denote by
C(\pi)
the number of cycles in
\pi
.

In recent years, there has been significant activity in the study of permutations sampled according to
cycle weights

[
58
,
54
,
37
,
10
,
41
,
44
,
43
,
22
,
13
,
15
,
51
,
49
]
; this model is related to the study of the quantum
Bose gas in statistical mechanics, see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
19
]
. To state the model, let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
be non-negative reals (not all zero).
The probability of a permutation
\pi
with respect to the weights
\theta_{i}
is defined to be

\mathbb{P}_{n,\theta_{i}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{h_{n}n!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{C_{i}(\pi)}=\frac{1}{h_{n}n!}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\pi\\
\text{cycle of }\pi\end{subarray}}\theta_{|c|}

(1.2)

where
h_{n}
is the normalization constant, known as the partition function, given by

h_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}^{C_{i}(\pi)}.

(1.3)

We let
\pi_{n,\theta_{i}}
be the random permutation whose probability distribution is (
1.2
). The measure
\mathbb{P}_{n,\theta_{i}}
is called a generalized Ewens measure.

We now describe an analogous measure on the positive integers up to
x
, which is the main object of study in this paper.
Given a positive integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
\mathcal{p}_{1}(m)\geq\mathcal{p}_{2}(m)\geq\ldots
the prime factors of
m
(repeated according to their multiplicity), arranged in non-increasing order. We have

\log\mathcal{p}_{1}(m)+\log\mathcal{p}_{2}(m)+\ldots=\log m.

(1.4)

We denote by
\Omega(m)
the number of prime factors of
m
, counted with multiplicity.
If
p^{k}\mid n
and
p^{k+1}\nmid n
, we write
p^{k}\mid\mid n
. This
k
is known as the multiplicity of
p
in
n
, and is denoted
\nu_{p}(n)
.

A function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is called multiplicative if
\alpha(1)=1
and
\alpha(nm)=\alpha(n)\alpha(m)
for every coprime
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Given a non-negative multiplicative function
\alpha
, we define a measure on the positive integers up to
x
by

\mathbb{P}_{x,\alpha}(m)=\frac{1}{S(x)}\alpha(m)=\frac{1}{S(x)}\prod_{p^{k}\mid\mid m}\alpha(p^{k})

(1.5)

where the product is over (maximal) prime powers dividing
m
, and
S(x)
is the normalization constant

S(x)=\sum_{m\leq x}\alpha(m).

(1.6)

We let

N_{x}=N_{x,\alpha}

be the random integer whose probability distribution is (
1.5
).
In this paper we consider two different families of multiplicative measures on the integers, and compare our results with corresponding generalized Ewens measures.

1.1
Constant mean value

We consider multiplicative functions
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying the following two conditions for some
\theta>0
,
d>-1
,
{a}\in(0,1)
,
\eta\in(0,1/2]
and
{r}\in(0,2)
:

\displaystyle\text{(I) }\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\alpha(p)\log p}{p^{d}}=\theta x+O\left(\frac{x}{\log^{{a}}x}\right),

(1.7)

\displaystyle\text{(II) }\frac{\alpha(p)}{p^{d}}=O(p^{1/2-\eta}),\quad\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{dk}}=O({r}^{k})\mbox{ for all $k\geq 2$.}

(1.8)

Here
p
denotes a prime number. Recall that the prime number theorem says that
\sum_{p\leq x}\log p\sim x
. Thus, (
1.7
) should be interpreted as
\alpha(p)/p^{d}
being, on average, of size
\theta
, and it is a common condition in multiplicative number theory. Condition (
1.8
) is of a more technical nature. We did not strive to find the most general conditions for our theorem to hold, but rather to find conditions which are easy to work with, lead to short proofs and are satisfied for natural examples. Our result for these weights is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying (
1.7
)–(
1.8
) with
\theta>0
,
d>-1
,
{a}\in(0,1)
,
\eta\in(0,1/2]
and
{r}\in(0,2)
. As
x\to\infty
we have

\frac{\Omega(N_{x})-\theta\log\log x}{\sqrt{\theta\log\log x}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1)

(1.9)

and

\left(\frac{\log\mathcal{p}_{1}(N_{x})}{\log x},\frac{\log\mathcal{p}_{2}(N_{x})}{\log x},\ldots\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{PD}(\theta),

(1.10)

where
\mathrm{PD}(\theta)
is the Poisson–Dirichlet distribution with parameter
\theta
(defined in §
2
).

Here the arrows indicate convergence in distribution. The proof of the first part of Theorem
1.1
applies to
\omega(N_{x})
as well, where
\omega(n)
counts the number of prime factors of
n

without
multiplicities, and the result is the same. The prototypical example of a function
\alpha
satisfying the conditions is
\theta^{\omega(n)}
(with
d=0
,
\eta=1/2
,
{r}=1
and any
{a}>0
).

The case
\alpha\equiv 1
of (
1.9
) is the Erdős–Kac theorem
[
23
]
. Our proof of it is a generalization of a proof given by Billingsley
[
11
]
to the original Erdős–Kac theorem with
\alpha\equiv 1
. The case
\alpha\equiv 1
of (
1.10
) is Billingsley’s theorem
[
12
]
. Our proof of it is a generalization of Donnelly and Grimmett’s proof
[
16
]
, who elucidated Billingsley’s result.

The Ewens measure with parameter
\theta
(
>0
) is a measure on
S_{n}
, which may be defined by taking
\theta_{i}=\theta
in the definition of the generalized Ewens measure. The partition function is
\binom{n+\theta-1}{n}
in this case.
This measure has first appeared in the study of population genetics
[
25
]
. The Ewens measure has found many practical applications, through its connection with Kingman’s coalescent process
[
36
]
and its occurrence in non-parametric Bayesian statistics
[
5
]
.

Theorem
1.1
should be compared with two results on the Ewens measure, one on
C(\pi_{n,\theta})
(the number of cycles in
\pi_{n,\theta}
) by Hansen
[
31
]
, and another on
(\ell_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta})/n,\ell_{2}(\pi_{n,\theta})/n,\ldots)
by Watterson
[
55
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Hansen, Watterson)
.

Let
\theta>0
. As
n\to\infty
we have

\frac{C(\pi_{n,\theta})-\theta\log n}{\sqrt{\theta\log n}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1)

(1.11)

and

\left(\frac{\ell_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta})}{n},\frac{\ell_{2}(\pi_{n,\theta})}{n},\ldots\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{PD}(\theta).

(1.12)

The first part of Theorem
1.2
was proven under more general conditions, e.g. when
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}/n\to\theta
sufficiently fast, see Lugo
[
37
]
and the works of Manstavičius
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

The similarity of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
is most apparent for functions
\alpha
where
\alpha(p)\approx\theta
. It suggests an analogy between permutations chosen according to the Ewens measure and integers chosen according to multiplicative weights. We now discuss previous works.

1.1.1
Erdős–Kac

In a series of works, Alladi
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
proved a generalization of Erdős–Kac involving weights
\alpha
as well. His proof uses the combinatorial sieve and he requires
\alpha
to satisfy a ‘level-of-distribution’ condition which is not always easily verified. A related (but simpler) sieve-theoretic approach to Erdős–Kac and its generalizations was introduced by Granville and Soundararajan
[
30
]
. This approach was used by Khan, Milinovich and Subedi to prove an Erdős–Kac theorem with weights being
d_{k}
, the
k
th divisor function
[
35
]
.

See Elliott
[
20
,
21
]
for a treatment of the Erdős–Kac theorem with weights being the standard divisor function
d_{2}
and its real powers. Tenenbaum proved in
[
53
, Cor. 2.5]
an impressively general weighted Erdős–Kac theorem, but unlike Theorem
1.1
, he requires
\alpha(p)/p^{d}
to be uniformly bounded. Both Elliott and Tenenbaum use characteristic functions and complex analysis while we avoid these.

1.1.2
Billingsley

Arratia, Kochman and Miller proved an analogue of Billingsley’s theorem for
normed arithmetic semigroups
satisfying certain growth conditions
[
7
, Thm. 2]
. A commutative semigroup
S
is called normed arithmetic semigroup if it contains an identity element and admits unique factorization into ‘prime’ elements. Furthermore, it should come equipped with a multiplicative norm function
s\mapsto|s|\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, such that
N(x)=\#\{s\in S:|s|\leq x\}
is a finite number for each
x>0
.
There is small overlap between
[
7
, Thm. 2]
and the second part of Theorem
1.1
as there are multiplicative functions
\alpha
satisfying (
1.7
)–(
1.8
) and coinciding with
\alpha_{S}(n):=\#\{s\in S:|s|=n\}
for some normed arithmetic semigroup
S
.

1.2
Polynomially-growing weights

In the permutation setting, the measure
\mathbb{P}_{n,\theta_{i}}
was studied extensively in the case of polynomially-growing cycle weights, that is

\theta_{n}\approx An^{\gamma},

(1.13)

see
[
24
,
41
,
22
,
15
,
13
]
. Ercolani and Ueltschi proved the following in
[
22
, Thm. 5.1]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Ercolani and Ueltschi)
.

Let
\gamma>0
, and take

\theta_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+n+1)}{n!}=(1+o(1))n^{\gamma}.

(1.14)

As
n\to\infty
we have

\mathbb{E}C(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})\sim n^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\gamma^{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

(1.15)

The specific choice (
1.14
) simplifies the computations greatly. Maples, Nikeghbali and Zeindler
[
41
, Cor. 1.2]
were able to prove that
C(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})
converges, after an explicit normalization, to a normal distribution (for
\theta_{n}
as in (
1.14
) and also scalar multiples of it).

Next we describe a result of Ercolani and Ueltschi
[
22
, Thm. 5.1]
about a permutation statistic which we have yet to discuss,
L_{1}(\pi)
. This is the length of the cycle of a permutation
\pi
which contains the element
1
. In the Ewens case
\theta_{i}=\theta
, it is known that
L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})/n
converges in distribution to a beta distribution
[
22
, §6]
. For polynomially-growing weights, Ercolani and Ueltschi proved that
L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})
exhibits a very different behavior. First, the order of magnitude of
L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})
in this case is
n^{1/(\gamma+1)}=o(n)
and not
n
. Second, the limiting distribution is a gamma distribution, whose definition is recalled in §
2
.

Theorem 1.4

(Ercolani and Ueltschi)
.

Let
\gamma>0
, and take
\theta_{n}
as in (
1.14
). Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})}{n^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\gammadist(\gamma+1,\Gamma(\gamma+1)^{1/(\gamma+1)}).

(1.16)

See Dereich and Mörters
[
15
]
for finer results about
L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})
for similar weights.

We derive number-theoretic analogues of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
Since there is no such thing as ‘a prime divisor of
n
containing a fixed element’, we must turn to a different interpretation of
L_{1}(\pi)
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=\{a_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative reals summing to
0<S<\infty
. A
size-biased sampling
of an element from
\mathbf{a}
is a random variable
X
whose distribution is given by

\mathbb{P}(X=a_{j})=\frac{a_{j}\cdot\left|\left\{i\geq 1\ \big|\ a_{i}=a_{j}\right\}\right|}{S}.

(1.17)

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}
is some conjugation-invariant measure on
S_{n}
(e.g.
\mathbb{P}_{n,\theta_{i}}
). If
\pi\in S_{n}
is sampled according to
\mathbb{P}
, then the distribution of
L_{1}(\pi)
coincides with the distribution of a typical cycle of
\pi
, that is: of a size-biased sampling of an element from
\{\ell_{i}(\pi)\}_{i\geq 1}
. See Lemma
2.3
below for the proof.
It is now clear how to define an integer analogue of
L_{1}(\pi_{n,\theta_{i}})
: given
N_{x}
, we define
P_{1}(N_{x})
by letting
\log P_{1}(N_{x})
be a size-biased sampling of an element from
\{\log\mathcal{p}_{i}(N_{x})\}_{i\geq 1}
. We think of
P_{1}(N_{x})
as a typical prime divisor of
N_{x}
.

In the integer setting, the polynomial weights (
1.13
) correspond to

\alpha(p)\approx K\log^{\gamma}p.

(1.18)

For our results, we require that for all primes
p
,

\displaystyle\text{(I) }\alpha(p)=K\log^{\gamma}p+O(\log^{-2}p),

(1.19)

\displaystyle\text{(II) }\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{k\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k}}=O\bigg(\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\bigg).

(1.20)

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) for some
K>0
,
\gamma>0
. As
x\to\infty
we have

\frac{\log P_{1}(N_{x})}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\gammadist(\gamma+1,(K\Gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/(\gamma+1)})

(1.21)

and

\mathbb{E}\Omega(N_{x})\sim(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\left(\frac{K\Gamma(\gamma)}{\gamma^{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

(1.22)

The proof of the second part of Theorem
1.6
applies to
\omega(N_{x})
as well. It is interesting that although the measure
\mathbb{P}_{x,\alpha}
assigns larger weights to larger primes, the typical prime factors of
N_{x}
are much smaller than in the case of uniformly drawn integers between
1
and
x
. Indeed, it follows from the first part of Theorem
1.6
that
\log P_{1}(N_{x})=o(\log x)
, while from Theorem
1.1
it follows that
\log P_{1}(N_{x})=\Theta(\log x)
for uniform integers.

The main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.6
is the asymptotics of
\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha(n)
for multiplicative
\alpha
obeying (
1.19
)–(
1.20
). This unusual sum was studied by Schwarz
[
50
]
and Marenich
[
42
]
.
As they both appeal to the same Tauberian theorem
[
34
, Thm. 1]
, no error term is obtained. We prove the following estimate.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) for some
K>0
,
\gamma>0
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
for which

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha(n)=(1+O((\log x)^{-\varepsilon}))A_{\alpha}\frac{\exp(B(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}})}{(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma+2}{2(\gamma+1)}}}

(1.23)

as
x\to\infty
, where
A_{\alpha}
is a positive constant and

B=\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)(K\Gamma(\gamma+1))^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

(1.24)

The proof of Theorem
1.7
shows that

A_{\alpha}=A\prod_{p}\frac{\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k}}}{\exp(\frac{K\log^{\gamma}p}{p})}

(1.25)

for a constant
A
depending only on
K
and
\gamma
. Additionally, one may take
\varepsilon=1/(\gamma+1)
if
\gamma>2
, and
\varepsilon
arbitrarily close to
\gamma/(2(\gamma+1))
otherwise.

Conventions

In the arguments below, we think of the function
\alpha
as fixed, and write
N_{x}
for
N_{x,\alpha}
. We denote the set of prime numbers by
\mathcal{P}
, and reserve the letter
p
for primes. The letters
C
and
c
always denote positive constants, which may vary from line to line. However,
C
and
c
depend only on the arithmetic function
\alpha
considered unless otherwise stated.
The arguments in the proofs always hold for sufficiently large
x
. The notation
A\gg 1
indicates that
A
is sufficiently large.

2
Preliminaries from probability theory

We denote by
\betadist(\alpha,\beta)
the beta distribution with shape parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
whose density with respect to Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
is given by

\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)x^{\alpha-1}\left(1-x\right)^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)},\quad x\in[0,1].

We denote by
\gammadist(\alpha,\beta)
the gamma distribution with shape parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
whose density with respect to Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
is given by

\frac{\beta^{\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)},\quad x\in[0,\infty).

We define the Poisson–Dirichlet distribution with parameter
\theta
, denoted by
\PD(\theta)
. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with
\betadist(1,\theta)
distribution. Define the sequence

Z_{j}=(1-Y_{1})\cdots(1-Y_{j-1})Y_{j},\quad j\geq 1.

(2.1)

Intuitively
Z_{1}
takes a
\betadist(1,\theta)
-distributed fraction of the unit interval. Conditioned on
Z_{1}
,
Z_{2}
takes a
\betadist(1,\theta)
-distributed fraction of the remaining part of the interval, etc. Finally, the
\PD(\theta)
distribution is defined to be the distribution of the sequence
(Z_{j})_{j\geq 1}
arranged in non-increasing order.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we establish the convergence in distribution to
\PD(\theta)
by convergence of a certain sequence to a sequence of independent
\betadist(1,\theta)
random variables. To this end we define the size-biased permutation of a sequence of random variables. Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be a non-increasing sequence of random variables such that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}=1,\quad\text{almost surely.}

(2.2)

A sized-biased permutation
(\tilde{X_{i}})_{i}
of the sequence
(X_{i})_{i}
is a random reordering of the elements of the sequence such that for any
j\geq 1

\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{X}_{1}=X_{j}\ |\ X_{1},X_{2},\dots\right)=X_{j}\cdot\left|\left\{j^{\prime}\geq 1\ \big|\ X_{j^{\prime}}=X_{j}\right\}\right|

(2.3)

and inductively for
k\geq 1

\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{X}_{k}=X_{j}\ |\ \tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{k-1},X_{1},X_{2},\dots\right)=\frac{X_{j}\cdot\left(\left|\left\{j^{\prime}\geq 1\ \big|\ X_{j^{\prime}}=X_{j}\right\}\right|-\left|\left\{j^{\prime}<k\ \big|\ \tilde{X}_{j^{\prime}}=X_{j}\right\}\right|\right)}{1-\tilde{X}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}_{k-1}}.

(2.4)

The sized-biased permutation can be used to reconstruct the
\betadist(1,\theta)
random variables from the
\PD(\theta)
distribution in the following sense.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be a non-increasing sequence of random variables with

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}=1,\quad\text{almost surely.}

(2.5)

Then, the sequence
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
has the
\PD(\theta)
distribution if and only if the sequence

\left(\frac{\tilde{X}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}_{j-1}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}

(2.6)

is an i.i.d. sequence of
\betadist(1,\theta)
random variables.

For a discussion and references for Proposition
2.1
see the introduction of
[
48
]
. We use the following result in order to prove the convergence to
\PD(\theta)
in Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
n\geq 1
, let
X_{1}^{(n)},X_{2}^{(n)},\dots
be a non-increasing sequence of random variables with

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}^{(n)}=1,\quad\text{almost surely.}

(2.7)

Suppose that

\left(\frac{\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j-1}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots),\quad n\to\infty,

(2.8)

where
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
is a sequence of i.i.d.
\betadist(1,\theta)
random variables. Then, we have

(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\PD(\theta),\quad n\to\infty.

(2.9)

Proof.

Consider the function
g\colon[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
defined by

g(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)=\big((1-y_{1})\cdots(1-y_{k-1})y_{k}\big)_{k=1}^{\infty},

(2.10)

and the function

r\colon\Big\{(z_{1},z_{2},\dots)\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}:\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}z_{j}\leq 1\Big\}\to[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}

(2.11)

that takes a sequence and returns the same sequence in non-increasing order. Since
r\circ g
is continuous in the product topology on
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
we have by (
2.8
) that

(r\circ g)\left(\bigg(\frac{\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j-1}}\bigg)_{j=1}^{\infty}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}(r\circ g)(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots),\quad n\to\infty.

(2.12)

By the definition of the Poisson–Dirichlet distribution
(r\circ g)(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots)\sim\PD(\theta)
and moreover, since

g^{-1}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots)=\left(\frac{z_{j}}{1-z_{1}-\ldots-z_{j-1}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty},

(2.13)

we have that

(r\circ g)\left(\bigg(\frac{\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}^{(n)}_{j-1}}\bigg)_{j=1}^{\infty}\right)=r(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2},\ldots)=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots).

(2.14)

Thus, (
2.12
) simplifies to (
2.9
), as needed.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a conjugation-invariant measure on

. Given

3.1
Multiplicative number theory

Theorem 3.1

(La Bretèche and Tenenbaum
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose a multiplicative function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies

\sum_{p\leq x}\alpha(p)\log p=\theta x+O\left(\frac{x}{\log^{{a}}x}\right),\qquad x\to\infty,

(3.1)

for some
\theta>0
and
{a}\in(0,1)
. Suppose further that

\sum_{p}\bigg(\frac{\alpha(p)^{2}}{p^{2\sigma}}+\sum_{\nu\geq 2}\frac{\alpha(p^{\nu})}{p^{\nu\sigma}}\bigg)<\infty

(3.2)

for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Then, for all sufficiently large
x
,

\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha(n)=(1+O(\log^{-{a}}x))\Gamma(\theta)^{-1}\prod_{p}\bigg(\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\alpha(p^{i})}{p^{i}}\bigg)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\theta}x(\log x)^{\theta-1}.

(3.3)

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose a multiplicative function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies (
1.7
)–(
1.8
) for some
\theta>0
,
d>-1
,
{a}\in(0,1)
,
\eta\in(0,1/2]
and
{r}\in(0,2)
. Then, for all sufficiently large
x
,

\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha(n)=(1+O(\log^{-{a}}x))A_{\alpha}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x

(3.4)

where
A_{\alpha}=(d+1)^{-1}\Gamma(\theta)^{-1}\prod_{p}(\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha(p^{i})/p^{i(d+1)})(1-1/p)^{\theta}>0
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{d}(n)=\alpha(n)/n^{d}
. This function is still multiplicative. If
\alpha
satisfies (
1.7
)–(
1.8
), then
\alpha_{d}
satisfies these conditions as well with the same parameters except that the parameter
d
is now
0
.

Evidently, the conditions of Theorem
3.1
hold for
\alpha_{d}
, with the same
\theta
and
{a}
, and with any
\sigma\in(0,1)
that satisfies
\sigma>\max\{\log{r}/\log 2,1-\eta\}
. Thus, we conclude from Theorem
3.1
that

\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha_{d}(n)=(1+O(\log^{-{a}}x))\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}\prod_{p}\bigg(\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{\alpha(p^{i})}{p^{i(d+1)}}\bigg)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\theta}x\log^{\theta-1}x

(3.5)

as
x\to\infty
. Using integration by parts and (
3.5
) we have

\begin{split}\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha(n)&=\sum_{n\leq x}n^{d}\alpha_{d}(n)=x^{d}\bigg(\sum_{n\leq x}\alpha_{d}(n)\bigg)-\int_{1}^{x}\bigg(\sum_{n\leq t}\alpha_{d}(n)\bigg)(t^{d})^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=(1+O(\log^{-{a}}x))A_{\alpha_{d}}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x-\int_{2}^{x}A_{\alpha_{d}}(1+O(\log^{-{a}}t))(t\log^{\theta-1}t)(dt^{d-1})\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(3.6)

which gives (
3.4
) with
A_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha_{d}}/(d+1)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose a multiplicative function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies (
1.7
)–(
1.8
) for some
\theta>0
,
d>-1
,
{a}\in(0,1)
,
\eta\in(0,1/2]
and
{r}\in(0,2)
. Let
p
be a prime. We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq\frac{C(1+\alpha(p)p^{-d})}{p}x^{d+1}(\log x)^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}}.

(3.7)

If furthermore
p\leq\sqrt{x}
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq\frac{C(1+\alpha(p)p^{-d})}{p}x^{d+1}(\log x)^{\theta-1}.

(3.8)

Proof.

By multiplicativity of
\alpha
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p^{k}\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor}\alpha(p^{k})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
p\nmid l\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor}\alpha(p^{k})\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l).

(3.9)

By Corollary
3.2
there exists a constant
C
such that

\sum_{n\leq y}\alpha(n)\leq Cy^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}y\leq Cy^{d+1}(\log(y+1))^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}}

(3.10)

for all
y\geq 1
(one needs to consider
y\in[1,2)
separately from
y\geq 2
). Note that the right-hand side of (
3.10
) is monotone increasing in
y
and so is
(\log(y+1))^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}}
. By (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k(d+1)}}x^{d+1}(\log(x+1))^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}}.

(3.11)

From (
1.8
) and (
3.11
) we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq Cx^{d+1}(\log(x+1))^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}}\bigg(\frac{\alpha(p)}{p^{d+1}}+\sum_{k\geq 2}\big(\frac{{r}}{p}\big)^{k}\bigg)\leq\frac{C(1+\alpha(p)p^{-d})}{p}x^{d+1}(\log(x+1))^{\max\{\theta-1,0\}},

(3.12)

and so (
3.7
) holds.
To prove (
3.8
), we split the right-hand side of (
3.9
) into two sums, one for terms with
p^{k}\leq\sqrt{x}
and another for the rest. Set
L:=\lfloor\log_{p}\sqrt{x}\rfloor+1\geq 2
. For
1\leq k<L
, we have
\log^{\theta-1}(x/p^{k})\leq C\log^{\theta-1}x
, and so Corollary
3.2
gives

\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l)\leq C\left(\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right)^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x.

(3.13)

From (
1.8
) we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\alpha(p^{k})\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l)\leq Cx^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k(d+1)}}\leq\frac{C(1+\alpha(p)p^{-d})}{p}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x.\end{split}

(3.14)

When
L\leq k\leq\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor
we use the bound

\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l)\leq C\left(\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right)^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x,

(3.15)

which follows from (
3.10
), to obtain, from (
1.8
) again, that

\begin{split}\sum_{k=L}^{\lfloor\log_{p}x\rfloor}\alpha(p^{k})\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor}\alpha(l)&\leq Cx^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x\sum_{k=L}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k(d+1)}}\leq Cx^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x\sum_{k=L}^{\infty}\left(\frac{{r}}{p}\right)^{k}\leq Cx^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x\left(\frac{{r}}{p}\right)^{L}\\
&\leq\frac{C}{p}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x\left(\frac{{r}}{p}\right)^{L/2}\leq\frac{C}{p}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta}x\left(\frac{{r}}{p}\right)^{(\log_{p}\sqrt{x})/2}\leq\frac{C}{p}x^{d+1}\log^{\theta-1}x.\end{split}

(3.16)

From (
3.14
) and (
3.16
) we obtain (
3.8
).
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and define a function
E
via

\sum_{p\leq x}\alpha(p)\log p=\theta x+E(x).

Let
I\subseteq[2,\infty)
be a finite closed interval and
g\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. Then

\left|\sum_{p\in I}\alpha(p)g(p)-\theta\int_{I}\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq\max_{t\in I}|g(t)\alpha(t)|+2\max_{t\in I}\left|\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right||E(t)|+\int_{I}\left|\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}\right||E(t)|\mathrm{d}t.

(3.17)

Proof.

We write
I=[x,y]
. We shall work with the half-open interval
(x,y]
; we may do so because the contribution of
[x,y]\setminus(x,y]=\{x\}
to
\sum_{p\in I}\alpha(p)g(p)
is at most
\max_{t\in I}|g(t)\alpha(t)|
. Using integration by parts,

\begin{split}\theta\intop_{x}^{y}\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\theta g(y)}{\log y}\intop_{x}^{y}1\,\mathrm{d}t-\intop_{x}^{y}\theta(t-x)\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\theta g(y)}{\log y}(y-x)-\intop_{x}^{y}\theta(t-x)\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(3.18)

and, by Abel’s summation formula,

\begin{split}\sum_{x<p\leq y}\alpha(p)g(p)&=\sum_{x<n\leq y}\mathds{1}_{\left\{n\mbox{ is prime}\right\}}\alpha(n)\log n\cdot\frac{g(n)}{\log n}\\
&=\frac{g(y)}{\log y}\bigg(\sum_{x<p\leq y}\alpha(p)\log p\bigg)-\intop_{x}^{y}\bigg(\sum_{x<p\leq t}\alpha(p)\log p\bigg)\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{g(y)}{\log y}\left(\theta(y-x)+E(y)-E(x)\right)-\intop_{x}^{y}\bigg(\sum_{x<p\leq t}\alpha(p)\log p\bigg)\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(3.19)

From (
3.18
), (
3.19
) and the definition of
E
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in(x,y]}\alpha(p)g(p)-\theta\intop_{x}^{y}\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{g(y)}{\log y}(E(y)-E(x))-\intop_{x}^{y}\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}(E(t)-E(x))\,\mathrm{d}t

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(y)}{\log y}\left(E(y)-E(x)\right)-\intop_{x}^{y}\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}E(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+E(x)\left(\frac{g(y)}{\log y}-\frac{g(x)}{\log x}\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(y)}{\log y}E(y)-\frac{g(x)}{\log x}E(x)-\intop_{x}^{y}\left(\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\right)^{\prime}E(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(3.22)

and now the required estimate follows by the triangle inequality.
∎

3.2.2
Conclusion of proof

From Corollary

it follows that for any given

as

Proposition 3.6
.

We have

\left(B_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}:=\left(\frac{\tilde{X}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}_{j-1}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots)

(3.29)

as

, where
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
is a sequence of i.i.d.
\betadist(1,\theta)
random variables and where
(\tilde{X}_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a sized-biased permutation (defined in §

) of the sequence
(X_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}=\left(\frac{\log\mathcal{p}_{j}(N_{x})}{\log N_{x}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

Remark 3.7
.

To connect this proposition with number-theoretic terms, we introduce a sequence
P_{j}
of ‘typical prime divisors’ of
N_{x}
, defined as
P_{j}={N_{x}}^{\tilde{X}_{j}}
. The asymptotic behavior of these typical primes might be of independent interest. It follows from the proposition that
P_{j}
for
j\geq 1
satisfy the following limit law

\left(\frac{\log P_{1}(N_{x})}{\log x},\frac{\log P_{2}(N_{x})}{\log x},\frac{\log P_{3}(N_{x})}{\log x},\ldots\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\big(Y_{1},(1-Y_{1})Y_{2},(1-Y_{1}-Y_{2})Y_{3},\dots\big),

(3.30)

as
x\to\infty
, where once again,
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
is a sequence of i.i.d.
\betadist(1,\theta)
.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
0<a_{j}<b_{j}<1
for any
1\leq j\leq k
.
By the Portmanteau Lemma it suffices to show that

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\ \ a_{j}\leq B_{j}\leq b_{j}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\ \ a_{j}\leq Y_{j}\leq b_{j}\right).

(3.31)

We have that

B_{j}=\frac{\tilde{X}_{j}}{1-\tilde{X}_{1}-\ldots-\tilde{X}_{j-1}}=\frac{\log(P_{j})}{\log\left(\frac{N_{x}}{P_{1}\cdots P_{j-1}}\right)}

(3.32)

and therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\ \ a_{j}\leq B_{j}\leq b_{j}\right)=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{x}=n,\,\forall j\ \ P_{j}=p_{j}\right),

(3.33)

where the inner sum is over a sequence of
k

p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
such that for any
1\leq j\leq k
we have
\left(n/(p_{1}\cdots p_{j-1})\right)^{a_{j}}\leq p_{j}\leq\left(n/(p_{1}\cdots p_{j-1})\right)^{b_{j}}
. Let
0<\delta<\min\{a_{1},\dots a_{k}\}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-b_{i})
and put
x_{j}:=x/(p_{1}\cdots p_{j-1})
for any
1\leq j\leq k+1
. We have the following lower bound:

\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\ \ a_{j}\leq B_{j}\leq b_{j}\right)\geq\sum_{\lceil\delta x\rceil\leq n\leq x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\mbox{ distinct}\\
x_{j}^{a_{j}}\leq p_{j}\leq(\delta x_{j})^{b_{j}},\ p_{j}\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{x}=n,\,\forall j\ \ P_{j}=p_{j}\right).

(3.34)

By the definition of a sized-biased permutation, when
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
are distinct and
p_{j}\mid\mid n
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{x}=n,\,\forall j\ \ P_{j}=p_{j}\right)=\frac{\alpha(n)}{\sum_{m\leq x}\alpha(m)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\log p_{j}}{\log\left(\frac{n}{p_{1}\cdots p_{j-1}}\right)}\geq\frac{\alpha(n)}{\sum_{m\leq x}\alpha(m)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\log p_{j}}{\log x_{j}}

(3.35)

for any
n\leq x
. Thus, changing the order of summation in (
3.34
) we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\ \ a_{j}\leq B_{j}\leq b_{j}\right)&\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\leq p_{1}\leq(\delta x_{1})^{b_{1}}\end{subarray}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}^{a_{k}}\leq p_{k}\leq(\delta x_{k})^{b_{k}}\\
p_{k}\notin\{p_{1},\dots p_{k-1}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lceil\delta x\rceil\leq n\leq x\\
p_{i}\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha(n)}{\sum_{m\leq x}\alpha(m)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\log p_{j}}{\log x_{j}}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{\sum_{m\leq x}\alpha(m)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\leq p_{1}\leq(\delta x_{1})^{b_{1}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p_{1}}{\log x_{1}}\cdots\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{k}^{a_{k}}\leq p_{k}\leq(\delta x_{k})^{b_{k}}\\
p_{k}\notin\{p_{1},\dots p_{k-1}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p_{k}}{\log x_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lceil\delta x\rceil\leq n\leq x\\
p_{i}\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n).\end{split}

(3.36)

Next, we would like to use Lemma
3.5
in order to lower bound the inner sum in (
3.36
). We have
x_{j}=p_{j}x_{j+1}\leq(\delta x_{j})^{b_{j}}x_{j+1}\leq x_{j}^{b_{j}}x_{j+1}
and therefore, for any

we have

where

Lemma 3.13

3.3.2
Conclusion of proof

By Lemma
3.9
and the first part of Lemma
3.8
with

and

, it follows that (

) holds if and only if

. Now let
D_{x}=\sqrt{\theta\log\log x}/\sigma_{x}
and

E_{x}=\frac{\theta\log\log x-\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{x}}\frac{\alpha(p)}{p^{d+1}}}{\sigma_{x}}-\frac{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{x}}\mathds{1}_{p\mid N_{x}}}{\sigma_{x}}.

We have

. Moreover, in the sum in the second fraction in (

, and so we may use Lemma
3.10
and (

) to obtain that

where the last expression tends to

by Lemma
3.4
with

. Hence

. Since

, it follows by Lemma

and the first part of Lemma

that

for each

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix a function

on primes such that
\log f(p)=o(\log p)
, and let

G_{f}(s)=\sum_{p}\frac{f(p)}{p^{s}},\quad\Re s>1.

(4.1)

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying

\begin{split}&\text{(I) }\alpha(p)=f(p)+O\left(\log^{-2}p\right),\\
&\text{(II) }\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{k\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k}}=O\left(\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\right),\end{split}

(4.2)

and define

F(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\alpha(n)}{n^{s}},

(4.3)

the Dirichlet series of
\alpha
. For
\Re s>1
we have

F(s)=\varphi(s)e^{G_{f}(s)}

(4.4)

where
\varphi
is differentiable, bounded and has bounded derivative on
\Re s\geq 1
.

Proof.

By multiplicativity of
\alpha
, we may write

F(s)=\prod_{p}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}\right)=e^{G_{f}(s)}\prod_{p}\left(1+E_{p}(s)\right),

(4.5)

where

Lemma 4.3

4.59
) and (
4.60
), we get

\varphi(\sigma+it)e^{K\cdot G(\sigma+it)}\frac{x^{\sigma+it}}{\sigma+it}=\varphi(1)x^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)-\frac{t^{2}}{2}K\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)}\left(1+O\left(\log^{-z}x\right)\right)

(4.61)

where
z:=\min\{\gamma-\delta,2\}/(2(\gamma+1))
. We thus have

I_{1}=\left(1+O\left(\log^{-z}x\right)\right)\frac{\varphi(1)x^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}}{2\pi}\intop_{-t_{x}}^{t_{x}}e^{-\frac{K\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)}{2}t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\left(1+O\left(\log^{-z}x\right)\right)\frac{\varphi(1)x^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}}{\sqrt{2\pi K\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)}},

(4.62)

which by Corollary
4.7
can be simplified to

I_{1}=\left(1+O\left(\log^{-z}x\right)\right)A_{\alpha}x(\log x)^{-\frac{\gamma+2}{2(\gamma+1)}}\exp\left[B(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\right],

(4.63)

where
B
is defined in (
1.24
), and
A_{\alpha}
is defined in (
1.25
).
Next we bound
I_{2}
. Using Lemma
4.3
with
s=\sigma+it
where
t_{x}\leq|t|\leq 1
, we get

\Re\left(G(\sigma+it)\right)\leq\left|G(\sigma+it)\right|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\left|\sigma-1+it\right|^{\gamma}}+C\leq\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\left|\sigma-1+it_{x}\right|^{\gamma}}+C\leq\left|G(\sigma+it_{x})\right|+C,

(4.64)

and so a second-order Taylor approximation shows that

\begin{split}\Re\left(G(\sigma+it)\right)&\leq\left|G(\sigma)+it_{x}G^{\prime}(\sigma)-\frac{t_{x}^{2}}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)\right|+C\\
&=\sqrt{G(\sigma)^{2}-t_{x}^{2}\left(G(\sigma)G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)-G^{\prime}(\sigma)^{2}\right)+t_{x}^{4}G^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)^{2}/4}+C\\
&\leq\sqrt{G(\sigma)^{2}-c(\log x)^{\frac{3\gamma+\delta}{3(\gamma+1)}}}+C\leq G(\sigma)-c(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{3(\gamma+1)}},\end{split}

(4.65)

where the second inequality holds for sufficiently small
\delta
and follows from Corollary
4.7
. Thus

|I_{2}|\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)-c(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{3(\gamma+1)}}}\leq\frac{C}{\log x}|I_{1}|.

(4.66)

We now show that the contribution from
I_{3}
is negligible as well. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that
8\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is strictly less than the constant
c
from Lemma
4.4
. Consider the set

S:=\{t>0:\Re\left(G(\sigma+it)\right)>(1-\varepsilon)G(\sigma)\}.

(4.67)

We have, by definition of
S
,

\begin{split}\intop_{\left[1,x^{2}\right]\setminus S}\left|\varphi(\sigma+it)e^{K\cdot G(\sigma+it)}\frac{x^{\sigma+it}}{\sigma+it}\right|\,\mathrm{d}t&\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{(1-\varepsilon)K\cdot G(\sigma)}\intop_{\left[1,x^{2}\right]\setminus S}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t+1}\\
&\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{(1-\varepsilon)K\cdot G(\sigma)}\log x\leq\frac{C}{\log x}|I_{1}|,\end{split}

(4.68)

where in the last inequality we used Corollary
4.7
. We now study the integral over
S
. Applying Lemma
4.6
with the sequence

a_{n}:=\mathds{1}_{\{n\text{ is prime}\}}\frac{\log^{\gamma}n}{n^{\sigma}}

(4.69)

we find that for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in S
we have that
\Re\left(G(\sigma+i(t_{1}-t_{2}))\right)\geq(1-8\sqrt{\varepsilon})G(\sigma)
and therefore, by Lemma
4.4
and the choice of
\varepsilon
, either
|t_{1}-t_{2}|\leq 1
or
|t_{1}-t_{2}|\geq e^{1/(\sigma-1)}
. It follows that

S\subseteq\bigcup_{j=0}^{\infty}[a_{j},b_{j}]

(4.70)

for some
a_{j+1}>b_{j}>a_{j}\geq 0
with

b_{j}-a_{j}\leq 1,\quad a_{j}\geq j\cdot e^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{0}=0.

(4.71)

Thus, for sufficiently large
x
,

\begin{split}\intop_{\left[1,x^{2}\right]\cap S}\left|\varphi(\sigma+it)e^{K\cdot G(\sigma+it)}\frac{x^{\sigma+it}}{\sigma+it}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|&\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}\sum_{1\leq j\leq x^{2}}\intop_{a_{j}}^{b_{j}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}\sum_{1\leq j\leq x^{2}}\frac{b_{j}-a_{j}}{a_{j}}\\
&\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq x^{2}}\frac{1}{j}\leq Cx^{\sigma}e^{K\cdot G(\sigma)}e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}\log x\\
&\leq\frac{C}{\log x}|I_{1}|.\end{split}

(4.72)

Combining the estimates for the integrals over
\left[1,x^{2}\right]\setminus S
and
\left[1,x^{2}\right]\cap S
, we obtain

|I_{3}|\leq\frac{C}{\log x}|I_{1}|.

(4.73)

We conclude the proof by plugging the estimates (
4.56
), (
4.63
), (
4.66
) and (
4.73
) in (
4.55
). ∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
1.6

4.2.1
Auxiliary results

An important step in the proof is understanding the asymptotic behavior of
\mathbb{P}(p\mid N_{x})
. We shall see that
\mathbb{P}(p\mid N_{x})\approx\alpha(p)S(x/p)/S(x)
, and so begin by studying the ratio
S(x/h)/S(x)
. Observe that

S(x/h)h\leq C\cdot S(x)

(4.74)

for
x\geq 1
,
h\geq 1
by Theorem
1.7
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) and suppose that
x
is sufficiently large. Let
2\leq h\leq x
. When
\log h\leq(\log x)^{(\gamma+4)/(4\gamma+4)}
, we have that

\frac{S(x/h)}{S(x)}=\frac{1}{h}\exp\bigg[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log h}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\bigg)\bigg].

(4.75)

When
\log h\geq(\log x)^{(\gamma+4)/(4\gamma+4)}
, we have that

\frac{S(x/h)}{S(x)}\leq\frac{1}{h}e^{-(\log x)^{c}}.

(4.76)

Proof.

Let
h_{x}:=\exp((\log x)^{(\gamma+4)/(4\gamma+4)})
. Suppose that
h\leq h_{x}
. By a first-order Taylor approximation, we get

(\log(x/h))^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}=(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\left(1-\frac{\log h}{\log x}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}=(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log h}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right).

(4.77)

Thus, by Theorem
1.7
applied with
x
and
x/h
, we obtain the first part of the lemma.
We turn to prove the second part of the lemma. Using the first part of the lemma and (
4.74
) we get that when
h\geq h_{x}

\frac{S(x/h)}{S(x)}=\frac{S(x/h)}{S(x/h_{x})}\frac{S(x/h_{x})}{S(x)}\leq\frac{Ch_{x}}{h}\frac{1}{h_{x}}\exp\left[-c\frac{\log h_{x}}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{h}e^{-(\log x)^{c}},

(4.78)

as needed.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a multiplicative function satisfying
\log\alpha(p)=o(\log p)
, (
1.20
) and (
4.74
). For sufficiently large
x
and any prime
p\leq x
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(p\mid N_{x}\right)=\frac{\alpha(p)S(x/p)}{S(x)}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\bigg).

(4.79)

Proof.

By (
4.74
) we have for any
y>1

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
p^{k}\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha(p^{k})S\Big(\frac{y}{p^{k}}\Big)\leq C\cdot S(y)\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k}}\leq\frac{C\cdot S(y)}{\sqrt{p}}.

(4.80)

Hence

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(p\mid\mid N_{x}\right)=\frac{1}{S(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid\mid n\end{subarray}}\alpha(n)=\frac{\alpha(p)}{S(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/p\\
p\nmid m\end{subarray}}\alpha(m)&=\frac{\alpha(p)S(x/p)}{S(x)}+O\left(\frac{\alpha(p)S(x/p)}{\sqrt{p}S(x)}\right)\\
&=\frac{\alpha(p)S(x/p)}{S(x)}+O\left(\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\right),\end{split}

(4.81)

where in the last inequality we use (
4.74
) again. Using the same arguments as in (
4.80
), we have that

\mathbb{P}(p^{2}\mid N_{x})\leq C\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k}}\leq\frac{C}{p\log^{2}p}.

(4.82)

As
\mathbb{P}(p\mid N_{x})=\mathbb{P}(p\mid\mid N_{x})+\mathbb{P}(p^{2}\mid N_{x})
, the proof is concluded.
∎

4.2.2
Conclusion of proof

We begin with the first part of the theorem. We abbreviate
P_{1}(N_{x})
as
P_{1}
. Fix
0<a<b<\infty
. It suffices to show that

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{\log P_{1}}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\leq b\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq Y\leq b\right)

(4.83)

where
Y
has
\gammadist(\gamma+1,(K\Gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/(\gamma+1)})
distribution.

For any prime
p
such that
a(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\leq\log p\leq b(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}
, we have, by Lemmas
4.8
and
4.9
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(P_{1}=p)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p\mid n\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha(n)}{S(x)}\frac{\nu_{p}(n)\log p}{\log n}\geq\frac{\log p}{\log x}\mathbb{P}(p\mid N_{x})\geq\frac{\log p}{\log x}\left(\frac{K\log^{\gamma}pS(x/p)}{S(x)}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\bigg)\right)\\
&\geq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right)\right)\frac{K\log^{\gamma+1}p}{p\log x}\exp\left[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log p}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right],\end{split}

(4.84)

where the error term
1/(p\log^{2}p)
is absorbed in the last error term. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{\log P_{1}}{(\log x)^{1/(\gamma+1)}}\leq b\right)\geq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right)\right)\sum_{n_{x}^{a}\leq p\leq n_{x}^{b}}\frac{K\log^{\gamma+1}p}{p\log x}\exp\left[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log p}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right]

(4.85)

where
n_{x}:=\exp((\log x)^{1/(\gamma+1)})
. By Lemma
3.4
with
\alpha\equiv 1
, the interval
[n_{x}^{a},n_{x}^{b}]
and

g(t)=\frac{K\log^{\gamma+1}t}{t\log x}\exp\bigg[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log t}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\bigg]

(4.86)

we have

\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\frac{\log P_{1}}{(\log x)^{1/(\gamma+1)}}\leq b\right)\geq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right)\right)\\
\left(\intop_{n_{x}^{a}}^{n_{x}^{b}}\frac{K\log^{\gamma}t}{t\log x}\exp\left[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log t}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+O(\log^{-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}x)\right).

(4.87)

The change of variables
t=n_{x}^{z}
in the last integral shows that it equals
\mathbb{P}(a\leq Y\leq b)
. Taking
x
to infinity in (
4.87
) we obtain (
4.83
), as needed.

We turn to the second part of the theorem. By Lemma
4.9
and (
1.19
) we have

\mathbb{E}\omega(N_{x})=\sum_{p\leq x}\mathbb{P}(p\mid N_{x})=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{K\log^{\gamma}pS(x/p)}{S(x)}+O\bigg(\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p\log^{2}p}\bigg).

(4.88)

The error term is bounded by a constant. In order to estimate the sum, we split it into three sums
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
and
S_{3}
, over the respective ranges
p<\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{1}})
,
\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{1}})\leq p\leq\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{2}})
and
\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{2}})<p\leq x
, where
\delta_{1}=1/(2(\gamma+1))
and
\delta_{2}=(\gamma+4)/(4\gamma+4)
. We bound
S_{1}
using (
4.74
):

S_{1}\leq C\sum_{p\leq\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{1}})}\frac{\log^{\gamma}p}{p}\leq C\log^{\delta_{1}\gamma}x,

(4.89)

where in the last inequality we used Lemma
3.4
with
\alpha\equiv 1
and
g(t)=\log^{\gamma}t/t
. We bound
S_{3}
using the second part of Lemma
4.8
, which gives

S_{3}\leq e^{-(\log x)^{c}}\sum_{\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{2}})<p\leq x}\frac{K\log^{\gamma}p}{p}\leq e^{-(\log x)^{c}}.

(4.90)

We now estimate
S_{2}
. By Lemma
4.8
,

S_{2}=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right)\right)\sum_{(\log x)^{\delta_{1}}\leq\log p\leq(\log x)^{\delta_{2}}}\frac{K\log^{\gamma}p}{p}\exp\left[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log p}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right].

(4.91)

From (
4.91
) and Lemma
3.4
with
\alpha\equiv 1
and

g(t)=\frac{K\log^{\gamma}t}{t}\exp\left[-\frac{B\gamma}{\gamma+1}\frac{\log t}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}\right]

(4.92)

we obtain

S_{2}=\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{c}}\right)\right)\left(\int_{\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{1}})}^{\exp((\log x)^{\delta_{2}})}\frac{g(t)}{\log t}\mathrm{d}t+O\left(\log^{\max\{0,\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\}}x\right)\right).

(4.93)

The change of variables
t=n_{x}^{z}
in the last integral shows that it equals

(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\intop_{(\log x)^{\delta_{1}-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}^{(\log x)^{\delta_{2}-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}}Kz^{\gamma-1}\exp\left(-\frac{B\gamma z}{\gamma+1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z\\
=(\log x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}K\Gamma(\gamma)\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{B\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}\left(1+O\left((\log x)^{\gamma(\delta_{1}-\frac{1}{\gamma+1})}\right)\right).

(4.94)

Since all the accumulated error terms are of order smaller than
(\log x)^{\gamma/(\gamma+1)}
, the expectation of
\omega
is estimated. The expectation of
\Omega
behaves the same by Lemma
4.2
. ∎
