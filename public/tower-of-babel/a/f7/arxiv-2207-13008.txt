Efficient Algorithms for Sparse Moment Problems without Separation
Zhiyuan Fan, Jian Li

Abstract
We consider the sparse moment problem of learning a k k -spike mixture in high-dimensional space from its noisy moment information in any dimension. We measure the accuracy of the learned mixtures using transportation distance. Previous algorithms either assume certain separation assumptions, use more recovery moments, or run in (super) exponential time. Our algorithm for the one-dimensional problem (also called the sparse Hausdorff moment problem) is a robust version of the classic Prony’s method, and our contribution mainly lies in the analysis. We adopt a global and much tighter analysis than previous work (which analyzes the perturbation of the intermediate results of Prony’s method). A useful technical ingredient is a connection between the linear system defined by the Vandermonde matrix and the Schur polynomial, which allows us to provide tight perturbation bound independent of the separation and may be useful in other contexts. To tackle the high-dimensional problem, we first solve the two-dimensional problem by extending the one-dimensional algorithm and analysis to complex numbers. Our algorithm for the high-dimensional case determines the coordinates of each spike by aligning a 1d projection of the mixture to a random vector and a set of 2d projections of the mixture. Our results have applications to learning topic models and Gaussian mixtures, implying improved sample complexity results or running time over prior work.

Background

We study the moment problem in which
we are given the (noisy) information of the moments of a measure
\mu
, and our goal is to recover
\mu
up to certain accuracy. The moment problem is a classical problem studied for over a century in mathematics, statistics, computer science, physics, control theory, medical image, etc. Various versions of the problem have found numerous applications in different domains (e.g.,
Schmüdgen 2017
;
Lasserre 2009
;
Ang et al. 2002
;
Pintarelli and Vericat 2016
;
Bessis et al. 1976
;
Natterer 2001
).
We study the sparse version of the problem, in which the underlying measure is a mixture of
k
discrete distributions over a common discrete domain
[d]=\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
.
In particular, we consider mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
which is a
k
-spike distribution supported on the
d-1
dimensional simplex

\Delta_{d-1}=\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1,x_{i}\geq 0\,\,\forall i\in[d]\}
. Each point in
\Delta_{d-1}
represents a discrete distribution over
[d]
.
We first introduce the sparse moment problem for
d=2

(also known as the sparse
Hausdorff moment
problem
or the
k
-coin model

(
Schmüdgen 2017
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Gordon et al. 2020
)
).

The sparse Hausdorff moment problem:

For
d=2
, the mixture is a
k
-spike distribution supported on
\Delta_{1}\cong[0,1]
.
We call the model
the
k
-coin model
(i.e., a mixture of
k
Bernoulli distributions).
Denote the underlying mixture as
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})
.
Here,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{k}\}

and
\boldsymbol{w}=\{w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{k}\}\in\Delta_{k-1}
,
where
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]

specifies the
i
th Bernoulli distribution
and
w_{i}\in[0,1]
is the probability of mixture component
\alpha_{i}
.
For mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})
,
the
t
th moment is defined as

M_{t}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\int_{[0,1]}\alpha^{t}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\alpha_{i}^{t}.

Our goal is to recover the unknown parameters
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})
of the mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
given the first
K
noisy moment values
M^{\prime}_{t}
with
|M^{\prime}_{t}-M_{t}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})|_{\infty}\leq\xi
for
1\leq t\leq K
.

We call
K
the
moment number
and
\xi
the moment precision/accuracy, both of which are essential parameters of the problem.
We measure the quality of our estimation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}

in terms of the transportation distance between probability
distributions, i.e.,
\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(\epsilon)
.
Using transportation distance as the metric is advantageous for several reasons:
(1) if the desired accuracy
\epsilon
is much smaller than the minimum separation
\zeta=\min\|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\|
, the estimation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
must be a per-spike recovery since it must contain a spike that is sufficiently close to an original spike in
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
(given the weight of the spike is lower bounded);
(2) if the desired accuracy
\epsilon
is larger than
\zeta
or the minimum weight
w_{\min}
, we are allowed to be
confused by two very close spikes or miss a spike with a very small weight, thus potentially avoiding the inverse dependency on
\zeta
and
w_{\min}
, which are otherwise unavoidable if we must recover every spike.
Generally, for this problem, we are interested in understanding
the relations and trade-offs among the moment number
K
, the moment accuracy/precision
\xi
,
and the accuracy
\epsilon
of our estimation

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
(i.e., transportation distance at most
\epsilon
), and designing efficient algorithms for recovering
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
to the desired accuracy.

The sparse moment problem in higher dimensions:

For the higher dimensional case,
suppose the underlying mixture is

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\}
(
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\in\Delta_{d-1}
which are
d
-dimensional points in a bounded domain) and

\boldsymbol{w}=\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}\in\Delta_{k-1}
.
For vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\ldots,\alpha_{i,d})
and multi-index
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
, we denote monomial
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\mathbf{t}}=\alpha_{i,1}^{t_{1}}\alpha_{i,2}^{t_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{i,d}^{t_{d}}
, and
the
\mathbf{t}
-moment of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
as

M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\int_{\Delta_{d-1}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathbf{t}}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\mathbf{t}}.

In the sparse moment problem, we are given noisy access of
\mathbf{t}
-moment

M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
for
\|\mathbf{t}\|_{1}\leq K
. Here we also call
K
the
moment number
.
Since there are
K^{O(d)}
different moments, when
d
is large, we also consider the case
where we have noisy access to the moments of the projections of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto lower-dimensional subspaces (typically 1d or 2d projections).
An affine transformation of the samples in concrete applications such as topic models can often obtain such noisy moments.

1.1
Applications and connections to prior work

The Hausdorff moment problem and its higher dimensional version have many applications in different areas, such as mathematics, statistics, computer science, physics, etc.
In recent years, the noisy sparse moment problem has found applications
to a variety of unsupervised learning scenarios, including learning topic models
(
Hofmann 1999
;
Blei et al. 2003
;
Papadimitriou et al. 2000
)
, learning Gaussian mixtures
(
Wu and Yang 2020
)
, collaborative filtering
(
Hofmann and Puzicha 1999
;
Kleinberg and Sandler 2008
)
, learning a mixture of product distributions
(
Feldman et al. 2008
;
Gordon et al. 2021a
)
and causal inference
(
Gordon et al. 2021b
)
.
Now, we first discuss the connection between the sparse moment problem and learning mixture models. Then we list some applications in other areas.

Applications to learning mixture models.:

Indeed, several prior results for the sparse moment problem we study in this paper were presented explicitly or implicitly in the context of learning mixture models
(e.g.,
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Gordon et al. 2020
;
Wu and Yang 2020
;
Doss et al. 2020
).
Here we use the problem of learning topic model as an example:
We are given a corpus of documents. We adopt the popular
“bag of words” model and take each document as an unordered multiset of words.
The assumption is that a small number of
k
“pure” topics are distributed over the underlying vocabulary of
d
words. A
K
-word document (i.e., a string in
[d]^{K}
) is generated by first selecting a
topic
p\in\Delta_{d-1}
from the mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, and then sampling

K
words from this topic.

K
is called
the snapshot number
and we call such a sample a
K
-snapshot
of
p
.
For all
\|\mathbf{t}\|_{1}\leq K
, we can obtain noisy estimates of
M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
or
M_{\mathbf{t}}(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))

(where
\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
is the projection of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto a lower dimensional subspace
R
) using
the
K
-snapshots (i.e., the documents in the corpus).
We aim to recover the mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
using as few samples as possible.
Once we have computed the noisy moments using samples, the problem reduces to the sparse moment problem.
We will discuss the applications
to learning topic models in Section
6.1
and to learning Gaussian mixtures in Section
6.2
.

Other Applications.:

We mention a few other problems where our results are directly or potentially useful:

1.

The moment problem can be used for recovering the initial condition for a class of heat diffusion equations
(
Ang et al. 2002
)
: Suppose the initial condition
f(x,t)
at
t=0
is a
k
-spike distribution (e.g.,
k
heat sources), and we can measure
f(x,t)
at
t=1
(with measurement noise), and the task is to recover the initial
k
-spike distribution
(
Ang et al. 2002
)
.
See Remark
G.3
for more details.
In some other PDE problems, the input moment information is computed using a particular numerical algorithm (now the noise comes from the error/precision of the numerical algorithms), and the moment problem is used as a sub-procedure for solving the PDE. For example, the moment problem can be used for approximately solving a class of Poisson equations
(
Pintarelli and Vericat 2016
)
, in which the moment information can be computed using numerical integration.

2.

The Hausdorff moment problem is used as a sub-procedure for bounding/locating the zeros of the partition function of the lattice Ising model (Lee-Yang theory)
(
Bessis et al. 1976
)
. Here the moments can be computed exactly for some special lattices and approximated for more general lattices.

3.

Our algorithm for higher dimensions is also closely related to computerized tomography, in which we would like to recover the 3d (or higher dimensional) object from its 1d or 2d projections
(
Natterer 2001
)
. Existing techniques used the information from a continuous set (or a net) of 1d or 2d projections for recovery (e.g., the classical Radon transform). Our technique (see Theorem
5.2
and Algorithm
3
) only use only
O(d)
1d and 2d projections.

Our Contributions

The
k
-Coin model.

We first discuss prior work on the
k
-coin model (
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
is supported on
[0,1]
),
and our results.

Rabani et al. 2014

showed that recovering a
k
-spike mixture
requires at least the first
K=2k-1
moments in the worst case
(even without any noise).
Moreover, they showed that for the topic learning problem,
if one use
K=c(2k-1)
snapshot samples (for any constant
c\geq 1)

and wish to achieve an accuracy

O(1/k)
in terms of transportation distance,

e^{\Omega(K)}
samples are required
(or equivalently, the moment accuracy should be at most
e^{-\Omega(K)}
).
On the positive side,
they solved the problem using sample complexity

\max\{(1/\zeta)^{O(k)},(k/\epsilon)^{O(k^{2})}\}

(or moment accuracy
\min\{\zeta^{O(k)},(\epsilon/k)^{O(k^{2})}\}
),
where
\zeta
is a lower bound of the minimum separation). In fact, their algorithm is a variant of the classic Prony’s method
and the (post-sampling) running time is
\poly(k)
where their bottleneck is solving a special convex quadratic programming instance.

Li et al. 2015
provided a linear programming-based algorithm
that requires the moment accuracy
(\epsilon/k)^{O(k)}
(or sample complexity

(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}
), matching the lower bound in
Rabani et al. 2014
.
However, after computing the noisy moments, the running time of their algorithm
is super-exponential
k^{O(k^{2})}
.
Motivated by a problem in
population genetics that could be reduced to the
k
-coin mixture problem,

Kim et al. 2019
analyzed the Matrix Pencil Method,
which requires moment accuracy
\zeta^{O(k)}\cdot w_{\min}^{2}\cdot\epsilon
.
The matrix pencil method requires solving a generalized eigenvalue problem,
which needs
O(k^{3})
time.

Wu and Yang 2020
studied the same problem in the context of learning Gaussian mixtures. In fact, they showed that learning 1d Gaussian mixture models with the same variance can be reduced to learning the
k
-coin model. Their algorithm achieves the optimal dependency on
the moment accuracy without separation assumption.
Their recovery algorithm is based on SDP and runs in
time
O(k^{2\omega})
for some
\omega<2.373
using the state-of-the-art SDP solver
(
Huang et al. 2022
)
.
Recently,
Gordon et al. 2020
showed that
under the separation assumption,
it is possible to recover all
k
spikes up to accuracy
\epsilon
, using
the first
2k
-moments with accuracy
\zeta^{O(k)}\cdot w_{\min}\cdot\epsilon
. Their algorithm is also a variant of Prony’s method and it runs in time
O(k^{2+o(1)})
.

Our techniques:

Our algorithm for the
k
-coin model is also based on the classic Prony’s method
(
de Prony 1795
)
.
Suppose the true mixture is
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k}]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}
.
In Prony’s method, if we know the moment vector
M
exactly,
every
\alpha_{i}
can be recovered from the roots
of a polynomial whose coefficients are the entries of the eigenvector
\boldsymbol{c}

(corresponding to eigenvalue 0)
of a Hankel matrix (see e.g., the matrix
{\mathcal{H}}_{k+1}
in
Gordon et al. 2020
).
However, if we only know the noisy moment vector
M^{\prime}
, we only get a perturbation of the original Hankel matrix.
Recent analyses of Prony’s method
(
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Gordon et al. 2020
)
aim to upper bound the error of recovery from the perturbed Hankel matrix.
If
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
(
i\neq j
) are very close, or any
w_{i}
is very small, the second smallest eigenvalue of the Hankel matrix is also very close to
0
. From matrix perturbation theory, we know that the eigenvector
\boldsymbol{c}
corresponding to eigenvalue
0
is extremely sensitive to small perturbation (see e.g.,
Stewart 1990
).
Hence, the recent analyses of Prony’s method
(
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Gordon et al. 2020
)

requires that
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are separated by at least
\zeta
, and the minimum
w_{i}
is lower bounded by
w_{\min}
to ensure certain stability of the eigenvector (hence the coefficients of the polynomial), and the corresponding sample complexity becomes unbounded when
\zeta
or
w_{\min}
approaches to 0.
Our algorithm (Algorithm
1
) can be seen as a robust version of Prony’s method. Instead of computing the smallest eigenvector of the perturbed Hankel matrix, we solve a ridge regression to obtain a vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
, which plays a similar role as
\boldsymbol{c}
(Line
1
in Algorithm
1
). However, instead of showing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
is close to
\boldsymbol{c}
(in fact, they can be very different), we adopt a more global analysis to show that the moment vector of the estimated mixture is close to the true moment vector
M
, and by a moment-transportation inequality
(see Section
B
),
we can guarantee the quantity of our solution in terms of transportation distance.

Another technical challenge lies in bounding the error in recovering the weight vector
\boldsymbol{w}
. In the noiseless case, the weight is simply
the solution of
V_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{w}=M
where
V_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a Vandemonde matrix
(see Equation (
2
)).
It is known that Vandermonde matrices tend to be badly ill-conditioned
(
Gautschi and Inglese 1987
;
Pan 2016
;
Moitra 2015
)
(with a large dependency on the inverse of the minimal separation). Hence, using standard condition number-based analysis, slight perturbations of
V_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
M
may result in an unbounded error.

1

Note that for Vandermonde matrix
V=(a^{j}_{i})_{0\leq i<k,1\leq j\leq k}
, its determinant
is
\det(V)=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}\prod_{p<q}(a_{p}-a_{q})
.
Hence,
\det(V)^{-1}
depends inversely on the separation.

However, we show interestingly, in our case, that we can bound the error independent of the separation (Lemma

) via the connection between Vandermonde linear systems and Schur polynomial (Lemma

).
Our technology may be useful in analyzing the perturbation of Vandermonde linear systems
in other contexts as well.

Higher Dimensions.

For the high-dimensional case,
especially when
d
is large, it is costly to obtain all moments

M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
for all
\mathbf{t}
with
\|\mathbf{t}\|_{1}\leq K
.
Indeed, most previous work

(
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Wu and Yang 2020
;
Gordon et al. 2020
;
Doss et al. 2020
)
on learning mixtures solved the problem using noisy moment information of the projections of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto some lower-dimensional subspaces (in fact, lines).
In particular, if one projects
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
to
O(k)
directions such as in
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Wu and Yang 2020
;
Gordon et al. 2020
,
existing techniques require the separation assumption for the recovery.

to an algorithm without a separation assumption by merging close-by spikes and removing

Our techniques:

We first solve the two-dimensional problem.
This is done by extending the previous one-dimensional algorithm and
its analysis to complex numbers. The real and imaginary parts of a complex location can represent the two dimensions, respectively.
While most ideas are similar to the one-dimensional case,
the analysis requires extra care in various places. In particular,
we need to extend the moment-transportation inequality to complex numbers
(Appendix
B
).

Our algorithm for the high-dimensional case uses
the algorithms for one-dimensional and two-dimensional cases as subroutines.
We first pick a random vector
\boldsymbol{r}

and learn the projection
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto
\boldsymbol{r}
using the
one-dimensional algorithm.
It is not difficult to show that for two spikes that are far apart, with reasonable probability,
their projections on
\boldsymbol{r}
are also far apart.
Hence, the remaining task is to recover the coordinates (in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) of each spike in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
.
So, we learn the 2d-linear map
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
\boldsymbol{r}
and the
t
th dimensional axis for each dimension
t\in[d]
.
Combining the information from the 1d projected measure
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
and the 2d projected measure
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t}
, we show that we can extract the
t
th coordinates of all spikes.

Application to Learning Topic Models.

Our result for the high-dimensional case
can be easily translated into an algorithm for topic modeling.
Previously,
Rabani et al. 2014
showed that
it is possible to produce an estimate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
of the original mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}

such that
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq\epsilon
,
using
\poly(n,k,1/\epsilon)
1- and 2-snapshot samples, and
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k^{2})}\cdot(w_{\min}\zeta)^{-O(k^{2})}

K
-snapshot samples, under minimal separation assumptions.

Li et al. 2015
provided
an LP-based learning algorithm which
uses almost the same number of samples (with a slightly worse polynomial for 2-snapshot samples), but
without requiring any separation assumptions.
The number of the
(2k-1)
-snapshot samples used by both
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
are
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k^{2})}

while that of the lower bound (for 1d) is only
e^{\Omega(k)}
.
Recently,
Gordon et al. 2020
showed that
under the minimal separation assumption,
the sample complexity could be reduced to
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}\cdot w_{\min}^{-O(1)}\cdot\zeta^{-O(k)}
.
We obtain for the first time the worst case optimal sample complexity for the high-dimensional case, improving previous work

(
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015

We also study the problem of learning the parameters of Gaussian mixtures.

We can leverage our algorithm for the
k
-coin model for the one-dimensional setting.
Our algorithm achieves the same sample complexity as in

O(k^{2\omega})
-time algorithm developed by
Wu and Yang 2020
.
See Theorem
G.2
for more details.

For the high-dimensional setting,
we can use the dimension reduction technique in
Li et al. 2015
or

Doss et al. 2020
to reduce the dimension

to
O(k)
.
The dimension reduction part is not a bottleneck and we assume

for the
the following discussion.
We show in Section

that we can utilize our algorithm for the high-dimensional sparse moment problem and obtain an algorithm without any separation assumption with sample complexity
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}
. Note that the algorithm in
Wu and Yang 2020
requires a sample size of

Recently,

removed the separation assumption

(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}
.
Compared with

Last but not least, we argue that improving the post-sampling running time is very important,
despite the exponential sample complexity for both problems.
During the sampling phase, we only need to keep track of the first few moments (e.g., using basic operations such as counting or adding numbers), hence the sampling phase can be easily distributed, streamed, or implemented in inexpensive computing devices. Our mixture recovery algorithm only requires the moment information (without storing the samples) and runs in time polynomial in

Moreover, one may well have other means to measure the moments to achieve the desired moment accuracy (e.g., via longer documents, prior knowledge, existing samples, etc.)
Exploiting other settings and applications is an interesting further direction.

We are given a statistical mixture

We use

in which
w_{i}

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
.
Since the dimension of
\Delta_{d-1}
is
d-1
, we say the
dimension
of the problem is
d-1
.
In addition, we use
\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{d-1},\Delta_{k-1})

to denote the set of all such mixtures where
\Delta_{d-1}
indicates
the domain of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
and
\Delta_{k-1}

\boldsymbol{w}
.
Since our algorithm may produce negative or complex weights as intermediate results, we further denote
\Sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{d-1}=\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\}

and

\Sigma^{\mathbb{C}}_{d-1}=\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\}
.

For the one-dimensional case (which is called the
k
-coin problem or
sparse Hausdorff moment problem),
we have

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{k-1})

where

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{k}\}
is a set of
k
discrete points in
[0,1]
.
In this scenario, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the
t
th moment as

M_{t}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\int_{[0,1]}\alpha^{t}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\alpha_{i}^{t}.

For the higher dimensional case, we use

\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
such that
\|\mathbf{t}\|_{1}=K(=2k-1)

to denote a multi-index.
In addition, we denote monomial

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\mathbf{t}}=\alpha_{i,1}^{t_{1}}\alpha_{i,2}^{t_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{i,d}^{t_{d}}
for every discrete distribution

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\alpha_{i,2},\cdots,\alpha_{i,d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The moment vector of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
is then defined as

\displaystyle M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\left\{M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right\}_{\|\mathbf{t}\|_{1}\leq K}\text{ where }M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\int_{\Delta_{d-1}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\mathbf{t}}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\mathbf{t}}.

(1)

We define the

between two mixtures
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}

as the

norm of the difference between corresponding moment vectors:

\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}):=\max_{|\mathbf{t}|\leq K}|M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})-M_{\mathbf{t}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})|.

Transportation Distance:

For any two probability measures
P
and
Q
defined over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
L_{1}
-transportation distance
\tran(P,Q)
is defined as

\tran(P,Q):=\inf\Big\{\int\|x-y\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x,y):\mu\in M(P,Q)\Big\}

where
M(P,Q)
is the set of all joint distributions (also called coupling) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
P
and
Q
.
Transportation distance is also called Rubinstein distance, Wasserstein distance
or earth mover distance in the literature.
We also need to define transportation distance for signed measures and
complex domains. See Appendix
A
.

4.1
Proof Sketch

In this section, we present a proof sketch of Theorem
4.1
.
The complete proof of the theorem can be found in Appendix
C.2
.
Firstly, using the relationship between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, one can show that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}-\widehat{\alpha}_{j})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{j}\alpha_{i}^{j}+\alpha_{i}^{k}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\widehat{c}_{i}-c_{i})\Bigl(A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\Bigr)_{i}

Since we use the ridge regression to get
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
(Line
1
), the norms of both
A_{M^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M^{\prime}}
and
\widehat{c}
are small. Note that
M^{\prime}
is close to
M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
, the norm of vector
A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}
is also small. Hence one can get

\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}-\widehat{\alpha}_{j})^{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\zeta.

The above inequality shows that every ground truth spike
a_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
has a close spike
\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\in\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
where the closeness depends on the weight of the spike
w_{i}
. We note that this property tolerates permutation and small spikes weights, enabling us to analyze mixtures without separation.

Next, note we can decompose
M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}M(\alpha_{i})
where
M(\alpha_{i})
is the moment vector generated by the single-spike distribution located at
\alpha_{i}
.
By triangular inequality, the error of linear regression (Line
5
)
can be decomposed by

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M(\alpha_{i})\|_{2},

Therefore, to bound
\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{2}\approx\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{2}
, it is sufficient to prove
\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M(\alpha_{i})\|
for each
\alpha_{i}
is bounded.
We use
\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-M(\alpha)\|_{2}
for a
specific
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
as its upper bound.

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is chosen to be the vector in which the first
k
rows of
V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
and
M(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
are equal
(
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is the solution of a linear system).
By binomial expansion, we can compute the
j
th row of
V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
for large
j>k
by

\displaystyle(V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}^{*})_{j}=\sum_{p=1}^{j}\binom{j}{p}\alpha_{i}^{j-p}\sum_{t=1}x_{t}^{*}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{i})^{p}.

Expressing
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
using the adjoint matrix, using Using Cramer’s rule, and plugging it into the right-hand side of the above equality, the resulting term appears to
be difficult to bound
(see the third equation in the analysis of Lemma
C.9
).
Fortunately, by leveraging a matrix equation derived from the Shur polynomial, one can get the following nice equation for the inner summation,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}x_{t}^{*}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{i})^{p}=\prod_{t=1}^{k}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{i})\cdot\sum_{s\subseteq(k)^{p-k}}\prod_{t=1}^{k}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{i})^{s_{t}},

where we use
(a)^{b}\subset\{0,\cdots,b\}^{a}
to denote the set that contains all vector
s
in which
\sum_{i=1}^{a}s_{i}=b
.
Since
(k)^{p-k}
has no more than
2^{O(p-k)}
elements, one can thus easily bound the approximation error by:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\|_{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}-\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}|

from the right-hand side of the above equation.

Note that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}
are
\widehat{\alpha}_{i}
close, combining all the above results gives
\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\sqrt{\zeta}
, indicating moment distance between the recovered distribution and the ground truth is small. Finally, one can reach the desired statement using the moment-transportation inequality (Lemma
B.1
), which asserts that if the moment distance between two measures is sufficiently small, the transportation distance between them is also small.

The algorithm for higher dimensions takes both the one-dimensional and two-dimensional algorithms as subroutines.

5.1
An Efficient Algorithm for the Two-Dimensional Problem

We first generalize the one-dimensional algorithm described in Section
4
to the dimension of two.
Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}:=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})

be the underlying mixture where

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}]^{\top}
for two-dimensional points
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\alpha_{i,2})
and weight vector
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{k-1}
.
The true moments is defined by
M_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t,1}^{i}\alpha_{t,2}^{j}
.
We assume the input noisy moments
M^{\prime}_{i,j}
satisfies
|M^{\prime}_{i,j}-M_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})|\leq\xi
for every
0\leq i,j,i+j\leq 2k-1
. We further assume that
M^{\prime}_{0,0}=M_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=1
and noise level
\xi\leq 2^{-\Omega(k)}
.

The key idea for our algorithm is that,
a distribution supported in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be mapped to a distribution supported in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, let complex simplex
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}=\{a+b{\mathfrak{i}}\mid(a,b)\in\Delta_{2}\}
.
We denote
\boldsymbol{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{k}]^{\top}:=[\alpha_{1,1}+\alpha_{1,2}{\mathfrak{i}},\cdots,\alpha_{k,1}+\alpha_{k,2}{\mathfrak{i}}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
and define
\boldsymbol{\phi}:=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k},\Delta_{k-1})
to be the complex mixture corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
.
The corresponding moments of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
can thus be defined as
G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{j}
, where
\beta_{t}^{\dagger}
is the complex conjugate of
\beta_{t}
.
We note
G_{i,j}
can be computed from
M_{i,j}
(see Lemma
D.1
).
Therefore, the task reduces to the recovery of the complex mixture
\boldsymbol{\phi}
given noisy moments
G^{\prime}_{i,j}
.
The algorithm and the proof of the following theorem are similar to that in Section
4
.
Handling complex numbers requires several minor changes, and we present the full details in Appendix
D
.
For the analysis, we also need to generalize the moment-transportation inequality to the complex domains (see Appendix
B.2
).

Theorem 5.1
.

(The Two-dimensional Problem)
Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})
.
Suppose we have noisy moments

(

5.2
An Efficient Algorithm for Dimension

Now, we present our algorithm for higher dimensions.
Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}:=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{d-1},\Delta_{k-1})

be the underlying mixture where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=[\alpha_{i,1},\cdots,\alpha_{i,d}]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}
. We denote
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{:,i}=[\alpha_{1,i},\cdots,\alpha_{k,i}]
as the vector of the
i
th coordinates of the
spikes and also write
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{:,1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{:,k}]^{\top}
.

Since the number of different moments is exponential in
d
,
we consider the setting in which one can
access the noisy moments of some linear maps of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto lower-dimensional subspaces, as in previous works
(
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
)

(such noisy moments can be easily obtained in applications such as topic modeling, see Section
6.1
, or in learning Gaussian mixtures, see
Section
6.2
).
For some
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Delta_{k-1})
and a vector
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}):=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathbb{R},\Delta_{k-1})
as the projected distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
along vector
\boldsymbol{r}
. For a matrix
R=[\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{r}_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
, we also denote
\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}):=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}R,\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\Delta_{k-1})
as the projected distribution of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
along multiple dimensions
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{r}_{p}
. In this section, we consider mappings to one-dimensional and two-dimensional subspaces, which suffice for our purposes.

The idea of our algorithm is as follows:
We first generate a random vector
\boldsymbol{r}
.
Then with some constant probability, the distance between spikes is roughly kept after the projection along
\boldsymbol{r}
.
This enables us to recover a projected measure
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
along
\boldsymbol{r}
of the discrete mixture by running the one-dimensional algorithm on the projected moments.
Next, we try to assign every spike of the projected measure a coordinate in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is done by running the two-dimensional algorithm over the plane spanned by
\boldsymbol{r}
and unit vector
e_{t}
for each dimension
t\in[d]
to get
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
, and assigning the
t
-th coordinate of each spike in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
the coordinate of the closet spike in
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
.
We use a geometric argument to show the recovered distribution is close to the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
in terms of the transportation distance (see Lemma

).

Theorem 5.2

6.1
Applications to Topic Models

This section discusses the application of our results to topic models.
Due to space limitations, the full details are deferred to Appendix
F
.
Consider the “bag of words” model in which we take each document as an unordered multiset of words.
Let
d
be the number of different words in the document.
We assume there are
k
topics
\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\cdots\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\}
that are distributed over
[d]
.
Each document is generated by first selecting a topic
i\in\Delta_{d-1}
from the mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, and then sampling
K
i.i.d. words according to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
from this topic.
Here,
K
is referred to as
the snapshot number
and we call such a sample a
K
-snapshot
of
p
.
Our goal is to recover
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{d-1},\Delta_{k-1})
that is a discrete distribution over
k
pure topics.
If
d\gg k
,
we can use the dimension reduction technique in
Li et al. 2015
to reduce the dimension
d
to
O(k)
, using
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-6}k^{3}d)
1-snapshots and
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-4}k^{2}d)
2-snapshots, respectively.
Then, we can apply Theorem
5.2
to derive the required
moment accuracy. Translating the moment accuracy to the sample complexity is standard. In sum, we can obtain the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

There is an algorithm that can learn an arbitrary
k
-spike mixture supported in
\Delta_{d-1}

for any
d
within
L_{1}
transportation distance
O(\epsilon)
with probability at least
0.99
using

\poly(d,k,\frac{1}{\epsilon})
,
\poly(d,k,\frac{1}{\epsilon})
,
many
1
-,
2
-,snapshots and
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}
many
(2k-1)
-snapshots.

6.2
Applications to Gaussian Mixture Learning

This section shows how to leverage our results
for sparse moment problems to obtain improved algorithms for learning Gaussian mixtures. The full details can be found in Appendix
G
.
We consider the following setting
studied in
Wu and Yang 2020
;
Doss et al. 2020
.
We parameterise a
k
-Gaussian mixture in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{N}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w},\Sigma)
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=\{w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{k}\}\in\Delta_{k-1}
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
w_{i}\in[0,1]
represents the mean and weight of
i
th component.
We assume all
k
mixture components share a common covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that is known in advance and the maximal eigenvalue of
\Sigma
is bounded by a constant.
We focus on parameter learning, that is, to learn the parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
given known covariance matrix
\Sigma
and a set of i.i.d. samples from
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}N(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\Sigma)
. We have the following result:

Theorem 6.2
.

There is an algorithm that can learn an arbitrary
d
-dimensional
k
-spike Gaussian mixture (with known common covariance matrix
\Sigma
) within transportation distance
O(\epsilon)
with probability at least
0.99

using
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}+\poly(1/\epsilon,d,k)
samples.

7
Concluding Remarks

We provide efficient algorithms for learning the
k
-mixture over discrete distributions from noisy moments.
We measure the accuracy in terms of transportation distance, and our analysis is independent of the minimal separation.
The techniques used in our analysis for the one-dimensional case may be useful in the perturbative analysis of other problems involving the Hankel matrix or the Vandermonde matrix (such as super-resolution).

Our problem is a special case of learning mixtures of product distribution
(
Gordon et al. 2021a
)
, which is further a special case of the general problem of learning mixtures of Bayesian networks
(
Gordon et al. 2021b
)
, which has important applications in causal inferences. In fact, the algorithms in
Gordon et al. 2021a
;
Gordon et al. 2021b
used the algorithm for sparse Hausdorff moment problem as a subroutine. It would be interesting to see if our techniques can be useful in these problems.

Appendix A
Transportation Distance for Non-Probability Measures

Let
1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}
be the set of 1-Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}:=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mid|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\|x-y\|_{1},\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
.
We need the following important theorem by Kantorovich and Rubinstein (see e.g.,
Dudley 2002
),
which states the dual formulation of transportation distance:

\displaystyle\tran(P,Q)=\sup\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}(P-Q):f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}\right\}.

(3)

The dual formulation (
3
) can be generalized to non-probability measures.
For two signed measures
P
and
Q
defined over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\int P\mathrm{d}x=\int Q\mathrm{d}x=1
, we still can use (
3
) to compute its transportation distance.

We now introduce the equivalent primal formulation as follows:
For some signed measure
R
, we define measure
R^{+}
as its positive components that
R^{+}(x)=\max\{R(x),0\}
. According to the duality formulation (
3
), one can see
\tran(P,Q)=\tran((P-Q)^{+},(Q-P)^{+})
where both
(P-Q)^{+}
and
(Q-P)^{+}
are non-negative. From the primal formulation of transportation distance, we conclude another definition for generalized transportation distance:

\displaystyle\tran(P,Q)=\inf\left\{\int\|x-y\|_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu(x,y):\mu\in M((P-Q)^{+},(Q-P)^{+})\right\}.

(4)

We also need to define the transportation distance over
complex measures.
Denote
\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}:|\alpha|\leq 1\}
.
For some complex measure
P
that
\int_{\mathbb{C}}\textrm{d}P(x)=1
. We denote the real and imaginary components of
P
as
P^{\textrm{r}}
and
P^{\textrm{i}}
respectively that
P(x)=P^{\textrm{r}}(x)+{\mathfrak{i}}P^{\textrm{i}}(x)
for
P^{\textrm{r}}(x),P^{\textrm{i}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
. We generalize transportation distance to complex weights by

\displaystyle\tran(P,Q)=\tran(P^{\textrm{r}},Q^{\textrm{r}})+\tran(P^{\textrm{i}},Q^{\textrm{i}}).

(5)

We also have this transportation distance in a dual form:

\displaystyle\tran(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int f^{\textrm{r}}\mathrm{d}(P^{\textrm{r}}-Q^{\textrm{r}}):f^{\textrm{r}}\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}\right\}+\sup\left\{\int f^{\textrm{i}}\mathrm{d}(P^{\textrm{i}}-Q^{\textrm{i}}):f^{\textrm{i}}\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\textrm{real}\left(\int(f^{\textrm{r}}-{\mathfrak{i}}f^{\textrm{i}})\mathrm{d}(P-Q)\right):f^{\textrm{r}},f^{\textrm{i}}\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}(P-Q):f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}_{\mathbb{C}}\right\}

where
1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}_{\mathbb{C}}:=\{f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mid|f(x)-f(y)|\leq|x-y|\text{ for any }x,y\in\mathbb{C}\}
is the complex 1-Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{C}
, and the last inequality holds since we can assign
f(x)=(f^{\textrm{r}}(x)-{\mathfrak{i}}f^{\textrm{i}}(x))/2
.

Appendix B
Moment-Transportation Inequalities

Our analysis needs to bound the transportation distance
by a function of moment distance.
We first present a moment-transportation inequality
in one dimension (Lemma
B.1
),
and then generalize the result to complex numbers and higher dimensions.
The first such inequality in one dimension is obtained
in
Rabani et al. 2014
(with worse parameters).
Lemma
B.1
is firstly proved by

Wu and Yang 2020
in the context of learning Gaussian mixtures.
Here, we prove the theorem in our notations for completeness,
and later we extend the proof to complex domains in Section
B.2
.

B.1
A One-Dimensional Moment-Transportation Inequality

We allow more general mixtures with spikes in
[-1,1]
and negative weights.
This slight generalization will be useful since some intermediate coordinates and weights may be negative. Recall that
\Sigma_{k-1}=\{\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}=1\}
.

Lemma B.1
.

(
Wu and Yang 2020
)
For any two mixtures with
k
components
\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Spike}([-1,1],\Sigma_{k-1})
, it holds that

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq O(k\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}).

We begin with some notations.
Let
\mathrm{Supp}=\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\cup\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})
and let
n=|\mathrm{Supp}|
(
n\leq 2k
).
Arrange the points
in
\mathrm{Supp}
as
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
where
\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
.

Definition B.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
such that
-1\leq\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}\leq 1
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
denote the set of polynomials of degree at most
n-1
and 1-Lipschitz over
the discrete points in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\{f\mid\deg f\leq n-1,f(\alpha_{1})=0;\,|f(\alpha_{i})-f(\alpha_{j})|\leq|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\,\,\forall i,j\in[n].\}

Note that we do not require
f
to be 1-Lipschitz over the entire interval
[-1,1]
.
From the dual formulation of transportation distance (see e.g.,
Dudley 2002
), we have the following proposition:

Proposition B.3
.

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})=\sup\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}):f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}\right\}=\sup\left\{\int p\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}):p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\}
.

We can view
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as a subset in the linear space of all polynomials of degree at most
n-1
.
In fact,
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a convex polytope
(a convex combination of two polynomials being 1-Lipschitz over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}

is also a polynomial that is 1-Lipschitz over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
).
The
height
of a polynomial
\sum_{i}c_{i}x^{i}
is the maximum absolute coefficient
\max_{i}|c_{i}|
.
As we will see shortly, the height of a polynomial in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is related to the required moment accuracy, and we need the height to be upper bounded by a value independent of the minimum separation

\zeta
. However, a polynomial in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
may have a very large height (depending on the inverse of the minimum
separation
1/|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|
).
This can be seen from the Lagrangian interpolation formula:

p(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p(\alpha_{j})\ell_{j}(x)

where
\ell_{j}
is the Lagrange basis polynomial

\ell_{j}(x):=\prod_{1\leq m\leq n,m\neq j}(x-\alpha_{m})/(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{m}).

To remedy this, one can show that for any polynomial in
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, there is an approximate polynomial with bounded height:

Lemma B.4
.

For any polynomial
p(x)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\eta>0
,
there is a polynomial
p_{\eta}(x)
of degree at most
n-1
such that the following properties hold:

1.

|p_{\eta}(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2\eta
, for any
i\in[n]
.

2.

The height of
p_{\eta}
is at most
n2^{2n-2}(\frac{n}{\eta})^{n-2}
.

Before proving Lemma
B.4
, we show Lemma
B.1
can be easily derived from
Proposition
B.3

and Lemma
B.4
.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

Fix any constant
\eta>0
.
Let
\xi=\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}}\int f\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})=\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\int p\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hfill(\text{Proposition~\ref{pro:simple}})

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta+\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\int p_{\eta}\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle(\text{Lemma~\ref{lm:decomposition}})

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta+\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}n2^{2n-2}\left(\frac{n}{\eta}\right)^{n-2}\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle(\text{Lemma~\ref{lm:decomposition}})

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta+2^{4k}(2k)^{2k}\frac{\xi}{\eta^{2k-2}}

\displaystyle(n\leq 2k)

By choosing
\eta=16k\xi^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}
,
we conclude that
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{2k-1}})
.
∎

Next, we prove Lemma
B.4
.
We introduce the following set
{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of special polynomials.
They are in fact the extreme points of the polytope
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Definition B.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
such that
-1\leq\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}\leq 1
.
Let

{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\{p\mid\deg p\leq n-1,p(\alpha_{1})=0;\,|p(\alpha_{i+1})-p(\alpha_{i})|=|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\,\,\forall i\in[n-1]\}

It is easy to see that
{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
|{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|=2^{n-1}
.
Now, we modify each polynomial

p\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
slightly, as follows.
Consider the intervals
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}],\cdots,[\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}]
.
If
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}\leq\eta/n
, we say the interval
[\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i}]
is
a
small interval
.
Otherwise, it is
large
.
We first merge all consecutive small intervals.
For each resulting interval, we merge it with the large interval to its right.
Note that we never merge two large intervals together.
Let
S=\{\alpha_{i_{1}}=\alpha_{1},\alpha_{i_{2}},\cdots,\alpha_{i_{m}}=\alpha_{n}\}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\alpha}

be the endpoints of the current intervals.
It is easy to see the distance between any two points in
S

is at least
\eta/n
(since the current interval contains exactly one original large interval).
Define a continuous piecewise linear function
L:[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{n}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:
(1)

L(\alpha_{1})=p(\alpha_{1})=0
.
(2)
The breaking points are the points in
S
;
(3)
Each linear piece of
L
has slope either
1
or
-1
;
for two consecutive breaking points
\alpha_{i_{j}},\alpha_{i_{j+1}}\in S
,
if
p(\alpha_{i_{j}})>p(\alpha_{i_{j+1}})
, the slope of the corresponding piece is
-1
.
Otherwise, it is
1
.

Lemma B.6
.

For any
\alpha_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
|L(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2\eta
.

Proof.

We prove inductively that
|L(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2i\eta/n
.
The base case (
i=1
) is trivial.
Suppose the lemma holds true for all value at most

i
and now we show it holds for
i+1
.
There are two cases.
If
\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}\leq\eta/n
,
we have

\displaystyle|L(\alpha_{i+1})-p(\alpha_{i+1})|

\displaystyle\leq|L(\alpha_{i+1})-L(\alpha_{i})|+|L(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|+|p(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i+1})|

\displaystyle=2|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|+|L(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2(i+1)\eta/n.

If
\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}>\eta/n
, we have
\alpha_{i+1}\in S
(by the merge procedure).
Suppose
\alpha_{j}
(
\alpha\leq i
) is the point in
S
right before
\alpha_{i+1}
.
We can see that (by the definition of
p
):

|p(\alpha_{j})-p(\alpha_{i+1})|\in|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\pm|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\subseteq|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|\pm(j-i)\eta/n.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle|L(\alpha_{i+1})-p(\alpha_{i+1})|

\displaystyle\leq|L(\alpha_{i+1})-L(\alpha_{j})+p(\alpha_{j})-p(\alpha_{i+1})|+|L(\alpha_{j})-p(\alpha_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq 2(i+1-j)\eta/n+2j\eta/n\leq 2(i+1)\eta/n,

where second inequality holds since
L(\alpha_{i+1})-L(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{i-1}-\alpha_{j}
when

and

L(\alpha_{i+1})-L(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i+1}
when
p(\alpha_{i+1})\leq p(\alpha_{j})
.
∎

Let
T_{\eta}(p)
be the polynomial with degree
\leq n-1
that interpolates

T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})=L(\alpha_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
. )

T_{\eta}(p)
is unique by this definition and

.
Now, we show the height of
T_{\eta}(p)
can be bounded, independent of the
minimum separation
|\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}|
, due to its special structure.

Lemma B.7
.

For any polynomial
p(x)\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, define
polynomial
T_{\eta}(p)
as the above.
Suppose

We have

for

Proof.

The key to the proof is Newton’s interpolation formula, which we briefly review here

Let

F[\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t+1}]=\frac{T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{t+1})-T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{t})}{\alpha_{t+1}-\alpha_{t}},\cdots,

Then, by Newton’s interpolation formula, we can write that

T_{\eta}(p)(x)=F[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}](x-\alpha_{1})+F[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}](x-\alpha_{1})(x-\alpha_{2})+\cdots+F[\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}]\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(x-\alpha_{i}).

By the definition of
T_{\eta}(p)
,
we know that every
2

is either
1
or
-1
.
Now, we show inductively that the absolute value of
the

for any

.
The base case is simple:

F[\alpha,\alpha_{t+1}]=\pm 1
.
Now, we prove it for

.
We distinguish two cases.

1.

If
\alpha_{t+i-1}-\alpha_{t}\leq\eta/n
,

Therefore, we can see that

F[\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t+1}]=F[\alpha_{t+1},\alpha_{t+2}]=\cdots=F[\alpha_{t+1},\alpha_{t+i-1}]
,
from which we can see any 3rd order difference (hence the 4th, 5th, up to the

Suppose

By the induction hypothesis, we have that

\displaystyle|F[\alpha_{t},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i-1}]|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{F[\alpha_{t},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i-2}]-F[\alpha_{t+1},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i-1}]}{\alpha_{t+i-1}-\alpha_{t}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 2^{i-3}\left(\frac{n}{\eta}\right)^{i-3}\cdot\frac{n}{\eta}\leq 2^{i-2}\left(\frac{n}{\eta}\right)^{i-2}.

Also since

∎

Proof of Lemma
B.4
.

For every polynomial

.
Let

B.2
Moment-Transportation Inequality over Complex Numbers

the mixture weight

can also be complex numbers.
Recall in the complex domain,
the definition of transportation distance is extended according to (
5
).

We first see that
n
complex numbers in
\mathbb{C}
can be clustered with the following guarantee.

Proposition B.8
.

For
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and any constant
\eta>0
, we can partition these
n
points into clusters such that:

1.

|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|<\eta
if
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are in the same cluster.

2.

|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|>\eta/n
if
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are in different clusters.

Let
\mathrm{Supp}=\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\cup\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})
and let
n=|\mathrm{Supp}|

(n\leq 2k)
. Arrange the points in
\mathrm{Supp}
as
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
such that each cluster lies in a continuous segment. In other words, if
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
lie in the same cluster for indexes
i<j
, then
\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
and
\alpha_{j^{\prime}}
also lie in the same cluster for all
i\leq i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq j
; if
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
lie in the different cluster for indexes
i<j
, then
\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
and
\alpha_{j^{\prime}}
also lie in the different cluster for all
i^{\prime}\leq i<j\leq j^{\prime}
.

Definition B.9
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
be the set of polynomials of degree at most
n-1
and Lipschitz over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\{P\mid\deg P\leq n-1,P(\alpha_{1})=0;\,|P(\alpha_{i})-P(\alpha_{j})|\leq|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\,\,\forall i,j\in[n]\}.

From the discussion in Appendix
A
, we have the following proposition on the generalized transportation distance.

Proposition B.10
.

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq 2\sup\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}):f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}_{\mathbb{C}}\right\}=2\sup\left\{\int p\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}):p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\}
.

Similar to the real case, we only need to focus on the extreme points of
{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Definition B.11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Let

{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\left\{p\large\mid\deg p\leq n-1,p(\alpha_{1})=0;\,\frac{p(\alpha_{i+1})-p(\alpha_{i})}{\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}}\in\{\pm 1,\pm{\mathfrak{i}}\}\,\,\forall i\in[n-1]\right\}.

It is easy to see that
|{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|=4^{n-1}
.

Again, we modify each polynomial
p\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
slightly.
We define a degree-
(n-1)
polynomial
T_{\eta}(p):\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by assigning values to all
\alpha_{i}
for
i\in[n]
as follows:

1.

If
\alpha_{i}
is one of the first two points in the cluster corresponding to
\alpha_{i}
, assign
T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})=p(\alpha_{i})
.

2.

Otherwise, denote
\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1}
as the first two points of the cluster, we would assign
T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})
to be the linear interpolation of
T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{j})
and
T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{j}+1)
. In concrete,

T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})=\frac{p(\alpha_{j+1})-p(\alpha_{j})}{\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})+p(\alpha_{j}).

Since we have fixed
n
points in
n-1
-degree polynomial
T_{\eta}(p)
, we can see that
T_{\eta}(p)
is uniquely determined.
The following lemma shows
T_{\eta}(p)
is close to
p
over the points in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Lemma B.12
.

|T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2\eta

for each
\alpha_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Proof.

We only need to prove the case that
\alpha_{i}
is not the first two points in the cluster.

\displaystyle|T_{\eta}(p)(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{p(\alpha_{j+1})-p(\alpha_{j})}{\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})+p(\alpha_{j})-p(\alpha_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{p(\alpha_{j+1})-p(\alpha_{j})}{\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})\right|+\left|p(\alpha_{j})-p(\alpha_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\leq 2\eta,

where the third inequality holds since
|p(\alpha_{j+1})-p(\alpha_{j})|=|\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{j}|
and the last inequality holds because
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are in the same cluster.
∎

Lemma B.13
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Suppose
T_{\eta}(p)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}x^{i}
. We have
|c_{t}|\leq n2^{2n-2}(\frac{n}{\eta})^{n-2}
.

Proof.

Again, we would use Newton’s interpolation polynomials.
Let
F[x_{1},\cdots,x_{i}]
be the
i
th order difference of
T_{\eta}(p)
.
By definition of
T_{\eta}(p)
, we know that every 2nd order difference of
T_{\eta}(p)
is in
\{\pm 1,\pm{\mathfrak{i}}\}
. Now, we show inductively that the absolute value of
i
th order difference absolute value is at most
(2n/\eta)^{i-2}
for any
i=3,\cdots,n
. We distinguish two cases:

1.

If
|\alpha_{t+i}-\alpha_{t}|<\eta/n
, then all
\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t+1},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i}
lie in the same cluster (according to the assigned order). Therefore, we can see that
F[\alpha_{t},\alpha_{t+1}]=F[\alpha_{t+1},\alpha_{t+2}]=\cdots=F[\alpha_{t+i-1},\alpha_{t+i}]
, from which any 3rd order difference (hence the 4th, 5th, up to the
i
th) in this interval is zero.

2.

Otherwise,
|\alpha_{t+i}-\alpha_{t}|>\eta/n
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have that

|F[\alpha_{t},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i}]|=\left|\frac{F[\alpha_{t+1},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i}]-F[\alpha_{t},\cdots,\alpha_{t+i-1}]}{\alpha_{t+i}-\alpha_{t}}\right|\leq 2\cdot(2n/\eta)^{i-3}\cdot(n/\eta)\leq(2n/\eta)^{i-2}.

Also since
|\alpha_{j}|\leq 1
, the absolute value of the coefficient of
x^{t}
in

\prod_{j=1}^{i}(x-\alpha_{j})
is less than
2^{n}
.
So, finally we have
c_{t}\leq n2^{2n-2}(\frac{n}{\eta})^{n-2}
.
∎

Lemma B.14
.

For any polynomial
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, there is a polynomial
p_{\eta}(x)
such that the following properties hold:

1.

|p_{\eta}(\alpha_{i})-p(\alpha_{i})|\leq 2\eta
, for any
i\in[n]
.

2.

The height of
g_{\eta}
is at most
n2^{2n-2}(\frac{n}{\eta})^{n-2}
.

Proof.

For every polynomial
p(x)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
let
\boldsymbol{g}=\frac{p(\alpha_{i+1})-p(\alpha_{i})}{\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}}
for
i=[n-1]
.
So
|g_{i}|\leq 1
, that means the point
\boldsymbol{p}=(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n-1})
is in the
n-1
dimensional
complex hypercube (which has
4^{n-1}
vertices).
Then, we apply exactly the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
B.4
.

∎

Theorem B.15
.

For any two mixtures with
k
components
\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{C}},\Sigma^{\mathbb{C}}_{k-1})
, it holds that

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq O(k\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}).

Proof.

Fix any constant
\eta>0
. Let
\xi=\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{f\in 1\text{-}\mathsf{Lip}_{\mathbb{C}}}\int f\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})=2\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\int p\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(4\eta+\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\int p_{\eta}\mathrm{d}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle(\textrm{Lemma}~\ref{lm:ge-decomposition})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(4\eta+\sup_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\sum_{p=0}^{2k-1}n2^{2n-2}\left(\frac{n}{\eta}\right)^{n-2}\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle(\textrm{Lemma}~\ref{lm:ge-decomposition})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(4\eta+2^{4k}(2k)^{2k}\frac{\xi}{\eta^{2k-2}}\right).

\displaystyle(n\leq 2k)

By choosing
\eta=16k\xi^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}
,
we conclude that
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{2k-1}})
.
∎

B.3
Moment-Transportation Inequality in Higher Dimensions

We generalize our proof for one dimension to higher dimensions.
Lemma
E.1
shows there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{r}
such that
the distance between spikes are still lower bounded (up to factors depending on
k
and

to high dimension.

Theorem B.16
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}
be two
k
-spike mixtures in
\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{d-1},\Delta_{k-1})
, and

. Then, the following inequality holds

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq O(k^{3}d\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}).

Proof.

Apply the argument in Lemma
E.1
to
\mathrm{Supp}=\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\cup\mathrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})
. There always exists vector
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
and constant

such that
|r^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})|\geq\frac{c}{k^{2}d}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\int\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):\mu\in M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\right\}

\displaystyle(\textrm{Definition})

\displaystyle\leq\inf\left\{\int\frac{k^{2}d}{c}|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})|\mathrm{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):\mu\in M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\right\}

\displaystyle(\textrm{Lemma }\ref{lm:prob-proj})

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}d}{c}\tran(\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq O(k^{3}d\Mdis_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}}).

\displaystyle(\textrm{Lemma }\ref{lm:inequality1d})

∎

In this subsection, we show how to implement Algorithm

We first perform a ridge regression to obtain

in Line

The explicit solution of this ridge regression is

\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}=(A_{M^{\prime}}^{\top}A_{M^{\prime}}+\xi^{2}I)^{-1}A_{M^{\prime}}^{\top}b_{M^{\prime}}

Since

Xu and Qiao 2008

O(k^{2})

In Line

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{i}x^{i}+x^{k}

We can use the algorithm in
Neff and Reif 1996

O(k^{1+o(1)}\cdot\log\log(1/\xi))

Line

. We can use the recent algorithm developed in
Meyer et al. 2022

In Line

closest to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
.
To achieve

running time, we limit the spike distribution to have support
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{+})

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-}

C.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Now, we start to bound the reconstruction error of the algorithm.
The following lemma is well known (see e.g.,
Chihara 2011
;
Gordon et al. 2020
). We provide a proof for completeness.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{k-1})
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k}]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{c}=[c_{0},\cdots,c_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}x^{i}+x^{k}
.
Then,

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}M_{i+j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{j}+M_{i+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=0

for any
i\geq 0
.
The equation can also be written as

A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}=\mathbf{0}

in the matrix form.

Proof.

By direct calculation,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}M_{i+j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{j}+M_{i+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t}^{i+j}c_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t}^{i+k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t}^{i}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}\alpha_{t}^{j}+\alpha_{t}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t}^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{j})=0.

The
i
th row of
A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}
is
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}M_{i+j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{j}+M_{i+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
, hence
the matrix form.
∎

Next, the following lemma shows that the intermediate result
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
a good estimation for the solution of
A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{x}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}=\boldsymbol{0}
with a small norm:

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{c}=[c_{0},\cdots,c_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the sequence of number in which
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}x^{i}+x^{k}
.
Suppose
\|M^{\prime}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{\infty}\leq\xi
.
Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}=[\widehat{c}_{0},\cdots,\widehat{c}_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the intermediate result (Line
1
) in Algorithm
1
. Then,
\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}\leq 2^{k}
,
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}\leq 2^{O(k)}
and
\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi
.

Proof.

From Vieta’s formulas (see e.g.,
Barbeau 2003
), we have

c_{i}=\sum_{S\in\binom{[k]}{k-i}}\prod_{j\in S}(-\alpha_{j}).
Thus,

\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|c_{i}|\leq\sum_{S\in 2^{[k]}}\prod_{j\in S}|\alpha_{j}|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+|\alpha_{i}|)\leq 2^{k}.

where the last inequality holds since
|\alpha_{i}|\leq 1
for all

according to the definition of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
.

From Lemma
C.1
, we can see that
\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}=0

Therefore, we can further get

\displaystyle\|A_{M^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{M^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}-A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}\|_{\infty}+\|b_{M^{\prime}}-b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}+\|M^{\prime}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{\infty}(\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi,

The second inequality holds since

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{M^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{k}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}.

Finally, we can bound

\displaystyle\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{M^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{M^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{M^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}-A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{\infty}+\|b_{M^{\prime}}-b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=[\widehat{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\alpha}_{k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\left(\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}M_{p+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\widehat{c}_{q}+M_{p+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right)+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}M_{p+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+M_{2k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\Bigl(A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\Bigr)_{p}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}M_{p+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+M_{2k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}M_{p+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+M_{2k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\left(-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}M_{p+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}\right)+\left(-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}M_{k+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}c_{q}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}M_{p+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\widehat{c}_{p}+M_{k+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}c_{q}\Bigl(A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\Bigr)_{q},

where the first equality dues to Lemma
C.1
in which
M_{p+k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}M_{p+q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}
.
Combining the above results, we finally get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\alpha_{i}-\widehat{\alpha}_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\widehat{c}_{i}-c_{i})\Bigl(A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\Bigr)_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(\|\widehat{c}\|_{1}+\|c\|_{1})\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(2^{k}+2^{O(k)})\cdot 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi

\displaystyle(\textrm{Lemma}~\ref{lm:dim1-step1})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi.

∎

We provide the following simple inequality that allows us to upper bound the impact of the injected noise.

Lemma C.4
.

Let
b>0
be some constant.
For
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,a_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a_{i}\in[0,b]
and
|a_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}|\leq\xi
holds for all
i
. In case that
\xi\leq n^{-1}
, then,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\prime}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+O(b^{k}\cdot k\xi).

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma 1 in
(
Li and Deshpande 2010
)
.

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\prime}-\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k],S\neq\emptyset}\prod_{i\in S}a_{i}\prod_{i\not\in S}(a_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i})\

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sum_{|S|=t}\prod_{i\in S}a_{i}\prod_{i\not\in S}(a_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i})

\displaystyle\leq b^{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\binom{k}{t}\xi^{k}\leq b^{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\frac{(k\xi)^{k}}{t!}

\displaystyle\leq b^{k}(e^{k\xi}-1)=O(b^{k}\cdot k\xi).

The third inequality holds because
\binom{k}{t}\leq\frac{k^{t}}{t!}
. The last inequality holds since
e^{x}=\sum_{i>0}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}
and the fact that
e^{f(x)}=1+O(f(x))
if
f(x)=O(1)
.
∎

Definition C.6

(Schur polynomial)
.

Given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{k})
where
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}\geq 0
, the Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
is defined by the ratio

s_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\left|\begin{matrix}x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}+k-1}&x_{2}^{\lambda_{1}+k-1}&\cdots&x_{k}^{\lambda_{1}+k-1}\\
x_{1}^{\lambda_{2}+k-2}&x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}+k-2}&\cdots&x_{k}^{\lambda_{2}+k-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{\lambda_{k}}&x_{2}^{\lambda_{k}}&\cdots&x_{k}^{\lambda_{k}}\end{matrix}\right|\cdot\left|\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x_{1}^{1}&x_{2}^{1}&\cdots&x_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{1}^{k-1}&x_{2}^{k-1}&\cdots&x_{k}^{k-1}\end{matrix}\right|^{-1}.

A Schur polynomial is a symmetric function because the numerator and denominator are
both
alternating
(i.e., it changes sign if we swap two variables), and a polynomial since all alternating polynomials are divisible by the Vandermonde determinant

\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})

Lemma C.8
.

For all
j\geq k
and distinct numbers
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
(or
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
),

\left|\begin{matrix}a_{1}^{j}&a_{2}^{j}&\cdots&a_{k}^{j}\\
a_{1}^{1}&a_{2}^{1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{k-1}&a_{2}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{k-1}\end{matrix}\right|\cdot\left|\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
a_{1}^{1}&a_{2}^{1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{k-1}&a_{2}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{k-1}\end{matrix}\right|^{-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\cdot\sum_{s\in(k)^{j-k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{s_{i}}

where
(a)^{b}\subset\{0,\cdots,b\}^{a}
denotes the set that contains all vector
s
in which
\sum_{i=1}^{a}s_{i}=b
.

Proof.

We provide a self-contained proof here for completeness.
According to the property of Vandermonde determinant, we have

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
a_{1}^{1}&a_{2}^{1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{k-1}&a_{2}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{k-1}\end{matrix}\right|

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}\prod_{p<q}(a_{p}-a_{q}).

By expansion along first row, we have

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}a_{1}^{j}&a_{2}^{j}&\cdots&a_{k}^{j}\\
a_{1}^{1}&a_{2}^{1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{k-1}&a_{2}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{k-1}\end{matrix}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i+1}a_{i}^{j}\left|\begin{matrix}a_{1}^{1}&\cdots&a_{i-1}^{1}&a_{i+1}^{1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{i-1}^{k-1}&a_{i+1}^{k-1}&\cdots&a_{k}^{k-1}\\
\end{matrix}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i+1}a_{i}^{j}(-1)^{(k-1)(k-2)/2}\prod_{t\neq i}a_{t}\prod_{p<q,p\neq i,q\neq i}(a_{p}-a_{q})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}\cdot(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{j}(-1)^{i+1}\prod_{t\neq i}a_{t}\prod_{p<q,p\neq i,q\neq i}(a_{p}-a_{q})

Thus,

\displaystyle\text{LHS}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{j}\cdot\prod_{t\neq i}\frac{a_{t}}{a_{i}-a_{t}}

Let
L(z)=\sum_{j\geq k}\text{LHS}(j)z^{j}
and
R(z)=\sum_{j\geq k}\text{RHS}(j)z^{j}
be the generating functions of LHS and RHS corresponding to
j
respectively. Then,

\displaystyle L(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{j\geq k}a_{i}^{j}z^{j}\right)\prod_{t\neq i}\frac{a_{t}}{a_{t}-a_{i}}=z^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{a_{i}^{k}}{1-a_{i}z}\prod_{t\neq i}\frac{a_{t}}{a_{i}-a_{t}}

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{i}^{j}z^{j}\right)=z^{k}\prod_{t=1}^{k}\frac{a_{t}}{1-a_{t}z}

Consider the quotient between two functions,

\displaystyle\frac{L(z)}{R(z)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{k-1}\prod_{t\neq i}\frac{1-a_{t}z}{a_{i}-a_{t}}.

For
z=a_{p}^{-1}
where
p\in[1,k]
, the product in each additive term with
i\neq p
equals
0
. As a result, we can see that

\displaystyle\frac{L(a_{p}^{-1})}{R(a_{p}^{-1})}=a_{p}^{k-1}\prod_{t\neq p}\frac{1-a_{t}a_{p}^{-1}}{a_{i}-a_{t}}=1.

Since
L(z)/R(z)
is polynomial of
z
of degree
k-1
, from the uniqueness of polynomial, we have

\frac{L(z)}{R(z)}=1\Rightarrow\text{LHS}=\text{RHS}

which directly proves the statement.

be a
k
-spike distribution over support
\mathcal{C}
.
Then, we have

\min_{\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\min_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathcal{C},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}).

Note that the minimization on the left is over support
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Proof.

Consider any
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=(\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widebar{\boldsymbol{w}})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathcal{C},\Delta_{k-1})
such that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=[\overline{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\overline{\alpha}_{k}]^{\top}
and
\widebar{\boldsymbol{w}}=[\widebar{w}_{1},\cdots,\widebar{w}_{k}]^{\top}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\Sigma_{k-1})}\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
. Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\Sigma_{k-1})
, we have
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
. From triangle inequality, we have

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})+\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})\leq 2\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}).

Now, we start to show
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\Delta_{k-1})
, i.e., the weight of spikes in the closest distribution to
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
with support
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
should be all non-negative.

Towards a contradiction, assume the optimal distribution
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}
contains at least one negative spike, that is, let
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{-}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\max\{-\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{0}\})
be the negative spikes of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}
, we have
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{-}\neq\mathbf{0}
. In this case, since
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
is a probability distribution,
\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})=\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}+(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{-},(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{+})
where
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{+}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\max\{\boldsymbol{w},\mathbf{0}\})
is positive spikes of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}
.

Let
\mu^{\prime}
be the optimal matching distribution corresponding to
\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}+(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{-},(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{+})
. From its definition,
\mu^{\prime}
is non-negative. Let
\mu^{\prime\prime}
be the matching distribution in
\mu^{\prime}
that maps
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
and
(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})^{+}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime\prime}
be the marginal distribution of
\mu^{\prime\prime}
other than
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
. In this case,
\mu^{\prime\prime}
is a valid matching distribution for
\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime\prime})
. Moreover,
\mu^{\prime\prime}
gives less transportation distance than
\mu^{\prime}
does since the cost on the eliminated terms is non-negative. Hence,
\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime\prime})<\tran(\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})
, which is a contradiction.

As a result,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\Delta_{k-1})
. Therefore,
\min_{\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\prime})
. By taking minimization over
\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\min_{\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\min_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathcal{C},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}).

∎

Finally, we are ready to bound the transportation distance error of the reconstructed
k
-spike distribution:

Lemma C.13
.

Let
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{w}})
be the final result
(Line
9
) in Algorithm
1
.
Then, we have

\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Proof.

Combining Lemma
B.1
and Lemma
C.11
, we can see

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\cdot(2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Moreover, from Lemma
C.12
, we have

\displaystyle\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\min_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Finally, by triangle inequality,
\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})+\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

∎

By choosing
\xi=(\epsilon/k)^{O(k)}
, the previous lemma directly implies Theorem
4.1
.

Appendix D
An Algorithm for the Two-Dimensional Problem

We can generalize the 1-dimension algorithm described in Section
4
to 2-dimension.
Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}:=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})

be the underlying mixture
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\}
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\alpha_{i,2})
and
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{k-1}
.
The true moments can be computed according to
M_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\alpha_{t,1}^{i}\alpha_{t,2}^{j}
.
The input is the noisy moments
M^{\prime}_{i,j}
in
0\leq i,j,i+j\leq 2k-1
such that
|M^{\prime}_{i,j}-M_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})|\leq\xi
. We further assume that
M^{\prime}_{0,0}=M_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=1
and
\xi\leq 2^{-\Omega(k)}
.

The key idea is simple:
a distribution supported in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be mapped to a distribution supported in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, we define the complex set
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}=\{a+b{\mathfrak{i}}\mid(a,b)\in\Delta_{2}\}
.
Moreover, we denote
\boldsymbol{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{k}]^{\top}:=[\alpha_{1,1}+\alpha_{1,2}{\mathfrak{i}},\cdots,\alpha_{k,1}+\alpha_{k,2}{\mathfrak{i}}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
, and define
\boldsymbol{\phi}:=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k},\Delta_{k-1})
to be the complex mixture corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
.
The corresponding moments of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
can thus be defined as
G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{j}
.

For
G=[G_{i,j}]^{\top}_{0\leq i\leq k;0\leq j\leq k-1}
, denote

\displaystyle A_{G}:=\begin{bmatrix}G_{0,0}&G_{0,1}&\cdots&G_{0,k-1}\\
G_{1,0}&G_{1,1}&\cdots&G_{1,k-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
G_{k-1,0}&G_{1,k-1}&\cdots&G_{k-1,k-1}\end{bmatrix},b_{G}:=\begin{bmatrix}G_{0,k}\\
G_{1,k}\\
\vdots\\
G_{k-1,k}\end{bmatrix},M_{G}:=\begin{bmatrix}G_{0,0}\\
G_{0,1}\\
\vdots\\
G_{0,k-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Algorithm 2

Reconstruction Algorithm in Two-Dimensional Problem

0:

number of spikes
k
, noisy moments
M^{\prime}(\cdot)
, noise level
\xi

0:

recovered spike distribution
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}

1:

G^{\prime}\leftarrow[G^{\prime}_{i,j}:=\sum_{p=0}^{i}\sum_{q=0}^{j}\binom{i}{p}\binom{j}{q}(-{\mathfrak{i}})^{i-p}{\mathfrak{i}}^{j-q}M^{\prime}_{p+q,i+j-p-q}]^{\top}_{0\leq i\leq k;0\leq j\leq k-1}

2:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\|A_{G^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{x}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}+\xi^{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\Rightarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}=[\widehat{c}_{0},\cdots,\widehat{c}_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

3:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\leftarrow\textrm{roots}(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{i}x^{i}+x^{k})

\Rightarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[\widehat{\beta}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\beta}_{k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

4:

\widebar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\leftarrow\textrm{project}_{\Delta_{1}^{\mathbb{C}}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\Rightarrow\widebar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[\widebar{\beta}_{1},\cdots,\widebar{\beta}_{k}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k}

5:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\leftarrow\widebar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\textrm{Noise}(\xi)

\Rightarrow\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[\widetilde{\beta}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{\beta}_{k}]^{\top}\in\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}^{k}

6:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}}\|V_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\boldsymbol{x}-M_{G^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}

\Rightarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}=[\widehat{w}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{w}_{k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

7:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\leftarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}/(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{w}_{i})

\Rightarrow\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=[\widetilde{w}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{w}_{k}]^{\top}\in\Sigma_{k-1}^{\mathbb{C}}

8:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\leftarrow(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}})

\Rightarrow\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}},\Sigma_{k-1}^{\mathbb{C}})

9:

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\leftarrow([\textrm{real}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),\textrm{imag}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})],\textrm{real}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}))

\Rightarrow\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Sigma_{k-1})

10:

\widecheck{\boldsymbol{w}}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{k-1}}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{x}))

11:

\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\leftarrow(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{w}})

\Rightarrow\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})

The pseudocode can be found in Algorithm
2
. The algorithm takes the number of spikes
k
and the error bound
\xi
to reconstruct the original spike distribution using empirical moments
M^{\prime}
.
Now, we describe the implementation details of our algorithm.

We first calculate the empirical complete moments
G^{\prime}
of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
in Line
1
. Since the process follows relationship between
G(\boldsymbol{\phi})
and
M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
(see Lemma
D.1
),
G^{\prime}
would be an estimation of ground truth
G(\boldsymbol{\phi})
. Since it is a two-dimensional convolution, this step can be implemented in
O(k^{3})
arithmetic operations.

Then, we perform a ridge regression to obtain
\boldsymbol{c}
in Line
2
. We note that
A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}=\mathbf{0}
. (see Lemma
D.2
). Hence,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
can be seen as an approximation of
\boldsymbol{c}
. The explicit solution of this ridge regression is
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}=(A^{\top}_{G^{\prime}}A_{G^{\prime}}+\xi^{2}I)^{-1}A^{\top}_{G^{\prime}}b_{G^{\prime}}
which can be computed in
O(k^{3})
time.

From Line
3
to Line
5
, we aim to estimate the positions of the spikes of complex correspondence, i.e.,
\beta_{i}
s. Similar to the 1-dimensional case, we solve the roots of polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{i}x^{i}+x^{k}
. Note that the roots we found may locate outside
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}
, which is the support of the ground truth. Thus, we use the description of
\Delta_{2}
to project the solutions back to
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}
and inject small noise to ensure that all values are distinct and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are still in
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}
. Any noise of size at most
\xi
suffices here. We note that from the definition of complex correspondence, the realized spikes in the original space are
[\textrm{real}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),\textrm{imag}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]
. For implementation, this step can be done using the numerical root-finding algorithm allowing
\xi
additive noise in
O(k^{1+o(1)}\cdot\log\log(1/\xi))
arithmetic operations.

After that, we aim to recover the weight of the spikes. Line
6
is a linear regression defined by Vandemonde matrix
V_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}
may be complex numbers, we would calculate over complex space. Again, we would apply moment inequality to bound the recovered parameter error. Hence, we normalize
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}
and get
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
in Line
9
. Note from our definition of transportation distance, the real components and imaginary components are considered separately. Hence, we can discard the imaginary parts of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
and reconstruct the
k
-spike distribution in Line
7
. Using linear regression, this step can be done in
O(k^{3})
time.

The remaining thing is to deal with the negative weights of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
. We find a close
k
-spike distribution in
\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})
in Line
7
. The optimization problem is equivalent to finding a transportation from
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-})
to
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{+})
where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{-}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{+}
are the negative components and positive components of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
respectively, i.e.,
\widetilde{w}^{-}_{i}=\max\{0,-\widetilde{w}_{i}\}
and
\widetilde{w}^{+}_{i}=\max\{0,\widetilde{w}_{i}\}
. This transportation can be found using the standard network flow technique, which takes
O(k^{3})
time.

Since the noise satisfies
\xi\leq 2^{-\Omega(k)}
, the whole algorithm requires
O(k^{3})
arithmetic operations.

D.1
Error Analysis

From now, we bound the reconstruction error of the algorithm. The following lemma presents the relationship between the moments of the original
k
-spike distribution and its complex correspondence.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})
and let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}},\Delta_{k-1})
be its complex correspondence. Then, the complete moment satisfies

\displaystyle G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{i}\sum_{q=0}^{j}\binom{i}{p}\binom{j}{q}(-{\mathfrak{i}})^{i-p}{\mathfrak{i}}^{j-q}M_{p+q,i+j-p-q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}).

Proof.

According to the definition,

\displaystyle G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{j}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\alpha_{t,1}-\alpha_{t,2}{\mathfrak{i}})^{i}(\alpha_{t,1}+\alpha_{t,2}{\mathfrak{i}})^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}\sum_{p=0}^{i}\binom{i}{p}\alpha_{t,1}^{p}\alpha_{t,2}^{i-p}(-{\mathfrak{i}})^{i-p}\sum_{q=0}^{j}\binom{j}{q}\alpha_{t,1}^{q}\alpha_{t,2}^{j-q}{\mathfrak{i}}^{j-q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{i}\sum_{q=0}^{j}\binom{i}{p}\binom{j}{q}(-{\mathfrak{i}})^{i-p}{\mathfrak{i}}^{j-q}M_{p+q,i+j-p-q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}).

∎

The following lemma is a complex extension of Lemma
C.1
.

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}},\Delta_{k-1})
where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{k}]^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}]^{\top}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{c}=[c_{0},\cdots,c_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
such that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}c_{i}x^{i}+x^{k}
.
Then,

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{j}+G_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}G_{j,i}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{j}^{\dagger}+G_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=0,

for all
i\geq 0
.
In matrix form, the equation can be written as:

A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}=\mathbf{0}.

Proof.

According to the definition of
G
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})c_{j}+G_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{j}c_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}\beta_{t}^{j}+\beta_{t}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\beta_{t}-\beta_{j})=0,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}G_{j,i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})c_{j}^{\dagger}+G_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{j}\beta_{t}^{i}c_{j}^{\dagger}+\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{k}\beta_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{j}c_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{k}w_{t}(\beta_{t}^{\dagger})^{i}\beta_{t}^{k}\right)^{\dagger}=0.

∎

Similar to Lemma
C.2
, we present some useful properties of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
.

Lemma D.3
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}=[\widehat{c}_{0},\cdots,\widehat{c}_{k-1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the intermediate result (Line
2
) in the algorithm. Then,
\|G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\xi
,
\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}\leq 2^{k}
,
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}\leq 2^{O(k)}
and
\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi
.

Proof.

From Vieta’s formulas, we have

c_{i}=\sum_{S\in\binom{[k]}{k-i}}\prod_{j\in S}(-\alpha_{j}).

Thus,

\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|c_{i}|\leq\sum_{S\in 2^{[k]}}\prod_{j\in S}|\beta_{j}|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+|\beta_{i}|)\leq 2^{k}.

where the last inequality holds because
|\beta_{i}|=\sqrt{\alpha_{i,1}^{2}+\alpha_{i,2}^{2}}\leq 1
for all
i
.

According to Lemma
D.1
,

\displaystyle|G^{\prime}_{i,j}-G_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\phi})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=0}^{i}\sum_{q=0}^{j}\left|\binom{i}{p}\binom{j}{q}(-{\mathfrak{i}})^{i-p}{\mathfrak{i}}^{j-q}\right|\|M^{\prime}-M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{i+j}\cdot\xi.

This shows that
\|G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\xi
.

From Lemma
C.1
, we can see that
\|A_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{M(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}=0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|A_{G^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{G^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}\|_{\infty}+\|b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}+\|(A_{G^{\prime}}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})})\boldsymbol{c}\|_{\infty}+\|b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{G^{\prime}}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}+\|b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}+\|G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{\infty}(\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi.

The fourth inequality holds since
(A_{G^{\prime}}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})})_{i,j}=(G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi}))_{i,j}
and
(b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})})_{i}=(G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi}))_{i,k}
.

From the definition of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}
, we can see that

\displaystyle\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}+\xi^{2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}+\xi^{2}\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq k\|A_{G^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{c}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\xi^{2}\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi^{2}.

The second inequality holds since
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}
and
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{k}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\infty}
holds for any vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Now, we get that

\displaystyle\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{k}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}.

Finally, we can bound
\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}
according to

\displaystyle\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{\infty}+\|b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{G^{\prime}}-A_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}+\|b_{G^{\prime}}-b_{G(\boldsymbol{\phi})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{G^{\prime}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}+b_{G^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|G^{\prime}-G(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{\infty}(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi,

which finishes the proof.
∎

We then show
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}
obtained in Line
7
is close to
the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Lemma D.4
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[\widehat{\beta}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{\beta}_{k}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
be the intermediate result (Line
3
) in Algorithm
2
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\beta_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{j}|^{2}\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi.

Proof.

Consider

\displaystyle\quad\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\beta_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{j}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\left|\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\beta_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{j})\right|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{j}\beta_{i}^{t}+\beta_{i}^{k}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{j}\beta_{i}^{t}+\beta_{i}^{k}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{j}\beta_{i}^{t}+\beta_{i}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\widehat{c}_{q}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{p}\beta_{i}^{q}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{p}\beta_{i}^{k}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\beta_{i}^{p}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}+(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}\beta_{i}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\widehat{c}_{q}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{p}\beta_{i}^{q}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{p}\beta_{i}^{k}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\beta_{i}^{p}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}(\beta_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}\beta_{i}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}G_{p,q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\widehat{c}_{q}+G_{p,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right)+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}G_{k,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+G_{k,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}^{\dagger}(A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})})_{p}+\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}G_{k,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+G_{k,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),

where the last equality holds from the definition of matrix
A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}
and vector
b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}
.
According to Lemma
D.2
,
G_{k,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}G_{q,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}^{\dagger}
, so

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}G_{k,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+G_{k,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\widehat{c}_{p}\left(-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}G_{q,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}^{\dagger}\right)+\left(-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}G_{q,k}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})c_{q}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}c_{q}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}G_{q,p}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\widehat{c}_{p}+G_{k,q}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}c_{q}^{\dagger}(A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})})_{q}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\beta_{i}-\widehat{\beta}_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\widehat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}-c_{i}^{\dagger})(A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})})_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(\|\widehat{c}\|_{1}+\|c\|_{1})\|A_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\widehat{c}+b_{G(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(2^{k}+2^{O(k)})\cdot 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi

\displaystyle(\text{Lemma~\ref{lm:dim2-step1}})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi.

∎

Lemma D.6

\displaystyle(\text{Lemma~\ref{lm:dim2-step1}})

Lemma D.8
.

Let
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{w}})
be the final result
(Line

) in Algorithm

Then, we have

\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Proof.

Apply Lemma
D.7
to the result of Lemma
B.15
. We have

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq O(k\cdot(2^{O(k)}\cdot\xi^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2k-1}})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Denote
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\textrm{real}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}))
as the result that discards all imaginary components from
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

\boldsymbol{\phi}
has no imaginary components. Thus, discarding all imaginary components from
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

\boldsymbol{\phi}

\displaystyle\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}},\boldsymbol{\phi}).

\displaystyle\quad\|[\textrm{real}(\beta_{i}),\textrm{imag}(\beta_{i})]-[\textrm{real}(\beta_{j}),\textrm{imag}(\beta_{j})]\|_{1}

\displaystyle=2|\beta_{i}-\beta_{j}|.

This shows the distance over
\mathbb{C}
is smaller than
2
times of the distance over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. As a result,

\displaystyle\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq 2\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

\displaystyle\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\min_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{2},\Delta_{k-1})}\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq 2\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Finally, from triangle inequality,
\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})+\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

∎

\|M^{\prime}(R)-M(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\|_{\infty}\leq\xi

M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0}}(R)=M_{\mathbf{0}}(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))=1

1
).

Let
e_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unit vector that takes value
1
in only the
t

0
in the remaining coordinates. Denote
\mathbf{1_{d}}=(1,\cdots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3
. The algorithm’s input consists of the number of spikes
k
, the moment error bound
\xi
, and the noisy moment oracle
M^{\prime}(\cdot)
mentioned above. Now, we describe the implementation details of our algorithm.

We first generate a random vector
\boldsymbol{r}
in Line
2
by sampling from unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}=\{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{2}=1\}
. Note that by a probabilistic argument, the distance between spikes is roughly kept after the projection along
\boldsymbol{r}
(see Lemma
E.1
). Then, we aim to use the one-dimension algorithm to recover
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
.
However, the support of
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
is contained in
[-1,1]
but not
[0,1]
.
In Line
3
, we apply the 1-dimension algorithm (Algorithm
1
)
to the noisy moments of a shifted and scaled map
\Pi_{(\boldsymbol{r}+\mathbf{1}_{d})/4}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
whose support is in
[0,1]
. Then we scale and shift back to obtain the result
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
in Line
4
. Intuitively,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is close to
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
.

Now, we try to recover the coordinates of the spikes.
In particular, we would like to find, for each spike of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, the coordinates of the corresponding location in
\Delta_{d-1}
. This is done by considering each dimension separately. For each coordinate
t\in[d]
, we run the two-dimension algorithm (i.e., Algorithm
2
) to recovery the projection

\Pi_{[\boldsymbol{r},e_{t}]}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
, that is a linear map of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
onto the subspace spanned by vector
\boldsymbol{r}
and axis

.
However,
[\boldsymbol{r},e_{t}]\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
is in

. Again, in Line

.

.

Next, we assemble the 1d projection
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and 2-d projections
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t},t\in[d]
.
Due to the noise,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
may have different supports along
\boldsymbol{r}
.
So we assign the coordinates of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
according to the closest spike of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
with relatively large weights in Line
7
to Line
12
.
Due to the error, the support of the reconstructed distribution
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
may not locate in
\Delta_{d-1}
.
Thus the final step in Line

\Delta_{d-1}

We note the bottleneck of this algorithm is Line

d

.

E.1
Error Analysis

First, we show the random vector
\boldsymbol{r}
has a critical property that two distant spikes are still distant after the projection with high probability.
This result is standard and similar statements are known in the literature (e.g., Lemma 37 in
Wu and Yang 2020
).

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\mathrm{Supp}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k})\subset\Delta_{d-1}
such that
d>3
.
For every constant
0<\eta<1
and a random vector
\boldsymbol{r}\leftarrow\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
, with probability at least
1-\eta
, for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathrm{Supp}
, we have that

|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})|\geq\frac{\eta}{k^{2}d}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1}.

Proof.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
be some constant depending on
k
and
d
which we fix later. Assume
\boldsymbol{r}=[r_{1},\cdots,r_{d}]^{\top}
. For a fixed pair
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
, we have

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}[|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})|<\epsilon\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{2}]=\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}[|r_{1}|<\epsilon].

Suppose
\boldsymbol{r}
is obtained by first sampling a vector from
unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
and then
normalizing it to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
.
We can see that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}[|r_{1}|<\epsilon]\leq\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{B}_{d}}[|r_{1}|<\epsilon]

where the RHS is equal to the probability that
\boldsymbol{r}
lies in
the slice
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
.

Let
V_{d}
be the volume of
d
-dimensional ball.
We have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{B}_{d}}[|r_{1}|<\epsilon]

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}V_{d-1}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{d}x}{V_{d}}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{d}

where the last inequality holds since
V_{d}/V_{d-1}\geq 2/\sqrt{d}
.

By union bound over all pairs
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})
, we can see that
the failure probability can be bounded by

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}[\exists\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathrm{Supp}:|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}(A_{i}-A_{j})|<\epsilon\|A_{i}-A_{j}\|_{2}]<k^{2}\epsilon\sqrt{d}.

Notice that
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}
for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
let
\epsilon=\frac{\eta}{k^{2}\sqrt{d}}
. We conclude that

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathbb{S}_{d-1}}[\forall\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathrm{Supp}:|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}(A_{i}-A_{j})|\geq\frac{\eta}{k^{2}d}\|A_{i}-A_{j}\|_{1}]>1-\eta.

∎

Next, we show that we can recover and reconstruct the higher dimension mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, using its 1d projection
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\sim\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
, by assigning each spike
(\widetilde{y}_{i},\widetilde{w}_{i})
in the one dimension distribution a location in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In a high level,
consider a clustering of the spikes in
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
. The spikes in one cluster still form a cluster in the original
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
by Lemma
E.1

since the projection approximately keeps the distance.
Thus assigning the clusters a location in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can produce a good estimation for
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
.
The following lemma formally proves that we can reconstruct the original mixture using two-dimensional projections.

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Delta_{k-1})
be the intermediate result (Line
15
) in Algorithm
3
.
Let
\epsilon
be the smallest number such that
\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}},\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq\epsilon
and
\tran(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t},\Pi_{[\boldsymbol{r},e_{t}]}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq\epsilon
for all
t
.
If Lemma
E.1
holds for
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
,
we have that

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\cdots,C_{m}
be a partition of
[k]
for
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that:

•

|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}|\leq k\sqrt{\epsilon}
if
i\in C_{l},j\in C_{l}
for some
l\in[m]
.

•

|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}|>\sqrt{\epsilon}
if
i\in C_{l},j\in C_{l^{\prime}}
for
l\neq l^{\prime}

For every cluster

, we construct another set
\widetilde{C}_{p}
for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
containing every index
j\in[k]
such that
\min_{i\in C_{p}}|\widetilde{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}|<\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}
. From the construction of the partition, for each spike
\widetilde{y}_{j}
, there exists at most one
C_{p^{\prime}}
such that
\min_{i\in C_{p^{\prime}}}|\widetilde{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}|<\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}
. Thus, we have all
\widetilde{C}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{C}_{m}
disjoint.

In this case, for every pair of
i\notin C_{p}
and
j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}
, we have
|\widetilde{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}|>\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{2}
. Since the total transportation distance between
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
is no more than
\epsilon
. Hence, a portion of at most
\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\epsilon}/2}
of the weight in
C_{p}
can be matched out of
\widetilde{C}_{p}
. This gives

\big|\sum_{i\in C_{p}}w_{i}-\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\widetilde{w}_{j}\big|<2\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Recall the transportation distance
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
is the solution of the following program over coupling distribution
\mu
.

min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}

s.t.

\displaystyle w_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j},\widetilde{w}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j},\mu\geq\mathbf{0}.

Consider a specific distribution
\mu
generated by the following two-stage procedure:
Initially, set
\mu_{i,j}=0
for every pair of
(i,j)

In the first stage, increase
\mu_{i,j}
for every
i\in C_{p}
and
j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}
whenever
w_{i}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j},\widetilde{w}_{j}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}
.
In the second stage, increase
\mu_{i,j}
arbitrarily for every pair of
(i,j)
to satisfy every constraint.
Since the restriction set is convex,
C_{p}
are disjoint, and
\widetilde{C}_{p}
are disjoint, for some specific
p
, this two-stage process ensures

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}=\min\{\sum_{i\in C_{p}}w_{i},\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\widetilde{w}_{j}\}.

As a result, the coupling distribution satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\notin\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}\leq|\sum_{i\in C_{p}}w_{i}-\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\widetilde{w}_{j}|\leq 2\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Now we start to show
\mu
is a good coupling distribution for
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})
.
Since
\{C_{p}\}
is a partition of
[k]
, we can decompose the transportation distance into:

\displaystyle\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}=\sum_{p}\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}.

Next, we bound the RHS by considering each cluster
C_{p}
.
If it holds
\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\leq 2k\sqrt{\epsilon}
, from
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq 1
, we can directly get

\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq 2k\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Otherwise, we have
\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}>2k\sqrt{\epsilon}
. We can decompose the last summation into:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\notin\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}+\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}.

For the first term, we have
\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\notin\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}\leq 2\sqrt{\epsilon}
as we had discussed above. Together with
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq 1
, we can bound the first term by
2\sqrt{\epsilon}
. For the second term, we have
\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}}\mu_{i,j}\leq 1
according to the property of coupling distribution and the following lemma shows
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}
is small. We delay the proof for readability.

Lemma E.3
.

If
\sum_{i\in C_{p}}w_{i}>2k\sqrt{\epsilon}
,
then
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon})
for all
i\in C_{p}
and
j\in\widetilde{C}_{p}
.

As a result, we can see that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in C_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{i,j}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Hence, we can conclude that

\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

∎

.

For some fixed cluster
C_{p}
with

and

and

\alpha_{i_{1},t}
is the dimension

of

Next, we take some
i_{2}\in C_{p}
for
j

In addition, since the weight of spike

\displaystyle\quad\ |\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i_{3}}|+|\alpha_{i_{3},t}-\widehat{\alpha}_{s^{*}_{j,t},t}|

\displaystyle\leq|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i_{2}}|+|\widetilde{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i_{2}}|+|\widetilde{y}_{j}-\widehat{y}_{s^{*}_{j,t},t}|+|\boldsymbol{r}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i_{3}}-\widehat{y}_{s^{*}_{j,t},t}|+|\alpha_{i_{3},t}-\widehat{\alpha}_{s^{*}_{j,t},t}|

for

s, we have that

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k^{2}d}{\eta}\cdot(3+2k)\sqrt{\epsilon}\leq O(k^{3}d\sqrt{\epsilon})

where the equality holds due to the assignment of

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{d}|\widetilde{\alpha}_{j,t}-\alpha_{i,t}|\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

∎

.

Theorem E.4
.

Let
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}})
be the final result
(Line
17
) in Algorithm
3
.
Then with probability at least
1-\eta
for any fixed constant
0<\eta<1
,

\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq(kd\xi^{\frac{1}{k}})^{O(1)}.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}
is generated by one dimension algorithm, according to Lemma
C.13
, we have

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime},\Pi_{\frac{\boldsymbol{r}+\mathbf{1}}{4}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Note that

\Pi_{\frac{\boldsymbol{r}+\mathbf{1}}{4}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
can be transformed to
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
by transforming the coordinates by
\alpha\rightarrow 4\alpha-1
. We have

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\Pi_{\boldsymbol{r}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq 4\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime},\Pi_{\frac{\boldsymbol{r}+\mathbf{1}}{4}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Similarly, according to Lemma
D.8
, we can see

\tran(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t},\Pi_{[\boldsymbol{r},e_{t}]}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}}).

Thus, from the definition of
\epsilon
, we have
\epsilon\leq O(k\xi^{\frac{1}{4k-2}})
. With Lemma
E.2
, we have

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq O(k^{3}d^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon})\leq(kd\xi^{\frac{1}{k}})^{O(1)}.

Note that
\Delta_{d-1}
is a convex set and
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}
is the projection of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}
to the same set. Thus,
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}\|_{1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{j}\|_{1}
holds for all
A_{i}\in\Delta_{d-1}
and
j
. Hence, the projection to

does not increase the transportation distance. As a result,

\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}).

Finally, by triangle inequality, we have

\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\leq\tran(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})+\tran(\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}})\leq(kd\xi^{\frac{1}{k}})^{O(1)}.

∎

By choosing

Appendix F
Applications to Topic Models

In this section, we discuss the application of our results to topic models.
Here, the underlying vocabulary consists of
d
words.
We are given a corpus of documents. We adopt the popular
“bag of words” model and take each document as an unordered multiset of words.
The assumption is that there is a small number
k

of “pure” topics, where each topic is a distribution over
[d]
.
A
K
-word document (i.e., a string in
[d]^{K}
) is generated by first selecting a
topic
i\in\Delta_{d-1}
from the mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, and then sampling

K
words i.i.d. according to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
from this topic.
Here,
K
is referred to as
the snapshot number
and we call such a sample a
K
-snapshot
of
p
.
Our goal here is to recover
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{Spike}(\Delta_{d-1},\Delta_{k-1})
,
which is a discrete distribution over
k
pure topics.
Again, we measure the accuracy in terms of
L_{1}
-transportation distance.

F.1
An Algorithm for
d=O(k)

In this section, we first directly handle the high-dimensional case using Algorithm
3
.
We will perform dimension reduction when
d\gg k
, in Section
F.2
.

Lemma F.1
.

There is an algorithm that can learn an arbitrary
k
-spike mixture
of discrete distributions supported in
\Delta_{d-1}

for any
d
within
L_{1}
transportation distance
\epsilon
with probability at least
0.99
using
(kd/\epsilon)^{O(k)}
many
(2k-1)
-snapshots.

The following lemma is a high-dimensional version for empirical error (see e.g.
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Gordon et al. 2020
).

Lemma F.2
.

For matrix
R=[\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{r}_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
with
\|R\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,
using
2^{O(kp)}\cdot\xi^{-2}\cdot\log(1/\eta)
samples, we can obtain transformed empirical moments
M^{\prime}(R)
satisfying

\max_{|\mathbf{t}|\leq K}|M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{t}}(R)-M_{\mathbf{t}}(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))|\leq\xi

with probability at least
1-\eta
.

Proof.

Assume the words of a document are
x_{1},\cdots,x_{K}
.
If the document is from some topic
i
,
we have
\mathbb{E}[x_{j}=c]=\alpha_{p,t}
for all
i,j,c
.
For each word
x_{j}
, we generate a i.i.d. random vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=[\beta_{1,j},\cdots,\beta_{p,j}]^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\beta_{c,j}]=r_{c,x_{j}}
. For
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{p})
with
|\mathbf{t}|\leq K
, we define observable
h_{\mathbf{t}}=\mathbf{1}[\forall c:\sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_{c,j}=t_{c}]
.

Note that
h_{\mathbf{t}}
is the normalized histogram for projected distribution
\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
. Hence, from the relation between normalized histogram and standard moments, we can conclude that

\displaystyle M(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))=\left(\bigotimes_{t=1}^{p}\textrm{Pas}\right)h

where
\textrm{Pas}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+1)\times(K+1)}
such that
\textrm{Pas}_{i,j}=[j\geq i]\binom{j}{i}\binom{K+1}{i}
and
\otimes
is the tensor product.
Since
\|\textrm{Pas}\|\leq 2^{O(k)}
, we have
\left(\bigotimes_{t=1}^{p}\textrm{Pas}\right)\leq 2^{O{(kp)}}
.

Let
\widehat{h}_{\mathbf{t}}
be the empirical average of
h_{\mathbf{t}}
.
Since
h_{\mathbf{t}}
is a Bernoulli variable, from Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

\Pr[|\widehat{h}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbb{E}[h_{\mathbf{t}}]|<\epsilon]\leq 2\exp(-2\epsilon^{2}s)
.
By applying union bound on
\mathbf{t}
, we can see that using
s>2^{O(kp)}\cdot\xi^{-2}\cdot\log(1/\eta)
samples, we have
|\widehat{h}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbb{E}[h_{\mathbf{t}}]|<\xi\cdot 2^{-\Omega(kp)}
with probability at least
1-\eta
.
Moreover, if we calculate
M^{\prime}
from
\widehat{h}
using the relationship between
M
and
h
, this leads to

\max_{|\mathbf{t}|\leq K}|M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{t}}(R)-M_{\mathbf{t}}(\Pi_{R}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}))|\leq\xi
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
F.1
.

Let
\xi=(\epsilon/kd)^{O(k)}
. From Theorem

, the recovered

k
-spike distribution
\widecheck{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
satisfies

F.2
Dimension Reduction When
d\gg k^{\Omega(1)}

In topic modeling, the number of words is typically much larger than the number of pure topics
(i.e.,
d\gg k
).
In this case, we face another challenge in recovering the mixture.
First, there are
\binom{d}{1}+\binom{d}{2}+\cdots+\binom{d}{k}=O(d^{k})
many different moments.
Obtaining all empirical moments accurately enough would require a huge number of
2k-1
-snapshot samples.
So if
d\gg k
, we reduce the dimension from
d
to
O(k)
.
Now, we prove the following theorem. It
improves on the result in
Rabani et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
, which uses
more than
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k^{2})}

(2k-1)
-snapshots,
and
Gordon et al. 2020
which use
(k/\epsilon)^{O(k)}\cdot(w_{\min}\zeta^{k})^{-O(1)}

2k
-snapshots and requires the minimum separation assumption.

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
be the target
k
-spike mixture in
\Delta_{d-1}
(
d\gg k
).
Suppose there is a learning algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfying the assumption in the theorem.
We show how to apply
{\mathcal{A}}
to the projection of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
to a subspace of dimension at most
k
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
However, an arbitrary subspace of dimension at most
k
is not enough, even the one spanning the
k
spikes of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
. This is because
L_{1}
distance is not rotationally invariant and not preserved under projection.
For example, the
L_{1}
distance between
(1/d,\ldots,1/d)
and
(2/d,\ldots,2/d,0,\ldots,0)
is 1 in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but only
\sqrt{1/d}
in the line spanned by the two points.
Hence, an accurate estimation of the projected measure
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{B}=\Pi_{B}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
may not translate to
an accurate estimation in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here, we can use the dimension reduction method developed by
Li et al. 2015
, which shows that
if suffices to project
the mixture to a special subspace
B
,
such that a unit
L_{1}
-ball in
B
(
L_{1}
measured in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is close to being an

\widetilde{O}(d^{-1/2})

L_{2}
-ball
(
\widetilde{O}
hides factors depending only on
k
and
\epsilon
, but not
d
).

Lemma F.3
.

(
Li et al. 2015
)
There is an algorithm that requires
\poly(d,k,\frac{1}{\epsilon})

many 1-,2- snapshots to construct a subspace
B
(
\dim(B)\leq k
)
with the following useful properties:

1.

Suppose

\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{m}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
B
where
m=\dim(B)\leq k

(such a basis can be produced by the above algorithm as well).
For any
i\in[m]
,
\|b_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq O(k^{3/2}\epsilon^{-2}d^{-1/2})
.

2.

Suppose we can learn an approximation
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{B}

\sspan(B)
, of
the projected measure
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{B}=\Pi_{\sspan(B)}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
such that
\tran(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{B},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{B})\leq\epsilon_{1}=\poly(1/k,\epsilon)
(here
L_{1}
is measured in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, not in the subspace)
using
N_{1}(d)
,
N_{2}(d)
and
N_{K}(d)
1-, 2-, and
K
-snapshot samples.

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
such that

-snapshot samples respectively.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

In light of Lemma
F.3
, we only need to show how to learn the projection
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{B}=\Pi_{\sspan(B)}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})

using the algorithm developed in Section

.

First, we show how to translate a
K
-snapshots from
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
to a
K
-snapshot
in some new
k
-dimensional mixture
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\text{new}}
(then we apply the algorithm in Section

for dimension
k
).
Let
L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|b_{i}\|_{\infty}

So
L\leq O(k^{5/2}\epsilon^{-2}d^{-1/2})
.
Let

.
For each word in the original
K
-snapshot, say

j

Then we apply our learning algorithm

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\text{new}}^{\prime}

with
\poly(k,1/\epsilon_{1})=\poly(k,1/\epsilon)
many

-snapshots.

Next we can see

(in
\mathbb{R}^{k}

\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{B}
(in
\Span(B)
)
are related by an affine transform.
For a spike

, one can see that
\Pi_{B}(\alpha_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i,j}b_{j}
,
where
a_{i,j}=\langle\alpha_{i},b_{j}\rangle

\alpha_{i}
produces a new spike
\beta_{i}
in
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\text{new}}
and the mapping is as follows:
for each

we have

\beta_{i,j}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\alpha_{i,t}f(b_{j,t})=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\alpha_{i,t}(\frac{b_{j,t}}{2mL}+\frac{1}{2m})=\frac{a_{i,j}}{2mL}+\frac{1}{2m}

(
\beta_{i,j}
denotes the
j

\beta_{i}

So

and
g(x)=2mLx-L
is the affine transformation.
Now we can translate
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\text{new}}^{\prime}
into a mixture in

by applying

in each coordinate of each

It remains to show

If we let

(where if

where the first inequality holds since

for any two point

G.1
Efficient Algorithm for

In the 1-dimensional case, we denote the known variance by
\sigma

which is upper bounded by some constant.
As shown by
Wu and Yang 2020
, the moments of Gaussian mixture have a close connection with the moments of corresponding discrete distributions

{\mathbb{E}}[H_{t}(x)]=\mu^{t}
for Hermite polynomial

\displaystyle H_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}h_{t,i}x^{i}=t!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor}\frac{(-1/2)^{j}}{j!(t-2j)!}x^{t-2j}.

(6)

For 1-dimensional Gaussian mixture

with variance

\displaystyle M_{t}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})={\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{N}}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{t}h_{t,i}\sigma^{t-i}x^{i}\right].

The following lemma guarantees the performance of estimating the moment by sampling.

Lemma G.1
.

(Lemma 5 of

Let

be
n

is an unbiased estimator for

, and the variance of this estimator can be bounded by

\operatorname{Var}[{\widetilde{M}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})}]\leq\frac{1}{n}(\sigma t)^{O(t)}.

Thus we can compute the moment of the original distribution and use our Algorithm
1
for recovering the parameter of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
. More concretely, we can replace the last two lines (an SDP) in Algorithm 2 of

1

The post-sampling time is improved from from

in

Theorem G.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{N}
be an arbitrary
k
-Gaussian mixture over
\mathbb{R}
with means
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
and known variance

There is an algorithm that can learn the parameter
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{w})
within transportation distance

Let

With

Remark G.3
.

(Connection between heat equations and Gaussian mixtures)

Consider the heat equation

\frac{\partial u(x,t)}{\partial t}=\Delta_{x}u(x,t),\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta_{x}u=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

Suppose at

k
-spike distribution

It is well known that for

Hence, if we know

Learning statistical mixture models has been studied extensively for the past two decades.
A central problem
in this area was the problem of learning a mixture of high-dimensional
Gaussians, even robustly

;

;

;

;

;

)
.
Many other structured mixture models have also been studied (see e.g.,

;
Cryan et al. 2001
;
Batu et al. 2004
;

;

.
Most work make

Our problem is also related to the super-resolution problem in which each measurement takes the form

where
\xi_{\ell}

.
There is a long history of the estimation problem. The noiseless version

(

)

)

measurements). The noisy case has

;
Candès and Fernandez-Granda 2012
;
Moitra 2015
;

;

)
. Various properties, such as the

(
Moitra 2015
)
. The relation between the Vandermonde matrix and Schur polynomial is also exploited in
Chen et al. 2016
.
