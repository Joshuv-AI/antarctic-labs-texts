Optimal temperature distribution for a nonisothermal Cahn–Hilliard system in two dimensions with source term and double obstacle potential


Abstract
In this note, we study the optimal control of a nonisothermal phase field system of Cahn–Hilliard type that constitutes an extension of the classical Caginalp model for nonisothermal phase transitions with a conserved order parameter. It couples a Cahn–Hilliard type equation with source term for the order parameter with the universal balance law of internal energy. In place of the standard Fourier form, the constitutive law of the heat flux is assumed in the form given by the theory developed by Green and Naghdi, which accounts for a possible thermal memory of the evolution. This has the consequence that the balance law of internal energy becomes a second-order in time equation for the thermal displacement or freezing index , that is, a primitive with respect to time of the temperature. Another particular feature of our system is the presence of the source term in the equation for the order parameter, which entails further mathematical difficulties because the mass conservation of the order parameter is no longer satisfied. In this paper, we study the case that the double-well potential driving the evolution of the phase transition is given by the nondifferentiable double obstacle potential, thereby complementing recent results obtained for the differentiable cases of regular and logarithmic potentials. Besides existence results, we derive first-order necessary optimality conditions for the control problem. The analysis is carried out by employing the so-called deep quench approximation in which the nondifferentiable double obstacle potential is approximated by a family of potentials of logarithmic structure for which meaningful first-order necessary optimality conditions in terms of suitable adjoint systems and variational inequalities are available. Since the results for the logarithmic potentials crucially depend on the validity of the so-called strict separation property which is only available in the spatially two-dimensional situation, our whole analysis is restricted to the two-dimensional case. Keywords: Optimal control, nonisothermal Cahn–Hilliard equation, thermal memory, Cahn–Hilliard equation with source term, Cahn–Hilliard–Oono equation. Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be some open, bounded, and connected set
having a smooth boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
and the outward unit normal field
\,{\bf n}
.
Denoting by
{\partial_{\bf n}}
the directional derivative in the direction of
{\bf n}
, and putting, with a fixed final time
T>0
,

Q_{t}:=\Omega\times(0,t)\,\mbox{ and }\,\Sigma_{t}:=\Gamma\times(0,t)\,\mbox{ for $\,t\in(0,T]$, \quad$Q:=Q_{T}$,\quad$\Sigma:=\Sigma_{T}$},

we study in this paper as
state system
the following initial-boundary value problem:

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}\varphi-\Delta\mu+\gamma\varphi=f\quad\mbox{in $Q$},

(1)

\displaystyle\mu=-\Delta\varphi+\xi+F^{\prime}(\varphi)+a-b{\partial_{t}}w,\quad\xi\in{\partial{I_{[-1,1]}}}(\varphi),\quad\mbox{in $Q$},

(2)

\displaystyle{\partial_{tt}}w-\Delta({\kappa_{1}}{\partial_{t}}w+{\kappa_{2}}w)+\lambda{\partial_{t}}\varphi=u\quad\mbox{in $Q$},

(3)

\displaystyle{\partial_{\bf n}}\varphi={\partial_{\bf n}}\mu={\partial_{\bf n}}({\kappa_{1}}{\partial_{t}}w+{\kappa_{2}}w)=0\quad\mbox{on $\Sigma$},

(4)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\quad w(0)=w_{0},\quad{\partial_{t}}w(0)=w_{1},\quad\mbox{in $\Omega.$}

(5)

The
cost functional
under consideration is given by

\displaystyle{\cal J}((\varphi,w),u):={}\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\int_{Q}|\varphi-\varphi_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi(T)-\varphi_{\Omega}|^{2}{}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\int_{Q}|w-w_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{4}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|w(T)-w_{\Omega}|^{2}{}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{\beta_{5}}{2}\int_{Q}|{\partial_{t}}w-w^{\prime}_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{6}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|{\partial_{t}}w(T)-w^{\prime}_{\Omega}|^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{Q}|u|^{2},

(6)

with nonnegative constants
\beta_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 6
, and
\nu
, which are not all zero, and where
\varphi_{\Omega},w_{\Omega},w^{\prime}_{\Omega}\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and

\varphi_{Q},w_{Q},w^{\prime}_{Q}\in L^{2}(Q)
denote given target functions.

For the distributed control variable
u
, we choose as control space

{\cal U}:=L^{\infty}(Q),

(7)

and the related set of admissible controls
is given by

{\cal U}_{\rm ad}:=\big\{u\in{\cal U}:u_{\rm min}\leq u\leq u_{\rm max}\,\mbox{ a.e. in }\,Q\big\},

(8)

where we generally assume throughout the paper that

u_{\rm min},u_{\rm max}\in{L^{\infty}(Q)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad u_{\rm min}\leq u_{\rm max}\,\mbox{ a.e. in }\,Q.

(9)

In particular,

{\cal U}_{\rm ad}
is bounded in
{L^{\infty}(Q)}
.

In summary, the control problem under investigation can be reformulated as follows:

(P)

\min_{u\in{\cal U}_{\rm ad}}{\cal J}((\varphi,w),u)
subject to the constraint that
(\varphi,\mu,\xi,w)

solves
the state      system (
1
)–(
5
).

Let us now spend some comments on the state system (
1
)–(
5
), which is a formal extension of the nonisothermal Cahn–Hilliard system introduced by Caginalp in
[
3
]

to model the phenomenon of nonisothermal phase segregation in binary mixtures (see also
[
2

4
]
and the derivation in
[

(4.44), (4.46)]
); it corresponds to the Allen–Cahn counterpart analyzed
in
[
18
]
. The unknowns in the state system have the following physical meaning:
\varphi
is a normalized difference between
the volume fractions
of pure phases in the binary mixture (the dimensionless
order parameter
of the phase transformation, which should attain its values in
the physical interval
[-1,1]
, where the extremes represent the pure phases of the mixture),

\mu
is the associated

chemical potential
, and
\,w\,
is the so-called
thermal displacement
(or

freezing index
), which is directly connected to the temperature
\vartheta
(which in the case of the Caginalp model is actually
a temperature difference) through the relation

w(\cdot,t)=w_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\vartheta(\cdot,s)\,ds,\quad t\in[0,T].

(10)

Moreover,
{\kappa_{1}}
and
{\kappa_{2}}
in (
3
) stand for prescribed positive coefficients related to the heat flux, which
is here assumed in the Green–Naghdi form
(see
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
)

\mathbf{q}=-\kappa_{1}\nabla({\partial_{t}}w)-\kappa_{2}\nabla w,

(11)

which accounts for a possible previous
thermal history of the phenomenon. Moreover,

\gamma
is a positive physical constant related to the intensity of the mass absorption/production of the source,
where the source term in (
1
) is
\,S:=f-\gamma\varphi
. This term reflects the fact that the system may not be isolated and a loss or production of mass is possible, which happens, e.g., in numerous liquid-liquid phase segregation problems that arise in cell biology
[
19
]

and in tumor growth models
[
20
]
. Notice that the presence of the source term entails that the property of mass conservation of the order
parameter is no longer valid; in fact, from (
1
) it directly follows that the mass balance has the form

\frac{d}{dt}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(t)\Bigr)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}{S(t)},\quad\mbox{for a.e. $t\in(0,T)$},

(12)

where
\,|\Omega|\,
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
.

In addition to the quantities already introduced,

\lambda
stands for the latent heat of the phase transformation,
a,b
are physical constants, and the control variable

\,u\,
is a distributed heat source/sink. Besides,
\varphi_{0},w_{0},
and
w_{1}
indicate some given initial values.
Moreover, the function
F
, whose derivative appears in (
2

\,F(r)=c_{1}-c_{2}r^{2}

with
c_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
c_{2}>0
, while
\,{\partial{I_{[-1,1]}}}\,

denotes the subdifferential of the indicator function
\,{I_{[-1,1]}}\,
of the real interval
[-1,1]
, which is given by

{I_{[-1,1]}}(r)=0\quad\mbox{if $|r|\leq 1$}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{I_{[-1,1]}}(r)=+\infty\quad\mbox{if $|r|>1$}\,.

(13)

The potential

F_{2obs}(r)={I_{[-1,1]}}(r)+F(r),

(14)

with
\,F\,
given as above, is then the typical double obstacle potential.

The state system (
1
)–(
5
) was recently analyzed in
[
9
]
concerning well-posedness and
regularity (see the results cited below in Section 2); in

[
10
]
the corresponding optimal control problem
(P)
has been analyzed for the simpler differentiable case
when the indicator function
{I_{[-1,1]}}
occurring in (

h_{\alpha}(r):=\alpha\,h(r),

(15)

with
\alpha>0

h(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1+r)\ln(1+r)+(1-r)\ln(1-r)&\quad\hbox{if $|r|<1$}\\
2\,\ln(2)&\quad\hbox{if $r\in\{-1,1\}$}\\
+\infty&\quad\hbox{if $|r|>1$}\end{array}\right.\,.

(16)

Clearly, in this case the subdifferential inclusion (
2
) has to be replaced by the equation

\mu=-\Delta\varphi+h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\varphi)+F^{\prime}(\varphi)+a-b{\partial_{t}}w\,.

(17)

For such logarithmic nonlinearities, in
[
10
]
results concerning existence of optimal controls, Fréchet differentiability of
the control-to-state operator, and meaningful first-order necessary optimality conditions
(in terms of the associated adjoint state problem and
variational inequality) have been derived, at least in the spatially two-dimensional situation. In this paper, we complement the
results of
[
10
]
by investigating the optimal control problem for the nondifferentiable double obstacle case.
While the existence of optimal controls is not too difficult to show, the derivation of
first-order necessary optimality conditions is a much more challenging task, since the existence of appropriate Lagrange multipliers
cannot be derived from the standard theory. We therefore
employ the so-called
deep quench approximation
, which has been successfully applied in a number of Allen–Cahn or
Cahn–Hilliard systems (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
8
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
). The general strategy of this approach is the following.
At first, we observe the following facts: it is readily seen that

\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\,h_{\alpha}(r)={I_{[-1,1]}}(r)\quad\forall\,r\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(18)

Moreover,
h^{\prime}(r)=\ln(\frac{1+r}{1-r})
and
h^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{2}{1-r^{2}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(r)=0\quad\,\hbox{for all }\,r\in(-1,1),

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\Bigl(\lim_{r\searrow-1}\,h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(r)\Bigr)=-\infty,\quad\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\Bigl(\lim_{r\nearrow 1}\,h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(r)\Bigr)=+\infty.

(19)

Hence, we may regard the graphs of the single-valued
\alpha
-dependent functions
h_{\alpha}^{\prime}
over
the interval
(-1,1)
as approximations to the graph of the subdifferential
\partial{I_{[-1,1]}}
from the interior of
(-1,1)
(in contrast to the
exterior approximation obtained via
the Moreau–Yosida approach).

In view of the convergence properties (
18
) and (
1
), it is to be expected that the solutions to the approximating system
(
1
), (
17
), (
3
)–(
5
) converge in a suitable topology to the solution of the state system
(
1
)–(
5
) as
\alpha\searrow 0
, and a similar behavior ought to be true for the corresponding minimizers of the
associated optimal control problems. It is then hoped that it is possible to pass to the limit as
\alpha\searrow 0
in the first-order
necessary optimality conditions for the approximating control problems in order to establish first-order conditions also for the double
obstacle case. It turns out that this general strategy works with suitable modifications.
Let us stress at this point that our approach makes use of the results obtained for the logarithmic case investigated in
[
10
]
;
since in that case the derivation of differentiability
properties of the associated control-to-state operator was only possible under the premise that the order parameter
\,\varphi\,
satisfies
the so-called
strict separation property
(meaning that
\,\varphi\,
attains its values in a compact subset of
(-1,1)
), and since
this property could only be shown in the spatially two-dimensional case, our analysis does not apply to three-dimensional domains

\,\Omega
.

The plan of the paper is as follows.
The next section is devoted to collect previous results concerning the well-posedness of the state system.
Then, in Section 3 and Section 4, we investigate the convergence properties of the deep quench approximations and of the associated optimal controls.
The final section brings the derivation of first-order necessary conditions of optimality for the problem

by employing the strategy explained above.

Remark 2

The above well-posedness result in fact refers to the natural variational form (

Let us conclude this section by collecting some useful tools that will be employed later on.
We make frequent use of the Young, Poincaré–Wirtinger and compactness inequalities:

\displaystyle ab\,\leq\,\delta a^{2}+\frac{1}{4\delta}\,b^{2}\quad\hbox{for every $a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and $\delta>0$},

(36)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{V}\,\leq\,C_{\Omega}\,\bigl(\|\nabla v\|+|\overline{v}|\bigr)\quad\hbox{for every $v\in V$},

(37)

\displaystyle\|v\|\,\leq\,\delta\,\|\nabla v\|+C_{\Omega,\delta}\,\|v\|_{*}\quad\hbox{for every $v\in V$ and $\delta>0$},

(38)

where
C_{\Omega}
depends only on
\Omega
,
C_{\Omega,\delta}
depends on
\delta
, in addition,
and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{*}
is the norm in
V^{*}
to be introduced below (see (
41
)).

Next, we recall an important tool which is commonly used when working with problems connected to the Cahn–Hilliard equation.
Consider the weak formulation of the Poisson equation
-\Delta z=\psi

with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions.
Namely, for a given
\psi\in V^{*}
(and not necessarily in
H
), we consider the problem:

\displaystyle\hbox{Find}\quad z\in V\quad\hbox{such that}\quad\int_{\Omega}\nabla z\cdot\nabla v=\langle\psi,v\rangle\quad\hbox{for every $v\in V$}.

(39)

Since
\Omega
is connected and regular, it is well known
that the above problem admits a family of solutions
z
if and only if
\psi
has zero mean value; among the solutions
z
there is only one with zero mean value.
Hence, we can introduce the associated solution operator
\cal N
, which turns out to be an isomorphism between the following spaces, by

\displaystyle{\cal N}:\mbox{dom}({\cal N}):=\{\psi\in V^{*}:\ \overline{\psi}=0\}\to\{z\in V:\ \overline{z}=0\},\quad{\cal N}:\psi\mapsto z,

(40)

where
z
is the unique solution to (
39
) satisfying
\overline{z}=0
.
Moreover, it follows that the formula

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{*}^{2}:=\|\nabla{\cal N}(\psi-\overline{\psi})\|^{2}+|\overline{\psi}|^{2}\quad\hbox{for every $\psi\in V^{*}$}

(41)

defines a Hilbert norm in
{V^{*}}
that is equivalent to the standard dual norm of
{V^{*}}
.
From the above properties, one can obtain the following identities:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla{\cal N}\psi\cdot\nabla v=\langle\psi,v\rangle\quad\hbox{for every $\psi\in\mbox{dom}({\cal N})$, $v\in V$},

(42)

\displaystyle\langle\psi,{\cal N}\zeta\rangle=\langle\zeta,{\cal N}\psi\rangle\quad\hbox{for every $\psi,\zeta\in\mbox{dom}({\cal N})$},

(43)

\displaystyle\langle\psi,{\cal N}\psi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\cal N}\psi|^{2}=\|\psi\|_{*}^{2}\quad\hbox{for every $\psi\in\mbox{dom}({\cal N})$},

(44)

as well as

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\!\langle{\partial_{t}}v(s),{\cal N}v(s)\rangle\mbox{d}s=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\!\langle v(s),{\cal N}({\partial_{t}}v(s))\rangle{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\|v(t_{2})\|_{*}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,\|v(t_{1})\|_{*}^{2}\,,

(45)

which holds for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
with
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
and every
v\in H^{1}(0,T;{V^{*}})
having zero mean value.

Finally, without further reference later on, we are going to employ the following convention: the capital-case symbol
C
is used to denote every constant
that depends only on the structural data of the problem such as

\Omega
,
T
,
a
,
b
,
{\kappa_{1}}
,
{\kappa_{2}}
,
\gamma
,
\lambda
, the shape of the
nonlinearities, and the norms of the involved functions. Therefore, its meaning may vary from line to line and even within the same line.
In addition, when a positive constant

enters the computation, then the related symbol

, in place of a general

, denotes constants
that depend on
\delta
, in addition.

\varphi

\,\frac{b}{\lambda}\,{\partial_{t}}w

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\|\varphi(t)\|_{*}^{2}\,+\,\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\|\varphi(s)\|_{*}^{2}{\rm d}s+\int_{Q_{t}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,+\,\frac{b}{\lambda}\int_{Q_{t}}|{\partial_{t}}w|^{2}{}

\displaystyle\quad+\,\frac{b\kappa_{1}}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w(t)|^{2}\,+\int_{Q_{t}}\bigl(h_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{1}})-h_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{2}})\bigr)\,\varphi

\displaystyle-\,\bigl(h_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{2}})-h_{\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{2}})\bigr)\,\varphi\,=\,(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\,h^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{2}})\,\varphi

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\int_{Q_{t}}\left|F^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{1}})-F^{\prime}(\varphi_{\alpha_{2}})\right|\,|\varphi|\,\leq\,C\int_{Q_{t}}|\varphi|^{2}

For any

, we pick an optimal control

in

First-order necessary optimality conditions

We now derive first-order necessary optimality conditions for
the control problem
(P)
, using the corresponding conditions
for
(
{\widetilde{\bf P}}_{\alpha}
)
as approximations. To this end, we generally assume that the conditions (

),
(
9
), and
(A1)
–
(A6)
are fulfilled. Moreover, we need an additional assumption:

(A7)

It holds that
\,\beta_{2}\,\varphi_{\Omega}\in V\,
and
\,\beta_{6}\,w^{\prime}_{\Omega}\in V

Notice that this assumption essentially requires a better regularity for the target data

and

Now let

with

(where only

and

\alpha\in(0,1]

Moreover, suppose that

)

(79)

with the convolution product
\circledast

Concerning this product, please note that
{\partial_{t}}(1\circledast r)=-r.

Let us, for convenience, introduce the abbreviations

(83)

Observe that the last summand of

is independent of time. By virtue of (
34
),
(A6)
, and
(A7)
, we have,
for every
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\|f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}\,+\,\|g_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}+\|\rho_{\alpha}\|_{V}\,+\,\|\pi_{\alpha}\|_{V}

(84)

where in the following we denote by

positive constants that may depend on the data of the system but not on
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

According to
[
10
, Thm. 4.5]
, the adjoint system has under the assumptions (
8
), (
9
) and
(A1)
–
(A7)

a unique weak solution with the regularity

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\in H^{1}(0,T;V^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;W),

(85)

\displaystyle q_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;V),

(86)

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\in H^{1}(0,T;H)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V).

(87)

Moreover, by virtue of
[
10
, Thm. 4.7]
, we know that the first-order optimality condition for the optimal control

is characterized by the variational inequality

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\left(r_{\alpha}+\nu u^{*}_{\alpha}+(u^{*}_{\alpha}-u^{*})\right)(v-u^{*}_{\alpha})\,\geq\,0\quad\forall\,v\in{\cal U}_{\rm ad}\,.

(88)

The next step consists in passing to the limit as
\alpha\searrow 0
in both the adjoint system (
75
)–(
79
)
and the variational inequality (
88
). To this end, uniform (with respect to
\alpha\in(0,1]
) estimates for the
adjoint variables
(p_{\alpha},q_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})
must be shown. A closer look at the system (
75
)–(
79
) reveals that
there is an inherent difficulty.
To this end, observe that (
76
) and (
78
) imply that
{\overline{q_{\alpha}}(t)}=0
for almost every
t\in(0,T)
.
Therefore, testing of (
75
)
with the constant function
v=\boldsymbol{1}/|\Omega|
, integration with respect to time over
[t,T]
, and application of
the well-known integration-by-parts rule for functions in
H^{1}(0,T;V^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)
, using the endpoint
conditions (
79
) along with the abbreviations (
81
)–(
83
),
yield the identity

\displaystyle{\overline{p_{\alpha}}(t)}\,

\displaystyle=\overline{\pi_{\alpha}}+\lambda\overline{\rho_{\alpha}}\,-\,\lambda\,{\overline{r_{\alpha}}(t)}\,+\,\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{Q^{t}}g_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad\,-\,\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{Q^{t}}\bigl[\gamma p_{\alpha}\,+\,h^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}\,+\,F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}\bigr]\,.

(89)

Here, and for the remainder of this paper, we put

Q^{t}:=\Omega\times(t,T)\quad\mbox{whenever $\,t\in[0,T)$.}

Apparently, the term
\,h^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}\,
cannot be controlled. Indeed, although
\varphi^{*}_{\alpha}
satisfies the strict separation
condition (
35
) for any fixed
\alpha>0
, a uniform bound cannot be expected, since it may well happen that the constants in

r_{*}(\alpha)\searrow-1
or

as

, in which case

may become unbounded. Consequently, we cannot hope to pass to the limit as
\alpha\searrow 0
in the system (
75
)–(
79
) as it
stands, not even in its weak form with test functions
v\in V
.
In order to overcome this difficulty, we employ an idea that goes back to
[
11
]
. To this end, recall that the mean value of
q_{\alpha}
vanishes
almost everywhere in
(0,T)
. Therefore, we deduce from (
76
) and (
78
) the identity

p_{\alpha}(t)-{\overline{p_{\alpha}}(t)}={\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t),

(90)

with the operator
{\cal N}
introduced in (
40
). Notice that
p_{\alpha}\in H^{1}(0,T;V^{*})
and
\overline{p_{\alpha}}\in H^{1}(0,T)
, whence we conclude
that also
{\cal N}q_{\alpha}\in H^{1}(0,T;V^{*})
.

The identity (
90
) enables us to eliminate
\overline{p_{\alpha}}
from the problem. For this purpose, we introduce the spaces

\displaystyle H_{0}:=\{v\in H:\overline{v}=0\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V_{0}:=\{v\in V:\overline{v}=0\}=V\cap H_{0}\,.

(91)

Now observe that the subspace
\mbox{span}\{\boldsymbol{1}\}
of spatially constant functions is the orthogonal complement of
H_{0}
with respect
to the inner product of
H
; moreover,
H_{0}
is a closed subspace of
H
and therefore a Hilbert space itself when equipped with the standard
inner product in
H
. In addition, owing to the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality (
37
), the expression

(v,w)_{0}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\quad\mbox{for $v,w\in V_{0}$}

(92)

defines an inner product on
V_{0}
whose associated norm is equivalent to the standard norm of
V
. Obviously,
V_{0}
becomes a Hilbert space when
endowed with the inner product
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{0}
, and since, according to
[
11
, Cor. 5.3]
,
V_{0}
is densely embedded in
H_{0}
,
we can construct the Hilbert triple
(V_{0},H_{0},V_{0}^{*})
with the dense and compact embeddings
\,V_{0}\subset H_{0}\subset V_{0}^{*}
and the usual
identification that

\langle v,w\rangle_{V_{0}}\,=\,(v,w)\quad\mbox{for all }\,v\in H_{0}\,\mbox{ and }\,w\in V_{0}.

(93)

The idea now is to change the standard variational version of the system (
75
)–(
79
) by not admitting every
v\in V
as
test function in (
75
), but only those having zero mean value. In this way,
we eliminate
\overline{p_{\alpha}}
from the problem; indeed, we easily find that the pair

(q_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})
solves the reduced system

\displaystyle\langle-{\partial_{t}}{\cal N}q_{\alpha},v\rangle_{V_{0}}\,+\int_{\Omega}\nabla q_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla v\,+\,\gamma\int_{\Omega}{\cal N}q_{\alpha}\,v-\,\lambda\int_{\Omega}{\partial_{t}}r_{\alpha}\,v

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{\Omega}\bigl(h_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}+F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}\bigr)\,v\,+\int_{\Omega}g_{\alpha}\,v

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{for all $v\in V_{0}$ and a.e. in $(0,T)$},

(94)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}{\partial_{t}}r_{\alpha}\,v\,+\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\kappa_{1}r_{\alpha}-\kappa_{2}(\boldsymbol{1}\circledast r_{\alpha}))\cdot\nabla v\,-\,b\int_{\Omega}q_{\alpha}\,v\,=\int_{\Omega}f_{\alpha}v

(95)

\displaystyle{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(T)\,=\,\pi_{\alpha}-\overline{\pi_{\alpha}}\,,\quad r_{\alpha}(T)=\,\rho_{\alpha}.

(96)

At this point, it is worth observing that, because of the zero mean value condition, the space
V_{0}
does not contain the space

, and therefore the variational equality with test functions

cannot be interpreted as an equation in the

)–(

note that all of the following calculations can be performed rigorously on the level of an approximating Faedo–Galerkin system using as
basis functions the eigenfunctions

with homogeneous Neumann conditions. That

\displaystyle-\Delta e_{j}=\lambda_{j}e_{j}\quad\mbox{in }\,\Omega,\qquad{\partial_{\bf n}}e_{j}=0\quad\mbox{on }\,\Gamma,\qquad\mbox{for all\, $j\in{\mathbb{N}}$},

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots,\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{j}=+\infty,\qquad{(e_{i},e_{j})}=\delta_{ij}\quad\mbox{for all\, }i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In this connection, observe that
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
forms a complete orthonormal system in

is obviously
a complete orthonormal system in the space

of functions having zero mean value, and the eigenspace associated with the eigenvalue

We now insert
v=q_{\alpha}(t)

(99)

Finally, integration by parts with respect to time and Young’s inequality yield the estimate

\displaystyle I_{4}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\,\frac{\lambda\kappa_{2}}{b}\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\boldsymbol{1}\circledast r_{\alpha})(t)\cdot\nabla r_{\alpha}(t)\,+\,\frac{\lambda\kappa_{2}}{b}\int_{Q^{t}}|\nabla r_{\alpha}|^{2}

Second estimate.
As a preparation for the next estimate, we introduce the space

{\cal Z}:=\{v\in H^{1}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{0}):\,\,v(0)=0\},

(104)

which, as a closed subspace, becomes a Hilbert space itself when endowed with the standard inner product
and norm of
H^{1}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{0})
. Notice that
{\cal Z}\subset C^{0}([0,T];H_{0})
which makes the
initial condition
v(0)=0
meaningful; we also have the dense and compact embeddings

\,{\cal Z}\subset L^{2}(0,T;H_{0})\subset{\cal Z}^{*}.

Moreover,
{\cal Z}
is dense in
L^{2}(0,T;V_{0})
since it
contains the dense subspace
H^{1}_{0}(0,T;V_{0})
. Therefore, the dual space
\,L^{2}(0,T;V_{0}^{*}){\cong}(L^{2}(0,T;V_{0}))^{*}

can be identified with a subspace of the dual space
{\cal Z}^{*}
in the usual way, i.e., such that

\langle v,w\rangle_{\cal Z}\,=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\langle v(t),w(t)\rangle_{V_{0}}\,{\rm d}t\quad\makebox{for all }\,v\in L^{2}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})\,\mbox{ and }\,w\in{\cal Z}\,.

(105)

Now, we take an arbitrary
v\in{\cal Z}
as test function in (
5
) and integrate over
(0,T)
. We obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle-{\partial_{t}}{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t),v(t)\rangle_{V_{0}}{\rm d}t\,+\int_{Q}\nabla q_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla v\,+\,\int_{Q}h_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}\,v

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\bigl(-\gamma\,{\cal N}q_{\alpha}\,+\,\lambda\,{\partial_{t}}r_{\alpha}-F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})q_{\alpha}+g_{\alpha}\bigr)\,v\,.

(106)

Next, we observe that

{\cal N}q_{\alpha}\in H^{1}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{0})

and integrate by parts in the first term of (
5
). With the help of (
5
), (
96
), and (
103
), we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\langle-{\partial_{t}}{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t),v(t)\rangle_{V_{0}}\,{\rm d}t=-(\pi_{\alpha},v(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\langle{\partial_{t}}v(t),{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t)\rangle_{V_{0}}\,{\rm d}t,

(107)

and, consequently, for every
v\in{\cal Z}
it holds

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\langle-{\partial_{t}}{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t),v(t)\rangle_{V_{0}}\,{\rm d}t\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\,\|\pi_{\alpha}\|\,\|v\|_{C^{0}([0,T];H_{0})}+\int_{0}^{T}\|{\cal N}q_{\alpha}(t)\|_{V_{0}}\,\|{\partial_{t}}v(t)\|_{V_{0}^{*}}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|v\|_{\cal Z}+C\,\|{\cal N}q_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}\,\|{\partial_{t}}v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})}\leq\,C\,\|v\|_{\cal Z}\,.

(108)

Hence, in view of the estimates (
5
) and (
108
),
we easily find from a comparison of terms in (
5
) that the linear functional

\Lambda_{\alpha}:{\cal Z}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad\langle\Lambda_{\alpha},v\rangle_{\cal Z}:=\int_{Q}h_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha})\,q_{\alpha}\,v\quad\mbox{for }\,v\in{\cal Z},

satisfies

\|\Lambda_{\alpha}\|_{\cal Z^{*}}\,\leq\,C\quad\forall\,\alpha\in(0,1].

(109)

By the estimates shown above, there exist a sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset(0,1]

such that

\alpha_{n}\searrow 0
and

\displaystyle q_{\alpha_{n}}\,

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly star in }\,L^{\infty}(0,T;V^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V),

(110)

\displaystyle r_{\alpha_{n}}\,

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly star in }\,H^{1}(0,T;H)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V),

(111)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}\circledast r_{\alpha_{n}}\,

\displaystyle\,\to\,\boldsymbol{1}\circledast r^{*}\,

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly star in }\,H^{2}(0,T;H)\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V),

(112)

\displaystyle{\cal N}q_{\alpha_{n}}\,

\displaystyle\,\to\,{\cal N}q^{*}\,

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly star in }\,L^{\infty}(0,T;V_{0})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)),

(113)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}}\,

\displaystyle\,\to\,\Lambda^{*}\,

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly in }\,{\cal Z}^{*}.

(114)

Moreover, in view of Theorem

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{n}}\to f^{*}:=\beta_{3}(\boldsymbol{1}\circledast(w^{*}-w_{Q}))+\beta_{4}(w^{*}(T)-w_{\Omega})+\beta_{5}({\partial_{t}}w^{*}-w_{Q}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{Q}\Bigl[{}-\gamma{\cal N}q^{*}+\lambda{\partial_{t}}r^{*}-F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*})q^{*}+g^{*}\Bigr]v\quad\mbox{for all $\,v\in{\cal Z}$},

(115)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{for all $v\in V$ and a.e. in $(0,T)$},

(116)

\displaystyle r^{*}(T)=\rho^{*}\,.

Theorem 6

Suppose that the conditions
(A1)
–
(A7)
, (
8
) and (

be a
minimizer of the optimal control problem
(P)
with the associated uniquely determined state components
\varphi^{*},w^{*}
.
Then there exist
\,q^{*},r^{*}
, and
\Lambda^{*}

such that the following holds true:

(i)

q^{*}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V_{0}^{*})\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)
,
\,{\cal N}q^{*}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V_{0})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))
,

,
\,\boldsymbol{1}\circledast r^{*}\in H^{2}(0,T;H)\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V)
,

\Lambda^{*}\in{\cal Z}^{*}
.

(ii)

The adjoint system (
5
)–(
117

)
are satisfied.

Remark 4

(i) Observe that the adjoint state variables
(q^{*},r^{*})
and the Lagrange multiplier
\Lambda^{*}
are not uniquely
determined. However, all possible
choices satisfy (
118
), i.e.,
u^{*}\,
is for
\nu>0
the
L^{2}(Q)
-orthogonal projection of
-{\nu}^{-1}r^{*}
onto the

{\cal U}_{\rm ad}
, and for a.e.
\,(x,t)\in Q

\displaystyle{}u^{*}(x,t)=\max\big\{u_{\rm min}(x,t),\min\{u_{\rm max}(x,t),-{\nu}^{-1}r^{*}(x,t)\}\big\}.

(ii) We have, for every

, the complementarity slackness condition

\langle\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}},q_{\alpha_{n}}\rangle_{{\cal Z}}=\int_{Q}h_{\alpha_{n}}^{\prime\prime}(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha_{n}})\,|q_{\alpha_{n}}|^{2}=\int_{Q}\frac{2\alpha_{n}}{1-(\varphi^{*}_{\alpha_{n}})^{2}}\,|q_{\alpha_{n}}|^{2}\,\geq\,0.

Unfortunately, our convergence properties for
