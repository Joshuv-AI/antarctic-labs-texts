Abstract
. In the paper, we describe the Drinfel’d double structure of the n n -rank Taft algebra and all of its simple modules, and then endow its R R -matrices with an application to knot invariants. The knot invariant we get is a generalization of the Jones polynomial, in particular, it coincides with the Jones polynomial in rank 1 1 case, while in rank 2 2 case, it is the one-parameter specialization of the two-parameter unframed Dubrovnik polynomial, and in higher rank case it is the composite ( n n -power) of the Jones polynomial.

1.
Introduction

Knots and
3
-manifolds are important research topics in low dimensional topology. In the early period, topologists found some classical knot invariants using some powerful tool, like homotopy groups or by means of elementary
algebraic combinatorics, like Alexander polynomials, later on, Jones,
Kauffman, HOMFLY polynomials appeared successively.
In the late 1980s, quantum invariants were discovered in the original work
[
RT1
,
RT2
]
and
[
Tu1
,
Wi
]
, where quantum groups and the relevant representations are used to construct knot invariants
and even invariants of
3
-manifolds. Many classical invariants of knots were recovered via the approach of quantum invariants. With the studies of quantum groups and their representation theories towards to categorifications,
the studies of knot theory have stepped into a new age (see
[
Tu2
,
Ka
,
Oht
,
Ro
,
Kh
,
CR
,
LQR
,
WW
,
Tub
]
, etc.) and some important progress, for example, of the Labastida-Marino-Ooguri-Vafa conjecture in string theory, see
[
LP
]
, and the volume conjecture in geometry, see
[
CL
]
,
[
CY
]
etc. and references therein.

In the paper, we adopt Kauffman-Radford’s construction of the oriented regular
invariants arising from the oriented quantum algebras in
[
KR1
]
, which strongly depends on the
quasitrangular structure of quantum groups. The main source of
quasitriangular Hopf algebras is the Drinfel’d double,
proposed by Drinfel’d
[
Dri
]
.
The oriented quantum algebras we used here are the Drinfel’d doubles of a certain kind of finite dimensional Hopf algebras,
the
n
-rank Taft algebras
we defined.
In 1971, Taft
[
Ta
]
found a famous non-semisimple Hopf algebra of prime square dimension.
That is the so-christened Taft algebra. In 2001, Hu
[
Hu2
]
defined the quantized universal enveloping algebras of abelian Lie algebras.
In particular, when
q
is a root of unity, there exists a finite dimensional quotient Hopf algebra, called
the
n
-rank Taft algebra
. In rank
1
case, it is the Taft algebra.

The paper is organized as follows. We first determine the Drinfel’d double
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
of the
n
-rank Taft algebra in Section 3 and give the sufficient and necessary condition for
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
to be a ribbon Hopf algebra with its ribbon element
\nu
(Theorem
3.7
). Also, we point out that

\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n)
is a Hopf 2-cocycle twist of the
n
-fold tensor product
\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(1)^{\otimes n}
(we say the
rank
n
Taft algebra) (Theorem
3.8
), thus they have equivalent Yetter-Drinfel’d module categories.
In Section 4, we recall the oriented quantum algebra and Kauffman-Radford’s approach to regular isotopic invariants of knots and links, and improve the Kauffman-Radford construction to yield the ambient isotopic invariants, the oriented ambient invariants associated with this kind of ribbon Hopf algebras (Proposition
4.3
).
Section 5 concentrates on exploring knot ambient invariants associated with the Drinfel’d double
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
.

In Section 5, we first determine all simple
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
-modules (Theorem
5.1
), and give the sufficient and necessary condition for a simple
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
-module to be self-dual (Theorem
5.3
).
Our aim is to obtain a kind of quantum knot invariants subordinating to this Drinfel’d double, which is a generalization of the Jones polynomial. In rank
1
case, we recover the Jones polynomial (Example
5.4
). For the
2
-rank Taft algebra, we work with the
4
-dimensional self-dual simple
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(2))
-module and obtain a new semisimple
16\times 16
braiding
R
-matrix with the minimal polynomial of degree
3
(Example
5.6
), from which we derive the same skein relation of Dubrovnik type. To our surprise, it is not related to the quantum groups of
BCD
type, but the latter is! We thus investigate the endomorphism algebra
\mbox{End}_{D(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(2))}\left(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(\ell-1)\epsilon_{2},i_{0}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})}^{\otimes r}\right)
of
D(\mathscr{\bar{A}}_{q}(n))
-module
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(\ell-1)\epsilon_{2},i_{0}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})}^{\otimes r}
with the dimension-square of the Temperley-Lieb algebra, which is different than the Birman-Wenzl-Murakami algebra (arising from the quantum groups of
BCD
type) but contains its quotient as the proper subalgebra (Theorem
5.8
, Proposition
5.9
). These distinguished features stimulate us to compute some knot invariants for those knots or links with lower crossings following Kauffman-Radford’s algorithm. The result indicates the square of the Jones polynomial in the rank
2
case (Theorem
5.12
). We argue it is true in generality in terms of the skein relations. As a generalization to higher rank, we can derive that the ambient isotopic invariant, associated to the double of
n
-rank Taft algebra, is the
composite (
n
-power) of the Jones polynomial (Corollary
5.13
).

2.
Notation and Definitions

Throughout the paper, let
k
be an algebraically closed field of
characteristic
0
. Due to Manin
[
Ma
]
, the

quantum affine
n
-space
over
k
is a quadratic algebra

k[A_{q}^{n|0}]=k\bigl\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\bigr\}/(x_{i}x_{j}-qx_{j}x_{i},\ i>j\,).

Now let
\Lambda=\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}\epsilon_{i}
be a free abelian group of
n
-rank, where
\epsilon_{i}=(\delta_{1i},\ldots,\delta_{ni})
, and
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol.
Hence
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
is a basis of
\Lambda
. Besides, for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
,
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\Lambda
,
we denote
\delta_{\alpha,\beta}=\delta_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}\cdots\delta_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}
.

As in
[
Hu1
]
, a skew bicharacter

\theta:\Lambda\times\Lambda\rightarrow k^{\times}
on
\Lambda
is defined as follows,

\theta(\alpha,\beta)=q^{\alpha*\beta-\beta*\alpha},\quad\alpha,\ \beta\in\Lambda,\quad q\in k^{\times},

where
\alpha*\beta=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i>j}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}
.
In particular,

(2.1)

\theta(\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j})=\begin{cases}q,&i>j\,,\\
1,&i=j\,,\\
q^{-1},&i<j\end{cases}

and

\theta(\alpha,\beta)^{-1}=\theta(\beta,\alpha)=\theta(-\alpha,\beta)=\theta(\alpha,-\beta),\quad\theta(0,\alpha)=\theta(\alpha,0)=\theta(\alpha,\alpha)=1.

Now let
\Lambda_{+}\coloneqq\bigl\{\alpha=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\epsilon_{i}\in\Lambda\mid\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}\bigr\}
,
and
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}

be any non-zero monomial in
k[A_{q}^{n|0}]
,
then
\bigl\{x^{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Lambda_{+}\bigr\}
constitutes
a canonical basis of
k[A_{q}^{n|0}]

. Moreover,

x^{\alpha}x^{\beta}=q^{\alpha*\beta}x^{\alpha+\beta}=\theta(\alpha,\beta)x^{\beta}x^{\alpha}
, by definition.

For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
, consider the algebra automorphism
K(\alpha)
of
k[A_{q}^{n|0}]
as follow,

K(\alpha)(x^{\gamma})=\theta(\alpha,\gamma)q^{\langle\alpha,\gamma\rangle}x^{\gamma},\qquad x^{\gamma}\in k[A_{q}^{n|0}],

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\Lambda\times\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is a symmetric bilinear form with
\langle\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
.

Apparently,
K(\alpha)K(\beta)=K(\alpha+\beta)=K(\beta)K(\alpha)
. When
q=1
,

K(\alpha)=\mbox{id}
.

As in
[
Hu2
]
, let
\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)
be an associative
k
-algebra with
1
,
generated by the symbol
K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm 1},x_{i}~(1\leq i\leq n)
,
and associated with a skew bicharacter
\theta
. It satisfies the relations

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{(r1)}&K(\epsilon_{i})K(\epsilon_{j})=K(\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{j})=K(\epsilon_{j})K(\epsilon_{i}),\quad K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm}K(\epsilon_{i})^{\mp}=K(0)=1,\\
\mbox{(r2)}&K(\epsilon_{i})^{\ell}=K(\ell\epsilon_{i})=1,\quad\bigl(\,\ell=char(q)\,\bigr)\\
\mbox{(r3)}&K(\epsilon_{i})x_{j}K(\epsilon_{i})^{-1}=\theta(\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j})q^{\delta_{ij}}x_{j},\\
\mbox{(r4)}&x_{i}x_{j}=\theta(\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{j})x_{j}x_{i},\end{array}

where
\mbox{char}(q)=\mbox{min}\bigl\{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}\mid q^{r}=1\bigr\}
. We view
\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)
as the
quantized universal enveloping algebra of an abelian Lie algebra of dimension
n
.
Obviously,

\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)
contains the quantum affine
n
-space
k[A_{q}^{n|0}]
as its subalgebra.
We have the Hopf algebra structure of
(\mathscr{A}_{q}(n),\Delta,\varepsilonup,s)
as follows

\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta:\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)\rightarrow\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)\otimes\mathscr{A}_{q}(n),\quad\Delta(K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm})=K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm}\otimes K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm},\quad\Delta(x_{i})=x_{i}\otimes 1+K(\epsilon_{i})\otimes x_{i},\\
\varepsilonup:\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)\rightarrow k,\quad\varepsilonup(K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm})=1,\quad\varepsilonup(x_{i})=0,\\
s:\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)\rightarrow\mathscr{A}_{q}(n),\quad s(K(\epsilon_{i})^{\pm})=K(\epsilon_{i})^{\mp},\quad s(x_{i})=-K(\epsilon_{i})^{-1}x_{i}.\end{array}

For convenience of quotation, we also give some definitions about Hopf algebras.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a Hopf algebra
H
over
k
,
\Gamma\in H
is called a
left integral
of
H
, if

h\,\Gamma=\varepsilonup(h)\,\Gamma
, for any
h\in H
.
Similarly, we can define right integrals of
H
.

Denote by
G(H)
the group consisting of group-like elements of Hopf algebra
H
.

Definition 2.2
.

When
H
is finite dimensional, there exists a unique group-like element
\alpha\in H^{*}
, satisfying

\Gamma\,h=\alpha(h)\,\Gamma
, for any
h\in H
,
where
\Gamma
is any non-zero left integral of
H
.

\alpha
is called the
distinguished group-like element
of
H^{*}
.

As the dual space of the finite dimensional Hopf algebra
H
,
H^{*}
has a natural Hopf algebra structure, we also have the concept of left or right integral of
H^{*}
.
But distinct from that of
H
, it usually defines the distinguished group-like element
g\in H
associated with a non-zero right integral
\lambda
of
H^{*}
, that is,

p\lambda=p(g)\lambda,\ \forall\;p\in H^{*}
.

Recall the notion of quasitriangular Hopf algebras (see
[
Ka
]
,
[
Mon
]
).

Definition 2.3
.

An
almost cocommutative Hopf algebra
over
k
is a pair
(H,R)
, where
H
is a Hopf algebra, and
R\in H\otimes H

is an invertible element, such that for arbitrary
h\in H
,

(2.2)

\tau(\Delta(h))=R\Delta(h)R^{-1},

where
\tau
is the flip map on
H\otimes H
.

In addition, if
R
satisfies:

(\Delta\otimes\mbox{Id})R=R^{13}R^{23}
,
(\mbox{Id}\otimes\Delta)R=R^{13}R^{12}
,

(H,R)
is called a
quasitriangular Hopf algebra
over
k
, and
R
is a
universal
R
-matrix
of
H
, where

R^{12}=\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}\otimes 1,R^{23}=\sum\nolimits_{i}1\otimes a_{i}\otimes b_{i},R^{13}=\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{i}\otimes 1\otimes b_{i}
, if set
R=\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
.

If
(H,R)
is quasitriangular,
R
satisfies the quantum Yang-Baxter equation

R^{12}R^{13}R^{23}=R^{23}R^{13}R^{12}.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a quasitriangular Hopf algebra
(H,R)
,
v\in H
is said a
quasi-ribbon element
,
if it satisfies the conditions

\begin{array}[]{l}\quad v^{2}=us(u),\quad s(v)=v,\quad\varepsilonup(v)=1,\quad\Delta(v)=(R^{21}R)^{-1}(v\otimes v).\end{array}

where
R^{21}=\tau(R)
, and
u=\sum_{i}s(b_{i})a_{i}
, called the
Drinfel’d element
.
When
v
lies in the center of
H
, it is called a
ribbon element
, and
(H,R,v)
a
ribbon Hopf algebra
over
k
.

Recall the definition of Drinfel’d double
D(H)
for any finite dimensional Hopf algebra
H
(see
[
Mon
,
\lx@sectionsign 10.3
]
).
Denote

D(H)=(H^{*})^{{\scriptsize\mbox{cop}}}\bowtie H
.
Its multiplication is defined as

(p\otimes h)(q\otimes k)=p\left(h_{(1)}\rightharpoonup q\leftharpoonup s^{-1}(h_{(3)})\right)\otimes h_{(2)}k,\quad\forall\;h,k\in H,\;p,q\in H^{*},

while the comultiplication coincides with that of
(H^{*})^{{\scriptsize\mbox{cop}}}\otimes H
. Here
\rightharpoonup
(resp.
\leftharpoonup
) denotes the left (resp. right) action of
H
on
H^{*}
given by

h\rightharpoonup p=p_{(2)}(h)p_{(1)}\,\quad(\text{resp. }p_{(1)}(h)p_{(2)}=p\leftharpoonup h).

D(H)
is quasitriangular, whose universal
R
-matrix is given by

(2.3)

\mathcal{R}=(\varepsilonup\otimes h_{i})\otimes(h^{i}\otimes 1),

where
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\}
is a
k
-basis of
H
,
and
\{h^{1},\ldots,h^{r}\}
is the dual basis in
H^{*}
with
r=\mbox{dim}H
. Note that

\mathcal{R}^{-1}=(\varepsilonup\otimes s(h_{i}))\otimes(h^{i}\otimes 1)
,
where we used the Sweedler’s notation:

\Delta(h)=h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)},\ \forall\,h\in H
, as well as
the Einstein’s sum convention to omit the symbol “
\sum
”.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a Hopf algebra
H
, a vector space
M
is called a
Yetter-Drinfel’d
H
-module
, if
M
has a left
H
-module structure
(M,\cdot)
, and a right
H
-comodule structure
(M,\rho)
, such that two structures satisfy the compatible condition

h_{(1)}\cdot m_{(0)}\otimes h_{(2)}m_{(1)}=(h_{(2)}\cdot m)_{(0)}\otimes(h_{(2)}\cdot m)_{(1)}h_{(1)},\quad\forall\;h\in H,\;m\in M

where
\rho(m)=m_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}\in M\otimes H
.
When
H^{{\scriptsize\mbox{op}}}
has antipode
\varsigmaup
, the condition above is equivalent to

\rho(h\cdot m)=h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)}\otimes h_{(3)}m_{(1)}\varsigmaup(h_{(1)}),\quad\forall\;h\in H,\;m\in M.

Denote by
{}_{H}\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{D}^{H}
the category of the Yetter-Drinfel’d
H
-modules,
whose morphisms are homomorphisms of both left
H
-modules and right
H
-comodules. By a theorem of Majid, for a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, a
k
-vector space
M
is a left
D(H)
-module if and only if
M\in{{}_{H}\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{D}^{H}}
. As is known,
M
has a right
H
-comodule structure
(M,\rho)
if and only if it has a left
H^{*}
-module structure
(M,\cdot)
, which is defined by

p\cdot m=p(m_{(1)})m_{(0)},\quad\forall\;p\in H^{*},\;m\in M.

Hence, the left
D(H)
-module structure of
M\in{{}_{H}\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{D}^{H}}
is defined by

(p\otimes h)\cdot m=p\cdot(h\cdot m)=p\left(h_{(3)}m_{(1)}\varsigmaup(h_{(1)})\right)h_{(2)}\cdot m_{(0)},\quad\forall\;h\in H,\;p\in H^{*},\;m\in M.

In addition, for any finite-dimensional representation
(M,\pi)
of
D(H)
, we get a solution of the Yang-Baxter equation

R_{M}=\tau\circ(\pi\otimes\pi)(\mathcal{R})\in\mbox{End}(M\otimes M),

where
\tau
is the flip map on
M\otimes M
. That means

\left(R_{M}\otimes\mbox{Id}_{M}\right)\left(\mbox{Id}_{M}\otimes R_{M}\right)\left(R_{M}\otimes\mbox{Id}_{M}\right)=\left(\mbox{Id}_{M}\otimes R_{M}\right)\left(R_{M}\otimes\mbox{Id}_{M}\right)\left(\mbox{Id}_{M}\otimes R_{M}\right).

Explicitly, for
\forall\;m,n\in M
, we have

(2.4)

\begin{split}R_{M}(m\otimes n)&=(h^{i}\otimes 1)\cdot n\otimes(\varepsilonup\otimes h_{i})\cdot m\\
&=h^{i}(n_{(1)})n_{(0)}\otimes h_{i}\cdot m=n_{(0)}\otimes n_{(1)}\cdot m\end{split}

3.
Drinfel’d double of the
n
-rank Taft algebra

When
q
is a primitive
\ell
-th root of unity, i.e.,
\ell=\mbox{char}(q)<\infty
, there exists a Hopf ideal of
\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)
generated by

x_{1}^{\ell},\cdots,x_{n}^{\ell}
, denoted by
I
(cf.
[
Hu2
]
). The quotient Hopf algebra

\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)=\mathscr{A}_{q}(n)/I,

is called the
n
-rank Taft algebra
. When
n=1
, it is the Taft algebra. Furthermore, if
\ell=2
, it is the Sweedler algebra of dimension
4
.

Recall the Gauss integers as follows,

\begin{array}[]{l}(0)_{q}=0,\quad(0)_{q}!=1,\\
(m)_{q}=1+\cdots+q^{m-1}=(1-q^{m})/(1-q),\\
(m)_{q}!=(1)_{q}\cdots(m)_{q},\quad\forall\;m\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0},\end{array}

Notice that
(m)_{q}!\neq 0,\ 0\leq m<\ell
, and
(m)_{q}!=0,\ m\geq\ell
.
One can define

{m\choose j}_{q}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(m)_{q}!}{(m-j)_{q}!(j)_{q}!},&\mbox{if}\quad 0\leq m<\ell,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Endow
\Lambda
with a natural partial order
\leq
as follows

\forall\;\alpha,\;\beta\in\Lambda,\quad\alpha\leq\beta\quad\mbox{\it if and only if}\quad\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i},\quad\forall\;1\leq i\leq n.

For
\forall\;\alpha\in\Lambda_{+}
,
define
\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\alpha}=\big\{\,\beta\in\Lambda_{+}\mid\beta\leq\alpha\,\big\}
,
and set

\kappa=(\ell-1,\ldots,\ell-1),\quad\iota=(\ell,\ldots,\ell)\in\Lambda_{+}.

Then
\big\{\,x^{\gamma}K(\alpha)\mid\alpha,\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\,\big\}
is a
k
-basis of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
. Hence,

\mbox{dim}\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)=\ell^{2n}
.

Recall the Hopf algebra structure of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
.
Thanks to

\Delta(x_{i}^{\gamma_{i}})=\sum\limits_{\xi_{i}=0}^{\gamma_{i}}{{\gamma_{i}}\choose{\xi_{i}}}_{q}x_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-\xi_{i}}K(\epsilon_{i})^{\xi_{i}}\otimes x_{i}^{\xi_{i}},~i=1,\ldots,n,

combining with relations
(r1)
–
(r4)
, we have the explicit comultiplication formula of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)

(3.1)

\Delta(x^{\gamma}K(\alpha))=\sum\limits_{\xi\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\gamma}}{\gamma\choose\xi}q^{-(\gamma-\xi)*\xi}x^{\gamma-\xi}K(\xi+\alpha)\otimes x^{\xi}K(\alpha),

where
\alpha,\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa},~{\displaystyle{{\gamma\choose\alpha}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\gamma_{i}\choose\alpha_{i}}_{q}}}
, together with the counit

(3.2)

\varepsilonup(x^{\gamma}K(\alpha))=\delta_{\gamma,0}.

Set
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\Lambda
.
By
(r1)
–
(r4)
, for
\forall\;\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}
,
we can get

(3.3)

\begin{split}s(x^{\gamma})&=(-1)^{|\gamma|}q^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\gamma,\gamma+\boldsymbol{1}\rangle}x^{\gamma}K(-\gamma)\\
&=(-1)^{|\gamma|}q^{\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\gamma,\gamma-\boldsymbol{1}\rangle}\theta(\gamma,\gamma)^{-1}K(-\gamma)x^{\gamma},\\
s^{2}(x^{\gamma})&=q^{-|\gamma|}x^{\gamma},\end{split}

where
|\gamma|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}
.

For the group-like elements of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n))
, it is easy to see that

Proposition 3.1
.

G(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n))=\left\{K(\alpha)~\big|~\alpha\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\right\}.

In particular,

(3.4)

G(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n))\cong G(T_{\ell})^{\times n}\cong\overbrace{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}^{n},

where
T_{\ell}
is the Taft algebra with dimension
\ell^{2}
.

Now consider the dual Hopf algebra
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}
,
with the canonical Hopf algebra structure induced by
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
.
For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
,
\beta
,
\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\in\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*},\qquad\mathcal{K}(\alpha)(x^{\gamma}K(\beta))=\delta_{\gamma,0}q^{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i}\in\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*},\qquad\mathcal{X}_{i}(x^{\gamma}K(\beta))=\delta_{\gamma,\epsilon_{i}}\quad(i=1,\cdots,n).

It is easy to check that
\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\in G\left(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}\right)
, and

[formula]

\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\mathcal{K}(\beta)=\mathcal{K}(\alpha+\beta),\quad\forall\;\alpha,\;\beta\in\Lambda.

By the definition of
\mathcal{K}(\alpha)
and Eq. (
3.2
), we know that
\mathcal{K}(0)=\varepsilonup
, which is the identity of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}
. In addition, we claim that the map
\alpha\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\mapsto\mathcal{K}(\alpha)
is injective. In fact, note that

\mathcal{K}(\alpha)(K(\epsilon_{i}))=q^{\alpha_{i}},~i=1,\ldots,n.

Hence,
\mathcal{K}(\alpha)=\varepsilonup,~-\kappa\leq\alpha\leq\kappa
if and only if
\alpha=0
. Together with
(*)
, the claim is true.

By Proposition
3.1
, we have

Proposition 3.2
.

G(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*})=\big\{\,\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\,\big\}\cong\overbrace{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}^{n}.

Proof.

First we see that any group-like element of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}
restricted to
G(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n))
is a group character. Since for
\forall\;\chi\in G(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*})
,

\chi(K(\alpha)K(\beta))=\Delta^{*}(\chi)(K(\alpha)\otimes K(\beta))=\chi(K(\alpha))\chi(K(\beta)),~\chi(1)=\varepsilonup^{*}(\chi)=1.

By Dedekind’s lemma
[
Mo
, p.19]
about linear independence of distinct group characters,
this means the linear independence of distinct group-like elements.
By isomorphism (
3.4
), the character group of

Definition 4.1

is the dual basis of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}
with

r=\mbox{dim}\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)=\ell^{2n}
.

In particular, by Proposition
3.4
, we can choose the bases
\bigl\{x^{\gamma}K(\alpha)\mid\alpha,\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\bigr\}
and

\bigl\{\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\mid\alpha,\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\bigr\}
dual to each other to give the formula of the universal
R
-matrix

(4.1)

\mathcal{R}=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}}\left(\varepsilonup\otimes x^{\gamma}K(\alpha)\right)\otimes\left(\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\otimes 1\right).

4.1.
Regular isotopy invariants from TOQA’s

Next, we simply introduce the construction of regular isotopy invariants arising from TOQA’s in
[
KR1
]
.
For the basic knowledge of knot theory, one can refer to
[
Kau2
]
. For the material about regular isotopy invariants, one can consult
[
Kau1
]
.

Given a TOQA
(A,\rho,D,U,G)
,
the first step is to construct a regular isotopy invariant of oriented 1-1 tangle diagrams,

\mbox{Inv}_{A}:\mbox{Tang}\rightarrow A,

where
Tang
is the collection of all oriented 1-1 tangle diagrams.
Figure 1 is a simple example of oriented 1-1 tangle.

Figure 1

In order to obtain
Inv
, Kauffman and Radford (
[

]
) first construct a formal product
\mbox{W}_{A}(\mbox{T}),\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Tang}
,
then they turn to the specialization
\mbox{w}_{A}(\mbox{T})\in A
,
and take
\mbox{Inv}_{A}(\mbox{T})=\mbox{w}_{A}(\mbox{T})
.

The formal product
\mbox{W}_{A}(\mbox{T})
is defined by an elaborate algorithm, which involves sliding labeled beads.
In detail, for
\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Tang}
, if it has no crossings,
let
\mbox{W}_{A}(\mbox{T})=1
. Otherwise, if
T
has
m\geq 1
crossings, traverse it in the direction of orientation,
and label the crossing lines
1,\ldots,2m
, successively.
Also give line
i
the decoration
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 2m
.
Here is an example of oriented 1-1 tangle completing decoration.

\textstyle{x_{2}}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{x_{1}}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{x_{4}}

Apart from the crossings, there are the following blocks left for an oriented tangle diagram, i.e., four oriented local extrema

\textstyle{\mbox{(u}_{-})}
\textstyle{(\mbox{u}_{+})}
\textstyle{(\mbox{d}_{+})}
\textstyle{(\mbox{d}_{-})}

For
1\leq i\leq 2m
,
let
u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(i)
be the number of local extrema of type
(\mbox{d}_{+})

minus the number of type
(\mbox{d}_{-})
encountered on the portion of the traversal from line
i
to the end.
Similarly,
u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(i)
is defined.
Now set

\mbox{W}_{A}(\mbox{T})=\left(D^{u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(1)}\circ U^{u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(1)}\right)(x_{1})\cdots\left(D^{u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(2m)}\circ U^{u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(2m)}\right)(x_{2m}).

When turning to the specialization of
\mbox{W}_{A}(\mbox{T})
in
A
, the decoration of the crossings is modified as

(4.2)

\textstyle{\bullet}

\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{E^{\prime}}
\textstyle{\bullet}

\textstyle{\bullet}

\textstyle{L_{+}}

Here
\rho^{-1}=E\otimes E^{\prime},~\rho=e\otimes e^{\prime}
.
As we demand
\mbox{Inv}_{A}
to be a regular isotopy invariant,
the decoration of other kind of oriented crossings should be computed from two standard cases above,
by adding some local extrema. For instance, we have

\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{e}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{U(e^{\prime})}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{U(E)}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{E^{\prime}}

Having changed
W_{A}(\mbox{T})
to
\mbox{Inv}_{A}(\mbox{T})
, one can be transferred to the case of oriented link diagrams.

Any oriented knot diagram can be viewed as an oriented 1-1 tangle diagram with ends joined.
And an oriented link diagram consists of one or more oriented knot diagrams, the components of the link diagram.
For example, if we join the ends of the tangle in Fig. 1, it becomes a left-handed Trefoil knot.

By
[
KR1
, Theorem 3]
, in order to convert an invariant of oriented tangles to the one of links, besides the twist element
G
, it still needs a
k
-valued trace-like function
tr
on
A^{*}
, satisfying
\mbox{tr}\circ D=\mbox{tr}=\mbox{tr}\circ U
. Here
f\in A^{*}
is a trace-like function if and only if
f(ab)=f(ba),\forall\,a,b\in A
.
To each component we have to choose a base point for computation. Using
G
to modify the trace-like function, one can show that the corresponding invariant is independent of the choice of base points.

Now let
Link
be the collection of all oriented link diagrams.
Assume that
\mbox{L}\in\mbox{Link}
has components
\mbox{L}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{L}_{r}
, then one can define the formal product
\mbox{W}(\mbox{L}_{\ell})
as follow.

When
\mbox{L}_{\ell}
has no crossings, let
\mbox{W}(\mbox{L}_{\ell})=1
. Otherwise,
one can choose a base point
P_{\ell}
on a vertical line of
\mbox{L}_{\ell}
for topological reasons.
The bead starts from
P_{\ell}
, slides in the direction of orientation, and stops when back to
P_{\ell}
.
The crossing lines encountered on the traversal are successively labeled as
(\ell:1),\ldots,(\ell:m_{\ell})
.
For
1\leq i\leq m_{\ell}
, we define
u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(\ell:i)
and
u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(\ell:i)
, similarly to the case of tangles. The only change is the involved portion of the traversal, now just from crossing line
(\ell:i)
to base point
P_{\ell}
.
Let
x_{(\ell:i)}
be the decoration on crossing line
(\ell:i)
, and set

\mbox{W}(\mbox{L}_{\ell})=\left(D^{u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(\ell:1)}\circ U^{u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(\ell:1)}\right)(x_{(\ell:1)})\cdots\left(D^{u_{\tiny\mbox{d}}(\ell:m_{\ell})}\circ U^{u_{\tiny\mbox{u}}(\ell:m_{\ell})}\right)(x_{(\ell:m_{\ell})}).

Similarly, using
\rho
and
\rho^{-1}
to get the specialization
\mbox{w}(\mbox{L}_{\ell})
in
A
, one then defines the invariant of oriented links as

(4.3)

\mbox{Inv}_{A,\scriptsize\mbox{tr}}(\mbox{L})=\mbox{tr}\left(G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{L}_{1})}\mbox{w}(\mbox{L}_{1})\right)\cdots\mbox{tr}\left(G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{L}_{r})}\mbox{w}(\mbox{L}_{r})\right),

where
\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{L}_{i})
is the
Whitney degree
of
\mbox{L}_{i}
,
i.e., the sum of the rotation numbers of its local extrema.
The function
Wd
is an elementary regular invariant of oriented link diagrams.
The rotation number of the local extrema of type
(\mbox{u}_{-})
and
(\mbox{d}_{-})
are
1/2
,
while those of the other two types
(\mbox{u}_{+})
and
(\mbox{d}_{+})
are
-1/2
.

In order to construct the invariant
Inv
of oriented links,
one needs a trace-like function
tr
on
D(H)
for any Hopf algebra
H
over
k
.
Note that a trace-like function on
D(H)
is just a cocommutative element in
D(H)^{*}
, where
H
is finite dimensional.

In particular, since
D(H)^{*}
is isomorphic to
(H^{*{{\scriptsize\mbox{cop}}}}\otimes H)^{*}=H^{*}\otimes H^{{\scriptsize\mbox{op}}}=\mbox{Hom}(H,H^{{\scriptsize\mbox{op}}})
as an algebra, there exists an algebra isomorphism

\mbox{f}:\mbox{Hom}(H,H^{{\scriptsize\mbox{op}}})\rightarrow D(H)^{*},\quad\mbox{f}(T)(p\otimes a)=p(T(a)),\quad\forall\;T\in\mbox{End}(H),\ p\in H^{*},\ a\in H,

where
\mbox{Hom}(H,H^{{\scriptsize\mbox{op}}})
has the canonical convolution algebra structure.

By
[
RW
, Thm. 2.2]
,
\mbox{f}(T)
is a cocommutative element in
D(H)^{*}
, if and only if

\displaystyle l(a_{(2)})\circ T\circ r(a_{(1)})=r(a_{(1)})\circ T\circ l(a_{(2)}),\quad\textit{and}

\displaystyle l(p_{(2)})\circ T^{*}\circ r(p_{(1)})=r(p_{(1)})\circ T^{*}\circ l(p_{(2)}),

hold for
\forall a\in H,\;p\in H^{*}
. Here
l
(resp.
r
) is the left (resp., right) multiplication transformation of
H
(or
H^{*}
).

On the other hand, as a
k
-vector space,
D(H)=H^{*}\otimes H
can be identified with
\mbox{End}(H)
.
Let
Tr
be the usual trace on
\mbox{End}(H)
, then

\mbox{f}(T)(p\otimes a)=p(T(a))=\mbox{Tr}(p\otimes T(a))=\mbox{Tr}(T\circ(p\otimes a)).

Hence,

(4.4)

\mbox{f}(T)\left(T^{\prime}\right)=\mbox{Tr}\left(T\circ T^{\prime}\right),\qquad\forall\;T^{\prime}\in\mbox{End}(H)=D(H).

From the discussion above, the usual candidates for a trace-like function on
D(H)
are

\mbox{f}(\mbox{Id}_{H})=\mbox{Tr},\qquad\mbox{f}(s^{-1})=\mbox{Tr}\circ s^{-1}.

4.2.
Ambient knot invariants from ribbon Hopf algebras

In general, to directly compute
\mbox{Inv}_{H,\scriptsize\mbox{tr}}
even for some usual link
L
when
H=\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
is rather difficult.
In order to jump out of the dilemma, we turn to representations of
H
, since quantum invariants often originate from representations of quantum groups.
In fact, around early 1990, Reshetinkhin, Turaev, etc. had given the prototype of quantum invariants, i.e., operator invariants, see
[
Tu1
]
,
[
Tu2
]
,
[
RT1
]
. That is, given any semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and a representation
(\pi,V)
of its quantized universal enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
, one can construct the corresponding operator invariant, called the quantum
(\mathfrak{g},\pi)
invariant in
[
Oht
]
. For example, the regular invariant, Kauffman bracket, can be derived from the quantum
(\mathfrak{sl}_{2},V_{2})
invariant, where
V_{2}
is the two dimensional fundamental representation of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
.

Given a ribbon Hopf algebra
(H,R,v)
and its finite-dimensional irreducible representation
(\pi,M)
, we modify the invariant
\mbox{Inv}_{H,\scriptsize\mbox{tr}}
to be

(4.5)

\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}(\mbox{L})=\chi_{M}(G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{L}_{1})}\mbox{w}(\mbox{L}_{1}))\cdots\chi_{M}(G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{L}_{r})}\mbox{w}(\mbox{L}_{r})),

where
G=u^{-1}v
and
\chi_{M}:=\mbox{Tr}\circ\pi
is just the character of the representation
(\pi,M)
, of course a trace-like function on
H
.

From
[
Rad1
]
, we know that for a given representation
\pi:H\rightarrow\mbox{End}(M)
, one can induce an oriented quantum algebra structure

\left(\mbox{End}(M),\,\pi\otimes\pi(R),\,\mbox{Id}_{M},\,\mbox{Int}_{\pi(u^{-1}v)}\right)

on
(\mbox{End}(M),\pi\otimes\pi(R))
, where
\mbox{Int}_{\pi(u^{-1}v)}
is the inner automorphism of
\mbox{End}(M)
defined by conjugating
\pi(u^{-1}v)
. For the TOQA

\left(\mbox{End}(M),\,\pi\otimes\pi(R),\,\mbox{Id}_{M},\,\mbox{Int}_{\pi(u^{-1}v)},\,\pi(u^{-1}v)\right),

we see that the invariant
\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}
is just
\mbox{Inv}_{\scriptsize\mbox{End}(M),\mbox{Tr}}
.

Meanwhile, this modification has its great benefit since one can normalize the regular invariant
\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}
to an ambient one. First, in order to distinguish knot types, we need the following result (see
[
Cro
, Thm. 3.8.4]
)

Two diagrams of an oriented link are regular isotopic to each other if and only if they have the same writhe
Wr
and Whitney degree
Wd
.

The
writhe

Wr
of an oriented link diagram is defined as the number of its positive crossings minus the number of its negative crossings. That is, the crossing
L_{-}
(resp.
L_{+}
) in (
4.2
) contributes
-1
(resp.
1
) to the writhe of the link.

Note that the parity of
\mbox{Wr}+\mbox{Wd}
is an ambient isotopy invariant. When two oriented knot diagrams portray the same knot type, i.e., they are
ambient isotopic
, one can add a certain number of
self-writhes
to one of them, such that they have the same writhe and Whitney degree.

Here adding self-writhes to an oriented knot diagram means adding a certain number of the following types of tangles to it.

\textstyle{\mbox{T}_{l,+}}
\textstyle{\mbox{T}_{l,-}}
\textstyle{\mbox{T}_{r,+}}
\textstyle{\mbox{T}_{r,-}}

The following important result is needed for us (see
[
RW
, Thm. 4.2 (a)]
).

Denote by
Curl
the collection of all 1-1 tangle diagrams constituted by the four types of oriented 1-1 tangles above. Then for any
\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Curl}
, the specialization of its formal product to
H
is

(4.6)

\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{T})=\mbox{h}^{\scriptsize-(\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})+\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T}))/2}u^{\scriptsize-\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})},

where
\mbox{h}=u^{-1}s(u)
. Note that for any
\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Curl}
,
\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})+\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T})
is always even.

Finally, we are in the position to do the
writhe-normalization
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Given a ribbon Hopf algebra
(H,R,v)
and its finite dimensional irreducible representation
(\pi,M)
, the TOQA structure

(H,\,R,\,\mbox{Id}_{H},\,s^{-2},\,u^{-1}v)

provides an ambient isotopy invariant of oriented knots (in fact even for oriented links)

(4.7)

\overline{\mbox{Inv}}_{H,M}=c^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wr}}\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}

with scalar
c\in k
given by the action of the ribbon element
v
on
M
.

Proof.

For any two knot diagrams
\mbox{K}_{1}
and
\mbox{K}_{2}
, which portray the same knot type, by adding a certain tangle diagram
\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Curl}
to
\mbox{K}_{2}
, one gets another knot diagram
\mbox{K}_{2}^{\prime}
regular isotopic to
\mbox{K}_{1}
, where
\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})=\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{1})-\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{2}),~\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T})=\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{1})-\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2})
.
Now
by the construction mentioned in Section 4, we have

\begin{split}G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{1})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{1})=G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2}^{\prime})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{2}^{\prime})&=G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{T})G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{2})\\
&=\left(\mbox{h}u^{2}\right)^{-\scriptsize(\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})+\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T}))/2}v^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{T})}G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{2})\\
&=v^{-\scriptsize\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{T})}G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{2})\\
&=v^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{2})-\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{1})}G^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wd}(\mbox{K}_{2})}\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{K}_{2}),\end{split}

where we use (
4.6
) and the fact that
G
and
\mbox{w}_{H}(\mbox{T})
,
\mbox{T}\in\mbox{Curl}
, commute.

By Schur’s lemma, the ribbon element
v
acts by multiplying a scalar
c\in k
. Hence,

\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}(\mbox{K}_{1})=c^{\scriptsize\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{2})-\mbox{Wr}(\mbox{K}_{1})}\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}(\mbox{K}_{2})

by (
4.5
), which also implies that
\overline{\mbox{Inv}}_{H,M}
defined by(
4.7
) is an ambient isotopy invariant.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

From
[
Rad3
, Thm. 4]
, we know that when turning to representations of the ribbon Hopf algebra
(H,R,v)
, the construction by bead-sliding has a close relation with the classical construction of quantum link invariants. In general, it coincides with the one constructed from a ribbon category, due to Reshetikhin-Turaev
[
RT1
]
. The regular invariant
\mbox{Inv}_{H,M}
serves as an invariant of oriented framed links, and when normalized by a “twist factor”, it becomes the one,
\overline{\mbox{Inv}}_{H,M}
, which does not depend on framing. One can also check the detail from
[
MPS
, Thm. 1.2]
.

We also note that the initial value
\overline{\mbox{Inv}}_{H,M}(\mbox{O})=\chi_{M}(u^{-1}v)
, for the unknot
O
, is just the usual
quantum dimension
of
(\pi,M)
.

5.
From the Drinfel’d double
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
to knot invariants

Now we are in the position to apply the construction introduced in the previous section to our target
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
.

5.1.
Representations of the Drinfel’d double
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})

Throughout the rest of paper, we will concentrate on the TOQA

\left(D(\bar{\mathscr{A}}),\,\mathcal{R},\,\mbox{Id}_{D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})},\,S^{2}\otimes s^{-2},\,u^{-1}v\right).

For convenience, we always assume that
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
is a ribbon Hopf algebra throughout the rest of this section, which requires
\ell
to be odd by Thm.
3.7
. In the later computation, when
q
is a primitive
\ell
-th root of unity, we see that
q^{(\ell\pm 1)i/2}=(-1)^{i}q^{\pm i/2},\,i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

From
[
Rad1
, Cor. 1]
, we see that for a special kind of graded Hopf algebras, the author had totally portrayed their simple Yetter-Drinfel’d modules. The description is the following.

Let
H=\bigoplus\nolimits_{i=0}^{\infty}H_{(i)}
be a (
\mathbb{Z}
-)graded Hopf algebra over an algebraically closed field
k
. Suppose that
H_{(0)}=kG
, where
G
is a finite abelian group,
H_{(0)}\simeq k\times\cdots\times k
as algebras, and
H_{(i)}=H_{(i+1)}=\cdots=0
for some
i>0
. Then

(\beta,g)\mapsto[H_{\beta,g}]

is a bijection between the Cartesian product of sets of group-like elements

G(H^{\circ})\times G=G(H^{\circ})\times G(H)
and the set of isomorphism classes of the simple Yetter-Drinfel’d
H
-modules, where
H^{\circ}
is the dual Hopf algebra of
H
.

Next we give the explicit definition of
H_{\beta,g}\in{{}_{H}\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{D}^{H}}
. First one can easily check that for any
\beta\in G(H^{\circ})
,
H_{\beta}=(H,\bullet_{\beta},\Delta)\in{{}_{H}\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{D}^{H}}
, where the module action
\bullet_{\beta}
is defined as

h\bullet_{\beta}a=(h_{(2)}\leftharpoonup\mathcal{K}(\beta))\,a\,\varsigmaup(h_{(1)}),\quad\forall\,h,a\in H.

Under such action, we know that
H_{\beta}
inherits the graded structure of
H
, and becomes a graded left
H
-module. Now take

H_{\beta,g}=H\bullet_{\beta}g,

then it is a simple Yetter-Drinfel’d
H
-submodule of
H_{\beta}
.

In the case when
H=\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
, take
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(i)}=\mbox{span}_{k}\bigl\{\,x^{\gamma}K(\alpha)\mid\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa},\,|\gamma|=i\,\bigr\}
, then

\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)=\bigoplus\nolimits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(i)}
is a graded Hopf algebra, with
the coradical
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(0)}=kG\left(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)\right)\cong k\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}^{n}
, by Proposition
3.1
, i.e.,
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
is pointed.
Moreover, the Jacobson radical of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
is

\bigoplus\nolimits_{i=1}^{\infty}\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(i)}
, with
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{(i)}=0
for all
i>n(\ell-1)
.

Since
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
is finite-dimensional, its dual is just
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)^{*}
.
Hence by
[
Rad1
, Cor. 1]
and Proposition
3.2
, we have

Theorem 5.1
.

Any left simple
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
-module has the form
\left(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)\right)_{\mathcal{K}(\alpha),K(\beta)}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}
, simply denoted by
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Let us start to study these simple left
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
-modules.

First, we should clarify the module action of
\left(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{\alpha,\beta},\cdot\right)
as a left
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
-module:

(5.1)

\begin{split}x^{\gamma}K(\xi)\bullet_{\mathcal{K}(\alpha)}K(\beta)&=\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\gamma}}{\gamma\choose\zeta}q^{-(\gamma-\zeta)*\zeta}\left(x^{\zeta}K(\xi)\leftharpoonup\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\right)K(\beta)\,s^{-1}\left(x^{\gamma-\zeta}K(\zeta+\xi)\right)\\
&=\mu(\gamma,\xi:\alpha,\beta)x^{\gamma}K(\beta-\gamma),\end{split}

where we set

\mu(\gamma,\xi:\alpha,\beta)=\sum\limits_{\zeta\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\gamma}}(-1)^{|\gamma-\zeta|}{\gamma\choose\zeta}q^{\tfrac{1}{2}\langle 2\beta-\gamma-\zeta+\boldsymbol{1},\gamma-\zeta\rangle+\langle\alpha,\xi+\zeta\rangle}\theta(\beta+\gamma-\zeta,\gamma-\zeta).

In addition,

(5.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{K}(\omega)\rightharpoonup x^{\eta}K(\beta-\eta)&=\sum\limits_{\xi\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\eta}}{\eta\choose\xi}q^{-(\eta-\xi)*\xi}\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{K}(\omega)\left(x^{\xi}K(\beta-\eta)\right)x^{\eta-\xi}K(\xi+\beta-\eta)\\
&={\eta\choose\gamma}q^{-(\eta-\gamma)*\gamma+\langle\omega,\beta-\eta\rangle}x^{\eta-\gamma}K(\beta+\gamma-\eta).\end{split}

Note that in Eq. (
5.2
), if
\gamma\nleq\eta
,
\displaystyle{{\eta\choose\gamma}=0}
, so the corresponding result should be interpreted as
0
.

Combining Eq. (
5.1
) with Eq. (
5.2
), we obtain the formula of the module action of
(\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{\alpha,\beta},\cdot)
as a left
D(\bar{\mathscr{A}})
-module

(5.3)

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{K}(\omega)\otimes x^{\eta}K(\xi)\right)\cdot K(\beta)&=\mu(\eta,\xi:\alpha,\beta)\left(\mathcal{X}^{\gamma}\mathcal{K}(\omega)\rightharpoonup x^{\eta}K(\beta-\eta)\right)\\
&={\eta\choose\gamma}q^{-(\eta-\gamma)*\gamma+\langle\omega,\beta-\eta\rangle}\mu(\eta,\xi:\alpha,\beta)x^{\eta-\gamma}K(\beta+\gamma-\eta),\end{split}

where
\gamma,\eta,\omega,\xi\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}
.

From Eq. (
5.1
), we can see that
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{\alpha,\beta}
lies in the subspace of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{q}(n)
expanded by
\bigl\{\,x^{\gamma}K(\beta-\gamma)\mid\gamma\in\Lambda_{+}^{\leq\kappa}\,\bigr\}
. In order to find a
k
-basis of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{\alpha,\beta}
, the key is to confirm when the coefficient
\mu(\gamma,\xi:\alpha,\beta)
does not vanish.

To this end, we consider

\begin{split}x_{i}\bullet_{\mathcal{K}(\alpha)}x^{\gamma}K(\beta-\gamma)&=\left(x_{i}\leftharpoonup\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\right)x^{\gamma}K(\beta-\gamma)K(\epsilon_{i})^{-1}\\
&\quad+\left(1\leftharpoonup\mathcal{K}(\alpha)\right)x^{\gamma}K(\beta-\gamma)\left(-x_{i}K(\epsilon_{i})^{-1}\right)\\
&=q^{\epsilon_{i}*\gamma}\left(q^{\langle\alpha,\epsilon_{i}\rangle}-\theta(\beta,\epsilon_{i})q^{\langle\beta-\gamma,\epsilon_{i}\rangle}\right)x^{\gamma+\epsilon_{i}}K(\beta-\gamma-\epsilon_{i}).\end{split}

Now let
