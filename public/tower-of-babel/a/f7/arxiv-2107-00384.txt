A variational non-linear constrained model for the inversion of FDEM data
A Buccini 1 and P Díaz de Alba 2

Abstract
Reconstructing the structure of the soil using non-invasive techniques is a very relevant problem in many scientific fields, like geophysics and archaeology. This can be done, for instance, with the aid of Frequency Domain Electromagnetic (FDEM) induction devices. Inverting FDEM data is a very challenging inverse problem, as the problem is extremely ill-posed, i.e., sensible to the presence of noise in the measured data, and non-linear. Regularization methods substitute the original ill-posed problem with a well-posed one whose solution is an accurate approximation of the desired one. In this paper we develop a regularization method to invert FDEM data. We propose to determine the electrical conductivity of the ground by solving a variational problem. The minimized functional is made up by the sum of two term: the data fitting term ensures that the recovered solution fits the measured data, while the regularization term enforces sparsity on the Laplacian of the solution. The trade-off between the two terms is determined by the regularization parameter. This is achieved by minimizing an ℓ 2 − ℓ q \ell_{2}-\ell_{q} functional with 0 < q ≤ 2 0<q\leq 2 . Since the functional we wish to minimize is non-convex, we show that the variational problem admits a solution. Moreover, we prove that, if the regularization parameter is tuned accordingly to the amount of noise present in the data, this model induces a regularization method. Some selected numerical examples on synthetic and real data show the good performances of our proposal.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider a severely ill-posed problem that arises in geophysics, namely the reconstruction of the electrical conductivity of the ground using a Frequency Domain Electromagnetic (FDEM) induction device. In this work we consider a two-dimensional vertical section of the ground and assume the magnetic permeability to be known and constant. The extension to the three-dimensional case and unknown magnetic permeability is straightforward and, for simplicity, we do not consider it here. The considered problem is of the form

(1)

\arg\min_{\Sigma\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)-\mathcal{B}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},

where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are Hilbert spaces,
\Sigma\in\mathcal{X}
represents the electrical conductivity,
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{Y}
collects the (possibly noisy) data measured by the FDEM device,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the norm defined on
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is a non-linear function. We assume that (
1
) is ill-posed, i.e., the solution may not be unique and its computation is very sensible to the presence of noise in the measured data. Therefore, the naive solution of (
1
) is usually a poor approximation of the exact electrical conductivity. To compute a meaningful solution we need to resort to regularization methods; see, e.g.,
[
18
]
and references therein for a more detailed discussion.

We represent the two-dimensional ground as the semi-infinite rectangle
[a,b]\times[0,\infty)
, where the first dimension is the horizontal space dimension (intuitively the “sea-level”) and the second dimension represents the depth. Our purpose is to reconstruct an “image” of the electrical conductivity on this rectangle. This can be done using a FDEM induction device called Ground Conductivity Meter (GCM). Its principle of operation is based on an alternating electrical current which flows through a small electric wire coil (the transmitter). A second coil (the receiver) is positioned at a fixed distance from the first one, and the two coil axes may be aligned either vertically or horizontally with respect to the subsurface. The transmitting coil generates an electromagnetic (EM) field above the surface of the ground, a portion of which propagates into it.
This EM field, called the primary field
H_{P}
, induces an alternating electrical current within the ground, generating in turn a secondary EM field
H_{S}
, which propagates back to the surface and the air above. The second wire coil acts as a receiver, measuring the amplitude and phase components of the ratio between the primary and secondary EM fields. The complex measurements obtained by a GCM depend on some instrument settings, like the orientation of the dipoles, the frequency of the alternating current, the inter-coil distance, and the height of the instrument above the ground.

We provide below a brief discussion on the functional
\mathcal{M}
presented in (
1
). As we will see, the described model is one-dimensional, i.e., the measured data depends exclusively on the electrical conductivity below the measuring point.

We assume that
N
equispaced measurement sets
\mathbf{b}_{j}
are performed in some interval
[a,b]
(in our experiments we set
a=0
m and
b=10
m). Each set is obtained by collecting several measurements using different configurations of the FDEM device. Denoting a sampling of the electrical conductivity of the ground by

\mathbf{\Sigma}=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}],

we can discretize the minimization problem (
1
) as follows

(2)

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})-\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
is the Euclidean vector norm,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times N}
,
\mathbf{B}=[\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{N}]\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times N}
, and the vector function
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=[M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}),\ldots,M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})]\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times N}
returns the readings predicted by the model in the same order they were arranged in the vector
\mathbf{b}_{j}
. This can be done because the model is one-dimensional, i.e., the values of the
j
th column of
\mathbf{B}
depend only on the entries of the
j
th column of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

Therefore, once discretized, the two-dimensional problem is reduced to
N
independent one-dimensional problems.

Although this may seem beneficial, since this decoupling can be greatly useful in lowering the computational complexity of the problem at hand, this is not completely true. A natural approach for solving the two-dimensional problem would be to solve each one-dimensional problem independently and then “stack” the obtained solutions in order to get a two-dimensional image; see
[
14
]
. However, as we show in our numerical examples, in practice this approach may lead to poor reconstructions. In fact, the problem is severely ill-posed and in real-world applications the measured data is affected by noise, thus the obtained solution can deviate (also substantially) from the exact
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}
and this results in the stacked image to be “spliced”. It is the purpose of this paper to propose a solution method that couples the
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}
in order to avoid this “splicing” and to counteract the ill-posedness of the problem. To this end, we consider the following variational problem

(3)

\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}\geq 0}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm,
\gamma>0
, and
0<q\leq 2
. With the notation
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
we denote the vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{nN}
defined by

\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=D(\mbox{{vec}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})),

with

{D}={L}\otimes{I}+{I}\otimes{L}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,
{I}
is the identity matrix,
vec
is the vectorization operator, and

{L}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2&-1\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-1&2&-1\\
&&&-1&2\end{bmatrix}.

The operator
\mathbf{D}
(or equivalently the matrix
D
) is a discretization of the Laplacian, thus we are considering a high order version of so-called Total Variation regularization
[
29
]
. When
q=2
the model (
3
) reduces to the well-known Tikhonov minimization for non-linear problems, here with the addition of the non-negativity constraint; see, e.g.,
[
30
]
for a first discussion on this kind of problems,
[
18
,
22
]
for a recent discussion on Tikhonov method and
[
1
]
for an algorithm for the solution of the non-negatively constrained Tikhonov minimization for linear problems. When
1<q<2
, the term
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q}
is convex and smooth, while for
q=1
it is convex, but non-smooth. For
0<q<1
, we define
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}^{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{j}|^{q}
and we still refer to this quantity as to
\ell_{q}
-norm, however, this is not a norm since it does not satisfy the triangular inequality. Therefore, if
0<q<1
, the second term in (
3
) is non-convex. Nevertheless, as shown in
[
25
,
8
,
5
,
7
,
9
,
10
]
, in imaging application it is beneficial to select
0<q<1
, this is due to the fact that, in this case, the
\ell_{q}
-norm approximates the
\ell_{0}
-norm, thus leading to reconstructions with sparse gradients. This property is desirable in an approximate reconstruction.

Finally, observe that, since we have used zero boundary conditions for the discretization of the Laplacian, it holds that
\mbox{{rank}}({D})=nN
. Moreover, note that, since we know that the electrical conductivity is positive, we imposed the non-negativity constraint.

The main contributions of this paper are the following. Firstly, we describe a new variational model for computing an approximate solution of (
1
). The novelty of the proposed model is the horizontal coupling of the columns of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
that allows us to obtain very accurate reconstructions of the electrical conductivity of the ground that are not affected by the “splicing” issue described above. To the best of our knowledge, this coupling has never been considered before in the literature for this kind of problem. Secondly, we prove the regularization properties of
\ell^{2}-\ell^{q}
minimization for non-linear problems. This properties has been shown firstly in
[
7
]
for linear problems and we extend this analysis to the non-linear case. Finally, we propose an algorithm for the solution of the problem (
3
) that extends to the non-linear case the one proposed in
[
25
]
that was constructed for linear problems.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
describes the functional
\mathcal{M}
and its discretization, in Section
3
we show the theoretical properties of (
3
). Section
4
provides an algorithm for the computation of an approximate solution of (
3
), in Section
5
we show some numerical examples to demonstrate the performances of the proposed approach, and we draw some conclusions and outline future research in Section
6
.

2
Modelization of the problem

We now briefly describe how the FDEM device can be modeled. The main non-linear forward model which describes the interaction between the soil and the FDEM induction device when the electrical conductivity and the magentic permeability are known, has been described in
[
23
]
.

In this model, the soil is assumed to have
n
layers which are charaterized by an electrical conductivity
\sigma_{i}
(measured in S/m) and a magnetic permeability
\mu_{i}
(measured in H/m), for
i=1,\ldots,n
; see
[
15
,
12
]
for more details. The thickness of each layer, measured in meters, is denoted by
d_{i}
, considering infinite the thikness of the deepest layer
d_{n}
. Finally, the distance between the coils is represented by
\rho
and the height at which the measurements are taken by
h
.

Let us now consider the propagation constant
u_{i}(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+{\mathrm{i}}\sigma_{i}\mu_{i}\omega}
, where
{\mathrm{i}}
denotes the imaginary unit,
{\rm Re}(u_{i}(\lambda))\geq 0
, and
\omega
is the angular frequency of the instrument, that is,
2\pi
times the frequency in Hertz. The variable
\lambda
is the variable of integration which ranges from zero to infinity measuring the ratio between the depth in meters below the ground surface and the inter-coil distance
\rho
.

The surface admittance
Y_{i}(\lambda)
at the top of each layer verifies the
recursion

(4)

Y_{i}(\lambda)=N_{i}(\lambda)\frac{Y_{i+1}(\lambda)+N_{i}(\lambda)\tanh(d_{i}u_{i}(\lambda))}{N_{i}(\lambda)+Y_{i+1}(\lambda)\tanh(d_{i}u_{i}(\lambda))},

for
i=n-1,\ldots,1
, where
N_{i}(\lambda)=u_{i}(\lambda)/({\mathrm{i}}\mu_{i}\omega)
represents the characteristic admittance at the
i
-th layer; see
[
31
]
. Note that this formulation of
Y_{i}
is not computationally stable and to solve practical problems different formulations have to be considered. However, our purpose here is to describe the model and we do not dwell further on the computational issues of the forward model. We refer the interested reader to
[
11
]
for a discussion on the numerical implementation of the forward model. At the last layer, i.e., when
i=n
, the characteristic admittance and the surface admittance coincide being
Y_{n}(\lambda)=N_{n}(\lambda)
used to initialize the recursion (
4
).
We remark here, that both the characteristic and the surface admittances are functions
of the frequency
\omega
via the functions
u_{i}(\lambda)
.

Now, the ratio of the secondary to the primary field for the vertical (
\nu=0
) and horizontal (
\nu=1
) orientation of the coils are given by

(5)

M_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\mu};h,\omega,\rho)=-\rho^{3-\nu}\mathcal{H}_{\nu}\left[\lambda^{1-\nu}e^{-2h\lambda}R_{\omega,0}(\lambda)\right](\rho),\qquad\nu=0,1,

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})^{T}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})^{T}
,

N_{0}(\lambda)=\lambda/({\mathrm{i}}\mu_{0}\omega)
,
\mu_{0}=4\pi\cdot 10^{-7}
H/m is the
magnetic permeability of free space. The reflection factor
R_{\omega,0}(\lambda)
is defined by

R_{\omega,0}(\lambda)=\frac{N_{0}(\lambda)-Y_{1}(\lambda)}{N_{0}(\lambda)+Y_{1}(\lambda)},

with
Y_{1}(\lambda)
computed by the recursion (
4
), and

\mathcal{H}_{\nu}[f](\rho)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\lambda)J_{\nu}(\rho\lambda)\lambda\,d\lambda,\qquad\nu=0,1,

is the Hankel transform, where
J_{0},J_{1}
are first kind Bessel functions of order
0 and 1, respectively.

Note that functions in (
5
) are complex valued functions. The imaginary part or quadrature component of the field ratio is usually interpreted as the apparent conductivity of the soil, while the magnetic permeability is related to the real or in-phase component.

Simultaneous measurements with different inter-coil distances or different operating frequencies can be recorded by recent FDEM devices at different heights.
We denote by
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m_{\rho}})^{T}
,
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m_{h}})^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m_{\omega}})^{T}
, the vectors containing the
loop-loop distances, the heights, and the angular frequencies at which the readings were taken. We consider the corresponding
m=2m_{\rho}m_{h}m_{\omega}
data points
b^{\nu}_{tls}
, where
t=1,\ldots,m_{\rho}
,
l=1,\ldots,m_{h}
,
s=1,\ldots,m_{\omega}
, while
\nu\in\{0,1\}
represents the vertical and horizontal orientations of the coils, respectively.
The observations
b^{\nu}_{tls}
are rearranged in a vector
\mathbf{b}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
.

In various papers this non-linear model has been studied for different device configurations and different techniques were applied; see, e.g.,
[
23
,
15
,
17
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. An algorithm for the regularized inversion of this model has been implemented in a Matlab package which includes a graphical user interface in
[
11
]
.

We remark that, for small values of the conductivity of the soil, a linear model has been introduced in
[
26
]
and has been solved first in
[
3
]
and later in
[
16
]
from the theoretical point of view, where an optimized solution method has been proposed.

In the following, it is assumed that the contribution of the permeability
distribution to the overall response is negligible, i.e.
\mu=\mu_{0}
, so that the measurements are considered to be sensitive merely to conductivity values. However, in principle, the regularization approach discussed here can be easily extended
to include also the inversion for the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
components; see
[
12
]
.

3
Regularization property

In this section we consider the model (
3
) in the presence of noise, that is

(6)

\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}\geq 0}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q},

where
\delta
denotes the noise present in the data, i.e.,

\left\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta.

We would like to show that the model (
6
) induces a regularization method. Namely, if
\gamma
is chosen depending on the amount of noise that corrupts the data, then the solutions of (
6
) converge to a minimum norm solution of the noise free problem (
3
) as
\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
or, equivalently,
\delta\rightarrow 0
. Denote by
\mathcal{J}
the functional minimized in (
6
), i.e.,

(7)

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}),

where
\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
is the indicator function of the non-negative cone, namely

\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if }\mathbf{\Sigma}\geq 0,\\
\infty&\mbox{else.}\end{array}\right.

Before showing the regularization property we need to first prove some auxiliary results. The analysis of the model can be derived from the ones in
[
7
,
24
]
, however, since some proofs are different, we report it here for the convenience of the reader.

We first recall the following result.

Lemma 1

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of elements of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and let
q>0
. If the
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|_{q}^{q}
are uniformly bounded, i.e., if there exists a constant
c>0
independent of
j
such that

\left\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|_{q}^{q}\leq c\quad\forall j\in{\mathbb{N}},

then
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is uniformly bounded.

We can now show our first preliminary result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be defined in (
7
), then
\mathcal{J}
admits a global minimizer.

Proof 3
.

By definition
\mathcal{J}
is lower semi-continuous and proper. Moreover, since
\mbox{{rank}}(D)=nN
, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{J}
is coercive. In fact, assume that the sequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is such that
\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\right\|_{F}^{2}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, then, since
\mbox{{rank}}(D)=nN
,
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently,
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{q}^{q}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
and thus
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.

Since
\mathcal{J}
is proper, there exists
\mathbf{\Sigma}
such that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma})<\infty
and we can define

\varphi=\inf_{\mathbf{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times N}}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}).

By lower semi-continuity of
\mathcal{J}
, there exists a sequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
M\geq 0
such that

(8)

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\rightarrow\varphi\;\mbox{as}\;j\rightarrow\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\leq M\;\forall j\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In particular,
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{q}^{q}\leq M
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and, thanks to Lemma
1
, there exists
\widetilde{M}
such that
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\widetilde{M}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since
\mbox{{rank}}({D})=nN
, the sequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is uniformly bounded and, thus, it admits a convergent subsequence denoted by
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}}\}_{j_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}}\rightarrow\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
as
j_{k}\rightarrow\infty
. We would like to show that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
. The definition of
\varphi
yields

\varphi\leq\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})=\lim_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})=\varphi,

where the second to last equality follows from the lower semi-continuity of
\mathcal{J}
and the last one follows from (
8
). This shows that
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})=\varphi
, i.e., that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
which concludes the proof.

We are now in position to show our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
0<q\leq 2
be fixed and
\{\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence such that, for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\left\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{F}\leq\delta_{j}
.
Assume that
\delta_{j}\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Let
\{\gamma_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that

\gamma_{j}\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\delta^{2}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad j\rightarrow\infty.

Denote by
\mathcal{J}_{j}
the functional

\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{j}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma})

and, for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma}).

Then there exists a convergent subsequence of
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, denoted by
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}}\}_{j_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, such that

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}}\rightarrow\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\quad\mbox{as}\quad j_{k}\rightarrow\infty

and

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\in\arg\min\{\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q}:\;\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\mathbf{B},\;\mathbf{\Sigma}\geq 0\},

assuming that this set is not empty.

Proof 4
.

First let us observe that, thanks to Proposition
2
, the sequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is well defined.

Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{J}_{j}
, we have that for all
\mathbf{\Sigma}
it holds

\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\leq\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma}).

In particular, let
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\in\arg\min\{\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right\|_{q}^{q}:\;\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\mathbf{B}\}
, then

\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\leq\mathcal{J}_{j}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger}).

Recalling that
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})=\mathbf{B}
and that
\left\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{F}\leq\delta_{j}
, for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have that there exists
j_{0}
such that, for all
j>j_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{j}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{j}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{j}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{j}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq C,

where
C
is a constant independent of
j
, where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\delta_{j},\gamma_{j}\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, and we observed that
\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})=\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})=0
. In particular, we have that, for all
j>j_{0}
,
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right\|_{q}^{q}\leq C
. Thanks to
\mbox{{rank}}({D})=nN
and Lemma
1
, we have that
\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\right\|_{F}\leq\widetilde{C}
for a certain constant
\widetilde{C}
. Since the sequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is uniformly bounded (for
j>j_{0}
), it admits a converging subsequence
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}}\}_{j_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and let
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
denote its limit. We first show that
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})=\mathbf{B}
.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})-\mathbf{B}\right\|^{2}_{F}\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j_{k}}}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j_{k}}}\right\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{\gamma_{j_{k}}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{j_{k}}}{2}+\frac{\gamma_{j_{k}}}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})=0,

i.e.,
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})=\mathbf{B}
. We now show that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
minimizes
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})\right\|_{q}^{q}
. Recall that
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}
is minimum, thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})\right\|_{q}^{q}\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\gamma_{j_{k}}}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j_{k}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma_{j_{k}}}\mathcal{J}_{j_{k}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma_{j_{k}}}\mathcal{J}_{j_{k}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\gamma_{j_{k}}}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta_{j_{k}}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j_{k}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{j_{k}}^{2}}{2\gamma_{j_{k}}}+\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})=\frac{1}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}.

We have shown that
\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})\right\|_{q}^{q}=\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\dagger})\right\|_{q}^{q}
. Moreover, since the non-negative cone is a closed set it is obvious that
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\geq 0
, this coupled with the fact that
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*})=\mathbf{B}
, concludes the proof.

4
Minimizing algorithm

We wish now to discuss how to compute a solution of (
6
). Consider the minimization problem

(9)

\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Xi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Xi})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Xi}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma}),

where
\beta>0
is a fixed parameter. Obviously, if
\beta
is large enough, the solutions of (
6
) and (
9
) are the same. This reformulation is commonly performed in optimization so that the alternating minimization algorithm can be used. By applying alternating minimization to solve (
9
), we obtain the following iterations

(10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})-\mathbf{B}^{\delta}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k)}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\\
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Xi}}\frac{\gamma}{q}\left\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{\Xi})\right\|_{q}^{q}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{\Xi}\right\|_{F}^{2}.\end{array}\right.

The convergence of the iterations in (
10
) is guaranteed by the results in
[
21
]
. In particular, it holds the following

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k)}
denote the iterates defined in (
10
), then

(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k)},\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k)})\rightarrow(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*},\mathbf{\Xi}^{*})\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\rightarrow\infty,

where
(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*},\mathbf{\Xi}^{*})
is a stationary point of the functional in (
9
) that depends on the initial guess
\mathbf{\Xi}^{(0)}
. Moreover, if
\beta
is large enough (possibly
\beta=\infty
),
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathbf{\Xi}^{*}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
is a stationary point of the functional in (
6
) that depends on
\mathbf{\Xi}^{(0)}
.

Remark.
Note that, since the minimized functional in (
9
) is non-convex it might have multiple global and local minima. Moreover, it may have several saddle points. However, in general, determining the global minimum of a non-convex function is a NP-hard problem. Therefore, in optimization theory, is usually considered a good enough result determining a stationary point of the minimized functional. Indeed, our numerical results show that the proposed approach is able to provide accurate reconstructions.

4.1
Implementation details

In the following, we detail how to numerically solve the two minimization subproblems in (
10
) at each iteration.

For the solution of the
\mathbf{\Sigma}
subproblem in (
10
) let us first observe that we can write

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=[M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}),\ldots,M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})],

where
M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})
is a column vector. We can then rewrite the subproblem as

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})-\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})\right],

where
\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j}
denote the
j
th column of
\mathbf{B}^{\delta}
and
\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k)}
, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}
is the
j
th column of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Thus, we can write

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|M(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\quad j=1,\ldots,N,

i.e., the
\mathbf{\Sigma}
subproblem decouples in
N
independent one-dimensional subproblems. This problem can be rewritten as

(11)

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}M(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\\
\sqrt{\beta}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\\
\sqrt{\beta}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\quad j=1,\ldots,N.

Observe that every
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1)}
subproblems are independent and thus can be solved in parallel. For the solution of the
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1)}
, we consider a slight modification of the algorithm proposed in
[
15
,
17
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
11
]
. Here we outline the algorithm and describe the modifications we made.

Let us first consider the following non-linear least-squares problem for a single column
M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}
of
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
and
\mathbf{B}^{\delta}
, respectively, i.e.

(12)

\min_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\iota_{0}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}).

We solve problem (
12
) by the Guass–Newton method.

We denote by
\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})={M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-\mathbf{b}^{\delta}_{j}
the complex residual vector as a function of the conductivity
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. At each step of the iterative algorithm we minimize the 2-norm of a linear approximation of the residual and compute
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l+1)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}+\mathbf{q}^{(l)}
, where

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}^{(l)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{q}}\left\|\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})+J(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2},

and
J(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})
is the Jacobian matrix of the function
\mathbf{r}
computed in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}
.

Being the residual function
\mathbf{r}
complex-valued, we solve
problem (
13
) by stacking the real and imaginary part of the
residual as follows (see
[
14
]
)

\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{Re}(\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\\
\mbox{Im}(\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m},\quad\widetilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{Re}(J(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\\
\mbox{Im}(J(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2m\times n}.

In the same way we set
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}
and
\widetilde{J}
, we rearrange the vectors
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\delta}
, and we denote them by
\mathbf{\widetilde{M}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{b}^{\delta}}
, respectively. So, we replace (
13
) by

(14)

\min_{\mathbf{q}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})+\widetilde{J}_{l}\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2},

with
\widetilde{J}_{l}=\widetilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})
,
and the iterative method becomes

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l+1)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}-\alpha_{l}\widetilde{J}_{l}^{\dagger}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}),

where
\widetilde{J}_{l}^{\dagger}
is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
\widetilde{J}_{l}
and

\alpha_{l}
is a damping parameter which ensures the convergence.
This parameter is determined by coupling the Armijo–Goldstein principle
[
2
]
to
the positivity constraint
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l+1)}\geq 0
(see
[
12
,
15
]
) so that

(15)

\left\|\mathbf{\widetilde{r}}(\mathbf{\sigma}^{(l)})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{\widetilde{r}}(\mathbf{\sigma}^{(l)}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)})\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{l}\left\|\widetilde{J}_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l+1)}\geq 0

is verified. The analytical expression of the Jacobian matrices with respect to the electrical conductivity and the magnetic permeability were computed in
[
15
]
and
[
12
]
, respectively, where it has also been proved that the computation of the analitical expression of the Jacobian matrices is faster than their finite difference approximations.

It is well known that the minimization problem (
14
) is
extremely ill-posed, meaning that the matrix
\widetilde{J}_{l}
is severely ill-conditioned. In order to overcome this difficulty, we apply the generalized truncated singular value decomposition (GTSVD), as in
[
15
,
17
,
12
,
14
,
11
]
, for stably computing an approximation of
\widetilde{J}_{l}^{\dagger}
. We introduce
a regularization matrix
\widehat{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 2n}
(
p\leq 2n
), whose null space approximately contains the sought solution
[
28
]
. Under the assumption
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{J}_{l})\cap\mathcal{N}(\widehat{L})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
problem (
14
) is replaced by

(16)

\min_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{S}}\left\|L\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{S}=\{\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\,:\,\widetilde{J}_{l}^{T}\widetilde{J}_{l}\mathbf{q}=-\widetilde{J}_{l}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})\}.

Note that, thanks to the assumption above on the null spaces of
\widetilde{J}_{l}
and
\widehat{L}
, the solution of (
16
) is unique. Very common choices for
L
are the discretization of the first or
second derivative operators.

Let the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD)
[
20
]
of the
matrix pair
(\widetilde{J}_{l},\widehat{L})
be

\widetilde{J}_{l}=U\Sigma_{J}Z^{-1},\qquad\widehat{L}=V\Sigma_{L}Z^{-1},

where
U
and
V
are matrices with orthonormal columns
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, respectively,
Z
is a non-singular matrix with columns
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, and
\Sigma_{J}
,
\Sigma_{L}
are diagonal matrices with diagonal entries
c_{i}
and
s_{i}
, which are the singular values of
\widetilde{J}
and
\widehat{L}
, respectively.
Under the assumption that
m=2m_{\rho}m_{h}m_{\omega}<2n
, common in the generality of cases, the truncated GSVD (TGSVD) solution
\mathbf{q}^{(l)}
(see
[
22
]
for details) can be written as

(17)

\mathbf{q}^{(l)}_{\rm GSVD}=-\sum_{i=p-\ell+1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)}}{c_{i-2n+\kappa}}\,\mathbf{z}_{i}-\sum_{i=p+1}^{2n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)})\,\mathbf{z}_{i},

where
\ell
is fixed,
\kappa=\mathop{\operator@font rank}\nolimits(\widetilde{J}_{l})
,
\ell=1,\ldots,\kappa+p-2n

is the regularization parameter, and
\mathbf{r}^{(l)}=\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)})
.

The resulting regularized damped Gauss–Newton method reads

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l+1)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(l)}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)}_{\rm GSVD},

with
\ell
fixed and
\alpha_{l}
determined at each step as in (
15
).

We now apply this algorithm to our specific case. In particular, we have to solve (
11
). We apply the previously described procedure to the following function getting

(18)

\widehat{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\\
\sqrt{\beta}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{bmatrix},\quad\widehat{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\\
\sqrt{\beta}I\end{bmatrix},

where
I
is the identity matrix. We remind here that
\widehat{M}
is not a complex valued function anymore since we already stacked the real and imaginary parts of
M
and
J
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)}
be an approximation of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1)}
. Denote by
\widehat{J}_{l}
the Jacobian of
\widehat{M}
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)}
and consider the GSVD of the pair
\{\widehat{J}_{l},\widehat{L}\}

\widehat{J}_{l}=\widehat{U}\widehat{\Sigma}_{J}\widehat{Z}^{-1},\qquad\widehat{L}=\widehat{V}\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\widehat{Z}^{-1}.

Let

\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)}=\widehat{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)})-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}M(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)})-\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\delta}\\
\sqrt{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}_{j})\end{bmatrix},

then we compute, analogously to (

\mathbf{q}^{(l)}_{\rm GSVD}=-\sum_{i=p-\ell+1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)}}{c_{i-2n+\kappa}}\,\mathbf{z}_{i}-\sum_{i=p+1}^{2n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)})\,\mathbf{z}_{i},

leading to the iteration

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k+1,l)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{(k,l)}+\alpha_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)}_{\rm GSVD},

where
\alpha_{l}
is determined by the Armijo-Goldstein rule so that

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{(l)}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{(l+1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{l}\left\|\widehat{J}_{l}\mathbf{q}^{(l)}_{\rm GSVD}\right\|_{2}^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k,l+1)}\geq 0.

We now move to the
\mathbf{\Xi}
subproblem. To solve this problem we consider the majorization-minimization algorithm proposed by Huang et al. in
[

and furtherly developed in
[

[
25

. To simplify the computations we consider a modified version of the
\mathbf{D}
operator, namely we impose reflexive boundary conditions to the discretization of the Laplacian. In this way the obtained matrix has an exploitable structure that helps in the computations. In detail, with abuse of notation, we write

{L}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&2&-1\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-1&2&-1\\
&&&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

Thanks to the structure of
L
the matrix
D
, which is defined by
D=L\otimes I+I\otimes L
, is the sum of a block Hankel with Hankel blocks matrix, a block Toeplitz with Hankel blocks matrix, a block Hankel with Toeplitz blocks matrix, and a block Toeplitz with Toeplitz blocks matrix. We recall that Toeplitz matrices are matrices that are constant on the diagonals and Hankel matrices are matrices that are constant on the anti-diagonals. Since
[-1\;2\;-1]
is symmetric, if
{C}
denotes the discrete cosine transform matrix, we have that

(19)

{D}={C}^{T}{\Lambda}{C},

where
{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix. The diagonal elements of
{\Lambda}
are computed as the cosine coefficients of the first column of
{D}
; see
[
27
]
for more details.

We now describe the Majorization-Minimization (MM) procedure that we use for solving the
\mathbf{\Xi}
subproblem. Let us first rewrite the minimization problem in (
10

(20)

\mbox{{vec}}(\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k+1)})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q\beta}\left\|{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{q}^{q},

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}=\mbox{{vec}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(k+1)})
. The MM algorithm generates a sequence of vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)}
that converges to an approximate solution of (
20
). Firstly, if
q\leq 1
, we need to smooth the
q
-norm so that it is differentiable. Let
\varepsilon>0
be a small constant, then, for
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it holds

\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}^{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{j}|^{q}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{j}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{q/2}=:\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q,\varepsilon}^{q}.

Note that the function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q,\varepsilon}^{q}
is differentiable everywhere. We consider the smoothed problem

(21)

\mbox{{vec}}(\mathbf{\Xi}^{(k+1)})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma}{q\beta}\left\|{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{q,\varepsilon}^{q}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

As pointed out in
[
8
]
, the solutions of (
20

21

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)}
be an approximate solution of (
21

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}
in
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)}
that majorizes it, i.e., a quadratic function
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})
such that

•

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})\geq\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}
;

•

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})=\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})
;

•

\nabla\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})=\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})
.

In
[
25
]
the authors provide two different choices for the construction of such a functional, here we considered the so-called fixed approach. Denote by
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(l)}=D\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)}

as

\mathbf{u}^{(l)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(l)}\left(1-\left(\frac{(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(l)})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{q/2-1}\right),

where all the operations are meant element-wise. It is possible to see that the function

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma\varepsilon^{q-2}}{2\beta}\left(\left\|{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{u}^{(l)},{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right)+c,

where
c
is a constant independent of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, is a quadratic tangent majorant; see
[
25
]
for a derivation. An improved approximation of the solution of (
21

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l)})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l+1)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\xi}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma\varepsilon^{q-2}}{2\beta}\left(\left\|D\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle\mathbf{u}^{(l)},{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}\\
\sqrt{\eta}{D}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}-\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}}\\
\sqrt{\eta}\mathbf{u}^{(l)}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\eta=\frac{\gamma\varepsilon^{q-2}}{\beta}
. Writing the normal equation yields

({I}+\eta{D}^{T}{D})\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l+1)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}+\eta D^{T}\mathbf{u}^{(l)}.

Using the factorization (

{C}^{T}\left({I}+\eta{\Lambda}^{T}{\Lambda}T\right){C}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k,l+1)}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(k+1)}+\eta{D}^{T}\mathbf{u}^{(l)}.

Thus, we can compute

Numerical examples

In the numerical tests illustrated in this section we consider both synthetic and experimental data. The two-dimensional representation of the electrical conductivity is determined under the assumption that the magnetic permeability is the one of the free space, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\equiv\mu_{0}
.

Throughout this section, we show the effectiveness of our method and we compare the results obtained from the algorithm presented in this paper with those obtained by applying the method described in
[
14
]
, i.e., by solving each one-dimensional problem independently and then “stacking” the obtained solutions.

All the computations are performed on an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6136 CPU @ 3.00GHz computer with 128Gb of RAM memory and 32 cores, running the Debian GNU/Linux operating system and Matlab R2020b.

5.1
Synthetic data

In the first test we consider two different configurations of the FDEM device. We show that the proposed algorithm is able to reconstruct an accurate approximation of the electrical conductivity for both devices. In out second test we report the solution obtained by varying the dimensions of the problem discretization for one configuration.

We generate the data matrix
\mathbf{B}_{\rm{exact}}
of a chosen dimension
m\times N
and we simulate the presence of noise in the data, by letting

\mathbf{e}=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{m}}\left\|\mathbf{B}_{\rm{exact}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\mathbf{w},

where
\mathbf{w}
is a vector with normally distributed entries having zero mean and unit variance, and
\delta
represents the noise level. The new data matrix is denoted by
\mathbf{B}^{\delta}
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have proposed a non-linear model for the inversion of FDEM data. Although the two-dimensional problem can be seen as a sequence of “stacked” independent one-dimensional problems, the proposed model couples them. This allows us to exploit the “horizontal” information to largely improve the quality of the computed solution avoiding the “splicing” that occurs when we deal with noisy data.

We were able to show that the outlined minimization problem has a solution, albeit non-unique, and that it induces a regularization method. We provided an algorithm for the solution of the problem and showed its performances on some synthetic and real data. From these experiments we were able to show that our method reliably computes accurate solutions even when compared with state-of-the-art method like the one in
[
14
]
.

Matters of future research include an efficient implementation exploiting Krylov subspaces. Moreover, we plan on using the Alternating Direction Multiplier Method (ADMM) and its accelerations (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
19
,
6
]
) for efficiently computing a solution of (
3
).
