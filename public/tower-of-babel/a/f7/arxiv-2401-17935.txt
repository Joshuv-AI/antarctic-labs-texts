Lipolysis on Lipid Droplets: Mathematical Modelling and Numerical Discretisations
Reymart Salcedo Lagunero, Klemens Fellner, Thomas Apel, Volker Kempf, Philipp Zilk

Abstract
Lipolysis is a life-essential metabolic process, which supplies fatty acids stored in lipid droplets to the body in order to match the demands of building new cells and providing cellular energy. In this paper, we present a first mathematical modelling approach for lipolysis, which takes into account that the involved enzymes act on the surface of lipid droplets. We postulate an active region near the surface where the substrates are within reach of the surface-bound enzymes and formulate a system of reaction-diffusion PDEs, which connect the active region to the inner core of lipid droplets via interface conditions. We establish two numerical discretisations based on finite element method and isogeometric analysis, and validate them to perform reliably. Since numerical tests are best performed on non-zero explicit stationary state solutions, we introduce and analyse a model, which describes besides lipolysis also a reverse process (yet in a physiologically much oversimplified way). The system is not coercive such that establishing well-posedness is a non-standard task. We prove the unique existence of global and equilibrium solutions. We establish exponential convergence to the equilibrium solutions using the entropy method. We then study the stationary state model and compute explicitly for radially symmetric solutions. Concerning the finite element methods, we show numerically the linear and quadratic convergence of the errors with respect to the H 1 H^{1} - and L 2 L^{2} -norms, respectively. Finally, we present numerical simulations of a prototypical PDE model of lipolysis and illustrate that ATGL clustering on lipid droplets can significantly slow down lipolysis.



Keywords:
lipid hydrolysis; lipolysis; entropy method; finite element method; transacylation; enzyme reaction.

Introduction

Fatty acids (FAs) are crucial for the production of adenosine triphosphates (ATP), synthesis of biological membranes, thermogenesis, and signal transduction
[
GL15
,
ZZE+12
]
. Animals and humans store FAs in the form of water-insoluble triglycerides (TGs) within cytosolic lipid droplets (LDs) of specialised fat cells called adipocytes, but also in other cell types. From a biochemical perspective, a TG molecule is composed of three FAs esterified to a glycerol (GL) backbone.

Lipolysis, also known as lipid hydrolysis, is the splitting of ester bonds in TGs by the addition of water releasing the fundamental components of three FAs and the glycerol backbone
[
SET+14
]
. Lipolysis is an essential metabolic process as the body requires FAs for the build-up of the phospholipid bilayer surrounding every cell and many other purposes. During times of starvation or high energy consuming activities, our body processes TGs stored in LDs and the released FAs are used for ATP production, which provides cellular energy in the body.

In white adipose tissues, lipolysis involves a coordinated process catalysed by three enzymes
[
ZSH+04
]
. Figure
1
provides a simplified schematic diagram: lipolysis is regulated by the activation of the enzyme adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) via the regulatory protein comparative gene identification-58 (CGI-58). The enzyme ATGL degrades TGs producing diglycerides (DGs, a glycerol backbone with two attached FAs) and a first free FA. Then, another enzyme called hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) hydrolyses DGs producing monoglycerides (MGs, a glycerol backbone with one attached FA) and a second free FA. Finally, the enzyme monoglyceride lipase (MGL) breaks down MGs to release the glycerol backbone and a third free FA.

Figure 1:
Lipolysis is a three-step process: ATGL hydrolyses TGs producing DGs and a first free FA; HSL hydrolyses DGs producing MGs and a second free FA; finally, MGL hydrolyses MGs releasing the backbone glycerol and a third free FA.

The scheme depicted in Figure
1
is a simplification of the
in vivo
situation: Firstly, other enzymes are known to degrade TGs, but in white adipose tissues, those are believed to be negligible in a first approach. Secondly, ATGL is capable to also hydrolyse DGs (besides TGs), yet significantly less efficiently than HSL. Similarly, HSL is able to also hydrolyse TGs (besides DGs), but much less prominently than ATGL, which is believed to be the key regulator of lipolysis.

Thirdly, ATGL is a remarkable enzyme in the sense that besides its hydrolytic activities with substrate TGs (and DGs, which we neglect), it catalyses a transacylation reaction which transfers an FA from one DG to another DG:

Figure 2:
ATGL also catalyses transacylation of two DG molecules into one TG and one MG.

The mechanism of lipolysis represented by Figure

has been first established in 2004
[
ZSH+04
]
. Previous mathematical models for lipolysis
[
KSK08
,
LEJ+21
]
, were formulated in terms of (spatially homogeneous) rate equations as part of system biology approaches. Recently, another ODE rate equations showed that transacylation can play a significant role in the lipolytic cascade
[
EFH+23
]
: On one hand, transacylation is a partial feedback mechanism forming TGs from DGs and is therefore able to slow down the lipolytic machinery. On the other hand, transacylation forms also MGs leading (under some conditions) to an increased production of downstream products by up to 80%
[
EFH+23
]
. Transacylation involves two DGs and its reaction rate is therefore a quadratic function in terms of the DG concentration. This is a significant difference to DG hydrolysis, which is modelled as Michaelis-Menten process with a linearly bounded reaction rate function. Therefore, the larger the DG concentration during lipolysis, the stronger will be the effects of DG transacylation.

Lipolysis is localised at lipid droplets, consisting of a core made of lipids and a layer of phospholipids, which connects the lipids to the surrounding aquatic environment, as illustrated on the right

All previous rate equation models
[
EFH+23
,
KSK08
,
LEJ+21
]
have the crucial drawback of not taking into account that TGs are stored in LDs and that ATGL and HSL act on or near the surface.

This article presents a first PDE modelling approach describing lipolysis on LDs based on reaction-diffusion equations. In good approximation of many
in vivo
situations, we model an LD as spherical in shape and refer to it as a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
for the rest of this text. The enzymes which regulate lipolysis are localised on the surface of the LD. Accordingly, we postulate a thin active region near the surface where the substrates (i.e. TG, DG, MG) can come in contact with the enzymes (i.e. ATGL, HSL, MGL). TGs that are sufficiently far from the surface of a LD are no immediate substrate, therefore substantially reducing the substrate availability compared to the models using ODE rate equations, which implicitly assume that enzymes and substrates are part of a three-dimensional solution.

A main part of this paper establishes a robust numerical scheme which is able to reliably simulate the dynamics of lipolysis on realistically sized LDs, for which the non-dimensional ratio of

For the verification of the numerical schemes, we would like to use an explicit non-trivial stationary state solution as test case. However, the stationary state solution to the lipolytic scheme in Figure
1
and Figure
2
is zero for the concentrations of TG, DG, and MG as they were all hydrolysed into GL and FAs.

Therefore, the first part of this paper discusses a testing model system with a non-trivial positive equilibrium state, which we use for numerical testing purposes. To achieve this, we assume that besides TGs producing DGs and FAs in the active region, the reverse process of DGs forming TGs by the uptake of an FA happens in the reservoir region. This is a much oversimplified physiological shortcut as the build up of TGs involves another set of enzymes (e.g. diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase (DGAT) 1 and 2) and requires the activation of FAs by the coenzyme A (CoA). Also, the build-up of TGs does not happen inside the LD. Finally, for the sake of simplicity, we will not consider MGs and GLs as part the testing model, as they constitute downstream products and can be post-calculated from knowing DGs.

We denote by
u:=u(x,t)
and
v:=v(x,t)
the (non-dimensional) concentrations of TGs and DGs in the reservoir region, respectively. Analogously,
w:=w(x,t)
and
z:=z(x,t)
for TGs and DGs in the active region, respectively. The active region is characterised by the presence of ATGL and we denote its concentration by
a_{3}:=a_{3}(x,t)
. We then consider the chemical network shown on the right of Figure
4

The corresponding reaction-diffusion-interface model reads for arbitrary (non-dimensional)
T>0

\begin{split}&\begin{cases}u_{t}-d_{3}\Delta u=\kappa v,&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ (x,t)\in\Omega_{1}\times(0,T],\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}u=\mu[w-u],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ (x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],\\
v_{t}-d_{2}\Delta v=-\kappa v,&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ (x,t)\in\Omega_{1}\times(0,T],\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}v=\nu[z-v],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ (x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],\\
\end{cases}\\
&\begin{cases}w_{t}-d_{3}\Delta w=-f(w),&(x,t)\in\Omega_{2}\times(0,T],\\
-d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w=-\mu[w-u],&(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T],\\
z_{t}-d_{2}\Delta z=f(w),&(x,t)\in\Omega_{2}\times(0,T],\\
-d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z=-\nu[z-v],&(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T],\end{cases}\end{split}

(1.1)

where
d_{2},\ d_{3}>0
are constant diffusion coefficients,
\mu,\ \nu>0
are constant flux rates at the interface,
\kappa>0
is a constant feedback rate of DG into TG in the reservoir region, and

f(w)=\dfrac{v_{3}a_{3}w}{k_{3}+w}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{with}\hskip 18.49988ptv_{3},\ k_{3}>0,

is a Michaelis-Menten substrate-enzyme reaction rate describing the action of ATGL,
v_{3}
is the maximum reaction velocity and
k_{3}
is the Michaelis constant. Note that the homogeneous boundary conditions at
\partial\Omega
are realistic since TGs and DGs are supposed to be too hydrophobic to leave the LDs.

System (
1.1
) is coupled with the initial data

\begin{cases}u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\hskip 9.24994pt\ v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\Omega_{1},\\
w(x,0)=w_{0}(x),\hskip 9.24994ptz(x,0)=z_{0}(x),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\Omega_{2}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

By adding the PDEs in system (
1.1
) and using integration by parts, we remark that any solution to system (
1.1
) conserves the total mass of glycerol contained in TGs and DGs for all time
t\geq 0
,

\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u(x,t)+v(x,t)]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w(x,t)+z(x,t)]\differential{x}=M_{0},

(1.3)

where
M_{0}=\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u_{0}(x)+v_{0}(x)]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w_{0}(x)+z_{0}(x)]\differential{x}>0
is the given initial total mass.

Since the Michaelis-Menten kinetics does not allow for explicit stationary state solutions, we let the ATGL concentration
a_{3}>0
to be constant and consider its linear approximation,

f(w)=\rho w\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{where}\hskip 9.24994pt\rho=\frac{v_{3}}{k_{3}}a_{3}>0\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{is constant,}\hskip 9.24994pt

(1.4)

which holds in the well-known Michaelis-Menten regime for small substrate concentrations, that is, when
w\ll k_{3}
. This is a plausible assumption in the active region where ATGL and phospholipids are expected to leave limited volume for TGs and DGs. We repeat that the reverse process
v\xrightarrow{\kappa}u
in the reservoir region is an oversimplified model for the build-up of TGs from DGs and chosen linear for the sake of constructing a non-negative explicit stationary state solution for numerical testing purposes. For this reason, unless otherwise explicitly stated, we therefore refer to system (
1.1
) as the linear parabolic system where
f
is as defined in (
1.4
).

Mathematical Preliminaries

Let us start by introducing relevant mathematical notations and the problem statement. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Let
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
and
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\smallsetminus\Omega_{1}

\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}

\norm{\Phi}_{\mathbf{L}^{p}}=\quantity(\norm{\varphi_{1}}_{L^{p}(\Omega_{1})}^{p} + \norm{\varphi_{2}}_{L^{p}(\Omega_{1})}^{p} + \norm{\varphi_{3}}_{L^{p}(\Omega_{2})}^{p} + \norm{\varphi_{4}}_{L^{p}(\Omega_{2})}^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}},

\norm{\Psi}_{\mathbf{H}^{m}}=\quantity(\norm{\psi_{1}}_{H^{m}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{\psi_{2}}_{H^{m}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{\psi_{3}}_{H^{m}(\Omega_{2})}^{2} + \norm{\psi_{4}}_{H^{m}(\Omega_{2})}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},

\Psi:=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3},\psi_{4})\in\mathbf{H}^{m}
. Note that
\mathbf{H}^{0}=\mathbf{L}^{2}
by convention. The inner product in

,

, or

is denoted by

.

We now consider the following definition of weak solutions: let
\mathbf{c}:=(u,v,w,z)
denote a tuple of functions where

\displaystyle u,v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega_{1}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega_{1})^{*}),

Then, a weak solution of the linear parabolic system (

) satisfies the weak formulation

\mathcal{B}(\dot{\mathbf{c}},\Phi)+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c},\Phi)=0\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{for all test functions}\hskip 9.24994pt\Phi\in\mathbf{H}^{1},

where the linear form

\mathcal{B}(\dot{\mathbf{c}},\Phi):=(\dot{u},\varphi_{1})_{\Omega_{1}}+(\dot{v},\varphi_{2})_{\Omega_{1}}+(\dot{w},\varphi_{3})_{\Omega_{2}}+(\dot{z},\varphi_{4})_{\Omega_{2}},

(2.2)

the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c},\Phi)

\displaystyle:=d_{3}(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi_{1})_{\Omega_{1}}+d_{2}(\nabla v,\nabla\varphi_{2})_{\Omega_{1}}+d_{3}(\nabla w,\nabla\varphi_{3})_{\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle+d_{2}(\nabla z,\nabla\varphi_{4})_{\Omega_{2}}+\kappa(v,\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})_{\Omega_{1}}+\rho(w,\varphi_{3}-\varphi_{4})_{\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle+\mu(w-u,\varphi_{3}-\varphi_{1})_{\Gamma}+\nu(z-v,\varphi_{4}-\varphi_{2})_{\Gamma},

3
Well-posedness

In this section, we discuss the existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on the initial datum of the weak solution of the linear parabolic system (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.1

(Continuity of
\mathcal{A}
)
.

There exists a constant
K>0
depending only on the diffusion coefficients, reaction rates, flux constants, and the domains
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
, such that

\absolutevalue{ \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c}, \Phi) }\leq K\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}\norm{\Phi}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{for all}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbf{c},\Phi\in\mathbf{H}^{1}.

(3.1)

Proof.

The proof follows from direct computations using the triangle and Hölder’s inequalities, and the fact that the
L^{2}
-norm is bounded above by the
H^{1}
-norm, e.g.

|d_{3}(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi_{1})_{\Omega_{1}}|\leq d_{3}\norm{u}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})}\norm{\varphi_{1}}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})}\leq d_{3}\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}\norm{\Phi}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}},

and the Trace Theorem, [see Theorem 1, Section 5.5 of
[
Eva10
]
], e.g.

\displaystyle|\mu(w-u,\varphi_{3}-\varphi_{1})_{\Gamma}|

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\leq K_{1}\mu\quantity(\norm{w}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{2})} + \norm{u}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})})\quantity(\norm{\varphi_{1}}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{1})} +\norm{\varphi_{3}}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{2})}),

for some constant
K_{1}>0
. Hence, we obtain the constant
K>0
such that estimate (
3.1
) holds.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Gårding Inequality)
.

There exist constants
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c})+\alpha\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\geq\beta\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}.

(3.2)

One admissible choice is
\alpha=1+\max\quantity{ \frac{\kappa}{2}, \frac{\rho}{2} }>0
and
\beta=\min\left\{1,d_{2},d_{3},\kappa,\rho\right\}>0
.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c})\geq d_{3}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+d_{2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+d_{3}\norm{\nabla w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+d_{2}\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+\kappa\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+\rho\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\quantity( 1 + \frac{\kappa}{2} )\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}-\quantity( 1 + \frac{\rho}{2} )\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\geq\beta\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}-\alpha\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2},

where
\beta=\min\left\{1,d_{2},d_{3},\kappa,\rho\right\}>0
and
\alpha=1+\max\quantity{ \frac{\kappa}{2}, \frac{\rho}{2} }>0
, proving inequality (
3.2
) as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Well-posedness of the Parabolic System)
.

Given any initial datum
\mathbf{c}_{0}:=(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0},z_{0})\in\mathbf{L}^{2}

\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\dot{\mathbf{c}}(t)}_{(\mathbf{H}^{1})^{*}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq C\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2},

1.2
), that is,
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}(0)=\mathbf{c}_{0}
. Thus, there exists a unique solution
\widetilde{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}
to the variational equation (
3.4
), and therefore to the original problem (
2.1
).

With the existence and uniqueness of solution, we now prove the stability estimate in (
3.3
). Multiplying the PDE for
u
with
e^{-\lambda t}u
for some
\lambda>0
(to be determined later) in system (
1.1
) and integrating with respect to space, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{u(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{u(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+e^{-\lambda t}d_{3}\norm{\nabla u(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle-e^{-\lambda t}\kappa(v(t),u(t))_{\Omega_{1}}-e^{-\lambda t}\mu(w(t)-u(t),u(t))_{\Gamma}=0.

(3.5)

Analogously for
v
,
w
, and
z
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{v(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{v(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+e^{-\lambda t}d_{3}\norm{\nabla v(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle+e^{-\lambda t}\kappa\norm{v(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-e^{-\lambda t}\nu(z(t)-v(t),v(t))_{\Gamma}=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{w(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{w(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+e^{-\lambda t}d_{2}\norm{\nabla w(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle+e^{-\lambda t}\rho\norm{w(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+e^{-\lambda t}\mu(w(t)-u(t),w(t))_{\Gamma}=0,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{z(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{z(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+e^{-\lambda t}d_{2}\norm{\nabla z(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle-e^{-\lambda t}\rho(z(t),w(t))_{\Omega_{2}}+e^{-\lambda t}\nu(z(t)-v(t),z(t))_{\Gamma}=0.

(3.8)

Adding Eqns. (
3.5
), (
3.6
), (
3.7
), and (
3.8
), we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\quantity( \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c}(t), \mathbf{c}(t)) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2} )=0.

Recalling the Gårding inequality (
3.2
) and choosing
\lambda=2\alpha
, we have

\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-2 \alpha t} \norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2})+2\beta e^{-2\alpha t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq 0.

Integration with respect to time over
(0,t)
and using
\mathbf{c}(0)=\mathbf{c}_{0}
, we obtain, for every
t\in(0,T)
,

e^{-2\alpha T}\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+2\beta\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\alpha\tau}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}.

Since

e^{-2\alpha T}\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\alpha\tau}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau},

we therefore get

\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq\frac{e^{2\alpha T}}{\min\{1,2\beta\}}\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}.

(3.9)

Now, observe that by the continuity of
\mathcal{A}
, we have

\absolutevalue{\mathcal{B}(\dot{\mathbf{c}}(t), \Phi)}=\absolutevalue{-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c}(t), \Phi)}\leq K\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}\norm{\Phi}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}},

and therefore, we obtain

\norm{\dot{\mathbf{c}}(t)}_{(\mathbf{H}^{1})^{*}}^{2}\leq K^{2}\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}.

Integration with respect to time implies that

\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\dot{\mathbf{c}}(\tau)}_{(\mathbf{H}^{1})^{*}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq K^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq K^{2}\frac{e^{2\alpha T}}{\min\{1,2\beta\}}\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}.

(3.10)

Hence, adding the estimates (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we have

\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\mathbf{c}(\tau)}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}}^{2}\differential{\tau}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\dot{\mathbf{c}}(\tau)}_{(\mathbf{H}^{1})^{*}}^{2}\differential{\tau}\leq C\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2},

where
C=(1+K^{2})\frac{e^{2\alpha T}}{\min\{1,2\beta\}}>0
, proving the stability estimate (
3.3
).

Finally, let us show the non-negativity of the solution provided the given initial datum is non-negative. In particular, given the non-negative initial datum
\mathbf{c}_{0}
, we multiply the PDE for
u
with
e^{-\lambda t}u^{-}
where
u^{-}:=-\min\{0,u\}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
denotes the negative part of
u
, and analogously following the steps as in the stability estimate proof above, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-\lambda t} \norm{\mathbf{c}^{-}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2})+e^{-\lambda t}\quantity( \mathcal{A}(\mathbf{c}^{-}(t), \mathbf{c}^{-}(t)) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\mathbf{c}^{-}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2} )=0.

As before, the Gårding inequality in Lemma
3.2
and the positivity of the exponential function imply that

\derivative{t}\quantity(e^{-2\alpha t} \norm{\mathbf{c}^{-}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2})\leq 0.

Integration with respect to time yields
\norm{\mathbf{c}^{-}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{2\alpha t}\norm{\mathbf{c}^{-}(0)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}=0
since we have that
\mathbf{c}^{-}(0)=0
due to non-negativity of
\mathbf{c}_{0}
. Hence,
\mathbf{c}^{-}(x,t)=0
a.e. in
\Omega
and thus, for all
t\in[0,T]
, the solution
\mathbf{c}
is non-negative a.e. in
\Omega
.
∎

Theorem 3.4

(Global
\mathbf{L}^{2}
bounds)
.

Given the non-negative initial datum
\mathbf{c}_{0}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
, we have

\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C,

(3.11)

that is, the global weak solution to system (
1.1
) is uniformly bounded in time.

Proof.

Take the time derivative of the quadratic function

\mathcal{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\quantity( \norm{u(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{v(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{w(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2} + \norm{z(t)}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2} ),

to obtain (suppressing the variable
t
in the notations)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-d_{3}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-d_{2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-d_{3}\norm{\nabla w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}-d_{2}\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\kappa\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\rho\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+\kappa(u,v)_{\Omega_{1}}+\rho(w,z)_{\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\mu\norm{w - u}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\nu\norm{z - v}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}.

(3.12)

Let us estimate the inner products and the
L^{2}
-norms at the interface
\Gamma
. Observe that by Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, we get

\displaystyle\kappa(u,v)_{\Omega_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\quantity(\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}),

\displaystyle\rho(w,z)_{\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\quantity(\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2} + \norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}),

\displaystyle\mu\norm{w - u}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mu\quantity(\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2} + \norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}),

\displaystyle\nu\norm{z - v}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\nu\quantity(\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2} + \norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}).

We use the following interpolation estimates, see for instance, Theorem 1.5.1.10 of
[
Gri11
]
: for all
u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
and
w,z\in H^{1}(\Omega_{2})
, for any
0<\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{4}<1
, there exist constants
C_{\varepsilon_{1}},C_{\varepsilon_{2}},C_{\varepsilon_{3}},C_{\varepsilon_{4}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{1}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{1}}\norm{u}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}^{2},

\displaystyle\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{2}}\norm{v}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}^{2},

\displaystyle\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{3}\norm{\nabla w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{3}}\norm{w}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}^{2},

\displaystyle\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{4}\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{4}}\norm{z}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}.

Similarly, we utilise the following mean Poincaré inequalities, see for instance, Theorem 1.5 of
[
Neč11
]
: for all
u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
and
z\in H^{1}(\Omega_{2})
, there exist constants
\varepsilon_{5},\varepsilon_{6}>0
such that

\displaystyle\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{5}\quantity(\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2} + \norm{u}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}),

\displaystyle\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{6}\quantity(\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2} + \norm{z}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}).

Due to the non-negativity of solutions and the total mass conservation law in (
1.3
), we also know that

\norm{u}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})},\ \norm{v}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})},\ \norm{w}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})},\ \norm{z}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}\leq M_{0}.

Therefore, going back to (
3.12
), and using the interpolation estimates and mean Poincaré inequalities above, we obtain

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\leq-d_{3}\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-d_{2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-d_{3}\norm{\nabla w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}-d_{2}\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\frac{\kappa}{2}\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+2\mu\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+2\nu\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+2\mu\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+2\nu\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\quantity(\frac{d_{3}}{2} - 2\mu\varepsilon_{1})\norm{\nabla u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-(d_{2}-2\nu\varepsilon_{2})\norm{\nabla v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-(d_{3}-2\mu\varepsilon_{3})\norm{\nabla w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}-\quantity(\frac{d_{2}}{2} - 2\nu\varepsilon_{4})\norm{\nabla z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\quantity(\frac{d_{3}}{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{5}} - \frac{\kappa}{2})\norm{u}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}\norm{v}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-\frac{\rho}{2}\norm{w}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}-\quantity(\frac{d_{2}}{2} \frac{1}{\varepsilon_{6}} - \frac{\rho}{2})\norm{z}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+2\mu C_{\varepsilon_{1}}\norm{u}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}+2\nu C_{\varepsilon_{2}}\norm{u}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+2\mu C_{\varepsilon_{3}}\norm{w}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}+2\nu C_{\varepsilon_{4}}\norm{z}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{2})}^{2}.

Note that
\frac{d_{3}}{2}-2\mu\varepsilon_{1}>0
provided
\varepsilon_{1}<\frac{d_{3}}{4\mu}
. Thus, choose
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\min\{1,\frac{d_{3}}{4\mu}\}
. Analogously, choose
0<\varepsilon_{2}<\min\{1,\frac{d_{2}}{2\nu}\}
,
0<\varepsilon_{3}<\min\{1,\frac{d_{3}}{2\mu}\}
, and
0<\varepsilon_{4}<\min\{1,\frac{d_{2}}{4\nu}\}
. Similarly,
\frac{d_{3}}{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{5}}-\frac{\kappa}{2}>0
provided
\varepsilon_{5}<\frac{d_{3}}{\kappa}
. Hence, choose
0<\varepsilon_{5}<\min\{\frac{d_{3}}{\kappa},C_{P}\}
and analogously,
0<\varepsilon_{6}<\min\{\frac{d_{2}}{\rho},C_{P}\}
where
C_{P}>0
is the Poincaré constant from Theorem 1.5 of
[
Neč11
]
. Therefore, we have that

\derivative{t}\mathcal{H}+a\mathcal{H}\leq b,

where
\displaystyle a=\min\quantity{ \frac{d_{3}}{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{5}} - \frac{\kappa}{2}, \frac{\kappa}{2}, \frac{\rho}{2}, \frac{d_{2}}{2} \frac{1}{\varepsilon_{6}} - \frac{\rho}{2} }>0
and
b=2M_{0}^{2}(\mu(C_{\varepsilon_{1}}+C_{\varepsilon_{3}})+\nu(C_{\varepsilon_{2}}+C_{\varepsilon_{4}}))>0
are both constants. By a classical Gronwall argument, we obtain the estimate

\mathcal{H}(t)\leq e^{-at}\mathcal{H}(0)+\frac{b}{a}(e^{-at}-1).

Since
e^{-at}\leq 1
for all
t\geq 0
, we have
\norm{\mathbf{c}(t)}_{\mathbf{L}^{2}}^{2}\leq C
for all
t\geq 0
where
C=\mathcal{H}(0)>0
as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.5

(Regularity)
.

The weak solution
\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)
to system (
1.1
) satisfies the following regularities:

\displaystyle u,v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega_{1}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega_{1})),

\displaystyle w,z\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega_{2}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega_{2})).

Proof.

The proof follows from classical arguments since
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{1})
and
w_{0},z_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{2})
, see for instance Section 9.2 of
[
Sal15
]
.
∎

We now prove the well-posedness of the corresponding elliptic problem of system (
1.1
). Our main result is summarised in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Well-posedness of the Elliptic System)
.

For any given positive total mass
M_{0}>0
, there exists a unique positive equilibrium
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}(M_{0}):=(u_{\infty},v_{\infty},w_{\infty},z_{\infty})
solving the corresponding elliptic system of (
1.1
):

\begin{split}&\begin{cases}-d_{3}\Delta u_{\infty}=\kappa v_{\infty},&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Omega_{1},\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}u_{\infty}=\mu[w_{\infty}-u_{\infty}],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Gamma,\\
-d_{2}\Delta v_{\infty}=-\kappa v_{\infty},&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Omega_{1},\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}v_{\infty}=\nu[z_{\infty}-v_{\infty}],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Gamma,\\
\end{cases}\\
&\begin{cases}-d_{3}\Delta w_{\infty}=-\rho w_{\infty},&x\in\Omega_{2},\\
-d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w_{\infty}=-\mu[w_{\infty}-u_{\infty}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w_{\infty}=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\\
-d_{2}\Delta z_{\infty}=\rho w_{\infty},&x\in\Omega_{2},\\
-d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z_{\infty}=-\nu[z_{\infty}-v_{\infty}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z_{\infty}=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.13)

where the total mass
M_{0}
corresponding to the total mass conservation law (
1.3
) of the evolution problem is given by

\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u_{\infty}(x)+v_{\infty}(x)]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w_{\infty}(x)+z_{\infty}(x)]\differential{x}=M_{0}.

(3.14)

Moreover, we have

u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1}),\hskip 9.24994ptw_{\infty},z_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{2}),

and for some constants
0<m\leq M<\infty
,

\displaystyle 0<m\leq u_{\infty}(x),v_{\infty}(x)\leq M\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{for all}\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\overline{\Omega}_{1},

\displaystyle 0<m\leq w_{\infty}(x),z_{\infty}(x)\leq M\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{for all}\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\overline{\Omega}_{2}.

Proof.

The existence of solutions to (
3.13
) is far from trivial due to the complex-balanced coupling of four equations in two subdomains (recall the right hand graph of Figure
4
). We are unaware of a general existence theory for such systems; regardless of (
3.13
) being linear. In the following, we use some ideas from
[
FT17
]
and prove existence via a fixed point argument applied to an iteration of auxiliary solutions. One possible choice of an auxiliary system would preserve the total mass throughout the iterations, yet not the non-negativity of the solutions as such. Therefore, we choose the following partially decoupled auxiliary system, which features non-negative solutions for given non-negative input:

\begin{split}&\begin{cases}-d_{3}\Delta u=\kappa v,&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Omega_{1},\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}u=\mu[w_{0}-u],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Gamma,\\
-d_{2}\Delta v=-\kappa v,&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Omega_{1},\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}v=\nu[z_{0}-v],&\hskip 9.24994pt\ \ x\in\Gamma,\\
\end{cases}\\
&\begin{cases}-d_{3}\Delta w=-\rho w,&x\in\Omega_{2},\\
-d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w=-\mu[w-u_{0}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\\
-d_{2}\Delta z=\rho w,&x\in\Omega_{2},\\
-d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z=-\nu[z-v_{0}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
d_{2}\partial_{\eta}z=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.15)

where the input functions
\mathbf{c}_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0},z_{0})\in\mathbf{Y}
are given from the space

\mathbf{Y}:=\{\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)\in\mathbf{H}^{3}:u,v,w,z\geq 0\}.

Note that the solution
\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)
cannot be expected to have the same total mass as the previous iteration
\mathbf{c}_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0},z_{0})
. The solution
\mathbf{c}
will even be zero if the trace of
\mathbf{c}_{0}
vanishes at
\Gamma
.

In order to prove the existence of solutions to (
3.13
) with positive total mass
M_{0}
as fixed point of an iteration scheme, we therefore show positivity of the solutions
\mathbf{c}
subject to positive
\mathbf{c}_{0}
by using the strong maximum principle, which requires higher regularity and classical solutions. In particular, we choose, as an initial approximation,
\mathbf{c}_{0}
to be positive, piecewise constant functions in the two subdomains such that for any
M_{0}>0
, the total mass conservation law holds,

\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u_{0}+v_{0}]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w_{0}+z_{0}]\differential{x}=M_{0}>0.

The existence and uniqueness of a non-negative solution
\mathbf{c}
to the auxiliary system (
3.15
) is shown as follows. Let us first consider the PDE for
v
which is independent of the unknown
u
in
\Omega_{1}
:

-d_{2}\Delta v+\kappa v=0\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{in}\hskip 9.24994pt\Omega_{1},\hskip 9.24994ptd_{2}\partial_{\eta}v+\nu v=\nu z_{0}\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{on}\hskip 9.24994pt\Gamma.

The Lax-Milgram theorem guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the weak solution
v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
to the variational problem: given
z_{0}\in H^{3}(\Omega_{2})
, find
v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
such that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}d_{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi_{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Gamma}\nu v\varphi_{2}\differential{s}+\int_{\Omega_{1}}\kappa v\varphi_{2}\differential{x}=\int_{\Gamma}\nu z_{0}\varphi_{2}\differential{s},

for all test functions
\varphi_{2}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
. To show non-negativity given
z_{0}\geq 0
, observe that using
\varphi_{2}=v^{-}:=-\min\{v,0\}
, the negative part of
v
as test function, and noting that
vv^{-}=-(v^{-})^{2}
and
\nabla v\cdot\nabla v^{-}=-\chi_{\{v<0\}}\absolutevalue{\nabla v}^{2}
, we obtain

-d_{2}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\chi_{\{v\leq 0\}}|\nabla v|^{2}\differential{x}-\kappa\int_{\Omega_{1}}(v^{-})^{2}\differential{x}-\nu\int_{\Gamma}(v^{-})^{2}\differential{s}=\int_{\Gamma}z_{0}v^{-}\differential{s}.

Since the left-hand side is non-positive and the right-hand side is non-negative, then both sides are equal to zero and thus,
\int_{\Omega_{1}}(v^{-})^{2}\differential{x}=0
and
v\geq 0
is non-negative in
\Omega_{1}
. Since
z_{0}\in H^{3-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)=H^{2+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
, by maximal regularity for elliptic systems, we have that
v\in H^{4}(\Omega_{1})
and the stability estimate

\norm{v}_{H^{4}(\Omega_{1})}\leq C\norm{z_{0}}_{H^{3}(\Omega_{2})},

for some constant
C>0
holds. By Sobolev Embedding Theorem for
n=2,3
, we get
v\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and thus,
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
.
Hence, the strong maximum principle
[
Pao92
]
implies that
v
is strictly positive on
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
whenever
z_{0}
is strictly positive on
\Gamma
.

With this unique positive solution
v
, we now consider the PDE for
u
:

-d_{3}\Delta u=\kappa v\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{in}\hskip 9.24994pt\Omega_{1},\hskip 18.49988ptd_{3}\partial_{\eta}u=\mu[w_{0}-u]\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{on}\hskip 9.24994pt\Gamma.

As before, the Lax-Milgram Theorem guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
to the variational problem: find
u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
such that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}d_{3}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Gamma}\mu u\varphi_{1}\differential{s}=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\kappa v\varphi_{1}\differential{x}+\int_{\Gamma}\mu w_{0}\varphi_{1}\differential{s},

for all test functions
\varphi_{1}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
. Since
v\in H^{4}(\Omega_{1})\subset H^{2}(\Omega_{1})
and
w_{0}\in H^{3-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)=H^{2+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
, by maximal regularity,
u\in H^{4}(\Omega_{1})
and

\norm{u}_{H^{4}(\Omega_{1})}\leq C\quantity{ \norm{w_{0}}_{H^{3}(\Omega_{2})} + \norm{z_{0}}_{H^{3}(\Omega_{2})} },

for some constant
C>0
. Hence, following the same embedding arguments as for
v
, we get
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
. To prove non-negativity of the unique solution we choose
\varphi_{1}=u^{-}
as test function and obtain

-\int_{\Omega_{1}}\chi_{\{u<0\}}d_{3}\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{2}\differential{x}-\int_{\Gamma}\mu(u^{-})^{2}\differential{s}=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\kappa vu^{-}\differential{x}+\int_{\Gamma}\mu w_{0}u^{-}.

Note that the left-hand side is again non-positive while the right-hand side is non-negative since
v,w_{0}\geq 0
. Hence, both sides must be equal to zero and as a consequence,
\int_{\Omega_{1}}\chi_{\{u<0\}}\absolutevalue{\nabla u}^{2}\differential{x}=0
and
\int_{\Gamma}\mu(u^{-})^{2}\differential{s}=0
, which implies that
u^{-}
is constant in
\Omega_{1}
and
u^{-}=0
on
\Gamma
. By continuity (up to the boundary), we have that
u^{-}=0
in
\Omega_{1}
, and hence,
u\geq 0
in
\Omega_{1}
. Finally, the strong maximum principles implies that
u
is strictly positive in
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
provided that either
v
is strictly positive in
\Omega_{1}
or
w_{0}
is strictly positive on
\Gamma
.

Following analogous arguments for
w
and
z
in
\Omega_{2}
, we therefore obtain a non-negative weak solution
\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)\in\mathbf{H}^{3}
to the auxiliary system (
3.15
), and in addition, due to maximal regularity, we have
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbf{H}^{4}
and the
a priori
estimate holds

\norm{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{H}^{4}}\leq C\norm{\mathbf{c}_{0}}_{\mathbf{H}^{3}},

(3.16)

for some constant
C>0
. By Sobolev embeddings for
n=2,3
, we have that
\mathbf{c}
is a classical solution to the auxiliary system (
3.15
). Finally, by the strong maximum principle, strictly positive input functions
\mathbf{c}_{0}
yield a strictly positive solution
\mathbf{c}
.

However, we cannot expect the solution
\mathbf{c}
to represent the same total mass
M_{0}>0
as the initial guess
\mathbf{c}_{0}
. Nevertheless, the positivity of
\mathbf{c}
implies that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u+v]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w+z]\differential{x}=M_{1}>0,

for some
M_{1}>0
. We can now rescale the input functions
\mathbf{c}_{0}
and the solutions to (
3.15
) by choosing a multiplier
\lambda=M_{0}/M_{1}>0
: by replacing
\mathbf{c}_{0}
with
\lambda\mathbf{c}_{0}
as input in the auxiliary system (
3.15
), we obtain (by linearity)
\lambda
-times the previous solution. Hence, the rescaled solution
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbf{Y}
contains the desired total mass

\int_{\Omega_{1}}[u+v]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}[w+z]\differential{x}=M_{0}.

Define the mapping
\mathcal{I}:\mathbf{Y}\to\mathbf{Y}
by
\mathcal{I}\mathbf{c}_{0}=\mathbf{c}
. Due to the
a priori
estimate (
3.16
),
\mathcal{I}
is a compact mapping. By the Schauder fixed point theorem, there exists a fixed point denoted by
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}:=(u_{\infty},v_{\infty},w_{\infty},z_{\infty})
of the mapping
\mathcal{I}
, and this fixed point is thus the non-negative weak solution of the elliptic system (
3.13
) with total mass
M_{0}
. The equilibrium solution
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}
enjoys higher regularity due to the maximal regularity, that is,
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}\in\mathbf{H}^{4}
. Thus, as above, the Sobolev Embedding Theorem implies that for
n=2,3
, we obtain

u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{and}\hskip 9.24994ptw_{\infty},z_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{2}).

Due to strong maximum principle, we also conclude that given
M_{0}>0
, we have that
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}>0
. In case
M_{0}=0
, we recover the trivial solution
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}=0
.

The upper bound
M>0
for the equilibrium solutions is due to the continuity of the functions and the compactness of their corresponding domains. The positive lower bound
m>0
follows from the strong maximum principle like for to the solutions of the auxiliary system (
3.15
).

Finally, we prove the uniqueness of the equilibrium solution to system (
3.13
) via the entropy structure of the parabolic system (
1.1
). Since system (
1.1
) describes the evolution of a complex balanced reaction network, it features the
relative entropy functional

\mathcal{E}

[
FPT17
]
given by:

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}_{1}|\mathbf{c}_{2})=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\quantity( \frac{u_{1}^{2}}{u_{2}} + \frac{v_{1}^{2}}{v_{2}} )\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\quantity( \frac{w_{1}^{2}}{w_{2}} + \frac{z_{1}^{2}}{z_{2}} )\differential{x},

(3.17)

where
\mathbf{c}_{1}:=(u_{1},v_{1},w_{1},z_{1})
and
\mathbf{c}_{2}:=(u_{2},v_{2},w_{2},z_{2})
are any two solutions of the parabolic system with respect to possibly different initial data. The evolution of the relative entropy functional computes according to the
entropy-production functional

\mathcal{D}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{c}_{1}|\mathbf{c}_{2})=-\derivative{t}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}_{1}|\mathbf{c}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{1}}2d_{3}u_{2}\quantity| \nabla\qty(\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}) |^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{1}}2d_{2}v_{2}\quantity| \nabla\qty(\frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}}) |^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{1}}\kappa v_{2}\quantity| \frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}} - \frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}} |^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}2d_{3}w_{2}\quantity| \nabla\qty(\frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}}) |^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{2}}2d_{2}z_{2}\quantity| \nabla\qty(\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}}) |^{2}\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\rho w_{2}\quantity| \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}} - \frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}} |^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma}\mu(w_{2}+u_{2})\quantity| \frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}} - \frac{w_{1}}{w_{2}} |^{2}\differential{s}+\int_{\Gamma}\nu(z_{2}+v_{2})\quantity| \frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}} - \frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}} |^{2}\differential{s}.

(3.18)

Next, let
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(1)}:=(u_{\infty}^{(1)},v_{\infty}^{(1)},w_{\infty}^{(1)},z_{\infty}^{(1)})
and
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(2)}:=(u_{\infty}^{(2)},v_{\infty}^{(2)},w_{\infty}^{(2)},z_{\infty}^{(2)})
be two solutions of the stationary state system (
3.13
). By being equilibrium solutions, we see that
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(1)}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(2)})=-\derivative{t}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(1)}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(2)})=0
independent of time. Thus, from the entropy-production functional (
3
), we obtain

\frac{u_{\infty}^{(1)}}{u_{\infty}^{(2)}}\equiv\frac{v_{\infty}^{(1)}}{v_{\infty}^{(2)}}\equiv\frac{w_{\infty}^{(1)}}{w_{\infty}^{(2)}}\equiv\frac{z_{\infty}^{(1)}}{z_{\infty}^{(2)}}\equiv k,

for some constant
0\neq k\in\mathbb{R}
. If the equilibrium solutions
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(2)}
satisfy the same total mass conservation law, then we see that
k\equiv 1
and thus,
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(1)}\equiv\mathbf{c}_{\infty}^{(2)}
, which proves uniqueness of the equilibrium solution for a fixed total mass.
∎

We end this section with the following property of the global weak solution of system (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.7

(Exponential Convergence to Equilibrium)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.6
, we further conclude that every global weak solution
\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)
to system (
1.1
) with given fixed initial total mass
M_{0}>0
converges exponentially to
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}=(u_{\infty},v_{\infty},w_{\infty},z_{\infty})
in the sense that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{1}}\quantity[ \frac{|u - u_{\infty}|^{2}}{u_{\infty}} + \frac{|v - v_{\infty}|^{2}}{v_{\infty}}]\differential{x}+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\quantity[ \frac{|w - w_{\infty}|^{2}}{w_{\infty}} + \frac{|z - z_{\infty}|^{2}}{z_{\infty}} ]\differential{x}\\
\leq C\exp(-\lambda t),\end{split}

for all
t>0
, where the constants
\lambda>0
and
C>0
can be explicitly computed.

Proof.

By definition, we have
\derivative{t}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})=0
. We use the result in Lemma 2.4 of
[
FT17
]
: given a fixed total mass
M_{0}>0
, the following entropy entropy-production estimate

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})\geq\lambda\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty}),

(3.19)

holds for any non-negative measurable functions
\mathbf{c}=(u,v,w,z)
satisfying the mass conservation law (
1.3
), where
\mathbf{c}_{\infty}=(u_{\infty},v_{\infty},w_{\infty},z_{\infty})
is as defined in Theorem
3.6
, and the constant
\lambda
can be explicitly estimated. With estimate (
3.19
), we have that

\derivative{t}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})+\lambda\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})\leq 0,

and by a classical Gronwall argument, we get
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\infty}|\mathbf{c}_{\infty})\leq C\exp(-\lambda t)
as desired.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Radially Symmetric Solutions)
.

The solutions of the boundary value problem (
4.5
) are given by

\displaystyle v(r)=k_{1}I_{0}(\alpha r),

\displaystyle u(r)=\frac{C_{1}}{d_{3}}-\frac{d_{2}}{d_{3}}v(r),

(4.6)

\displaystyle w(r)=k_{2}I_{0}(\beta r)+k_{3}K_{0}(\beta r),

\displaystyle z(r)=\frac{C_{2}}{d_{2}}-\frac{d_{3}}{d_{2}}w(\beta r),

(4.7)

where
\alpha^{2}=\kappa/d_{2}
,
\beta^{2}=\rho/d_{3}
,
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
,
k_{3}
are explicitly computed constants,
I_{0}
and
K_{0}
are the modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind (of order zero), respectively, and
C_{1},C_{2}
are the constant total substrate concentrations in
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

The derivation of the explicit solutions is straightforward. Observe that adding the ODEs for
u
and
v
(similarly, for
w
and
z

and one. Since we want non-negative solutions, we disregard

. Hence, we obtain the scaled conservation of total concentrations in

and

(4.8)

We now solve the ordinary differential system for
u
and
v
. The general solution of the ODE for

is given by

, where
\alpha^{2}=\kappa/d_{2}
,

and

are the

of order

(first kind and second kind, respectively). Since

in (

\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\

5
Effects of ATGL Clustering on Lipolysis

In this section, we illustrate the effects of ATGL clustering on the surface of LDs via numerical examples. ATGL molecules are known to be able to form sizeable clusters and there is experimental evidence of spatially heterogeneous distributions of ATGL on LDs
[
KRV+21
,
PWL+18
]
. However, a quantitative assessment of the effects of ATGL heterogeneities to lipolysis is far beyond the currently available experimental protocols, which are only able to quantitatively measure substrate and enzyme concentrations of entire populations of LDs.

In the following, we present a prototypical model for lipolysis, which processes TGs over DGs and MGs to GLs and three FAs according to hydrolysis in Figure
1
and transacylation in Figure
2
. The PDE model reads for all
t\in(0,T]
and some arbitrary observation time
T>0

\begin{split}\begin{cases}\partialderivative{t}q_{3i}-d_{3}\Delta q_{3i}=0,&x\in\Omega_{1},\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}q_{3i}=\mu[q_{3}-q_{3i}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
\partialderivative{t}q_{3}-d_{3}\Delta q_{3}=-\dfrac{v_{3}a_{3}(x)q_{3}}{k_{3}+q_{3}}+\sigma a_{3}(x)q_{2}^{2},&x\in\Omega_{2},\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}q_{3}=\mu[q_{3}-q_{3i}],&x\in\Gamma,\\
d_{3}\partial_{\eta}q_{3}=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\\

d_{2}\partial_{\eta}q_{2}=0,&x\in\Gamma,\\

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we presented a first PDE model for lipolysis on lipid droplets by postulating a thin active layer near the surface, where the enzymes can get into contact with the substrates. The PDE model in system (
1.1
) served as numerical testing case in preparation for a prototypical model of lipolysis in system (
5.1
) incorporating hydrolysis and DG transacylation. For the linear parabolic system (
1.1
), the classical theory is applicable to prove the well-posedness of the problem, while for the corresponding elliptic system, difficulties arise from the noncoercivity of the bilinear form, which prevents the use of the classical Lax-Milgram Theorem. Instead, we adapt the arguments of
[
FT17
]
for which a fixed point argument was used to prove existence of solutions and the entropy method for the uniqueness. We also used the entropy method to prove the exponential convergence of solutions to the complex-balanced equilibrium.

We have provided numerical simulations to demonstrate the theoretical results. In particular, we implemented a classical finite element method using the computing package
FEniCS
and an isogeometric method using
GeoPDEs
. The main reason of using two different methods is to compare the numerical results. While the results from the
FEniCS
implementation are already enough for a numerical test model, the results from
GeoPDEs
improved the numerical errors in the sense that the computational domain is exactly represented and hence, the error only comes from the finite-dimensional approximation of the continuous problem. Finally, we explored, through numerical simulations, the effects of ATGL clustering in the active region, which shows a significant slow down of lipolysis.

The proof for the existence and uniqueness of discrete solutions is an open problem in this paper as well as for the error estimates. Nonetheless, we have numerically shown the linear and quadratic convergence of the discretisation error in the
H^{1}
- and
L^{2}
- norms. Additionally, we have shown the improved convergence for using higher order NURBS basis functions.
