A framework for fitting quadratic-bilinear systems with applications to models of electrical circuits
Dimitrios S. Karachalios, Ion Victor Gosea, Athanasios C. Antoulas

Abstract
In this contribution, we propose a data-driven procedure to fit quadratic-bilinear surrogate models from data. Although the dynamics characterizing the original model are strongly nonlinear, we rely on lifting techniques to embed the original model into a quadratic-bilinear format. Here, data represent generalized transfer function values. This method is an extension of methods that do bilinear, or quadratic inference, separately. It is based on first fitting a linear model with the classical Loewner framework, and then on inferring the best supplementing nonlinear operators, in a least-squares sense. The application scope of this method is given by electrical circuits with nonlinear components (such as diodes). We propose various test cases to illustrate the performance of the method.



\novelty

Many classes of nonlinear systems that are described by analytic nonlinearities can be represented equivalently as quadratic-bilinear systems (by means of lifting). The proposed method combines the Loewner framework with the Volterra series theory and constructs reduced quadratic-bilinear systems from input-output time-domain data that approximate the original nonlinear system.

Data-Driven Methods, Non-Intrusive Modeling, Model Reduction, Nonlinear Dynamics, Quadratic-Bilinear Systems, System Identification, Lift and Learn Approach.

Introduction

System Identification (SI) and data-driven Model Order Reduction (MOR) are two fairly established methodologies that aim at discovering/computing robust surrogates of dynamical systems from data. This is performed without having an exact access to the system’s structure or matrices scaling the various terms. In the case of SI, the aim of discovery of known classes of dynamical systems that constitute an appropriate mathematical formalism capable of describing dynamical phenomena. We refer the reader to
[
21
]
and to
[
19
]
for more details on various methodologies. In the case of MOR, the need for approximating the underlying dynamical system is dictated mostly by the increased dimension under hand (the number of internal variables). Conventional MOR methods are indeed intrusive, in the sense that they require an explicit formulation of the dynamical system to be reduced (in terms of matrices or various operators). We refer the reader to
[
7
]
and to
[
1
]
, for more details. However, data-driven MOR methods are generally non-intrussive, since they require only data (snapshots of the states, input-output measurements, etc.), and not the full/exact description of the model. Methods that fall into this category range from DMD (dynamical mode decomposition), OpInf (operator inference), to LF (the Loewner framework). Such data-driven methods can be used for identifying surrogate models without having exact access to the original operators.
Starting with
[
14
]
, many algorithms have been developed to identify linear dynamical systems in the state-space realization:

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{{\textbf{x}}}(t)&={\textbf{A}}{\textbf{x}}(t)+{\textbf{B}}u(t),\ \ y(t)={\textbf{C}}{\textbf{x}}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
x
is the state variable of dimension
n
, while the system matrices are given by
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n},~{\textbf{B}},{\textbf{C}}^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times 1}
. We refer to , for more details on various methodologies. In recent years, the ideas for developing methods for linear systems have steadily expanded to fit certain classes of nonlinear systems (such as polynomial).
We consider nonlinear system can be written in the following state-space representation

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{{\textbf{x}}}(t)&={\textbf{A}}{\textbf{x}}(t)+{\textbf{f}}({\textbf{x}}(t),{\textbf{u}}(t))+{\textbf{B}}u(t),\ \ y(t)={\textbf{C}}{\textbf{x}}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

where

Additionally, many useful phenomena of nonlinear nature can not be accurately approximated by means of linearization methods (that could be performed only locally). Two sub-class of polynomial models that belong to the class described in Eq. (

The operators that we will be concerned with in this study are given as follows:

•

Quadratic case:
{\textbf{f}}_{q}({\textbf{x}}(t),u(t))={\textbf{Q}}({\textbf{x}}(t)\otimes{\textbf{x}}(t))

\alpha f_{q}+\beta f_{b}

transformations. More specifically, auxiliary variables and equations are introduced in order to reformulate the equations in desired form. This allows to apply conventional MOR methods to more general nonlinear systems. Specific lifting transformations were discussed in

A viable alternative is to employ data-driven methods is the Loewner framework (LF), where the construction of low-order models can be achieved directly from data. It is to be noted that LF has been recently extended to fit certain classes of nonlinear systems from data, such as bilinear systems in

Another data-driven method that has emerged in recent years is OpInf, which uses time-domain state measurements (snapshots of the state variable), and then fits a particular nonlinear model (quadratic or quadratic-bilinear)
by computing the appropriate matrices.

inference method can be found in

]

2
Quadratic-bilinear systems and the Loewner framework

2.1
State-space format and properties of QB systems

We analyze in what follows dynamical systems as in (
2
), with quadratic-bilinear (QB) nonlinearities for which
f
is given as:
{\textbf{f}}({\textbf{x}}(t),u(t))={\textbf{Q}}({\textbf{x}}(t)\otimes{\textbf{x}}(t))+{\textbf{N}}{\textbf{x}}(t)u(t)
. More precisely, let the state-space representation of such a system be given as:

\small\left\{\begin{aligned} {\textbf{E}}\dot{{\textbf{x}}}(t)&={\textbf{A}}{\textbf{x}}(t)+{\textbf{Q}}({\textbf{x}}(t)\otimes{\textbf{x}}(t))+{\textbf{N}}{\textbf{x}}(t)u(t)+{\textbf{B}}u(t),\\
y(t)&={\textbf{C}}{\textbf{x}}(t),\end{aligned}\right.,

(3)

where
{\textbf{x}}(0)={\textbf{x}}_{0}=\textbf{0}
and the matrix
{\textbf{E}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is non-singular,
{\textbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n},~{\textbf{Q}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n^{2}},~{\textbf{N}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n},~{\textbf{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times 1}
and
{\textbf{C}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times n}
. Moreover, assume that
Q
satisfies the property
{\textbf{Q}}({\textbf{v}}\otimes{\textbf{w}})={\textbf{Q}}({\textbf{w}}\otimes{\textbf{v}})
, i.e., it is represented in a ”symmetrizable format”.

The first two generalized symmetric transfer functions functions of a QB system as in (
3
) (sometimes referred to as Volterra kernels in the frequency domain) are:

\displaystyle\small H_{1}(s_{1})

\displaystyle={\textbf{C}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{1}){\textbf{B}},

(4)

\displaystyle H_{2}(s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle={\textbf{C}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{1}+s_{2}){\textbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{1}){\textbf{B}}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{2}){\textbf{B}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\textbf{C}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{1}+s_{2}){\textbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{1}){\textbf{B}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s_{2}){\textbf{B}}).

For more details on deriving such functions we refer the reader to
[
8
]
and to
[
11
,
2
]
. An important property of these particular functions (sometimes called symmetric transfer functions), is that their samples can be inferred from the spectrum of the observed output when using a purely oscillatory control input.

2.2
The Loewner framework

We start with a brief summary of the Loenwer framework (LF) for fitting linear systems as in (
10
). For more details, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
. The starting point for LF is to collect measurements corresponding to the (first) transfer function, which can be inferred in practice from the first harmonic. The data are first partitioned into two disjoint subsets, as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\textrm{r}ight\ data}&:(\lambda_{j};w_{j}),~j=1,\ldots,k,~{\textrm{a}nd},\\
{\textrm{l}eft\ data}&:(\mu_{i};v_{i}),~i=1,\ldots,k,\end{split}

(5)

find the function

{\textbf{H}}(s)
, such that the following interpolation conditions are (approximately) fulfilled:

{\textbf{H}}(\mu_{i})=v_{i},~~~{\textbf{H}}(\lambda_{j})=w_{j}.

(6)

The Loewner matrix
{\mathbb{L}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k\times k}
and the shifted Loewner matrix
{\mathbb{L}}_{s}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k\times k}
are defined as follows

{\mathbb{L}}_{(i,j)}=\frac{v_{i}-w_{j}}{\mu_{i}-\lambda_{j}},\ {\mathbb{L}}_{s(i,j)}=\frac{\mu_{i}v_{i}-\lambda_{j}w_{j}}{\mu_{i}-\lambda_{j}},

(7)

while the data vectors
{\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{W}}^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
are introduced as

{\mathbb{V}}_{(i)}=v_{i},\ \ {\mathbb{W}}_{(j)}=w_{j},~\text{for}~i,j=1,\ldots,k.

(8)

The Loewner model is hence constructed as follows:

\displaystyle{\textbf{E}}=-{\mathbb{L}},~~{\textbf{A}}=-{\mathbb{L}}_{s},~~{\textbf{B}}={\mathbb{V}},~~{\textbf{C}}={\mathbb{W}}.

Provided that enough data is available, the pencil
({\mathbb{L}}_{s},\,{\mathbb{L}})
is often singular. In these cases, a singular value decomposition (SVD) of Loewner matrices is needed to compute projection matrices
{\textbf{X}}_{r},{\textbf{Y}}_{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{k\times r}
. Here,
r<n
represents the truncation index.

Then, the system matrices corresponding to a projected Loewner model of dimension
r
can be computed using matrices
{\textbf{X}}_{r}
and
{\textbf{Y}}_{r}
, as:

\small\hat{{\textbf{E}}}=-{\textbf{X}}_{r}^{*}{\mathbb{L}}{\textbf{Y}}_{r},\ \ \hat{{\textbf{A}}}=-{\textbf{X}}_{r}^{*}{\mathbb{L}}_{s}{\textbf{Y}}_{r},\ \ \hat{{\textbf{B}}}={\textbf{X}}_{r}^{*}{\mathbb{V}},\ \ \hat{{\textbf{C}}}={\mathbb{W}}{\textbf{Y}}_{r},

(9)

and hence, directly find a state-space realization corresponding to the reduced-order system of equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\hat{\textbf{E}}}\dot{{\hat{\textbf{x}}}}(t)&={\hat{\textbf{A}}}{\textbf{x}}(t)+{\hat{\textbf{B}}}u(t),\ \ \hat{y}(t)={\hat{\textbf{C}}}{\hat{\textbf{x}}}(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(10)

More implementation details and properties on the LF procedure can be found in
[
4
]
and in
[
15
]
.

3
The proposed hybrid method based on the Loewner framework and LS solves

The idea is to recover all the operators from measurements. The Loewner framework is capable of recovering the linear part by providing a fitted realization of dimension
r
as in (
9
), denoted with
({\hat{\textbf{E}}},{\hat{\textbf{A}}},{\hat{\textbf{B}}},{\hat{\textbf{C}}})
. Now, based on these matrices, introduce
{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(s)=(s{\hat{\textbf{E}}}-{\hat{\textbf{A}}})^{-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}
.

The sampling domain is denoted with
\Omega=\{(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}|1\leq i\leq K\}
.
For a particular pair of sampling points
(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, we define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\hat{\cal O}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})&:={\hat{\textbf{C}}}{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}+\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times r},\\
{\hat{\cal R}}_{q}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})&:=({\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{B}}}\otimes{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{2}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{B}}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r^{2}\times 1},\\
{\hat{\cal R}}_{b}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})&:=({\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{B}}}+{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{2}^{(i)}){\textbf{B}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times 1}.\end{split}

(11)

It is to be noted that the vectors introduced in (
11
) are computed solely in terms of the matrices
({\hat{\textbf{E}}},{\hat{\textbf{A}}},{\hat{\textbf{B}}},{\hat{\textbf{C}}})
corresponding to the data-driven Loewner surrogate model.
Let

be the vector of data measurements, i.e., containing samples of the second symmetric transfer function

evaluated on the
\Omega

. Now, since we would like to fit a reduced-order QB model to interpolate the 2D data, it follows that

\displaystyle\underbrace{{\hat{H}}_{2}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})}_{{\textbf{v}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}}=\underbrace{{\hat{\textbf{C}}}{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(s_{1}+s_{2})}_{{\hat{\cal O}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times r}}{\hat{\textbf{Q}}}\underbrace{({\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{B}}}\otimes{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(s_{2}){\hat{\textbf{B}}})}_{{\hat{\cal R}}_{q}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r^{2}\times 1}}

(12)

\displaystyle+(1/2)\underbrace{{\textbf{C}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}+\zeta_{2}^{(i)})}_{{\hat{\cal O}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times r}}{\hat{\textbf{N}}}\underbrace{({\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{B}}}+{\hat{\mathbf{\Phi}}}(\zeta_{2}^{(i)}){\textbf{B}})}_{{\hat{\cal R}}_{b}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times 1}},

\displaystyle={\hat{\cal O}}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)}){\hat{\textbf{Q}}}{\hat{\cal R}}_{q}(\zeta_{1}^{(i)},\zeta_{2}^{(i)})

such as

, it follows that we can put together a linear system of equations in

{\cal T}{\textbf{z}}={\textbf{v}},

where

[

]
, together with the Tikhonov regularization scheme
[

]
and the tQR (truncated QR decomposition) approach.

Finding a viable solution for the least-squares problem stated in (

(the column rank of matrix

4
A simple model of a non-linear circuit

We consider a simple circuit constructed from two blocks connected in series. Each block contains a capacitor in parallel with a diode. This is modeled as a dynamical system in two variables given by the voltage drops on each block. The input is given by the current through the circuit, while the observed output is the sum of the two variables. More precisely, we can write the differential equations characterizing the dynamics, as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} C_{1}\frac{dV_{1}(t)}{dt}&=I(t)-I_{r_{1}}\left(e^{\frac{1}{V_{t_{1}}}V_{1}(t)}-1\right),\\
C_{2}\frac{dV_{2}(t)}{dt}&=I(t)-I_{r_{2}}\left(e^{\frac{1}{V_{t_{2}}}V_{2}(t)}-1\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

while the output is
y(t)=V_{1}(t)+V_{2}(t)
. The capacitance values are denoted with
C_{i},1\leq i\leq 2
, while other constants are denoted with
I_{r_{i}}
and
V_{t_{i}}
. Next, we introduce
x_{1}(t)=V_{1}(t)/V_{t_{1}},~x_{2}(t)=V_{2}(t)/V_{t_{2}}
, and let
a=\frac{1}{C_{1}V_{t_{1}}},~b=\frac{1}{C_{2}V_{t_{2}}},~c=I_{r_{1}},~d=I_{r_{2}}
. Using all of these, the nonlinear system in (
20
) is rewritten as:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_{1}(t)&=aI(t)-ac\left(e^{x_{1}(t)}-1\right),\\
\dot{x}_{2}(t)&=bI(t)-bd\left(e^{x_{2}(t)}-1\right),\\
y(t)&=x_{1}(t)V_{t_{1}}+x_{2}(t)V_{t_{2}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

4.1
Analysis based on Taylor series truncation

The methods discussed here are inexact, i.e., based on approximation (e.g., on truncating the Taylor series associated to the nonlinearity). First, by using a truncated Taylor series given by the formula
e^{x_{i}(t)}\approx 1+x_{i}(t)
, and substitute it in (
21
), it follows that the following linear dynamical system is obtained:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_{1}(t)&=aI(t)-acx_{1}(t),\\
\dot{x}_{2}(t)&=bI(t)-bdx_{2}(t),\\
y(t)&=x_{1}(t)V_{t_{1}}+x_{2}(t)V_{t_{2}}+R\cdot I(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

Then, identify the following linear realization:

\displaystyle{\textbf{A}}_{L}=\left[\begin{matrix}-ac&0\\
0&-bd\end{matrix}\right],\ \ {\textbf{B}}_{L}=\left[\begin{matrix}a\\
b\end{matrix}\right],\ \ \ {\textbf{C}}_{L}=\left[\begin{matrix}V_{t_{1}}&V_{t_{2}}\end{matrix}\right].

Next, we discuss Carleman’s linearization proposed in
[
9
]
. It is a method used to embed a nonlinear system of differential equations of finite dimension
into a system of bilinear differential equations of infinite dimension. By truncating the hence obtained bilinear system at finite orders, one finds a systematic way of achieving an arbitrary-order approximation. By following this procedure, introduce the augmented new state by stopping at quadratic terms (and ignoring all other higher powers), as

{\textbf{x}}^{C}=\left[\begin{matrix}{\textbf{x}}\\
{\textbf{x}}\otimes{\textbf{x}}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}^{2}&x_{1}x_{2}&x_{2}x_{1}&x_{2}^{2}\end{matrix}\right]^{T},

(23)

and appropriately compute the derivatives of the last four entries as, for example, of:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}^{C}_{3}(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}x_{1}^{2}(t)=2x_{1}(t)\dot{x}_{1}(t)\\
&=2a{\textbf{x}}_{1}(t)I(t)-2acx_{1}^{2}(t)-acx_{1}^{3}(t).\end{split}

(24)

Finally, the approximation steps follows, i.e., we neglect the powers in
x_{i}(t)
higher than 3, and write:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}^{C}_{3}(t)&\approx 2ax_{1}(t)I(t)-2acx_{1}^{2}(t)\\
&\Rightarrow\dot{x}^{C}_{3}(t)\approx 2ax^{C}_{1}(t)I(t)-2acx_{2}^{C}(t),\end{split}

(25)

which includes a linear term, i.e.,
x_{2}^{C}(t)
, and a bilinear term:
x^{C}_{1}(t)I(t)
. The same procedure is applied for the other entries of the derivative of the new state vector
\dot{{\textbf{x}}}^{C}(t)
. Hence, an approximate bilinear systems is derived:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}^{C}(t)&={\textbf{A}}{\textbf{x}}^{C}(t)+{\textbf{N}}{\textbf{x}}^{C}(t)u(t)+{\textbf{B}}u(t),\ \ y(t)={\textbf{C}}{\textbf{x}}^{C}(t).\end{aligned}\right.

4.2
Polynomial lifting

The first step towards implementing a ”lifting approach” is to identify the quantities that depend non-linearly on the original states, and to consider them as new artificial states. For example, we introduce the auxiliary variables

x_{3}(t):=e^{x_{1}(t)}-1,\ \ \ \ x_{4}(t):=e^{x_{2}(t)}-1.

(26)

In this way, we also enforce zero initial conditions. The augmented system is hence written as:

\hskip 5.69054pt\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{x}_{1}(t)&=aI(t)-acx_{3}(t),\ \ \dot{x}_{2}(t)=bI(t)-bdx_{4}(t),\\
\dot{x}_{3}(t)&=-acx_{3}(t)-acx_{3}^{2}(t)+aI(t)+ax_{3}(t)I(t)\\
\dot{x}_{4}(t)&=-bdx_{4}(t)-bdx_{4}^{2}(t)+bI(t)+bx_{4}(t)I(t)\\
y(t)&=x_{1}(t)V_{t_{1}}+x_{2}(t)V_{t_{2}}+R\cdot I(t).\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

The system in (
27
) can be written in the general QB form:

6
Conclusion and future endeavours

A non-intrusive data-driven method that constructs nonlinear models with the quadratic-bilinear structure from input-output time-domain data was presented. The new method was based on the Loewner and Volterra frameworks. The second symmetric kernel can be inferred as the
2
nd harmonic of the transformed output and hence, the problem of estimating the nonlinear operators can be resolved into a linear LS system. Although this LS system may be under-determined, it contains enough information for estimating the nonlinear operators with the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse (truncated SVD), thus providing good approximations. The use of higher harmonics (e.g.,
3
rd kernel, etc.) that results in a nonlinear optimization problem along with the use of other regularization techniques as the one proposed by Tikhonov, will be the topics of future research endeavors in connection also with the analysis on the sensitivity of noise that real data contain.
