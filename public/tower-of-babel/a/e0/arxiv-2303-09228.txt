NMR in 3 He-B
V. V. Zavjalov

Abstract
This text contains a collection of equations useful for understanding Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) experiments in superfluid 3 He-B. This is a part of my notebook where I try to describe some parts of this sophisticated system.



Introduction

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) in superfluid
3
He is a very powerful
tool used for studying this system since its discovery in 1972.
Superfluidity in fermionic
3
He is formed via Cooper pairing of atoms, with
pairs having spin 1 and orbital momentum 1. As a result the order
parameter of the system includes both spin and orbital degrees of freedom
and can be written as 3x3 complex matrix. A few different superfluid
phases with different broken symmetries are possible. For the B-phase
degenerate space of the order parameter includes an arbitrary 3D rotation
matrix which describes fixed mutual orientation of spin and orbital
spaces. In NMR experiments we observe motion of this matrix, which means
three degrees of freedom and three spin-wave modes. This is different
from many other magnetic materials where we study motion of magnetization
vector with only two degrees of freedom. Another important feature of
this system is possibility of non-uniform spatial distribution and
topological defects in the order-parameter field (so-called textures).
This can be clearly observed in linear NMR where small oscillations of
the order parameter are happening around the equilibrium texture. One
more feature of this system is spin-orbit interaction. It introduces an
additional non-linear force acting on the order parameter and leads to
important effects such as longitudinal NMR or Homogeneously Precessing
Domain (HPD).

This text contains two almost independent parts. The first one is about
linear NMR. It was written during my work in Vladimir Eltsov’s group in
Aalto University (Finland) in 2012-2015. We had a really nice
experimental system for measuring optical magnons trapped in a harmonic
potential formed by the order parameter texture and magnetic field. It was
possible to control shape of the trap and population of individual levels
in it, to observe Bose condensation of magnons, parametric excitation of
other spin-wave modes, interaction of the magnon condensate with texture,
quantized vortices, and free surface of helium. I would like to thank
Petri Heikkinen, Samuli Autti, and Jere Mäkinen who also worked on this
project.

The second part contains Leggett equations for non-linear
NMR and many things related to Homogeneously
Precessing Domain (HPD), a unique coherent
state which is also a very useful tool
for various
3
He studies. I worked with HPD in Vladimir Dmitriev’s
group in Kapitza Institute (Russia) in 2000-2006, and in Pertti Hakonen’s
group in Aalto University in 2016-2019.

The text contains many references to Vollhardt and Wölfle
book
[
1

number>). Many derivations require straightforward but huge

Part 1. Linear spin waves in
3
He-B

At energy scales much less then energy gap
\Delta
the state of
superfluid
3
He-B is described by the order parameter:

A_{aj}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\ \Delta\ e^{i\varphi}R_{aj}

(1)

where
\varphi
is phase, and
R_{aj}

is a rotation matrix which can be written in terms of rotation axis
\bf n

and rotation angle
\vartheta
as

R_{aj}=\cos\vartheta\ \delta_{aj}+(1-\cos\vartheta)\ n_{a}n_{j}-\sin\vartheta\ e_{ajk}n_{k}.

(2)

In small magnetic fields,
\hbar\gamma H\ll\Delta
the gap
\Delta
and
magnetic susceptibility
\chi_{B}
are isotropic. There are four degrees of
freedom in the system, the phase
\varphi
, the unit vector
\bf n
and
the angle
\vartheta
. Oscillations of the phase is sound (here we do not
consider normal component and thus have only one sound mode), and
oscillations of the
R_{aj}({\bf n},\vartheta)
matrix are spin waves. In
the following discussion we are interested only in these three spin-wave
modes. Hamiltonian of the system can be written as a combination of three
components: magnetic energy, energy of spin-orbit interaction and
gradient energy (see VW 6.103, VW 7.17).

\mathcal{H}=F_{M}+F_{SO}+F_{\nabla},

(3)

\displaystyle F_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-({\bf S}\cdot\gamma{\bf H})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\chi_{B}}{\bf S}^{2},

(4)

\displaystyle F_{SO}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{D}\Delta^{2}\Big[R_{jj}R_{kk}+R_{jk}R_{kj}-\frac{2}{3}R_{jk}R_{jk}\Big]=g_{D}\Delta^{2}\Big[R_{jj}R_{kk}+R_{jk}R_{kj}]+\mbox{const.},

(5)

\displaystyle F_{\nabla}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}\Big[K_{1}(\nabla_{j}R_{ak})(\nabla_{j}R_{ak})+K_{2}(\nabla_{j}R_{ak})(\nabla_{k}R_{aj})+K_{3}(\nabla_{j}R_{aj})(\nabla_{k}R_{ak})\Big],

(6)

where
\bf S
is spin and
\bf H
is magnetic field.

We are going to write equations of motion for this system. Spin is a not
a canonical variable, its components do not commute with each other and
thus we have to use equations with Poisson brackets. In this approach
evolution of any parameter
a
can be written as
\dot{a}=\{\mathcal{H},a\}
. Poisson brackets can be found from microscopic
considerations, from commutation rules in quantum mechanics, or from
symmetry
[
4
,
5
]
.

The matrix
R_{aj}
for any value of index
j
can be treated as a
vector in spin space. For such a vector commutation rules can be
written as:

\{S_{a},S_{b}\}=-e_{abc}S_{c},\quad\{R_{aj},S_{b}\}=\{S_{a},R_{bj}\}=-e_{abc}R_{cj},\quad\{R_{aj},R_{bk}\}=0.

(7)

It is possible to write equations of motion for the spin
\bf S
and
matrix
R_{aj}
(see later, in the second part of this text), but here we
will use small rotations
\theta^{s}
of the spin space as coordinates.

Using small rotations
\theta^{s}_{a}
as coordinates

We follow derivation of Theodorakis and Fetter

[
6
]
, see also VW 8.4.6.
Consider some equilibrium distribution of the order parameter matrix

R^{0}
. We are going to study small oscillations around the equilibrium.
Arbitrary change of the rotation matrix can be represented as an
additional rotation:

R_{aj}=R_{ab}({\bf\theta^{s}})R^{0}_{bj}.

(8)

Here
\theta^{s}
is a vector and
R_{ab}(\theta^{s})
is a rotation matrix around the
direction of
\theta^{s}
by angle
|\theta^{s}|
. We will use small rotations,

|\theta^{s}|\ll 1
. Using formula (
2
) for
R_{ab}(\theta^{s})
one can write
up to second order terms in
|\theta^{s}|
:

R_{aj}\quad=\quad\left(\delta_{ab}-e_{abc}\theta^{s}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{s}_{a}\theta^{s}_{b}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ab}\theta^{s}_{c}\theta^{s}_{c}+o({\theta^{s}}^{2})\right)R^{0}_{bj}

(9)

The Hamiltonian can be written as a function of the spin and angles
\theta^{s}
.
Poisson brackets in the equations of motion can be expanded as

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H},S_{a}\}\quad=\quad\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta S_{b}}\{S_{b},S_{a}\}+\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\theta^{s}_{b}}\{\theta^{s}_{b},S_{a}\}

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}^{s}_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H},\theta^{s}_{a}\}\quad=\quad\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta S_{b}}\{S_{b},\theta^{s}_{a}\}+\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\theta^{s}_{b}}\{\theta^{s}_{b},\theta^{s}_{a}\}

(11)

We are going to obtain linear equations for small
\theta^{s}
. Derivatives of
the Hamiltonian are zero in the equilibrium (energy is in the minimum
at
\theta^{s}=0
) and thus we have only first-order terms there. Thus in the
Poisson brackets we need only zero-order terms. As the matrix
R_{aj}
is a
function of
\theta^{s}
one can write

\{R_{aj},S_{b}\}=\frac{dR_{aj}}{d\theta^{s}_{c}}\{\theta^{s}_{c},S_{b}\}.

(12)

Using (
7
) and (
9
) one can find commutation
rules for our set of coordinates:

\{S_{a},S_{b}\}=-e_{abc}S_{c},\quad\{\theta^{s}_{a},S_{b}\}=-\{S_{a},\theta^{s}_{b}\}=-\delta_{ab},\quad\{\theta^{s}_{a},\theta^{s}_{b}\}=0.

(13)

It is easy to find the derivative
\delta\mathcal{H}/\delta S_{b}
. Using it
and the commutation rules we have:

\displaystyle\dot{\bf S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[{\bf S}\times\gamma{\bf H}]-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta{\bf\theta^{s}}}

(14)

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{\theta}^{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\frac{\gamma}{\chi_{B}}{\bf S}-{\bf H}\right)\quad=\quad\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}{\bf\delta S}

(15)

where we introduced
\delta{\bf S}
, deviation of the spin from its
equilibrium value
{\bf S^{0}}=\chi_{B}{\bf H}/\gamma
. These equations
describe a simple Larmor precession with the additional term

-\delta\mathcal{H}/\delta\theta^{s}
— a torque acting on the spin because
of gradient and spin-orbit interactions. This derivative is not trivial,
and will be calculated below.

Variational derivative for small rotations

Since the energy depends on both angles
\theta^{s}_{a}
and their gradients

\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}
we have to use a so-called variational derivative

\delta\mathcal{H}/\delta\theta^{s}_{a}

[
7
]
. An additional
difficulty appears because rotations
\theta^{s}
do not commute. Let us take
this into account. Using formula (
9
) one can check that two
small successive rotations,
\theta_{A}
and then
\theta_{B}
are equivalent to the
rotation by the angle
\theta_{A}+\theta_{B}+\frac{1}{2}\theta_{A}\times\theta_{B}
up to second
order terms in the angles:

R_{ac}(\theta_{B})R_{cb}(\theta_{A})\quad=\quad R_{ab}\left(\theta_{A}+\theta_{B}+\frac{1}{2}[\theta_{A}\times\theta_{B}]+o(\theta_{A}^{2},\theta_{B}^{2})\right).

(16)

Two rotations done in a different order produce a difference
[\theta_{A}\times\theta_{B}]
.
This can be written as a commutation rule for differentials (see VW 9.14):

\delta\nabla\theta^{s}-\nabla\delta\theta^{s}=[\delta\theta^{s}\times\nabla\theta^{s}].

(17)

Consider a small non-uniform rotation,
\delta\theta^{s}({\bf r})
,
with boundary condition
\delta\theta^{s}=0
which changes gradients by
\delta\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}
.
The functional derivative
\delta\mathcal{H}/\delta\theta^{s}_{a}
is given by

\int_{V}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\theta^{s}_{a}}\delta\theta^{s}_{a}\ dr=\int_{V}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta^{s}_{a}}\ \delta\theta^{s}_{a}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}}\ \delta\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}\right)\ dr

(18)

From mechanical point of view this means that work produced against a
torque
T_{a}=-\delta\mathcal{H}/\delta\theta^{s}_{a}
equals to the total change
of energy. Now we swap differentials
\delta\nabla\theta^{s}
using the
formula (
17
) and integrate this term by parts using the
zero boundary condition.

\int_{V}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\theta^{s}_{a}}\delta\theta^{s}_{a}\ dr=\int_{V}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta^{s}_{a}}\ \delta\theta^{s}_{a}-\nabla_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}}\ \delta\theta^{s}_{a}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}}e_{abc}\ \delta\theta^{s}_{b}\ \nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{c}\right)dr

(19)

This equation is true for any possible variations
\delta\theta^{s}
. This means that
the integrands are equal at any point and the derivative is:

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\theta^{s}_{a}}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta^{s}_{a}}-\nabla_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{a}}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{c}}e_{abc}\ \nabla_{j}\theta^{s}_{b}

(20)

Uniform precession

If we neglect all gradient terms then
\Lambda_{ab}=-\Omega_{B}^{2}n_{a}n_{b}
and
equation (
28
) is (compare with
[
8
]
):

\omega^{6}-\omega^{4}\big(\omega_{L}^{2}+\Omega_{B}^{2}\big)+\omega^{2}\omega_{L}^{2}\Omega_{B}^{2}n_{z}^{2}=0.

(34)

NMR experiments are often done at a fixed frequency
\omega
. We can solve the equation
for
\gamma H=\omega_{L}
and find magnetic field where the resonance is observed:

\gamma H=\omega\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}-\Omega_{B}^{2}}{\omega^{2}-\Omega_{B}^{2}n_{z}^{2}}}.

(35)

At a fixed magnetic field we solve the equation for
\omega
and find three spin-wave modes:

\omega^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\omega_{L}^{2}+\Omega_{B}^{2})\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(\omega_{L}^{2}+\Omega_{B}^{2})^{2}-\omega_{L}^{2}\Omega_{B}^{2}n_{z}^{2}},\qquad\omega^{2}=0

(36)

For
\gamma H\gg\Omega_{B}
we have a well-known expressions for transverse and longitudinal NMR frequency:

\omega=\gamma H+\frac{\Omega_{B}^{2}}{2\gamma H}\ (1-n_{z}^{2}),\qquad\omega=\Omega_{B}\ n_{z},\qquad\omega=0,

(37)

Here meaning of
\Omega_{B}
becomes clear, it is frequency of the
longitudinal NMR in a texture with
\bf n||H
.

Separating equations for transverse and longitudinal magnons

Let’s use complex
spherical coordinates
.
For an arbitrary vector
A

A_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_{x}\pm iA_{y}),\quad A_{0}=A_{z},\qquad A_{i}B_{i}=A_{q}B^{*}_{q},\quad\mbox{where $q=0,+,-$}

(38)

In terms of polar and azimuthal angles
\alpha
and
\beta

A_{\pm}=\frac{|A|}{\sqrt{2}}\sin\beta\exp(\pm i\alpha),\qquad A_{0}=|A|\cos\beta

(39)

If field

[A\times H]_{q}=-qiA_{q}H
.
Now one can write the equation in spherical coordinates for a harmonic

\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\chi_{B}}\big[-K\ \delta_{qp}\nabla_{j}\nabla_{j}+K^{\prime}\ R^{0}_{qj}R^{0}_{p^{*}k}\nabla_{j}\nabla_{k}\big]+\Omega_{B}^{2}\ n_{q}n_{p}^{*}\right]s_{p}=\omega(\omega-q\gamma H)s_{q}

\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}}{\chi_{B}}\big[-K\ \nabla_{j}\nabla_{j}+K^{\prime}\ R^{0}_{qj}R^{0}_{q^{*}k}\nabla_{j}\nabla_{k}\big]+\Omega_{B}^{2}\ |n_{q}|^{2}\right]s_{q}=\omega(\omega-q\gamma H)s_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(\omega-\gamma H)\ s_{+}

Spin-wave velocities in a weak coupling approximation

Gradient energy coefficients
have been obtained in
[
9
]
(see also VW 7.25).

2K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}=-\frac{2}{\Delta^{2}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}(4+\delta)c,\qquad K_{2}=-\frac{2}{\Delta^{2}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}c,\qquad K_{3}=-\frac{2}{\Delta^{2}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}(1+\delta)c

c_{\parallel}^{2}=C_{\perp}^{2}=-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}(4+\delta)c,\quad c_{\perp}^{2}=-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}(3+\delta/2)c,\quad C_{\parallel}^{2}=-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2m}\right)^{2}2c

Without Fermi liquid corrections

, and motion of
\vartheta

is is small if the texture is close to the Leggett configuration

In the equilibrium dipolar energy stays at the minimum, where

(so-called Leggett angle). In the spin wave it goes out of the minimum

\langle F_{D}-F_{D}^{0}\rangle=\frac{15}{4}g_{D}\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma H}{\omega}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\beta_{N}\ \beta_{M}^{2}

\bf\hat{S}^{0}=\hat{z}

\cos\beta_{L}=\hat{L}^{0}_{z}=R^{0}_{zz}=\cos\vartheta+(1-\cos\vartheta)\cos^{2}\beta_{N}.

Schrödinger equation for magnons

Consider a long-wave optical magnons with frequencies

\omega\approx\gamma H
in the texture where
\bf n
is close to
vertical. In this case we can write equation for transverse spin
waves (
43
) in a form of a Schrödinger equation for
magnon quasiparticles, where complex transverse spin
s_{+}
plays role
of a wave function and precessing frequency
\omega
plays role of an
energy. Effect of texture on the gradient terms is neglected because
it adds only a small correction to the total gradient energy. The dipolar
energy, which also depends on texture, can be of the same order as the total
gradient energy.

\left[-\frac{c_{\perp}^{2}}{\gamma H}\ (\nabla_{x}^{2}+\nabla_{y}^{2})-\frac{c_{\parallel}^{2}}{\gamma H}\ \nabla_{z}^{2}+\frac{\Omega_{B}^{2}}{2\gamma H}\sin^{2}\beta_{N}+\gamma H\right]s_{+}=\omega\ s_{+}

(62)

s_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ \frac{\chi_{B}H}{\gamma}\sin\beta_{M}\ e^{i(\alpha_{M}+\omega t)}

(63)

Both texture (spatial distribution of
\beta_{N}
) and magnetic field form
a potential for magnons. It our experiment we used a combined effect of
a flare-out texture in the cylindrical cell and non-uniform field of
a small longitudinal coil to create a harmonic energy trap for magnons.

Magnons in a harmonic trap

Schrödinger equation in a cylindrical harmonic potential can be solved analytically.
Let us write it in the form:

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{\perp}}\ (\nabla_{x}^{2}+\nabla_{y}^{2})-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{\parallel}}\ \nabla_{z}^{2}+\hbar\omega_{0}+\frac{m_{\perp}\omega_{r}^{2}}{2}\ r^{2}+\frac{m_{\parallel}\omega_{z}^{2}}{2}\ z^{2}\right]s_{+}=\hbar\omega\ s_{+}

(64)

where parameters
\omega_{0}
,
\omega_{r}
and
\omega_{z}
describes the potential and
masses
m_{\perp,\parallel}
in the case of magnons are:

m_{\perp,\parallel}=\frac{\hbar}{2c_{\perp,\parallel}^{2}}\gamma H.

(65)

Then eigenvalues are

\omega=\omega_{0}+\omega_{r}(2n_{r}+|n_{\phi}|+1)+\omega_{z}(n_{z}+1/2),

(66)

for
n_{r},n_{z}=0,1,2\ldots
,
n_{\phi}=0,\pm 1,\pm 2\ldots
,
and normalized solutions are:

s_{+}=s_{+}^{r}(r)\ s_{+}^{\phi}(\phi)\ s_{+}^{z}(z),

(67)

where

\displaystyle s_{+}^{r}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{r}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ n_{r}!}{(n_{r}+|n_{\phi}|)!}}\ \left(\frac{r}{a_{r}}\right)^{|n_{\phi}|}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2a_{r}^{2}}\right)L_{n_{r}}^{|n_{\phi}|}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{a_{r}^{2}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle s_{+}^{\phi}(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(in_{\phi}\phi)

(69)

\displaystyle s_{+}^{z}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sqrt{\frac{1}{a_{z}\sqrt{\pi}\ 2^{n_{z}}\ n_{z}!}}\ \exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2a_{z}^{2}}\right)H_{n_{z}}\left(\frac{z}{a_{z}}\right),

L_{n}^{m}(x)
and
H_{n}(x)
are Laguerre and Hermite polynomials:

L_{n}^{m}(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\ x^{-m}\ e^{x}\ \frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(x^{n+m}\ e^{-x}),\qquad H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}\ e^{x^{2}}\ \frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\ e^{-x^{2}},

(70)

and
a_{r}
and
a_{z}
are sizes of the wave:

a_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m_{\perp}\omega_{r}}}=c_{\perp}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{r}\gamma H}},\qquad a_{z}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m_{\parallel}\omega_{z}}}=c_{\parallel}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{z}\gamma H}}.

(71)

Usually we are interested only in non-antisymmetric states which
have non-zero integral transverse magnetization and can be excited and
observed in NMR experiments. For these states
n_{\phi}=0
,
n_{z}=0,2,4\ldots
, and

s_{+}(r,z)=\ \left(\pi^{3/2}a_{r}^{2}a_{z}\ 2^{n_{z}}\ n_{z}!\right)^{-1/2}\ \exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2a_{r}^{2}}-\frac{z^{2}}{2a_{z}^{2}}\right)L_{n_{r}}^{0}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{a_{r}^{2}}\right)H_{n_{z}}\left(\frac{z}{a_{z}}\right),

(72)

\omega=\omega_{0}+\omega_{r}(2n_{r}+1)+\omega_{z}(n_{z}+1/2),\qquad n_{r}=0,1,2\ldots,n_{z}=0,2,4\ldots

(73)

{\bf w}=R{\bf\hat{z}}+\frac{1}{\omega}\left[{\bf n}\ \dot{\vartheta}+\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\sin\vartheta}\ (R{\bf\dot{n}}+{\bf\dot{n}})\right]

(106)

Or in original notation:

\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}{\bf S}=\gamma{\bf H}+{\bf n}\ \dot{\vartheta}+(1-\cos\vartheta)\ {\bf n}\times[{\bf\dot{n}}+\omega{\bf n}\times{\bf\hat{z}}]+\sin\vartheta\ [{\bf\dot{n}}+\omega{\bf n}\times{\bf\hat{z}}]

(107)

\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}{\bf S}=\gamma{\bf H}+\omega\ (R{\bf\hat{z}}-{\bf\hat{z}})+{\bf n}\ \dot{\vartheta}+\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\sin\vartheta}\ (R{\bf\dot{n}}+{\bf\dot{n}})

(108)

Non-uniform equilibrium in the rotating frame

Equilibrium state can be found by setting all time derivatives to zero.
From (
106
) we have

w_{a}=R_{a3},

(109)

it means that in the equilibrium
|{\bf w}|=1
.

Using this equation we can exclude
\bf w
from the first equation and
write equilibrium problem using only
R_{aj}
(or
\bf n
and
\vartheta
) coordinates:

e_{abc}R_{bj}\Big[\frac{\omega\chi_{B}}{\gamma^{2}\Delta^{2}}\left(\delta_{c3}\,(\omega_{z}-\omega)+\delta_{c1}\,\omega_{x}\right)\delta_{j3}-2g_{D}\ (\delta_{cj}R_{kk}+R_{jc})+K_{1}R_{cj}^{\prime\prime}+(K_{2}+K_{3})\delta_{j3}R_{c3}^{\prime\prime}\Big]=0.

(110)

Here we use expressions (
85
) for dipolar and gradient
torques and keep only gradients in
\bf\hat{z}
direction, represented by
primes.

This can be written in term of Leggett frequency and spin-wave velocities:

e_{abc}R_{bj}\Big[\omega(\left(\omega_{z}-\omega)\ \delta_{c3}+\omega_{x}\ \delta_{c1}\right)\delta_{j3}-\frac{2}{15}\Omega_{B}^{2}\ (\delta_{cj}R_{kk}+R_{jc})+\frac{1}{2}C_{\parallel}^{2}R_{cj}^{\prime\prime}+(C_{\perp}^{2}-C_{\parallel}^{2})\delta_{j3}R_{c3}^{\prime\prime}\Big]=0.

(111)

This is a set of three equations for three parameters (
\bf n
and
\vartheta
). It
describes non-uniform texture of a precessing state and
can be used for calculating HPD boundaries or solitons.

Uniform equilibrium

Let’s remove gradient terms in the first equation
in (
104
) and use expression (
100
) for
dipolar torque:

{\bf w}\times({\bf\hat{z}}\,(\omega_{z}-\omega)+{\bf\hat{x}}\,\omega_{x})=\frac{4}{15}\frac{\Omega_{B}^{2}}{\omega}\ \sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)\ {\bf n},

(112)

By multiplying it by
\bf w
we have left-hand side equals zero and three cases:

{\bf n}\cdot{\bf w}=0
, or
\cos\vartheta=-1/4
, or
\sin\vartheta=0
.
The last one is unstable equilibrium, it corresponds to maximum of
dipolar energy. Two other cases are called HPD and NPD, now we can
find them.

HPD (Homogeneously-precessing domain):

We already showed in (
109
) that

{\bf w}=R{\bf\hat{z}}
. Using this, (
112
), and HPD condition
{\bf n}\cdot{\bf w}=0

we can find

{\bf n}=\pm{\bf\hat{y}},\qquad{\bf w}=\cos\vartheta\ {\bf\hat{z}}+n_{y}\sin\vartheta\ {\bf\hat{x}}.

(113)

\cos\vartheta=-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{15}{16}\frac{\omega}{\Omega_{B}^{2}}\ \left((\omega-\omega_{z})+n_{y}\frac{\cos\vartheta}{\sin\vartheta}\,\omega_{x}\right).

(114)

We can use only positive sign of
\vartheta
because matrix
R({\bf n},\vartheta)
is same as
R(-{\bf n},-\vartheta)
. Later it will be shown
that state with
\vartheta>0
and
{\bf n}=-{\bf\hat{y}}
is unstable.
To check it we will keep parameter
n_{y}=\pm 1
, which can be always set to 1
for HPD equilibrium state.

Usually RF-pumping
\omega_{x}
is much smaller then frequency shift
\omega-\omega_{z}
,

\cos\vartheta
is close to -1/4 and we can use
\cos\vartheta/\sin\vartheta=-1/\sqrt{15}
in the right part
of the last equation:

\cos\vartheta=-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{15}{16}\frac{\omega}{\Omega_{B}^{2}}\ \left(\omega-\omega_{z}-\frac{n_{y}}{\sqrt{15}}\,\omega_{x}\right).

(115)

In arbitrary case to find
\vartheta
we can do substitutions

t=\tan\vartheta/2,\qquad A=\frac{15}{16}\frac{\omega(\omega-\omega_{z})}{\Omega_{B}^{2}},\qquad B=\frac{15}{16}\frac{\omega}{\Omega_{B}^{2}}n_{y}\,\omega_{x},

(116)

and obtaining 4-th order equation for
t
:

Bt^{4}-\frac{1}{2}(3+4A)t^{3}+\frac{1}{2}(5-4A)t-B=0.

(117)

NPD (Non-precessing domain), Brinkman-Smith mode:

Using the NPD condition
\cos\vartheta=-1/4
we have:

w_{1}=\frac{-\omega_{x}}{\sqrt{\omega_{x}^{2}+(\omega_{z}-\omega)^{2}}},\qquad w_{2}=0,\qquad w_{3}=\frac{-(\omega_{z}-\omega)}{\sqrt{\omega_{x}^{2}+(\omega_{z}-\omega)^{2}}},

(118)

n_{1}=\frac{w_{1}n_{3}}{1+w_{3}},\qquad n_{2}=\ \frac{w_{1}\sqrt{3/5}}{1+w_{3}},\qquad n_{3}=\pm(4w_{3}+1)/5.

(119)

(There is also solution with opposite signs of
w_{1}
and
w_{3}
which
corresponds to unstable equilibrium).

Here
\bf n
is precessing around
\bf\hat{z}
axis. This is
case of a “vertical” texture. In real NMR experiments if RF pumping is
small enough then texture is determined by boundary conditions in the
experimental cell and possible topological defects such as
\bf n
-solitons. It can have any equilibrium orientation of
\bf n
vector
and small oscillations around this equilibrium. These solutions can not
be found as an equilibrium in our rotating frame.

Small oscillations of HPD in the rotating frame

This section follows calculations in my PhD thesis (2012, available only
in Russian).

Let’s again consider a uniform case with zero gradient terms. Introduce
dimensionless parameters:

Then equations (

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ {\bf n}\times({\bf w}+{\bf\hat{z}})-\frac{1}{2}\ \frac{\sin\vartheta}{1-\cos\vartheta}\ \Big[{\bf n}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-{\bf\hat{z}}))-({\bf w}-{\bf\hat{z}})\Big],

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-{\bf\hat{z}}),

Let’s rewrite equation (

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{x}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F\,b\ n_{x}-w_{y}d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F\,b\ n_{y}+w_{z}h+w_{x}d

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{z}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F\,b\ n_{z}-w_{y}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{y}(w_{z}+1)+n_{z}w_{y}-C\ n_{x}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-{\bf\hat{z}}))+C\ w_{x}

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-{\bf\hat{z}}).

where

(124)

\dot{\bf n}

. This
can be checked by calculating

Lets write the equations for small deviations

from the equilibrium. Here we keep only first-order terms with the deviations.
Also we put

and omit equation for

\displaystyle d\dot{w}_{x}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{0}\,b\ dn_{x}-d\,dw_{y},

(125)

\displaystyle d\dot{w}_{y}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dF\,b\ n^{0}_{y}+h\,dw_{z}+d\,dw_{x},

\displaystyle d\dot{w}_{z}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{0}\,b\ dn_{z}-h\,dw_{y},

\displaystyle 2d\dot{n}_{x}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n^{0}_{y}\ dw_{z}+C^{0}\ dw_{x}+w^{0}_{x}\ dC,

\displaystyle 2d\dot{n}_{z}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{0}_{y}\ dw_{x}+C^{0}\ dw_{z}+(w_{z}-1)\ dC,

\displaystyle w^{0}_{x}\ dn_{x}+(w^{0}_{z}-1)\ dn_{z}+n^{0}_{y}\ dw_{y}.

\left|\begin{array}[]{rrrrrr}-ix&-d&0&b\,F^{0}&0&0\\

where

Leggett-Takagi relaxation

Leggett-Takagi equations
[
13
]
can be made from Leggett equations (
91
)
by adding a relaxation term with parameter
\tau
to the equation for
\dot{\vartheta}
.

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot\Big(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}{\bf S}-\gamma{\bf H}\Big)+\frac{4}{15}\ \sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)\ \frac{1}{\tau}.

(130)

Switching to the rotating frame and using dimensionless parameters
{\bf w},d,h,b
(
103
,
120
) we can write equations of
motion (
121
) with the relaxation term:

\displaystyle\dot{\bf w}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf w}\times(-d\,\hat{\bf z}+h\,\hat{\bf x})+\frac{4}{15}b\ \sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)\ {\bf n}

(131)

\displaystyle\dot{\bf n}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\ {\bf n}\times({\bf w}+\hat{\bf z})-\frac{1}{2}\ \frac{\sin\vartheta}{1-\cos\vartheta}\ \Big[{\bf n}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z}))-({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z})\Big]

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z})+\frac{4}{15}\ \sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)\ \frac{1}{\omega\tau},

or in coordinates with
C
and
F
defined in (
124
):

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{x}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\ w_{y}+bF\ n_{x}

(132)

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{y}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\ w_{z}+d\ w_{x}+bF\ n_{y}

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{z}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-h\ w_{y}+bF\ n_{z}

\displaystyle 2\dot{n}_{x}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{y}(w_{z}+1)+n_{z}w_{y}-C\ n_{x}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z}))+Cw_{x}

\displaystyle 2\dot{n}_{y}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{z}w_{x}+n_{x}(w_{z}+1)-C\ n_{y}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z}))+Cw_{y}

\displaystyle 2\dot{n}_{z}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{x}w_{y}+n_{y}w_{x}-C\ n_{z}\ ({\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z}))+C(w_{z}-1)

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}/\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf n}\cdot({\bf w}-\hat{\bf z})+\frac{F}{\omega\tau}.

For the equilibrium state we have:

{\bf n}^{0}\cdot{\bf w}^{0}=0,\qquad n^{0}_{z}=\frac{F^{0}}{\omega\tau},\qquad n^{0}_{x}=\frac{d}{h}\ \frac{F^{0}}{\omega\tau},\qquad w^{0}_{y}=\frac{b}{h}\ \frac{(F^{0})^{2}}{\omega\tau}.

(133)

One can see that equilibrium position of vector
\bf n
is rotated from

{\bf\hat{y}}
direction by Leggett-Takagi relaxation, and vector
{\bf w}^{0}
stays perpendicular to
{\bf n}^{0}
. Note that even at low
relaxation
n^{0}_{x}
can be large because of
d/h
factor. This probably
determines maximum frequency shift where HPD can exist for a given RF
pumping and Leggett-Takagi relaxation. It is very interesting to measure
this effect in experiment.

\vartheta
-solitons

Minimum of spin-orbit interaction corresponds to
\cos\vartheta=-1/4
. It is
possible to have a so-called theta-soliton
[
14
]

there
\vartheta
changes between two minima,
\vartheta_{L}=\cos^{-1}(-1/4)
and
2\pi-\vartheta_{L}
, as a solution of equilibrium
Leggett equations.

Let’s assume
{\bf n}=\hat{\bf z}
,
{\bf H}=H_{z}\hat{\bf z}
, and
{\bf S}=\chi_{B}{\bf H}/\gamma
. Only
\vartheta
is changing along
\hat{\bf z}
axis.

Using usual expression
R_{aj}=\cos\vartheta\ \delta_{aj}+(1-\cos\vartheta)\ n_{a}n_{j}-\sin\vartheta\ e_{ajk}n_{k}
,
and
n_{j}=\delta_{j3}
one can find that only
J_{33}
component of spin current is non-zero:

\displaystyle J_{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\Delta^{2}K_{1}\vartheta^{\prime}

(134)

Second and third of Leggett equations are satisfied by chosen
values of
{\bf n}
and
{\bf S}
. The first equation gives
non-trivial expression for distribution of
\vartheta
:

T^{D}_{3}=J_{33}^{\prime}

(135)

By substituting spin current and dipolar torque we obtain a second-order
differential equation for
\vartheta
:

\vartheta^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{2g_{D}}{K1}\ \sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)

(136)

or with usual definition of dipolar length
\xi_{D}^{2}=\frac{13}{24}\frac{K_{1}}{g_{D}}
.

\frac{12}{13}\xi_{D}^{2}\ \vartheta^{\prime\prime}=-\sin\vartheta(4\cos\vartheta+1)

(137)

To solve this equation we multiply both sides by
\vartheta^{\prime}
and integrate using
boundary conditions at
z=\pm\infty
:
\cos\vartheta=-1/4
,
\vartheta^{\prime}=0
:

\sqrt{\frac{12}{13}}\xi_{D}\ \vartheta^{\prime}=\pm 2(\cos\vartheta+1/4)

(138)

Second integration will give us formula of the
\vartheta
-soliton.
Integration constant determines position of the soliton, we choose it to
localize the soliton at
z=0
. There are two solutions, one goes from

-\theta_{L}
to
+\theta_{L}
, another from
+\theta_{L}
to
2\pi-\theta_{L}
.
To find the second solution one can make substitution
\bar{\theta}=\theta-\pi
and do similar integration. Result is:

\vartheta=2\tan^{-1}\left[\sqrt{\frac{5}{3}}\tanh\left(\sqrt{\frac{65}{64}}\ \frac{\pm z}{\xi_{D}}\right)\right],\qquad\vartheta=\pi+2\tan^{-1}\left[\sqrt{\frac{3}{5}}\tanh\left(\sqrt{\frac{65}{64}}\ \frac{\pm z}{\xi_{D}}\right)\right].

(139)

The first solution is not topologically protected because it is possible
to move from
-\theta_{L}
to
+\theta_{L}
by rotating
\bf n
vector,
always staying in the minimum of spin-orbit interaction. If vector
\bf n
is oriented along
\bf\hat{z}
for example by magneto-dipolar energy
(not discussed in this text yet) or by spin precession, it will form a
so-called
\bf n
-soliton which has much smaller energy and bigger size.
The second solution is the
\vartheta
-soliton.

\vartheta
-solitons can exist in HPD state. In this case they have small
core where
\vartheta
is changing and large tails where
\bf n
vector
is rotated. This have been discussed in
[
15
]
and
numerically calculated in
[
16
]
.

Motion of arbitrary soliton (in the rotating frame)

Consider a soliton with some distribution
n(z)
and
\vartheta(z)
which
are equilibrium solution of Leggett equations.

Consider a soliton which has the same distribution of
n
and
\vartheta
and moves with
velocity
v
. For the moving soliton we can write
n(z,dt)=n(z+v\,dt)
and
\dot{n}(z)=n^{\prime}\,v
, and same for theta. Now put this into (
108
) and find
spin
\bf S
distribution of the moving soliton:

\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}{\bf S}(v)=\Big[{\bf n}\ \vartheta^{\prime}+(1-\cos\vartheta)\ {\bf n}\times{\bf n^{\prime}}+\sin\vartheta\ {\bf n^{\prime}}\Big]v+\gamma{\bf H}+\omega\ (R{\bf\hat{z}}-{\bf\hat{z}})

(140)

Calculate kinetic energy of the soliton using (
77
), as an
energy difference between moving and static soliton. Note that
expression for energy is same in the rotating frame:

E_{K}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[-({\bf S}(v)\cdot\gamma{\bf H})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\chi_{B}}{\bf S}(v)^{2}\right]dz-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[-({\bf S}(0)\cdot\gamma{\bf H})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\chi_{B}}{\bf S}(0)^{2}\right]dz

(141)

=-\frac{\chi_{B}}{\gamma^{2}}\ \omega v\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2(1-\cos\vartheta)[{\bf n}\times{\bf n^{\prime}}]_{z}\ dz+\frac{\chi_{B}}{\gamma^{2}}\ \frac{v^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[{\bf n}\ \vartheta^{\prime}+(1-\cos\vartheta)\ {\bf n}\times{\bf n^{\prime}}+\sin\vartheta\ {\bf n^{\prime}}\right]^{2}\ dz

The first term is proportional to
\omega\,v
, which is interesting: it
says that if the soliton in the rotating frame has a helical structure
where
\bf n
rotates around
\bf\hat{z}
axis then energy minimum
corresponds to a non-zero velocity. The second term is proportional to

v^{2}
, from this term we can get effective mass of the soliton:

m=\frac{\chi_{B}}{\gamma^{2}}\ \int_{z}\left[(\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}+2(1-\cos\vartheta)\ ({\bf n^{\prime}})^{2}\right]dz

(142)

This calculation is valid for small velocities. Arbitrary motion of

\vartheta
-solitons have been calculated in
[
17
]
.

Motion of
\vartheta
-solitons

Let’s look at a 3D case: a soliton perpendicular to
\bf\hat{z}
direction
in an experimental cell. It can be considered as a membrane attached to
walls because of some pinning effects. Let’s calculate effective mass and
tension of this membrane using profile of the soliton
\vartheta(z)

from (
139
).

Tension of the soliton membrane is its total potential energy (spin-orbit + gradient)
per unit area. If
\bf n
is uniform the gradient energy (
79
) is

E_{\nabla}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}\big[(2K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3})(\nabla\vartheta)^{2}-(K_{2}+K_{3})({\bf n}\cdot\nabla\vartheta)^{2}\big].

(143)

For the soliton (
136
-
139
) it is

F_{\nabla}=\Delta^{2}K_{1}(\vartheta^{\prime})^{2}=8\Delta^{2}g_{D}(\cos\vartheta+1/4)^{2}

(144)

Energy of spin-orbit interaction (
78
) is also

F_{SO}=8\Delta^{2}g_{D}(\cos\vartheta+1/4)^{2}

(145)

And tension is integral of total energy density over
z
:

T=16\Delta^{2}g_{D}\ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\cos\vartheta(z)+1/4)^{2}\,dz

(146)

Mass of the soliton per unit area can be found by integrating (
142
):

M=8\frac{\chi_{B}}{\gamma^{2}}\frac{g_{D}}{K_{1}}\ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\cos\vartheta+1/4)^{2}dz

(147)

Speed of sound is square root of tension divided by mass:

C^{2}=2\frac{\Delta^{2}\gamma^{2}}{\chi_{B}}K_{1}=2c_{\perp}^{2}-c_{\parallel}^{2}

(148)

The first oscillation mode of a circular membrane with radius
R
is
2.405\,C/R
.
