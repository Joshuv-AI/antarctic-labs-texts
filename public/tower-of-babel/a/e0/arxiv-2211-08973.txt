1.
Introduction

A positive integer is called
y
-smooth if each of its prime factors does not exceed
y
. We denote the number of
y
-smooth integers not exceeding
x
by
\Psi(x,y)
. We assume throughout
x\geq y\geq 2
.
Let
\rho\colon[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be the Dickman function, defined as
\rho(t)=1
for
t\leq 1
and via the delay differential equation
t\rho^{\prime}(t)=-\rho(t-1)
for
t>1
.
Dickman
[
8
]
showed that

(1.1)

\Psi(x,y)\sim x\rho(\log x/\log y)\qquad(x\to\infty)

holds when
y\geq x^{\varepsilon}
. For this reason, it is useful to introduce the parameter

u:=\log x/\log y.

The range of validity of (
1.1
) was considerably improved by de Bruijn
[
5
]
and H. Maier (unpublished), and the state of the art is due to Hildebrand who showed that
[
19
]

(1.2)

\Psi(x,y)=x\rho(u)\left(1+O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log(u+1)}{\log y}\right)\right)

holds when
\log y\geq(\log\log x)^{\frac{5}{3}+\varepsilon}
. In
[
17
]
, Hildebrand showed that the Riemann Hypothesis (RH) implies that (
1.2
) holds in

(1.3)

y\geq(\log x)^{2+\varepsilon}.

In fact, in this range he proves the slightly stronger estimate

(1.4)

\Psi(x,y)=x\rho(u)\exp\left(O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log(u+1)}{\log y}\right)\right).

The reverse implication is also true: if even the weaker estimate
\Psi(x,y)=x\rho(u)\exp(O_{\varepsilon}(y^{\varepsilon}))
holds in the range (
1.3
) for every
\varepsilon>0
then RH must be true. In
[
19
, p. 290]
, Hildebrand speculates that
\Psi(x,y)\asymp x\rho(u)
does not hold for
y\leq(\log x)^{2-\varepsilon}
. He writes

If the Riemann hypothesis is assumed, the range for
u
can be further extended to
1\leq u\leq\log x/(2+\varepsilon)\log\log x
, but it seems likely that then the critical limit is attained: it may be conjectured that for
u>\log x/(2-\varepsilon)\log\log x
, the relation
\Psi(x,x^{1/u})\sim x\rho(u)
no longer holds.

This conjecture is repeated in
[
18
]
and by Granville in
[
14

15
]
. We confirm it in stronger form. To state our result we recall the function

K\colon(-1,0]\to\mathbb{R},\qquad K(t)=t\zeta(t+1)/(t+1),\qquad K(0)=1,

[

. It is strictly decreasing: the identity
\zeta(s)=s/(s-1)-s\int_{1}^{\infty}\{x\}x^{-1-s}dx

to indicate a quantity

, respectively. We write

(resp.

) to indicate a function

(resp.

). A function

is

if

. Throughout

We denote

\vartheta=\sup_{\zeta(\rho)=0}\Re\rho\in[1/2,1].

For
y\geq 2
and
\Re s>0
we define the partial zeta function

\zeta(s,y)=\prod_{p\leq y}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}=\sum_{n\text{ is }y\text{-smooth}}n^{-s}.

We define
\xi\colon[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
via

e^{\xi(v)}=1+v\xi(v).

We define the entire function

We denote the Euler–Mascheroni constant by
\gamma
.
The Laplace transform of
\rho
is given by
[

\hat{\rho}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(v)e^{-sv}dv=\exp(\gamma+I(-s))

for

. We define

for

where

We write
\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)
for the Chebyshev function.
We set

If a meromorphic function has a removable singularity (e.g.
\zeta(s)(s-1)
at

), we identify its value there with its limit.
When we differentiate a bivariate function (e.g.
\zeta(s,y)

2.
A formula and its investigation

2.1.
On two saddle points

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

(2.1)

f(t):=t\log x+\log(F_{2}(t,y)).

It satisfies

(2.2)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\log x-(\log y)I^{\prime}((1-t)\log y),\qquad f^{\prime\prime}(t)=(\log y)^{2}I^{\prime\prime}((1-t)\log y).

The function
f
is convex since

. We define

\beta=\beta(x,y)=1-\frac{\xi(u)}{\log y}

for

. We often shorten

to
\xi

The function

attains its global minimum at
\beta
because it is readily verified that

(2.3)

f^{\prime}(\beta)=0.

The asymptotics of

Lemma 2.1
.

[
20
, Lem. 1]
[
26
, Lem. 4.5]

For

(2.4)

\displaystyle\xi(v)

\displaystyle=\log v+\log\log v+O(\log\log v/\log v),

(2.5)

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime}(\xi(v))

\displaystyle=v(1+O(1/\log v)).

Corollary 2.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.

(1)

If
x\geq y\geq(1+\varepsilon)\log x
then

(2.6)

\beta=\frac{\log\big(\frac{y}{\log x}\big)}{\log y}\bigg(1+O_{\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{\log\log(y+1)}{\log y}\bigg)\bigg).

(2)

If
2\log x\geq y\geq\varepsilon\log x
then
\beta=O_{\varepsilon}(1/\log y)
.

(3)

We have
\beta\leq 1
. Equality occurs if and only if
y=x
.

(4)

We have
\beta\geq 0
if and only if
y\geq 1+\log x
, and
\beta=0
if and only if
y=1+\log x
.

Proof.

If
u<3
then (

) means
\beta=1+O(\log\log(x+1)/\log x)
which follows from
\xi(u)=O(1)
. If

) to write

\beta=\frac{\log(y/(u\log u))+O(\log\log(u+1)/\log u)}{\log y}

and reduce (

\log\left(\frac{\log y}{\log u}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log\log(u+1)}{\log u}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{y}{\log x}\right)\frac{\log\log(y+1)}{\log y}.

If
x\ll_{\varepsilon}1
then
y\ll_{\varepsilon}1
and (

.
If

\gg_{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{y}{\log x}\right)\frac{\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}.

Since

\log\left(\frac{\log y}{\log u}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{y}{\log x}\right)\frac{\log\log\log x}{\log\log x}.

We write

\frac{\log y}{\log u}=\frac{\log y}{\log\log x}\big(1-\frac{\log\log y}{\log\log x}\big)^{-1}=\big(1+\frac{\log(y/\log x)}{\log\log x}\big)(1-\frac{\log\log y}{\log\log x}\big)^{-1}

The third part follows from

∎
