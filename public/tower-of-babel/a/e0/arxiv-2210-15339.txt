Generalizing the German Tank Problem
Anthony Lee, Steven J. Miller

Abstract
. The German Tank Problem dates back to World War II when the Allies used a statistical approach to estimate the number of enemy tanks produced or on the field from observed serial numbers after battles. Assuming that the tanks are labeled consecutively starting from 1, if we observe k k tanks from a total of N N tanks with the maximum observed tank being m m , then the best estimate for N N is m ⁡ ( 1 + 1 / k ) − 1 m(1+1/k)-1 . We explore many generalizations. First, we looked at the discrete and continuous one dimensional case. We attempted to improve the original formula by using different estimators such as the second largest and L ​ th L\textsuperscript{{\rm th}} largest tank, and applied motivation from portfolio theory by seeing if a weighted average of different estimators would produce less variance; however, the original formula, using the largest tank proved to be the best; the continuous case was similar. Then, we attempted to generalize the problem into two dimensions, where we pick pairs instead of points. We looked at the discrete and continuous square and circle variants. There were more complications, as we dealt with problems in geometry and number theory, such as dealing with curvature issues in the circle, and the problem that not every number is representable as a sum of two squares. In some cases, we concentrated on the large N N limit (with fixed k k ) by deriving approximate formulas. For the discrete and continuous square, we tested various statistics, but found that the largest observed component of our pairs is the best statistic to look at; the scaling factor for both cases is ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) / 2 ​ k (2k+1)/2k . For the circle we used motivation from the equation of a circle; for the continuous case, we looked at X 2 + Y 2 \sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}} and for the discrete case, we looked at X 2 + Y 2 X^{2}+Y^{2} and took a square root at the end to estimate for r r . The discrete case was especially involved because we had to use approximation formulas that gave us the number of lattice points inside the circle. Interestingly, the scaling factors were different for the cases. Lastly, we generalized the problem into L L dimensional squares and circles. The discrete and continuous square proved similar to the two dimensional square problem. However, for the L ​ th L\textsuperscript{{\rm th}} dimensional circle, we had to use formulas for the volume of the L L -ball, and had to approximate the number of lattice points inside it. The formulas for the discrete circle were particularly interesting, as there was no L L dependence in the formula.

Introduction

We study a problem where we are given some information about observations, and we have to estimate the number of objects. The motivation comes from the German Tank problem, a classic problem in probability. During World War II, Germans used powerful tanks to their advantage. To develop appropriate military strategies against the Germans, the Allies had to estimate the number of tanks produced on various battlefields. Initially, spies were used to figure out the number of tanks. However, using statistics to estimate the number of tanks proved to be much more accurate; see

The German Tank problem is an excellent example of how statistical inference can be applied to real world problems. We attempt to generalize the well known German Tank Problem. Previously, Clark, Gonye, and Miller

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ s\bigg(1+\frac{2}{k-1}\bigg)\ -\ 1.

We start by recalling the derivation of the original German tank problem, as we will be extending those calculations. We then see if we can improve the one dimensional formula by looking at different estimators and using motivation from portfolio theory in financial mathematics, specifically looking at weighted sums of estimators to see if we can find a new statistic that has the same mean of its predictions but smaller variance; see Appendix
B

E

Generalizing further, we looked at what would happen if we modified some of the assumptions of the problem. First, we modified the condition that all serial numbers are consecutive integers and we looked at the standard German Tank Problem in the continuous setting. The problem changed slightly as we sample
k

0

N

Interestingly, the continuous case turned out to be very similar to the discrete case, as the best statistic to look at in the continuous case was the largest observed tank, as it produced a formula with the least variance. Furthermore, the scaling factor for the discrete and continuous case were both
(k+1)/k
, which shows the similarity between the two cases. The main conclusion here was that even in the continuous case, the largest observed tank is the best statistic to study.

Moving on from the one dimensional case, we generalized the German Tank problem further into two dimensions. In the two dimensional case, we select pairs instead of points, and the main condition is that we select
k
pairs without replacement. Specifically, we looked at the square and circle, as we have explicit closed form and asymptotic expressions for the area and number of lattice points inside. The main strategy of deriving formulas was using the CDF method for the discrete and continuous case. (See Lemma
2.7

(1,1)
and upper right vertex at
(N,N)
and looked at the number of lattice points inside the square. From inspiration from the one dimensional problem, we looked at the largest observed component. Unfortunately, because the closed form expressions that we get are very complex, which makes it difficult to invert, we approximate by focusing on the main term and sometimes second order, as that’s enough to get a pretty good approximation of what’s going on. Thus, for the discrete square, we provide a approximate formula that still does a very good job estimating. The continuous square problem was much easier as calculating integrals was easier than calculating sums. We set the bottom left vertex as
(0,0)
and the upper right vertex as
(N,N)
. We also looked at the largest observed component for this case too. However, instead of looking at the number of lattice points as we did in the discrete case, we looked at the area, which was much easier. Interestingly, the scaling factor for both the discrete and continuous square was the same, and we state both formulas.

(1.3)

\displaystyle{\rm\textbf{Discrete Square}:}\ \mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \frac{2k+1}{2k}(m-1)\ \ \ \ {\rm\textbf{Continuous Square}:}\ \mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \frac{2k+1}{2k}\cdot m.

However, we faced problem of curvature because we had to split the ranges of the side of the square to see if the square completely fit in a circle or not. Thus, we decided that this isn’t the best statistic to look at. We wanted to look at a statistic similar to

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^{2}].

The variance can be computed by

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X)\ =\ \mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\ -\ \mathbb{E}[X]^{2}.

See Lemma

Definition 2.3
.

For two jointly distributed real valued random variables
X

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(X,Y)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\right)\cdot\left(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)\right].

.

The covariance can be computed by

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(X,Y)\ =\ \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y].

See Lemma

Theorem 2.5
.

(Linearity of expectation)
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be random variables, and let
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
be functions such that
\mathbb{E}[|g_{i}(X_{i})|]
exists and is finite, and let

be any real numbers; note the random variables do not have to be independent. Then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[a_{1}g_{1}(X_{1})+\dots+a_{n}g_{n}(X_{n})\right]\ =\ a_{1}\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(X_{1})]+\cdots+a_{n}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(X_{n})].

Theorem 2.6
.

(Joint Probability Density Function)
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be continuous random variables with densities
f_{X_{1}},f_{X_{2}},\dots,f_{X_{n}}
defined on
\mathbb{R}
. The joint density function of the tuple
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is a non-negative integrable function
f_{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}}
such that for every nice set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}((X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in S)\ =\ \idotsint_{S}f_{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n},

and

(2.7)

\displaystyle f_{X_{i}}(x_{i})\ =\ \int_{x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})dx_{1}\cdots dx_{i-1}dx_{i+1}\cdots dx_{n}.

For discrete random variables, we can just replace integrals with sums.

Definition 2.7
.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of a random variable
X
with density
f
, denoted
F

(2.8)

\displaystyle F(x)\ :=\ {\rm Prob}(X\leq x)\ =\ \int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt,\ {\rm for\ any}\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

In many situations it is easy to compute the CDF, and thus by using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus we can determine the probability density function. 2.2.
Analysis Review

Unfortunately many of the sums we encounter are very complex and difficult to invert when expressed in a closed form, and thus we estimate using the main term and the second order. The following frequently provides a good, easily computed bound; see for example
[

.

Theorem 2.8
.

(Euler-Maclaurin formula) For
p
a positive integer and a function
f(x)
that is
p
times continuously differentiable on the interval
[a,b]
, we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a}^{b}f(i)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx+\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}\ +\ \sum_{q=1}^{\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor}\frac{B_{2q}}{(2q)!}(f^{2q-1}(b)-f^{2q-1}(a))+R_{p},

and

(2.10)

\displaystyle|R_{p}|\ \leq\ \frac{2\zeta(p)}{(2\pi)^{p}}\int_{m}^{n}|f^{(p)}(x)|\,dx.

The estimation below is needed in computing upper and lower bounds in applications of the Euler-Maclaurin formula later.

Lemma 2.9
.

For
m,L\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\bigg(m^{Lk}-m^{Lk-L}\bigg(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\bigg)\bigg)\ \leq\ m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1)\leq m^{Lk}.

Proof.

The upper bound follow trivially as

(2.12)

\displaystyle m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1))\ \leq\ (m^{L})(m^{L})\cdots(m^{L}).

To justify the lower bound, we want to prove the inequality

(2.13)

\displaystyle m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(r-1))\ \geq\ m^{Lr}-\frac{r(r-1)}{2}m^{Lr-L}.

The base case is satisfied when
r=1
as
m^{2}\ \geq\ m^{2}
. We assume that the case when
r=k
is true:

(2.14)

\displaystyle m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1))\ \geq\ m^{Lk}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}m^{Lk-L}.

We must show it holds when
r=k+1
; namely we must show

(2.15)

\displaystyle m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-k)\ \geq\ m^{Lk+L}\ -\ \frac{(k+1)k}{2}m^{Lk}.

This follows immediately by substitution and expansion:

(2.16)

\displaystyle m^{L}(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1))(m^{L}-k)

\displaystyle\ \geq\

\displaystyle\bigg(m^{Lk}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}m^{Lk-L}\bigg)(m^{L}-k)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle m^{Lk+L}\ -\ \frac{k(k+1)}{2}m^{Lk}\ +\ \frac{k^{2}(k-2)}{2}m^{Lk-L}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle m^{Lk+L}\ -\ \frac{k(k+1)}{2}m^{Lk}.

∎

Finally, in many of our generalizations it is impossible to obtain simple closed form expressions such as those in the original problem. We thus investigate the large
N

Definition 2.10
.

(Big-Oh Notation)
Suppose

(read “

3.
Derivation of Original German Tank Problem

We derive below the formula for the original German tank problem where
m
represents the largest tank observed,
k
the number of tanks observed, and
N
the number of tanks (which are numbered consecutively from 1 to
N
). We use
\mathaccent 866{N}
to represent our estimate for the total number of tanks,
N
. It is useful to run through this argument before we generalize the inference problem, and we follow the exposition in
[
CGM
]
. We prove

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m_{k}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{k}\bigg)\ -\ 1.

We quickly check some extreme cases to see if this formula is reasonable. When we observe just one tank, the estimation formula is
2m-1
. We expect the observation to be
N/2
, so multiplying by 2 is reasonable. When we observe all
N
tanks (so
k=N
), the estimation is just
m
, which is also reasonable because we know the number all the tanks. We use Pascal’s identity and the hockey stick identity frequently in calculations; see Appendix
A
for proofs.

3.0.1.
The PDF of the Largest Observed Tank

Let
M_{k}
be the random variable for the largest tank observed, and let
m_{k}
be its observed value. Thus
m_{k}
is the largest tank serial number that we observe. Our goal is to find a formula to estimate
N
from
m_{k}
and
k
. We first compute the PDF for
M_{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
k\leq m_{k}\leq n
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M=m_{k})\ =\ \frac{\binom{m_{k}-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N}{k}}.

Proof.

We know that the total number of ways to select
k
tanks from
N
possible tanks is
\binom{N}{k}
. If the largest tank we observe is
m
, then we have to choose
k-1
tanks from
m_{k}-1
possibilities. Therefore, the probability that the largest tank we observe is
m
is
\binom{m_{k}-1}{k-1}/\binom{N}{k}
, thus proving the claim.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

It is worth remarking that the process of deriving the formula is different from the application. In the application, we use tanks we observe,
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}
and estimate for
N
by applying the formula. However, when we are deriving the formula, we use
N
and
k
to compute the expected value of
M_{k}
, and then we invert the equation to find the formula for
N
.

3.0.2.
Derivation

Now we calculate the expected value of
M_{K}
in order to find a equation with
N
and
k
. To calculate the expected value, we multiply each value of the random variable by its probability and add the products. We expand the binomials, and regroup so that we can use the hockey stick identity. We find

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}{\rm Prob}(M_{k}=m_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}\frac{\binom{m_{k}-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}\frac{m_{k}!}{(k-1)!(m_{k}-k)!}\frac{k}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}\frac{m_{k}!}{(k)!(m_{k}-k)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}\binom{m_{k}}{k}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ the\ hockey\ stick\ identity,\ recall\ Lemma\ \ref{lem:fourhockeystickidentity},\ to\ evaluate\ the\ sum}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\binom{N}{k}}\binom{N+1}{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\Bigg(\frac{N+1}{k+1}\Bigg).

Using our formula for
M_{k}
as a function of
N
and
k
, we can invert and obtain the formula for
N
in terms of
M_{k}
and
k
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \mathbb{E}[M_{k}]\Bigg(\frac{k+1}{k}\Bigg)\ -\ 1.

To obtain our estimate
\mathaccent 866{N}
we substitute the observed
m_{k}
for
\mathbb{E}[M_{k}]
:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m_{k}\Bigg(\frac{k+1}{k}\Bigg)\ -\ 1.

4.
Estimating with more tanks

There are two natural approaches to try to improve the our prediction for the original problem. One is to find another estimator; perhaps the mean or median might do better. We pursue this and investigate statistics derived from the
L\textsuperscript{{\rm th}}
largest tank, value does better.

Another approach is to use some motivation from portfolio theory; see for example
[
H
]
. Imagine we have two independent stocks with the same expected return. By looking at a linear combination, the combined portfolio will still have the same expected return, but if the weights are chosen properly a smaller variance; we provide details in Appendix
B
. The idea of weighing two random variables to create a combined one with less variance is a common method, and we apply that to our problem to see if we can improve the quality of the estimator for the German tank problem by looking at a combination of two tanks. The computation is a bit more involved than the two stock example, as the values of the two tanks are not independent; if we know the largest tank value is
m_{k}
then clearly
m_{k-1}<m_{k}
. We provide complete details of the analysis of exploring statistics involving the two largest observed serial numbers, and find that the quality of the estimator is
not
improved by incorporating both values.

4.1.
Estimation from Various Tanks

Let
M_{k-1}
be the random variable for the value of the second largest tank observed and let
m_{k-1}
be the value we observe. We find the probability that the second largest tank is
m_{k-1}.
We claim that for
k-1\leq m_{k-1}\leq n-1
, the probability that
M_{k-1}=m_{k-1}
is

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M_{k-1}=m_{k-1})\ =\ \frac{\binom{m_{k-1}-1}{k-2}\binom{N-m_{k-1}}{1}}{\binom{N}{k}}.

Clearly the probability is zero for tanks outside this range. There are
\binom{N}{k}
ways to choose
k
tanks from
N
. We need
k-2
tanks to be smaller than
m_{k-1}-1
; there are
\binom{m_{k-1}-1}{k-2}
ways for that to happen. We then have to choose tank
m_{k}
, which has to be larger than tank
m_{k-1}
. Thus, the range from
m_{k}
goes from
m_{k-1}+1
to
N
, and there are
\binom{N-m_{k-1}}{1}
ways to do this.
\Box

We can now calculate the expected value of
M_{k-1}
:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{N-1}m_{k-1}{\rm Prob}(M_{k-1}\ =\ m_{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{N-1}m_{k-1}\frac{\binom{m_{k-1}-1}{k-2}(N-m_{k-1})}{\binom{N}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{N-1}\frac{k-1}{k-1}\frac{m_{k-1}!}{(k-2)!(m_{k-1}-k+1)!}(N-m_{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{N-1}\frac{m_{k-1}!}{(k-1)!(m_{k-1}-k+1)!}(N-m_{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{N-1}\binom{m_{k-1}}{k-1}\binom{N-m_{k-1}}{1}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ Identity\ IV}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{\binom{N}{k}}\binom{N+1}{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(N+1)\frac{k-1}{k+1}.

Therefore, the formula for our estimator of
N
, in terms of
m
and
k
, coming from
M_{k-1}
is

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m_{k-1}\frac{k+1}{k-1}\ -\ 1.

We attach some code to see the mean and variances of the formula using the second largest tank.

Figure 3.
Results for formula using second largest tank.

Remark 4.1
.

Comparing this result to the variance of the formula using the largest tank, we see that the formula using the second largest tank has higher variance. Later, we confirm this finding by theory, and not just by code.

Calculating similarly, we obtain the formula for the
L\textsuperscript{th}
largest tank (see Appendix
D
for details):

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m_{k-L+1}\frac{k+1}{k-L+1}\ -\ 1.

We calculate the variances for statistics arising from the different tank (from largest observed to smallest); we scale each of these so that the mean is
N
, and see which has the least variance. We let
X_{k}
be the estimation from using the largest tank,
X_{k-1}
from using the second largest tank, and so on. To calculate the variances of these formulas, we calculate the variances of
M_{k}
, the largest tank,
M_{k-1}
, the second largest tank, and so on; we then scale by a multiplicative factor to get the variances we want.

4.1.1.
Variance of
M_{k}

: We use Lemma
2.2
, to compute the variance. We find

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(M_{k})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k}^{2}]\ -\ \mathbb{E}[M_{k}]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}^{2}{\rm Prob(M_{k}\ =\ m_{k})}\ -\ \bigg[\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}{\rm Prob(M_{k}\ =\ m_{k})}\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ know\ the\ second\ term,\ as\ we\ calculated\ it\ earlier.}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ just\ have\ to\ calculate\ the\ first\ term.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}^{2}\frac{\binom{m_{k}-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N}{k}}\ -\ \bigg[\frac{k(N+1)}{k+1}\bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ Identity\ III\ to\ calculate\ the\ first\ term.}

\displaystyle{\rm After\ substituting\ in\ and\ simplifying,\ we\ get}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=\ N^{2}\ -\ (2N-1)\frac{N-k}{k+1}\

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +\ 2\frac{(N-k)(N-k-1)}{(k+1)(k+2)}\ -\ \frac{k^{2}(N+1)^{2}}{(k+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(k)(N-k)(N+1)}{(k+1)^{2}(k+2)}.

Now that we have the variance for
M_{k}
, we easily scale the formula and obtain the variance of
X_{k}=m_{k}(k+1)/k
, as the variance of
a
times
X
is
a^{2}
times the variance of
X
:

(4.6)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{k})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(M_{k})\cdot\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(k)(N-k)(N+1)}{(k+1)^{2}(k+2)}\cdot\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-k)(N+1)}{(k)(k+2)}.

Through similar calculations, we obtain the variance of
X_{k-1}
(see Appendix
D
for details):

(4.7)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{2(N-k)(N+1)}{(k+2)(k-1)}.

We see that the estimator using
m_{k-1}
, and more generally using
m_{k-L+1}
, is worse than using
m_{k}
, as the variance is larger. We can simply plug in some values of
N
and
k
and compare the variances. Also, we see that the variance for
X_{k-1}
is roughly two times the variance of
X_{k}
, which shows that
X_{k}
is a better statistic. We can also compare variances easily by writing some code and numerically exploring. We made a simulation where we sample the
k
tanks from 1 to
N
when all tanks are equally likely to be seen. This simulation is attached in the Appendix
E
. Thus, we conclude that if we are only going to use one observed value, it is best to use the largest.

This leads to our second question: can we do better if we create a statistic combining two or more observed values?

4.2.
Weighted Statistic

Previously, we’ve only considered using one tank to estimate for
N
. In this section, we create a statistic using the largest and second largest tank values, and show that this weighted statistic isn’t better.

Before we state the weighted formula, we set our notation.

•

N
= Total number of tanks

•

k
= Numbers of tanks observed

•

M_{k}
= Largest Observed Tank

•

M_{k-1}
= Second largest Observed Tank

•

X_{k}
= Statistic to estimate
N
using
M_{k}
:
M_{k}(\frac{k+1}{k})
- 1

•

X_{k-1}
= Statistics to estimate
N
using
M_{k-1}
:
M_{k-1}(\frac{k+1}{k-1})
- 1

We state the weighted statistic. Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
and define the weighted statistic
X_{\alpha}
by

(4.8)

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}\ :=\ \alpha X_{k}\ +\ (1-\alpha)X_{k-1}.

From the formula, we see that when
\alpha
is 1, this collapses to the formula for
N
using
m_{k}
, and similarly when
\alpha
is 0, we get the
m_{k-1}
formula. In order to see if there is a better estimation, we find the optimal
\alpha
value, the value that minimizes the variance of
X_{\alpha}
. If the optimal
\alpha
value is 1, then we can conclude that the
m_{k}
formula is the best we can do to estimate for
N
. We compute the variance of
X
:

(4.9)

\displaystyle{\rm Var\ X}_{\alpha}\ =\ \alpha^{2}{\rm Var\ X_{k}}\ +\ (1-\alpha)^{2}{\rm Var\ X_{k-1}}\ +\ 2\ \alpha(1-\alpha){\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1}).

As We have already calculated the variances of
X_{k}
and
X_{k-1}
, we just have to calculate the covariance term.

4.2.1.
Covariance term

We calculate the term,
{\rm Cov}[X_{k},X_{k-1}]
separately first. By Lemma
2.4
, we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}[X_{k},X_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\cdot X_{k-1}]\ -\ \mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{k-1}].

Recall that

(4.11)

\displaystyle X_{k}\ =\ m_{k}\bigg(\frac{k+1}{k}\bigg)-1\ ,\ \ \ \ X_{k-1}\ =\ m_{k-1}\bigg(\frac{k+1}{k-1}\bigg)-1.

We know that the second term of the covariance,
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{k-1}]
is
N^{2}
because they are both estimation formulas. Thus, we only have to calculate the first term.

We use linearity of expectation to expand. Recall Theorem
2.5
, linearity of expectation.

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\cdot X_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(m_{k}\bigg(\frac{k+1}{k}\bigg)-1\bigg)\cdot\bigg(m_{k-1}\bigg(\frac{k+1}{k-1}\bigg)-1\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{k(k-1)}\mathbb{E}[m_{k}\cdot m_{k-1}]\ -\ \frac{k+1}{k}\mathbb{E}[m_{k}]\ -\ \frac{k+1}{k-1}\mathbb{E}[m_{k-1}]\ +\ \mathbb{E}[1].

We calculate term by term. We use the joint PDF, recall Theorem
2.6
, to calculate
\mathbb{E}[m_{k}\cdot m_{k-1}]
.

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k}\cdot M_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{m_{k}-1}m_{k}m_{k-1}{\rm Prob}(M_{k-1}=m_{k-1},M_{k}=m_{k})

\displaystyle{\rm This\ is\ the\ joint\ probability\ density\ function\ of\ m_{k}\ and\ m_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{m_{k}-1}m_{k}m_{k-1}\frac{\binom{m_{k-1}-1}{k-2}}{\binom{N}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm The\ probability\ is\ the\ following\ because\ after\ selecting\ m_{k}\ and\ m_{k-1},\ we}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ {\rm have\ to\ select\ (k-2)\ more\ tanks\ from\ the\ possible\ (m_{k-1}-1)\ tanks.}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ {\rm The\ range\ of\ m_{k-1}\ is\ dependent\ on\ the\ value\ of\ m_{k}\ because}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ {\rm m_{k-1}\ \leq\ m_{k}\ is\ always\ satisfied.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{m_{k}-1}m_{k-1}\binom{m_{k-1}-1}{k-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}\sum_{m_{k-1}=k-1}^{m_{k}-1}\binom{m_{k-1}}{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{m_{k}=k}^{N}m_{k}\binom{m_{k}}{k}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ Identity\ II\ to\ calculate}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(k-1)(N+2)(N+1)}{(k+2)}\ -\ \frac{(k-1)(N+1)}{k+1}.

Now that we’ve calculated the joint PDF, we know all the terms and find

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\cdot X_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{k(k-1)}\bigg[\frac{(k-1)(N+2)(N+1)}{(k+2)}-\frac{(k-1)(N+1)}{k+1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ -\ \frac{k+1}{k}\frac{(N+1)k}{k+1}\ -\ \frac{k+1}{k-1}\frac{(N+1)(k-1)}{k+1}\ +\ 1,

and of course from our normalizations we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle N^{2}.

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\cdot X_{k-1}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[X_{k-1}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{k}\frac{(N+2)(N+1)}{(k+2)}\ -\ \frac{(k+1)}{k}{(N+1)}\ -(N+1)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(n+1)(n-k)}{k(k+2)}.

4.2.2.
Finding optimal alpha

Now that we’ve calculated
{\rm Var}(X_{k})
,
{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})
, and
{\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})
, we find the optimal
\alpha
value (that minimizes the variance of
X_{\alpha}
). To find the optimal alpha value, we optimize by taking the derivative and finding the unique value that gives the least variance. Let
\alpha_{k,{k-1}}
denote the specific value of
\alpha
that minimizes the variance. We have

(4.17)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}{\rm Var}(X_{k})\ +\ (1-\alpha)^{2}{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})+2\alpha(1-\alpha){\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}({\rm Var}(X_{k})+{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})-2\ {\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1}))

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\ 2\alpha({\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})\ -\ {\rm Var}(X_{k-1}))\ +\ {\rm Var}(X_{k-1}).

Taking the derivative with respect to
\alpha
yields

(4.18)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{\alpha})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle 2\alpha_{k,{k-1}}({\rm Var}(X_{k})+{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})-2\ {\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\ 2\ ({\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})\ -\ {\rm Var}(X_{k-1})).

Because we want to find the optimal
\alpha
value, we solve for
{\rm Var}(X)^{\prime}=0
(and of course also check the endpoints of
\alpha=0
or
1
). After substituting in the formulas and doing some algebra, we see that the optimal alpha value is 1, which is in fact one of our endpoints and corresponds to only using the largest tank. Thus if we use both the largest and second largest values observed we do worse than just using the largest:

(4.19)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,{k-1}}\ =\ \frac{{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})\ -\ {\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})}{{\rm Var}(X_{k})+{\rm Var}(X_{k-1})-2\ {\rm Cov}(X_{k},X_{k-1})}\ =\ 1.

Similar calculations hold for other weighted combinations. Therefore, in the discrete one dimensional German Tank problem where we sample without replacement, we conclude that the formula using the largest tank does the best job.

5.
Continuous One Dimensional Problem

We now explore our first generalization of the German Tank Problem and consider a continuous one dimensional analogue. In the original formulation, the serial numbers of the tanks were all integers drawn from 1 to
N
. We now consider a continuous version, where we select
k
tanks from the interval
[0,N]
with
N
unknown. Thus our goal is to find a statistic to estimate
N
. We discuss the effectiveness of various statistics and compare the continuous formulas to the discrete ones; the scaling factor is the same in the continuous and discrete one dimensional problems.

5.1.
Formulas Using Largest and Second Largest Observations.

We begin by estimating using the largest and second largest tanks by using the CDF method to find the PDF. In the continuous case, to find the PDF, we take the derivative of the CDF.

5.1.1.
Formula from Largest

: We first find the CDF by computing the probability that all the tanks are at most
m_{k}
:

(5.1)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M_{k}\leq m_{k})\ =\ \bigg(\frac{m_{k}}{N}\bigg)^{k};

this is because in the continuous case we can view all
k
observations as independent, and the probability any is at most
m_{k}
is just
m_{k}/N
.

Taking the derivative gives the PDF:

(5.2)

\displaystyle f(m_{k})\ =\ PDF_{M_{k}}(m_{k})\ =\ CDF_{M_{k}}(m_{k})^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{km_{k}^{k-1}}{N^{k}}.

Now that we have the PDF, we calculate the expected value of
m_{k}
:

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{k}]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{N}m_{k}f(m_{k})\,dm_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{N}m_{k}\frac{km_{k}^{k-1}}{N^{k}}\,dm_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{N^{k}}\int_{0}^{N}m_{k}^{k}\,dm_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k+1}\cdot N.

Thus we obtain

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m\cdot\bigg(1+\frac{1}{k}\bigg).

Remark 5.1
.

Comparing the continuous formula to the discrete, the only difference is that there is a
-1
in the discrete formula. This difference is due to in the discrete case we sample from tanks numbered from 1 to
N
while in the continuous case wehave chosen to have the interval to be
[0,N]
.

5.1.2.
Formula using Second Largest

: We first find the CDF by computing the probability that the second largest tank is at most
m_{k-1}
. There are two possibilities – all the tanks are less than
m_{k-1}
, or one tank (and there is
\binom{N}{1}
ways to choose which of the
k
tanks that is) is larger than
m_{k-1}
and the rest are
m_{k-1}
or smaller. Thus

(5.5)

\displaystyle CDF_{M}(m_{k-1})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M_{k-1}\leq X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{X}{N}\bigg)^{k}\ +\ \binom{k}{1}\bigg(\frac{X}{N}\bigg)^{k-1}\frac{N-X}{N}.

We take the derivative of the CDF in order to find the PDF. After standard integration, we find

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m_{k-1}\cdot\frac{k+1}{k-1}.

Remark 5.2
.

For the continuous formula using
m_{k-1}
, we see that there is a difference arising from a factor of
-1
from the discrete case. The same explanation of the difference applies here too. Also, as we saw in the discrete case, because the continuous and discrete formula are essentially the same formulas, the variance for the formula using
m_{k-1}
is larger.

5.2.
Continuous weighted formula

:

As we did in the discrete one dimensional case, we see if constructing a statistic that is a linear combination of
m_{k}
and
m_{k-1}
does a better job estimating
N
. Similar to before, the best value is again when
\alpha=1
, meaning that the formula using only
m_{k}
gives the least variance and there is no benefit to including
m_{k-1}
. The calculation is similar to the discrete weighted problem; see Appendix
D
.

6.
Two Dimensional Discrete Generalizations

We now generalize the German Tank Problem to two dimensions, after which it will be easy to extend to higher dimensions. We look at the discrete and continuous versions of the square and the circle. We find for each problem which statistic gives the best estimate for
N
, and compare the formulas of the discrete two dimensional square to that of the one dimensional discrete square. Note that if we use all the terms in the calculation, we would not get a clean closed form, as we have many different powers of
m
, making the equation uninvertible. In order to avoid this, we approximate using the main term and get formulas for fixed
k
and
N
tending to infinity.

6.1.
Square Problem

We first consider the case of the square from
(1,1)
to
(N,N)
as the natural generalization of the one-dimensional set
\{1,\dots,N\}
. Thus there are
N^{2}
pairs, we select
k
of them without replacement. We call the two components the
X
and the
Y
list and use the pairs to find the best estimate for
N
. We investigated two statistics: the largest number from the two lists, and a recursive method where we start with a estimate of
N
and use the largest
L
to estimate for
N
again until the value of our estimate for
N
stabilizes.

6.1.1.
Maximum from Lists:

The motivation of looking at the largest observed component in the two dimensional square comes from the one dimensional German tank problem, where looking at the largest tank gave the most accurate estimation. To calculate the formula, we use the CDF method. The CDF method in the discrete case is slightly different from the one in the continuous case. The statistic we look at is the largest observed component of the
X
list and
Y
list, which we denote by
M
. To calculate the probability that the largest observed component is at most
m
, instead of taking the derivative to find the PDF as we did in the continuous case, in the discrete case we calculate

(6.1)

{\rm Prob}(M\leq m)\ -\ {\rm Prob}(M\leq m-1)

to get the
{\rm Prob}({\rm Max}=m)
. The PDF is computed similarly as in previous arguments, giving

(6.2)

\displaystyle{\rm PDF}_{M}(m)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M\leq m)\ -\ {\rm Prob}(M\leq m-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{m^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\ -\ \frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}.

Thus the expected value of
M
is

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}m\cdot{\rm PDF}_{M}(M=m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{m\binom{m^{2}}{k}\ -\ m\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{m\binom{m^{2}}{k}\ -\ (m-1)\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ telescope\ the\ first\ term.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N\binom{N^{2}}{k}\ -\ (\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil-1)\binom{(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}.

We now determine the second term above; it is

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}\binom{m^{2}}{k}

\displaystyle{\rm Range\ for\ m\ starts\ at\ \lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil\ because\ if\ m\ is\ \lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil-1,\ we\ get\ 0.}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{2}(m^{2}-1)\cdots(m^{2}-(k-1)).

We use Lemma
2.9
to provide upper and lower bounds for the sum

(6.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}\bigg(m^{2k}-m^{2k-2}\bigg(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\bigg)\bigg)\leq\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{2}(m^{2}-1)\cdots(m^{2}-(k-1)\leq\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{2k},

and we now use the Euler-Maclaurin formula, Lemma
2.8
, to approximate the sums with integrals, and bound the error of the approximation in terms of the derivative of the function at the boundary points. We take
p\ =\ 2
, as for the range we are studying this gives an excellent bound and an expression that is easy to work with.

Remark 6.1
.

When we calculate the upper and lower bounds, we see that the first order of the upper and lower are the same, which is why we can set these bounds.

We state a result for a general sum using Euler-Maclaurin, and then will choose appropriate values of the constants. We can just plug in values of
w
and
y
, which are the powers of
m
, and we also plug in
a
and
b
in order to make the calculation easier.

(6.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a}^{b}\bigg[m^{w}-c\cdot m^{y}\bigg]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}(x^{w}-c\cdot x^{y})\,dx\ +\ \frac{a^{w}+b^{w}-c\cdot a^{y}-c\cdot b^{y}}{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +\ \frac{B_{2}}{2}(f^{\prime}(b)-f^{\prime}(a))\ +\ R_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b^{w+1}-a^{w+1}}{w+1}\ -\ \frac{c\cdot b^{y+1}-c\cdot a^{y+1}}{y+1}

\displaystyle\ \ +\ \frac{a^{w}+b^{w}-c\cdot a^{y}-c\cdot b^{y}}{2}\ +\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg(w\cdot b^{w-1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ -\ c\cdot y\cdot b^{y-1}\ -\ w\cdot a^{w-1}+c\cdot y\cdot a^{w-1}\bigg)\ +\ R_{2}.

We calculate the remainder term for the general equation:

(6.7)

\displaystyle|R_{2}|

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{2\zeta(2)}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}|f^{2}(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\cdot\bigg[\int_{a}^{b}\bigg(w(w-1)m^{w-2}-c\cdot y\cdot(y-1)m^{y-2}\bigg)\,dm\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\bigg[w\cdot b^{w-1}-c\cdot y\cdot b^{y-1}-w\cdot a^{w-1}+c\cdot y\cdot a^{y-1}\bigg].

Now that we’ve calculated the main term and remainder of the general equation, we calculate the lower and upper bounds using the general Euler-Maclaurin formula.

We first do the lower bound:

(6.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}\bigg(m^{2k}-m^{2k-2}\bigg(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\ -\ \bigg(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\bigg)\cdot\frac{(N-1)^{2k-1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}}{2k-1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\ \frac{\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil^{2k}+(N-1)^{2k}}{2}\ -\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil^{2k-2}+(N-1)^{2k-2}}{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg[2k(N-1)^{2k-1}-2k\cdot\ (\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}-k(k-1)^{2}(N-1)^{2k-3}

\displaystyle\ \ +k(k-1)^{2}(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-3}\bigg]\ +\ R_{2},

where we have the following bound for the remainder term:

(6.9)

\displaystyle|R_{2}|

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\bigg[2k\cdot(N-1)^{2k-1}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}(2k-2)(N-1)^{2k-3}\ -\ 2k\cdot(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}

\displaystyle\ \ +\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot(2k-2)\cdot(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-3}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\bigg[2k\cdot(N-1)^{2k-1}-2k(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}-k(k-1)^{2}(N-1)^{2k-3}

\displaystyle\ +k(k-1)^{2}(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-3}\bigg].

We now calculate the main and remainder term of the upper bound:

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{2k}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\ +\ \frac{(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k}+(N-1)^{2k}}{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg(2k\cdot(N-1)^{2k-1}-2k\cdot(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}\bigg)\ +\ R_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\ +\ \frac{(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k}+(N-1)^{2k}}{2}

\displaystyle\ \ +\ \frac{k}{6}\bigg[(N-1)^{2k-1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}\bigg]\ +\ R_{2},

with the remainder term bounded by

(6.11)

\displaystyle|R_{2}|

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\bigg[2k\cdot(N-1)^{2k-1}-2k\cdot(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{k}{6}\bigg[(N-1)^{2k-1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k-1}\bigg].

Now that we have the upper and lower bounds, we use the main term (which as
N\to\infty
dominates the error terms) to find an equation of
m
in terms of
N
and
k
:

(6.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{2k}

\displaystyle\ \approx\

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}-(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}

\displaystyle{\rm Because\ we\ assumed\ that\ k\ is\ fixed,\ if\ N\ is\ very\ large}

\displaystyle{\rm the\ other\ terms\ are\ negligible.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}.

We plug this estimation back into the formula for
\mathbb{E}[M]
:

(6.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{N\binom{N^{2}}{k}\ -\ (\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil-1)\binom{(\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ the\ same\ argument\ as\ above}

\displaystyle{\rm to\ say\ that\ the\ term\ with\ k\ is\ negligible.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{2}}{k}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ plug\ in\ the\ estimate\ of\ the\ second\ term}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\ -\ \frac{\frac{(N-1)^{2k+1}}{2k+1}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}\cdot k!}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\bigg[1\ -\ \frac{1}{2k+1}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{N}\bigg)^{2k+1}\bigg]

\displaystyle{\rm We\ expand\ out\ the\ first\ two\ terms\ to\ estimate\ for\ (1-1/N)^{2k+1}.}

\displaystyle{\rm If\ we\ use\ more\ terms,\ the\ accuracy\ slightly\ increases,\ but\ the\ equation}

\displaystyle{\rm will\ not\ be\ invertible.\ Thus,\ we\ only\ write\ the\ first\ two\ terms.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\bigg[1-\frac{1}{2k+1}\bigg(1-\frac{2k+1}{N}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\bigg[\frac{2k}{2k+1}\bigg]\ +\ 1

We obtain a good estimate of
(1-1/N)^{2k+1}
by using two terms. Inverting the equation, we get

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \frac{2k+1}{2k}(m-1)

Now that we have a estimation formula for
\mathaccent 866{N}
, we run some simulations to see how accurate this estimation formula is. See Appendix
E
for the simulation. We see that the two dimensional formula does well as the variance is small. In the next subsubsection, we compare the one dimensional formula to the two dimensional formula and see which one does better.

N
. For the

2k
components of

2k
tanks from
N^{2}
possible tanks. This will give us a estimate for
N^{2}
, and we take the square root to find the estimate for

6.1.3.
Recursive Argument:

For another approach, we start with an initial estimate for
N
, which we call
N_{0}
. We construct a formula to recursively generate new estimates of
N
from previous; thus let
N_{1}
be our next guess. We investigate if the values of
N
converge, and if so if they converges to a more accurate estimate.

We first transform the
N^{2}
pairs into a list of numbers from 1 to
N^{2}
. For each pair (
X
,
Y
), we write the tanks as
(X-1)\ +\ N(Y-1)\ +\ 1
. This way, we can represent all tanks from 1 to
N^{2}
, as this maps the
N^{2}
pairs uniquely to the integers from 1 to
N^{2}
. Unfortunately, when we use observed tank values to estimate, we do not know the value of
N
so we cannot immediately use this formula. Instead we replace
N
with our estimate. With that estimate, we express a new
N
with the largest observed component. As before, let
M
denote the maximum of the two lists. Then

(6.15)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M=L)\ =\ \frac{\binom{L-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}.

Thus

(6.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{L=k}^{N^{2}}L\cdot\frac{\binom{L-1}{k-1}}{\binom{N^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ calculate\ and\ get}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\cdot\frac{N^{2}+1}{k+1}.

Thus

(6.17)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[L]\cdot\frac{k+1}{k}-1}.

In order to create a iterative process, we plug in
\mathbb{E}[L]\ =\ {\rm Max}X+({\rm current\ estimation\ for\ N})({\rm Max}Y-1)
. We rewrite our estimation for
\mathaccent 866{N}

(6.18)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \sqrt{\bigg[{\rm Max}X+({\rm current\ estimation\ for\ N})({\rm Max}Y-1)\bigg]\cdot\frac{k+1}{k}-1}.

Now, we’ve got a recursive function of
N
. We can use the preliminary estimation of
N
to get another estimate for
N
. Therefore, by starting with a value of
N
, we can continue to produce estimates of
N
. The hope is that by producing more values of
N
, the values converge to the actual number
N
. To see how well this process does, we attached the code for simulation in the Appendix
E
. Using the simulation, we plugged in different values of Max(
X
) and Max(
Y
). However, though the results converge, they do not do a better job as often the value it converges to is off from the actual
N
.

6.1.4.
Continuous Square Problem

The continuous square problem is slightly different from the discrete square as we can pick points from
(0,0)
to
(N,N)
. To calculate the PDF, we use the CDF method by calculating the CDF and taking the derivative. Let
m
denote the largest component observed:

(6.19)

\displaystyle CDF_{M}(m)\ =\ {\rm Prob}(M\leq m)\ =\ \left(\frac{m^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)^{k}\ =\ \frac{m^{2k}}{N^{2k}},

and thus

(6.20)

\displaystyle{\rm PDF}_{M}(m)\ =\ {\rm CDF_{M}}^{\prime}(m)\ =\ \frac{2k\cdot m^{2k-1}}{N^{2m}}.

Therefore

(6.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{N}m\cdot\frac{2k\cdot m^{2k-1}}{N^{2k}}\,dm

\displaystyle{\rm We\ calculate\ and\ get:}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k}{2k+1}\cdot N.

Solving yields

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m\cdot\frac{2k+1}{2k}.

Remark 6.3
.

We see that the scaling factor for the continuous case is the same as the scaling factor for the discrete case. The scaling factor of
(2k+1)/(2k)
is reasonable, and we see this by comparing this formula to the one dimensional formula. In the one dimensional case, the scaling factor was
(k+1)/(k)
, which is larger than
(2k+1)/(2k)
. However, because in the two dimensional case we are looking at the largest of both components, we have more data points and therefore we will likely get a larger
M
value. Therefore, in the two dimensional case, we would have to scale by a value smaller than
(k+1)/k
, and scaling by
(2k+1)/(2k)
makes sense.

6.2.
Circle Problem

6.2.1.
Discrete Circle Problem

The goal of the discrete circle problem is to find a formula that estimates the radius. We assume the circle is centered at
(0,0)
with radius
r
and we select
k
different lattice points contained in the circle without replacement. We look at
X^{2}+Y^{2}
as our statistic, because the resulting values are integers, and of course we can then take a square-root at the end.

We let
m_{1}=X^{2}+Y^{2}
. However, some elementary number theory enters in two dimensions and not all values of
m_{1}
are attainable. Notice that
X^{2}
and
Y^{2}
are each 0 or 1 modulo 4, so any attainable
m_{1}
is either 0, 1 or 2 modulo 4.

The number of lattice points inside a circle with radius
r
and center
(0,0)
is the well studied Gauss Circle problem
[
Co
]
. Let
P(r)
be the number of lattice points inside a circle in plane of radius
r
and center at the origin, i.e.,

(6.23)

\displaystyle P(r)\ =\ {\rm Number\ of}\ ({(q,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}|q^{2}+n^{2}\leq r^{2}}).

The number of lattice points inside the circle is well estimated by the area of the circle,
\pi r^{2}
; the challenge is determining the size of the error.

We have

(6.24)

\displaystyle P(r)\ =\ \pi r^{2}\ +\ E(r).

We do not need the best known results, so we write
E(r)
as
O(r^{\delta})
where
(0\leq\delta\leq 1)
, using Big-Oh notation (see Definition
2.10
); the current world record has
.5<\delta<.63
. Note
P(m)\ =\ CDF_{M_{m_{1}}}(M\leq m_{1})
, where
M
is the random variable that is the value of
X^{2}+Y^{2}
. We see that the
PDF_{M}(m)
is
P(m_{1})-P(m_{1}-1)
. Therefore, if we have
m_{1}\equiv 3\ ({\rm mod\ 4})
, then the
PDF_{M}(m_{1})=0
, and thus we don’t have to worry about this case, though for ease we do include it below.

We calculate the expected value of
M
:

(6.25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}m_{1}\cdot{\rm Prob}(M=m_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[P(1)-P(0)]+[2P(2)-2P(1)]+\dots+r^{2}[P(r^{2})-r^{2}P(r^{2}-1)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}P(r^{2})-[P(1)+P(2)+\dots+P(r^{2}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{1}{\binom{\pi r^{2}+O(r^{\delta})}{k}}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\pi(m-1)+O((m-1)^{\delta})}{k}.

We estimate the summation part of
\mathbb{E}[M]
by using Lemma
2.9
:

(6.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\pi(m_{1}-1)+O(m_{1}^{\delta})}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))\dots(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})-(k-1)).

Note we have

(6.27)

\displaystyle(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))^{k}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))^{k-1}

\displaystyle\ \ \leq\ (\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))\dots(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}-(k-1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))^{k}.

We use Euler-Maclaurin to obtain upper and lower bounds. As the analysis is similar to what we have done earlier, we omit the bounding of the error terms and concentrate on the main term:

(6.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))^{k}

\displaystyle\ \approx\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r^{2}}(\pi(m_{1}-1)+O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta}))^{k}\,dm

\displaystyle{\rm We\ neglect\ the\ Big\ O\ term,\ as\ it\ doesn^{\prime}t}

\displaystyle{\rm affect\ the\ final\ calculation\ by\ much}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\pi^{k}\int_{0}^{r^{2}}(m_{1}-1)^{k}

\displaystyle{\rm After\ evaluating\ the\ integrals,\ we\ get}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\pi^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}.

Now that we’ve estimated the main term, we calculate
\mathbb{E}[M]
.

(6.29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ \approx\

\displaystyle r^{2}-\frac{1}{\binom{\pi r^{2}+O(r^{\delta})}{k}\cdot k!}\cdot(\pi^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{\pi^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}}{\pi^{k}\cdot(r^{2})^{k}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\bigg[1-\frac{1}{k+1}\cdot(1-\frac{k+1}{r^{2}})\bigg]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\cdot\frac{k}{k+1}+1,

and thus

(6.30)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{r}\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{k+1}{k}(m_{1}-1)}.

Remark 6.4
.

We analyze the formula from the discrete circle problem. The formula is quite interesting, because we have a square root involved, and unlike other cases, the continuous and discrete setting have different scaling factors. First, we have the square root of
\mathbb{E}[M]
which is a value similar to
\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}}
, and is similar to
r
. Also, the scaling factor for the discrete case is
\sqrt{(k+1)/k}
, which is similar to
(2k+1)/(2k)
as we take the square root because taking the square root decreases the value by a little bit. Though the formula doesn’t completely align with the continuous circle, this formula makes a lot of sense, and the difference likely results from the different statistics that we looked at for the discrete and continuous circle.

6.2.2.
Continuous Circle Problem

The continuous circle problem has similar conditions as the discrete circle problem, but we can select any points contained in the circle; the points don’t necessarily have to be lattice points. We approach the continuous circle problem similarly as the continuous square problem. We look at
m_{2}=\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}}
because that is the formula for the radius. Let
m_{2}
be the largest observed statistic, and
M
the corresponding random variable. Then

(6.31)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob(\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}}\leq m_{2})}\ =\ \frac{(m_{2}^{2}\pi)^{k}}{(r^{2}\pi)^{k}}\ =\ \frac{m_{2}^{2k}}{r^{2k}}.

Thus

(6.32)

\displaystyle{\rm PDF}_{M}(m_{2})\ =\ {\rm CDF}_{M}^{\prime}(m_{2})\ =\ \frac{2k\cdot m_{2}^{2k-1}}{r^{2k}}.

The calculation for the expected value is the same as the continuous square, so we omit it, and we find

(6.33)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{r}\ =\ m_{2}\cdot\frac{2k+1}{2k}.

Remark 6.5
.

We compare the continuous circle formula to the discrete circle formula. They don’t look similar, as the discrete formula has a square root. Note that
m_{1}=m_{2}^{2}
, by the values of statistics we look at. Also, if we taylor expand
\sqrt{1+1/k}
, we get
\sqrt{1+1/k}\ =\ 1+1/2k+\dots
. In the discrete circle, as the value of
k
gets very very large, the
\sqrt{1+1/k}
looks like
1+1/2k
, which is the formula for the continuous circle. Thus, though the formulas look different, if we take the limit as
k
gets large, we see how similar these are.

7.1.
Generalized Square Problem

7.1.1.
Discrete Square Problem:

To calculate the formula for the discrete
L
-dimensional square, we use similar strategies as the two dimensional square. We let
M
be the the largest observed coordinate. Thus

(7.1)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M=m)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle F(m)-F(m-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{m^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}\ -\ \frac{\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{m^{L}}{k}-\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}.

The mean is thus

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}m\cdot{\rm PDF}_{M}(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{m\binom{m^{L}}{k}-m\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{m\binom{m^{L}}{k}-(m-1)\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}\ -\ \sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}\bigg[N\binom{N^{L}}{k}-(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)\binom{\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{L}}{k}\bigg]\ -\ \sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}.

We use Lemma
2.9
to bound the sums:

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N-1}\binom{m^{L}}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N-1}(m^{L})(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1)).

We have

(7.4)

\displaystyle m^{Lk}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}m^{Lk-L}\ \leq\ (m^{L})(m^{L}-1)\cdots(m^{L}-(k-1)\ \leq\ m^{Lk}.

We apply Euler-Maclaurin to the lower bound:

(7.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N-1}\bigg[m^{Lk}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}m^{Lk-L}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{Lk+1}-(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk+1}}{Lk+1}\ -\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\frac{(N-1)^{Lk-L+1}-(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-L+1}}{Lk-L+1}

\displaystyle+\ \frac{(N-1)^{Lk}+(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk}}{2}\ -\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\frac{(N-1)^{Lk-L}+(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-L}}{2}

\displaystyle\ +\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg[Lk\cdot(N-1)^{Lk-1}-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}(Lk-L)(N-1)^{Lk-L-1}

\displaystyle\ -\ Lk\cdot(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-1}\ +\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot(Lk-L)\cdot(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-1}\bigg]\ +\ R_{2}.

We bound the remainder term:

(7.6)

\displaystyle|R_{2}|

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\bigg[Lk\cdot(N-1)^{Lk-1}\ -\ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot(Lk-L)\cdot(N-1)^{Lk-L-1}

\displaystyle\ -\ (Lk)(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-1}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}(Lk-L)(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-L-1}\bigg].

Similarly we calculate the main term for the upper bound:

(7.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{Lk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{Lk+1}-(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk+1}}{Lk+1}\ +\ \frac{(N-1)^{Lk}+(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk}}{2}

\displaystyle+\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg[Lk\cdot(N-1)^{Lk-1}-Lk\cdot(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-1}\bigg]\ +\ R_{2}.

We bound the remainder term by

(7.8)

\displaystyle|R_{2}|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{12}\bigg[Lk\cdot(N-1)^{Lk-1}-Lk\cdot(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk-1}\bigg].

Now that we have fairly tight upper and lower bounds, we use the main term to find an equation of
m
in terms of
N
and
k
. As we are only interested in the case of
k
fixed and
N
tending to infinity, we can just keep the main term in the analysis below. We find

(7.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N-1}m^{Lk}

\displaystyle\ \approx\

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{Lk+1}-(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil)^{Lk+1}}{Lk+1}

\displaystyle{\rm Because\ we\ assumed\ that\ k\ is\ fixed,\ if\ N\ is\ very\ large}

\displaystyle{\rm the\ term\ with\ k\ is\ negligible.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)^{Lk+1}}{Lk+1}

We plug this estimation back into the formula for
\mathbb{E}[M]
:

(7.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{N\binom{N^{L}}{k}\ -\ (\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil-1)\binom{(\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ the\ same\ argument\ as\ above}

\displaystyle{\rm to\ say\ that\ the\ term\ with\ k\ is\ negligible.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\ -\ \sum_{m=\lceil\sqrt[L]{k}\rceil}^{N}\frac{\binom{(m-1)^{L}}{k}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ plug\ in\ the\ estimate\ of\ the\ second\ term}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\ -\ \frac{\frac{(N-1)^{Lk+1}}{Lk+1}}{\binom{N^{L}}{k}\cdot k!}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\bigg[1\ -\ \frac{1}{Lk+1}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{N}\bigg)^{Lk+1}\bigg]

\displaystyle{\rm We\ expand\ out\ the\ first\ two\ terms\ to\ estimate\ for\ (1-1/N)^{Lk+1}.}

\displaystyle{\rm If\ we\ use\ more\ terms,\ the\ accuracy\ slightly\ increases,\ but\ the\ equation}

\displaystyle{\rm will\ not\ be\ invertible.\ Thus,\ we\ only\ write\ the\ first\ two\ terms.}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle N\bigg[1-\frac{1}{Lk+1}\bigg(1-\frac{Lk+1}{N}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\bigg[\frac{Lk}{Lk+1}\bigg]\ +\ 1

Inverting the equation, we get

(7.11)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ \frac{Lk+1}{Lk}(m-1).

7.1.2.
Continuous Square Problem:

The continuous problem is easily generalized to
L
dimensions. We select
k
tuples of length
L
and let
M
be the largest observed component:

(7.12)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(M\leq m)\ =\ \left(\frac{m^{L}}{N^{L}}\right)^{k}\ =\ \frac{m^{Lk}}{N^{Lk}}.

(7.13)

\displaystyle{\rm PDF}_{M}(m)\ =\ {\rm CDF}_{M}^{\prime}(m)\ =\ \frac{Lk\cdot m^{Lk-1}}{N^{Lk}}.

Thus

(7.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]\ =\ \int_{0}^{N}m\cdot\frac{Lk\cdot m^{Lk-1}}{N^{Lk}}\,dm.

We solve the integral and we get:

(7.15)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{N}\ =\ m\cdot\frac{Lk+1}{Lk}.

Remark 7.1
.

We see that the scaling factors for the discrete
L
dimensional square continuous
L
dimensional square are the same. This scaling factor makes sense because if we have
L
dimensions, we have many more components to choose from. Thus by scaling by
(Lk+1)/(Lk)
, which is a value smaller than
(2k+1)/(2k)
, and is close to 1, we get a good estimate for
N
.

7.2.
Generalized Circle Problem

7.2.1.
Discrete Circle Problem

: We look at the generalized
L
dimensional circle problem. We study a statistic similar to the two dimensional circle:

(7.16)

\displaystyle X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}+\cdots+X_{L}^{2}\ =\ m_{1}

We see that all values of
m_{1}
are integers. Using this statistic, we end up estimating for
r^{2}
, so after getting the formula, we have to take the square root of
m_{1}
to get a estimation for
r
. Let
P(r)
be the number of lattice points inside an
L
-ball, a
L
-dimensional sphere with radius
r
. We use the volume of the
L
-ball to find a approximate value for the number of lattice points inside the
L
-ball. The formula for the volume of a
L
- ball is
[
Gi
]

(7.17)

\displaystyle V(n)\ =\ \frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}.

We use this formula to find
P(r)
. We denote the bounds with big O notation.

(7.18)

\displaystyle P(r)\ =\ \frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta}).

We now calculate the density using the CDF method.

(7.19)

\displaystyle{\rm PDF}_{M_{m_{1}}}\ =\ P(m_{1})-P(m_{1}-1).

We calculate the expected value

(7.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}m_{1}\cdot{\rm Prob}(M=m_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}m_{1}\cdot\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}m_{1}\ +\ O(m_{1}^{\delta})}{k}-\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}}{{\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta})}{k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta})}{k}}}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\bigg[m_{1}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}m_{1}\ +\ O(m_{1}^{\delta})}{k}

\displaystyle\ \ -\ (m_{1}-1)\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}\bigg]

\displaystyle\ -\ \frac{1}{{\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta})}{k}}}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ telescope\ and\ get}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{1}{{\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta})}{k}}}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}.

We calculate the summation part.

(7.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})\bigg)\dots

\displaystyle\ \bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})-(k-1)\bigg).

We use Lemma
2.9
to set bounds.

(7.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}^{k}

\displaystyle\ -\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}^{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \dots\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})-(k-1)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})}{k}^{k}.

We apply Euler-Maclaurin to both bounds. We omit the calculation here, and just show the approximation using the main term.

(7.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{r^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})\bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle\ \approx\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\ +\ O((m_{1}-1)^{\delta})\bigg)^{k}\,dm_{1}

\displaystyle{\rm We\ neglect\ the\ Big\ O\ term}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}(m_{1}-1)\bigg)^{k}\,dm_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}\bigg)^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}-(-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}\bigg)^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}.

We calculate the expected value.

(7.24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M]

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{1}{k!\binom{\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}r^{L}\ +\ O(r^{\delta})}{k}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}\bigg)^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{1}{\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}\bigg)^{k}\cdot r^{2k}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\pi^{\frac{L}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+1)}\bigg)^{k}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\ -\ \frac{1}{k+1}\cdot\frac{(r^{2}-1)^{k+1}}{r^{2k}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\bigg[1-\frac{1}{k+1}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\bigg)^{k+1}\bigg]

\displaystyle{\rm We\ use\ the\ first\ two\ terms\ of\ the\ binomial\ expansion}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\bigg[1-\frac{1}{k+1}\bigg(1-\frac{k+1}{r^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle r^{2}\cdot\frac{k}{k+1}+1.

We invert the relationship to get

(7.25)

\displaystyle\mathaccent 866{r}\ =\ \sqrt{(m_{1}-1)\cdot\frac{k+1}{k}}.
