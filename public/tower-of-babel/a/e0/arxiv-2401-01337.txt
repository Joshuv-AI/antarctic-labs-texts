Diagonal Gaussian Mixture Models and Higher Order Tensor Decompositions
Bingni Guo † \dagger, Jiawang Nie † \dagger, Zi Yang ‡ \ddagger

Abstract
. This paper studies how to recover parameters in diagonal Gaussian mixture models using tensors. High-order moments of the Gaussian mixture model are estimated from samples. They form incomplete symmetric tensors generated by hidden parameters in the model. We propose to use generating polynomials to compute incomplete symmetric tensor approximations. The obtained decomposition is utilized to recover parameters in the model. We prove that our recovered parameters are accurate when the estimated moments are accurate. Using high-order moments enables our algorithm to learn Gaussian mixtures with more components. For a given model dimension and order, we provide an upper bound of the number of components in the Gaussian mixture model that our algorithm can compute.

Introduction

Gaussian mixture models are widely used in statistics and machine learning
[
21
,
40
,
42
,
50
,
23
,
48
]
because of their simple formulation and superior fitting ability. Every smooth density can be approximated by Gaussian mixture models with enough components
[
16
]
. A Gaussian mixture model is a mixture of a few Gaussian distribution components. We consider a Gaussian mixture model of dimension
d
with
r
components. Suppose that the
i
th component obeys the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and the probability of the
i
th component is
\omega_{i}>0
. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the random vector for the Gaussian mixture, then its density function is

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}\det\Sigma_{i}}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}(y-\mu_{i})^{T}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(y-\mu_{i})\Big\}.

Learning the Gaussian mixture model is to recover the unknown model parameters
\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}
for
i\in[r]
from given independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples from the Gaussian mixture. The curse of dimensionality
[
3
]
is a challenging problem when learning Gaussian mixtures with full covariance matrices since the size of full covariance matrices grows quadratically with respect to the dimension
d
. Therefore, using a diagonal matrix to approximate the full variance matrix is a common approach to fit high dimensional Gaussian mixture models
[
38
]
.

Throughout this paper, we focus on learning Gaussian mixture models with diagonal covariance matrices, i.e.,

\Sigma_{i}=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{i1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{id}^{2}),\quad i=1,\ldots,r.

Let
M_{m}:=\mathbb{E}[y^{\otimes m}]
be the
m
th order moment of the Gaussian mixture and

(1.1)

{\mathcal{F}}_{m}:=\omega_{1}\mu_{1}^{\otimes m}+\cdots\omega_{r}\mu_{r}^{\otimes m}.

The third order moment
M_{3}
satisfies that

(1.2)

(M_{3})_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}=({\mathcal{F}}_{3})_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}\quad\text{for }i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq i_{3}\neq i_{1}.

Based on the property, the work
[
17
]
performs the incomplete tensor decomposition to learn diagonal Gaussian mixture models using partially given entries of the moment tensor when
r\leq\frac{d}{2}-1
. This result uses the first and third-order moments to recover unknown model parameters. However, the third order moment
M_{3}
is insufficient when
r>\frac{d}{2}-1
.

In this paper, we propose to utilize higher-order moments to learn Gaussian mixture models
with more components. The higher-order moments can be expressed
by means and covariance matrices as in the work
[
15
]
.
Let
{z}=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{t})
be a multivariate Gaussian random vector
with mean
\mu
and covariance
\Sigma
, then

(1.3)

\mathbb{E}[z_{1}\cdots z_{t}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in P_{t}\\
\lambda=\lambda_{p}\cup\lambda_{s}\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{(u,v)\in\lambda_{p}}\Sigma_{u,v}\prod\limits_{c\in\lambda_{s}}\mu_{c},

where
P_{t}
contains all distinct ways of partitioning
z_{1},\cdots,z_{t}
into two parts,
one part
\lambda_{p}
represents
p
pairs of
(u,v)
,
and another part
\lambda_{s}
consists of
s
singletons of
(c)
,
where
p\geq 0,\,s\geq 0
and
2p+s=t
.

We denote the label set
\Omega_{m}
for
m
th order tensor
M_{m}

(1.4)

\Omega_{m}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}):i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\text{ are distinct from each other}\}.

Let
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i})
be the random vector for the
i
th component of the diagonal Gaussian mixture model. For
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega_{m}
,
the expression
1.3
implies that

\displaystyle(M_{m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}[z_{i}^{\otimes m}]\right)_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\mathbb{E}[(z_{i})_{i_{1}}\cdots(z_{i})_{i_{m}}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in P_{t}\\
\lambda=\lambda_{p}\cup\lambda_{s}\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{(u,v)\in\lambda_{p}}(\Sigma_{i})_{u,v}\prod\limits_{c\in\lambda_{s}}(\mu_{i})_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}(\mu_{i})_{i_{1}}\cdots(\mu_{i})_{i_{m}}

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}},

where
P_{t}
contains all distinct ways of partitioning
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}
into two parts and
\lambda_{p},\lambda_{s}
are similarly defined as in
1.3
. When
\lambda_{p}\neq\emptyset
, we have
(\Sigma_{i})_{u,v}=0
for diagonal covariance matrices. Thus, we only need to consider
\lambda_{p}=\emptyset
and
\lambda_{s}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}
. It demonstrates the above equations. We conclude that the moment tensors for diagonal Gaussian mixtures satisfy

(1.5)

(M_{m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}=({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\mu_{i}^{\otimes m}
and
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega_{m}
.

When learning the Gaussian mixture from samples, the moment tensors
M_{m}
can be well approximated by samples. Therefore, the entries
({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}
can be obtained from the approximation of
M_{m}
by (
1.5
). This leads to the incomplete tensor decomposition problem. For an
m
th order symmetric tensor,
{\mathcal{F}}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
is known if
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega_{m}
, we wish to find vectors
q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}
such that

(1.6)

{\mathcal{F}}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}=(q_{1}^{\otimes m}+\cdots+q_{r}^{\otimes m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}\quad\text{for all }(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega_{m}.

To solve the above incomplete tensor decomposition, one approach is
to complete the unknown entries first and then do the regular tensor decomposition.
We refer to
[
13
,
27
,
29
,
46
,
49
]
for low rank tensor completion.
However, the low-rank completion is not guaranteed.

In this paper, we propose an algorithm to solve the incomplete tensor decomposition problem by generating polynomials and then utilize the decomposition to recover parameters in the Gaussian mixture. They are described in Algorithm
3.4
and Algorithm
5.1
. For a fixed dimension and tensor order, we provide the largest number of components, i.e., the tensor rank, our algorithm can compute in Theorem
3.7
.

Related Work

The Gaussian mixture model is a classic statistical model with broad applications in applied statistics and machine learning. Many techniques have been proposed to estimate unknown parameters for Gaussian mixture models given samples.

Expectation-maximization(EM) algorithm is one popular method that begins with some initial estimates of the mixture of Gaussians and then iteratively updates the approximation to improve its likelihood
[
11
]
. It is well known that it often gets stuck in local optima or fails to converge
[
41
]
. Dasgupta
[
8
]
proposed an algorithm to recover parameters in randomly low-dimensional projected subspace and required certain separation conditions for the mean vectors of the model. We refer to
[
9
,
43
,
47
,
1
]
more works on the EM algorithm.

The method of moments is another approach, introduced by Pearson
[
37
]
.
Moitra and Valiant
[
26
]
proposed a learning algorithm
to search for the best parameters over a discretized set to match the empirical moments. Kalai, et al.
[
20
]

proposed a randomized algorithm for a mixture of two high dimensional Gaussians with arbitrary covariance matrices.
The algorithm reduces the model to a univariate problem and solves it using moments up to order six. Hsu and Kakade
[
19
]
and Anandkumar et al.
[
2
]
used moments up to order three to learn Gaussian mixtures of spherical covariances
(\Sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}I)
. Ge et al.
[
15
]
provided a learning algorithm for Gaussian mixtures of general covariances using moments up to order six.
This algorithm requires
d\geq\Omega(r^{2})
. Algebraic methods using moments and polynomials can be applied to fit diagonal or spherical Gaussian mixtures, as in Guo et at.
[
17
]
, Khouja et al.
[
22
]
, Lindberg et al.
[
25
]
, Belkin and Sinha
[
4
]
, and Bhaskara et al.
[
5
]
.
Recently, Pereira et al.
[
39
]
, Sherman and Kolda
[
44
]

proposed moment-matching approaches based on numerical optimization.
These approaches do not need the explicit formulation of large symmetric tensors, for reducing computation and storage costs.

Contributions

This paper proposes a novel algorithm to learn diagonal Gaussian mixture models. The proposed algorithm is based on high-order moment tensors, incomplete tensor decompositions, and generating polynomials. Let
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
be i.i.d. samples from the diagonal Gaussian mixture. Learning the Gaussian mixture is to estimate the model parameters
\{\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
, where each
\Sigma_{i}
is diagonal.

Let
M_{m}
be the
m
th order moment of the Gaussian mixture respectively. For the tensors in (
1.1
), the entries
({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{i_{1}\ldots i_{m}}
are known from
M_{m}
for distinct labels
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}
by (
1.5
). We use generating polynomials to compute the decomposition of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
. We first construct a set of linear systems that only use the known entries in
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and then solve the linear systems to get a set of special generating polynomials. The common zeros of the generating polynomials can be obtained from corresponding eigenvalue decompositions. Under some genericity conditions, we can recover the decomposition of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
using the common zeros. Finally, the unknown parameters
\{\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
can be obtained by solving linear systems using the tensor decomposition and lower order moments. In real applications, the moment
M_{m}
is estimated from samples, which are not accurately given. In such a case, our algorithm can still find a good approximation of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and use the approximation to estimate the unknown parameters. We prove that the estimated parameters are accurate provided that the estimated moment tensors are accurate.

The previous work
[
17
]
only uses the first and third order moments to learn the Gaussian mixture model. Thus, the number of components must satisfy
r\leq\frac{d}{2}-1
. The proposed algorithms in this work use high order moments and hence can learn Gaussian mixture models with more components. For a given highest order
m
, we provide the largest number of components our algorithm can compute in Theorem
3.7
. Conversely, Theorem
3.7
can be used to find the smallest order
m
needed for the given number of components.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review symmetric tensor decompositions and generating polynomials. In Section
3
, we develop a novel algorithm that uses generating polynomials to solve the incomplete tensor decomposition problem arising from the Gaussian mixture problem. Section
4
discusses how to find the tensor approximation when errors exist and provides the error analysis. Section
5
provides the algorithm to learn Gaussian mixture models using incomplete symmetric tensor decompositions. Section
6
presents the numerical experiments for the proposed algorithms.

3.
Incomplete Symmetric Tensor Decompositions

In this section, we discuss how to solve the incomplete symmetric tensor decomposition problem arising from learning Gaussian mixtures with parameters
\{\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}\in S^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the symmetric tensor. In the following, we discuss how to obtain the decomposition of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
given entries
({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}
.

For convenience, we denote
n\coloneqq d-1
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
has the decomposition

(3.1)

{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\omega_{1}\mu_{1}^{\otimes m}+\cdots\omega_{r}\mu_{r}^{\otimes m},

where
\mu_{i}=((\mu_{i})_{0},(\mu_{i})_{1},\ldots,(\mu_{i})_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.
When the leading entry of each
\mu_{i}
is nonzero, we can write the decomposition (
3.1
) as

(3.2)

{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\lambda_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
u_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\otimes m}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
u_{r}\end{bmatrix}^{\otimes m},

where
\lambda_{i}=\omega_{i}((\mu_{1})_{0})^{m}
, and
u_{i}=((u_{i})_{1},\ldots,(u_{i})_{n})=(\mu_{i})_{1:n}/(\mu_{i})_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Let
1\leq p\leq m-2
and
p\leq k\leq n-m-p

be numbers such that

\binom{k}{p}\geq r\quad\text{and}\quad\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\geq r.

Define the set

(3.3)

\mathscr{B}_{0}\subseteq\{x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{p}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq k\}

such that
\mathscr{B}_{0}
consists of the first
r
monomials in the graded lexicographic order.
Correspondingly, the set
\mathscr{B}_{1}
is defined as

(3.4)

\mathscr{B}_{1}\coloneqq\{x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{p+1}}:1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{p}\leq k<j_{p+1}\leq n\}.

For convenience, we say
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is in
\mathscr{B}_{0}
(resp.
\mathscr{B}_{1}
)
if
x^{\alpha}\in

\mathscr{B}_{0}
(resp.
\mathscr{B}_{1}
).
Let
\alpha=e_{j_{1}}+\cdots+e_{j_{p}}+e_{j_{p+1}}\in\mathscr{B}_{1}
and

G\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathscr{B}_{0}\times\mathscr{B}_{1}}

be a matrix labelled by monomials in
\mathscr{B}_{0}
and
\mathscr{B}_{1}
. We consider the polynomial

\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{p}j_{p+1}}[G](x)\,\coloneqq\,\sum\limits_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in\mathscr{B}_{0}}G(\sum_{t=1}^{p}e_{i_{t}},\,\sum_{t=1}^{p+1}e_{j_{t}})x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{p}}-x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{p}}x_{j_{p+1}}.

Recall that
\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{p+1}}[G](x)

is a generating polynomial for
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
if it satisfies (
2.3
), i.e.

\langle\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{p}j_{p+1}}[G](x)\cdot x^{\beta},\ \mathcal{F}_{m}\rangle=0\quad\forall\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{m-p-1}^{n}.

The matrix
G
is called a generating matrix if
\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{p+1}}[G](x)
is a generating polynomial.
If the matrix
G
is a generating matrix of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
, it should satisfy the equations

(3.5)

\langle x_{s_{1}}\cdots x_{s_{m-p-1}}\varphi_{j_{1}\cdots j_{p+1}}[G](x),\,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}\rangle=0

for each
\alpha=e_{j_{1}}+\cdots+e_{j_{p}}+e_{j_{p+1}}\in\mathscr{B}_{1}
and each tuple
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-p-1})\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
, where

\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\left\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-p-1}):\begin{array}[]{l}k+1\leq s_{1}<\ldots<s_{m-p-1}\leq n,\\
s_{1}\neq j_{p+1},\ldots,s_{m-p-1}\neq j_{p+1}\end{array}\right\}.

Define the matrix
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
and the vector

be such that

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]_{\gamma,\beta}&\coloneqq({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\beta+\gamma},\quad\forall(\gamma,\beta)\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}\times\mathscr{B}_{0}\\
b[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]_{\gamma}&\coloneqq({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\alpha+\gamma},\quad\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}.\end{aligned}\right.

The dimension of
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
is
\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\times r
and the equations in (
3.5
) can be equivalently written as

(3.7)

A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]\cdot G(:,\alpha)=b[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}].

Lemma
3.1
proves that the matrix

in (

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
\binom{k}{p}\geq r
and
\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\geq r
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
be the tensor with the decomposition (
3.2
).
If vectors
\{[u_{i}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and
\{[u_{i}]_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\alpha}}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are both linearly independent, then the matrix
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
as in (

Proof.

The matrix

can be written as

A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}[u_{i}]_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\alpha}}[u_{i}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}^{T}.

Therefore,
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
has full column rank.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

A successful construction of

requires that

The vectors

Under the condition of Lemma

Theorem 3.3

Let

for the tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
.

Proof.

We first prove the existence of

. For
1\leq i\leq r
,
k+1\leq j\leq n
, we denote

d_{j}=((u_{1})_{j},\ldots,(u_{r})_{j}).

Under the assumption of Lemma
3.1
, we can define

N_{j}=([u_{1}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}},\ldots,[u_{r}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}})\mbox{diag}(d_{j})([u_{1}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}},\ldots,[u_{r}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}})^{-1}.

The matrix
G
is constructed as

G(\beta,\nu+e_{j})=N_{j}(G)_{\nu,\beta}

for
j=k+1,\ldots,n
and
\nu,\beta\in\mathscr{B}_{0}
. For every
\alpha=\nu+e_{j}\in\mathscr{B}_{1}
, it holds that

\sum_{\beta\in{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}G(\beta,\alpha)u_{i}^{\beta}-u_{i}^{\alpha}=N_{j}(G)_{\nu,:}[u_{i}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}-(u_{i})_{j}u_{i}^{\nu}=0

Thus, for every
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{m-p-1}
, it holds that

\langle x^{\gamma}\varphi_{\alpha}[G](x),{\mathcal{F}}_{m}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}u_{i}^{\gamma}\sum_{\theta\in{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}(G(\theta,\alpha)u_{i}^{\theta}-u_{i}^{\alpha})=0.

It proves that the matrix
G
is a generating matrix for
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
.

Next, we show the uniqueness. The matrix
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
has full column rank by Lemma
3.1
, so the generating matrix
G
is uniquely determined by linear systems in (
3.7
). It proves the uniqueness of

.
∎

By Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
3.1

).
Let

Then we have

for the vectors (

(3.9)

Lemma 3.6
.

If
n\geq\max\{2m-1,\frac{m^{2}}{4}-1\}
, then

(3.16)

\displaystyle\max\left(\binom{k^{*}}{p^{*}},\binom{n-k^{*}-2}{m-p^{*}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{p\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,m-2]}\max\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cap[p,n-m+p]}\left(\min\left(\binom{k}{p},\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\right)\right),

where
p^{*}=\lfloor\frac{m-1}{2}\rfloor
and
k^{*}
is largest
k
such that

\binom{k}{p^{*}}\leq\binom{n-k-1}{m-p^{*}-1}
.

Proof.

For a fixed
p\in[1,\frac{m-1}{2}]\cap\mathbb{N}
, it holds that
\binom{k}{p}
is increasing in
k
and
\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}
is decreasing in
k
. For the fixed
p
, let
k_{p}
be the largest
k
such that

\binom{k}{p}\leq\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}.

It holds that

r_{p}:=\max\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cap[p,n-m+p]}\left(\min\left(\binom{k}{p},\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\right)\right)=\max\left(\binom{k_{p}}{p},\binom{n-k_{p}-2}{m-p-1}\right).

For
p\in(\frac{m-1}{2},m-2]\cap\mathbb{N}
and
k\in[p,n-m+p]
, let
p^{\prime}=m-p-1
and
k^{\prime}=n-k-1
. We can verify that
p^{\prime}\in[1,\frac{m-1}{2}]
,
k^{\prime}\in[p^{\prime},n-m+p^{\prime}]
, and

\min\left(\binom{k}{p},\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\right)=\min\left(\binom{k^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}},\binom{n-k^{\prime}-1}{m-p^{\prime}-1}\right).

Therefore, it holds that
\max_{p\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,m-2]}r_{p}=\max_{p\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,p^{*}]}r_{p}
. Next, we will prove
\max_{p}r_{p}=r_{p*}
by showing
r_{p}\geq r_{p-1}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}\cap[2,p^{*}]
.

When
p\leq\frac{m-1}{2}
and
n\geq 2m-1
, we have
p\leq m-p-1\leq n-1-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor-p
. Hence,

\binom{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\leq\binom{n-1-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}{p}=\binom{n-1-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}{n-1-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor-p}\leq\binom{n-1-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}{m-p-1}.

The above equation implies
k_{p}\geq\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor\geq\frac{n-2}{2}
.

If
k_{p-1}<k_{p}
, then it holds that

r_{p-1}\leq\binom{k_{p-1}+1}{p-1}\leq\binom{k_{p}}{p-1}=\binom{k_{p}}{p}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}\leq\binom{k_{p}}{p}\frac{m-1}{n-m}\leq\binom{k_{p}}{p}\leq r_{p}.

In the following proof, we show
r_{p-1}\leq r_{p}
if
k_{p-1}\geq k_{p}
.

Case 1:

\binom{k_{p}}{p}>\binom{n-k_{p}-2}{m-p-1}
. In this case,
r_{p}=\binom{k_{p}}{p}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1},

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}}{p}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}+i}{k_{p}-p+1+i},

for
C\geq 0,p^{\prime}=m-p-1,k_{p}^{\prime}=n-k_{p}-1
. By direct computation, we have

\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1}\frac{k_{p}+i}{k_{p}-p+1+i}\leq 1\Leftrightarrow k_{p}m-np+n-k_{p}+im-i\geq 0.

The inequalities
n-m+p\geq k_{p}\geq\frac{n-2}{2},p\leq\frac{m-1}{2},n\geq 2m-1,i\geq 0
imply

k_{p}m-np+n-k_{p}+im-i\geq n-m+i(m-1)+1\geq 0.

It proves

(3.17)

\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1}\frac{k_{p}+i}{k_{p}-p+1+i}\leq 1,\,\text{for $i\geq 0$. }

If
p=\frac{m-1}{2}
, then
p=m-p-1=p^{\prime}
and
k_{p}=\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
. If
n
is even, then
k_{p}=\frac{n-2}{2}
and
\binom{k_{p}}{p}=\binom{n-k_{p}-2}{m-p-1}
, which does not satisfy the assumption of Case 1. If
n
is odd, then
k_{p}=\frac{n-1}{2}
and
k_{p}=k_{p}^{\prime}
. Thus, we have
\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}}=\binom{k_{p}}{p}
and
\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}=\frac{k_{p}-p}{p+1}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}<1
. These inequalities and (
3.17
) imply that

\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1}\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}<\binom{k_{p}}{p}^{2}\Rightarrow\min\left(\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1},\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}\right)<r_{p}.

Then, we consider
p\leq\frac{m-2}{2}
. Under the assumption of Case 1, we have

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\binom{k_{p}}{p}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-i-p^{\prime}}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i+1}.

It holds that
\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}\leq 1
if and only if
k_{p}m+m+(p-m-n)p\geq 0
. We observe that
k_{p}m+m+(p-m-n)p
is increasing in
k_{p}
and decreasing in
p
. When
p\leq\frac{m-2}{2}
, we have

k_{p}m+m+(p-m-n)p\geq\frac{n-2}{2}m+m+(\frac{m-2}{2}-m-n)\frac{m-2}{2}=n+1-\frac{m^{2}}{4}.

As a result,
\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}+1}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+1}\leq 1
when
n\geq\frac{m^{2}}{4}-1
. This inequality and (
3.17
) imply

\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1}\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}<\binom{k_{p}}{p}^{2}\Rightarrow\min\left(\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1},\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}\right)<r_{p}.

For all choices of
p
, the above inequality holds. Under the assumption that
k_{p-1}\geq k_{p}
, we have
r_{p-1}=\min\left(\binom{k_{p}+C}{p-1},\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-C}{p^{\prime}+1}\right)
for some
C\geq 0
.
It proves that
r_{p}>r_{p-1}
when
n\geq\max\{2m-1,\frac{m^{2}}{4}-1\}
under the assumption of Case 1.

Case 2:

\binom{k_{p}}{p}\leq\binom{n-k_{p}-2}{m-p-1}
. In this case,
r_{p}=\binom{n-k_{p}-2}{m-p-1}
. Similar to Case 1, we can show

\binom{k_{p}+1+C}{p-1}<\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}+1}{k_{p}+1-p}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+2}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}+1+i}{k_{p}-p+2+i}

\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-1-C}{p^{\prime}+1}<\binom{k_{p}^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{C}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}-1-i}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i}

and
\frac{k_{p}+1+i}{k_{p}-p+2+i}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}-1-i}{k_{p}^{\prime}-i}\leq 1
for
i\geq-1
. We observe that
\frac{k_{p}+1}{k_{p}+1-p}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+2}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}+1}
is decreasing in
k_{p}
and increasing in
p
. Recall that
k\geq\frac{n-2}{2},p\leq\frac{m-1}{2}
, so we can show

(3.18)

\frac{k_{p}+1}{k_{p}+1-p}\frac{p}{k_{p}-p+2}\frac{k_{p}^{\prime}-p^{\prime}-1}{p^{\prime}+1}\leq 1\Leftarrow 4n^{2}-\beta n+\gamma\geq 0,

where
\beta=m^{2}-2m-8
and
\gamma=m^{3}-4m^{2}-4m+16
.
The inequality on the right of (

) is quadratic in

, so it holds when

or

The assumption

implies that

n\geq\frac{m^{2}}{4}-1\geq\frac{\beta}{4}\geq\frac{\beta+\sqrt{\beta^{2}-16\gamma}}{8}.

Therefore, the inequality (
3.18
) holds. Similar to Case 1, it concludes the proof of

for Case 2.

Summarizing everything above, we prove that (
3.16
) holds for

.
∎

Incomplete Tensor Approximations and Error Analysis

When learning Gaussian mixture models, the subtensor

is estimated from samples and is not exactly given. In such case, Algorithm
3.4
can still find a good low-rank approximation of

Let
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}
be approximations of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
. Given the subtensor
(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}
, we can find a low-rank approximation of
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
following Algorithm
3.4
. We define the matrix
A[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]
and the vector

in the same way as in (
3.6
), for each
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}_{1}
. Then we have the following linear least square problem

(4.1)

\min_{g_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}}\quad\left\|A[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]\cdot g_{\alpha}-b[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]\right\|^{2}.

For each
\alpha\in{\mathscr{B}}_{1}
, we solve (
4.1
) to get
\widehat{G}[:,\alpha]
which is an approximation of
G[:,\alpha]
. Combining all
\widehat{G}[:,\alpha]
’s, we get
\widehat{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}\times{\mathscr{B}}_{1}}
approximating the generating matrix
G
. Similar to (
3.8
), for
l=k+1,\ldots,n
, we define
N_{l}(\widehat{G})
as an approximation of

and let

(4.2)

\widehat{N}(\xi)\coloneqq\xi_{k+1}N_{k+1}(\widehat{G})+\cdots+\xi_{n}N_{n}(\widehat{G}),

where
\xi=(\xi_{k+1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is a generic vector. Let
\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{r}

(4.3)

\hat{w}_{i}\coloneqq(\hat{v}_{i}^{H}N_{k+1}(\widehat{G})\hat{v}_{i},\cdots,\hat{v}_{i}^{H}N_{n}(\widehat{G})\hat{v}_{i})\quad i=1,\ldots,r.

For the sets

defined in (

), we solve the linear least square problem

(4.4)

\min\limits_{(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r})}\left\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{J_{1}\cdot J_{2}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{i}\otimes[\hat{w}_{i}]_{J_{3}}\right\|^{2}.

Let
(\hat{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{r})
be the minimizer of the above problem. Then, we consider the following linear least square problem

(4.5)

\min\limits_{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})}\left\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{x_{j}\cdot J_{1}^{-j}\cdot J_{2}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}(v_{i})_{j}\cdot\hat{\gamma}_{i}\otimes[\hat{w}_{i}]_{J_{3}}\right\|^{2}.

We obtain
(\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{r})
by solving the above problem for
j=1,\ldots,k
. Let
\hat{u}=(\hat{v},\hat{w})
. Then, we have the following linear least square problem

(4.6)

\min\limits_{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})}\left\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\cdot\left(\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\hat{u}_{r}\end{bmatrix}^{\otimes m}\right)_{\Omega_{m}}\right\|^{2}.

Denote the minimizer of (
4.6
) as
(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{r})
.
For
i=1,\ldots,r
, let

\hat{q}_{i}\coloneqq(\hat{\lambda}_{i})^{1/m}(1,\hat{v}_{i},\hat{w}_{i}).

Now, we obtain the approximation of the tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}

{\mathcal{F}}_{m}\approx(\hat{q}_{1})^{\otimes m}+\cdots+(\hat{q}_{r})^{\otimes m}.

This result may not be optimal due to sample errors. We can get a more accurate approximation by using
(\hat{q}_{1},\ldots,\hat{q}_{r})
as starting points to solve the nonlinear optimization

(4.7)

\min\limits_{(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})}\,\left\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}^{\otimes m})_{\Omega_{m}}\right\|^{2}.

We denote the minimizer of the optimization (
4.7
) as
(q_{1}^{*},\ldots,q_{r}^{*})
.

We summarize the above calculations as a tensor approximation algorithm in Algorithm
4.1
.

Algorithm 4.1
.

(Incomplete symmetric tensor approximation.)

Input:

The rank
r
, the dimension
d
, the constant
p
, and the subtensor

as in (

Step 1.

Determine the generating matrix
\widehat{G}

) for each
\alpha\in\mathscr{B}_{1}

Step 2.

Choose a generic vector

) and (

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\omega_{1}(\mu_{1})^{\otimes m}+\cdots+\omega_{r}(\mu_{r})^{\otimes m}
as in (
3.1
) and constants

be such that
\min\left(\binom{k}{p},\binom{n-k-1}{m-p-1}\right)\geq r
. We assume the following conditions:

(i)

the scalars
\omega_{i}
and the leading entry of each

are nonzero;

(ii)

the vectors
\{[(\mu_{i})_{1:n}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}\}_{i=1}^{r}

(iii)

the vectors
\{[(\mu_{i})_{1:n}]_{\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}}\}_{i=1}^{r}

\alpha\in{\mathscr{B}}_{1}
;

(iv)

the eigenvalues of the matrix
N(\xi)
in (

)
are distinct from each other.

Let

and

.
If the distance
\epsilon:=\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{{\Omega}_{m}}\|
is small enough, then there exist scalars
\tilde{\eta}_{i},\eta_{i}^{*}
such that

(\hat{\eta}_{i})^{m}=(\eta_{i}^{*})^{m}=1,\quad\|\hat{\eta}_{i}\hat{q}_{i}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),\quad\|\eta_{i}^{*}{q}^{*}_{i}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),

up to a permutation of
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})
,
where the constants inside

only depend on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and the choice of
\xi
in Algorithm
4.1
.

Proof.

The vectors
(1,u_{1}),\,\ldots,\,(1,u_{r})
in (

) are scalar multiples of
\mu_{1},\,\ldots,\mu_{r}
respectively. By Conditions (ii) and (iii), the vectors
\{[u_{i}]_{{\mathscr{B}}_{0}}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and
\{[u_{i}]_{{\mathcal{O}}_{\alpha}}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are both linearly independent, which satisfies the condition of Lemma
3.1
. Thus Conditions (i)-(iii) imply that there exists a unique generating matrix
G
for
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
by Theorem
3.3
and it can be calculated by (
3.7
). By Lemma
3.1
, the matrix
A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]
has full column rank. It holds that

(4.8)

\|A[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]-A[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]\|\leq\epsilon,\,\|b[\alpha,{\mathcal{F}}_{m}]-b[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]\|\leq\epsilon,

for
\alpha\in{\mathscr{B}}_{1}
. When
\epsilon
is small enough, the matrix
A[\alpha,\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}]
also has full column rank. Then the linear least square problems (
4.1
) have unique solutions and the collection of solutions
\widehat{G}
satisfies that

\|G-\widehat{G}\|=O(\epsilon),

where

depends on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
(see
[
10
, Theorem 3.4]
). Since
N_{l}(\widehat{G})
is part of the generating matrix
\widehat{G}
for each

, we have

\|N_{l}(\widehat{G})-{N}_{l}(G)\|\leq\|\widehat{G}-{G}\|=O(\epsilon),\quad l=k+1,\ldots,n,

which implies that
\|\widehat{N}(\xi)-{N}(\xi)\|=O(\epsilon)
. By condition (iv) we know that the matrix
\widehat{N}(\xi)
has distinct eigenvalues
\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{r}
if
\epsilon
is small enough. So the matrix
N(\xi)
has a set of eigenvalues
\tilde{w}_{i}
such that

\|\hat{w}_{i}-\tilde{w}_{i}\|=O(\epsilon).

This follows from Proposition 4.2.1 in
[
6
]
. The constants inside the above

depend only on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and
\xi
. The vectors
\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{w}_{r}
are multiples of the vectors

(\mu_{1})_{k+1:n},\ldots,(\mu_{r})_{k+1:n}

respectively. Thus, we conclude that
[\tilde{w}_{1}]_{J_{3}},\ldots,[\tilde{w}_{r}]_{J_{3}}
are linearly independent by condition (iii). When
\epsilon
is small, the vectors

[\hat{w}_{1}]_{J_{3}},\ldots,[\hat{w}_{r}]_{J_{3}}

are also linearly independent. For optimizers
\hat{\gamma}_{i},\,\hat{v}_{i},\,\hat{\lambda}_{i}
of linear least square problems (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), by
[
10
, Theorem 3.4]
, we have

\|\hat{\gamma}_{i}-\gamma_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),\,\|\hat{v}_{i}-v_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),\,\|\hat{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),

where constants inside
O(\cdot)
depend on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and
\xi
. By Theorem
3.5
, we have
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{q}_{i}^{\otimes m}
where
\tilde{q}_{i}=(\tilde{\lambda})^{1/m}(1,\tilde{v}_{i},\tilde{w}_{i})
.The rank-

decomposition of

is unique up to scaling and permutation by Theorem

. Thus, there exist scalars
\hat{\eta}_{i}
such that
(\hat{\eta}_{i})^{m}=1
and
\hat{\eta}_{i}\tilde{q}_{i}=q_{i}
, up to a permutation of
q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}
. Then for
\hat{q}_{i}=(\hat{\lambda})^{1/m}(1,\hat{v}_{i},\hat{w}_{i})
, we have
\|\hat{\eta}_{i}\hat{q}_{i}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
where constants inside

depend on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and
\xi
.

Since
\|\hat{\eta}_{i}\hat{q}_{i}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)

\|{\mathcal{F}}_{m}-(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\hat{q}_{i})^{\otimes m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|=O(\epsilon)
. For the minimizer
(q_{1}^{*},\ldots,q_{r}^{*})
of (

), it holds that

\left\|\left(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m}\right)_{\Omega_{m}}\right\|\leq\left\|\left(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\hat{q}_{i})^{\otimes m}\right)_{\Omega_{m}}\right\|=O(\epsilon).

For the tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m}
, we have

\|({\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|\leq\|({\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*}-\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|+\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|=O(\epsilon)

If we apply Algorithm
4.1
to

, we will get the exact decomposition

. By repeating the above argument, similarly we can obtain that
\|\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
for some
\eta_{i}^{*}
such that
(\eta_{i}^{*})^{m}=1
, where the constants in

only depend on
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
and
\xi
.

5.
Learning General Diagonal Gaussian Mixture

Let
y
be the random variable of a diagonal Gaussian mixture model and

y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
be i.i.d. samples drawn from the model.
The moment tensors
M_{m}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[y^{\otimes m}]
can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}(y_{1}^{\otimes m}+\cdots+y_{N}^{\otimes m}).

Recall that
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\mu_{i}^{\otimes m}
. By Corollary
1.3
and (
1.5
), we have

\displaystyle(M_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}},

where
\Omega_{m}
is the index set defined in (
1.4
). Let
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}
be such that

(5.1)

(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\coloneqq(\widehat{M}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}.

We can apply Algorithm
4.1
to find the low-rank approximation of
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}
. Let
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m}
be the tensor approximation generated by Algorithm
4.1
. By Theorem
4.2
, when
\epsilon=\|(\widehat{M}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}-({M}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|
is small, there exists
\eta_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\eta_{i}^{m}=1
and
\|\eta_{i}q_{i}^{*}-(\omega_{i})^{1/m}\mu_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
. The
\eta_{i}
appears here because the vector
q_{i}^{*}
can be complex even though
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}
is a real tensor. But
\omega_{i},\mu_{i}
are both real in Gaussian mixture models. In practice, we can choose the
\eta_{i}
from all
m
th roots of
1
that minimizes
\|{\text{Im}}(\eta_{i}q_{i}^{*})\|
. Let

(5.2)

\check{q}_{i}\coloneqq\real(\eta_{i}q_{i}^{*}).

We expect that
\check{q}_{i}\approx(\omega_{i})^{1/m}\mu_{i}
. Then, we consider the tensor

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}=\omega_{1}\mu_{1}^{\otimes t}+\cdots+\omega_{r}\mu_{r}^{\otimes t}\approx(\omega_{1})^{\frac{m-t}{m}}(\check{q}_{1})^{\otimes t}+\cdots+(\omega_{r})^{\frac{m-t}{m}}(\check{q}_{r})^{\otimes t},

where
t
is the smallest number such that
\binom{d}{t}\geq r
. It holds that
({{\mathcal{F}}}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}=({M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\approx(\widehat{M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}
, so we obtain the scalars
(\omega_{i})^{\frac{m-t}{m}}
by solving the linear least square problem

(5.3)

\min\limits_{(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+}}\,\left\|(\widehat{M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}\left((\check{q}_{i})^{\otimes t}\right)_{\Omega_{t}}\right\|^{2}.

Let the optimizer of (
5.3
) be
(\beta_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{r}^{\ast})
, then

(5.4)

\hat{\omega}_{i}=(\beta^{*})^{\frac{m}{m-t}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mu}_{i}=q^{*}_{i}/(\beta_{i}^{*})^{\frac{1}{m-t}}

should be reasonable approximations of
\omega_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
respectively.

To obtain more accurate results, we can use
(\hat{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\omega}_{r},\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{r})
as starting points to solve the following nonlinear optimization

(5.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r},\\
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}\end{subarray}}&\|(\widehat{M}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}(\mu_{i}^{\otimes m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|^{2}+\|(\widehat{M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}(\mu_{i}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&\omega_{1}+\cdots+\omega_{r}=1,\,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{r}\geq 0,\end{array}\right.

and obtain the optimizer
(\omega_{1}^{*},\ldots,\omega_{r}^{*},\mu_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mu_{r}^{*})
.

Next, we will show how to calculate the diagonal covariance matrices. We define a label set

L_{j}=\{(j,j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-2}):1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m-2}\leq d,\text{and }i_{1}\neq j,\ldots,i_{m-2}\neq j\}.

For
(j,j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-2})\in L_{j}
, we have

(M_{m})_{j,j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-2}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\left((\mu_{i})_{j}(\mu_{i})_{j}(\mu_{i})_{i_{1}}\cdots(\mu_{i})_{i_{m-2}}+\Sigma^{(i)}_{jj}(\mu_{i})_{i_{1}}\cdots(\mu_{i})_{i_{m-2}}\right).

The above equation is a direct result of (
1.3
) since all covariance matrices are diagonal. Let

(5.6)

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq M_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\widehat{M}_{m}-(\check{q}_{1})^{\otimes m}-\cdots-(\check{q}_{r})^{\otimes m}.

To get the estimation of covariance matrices
\Sigma_{i}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{i1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{id}^{2})
, we solve the nonnegative linear least square problems
(j=1,\ldots,d)

(5.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{(\theta_{1j},\ldots,\theta_{rj})}&\left\|\left(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\right)_{L_{j}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\theta_{ij}\omega^{*}_{i}\left((\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m-2}\right)_{\hat{L}_{j}}\right\|^{2}\\
\mbox{subject to}&\theta_{1j}\geq 0,\ldots,\,\theta_{rj}\geq 0\end{array}\right.

where
\hat{L}_{j}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-2}):(j,j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-2})\in L_{j}\}
. The vector
((\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m-2})_{\hat{L}_{j}}
has length
\binom{n}{m-2}\geq\binom{k}{p}\geq r
, where
k,p
are constants in Algorithm
4.1
. Therefore,
((\mu_{1}^{*})^{\otimes m-2})_{\hat{L}_{j}},\ldots,((\mu_{r}^{*})^{\otimes m-2})_{\hat{L}_{j}}
are generically linearly independent and hence (
5.7
) has a unique optimizer. Suppose the optimizer is
(\theta_{1j}^{*},\ldots,\theta_{rj}^{*})
. The covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{i}
can be approximated as

(5.8)

\Sigma^{*}_{i}\coloneqq\{\mbox{diag}((\theta_{i1}^{*},\ldots,\theta_{id}^{*}))\},\,\sigma_{ij}^{*}\coloneqq\sqrt{\theta_{ij}^{*}}.

The following is the complete algorithm to recover the unknown parameters
\{\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i},\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
.

Algorithm 5.1
.

(Learning diagonal Gaussian mixture models.)

Input:

The
m
th order sample moment tensor
\widehat{M}_{m}
, the
t
th order sample moment tensor
\widehat{M}_{t}
, and the number of components
r
.

Step 1.

Apply Algorithm
4.1
to subtensor
(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}
defined in (
5.1
). Let
(q_{1}^{*})^{\otimes m}+\cdots+(q_{r}^{*})^{\otimes m}
be the output incomplete tensor approximation for
\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}
.

Step 2.

For
i=1,\ldots,r
, we choose
\eta_{i}
such that
\eta_{i}^{m}=1
and it minimizes
\|\text{Im}(\eta_{i}q_{i}^{*})\|
. Let
\check{q}_{i}=\text{Re}(\eta_{i}q_{i}^{*})
as in (
5.2
).

Step 3.

Solve (
5.3
) to get the optimizer
(\beta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\beta_{r}^{*})
and compute
\hat{\omega}_{i}
,
\hat{\mu}_{i}
as in (
5.4
) for
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Step 4.

Use
(\hat{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\omega}_{r},\hat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{r})
as starting points to solve (
5.5
) to obtain the optimizer
(\omega_{1}^{*},\ldots,\omega_{r}^{*},\mu_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mu_{r}^{*})
.

Step 5.

Solve the optimization (
5.7
) to get optimizers
\theta_{ij}^{*}

and then compute
\Sigma^{*}_{i}
as in (
5.8
).

Output:

Mixture Gaussian parameters

(\omega^{*}_{i},\mu^{*}_{i},\Sigma^{*}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,r
.

When the sampled moment tensors are close to the accurate moment tensors, the parameters generated by Algorithm
5.1
are close to the true model parameters. The analysis is shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Given a
d
-dimensional diagonal Gaussian mixture model with parameters
\{(\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}):i\in[r]\}
and
r
no greater than the
r_{\max}
in (
3.19
). Let
\{(\omega^{*}_{i},\mu^{*}_{i},\Sigma^{*}_{i}):i\in[r]\}
be the output of Algorithm
5.1
. If the distance
\epsilon:=\max(\|\widehat{M}_{m}-M_{m}\|,\|\widehat{M}_{t}-M_{t}\|)
is small enough,
(\mu_{1}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}},\ldots,(\mu_{r}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}}
are linearly independent, and the tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\mu_{i}^{\otimes m}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, then

\|\mu^{*}_{i}-\mu_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),\|\omega_{i}^{*}-\omega_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),\|\Sigma^{*}_{i}-\Sigma_{i}\|=O(\epsilon),

where the constants inside
O(\cdot)
depend on parameters
\{(\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}):i\in[r]\}
and the choice of
\xi
in Algorithm
5.1
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|=\|(\widehat{M}_{m}-M_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|\leq\epsilon,

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}-{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|=\|(\widehat{M}_{t}-M_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|\leq\epsilon.

and
{\mathcal{F}}_{m},\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
satisfy conditions of Theorem
4.2
. They imply that
\|\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
for some
(\eta_{i}^{*})^{m}=1
by Theorem
4.2
. The constants inside
O(\epsilon)
depend on the parameters of the Gaussian model and vector
\xi
. Since vectors
q_{i}
are real, we have
\|\text{Im}(\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*})\|=O(\epsilon)
. When
\epsilon
is small enough, such
\eta_{i}^{*}
minimizes
\|\text{Im}(\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*})\|
and we have

\|\text{Re}(\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*})-q_{i}\|\leq\|\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon).

Let
\check{q}_{i}\coloneqq\text{Re}(\eta_{i}^{*}q_{i}^{*})
. When
\epsilon
is small, vectors
(\check{q}_{1}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}},\ldots,(\check{q}_{r}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}}
are linearly independent since
(\mu_{1}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}},\ldots,(\mu_{r}^{\otimes t})_{\Omega_{t}}
are linearly independent by our assumption. It implies that the problem (
5.3
) has a unique solution. The weights
\hat{\omega}_{i}
and mean vectors
\hat{\mu}_{i}
can be calculated by (
5.4
). Since
\|(\widehat{M}_{t}-M_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|\leq\epsilon
and
\|\check{q}_{i}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
, we have
\|\omega_{i}-\hat{\omega}_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
(see
[
10
, Theorem 3.4]
). The approximation error for the mean vectors is

\|\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{i}\|=\|\check{q}_{i}/(\hat{\omega}_{i})^{1/m}-{q}_{i}/(\omega_{i})^{1/m}\|=O(\epsilon).

The constants inside
O(\epsilon)
depend on parameters of the Gaussian mixture model and
\xi
.

We obtain optimizers
\omega_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
by solving the problem (
5.5
), so it holds

\left\|(\widehat{M}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\omega^{*}_{i}\left((\mu^{*}_{i})^{\otimes m}\right)_{\Omega_{m}}\right\|=O(\epsilon).

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}^{*}(\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes m}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}^{*}(\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes t}
, then

\displaystyle\|({\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|\leq\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m})_{\Omega_{m}}\|+\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m}^{*})_{\Omega_{m}}\|=O(\epsilon).

\displaystyle\|({\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{*}-{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|\leq\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}-{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|+\|(\widehat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}-{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{*})_{\Omega_{t}}\|=O(\epsilon).

By Theorem
4.2
, we have
\|(\omega_{i}^{*})^{1/m}\mu_{i}^{*}-q_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
. Since we are optimizing (
5.5
), it also holds that

\left\|(\widehat{M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\omega_{i}\left((\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes t}\right)_{\Omega_{t}}\right\|=\left\|(\widehat{M}_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\omega_{i}^{*})^{\frac{m-t}{m}}\left(((\omega_{i}^{*})^{1/m}\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes t}\right)_{\Omega_{t}}\right\|=O(\epsilon).

Combining the above with
\|(\widehat{M}_{t}-M_{t})_{\Omega_{t}}\|=O(\epsilon)
, we get
\|(\omega_{i}^{*})^{\frac{m-t}{m}}-\omega_{i}^{\frac{m-t}{m}}\|=O(\epsilon)
by
[
10
, Theorem 3.4]
and hence
\|\omega_{i}^{*}-\omega_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
. For mean vectors
\mu_{i}
we have

\|\mu_{i}^{*}-\mu_{i}\|=\|((\omega_{i}^{*})^{1/m}\mu_{i}^{*})/(\omega_{i}^{*})^{1/m}-q_{i}/(\omega_{i})^{1/m}\|=O(\epsilon).

The constants inside the above
O(\cdot)
only depend on parameters
\{(\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}):i\in[r]\}
and
\xi
.

We obtain the covariance matrices
\Sigma_{i}
by solving (
5.7
). It holds that

\displaystyle\|\omega^{*}_{i}(\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes(m-2)}-\omega_{i}\mu_{i}^{\otimes(m-2)}\|=O(\epsilon),

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{A}\|\leq\|\widehat{M}_{m}-M_{m}\|+\|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}^{*})^{m}-{\mathcal{F}}_{m}\|\leq O(\epsilon),

where
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{A}
are defined in (
5.6
). The tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{m}
satisfies the condition of Theorem
4.2
, so the tensors
\mu_{1}^{\otimes(m-2)},\ldots,\mu_{r}^{\otimes(m-2)}
are linearly independent. It implies that
\{\omega^{*}_{i}(\mu_{i}^{*})^{\otimes(m-2)}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are linearly independent when
\epsilon
is small. Therefore, (
5.7
) has a unique solution for each
j
. By
[
10
, Theorem 3.4]
, we have

\|(\sigma_{ij}^{*})^{2}-(\sigma_{ij})^{2}\|=O(\epsilon).

It implies that
\|\Sigma_{i}^{*}-\Sigma_{i}\|=O(\epsilon)
, where the constants inside
O(\cdot)
only depend on parameters
\{(\omega_{i},\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}):i\in[r]\}
and
\xi
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Given the dimension
d
and the highest order of moment
m
,
the largest number of components in the Gaussian mixture model that
Algorithm
5.1
can learn is the same as
the largest rank
r_{max}
as in Theorem
3.7
, i.e.,

r_{\text{max}}=\max(\binom{k^{*}}{p^{*}},\binom{d-3-k^{*}}{m-1-p^{*}}),

where
p^{*}=\lfloor\frac{m-1}{2}\rfloor
and
k^{*}
is largest integer
k
such that

\binom{k}{p^{*}}\leq\binom{d-k-2}{m-p^{*}-1}.

Given a
d
-dimensional Gaussian mixture model with
r
components,
we can use Theorem
3.7
to obtain the smallest order
m

required for the Algorithm
5.1
and then apply
Algorithm
5.1
to learn the Gaussian mixture model using the
m
th order moment.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to learn diagonal Gaussian mixture models using tensor decompositions. We first formulate the problem as an incomplete tensor decomposition problem and develop Algorithm
3.4
to recover the low-rank decomposition given partial entries in the tensor. For a given dimension
d
and order
m
, we find the largest rank our algorithm can compute in Theorem
3.7
. Then, we extend the tensor decomposition algorithm to find low-rank tensor approximations when errors exist in the given subtensor. It is summarized in Algorithm
4.1
. Theorem
4.2
proves that the low-rank approximation generated by Algorithm
4.1
is highly accurate if the input subtensor is close enough to the exact subtensor. Finally, we apply the incomplete tensor approximation algorithm to recover unknown parameters in Gaussian mixture models from samples. The unknown parameters are obtained by solving linear equations. It is presented in Algorithm
5.1
. We prove in Theorem
5.2
that the recovered parameters are close to the true parameters when the estimated high-order moments are accurate.

The proposed algorithms for learning diagonal Gaussian mixtures can handle a large number of Gaussian components by utilizing higher order moments. For general Gaussian mixture models with non-diagonal covariances, how to learn their unknown parameters using symmetric tensor decompositions and generating polynomials is an interesting question to investigate in the future.
