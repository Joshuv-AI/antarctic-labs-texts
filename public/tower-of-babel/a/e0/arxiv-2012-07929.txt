Exact Exchange with Range-separated Algorithm for Thermodynamic Limit of Periodic Hartree-Fock Theory
Qiming Sun

Abstract
The expensive cost of computing exact exchange in periodic systems limits the application range of density functional theory with hybrid functionals. To reduce the computational cost of exact change, we present a range-separated algorithm to compute electron repulsion integrals for Gaussian-type crystal basis. The algorithm splits the full-range Coulomb interactions into short-range and long-range parts, which are respectively computed in real and reciprocal space. This approach significantly reduces the overall computational cost as integrals can be efficiently computed in both regions. The algorithm can efficiently handle large numbers of 𝐤 \mathbf{k} points with limited CPU and memory resources. As a demonstration, we performed an all-electron 𝐤 \mathbf{k} -point Hartree-Fock calculation for LiH crystal with 1 million Gaussian basis functions, which was completed on a desktop computer in 1400 CPU hours.



I
Introduction

Density functional theory (DFT) is the most widely used tool for modeling systems
with periodic boundary conditions (PBC).
Large scale DFT simulations without Hartree-Fock exchange (HFX) were
available for PBC systems of millions of basis functions
Bowler and Miyazaki 2010
;
VandeVondele, Borštnik, and Hutter 2012
.
The use of hybrid functionals in DFT, which includes HFX contributions in the
DFT exchange-correlation functional, can significantly improve the accuracy of
DFT and widen the range of applications.
However, accurately and efficiently evaluating HFX is a major challenge for PBC DFT calculations.
Problems like numerical divergence and singularity in

\mathbf{k}
-point sampling may be found in HFX with PBC and various treatments
were developed to overcome these
problems
Pisani and Dovesi 1980
;
Dovesi et al. 1983
;
Gygi and Baldereschi 1986
;
Heyd and Scuseria 2004
;
Paier et al. 2005
;
Izmaylov, Scuseria, and Frisch 2006
;
Spencer and Alavi 2008
;
Guidon, Hutter, and VandeVondele 2009
;
Sundararaman and Arias 2013
;
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
;
Gygi 2009
;
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Gygi and Duchemin 2013
;
DiStasio et al. 2014
.
The concern of using hybrid functional DFT in PBC application is mainly the
computational cost of HFX which can be several orders of magnitude higher then
the rest part.

In crystal simulations, planewave (PW) basis is a natural choice due to its periodicity.
Computing HFX with PW basis
Görling 1996
;
Chawla and Voth 1998
;
Wu, Selloni, and Car 2009
;
Lin 2016
;
Hu et al. 2017
;
Barnes et al. 2017

is extremely demanding and generally considered impractical.
Due to the locality of Gaussian functions, typically less Gaussian functions are
required to describe the atomic characters in a system. It is
more affordable to computing HFX in Gaussian basis than in PW basis.
Gaussian-type crystal basis are widely adopted in many program
packages
Kühne et al. 2020
;
Dovesi et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Blum et al. 2009
;
Balasubramani et al. 2020
;
VandeVondele et al. 2005
.
For PBC calculations, Gaussian and planewave method
LIPPERT, PARRINELLO, and MICHELE 1997
;
Dominik Marx 2009
(GPW) is a
commonly applied method to compute electron repulsion integrals for Gaussian basis.
In GPW method, PW basis serves as the auxiliary basis of density fitting method.
Discrete Fourier transform is often employed to evaluate the density and
Coulomb potential on PW basis, which has
\mathcal{O}(N_{G}\log(N_{G}))
complexity
for
N_{G}
PW basis (or the number of integration grids).
For a calculation of
N_{A}
atoms and
N_{k}

\mathbf{k}
points, the
cost of HFX with the GPW method scales as at least
N_{A}^{2}N_{k}^{2}N_{G}\log(N_{G})
.

The GPW method is not suitable for all-electron calculations as it requires a
high energy cutoff for accurate description of core orbitals, which leads to a
large number of auxiliary PW basis. To reduce the energy cutoff,
pseudopotentials
Hartwigsen, Goedecker, and Hutter 1998
;
Willand et al. 2013
;
Gao and Chelikowsky 2018
;
Prandini et al. 2018
;
Lu et al. 2019
(PP)
become critical in PW basis methods as well as the GPW method.
Although PPs are commonly used in crystal calculations, there are several
issues for applications with PP.
Accuracy and reliability of DFT calculations are badly affected by the quality of PP.
Results obtained from different PPs are often not comparable
Lejaeghere et al. 2016
.
Applications with PP are limited to valence properties only. It is impossible to study
properties of core electrons with PP.
As such, all-electron methods receive significant
attentions
Krack and Parrinello 2000
;
Kucukbenli et al. 2014
;
Levchenko et al. 2015
;
Biktagirov, Schmidt, and Gerstmann 2018
;
Yeh et al. 2022
;
Gygi 2023

since they can naturally address all of these issues.

Gaussian and augmented plane wave (GAPW) method
Lippert, Hutter, and Parrinello 1999
;
Kresse and Joubert 1999
;
Krack and Parrinello 2000
was
developed to address the energy cutoff issue in GPW.
This method uses Gaussian functions to describe electron density in the core
region and calls the GPW method for the valence region only.
However, GAPW cannot be used to compute HFX.
Gaussian density fitting (GDF) were considered
Towler, Zupan, and Causà 1996
;
Schwegler and Challacombe 1999
;
Heyd, Scuseria, and Ernzerhof 2003
;
Tymczak et al. 2005
;
Izmaylov, Scuseria, and Frisch 2006
;
Maschio et al. 2007
;
Varga 2008
;
Maschio and Usvyat 2008
;
Guidon, Hutter, and VandeVondele 2009
;
Ben, Hutter, and VandeVondele 2013
;
Yamada, Brewster, and Hirata 2013
;
Sun et al. 2017
;
Irmler, Burow, and Pauly 2018
;
Wang, Lewis, and Valeev 2020
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Sharma, White, and Beylkin 2022

due to its great success for HFX and integral evaluation in molecular systems.
It should be noted that if integrals in GDF are computed with the GPW algorithm,
GDF method may still suffer from the energy cutoff issue in all-electron calculations.
GDF algorithms specialized on all-electron methods were
developed to solve the energy cutoff issue
Sun et al. 2017
;
Ye and Berkelbach 2021a
;
Ye and Berkelbach 2021b
;
Bintrim, Berkelbach, and Ye 2022
.

Despite that GPW and GDF methods provide the possibility to compute HFX with PBC
Gaussian basis, their computational requirements of HFX still make them
impractical when targeting the thermodynamic limit in periodic systems.
The GDF method requires a storage of
\mathcal{O}(N_{AO}^{3}N_{k}^{2})
for the
intermediate tensors and
\mathcal{O}(N_{AO}^{4}N_{k}^{2})
FLOPs for tensor contraction in HFX matrix.
As a result, the feasible problem size of k-point hybrid DFT calculations with
GDF methods is limited to around 100k basis functions.

In this work, we present a range-separated algorithm for the computation
electron repulsion integrals with Gaussian crystal basis.
This algorithm enables us to compute HFX for large periodic systems.
Formulas, possible variants, and integral screening schemes involved in this algorithm
are discussed in Section
II
.
In Section
III
, we demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of this
algorithm by performing an all-electron HF calculation for LiH crystal with a million basis functions.

II
Theory

II.1
Fock matrix with range-separated algorithm

We consider here a periodic system which consists of repeated unit cells
in terms of the translation vector
\mathbf{m}
and lattice vectors
\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{m}=m_{x}\mathbf{a}_{x}+m_{y}\mathbf{a}_{y}+m_{z}\mathbf{a}_{z}.

(1)

Crystal orbitals for a crystal of
N\rightarrow\infty
unit cells can be
expanded with a set of Bloch orbitals

\phi_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})^{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\mu^{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{r})

(2)

where
\mu^{\mathbf{m}}
is an real Gaussian-type orbital centered at

\mathbf{R}_{\mu}
in cell
\mathbf{m}

\mu^{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{r})=\mu(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{\mu}-\mathbf{m}).

(3)

Solving the periodic HF model can lead to the

\mathbf{k}
-point adapted Roothaan equations

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbf{S}^{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{C}^{\mathbf{k}}\epsilon^{\mathbf{k}},

(4)

The Fock matrix and overlap matrix at
\mathbf{k}
are

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbf{T}^{\mathbf{k}}+\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{Nuc}}^{\mathbf{k}}+\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{k}}-\mathbf{K}^{\mathbf{k}},

(5)

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}=\langle\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}}|\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}\rangle.

(6)

The overlap matrix can be computed in terms of the lattice sum over overlap
integrals in real space

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}S_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{m}}},

(7)

\displaystyle S_{\mu^{0}\nu^{m}}=\langle\mu^{\mathbf{0}}|\nu^{\mathbf{m}}\rangle.

(8)

In a similarly way, we can get the kinetic matrix in
\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}T_{\mu^{0}\nu^{m}}.

(9)

When computing the nuclear attraction matrix
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{Nuc}}^{\mathbf{k}}
,
we use model charge to screen the long-range interactions then compute
separately the long-range and short-range parts of nuclear attraction integrals.
Relevant formulas have been documented in
Sun et al. 2017
.

Given
\mathbf{k}
denoted density matrices
D^{\mathbf{k}}
,
the HF Coulomb and exchange matrices in
\mathbf{F}^{\mathbf{k}}
are

\displaystyle J_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\kappa\lambda}D_{\lambda\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}|\phi_{\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\phi_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}),

(10)

\displaystyle K_{\mu\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\kappa\nu}D_{\nu\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|\phi_{\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\phi_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}}).

(11)

The four-center two-electron repulsion integrals (ERI) in above equations are

\displaystyle(\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}_{\mu}}\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}_{\nu}}|\phi_{\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}_{\kappa}}\phi_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}_{\lambda}})=\iint\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}_{\mu}}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}_{\nu}}(\mathbf{r}_{1})g(\mathbf{r}_{12})\phi_{\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}_{\kappa}}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\phi_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}_{\lambda}}(\mathbf{r}_{2})d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{1}d^{3}\mathbf{r}_{2}.

(12)

This integral can be evaluated in reciprocal space in two steps
LIPPERT, PARRINELLO, and MICHELE 1997
;
Dominik Marx 2009
:

1.

Fourier transform (FT) the orbital product
\rho_{\mu\nu}
against the
PW basis
|\mathbf{G}\rangle=\frac{(2\pi)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{N}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-i\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}~*}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})d^{3}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}e^{i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{n}}\rho_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}),

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\rho_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}\nu^{0}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{k}^{\prime},

(15)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\mu(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{m})\nu(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{n})d^{3}\mathbf{r};

(16)

2.

A summation over PW basis inside the volume
\Omega
of the unit cell

\displaystyle(\phi_{\mu}^{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\nu}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|\phi_{\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\phi_{\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}})=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\rho_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{G})\rho_{\kappa\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{G}).

(17)

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G})
in the equation above is the Coulomb kernel in reciprocal space.
The Coulomb metric
g(\mathbf{r}_{12})
can be split into long-range (LR) plus
short-range (SR) contributions

g(\mathbf{r}_{12})=\frac{1}{r_{12}}=\frac{\mathrm{erf}(\omega r_{12})}{r_{12}}+\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\omega r_{12})}{r_{12}}.

(18)

When computing ERIs with Eq. (
17
), the Fourier transformed
full-range Coulomb kernel as well as the LR and SR Coulomb kernels are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{12}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G})=\begin{cases}\frac{4\pi}{G^{2}}&|\mathbf{G}|\neq 0\\
0&|\mathbf{G}|=0\end{cases},

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{erf}(\omega r_{12})}{r_{12}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\text{LR}}(\mathbf{G})=\begin{cases}\frac{4\pi}{G^{2}}\exp(-\frac{G^{2}}{4\omega^{2}})&|\mathbf{G}|\neq 0\\
0&|\mathbf{G}|=0\end{cases},

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\omega r_{12})}{r_{12}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\text{SR}}(\mathbf{G})=\begin{cases}\frac{4\pi}{G^{2}}(1-\exp(-\frac{G^{2}}{4\omega^{2}}))&|\mathbf{G}|\neq 0\\
\frac{\pi}{\omega^{2}}&|\mathbf{G}|=0\end{cases}.

(21)

The treatment of
\mathbf{G}=0
in the SR Coulomb kernel is different to the other two.
When adding the LR and SR integrals to produce the integrals for full-range Coulomb,
an additional contribution corresponding to the FT term at
\mathbf{G}=0

needs to be subtracted from the SR integrals

(\phi^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu}\phi^{\mathbf{k}}_{\nu}|\phi^{\mathbf{k}}_{\kappa}\phi^{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda})^{\text{SR}}\Big|_{\mathbf{G}=0}=\frac{\pi}{\Omega\omega^{2}}S_{\mu\nu}^{\mathbf{k}}S_{\kappa\lambda}^{\mathbf{k}}.

(22)

Due to the exponential decay term
\exp(-\frac{G^{2}}{4\omega})
in the LR Coulomb kernel,
we can expect that the contributions from large
\mathbf{G}
in Eq. (
17
) decrease rapidly.
The summation over PWs in Eq. (
17
) can be truncated early thus a
small energy cutoff is required to compute LR ERI in reciprocal space.
Sparsity of SR ERIs in real space can be utilized to gain good efficiency
Izmaylov, Scuseria, and Frisch 2006
.
By choosing an appropriate value for
\omega
and appropriate algorithms that we
will discuss below, computing SR ERIs in real space and LR ERIs in
reciprocal space appears to be an efficient approach.

In this approach, we use Ewald probe-charge correction
Paier et al. 2005
to
regularize the Coulomb singularity in HFX.
It only requires to modify the FT integrals at
\mathbf{G}=0
.
Other attractive techniques such as the Wigner-Seitz truncated Coulomb interactions
Sundararaman and Arias 2013

are incompatible to the current range-separated algorithm.
They require more PWs to describe the Coulomb kernel while this algorithm only
use a small number of PWs for LR ERIs.

II.2
HFX in reciprocal space

Computing HFX in reciprocal space involves two time-consuming steps:
analytical FT for orbital product
\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})

with
\mathcal{O}(N_{k}^{2}N_{c}N_{AO}^{2}N_{G})
complexity as shown by Eq. (
13
),
and the tensor contraction for three tensors which scales as
N_{k}^{2}N_{AO}^{3}N_{G}

K^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\lambda}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{\nu\kappa\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}D^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu\kappa}\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\kappa\lambda}(-\mathbf{G}).

(23)

The number of image cells in lattice-sum,
N_{c}
, is related to distance cutoff.
The number of PW basis
N_{G}
needs to be determined by energy
cutoff. Distance cutoff and energy cutoff are influenced by the Coulomb
attenuation parameter
\omega
and the compactness of the
Gaussian basis functions
Sun 2023
.
It should be noted that the FT for orbital products has to be evaluated
analytically. Using discrete FT here can cause significant errors
Sun et al. 2017
.
This is because we only use a small number of PW basis for the
ERIs evaluated in reciprocal space. The FT associated real space grids are not
dense enough to describe all orbital products.

To optimize the performance of FT for orbital product, utilizing the symmetry
below can reduce the cost by a factor of two

[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})]^{*}=\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu\mu}(-\mathbf{G}).

(24)

Thereby, the orbital product tensor can be computed once and used four times for
exchange matrices at two different
\mathbf{k}
points

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\lambda}=\sum_{\nu\kappa\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}D^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu\kappa}\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\lambda\kappa}(\mathbf{G})]^{*},

(25)

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu\kappa}=\sum_{\mu\lambda\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}}D^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\lambda}\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu\mu}(-\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}((-\mathbf{G})_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}})[\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\kappa\lambda}(-\mathbf{G})]^{*}.

(26)

During the self-consistent field (SCF) iteration, density matrices are
constructed from occupied SCF orbitals (denoted as

C^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}
below) and orbital occupancies (denoted as

\Lambda^{\mathbf{k}}_{j}
).
A common optimization practice is to use the
occupied orbitals directly in the construction of exchange matrix.
We can first carry out the contraction between the
N_{occ}
occupied orbitals and the orbital product tensor

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})C^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu j},

(27)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{j\nu}(-\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}C^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}~*}_{\mu j}\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\nu}(-\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\mu}C^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}~*}_{\mu j}[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu\mu}(\mathbf{G})]^{*}=[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu j}(\mathbf{G})]^{*},

(28)

then obtain the exchange matrix with a contraction between the resultant tensors
and another orbital product tensor

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{j\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Lambda^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{j}\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{j\nu}(-\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{j\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Lambda^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{j}\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\nu j}(\mathbf{G})]^{*}.

(29)

This formula results in a reduction of the computational cost to

N_{k}^{2}N_{AO}^{2}N_{occ}N_{G}
for the step of tensor contraction.
The permutation symmetry Eq. (
24
) for orbital product tensor can
still be utilized, leading to the contraction for the exchange matrix at

\mathbf{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}(-\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\mu}[\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{G})]^{*}C^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j},

(30)

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{j\mathbf{G}\mathbf{k}}\Lambda^{\mathbf{k}}_{j}\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}(-\mathbf{G})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})[\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}(-\mathbf{G})]^{*}.

(31)

By changing the contraction order in Eq. (
27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\nu\mathbf{n}}\rho_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})C_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}j^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}},

(32)

\displaystyle C_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{n}}C^{\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}.

(33)

It is noticeable that the sparsity in the FT orbital product tensor can be
exploited to reduce the computational cost.
The FT integral
\rho_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})
in
Eq. (
16
) may be negligible for well-separated

\mu(\mathbf{r-\mathbf{m}})
and

\nu(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{n})
.
Although the tensor contraction still has the scaling
N_{k}^{2}N_{c}N_{AO}^{2}N_{G}
, the actual
cost is much lower due to the sparsity of

\rho_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})
.

For SCF orbitals which preserve the time-reversal symmetry between
\mathbf{k}

and
\bar{\mathbf{k}}
(
=-\mathbf{k}
), it is possible to achieve an additional
reduction in computational cost.
The time-reversal symmetry ensures that the SCF wavefunction defined by the

\mathbf{k}
-point orbitals can be transformed to
an identical representation at certain
\Gamma
-point with real orbital
coefficients
\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle B_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}+C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}}),

(34)

\displaystyle B_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}-C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}}).

(35)

This property reduces the cost of comupting

\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})
by roughly a factor of two as we only
need to compute the contraction between a complex tensor and a real matrix

\displaystyle L^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\nu\mathbf{n}}\rho_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{n}}}(\mathbf{G})B_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}j^{\mathbf{k}}},

(36)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}L^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}L^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}).

(37)

In addition, the intermediates
L^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}
can be reused
for the tensor contraction in Eq. (
30
),
providing another factor of two reduction in computational cost.
We first employ the FT Eq. (
14
)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}(-\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\mu\mathbf{m}}[\rho_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}\nu^{0}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{m}}]^{*}C^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}=\sum_{\mu\mathbf{m}}[\rho_{\nu^{0}\mu^{\mathbf{m}}}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}]^{*}.

(38)

Realizing that the orbital coefficients
\mathbf{B}
are real,

\displaystyle C_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(B_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}}+iB_{\mu^{\mathbf{m}}j^{\mathbf{k}}})

(39)

we then simplify Eq. (
30
) to

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}(-\mathbf{G})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[L^{\mathbf{k}}_{\nu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})]^{*}-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}[L^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}_{\nu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})]^{*}.

(40)

By using all the features discussed above, we would obtain an overall
reduction which rougly equals a factor of 8 plus speed-ups from tensor sparsity.
Finally, we derive the exchange matrices

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}+i\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\bar{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}-i\tilde{K}^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}+\tilde{K}^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}\bar{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}),

(41)

\displaystyle K^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu\mu}+i\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}\bar{\mathbf{k}}}_{\nu\mu}-i\tilde{K}^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{k}}_{\nu\mu}+\tilde{K}^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}}_{\nu\mu}),

(42)

where
\tilde{K}
is the intermediate based on
L^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mu j}

\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}_{1}\mathbf{k}_{2}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{j\mathbf{G}}\Lambda^{\mathbf{k}_{1}}_{i}L^{\mathbf{k}_{1}}_{\mu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})[L^{\mathbf{k}_{2}}_{\nu j}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})]^{*}.

(43)

For sufficient
\mathbf{k}
points, this intermediate can approximately lower
25% of the cost due to the relation

\tilde{K}^{\bar{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{k}}_{\mu\nu}=[\tilde{K}^{\mathbf{k}\bar{\mathbf{k}}}_{\nu\mu}]^{*}.

(44)

II.3
SR integrals evaluation

Following to the idea proposed in
Irmler, Burow, and Pauly 2018
,
we first evaluate SR Coulomb integrals as well as the Coulomb and exchange
matrix in the Born-von Karman (BvK) supercell then transform them to the

\mathbf{k}
-point adapted quantities. For example,
the matrix element of SR HFX
K_{\mu\nu}^{\text{SR},\mathbf{k}}
can be
computed

\displaystyle K_{\mu^{0}\lambda^{\mathbf{l}}}^{\mathrm{SR}}=\sum_{\nu\kappa}\sum_{\mathbf{L}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{M}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{N}}D_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa^{\mathbf{m}}}(\mu^{\mathbf{0}}\nu^{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{N}}|\kappa^{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{M}}\lambda^{\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{M}})^{\mathrm{SR}},

(45)

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{\text{SR},\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{l}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{l}}K^{\text{SR}}_{\mu^{0}\nu^{\mathbf{l}}}.

(46)

Here the lattice summation involves two types of translation vectors, the super-lattice translation
vectors (
\mathbf{L},\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N}
) for BvK supercell and the
translation inside a single BvK supercell (
\mathbf{l},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}
).

D_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa^{\mathbf{m}}}
in Eq. (
45
)
represents the density matrix elements in the BvK supercell.
Considering the BvK supercell being filled with
N_{\mathrm{BvK}}
primitive cells,

D_{\nu^{\mathbf{l}}\kappa^{\mathbf{m}}}
is obtained via an inverse FT of the

\mathbf{k}
-point adapted density matrices

D_{\nu^{\mathbf{n}}\kappa^{\mathbf{m}}}=D_{\nu^{\mathbf{0}}\kappa^{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{n}}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{BvK}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{n})}D_{\nu\kappa}^{\mathbf{k}}.

(47)

The cost of computing SR integrals in real space mainly depends on the range of
lattice-sum and integral screening of the SR integrals. The number of

\mathbf{k}
points has relatively low impact on the computational cost.
The value of Coulomb attenuation parameter
\omega

and the compactness of Gaussian basis functions are the two main factors
to determine the distance cutoff (as well as the range of
lattice-sum)
Sun 2023
.
Small value of
\omega
and diffused basis functions can lead to more image
cells in lattice-sum.
Although the size of BvK super-cell is proportional to the size of
\mathbf{k}

points, the overall number of image cells in the lattice-sum, denoted by the
compound index
\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{M}
, remains largely unchanged.

To further reduce the cost of SR integrals, we also take the locality of basis
functions into account.
The Gaussian basis functions can be partitioned into two groups, labelled as
D
for
the diffused functions and
C
for the compact functions.
The products of two functions
\rho_{\mu\nu}
have four possible
combinations: the product of two diffused functions can lead to a diffuse
density
\rho_{D}
while the rest three
DC
,
CD
and
CC
all lead to compact
densities
\rho_{C}
. Here we developed three recipes to evaluate these SR
integrals. Each recipe is adequate in specific scenarios, depending on the size of

\mathbf{k}
-point mesh.

•

Recipe I: Analytically evaluating the 9 types of SR integrals which only
consist of compact densities
(DC|DC)
,
(DC|CD)
,

(DC|CC)
,
(CD|DC)
,
(CD|CD)
,
(CD|CC)
,
(CC|DC)
,
(CC|CD)
,
(CC|CC)

in real space while leaving the remaining 7 types of SR integrals
to the reciprocal space prescription we presented in Section
II.2
.
Since the 9 types of analytical integrals involve only compact density
distributions, lattice-sum can be rapidly truncated.

For the integrals involving diffuse densities, for instance the

(CC|DD)
-type integrals, the computation of SR integrals and their LR
counterparts can be merged into one pass for
(CC|DD)
with the full-range
Coulomb kernel since both the SR and LR integrals are evaluated in reciprocal
space. It is important to choose an
appropriate energy cutoff
E_{cut}
to ensure the accuracy of the

(CC|DD)
-type full-range ERIs. Given the precision requirement
\epsilon
, the energy
cutoff for four-center ERIs can be estimated
Sun 2023

\displaystyle 16\pi^{2}\theta E_{cut}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{E_{cut}}{2\alpha_{C}})^{2l_{C}}(\frac{E_{cut}}{2\alpha_{D}})^{2l_{D}}e^{\frac{E_{cut}}{2\theta}}<\epsilon,

(48)

\displaystyle\theta=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{C}^{-1}+\alpha_{D}^{-1})^{-1},

(49)

where
\alpha_{C}
and
l_{C}
are the exponent and the angular moment of the most
compact basis function. We can solve the inequality (
48
) for
each diffused function and find an overall energy cutoff for the reciprocal
space prescription.

When computing exchange matrix with this recipe, it is inconvenient to
merely compute the 7 types of diffuse-density associated SR integrals with the
reciprocal space prescription.
We instead use the reciprocal space prescription to compute the HFX of all
integrals as well as the SR HFX of the 9 types of compact SR integrals.
Then, we take the difference between the two results
to obtain the contribution of the 7-type SR integrals.

This recipe lowers the cost of analytical integral evaluation while it
introduces an extra HFX computation in reciprocal space.
The cost for the later one is proportional to
N_{k}^{2}
and it can dominate
the HFX computation if the SCF calculation involves many

\mathbf{k}
points. Therefore, this recipe is only adequate for SCF
calculations with a small number of
\mathbf{k}
points.

•

Recipe II: Computing the
(DD|DD)
-type SR integral with
the reciprocal space prescription while handling the rest 15 types in real
space.
(DD|DD)
-type SR integrals in real space often involves a large
number of image cells in the triple lattice-sum as it requires the largest
distance cutoff. Excluding it from the real space integral evaluation can
save computational cost.
Meanwhile, evaluating
(DD|DD)
-type SR integrals in reciprocal space only adds a
small overhead, as the diffused functions typically constitute a small
portion of the regular basis set.
This algorithm is helpful in reducing the computation time of Fock build for
a moderate number of
\mathbf{k}
points.

•

Recipe III: Evaluating all SR integrals in real space.
When a large number of
\mathbf{k}
points is present in
the SCF calculation, the effort of computing exchange matrix in reciprocal
space become dominant.
To reduce the cost of reciprocal space part, we
not only evaluate all SR integrals in real space, but also tend to use
small energy cutoff to decrease the required number of PWs which is proportional
to the computational cost of reciprocal space algorithm.

The value of Coulomb attenuation parameter
\omega
, energy cutoff, and the
number of PWs are correlated.
Although less PWs means less computational efforts in reciprocal space
part, a lower energy cutoff can lead to more computation
time in real space as it requires a small
\omega
to ensure the integral
accuracy.
To gain the optimal performance, different
\omega
should be used in different

\mathbf{k}
-point calculations.

II.4
Integral screening

Integral screening is a crucial factor for the performance of real space
integral program. To enhance the performance of SR integral computation, we have
developed a three-layer hierarchical screening prescription:

•

First, we filter integrals with the regular Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle(\mu\nu|\kappa\lambda)^{\text{SR}}<\sqrt{(\mu\nu|\mu\nu)^{\text{SR}}(\kappa\lambda|\kappa\lambda)^{\text{SR}}}.

(50)

•

Next, we apply the mutated Schwarz inequality to estimate SR integral value

\displaystyle(\mu\nu|\kappa\lambda)^{\text{SR}}<\min(\sqrt{(\mu\mu|\kappa\kappa)^{\text{SR}}(\nu\nu|\lambda\lambda)^{\text{SR}}},\sqrt{(\mu\mu|\lambda\lambda)^{\text{SR}}(\nu\nu|\kappa\kappa)^{\text{SR}}}).

(51)

This condition is not useful for ERIs of full Coulomb metric due to the
long range character of Coulomb interactions.
However, for SR integrals, this condition can filter out approximately 50% of
the terms.

•

Finally, the SR integral estimator developed in
Sun 2023
is
utilized as the last integral filter

(\mu\nu|\kappa\lambda)^{\text{SR}}\lesssim\frac{Q_{\mu\nu}Q_{\kappa\lambda}e^{-\theta_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}R^{2}}}{R^{2}}

(52)

where
Q_{\mu\nu}
is a factor that behaves like the overlap between two primitive
Gaussian functions. Its detailed definition can be found in
Sun 2023
.
Other quantities can be computed

\displaystyle\theta_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}=((\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu})^{-1}+(\alpha_{\kappa}+\alpha_{\lambda})^{-1}+\omega^{-2})^{-1},

(53)

\displaystyle R=|\mathbf{P}_{\mu\nu}-\mathbf{P}_{\kappa\lambda}|,

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\alpha_{\mu}\mathbf{R}_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu}\mathbf{R}_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu}}.

(55)

The reason that the screening filters are applied in this specific order is that each
screening filter has a different computational overhead.
We have observed that the time spent on the screening testing is not
negligible due to the large number of integral quartets that needs to be tested.
The regular Schwarz inequality is applied first, as it is not only
effective in filtering out most small integrals but also the fastest.
The regular Schwarz inequality test requires only
one multiplication and two memory accesses. The mutated Schwarz inequality is
slightly less efficient as it requires two multiplications and four memory accesses.
The integral estimator works slowest.
Even though some intermediates are precomputed and cached,
it requires six memory accesses to obtain coordinates for
R
, two
memory accesses for paired quantities
Q
, one call to the exponential function, and ten
or more multiplication/addition/division operations to aggregate them.

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we presented an efficient range-separated algorithm to compute
electron repulsion integrals and Fock matrix for crystalline Gaussian basis.
In this algorithm, the full-range Coulomb interactions were split into the
short-range and long-range parts, which are respectively computed in real and
reciprocal space.
Additionally, we proposed several methods to improve the computational efficiency.
For integrals evaluated in reciprocal space,
we reformulated the Fourier transform and the tensor contraction equations
to utilize sparsity in Gaussian orbital products.
For short-range integrals,
we partitioned basis functions according to their locality in real space and
developed three recipes to utilize the localities of basis functions.
Different recipes are adequate for different scenarios depending on the
number of
\mathbf{k}
points.
We developed a three-level integral screening scheme to filter SR electron
repulsion integrals which has significant impacts on the computational
efficiency for short-range integrals.
This algorithm enables us to perform all-electron HF and hybrid functional
DFT calculations with a large number of
\mathbf{k}
points.
To assess the performance of this algorithm, we performed
\mathbf{k}
-point HF
calculations for LiH crystal with up to 1m basis functions.
The current algorithm only supports the Ewald probe-charge correction in the
treatment of Coulomb singularity.
Combining this algorithm with other Coulomb singularity treatments will be
considered in the future.
