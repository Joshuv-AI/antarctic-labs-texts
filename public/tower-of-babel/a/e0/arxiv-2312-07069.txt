Context Matters: Data-Efficient Augmentation of Large Language Models for Scientific Applications
Xiang Li, Haoran Tang, Siyu Chen, [3pt] Ziwei Wang, Anurag Maravi, Marcin Abram, [4pt] Department of Physics, Astronomy, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA, [2pt] Department of Computer Science, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA, [2pt] Information Sciences Institute, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA[4pt] {limike,haoranta,schen809,zwang476,maravi,mjabram}@usc.edu

Abstract
In this paper, we explore the challenges inherent to Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4, particularly their propensity for hallucinations, logic mistakes, and incorrect conclusions when tasked with answering complex questions. The capacity of LLMs to present erroneous answers in a coherent and semantically rigorous manner further complicates the detection of factual inaccuracies. This issue is especially pronounced in fields that require specialized expertise. Our work delves into these challenges, aiming to enhance the understanding and mitigation of such errors, thereby contributing to the improvement of LLM accuracy and reliability in scientific and other specialized domains. Our findings reveal a non-linear relationship between the context’s relevancy and the answers’ measured quality. In addition, we demonstrate that with the correct calibration, it is possible to automate the grading procedure – a finding suggesting that, at least to some degree, the LLMs can be used to self-examine the quality of their own performance. Finally, we describe an experimental platform that can be seen as a proof-of-concept of the techniques described in this work.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 suffer from hallucinations, logic mistakes, and incorrect conclusions when answering challenging questions

)
.
In addition, similar to a style adoption

(

Scheurer et al. 2023

We have many available benchmarks measuring the performance of LLMs, including AGIEval
(
Zhong et al. 2023

, ChemLLMBench
(

(

Among more flexible methods, we can mention HaluEval developed by
Li et al. 2023b
. However, this method does not scale well since it requires laborious and expensive human annotation.
Automated methods of evaluations include BiLingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU)
(
Papineni et al. 2001
)
, BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, and LLM-Eval
(
Lin and Chen 2023
)
. While they can be easily run at scale, they are usually restricted to a group of specific sub-tasks (e.g., machine translation, text summarization, or dialogue generation).
Due to those constraints, the question of LLMs evaluation is still an active research area.

In many areas, the tendency of LLMs to hallucinate and its typical overconfidence when presenting erroneous answers can cause a lot of harm (e.g., in medical
(
Thapa and Adhikari 2023
)
, legal
(
Weidinger et al. 2022
)

applications). In this work, we focus on yet another area characterized by a low tolerance for erroneous answers:
scientific applications
, with the aim to develop a technique that increases the coherence and reliability of answers produced by popular LLMs, such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.

We tackle this challenge by employing a version of prompt engineering known as

To illustrate our approach, we developed an online platform that can serve as a proof-of-concept, testing the techniques described in this paper. As a choice of our scientific domain, we selected graduate-level quantum physics. In Section

Characteristic Challenges and Research Opportunities

Arguably, the fastest adoption of LLMs in the past year could be noticed in the areas of translation
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, code generation
(
Koziolek and Koziolek 2023
;
Siddiq and Santos 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
, database query optimization
(
Li et al. 2023a
)
, and text summarization
(
Sahu et al. 2023
)
as well as in the development of general personal assistants
(
Li et al. 2023c
;
Chan et al. 2023
)
.

However, when targeting
scientific applications
, we must be aware of some distinctive characteristics of that domain that set it apart from typical business operations. For example, when answering scientific questions, we might face some inherent limit on the size (or variability) of the known datasets. While in many business applications, we can get more trainable examples by committing more money or time (e.g., by taking more photos and labeling them or by scanning more books to get more natural text), in some scientific domains, the number of existing examples can be strictly limited. For example, we might only know so many examples of superconducting materials, and we can not easily increase that number (not without first discovering new types of superconductors).
Another important difference is that science does not follow the typical majority voting rule. If we ask people to classify pictures of cats and dogs, even if some human labelers might disagree when assessing a particular photo, we can still use majority voting to determine whether a given picture depicts a dog or a cat. The same can be done to quantify the value of continuous variables
(
Surowiecki 2004
)
. In contrast, in science, a single opinion, if sufficiently motivated, can weigh more than a popular opinion of the majority (as it happened, e.g., with the discovery of quasicrystals by
(
Shechtman et al. 1984
)
, a discovery that challenged the existing status quo, and resulted in a major shift in our understanding of solid-state physics).
On the other hand, in the scientific domain, we can rely on some known (even if sometimes only in an approximate form

Considering all those differences, we must leverage the few-shot learning ability of LLMs

The algorithm that we propose must be data-efficient. We can not afford to retrain the entire LLM using millions of examples, as simply they might not exist. We must leverage the few-shot learning ability of LLMs. Instead, we focus on providing LLMs with the proper context, as described in the following sections.

Related Work

Prompt engineering.

Previous work has demonstrated that prompt engineering and chain of thought techniques can greatly improve LLMs’ performance in various domains
(
Kojima et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Besta et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2023
;
Imani et al. 2023
)
. However, very few works focus on the evaluation of these techniques when applied to the scientific domain.

In-Context Learning.

The few-shot learning capabilities of LLMs enable enhanced performance across various tasks through tailored prompting
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
.
Drori et al. 2022
employed OpenAI’s Codex to tackle University Math Problems, achieving human-level performance and elevating benchmark accuracy from
18.8\%
to
81.1\%
.

Imani et al. 2023
developed a “math-prompter” method for LLMs, significantly improving performance on the MultiArith dataset from
78.7\%
to
92.5\%
.
Zhang et al. 2023
developed a concise two-stage framework that first generates rationales and then uses them to enhance answer inference, effectively integrating multimodal information. However, today’s state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) still struggle to solve advanced, graduate-level physics problems, as demonstrated in this paper. Key issues include its poor handling of physical units, coding errors, and its tendency to create convincing yet incorrect answers.

Evaluation of LLMs’ physics reasoning capacities.

Creating an effective evaluation framework for LLMs in scientific domains, particularly for physical reasoning, is challenging due to the implicit nature of human knowledge about the physical world and its representation
(
Bisk et al. 2020
)
. Benchmarks like those created by
Clark et al. 2018
have focused on assessing LLMs using physics questions.
Aroca-Ouellette et al. 2021
specifically test models on physical commonsense and reasoning concepts.
Liang et al. 2023
;
Ichter et al. 2022
focus applications for physically grounded contexts. However, most of those benchmarks target basic school- or undergraduate-college-level questions.
In contrast, in this work, we focus on the topic of upper-level undergraduate- and graduate-level quantum physics to provide LLM with more nuanced questions that often do not have a single, straight answer.

4
Methodology

4.1
Approach

To assess the importance of context, we compare the answers of GPT-4 in two settings: (1) without any modifications, a version that can be seen as zero-shot learning, and (2) using prompt engineering to provide the context. We vary the quality of the context, from “irrelevant”, through “vague” to “insightful”. We score the quality of the response using developed grading rubrics. In addition, we test a few variants of automatic grading, measuring its consistency with the manual expert grading. Finally, we test the impact of summarization techniques employed to limit the length of the context.

4.2
Data collection

We created a question bank containing selected problems from
(
Griffiths and Schroeter 2018
)
, a popular upper-undergraduate or intro-graduate-level textbook on quantum mechanics. Next, for each question, we created three hints with different levels of relevance.

The selected questions cover the following areas: quantum mechanics in three dimensions, identical particles, symmetries and conservation laws, time-independent perturbation theory, and the variational principle (cf. the sample question in the Supplementary Material). The solutions to the questions usually involve a combination of complex calculations and application of conceptual knowledge.

4.3
Hints structure

Hints for each question are divided into three classes:
irrelevant hint
,
vague hint
, and
insightful hint
. Insightful hints are hints that contain information highly relevant to the question. These hints can contain a sample solution for a similar problem, key insights into the formation of the question, or methodology that can be applied to the answer. Vague hints are hints relevant to the topic of the question but not the question itself. For example, if the question asks about the variational principle, a vague hint can be the sample solution to a completely different variational principle question or a textbook description of the variational principle. Unrelated hints include the solution to a difficult quantum physics problem, which is not relevant to the topic of the question.

You can find examples of hints representing each category in the Supplementary Material.

4.4
Evaluation

To assess different aspects of answers produced by the LLM, we developed grading rubrics evaluating not only the semantic accuracy of the responses but also the factual correctness and their logical consistency. Namely, our evaluation system comprised:

•

Completness and Relevancy (3 points):
Three points for a relevant and correct response. Zero points if the answer is irrelevant or incorrect, with partial points for partial correctness.

•

Logic and Reasoning (4 points):
We start with four points and deduct two points for each logical inconsistency and one point for each minor error in reasoning.

•

Truthfulness (lack of hallucination, 3 points):
We start with three points and deduct one point for each incorrect statement in the response.

We graded each response using both manual and automatic approaches. As for manual grading
(manual)
, we shuffled the order of responses to reduce bias (so the grader does not know which response is the result of which). The grader then used the evaluation criteria and sample answers to grade. For automatic grading, we have implemented two approaches. The first approach
(auto1)
involved providing GPT-4 with an answer and rubric while prompting GPT-4 to grade in a zero-shot manner. In the second approach
(auto2)
, we first prompted GPT-4 to generate an answer based on the sample answer and then used its answer as the key to grade the response. In this way, the grader kept a better version of its response as the benchmark (assuming the quality of the response is directly and positively correlated to the relevance and correctness of the prompt).

Since we have four levels of context (“no context”, “insightful hint”, “vague hint”, and “irrelevant hint”), a total of four responses were generated by GPT-4 for each question.
You can find a sample of grading with our evaluation criteria in Supplementary Material.

5
Results

5.1
Context matters

We provide GPT-4 with different levels of context when answering the queries from our question bank. The context is provided to GPT-4 API with an “assistant” role, followed by a “user” call to answer the question. For each question, we test the response, providing four different levels of context. In the first level (no hint), we provide no context. In the second level (irrelevant hint), the context contains a hint that is relevant to quantum physics but not relevant to the topic of the question. In the third level (vague hint), the context contains a hint that is relevant to the topic of the question but not directly applicable or related to the question. In the fourth level (insightful hint), the context contains a relevant and applicable hint to the solution.

5.3
Consensus between manual grading and automatic grading

Consensus across all three grading strategies highlighted two key insights: firstly, insightful hints notably enhance GPT-4’s performance in terms of mean scores; secondly, responses generated without hints performed the worst.

These results corroborate our initial hypothesis that highly relevant hints significantly boost GPT-4’s efficiency in addressing questions of scientific domains. Interestingly, our results also suggest that “irrelevant hints” boost the performance of our LLM in a similar way as “vague hints” (cf. again Figure
1
). This unexpected outcome could imply a misalignment between human perception of hint relevance and the model’s interpretation. It raises the possibility that what seems relevant and helpful to a human may not necessarily resonate in the same way with GPT-4, underscoring the nuanced dynamics of human-AI interaction in educational contexts.

QuantumGPT platform

6.2
The Credibility-Enhanced Feedback Architecture, CEFA

The accuracy and quality of data are critical to the performance. Some previous works have discussed the threat of data poisoning attacks
(
Chan et al. 2020
;
Fowl et al. 2021
)
. Even without a malicious attack, we are still vulnerable to unintentional inaccurate information. To address this concern and improve the data quality collected, we propose a Credibility-Enhanced Feedback Architecture (CEFA), as outlined in Figure
4
. It consists of three main modules: the distillation algorithm, relevance evaluation algorithm, and optimization algorithm. There is also a separate credibility system to examine the interaction between users and evaluate the credibility of each user. The distillation algorithm distills insights from users’ comments. Insights are then passed to the relevance evaluation algorithm to generate a relevance score. Finally, insights with their respective relevance and credibility scores are passed to the optimization algorithm to engineer the prompt.

Figure 4:
The schematics of the CEFA architecture.

Distillation Algorithm

The Distillation Algorithm module is developed to optimize the utility of user-generated content within QuantumGPT, aiming to refine the information flow by reducing the token count of user comments while preserving their intrinsic insights. This algorithm is adept at processing raw user comments, transforming them into a distilled format that retains the core insights but minimizes unnecessary verbosity and redundancy. Such a transformation is instrumental in significantly enhancing the data handling efficiency within the QuantumGPT framework.

The deployment of these summarized insights contributes notably to the overall performance of QuantumGPT. By reducing the length of the inputs, the algorithm effectively diminishes the computational burden on the system. This reduction in computational load directly translates into an enhanced speed of response generation, thereby elevating the system’s capability to handle complex user interactions swiftly and accurately.

Relevance Evaluation Algorithm

The Relevance Evaluation Algorithm is designed to ensure precise and relevant dialogue in quantum physics. It utilizes advanced Natural Language Processing techniques to assess the semantic relevance between user queries and responses, going beyond traditional keyword matching to include semantic similarity and contextual embedding models.

The algorithm employs sentence-transformer models to generate embeddings from user inputs, analyzing semantic relationships through cosine similarity measures. This process allows for the generation of relevance scores, which indicate the semantic alignment between questions and comments, ensuring contextually relevant and precise responses from QuantumGPT.

Credibility Algorithm

The Credibility Algorithm is designed to enhance online discourse quality by assessing user interactions. This algorithm identifies three ways for users to gain credibility: prior credentials (such as a relevant university affiliation), experience using the platform, and interactions with other users.

Figure 5:
The depiction of the Credibility System.

The algorithm operates within a dynamic feedback mechanism, as depicted in the Figure
5
. It functions by analyzing the sentiment of a comment from User 1 towards User 2. If User 1, who holds a high credibility standing, expresses a positive sentiment in their comment, the algorithm activates the Update Score Calculator. This calculator, in turn, increases User 2’s credibility score, provided the sentiment analysis results are positive.

For the purpose of the sentiment analysis, we use the “distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english” language model. This model, a streamlined version of the BERT framework, has been fine-tuned on the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2) dataset, making it highly effective in analyzing user comments. The sentiment scores derived from this analysis are used to adjust the credibility scores of users, with a particular emphasis on positive feedback from highly credible users. This process ensures that the platform’s credibility ratings reflect constructive and positive user interactions.

Context Window and Summarization Methods

Each LLM, including GPT-4, can only accept a finite-size input shorter than a specific context window. Therefore, in any practical implementation, we must implement a summarization technique to keep the context concise. In this analysis, we test the impact of the context summarization on the performance of the LLM.

An additional advantage of context summarization is that shorter input reduces the computational load on the system and increases the speed of response generation. For our preliminary methodology, as our primary summarization model, we utilize BART, which has been fine-tuned on the CNN/Daily Mail dataset. This model is not explicitly trained on scientific texts –- a domain that is relatively underrepresented in text summarization models. Despite this limitation, the model demonstrates proficiency in rendering concise summaries.

Our experiment entailed utilizing the BART model on three user hints ranked in relevancy to each question. We measured the outcomes by assessing the extent of length reduction and the effectiveness in preserving critical concepts.
For the purpose of this assessment, we modified our auto-grading technique by incorporating the manual chain of thought technique
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)

(auto3)
. This adaptation involves including our manual grading of a response as an example within the auto-grader system. This modification aims to align the results of the auto grader more closely with the outcomes of manual grading. This approach is expected to enhance the accuracy and reliability of
(auto3)
by incorporating human-like reasoning processes into its grading methodology.

8
Discussion

8.1
Significance of Context in LLM Performance

Our findings underscore the critical role of context in enhancing the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4, particularly in the specialized domain of quantum physics. The study reveals that providing contextually relevant hints can significantly improve the accuracy and robustness of LLM responses. However, our result also shows that hints relevant to the topic do not provide any more performance boost when compared to irrelevant hints. One hypothesis is that the determining factor to the helpfulness of the hint is deep insights into the methodology relevant to the question, and insights into the methodology can exist in hints that are not relevant to the topic. Currently, we are working on expanding our dataset to increase the statistical significance of our results.

8.2
Promise of Automatic Grading

The exploration of automatic grading methods presents great opportunities. Our experiments with auto1 and auto2 grading methods align decently well with manual grading outcomes (at least, in terms of the perceived sensitivity of this method). This suggests the potential of these methods as cost-effective and scalable alternatives to manual grading. However, the observed discrepancies, particularly in assessing unrelated and vague hints, highlight the need for further refinement and understanding of how LLMs interpret and respond to different types of context.

8.3
Challenges and limitations

Although our preliminary results are interesting, we are aware of the small size of our data set (10 entries and 40 responses in total). It should be pointed out that creating hints and conducting manual evaluation is a difficult task that requires domain knowledge. We are currently working on expanding the dataset and collecting questions and feedback regarding our experimental platform, QuantumGPT.

Additionally, while using BART, fine-tuned on the CNN/Daily Mail dataset, is effective for condensing hints into insightful summaries, we have observed a notable loss of critical information, including equations and scientific notations. Given the scarcity of models trained specifically on scientific texts, there is a compelling case for developing a specialized model. Such a model should be adept at summarizing scientific content, striking a balance between brevity and the retention of rich contextual information. This approach would address the gap in processing and summarizing complex scientific sentences.

8.4
Future Work

We have identified three pivotal areas for future research. Firstly, the investigation of insight distillation methods. This involves refining algorithms to more effectively condense complex scientific information into concise yet comprehensive insights, enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of LLM responses. Secondly, the further development of automatic grading systems. Lastly, an experiment with the CEFA architecture to test the users’ incentives. Another area of interest is the system’s robustness, e.g., its sensitivity to noise or inaccurate information.
