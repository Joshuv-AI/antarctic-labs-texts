Mesoscopic fluctuations in entanglement dynamics
Lih-King Lim, Cunzhong Lou, Chushun Tian

Abstract
Understanding fluctuation phenomena plays a dominant role in the development of many-body physics. The time evolution of entanglement is essential to a broad range of subjects in many-body physics, ranging from exotic quantum matter to quantum thermalization. Stemming from various dynamical processes of information, fluctuations in entanglement evolution differ conceptually from out-of-equilibrium fluctuations of traditional physical quantities. Their studies remain elusive. Here we uncover an emergent random structure in the evolution of the many-body wavefunction in two classes of integrable — either interacting or noninteracting — lattice models. It gives rise to out-of-equilibrium entanglement fluctuations which fall into the paradigm of mesoscopic fluctuations of wave interference origin. Specifically, the entanglement entropy variance obeys a universal scaling law, in each class, and the full distribution displays a sub-Gaussian upper and a sub-Gamma lower tail. These statistics are independent of both the system’s microscopic details and the choice of entanglement probes, and broaden the class of mesoscopic universalities. They have practical implications for controlling entanglement in mesoscopic devices.

Here, we develop an analytical theory for long-time dynamics of entanglement in two classes of integrable lattice models. One class of models, including the Rice-Mele model and the transverse field Ising chain, can be mapped to noninteracting fermions; the other class includes interacting spin chains, with the spin-
1/2

Treating various information-theoretic observables as unconventional mesoscopic probes, we show that their out-of-equilibrium fluctuations fall into the paradigm of universal mesoscopic fluctuations in disordered or chaotic systems. Our findings have immediate implications for controlling entanglement in quantum simulation platforms.

Results

Description of main results

Emergent mesoscopic fluctuations.
We find that the many-body wavefunction evolution endows the correlation matrix (the reduced density matrix) a random structure for noninteracting (interacting) models, even though the system is neither chaotic nor disordered. Specifically, for noninteracting models the time dependence enters through
N\approx{L\over 2}

However, there are important differences between ordinary quenched disorders and the randomness emergent from entanglement evolution. As shown below, the latter has a strength

Discussion

Our theory essentially hinges upon the relation between the wavefunction evolution and the trajectory
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
on a high-dimensional torus, and the information-theoretic observable as a function on that torus. So it can be extended to more general contexts. First, it applies to other characteristics of entanglement especially the subsystem complexity, studies of whose temporal fluctuations have been initiated recently
Tonni21
. Secondly, our ongoing studies have shown that there are no principal difficulties to generalize the present theory to nonintegrable interacting spin chains. Thirdly, experiments show that the entanglement dynamics of Bose-Hubbard model exhibits temporal fluctuations
Kaufman16
; it is interesting to generalize the present results to that model and compare them with existing measurements. Fourth, our ongoing studies have also shown that the general theory developed for interacting models, while based on the full reduced density of matrix, should be capable of unifying results for noninteracting and interacting models. Indeed, for noniteracting models, provided the initial state is Gaussian we can use that theory to reproduce Eq. (
Mesoscopic fluctuations in entanglement dynamics
); for non-Gaussian initial state, we can show that the emergent disorder carries the same structure as in the Gaussian case, which is a key leading to the scaling law (
Mesoscopic fluctuations in entanglement dynamics
) and suggests its robustness. Finally, because each virtual disordered sample corresponds to a pure state, our work suggests a simple way of producing a random pure-state ensemble to which great experimental efforts
Choi22
are made. That is, we evolve an initial pure state by a single Hamiltonian and collect states at distinct sufficiently long time.

\displaystyle C(t)=\tilde{C}(\bm{\omega}t).

(16)

We shall call
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
the correlation matrix as well. Its physical meanings will become clear later.

Note that by definition
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
is periodic in each argument
\varphi_{m}
,

\tilde{C}(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{m},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})=\tilde{C}(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{m}+2\pi,\ldots,\varphi_{N-1}).

(17)

Thus, these phases
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
in
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
completely determine
C(t)
and entail a classical motion that is a rotation in the
N
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
with constant angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
(cf. Fig. 1(d) in the main text):

\displaystyle C(t)\leftrightsquigarrow\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t\in\mathbb{T}^{N}.

(18)

In other words, given the initial state
\Psi(0)
, the correlation matrix corresponds one-to-one to a point
\bm{\omega}t
along the classical trajectory. This relation plays important roles in investigating fluctuations in long-time entanglement evolution. We remark that this relation makes no reference to the number-theoretical properties of
\bm{\omega}
, e.g. commensurate or incommensurate, but, as we will demonstrate later, the behaviors of long-time entanglement evolution are very sensitive to these properties. We note that similar correspondence also holds generally for the evolution of quantum expectation value
\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle
of
one-body
operator
{\cal A}
upon a global quench, see

Supplementary Appendix C
.

II.2
Time evolution of entanglement

II.2.1
Reduced density of matrix and entanglement entropy

The time evolution of the correlation matrix completely determines the entanglement evolution. Indeed, the instantaneous reduced density matrix provides full information on entanglement evolution. It is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(t)={{\rm e}^{-H_{A}(t)}/Z},\quad Z\equiv{\rm Tr}_{A}{\rm e}^{-H_{A}(t)}

(19)

with the trace restricted to the subsystem A, where
H_{A}(t)
is the instantaneous entanglement Hamiltonian. Because the systems considered are noninteracting and fermionic and the evolving state
\Psi(t)
is Gaussian,
H_{A}(t)
takes a quadratic form [4]:

\displaystyle H_{A}(t)=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{L_{A}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}={\bar{A}},{\bar{B}}}({\cal H}_{A}(t))_{i\sigma,i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}c_{i^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}},

(20)

and the
2L_{A}\times 2L_{A}
matrix
{\cal H}_{A}(t)
is determined by the correlation matrix
C(t)
via

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{A}(t)=\ln(C^{-1}(t)-\mathbb{I}).

(21)

So we see that the instantaneous reduced density of matrix is a
functional
of the instantaneous correlation matrix. Moreover, all
2L_{A}
instantaneous eigenvalues of
C(t)
, denoted as
p_{\nu}(t)
(
\nu=1,\ldots,2L_{A}
), belong to the interval
[0,1]
. Physically,
p_{\nu}(t)
gives the occupation probability of the instantaneous eigenmode
\nu
of the correlation matrix.

In most of this work, we study the entanglement entropy, which is the von Neumann entropy associated with the reduced density matrix and defined as

\displaystyle S(t)\equiv-{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(\rho_{A}(t)\ln\rho_{A}(t)\right).

(22)

Combining it with Supplementary Eqs. (
19
)-(
21
) we find that

\displaystyle S(t)=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{2L_{A}}\left(p_{\nu}(t)\ln p_{\nu}(t)+(1-p_{\nu}(t))\ln(1-p_{\nu}(t))\right)

(23)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle S(t)=-{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(C(t)\ln C(t)+(\mathbb{I}-C(t))\ln(\mathbb{I}-C(t))\right).

(24)

So, it is again a functional of
C(t)
, like
\rho_{A}(t)
. Furthermore, with the introduction of the binary entropy function

\displaystyle e(\lambda)\equiv-\lambda\ln\lambda-(1-\lambda)\ln(1-\lambda),

(25)

one can readily express Supplementary Eq. (
23
) as

\displaystyle S(t)=\int d\lambda\,e(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-C(t)\right).

(26)

For the reduced density matrix, we also have a relation similar to Supplementary Eq. (
16
). Indeed, due to Supplementary Eqs. (
16
) and (
21
) we have

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{A}(t)=\tilde{{\cal H}}_{A}(\bm{\omega}t),\quad\tilde{{\cal H}}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})\equiv\ln(\tilde{C}^{-1}(\bm{\varphi})-\mathbb{I}),

(27)

where
\tilde{\cal H}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
may be regarded as the entanglement Hamiltonian associated with

\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
. Then, upon introducing the following reduced density of matrix associated with

\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})\equiv{{\rm e}^{-\tilde{\cal H}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})}\over{\rm Tr}_{A}{\rm e}^{-\tilde{\cal H}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})}},

(28)

we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(t)=\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\omega}t).

(29)

By the same token, we can introduce the following entanglement entropy associated with

\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(\bm{\varphi})\equiv\int d\lambda\,e(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})\right),

(30)

then by Supplementary Eq. (
16
) the relation:

\displaystyle S(t)=\tilde{S}(\bm{\omega}t)

(31)

follows. Since all entanglement probes associated with
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
such as
\tilde{{\cal H}}_{A}
,
\tilde{\rho}_{A}
and
\tilde{S}
are obtained from their counterparts associated with
C(t)
by the replacement:
C(t)\rightarrow\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
, hereafter we use the same symbols:
{\cal H}_{A},\,\rho_{A},\,S
, etc. as corresponding probes associated with
C(t)
, keeping in mind the difference in the arguments.

II.2.2
More general entanglement probes

Let us consider a larger class of entanglement probes or information-theoretic observables
O(t)
, which are required only to be a functional of
C(t)
, i.e.

O(t)\equiv O[C(t)],

(32)

like Supplementary Eqs. (
21
) and (
24
). In words,
O(t)
depends on the time parameter only through
C(t)
, albeit its dependence on
C(t)
is highly nonlinear in general. Therefore,
O(t)
and
C(t)
can be diagonalized simultaneously at any
t
. Besides the entanglement entropy and the reduced density of matrix, such entanglement probes include the
n
-th order R
\acute{\rm e}
nyi entropy

\displaystyle S_{n}(t)\equiv{1\over 1-n}\ln{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(\rho_{A}(t)\right)^{n}

(33)

and the entanglement spectrum, namely, the eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbf{m}}(t)=\prod_{\nu=1}^{2L_{A}}\left(p_{\nu}(t)\right)^{\delta_{1n_{\nu}}}\left(1-p_{\nu}(t)\right)^{\delta_{0n_{\nu}}}

(34)

labelled by
\mathbf{m}=\{n_{\nu}\}
, which is the configuration of occupation numbers
n_{\nu}\,(=0,1)
at the eigenmode
\nu
. Owing to the relation Supplementary Eq. (
16
),
O(t)
must depend on
t
via
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
, i.e. in the same fashion as Supplementary Eqs. (
29
) and (
31
). That is, with the introduction of the
N
-variable function:
O(\bm{\varphi})\equiv O[\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})]
, the relation:

\displaystyle O(t)=O(\bm{\varphi})|_{\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t}.

(35)

then follows. Note that by definition
O(\bm{\varphi})
is
2\pi
-periodic in each
\varphi_{m}
also.

In order to appreciate more the importance of
C(t)
to the time evolution of generic entanglement probe more, we proceed to find explicitly the functional dependence of
S_{n}(t)
on
C(t)
. First of all, because of
-\ln Z(t)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{2L_{A}}\ln(1-p_{\nu}(t))
, we have

\displaystyle-\ln Z(t)=\int d\lambda\,\ln(1-\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-C(t)\right).

(36)

Moreover, from Supplementary Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
) we obtain

\displaystyle\ln{\rm Tr}_{A}{\rm e}^{-nH_{A}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{2L_{A}}\ln\left(1+\left({p_{\nu}(t)\over 1-p_{\nu}(t)}\right)^{n}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\lambda\,\ln\left(1+\left({\lambda\over 1-\lambda}\right)^{n}\right)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-C(t)\right).

Then, by substituting Supplementary Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
) into Supplementary Eq. (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle S_{n}(t)=\int d\lambda\,e_{n}(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-C(t)\right),

(38)

where

e_{n}(\lambda)\equiv{1\over 1-n}\ln\left(\lambda^{n}+(1-\lambda)^{n}\right).

(39)

In the limit:
n\rightarrow 1
,
e_{n}(\lambda)\rightarrow e(\lambda)
. Therefore, we recover the expression for the entanglement entropy Supplementary Eq. (
26
).

Observing the right-hand sides of Supplementary Eqs. (
26
) and (
36
)-(
38
), we find that they have the common structure:

\displaystyle O(t)=\int d\lambda\,\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-C(t)\right),

(40)

and differ only in the function
\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)
. Remarkably, this structure relates the time evolution of different entanglement probes to the same quantity, the instantaneous spectral density of the correlation matrix. Likewise, all
O(\bm{\varphi})
can be related to the same quantity, the spectral density of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
, in the way as

\displaystyle O(\bm{\varphi})=\int d\lambda\,\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})\right).

(41)

This expression bears a firm analogy to the expression for canonical quantities in mesoscopic physics [5-10], as we will demonstrate in Supplementary Note
4.3
.
So the evolving correlation matrix
C(t)
is the building block of our out-of-equilibrium fluctuation theory. Moreover, as we shall see in the next Supplementary Note, the relation Supplementary Eq. (
35
) allows us to trade out-of-equilibrium entanglement fluctuations to mesoscopic fluctuations from one virtual disordered sample to another, which are seemingly unrelated to the dynamics of entanglement at first glance.

Supplementary Eqs. (
16
), (
18
), (
35
), (
40
) and (
41
) constitute a formalism connecting various information-theoretic observables, that characterize entanglement evolution, to classical rotation on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
.

III
Statistical equivalence

It is well known that dynamical properties of classical rotation on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
are extremely sensitive to the number-theoretic properties of the angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
. Notably, if the equation:
\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}x_{m}\omega_{m}=0
has no nontrivial integer solutions, i.e.

\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}x_{m}\omega_{m}=0,\quad x_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}\,\Rightarrow\,x_{1}=\ldots=x_{N-1}=0,

(42)

then
\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{N-1}
are incommensurate or said to bear the
arithmetic linear independence
[11], which should be distinguished from the concept of
statistical independence
to be introduced shortly; if the condition Supplementary Eq. (
42
) is not satisfied, the
N
frequencies are commensurate. For incommensurate
\bm{\omega}
the rotation is ergodic, while for commensurate
\bm{\omega}
it is periodic [12]. By the dispersion relation Supplementary Eq. (
4
) the angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
governing the evolution of
C(t)
(cf. Supplementary Eq. (
3
)) is incommensurate. So we will focus on the incommensurate case from now on. In
VIII

we will study the consequences of commensurate
\bm{\omega}
.

By definition
O(\bm{\varphi})
introduced in Supplementary Note
2.2

is
2\pi
-periodic in each argument
\varphi_{m}
. As a result,
O(t)
is a
quasiperiodic
function of
t
with
frequency basis

\bm{\omega}
; in other words, after the initial process the time evolution
O(t)
transits to quasiperiodic oscillations [13], as exemplified by the entanglement entropy (Fig. 1 of the main text) and the second-order R
\acute{\rm e}
nyi entropy (see Supplementary Figs.
1
(a) and
1
(b) below): These quasiperiodic oscillations are conceptually different from the entanglement entropy oscillations due to the traversal of quasiparticle pairs or the incomplete revival of system’s wavefunction; the latter oscillations rapidly damp in the course of time [14]. The quasiperiodic oscillations do not exclude the occurrence of a big bump or dip at certain times, and the big dip may signal the recurrence. It should be emphasized that the quasiperiodic oscillations of different probes, all of which can be attributed to those of the correlation matrix, are quantum oscillations (cf. Supplementary Eq. (
14
)).

For quasiperiodic
O(t)
we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}O(t)=\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}O(\bm{\varphi})=\langle O\rangle

(43)

by the ergodic theorem [12]. Here

\langle\cdot\rangle\equiv\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}(\cdot)

(44)

stands for the average with respect to the probability measure
\mathbf{P}
over
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, which has a uniform density of
1/(2\pi)^{N}
. Therefore,
O(t)
quasiperiodically oscillates around
\langle O\rangle
. The ergodic theorem further gives that for arbitrary interval
\Delta
,

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{O(t)\in\Delta}\frac{dt}{T}=\int_{O(\bm{\varphi})\in\Delta}{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}.

(45)

Here the left-hand side gives the frequency for the time series
O(t)
to appear in
\Delta
, while the right-hand side gives the probability for
O(\bm{\varphi})
to be in the same interval, i.e.
\mathbf{P}(O(\bm{\varphi})\in\Delta)
. Consequently, out-of-equilibrium fluctuations displayed by the quasiperiodic oscillations
O(t)
must have the same statistics as fluctuations of
O(\bm{\varphi})
with
\bm{\varphi}
, provided
\bm{\varphi}
is drawn randomly from
\mathbf{P}
. This statistical equivalence holds for any entanglement probes introduced in
II.2
, and is confirmed numerically (Fig. 1a in the main text). More generally, for any set
{\cal A}\subset\mathbb{T}^{N}
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\bm{\omega}t\in{\cal A}}\frac{dt}{T}=\int_{\bm{\varphi}\in{\cal A}}{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\equiv\mathbf{P}(\bm{\varphi}\in{\cal A}),

(46)

with Supplementary Eq. (
45
) as a special case.

The right-hand sides of Supplementary Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
) entail a uniform joint probability density,
1/(2\pi)^{N}
, for the
N
components of
\bm{\varphi}
. Therefore, (with respect to this probability) these components are statistically independent, and each of them is uniformly distributed over the
1
D torus
\mathbb{T}
. That is, the above probability measure
\mathbf{P}
is a product of
N
uniform probability measures over
\mathbb{T}
. Such product structure of probability measures allows the applications of the theory of concentration of measure [15-17], and leads to important consequences later on.

IV
Emergence of mesoscopic fluctuations

The Rice-Mele model, the Su-Schriefer-Heeger model, and TFIC are all integrable. Besides, they are deterministic, namely, free of any
extrinsic
randomness or stochasticity. Despite of these, armed with the general principle established in the last Supplementary Note we show in this Supplementary Note that some canonical quantum chaotic phenomena can still emerge from the time evolution of the correlation matrix. Most importantly, we show that out-of-equilibrium fluctuations of various entanglement probes can be traded to their fluctuations from one virtual, mesoscopic disordered sample to another.

IV.1
Emergent of ensemble of random correlation matrices

According to Supplementary Eq. (
15
), the correlation matrix
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
can be separated into two parts, i.e.

\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})=C_{0}+C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}).

(47)

Each part inherits the block-Toeplitz matrix structure from
\tilde{C}
, i.e.
(C_{0,1})_{ii^{\prime}}=(C_{0,1})_{i-i^{\prime}=l}
with
(C_{0,1})_{l}
being
2\times 2
blocks defined in the sublattice sector. The first part,
C_{0}
, has no dependence on
\bm{\varphi}
and is well defined for
L\rightarrow\infty
,

(C_{0})_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k}{\rm e}^{ikl}\gamma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}{\rm e}^{ikl}\gamma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k),

(48)

whereas the second part,
C_{1}
, depends on
\bm{\varphi}
,

\displaystyle(C_{1})_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\biggl(1-{1\over 2}\,\delta_{m0}\biggr)\biggl(\left({\rm e}^{ik_{m}l}\alpha_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k_{m})+(m\rightarrow-m)\right)\cos\varphi_{m}\quad

\displaystyle+\left({\rm e}^{ik_{m}l}\beta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k_{m})+(m\rightarrow-m)\right)\sin\varphi_{m}\,\biggr),

(49)

and has no limiting behaviors for
L\rightarrow\infty
because of the incommensurality of
\bm{\omega}
. Using Supplementary Eqs. (
263
), (
264
), (
267
) and (
268
), we find after lengthy but straightforward calculations that the coefficient of
\cos\varphi_{m}
(
\sin\varphi_{m}
) in the summand is real (purely imaginary), which is a function of
k_{m}
and denoted as
R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k_{m})
(
I_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(k_{m})
). So Supplementary Eq. (
49
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle(C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\,\cos\varphi_{m}+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}I_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\,\sin\varphi_{m}\equiv\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})+i\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi}),

(50)

where
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
are the real and imaginary part of
(C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
, respectively. It is easy to show that for
l=0
and
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
the imaginary part vanishes, i.e. the diagonal of
C_{1}
is real. By Supplementary Eqs. (
16
) and (
47
), we have

C(t)=C_{0}+C_{1}(\bm{\omega}t),

(51)

with

\displaystyle(C_{1}(\bm{\omega}t))_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\,\cos(\omega_{m}t)+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}I_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\,\sin(\omega_{m}t)\equiv\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)+i\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t).

(52)

Therefore,
C(t)
displays quasiperiodic oscillations around
C_{0}
.

In

Supplementary Appendix D
we show that, thanks to Supplementary Eq. (
46
), both
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)
or correspondingly
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
are sub-Gaussian centered random variables. More precisely, they have zero mean and their deviation probabilities satisfy the following concentration inequalities:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\geq\epsilon}\frac{dt}{T}=\mathbf{P}\left(|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\rm e}^{-\frac{L}{\eta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}}\,\epsilon^{2}},

(53)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\geq\epsilon}\frac{dt}{T}=\mathbf{P}\left(|\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\rm e}^{-\frac{L}{\eta^{\prime}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}}\,\epsilon^{2}},

(54)

for large
L
and for any positive
\epsilon
. Here

\displaystyle\eta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{dk\over 2\pi}\left({\rm e}^{ikl}\alpha_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(kl)+{\rm e}^{-ikl}\alpha_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(-kl)\right)^{2},

(55)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle-\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{dk\over 2\pi}\left({\rm e}^{ikl}\beta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(kl)+{\rm e}^{-ikl}\beta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(-kl)\right)^{2},

(56)

whose numerical coefficients have no dependence on
L
and thus are not important. Both
\eta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
and
\eta^{\prime}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
are positive. We see that

C_{1}(\bm{\varphi})
varies around zero with variance
\sim 1/L
, i.e.

{\rm Var}(\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}})\sim{\rm Var}(\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}})\sim\frac{1}{L}.

(57)

Physically, this scaling can be understood as follows. When
\bm{\omega}
is incommensurate,
\cos(\omega_{0}t)
,
\cos(\omega_{1}t)
, …,
\cos(\omega_{N-1}t)
are
N
statistically independent random numbers of zero mean [11]. As a result, Supplementary Eq. (
52
), that corresponds to
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
(respectively
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
), is a sum of
N\sim L
random numbers. For large
N
one may expect the central limiting theorem to apply, which then gives
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\sim\sqrt{N}/L\sim 1/\sqrt{L}
and likewise for
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
.

In

Supplementary Appendix E
we further show that any random variable
\mathfrak{R}
or
\mathfrak{I}
in the block
(C(t))_{l}
(respectively
(\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l}
) is statistically independent of that in any distinct block
(C(t))_{l^{\prime}\neq l}
(respectively
(\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l^{\prime}\neq l}
). In contrast, from Supplementary Eq. (
52
) we see that random variables
\mathfrak{R},\,\mathfrak{I}
in the same block
(C(t))_{l}
(respectively
(\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l}
) are
not
statistically independent.

So, when the classical trajectory:
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
sweeps out
\mathbb{T}^{N}
uniformly, the infinite time series
C(t)
generates a specific ensemble of random block-Toeplitz matrices
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
, denoted as
{\cal E}
. For each member of
{\cal E}
, its
\bm{\varphi}
-dependent part,
C_{1}(\bm{\varphi})
, is composed of
L_{A}
statistically independent
2\times 2
blocks
(C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l}
(
l=0,1,\ldots,L_{A}-1
), the real or imaginary part of whose matrix elements are sub-Gaussian centered random variables, with the variance
\propto\eta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}/L,\eta^{\prime}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}/L
. The probability measure over
{\cal E}
is induced by the uniform probability measure
\mathbf{P}
over
\mathbb{T}^{N}
in the way as follows: Given arbitrary intervals
\Delta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{r,\,i}
for every
l,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
, the probability for
\tilde{C}
to satisfy:
\textrm{Re}(C_{1})_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\in\Delta^{r}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
and
\textrm{Im}(C_{1})_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\in\Delta^{i}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
is
\mathbf{P}(\bm{\varphi}\in{\cal B})
, where the set
{\cal B}
is defined as

{\cal B}\equiv\bigcap_{l,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\{{\rm Re}(C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\in\Delta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{r},\,{\rm Im}(C_{1}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\in\Delta_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{i}\}.

(58)

With this induced probability measure, the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is equivalent to the time series
C(t)
statistically. This ensemble differs from the ensemble of random hermitian Toeplitz matrices studied very recently [18,19] in two aspects. First, each member in
\mathcal{E}
, though being hermitian and Toeplitz-type, carries a block structure; that is, the element of Toeplitz matrix is now a
2\times 2
block, instead of a complex number. Second, according to Supplementary Eq. (
57
) at fixed ratio
L_{A}/L
the variance of its matrix elements decays with the matrix dimension and vanishes in the large matrix dimension limit, whereas in canonical studies of random matrices that variance is independent of the matrix dimension.

IV.2
Spectral statistics of correlation matrix

We proceed to study the statistical properties of the spectrum of the correlation matrices from either the time series
C(t)
or
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
drawn randomly from the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
. Since they are statistically equivalent, we will mainly focus on the properties derived from the time series
C(t)
. In this work it suffices to consider the nearest-neighbor spacing distribution,
P_{0}(s)
, with the eigenvalues rescaled by the mean eigenvalue spacing.

In order to spell out the block-Toeplitz structure in a transparent way,
the correlation matrix
C(t)
can be rewritten in the following form:

\displaystyle C(t)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{I}+{\Gamma}(t),

(59)

where

\displaystyle{\Gamma}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\check{\Pi}_{0}&\check{\Pi}_{1}&\ldots&\check{\Pi}_{L_{A}-2}&\check{\Pi}_{L_{A}-1}\\
\check{\Pi}_{-1}&\check{\Pi}_{0}&\ldots&\check{\Pi}_{L_{A}-3}&\check{\Pi}_{L_{A}-2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\check{\Pi}_{-(L_{A}-2)}&\check{\Pi}_{-(L_{A}-3)}&\ldots&\check{\Pi}_{0}&\check{\Pi}_{1}\\
\check{\Pi}_{-(L_{A}-1)}&\check{\Pi}_{-(L_{A}-2)}&\ldots&\check{\Pi}_{-1}&\check{\Pi}_{0}\end{array}\right)_{L_{A}\times L_{A}},\textrm{\ \ \ \ }

with

\displaystyle\check{\Pi}_{l}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{l0}+f_{l}&g_{l}\\
g_{-l}^{*}&\frac{1}{2}\delta_{l0}-f_{l}\end{array}\right)

defined in the sublattice sector and

\displaystyle f_{l}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k}\,{\rm e}^{ikl}\,|\tilde{u}_{fk}(t)|^{2},\textrm{\ \ \ }g_{l}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k}\,{\rm e}^{ik(l+{1\over 2})}\,\tilde{u}^{*}_{fk}(t)\tilde{v}_{fk}(t).

(69)

with expressions of
\tilde{u}_{fk}(t)
,
\tilde{v}_{fk}(t)
given in

Supplementary Appendix B
.
The statistical property
C(t)
is therefore determined by
\Gamma(t)
. In parallel, we can generalize the above expressions Supplementary Eqs. (
IV.2
) and (
IV.2
) to define the corresponding
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{I}+\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{\varphi})
and
\tilde{\Pi}_{l}(\bm{\varphi})
.

Owing to the structure described by Supplementary Eqs. (
IV.2
) and (
IV.2
), the eigenvalues of
\Gamma(t)
come in pair: one positive and the other negative, with the same absolute value. Therefore, its spectrum splits into two subspectra symmetric with respect to zero (cf. Supplementary Fig.
3
(b) below): One consists of negative eigenvalues and the other positive. We focus on the former below.

First, we follow the method of Ref. [19] to estimate small-
s
behaviors of
P_{0}
. For this purpose we need to consider only block-Toeplitz matrices of minimal dimension. Such matrices carry the structure of
\Gamma
but in lower matrix dimension, and are perturbed from the block-diagonal matrix, with each block being
{\rm diag}(\vartheta,-\vartheta)
and
\vartheta<0
being some fixed constant. To satisfy the requirements we set
L_{A}
in Supplementary Eq. (
IV.2
) to be two and the diagonal elements of
\check{\Pi}_{0}
to be
\pm(\vartheta+\delta\vartheta)
,
with
\delta\vartheta
being real. Because of
L_{A}=2
the subspectra consist of two eigenvalues. By analysis in Supplementary Note
4.1
,
these matrices have only two independent random variables, one from the block
\check{\Pi}_{0}
and the other from
\check{\Pi}_{1}
. So the joint probability of the two eigenvalues can be expressed as an integral of these two variables. Thus the probability for both eigenvalues to be within a small distance of
s
from
\vartheta
is
\sim s^{2}
, with the power
2
accounting for the number of independent random variables. Since the joint probability is the cumulative spacing distribution
\int_{0}^{s}P_{0}(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}
, we find that

P_{0}(s\rightarrow 0)\,{\sim}\,s.

(70)

Thus
P_{0}
vanishes at
s=0
. This is the so-called level repulsion phenomenon — a defining property of quantum chaos [20]. Strikingly, here the phenomenon occurs in a peculiar system, such that it is integrable and free of any extrinsic randomness and thus has nothing to do with chaos, at first glance.

Next, we estimate large-
s
behaviors of
P_{0}
. For this purpose we return to the original matrix, and note that the matrix elements in
\check{\Pi}_{l}
and
\tilde{\Pi}_{l}
decay rapidly with
l
, namely, the distance to the main diagonal for which
l=0,\,\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
. So
\Gamma
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
are random band matrices, and their matrix elements are short-ranged correlated since as shown in

Supplementary Appendix E
the matrix elements of distinct blocks are statistically independent. Recall that for random band matrices with all their elements being statistically independent, it is generally believed that the eigenvectors may exhibit localization [21]. But it is not clear to us whether this might occur here, because the random Toeplitz band matrix is structured, i.e. all its elements of the same distance to the main diagonal are identical, and additionally the disorder strength has a size dependence shown in Supplementary Eq. (
57
). From the Toeplitz structure we can expect only that the level repulsion exists up to the scale of mean eigenvalue spacing. So we can follow the arguments of Ref. [22] to estimate large-
s
behaviors. Supposing that a band of large width
s
includes
\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle
eigenvalues on the average, by the central limiting theorem we find that the variance of the number of eigenvalues within this band
{\rm Var}({\cal N}(s))\sim\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle
. Since the probability for the band to include
{\cal N}(s)
eigenvalues is
\sim{\rm e}^{-({\cal N}(s)-\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle)^{2}/{\rm Var}({\cal N}(s))}
, the probability for the band to be empty is
\sim{\rm e}^{-\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle^{2}/{\rm Var}({\cal N}(s))}\sim{\rm e}^{-const.\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle}
, with
const.
being some positive constant. Since
P_{0}(s)
is the probability for the band to be empty and
\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle=s
, a Poissonian tail,

P_{0}(s\gg 1)\,\sim\,{\rm e}^{-const.\langle{\cal N}(s)\rangle}={\rm e}^{-const.s}

(71)

then follows. The full Poissonian distribution was conjectured for random real symmetric Toeplitz matrices in Ref. [23].

Finally, the limiting behaviors described by Supplementary Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
) can be unified via the following simple form:

P_{0}(s)=4s{\rm e}^{-2s}

(72)

with the numerical coefficients fixed by the conditions:
\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{0}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}sP_{0}(s)ds=1
. The distribution Supplementary Eq. (
72
), that holds for arbitrary
s
, is called semi-Poissonian distribution [24]. It was seen for the first time in Ref. [18] for truly random hermitian Toeplitz matrices, but without the block structure. This distribution is confirmed in simulations of the spectral statistics of the evolving correlation matrix
C(t)
(see Fig. 1(c) in the main text).

IV.3
A new class of mesoscopic fluctuations

We have shown that for long time various entanglement probes
O(t)
quasiperiodically oscillate around their means
\langle O\rangle
. This implies that when the entanglement evolution enters into the stationary state at long time, various probes are
not
time independent but display fluctuations around the stationary value. As shown above the statistics of these out-of-equilibrium fluctuations is equivalent to that of the fluctuations of the same probe with
\bm{\varphi}
, provided
\bm{\varphi}
is drawn from
\mathbb{T}^{N}
with a uniform probability. Now we show that these fluctuations with
\bm{\varphi}
connect the entanglement evolution and mesoscopic physics.

To this end we observe that the fluctuations of entanglement probes with
\bm{\varphi}
have following prominent features. First, because a generic probe
O(\bm{\varphi})
is a functional of the correlation matrix
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
, the fluctuation statistics of different probes can all be attributed to that of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
or more precisely its spectral density. Second, because the random, namely,
\bm{\varphi}
-dependent part of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
vanishes in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
, these fluctuation phenomena can occur only for finite
L_{A}
at fixed ratio
L_{A}/L\,(>0)
. Third, these phenomena inherit the quantum coherence nature from quasiperiodic oscillations. By these three features the fluctuations of various entanglement probes with
\bm{\varphi}
fall into the paradigm of mesoscopic
sample-to-sample
fluctuations [5-10]; see Supplementary Table
1
for comparisons. Indeed, in mesoscopic electronics or photonics various transport characteristics (e.g. the conductance or transmittance, the shot-noise power, etc.) can be expressed in the same form as the right-hand side of Supplementary Eq. (
41
). Specifically, upon passing to various mesoscopic transport probes, the hermitian matrix:
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
is replaced by the hermitian matrix:
T\equiv\mathbf{t}\mathbf{t}^{\dagger}
, whose dimension gives the number of channels for wave transport in disordered samples and is finite also [25, 26]. Here
\mathbf{t}
is the transmission matrix that encodes all information of wave propagation from the input to the output end. Similar to that
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
is determined by
\bm{\varphi}
randomly distributed over
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, the matrix
\mathbf{t}
and thereby
T
are determined by the disordered potential or the dielectric configuration, denoted as
\bm{V}
; furthermore, since typically the potential and dielectric fluctuations at distinct spatial points are independent and Gaussian, each disorder realization can be viewed as a vector in a high-dimensional Euclidean space,
\bm{V}\equiv(V_{\bm{r}_{1}},V_{\bm{r}_{2}},\ldots)
with
\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\ldots
labelling distinct spatial points, Gaussian distributed in that space [27].
So, similar to that varying
\bm{\varphi}
gives rise to an ensemble of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
, varying
\bm{V}
gives rise to an ensemble of random matrices
T(\bm{V})
, and the distribution of
\bm{V}
induces the distribution of
T(\bm{V})
, albeit in a highly complicated manner. With the replacement described above, the right-hand side of Supplementary Eq. (
41
) gives various transport characteristics, e.g. the conductance or transmittance for
\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)=\lambda
and the shot-noise power for
\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)=\lambda(1-\lambda)
[10]. As such,
T(\bm{V})
represents a finite disordered sample. In the same fashion, as
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
determines various entanglement probes
O(\bm{\varphi})
, it mimics a finite disordered sample, with
\bm{\varphi}
being the ‘disorder realization’. So the fluctuations of various entanglement probes with
\bm{\varphi}
are traded to mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations. Furthermore, since the former fluctuations are statistically equivalent to the out-of-equilibrium fluctuations displayed by quasiperiodic oscillations, those oscillations have the same nature as the mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations.

However, standard random matrix theories require the variance of matrix elements to be independent of the matrix dimension. This is totally opposed to the present situations: Supplementary Eq. (
57
) shows that at fixed ratio
L_{A}/L>0
the variance of the matrix elements of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
decays with the matrix dimension
2L_{A}
as
\sim 1/L_{A}
, so that nonrandom
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
results in the limit
L_{A}\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, analytical or mathematical studies of random Toeplitz matrices are highly challenging, as put explicitly by Bogolmony and Giraud: “seem to be inaccessible to known analytic methods” [19]; even for the simplest random Toeplitz matrices, only recently have the studies of their spectral statistics been initiated and very few results been reported [18, 19]. For these reasons, we are prohibited to invoke standard mesoscopic theories to study the mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations of entanglement probes. Therefore the long-time dynamics of entanglement brings us a new class of mesoscopic fluctuations.

V
Statistics of emergent mesoscopic fluctuations: general theory

To overcome the difficulties discussed in the end of last Supplementary Note with the unusual disorder structure, below we develop an approach to study the statistics of mesoscopic fluctuations emergent from entanglement evolution. The idea starts from that a generic evolving entanglement probe
O(t)
is given by a
N
-variable function,
O(\bm{\varphi})
, over
\mathbb{T}^{N}
according to Supplementary Eq. (
35
). So, when
\mathbb{T}^{N}
is equipped with uniform probability, as imposed by Supplementary Eq. (
46
),
O(\bm{\varphi})
becomes a random variable depending on
N
— which is finite but large — statistically independent angular variables
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1}
. Note that, however, the dependence of
O
on those angular variables is highly nonlinear. Then, taking the advantage of the very fact that
N
(i.e.
L
) is finite, we can combine the continuity properties of the function
O(\bm{\varphi})
with the nonasymptotic probability theory, so-called concentration inequality [28], to study the statistical properties of fluctuations of
O
with
\bm{\varphi}
. A similar idea has recently been developed to find the universal
wave-to-wave
fluctuations in mesoscopic transport [27].

V.1
Concentration inequalities

To implement the idea above, we introduce the logarithmic moment-generating function defined as

G(u)\equiv\ln\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle,\quad u\in\mathbb{R}

(73)

for a generic entanglement probe
O
. Because
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1}
are independent and identically distributed random variables, we then have the so-called modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality [28], read

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq{1\over u^{2}}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m})\bigr)\rangle}{\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle}.

(74)

Here

\phi(x)\equiv{\rm e}^{x}-x-1.

(75)

O_{m}
is an arbitrary function of
\bm{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m-1},\varphi_{m+1},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
, independent of
\varphi_{m}
. By definition it is also a random variable, but depending on (
N-1
) instead of
N
independent
\varphi
’s. It is important that the Supplementary inequality (
74
) holds for any
N
. This is fundamentally different from the situations in traditional probability theory, where the number of independent random variables has to be sent to infinity eventually. In other words, the traditional probability theory is a limiting theory, whereas the concentration inequality is not. In this Supplementary Note we need two special classes of
O_{m}
’s, defined as the infimum (supremum) of
O(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi^{\prime}_{m},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
over
\varphi^{\prime}_{m}
with the rest of
(N-1)
unprimed variables held fixed. The function is denoted as
O^{+}_{m}
(
O^{-}_{m}
) correspondingly. For the convenience below we also introduce the following quantity:

b_{\pm}\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\right\rangle.

(76)

Roughly speaking, it is the total variance of the probe
O
with respect to each argument.

V.1.1
The upper tail of the distribution

Following Ref. [28], we set
u>0
and
O_{m}=O_{m}^{+}
for the Supplementary inequality (
74
) to study the distribution of the upward fluctuations (i.e.
O-\langle O\rangle>0
). However, our subsequent treatments of that inequality differ substantially from those in Ref. [28]. Observing that
\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)
can be Taylor expanded as

\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)={u^{2}\over 2}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n}\over n!}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{n},

(77)

we separate from
\phi
the contribution:
{u^{2}\over 2}\langle(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\rangle
to the first term, and rewrite the Supplementary inequality (
77
) as

\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)={u^{2}\over 2}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\right\rangle+{u^{2}\over 2}\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n}\over n!}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{n},

(78)

with
\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\equiv(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}-\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}\right\rangle
characterizing the deviation of
(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}
from its mean. Substituting Supplementary Eq. (
78
) into the right-hand side of the Supplementary inequality (
74
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq\frac{b_{+}}{2}+\delta F(u),

(79)

where

\delta F(u)=\left\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\right\rangle^{-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\left(\delta(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{(-u)^{n}\over(n+2)!}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{n+2}\right)\right\rangle.

(80)

It is important to note that although the right-hand side of the Supplementary inequality (
79
) bears some similarities to familiar ones in mathematical literatures [28], there are essential differences. In particular, we do not resort to the bounding property of the function:
\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}(O-O_{m}^{+})^{2}
, and consequently the first term makes no references to the bounding properties of that function. Whereas in mathematical literatures
b_{+}
is determined by the bounding properties of that function.

Next, noting that
O-O_{m}^{+}\geq 0
and the equal sign is taken only at a set of zero (Lebesgue) measure, we find that the right-hand side of the Supplementary inequality (
74
)

\displaystyle{1\over u^{2}}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)\rangle}{\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u\rightarrow+\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}{1\over u}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\bigl(O-O_{m}^{+}\bigr)\rangle}{\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle}.

(81)

As a result, there must exist some constant
const.
and some sufficiently large
u^{*}
, so that

{1\over u^{2}}\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\langle\,{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\,\phi\bigl(-u(O-O_{m}^{+})\bigr)\rangle}{\langle{\rm e}^{u(O-\langle O\rangle)}\rangle}\leq{const.\over u},\quad{\rm for}\,\,u>u^{*}.

(82)

This implies that the right-hand side of the modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality is bounded by the curve
\sim{1\over u}
in the large
u
regime. On the other hand, it is easy to show that for
u>0
,
{dG(u)\over du}>0
and grows unboundedly with
u
. Taking this and the Supplementary inequality (
82
) into account, we find that the function
\delta F/{dG\over du}
must be bounded for
u>0
. Let its supremum be
c_{+}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\delta F(u)\leq c_{+}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du}.

(83)

As we shall see immediately, that
dG/du
is positive definite (for
u>0
) entails the sign of
c_{+}
important consequences.

Combining the Supplementary inequalities (
79
) and (
83
), we arrive at the following differential inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq\frac{b_{+}}{2}+c_{+}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du}.

(84)

Moreover, by Supplementary Eq. (
73
) this differential inequality is implemented by the boundary condition:
{G(u)\over u}=0
at
u=0
. Now, let us study the cases of
c_{+}<0
and
c_{+}>0
separately:

•

c_{+}<0
: Thanks to
{dG\over du}>0
the Supplementary inequality (
84
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}\frac{G(u)}{u}\leq\frac{b_{+}}{2}.

(85)

It can be solved readily. The result is

\displaystyle G(u)\leq\frac{b_{+}u^{2}}{2}.

(86)

Then it is a standard exercise to use the Markov inequality [28] to obtain the concentration inequality for the upper tail of the distribution. Specifically, by the Markov inequality we have for arbitrary positive
\epsilon
and
u
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-u\epsilon+G(u)}.

(87)

Combining it with the Supplementary inequality (
86
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-u\epsilon+\frac{b_{+}u^{2}}{2}}.

(88)

Since
u
is arbitrarily positive, upon minimizing the exponent over positive
u
it gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2b_{+}}}.

(89)

This concentration inequality defines a sub-Gaussian upper tail distribution.

•

c_{+}>0
: We solve the Supplementary inequality (
84
). The result is

\displaystyle G(u)\leq\frac{b_{+}}{2}\frac{u^{2}}{1-c_{+}u}.

(90)

This kind of bounds hold also for Gamma random variables, and thus generalize the tail behaviors of the Gamma distribution, giving the so-called sub-Gamma tail distribution [28]. Specifically, by the same method of deriving the Supplementary inequality (
89
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{+}+c_{+}\epsilon)}}.

(91)

The constants
b_{+}
and
c_{+}
are called the
variance factor
and
scale parameter
, respectively [28]. Of course, it does not mean that
b_{+}
gives the exact variance, but gives a bound for the exact variance in general. In the present work, by the choice of
b_{\pm}
defined by Supplementary Eq. (
76
) (
b_{-}
is for the lower tail; see the concentration inequalities, namely, Supplementary (
93
) and (
94
) below), which are substantially different from canonical choices in mathematical literatures, we further find that
b_{\pm}
are proportional to the exact variance, with the help of numerical simulations (see Supplementary Note
6.5

for detailed discussions). Finally, we remark that as
\epsilon
increases, the sub-Gamma bound displays a crossover from the Gaussian form
{\rm e}^{-\epsilon^{2}/(2b_{+})}
to the exponential form
{\rm e}^{-\epsilon/(2c_{+})}
form at
\epsilon\sim b_{+}/c_{+}
.

V.1.2
The lower tail of the distribution

The distribution of downward fluctuations (i.e.
\langle O\rangle-O>0
) can be studied in the same way. We set
u<0
and
O_{m}=O_{m}^{-}
for the modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality, i.e. Supplementary (
74
). With these two replacements, we have

\displaystyle\delta F(u)\leq c_{-}\,\frac{dG(u)}{du}

(92)

with
dG/du<0
, similar to the bounding described by Supplementary (
83
). The subsequent analysis can be carried out in exactly the same way. But the results are reversed:

•

c_{-}<0
: We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\langle O\rangle-O\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{-}+|c_{-}|\epsilon)}},

(93)

i.e. a sub-Gamma lower tail.

•

c_{+}>0
: We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\langle O\rangle-O\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2b_{-}}},

(94)

i.e. a sub-Gaussian lower tail.

V.2
Calculations of the variance factors
\bm{b_{\pm}}

In this part we study the variance factors
b_{\pm}
defined by Supplementary Eq. (
76
) in details. First of all, we keep in mind that
O_{m}^{\pm}(\bm{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})
in Supplementary Eq. (
76
) actually belongs to a special class of (real-valued) functions of
\bm{\varphi}
, such that they are independent of
\varphi_{m}
. So
(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}
is a function of
\bm{\varphi}
also, i.e.
(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\equiv(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}(\bm{\varphi})
. Let
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
be the value of
\varphi_{m}
, at which
O(\bm{\varphi})=O^{\pm}_{m}(\bm{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m})
. Note that by definition
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
depends on
\bm{\varphi}^{\prime}_{m}
as well as
O
. Then, by the mean value theorem there exists
{\bar{\varphi}}_{m}^{\pm}
between
\varphi_{m}
and
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
which depends on
\bm{\varphi}
, so that

(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}(\bm{\varphi})=(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\left(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O|_{{\varphi}_{m}=\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}}\right)^{2}.

(95)

Here the subscript:
{{\varphi}_{m}=\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}}
stands for that the arguments of
\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O
are
(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
, and to make the formula compact we suppress all the arguments on the right-hand side except
\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}
. From Supplementary Eq. (
95
)

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\left(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O|_{{\varphi}_{m}=\bar{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle

(96)

follows immediately.

Then we perform the Fourier expansion of
\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O
with respect to
\varphi_{m}
. Taking into account the general expression of
O
(cf. Supplementary Table
1
) and
C_{1}={\cal O}(1/\sqrt{L})
, we can expand
O
in
C_{1}
for large total system size
L\gg 1
. Keeping the expansion up to the second order (see Supplementary Note
6.1

for explicit calculations for the entanglement entropy) gives

\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O=a_{0}+a_{1}\sin(\varphi_{m})+b_{1}\cos(\varphi_{m})+a_{2}\sin(2\varphi_{m})+b_{2}\cos(2\varphi_{m}),

(97)

where we have made use of Supplementary Eq. (
50
) and the coefficients
a
’s,
b
’s are
\varphi_{m}
independent. We can rewrite Supplementary Eq. (
97
) as

\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O=A_{0}+A_{1}\sin(\varphi_{m}+\phi_{m1})+A_{2}\sin(2\varphi_{m}+\phi_{m2}),

(98)

where
A
’s and
\phi
’s are determined by
a
’s and
b
’s. With the help of this expression it can be readily shown that

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\leq(|A_{0}|+|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)^{2}\leq 3(A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2})

(99)

and

\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}=A_{0}^{2}+{1\over 2}(A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}).

(100)

The Supplementary inequality (
99
) and Supplementary Eq. (
100
) give

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\leq 6\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}.

(101)

Actually the factor
6
can be improved but this is not important. This bound suggests that (with the components
{\varphi}_{n\neq m}
treated as parameters)
(\partial_{{{\varphi}}_{m}}S)^{2}
at given
{\varphi}_{m}
is the same order as its
{\varphi}_{m}
-average, i.e.

(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}=r_{m}(\bm{\varphi})\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2},\quad r_{m}(\bm{\varphi})={\cal O}(1).

(102)

Substituting it into Supplementary Eq. (
96
) we obtain

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle\left((\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\bm{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right)\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\right\rangle,

(103)

where
\bm{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\ldots,\bar{\varphi}^{\pm}_{m},\ldots,\varphi_{N-1})
. Because the first factor characterizes the distance between
\varphi_{m}
and
\varphi_{m}^{\pm}
and the second describes the
\varphi_{m}
average of
(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}
, we assume that they are statistically uncorrelated and obtain

b_{\pm}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\bm{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle\left\langle\int\frac{d\varphi_{m}}{2\pi}(\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O)^{2}\right\rangle.

(104)

Furthermore, by symmetry
\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\bm{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle
must be the same for distinct
m
, at least approximately. As a result, we reduce Supplementary Eq. (
104
) to

b_{\pm}=R^{\pm}\left\langle|\partial_{\bm{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle,

(105)

where

R^{\pm}={1\over N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{m}^{\pm})^{2}r_{m}(\bm{\varphi}_{m}^{\pm*})\right\rangle

(106)

is an overall numerical coefficient. As mentioned above, in the present work owing to the special choice of
b_{\pm}
, which are given by Supplementary Eq. (
76
),
b_{\pm}
are proportional to the variance
{\rm Var}(O)
. As a result,

{\rm Var}(O)\propto\left\langle|\partial_{\bm{\varphi}}O|^{2}\right\rangle,

(107)

where
|\partial_{\bm{\varphi}}O|^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}O(\bm{\varphi}))^{2}
. This formula relates fluctuations of the entanglement probe
O
to the continuity of the
N
-variable function
O({\bm{\varphi}})
defined by Supplementary Eq. (
41
). As such, the mesoscopic fluctuations emergent from entanglement evolution fall into the class of the so-called concentration-of-measure phenomena, where fluctuations of an observable are controlled by its
Lipschitz continuity
. The concentration-of-measure phenomena are a new perspective of probability theory [15], and are first reported for mesoscopic electronic and photonic transport in Ref. [27]. In Fig. 3(a) of the main text, we provide the numerical verification of Supplementary Eq. (
107
) for the entanglement entropy. In Supplementary Fig.
2
(a) we provide further numerical verifications for the
n
-th Rényi entropy
S_{n}
.

VI
Statistics of entanglement entropy fluctuations

In this Supplementary Note, we apply the general theory developed in the last Supplementary Note to study the fluctuations of entanglement entropy. In particular, we would like to address their universalities, e.g. to what extent they depend on system’s macroscopic and microscopic parameters. The studies in this Supplementary Note can be straightforwardly generalized to
S_{n}
, and the results do not change.

VI.1
Two contributions to

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\ln\left(C_{0}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right),

\displaystyle(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}S)_{2}

\displaystyle-\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\left(\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right)C_{0}\right)^{-1}C_{1}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\left(\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right)^{-1}[\ln\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right),C_{1}]+C_{0}^{-1}[\ln C_{0},C_{1}]\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right).

Taking into account Supplementary Eq. (

(114)

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{2}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle const.\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\bigg\langle\bigg[\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\left((\mathbb{I}-C_{0})C_{0}\right)^{-1}C_{1}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)

(115)

\displaystyle+{1\over 4}\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\left(\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right)^{-1}[\ln\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right),\left(\mathbb{I}-C_{0}\right)^{-1}C_{1}]+C_{0}^{-1}[\ln C_{0},C_{0}^{-1}C_{1}]\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)\bigg]^{2}\bigg\rangle,

VI.2
The contribution
\bm{{\rm Var}_{1}(S)}

To calculate Supplementary Eq. (
114

{\rm Tr}_{A}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{L_{A}}\sum_{\sigma}(\cdot)
. That is, we perform that operation in two steps: In the first step, we sum over the sublattice index
\sigma={\bar{A}},{\bar{B}}
; in the second, we sum over the cell index. Now, because the matrix block
(C_{0})_{ij}
decays rapidly with
|i-j|
, we can divide the sum over

into two contributions, one from the bulk of the subsystem A and the other from its edge. The size of the edge,
L_{e}
, is order of the decay length of
C_{0}
, and thus is determined merely by the parameters of the Hamiltonian; while the size of the bulk is
\approx L_{A}

A_{b}
and
A_{e}

A_{b}
are labelled by

and in

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(\ln\left(C_{0}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)=\sum_{i_{b}\in A_{b}}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\ln\left(C_{0}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)_{i_{b}\sigma,i_{b}\sigma}+\sum_{i_{e}\in A_{e}}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\ln\left(C_{0}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)_{i_{e}\sigma,i_{e}\sigma}.

For the bulk term, owing to the rapid decay of

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{b}\in A_{b}}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\ln\left(C_{0}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}\right)\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)_{i_{b}\sigma,i_{b}\sigma}

\displaystyle\frac{L_{A}}{L}\biggl(1-{1\over 2}\,\delta_{m0}\biggr)\textrm{tr}\biggl[-\biggl(\ln(\check{\gamma}_{m}^{-1}-\mathbb{I})\,\check{\alpha}_{m}+(m\rightarrow-m)\biggr)\sin\varphi_{m}+\biggl(\ln(\check{\gamma}_{m}^{-1}-\mathbb{I})\,\check{\beta}_{m}+(m\rightarrow-m)\biggr)\cos\varphi_{m}\biggr],\quad

(or

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle\left[\textrm{Tr}_{A}\left(\left((\mathbb{I}-C_{0})C_{0}\right)^{-1}C_{1}\partial_{\varphi_{m}}C_{1}\right)\right]^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L_{A}^{3}}{16L^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,n\neq 0,\\

where

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\textrm{tr}\Bigl[\kappa(\check{\alpha}_{n}\check{\alpha}_{m}+\check{\alpha}_{-n}\check{\alpha}_{-m})\Bigr]\right)^{2}+\left(\textrm{tr}\Bigl[\kappa(\check{\alpha}_{n}\check{\alpha}_{-m}+\check{\alpha}_{-n}\check{\alpha}_{m})\Bigr]\right)^{2},

Interestingly, the scaling behavior

coincides with a previous result about the entanglement entropy variance in a completely different context [29]. In that context fluctuations are due to randomly drawing a member from a free-fermion eigenstate ensemble; thus that result is kinematic and has nothing to do with the dynamics of entanglement.

VI.4
Universal scaling law for
\bm{{\rm Var}(S)}

Combining Supplementary Eqs. (
138
) and (
149
), we obtain the variance of the entanglement entropy,

{\rm Var}(S)=aL^{-1}+bL_{A}^{3}/L^{2}.

(150)

Importantly, from the derivations above we have seen that the first and second term describe a subsystem’s edge and bulk effect, respectively. Upon the rescaling:
\tilde{L}\equiv L/\ell,\,\tilde{L}_{A}\equiv L_{A}/\ell
(
\ell\equiv\sqrt{a/b}
), Supplementary Eq. (
150
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle{{\rm Var}(S)/s_{0}}=1/\tilde{L}+\tilde{L}_{A}^{3}/\tilde{L}^{2}

(151)

with
s_{0}\equiv\sqrt{ab}
, which is Eq. (2) in the main text (where we used the same symbols
L,\,L_{A}
for notational simplicity). This result is universal with respect to system’s detailed constructions and initial states (required to be Gaussian, however), that enter only into the microscopic parameters
\ell
and
s_{0}
. It is thus suggested that the behaviors of entanglement entropy fluctuations are completely controlled by two dimensionless macroscopic lengths,
\tilde{L}
and
\tilde{L}_{A}
.

Supplementary Eq. (
151
) implies that in the regime of
\tilde{L}_{A}\ll\tilde{L}^{1/3}
, the edge term dominates over the bulk term and
{\rm Var}(S)\sim\tilde{L}^{-1}
. Thus in this regime the fluctuations are very weak. In the special case of
\tilde{L}\rightarrow\infty
at fixed
\tilde{L}_{A}
, the variance vanishes. Combined with the fact that
S(t)
oscillates quasiperiodically, this result implies that fluctuations are fully suppressed and the entanglement entropy is strictly a constant beyond some critical time, in agreement with a celebrated result in entanglement evolution [1, 3]; see

Supplementary Appendix H
for details. In the regime of
\tilde{L}_{A}\gg\tilde{L}^{1/3}
the fluctuation behaviors are totally opposite. In this case the bulk term dominates over the edge term and
{\rm Var}(S)\sim\tilde{L}_{A}^{3}/\tilde{L}^{2}
. So when the ratio
\tilde{L}_{A}/\tilde{L}=L_{A}/L
is fixed, the variance increases linearly with
\tilde{L}
, and thus enlarging the entire system can drive very strong entanglement fluctuations. This is contrary to the belief [30] that temporal fluctuations observed in experiments on entanglement evolution of finite systems would eventually diminish by increasing the system size. Thus, we see that the behaviors of out-of-equilibrium entanglement entropy fluctuations can be completely different in approaching the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
, depending on how
L_{A}
scales with
L
.

The situations above resemble those in the conductance fluctuations of quasi-one-dimensional disordered wires in several aspects. First, the conductance fluctuations are also controlled by some dimensionless macroscopic parameter, namely, the sample length rescaled by the localization length, and the microscopic details of the wire, notably, the disorder strength, enter only into the localization length. Second, varying the rescaled sample length leads to distinct fluctuation behaviors: Increasing the sample length drives the wire from a metallic regime, where the universal conductance fluctuations [5, 6] follow, to a localized regime, where the conductance distribution is very broad so that the variance of logarithmic conductance increases with the sample length linearly [10].

The tail behaviors described above can be understood in a simple picture based on the concept of the coherent entangled quasiparticle pair [1] as follows. Upon quench such pairs are locally created across the whole system. Because they have opposite group velocities and, moreover the system is finite, so that a quasiparticle reenters into the system after reaching system’s boundary, after long time a stationary configuration of entangled pairs is form. In this configuration the pairs are randomly distributed in the system subjected to Pauli’s exclusion principle. Let
\delta
be the probability for a quasiparticle inside subsystem
A
to pair with
another outside — an event contributing to the entanglement entropy
S
, and
(1-\delta)
be the probability for the exclusive event, namely, an entangled pair staying inside the subsystem A. Then
S
is given by the total ‘successful’ events for a fixed, but large, number of trials, resulting in a binomial distribution. Now, for
L_{A}/L=1/2
an entangled pair has equally the same probability to cross subsystem’s boundary or stay inside A, i.e.
\delta=1/2
; it is well known that in this case the binomial distribution reduces to a Gaussian distribution. For small
L_{A}/L
,
\delta\approx 1
and thus for an entangled pair to stay in A is a rare event. This results in a heavier lower tail of the
S
distribution which is exponential, in the same fashion as the binomial distribution approaches its Poissonian limit. So this picture well explains the asymptotic behaviors of the tail bounds and their evolution with
L_{A}/L
. This suggests that the bounds yield the form of large deviation probability for the upper and the lower tail, respectively. That is, the Supplementary inequalities (
89
) and (
93
) can be promoted to equalities, i.e. for sufficiently large
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(|S-\langle S\rangle|\geq\epsilon)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2\mathfrak{b}_{+}}},&{\rm for}\,S-\langle S\rangle>0\\
{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon^{2}\over 2(\mathfrak{b}_{-}+\mathfrak{c}\epsilon)}},&{\rm for}\,S-\langle S\rangle<0\end{array}\right.,

with the parameters
\mathfrak{b}_{\pm}\propto b_{\pm}
and
\mathfrak{c}\propto|c_{-}|
and the proportionality coefficients being absolute constants. This gives Eq. (1) in the main text, which was confirmed by simulations (see Fig. 2(c) in the main text). In Supplementary Note
7.2

we provide further simulation results to show that Supplementary Eq. (
VI.5
) holds also for the second-order R
\acute{\rm e}
nyi entropy
S_{2}
.

Let us further discuss semi-quantitatively how the rate of the exponential decay
\sim{\rm e}^{-{\epsilon\over 2\mathbf{c}}}\,(\epsilon\gg\mathfrak{b}_{-}/\mathfrak{c})
of the lower tail behaves, when
L
approaches the infinite and the ratio
L_{A}/L
is fixed. First of all, Supplementary Eq. (
150
) implies that
{\rm Var}(S)\sim L
for sufficiently large
L
and fixed
L_{A}/L
. Then, expanding
dG/du
in

we obtain
dG/du=u{\rm Var}(S)+{\cal O}(u^{2})
. So the coefficient of the leading term is
\propto L
, and thus the leading expansion of
L^{-1}dG/du
is well defined in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
. It is natural to expect that the entire expansion of
L^{-1}dG/du
is well defined in such limit, although this is difficult to prove. This implies that all the coefficients of higher order terms cannot grow faster than
L
. Next, according to Supplementary Eq. (
80
),
\delta F(u)
is a sum of
N\sim L
terms. Thus we estimate
\delta F(u)\sim L
. Finally, combining the estimations for
dG/du
and
\delta F(u)
with the Supplementary inequality (
92
), we find that
\mathfrak{c}={\cal O}(L^{0})
, i.e. depends only on the ratio
L_{A}/L
. This is in agreement with the data in Supplementary Table
2
.

Entanglement evolution with commensurate

So far we have studied the dynamics of entanglement where the frequencies governing the evolution of the correlation matrix

\bm{\omega}

14

is periodic in time. Thus all entanglement probes defined by Supplementary Eq. (
32

Since it is impossible to tune parameters of the physical Rice-Mele model to obtain an energy spectrum with commensurate frequencies, we modify directly the frequencies in the dynamical phases. Specifically, at the Bloch momentum
k_{m}
the frequency
\omega_{m}
is set to
\omega_{0}+m\,r

r
being a suitably chosen rational number and
\omega_{0}
being the zero-momentum eigenfrequency in the incommensurate case. In this way, the resulting spectrum simulates as closely as the physical model (see inset of Supplementary Fig.
4
(a)). We assume the eigenstate properties are not modified in any qualitative way.

In Supplementary Fig.
4
(a), the result for the entanglement entropy evolution
S(t)
confirms that it is periodic and displays complete revival to the initial value
S(0)
at multiple periods (yellow curve). The time profile is completely different from the incommensurate case (blue curve), although in both cases the system has the same degrees of freedom.

We also show the evolving spectrum of
C(t)
in Supplementary Figs.
4
(b) and
4
(c), which clearly show a high degree of regularity. Finally, this regularity results in a very different nearest-neighbor level statistics shown in Supplementary Fig.
4
(d), in stark contrast to the incommensurate case giving the semi-Poissonian statistics shown in Fig. 1(c) of the main text.

IX
Generalization to XXZ model

In this Supplementary Note we generalize the analytical theory developed above to an important interacting system, namely, the antiferromagnetic XXZ spin-
{1\over 2}
chain. Furthermore, we perform numerical experiments to test analytical predictions obtained from the generalized theory. An XXZ model of size
L
is described by the following Hamiltonian [36],

\displaystyle{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}H_{i},\quad H_{i}={S}_{i}^{x}{S}_{i+1}^{x}+{S}_{i}^{y}{S}_{i+1}^{y}+\Delta\left({S}_{i}^{z}{S}_{i+1}^{z}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(155)

where
S_{i}^{x,y,z}={1\over 2}\sigma_{i}^{x,y,z}
are spin-
{1\over 2}
operators, with
\sigma^{x,y,z}_{i}
being the Pauli matrices at site
i
, and
\Delta>0
is the anisotropy parameter. When
\Delta=1
this system reduces to the isotropic Heisenberg chain (namely, the XXX model), and a
SU(2)
symmetry arises [37]. But it plays no roles in our work and thus we shall not discuss it further. Below we focus on small but not vanishing
\Delta
, and on even
L\gg 1
and even
L_{A}
with
L_{A}/L\ll 1
. The system is subjected to the periodic boundary condition
S_{L+1}^{\alpha}=S_{1}^{\alpha}
.

The generalized theory differs from the theory developed in previous Supplementary Notes in two key aspects. First, the correlation function plays no roles; rather, the generalized theory is built upon the reduced density matrix. Secondly, it makes no reference to whether the initial state is Gaussian or not. We can further generalize the theory below to other interacting models, such as the XXZ model with an integrability-breaking term and the antiferromagnetic chain with next-to-nearest neighbor interaction [38]. But we shall not discuss this here for simplicity.

IX.1
Emergence of mesoscopic fluctuations

IX.1.1
Reduced density matrix and entanglement dynamics

Let the eigenenergy spectrum of
{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}
be
\{\omega_{m}\}
and the eigenstates be
\{\Psi_{m}\}
. A generic initial state
\Psi(0)
is superposed by
\Psi_{m}
’s,

\displaystyle\Psi(0)=\sum_{m}\chi_{m}\Psi_{m},

(156)

where
\chi_{m}
’s are superposition coefficients. At later time
t
the wavefunction is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=\sum_{m}\chi_{m}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{m}t}\Psi_{m}.

(157)

At this state the density matrix reads

\displaystyle{\rho}(t)=\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi(t)\right|=\sum_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{-i(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})t}\,\chi_{m}\chi_{n}^{\ast}\left|\Psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{n}\right|,

(158)

and the reduced density matrix reads

\displaystyle{\rho}_{A}(t)=\sum_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{-i(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})t}\,\chi_{m}\chi_{n}^{\ast}{\rm Tr}_{B}\left|\Psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{n}\right|.

(159)

The evolution of
{\rho}_{A}(t)
provides full information on entanglement evolution. Notably, it gives the evolution of the entanglement entropy
S(t)
and the
n
-th order R
\acute{\rm e}
nyi entropy
S_{n}(t)
defined by Supplementary Eqs. (
22
) and (
33
), respectively.

To study the evolution Supplementary Eq. (
159
) it is necessary to understand the number of eigenbases excited by
\Psi(0)
, denoted as
D
. This number may be estimated as the participation ratio in the eigenstate space,

\displaystyle D\approx\left(\sum_{m}\left|\chi_{m}\right|^{4}\right)^{-1}.

(160)

It is much smaller than the dimension of full Hilbert space, i.e.
2^{L}=\mathrm{e}^{\ln 2\,L}\approx\mathrm{e}^{0.7L}
, in general. But similar to the latter,
D
increases exponentially with
L
, i.e.

\displaystyle D\sim\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{D}L},\quad\kappa_{D}>0.

(161)

To see this let us first consider a random
\Psi(0)
without polarization (i.e. the total magnetization
S^{z}=\langle\Psi(0)|\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}S_{i}^{z}|\Psi(0)\rangle=0
); see Supplementary Note
9.5.1

below for its detailed constructions. So,

\langle\Psi(0)|H_{\mathrm{xxz}}|\Psi(0)\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\langle\Psi(0)|H_{i}|\Psi(0)\rangle.

(162)

Because
\Psi(0)
is random
\langle\Psi(0)|H_{i}|\Psi(0)\rangle
(
i=0,\cdots,L-1
) are random numbers with nonvanishing mean, implying that
\langle\Psi(0)|H_{\rm xxz}|\Psi(0)\rangle
is the sum of
L
independent random number. Since each random number is
{\cal O}(L^{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi(0)|H_{\rm xxz}^{2}|\Psi(0)\rangle-\langle\Psi(0)|H_{\rm xxz}|\Psi(0)\rangle^{2}={\cal O}(L)

(163)

for
L\gg 1
. This scaling also holds, when
\Psi(0)
is a Neel state

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Neel}\right\rangle\equiv\left|1010\cdots 10\right\rangle,

(164)

with the number
1\,(0)
denoting an up (down) spin at a lattice site. More precisely, one may readily show that

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Neel}|{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{xxz}}|\mathrm{Neel}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{Neel}|{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}|\mathrm{Neel}\rangle^{2}=\frac{L}{4}.

(165)

On one hand,
Supplementary Eqs. (
163
) and (
165
) show that the width of the energy window excited by
\Psi(0)
increases with
L
as
\sim\sqrt{L}
. On the other hand, the dimension of Hilbert space grows exponentially with
L
. Moreover, since the spectrum
\{\omega_{m}\}
is bounded from both above and below, with the bounds growing as
\sim L
, the typical level spacing must decrease exponentially with
L
. Since the exponential decreasing of the level spacing dominates over the linear growth of the width of the energy window, we have Supplementary Eq. (
161
).

IX.1.2
Relating entanglement dynamics to classical trajectory on
\mathbb{T}^{N}

Supplementary Eq. (
161
) shows that the number of frequency mismatches
\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}
in Supplementary Eq. (
158
), where
m,n
run from
1
to
D
, is
D(D-1)/2\sim\mathrm{e}^{2\kappa_{D}L}
. Noting that

\displaystyle\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}=\omega_{m1}-\omega_{n1},\quad\omega_{m1}\equiv\omega_{m}-\omega_{1},

(166)

we find that all
D(D-1)/2
frequency mismatches are completely determined by a much smaller set of frequency mismatches,
\{\omega_{m1}\}_{m=2}^{D}
, with a number (for sufficiently large but finite
L
)

N=D-1\sim\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{D}L}

(167)

much smaller than
D(D-1)/2
. Identifying this set, we see that similar to the free fermion case, the time parameter in the reduced density matrix enters through the
N
dynamical phases
\bm{\omega}t
, with

\bm{\omega}\equiv(\omega_{21},\cdots,\omega_{D1}).

(168)

Now we can generalize Supplementary Eqs. (
15
)-(
18
). Specifically, we introduce a (matrix-valued) function on the
N
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}\ni\bm{\varphi}\equiv(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\cdots,\varphi_{N-1})
:

\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})=\rho_{A0}+\tilde{\rho}_{A1}(\bm{\varphi}),

(169)

where

\displaystyle{\rho}_{A0}=\sum_{m}|\chi_{m}|^{2}\,{\rm Tr}_{B}\left|\Psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{m}\right|

(170)

has no
\bm{\varphi}
dependence, with B being the complement of subsystem A, and

\tilde{\rho}_{A1}(\bm{\varphi})=\sum_{m\neq n}{\rm e}^{-i(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{n})}\chi_{m}\chi_{n}^{*}\,\text{Tr}_{B}|\Psi_{m}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|

(171)

has. Then we obtain the instantaneous reduced density matrix Supplementary Eq. (
159
) from
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
via

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(t)=\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\omega}t),

(172)

which is the interacting version of Supplementary Eq. (
29
). By definition
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
is periodic in each argument
\varphi_{m}
. Thus the
N
phases
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
in
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
completely determine
\rho_{A}(t)
and entail a classical motion — a rotation in
\mathbb{T}^{N}
with constant angular velocity
\bm{\omega}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(t)\leftrightsquigarrow\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t\in\mathbb{T}^{N}.

(173)

This is in spirit fully parallel to Supplementary Eq. (
18
) in the free fermion case, shown schematically in Fig. 1(d) in the main text. That is, the evolution of the reduced density matrix corresponds to a classical trajectory on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
.

Now a generic information-theoretic observables
O(t)
is a functional of
\rho_{A}(t)
,

O(t)\equiv O[\rho_{A}(t)],

(174)

in contrast to Supplementary Eq. (
32
). Owing to the relation Supplementary Eq. (
172
),
O(t)
must depend on
t
via
\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t
. That is, with the introduction of the
N
-variable function:

O(\bm{\varphi})\equiv O[\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})],

(175)

e.g. for the entanglement entropy:

S(\bm{\varphi})=-\text{Tr}_{A}(\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})\ln\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})),

(176)

and for the
n
-th order R
\acute{\rm e}
nyi entropy:

S_{n}(\bm{\varphi})=-{1\over n-1}\ln\text{Tr}_{A}(\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi}))^{n},

(177)

the relation Supplementary Eq. (
35
) follows again, which we rewrite here,

\displaystyle O(t)=O(\bm{\varphi})|_{\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t}

(178)

for the convenience of readers, although they have completely different meanings. We remark that throughout this Supplementary Note the partial trace
\text{Tr}_{A}
is over the subsystem Hilbert space, while in previous Supplementary Notes, namely, Supplementary Eqs. (
24
), (
38
), (
40
) and (
41
) and ensuing equations, it is over the subsystem lattice.

Observing Supplementary Eqs. (
176
) and (
177
), we find that all information-theoretic observables
O(\bm{\varphi})
can be related to the same quantity, the spectral density of
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle O(\bm{\varphi})=\int d\lambda\,\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)\,\textrm{Tr}_{A}\,\delta\left(\lambda-\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})\right),

(179)

where
\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)=-\lambda\ln\lambda
for
O=S
and
\mathfrak{O}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n}
for
O={\rm e}^{-(n-1)S_{n}}
. This expression is an analog of Supplementary Eq. (
41
).

IX.1.3
Emergence of disordered samples and mesoscopic fluctuations

In general, the
N
frequencies Supplementary Eq. (
168
) are incommensurate. In combination with the relation Supplementary Eq. (
178
), this implies that at long time an entanglement probe
O(t)
display quasiperiodic oscillations, like in the free fermion case. These quasiperiodic oscillations are conceptually different from the oscillations of conventional [39] and unconventional [40] entanglement probes in interacting models, which arise from the traversal of quasiparticle pairs or the incomplete revival of system’s wavefunction, and rapidly damp in the course of time. Most importantly, the statistical equivalence established in
III
applies here also. That is, out-of-equilibrium fluctuations displayed by the quasiperiodic oscillations of
O(t)
must have the same statistics as fluctuations of
O(\bm{\varphi})
with
\bm{\varphi}
, provided
\bm{\varphi}
is drawn randomly from the uniform probability measure
\mathbf{P}
on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
.

However, for the XXZ model each virtual disordered sample is represented by a random matrix
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
, namely, Supplementary Eq. (
169
), instead of
\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi})
for free fermion models. This matrix has two parts: the nonrandom
{\rho}_{A0}
defined by Supplementary Eq. (
170
), and the random
\tilde{\rho}_{A1}(\bm{\varphi})
defined by Supplementary Eq. (
171
). The latter depends on the disorder realization,
\bm{\varphi}
, uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{T}^{N}
. The entanglement properties of a disordered sample are characterized by various information-theoretic observables:
S(\bm{\varphi})
,
S_{n}(\bm{\varphi})
, etc., which by Supplementary Eq. (
175
) are functionals of
\tilde{\rho}_{A}(\bm{\varphi})
. So the
\bm{\varphi}
fluctuations of an information-theoretic observable represent a new class of mesoscopic sample-to-sample fluctuations also, although their detailed mathematical description is very different from that for free fermion models summarized in Supplementary Note
4.3
.

To understand the scaling behaviors of the disorder strength we first estimate the number of pairs
(m,n)
, denoted as
{\cal W}
, dominate the double sum in Supplementary Eq. (
171
). Is it
\sim D^{2}
, as naively expected from that
m,n
run from
1
to
D
? Consider an arbitrary local observable
{O}
in the subsystem A. The time-dependent part of its expectation
\langle\Psi(t)|{O}|\Psi(t)\rangle
is

\displaystyle\sum_{m\neq n}\mathrm{e}^{-i(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})t}\chi_{m}\chi_{n}^{\ast}\langle\Psi_{n}|{O}|\Psi_{m}\rangle={\rm Tr}_{A}\left({O}{\rho}_{A1}(t)\right).

(180)

From this we see that
{\cal W}
is the number of pairs
(m,n)
dominating the sum on the left-hand side of Supplementary Eq. (
180
). Thus we turn to study the off-diagonal element
\langle\Psi_{n}|{O}|\Psi_{m}\rangle
(
m\neq n
). Because of

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{n}|[O,{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}]|\Psi_{m}\rangle=(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})\langle\Psi_{n}|O|\Psi_{m}\rangle,

(181)

we have

\displaystyle\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}=\frac{\langle\Psi_{n}|[O,H_{\mathrm{xxz}}]|\Psi_{m}\rangle}{\langle\Psi_{n}|O|\Psi_{m}\rangle}.

(182)

A key point is that
{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}
is additive and, as a result,

\displaystyle[{O},{H}_{\mathrm{xxz}}]=[{O},\sum_{i=0}^{L_{A}-1}{H}_{i}],

(183)

where
\sum_{i=0}^{L_{A}-1}{H}_{i}
may be considered as the XXZ chain restricted to A. Substituting it into Supplementary Eq. (
182
), we find that

\displaystyle\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{L_{A}-1}\frac{\langle\Psi_{n}|[O,H_{i}]|\Psi_{m}\rangle}{\langle\Psi_{n}|O|\Psi_{m}\rangle}.

(184)

The right-hand side cannot grow faster than
\sim L_{A}
. So

\displaystyle|\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}|\lesssim L_{A},

(185)

i.e.
|\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}|
must be smaller or comparable to
L_{A}
. This gives a window
\sim L_{A}
of the eigenenergy mismatch, which
is much smaller than the width of the excited energy window
\sim\sqrt{L}
(for
L_{A}\ll L
) given by Supplementary Eq. (
163
) or Supplementary Eq. (
165
). Then the question is given
m
how many
n
there are, such that not only is the condition Supplementary Eq. (
185
) satisfied, but also
\langle\Psi_{n}|O|\Psi_{m}\rangle
takes a significant value and thus can dominate the sum in the left-hand side of Supplementary Eq. (
180
). These two conditions essentially define a sub-spectrum of
H_{\rm xxz}
. Let the typical spacing of two nearest eigenvalues in this sub-spectrum be
s^{*}
. The desired number of
n
can be estimated as
L_{A}/s^{*}
, and

\displaystyle{\cal W}\sim DL_{A}/s^{*},

(186)

where the first factor
D
accounts for that the index
m
runs from
1
to
D
.

We proceed to calculate
s^{*}
for fixed
m
. Let us write an unpolarized eigenstate
|\Psi_{m}\rangle
as

\displaystyle|\Psi_{m}\rangle=\sum_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\,{t}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{m}|{\mathfrak{m}}\rangle,

(187)

with the sum over all the product basis vectors
|{\mathfrak{m}}\rangle\equiv|s_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{L-1}\rangle
(
s_{i}=0,1
) in
\mathscr{H}_{{S^{z}=0}}
. Here each
|{\mathfrak{m}}\rangle
is composed of
L/2
symbols ‘
0
’ (down spins) and
L/2
symbols ‘
1
’ (up spins). The complex expansion coefficients
t_{\mathfrak{m}}^{m}
are constrained by the normalization condition:

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\,|{t}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{m}|^{2}=1.

(188)

This defines a (
2D_{s}-1
)-dimensional hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{2D_{s}-1}
, where

D_{s}=\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L\\
L/2\\
\end{array}\Big)={L!\over((L/2)!)^{2}}

(189)

is the dimension of
\mathscr{H}_{{S^{z}=0}}
. Let

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{n}|=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}}t_{\mathfrak{n}}^{n\ast}\langle\mathfrak{n}|.

(190)

We substitute Supplementary Eqs. (
187
) and (
190
) into the left-hand side of Supplementary Eq. (
180
). Since
\langle\Psi_{n}|O|\Psi_{m}\rangle
takes a significant value, there must exist a large number of
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{n}
, such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{n}|O|\mathfrak{m}\rangle\neq 0.

(191)

Because
O
is a local observable in the subsytem A and
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{n}
are product states, this can be satisfied only if
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{n}
differ only in their spin configurations in A. Thus the spin configuration of
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{n}
are almost the same, that can also be readily seen from the definition Supplementary Eq. (
171
) of
\tilde{\rho}_{A1}(\bm{\varphi})
. This is a strong condition. Thanks to it
s^{*}
must be much larger the level spacing of eigenenergies excited by
\Psi(0)
, which is
\sim D^{-1}
. Furthermore, similar to the latter
s^{*}
must decrease exponentially with
L
. Thus
s^{*}\sim D^{-\nu}
for fixed
L_{A}
, with
0\leq\nu<1
. How does
s^{*}
scale with
L_{A}
, when
L
is fixed? Note that two nearest eigenstates in the subspectrum may be related by the motion of a quasiparticle [37] in A. The volume available for such a particle is
\sim L_{A}^{\delta}
, with
0\leq\delta\leq 1
, depending on
\Psi(0)
and the parameter
\Delta
. Thus
s^{*}\sim L_{A}^{-\delta}
for fixed
L
. Taking all these into account, we have the estimation:

s^{*}\sim L_{A}^{-\delta}D^{-\nu},

(192)

giving

L_{A}/s^{*}\sim L_{A}^{\mu}D^{\nu},\quad\mu=1+\delta.

(193)

Substituting it into Supplementary Eq. (
186
) we have

\displaystyle{\cal W}\sim L_{A}^{\mu}D^{1+\nu},\quad 1\leq\mu\leq 2.

(194)

So the increase of
{\cal W}
with
D
is slower than
\sim D^{2}
. By definition the power
\mu
depends on both
\Delta
and
\Psi(0)
.

IX.2
Statistical distribution of mesoscopic fluctuations

With the random structure described above uncovered, we can also use the modified logarithmic Sobolov inequality namely Supplementary (
74
) to study the mesoscopic fluctuations of entanglement probes
O(\bm{\varphi})=S(\bm{\varphi}),\,S_{n}(\bm{\varphi})
, etc., since (
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{N-1}
) are independent and identically distributed random variables, and the entanglement probe
O(\bm{\varphi})
may be regarded as a — highly nonlinear — function of these
N
angular variables. Specially, following the analysis in Supplementary Note
5.1
,
for generic
O
we still have the concentration inequalities namely Supplementary (
89
) and (
91
) for the upper tail and Supplementary (
93
) and (
94
) for the lower. The difference is that in Supplementary Eq. (
76
), which we rewrite here:

\displaystyle b_{\pm}\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left\langle(O-O_{m}^{\pm})^{2}\right\rangle,

(197)

(recall that the average is over the torus
\mathbb{T}^{N}
with uniform probability measure
\bf P
.)
the dimension
N
of the torus increases with
L
exponentially for sufficiently large but finite
L
(cf. Supplementary Eq. (
167
)), whereas in Supplementary Eq. (
76
)
N
increases with
L
linearly (cf. Supplementary Eq. (
12
)). As we shall see below, this difference is responsible for the arising of completely different scaling behaviors of mesoscopic fluctuations.

For
O=S
,
S_{n}
, on general grounds we expect that the sign of
c_{\pm}
in the concentration inequalities stay the same as that in free fermion models, i.e.
c_{\pm}<0
for both upper and lower tails. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(O-\langle O\rangle\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2b_{+}}}}

(198)

for the upper tail and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\langle O\rangle-O\geq\epsilon)\leq{\rm e}^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(b_{-}+|c_{-}|\epsilon)}}

(199)

for the lower. Similar to the situations in the free fermion case, simulations further show (see Fig. 5c in the main text and Supplementary Note
9.5

below) that for large
\epsilon
the exact deviation probability for upward fluctuations agrees with the form given by the right-hand side of Supplementary (
198
) and for downward by the right-hand side of Supplementary (
199
), with
b_{\pm}
and
c_{-}
as fitting parameters. So we receive Eq. (2) in the main text for
S
,
S_{n}
.

IX.3
A general result for the variance of mesoscopic fluctuations

Next, we generalize the method developed in Supplementary Note
5.2

to study the variance of a generic entanglement probe
O
. From Supplementary Eq. (
197
) we also arrive at Supplementary Eq. (
96
).
To calculate the latter, we substitute Supplementary Eq. (
169
) into Supplementary Eq. (
176
) or Supplementary Eq. (
177
), and perform the derivative
\partial_{\varphi_{m}}O
for
O=S,\,S_{n}
. In doing so, we encounter an essential technical difference intrinsic to the random structure associated with Supplementary Eq. (
171
). That is, when we perform the Fourier expansion of
\partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O
with respect to
\varphi_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{{\varphi}_{m}}O&=a_{0}+\sum_{n\neq m}\left(a_{1n}\sin(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{n})+b_{1n}\cos(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{n})\right)\\
&=a_{0}+\sum_{n\neq m}A_{1n}\sin(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{n}+\phi_{mn1})\end{aligned}

(200)

instead of Supplementary Eq. (
97
). Here
a_{1n}
and
b_{1n}
are coefficients independent of
\varphi_{m}
,
\varphi_{n}
, and
A_{1n}
and
\phi_{mn1}
are determined by
a_{1n}
,
b_{1n}
and can be easily found. The explicit form of
a_{1n}
,
b_{1n}
,
A_{1n}
and
\phi_{mn1}
are not needed for subsequent discussions. For simplicity we have kept the expansion up to the first-order harmonic, since the disorder strength is exponentially small for large

IX.4
Universal behaviors of variances

We are now ready to study the mesoscopic universality exhibited by the variance of various information-theoretic observables.

IX.4.1
The nonrandom part of the reduced density matrix

For
D_{s}\gg 1
(recall Supplementary Eq. (
189

\acute{\rm e}

\mathbb{S}^{2D_{s}-1}
displays strong concentration [16, 17] for

{\rm Tr}_{B}|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|

D_{s}^{-1}{\rm Tr}_{B}\,\mathbb{I}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the unity matrix in
\mathscr{H}_{{S^{z}=0}}
, provided the subsystem complement
B
is sufficiently large, i.e.
L_{A}/L\ll 1
. Let
\Phi
be the eigenstate
\Psi_{m}
Supplementary Eq. (
187

187
) satisfy this property, i.e. are
typical
with respect to the Haar measure: This expectation is indeed confirmed numerically by computing the trace distance [42] between
{\rm Tr}_{B}|\Psi_{m}\rangle\langle\Psi_{m}|
and
D_{s}^{-1}{\rm Tr}_{B}\,\mathbb{I}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{B}\left|\Psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{m}\right|\approx D_{s}^{-1}{\rm Tr}_{B}\,\mathbb{I}.

(207)

Now, if the following condition:

(
*
) Most weight
|\chi_{m}|^{2}
in the initial state
\Psi(0)
goes to typical eigenstates.

is postulated, we can substitute Supplementary Eq. (
207
) into Supplementary Eq. (
170
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\rho}_{A0}\approx D_{s}^{-1}{\rm Tr}_{B}\,\mathbb{I}.

(208)

It implies that
{\rho}_{A0}
is diagonal.

The remainder is to calculate the diagonal element
({\rho}_{A0})_{II}
. This is essentially the number of bases
|{\mathfrak{m}}\rangle
, whose configuration in A, i.e.
s_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{L_{A}-1}
is
I
. Let the magnetization of A be
S_{A}^{z}
. Because
L_{A}
is an even number,
S_{A}^{z}
must be an integer. In addition,
|S_{A}^{z}|\leq L_{A}/2
. The number of up- and down-spins in

are given respectively by

\displaystyle L_{A}^{\uparrow}=\frac{2S_{A}^{z}+L_{A}}{2},\quad L_{A}^{\downarrow}=\frac{-2S_{A}^{z}+L_{A}}{2}.

(209)

So the number of up- and down-spins in the complement

are, respectively,

\displaystyle L_{B}^{\uparrow}=\frac{L}{2}-L_{A}^{\uparrow}=\frac{L-L_{A}-2S_{A}^{z}}{2},\quad L_{B}^{\downarrow}=\frac{L}{2}-L_{A}^{\downarrow}=\frac{L-L_{A}+2S_{A}^{z}}{2}.

(210)

Thus

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} ({\rho}_{A0})_{II}&={1\over D_{s}}\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L_{B}^{\uparrow}\\
L-L_{A}\\
\end{array}\Big)={1\over D_{s}}\frac{(L-L_{A})!}{\left((L-L_{A}+2S_{A}^{z})/2\right)!\left((L-L_{A}-2S_{A}^{z})/2\right)!}\\
&=\frac{((L-L_{A})/2)\left((L-L_{A})/2-1\right)\cdots\left((L-L_{A})/2-(|S_{A}^{z}|-1)\right)}{\left((L-L_{A})/2+|S_{A}^{z}|\right)\left((L-L_{A})/2+(|S_{A}^{z}|-1)\right)\cdots\left((L-L_{A})/2+1\right)}{\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L-L_{A}\\
(L-L_{A})/2\\
\end{array}\Big)\over D_{s}}\\
&=\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+|S_{A}^{z}|}\right)\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+(|S_{A}^{z}|-1)}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+1}\right)\,{\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L-L_{A}\\
(L-L_{A})/2\\
\end{array}\Big)\over D_{s}}.\end{aligned}

(211)

Note that
{\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L-L_{A}\\
(L-L_{A})/2\\
\end{array}\Big)/D_{s}}\equiv{\rho}_{A0}^{*}
gives the diagonal element
({\rho}_{A0})_{II}
with the configuration

satisfying
S_{A}^{z}=0
. According to Supplementary Eq. (
211

({\rho}_{A0})_{II}
depends on three parameters, i.e.

and
S_{A}^{z}
only. For
L\gg L_{A}
,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+|S_{A}^{z}|}\right)\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+(|S_{A}^{z}|-1)}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{|S_{A}^{z}|}{(L-L_{A})/2+1}\right)\approx\left(1-\frac{2|S_{A}^{z}|}{L}\right)^{|S_{A}^{z}|},

(212)

and Supplementary Eq. (

\displaystyle({\rho}_{A0})_{II}/{\rho}_{A0}^{*}\approx\left(1-\frac{2|S_{A}^{z}|}{L}\right)^{|S_{A}^{z}|}.

(213)

Because of

. So Supplementary Eq. (

{\rho}_{A0}^{*}

\displaystyle{\rho}_{A0}={\rho}_{A0}^{*}\,\mathbb{I},

(214)

with

To determine

\displaystyle D_{A}{\rho}_{A0}^{*}=1\,\Rightarrow\,D_{A}={D_{s}\over\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}L-L_{A}\\
(L-L_{A})/2\\

\displaystyle{\rho}_{A0}={\mathbb{I}/D_{A}},

Consider the entanglement entropy

We proceed to study Supplementary Eq. (

where

satisfying Supplementary Eq. (
185

IX.5.1
More details of numerical methods

Because long-time simulations of entanglement dynamics are not well controlled within the framework of density matrix renormalization group, we perform the exact diagonalization of the XXZ model with the use of standard numerical methods [41]. This allows us to find the eigenvalue spectrum
\{\omega_{m}\}
and the eigenstates
\{\Psi_{m}\}

in Supplementary Eq. (
156

where the complex coefficients

Supplementary Appendix A
Entanglement evolution in transverse field Ising chain

The TFIC Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{i+1}^{x}+h\sigma_{i}^{z}),

(233)

with the periodic boundary condition
\sigma_{L+1}^{\alpha}=\sigma_{1}^{\alpha}
, and the Pauli matrices
\sigma^{x,z}_{i}
act on the spin at site
i
belonging to a spin chain of length
L
.
h
is the external magnetic field. We also carried a parallel study of the entanglement entropy evolution of TFIC with the use of the numerical solution of the correlation matrix. The method of finding that solution is described in standard textbooks and we simply use analytical expressions given in Ref.
Cardy05
. The key formulae for the numerical computations are Eqs. (3.3) – (3.6) in that work. The expressions for finite
L
simply amount to converting the continuous momentum variable
\varphi
in those formulae back to a discrete one. That is, for a generic expression
F(\varphi)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\,F(\varphi)\,\,\,\rightarrow\,\,\,\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=-L/2}^{L/2-1}\,F\left(\left(n+{1\over 2}\right){2\pi\over L}\right).

(234)

Note that on the right-hand side the Bloch momenta are quantized at half-integers. This arises from the antiperiodic boundary condition for the Jordan-Wigner fermions in the even fermion number sector. For studies of the time-dependent correlation matrix, we stay in the paramagnetic phase with
h>1
when quenching the magnetic field. Moreover, the pre-quench state is the ground state of the pre-quench Hamiltonian.

(238)

The energy spectrum of the Bloch Hamiltonian Supplementary Eq. (

\displaystyle\pm E_{k}=\pm|\bm{B}(k)|=\pm\sqrt{M^{2}+J^{2}+J^{\prime 2}+2JJ^{\prime}\cos k}.

(239)

The

That is, the spectrum bears the reflection symmetry. The eigenstates are

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u^{-}_{k}\\

{\rm e}^{i\phi_{k}}\sin\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\\

(251)

With the help of

) and (

\displaystyle a_{k}=(u^{-*}_{fk},v^{-*}_{fk})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u^{-}_{0k}\\

For

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}_{fk}(t)|^{2}=|a_{k}|^{2}(u_{fk}^{-})^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2}(u_{fk}^{+})^{2}+u_{fk}^{+}u_{fk}^{-}(a_{k}b_{k}^{*}+a_{k}^{*}b_{k})\cos(2E_{fk}t)+iu_{fk}^{+}u_{fk}^{-}(a_{k}b_{k}^{*}-a_{k}^{*}b_{k})\sin(2E_{fk}t).

\displaystyle\gamma_{{\bar{A}}{\bar{A}}}(k)=1-\gamma_{{\bar{B}}{\bar{B}}}(k)=|a_{k}|^{2}(u_{fk}^{-})^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2}(u_{fk}^{+})^{2},

(262)

(263)

(264)

Supplementary Appendix C
Some general remarks on quantum expectation value of operators upon a quench

When a generic isolated many-body system with Hamiltonian
H_{f}
is driven out of equilibrium, under Schrödinger evolution it evolves from an initial state
\Psi(0)
to a state
\Psi(t)
at time
t
, given by

\displaystyle\Psi(t)={\rm e}^{-iH_{f}t}\,\Psi(0).

(269)

In the basis of many-body eigenstates
\Psi_{\bf m}
(labelled by
m
), Supplementary Eq. (
269
) is written as

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=\sum_{\bf m}{\rm e}^{-i\mathcal{E}_{\bf m}t}\,w_{\bf m}\,\Psi_{\bf m},

(270)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\bf m}
’s are the many-body eigenenergies and
w_{\bf m}
’s are the superposition coefficients of
\Psi(0)
. Under this evolution, the expectation value of a generic operator
{\cal A}
at time
t
is

\displaystyle\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{{\bf m}{\bf m}^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{-i(\mathcal{E}_{\bf m}-\mathcal{E}_{{\bf m}^{\prime}})t}\,w_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}^{*}\,w_{\bf m}\,\langle\Psi_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}|{\cal A}|\Psi_{\bf m}\rangle.

(271)

So the time parameter enters the evolution
\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle
through a large number
\sim{\cal L}^{2}
of dynamical phases:
(\mathcal{E}_{\bf m}-\mathcal{E}_{{\bf m}^{\prime}})t
with
\mathcal{E}_{\bf m}\neq\mathcal{E}_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}
. Here
{\cal L}
is the number of many-body eigenstates superposing the initial state. Investigations of the emergence of statistical mechanics from such evolving quantum expectation values were initiated in Ref. [31]. Nowadays it is accepted [32-35] that the quantum expectation can equilibrate after long time, when out-of-equilibrium fluctuations around the equilibrium value are small. However, despite of some progresses it remains a difficult problem to demonstrate under what circumstances this scenario would emerge from quantum dynamics of an isolated system.

Compared to Supplementary Eq. (
271
), we find that the correlation matrix Supplementary Eq. (
3
) is simplified substantially. The general reasons underlying this simplification are as follows. First, the Rice-Mele model is composed of free fermions, and many-body effects enter through the so-called exchange interaction, namely, the particle indistinguishability [43]. Second, the system is driven out of equilibrium by global quench. For the first reason each many-body eigenstate
{\bf m}
corresponds to a configuration,
\{n^{\zeta}_{k}\}
, of the occupation number
n^{\zeta}_{k}=0,1
at the single-particle eigenstate
(u^{\zeta}_{fk},v^{\zeta}_{fk})^{T}
of
H_{f}
, with
\zeta=+\,(-)
denoting the particle (hole) band and the superscript
T
denoting the transpose (see

Supplementary Appendix B
). Because the quench is global and the pre-quench state
\Psi(0)
is a half-filling ground state,
the configurations corresponding to the many-body eigenstates of
H_{f}
superposing
\Psi(t)
—
not
all the many-body eigenstates — must satisfy

n^{+}_{k}+n^{-}_{k}=1,\quad{\rm for\,\,every\,\,Bloch\,\,momentum\,}\,k,

(272)

and the corresponding many-body eigenenergies are given by

{\cal E}_{\bf m}=\sum_{k}(n^{+}_{k}-n^{-}_{k})E_{fk}=\sum_{k}(1-2n^{-}_{k})E_{fk},

(273)

where to derive the first equality we have used the reflection symmetry Supplementary Eq. (
240
) and to derive the second we have used Supplementary Eq. (
272
). Taking these into account we simplify Supplementary Eq. (
271
) to

\displaystyle\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{\{{n_{k}^{-}}{}^{\prime}\}}\sum_{\{{n^{-}_{k}}\}}{\rm e}^{it\sum_{k}({n^{-}_{k}}-{n_{k}^{-}}{}^{\prime}){2E_{fk}}}\,w_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}^{*}\,w_{\bf m}\,\langle\Psi_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}|{\cal A}|\Psi_{\bf m}\rangle.

(274)

Note that due to Supplementary Eq. (
272
) the many-body eigenstate
{\bf m}
(or
{\bf m}^{\prime}
) here is completely fixed by the occupation number configuration of the hole band
\{n_{k}^{-}\}\equiv{\bf m}^{-}
(or
\{{n_{k}^{-}}{}^{\prime}\}\equiv{\bf m}^{\prime}{}^{-}
). In Supplementary Eq. (
274
), the dynamical phases are governed by the frequencies associated with the energy gaps
2E_{fk}
of the single-particle band structure and the difference in hole numbers
{n^{-}_{k}}-{n_{k}^{-}}{}^{\prime}
between two states
{\bf m},\,{\bf m}^{\prime}
.

Then we apply Supplementary Eq. (
274
) to one-body operators, e.g. the correlation function
C(t)
which belongs to this class. Such operators take the general form as

{\cal A}=\sum_{\zeta\zeta^{\prime}}\sum_{kk^{\prime}}{\cal A}_{kk^{\prime}}^{\zeta\zeta^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{i(kj-k^{\prime}j^{\prime})}d_{k\zeta}^{\dagger}d_{k^{\prime}\zeta^{\prime}},

(275)

where
d^{\dagger}_{k\zeta}\,,d_{k\zeta}
are, respectively, fermionic creation and annihilation operators at the Bloch momentum
k
and at the band
\zeta
, the coefficients
{\cal A}_{kk^{\prime}}^{\zeta\zeta^{\prime}}
are complex in general, and
j,j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,L
label the unit cells. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}|d_{k\zeta}^{\dagger}d_{k^{\prime}\zeta^{\prime}}|\Psi_{\bf m}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\langle\Psi_{{\bf m}^{\prime}}|d_{k\zeta}^{\dagger}d_{k\zeta^{\prime}}|\Psi_{\bf m}\rangle

\displaystyle\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n_{k}^{\zeta}\prod_{k^{\prime}}\delta_{{n_{k^{\prime}}^{-}}{}^{\prime}{n_{k^{\prime}}^{-}}},&{\rm for}\,\,\zeta=\zeta^{\prime}\\
n_{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}}\delta_{(1-{n_{k}^{-}}{}^{\prime})n_{k}^{-}}\prod_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\delta_{{n_{k^{\prime}}^{-}}{}^{\prime}{n_{k^{\prime}}^{-}}},&{\rm for}\,\,\zeta=-\zeta^{\prime}\end{array}\right.,

due to the constraint Supplementary Eq. (
272
). The product of Kronecker symbols in the second line shows that
{\bf m}^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by
{\bf m}
(more precisely, its occupation number configuration
{\bf m}^{-}
at the hole band) for given
\zeta,\,\zeta^{\prime}
, and thus we denote it as
{\bf m}^{\prime}({\bf m}^{-})
. With the substitution of Supplementary Eqs. (
275
) and (
Supplementary Appendix C
) into Supplementary Eq. (
274
), we obtain

\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle={\cal A}_{0}+\delta{\cal A}(t),

(279)

where
{\cal A}_{0}
has no time dependence,

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{0}=\sum_{k}{\rm e}^{ik(j-j^{\prime})}\sum_{\zeta}{\cal A}_{kk}^{\zeta\zeta}\sum_{{\bf m}^{-}}n_{k}^{\zeta}|w_{\bf m}|^{2},

(280)

and
\delta{\cal A}
depends on time,

\displaystyle\delta{\cal A}(t)=\sum_{k}{\rm e}^{ik(j-j^{\prime})}\sum_{\zeta}{\cal A}_{kk}^{\zeta(-\zeta)}\sum_{{\bf m}^{-}}n_{k}^{-\zeta}\,w_{{\bf m}^{\prime}({\bf m}^{-})}^{*}\,w_{\bf m}\,{\rm e}^{it(2n_{k}^{-}-1){2E_{fk}}}.

(281)

The first factors in Supplementary Eqs. (
280
) and (
281
),
{\rm e}^{ik(j-j^{\prime})}
, imply that the expectation value is translationally invariant in the unit cell index. Most importantly, we can rewrite Supplementary Eq. (
281
) as

\displaystyle\delta{\cal A}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}{\rm e}^{ik(j-j^{\prime})+i{2E_{fk}t}}\,{\cal A}_{kk}^{+-}\,\sum_{{\bf m}^{-}}\delta_{1n_{k}^{-}}\,w_{{\bf m}^{\prime}({\bf m}^{-})}^{*}\,w_{\bf m}

(282)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k}{\rm e}^{ik(j-j^{\prime})-i{2E_{fk}t}}\,{\cal A}_{kk}^{-+}\,\sum_{{\bf m}^{-}}\delta_{0n_{k}^{-}}w_{{\bf m}^{\prime}({\bf m}^{-})}^{*}\,w_{\bf m}.

Taking into account the symmetries Supplementary Eq. (
239
) and (
240
), we thus arrive at the same physical picture as in Supplementary Note
2.1
.
That is, for a generic one-body operator
{\cal A}
,

\displaystyle\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle\leftrightsquigarrow\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\omega}t\in\mathbb{T}^{N},

(283)

i.e. the quantum expectation
\langle\Psi(t)|{\cal A}|\Psi(t)\rangle
corresponds one-to-one to a point
\bm{\omega}t
along the classical trajectory on
\mathbb{T}^{N}
.

Supplementary Appendix D
Proof of two concentration inequalities: Supplementary (
53
) and (
54
)

We shall prove the Supplementary inequality (
53
) only. The Supplementary inequality (
54
) can be proved in the same way. Since
L
and thereby
N
— the number of independent random variables
\varphi_{m}
— are finite, we adopt the theory of concentration of measure [15-17].

First of all, because
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)
is a quasiperiodic in
t
, applying Supplementary Eq. (
43
) gives

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)=\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})=0.

(284)

Then, by Supplementary Eq. (
46
) we have

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\geq\epsilon}\frac{dt}{T}=\mathbf{P}\left(|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})|\geq\epsilon\right).

(285)

The rest of the proof is to bound the probability on the right-hand side. Using Supplementary Eq. (
52
) we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{\varphi_{m}}\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})=-\frac{1}{L}R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\,\sin\varphi_{m}.

(286)

Thus

\displaystyle|\partial_{\bm{\varphi}}\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}(\partial_{\varphi_{m}}\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi}))^{2}=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left(R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\right)^{2}\,\sin^{2}\varphi_{m}

(287)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left(R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\right)^{2}.

For
L\gg 1
the sum converges well to the integral, giving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left(R_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k_{m}\right)\right)^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\left({\rm e}^{ikl}\alpha_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(kl)+{\rm e}^{-ikl}\alpha_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(-kl)\right)^{2}\equiv\|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\|_{\rm Lip}^{2}.

(288)

Since
\mathbb{T}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{N}/\mathbb{Z}^{N}
inherits the Riemann structure from the flat Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})-\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi}^{\prime})|\leq\|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\|_{\rm Lip}\|\bm{\varphi}-\bm{\varphi}^{\prime}\|.

(289)

That is, the function
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
, and
\|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\|_{\rm Lip}
is called the
Lipschitz constant
. Combining this continuity property with a well-known theorem in the theory of concentration of measure [17], we find that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2{\rm e}^{-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{\|\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\|_{\rm Lip}^{2}}\,\epsilon^{2}},

(290)

where
\delta^{\prime}
is some absolute constant of order unity. With the substitution of Supplementary Eq. (
288
) into the right-hand side, we justify the Supplementary inequality (
53
) and Supplementary Eq. (
55
).

Supplementary Appendix E
Statistical independence of correlation matrix elements

In this Supplementary Appendix we show that for large
L
any random variable
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}},\,\mathfrak{I}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
in the block
(C(t))_{l_{1}}
is statistically independent of
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}},\,\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
in a distinct block
(C(t))_{l_{2}}
(
l_{1}\neq l_{2}
), or equivalently, any random variable
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}},\,\mathfrak{I}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
in the block
(\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l_{1}}
is statistically independent of
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}},\,\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
in a distinct block
(\tilde{C}(\bm{\varphi}))_{l_{2}}
. Without loss of generality we prove the statistical independence of
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
below. The statistical independence of
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
and
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
and of
\mathfrak{I}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
can be proven in the same way.

Consider the characteristic function of those two random variables, defined as

\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\equiv\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}{dt\over T}\exp\left(i\left(z_{1}\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(t)+z_{2}\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(t)\right)\right).

(291)

Applying Supplementary Eq. (
43
) we obtain

\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})=\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\exp\left(i\left(z_{1}\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})+z_{2}\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})\right)\right).

(292)

With the substitution of
\mathfrak{R}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})
given by Supplementary Eq. (
50
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\exp\left({i\over L}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\left(z_{1}R_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\cos\varphi_{m}-iz_{2}I_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\sin\varphi_{m}\right)\right)

(293)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{m=0}^{N-1}J_{0}\left({1\over L}\sqrt{\left(z_{1}R_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}-\left(z_{2}I_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}}\right),

where
J_{0}(x)
is the zero-order Bessel function. Recall that
I_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma^{\prime}_{2}}
is purely imaginary. Because of
J_{0}(x\ll 1)\approx 1-{x^{2}\over 4}\approx{\rm e}^{-{x^{2}\over 4}}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L\gg 1}}{{\longrightarrow}}\prod_{m=0}^{N-1}\exp\left(-{1\over 4L^{2}}\left(z_{1}R_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}-\left(z_{2}I_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}\right).

(294)

Next, we consider the characteristic function of the single random variable
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}
, defined as

\Phi_{1}(z_{1})\equiv\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}{dt\over T}\exp\left(iz_{1}\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(t)\right),

(295)

which is

\Phi(z_{1})=\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\exp\left(iz_{1}\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})\right).

(296)

By the same token we find that

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(z_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L\gg 1}}{{\longrightarrow}}\prod_{m=0}^{N-1}\exp\left(-{1\over 4L^{2}}\left(z_{1}R_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}\right).

(297)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(z_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}{dt\over T}\exp\left(iz_{2}\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(t)\right)=\int{d\bm{\varphi}\over(2\pi)^{N}}\exp\left(iz_{2}\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(\bm{\varphi})\right)

(298)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L\gg 1}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\prod_{m=0}^{N-1}\exp\left({1\over 4L^{2}}\left(z_{2}I_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(k_{m})\right)^{2}\right).

(299)

for the characteristic function of
\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}
. From Supplementary Eqs. (
294
), (
297
) and (
299
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L\gg 1}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Phi_{1}(z_{1})\Phi_{1}(z_{2})

(300)

That is,
\Phi_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})
factorizes into
\Phi_{1}(z_{1})\Phi_{1}(z_{2})
for large
L
. This justifies the statistical independence of
\mathfrak{R}_{l_{1},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}(t)
and
\mathfrak{I}_{l_{2},\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}(t)
. By Supplementary Eqs. (
292
), (
296
) and (
298
) it is equivalent to the statistical independence of

Supplementary Appendix H
Time evolution of generic entanglement probes for
\bm{L\rightarrow\infty}

For a generic entanglement probe
O
such as
S
and
S_{n}
, as shown in Supplementary Notes
6
and
7

its variance obeys the same scaling law as Supplementary Eq. (
150
). (Note that the unimportant coefficients
a,b
in Supplementary Eq. (
150
) change, when
S
is replaced by
O
.) As a result, at fixed
L_{A}
a vanishing variance results in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
, i.e.

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}{dt\over T}\left(O(t)-\langle O\rangle\right)^{2}=0.

(314)

Because
O(t)
is continuous in time,
O(t)\neq\langle O\rangle
cannot occur in a zero measure set. Therefore,
(O(t)-\langle O\rangle)^{2}
must decay for sufficiently large
t
(otherwise the limit does not exist), but this contradicts the quasiperiodic behavior of
O(t)
. So the only possibility for Supplementary Eq. (
314
) to hold is that there exists a critical time
t_{c}
, such that

O(t\geq t_{c})=\langle O\rangle.

(315)

In words,
O(t)
is strictly a constant
\langle O\rangle
beyond
t_{c}
. This kind of phenomena were first found numerically in Ref. [1] and proven analytically in Ref. [3], but only for the entanglement entropy
S
.
