Tianlin Liu 1 , Anadi Chaman 2 , David Belius 1 and Ivan Dokmanić 1 2

Abstract
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been tremendously successful in solving imaging inverse problems. To understand their success, an effective strategy is to construct simpler and mathematically more tractable convolutional sparse coding (CSC) models that share essential ingredients with CNNs. Existing CSC methods, however, underperform leading CNNs in challenging inverse problems. We hypothesize that the performance gap may be attributed in part to how they process images at different spatial scales: While many CNNs use multiscale feature representations, existing CSC models mostly rely on single-scale dictionaries. To close the performance gap, we thus propose a multiscale convolutional dictionary structure. The proposed dictionary structure is derived from the U-Net, arguably the most versatile and widely used CNN for image-to-image learning problems. We show that incorporating the proposed multiscale dictionary in an otherwise standard CSC framework yields performance competitive with state-of-the-art CNNs across a range of challenging inverse problems including CT and MRI reconstruction. Our work thus demonstrates the effectiveness and scalability of the multiscale CSC approach in solving challenging inverse problems.

II-A

Sparse representation models

Sparse representation has been extensively studied and widely used in imaging inverse problems
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. It is motivated by the idea that many signals, images being a prime example, can be approximated by a linear combination of a few elements from a suitable overcomplete basis. The sparse representation framework posits that we can decompose a signal of interest
1
1

1

For simplicity we write all signals as 1d vectors, but the formulation is valid in any dimension.

\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, where
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
is an overcomplete dictionary of
N
atoms (
N>d
) and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a sparse vector with few non-zero entries. Learning a sparse representation model thus comprises two sub-problems: (i) given a dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
, encode the signal
\boldsymbol{z}
into a sparse vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
(the
sparse coding
problem), and (ii) given a set of signals, learn an appropriate dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
that sparsifies them (the
dictionary learning
problem). We briefly review these two problems and show how they are related to neural network models such as
CNNs
.

II-B

The sparse coding problem

The sparse coding problem is often formulated as basis pursuit denoising
[
40
]
or Lasso regression
[
41
]
. Most relevant to our work is its formulation with non-negative constraints on the sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
:

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\geq 0}~~\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{1}.

(1)

Here, the first term
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
ensures that the code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
yields a faithful representation of
\boldsymbol{z}
, the second term
\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{1}
controls the sparsity of the code, and the two terms are balanced by a parameter
\lambda>0
.
An effective solver for the minimization problem (
1
) is the
iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (ISTA)

[
42
]
, which executes the following iteration

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[k+1]}&\coloneqq\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[k]},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\\
&\coloneqq\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[k]}+\eta\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[k]})-\eta\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\end{split}

(2)

where the superscript
[k]
denotes the iteration number,
\eta
is a step-size parameter,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is a vector whose entries are all
\lambda
, and
\sigma(x)\coloneqq\max(x,0)
is a component-wise rectifier function. For simplicity, we use
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
to denote one execution of ISTA with measurement
\boldsymbol{z}
, sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
, and threshold
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. The ISTA algorithm is a composition of such executions; we write
\mathrm{ISTA}_{K}
for the
K
-fold composition of
\mathcal{S}
with itself:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{ISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\\
&\coloneqq\underbrace{\Big(\mathcal{S}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{S}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\Big)}_{K\text{ times}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[0]}),\end{split}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[0]}
is the initial sparse code; throughout this work, this initial code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[0]}
is assumed to contain zero in all entries. We emphasize that ISTA is a nonlinear transform of its input
\boldsymbol{z}
.

II-C

The task-driven dictionary learning problem

We now briefly recall the task-driven dictionary learning framework
[
43
]
. Consider a supervised learning setting, in which we aim to identify a parametric function that associates each input
\boldsymbol{z}
(e.g., a corrupted image) with its target
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
(e.g., a clean image) for all
(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
drawn from some joint distribution. In the task-driven framework, we proceed by first representing the signal
\boldsymbol{z}
by a sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
with respect to a dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
. One way to achieve this is to let

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\geq 0}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{1},

(4)

which can be approximated by
K
iterations of
ISTA
as in (
3
). Next, we approximate the desired target
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
using the sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
through a regression model
f(\cdot,\boldsymbol{w})
with learnable parameter
\boldsymbol{w}
. For instance,
f(\cdot,\boldsymbol{w})
could be a linear regression model with weights and biases
\boldsymbol{w}
. The model output
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\boldsymbol{w})
thus depends on the regression model parameters
\boldsymbol{w}
as well as the sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, which in turn depends on the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
through the ISTA iterations. In this way, the regression parameters
\boldsymbol{w}
and dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
can be
jointly
optimized, for instance, with respect to the quadratic loss objective evaluated on a dataset of
M
input-target pairs
\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
:

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{D}}~~\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}},\boldsymbol{w})-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5)

Importantly, the task-driven objective in (
5
) implies that the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
is optimized to solve the supervised learning task and not just to sparsely represent data.

II-D

Convolutional sparse coding

Our work is inspired by the convolutional sparse coding (CSC) model
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
8
]
, which bridges deep
CNNs
and sparse representation models. Concretely, Papyan et al.
[
8
]
noticed that if the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
has a convolutional structure and if the sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is assumed to be non-negative, a single iteration of
ISTA
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[0]}
initialized as a zero vector and step-size
\eta=1
is equivalent to the forward pass of a single-layer convolutional network

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{b}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{b}
is a vector whose components are
-\lambda
(cf. Equation (
2
)). This single-layer formulation can be extended to characterize a deep
CNN
of multiple layers. Specifically, the forward-pass of a deep
CNN
of
L
-layers can be interpreted to approximate the sparse codes
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{L}
sequentially with respect to different dictionaries
\boldsymbol{D}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{D}_{L}
; the back-propagation pass is interpreted as an update to these dictionaries
\{\boldsymbol{D}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
in a task-driven way.

II-E

CNNs for solving inverse problems

Deep
CNNs
achieve state-of-the-art performance in many image processing tasks
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
. In particular, the U-Net
[
31
]
and its variants
[
32
,
33
,
52
]
are among the most extensively used
CNN
architectures in solving image-to-image learning tasks. U-Nets represent images via multiscale features computed from measurements using an encoding (or downsampling) branch and a synthesized into an estimated image in a decoding (or upsampling) branch (Figure
1
a
). In the downsampling branch, the spatial resolutions of feature maps are reduced while the number of feature maps is increased; in the decoding branch, these features are recombined with previous high-resolution features via channel concatenation (“skip connections”) and convolution. Heuristically, low-resolution feature maps of a U-Net capture large-scale image properties, whereas the high-resolution feature maps capture more fine-grained image properties
[
52
]
. In a related line of work, Ye et al.
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
proposed to use the framelets formalism
[
56
]
to study aspects of U-Net-like encoder-decoder
CNNs
. A key observation they make is that a U-Net model is closely related to convolutional framelets whose frame basis selection depends non-linearly on input data.

III
CSC with multiscale dictionaries

The structure of a convolutional dictionary is crucial to a
CSC
model since the dictionary atoms characterize the signals that can be represented sparsely. In the existing formulation of CSC, atoms of a convolutional dictionary have a single scale, in the sense that they all share the same spatial shape. However, many image classes and imaging artifacts exhibit structured correlations over multiple scales. To exploit these correlations in imaging inverse problems, we construct multiscale convolutional dictionaries.

Our construction is based on the U-Net reviewed in Section
II
. Indeed, the tremendous success of U-Nets has in part been attributed to their ability to represent images at multiple scales
[
33
,
55
]
, which is achieved by using up- and downsampling operations together with skip connections as in Figure
1
a
. Another property of the U-Net is its shared parameters across scales: Low-resolution features (the grey boxes at the bottom of Figure
1
a
) and high-resolution features (the grey boxes at the top of Figure
1
a
) undergo an overlapping synthesizing path parameterized by shared weights. This weight-sharing strategy has not been employed by existing proposals for multiscale CSC dictionaries
[
29
,
30
]
. In what follows, we describe the construction process of a
linear
dictionary inspired by and closely following the standard U-Net.

III-A

Encoder–decoder dictionaries

We denote the encoding branch of the U-Net by
f_{\mathrm{enc}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{enc}}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{enc}}
; the encoding branch maps the input
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to convolutional feature maps
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}=f_{\mathrm{enc}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{enc}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, illustrated as the dark grey boxes in Figure
1
a
. Note that, for a U-Net, the intermediate feature map dimension
N
(number of scalar coefficients in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
) is typically much greater than the image dimension
d
. These feature maps are then fed into the decoding branch of the U-Net either through skip connections or through the bottleneck layer. To describe this process, we write the decoding branch of the U-Net as a function
f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}}):\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}}
. That is, the function
f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}})
takes the convolutional feature maps produced by the encoding branch and transforms them to produce the model output. We can thus write the output produced by a U-Net as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}\coloneqq f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}})=f_{\mathrm{dec}}\Big(f_{\mathrm{enc}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{enc}}),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}}\Big).

We now focus on the image synthesis process of the U-Net, described by the decoding function
f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}})
. This function synthesizes convolutional feature maps at different spatial scales through skip connections and upsampling. As such, the decoding branch of the U-Net approximates an image
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using multiscale feature maps
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
of a much higher dimension, so that
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}\approx f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}})
. Conceptually, this representation is similar to the sparse and overcomplete representation in a dictionary, except that the U-Net decoder is non-linear.

To construct a multiscale dictionary, we thus consider a stripped-down version of the image synthesis process of U-Net by removing all non-linearities, batch normalization, and additive biases from the function
f_{\mathrm{dec}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{dec}})
, as shown in Figure
1
b
; to further simplify the architecture, at each spatial scale, we additionally remove a convolution and halve the number of convolutional channels for all convolutions. The resulting function is then simply a
linear
transformation

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4})\mapsto\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4}
are sparse code having different resolutions (visualized as the grey boxes in Figure 1
b
). This dictionary shares the essential ingredients of convolution, multiscale representation, and skip connections with the U-Net decoding branch and therefore we refer to it as the decoder dictionary. A precise definition of the decoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
through convolution and upsampling is provided in Appendix
A
.

III-B

The dictionary-based sparsity prior

With a given decoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
to describe the image synthesis process, we next consider how to infer an associated sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, so that
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a good approximation of the image we wish to model. In a supervised learning setting where the input image
\boldsymbol{z}
is given, it is natural to interpret
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as an encoded representation of
\boldsymbol{z}
. Since the encoding must produce a coefficient vector whose structure is compatible with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we endow an encoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
with the same structure of
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
albeit with a different set of atoms. This setup is analogous to U-Net’s encoding and decoding branches: the encoder and decoder dictionaries
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
are employed to process input signals and produce output signals, respectively. The sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
induced by an input
\boldsymbol{z}
and the encoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
then facilitate the subsequent task for approximating the ground-truth image
\boldsymbol{x}
:

\boldsymbol{z}\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84526pt\textrm{ Sparse coding with }\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\hskip 2.84526pt}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84526pt\textrm{ Synthesis with }\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\hskip 2.84526pt}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}.

(8)

In what follows, we derive a supervised learning method that turns each
\boldsymbol{z}
into a prediction
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
using encoder and decoder dictionaries.

III-C

The task-driven dictionary learning objective

Under the task-driven framework introduced in Section
II
, we formulate a supervised learning problem via sparse coding and dictionary learning. We consider the following minimization problem over a dataset of
M
input-target pairs
\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\},\boldsymbol{\lambda}>0}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\text{where}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}\coloneqq\mathrm{ISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

The objective in (
9
) penalizes the discrepancy between the ground-truth signal
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
and the model prediction
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, where the latter is a signal synthesized from a sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
via the decoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
; the code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is a sparse representation of the input image
\boldsymbol{z}
with respect to the encoder dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
by unrolling a fixed number
K
of
ISTA
iterations. The sparsity-controlling parameter
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is multi-dimensional, weighting codes component-wise. The intuition behind this choice is that the different convolutional features, especially those at different scales, should be thresholded differently. The sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, illustrated as the grey boxes in Figure
1
b
, is a collection of multi-dimensional tensors, each corresponds a spatial scale.

The task driven objective (
9
) defines a computational graph that transforms an input image
\boldsymbol{z}
into a prediction
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}
. We term this computational graph MUSC, since it involves
m
ultiscale
U
-Net-like
s
parse
c
oding. We note the MUSC is an instance of
optimization-driven networks

[
26
]
derived by unrolling an optimization algorithm. It incorporates two modules with meaningful objectives, one implementing sparse coding and the other dictionary-based synthesis. This composition is arguably conceptually more interpretable than end-to-end layerwise composition of deep networks.

While a traditional compressed sensing approach uses
a single dictionary
for reconstruction, our approach uses
two dictionaries

\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
in the task-driven learning objective (
9
). This discrepancy is due to different assumptions in measurement-to-image reconstruction (the compressed sensing approach) and image-to-image reconstruction (our approach). Consider an inverse problem with a forward operator
\boldsymbol{A}
, a unknown ground-truth signal
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
, and measurements
\boldsymbol{y}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
; in CT reconstruction,
\boldsymbol{A}
is the Radon transform and
\boldsymbol{y}
is the measured sinogram. The compressed sensing approach estimates
\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}
as
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\ast}
for some dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
, where

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\ast}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert_{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\rVert_{1}

(10)

is the inferred sparse code based on the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
. Note that (
10
) and the synthesis
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
require only a single dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
. However, this approach assumes that we know the measurements
\boldsymbol{y}
and the forward operator
\boldsymbol{A}
.

If we were to apply a single dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}=\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
in our image-to-image learning approach in (
9
), we would find a sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
such that
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\approx{\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\approx{\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}}
. This is difficult when
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}
significantly differs from the identity operator as in the case of highly ill-posed problems. On the other hand, using two dictionaries
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
in (
9
) requires finding a sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
such that
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\approx{\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\approx{\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}}
, a formulation that is more flexible when
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}\boldsymbol{A}
substantially differs from the identity. Experiments in Section
IV-E
confirm that allowing
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\neq\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
yields better performance. We note that our approach is morally related to setting
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{D}
in (
10
), but since we do not know
\boldsymbol{A}
we have to learn
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
from samples together with
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
. Such a learned encoder dictionary captures information about
\boldsymbol{A}
, entangled with information about the data distribution.

III-D

Relaxation on dictionaries

We now describe computational techniques that stabilize the gradient-descent-based dictionary learning of MUSC. Following earlier work
[
57
,
10
,
26
,
25
,
6
]
, we untie the encoder dictionary from its adjoint during dictionary update. That is, we replace the execution in (
2
) by

\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\coloneqq\sigma(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\eta\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\eta\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

(11)

where the dictionary
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}}
is initialized to be identical to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
but is allowed to evolve independently during training. Even though the theoretical effects of this relaxation remain unclear, the dictionary
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}}
can be interpreted as a learned preconditioner that accelerates training
[
26
,
25
]
; see also the investigation in
[
58
,
59
,
6
]
. The
learned ISTA
(LISTA) algorithm
[
57
]
corresponding to (
11
) is written as

\begin{split}&\mathrm{LISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\\
&\small\coloneqq\underbrace{\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{K})\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})\Big)}_{K\text{ times}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[0]}),\end{split}

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{K}
are the soft-thresholding parameters for each ISTA execution. Note that, in (
12
), the soft-thresholding parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
depend on the execution step. As shown in
[
6
]
, incorporating step-dependent soft-thresholding parameters can be beneficial. While
[
6
]
uses a homotopy continuation strategy to adjust these parameters we treat them as learnable parameters for simplicity. Taking these considerations into account, we define a new regression loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}=\mathrm{LISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

Unless mentioned otherwise, we use the loss (
13
) to train MUSC throughout our paper. In Section
IV-E
, we compare the performance of trained model using (
13
) and (
9
).

III-E

Training the MUSC

Training the MUSC entails the following three steps:

1.

Dictionary initialization:
We randomly initialize the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
and initialize
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
, and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}}
as identical copies of
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
.

2.

Model forward pass:
For each input image
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
, we evaluate the model prediction
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}
as in Equation (
13
). For ISTA executions, we initialize all sparse code
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[0]}
as a collection of all-zero tensors; the ISTA step-size parameter
\eta
is initialized as the inverse of the dominant eigenvalue of the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
, which can be approximated using by power iteration (Appendix
C
).

3.

Task-driven dictionary learning:
For a mini-batch of input-target pairs, solve the optimization problem in (
13
) with gradient descent.

IV
Experiments

We report the performance of MUSC on deraining, CT reconstruction, and MRI reconstruction tasks. The motivations for choosing these tasks are as follows. First, we note that single-scale
CSC
models have recently been applied to the deraining task, achieving performance slightly worse than state-of-the-art deep networks
[
27
]
; we thus aim to test the capability of our multiscale approach on the same task. We additionally choose CT and MRI reconstruction tasks as there exist challenging, large-scale, and up-to-date benchmark datasets for these tasks. Two such datasets that we use are the LoDoPaB-CT
[
64
]
and the fastMRI
[
65
]
. An additional strength of these two datasets is that the model evaluation process is carefully controlled: The evaluation on the challenge fold (for LoDoPaB-CT) or the test fold (for fastMRI) is restricted through an online submission portal with the ground truth hidden from the public. As a result, overfitting to these evaluation folds is difficult and quantitative comparisons are transparent.

Throughout this section, we use the MUSC architecture whose encoder and decoder dictionaries are displayed in Figure
1
b
and mathematically defined in Appendix
A
. Hyper-parameter choices for the experiments are provided in Appendix
D
. For each task, we use well-known
CNN
models as baselines. We note that, for the CT and MRI reconstruction tasks, there are two major approaches to employ
CNNs
. In the first,
model-based
approach, one applies neural networks on raw measurement data (sinogram data in CT and k-space data in MRI) by embedding a task-dependent forward operator (the Radon transform for CT and the Fourier transform for MRI) into multiple layers or iterations of the network. Learning methods of this approach can be highly performant at the cost of being computationally expensive, especially during training, since one needs to apply the forward operator (and the adjoint of its derivative) repeatedly
[
49
]
. In the second,
model-free
approach, the (pseudoinverse of the) forward operator is used at most
once
during data preprocessing and is never used during subsequent supervised training. These preprocessed images contain imaging artifacts. During supervised learning, one applies a
CNN
directly on these preprocessed images. The proposed MUSC is in this sense a model-free approach and we compare it to model-free baselines. We note that in this case one does not need to know the forward operator at all. The leading model-free baseline
CNN
methods in this approach are typically U-Net variants tuned to the task at hand. For a more thorough comparison, we also implemented the original U-Net architecture proposed in
[
31
]
(schematically illustrated in Figure
1
a
) in these tasks as additional baselines.

While model-free approaches perform somewhat worse than model-based ones, our purpose here is to show that a general-purpose multiscale convolutional model can perform as well as convolutional neural networks
ceteris paribus
, rather than to propose state-of-the-art reconstruction algorithms for specific problems. This general-purpose approach further allows us to tackle structured denoising problems such as deraining where the forward operator is simply the identity.

\boldsymbol{z}

[

[

[

[

[

[

[

IV-D

Single-image super-resolution

We have additionally tested the MUSC on a standard super-resolution task, whose results are deferred to Appendix
G
. The goal of this task is to recover a high-resolution image from its degraded, low-resolution version. Unlike tasks such as CT and MRI reconstruction, in which the image degradation processes introduce long-range spatially correlated noise like streak artifacts, the blurring process in the super-resolution task is spatially local.

In this case, we do not observe a performance gain of using a multiscale model – either U-Net or MUSC – over state-of-the-art single-scale
CSC
models. Interestingly, MUSC outperforms the U-Net, but is up to 0.5 dB worse than single-scale CSC.

In subsection
IV-F
, we study this phenomenon by analyzing the sparse code yielded by MUSC. In the super-resolution tasks, the nonzeros in sparse codes are confined to high-resolution channels, or, equivalently, small filter supports which only leverage local information. This is well aligned with the intuition that the blurring forward operator mixes information only locally. It suggests that the right strategy is to use a large number of small-support filters just like CSC does, instead of “wasting” trainable parameters on unused large scales. We similarly find that a single-scale
CSC
model works better than MUSC on a denoising (Gaussian noise removal) task. Together, these findings suggest that multiscale features are no panacea for imaging inverse problems; the configuration of scales needs to resonate with the task-dependent forward operator that we aim to invert.

Ablations on the choices of model components

In Figure
5

There, Case 2 is the off-the-shelf setup we have used in all other sections of this paper; this option has the fastest learning speed and highest end-point accuracy. Consistent with findings in
[

IV-F

Probing multiscale dictionary-based representations

Thus far, we have shown that our proposed multiscale
CSC
approach, dubbed MUSC, performs comparably to state-of-the-art
CNNs
in a range of imaging inverse problems. This is noteworthy, as the strong performance is achieved simply by employing a multiscale dictionary – as opposed to a single-scale one – in an otherwise standard CSC paradigm. The strong performance suggests the usefulness of the multiscale representation. We now analyze our learned dictionaries and their induced sparse representations.

Visualizing dictionary atoms

We visualize dictionary atoms of the MUSC. To extract a dictionary atom from a dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
, we first prepare an
indicator code

\boldsymbol{\delta}
, which is a collection of multichannel tensors that takes a value
1
at a certain entry and
0
elsewhere; a dictionary atom corresponds to that entry is computed as
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\delta}
. Note that, different positions of the nonzero entry may give rise to atoms of different support sizes. This can be seen in Figure
1
b
: The indicator code is illustrated as the grey boxes; depending on the grey box the nonzero entry resides in, the sparse code activates different receptive fields under composite convolutions and transposed convolutions. If the nonzero entry resides in the top-most box, then the support of the atom is 3 as it undergoes only a single 3 × 3 convolution; if the nonzero entry is in one of the lower boxes, the support of the atom is larger as the code undergoes multiple convolutions and one or more transposed convolutions.

In Figure
6
, we show samples of multiscale atoms in
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
of varying sizes – we crop these atoms to only show their nonzero support regions. As can be seen in Figure
6
b
-
d
, the learned dictionaries contain Gabor-like or curvelet-like atoms with different spatial widths, resolutions, and orientations. Thus the learned dictionaries indeed exploit multiscale features. For comparison, we also show a randomly initialized dictionary (Figure
6
a
). Unlike a learned dictionary, a random dictionary does not exhibit structures in atoms. We also visualize atoms of encoder dictionaries
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{D}}_{\textrm{enc}}
in Appendix
E
. Using a similar technique, we also probe the multiscale representations learned by U-Nets in Appendix
F
.

Sparsity levels of representations

We anticipate that the trained dictionaries induce different sparsity levels at different resolution levels in a task-dependent manner: More non-zeros associated with large-support atoms are useful when imaging artifacts have long-range correlations (e.g., streak artifacts in CT) than when the artifacts are localized (e.g., deraining or super-resolution).

Figure
7
shows the sparsity levels across tasks, both before and after dictionary learning. We observe that, prior to any learning, the sparsity levels induced by randomly initialized dictionaries (grey bars) are approximately uniform across scales. After learning, the sparsity levels of feature maps differentiate in a task-dependent way (orange bars in all panels). This task-dependent differentiation suggests the usefulness of multiscale representations – the learned sparsity levels are neither collapsed to a single scale nor remain uniform across spatial scales; instead, they are weighted and combined across scales in a problem-dependent way. A curious effect of multiscale learning arises in super-resolution (panel
d
): the activations are nonzero
only in high-resolution features
(“Up-2”, “Up-3”, and “Up-4”), corroborating the intuition that low-resolution features are not important for this task. Additionally, comparing the “Middle” bars across panels, we see that CT and MRI reconstruction tasks indeed use more nonzero coefficients on large-support atoms than tasks such as deraining and super-resolution.

V
Discussion

The
CSC
paradigm provides a natural connection between sparse modeling and
CNNs
. Despite being mathematical principled, existing
CSC
models still fall short competing with
CNNs
in terms of empirical performance on challenging inverse problems. In this work, we report one simple and effective way to close the performance gap between
CSC
and
CNN
models: incorporating a multiscale structure in the
CSC
dictionaries. Crucial to our approach is the structure of our constructed multiscale dictionary: It takes inspiration from and closely follows the highly successful U-Net model. We show that the constructed multiscale dictionary performs on par with leading
CNNs
in major imaging inverse problems. These results suggest a strong link between dictionary learning and
CNNs
– in both cases, multiscale structures are essential ingredients.

Beyond empirical performance, we believe that the interpretability of the proposed MUSC is showing the way towards an interpretable deep learning model. An interpretable model consists of components whose objectives and functionality have nominal values. The MUSC fulfills this desideratum by incorporating two modules with well-understood objectives, one implementing sparse coding and the other dictionary-based synthesis.

Overall, our work demonstrates the effectiveness and scalability of
CSC
models on imaging inverse problems. While deep neural networks are profoundly influencing image reconstruction, our work shows promise in a different direction: the principles of sparsity and multiscale representation developed decades ago are still useful in designing performant, parameter-efficient (compared to mainstream CNNs), and interpretable architectures that push the current limits of machine learning for imaging inverse problems.

Appendix A
The definition of the MUSC dictionary

In the main text, we illustrate the architecture of a U-Net (Figure
1
a
) and the corresponding MUSC decoder dictionary (Figure
1
b
). Loosely speaking, the decoder dictionary is the decoding branch of a standard U-Net with all ReLU activations, batch normalization, and some convolution operations removed. Here we provide a formal definition of the decoder dictionary. While in the main text we assume for simplicty that signals are 1D vectors, in this section we represent RGB images as multichannel tensors. To that end, we first consider
single-input single-output (SISO)
operations whose input and output are 2D signals having a single channel. We follow by considering
multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)
operations whose input and output are 3D tensors having multiple channels.

SISO convolution and transposed convolution

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
be a 2D signal with height
H
and width
W
. We regard
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as a function defined on the discrete domain
\{1,\ldots,H\}\times\{1,\ldots,W\}
. With standard zero padding, we extend the domain of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. The notation
\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]
represents the value of the function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
at the coordinate
(i,j)
.

SISO convolution

Given a 2D signal
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
and parameters (filter weights, filter impulse response)
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, the convolution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
is defined as

(\boldsymbol{w}\ast\boldsymbol{\xi})[i,j]=\sum_{p=-1}^{1}\sum_{q=-1}^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}[i+p,j+q]\cdot\boldsymbol{w}[p,q].

(14)

SISO transposed convolution

A transposed convolution in the U-Net consists of a bed-of-nails upsampling by a factor of two followed by a 2-by-2 convolution with an interpolating filter. The bed-of-nails upsampling interleaves zeros between samples, which can be written as
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
with
\otimes
denoting the Kronecker product. The 2-by-2 convolution between the filter
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and signal
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
is written as

(\boldsymbol{v}\circledast\boldsymbol{\xi})[i,j]=\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}[i+p,j+q]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}[p,q].

(15)

Putting these together, we have the following definition for a transposed convolution.

Definition 1
.

A transposed convolution
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
parameterized by the convolutional filter
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is a map defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}\circledast(\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}).

The following technical result simplifies the upsampling calculation.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be a matrix and let
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}
be its vertically and horizontally flipped version, i.e.,
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}[i,j]=\boldsymbol{v}[3-i,3-j]
for
(i,j)\in\{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a matrix with dimension
n\geq 2
. Then the following equation holds:

\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}.

(16)

Proof.

Observe that the result of LHS and RHS are both
2n
-by-
2n
matrices, each composed of
n\times n
number of 2-by-2 submatrices. It is enough to show that each
2
-by-
2
sub-matrix of the LHS equals that of the RHS if they have the same position indices.

We first expand the RHS. By definition of Kronecker product, we have

\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}={\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}[1,1]\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\xi}[1,n]\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}[n,1]\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\xi}[n,n]\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\end{bmatrix}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n},

where the
(i,j)
-th 2-by-2 sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}
has the form

\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}[2,2]&\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}[2,1]\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}[1,2]&\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}[1,1]\end{bmatrix},

(17)

for each
(i,j)\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\times\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

For the LHS, we have
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{v}\circledast(\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix})
by definition. Writing out the Kronecker product, we see that the
(i,j)
-th 2-by-2 submatrix of the LHS has the form

\boldsymbol{v}\circledast\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}[2,2]&\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}[2,1]\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}[1,2]&\boldsymbol{\xi}[i,j]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}[1,1]\end{bmatrix},

(18)

for each
(i,j)\in\{1,\cdots,n\}\times\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
Comparing Equation (
17
) and Equation (
18
), we see the each 2-by-2 sub-matrix of the LHS and RHS of Equation (
16
) are identical as claimed.
∎

MIMO convolution and transposed convolution

To extend our formulation to the
MIMO
case, we denote by
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{C})\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
a multi-channel signal.

MIMO convolution

We let
\boldsymbol{W}=(\boldsymbol{w}_{11},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{1C},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{M1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{MC})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times C\times 3\times 3}
be a multi-channel convolutional kernel of 3-by-3 filters, where
C
denotes the number of input channels and
M
denotes the number of output channels. The MIMO convolution is defined as

\text{Conv}(\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{1c}\ast\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{Mc}\ast\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times H\times W}.

MIMO transposed convolution

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{C})\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
be a multi-channel signal. We let
\boldsymbol{V}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{11},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{1C},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{M1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{MC})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times C\times 2\times 2}
be a multi-channel convolutional kernel of 2-by-2 filters. The MIMO transposed convolution is defined as

\text{TConv}(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c}\otimes\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1c},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c}\otimes\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{Mc}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 2H\times 2W}.

Up-block

An up-block receives multichannel input from (i) low-resolution features in the shape of
(2C,H,W)
and (ii) skip-connection features in the shape of
(C,2H,2W)
. With these input features, an up-block applies a transposed convolution to low-resolution features that halves the number of channels, concatenates the resulting features with skip-connection features, and then submits the concatenated results for another convolution. This can be written as

\displaystyle\text{Up}_{\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{V}}:\mathbb{R}^{C\times 2H\times 2W}\times\mathbb{R}^{2C\times H\times W}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{C\times 2H\times 2W}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\mapsto\text{Conv}([\boldsymbol{\beta};\text{TConv}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{V})],\boldsymbol{W}),

where
\boldsymbol{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{2C\times C\times 3\times 3}
and
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{2C\times C\times 2\times 2}
are parameters and
[\cdot;\cdot]
denotes channel-wise concatenation.

The multiscale dictionary

We now define the multiscale dictionary

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}:\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{C/2^{4}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{C^{\textrm{out}}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4})

\displaystyle\mapsto\boldsymbol{D}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4})

(19)

that is used as encoders and decoders in our work. Here,
C^{\textrm{out}}=1\textrm{~or~}3
for grayscale and RGB images. We construct the dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}
as a linear transform
\boldsymbol{R}
that is independent of
C^{\textrm{out}}
followed by a one-by-one convolution that produce
C^{\textrm{out}}
channels:

\boldsymbol{D}=\texttt{Conv1x1}\circ\boldsymbol{R}.

(20)

The linear map
\boldsymbol{R}
is defined by cascading
4
Up-blocks with parameters
\boldsymbol{W}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{i},i=1,\ldots,4
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}:\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{C/2^{4}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{C/2^{4}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4})

\displaystyle\mapsto\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4},

(21)

where

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}=\text{Up}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{i},\boldsymbol{V}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i-1})

for
i=1,\ldots 4
with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0}
. That is, the function
\boldsymbol{R}
transforms multiscale sparse code
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{4})
to a tensor
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4}
of shape
\mathbb{R}^{C/2^{4}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}
. The
1\times 1
convolution operator
\boldsymbol{C}
next synthesizes these features into a tensor with channel number 1 or 3:

\texttt{Conv1x1}:\mathbb{R}^{C/2^{4}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}\to\mathbb{R}^{C^{\textrm{out}}\times 2^{4}\cdot H\times 2^{4}\cdot W}.

(22)

Appendix B
Can a learned sparse coder yield dense outputs?

In the main text, we introduced the
K
-fold ISTA algoritm for sparse coding. The sparse coding result reads
\mathrm{ISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, where
\boldsymbol{z}
is a given input,
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
is an encoding dictionary, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is a threshold tensor. Since we also learn a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
from data during training, one concern is whether a learned
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be zero. Indeed, if this were to occur, the sparse coding results would be dense and it would defeat the purpose of sparse coding.

In this section, we argue that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
is (i) not optimal for many inverse problems and (ii) unlikely to be learned from data. To see the non-optimality of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
, we observe that with the iteration number
K
is large enough, with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
, and with zero initialization of sparse code, ISTA yields the smallest
\ell^{2}
-norm solution which can be written via the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathrm{ISTA}_{K}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{+}\boldsymbol{z}.

Our task-driven dictionary learning problem thus has the form

\minimize_{\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}},\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}}\quad\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\dagger}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

This implies that
\lambda=0
results in a linear reconstruction method which comes with all the known drawbacks of linear methods. In particular, it cannot do better than the linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) estimator (a generalized Wiener filter). Since regularization in ill-posed inverse problems entails the use of data models, and most useful data models are nonlinear (e.g., natural and medical images are known to be sparse or compressible in wavelet frames, but they do not belong to any linear subspace), these problems demand
\boldsymbol{\lambda}>0
.

One may still wonder whether our learning procedure will overfit finite datasets with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
. To see that this will not happen, note that
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{dec}}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
are constrained to have a specific structure: They are multiscale variants of block-Toeplitz matrices. Additionally, filters at high resolutions are convolutions of filters at lower resolutions which induces a rather complicated algebraic structure. As a result, the set of valid dictionaries in our model has a much smaller dimension than the set of all possible dictionaries of correct size, and solving (
9
) cannot be reduced to finding two generic overcomplete dictionaries that “overfit” to training data to achieve zero loss. In fact, forcing
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
typically incurs a large loss in (
9
), so
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=0
is unlikely to be learned from data. This is the magic of multiscale convolutional sparsity.

Appendix C
Power iteration

We describe how to approximate the dominant eigenvalue of the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}
using by power iteration. We achieve this by first estimating the eigenvector associated with the dominant eigenvalue using the power iteration method, by recursively calculating

\boldsymbol{b}_{k+1}=\frac{\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\boldsymbol{b}_{k}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\boldsymbol{b}_{k}\right\|_{2}}

(23)

up to some step
K
with
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
being an all-zero vector. The estimated dominant eigenvalue can then be derived from

\lambda_{K}=\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{K}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}_{\textrm{enc}}\boldsymbol{b}_{K}}{\boldsymbol{b}_{K}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}_{K}}.

(24)
