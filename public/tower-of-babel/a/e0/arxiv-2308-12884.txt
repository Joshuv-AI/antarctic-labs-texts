Abstract
. We present a second-order strictly length-preserving and unconditionally energy-stable rotational discrete gradient (Rdg) scheme for the numerical approximation of the Oseen-Frank gradient flows with anisotropic elastic energy functional. Two essential ingredients of the Rdg method are reformulation of the length constrained gradient flow into an unconstrained rotational form and discrete gradient discretization for the energy variation. Besides the well-known mean-value and Gonzalez discrete gradients, we propose a novel Oseen-Frank discrete gradient, specifically designed for the solution of Oseen-Frank gradient flow. We prove that the proposed Oseen-Frank discrete gradient satisfies the energy difference relation, thus the resultant Rdg scheme is energy stable. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the proposed Rdg method and its capability for providing reliable simulation results with highly disparate elastic coefficients.

1.
Introduction

The dynamics of liquid crystals involve the evolution of local anisotropy generated by nonuniform orientational distribution.
For uniaxial nematics, the equilibrium orientational distribution is axisymmetric and is allowed to rotate freely as a whole.
A simplified setting for the dynamics of uniaxial nematics is to assume that the local anisotropy is kept at the equilibrium state and only rotations of the state is allowed.
Under this rationale, the local anisotropy is sufficiently described by a unit vector field
\bm{n}(\bm{x})
.
The well-known Ericksen-Leslie model
[
13
,
25
]
couples the velocity and the unit vector field.
The evolution of the vector field is given by

\displaystyle\bm{n}\times\Big(\gamma_{1}\big(\bm{n}_{t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{n}+\overline{\overline{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{n}\big)+\gamma_{2}\overline{\overline{\tau}}\cdot\bm{n}+\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)=0,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle|\bm{n}|=1.

(1.1b)

In the above,

\overline{\overline{\Omega}}=(\nabla\bm{v}-\nabla\bm{v}^{\intercal})/2
, and
\overline{\overline{\tau}}=(\nabla\bm{v}+\nabla\bm{v}^{\intercal})/2
, where
\bm{v}
is the velocity of the fluid governed by the Navier–Stokes equation that we do not write down here.
The force by the interaction of local anisotropy is characterized by variational derivative of the Oseen–Frank energy
[
30
,
16
]
,

\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}k_{1}(\nabla\cdot\bm{n})^{2}+k_{2}|\bm{n}\cdot(\nabla\times\bm{n})|^{2}+k_{3}|\bm{n}\times(\nabla\times\bm{n})|^{2}+(k_{2}+k_{4})\big[{\rm tr}\big((\nabla\bm{n})^{2}\big)-(\nabla\cdot\bm{n})^{2}\big]dV.

(1.2)

The first three terms can be explained as excess energy density for three typical deformations specifically for unit vector fields: splay, twist and bend
[
8
]
.
Therefore, it is the three constants
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
that characterize the elasticity of a certain material.
They are closely related to physical parameters, whose relative magnitudes may vary in a wide range by previous experimental or theoretical results
[
17
,
10
,
37
,
22
,
23
,
29
,
3
,
33
,
20
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
26
]
.
The last term can be rewritten as a surface integral, which is usually not considered as it vanishes under periodic or some other commonly adopted boundary conditions.

When the velocity is small, one may approximate by assuming
\bm{v}=0
, so that the Ericksen-Leslie model is reduced to

\displaystyle\bm{n}\times\bm{n}_{t}=-\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}},

(1.3a)

\displaystyle|\bm{n}|=1,

(1.3b)

where
\gamma_{1}
is also eliminated with suitable rescaling.
This equation can be interpreted as a gradient flow driven by the Oseen–Frank energy with an explicit length constraint, which we shall clarify later.
Notably, it is desirable for a numerical scheme to keep the vector length and the energy dissipation.
In particular, the deviation of vector length would bring ambiguity when explaining the results because the pictures of splay, twist and bend terms are no longer valid for vector fields with varying lengths.
However, both the
k_{i}
terms and the length constraint give rise to strong nonlinearity that is not easy to handle.

Most of the previous works on the analysis and numerical methods of nematic liquid crystal problems study the isotropic elasticity (see e.g.
[
27
,
2
,
28
,
9
,
15
,
19
,
42
]
), i.e.
k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3}=k
and
k_{4}=0
making
\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]
reduce to the squared gradient,

\mathcal{F}_{D}[\bm{n}]=\frac{k}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\bm{n}|^{2}dV.

(1.4)

There are only limited studies on numerical methods for anisotropic elasticity (see
[
32
,
1
]
).
Even assuming the isotropic elasticity, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, in existing works the length contraint and energy dissipation are rarely satisfied simultaneously.
One popular choice is to relax the model by substituting the explicit length contraint with a penalty term in the energy
[
28
,
9
,
15
,
19
,
42
]
.
As a result, we obtain an equation of the Ginzburg–Landau type,

\bm{n}_{t}=-k\Delta\bm{n}+F^{\prime}_{{\varepsilon}}(\bm{n}),\quad F_{{\varepsilon}}(\bm{n})=\big(|\bm{n}|^{2}-1\big)^{2}/\big(4{\varepsilon}^{2}\big).

(1.5)

We can see that the nonlinearity on length constraint is converted into a nonlinear term in the energy, and the spatial derivative term is linear when the isotropic elasticity is assumed.
The problem then becomes standard as one only needs to focus on energy dissipation, for which various techniques are available, including convex splitting
[
12
,
14
]
, linear stablization
[
36
,
6
]
, and auxiliary variable such as IEQ
[
41
,
42
]
and SAV
[
34
,
35
]
.
However, it is worthwhile to note that the relaxation allows the vector length to deviate.
To date, it is still not definite how such a relaxation approximate the original Oseen–Frank gradient flow.
On the other hand, some works put more emphasis on the unit-vector constraint.
One simple idea is projection
[
11
]
, which follows the predictor-corrector routine. Ignoring the unit-vector constraint, one firstly obtains in the prediction step an intermediate approximate solution, followed by the normalization on the vector length.
Despite its simplicity, it is highly nontrivial to extend the projection method to high-order schemes.
Another straightforward approach is to adopt the spherical coordinates method by representing the vector field in terms of the orientation angles

\bm{n}=\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi)\bm{e}_{x}+\cos(\theta)\sin(\phi)\bm{e}_{y}+\sin(\theta)\bm{e}_{z}
, and to numerically evolve the angles.
The unit-vector constraint can then be naturally guaranteed.
However, as pointed out in
[
32
]
, such a representation degenerates when
\cos(\theta)=0
and the orientation angle is discontinuous across
\theta+\pi
and
\phi+2\pi
.
In addition, the energy dissipation cannot be guaranteed theoretically mainly because the energy takes a form much more complicated.
It is also possible to introduce a Lagrange multiplier for the length constraint, which transforms the original equation into a saddle-point form
[
4
,
5
]
.
Currently, this approach requires solving within the framework of mixed elements, which is designed specially for the isotropic squared gradient term.
To investigate and compare the dynamics of nematics formed by different materials, it calls for reliable and efficient schemes to deal with anisotropic elasticity, but it turns out that the existing approaches are far from satisfactory.

In this paper, we propose a novel second-order rotational discrete gradient (Rdg) scheme for the Oseen-Frank gradient flows with anisotropic elasticity, which is strictly length-preserving meanwhile unconditionally energy stable.
This is achieved by reformulating the gradient flow with unit-vector constraint into an unconstrained rotational form and dealing with the variational derivative via discrete gradients.
For functional of multiple variables, the construction of discrete gradient is not unique.
We compare three different discrete gradients in the method we propose, including the widely-used mean-value and Gonzalez discrete gradients, together with one particularly designed for the Oseen-Frank energy.
Although theoretically they all satisfy the two desired properties, in practice nonlinear equations need to be solved, leading to difference in performance.
Our numerical tests indicate that the specially designed Oseen-Frank discrete gradient outperforms the other two discrete gradients in length error and efficiency.
On the other hand, we examine the dynamics with different elastic constants, and find that anisotropic elasticity can lead to completely different dynamics.
Since the Oseen–Frank gradient flow characterizes the dissipation of the unit vector field in the Ericksen–Leslie model, the proposed method is expected to be a cornerstone for efficient and robust schemes of the Ericksen–Leslie equation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce an equivalent unconstrained rotational form of general gradient flows with unit-vector constraint. We then present the Rdg scheme for temporal discretization of the reformulated system and rigorously prove that it is strictly length preserving and unconditionally energy stable. A specifically designed second-order Oseen-Frank discrete gradient approximation for the energy variation is presented. Efficient time-adaptive strategy is also discussed. In Section
3
, we conduct ample numerical experiments of Oseen-Frank gradient flow involving highly disparate elastic coefficients to demonstrate the accuracy, efficiency, exact length preservation and energy stability of Rdg method. Concluding remarks are given in Section
4
.

2.
The rotational discrete gradient method

In this section, we shall develop an unconditionally energy-stable and length-preserving rotational discrete gradient method (Rdg) for the system (
1.3
).
The proposed method is constructed upon the rotational form of the system (
1.3
) where the length constraint becomes intrinsic.
The rotational form leads to a natural second-order time discretization that maintains the vector length while keeping the structure of the gradient flow.
As a fine result, the techniques for energy dissipative schemes can be built in.
Since the discretization for the rotational form is already nonlinear, it is meaningless to pursue linear discretization of the variational derivative.
Thus, we choose to construct discrete gradients that are second-order accurate in time to arrive at the rotational discrete gradient method.
This method can be applied to general length-constrained gradient flows without major difficulty.
Below, we first write down the rotational form and the corresponding discretization, followed by discussions on discrete gradients.

2.1.
Rotational form

We begin with writing down the rotational form and showing the equivalence with system (
1.3
).

Proposition 2.1
.

The system (
1.3
) is equivalent to the following unconstrained equation

\bm{n}_{t}=-\Big(\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)\times\bm{n},

(2.1)

provided that the initial vector field is of unit length, i.e.
|\bm{n}(\bm{x},0)|=1.

Proof.

On one hand, we start from (
1.3
) to derive (
2.1
).
Using the identity of double cross product, we have

(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})\times\bm{a}=|\bm{a}|^{2}\bm{b}-(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{a})\bm{a}=\bm{b}\cdot(|\bm{a}|^{2}I-\bm{a}\otimes\bm{a}),\quad\forall\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(2.2)

Taking the right cross product of
\bm{n}
on both sides of (
1.3a
), we obtain

\bm{n}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\big(|\bm{n}|^{2}\big)_{t}\,\bm{n}=-\Big(\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)\times\bm{n}.

(2.3)

The constraint (
1.3b
) implies
\big(|\bm{n}|^{2}\big)_{t}=0
, so that we arrive at (
2.1
).

On the other hand, one can recover (
1.3
) from (
2.1
).
Taking the left cross product by
\bm{n}
on (
2.1
), we deduce that

\bm{n}\times\bm{n}_{t}=-\bm{n}\times\Big(\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)\times\bm{n}=-\bigg(\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}-\Big((\bm{n}\times\bm{n})\cdot\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)\bm{n}\bigg)=-\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}},

(2.4)

for which we have utilized (
2.2
) and the following identities

\bm{a}\times\bm{b}\cdot\bm{c}=\bm{c}\times\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b},\quad\bm{a}\times\bm{a}=0,\quad\forall\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(2.5)

Then, we take the dot product with
\bm{n}
on (
2.1
), which yields

\frac{1}{2}\big(|\bm{n}|^{2}\big)_{t}=-\Big(\bm{n}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big)\times\bm{n}\cdot\bm{n}=0,

where identities in (
2.5
) have also been used.
Together with the initial condition that
|\bm{n}(\bm{x},0)|=1
, the length constraint is derived, so that we obtain the original constrained system (
1.3
).
∎

Next, let us recognize the gradient flow structure from the rotational form.
The right-hand side of (
2.1
) is actually the opposite of variational derivative under the constraint
|\bm{n}|=1
.
Actually, let us consider a general energy functional
\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}(\bm{x})]
under the constraint
|\bm{v}|=C
is constant.
Choose a parameterized curve
\bm{v}(\bm{x},s)
such that
|\bm{v}(\bm{x},s)|=C
and
\bm{v}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{v}
.
The definition of variational derivative without constraint yields

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}(\bm{x},s)]-\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}(\bm{x},0)]}{s}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\cdot\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}dV.

(2.6)

Taking the derivative w.r.t.
s
on
|\bm{v}(\bm{x},s)|^{2}=C^{2}
yields

\bm{v}\cdot\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}=0.

(2.7)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\cdot\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}dV=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\cdot\Big(I-\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\otimes\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\Big)\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\Omega}\Big(I-\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\otimes\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\Big)\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\cdot\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\Big)\times\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\cdot\frac{d\bm{v}}{ds}dV,

(2.8)

where for the last equality we have used (
2.2
).
Note that
\big(I-\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\otimes\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\big)\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}=\big(\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}\times\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}[\bm{v}]}{\delta\bm{v}}\big)\times\frac{\bm{v}}{|\bm{v}|}
lies within the tangent space of the surface
|\bm{v}|=C
, it indeed gives the variational derivative under the constraint.

The two properties of (
2.1
) is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

The gradient flow (
2.1
) with initial condition
|\bm{n}(\bm{x},0)|=1
obeys two fundamental physical properties, i.e.

(1)

the length constraint
|\bm{n}(\bm{x},t)|=1
for
t\geq 0
;

(2)

the energy dissipation law

\frac{d\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{dt}=-\int_{\Omega}\Big|\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\times\bm{n}\Big|^{2}dV\leq 0.

(2.9)

Proof.

The length constraint has been shown above.
For the energy dissipation, we take inner product with
{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}/{\delta\bm{n}}
on (
2.1
) to obtain

\frac{d\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{dt}=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\cdot\bm{n}\times\Big(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\times\bm{n}\Big)dV=-\int_{\Omega}\Big|\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}\times\bm{n}\Big|^{2}dV\leq 0,

(2.10)

where the elementary identity (
2.5
) has been invoked.
∎

2.2.
Length-preserving and energy stable discretization

We now propose a second-order unconditionally energy stable and length-preserving scheme for the rotational form (
2.1
) of the gradient flow.
Denote by
\tau
the time step, and use the superscript
m
to represent the approximation at the time
t_{m}
.

Based on the rotational form (
2.1
), we consider the following discretization:

\frac{\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}}{\tau}=-\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\times\Big(D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\times\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\Big),

(2.11)

where
\bm{n}^{m+1/2}=(\bm{n}^{m+1}+\bm{n}^{m})/2
, and
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
is a discretized variational derivative that will be discussed later.
Regardless of how the
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
is constructed, the above discretization automatically keeps the vector length.
Indeed, we take dot product by
\bm{n}^{m+1/2}
. The right-hand side vanishes due to (
2.2
), yielding

|\bm{n}^{m+1}|^{2}-|\bm{n}^{m}|^{2}=0.

(2.12)

Therefore, we could focus on
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
targeting the energy dissipation.
We consider the discretized variational derivative satisfying the following relation.

\int_{\Omega}D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV\geq\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}].

(2.13)

Theorem 2.2
.

The scheme (
2.11
) satisfying (
2.13
) is length-preserving and energy stable, i.e. for
m=1,2,\cdots
, it holds

(1)

|\bm{n}^{m+1}|=|\bm{n}^{m}|,\;\;

(2)

\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}]\leq-\tau\Big\|D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\times\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\Big\|^{2}
.

Proof.

The first property has been shown above.
Using (
2.13
) and (
2.5
), we take the inner product between (
2.1
) and
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
, which directly leads to the desired energy dissipation law.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem
2.2
does not rely on the specific form of the energy. It holds for general gradient flows constrained on a sphere.

What is remaining is to construct a
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
satisfying (
2.13
).
Since we no longer need to care about the constraint, the techniques for energy dissipation schemes can be adopted.
The form of (
2.11
) indicates that the scheme is already nonlinear and could be second-order accurate in time.
Therefore, we shall aim to obtain a second-order approximation
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}
such that the equality holds for (
2.13
), i.e.

\int_{\Omega}D_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV=\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}].

(2.14)

To this end, the discrete gradient technique is the most suitable approach, and we would like to call it the rotational discrete gradient (Rdg) scheme.

2.3.
Construction of discrete gradients

In what follows, we consider three different kinds of discrete gradients, which satisfy the energy difference relation (
2.14
).

2.3.1.
Common approaches

There are two well-known approaches in the literature.
The first is the mean-value discrete gradient
[
21
,
7
]
:

D^{M}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}\big[(1-s)\bm{n}^{m+1}+s\bm{n}^{m}\big]ds.

(2.15)

The relation (
2.14
) is deduced by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}\big[(1-s)\bm{n}^{m+1}+s\bm{n}^{m}\big]\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})ds\,dV

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d\mathcal{F}}{ds}\big[(1-s)\bm{n}^{m+1}+s\bm{n}^{m}\big]ds\,dV=\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}].

The implementation of the mean-value discrete gradient relies on numerical integration. For instance, one can adopt the Gauss quadrature as follows

D^{M}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{g}}w_{k}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\bm{n}^{m}+\bm{n}^{m+1})+\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}(\bm{n}^{m}-\bm{n}^{m+1})\Big],

(2.16)

where
\{\xi_{k},w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{g}}
are the Legendre-Gauss points and weights, respectively. Thus, in practice, the mean-value discrete gradient preserves the discrete energy dissipation law up to a Gauss numerical quadrature error. In addition, the evaluation of numerical integration requires calculating
N_{g}
energy variations. Even if we choose
N_{g}=2
, it results in a substantial increase in the computational cost in each iteration when we solve the nonlinear scheme. This deficiency will be demonstrated clearly through numerical experiments in Section
3
.

The second is the Gonzalez discrete gradient
[
18
]
:

D^{G}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}]-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}]\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV}{\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)\cdot\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)dV}(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})+\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}].

(2.17)

It is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}D^{G}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}]-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}]\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV}{\int_{\Omega}\big|\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big|^{2}dV}\int_{\Omega}|\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}|^{2}dV

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}]\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}].

For the Gonzalez’s discrete gradient, the computational cost is lower than the mean-value discrete gradient.
However, the Gonzalez’s discrete gradient is more sensitive to roundoff errors and suffers from convergence issue, as will be shown in Section
3
. Actually, one can aware that when the gradient flow system tends to equilibrium, the term
\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)\cdot\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)dV
on the denominator of equation (
2.17
) may become rather small, causing severe loss of significant digits. Practically, one often modifies equation (
2.17
) by

D^{G}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}]-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}]\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV}{\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)\cdot\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\big)dV+\varepsilon_{0}}(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})+\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\bm{n}}[\bm{n}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}].

(2.18)

with
\varepsilon_{0}
a user-specified small value.

2.3.2.
The Oseen-Frank discrete gradient

We recommend using a discrete gradient deduced from the specific form of the Oseen–Frank energy. As indicated by numerical tests in Section
3.2
, the proposed discrete gradient outperforms the previous two candidates in reducing the computational cost and enhancing the length preservation.
To facilitate the derivation below, we write down the integration by parts involving the curl operator,

\int_{\Omega}\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{v}dV=\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nu}\times\bm{v}dS+\int_{\Omega}\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{u}dV.

(2.19)

where we use
\bm{\nu}
to represent the outward normal unit vector.
To fix the idea, we assume periodic boundary conditions so that the last term in (
1.2
) does not appear and surface integrals vanish when doing integration by parts.
In this case, the variational derivative is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]}{\delta\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=-k_{1}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n})+k_{2}\Big((\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n})(\nabla\times\bm{n})+\nabla\times\big((\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n})\bm{n}\big)\Big)

(2.20)

\displaystyle+k_{3}\Big((\nabla\times\bm{n})\times(\bm{n}\times\nabla\times\bm{n})+\nabla\times\big((\bm{n}\times\nabla\times\bm{n})\times\bm{n}\big)\Big).

Proposition 2.2
.

The Oseen-Frank discrete gradient takes the form

\displaystyle D^{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle-k_{1}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m+1/2})+{k_{2}}\Big\{\beta^{m+1/2}\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}+\nabla\times(\beta^{m+1/2}\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\Big\}

(2.21)

\displaystyle+{k_{3}}\Big\{(\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\times\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}+\nabla\times\big(\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}\big)\Big\},

where
\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}
and
\beta^{m+1/2}
are defined as

\begin{split}&\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}=\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}+\bm{n}^{m}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}\big)/2,\\
&\beta^{m+1/2}=\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}+\bm{n}^{m}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}\big)/2.\end{split}

(2.22)

It is a second-order approximation of the Oseen-Frank energy variation (
2.20
) and satisfies the discrete energy difference relation

\int_{\Omega}D^{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV=\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}].

(2.23)

Proof.

Let us first construct the proposed discrete gradient and show that it satisfies the discrete energy relation (
2.14
). Neglecting the boundary terms, one splits the Oseen-Frank energy functional (
1.2
) into three parts

\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}]=\frac{k_{1}}{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}]+\frac{k_{2}}{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}[\bm{n}]+\frac{k_{3}}{2}\mathcal{F}_{3}[\bm{n}]

(2.24)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}]=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\bm{n})^{2}dV,\quad\mathcal{F}_{2}[\bm{n}]=(\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n})^{2}dV,\quad\mathcal{F}_{3}[\bm{n}]=(\bm{n}\times\nabla\times\bm{n})^{2}dV.

(2.25)

For the first term, it is straightforward to derive that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}^{m}]=\int(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m+1}+\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m})(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m+1}-\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int\nabla\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\nabla\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV=\int-2\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV,

where integration by parts is done in the last equality. Let us define

D_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=-2\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}^{m+1/2})

(2.26)

and one readily observes that

\int D_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV=\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{1}[\bm{n}^{m}].

(2.27)

For the second term, one has the identity

\displaystyle|\bm{n}^{m+1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}|^{2}-|\bm{n}^{m}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\Big((\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}+\bm{n}^{m+1/2}\cdot\nabla\times(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})\Big)\beta^{m+1/2},

where
\beta^{m+1/2}
is defined in equation (
2.22
).
Thus, using (
2.19
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{2}[\bm{n}^{m}]=2\int\Big\{\beta^{m+1/2}\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}+\nabla\times(\beta^{m+1/2}\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\Big\}\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV.

Similarly, one can define the associated discrete gradient for the second term

D_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+1/2}=2\Big\{\beta^{m+1/2}\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}+\nabla\times(\beta^{m+1/2}\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\Big\}.

(2.28)

For the third term, we begin with

\displaystyle|\bm{n}^{m+1}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}|^{2}-|\bm{n}^{m}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}|^{2}=\big(\bm{n}^{m+1}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}\big)\cdot 2\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}.

We express

\displaystyle\bm{n}^{m+1}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m}=(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})\times\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}+\bm{n}^{m+1/2}\times\nabla\times(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m}).

Then we use (
2.5
) and (
2.19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}[\bm{n}^{m+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{3}[\bm{n}^{m}]

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\big((\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\times\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\big)\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})+(\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\cdot\nabla\times(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\big((\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\times\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\big)\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})+\nabla\times(\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\cdot(\bm{n}^{m+1}-\bm{n}^{m})dV.

Thus, we can define

D_{\mathcal{F}_{3}}(\bm{n})\Big|^{m+1/2}=2\Big\{(\nabla\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2})\times\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}+\nabla\times\big(\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}\times\bm{n}^{m+1/2}\big)\Big\}.

(2.29)

Combing equations (
2.26
), (
2.28
) and (
2.29
), one arrives at the proposed Oseen-Frank discrete gradient (
2.21
)-(
2.22
) and the discrete energy relation (
2.14
).

Notice that the form of the discrete gradient resembles that of the energy variation (
2.20
) at time step
m+1/2
.
In particular, the terms
\bm{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}\times\nabla\times\bm{n}
are replaced by
\beta^{m+1/2}
and
\bm{\omega}^{m+1/2}
in equation (
2.22
), respectively, which follows the trapezoid formula.

Thus, it is straightforward to see that the proposed Oseen-Frank discrete gradient is of second-order accuracy of the continuous energy variation (
2.20
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The derivation also applies for Dirichlet boundary conditions or natural boundary conditions (see (
5.3
)), which we explain briefly in Appendix.

Remark 2.3
.

We only present time discretization. For spatial discretization, we use the Fourier spectral method since we adopt periodic boundary conditions.
For boundary conditions of other types, it has no essential difficulty to incorporate spatial discretizations of Galerkin type with Lagrange basis at discrete points.
In this case, the preservation of length constraint is guaranteed at these discrete points.

2.4.
Time adaptive solution algorithm

The proposed Rdg scheme (
2.11
) is nonlinear, we can solve the resultant nonlinear system efficiently by inexact Newton-Krylov (INK) method combined with Armijo line search technique (see e.g.
[
24
]
). It is worthwhile to point out that though the Rdg scheme is unconditionally energy stable, a large time step size may slow down the INK solution algorithm.
Moreover, numerical experiments in the next section demonstrate that the vector field often exhibits multi-stage evolution phenomenon. It may evolve quickly in transient stage and remain almost unchanged for the rest of the simulation time. Thus, it is highly desirable to adjust the time step sizes adaptively for efficient and accurate simulations. Inspired by
[
31
]
, we propose the following time-adaptivity strategy

\tau_{m+1}={\rm max}\bigg(\tau_{\rm min},\frac{\tau_{\rm max}}{\sqrt{1+\alpha|(\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m}]-\mathcal{F}[\bm{n}^{m-1}])/\tau_{m}|^{2}}}\bigg),

(2.30)

where
\tau_{m}
is the
m
-th time step,
\alpha
is a constant chosen to adjust the speed of change of the step size, and
\tau_{\rm max}
and
\tau_{\rm min}
are the upper and lower bounds of the time step sizes, respectively.

3.
Numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct several numerical experiments to demonstrate the accuracy, efficiency and robustness of the proposed rotational discrete gradient method for the Oseen-Frank gradient flows, with particular emphasis on its properties of energy stability and exact length preservation of the vector field.
A manufactured solution is firstly employed to show the spatial and temporal convergence rates of this method with the mean-value discrete gradient (
2.15
) (dubbed as ”MvRdg” method), the Gonzalez’s discrete gradient (
2.17
) (dubbed as ”GonRdg” method) and the proposed Oseen-Frank discrete gradient (
2.21
) (dubbed as ”OFRdg” method).
In MvRdg, we set the number of the Gauss quadrature points
N_{g}=4
in order to acquire adequate accuracy.
The performance of the rotational discrete gradient method with these three different discrete gradients are compared to show the superiority of the proposed OFRdg method on accuracy and robustness.
The efficiency of the adaptive time stepping strategy is also demonstrated using simulations with various initial conditions and elastic coefficients
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
.
Moreover, we investigate the influence of the elastic constants on the dynamics of vector field, where multi-stage evolution phenomena of the vector field can be observed, especially when the magnitudes of the elastic coefficients are highly disparate.

4.
Concluding Remarks

We propose a second-order rotational discrete gradient method for the Oseen–Frank gradient flow, unconditionally preserving the unit vector constraint and energy dissipation.
The scheme is built by rewriting the gradient flow in the rotational form.
A discrete gradient specifially designed for the Oseen–Frank energy is given, which proves to be more efficient and robust than the two general formulae for discrete gradients by several numerical experiments.
The role of anisotropic elasticity is examined in a couple of cases, where distinct dynamical behaviors are observed.
The effect of anisotropic elasticity on dynamics would be an interesting problem to investigate further, for which the scheme we propose here is believed to be a useful tool.
The scheme is also expected to be a fundamental step for the design of efficient and robust schemes for the Ericksen–Leslie equation involving the incompressible flow, which we aim to study in the near future.
