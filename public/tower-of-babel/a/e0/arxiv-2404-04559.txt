Spectral GNN via Two-dimensional (2-D) Graph Convolution
Guoming Li, Jian Yang, Shangsong Liang, Dongsheng Luo

Abstract
Spectral Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have achieved tremendous success in graph learning. As an essential part of spectral GNNs, spectral graph convolution extracts crucial frequency information in graph data, leading to superior performance of spectral GNNs in downstream tasks. However, in this paper, we show that existing spectral GNNs remain critical drawbacks in performing the spectral graph convolution. Specifically, considering the spectral graph convolution as a construction operation towards target output, we prove that existing popular convolution paradigms cannot construct the target output with mild conditions on input graph signals, causing spectral GNNs to fall into suboptimal solutions. To address the issues, we rethink the spectral graph convolution from a more general two-dimensional (2-D) signal convolution perspective and propose a new convolution paradigm, named 2-D graph convolution. We prove that 2-D graph convolution unifies existing graph convolution paradigms, and is capable to construct arbitrary target output. Based on the proposed 2-D graph convolution, we further propose ChebNet2D, an efficient and effective GNN implementation of 2-D graph convolution through applying Chebyshev interpolation. Extensive experiments on benchmark datasets demonstrate both effectiveness and efficiency of the ChebNet2D.

Introduction

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
(
Wu et al., 2021
)
have emerged as a powerful tool in machine learning, adept at handling structured data represented as graphs.
Among these, spectral GNNs, which process graph data as signals on graph (namely graph signals) in the frequency domain, have shown remarkable performance in various graph learning tasks
(
Defferrard et al., 2016b
;
Wang et al., 2018
;
Cao et al., 2020
;
Cao et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2022b
;
Tang et al., 2022
)
.
These methods leverage the principles of Graph Signal Processing (GSP)
(
Ortega et al., 2018
)
to perform the key spectral graph convolution
(
Shi & Moura, 2019
)
to handle the graph signals, significantly enhancing learning outcomes.
However, a notable gap exists in this spectral graph convolution, the core component of spectral GNNs.
In the conventional GSP domain, the spectral graph convolution is designed on vector-type graph signals
(
Gama et al., 2020
;
Dong et al., 2020
)
.
In contrast, practical graph learning tasks often deal with matrix-type signals, such as node feature matrices
(
Hamilton et al., 2018
;
Wu et al., 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Zhu et al., 2022
)
.
This discrepancy leads to varying convolution paradigms in existing spectral GNNs that have been implemented (discussed in Section
2.2
), which further raises a critical question:

Are those existing spectral graph convolution paradigms appropriate for practical matrix-type graph signals?

Motivated by this question, in this paper, we revisit the popular convolution paradigms of existing spectral GNNs and show the critical issues of those paradigms.
Specifically, considering the spectral graph convolution as construction operation to the target output, we prove that existing convolution paradigms cannot construct the target output when mild conditions on input graph signals hold, which further causes spectral GNNs to fall into suboptimal solutions.

To address the critical issues of existing convolution paradigms, we rethink the spectral graph convolution from a more general two-dimensional (2-D) signal convolution perspective, and propose a new convolution paradigm, named 2-D graph convolution.
Theoretically, we prove that the proposed 2-D graph convolution unifies the popular spectral graph convolution paradigms, and is always capable of constructing arbitrary target output.
Moreover, we prove that the parameter number of 2-D graph convolution is irreducible for constructing arbitrary target output, which examines the parameter efficiency of our 2-D graph convolution.

Further, we propose ChebNet2D, a novel spectral GNN applying the 2-D graph convolution.
ChebNet2D implements the convolution operators in 2-D graph convolution with Chebyshev interpolation, leading to a noticeably streamlined model architecture with marginal complexity increase against other spectral GNNs.
By conducting node classification experiments on
18
datasets, we show that ChebNet2D outperforms SOTA methods, verifying the superiority of 2-D graph convolution against other convolution paradigms.

2.1
Spectral Graph Convolution in Graph Signal Processing

Graph signal processing (GSP)
is developed to analyze and process the challenging
graph signals
, i.e., the signals generated on graphs and accompanied with complicated topologies
(
Ortega et al., 2018
;
Dong et al., 2020
)
.
As a fundamental technique in GSP,
spectral graph convolution

(
Shi & Moura, 2019
;
Gama et al., 2020
)
processes graph signals by performing convolution operation (also called filtering operation) on them, which is defined based on
graph Fourier transform (GFT)
from spectral graph theory
(
Chung, 1997
)
.
Specifically, given an
N
-dimensional graph signal vector
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
on graph
\mathcal{G}
, the GFT of
\boldsymbol{f}
and its inverse transform are formulated as below:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}\ ,

(1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{U}\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}\ ,

(2)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{f}}
denotes the GFT of
\boldsymbol{f}
, and
\boldsymbol{U}
is eigenvectors of graph Laplacian.
The GFT transforms graph signals from the original vertex domain into an alternative
frequency domain
, whilst providing a novel perspective in analyzing graph signals.
Based on the GFT, the spectral graph convolution can be thereby defined: given graph signal
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
on graph
\mathcal{G}
and
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
called
spectral graph filter
, the spectral graph convolution of
\boldsymbol{f}
with filter
\boldsymbol{g}
is formulated as
(
Shi & Moura, 2019
;
Gama et al., 2020
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}\ast_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}\doteq\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{f}\ ,

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
is called
convolution operator
associated with filter
\boldsymbol{g}
on graph
\mathcal{G}
.

2.2
Spectral Graph Neural Networks via Spectral Graph Convolution

As an important branch of GNNs, spectral GNNs process the graph signals through conducting spectral graph convolution operation as Eq.
3
.
Notably, the practical graph signals in graph learning tasks are generally in matrix forms (called node feature)
(
Hamilton et al., 2018
;
Wu et al., 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Zhu et al., 2022
)
, while conventional spectral graph convolution is defined on vector-type signals (see Eq.
3
).
This gap leads to diverse spectral graph convolution paradigms proposed in previous spectral GNNs.
Specifically, let
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
be input graph signal matrix and convolution output, there are three popular convolution paradigms in previous spectral GNNs:

•

Paradigm (I):

\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F}\ ,

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}
is convolution operator.
As the most widely-used paradigm of spectral GNNs, Paradigm
(I)
has achieved great success on graph learning tasks
(
Gasteiger et al., 2019
;
Zhu & Koniusz, 2021
;
Zhu et al., 2021
;
Chien et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2022a
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
.

•

Paradigm (II):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{R}\ ,

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
denotes linear transformation on
\boldsymbol{F}
.
This linear transformation
\boldsymbol{R}
models column-wise correlation of the graph signals, leading to better performance of spectral GNNs
(
Monti et al., 2017
;
Wu et al., 2019
;
Dong et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2021b
;
Yang et al., 2022
)
.

•

Paradigm (III):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}_{:c}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{F}_{:c}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}^{(c)}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(c)})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
,
c=1,2,...,C
, are individual convolution operators on each column of
\boldsymbol{F}
.
By applying diverse operators to performing individual filtering on each column of
\boldsymbol{F}
, spectral GNNs as Paradigm
(III)
have achieved promising results
(
Xu et al., 2019
;
Zhao et al., 2021
;
Wang & Zhang, 2022
)
.

To match our analysis with majority of research on spectral GNNs, we focus on the mainstream model architecture of spectral GNNs that decouples feature transformation and spectral graph convolution, which means
\boldsymbol{F}
is obtained by conducting dimension reduction on node feature
\boldsymbol{X}

(
Gasteiger et al., 2019
;
Zhu & Koniusz, 2021
;
Zhu et al., 2021
;
Chien et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2021
;
Lei et al., 2022
;
Wang & Zhang, 2022
;
He et al., 2022a
;
Zheng et al., 2023
;
Huang et al., 2023
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
.

3
Two-dimensional (2-D) Graph Convolution

In this section, we first analyze Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
,
(III)
from the perspective of target construction and prove that target output can
never
be constructed by those paradigms in certain cases.
After that, we rethink the target construction problem from the perspective of 2-D signal convolution and propose our 2-D graph convolution.
With rigorous theoretical analysis, we prove that 2-D graph convolution is a generalized paradigm of Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
, and can construct arbitrary output with
0
error.

3.1
Analyzing Convolution Paradigms in the Lens of Target Output Construction

By decomposing feature transformation and convolution operation, we restate the spectral graph convolution as a construction task to the target output with graph convolution operation.
Formally, let
\boldsymbol{F}
be graph signal obtained with feature transformation on
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
with parameter
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
be function that performs graph convolution, the goal of spectral graph convolution is to minimize the error as below:

\displaystyle||\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{F})-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}||_{F}\ ,

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
denotes the target output, and
||\cdot||_{F}
is Frobenius norm
(
Strang, 2006
)
.
Similar learning goal is introduced in previous works on spectral GNNs
(
Wang & Zhang, 2022
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
, while they focus on achieving minimal rather than
0
error with strong assumption on
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
can be constructed with existing convolution paradigms.
In contrast, our problem statement aims to achieve a more challenging setting with
0
error to arbitrary
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
.

Based on this problem statement, we analyze the capability of Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
from the perspective of target output construction, and obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
be the parameterized function that performs graph convolution as Paradigm
(I)
or
(II)
or
(III)
on graph signal matrix
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
denotes the parameters.
For the
\boldsymbol{F}
which is nontrivial on frequency, i.e., no zero row exists in
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}
, the convolution output
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{F})
fails to construct the target output
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
with certain conditions on
\boldsymbol{F}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
.
That is, in certain cases, the construction error
||\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}(\boldsymbol{F})-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}||_{F}
cannot achieve
0
for all parameter values of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
.

We prove this theorem in Appendix
A.1
.
Theorem
3.1
demonstrates that existing three paradigms contain issues in constructing the target output when certain conditions on
\boldsymbol{F}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
hold.
Moreover, note that
\boldsymbol{F}
is obtained with MLP or linear transformation on
\boldsymbol{X}
, and the target
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
is unseen towards the learning process.
Thus, both
\boldsymbol{F}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
contain substantial uncertainties on practical properties, causing unexpected risk of falling into a suboptimal solution to the model with Paradigm
(I)
or
(II)
or
(III)
.
In particular, we further achieve a corollary as follows:

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{T}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{T}^{(3)}
be the parameterized functions performing spectral graph convolution as Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
, respectively.
For the spectral graph convolution function constructed as
(\mathcal{T}^{(i)}+\mathcal{T}^{(j)})
,
i,j\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
, there are certain cases of
\boldsymbol{F}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
, making the convolution output
(\mathcal{T}^{(i)}+\mathcal{T}^{(j)})(\boldsymbol{F})
fails to construct the target output
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
for all parameter values.

Especially, for the convolution function constructed as
(\mathcal{T}^{(1)}+\mathcal{T}^{(2)}+\mathcal{T}^{(3)})
, the cases leading to failure on target output construction still exists.

The proof of this corollary is moved to Appendix
A.2
.
Corollary
3.2
further proves that the drawbacks cannot be tackled through naively increasing complexity with existing convolution paradigms.
Hence, based on the theoretical results above, developing an advanced paradigm of spectral graph convolution which can avoid potential failures on target output construction is thereby necessary.

3.2
From 2-D Signal Convolution to 2-D Graph Convolution

Instead of conventionally considering vector-wise convolution as Eq.
3
, we view
\boldsymbol{F}
as a whole two-dimensional (2-D) signal and start with the convolution operation on general 2-D signal.
Particularly, we consider fully-connected network (FCN), a fundamental framework for processing 2-D signals (e.g., image signals), which can be considered as a general paradigm of convolution on 2-D signals
(
Goodfellow et al., 2016
;
Ma & Lu, 2017
;
Petersen & Voigtlaender, 2020
)
.
Specifically, given input 2-D signal
\boldsymbol{F}
, the output of FCN convolution
\boldsymbol{Z}
is formulated as follows:

\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}\boldsymbol{F}_{nc}\ .

(8)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}
is the element of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(i,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
denoting the coefficient matrix for generating
\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}
.
Numerous works have demonstrated the significant capability and necessity of FCN convolution on 2-D signal processing
(
Sermanet & LeCun, 2011
;
Ciregan et al., 2012
;
Krizhevsky et al., 2012
;
Lin et al., 2015
;
Li et al., 2021a
;
An et al., 2022
)
.
Specially, we derive Eq.
8
to a more intuitive paradigm as follows:

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{Z}\right)=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1,1)}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(C,1)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1,C)}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(C,C)}\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\ .

Here,
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
(
c,j=1,2,...,C
) is coefficient matrix whose elements
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in}
are equal to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}
in Eq.
8
;
\textit{Vec}(\cdot)
denotes the vectorization operator
(
Strang, 2006
)
.
We prove the equivalence between Eq.
8
and Eq.
3.2
in Appendix
A.3
.
Note that operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}
in Eq.
3.2
performs convolution operation to the columns of
\boldsymbol{F}
, which is consistent to the vector-wise convolution as Eq.
3
.
Based on this insight, we thereby replace
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}
with graph convolution operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
, thus proposing our 2-D graph convolution defined as follows:

Definition 3.3
.

(2-D Graph Convolution)
The 2-D graph convolution on input graph signal matrix
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times C}
is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1,1)}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(C,1)}_{\mathcal{G}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1,C)}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(C,C)}_{\mathcal{G}}\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\right)\ ,

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
,
c,j=1,2,...,C
, is graph convolution operator with parameterized graph filter
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}
denotes convolution output,
\textit{Vec}^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse of
\textit{Vec}(\cdot)
making the equation

(\textit{Vec}\circ\textit{Vec}^{-1})(\boldsymbol{M})=(\textit{Vec}^{-1}\circ\textit{Vec})(\boldsymbol{M})=\boldsymbol{M}\

(15)

holds for arbitrary matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
.

Similar to Eq.
8
, the 2-D graph convolution constructs
\boldsymbol{Z}
through involving all elements of the input
\boldsymbol{F}
, making it a novel spectral graph convolution paradigm on 2-D graph signals.
In the following section, we will show the proposed 2-D graph convolution is a generalized paradigm of the mentioned paradigms and has significant capability.

3.3
Theoretical Investigation of 2-D Graph Convolution

3.3.1
2-D graph convolution as generalized convolution operation

We refer to the proposed 2-D graph convolution as a generalized graph convolution framework of Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
, which is summarized in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.4
.

By adjusting the
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
with specific constraints, 2-D graph convolution can perform the three graph convolution paradigms, i.e., Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
.

We prove the Proposition
3.4
holds with derivation in Appendix
A.4
.
Thus, based on the Proposition
3.4
, we show that our 2-D graph convolution unifies the existing popular graph convolution paradigms into one generalized framework, where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
serves as key role in such representation.

3.3.2
2-D graph convolution on error-free target output construction

With considering the convolution operation as target output construction with input and convolution parameters, we call that the proposed 2-D graph convolution is error-free construction towards arbitrary target output.
Specifically, our statement is guaranteed by the following theorem:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{F}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
be the same definitions as Theorem
3.1
, and
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
denotes the function performing 2-D graph convolution as Eq.
3.3
, where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
denotes parameter set.
There always exists at least one parameter value of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
(denoted as
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
), making
\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}(\boldsymbol{F})|_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}=\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
.
That is, the 2-D graph convolution is always capable of constructing
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
with
0
construction error.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
A.5
.
Theorem
3.5
shows that the representation range of 2-D graph convolution always covers the target output, demonstrating the significant capability of 2-D graph convolution on (arbitrary) target output construction.
Furthermore, compared to other paradigms associated with certain failure cases in constructing target output (Theorem
3.1
, Corollary
3.2
), our 2-D graph convolution is capable to perform
0
construction error, demonstrating its superiority against other paradigms.

3.3.3
Irreducibility of parameter number in 2-D graph convolution

We call the number of parameters in 2-D graph convolution irreducible for performing
0
construction error towards target output, which is summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
be the parameter set of 2-D graph convolution.
For each subset
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}\subset\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, let
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}}
be the 2-D graph convolution with partial parameters
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
(i.e., parameters in
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
are constant or shared by
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
).
For any
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
, there always exists certain cases of
\boldsymbol{F}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
, making construction error
||\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}}(\boldsymbol{F})-\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}||_{F}
cannot achieve
0
for all parameter values of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
.

We prove this theorem in Appendix
A.6
.
Theorem
3.6
proves that any special cases of the 2-D graph convolution, e.g., Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
, are inappropriate paradigm on target output construction.
Therefore, despite involving more parameters than other paradigms, the proposed 2-D graph convolution is reasonable and necessary.
On the other hand, since the paradigms with adding further parameters on 2-D graph convolution can also achieve
0
construction error, the irreducibility of parameter number implies that our 2-D graph convolution is parameter-efficient compared to more complicated paradigms achieving
0
error.

4
ChebNet2D

To implement the 2-D graph convolution in practical scenarios, in this section we propose ChebNet2D, a spectral GNN performing efficient and effective construction of 2-D graph convolution with Chebyshev polynomial
(
Rivlin, 2020
)
.
We highlight each essential part of ChebNet2D to provide a comprehensive introduction to our method.

4.1
Filter Construction via Polynomial Approximation

Chebyshev polynomial-based graph filter.
According to Eq.
3.3
, implementation of 2-D graph convolution involves
NC^{2}
number of learnable parameters, leading to critical issues in high model complexity.
Inspired by ChebNet
(
Defferrard et al., 2016a
)
, we perform efficient construction to the filter
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}
with the same Chebyshev polynomial approximation
(
Rivlin, 2020
)
, making the filter construction be
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}\doteq\sum_{d=0}^{D}\theta_{d}^{(c,j)}T_{d}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, where
T_{d}(\cdot)
is the
d
-th order Chebyshev polynomial with
\theta_{d}^{(c,j)}
as learnable coefficient, and
D
is the degree of truncated polynomial.
Accordingly, the convolution operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{d=0}^{D}\theta_{d}^{(c,j)}T_{d}(\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}),

(16)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}=\boldsymbol{L}-\boldsymbol{I}
is set to match the convergence domain of Chebyshev polynomial
(
Smyth, 1998
;
Phillips, 2003
;
Rivlin, 2020
)
.
With the polynomial approximation above, the parameter number of 2-D graph convolution is reduced to
(D+1)C^{2}
far less than the original
NC^{2}
, making significant improvement on parameter efficiency.
Moreover, Chebyshev polynomial maintains
orthogonality
on polynomial basis
(
Szeg, 1939
;
Chihara, 2011
)
, guaranteeing superior convergence rate on filter construction
(
Wang & Zhang, 2022
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
.

High quality construction with Chebyshev interpolation.
As discussed in
(
He et al., 2022a
)
, constructing graph filters with high degree Chebyshev polynomial may encounter Runge Phenomenon
(
Epperson, 1987
)
, which leads to a detrimental effect on the construction quality and can be alleviated with involving Chebyshev interpolation
(
Gil et al., 2007
)
defined as follows:

Definition 4.1
.

(
Chebyshev interpolation
)
(
He et al., 2022a
)
Given a continuous filter function
g(x)
defined on
(-1,1)
, let
x_{b}=\cos(\frac{b+1/2}{K+1}\pi)
,
b=0,1,...,D
denote the Chebyshev nodes for
T_{D+1}(x)
, i.e., the zeros of
T_{D+1}(x)
, and
g(x_{b})
denotes the function value at
x_{b}
.
The Chebyshev interpolation of
g(x)
is defined as below:

g(x)=\sum_{d=0}^{D}\theta_{d}^{{}^{\prime}}T_{d}(x),\ \theta_{d}=\frac{2}{D+1}\sum_{b=0}^{D}g(x_{b})T_{d}(x_{b}),

(17)

where the prime indicates the first term is to be halved, i.e.,
\theta_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}=\theta_{0}/2
,
\theta_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}=\theta_{1}
,…,
\theta_{D}^{{}^{\prime}}=\theta_{D}
.

Thus, similar to
(
He et al., 2022a
)
, through replacing
g(x_{b})
in Eq.
17
with learnable parameter
\theta_{b}^{(c,j)}
, the construction of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
with Chebyshev interpolation is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle\doteq\frac{2}{D+1}\sum_{d=0}^{D}\sum_{b=0}^{D}\theta_{b}^{(c,j)}T_{d}(x_{b})T_{d}(\hat{\boldsymbol{L}}).

(18)

Streamlined paradigm of 2-D graph convolution.
Though being simplified with polynomial approximation, Eq.
3.3
involves complicated vectorization operation and large size operator matrix, leading to critical complexity issues on practical implementation.
To achieve easy-to-implement framework, based on Eq.
18
, we reformulate a streamlined formulation of Eq.
3.3
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=\sum_{d=0}^{D}T_{d}(\hat{\boldsymbol{L}})\boldsymbol{F}\left(\sum_{b=0}^{D}T_{d}(x_{b})\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{::b}\right).

(19)

Here, the
3
D tensor
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C\times(D+1)}
is named
parameterized coefficient tensor
with
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{cjb}=\theta_{b}^{(c,j)}
.
We defer the derivation of obtaining Eq.
19
based on Eq.
3.3
and Eq.
18
in Appendix
A.7
.
Since
T_{d}(x_{b})
can be precomputed and
\sum_{b=0}^{D}T_{d}(x_{b})\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{::b}
involves only the sum of matrices, Eq.
19
provides a significantly streamlined paradigm for the implementation of 2-D graph convolution.

4.2
Overall Model of ChebNet2D

Based on Section
4.1
, we introduce the model paradigm of our ChebNet2D.
Specifically, given input graph
\mathcal{G}
with node feature
\boldsymbol{X}
and normalized graph Laplacian
\boldsymbol{L}
, the ChebNet2D generates output
\boldsymbol{Z}
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=\sum_{d=0}^{D}T_{d}(\hat{\boldsymbol{L}})h_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{X})\left(\sum_{b=0}^{D}T_{d}(x_{b})\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{::b}\right)\ ,

(20)

where
h_{\eta}
denotes an MLP with
\eta
as parameters.
With taking
\boldsymbol{F}=h_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{X})
, the ChebNet2D decouples feature transformation and spectral graph convolution, making the model consistent to our theoretical analysis.
Similar decoupling paradigm has been widely used in recent works and has achieved leading performance
(
Gasteiger et al., 2019
;
Chien et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2022a
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
, thereby guaranteeing the effectiveness of such paradigm.

Complexity analysis.
The computation complexity and parameter complexity of ChebNet2D are
\mathcal{O}(NH(F+C)+DN^{2}C+(D+1)NC^{2})
and
\mathcal{O}(H(F+C)+(D+1)C^{2})
respectively, where
H
denotes the hidden layer dimension of MLP,
C
is the output dimension, and
D
is the order of polynomial.
While the spectral GNNs with similar decoupling paradigm
(
Gasteiger et al., 2019
;
Chien et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2021
;
He et al., 2022a
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
are less complicated with
\mathcal{O}(NH(F+C)+DN^{2}C)
on computation complexity and
\mathcal{O}(H(F+C))
on parameter complexity, the ChebNet2D is still comparable in efficiency.
Specifically, as both
N
and
F
are significantly larger than
C
,
D
in practical scenarios, compared to the counterparts, the complexity increases on the ChebNet2D, i.e.,
(D+1)NC^{2}
and
(D+1)C^{2}
, are trivial against the major terms
NH(F+C)+DN^{2}C
and
H(F+C)
.
In the following section, by showing the training time of ChebNet2D and its counterparts, we verify the competitive efficiency of ChebNet2D.

5
Related Works

Spectral GNNs.
Spectral GNNs are GNNs that perform essential spectral graph convolution on input graph data (signals).
Previous research focused on designing various spectral graph filters used for the convolution operation, which are categorized into two classes following
(
He et al., 2021
;
Wang & Zhang, 2022
)
:

•

Spectral GNNs with fixed filters
: This class of spectral GNNs are constructed by involving pre-defined and fixed spectral graph filters, leading to significantly simple and easy-to-implement model architectures.
For instance, APPNP
(
Gasteiger et al., 2019
)
utilizes Personalized PageRank (PPR)
(
Page et al., 1999
)
to build filter functions.
ADC
(
Zhao et al., 2021
)
involves pre-defined convolution (diffusion) operators and constructs hidden representation with modifying variable propagation coefficients.
GNN-HF/LF
(
Zhu et al., 2021
)
constructs filter weights from the perspective of graph optimization functions, which simulates both high- and low-pass filters.

•

Spectral GNNs with learnable filters:
This class of spectral GNNs perform convolution with learnable filters adaptive to datasets, leading to flexible models and better task performance.
For instance, GPRGNN
(
Chien et al., 2021
)
learns a polynomial filter by directly performing gradient descent on the polynomial coefficients.
DSGC
(
Li et al., 2021b
)
performs both row-wise and column-wise graph convolution based on multi-dimensional GFT
(
Kurokawa et al., 2017
)
.
BernNet
(
He et al., 2021
)
expresses the filtering operation with Bernstein polynomials.
ChebNetII
(
He et al., 2022a
)
improves ChebNet
(
Defferrard et al., 2016a
)
with applying Chebyshev interpolation
(
Gil et al., 2007
)
.
JacobiConv
(
Wang & Zhang, 2022
)
removes non-linearity and applies Jacobian polynomial
(
Szeg, 1939
)
to perform column-wise convolution.
OptBasis
(
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
learns optimal polynomial basis specific to each dataset with Favard’s theory, leading to state-of-the-art GNN.

Non-spectral GNNs.
Non-spectral GNNs, also called spatial GNNs, are GNNs designed based on the principle of message-passing neural network (MPNN)
(
Gilmer et al., 2017
)
.
Previous works concentrated on performing message aggregation and propagation associated with the spatial structure of graphs
(
Wu et al., 2021
)
.
For instance, GCN
(
Kipf & Welling, 2017
)
are constructed through stacking
1
-order ChebNet layer
(
Defferrard et al., 2016a
)
and non-linearity functions.
GCNII
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
employs residual connection and identity mapping to GCN, leading to substantial deep-layer GNN with promising performance.
LINKX
(
Lim et al., 2021
)
is a simple MLP-based model that embeds both graph structure (adjacency) and node feature to generate output.
Nodeformer
(
Wu et al., 2022
)
propagates messages between arbitrary node pairs in layer-specific latent graphs, which is the first Transformer model that scales all-pair message passing to large graphs.
GloGNN++
(
Li et al., 2022
)
generates a node’s embedding by aggregating information from global nodes in the graph.

Conclusion

In this paper, we first revisit spectral graph convolution paradigms of existing spectral GNNs.
With regarding convolution operation as construction to target output, we prove that existing popular convolution paradigms cannot construct the target output with mild conditions on input graph signals, which causes spectral GNNs to fall into suboptimal solutions.
To address issues of existing convolution paradigms, we propose a new convolution paradigm named 2-D graph convolution.
The proposed 2-D graph convolution unifies previous convolution paradigms, and is provably capable to construct arbitrary output with significant parameter efficiency.
Based on 2-D graph convolution, we propose ChebNet2D, an effective and efficient spectral GNN implementation of 2-D graph convolution.
ChebNet2D outperforms previous SOTA spectral GNNs on
18
datasets, which verifies the superiority of our 2-D graph convolution against other convolution paradigms.

is unique regarding the index
\{c,j,i,n\}
, with comparison between Eq.
8
and Eq.
117
, we find a
surjective
mapping that maps
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in}
to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}
and satisfies
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)})
.
Furthermore, as both the number of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}
are
N\times N\times C\times C=N^{2}C^{2}
, the
surjective
mapping becomes
bijective
mapping, indicating
N^{2}C^{2}
unique mapping pairs
(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)})
that satisfy
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{in}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{nc}^{(i,j)}
.
Thus, the equivalence between Eq.
8
and Eq.
3.2
is proved.
∎

A.4
Proof of Proposition
3.4

Proof.

In this proof, we show how 2-D graph convolution can perform other graph convolution paradigms in the order of Paradigm
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
.

①-Performing Paradigm (I):

We set
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
with the following constraints:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,c)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(118)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{O}\ ,\quad c,j=1,2,...,C\ ,\quad c\neq j\ .

(119)

Thus, the 2-D graph convolution with the above constraints is formulated as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{O}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\right)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F},

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Column scale}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad\boldsymbol{Z}_{:c}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F}_{:c}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(123)

which is equivalent to Eq.
4
, i.e., Paradigm
(I)
.

②-Performing Paradigm (II):

We set
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
with the following constraints:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}=r_{j,c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}},\quad r_{j,c}\in\mathbb{R},\quad c,j=1,2,...,C\ .

(124)

Thus, the 2-D graph convolution with the above constraints is formulated as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}r_{1,1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&r_{C,1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
r_{1,C}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&r_{C,C}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Column scale}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad\boldsymbol{Z}_{:c}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{C}r_{j,c}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F}_{:j}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ .

(128)

With taking
r_{j,c}=\boldsymbol{R}_{jc}
, the above equation is equivalent to Eq.
5
, i.e., Paradigm
(II)
.

③-Performing Paradigm (III):

We set
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
with the following constraints:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,c)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c)}_{\mathcal{G}}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(129)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{O}\ ,\quad c,j=1,2,...,C\ ,\quad c\neq j\ .

(130)

Thus, the 2-D graph convolution with the above constraints is formulated as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{G}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{O}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{O}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(C)}_{\mathcal{G}}\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{F}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Column scale}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad\boldsymbol{Z}_{:c}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c)}_{\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{F}_{:c}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(134)

which is equivalent to Eq.
6
, i.e., Paradigm
(III)
.

Based on the derivations above, we prove that the 2-D graph convolution can perform other convolution paradigms with setting
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(c,j)}_{\mathcal{G}}
to be special cases, indicating our 2-D graph convolution is a generalized framework of formal spectral graph convolution.
∎

A.5
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

To begin with, note the graph convolution operator
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}
can be decomposed as
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathcal{G}}=\boldsymbol{U}diag(\boldsymbol{g})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
, we can thereby investigate an equivalent statement on frequency domain, i.e., constructing target
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
with given graph signal
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}
.
Next, we prove the Theorem
3.5
from the perspective of element construction of
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}
.
We first derive the 2-D graph convolution as Eq.
3.3
to be the following formulation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{Z}=

\displaystyle\textit{Vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(1,1)})&\cdots&diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(C,1)})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(1,C)})&\cdots&diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(C,C)})\end{array}\right]\cdot\textit{Vec}\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}\right)\right),

where
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the parameterized graph filter.
With the above formulation, proving the statement is thereby equivalent to proving the following relations hold:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{:c}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{C}diag(\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)})\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{:j}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(138)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Element scale}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{C}\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(139)

where
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}
denotes the
n
-th element of filter
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}
.

Note that
\boldsymbol{F}
is nontrivial on frequency, i.e., no zero row exists in
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}
, the
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}
(
j=1,2,...,C
) are not all
0
.
On the other hand,
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}
is unique towards
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}
in constructing
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}
, indicating the independence of parameter selection of
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}
.
Therefore, with considering the parameter
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, similar to solving linear equations, we can always choose appropriate
\{\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,1)}_{n},\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,2)}_{n},...,\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,C)}_{n}\}
to make Eq.
139
hold (and further make Eq.
138
hold), indicating that 2-D graph convolution can always construct all elements of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}
with
0
construction error.
Furthermore, note that the number of parameter values satisfying both Eq.
138
and Eq.
139
are at least one.
Therefore, for the parameter set of 2-D graph convolution, i.e.,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
, we can always find at least one appropriate parameter value
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, making
0
construction error hold.
∎

A.6
Proof of Theorem
3.6

Proof.

We provide constructive proof to Theorem
3.6
.
To begin with, following the proof of Theorem
3.5
above, we consider the element-wise construction of
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}
with 2-D graph convolution as below:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{C}\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}\ ,\quad c=1,2,...,C\ ,

(140)

where
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}
denotes the
n
-th element of filter
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}
.
Next, we divide the proof into two parts based on the mentioned two types of parameter reduction on
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
: ①-setting
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
to be constant; ②-sharing
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
to
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
.

①-Setting
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
to be constant:

Suppose
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
is set to be constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Accordingly, Eq.
140
can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}=

\displaystyle\underbrace{a\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}}_{\text{constant}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq C\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,i)}_{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ni}\ .

(141)

Based on the equation above, we can construct
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ni}=0\ ,\quad 1\leq i\leq C\ ,\quad i\neq j\ ,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}\neq 0\ ,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}\neq a\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}\ .

(142)

With the conditions above, Eq.
140

never
hold for all parameter values, indicating that failure cases exist in the 2-D graph convolution with partial constant parameters, and the proof of (I) is finished.

②-Sharing
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
to
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
:

Suppose the value of
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\in\boldsymbol{\Psi}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
is shared by
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c^{{}^{\prime}},j^{{}^{\prime}})}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}}\in\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\text{sub}}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\equiv\boldsymbol{g}^{(c^{{}^{\prime}},j^{{}^{\prime}})}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}}
.
Accordingly, we have seven different cases, i.e.,
(n\neq n^{{}^{\prime}},c\neq c^{{}^{\prime}},j\neq j^{{}^{\prime}})
,
(n\neq n^{{}^{\prime}},c\neq c^{{}^{\prime}},j=j^{{}^{\prime}})
,
(n\neq n^{{}^{\prime}},c=c^{{}^{\prime}},j\neq j^{{}^{\prime}})
,
(n\neq n^{{}^{\prime}},c=c^{{}^{\prime}},j=j^{{}^{\prime}})
,
(n=n^{{}^{\prime}},c\neq c^{{}^{\prime}},j\neq j^{{}^{\prime}})
,
(n=n^{{}^{\prime}},c\neq c^{{}^{\prime}},j=j^{{}^{\prime}})
and
(n=n^{{}^{\prime}},c=c^{{}^{\prime}},j\neq j^{{}^{\prime}})
.
Because the proofs of those cases are similar, for convenience, we provide the proof for the most complicated case
(n\neq n^{{}^{\prime}},c\neq c^{{}^{\prime}},j\neq j^{{}^{\prime}})
here.
Specifically, we can construct
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}
satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{ni}=0\ ,\quad 1\leq i\leq C\ ,\quad i\neq j\ ,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}\neq 0\ ,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}i^{{}^{\prime}}}=0\ ,\quad 1\leq i^{{}^{\prime}}\leq C\ ,\quad i^{{}^{\prime}}\neq j^{{}^{\prime}}\ ,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}j^{{}^{\prime}}}\neq 0\ ,

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}}\neq\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}c^{{}^{\prime}}}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}j^{{}^{\prime}}}}\ .

(143)

With the conditions above, achieving error-free construction implies the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{nc}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{nj}}=\boldsymbol{g}^{(c,j)}_{n}\neq\boldsymbol{g}^{(c^{{}^{\prime}},j^{{}^{\prime}})}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}}=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}c^{{}^{\prime}}}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n^{{}^{\prime}}j^{{}^{\prime}}}}\ .

(144)

which contradicts to the condition of sharing parameter.
Thus, in these special cases of
\hat{\boldsymbol{F}}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{*}
, the construction error
never
achieves
0
for all parameter values.
As the rest cases can be proved by constructing conditions similar to those in Eq.
143
, we complete the proof of (II).

Thus, based on ① and ②, we prove that reducing parameters in 2-D graph convolution results in indeed failure cases on target output construction, demonstrating that the 2-D graph convolution is irreducible in the number of parameters.
∎

Hyperparameter settings.
Both general hyperparameters and specific hyperparameters in baselines are same as the pipeline in Section
B.3
.
For specific hyperparameters in ChebNet2D, we optimize the layer number of MLP on
\left\{2,3\right\}
, hidden size of MLP on
\left\{128,256,512,1024,2048\right\}
, truncated polynomial order
D
on
\left\{2,4,8,10,12,16\right\}
.
All parameters are optimized with grid-search.

Training details.
We apply batch training for the experiments on large-scale datasets, where the batch training size (number of nodes) will be optimized on
\left\{10000,50000\right\}
.
Especially, for the training of ChebNet2D on large-scale datasets, we follow the scaled-up strategy used in
(
He et al., 2022a
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
and precompute polynomial bases to speed up the training.
For the training on the rest datasets, the settings of ChebNet2D are the same as Section
B.3
.
The same Cross Entropy loss, Adam optimizer, and training epoch settings are used as Section
B.3
.

B.5
Experimental Settings in Section
6.3

Hyperparameter settings.
Both general hyperparameters and specific hyperparameters in baselines are the same as the pipeline in Section
B.3
.
Please note that all baselines are re-implemented into same decoupling model architecture with MLP as feature transformation.
Thus, to keep fairness, we follow the same search range as ChebNet2D in Section
B.3
and
B.4
for the MLP in baselines, and involve similar batch training for experiments on large datasets, where all parameters are optimized by grid-search.

Training details.
To keep fair comparison, for experiments on large-scale datasets, we adopt the same scaled-up strategy as
(
He et al., 2022a
;
Guo & Wei, 2023
)
to all baselines, and precompute polynomial bases to speed up the training.
For experiments on the rest datasets, the same settings as ChebNet2D in Section
B.3
are used for all models.
The same Cross Entropy loss, Adam optimizer, and training epoch settings are used as Section
B.3
.
