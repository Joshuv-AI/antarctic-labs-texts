New graphical criterion for the selection of complete sets of polarization observables and its application to single-meson photoproduction as well as electroproduction
Y. Wunderlich

Abstract
This paper combines the graph-theoretical ideas behind Moravcsik’s theorem with a completely analytic derivation of discrete phase-ambiguities, recently published by Nakayama. The result is a new graphical procedure for the derivation of certain types of complete sets of observables for an amplitude-extraction problem with N N helicity-amplitudes. The procedure is applied to pseudoscalar meson photoproduction ( N = 4 N=4 amplitudes) and electroproduction ( N = 6 N=6 amplitudes), yielding complete sets with minimal length of 2 ​ N 2N observables. For the case of electroproduction, this is the first time an extensive list of minimal complete sets is published. Furthermore, the generalization of the proposed procedure to processes with a larger number of amplitudes, i.e. N > 6 N>6 amplitudes, is sketched. The generalized procedure is outlined for the next more complicated example of two-meson photoproduction ( N = 8 N=8 amplitudes).

An interesting alternative approach for the deduction of complete sets of observables is given by Moravcsik’s theorem
Moravcsik:1984uf
. This theorem has been reexamined in a recent work
Wunderlich:2020umg
, where it has received slight corrections for the case of an odd number of amplitudes
N
. The theorem is formulated in the language of a ’geometrical analog’

N
. Furthermore, it can be fully automated on a computer. However, the approach also has it’s drawbacks: for larger

(i.e.
N>6
)

;

;

;

(N-1)!/2

Wunderlich:2020umg
. This alone makes the computations quite expensive for more involved amplitude-extraction problems. Another drawback of (the modified form of) Moravcsik’s theorem consists of the fact that for
N\geq 4
, the derived complete sets do not have the minimal length
2N
any more, but are rather slightly over-complete (see in particular section VII of reference
Wunderlich:2020umg
). The reason for the latter fact is that Moravcsik directly considered just the bilinear products
b_{i}^{\ast}b_{j}
of amplitudes. However, polarization observables for
N\geq 4

The present work is an attempt to devise an approach similar to (the modified form of) Moravcsik’s theorem
Wunderlich:2020umg

2N

N\geq 4

even
number of amplitudes

. In order to achieve this, we combine the graph-theoretical ideas according to references
Moravcsik:1984uf
;
Wunderlich:2020umg

Nakayama:2018yzw

A crucial new ingredient for the procedure proposed in this work is that the graphs have to be embued with additional directional information. The graphical criterion formulated in this work, together with the types of graphs needed for it, are to our knowledge new.

VI
Conclusions and Outlook

We have introduced a generalization of the graphs originally introduced by Moravcsik
Moravcsik:1984uf
, which has lead to a new graphical criterion that allows for the determination of minimal complete sets of length
2N
, for an amplitude-extraction problem with
N\leq 6
complex amplitudes, where furthermore
N
has to be
even
. The new method rests heavily on the known discrete phase-ambiguities implied by the selection of any pair of observables from a non-diagonal shape-class composed of four quantities. In order to achieve the selection of minimal complete sets for
N\leq 6
, the considered graphs must be provided with additional directional information. Our new criterion has been applied to single-meson photoproduction (
N=4
amplitudes) as well as electroproduction (
N=6
amplitudes), with success. In particular, we were able to determine for the first time an extensive list of complete sets with minimal length
2N=12
for the case of electroproduction.

However, the generalization of our new criterion to problems involving a larger number of
N>6
amplitudes is difficult, due to the fact that one has to perform new and more involved algebraic derivations for the ambiguity structure implied by the then appearing larger shape-classes, i.e. with more than four observables in each class. It is possible to decouple such a problem, in order to reduce it to the already known case of the above-mentioned shape-classes of four. However, the complete sets of observables determined in this way are generally not of minimal length
2N
any more.

Once the algebraic derivation of the full ambiguity structure allowed by such larger shape-classes has been performed successfully, the selection of complete sets according to a graphical criterion similar to the one proposed in this work is straightforward. The derivation of the ambiguity structure is actually the only significant hurdle in the treatment of the more complicated problems. Once this hurdle is taken, graphical criteria can be applied with full effect.

This work can be extended into multiple directions. The obvious first choice would be to work out the new graphical criteria for more complicated reactions with
N>6
amplitudes, such as two-meson photoproduction (
N=8
) or even vector-meson photoproduction (
N=12
), and to try to extract minimal complete sets with length
2N
for these reactions. For this, some algebraic ingenuity is needed to treat the larger shape-classes. Another possible direction of research would be to try to establish a closer contact to mathematicians, in order to see whether they have deeper insights into the discussed matters. When Moravcsik published his paper in 1985
Moravcsik:1984uf
, he called the mathematical theory for the ambiguities of a set of bilinear algebraic equations for several unknowns to be ’nonexistent’ (see the second-last paragraph in the introduction of reference
Moravcsik:1984uf
). However, this may have changed in the most recent years and it could be that mathematicians use the objects and approaches introduced in this work, but in different guises. Nevertheless, we have to admit that also the present work seems to lead to a kind of more general theory for the unique solvability of systems of bilinear equations.

N

2

(C2)

The connected graph has to contain an odd number of dashed lines

It is irrelevant which of the bilinear amplitude-products are represented by the dashed lines, as long as the overall number of dashed lines is odd.

The requirement of a connected topology for the graph considered in Theorem 2 is crucial, due to the fact that it directly forbids combinations of relative-phases corresponding to multiple disconnected sub-sets of amplitudes in the complex plane, where the relative-phases among all amplitudes within one sub-set are uniquely fixed, but the multiple sub-sets are still allowed to rotate freely relative to each other. ;
Wunderlich:2020umg
among all the occurring relative-phases. The generic form as well as the importance of such consistency relations is elaborated in more detail in the beginning of section
II
of the main text.

We note that there can exist certain singular surfaces in the parameter space composed of the relative-phases, on which Theorem 2 no longer holds. Such singular configurations have been mentioned in section III and appendix A of reference
Wunderlich:2020umg
. However, we will disregard such cases in the following discussion, since the measure of such singular surfaces is always negligible, when compared to the full parameter space.

The modified form of Moravcsik’s theorem (i.e. Theorem 2) has turned out to be very useful, since it leads to a fully automated procedure for the construction of complete sets for in principle
any
amplitude-extraction problem with a general number of
N
complex amplitudes. However, when applied to realistic processes with
N\geq 4
amplitudes, this theorem has lead to sets which are slightly over-complete when compared to complete sets of minimal length
2N
(see in particular section VII of reference
Wunderlich:2020umg
, as well as the discussion in reference
Kroenert:2020ahf
). This happens essentially due to fact that isolated real- and imaginary parts of bilinear amplitude-products (equations (
72
) and (
74
)) enter the statement of Theorem 2, while the observables encountered in processes with
N\geq 4
amplitudes are generally linear combinations of such bilinear products. The new graphical criterion, which is developed in section
II
of the main text, represents an attempt to improve this situation by allowing for a direct selection of fully complete sets with minimal length
2N
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the phase-ambiguities for the simplest non-trivial shape-class (eqs (
1
) - (
4
))

In the following, we repeat the derivations from section III of Nakayama’s work
Nakayama:2018yzw
. First, we consider the example-case ’B.1’ (selection of
\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+},\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)
) mentioned in section
II
. We define
B_{ij}:=\left|b_{i}\right|\left|b_{j}\right|
and consider the following pair of observables:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\sin\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\sin\phi_{kl},

(76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\cos\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\cos\phi_{kl}.

(77)

When combining both of these definitions, a basic addition-theorem for the cosine leads to the following expression:

\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)^{2}=B_{ij}^{2}+B_{kl}^{2}+2B_{ij}B_{kl}\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right).

(78)

We define the length

N\equiv N^{n}_{1+,2+}:=\sqrt{\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)^{2}}.

(79)

Then, we formally introduce the transitional angle
\zeta\equiv\zeta^{n}_{1+,2+}
via:

\cos\zeta\equiv\frac{\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}}{N}\text{, }\sin\zeta\equiv\frac{\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}}{N}.

(80)

Compare this definition to the comments made below equation (
11
), as well as to the graphical representation shown in Figure
2
, in section
II
. One should always keep in mind that the general angle
\zeta^{n}_{\nu\pm,\nu^{\prime}\pm}
and the length
N^{n}_{\nu\pm,\nu^{\prime}\pm}
both depend on the considered pair of observables.

With these definitions, the observables given above can be re-expressed as

\displaystyle N\cos\zeta

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\sin\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\sin\phi_{kl},

(81)

\displaystyle N\sin\zeta

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\cos\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\cos\phi_{kl}.

(82)

Next, we multiply the first of these equations by
\sin\phi_{kl}
and the second one by
\cos\phi_{kl}
and then add both equations, which leads to:

\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)=\frac{-B_{kl}+N\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{kl}\right)}{B_{ij}}.

(83)

Inserting this result in equation (
78
), one obtains:

\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{kl}\right)=\frac{N^{2}-B_{ij}^{2}+B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}}.

(84)

Applying the
\arcsin
-function to this equation, one can derive the phase-ambiguity

\phi_{kl}=\begin{cases}-\zeta+\alpha_{kl},\\
-\zeta-\alpha_{kl}+\pi,\end{cases}

(85)

where the right-hand-side of equation (
84
) uniquely fixes
\alpha_{kl}
on the interval
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
.

In exactly the same way that has lead to equation (
84
), one can prove the following constraint:

\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{ij}\right)=\frac{B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}.

(86)

This constraint leads to the following discrete phase-ambiguity

\phi_{ij}=\begin{cases}-\zeta+\alpha_{ij},\\
-\zeta-\alpha_{ij}+\pi,\end{cases}

(87)

where the right-hand-side of equation (
86
) uniquely specifies
\alpha_{ij}
on the interval
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
.

Up to now, the discrete phase-ambiguity given by equations (
85
) and (
87
) looks like a four-fold one, but this is not true due to the fact that the relative-phases
\phi_{ij}
and
\phi_{kl}
are
not
independent. Rather, equation (
78
) fixes a constraint for
\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)
. In other words, one has
\pm\tilde{\alpha}\equiv\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}
, where
\tilde{\alpha}
is uniquely specified on the interval
[0,\pi]
. The ambiguities (
85
) and (
87
) leave the following four possibilities for the difference of the relative phases:

\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl},\\
\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{kl}-\pi,\\
-\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl}+\pi,\\
-\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{kl}.\end{cases}

(88)

After taking out the indeterminacy of the sign of
\pm\tilde{\alpha}
, which is true due to the symmetry of the cosine, one is left with the following set of two non-redundant cases for the quantity
\tilde{\alpha}
:

\tilde{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl},\\
\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{kl}-\pi.\end{cases}

(89)

We evaluate the cosine of the second possibility for
\tilde{\alpha}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{kl}-\pi\right)=-\cos\alpha_{ij}\cos\alpha_{kl}+\sin\alpha_{ij}\sin\alpha_{kl}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\left(1-\sin^{2}\alpha_{ij}\right)\left(1-\sin^{2}\alpha_{kl}\right)}+\sin\alpha_{ij}\sin\alpha_{kl}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\left(1-\left[\frac{B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}\right]^{2}\right)\left(1-\left[\frac{N^{2}-B_{ij}^{2}+B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}}\right]^{2}\right)}+\frac{B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}\frac{N^{2}-B_{ij}^{2}+B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}-B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}}{2B_{ij}B_{kl}}.

(90)

The calculation needed to get from the third to the fourth step in equation (
90
) is quite involved. The numerator of the square-root term becomes the absolute value
\left|B_{ij}^{4}+\left(B_{kl}^{2}-N^{2}\right)^{2}-2B_{ij}^{2}\left(B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}\right)\right|
. Once we insert the equation for
N^{2}
, i.e. eq. (
78
), into the term within the absolute value, we see that this term equals
-4B_{ij}^{2}B_{kl}^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)
, which is definitely a negative number. Thus, evaluating the absolute value amounts to flipping the sign of this term. Keeping track of this sign-change, it is then possible to derive the correct result (
90
).

We observe that the second of the two non-redundant possibilities (
89
) satisfies the correct constraint (
78
) for
\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)
. In exactly the same way as done above, one can check that the first possibility in (
89
) leads to an equation which is different from (
78
). Thus, we have derived that the difference of the relative-phases
\phi_{ij}
and
\phi_{kl}
always has to satisfy:

\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}=\pm\left(\alpha_{ij}+\alpha_{kl}-\pi\right).

(91)

The fact that this relation has to hold reduces the four-fold discrete ambiguity derived initially to a two-fold one. The remaining constraint (
91
) is only generally fulfilled by the following two cases:

\begin{cases}\phi_{ij}=-\zeta+\alpha_{ij},\\
\phi_{kl}=-\zeta-\alpha_{kl}+\pi,\end{cases}\text{or }\begin{cases}\phi_{ij}=-\zeta-\alpha_{ij}+\pi,\\
\phi_{kl}=-\zeta+\alpha_{kl}.\end{cases}

(92)

Consider next the case ’B.4’ described in section
II
, i.e. the combination of observables
\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1-},\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2-}\right)
. From their definitions

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1-}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\sin\phi_{ij}-B_{kl}\sin\phi_{kl},

(93)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2-}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\cos\phi_{ij}-B_{kl}\cos\phi_{kl},

(94)

we see that the entire derivation given above for the case ’B.1’ can be mimicked, with the only change needed being a flip of the sign of
B_{kl}
in every intermediate step. Thus, we can infer the following two constraints:

\displaystyle\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{kl}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-N^{2}+B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}},

(95)

\displaystyle\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}+B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}.

(96)

Applying the
\arcsin
-function yields here again an apparent four-fold discrete phase-ambiguity, with formally the same expressions as given in equations (
85
) and (
87
). However, now the derivation (
90
) changes. We again consider the two non-redundant cases for the
\tilde{\alpha}
-variable given in equation (
89
). However, now some quite involved algebra shows that:

\displaystyle\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl}\right)=\cos\alpha_{ij}\cos\alpha_{kl}+\sin\alpha_{ij}\sin\alpha_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(1-\sin^{2}\alpha_{ij}\right)\left(1-\sin^{2}\alpha_{kl}\right)}+\sin\alpha_{ij}\sin\alpha_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(1-\left[\frac{B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}\right]^{2}\right)\left(1-\left[\frac{-N^{2}+B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}}\right]^{2}\right)}+\frac{B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}+N^{2}}{2NB_{ij}}\frac{-N^{2}+B_{ij}^{2}-B_{kl}^{2}}{2NB_{kl}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-N^{2}+B_{ij}^{2}+B_{kl}^{2}}{2B_{ij}B_{kl}}.

(97)

The second possibility mentioned in equation (
89
) leads to the wrong constraint for
\cos\left(\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}\right)
. Therefore, in the case ’B.4’, the following constraint has to hold:

\phi_{ij}-\phi_{kl}=\pm\left(\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl}\right).

(98)

This constraint leaves only the following two-fold phase-ambiguity for the case ’B.4’:

\begin{cases}\phi_{ij}=-\zeta+\alpha_{ij},\\
\phi_{kl}=-\zeta+\alpha_{kl},\end{cases}\text{or }\begin{cases}\phi_{ij}=-\zeta-\alpha_{ij}+\pi,\\
\phi_{kl}=-\zeta-\alpha_{kl}+\pi.\end{cases}

(99)

Now, we treat the case ’B.2’ from section
II
, i.e. we consider the following pair of observables

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\sin\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\sin\phi_{kl},

(100)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2-}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\cos\phi_{ij}-B_{kl}\cos\phi_{kl}.

(101)

The derivation for this case corresponds to the expressions derived for ’B.1’ above, with both the signs of
B_{kl}

\displaystyle\sin\left(\zeta+\phi_{ij}\right)

\phi_{ij}+\phi_{kl}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{ij}-\alpha_{kl},\\

\begin{cases}\phi_{ij}=-\zeta+\alpha_{ij},\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1-}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\sin\phi_{ij}-B_{kl}\sin\phi_{kl},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}

\displaystyle=B_{ij}\cos\phi_{ij}+B_{kl}\cos\phi_{kl}.

In this appendix, we derive the discrete phase-ambiguities for observables selected from the shape-class given in equations (

\zeta
-angles in the ambiguity-formulas (more general compared to those listed in section

of the main text.

The first and simplest way of deriving the phase-ambiguities for combinations of observables with flipped signs proceeds via consideration of equation (

) from appendix
B
. Suppose we reverse the sign of the second observable in this formula, i.e. we consider the observables
\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+},-\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)

Suppose now that another angle, called ’
\tilde{\zeta}
’, exists for which the equation retains its original form:

\cos\tilde{\zeta}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}}{N}\text{, }\sin\tilde{\zeta}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}}{N}.

\cos\tilde{\zeta}=\cos\zeta\text{, and }\sin\tilde{\zeta}=-\sin\zeta,

(120)

or

\tilde{\zeta}=-\zeta.

(121)

We see that the
\zeta
-angle has reversed its sign when going from

\left(\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+},-\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)

\cos\zeta\equiv\frac{-\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+}}{N}\text{, }\sin\zeta\equiv\frac{\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}}{N}.

\tilde{\zeta}=\pi-\zeta.

(123)

An additional summand of
\pi
appears now in the ambiguity-formulas, which is however irrelevant. The important point is that also in this case, the sign of the

\left(-\mathcal{O}^{n}_{1+},-\mathcal{O}^{n}_{2+}\right)

\zeta
