Plücker-type inequalities for mixed areas and intersection numbers of curve arrangements
Gennadiy Averkov, Ivan Soprunov

Abstract
. Any collection of n n compact convex planar sets K 1 , … , K n K_{1},\dots,K_{n} defines a vector of ( n 2 ) {n\choose 2} mixed areas V ⁡ ( K i , K j ) \V(K_{i},K_{j}) for 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n 1\leq i<j\leq n . We show that for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 these numbers satisfy certain Plücker-type inequalities. Moreover, we prove that for n = 4 n=4 these inequalities completely describe the space of all mixed area vectors ( V ( K i , K j ) : 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 4 ) (\V(K_{i},K_{j})\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq 4) . For arbitrary n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 we show that this space has a semialgebraic closure of full dimension. As an application, we show that the pairwise intersection numbers of any collection of n n tropical curves satisfy the Plücker-type inequalities. Moreover, in the case of four tropical curves, any homogeneous polynomial relation between their six intersection numbers follows from the corresponding Plücker-type inequalities.

1.
Introduction

Let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of nonempty compact convex sets and consider
their Minkowski linear combination with some non-negative real coefficients

\alpha_{1}K_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}K_{n}:={\left\{\alpha_{1}p_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}p_{n}\,:\,p_{i}\in K_{i}\right\}}.

A fundamental result in the theory of convex sets asserts that the
d
-dimensional volume
\Vol_{d}(\alpha_{1}K_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}K_{n})

is a homogeneous polynomial in
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
of degree
d
. This polynomial can be written as

(1.1)

\Vol_{d}(\alpha_{1}K_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}K_{n})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leq d}\!\V(K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{d}})\,\alpha_{i_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{i_{d}},

where the coefficients
\V(K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{d}})
are chosen to be symmetric in the
d
sets they depend on, see
[
Sch14
, Th 5.1.7]
.

This gives rise to the notion of the mixed volume: a symmetric functional which sends every
d
-tuple of compact convex sets
(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})
to
the coefficient
\V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})
.
Mixed volumes are a unifying concept behind various metric or measure-related functionals considered in the theory of convex sets and
stochastic geometry,
[
San04
]
.
Remarkably, they also appear in algebraic geometry due to the interpretation of the mixed volumes of lattice polytopes in terms of the number of solutions of sparse polynomial systems, provided by the celebrated Bernstein-Khovanskii-Kushnirenko (BKK) theorem
[
Ber75
,
Hov78
,
Kus76
]
. More recently, mixed volumes
have become one of the key objects in tropical intersection theory
[
BB13
,
MS15
]
.

Understanding relationships between mixed volumes in terms of inequalities is a central topic in the theory of mixed volumes. While some of the inequalities, like the Aleksandrov-Fenchel inequality, have long been known and widely used, it appears that many inequalities
are yet to be discovered. Currently, there is no complete understanding of all relations between mixed volumes, besides the cases of small
n
in
dimension
d=2
.

The problem that we address in this paper goes back to the work of Heine
[
Hei38
]
and
Shephard
[
She60
]
, whereas the format of the problem is in the spirit of the so-called Blaschke-Santaló diagrams,
[
Bla16
,
San61
]
. Having a family of functionals
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}:S\to\mathbb{R}
on a certain space
S
, one can ask for
all relationships between these functionals, for example, by providing an inequality description for the set

D={\left\{(\phi_{1}(s),\ldots,\phi_{m}(s))\,:\,s\in S\right\}}
.
Santaló called
D
the
diagram
for the functionals
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
and studied such diagrams for the space of planar compact convex sets and triples of functionals common in convexity theory (such as area, perimeter, diameter, width etc.), see
[
San61
]
. If an inequality description of
D
can be established, one has a complete system of best-possible inequalities that link

\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}
.

Our ultimate goal is to understand diagrams of mixed volume functionals on the space of
n
-tuples
K=(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})
of compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There is a total of
\binom{n+d-1}{d}
mixed volumes in (
1.1
) obtained by choosing
d
out of the
n
convex sets
with repetitions allowed. We call the corresponding diagram
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n+d-1}{d}}
the
mixed volume configuration space
.
If we do not allow repetitions, there are
\binom{n}{d}
mixed volumes which form, what we call, the

pure mixed volume configuration space

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,d)\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{d}}
.

In this paper we focus on dimension
d=2
, which already offers many challenges. In this case, the elements of

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
are the pure mixed areas
\V(K_{i},K_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Together with the usual areas

\Vol_{2}(K_{i})=\V(K_{i},K_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
they form the space
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
.
At the moment, a description of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
in terms of inequalities is only known for
n\leq 3
.
For
n=2
, it is not hard to see that
{\mathcal{MV}}(2,2)
is described by the Minkowski inequality, see Example
2.4
. In
[
Hei38
]
Heine
gave a complete description for
{\mathcal{MV}}(3,2)
in terms of the Minkowski inequalities and determinantal inequalities. All other cases remain open.

We summarize the main results of this paper below.

(1)

We introduce new inequalities for mixed areas, which we call Plücker-type inequalities, as they resemble the Plücker relations for the Grassmannian. The distinctive feature of the Plücker-type inequalities is that they only involve pure mixed areas, that is, none of the mixed volume functionals contains a repetition of a set. To the best of our knowledge, no inequalities of such a form have been previously known.

(2)

We prove that the Plücker-type inequalities provide a complete description of
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
in terms of inequalities for
n=4
,
but are not enough for
n\geq 8
. Whether they provide a complete description when
n=5,6,7
is open.

(3)

A priori, it is not clear whether
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
always has a description in algebraic terms, although the semialgebraicity of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
looks like a plausible conjecture. For example, all known tight inequalities in convexity always turn out to be polynomial inequalities.
While we leave the verification of the semialgebraicity of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
as an open problem, we can at least confirm that

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
(being a projection of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
) has the closure that is a semialgebraic set.

(4)

We show that the sets
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
and
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
have full topological dimension. In particular, this means that there are no polynomial equations that the coefficients of the mixed volume polynomial (
1.1
) satisfy.

(5)

According to the BKK theorem and its tropical analog, the (normalized) mixed area has an interpretation as the intersection number
of two generic algebraic curves in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}
with given Newton polytopes, as well as the intersection number of two tropical curves intersecting transversally.
Our results have the following application in this setting.
Every collection of
n
(tropical) curves defines a vector of
{n\choose 2}
pairwise intersection numbers, so one can consider the
configuration space
\cal I(n,2)
of all such vectors. We show that the smallest (with respect to inclusion) positively homogeneous closed
set containing
\cal I(n,2)
is semialgebraic. Moreover, for
n=4
it is described by three Plücker-type inequalities.

The scope of our studies can be naturally extended to studying
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)
and
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,d)
in a general dimension
d
. This leads to even more challenging problems. For example, there is no complete description available
neither for
{\mathcal{PMV}}(5,3)
nor for
{\mathcal{MV}}(3,3)
. The volume polynomial (
1.1
) is a special case of a class of polynomials, called Lorenzian polynomials, which have been extensively studied in connection with algebraic geometry and algebraic combinatorics, see
[
BH20
]
. This provides another motivation for studying polynomial relations between the coefficients of the volume polynomial.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the main objects of study and provide some preliminary results.

Throughout the paper we use
[n]
for the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Also, we will often index the elements of
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
using a pair of indices, either as
v_{i,j}
or, simply,
v_{ij}
for all
\{i,j\}\subset[n]
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
be the set of all nonempty compact convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
let
\Vol_{d}(K)
denote the Euclidean
d
-dimensional volume of
K
. Given two subsets
A,B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their
Minkowski sum

A+B
is the vector sum
A+B=\{a+b\,:\,a\in A,b\in B\}
.

The
mixed volume

\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
is the unique symmetric and multilinear (with respect to Minkowski addition) function on
d
elements
K_{1},\dots,K_{d}
of
{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
, which coincides with
\Vol_{d}
on the diagonal, i.e.,

\V(K,\dots,K)=\Vol_{d}(K)
for any
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
. One can write an explicit “polarization” formula for the mixed volume
[
Sch14
, Sec 5.1]
:

(2.1)

\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}(-1)^{d+\ell}\!\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}}\Vol_{d}(K_{i_{1}}+\dots+K_{i_{\ell}}).

In particular, for
d=2
we have

(2.2)

\V(K_{1},K_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Vol_{2}(K_{1}+K_{2})-\Vol_{2}(K_{1})-\Vol_{2}(K_{2})\right).

Some of the basic properties of the mixed volume we will need are the following, see
[
Sch14
, Sec 5.1]
:

(a)

\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
is non-negative,

(b)

\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
is monotone with respect to inclusion in each entry,

(c)

\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
is invariant under independent translation of each
K_{i}
,

(d)

\V(\phi(K_{1}),\dots,\phi(K_{d}))=|\det(\phi)|\V(K_{1},\dots,K_{d})
for any linear transformation
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
K\in{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
. Recall that the
support function

h_{K}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
of
K
is defined by

h_{K}(u)=\max\{\langle u,x\rangle\,:\,x\in K\},

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere and
\langle u,x\rangle
is the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Consider a convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
U_{P}
be the set of the outer normals to the facets of
P
and

P^{u}
be the facet with the outer unit normal
u
. Then (see, for example,
[
Sch14
, Eq. (5.2)]
)

(2.3)

\Vol_{d}(P)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{u\in U_{P}}h_{P}(u)\Vol_{d-1}(P^{u}).

Geometrically, (
2.3
) expresses the volume of
P
as the sum of the volumes of pyramids over the facets of
P
with the same apex lying in the interior of
P
. There is a similar formula for the mixed volume
\V(K_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{d})
where

K_{1}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
and
P_{2},\dots,P_{d}
are convex polytopes.
Let
U_{P_{2},\dots,P_{d}}
be the set of the outer unit normals to the facets of
P_{2}+\dots+P_{d}
and let

P_{i}^{u}=\max\{\langle u,x\rangle\,:\,x\in P_{i}\}
be the face of
P_{i}
corresponding to
u
, for
2\leq i\leq d
.
Then

(2.4)

\V(K_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{d})=\frac{1}{d}\!\sum_{u\in U_{P_{2},\dots,P_{d}}}\!h_{K_{1}}(u)\V(P_{2}^{u},\dots,P_{d}^{u}),

see, for example,
[
Sch14
, Eq. (5.23)]
.
For
d=2
, (
2.4
) becomes particularly simple:

(2.5)

\V(K,P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u\in U_{P}}h_{K}(u)|P^{u}|,

where the sum is over the outer normals to the sides of
P
and
|P^{u}|=\Vol_{1}(P^{u})
is the length of the side
P^{u}
.

We will need the following simple observations. We say a convex set
K
is
inscribed
in a polygon
P
if

K\subseteq P
and every side of
P
has a nonempty intersection with
K
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K,P\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
where
P
is a convex polygon. If
K
is inscribed in
P
then

\V(K,P)=\Vol_{2}(P).

Proof.

Since
K
is inscribed in
P
we have
h_{K}(u)=h_{P}(u)
for every outer unit normal
u
to a side of
P
. The lemma now follows
by comparing (
2.3
) for
d=2
and (
2.5
).
∎

Recall that the
width
of
K
in the direction of unit vector
u
is defined by

w_{u}(K)=\max\{\langle u,x\rangle:x\in K\}-\min\{\langle u,x\rangle:x\in K\}.

We have

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K,I\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
where
I
is a segment of length
\lambda
, orthogonal to
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then

\V(K,I)=\frac{1}{2}w_{u}(K)\lambda.

Proof.

This follows directly from (
2.5
) since the outer normals of
I
are
\{u,-u\}
and

h_{K}(u)=\max\{\langle u,x\rangle:x\in K\}
and
h_{K}(-u)=\max\{\langle-u,x\rangle:x\in K\}=-\min\{\langle u,x\rangle:x\in K\}
.
∎

Now consider an
n
-tuple
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{n})
of sets in
{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
. Then any choice of
n
non-negative integers

d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
satisfying
d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}=d
gives rise to a value of the mixed volume

\V_{K}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}):=\V(\underbrace{K_{1},\ldots,K_{1}}_{d_{1}},\ldots,\underbrace{K_{n},\ldots,K_{n}}_{d_{n}}).

There are
{n+d-1\choose d}
such choices which produce the vector

\V_{K}:=\big(\V_{K}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\,:\,d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}=d\,\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+n-1\choose d}

which we call the
mixed volume configuration vector
corresponding to
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{n})
. For example, when
n=2
,
d=3
, and

K=(K_{1},K_{2})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{3}}^{2}
, we obtain the vector

\V_{K}=\Big(\V_{K}(3,0),\V_{K}(2,1),\V_{K}(1,2),\V_{K}(0,3)\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}

where the first and the last entries are the 3-dimensional volumes of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, and the middle ones are
\V(K_{1},K_{1},K_{2})
and
\V(K_{1},K_{2},K_{2})
, respectively.

Definition 2.3
.

The mixed volume configuration space is the set of all mixed volume configuration vectors over all possible
n
-tuples
K\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{d}}^{\!n}

{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)=\left\{\V_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+n-1\choose d}\,:\,K\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{d}}^{\!n}\right\}.

Example 2.4
.

Let
n=d=2
. According to the Minkowski inequality
[
Sch14
, Th 7.2.1]
,

(2.6)

\V(K_{1},K_{2})^{2}\geq\Vol_{2}(K_{1})\Vol_{2}(K_{2})

for any
K_{1},K_{2}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
. This implies that

{\mathcal{MV}}(2,2)\subseteq\left\{(v_{11},v_{12},v_{22})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}\,:\,v_{12}^{2}\geq v_{11}v_{22}\right\}.

It is not hard to see that the above inclusion is, in fact, equality. For this it is enough to
take
K_{1},K_{2}
to be rectangular boxes,
K_{1}=[0,a]\times[0,b]
and
K_{2}=[0,c]\times[0,d]
. In this case

\Vol_{2}(K_{1})=ab
,
\V(K_{1},K_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(ad+bc)
, and
\Vol_{2}(K_{2})=cd
.
One can then check that for any non-negative
(v_{11},v_{12},v_{22})
satisfying the Minkowski inequality, the system

\{ab=v_{11},ad+bc=2v_{12},cd=v_{22}\}
reduces to a quadratic equation with discriminant
v_{12}^{2}-v_{11}v_{22}
and has a
solution
(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}
.

We next introduce the main object of this paper, the pure mixed volume configuration space
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,d)
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given an
n
-tuple
(K_{1},\dots,K_{n})
of convex sets in
{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
, define the pure mixed volume configuration vector

(2.7)

W_{K}:=\big(\V(K_{i_{1}},\dots,K_{i_{d}})\,:\,\{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}\}\subset[n]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\choose d}.

The pure mixed volume configuration space is the set of all pure mixed volume configuration vectors over all possible
n
-tuples
K\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{d}}^{n}

\mathcal{PMV}(n,d)=\left\{W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\choose d}\,:\,K\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{d}}^{\!n}\right\}.

Note that
\mathcal{PMV}(n,d)
is empty for
n<d
and
\mathcal{PMV}(d,d)=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Also, for
n\geq d
,
\mathcal{PMV}(n,d)
is the projection of

{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)
onto a coordinate subspace.

Example 2.6
.

The first non-trivial case is the case of three planar convex sets, i.e.
n=3
and
d=2
.
In this case
\mathcal{PMV}(3,2)=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
. This can be realized using segments and points only.
Indeed, consider a vector
(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
. We need to choose

K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
such that
v_{ij}=\V(K_{i},K_{j})
for all
1\leq i<j\leq 3
.
If all
v_{ij}=0
we may take
K_{1}=K_{2}=K_{3}=\{0\}
.
If
v_{12}>0
,
v_{13}\geq 0
and
v_{23}=0
we take

K_{1}=[0,\sqrt{2}v_{12}e_{1}]
,
K_{2}=[0,\sqrt{2}e_{2}]
, and
K_{3}=[0,\sqrt{2}(v_{13}/v_{12})e_{2}]
.
Finally, when all
v_{ij}>0
we may take

K_{1}=\left[0,\frac{\sqrt{2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23}}}{v_{23}}e_{1}\right],\ K_{2}=\left[0,\frac{\sqrt{2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23}}}{v_{13}}e_{2}\right],\ K_{3}=\left[0,\frac{\sqrt{2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23}}}{v_{12}}(e_{1}+e_{2})\right].

It is not hard to extend this argument to show that
\mathcal{PMV}(n,n-1)=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
for any
n\geq 3
.

3.
Statements of main results

It is known that if
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
then
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)
is a proper subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n+d-1\choose d}
as there are many algebraic inequalities relating the mixed volumes (the entries of
\V_{K}
). One of the most important ones is the Aleksandrov-Fenchel inequality:

(AF)

\V(A,B,K_{3},\dots,K_{d})^{2}\geq\V(A,A,K_{3},\dots,K_{d})\V(B,B,K_{3},\dots,K_{d}),

for any
K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{d}
,
[
Sch14
, Ch 7]
. It generalizes the Minkowski inequality (
2.6
), as well as the classical isoperimetric and Brunn-Minkowski inequalities. However, starting with
n\geq 3
these inequalities do not describe
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,d)
completely, and more inequalities are needed, see for example,

[
ABS20
, Cor 4.2]
. When we consider the pure mixed volume configuration space
\mathcal{PMV}(n,d)
the Aleksandrov-Fenchel inequalities are absent,
as the mixed volumes in the right hand side of (
AF
) are not part of the coordinates of
W_{K}
.

As we saw in Example
2.6
, for
n=3
the space
\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)
is the entire positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
.
However, starting with
n\geq 4
it is a proper subset of the orthant
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}
, as the mixed areas
satisfy certain inequalities,
which we call the Plücker-type inequalities. Below and throughout the paper
A\sqcup B
denotes the disjoint union of sets
A
and
B
.

Definition 3.1
.

The Plücker-type inequalities on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}
are

(PT)

v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\ \ \text{ for all }\ \{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[n].

For
n=4
we have exactly three such inequalities, which one can also interpret as the triangle inequalities for the three products

v_{12}v_{34}
,
v_{13}v_{24}
, and
v_{14}v_{23}
:

(3.1)

\begin{matrix}\,\,\,v_{12}v_{34}+v_{13}v_{24}-v_{14}v_{23}\geq 0\\
\,\,\,v_{12}v_{34}-v_{13}v_{24}+v_{14}v_{23}\geq 0\\
-v_{12}v_{34}+v_{13}v_{24}+v_{14}v_{23}\geq 0.\end{matrix}

Clearly, the Plücker-type inequalities are invariant under the permutations of
\{1,2,3,4\}
.

Now let
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
be nonempty compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
V_{ij}

denote the mixed area
\V(K_{i},K_{j})
.
One of the key observations is that any linear combination of the form

\pm V_{12}V_{34}\pm V_{13}V_{24}\pm V_{14}V_{23}

is multilinear, that is it is Minkowski-additive
in each
K_{i}
and is homogeneous with respect to rescaling each
K_{i}
by a positive real factor.
This property does not hold for expressions involving mixed volumes with repetitions, as in (
AF
).

Here is the first main result of the paper.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have

(3.2)

\mathcal{PMV}(4,2)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4\choose 2}\,:\,v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\ \text{\rm for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]\right\}.

In particular, this shows that
{\mathcal{PMV}}(4,2)
is a basic closed semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
(see Section
7
for the definition of basic semialgebraic sets).

Here are the main steps of the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
Recall that planar compact convex sets can be approximated by convex polygons in Hausdorff metric (see
[
Sch14
, Th 1.8.16]
)
and the mixed volume functional is continuous (see the proof of Theorem 5.1.7 in
[
Sch14
]
).
Thus, to prove the direct inclusion in (
3.2
) it is enough to restrict ourselves to the case of convex polygons
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
. Furthermore,
each convex polygon in the plane is the Minkowski sum of segments and triangles (see, for example,
[
Sch14
, Th 3.2.14]
). Since the Plücker-type inequalities are linear in each
K_{i}
the problem further reduces to the case when each
K_{i}
is either a segment or a triangle. We deal with this situation in Section
4
.
To prove the reverse inclusion in (
3.2
) we first put a combinatorial structure on the space
defined by the Plücker-type inequalities. This allows us to handle the case distinction when some of the
v_{ij}
become zero, similarly to
what we did in Example
2.6
. We do this in Section
5
.

Our second main result gives an insight about the pure mixed volume configuration space for more than four planar convex sets.
Using the same arguments as in Section
4
,
we see that
\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)
is contained
in the semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
described by the
3{n\choose 4}
Plücker-type inequalities.
However, for
n\geq 8
more inequalities are needed to describe
\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)
completely.
Whether the Plücker-type inequalities describe
\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)
for
n=5,6,7
remains open. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
n\geq 4
, we have

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)\subseteq\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}\,:\,v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\ \text{\rm for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[n]\right\}.

Moreover, this containment is proper for
n\geq 8
.

We prove this result in Section
7
. In Section
8
we show that both
\mathcal{MV}(n,2)
and
\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)
have full topological dimension.
Finally, we discuss applications of our results to intersection numbers of algebraic curves in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}
and
tropical curves in Section
9
.

4.
Plücker-type inequalities for four planar convex sets

In this section we prove that for any
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{4})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!4}
, the corresponding pure mixed area configuration
vector

W_{K}=(V_{12},V_{13},V_{14},V_{23},V_{24},V_{34}),\quad\text{where }V_{ij}=\V(K_{i},K_{j}),

satisfies the three Plücker-type inequalities (
3.1
).
We start with the case when
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
are centrally-symmetric. Although we will not need it to prove the general case, we include the proof
as it is short, transparent, and explains why we call the inequalities Plücker-type.

4.1.
The symmetric case

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}^{sym}
denote the set of compact convex centrally-symmetric sets in the plane.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{4})\in({\mathcal{K}}_{2}^{sym})^{4}
. Then the pure mixed area configuration vector

W_{K}=(V_{12},V_{13},V_{14},V_{23},V_{24},V_{34})
satisfies the Plücker-type inequalities (
3.1
).

Proof.

First suppose that
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
are segments with direction vectors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}
.
Since the Plücker-type inequalities are invariant under the permutations of the
K_{i}
, we may assume that

v_{1},\dots,v_{4}
are ordered counterclockwise in such a way that
\det(v_{i},v_{j})\geq 0
for

1\leq i<j\leq 4
. Note that
\V(K_{i},K_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\det(v_{i},v_{j})
in this case (see (
2.2
)). Consider the
2\times 4
matrix with columns
v_{1},\dots,v_{4}
. Its maximal minors

v_{ij}=\det(v_{i},v_{j})
satisfy the Plücker relation (see
[
MS05
, Ch 14]
):

(4.1)

v_{12}v_{34}-v_{13}v_{24}+v_{14}v_{23}=0.

Thus, the middle inequality in (
3.1
) holds with equality. It remains to check the other two inequalities in (
3.1
).
Indeed, by (
4.1
) we have

0\leq 2v_{12}v_{34}=2v_{12}v_{34}-\left(v_{12}v_{34}-v_{13}v_{24}+v_{14}v_{23}\right)=v_{12}v_{34}+v_{13}v_{24}-v_{14}v_{23}.

The other inequality is similar.

Now, if
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
are centrally-symmetric convex polygons then each of them can be decomposed as the Minkowski sum of segments.
As the Plücker-type inequalities are linear in each
K_{i}
, the proof reduces to the case of segments considered above. Finally,
the general case of centrally-symmetric sets follows by approximating them (in the Hausdorff metric) by convex polygons.
∎

4.2.
Special cases

Now let
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
be planar compact convex sets, not necessarily centrally-symmetric.
First we will look at a case when two of the
K_{i}
are homothetic.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{4})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\!}^{4}
and assume that
K_{3}
and
K_{4}
are homothetic. Then the pure mixed area configuration vector

W_{K}=(V_{12},V_{13},V_{14},V_{23},V_{24},V_{34})
satisfies the Plücker-type inequalities.

Proof.

By translating and rescaling
K_{4}
we may assume that
K_{3}=K_{4}
. Then the first two inequalities in (
3.1
)
are trivial and the third one states
2V_{13}V_{23}\geq V_{12}V_{33}
. This inequality is proved in
[
SZ16
, Th 6.2]

and is an instance of a Bezout-type inequality for mixed volumes. See also

[
BGL18
, Lem 5.1]
or
[
ABS20
, Lem 4.1]
for higher dimensional Bezout-type inequalities.
∎

Our next special case is when one of the
K_{i}
is a segment.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{4})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\!}^{4}
and assume that
K_{4}
is a segment. Then the pure mixed area configuration vector

W_{K}=(V_{12},V_{13},V_{14},V_{23},V_{24},V_{34})
satisfies the Plücker-type inequalities.

Proof.

By rescaling the sets in the tuple
K
we may assume that
K_{4}
is a unit segment and the
K_{i}
have width
w_{u_{4}}(K_{i})=2
where
u_{4}
is the direction orthogonal to
K_{4}
. Then
V_{i4}=1
for
1\leq i\leq 3
(see Lemma
2.2
) and, up to permutations of the
K_{i}
, it is enough to prove

(4.2)

V_{12}\leq V_{13}+V_{23}.

Note that we may assume that
K_{3}
is a segment, as replacing
K_{3}
with a subset (and keeping the width equal to 2) only strengthens the inequality, by the monotonicity of the mixed volume. Let
w_{u_{3}}(K_{i})=2a_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, where
u_{3}
is the direction orthogonal to
K_{3}
. Similarly, for
i=1,2
, we may replace

K_{i}
with the parallelogram containing
K_{i}
and having the same widths as
K_{i}
in the directions
u_{3}
and
u_{4}
, as this does not change
V_{i3}
(by Lemma
2.2
) and may only increase
V_{12}
. Now we may apply Proposition
4.1
or simply note that, by Lemma
2.2
,
V_{i3}=a_{i}\lambda
and
V_{12}=(a_{1}+a_{2})\lambda
where

\lambda
is the length of
K_{3}
. In particular, (
4.2
) holds with equality in this case.
∎

4.3.
The case of triangles

In this subsection we assume that each
K_{i}
is a triangle, which is the main case we need to consider.
We start with a special case of relative positions of the outer unit normals of the triangles.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{4}
be unit vectors (not necessarily distinct) contained in a half-plane and
ordered counterclockwise. Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
be triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Suppose
u_{1},u_{4}
are outer unit normals of
K_{i}
and
u_{2},u_{3}
are outer unit normals of
K_{j}
, for
1\leq i\neq j\leq 4
. Then

V_{ij}V_{kl}\leq V_{ik}V_{jl}+V_{il}V_{jk},

where
\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]
.

Proof.

After possible translations and dilations we may assume that
K_{i}
is inscribed in
K_{j}
. By the assumption on the outer normals,
K_{i}
and
K_{j}

share a vertex. Then we can replace
K_{i}
with a segment contained in
K_{i}
and inscribed in
K_{j}
. In this case
V_{ik}
and
V_{il}
may only decrease and
V_{ij}
does not change (see Lemma
2.1
). The lemma now follows from Proposition
4.3
.
∎

To handle the general case we use linear deformations of
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
which reduce the problem to special configurations of the triangles.
Let
L
be a triangle with vertices
A,B,C
and outer normals
u_{AB}
,
u_{BC}
, and
u_{AC}
to the corresponding sides of
L
.
We are interested in deformations that fix two of the normals, say
u_{AB}
and
u_{AC}
, and change the third one,
u_{BC}
.
We achieve this by replacing the vertex
C
with a vertex
C_{t}
which depends on a parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
in such a way
that
C_{0}=C
,
|{AC_{t}}|>|{AC}|
for
t>0
, and
|{AC_{t}}|<|{AC}|
for
t<0
, see Figure
4.1
.
We also assume that the coordinates of
C_{t}
are linear functions in
t
. We use
L(t)
to denote such a deformation of
L
. Note that
L(t)
is defined for all
t
in an interval

[t_{0},\infty)
, where
L(t_{0})
is a segment (when
B=C_{t_{0}}
) and
L(t)
approaches
an infinite “half-strip”, as
t\to\infty
.

Figure 4.1.
A linear deformation
L(t)
of a triangle
L=\triangle ABC

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
L,M
be triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
let
L(t)
,
t\in[t_{0},\infty)
be a linear deformation of
L
, as above.
Let
v_{1},v_{2}
be outer unit normals of
M
and
assume that for all
t
in a subset
T\subseteq[t_{0},\infty)

the normal
u_{BC_{t}}
lies in the sector
[v_{1},v_{2}]
.
Then the mixed area
\V(L(t),M)
is a linear function of
t
on
T
.

Proof.

By (
2.5
) for
K=M
and
P=L(t)
we have

2\V(L(t),M)=h_{M}(u_{AB})|AB|+h_{M}(u_{BC_{t}})|BC_{t}|+h_{M}(u_{AC_{t}})|AC_{t}|.

The first term does not depend on
t
. For the second term, note that
when
u_{BC_{t}}
lies in
[v_{1},v_{2}]
there is a unique vertex

x
of
M
, independent of
t
, where the value
h_{M}(u_{BC_{t}})
is attained.
Then

h_{M}(u_{BC_{t}})|BC_{t}|=\langle|BC_{t}|u_{BC_{t}},x\rangle.

As the coordinates of
|BC_{t}|u_{BC_{t}}
are linear in the coordinates of
C_{t}
, we see that

h_{M}(u_{BC_{t}})|BC_{t}|
is a linear function in
t
.
The situation with the third term is similar and even simpler since
u_{AC_{t}}
is constant.
∎

Now
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
be triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The outer unit normals to the sides of the
K_{i}
define a configuration
of 12 unit vectors. We enumerate them counterclockwise
u_{0},\dots,u_{11}
, where the indices
are defined mod 12. Pick a triangle
K_{i}
and its normal
u_{\ell}
. Consider a deformation

K_{i}(t)
of
K_{i}
with “moving” normal
u_{\ell}(t)
, for
t\in[t_{0},\infty)
, as above. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{4}
be triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
K_{i}(t)
be a deformation of
K_{i}
as above.
Assume
u_{\ell}(t)\in[u_{\ell-1},u_{\ell+1}]
for all
t\in[t_{1},\infty)
for some
t_{1}\geq t_{0}
.
Then, if the function
-V_{12}V_{34}+V_{13}V_{24}+V_{14}V_{23}
is non-increasing as
t\to\infty
, then it is constant
on
[t_{1},\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
F(t)=-V_{12}V_{34}+V_{13}V_{24}+V_{14}V_{23}
.
The only terms in
F(t)
that depend on
t
are the mixed areas
V_{ij}
for
1\leq j\leq 4
,
j\neq i
.
By Lemma
4.5
,
F(t)
is linear on
[t_{1},\infty)
and, hence,
we can write
F(t)=\lambda_{1}t+\lambda_{0}
. Moreover, if
F(t)

is non-increasing as
t\to\infty
, then
\lambda_{1}\leq 0
.
If we rescale
K_{i}(t)
by a factor
1/t
we obtain the function
F(t)/t
.
As
t\to\infty
, the triangle
\frac{1}{t}K_{i}(t)
degenerates to a segment. Thus,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{F(t)}{t}\geq 0
, by Proposition
4.3
. Therefore
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
, which shows that
F(t)
is a constant function on
[t_{1},\infty)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{4})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!4}
be a tuple of triangles. Then the pure mixed area configuration vector

W_{K}=(V_{12},V_{13},V_{14},V_{23},V_{24},V_{34})
satisfied the Plücker-type inequalities.

Proof.

As before, let
u_{0},\dots,u_{11}
be the outer unit normals to the sides of the triangles.
Pick any
u_{\ell}
which is strictly between

u_{\ell-1}
and
u_{\ell+1}
and let
K_{i}
be the triangle with this normal.
Consider a linear deformation
K_{i}(t)
with the moving normal
u_{\ell}(t)
, as above.
We either increase
t
in the positive direction or degrease
t
in the negative direction in such a way that the function
F(t)=-V_{12}V_{34}+V_{13}V_{24}+V_{14}V_{23}
is non-increasing, until either

K_{i}(t)
degenerates to a segment or
u_{\ell}(t)
coincides with
u_{\ell-1}
or
u_{\ell+1}
.
Note that we can always avoid the case when
K_{i}(t)
degenerates to an infinite half-strip, since
if
u_{\ell}(t)
stays between
u_{\ell-1}
and
u_{\ell+1}
for all
t\to\infty
then, by Lemma
4.6
,

F(t)
remains constant and we can choose the negative direction of
t
instead.

Repeating the process we either arrive at the case when one of the
K_{i}
is a segment, in which case the
statement follows from Proposition
4.3
, or all
K_{i}
are triangles and
in the configuration
\{u_{0},\dots,u_{11}\}
each normal is repeated at least once.
Moreover, if a triple of normals in

\{u_{0},\dots,u_{11}\}
belongs to the same triangle, then
the normals positively span
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(i.e. not all lie in a half-plane).

We next sort out all such configurations. We call the
multiplicity
of the normal in the configuration the number of times it is repeated.
First, assume that there is a normal with multiplicity 4. Then we are in the
case when Lemma
4.4
applies to every pair
K_{i}
,
K_{l}
,
1\leq i\neq l\leq 4
, hence, all three Plücker-type inequalities hold.

Now we assume that each multiplicity is either 2 or 3. Suppose, for some
\ell
the normals
u_{\ell-1},u_{\ell},u_{\ell+1}
belong to the same triangle, say
K_{4}
. This means that each normal of the other three triangles is either contained in the sector
[u_{\ell+1},u_{\ell-1}]
or coincides with
u_{\ell}
. As

\{u_{\ell+1},u_{\ell-1}\}
does not positively span
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, exactly one of the normals of
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
, and
K_{3}
must coincide with
u_{\ell}
.
In other words,
u_{\ell}
has multiplicity 4. Therefore, no three consecutive normals may belong to the same triangle. This argument also shows that
a configuration with four normals, each with multiplicity 3 cannot exist.

Suppose there are six normals with multiplicity 2 each. To encode a possible configuration we use
a
4\times 6
matrix
M
whose
(i,j)
-th entry is 1 if
j
-th normal belongs to
K_{i}
, and is 0, otherwise. We may assume that
no two rows of
M
are the same, as this corresponds to two triangles begin homothetic, which is already covered in Proposition
4.2
. Moreover, it is enough to consider classes of such matrixes under the action of
S_{4}
on the rows of

and of the dihedral group

(as a subgroup of
S_{6}
) on the columns of

.

,

5.
Plücker space
\Pl_{n}
and its graph representation

We next describe a combinatorial structure of the space defined by the Plücker-type inequalities which turns out to be useful when
showing the reverse inclusion in Theorem
3.2
.
Denote

\Pl_{n}:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\choose 2}\,:\,v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\text{ for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[n]\right\}.

Note that
\Pl_{2}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\Pl_{3}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
. For convenience we set
\Pl_{1}:=\{0\}
.

5.1.
Graph representation

To each
v=(v_{ij})\in\Pl_{n}
we associate a weighted graph
G_{v}
with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
as follows. We put
V=\{1,\dots,n\}

and
\{i,j\}\in E
if and only if
v_{ij}>0
, in which case
v_{ij}
is the weight of the edge
\{i,j\}
. We call
G_{v}
the

graph representing
v\in\Pl_{n}
.

Throughout this section we use
\KG_{n}
to denote the complete graph on
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Furthermore,

\KG_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}
will denote a complete multipartite graph on
\{1,\dots,m\}
corresponding to a partition

m=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s}
with
s\geq 2
and
1\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{s}
.

The following proposition shows that the graphs
G_{v}
have a special combinatorial property.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
v\in\Pl_{n}
the graph
G_{v}
is a union of a complete multipartite graph and some isolated nodes.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\{m+1,\dots,n\}
are the isolated nodes of
G_{v}
for some
0\leq m\leq n
. Let
G

be the induced subgraph of
G_{v}
on
\{1,\dots,m\}
which has no isolated nodes. We need to show that
G
is a complete multipartite graph.
Consider the Plücker-type inequality
v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}
for some
\{i,j,k,l\}
satisfying
\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[m]
.
Note that if
v_{ik}=v_{il}=0
then either
v_{ij}=0
or
v_{kl}=0
(or both). Combinatorially, this means that
G
cannot contain
the following graphs on four nodes as induced subgraphs: (a) the union of
\KG_{3}
and
\KG_{2}
sharing exactly one node; (b) the path of length three; (c) the disjoint union of two copies of
\KG_{2}
, see Figure
5.1
.

Figure 5.1.
Three forbidden graphs

To check that
G
is a complete multipartite graph, we need to show that each connected component of the complement of
G
is a complete graph.
So let
a,b,c
be three distinct nodes such that both
\{a,b\}
and
\{b,c\}
are edges of the complement
\overline{G}
. It suffices to show that
\{a,c\}
is also an edge of
\overline{G}
.

Since neither
a
nor
b
is an isolated node of
G
, each of them has a neighbor in
G
, which we denote by
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
, respectively. If
a^{\prime}\neq b^{\prime}
, then consider the subgraph of
G
induced by
\{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\}
. Then
\{a^{\prime},b\}
must be an edge of
G
, otherwise
G
would contain one of
the graphs (a)–(c) as an induced subgraph. We, thus, have shown that
a
and
b
must share a neighbor in
G
.
Using the same argument for
c
in place of
b
, we conclude that
\{a^{\prime},c\}
must also be an edge of
G
. Summarizing, we conclude that

\{a^{\prime},a\}
,
\{a^{\prime},b\}
, and
\{a^{\prime},c\}
are edges of
G
, whereas
\{a,b\}
and
\{b,c\}
are not edges of
G
.
It follows that
\{a,c\}
is not an edge of
G
, as otherwise the subgraph induced by
\{a,a^{\prime},b,c\}
would be the graph (a), which is impossible.
∎

We will need the following simple observation.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
v\in\Pl_{n}
such that
v_{ik}=0
for some
\{i,k\}\subset[n]
. Then for any
\{j,l\}
satisfying

\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[n]
we have
v_{ij}v_{kl}=v_{il}v_{jk}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the two Plücker-type inequalities
v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}

and
v_{il}v_{jk}\leq v_{ij}v_{kl}+v_{ik}v_{jl}
.
∎

There is a natural embedding of
\Pl_{n-1}
as a subset of
\Pl_{n}
via

(5.1)

\iota_{n}:\Pl_{n-1}\to\Pl_{n},\quad v\mapsto(v_{ij}),\ \text{where}\ v_{in}=0\ \text{for all}\ i\in[n-1].

Indeed, if
v_{in}=0
for all
i\in[n-1]
then all the Plücker-type inequalities of the form

v_{ij}v_{nl}\leq v_{in}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jn}

become trivial and only the defining inequalities for
\Pl_{n-1}
remain.
On the combinatorial side,
G_{\iota_{n}(v)}
is the graph obtained from
G_{v}
by appending the
n
-th node as an isolated node.

5.2.
Action of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\times\Sym_{n}

We next consider the action of the group
\Gamma_{n}:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\times\Sym_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
given by

(\lambda,\sigma)\cdot(v_{ij})=\left(\lambda_{\sigma(i)}\lambda_{\sigma(j)}v_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\right),\quad\text{for }(v_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}.

Here
\Sym_{n}
is the symmetric group on
n
elements. It is easy to see that both

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
and
\Pl_{n}
are invariant under the action of
\Gamma_{n}
. For
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
it follows since we can permute and rescale by positive
scalars the convex sets in the tuple
K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{n})
. For
\Pl_{n}
this is true as the Plücker-type inequalities (
PT
)
are symmetric and homogeneous. Therefore, when proving
\Pl_{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
it is enough to check that
a representative from each
\Gamma_{n}
-orbit in
\Pl_{n}
lies in
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
.

Let
\tilde{\Pl}_{n}=\Pl_{n}/\Gamma_{n}
be the quotient space. Using the embedding (
5.1
) we can identify
\tilde{\Pl}_{n-1}
with a subset of
\tilde{\Pl}_{n}
. We have the following ascending chain

\tilde{\Pl}_{1}\subset\tilde{\Pl}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\tilde{\Pl}_{n-1}\subset\tilde{\Pl}_{n}\subset\cdots

The next proposition describes the difference set
\tilde{\Pl}_{n}\setminus\tilde{\Pl}_{n-1}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

For a partition
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s}
with
s\geq 2
and
1\leq n_{1}\leq\dots\leq n_{s}
, let

\Pl_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}:=\{v\in\Pl_{n}\,:\,G_{v}\cong\KG_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}\}.

Then

\tilde{\Pl}_{n}\setminus\tilde{\Pl}_{n-1}=\bigcup_{n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s}}\tilde{\Pl}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}},

where the union is over all partitions of
n
as above. Moreover,
\dim\tilde{\Pl}_{n_{1},n_{2}}=0
and

\dim\tilde{\Pl}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}\leq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq s}n_{i}n_{j}-n.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Proposition
5.1
. For the second statement, first note that

\dim\Pl_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}
is at most the number of edges in
G:=\KG_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}
, which is
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq s}n_{i}n_{j}
. Let
s>2
.
We claim that for any
v\in\Pl_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}
there exists
g\in\Gamma_{n}
such that
n
of the coordinates of
g\cdot v
are equal to
1
. This implies the bound for
\dim\tilde{\Pl}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{s}}
.

To prove the
claim, we show that there exist a set
E^{\prime}
of
n
edges of
G
and a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
such that
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}v_{ij}=1
for all
\{i,j\}\in E^{\prime}
. Let
M
be the incidence matrix of
G
whose rows correspond to the edges of
G
. It is well-known that
{\mathrm{rk}}(M)=n
when
G
contains an odd cycle (which happens if and only if
s>2
) and
{\mathrm{rk}}(M)=n-1
, otherwise. (See, for example,
[
OH98
, Prop. 1.3]
.)
Let
E^{\prime}
be a subset of edges of
G
for which the corresponding
n\times n
submatrix
M^{\prime}
is of full rank. We obtain a polynomial system in
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n

\{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}v_{ij}=1\,:\,\{i,j\}\in E^{\prime}\}.

By taking the natural logarithm of both sides we can reduce it to an
n\times n

linear system in
t_{i}=\ln\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n

\{t_{i}+t_{j}=-\ln(v_{ij})\,:\,\{i,j\}\in E^{\prime}\}.

Since
{\mathrm{rk}}(M^{\prime})=n
, this system has a solution for any values of
v_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\{i,j\}\in E^{\prime}
, which proves the claim.

Now let
s=2
, i.e.
G
is bipartite. Assume
V_{1}:=\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
and
V_{2}:=\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n\}
are the two parts of the nodes of
G
. Consider
the set of
n-1
edges

E^{\prime}=\left\{\{1,j\},\{i,n_{1}+1\}\,:\,i\in V_{1},j\in V_{2}\right\},

and let
M^{\prime}
be the corresponding
(n-1)\times n
submatrix of the incidence matrix of
G
. Since
E^{\prime}
is a tree,
M^{\prime}
is of full rank. As above,
this implies that, after the action by an element of
\Gamma_{n}
, the coordinates of
v
satisfy
v_{1,j}=v_{i,n_{1}+1}=1
for all
i\in V_{1}
,
j\in V_{2}
.
By Lemma
5.2
, we have
v_{i,j}v_{1,n_{1}+1}=v_{i,n_{1}+1}v_{1,j}
which implies
v_{i,j}=1
for all
i\in V_{1}
,
j\in V_{2}
. In other words,

\dim\tilde{\Pl}_{n_{1},n_{2}}=0
.
∎

This proposition shows that one can view
\tilde{\Pl}_{n}
as a union of non-labeled weighted graphs on
n
nodes, where each graph is a complete multipartite graph on
m
nodes together with
n-m
isolated nodes, for some
0\leq m\leq n
. Moreover, if the graph is non-bipartite,
m
of the weights can be chosen to be 1; and if it is bipartite all of the weights can be chosen to be 1. Note, that the bound on the dimension can be strict. For example, consider

\tilde{\Pl}_{1,1,2}
. Using the group action we can make four of the weights to be
1
(as long as the corresponding edges do not form a 4-cycle), e.g.,
we can put
v_{12}=v_{23}=v_{13}=v_{24}=1
. (Here we chose the partition
\{2\}\sqcup\{3\}\sqcup\{1,4\}=[4]
.) Since
v_{14}=0
, Lemma
5.2
implies that
v_{34}=1
as well. Therefore,
\dim\tilde{\Pl}_{1,1,2}=0
.
Applying Proposition
5.3
for
n=4
we see that
\tilde{\Pl}_{4}
consists of seven 0-dimensional and one 2-dimensional components corresponding to the eight weighted graphs depicted in Figure

7.
Semialgebraicity of
\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}

In this section we study semialgebraicity of the pure mixed volume configuration space.
Recall that a subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is called a
basic closed semialgebraic set
if there exist polynomials

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
such that
S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,:\,f_{i}(x)\geq 0,i\in[m]\}
. A
semialgebraic set
is a
set generated by a finite sequence of set-theoretic operations (union, intersection, and complement) on
basic closed semialgebraic sets. We refer to
[
BCR98
]
for all necessary definitions regarding semialgebraicity.

As we have seen in the previous section,
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)=\Pl_{n}
for
n=2,3,4
. In particular, it is a basic closed semialgebraic set.
For the general case, the results of
Section
4
imply that
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)\subseteq\Pl_{n}
for any
n\geq 4
. In Theorem
7.3
below we show that
this inclusion is proper starting with
n\geq 8
. We do not know whether
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)=\Pl_{n}
for
n=5,6,7
. Neither do we know if

{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
is closed for
n\geq 5
. Nevertheless, we can show that the closure
\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}
is a semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
, for all
n\geq 2
, see Theorem
7.2
.

We start with a slightly more general situation. We say a map
\phi:{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}

is
additive
if

\phi(\alpha K+\beta L)=\alpha\,\phi(K)+\beta\,\phi(L),\quad\text{for all }K,L\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\text{ and }\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

Note that the image
\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})
is a cone in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We say that
\phi
is
continuous
if it is continuous with respect to the Hausdorff metric
in
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
and Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
denote the space of all convex polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}
the subset of convex polytopes with at most
m
vertices. We have the following lemma.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\phi:{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be additive. Then
\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2})=\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})
. Consequently,
when
\phi
is additive and continuous, the closure
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
coincides with
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})}
.

Proof.

As we mentioned before, every convex polygon
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is the Minkowski sum of segments and triangles, thus we can write

P=\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
T_{i}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2,3}
. By additivity, this implies that
\phi(P)
lies in the
cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
generated by
\phi(T_{1}),\dots,\phi(T_{N})
. By the Carathéodory theorem for cones, there exist
I\subseteq[N]
of
size
|I|\leq k
and
\{\alpha_{i}\,:\,i\in I\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, such that

\phi(P)=\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\phi(T_{i})=\phi\Big(\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}T_{i}\Big).

As the polytope
\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}T_{i}
has at most
3k
vertices, we see that
\phi(P)\in\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})
, as claimed.

It remains to show
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})}=\overline{\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})}
. The containment
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})}\subseteq\overline{\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})}

is clear, as
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
. Conversely, since
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}=\overline{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}}
and
\phi
is continuous, we have

\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})=\phi(\overline{{\mathcal{P}}_{2}})\subseteq\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2})}=\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})},

which implies
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{K}}_{2})}\subseteq\overline{\phi({\mathcal{P}}_{2,3k})}
.
∎

Recall that a map between two semialgebraic sets is called semialgebraic if its graph is a semialgebraic set, see
[
BCR98
, Sec 2]
. The above proposition shows that to understand semialgebraicity of an additive continuous map

\phi:{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
it is enough to consider its restriction to the finite-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}\subset{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
. Note that the space
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}
is parametrized by
2m
real numbers, the coordinates of the vertices of polytopes in
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}
. It is not hard to see that the area functional
\Vol_{2}:{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}\to\mathbb{R},P\mapsto\Vol_{2}(P)
is semialgebraic. Since the mixed area is a composition of Minkowski addition, the area functional, and arithmetic operations (see (
2.2
)), it follows that the mixed area functional

\V:{{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}\times{{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(P_{1},P_{2})\mapsto\V(P_{1},P_{2})
is also semialgebraic.
The proofs of these statements, although straightforward, are rather technical, so we put them in Appendix
10
.

The next theorem is the main result of this section.

Theorem 7.2
.

For any
n\geq 2
the closure of the pure mixed volume configuration space
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
is a semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
.

Proof.

Let
P=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be a tuple of convex polytopes in
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}

and
W_{P}
the corresponding pure mixed area configuration vector, see (
2.7
).
Consider the map
\phi:{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
which sends
P_{i}
to the
n-1
mixed areas
(\V(P_{i},P_{j})\,:\,j\in[n],j\neq i)
.
This map is additive and continuous. By Proposition
7.1
, there exists
Q_{i}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}
such that

\phi(K_{i})=\phi(Q_{i})
, where
m=3(n-1)
.
Repeating this argument for every
i\in[n]
we see that there exists
Q=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})\in{{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}^{\!\!\!n}

such that
W_{P}=W_{Q}
. Let
\psi:{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}
be the map defined by
\psi(K)=W_{K}
, for
K\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n}
.
We have, thus, shown that
\psi({{\mathcal{P}}_{2}}^{\!n})=\psi({{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}^{\!\!\!n})
.
Similarly to the proof of Proposition
7.1
, we have
\overline{\psi({{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}^{\!\!\!n})}=\overline{\psi({{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n})}=\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}
.
Since the closure of a semialgebraic set is semialgebraic, it remains to check the semialgebraicity of
\psi({{\mathcal{P}}_{2,m}}^{\!\!\!n})
, which
follows immediately from the semialgebraicity of the mixed area, see Proposition
10.1
.
∎

Theorem 7.3
.

For any
n\geq 4
we have

\mathcal{PMV}(n,2)\subseteq\Pl_{n}:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}\,:\,v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\ \text{\rm for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}\subseteq[n]\right\}.

Moreover, for
n\geq 8
the above inclusion is proper.

Proof.

The inclusion follows from the proof of Theorem
3.2
. Suppose
n=8
and consider the subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{8\choose 2}

given by

X=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{8\choose 2}\,:\,v_{i,i+4}=0,v_{i,l}=v_{i+4,l},\text{ for }i\in[4],\ l\in[8]\setminus\{i,i+4\}\right\}.

We claim that
\dim\left(X\cap\Pl_{8}\right)=6
while
\dim\left(X\cap{\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)\right)=5
, hence,

{\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)\subsetneq\Pl_{8}
. Indeed, the former is clear since
X\cap\Pl_{8}
can be identified
with
\Pl_{4}
(each coordinate of
v\in X
is either zero or equals
v_{ij}
for
some
\{i,j\}\subset[4]
; and the Plücker-type inequalities restricted to
X
are either trivial or coincide with
the ones for
\Pl_{4}
). For the latter, note that
the conditions on the mixed areas
\V(K_{i},K_{i+4})=0

imply that
K_{i}
and
K_{i+4}
are parallel segments and the conditions
\V(K_{i},K_{l})=\V(K_{i+4},K_{l})

ensure that
K_{i}
and
K_{i+4}
have the same lengths. Thus,
X\cap{\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)

can be identified with the subset

Y=\left\{\V(K_{i},K_{j})\in{\mathcal{PMV}}(4,2)\,:\,K_{1},\dots,K_{4}\text{ are segments}\right\}.

But in this case
\V(K_{i},K_{j})

satisfy the Plücker relation (
4.1
) and, therefore,

\dim\left(X\cap{\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)\right)=\dim Y=5.

For
n\geq 8
, let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n\choose 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{8\choose 2}
be the projection given by
\pi(v)=(v_{ij}\,:\,\{i,j\}\subset[8])
.
Clearly,
\pi({\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2))={\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)
. Also,
\pi(\Pl_{n})=\Pl_{8}
, as
\Pl_{8}
can embedded into
\Pl_{n}
using the
map (
5.1
) iterated
n-8
times. Since
{\mathcal{PMV}}(8,2)\neq\Pl_{8}
, it follows that
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)\neq\Pl_{n}
as well.
∎

8.
Dimensions of
{{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)}
and
{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}

In this section we show that
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1\choose 2}
has a nonempty interior and, hence,
has full topological dimension. Since
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
is the projection of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
onto
{n\choose 2}
coordinates, it is full-dimensional as well.

Theorem 8.1
.

For any
n\geq 2
the topological dimension of
{\mathcal{MV}}(n,2)
equals
{n+1\choose 2}
. Consequently,
the topological dimension of
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
equals
{n\choose 2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2}
be the space of quadratic forms in
n
-variables with real coefficients.
Consider the map
\V:{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n}\to\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2}
which sends an
n
-tuple

K=(K_{1},\dots,K_{n})
to the polynomial
f_{K}=\Vol_{2}(x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n})
.

As we mentioned in the introduction,
all mixed areas
\V(K_{i},K_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
appear in
the coefficients of
f_{K}
:

(8.1)

f_{K}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Vol_{2}(K_{i})x_{i}^{2}\,+\,2\!\!\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\!\!\V(K_{i},K_{j})x_{i}x_{j}.

The idea of the proof is to consider a finite-dimensional subspace
\cal L\subset{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n}
such that
the restriction
\V:\cal L\to\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2}
is locally surjective.

We start with the
n
-tuple of segments
I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{n})\in{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}^{\!\!n}
, where
I_{i}=[0,e_{1}+ie_{2}]
.
Let
\cal L
be the space of
n
-tuples of polytopes which are Minkowski sums of the
I_{i}
dilated by some non-negative factors:

\cal L=\left\{\left(a_{11}I_{1}+\dots+a_{n1}I_{n},\ \ldots,\ a_{1n}I_{1}+\dots+a_{nn}I_{n}\right)\,:\,a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

It is convenient to use matrix multiplication notation
IA
for the
n
-tuple above, where
A\in\M(n,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. Similarly, we denote

Kx:=x_{1}K_{1}+\dots+x_{n}K_{n}
. Then our goal is to show that the map
\V
which sends
IA
to
f_{IA}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2}
is locally surjective.

Fix a matrix
A\in\GL(n,\mathbb{R}_{>0})
. Note that
f_{IA}(x)=\Vol_{2}(IAx)=f_{I}(Ax)
. Thus, for any
H\in\M(n,\mathbb{R})
we can write using Taylor expansion

f_{I}((A+H)x)=f_{I}(Ax)+\langle\nabla f_{I}(Ax),Hx\rangle+O(\|Hx\|^{2}).

This shows that the Frechét derivative of
\V
at point
IA
is the linear map
L_{A}:H\mapsto\langle\nabla f_{I}(Ax),Hx\rangle
.
To see that
\V
is locally surjective near
IA
we need to check that
L_{A}
is surjective.

Indeed, by (
8.1
),

f_{I}(x)=2\!\!\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\!\!(j-i)x_{i}x_{j}=x^{\rm T}Tx,

where
T=(|i-j|)_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\GL(n,\mathbb{R})
is a Toeplitz matrix.
Hence,
\nabla f_{I}(x)=2Tx
and we can write
L_{A}(H)=2\langle TAx,Hx\rangle
. Since
TA\in\GL(n,\mathbb{R})
we can apply a change
of variables
y=TAx
so that
L_{A}(HTA)=2\langle TAx,HTAx\rangle=2\langle y,Hy\rangle
. This shows that in appropriate bases for

\M(n,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{2}
, the map
L_{A}
is simply
H\mapsto 2\langle x,Hx\rangle
, which is clearly surjective.
∎

9.
Application to Algebraic and Tropical Geometry

In this section we discuss direct consequences of our results in the intersection theory of algebraic curves and tropical curves. For all necessary definitions in toric and tropical geometry and interconnection between the two areas
we refer the reader to introductory articles
[
RGST05
,
BB13
,
BS14
]
or popular textbooks
[
Stu02
,
MS15
]
.

Prior to formulating the consequences, we shortly introduce the basic notions of tropical geometry.
The tropical semiring is the set
{\mathbb{T}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
endowed with the tropical addition
\oplus:=\min
and the tropical multiplication
\odot:=+
, where
+\infty
is neutral with respect to the tropical addition and thus is the tropical zero. A
tropical Laurent polynomial

is an expression of the form

f:=\bigoplus_{(a,b)\in S}c_{a,b}\odot x^{a}\odot y^{b},

where
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a finite set and
c_{a,b}\in\mathbb{R}
. The convex hull
\conv(S)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

is called the Newton polytope of
f
and is denoted by
P_{f}
.

In the classical arithmetic operations over reals,
f
is expressed as the piecewise-linear function

(9.1)

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\min{\left\{c_{a,b}+ax+by\,:\,(a,b)\in S\right\}}.

We define the
tropical curve

V(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
associated to
f
as the set of all points
(x,y)
, in which the piecewise linear function
f
is not differentiable, that is where the minimum in (
9.1
) is attained at least twice.
The set
V(f)
has the structure of a one-dimensional polyhedral complex; it has vertices and edges. The polyhedral complex
V(f)
is dual to the regular polyhedral subdivision of
P_{f}
obtained by lifting
(a,b)
to height
c_{a,b}
. This subdivision induces weights on vertices and edges of
V(f)
. The weight
\omega(v)
of a vertex
v
in
V(f)
is the normalized area of the respective two-dimensional cell in the regular subdivision. The weight
\omega(e)
of an edge
e
in
V(f)
is the lattice length of the respective edge in the regular subdivision.

For tropical bivariate polynomials
f
and
g
, the tropical curves
V(f)
and
V(g)
are said to intersect transversally if
V(f)\cap V(g)
is a finite set and if each intersection point
p\in V(f)\cap V(g)
lies in the relative interior of an edge
e
of
V(f)
and an edge
h
of
V(g)
. The
multiplicity
of a transversal intersection point
p
is defined to be
\omega(e)\omega(h)|\det(u,v)|
, where
u
and
v
are primitive vectors in the direction of
e
and
h
, respectively. The total number of intersection points, counting multiplicities, is called the
intersection number
of
V(f)
and
V(g)
and is denoted
I(V(f),V(g))
.

The following result connects the intersection number of tropical curves and the mixed area.

Theorem 9.1

(Tropical BKK theorem in the plane)
.

Let
f,g
be bivariate tropical polynomials, for which the tropical curves
V(f)
and
V(g)
intersect transversally. Then the intersection number

I(V(f),V(g))
is equal to
2\V(P_{f},P_{g})
.

We now can consider arrangements of
n
tropical curves
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
whose pairwise intersections are transversal. We call such an arrangement transversal. For
\{i,j\}\subset[n]
, let
I(C_{i},C_{j})
be the intersection number of
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
. In this way,
with the arrangement
C=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})
we associate the configuration vector

I_{C}:=\Bigl(I(C_{i},C_{j})\,:\,\{i,j\}\subset[n]\Bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\binom{n}{2}}

of the intersection numbers of
C
. Analogously to
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
, we can consider the set of all such configuration vectors over all transversal arrangements of
n
tropical curves:

\cal I(n,2):={\left\{I_{C}\,:\,C\ \text{transversal arrangement of $n$ planar tropical curves}\right\}}.

Note that finding a description of
\cal I(n,2)
is a more intricate task than describing
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
. Because of the discrete nature of
\cal I(n,2)
, it is expected that number-theoretic aspects would be involved when describing
\cal I(n,2)
. Nevertheless,
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
is intimately related to
\cal I(n,2)
in the following sense.

We say that a subset
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
positively homogeneous
if
x\in H
implies
\lambda x\in H

for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Clearly, for any
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the set

\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,X=\{\lambda x\,:\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\,x\in X\}

is the minimal, with respect to inclusion, positively homogeneous set containing
X
. We have the following proposition.

Proposition 9.2
.

The inclusion-minimal positively homogeneous closed set containing
\cal I(n,2)
coincides with the topological closure of
{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
. In other words,

\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\cal I(n,2)}=\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.1
,

\cal I(n,2)={\left\{2\V_{P}\,:\,P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})\ \text{is an $n$-tuple of planar lattice polytopes}\right\}},

where the polytope can be a polygon, a segment, or a point.
This implies
\cal I(n,2)\subseteq{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)
and so
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\cal I(n,2)}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}
.

Conversely, note that
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\cal I(n,2)
contains the set

{\left\{\V_{P}\,:\,P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})\ \text{is an $n$-tuple of planar rational polytopes}\right\}}.

Since the set of all planar rational polytopes is dense in the set of all nonempty planar compact convex sets and since the mixed area is continuous in each argument with respect to the Hausdorff metric, we see
that
\overline{{\mathcal{PMV}}(n,2)}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,\cal I(n,2)}
.

∎

Corollary 9.3
.

The Plücker space

\Pl_{4}=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4\choose 2}\,:\,v_{ij}v_{kl}\leq v_{ik}v_{jl}+v_{il}v_{jk}\ \text{\rm for }\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=[4]\right\}

is the inclusion-minimal positively homogeneous closed set containing
\cal I(4,2)
.

Proof.

This is a direct corollary of Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
9.2
.
∎

One can interpret this corollary as follows: The closure of the subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{6}
described by all (infinitely many) homogeneous polynomial inequalities valid for the six intersection numbers of any quadruple of tropical curves, is a semialgebraic set determined by the three Plücker type inequalities (
3.1
).

The application in toric geometry is completely analogous. A Laurent polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]
of the form

f=\sum_{(a,b)\in S}c_{a,b}x^{a}y^{b},\ \ \text{with}\ c_{a,b}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}

defines the curve

V(f):={\left\{(x,y)\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}\,:\,f(x,y)=0\right\}}
in the algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}
. As before, the finite subset
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}

is the support of
f
and its convex hull
P_{f}:=\conv(S)
is the Newton polytope of
f
.

The next theorem is a 2-dimensional case of the BKK theorem for the number of solutions of generic polynomial systems with fixed supports.
In order to make the notion “generic” precise, we recall the definition of a
facial subsystem
in our situation.
Let
f
be a bivariate Laurent polynomial with Newton polytope
P_{f}
. Given
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, let

P_{f}^{u}=\max\{\langle u,v\rangle\,:\,v\in P_{f}\}
be the corresponding face of
P_{f}
. Furthermore, let
f^{u}
be the Laurent
polynomial consisting of those terms of
f
whose support lies in
P_{f}^{u}

f^{u}=\sum_{(a,b)\in S\cap P_{f}^{u}}c_{a,b}x^{a}y^{b}.

If
f,g
are two Laurent polynomials then
any
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
defines a facial subsystem
f^{u}=g^{u}=0
.

Theorem 9.4

(BKK theorem in the plane)
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]
be Laurent polynomials with Newton polytopes
P_{f}
and
P_{g}
such that
for any
u\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
the facial subsystem
f^{u}=g^{u}=0
has no solutions in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}
.
Then
V(f)\cap V(g)
is a subset of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{2}
consisting of exactly
2\V(P_{f},P_{g})
points, counting multiplicities.

The condition of Theorem
9.4
is called the BKK condition. Note that using an appropriate
monomial change of variables each facial subsystem
f^{u}=g^{u}=0
can be turned to a univariate system. It has a solution
if and only if the resultant of the two polynomials vanishes. Therefore, the BKK condition is satisfied
for any
f,g
whose coefficients lie outside of an algebraic hypersurface in the coefficient space.

Theorem
9.4
provides yet another interpretation of
\cal I(n,2)
:

\cal I(n,2):={\left\{|V(f)\cap V(g)|\,:\,f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]\ \text{satisfy the BKK condition}\right\}}.

In light of this interpretation, Corollary
9.3
can be viewed as a toric geometry result.

We end with an example illustrating Corollary
9.3
.

f_{1},\dots,f_{4}

Appendix

In this appendix we give a formal proof of the semialgebraicity of the area and mixed area functionals.

Recall that a
first-order formula (over reals)
is a formula obtained from a finite set of polynomial inequalities
f(x)\geq 0
for
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
by applying a finite number of negations
\neg
, conjunctions
\wedge
, disjunctions
\lor
, and quantifiers
\exists
,
\forall
on the variables. See
[
BCR98
, Sec 2]
for details.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
s,t\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the following conditions and functions depending on

p_{1},\dots,p_{s},q_{1},\dots,q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(a)

\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s}):=
“
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
are vertices of a convex
s
-gon listed
in an counterclockwise order”,

(b)

\text{\rm EqConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t}):=
“
\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
equals
\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})
”,

(c)

\text{\rm VolConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s}):=\Vol_{2}(\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s}))
,

(d)

\text{\rm VolSumConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t}):=\Vol_{2}(\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})+\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t}))
,

(e)

\text{\rm MixedArea}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t}):=\V(\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s}),\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t}))
.

Then the conditions (a) and (b) can be expressed by first-order formulas and the functions (c), (d) and (e) are semialgebraic.

Proof.

(a) As the cases
s=1,2
are trivial, so we assume
s\geq 3
. Then we have the following first-order formula
for
\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
:

\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{s}\bigwedge_{j\in[s]\setminus\{i,i+1\}}\det(p_{i+1}-p_{i},p_{j}-p_{i})>0,

where the indices are considered modulo
s
.

(b) Note that
\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
equals
\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})
if and only if
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
lie in

\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})
and
q_{1},\dots q_{t}
lie in
\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
. Therefore we can express

\text{\rm EqConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t})
as the conjunction of

\bigwedge_{i=1}^{s}\left(\exists\lambda_{i_{1}},\dots,\exists\lambda_{i_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:(\lambda_{i_{1}}+\dots+\lambda_{i_{t}}=1)\wedge(p_{i}=\lambda_{i_{1}}q_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{i_{t}}q_{t})\right)

and

\bigwedge_{j=1}^{t}\left(\exists\mu_{j_{1}},\dots,\exists\mu_{j_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:(\mu_{j_{1}}+\dots+\mu_{j_{s}}=1)\wedge(q_{j}=\mu_{j_{1}}p_{1}+\dots+\mu_{j_{s}}p_{s})\right),

which is a first-order formula.

(c) Let
P=\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
and assume that
q_{1},\dots,q_{t}
are the vertices of
P
in the counterclockwise order, in particular,
\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})
is true. Then
\Vol_{2}(P)=0
for
t=1,2
and

\Vol_{2}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}\det(q_{i+1}-q_{1},q_{i}-q_{1}),\text{ for }t\geq 3,

which is clearly a polynomial in the coordinates of the
q_{i}
. Now,
since
P
has at most
s
vertices, the statement

\exists q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\dots,\exists q_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,\bigvee_{t=1}^{s}\left(\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})\wedge\text{\rm EqConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t})\right)

is true. Therefore, for
V\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
we can express the condition
V=\Vol_{2}(P)
as

\displaystyle\exists q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\dots,\exists q_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,

\displaystyle\bigvee_{t=1}^{s}\Big(\text{\rm OrdVertSet}(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})\wedge\text{\rm EqConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s};q_{1},\dots,q_{t})\wedge\big(V=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}\det(q_{i+1}-q_{1},q_{i}-q_{1})\big)\Big),

which is a first-order formula. This shows that the graph of the function
\text{\rm VolConv}(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})

is semialgebraic, i.e. it is a semialgebraic function.

(d) We have
\conv(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})+\conv(q_{1},\dots,q_{t})=\conv(p_{i}+q_{j}:1\leq i\leq s,1\leq j\leq t)
. Thus,
the function
VolSumConv
is the composition of the linear map

\phi:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{s+t}\to(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{st},\quad(p_{1},\dots,p_{s},q_{1},\dots,q_{t})\mapsto(p_{i}+q_{j}:1\leq i\leq s,1\leq j\leq t)

and the function
VolConv
from part (c). Therefore, the semialgebraicity of
VolSumConv

follows from that of
VolConv
.

(e) This immediately follows from parts (c) and (d) and the formula (
2.2
) for the mixed area

\V(P_{1},P_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Vol_{2}(P_{1}+P_{2})-\Vol_{2}(P_{1})-\Vol_{2}(P_{2})\right).

∎

We remark that with some additional work the proofs can be extended to the case of
d
-dimensional volume and mixed volume, but
it is not needed for the purposes of this paper.
