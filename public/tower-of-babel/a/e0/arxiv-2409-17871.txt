On factorisations of Left dead ends
Alfie Davies

Abstract
Losing a game is difficult. Recent work of Larsson, Nowakowski, and Santos, as well as that of Siegel, has opened up enticing lines of research into partizan misère theory. The algebraic structure of misère monoids is not yet well understood. A natural object to study is a universe of games—a set of games satisfying various closure properties. We prove that a universe is determined by its Left ends, thus motivating a study of such games, which was also noted by Siegel for dead ending universes in particular. We initiate this study by investigating the atomic structure of the monoid of Left dead ends, showing that it is reduced, pocancellative, and an FF-monoid. We give sharp bounds on the lengths of factorisations, and also build tools to find atoms. Some results require the introduction of a new concept called flexibility , which is a measure of the distance from a game to integers. We also discover large families of games admitting unique factorisations; this includes the uncovering of a prime element: 1 ¯ \overline{1} .

Proposition

If

An immediate consequence of this is that there exists a bijective
correspondence between universes and sets of Left ends that are additively
closed and hereditary (with respect to Left ends; i.e. for all Left ends in
the set, all subpositions that are Left ends must also be in the set). Thus, to
understand universes, it is paramount that we understand Left ends. But this is
not an easy problem. Consider, for example, any game
G\in\mathcal{M}
. Then

\{\cdot\mid G\}
is a Left end. And, to study a game form, one usually needs
to also study its subpositions, so understanding Left ends appears no different
than understanding general misère forms. What are we to do? Luckily for us,
there are natural families of Left ends that lend themselves much more
willingly to analysis: one such set is the family of
Left dead ends
,
whose subpositions are all Left ends.

Crucially, the options of a Left dead end are necessarily themselves Left dead
ends; we can no longer have arbitrary forms as options, as in our earlier
example of
\{\cdot\mid G\}
. By our correspondence between sets of Left ends
and universes, it is clear that (additively closed and hereditary) sets of Left
dead ends correspond precisely with dead-ending universes. Our lofty goal in
this paper then is to understand the structure of these Left dead ends, which
we begin to in the proceeding sections, so that we may better understand these
(dead-ending) universes. Siegel
[
30

Our study has an algebraic slant; when equipped with the usual disjunctive

the monoid of Left dead ends.

The algebraic object of importance to us here is the commutative pomonoid (also

Of course, a pomonoid is trivially also a monoid. Writing

recall that

•

•

•

Note, of course, that, if every element of

It is a simple result that, in an atomic monoid, every prime is an atom. It is
not the case, in general, that every atom is prime. A key question we will ask

in such a monoid, there is a clear dichotomy: every element has a
factorisation, but not necessarily uniquely; conversely, not every element has
a prime factorisation, but, when it does, it is uniquely factorisable.

0\leq k\leq\tilde{\birth}(G)
. Then, as a simple corollary of
Theorem

2.4
, we
have the following result.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, and
k
is an integer
satisfying
0\leq k\leq\tilde{\birth}(G)
, then, for any run

(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
of
G
of length
k
, there exists a run

(H_{0},H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
of
H
of length
k
such that
G_{i}\geq H_{i}
for
every
0\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

Take a run
(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
of
G
of length
k
. By hypothesis, we
have
G_{0}\geq H
. Then, by
Theorem

2.4
, we know that there exists an
option
H_{1}
of
H
such that
G_{1}\geq H_{1}
. By induction on the formal
birthday, given the run
(G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
of
G_{1}
of length
k-1
, there
exists a run
(H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
of
H_{1}
of length
k-1
such that
G_{i}\geq H_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Thus, the run
(H,H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
of
H
of
length
k
satisfies the requirements of the conclusion, yielding the
result.
∎

It will simplify our later exposition if we give the following definition of a

good
option. Without it, we would have to make copious references to the
canonical form of a game, which the reader is free to do themselves if they so
wish.

Definition 3.3
.

If
G
is a combinatorial game, then we will say that a (Left, respectively
Right) option
G^{\prime}
of
G
is
good
if it is not strictly dominated by
another (Left, respectively Right) option of
G
.

So, an option
G^{\prime}
of a Left dead end
G
is good if there exists no option

G^{\prime\prime}
with
G^{\prime}>G^{\prime\prime}
. Equivalently,
G^{\prime}
is good if it is equal to some option
that appears in the canonical form of
G
; this follows from
[
30
, Theorem 3.7
on p. 8]
, which establishes that the canonical form of a Left dead
end is itself a Left dead end, and is obtained by removing all dominated
options from all subpositions of the game.

Given
Definition

3.3
, we will state another obvious corollary of

Theorem

2.4
that we will have use for later on. Note, of course, that
every non-zero Left dead end has at least one good option.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, and
G^{\prime}
is a good option
of
G
, then there exists a (good) option
H^{\prime}
of
H
such that
G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime}
. In particular, if
G=H
, then
G^{\prime}=H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.4
, there exists some option
H^{\prime\prime}
of
H
such that

G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime\prime}
. By
Definition

3.3
, there exists a good option
H^{\prime}
of

H
(possibly
H^{\prime\prime}
itself) such that
H^{\prime\prime}\geq H^{\prime}
. By transitivity of the
relation, it is immediate that
G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime}
.

If
G=H
, then, in addition to our previous argument, we know also that

H\geq G
, and so there must exist some good option
G^{\prime\prime}
of
G
such that

G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime}\geq G^{\prime\prime}
. Since
G^{\prime}
is a good option by hypothesis, it follows
that
G^{\prime}=G^{\prime\prime}
straight from
Definition

3.3
. Thus,
G^{\prime}=H^{\prime}
.
∎

It is unknown for most misère monoids whether they exhibit cancellativity or
not. Luckily for us, Left dead ends are very well-behaved, and we prove shortly
that the monoid of Left dead-ends is pocancellative. This would also follow
from recent work by the present author, McKay, Milley, Nowakowski, and Santos

[
8
]
, which shows that
\mathcal{E}
is
pocancellative—whereas
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{M}
are not. We do not
need all this machinery, however, and we include a concise proof here for our
special case.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is a universe,
G,H\in\mathcal{U}
are Left dead ends, and

J
is a pocancellative element of
\mathcal{U}
satisfying
G+J\geq H+J
,
then
G\geq H
.

Proof.

Observe that
G+J\geq H+J
implies
G+J\geq_{\mathcal{U}}H+J
by

Corollary

2.5
. Since
J
is pocancellative in
\mathcal{U}
, it
follows that
G\geq_{\mathcal{U}}H
, yielding the result via

Corollary

2.5
.
∎

It is not necessary for us here, but an analogous lemma holds where
pocancellativity is replaced with cancellativity, whose proof is almost
identical. We simply state it below.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is a universe,
G,H\in\mathcal{U}
are Left dead ends, and

J
is a cancellative element of
\mathcal{U}
satisfying
G+J=H+J
, then

G=H
.

Recalling a result of Milley and Renault
[
20
, Theorem 6 on
p. 2226]
that says Left dead ends are invertible in

\mathcal{E}
(the dead ending universe), we now have the tools to show that
our monoid is pocancellative.

Theorem 3.7
.

The monoid of Left dead ends
\mathcal{L}
is pocancellative.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from
Lemma

3.5
after
remarking that Left dead ends are invertible, and hence pocancellative, in

\mathcal{E}
.
∎

While we have used the invertibility of Left dead ends in
\mathcal{E}
to
apply
Lemma

3.5
, it is unclear how, in an arbitrary universe,
pocancellativity differs from invertibility (the latter, of course, always
implies the former). See
Appendix

E
for a brief discussion.

3.2
The group of differences

We take a very brief detour to prove a statement about the group of differences
of our monoid. Recall that, given a commutative monoid, we can always construct
an abelian group by simply introducing a formal inverse for each element; this
group is called the
group of differences
, or the
Grothendiek
group
, of the monoid. Since our monoid of Left dead ends is also cancellative
(
Theorem

3.7
), we in fact know that it must embed in its group of
differences.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
n\cdot G=n\cdot H
for some
n>0
,
then
G=H
.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then the result is immediate, so assume now that
n>1
.

If
G=0
, then
n\cdot 0=0=H
yields the result immediately. Otherwise, by

Corollary

3.4
, there exist options
G^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
such that

G^{\prime}+(n-1)\cdot G=H^{\prime}+(n-1)\cdot H.

(1)

Multiplying by
n
yields

n\cdot G^{\prime}+n(n-1)\cdot G=n\cdot H^{\prime}+n(n-1)\cdot H.

Since
n\cdot G=n\cdot H
by hypothesis, it is clear that
n(n-1)\cdot G=n(n-1)\cdot H
, and hence it follows by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
) that
n\cdot G^{\prime}=n\cdot H^{\prime}
. By induction, we
have
G^{\prime}=H^{\prime}
. It now follows from
Eq.

1
that
(n-1)\cdot G=(n-1)\cdot H
, from which it follows by induction again that
G=H
, which
is the result.
∎

This result, and indeed its proof, is very similar to a result of Siegel’s in
the impartial misère monoid; in particular, see
[
29
, Theorem 29 on
p. 16]
for Siegel’s result, and

Appendix

A
for a further discussion on the similarities
between our two monoids.

Corollary 3.9
.

The Grothendiek group (i.e. the group of differences) of the monoid of
Left dead ends is torsion free.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{D}
for the Grothendiek group. Let
G-H\in\mathcal{D}
, and
suppose that
n\cdot(G-H)=0
for some
n
. Then
n\cdot G=n\cdot H
, and we
have by
Proposition

3.8
that
G=H
, and hence
G-H=0
.
∎

As such, the Grothendiek group of our monoid of Left dead-ends is necessarily a
(countable) torsion-free abelian group of countably infinite rank.

For the impartial misère monoid, Siegel proved that
*
is the only torsion
element of the group of differences
[
29
, Corollary 30 on
p. 16]
. As in the impartial case, we leave open the following
problem.

Open problem 3.10
.

What is the isomorphism type of the group of differences of the monoid of
Left dead ends?

3.3
Terminal lengths

As it stands, we have shown that our commutative pomonoid
\mathcal{L}
is
reduced and pocancellative. Before we show that it is also an FF-monoid, we
first develop some useful notions.

Definition 3.11
.

If
G
is a combinatorial game and
(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
is a run of
G

such that
G_{k}\cong 0
, then we say the run is
terminal
.

This is not to be confused with a
play
(cf.
[
27
, Definition 1.4 on
p. 9]
); we are not requiring the run to be alternating,
and we are requiring it to terminate at 0 rather than there just being no
options for the player whose turn it is.

Definition 3.12
.

If
G
is a short game, then we define the set of
terminal lengths

of
G
, denoted
\terminal(G)
, as the set of all
n
such that there
exists a terminal run of
G
of length
n
.

Note that the set of terminal lengths is defined on the strict form of
G
, and
not on its equivalence class modulo equality. For Left dead ends, however, we
will soon see in
Theorem

3.17
that it is indeed
well-defined up to equality. It is useful to remark that no game can have empty
terminal lengths; the empty game satisfies
\terminal(0)=\{0\}
.

Of course, we could have also defined terminal lengths recursively in the
following way, which will also provide a useful viewpoint. This is entirely
analogous to how one could define the formal birthday
\tilde{\birth}(G)
of a
short game
G
either as the height of the game tree, or recursively as one
more than the maximum formal birthday among its options (with the base case

\tilde{\birth}(0)\coloneq 0
). Recall that we are using Minkowski sums (sumset
notation) here.

Lemma 3.13
.

If
G
is a non-zero Left dead end, then

\terminal(G)=\bigcup(\terminal(G^{\prime})+1),

where
G^{\prime}
ranges over all options of
G
. In particular, it holds that

\terminal(G^{\prime})\subseteq\terminal(G)-1
for every option
G^{\prime}
of
G
.

Proof.

Observing that
(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
is a run of
G
if and only if
G_{1}

is an option of
G_{0}
and
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
is a run of
G_{1}
yields the
result immediately.
∎

For any short game
G
, we clearly have the identity

\tilde{\birth}(G)=\max(\terminal(G))
. We introduce an opposing notion that will
oft be useful in our study of Left dead ends: the
race
of a game, which
measures how quickly one can
race
to the finish.
6
6

6

It is
interesting to compare this idea with
[
20
, Definition 2 on
p. 2225]
.

Definition 3.14
.

If
G
is a short game, define the
race
of
G
, denoted
\race(G)
,
as

\race(G)\coloneq\min(\terminal(G)).

Of course, we could have defined
\race(G)
recursively as one more than the
minimum race amongst its options (with the base case
\race(0)\coloneq 0
), and
the reader is invited to confirm that this is equivalent to the definition
given above. For a brief discussion of why we have not created a split for race
and
formal
race, like there is for birthday and formal birthday, see

Appendix

B
.

We will say that a Left dead end is an
integer
if it is isomorphic
either to
0\coloneq\{\cdot\mid\cdot\}
or to

\overline{n}\coloneq\{\cdot\mid\overline{n-1}\}
for some
n\geq 1
(where

\overline{0}\coloneq 0
). It is then clear that

\race(\overline{n})=n=\tilde{\birth}(\overline{n})
. The following lemma
captures this observation and will prove useful later.

Lemma 3.15
.

A Left dead end
G
is an integer if and only if
|\terminal(G)|=1
. In
particular,
G=\overline{n}
if and only if
\terminal(G)=\{n\}
.

Proof.

The only terminal run of
\overline{n}
is

(\overline{n},\overline{n-1},\dots,0)
, which has length
n
. Thus, if
G

is an integer, then
|\terminal(G)|=1
.

Now suppose instead that
|\terminal(G)|=1
; say
\terminal(G)=\{n\}
. If

n=0
, then
G=0
. Otherwise, by
Lemma

3.13
, every option

G^{\prime}
of
G
satisfies
\terminal(G^{\prime})=\{n-1\}
. Hence
G^{\prime}=\overline{n-1}
by
induction, yielding that
G=\overline{n}
.
∎

Another family of Left dead ends that will be important to us is the class of

waiting games
(see
[
16
, Definition 3 on
p. 9]
, but note the different name and notation):
we write
W_{n}\coloneq\{\cdot\mid 0,W_{n-1}\}
for the waiting game of rank
n

for every
n>0
, with
W_{0}\coloneq 0
. It is a simple observation that

\terminal(W_{n})=\{t\in\mathbb{N}:1\leq t\leq n\}
for every
n>0
.

To confirm the reader’s understanding up to this point, they may wish to verify
that the set of terminal lengths of
G=\{\cdot\mid W_{2},\overline{3}\}
is the
set
\terminal(G)=\{2,3,4\}
. In particular,
\race(G)=2
and

\tilde{\birth}(G)=4
.

We state some obvious properties of terminal lengths relating to 0 that we will
refer back to later.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then the following are equivalent:

1.

G=0
;

2.

G\cong 0
;

3.

0\in\terminal(G)
;

4.

\race(G)=0
;

5.

\birth(G)=0
.

Proof.

We have already seen that (1) and (2) are equivalent by a result of Mesdal
and Ottaway
[
18
, Theorem 7 on p. 5]
.
Additionally, it is trivial that (1) and (5) are equivalent. Clearly

(2)\implies(3)
and
(4)
. Then,
(3)\implies(4)
by definition of race.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
(4)\implies(2)
. But if

\race(G)=0
, then no moves can be made, and so immediately we have

G\cong 0
.
∎

We now prove a powerful and surprising implication of two Left dead ends being
comparable, namely that their terminal lengths are also comparable (by
inclusion).

Theorem 3.17
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, then

\terminal(G)\subseteq\terminal(H)
.

Proof.

If
G\cong 0
, then
H\cong 0
by
Theorem

2.4
, and we have the result.
Otherwise, again by
Theorem

2.4
, for every option
G^{R}
, there
exists some option
H^{R}
with
G^{R}\geq H^{R}
. By induction, we must have

\terminal(G^{R})\subseteq\terminal(H^{R})
. Then, by

Lemma

3.13
, observe

\displaystyle\terminal(G)

\displaystyle=\bigcup(\terminal(G^{\prime})+1)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup(\terminal(H^{\prime})+1)

\displaystyle=\terminal(H),

where
G^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
range over all options of
G
and
H
respectively,
which yields the result.
∎

For an alternative proof that constructs an explicit distinguishing game, see

Appendix

C
.

Corollary 3.18
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, then

\race(G)\geq\race(H)
, and
\tilde{\birth}(G)\leq\tilde{\birth}(H)
. Hence
also
\birth(G)\leq\birth(H)
.

Proof.

Since
\terminal(G)\subseteq\terminal(H)
by

Theorem

3.17
, it is clear from
Definition

3.14
that

\displaystyle\race(G)

\displaystyle=\min(\terminal(G))

\displaystyle\leq\min(\terminal(H))

\displaystyle=\race(H).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\tilde{\birth}(G)

\displaystyle=\max(\terminal(G))

\displaystyle\leq\max(\terminal(H))

\displaystyle=\tilde{\birth}(H),

from which it follows that all Left dead ends in a given equivalence class
must have equal formal birthday, and hence so does the canonical form
(which must also be a Left dead end). Thus,
\birth(G)\leq\birth(H)
.
∎

Of course, if we have two Left dead ends
G
and
H
with
G=H
, then

Theorem

3.17
tells us that
\terminal(G)=\terminal(H)
,
and
Corollary

3.18
says in particular that

\race(G)=\race(H)
and
\tilde{\birth}(G)=\tilde{\birth}(H)
. It is from this
point onwards that we will no longer refer to the formal birthday of a game,
and will instead use only its birthday, since
Corollary

3.18

shows that these two functions agree on Left dead ends. The need for a
distinction between birthday and formal birthday in other contexts in CGT is
precisely because of the lack of this well-definedness up to equality there.

Siegel also proved the special case of
Corollary

3.18
as it
pertains to the birthday specifically; see
[
30
, Propositions 3.3 and 3.4 on
p. 7]
.

A weakened converse of
Theorem

3.17
is explored in

Proposition

5.2
. Unsurprisingly, two Left dead ends having equal
terminal lengths does not imply the two games are equal. Take, for example,

G=\overline{1}+W_{2}
and
H=\{\cdot\mid\overline{1},\overline{2}\}
, where both
games have terminal lengths
\{2,3\}
, but
H>G
.
Proposition

5.2
,
however, will allow us to conclude equality for exactly those Left dead ends
that are weakly 1-flexible (see
Definition

5.1
).

We now prove another simple consequence of
Theorem

3.17

that combines itself with
Corollary

3.2
to provide a simple tool that
can often be useful when trying to show that
G\not\geq H
for some Left dead
ends
G
and
H
.

Corollary 3.19
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, and
k
is an integer
satisfying
0\leq k\leq\tilde{\birth}(G)
, then, for any run

(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
of
G
of length
k
, there exists a run

(H_{0},H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
of
H
of length
k
such that

\terminal(G_{i})\subseteq\terminal(H_{i})
for every
0\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

3.2
, we know that there exists a run

(H_{0},H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
of
H
such that
G_{i}\geq H_{i}
for every
0\leq i\leq k
.
Theorem

3.17
then yields the result immediately.
∎

It is tempting to try and prove the converse of this statement. But, alas, it
is not true in general. For example, take

\displaystyle G=\{\cdot\mid\{\cdot\mid W_{2},\{\cdot\mid\overline{1},\overline{2}\}\}\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle H=\{\cdot\mid\{\cdot\mid W_{2},\overline{3}\},\{\cdot\mid\overline{1},\{\cdot\mid\overline{1},\overline{2}\}\}\},

where the reader may verify that
G\not\geq H
, but, for every run

(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{k})
of
G
, there exists a run
(H_{0},H_{1},\dots,H_{k})
such
that
\terminal(G_{i})\subseteq\terminal(H_{i})
for every
0\leq i\leq k
.

It will be prudent of us here to remark upon the terminal lengths of sums of
games.

Proposition 3.20
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends, then

\terminal(G+H)=\terminal(G)+\terminal(H).

In particular,
\race(G+H)=\race(G)+\race(H)
and

\birth(G+H)=\birth(G)+\birth(H)
.

Proof.

When playing the sum
G+H
, observe that
G+H
is terminated if and only if

G
and
H
are both terminated. Thus, the only way to terminate
G+H
is
by terminating
G
in any
a\in\terminal(G)
number of moves and
terminating
H
in any
b\in\terminal(H)
number of moves.

Finally, observe that

\displaystyle\race(G+H)

\displaystyle=\min(\terminal(G+H))

\displaystyle=\min(\terminal(G))+\min(\terminal(H))

\displaystyle=\race(G)+\race(H),

and similarly for the birthday.
∎

Siegel proved the special case of
\birth(G+H)=\birth(G)+\birth(H)
in

[
30
, Proposition 3.8 on p. 8]
.

Corollary 3.21
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends, then

|\terminal(G+H)|\geq\max(|\terminal(G)|,|\terminal(H)|),

with equality if and only if at least one of
G
and
H
is an integer.

Proof.

Suppose, without loss of generality, that
G
is an integer. It follows
from
Lemma

3.15
that
\terminal(G)=\{n\}
for some
n
.

Proposition

3.20
then yields

\displaystyle|\terminal(G+H)|

\displaystyle=|\{n+b:b\in\terminal(H)\}|

\displaystyle=|\terminal(H)|

\displaystyle=\max(|\terminal(G)|,|\terminal(H)|).

Suppose now that neither
G
nor
H
is an integer. So, without loss of
generality,
1<|\terminal(G)|\leq|\terminal(H)|
by
Lemma

3.15
.
Then, the union

(\race(G)+\terminal(H))\cup\{\birth(G+H)\}\subseteq\terminal(G+H)

has cardinality
|\terminal(H)|+1
. Recalling that

|\terminal(H)|=\max(|\terminal(G)|,|\terminal(H)|)
, we obtain the result.
∎

Corollary

3.21
can actually also be a viewed as a result
of additive number theory; in particular, see
[
23
, Theorem 1.4 on
p. 8]
. It might be worthwhile to investigate these
relationships further.

3.4
Finite factorisation monoid

We now return more resolutely to proving that the monoid of Left dead ends is
an FF-monoid, having amassed more than enough firepower.

Lemma 3.22
.

If
G,H
, and
K
are non-zero Left dead ends with
G=H+K
, then

\birth(H)<\birth(G)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
\birth(H)\geq\birth(G)
. It then
follows from
Proposition

3.20
that

\birth(H+K)=\birth(H)+\birth(K)>\birth(G)
, contradicting

Corollary

3.18
.
∎

Lemma 3.23
.

The monoid of Left dead ends
\mathcal{L}
is atomic.

Proof.

We will proceed by induction on the birthday of a Left dead end. The empty
game trivially has a factorisation: the empty sum. Suppose all Left dead
ends of birthday strictly less than
n>0
admit factorisations. Now choose
an arbitrary Left dead end
G
with
\birth(G)=n
. If
G
cannot be written
in the form
H+K
, for some non-zero Left dead ends
H
and
K
, then
G

is itself an atom by definition. Otherwise, it follows from

Lemma

3.22
that
\birth(H),\birth(K)<n
. By induction,
H

and
K
admit factorisations, and, thus, so does
G
.
∎

Lemma 3.24
.

The monoid of Left dead ends
\mathcal{L}
is a BF-monoid. In particular,
if
G
is a Left dead end, then the length of each factorisation of
G
is
at most
\race(G)
; that is,
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\race(G)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}
is atomic (
Lemma

3.23
), and let

G_{1}+\dots+G_{n}
be an arbitrary factorisation of
G
. By

Proposition

3.20
, it follows that

\race(G)=\race(G_{1})+\dots+\race(G_{n})\geq n
. Thus, there can be at most

\race(G)
atoms in a factorisation of
G
, or else

Corollary

3.18
would be violated.
∎

While the bound in
Lemma

3.24
is sharp, we will have more to say in

Section

6
on improving this for many families; see

Theorems

6.2
and
6.3
.

Corollary 3.25
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with
\race(G)=1
, then
G
is an atom.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.24
, the length of each factorisation of
G
is at most 1,
yielding the result immediately.
∎

Another way of phrasing this is that, if
G
has an option to the empty game,
then
G
is an atom. Siegel calls these games
terminable
, and
essentially also proves this proposition, albeit in a different language and
context, in
[
30
, Proposition 3.9 on p. 8]
. For example,
W_{n}
is a
(terminable) atom for every positive
n
. This result (
Corollary

3.25
)
will be strengthened further in
Proposition

7.11
.

Finally, we prove the main result of the section.

Theorem 3.26
.

The monoid of Left dead ends
\mathcal{L}
is an FF-monoid.

Proof.

Let
G
be a Left dead end. Recall that
\mathcal{L}
is a BF-monoid
(
Lemma

3.24
). By
Lemma

3.22
, it follows that each atom in
each factorisation of
G
must have birthday at most
\birth(G)
(there is
equality precisely when
G
is itself an atom). There are finitely many
short game forms of any given birthday, and hence only finitely many atoms
of birthday at most
\birth(G)
.
Lemma

3.24
tells us that the set of
lengths of all factorisations of
G
is finite. Each factorisation of
G

is thus obtained by choosing a finite number of atoms from a finite set.
There must, therefore, exist only finitely many factorisations of
G
.
∎

So, every Left dead end can be written as a (necessarily finite) sum of atoms
in only finitely many ways. But what do these atoms look like? How many of them
are there? We will soon investigate both of these questions, but we first we
make a swift detour in proving a powerful property of
\overline{1}
that will
yield the only known prime element in our monoid.

4
In search of a prime

We first describe the property of
\overline{1}
that we are about to prove,
since it is perhaps surprising: given any sum of Left dead ends

\overline{1}+G_{1}+\dots+G_{n}
, it is
always
the best move to play on

\overline{1}
. That is in every sum of Left dead ends containing

\overline{1}
, there is exactly one good move.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then
\overline{1}+G^{\prime}\geq G
for every option

G^{\prime}
of
G
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the birthday of a Left dead end. If
G^{\prime}=0
, then

\overline{1}+G^{\prime}=\overline{1}\geq G
since the options of
\overline{1}

form a (non-empty) subset of the options of
G
. Assume now that
G^{\prime}
is
non-zero.

The options of
\overline{1}+G^{\prime}
are
G^{\prime}
and
\overline{1}+G^{\prime\prime}
, where

G^{\prime\prime}
ranges over all options of
G^{\prime}
. Observe that
G^{\prime}\geq G^{\prime}
, and also
that
\overline{1}+G^{\prime\prime}\geq G^{\prime}
by induction. The result is thus yielded by

Theorem

2.4
.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then
\overline{1}+G=\{\cdot\mid G\}
.

Proof.

Since the options of
\{\cdot\mid G\}
form a (non-empty) subset of the
options of
\overline{1}+G
, it is immediate that
\{\cdot\mid G\}\geq\overline{1}+G
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we have

\overline{1}+G\geq\{\cdot\mid G\}
, yielding the result.
∎

It will be useful for us to reframe
Proposition

4.2
to talk about the
number of good options of a Left dead end
G
, which we do so now.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
G
and
G^{\prime}
are Left dead ends, then
G
has exactly one good option
(equal to)
G^{\prime}
if and only if
G=\overline{1}+G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First suppose that
G
has exactly one good option
G^{\prime}
, then clearly

G=\{\cdot\mid G^{\prime}\}
. By
Proposition

4.2
, it then follows that

G=\overline{1}+G^{\prime}
.

Now suppose instead that
G=\overline{1}+G^{\prime}
.
Proposition

4.2
then
tells us that
G=\{\cdot\mid G^{\prime}\}
, which has exactly one good option, and
hence it follows from
Corollary

3.4
that
G
has exactly
one good option, which must be equal to
G^{\prime}
, completing the proof.
∎

What is surprising is that
Proposition

4.2
is powerful enough to prove
that
\overline{1}
is prime—we remind the reader that a game
G
is prime
if, whenever
G\mid H+K
, it follows that
G\mid H
or
G\mid K
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The game
\overline{1}
is prime in the monoid of Left dead ends

\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Let
H
and
K
be Left dead ends. If
\overline{1}\mid H+K
, then there
exists a Left dead end
G
such that
\overline{1}+G=H+K
. Now,

\overline{1}+G=\{\cdot\mid G\}
by
Proposition

4.2
, so it has
exactly one good option by
Proposition

4.3
. It follows from

Corollary

3.4
that
H+K
must then also have only one good
option; say, without loss of generality, some
H^{\prime}+K
. Thus,

\displaystyle H+K

\displaystyle=\{\cdot\mid H^{\prime}+K\}

\displaystyle=\overline{1}+H^{\prime}+K

by
Proposition

4.2
. And so, by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
),
H=\overline{1}+H^{\prime}
, which means precisely
that
\overline{1}\mid H
.
∎

It follows from
Theorem

4.4
that every integer is uniquely
factorisable; we know now that
\overline{n}
admits a prime factorisation

n\cdot\overline{1}
for every
n\geq 0
, and such factorisations must be
unique!

It is unclear how we might prove that there exists an atom other than

\overline{1}
that is prime. Similarly, all efforts at proving that there
exists an atom that is
not
prime have also failed. We leave this as an
open problem.

Open problem 4.5
.

Does there exist a prime element other than
\overline{1}
? From another
angle, can we show that there exists an atom that is not prime?

We now turn back towards our study of atoms, first introducing a new concept
called
flexibility
that will aid us in our quest.

5
Flexibility

The formal birthday of a short game—the height of its game tree—is a
measure of how far away a game is from 0. When playing a Left dead end, once
Right has moved to an integer, he no longer has any
flexibility
in how
to play; he is stuck with a rigid structure of having to play a fixed number of
moves until the component terminates. We have seen in
Lemma

3.15
that a
Left dead end is an integer if and only if every terminal run has the same
length. It seems natural then to consider not the distance of a Left dead end
to 0, but instead its distance to an integer. We define this precisely now.

Definition 5.1
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then we define the
flexibility
of
G
,
written
\flex(G)
, recursively as

\flex(G)=1+\max(\flex(G^{\prime})),

where
G^{\prime}
ranges over all options of
G
, when
G
is not an integer, and
as
\flex(G)\coloneq 0
otherwise.

If
\flex(G)=k
, then we say
G
is
k
-flexible
. If
\flex(G)\leq k
, then we say
G
is
weakly
k
-flexible
.

As a simple example, the reader may verify that
\flex(W_{n})=n-1
for every

n\geq 1
, and also that
\flex(\{\cdot\mid W_{3},\overline{7}\})=3
. We can also
immediately remark that the only 0-flexible atom is
\overline{1}
: by

Corollary

3.25
, it is clear that
\overline{1}
is an atom; and, if

n>0
, then
\overline{n}=\overline{n-1}+\overline{1}
.

We could have used a construction analogous to how the set of short games is
typically constructed in the following way: define

\mathcal{F}_{0}\coloneq\{\overline{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, and then,
recursively,

\mathcal{F}_{n}\coloneq\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\cup\{\{\cdot\mid\mathscr{G}\}:\mathscr{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\text{ and
}\mathscr{G}\text{ is finite}\}

for
n>0
; the flexibility of
G
is then the smallest integer
n
such that

G\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. This alternative definition may be good to keep in mind,
but it is not how we choose to frame things here.

Those games that are weakly 1-flexible have a useful property: of all the Left
dead ends whose terminal lengths are a superset of some fixed set, there is a
unique, maximal such Left dead end (ordered by ‘
\geq
’), and it is weakly
1-flexible.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
\flex(G)\leq 1
and

\terminal(G)\subseteq\terminal(H)
, then
G\geq H
.

Proof.

Firstly, if
G=0
, then the hypothesis implies
H=0
by
Lemma

3.16
,
and so we are done.

Otherwise, let
G^{\prime}
be an arbitrary option of
G
. Now,

\terminal(G^{\prime})\subseteq\terminal(G)-1\subseteq\terminal(H)-1

by
Lemma

3.13
and our hypothesis. Since
\flex(G)\leq 1
, it
follows that
\flex(G^{\prime})=0
; hence
G^{\prime}
must be an integer, and so

|\terminal(G^{\prime})|=1
by
Lemma

3.15
. Thus, it follows again by

Lemma

3.13
that there must exist some option
H^{\prime}
of
H

such that
\terminal(G^{\prime})\subseteq\terminal(H^{\prime})
. By induction,
G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime}
,
and we have the result by
Theorem

2.4
.
∎

So, to be explicit, if we consider the set of all Left dead ends whose terminal
lengths are a superset of the set
T=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
, then the unique,
maximal such Left dead end is given by

\{\cdot\mid\overline{t_{1}},\dots,\overline{t_{n}}\}
. This is weakly 1-flexible;
it is 1-flexible when
n>1
, and 0-flexible otherwise.

Just as we may sometimes like to consider those options of a game of greatest
birthday, we may also like to consider those options of greatest flexibility.
We say such an option is
versatile
.

Definition 5.3
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then we say an option
G^{\prime}
of
G
is

versatile
if
\flex(G^{\prime})\geq\flex(G)-1
.

That is, a versatile option of a game
G
is simply an option of maximum
flexibility amongst all of its options. It is immediate from
Definition

5.1

that every non-zero Left dead end has a versatile option. Note also that, if

G
is not an integer, then a versatile option necessarily has flexibility

\flex(G)-1
. But, if
G
is an integer, then all of its subpositions have
flexibility 0.

It is a simple observation that
\flex(G)<\birth(G)
for any non-zero Left dead
end
G
(there is equality in the case of the empty game), which we now prove.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then
\flex(G)\leq\birth(G)
, with equality if
and only if
G=0
.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\flex(0)=0=\birth(0)
, and also that

\flex(\overline{n})=0<n=\birth(\overline{n})
for every
n>0
. Proceeding
via induction on the birthday, suppose
G
is a non-integer Left dead end
of birthday
n>1
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a non-zero versatile option of
G
; so

\birth(G^{\prime})<\birth(G)
(recall
Corollary

3.18
). By
induction,
\flex(G^{\prime})<\birth(G^{\prime})
. Since
G^{\prime}
is versatile and
G
is not
an integer, it is clear that
\flex(G)=\flex(G^{\prime})+1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\flex(G)

\displaystyle=\flex(G^{\prime})+1

\displaystyle\leq\birth(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle<\birth(G),

which completes the proof.
∎

Just as the terminal lengths of two comparable Left dead ends are comparable,
we obtain a similar result for flexiblity.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, then

\flex(G)\leq\flex(H)
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\flex(H)=0
; so
H
is an integer
\overline{n}
, with
terminal lengths
\terminal(H)=\{n\}
. Since
G\geq H
, it follows from

Theorem

3.17
that
\terminal(G)=\{n\}
, and so

G=\overline{n}
by
Lemma

3.15
, yielding the result. Assume now
that
\flex(H)>0
.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
\flex(G)>\flex(H)
. We may assume

(G,H)
is a counter-example minimising the birthday of
G+H
. Let
G^{\prime}
be
some versatile option of
G
. By
Theorem

2.4
, there must exist some
option
H^{\prime}
of
H
with
G^{\prime}\geq H^{\prime}
. Since
\birth(G^{\prime}+H^{\prime})<\birth(G+H)
, it
follows that
\flex(G^{\prime})\leq\flex(H^{\prime})
. But
G^{\prime}
is versatile, so, by
supposition,

\displaystyle\flex(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\flex(G)-1

\displaystyle\geq\flex(H)

\displaystyle>\flex(H^{\prime}),

which is the contradiction we were after.
∎

Speaking practically, this result tells us that when we are looking to remove
dominated options from a Left dead end to arrive at a canonical form, an option
can never be dominated by a less flexible one. It is also immediate that
flexibility is an invariant up to equality, as opposed to just
isomorphism—much like the race and birthday (and terminal lengths) of Left
dead ends.

It can sometimes be useful to view flexibility as the length of a longest run
that doesn’t visit an integer except at the end, which we make precise here.

Definition 5.6
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then we say a run
(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
of
G

of length
n>0
is
flexible
if
G_{i}
is not an integer for
i<n
. We
define all runs of length 0 to be flexible.

Furthermore, we say that a flexible run is
maximal
if it ends at an
integer; that is, if it cannot be extended to a longer flexible run. If a
flexible run is not maximal, then we say it is
extendable
.

Lemma 5.7
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then
\flex(G)
is equal to the length of a
longest flexible run of
G
.

Proof.

If
G
is an integer, then the result is immediate from comparing

Definitions

5.1
and
5.6
.

Suppose now that
G
is not an integer. In any flexible run

(G_{0},G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
of
G
, it is clear that
\flex(G_{i})>\flex(G_{i+1})

for every
0\leq i<n
. Thus, the length of a flexible run will be maximised
precisely by picking successive versatile options; i.e. pick
G_{i}
to be a
versatile option of
G_{i-1}
for every
0<i\leq n
. This will yield a
flexible run of length
\flex(G)
, and hence also the result.
∎

It will be convenient to also give names to those options of minimum race and
maximum birthday. We have need only for the latter at this moment, but we
introduce both now given their similarity; and we shall need the former later
on.

Definition 5.8
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then we define a
racing option
of
G
to
be an option
G^{\prime}
such that
\race(G^{\prime})=\race(G)-1
.

Definition 5.9
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then we define a
stalling option
of
G

to be an option
G^{\prime}
such that
\birth(G^{\prime})=\birth(G)-1
.

So, a racing option is an option that moves towards the empty game as quickly
as possible, whereas a stalling option is an option that keeps as far away as
possible from the empty game. It is immediate from the definition of race
(
Definition

3.14
) and the definition of birthday (and its equivalence with
formal birthday on the set of Left dead ends via

Corollary

3.18
) that every non-zero Left dead end has a
racing option and a stalling option (which are not necessarily distinct).

Proposition 5.10
.

If
G
and
H
are non-integer Left dead ends, then

1.

\flex(G+\overline{n})=\flex(G)+n
; and

2.

\flex(G+H)=\max({\birth(G)+\flex(H),\flex(G)+\birth(H)})
,

for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

First we prove (1) by induction on
n
. If
n=0
, then the result is
trivial. Assume now that
n>0
.

Recall by
Proposition

4.2
that
G+\overline{n}=\{\cdot\mid G+\overline{n-1}\}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\flex(G+\overline{n})

\displaystyle=\flex(\{\cdot\mid G+\overline{n-1})\quad\text{[\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{prop:flex-relation}]}

\displaystyle=1+\flex(G+\overline{n-1})\quad\text{[\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{def:flex}]}

\displaystyle=\flex(G)+n\quad\text{[by induction]}.

We now prove (2). Suppose, without loss of generality, that

\birth(G)+\flex(H)\geq\flex(G)+\birth(H),

where we now write
M
for
\birth(G)+\flex(H)
. First we will show that

\flex(G+H)\geq M
. Consider a run on
G+H
given by playing
\birth(G)

stalling moves on
G
, followed by
\flex(H)
versatile moves on
H
. Since
neither
G
nor
H
is an integer, it follows clearly that this is a
(maximal) flexible run of
G+H
. So,
\flex(G+H)\geq M
by

Lemma

5.7
.

Suppose now, for a contradiction, that
\flex(G+H)>M
. Then, again by

Lemma

5.7
, there must exist an extendable
flexible run of
G+H
of length
M
. In such a run, at most
\birth(G)

moves may ever be played on
G
. If exactly
\birth(G)
moves were to be
played on
G
, then exactly
\flex(H)
moves would be played on
H
, and so
it is clear that the run would not be extendable. Thus, for some
k>0
,
there are
\birth(G)-k
moves played on
G
, and
\flex(H)+k
moves played
on
H
; necessarily,

\flex(H)+k\leq\birth(H).

(i)

If
\birth(G)-k\geq\flex(G)
, then it is clear that, again, the run would
not be extendable. Thus,

\birth(G)-k<\flex(G).

(ii)

But adding
Eqs.

i
and
ii
yields

\birth(G)+\flex(H)<\flex(G)+\birth(H),

contradicting our earlier supposition, and completing the proof.
∎

In the proof above, we could have proved the second item by induction as well,
but it doesn’t provide much more efficiency, and it is worthwhile to see both
techniques here.

Note that the first item of
Proposition

5.10
could be written as

\flex(G+\overline{n})=\flex(G)+\birth(\overline{n})
to look more similar to
the second item. But it is important to realise that they are not the same:
considering the example
\{\cdot\mid 0,\overline{2}\}+\overline{1}
, we see that

\displaystyle\flex(\{\cdot\mid 0,\overline{2}\}+\overline{1})

\displaystyle=\flex(\{\cdot\mid 0,\overline{2}\})+1

\displaystyle=2,

but
\birth(\{\cdot\mid 0,\overline{2}\})+\flex(\overline{1})=3
. (And hence we
do need the distinction between the two items of
Proposition

5.10
.)

5.1
Using flexibility to find atoms

We now seek to investigate the behaviour of Left dead ends of different
flexibilities, and, importantly, find some atoms. Perhaps surprisingly, every
1-flexible Left dead end is an atom, which will be yielded swiftly by the
following proposition.

Proposition 5.11
.

If
G,H,
and
K
are Left dead ends such that

1.

H
is non-zero;

2.

K
is not an integer; and

3.

H+K\geq G
;

then
\birth(H)<\flex(G)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
\birth(H)\geq\flex(G)
. Then, we may
take a run of
H+K
of length
\flex(G)
played entirely on the
H

component; say to
H^{\prime}+K
. By
Corollary

3.19
, since
H+K\geq G
, there exists a run of
G
of length
\flex(G)
such that, in
particular, the resulting position
G^{\prime}
satisfies

\terminal(H^{\prime}+K)\subseteq\terminal(G^{\prime})
. By definition of
\flex(G)
, the
position
G^{\prime}
must be an integer; so
|\terminal(G^{\prime})|=1
by

Lemma

3.15
. But
K
is not an integer by hypothesis, and so

|\terminal(H^{\prime}+K)|\geq|\terminal(K)|>1
by

Corollary

3.21
and, again,
Lemma

3.15
. It is
thus clear that
\terminal(H^{\prime}+K)\not\subseteq\terminal(G^{\prime})
, which is a
contradiction, yielding the result.
∎

Proposition

5.11
is saying something peculiar: if
G
,
H
, and
K

are Left dead ends (forgetting the condition on
K
in

Proposition

5.11
), where
H+K
is a molecule and
H+K\geq G
, then,
letting
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of all atoms that appear in factorisations
of
H+K
, it follows that

\birth(H+K)<\flex(G)+\min\{\birth(J):\overline{1}\neq J\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Of course,
\min\{\birth(J):\overline{1}\neq J\in\mathcal{A}\}
could be
arbitrarily large, but it is curious that the least birthday of a non-integer
part of
H+K
, along with the flexibility of
G
, could work together to bound
the birthday of
H+K
in this way.

We now prove some simple corollaries than can oft be useful in determining
whether games are atoms or not.

Corollary 5.12
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with more than one good option, then, for any
non-zero Left dead ends
H
and
K
with
H+K\geq G
, it holds that

\birth(H),\birth(K)<\flex(G)
. Additionally, if
H+K=G
, then

1<\birth(H),\birth(K)
.

Proof.

Since
G
has more than one good option, it follows from

Proposition

4.2
that it cannot be split by
\overline{1}
; hence
neither
H
nor
K
is an integer. We then obtain

\birth(H),\birth(K)<\flex(G)
from
Proposition

5.11
.

Suppose now that
H+K=G
. If either
H
or
K
had birthday 1, then they
would be equal to
\overline{1}
; hence
G
would have exactly one good
option by
Proposition

4.3
, contradicting the hypothesis.
∎

Corollary 5.13
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with more than one good option, and

\birth(G)>2\cdot\flex(G)-2
, then
G
is an atom.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
G=H+K
for some non-zero Left dead ends

H
and
K
. By
Corollary

5.12
, since
G
has more than one
good option, it follows that
\birth(H),\birth(K)<\flex(G)
. But

2\cdot\flex(G)-2<\birth(G)=\birth(H+K)=\birth(H)+\birth(K)<2\cdot\flex(G)-1

by
Proposition

3.20
, which is the contradiction we were
seeking.
∎

These results let us begin to classify those
k
-flexible games that are atoms.
We could, of course, extend the following indefinitely, but we just give a few
examples for some low flexibilities that can be helpful in practice when
analysing games.

Proposition 5.14
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then the following statements are true:

•

if
\flex(G)=1
, then
G
is an atom;

•

if
\flex(G)=2
, then either
G
has exactly one good option (i.e.
\overline{1}\mid G
), or
G
is an atom;

•

if
\flex(G)=3
, then either
G
has exactly one good option,

G=2\cdot W_{2}
, or
G
is an atom.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\flex(G)=1
. Since each option of
G
is an integer, it
must follow that
G
has more than one good move, or else
G
would be an
integer, and hence would be 0-flexible. It is then immediate from

Corollary

5.13
that
G
must be an atom.

Suppose now that
\flex(G)=2
, and that
G
has more than one good option.
By
Lemma

, we know that
2<\birth(G)
, and hence

is an

Corollary

.

Finally, suppose that
\flex(G)=3

The fact that all 1-flexible games are atoms will actually also follow

).

At the end of

, one of the questions we asked was how many
atoms there are in our monoid. Indeed, given our introduction of flexibility,

-flexible atoms?

Yes: if we take any non-zero,

We are not yet finished with our search for atoms, but we take a brief
interlude in the next section to discuss the lengths of factorisation.

6
Lengths of factorisations

In this short section we provide tools to bound the lengths of factorisations
of Left dead ends. Recall that we write
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}
for the length of a
longest factorisation of
G
. We have already seen in
Lemma

3.24
that

\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\race(G)
.

Of course, a Left dead end
G
is an atom if and only if
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=1
; it is
a molecule if and only if (it is non-zero and)
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\neq 1
. We write

\overline{\mathsf{L}(0)}=0
, since its unique factorisation is the empty sum.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+1
for every
good option
G^{\prime}
of
G
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists a good option
G^{\prime}
of
G

and a factorisation
G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}
of
G
with
k\geq\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+2
.
Since
G^{\prime}
is a good option of
G
, there must exist, without loss of
generality, some option
G_{1}^{\prime}
of
G_{1}
such that
H\coloneq G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}=G^{\prime}
by
Corollary

3.4
. Clearly

\overline{\mathsf{L}(H)}\geq k-1>\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}
, and hence
H\neq G^{\prime}
, which is the
contradiction we were seeking.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with exactly one good option
G^{\prime}
, then

\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+1
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.2
, it follows that
G=\overline{1}+G^{\prime}
. Thus,
writing a longest factorisation of
G^{\prime}
will yield the result immediately
by
Lemma

6.1
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with more than one good option, then

\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\min\left\{\race(G),\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+1,|\terminal(G)|-1,\frac{\flex(G)+1}{2}\right\},

where
G^{\prime}
ranges over all good options of
G
.

Proof.

We know that
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\min\{\race(G),\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+1\}
from

Lemmas

3.24
and
6.1
.

We will write
G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}
for a longest factorisation of
G
. None of
the
G_{i}
is equal to
\overline{1}
, otherwise
G
would have exactly one
good option by
Proposition

4.3
, contradicting the hypothesis.

We show first that
k\leq|\terminal(G)|-1
. Suppose, for a contradiction,
that
k\geq|\terminal(G)|
. It follows from
Lemma

3.15
that

|\terminal(G_{i})|\geq 2
for every
i
. By

Corollary

3.21
, it then follows that

\displaystyle|\terminal(G_{1}+\dots+G_{k})|

\displaystyle\geq k+1

\displaystyle\geq|\terminal(G)|+1,

which is the contradiction we were seeking (it contradicts

Theorem

3.17
).

We now show that
2\cdot k\leq\flex(G)+1
. If
G
is an atom, then

,
and so the inequality holds unless perhaps
\flex(G)=0
. But
G
has more
than one good option by hypothesis, and hence
G
is not an integer by

4.3
; so,
\flex(G)\geq 1
and the inequality holds.

Assume now that
G
is a molecule. It follows from

5.12
that
\birth(G_{i})\geq 2
for every

5.11
and

, we obtain

\displaystyle\flex(G)

\displaystyle>\birth(G_{1}+\dots+G_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\birth(G_{1})+\dots+\birth(G_{k-1})

\displaystyle\geq 2k-2,

which yields the result. (Since we can reorder the elements in the
factorisation however we like, this yields precisely that

\flex(G)+1=2\cdot k
if and only if

for every

equality implies
\birth(G_{i})=2
for every

is the only atom
of birthday 2; conversely, if

.)
∎

At this point, we’ve seen some atoms, we’ve seen a prime element, and we’ve

7
Uniqueness of factorisations

In this section we prove a number of results related to the uniqueness of
factorisations. We jump in at the deep end with what may appear to be a
complicated proposition, but we will soon see that it is quite simple. We saw
earlier in
Lemma

6.1
that
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq\overline{\mathsf{L}(G^{\prime})}+1
for every good
option
G^{\prime}
of
G
; the following result tells us what we can infer when we
have equality.

Proposition 7.1
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with a good option
H
such that

\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}+1
, then

1.

H
is a racing option of
G
;

2.

there exists a unique atom
K
such that
G=H+K
, and
K
is
terminable;

3.

every longest factorisation of
G
is of the form

H_{1}+\dots+H_{k}+K
, where
H_{1}+\dots+H_{k}
is a longest factorisation
of
H
; and

4.

if
H
admits a unique longest factorisation, then so does
G
.

Proof.

Let
G_{1}+\dots+G_{n}
be an arbitrary longest factorisation of
G
; i.e. such that
n=\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=\overline{\mathsf{L}(H)}+1
. We prove each of the claims in
order, using the previous ones implicitly in each proof of the next.

We first prove (1). Since
H
is a good option of
G
, there must exist,
without loss of generality, an option
G_{1}^{\prime}
of
G_{1}
such that

G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{n}=H

by
Corollary

3.4
. But
n=\overline{\mathsf{L}(H)}+1
, and so it must
follow that
G_{1}^{\prime}=0
, and then
H=G_{2}+\dots+G_{n}
. Thus, we obtain

G=H+G_{1}
, where
G_{1}
is a terminable atom; so
\race(G)=\race(H)+1
,
proving (1).

We now prove (2) and (3). Suppose we write
G=H+K
, where
K
is an atom.
Then, by cancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
), we obtain
G_{1}=K
,
which simultaneously proves (2) and (3).

Finally, to prove (4), suppose that
H
admits a unique longest
factorisation. This must be
G_{2}+\dots+G_{n}
. The result is thus immediate.
∎

In
Proposition

7.1
, if
G
has exactly one good option
H
,
then
\overline{1}
is the unique terminable atom
K
where
G=H+K
; this is
simply
Proposition

4.3
.

The following corollaries can be useful in practice to determine whether games
are atoms, or indeed to construct atoms.

Corollary 7.2
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with a good option to an atom
H
, then either

G
is an atom, or
H
is a racing option of
G
and there exists a unique
(terminable) atom
K
such that
G=H+K
; in particular,
G
is uniquely
factorisable.

Proof.

By
Lemma

6.1
, we know that
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}\leq 2
. If
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=1
,
then
G
is an atom. Otherwise
\overline{\mathsf{L}(G)}=2
, and the result follows
immediately from
Proposition

7.1
.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with good options to distinct atoms
H
and
K
,
then either
G
is an atom, or
H+K
is the unique factorisation of
G
. In
particular, if either of
H
or
K
is not terminable (i.e. if

\race(G)\neq 2
), then
G
is an atom.

Proof.

If
G
is an atom, then we are done. Otherwise, by applying

Corollary

7.2
twice, it follows that
G=H+J=K+R
, where
J
and

R
are terminable atoms. But
Corollary

7.2
also tells us that
these factorisations are unique. Since
H
and
K
are distinct by
hypothesis, it must then follow that
G=H+K
(i.e. that
H=R
and
J=K
).
In particular,
H
and
K
must both be terminable, otherwise we would have
reached a contradiction, and would conclude that
G
is an atom.
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with at least three good options to pairwise
distinct atoms, then
G
is an atom.

Proof.

If
G
is an atom, then we are done. Otherwise, write
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and

G_{3}
for three of the atoms that are pairwise distinct good options of

G
. By
Corollary

7.3
, it follows that
G=G_{1}+G_{2}=G_{2}+G_{3}
.
By cancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
), we obtain
G_{1}=G_{3}
,
contradicting the hypothesis that
G_{1}
and
G_{3}
are distinct.
∎

Corollaries

7.2
,
7.3
and
7.4
yield an
extraordinary number of atoms (at least at low birthdays), which is discussed
briefly in
Appendix

D
.

Fascinatingly, there is also a great deal of similarity between

Corollaries

7.2
,
7.3
and
7.4
and some of
Siegel’s results pertaining to the monoid of impartial games
[

, Proposition
35 and Corollary 36 on pp. 17–18]
. See

Appendix

A
for a further discussion.

7.1
Good prime-factorisable options

We are now going to investigate some of the consequences of having good options
that are prime-factorisable. We remind the reader that, since
\overline{1}
is
the only Left dead end currently known to be prime, there is no currently known
difference in meaning here between the words ‘prime-factorisable’ and
‘integer’. Where possible, we will write our results using
‘prime-factorisable’, which serves as prophylaxis against more primes possibly
being discovered in the future.

Lemma 7.5
.

If
G\coloneq G_{1}+\dots+G_{n}=H_{1}+\dots+H_{m}\eqcolon H
are factorisations of
a Left dead end
G
, and there is a good option of
G
playing on the
component
G_{1}
to some prime-factorisable
G_{1}^{\prime}
, then there exists an
index
j
such that
G_{1}=H_{j}
.

Proof.

If
G_{1}
is prime, then the result is immediate. Otherwise, by
cancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
), we may assume that no
G_{i}
nor

H_{j}
is prime. Without loss of generality, there must exist, by

Corollary

3.4
, a good option of
H
playing on the
component
H_{1}
, say to
H_{1}^{\prime}
, such that

G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{n}=H_{1}^{\prime}+H_{2}+\dots+H_{m}.

(1)

Since
G_{1}^{\prime}
is prime-factorisable, each atom appearing in its prime
factorisation divides one of
H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2},\dots,H_{m}
. By assumption,

H_{2},\dots,H_{m}
are atoms but not prime, and so it must follow that

H_{1}^{\prime}=G_{1}^{\prime}+R
, where
R
is some Left dead end. Thus, by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
),
Eq.

1
becomes

G_{2}+\dots+G_{n}=R+H_{2}+\dots+H_{m}.

We then add
G_{1}
to both sides and use the fact that
G=H
in order to
obtain

H_{1}+\dots+H_{m}=G_{1}+R+H_{2}+\dots+H_{m},

from which it is clear that
H_{1}=G_{1}+R
by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
). We know that
H_{1}
is an atom, and so
necessarily
R=0
(since
G_{1}\neq 0
). Thus,
H_{1}=G_{1}
, and we have the
result.
∎

We now give one of our main results, which gifts us infinitely many uniquely
factorisable games that we haven’t yet seen, and is also practical when
analysing games by hand. We will explore two special cases of the result very
soon afterwards (
Theorems

7.8
and
7.9
).

Theorem 7.6
.

If
G\coloneq G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}
is a factorisation of a Left dead end such
that, for every
1\leq i\leq k
, there exists a good option of

G_{i}+\dots+G_{k}
on the component
G_{i}
to some prime-factorisable
G_{i}^{\prime}
,
then
G
is uniquely factorisable.

Proof.

We will proceed by induction on the length of such a factorisation

G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}
. If
k=1
, then the factorisation is necessarily unique.
Now suppose
k>1
. Write
H_{1}+\dots+H_{n}
for an arbitrary factorisation of

G
. We will show that
k=n
and also that
H_{i}=G_{i}
for every
i
.

By hypothesis, there exists a good option of
G
moving on
G_{1}
to some
prime-factorisable
G_{1}^{\prime}
.
Lemma

7.5
then yields, without loss
of generality, that
G_{1}=H_{1}
. By cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
), we obtain

G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}=H_{2}+\dots+H_{n},

where
G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}
is a factorisation of length
k-1
that necessarily
satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem, and hence it is unique by
induction. Therefore, without loss of generality, it follows that
k=n
and

G_{i}=H_{i}
for every
i
, which is what we set out to prove.
∎

Before discussing two practical cases of
Theorem

7.6
, we
first need a quick lemma about where the
good
options in a sum can be.

Lemma 7.7
.

If
G+H
is a sum of Left dead ends, and
G^{\prime}+H
is
a good option of
G+H
, then
G^{\prime}
is a good option of
G
.

Proof.

Let
G^{\prime\prime}
be an option of
G
satisfying
G^{\prime}\geq G^{\prime\prime}
. Then,
G^{\prime}+H\geq G^{\prime\prime}+H
. Since
G^{\prime}+H
is a good option of
G+H
, it follows that

G^{\prime}+H=G^{\prime\prime}+H
, and hence
G^{\prime}=G^{\prime\prime}
by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
).
∎

The converse of
Lemma

7.7
is not true in general: for
example, 0 is a good option of
W_{2}
, but
\overline{1}
is not a good option
of
\overline{1}+W_{2}
. That is, just because a move is good on a component, it
does not mean that the move is good on the sum; but

Lemma

7.7
tells us that good moves on sums are indeed good
moves on components.

Recall from
Definitions

5.8
and
5.9
that a racing option
of a Left dead end is an option of minimum race, and a stalling option is an
option of maximum birthday. Given a set of atoms
\mathcal{S}
, we will say
that a sum of elements of
\mathcal{S}
is an

\mathcal{S}
-factorisation
.

Theorem 7.8
.

If
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is a set of atoms such that every good
racing option of every
G\in\mathcal{S}
is prime-factorisable, then every

\mathcal{S}
-factorisation is uniquely factorisable.

Proof.

Let
G=G_{1}+\dots+G_{n}
be an
\mathcal{S}
factorisation. If
G=0
, then the
conclusion is trivial, so assume
G
is non-zero. In this case, it follows
from
Theorem

3.17
that there must exist a
good

racing option of
G
, say on
G_{1}
to
G_{1}^{\prime}
(see

Lemmas

7.7
and
3.20
). By hypothesis,

G_{1}^{\prime}
is prime-factorisable. Thus,
Theorem

7.6
yields
the result.
∎

We can prove a dual result, replacing ‘racing option’ with ‘stalling option’ in
each instance. The proof is identical.

Theorem 7.9
.

If
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is a set of atoms such that every good
stalling option of every
G\in\mathcal{S}
is prime-factorisable, then
every
\mathcal{S}
-factorisation is uniquely factorisable.

Theorems

7.8
and
7.9
are quite powerful, and they allow
us to say some pretty things. It is important to note that their hypotheses are
not disjoint from one another, but each theorem does currently give some
uniquely factorisable games that the other does not. That is, while

\overline{1}
remains the only known prime, the two results are distinct; if
every atom turns out to be prime, then every game would be uniquely
factorisable, in which case both of these results would be irrelevant. We give
some examples now.

As an example of their hypotheses overlapping, we observe that both theorems
yield that every game that can be factorised into 1-flexible atoms is uniquely
factorisable. This includes sums like

\overline{n}+\{\cdot\mid\overline{1},\overline{4}\}+\{\cdot\mid\overline{7},\overline{8}\}
,
and it is because all of their options (and hence all of their racing and
stalling options) are integers, which must be prime-factorisable since

\overline{1}
is prime (
Theorem

4.4
).

For
Theorem

7.8
in particular, it is immediate that every sum of
waiting games is uniquely factorisable: so, any game that can be written in the
form
\sum_{i}^{n}a_{i}\cdot W_{i}
is uniquely factorisable. This is because the only
racing option of a (non-zero) waiting game is 0, which is trivially
prime-factorisable. We cannot currently conclude the same result from

Theorem

7.9
since the stalling moves of waiting games (of rank at
least 3) are not known to be prime.

Finally, for
Theorem

7.9
, we can say that sums like
\{\cdot\mid W_{4},\overline{10}\}+\overline{n}+\{\cdot\mid W_{2}+W_{3},\overline{7}\}
must be
uniquely factorisable since the (only) stalling option of each atom in that sum
must be an integer, which is prime factorisable since
\overline{1}
is prime
(
Theorem

4.4
). Similarly to before, we cannot currently conclude the
same result from
Theorem

7.8
since the racing move in a game like

\{\cdot\mid W_{4},\overline{10}\}
is
W_{4}
, which is not known to be prime.

One will naturally ask whether there exists a way to generalise ‘stalling
options’ and ‘racing options’ in the hypotheses here (of

Theorems

7.8
and
7.9
), and indeed there does, but a full
treatment requires the introduction of a significant number of new ideas, and,
crucially, it does not appear to yield anything stronger than what we have
already proved.

Proposition 7.10
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with a good prime-factorisable option
H
, then

G
is uniquely factorisable.

Proof.

If
G
is an atom, or
H=0
(which would imply
G
is an atom by

Corollary

3.25
), then we are done. Otherwise, let
G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}

be a longest factorisation of
G
, where
k>1
. Since
H
is a good option
of
G
, there must exist, without loss of generality, an option
G_{1}^{\prime}
of

G_{1}
such that

G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}=H

by
Corollary

3.4
. By hypothesis,
H
is
prime-factorisable, and hence
G_{i}
must be prime for every
i\geq 2
. Since
each of these primes divides
G
, it follows that every factorisation of

G
has length
k
and is of the form
J+G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}
, where
J
is an
atom. But, by cancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
), we obtain

G_{1}=J
. Thus,
G
admits a unique factorisation.
∎

Interestingly, if we swap the word ‘prime-factorisable’ for ‘integer’ in the
hypothesis of
Proposition

7.10
, then we obtain a stronger
conclusion—that either
G
is itself an integer, or else
G
is an atom—by
exploiting the powerful properties of
\overline{1}
that are not present in a
generic prime element of a monoid. Of course, since
\overline{1}
is the only
game known to be prime, it is unclear whether ‘prime-factorisable’ is any more
general a term here than ‘integer’.

Proposition 7.11
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with a good option
\overline{n}
, then either

G=\overline{n+1}
, or else

is an atom.

Proof.

If
G
has an option to 0, then
G
is an atom by
Corollary

3.25
.
So, suppose now that
G
has a good option to
\overline{n}
with
n>0
.
Suppose further that
G
is not an atom; we will show that

G=\overline{n+1}
.

Since
G
is not an atom, we may write a factorisation
G_{1}+\dots+G_{k}
of

G
for some
k>1
. Since
\overline{n}
is a good option of
G
, there
must exist, without loss of generality, an option
G_{1}^{\prime}
of
G_{1}
such that

G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{k}=\overline{n}

by

3.4
. Since
\overline{n}

), it follows that

. Our factorisation of

. But

by

. Since

is a good option of

,

∎

7.2
Strong atoms

In the final stretch of this section, we discover swaths of strong atoms. But
first, a simple lemma.

Lemma 7.13
.

If
G
is a Left dead end, then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
,
G^{\prime}
is a
good option of
G
if and only if
G^{\prime}+(n-1)\cdot G
is a good option of

n\cdot G
.

Proof.

By pocancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
), for every pair of options

G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
of
G
, it follows that
G^{\prime}\geq G^{\prime\prime}
if and only if

G^{\prime}+(n-1)\cdot G\geq G^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\cdot G
, yielding the result immediately.
∎

So,
Lemma

7.13
says what we would expect: the good options on
a sum of copies of
G
correspond precisely to the good options of
G
.

Lemma 7.14
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends, where
G
is uniquely factorisable, and

H
is prime-factorisable, then
G+H
is uniquely factorisable.

Proof.

Since
H
is prime-factorisable, we observe that every factorisation of

G+H
must be of the form
J+H
. Necessarily,
G+H=J+H
, and hence by
cancellativity (
Theorem

3.7
) we have
G=J
. Since
G
is
uniquely factorisable, so is
J
, and we have the result.
∎

Recall that a strong atom is an atom such that each of its multiples is
uniquely factorisable.

Lemma 7.15
.

If
G
is a Left dead end that is an atom with a good prime-factorisable
option, then
G
is a strong atom.

Proof.

We need to show that
n\cdot G
is uniquely factorisable for every

n\geq 1
. By
Lemma

7.13
, it is clear that, for every

1\leq i\leq n
, there is a good option of
i\cdot G
playing on
G
to
some prime-factorisable
G^{\prime}
.
Theorem

7.6
then yields
the result.
∎

Theorem 7.16
.

If
G
is a Left dead end with a good prime-factorisable option, then

n\cdot G
is uniquely factorisable for all
n
.

Proof.

If
G
is itself prime-factorisable, then the result is immediate, and so
we assume that
G
is not prime-factorisable. Additionally, if
n=1
, then
we have the result by
Proposition

7.10
, so assume that

n>1
.

By hypothesis, we may write, without loss of generality, that

G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{t}
is a good prime-factorisable option of
G
; since each

G_{i}
is an atom, it then follows that
G_{i}
must be prime for

.
Since
G
is not prime-factorisable by our earlier assumption, we know that

G_{1}
cannot be prime (but
G_{1}^{\prime}
is prime-factorisable, of course).

Observe that, since
G_{1}+\dots+G_{t}
is a factorisation of
G
, it must
follow that
n\cdot G_{1}+\dots+n\cdot G_{t}
is a factorisation of
n\cdot G
.
Since
G_{1}^{\prime}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{t}
is a good option of
G_{1}+\dots+G_{t}
, we know by

that

is a good option of

. Since

is necessarily prime-factorisable, it then follows from

7.15
that

must be a strong atom. In particular,

is uniquely factorisable; hence, it follows from

that

It is important to point out, again, that it is not currently known whether
‘prime-factorisable’ means anything different to ‘integer’. And, due to

7.11
, if

turns out to be the only

7.16
doesn’t say anything more than we already knew
from

7.15
and

; i.e. that an atom with a

8
Final remarks

In this paper, we have investigated the structure of Left dead ends, and in
particular through the lense of factorisation theory. But the study was not
conclusive, and there remain many open questions to be resolved. The most
important of which, in this author’s opinion, is the question of uniqueness: is
every Left dead end uniquely factorisable? That is, is our monoid a unique
factorisation monoid (sometimes also called a
factorial
monoid)?

Open problem 8.1
.

Is every Left dead end uniquely factorisable?

While we have seen that the Left dead ends have a rigid structure and are
unreasonably well-behaved when compared with some other misère structures, it
would be quite remarkable indeed if this monoid would be a unique factorisation
monoid. It would imply that the monoid of Left dead ends is a free commutative
monoid (on a countably infinite number of generators); it would be isomorphic
to the monoid natural numbers (indexed from 1) under multiplication, with the
(Left dead end) atoms mapping to the prime numbers.

So, if one were to try and disprove a claim of uniqueness, where should one
look? If one is trying to find a minimal counter-example, then certainly the
game must have more than one good option; if it had exactly one, then the prime

\overline{1}
would be a factor, and hence removing the
\overline{1}
would
yield a smaller counter-example, contradicting minimality. Similarly, a minimal
counter-example must have at least three atoms that are factors; if it had one,
then it would clearly be uniquely factorisable; and if it had two, then it
would follow (from
Propositions

3.8
and
3.7
) that we could
find two factorisations with a common factor, and hence cancelling this factor
would yield a smaller counter-example, contradicting minimality.

Additionally, by
Corollary

7.2
, every Left dead end with a good
option to an atom is uniquely factorisable. Thus, a counter-example must not
have a good option to an atom; every good option must be a molecule. And herein
lies the first computational issue: at low birthdays, there are relatively few
molecules (compared to the number of atoms), and so there simply aren’t many
opportunities for a counter-example to present itself. But one cannot go to
high birthdays without contending with the combinatorial explosion.Worse still,
we saw in
Section

7
that large families of games not covered by the
above discussion are also uniquely factorisable. So, perhaps some more
ingenuity than a brute-force search is required.

In terms of the birthday of a minimal counter-example, computations performed
thus far show that one would need to start at those Left dead ends born on day
8. This was an exhaustive check performed using gemau

[
5
]
, the details of which can be found in the ancillary files
attached to this work.

Thus, from the above discussion, if the reader seeks an answer to

Open problem

8.1
, then a minimal counter-example (if it is to
exist) must be a Left dead end of birthday at least 8 that has at least three
factors that are atoms, and whose good options are all molecules. Instead of
brute-force, perhaps randomly generating games, or developing some other ideas,
would prove fruitful.

Finally, besides understanding the uniqueness of factorisations, it would also
be interesting to further investigate the the number of Left dead ends born by
day
n
. We did not discuss this much in this paper, aside from our comments at
the end of
Section

5
regarding the sets of
n
-flexible games, but
the reader is invited to
Appendix

D
for a brief overview.

Open problem 8.2
.

How many atoms and molecules are born by day
n
? More generally, given a
finite set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{1}
, how many atoms and molecules
G
are
there with
\terminal(G)=S
?

Our motivation for this paper came from wanting to better understand the
structure of misère universes. Instead, we have gotten lost in the wonderful
world of Left dead ends. The present author hopes that much more will soon be
discovered. Misère theory is full of surprises!

Appendix A
Similarities with the impartial monoid

It was pointed out to the current author by Aaron Siegel (in a private
communication) that there is a striking similarity between some of our results
here for Left dead ends and the results on the monoid of impartial misère
forms. This similarity later transpired to be even greater than it appeared at
first glance. See
[
29
, §6–7 on pp. 15–20]
in particular,
which contains most of the commonalities.

The results may not immediately appear as similar as they actually are because
the language of
[
29
]
is not congruent with the langugage we
have used here—the language more commonly found in the algebra literature.
For example, Siegel’s ‘prime’ is our ‘atom’, and ‘prime partition’ is our
‘factorisation’. It would be helpful to standardise this, so that comparisons
can be made more easily. Perhaps some of our results here could also be used to
extend the results for the impartial monoid. Further investigation is highly
warranted.

It is important to note that it is not just the theorem
statements
that
are almost identical, but the
proofs
, too. (We should point out,
however, that these really are distinct monoids; the monoid of Left dead ends
is reduced, while the impartial monoid has a torsion element, for example.) The
question then is why are these theorems and proofs so similar? This can partly
be attributed to how simple the two monoids are, and in particular to how they
are both ‘one-sided’: each impartial game can be identified with its set of
options (there is no need for a distinction between Left and Right options,
since they are always the same); similarly, each Left dead end can be
identified with its Right options (since there are never any Left options to
involve in our arguments).

But perhaps it has more to do with the fact that these are not your typical
monoids; these are monoids of combinatorial games, whose elements are defined
in a strange, recursive manner (by their Left and Right options). Is it
possible that the techniques can work for more games than just impartial games
and Left dead ends? That is, is it possible that a more general factorisation
theory of misère monoids can be developed? After all, this study of Left dead
ends, along with Siegel’s study of the impartial monoid, are so far the only
two efforts at a misère factorisation theory.

It is notable, however, that there was a recent investigation into a
factorisation theory for normal play games by Fisher, McKay, Milley,
Nowakowski, and Santos
[
9
]
. Of course, the
set of values in normal play forms a group, and so the typical factorisation
theory of monoids doesn’t apply (since everything is a unit). As such, they had
to
force
the factorisations to work by restricting the birthdays of the
factors, which is natural. Our definition of
molecule
is effectively
their
strongly decomposable
, which the reader can compare

[
9
, Definition 2 on p. 3]
.

We also remark that there is a natural bijection between the monoid of Left
dead ends and the monoid of impartial forms: given an impartial form, simply
remove every Left option from every subposition; what remains must be a Left
dead end. Conversely, given a Left dead end, one can
mirror
all of the
right options, starting from the bottom of the game tree and working up, to
necessarily obtain an impartial form. This correspondence, however, appears to
be superficial: the two monoids are fundamentally different; the monoid of Left
dead ends is partially ordered, whereas there is no way to have
G>H
for
impartial forms
G
and
H
(they are either equal or incomparable).

We conclude with a short subsection lifting a result from Siegel’s work that
the present author had not thought of for the case of Left dead ends, which
yields a reasonably efficient algorithm for finding the factors of a Left dead
end.

A.1
Factoring algorithm

All we need to do here is repurpose Siegel’s Difference Lemma
[
29
, Lemma 22 on
p. 14]
for Left dead ends. The statement here is arguably
slightly simpler.

Lemma A.1
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends satifying
H\mid G
, then either:

1.

there exist good options
G^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
such that
G+H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}+H
; or
else

2.

for each good option
G_{i}^{\prime}
(of
G
), there exists a Left dead end

X_{i}
such that
G_{i}^{\prime}=H+X_{i}
, and
G=H+\{\cdot\mid X_{i}\}
(where

X_{i}
here ranges over all
i
).

Proof.

If
G=0
, then the result is immediate, so assume otherwise. We may also
assume that there exist no good options
G^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
such that

G+H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}+H
. This implies that
G\neq H
.

Now, since
H\mid G
and
G\neq H
, there must exist some non-zero Left
dead end
K
such that
G=H+K
. By
Corollary

3.4
, for each
good option
G_{i}^{\prime}
, either there exists some (good) option
H_{i}^{\prime}
with

G_{i}^{\prime}=H_{i}^{\prime}+K
, or else there exists some (good) option
K_{i}^{\prime}
with

G_{i}^{\prime}=H+K_{i}^{\prime}
. If we are ever in the first case, with
G_{i}^{\prime}=H_{i}^{\prime}+K
, then
adding
H
to both sides yields
G_{i}^{\prime}+H=H_{i}^{\prime}+G
, contradicting our initial
assumption. Thus, we must always be in the second case where
G_{i}^{\prime}=H+K_{i}^{\prime}
,
from which it follows (from
Theorem

2.4
) that
G\geq H+\{\cdot\mid K_{i}^{\prime}\}
(where
K_{i}^{\prime}
ranges over all
i
). Now, clearly
\{\cdot\mid K_{i}^{\prime}\}\geq K
, and hence

\displaystyle H+\{\cdot\mid K_{i}^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle\geq H+K

\displaystyle=G,

from which we obtain
G=H+\{\cdot\mid K_{i}^{\prime}\}
, which is the result.
∎

Then, as Siegel notes for the impartial monoid, we too now have a reasonable
algorithm for finding the factors of an arbitrary Left dead end. Of course, we
could always use a brute-force algorithm to find all factors (by generating all
games born before the game we are factoring, which must be finite, and then
trying every possible combination), but the algorithm yielded for us here is
much more practical. We can follow Siegel’s instructions almost exactly, but we
write it out here explicitly. The idea is that we are going to recursively
compute all of the factors of each good option
G^{\prime}
of
G
, and then
reconstruct the possible factors of
G
using
Lemma

A.1
.

Let
G
be a Left dead end. If
G
is an atom, then we already have all of the
factors of
G
(they are 0 and
G
itself). Otherwise, we may write
G=H+K
for
some non-zero Left dead ends
H
and
K
. We need to show that we can reover

H
and
K
. Obviously, we have
H\mid G
and
K\mid G
, and so we may apply

Lemma

A.1
.

Suppose first that both
H
and
K
satisfy the first item in

Lemma

A.1
. In particular, for
H
, we see that
G+H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}+H
. Adding

K
to both sides yields
G+H^{\prime}+K=G^{\prime}+G
. Then, by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
), we obtain
H^{\prime}+K=G^{\prime}
. Thus, we can recover
K
as a
factor of some option
G^{\prime}
of
G
. We can apply an identical argument in order
to recover
H
as a factor of some (perhaps different) option of
G
.

Now suppose instead, without loss of generality, that
H
does not satisfy the
first item in
Lemma

A.1
. Then
H
must satisfy the second item: for
each good option
G_{i}^{\prime}
of
G
, there exists some Left end
X_{i}
such that

G_{i}^{\prime}=H+X_{i}
, and
G=H+\{\cdot\mid X_{i}\}
. So, we can recover
H
and each

X_{i}
from the factors of the
G_{i}^{\prime}
, and we can also then recover
K
; we have

H+K=G=H+\{\cdot\mid X_{i}\}
, and so
K=\{\cdot\mid X_{i}\}
by cancellativity
(
Theorem

3.7
).

Thus, a shortcut to factorising a Left dead end is to compute all of the
factors of its good options recursively, and then reconstruct the possible
factors of the Left dead end according to the strategy above.

Appendix B

When we have a short game, in normal play or in misére play, and we apply
some reduction to it (removing a dominated option, or bypassing a reversible
one), it is impossible to strictly increase the formal birthday. It thus
follows that the birthday of a game is exactly the formal birthday of the
game’s canonical form.

Unfortunately, it is not so simple for the race of a game. Consider, in normal
play, the form
G=\{0,1\mid\cdot\}
. Clearly, the formal race of this game is
1, since Left can terminate the game immediately. But, in normal play,

G=\{1\mid\cdot\}
, which clearly has formal race 2. Since
\{1\mid\cdot\}
is
the canonical form of
G
, we have seen that canonical forms may have higher
formal race than other games in the equivalence class. Must it be the highest?
Alas, it need not be, for we also have the equality
G=\{3/2\mid\cdot\}
, which
has formal race 3. Thus, the formal race of a game’s canonical form could be
higher or lower than the formal race of the game.

As such, instead of creating a split for race and formal race, we have chosen
to discuss only a single concept, which is functionally equivalent to formal
race. This is the same motivation for how we defined the terminal lengths of a
game, without discussing
formal
terminal lengths. Perhaps creating a
distinction could lead to some merit elsewhere, but that merit would not be
realised in this study of Left dead ends.

Appendix C

While the inductive proof of
Theorem

3.17
is easy on the
eyes, we provide here an alternative proof that constructs a distinguishing
game. The intuition is simply the observation that a player would sometimes
like the option to pass when playing a sum of combinatorial games. We can
extend this idea and construct a game
C_{n}
in which Right would want to pass
exactly
n
times before having to play again. Thus, if
G
is a Left dead end
with
n\in\terminal(G)
, and
H
is a Left dead end with

n\not\in\terminal(H)
, then Right will win playing first on
G+C_{n}
, since he
can pass
n
times, but he cannot do the same on
H+C_{n}
.

Theorem

3.17
.

If
G
and
H
are Left dead ends with
G\geq H
, then

\terminal(G)\subseteq\terminal(H)
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
G\geq H
and
\terminal(G)\not\subseteq\terminal(H)
. There must then exist some

n\in\terminal(G)\backslash\terminal(H)
, and so, by
Theorem

2.4
and

Lemma

3.16
, it follows that both
G
and
H
are non-zero. Now
construct a game (to be our distinguishing game)
C_{0}\coloneq\{0,*\mid*\}
,
and then, recursively,

C_{k}\coloneq\{C_{k-1}\mid 0\}

for
k>0
. It is clear that

\outcomeR(G+C_{n})=\mathscr{R}<\mathscr{L}=\outcomeR(H+C_{n}),

and so
G\not\geq H
, yielding the desired contradiction.
∎

Note that our distinguishing game (in the proof above) was in the dicot
universe
\mathcal{D}
. Since the partial order of Left dead ends is
multiversal (i.e. given
Corollary

2.5
), it must be the case that two
Left dead ends are distinguishable if and only if they are distinguishable
modulo
\mathcal{D}
(recall that
\mathcal{D}
is a subuniverse of

every
universe).

Appendix E

It is not known exactly how pocancellativity and invertibility differ within a
given universe (invertibility, of course, always implies pocancellativity). In
the recent work by the present author, McKay, Milley, Nowakowski, and Santos

[
8
]
, however, the universe
\mathcal{E}
was
shown to be pocancellative (with ‘
\geq_{\mathcal{E}}
’ as the partial order,
naturally). But
\mathcal{E}
does not form a group, and so we have an example
of a universe where the submonoid of pocancellative elements (i.e. the
pocancellative submonoid) differs from the submonoid of invertible elements
(i.e. the invertible submonoid).

The other two universes studied in the paper were
\mathcal{D}
and

\mathcal{M}
, which were shown not to be pocancellative. Even though

\mathcal{M}
was shown to have a sizable submonoid that is pocancellative (the
set of blocking games
\mathcal{B}
), there were no pocancellative elements
discovered of the full monoid. The situation for
\mathcal{D}
was more dire:
no pocancellative submonoids were discovered at all, let alone pocancellative
elements.

Given that we have characterisations of the invertible elements of

\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
, we have an initial question: what do the
pocancellative elements of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
look like, and how
do they differ from the invertible elements of the two monoids respectively? A
grander question, still, is the following.

Open problem E.1
.

In a universe
\mathcal{U}
, which elements, if any, are pocancellative but
not invertible?
