Another criterion for supersolvability of finite groups
Marius Tărnăuceanu

Abstract
Let o ⁡ ( G ) o(G) be the average order of a finite group G G . In this paper, we prove that if o ⁡ ( G ) < 31 12 o(G)<\frac{31}{12} , then G G is supersolvable. Moreover, we have o ⁡ ( G ) = 31 12 o(G)=\frac{31}{12} if and only if G ≅ A 4 G\cong A_{4} . We also classify finite groups G G satisfying o ⁡ ( G ) < 31 12 o(G)<\frac{31}{12} .

Proofs of the main results

We start with the following easy but important lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
1=d_{1}<d_{2}<\ldots<d_{r}
be the element orders of
G
and
n_{d_{i}}(G)
be the number of elements of order
d_{i}
in
G
,
\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,r
. Assume that
r\geq 3
and take a positive integer
s
with
3\leq s\leq r
. If
o(G)<c
, where
c>0
is a real number, then

n_{d_{s-1}}(G)>\frac{d_{s}-c}{d_{s}-d_{s-1}}\,|G|-\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}\frac{d_{s}-d_{i}}{d_{s}-d_{s-1}}\,n_{d_{i}}(G).

(3)

Proof.

We have
|G|=1{+}n_{d_{2}}(G){+}\ldots{+}n_{d_{s}}(G){+}\ldots{+}n_{d_{r}}(G)
, so we deduce that

\displaystyle\psi(G)

\displaystyle=1{+}d_{2}n_{d_{2}}(G){+}\ldots{+}d_{s}n_{d_{s}}(G){+}\ldots{+}d_{r}n_{d_{r}}(G)

\displaystyle\geq 1{+}d_{2}n_{d_{2}}(G){+}\ldots{+}d_{s-1}n_{d_{s-1}}(G){+}d_{s}(n_{d_{s}}(G){+}\ldots{+}n_{d_{r}})(G)

\displaystyle=1{+}d_{2}n_{d_{2}}(G){+}\ldots{+}d_{s-1}n_{d_{s-1}}(G){+}d_{s}(|G|{-}1{-}n_{d_{2}}(G){-}\ldots{-}n_{d_{s-1}}(G)).

Since
o(G)<c
, it follows that
\psi(G)<c|G|
. Therefore we have

c|G|>1{+}d_{2}n_{d_{2}}(G){+}\ldots{+}d_{s-1}n_{d_{s-1}}(G){+}d_{s}(|G|{-}1{-}n_{d_{2}}(G){-}\ldots{-}n_{d_{s-1}}(G)).

Clearly, the last inequality is equivalent to (3), completing the proof.
∎

Our second lemma collects information about some particular classes of finite groups
G
satisfying
o(G)<\frac{31}{12}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given a finite group
G
such that
o(G)<\frac{31}{12}
, the following statements hold:

a)

If
|G|
is odd, then
G\cong C_{3}
.

b)

If
G
is abelian, then either
G\cong C_{3}
or
G
is an elementary abelian
2
-group.

c)

If
G
is a
2
-group, then
G^{\prime}=\Phi(G)
and
n_{2}(G)>\frac{17}{24}\,|G|-\frac{3}{2}
.

d)

If
G
is supersolvable of even order but not a
2
-group, then
G\cong K\rtimes H
is a Frobenius group whose kernel
K
is an elementary abelian
3
-group and the complement
H
is cyclic of order
2
; moreover, in this case we have

o(G)=\frac{5\cdot 3^{m}-2}{2\cdot 3^{m}}\,,

where
|K|=3^{m}
.

Proof.

a)

It suffices to observe that if
|G|\geq 5
, then

o(G)\geq\frac{1+3(|G|-1)}{|G|}>\frac{31}{12}\,.

b)

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}\times\cdots\times G_{k}
, where
G_{i}
is an abelian
p_{i}
-group,
\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,k
. By using (1), it follows that if
k\geq 2
, then

o(G)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}o(G_{i})>\frac{31}{12}\,.

So, we can assume that
k=1
, i.e.
G
is an abelian
p
-group. For
p\geq 3
we get
G\cong C_{3}
from a), while for
p=2
and
\exp(G)\neq 2
we have

n_{2}(G)>\frac{4-\frac{31}{12}}{4-2}\,|G|-\frac{4-1}{4-2}=\frac{17}{24}\,|G|-\frac{3}{2}

by (3). Then

|\Omega_{1}(G)|=n_{2}(G)+1>\frac{17}{24}\,|G|-\frac{1}{2}>\frac{1}{2}\,|G|.

Since
\Omega_{1}(G)\leq G
, it follows that
\Omega_{1}(G)=G
, a contradiction. Thus
\exp(G)=2
, i.e.
G
is an elementary abelian
2
-group.

c)

Since
\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}
is abelian and
o\!\left(\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)\leq o(G)<\frac{31}{12}
, from b) we deduce that
\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}
is elementary abelian and so
G^{\prime}=\Phi(G)
. Obviously, the inequality
n_{2}(G)>\frac{17}{24}\,|G|-\frac{3}{2}
holds if
G
is elementary abelian. We observe that it also holds if
\exp(G)\neq 2
by applying (3) for
s=3
.

d)

Since
G
is supersolvable, we have
G\cong K\rtimes H
, where
K
is the characteristic subgroup consisting of all elements of odd order in
G
and
H
is a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
. Also, we know that
G
has quotients isomorphic to
C_{2}
, which implies that
2\mid|\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}|
and therefore
\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\cong C_{2}^{q}
, where
q\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. This leads to
K\subseteq G^{\prime}
. Let
H_{1}
be a complement of
K
in
G^{\prime}
. Then
G^{\prime}=K\times H_{1}
because
G^{\prime}
is nilpotent. It follows that
H_{1}
is characteristic in
G
and we have

\frac{G}{H_{1}}\cong K\rtimes\frac{H}{H_{1}}\cong K\rtimes C_{2}^{q}\mbox{ and }o\!\left(\frac{G}{H_{1}}\right)\leq o(G)<\frac{31}{12}\,.

Let
\pi(G)=\{2=p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r}\}
be the set of primes dividing
|G|
, where
p_{1}<p_{2}<\ldots<p_{r}
. By induction on
r
, we infer that
G
has a quotient of type
K_{r}\rtimes C_{2}^{q}
, where
K_{r}
is a Sylow
p_{r}
-subgroup of
G
. Take
K_{r1}\lhd K_{r}\rtimes C_{2}^{q}
with
|K_{r1}|=p_{r}
. Then

\frac{K_{r}\rtimes C_{2}^{q}}{K_{r1}}\cong\frac{K_{r}}{K_{r1}}\rtimes C_{2}^{q}\mbox{ and }\left|\frac{K_{r}}{K_{r1}}\right|<|K_{r}|.

By repeating this process, we get that
G
has a quotient of type
C_{p_{r}}\rtimes C_{2}^{q}
. Now it is easy to see that the condition
o(C_{p_{r}}\rtimes C_{2}^{q})<\frac{31}{12}
implies
q=1
and
p_{r}=3
. Thus
H_{1}
is a maximal subgroup of
H
. Suppose that

contains a maximal subgroup
M\neq H_{1}
. Then
KM
is a normal subgroup of index
2
in
G
and so
\frac{G}{KM}\cong C_{2}
. This shows that
G^{\prime}\subseteq KM
, which leads to
G^{\prime}=KM
. Then
M\subseteq G^{\prime}
and therefore
H=MH_{1}\subseteq G^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Consequently,
H_{1}
is the unique maximal subgroup of

, i.e.
H
is cyclic. Moreover, we remark that
H\cong C_{2}
because

o(C_{2^{t}})=\frac{2^{2t+1}+1}{3\cdot 2^{t}}>\frac{31}{12}\,,\forall\,t\geq 2.

Hence
G\cong K\rtimes H
, where
|K|=3^{m}
and
H\cong C_{2}
.

Since the first three element orders of
G
are
d_{1}=1
,
d_{2}=2
and
d_{3}=3
, by applying (3) for
s=3
we obtain

n_{2}(G)>\frac{5}{12}\,|G|-2=\frac{5}{6}\,3^{m}-2.

Assume that
n_{2}(G)\neq 3^{m}
. Then
n_{2}(G)\leq 3^{m-1}
because
n_{2}(G)\mid 3^{m}
, which implies that

\frac{5}{6}\,3^{m}-2<3^{m-1}.

This leads to

, i.e.

n_{2}(G)=3^{m}
. We also observe that

cannot have elements of order

Assume that
\exp(K)\neq 3
. Then
d_{4}=9
and from (3) for

it follows that

\displaystyle n_{3}(K)=n_{3}(G)

\displaystyle>\frac{77}{72}\,|G|-\frac{4}{3}-\frac{7}{6}\,3^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{35}{36}\,3^{m}-\frac{4}{3}

\displaystyle>\frac{7}{9}\,3^{m}-1,

contradicting Theorem D, 2). Consequently,
\exp(K)=3
and so
\pi_{e}(G)=\{1,2,3\}
. Thus

is a Frobenius group with kernel
K\cong C_{3}^{m}
and complement

by Theorem D, 3). Also, it is clear that

o(G)=\frac{1+2\cdot 3^{m}+3(3^{m}-1)}{2\cdot 3^{m}}=\frac{5\cdot 3^{m}-2}{2\cdot 3^{m}}\,,

completing the proof.

|G|
. Let
1=d_{1}<d_{2}<\ldots<d_{r}
be the element orders of
G
. Then

\psi(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}n_{d_{i}}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\varphi(d_{i})n_{d_{i}}^{\prime}(G),

where
n_{d_{i}}^{\prime}(G)
denotes the number of cyclic subgroups of order
d_{i}
in
G
,
\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,r
. Since
\varphi(d_{i})
is even for all
d_{i}>2
, we infer that
\psi(G)\equiv\,1\,({\rm mod}\,2)
. This shows that
|G|=4n
, where
n
is odd and divisible by
3
.

If
G
is supersolvable, then we get
G\cong G^{\prime}\rtimes C_{2}^{2}
, as in the proof of Lemma 2.2, d). It follows that
G
has a quotient
G_{1}
of order
12
. Then
o(G_{1})\leq\frac{31}{12}
, contradicting the fact that
o(X)>\frac{31}{12}
for all supersolvable groups
X
of order
12
.

If
G
is not supersolvable, then it is a just non-supersolvable group and therefore
G\cong N\rtimes M
with
M
and
N
as above. It is easy to see that the unique possibility to have
o(G)=\frac{31}{12}
appears in
Case 1
for
r=2
, i.e. for
G\cong C_{2}^{2}\rtimes C_{3}\cong A_{4}
, as desired.
