Abstract
. Using the technique of pedal coordinates we investigate the orbits of a free double linkage. We provide a geometrical construction for them and also show a surprising connection between this mechanical system and orbits around a Black Hole and solutions of Dark Kepler problem.

The problem we choose is that of movement of a
free double linkage
– i.e. a mechanical system with three point-masses, two of which are linked to the central node by massless rigid rods. One can also think of this as a double pendulum but unattached. No outside force is acting on the linkage, hence it is “free”. Figure

We will consider only the planar case – i.e. when all three points, as well as their velocity vectors, lie in a common plane. The main object of our study is the orbit of an outside node in the center of mass (CM) reference frame.

The double linkage can produce a surprisingly rich family of curves – as demonstrated by a small gallery of cases in Figure

We provide pedal equation for a general case of arbitrary masses and arbitrary lengths of the rods in Theorem
1
. In Corollary
3
we also show that in the special case of zero total angular momentum, the orbit of an outside node can be fully described by an interesting class of curves, which we call
ellipses in generalized rotational frame of reference
(GRFR) (Figure

Figure 3.
Ellipse in a generalized rotation frame of reference

An (ordinary) ellipse in a rotational frame of reference (RFR) is a curve produced just by rotating every point on an ellipse by the amount of area
S
swept by the radial vector (emanating from the center) up to that point. These curves are exactly what we get when solving Hooke’s law, i.e. a dynamical system of the form

{\bf\ddot{x}}=-\omega{\bf x},

in RFR. Hence the name. Remember that the trajectory of any central force problem (which Hooke’s law is an example of) is parameterized so that equal areas are swept in equal time. Ellipse in GRFR is basically the same curve but with the area computed on a different co-axial ellipse (see Figure
3
).

Using pedal coordinates we are also able to show a link between trajectories of a free double linkage and orbits of a test particle inside a Schwarzschild Black hole (see Section
4
). We have also found that the free double linkage provides a particular solution to the “Dark Kepler problem” (discussed in
[
7
]
). Although certainly just a mathematical coincidence, it is amazing that a connection between such dramatically different problems exists at all. This is perhaps pointing towards certain conservatism of Nature in choosing curves for its orbits.

2.
Equation of motion and conservation laws

Consider the dynamical system of the free double linkage, i.e. three point masses
{\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, two of which are connected by massless rods:
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}
\displaystyle F_{1}
\displaystyle F_{1}
\displaystyle F_{2}
\displaystyle F_{2}
\displaystyle l_{1}
\displaystyle l_{2}

As there are no outside forces acting on the system, the equations of motion are of the form:

\displaystyle m_{x}{\bf\ddot{x}}

\displaystyle=F_{1}({\bf y}-{\bf x}),

\displaystyle m_{y}{\bf\ddot{y}}

\displaystyle=-F_{1}({\bf y}-{\bf x})+F_{2}({\bf z}-{\bf y}),

\displaystyle m_{z}{\bf\ddot{z}}

\displaystyle=-F_{2}({\bf z}-{\bf y}),

where the internal forces
F_{1},F_{2}
are such that the length of the massless rods remains constant:

(1)

\left|{\bf x-\bf y}\right|=l_{1},\qquad\left|{\bf z-\bf y}\right|=l_{2}.

It is easy to see that the
total kinetic energy

K

K:=\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{x}\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}+m_{y}\left|{\bf\dot{y}}\right|^{2}+m_{z}\left|{\bf\dot{z}}\right|^{2}\right),

is conserved:

\dot{K}=m_{x}{\bf\ddot{x}}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}+m_{y}{\bf\ddot{y}}\cdot{\bf\dot{y}}+m_{z}{\bf\ddot{z}}\cdot{\bf\dot{z}}=-F_{1}({\bf y}-{\bf x})\cdot({\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}})-F_{2}({\bf z}-{\bf y})\cdot({\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}})=0,

where the last equality is a differential consequence of (
1
).

By summing all equations of motion we find

m_{x}{\bf\ddot{x}}+m_{y}{\bf\ddot{y}}+m_{z}{\bf\ddot{z}}=0,\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad{\bf\ddot{T}}=0,

that the center of mass
{\bf T}
:

{\bf T}:=\frac{m_{x}{\bf x}+m_{y}{\bf y}+m_{z}{\bf z}}{m_{x}+m_{y}+m_{z}},

is moving uniformly in a straight line.

We can also say that the
total linear momentum

{\bf\dot{T}}
, is conserved.

Another conserved vector is the
total angular momentum

{\bf L}:=m_{x}{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}+m_{y}{\bf y}\times{\bf\dot{y}}+m_{z}{\bf z}\times{\bf\dot{z}}.

{\bf\dot{L}}=m_{x}{\bf x}\times{\bf\ddot{x}}+m_{y}{\bf y}\times{\bf\ddot{y}}+m_{z}{\bf z}\times{\bf\ddot{z}}=F_{1}({\bf x}-{\bf y})\times({\bf y}-{\bf x})+F_{2}({\bf y}-{\bf z})\times({\bf z}-{\bf y})=0.

Thus we have altogether 9 conserved quantities:

l_{1}\ ,l_{2}\ ,K\ ,{\bf\dot{T}},{\bf L}.

Differential consequences of (
1
) are:

({\bf y}-{\bf x})\cdot({\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}})=0,\qquad({\bf z}-{\bf y})\cdot({\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}})=0,

which tells us that the displacement vectors
{\bf u}:={\bf y}-{\bf x}
,
{\bf v}:={\bf z}-{\bf y}
between points
{\bf y},{\bf x}
a
{\bf z},{\bf y}
are orthogonal to their respective velocities:

{\bf u}\cdot{\bf\dot{u}}=0,\qquad{\bf v}\cdot{\bf\dot{v}}=0.

Differentiating (
1
) second time we get consequences from which it is possible to extract the internal forces
F_{1},F_{2}
:

\displaystyle 0=\left|{\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}+({\bf y}-{\bf x})\cdot({\bf\ddot{y}}-{\bf\ddot{x}}),

\displaystyle 0=\left|{\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}}\right|^{2}+({\bf z}-{\bf y})\cdot({\bf\ddot{z}}-{\bf\ddot{y}}),

\displaystyle\left|{\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}=F_{1}\frac{m_{x}+m_{y}}{m_{x}m_{y}}l_{1}^{2}+F_{2}l_{1}l_{2}\frac{\cos\omega}{m_{y}},

\displaystyle\left|{\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}}\right|^{2}=F_{2}\frac{m_{z}+m_{y}}{m_{z}m_{y}}l_{2}^{2}+F_{1}l_{1}l_{2}\frac{\cos\omega}{m_{y}},

\displaystyle F_{1}=\frac{m_{y}}{m_{z}}\frac{\tilde{m}_{z}l_{2}^{2}\left|{\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}-\cos\omega l_{1}l_{2}m_{z}\left|{\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}}\right|^{2}}{l_{1}^{2}l_{2}^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}\tilde{m}_{z}}{m_{x}m_{z}}-\cos^{2}\omega\right)},

\displaystyle F_{2}=\frac{m_{y}}{m_{x}}\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}l_{1}^{2}\left|{\bf\dot{z}}-{\bf\dot{y}}\right|^{2}-\cos\omega l_{1}l_{2}m_{x}\left|{\bf\dot{y}}-{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}}{l_{1}^{2}l_{2}^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}\tilde{m}_{z}}{m_{x}m_{z}}-\cos^{2}\omega\right)},

where, for the sake of brevity, we have introduced the notation:

\tilde{m}_{x}:=m_{x}+m_{y},\qquad\tilde{m}_{z}:=m_{z}+m_{y},

and
\omega
is the angle between the two rods:

\cos\omega:=\frac{({\bf x-\bf y})\cdot({\bf z-\bf y})}{l_{1}l_{2}}.

2.1.
Center of mass frame of reference

Without the loss of generality we can move to an inertial frame of reference in which the center of mass is at rest at the origin:

\displaystyle{\bf T}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle{\bf y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m_{x}{\bf x}+m_{z}{\bf z}}{m_{y}},

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{T}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{y}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m_{x}{\bf\dot{x}}+m_{z}{\bf\dot{z}}}{m_{y}}.

The equations of motion become:

\displaystyle m_{x}{\bf\ddot{x}}

\displaystyle=-F_{1}\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}{\bf x}+m_{z}{\bf z}}{m_{y}},

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{m}_{z}l_{2}^{2}\left|\tilde{m}_{x}{\bf\dot{x}}+m_{z}{\bf\dot{z}}\right|^{2}-\cos\omega l_{1}l_{2}m_{z}\left|\tilde{m}_{z}{\bf\dot{z}}+m_{x}{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}}{m_{y}m_{z}l_{1}^{2}l_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0}-\cos^{2}\omega)},

\displaystyle m_{z}{\bf\ddot{z}}

\displaystyle=F_{2}\frac{\tilde{m}_{z}{\bf z}+m_{x}{\bf x}}{m_{y}},

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}l_{1}^{2}\left|m_{x}{\bf\dot{x}}+\tilde{m}_{z}{\bf\dot{z}}\right|^{2}-\cos\omega l_{1}l_{2}m_{x}\left|m_{z}{\bf\dot{z}}+\tilde{m}_{x}{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}}{m_{y}m_{x}l_{1}^{2}l_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0}-\cos^{2}\omega)},

\displaystyle\cos\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tilde{m}_{z}{\bf z}+m_{x}{\bf x})\cdot(m_{z}{\bf z}+\tilde{m}_{x}{\bf x})}{m_{y}^{2}l_{1}l_{2}}.

Our conserved quantities are now in the form:

(2)

\left|\tilde{m}_{x}{\bf x}+m_{z}{\bf z}\right|=l_{1}m_{y},\qquad\left|m_{x}{\bf x}+\tilde{m}_{z}{\bf z}\right|=l_{2}m_{y}.

(3)

K=\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}m_{x}}{2m_{y}}\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}+\frac{\tilde{m}_{z}m_{z}}{2m_{y}}\left|{\bf\dot{z}}\right|^{2}+\frac{m_{x}m_{z}}{m_{y}}({\bf\dot{x}}\cdot{\bf\dot{z}}).

(4)

{\bf L}=\frac{\tilde{m}_{x}m_{x}}{m_{y}}({\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}})+\frac{\tilde{m}_{z}m_{z}}{m_{y}}({\bf z}\times{\bf\dot{z}})+\frac{m_{x}m_{z}}{m_{y}}({\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{z}}+{\bf z}\times{\bf\dot{x}}).

2.2.
Coplanar case

With a loss of generality, we now focus on the case, where the movement of the whole system is confined to a plane. Let us define the following notation:

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\frac{({\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}})\times{\bf x}}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|}=\frac{{\bf\dot{x}}\left|{\bf x}\right|^{2}-{\bf x}({\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}})}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|},

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\frac{({\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}})\times{\bf\dot{x}}}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|}=\frac{-{\bf x}\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{2}+{\bf\dot{x}}({\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}})}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|}.

The vector
{\bf x}^{\perp}
is perpendicular to
{\bf x}
and clearly lies in the plane to which belong both
{\bf x}
and
{\bf\dot{x}}
. And similarly for
{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}
.

It is a stimulating exercise to verify the following properties:

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf x}=0,

\displaystyle\left|{\bf x}^{\perp}\right|=\left|{\bf x}\right|,

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}=0,

\displaystyle\left|{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}\right|=\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|,

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}=\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|,

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}={\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}},

\displaystyle{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}=\frac{({\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}})}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|}{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}},

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}\times{\bf\dot{x}}=-\frac{({\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}})}{\left|{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}\right|}{\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}},

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}\times{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}={\bf x}\times{\bf\dot{x}}.

We are considering the coplanar case specifically for the possibility to use the so called pedal coordinates
r,p
, which are a special type of coordinates that do not determine the position of each point on a curve (as Cartesian or polar coordinates do) but, instead, measures the distance of a point from the origin (
r
) and the distance of a tangent in that point to the origin (
p
). This clearly shows that the pedal coordinates can describe planar curves only. They are not general purpose coordinates.

Specifically, let us define for every point
{\bf x}
and its tangent vector
{\bf\dot{x}}
the following quantities:

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle:=\left|{\bf x}\right|,

The distance form the origin.

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}}{\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|},

The oriented distance of the tangent from the origin.

\displaystyle p_{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\bf x}\cdot{\bf\dot{x}}}{\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|},

The oriented distance of the normal from the origin.

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\bf\dot{x}}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf\ddot{x}}}{\left|{\bf\dot{x}}\right|^{3}},

The oriented curvature.

The so-called contrapedal coordinate
p_{c}
is not an independent variable, but in some cases it is more convenient to use. Its relation with
(r,p)
is as follows:

p^{2}+p_{c}^{2}=r^{2}.

One of the many advantages of pedal coordinates is that computation of the curvature is much more simple.

\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\rm d}p}{{\rm d}r}=\kappa.

3.
Pedal coordinates

To understand what this result is telling us, we must first better understand the pedal coordinates and their many advantages.

Let us introduce again for any point
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
on a plane curve
\gamma
the following symbols:

\displaystyle{\bf x}

\displaystyle:=(x,y),

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=(-y,x),

\displaystyle{\bf x}\cdot{\bf y}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=-{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf y},

\displaystyle\left({\bf x}^{\perp}\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=-{\bf x}.

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\bf x}^{\perp}\cdot{\dot{\bf x}}}{\left|\dot{\bf x}\right|},

\displaystyle p_{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\bf x}\cdot{\dot{\bf x}}}{\left|\dot{\bf x}\right|},

3.2.2.
Pedal

Pedal transform maps any point on a given curve
{\bf x}
to the orthogonal projection
{\bf\tilde{x}}
of the origin to the tangent at
{\bf x}
. In the diagram at the beginning of the section,
{\bf\tilde{x}}
is denoted by
P({\bf x})
.

Algebraically, the new point is given by

P:\qquad{\bf\tilde{x}}={\bf x}-p_{c}\frac{\bf\dot{x}}{\left|\bf\dot{x}\right|}.

This transformation has very nice properties. It maps a focal ellipse or a hyperbola (that is with the origin at the focus) to its circumcircle. The pedal of a parabola is a line – not the directrix, though; this line is parallel to the directrix and passes through the vertex. A central rectangular hyperbola is mapped to the Lemniscate of Bernoulli and so on.

In fact, pedal coordinates was originally devised (as the name suggests) to make the pedal transformation easy to do. In Cartesian coordinates it is generally required to solve a differential equation. But the same thing can be by done algebraically in pedal coordinates.

Specifically (see
[
14
, p. 228]
), to any curve given by the equation

f(p,r,p_{c})=0,

the pedal curve satisfies the equation

f\left(r,\frac{r^{2}}{p},\frac{r}{p}p_{c}\right)=0.

With this information, checking some of our claims is an elementary exercise. For instance, the following line of computation:

\frac{L^{2}}{p^{2}}=\frac{M}{r}+c\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}=\frac{Mp}{r^{2}}+c\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad Mp=-cr^{2}+L^{2}.

proves that the pedal of a (focal) conic is, indeed, a circle. Or, inversely, taking for granted that the pedal of a central rectangular hyperbola (
\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{p^{2}}=r^{2}
) is the Lemniscate of Bernoulli, we can in no time produce a pedal equation for it:

\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{p^{2}}=r^{2}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad\frac{a^{2}b^{2}}{r^{2}}=\frac{r^{4}}{p^{2}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad r^{3}=abp.

3.2.3.

Another important transform is

3.2.4.

\displaystyle:=\left|\tilde{z}\right|=\left|\sqrt{z}\right|=\sqrt{r}.

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}z^{-\frac{1}{2}}\dot{z}.

\displaystyle\left|\dot{\tilde{z}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left|\dot{z}\right|.

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{c}+{\rm i}\tilde{p}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\tilde{z}\overline{\dot{\tilde{z}}}}{\left|\dot{\tilde{z}}\right|}=\frac{z^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{2}\bar{z}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\dot{z}}{\frac{1}{2}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left|\dot{z}\right|}=r^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{z\dot{\bar{z}}}{\left|\dot{z}\right|}=r^{-\frac{1}{2}}(p_{c}+{\rm i}p).

(23)

\displaystyle f(p,r,p_{c})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M_{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}

ab\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=-\frac{(r^{2}-a)(r^{2}-b)}{r^{2}},\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M_{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad ab\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=-\frac{(r-a)(r-b)}{r^{2}}.

(\ref{eGRFR})\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R_{\omega}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad(ab)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(a^{2}-r^{2})(r^{2}-b^{2})}{r^{2}}.

So this curve looks complicated but in the correct rotational frame it is just a central ellipse.

4.
Zero angular momentum case

Assuming
{\bf L}=0
, our solution of free double linkage (
5
) can be brought into a very simple form.

C
OROLLARY
2
.

The curve traced by the node
{\bf x}
of a coplanar free double linkage with zero total angular momentum
{\bf L}=0
as viewed from the center of mass is given by:

(31)

a^{2}\left(r^{2}+r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r^{2}-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r^{2})(br^{2}+a)^{2}}{r^{2}},

where

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=Mm_{y}(r_{1}-r_{0})^{2},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=4m_{x}m_{z},

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{1}(m_{z}+m_{y})+l_{2}m_{z}}{M},

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{l_{1}(m_{z}+m_{y})-l_{2}m_{z}}{M}.

Proof.

Trivial.
∎

Let us discuss few interesting special cases.

4.1.
Central ellipse

Clearly, setting
a=br_{0}r_{1}
we can eliminate the term
(br^{2}+a)^{2}
from both sides of (
31
) and obtain:

(32)

\left(r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r^{2}-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r^{2})}{r^{2}},\qquad a=br_{0}r_{1},

i.e. central ellipse with semi axis
r_{0},r_{1}
(see (
19
)).

However, let us say that we cannot recognize this curve to be an ellipse. In that case (which happens all the time) we can use some transforms introduced in Section
3
to try to simplify the result. For instance, using the complex square root
M_{\frac{1}{2}}
(see (
23
)):

\left(r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r^{2}-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r^{2})}{r^{2}}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M_{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad\left(r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r)}{r^{2}},

we obtain the equation for the focal ellipse (see (
17
)). But say that even this we cannot identify. Using dual parallel transform
E^{*}_{\alpha}
(
20
) we get:

\left(r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r)}{r^{2}}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle E^{*}_{\alpha}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad\frac{r_{0}^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{p^{2}}=\frac{(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2})^{2}}{4r_{0}^{2}r_{1}^{2}},\qquad\alpha:=-\frac{r_{0}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}}{2r_{0}^{2}r_{1}^{2}},

which is clearly a line.

Taking the inverse transforms we can thus conclude that our solution is a complex square of a dual parallel of a line – which is perhaps the most cumbersome description of a central ellipse there is. But it contains information about the construction.

As we will see, the exact same transform, that is a square root
M_{\frac{1}{2}}
followed by a dual parallel transform
E^{*}_{\alpha}
for suitable
\alpha
will always produce something interesting. Perhaps the transform
E^{*}_{\alpha}M_{\frac{1}{2}}
is somewhat natural for double linkage.

4.2.
Kepler problem in general relativity

Take, for instance, the case
r_{0}=0
, meaning that the point
{\bf x}
is allowed to pass through the center of mass of the linkage.

Putting
r_{0}=0
into (
31
) we obtain

(33)

a^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r_{1}^{2}-r^{2})(br^{2}+a)^{2}}{r^{4}},\qquad r_{0}=0.

Using the square root transform
M_{\frac{1}{2}}
we get

(34)

(\ref{r0case})\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M_{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad a^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r_{1}^{2}-r)(br+a)^{2}}{r^{3}}.

This equation is actually of the same form as the so-called “Kepler problem in General relativity”, i.e. the relativistic correction to a classical Kepler problem.

Kepler problem in GR is the problem of orbits (or geodesics) around a non-rotating compact body described by the Schwarzschild solution of the Einstein equations of General relativity
[
19
]
:

(35)

{r^{\prime}_{\varphi}}^{2}=\frac{r^{4}}{b_{s}^{2}}-\left(1-\frac{r_{s}}{r}\right)\left(\frac{r^{4}}{a_{s}^{2}}+r^{2}\right),

where

r_{s}:=\frac{2GM}{c^{2}},\qquad a_{s}:=\frac{L}{GMc},\qquad b_{s}:=\frac{cL}{E}.

The quantity
r_{s}
is the Schwarzschild radius,
L
is the specific angular momentum and
E
is the specific energy of a test particle.

As shown in
[
7
]
, the orbits are given in pedal coordinates as follows:

(36)

(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r-\rho_{0})(r-\rho_{1})(r-\rho_{2})}{r^{3}},

where

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{s}a^{2}_{s}b^{2}_{s}}{b_{s}^{2}-a_{s}^{2}}.

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\rho_{1}+\rho_{0}\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}_{s}b^{2}_{s}}{b_{s}^{2}-a^{2}_{s}}.

\displaystyle\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{s}b_{s}^{2}}{b_{s}^{2}-a_{s}^{2}}.

Choosing
\rho_{0}=r_{1}^{2}
,
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=-a/b
we get

b(2a-br_{1}^{2})\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r_{1}^{2}-r)(br+a)^{2}}{r^{3}},

almost a match with (
36
). To get precise equality we must perform on (
34
) the dual parallel transform
E^{\star}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=2b/(3a)
.

The two problems, i.e.
r_{0}=0
case of zero angular momentum free double linkage (
33
) and Kepler problem in general relativity (
36
) comes obviously from very different origins. And yet, there is a connection. This connection is almost certainly just a mathematical coincidence, but it is kind of appealing to think that a movement of a double linkage is somehow connected with the movement inside a Black Hole. (Remember
r_{0}=0
.)

4.3.
Dark Kepler problem

There is a general way how to get rid of the magnetic term
(r+r_{0}r_{1})^{2}
in (
31
) – namely using the transform
E^{\star}_{\alpha}M_{\frac{1}{2}}
.

First the square root transform:

(\ref{L0casepedgen})\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M_{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad a^{2}\left(r+r_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{(r-r_{0}^{2})(r_{1}^{2}-r)(br+a)^{2}}{r^{2}}.

Rewrite the result as follows:

a^{2}\left(1+\frac{r_{0}r_{1}}{r}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\left(1-\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{r}\right)\left(\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r}-1\right)\left(b+\frac{a}{r}\right)^{2},

we can easily apply a dual parallel transform:

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle E^{\star}_{-\frac{1}{r_{0}r_{1}}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\qquad a^{2}\left(\frac{r_{0}r_{1}}{r}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\left(1-\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{r}+\frac{r_{0}}{r_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r}-\frac{r_{1}}{r_{0}}-1\right)\left(b+\frac{a}{r}-\frac{a}{r_{0}r_{1}}\right)^{2}.

Tidying this up we get

(37)

a^{2}(r_{0}r_{1})^{5}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)=\frac{\left((r_{0}+r_{1})r-r_{1}r_{0}^{2}\right)\left(r_{1}^{2}r_{0}-(r_{0}+r_{1})r\right)\left((br_{0}r_{1}-a)r+ar_{0}r_{1}\right)^{2}}{r^{2}},

which is exactly the Dark Kepler problem (
27
). Or rather its special case with a double root. Thus we have discovered a particular solution.

4.4.
General construction

C
OROLLARY
3
.

Provided
r_{0}\not=0
, curves given by (
31
) are ellipses in GRFR with
r_{1},r_{0}
being the semi-axes of the original ellipse and with the ellipse of rotation given by

A=\sqrt{\left|\frac{2r_{1}}{r_{0}}\frac{{a-br_{0}r_{1}}}{br_{0}^{2}+a}\right|},\qquad B=\sqrt{\left|\frac{2r_{0}}{r_{1}}\frac{{a-br_{0}r_{1}}}{br_{1}^{2}+a}\right|}.

Proof.

Multiplying the equation (
31
) by some nonzero number
\lambda^{2}
and comparing with (
30
) – where we set
a=r_{1},b=r_{0}
we obtain three equations in three unknowns
(A,B,\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\lambda a

\displaystyle=r_{0}r_{1}\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}(AB)^{2}(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2})-D\right),

\displaystyle\lambda a

\displaystyle=(A^{2}-B^{2})(r_{0}r_{1})^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda b

\displaystyle=-D=r_{1}^{2}B^{2}-r_{0}^{2}A^{2}.

The reader can easily verify that the desired
A,B
solves this system together with

\lambda=\frac{2r_{0}r_{1}(r_{1}^{2}-r_{0}^{2})\left(a-br_{0}r_{1}\right)}{(a+r_{1}^{2}b)(a+r_{0}^{2}b)}.

The
\pm
sign in the first equation must be chosen so that a solution exists and it indicates clockwise (-) or anti-clockwise (+) orientation of the rotation.
∎

Note that even though solutions of
{\bf L}=0
double linkage are ellipses in GRFR, the reverse is not true. Not all ellipses in GRFR solve
{\bf L}=0
double linkage. The following connection between the original ellipse and the ellipse of rotation must hold:

\frac{r_{1}-r_{0}}{2}=\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r_{0}A^{2}}-\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{r_{1}B^{2}}.

5.
Conclusion

As we saw, the movement of a free double linkage is far from trivial, even if we restrict ourselves to the case of zero total angular momentum
{\bf L}=0
.

We have demonstrated that solutions can be linked to both the Kepler problem in general relativity and to the Dark Kepler problem. Furthermore, we have fully described the resulting orbits as ellipses in generalized rotation frame of reference.

The case
{\bf L}\not=0
for which we were able to provide algebraic closed solution in pedal coordinates (
5
) is more challenging to fully describe and further research is needed.
