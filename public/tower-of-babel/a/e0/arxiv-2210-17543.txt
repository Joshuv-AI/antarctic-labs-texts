Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a new simple approach to developing and establishing the convergence of splitting methods for a large class of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), including additive, diagonal and scalar noise types. The central idea is to view the splitting method as a replacement of the driving signal of an SDE, namely Brownian motion and time, with a piecewise linear path that yields a sequence of ODEs – which can be discretized to produce a numerical scheme. This new way of understanding splitting methods is inspired by, but does not use, rough path theory. We show that when the driving piecewise linear path matches certain iterated stochastic integrals of Brownian motion, then a high order splitting method can be obtained. We propose a general proof methodology for establishing the strong convergence of these approximations that is akin to the general framework of Milstein and Tretyakov. That is, once local error estimates are obtained for the splitting method, then a global rate of convergence follows. This approach can then be readily applied in future research on SDE splitting methods. By incorporating recently developed approximations for iterated integrals of Brownian motion into these piecewise linear paths, we propose several high order splitting methods for SDEs satisfying a certain commutativity condition. In our experiments, which include the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model and additive noise SDEs (noisy anharmonic oscillator, stochastic FitzHugh-Nagumo model, underdamped Langevin dynamics), the new splitting methods exhibit convergence rates of O ⁡ ( h 3 / 2 ) O(h^{3/2}) and outperform schemes previously proposed in the literature.

, is deterministic.

2.

\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}

scales with the step size
h
and the increment in the space component,
\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}
, has finite even moments scaling with
h
. Concretely, we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}=O(h),\hskip 7.11317pt\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\big[|(\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}})_{j}|^{2k}\big]=O(h^{k}),

for every
j\in\{1,\cdots,d\}
.

3.

When a CDE driven by
\gamma
is considered,
y_{0}^{\gamma}
and
\gamma
are assumed independent.

Remark 5
(Comment on Assumption
A1
).

We impose that
\gamma^{\tau}
is deterministic for convenience and, inspecting the proof, one may be able to lift this constraint. Moreover, we expect our methodology can accommodate for randomised algorithms (see
[
6
,
41
,
51
,
52
,
77
]
for examples of SDE solvers with a randomised time component).

We now present the main result of this section – a moment bound for the CDE,
which will be used to control remainder terms of the Taylor expansion discussed later. Following the approach of
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
, we obtain our main result, Theorem
6
.

Theorem 6
(Fourth moment bound for CDEs).

Let
\gamma
satisfy Assumption
A1
and let
y^{\gamma}
denote the solution to (
3
) with
y^{\gamma}_{0}\in L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{e})
. Suppose that
f
and
g
satisfy

\displaystyle\|f(y)\|\leq C(1+\|y\|),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|g(y)\|\leq C(1+\|y\|),

(13)

with
\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(16\hskip 0.7113ptC\int_{0}^{1}|d\gamma(u)|\big)\big]<\infty
.
Then there exists a positive constant
\widetilde{C}>0
, depending only on the path
\gamma
and growth constant
C
in (
13
), such that for
r\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\hskip 0.7113pt\|y_{r}^{\gamma}-y_{0}^{\gamma}\|^{4}\big]\leq\widetilde{C}\hskip 0.7113pth^{2}\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{0}^{\gamma}\|^{4}\big]\big)\hskip 0.7113pt.

(14)

Proof.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{e}\to\mathbb{R}^{e\times(d+1)}
have first column given by
f:\mathbb{R}^{e}\to\mathbb{R}^{e}
and the rest of the matrix given by
g:\mathbb{R}^{e}\to\mathbb{R}^{e\times d}
. Then the growth assumption (
13
) implies that
\|G(y)\|\leq C(1+\|y\|)
. Thus, by direct application of
[
34
, Theorem 3.7]
, we have

\displaystyle\|y^{\gamma}_{r}-y^{\gamma}_{0}\|\leq C(1+\|y^{\gamma}_{0}\|)\exp\left(2\hskip 0.7113ptC\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\right)\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\hskip 0.7113pt,

for
r\in[0,1]
. Since
y_{0}^{\gamma}
is independent of
\gamma
, we can estimate the fourth moment as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y^{\gamma}_{r}-y^{\gamma}_{0}\|^{4}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C^{4}\hskip 0.7113pt\mathbb{E}\left[\big(1+\|y^{\gamma}_{0}\|\big)^{4}\exp\left(8\hskip 0.7113ptC\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\right)\left(\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C^{4}\hskip 0.7113pt\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{0}^{\gamma}\|^{4}\big]\big)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(16\hskip 0.7113ptC\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\bigg)\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\bigg)^{8}\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip-2.84526pt,

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. We now assume for a contradiction that there exists
0\hskip 0.7113pt=\hskip 0.7113pts_{0}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pts_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pts_{M}\hskip 0.7113pt=\hskip 0.7113pt1
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\big\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|<\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\big\|\gamma_{s_{j}\hskip 0.7113pt,s_{j+1}}\big\|
. As each piece
\{\gamma(t):t\in[r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}]\}
is linear, adding points does not change the sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\big\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\big\|\gamma_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,t_{k+1}}\big\|,\hskip 7.11317pt\text{where}\hskip 7.11317pt\{t_{k}\}:=\{r_{j}\}\cup\{s_{i}\}.

We also note that each increment
\gamma_{s_{j}\hskip 0.7113pt,s_{j+1}}
can be expressed as a sum of increments from
\{\gamma_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,t_{k+1}}:s_{j}\leq t_{k}<s_{j+1}\}
. Therefore, by the triangle inequality, it follows that
\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\big\|\gamma_{s_{j}\hskip 0.7113pt,s_{j+1}}\big\|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\big\|\gamma_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,t_{k+1}}\big\|\implies\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\big\|\gamma_{s_{j}\hskip 0.7113pt,s_{j+1}}\big\|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\big\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|
.
From this contradiction, we have
\|\gamma\|_{1\text{-var},[0,1]}\hskip 0.7113pt=\int_{0}^{1}|d\gamma(u)|=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\big\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|
and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{r}|d\gamma(u)|\bigg)^{8}\hskip 0.7113pt\Bigg]\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}|d\gamma(u)|\bigg)^{8}\hskip 0.7113pt\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\big\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|\bigg)^{8}\hskip 0.7113pt\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq m^{7}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\gamma_{r_{i}\hskip 0.7113pt,r_{i+1}}\big\|^{8}\Big]=O(h^{4}),

using Jensen’s inequality and Assumption
A1
. Since
\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(16\hskip 0.7113ptC\int_{0}^{1}|d\gamma(u)|\big)\big]<\infty
, it now follows that there exists
\widetilde{C}>0
, not depending on
y_{0}^{\gamma}
, such that for
r\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y^{\gamma}_{r}-y^{\gamma}_{0}\|^{4}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}\hskip 0.7113pth^{2}\big(1+\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{0}^{\gamma}\|^{4}\big]\big).

3
Taylor expansions and error analysis

We consider the Taylor expansions of both the Stratonovich SDE (
1
) and the CDE (
3
) driven by a splitting path
\gamma
. By matching the lower order terms in the Taylor expansions and showing that the remainder terms are higher order, we can bound local errors for our splitting schemes. We then apply Milstein and Tretyakov’s framework for mean-square error analysis
[
63
]
to obtain a global strong convergence rate – which is our main result in Theorem
14
.

3.1
Stratonovich Taylor expansion

Letting
y
denote the solution to (
1
), we have the usual chain rule (see
[
50
, Theorem 5.6.1]
with
\mathcal{A}=\{\emptyset\}
) for
F\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{e})
,

\displaystyle F(y_{r})=F(y_{0})+\int_{0}^{r}F^{\prime}(y_{s})\circ dy_{s}\hskip 0.7113pt.

(15)

By expanding “
dy_{s}
” and iteratively applying (
15
), we obtain the Taylor expansion.

Proposition 7
(Stochastic Taylor expansion of the Stratonovich SDE (
1
)
[
5
, Proposition 1.1]
,
[
50
, Theorem 5.6.1]
).

Let
p\in\{\frac{k}{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{\lceil p-1\rceil}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e\times d})
. The Stratonovich Taylor expansion of (
1
), up to order
p
, is

\displaystyle y_{h}=y_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\hskip 0.7113pt\in\mathcal{A}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)\leq\,p\end{subarray}}V(\alpha)(y_{0})I_{\alpha}(1)+R_{p}(h,y_{0}),

(16)

where, we recall the definition of
\textrm{ord}(\alpha):=|\alpha|_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|_{\omega}\hskip 0.7113pt
after equation (
12
), and

\displaystyle R_{p}(h,y_{0}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\hskip 0.7113pt\in\mathcal{A}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)=p\end{subarray}}J_{\alpha}(V(\alpha))\hskip 0.7113pt,

(17)

with the vector field derivatives
V(\alpha):\mathbb{R}^{e}\to L((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes|\alpha|_{\omega}},\mathbb{R}^{e})
defined for multi-indices recursively by
V(\tau)(y):=f(y)
,
V(\omega)(y):=g(y)
and

\displaystyle V(l\beta)(y)=V(\beta)^{\prime}V(l)(y),

where
l\in\{\tau,\omega\}
and
l\beta:=(l,\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})
denotes concatenation.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{1/2}=O(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}})\hskip 0.7113pt.

(18)

3.2
Controlled Taylor expansion

We now present a CDE Taylor expansion. Just as with the Stratonovich SDE, we have the following chain rule for
F\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{e})
,

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636ptF(y^{\gamma}_{r})=F(y^{\gamma}_{0})+\int_{0}^{r}F^{\prime}(y^{\gamma}_{s})\,dy^{\gamma}_{s}\hskip 0.7113pt,\hskip 14.22636ptr\in[0,1]\hskip 0.7113pt,

(19)

where
y^{\gamma}
denotes the solution to the CDE (
3
). Again, just as in the SDE setting, by expanding “
\hskip 0.7113ptdy^{\gamma}_{s}\hskip 0.7113pt
” and iteratively applying (
19
), we can obtain a Taylor expansion.

Proposition 8
.

Let
p\in\{\frac{k}{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{\lceil p-1\rceil}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e\times d})
. Then the (controlled) Taylor expansion of the CDE (
3
) up to order
p
is given by

\displaystyle y^{\gamma}_{1}=y^{\gamma}_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\hskip 0.7113pt\in\mathcal{A}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)\leq\,p\end{subarray}}V(\alpha)(y^{\gamma}_{0})I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)+R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0}^{\gamma})\hskip 0.7113pt,

(20)

where, using the same notation for vector field derivatives as Proposition 3.1, we have

\displaystyle R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0}^{\gamma}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\hskip 0.7113pt\in\mathcal{A}\,,\\[2.0pt]
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)=p\end{subarray}}J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(V(\alpha))\hskip 0.7113pt.

(21)

Remark 9
.

We note the proof of Proposition
8
is essentially identical to that of the Stratonovich Taylor expansion (but with
t\mapsto(t,W_{t})
replaced by
r\mapsto(\gamma_{r}^{\tau},\gamma_{r}^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega})
).
Moreover, we consider Stratonovich SDEs precisely because we can apply the “same” chain rule and integration by parts formula as for standard Riemann-Stieltjes integrals.

We now show that the size of the terms in the CDE expansion (
20
) are dictated by the order of
\alpha
. This will allow us to obtain a bound on the remainder term
R^{\gamma}_{p}
which we will then use to establish the (strong) convergence rate of the CDE approximation.
To begin, we shall consider the iterated integrals
I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}\hskip 0.7113pt
, which do not depend on
f
or
g
.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose the path
\gamma
satisfies Assumption
A1
and let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)\|^{2}\big]=O\big(h^{2\hskip 0.7113pt\textrm{ord}(\alpha)}\big)\hskip 0.7113pt.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
is piecewise linear, we may split the iterated integral of
\gamma
into a finite sum of iterated integrals over intervals where the derivatives of
\gamma
are constant. This directly follows by the standard additive property of Riemann-Stieltjes integrals. We may convert these path integrals into regular (deterministic) integrals over these intervals as
d\gamma^{\tau}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
and
d\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
for
r\hskip-0.05692pt\in\hskip-0.05692pt[r_{i},r_{i+1}]
. By Assumption
A1
,
\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}=O(h)
is a deterministic constant and we therefore have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2|\alpha|_{\tau}}\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{\textrm{intervals}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\|\bigotimes_{j=1}^{{|\alpha|_{\omega}}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r^{j}_{i},r^{j}_{i+1}}\Big\|^{2}\bigg]\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle=Ch^{2|\alpha|_{\tau}}\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{\textrm{intervals}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{d}\dots\sum^{d}_{i_{|\alpha|_{\omega}}=1}\hskip-2.84526pt\big(\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r^{1}_{i},r^{1}_{i+1}}\big)_{i_{1}}^{2}\times\cdots\times\big(\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r^{|\alpha|_{\omega}}_{i},r^{|\alpha|_{\omega}}_{i+1}}\big)_{i_{|\alpha|_{\omega}}}^{2}\bigg]\hskip 0.7113pt,

where “intervals” refers to the finite collection of subdomains of the simplex with
d\gamma^{\tau}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
and
d\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
.
We can then estimate the
\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}
terms by iteratively applying Hölder’s inequality to the expectation and applying the assumption that
\mathbb{E}\big[|(\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}})_{j}|^{2k}\big]=O(h^{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)\|^{2}\big]\leq C_{d,m,|\alpha|}h^{2\hskip 0.7113pt\textrm{ord}(\alpha)}.

We now consider the
J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}
terms, which will follow in much the same way as for
I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
6
hold. Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
F:\mathbb{R}^{e}\to L\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes b},\mathbb{R}^{e}\big)
be a globally Lipschitz continuous map for some
b\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(F)\|^{2}\big]=O\big(h^{2\hskip 0.7113pt\textrm{ord}(\alpha)+1}\big)\hskip 0.7113pt.

Proof.

Just as in the previous proof, we may split the iterated integral of the piecewise linear path
\gamma
into a finite sum of iterated integrals over the intervals where both
d\gamma^{\tau}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\tau}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
and
d\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}(r)=\frac{1}{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r_{i},r_{i+1}}dr
for all
r\in[r_{i},r_{i+1}]
.
Applying Jensen’s and Hölder’s inequalities to the finite sum in
\mathbb{E}\big[\|J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(F)\|^{2}\big]
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(F)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2|\alpha|_{\tau}}\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{\textrm{intervals}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\,\bigg\|\underset{0\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{|\alpha|}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt1}{\idotsint}\hskip-1.42262pt\big(F(y^{\gamma}_{r_{|\alpha|}})-F(y^{\gamma}_{0})\big)\,dr_{|\alpha|}\cdots dr_{1}\bigg\|^{4}\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\times\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg\|\bigotimes_{j=1}^{|\alpha|_{\omega}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r^{j}_{i},r^{j}_{i+1}}\bigg\|^{4}\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By applying Jensen’s inequality to the uniform distribution on
[s,t]
, we have that
\big\|\int_{s}^{t}z_{r}\hskip 0.7113ptdr\big\|^{4}\leq(t-s)^{3}\int_{s}^{t}\|z_{r}\|^{4}\,dr
for any continuous integrable process
z_{r}\hskip 0.7113pt
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(F)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}h^{2|\alpha|_{\tau}}\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{\textrm{intervals}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\,\,\underset{0\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{|\alpha|}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt1}{\idotsint}\big\|F(y^{\gamma}_{r_{|\alpha|}})-F(y^{\gamma}_{0})\big\|^{4}\hskip 0.7113ptdr_{|\alpha|}\cdots dr_{1}\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\times\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg\|\bigotimes_{j=1}^{|\alpha|_{\omega}}\gamma^{\hskip 0.7113pt\omega}_{r^{j}_{i},r^{j}_{i+1}}\bigg\|^{4}\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(22)

By repeatedly applying Hölder’s inequality, we can estimate the term (
22
) as
O(h^{|\alpha|_{\omega}})
.
Since
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)=|\alpha|_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|_{\omega}
, it follows from the global Lipschitz continuity of
F
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|J^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(F)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\|F\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}\text{-}1}h^{2\hskip 0.7113pt\textrm{ord}(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\sum_{\textrm{intervals}}\Bigg(\,\,\underset{0\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{|\alpha|}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113ptr_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.7113pt1}{\idotsint}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|y^{\gamma}_{r_{|\alpha|}}-y^{\gamma}_{0}\big\|^{4}\Big]\hskip 0.7113ptdr_{|\alpha|}\cdots dr_{1}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By Theorem
6
, we have
\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|y^{\gamma}_{r_{|\alpha|}}-y^{\gamma}_{0}\big\|^{4}\big]=O(h^{2})
and thus the result follows.

3.3
Main result

Now that we have Taylor expansions for both the CDE and the Stratonovich SDE, along with control over the size of the remainder terms in each,
we can establish the strong convergence properties of path-based splitting schemes.
We first obtain local strong and weak error estimates using a direct application of Lemmas
10
and
11
before applying the framework of Milstein and Tretyakov
[
63
]
, which allows us to prove a global strong convergence rate for the approximating CDE.

Theorem 12
(Local error estimates).

Suppose that the path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
satisfies Assumption
A1
and for a fixed
p\in\{\frac{k}{2}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\hskip 0.7113pt
, let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{\lceil p-1\rceil}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{e},\mathbb{R}^{e\times d})
. Suppose also that the assumptions of Theorem
6
hold and the integrals
I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)
and
I_{\alpha}(1)
agree almost surely for
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
with
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)\leq p-\frac{1}{2}
and in expectation for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
with
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)=p
. Let
Y_{1}
denote an approximation (e.g. using an ODE solver) of the CDE solution
\{y^{\gamma}_{r}\}_{r\in[0,1]}
driven by
\gamma
, such that
y_{0}^{\gamma}=y_{0}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y^{\gamma}_{1}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(h^{p}),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\big\|\mathbb{E}[\hskip 0.7113pty^{\gamma}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\big\|=O\big(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}}\big),

(23)

where
y=\{y_{t}\}_{t\in[0,h]}
is the solution of the SDE (
1
) and
h>0
is the step size. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(h^{p}),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\big\|\mathbb{E}[y_{h}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\big\|=O\big(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}}\big).

Remark 13
.

In the above,
Y_{1}
could represent the approximation of
y^{\gamma}_{1}
obtained by applying one step of a standard Runge-Kutta method along each linear piece of
\gamma
. This Runge-Kutta method should be of sufficiently high order so that (
23
) can hold.

Proof.

We start by proving the local strong error. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-y^{\gamma}_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbb{E}\big[\|y^{\gamma}_{1}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-y^{\gamma}_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(h^{p}),

as the second term is the difference between the CDE solution and its approximation.

Recall the remainder terms
R_{p}(h,y_{0})
and
R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})
in Propositions
7
and
8
. Then, by another two applications of the triangle inequality, it directly follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|(y_{h}-R_{p}(h,y_{0}))-(y^{\gamma}_{1}-R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0}))\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbb{E}\big[\|R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(h^{p}),

where the first term is simply the difference in the Taylor expansions, up to order
p
, of the SDE solution
y_{h}
and the CDE solution
y_{1}^{\gamma}
. Therefore, by the assumption that all integrals of the form
I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)
are matched almost surely for
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)\leq p-\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113pt
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mathbb{E}\big[\|R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}+O(h^{p}).

By Proposition
7
, the SDE remainder term will satisfy
\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}})
.
On the other hand,
R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})
is given by (
21
) and therefore, by Lemma
11
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O\big(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}}\big).

This gives the desired result for the local strong error, that
\mathbb{E}\big[\|y_{h}-Y_{1}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(h^{p})
.

We now turn our attention to the local weak error. Using the triangle inequality and the same Taylor expansions as in the proof of local strong error, it follows that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}[y_{h}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[y_{h}-R_{p}(h,y_{0})\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[y^{\gamma}_{1}-R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})\big]\big\|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\big\|\mathbb{E}[y^{\gamma}_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\big\|+\big\|\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(h,y_{0})]\big\|+\big\|\mathbb{E}[R^{\gamma}_{p}(h,y_{0})]\big\|.

From our assumption, the
I^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(1)
terms in the SDE and CDE Taylor expansions are matched in expectation for
\textrm{ord}(\alpha)\leq p
and, therefore, the first term disappears.
Moreover, we assume
\|\mathbb{E}[y_{1}^{\gamma}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\|=O\big(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}}\big)
and, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(h,y_{0})]\|\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}},\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581pt\|\mathbb{E}[R_{p}^{\gamma}(h,y_{0})]\|\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|R_{p}^{\gamma}(h,y_{0})\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip 0.7113pt.

Since the above terms were previously shown to be
O(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}})
, the result follows.

Using the local estimates given by Theorem
12
and following the mean-square analysis of Milstein and Tretyakov

Theorem 14
(Global strong error estimate).

Given a fixed number of steps
N
, we define a numerical solution

for the SDE (

[0,T]
as follows,

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}:=\big(\text{Solution at }r=1\text{ of CDE (\ref{eq:intro_CDE}) driven by }\gamma_{k}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\hskip 0.7113pt\big)\big(Y_{k}\big)+E_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,

where
Y_{0}:=y_{0}
and, for a fixed

uniformly satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|E_{k}\|^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(h^{p}),\hskip 28.45274pt\big\|\mathbb{E}[E_{k}]\big\|=O\big(h^{p+\frac{1}{2}}\big),

and each path

is expressible as
\gamma_{k}=\varphi\big(\big\{(t,W_{t}):t\in\big[\frac{kT}{N},\frac{(k+1)T}{N}\big]\big\}\big)

uniformly satisfy Assumption

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\hskip 0.7113pt\|y_{kh}-Y_{k}\|^{2}\big]^{1/2}=O\big(h^{p-\frac{1}{2}}\big).

Remark 15
.

Here, the “CDE errors”

represent the difference between the exact solution of the CDE and the numerical approximation obtained by discretizing each ODE in the splitting. Of course, if we can solve the ODEs exactly, then

Remark 16
(Weak error estimates).

Whilst it is not the focus of this paper, global weak error estimates can also be established for path-based splitting methods.
Moreover, to achieve high order weak convergence, splitting paths need only match moments of Brownian iterated integrals – just as in “Cubature on Wiener Space”
[

We refer the reader to the PhD thesis of the final author
[

for details.

Remark 17
(Infinite time horizon).

The convergence results in this paper are established over a finite time horizon

For instance, if the SDE is ergodic with an exponential contraction property then contributions of local errors to the global error are reduced (exponentially in time). An extension of the classical Milstein-Tretyakov mean-square error analysis

to the infinite time horizon case for such contractive SDEs is given by
[

. See also
[

\displaystyle I_{ij}

\displaystyle I_{ijk}

Remark 19

Just as in Theorem

5
Examples

We demonstrate the proposed splitting methods on several SDEs.
In each example, we consider just one high order splitting, which is chosen as follows:

•

For the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) model, we will consider the “linear” high order Strang splitting due to its provable weak approximation properties (Theorem
34
).

•

For general additive-noise SDEs, we will consider the “Shifted ODE” splittings
because they only require a single non-trivial ODE to be discretized in each step.
(and specifically (
33
) for a scalar oscillator as it uses optimal integral estimators).

•

For the stochastic FitzHugh-Nagumo (FHN) model, we consider the “non-linear” high order Strang splitting as it uses optimal integral estimators and produces “drift ODEs” which can be resolved using the Strang splitting technique from
[
8
]
.

•

For underdamped Langevin dynamics, which has special structure, we apply the Shifted ODE splitting (
35
) since it achieves third order strong convergence
[
32
]
.

For the last three examples, the “non-diffusion” ODEs that come from the SDE splitting will not admit a closed-formed solution and thus must be further discretized.
We will show that such ODEs can be resolved by Runge-Kutta or splitting methods.

Throughout, we shall compare methods using the following strong error estimator:

Definition 33
(Strong error estimator for SDEs).

For
N\geq 1
, let
Y_{N}
denote a numerical solution to the SDE (
1
) computed at time
T
with a fixed step size
h=\frac{T}{N}\hskip 0.7113pt
.
Then we define the following estimator for quantifying the strong convergence of
Y_{N}
:

\displaystyle S_{N}:=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(Y_{N}-Y_{T}^{\text{fine}}\big)^{2}\Big]},

(36)

where
Y_{T}^{\text{fine}}
denotes a numerical solution to (
1
) computed with a finer step size,
h^{\text{fine}}\leq\frac{1}{10}\hskip 0.7113pth
, but using the same Brownian motion (so that
Y_{N}
and
Y_{T}^{\text{fine}}
are close).
In our examples, the expectation in (
36
) will be estimated by standard Monte Carlo.

All the experiments were conducted on a laptop in either C++, Python or R. Associated code is available at
github.com/james-m-foster/high-order-splitting

and
github.com/james-m-foster/high-order-langevin
for the Langevin dynamics example.

5.1
Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model

The Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (or CIR) model is a popular one-factor short rate model used
in mathematical finance for modelling interest rates
[
18
]
and stochastic volatility
[
40
]
. It is given by the following Itô SDE:

\displaystyle dy_{t}=a(b-y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{y_{t}}\,dW_{t}\hskip 0.7113pt,

(37)

where the parameters
a,b,\sigma\geq 0
describe the mean reversion speed/level and volatility.
Over the years, a variety of numerical methods have been proposed for the CIR model and we refer the reader to some of these approaches
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
19
,
23
,
27
,
39
,
45
,
62
,
71
]
.
As we propose splitting methods, we first rewrite the SDE (
37
) in Stratonovich form:

\displaystyle dy_{t}=a(\hskip 0.7113pt\widetilde{b}-y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{y_{t}}\circ dW_{t}\hskip 0.7113pt.

where
\widetilde{b}:=b-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4a}\hskip 0.7113pt
. To ensure non-negativity of
\widetilde{b}
and our scheme, we assume
\sigma^{2}\leq 4ab
.
By driving (
37
) with the piecewise linear path (
28
), we obtain the splitting method:

\displaystyle Y_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=e^{-\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}ah}Y_{k}+\widetilde{b}\big(1-e^{-\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}ah}\big),

\displaystyle Y_{k}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\sqrt{Y_{k}^{(1)}}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}W_{k}+\sqrt{3}H_{k}\Big)\bigg)^{2},

\displaystyle Y_{k}^{(3)}

\displaystyle:=e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}ah}Y_{k}^{(2)}+\widetilde{b}\big(1-e^{-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}ah}\big),

\displaystyle Y_{k}^{(4)}

\displaystyle:=\bigg(\sqrt{Y_{k}^{(3)}}+\frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}W_{k}-\sqrt{3}H_{k}\Big)\bigg)^{2},

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=e^{-\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}ah}Y_{k}^{(4)}+\widetilde{b}\big(1-e^{-\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}ah}\big),

(38)

since for the CIR model, the drift and diffusion ODEs admit closed-form solutions.
The resulting numerical method (
5.1
) is then straightforward to implement and we might expect each step to be roughly twice as expensive as previous methodologies (since the above method requires generating two Gaussian random variables per step).
We also note that the method (
5.1
), and each of its stages, preserves non-negativity.
In addition, one can compute the mean and variance of both the SDE (
37
) and (
5.1
).

Theorem 34
.

The numerical solution given by (
5.1
) has the following moments:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}]

\displaystyle=e^{-ah}Y_{k}+b\big(1-e^{-ah}\big)+R_{k}^{E}\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(Y_{k+1}|Y_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a}\big(e^{-ah}-e^{-2ah}\big)Y_{k}+\frac{b\sigma^{2}}{2a}\big(1-e^{-ah}\big)^{2}+R_{k}^{V}\hskip 0.7113pt,

where the remainder terms
R_{k}^{E}
and
R_{k}^{V}
can be estimated as
O(h^{5})
in an
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
sense.

Proof.

See the proof of Theorem
51
in the appendix.

Remark 35
.

The associated mean and variance of the CIR model (
37
) are also known analytically
[
18
]
and given by the same formulae, but without the
O(h^{5})
terms.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y_{t_{k+1}}|y_{t_{k}}]

\displaystyle=e^{-ah}y_{t_{k}}+b\big(1-e^{-ah}\big)\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(y_{t_{k+1}}|y_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{a}\big(e^{-ah}-e^{-2ah}\big)y_{t_{k}}+\frac{b\sigma^{2}}{2a}\big(1-e^{-ah}\big)^{2}\hskip 0.7113pt.

Therefore, even though the primary focus of the paper is on strong approximation, we see that the splitting (
5.1
) is also a high order weak approximation of the CIR model.
Furthermore, due to the non-Lipschitz diffusion vector field in the CIR model, we can not directly apply our main result to establish the strong convergence of our method.
We thus leave the error analysis of path-based splitting methods in the “non-smooth” setting as future work. Similar to
[
8
]
, we expect results can be obtained in cases where the drift has polynomial growth and satisfies a (global) one-sided Lipschitz condition.

We will now present our experiment, comparing the strong convergence of the splitting (
5.1
) against several well-known methods. We use the following parameters:

\displaystyle a=1,\hskip 14.22636ptb=1,\hskip 14.22636pt\sigma=1,\hskip 14.22636pty_{0}=1,\hskip 14.22636ptT=1.

5.2
Additive noise SDEs, such as a stochastic anharmonic oscillator

We first consider an arbitrary SDE with additive noise (that is,
g(\cdot)
is a fixed matrix)

\displaystyle dy_{t}=f(y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptdW_{t}\hskip 0.7113pt,

(39)

where
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{e\times d}
. As (
39
) has additive noise, it is in both Itô and Stratonovich form.
Since each piece in
\gamma
yields an ODE, we would like to minimize the number of pieces.
Moreover, it was shown in
[
27
, p97 and Appendix A]
that two-piece paths are unable to match the various iterated integrals of Brownian motion required in Theorem
18
.
Thus, in general, we propose driving (
39
) by a piecewise linear path with three pieces.
So unless the SDE has special structure, such as underdamped Langevin dynamics, we recommend either the paths (
32
) or (
33
) to achieve 3/2 order strong convergence.
In either case, we obtain a splitting method for the SDE (
39
) with the following form:

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}:=\exp\big(f(\cdot)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\big)\big(Y_{k}+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{3}\hskip 0.7113pt,

(40)

where the vectors
C_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,C_{2}\hskip 0.7113pt,C_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
correspond to the increments of the driving path.

In this section, we will consider the general setting where the ODE governed by
f(\cdot)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}
in (
40
) does not admit a closed-form solution and must be discretized.
Expanding terms in the Taylor expansion of this ODE, we see that (
40
) is equal to

\displaystyle\bigg(\hskip-0.7113ptI_{d}(\cdot)+f(\cdot)h+\sigma C_{2}+\frac{1}{2}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}(\cdot)\big(f(\cdot)h+\sigma C_{2}\big)h+\frac{1}{6}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}(\cdot)\big(\sigma C_{2}\big)^{\hskip-0.28453pt\otimes 2}h\bigg)\hskip-0.7113pt\big(Y_{k}+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{3}

\displaystyle=\hskip-0.56905ptY_{k}\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.42677pt\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.56905pt\bigg(\hskip-0.56905ptf\big(Y_{k}\big)\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.42677ptf^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.42677pt\frac{1}{2}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)^{\hskip-0.42677pt\otimes 2}\hskip-0.7113pt\bigg)\hskip-0.7113pth\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.42677pt\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\hskip-0.42677pt+\hskip-0.42677pt\frac{1}{2}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)f\big(Y_{k}\big)h^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{1}{2}\Big(f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}+f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\big(\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\big)\otimes\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)\big)\Big)h+\frac{1}{6}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\big(\sigma C_{2}\big)^{\hskip-0.28453pt\otimes 2}h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{3}+\cdots

Rearranging these terms then gives the following expansion for the splitting method,

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}\approx Y_{k}

\displaystyle+f\big(Y_{k}\big)h+\sigma\big(C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3}\big)+\frac{1}{2}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\bigg(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\bigg)h

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptf^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)f\big(Y_{k}\big)h^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptf^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}\big(Y_{k}\big)\bigg(\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)^{2}+\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\big)\otimes\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\big)+\frac{1}{3}\big(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\big)^{2}\bigg)h,

where the final line follows as the second derivative
f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}(Y_{k})
is symmetric and bilinear.

Since we intend to discretize the ODE map
x\mapsto\exp\big(f(\cdot)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\big)x
, we would like the Taylor expansion of the numerical ODE solver to coincide with the above.
For example, if we apply an explicit two-stage second order Runge-Kutta method (determined by a parameter
\alpha
), then this will result in the following Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle x^{\text{RK}}

\displaystyle=x+\Big(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\Big)\big(f(x)h+\sigma C_{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\Big(f\big(x+\alpha\big(f(x)h+\sigma C_{2}\big)\big)h+\sigma C_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=x+f(x)h+\sigma C_{2}+\frac{1}{2}f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}(x)\big(f(x)h+\sigma C_{2}\big)h+\frac{1}{4}\alpha\hskip 0.7113ptf^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime\prime}(x)(\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2})^{\otimes 2}h+\cdots.

Thus, the only explicit two-stage Runge-Kutta method matching the Taylor expansion of the splitting method (
40
) is Ralston’s method
[
74
]
(which corresponds to
\alpha=\frac{2}{3}
).
Hence, we propose the following numerical method for the additive noise SDE (
39
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{k}^{\text{SR}}

\displaystyle:=Y_{k}^{\text{SR}}+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{k+\frac{2}{3}}^{\text{SR}}

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{Y}_{k}^{\text{SR}}+\frac{2}{3}\Big(f\big(\widetilde{Y}_{k}^{\text{SR}}\big)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}\Big)\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{SR}}

\displaystyle:=Y_{k}^{\text{SR}}+\frac{1}{4}f\big(\widetilde{Y}_{k}^{\text{SR}}\big)h+\frac{3}{4}f\big(\widetilde{Y}_{k+\frac{2}{3}}^{\text{SR}}\big)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptW_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,

(41)

where
C_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the first two increments of the driving piecewise linear path
\gamma
.
Note that the final line is obtained by combining the second stage of Ralston’s method with the terms coming from the last vertical piece of
\gamma
(assuming
W_{k}=C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3}
).

Remark 36
.

We can extend this method to time-varying diffusions with
\sigma\equiv\sigma(t)
. For example, the terms
\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}
and
\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptW_{k}
may be replaced by
\sigma(t_{k})\hskip 0.7113ptC_{1}
,
\sigma(t_{k})\hskip 0.7113ptC_{2}
and

\displaystyle\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\sigma(r)\,dW_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma(t_{k})+\sigma(t_{k+1})\big)W_{k}+\big(\sigma(t_{k+1})-\sigma(t_{k})\big)H_{k}+O\big(h^{2.5}\big)\hskip 0.7113pt.

(42)

Although we shall not formally present an error analysis, it is clear from the above Taylor expansions that the method (
5.2
) achieves a 3/2 strong order convergence rate.
Similarly, we consider the case where the splitting path (
34
) is applied to the additive noise SDE (
39
) and the resulting ODE is discretized using Euler’s method. This gives

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{SE}}:=Y_{k}^{\text{SE}}+f\Big(Y_{k}^{\text{SE}}+\sigma\Big(\frac{1}{2}W_{k}+H_{k}\Big)\Big)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptW_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt.

(43)

We refer to the numerical method (
43
) as the “Shifted Euler” discretization of (
39
). Whilst the Shifted Euler method will have the same first order of convergence as the Euler-Maruyama method, we expect it to be more accurate (for sufficiently small
h
).
This is due to the shifted Euler method (
43
) having the following Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{SE}}=Y_{k}^{\text{SE}}+f\big(Y_{k}^{\text{SE}}\big)h+\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptW_{k}+f^{\hskip 0.7113pt\prime}\big(Y_{k}^{\text{SE}}\big)\bigg(\sigma\underbrace{\bigg(\frac{1}{2}W_{k}+H_{k}\bigg)h}_{=\,\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}W_{t_{k},r}\,dr}\,\bigg)+O(h^{2}).

Thus, the Shifted Euler scheme has a local error of
O(h^{2})
whereas the Euler-Maruyama method produces a local error of
O(h^{\frac{3}{2}})
, which is due to the integral highlighted above.

Remark 37
.

The shifted Euler method can be extended to time-varying diffusion coefficients by replacing the terms
\,\sigma\hskip 0.7113ptW_{k}\,
and
\,\sigma\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}W_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,r}\,dr\,
with
\,\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\sigma(r)\hskip 0.7113ptdW_{r}\,
and
\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\int_{t_{k}}^{s}\sigma(r)\,dW_{r}\,ds=\sigma(t_{k})\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}W_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,r}\,dr+\frac{1}{6}\big(\sigma(t_{k+1})-\sigma(t_{k})\big)W_{k}\hskip 0.7113pth^{2}+O(h^{\frac{7}{2}})
.

Moreover, in applications where evaluating
f
is significantly faster than generating Gaussian random vectors, we can remove the space-time Lévy area
H_{k}
from (
43
).
The resulting “increment-only” Shifted Euler method will then have the same cost per step as the traditional Euler-Maruyama method. However, for sufficiently small
h
, it will still be more accurate as it gives
\frac{1}{2}hW_{k}\approx\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}W_{t_{k}\hskip 0.7113pt,r}\,dr
in its Taylor expansion.

6
Conclusion and future work

We have presented a new simple framework for developing and analysing splitting methods for stochastic differential equations.
The key idea is to replace the system’s Brownian motion with a piecewise linear path,
which results in a path-based splitting method that is straightforward to analyse using its “controlled” Taylor expansion (a well-known technique within rough path theory).
Moreover, for SDEs that satisfy a commutativity condition, this led to several high order splitting methods which displayed state-of-the-art convergence in experiments.
As part of this investigation, we also detailed how recently developed estimators for iterated integrals of Brownian motion can be directly incorporated into such methods.
Since these estimators were simply obtained as the expectation of iterated integrals conditional on the generatable random variables, they are optimal in an
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
sense.
The technical details underlying the integral estimators are presented in Appendix
B
.

Furthermore, the results from this paper lead to several topics of future research:
