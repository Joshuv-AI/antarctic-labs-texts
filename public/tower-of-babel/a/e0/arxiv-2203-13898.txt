The Spectrum of an Almost Maximally Open Quantized Cat Map
Yonah Borns-Weil

Abstract
. We consider eigenvalues of a quantized cat map (i.e. hyperbolic symplectic integer matrix), cut off in phase space to include a fixed point as its only periodic orbit on the torus. We prove a simple formula for the eigenvalues on both the quantized real line and the quantized torus in the semiclassical limit as h → 0 h\to 0 . We then consider the case with no fixed points, and prove a superpolynomial decay bound on the eigenvalues. The results are illustrated with numerical calculations.



1.
Introduction

The study of the classical-quantum correspondence dynamics consists of comparing quantum evolution to classical dynamical systems. One example of this correspondence is the relationship between continuous-time classical dynamics and the theory ofscattering resonances – see the textbook by Dyatlov and Zworski
[
8
]
for an introduction. Recently, there has been interest in the discrete-time analogue given by
open quantum maps
. These maps correspond to quantizations of symplectomorphisms on a given symplectic manifold, which are “opened” by designating some of the points to “escape to infinity”. Of particular interest are maps corresponding to
hyperbolic
symplectomorphisms, where one may study the relationship between quantum and classical dynamics near a hyperbolic trapped set. See the paper by Nonnenmacher
[
15
]
for a general overview of the literature.

Perhaps the most fundamental example in classical hyperbolic dynamics is the generalized
Arnol’d cat map
given by a matrix in
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, which act on both
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and have a single hyperbolic fixed point at the origin. The natural quantum-dynamical analogue is a class of metaplectic operators, known as
quantized cat maps
. On the quantized torus, such maps were treated by Bouzouina and De Biévre
[
2
]
, who proved a version of quantum ergodicity in this setting (see also Dyatlov–Jézéquel
[
7
]
and Faure–Nonnenmacher–De Biévre
[
9
]
). These quantum cat maps can then be “opened” as part of the general framework of open quantum maps, which corresponds to sending some points “to infinity” to mimic scattering. Once adjusted in this manner, such maps enjoy many similar results to continuous scattering problems, and are a subject of current research. In the physics literature, open quantum maps are used as a toy computational model for quantum chaos, see Saraceno–Vallejos
[
19
]
and Nonnenmacher–Zworski
[
18
]
and references given there. In addition, in Nonnenmacher, Sjöstrand, and Zworski
[
16
]
reduce the study of some continuous-time systems that op open quantum maps. Schenck
[
20
]
considers the similar case of partially open quantum maps, which gives a discrete-time analogue to a damped wave equation.

In this paper, we consider a distinct but related problem, of determining the quantum behavior of a cat map very near its hyperbolic fixed point. Specifically, suppose
M
is a cat map and
\widehat{M}
is its quantization, acting either on the quantized real line or the quantized torus. We may consider
\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{M}
or
\Op_{N}\chi\widehat{M}_{N}
respectively, where
\chi
is a bump function equal to
1
near the origin and
\Op
refers to semiclassical quantization, see Sections
2.1
and
3.1
. This has the effect of isolating the behavior near the hyperbolic fixed point, and producing a compact operator whose spectrum can be studied.

The case of such an “almost maximally open” map has been studied before in the case of Schrödinger operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The first results in the complicated setting of obstacle problems were provided by Bardos–Guillot–Ralston
[
1
]
, and the problem was settled by Ikawa
[
13
]
with improvements by Gerard
[
11
]
. The semiclassical case was then handled by Gerard–Sjöstrand
[
12
]
and Sjöstrand
[
21
]
, see also Louati and Rouleux
[
14
]
. Surprisingly, to the author’s knowledge, the analogous situation for an open quantum map has never been studied before. We shall show that the situations on the torus and on the real line are similar, which matches our intuition that everything away from a small local region had “escaped”. When localized by a small enough microlocal cutoff operator and for small enough
h
, the spectrum is discrete away from the origin, and the eigenvalues are given up to
O(h^{\infty})
error by
\lambda^{-\frac{2k+1}{2}}
, where
\lambda
is the positive eigenvalue of the underlying symplectic transformation.

In addition, we consider the “maximally open” case of
\Op_{N}\nu\widehat{M}_{N}
, where
\nu
is a bump function away localized in a small annulus about the hyperbolic fixed point. This map corresponds to a discrete-time analogue of the “nontrapping estimates” seen for scattering resonances in Section 6.4 of
[
8
]
. In this case, the eigenvalues decay to zero, and we shall show in Section
4
that they in fact decay to zero like
O(h^{\infty})
.

1.1.
The Classical Cat Map

The original Arnol’d cat map was defined as the matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and more interestingly also acting on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. As
M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, one sees that
M
is invertible and measure-preserving with respect to the Lebesgue measure. When considered as a measure-preserving map on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
M
is mixing, which in particular implies that it is ergodic. In addition,
M
is topologically transitive and has a dense set of periodic points, which gives it to be
chaotic
as defined by e.g. Teschl
[
23
]
.

These above properties hold for any
M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
with eigenvalues
\lambda,\frac{1}{\lambda}\not\in S^{1}
, so we call such matrices
generalized cat maps
, or for convenience just
cat maps
. We discuss the set of fixed and periodic points in further detail. By an argument using the pigeonhole principle, we may see that points on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
are periodic points of
M
if and only if they have rational coordinates. We now provide an elementary proposition about periodic orbits that will motivate our main result.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
M=Q^{-1}DQ
be a generalized cat map with
D
diagonal and
\det Q=1
. Then the only periodic orbit of
M
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
contained in
B_{\frac{1}{4\lambda\|Q\|^{2}}}(0)
is the fixed point at
0
.

Proof.

We begin by proving this for symmetric
M
, which is equivalent to
\|Q\|=1
. Write
v\in B_{\frac{1}{4\lambda}}(0)
as
v=a_{1}v_{1}+a_{2}v_{2}
, where
v_{1},v_{2}
are the normalized eigenvectors of
M
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
corresponding to
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
, so

M^{n}v=\lambda^{n}a_{1}v_{1}+\lambda^{-n}a_{2}v_{2}.

(1.1)

If
v
is a periodic point, we can easily see that if one of
a_{1}
is
0
then
a_{2}
must also be, so without loss of generality assume
a_{1},a_{2}\neq 0
, and note that
|a_{1}|,|a_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{4\lambda}
. Let
n=\left\lfloor\log_{\lambda}\frac{1}{4|a_{1}|}\right\rfloor
, so

\frac{1}{4\lambda}<\lambda^{n}|a_{1}|\leq\frac{1}{4}

(1.2)

and also

|\lambda^{-n}a_{2}|\leq|a_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{4\lambda}.

(1.3)

Combining (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) with (
1.1
) gives that
\frac{1}{4\lambda}<\|M^{n}v\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, which proves the result for this case.

In the general case, we let
M=Q^{-1}DQ
. Let
v\in Q^{-1}B_{\varepsilon}(0)
. Then after scaling, the proof for the symmetric case says that there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D^{n}Qv\in B_{2\lambda\varepsilon}(0)\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(0)
, or equivalently that
M^{n}v\in Q^{-1}B_{2\lambda\varepsilon}(0)\setminus Q^{-1}B_{\varepsilon}(0)
. Then this gives that any point int
B_{\frac{\varepsilon}{\|Q\|}}(0)
will be taken to
Q^{-1}B_{2\lambda\varepsilon}(0)\setminus Q^{-1}B_{\varepsilon}(0)
which is itself contained in
B_{2\lambda\varepsilon\|Q\|}(0)
. Letting
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4\lambda\|Q\|}
gives the desired result.
∎

1.2.
Overview of Theorems and Proofs

We work in the setting of operators on a quantum “state space,” which is a Hilbert space on which “quantized” observables act. In the case of
T^{*}\mathbb{R}
, the state space is simply
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, while in the case of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, it is a space
H_{N}
which is
N
-dimensional and spanned by the Dirac comb distributions
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}}
. Observables are given by semiclassical pseudodifferential operators
\Op_{h}\chi
(on the real line) and
\Op_{N}\chi
(on the torus). From that framework we can then define metaplectic operators
\widehat{M}
,
\widehat{M_{N}}
that “quantize” linear symplectic transformations, which on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
are precisely the cat maps discussed in Section
1.1
.

Our result on the real line is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\widehat{M}
be a metaplectic operator with eigenvalues
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
of
M
where
\lambda>1
, and let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
equal to
1
near the origin. Then for all
\delta>0
there is an
h_{0}>0
such that if
0<h<h_{0},

\Spec(\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{M})\cap\left\{|z|\geq\delta\right\}=\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots\}\cap\left\{|z|\geq\delta\right\},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}=\lambda^{\frac{2k+1}{2}}+O(h^{\infty}).

On the quantized torus, the analogous result is:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\widehat{M}_{N}
be a metaplectic operator on
H_{N}
with eigenvalues
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
of
M
where
\lambda>1
, and let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
equal to
1
near the origin with support on a small enough neighborhood of
0
. Then for all
\delta>0
, there is an
h_{0}>0
such that if
0<h=\frac{1}{2\pi N}<h_{0}
,

\Spec(\Op_{N}\chi\widehat{M}_{N})\cap\left\{|z|\geq\delta\right\}=\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots\}\cap\left\{|z|\geq\delta\right\},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}=\lambda^{\frac{2k+1}{2}}+O(h^{\infty}).

For exact definitions of the terms in Theorems
1
and
2
, see Sections
2.1
and
3.1
respectively.

We give an overview of the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
. There is a strong similarity to the methods used in
[
11
]
, and Theorem
1
could be proven using his methods, though we elect to use different underlying function spaces. To find the spectrum of
\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{M}
, it is enough to consider the special case where
M=F
is a diagonal matrix. Our strategy will be to build a Grushin problem acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K}
given by

\mathcal{P}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}&\tilde{R}_{-}\\
\tilde{R}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Provided
\mathcal{P}(z)
is invertible with inverse

\mathcal{E}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}E(z)&\tilde{E}_{+}(z)\\
\tilde{E}_{-}(z)&E{-+}(z)\end{pmatrix},

then
I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}
is invertible if and only if
E_{-+}
is, reducing the study of the spectrum to the invertibility of the matrix
E_{-+}
. But
E_{-+}
is (up to
O(h^{\infty})
errors) conjugate to a simple diagonal matrix, which gives the desired spectrum. To construct the operators
\tilde{R}_{-},\tilde{R}_{+}
, we use the now standard approach as outlined by Sjöstrand and Zworski in Section 3.4 of
[
22
]
. The main difficulty is showing well-posedness; in other words, showing that
\mathcal{P}(z)
is invertible. This is achieved through careful bounding of
\chi(x,hD)\hat{F}
on the range of
I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}
with respect to an unusual norm, which is different from but fulfills an analogous role to the one seen in
[
11
]
. The necessary inequalities are collected and proven in Section
2.2
.

To solve the corresponding problem on the quantized torus, we make use of “sampling operators”
S_{\psi}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H_{N}
defined in Section
3.2
. The power of such operators comes from their interplay with others; in particular,
S_{\psi}
and
S_{\psi}S_{\psi}^{*}
act as pseudodifferential operators on
H_{N}
as a subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, and hence obey the rules of pseudodifferential calculus. They can then be microlocally cut off to form “spectral operators”
T:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H_{N}
and
T:H_{N}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which map our Grushin problem on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to one on
H_{N}
. The study of the spectrum can then be carried out exactly as it was for Theorem
1
.

For completeness, we also provide an analysis of the “maximally open” or “nontrapping” case, corresponding to the classical situation of no periodic orbits, such that every path escapes to infinity. It is not surprising that in this case the eigenvalues will decay to
0
as
h\to 0
. In fact, we may strengthen that result by providing a superpolynomial rate of decay. Though an analogous result should hold on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we only prove this fact on the quantized torus.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\widehat{M}_{N}
be a metaplectic operator on
H_{N}
with eigenvalues
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
of
M
where
\lambda>1
, and let
\nu\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
equal to
0
near the origin with support on a small enough neighborhood of
0
. Then

\sup_{\lambda\in\Spec\Op_{N}\nu\widehat{M}_{N}}|\lambda|=O(h^{\infty}).

A discussion and proof of Theorem
3
is given in Section
4
.

Finally, we discuss how small the support of
\chi
must be in Theorem
2
. We shall show that there is a constant
c
such the results holds for
\supp\chi,\supp\nu
respectively contained in a
\frac{c}{\lambda\|Q\|^{2}}
-neighborhood of the origin, where
Q
is the matrix of eigenvectors of
M
, normalized to have determinant
1
(this doesn’t quite fix
Q
but bounds its norm below). The reciprocal dependence on
\lambda
and
\|Q\|^{2}
matches the intuition that we are isolating a single fixed point in a region with no other periodic orbits, as discussed in Section
1.1
.

2.
The Operator on the Real Line

2.1.
Review of Pseudodifferential and Metaplectic Operators

We recall briefly the semiclassical pseduodifferential and metaplectic calculi on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which will be used repeatedly throughout the paper. The reader may refer to the book by Zworski
[
24
]
for a complete introduction.

A function
m:T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
is called an
order function
if
m(x,\xi)\leq C\langle|x-y,\xi-\eta|\rangle^{N}m(y,\eta)
for some
C,N
. The corresponding
symbol class
of
m
is defined as

S(m):=\{a\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}):\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\,|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a(x,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha\beta}m(x,\xi)\}.

Note that elements of
S(m)
(called
symbols
) may depend on
h
, and in such a case the constant
C_{\alpha\beta}
in the definition must be uniform in
h
.

The
Weyl quantization
of a symbol is an operator
\Op_{h}a:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(sometimes written
a(x,hD)
) given by

(\Op_{h}(a)u)(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{n}}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi.

These operators are called
semiclassical pseudodifferential operators
.

A classical result of Calderon and Vaillancourt (see
[
24
]
) gives that for
a\in S(1)
,
\Op_{h}a:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is bounded with norm independent of
h
. In addition, if
a
is real,
\Op_{h}a
is a (possibly unbounded) symmetric on
L^{2}
. In general, the formal adjoint of
\Op_{h}a
is
(\Op_{h}a)^{*}
.

We may also consider the
left quantization
(also called the
standard quantization
), given by

(\Op^{L}_{h}(a)u)(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{n}}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}(x-y)\xi}a\left(x,\xi\right)u(y)\,dy\,s\xi.

We can go from one quantization to the other via the
change of quantization formula

\Op_{h}a=\Op^{L}_{h}a_{L}

with

a_{L}=e^{\frac{ih}{2}\langle D_{x},D_{\xi}\rangle}a

(2.1)

Let
a\in S(m_{1}),b\in S(m_{2})
. The composition of Weyl quantizations is given by the following formula:

\Op_{h}a\Op_{h}b=\Op_{h}{a\#b}

where
a\#b\in S(m_{1}m_{2})
is the
Moyal product
given by

a\#b(x,\xi):=e^{ih\sigma(D_{x},D_{\xi},D_{y},D_{\eta})}(a(x,\xi)b(y,\eta))|_{y=x,\eta=\xi}

which gives the asymptotic formula

a\#b(x,\xi)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{k}h^{k}}{k!}\sigma(D_{x},D_{\xi},D_{y},D_{\eta})^{k}(a(x,\xi)b(y,\eta))|_{y=x,\eta=\xi}

in
S(m_{1}m_{2})
.
In particular, if
a,b\in S(1)
have disjoint supports, then

\|\Op_{h}(a)\Op_{h}(b)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=O(h^{\infty})

It also follows that if
a,b\in S(1)
, the commutator of quantizations is given by

[\Op_{h}a,\Op_{h}b]=-ih\Op_{h}(\{a,b\})+O(h^{3})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is the Poisson bracket.

Furthermore, if
a\in S
is bounded below by
c_{0}>0
, the
Gårding inequality
gives that for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
C>0
such that for
h
small enough,

\langle\Op_{h}(a)u,u\rangle\geq C(c_{0}-\varepsilon)\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(2.2)

We remark that (
2.2
) may be strengthened to the
Sharp Gårding inequality
and furthermore to the
Fefferman-Phong inequality
, which give negative
h
-dependent lower bounds when
\varepsilon=0
(see Theorem 4.32 in
[
24
]
), though we will not need such bounds for this paper.

The exponential of a pseudodifferential operator is also a pseudodifferential operator. If
g=\log m
for order function
m
, then
e^{t\Op_{h}g}
is the semiclassical quantization of some
b_{t}\in S(m^{t})
. In particular, if
g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then there is a
C
such that

\|e^{t\Op_{h}g}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{C|t|}.

Finally, we discuss the slightly less standard theory of functional calculus applied to Weyl pseudodifferential operators.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
a\in S(1)
or
a\in S(m)
is elliptic in the sense that
a\geq\frac{1}{C}m-C
, and
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\phi(\Op_{h}a)
is a Weyl pseudodiffernetial operator with symbol in
S(m^{-k})
for every
k
. Suppose
b\in S^{1}
such that
\supp b\cap a^{-1}(\supp\phi)=\emptyset
. Then
\phi(\Op_{h}a)\Op_{h}b=O(h^{\infty})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
follows from the Helffer-Sjöstrand formula ((8.13) in
[
6
]
), together (8.15–8.16) in
[
6
]
which provide the asymptotic expansion for the symbol of
\phi(\Op_{h}a)
as

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p_{j}
with

p_{j}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{(2j)!}\partial_{t}^{2j}\left(q_{j}(x,\xi,t)\phi(t)\right)|_{t=a_{0}(x,\xi)},

where
a_{0}=\lim_{h\to 0}a
and
q_{j}
are symbols coming from terms in the semiclassical expansion of the resolvent
(z-\Op_{h}a)^{-1}
.

We now discuss the calculus of metaplectic operators. Let
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})
be a real symplectic matrix. Then the
semiclassical metaplectic operators associated to
M
are
\widehat{M}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given in
[
24
]
[
7
]
. In the special case when
\det A\neq 0
, we have (up to a global phase) that

\widehat{M}u(y)=\frac{|\det A|^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{(2\pi h)^{n}}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle CA^{-1}x,x\rangle+\langle A^{-1}x,\eta\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle A^{-1}B\eta,\eta\rangle-\langle y,\eta\rangle\right)}u(y)\,dy\,d\eta.

These operators are unitary, and form a representation of the
metaplectic group
, which is a double cover of the symplectic group. The importance of metaplectic operators is that they “quantize” linear changes of phase variables, in the sense given by the
exact Egorov theorem

[
24
]
[
7
]

\Op_{h}(a)\widehat{M}=\widehat{M}\Op_{h}(a\circ M).

2.2.
Preliminary Inequalities

We begin by studying the operator
\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}
on
\mathbb{R}
, where
\widehat{F}u(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}u\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)
and
\chi\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R})
. A similar operator on different spaces was considered by Gerard
[
11
]
and on yet different spaces by Faure and Tsujii
[
10
]
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the following weighted Sobolev-type norm:

\|u\|_{Y}^{2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.

Denote by
Y
the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
in the
Y
-norm.

Fix
\Lambda>0
, and define the “cut-off” version of the
Y
-norm as follows:

\|u\|_{B}^{2}:=\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u\|_{Y}^{2}+\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Here
P_{h}=P(x,hD)
is the semiclassical harmonic oscillator
h^{2}\Delta+x^{2}
, and
U_{h}u(x)=h^{\frac{1}{4}}u(h^{\frac{1}{2}}x)
is a unitary semiclassical rescaling. We remark that the
B
-norm depends on
h,\Gamma
, and
m
, though we generally suppress these constants in writing.

In fact, the
B
-norm is just a dressed up
L^{2}
norm, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 2.2
.

There is a
C=C(m,\Lambda)
such that

C^{-1}h^{\frac{m}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|u\|_{B}\leq Ch^{-\frac{m}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

The purpose of Proposition
2.2
is to be able to write
O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
and
O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{B}
indistinguishably, which we do throughout the remainder of the paper.

Proof.

We first prove the lower bound. By subtracting off
\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
from both sides, we must only show that for some
c>0
,

\|U_{h}w\|_{Y}^{2}\geq ch^{m}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

(2.3)

for
w=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u
. But

\displaystyle\|U_{h}w\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial^{k}(U_{h}w)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}U_{h}w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=h^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\langle x\rangle^{-2m}\left|w\left(h^{\frac{1}{2}}x\right)\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle h^{-\frac{1}{2}}x\right\rangle^{-2m}|w(x)|^{2}\,dx=\left\|\left\langle h^{-\frac{1}{2}}x\right\rangle^{-m}w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\geq h^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Now let
\phi,\tilde{\phi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\{|x|<2\Lambda\})
such that
\tilde{\phi}=1
on
\{|x|\leq\Lambda\}
,
\phi=1
on
\supp\tilde{\phi}
. Then
w=\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})w
, and we have

\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\left\|\left(\langle x\rangle^{-m}\phi(P_{h})+I-\phi(P_{h})\right)\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Consider
A=\langle x\rangle^{-m}\phi(P_{h})+I-\phi(P_{h})
as a semiclassical pseudodifferential operator. We have by the Helffer-Sjöstrand construction (see
[
6
]
Chapter 8) that
\phi(P_{h})
is a smoothing operator with principal symbol
\phi(x^{2}+\xi^{2})
, so in particular is in
S(1)
, and hence
A
has
S(1)
symbol equal to
\langle x\rangle^{-m}\phi(x^{2}+\xi^{2})+1-\phi(x^{2}+\xi^{2})+O_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}(h)
. This symbol is elliptic, so we get for
h
small enough that:

\left\|\left(\langle x\rangle^{-m}\phi(P_{h})+I-\phi(P_{h})\right)\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq C\|\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

Putting the inequalities together gives (
2.3
).

We now prove the upper bound. As before, by subtracting off
\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
from both sides, we must only show that

\|U_{h}w\|_{Y}^{2}\leq Ch^{-m}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

(2.4)

for
w=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u
. But

\displaystyle\|U_{h}w\|_{Y}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial^{k}(U_{h}w)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\partial^{k}(U_{h}w)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|U_{h}h^{\frac{k}{2}}\partial^{k}w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}h^{-k}\left\|(hD)^{k}w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Let
\phi
be as before, so
(hD)^{k}w=(hD)^{k}\phi(P_{h})w
. As
\phi(P_{h})
has symbol
\phi(x^{2}+\xi^{2})\in S(\langle\xi\rangle^{-k})
,
(hD)^{k}\phi(P_{h})
has symbol in
S(1)
and hence is bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This gives that

\|U_{h}w\|_{Y}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}h^{-k}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq Ch^{-m}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

which is (
2.4
).
∎

The key property of the
Y
norm is that we can find sharp bounds for
\widehat{F}
on certain closed subspaces. Remark that by Sobolev embedding, functions in
Y
have
C^{K-1}
smoothness for
K\leq m
, so we may define the subspace

V_{K}:=\{u\in Y:u^{(k)}(0)=0\quad\forall k<K\}.

(2.5)

The following inequality is inspired by Proposition 3.5 in
[
11
]
. Although seemingly elementary, is in some sense the crux of the entire argument to follow.

Lemma 2.3
.

There is a
C_{0}
such that for
K\leq m
,
u\in V_{K}
,

\|\widehat{F}u\|_{Y}\leq C_{0}\lambda^{-\frac{M}{4}}\|u\|_{Y}.

(2.6)

Proof.

We first write:

\displaystyle\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial_{x}^{k}\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2k}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\widehat{F}\partial_{x}^{k}u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\lambda^{-2k-1}\int\langle x\rangle^{-2m}\left|u^{(k)}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2k}\int\langle\lambda x\rangle^{-2m}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

When
k\geq K
, we then directly see that

\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial_{x}^{k}\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\lambda^{-2K}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

When
k<K
, we require an additional argument.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
r<1
,
u\in V_{K}
,
0\leq k<K
,

\|u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}([-r,r])}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}r^{K-k+\frac{1}{2}}}{(K-k)!}\left(\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}+\|u^{(K+1)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\right)

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

We easily see that

\|u^{(k)}\|_{L^{2}([-r,r])}\leq\sqrt{2r}\|u^{(k)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}.

(2.7)

But

\displaystyle u^{(k)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(K-k-1)!}\int_{0}^{x}u^{(K)}(t)(x-t)^{K-k-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{K-k}}{(K-k-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}u^{(K)}(sx)(1-s)^{K-k-1}\,ds.

This gives that

\|u^{(k)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}\leq\frac{r^{K-k}}{(K-k)!}\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}.

(2.8)

But by the Sobolev Inequality,

\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}\leq 2\left(\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}+\|u^{(K+1)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\right).

(2.9)

Combining (
2.7
), (
2.8
), and (
2.9
) gives the inequality.
∎

Fix
s=s(\lambda)\in\left(1,\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}+1}{2}}\right)
to be chosen later, and let
r=\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}-1}{\lambda-s^{2}}}<1
, which is chosen such that
x>r\implies\langle\lambda x\rangle>s\langle x\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial_{x}\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2k}\int_{|x|\leq r}\langle\lambda x\rangle^{-2m}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\lambda^{-2k}\int_{|x|>r}\langle\lambda x\rangle^{-2m}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2k}\int_{|x|\leq r}\langle\lambda x\rangle^{-2m}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\lambda^{-2k}s^{-2m}\int_{|x|>r}\langle x\rangle^{-2m}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

But by Lemma
2.4
the first term is

\displaystyle\lambda^{-2k}\int_{|x|\leq r}\langle\lambda x\rangle^{-2m}

\displaystyle|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx\leq\lambda^{-2k}\int_{|x|\leq r}|u^{(k)}(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2k}\left(2\sqrt{2}\frac{r^{K-k+\frac{1}{2}}}{(K-k)!}\left(\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}+\|u^{(K+1)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 16\lambda^{-2k}\frac{r^{2K-2k+1}}{(K-k)!^{2}}\left(\|u^{(K)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}+\|u^{(K+1)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}\right).

We now wish to optimize
r
and
s
to get the best possible bound. Set
r=s^{-1}
and get
s=\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}
,
r=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{4}}
. Summing everything together gives the bound (
2.6
).
∎

We also need a bound for a smoothing operator in the
Y
-norm.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R})
, and define operator
\widetilde{\chi}
by
\widetilde{\chi}=\chi(\sqrt{h}x,\sqrt{h}D)
. Then there are
C>0
,
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\|\widetilde{\chi}u\|_{Y}\leq(C+f(m)\sqrt{h})\|u\|_{Y}.

(2.10)

Proof.

Let
\tilde{h}=\sqrt{h}
. We compute the commutator of
\widetilde{\chi}
with a derivative:

\displaystyle[D_{x},\widetilde{\chi}]u(x)

\displaystyle=D_{x}\iint e^{i(x-y)\xi}\chi(\tilde{h}\frac{x+y}{2},\tilde{h}\xi)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi

\displaystyle\quad-\iint e^{i(x-y)\xi}\chi(\tilde{h}\frac{x+y}{2},\tilde{h}\xi)D_{y}u(y)\,dy\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\iint D_{x}\left(e^{i(x-y)\xi}\chi(\tilde{h}\frac{x+y}{2},\tilde{h}\xi)\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\iint D_{y}\left(e^{i(x-y)\xi}\chi(\tilde{h}\frac{x+y}{2},\tilde{h}\xi)\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}\iint\left(e^{i(x-y)\xi}\chi^{\prime}(\tilde{h}\frac{x+y}{2},\tilde{h}\xi)\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi.

By an easy induction, we see that

[D_{x}^{k},\widetilde{\chi}]=\tilde{h}\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)

(2.11)

where
\chi_{k}
is a compactly supported smooth function independent of
h
.

We then have:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\chi}u\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}D^{k}\widetilde{\chi}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}(\widetilde{\chi}D^{k}+[D^{k},\widetilde{\chi}])u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\widetilde{\chi}D^{k}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}[D^{k},\widetilde{\chi}]u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\widetilde{\chi}D^{k}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+2\tilde{h}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

(2.12)

Define function
\chi_{s}(x,\xi)=\chi(\tilde{h}x,\xi)
, so
\widetilde{\chi}=\Op_{\tilde{h}}\chi_{s}
. Note that while
\chi_{s}
is
\tilde{h}
-dependent, this dependence only contributes to further decay of its derivatives, so it is still in all the same symbol classes as
\chi
. Note also that
\langle x\rangle^{-m}\in S(\langle x\rangle^{-m})
, so we have (with
\cdot
referring to the
x
variable):

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle^{-m}\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle^{m}=\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)+[\langle x\rangle^{-m},\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)]\langle x\rangle^{m}

\displaystyle=\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)+\left(\frac{\tilde{h}}{i}\{\langle\cdot\rangle^{-m},\chi_{s}\}(x,\tilde{h}D)+O_{S(\langle x\rangle^{-m})}(\tilde{h}^{3})\right)\langle x\rangle^{m}

\displaystyle=\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)+\left(\frac{\tilde{h}}{i}(m\langle\cdot\rangle^{-m-2}(\cdot)\partial_{\xi}\chi_{s})^{w}(x,\tilde{h}D)+O_{S(\langle x\rangle^{-m})}(\tilde{h}^{3})\right)\langle x\rangle^{m}

\displaystyle=\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)+\left(O_{S(\langle x\rangle^{-m})}(\tilde{h})\right)\langle x\rangle^{m}.

Then
\langle x\rangle^{-m}\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)\langle x\rangle^{m}
is bounded by
C+O(\tilde{h})
from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
where the big-O depends on
m
but the
C
does not. This proves that:

\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\widetilde{\chi}D^{k}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq(C+O(\tilde{h}))\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}D^{k}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(2.13)

Similarly we see that

\langle x\rangle^{-m}\chi_{k,r}(x,\tilde{h}D)\langle x\rangle^{m}\leq\chi_{k,r}(x,\tilde{h}D)+\left(O_{S(\langle x\rangle^{-m})}(\tilde{h})\right)\langle x\rangle^{m}

where
\chi_{k,r}(x,\xi)=\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\xi)
, which gives

\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\widetilde{\chi}_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}\xi)u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq(C+O(\tilde{h}))\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(2.14)

Combining (
2.12
), (
2.13
), and (
2.14
) gives (
2.10
).
∎

We put the pieces together to prove an estimate for
\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}
in the
B
-norm.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose that
\supp\chi\subseteq\{|x|^{2}+|\xi|^{2}<\Lambda/(2\lambda^{2})\}
. Then there are
C>0,f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that if
u\in Y\cap V_{K}
, then

\|\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{B}\leq\left(C\lambda^{-\frac{K}{4}}+f(m)\sqrt{h}\right)\|u\|_{B}.

(2.15)

We remark that the independence of
C
from
m
in Proposition
2.6
(and hence also in Proposition
2.5
) is crucial, as we will eventually take
K
very large to make the left hand side of (
2.15
) small, but require that
m\geq K
for
V_{K}
to even be defined.

Proof.

Recall that

\|\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{B}^{2}=\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{Y}^{2}+\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

By Theorem 6.6 in
[
24
]
(or an argument similar to that of Proposition
2.2
),

\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}}

so by Proposition
2.2
we must only bound
\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\|_{Y}
.

But

\left\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}\leq\left\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}+\left\|U_{h}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}.

(2.16)

We bound the first term in (
2.16
) first. We have:

\displaystyle\left\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}\partial^{k}(U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\partial^{k}(U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}h^{-k}\left\|(hD)^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\{|x|<\Lambda\})
have
\phi=1
on
\supp\chi
, and let
\tilde{\phi}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\{|x|<2\Lambda\})
have
\tilde{\phi}=1
on
\{|x|<\Lambda\}
.

Then
\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}(I-\phi(P_{h}))
, so

\displaystyle\left\|(hD)^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|(hD)^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}(I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(hD)^{k}((I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\left\|(hD)^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}(I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\left\|(hD)^{k}(I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\left\|(hD)^{k}\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})(I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

But by pseudodifferential calculus,
\|(hD)^{k}(I-\phi(P_{h}))\chi(x,hD)\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
and
\|(hD)^{k}\tilde{\phi}(P_{h})\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
are both
O(h^{\infty})
, and all the other operators are uniformly bounded, so we have for each
k
,

\left\|(hD)^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}}

and hence by Lemma
2.2
:

\left\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}=O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{B}

To handle the second term in (
2.16
), we commute terms and apply Lemmas
2.5
and
2.3
:

\displaystyle\left\|U_{h}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}

\displaystyle=\left\|U_{h}\chi(x,hD)\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\lambda^{2}(hD)^{2}\leq\Lambda}\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\left\|\widetilde{\chi}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\lambda^{2}(hD)^{2}\leq\Lambda}\widehat{F}u\right\|_{Y}+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq(C+f(m)\sqrt{h})\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\lambda^{2}(hD)^{2}\leq\Lambda}\widehat{F}u\|_{Y}+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=(C+f(m)\sqrt{h})\|\widehat{F}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u\|_{Y}+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq(C\lambda^{-\frac{K}{4}}+f(m)\sqrt{h})\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u\|_{Y}+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

This gives (
2.15
).
∎

Finally, we will need pointwise control of
\widetilde{\chi}u
near
x=0
. We assume from now on that
m\geq K+2
.

Lemma 2.7
.

There is an
f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for
k<m-1
,

\left|\partial_{x}^{k}\widetilde{\chi}u(0)-\partial_{x}^{k}u(0)\right|\leq f(m,\varepsilon)h^{\frac{m-k-1}{2}-\varepsilon}\|u\|_{Y}

(2.17)

where
\widetilde{\chi}=\chi(\sqrt{h}x,\sqrt{h}D)
as in Lemma
2.5
.

Proof.

We recall the notation form Lemma
2.5
and write
\tilde{h}:=\sqrt{h}
and
\chi_{s}(x,\xi):=\chi(\tilde{h}x,\xi)
so
\widetilde{\chi}=\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)
.

We first prove the result for
k=0
. Write
\chi_{s}(x,\tilde{h}D)=\Op^{L}_{\tilde{h}}(\chi_{L})+O(\tilde{h}^{\infty})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, where
\Op^{L}
is the left quantization, and let
\psi(x)=\check{\chi}_{L}(0,x)
, where the inverse Fourier transform is in the second variable. We then compute:

\widetilde{\chi}u(0)=\int\chi_{L}(0,\tilde{h}\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi+O(\tilde{h}^{\infty})\|u\|_{Y}.

By Parseval’s Identity and Taylor’s theorem:

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\chi_{L}(0,\tilde{h}\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi=\tilde{h}^{-1}\int\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)u(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}^{-1}\int\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)\left(u(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}u^{(j)}(0)+\frac{x^{p}}{(p-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)\,dt\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=u(0)+\frac{\tilde{h}^{-1}}{(p-1)!}\int\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)x^{p}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)\,dt\,dx

(2.18)

where
p<m
.

We split the integral error term in (
2.18
) as follows. Let:

I_{1}:=\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|<r}\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)x^{p}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)\,dt\,dx

I_{2}:=\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)x^{p}\int_{0}^{\frac{r}{x}}(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)\,dt\,dx

I_{3}:=\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)x^{p}\int_{\frac{r}{x}}^{1}(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)\,dt\,dx

where
0<r<1
is to be chosen later, so by (
2.18
),

\widetilde{\chi}u(0)-u(0)=\frac{1}{(p-1)!}(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3})+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{Y}

(2.19)

We must bound each
I_{j}
in turn. Note that as the result is the same for
\psi(-\cdot),u(-\cdot)
, we may only bound the integrals where
x\geq 0
. We then have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}|I_{1}|&\leq\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|<r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)||x|^{p}\int_{0}^{1}|(1-t)^{p-1}u^{(p)}(tx)|\,dt\,dx\\
&\leq r^{p}\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|<r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)|\,dx\|u^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}\\
&\leq Cr^{p}\left(\|u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}+\|u^{(p+1)}\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\right)\\
&\leq C(m)r^{p}\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p+1)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right).\end{split}

(2.20)

Also

\displaystyle\begin{split}|I_{2}|&\leq\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)|\int_{0}^{r}|x-t|^{p-1}|u^{(p)}(t)|\,dt\,dx\\
&\leq C(p)\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)|\langle x\rangle^{p}\,dx\|u^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}([-r,r])}\\
&\leq C(p)\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)|\langle\tilde{h}^{-1}x\rangle^{p}\,dx\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p+1)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\\
&=C(p)\int_{|y|\geq\tilde{h}^{-1}r}|\psi(y)|\langle y\rangle\,dy\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p+1)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\\
&\leq C(m)O(\tilde{h}^{\infty})\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p+1)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)\end{split}

(2.21)

provided
r>\tilde{h}^{1-\varepsilon}
.

Finally we get

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{|x|\geq r}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)|\int_{r}^{x}|x-t|^{p-1}|u^{(p)}(t)|\,dt\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{t\geq r}\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{t}^{\infty}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)||x-t|^{p-1}\,dx|u^{(p)}(t)|\,dt.

But

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{t}^{\infty}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)||x-t|^{p-1}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{h}^{-1}\int_{t}^{\infty}|\psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}x)||\tilde{h}^{-1}(x-t)|^{p-1}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{h}^{-1}t}^{\infty}|\psi(y)||y-\tilde{h}^{-1}t|^{p-1}\,dy

\displaystyle=\Psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}t)

for
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
so

\displaystyle\begin{split}|I_{3}|&\leq\int_{t\geq r}\Psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}t)|u^{(p)}(t)|\,dt\\
&\leq\int_{t\geq r}\Psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}t)\langle\tilde{h}^{-1}t\rangle^{m}\langle t\rangle^{-m}|u^{(p)}(t)|\,dt\\
&\leq\left(\int_{t>r}\left|\Psi(\tilde{h}^{-1}t)\right|^{2}\langle\tilde{h}^{-1}t\rangle^{2m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{t>r}\langle t\rangle^{-2m}\left|u^{(p)}(t)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&=C(m)O(\tilde{h}^{\infty})\|\langle x\rangle^{-m}u^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.\end{split}

(2.22)

The bounds (
2.20
), (
2.21
), (
2.22
) together with (
2.19
) give (
2.17
) for the case
k=0
, by choosing
p
as large as possible and
r=\tilde{h}^{1-\varepsilon}
.

For larger powers, we use the fact from the proof of Lemma
2.5
, specifically (
2.11
), which says that

[D_{x}^{k},\widetilde{\chi}]=\tilde{h}\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)
where
\chi_{k}(x,\xi)=0
near
x,\xi=0
. Then

\left|\partial_{x}^{k}\widetilde{\chi}u(0)-\partial_{x}^{k}u(0)\right|\leq\left|\widetilde{\chi}\partial_{x}^{k}u(0)-\partial_{x}^{k}u(0)\right|+|\tilde{h}\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u(0)|,

(2.23)

and we may simply apply the result for
k=0
on the first term. On the second term, we write
\chi_{k}=\chi_{k}+\phi-\phi
where
\phi=1
near
0
and
\supp\phi\subseteq\chi_{k}^{-1}(0)
. Then by (
2.23
) we get

\displaystyle|\tilde{h}\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)

\displaystyle u(0)|=\tilde{h}|\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u(0)+\phi(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u(0)-\phi(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u(0)|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{h}\left|\left(\chi_{k}(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)+\phi(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)\right)u(0)-u(0)\right|+\left|\phi(\tilde{h}x,\tilde{h}D)u(0)-u(0)\right|

on which we can again apply the result for
k=0
. This proves (
2.17
) in general.
∎

2.3.
Grushin Problems

To determine the spectrum of
\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{F}
, we use the technique of building a Grushin problem, which relies on the well-known Schur complement formula. We briefly review these techniques here, and refer to Sjöstrand and Zworski
[
22
]
for a more extensive exposition. We also remark that the particular Grushin problem in this section is similar to those in
[
11
]
.

The
Schur complement formula
in elementary linear algebra says that for block matrices

\mathcal{P}:=\begin{pmatrix}P&R_{-}\\
R_{+}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{P}^{-1}:=\mathcal{E}:=\begin{pmatrix}E&E_{+}\\
E_{-}&E_{-+}\end{pmatrix},

(2.24)

P
is invertible if and only if
E_{-+}
is, and in fact we have the formulas

P^{-1}=E-E_{+}E_{-+}^{-1}E_{-},\quad E_{-+}^{-1}=-R_{+}P^{-1}R_{-}.

More generally, the above formula applies even if the operators act on infinite-dimensional spaces. Specifically, let
H_{1},H_{2},H_{+},H_{-}
be Hilbert spaces, and let
P:H_{1}\to H_{2}
be an operator. Let
R_{-}:H_{-}\to H
,
R_{+}:H\to H_{+}
, and define
\mathcal{P}:H_{1}\oplus H_{-}\to H_{2}\oplus H_{+}
by (
2.24
). If
\mathcal{P}
is invertible, we say that the Grushin problem is
well-posed
. In practice,
H_{\pm}
are often finite-dimensional or otherwise simpler spaces, and we refer to
E_{-+}
as the
effective Hamiltonian
of
P
. We remark in particular that if
P
is a Fredholm operator, it is always possible to construct a Grushin problem with finite-dimensional
H_{\pm}
, which matches the intuition of Fredholm operators being “effectively finite-dimensional.”

The starting point for our computation are the following simple Taylor coefficient operators:

R_{-}:\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\quad R_{+}:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}

R_{-}(a_{0},\dots,a_{K}):=\sum_{j=0}^{K}a_{j}\frac{x^{j}}{j!},\quad R_{+}u:=\left(u(0),u^{\prime}(0),\dots,u^{(K)}(0)\right)

Note that
R_{+}R_{-}=I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}
and
R_{-}R_{+}
returns the
K
th order Taylor polynomial at
0
. In particular, this means that
\mathrm{Ran}(I-R_{-}R_{+})\subseteq V_{K+1}
as defined in (
2.5
).

We now define the “Gaussian” versions of
R_{\pm}
as follows:

\hat{R}_{-}(a_{0},\dots,a_{K}):=e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}R_{-}(a_{0},\dots,a_{K}),\quad\hat{R}_{+}u:=R_{+}\left(e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}u\right).

The property

\hat{R}_{+}\hat{R}_{-}=I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}

(2.25)

still holds, but
\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+}
is more complicated. We nevertheless still have that
\mathrm{Ran}(I-\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+})\subseteq V_{K+1}
, as for any
u\in C^{\infty}
,

(I-\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+})u=u-e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}R_{-}R_{+}(e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}u)=e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}(I-R_{-}R_{+})(e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}u)\in V_{K+1}.

(2.26)

We also note the relation
\hat{R}_{+}=B_{K}R_{+}
, where
B_{K}
is an explicitly computable lower-triangular matrix with all
1
’s on the diagonal.

We are now ready to define the “localized”
R_{\pm}
operators. Define

\tilde{R}_{-}:\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\tilde{R}_{+}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}

given by

\tilde{R}_{-}:=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}U_{h}^{*}\hat{R}_{-},\quad\tilde{R}_{+}:=\hat{R}_{+}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda},

where we note that for
h\leq\frac{\Lambda}{2(K+1)}
the
\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}
in the definition of
\tilde{R}_{-}
is actually superfluous.
We see that by the Sobolev inequality

\displaystyle(R_{+}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u)_{k}

\displaystyle=\partial^{k}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u(x)|_{x=0}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\partial^{k}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u(x)\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}+\|\partial^{k+1}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u(x)\|_{L^{2}([-1,1])}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{B}

with constants independent of
h
, so

\tilde{R}_{+}:B\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}

is bounded independent of
h
. Similarly

\|\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}U_{h}^{*}\hat{R}_{-}v\|_{B}=\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}U_{h}^{*}\hat{R}_{-}v\|_{Y}=\|\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}\hat{R}_{-}v\|_{Y}=\|\hat{R}_{-}v\|_{Y}

for
h
small enough, which gives in that case that

\tilde{R}_{-}:\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to B

is also bounded independent of
h
.

The analogues of
(\ref{RmRp})
and
(\ref{RpRm})
for
\tilde{R}_{\pm}
are given in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.8
.

If
h\leq\frac{\Lambda}{2(K+1)}
, then:

\tilde{R}_{+}\tilde{R}_{-}=I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}

and
\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}
has range
V_{K+1}
.

Proof.

We have that for
h
small enough:

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{+}\tilde{R}_{-}=\hat{R}_{+}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}\hat{R}_{-}=I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}

and also

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}U_{h}^{*}\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}=U_{h}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}U_{h}

hence

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}

\displaystyle=U_{h}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}^{2}U_{h}-U_{h}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}U_{h}

\displaystyle=U_{h}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}(I-\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+})\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}U_{h}

\displaystyle=U_{h}^{*}(I-\hat{R}_{-}\hat{R}_{+})\mathbbm{1}_{P\leq\frac{\Lambda}{h}}U_{h}

which has range
V_{K+1}
.
∎

In the argument to come it will be necessary to apply
\tilde{R}_{+}
to
\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u.
The analysis is given by the following lemma, which makes use of Lemma
2.7
in a fundamental way.

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\tilde{R}_{+}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u=L_{K}\tilde{R}_{+}u+Ru

(2.27)

where
L_{K}
is a lower triangular matrix independent of
u
and
|Ru|\leq C_{\varepsilon}h^{\frac{m-K-1}{2}-\varepsilon}\|u\|_{B}
.

Proof.

We compute the following (using analogous arguments to those of Lemma
2.2
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{R}_{+}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u&=\hat{R}_{+}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u\\
&=\hat{R}_{+}U_{h}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}u+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&=\hat{R}_{+}\widehat{F}U_{h}\chi_{\lambda}(x,hD)u+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&=\hat{R}_{+}\widehat{F}U_{h}\chi_{\lambda}(x,hD)\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&=\hat{R}_{+}\widehat{F}\widetilde{\chi_{\lambda}}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&=B_{K}D_{K}R_{+}\widetilde{\chi_{\lambda}}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u+O(h^{\infty})\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\end{split}

(2.28)

where
D_{K}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\mathrm{diag}(1,\frac{1}{\lambda},\dots,\frac{1}{\lambda^{K}})
.

We must now commute
R_{+}
and
\widetilde{\chi_{\lambda}}
. We have by Lemma
2.7
:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{k}\widetilde{\chi_{\lambda}}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u(x)|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{k}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u(x)|_{x=0}+O_{m}(h^{\frac{m-k-1}{2}-\varepsilon})\|U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u\|_{Y}.

Then

B_{K}D_{K}R_{+}\widetilde{\chi_{\lambda}}U_{h}\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u=B_{K}D_{K}B_{K}^{-1}\tilde{R}_{+}u+O_{m}(h^{\frac{m-K-1}{2}-\varepsilon})\|u\|_{B}.

(2.29)

Combining (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) gives (
2.27
).
∎

2.4.
Inverting the Grushin Problem

Let

\mathcal{P}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}&\tilde{R}_{-}\\
\tilde{R}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Split
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
with
u_{1}=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}u
and
u_{2}=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}u
, and similarly for
v=v_{1}+v_{2}
. Consider
\mathcal{P}
as an operator acting on
\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
. Let

\mathcal{P}(z)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
u_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v\\
v_{+}\end{pmatrix}

so

(I-z\chi\widehat{F})(u_{1}+u_{2})+\tilde{R}_{-}u_{-}=v_{1}+v_{2}

\tilde{R}_{+}(u_{1}+u_{2})=v_{+}.

The equations decouple (up to
O(h^{\infty})
errors) as

(I-z\chi\widehat{F}+R_{0})u_{1}+R_{1}u_{2}+\tilde{R}_{-}u_{-}=v_{1}

(I+R_{3})u_{2}+R_{2}u_{1}=v_{2}

\tilde{R}_{+}u_{1}=v_{+}.

where
R_{0},R_{1},R_{2},R_{3}=O(h^{\infty})_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
.

Define the following operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}=\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
:

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{E}&0&\tilde{E}_{+}\\
0&I&0\\
\tilde{E}_{-}&0&\tilde{E}_{-+}\end{pmatrix}

(2.30)

and define the nontrivial entries as follows:

\tilde{E}:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})

\tilde{E}_{+}:=\tilde{R}_{-}-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(I-z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}

\tilde{E}_{-}:=\tilde{R}_{+}

\tilde{E}_{-+}:=-(I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}-zL_{K}).

Recall that by Proposition
2.8
,
I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}
has range
V_{K+1}
, so by Proposition
2.6
, there is a
c>0
independent of
m
such that for
h
small enough

\tilde{E},\tilde{E}_{+}\text{ defined and bounded for }|z|\leq c\lambda^{\frac{K+1}{4}}.

(2.31)

Lemma 2.10
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N},K\leq m-2
, there is an
r_{K}
with
r_{K}\to\infty
such that for
|z|\leq r_{K}
,
h
small enough, the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)
is well-defined and satisfies

\mathcal{P}(z)\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)=I+\mathcal{T}(z)

with

\|\mathcal{T}(z)\|_{B\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to B\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}=O(h^{\frac{m-K-1}{4}-\varepsilon}).

(2.32)

Proof.

Let
r_{K}=c\lambda^{\frac{K}{8}}
, where
c
is given in (
2.31
).

Let

\mathcal{T}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}&T_{1+}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}&T_{2+}\\
T_{+1}&T_{+2}&T_{++}\end{pmatrix}

on
\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
, so expanded out we have:

\begin{pmatrix}I+T_{11}&T_{12}&T_{1+}\\
T_{21}&I+T_{22}&T_{2+}\\
T_{+1}&T_{+2}&I+T_{++}\end{pmatrix}=\\
\begin{pmatrix}I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F}+R_{0}&R_{1}&\tilde{R}_{-}\\
R_{2}&I+R_{3}&0\\
\tilde{R}_{+}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{E}&0&\tilde{E}_{+}\\
0&I&0\\
\tilde{E}_{-}&0&\tilde{E}_{-+}\end{pmatrix}

(2.33)

It remains to compute the product entries in (
2.33
).
We have by telescoping that:

\displaystyle\begin{split}I+T_{11}&=(I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})\right)+\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}+R_{0}\tilde{E}\\
&=I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}+\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}+R_{0}\tilde{E}\\
&=I+O(h^{\infty}).\end{split}

The equation

T_{12}=R_{1}=O(h^{\infty})

is trivial, and for
T_{1+}
we compute by Lemma
2.9
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{1+}&=(I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}-(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(I-z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}\\
&\quad+\tilde{R}_{-}(-I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}+zL_{K})+R_{0}\tilde{E}_{+}\\
&=(I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}-(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}+\tilde{R}_{-}(-I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}+zL_{K})\\
&\quad+(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(I-z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}+R_{0}\tilde{E}_{+}\\
&=\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}(I-z\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}+\tilde{R}_{-}(-I_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}+zL_{K})\\
&\quad-(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}+R_{0}\tilde{E}_{+}\\
&=\tilde{R}_{-}R\tilde{R}_{-}-(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\geq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}+R_{0}\tilde{E}_{+}\\
&=O(h^{\infty}).\end{split}

The next three

T_{21}=R_{2}\tilde{E},\quad I+T_{22}=I+R_{3},\quad T_{2+}=R_{2}\tilde{E}_{+}

are trivially all equal to
O(h^{\infty})
, and
T_{+2}=0
.

The bounds on
T_{+1}
and
T_{+3}
require a more delicate argument. Fix
J
to determined later. Then we have:

\displaystyle T_{+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{R}_{+}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{J}z^{j}\tilde{R}_{+}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})+\tilde{R}_{+}\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+}).

By Lemma
2.6
, for
|z|\leq r_{k}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|\tilde{R}_{+}\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})\right\|_{B\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}\\
&\leq C\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}z^{j}\|(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})\|_{B\to B}\leq C\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{-\frac{K}{8}}\leq C\lambda^{-\frac{JK}{8}}.\end{split}

(2.34)

Meanwhile repeated application of Lemma
2.9
gives

R_{+}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}=L^{j}\tilde{R}_{+}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}L^{i}R(\chi(x,hD)\hat{F})^{j-1-i}

with

\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}L^{i}R(\chi(x,hD)\hat{F})^{j-1-i}\right\|_{B\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}\leq CJh^{s}

for
s=\frac{m-K-1}{2}-\varepsilon
and
C=C(\varepsilon)
. Then

\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{J}z^{j}\tilde{R}_{+}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})\right\|_{B\to\mathbb{C}^{K}}\leq CJr_{K}^{J}h^{s}=CJ\lambda^{\frac{JK}{8}}h^{s}.

(2.35)

Combining (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) gives:

\|T_{+1}\|_{B\to\mathbb{C}^{K}}\leq C\left(\lambda^{-\frac{JK}{8}}+J\lambda^{\frac{JK}{8}}h^{s}\right).

To optimize, set
J=\frac{4s}{K\log\lambda}\log\frac{1}{h}
, which gives the bound

\|T_{+1}\|_{B\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}\leq Ch^{\frac{s}{2}}\log\frac{1}{h}.

(2.36)

Finally to bound
T_{++}
we use (
2.36
) to get:

\displaystyle\begin{split}I+T_{++}&=\tilde{R}_{+}\tilde{R}_{-}-\tilde{R}_{+}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j}(\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})^{j}(I-\tilde{R}_{-}\tilde{R}_{+})(I-z\mathbbm{1}_{P_{h}\leq\Lambda}\chi(x,hD)\widehat{F})\tilde{R}_{-}\\
&=I-T_{+1}\tilde{R}_{-}\\
&=I+O\left(h^{\frac{s}{2}}\log\frac{1}{h}\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{K+1}}.\end{split}

By choosing a new
\varepsilon
, this proves the bound (
2.32
) for
h
small enough, and completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N},K\leq m-2
, there is an
r_{K}
with
r_{K}\to\infty
, such that for
h
small enough there is a
\mathcal{E}(z):L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{C}^{K+1}
defined on
\{|z|\leq R_{K}\}
such that

\mathcal{P}(z)\mathcal{E}(z)=\mathcal{E}(z)\mathcal{P}(z)=I.

Proof.

We first find a right inverse via Neumann series. For
h
small enough we may define

\mathcal{E}(z):=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\mathcal{T}(z)^{j}\right)

(2.37)

which gives that
\mathcal{P}(z)\mathcal{E}(z)=I.

We now claim that
\mathcal{P}(z)
is an analytic family of Fredholm operators of index
0
. For indeed for
z=0
we have

\mathcal{P}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}I&\tilde{R}_{-}\\
\tilde{R}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix}

\tilde{R}_{+}&-I\end{pmatrix}

This shows that the right inverse

We may now estimate the spectrum of

\left(\mathcal{T}^{j}\right)_{+-}=O(h^{pj})

\frac{1}{z}\in\sigma_{h^{p}}(D_{K})
(where

\frac{1}{z}=\lambda^{\frac{2k+1}{2}}+O(h^{p})

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We generalize the above to metaplectic operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

have eigenvalues

with

, where

\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{M}=\Op_{h}\chi\widehat{Q^{-1}}\widehat{F}\widehat{Q}=\widehat{Q^{-1}}\Op_{h}(\chi\circ Q^{-1})\widehat{F}\widehat{Q}.

(2.38)

and hence has the same spectrum as

Thus by picking

∎

3.
The operator on the torus

3.1.
Quantization of the Torus

We describe the space of quantum states on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, as seen in
[
4
]
. Let

H_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}}:n\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}\right\}

with
h=\frac{1}{2\pi N}
. These are precisely the elements of
\mathcal{S}
which are periodic in physical and phase space under the semiclassical Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{h}
. For simplicity, define
Q_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}}
, which we decide by definition to be an orthonormal basis of
H_{N}
. One may remark that this is an elementary example of the general geometric Toeplitz quantization studied in
[
5
]
.

We review the pseudodifferential calculus on
H_{N}
, which also may be found in
[
3
]
,

[
7
]
,
[
20
]
. Let
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Then
a
lifts to a doubly-periodic function
\tilde{a}
on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}
, which is seen to be in the symbol class
S(1)
. Define

\Op_{N}a=\Op_{h}a|_{H_{N}}

which is a map from
H_{N}
to itself that is also given in coordinates by

\Op_{N}(a)Q_{j}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}A_{mj}Q_{m}

with

A_{mj}=\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{a}(k,j-m-lN)(-1)^{kl}e^{\pi i\frac{(j+m)k}{N}}.

Most results from pseudodifferential calculus carry over to
H_{N}
, see
[
3
]
,

[
7
]
for details. In particular, there is an analogue of Lemma
2.1
, given as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
a\in S(1)
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then
\phi(\Op_{N}a)
is a Weyl pseudodiffernetial operator on
H_{N}
. Suppose
b\in S(1)
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
such that
\supp b\cap a^{-1}(\supp\phi)=\emptyset
. Then
\phi(\Op_{N}a)\Op_{N}b=O(h^{\infty})_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}
.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
essentially consists of showing that the action of
\phi(\Op_{N}a)
on
H_{N}
is the same as the action of the matrix
\phi
applied to the matrix with entries
\langle\phi(\Op_{N}a)Q_{j},Q_{k}\rangle
. The details are given (in a slightly more general situation) as Lemma 2.8 in
[
3
]
.

In addition, a particular class of metaplectic operators can be defined on
H_{N}
. Let
M\in Sp(2n,\mathbb{Z})
be an integer symplectic matrix, and let
N
be even. Define
\widehat{M}_{N}=\widehat{M}|_{H_{N}}
, which is a unitary map on
H_{N}
. Consequently, we have an analogous exact Egorov’s theorem:

\Op_{N}(a)\widehat{M}_{N}=\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}(a\circ M).

3.2.
Transplantation Operators

We consider the following “sampling” maps between the torus and the real line. Let
\psi\in\mathbb{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{*}\mathbb{R})
be a bump function at
(0,0)
. The sampling operator
S_{\psi}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to H_{N}
is defined by

S_{\psi}u(x)=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}Q_{n}

where

a_{n}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(\Op_{h}(\psi)u)(k+\frac{n}{N})

and as usual
h=\frac{1}{2\pi N}
.
We may also write
S_{\psi}=\Pi_{N}\Op_{h}\psi
, where
\Pi_{N}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\to H_{N}
given by

\Pi_{N}u=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}u(k+\frac{n}{N})Q_{n}

are the projection operators considered in
[
7
]
.
As
\Op_{h}(\psi):L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, we easily see that
S_{\psi}
is well defined and bounded. In fact, we shall soon show that it is bounded independent of
h
.

The benefit of working with
S_{\psi}
comes from the particularly nice form of
S_{\psi}^{*}
and
S_{\psi}S_{\psi}^{*}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The adjoint of
S_{\psi}
is
\Op_{h}\overline{\psi}
, acting on
H_{N}
as a subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Let
K(x,y)
be the Schwartz kernel of
\Op_{h}\psi
. Then for
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
v=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}Q_{n}
, we have

\langle S_{\psi}u,v\rangle_{H_{N}}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\overline{v_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(\Op_{h}(\psi)\,u)(k+\frac{n}{N})N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\overline{v_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}K(k+\frac{n}{N},y)u(y)\,dy

which gives

S_{\psi}^{*}v(y)=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{K(k+\frac{n}{N},y)}v_{n}\\
=(\Op_{h}(\overline{\psi})v)(y)

as desired.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

If
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\subseteq C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

\Pi_{N}\Op_{h}\psi|_{H_{N}}=\Op_{N}\tilde{\psi},

(3.1)

where
\tilde{\psi}(x,\xi)=\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(x+k,\xi+l)
is the periodization of
\psi
.

Proof.

Let
u\in H_{N}
.
By periodicity of
u
, we have formally:

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}\Op_{h}\psi u

\displaystyle=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2\pi h}\iint e^{i(k+\frac{n}{N}-y)\xi}\psi\left(\frac{k+\frac{n}{N}+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi Q_{n}

\displaystyle=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2\pi h}\iint e^{i(\frac{n}{N}-y)\xi}\psi\left(\frac{\frac{n}{N}+y}{2}+k,\xi\right)u(y+k)\,dy\,d\xi Q_{n}

\displaystyle=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{2\pi h}\iint e^{i(\frac{n}{N}-y)\xi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi\left(\frac{\frac{n}{N}+y}{2}+k,\xi\right)u(y)\,dy\,d\xi Q_{n}

\displaystyle=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\Op_{h}(\psi_{p})u)Q_{n}

where
\psi_{p}(x,\xi)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(x+k,\xi)

Now let

and remark that

is also periodic in the

\displaystyle N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\Op_{h}^{L}\psi_{L}u)Q_{n}

\displaystyle=N^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}}\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int e^{\frac{i}{h}x\xi}\chi_{L}(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{x=\frac{m}{n}}\int e^{\frac{i}{h}x\xi}\chi_{L}(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=N\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi iNmx}\int e^{\frac{i}{h}x\xi}\chi_{L}(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\frac{i}{h}x(\xi+m)}\chi_{L}(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi h}\int\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\frac{i}{h}x\xi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{L}(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi+m)\,d\xi,

But as

(3.6)

where

4.
Rates of Decay for Nontrapping Eigenvalues

We now move on to the proof of Theorem
3
. We use the technique of a microlocal weight function, similar to (but simpler than) that used by Nonnenmacher, Sjöstrand, and Zworski in
[
17
]
and also to Section 6.4 of
[
8
]
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R})
have
g(x,\xi)=x^{2}-\xi^{2}
on a neighborhood of
\supp\nu
. Let

M_{tg}:=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}\Op_{N}\nu\widehat{M}_{N}e^{t\Op_{N}g}=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}(M^{*}\nu)e^{t\Op_{N}g}

for some
t
that may depend on
N
. We shall bound the norm of
M_{tg}
and optimize by taking
t=K^{\prime}\log\left(\frac{1}{h}\right)
at the end. Recall that by results in Section
2.1
adapted to the calculus on
H_{N}
,
e^{\pm t\Op_{N}g}
are semiclassical pseudodifferential operators with norm at most
O\left(e^{C|t|}\right)
.
We compute:

\displaystyle M_{tg}=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}\widehat{M}_{N}e^{t\Op_{N}g}e^{-t\Op_{N}g}\Op_{N}(M^{*}\nu)e^{t\Op_{N}g}

\displaystyle=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}e^{t\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)}\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}\nu_{tg}

\displaystyle=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}e^{t\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)}e^{t\left(\Op_{N}g-\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)}e^{-t\left(\Op_{N}g-\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)}\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}\nu_{tg}.

where
\Op_{N}\nu_{tg}=e^{-t\Op_{N}g}\Op_{N}(M^{*}\nu)e^{t\Op_{N}g}
is a pseudodifferential operator and the second equality comes from the exact Egorov’s theorem applied to the functional calculus expression
e^{t\Op_{N}g}
. Note also that if
t=O(\log\frac{1}{h})
, then
\|e^{\pm t\Op_{N}g}\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}\leq O(h^{M})
for some
M
, so the microlocal support of
\Op_{N}\nu
is preserved.

We claim that
e^{-t\Op_{N}g}e^{t\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)}e^{t\left(\Op_{N}g-\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)}
is bounded independent of
t
and
N
. For convenience let
G_{1}=\Op_{N}g
,
G_{2}=\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)
, so we must bound
W(t):=e^{-tG_{1}}e^{tG_{2}}e^{t(G_{1}-G_{2})}
. We have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}W(t)&=-G_{1}e^{-tG_{1}}e^{tG_{2}}e^{t(G_{1}-G_{2})}+e^{-tG_{1}}e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{t(G_{1}-G_{2})}\\
&=\left(-G_{1}+e^{-tG_{1}}e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}e^{tG_{1}}\right)W(t)\\
&:=Z(t)W(t)\end{split}

(4.1)

where
Z
is a pseudodifferential operator. To establish bounds on
Z(t)
, we argue as in Theorem 6.21 in
[
8
]
. We see that

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=-G_{1}+e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}-e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}+e^{-tG_{1}}e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}e^{tG_{1}}

\displaystyle=(-I+e^{\ad_{tG_{2}}})G_{1}+(-I+e^{\ad_{-tG_{1}}})\left(e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}\right).

(4.2)

We bound the first term of (
4.2
), and the second will follow similarly. Fix
K
large, and expand using Taylor’s formula:

(-I+e^{\ad_{tG_{2}}})G_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\ad_{G_{2}}^{k}G_{1}+\frac{t^{K+1}}{K!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{K}e^{-tsG_{2}}\ad_{G_{2}}^{K+1}G_{1}e^{tsG_{2}}\,ds.

(4.3)

Note that by pseudodifferential calculus
\|\ad_{G_{2}}^{k}G_{1}\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}=O(h^{k})
. Plugging into (
4.3
) gives for some
C>0

\|(-I+e^{\ad_{tG_{2}}})G_{1}\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}=O(th+(th)^{K}e^{Ct})

and an analogous argument for
(-I+e^{\ad_{-tG_{1}}})\left(e^{tG_{2}}G_{1}e^{-tG_{2}}\right)
gives that

\|Z(t)\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}=O(th+(th)^{K}e^{Ct}).

Now choose
t=K^{\prime}\log\frac{1}{h}
for fixed
K^{\prime}
. Making
K
large enough, we have
\|Z(s)\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}=O(h\log\frac{1}{h})
uniformly in
s\leq t
. By (
4.1
) and Gronwall’s inequality, we see that

\|W(t)\|_{H_{N}\to H_{N}}\leq e^{tCh\log\frac{1}{h}}=e^{C_{0}h\left(\log\frac{1}{h}\right)^{2}}\leq C_{1}.

We now show the norm of
e^{-t\left(\Op_{N}g-\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)}\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}\nu_{tg}
to be small as
t
grows large. Note that
g-\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\geq c_{0}
on a neigborhood away from the origin, which we may take as
\supp\nu_{tg}
up to
O(h^{\infty})
errors, so by the same method as Lemma 3.6 in
[
17
]
we may replace it with
\left(g-\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)\nu_{1}+(1-\nu_{1})
with
\nu_{1}=1
on
\supp\nu
and
g=x^{2}-\xi^{2}
on
\supp\nu_{1}
. Then by the Gårding inequality,
\Op_{N}(g-(M^{-1})^{*}g)\nu_{1}+(1-\nu_{1})
has spectrum bounded below by
\frac{c_{0}}{2}
for
h
small enough. This gives

\left\|e^{-t\left(\Op_{N}g-\Op_{N}\left(\left(M^{-1}\right)^{*}g\right)\right)}\right\|\leq e^{-\frac{t}{2}c_{0}}=h^{K^{\prime}\frac{c_{0}}{2}}

where
K^{\prime}
can be chosen arbitrarily large, which completes the proof.
∎
