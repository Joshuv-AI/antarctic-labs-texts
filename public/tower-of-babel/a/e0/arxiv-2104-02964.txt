Numerics for Stochastic Distributed Parameter Control Systems: a Finite Transposition Method
Qi Lü, Penghui Wang, Yanqing Wang and Xu Zhang

Abstract
In this chapter, we present some recent progresses on the numerics for stochastic distributed parameter control systems, based on the finite transposition method introduced in our previous works. We first explain how to reduce the numerics of some stochastic control problems in this respect to the numerics of backward stochastic evolution equations. Then we present a method to find finite transposition solutions to such equations. At last, we give an illuminating example.



Key words:
Numerics, stochastic distributed parameter control system, backward
stochastic evolution equation, transposition
solution, finite transposition method.

AMS 2010 subject
classifications:
93E20, 60H15, 65M60

1
Introduction

In this chapter, we study the numerics for stochastic distributed parameter control systems, including particularly
controlled stochastic evolution equations in infinite dimensions. Although some
pioneer works appeared in 1960s
(
Bensoussan 1969
;
Kushner 1968
;
Tzafestas and Nightingale 1968
, e.g.,)
,
the theory for
stochastic distributed parameter control systems is far from
mature till now. Lots of interesting and
important problems therein are not well studied. One of
them is the numerical computation on
control problems of such systems, for which one will meet many
substantially difficulties:

•

For stochastic control systems,
numerical schemes should keep the adaptedness
of states and controls with respect to the
filtration. This restricts the application of
many implicit schemes.

•

Unlike the deterministic setting, solutions to
stochastic evolution equations (even in finite
dimensions) are usually non-differentiable with respect to
the time variable. This leads to some serious troubles
in the analysis of the convergence rate.

•

In the continuous-time setting, similar to the deterministic situation, a typical way to solve many stochastic control problems is to introduce the corresponding dual systems, which are however backward
stochastic evolution equations usually
(see
Lü and Zhang 2020
, for more details)
.
Numerically, it is
very hard to compute the correction terms in such sort of backward equations
since there is
no efficient method to compute the conditional
expectation of a general random variable.

Due to these difficulties, there are very few
works on the numerical analysis for
stochastic distributed
parameter control systems

(
Dunst and Prohl 2016
;
Prohl and Wang 2020a
;
Prohl and Wang 2020b
;
Li and Zhou 2020
, e.g.,)
.

Because of the importance of backward
stochastic evolution equations in the study of stochastic distributed
parameter control systems, in this chapter we shall focus on the numerical solutions to such equations and the related theoretical analysis.

Up to now, there exist quite a number
of numerical schemes for backward
stochastic differential
equations, i.e. backward
stochastic evolution equations in finite dimensions, such as
the method of four-step scheme
(
Douglas et al. 1996
, e.g.,)
, the
quantization tree method
(
Bally and Pagès 2003
, e.g.,)
,
the forward/backward Euler method
(
Zhang 2004
;
Gobet et al. 2005
;
Bender and Denk 2007
, e.g.,)
, the Malliavin
calculus based method
(
Bouchard and Touzi 2004
;
Hu et al. 2011
, e.g.,)
, the fully time-space discretization method

(
Zhao et al. 2006
;
Zhao et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
, the finite
transposition method
(
Wang and Zhang 2011
, e.g.,)
, the Wiener
chaos decomposition method
(
Briand and Labart 2014
, e.g.,)
, the machine
learning method
(
E et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, etc. However,
numerical schemes for backward
stochastic evolution equations in infinite dimensions (including typically backward
stochastic partial differential
equations) are quite
limited
(
Wang 2013
;
Wang 2016
;
Dunst and Prohl 2016
)
.

Based on the stochastic transposition
method introduced in

Lü and Zhang 2013
;
Lü and Zhang 2014
, in this chapter, we propose a new numerical
algorithm to solve backward
stochastic evolution equations, which can be
used to compute the desired control of some
controllability/optimal control problems for stochastic distributed
parameter systems numerically.

To present the key idea in the simplest
way, we do not pursue the full
technical generality. Firstly, we
consider only the simplest case of one
dimensional standard Brownian motion
(with respect to the time variable
t
). It
would not be difficult to extend the
results to the case of the
Q
-Brownian motion and the
cylindrical Brownian motion. Secondly,
we impose considerably strong
regularity and boundedness assumptions
on data appeared in the
state equations and the
cost functionals. Thirdly, we
assume that the control operators are bounded linear
ones, which cannot cover the case
of stochastic partial differential
equations with boundary/pointwise
controls. All of these
assumptions can be considerably relaxed. Fourthly, we only
consider a null controllability and a
linear quadratic optimal control problem. More
general controllability and optimal
control problems can be studied by the
method presented in this chapter.

The rest of this chapter is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we shall introduce the control problems and the corresponding backward stochastic evolution equations to be considered in this chapter.
In Section
3
, we firstly
give an outline of the finite
transposition method for solving backward stochastic evolution equations numerically, and then provide
a choice of finite transposition space.
In Section
4
, based on the
finite transposition space, we define
the finite transposition solution to
backward stochastic evolution equations, and
then present the existence and uniqueness of
this solution as well as the convergence rate of this numerical method.
Finally, in Section
5
, as an application, we propose a numerical scheme
for an optimal control problem, based on our finite transposition
method.

2
Adjoint equations for stochastic distributed parameter control problems

We begin with some notations and notions
to be used later.
Let
T>0
and
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbf{F},{\mathbb{P}})

(with
\mathbf{F}=\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
) be a
filtered probability space so that
(\Omega,{\cal F},{\mathbb{P}})
is
complete,
{\cal F}_{0}
contains all
{\mathbb{P}}
-null sets
in
{\cal F}
, and
\mathbf{F}
is right continuous.
Let
\{W(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
be an

\mathbf{F}
-adapted
1
-dimensional standard
Brownian motion on
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbf{F},{\mathbb{P}})
.
Denote by
{\mathbb{F}}
the progressive

\sigma
-field (in
[0,T]\times\Omega
)
with respect to
\mathbf{F}
, by
{\mathbb{E}}\xi
the (mathematical) expectation of an
integrable random variable

\xi:(\Omega,{\cal F},{\mathbb{P}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and by

{\cal C}
a generic positive constant,
which may vary from one place
to another.

For a Banach space
{\cal X}
, we write
\|\cdot\|_{\cal X}
for its norm. If
{\cal X}
is a Hilbert space, we denote its inner product by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\cal X}
(When
{\cal X}
is a
Euclidean space, for simplicity, we denote its norm and inner product by
|\cdot|
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, respectively).
For any filtration
\mathbf{G}=\{{\cal G}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}\subseteq\mathbf{F}
,

p,q\in[1,\infty)
and
t\in[0,T]
, let

L_{{\cal G}_{t}}^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X})\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}L^{p}(\Omega,{\cal G}_{t},{\mathbb{P}};{\cal X})
, and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle L^{q}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\Big\{\varphi:(0,T)\times\Omega\!\to{\cal X}\,\bigm|\,\varphi(\cdot)\hbox{
is $\mathbf{F}$-adapted}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{ and
}\int_{0}^{T}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\|\varphi(t)\|_{\cal X}^{p}\Big)^{\frac{q}{p}}dt<\infty\Big\},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle L^{p}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\cal X}))\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\Big\{\varphi:[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\cal X}\,\bigm|\,\varphi(\cdot)\hbox{
is continuous, }\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathbf{F}\mbox{-adapted and
}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{C([0,T];{\cal X})}^{p}\big)<\infty\Big\},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle C_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\Big\{\varphi:[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\cal X}\,\bigm|\,\varphi(\cdot)\hbox{
is $\mathbf{F}$-adapted }\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{ and }\varphi(\cdot):[0,T]\to L^{p}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;{\cal X})\mbox{ is
continuous}\Big\}.\end{array}

In a similar way, we can define

L^{\infty}_{{\cal G}_{t}}(\Omega;{\cal X})
and
L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega;{\cal X})).

One can show that, for
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
,

L^{p}_{{\cal G}_{t}}(\Omega;{\cal X})
and
L^{q}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))
are Banach spaces (with the canonical norms).
Further, both
L^{p}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\cal X}))
and

C_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))
are
Banach spaces with norms given by

\|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{L^{p}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\cal X}))}=\big({\mathbb{E}}(\|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{C([0,T];{\cal X})}^{p})\!\big)^{1/p}
and

\|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{C_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))}=\max_{t\in[0,T]}\big({\mathbb{E}}(\|\varphi(t)\|_{\cal X}^{p})\big)^{1/p}
,
respectively.
Also, we write

D_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))
for the
Banach space of all

L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X})
-valued,

\mathbf{F}
-adapted, càdlàg
stochastic processes
\varphi(\cdot)
such
that

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\big({\mathbb{E}}(\|\varphi(t)\|_{\cal X}^{p})\big)^{1/p}<\infty
, with the canonical norm.
In the
sequel, we shall simply denote

L^{p}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))
by

L^{p}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal X})
; and further simply denote
L^{p}_{{\cal G}_{t}}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}})
,

L^{p}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\mathbb{R}})
and
L^{p}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}))
by
L^{p}_{{\cal G}_{t}}(\Omega)
,
L^{p}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T)

and
L^{p}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T]))
, respectively.

In what follows, we assume that
H

and
U
are (real) separable Hilbert spaces, and that
A
is
an unbounded linear operator (with domain
D(A)\subseteq H
), which generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup

\{e^{At}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
H
. Write
A^{*}
for the
adjoint operator of
A
.
For any
y_{0}\in H
, consider the following
controlled linear stochastic evolution equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dy(t)=\big(Ay(t)+Bu(t)\big)dt+\big(Cy(t)+Du(t)\big)dW(t),\quad t\in(0,T],\\
&y(0)=y_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

In (
2.1
),
C(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal L}(H))
,
B(\cdot),D(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal L}(U;H))
,
u(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;U)
is the control
variable, and
y(\cdot)=y(\cdot;y_{0},u(\cdot))
is the
state variable. By the well-posedness result for
stochastic evolution equations

(e.g.,
Lü and Zhang 2020
, Section 3.2)
, the system
(
2.1
) admits a unique mild solution

y(\cdot)\in C_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
.

Now, we recall the notion of null
controllability of (
2.1
).

Definition 2.1
.

System (
2.1
) is called null
controllable at time
T
if for any
y_{0}\in H
,
there exists
u(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;U)

such that the
corresponding mild solution to (
2.1
)
satisfies
y(T)=0
, a.s.

Next, we introduce
the following quadratic cost functional

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\cal J}(y_{0};u(\cdot))=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\big(\big\langle Qy(t),y(t)\big\rangle_{H}+\big\langle Ru(t),u(t)\big\rangle_{U}\big)dt+\langle Gy(T),y(T)\rangle_{H}\Big],\end{array}

(2.2)

where
Q(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal L}(H))
and
R(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal L}(U))
are self-adjoint
operator-valued stochastic processes, while

G\in L^{\infty}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;{\cal L}(H))
is a
self-adjoint operator-valued random variable.
Consider the linear quadratic
optimal control problem (SLQ problem
for short) as follows:

Problem (SLQ):
For each
y_{0}\in H
, find a
\bar{u}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;U)
such
that

{\cal J}\big(y_{0};\bar{u}(\cdot)\big)=\inf_{u(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;U)}{\cal J}\big(y_{0};u(\cdot)\big).

(2.3)

Any
\bar{u}(\cdot)
satisfying (
2.3
)
is called an
optimal control
, the
corresponding state
\bar{y}(\cdot)
is called an

optimal state
, and
\big(\bar{y}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot)\big)
is called an
optimal pair
.

As in the deterministic case, in order to
solve the above null controllability
and optimal control problems, one may
employ the duality argument. For this
purpose, people introduce respectively the
following two backward stochastic
evolution equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dz(t)=-\big(A^{*}z(t)+C^{*}Z(t)\big)dt+Z(t)dW(t),\quad t\in[0,T),\\
&z(T)=z_{T}(\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)),\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dz(t)=-\big(A^{*}z(t)-Qy(t)+C^{*}Z(t)\big)dt+Z(t)dW(t),\quad t\in[0,T),\\
&z(T)=-Gy(T).\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

These two equations can be used to solve aforementioned two stochastic control problems respectively (see Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
).

As far as we know, the study of backward stochastic
evolution equations is stimulated by
the works

Bensoussan 1983
;
Hu and Peng 1991
. Now, backward stochastic
evolution equations and its
variants play fundamental
roles in the theory of stochastic distributed parameter
control systems

(
Lü and Zhang 2020
, e.g.,)
.

Since neither the usual
natural filtration condition nor the
quasi-left continuity is assumed for
the filtration
\mathbf{F}
, one cannot
apply the existing results on infinite
dimensional backward stochastic
evolution equations

(
Hu and Peng 1991
;
Al-Hussein 2009
, e.g.,)
to
obtain the well-posedness of the equations (
2.4
) and
(
2.5
) in
the sense of mild or weak solutions. Therefore, we shall employ the
stochastic transposition method,
developed first in our paper

Lü and Zhang 2013
for backward stochastic differential equations, then
in
Lü and Zhang 2014
for backward
stochastic evolution equations, to
study the well-posedness of the equations (
2.4
) and
(
2.5
).

Consider the following backward stochastic
evolution equation (in a general form):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dz(t)=-A^{*}z(t)dt+F(t,z(t),Z(t))dt+Z(t)dW(t),\quad t\in[0,T),\\
&z(T)=z_{T}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.6)

Here
z_{T}\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)
and

F:[0,T]\times\Omega\times H\times H\to H
is a
given function satisfying that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &F(\cdot,0,0)\in L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;H)),\\
&\|F(t,y_{1},z_{1})-F(t,y_{2},z_{2})\|_{H}\leq{\cal C}(\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{H}+\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{H}),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\;y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2}\in{H},\ \ \hbox{\rm a.e.{ }}(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.7)

To define the transposition solution to
(
2.6
), we introduce the following
stochastic evolution equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &d\varphi(s)=(A\varphi(s)+v_{1}(s))ds+v_{2}(s)dW(s),\quad s\in(t,T],\\
&\varphi(t)=\eta,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.8)

where
t\in[0,T]
,
v_{1}(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega;H))
,

v_{2}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;H)
and

\eta\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{t}}(\Omega;H)
. By
the classical well-posedness result for
stochastic evolution equations

(e.g.,
Lü and Zhang 2020
, Section 3.2)
, the equation (
2.8
)
admits a unique mild solution

\varphi(\cdot)\in C_{\mathbb{F}}([t,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{C_{\mathbb{F}}([t,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))}\leq{\cal C}\big(\|\eta\|_{L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{t}}(\Omega;H)}+\|v_{1}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega;H))}+\|v_{2}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;H)}\big).\end{aligned}

If (
2.6
) admits a classical
mild solution in

Hu and Peng 1991
;
Al-Hussein 2009
(say,
when
\mathbf{F}
is the natural filtration of
the Brownian motion
W(\cdot)
), then by
Itô’s formula, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big\langle\varphi(T),z_{T}\big\rangle_{H}-{\mathbb{E}}\int_{t}^{T}\big\langle\varphi(s),F(s,z(s),Z(s))\big\rangle_{H}ds\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big\langle\eta,z(t)\big\rangle_{H}+{\mathbb{E}}\int_{t}^{T}\big\langle v_{1}(s),z(s)\big\rangle_{H}ds+{\mathbb{E}}\int_{t}^{T}\big\langle v_{2}(s),Z(s)\big\rangle_{H}ds.\end{array}

(2.9)

Motivated by this, we introduce the
following notion.

Definition 2.2
.

We call
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))\in D_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))\times L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;H)
a transposition
solution to the equation
(
2.6
) if for any
t\in[0,T]
,
v_{1}(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega;H))
,

v_{2}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;H)
,

\eta\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{t}}(\Omega;H)
and the
corresponding solution
\varphi(\cdot)\in C_{{\mathbb{F}}}([t,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
to
(
2.8
), the equality
(
2.9
) holds.

The space for the
first component of the solution is
chosen to be
D_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
rather than
C_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))
. This is quite natural
because the filtration
\mathbf{F}
is
assumed only to be right-continuous.

It is easy to see that if
(
2.6
) has a mild solution,
then such a solution is also a
transposition solution to (
2.6
).

Remark 2.1
.

The above stochastic transposition method is stimulated by the
classical transposition method to solve the
non-homogeneous boundary value problems for
deterministic partial differential equations
(
Lions and Magenes 1972
, see, e.g.,)
.
This method is a variant of the
standard duality method, and in some
sense it provides a way to see
something which is not easy to be
detected directly. Specifically, for
the equation (
2.6
), the
point of this method is to interpret
the solution to a backward stochastic
evolution equation in terms of a
forward stochastic evolution equation
which is well studied. The key tool to do this is the Riesz type representation theorem for
L^{q}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega;{\cal X}))

(1\leq p,q<\infty)

(
Lü et al. 2012
;
Lü et al. 2018
, see)
. On the other
hand, the equality (
2.9
)
can be regarded as a variational formulation
of the equation (
2.6
). As
pointed out in
(
Lü and Zhang 2013
, Remark
3.2)
, it provides a way to
solve the equation (
2.6
)
numerically.

We have the following well-posedness result for
the equation (
2.6
).

Theorem 2.1

(
Lü and Zhang 2014
)
.

For any
z_{T}\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)
, the
equation (
2.6
) admits a unique
transposition solution
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))\in D_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))\times L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;H)
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))\|_{D_{{\mathbb{F}}}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;H))\times L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;H)}\leq{\cal C}\big(\|z_{T}\|_{L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)}+\|F(\cdot,0,0)\|_{L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega;H))}\big).

From Theorem
2.1
, it follows that both
(
2.4
) and (
2.5
) are
well-posed in the sense of transposition
solution. Then we can study the null
controllability and optimal control problems by
(
2.4
) and (
2.5
),
respectively.

We first consider the problem of finding the
control which drives the state of the system
(
2.1
) to rest. To this end, define
a functional on
L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(z_{T})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int^{T}_{0}\|B^{*}z(t)+D^{*}Z(t)\|^{2}_{U}dt+{\mathbb{E}}\langle y_{0},z(0)\rangle_{H},\quad\forall\,z_{T}\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H),

(2.10)

where
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))=(z(\cdot;z_{T}),Z(\cdot;z_{T}))
is the transposition
solution to the equation
(
2.4
) (corresponding to the final datum
z_{T}
).

The following result holds:

Theorem 2.2

(
Lü and Zhang 2020
)
.

If the system (
2.1
) is null
controllable at time
T
, then, among all
controls transferring the state of
(
2.1
) from
y_{0}
to
0
at time
T
,
the one given by

\displaystyle u(t)=B^{*}z(t;\hat{z}_{T})+D^{*}Z(t;\hat{z}_{T}),\quad\hbox{\rm a.e.{ }}(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega

(2.11)

has the minimal
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;U)
-norm, where
\hat{z}_{T}\in L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;H)
is the minimizer of
the functional
\mathfrak{J}(\cdot)
.

Remark 2.2
.

In this chapter, since we focus on the
numerics, we will not consider problem that when (
2.1
) is null controllable, which can be reduced to
an observability estimate of the
equation (
2.4
)

(e.g.,
Lü and Zhang 2020
, Theorem 7.17)
.

Theorem
2.2
provides an explicit
formula of the desired control for null controllability of (
2.1
). Therefore,
to get such a control, one has to solve the
following two problems:

(1)

Finding the minimizer
\hat{z}_{T}
of the functional
\mathfrak{J}(\cdot)
;

(2)

Solving (
2.4
) to obtain
B^{*}z(t;\hat{z}_{T})+D^{*}Z(t;\hat{z}_{T})
.

If we know how to solve the equation
(
2.4
), then we can adopt the
gradient method/Newton’s method to
find the minimizer of the functional

\mathfrak{J}(\cdot)
numerically. Hence, the key
point to compute the control (
2.11
) is
how to solve the equation (
2.4
).

Next, we consider Problem (SLQ). With the aid
of (
2.5
), we have the following
result.

Theorem 2.3

(
Lü and Zhang 2015
)
.

Let
(\bar{y}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot))
be an optimal
pair of Problem (SLQ). Then, for the
transposition solution
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))
to
(
2.5
) with
y(\cdot)
replaced by
\bar{y}(\cdot)
,
it holds that

R(t)\bar{u}(t)-B(t)^{*}z(t)-D(t)^{*}Z(t)=0,\quad\hbox{\rm a.e.{ }}(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega.

(2.12)

According to Theorem
2.3
, by (
2.1
) and (
2.5
), when
R(\cdot)^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\cal L}(U))
, in order for finding the optimal
pair
(\bar{y}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot))
of Problem (SLQ), it suffices to solve the following coupled forward-backward stochastic
evolution equation:

\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle d\bar{y}(t)=\big(A\bar{y}(t)+BR^{-1}B^{*}z(t)+BR^{-1}D^{*}Z(t)\big)dt\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\hskip 31.2982pt+\big(C\bar{y}(t)+DR^{-1}B^{*}z(t)+DR^{-1}D^{*}Z(t)\big)dW(t)&\quad t\in(0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle dz(t)=-\big(A^{*}z(t)-Q\bar{y}(t)+C^{*}Z(t)\big)dt+Z(t)dW(t)&\quad t\in[0,T),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\bar{y}(0)=y_{0},\qquad z(T)=-G\bar{y}(T).\end{array}\right.

(2.13)

Nevertheless, a key
point to solve (
2.13
) is to handle the backward equation (for
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))
) therein.

From the above discussion, we see that it is very
important to solve backward stochastic evolution
equations in the study of control problems
for stochastic evolution equations.
Note that, for almost all backward stochastic
evolution equations, it is impossible to find an
explicit formula of the solutions. Hence, for practical applications, it is
crucial to find numerical solutions to these
equations.

3
The space of finite transposition

In the following two sections, based on
the transposition method, we shall present the

finite transposition method
, introduced in
(
Wang and Zhang 2011
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
for backward stochastic differential equations, to find
numerical solutions for backward
stochastic evolution equations. To
present this method clearly, we only
consider the following backward
stochastic heat equation:

\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle dz(t,\!x)=\left(-\Delta z(t,\!x)\!+F(t,x,z(t,\!x),Z(t,\!x))\right)dt\!+\!Z(t,\!x)dW(t),&(t,x)\in[0,T)\times D,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle z(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\partial D,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle z(T,x)=z_{T}(x),&x\in D,\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

where
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
(for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
) is a bounded domain
with a
C^{2}
boundary
\partial D
.

Let us first introduce the following
two assumptions:

(A1)

\mathbf{F}
is the natural filtration generated by
\{W(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
,
z_{T}\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Omega;H_{0}^{1}(D))
, and
F:[0,T]\times D\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous with respect to
t
and Lipchitz continuous with respect to
z
and
Z
. Moreover,
F(\cdot,\cdot,0,0)\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(D))
.

(A2)
For the (equal time-interval) partition
\pi:\,0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
with
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\tau=\frac{T}{N}
and
t_{i}=i\tau
(for
i=0,1,\cdots,N
),
there exists a constant
{\cal C}>0
such that the correction term
Z
in the equation (
3.1
) satisfies

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\left(\|Z(t)-Z(t_{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|Z(t)-Z(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\right)dt\leq{\cal C}\tau.

(3.2)

Remark 3.1
.

(1).
The filtration
\mathbf{F}
can be generalized to quasi-left continuous one, and
F(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
can be
stochastic. But we do not consider these general case to avoid technical complexity.

(2).
In this chapter, we adopt the equipartition for simplicity. Our method can also be applied to quasi-uniform partition, i.e.

\displaystyle\max_{k=0,1,\cdots,N-1}\{t_{k+1}-t_{k}\}\leq{\cal C}\min_{k=0,1,\cdots,N-1}\{t_{k+1}-t_{k}\}.

(3).
In
Zhang 2004
, the estimate in the form of (
3.2
) is called the

L^{2}
-regularity of
Z(\cdot)
. Under
some suitable conditions, (
3.2
)
can be guaranteed
(
Wang 2016
;
Wang 2020
, see, e.g.,)
. When

Z(\cdot)
appears in the drift term,
(
3.2
) is crucial to prove rates of
convergence for temporal
discretization. If
Z(\cdot)
does not
appear in the drift term, when proving
the rate for
z(\cdot)
, we do not need the condition
(
3.2
)
(
Prohl and Wang 2020b
, see)
.

We shall use the following notations: For
k=0,1,\cdots,N-1
,
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}),

\Delta_{k+1}W=W(t_{k+1})-W(t_{k}),\;\;

(3.3)

\nu(t)=t_{k},\;\;\mu(t)=t_{k+1},\;\;\pi(t)=k,

(3.4)

and

\nu(T)=\mu(T)=T,\;\;\pi(T)=N.

The numerical method (based on the
transposition solutions to backward stochastic differential equations) given
in
Wang et al. 2020

(
Lü and Zhang 2013
;
Wang and Zhang 2011
, see also)

can be regarded as a Galerkin method, and hence we call it the
finite transposition method. Let us propose below the
outline of the finite
transposition method to solve the equation (
3.1
):

(1)

Determine a finite dimensional subspace
{\mathbb{S}}

of
L^{2}(D)
.

(2)

Choose a
finite transposition space
{\mathbb{H}}=\mbox{\rm span}\{e_{i}\}
,
which is a finite dimensional subspace
of
L_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{S}})
(in analogy with the finite element space of the finite element method in solving partial differential equations).

(3)

Introduce the following variational equation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle\varphi(T),z_{T}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\langle\varphi(t),F(\mu(t),\cdot,z(t),Z(t))\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}+\langle v_{1}(t),z(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}+\langle v_{2}(t),Z(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}\Big]dt,

where

\varphi(t)=\int_{0}^{\mu(t)}\left(\Delta\varphi(\nu(s))+v_{1}(s)\right)ds+\int_{0}^{\nu(t)}v_{2}(s)dW(s),

with
v_{1}(\cdot)
and
v_{2}(\cdot)
being stochastic processes in
suitable finite dimensional subspaces
{\mathbb{H}}_{1}\subset L^{1}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{S}}))
and
{\mathbb{H}}_{2}\subset L_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathbb{S}})
, respectively, and
\nu(\cdot)
and
\mu(\cdot)
being piecewise constant functions defined in (
3.4
).
Based on this variational
equation, one can prove the existence and
uniqueness of approximate solution
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))
, called the
finite transposition solution
, to the
equation (
3.1
) in the form

\displaystyle z(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\mbox{dim}({\mathbb{H}}_{1})}\alpha_{i}e_{i},\quad Z(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\mbox{dim}({\mathbb{H}}_{2})}\beta_{i}e_{i}.

(4)

Find coefficients
\alpha_{i},\,\beta_{i}
of the
finite transposition solution
via the variational equation (
3.5
),
and prove the rate of convergence.

Remark 3.2
.

There is a useful method — the stochastic finite
element method — to solve partial differential equations with random parameter
(
Ghanem and Spanos 1991
, see, e.g.,)
. A main ingredient in this method is the
orthogonal expansions, such as
polynomial chaos expansion,
Karhunen-Loève expansion, etc.
Let
\{{\bf e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be an
orthogonal basis for

L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;L^{2}(D))
. Then for any
stochastic process
X(\cdot)
, there
exists an expansion

\displaystyle X(\cdot)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}(\cdot){\bf e}_{i}.

Since the solutions
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))

to backward stochastic evolution
equations are progressively measurable stochastic
processes, people need to find the
orthogonal basis of

L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{2}(D))
. However, it
seems that there is no simple explicit
orthogonal basis for such a Hilbert
space. Note that
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{2}(D))
is much
more complicated than

L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega;L^{2}(D))
, and hence
the classical
“stochastic finite element method”
does not work for our problem.

Now we give details for the above
outline of the finite
transposition method.

Firstly, we determine a finite
dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(D)
based
on the Galerkin method. Define

A:\,D(A)=H_{0}^{1}(D)\cap H^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D),\quad A\varphi=\Delta\varphi,\quad\forall\varphi\in D(A).

(3.6)

Let

\{(-\lambda_{i},\phi_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be
the sequence of eigenvalues and
eigenfunctions of operator
A
, such
that
\|\phi_{i}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
for

i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
constitutes
an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(D)
and

\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is also an
orthogonal basis of
H_{0}^{1}(D)
.
Take
the subspace

{\mathbb{S}}_{m}=\mbox{span}\{\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{m}\},\qquad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.7)

Secondly, we construct a
finite transposition space
by the
Wiener chaos. To this end, we
review some notations and results on
Wiener chaos
(see
Nualart 2006
, for more details)
.
Recall that
{\cal F}_{T}=\sigma\{W(t);0\leq t\leq T\}
. Define the Itô isometry

\mathbb{W}:L^{2}(0,T)\to L^{2}_{{\cal F}_{T}}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(h)=\int_{0}^{T}h(t)dW(t).

For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let

\displaystyle H_{n}(x)=\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}),&n>0,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle 1,&n=0\end{array}\right.

be the
n
-th Hermite polynomial.
Denote by
{\cal I}
the set of all
sequences

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots)
,

\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, such that all
the terms, except a finite number of
them, vanish. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

{\cal I}(k)
be the set of all sequences
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{k}),\,\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. For all

\alpha\in{\cal I}\;\,({\it resp.}\;\alpha\in{\cal I}(k))
, write

\displaystyle\alpha!\equiv\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}!\;\,\Big({\it resp.}\;\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}!\Big),\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\alpha|\equiv\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\;\,\Big({\it resp.}\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\Big),

and for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
,

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(x)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}H_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}),\;\;\alpha\in{\cal I}\;\,\Big({\it resp.}\;\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}H_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}),\;\;\alpha\in{\cal I}(k)\Big),

which is called the generalized Hermite polynomial.
Also, we put

\displaystyle g_{i}(\cdot)=\frac{\chi_{[t_{i-1},t_{i})}(\cdot)}{\sqrt{\tau}},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,N.

Furthermore, for any
k=1,2,\cdots,N
,
define the
Wiener chaos of order

n
in
H
as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{n}(k;H)\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\hbox{\rm span$\,$}\Big\{\prod_{i=1}^{k}H_{\alpha_{i}}({\mathbb{W}}(g_{i}))\phi\;\Big|\;\alpha\in{\cal I}(k),\,|\alpha|=n,\,\phi\in H\Big\}.

Set
{\cal H}_{n}(0;H)=H
.
Take

\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})
, and
for
k=1,2,\cdots,N
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t_{k}}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\sigma\{\Delta_{1}W,\Delta_{2}W,\cdots,\Delta_{k}W\},

where
\Delta_{k}W
is defined in
(
3.3
).
By virtue
of the Wiener chaos of order
n
, we
have the following orthogonal
decomposition result.

Theorem 3.1

(
Nualart 2006
)
.

For any
N,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
k=0,1,\cdots,N
, it holds that

\displaystyle L^{2}_{{\cal G}_{t_{k}}}(\Omega;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})=\bigoplus\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal H}_{n}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m}).

Based on Theorem
3.1
, we set

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\cal H}^{M}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\bigoplus\limits_{n=0}^{M}{\cal H}_{n}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m}),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle{\cal H}^{M}_{N}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\hfil\lx@intercol\hbox{\rm span$\,$}\left\{\chi_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(\cdot)\xi\;\big|\;\xi\in{\cal H}^{M}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})\right\},\end{array}

and the desired
finite transposition space
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{m})\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\bigoplus\limits_{k=0}^{N-1}{\cal H}^{M}_{N}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})\subset L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;{\mathbb{S}}_{m}).\end{array}

(3.8)

For simplicity, we denote

{\cal H}_{n}(k;{\mathbb{R}})
,
{\cal H}^{M}(k;{\mathbb{R}})
,

{\cal H}_{N}^{M}(k;{\mathbb{R}})
and
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{R}})

by
{\cal H}_{n}(k)
,
{\cal H}^{M}(k)
,
{\cal H}_{N}^{M}

and
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}
, respectively.
We write
\big({\cal H}^{M}(k)\big)^{\otimes n}
and
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}^{\otimes n}

for the
n
-copies of
{\cal H}^{M}(k)
and
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}
, respectively.

Remark 3.3
.

In Theorem
3.1
, we only list a decomposition of
L^{2}_{{\cal G}_{t_{k}}}(\Omega;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})
, which is a subspace of

L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Omega;L^{2}(D))
. For the latter space, by letting

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{n}(L^{2}(D))\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\hbox{\rm span$\,$}\Big\{

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}H_{\alpha_{i}}({\mathbb{W}}(g_{i}))\phi_{j}\;\Big|\;\alpha\in{\cal I},\,|\alpha|=n,\,\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\mbox{ is an orthonormal }

\displaystyle\mbox{ basis of }L^{2}(0,T),\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\mbox{ is an orthonormal basis of }L^{2}(D)\Big\},

we have

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Omega;L^{2}(D))=\bigoplus\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal H}_{n}(L^{2}(D)).

For the projection operator

\Gamma_{M}:L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Omega;L^{2}(D))\rightarrow\bigoplus\limits_{n=0}^{M}{\cal H}_{n}(L^{2}(D)),

(3.9)

it is easy to check
that

\Gamma_{M}\big(L^{2}_{{\cal G}_{T}}(\Omega;{\mathbb{S}}_{m}))={\cal H}^{M}(N;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})
. This fact is
crucial in the sequel.

Remark 3.4
.

By the construction of the finite transposition space
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}
, it is easy to see that the orthonormal
basis of
{\cal H}^{M}(k)
is

\{h_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{k}}=\bigg\{\sqrt{\alpha!}\prod_{i=1}^{k}H_{\alpha_{i}}({\mathbb{W}}(g_{i}))\;\Big|\;\alpha\in{\cal I}(k),|\alpha|\leq M\bigg\},

which is a finite dimensional subspace of
L^{2}_{{\cal G}_{t_{k}}}(\Omega)
.
Note that
M_{k}
depends on
M
. For example, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle M_{k}=\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle k+1,&M=1,\\
\displaystyle\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2},&M=2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{k^{3}+8k^{2}+19k+6}{6},&M=3.\end{array}\right.

Hence the orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}
is

\{e_{k,i}(\cdot)\}_{k,i}=\left\{\chi_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(\cdot)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}h_{k,i}\;\Big|\;1\leq i\leq M_{k},0\leq k\leq N-1\right\},

which is also a finite dimensional
subspace of
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T)
.

By virtue of
\{h_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{k}}
,

\{e_{k,i}(\cdot)\}_{1\leq i\leq M_{k},0\leq k\leq N-1}
and

\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, we can obtain the
orthonormal basis of
{\cal H}^{M}(k;{\mathbb{S}}_{m})

and
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{m})
.

Remark 3.5
.

In this chapter, since we mainly focus on presenting the idea of handling the difficulties caused by the stochastic setting, we choose the finite
dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(D)
given by (
3.7
). One can also choose that space as the one in other numerical methods for solving deterministic partial differential equations, such as the finite element space.

For the infinite dimensional space of
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(0,T)
, subspace constructed by characteristic functions instead of
Wiener chaos is also a choice
(
Dai et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
.

Remark 3.6
.

A Wiener chaos decomposition method is proposed in
Briand and Labart 2014
.
It should be pointed out that, although in both
Briand and Labart 2014
and the present chapter, some tools of
Wiener chaos expansion are used, the numerical method in
Briand and Labart 2014
(based on Malliavin analysis) is
totally different from the finite transposition method in this chapter (based on the variational equation (
3.5
)).

4
Finite transposition method for backward stochastic evolution equations

Based on the finite transposition space
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
, we can provide the definition of the finite transposition
solution.

Definition 4.1
.

For any
N,M,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a couple
(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})\times{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M-1}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
is called a finite transposition
solution to the equation (
3.1
), if for any

v_{1}(\cdot)\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
and
v_{2}(\cdot)\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M-1}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
, the following variational equation
holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle\varphi(T),z_{N,T}^{\pi}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\left\langle\varphi(t),F(\mu(t),\cdot,z(t),Z(t))\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}+\left\langle v_{1}(t),z(t)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}+\left\langle v_{2}(t),Z(t)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}\Big]dt.

Here
z^{\pi}_{N,T}(\in{\cal H}^{M}(N;{\mathbb{S}}_{n}))

is an approximation of
z_{T}
, and
\varphi(\cdot)
solves the following forward equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi(t_{k})=\int_{0}^{t_{k+1}}\left(A\varphi(\nu(t))+v_{1}(t)\right)dt+\int_{0}^{t_{k}}v_{2}(t)dW(t),\quad k=1,2,\cdots,N-1,\\
&\varphi(0)=0,\\
&\varphi(t)=\varphi(\nu(t)),\quad t\in(0,T],\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2)

where
A
is defined in (
3.6
).

The finite transposition method for equation (
3.1
) is the algorithm
to obtain the finite transposition solution by the variational equation (
4.1
).

The rest of this section is divided into two subsections. In the first one, we discretize
the original equation with respect to space variables. In the second one, we discretize the obtained equation with respect to the time
variable.

4.1
Spacial discretization of (
3.1
)

Denote by
\Pi_{n}
the orthognonal projection from
L^{2}(D)
to
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
and define
A_{n}
by

A_{n}=A|_{{\mathbb{S}}_{n}}
.
The semi-discrete problem in space corresponding to the equation (
3.1
) is to find
a pair
(z_{n}(\cdot),Z_{n}(\cdot))\in L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\mathbb{S}}_{n}))\times L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;{\mathbb{S}}_{n})
solving the following equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dz_{n}(t)=\big(-A_{n}z_{n}(t)+\Pi_{n}F(t,z_{n}(t),Z_{n}(t))\big)\mathrm{d}t+Z_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
\displaystyle&z_{n}(T)=\Pi_{n}z_{T}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.3)

The following result states the rate
of convergence for the solution of the equation (
4.3
) to the one of the equation (
3.1
).

Theorem 4.1

(
Wang 2013
,
Wang 2016
)
.

Suppose that
{\rm(A1)}
holds. Let

(z(\cdot),Z(\cdot))
and

(z_{n}(\cdot),Z_{n}(\cdot))
be solutions to
the equations (
3.1
) and
(
4.3
), respectively. Then the
following estimate holds

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|z(\cdot)-z_{n}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];L^{2}(D)))\cap L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(D))}+\|Z(\cdot)-Z_{n}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;L^{2}(D))}\\
&\leq\frac{{\cal C}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\Big(\|z_{T}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Omega;H_{0}^{1}(D))}+\|F(\cdot,\cdot,0,0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(D))}\Big).\end{aligned}

As we have explain in Remark
3.5
, to figure out the main part,
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
in the finite transposition space
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
is constructed by the first
n

eigenfunctions of the operator
A
. We can also choose other finite element spaces
{\mathbb{V}}_{h}
, and construct
the finite transposition space
{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{V}}_{h})
. The readers are referred to
Dunst and Prohl 2016
;
Prohl and Wang 2020b

for error estimates for finite-element based spacial discretization.

4.2
Temporal discretization of (
4.3
)

In this part, firstly we rewrite the
definition of the finite transposition
solution and the finite transposition
method for the backward stochastic
differential equation (
4.3
).
Then we present the existence and
uniqueness of the finite transposition
solution. Finally, we give the rate
of convergence.

Since
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}=\mbox{span}\{\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{n}\}
, and the solution
(z_{n}(\cdot),Z_{n}(\cdot))
to the
equation (
4.3
) is
in the space
L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T];{\mathbb{S}}_{n}))\times L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T;{\mathbb{S}}_{n})
, we may take
(z_{n}(\cdot),Z_{n}(\cdot))
to be the following form

\displaystyle z_{n}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{n,j}(\cdot)\phi_{j},\quad Z_{n}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{n,j}(\cdot)\phi_{j},

(4.4)

where
a_{n,j}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(\Omega;C([0,T]))
and
b_{n,j}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(0,T)
, for
j=1,2,\cdots,n
.
Set

\displaystyle a_{n}(\cdot)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{n,1}(\cdot)\\
a_{n,2}(\cdot)\\
\vdots\\
a_{n,n}(\cdot)\end{pmatrix},\quad b_{n}(\cdot)=\begin{pmatrix}b_{n,1}(\cdot)\\
b_{n,2}(\cdot)\\
\vdots\\
b_{n,n}(\cdot)\end{pmatrix},\quad\Lambda_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-\lambda_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&-\lambda_{n}\\
\end{pmatrix},

and

\displaystyle\negthinspace\negthinspace\negthinspace b_{n,N,M-1}(\cdot)\negthinspace\negthinspace\negthinspace

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{k}^{\prime}}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big\langle\Lambda_{0}^{N-k-1}\frac{\Delta_{k+1}W}{\sqrt{\tau}}h_{k,i}^{\ell},a_{n,T}^{\pi}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int_{t_{k+1}}^{T}\Lambda_{0}^{\pi(t)-k}\Gamma_{M}F_{n}(\mu(t),a_{n,N,M}(t),b_{n,N,M-1}(t))dt\Big]\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\chi_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(\cdot)\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\Delta_{k+1}W}{\tau}a_{n,N,M}(t_{k+1})\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}\Big),

which is (

Step 3.
In this step, by means of (

) and (

5
Numerical method for optimal controls

In this section, we present an
application of the finite transposition
method. To avoid technical complexity,
we consider a simple SLQ problem. To
be specific, we consider a cost
functional

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(y_{0};u(\cdot))=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\big(\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|u(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\big)dt+\|y(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\Big]

subject to the (controlled forward)
stochastic heat equation with
an additive noise

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dy(t)=\big(Ay(t)+u(t)\big)dt+\sigma dW(t),\quad t\in(0,T],\\
&y(0)=y_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.1)

Here
A
is defined in (
3.6
), and

y_{0},\,\sigma\in H_{0}^{1}(D)\cap H^{2}(D)
. Let
us now state a SLQ problem as follows:

Problem (SLQ)
. Search for

\bar{u}(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{2}(D))
,
such that

\displaystyle{\cal J}(y_{0};\bar{u}(\cdot))=\inf_{u(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;L^{2}(D))}{\cal J}(y_{0};u(\cdot)).

By
Lü and Zhang 2015
, the solvability
of Problem (SLQ) is equivalent to the
solvability of the following
forward-backward stochastic evolution
equation:

\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle d\bar{y}(t)=\big(A\bar{y}(t)+\bar{u}(t)\big)dt+\sigma dW(t),&t\in(0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle dz(t)=\big(-Az(t)+\bar{y}(t)\big)dt+Z(t)dW(t),&t\in[0,T),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\bar{y}(0)=y_{0},\qquad z(T)=-\bar{y}(T),&\end{array}\right.

(5.2)

with the condition

\bar{u}(t)-z(t)=0,\quad\hbox{\rm a.e.{ }}(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega.

(5.3)

In the following, we mainly propose the
discretization of
(
5.2
)-(
5.3
). To be
specific, for the forward equation
(
5.2
)
1
, we adopt the
time-implicit Galerkin method
(
Grecksch and Kloeden 1996
, see, e.g.,)
, while
for the backward one (
5.2
)
2
,
we apply the finite transposition
method proposed in Section
4
;
see also Algorithm
1
below.

Since (
5.2
)-(
5.3
) is
coupled, how to obtain the convergence
of this discretization strategy?
In
what follows, we adopt optimal control
theory to deduce the convergence rates.
To do this, we discretize Problem (SLQ)
within two steps: firstly, we get the
spacial semi-discretization (which is
referred to as Problem (SLQ)
S
); secondly, we obtain a
spatio-temporal discretization (which
is referred to as Problem
(SLQ)
ST
). Now, we present these two
discretizations.

Problem (SLQ)
S
.
For a
fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, minimize the
following cost functional over

L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;

{\mathbb{S}}_{n})
:

\displaystyle{\cal J}(\Pi_{n}y_{0};u(\cdot))=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\big(\|y_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|u_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big)dt+\|y_{n}(T)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\Big]

subject to the following

{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
-valued stochastic differential
equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dy_{n}(t)=\big(A_{n}y_{n}(t)+u_{n}(t)\big)dt+\Pi_{n}\sigma dW(t),\quad t\in[0,T],\\
&y_{n}(0)=\Pi_{n}y_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle y_{0},\phi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}\phi_{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.4)

By
Yong and Zhou 1999
, the solvability
of Problem (SLQ)
S
is equivalent to
the solvability of the following
forward-backward stochastic
differential equation:

\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle d\bar{y}_{n}(t)=\big(A_{n}\bar{y}_{n}(t)+\bar{u}_{n}(t)\big)dt+\Pi_{n}\sigma dW(t),&t\in(0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle dz_{n}(t)=\big(-A_{n}z_{n}(t)+\bar{y}_{n}(t)\big)dt+Z_{n}(t)dW(t),&t\in[0,T),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\bar{y}_{n}(0)=\Pi_{n}y_{0},\qquad z_{n}(T)=-\bar{y}_{n}(T),&\end{array}\right.

(5.5)

with the condition

\bar{u}_{n}(t)-z_{n}(t)=0,\quad\hbox{\rm a.e.{ }}(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega.

(5.6)

The following result is on the rate of
convergence for spacial
discretization of Problem (SLQ).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(\bar{y}(\cdot),\,\bar{u}(\cdot))
be the optimal pair of Problem
(SLQ) and
(\bar{y}_{n}(\cdot),\,\bar{u}_{n}(\cdot))
be the solution of Problem
(SLQ)
S
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, there
exists a constant
{\cal C}
such that

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\bar{u}(t)-\bar{u}_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big)\leq\frac{{\cal C}}{\lambda_{n+1}},

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\bar{y}(t)-\bar{y}_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big)+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\bar{y}(t)-\bar{y}_{n}(t)\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}dt\leq\frac{{\cal C}}{\lambda_{n+1}}.

Proof.

Applying Itô’s
formula to
\|\nabla\bar{y}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|\bar{y}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{y}(t)\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}\leq{\cal C}\Big[\|y_{0}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|\bar{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\right)dt\Big].

On the other hand,
Theorem
4.1
yields that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|z(t)-z_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big)\leq\frac{{\cal C}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\|\bar{y}(T)\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\bar{y}(t)\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}dt\Big).\end{aligned}

The maximum conditions (
5.3
)
and (
5.6
) and above two inequalities lead to assertion (i).

Itô’s formula to

\|\bar{y}(\cdot)-\bar{y}_{n}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}
,
Gronwall’s inequality and assertion (i) lead to

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{y}(t)-\bar{y}_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla(\bar{y}(s)-\bar{y}_{n}(s))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}ds

(5.7)

\displaystyle\leq{\cal C}\Big[\|y_{0}-\Pi_{n}y_{0}\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\big[\|\bar{u}(s)-\bar{u}_{n}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}+\|\sigma-\Pi_{n}\sigma\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\big]ds\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\cal C}}{\lambda_{n+1}}.

Applying Itô’s formula to
\|\bar{y}(\cdot)-\bar{y}_{n}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}
again, then using Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and (
5.7
),
we can derive assertion (ii).
∎

Before presenting the spatio-temporal
discretization of Problem (SLQ), we
introduce the following two spaces:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\left\{x(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(0,T;{\mathbb{S}}_{n}\big)\;\big|\;x(t)=x(t_{k}),\,\,\forall t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}),\,\,k=0,1,\cdots,\,N-1\right\},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\left\{u(\cdot)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(0,T;{\mathbb{S}}_{n}\big)\;\big|\;u(t)=u(t_{k}),\,\,\forall t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}),\,\,k=0,1,\cdots,\,N-1\right\},\end{array}

and for any
x(\cdot)\in{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}
and
u(\cdot)\in{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\|x(\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\Big(\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\|x(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}(D)}\Big)^{1/2},\qquad\|u(\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\Delta}}}\Big(\tau\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\|u(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}(D)}\Big)^{1/2}.

Problem (SLQ)
ST
.
For fixed
N,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, minimize
the following functional over

{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\cal J}_{ST}(\Pi_{n}y_{0};u_{nN}(\cdot))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|y_{nN}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}}+\|u_{nN}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|y_{nN}(T)\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},\end{aligned}

where

(y_{nN}(\cdot),\,u_{nN}(\cdot))
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{nN}(t_{k+1})-y_{nN}(t_{k})=\tau\big[A_{n}y_{nN}(t_{k+1})+u_{nN}(t_{k})\big]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Pi_{n}\sigma\Delta_{k+1}W,\qquad k=0,1,\cdots,N-1,\\
&y_{nN}(0)=\Pi_{n}y_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.9)

The following result, which guarantees the
solvability of Problem (SLQ)
ST
, is
a variant of
(
Prohl and Wang 2020b
, Theorem
4.2)
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Problem (SLQ)
ST
admits a
unique minimizer
(\bar{y}_{nN}(\cdot),\bar{u}_{nN}(\cdot))\in{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}\times{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}
, which satisfies the following coupled equation
for
0\leq k\leq N-1
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bar{y}_{nN}(t_{k+1})=\Lambda_{0}\bar{y}_{nN}(t_{k})+\tau\Lambda_{0}\bar{u}_{nN}(t_{k})+\Lambda_{0}\Pi_{n}\sigma\Delta_{k+1}W,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle z_{nN}(t_{k})=\Lambda_{0}\mathbb{E}\left(z_{nN}(t_{k+1})-\tau\bar{y}_{nN}(t_{k+1})\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}\right),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\bar{y}_{nN}(0)=y_{nN}(0),\quad z_{nN}(T)=-\bar{y}_{nN}(T),\end{cases}

(5.10)

together with the maximum condition

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{nN}(t_{k})-z_{nN}(t_{k})=0\qquad k=0,1,\cdots,N-1,\quad\hbox{\rm a.s.{ }}\!\!,

where

(e.g.,

, Theorem

.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any

n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, assume that
(\bar{y}_{n}(\cdot),\,\bar{u}_{n}(\cdot))
and
(\bar{y}_{nN}(\cdot),\,\bar{u}_{nN}(\cdot))
are
the optimal pairs of Problem (SLQ)
S
and
(SLQ)
ST
, respectively. Then, it
holds that

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\bar{u}_{n}(t)-\bar{u}_{nN}(t_{k})\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}dt\leq{\cal C}\tau;

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bar{y}_{n}(t_{k})-\bar{y}_{nN}(t_{k})\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+\tau\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\bar{y}_{n}(t_{k})-\bar{y}_{nN}(t_{k})\right\|_{H_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}\leq{\cal C}\tau.

Remark 5.1
.

The equation (
5.10
) is the
implicit Euler scheme for the equation
(
5.5
) and the Galerkin based implicit Euler method for (
5.2
).
Notice that (
5.2
) and (
5.5
) are coupled forward-backward equations.
Numerical methods for these coupled equations and the convergence analysis is highly nontrivial. To deduce the rates of convergence, additional conditions such as sufficiently small
T
, or equations’ weak coupling, are needed
(
Bender and Zhang 2008
, see, e.g.,)
. In this section, we
show an idea to solve these equations. To be specific, if a coupled equation can be transferred
to an optimal control problem, one can try to utilize tools in optimal control theory.

The equation (
5.10
) is a coupled equation and contains conditional expectations. There are some methods to
deal with these conditional expectations. But for coupled forward-backward equation, even for that in finite dimensions,
numerical schemes are rare and the convergence analysis is far from complete
(
Bender and Zhang 2008
, see, e.g.,)
.
Here we can adopt the finite transposition method and gradient method to solve (
5.2
).

Algorithm 1

Solving Problem (SLQ) by Galerkin based finite transposition method and the gradient method

Fix
N,M,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\kappa>0
. Let
\widetilde{u}^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
. For any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
update
\widetilde{u}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
as follows:

1.

Compute
\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
via time-implicit Galerkin method:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}(t_{n+1})-\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}(t_{n})=\tau A_{n}\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}(t_{n+1})+\Pi_{n}\sigma\Delta_{n+1}W,\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,\\
&\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}(0)=\Pi_{n}y_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

2.

Utilize
\widetilde{y}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
in (
5.2
)
2
to compute
\widetilde{z}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
via the finite transposition method.

3.

Compute the update
\widetilde{u}^{(\ell+1)}\in{\mathbb{H}}_{N,M}({\mathbb{S}}_{n})
via

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}^{(\ell+1)}=\big(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}\big)\widetilde{u}^{(\ell)}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\widetilde{z}^{(\ell)}\,.

In Problem (SLQ),
\kappa
can be taken as
1+T+T^{2}
. We can
prove that
\left(\widetilde{u}^{(k)}(\cdot),\widetilde{y}^{(k)}(\cdot)\right)
converges to

\left(\bar{u}(\cdot),\bar{y}(\cdot)\right)
in
{\mathbb{X}}_{\tau}\times{\mathbb{U}}_{\tau}
as
k\to\infty

(e.g.,
Prohl and Wang 2020b
, Section
5)
.
