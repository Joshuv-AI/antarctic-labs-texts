On an extension of a theorem by Ruelle to long-range potentials
Aernout C.D. van Enter, Roberto Fernández, Mirmukhsin Makhmudov, Evgeny Verbitskiy

Abstract
. Ruelle’s transfer operator plays an important role in understanding thermodynamic and probabilistic properties of dynamical systems. In this work, we develop a method of finding eigenfunctions of transfer operators based on comparing Gibbs measures on the half-line ℤ + \displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+} and the whole line ℤ \displaystyle\mathbb{Z} . For a rather broad class of potentials, including both the ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic long-range Dyson potentials, we are able to establish the existence of integrable, but not necessarily continuous, eigenfunctions. For a subset thereof we prove that the eigenfunction is actually continuous.



1.
Introduction

One of the main issues of equilibrium statistical mechanics is to derive and describe the properties of the possible (global) states of a macroscopic system
starting from the
knowledge of the finite-volume (local) states of the system.
In order to give a mathematical framework to this problem, R. Dobrushin, O. Lanford, and D. Ruelle developed the so-called DLR formalism in the second half of the last century.

Shortly after their introduction of Gibbs measures,
Spitzer and Averintsev
[
1
,
33
]
characterised Gibbs measures for short-range potentials in terms of measures having Markov properties.
These results then were simplified and generalised, in various directions by Hammersley and Clifford
[
21
]
, by Sullivan, Kozlov
[
24
,
34
]
, and by Geoffrey Grimmett in his first paper
[
20
]
.

The novel DLR formalism was immediately adapted to the theory of Dynamical Systems by Ya. Sinai
[
32
,
31
,
30
]
.
There are, however, two important differences between the models typically studied in Statistical Mechanics and those studied in Dynamical Systems.
First, in Dynamical Systems one is typically interested in one-dimensional systems, that is, systems
with configuration spaces
\displaystyle E^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\displaystyle E
is the set of possible spin values and the spatial dimension
represents time. The conditional probabilities in Dynamical Systems are typically "one-sided" (from past to future), those in Statistical Mechanics "two-sided" (from outside to inside). For short-range potentials this does not make much of a difference, although in general regularity properties between one-sided and two-sided conditional probabilities may differ
[
3
,
4
,
36
,
37
,
13
]
.
Second, and perhaps more important, the natural description of dynamical systems often involves

half-line

configuration spaces
\displaystyle E^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, rather than
whole-line

configuration spaces of the form
\displaystyle E^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Already in his original papers, Sinai addressed these questions,
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
He showed that
Gibbs equilibrium states for
exponentially decaying interactions

are, in fact, equilibrium states for half-line potentials as well.
The issue of half-line versus whole-line Gibbsianness is, therefore, as old as the theory of thermodynamic formalism in Dynamical Systems.
For more recent results established in this area see, e.g.,
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
14
,
3
]
.
One of the most important Dynamical Systems tools used in the study of Gibbs equilibrium states is the
so-called Ruelle’s transfer operator
[
29
,
41
]
. Various probabilistic properties
of chaotic dynamical systems can be characterized in terms of transfer
operators. In particular, a central issue is to characterize those potentials
(interactions) for which transfer operators have positive continuous
eigenfunctions that is, finding those potentials for which Ruelle’s theorem holds.
This question has been answered by different authors
[
41
,
40
,
39
,
12
,
27
,
26
]
for different regularity classes of potentials.
In this paper, we answer it for potentials beyond these earlier studied classes.

Our paper is organized as follows:

•

In Section
2
, Section
3
, and Section
4
we introduce the notions of thermodynamic formalism that are important for this paper.

•

In Section
5
,
we discuss the relationship between half-line and whole-line Gibbs measures and formulate the first part of the main results (Theorem
A
and

B
).

•

In Section
6
, we discuss when whole-line Gibbs measures are absolutely continuous with respect to the product of two half-line ones, and we formulate the second series of our main results (Theorem
C
,

D

and

E
).

•

In Section
7
, the Dyson model, the main example of the paper, is discussed.
We then discuss what is the behaviour in other regimes of the phase transitions.

•

Section
8
and Section
9
are dedicated to the proofs of our main results and the final remarks.

2.
Basic notions: I. Specifications

The Dobrushin-Lanford-Ruelle definition of Gibbs states via specifications goes well beyond the standard lattices
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Consider the
lattice system

\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{L}}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
is an at most countable set (lattice)
and
\displaystyle E
is a set of possible spin values.
In this paper, we will focus on
finite

\displaystyle E
.
We denote the Borel
\displaystyle\sigma-
algebra of the measurable subsets of
\displaystyle\Omega
by
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
.
The specification is a
consistent
family of probability kernels (conditional probabilities) indexed by finite subsets
\displaystyle\Lambda
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
denoted by
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}
.
The consistency condition is the requirement that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}\gamma_{\Delta}=\gamma_{\Lambda}
for all
\displaystyle\Delta\subset\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}

[
18
, Chapter 1]
.

In the sequel, for
\displaystyle\Delta,\Lambda\subset\mathbb{L}
, we will denote
the concatenation of strings
\displaystyle\xi_{\Delta}\in E^{\Delta}
,
\displaystyle\eta_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}
by
\displaystyle\xi_{\Delta}\eta_{\Lambda}
, namely,
\displaystyle\xi_{\Delta}\eta_{\Lambda}
is a string such that
\displaystyle(\xi_{\Delta}\eta_{\Lambda})_{i}=\xi_{i}
if
\displaystyle i\in\Delta
and
\displaystyle(\xi_{\Delta}\eta_{\Lambda})_{i}=\eta_{i}
if
\displaystyle i\in\Lambda
.
Given the specification

\displaystyle\gamma=\{\gamma_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}}
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{L}}
, we say that a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle\Omega
is Gibbs for the specification

\displaystyle\gamma
or, equivalently, that
\displaystyle\mu
is consistent with
\displaystyle\gamma
, if

\mu(\sigma_{\Lambda}|\sigma_{\Lambda^{c}})=\gamma_{\Lambda}(\sigma_{\Lambda}\sigma_{\Lambda^{c}})\quad\text{for $\displaystyle\mu$-a.a. }\sigma\in\Omega,

or, equivalently, if the DLR equations hold:

\int\gamma_{\Lambda}f\,d\mu=\int f\,d\mu,

for all
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mu)
, and every
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}
, where

\gamma_{\Lambda}f(\sigma)=\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}}\gamma_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Lambda}|\sigma_{\Lambda^{c}})f(\xi_{\Lambda}\sigma_{\Lambda^{c}}),

note that for
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
and
\displaystyle\Lambda\subset\mathbb{L}
,
\displaystyle\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}
denotes the (infinite) string
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbb{L}\setminus\Lambda}
.
The set of all Gibbs measures for
\displaystyle\gamma
will be denoted by
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\Omega,\gamma)
.
For any probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
one can find at least one specification
\displaystyle\gamma
such that

\displaystyle\mu
is Gibbs for
\displaystyle\gamma

[
19
]
.
However, useful and interesting specifications have additional properties
such as finite energy (non-nullness) and quasi-locality (continuity).
We now turn to two particular ways of defining specifications as used in Statistical Mechanics and Dynamical Systems.

2.1.
Gibbs(ian) specifications in Statistical Mechanics

An interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
is a family of functions
\displaystyle\{\Phi_{\Lambda}\}
, indexed by finite subsets

\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}
, such that each function
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Lambda}
,
depends only on values of
\displaystyle\sigma
in
\displaystyle\Lambda
, that is, with a slight abuse of notation,
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Lambda}(\sigma)=\Phi_{\Lambda}(\sigma_{\Lambda})
. One needs to impose some additional summability conditions on
the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
:
\displaystyle\Phi
is said to be
uniformly absolutely convergent
(UAC) if
\displaystyle\sup_{i\in\mathbb{L}}\sum_{V\Subset\mathbb{L},V\ni i}||\Phi_{V}||_{\infty}<\infty
.
For an UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
, the specification (specification density)
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}=\{\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}}
is defined as follows, for
\displaystyle\omega,\eta\in\Omega
,

(1)

\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\eta_{\Lambda^{c}}):=\frac{e^{-H_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}(\omega_{\Lambda}\eta_{\Lambda^{c}})}}{Z^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\eta)},

where

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}(\omega):=\sum_{V\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset}\Phi_{V}(\omega)
is the Hamiltonian in the volume
\displaystyle\Lambda
, and
\displaystyle Z_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}
is a normalization constant (the partition function), i.e.,
\displaystyle Z_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}(\eta):=\sum_{\bar{\omega}_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}}e^{-H_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}(\bar{\omega}_{\Lambda}\eta_{\Lambda^{c}})}
.

It should be stressed that a Gibbsian specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
is always
quasilocal

[
2
,
35
,
18
]
.
In the current setting, in which
\displaystyle E
is finite, this property is equivalent to the fact that for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}
and
\displaystyle\omega_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\eta)
is a continuous function of the boundary condition
\displaystyle\eta\in\Omega
.
Another important property of the Gibbsian specifications is
non-nullness
, which means that for all volumes
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\eta,\omega\in\Omega}\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\eta_{\Lambda^{c}})>0
.

We denote the set of Gibbs states for the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
by
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\Omega,\Phi)
(or
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\Phi)
). It is a convex set —in fact a simplex— which is always non-empty if, as is the case in this paper, the spin space
\displaystyle E
is compact.

Depending on the symmetries of the lattice
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
and the spin space
\displaystyle E
, the interactions and specifications may also exhibit some symmetries. For example, if
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}
, then an interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle X:=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
is called
translation-invariant
if for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,
every
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in X
,
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Lambda+k}(\omega)=\Phi_{\Lambda}(S^{k}(\omega))
, where
\displaystyle\Lambda+k:=\{i+k:i\in\Lambda\}
.
Respectively, a specification
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle X=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
is called
translation-invariant
if for all
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{F}
,
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in X
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda+k}(B|\omega)=\gamma_{\Lambda}(S^{k}(B)|S^{k}(\omega))
.
Translation-invariant interactions give rise to translation-invariant specifications.
To some extent, the opposite statement is also true: Sullivan showed (
[
34
,
2
]
) that
for a quasilocal translation-invariant specification on
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
, one can find a translation-invariant
interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
such that
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma^{\Phi}
. Recently, however, it was shown in
[
2
]
that this interaction
is not necessarily uniformly absolutely convergent.

It should be noted that Gibbsian specifications can be uniquely recovered from a consistent family of
single-site probability kernels (densities)
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\{i\}}:\,i\in\mathbb{L}\}

[
14
]
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to study only the single-site densities of a Gibbsian specification instead of studying
all
densities.
Due to this fact, it is worth defining the single-site densities of a specification separately from the concept of specification as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

[
15
]

A collection
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{L}}
of positive functions
\displaystyle\gamma_{\{i\}}(\cdot|\cdot):E\times E^{\mathbb{L}\setminus\{i\}}\to(0,1)
is called the family of single-site densities of a specification if

(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{i}\in E}\gamma_{\{i\}}(a_{i}|\omega_{\{i\}^{c}})=1
for all
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega=E^{\mathbb{L}}
,
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{L}
,

(ii)

and for all
\displaystyle i,j\in\mathbb{L}
,
\displaystyle\alpha,\omega\in\Omega
the following holds

\frac{\gamma_{\{i\}}(\alpha_{i}|\alpha_{j}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}{\sum_{\beta_{\{i,j\}}}\frac{\gamma_{\{j\}}(\beta_{j}|\beta_{i}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})\gamma_{\{i\}}(\beta_{i}|\alpha_{j}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}{\gamma_{\{j\}}(\alpha_{j}|\beta_{i}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}}=\frac{\gamma_{\{j\}}(\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{i}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}{\sum_{\beta_{\{i,j\}}}\frac{\gamma_{\{i\}}(\beta_{i}|\beta_{j}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})\gamma_{\{j\}}(\beta_{j}|\alpha_{i}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}{\gamma_{\{i\}}(\alpha_{i}|\beta_{j}\omega_{\{i,j\}^{c}})}}.

The following theorem signifies the importance of single-site densities of a specification, and it will be useful later.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
14
]

Let
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{L}}
be single-site densities of a specification.
There is a unique non-null specification
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
having
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{L}}
as its single-site densities.
Furthermore,
\displaystyle\gamma
is quasilocal if and only if all functions in the collection
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{L}}
are continuous, and a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is consistent with
\displaystyle\gamma
if and only if it is consistent with all single-site probability kernels
\displaystyle\gamma_{\{i\}}
,
\displaystyle{i\in\mathbb{L}}
.

2.2.
Gibbs(ian) specifications in Dynamical Systems and Transfer operators

As already mentioned above, in Dynamical Systems, the ’
natural
’ lattice is the half-line
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Let
\displaystyle X_{+}=E^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be the space of one-sided sequences
\displaystyle\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in alphabet
\displaystyle E
.
We equip
\displaystyle X_{+}
with the metric
\displaystyle d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}[\omega_{n}\neq\omega^{\prime}_{n}]2^{-n}
.
We also define the left-shift
\displaystyle S
on
\displaystyle X_{+}
by
\displaystyle y=Sx
where
\displaystyle y_{i}=x_{i+1}
for all
\displaystyle i\geq 0
.
Let
\displaystyle\phi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function (potential). Following
[
10
,
9
,
41
]
, we define the corresponding specification
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi}:=\{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{[0,n-1]}^{\phi},\ n\geq 1\},

by

(2)

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(a_{0}^{n-1}|x_{n}^{\infty})=\frac{\exp\left((S_{n}\phi)(a_{0}^{n-1}x_{n}^{\infty})\right)}{\sum_{\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}}\exp\left((S_{n}\phi)(\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}x_{n}^{\infty})\right)},\quad\text{where \/}(S_{n}\phi)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi(S^{k}x).

This gives a family of probability kernels on finite intervals
\displaystyle[0,n-1]
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. However, the definition extends to general volumes
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
by

(3)

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(a_{\Lambda}|x_{\Lambda^{c}})=\frac{\exp{((S_{n+1}\phi)(a_{\Lambda}x_{\Lambda^{c}}))}}{\sum_{\bar{a}_{\Lambda}}\exp{((S_{n+1}\phi)(\bar{a}_{\Lambda}x_{\Lambda^{c}}))}},\;\;a_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda},x\in X_{+},

where
\displaystyle n=\max\Lambda
.

For
\displaystyle f\in C(X_{+},\mathbb{R})
, one has

(4)

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(f)(x)=\frac{\sum_{\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}}\exp\left((S_{n}\phi)(a_{0}^{n-1}x_{n}^{\infty})\right)f(\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}x_{n}^{\infty})}{\sum_{\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}}\exp\left((S_{n}\phi)(\bar{a}_{0}^{n-1}x_{n}^{\infty})\right)}.

It turns out that
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(f)
can naturally be expressed in terms of the Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius transfer operator. This is the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
acting on the space of continuous functions
\displaystyle C(X_{+},\mathbb{R})
as

(5)

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}f(x)=\sum_{y\in S^{-1}x}e^{\phi(y)}f(y)=\sum_{a\in E}e^{\phi(ax)}f(ax),

where the configuration
\displaystyle ax
is obtained by the concatenation of the letter
\displaystyle a
and the configuration
\displaystyle x
.
Thus, for any
\displaystyle n\geq 1
,

\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\phi}f(x)=\sum_{a_{0}^{n-1}\in E^{n}}e^{S_{n}\phi(a_{0}^{n-1}x)}f(a_{0}^{n-1}x),\text{ and hence, }\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(f)(x)=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\phi}f(S^{n}x)}{\mathcal{L}^{n}_{\phi}\mathbf{1}(S^{n}x)}.

One readily checks that the family of probability kernels

\displaystyle\{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}\}
has the standard properties of specifications;
most importantly, the consistency condition

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{m}(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(f))=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{n}(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{m}(f))=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}_{m}(f)

for all
\displaystyle f\in C(X_{+},\mathbb{R})
and every
\displaystyle m\geq n\geq 1
, and this particular property can be readily validated using properties of transfer operators
[
41
, Theorem 2.1]
.

The set of all Gibbs states on
\displaystyle X_{+}
for potential
\displaystyle\phi
, i.e., the set of measures consistent with the specification

\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi}
, will be denoted by
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
.
The set of Gibbs measures
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
is a closed convex set and the extremal points
of
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
are tail-trivial.

Transfer operators allow for a
dual view
on Gibbs measures
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
. Define the dual operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}
, acting on the space
of measures
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X_{+})
by

\int f\,d(\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}\nu)=\int\mathcal{L}_{\phi}f\,d\nu\quad\text{ for all }f\in C(X_{+},\mathbb{R}).

It is well-known that there exists at least one eigenprobability
\displaystyle\nu
on
\displaystyle X_{+}

for the maximal eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda=e^{P(\phi)}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}\nu=e^{P(\phi)}\nu,

where
\displaystyle P(\phi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{x\in X_{+}}\mathcal{L}^{n}\mathbf{1}(x)
is the
so-called topological pressure of
\displaystyle\phi
.
Combining the results of
[
41
, Corollary 2.3]
and
[
9
, Theorem 4.8]
, we can conclude that
the sets of probability eigenmeasures of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}
and the Gibbs states for
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle X_{+}
coincide:

\nu\in\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)\text{ if and only if \/}\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}\nu=\lambda\nu.

There is an interesting phenomenon in the theory of Gibbs measures on one-sided (half-line) symbolic spaces, which has no direct analogue in the two-sided (whole-line) context.
Let us say that a continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is quasi-normalized if
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}\mathbf{1}
is a constant function on
\displaystyle X_{+}
.
It turns out that
\displaystyle\phi
is quasi-normalized if and only if all Gibbs measures in
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
are translation invariant.

2.3.
Relation between Gibbsian specifications

Specifications discussed above are defined on different spaces:
\displaystyle X=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
vs
\displaystyle X_{+}=E^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
,
as well as, in different terms: namely, the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
vs the potential
\displaystyle\phi
.
What is the relation between these classes of specifications?

For a given interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
, the potential
\displaystyle\phi
should
be interpreted as minus the contribution to the energy from (the
neighborhood of) the origin
[
29
, Section 3.2]
,
[
35
, Section 2.4.5]
.
In fact there are multiple possibilities to define relevant
\displaystyle\phi
,
e.g.,

(6)

\phi(\omega):=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{|V|}\Phi_{V}(\omega_{V}),

or,

(7)

\phi(\omega):=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}(\omega_{V}).

In the setup of Thermodynamic Formalism, the second choice is particularly convenient, and it will be used in this paper.

It is worth mentioning that for every
\displaystyle\phi\in C(E^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}},\mathbb{R})
there exists a translation-invariant interaction
\displaystyle\Phi^{\phi}
on
\displaystyle X=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying (
7
) such that

\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{|V|}||\Phi^{\phi}_{V}||_{\infty}<\infty,

nonetheless, such
\displaystyle\Phi^{\phi}
does not need to be unique
[
29
]
.
The reciprocal property
would be more interesting, namely that for each
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+})
there would exist
a translation-invariant UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle X
satisfying (
7
).
Unfortunately, there exist counterexamples showing this to be false (c.f. Proposition
6.2
and Section
9
).

3.
Basic notions II: Ruelle’s theorem and Equilibrium states

Gibbs measures are eigenmeasures of the duals of transfer operators corresponding to the maximal eigenvalue.
What can we say about the eigenfunctions of transfer operators?
Ruelle established the first result
for
smooth potentials

[
27
,
29
]
: if
\displaystyle\phi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is Hölder continuous, then the transfer
operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
has a positive continuous eigenfunction
\displaystyle h
;
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}h=\lambda h
, with
\displaystyle\lambda=e^{P(\phi)}
. The existence of continuous eigenfunctions has been established for larger classes
of smooth potentials: for example, by Walters for potentials with summable variations (
[
40
,
39
]
). For less regular potentials – the so-called Bowen class – Walters
established the existence of bounded measurable eigenfunctions.

There is an interesting
principal
relation between the Gibbs measures
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
, eigenfunctions of transfer operators,
and translation invariant equilibrium states.

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider a continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+})
, and suppose

\displaystyle\nu\in\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
or, equivalently,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}^{*}\nu=e^{P(\phi)}\nu
.
Then

1)

If there exists a non-negative eigenfunction
\displaystyle h\in L^{1}(X_{+},\nu)
of the transfer operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}h=e^{P(\phi)}h
,
then
\displaystyle\mu=h\cdot\nu
(i.e.,
\displaystyle d\mu=hd\nu
) is a translation invariant equilibrium state of
\displaystyle\phi
:
namely,
\displaystyle\mu
is the measure satisfying the variational principle

h(S,\mu)+\int\phi d\mu=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X_{+},S)}\Bigl[h(S,\rho)+\int\phi d\rho\Bigr]=:P(\phi).

where
\displaystyle h(S,\cdot)
is the Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy, the supremum is taken over the set of all
translation invariant probability measures on
\displaystyle X_{+}
, and
\displaystyle P(\phi)
is the (topological) pressure of
\displaystyle\phi
.

2)

If there exists a translation invariant measure
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X_{+},S)
such that
\displaystyle\mu\ll\nu
, then
\displaystyle\mu
is an equilibrium state for
\displaystyle\phi
and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\displaystyle h=\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}
is the eigenfunction of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}h=e^{P(\phi)}h
.

Therefore, the condition that the transfer operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
has an eigenfunction for maximal eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda=e^{P(\phi)}
is equivalent to the existence of an equilibrium state for
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle X_{+}
having this eigenfunction as Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to some Gibbs state
\displaystyle\nu\in\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
.

We should note that the approach based on Proposition
3.1
has already been used in at least two particular cases: for Dyson potentials by A. Johansson, A. Öberg, M. Pollicott
[
23
]
, and for product-type potentials by L. Cioletti, M. Denker, A. Lopes, M. Stadlbauer
[
8
]
. Let us now recall these results. The Dyson potential
\displaystyle\phi^{D}:\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(8)

\phi^{D}(x):=\beta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{0}x_{n}}{n^{\alpha}},

where
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
is the inverse temperature and
\displaystyle\alpha>1
is the model parameter.
The Dyson potential
\displaystyle\phi^{D}
originates from the standard Dyson interaction
\displaystyle\Phi

(9)

\Phi_{\Lambda}(\omega):=\begin{cases}-\frac{\beta\omega_{i}\omega_{j}}{|i-j|^{\alpha}},&\text{ if }\Lambda=\{i,j\}\subset\mathbb{Z},\ i\neq j;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
\displaystyle\alpha>2

\displaystyle\phi^{D}
has summable variations, and, hence, the Ruelle-Walters theorem applies, and the transfer operator
has a unique positive continuous eigenfunction

]
. For
\displaystyle\alpha\in(1,2]

\displaystyle\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

]
, and no general
result in Dynamical Systems applies. The main result of
[

]
reads

Theorem 3.2
.

[

]

For
\displaystyle\alpha\in\big(\frac{3}{2},2\big]
and all sufficiently small

there exists a positive continuous eigenfunction of the Perron-Frobenius transfer operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi^{D}}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.2
holds for all
\displaystyle\beta<\beta_{c}^{1}
, where
\displaystyle\beta_{c}^{1}
is the critical value for a certain long-range Bernoulli percolation.
For further details see Section

and
[

]
.

In
[

, the authors introduced a product-type potential
\displaystyle\phi^{P}:\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\to\mathbb{R}

where again

and

while the unique equilibrium state
for

is the Bernoulli measure

on
\displaystyle\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
with

Theorem 3.4
.

[

(i)

For

4.
Basic notions III: Equivalence of interactions. Dobrushin uniqueness condition

4.1.
Equivalent specifications

We start this section with the notion of equivalent Gibbsian specifications; we follow closely the book by Georgii

[
18
, Chapter 7]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Two Gibbsian specifications
\displaystyle\gamma,\tilde{\gamma}
are called equivalent,
denoted by
\displaystyle\gamma\simeq\tilde{\gamma}
if there exists a constant
\displaystyle C>1

such that

c^{-1}\gamma_{\Lambda}(A\mid\cdot)\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(A\mid\cdot)\leq c\gamma_{\Lambda}(A\mid\cdot)

for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{F}
.

In particular, if
\displaystyle\Phi
and
\displaystyle\Psi
are both UAC interactions, and
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma^{\Phi}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma^{\Psi}
are the corresponding Gibbsian specifications, then

\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}\simeq\gamma^{\Psi}
if

(11)

\sup_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\left\|H_{\Lambda}^{\Phi}-H_{\Lambda}^{\Psi}\right\|<\infty.

The sufficient condition (
11
) certainly holds if the collection of sets

\left\{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}:\Phi_{\Lambda}(\cdot)\neq\Psi_{\Lambda}(\cdot)\right\}

is finite. In this case, we say that
\displaystyle\Psi
is a
finite perturbation
of
\displaystyle\Phi
, and vice versa.

The following theorem summarizes the properties of sets of Gibbs measures of two equivalent specifications.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
18
, Theorem 7.3]

Let
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}
be two equivalent specifications.
Then
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\gamma)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\tilde{\gamma})\neq\emptyset
, and in this case there is an affine bijection
\displaystyle\mu\leftrightarrow\tilde{\mu}
between
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\gamma)
and
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\tilde{\gamma})

such that
\displaystyle\mu=\tilde{\mu}
on
\displaystyle\mathscr{T}
.
In particular,
\displaystyle|\operatorname{ex}\mathscr{G}(\gamma)|=|\operatorname{ex}\mathscr{G}(\tilde{\gamma})|
.

4.2.
Dobrushin uniqueness condition and its corollaries

This is one of the most general criteria for the uniqueness of Gibbs states. We discuss it in the framework of a general countable set
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
of sites and a configuration space
\displaystyle\Omega:=E^{\mathbb{L}}
.
Consider a uniformly absolutely convergent (UAC) interaction
\displaystyle\Phi=\{\Phi_{\Lambda}(\cdot):\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}\}
on
\displaystyle\Omega
and let
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
be the corresponding Gibbsian specification. For any sites
\displaystyle i,j\in\mathbb{L}
, define

C(\gamma^{\Phi})_{i,j}:=\sup_{\eta_{\mathbb{L}\setminus\{j\}}=\overline{\eta}_{\mathbb{L}\setminus\{j\}}}||\gamma_{\{i\}}^{\Phi}(\cdot|\eta)-\gamma^{\Phi}_{\{i\}}(\cdot|\overline{\eta})||_{\infty},

where
\displaystyle||\cdot||_{\infty}
is the supremum norm on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
defined by
\displaystyle||\tau||_{\infty}:=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)}|\tau(B)|
for any finite signed Borel measure
\displaystyle\tau
.
The infinite matrix
\displaystyle C(\gamma^{\Phi}):=(C(\gamma^{\Phi})_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{L}}
is called the
Dobrushin interdependence matrix
.

Definition 4.3
.

The specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
satisfies the
Dobrushin uniqueness (contraction) condition
if

(12)

c(\gamma^{\Phi}):=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{L}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{L}}C(\gamma^{\Phi})_{i,j}<1.

The Dobrushin uniqueness condition admits a slightly stronger — and easy to check— form:

Proposition 4.4
.

[
18
, Proposition 8.8]

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
be any countable set, and suppose
\displaystyle\Phi=\{\Phi_{\Lambda}(\cdot):\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{L}\}
is such that

(13)

\bar{c}(\Phi):=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i\in\mathbb{L}}\sum_{\Lambda\ni i}(|\Lambda|-1)\,\delta(\Phi_{\Lambda})<1,

where
\displaystyle\delta(f):=\sup\{|f(\xi)-f(\eta)|:\xi,\eta\in E^{\mathbb{L}}\}
is the variation of
\displaystyle f:E^{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition.

The proof of Proposition
4.4
boils down to
showing that for all
\displaystyle i,j
,
\displaystyle i\neq j
,

C(\gamma^{\Phi})_{ij}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Lambda\ni\{i,j\}}\delta({\Phi_{\Lambda}})=:\bar{C}(\Phi)_{ij},

and hence

\displaystyle c(\gamma^{\Phi})=\sup_{i}\sum_{j}C(\gamma^{\Phi})_{ij}\leq\sup_{i}\sum_{j}\bar{C}(\Phi)_{ij}=\bar{c}(\Phi)
.
Notice that the non-negative matrix
\displaystyle\bar{C}(\Phi):=(\bar{C}(\Phi)_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{L}}
is symmetric.

Note that by transitioning to condition (
13
), we are reinforcing the primary condition of this paper, which is the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
12
), but with a particular purpose.
The condition (
13
) is stable under a
perturbation
of the underlying model/interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
.
Indeed, let
\displaystyle\Psi=\{\Psi_{V}\}_{V\Subset\mathbb{L}}
be an interaction such that for
\displaystyle V\Subset\mathbb{L}
, either
\displaystyle\Psi_{V}=\Phi_{V}
or
\displaystyle\Psi_{V}=0
, then it is straightforward to check that
\displaystyle\bar{c}(\Psi)\leq\bar{c}(\Phi)
.
Thus
\displaystyle\Psi
inherits the Dobrushin uniqueness condition from
\displaystyle\Phi
as long as
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies (
13
).

The crucial property of the Dobrushin uniqueness condition is that it provides the uniqueness of the compatible probability measures with the specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
.
In fact, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

[
18
, Chapter 8]
If
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
12
), then

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{G}(\gamma^{\Phi})\right|\leq 1
.

If
\displaystyle E
is compact, as is the case in this paper, there is always at least one Gibbs state; hence, the inequality becomes an equality.

The validity of Dobrushin’s criterion yields two important properties of the unique Gibbs state:
concentration inequalities and explicit bounds on the decay of correlations.

The first property involves the coefficient
\displaystyle\bar{c}({\Phi})
, and it provides the tail bounds for the unique Gibbs measure.
Let

(14)

\delta_{k}F:=\sup\bigl\{F(\xi)-F(\eta):\xi_{j}=\eta_{j},\;j\in\mathbb{L}\setminus\{k\}\bigr\}

denote the variation of a local function
\displaystyle F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
at a site
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{L}
, where
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{L}}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

[
25
]

Suppose
\displaystyle\Phi
is a UAC interaction satisfying (
13
) and let

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}
be its unique Gibbs measure.
Set

(15)

D:=\frac{4}{(1-\bar{c}(\Phi))^{2}}\;.

Then, for all
\displaystyle t>0
and every continuous function
\displaystyle F
on
\displaystyle\Omega
, one has

(16)

\mu_{\Phi}\Big(\Big\{\omega\in\Omega:\;F(\omega)-\int_{\Omega}Fd\mu_{\Phi}\;\geq\;t\Big\}\Big)\;\leq\;e^{-\frac{2t^{2}}{D||\underline{\delta}(F)||_{2}^{2}}},

where
\displaystyle||\underline{\delta}(F)||^{2}_{2}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{L}}(\delta_{k}F)^{2}
.

It is a well-known fact that (
16
) implies that
\displaystyle F
is
sub-Gaussian
and
\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}
has the
moment concentration bounds
as stated in the following theorem(
[
38
, Proposition 2.5.2]
).

Theorem 4.7
.

[
38
]

Assume that a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}
satisfies (
16
) with a constant
\displaystyle D=D(\mu_{\Phi})>0
.
Then:

(i)

\displaystyle\mu_{\Phi}
satisfies a
Gaussian Concentration Bound
with the constant
\displaystyle D
, i.e., for any continuous function
\displaystyle F
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{L}}
, one has

(17)

\int_{\Omega}e^{F-\int_{\Omega}Fd\mu_{\Phi}}d\mu_{\Phi}\leq e^{D||\underline{\delta}(F)||^{2}_{2}}.

(ii)

for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
and any continuous function
\displaystyle F
on
\displaystyle\Omega
, one has

(18)

\int_{\Omega}\Big|F-\int_{\Omega}Fd\mu_{\Phi}\Big|^{m}d\mu_{\Phi}\leq\Big(\frac{D||\underline{\delta}(F)||_{2}^{2}}{2}\Big)^{\frac{m}{2}}m\Gamma\Big(\frac{m}{2}\Big),

where
\displaystyle\Gamma
is Euler’s gamma function.

We present the second property in the particular setup
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}
, and it involves the
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
matrix

(19)

D(\gamma^{\Phi})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C(\gamma^{\Phi})^{n}\;.

The sum of the
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
matrices in the right-hand side converges due to the Dobrushin condition (
12
).

Proposition 4.8
.

[
16
,
18
]
Consider a UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle X=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(i)

Assume the specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
satisfies the Dobrushin condition (
12
) and let
\displaystyle\mu
be its unique Gibbs measure.
Then, for all
\displaystyle f,g\in C(X)
and
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(20)

\Big|\text{cov}_{\mu}(f,g\circ S^{i})\Big|\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k,j\in\mathbb{Z}}D(\gamma^{\Phi})_{jk}\cdot\delta_{k}f\cdot\delta_{j-i}g.

(ii)

Suppose
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies (
13
) and define the non-negative symmetric
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
-matrix by

(21)

\bar{D}(\Phi):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\bar{C}(\Phi)^{n}\;.

Then,

(22)

\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\bar{D}(\Phi)_{ij}\leq\frac{1}{1-\bar{c}(\Phi)}.

5.
Main results I: From half-line to whole-line specifications and measures

5.1.
From half-line to whole-line specifications

The main results of our paper are grouped into two parts. In the first part, we consider a
half-line potential
\displaystyle\phi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying minor
technical assumptions and we identify the natural translation-invariant specification on
\displaystyle X=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
which
extends

\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi}
.
In the second part we provide sufficient conditions for the
Gibbs/equilibrium state
on
\displaystyle X_{+}
to be absolutely continuous/equivalent with respect to the corresponding half-line Gibbs state
\displaystyle\nu
.
Then Proposition 3.1 allows us to conclude that the transfer operator admits an eigenfunction.

First, we consider the issue of whether there is a whole-line specification naturally associated with a half-line specification
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi}
. It turns out that under a very mild condition on
\displaystyle\phi
we obtain an affirmative answer.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the
extensibility
condition if for all
\displaystyle a_{0},b_{0}\in E
the sequence

F_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(x):=S_{n+1}\phi(x_{-n}^{-1}b_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})-S_{n+1}\phi(x_{-n}^{-1}a_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\bigl(\phi(x_{-i}^{-1}b_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})-\phi(x_{-i}^{-1}a_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})\bigr)

converges uniformly on
\displaystyle x\in X
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.

This condition has first appeared in
[
3
]
in connection
to a related question of whether
\displaystyle g
-measures are also Gibbs.
In terms of the extensibility condition, we can reformulate the main result of
[
3
]
as follows.

Theorem 5.2
.

[
3
]

A
\displaystyle g-
measure
\displaystyle\mu
(the natural extension) for a normalized function
\displaystyle g\in\mathscr{G}(X_{+})
becomes a Gibbs measure on
\displaystyle X
if and only if
\displaystyle\log g
satisfies the extensibility condition.

In
[
3
]
, the authors identified several sufficient conditions for
\displaystyle\phi
to satisfy the extensibility condition, such as the Walters condition and the so-called Good Future condition.
Note that a function
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+},\mathbb{R})
satisfies the Walters condition and the Good Future condition if
\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq 1}v_{n+p}(S_{n}\phi)=0
and
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}(\phi)<\infty
, respectively. Here

(23)

v_{k}(\varphi):=\sup_{x_{0}^{k-1}=y_{0}^{k-1}}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|

is the
\displaystyle k^{\text{th}}-
variation (or the oscillation in the volume
\displaystyle[0,k-1]
) of a function
\displaystyle\varphi:X_{+}\to\mathbb{R}

and

(24)

\delta_{k}(\varphi)\equiv\sup_{x\in X_{+},a_{k},b_{k}\in E}\left|\varphi(x_{0}^{k-1}a_{k}x_{k+1}^{\infty})-\varphi(x_{0}^{k-1}b_{k}x_{k+1}^{\infty})\right|.

is the oscillation of
\displaystyle\varphi
at the site
\displaystyle k
.
Interesting examples of potentials satisfying the extensibility condition are the Dyson potential (
8
) and the product-type potential (
10
).
Note that both potentials are in the Walters class if and only if
\displaystyle\alpha>2
. However, both satisfy the Good Future condition, and thus the extensibility condition as well, for all admissible values of the parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
, since
\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\phi^{D})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha})
and
\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\phi^{P})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha})
.

Gibbsianness for potentials satisfying the extensibility condition stems from the fact that they lead to a natural whole-line translation-invariant specification. Indeed, if
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+},\mathbb{R})
satisfies the extensibility condition the limits

(25)

\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{i\}}(\sigma_{i}|\omega_{\{i\}^{c}}):=\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{e^{S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\sigma_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{\infty})}}{\sum_{\bar{\omega}_{i}}e^{S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\bar{\omega_{i}}\omega_{i+1}^{\infty})}}

are well defined for all
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
. In turn, they lead to a full specification.

Proposition 5.3
.

There is a unique translation-invariant quasilocal non-null specification
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}
on
\displaystyle X
such that the functions (
25
) become its single-site densities.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, it is sufficient to check that
\displaystyle\{\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy conditions (i) and (ii) of Definition
2.1
. Clearly,
\displaystyle\{\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{i\}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the first condition
In order to check the second condition, consider arbitrary
\displaystyle i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\displaystyle i<j
, and
\displaystyle p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle i,j\gg-p
. Then
a straightforward computation shows that

\frac{e^{S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})}}{\sum_{\beta_{i,j}}e^{S_{j+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\beta_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\beta_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})+S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\beta_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})-S_{j+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\beta_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})}}=\\
=\frac{e^{S_{j+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\alpha_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})}}{\sum_{\beta_{i,j}}e^{S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\beta_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\beta_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})+S_{j+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\beta_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})-S_{i+p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{i-1}\alpha_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{j-1}\beta_{j}\omega_{j+1}^{\infty})}}.

The limit
\displaystyle p\to\infty
of this identity yields condition (ii).

Finally, note that for all
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{0\}}((S^{i}\sigma)_{0}|S^{i}(\omega)_{\{0\}^{c}})=\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{\exp{(S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{i-p}^{i-1}\sigma_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{\infty}))}}{\sum_{\bar{\sigma}_{i}}\exp{(S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{i-p}^{i-1}\bar{\sigma}_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{\infty}))}}=\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{i\}}(\sigma_{i}|\omega_{\{i\}^{c}})

and hence
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}
is a translation-invariant specification.
∎

Example 5.4
.

Let us illustrate the construction in the previous proof with the Dyson potential
\displaystyle\phi
defined in (
8
).
In this case a straightforward computation shows that for all
\displaystyle p\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\in\{-1,1\}
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\frac{\exp\left({S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{-1}\sigma_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\right)}{\exp\left({S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{-1}\bar{\sigma}_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\right)}=\frac{\exp\left(\beta\sum_{k=-p}^{-1}\frac{\sigma_{0}\omega_{k}}{|k|^{\alpha}}+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\sigma_{0}\omega_{k}}{|k|^{\alpha}}\right)}{\exp\left(\beta\sum_{k=-p}^{-1}\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{0}\omega_{k}}{|k|^{\alpha}}+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{0}\omega_{k}}{|k|^{\alpha}}\right)}.

Thus

\frac{\exp\left({S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{-1}\sigma_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\right)}{{\exp\left({S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{-1}\sigma_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\right)}+\exp\left({S_{p+1}\phi(\omega_{-p}^{-1}\bar{\sigma}_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\right)}\to\gamma_{\{0\}}(\sigma_{0}|\omega_{-\infty}^{-1},\omega_{1}^{\infty})

as
\displaystyle p\to\infty
, where

\gamma_{\{0\}}(\sigma_{0}|\omega_{-\infty}^{-1},\omega_{1}^{\infty})=\frac{\exp\left(\beta\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\sigma_{0}(\omega_{k}+\omega_{-k}}{k^{\alpha}}\right)}{\exp\left(\beta\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\sigma_{0}(\omega_{k}+\omega_{-k}}{k^{\alpha}}\right)+\exp\left(\beta\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{0}(\omega_{k}+\omega_{-k}}{k^{\alpha}}\right)}.

5.2.
Whole-line Gibbs measures as limits of half-line ones

Once the correspondence between
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}
is established, it is interesting to understand the relation between
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\gamma}^{\phi})\Big(=\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)\Big)
and
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X,\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi})
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\nu
is a Gibbs probability measure on
\displaystyle X_{+}
for some potential
\displaystyle\phi
satisfying the extensibility condition,
and consider an arbitrary measure
\displaystyle\rho
on
\displaystyle X_{-}=E^{-\mathbb{N}}
(e.g. a uniform Bernoulli measure),
and consider the measure
\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\rho\times\nu
on
\displaystyle X=X_{-}\times X_{+}
.

For any
\displaystyle n\geq 0
, let

\mu_{n}=\mu_{0}\circ S^{-n},

This is a sequence of Borel probability measures
\displaystyle\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
on the compact
metric space
\displaystyle X
, and hence it has weak
∗
-converging subsequences
\displaystyle\{\mu_{n_{k}}\}
. It turns out that their limits are bona fide whole-line Gibbs measures.

Theorem A
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\phi
satisfies the extensibility condition and

\displaystyle\nu\in\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\phi)
. Consider
\displaystyle\mu_{0}:=\rho\times\nu
, where
\displaystyle\rho
is uniform Bernoulli measure on
\displaystyle X_{-}
.
Assume that a subsequence
\displaystyle\{\mu_{n_{k}}=\mu_{0}\circ S^{-n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to a probability measure
\displaystyle\mu
in the weak
∗
topology as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
.
Then for
\displaystyle{\mu}
-almost all
\displaystyle x
:

\mu(x_{0}|x_{-\infty}^{-1},x_{1}^{\infty})=\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{0\}}(x_{0}|x_{-\infty}^{-1},x_{1}^{\infty})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\exp\left(S_{n+1}\phi(x_{-n}^{-1}x_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})\right)}{\sum_{\bar{a}_{0}}\exp\left(S_{n+1}\phi(x_{-n}^{-1}\bar{a}_{0}x_{1}^{\infty})\right)}.

Hence
\displaystyle\mu
is a whole-line Gibbs measure for the whole-line specification defined by the kernels

\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}_{\{0\}}
.

Now we turn to translation invariant measures.

Theorem B
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
A
, let
\displaystyle\mu
be a weak
∗
limit point of the following sequence of measures

\tilde{\mu}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu_{0}\circ S^{-i}.

Then
\displaystyle\mu
is a translation-invariant Gibbs measure for
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}):=\mathscr{G}(\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi})\cap\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,S)
.

We shall give the proofs of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
in Section

6.
Main results II: Eigenfunctions of transfer operators and absolute continuity of Gibbs measures

As mentioned in the introduction,
we can show the existence of an eigenfunction
if we can show that the equilibrium state
\displaystyle\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
the half-line non-translation invariant
Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\nu
.
This approach has recently been used by Johansson, Öberg and Pollicott
[
23
]
to show Theorem
3.2
. We also use this approach to prove the following theorem.

Theorem C
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi
be the Dyson potential (
8
). Suppose
\displaystyle\alpha>1
and
\displaystyle\beta>0
is sufficiently small.
Then,

(i)

the half-line Dyson model
\displaystyle\phi
on
\displaystyle X_{+}=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
admits a unique equilibrium state
\displaystyle\mu_{+}
and Gibbs state
\displaystyle\nu
;

(ii)

for all
\displaystyle\alpha>1
,
\displaystyle\mu_{+}
is equivalent to
\displaystyle\nu
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mu_{+}\ll\nu
and
\displaystyle\nu\ll\mu_{+}
, and thus the Perron-Frobenius transfer operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
has an eigenfunction in
\displaystyle L^{1}(X_{+},\nu)
;

(iii)

furthermore, if
\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{3}{2}
,
there exists a continuous version
of the Radon-Nikodym density
\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{+}}{d\nu}
, and thus the Perron-Frobenius transfer operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
has a continuous eigenfunction.

Remark 6.1
.

In fact, we prove Theorem
C
under the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (c.f. (
12
)-(

)).
Therefore, the parameter
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
should be sufficiently small so that the corresponding whole-line Dyson interaction on
\displaystyle X=\{\pm 1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
13
).

We will show a much more general statement (Theorem
E
below), from which one can easily obtain the first two parts of Theorem
C
.
Our method of proof of statement (iii) differs from that of
[

]
, and can be generalized to other models.
The proof of Theorem

will be presented in Section
8
.

6.1.
From
\displaystyle C(X_{+})
to the space of interactions on
\displaystyle X

It is clear from Section
5
that every (half-line) translation-invariant UAC interaction
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}
(i.e.,
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}_{\Lambda}\circ S=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}_{\Lambda+1}

) on
\displaystyle X_{+}
, yields a potential
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+})
with
\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}_{V}
, such that
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}=\gamma^{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}}
.

In the opposite direction, it is known that every potential
\displaystyle\phi
yields an equivalent interaction
\displaystyle\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\Phi}
on
\displaystyle X_{+}

[
29
]
which in its turn can be extended by translations to an interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle X
. This extension, however, is only guaranteed to belong to the so-called
\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{0}(X)
class —-i.e., is such that
\displaystyle\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\frac{1}{|V|}||\Phi_{V}||_{\infty}<\infty
— but it may fail to be UAC.

The determination of necessary and sufficient conditions for
\displaystyle\phi
to yield a UAC potential
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle X
is an open problem that we do not address here. Rather, in the sequel we determine a
class of potentials
\displaystyle\phi
that admit a translation invariant UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

(26)

\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}.

The following proposition shows that potentials in this class satisfy the extensibility condition.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in C(X_{+})
such that there exists a translation invariant UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}
.
Then
\displaystyle\phi
satisfies the
\displaystyle extensibility
condition.
Furthermore,
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}=\gamma^{\Phi}
.

Proof.

Note that

S_{n+1}\phi=-\sum\limits_{V\cap[0,n]\neq\emptyset;\;V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}.

Thus since the interaction is translation-invariant (
\displaystyle\Phi\circ S^{-n}=\Phi_{V-n}
)

S_{n+1}\phi\circ S^{-n}=-\sum\limits_{V\cap[-n,0]\neq\emptyset;\;V\Subset[-n,\infty]}\Phi_{V}.

(The LHS of the above equation should be considered on
\displaystyle X
, otherwise,
\displaystyle S^{-n}
is not defined on
\displaystyle X_{+}
.)

Pick any
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in X
, such that
\displaystyle\xi_{j}=\eta_{j}
if
\displaystyle j\neq 0
.
Then

S_{n+1}\phi(\xi_{-n}^{\infty})-S_{n+1}\phi(\eta_{-n}^{\infty})=\sum\limits_{V\cap[-n,0]\neq\emptyset;\;V\Subset[-n,\infty]}(\Phi_{V}(\eta)-\Phi_{V}(\xi))=\sum\limits_{0\in V\Subset[-n,\infty]}(\Phi_{V}(\eta)-\Phi_{V}(\xi)).

Since
\displaystyle\Phi
is UAC,
\displaystyle\sum\limits_{0\in V\Subset[-n,\infty]}[\Phi_{V}(\xi)-\Phi_{V}(\eta)]
converges uniformly to
\displaystyle H^{\Phi}_{\{0\}}(\xi)-H^{\Phi}_{\{0\}}(\eta)=\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}(\Phi_{V}(\xi)-\Phi_{V}(\eta))
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.
Thus it is also clear that
\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}=\gamma^{\Phi}
.
∎

Example 6.3
.

Consider the Dyson potential
\displaystyle\phi
(c.f. (
8
)) with the state space
\displaystyle E=\{-1,1\}
, and the standard (whole-line) Dyson interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
(c.f. (
9
)).
Then it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{\Lambda}.

6.2.
Decoupling across the origin

To every
UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
we can associate a sequence
\displaystyle{\Psi}^{(k)}
of interactions obtained by removing all bonds linking
\displaystyle-\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and adding them one at a time. Formally, we consider the family

\mathcal{A}=\bigl\{\Lambda\Subset Z:\,\min(\Lambda)<0,\ \max\Lambda\geq 0\bigr\}.

indexed according to some arbitrary order:
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots\}.

Then define, for each
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(27)

{\Psi}^{(k)}_{\Lambda}=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\Lambda\in\{\Lambda_{i}:i\geq k+1\},\\
\Phi_{\Lambda},&\quad\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\;

In particular,
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
has no interaction between the left and right half lines.
Clearly, all the constructed interactions are UAC and in addition have the following properties:

Remark 6.4
.

1)

Every
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}
in (
27
) is a local (finite) perturbation of
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
. Moreover, the sequence
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}
tends to
\displaystyle\Phi

as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
, in the sense that

\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}_{\Lambda}\rightrightarrows\Phi_{\Lambda}

for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
.

2)

For any finite volume
\displaystyle V
,

||H^{\Psi^{(k)}}_{V}-H^{\Phi}_{V}||_{\infty}\leq\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda_{j}\cap V\neq\emptyset\\
j\geq k\end{subarray}}||\Phi_{\Lambda_{j}}||_{\infty}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.

3)

The specifications
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Psi^{(k)}}
converge to
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}
as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
. More precisely, for all
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{F}
and
\displaystyle V\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,

\gamma^{\Psi^{(k)}}_{V}(B|\omega)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\gamma^{\Phi}_{V}(B|\omega)\;\text{
uniformly in the boundary conditions }\omega\in X.

4)

In addition, if
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}
is a Gibbs measure for
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}
, then by Lemma 4.2, any weak
∗
-limit point,
\displaystyle\mu
of the sequence
\displaystyle\{\nu^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a Gibbs measure for the potential
\displaystyle\Phi
.

Another important observation applies to the interaction
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
. As it is
constructed from
\displaystyle\Phi
by removing all interaction between
\displaystyle-\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the corresponding specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\Psi^{(0)}}
is of product type
[
18
, Example 7.18]
:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\Psi^{(0)}}=\gamma^{\Phi^{-}}\times\gamma^{\Phi^{+}}
, where
\displaystyle\Phi^{-}
and
\displaystyle\Phi^{+}
are the restrictions of
\displaystyle\Phi
respectively to the negative and positive half-lines. Thus,
the extreme Gibbs measures also factorize
[
18
, Example 7.18]
:

(28)

\text{ex}\;\mathscr{G}(\gamma^{\Psi^{(0)}})=\{\nu_{-}\times\nu_{+}:\nu_{-}\in\text{ex}\;\mathscr{G}(X_{-},\gamma^{\Phi^{-}}),\;\;\nu_{+}\in\text{ex}\;\mathscr{G}(X_{+},\gamma^{\Phi^{+}})\}\;.

6.3.
Absolute continuity

If
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
does not exhibit phase transitions, then by Theorem
4.2
, all the interactions
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}
,
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, do not exhibit phase transitions as well.
Let us denote by
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}
the unique Gibbs state for
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

If the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness criterion (
13
), so do all interactions
\displaystyle\Psi^{(k)}

and, furthermore,
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}
and
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
are equivalent with

(29)

\frac{d\nu^{(k)}}{d\nu^{(0)}}=\frac{e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}}{\int_{X}e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}d\nu^{(0)}}\quad,\quad\frac{d\nu^{(0)}}{d\nu^{(k)}}=\frac{e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}}{\int_{X}e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}d\nu^{(k)}}\;.

Proof.

The hereditary character of Dobrushin’s criterion follows from the obvious fact
that, for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\displaystyle\bar{c}(\Psi^{(k)})\leq\bar{c}(\Phi)
.

The proof of (
29
) follows, telescopically, from the partial Radon-Nikodym derivatives

(30)

\frac{d\nu^{(k)}}{d\nu^{(k-1)}}=\frac{e^{-\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}}}{\int e^{-\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}}d\nu^{(k-1)}}\quad,\quad\frac{d\nu^{(k-1)}}{d\nu^{(k)}}=\frac{e^{\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}}}{\int e^{\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}}d\nu^{(k)}}\;,

which is a particular case of the following elementary lemma.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\displaystyle{\Psi}
be a UAC interaction that does not exhibit phase transitions.
Consider a perturbed interaction of the form
\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}=\Psi+\mathfrak{P}
with
\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}
a finite interaction supported on
\displaystyle A\Subset\mathbb{Z}
.
If
\displaystyle\{{\nu}\}=\mathscr{G}({\Psi})
and
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}\in\mathscr{G}(\bar{\Psi})
, then
\displaystyle\bar{\nu}\ll{\nu}
, and

(31)

\frac{d\bar{\nu}}{d{\nu}}=\frac{e^{-H_{A}^{\mathfrak{P}}}}{\int_{X}e^{-H_{A}^{\mathfrak{P}}}d{\nu}}.

Proof.

First, note that by Theorem
4.2
, the model
\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}
does not exhibit phase transitions since it is a finite perturbation of
\displaystyle\Psi
. Thus
\displaystyle\{\bar{\nu}\}=\mathscr{G}(\bar{\Psi})
. By uniqueness of the Gibbs state, we have that, for both interactions, the Gibbs measures are achieved through limits

(32)

{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\xrightharpoonup[\Lambda\uparrow\mathbb{Z}]{}}}{\nu}\;\mbox{and}\ \bar{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\xrightharpoonup[\Lambda\uparrow\mathbb{Z}]{}}}\bar{\nu}

where
\displaystyle{\gamma}:=\gamma^{{\Psi}}
and

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}:=\gamma^{\bar{\Psi}}
are the Gibbsian kernels corresponding to the interactions
\displaystyle{\Psi}
and
\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}
and free boundary conditions (that is, considering only bonds within
\displaystyle\Lambda
). Consider any
\displaystyle V\subset\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
. As

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}^{\bar{\Psi}}=H_{A}^{\mathfrak{P}}+H_{\Lambda}^{\Psi}
, for any cylindrical event
\displaystyle[\sigma_{V}]
, we have that

\bar{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(\sigma_{V})\;=\;\frac{\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{V}}\,e^{-H^{\mathfrak{P}}_{A}(\xi_{A})}\,e^{-H_{\Lambda}^{{\Psi}}(\xi_{\Lambda})}}{\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}}e^{-H^{\mathfrak{P}}_{A}(\xi_{A})}\,e^{-H_{\Lambda}^{{\Psi}}(\xi_{\Lambda})}}.

Dividing top and bottom by
\displaystyle\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}}e^{-H_{\Lambda}^{{\Psi}}(\xi_{\Lambda})}
we get

\bar{\gamma}_{\Lambda}(\sigma_{V})\;=\;\frac{\gamma_{\Lambda}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{V}}\,e^{-H^{\mathfrak{P}}_{A}}\bigr)}{\gamma_{\Lambda}\bigl(e^{-H^{\mathfrak{P}}_{A}}\bigr)}.

Taking limits over
\displaystyle\Lambda
and using (
32
) we obtain

\bar{\nu}(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{V}})\;=\;\nu(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{V}}\,f)

where
\displaystyle f
is, precisely, the right-hand side of (
31
). This concludes the proof because cylindrical events uniquely determine the measures.
∎

Now we turn to the restrictions
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}_{+}
of measures
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}
to the half-line
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, i.e., for
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{F}_{+}
,
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}_{+}(B):=\nu^{(k)}(X_{-}\times B)
.
Note that for the cylindrical sets
\displaystyle[\sigma_{\Lambda}]
,
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, one has
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)}_{+}([\sigma_{\Lambda}])=\nu^{(k)}([\sigma_{\Lambda}])
.
Similarly, one can define the restrictions to the left half-line
\displaystyle-\mathbb{N}
.
If the model
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
does not exhibit phase transitions, then
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
is a product measure, i.e.,
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}=\nu^{(0)}_{-}\times\nu^{(0)}_{+}
(c.f. (
28
)), where
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}_{-}
is the unique Gibbs measure for the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi^{-}
on
\displaystyle X_{-}
, and
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}_{+}
is the unique Gibbs measure for
\displaystyle\Phi^{+}
on
\displaystyle X_{+}
. If this is the case, then one can compute the Radon-Nikodym density
\displaystyle f^{(k)}_{+}:=\frac{d\nu^{(k)}_{+}}{d\nu^{(0)}_{+}}
, in fact, for all
\displaystyle\sigma\in X_{+}
,
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(33)

f^{(k)}_{+}(\sigma)=\frac{\int_{X_{-}}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{j}}(\xi,\sigma)}\nu_{-}^{(0)}(d\xi)}{\int_{X_{+}}\int_{X_{-}}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{j}}(\xi,\zeta)}\nu^{(0)}_{-}(d\xi)\nu^{(0)}_{+}(d\zeta)}.

By Theorem
6.5
, all the measures
\displaystyle\nu^{(k)},\;k\geq 0
are equivalent to
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
.
However, it is not clear whether the weak
∗
-limit points of the sequence
\displaystyle\{\nu^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
or not.
The following theorem provides sufficient conditions.

Theorem D
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
13
).
Suppose the family
\displaystyle\{f^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu^{(0)})
.
Then the weak
∗
limit point of the sequence
\displaystyle\{\nu^{(k)}\}
is a Gibbs measure for
\displaystyle\Phi
and absolutely continuous with respect to
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
.

The next theorem is the main theorem of this section and it provides sufficient conditions for uniform integrability of the family
\displaystyle\{f^{(k)}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, and thus absolute continuity of the weak
∗
limit points with respect to
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
.

Theorem E
.

Assume the following

1)

the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
13
);

2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}||\underline{\delta}(\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}})||_{2}^{2}<\infty
;

3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_{k}<\infty
, where
\displaystyle\rho_{k}:=\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\int_{X}\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}d{\nu}^{(n)}\right|
.

Then
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
does not exhibit phase transitions, and
\displaystyle\{f^{(k)}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is uniformly integrable in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu^{(0)})
.

Remark 6.7
.

Note that the third, summability condition in Theorem
E

is important.
To illustrate this, consider the product-type potential
\displaystyle\phi
defined by (
10
).
One can readily verify that the potential
\displaystyle\phi
coincides with the half-line mean energy at
\displaystyle 0
— that is,
\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}
— for the
translation-invariant UAC interaction:

\Phi_{\Lambda}(\omega)=\begin{cases}-\frac{\beta\omega_{j}}{|i-j|^{\alpha}},&\quad\Lambda=\{i,j\}\subset\mathbb{Z},\;j>i,\\
0,&\quad\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}\;

This interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
, which is not spin-flip invariant, satisfies the first and second conditions of Theorem
E
for
\displaystyle\beta
sufficiently small.
In fact, for all
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
, both Dobrushin interdependence matrices
\displaystyle C(\gamma^{\Phi})
and
\displaystyle C(\gamma^{\phi})
are zero matrices, thus, both specifications (not the interactions) satisfy the condition (
12
) for all
\displaystyle\beta
’s.
However,
\displaystyle\Phi
does not satisfy the last condition of the theorem.
Indeed, as the unique Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\mu
for
\displaystyle\Phi
is Bernoulli with

\mu([1]_{0})=\frac{e^{\beta\zeta(\alpha)}}{2\cosh(\beta\zeta(\alpha))},

one has

\int_{X}\sigma_{0}d\mu=\tanh(\beta\zeta(\alpha))>0.

Therefore,

\rho_{-i,j}\geq\Big|\int_{X}\Phi_{\{-i,j\}}d\mu\Big|=\frac{\beta}{(i+j)^{\alpha}}\tanh(\beta\zeta(\alpha)),

for all
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and thus the sum
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho_{-i,j}
diverges.

The combination of Theorems
E
and
D
implies that if
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies the conditions of the former,
the unique Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}(\Phi)
is absolutely continuous with
respect to
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
.
Note that, as a consequence, Theorem
E
can not be
true for the previous interaction if
\displaystyle 1<\alpha\leq 3/2
. Indeed, the second
part of Theorem
3.4
directly implies that the
measures
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
are singular for those values of
\displaystyle\alpha
.

It is natural to ask whether, reciprocally,
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
is also absolutely continuous with respect to
\displaystyle\mu
.
The answer is affirmative modulo conditions comparable to those of Theorem
E
. The argument resorts also to a sequence of interactions but these time obtained by removing one by one the bonds in the volumes in
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}:\min\Lambda<0,\max\Lambda\geq 0\}
instead of adding them to
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
. More precisely, at each step
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we construct a new interaction
\displaystyle\Phi^{(k)}
as follows:

{\Phi}^{(k)}_{\Lambda}=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\Lambda\in\{\Lambda_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\},\\
\Phi_{\Lambda},&\quad\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\;

Then as previously, for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the matrix
\displaystyle\bar{C}(\Phi^{(k)})
is dominated by
\displaystyle\bar{C}(\Phi)
, thus if
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies the Dobrushin uniqueness condition (
13
) so do all the interactions
\displaystyle\Phi^{(k)}
.
Furthermore, Remark
6.4
still remains valid interchanging
\displaystyle\Phi
and
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
.
Thus the sequence
\displaystyle\mu^{(k)}
of unique Gibbs measures for each
\displaystyle\Phi^{(k)}
converges in weak
∗
sense to a Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\mu
for
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
.
In addition, by Lemma
6.6
, all the measures
\displaystyle\mu^{(k)}
are equivalent to
\displaystyle\mu
and their Radon-Nikodym derivatives are given by

f^{(k)}_{\mu}:=\frac{d\mu^{(k)}}{d\mu}=\frac{e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}}{\int_{X}e^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{\Lambda_{i}}}d\mu}.

To conclude, we present the following analogue of Theorem
E

which can be proven in a similar way.

Theorem F
.

Assume that

1)

the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies (
13
);

2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}||\underline{\delta}(\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}})||_{2}^{2}<\infty
;

3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_{k}^{\mu}<\infty
, where
\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{\mu}:=\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Big|\int_{X}\Phi_{\Lambda_{k}}d{\mu}^{(n)}\Big|
.

Then
\displaystyle\Psi^{(0)}
does not exhibit phase transitions, and
\displaystyle\{f^{(k)}_{\mu}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is uniformly integrable in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mu)
. In particular,
\displaystyle\nu^{(0)}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\displaystyle\mu
.

We postpone the proofs of Theorem
D
and Theorem
E
until Section
8
.

7.
Application: Dyson model

This was our motivating example. Let us
recall that the Dyson potential
\displaystyle\phi
is defined on the half-line configuration space
\displaystyle X_{+}=\{-1,+1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
as

\phi(x)=\beta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{0}x_{n}}{n^{\alpha}},\quad x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in X_{+},

for some
\displaystyle\alpha>1
.
As mentioned in Example
6.3
, this potential is
related to the whole-line Dyson model
\displaystyle\Phi
, defined in (
9
), by

\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}
,
and thus

\displaystyle\overset{\rightleftharpoons}{\gamma}^{\phi}=\gamma^{\Phi}
.
Hence
by Theorem
A
, for all
\displaystyle\nu\in\mathscr{G}(\phi)
,
any weak* accumulation point of the sequence
\displaystyle\{\nu\circ S^{-n}\}

is a restriction of some
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}(\Phi)
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

The Dyson potential satisfies the Good Future condition because its oscillations (
24
)
\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\phi)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha})
are summable. As a consequence, it satisfies the extensibility condition.
For
\displaystyle\alpha>2
,
furthermore, its variations
\displaystyle v_{n}(S_{n}\phi):=\sup\{S_{n}\phi(x)-S_{n}\phi(y):x_{0}^{n-1}=y_{0}^{n-1}\}
are also summable and, therefore, the standard theory applies
[
40
]
.
The case of
\displaystyle\alpha\in(1,2]
is significantly more subtle, and its theory is less developed.
Its most recent advance is (Theorem
3.2
above), obtained by Johansson, Öberg and Pollicott
[
23
]

through
the random-cluster representation for the whole-line Dyson model
\displaystyle\Phi
(c.f. (
9
)).

Now, we recall some important properties of the Dyson model and its phase diagram.
It is clear that the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
is translation and spin-flip invariant.
One of the most interesting properties of the associated Gibbs measures is that all the measures in
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\Phi)
are translation-invariant for all the values of the parameters
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
.
However, this statement is not true as regards spin-flip invariance.
In fact, there is only one spin-flip invariant Gibbs measure in
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(\Phi)
which is the cause of the phase transitions for high
\displaystyle\beta
’s.
By applying FKG inequalities, it can be shown that the (weak
∗
) limits
\displaystyle\mu^{+}:=\lim_{\Lambda\uparrow\mathbb{Z}}\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\cdot|+)
and
\displaystyle\mu^{-}:=\lim_{\Lambda\uparrow\mathbb{Z}}\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\cdot|-)
exist, and both are extremal.
In fact,
\displaystyle\mu^{-}
and
\displaystyle\mu^{+}
are the only extremal elements of

The key to establishing properties of transfer operators for the Dyson potential is, in our opinion,
a proper understanding of the probabilistic properties of the
left-right interaction energy
function:

W(\xi,\sigma)=\sum_{i<0\leq j}-\frac{\beta\xi_{i}\sigma_{j}}{(j-i)^{\alpha}},\quad\xi\in X_{-},\,\sigma\in X_{+}.

For example, in the case
\displaystyle\alpha>2
, the results follow almost immediately from the simple observation that the interaction energy function is uniformly bounded

\sup_{\xi\in X_{-},\sigma\in X_{+}}|W(\xi,\sigma)|=\beta\sum_{i<0\leq j}\frac{1}{(j-i)^{\alpha}}<\infty,

and all expressions for densities above automatically lead to continuous functions.
The next interesting ’critical value’
\displaystyle\alpha=3/2
also appears quite naturally:
The left-right interaction energies
\displaystyle W(\xi,\sigma)
for a fixed
\displaystyle\sigma\in X_{+}
, but
\displaystyle\nu_{-}
-random configurations
\displaystyle\xi\in X_{-}
, can be represented as

W(\xi,\sigma)=\sum_{i<0}Z_{i},\quad Z_{i}=\xi_{i}\Bigl(-\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{(j-i)^{\alpha}}\Bigr).

The variances
\displaystyle\textsf{var}(Z_{i})=\mathcal{O}(|i|^{-2(\alpha-1)})
become summable for
\displaystyle\alpha>3/2
[c.f. (
60
)].
Hence, assuming weak correlations, the condition
\displaystyle\alpha>3/2
corresponds to the almost sure existence of the left-right interaction energy for random conditions on the left half-line interacting with any (in particular, the all plus or all minus) configuration on the right half-line.
The concentration inequality can be interpreted as the rigorous transcription of this observation, and the Dobrushin condition as the guarantor of weak correlations.

We strongly believe that the analysis of the left-right interaction energy function can be extended to
the whole subcritical regime
\displaystyle\beta<\beta_{c}(\alpha)
.
