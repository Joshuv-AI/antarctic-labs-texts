Minimal angle spread in the probability simplex with respect to the uniform distribution
Heinz H. Bauschke, Peter A. V. DiBerardino

Abstract
We compute the minimal angle spread with respect to the uniform distribution in the probability simplex. The resulting optimization problem is analytically solved. The formula provided shows that the minimal angle spread approaches zero as the dimension tends to infinity. We also discuss an application in cognitive science.

1
Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that

X:=\mathbb{R}^{n}

with inner product

\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
,

(1)

and induced Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
.
We also define the probability simplex by

\Delta:=\Delta_{n}:=\big\{{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}~\big|~{x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{n}=1}\big\},

(2)

where
\mathbb{R}_{+}=\big\{{x\in\mathbb{R}}~\big|~{x\geq 0}\big\}
.
The probability simplex is of central importance in
Statistics, Optimization, and Information Theory; see, e.g.,

[
4
]
and
[
11
]
.
(We write
\Delta_{n}
if we wish to emphasize the dimension
n
.)
It will be convenient to set

\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad u:=\big(\tfrac{1}{n},\tfrac{1}{n},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{n}\big)\in\Delta_{n}.

(3)

The problem we investigate is the following:
Given
p\in\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}
,
there exist two unique points
a=a(p)
and
b=b(p)
in
\Delta

such that

\{u,p\}\subseteq[a,b]
and
\|a-b\|
is maximal.
(Let us note that when, e.g.,
n=3
, then the mapping

\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}\colon\mathbb{R}\colon p\mapsto\|a(p)-b(p)\|
is continuous; however,
it is not possible to extend it continuously — let alone in a smooth manner — at the point
u
.)
The quantity
\|a-b\|
can be thought of as the
“width“ of
\Delta
with respect to
p
and

\cos\big(\measuredangle(a,b)\big)=\frac{\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle}{\|a\|\|b\|}

(4)

as the cosine of the “angle spread” with respect to
p
.

The
aim of this paper
is to
minimize the angle spread
,
which equivalently corresponds to
maximizing its cosine

\max_{p\in\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}}{\frac{\left\langle{a(p)},{b(p)}\right\rangle}{\|a(p)\|\|b(p)\|}}.

(P)

When
n=2
, it is
clear that the maximum value of
Eq.

P
is
\cos(\pi/2)=0

and that every
p\in\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}
solves
Eq.

P
.
Thus, we assume henceforth that

n\in\{3,4,\ldots\}.

(5)

Our main result can now be stated:

Theorem 1.1
.

The maximum value of
Eq.

P

is

\frac{n-2}{n+2}

(6)

and a pair realizing this maximum is

a^{*}=\tfrac{1}{n}(2,1,1,\ldots,1,0)
and

b^{*}=\tfrac{1}{n}(0,1,1,\ldots,1,2)
.
Consequently, the minimal angle spread is

\arccos\Big(\frac{n-2}{n+2}\Big).

(7)

See
Fig.

1
for an illustration of
Theorem

1.1
when
n=3
.
We note that as the dimension of the space
n
increases to infinity,
the minimal angle spread approaches
\arccos(1)=0
.
We also note that when
n=1,2,4
, then

\pi-\arccos((n-2)/(n+2))
coincides with the dihedral angle of
the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, respectively. However, dihedral
angles of other classical polyhedra (see
[
16
]
) do not seem to be
related to the angles provided by
Eq.

7
.

Our strategy to prove
Theorem

1.1
is to reduce the complexity of the problem
in stages by exploiting its structure.
Eventually, we are led to a one-dimensional problem
which we then solve by Calculus.

Figure 1
:
An illustration of
Theorem

1.1
when
n=3
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we collect a technical optimization result that will
be used later. The computation of
[a,b]
is carried out in
Section

3
.
In
Section

4
, we set up the cosine quotient
Eq.

4
in a
more tractable form. The optimization is then tackled in

Section

5
where we keep
p_{1}
and
p_{n}
fixed.
At last, the proof is completed in
Section

6
.
In
Section

7
, we sketch an application of our results
— which in fact motivated this note — in cognitive science.
The presented results are a first contribution to using the minimal
angle spread as a nonsmooth discrepancy term in this area.

Finally, we note that a reviewer pointed out that another possible departure point
is to study sufficient conditions for strong convexity of integral functionals
in two-stage stochastic linear programming. In particular, recent work by
Claus and Spürkel
[
3
]
features minimal angles of similar though different kind.
This is a promising direction to explore in future research.

The notation in this paper is fairly standard and follows largely
[
1
]
.

2
An auxiliary result

It will be convenient to have the following result ready for future use.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, set
\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Set
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon x\mapsto\|x-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}
,
let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and set

y:=(\eta,\eta,\ldots,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\text{where}\quad\eta:=\tfrac{1}{m}(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{m}).

Then

h(y)\leq h(x).

Proof 2.2
.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle h(y)-\gamma^{2}\|\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|y\|^{2}-2\gamma\left\langle{y},{\boldsymbol{1}}\right\rangle=m\eta^{2}-2\gamma m\eta

\displaystyle=m\big(\tfrac{1}{m}(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{m})\big)^{2}-2\gamma(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{m})

\displaystyle\leq m\tfrac{1}{m}\big(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{m}^{2}\big)-2\gamma\left\langle{x},{\boldsymbol{1}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\|x\|^{2}-2\gamma\left\langle{x},{\boldsymbol{1}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=h(x)-\gamma^{2}\|\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2},

where the inequality follows from the convexity of the square function.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
,
and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
Set

A\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon x\mapsto(\eta,\eta,\ldots,\eta)
,
where
\eta=\tfrac{1}{m}(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{m})
, and
let
C
be a nonempty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

A(C)\subseteq C
.
Set

\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Then to

\text{minimize}\;\;\|x-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}\;\;\text{over $x\in C$}

(8)

is the same as to

\text{minimize}\;\;\|x-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}\;\;\text{over $x\in C\cap\mathbb{R}\,\boldsymbol{1}$}

(9)

in the sense that the optimal values for both problems
are identical and if the first problem has a solution, then
it also has a solution that solves also the second problem.

Proof 2.4
.

Clear from
Lemma

2.1
.

Remark 2.5
.

We point out in passing that the operator
A
from

Corollary

2.3
is the projection operator of the set
\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{1}
.

3
Determining the relative boundary points
\scriptstyle a
and
\scriptstyle b

In this section, we fix

p\in\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}.

(10)

Then there exist indices
i
and
j
in
I:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that

p_{i}<\tfrac{1}{n}<p_{j}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that

0\leq p_{1}=\min_{i\in I}p_{i}<\tfrac{1}{n}<p_{n}=\max_{i\in I}p_{i}\leq 1.

(11)

Set

(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R})\quad q(\lambda):=(1-\lambda)u+\lambda p=u+\lambda(p-u).

(12)

Note that

q(0)=u
,
q(1)=p
,

(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R})(\forall i\in I)\quad q_{i}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)u_{i}+\lambda p_{i}=u_{i}+\lambda(p_{i}-u_{i}),

(13)

and hence

q_{1}(\lambda)+q_{2}(\lambda)+\cdots+q_{n}(\lambda)=1.

(14)

We wish to find the smallest and largest
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

q(\lambda)\in\Delta
.
Let
i\in I
.
Suppose that
p_{i}>u_{i}
.
Then
(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+})

q_{i}(\lambda)>u_{i}
.
So if
\lambda<0
, then

q_{i}(\lambda)=0

\Leftrightarrow

-u_{i}=\lambda(p_{i}-u_{i})

\Leftrightarrow

\lambda=-u_{i}/(p_{i}-u_{i})=-(1/n)/(p_{i}-(1/n))=-1/(np_{i}-1)\leq-1/(np_{n}-1)
.
Hence the smallest value
\lambda
still guaranteeing
q(\lambda)\in\Delta
is

\lambda_{-}:=\frac{-1}{np_{n}-1}=\frac{1}{1-np_{n}}<0.

(15)

Analogously, the largest value
\lambda
still guaranteeing
q(\lambda)\in\Delta
is

\lambda_{+}:=\frac{1}{1-np_{1}}>0.

(16)

The corresponding vectors

a:=q(\lambda_{-})=u+\lambda_{-}(p-u)

(17)

and

b:=q(\lambda_{+})=u+\lambda_{+}(p-u)

(18)

thus form the largest segment
[a,b]\subseteq\Delta

such that
\{u,p\}\subseteq[a,b]
.
Note that
a
and
b
are depending on
p
— when we want
to stress this, then we’ll write
a(p)
and
b(p)
.

For
i\in I
, we simplify

a_{i}=u_{i}+\lambda_{-}(p_{i}-u_{i})=\tfrac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{1-np_{n}}\big(p_{i}-\tfrac{1}{n}\big)=\frac{1-np_{n}+n\big(p_{i}-\tfrac{1}{n}\big)}{n(1-np_{n})}=\frac{p_{n}-p_{i}}{np_{n}-1}

(19)

and similarly

b_{i}=\frac{p_{i}-p_{1}}{1-np_{1}}.

(20)

Thus

a=\frac{1}{np_{n}-1}(p_{n}\boldsymbol{1}-p)\;\;\text{and}\;\;b=\frac{1}{1-np_{1}}(p-p_{1}\boldsymbol{1}),

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Next,

\displaystyle\|a\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)^{2}}\|p_{n}\boldsymbol{1}-p\|^{2}=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)^{2}}\big(p_{n}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}-2p_{n}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{1}},{p}\right\rangle+\|p\|^{2}\big)

(22a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)^{2}}\big(np_{n}^{2}-2p_{n}+\|p\|^{2}\big)

(22b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)^{2}}\Big(p_{1}^{2}+\big(p_{2}^{2}+\cdots+p_{n-1}^{2}\big)+(n+1)p_{n}^{2}-2p_{n}\Big)

(22c)

and similarly

\|b\|^{2}=\frac{1}{(1-np_{1})^{2}}\Big((n+1)p_{1}^{2}-2p_{1}+\big(p_{2}^{2}+\cdots+p_{n-1}^{2}\big)+p_{n}^{2}\Big).

(23)

4
Setting up the cosine quotient

We uphold the assumptions and notation from the previous section.
It is convenient to abbreviate

z:=(p_{2},\ldots,p_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2}.

(24)

Note that

(\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-2\})\;\;p_{1}\leq z_{i}\leq p_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}z_{i}=1-p_{1}-p_{n}\geq 0

(25)

because of
Eq.

10
and
Eq.

11
.
This allows us to rewrite
Eq.

22
and
Eq.

23
more
succinctly as

\|a\|^{2}=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)^{2}}\big(p_{1}^{2}+\|z\|^{2}+(n+1)p_{n}^{2}-2p_{n}\big)

(26)

and

\|b\|^{2}=\frac{1}{(1-np_{1})^{2}}\big((n+1)p_{1}^{2}-2p_{1}+\|z\|^{2}+p_{n}^{2}\big).

(27)

Next, using
Eq.

19
and
Eq.

20
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_{n}-p_{i}}{np_{n}-1}\cdot\frac{p_{i}-p_{1}}{1-np_{1}}

(28a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)(1-np_{1})}\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}(p_{n}-p_{i})(p_{i}-p_{1})

(28b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(np_{n}-1)(1-np_{1})}\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(p_{n}-z_{i})(z_{i}-p_{1}).

(28c)

Using
Eq.

28
,
Eq.

26
, and

Eq.

27
,
we now set up the quotient of interest from
Eq.

4
:

\frac{\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle}{\|a\|\|b\|}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(p_{n}-z_{i})(z_{i}-p_{1})}{\sqrt{p_{1}^{2}+\|z\|^{2}+(n+1)p_{n}^{2}-2p_{n}}\sqrt{(n+1)p_{1}^{2}-2p_{1}+\|z\|^{2}+p_{n}^{2}}}.

(29)

5
Maximizing the cosine quotient (with
\scriptstyle p_{1}
and
\scriptstyle p_{n}
fixed)

We uphold the notation of the previous section.
Now we turn toward maximizing the cosine quotient
Eq.

29
,
with
p_{1}
and
p_{n}
fixed.
In view of
Eq.

25
,
z=(p_{2},\ldots,p_{n-1})
must belong to the
compact convex set

C:=\bigg\{{w\in[p_{1},p_{n}]^{n-2}}~\bigg|~{\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}w_{i}=1-p_{1}-p_{n}}\bigg\}.

(30)

The continuity of
Eq.

29
as a function of
z

coupled with the compactness of
C
guarantees the existence of
a maximizer of the cosine quotient.
Note that

A(C)\subseteq C,

(31)

where
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\colon w\mapsto(\eta,\eta,\ldots,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2}

and
\eta=\tfrac{1}{n-2}(w_{1}+w_{2}+\cdots+w_{n-2})
.

In general, maximizing a quotient is more involved;
however, we will get a lucky break for our problem:
It turns out we can maximize the numerator and minimize the denominator
of
Eq.

29
with respect to
z
and we luckily obtain
the same optimal vector!

For convenience, set

\alpha:=p_{1}
,
\beta:=p_{n}
,
\gamma:=(\alpha+\beta)/2
,
and
\boldsymbol{1}:=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
.
Then the numerator of
Eq.

29
— as a function of
z
— is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(\beta-z_{i})(z_{i}-\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}z_{i}^{2}+(\alpha+\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}z_{i}+\text{constant}

(32a)

\displaystyle=-\|z\|^{2}+(\alpha+\beta)\left\langle{\boldsymbol{1}},{z}\right\rangle+\text{constant}

(32b)

\displaystyle=-\|z-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}+\text{constant}.

(32c)

We want to maximize the numerator over
z\in C
;
equivalently, we want to minimize
\|z-\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}

over
z\in C
.
By
Corollary

2.3
, we may restrict our attention
to

z\in C\cap\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{1}.

(33)

On to the denominator of
Eq.

29
!
It is clear that the denominator becomes small when
\|z\|
becomes small.
So minimizing the denominator corresponds to
minimizing
\|z\|^{2}=\|z-0\boldsymbol{1}\|^{2}
.
Again by
Corollary

2.3
, we may restrict our attention
to
Eq.

33
!
But if
z\in C\cap\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{1}
, say
z=\zeta\boldsymbol{1}

for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, then
the requirement that
\zeta\boldsymbol{1}\in C
forces

\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}z_{i}=(n-2)\zeta=1-p_{1}-p_{n}
, i.e.,

\zeta=(1-p_{1}-p_{n})/(n-2)
.
Because
(n-1)p_{1}+p_{n}\leq 1\leq p_{1}+(n-1)p_{n}
, it follows that

p_{1}\leq\zeta\leq p_{n}
.
Altogether,

z=\frac{1-p_{1}-p_{n}}{n-2}(1,1,\ldots,1)\in C\cap\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-2}

(34)

maximizes
Eq.

29

and

\|z\|^{2}=\frac{(1-p_{1}-p_{n})^{2}}{n-2}.

(35)

To sum up, when restricted to the one-dimensional slice
C\cap\mathbb{R}\boldsymbol{1}
(see
Eq.

33
),
we obtain the unique solution
z
given by
Eq.

34
.
Our next step is to plug
Eq.

34
and
Eq.

35

back into
Eq.

29
.
We start with the numerator of
Eq.

29
:

\displaystyle\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\Big(p_{n}-\frac{1-p_{1}-p_{n}}{n-2}\Big)\Big(\frac{1-p_{1}-p_{n}}{n-2}-p_{1}\Big)

(36a)

\displaystyle=(n-2)\Big(\frac{(n-2)p_{n}-(1-p_{1}-p_{n})}{n-2}\Big)\Big(\frac{1-p_{1}-p_{n}-(n-2)p_{1}}{n-2}\Big)

(36b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-2}\big(p_{1}+(n-1)p_{n}-1\big)\big(1-p_{n}-(n-1)p_{1}\big).

(36c)

Using
Eq.

35
, we see that the square of the denominator of
Eq.

29

is

\displaystyle\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg(p_{1}^{2}+\frac{(1-p_{1}-p_{n})^{2}}{n-2}+(n+1)p_{n}^{2}-2p_{n}\bigg)

(37a)

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg((n+1)p_{1}^{2}-2p_{1}+\frac{(1-p_{1}-p_{n})^{2}}{n-2}+p_{n}^{2}\bigg)

(37b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-2)^{2}}

(37c)

\displaystyle\quad\times\big((n-2)p_{1}^{2}+(1-p_{1}-p_{n})^{2}+(n-2)(n+1)p_{n}^{2}-2(n-2)p_{n}\big)

(37d)

\displaystyle\quad\times\big((n-2)(n+1)p_{1}^{2}-2(n-2)p_{1}+(1-p_{1}-p_{n})^{2}+(n-2)p_{n}^{2}\big).

(37e)

Abbreviating
x=p_{1}
and
y=p_{n}
,
we use
Eq.

36
and
Eq.

37
to write the square of
Eq.

29
as

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle^{2}}{\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\big(x+(n-1)y-1\big)^{2}\big(1-y-(n-1)x\big)^{2}}{\big((n-2)x^{2}+(1-x-y)^{2}+(n-2)(n+1)y^{2}-2(n-2)y\big)}}{\big((n-2)(n+1)x^{2}-2(n-2)x+(1-x-y)^{2}+(n-2)y^{2}\big)}.

(38)

In the next section, we will do the final maximization by
setting
p_{1}
and
p_{n}
, i.e.,
x
and
y
, loose.

6
Maximizing the cosine quotient (concluded)

While
Section

5
looks like a huge mess, we can invoke one small
but useful optimization: the objective function value of the
cosine quotient is by construction constant on
[a,b]
.
Now both
a
and
b
have at least one coordinate equal to
0
,
namely
a_{n}=0
and
b_{1}=0
(see
Eq.

21
).
Because the objective function in
Eq.

P
is constant on

\Delta\smallsetminus\{u\}
intersected with any line passing
through
u
,
we may and do finally assume that
a=p
and thus
p_{1}=x=0
.
Then
Section

5
simplifies to

\frac{\left\langle{a},{b}\right\rangle^{2}}{\|a\|^{2}\|b\|^{2}}=\frac{\big((n-1)y-1\big)^{2}\big(1-y\big)^{2}}{\big((1-y)^{2}+(n-2)(n+1)y^{2}-2(n-2)y\big){\big((1-y)^{2}+(n-2)y^{2}\big)}}=:Q(y).

(39)

The constraints on
p
are

0\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}=\cdots=p_{n-1}=(1-p_{1}-p_{n})/(n-2)\leq p_{n}\leq 1
.
With our assumption that

p_{1}=0=x
and
p_{n}=y
, these simplify to

0\leq(1-y)/(n-2)\leq y\leq 1
and then to

\frac{1}{n-1}\leq y\leq 1.

(40)

Because
n\geq 3>2
, we have

\frac{1}{n-1}<\frac{2}{n}<1.

(41)

So our remaining goal is to

\text{maximize\;\;}Q(y)\;\;\text{subject to}\;\;\frac{1}{n-1}\leq y\leq 1

(42)

where
Q(y)
is defined in
Eq.

39

Using the chain quotient rule to compute
the derivative of
Q(y)
followed by
factoring yields

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-2)\big((n-1)^{2}y^{2}-ny+1\big)\big((n-1)y-1\big)(ny-2)(y-1)y}{\big((n^{2}-n-1)y^{2}+2(1-n)y+1\big)^{2}\big((n-1)y^{2}-2y+1\big)^{2}}.

(43)

Note that
Q^{\prime}(y)
has six roots, namely

\bigg\{\frac{n\pm\sqrt{-(3n-2)(n-2)}}{2(n-1)^{2}},0,\frac{1}{n-1},\frac{2}{n},1\bigg\}.

(44)

Because
n>2
implies
-(3n-2)(n-2)<0
,
we note that there are exactly four real roots.
Our constraint
Eq.

40
excludes
0
.
The remaining three roots include the endpoints of
interval constraint.
Thus, the maximum value of
Eq.

42
is found
by substituting for
y
the values
{1}/(n-1),{2}/{n},1
into

Q(y)
which results in
0,(n-2)^{2}/(n+2)^{2},0
.

To sum up,
y=\frac{2}{n}
is the solution of
Eq.

42
,
with maximum value
(n-2)^{2}/(n+2)^{2}
.
Using
Eq.

34
, we see this gives rise
to the probability distribution

p^{*}=\tfrac{1}{n}\big(0,1,1,\ldots,1,2\big).

(45)

We have shown that the angle spread is minimized
for
p^{*}
given by
Eq.

45
.
In view of
Eq.

19
and
Eq.

20
,
this gives rise to

a^{*}:=a(p^{*})=\tfrac{1}{n}\big(2,1,1,\ldots,1,0\big)\;\;\text{and}\;\;b^{*}:=b(p^{*})=\tfrac{1}{n}\big(0,1,1,\ldots,1,2\big)=p^{*}.

(46)

These vectors give the optimal value

\cos\big(\measuredangle(a^{*},b^{*})\big)=\frac{\left\langle{a^{*}},{b^{*}}\right\rangle}{\|a^{*}\|\|b^{*}\|}=\frac{n-2}{n+2}

(47)

which we claimed in the introduction.
The proof of
Theorem

1.1
is thus complete.

An application in cognitive science

Our main result (
Theorem

1.1
) provides an important theoretical and methodological advancement in the study of probabilistic belief models in humans. Human learning is often modeled with Bayesian statistics, where beliefs are represented as probability distributions over possible outcomes
[
8
,
9
,
13
,
15
]
.

A motivating example

Consider the expected returns on a $100 investment over 1 year. A “bull” investor believes market prices will rise, while a “bear” investor believes market prices will fall. However, a bull still surely accepts prices
could
fall, despite holding this belief to a lesser degree than the bear. In Bayesian terms, the beliefs of our two investors can be represented as probability distributions over the domain of potential investment returns. The bull’s “prior” (initial belief) will have most of its probability mass over positive returns, while the opposite is true for the bear’s prior.
These priors change over time in light of new evidence. While it is theoretically optimal to update according to the laws of probability, the empirical question remains as to how people actually represent and update probabilistic beliefs.

Previous work

Much of the experimental work in this field infers prior and posterior beliefs from sequential participant actions, or elicits them with insufficient detail
[
2
,
6
,
12
]
. This requires experimenters to make unwarranted assumptions about the way that probabilistic beliefs are parameterized as probability distributions, and the nature of how they are updated with new data. It may be that a strict Bayesian model of human cognition is inappropriate when these assumptions are relaxed. Recent work by DiBerardino, Filipowicz, Danckert, and Anderson
used a computerized version of the game “Plinko”, where at each trial a ball falls through an array of pegs into one of
n=40
slots below.
Participants were tasked with estimating the distribution of future ball drops by explicitly drawing a probability distribution that represents their beliefs after each trial
[
5
]
. This work found that initial beliefs (priors) vary across individuals and that these differences indicated future ball drop learning accuracy, thus demonstrating the importance of directly measuring individual beliefs in a theoretically agnostic manner
[
5
]
.

However,
[
5
]
cannot make any additional claims about
why
participants with some priors appear to learn better than others. Its main limitation is that some participants’ priors are more similar to the forthcoming ball drop distribution than others. As a result, it cannot be determined whether or not the observed differences in learning ability across different priors are due to participant “state” or “trait” differences. It may be that some participants are better probabilistic learners, which is a trait that can be detected by the shape of their prior beliefs. But it may also be that the participants who performed the best only did so because they just happened to have an initial state of belief more amenable to learning the forthcoming distribution.

On-going work

Our main result (
Theorem

1.1
) allows for a new Plinko experiment that presents each participant with a ball drop distribution of a set level of similarity to any arbitrary participant’s prior. Specifically, when measuring similarity between discrete probability distributions as the angular spread between representative Euclidean vectors, as was done in
[
5
]
, we can determine the maximum possible rotation (dissimilarity) for any given vector (participant’s prior) with respect to the uniform distribution. We require the following result.

(48)

towards

v
.
Finally, if

, then

\measuredangle(u,b)>\alpha_{n}/2
and we rotate
u

, if a participant produces a prior

More psychological research is required to determine the precise measure of
similarity humans use to compare probability distributions. Angular similarity
is only one such possibility. This result also poses an interesting theoretical
development: Does the shrinking minimal angle spread as the dimension tends to
infinity create any difficulties for people to perceive or act upon any
particular set of probability distributions? It may be that thinking about
opposite/dissimilar probability distributions becomes more difficult as the
number of discrete histogram bins approaches infinity if these mathematics
correspond to mechanisms of human inference.
