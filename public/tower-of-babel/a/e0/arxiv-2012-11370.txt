Direct derivation of Liénard–Wiechert potentials, Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law Journal: arXiv
Hrvoje Dodig

Abstract
In 19 th century Maxwell derived famous Maxwell equations from the knowledge of three experimental physical laws: the electrostatic Coulomb’s law, Ampere’s force law and Faraday’s law of induction. However, theoretical basis for Ampere’s force law and Faraday’s law remains unknown to this day. Furthermore, the Lorentz force is considered as summary of experimental phenomena, the theoretical foundation that explains generation of this force is still unknown. To answer these fundamental theoretical questions, in this paper we derive relativistically correct Liénard – Wiechert potentials, Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force from two simple postulates: (a) when all charges are at rest the Coulomb’s force acts between the charges, and (b) that disturbances caused by charge in motion propagate away from the source with finite velocity. The special relativity was not used in our derivations nor the Lorentz transformation. In effect, it was shown in this paper that all the electrodynamic laws, including the Lorentz force, can be derived from Coulomb’s law and time retardation. This was accomplished by analysis of hypothetical experiment where test charge is at rest and where previously moving source charge stops at some time in the past. Then the generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem, also derived in this paper, was applied to reformulate Coulomb’s force acting at present time as the function of positions of source charge at previous time when the source charge was moving. From this reformulation of Coulomb’s law the Liénard–Wiechert potentials and Maxwell’s equations were derived by careful mathematical manipulation. In the second part of this paper, the energy conservation principle valid for moving charges is derived from the knowledge of electrostatic energy conservation principle valid for stationary charges. This again was accomplished by using generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem. From this dynamic energy conservation principle the Lorentz force is finally derived.

Introduction

In his famous
Treatise

Maxwell derived equations of electrodynamics based on the knowledge about the

the electric force between charges at rest; Ampere’s law describing the force
between current carrying wires, and the Faraday’s law of induction. Prior to
Maxwell, magnetism and electricity were regarded to as separate phenomena. It
was James Clerk Maxwell who unified these seemingly disparate phenomena into
the set of equations collectively known today as Maxwell’s equations. In
modern vector notation, the four Maxwell’s equations that govern the behavior
of electromagnetic fields are written as:

\displaystyle=\rho

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=\mu\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}}{\partial t}

(4)

where symbol

is

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}{\partial t}

(6)

In standard electromagnetic theory, if a point charge
q_{s}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)
the scalar

\phi

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon}\left(\frac{q_{s}}{\left(1-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{s}(t_{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{s}(t_{r})\right)\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{r})\right|}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{\mu c}{4\pi}\left(\frac{q_{s}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{s}(t_{r})}{\left(1-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}_{s}(t_{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{s}(t_{r})\right)\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{r})\right|}\right)

These equations were almost simultaneously discovered by Liénard and Wiechert
around 1900’s and they represent explicit expressions for time-varying
electromagnetic fields caused by charge in arbitrary motion. Nevertheless,
Liénard-Wiechert potentials were derived from retarded potentials, which in
turn, are derived from Maxwell equations.

Maxwell’s electrodynamic equations provide the complete description of
electromagnetic fields, however, these equations say nothing about mechanical
forces experienced by the charge moving in electromagnetic field. If the
charge
q
is moving in electromagnetic field with velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
then
the force
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}
experienced by the charge
q
is:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}=q\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}\right)

(11)

The force described by equation (
11
) is well known Lorentz
force. Discovery of this electrodynamic force is historically credited to H.A.
Lorentz
[
5
]
, however, the similar expression for electromagnetic
force can be found in Maxwell’s
Treatise
, article 598
[
2
]
. The difference between the two is that Maxwell’s electromotive force acts on moving circuits and Lorentz force acts on moving charges.

However, it is not yet explained what causes the Lorentz force, Ampere’s force law and Faraday’s law. Maxwell derived his expression for electromotive force along moving circuit from the knowledge of experimental Faraday’s law. Later, Lorentz extended Maxwell’s reasoning to discover the force acting on charges moving in electromagnetic field
[
5
]
. Nevertheless, it would be impossible for Lorentz to derive his force law without the prior knowledge of Maxwell equations
[
6
]
.

Nowadays, the Lorentz force (
q\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}
term) is commonly viewed as an effect of Einstein’s special relativity. For example, an observer co-moving with source charge would not measure any magnetic field, while on the other hand, the stationary observer would measure the magnetic field caused by moving source charge. However, in this work, we demonstrate that the special relativity is not needed to derive the Lorentz force and Maxwell equations. In fact, we derive Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force from more fundamental principles: the Coulomb’s law and time retardation.

There is another reason why the idea to derive Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law may seem plausible. Because of mathematical similarity between
Coulomb’s law and Newton’s law of gravity many researchers thought that if Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force could be derived from Coulomb’s law that this would be helpful in understanding of gravity. These two inverse-square physical laws are written:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\right|^{3}}

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{G}

\displaystyle=-Gm_{1}m_{2}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\right|^{3}}

(13)

The expressions for Coulomb’s force and Newton’s gravitational force are
indeed similar, however, these two forces significantly differ in physical
nature. The latter force is always attractive while the former can be either
attractive or repulsive. Nevertheless, a number of researchers attempted to
derive Maxwell’s equations from Coulomb’s law, and most of these attempts rely
on Lorentz transformation of space-time coordinates between the rest frame of
the moving charge and laboratory frame.

The first hint that Maxwell’s equations could be derived from Coulomb’s law
and Lorentz transformation can be found in Einstein’s original 1905 paper on
special relativity
[
7
]
. Einstein suggested that the Lorentz
force term (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}
) is to be attributed to Lorentz
transformation of the electrostatic field from the rest frame of moving
charge to the laboratory frame where the charge has constant velocity. Later,
in 1912, Leigh Page derived Faraday’s law and Ampere’s law from Coulomb’s law
using Lorentz transformation
[
8
]
. Frisch and Willets discussed the
derivation of Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law using relativistic
transformation of force
[
9
]
. Similar route to derivation of
Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law was taken by Elliott
in 1966
[
10
]
. Kobe in 1986 derives Maxwell’s equations as the
generalization of Coulomb’s law using special relativity
[
11
]
.
Lorrain and Corson derive Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law, again, by using
Lorentz transformation and special relativity
[
12
]
. Field in 2006
derives Lorentz force and magnetic field starting from Coulomb’s law by
relating the electric field to electrostatic potential in a manner consistent
with special relativity
[
13
]
. The most recent attempt comes from
Singal
[
14
]
who attempted to derive electromagnetic fields of
accelerated charge from Coulomb’s law and Lorentz transformation.

All of the mentioned attempts have in common that they attempt to derive
Maxwell equations from Coulomb’s law by exploiting Lorentz transformation or
Einstein’s special theory of relativity. However, historically the Lorentz
transformation was derived from Maxwell’s equations
[
15
]
, thus,
the attempt to to derive Maxwell’s equations using Lorentz transformation
seems to involve circular reasoning
[
16
]
.
The strongest criticism came from Jackson who pointed out that it should be
immediately obvious that, without additional assumptions, it is impossible to
derive Maxwell’s equations from Coulomb’s law using theory of special
relativitiy
[
17
]
. Schwartz addresses these additional
assumptions and starting from Gauss’ law of electrostatics and by exploiting
the Lorentz invariance and properties of Lorentz transformation he derives the
Maxwell’s equations
[
18
]
.

In addition to the criticism above, we point out that the derivations of
Maxwell’s equations from Coulomb’s law using Lorentz transformation should
only be considered valid for the special case of the charge moving along the
straight line with constant velocity. This is because the Lorentz
transformation is derived under the assumption that electron moves with
constant velocity along straight line
[
15
]
. For example, if the
particle moves with uniform acceleration along straight line the
transformation of coordinates between the rest frame of the particle and the
laboratory frame takes the different mathematical form than that of the
Lorentz transformation
[
19
]
. If the particle is in uniform
circular motion yet another coordinate transformation from the rest frame to
laboratory frame, called Franklin transform, is valid
[
20
]
.
None of the above cited papers consider the fact that Lorentz transformation
is no longer valid when the charge is not moving along straight line with
constant velocity.

To circumvent problems with special relativity and Lorentz transformation we
take entirely different approach to derive Liénard–Wiechert potentials and
Maxwell’s equations from Coulomb law. We start our derivation from the
analysis of the following hypothetical experiment: consider two charges at
rest at present time, one called the test charge, and the other called the
source charge. The source charge was moving in the past but it is at rest at
present time. Because both charges are at rest at present the force
acting on test charge at present time is the Coulomb’s force.

However, in the past when the source charge was moving, we assume that the force acting on test charge was not the Coulomb’s force. To discover the mathematical form of this "unknown" electrodynamic force acting in the past
from the knowledge of known electrostatic force (Coulomb’s law) acting at present time the generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem was applied. This
theorem, derived in
A
, allowed us to relate
Coulomb’s force acting at present time to the positions of source charge at past
time. From here, Liénard–Wiechert potentials and Maxwell’s equations were
derived by careful mathematical manipulation.

It should be emphasized that we did not resort to theory of special relativity
nor to Lorentz transformation in our derivation of Maxwell’s equations. Not
less importantly, the presented derivation of Maxwell’s equations from
Coulomb’s law is valid for charges in arbitrary motion. In effect, we may say
that more general physical law (Maxwell’s equations) acting at
past time is derived from the knowledge of limited physical law (Coulomb’s law) acting
at present time.

However, from Maxwell’s equations, it is very difficult, if not entirely impossible, to derive
the Lorentz force without resorting to some form of energy conservation law.
As shown in Fig.
1
a, at present time, the single stationary charge creates Coulomb’s electrostatic field. Known energy conservation law valid at present time states that contour integral of Coulomb’s field along closed contour
C
is equal to zero.

But, this electrostatic energy conservation law valid at present is not necessarily valid in the past when the source charge was moving. Thus, in the second part of this paper we derive this "unknown" dynamic energy
conservation principle valid in the past from the knowledge of electrostatic energy conservation principle
valid at present time. This was again achieved
by the careful application of generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem which allowed us to transform electrostatic energy conservation law valid at present to dynamic energy conservation law valid in the past.

This dynamic
energy conservation law states that the work of non-conservative force along
closed contour is equal to the time derivative of the flux of certain vector
field through the surface bounded by this closed contour. From this dynamic energy conservation law the
Lorentz force was finally derived.

2
Generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem

Because generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem is central for deriving Maxwell equations and Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law, in this section, we briefly present this important theorem while the derivation itself is moved to
A
.
There have been several previous attempts in the literature to generalize
classical Helmholtz decomposition theorem to time dependent vector fields

[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. However, in none of the cited articles the
Helmholtz theorem for functions of space and time is presented in the mathematical
form usable for the mathematical developments described in this paper. This is
probably caused by difficulties in stating such a theorem and this was clearly
stated in
[
22
]
: "There does not exist
any simple generalization of this theorem for time-dependent
vector fields".

However, we show that there indeed exists the simple
generalization of Helmholtz decomposition theorem for time-dependent vector
fields and that it can derived from time-dependent inhomogeneous wave equation.
To improve the clarity of this paper, the complete derivation of Helmholtz
decomposition theorem for functions of space and time is moved to

A
, subsection
A.2
. As it was shown in
A
, the
generalization of Helmholtz decomposition theorem for the vector function of
space and time
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
can be written as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=

\displaystyle-\nabla\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\bigg(\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigg)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(14)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+\nabla\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\bigg(\nabla^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigg)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

where scalar function
G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is the fundamental solution
of time-dependent inhomogeneous wave equation given as:

\nabla^{2}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(15)

In the equation above,

\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(y-y^{\prime})\delta(z-z^{\prime})
is 3D Dirac
delta function, and
\delta(t-t^{\prime})
is Dirac delta function in one dimension.
Fundamental solution
G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, sometimes called Green’s
function, represents the retarded in time solution of the inhomogeneous time
dependent wave equation and it can be written as:

G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\left(t^{\prime}-t+\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|}{c}\right)}{4\pi\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|}

(16)

where position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
is the location of the source at time
t^{\prime}
.

From equation (
14
) it is evident that the Helmholtz
decomposition theorem for functions of space and time can be regarded to as a
mathematical tool that allows us to rewrite any vector function that is
function of present time
t
and of present position
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
as vector
function of previous time
t^{\prime}
and of previous position
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r^{\prime}}}
.
Furthermore, the generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem
(
14
) comes with additional limitation that it is valid if
vector function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
approaches zero faster than

1/\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|
as

\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|\rightarrow\infty
.

Very similar theorem was presented in article written by Heras

[
24
]
; the difference is that in Heras’ article the time integrals
in equation (
14
) were a priori evaluated at retarded time

t^{\prime}=t-\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|/c
. As such, the generalized Helmholtz
theorem presented in
[
24
]
is not suitable for the derivation of
Maxwell equations and Lorentz force from the Coulomb’s law. Reason for this,
as it will become evident later in this paper, is that if we immediately
evaluate the time integrals in equation (
14
) the
important information is lost from the equation.

3
Derivation of Maxwell Equations from Coulomb’s law

In this section we derive Maxwell equations from Coulomb’s law using
generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem represented by equation
(
14
). To begin the discussion, we consider hypothetical
experiment shown in Fig.
2
, where source charge
q_{s}
is
moving along trajectory
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)
and it stops at some past time
t_{s}
.
The test charge
q
is stationary at all times. We assume that the
disturbances caused by moving source charge propagate outwardly from the
source charge with finite velocity
c
. These disturbances
originating from the source charge at past time manifest itself as the force acting on stationary test charge at present
time. This means that there is
a time delay
\Delta t
between the past time
t_{s}
when the source charge
has stopped and the present time
t_{p}
when this disturbance has propagated to
the test charge:

\Delta t=t_{p}-t_{s}=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{s})\right|}{c}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)=\frac{qq_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right|^{3}}=

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt=

\displaystyle-\nabla\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\bigg(\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\frac{qq_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\right|^{3}}\bigg)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu c}{4\pi}\frac{q_{s}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{s}(t_{r})}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{r})\right|\left(1-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}(t_{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}(t_{r})\right)}

(53)

With the aid of scalar function
\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
, vector function

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
, and expression
\mu\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}
given by equation
(
51
) the equation (
50
) can be
written as:

\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\mu\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(-\nabla\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\right)

(54)

Furthermore, we now define two vector functions
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(55)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(56)

Using definitions of vector functions
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
given by
equations (
55
) and (
56
) we can rewrite
equation (
54
) as:

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}}{\partial t}

(57)

We shall now investigate the mathematical properties of vector fields
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
. Note that
because for any differentiable vector field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
we can write

\nabla\cdot\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}=0
, from equation (
55
)
it follows that:

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}=0

(58)

The curl of the gradient of any differentiable scalar function
\psi
is
equal to zero, i.e.
\nabla\times\nabla\psi=0
. Thus, taking the curl of
equation (
56
) yields:

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}=-\nabla\times\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}}{\partial t}

(59)

Finally, in Appendix
C
, subsection

C.4
, we have shown that the divergence of vector
field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
is:

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}=\frac{q_{s}}{\epsilon}\delta\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right)=\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)}{\epsilon}

(60)

which completes the derivation of electrodynamic equations from Coulomb’s law.

Thus, it should be evident by now that we have derived Maxwell equations

Furthermore, it should be noted that we have derived the Maxwell equations and Liénard–Wiechert potentials directly from Coulomb’s law without resorting to special relativity or Lorentz transformation.

Derivation of Electrodynamic Energy Conservation Law and Lorentz Force

To derive the electrodynamic energy conservation law from Coulomb’s law we
first consider hypothetical physical setting shown in Fig.
3

where the source charge
q_{s}
is moving along arbitrary trajectory

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)
. Then the source charge
q_{s}
stops at some time in the past
t_{s}
. In this physical setting, closed contour
C
is at rest at
all times. At
present time
t_{p}>t_{s}
all the points inside the sphere of radius
R=c(t_{p}-t_{s})

are affected only by electrostatic Coulomb’s field. The known energy conservation law valid at present
dictates that contour integral of electrostatic field along any closed
contour immersed inside the sphere of radius
R
equals to zero:

Figure 3:
Source charge
q_{s}
is moving along arbitrary trajectory

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)
and it stops at past time
t_{s}
. Closed contour
C
is at rest
at all times. All the points
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
on contour
C
are inside the sphere of
radius
R=c(t_{p}-t_{s})
. At present time
t_{p}>t_{s}
all the points on contour
C

are affected only by Coulomb’s electrostatic field.

\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p})\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\oint_{C}{\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{s})}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{s})\right|^{3}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=0

(61)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}
is Coulomb’s electrostatic field,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t_{s})
is the
position vector of source charge when it stopped moving, and vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}

is the position vector of the point on contour
C
. This electrostatic energy conservation law, valid at present time
t_{p}
,
states that no net work is done in transporting the unit charge along any
closed contour immersed in electrostatic field.

To proceed, we assume that in the past, when the source charge was moving, the energy conservation law is unknown. However, generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem allows us to derive this "unknown" electrodynamic energy conservation law valid in the past from the knowledge of electrostatic energy conservation law valid at present. To derive this unknown electrodynamic electrodynamic conservation law we
consider the contour integral (
61
) at the moment
t

infinitesimally before the time when the source charge stopped:

t=t_{s}-\delta t

(62)

where
\delta t
is infinitesimally small time interval.
If time interval
\delta t
approaches zero (
\delta t\rightarrow 0
) we can
rewrite the contour integral (
61
) as the function of time
t
:

\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\oint_{C}{\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right|^{3}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=0

(63)

Because the integrand on the right hand side of equation
(
63
) is the function of varying time
t
and position vector

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
the generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem can be applied to
rewrite this integrand as the function of past positions and velocities of the
source charge. In fact, such expression is already derived in previous
section as equation (
33
), repeated here for clarity:

\displaystyle\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right|^{3}}=

\displaystyle-\nabla\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\frac{q_{s}}{\epsilon}\delta\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r^{\prime}}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\right)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(64)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\frac{q_{s}}{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\delta\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\right)G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\left[\nabla^{\prime}\times\nabla^{\prime}\times\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\right|}\right]G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

Substituting the first two right hand side terms of equation
(
64
) with equations (
48
) and
(
49
) and combining the result with equations
(
52
) and (
53
), and using

c^{2}=1/\mu\epsilon
yields:

\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right|^{3}}=-\nabla\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(65)

where vector function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
is equal to the last right hand
side term of equation (
64
):

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\left[\nabla^{\prime}\times\nabla^{\prime}\times\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})\right|}\right]G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(66)

Replacing the first two terms on the right hand side of equation
(
65
) with equation (
56
) yields:

\frac{q_{s}}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)}{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t)\right|^{3}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(67)

Then, by inserting equation (
67
) into right hand side of
equation (
63
) it is obtained that:

0=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\oint_{C}{\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\right)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}

(68)

The space-time integral on the right hand side of equation
(
66
) is very difficult to evaluate. However, we can eliminate
vector field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
from the right hand side of equation
(
68
) by the application of Stokes’ theorem:

0=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}+\int_{S}{\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}

(69)

From here, we take the curl of both sides of equation (
67
) and
by combining with equation (
59
) it is obtained that:

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(70)

Because surface
S
and contour
C
are stationary we can write that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
.
Inserting equation (
70
) into equation (
69
)
and taking into account that surface
S
and contour
C
are not moving
yields:

0=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}

(71)

The right hand side of equation (
71
) is unknown energy
conservation principle valid for varying in time dynamic fields

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
and it is derived from
electrostatic energy conservation principle valid at present time. If

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
is replaced by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}
and if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
is replaced by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}

it can be seen that we have just obtained the physical law known in
electrodynamics as Faraday’s law.

From equation (
71
) the conclusion can be drawn about the
nature of Faraday’s law. It represents the energy conservation principle valid
for non-conservative dynamic fields and it is dynamic equivalent of
electrostatic energy conservation principle valid for Coulomb’s electrostatic field.

However, even the Faraday’s law itself can be considered as consequence of
something else.
To see this, consider simply connected volume
V
bounded by surface

\partial V
as shown in Fig.
4
. The surface
\partial V
is union
of two surfaces
S
and
S_{1}
bounded by respective contours
C
and
C_{1}
.
Contours
C
and
C_{1}
consist of exactly the same spatial points, however,
the Stokes’ orientation of these contours is opposite
C=-C_{1}
. Then, using

\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

the first right hand side contour integral of equation (
71
)
can be written as:

\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=-\oint_{C_{1}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=-\int_{S_{1}}{\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S_{1}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}

(72)

Replacing the first right hand side term of equation (
71
)
with equation (
72
) yields different form of dynamic energy
conservation law:

0=\oint_{C}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}_{c}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t_{p}-\delta t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{\partial V}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}

(73)

If we replace
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}
we see that right hand side
of equation (
73
) is time derivative of Gauss’ law for
magnetic fields. The standard interpretation of Gauss’ law for magnetic fields
is that magnetic monopoles do not exist. However, from equation
(
73
) we conclude that alternative interpretation of this law
is that its time derivative represents the dynamic energy conservation law.
From the derivations presented, we might even say that Faraday’s law is the
consequence of Gauss’ law for magnetic fields. It should be noted that these
energy-conservation equations were all derived from simple electrostatic
Coulomb’s law.

Closed surface
\partial V
that bounds volume

bounds surface
S
and contour
C_{1}

bounds surface
S_{1}
. Contours

and

From dynamic energy conservation law the derivation of Lorentz force is
straightforward: we now assume that all the points on surface
\partial V

shown in Fig.
4
have some definite velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
such that

|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}|<<c
. Then the surface
\partial V
is the function of time, hence,

C=C(t)
and
S=S(t)
. Hence, we can rewrite equation (
73
) as the sum of two surface integrals:

\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{\partial V}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}=\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{S(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}+\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{S_{1}(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}=0

(74)

where
\partial V=S(t)\cup S_{1}(t)
.
The Leibniz identity
[
25
]
for moving
surfaces states that for any differentiable vector field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
we can
write:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}=\int_{S(t)}{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}+\left(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\right]\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}-\oint_{C(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}

(75)

Applying the Leibniz identity to the surface integral over surface
S_{1}(t)

in equation (
74
) and using
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=0
yields:

\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{S(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}+\oint_{S_{1}(t)}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}-\oint_{C_{1}(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=0

(76)

Using the result from previous section, i.e.
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
, and applying the Stokes’ theorem yields:

\frac{d}{dt}\oint_{S(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}-\oint_{C_{1}(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}-\oint_{C_{1}(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=0

(77)

Because curves
C_{1}(t)
and
C(t)
comprise of same points, however, Stokes’ orientation of curves
C_{1}(t)
and
C(t)
is opposite, i.e.
C_{1}(t)=-C(t)
, we can rewrite equation (
77
) as:

\oint_{C(t)}{\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\right]\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S(t)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}=0

(78)

Note that equation (
78
) could not be derived from the right
hand side of equation (
71
), i.e. from Faraday’s law, even with Leibniz rule. For that
reason, we might take that the energy conservation law on the right hand side
of equation (
73
) is perhaps more general than the one given
by equation (
71
).

Furthermore, note that the time derivative of the surface integral in equation (
78
) does not represent the work of any force. However, from equation (
73
) we know that the terms in equation (
78
) have dimensions of the work done by electrodynamic force in moving the unit charge along contour
C(t)
. Because the first term in equation (
78
) is contour integral of vector field we can conclude that this term represents the non-zero work done by non-conservative electrodynamic force in transporting the unit charge along contour
C(t)
.

Hence,
just as the left hand side of equation (
73
) represents the
work done by conservative electrostatic force in transporting the unit charge
along contour
C
, the contour integral on the left of equation
(
78
) represents the work done by non-conservative
electrodynamic force in transporting the unit charge along the same contour.
The purpose of surface integral on the left hand side of equation
(
78
) is to balance non-zero work of non-conservative
electrodynamic force along contour
C
.
Thus, it can be concluded that the electrodynamic force
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{D}
on charge

q
moving with velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
along contour
C
is:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{D}=q\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)+q\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)

(79)

Finally, in previous section we have shown that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}
and that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}
. Thus, by replacing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}
it is obtained that:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}_{D}=q\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}\right)

(80)

which is expression for well known Lorentz force.
It was derived theoretically from the knowledge of electrostatic energy conservation law which, in turn, can be derived from Coulomb’s law. Thus, we may say that we have just derived the Lorentz force from simple electrostatic Coulomb’s law.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we have presented the theoretical framework that explains Maxwell equations and the Lorentz force on more fundamental level than it was previously done. Maxwell derived Maxwell equations from experimental Ampere’s force law and experimental Faraday’s law, and Lorentz continued work on Maxwell’s theory to discover the Lorentz force. In last 150 years, no successful theory was presented that would explain Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force on more fundamental level.

To accomplish this, relativistically correct Liénard–Wiechert potentials, Maxwell
equations and the Lorentz force were derived directly from electrostatic
Coulomb’s law.
In contrast to frequently criticized previous attempts to derive Maxwell’s
equations from Coulomb’s law using special relativity and Lorentz
transformation, the Lorentz transformation was not used in our derivations
nor the theory of special relativity. In fact, in this work, dynamic
Liénard–Wiechert potentials, Maxwell equations and Lorentz force were derived
from Coulomb’s law using the following two simple postulates:

(a)

when charges are at rest the Coulomb’s law describes the force acting
between charges

(b)

disturbances caused by moving charges propagate outwardly from moving
charge with finite velocity

The derivation of these dynamic physical laws from electrostatic Coulomb’s law
would not be possible without generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem also
derived in this paper. This theorem allows the vector function of present
position and present time to be written as space-time integral of positions
and velocities at previous time. In contrast, standard Helmholtz decomposition
theorem is valid for functions of space only and it ignores time.

To derive the Lorentz force from Coulomb’s law, in section
4
, the "unknown" dynamic energy conservation
law valid in the past was derived from the knowledge of electrostatic energy
conservation law valid at present. The link between the present and the past
was again provided by generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem. This
"unknown" dynamic energy conservation principle turned out to be Faraday’s law
of induction. Additionally, it was shown that Faraday’s law of induction can
be considered equivalent to time derivative of Gauss’ law for magnetic field. From these energy
conservation considerations the
Lorentz force was derived.

From the presented analysis one important question naturally arises: are
Maxwell’s equations and Lorentz force the consequence of electrostatic
Coulomb’s law? They are most probably not. It is rather the opposite,
Coulomb’s law is the limiting case of Lorentz force when the source charge
becomes stationary. However, as it was shown in this paper, it is entirely
possible to deduce dynamic Maxwell equations and Lorentz force from the
knowledge of simple electrostatic Coulomb’s law.

Finally, this paper attempts to answer another important question:
how can we deduce more general dynamic physical laws from
the limited knowledge provided by static physical law?
The significance of answering this question is that in the future it will
perhaps become possible that similar reasoning could deepen the understanding
of physical laws other than Maxwell equations and Lorentz force.

Derivation of generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem

In this appendix, we derive the generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem

A.1
Preliminary considerations

To clarify notation used throughout this paper we first define position vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=x\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}+y\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}+z\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}

(A.1.81)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}+y^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}+z^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}

(A.1.82)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
are Cartesian, mutually orthogonal, unit basis vectors. Variables
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
are linearly independent variables. Furthermore, throughout this paper we use position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
to indicate the position of the source charge. This position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
is defined as:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=x_{s}(t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}+y_{s}(t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}+z_{s}(t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}

(A.1.83)

where
x_{s}(t^{\prime})
,
y_{s}(t^{\prime})
and
z_{s}(t^{\prime})
are all functions of real variable
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
which is independent of variables
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. The time derivative of position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
is velocity
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
of the source charge:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})}{\partial t^{\prime}}

(A.1.84)

On many occasions in this paper we have used differential operators
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\prime}
defined as:

\displaystyle\nabla

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

(A.1.85)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{\prime}}

(A.1.86)

Operator
\nabla
acts only on functions of variables
x,y,z
, hence, on functions of position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
. On the other hand, operator
\nabla^{\prime}
acts only on functions of variables
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}
, thus, it acts on functions of position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
. For example, if function
f
is the function of position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, that is
f=f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})
we can generally write:

\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})\neq 0\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla^{\prime}f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})=0

(A.1.87)

On the other hand, if function
f
is the function of position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, that is if
f=f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
we can write:

\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=0\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla^{\prime}f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\neq 0

(A.1.88)

Furthermore, because variable
t^{\prime}
is independent of variables
x,y,z
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}
neither operator
\nabla
nor
\nabla^{\prime}
acts on position vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
and velocity vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})
. Using these considerations we see that the following equations are correct:

\begin{array}[]{rcr}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=0&&\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=0\\
\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=0&&\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{s}(t^{\prime})=0\end{array}

(A.1.89)

However, both operators
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\prime}
act on Green’s function

given by equation (
16
). In fact, one can easily verify that the following equations hold:

\begin{array}[]{rcr}\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})&&\nabla^{2}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\nabla^{\prime 2}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})&&\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{\prime 2}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{array}

(A.1.90)

A.2
Generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem

To start deriving generalized Helmholtz decomposition theorem for vector functions of space and time we first consider inhomogeneous transient wave equation:

\nabla^{2}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(A.2.91)

where
G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{t}};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is the function called fundamental solution or Green’s function and
\delta
is Dirac’s delta function. The Green’s function for inhomogeneous wave equation is well known and it represents an outgoing diverging spherical wave:

G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\left(t-t^{\prime}-\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|}{c}\right)}{4\pi\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|}

(A.2.92)

Let us now suppose that vector field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}
is the function of both space
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
and time
t
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
. Using sifting property of Dirac delta function we can write vector function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)
as the volume integral over infinite volume
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and over all the time
\mathbb{R}
as:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.93)

where differential volume element
dV^{\prime}
is
dV^{\prime}=dx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime}
.
We now replace
\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
in equation above with left hand side of equation (
A.2.91
) to obtain:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\left[\nabla^{2}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right]dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.94)

From the discussion presented in section
A.1
of this appendix, we know that D’Alambert operator
\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}
does not act on variables
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
,
z^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
nor does it act on vector function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
. Hence, we can write the D’Alambert operator
\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}
in front of the integral:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=-\left(\nabla^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.95)

Using standard vector calculus identity
\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
we can rewrite equation (
A.2.95
) as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=

\displaystyle\nabla\times\nabla\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.96)

\displaystyle-\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

Because operators
\nabla
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
do not act on variables
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
,
z^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
we can move operator
\nabla
and partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
under right hand side integrals in equation (
A.2.96
). Then using standard vector calculus identities
\nabla\times(\psi\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\nabla\psi\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}+\psi\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and
\nabla\cdot(\psi\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\nabla\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}+\psi\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
, and noting that
\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=0
and
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=0
we can rewrite equation (
A.2.96
) as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=

\displaystyle\nabla\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.97)

\displaystyle-\nabla\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

We now use identities
\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{t}};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{t}};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{t}};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{t}};\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

A.2.97
) as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t)=

\displaystyle-\nabla\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.98)

\displaystyle+\nabla\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\times d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\nabla^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}

Note that the surface
\partial\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an infinite surface that bounds an infinite volume
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{3}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\nabla^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}

(A.2.100)

Using similar considerations, vector calculus identity
\nabla\cdot(\psi\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\nabla\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}+\psi\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and standard divergence theorem
\int_{V}{\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}dV}=\oint_{\partial V}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{\prime}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t^{\prime})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}

(A.2.101)

To treat the last integral on the right hand side of equation (
A.2.98
) we use the following identity:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t^{\prime})=\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t^{\prime})\right)-G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(A.2.102)

Using the identity above and noting that
t^{\prime}
is independent of
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}

(
A.2.98
) as:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

(A.2.103)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

By integrating over
t^{\prime}
, it can be shown that the first right hand side integral in equation (
A.2.103
) vanishes :

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}=\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}\right]_{t^{\prime}\rightarrow-\infty}^{t^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}

t

, thus, the right hand side of equation (

\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}{dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},t;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})dV^{\prime}}}

Appendix B
Novel vector calculus identities

In this appendix we prove two novel vector calculus identities, without which, it would be very difficult, perhaps even not possible, to derive Maxwell’s equations from Coulomb’s law. These two novel vector calculus identities are given by the following two equations:

\displaystyle\int_{V}{\psi\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}dV}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial V}{\psi\left(d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}}-\int_{V}{\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}dV}

(B.107)

\displaystyle\int_{V}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\nabla^{2}\psi dV}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial V}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\right)}-\int_{V}{\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}dV}

(B.108)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
is differentiable vector field,
\psi
is differentiable scalar function,
volume
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is simply connected volume,
\partial V
is the bounding surface of volume
V
and
d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
is differential surface element of
\partial V
such that
d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}dS
. Vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}
is an outward unit normal to the surface
\partial V
. In Cartesian coordinate system the product
\psi\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
can be written in terms of Cartesian components as:

\psi\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\psi\nabla^{2}P_{x}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\psi\nabla^{2}P_{y}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}\psi\nabla^{2}P_{z}

(B.109)

where
P_{x}
,
P_{y}
and
P_{z}
are Cartesian components of vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
are Cartesian unit basis vectors. Using standard vector calculus identity
\nabla\cdot f\boldsymbol{\mathbf{T}}=\nabla f\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{T}}+f\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{T}}
, valid for some scalar function
f
and for some vector function
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{T}}
, we can rewrite equation (
B.109
) as:

\displaystyle\psi\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\nabla\cdot\left(\psi\nabla P_{x}\right)-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla P_{x}

(B.110)

\displaystyle+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\nabla\cdot\left(\psi\nabla P_{y}\right)-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla P_{y}

\displaystyle+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla\cdot\left(\psi\nabla P_{z}\right)-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla P_{z}

To proceed, we now expand the identity
\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
in terms of its Cartesian components as:

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}P_{x}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}P_{y}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}P_{z}\right)

(B.111)

Appendix C

C.1
Derivation of equation (
28

Equation (
24
) allows us to rewrite

,
y^{\prime}

C.1.122
) into equation (

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}

C.1.124
