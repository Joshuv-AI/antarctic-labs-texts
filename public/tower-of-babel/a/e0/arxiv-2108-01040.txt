Bounds for theta sums in higher rank I Research supported by EPSRC grant EP/S024948/1
Jens Marklof, Matthew Welsh

Abstract
Theta sums are finite exponential sums with a quadratic form in the oscillatory phase. This paper establishes new upper bounds for theta sums in the case of smooth and box truncations. This generalises a classic 1977 result of Fiedler, Jurkat and Körner for one-variable theta sums and, in the multi-variable case, improves previous estimates obtained by Cosentino and Flaminio in 2015. Key steps in our approach are the automorphic representation of theta functions and their growth in the cusps of the underlying homogeneous space.



1
Introduction

Consider the exponential sum

\theta_{f}(M,X,\bm{x},\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}f(M^{-1}(\bm{m}+\bm{x}))\;\mathrm{e}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{m}X\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{m}}+\bm{m}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}}\right),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a rapidly decaying cut-off function,
M\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
X
a real symmetric
n\times n
matrix, and
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(represented as row vectors). We also use the shorthand
\mathrm{e}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}z}
.

We refer to
\theta_{f}
as a
theta sum
. If, for example
f(\bm{x})=\exp(-\pi\bm{x} P \transpose{\!\bm{x}} )
for some positive definite matrix
P
, we obtain the classical Siegel theta series

\theta_{f}(M,X,\bm{0},\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\mathrm{e}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{m}Z\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{m}}+\bm{m}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}}\right),

(1.2)

with
Z=X+\mathrm{i}Y
and
Y=M^{-2}P
.
If, on the other hand,
f=\chi_{\mathcal{B}}
is the characteristic function of a bounded set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have the finite sum

\theta_{f}(M,X,\bm{x},\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap(M\mathcal{B}-\bm{x})}\mathrm{e}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{m}X\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{m}}+\bm{m}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}}\right).

(1.3)

In this case we will also use the notation
\theta_{f}=\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
.

The following theorem, which is our first main result, gives an upper bound on the values of theta sums in the limit of large
M
, when the truncation function is in the class of complex-valued Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
let
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
be an increasing function such that series

\sum_{k\geq 0}\psi(k)^{-(2n+2)}

(1.4)

converges.
Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{X}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\mathrm{sym}}
of full Lebesgue measure such that
for
M\geq 1
,
X\in\mathcal{X}(\psi)
,
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\theta_{f}(M,X,\bm{x},\bm{y})=O_{f,X}\big(M^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi(\log M)\big)

(1.5)

The implied constants in (
1.5
) are independent of
M
,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
.

This theorem follows from a geometric representation of
\theta_{f}
as an automorphic function and an application of a dynamical Borel-Cantelli lemma for flows on homogeneous spaces, theorem 1.7 in
[
8
]
. The special case of theorem
1.1
for general smooth theta sums in one variable was considered in
[
14
]
.

Upper bounds for smooth multi-variable theta sums, with an additional linear average in
X
, have played an important role in understanding the value distribution of quadratic forms, see for example the work of Götze
[
6
]
, Buterus, Götze, Hille and Margulis
[
1
]
and the first named author
[
12
,
13
]
.

The second main result of this paper deals with the subtler case when
f
is the characteristic function of a rectangular box
\mathcal{B}
. In this setting Cosentino and Flaminio
[
3
]
established the bound

\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(M,X,\bm{0},\bm{y})=O_{X,\epsilon}\big(M^{\frac{n}{2}}(\log M)^{n+\frac{1}{2n+2}+\epsilon}\big)

(1.6)

for the unit cube
\mathcal{B}=[0,1]^{n}
, any
\epsilon>0
and almost every
X
. The following theorem improves on this by a factor of
(\log M)^{n}
and produces a uniform bound for rectangular boxes of the form
\mathcal{B}=(0,b_{1})\times\cdots\times(0,b_{n})
, with
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
ranging over compacta.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, and choose

\psi
as in theorem
1.1
.
Then there exists a subset
\mathcal{X}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\mathrm{sym}}
of full Lebesgue measure such that

\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(M,X,\bm{x},\bm{y})=O_{X}\big(M^{\frac{n}{2}}\psi(\log M)\big)

(1.7)

for all
M\geq 1
,
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathcal{K}
,
X\in\mathcal{X}(\psi)
,
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The implied constants are independent of
M
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
.

To compare this with the bound obtained in
[
3
]
, note that
\psi(t)=t^{\frac{1}{2n+2}+\epsilon}
satisfies (
1.4
) and thus resulting bound (
1.7
) indeed improves (
1.6
) by a factor of
(\log M)^{n}
.
The paper
[
3
]
also established the stronger bound

\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(M,X,\bm{x},\bm{y})=O_{X}\big(M^{\frac{n}{2}}\big)

(1.8)

for “bounded-type”
X
that are badly approximable by rationals (these form a set of measure zero), and weaker bounds for
X
that satisfy more relaxed Diophantine conditions. These same bounds can also be obtained from our techniques, but with no further improvements.

In the case
n=1
our estimate (
1.7
) matches the optimal results found by Fiedler, Jurkat and Körner
[
5
]
. For
n>1
, obtaining the lower bounds in these papers (which follow from the harder part of the Borel-Cantelli lemma) is more subtle, and we hope to develop an approach to this elsewhere.

The bounds for the theta sum in (
1.2
) and (
1.5
) are uniform in the shift
\bm{x}
and the linear phase
\bm{y}
.
In forthcoming work
[
15
]
we will consider improved bounds valid for almost all
(X,\bm{x},\bm{y})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, generalising the results for
n=1
found in
[
4
]
.

This paper is organised as follows. We begin in section
2
by recalling some basic facts about the Heisenberg group and symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
as well as their semi-direct product, the Jacobi group, including the Iwasawa decomposition, Haar measure, and parabolic subgroups.
We then review the Schrödinger and Segal-Shale-Weil representations of the Heisenberg and symplectic group, respectively.
Following the method of
[
11
]
, these representations are used to define theta functions in section
4
.

The theta functions satisfy an automorphy condition on a certain, morally-speaking discrete subgroup of the Jacobi group.
This subgroup is discussed in section
3
.
Its projection to the symplectic group is just the integral symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(n,\mathbb{Z})
.
The bulk of section
3
concerns a fundamental domain (a slight modification of Siegel’s classic fundamental domain
[
18
]
based on the work of
[
7
]
) and its properties.

In section
4
we define the theta functions and state their automorphy properties before analysing their asymptotic behaviour.
While for the proof of theorems
1.1
and
1.2
we only need the upper bound contained in corollary
4.5
, the full asymptotics contained in theorem
4.4
may be of independent interest.
The proof of theorem
4.4
combines the properties of the fundamental domain constructed in section
3
and basic estimates for sums over integers together with the Langlands decompositions of the maximal parabolic subgroups of the symplectic group.

We prove theorems
1.1
and
1.2
in section
5
.
Apart from the upper bound in corollary
4.5
, our method relies on upper bounds for the measure of rapidly diverging orbits under a particular one-parameter diagonal action in the symplectic group as well as (for theorem
1.2
) a resolution of the singular cutoff function in (
1.3
) using an
n
-parameter diagonal action.
The estimates for the first part are largely based on the easy part of the proof of theorem 1.7 in
[
8
]
, which is also a main input into the method in
[
3
]
.
The complications arising from the
n
-parameter flow however prevent a straightforward application of this theorem, so we instead proceed more directly with a self-contained proof.

2
Heisenberg, symplectic, and Jacobi groups

We define the (
2n+1
)-dimensional Heisenberg group
H
to be the set
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
with multiplication given by

(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1},t_{1})(\bm{x}_{2},\bm{y}_{2},t_{2})=\big(\bm{x}_{1}+\bm{x}_{2},\bm{y}_{1}+\bm{y}_{2},t_{1}+t_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{y}_{1}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{x}_{2}}-\bm{x}_{1}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}_{2}})\big).

(2.1)

The rank
n
symplectic group
G=\mathrm{Sp}(n,\mathbb{R})
is defined by

G=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R}):gJ_{0}\prescript{t}{}{\!g}=J_{0}\}

(2.2)

where

J_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}

(2.3)

with
I
the
n\times n
identity matrix.
We have the alternative characterization

G=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}:A\prescript{t}{}{\!B}=B\prescript{t}{}{\!A},\ C\prescript{t}{}{\!D}=D\prescript{t}{}{\!C},\ A\prescript{t}{}{\!D}-B\prescript{t}{}{\!C}=I\right\}.

(2.4)

The group
G
acts by on
H
via

(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)^{g}=(\bm{x}A+\bm{y}C,\bm{x}B+\bm{y}D,t)

(2.5)

where

g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}.

(2.6)

Since
g
preserves the symplectic form
J_{0}
used to define the multiplication (
2.1
), this action is by automorphisms, i.e.
(h_{1}h_{2})^{g}=h_{1}^{g}h_{2}^{g}
.
We define the semi-direct product group
H\rtimes G
, called the Jacobi group, to be the set of all
(h,g)
,
h\in G
and
g\in G
, with multiplication given by

(h_{1},g_{1})(h_{2},g_{2})=(h_{1}h_{2}^{g_{1}^{-1}},g_{1}g_{2}).

(2.7)

2.1
Iwasawa decomposition and Haar measure

The intersection
K=G\cap\mathrm{O}(2n)
is a maximal compact subgroup of
G
and

Q\mapsto k(Q)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Re}(Q)&-\mathrm{Im}(Q)\\
\mathrm{Im}(Q)&\mathrm{Re}(Q)\end{pmatrix}

(2.8)

defines an isomorphism from the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(n)
to
K
.
The Iwasawa decomposition of
G
with respect to
K
implies that any
g\in G
can be written uniquely as

g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{Y}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}k(Q)

(2.9)

where
X
and
Y
are symmetric,
Y
is positive definite, and
Q\in\mathrm{U}(n)
.
Here we have chosen
Y^{\frac{1}{2}}
to by upper-triangular with positive diagonal entries, and we often further decompose
Y=UV\prescript{t}{}{U}
with
U
upper-triangular unipotent and
V
positive diagonal.
We also note that
Y^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is always interpreted as
(Y^{\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}
, not
(Y^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
We make frequent use of the following expressions for the
X
,
Y
, and
Q
coordinates,

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=(C\prescript{t}{}{\!C}+D\prescript{t}{}{\!D})^{-1}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(A\prescript{t}{}{\!C}+B\prescript{t}{}{\!D})(C\prescript{t}{}{\!C}+D\prescript{t}{}{\!D})^{-1}

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=(C\prescript{t}{}{\!C}+D\prescript{t}{}{\!D})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(D+iC),

(2.10)

where as before
(C\prescript{t}{}{\!C}+D\prescript{t}{}{\!D})^{\frac{1}{2}}
is chosen to be upper-triangular with positive diagonal entries.

The Haar measure on
G
can be easily expressed in terms of the Iwasawa decomposition.
For

g=\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}UV^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{U}^{-1}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}k(Q),

(2.11)

the Haar measure
\mu
on
G
is given by

\differential\mu(g)=\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\differential x_{ij}\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\differential u_{ij}\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq n}v_{jj}^{-n+j-2}\differential v_{jj}\right)\differential Q.

(2.12)

Here
\differential Q
denotes the Haar measure on
\mathrm{U}(n)
and
\differential x_{ij}
,
\differential u_{ij}
,
\differential v_{jj}
are respectively the Lebesgue measures on the entries of
X
,
U
,
V
.

We note that if
g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}
with
D
invertible, then we can write

g=\begin{pmatrix}I&BD^{-1}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\prescript{t}{}{\!D}^{-1}&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
D^{-1}C&I\end{pmatrix}.

(2.13)

Therefore the set of
g\in G
having the form

g=\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
T&I\end{pmatrix}

(2.14)

for
X
,
T
symmetric and
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
is open and dense in
G
.
We claim that in these coordinates we have, up to multiplication by a positive constant,

\differential\mu(g)=(\det A)^{-2n-1}\left(\prod_{i\leq j}\differential x_{ij}\right)\left(\prod_{i,j}\differential a_{ij}\right)\left(\prod_{i\leq j}\differential t_{ij}\right).

(2.15)

where
\differential x_{ij}
,
\differential a_{ij}
,
\differential t_{ij}
are the Lebesgue measure on the entries of
X
,
A
,
T
.

To verify (
2.15
) up to a positive constant it suffices to check that the right side is invariant under left multiplication by generators of
G
.
The invariance under matrices
\begin{pmatrix}I&X_{1}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}
with
X_{1}
symmetric is obvious, and the invariance under matrices
\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}_{1}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
follows from

\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}_{1}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\\
=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}X\prescript{t}{}{\!A}_{1}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}A&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}_{1}^{-1}\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

(2.16)

and that the replacements
X\leftarrow A_{1}^{-1}X\prescript{t}{}{\!A}_{1}^{-1}
,
A\leftarrow A_{1}^{-1}A
change

\prod_{i\leq j}\differential x_{ij}\leftarrow(\det A_{1})^{-n-1}\prod_{i\leq j}\differential x_{ij},\quad\prod_{i,j}\differential a_{ij}\leftarrow(\det A_{1})^{-n}\prod_{i,j}\differential a_{ij}.

(2.17)

To verify the invariance under
\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}
we may restrict further to the set of
g
of the form (
2.14
) with
X
invertible, as this is still an open, dense set.
We then have

\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&X\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
T&I\end{pmatrix}\\
=\begin{pmatrix}I&-X^{-1}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X^{-1}A&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!X}\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
T+\prescript{t}{}{\!A}X^{-1}A&I\end{pmatrix}.

(2.18)

The invariance then follows from the fact that the replacement
X\leftarrow X^{-1}
changes

\prod_{i\leq j}\differential x_{ij}\leftarrow(\det X)^{-n-1}\prod_{i\leq j}\differential x_{ij}.

(2.19)

2.2
Parabolic subgroups

We recall that conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups of
G
are in bijection with subsets of the
n
positive simple roots, see for example section 4.5.3 of
[
19
]
.
Here we make the choice of positive simple roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
where, for
1\leq l<n
,

\alpha_{l}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=a_{l}a_{l+1}^{-1}

(2.20)

and

\alpha_{n}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=a_{n}^{2}.

(2.21)

Here

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}

(2.22)

is positive diagonal.
See for example section 5.1 of
[
19
]
.

The parabolic corresponding to a subset
L\subset\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
is given by

P_{L}=N\bigcap_{\alpha\in L}Z(\mathrm{ker}(\alpha))

(2.23)

where
Z(\mathrm{ker}(\alpha))
is the centraliser in
G
of the kernel of the root
\alpha
and

N=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}U&X\prescript{t}{}{U}^{-1}\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{U}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}:\\
U\ \mathrm{upper\ triangular\ unipotent},\ X\ \mathrm{symmetric}\bigg\}.

(2.24)

The maximal parabolic subgroups correspond to subsets
L
of size
n-1
and we denote them by
P_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq n
corresponding to root
\alpha_{l}
not in the set
L
.
For
1\leq l<n
, we write an arbitrary element of
P_{l}
as

\begin{pmatrix}I&R_{l}&T_{l}-S_{l}\prescript{t}{}{\!R}_{l}&S_{l}\\
0&I&\prescript{t}{}{\!S}_{l}&0\\
0&0&I&0\\
0&0&-\prescript{t}{}{\!R}_{l}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{l}I&0&0&0\\
0&I&0&0\\
0&0&a_{l}^{-1}I&0\\
0&0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{l}&0&0&0\\
0&A_{l}&0&B_{l}\\
0&0&\prescript{t}{}{U}_{l}^{-1}&0\\
0&C_{l}&0&D_{l}\end{pmatrix}

(2.25)

where
R_{l}
and
S_{l}
are
l\times(n-l)
matrices,
T_{l}
is
l\times l
symmetric,
a_{l}>0
,
U_{l}\in\mathrm{GL}(l,\mathbb{R})
with
\det U_{l}=\pm 1
, and
g_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{l}&B_{l}\\
C_{l}&D_{l}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp}(n-l,\mathbb{R})
.
For
l=n
, we write an arbitrary element of
P_{n}
as

\begin{pmatrix}I&T_{n}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}I&0\\
0&a_{n}^{-1}I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{n}&0\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!U_{n}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

(2.26)

where
T_{n}
is
n\times n
symmetric,
a_{n}>0
, and
U_{n}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
with
\det U_{n}=\pm 1
.
The factorizations (
2.25
), (
2.26
) are in fact the Langlands decompositions of
P_{l}
,
P_{n}
, which write an arbitrary element of the parabolic subgroup as a product of elements of a nilpotent subgroup, a diagonal subgroup, and a semi-simple subgroup.
For general considersations regarding the Langlands decomposition, see section 7.7 of
[
10
]
.
The author’s lecture notes
[
16
]
contain explicit calculations for the symplectic group
G
along these lines.

2.3
Schrödinger and Segal-Shale-Weil representations

The Schrödinger representation
W
of
H
acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by the unitary transformations

W(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)f(\bm{x}_{0})=\mathrm{e}(-t+\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{x}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}}+\bm{x}_{0}\prescript{t}{}{\bm{y}})f(\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{x}).

(2.27)

We remark that this definition of the Schrödinger representation differs slightly from the conventional one; they are of course unitarily equivalent.

Given
g\in G
, we obtain another representation
W^{g}
of
H
by
W^{g}(h)=W(h^{g})
.
By the Stone-von Neumann theorem, there exists unitary operators
R(g)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

W^{g}=R(g)^{-1}WR(g).

(2.28)

The relation (
2.28
) actually defines
R(g)
up to a scalar multiple.
Regardless of the choice of this scalar (which we make below), we have

R(g_{1}g_{2})=\rho(g_{1},g_{2})R(g_{1})R(g_{2})

(2.29)

for a nontrivial, unitary cocycle
\rho:G\times G\to\mathbb{C}
.
Thus
R
defines a projective representation of
G
, which is called the Segal-Shale-Weil representation.
The projective representation
R
can be extended to a true representation of the metaplectic group – the simply connected double cover of
G
, but we do not make use of this construction.

The following proposition gives expressions for
R(g)
for certain
g
and on a dense subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
In particular the proposition makes precise the choice of scalar multiple in our definition of
R
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then for

g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(2.30)

we have

R(g)f(\bm{x})=|\det A|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}A\prescript{t}{}{\!B}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{x}}\right)f(\bm{x}A),

(2.31)

and for

g=J_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-I\\
0&0&I&0\\
0&I&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.32)

with square blocks of size
l
,
n-l
,
l
, and
n-l
along the diagonal,
0\leq l\leq n
, we have

R(g)f(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-l}}f(\bm{x}^{(1)},\bm{y}^{(2)})\mathrm{e}(-\bm{x}^{(2)}\prescript{t}{}{\!\bm{y}}^{(2)})\differential\bm{y}^{(2)}

(2.33)

with
\bm{x}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}^{(1)}&\bm{x}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}
.
Moreover, for
g
of the form (
2.30
) and any
g^{\prime}\in G
, we have
\rho(g,g^{\prime})=\rho(g^{\prime},g)=1
.

This proposition is a summary of various calculations found in
[
11
]
.
The forthcoming lecture notes by the authors
[
16
]
will give self-contained proofs.

We remark that together with the Bruhat decomposition

G=\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq n}P_{n}J_{l}P_{n}

(2.34)

where
P_{n}
is the maximal parabolic subgroup (
2.26
), proposition
2.1
allows one to compute
R(g)
for any
g\in G
.
For example, for

g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&AC^{-1}\\

,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\neq 0\end{subarray}}(v(m+x)^{2}+y)^{-A}\ll_{A}v^{-\frac{1}{2}}(v+y)^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.13)

and, if in addition

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(v(m+x)^{2}+y)^{-A}\ll_{a,A}v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A}

for

.

Proof.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\neq 0\end{subarray}}(v(m+x)^{2}+y)^{-A}\leq\sum_{0<|m|\leq\sqrt{\frac{y}{v}}}y^{-A}+\sum_{|m|>\sqrt{\frac{y}{v}}}v^{-A}|m+x|^{-2A}.

(4.15)

The first sum here is
0
if
y<v
, otherwise it is at most
v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}
.
The second sum is at most

2^{2A}v^{-A}\sum_{|m|>\sqrt{\frac{y}{v}}}|m|^{-2A},

(4.16)

which is
\ll_{A}v^{-A}
if
y<v
and
\ll_{A}v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}
otherwise.
The estimate (
4.13
) now follows as
v^{-A}
, respectively
v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}
, is
\ll v^{-\frac{1}{2}}(v+y)^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}
if
y<v
, respectively
y\geq v
.

Turning to (
4.14
), we have

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(v(m+x)^{2}+y)^{-A}\leq\sum_{|m|\leq\sqrt{\frac{ay}{v}}}y^{-A}+\sum_{|m|>\sqrt{\frac{ay}{v}}}v^{-A}|m+x|^{-2A}.

(4.17)

The first sum here is
\ll_{a}v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A+\frac{1}{2}}
, while the second sum is at most

2^{2A}v^{-A}\sum_{|m|>\sqrt{\frac{ay}{v}}}|m|^{-2A}\ll_{a,A}v^{-\frac{1}{2}}y^{-A+\frac{1}{2}},

(4.18)

so (
4.14
) follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

We have

Proof.

We recall that
g\in\mathcal{D}
is written as

g=\begin{pmatrix}U&X\prescript{t}{}{U}^{-1}\\

0&V^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}k(Q)

(5.4)

V=V(g)=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&v_{n}\end{pmatrix}

(5.5)

The Haar measure
\mu
is then Lebesgue measure with respect to the entries of
X
and the off-diagonal entries of
U

\mathrm{U}(n)

Q
, and the measure given by

From the construction of
\mathcal{D}
, it is clear that the entries of
U
and
X
are constrained to a compact region.
Since
\mathrm{U}(n)
is also compact, we have by proposition
3.1

\mu(\{g\in\mathcal{D}_{n}:\det V(g)\geq R\})\\
\ll\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{j}\geq\frac{3}{4}v_{j+1}\\
v_{1}\cdots v_{n}\geq R\end{subarray}}{\int\cdots\int}v_{1}^{-n-1}v_{2}^{-n}\cdots v_{n}^{-2}\differential v_{1}\differential v_{2}\cdots\differential v_{n}.

(5.7)

Changing variables

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{j}-u_{j+1}\geq\log\frac{3}{4}\\

This transformation has determinant

(5.10)

As

) follows.
∎

0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{s}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}R&-\mathrm{e}^{-s}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}S\\
\mathrm{e}^{s}\prescript{t}{}{Y}^{-\frac{1}{2}}S&\mathrm{e}^{-s}\prescript{t}{}{Y}^{-\frac{1}{2}}R\end{pmatrix},

(5.16)

so in view of (

Y(g_{s})=Y^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{2s}S\prescript{t}{}{\!S}+\mathrm{e}^{-2s}R\prescript{t}{}{\!R}\right)^{-1}\prescript{t}{}{Y}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.17)

The ratio of the right to the left side of (
5.13
) is then

\det( \e^{2s} S \transpose{\!S} + \e^{-2s} R \transpose{\!R}).

(5.18)

Using the diagonalization argument from the proof of lemma
4.2
, we can multiply by orthogonal matrices to make
R
and
S
diagonal with entries
\cos\phi_{j}
and
\sin\phi_{j}
.
The determinant (

) is then

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n}(\mathrm{e}^{2s}\sin^{2}\phi_{j}+\mathrm{e}^{-2s}\cos^{2}\phi_{j}).

(5.19)

Since
|s|\leq 1
, this is clearly bounded from above by a constant depending on
n
, and since
\sin^{2}\phi_{j}
and
\cos^{2}\phi_{j}
cannot both be less than
\frac{1}{2}
, it is also bounded away from

This establishes (
5.13
).

Now we have

D(\Gamma g_{s})=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\det V(\gamma g_{s})=\det V(\gamma_{0}g_{s})

(5.20)

for some
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
.
By (
5.13

.
The same reasoning with
g_{s}
replaced by
g
leads to the reverse inequality, establishing (
5.11
).
∎

The following lemma is similar to lemma
5.2
in that we control the change in

under a particular action.
Here the action is more general, however we only need to consider small neighbourhoods in
\Gamma\backslash G
.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a constant
\epsilon_{n}>0
depending only on

such that for all
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G
,

satisfying

0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
T&I\end{pmatrix}\bigg)\asymp D(\Gamma g).

Proof.

As in the proof of lemma

0&\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad g_{T}=g\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
T&I\end{pmatrix}.

We write

0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\

=\prescript{t}{}{Y}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left((SA+R\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}T)\prescript{t}{}{\!(SA+R\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}T)}+R\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}A^{-1}\prescript{t}{}{\!R}\right)Y^{-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\quad=

(5.26)

if

can be made sufficiently small so that the symmetric matrix

+R\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}T\prescript{t}{}{\!A}\prescript{t}{}{\!S}+SATA^{-1}\prescript{t}{}{\!R}+R\prescript{t}{}{\!A}^{-1}T^{2}A^{-1}\prescript{t}{}{\!R}

∎

5.3
Proof of theorem

We record the following lemma that dyadically decomposes the indicator function of the open interval

, noting that both the singularities at

and

need to be resolved.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists a smooth, compactly supported function
f_{1}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\chi_{1}(x)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\left(f_{1}\left(2^{j}x\right)+f_{1}\left(2^{j}(1-x)\right)\right),

(5.35)

where
\chi_{1}
is the indicator function of the open unit interval
(0,1)

We note that
[
2
]
has an explicit construction of a function

satisfying (
5.35
) that is however only twice differentiable.
As we make no effort here to determine constants in our estimates, we sacrifice explicitness for smoothness.

Proof.

We let
f_{0}
be a non-negative, smooth function such that
f_{0}(x)=0
for
x\leq 0
,
f_{0}(x)=1
for
x\geq 1
, and

f_{0}(x)+f_{0}(1-x)=1

(5.36)

for all
0\leq x\leq 1
.
We then define the smooth function

on the interval by

f_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mathrm{if\ }x\leq\frac{1}{6}\\
f_{0}(6x-1)&\mathrm{if\ }\frac{1}{6}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{3}\\
f_{0}(2-3x)&\mathrm{if\ }\frac{1}{3}\leq x\leq\frac{2}{3}\\
0&\mathrm{if\ }\frac{2}{3}\leq x.\end{cases}

(5.37)

Let us now consider the expression

\sum_{j\geq 0}\left(f_{1}(2^{j}x)+f_{1}(2^{j}(1-x))\right),

(5.38)

which is clearly
0
if
x\not\in(0,1)
.
If
0<x\leq\frac{1}{3}
then
f_{1}(2^{j}(1-x))=0

is nonzero for exactly two values of

and
j_{0}+1

We have

f_{1}(2^{j_{0}}x)+f_{1}(2^{j_{0}+1}x)=f_{0}(2^{j_{0}}6x-1)+f_{0}(2-2^{j_{0}}6x)=1

by (

).
We similarly find that (

for
\frac{2}{3}\leq x<1
.
When

, only the

) is nonzero.
We have

f_{1}(x)+f_{1}(1-x)=f_{0}(2-3x)+f_{0}(3x-1)=1

by the condition (
5.36
).
∎

For a subset
