Counting pairs of cycles whose product is a permutation with restricted cycle lengths
Miklós Bóna and Boris Pittel

Abstract
. We find exact and asymptotic formulas for the number of pairs ( s , z ) (s,z) of N N -cycles s s and z z such that all cycles of the product s ⋅ z s\cdot z have lengths from a given set of integers. We then apply one of these results to prove a surprisingly high lower bound for the number of permutations whose block transposition distance from the identity is at least ( N + 1 ) / 2 (N+1)/2 .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Permutations with restricted cycle lengths

We are interested in counting
some
of the permutations of a set
[N]
whose cycle lengths belong to a given subset
A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots\}
. Permutations of this kind were studied by Sachkov
[
14
]
,
under the name of
A
-permutations. We stress ”some”, since our focus is on permutations of
[N]
that are products of pairs of
N
-long cycles. Some special cases have direct consequences
in the theory of biologically motivated sorting algorithms. We will explore a very surprising one of these,
related to sorting a permutation by block transpositions, in Section
3
. One of our permutation-counting
results will imply the existence of a high number of permutations of length
N
that take at least
(N+1)/2

block transpositions to sort. This is remarkable, because for most values of
N
, there are no known examples
of permutations of length
N
that would take more than that many block transpositions to sort.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{N}(A)
denote the total number of
all
such
permutations. It is well known (cf. Bóna
[
4
]
) that

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}_{N}(A)=N!\cdot[x^{N}]\exp(F_{A}(x)),\quad F_{A}(x):=\sum_{r\in A}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}.

Equivalently,
p_{N}(A)
, the probability that the uniformly random permutation of
[N]
has all its cycle lengths from
A
,
is given by

(1.2)

p_{N}(A)=[x^{N}]\exp(F_{A}(x)).

We will show that this leads to the explicit formulas

(1.3)

\displaystyle p_{k}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu_{r}\geq 0,\,r\in A^{c}\atop\sum_{r}r\mu_{r}\leq k}\prod_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{(-1)^{\mu_{r}}}{r^{\mu_{r}}\mu_{r}!},

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(A^{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sum_{r\in A^{c}}r\mu_{r}=\ell}\,\prod_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{1}{r^{\mu_{r}}\mu_{r}!},

where
A^{c}:=\{1,2,\dots\}\setminus A
is the complement of
A
in the set of all positive integers.
In the context of sorting algorithms, particularly relevant are the cases
A=\mathcal{E}
, the set of even numbers, and
A=\mathcal{O}
, the set of odd numbers.

The task of counting
all
such permutations is well resolved. It is well known
[
4
,
18
]
that

(1.4)

F_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\log(1-x^{2})^{-1/2},\quad F_{\mathcal{O}}(x)=\log\bigl(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\bigr)^{1/2}.

It follows that

(1.5)

\displaystyle p_{N}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\binom{N}{N/2},\quad N\text{ even},

\displaystyle p_{N}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &2^{-N}\binom{N}{N/2},&&N\text{ is even},\\
&2^{-N+1}\binom{N-1}{(N-1)/2},&&N\text{ is odd}.\end{aligned}\right.

We point out that the enumeration formulas that follow from (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) have known combinatorial proofs. The interested reader should consult the references given in the solution Exercise 5.10 in
[
18
]
.

It is significantly more challenging to obtain a tractable counterpart of (
1.2
) for
q_{N}(A)
,
the probability that the product of two independent, uniformly random
N
-long cycles has all its cycle lengths from a given set

A
. Our main result consists of two complementary parts. First, for an arbitrary
A\subset\{1,2,\dots\}
, we will
get a sum-type formula for the numbers
q_{N}(A)
in terms of the numbers
p_{k}(A)
and
p_{\ell}(A^{c})
, including a wide range extension of well known results for
A=\{2,3,\dots\}
. While explicit, the expression does not lend itself easily to asymptotic analysis, since the summands have alternating signs. For two important cases,
A
being either the set of even numbers or the set of odd numbers, we find a way to obtain the sum-type formulas with positive summands only,
much more conducive to asymptotic computations. We will present these formulas and their proofs next.

1.2.
Products of Maximal Cycles

The following theorem summarizes the formulas we will obtain for
q_{N}(A)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all positive integers
N
, the following hold.

(
a
)

For a given
A\subset\{1,2,\dots\}
,

(1.6)

q_{N}(A)=\tfrac{N}{N+1}\biggl(\sum_{k+\ell=N\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N}{\ell}^{-1}p_{k}(A)\bigl(p_{\ell}(A^{c})-p_{\ell-1}(A^{c})\bigr)\biggr),\\

where
p_{0}(A)=p_{0}(A^{c})=1
,
p_{1}(A^{c}):=0
; for
k,\ell\geq 1
,
p_{k}(A),\,p_{\ell}(A^{c})
are given by (
1.3
).

(
b
)

For
A=\mathcal{E}
(even positive integers) and
A=\mathcal{O}
(odd positive integers), we have

(1.7)

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2N}{(N+1)2^{N}}\sum_{k+\ell=N/2,\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}\tfrac{1}{k}\binom{2(k-1)}{k-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\biggl(1-\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}\biggr)\!,\,N\text{ even},

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{N}{2^{N}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N,\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{N}{2^{N-2}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2,\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell+1}^{-1},\,N\text{ even},

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{N}{(N+1)2^{N-1}}\!\!\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1,\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}\!\!\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}\biggl(\!\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}\!\!+\binom{N}{2\ell+1}^{-1}\biggr),\,N\text{ odd}.

In each of the above sums the dominant terms are those with the smallest admissible
k
.
Consequently
q_{N}(\mathcal{E}),\,q_{N}(\mathcal{O})\sim(\pi N/2)^{-1/2}
, as
N\to\infty
.

The set
\mathcal{E}
is a special case of
\mathcal{D}
, the full set of positive integers divisible by a fixed
d>1
. So, we count the permutations whose cycle lengths are all divisible by
d
. Here

(1.8)

F_{\mathcal{D}}=\tfrac{1}{d}\log(1-x^{d})^{-1},

and consequently, for
N\equiv 0(\text{mod }d)
,

p_{N}(\mathcal{D})=\tfrac{1}{(N/d)!}\prod_{r=0}^{N/d-1}(r+1/d).

Similarly to the proof for
q_{N}(\mathcal{E})
, we will show in the Appendix 1 that

(1.9)

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{N}{d(N+1)}\sum_{dk+d\ell=N,\atop 0\leq k,\ell\leq N}\tfrac{1}{k!\ell!}\biggl(1-(-1)^{dk}\binom{N}{d\ell}^{-1}\biggr)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/d)\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/d),

and for
d=2
the equation (
1.9
) yields the top formula in (
1.7
).

Note. For
A=\{2,3,\dots\}
, a permutation
p
of
[N]
is type-A if and only if
p
has no cycles of length
1
, that is, when
p
is a derangement. In this case, the formula (
1.3
) becomes

(1.10)

p_{k}(A)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{k}\tfrac{(-1)^{\mu}}{\mu!},\quad p_{\ell}(A^{c})=\tfrac{1}{\ell!}.

With some work (see Appendix 2), the equations (
1.6
) and (
1.10
) imply

(1.11)

(N-1)!\,q_{N}(A)=(-1)^{N-1}\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{j\leq k<N}(-1)^{k}k!.

The reason we are interested in
(N-1)!\,q_{N}(A)
is that it equals the number of
N
-long cycles
p
such that
the product
(12\dots N)p
is a derangement. (Comfortingly, the RHS of (
1.11
) is indeed an integer for all
N
, since
k!/j!
is an integer for
k\geq j
.) And it is well-known that this property holds if and only there is no
i\in[N]
such that
p(i)=i-1(\text{mod }N)
, see for instance Stanley
[
16
]
, or Charalambides
[
7
]
. It follows from the inclusion-exclusion principle that
C_{N}
, the total number of such maximal cycles
p
is given by

(1.12)

C_{N}=N!\sum_{\mu=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{(-1)^{\mu}}{(N-\mu)\mu!}+(-1)^{N}.

Consequently,
(N-1)!\,q_{N}(A)=C_{N}
, which is not obvious at all. Using Maple, we checked that this identity holds for all
N\leq 10
. At this moment it is unclear to us how
to prove this identity formally, that is, without using the fact that both sides count the same objects.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We start with the following general formula, Pittel
[
13
]
and Zagier
[
19
]
. Let
S_{N}
be the set of all permutations on
[N]
, and let
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
be some conjugacy classes from
S_{N}
. Let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
be the random
independent
elements of
S_{N}
, where
\sigma_{j}
is distributed
uniformly
on
C_{j}
. For

\sigma:=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}
, we have

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}(\sigma=s)=P_{\sigma}(s):=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda})^{-k+1}\chi^{\lambda}(s)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\chi^{\lambda}(C_{j}).

Here the sum is taken over all integer partitions (Young diagrams) of
N
, and
f^{\lambda}
is the dimension of the irreducible representation of
S_{N}
associated with
\lambda
, given by the hook formula

(2.2)

f(\lambda)=\tfrac{N!}{\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}h_{i,j}}.

Here
h_{i,j}
is the cardinality of the hook for a cell
(i,j)
, that is,
1
plus the number of cells in
\lambda
to the east and to the south from
(i,j)
. Furthermore,
\chi^{\lambda}(s)
and
\chi^{\lambda}(C_{j})
are the values of the associated character
\chi^{\lambda}
at
s
and at the elements of the conjugacy classes
C_{j}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{p}}
be a generic conjugacy class consisting of all permutations of
[N]
, with cycle lengths
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots
, with
\sum_{j}p_{j}=N
. By the Murnaghan-Nakayama rule, (see Macdonald
[
12
]
, Sagan
[
15
]
. Stanley
[
17
]
), the common value of
\chi^{\lambda}(s)
for
s\in\mathcal{C}_{p}
is given by

(2.3)

\chi^{\lambda}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{p}})=\sum_{T}(-1)^{\text{ht}(T)}.

Here the sum is over all
rim hook
diagrams
T
of shape
\lambda
and type
\mathbb{p}
, that is, over all ways to empty the diagram
\lambda
by successive deletions of
border strips
, (strips consisting of only current border cells), one strip at a time, of
weakly increasing
lengths
p_{j}
. Further,
\text{ht}(T)
is the sum of heights of the individual border strips (number of their rows minus
1
) in
T
. We will see that, contrary to its forbidding appearance, the formula (
2.3
)
alone
leads to a surprisingly simple expression for the values
\chi^{\lambda}(\mathcal{C})
, if
\mathcal{C}
is the conjugacy classs of one-cycle permutations.

We focus on
k=2
, and
C_{1}=C_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{N}
, the set of all maximal,
N
-long, cycles. Here the composition
\mathbb{p}
consists of a single component
N
, and the range of
T
is empty unless the diagram
\lambda
is a single hook
\lambda^{*}
, with one horizontal row of length
\lambda_{1}
and one column of height
\lambda^{1}
,
\lambda_{1}+\lambda^{1}=N+1
, in which case
\chi^{\lambda*}(\mathcal{C}_{N})=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}
. And by the hook formula (
2.2
), we obtain

(2.4)

f(\lambda^{*})=\tfrac{N!}{N\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\lambda^{1}-1}\!\!i\,\,\,\times\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\lambda_{1}-1}\!j}=\binom{N-1}{\lambda_{1}-1}.

Furthermore, by (
2.3
), given one-hook diagram
\lambda^{*}
, the value of
\chi^{\lambda^{*}}(s)
depends on a generic permutation
s
only through
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}(s)
, where
\nu_{r}=\nu_{r}(s)
is the total number of
r
-long cycles in
s
. We need the following tool.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{p}}
be a generic conjugacy class consisting of all permutations of
[N]
, with cycle lengths
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots
, with
\sum_{j}p_{j}=N
.
Let
\nu_{j}
be the number of
p_{i}
’s equal to
j
. Then

(2.5)

\chi^{\lambda^{*}}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{p}})=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}}]\tfrac{x}{1-x}\prod_{r\geq 1}(x^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}},

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda^{1}
are the arm length and the leg length of the hook
\lambda^{*}
.

Proof.

The argument is an edited proof of this identity in
[
13
]
. In a generic rim hook diagram
T
, the last rim hook is a hook diagram with a row and a column of sizes
\mu_{1}
and
\mu^{1}
, with
\mu_{1}\leq\lambda_{1}
and
\mu^{1}\leq\lambda^{1}
. Define
\ell
by
\ell+1=\mu_{1}+\mu^{1}
. All the preceding rim hooks in
T
are horizontal and vertical segments of the
arm and of the leg of
\lambda^{*}
. Let us evaluate the total number of the rim hooks diagrams with
h_{r}
, the number of the horizontal hooks of size
r
; so
h_{r}\in[0,\nu_{r}]
for
r\neq\ell
and
h_{\ell}\in[0,\nu_{\ell}-1]
. The admissible
\mathbb{h}:=\{h_{r}\}
must meet the additional constraint

(2.6)

\sum_{r}h_{r}r+\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}.

If
v_{r}
is similarly defined as the total number of vertical hooks of size
r
, then the preceding constraint implies that

\sum_{r}v_{r}r+\mu^{1}=\lambda^{1}
. The total number
of the rim hook diagrams
T
, with parameters
\mu_{1}
,
\mu^{1}
, and
\mathbb{h}
, is

\binom{\nu_{\ell}-1}{h_{\ell}}\prod_{r\neq\ell}\binom{\nu_{r}}{h_{r}}=\prod_{r}\binom{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}{h_{r}},\quad\delta_{\ell,r}=\delta_{\ell,r}:=\mathbb{I}(r=\ell).

We do not have to multiply the above product by the multinomial coefficient
\binom{\nu-1}{\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{\ell-1},\nu_{\ell}-1,\nu_{\ell+1},\dots}
since the rim hooks are deleted in the fixed, increasing order of their lengths. Further, the
\mu^{1}
-long leg of the last (longest) hook contributes
\mu^{1}-1
to the height
T
, while the total contribution
of the vertical rim hooks is
(\lambda^{1}-\mu^{1})
, the sum of their sizes, minus
\sum_{r}(\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}-h_{r})
, their total number. So

\displaystyle ht(T)

\displaystyle=(\mu^{1}-1)+(\lambda^{1}-\mu^{1})-\sum_{r}(\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}-h_{r})

\displaystyle\equiv\lambda^{1}-1+\sum_{r}(\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}-h_{r})\,\,(\text{mod }2).

Therefore the total contribution to
\chi^{\lambda^{*}}(s)
from the rim hook diagrams with the last rim hook
\mu^{*}
is

\displaystyle(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}\sum_{\mathbb{h}\text{ meets }\eqref{5.1}}\prod_{r}(-1)^{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}-h_{r}}\binom{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}{h_{r}}

\displaystyle=\,\,(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1}}]\prod_{r}\sum_{h_{r}}(-1)^{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}-h_{r}}(\xi^{r})^{h_{r}}\binom{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}{h_{r}}

\displaystyle=\,\,(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1}}]\prod_{r}(\xi^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}.

Since
\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1}\in[\lambda_{1}-\min(\ell,\lambda_{1}),\lambda_{1}-1]
, we have

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda^{*}}(s)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}\sum_{t=\lambda_{1}-\min(\ell,\lambda_{1})}^{\lambda_{1}-1}[\xi^{t}]\prod_{r}(\xi^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\sum_{\ell}\biggl(\,\sum_{u=0}^{\min(\ell,\lambda_{1})-1}\xi^{u}\biggr)\prod_{r}(\xi^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}-\delta_{\ell,r}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\,\sum_{\ell}\tfrac{\xi^{\min(\ell,\lambda_{1})}-1}{(\xi-1)(\xi^{\ell}-1)}\prod_{r}(\xi^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}.

Here

\tfrac{\xi^{\min(\ell,\lambda_{1})}-1}{(\xi-1)(\xi^{\ell}-1)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tfrac{1}{\xi-1},&&\ell\leq\lambda_{1},\\
&\tfrac{\xi^{\lambda_{1}}-1}{(\xi-1)(\xi^{\ell}-1)},&&\ell>\lambda_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

Since

\tfrac{\xi^{\lambda_{1}}-1}{(\xi-1)(\xi^{\ell}-1)}=\tfrac{1}{\xi-1}+\sum_{j\geq\lambda_{1}}c_{j}\xi^{j},

we see that

\chi^{\lambda^{*}}(s)=(-1)^{\lambda^{1}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{1}{\xi-1}\prod_{r}(\xi^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}.

∎

Substituting formulas (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) into (
2.1
) and setting
\nu=\sum_{r}\nu_{r}
, we obtain the equality

(2.7)

\mathbb{P}(\sigma=s)=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}\binom{N-1}{\lambda_{1}-1}^{-1}\!\!(-1)^{\lambda^{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}}]\tfrac{x}{1-x}\prod_{r}(x^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}.

Furthermore, using the terminology in Sachkov
[
14
]
, for a given
A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots\}
, we call
s
an
A
-permutation if
\nu_{r}=0
for all
r\notin A
. Let us use (
2.7
) to obtain a hopefully tractable formula for
q_{N}(A)
, the probability that the product
s
of two
N
-cycles is an
A
-permutation.
To this end, we need to sum the right-hand side of (
2.7
) over all
\boldsymbol{\nu}
with
\nu_{r}=0
for
r\notin A
.
To begin, notice that the RHS of (
2.7
) depends on the cycle-type
\{\nu_{r}(s)\}
of the permutation
s
only via the rightmost
product
\prod_{r}(x^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}
. Observing that
the total number of permutations with given cycle counts
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\{\nu_{r}\}
is

\tfrac{N!}{\prod\limits_{r}(r!)^{\nu_{r}}\nu_{r}!}\cdot\prod_{r^{\prime}}((r^{\prime}-1)!)^{\nu_{r^{\prime}}}=\tfrac{N!}{\prod\limits_{r}r^{\nu_{r}}\nu_{r}!},

we have

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}:\,\nu_{r}=0,\,\forall\,r\notin A\atop\sum_{r}r\nu_{r}=N}\tfrac{N!}{\prod\limits_{r}r^{\nu_{r}}\nu_{r}!}\prod_{r}(x^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}=N!\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}:\,\nu_{r}=0,\,\forall\,r\notin A\atop\sum_{r}r\nu_{r}=N}\prod_{r}\bigl(\tfrac{x^{r}-1}{r}\bigr)^{\nu_{r}}\tfrac{1}{\nu_{r}!}\\
=N!\,[y^{N}]\prod_{r\in A}\sum_{m\geq 0}\bigl(\tfrac{x^{r}-1}{r}\bigr)^{m}\cdot\tfrac{y^{mr}}{m!}=N!\,[y^{N}]\prod_{r\in A}\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{y^{r}(x^{r}-1)}{r}\Bigr)\\
=N!\,[y^{N}]\exp\bigl(F(xy)-F(y)\bigr),\quad F(\eta):=\sum_{r\in A}\tfrac{\eta^{r}}{r}.

Consequently,

(2.8)

q_{N}(A)=\sum_{s\text{ is type}-A}\mathbb{P}(\sigma=s)\\
=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}\binom{N-1}{\lambda_{1}-1}^{-1}(-1)^{\lambda^{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}}]\tfrac{x}{1-x}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}:\,\nu_{r}=0,\,\forall\,r\notin A\atop\sum_{r}r\nu_{r}=N}\tfrac{N!}{\prod\limits_{r}r^{\nu_{r}}\nu_{r}!}\prod_{r}(x^{r}-1)^{\nu_{r}}\\
=[y^{N}]\ e^{-F(y)}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}\binom{N-1}{\lambda_{1}-1}^{-1}\!\!(-1)^{\lambda^{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}}]\tfrac{x}{1-x}e^{F(xy)}.\\

Here

\displaystyle\binom{N-1}{\lambda_{1}-1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{(\lambda_{1}-1)!(N-\lambda_{1})!}{(N-1)!}=N\int_{0}^{1}z^{\lambda_{1}-1}(1-z)^{N-\lambda_{1}}\,dz,

\displaystyle(-1)^{\lambda^{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}}]\tfrac{x}{1-x}e^{F(xy)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N+1-\lambda_{1}}[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{e^{F(xy)}}{1-x}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N}(-1)^{\lambda_{1}-1}[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{e^{F(xy)}}{1-x}=(-1)^{N}[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(-xy))}{1+x}.

Therefore the sum in the last row of (
2.8
) equals

(-1)^{N}N\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}z^{\lambda_{1}-1}(1-z)^{N-\lambda_{1}}\,dz\biggr)[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(-xy))}{1+x}\\
=(-1)^{N}N\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}z^{\lambda_{1}-1}[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(-xy))}{1+x}(1-z)^{(N-1)-(\lambda_{1}-1)}\,dz\\
=(-1)^{N}N\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in[N]}[x^{\lambda_{1}-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(-xyz))}{1+xz}\times\bigl[x^{(N-1)-(\lambda_{1}-1)}\bigr]\tfrac{1}{1-x(1-z)}\,dz\\
=(-1)^{N}N\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(-xyz))}{(1+xz)(1-x(1-z))}\,dz\\
=-N\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]\tfrac{\exp(F(xyz))}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz.

In combination with (
2.8
) we have proved

(2.9)

q_{N}(A)=-N[y^{N}]\,e^{-F(y)}\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]\,\tfrac{e^{F_{A}(xyz)}}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz.

(a)
Let us compute the integral on the right-hand side of (
2.9
) for a general
A
. Since

(2.10)

\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}=\tfrac{1}{x}\tfrac{1}{1-xz}-\tfrac{1}{x}\cdot\tfrac{1}{1+x(1-z)}\\
=\tfrac{1}{x}\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}-\tfrac{1}{x}\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}x^{j}(1-z)^{j},

we have

[x^{N-1}]\,\tfrac{\exp(F_{A}(xyz)}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}=[x^{N}]e^{F_{A}(xyz)}\biggl(\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}-\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}x^{j}(1-z)^{j}\biggr)\\
=\sum_{k+j=N}(yz)^{k}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\bigl(z^{j}-(-1)^{j}(1-z)^{j}\bigr).\\

So, the integral on the right-hand side of (
2.9
) equals

\sum_{k+j=N}y^{k}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\int_{0}^{1}z^{N}\,dz-\sum_{k+j=N}(-1)^{j}y^{k}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\int_{0}^{1}z^{k}(1-z)^{j}\,dz\\
=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+j=N}y^{k}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}-\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+j=N}(-1)^{j}\binom{N}{j}^{-1}y^{k}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}.

Then, since
e^{-F_{A}(y)}=\sum_{\ell}y^{\ell}[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}
,

[y^{N}]\,e^{-F_{A}(y)}\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]\,\tfrac{e^{F_{A}(xyz)}}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+\ell=N}[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\\
-\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+j=N\atop k+\ell=N}(-1)^{j}\binom{N}{j}^{-1}[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\\
=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+\ell=N}[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}\\
-\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k+\ell=N}(-1)^{j}\binom{N}{\ell}^{-1}[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}.

The first sum equals
[x^{N}]e^{-F_{A}(x)+F_{A}(x)}=0
. In the second sum,
[u^{k}]e^{F_{A}(u)}=p_{k}(A)
for

k\geq 1
, and
[u^{0}]e^{F_{A}(u)}=1=:p_{0}(A)
. Furthermore, recalling that
A^{c}
denotes the complement of
A
in
\{1,2,\dots\}
, we get that

[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}=[v^{\ell}]\exp\biggl(-\log\tfrac{1}{1-v}+\sum_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{v^{r}}{r}\biggr).

So recalling that
p_{0}(A^{c}):=1
and
p_{-1}(A^{c}):=0
, we have

[v^{\ell}]e^{-F_{A}(v)}=[v^{\ell}](1-v)e^{F_{A^{c}}(v)}=p_{\ell}(A^{c})-p_{\ell-1}(A^{c}),\quad\ell\geq 0.

Collecting the pieces, we obtain

(2.11)

q_{N}(A)=\tfrac{N}{N+1}\sum_{k+\ell=N}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N}{\ell}^{-1}p_{k}(A)\bigl(p_{\ell}(A^{c})-p_{\ell-1}(A^{c})\bigr).

Thus to evaluate the probability that the product of two cycles is an
A
-permutation, it suffices to know the probabilities that the uniformly distributed permutations of various length are
A
-permutations or

A^{c}
-permutations.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle p_{k}(A)

\displaystyle:=[x^{k}]\exp(F_{A}(x)]=[x^{k}]\exp\biggl(\sum_{r>1}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}-\sum_{r<m}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)

\displaystyle=[x^{k}]\tfrac{1}{1-x}\exp\biggl(-\sum_{r<m}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)=\sum_{j\geq 0}[x^{k-j}]\exp\biggl(-\sum_{r<m}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\geq 0}\tfrac{(-1)^{\mu}}{\mu!}\sum_{j\geq 0}[x^{k-j}]\biggl(\sum_{r<m}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\geq 0}(-1)^{\mu}\sum_{j\leq k}\sum_{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m-1}\atop\sum_{r}\mu_{r}=\mu,\,\sum_{r}r\mu_{r}=k-j}\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}\tfrac{1}{r^{\mu_{r}}\mu_{r}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu_{r}\geq 0,\,r\in A^{c}\atop\sum_{r}r\mu_{r}\leq k}\prod_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{(-1)^{\mu_{r}}}{r^{\mu_{r}}\mu_{r}!},

and

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(A^{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[x^{\ell}]\exp\biggl(\sum_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)=\sum_{j}[x^{\ell}]\tfrac{\biggl(\sum_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{x^{r}}{r}\biggr)^{j}}{j!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sum_{r\in A^{c}}r\mu_{r}=\ell}\,\prod_{r\in A^{c}}\tfrac{1}{r^{\mu_{r}}\mu_{r}!}.

(b)
For
A=\mathcal{E}
and
A=\mathcal{O}
, we get explicit formulas for
q_{N}(A)

by combining (
2.11
) with (
1.5
) for those
A
’s. However it turns out possible to obtain considerably simpler, sum-type, formulas
for
q_{N}(\mathcal{E})
and
q_{N}(\mathcal{O})
with only positive summands. It is done by properly changing the order of steps in the derivation above.

Start with
q_{N}(\mathcal{E})
. In this case, we carry the operation

[y^{N}]
inside the integration in (
2.9
):

(2.12)

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=-N\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}y^{N}]\tfrac{(1-y^{2})^{1/2}}{(1-(xyz)^{2})^{1/2}}\cdot\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz

\displaystyle=-N\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]H_{N}(xz)\cdot\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz,

where

(2.13)

H_{N}(xz):=[y^{N}]\tfrac{(1-y^{2})^{1/2}}{(1-(xyz)^{2})^{1/2}}\\
=[y^{N}]\biggl(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\binom{1/2}{k}(-y^{2})^{k}\biggr)\cdot\biggl(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\binom{-1/2}{\ell}(-(xyz)^{2})^{\ell}\biggr)\\
=[y^{N}]\biggr(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\tfrac{y^{2k}}{k!}\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/2)\biggr)\cdot\biggl(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\tfrac{(xyz)^{2\ell}}{\ell!}\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2)\biggr)\\
=\tfrac{(xz)^{N}}{(N/2)!}\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{N/2-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1\atop 2k+2\ell=N}\tfrac{(xz)^{2\ell}}{k!\ell!}\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/2)\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2).

So, combining (
2.13
) and (
2.10
), we evaluate

[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}\bigl[z^{j}-(-1)^{j}(1-z)^{j}\bigr]\\
=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1,2k+2\ell=N,\atop j+2\ell=N}\tfrac{z^{2\ell}}{k!\ell!}\bigl[z^{j}-(-1)^{j}(1-z)^{j}\bigr]\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/d)\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/d)\\
=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1,\,2k+2\ell=N}\tfrac{1}{k!\ell!}\,\bigl[z^{N}-(-1)^{2k}\,(1-z)^{2k}z^{2\ell}\bigr]\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/2)\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2).\\

Integrating over
z\in[0,1]
, using

\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)^{2k}z^{2\ell}\,dz=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1},

and multiplying by
-N
, we obtain

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{N}{2(N+1)}\sum_{k\geq 1,\,2k+2\ell=N}\tfrac{1}{k!\ell!}\biggl(1-(-1)^{2k}\binom{N}{2k}^{-1}\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/2)\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2).

Since

\tfrac{1}{k!}\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}(r-1/2)=\tfrac{1}{2^{2(k-1)}k}\binom{2(k-1)}{k-1},\quad\tfrac{1}{\ell!}\prod_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-1}(r^{\prime}+1/2)=\tfrac{1}{2^{2\ell}}\binom{2\ell}{\ell},

we conclude that

q_{N}(\mathcal{E})=\tfrac{2N}{(N+1)2^{N}}\sum_{k+\ell=N/2}\tfrac{1}{k}\binom{2(k-1)}{k-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\biggl(1-\binom{N}{2k}^{-1}\biggr)\!,\quad N\text{ even}.

Consider
A=\mathcal{O}
. This time,

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(F_{\mathcal{O}}(xyz)-F_{\mathcal{O}}(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{1+xyz}{1-xyz}}\cdot\sqrt{\tfrac{1-y}{1+y}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1+xyz}{\sqrt{1-(xyz)^{2}}}\cdot\tfrac{1-y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}},

and the equation (
2.9
) yields

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle=-[x^{N-1}y^{N}]N\int_{0}^{1}\tfrac{1+xyz}{\sqrt{1-(xyz)^{2}}}\cdot\tfrac{1-y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\cdot\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz

\displaystyle=-[x^{N-1}]N\int_{0}^{1}H_{N}(xz)\cdot\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\,dz,

where

(2.14)

H_{N}(xz):=[y^{N}]\tfrac{1+xyz}{\sqrt{1-(xyz)^{2}}}\cdot\tfrac{1-y}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\\
=[y^{N}](1+xyz)(1-y)\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\bigl(\tfrac{(xyz)^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{\ell}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}\bigl(\tfrac{y^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{k}.

(b1)
If
N
is even, then (
2.14
) yields

H_{N}(xz)=[y^{N}](1-xy^{2}z)\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\bigl(\tfrac{(xyz)^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{\ell}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}\bigl(\tfrac{y^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{k}\\
=2^{-N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(xz)^{2\ell}-2^{-N+2}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(xz)^{2\ell+1}.

Therefore

[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}=2^{-N}z^{N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\\
-2^{-N+2}z^{N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell},\\

and

[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}x^{j}(1-z)^{j}=2^{-N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(1-z)^{2k}z^{2\ell}\\
+2^{-N+2}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(1-z)^{2k+1}z^{2\ell+1}.\\

Therefore

\displaystyle q_{N}(\text{odd})=-N\int_{0}^{1}[x^{N-1}]\Bigl(H_{N}(xz)\cdot\tfrac{1}{(1-xz)(1+x(1-z))}\Bigr)\,dz

\displaystyle=-N\int_{0}^{1}\biggl[2^{-N}z^{N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}-2^{-N+2}z^{N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-2^{-N}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(1-z)^{2k}z^{2\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-2^{-N+2}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}(1-z)^{2k+1}z^{2\ell+1}\biggr]\,dz.

Integrating over
z\in[0,1]
, we obtain

\displaystyle q_{N}(\mathcal{O})=-\tfrac{N}{2^{N}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}+\tfrac{N}{2^{N-2}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\tfrac{N}{2^{N}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\tfrac{N}{2^{N-2}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell+1}^{-1}.

Here

\displaystyle\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}=[\eta^{N/2}]\biggl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}\eta^{k}\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle=[\eta^{N/2}]\tfrac{1}{1-4\eta}=4^{N/2}=2^{N},

and likewise

\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}=2^{N-2}.

Therefore the formula for
q_{N}(\text{odd})
simplifies to

(2.15)

q_{N}(\mathcal{O})=\tfrac{N}{2^{N}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}\\
+\tfrac{N}{2^{N-2}(N+1)}\sum_{2k+2\ell=N-2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{N}{2\ell+1}^{-1}.

(b2)
If
N
is odd, then (
2.14
) yields

H_{N}(xz)=[y^{N}](y(xz-1))\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\bigl(\tfrac{(xyz)^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{\ell}\cdot\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}\bigl(\tfrac{y^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{k}\\
=\tfrac{xz-1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}(xz)^{2\ell}.

Consequently

[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}=\tfrac{1}{2^{N-1}}\biggl[z[x^{N-1}]\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}(xz)^{2\ell}\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}\\
-[x^{N}]\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}(xz)^{2\ell}\sum_{j\geq 0}(xz)^{j}\biggr]\\
=\tfrac{1}{2^{N-1}}\biggl[z\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}z^{2\ell+2k}-z^{N}\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}\biggr]=0.

And similarly

[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}x^{j}(1-z)^{j}\\
=\tfrac{1}{2^{N-1}}\biggl[\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}z^{2\ell+1}(1-z)^{2k}\\
+\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}z^{2\ell}(1-z)^{2k+1}\biggr].

So, integrating
-[x^{N}]H_{N}(xz)\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}x^{j}(1-z)^{j}
over
z\in[0,1]
and multiplying the result by
-N
, we obtain

(2.16)

q_{N}(\mathcal{O})\!=\!\tfrac{N}{(N+1)2^{N-1}}\!\!\sum_{2\ell+2k=N-1}\!\!\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\binom{2k}{k}\biggl(\!\binom{N}{2\ell}^{-1}\!\!+\binom{N}{2\ell+1}^{-1}\biggr).

The proof of the part
(b)
is complete, and so is the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(p,q)

\mathcal{C}_{N}\times\mathcal{C}_{N}

3.
Block Transposition Sorting

Our motivation for obtaining exact and asymptotic results regarding the product of two long cycles comes from
a biologically motivated sorting algorithms called
block transposition sorting
, Bafna and Pevzner
[
1
]
. A step in this sorting algorithm consists of swapping
adjacent
blocks of entries. The lengths of the two blocks do not have to be the same.
That is,

u=u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{i}u_{i+1}\cdots u_{j}u_{j+1}\cdots u_{k}u_{k+1}\cdots u_{N}

is turned into the permutation

v=u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{i}u_{j+1}\cdots u_{k}u_{i+1}\cdots u_{j}u_{k+1}\cdots u_{N}.

If
u,v\in S_{N}
, and
t
is the smallest integer so that there exists a sequence of

t
block transpositions that turn
u
into
v
, then we say that

t=\mathrm{btd}(u,v)
is the
block transposition distance
of
u
and
v
.

The block transposition
distance of two permutations is
left-invariant
, that is, if
u
,
v
and
r
are permutations of length
N
, then

\mathrm{btd}(u,v)=\mathrm{btd}(ru,rv)
. Selecting
r=v^{-1}
, this means that
\mathrm{btd}(u,v)=\mathrm{btd}(v^{-1}u,\mathrm{id})
, where
\mathrm{id}
denotes the identity permutation.
This reduces our distance-measuring problem to a
sorting problem
, that is, we can focus, without loss of generality, on the block transposition distance of a given permutation
from the
identity permutation
. Therefore, we will write
\mathrm{btd}(u)
for
\mathrm{btd}(u,\mathrm{id})
.

Several natural and difficult questions arise. Which permutation
u
is the furthest away from
\mathrm{id}
? What is
\mathrm{td}(N)=\max_{u\in S_{N}}\mathrm{btd}(u)
?
It is known, Eriksson et al.,
[
11
]
, that
\mathrm{td}(N)\leq\lfloor 2N/3\rfloor
. On the lower end
[
11
]
,

\mathrm{btd}(N\cdots 21)=\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
, which led to the conjecture
\mathrm{(}td)(N)=\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
. However, this has been disproved, Elias and Hartman
[
10
]
, for
N=4k+1\geq 17
.
For such
N
, there is a permutation
u\in S_{N}
so that
\mathrm{btd}(u)=2k+2=\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil+1
. So there is a considerable gap between the best known lower and upper bound for

\mathrm{td}(N)
.

As there are almost no known permutations
u
satisfying the inequality
\mathrm{btd(u)}>(N+1)/2
, there is intrinsic interest in finding out how many permutations
v

satisfy
\mathrm{btd}(v)=\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
. In this paper, we will find a lower bound for the number of such permutations. This lower bound will be significantly higher than what could be
deduced before, based on the results in Cranston et al.
[
8
]
and Christie
[
3
]
.

Crucially, there is a bijection
C
from the set of all permutations of length
N
to the set of all
permutations of length
N+1
that are of the form
s\cdot z
, where
s=(12\cdots(N+1))
and
\nu(z)=1
, that is,
z
is a
cycle of length
N+1
. This bijection is defined
[
3
]
with the help of a graph called the
cycle graph
, but we will not need that definition in this paper.
Let
\nu_{odd}(\pi)
be the number of odd cycles of the
permutation
\pi
. The following lemma shows the importance of the bijection
C
for us.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
1
]

For all permutations
u
of length
N
, the inequality

\mathrm{btd}(u)\geq\frac{N+1-\nu_{odd}(C(u))}{2}
holds.

Clearly, the upper bound of Lemma
3.1
is the strongest when
\nu_{odd}(C(u))=0
, that is, when
all cycles of
C(u)
are even. We are now in a position to prove a lower bound for the number of such permutations
u
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The number of permutations of length
n
that take at least
\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
block transpositions to sort is at least
N!\,q_{N+1}(\mathcal{E})
, where
q_{\cdot}(\mathcal{E})
is defined by the first equation in
(\ref{5*})
.

Consequently, the fraction of such permutations among all permutations of length
n
is asymptotically at least
1/\sqrt{\pi N/2}
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.1
shows that
\mathrm{btd}(u)\geq\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
when all cycles of
C(u)
are even. The existence of bijection
C
shows that the number of such permutations
equals the number of pairs
q
that consist of a single cycle of length
N+1
so that

(12\cdots(N+1))\cdot q
has even cycles only. And, by the definition of
q_{N+1}(\mathcal{E})
, the number of such pairs is
N!\,q_{N+1}(\mathcal{E})
, and their portion
among all
N!
permutations is asymptotically equal to
(\pi N/2)^{-1/2}
,
see Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Note that Theorem
3.2
is significant for two reasons.

First, the result of Theorem
3.2
is significantly stronger than what was known before. In particular, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that
\mathrm{btd}(u)\geq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
holds when

C(u)
has exactly one odd cycle. The number of such permutations
u
is known, Boccara
[
2
]
, Bóna and Pittel
[
6
]
, to be
2N!/(N+2)
if
N
is even, and 0 if
N
is odd, so the ratio of such permutations among all permutations of length
N
is
2/(N+2)
if
n
is even. This lower bound is weaker than what we have just proved, by a factor of

c/\sqrt{N}
. Another lower bound, also significantly weaker than the one we just proved, can be deduced from the results in
[
8
]
,
where the authors considered
cut-and-paste
sorting, an operation that is stronger than block transposition sorting.
However, that lower bound just shows that the relevant ratio is at least
\sqrt{N}/2^{N}
, which is again weaker than the result above.

Second, and probably more importantly, we have just proved the existence of a lot of permutations that are very close to the known worst case. The decreasing permutation

N\cdots 21
takes
\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
block transpositions to sort, and as we mentioned before, no permutation of length
N
is known to be harder to sort unless
N=4k+1\geq 17
,
and even in that case, only one permutation is known to be harder to sort, and that permutation needs only one additional block transposition to be sorted
[
10
]
.
So, Theorem

3.2
shows that at least
1/\sqrt{\pi N/2}
of all permutations are either equal to the strongest
known construction for worst case, or are just one step away from it.

4.
Further directions

It is natural to ask if other special cases Theorem
1.1
have application in sorting algorithms. The answer is in the affirmative. A
block interchange
is like a block transposition except that the blocks that are swapped do not have to be consecutive. Then a theorem in
[
3
]
shows that
(N+1-\nu(C(u))/2
is
equal

to the
block interchange distance of the permutation
u
of length
n
to the identity. It follows that if

C(u)
has at least one even cycle, then the block interchange distance and the block transposition distance of
u
to the identity must be different. We hope to explore this fact in a subsequent paper.

\displaystyle(N-1)!\,q_{N}(A)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-1)^{N-k-1}(N-k-1)k!\sum_{j\leq k}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\biggl[\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{N-k-1}(N-k-1)\,k!\biggr].

Here

\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{N-k-1}(N-k-1)\,k!\\
=N(-1)^{N-1}\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{k}k!-(-1)^{N}\sum_{j+1\leq k\leq N+1}(-1)^{k}k!\\
=(-1)^{N-1}\biggl[(N+1)\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{k}k!-(-1)^{j}\,j!+(-1)^{N+1}\,(N+1)!\biggr].

Therefore

(N-1)!\,q_{N}(A)=\tfrac{(-1)^{N-1}}{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\\
\times\biggl[(N+1)\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{k}k!-(-1)^{j}\,j!+(-1)^{N+1}\,(N+1)!\biggr]\\
=(-1)^{N-1}\biggl[\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{j\leq k\leq N}(-1)^{k}\,k!-1\biggr]+N!\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\\
