The virtual element method for the 3D resistive magnetohydrodynamic model
Lourenço Beirão da Veiga, Franco Dassi, Gianmarco Manzini, Lorenzo Mascotto

Abstract
We present a four-field virtual element discretization for the time-dependent resistive Magnetohydrodynamics equations in three space dimensions, focusing on the semi-discrete formulation. The proposed method employs general polyhedral meshes and guarantees velocity and magnetic fields that are divergence free up to machine precision. We provide a full convergence analysis under suitable regularity assumptions, which is validated by some numerical tests. AMS subject classification : 65N12; 65N15; 76E25; 76W05. Keywords : resistive magnetohydrodynamics; Navier-Stokes equations; Maxwell’s equations; virtual element method; polyhedral meshes.



1
Introduction

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the physical-mathematical framework that
describes the dynamics of magnetic fields in electrically conducting
fluids, e.g., plasmas and ionized
gases
[
17
,
18
]
.
Indeed, the electrically charged particles moving in a plasma
generate an electromagnetic field that self-consistently interacts
with the fluid motion through the Lorentz force acting on such
particles.
The theoretical framework of MHD has widely been used to develop
predictive mathematical models, for example, in astrophysics (solar
wind and space weather
[
41
, Chapter 1]
, galactic
jets
[
56
]
), geophysics (dynamo
theory
[
57
]
), and nuclear fusion (design of
fusion reactors, ignition in
inertial confinement
fusion

[
33
,
55
,
43
]
).
Roughly speaking, MHD resorts on a strongly nonlinear coupling of a
fluid flow submodel and an electromagnetic one.
In a general setting, as the one considered in this work, the fluid
flow submodel is based on the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
and the electromagnetic submodel on the Maxwell equations.
A thorough description of the MHD models, their variants, derivation,
and physical and mathematical properties can be found in many
textbooks and review papers,
e.g.,
[
36
,
65
,
17
,
18
]

just to mention a few.

Despite its ubiquity in scientific applications, which is reflected by
the number of papers and books published in the last few decades, the
computer resolution of the system of the MHD equations is still a
formidable and challenging task and may pose pitfalls to the numerical
scientists.
Here, we shortly review some of the more critical points, with
particular attention on the usefulness of polyhedral meshes and
related schemes, always referring all interested readers to the rich
technical literature for specific and detailed expositions.

First, vorticial and/or shear flows may lead to large domain
deformations, i.e., severe mesh deformations, also with the occurence
of non-planar mesh faces, in the Lagrangian and Arbitrary
Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) numerical
frameworks
[
49
,
69
]
.
Such mesh deformations are treated by remeshing the computational
domain; then, by remapping the unknowns to the new
mesh
[
68
]
.
An optimal remap algorithm should be accurate, cheap, and
feature-preserving, i.e., it should preserve the divergence-free
constraints, the solution positivity, and conserve mass, momentum, and
energy, cf.
[
22
]
.
However, current remap algorithms can be too expensive, prone to
significant accuracy losses, and not feature-preserving.
Therefore, we infer that a good approximation method should be
accurate and stable even in presence of severe mesh deformations in
order to reduce the number of remeshing steps in a numerical
simulation.

Next, low-order accurate algorithms can be affected by an excessive
numerical diffusion and dispersion.
For example, numerical diffusion can smear physical vorticity and
shocks at an unacceptable level and even force the magnetic
reconnection in ideal MHD models, which is clearly an artificial and
physically meaningless effect.
Numerical diffusion and dispersion can be reduced by increasing the
order of the approximation and by using carefully selected polygonal
or polyhedral meshes.
In
[
38
,
54
]
, the finite difference time domain (FDTD) method
[
76
]
is applied to a grid of hexagonal
prisms and yields much less numerical dispersion and anisotropy than
on regular hexahedral grids where such method is normally considered.

The final major point that we want to consider is related to the
divergence-free nature of both the fluid velocity and the magnetic flux fields.
If such constraints are not accurately satisfied, possibly at the
machine precision level, unreliable or even unphysical solutions may
result from a numerical simulation.
The consequence of the violation of the divergence-free constraint for
the magnetic flux field has widely been investigated in the literature.
It was seen that the numerical simulations are prone to significant
errors, see, e.g.,

[
18
,
19
,
34
,
73
]
,
as fictitious forces and an unphysical behavior may appear,
cf.
[
34
]
.
For this reason, in the last two decades, a great effort has been
devoted to the development of divergence-free numerical
approximations.
Just to mention a few possible approaches, the divergence-free
constraint of the magnetic flux field can explicitly be enforced by
introducing a Lagrange multiplier in the set of the unknowns,
cf.
[
37
]
; by using
a special flux limiter in the formulation of the numerical scheme,
cf.
[
58
]
; by minimizing a special energy
functional in a least squares finite element formulation,
cf.
[
53
]
.
However, such fixing strategies can result in costly inefficient
schemes, they can be in conflict with each other or not suitable to
polyhedral meshes.
A major breakthrough was provided by realizing that “classical”
numerical discretizations fail to reproduce the divergence-free nature
of these fields if the discrete version of the divergence applied to
the discrete version of the rotational operator does not annihilate at
the zero machine precision.
In fact, a small but not zero remainder can significantly accumulate
over the many cycles of a long-time calculation, thus breaking the
constraint.
This understanding has led to the design of numerical approximation
techniques in the framework of compatible/mimetic methods, as for
example
in
[
68
,
69
,
60
]
.
A major drawback of compatible/mimetic discretizations
is that they are only low-order accurate and can be too dispersive
and diffusive.
The discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method offers high-order accurate
discretizations suitable to general polyhedral meshes that can reduce
the overall numerical diffusion and dispersion.
Nonetheless, DG methods are not compatible and may require a costly
divergence-cleaning procedure based on solving an additional global
equation, thus resulting in very expensive calculations.
Finally, we note that the FEM literature for MHD models on tetrahedral
and hexahedral meshes is very broad.
Here, we limit ourselves to recalling the divergence-free schemes
in
[
50
,
45
,
48
]
,
see the related work
[
70
]
as well, and the partial
convergence analysis results from
[
46
,
66
]

In this work, we start exploring and presenting the design of
numerical approximations of the MHD equations based on the virtual element method (VEM).
Our goal is to address at least part of the issues discussed so far,
but in a framework that makes it possible to address all of them in
the future developments of this research project.
The VEM, initially proposed
in
[
4
]
for
the numerical approximation of elliptic problems, is a Galerkin
projection method such as the finite element method (FEM).
The major difference between the VEM and the FEM is that in the formulation
and practical implementation of the VEM we do not need an explicit
knowledge of the basis functions that generate the finite element
approximation spaces.
In fact, all the bilinear forms and linear functionals of the discrete
variational formulation are built using suitable polynomial
projections that are always computable from a careful choice of the
degrees of freedom.
For these reasons, such approximation spaces and the method itself are
dubbed as “virtual”.
Local polynomial consistency and an additional stability term provide
the well-posedness of the final discretization.
Since the formulation of the method does not need a closed form of the
basis functions, the resulting computational framework is extremely
powerful and offers important advantages with respect to the FEM.
We can build approximation spaces that are in principle of any order
of accuracy and global regularity, suitable to general polyhedral meshes,
even including nonconvex or nonconforming elements in 2D and 3D, and,
importantly, satifying other additional properties such as being part
of a discrete de Rham chain.
This last property has the major consequence that both the numerical
approximation of the fluid velocity and the magnetic flux field are
intrinsically divergence free, a fact that we can verify numerically
up to the machine precision.

The discrete divergence-free property in the VEM has been firstly
analyzed
in
[
6
,
7
]

(see also
[
3
]
) in the
framework of the Stokes and Navier-Stokes problems.
Later, it has been extended to other fluid flow models, a short list
of representatives
being
[
26
,
62
,
31
,
61
,
23
,
25
,
44
,
75
,
74
,
24
,
10
,
15
,
52
,
35
]
.
On the other hand, De-Rahm complexes in the virtual element setting
have been introduced in
[
13
]

and then improved (and applied to the analysis of magnetostatic
problems) in

[
5
,
12
,
11
]
.
The Maxwell’s equations were the target
of
[
14
]
,
see also
[
28
,
29
]
, and their analysis hinges upon
technical tools from
[
8
]
. Other
interesting results along these directions are contained
in
[
30
]
that paves the way for a VEM discretization
of the Bianchi-Einstein equations.

In the present contribution we present a low-order four-field VEM
formulation of the time-dependent MHD equations in three dimensions,
in the spirit of
[
50
]
.
Compared to
[
50
]
, we further prove convergence of the semi-discrete scheme
and employ a convenient divergence-free discretization of the velocity field
(whereas the Taylor-Hood element is used in
[
50
]
).
In order to discretize the unknown fields (pressure, and velocity,
magnetic, and electric fields), we exploit the compatible VEM discrete
spaces outlined above.
Since the novelty of the method is in the space discretization
approach, we focus on the semi-discrete version of the scheme, and
leave the development of different time advancing techniques, together
with the associated linear and nonlinear solvers, to future
publications.
The ensuing VEM scheme employs general polyhedral meshes and,
differently from the majority of the FEM schemes available in the literature,
guarantees that both the velocity and magnetic fields are divergence
free (up to machine precision).
An important achievement of the present contribution is that, under
suitable regularity conditions on the exact solution, we are able to
prove the linear convergence of the scheme in the “natural” norms of
the problem.
To the best of our knowledge, there are few convergence analyses of FEM schemes for the time-dependent MHD model in the literature,
and none for the 4-field formulation here presented.
For instance, in
[
51
]
, a lowest order FEM (with discrete velocity that is not divergence free) has been designed and analyzed for the
stationary
MHD system.
Thus, a major contribution of this work is that we also provide the
details for the convergence analysis for the fully nonlinear model;
such an analysis can be simplified to the FE setting, thus leading to
possibly useful results also for FEM.
Clearly, our analysis has the drawback of requiring a sufficiently
regular exact solution; thus, our theoretical results should be
intended as a guarantee that, at least in good conditions, the method
delivers an accurate solution.
In the final part of the article, we present some numerical tests that
show the good performance of the proposed method.

The paper is organized as follows.
After discussing some basic notation and definitions in Section
2
,
we review the continuous problem in
Section
3
.
In Section
4
, we introduce the
velocity/pressure discrete fields and some theoretical results
essential to the approximation of the convection term.
In Section
5
, after presenting the discrete
spaces adopted for the electromagnetic fields and some related
property, we state the discrete method.
In Section
6
, we show and prove the
theoretical convergence results, which we validate in
Section
7
with some numerical tests.
Finally, in Section
8
, we draw some
conclusions and outline future developments.

Sobolev spaces

According to

\displaystyle H^{s}(\div,D)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathbf{v}\in\big[H^{s}(D)\big]^{3}\mid\div\mathbf{v}\in H^{s}(D)\Big\},

\displaystyle H^{s}(\curl,D)

\Pi^{0,\omega}_{\ell}:\big[L^{2}(\omega)\big]^{d}\to\big[\mathbbm{P}_{\ell}(\omega)\big]^{d}

and

\Pi^{\nabla,\omega}_{1}:\big[H^{1}(\omega)\big]^{d}\to\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(\omega)\big]^{d}
,
d=2,3
.
In particular, we shall use the orthogonal projection

\Pi^{0,K}_{0}\nabla v
of the gradient of a function
v\in H^{1}(K)
,
which is the constant vector polynomial solving the variational problem:

\displaystyle\big(\Pi^{0,K}_{0}\nabla v-\nabla v,\mathbf{q}\big)_{K}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{q}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{0}(K)\big]^{3}.

For
\ell=0,1
, we also define the global projection operators

\Pi^{0}_{\ell}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbbm{P}_{\ell}(\Omega_{h})
and

\Pi^{0}_{\ell}:\big[L^{2}(\Omega)\big]^{d}\to\big[\mathbbm{P}_{\ell}(\Omega_{h})\big]^{d}

as the operators respectively satisfying

{\big(\Pi^{0}_{\ell}v\big)}_{|K}=\Pi^{0,K}_{\ell}\big({v}_{|K}\big)

and

{\big(\Pi^{0}_{\ell}\mathbf{v}\big)}_{|K}=\Pi^{0,K}_{\ell}\big({\mathbf{v}}_{|K}\big)

for all mesh elements
K\in\Omega_{h}
.

Similarly,
\Pi^{\nabla}_{1}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbbm{P}_{1}(\Omega_{h})
and

\Pi^{\nabla}_{1}:\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{d}\to\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(\Omega_{h})\big]^{d}

are the global projection operators respectively satisfying

{\big(\Pi^{\nabla}_{1}v\big)}_{|K}=\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\big({v}_{|K}\big)

and

{\big(\Pi^{\nabla}_{1}\mathbf{v}\big)}_{|K}=\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\big({\mathbf{v}}_{|K}\big)

for all mesh elements
K\in\Omega_{h}
.

Some names for constants

In some of the forthcoming estimates, we shall occasionally write explicit constants and denote them with different symbols, depending on their meaning.
Notably, we shall use the following notation:

•

C_{S}
denotes a constant depending on a Sobolev embedding;

•

C_{D}
denotes a constant depending on the shape of a domain;

•

C_{P}
denotes a constant appearing in a Poincaré inequality;

•

C_{I}
denotes a constant depending on an interpolation estimate with respect to functions in virtual element spaces;

•

C_{\inv}
denotes a constant depending on an inverse estimate;

•

C_{\appr}
denotes a constant depending on a polynomial approximation estimate.

We deem that this notation might help the reader to better follow some steps in the forthcoming proofs.

Henceforth, we use the letter “
C
” to denote a strictly
positive constant that can take a different value at any occurrence.
The constant
C
is independent of the mesh size parameter
h
but
may depend on the other parameters of the differential problem and
virtual element discretization such as the domain shape, the mesh
regularity constant
\gamma
, and the coercivity and continuity
constants of the bilinear forms used in the variational formulations
that will be introduced in the next sections.

3
The continuous problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a polyhedral domain with Lipschitz continuous boundary
\partial\Omega
.
We model the interaction of an electrically charged, incompressible
fluid having velocity
\mathbf{u}
and pressure
p
with the
self-consistently generated electric field
\mathbf{E}
and magnetic flux
field
\mathbf{B}
.
We denote the viscous Reynolds number by
Re
, the magnetic Reynolds
number by
Re_{m}
, and the Hartman number by
s
.
Furthermore, we define the electric current density

\mathbf{j}:=\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}.

(5)

Henceforth, the subscript
t
as in
\mathbf{u}_{t}
and
\mathbf{B}_{t}
denotes the time
derivative and
[0,T]
is the time integration interval for a given
final time
T>0
.

The MHD problem reads as follows:
For all times
t\in(0,T]
,
find
\mathbf{u}
,
p
,
\mathbf{E}
, and
\mathbf{B}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}-Re^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{u}-s\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}\phantom{0\mathbf{0}}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}-Re_{m}^{-1}\curl\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\phantom{0\mathbf{f}}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(6b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t}+\curl\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\phantom{0\mathbf{f}}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(6c)

\displaystyle\div\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=0\phantom{\mathbf{0}\mathbf{f}}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(6d)

\displaystyle\div\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0\phantom{\mathbf{0}\mathbf{f}}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(6e)

where
(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}=\sum_{i,j}(\partial_{j}u_{i})u_{j}
.
The MHD equations (
6
) are completed
by the set of initial conditions for the velocity and the magnetic flux fields

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,

(7)

and the homogeneous boundary conditions

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{0},\qquad\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=0,\qquad\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x},t)\times\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}=\mathbf{0}\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

(8)

Equations (
6a
)
and (
6e
) describe the hydrodynamic
behavior of an electrically charged, incompressible fluid under the
action of an external force
\mathbf{f}
and the electromagnetic force

\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}
multiplied by the Hartmann number, which acts as a
coupling coefficient.
The electromagnetic force is
self-consistently generated by the electromagnetic fields
\mathbf{E}
and

\mathbf{B}
satisfying equations (
6b
),
(
6c
), and
(
6d
).
These last three equations describe the electromagnetic submodel in
the magneto-hydrodynamics approximation
[
17
]
.
The incompressibility of the fluid velocity and the solenoidal nature
of the magnetic field require that both
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathbf{B}_{0}

in (
7
) are divergence free.

The weak formulation of
problem (
6
)-(
8
)
reads as follows:

For almost every
t\in[0,T]
, find

(\mathbf{u}(t),p(t)
,
\mathbf{E}(t),\mathbf{B}(t))\in\big[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\times H_{0}(\curl,\Omega)\times H_{0}(\div,\Omega)

such that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathbf{v})+Re^{-1}a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})+b(\mathbf{v},p)+c(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})-s(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})\phantom{0}\qquad\forall\mathbf{v}\in\big[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]^{3},

(9a)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{F})-Re_{m}^{-1}(\mathbf{B},\curl\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle=0\phantom{(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})}\qquad\forall\mathbf{F}\in H_{0}(\curl,\Omega),

(9b)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}_{t},\mathbf{C})+(\curl\mathbf{E},\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle=0\phantom{(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})}\qquad\forall\mathbf{C}\in H_{0}(\div,\Omega),

(9c)

\displaystyle b(\mathbf{u},q)

\displaystyle=0\phantom{(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})}\qquad\forall q\in L_{0}(\Omega),

(9d)

where
\mathbf{j}
is defined in (
5
).
The bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the
trilinear form
c(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
are defined as

\displaystyle a:\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\times\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}:

\displaystyle\quad a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=(\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{v})=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{u}:\nabla\mathbf{v},

\displaystyle b:\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}:

\displaystyle\quad b(\mathbf{v},q):=-(\div\mathbf{v},q)=\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}q\div\mathbf{v},

\displaystyle c:\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\times\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\times\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\to\mathbb{R}:

\displaystyle\quad c(\mathbf{w};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=((\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{v},

where
\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{B}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}B_{ij}
and
(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}b_{j}c_{i}
for the matrices

\mathbf{A}=(A_{ij})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{ij})
, and the vectors
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})

and
\mathbf{c}=(c_{i})
.
We also consider the local forms that are defined by splitting the
above forms on the mesh elements
K\in\Omega_{h}
:

\displaystyle a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}a^{K}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\quad\textrm{and}\quad a^{K}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=(\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{v})_{K},

\displaystyle b(\mathbf{v},q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}b^{K}(\mathbf{u},q)\quad\textrm{and}\quad b^{K}(\mathbf{u},q)=-(\div\mathbf{v},q)_{K},

\displaystyle c(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}c^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\quad\textrm{and}\quad c^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=((\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})_{K}.

Several (partial) results are available from the technical literature
about the well-posedness of the MHD model in the strong and weak
formulation, e.g., problems (
6
)
and (
9
) and their variants, cf.

[
39
,
72
,
63
,
67
,
47
,
42
]

and the citations therein.
Some of these results concerning existence, uniqueness and stability
of the solution fields
(\mathbf{u},p,\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})
have been derived under
specific assumptions that may reduce the generality of the model.
To the best of our knowledge, the full mathematical understanding of
the MHD model is still an open issue and an active research area.
This topic is beyond the scope of our work; thus, we shall simply
assume that the MHD model is well-posed, at least in the setting that
we are using in this paper.

4
Virtual element spaces for the incompressible flow equations

4.1
The discrete pressure space

We approximate the pressure unknown in the space of piecewise
discontinuous, constant functions with zero average on
\Omega
:

\displaystyle Q_{h}:=\Big\{q_{h}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\,\mid\,{q_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathbbm{P}_{0}(K)\;\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\Big\}=\mathbbm{P}_{0}(\Omega_{h})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega).

Every
q_{h}\in Q_{h}
is uniquely determined by the set of constant
values
\big({q_{h}}_{|K}\big)
.
Accordingly, we can approximate any scalar function

q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
by its averages over the mesh elements.

4.2
The discrete velocity space

Here, we consider the “lowest order” version of the spaces
introduced
in
[
7
,
15
]
.
This includes an “enhancement” procedure that allows for the
computation of an
L^{2}
linear polynomial projector.
We refer to such papers for a better understanding of the motivations
behind this construction.

We define the nodal velocity space on a face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{h}(F):=\Big\{\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[H^{1}(F)\big]^{3}\,\mid\,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}_{h}}_{|\partial F}\in\big[C^{0}(\partial F)\big]^{3},\;{\mathbf{w}_{h}}_{|e}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(e)\big]^{3}\,\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{F}_{h},\quad

\displaystyle\DeltaF\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{2}(F)\big]^{3},

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{w}_{h,\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\Pi}^{\nabla,F}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{h,\bm{\tau}},\mathbf{q}\big)_{0,F}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{q}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{2}(F)\big]^{2},

\displaystyle\big(w_{h,\mathbf{n}}-\Pi^{\nabla,F}_{1}w_{h,\mathbf{n}},q\big)_{0,F}=0\quad\forall q\in\mathbbm{P}_{2}(F)\setminus\mathbb{R}\Big\}.

Above, we denoted the tangential and normal components of a given
field
\mathbf{w}
by
\mathbf{w}_{h,\bm{\tau}}=(\mathbf{n}_{F}\times\mathbf{w})\times\mathbf{n}_{F}

and
w_{h,\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}_{F}\cdot\mathbf{w}
, respectively.
We define the nodal velocity space on the whole boundary
\partial K

of a mesh element
K
as

\mathbf{W}_{h}(\partial K):=\Big\{\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[C^{0}(\partial K)\big]^{3}\,\mid\,{\mathbf{w}_{h}}_{|F}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(F)\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}^{K}_{h}\Big\}.

Finally, we introduce the nodal velocity space on the
element
K\in\Omega_{h}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{h}(K):=\Big\{\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[H^{1}(K)\big]^{3}\,\mid\,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}_{h}}_{|\partial K}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(\partial K),\quad

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta\mathbf{w}_{h}+\nabla s\in\mathbf{x}\times\big[\mathbbm{P}_{0}(K)\big]^{3}\text{\penalty\ for\penalty\ some\penalty\ }s\in L^{2}_{0}(K),\\
\div\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbbm{P}_{0}(K),\end{cases}

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{w}_{h}-\bm{\Pi}^{\nabla,K}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{h},\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{q}\big)_{0,K}=0\quad\forall\mathbf{q}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{0}(K)\big]^{3}\,\Big\}.

Every virtual element vector field
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
is uniquely
determined by the set of values

\big(\,(\mathbf{w}_{\textsf{v}})_{\textsf{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{K}_{h}},(w_{F})_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{K}_{h}}\,\big)
, where

•

\mathbf{w}_{\textsf{v}}=\mathbf{w}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{\textsf{v}})
is the value of
\mathbf{w}_{h}
at the vertex

\textsf{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{K}_{h}
(with position vector
\mathbf{x}_{\textsf{v}}
);

•

w_{F}
is the scaled zero-th order moment of the normal component of

\mathbf{w}_{h}
on the face
F\in\mathcal{F}^{K}_{h}
, given by

\displaystyle w_{F}=\frac{1}{|F|}\int_{F}w_{h,\mathbf{n}}.

The unisolvence of these degrees of freedom can be proved as in
[
3
,
15
]
.
For any
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
, the elliptic projection

\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(K)\big]^{3}
, the
L^{2}
-orthogonal
projection
\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(K)\big]^{3}
, and the
divergence of
\mathbf{w}_{h}
are directly computable from such degrees of
freedom.
Finally, we define the global nodal velocity space by an

H^{1}
-conforming coupling of the local degrees of freedom:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{h}:=\Big\{\,\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\big[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\,\mid\,{\mathbf{w}_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\quad\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\,\Big\}.

Remark 4.1
.

As in
[
7
,
15
]
, the couple of spaces
\mathbf{W}_{h}\times Q_{h}
satisfies a discrete inf-sup condition, i.e., there exists
\beta>0
independent of
h
such that

\beta\leq\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup_{\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}}\frac{b(\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{1}\|q_{h}\|}.

Furthermore, we also have the important property

\div\mathbf{W}_{h}\subseteq Q_{h}.

4.3
The virtual element bilinear forms
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
m_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)

We use the elliptic projection operator
\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}
to define the
elemental bilinear form
a_{h}^{K}:\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\times\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
, which
mimics the
H^{1}
-inner product on the element
K
:

a_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=\big(\nabla\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{h},\nabla\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{h}\big)_{K}+S^{K}\big((I-\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1})\mathbf{u}_{h},(I-\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1})\mathbf{v}_{h}\big)\\
\quad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K).

(10)

Here,
S^{K}:\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\times\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
can be any computable symmetric bilinear form such that there exist two positive constants
\sigma_{*}
and
\sigma^{*}
independent of
h_{K}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}|\mathbf{v}_{h}|^{2}_{1,K}\leq S^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})\leq\sigma^{*}|\mathbf{v}_{h}|^{2}_{1,K}\quad\quad\forall\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\ker\big(\Pi^{\nabla,K}_{1}\big)\cap\mathbf{W}_{h}(K).

A “standard” choice for the stabilization is given by
[
15
]

\displaystyle S^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=h_{K}\sum_{i}\dofiP(\mathbf{u}_{h})\,\dofiP(\mathbf{v}_{h}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K),

where the summation on the index
i
is carried over all the elemental
degrees of freedom and
\dofiP(\mathbf{w}_{h})
is the bounded, linear
functional defined on
\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
providing the
i
-th degree of
freedom of
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
.
The summation term in
S^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is multiplied by
h_{K}
to
have a consistent scaling for both terms of
a_{h}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
the right-hand side of (
10
) with respect to the
element size.

The local bilinear form
a_{h}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the two
fundamental properties of consistency and stability:

•

consistency
: for all
\mathbf{q}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(K)\big]^{3}
and
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
,

\displaystyle a_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{w}_{h})=a^{K}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{w}_{h});

(11)

•

stability
: for all
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{*}\|\mathbf{w}_{h}\|_{1,K}^{2}\leq a_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{w}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})\leq\alpha^{*}\|\mathbf{w}_{h}\|_{1,K}^{2},

(12)

with
\alpha_{*}=\min(1,\sigma_{*})
and
\alpha^{*}=\max(1,\sigma^{*})
.

Finally, we define the global bilinear form

a_{h}:\mathbf{W}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
that we use in the virtual element
formulation of the MHD model:

\displaystyle a_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}a_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})\quad\quad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}.

In what follows, we use the discrete norm

\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{h}}^{2}:=a_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})\qquad\forall\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}.

We use the
L^{2}
-projection operator
\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}
to define the
bilinear form
m_{h}^{K}:\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\times\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
, which mimics the
local
L^{2}
-inner product on the element
K
:

\displaystyle m_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=\big(\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{h},\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{h}\big)_{K}+\tilde{S}^{K}\Big((I-\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1})\mathbf{u}_{h},(I-\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1})\mathbf{v}_{h}\Big)\quad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K).

(13)

Here,
\tilde{S}^{K}:\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\times\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)\to\mathbb{R}
can be any computable bilinear form such that there exist two positive
constants
\widetilde{\sigma}_{*}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}^{*}
independent of
h_{K}
satisfying

\widetilde{\sigma}_{*}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,K}\leq\tilde{S}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})\leq\widetilde{\sigma}^{*}\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,K}\quad\quad\forall\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\ker\big(\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\big)\cap\mathbf{W}_{h}(K).

A “standard” choice for the stabilization term
\tilde{S}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)

is given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=h_{K}^{3}\sum_{i}\dofiP(\mathbf{u}_{h})\,\dofiP(\mathbf{v}_{h}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\,\mathbf{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K),

where the summation on the index
i
is again on all the elemental
degrees of freedom and
\dofiP(\cdot)
is the same functional used in
the definition of the stabilization term
S^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
The summation term in
\tilde{S}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
is multiplied by
h_{K}^{3}

to have a consistent scaling for both terms of
m_{h}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
on the right-hand side of (
13
)
with respect to the element size.
The stability bounds above are shown in
[
9
]
.

The local bilinear form
m_{h}^{K}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the two
fundamental properties of consistency and stability:

•

consistency
: for all
\mathbf{q}\in\big[\mathbbm{P}_{1}(K)\big]^{3}
and

\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
,

\displaystyle m_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{w}_{h})=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{w}_{h})_{0,K};

(14)

•

stability
: for all
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{*}\|\mathbf{w}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,K}\leq m_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{w}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})\leq\mu^{*}\|\mathbf{w}_{h}\|^{2}_{0,K},

(15)

with
\mu_{*}=\min(1,\widetilde{\sigma}_{*})
and

\mu^{*}=\max(1,\widetilde{\sigma}^{*})
.

Finally, we define the global inner product

m_{h}:\mathbf{W}_{h}\times\mathbf{W}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
that we use in the virtual element
formulation of the MHD model:

\displaystyle m_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}):=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}m_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}).

Associated with
m_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we define the corresponding discrete norm

\|\mathbf{v}_{h}\|_{m_{h}}^{2}:=m_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h}).

4.3.1
Interpolation in
\mathbf{W}_{h}
and a stability result

We define the (energy) interpolant
\mathbf{u}_{I}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}
of a sufficiently smooth vector-valued field
\mathbf{u}
as the unique function in
\mathbf{W}_{h}
sharing the same degrees of freedom of
\mathbf{u}
.
We have the following local and global interpolation property;
see
[
9
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\big[H^{2}(\Omega)\big]^{3}
and
\mathbf{u}_{I}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}
be its degrees of freedom interpolant.
Then, a real, positive constant
C_{I}
independent of
h
exists such that

\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{0,K}+h_{K}|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}|_{1,K}\leq C_{I}h_{K}^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{2,K}\quad\forall K\in\Omega_{h};\quad\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|+h\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{1}\leq C_{I}h^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{2}.

The constant
C_{I}
depends on the mesh parameter
\gamma
.

An important consequence of the definition of the interpolant
\mathbf{u}_{I}

is that, if
\mathbf{u}
is divergence free, then (see also
Remark
4.1
)

b(\mathbf{u}_{I},q_{h})=b(\mathbf{u},q_{h})=0\qquad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\div\mathbf{u}_{I}=0.

(16)

We conclude this section with a technical lemma that will be useful in Section
6
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have a stability bound for the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the elemental
L^{2}
polynomial projection of
\mathbf{u}_{I}
: for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

\quad\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\inv}C_{D}\big(C_{I}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,3}}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}).

Proof.

On each element
K\in\Omega_{h}
, standard manipulations imply

\displaystyle\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{\ell}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}+\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{\ell}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{0,K}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{0,K}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\inv}\big(C_{I}h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\mathbf{u}|_{1,K}+h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{0,K}\Big)\leq C_{\inv}\big(C_{I}C_{D}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,3}(K)}+C_{D}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\big).

Taking the maximum over all elements gives the assertion.
∎

4.4
The discrete trilinear forms

We introduce the local continuous and discrete trilinear forms on the element
K
:

\displaystyle c^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle:=\int_{K}(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}\qquad\forall\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\big[H^{1}(K)\big]^{3},

\displaystyle c_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\int_{K}(\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{u}_{h})\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{h}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{h}\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K).

We also define their skew-symmetric counterparts

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(c^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})-c^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\right)\qquad\forall\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\big[H^{1}(K)\big]^{3},

(17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})-c_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{w}_{h},\mathbf{u}_{h})\right)\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}(K),

(18)

and the associated global skew-symmetric trilinear forms

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\widetilde{c}^{K}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\qquad\forall\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3},

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\widetilde{c}_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})\qquad\forall\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbf{W}_{h}.

If
\mathbf{v}\in[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)]^{3}
is divergence free, then

\widetilde{c}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=c(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}).

In the remainder of the section, we discuss three properties of these trilinear forms.
For the sake of exposition, we consider the extension of the virtual
element bilinear and trilinear forms to
\big[H^{1}(K)\big]^{3}
and

\big[H^{1}(\Omega)\big]^{3}
.

The first property is the continuity of the trilinear form, which can be proved as in
[
7
, Proposition
3.3
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

The following continuity bound is valid:

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{w}_{h})\right|\lesssim|\mathbf{u}_{h}|_{1}\,|\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{1}\,|\mathbf{w}_{h}|_{1},

where the hidden constant is independent of
h
.

The second property is the measure of the variational crime
perpetrated in the discretization of the trilinear form.
The proof is a modification of the proof
of
[
7
, Lemma
4.3
]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Given
\mathbf{w}\in\big[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]^{3}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\big[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]^{3}\cap\big[H^{2}(\Omega)\big]^{3}
,
we have the following bound

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{c}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\right|\leq C_{C}h\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}\,|\mathbf{w}|_{1},

where the constant
C_{C}
depends only on the constant of inverse estimates, approximation bounds, and Sobolev embedding inequalities, but is independent of
h_{K}
.

Proof.

A straightforward manipulation of
relations (
17
)-(
18
) defining
the skew-symmetric trilinear forms yields

\begin{split}\left|\widetilde{c}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\right|&\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|c(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})-c_{h}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\right|+\left|c(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})-c_{h}(\mathbf{v};\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v})\right|\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textrm{T}_{1}+\textrm{T}_{2}\right).\end{split}

(19)

We estimate the two terms on the right-hand side separately. We begin with the first one.
Using the definition of the orthogonal projection
\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v}
, and adding and subtracting
(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}
to the integral argument, we write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{T}_{1}&=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}-\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{v})\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\right|\\[5.0pt]
&=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}-\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\right|\\[5.0pt]
&\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{w}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w})\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\right|=:\textrm{T}_{1,1}+\textrm{T}_{1,2}.\end{split}

Again, we focus on the two terms on the right-hand side separately.
Using the Hölder inequality, polynomial approximation properties, and the Sobolev embedding theorem gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{T}_{1,1}&\leq\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\,\|\mathbf{w}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\|\leq C_{\appr}h\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}\leq C_{S}^{2}C_{\appr}h\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}.\end{split}

Next, for each element
K\in\Omega_{h}
,
we apply a polynomial inverse inequality, the Hölder inequality, and standard manipulations:

\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\leq C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{0,K}\leq C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\|1\|_{L^{4}(K)}\,\|\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\leq C_{\inv}C_{D}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}(K)}.

This entails

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{1,2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\|\nabla\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{0,K}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\,\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\inv}C_{D}C_{\appr}h\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{v}|_{2,K}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\,\|\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}(K)}.

We apply a sequential
\ell^{2}-\ell^{4}-\ell^{4}
Hölder inequality and the Sobolev embedding theorem twice, and deduce

\textrm{T}_{1,2}\leq C_{\inv}C_{D}C_{\appr}h|\mathbf{v}|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\,\|\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{4}}\leq C_{\appr}C_{\inv}C_{D}C_{S}^{2}h\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}.

This step concludes the derivation of a bound on the term
\textrm{T}_{1}
in (
19
).

Next, we focus on the term
\textrm{T}_{2}
.
Adding and subtracting
\big(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{w}\big)\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}
yields

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}-\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{w}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v})\right|=:\textrm{T}_{2,1}+\textrm{T}_{2,2}.

We derive the bounds on the terms
\textrm{T}_{2,1}
and
\textrm{T}_{2,2}
by using the approximation properties of the orthogonal projections, and theoretical tools similar to those used for the bound on
\textrm{T}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v})\right|\leq|\mathbf{w}|_{1}\ \|\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v})\|\leq C_{\appr}h|\mathbf{w}|_{1}\ |\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\appr}h|\mathbf{w}|_{1}\ \|\mathbf{v}\|_{W^{1,4}}^{2}\leq C_{\appr}C_{S}^{2}h\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}|\mathbf{w}|_{1}.

On the other hand, given
\mathbf{v}_{\pi}
the best piecewise linear approximant in
L^{4}
of
\mathbf{v}
over
\Omega_{h}
, we also have

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{w}|_{1,K}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\,\|\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{w}|_{1,K}\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\left(\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\pi}\|_{L^{4}(K)}+\|\mathbf{v}_{\pi}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\right).

Observe that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{\pi}-\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{4}}(\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\pi}\|_{0,K}+\|\mathbf{v}-\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{v}\|_{0,K})

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{4}}\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\pi}\|_{0,K}\leq 2C_{\inv}C_{D}\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\pi}\|_{L^{4}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\inv}C_{D}C_{\appr}h_{K}|\mathbf{v}|_{W^{1,4}(K)}.

Inserting this bound above and using polynomial approximation estimates yield

\textrm{T}_{2,2}\leq(1+2C_{\inv}C_{D})C_{\appr}h\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{w}|_{1,K}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}(K)}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{W^{1,4}(K)}.

We apply a sequential
\ell^{2}-\ell^{4}-\ell^{4}
Hölder inequality and the Sobolev embedding theorem, and deduce

\textrm{T}_{2,2}\leq(1+2C_{\inv}C_{D})C_{\appr}h|\mathbf{w}|_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{4}}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{W^{1,4}}\leq(1+2C_{\inv}C_{D})C_{\appr}C_{S}^{2}h\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}.

Collecting all the bounds together yields the assertion.
∎

The last property measures the distance between the continuous and the
discrete solutions through the discrete trilinear form.
The proof differs from that of
[
7
, Lemma
4.4
]
,
since here we are interested in the analysis of the time-dependent case.
For this reason, we also use some techniques from
[
71
, Lemma
4.1
]
.
Further, we require some extra regularity on the exact velocity solution
\mathbf{u}
to (
6
).

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in W^{2,3}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}:=\mathbf{u}_{h}-\mathbf{u}_{I}
, where
\mathbf{u}_{I}
is the degrees of freedom interpolant of
\mathbf{u}
; see Lemma
4.2
.
Then, for every positive
\varepsilon
,
there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
independent of
h
such that

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|\leq\varepsilon|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)C_{1}R_{1}(\mathbf{u})\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)C_{2}R_{2}(\mathbf{u})h^{2},

where

R_{1}(\mathbf{u}):=\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}+1,\qquad R_{2}(\mathbf{u}):=\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{4}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2}.

Proof.

The triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|+\left|\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-\widetilde{c}_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|=:\textrm{T}_{1}+\textrm{T}_{2}.

We estimate the two terms on the right-hand side separately and begin by splitting
\textrm{T}_{1}
into the sum of two further contributions, due to the definition of the skew symmetric form
\widetilde{c}_{h}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
:

\textrm{T}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|+\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{u})-c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{u})\right|)=:\frac{1}{2}(\textrm{T}_{1,1}+\textrm{T}_{1,2}).

Using standard manipulations, we get

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h})\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\|\,\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|+\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|\right)\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|\leq C_{I}h|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}\,|\mathbf{u}|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|+|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}h^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1}^{2}+(|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}+C_{I})\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}C_{S}^{2}h^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{2}+(|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,\infty}}+C_{I})\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}.

As for the term
\textrm{T}_{1,2}
, we write

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}))\cdot\mathbf{u}\right|\leq|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{h}\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}\,\left(\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|+\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|\right)\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{I}h|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}\,|\mathbf{u}|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}+|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{1}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C_{I}^{2}C_{S}^{2}h^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{4}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}.

Next, we focus on the term
\textrm{T}_{2}
. Note that

\widetilde{c}_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})=\widetilde{c}_{h}^{K}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}),

(20)

which is a consequence of the skew symmetry of
\widetilde{c}_{h}^{K}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
and (
16
).

We split the term
\textrm{T}_{2}
into two contributions, due to the definition of the skew symmetric form
\widetilde{c}_{h}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
. Recalling (
20
), we write

\textrm{T}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})-c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{u}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|+\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{u})-c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{h};\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|\right)=:\frac{1}{2}(\textrm{T}_{2,1}+\textrm{T}_{2,2}).

(21)

Using standard manipulations as above, we get

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,1}\leq\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}};\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|+\left|c_{h}(\mathbf{u}_{I};\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I},\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\right|=:\textrm{T}_{2,1,1}+\textrm{T}_{2,1,2}.

Preliminary, for all
K\in\Omega_{h}
, we observe that

\begin{split}\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}&\leq C_{D}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{2}}|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}|_{1,K}\leq C_{D}C_{I}h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\mathbf{u}|_{2,K}\leq C_{D}^{2}C_{I}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}(K)}.\end{split}

By taking the maximum over all the elements on both sides entails

\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{D}^{2}C_{I}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,1,1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|\,\left|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\right|\,\left|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{D}^{2}C_{I}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}.

Next, we apply Lemma
4.3
and write

\|\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C_{\inv}C_{D}\Big(C_{I}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,3}}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big).

By means of the above inequality, we deduce the bound

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,1,2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|\,\left|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{I}\right|\,\left|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\right|\leq\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|\,\|\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}h|\mathbf{u}|_{2}C_{\inv}C_{D}(C_{I}+1)(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{1,3}}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}^{2}C_{\inv}^{2}C_{D}^{2}(C_{I}+1)^{2}h^{2}|\mathbf{u}|_{2}^{2}(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{1,3}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2})+\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}^{2}C_{\inv}^{2}C_{D}^{2}(C_{I}+1)^{2}(C_{S}^{2}+1)h^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{4}+\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}.

Next, we focus on the term
\textrm{T}_{2,2}
appearing on the right-hand side of (
21
):

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|+\left|\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}})\mathbf{u}_{I}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\right|=:\textrm{T}_{2,2,1}+\textrm{T}_{2,2,2}.

We have the bounds

\displaystyle\textrm{T}_{2,2,1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\|\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{0}\nabla\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0,K}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0,K}\,\|\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0,K}\,C_{\inv}h_{K}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\,\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0,K}C_{\inv}C_{I}h_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\mathbf{u}|_{1,K}\leq C_{\inv}C_{I}C_{D}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,3}}\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\inv}C_{I}C_{D}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{1,3}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C_{\inv}^{2}C_{I}^{2}C_{D}^{2}\,|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

and, with similar computations,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{T}_{2,2,2}&\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}\,\|\mathbf{u}_{I}\|_{0,K}\,\|\bm{\Pi}^{0,K}_{1}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{I})\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\\
&\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}(1+C_{I}h_{K})\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,K}\,C_{\inv}C_{I}h_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathbf{u}|_{2,K}\\
&\leq\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1,K}(1+C_{I}h_{K})\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1,K}\,C_{\inv}C_{I}C_{D}h_{K}|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}(K)}\\
&\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+C_{I}h_{K})^{2}C_{\inv}^{2}C_{I}^{2}C_{D}^{2}h^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1}^{2}\,|\mathbf{u}|_{W^{2,3}}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}|\mathbf{e}_{h}^{\mathbf{u}}|_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+C_{I}h_{K})^{2}C_{\inv}^{2}C_{I}^{2}C_{D}^{2}h^{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{W^{2,3}}^{4}.\end{split}

We collect the bounds on all the terms above and the assertion follows.

5.2.1
Virtual element inner product

According

\gamma^{*}

\frac{\|\mathbf{u}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{h}\|}{\|\mathbf{u}\|};\qquad\frac{\|\mathbf{E}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\mathbf{E}_{h}\|}{\|\mathbf{E}\|};\qquad\frac{\|\mathbf{B}-\bm{\Pi}^{0}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{h}\|}{\|\mathbf{B}\|};\qquad\frac{\|p-p_{h}\|}{\|p\|}.

8
Conclusions

We constructed a virtual element method for the approximation of the
solutions to the 3D time-dependent resistive magnetohydrodynamic
model.
The scheme guarantees “exactly” divergence-free velocity and
magnetic fields and is suitable to general polyhedral meshes. The
convergence analysis hinges upon the exact sequence structure of the
employed virtual element spaces.
We devoted particular attention to the treatment of the two nonlinear
terms: one is the usual convective term appearing in the Navier-Stokes
equations; the second couples the velocity, magnetic, and electric
fields.
We also validated the theoretical convergence results with a numerical
experiment on a manufactured solution.
Future works will thoroughly cope with the numerical performance of
the method: we shall present more involved and realistic benchmarks
and investigate different approaches to deal with the nonlinear terms.
Further investigations will also cover the design of suitable (direct, iterative, and parallel) solvers to improve the computational efficiency in solving the final linear systems.
