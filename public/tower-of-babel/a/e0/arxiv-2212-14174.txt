Supermartingale Brenier’s Theorem with full-marginals constraint Thanks: The authors are grateful to Xiaolu Tan for fruitful discussions.
Erhan Bayraktar, Shuoqing Deng, Dominykas Norgilas

Abstract
We explicitly construct the supermartingale version of the Fréchet-Hoeffding coupling in the setting with infinitely many marginal constraints. This extends the results of Henry-Labordère et al. [ 41 ] obtained in the martingale setting. Our construction is based on the Markovian iteration of one-period optimal supermartingale couplings. In the limit, as the number of iterations goes to infinity, we obtain a pure jump process that belongs to a family of local Lévy models introduced by Carr et al. [ 22 ] . We show that the constructed processes solve the continuous-time supermartingale optimal transport problem for a particular family of (path-dependent) cost functions. The explicit computations are provided in the following three cases: the uniform case, the Bachelier model and the Geometric Brownian Motion case.



Key words.
Supermartingale optimal transport, Brenier’s Theorem, PCOCD.

Introduction

The classical optimal transport (OT) problem, first introduced by Monge, and then relaxed by Kantorovich, has the following formulation: given two probability measures
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}
and a cost (or payoff) function
c:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the goal is to minimize (or maximize) the value
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X_{0},X_{1})]
among all probability measures
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{0}^{-1}=\mu_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{1}^{-1}=\mu_{1}
. Under the Spence-Mirrlees condition
c_{xy}>0
, the maximizing transport plan is characterized by the Brenier’s Theorem (see Brenier
[
18
]
and Rachev and Rüschendorf
[
61
]
) and corresponds to the Fréchet-Hoeffding (or quantile) coupling
\pi^{FH}
.

Motivated by the applications in financial mathematics, the Martingale Optimal Transport (MOT) problem was introduced by Beiglböck et al.
[
8
]
(in the disrete-time setting) and Galichon et al.
[
36
]
(in the the continuous-time setting), and has been widely studied since then. Given two probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
which are increasing in convex order, the problem consists in minimizing (or maximizing)
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X_{0},X_{1})]
among all martingale measures
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{0}^{-1}=\mu_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{1}^{-1}=\mu_{1}
. Under the so-called martingale Spence-Mirrlees condition
c_{xyy}>0
, the maximizer corresponds to the so-called left-curtain martingale coupling
\pi^{lc}
, introduced by Beiglböck and Juillet
[
12
]
.
\pi^{lc}
was explicitly constructed by Henry-Labordère and Touzi
[
42
]
using ODE arguments (and under some technical assumptions). The general construction was later obtained by Hobson and Norgilas
[
48
]
using geometric arguments and properties of the potential functions of marginals.

The financial motivation of MOT comes from the robust sub/super-replication of financial derivatives in a market where the underlying asset and the corresponding vanilla options with certain maturities are available for trading. From the well-known Breenden-Litzenberger formula (see
[
17
]
), the marginal distributions of the underlying asset can be then obtained using the prices of vanilla options. Each sensible pricing model should be calibrated to the given market data, and thus should produce the same marginal distributions at fixed times. The search of a model that produces the highest no-arbitrage price of an exotic claim, among all calibrated models, then naturally corresponds to the MOT problem. For more recent developments of MOT problems, see for example Acciaio et al.

, Backhoff-Veraguas et al.

The problem of finding the models that give robust no-arbitrage bounds for the prices of exotic derivatives was initially studied in the seminal work of Hobson
[
49
]
by means of the Skorokhod Embedding Problem (SEP). SEP consists in finding a stopping time
\tau
of a Brownian motion
B
, such that
B_{\tau}
has a prescribed law. This approach generated developments in many (probabilistic and financially motivated) directions, see, for example, Brown et. al
[
19
]
, Madan and Yor
[
56
]
, Cox et al.
[
23

[
59
]
and Hobson
[
44
]
.

More recently, the Supermartingale Optimal Transport(SOT) problem was introduced by Nutz and Stebegg
[
57
]
. Given two probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
which are increasing in convex-decreasing order, the problem consists in minimizing (or maximizing)
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X_{0},X_{1})]
among all supermartingale measures
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{0}^{-1}=\mu_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}\circ X_{1}^{-1}=\mu_{1}
. In particular, the authors of
[
57
]
introduced two canonical supermartingale couplings, namely the increasing supermartingale coupling
\pi^{I}
and the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\pi^{D}
. These transport plans are canonical in the sense that they can be equivalently characterised by any, and then all of the following properties: the optimality (in terms of minimization or maximization problem) for a large class of cost functions, the monotonicity of the support, and the order-theoretic minimality. (The optimal couplings in the OT and MOT settings have similar characterizations.)

In this paper, we shall focus on the continuous-time case, which corresponds to a suitable limit of the multi-marginal SOT problem. Given a continuous family of marginal distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, which is non-decreasing in convex-decreasing order, we call a stochastic process that is calibrated to all marginals
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
a
PCOCD
(“Processus Croissant pour l’Ordre Convexe Décroissant” in French). Notice that in the martingale case such process is baptized as a peacock (
PCOC
for short) and is extensively studied in the book of Hirch et al.
[
43
]
.

We will closely follow Henri-Labordère et al.
[
41
]
: in the martingale setting, by considering a Markovian iteration of the one-period left-curtain martingale couplings
\pi^{lc}
, the authors obtained a pure jump process in the spirit of the local Lévy models introduced by Carr et al.
[
22
]
and showed that it solves a particular continuous-time MOT problem. Our main goal is to extend the results of Henri-Labordère et al.
[
41
]
to the supermartingale setting.

In the supermartingale case, the construction is different in the following aspects. First, in the martingale case, the left-curtain and the right-curtain transport plans are symmetric, see Henry-Labordr̀e et al.
[
41
, Remark 3.12]
. More precisely, the limiting process, associated to the left-curtain coupling, is driven by downward jumps and upward drift, while in the right-curtain case it is the upward jumps and downward drift that drive the limiting process. On the other hand, in the supermartingale case, the increasing supermartingale transport plan and the decreasing supermartingale transport plan demonstrate different behaviour see Bayraktar et al.
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
. In the increasing case, the limiting process is a martingale in the interior of the support of marginals, and possesses strict supermartingale characteristics when it escapes to the upper boundary of the support (it jumps from the upper all the way down to the lower boundary). In the case of the decreasing supermartingale coupling, there are martingale and supermartingale regions in the interior of the support. Once the boundary curve (that separates these two regions) is hit, the process becomes deterministic and follows a decreasing (w.r.t. time) curve.

Second, in case of the the decreasing supermartingale coupling, there might exist multiple
phase transition curves
for the limiting process, that divide the martingale and strict supermartingale regions. To obtain explicit solutions, in this article we will impose certain conditions that will guarantee the uniqueness of the phase transition curve. On the other hand, if the increasing supermartingale coupling is used for construction, then the regime switching boundary is unique, and is always given by the upper boundary of the support of the marginal distributions.

In financial terms, the dual SOT problem corresponds to the super-replication of an exotic payoff using semi-static strategies with no short-selling constraints.
In order to prove the optimality of the limiting process, we will modify the arguments of Henry-Labordère et al.
[
42
]
and explicitly construct a candidate optimal dual strategy, for a large (but very particular) class of cost functions. The choice of dual variables is closely related to the martingale and strict supermartingale regions of a a candidate optimal transport plan. In the case of the decreasing supermartingale coupling, the dual methods are related to those of the dual MOT problem when the right-curtain martingale coupling is optimal, and also the classical OT duality when the quantile coupling is an optimizer. The strategies must be modified accordingly, if the construction of the limiting process is based on the increasing supermartingale coupling.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the formulation of the discrete-time supermartingale transport problem and introduce the continuous-time SOT problem. In Section
3
, we provide the convergence theorem which gives the characterization of the limiting process, and the optimality theorem which shows that the limiting process solves a continuous-time SOT problem for a class of costs functions. In Section
4
, we explicitly obtain the limiting process when the marginals correspond to uniform measures on bounded support, Normal distributions with decreasing means (Brownian motion with drift) and log-Normal distributions (geometric Brownian motion). In Appendix
A
and
B
we collect some important results regarding one-period OT, MOT and SOT problems.

2
Supermartingale optimal transport problem

2.1
Discrete-time supermartigale optimal transport

Let
{\cal M}
(resp.
{\cal P}
) be the set of finite (resp. probability) Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
with finite first moments. The support of
\eta\in{\cal M}
, denoted by supp
(\eta)
, is the smallest closed set
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\eta(I)=\eta(\mathbb{R})
. We set
\ell_{\eta}:=\inf\{k\in\textrm{supp}(\eta)\}\in[-\infty,\infty)
and
r_{\eta}:=\sup\{k\in\textrm{supp}(\eta)\}\in(-\infty,\infty]
. The mean of
\eta\in{\cal M}
is denoted by
\overline{\eta}
, so that the barycenter of
\eta
is given by
\overline{\eta}/\eta(\mathbb{R})
.

Throughout the paper, for any random variable
\xi
, the expectation of
\xi
is defined as
\mathbb{E}[\xi]:=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[\xi^{-}]
with the convention
\infty-\infty=-\infty
.

The canonical process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is denoted by
(X_{0},X_{1})
, so that, for
i=0,1
,
X_{i}(x_{0},x_{1})=x_{i}
for all
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in{\cal P}
, let
{\cal P}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
be the set of (Borel) probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with first and second marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, respectively. (Each
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is often called a transport plan or coupling of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
.) Then
X_{0}\sim^{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{0},~X_{1}\sim^{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{1}
for all
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
. We also introduce the set of supermartingale couplings of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
:

{\cal S}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X_{1}|X_{0}]\leq X_{0}~\mathbb{P}\textrm{-a.s.}\}.

By the classical result of Strassen
[
62
]
,
{\cal S}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is non-empty if and only if
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\mu_{1}
, i.e.,
\mu_{0}
is smaller than
\mu_{1}
in convex-decreasing order. Recall that
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\mu_{1}
if
\mu_{0}(\phi)\leq\mu_{1}(\phi)
for all convex and non-increasing
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, where for a measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\eta\in{\cal M}
we write
\eta(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}fd\eta
. Note that
\overline{\mu}_{0}\leq\overline{\mu}_{1}
whenever
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\mu_{1}
. On the other hand, if
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\mu_{1}
and
\overline{\mu}_{0}=\overline{\mu}_{1}
, then
{\cal S}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
reduces to the set of martingale couplings of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Indeed, every supermartingale with constant mean is a martingale.

For a (Borel) measurable reward (or cost) function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, a two-marginal supermartingale transport problem is defined by

\mathbf{P}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[c(X_{0},X_{1})\right].

(2.1)

Suppose that there exists
a_{0},a_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that are
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
-integrable, respectively, and such that
\lvert c(x,y)\lvert\leq a_{0}(x)+a_{1}(y)
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Then in the case
c
satisfies the following
supermartingale Spence-Mirrlees
condition

c(x^{\prime},\cdot)-c(x,\cdot)\textrm{ is decreasing and convex for all }x<x^{\prime},

Nutz and Stebegg
[
57
]
proved that the supremum in (
2.1
) is attained by the so-called
increasing
supermartingale coupling
\pi^{I}
. Note that, in the case
c
is smooth, supermartingale Spence-Mirrlees condition can be equivalently stated in terms of cross-derivatives:
c_{xy}\leq 0
and
c_{xyy}\geq 0
. On the other hand, if
-c
is supermartingale Spence-Mirrlees, then the optimizer is given by the
decreasing
supermartingale coupling
\pi^{D}
. The main focus of this paper is on
\pi^{D}
(see Appendix
B.1
).

We now describe the dual problem that is associated to (
2.1
). We write
(\phi,\psi,h)\in{\cal D}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
if
\phi,\psi,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are (Borel) measurable functions such that
(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi\vee 0)d\mu_{0})\vee(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\psi\vee 0)d\mu_{1})<\infty
,
h\geq 0
and

c(x,y)\leq\phi(x)+\psi(y)+h(x)(y-x),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Then the weak duality holds

\mathbf{P}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\leq\mathbf{D}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\inf_{(\varphi,\psi,h)\in{\cal D}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\{\mu_{0}(\varphi)+\mu_{1}(\psi)\}.

(2.2)

Nutz and Stebegg
[
57
]
showed that the strong duality (i.e., equality in (
2.2
)) holds under some additional assumptions (for example, if
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\mu_{1}
are
irreducible
). In general (and without the irreducibility condition), the strong duality and the existence of dual optimizers can still be obtained, provided one agrees to switch from a point-wise to a quasi-sure formulation of the dual problem. An explicit construction of optimal dual variables is (under some further assumptions) provided in Appendix
B.2
.

The problem (
2.1
) can be easily extended to a
T
-period (or
(T+1)
-marginal) setting if we restrict to cost functions
c:\mathbb{R}^{T+1}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
c(x_{0},\ldots,x_{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{T}c_{i}(x_{i-1},x_{i})
,
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{T+1}
, for a collection of one-period (Borel) cost functions
c_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,...,T
. More precisely, we are given a sequence of probability measures
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{T}\in{\cal P}
satisfying
\mu_{0}\leq_{cd}\ldots\leq_{cd}\mu_{T}
, and the goal is to maximize

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X_{0},\ldots,X_{T})]=\sum^{T}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c_{i}(X_{i-1},X_{i})]

(2.3)

over all (Borel) probability measures
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathbb{R}^{T+1}
such that
X_{i}\sim^{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{i}
,
i=0,...,T
, and satisfying
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X_{i}\lvert X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}]\leq X_{i-1}

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., for all
i=1,...,T
. (For the martingale version with general cost functions
c:\mathbb{R}^{T+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, see Nutz et al.
[
58
]
, while a corresponding continuous-time extension is given by Juillet et al.
[
20
]
.) Then, if each
c_{i}
(or
-c_{i}
) is supermartingale Spence-Mirrlees, the optimal supermartingale coupling (with fixed
T+1
marginals) can be obtained by a Markovian iteration of the increasing (or decreasing) one-period supermartingale transport plans.

2.2
Continuous-time supermartigale optimal transport

In this section we introduce a continuous-time supermartingale optimal transport problem under full marginals constraint, as the limit of the multi-period supermartingale optimal transport introduced at the end of Section
2.1
.

Let
\Omega:=\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
be the canonical space of all càdlàg paths on
[0,1]
,
X
the canonical process and
\mathbb{F}=({\cal F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
the canonical filtration generated by
X
, i.e.
{\cal F}_{t}:=\sigma(\{X_{s},0\leq s\leq t\})
. We denote by
{\cal S}_{\infty}
the collection of all supermartingale measures on
\Omega
, i.e., under each
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}
the canonical process
X
is a supermartingale. We equip
{\cal S}_{\infty}
with the weak convergence topology throughout the paper. By the calssical results (see, for example, Karandikar
[
54
]
), there is a non-decreasing process
([X]_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, defined on
\Omega
, and which coincides with the
\mathbb{P}
-quadratic variation of
X
,
\mathbb{P}
-
a.s.
for every supermartingale measure
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}
. Denote by
[X]^{c}_{.}
the continuous part of
[X]_{.}
.

Given a family of (integrable) probability measures
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, we denote by
{\cal S}_{\infty}(\mu)\subset{\cal S}_{\infty}
the collection of all supermartingale measures on
\Omega
such that
X_{t}\sim^{\mathbb{P}}\mu_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. In particular, from Ewald and Yor
[
33
]
, we know that
{\cal S}_{\infty}(\mu)
is non-empty if and only if the family
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is non-decreasing in convex-decreasing order and
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
is right-continuous.

For all
t\in[0,1]
, we denote by
-\infty\leq\ell_{t}:=\ell_{\mu_{t}}\leq r_{\mu_{t}}=:r_{t}\leq\infty
the left and right extreme boundaries of supp
(\mu_{t})
. Suppose that
\mu_{s}\leq_{cd}\mu_{t}
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
. Then since
x\mapsto p_{k}(x):=(k-x)^{+}
is convex and non-increasing for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
\mu_{s}(p_{k})\leq\mu_{t}(p_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\mu_{s}((-\infty,k))>0
implies that
\mu_{t}((-\infty,k))>0
, from which it follows that
t\mapsto\ell_{t}
is non-increasing. Note that, in general,
t\mapsto r_{t}
may fail to be non-decreasing. In addition, if
\mu_{t}
admits a density function for all
t\in[0,1]
and
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
is continuous w.r.t. the weak convergence topology, then
t\mapsto l_{t}
and
t\mapsto r_{t}
are continuous (see Henry-Labordère and Touzi
[
42
, p. 2805]
).

As in Henry-Labordère et al.
[
41
]
(see also Hobson and Klimmek
[
45
]
), our continuous-time SOT problem arises as a limit of the multi-period SOT problems, by considering the limit of the reward function
\sum_{i=1}^{T}c_{i}(x_{t_{i-1}},x_{t_{i}})
as in (
2.3
), where
(t_{i})_{0\leq i\leq T}
is a partition of
[0,1]
with
\max_{1\leq i\leq T}\lvert t_{i}-t_{i-1}\lvert\to 0
as
T\to\infty
. To obtain the convergence we need the pathwise Itô calculus.

Definition 2.1

(Föllmer
[
35
]
)
.

Let
\pi_{n}=(0=t_{0}^{n}<\cdots<t_{n}^{n}=1)
,
n\geq 1
be partitions of
[0,1]
with
|\pi_{n}|:=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|t_{k}^{n}-t_{k-1}^{n}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
A càdlàg path
\mathbf{x}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
has a finite quadratic variation along
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
if the sequence of measures on
[0,1]
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}^{n}}-\mathbf{x}_{t_{k-1}^{n}})^{2}\delta_{\{t_{k-1}\}}(dt),

converges weakly to a measure
[\mathbf{x}]^{F}
on
[0,1]
. For each
t\in[0,1]
, set
[\mathbf{x}]_{t}^{F}:=[\mathbf{x}]^{F}([0,t])
, and let
[\mathbf{x}]^{F,c}_{.}([0,t])
be the continuous part of this non-decreasing path.

The following will be one of our standing assumptions.

Assumption 2.2
.

The cost function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is in
C^{3}((l_{1},\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}r_{t})\times(l_{1},\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}r_{t}))
and satisfies

c(x,x)=c_{y}(x,x)=0,~c_{xyy}<0,~c_{xy}>0,{\quad(x,y)\in(l_{1},\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}r_{t})\times(l_{1},\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}r_{t}).}

Following Hobson and Klimmek
[
45
]
we then have (in fact, a weaker assumption on

would suffice; see Touzi et al.
[
42
]
)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let Assumption
2.2
hold ture. Then for every path
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
with finite quadratic variation
[\mathbf{x}]^{F}
along a sequence of partitions
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c(\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}^{n}},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k-1}^{n}})\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}c_{yy}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t})d[\mathbf{x}]^{F,c}_{t}+\sum_{0\leq t\leq 1}c(\mathbf{x}_{t-},\mathbf{x}_{t}).

The non-decreasing process
[X]_{\cdot}
, as in Karandikar
[
54
]
, is defined for every càdlàg path
\mathbf{x}
and coincides
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. with both, the quadratic variation and
[\mathbf{x}]^{F}
, for every
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}
. This and Lemma
2.3
motivates us to define the following continuous-time reward function:

C(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}c_{yy}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t})d[\mathbf{x}]^{c}_{t}+\sum_{0\leq t\leq 1}c(\mathbf{x}_{t-},\mathbf{x}_{t}),~~~\mbox{for all }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,

where the integral and the sum are defined using positive and negative parts with a convention
\infty-\infty=-\infty
.

The continuous-time supermartingale optimal transport problem is now given by

\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\mu):=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}(\mu)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[C(X)\right].

(2.4)

Note that
\sum_{0\leq t\leq 1}c(X_{t-},X_{t})<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}
; see Henry-Labordèrè et al.
[
41
, Remark 2.5]
.

We now follow Henry-Labordèrè et al.
[
41
]
and introduce the dual formulation of the problem (
2.4
). First we introduce the set of admissible semi-static trading strategies. Let
\mathbb{H}_{0}
be the class of
\mathbb{F}
-predictable and locally bounded processes
H:[0,1]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e., there exists an increasing family of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
taking values in
[0,1]\cup\{\infty\}
such that

and under every supermartingale measure
\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}
, one can define the integral, denoted by

, see Jacod and Shiryaev
[
51

. Define

{\cal H}:=\{H\in\mathbb{H}_{0}:H\geq 0\mbox{ and }H\cdot X\mbox{ is a }\mathbb{P}\mbox{-supermartingale for every }\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}\}.

For the static strategy, we denote by

the space of all finite signed measures on
[0,1]
which is a Polish space under the weak convergence topology, and by
\Lambda
the class of all measurable maps
\lambda:\mathbb{R}\to M([0,1])
which admit a representation
\lambda(x,dt)=\lambda_{0}(t,x)\gamma(dt)

[0,1]

\Lambda(\mu):=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mu(\lvert\lambda\lvert)<\infty\},~~\mbox{where }\mu(|\lambda|):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\lambda_{0}(t,x)|\mu_{t}(dx)\gamma(dt).

We also introduce a family of random measures

The set of admissible superhedging strategies with no short-selling constraint is defined by

{\cal D}_{\infty}:=\{(H,\lambda)\in{\cal H}\times\Lambda(\mu):\delta^{X}(\lambda)+(H\cdot X)_{1}\geq C(X_{\cdot}),\mathbb{P}\mbox{-a.s.},\forall\mathbb{P}\in{\cal S}_{\infty}\},

and then the dual problem is given by

\mathbf{D}_{\infty}(\mu):=\inf_{(H,\lambda)\in{\cal D}_{\infty}(\mu)}\mu(\lambda).

(2.5)

Furthermore, the weak duality holds:
\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\mu)\leq\mathbf{D}_{\infty}(\mu)

3
Main results

Our contribution splits into three parts. First we construct a continuous-time supermartingale with given marginals
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. This is achieved as an accumulation point of a sequence
(\mathbb{P}^{n})_{n}
of solutions of
n
-period supermartingale transport problems. We then characterize the limiting law further and show that it corresponds to the distribution of a local Lévy process. Finally we prove that this limit solves the continuous-time supermartingale optimal transport problem (
2.4
). The last point is achieved by proving the strong duality
\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\mu)=\mathbf{D}_{\infty}(\mu)
, and explicitly constructing an optimal superhedging strategy.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be a family of probability measures in
{\cal P}
which are increasing in convex-decreasing order. Then
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is uniformly integrable, i.e.

\lim_{K\to+\infty}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\int|x|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq K\}}\mu_{t}(dx)=0.

Proof.

Using the inequality
|x|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq K\}}\leq 2(|x|-\frac{K}{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq\frac{K}{2}\}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\int|x|\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq K\}}\mu_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\int 2(|x|-\frac{K}{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|\geq\frac{K}{2}\}}\mu_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\int(x-\frac{K}{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq\frac{K}{2}\}}\mu_{t}(dx)+2\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\int(-x-\frac{K}{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq-\frac{K}{2}\}}\mu_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle=2\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}C_{\mu_{t}}(\frac{K}{2})+2\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}P_{\mu_{t}}(-\frac{K}{2})

\displaystyle=2C_{\tilde{\mu}}(\frac{K}{2})+2P_{\mu_{1}}(-\frac{K}{2})\to 0,\mbox{ when }K\to\infty,

where
\tilde{\mu}
is some probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with the same mass and mean with
\mu_{0}
. We now explain the last equality. First,
x\mapsto(-x-\frac{K}{2})^{+}
is convex and non-increasing, and thus using the convex-decreasing ordering of
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
we obtain that
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}P_{\mu_{t}}(-\frac{K}{2})=P_{\mu_{1}}(-\frac{K}{2})
. On the other hand,
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}C_{\mu_{t}}(\frac{K}{2})=C_{\tilde{\mu}}(\frac{K}{2})
follows from the fact that the point-wise supremum of a family of convex functions is convex, and
\lim_{\lvert x\lvert\to\infty}\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}C_{\mu_{t}}(x)-\{\overline{\mu_{0}}-\mu_{0}(\mathbb{R})x\}\}=0
. Therefore, there exists some probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
, such that
\tilde{\mu}(\mathbb{R})=\mu_{0}(\mathbb{R})
,
\overline{\mu_{0}}=\overline{\tilde{\mu}}
and
C_{\tilde{\mu}}=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}C_{\mu_{t}}
.
∎

Recall that

Proof of Proposition

.

Recall that
\mathbb{P}^{n}

For given positive constants

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n}(C,\theta):=

\displaystyle\inf\{\Pi_{i=j}^{k-1}(1-C(t_{i+1}^{n}-t_{i}^{n})):\mbox{ for some }s\in[0,1)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq k\leq n\mbox{ such that }s\leq t_{j}^{n}\leq t_{k+1}^{n}\leq s+\theta\}.

\displaystyle K\mathbb{P}^{n}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|X_{t}|\geq K\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\left[|X_{\tau}|\right]

. Finally, by the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{0}}[\varphi(s,X_{\cdot})(X_{t}-X_{s}))]

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{0}}[\varphi(s,X_{\cdot})(X_{t}^{K}-X_{s}^{K}))]

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\to\infty}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}[\varphi(s,X_{\cdot})(X_{t}^{K}-X_{s}^{K}))]

\displaystyle\leq 0.

As
\varphi
is arbitrary, we conclude that
X
is a
\mathbb{P}^{0}
-supermartingale.

∎

3.2
Characterization of the limiting process

To further analyse the limiting process of the previous section, we consider the decreasing supermartingale coupling (see Appendix
B
) of two marginals
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t+\epsilon}
. Recall that
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
denotes the unique phase transition point which divides the martingale region (to the right of
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
) and the supermartingale region (to the left of
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
). In the supermartingale region, the mass at
x\leq x^{\epsilon}_{1}(t)
is transported (through the quantile coupling) to the destination
T_{d}^{\epsilon}(t,x)=F^{-1}(t+\epsilon,F(t,x))
. In the martingale region, the two supporting maps
T_{u}^{\epsilon}
and
T_{d}^{\epsilon}
are characterized in Proposition
B.4
.

Set
\epsilon_{0}:=\epsilon_{1}\wedge\epsilon_{2}
, where
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
are as in Assumptions
3.1
and
3.3
, respectively.

Assumption 3.7
.

(i)

The map
(t,\epsilon)\mapsto x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
is uniformly continuous (with continuity modulus
\rho_{1}
) and also uniformly bounded on
\{(t,\epsilon):0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0},0\leq t\leq 1-\epsilon\}

(ii)

For every
t\in[0,1]
,
r_{t}=r\in(-\infty,\infty]
.

(iii)

For every
t\in[0,1]
, we have
\partial_{tx}f(t,m_{t})>0
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.1
,
3.3
,
3.7
(i) and (ii) hold. Then the limit
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
exists. Moreover, if we denote the limit by
x_{1}(t)
, then
t\mapsto x_{1}(t)
is continuous and uniquely determined by the equation

\int_{x_{1}(t)}^{+\infty}(x_{1}(t)-\xi)\partial_{t}f(t,\xi)d\xi=0,\quad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
B.3
that
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
is unique for all
t\in[0,1)
,
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
. From Assumption
3.7
(i), using the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, we have that
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
converges when
{\epsilon}\to 0
, and the limit remains a continuous function.

Using the properties of the decreasing supermartingale coupling (see Appendix
B
), we have that
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
and
y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
satisfy the mean and mass preservation conditions:

\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{r_{t}}f(t,x)dx=\int_{y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{r_{t+\epsilon}}f(t+{\epsilon},x)dx,~~~\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{r_{t}}xf(t,x)dx=\int_{y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{r_{t+\epsilon}}xf(t+\epsilon,x)dx.

Rearranging, and using that
r_{t}=r_{t+\epsilon}
, gives

\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{r_{t}}(f(t,x)-f(t+\epsilon,x))dx=\int_{y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}f(t+\epsilon,x)dx,~~~\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{+\infty}x(f(t,x)-f(t+\epsilon,x))dx=\int_{y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}xf(t+\epsilon,x)dx.

Using Taylor’s expansion
f(t+\epsilon,x)-f(t,x)=\partial_{t}f(t,x)\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{tt}f(\xi_{1},x)\epsilon^{2}
(with
\xi_{1}\in(t,t+\epsilon)
) in the first equation above and applying the mean value theorem for definite integrals, we get that

-\epsilon\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}f(t,x)dx-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{+\infty}\partial_{tt}f(\xi_{1},x)dx=\left(x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)-y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)\right)f(t,\xi_{2}),

where
\xi_{2}
lies in
[y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t),x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)]
. Letting
\epsilon
tend to
0
on both sides, the l.h.s. tends to
0
, and consequently r.h.s. also tends to
0
. It follows that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
. Dividing both sides by
\epsilon
we get

-\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{+\infty}\partial_{t}f(t,x)dx=\frac{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)-y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}{\epsilon}f(t,\xi_{2})+O(\epsilon),

(3.6)

Similarly,

-\int_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}^{+\infty}x\partial_{t}f(t,x)dx=\frac{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)-y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}{\epsilon}\xi_{2}f(t,\xi_{2})+O(\epsilon).

(3.7)

Note that Assumption
3.7
(ii) ensures that
x\mapsto\partial_{t}f(t,x)
is not a constant.

Combining (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), and letting
{\epsilon}
tend to

\int_{x_{1}(t)}^{+\infty}(x-x_{1}(t))\partial_{t}f(t,x)dx=0.\vskip-28.45274pt

∎

Remark 3.9
.

The Assumption

(ii) can be relaxed. For example, if

is differentiable, then the equation that characterizes

Consequently, the subsequent arguments would have to be adjusted. Since our main motivation is the examples that satisfy Assumption

We also introduce

\int_{x}^{T_{u}(t,x)}(x-\xi)\partial_{t}f(t,\xi)d\xi=0,

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.1
,

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle M_{t}(\varphi,\mathbf{x}):=\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}j_{d}(s,\mathbf{x}_{s-})D\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{s-})\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(s)<\mathbf{x}_{s-}<m_{s}\}}ds-\int_{0}^{t}\large[[\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{s-}+j_{u}(s,\mathbf{x}_{s-}))

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}_{t_{k}^{n}}[\{\varphi(X_{t^{n}_{k}}-J_{d}^{\epsilon^{n}_{k}}(t^{n}_{k},X_{t^{n}_{k}}))-\varphi(X_{t^{n}_{k}})\}\frac{J_{u}^{\epsilon^{n}_{k}}}{J_{d}^{\epsilon^{n}_{k}}+J_{u}^{\epsilon^{n}_{k}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}^{\epsilon_{k}^{n}}(t^{n}_{k})<X_{t_{k}^{n}}<m^{\epsilon_{k}^{n}}(t^{n}_{k})\}}],

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}_{t_{k}^{n}}[\{\varphi(X_{t^{n}_{k}}-J_{d}^{\epsilon^{n}_{k}}(t^{n}_{k},X_{t^{n}_{k}}))-\varphi(X_{t^{n}_{k}})\}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{t_{k}^{n}}\leq x_{1}^{\epsilon_{k}^{n}}(t^{n}_{k})\}}],

By the continuity of

and noticing that the density

\epsilon^{n}_{k}
small and for all

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}_{t_{k}^{n}}[\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}^{\epsilon_{k}^{n}}(t^{n}_{k})<X_{t_{k}^{n}}<m^{\epsilon_{k}^{n}}(t^{n}_{k})\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(s)<X_{s}<m_{s}\}}]|

\displaystyle\leq

3.3
Optimality of the local Lévy process

In this section, we establish the optimality of the previously obtained limiting process to the continuous-time primal problem
\mathbf{P}_{\infty}
.

We will need the optimal dual strategy (in discrete time one-period case) which is given in Lemma
B.5
; in the following, we denote
h^{\epsilon}:=\hat{h}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}},\varphi^{\epsilon}:=\hat{\varphi}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}},\psi^{\epsilon}:=\hat{\psi}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}
.

Now let us introduce the optimal dual strategies for the continuous-time transport problem. First, we define the dynamic strategy
h^{*}
by

h^{*}(t,x)=0,~~\mbox{when }x\leq x_{1}(t),

\partial_{x}h^{*}(t,x)=\frac{c_{x}(x,T_{u}(t,x))-c_{x}(x,x)}{j_{u}(t,x)},\quad x_{1}(t)<x<m_{t},

\lim_{x\downarrow x_{1}(t)}h^{*}(t,x)=0,

h^{*}(t,x)=h^{*}(t,T_{u}^{-1}(t,x))-c_{y}(T_{u}^{-1}(t,x),x),~~\mbox{when }x\geq m_{t}.

We remark that from the above definition, for all
t\in[0,1]
,

is continuous both at
x_{1}(t)
and
m_{t}
. In addition,
\psi^{*}
is defined, up to a constant, by

\partial_{x}\psi^{*}(t,x):=-h^{*}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in E=\{(t,x):t\in[0,1],x\in(l_{t},r_{t})\}.

Now using the same arguments as
[
41
, Corollary 3.10]
together with the fact that
h^{*}(t,x)=0
for
t\in[0,1]
and
x\leq x_{1}(t)
, we have that
\psi^{*}\in C^{1,1}(E)
.

For the (static part of the) dual limiting strategy, let
\gamma^{*}(dt):=\delta_{\{0\}}(dt)+\delta_{\{1\}}(dt)+Leb(dt)
be a finite measure on
[0,1]
, where

denotes the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]

One further defines
\lambda_{0}^{*}
and
\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{*}
by
\lambda_{0}^{*}(0,x):=\psi^{*}(0,x)
,
\lambda_{0}^{*}(1,x):=\psi^{*}(1,x)
,
\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{*}(0,x):=|\psi^{*}(0,x)|
, and
\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{*}(1,x):=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\psi^{*}(t,x)|
; and for all
(t,x)\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{*}(t,x):=\partial_{t}\psi^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<x\leq m_{t}\}}\left(\partial_{x}\psi^{*}j_{d}+\nu[\psi^{*}-\psi^{*}(\cdot,T_{u})+c(\cdot,T_{u})]\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq x_{1}(t)\}}\partial_{x}\psi^{*}j_{d},

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{*}:=|\partial_{t}\psi^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<x<m_{t}\}}\left(\partial_{x}\psi^{*}j_{d}+\nu[\psi^{*}-\psi^{*}(\cdot,T_{u})]\right)|+\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<x<m_{t}\}}\nu[|c(\cdot,T_{u})|]

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}_{x\leq x_{1}(t)}|\partial_{x}\psi^{*}j_{d}|.

Finally we define
\lambda^{*}(x,dt):=\lambda_{0}^{*}(t,x)\gamma^{*}(dt)
and
\bar{\lambda}^{*}(x,dt):=\bar{\lambda}_{0}^{*}(t,x)\gamma^{*}(dt)

The next result shows that the limiting process of the previous sections solves the continuous-time supermartingale optimal transport problem. Let
H^{*}
be the
\mathbb{F}
-predictable process on
\Omega
defined by

Theorem 3.15
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.2
,
3.1
,
3.3
,
3.7
hold, and that

(3.12)

Then the supermartingale transport problem (

) is solved by the local Lévy process (
3.11
). Moreover, we have that

and the following duality is valid:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{0}}[C(X_{\cdot})]=\mathbf{P}_{\infty}(\mu)=\mathbf{D}_{\infty}(\mu)=\mu(\lambda^{*}),

and the optimal value is given by

\mu(\lambda^{*})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x_{1}(t)}^{m_{t}}\frac{j_{d}(t,x)}{j_{u}(t,x)}c(x,x+j_{u}(t,x))f(t,x)dxdt.

In order to prove Theorem
3.15
, we consider the following partition of

\pi_{n}=(t_{k}^{n})_{0\leq k\leq n}
, where
t_{k}^{n}:=k\epsilon
with

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{j_{d}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})}{j_{u}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<\mathbf{x}_{t}<m_{t}\}}\left(\psi^{*}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})-\psi^{*}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t}+j_{u}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t}))+c(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}+j_{u}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t}))\right)dt.

\displaystyle T_{u}^{\epsilon}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)<x<m^{\epsilon}_{t}\}}\to T_{u}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<x<m_{t}\}},\ h^{\epsilon}\to h^{*},\ \partial_{t}\psi^{\epsilon}\to\partial_{t}\psi^{*},\ \psi^{\epsilon}\to\psi^{*}

locally uniformly on

.

Suppose Assumptions

,

The arguments are almost identical to those of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\varphi^{\epsilon}(t_{k},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}})+\psi^{\epsilon}(t_{k},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k+1}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\varphi^{\epsilon}(t_{k},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}})+\psi^{\epsilon}(t_{k},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}})\right)+\psi^{\epsilon}(t_{n-1},\mathbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\psi^{\epsilon}(t_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}})-\psi^{\epsilon}(t_{k},\mathbf{x}_{t_{k}})\right)-\psi^{\epsilon}(0,\mathbf{x}_{0}).

3.16
and the arguments of
[
41

4
Examples

In this section, we will consider three examples of supermartingales matching a given PCOCD. In contrary to the martingale case of
[
52
,
41
]
(that covers left-curtain and right-curtain couplings), in the supermartingale context, the increasing and decreasing couplings provide significantly different dynamics in continuous-time.

4.1
Uniform distribution with bounded support

In this section the family of marginal distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfies

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{e^{t}+e^{2t}}\lambda_{[-e^{2t},e^{t}]},\quad t\in[0,1].

(4.14)

(This example was also considered in the martingale case by Juillet
[
52
]
.) Our goal is to explicitly determine the quantities that define the SDE given in Theorem
3.14
.

Note that
\ell_{t}=-e^{2t}
and
r_{t}=e^{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Furthermore,
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
with
m_{\epsilon}(t)=\ell_{t}
and
m^{\epsilon}(t)=r_{t}
, for each
t\in[0,1)
and
\epsilon\in(0,1-t]
. Recall that
m_{\epsilon},m^{\epsilon}
maximizes and minimizes, respectively, the function
x\mapsto F(t+\epsilon,x)-F(t,x)
. Observe that
m_{\epsilon},m^{\epsilon}
are independent of
\epsilon>0
. By direct computation we have that, for all
t\in(0,1)
,
\partial_{t}F(t,\cdot)
is minimized and maximized at
m_{t}=e^{t}
and
\tilde{m}_{t}=-e^{2t}
. Then
m^{0}(t):=m_{t}=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}m^{\epsilon}(t)=e^{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. It is now easy to see that
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfies Assumption
3.3
.
However, since
t\mapsto r_{t}
is not constant and
m_{t}=r_{t}
, Assumption
3.7
does not hold. For this reason, most of the results of Section
3.2
(that lead to Theorem
3.14
) cannot be applied, and thus we will prove the relevant statements by direct calculations.

Now fix
t\in[0,1)
and
\epsilon\in(0,1-t]
, and consider the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}
of
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t+\epsilon}
(see Appendix
B.1
). Since, Assumption
3.1
is satisfied, the phase transition point, that separates the martingale and supermartingale regions, is unique; see Lemma
B.3
. The transition threshold is denoted by

x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t):=x_{1}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}},

see (
B.50
).

Lemma 4.1
.

If
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is given by (
4.14
), then the phase transition
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
is given by

x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)=\frac{e^{t}(e^{2(t+\epsilon)}-e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{t})+e^{2t}(e^{t}-2e^{t+\epsilon})}{e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{2(t+\epsilon)}-e^{t}-e^{2t}}.

(4.15)

Furthermore, the limit
x_{1}(t):=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)
exists and is given by

x_{1}(t)=\frac{-e^{t}}{1+2e^{t}}\in(-e^{2t},e^{t}),\quad t\in[0,1].

(4.16)

Proof.

To determine
x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)\in(\ell_{t},r_{t})
we use the fact that under the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}
,
\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t),r_{t})}
is mapped to
\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t),r_{t+\epsilon})}
in a martingale way, where
y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)=y_{1}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}
(see (
B.51
)). In particular, the pair
(x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t),y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t))
is uniquely determined by the mass and mean preservation condition

\int^{r_{t}}_{x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}z^{i}\mu_{t}(dz)=\int^{r_{t+\epsilon}}_{y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}z^{i}\mu_{t+\epsilon}(dz),\quad i=0,1.

(4.17)

Direct computation leads to

\frac{e^{t}-x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}{e^{t}+e^{2t}}=\frac{e^{t+\epsilon}-y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)}{e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{2(t+\epsilon)}},\quad\frac{e^{2t}-(x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t))^{2}}{e^{t}+e^{2t}}=\frac{e^{2(t+\epsilon)}-(y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t))^{2}}{e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{2(t+\epsilon)}}.

Solving the above equations gives

x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)=\frac{e^{t}(e^{2(t+\epsilon)}-e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{t})+e^{2t}(e^{t}-2e^{t+\epsilon})}{e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{2(t+\epsilon)}-e^{t}-e^{2t}},\quad y_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)=x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t)-e^{t+\epsilon}+e^{t}.

By letting
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, direct calculation (using the L’Hôpital’s rule) gives that
x_{1}(t)=\frac{-e^{t}}{1+2e^{t}}
(note that
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}y^{\epsilon}_{1}(t)=x_{1}(t)
), which concludes the proof.
∎

Even though not all the Assumptions
3.1
,
3.3
,
3.7
are satisfied in the case of (
4.14
), the following result shows that the statement of Theorem
3.14
remains true.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is given by (
4.14
), then the statement of Theorem
3.14
holds, and the corresponding SDE is explicitly given by

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s-}>x_{1}(s)\}}j_{u}(s,X_{s-})(dN_{s}-\nu_{s}ds)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s-}\leq x_{1}(s)\}}\tilde{\nu}_{s}ds,

where
(N_{s})_{0\leq s\leq 1}
is a unit size jump process with predictable compensated process
(\nu_{s})_{0\leq s\leq 1}
given by
\nu_{s}:=\frac{j_{d}}{j_{u}}(s,X_{s-})
, with
j_{d}(s,x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{s}-x}{1+e^{s}}(1+2e^{s})
,
j_{u}(s,x)=e^{s}-x
, and
\tilde{\nu}_{s}:=\frac{e^{2s}-x(1+2e^{s})}{1+e^{s}}
.

In addition, if Assumption
2.2
and the integrability condition (
3.12
) are satisfied, then the above process is the optimal solution for the
primal problem
(
2.4
)
and the conclusion of Theorem
3.15
holds.

Proof.

Since, for
t\in[0,1]
, we have that
m_{t}=r_{t}
, it follows that
\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1}(t)<X_{t-}<m_{t}\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{t-}>x_{1}(t)\}}
a.s. We now determine
(j_{d},j_{u})
.

For
t\in[0,1)
and
\epsilon\in(0,1-t]
, we consider the one-period decreasing supermartingale coupling
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}
, which is supported on the graphs of two functions
T_{u}^{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)=T_{u}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}(\cdot)
,
T_{d}^{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)=T_{d}^{\mu_{t},\mu_{t+\epsilon}}(\cdot)
; see Appendix
B.1
. On
(x_{1}^{\epsilon}(t),m^{\epsilon}(t)=r_{t})
,
(T_{d}^{\epsilon}(t,\cdot),T_{u}^{\epsilon}(t,\cdot)
can be determined from the mass and mean preservation condition

\int^{T^{\epsilon}_{u}(t,x)}_{x}z^{i}\mu_{t}(dz)=\int^{T^{\epsilon}_{u}(t,x)}_{T^{\epsilon}_{d}(t,x)}z^{i}\mu_{t+\epsilon}(dz),\quad i=0,1.

Note that, since
T_{u}^{\epsilon}(t,x)\geq m^{\epsilon}(t)=r_{t}
for
x\leq r_{t}
, we have that
\int^{T^{\epsilon}_{u}(t,x)}_{x}z^{i}\mu_{t}(dz)=\int^{r_{t}}_{x}z^{i}\mu_{t}(dz)
for
i=0,1

Lemma 4.8
.

For

Proof.

Let

be defined as in (

is continuous, where
F(t,\epsilon,\hat{x}^{\epsilon}_{1}(t))=0

We claim that
(t,\epsilon,x)\mapsto F(t,\epsilon,x)
is continuously differentiable.

First, for
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
, we have that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\epsilon}(t,\epsilon,x)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{2}(t+\epsilon)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon-(\sqrt{t+\epsilon}-\sqrt{t})}{\epsilon^{2}},

and it follows that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\epsilon}(t,\epsilon,x)=-\Phi^{\prime}(x)\frac{1}{4}t^{-\frac{3}{2}}
. On the other hand,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\epsilon}(t,0,x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{F(t,\epsilon,x)-F(t,0,x)}{\epsilon}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{2}t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon-(\sqrt{t+\epsilon}-\sqrt{t})}{\epsilon^{2}}=-\Phi^{\prime}(x)\frac{1}{4}t^{-\frac{3}{2}},

and hence

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}
and

follow similarly by the direct computations.

Now we check that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}(t,\epsilon,x)|_{F(t,\epsilon,x)=0}=\Phi^{\prime}(x)\left[x\frac{1-\Phi(x)}{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}-1\right]<0,

where for the inequality we use the fact that

By the implicit function theorem, we get that

. ∎

Remark 4.10

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\in(0,1]}
be specified by (

Lemma 4.13

Let
G

4.37

4.38

(t,\epsilon)\mapsto\hat{x}^{\epsilon}_{1}(t)
is continuous, where
G(t,\epsilon,\hat{x}^{\epsilon}_{1}(t))=0

We claim that
(t,\epsilon,x)\mapsto G(t,\epsilon,x)
is continuously differentiable. Indeed,

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\epsilon}(t,\epsilon,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}(1-\Phi(x))+\frac{1}{8}\Phi^{\prime}(x)t^{-\frac{3}{2}}(2t+1)+\frac{1}{8}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)t^{-1}

∎

Appendix A
Brenier’s Theorem

A.1
Brenier’s Theorem in Optimal Transport (OT)

Let
(X,Y)
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
{\cal P}_{2}
be the set of all (Borel) probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We denote respectively
\mu
and
\nu
the (integrable) marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
.

Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a (measurable) cost, or payoff, function. The primal Optimal Transport problem corresponds to

P_{OT}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}_{2}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X,Y)],~~~~\mbox{ where }{\cal P}_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}_{2}:X\sim_{\mathbb{P}}\mu,Y\sim_{\mathbb{P}}\nu\}.

The above optimisation problem has a dual problem, which is defined by

D_{OT}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{(\varphi,\psi)\in{\cal D}_{s}}\{\mu(\varphi)+\nu(\psi)\},\mbox{ where }{\cal D}_{s}:=\{(\varphi,\psi):\mu(\varphi\vee 0)+\nu(\psi\vee 0)<\infty,\varphi\oplus\psi\geq c\}.

In the above,
\varphi\oplus\psi(x,y):=\varphi(x)+\psi(y)
and
\chi(f):=\int fd\chi,
for a (Borel) measure
\chi
on
\mathbb{R}
and a (Borel) measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Under mild conditions, it can be proved that the duality
P_{OT}(\mu,\nu)=D_{OT}(\mu,\nu)
holds. (The notation
{\cal D}_{s}
is motivated by the fact that, in financial terms,
(\varphi,\psi)\in{\cal D}_{s}
corresponds to a
static
hedging strategy.)

The Brenier’s theorem gives the explicit expressions of the optimizers of both the primal and dual problems. In the case
\mu
is atomless, a candidate primal optimizer is given by the Fréchet-Hoeffding (or quantile) coupling
\mathbb{P}_{*}
defined by

\mathbb{P}_{*}(dx,dy):=\mu(dx)\delta_{T_{*}(x)}(dy),

(A.39)

push-forward map from
\mu
to
\nu
is where
T_{*}:=F_{\nu}^{-1}\circ F_{\mu}
. Here
F_{\nu}^{-1}
is the right-continuous inverse of (the c.d.f. of
\nu
)
F_{\nu}
:
F_{\nu}^{-1}(t):=\inf\{y:F_{\nu}>t\}
.

We further introduce candidate dual optimizers
\varphi_{*}
and
\psi_{*}
(up to a constant) by setting

\varphi_{*}(x):=c(x,T_{*}(x))-\psi_{*}\circ T_{*}(x),\quad\psi^{\prime}_{*}(y):=c_{y}(T_{*}^{-1}(y),y),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.40)

Theorem A.1

(Brenier
[
18
]
, Rachev and Rüschendorf
[
61
]
)
.

Suppose
\mu
has no atoms. Let
c
be an upper semi-continuous function with (at most) linear growth. Assume that the partial derivative
c_{xy}
exists and satisfies the Spence-Mirrlees condition
c_{xy}>0
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{*}
be as in (
A.39
),
(\varphi_{*},\psi_{*})
as in (
A.40
), and assume that
(\mu(\varphi_{*}\vee 0)+\nu(\psi_{*}\vee 0))<\infty
.

Then
\mathbb{P}_{*}\in{\cal P}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
,
(\varphi_{*},\psi_{*})\in{\cal D}_{s}
and

\int c(x,T_{*}(x))\mu(dx)=P_{OT}(\mu,\nu)=D_{OT}(\mu,\nu)=\mu(\varphi_{*})+\nu(\psi_{*}).

Remark A.2
.

In the case
c_{xy}<0
, then
P_{OT}=D_{OT}
and the optimal coupling is the antitone coupling which is supported on
x\mapsto T^{*}(x):=F^{-1}_{\nu}\circ(1-F_{\mu}(x))
. The dual optimizers are still defined through (
A.40
), but with
T^{*}
in place of
T_{*}
.

A.2
Brenier’s Theorem in Martingale Optimal Transport (MOT)

Two (integrable) probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
are increasing in convex order, denoted by
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
, if for all convex functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that
\mu(f)\leq\nu(f)
. By the Strassen’s classical result
[
62
]
,
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
is equivalent to non-emptiness of the set
{\cal M}_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal P}_{2}(\mu,\nu):\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[Y|X]=X\}
.

In this section we suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
have finite first moments,
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
, and the following Dispersion Assumption holds:

Assumption A.3

(Dispersion)
.

\mu,\nu
admit densities
f_{\mu},f_{\nu}
that satisfy

1.

f_{\mu}>0
on
(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu})
,
f_{\nu}>0
on
(\ell_{\nu},r_{\nu})
, and
\ell_{\nu}\leq\ell_{\mu}<r_{\mu}\leq r_{\nu}
.

2.

There exists
\ell_{\mu}<\underline{m}:=\underline{m}^{\mu,\nu}<\overline{m}:=\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}<r_{\mu}
such that

f_{\mu}>f_{\nu},\textrm{ on }(\underline{m},\overline{m}),\quad f_{\nu}>f_{\mu}\textrm{ on }(\ell_{\nu},\underline{m})\cup(\overline{m},r_{\nu}),\quad f_{\mu}=f_{\nu},\textrm{ on }\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\}.

Remark A.4
.

Note that if
\overline{\mu}=\overline{\nu}
and Assumption
A.3
holds, then
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
. Furthermore, the difference of distribution functions
y\mapsto F_{\nu}(y)-F_{\mu}(y)
attains the (unique) maximum and minimum at
\underline{m}^{\mu,\nu}
and
\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}
, respectively.

Given a (measurable) payoff function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the primal problem of MOT is defined by

P_{MOT}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in{\cal M}_{2}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[c(X,Y)].

The MOT problem also has a dual problem:

D_{MOT}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{(\varphi,\psi,h)\in{\cal D}_{ss}}\{\mu(\varphi)+\nu(\psi)\},

where the set of dual variables is defined as:

{\cal D}_{ss}:=\{(\varphi,\psi,h):\mu(\varphi\vee 0)+\nu(\psi\vee 0)<\infty,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\varphi\oplus\psi+h^{\otimes}\geq c\}

with
\varphi\oplus\psi(x,y):=\varphi(x)+\psi(y)
, and
h^{\otimes}(x,y):=h(x)(y-x)
. (The notation
{\cal D}_{ss}
is motivated by the fact that, in financial terms,
(\varphi,\psi,h)\in{\cal D}_{ss}
corresponds to a
semi-static
hedging strategy, where the static part is
(\varphi,\psi)
and the dynamic part corresponds to
h
.)

We now introduce candidate optimal quantities. We start with a pair of functions
(T_{d}^{\mu,\nu},T_{u}^{\mu,\nu})
that will support the optimal coupling in the case
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
satisfy Assumption
A.3
.

Set

g^{\mu,\nu}(x,y):=F_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mu}(x)+F_{\nu}(y)-F_{\mu}(y)),\quad x\in(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu}),y\in(\ell_{\nu},r_{\nu}).

(A.41)

Define

T^{\mu,\nu}_{d}(x)=T^{\mu,\nu}_{u}(x)=x,\quad x\in[\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu}).

(A.42)

For
x\in(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu})
, let
T_{d}^{\mu,\nu}(x)
be the (unique) scalar such that

\int_{-\infty}^{x}[F_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mu}(\xi))-\xi]f_{\mu}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{-\infty}^{T_{d}(x)}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}]}(\xi)[g^{\mu,\nu}(x,\xi)-\xi](f_{\nu}(\xi)-f_{\mu}(\xi))d\xi=0

(A.43)

and set

T_{u}^{\mu,\nu}(x)=g^{\mu,\nu}(x,T_{d}^{\mu,\nu}(x)),\quad x\in(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}).

(A.44)

Remark A.5
.

The existence and uniqueness of (
A.43
) is given by Henry-Labordère and Touzi
[
42
]
. Furthermore,
T_{d}(x)\leq x\leq T_{u}(x)
for all
x\in(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu})
, and, under Assumption
A.3
,
T_{d},T_{u}
are both continuous,
T_{d}
(resp.
T_{u}
) is strictly increasing (resp. decreasing) on
(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}]
. See Henry-Labordère and Touzi
[
42
, Section 4]
(note, however, that
[
42
]
constructed the left-curtain martingale coupling, while in the present paper we are working with the right-curtain coupling.)

Using the pair
(T^{\mu,\nu}_{d},T^{\mu,\nu}_{u})
we now define a candidate martingale coupling through its disintegration w.r.t. the first marginal
\mu
. Define a measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mu,\nu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mu,\nu}(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=\mu(dx)\mathbf{1}_{[\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})}(x)\delta_{x}(dy)

(A.45)

\displaystyle+\mu(dx)\mathbf{1}_{(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu})}(x)\left[q^{\mu,\nu}(x)\delta_{T^{\mu,\nu}_{u}(x)}(dy)+(1-q^{\mu,\nu}(x))\delta_{T^{\mu,\nu}_{d}(x)}(dy)\right],

where

q^{\mu,\nu}(x):=\frac{x-T^{\mu,\nu}_{d}(x)}{T^{\mu,\nu}_{u}(x)-T^{\mu,\nu}_{d}(x)},\quad x\in(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}),

(A.46)

corresponds to the (conditional) martingale probability of jumping to
T^{\mu,\nu}_{u}(x)
from
x
.

We now introduce a candidate triple
(\varphi_{*},\psi_{*},h_{*})=(\varphi^{\mu,\nu}_{*},\psi^{\mu,\nu}_{*},h^{\mu,\nu}_{*})
for the dual problem. To ease the notation we will write
(T_{d},T_{u})=(T_{d}^{\mu,\nu},T_{u}^{\mu,\nu})
.

Define
h_{*}
, up to a constant, by

\displaystyle h_{*}

\displaystyle=h_{*}\circ T_{d}^{-1}+c_{y}(\cdot,\cdot)-c_{y}(T_{d}^{-1},\cdot)\quad\mbox{on }[\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu}),

(A.47)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{x}(\cdot,T_{u})-c_{x}(\cdot,T_{d})}{T_{u}-T_{d}}\quad\mbox{on }(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}).

(Note that the constant can be chosen such that

is continuous.) The (continuous) function
\psi_{*}
is then given (also up to a constant) by

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}_{*}

\displaystyle=c_{y}(T_{d}^{-1},\cdot)-h_{*}\circ T_{d}^{-1}\quad\mbox{on }[\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu}),

(A.48)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}_{*}

\displaystyle=c_{y}(T_{u}^{-1},\cdot)-h_{*}\circ T_{u}^{-1}\quad\mbox{on }(\ell_{\mu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu}).

Finally the function
\varphi_{*}
is defined by

\displaystyle\varphi_{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\mu,\nu}}[c(X,Y)-\psi_{*}(Y)\lvert X=x],\quad x\in(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu}).

(A.49)

Theorem A.6

(Henry-Labordère and Touzi
[
42
, Theorems 4.5, 5.1]
)
.

Suppose
\mu\leq_{c}\nu
satisfies Assumption
A.3
. Assume further that
\int\varphi_{*}\vee 0d\mu+\int\psi_{*}\vee 0d\nu<\infty
, and that the partial derivative

exists and
c_{xyy}<0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{*}=\mathbb{P}^{\mu,\nu}
be as in (

) and
(\varphi_{*},\psi_{*},h_{*})
as in (

Then
\mathbb{P}_{*}\in{\cal M}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
,
(\varphi_{*},\psi_{*},h_{*})\in{\cal D}_{ss}
and the strong duality holds:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}_{*}}[c(X,Y)]=P_{MOT}(\mu,\nu)=D_{MOT}(\mu,\nu)=\mu(\varphi_{*})+\nu(\psi_{*}).

Theorem B.1

]
)

We assume that

We now argue that

S^{\nu}(\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})})=\mu\lvert_{[\overline{m},r_{\mu})}+S^{\nu-\mu\lvert_{[\overline{m},r_{\mu})}}(\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\overline{m})}).

Thanks to the properties of the shadow measure (
S^{\nu-\mu\lvert_{[\overline{m},r_{\mu})}}(\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\overline{m})})

for some

, then

\mu
is atomless and using the construction of Bayraktar et al.
[
5
]
, we have that the decreasing supermartingale coupling is supported on the graph of two functions
\hat{T}_{d}=\hat{T}_{d}^{\mu,\nu},\hat{T}_{u}=\hat{T}_{u}^{\mu,\nu}:(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu})\to(\ell_{\nu},r_{\nu})
. If, in addition, the Dispersion Assumption holds, then using Lemma
B.3
and utilising the construction and properties of the right-curtain martingale coupling
P^{\mu,\nu}
(as in (
A.45
)) we have that
(\hat{T}_{d},\hat{T}_{u})
satisfies some useful properties.

First,

\hat{T}_{d}(x)\leq x\leq\hat{T}_{u}(x)\textrm{ for all }x\in(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu}),\quad\textrm{and}\quad\hat{T}_{d}=\hat{T}_{u}\textrm{ on }[\bar{m},r_{\mu}).

(B.52)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{d}\textrm{ is continuous and strictly increasing on }(\ell_{\mu},r_{\mu}),

(B.53)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{d}(x)=F^{-1}_{\nu}\circ F_{\mu}\textrm{ on }(\ell_{\mu},x_{1}^{\mu,\nu})

and

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{u}\textrm{ is continuous on }(x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu}),\textrm{ strictly decreasing on }(x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\overline{m}),

(B.54)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{u}\equiv\infty\textrm{ on }(\ell_{\mu},x_{1}^{\mu,\nu}].

In particular, in terms of
(\hat{T}_{d},\hat{T}_{u})
, the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\mathbb{P}^{\mu,\nu}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=\mu(dx)\mathbf{1}_{(\ell_{\mu},x_{1}^{\mu,\nu}]\cup[\overline{m},r_{\mu})}(x)\delta_{\hat{T}_{d}(x)}(dy)

(B.55)

\displaystyle+\mu(dx)\mathbf{1}_{(x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\overline{m})}\left\{\frac{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-x}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}\delta_{\hat{T}_{d}(x)}(dy)+\frac{x-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}\delta_{\hat{T}_{u}(x)}(dy)\right\}.

Equivalently, we can express
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
in terms of the right-curtain coupling
\mathbb{P}^{\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})},\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}^{\mu,\nu}r_{\nu})}}
of
\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})}
and
\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}^{\mu,\nu}r_{\nu})}
(see (
A.45
)):

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=\mu(dx)\mathbf{1}_{(\ell_{\mu},x_{1}^{\mu,\nu}]}(x)\delta_{F^{-1}_{\nu}(F_{\mu}(x))}(dy)+\mathbf{1}_{(x^{\mu,\nu}_{1},r_{\mu})}\mathbb{P}^{\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})},\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}^{\mu,\nu}r_{\nu})}}(dx,dy).

(B.56)

While the pair
(\hat{T}_{d},\hat{T}_{u})
can be explicitly constructed using potential functions of the underlying measures (see Bayraktar et al.
[
5
]
), the following representation is more useful in the present context.

Recall the definition of
g=g_{\mu,\nu}
(see (
A.41
)).

Proposition B.4
.

For each
x\in[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\overline{m}^{\mu,\nu})
,
\hat{T}_{u}(x)
is uniquely characterised by the integral equation:

\int_{x}^{\infty}[F^{-1}_{\nu}(F_{\mu}(\xi))-\xi]f_{\mu}(\xi)d\xi+\int_{\hat{T}_{u}(x)}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{(x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},\infty)}(\xi)[g(x,\xi)-\xi](f_{\nu}(\xi)-f_{\mu}(\xi))d\xi=0,

and
\hat{T}_{d}(x)=g(x,\hat{T}_{u}(x))
.

Proof.

Since
\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})}\leq_{c}\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}(\mu,\nu),r_{\nu})}
and the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu,\nu}
of
\mu\leq_{cd}\nu
coincides with the right-curtain coupling of
\mu\lvert_{[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})}\leq_{c}\nu\lvert_{[y_{1}(\mu,\nu),r_{\nu})}
on
[x_{1}^{\mu,\nu},r_{\mu})
, the result follows immediately from the construction of the latter, which is given in Section
A.2
(see (
A.43
) and (
A.44
)).
∎

B.2
Optimal dual strategy for decreasing SMOT

We introduce now the optimal dual strategies to the dual problem (
2.5
) in the case the decreasing supermartingale coupling
\hat{\mathbb{P}}=\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu,\nu}
(see Theorem
B.1
) is optimal. Recall that the pair
(\hat{T}_{d},\hat{T}_{u})
represents the supporting functions of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
, while
x_{1}=x_{1}^{\mu,\nu}
is the unique threshold that separates martingale and supermartingale regions.

In the following we suppose that
\mu\leq_{cd}\nu
satisfy Assumption
A.3
.

We introduce the following triple
(\hat{\varphi},\hat{\psi},\hat{h})=(\hat{\varphi}(\mu,\nu),\hat{\psi}(\mu,\nu),\hat{h}(\mu,\nu))
, as the candidate optimal dual strategies for
\mathbf{D}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
(with
\mathbf{D}_{2}
defined in (
2.2
)):

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{\prime}(x):=\frac{c_{x}(x,\hat{T}_{u}(x))-c_{x}(x,\hat{T}_{d}(x))}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)},\quad x_{1}<x<\overline{m};

(B.57)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow x_{1}}\hat{h}(x)=0;

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x):=\hat{h}((\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x))-c_{y}((\hat{T}_{u}^{-1}(x),x),~~x\geq\overline{m};

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x):=0,~~x\leq x_{1}.

Note that, when
x=x_{1}
,
\hat{T}_{u}(x)=\infty
and therefore
\hat{h}^{\prime}(x)=0
. Furthermore,
\hat{h}
is continuous at
x\in\{x_{1},\overline{m}\}
(note that
c_{y}(x,x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\overline{m}}(\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x)=\overline{m}
).

Denoting
L^{-1}(x):=(\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq\overline{m}\}}+(\hat{T}_{d})^{-1}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x<\overline{m}\}}
, we introduce
\hat{\psi}
, up to a constant, by

\hat{\psi}^{\prime}(x):=c_{y}(L^{-1}(x),x)-\hat{h}(L^{-1}(x)).

Note that
\hat{\psi}
can be chosen to be continuous, which then also makes

c(\cdot,\hat{T}_{u}(\cdot))-\hat{\psi}(\hat{T}_{u}(\cdot))-c(\cdot,\hat{T}_{d}(\cdot))+\hat{\psi}(\hat{T}_{d}(\cdot))-(\hat{T}_{u}(\cdot)-\hat{T}_{d}(\cdot))\hat{h}(\cdot)

continuous.

Finally, we define

\hat{\varphi}(x):=\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[c(X,Y)-\psi(Y)\lvert X=x],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that, for
x_{1}\leq x\leq\overline{m}

\hat{\varphi}(x)=\frac{x-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}\left(c(x,\hat{T}_{u}(x))-\hat{\psi}(\hat{T}_{u}(x))\right)+\frac{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-x}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}\left(c(x,\hat{T}_{d}(x))-\hat{\psi}(\hat{T}_{d}(x))\right),

\hat{\varphi}(x):=c(x,x)-\hat{\psi}(x)=-\hat{\psi}(x),\quad x>\overline{m}

and

\hat{\varphi}(x):=c(x,\hat{T}_{d}(x))-\hat{\psi}(\hat{T}_{d}(x)),\quad x<x_{1}.

From our definitions, we immediately have that
\mathbb{E}^{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}[c(X,Y)]=\mu(\hat{\varphi}(X))+\nu(\hat{\psi}(Y))
. It is left to show that the triple
(\hat{\varphi},\hat{\psi},\hat{h})
defines a superhedge.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
\mu\leq_{cd}\nu
be such that Assumption
A.3
holds. Suppose that the reward function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy Assumption
2.2
. Assume further that
\int\hat{\varphi}\vee 0d\mu+\int\psi\vee 0d\nu<\infty
.

We have that
(\hat{\varphi},\hat{\psi},\hat{h})\in{\cal D}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
.

Proof.

To obtain the proof we need to show that
\hat{h}\geq 0
and that
c(x,y)\leq\hat{\varphi}(x)+\hat{\psi}(y)+\hat{h}(x)(y-x)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. The second point, however, follows immediately from the arguments of the classical and martingale versions of Brenier’s theorem (see Theorems
A.1
and
A.6
). We now verify that
h\geq 0
; we will use that
c(\cdot,\cdot)=c_{y}(\cdot,\cdot)=0
and
c_{xy}>0
.

First, as
c_{xy}>0
, we have that when
x_{1}\leq x<\overline{m}
,

\hat{h}^{\prime}(x):=\frac{c_{x}(x,\hat{T}_{u}(x))-c_{x}(x,\hat{T}_{d}(x))}{\hat{T}_{u}(x)-\hat{T}_{d}(x)}>0.

Hence, since
\hat{h}(x_{1})=0
, we have that
\hat{h}(x)\geq 0
for all
x_{1}\leq x<\overline{m}
.

For
x\geq\overline{m}
, as
x_{1}\leq(\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x)\leq\overline{m}
, we have that
\hat{h}((\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x))\geq 0
. In addition, as
c_{y}(x,x)=0
and
c_{xy}>0
, we have that
c_{y}((\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x),x)\leq 0
. It follows that

\hat{h}(x)=\hat{h}((\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x))-c_{y}((\hat{T}_{u})^{-1}(x),x)\geq 0.

Since
\hat{h}\equiv 0
on
(-\infty,x_{1})
, the no short-selling constraint is satisfied.
∎
