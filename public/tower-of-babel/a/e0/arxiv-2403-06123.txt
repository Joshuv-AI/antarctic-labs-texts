Periodically driven three-dimensional Kitaev model
Soumya Sasidharan, Naveen Surendran

Abstract
We study the dynamics of a three-dimensional generalization of Kitaev’s honeycomb lattice spin model (defined on the hyperhoneycomb lattice) subjected to a harmonic driving of J z J_{z} , one of the three types of spin-couplings in the Hamiltonian. Using numerical solutions supported by analytical calculations based on a rotating wave approximation, we find that the system responds nonmonotonically to variations in the frequency ω \omega (while keeping the driving amplitude J J fixed) and undergoes dynamical freezing, where at specific values of ω \omega , it gets almost completely locked in the initial state throughout the evolution. However, this freezing occurs only when a constant bias is present in the driving, i.e., when J z = J ′ + J ​ cos ⁡ ω ​ t J_{z}=J^{\prime}+J\cos\omega t , with J ′ ≠ 0 J^{\prime}\neq 0 . Consequently, the bias acts as a switch that triggers the freezing phenomenon. Dynamical freezing has been previously observed in other integrable systems, such as the one-dimensional transverse-field Ising model.



I
Introduction

Kitaev’s honeycomb lattice model consists of spin-1/2 degrees of freedom located at the vertices of the lattice
Kitaev 2006
. Nearest neighbor spins interact via anisotropic Ising-like couplings, with the anisotropy direction dependent on the orientation of the link between the spins. Remarkably, the model is exactly solvable and has a quantum spin liquid ground state. Quantum spin liquids (QSL) are characterized by the absence of any magnetic ordering while having long-range entanglement, which can give rise to elementary excitations with fractional quantum numbers
Savary and Balents 2016
;
Takagi et al. 2019
. Depending on the relative strength of the spin interactions, Kitaev model exists in two distinct phases: a gapped phase supporting abelian anyons and a gapless phase supporting non-abelian anyons as elementary excitations.

Over the last few decades, experimental search for QSLs has identified several candidate materials. Examples include
\kappa
-
(\mathrm{BEDT}
-
\mathrm{TTF})_{2}\mathrm{Cu}_{2}(\mathrm{CN})_{3}

Shimizu et al. 2003
,
\mathrm{ZnCu}_{3}(\mathrm{OH})_{6}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}

Fu et al. 2015
,
\mathrm{NaYbSe}_{2}

Zhu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2021
;
Jia et al. 2020
, and
\mathrm{NaYbS}_{2}

Wu et al. 2022
. Since the discovery of Kitaev model, the search for QSLs has expanded to potential physical realizations of the model. Even though the strong and non-uniform anisotropy of the spin interactions in the model initially appeared unrealistic, Jackeli and Khaliullin showed that such interactions can arise from strong spin-orbit coupling and, in particular, become dominant in certain iridates
Jackeli and Khaliullin 2009
;
Winter et al. 2017
. Following their proposal, new materials such as
\mathrm{Na}_{2}\mathrm{IrO}_{3}

Singh and Gegenwart 2010
,
\alpha
-
\mathrm{Li}_{2}\mathrm{Ir}O_{3}

Singh et al. 2012
, and
\mathrm{H}_{3}\mathrm{LiIr}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{6}

Kitagawa et al. 2018
have been synthesized and studied as possible realizations of two-dimensional Kitaev QSL.
\alpha
-RuCl
3
is another material that has been extensively studied as a strong candidate to be a Kitaev QSL
Banerjee et al. 2016
;
Banerjee et al. 2017
;
Kasahara et al. 2018
. For comprehensive reviews of Kitaev materials, see Refs.
Hermanns et al. 2018
;
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Wen et al. 2019
;
Trebst and Hickey 2022
.

Kitaev’s construction has been extended to three dimensions based on various approaches
Ryu 2009
;
Wu et al. 2009
;
Mandal and Surendran 2009
. One particular extension involves defining the Hamiltonian on a hyperhoneycomb lattice and using the same type of interactions as in the original model
Mandal and Surendran 2009
. The hyperhoneycomb lattice model is also exactly solvable and, as in the case of the honeycomb lattice model, has two phases, respectively having a gapped and a gapless Majorana fermion spectrum. Furthermore, due to the higher dimension, the flux excitations form loops and are fermionic in nature
Mandal and Surendran 2014
.

The iridate compound
\beta
-
\mathrm{Li_{2}IrO_{3}}
has been found to be an approximate realization of the hyperhoneycomb lattice Kitaev model
Takayama et al. 2015
;
Katukuri et al. 2016
.
\gamma
-
\mathrm{Li_{2}IrO_{3}}
is another three-dimensional realization of the Kitaev model, on the stripy-honeycomb lattice
Modic et al. 2014
;
Biffin et al. 2014
.

To probe the excitations (Majorana fermions and the flux loops), we can explore the dynamical response of the system. Previously, this has been done for the hyperhoneycomb lattice Kitaev model by calculating the dynamical structure factor
Smith et al. 2016
and by examining the defect generation during a linear quench
Sarkar et al. 2020
. In this paper, we consider a different dynamical aspect of the model: how it responds when subjected to a periodic drive.

Certain integrable many-particle systems, when subjected to periodic driving, show an unusual behaviour when the driving frequency is varied
Das 2010
;
Haldar and Das 2017
;
Haldar and Das 2022
. As driving frequency increases, such systems do not respond monotonically. In particular, for certain combinations of driving frequency and amplitude, the system becomes locked in its initial state. This phenomenon, referred to as dynamical freezing, contradicts classical intuition. Classically, one would expect that as the driving frequency increases, the system’s response would diminish and eventually vanish when the driving period becomes significantly shorter than the characteristic time scales within the system, with the system then remaining in its initial state for the entire duration of driving.

Dynamical freezing in an integrable many-particle system was first observed in the one-dimensional transverse-field Ising model
Das 2010
. In this model, when the magnetic field is harmonically driven at a fixed amplitude, the long-term average of the magnetization shows a distinct pattern of peaks and valleys as a function of the frequency, with the peaks indicating near-total saturation of the magnetization. Moreover, similar behavior was observed when periodic quenches replaced harmonic driving
Bhattacharyya et al. 2012
.

Several aspects of dynamical freezing such as the effect of disorder
Roy and Das 2015
, the emergence of slow solitary oscillations
Bhattacharyya et al. 2012
, the effect of interactions
Haldar et al. 2021
, and the ability to switch the freezing on and off by tuning parameters in the Hamiltonian
Das and Moessner 2012
have been investigated in recent years. Notably, dynamical freezing has been demonstrated experimentally in a driven Ising chain
Hegde et al. 2014
.

It is worth mentioning that dynamical freezing can be considered as a many-particle counterpart of single-particle phenomena studied in the past, such as the dynamical localization of a particle moving on a lattice in the presence of an alternating electric field
Dunlap and Kenkre 1986
;
Eckardt et al. 2009
, and the coherent destruction of tunneling of a particle moving in a periodically driven double-well potential
Grossmann et al. 1991
;
Großmann and Hänggi 1992
.

The dynamics of the 1D transverse-field Ising model, which has been extensively studied in the context of dynamical freezing, can be reduced to a two-level problem for each momentum value. However, dynamical freezing goes beyond two-level systems and has been demonstrated to occur in a bilayer graphene system, which features four energy bands
Sasidharan and Surendran 2023
. The three-dimensional Kitaev model, whose dynamics we investigate in this paper, is also a four-band system.

To make the Kitaev Hamiltonian periodic in time, we harmonically drive one of the spin-couplings as follows:
J_{z}(t)=J^{\prime}+J\cos\omega t
, where
J^{\prime}
is a constant that contributes a nonzero average to
J_{z}(t)
. For nonzero
J^{\prime}
, we observe dynamical freezing in the system. When driven at a fixed amplitude
J
, the system shows a nonmonotonic response to changes in the driving frequency
\omega
, and freezes almost completely at some particular frequencies. The bias parameter
J^{\prime}
acts as a switch for the freezing—when
J^{\prime}=0
, there is no freezing. Our results follow from numerically solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation and analytical calculations based on a rotating wave approximation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we briefly review the 3D Kitaev model defined on a hyperhoneycomb lattice. In Sec.
III
, we present our results for the model subjected to a periodic drive, and we conclude with a discussion in Sec.
IV
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-J_{x}\sum_{<j,k>_{x}}\sigma_{j}^{x}\sigma_{k}^{x}-J_{y}\sum_{<j,k>_{y}}\sigma_{j}^{y}\sigma_{k}^{y}-J_{z}\sum_{<j,k>_{z}}\sigma_{j}^{z}\sigma_{k}^{z},

(1)

The elementary loops (called the plaquettes) in the hyperhoneycomb lattice contain ten sites (see Fig.
1
). The Hamiltonian commutes with the
Z_{2}
flux operators
W_{p}
defined on the plaquettes as follows:

\displaystyle W_{p}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in p}\sigma_{j}^{\beta_{j}},

(2)

where

\beta_{j}
is the label for the link going out of the plaquette
p

j

The above Hamiltonian can be solved exactly

using a Majorana fermion representation of spin-1/2
Kitaev 2006
. Kitaev introduced four species of Majorana fermions at each site:
c_{j},b_{j}^{x},b_{j}^{y}
and
b_{j}^{z}
. Then, the operators defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{\alpha}=ib_{j}^{\alpha}c_{j}

(3)

satisfy the spin-1/2 algebra when projected to the subspace corresponding to
b_{j}^{x}b_{j}^{y}b_{j}^{z}c_{j}=1
. Writing the Hamiltonian in terms of the Majorana operators, it is straightforward to show that the operators
u_{jk}=ib_{j}^{\alpha}b_{k}^{\alpha}
, defined on the link connecting sites
j
and
k

\alpha

H
as well as among themselves. In the Majorana representation, the plaquette operator becomes

\displaystyle W_{p}

\displaystyle=\prod_{<j,k>\in p}u_{jk},

(4)

where
<j,k>
runs over the links in
p
.

It has been shown that for the ground state
W_{p}=1
for all
p
. This corresponds to (for a particular gauge choice)
u_{jk}=1
for all links
<j,k>
. Then, in terms of the Fourier modes of the Majorana fermions (which are standard complex fermions), the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bf k}H({\bf k}),

where,

\displaystyle H({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{k}}\Bigg[\frac{i}{2}\left\{e^{ik_{3}}\delta_{1}c_{1}^{\dagger}(\textbf{k})c_{4}(\textbf{k})+\delta_{2}c_{3}^{\dagger}(\textbf{k})c_{2}(\textbf{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{i}{2}\left\{J_{z}c_{3}^{\dagger}(\textbf{k})c_{4}(\textbf{k})+J_{z}c_{1}^{\dagger}(\textbf{k})c_{2}(\textbf{k})\right\}+h.c.\Bigg],

(5)

where the subscript on the fermion operators labels the site index within a unit cell (see Fig.
1
). Further,

\displaystyle\textbf{k}=k_{1}\thinspace\textbf{b}_{1}+k_{2}\thinspace\textbf{b}_{2}+k_{3}\thinspace\textbf{b}_{3},

(6)

with
k_{i}=2\pi m_{i},~m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

\displaystyle\delta_{j}=J_{x}+J_{y}e^{-ik_{j}},\mathrm{~for}~j=1,2.

(7)

Here

\displaystyle\textbf{b}_{1}=\dfrac{2\hat{\thinspace{{x}}}-\hat{z}}{4},~\textbf{b}_{2}=\dfrac{2\thinspace\hat{y}-\hat{z}}{4},\thinspace\textbf{b}_{3}=\dfrac{\hat{z}}{2}

(8)

form a basis for the reciprocal lattice. The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
5

III
Periodic driving

We drive
J_{z}
periodically, keeping
J_{x}=J_{y}=1
. Further, we focus on the regime where the driving amplitude is large compared to
J_{x}
and
J_{y}
, and choose the initial state as the initial Hamiltonian’s ground state.

The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
5
) conserves the fermion number. In the ground state, the two negative energy lower bands are filled. Thus, if the initial state is the ground state at
t=0
, the dynamics for a given
{\bf k}
will be restricted to the corresponding six-dimensional two-particle sector.

To study the response to the driving, we define the following quantities
Sasidharan and Surendran 2023
:

\displaystyle q({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=|\bra{\psi_{{\bf k}}(0)}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}(t)}|^{2},

(9)

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\bf k}q({\bf k},t),

(10)

\displaystyle\bar{q}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}q({\bf k},t)~dt,

(11)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{\bf k}\int_{0}^{T}dt~q({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt~\tilde{q}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bf k}\bar{q}({\bf k}),

(12)

where
\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(0)}
is the ground state of
H({\bf k})
at
t=0
and
N
is the number of unit cells. The quantity
Q
gives a measure of the extent of freezing; it is obtained by calculating the probability of the state with momentum
{\bf k}
to remain in the initial state at a later time
t
and then averaging over both
{\bf k}
and
t
, where the time-average is taken over a long duration
T
. The maximum value of
Q
is one, which would imply that the freezing is absolute and the system is stuck in the initial state throughout the evolution.

Numerical analysis

In an appropriately chosen basis, the two-particle Hamiltonian is given by (see Appendix
A

\displaystyle H_{2p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-J_{z}&\beta^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\beta_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
\beta_{\bf k}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&-\beta_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
-\beta^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\beta^{*}_{\bf k}\\
\\

(13)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bf k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-e^{ik_{3}}(1+e^{-ik_{1}})-(1+e^{ik_{2}})}{4},

(14)

\displaystyle\beta_{\bf k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{ie^{ik_{3}}(1+e^{-ik_{1}})-i(1+e^{ik_{2}})}{4}.

(15)

We now drive the system by making

is the state
[1~0~0~0~0~0]
. We numerically compute

, the probability of remaining in the initial state at time

, for various values of driving frequency
\omega
, keeping

In Fig.

and

. (Throughout this paper, we use units in which
\hbar=1

. In Fig.

The absence of freezing with zero bias also occurs in the case of bilayer graphene, where the driving parameter is a chemical potential
Sasidharan and Surendran 2023
. In that case, the lack of freezing is shown to be related to a ground state degeneracy in a frame rotating with the driving. This prompts us to analyze our problem in an appropriate rotating frame. Moreover, we can then apply the method of rotating wave approximation in the high-frequency limit and obtain analytic solutions.

III.2
Rotating wave approximation

In the Rotating Wave Approximation (RWA)
Ashhab et al. 2007
;
Sen et al. 2021
, we work in the interaction picture by transforming to a “rotating” frame and then take the high-frequency limit. Then the Hamiltonian becomes (see Appendix
B
for the details of the calculations in this section)

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{2p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

where
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=\beta_{\bf k}J_{0}\left(J/\omega\right)
, with
J_{0}\left(J/\omega\right)
being the zeroth order Bessel’s function of the first kind. As shown in Appendix
B
, we find that for all values of
{\bf k}
and
\omega
,
Q
satisfies the following bound in the thermodynamic limit:

\displaystyle\frac{5}{16}\leq Q\leq\frac{3}{8}.

(17)

Even though we have not explicitly calculated
Q
, our RWA analysis tightly bounds
Q
between 0.3125 and 0.375. However, the numerically computed values of
Q
(Fig.
2
a) are above the RWA upper bound for all values of
\omega
. As we argue below, this is not a failure of our RWA calculations, where the time averaging is done in the infinite-time limit, but a manifestation of the finite time over which the numerical averages are calculated.

III.2.1

III.3
Driving with bias

We have found that when
J_{z}
is driven as
J_{z}(t)=J\cos{\omega t}
, the system does not freeze under any driving condition, even though the dynamics slows down for certain combinations of
J
and
\omega
. When
J_{0}\left(J/\omega\right)=0
, the initial state becomes an eigenstate of the rotating wave Hamiltonian for all values of
{\bf k}
, which would indicate that the system freezes. However, at the same time the corresponding eigenvalue also becomes degenerate, which restores the dynamics, as we have described in Appendix
B
.

The degeneracy in the Hamiltonian can be lifted by adding a bias
J^{\prime}
to the driving
Sasidharan and Surendran 2023
:
J_{z}(t)=J^{\prime}+J\cos{\omega t}
. The rotating wave Hamiltonian in the two-particle sector then becomes

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{2p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&J^{\prime}&-\alpha_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&J^{\prime}&0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&J^{\prime}&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&J^{\prime}&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&2J^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(18)

where, as defined earlier,
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=J_{0}(J/\omega)\beta_{\bf k}
, and
\alpha_{\bf k}
and
\beta_{\bf k}
are given in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
). When
J_{0}(J/\omega)=0
, the initial state is still an eigenstate of
H^{\prime}_{2p}({\bf k})
, but, unlike in the bias-free case, this state no longer has a degenerate partner due to the presence of
J^{\prime}
. Therefore,
q({\bf k},t)=1
for all
{\bf k}
, and thus
Q=1
.

IV
Summary and discussion

In this paper, we have studied the response of the three-dimensional Kitaev model on a hyperhoneycomb lattice to strong and fast driving of one of the spin-couplings. When the driving has no bias, there is no dynamical freezing; the system oscillates among the initial ground state and other excited states, and, for a given driving amplitude, its response is nearly independent of the driving frequency. Our calculations based on a rotating wave approximation support these observations. The rotating wave analysis further shows that dynamical freezing is switched on by introducing a bias to the driving. In the presence of a bias, the system freezes almost absolutely for certain ratios between the amplitude and frequency of the driving. These ratios match well with the values predicted by the rotating wave analysis.

The switching of dynamical freezing is illustrated in Fig.
4
, which plots the degree of freezing
Q
against the bias parameter
J^{\prime}
, for a value of frequency at which system freezes for nonzero
J^{\prime}
. Such switching of dynamical freezing has been previously observed in the transverse-field
X
-
Y
model
Das and Moessner 2012
and in a tight-binding model of bilayer graphene. We also note that the two-dimensional Kitaev model undergoes dynamical freezing even under bias-free driving and, therefore, exhibits no switching. This is because the one-particle Hamiltonian for the 2D model corresponds to a two-level system and is analogous to the transverse-field Ising model.

We conclude with a discussion of the experimental implications of our model. As mentioned in the introduction,
\beta
-
\mathrm{Li_{2}IrO_{3}}
is considered to be a good candidate to realize the hyperhoneycomb lattice Kitaev Hamiltonian. However, for the driven Hamiltonian, a more plausible realization is via cold atoms on an optical lattice. For the honeycomb lattice Kitaev Hamiltonian, Duan, et. al.
Duan et al. 2003
have proposed a technique that exploits spin-dependent tunneling between neighboring atoms in an optical lattice to induce the anisotropic spin interactions.
This method could in principle be extended to realize the three-dimensional model as well. The tunneling amplitudes in an optical lattice can be made time-periodic by introducing an oscillating frequency difference between the two counter-propagating lasers that create the lattice potential in a given direction
Lignier et al. 2007
. In general, this will make all parameters in the effective spin-Hamiltonian time dependent. In this paper, we have considered the simplest scenario in which only one of the couplings (
J_{z}
) is driven while keeping the other two (
J_{x}
and
J_{y}
) fixed. Modeling the periodically driven optical lattice system will require the implementation of more complex driving protocols.

Appendix A
Two-particle Hamiltonian

To compute the two-particle Hamiltonian, first, we write down the matrix representation of the one-particle Hamiltonian. For each
{\bf k}
-value, we label the basis states using the occupation number
n_{i}({\bf k})
for each sublattice:
\ket{n_1({\bf k}), n_2({\bf k}), n_3({\bf k}), n_4({\bf k})}
.
Then the four basis states in the one-particle sector are

\displaystyle\ket{1}_{1p}=\ket{ 1 0 0 0},

\displaystyle~~~~~\ket{2}_{1p}=\ket{ 0 1 0 0},

\displaystyle\ket{3}_{1p}=\ket{ 0 0 1 0},

\displaystyle~~~~~\ket{4}_{1p}=\ket{ 0 0 0 1}.

Then,

\displaystyle H_{1p}({\bf k})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\dfrac{i}{2}J_{z}&0&\dfrac{i}{2}e^{ik_{3}}\delta_{1}\\
\\
\dfrac{-i}{2}J_{z}&0&\dfrac{-i}{2}\delta_{2}^{*}&0\\
\\
0&\dfrac{i}{2}\delta_{2}&0&\dfrac{i}{2}J_{z}\\
\\
-\dfrac{i}{2}e^{-ik_{3}}\delta_{1}^{*}&0&\dfrac{-i}{2}J_{z}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

After employing the unitary transformation

\displaystyle U=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-i&0&0\\
0&0&1&-i\\
-i&1&0&0\\
0&0&-i&1\end{bmatrix},

the time-dependent
J_{z}
-terms in the Hamiltonian become diagonal:

\displaystyle H_{1p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\dfrac{J_{z}}{2}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&\beta_{\bf k}\\
\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&-\dfrac{J_{z}}{2}&\beta_{\bf k}^{*}&0\\
0&\beta_{\bf k}&\dfrac{J_{z}}{2}&-\alpha_{\bf k}\\
\beta_{\bf k}^{*}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&\dfrac{J_{z}}{2}\end{bmatrix},

(19)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bf k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-e^{ik_{3}}(1+e^{-ik_{1}})-(1+e^{ik_{2}})}{4},

(20)

\displaystyle\beta_{\bf k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{ie^{ik_{3}}(1+e^{-ik_{1}})-i(1+e^{ik_{2}})}{4}.

(21)

Choosing the two-particle basis states as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lcl}\ket{1}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1}_{1p}\otimes\ket{2}_{1p}-\ket{2}_{1p}\otimes\ket{1}_{1p}\right)\\
\ket{2}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1}_{1p}\otimes\ket{3}_{1p}-\ket{3}_{1p}\otimes\ket{1}_{1p}\right)\\
\ket{3}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1}_{1p}\otimes\ket{4}_{1p}-\ket{4}_{1p}\otimes\ket{1}_{1p}\right)\\
\ket{4}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{2}_{1p}\otimes\ket{3}_{1p}-\ket{3}_{1p}\otimes\ket{2}_{1p}\right)\\
\ket{5}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{2}_{1p}\otimes\ket{4}_{1p}-\ket{4}_{1p}\otimes\ket{2}_{1p}\right)\\
\ket{6}_{2p}&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{3}_{1p}\otimes\ket{4}_{1p}-\ket{4}_{1p}\otimes\ket{3}_{1p}\right),\end{array}

we obtain the following two-particle Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{2p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-J_{z}&\beta^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\beta_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
\beta_{\bf k}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&-\beta_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
-\beta^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\beta^{*}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\beta^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\beta_{\bf k}&J_{z}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(28)

Appendix B
RWA calculation of
\bar{q}({\bf k})
for
J^{\prime}=0

To implement the rotating wave approximation, we first transform to a rotating frame by doing the following time-dependent unitary transformation in the one-particle subspace:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\exp[-i\left(\frac{J}{2\omega}\sin{(\omega t)}\right)(\sigma^{z}\otimes I)].

(29)

Then, the effective one-particle Hamiltonian that governs the dynamics in the rotating frame, given by
H_{1p}^{\prime}=UH_{1p}U^{\dagger}+i\partial_{t}UU^{\dagger}
, becomes

H^{\prime}_{1p}({\bf k})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&\beta_{\bf k}e^{-2i\theta}\\
\\
\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&0&\beta_{\bf k}^{*}e^{-2i\theta}&0\\
\\
0&\beta_{\bf k}e^{2i\theta}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}\\
\\
\beta_{\bf k}^{*}e^{2i\theta}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(30)

where
\theta=\left(J/2\omega\right)\sin{\omega t}
. Next we do an expansion of
\exp[i(J/\omega)\sin({\omega t})]
in terms of
e^{in\omega t}
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle\exp[i\left(\dfrac{J}{\omega}\right)\sin(\omega t)]=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}\left(\dfrac{J}{\omega}\right)e^{in\omega t},

(31)

where
J_{n}(J/\omega)
are the Bessel’s functions of the first kind.

For large
\omega
, the predominant term in the above sum is when
n=0
. In the rotating wave approximation (RWA), we neglect all faster-oscillating terms, which correspond to
n>0

Ashhab et al. 2007
. Then, the Hamiltonian becomes

H^{\prime}_{1p}({\bf k})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{*}&0\\
\\
0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}\\
\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{*}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}^{*}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(32)

where
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=\beta_{\bf k}J_{0}\left(J/\omega\right)
. The corresponding Hamiltonian in the two-particle sector is then

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{2p}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&0&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0\\
\\
-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

The sublattice symmetry is made manifest by interchanging indices 2 and 6:

\displaystyle H_{2p}^{\prime}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&0&0&0&\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&0&0&0&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha_{\bf k}\\
\\
0&0&0&0&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}\\
\\
-\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}&\alpha_{\bf k}&-\alpha_{\bf k}&0&0\\
\\
\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}&-\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

The eigenvalues of
H_{2p}({\bf k})
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0,\\
&\mu_{3}=\sqrt{2\left(|\alpha_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{2}-|\alpha_{\bf k}^{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{2}|\right)},\\
&\mu_{4}=-\sqrt{2\left(|\alpha_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{2}-|\alpha_{\bf k}^{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{2}|\right)},\\
&\mu_{5}=\sqrt{2\left(|\alpha_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\alpha_{\bf k}^{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{2}|\right)},\\
&\mu_{6}=-\sqrt{2\left(|\alpha_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{2}+|\alpha_{\bf k}^{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{2}|\right)},\end{split}

(35)

and the respective eigenvectors are,

\displaystyle\ket{\mu_1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},~\ket{\mu_2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\\
1\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\ket{\mu_3}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\begin{bmatrix}-(\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
{\alpha}_{\bf k}-{\alpha}_{\bf k}^{*}\eta\\
-({\alpha}_{\bf k}-{\alpha}_{\bf k}^{*}\eta)\\
\mu_{3}\\
\eta\mu_{3}\end{bmatrix},~\ket{\mu_4}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\begin{bmatrix}-(\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}-\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
{\alpha}_{\bf k}-{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
-({\alpha}_{\bf k}-{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
-\mu_{3}\\
-\eta\mu_{3}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\ket{\mu_5}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}-(\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}+\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}+\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
{\alpha}_{\bf k}+{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
-({\alpha}_{\bf k}+{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
\mu_{5}\\
-\eta\mu_{5}\end{bmatrix},~\ket{\mu_6}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}-(\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}+\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}+\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
{\alpha}_{\bf k}+{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta\\
-({\alpha}_{\bf k}+{\alpha}^{*}_{\bf k}\eta)\\
-\mu_{5}\\
\eta\mu_{5}\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
are normalization factors, and

\displaystyle\eta=\dfrac{|{\alpha}_{\bf k}^{2}+\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}^{2}|}{{\alpha}^{*2}_{\bf k}+\tilde{\beta}^{*2}_{\bf k}}.

Let
\ket{i}
denote the canonical basis vectors
(0,\dots~1,\dots~0)
, where the
i
-th element is 1 and the rest zero, and let

\displaystyle x_{i,j}=\frac{\bra{i}\ket{\mu_j}}{\bra{\mu_j}\ket{\mu_j}^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

The initial state
\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(0)}=\ket{1}
, therefore,

\displaystyle{\bra{\psi_{\bf k}(0)}\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}|x_{1,j}|^{2}e^{-i\mu_{j}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+2|x_{1,3}|^{2}\cos{\mu_3 t}+2|x_{1,5}|^{2}\cos{\mu_5 t},

since
|x_{1,4}|^{2}=|x_{1,3}|^{2}
,
|x_{1,6}|^{2}=|x_{1,5}|^{2}
,
\mu_{4}=-\mu_{3}
, and
\mu_{6}=-\mu_{5}
.
Normalization of
\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(t)}
implies that

\displaystyle|x_{1,3}|^{2}+|x_{1,5}|^{2}=1/4.

(36)

Let
|x_{1,3}|^{2}=u_{\bf k}
. Then,

\displaystyle{\bra{\psi_{\bf k}(0)}\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(t)}}=\frac{1}{2}+2u_{\bf k}\cos{\mu_3 t}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-2u_{\bf k}\right)\cos{\mu_5 t},

(37)

where

\displaystyle u_{\bf k}=\dfrac{2|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{2}|{{\alpha}_{\bf k}}^{2}+{\tilde{\beta}}_{\bf k}^{2}|-\left(2|\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}|^{4}+{{\alpha}^{*2}_{\bf k}}{\tilde{\beta}}_{\bf k}^{2}+{{\alpha}_{\bf k}}^{2}{{\tilde{\beta}}^{*2}_{\bf k}}\right)}{8|{{\alpha}_{\bf k}}^{2}+{\tilde{\beta}}_{\bf k}^{2}|\left(|{\alpha}_{\bf k}|^{2}+|{\tilde{\beta}}_{\bf k}|^{2}-|{\alpha}_{\bf k}^{2}+{\tilde{\beta}}_{\bf k}^{2}|\right)}.

(38)

Evaluating
q({\bf k},t)
, we get

\displaystyle q({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=|\bra{\psi_{{\bf k}}(0)}\ket{\psi_{{\bf k}}(t)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}+2u_{\bf k}\cos{\mu_3 t}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-2u_{\bf k}\right)\cos{\mu_5 t}\Bigg]^{2}.

(39)

Next, we calculate
\bar{q}({\bf k})
, the time-average of
q({\bf k},t)
.
q({\bf k},t)
contains four frequencies:
\mu_{3},\mu_{5},(\mu_{3}+\mu_{5})
, and
(\mu_{3}-\mu_{5})
. When none of these frequencies is zero, i.e.,
\mu_{3}\neq 0,\mu_{5}\neq 0
, and
\mu_{3}\neq\mu_{5}
, and as
T\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\bar{q}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt~\left|{}_{2p}{\bra{\psi_{\bf k}(0)}\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(t)}}_{2p}\right|^{2},

\displaystyle=\dfrac{3}{8}+4u_{\bf k}^{2}-u_{\bf k}.

(40)

Normalization of
\ket{\psi_{\bf k}(t)}_{2p}
[Eq. (
36
)] implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq u_{\bf k}\leq 1/4.

(41)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{5}{16}\leq\bar{q}({\bf k})\leq\frac{3}{8}.

(42)

We now consider the cases where some frequency in
q({\bf k},t)
is zero. There are three possibilities:
a)

\mu_{3}=\mu_{5}\neq 0
,
b)

\mu_{3}=0
, and
c)

\mu_{5}=0
.

\mu_{3}=\mu_{5}\neq 0
implies that either
k_{1}=\pi
or
k_{2}=\pi
, but not both. Then,

\displaystyle q({\bf k},t)=\frac{1}{4}(1+\cos\mu_{3}t)^{2},

and

\displaystyle\bar{q}({\bf k})=\frac{3}{8},

which coincides with the upper bound for the generic case given by Inequality (
42
).

\mu_{3}=0
implies that

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bf k}\tilde{\beta}^{*}_{\bf k}=\alpha^{*}_{\bf k}\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}.

(43)

The above condition can be satisfied in two ways: 1)
k_{1}=k_{2}
, or
k_{1}+k_{2}=2\pi
for arbitrary
J
and
\omega
, and 2)
J_{0}(J/\omega)=0
(and, consequently,
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=0
), for all values of
{\bf k}
. In the first case,

\displaystyle q({\bf k},t)=\Bigg[\frac{1}{2}+2u_{\bf k}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-2u_{\bf k}\right)\cos{\mu_5 t}\Bigg]^{2},

(44)

and

\displaystyle\bar{q}({\bf k})=\dfrac{3}{8}+u_{\bf k}+6u_{\bf k}^{2}.

(45)

From the bound on
u_{\bf k}
given by the Inequality (
41
), it immediately follows that
3/8\leq\bar{q}({\bf k})\leq 1
, which lies above the upper bound in the generic case. However, since
k_{1}=k_{2}
or
k_{1}+k_{2}=2\pi
when
\mu_{3}=0
, the number of modes satisfying this condition is
O(N^{\frac{2}{3}})
, therefore, their contribution to
Q
can be neglected in the thermodynamic limit.

In the second scenario, Eq. (
43
) is satisfied via
J_{0}(J/\omega)=0
independent of
{\bf k}
. Then, the initial state
[1~0~0~0~0~0]
becomes an eigenstate of the
H^{\prime}_{2p}({\bf k})
[Eq. (
33
)] for all
{\bf k}
, which would have implied that the state is stationary and therefore
\bar{q}({\bf k})=1
. However, when
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=0
, the initial state becomes degenerate with the state
[0~0~0~0~0~1]
. Then, for each
{\bf k}
, the system will fully oscillate between these two states for arbitrarily small values of
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}
.

Therefore, to obtain the correct physical values, we need to take the limit
\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\rightarrow 0
of the general expression for
\bar{q}({\bf k})
[Eq. (
40
)]. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}\rightarrow 0}\bar{q}({\bf k})=\frac{3}{8}.

(46)

\mu_{5}=0
implies
\alpha_{\bf k}=\tilde{\beta}_{\bf k}=0
, and then
\mu_{3}
is also zero. Then the rotating wave Hamiltonian vanishes, and there is no dynamics:
q({\bf k},t)=1
. This occurs when
k_{1}=k_{2}=\pi
, and the number of modes satisfying this condition is
\sim N^{\frac{1}{3}}
.

To summarize: for generic driving conditions,
5/16\leq\bar{q}({\bf k})\leq 3/8
, for all values of
{\bf k}
except those satisfying the condition
k_{1}+k_{2}=2\pi
(the number of such
{\bf k}
-values
\sim N^{\frac{2}{3}}
); when the driving parameters are such that
J_{0}(J/\omega)=0
, then
\bar{q}({\bf k})=3/8
, for all values of
{\bf k}
. Since
Q
is the average of
\bar{q}({\bf k})
over
{\bf k}
, we conclude that, under all driving conditions, and as
N\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{5}{16}\leq Q\leq\frac{3}{8}.

(47)
