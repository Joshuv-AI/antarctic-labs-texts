Statistical properties of the gravitational force in a random homogeneous medium
Constantin Payerne

Abstract
We discuss the statistical distribution of the gravitational (Newtonian) force exerted on a test particle in a infinite random and homogeneous gas of non-correlated particles. The exact solution is known as the Holtsmark distribution at the limit of infinite system corresponding to the number of particle N N within the volume and the volume going to infinity. The statistical behaviour of the gravitational force for scale comparable to the inter-distance particle can be analyzed through the combination of the n n -th nearest neighbor particle contribution to the total gravitational force, which can be derived from the joint probability density of location for a set of N N particles. We investigate two independent approaches to derive the joint probability density of location for a set of N N neighbors using integral forms and order statistics to give a general expression of such probability distribution with generalised dimension of space. We found that the non-finite dispersion of the Holtsmark distribution is due to the single contribution of the first nearest neighbor in the total gravitational force.



I
Introduction

The knowledge of the evolution and the statistical properties of the gravitational field in a infinite self-gravitating system with Poisson-like spatial distribution of masses (stars, galaxies, …) presents an interesting and widely investigated subject of research. The basic mechanism for the emergence of coherent structure in gravitational
N
-body system with long-range interacting sources from an initial spatial and mass distribution, and subject to small fluctuations of density still presents open problems in stellar dynamics, in cosmological
N
-body simulations.

In section
II
, we review the two-point statistics of a random density field in a homogeneous-universe. We also recall the one-point statistics of a Poisson-like random field, which is the case we are study in this paper. In section
III
, we recall the derivation of the probability density function of the total gravitational force exerted on an arbitrary particle within a homogeneous isotropic gas of non-correlated particles, known as the Holtsmark distribution. Additional information can be obtained by considering order statistics such as the contribution of a single
n
-th nearest neighbor particle to the total gravitational force exerted on a test particle. In section
IV
, we recall the properties of the first nearest neighbor spatial probability density function. The nearest neighbor statistical properties is related to the two point auto-correlation function, which has an important status in astrophysics and cosmology. To evaluate each neighbor’s contribution, it requires to get a general expression of the joint probability density function to find the set of
N
nearest neighbors at respective locations with general dimension of space
d
, from which the corresponding distribution of force can be simply derived. To do so, in section
V
, we generalize to the
n
-th nearest neighbor spatial probability density function. From the results obtained in section
V
, we study in section
VI
the probability density function of the gravitational force exerted by each of the nearest neighbor and derive the dispersion of the total gravitational force in terms of single contributions. We conclude in section
VII
.

II
Density as a stochastic variable

We consider a
d
-volume
v_{d}
filled with
N
particles.
The local density can be assimilated to a random variable with specific probability distribution. We note the density at location
x
as the random stochastic variable
\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})
given by the number of particle within the elementary volume
\mbox{d}V=\mbox{d}x_{1}\times...\times\mbox{d}x_{d}
. When each particle with index
i
is at location
\textbf{x}_{i}
, the stochastic density can be expressed by the sum

\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{i}),

(1)

where
\delta^{(d)}
is the Dirac delta distribution in dimension
d
.
The local average density
\rho(\textbf{x})
is simply given by the ensemble average
\langle\cdot\rangle
over all the configurations of particles in the volume
v_{d}
such as

\rho(\textbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\langle\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{i})\rangle.

(2)

Giving
f(\textbf{x}_{i})
the probability density function for finding the particle denoted by
i
within the elementary volume
\mbox{d}V
around
\textbf{x}_{i}
, the ensemble average is given by

\langle\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{i})\rangle=\int_{v_{d}}\mbox{d}^{d}\textbf{x}_{i}f(\textbf{x}_{i})\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{i}).

(3)

When particles are non-correlated, the probability for occupying the volume
\mbox{d}V
does not depend on position. Then
f(\textbf{x}_{i})
is a constant over space, and the ensemble average of the Dirac distribution results in
\langle\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}_{i})\rangle=1/v_{d}
. The local average density is deduced by the sum over the
N
indexes
i
giving
\rho_{0}(\textbf{x})=\rho_{0}=N/v_{d}
.

II.1
Auto-correlation function

we define the reduced correlation function
\xi
as the ensemble average
(
Sylos Labini & Pietronero 2008
)

\xi(r)=\langle\delta(\textbf{x}_{1})\delta(\textbf{x}_{2})\rangle,

(4)

where
r=|\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{2}|
and
\delta(\textbf{x})=(\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})-\rho_{0})/\rho_{0}
is the reduced density perturbation over space. the radial dependence
\xi(\textbf{x})=\xi(r)
is due to the assumption of homogeneous isotropic gas of particles. By using the definition of the local density in Eq. (
1
) and the joint probability density in Eq. (
21
), the ensemble average
\langle\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x}_{1})\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x}_{2})\rangle
can be expressed after development

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x}_{1})\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x}_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}\langle\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{i})\delta^{(d)}(\textbf{x}_{2}-\textbf{x}_{j})\rangle

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}f_{i,i}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2})+\sum\limits_{i\neq j=1}^{N}f_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2}),

(6)

where
f_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2})
is the joint probability density function to find respectively the
i-
th and the
j-
th particle at location
\textbf{x}_{1}
and
\textbf{x}_{2}
.
In the case of a random homogeneous media, the marginal density
f_{i}(\textbf{x})=1/v_{d}
. Then, without loss of generality we can write

f_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2})=\frac{1}{v_{d}}w_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1}|\textbf{x}_{2}),

(7)

where
w_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1}|\textbf{x}_{2})
is the conditional probability to find the
i-
th particle at location
\textbf{x}_{1}
knowing that the
j-
th particle is at location
\textbf{x}_{2}
. For homogeneous and isotropic gas, probability density function
f_{i,j}
does not depend on indexes
i
and
j
when
i\neq j
, then we have

w_{i,j}(\textbf{x}_{1}|\textbf{x}_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\delta^{(d)}(|\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{2}|)&\mbox{if }\ i=j\\
w(|\textbf{x}_{1}-\textbf{x}_{2}|)&\mbox{if }\ i\neq j\end{array}\right.

(8)

Then we can derive

\displaystyle\langle\rho(\textbf{x}_{1})\rho(\textbf{x}_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\delta^{(d)}(r)+\frac{N(N-1)}{v_{d}}w(r)

(9)

\displaystyle\approx\rho_{0}\delta^{(d)}(r)+N\rho_{0}w(r).

(10)

The function
w
is the probability density to find a given particle within an elementary volume at a distance
r
and verifies the normalization

\int_{v_{d}}\mbox{d}^{d}\textbf{x}w(|\textbf{x}|)=1.

(11)

The correlation function
\xi(r)
expresses as

\xi(r)=\frac{\delta^{(d)}(r)}{\rho_{0}}+\frac{N}{\rho_{0}}w(r)-1.

(12)

Then, the
w
function reaches the expression in term of
\xi
giving

w(r)=\frac{1}{v_{d}}[1+\xi(r)].

(13)

The function
\xi
appears as a probability excess. It characterises a deviation to the probability density function
1/v_{d}
and following this convention, we have three possible cases,

\xi(r):\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&\mbox{particles tend to attract each other}\\
=0&\mbox{particles are uncorrelated}\\
<0&\mbox{particles tend to repel each other}\end{array}\right.

(14)

and the
\xi
function verifies the integration

\frac{1}{v_{d}}\int_{v_{d}}\xi(r)\mbox{d}^{d}\textbf{x}=0.

(15)

We now get the probability
w(r)
to find a given particle at distance
r
knowing a particle is at location
r=0
. The probability density function
G
to find a particle at location
r
regardless is index
i
is the sum over all the particles in the gas of
w_{i}
(excepted for the center particle), such that

G(r)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}w_{i}(r)\approx\rho_{0}[1+\xi(r)]

(16)

II.2
The Poisson point process

The probability
q
for a given particle for being in a elementary volume
\mbox{d}V
within the total volume
v_{d}
is
q=\mbox{d}V/v_{d}
. We consider the random variable
\mathcal{N}
as the number of particles within the elementary volume
\mbox{d}V
. When particles are non-correlated (independent),
\mathcal{N}
follows a binomial law with parameter
q
given by

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}=n)=\frac{N!}{n!(N-n)!}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}.

(17)

The Poisson limit Theorem is valid when the total number of particles
N\gg n
and the probability
q\ll 1
. We consider the number of particle
N
and the volume
v_{d}
going to infinity, as the density
\rho_{0}
keeps constant. Noting the average of the binomial law as
\lambda=Nq=\rho_{0}\mbox{d}V
, and verifying
\lambda\ll 1
, the Poisson limit theorem gives the distribution

\lim_{N,v_{d}\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}=n)=\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\exp[-\lambda]\approx\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}(1-\lambda),

(18)

with
\mathbb{P}(0)
and
\mathbb{P}(1)
much higher than
\mathbb{P}(n>1)
which is proportional to
(\rho_{0}\mbox{d}V)^{n}
. The stochastic local density
\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})
verifies the probability distribution

\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{1}{\mbox{d}V}&\mbox{with probability}\ \rho_{0}\mbox{d}V\\
0&\mbox{with probability}\ 1-\rho_{0}\mbox{d}V\end{array}\right.

Either the volume is occupied by a particle with probability
\rho_{0}\mbox{d}V
or it is empty with the complementary probability
1-\rho_{0}\mbox{d}V
. The average gives the density
\langle\hat{\rho}(\textbf{x})\rangle=\rho_{0}
.

III
The Holtsmark distribution

In dimension
d=3
, we note
v_{3}=V
. We consider a test particle of mass
m
located at
x
in a gas of identical particles of mass
m
denoted by
i
. The total gravitational force
F
exerted on the test particle by its neighborhood can be obtained by the vectorial sum over all the particle contributions at respective location
\textbf{x}_{i}
and is given by

\textbf{F}(\textbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Gm^{2}}{|\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{x}|^{3}}(\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\textbf{F}_{i},

(19)

with the gravitational constant
G=6,674\times 10^{-11}\ \mbox{m}^{3}.\mbox{kg}^{-1}.\mbox{s}^{-2}
. We can give a general expression to the probability density function
W(\textbf{F})
to find the gravitational force exerted on the test particle comprised in a small volume
\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{F}
around
F
due to all the
N
particles in a sphere centered on it. Such probability can be expressed as
(
Pietronero et al. 2002
)

W(\textbf{F})=\int_{V}...\int_{V}\mbox{d}\textbf{x}^{3N}f(\textbf{x}_{1},...,\textbf{x}_{N})\delta\left(\textbf{F}-\sum\limits_{i}^{N}\textbf{F}_{i}\right),

(20)

where
f(\textbf{x}_{1},...,\textbf{x}_{N})
is the joint probability density function to find the
N
particles at location
\textbf{x}_{1},...,\textbf{x}_{N}
.

We present the steps to reach the expression of the probability density function of force
F
for non-correlated particles with equal mass
m
. In this case, the joint probability function
f
is the product of the constant probability density function for each particle given by
1/V
. The probability that the
n
particle occupies the volume
d\textbf{x}^{3N}
is given by

f(\textbf{x}_{1},...,\textbf{x}_{N})\mbox{d}\textbf{x}^{3N}=\frac{\mbox{d}\textbf{x}^{3N}}{V^{N}}.

(21)

The distribution of force is then given by the integral over
N
volumes

W_{H}(\textbf{F})=\int_{V}...\int_{V}\left[\frac{\mbox{d}\textbf{x}^{3N}}{V^{N}}\right]\delta\left(\textbf{F}-\sum\limits_{i}^{N}\textbf{F}_{i}\right).

(22)

By giving the expression of
W_{H}
as a inverse Fourier transform, we get

W_{H}(\textbf{F})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\Omega(\textbf{k})}\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{k}A(\textbf{k})\exp(-i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}),

(23)

where
A(\textbf{k})
is given by

\displaystyle A(\textbf{k})

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{V}\frac{\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{x}}{V}\exp(i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}_{i}(\textbf{x}))\right]^{N}

(24)

\displaystyle=\left[1-\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}V}\int_{V}\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{x}[1-\exp(i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}_{i}(\textbf{x}))]\right]^{\rho_{0}V}.

(25)

By using the definition of the exponential function,
for infinite volume
v_{d}
, infinite number of particle
N
, and constant density
\rho_{0}
, we get

A(\textbf{k})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left[1-\frac{\rho_{0}C(\textbf{k})}{N}\right]^{N}=\exp(-\rho_{0}C(\textbf{k})),

(26)

with

C(\textbf{k})=\int_{V}\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{x}[1-\exp(i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}_{i}(\textbf{x}))].

(27)

By considering an arbitrary axis given by the vector
k
, integrating over all the directions gives

\displaystyle C(k)

\displaystyle=2\pi(kGm^{2})^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mbox{d}u}{u^{5/2}}\left[u-\sin(u)\right]

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{15}(2\pi kGm^{2})^{3/2}.

(29)

where we used Eq. (
151
) to identify

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mbox{d}u}{u^{5/2}}\left[u-\sin(u)\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(u)}{u^{3/2}}\mbox{d}u=\frac{4\sqrt{2\pi}}{15}.

(30)

The Holtsmark distribution is given by the integral

W_{H}(\textbf{F})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\Omega(\textbf{k})}\mbox{d}^{3}\textbf{k}\exp[-i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}-\frac{4\rho_{0}}{15}(2\pi kGm^{2})^{3/2}].

(31)

By considering, as before, an arbitrary axis oriented along
F
giving
\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{F}=kF\cos{\theta}
, integrating over the directions and radial variable
k
gives the expression

W_{H}(\textbf{F})=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}F^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}x(x\sin{x})\exp\left[-(x/\beta)^{3/2}\right]

(32)

where
\beta=F/F_{0}
and the typical force
F_{0}
defined as

F_{0}=2\pi\left(\frac{4}{15}\right)^{2/3}\frac{Gm^{2}}{\lambda^{2}},

(33)

with
\lambda=\rho_{0}^{-1/3}
the length measure associated to the average inter-particle separation.
The Holtsmark distribution (e.g.
Chandrasekhar 1943
,
Sylos Labini & Pietronero 2008
,
Chavanis 2009
) for the modulus
F=|\textbf{F}|
(represented in Figure
2
) is given by integrating overall the directions

W_{H}(F)=\frac{2}{\pi F}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}x(x\sin{x})\exp[-(x/\beta)^{3/2}].

(34)

The average force is given by the integral

\langle F\rangle=F_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta W_{H}(\beta)\mbox{d}\beta=\frac{4F_{0}}{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\approx 3.412F_{0}.

(35)

The dispersion of the Holtsmark distribution is not finished.
We can investigate two specific regimes; for
F\rightarrow 0
, it is required to develop
W
in series by using the the Taylor expansion of
\sin(x)
in Eq. (
149
) giving

W_{H}(F)=\frac{2}{\pi\beta F_{0}}\sum\limits_{n=0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}\int^{\infty}_{0}x^{2n+1}\exp[-(x/\beta)^{3/2}]\mbox{d}x.

(36)

Recognizing the Gamma-Euler function in Eq. (
146
), the sum becomes

W_{H}(F)=\frac{4}{3\pi F_{0}}\sum\limits_{n=0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)!}\Gamma\left(\frac{2n+3}{3/2}\right)\beta^{2n+2}.

(37)

The first two term of the sum are reported in the case of the regime
\beta\rightarrow 0
, giving

W_{H}(F)\approx\frac{4}{3\pi F_{0}}\beta^{2}-\frac{2}{9\pi F_{0}}\Gamma\left(\frac{10}{3}\right)\beta^{4}.

(38)

For
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, we now use the Taylor expansion of the exponential function in Eq. (
150
) giving

W_{H}(F)=\frac{2}{\pi F_{0}}\sum\limits_{n=0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\beta^{-(3n/2+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{3n/2+1}\sin x\mbox{d}x,

(39)

giving the explicit formula for
W_{H}(F)
using Eq. (
151
)

\displaystyle W_{H}(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi F_{0}}\sum\limits_{n=0}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n!}\sin\left(\frac{3\pi n}{4}\right)\times

(40)

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{3n}{2}+2\right)\beta^{-(3n/2+1)}.

(41)

Reporting the two first non-zero terms for large
F
, we get

W_{H}(F)\approx\frac{15}{8F_{0}}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}\beta^{-5/2}-\frac{24}{\pi F_{0}}\beta^{-4}.

(42)

IV
First nearest neighbor density function

We investigate the statistical properties of the first nearest neighbor of a random particle in a gas with Poisson-like distribution of particles in general dimension
d
. We first consider a homogeneous and isotropic gas of particle, and then we treat the Poisson-like case.
The probability density function
w_{1}(r)
to find the nearest neighbor particle at a distance comprises between
r
and
r+dr
from an arbitrary particle is given by the ensemble average (
Chandrasekhar 1943
,
Mazur 1992
)

w_{1}(r)=\left\langle\sum\limits_{j=2}^{N}\delta(r-r_{j})\prod\limits^{N}_{k\neq(i,j)}\left[1-\int_{0}^{r}\delta(x-r_{k})\mbox{d}x\right]\right\rangle,

(43)

where
r_{j}
is the distance of the
j
-th particle around the center particle. The nearest neighbor density function takes the form

w_{1}(r)=\left[1-\int_{0}^{r}w_{1}(u)\mbox{d}u\right]G(r)\alpha_{d}r^{d-1}.

(44)

The general solution of function defined such that

g(x)=f(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}g(u)\mbox{d}u,

(45)

with
K
an appropriated constant. We then get

g(x)=Kf(x)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}f(u)\mbox{d}u\right).

(46)

We define
N(r)=\rho_{0}\alpha_{d}r^{d}/d=(r/\lambda_{d})^{d}
the average number of particles within the
d
-volume or radius
r
with the typical measure of length
\lambda_{d}
given by

\lambda_{d}=\left[\frac{d}{\rho_{0}\alpha_{d}}\right]^{1/d}.

(47)

We can write the density
w_{1}(r)
after normalization

w_{1}(r)=N^{\prime}(r)(1+\xi(r))\mbox{e}^{-N(r)+\int_{0}^{r}N^{\prime}(x)\xi(x)\mbox{d}x}.

(48)

The assumption of a Poisson distribution of particle means particles are uncorrelated, such that the auto-correlation function gets
\xi(r)=0
if
r\neq 0
. We can write the density
w_{1}(r)
as

w_{1}(r)=N^{\prime}(r)\exp[-N(r)]=(1-\exp[-N(r)])^{\prime}.

(49)

In dimension
d=3
, we get naturally

w_{1}(r)=4\pi\rho_{0}r^{2}\exp\left[-\frac{4\pi\rho_{0}r^{3}}{3}\right].

(50)

Giving the average distance
\langle r\rangle_{1,d}
of the nearest neighbor particle as the integral

\displaystyle\langle r\rangle_{1,d}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{1}(r)r\mbox{d}r=\lambda_{d}\Gamma\left(\frac{d+1}{d}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle\approx 0.554\rho_{0}^{-1/3}\ (\mbox{for $d=3$}),

(52)

and the average square distance
\langle r^{2}\rangle_{1,d}

\displaystyle\langle r^{2}\rangle_{1,d}

\displaystyle=[\lambda_{d}]^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d+2}{d}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\approx 0.347\rho_{0}^{-2/3}\ (\mbox{for $d=3$}).

(54)

The dispersion
\sigma_{1,d}
is obtained by combining previous results, such as

\displaystyle\sigma_{1,d}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{d+2}{2}\right)-\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{d+1}{d}\right)\right]^{1/2}

(55)

\displaystyle\approx 0.202\rho_{0}^{-1/3}\ (\mbox{for $d=3$}).

(56)

As an example, We detail in appendix
A
the derivation of the mean inter-distance particle when considering non-zero auto-correlation function, describing the statistical properties of un-correlated particles but with finite size with given radius
a
.

V
The
n>1
nearest neighbor particles

We now consider a set of
N
particles denoted by respective distance
r_{i}
from a particle at
r=0
.
The joint probability density function of finding the
N
neighbors at distance
r_{1},...,r_{N}
must fulfill the condition

w(r_{1},...,r_{N})=\prod_{i>j}^{N}\Theta(r_{i}-r_{j})w(r_{1},...,r_{N}),

(57)

where the normalization can be written as

1=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}r_{1}...\int_{r_{N-2}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}r_{N-1}\int_{r_{N-1}}^{\infty}\mbox{d}r_{N}w(r_{1},...,r_{N}).

(58)

We give the final result before developing the mathematical aspects

w(r_{1},...,r_{N})=\prod_{i=N}^{1}N^{\prime}(r_{i})\mbox{e}^{-N(r_{N})}.

(59)

First, we try to determine the joint probability density function
w(r_{1},r_{2})
for finding the first neighbor at a distance
r_{1}
and the second nearest neighbor at
r_{2}
. It is necessary that the density verifies
w(r_{1},r_{2})=0\ \mbox{if}\ r_{1}>r_{2}
.
The joint probability density function
w(r_{1},r_{2})
can be written in general terms using conditional probability (using the Bayes theorem)

\displaystyle w(r_{1},r_{2})

\displaystyle=w_{1}(r_{1})g_{2}(r_{2}|r_{1})

(60)

\displaystyle=w_{2}(r_{2})g_{1}(r_{1}|r_{2}),

(61)

where the
g(a|b)
are the conditional probability density function of the occurrence of event
a
assuming event
b
to occur. We can write an equivalent expression, for the conditional probability
g_{2}(r_{2}|r_{1})
to find the second nearest neighbor at
r_{2}
assuming the first nearest neighbor is at
r_{1}
, verifying the integral equation of the same form as in Eq. (
45
) giving

g_{2}(r_{2}|r_{1})=\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})\int_{r_{2}}^{\infty}g(x|r_{1})\mbox{d}x,

(62)

verifying the general solution given by (
46
)

g_{2}(r_{2}|r_{1})=\mu(r_{1})\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})\mbox{e}^{-\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\varphi(r_{1},x)\mbox{d}x}.

(63)

Here,
\mu
is a function of the variable
r_{1}
. The normalization for the conditional probability imposes that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{1}}^{\infty}g_{2}(y|r_{1})\mbox{d}y=-\mu(r_{1})\left[\mbox{e}^{-\int_{r_{1}}^{y}\varphi(r_{1},x)\mbox{d}x}\right]^{\infty}_{r1}

(64)

\displaystyle=\mu(r_{1})(1-\exp[-I(r_{1})]),

(65)

where
I(r_{1})
is the integral

I(r_{1})=\int_{r_{1}}^{\infty}\varphi(r_{1},x)\mbox{d}x

(66)

We can rewrite the joint probability density function
w(r_{1},r_{2})
in terms of the function
\varphi

w(r_{1},r_{2})=\frac{w_{1}(r_{1})}{1-\exp[-I(r_{1})]}\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})\mbox{e}^{-\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\varphi(r_{1},x)\mbox{d}x}.

(67)

We note that
\varphi
has the dimension of a density for a single variable (
\sim L^{-1}
). We can express the
\varphi
function in terms of the dimensionless quantity
\phi(r_{1},r_{2})>0
by using the probability
N^{\prime}(r_{2})
to find a particle at
r_{2}
regardless its index

\varphi(r_{1},r_{2})=N^{\prime}(r_{2})\phi(r_{1},r_{2}).

(68)

This expression leads to the following system; knowing that
f
verifies
f(0)=0
and admits first derivative it is possible to find another function
\nu
verifying

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y}f^{\prime}(x)\mbox{d}x,

(69)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y}\nu(x,y)\mbox{d}x.

(70)

We note that this situation happens when we express the marginal density
w_{2}
in the two different ways

\displaystyle w_{2}(r_{2})

VII
Conclusions

The Holtsmark distribution provides group effect information on the statistics of the gravitational force in a random homogeneous gas with Poisson-like spatial distribution of point particles. We discussed the statistical properties of location in a
d
-dimension space of constant density
\rho_{0}
of a set of
N
particles considering the neighborhood of a arbitrary center particle. We generalized the joint probability density function for finding the
N
nearest neighbors at respective location. Side-by-side particle joint probability density function of location validates that the radial coordinates of 2 nearest neighbor with successive index
n
are strongly correlated when
n
is high. In dimension
d=3
, we analyzed the
n
-th contribution to the total gravitational force exerted on a test particle. We noticed that the non-finite dispersion of the Holtsmark distribution is due to the non-finite dispersion of the distribution of force exerted by the first nearest neighbor. We may pursue the study of the gravitational field by using the joint probability distribution for any number of neighbor to investigate the instabilities in a gas of gravitational-bounded particles with homogeneous initial spatial distribution, leading to the emergence of coherent structures (filament). The influence of a continuous mass distribution within the gas may be implemented to the study of statistics of gravitation.

p(s)
and ordered in a list. We define the probability density function
f_{n}
that verifies the equality

\mbox{P}_{n}(\mathcal{X}\in[x,x+\mbox{d}x[)=f_{n}(x)\mbox{d}x.

(134)

We can write the general expression for
f_{n}(x)

\displaystyle f_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=Kp(x)\left[\int_{0}^{x}p(s)\mbox{d}s\right]^{n-1}\left[\int_{x}^{1}p(s)\mbox{d}s\right]^{N-n}

(135)

\displaystyle=Kdx^{dn-1}[1-x^{d}]^{N-n}.

(136)

With
K
an appropriated normalization constant. By using the integral in Eq. (
152
), we derive the final result

f_{n}(x)=[nC_{N}^{n}]dx^{dn-1}(1-x^{d})^{N-n}.

(137)

Now we investigate the change of variable
x\rightarrow r
to get the probability density function
w_{n}(r)
that must verify

f_{n}(x)\mbox{d}x=w_{n}(r)\mbox{d}r.

(138)

We can express a general form for
w_{n}
such as:

w_{n}(r)=KC_{N}^{n}n\frac{dr^{dn-1}}{R^{dn}}[1-(r/R)^{d}]^{N-n},

(139)

where
K
is a normalization constant associated to the change of space (change from a interval with finite borders
x\in[0,1]
to an interval with an infinite border
r\in[0,\infty[
). We note that the following expression depends on the two extensive properties of the system
R\ \mbox{and}\ N
. The limit of infinite system
N,R\rightarrow\infty
and
N/v_{d}=\rho_{0}
can be considered by developing
1-x\approx\exp[-x]
when
x=r/R\ll 1
, then

w_{n}(r)\approx KC_{N}^{n}n\frac{dr^{dn-1}}{R^{dn}}\exp[-(N-n)(r/R)^{d}].

(140)

For
n
small enough compared to
N
, the difference
N-n\approx N\gg 1
, then we get the exponent

(N-n)\left[\frac{r}{R}\right]^{d}\approx\left[\frac{R}{\lambda_{d}}\right]^{d}\left[\frac{r}{R}\right]^{d}=N(r).

(141)

The
w_{n}
density function can be written as

w_{n}(r)=\frac{Kd}{(n-1)!}\frac{dr^{dn-1}}{R^{dn}}\mbox{e}^{-N(r)}.

(142)

The normalization constant
K
is given by the integral of
w_{n}
from 0 to
\infty
, giving
K=(R/\lambda_{d})^{dn}
. The
w_{n}
function is given by the expression

\displaystyle w_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{(n-1)!}\left[\frac{r}{\lambda_{d}}\right]^{dn}\frac{1}{r}\mbox{e}^{-N(r)}

(143)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}N(r)^{n-1}N^{\prime}(r)\mbox{e}^{-N(r)}.

(144)

We retrieve the same result as before by considering statistical properties of ordered list elements distributed following the density
p(s)
.
