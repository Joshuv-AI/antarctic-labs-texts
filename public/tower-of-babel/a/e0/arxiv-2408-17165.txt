Efficient Testable Learning of General Halfspaces with Adversarial Label Noise
Ilias Diakonikolas, Daniel M. Kane, Sihan Liu, Nikos Zarifis

Abstract
We study the task of testable learning of general — not necessarily homogeneous — halfspaces with adversarial label noise with respect to the Gaussian distribution. In the testable learning framework, the goal is to develop a tester-learner such that if the data passes the tester, then one can trust the output of the robust learner on the data. Our main result is the first polynomial time tester-learner for general halfspaces that achieves dimension-independent misclassification error. At the heart of our approach is a new methodology to reduce testable learning of general halfspaces to testable learning of nearly homogeneous halfspaces that may be of broader interest.



Presented to COLT’24.

Introduction

A (general) halfspace or Linear Threshold Function (LTF) is any
Boolean function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{\pm 1\}
of the form

h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t\right)
,
where
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the defining vector,
t\in\mathbb{R}
is the threshold,
and
\mathrm{sign}:\mathbb{R}\to\{\pm 1\}
is defined as
\mathrm{sign}(t)=1
if
t\geq 0

and
\mathrm{sign}(t)=-1
otherwise.
The family of halfspaces is one of the most basic concept classes in computational
learning theory with history dating back to Rosenblatt’s perceptron algorithm
[
22
]
.
While halfspaces are efficiently learnable
in Valiant’s (realizable) distribution-free PAC model
[
25
]

(i.e., with clean labels), in the agnostic (or adversarial label noise) model
[
18
,
21
]
even
weak
learning is intractable
[
3
,
7
,
24
]
, unless one makes assumptions on the distribution of feature vectors.

A long line of work, starting with
[
19
]
,
has developed efficient halfspace learners in the distribution-specific
agnostic model. Concretely,
[
19
]
gave an agnostic learner
within 0-1 error of
\mathrm{opt}+\epsilon
—
where
\mathrm{opt}
is the 0-1 error of the best-fitting halfspace —
with complexity
d^{O(1/\epsilon^{2})}
if the distribution on feature vectors
is the standard Gaussian. Unfortunately, if one insists on optimal error of
\mathrm{opt}+\epsilon
,
computational limitations arise even in the Gaussian setting. Specifically, the
exponential complexity dependence on
1/\epsilon
has been recently shown to be
inherent
[
13
,
14
,
8
,
9
]
.

By relaxing the desired accuracy to
f(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
,
for an appropriate function
f(t)
that goes to
0
when
t\rightarrow 0
,
it is possible to obtain
\mathrm{poly}(d/\epsilon)
(i.e.,
fully
polynomial)
time algorithms
[
20
,
1
,
2
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
Specifically, for the class of
homogeneous
halfspaces — corresponding to
the case that the threshold
t=0
or equivalently that the separating hyperplane goes through the origin —
[
1
]
first gave a
\mathrm{poly}(d/\epsilon)
time algorithm
with error
C\mathrm{opt}+\epsilon
, for some universal constant
C>1
, that succeeds
under the standard Gaussian (and, more generally, isotropic logconcave distributions).
Interestingly, the case of
general
halfspaces turns out to be more challenging:
with the exception of
[
10
,
12
]
, all prior approaches inherently fail for non-homogeneous halspaces. We will return to this point in the subsequent discussion.

The model of
testable learning

[
23
]
was defined
to alleviate the following conceptual limitation of
distribution-specific agnostic learning:
if the assumptions on the marginal distribution
on examples (e.g., Gaussianity or log-concavity) are not satisfied, a vanilla
agnostic learner provides no guarantees.
Ideally, one would like to guarantee the following desiderata:
(a) if the learner accepts, then we can trust its output,
and (b) it is unlikely that the learner rejects if the data satisfies the distributional assumptions. This has led to the following definition:

Definition 1.1

(Testable Learning with Adversarial Label Noise
[
23
]
)
.

Fix
\epsilon,\tau\in(0,1]
and let
f:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
A tester-learner
\cal{A}
(approximately) testably learns a concept class

\mathcal{C}
with respect to the distribution
D_{\mathbf{x}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

with
N
samples, and failure probability
\tau

if the following holds.
For any distribution
D
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
,
the tester-learner
\cal A
draws a set
S
of
N
i.i.d. samples from
D
.
In the end, it either rejects
S
or accepts
S
and produces a hypothesis
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\{\pm 1\}
. Moreover, the following conditions must be met:

•

(Completeness) If
D
truly has marginal
D_{\mathbf{x}}
,
\cal{A}
accepts with probability at least
1-\tau
.

•

(Soundness)
The probability that
\cal A
accepts and outputs a hypothesis
h
for which
\pr_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[h(\mathbf{x})\neq y]>f(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
, where

\mathrm{opt}:=\min_{g\in\cal{C}}\pr_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[g(\mathbf{x})\neq y]
is at most
\tau
.

The probability in the above statements is over the randomness of the sample
S

and the internal randomness of the tester-learner
\cal A
.

Since the introduction of the model, algorithmic aspects of
testable learning have been investigated in a number of
works
[
23
,
15
,
6
,
16
,
17
]
. The earlier works
[
23
,
15
]
studied
the agnostic setting where
f(t)=t
(corresponding to error
\mathrm{opt}+\epsilon
).
These works developed general moment-matching methodology that
gives testable learners for general halfspaces (and other concept classes).
Since testable learning is at least as hard as the standard agnostic setting,
the resulting algorithms necessarily incur
exponential dependence in
1/\epsilon
.
Subsequent work
[
6
,
16
,
17
]

developed testable learners with weaker error guarantees that run in fully-polynomial
time. Specifically, these algorithms achieve error
O(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
under the Gaussian distribution, and more recently, for a subclass of log-concave distributions.
Importantly, all of these algorithms work for homogeneous halfspaces
and inherently fail for general halfspaces.

The distinction between homogeneous and general halfspaces might seem
inconsequential at first glance.
Indeed, one can trivially reduce a general halfspace to a homogeneous
one by adding an extra constant coordinate to each datapoint.
While this is a valid reduction for distribution-free learning,
it alters the marginal distribution on the feature vectors.
Therefore, it does not generally work in the distribution-specific setting
we study here. Beyond this, as we will elaborate in the proceeding section, the
techniques underlying previous testable learning algorithms are insufficient to obtain any non-trivial error guarantee for general halfspaces.

Motivated by this gap in our understanding,
in this work we ask whether it is possible to obtain a fully-polynomial time testable learner for general halfspaces with dimension-independent error.
Specifically, we study the following question:

Is there a
\mathrm{poly}(d/\epsilon)
time tester-learner for general halfspaces with error
f(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
?

As our main result, formally stated below, we provide an affirmative answer to this question.

Theorem 1.2

(Testable Learning General Halfspaces under Gaussian Marginals)
.

Let
\epsilon,\tau\in(0,1)
and
\mathcal{C}
be the class of general halfspaces on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
There exists a tester-learner
for
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
up to
0\text{-}1
error
\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}}\right)+\epsilon
, where
\mathrm{opt}
is the
0\text{-}1
error of the best fitting function in
\mathcal{C}
, that fails with probability at most
\tau
.
Furthermore, the algorithm draws
N=\mathrm{poly}(d,1/\epsilon)\log(1/\tau)
samples, and runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(N)
.

We reiterate that this is the first polynomial-time testable learner
for
general
halfspaces that achieves non-trivial (i.e., dimension-independent) error
guarantee. As already mentioned, prior works either focus on

homogeneous
halfspaces or incur super-polynomial runtime.

We leave open the questions of obtaining quantitatively better error guarantee (with
O(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
as the ideal goal) and extending to broader classes of distributions beyond the Gaussian. Either potential improvement stumbles upon non-trivial obstacles; see
Remark

3.1
and
3.2
.

1.1
Some natural attempts and why they fail

One may wonder whether previously known testable learners
for homogeneous halfspaces can be applied
(directly or with minor modifications) in our setting
to achieve non-trivial learning error, e.g.,
\mathrm{opt}^{c}
for some
c\in(0,1)
.
We will demonstrate below that known methods fail to achieve testable learning for
general halfspaces with even (sufficiently small) constant error.

Difficulty of Estimating Chow Parameters

For learning general halfspaces under the Gaussian distribution
with adversarial label noise,
the only prior works that achieve dimension-independent error are
[
10
]
and
[
12
]

(in the non-testable setting). In both of these works,
the algorithms are required at some point to estimate the
Chow-parameters, i.e.,
\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[y\mathbf{x}]
.
The intuition behind this choice is that for any halfspace
h(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t)
, we have that

\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[h(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}]=\mathbf{w}G(t)
,
where
G(t)
is the pdf of the standard normal. In the non-testable regime, this expectation is easily computable with
\mathrm{poly}(d/\epsilon)
samples and runtime. Unfortunately, in the testable regime, one needs to first certify that the marginal distribution
D_{\mathbf{x}}
is similar to a Gaussian in the sense that
\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim D_{\mathbf{x}}}[h(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}]\sim\mathbf{w}G(t)
for any halfspace
h
.
To the best of our knowledge, the only way to certify this property
is to certify that at least
\Omega(1/G(t))
moments
of the distribution
D_{\mathbf{x}}
match those
of the standard normal (see
[
15
]
),
which requires at least
d^{\Omega(1/G(t))}
samples and runtime. In other words, this approach would lead to exponential runtime
if
1/G(t)
is on the order of
\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon)
.

Adapting Testable Learners for Homogeneous Halfspaces

For testable learning of homogeneous halfspaces, two techniques have been used in prior work.
The first one uses an adaptive localization method
[
6
]
, and the second
is to construct non-convex SGD tester-learners
[
16
,
17
]
. At a high-level,
both of these techniques localize in a band
close to the origin
in order to maximize the error conditioned on the band of the current hypothesis and the optimal one. Unfortunately, for general halfspaces,
one cannot localize to maximize the error,

unless
the angle between the current hypothesis
(weight vector)
and the optimal one is sufficiently small.
This holds because if the angle is large,
then no band can contain large enough error,
except bands that are very far away (which due to the noise model can be arbitrarily noisy).
The only known method to obtain a good enough
initialization point is to rely on Chow-parameters —
but as we discussed above, this is hard in the testable regime.

1.2
Overview of Techniques

Reduction to Nearly Homogeneous Halfspaces

The overall strategy of our algorithmic approach is to efficiently reduce the testable learning of general halfspaces
to the testable learning of “nearly” homogeneous halfspaces, i.e., halfspaces with thresholds of size
\epsilon

1
1

1

Our main technical contribution is to show that we can efficiently construct a list of
\tilde{O}(1/\epsilon)
candidate learning instances one of which is guaranteed to correspond to a halfspace with a threshold of size at most
\epsilon
; and reduce testable learning of the unknown general halfspace to testable learning of these nearly homogeneous halfspaces.
.
Assuming such an efficient reduction,
our main theorem then follows via a careful analysis
showing that (essentially)
the main algorithm of the prior work by
[
6
]
(on testable learning of homogeneous
halfspaces)
can testably learn halfspaces with small biases.
The key notion in implementing such a reduction is what we refer to as
Good Localization Centers
(
Definition

2.1
).
In short, a good localization center is a point that is
close to the decision boundary of the target halfspace
and at the same time not too far from the origin. As an
example, the intersection between the line along the defining
vector of the target halfspace
and the
separating hyperplane of the halfspace will be a good localization center.

Then, leveraging the technique developed in
[
10
]
, one can localize to regions around
the good localization center via rejection sampling.
After such a localization procedure, using the fact that the good localization center is almost on the target halfspace,
one can show that a general halfspace
will become nearly homogeneous.
Moreover, since the center is also required to be not too far from the origin, one can further demonstrate that a decent fraction of the samples can still survive the rejection sampling, ensuring that the proceeding invocation of the homogeneous halfspace tester-learner is sample and computationally efficient.

Our main technical contribution is an efficient testable learner that computes a list of candidates containing at least one good localization center.
This implies that the learner has inherently learned a good constant approximation to the defining vector of the unknown halfspace, which is the major obstacle against applying prior methods (developed for homogeneous halfspaces)
in the general halfspace setting
(see
Section

1.1
for more details).

Finding a Good Localization Center

We now describe a testable learning procedure that either produces a good localization center or reports that the underlying distribution is not Gaussian.
We can assume that the optimal halfspace is

h(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}-t^{\ast})

for some
t^{\ast}>0
. As mentioned in the preceding discussion,
the ideal localization center would be
the intersection between the line along the defining vector
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
and the separating hyperplane of the target halfspace.

Naively, one may try to estimate the Chow parameters

\mathbf{\tilde{v}}:=\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[y\mathbf{x}]
,
and pick some point along the direction of
\mathbf{\tilde{v}}

— as the angle between
\mathbf{\tilde{v}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
ought to be small when the underlying
\mathbf{x}
-marginal is Gaussian.
Yet, as we discussed in
Section

1.1
, this cannot be done
efficiently in the testable regime.
For this reason, we instead calculate the mean of the points that are positively
labeled (formally, the tail points; see
Definition

2.8
),
i.e.,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}=\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[\mathbf{x}\mid y=1]
.
In the absence of corrupted labels, this gives us a point located in the positive side of the halfspace,
i.e.,
h(\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+})=1
,
as
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}=\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}\mid h(\mathbf{x})=1]\geq t^{\ast}
.
In other words, there exists a
\lambda\in(0,1)
so that

\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\ast}=t^{\ast}
,
and if we center our distribution at the point
\mathbf{w}=\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}

via rejection sampling (see
Definition

2.2
),
then the optimal halfspace becomes exactly homogeneous (
Figure

1
).

There are two obstacles that we need to circumvent in order for such an approach to work: (i) We do not have access to the true labels
and, by conditioning on the positive labels,
the noise can make the corrupted mean

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}=\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[\mathbf{x}\mid y=1]
appear on the negative side, i.e.,
h(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+})=-1
; and
(ii) the point
\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}
can be very far from the origin,
i.e.,
\|\lambda\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}\|_{2}\gtrsim\sqrt{\log(1/\mathrm{opt})}
,
and applying rejection sampling to re-center the distribution at this point
can result in a corruption level that is far worse than the current one.
Fortunately, we can ensure that these situations cannot happen
by certifying the following two properties of the empirical distribution
over samples:
(i) the distribution has bounded covariance, and
(ii) the cumulative density function of the distribution projected
along the direction of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}
is sufficiently close
to that of the standard Gaussian.

First, if we assume that (a) the mass of the positive points is at least

B\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}}\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\mathrm{opt}))
, and (b) the sample covariance has
spectral norm bounded from above by
2
,
then we can ensure that
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}\|_{2}\leq 1/\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\mathrm{opt}))
via the following simple calculation:
for any unit vector
\mathbf{u}
,
|\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}(\mathbbm{1}\{h(\mathbf{x})=1\}-\mathbbm{1}\{y=1\})]|\leq\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[(\mathbbm{1}\{h(\mathbf{x})=1\}-\mathbbm{1}\{y=1\})^{2}]^{1/2}\E_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{opt}}
; hence,
the error
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{+}\|_{2}
is at most

\sqrt{2\mathrm{opt}}/B\leq 1/\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\mathrm{opt}))
. Note that
assumption (b) can be efficiently verified and
assumption (a) on
B\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}}\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\mathrm{opt}))
is without loss of generality — otherwise, the constant hypothesis,
i.e.,
h(\mathbf{x})=-1
for all
\mathbf{x}
, would get
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}})
error

2

In the end, we can simply compare the halfspace learned
via localization with the constant hypothesis in terms
of their empirical errors to select the best.
.
This suffices to show that the intersection point
\mathbf{w}

is close to
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}
.
This sketch is formalized in
Lemma

2.9
.

It remains to show that
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}\|_{2}
is not very large.
To this end, we certify that the empirical distribution projected
along
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}
has its cumulative density function
close to that of the standard Gaussian.
If so,
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}\right\|_{2}
can then be bounded from below
by
\Phi^{-1}(M)
, where

denotes the mass of the samples
inside the region

.
Conditioned on that the “Gaussian closeness” CDF test passes,

\boldsymbol{\mu}
in the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-direction.
Thus,
\{\mathbf{x}\in S:\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{x}>\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\|_{2}^{2}\}

must contain a large fraction of the tail points, allowing us to derive a lower

-rejection
procedure is as follows: given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\exp\left(-(\sigma^{-2}-1)(\mathbf{w}/\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}/(1-\sigma^{2}))^{2}/2\right)\,,

and rejects it otherwise.

At a high level, if one performs
(\mathbf{w},\sigma)
-rejection sampling on a Gaussian distribution, the resulting distribution will be
G:=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, where
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is the matrix with eigenvalue
\sigma^{2}

in the

in all orthogonal directions.
This follows from the fact that the formula of the acceptance probability in

is precisely the ratio between the probability density functions of
G
and the standard Gaussian.
Let
S_{h}
be the separating hyperplane of

Assume that

\alpha

.
This immediately implies that

will be at most
\alpha

.
If we transform the space to make

will then consequently get transformed into a new halfspace with small threshold.
This is formally shown in the following lemma, whose proof can be found in Appendix

Lemma 2.3

(Localization With A Good Center)
.

Let
\mathbf{w}
be an
(\alpha,\beta)
-localization center with respect to

h(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t^{\ast})
and

\sigma\coloneqq\min(\|w\|_{2}^{-1},\sqrt{1/2})
. The following hold:

1.

The
(\mathbf{w},\sigma)
-rejection sampling procedure
(

results in a distribution

with

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}-(1-\sigma^{2})\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}/\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Moreover, the acceptance probability is

, and
\sigma\geq\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log(1/\beta)}).

2.

Applying the transformation

(\mathbf{x},y)\mapsto(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}\right),y)

\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

Moreover, the resulting halfspace

has its threshold size bounded
from above by

|\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{w}+t^{\ast}|/\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\|_{2}=O(\alpha\sqrt{\log(1/\beta)}

Given a good localization center,

allows us to transform the original halfspace into one with a small threshold.
We show that such a nearly homogeneous halfspace can be testably learned effectively in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.4

(Testable Learning of Nearly Homogeneous Halfspaces)
.

Let
\epsilon,\tau\in(0,1)

Let
D
be a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
and
h
be a halfspace that achieves error
\mathrm{opt}
under

with
h(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t^{\ast})
, where
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is some unit vector, and
\left|t\right|<\epsilon
.
Then, given
N=\mathrm{poly}(d/\epsilon)\log(1/\tau)
many i.i.d. samples from
D
, there exists an efficient tester-learner that runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(N)
, and
either reports
D_{\mathbf{x}}
is not Gaussian or outputs a vector
\mathbf{v}
.
Moreover, with probability at least
1-\tau

is not Gaussian, the report is correct, and (ii) if the algorithm returns a vector
\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{v}

satisfies that
\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\right\|_{2}\leq O(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
.

This proposition is established by a careful analysis of the testable learner for homogeneous halfspaces by
[
6
]
.
The main idea is that whenever the threshold of the optimal halfspace is bounded by some absolute constant, the Chow parameters will be a constant multiplicative factor of the defining vector.
We can testably learn the Chow parameters up to constant error, which then gives a constant approximation to the defining vector of the halfspace.
After that, the algorithm proceeds in an iterative manner.
In particular, it performs localization via rejection
sampling using the current estimate of the defining vector.
The localization steps may blow up the threshold
t^{\ast}

of the optimal halfspace by at most a factor of
1/\epsilon
.
Since
t^{\ast}
is initially bounded from above by
\epsilon
,
the threshold stays bounded from above by some absolute
constant. This ensures that the Chow-parameters
vector stays within a constant multiplicative factor of the
defining vector, thereby allowing us to iteratively
approximate the defining vector by testably learning the
Chow parameters to constant error.
The detailed proof can be found in Appendix
A
.

Since

gives the form of the transformation on the defining vector exactly,
after learning the defining vector of the nearly homogeneous halfspace,
we can revert the transformation to get back the defining vector of the original halfspace.
While the argument for bounding the error of the vector after reverting the transformation is a standard piece in most localization based learning procedures (see, e.g.,
[

one technical issue in our scenario is that such an argument usually requires the angle between the localization direction

and the defining vector

\mathbf{w}
and

To deal with this issue, we require the following lemma
which carefully bounds how the error grows as the angle

Lemma 2.5

(Transformation Error)
.

Let
\sigma,\delta\in(0,1)
,
\beta\in(0,\sqrt{2})

\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}-(1-\sigma^{2})\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{T}

Then it holds

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}/\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}-\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\|\leq O(\delta)(\sigma+\beta)\;\big(\sigma^{-1}\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^{2}/2}+1\big).

After learning the defining vector, we search for the
threshold of the halfspace in a brute-force manner.
In particular, we construct a list of candidate halfspaces

\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon)

select the one with the smallest empirical error after

The list is guaranteed to contain some halfspace

whose defining vector and threshold are both close to those
of the optimal halfspace, and such a search procedure based
on the empirical error is guaranteed to yield some

The last remaining piece is a procedure to certify that

Lemma 2.6

(Wedge Bound for General Halfspaces)

Let

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}

Let

In the end, we simply draw some fresh samples, and output the halfspace with the smallest testing error.
The detailed proof and pseudocode can be found in Appendix
D
.

3
Additional Remarks Regarding
Theorem

1.2

We provide some additional remarks regarding our main theorem, addressing some of its limitations.

Remark 3.1
.

It is natural to ask whether our algorithmic result can be generalized to hold for other structured distributions, e.g., for a large subclass of isotropic log-concave distributions. Such a generalization turns out to be possible for
homogeneous halfspaces
; see
[
17
]
.
It is important to note, however, that obtaining polynomial-time agnostic learners for general halfspaces under non-Gaussian distributions is a challenging task — even without the testable requirement.
In particular, the only known efficient agnostic learners for general halfspaces that achieve dimension-independent error are the ones from
[
10
,
12
]
,
which both work only under the Gaussian distribution.

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem

1.2
achieves error of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}})+\epsilon
.
On the other hand, error of
O(\mathrm{opt})+\epsilon
can be achieved in the same setting for
homogeneous halfspaces. We discuss here a technical barrier of our approach
preventing further improvements. A critical ingredient in our algorithm is a procedure which robustly estimates the mean of samples sharing
the same label (in a testable way),
in order to extract directional information about the weight vector of the optimal halfspace. Our current method, relying on certifying bounded
second moments of the samples, ceases to work when the amount of corruption approaches
\sqrt{B}
, where
B
is the probability mass of the points having that label. It is plausible that certifying boundedness of higher moments is an avenue for progress here.

Testable Learner for Nearly Homogeneous Halfspaces

We restate and show the following:

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.
Let
D
be a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
and
h
be a halfspace that achieves error
\mathrm{opt}
under
D

.

Proof.

The algorithm of
Proposition

2.4
is identical to the algorithm of
[
6
]
, but we need to argue that the small offset does not make the algorithm to fail. If the distribution was Gaussian, then trivially the
\pr[\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t)\neq\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x})]=O(\epsilon)
, therefore, we could just assume that there exists a homogeneous halfspace with error
\mathrm{opt}+O(\epsilon)
. Unfortunately, in our setting we cannot do that. The reason is that, we do not have any guarantee that all the bands of the form
\{|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}|\leq\epsilon\}
have mass of order
\epsilon
for all the vectors
\mathbf{u}
.

The whole approach of
[
6
]
boils down into calculating the
\E[y\mathbf{x}]
up to a good accuracy. This is done in Proposition 2.1 of this work. We need to show that if the optimal halfspace is of the form
\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t)
with
|t|\leq 10
, then we can robustly estimate
\E[y\mathbf{x}]
. We first need to show that small
|t|
does not change the
\E[y\mathbf{x}]
by a lot. We use the following fact

(see, e.g., Lemma 4.3 of
[
10

.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a unit vector and

be the corresponding halfspace.
If
\mathbf{x}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

then we have that

\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[h(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}\right]=2G(t)~\mathbf{v}

is the pdf of the standard Gaussian.

Note that from

A.2
, we can see that if
|t^{\prime}|\leq 10
then
G(t^{\prime})\geq C
, for some sufficiently small absolute constant
C>0
. Therefore, the proof of Proposition 2.1 remains the same as it only changes by a constant factor the calculation of
\E[y\mathbf{x}]

6

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}-(1-\sigma^{2})\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}

.

10

\displaystyle={\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{-1}}\;\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\;\left(1+\frac{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{-2}}{(1-\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{-2})}\right)\right)

Combining our analysis then gives the new halfspace

, which further implies that
a<O(\sqrt{\log(1/B)})
.
Combining the two observations we have that
a/b\leq O(\sqrt{\log(1/B)})
.
Let
x=b/a
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{v}^{\ast}-\mathbf{w}/\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\right)^{2}=1+\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}+\frac{x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}

\displaystyle=2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\leq 2-\frac{2}{O(\sqrt{\log(1/B)})}.

For this particular localization center
\mathbf{w}
, we also have
\sigma\geq\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log(1/B)})\geq\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log(1/\mathrm{opt})})
by
Lemma

2.3
.

We can therefore apply
Lemma

2.5
with

\beta=\sqrt{2-\frac{2}{O(\sqrt{\log(1/B)})}}
,

\delta=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}})+\epsilon

and
\sigma=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log(1/\mathrm{opt})})
.
Note that

\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^{2}/2}\leq O(\sqrt{\log(1/B)}).

This implies that, after reverting the transformation, we obtain a vector
\mathbf{v}
satisfying that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O(1)\;(\sigma+\beta)\;\left(\delta\sigma^{-1}\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^{2}/2}+\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq O(\delta)\;\left(\sqrt{\log(1/\mathrm{opt})}\;\sqrt{\log(1/\mathrm{opt})}\right)\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}}+\epsilon).

We then guess the offset of the optimal halfspace up to accuracy
\epsilon
.
Denote the best guess as
\bar{t}
.
Applying
Lemma

2.6
, we have that

\pr_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim D}[\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\bar{t})\neq\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\cdot\mathbf{x}+t^{\ast})]\leq O(1)\;\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\ast}\right\|_{2}+\left|\bar{t}-t^{\ast}\right|\right)\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}}+\epsilon).

The above concludes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
for constant failure probability
\tau
.
In order to boost the success probability, we will run the algorithm
10\;\log(1/\tau)
many times. If the algorithm outputs reject for more than half of the time, we output reject. Otherwise, we collect the halfspaces learned, draw
O\left(d\log\log(1/\tau)/\epsilon^{2}\right)
many i.i.d. samples, and output the halfspace that achieves the smallest empirical error.
If
D
truly has marginal
D_{\mathbf{x}}
, each invocation of the algorithm outputs accepts with high constant probability. Since we only output reject when the algorithm outputs reject in more than half of the trials, it follows that we reject with probability at most
\tau
.
If we do not output reject, we must have collected at least
5\log(1/\tau)
many halfspaces, and each halfspace achieves the required learning error with high constant probability. So there exists a halfspace in the learned list with error
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}})+\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\tau/2
.
Besides, with probability at least
1-\tau/2
, we can estimate the error of these halfspaces up to accuracy
\epsilon
.
Conditioned on the above two events, which hold simultaneously with probability at least
1-\tau
by the union bound, it then follows that the final halfspace picked will have error at most
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\mathrm{opt}})+2\epsilon
.
This concludes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
∎
