Deterministic Sparse Pattern Matching via the Baur-Strassen Theorem
Nick Fischer

Abstract
How fast can you test whether a constellation of stars appears in the night sky? This question can be modeled as the computational problem of testing whether a set of points P P can be moved into (or close to) another set Q Q under some prescribed group of transformations. Problems of this kind are subject to intensive study in computational geometry and enjoy countless theoretical and practical applications. Consider, as a simple representative, the following problem: Given two sets of at most n n integers P , Q ⊆ [ N ] P,Q\subseteq[N] , determine whether there is some shift s s such that P P shifted by s s is a subset of Q Q , i.e., P + s = { p + s : p ∈ P } ⊆ Q P+s=\{p+s:p\in P\}\subseteq Q . This problem, to which we refer as the Constellation problem, can be solved in near-linear time O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) by a Monte Carlo randomized algorithm [Cardoze, Schulman; FOCS ’98] and time O ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ N ) O(n\log^{2}N) by a Las Vegas randomized algorithm [Cole, Hariharan; STOC ’02]. Moreover, there is a deterministic algorithm running in time n ⋅ 2 O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ​ log ⁡ log ​ N ) n\cdot 2^{O(\sqrt{\log n\log\log N})} [Chan, Lewenstein; STOC ’15]. An interesting question left open by these previous works is whether Constellation is in deterministic near-linear time (i.e., with only polylogarithmic overhead). We answer this question positively by giving an O ⁡ ( n ​ polylog ⁡ ( N ) ) O(n\polylog(N)) -time deterministic algorithm for the Constellation problem. Our algorithm extends to various more complex Point Pattern Matching problems in higher dimensions, under translations and rigid motions, and possibly with mismatches, and also to a near-linear-time derandomization of the Sparse Wildcard Matching problem on strings. We find it particularly interesting how we obtain our deterministic algorithm. All previous algorithms are based on the same baseline idea, using additive hashing and the Fast Fourier Transform. In contrast, our algorithms are based on new ideas, involving a surprising blend of combinatorial and algebraic techniques. At the heart lies an innovative application of the Baur-Strassen theorem from algebraic complexity theory.

Technical Highlights

In this section we briefly sketch the technical highlights of our paper. Our approach can be neatly split into two independent steps.

Step 1: From Pattern Matching to Convolutions

Almost 50 years ago, Fischer and Paterson

•

In the

By now, both problems have received considerable attention in the literature with quite different outcomes: On the one hand, the Sparse Convolution problem underwent a series of algorithmic improvements

In light of this, a reasonable approach for the Constellation problem is to look for a reduction to the Sparse Convolution problem. However, despite much focus and the apparent connection that both problems share (for instance in terms of algorithmic tools like additive hashing and FFT), previous work fell short of giving the desired reduction.

Our first contribution is that we establish the missing reduction, but with an unexpected twist: We prove that the Constellation problem reduces to a logarithmic number of instances of the
Partial

The Baur-Strassen Theorem

The Baur-Strassen theorem is a powerful tool from algebraic complexity, stating intuitively that we can compute the partial derivatives of a function with the same complexity as the function itself:

1.10
Baur-Strassen
[
11
,
49
]
.

For any arithmetic circuit
C
computing
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, there is a circuit
C^{\prime}
that simultaneously computes the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. The circuit
C^{\prime}
has size
|C^{\prime}|\leq O(|C|)
and can be constructed in time
O(|C|)
.

Baur and Strassen established this result in 1983
[
11
]
, and a simplified constructive proof followed shortly after
[
49
]
. Their original intention was to prove arithmetic circuit
lower bounds
(in particular, the Baur-Strassen theorem implies that matrix multiplication and determinant computation have the same asymptotic complexity). But also in algorithm design, the Baur-Strassen theorem had an exciting appearance: Cygan, Gabow and Sankowski designed simple and fast algorithms for several graph problems including finding shortest cycles, computing the radius and diameter, and finding minimum-weight perfect matchings
[
24
]
. Their algorithms are based on the Baur-Strassen theorem combined with fast matrix multiplication. Our work constitutes only the second application in algorithm design that we are aware of, and the first application in combination with an FFT-like circuit.

Intuitively, the Baur-Strassen theorem allows to
invert the flow
of a circuit, and to propagate information from the outputs to the inputs—After all, this is exactly what a partial derivative does: It measures how the output is affected by an input. Under the name
back-propagation
this usage of the Baur-Strassen theorem is omni-present in machine learning
[
65
,
59
,
66
]
. More relevant to us is the consequence that a bilinear circuit computing a function
z_{k}=\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j,k}\cdot x_{i}\cdot y_{j}
can be transformed into a circuit computing the “rotated” function
x_{i}=\sum_{j,k}a_{i,j,k}\cdot y_{j}\cdot z_{k}
; details follow in the paragraph. In contrast to the typical use cases in algebraic complexity, we here critically exploit that the new circuit can also be efficiently constructed.

Step 2: Partial Convolutions via the Baur-Strassen Theorem

Let us come back to the algorithm. The Baur-Strassen theorem is only useful in combination with an arithmetic circuit; in our case that circuit should naturally compute the convolution of two sparse vectors. Such circuits exist, and they have already been used by Bringmann, Fischer and Nakos
[
14
]
to obtain a deterministic near-linear algorithm for the Sparse Convolution problem (on nonnegative vectors). Specifically, the sparse convolution technology yields a circuit of size
\widetilde{O}(|C-B|)
with inputs
y_{b}
(for
b\in B
) and
z_{c}
(for
c\in C
) and outputs
w_{a}
(for
a\in C-B
) that computes

w_{a}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a=c-b\end{subarray}}y_{b}\cdot z_{c}.

We will now modify this circuit in two steps. First, we add new inputs
x_{a}
(for
a\in A
) and with small overhead let the circuit compute

w:=\sum_{a\in A\cap(C-B)}x_{a}\cdot w_{a}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}\cdot z_{c}.

Then observe that the partial derivatives

\frac{\partial w}{\partial z_{c}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}

are exactly what we want to compute. We can thus apply the Baur-Strassen theorem to let our circuit compute the derivatives of
w
with respect to all inputs
z_{c}
. The size has not increased asymptotically and is still
\widetilde{O}(|C-B|)
.

Following this insight, our deterministic algorithm explicitly constructs this arithmetic circuit (applying the Baur-Strassen theorem in the process), and evaluates it on the given vectors
x,y
. Unfortunately, while this overview seems very simple, the implementation of this idea suffers from many technical complications due to insufficiencies in the underlying computer algebra machinery. One of the main problems is that we need to perform all computations over a finite field, but we cannot assume deterministic access to a prime number of size
N

3SUM on Structured Instances

A notable consequence of this idea is for the 3SUM problem on structured instances. The 3SUM problem is to decide for three integer sets
A,B,C
of size at most
n
, whether there is a triple
(a,b,c)\in A\times B\times C
with
a+b+c=0
. It is a central conjecture in fine-grained complexity theory that this problem requires quadratic time
n^{2-o(1)}

[
28
]
, and many conditional hardness results are known based on this conjecture. On the positive side, it is known that for structured sets, 3SUM can be solved faster. Specifically, there is a (deterministic) algorithm in time
\widetilde{O}(n+\min\{|A+B|,|A+C|,|B+C|\})
that simply computes the smallest of the three sumsets via a sparse convolution algorithm. 3SUM algorithms of this type have recently found applications in fine-grained lower bounds
[
1
,
43
]
. With our novel approach, we can solve the following “count-all-numbers” version of 3SUM in the same running time; prior to our work this statement was not even known in terms of randomized algorithms, as far as we are aware:

1.11
#AllNumbers3SUM.

Given three sets
A,B,C\subseteq\{-n^{O(1)},\dots,n^{O(1)}\}
of size at most
n
, we call a triple
(a,b,c)\in A\times B\times C
that satisfies
a+b+c=0
a
3-sum
. There is an algorithm that computes for each
z\in A\cup B\cup C
in how many 3-sums it participates, and runs in deterministic time
\widetilde{O}(n+\min\{|A+B|,|A+C|,|B+C|\})
.

From Constellation to Partial Convolution

In this section we give our reduction from the Constellation problem to Partial Convolution. We start with the reduction in

See

Proof.

Throughout this proof, let us consider the Constellation problem for cyclic groups
\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}
. That is, we are given sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}
and the task is to list all shifts
s\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}
satisfying
P+s\subseteq Q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)
(i.e., that for all
p\in P
there exists some
q\in Q
with
p+s\equiv q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)
). Let us further assume that
N
is a power of two. An algorithm for this problem directly leads to an algorithm for the standard Constellation problem with the same asymptotic running time, by choosing
N
to be the smallest power of two larger than
2\cdot\max(P\cup Q)
(as for this choice of
N
there is no wrap-around).

The key idea behind the reduction is to apply a
scaling trick.
If
N
is smaller than some constant, we solve the instance by brute-force. Otherwise we will
recursively
solve a Constellation instance over a smaller cyclic group of size
N^{\prime}=\frac{N}{2}
, to obtain a good approximation to the original instance. More specifically, construct the sets

\displaystyle P^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{p\bmod N^{\prime}:p\in P\}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N^{\prime}\mathbf{Z},

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{q\bmod N^{\prime}:q\in Q\}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}.

We view
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
as a Constellation instance and solve it recursively. In this way we compute the maximal set
S^{\prime}
satisfying
P^{\prime}+S^{\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Note that whenever
s
is a feasible shift in the original instance
(P,Q)
, then
s\bmod N^{\prime}
is a feasible shift in the new instance
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
(but not necessarily vice versa). Therefore, the set
S^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime}\}
surely contains all feasible shifts, plus possibly some false positives, and the remaining task is to filter all non-feasible shifts. To this end, we construct a Partial Convolution instance with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=Q\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\One_{Q}\quad\text{(the indicator vector of $Q$)},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=-P\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\One_{-P}\quad\text{(the indicator vector of $-P$)},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=S^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}.

Using the oracle, we can efficiently compute
(x\star_{N}y)[s]
for all
s\in C
. (Here we use the previous observation that computing cyclic and non-cyclic convolutions is equivalent.) Finally, we return

S=\{s\in C:(x\star_{N}y)[s]=|P|\}.

This completes the description of the algorithm; in the next two steps we will prove that it is correct and efficient.

3.1
Correctness.

S=\{s\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}:P+s\subseteq Q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)\}
.

Proof.

As mentioned before, for any shift
s
with
P+s\subseteq Q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)
it is clear that
P^{\prime}+(s\bmod N^{\prime})\subseteq Q^{\prime}\;\,({\bmod}\>N^{\prime})
. Therefore,
C
is a superset of the feasible shifts. Consider any shift
s\in C
. By a straightforward calculation, we have that

(x\star_{N}y)[s]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z}\\
a+b\equiv s\;\,({\bmod}\>N)\end{subarray}}x[a]\cdot y[b]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in Q\\
p\in P\\
q-p\equiv s\;\,({\bmod}\>N)\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P\\
q\in Q\\
p+s\equiv q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)\end{subarray}}1.

That is,
(x\star_{N}y)[s]
equals the number of solutions to the equation
p+s\equiv q\;\,({\bmod}\>N)
, for
p\in P
and
q\in Q
. If
s
is feasible, then clearly there must be a solution for every
p\in P
, and the number of solutions is exactly
|P|
. On the other hand, if
s
is not feasible, then there exists some
p\in P
with
p+s\not\in Q
, and therefore the number of solutions is strictly less than
|P|
.
∎

3.2
Running Time.

The algorithm runs in time
O(n\log^{c+1}N)
.

Proof.

Recall that solving the Partial Convolution instance takes time
O((|A|+|C-B|)\cdot\log^{c}N)
. While it looks like
|C-B|
could have quadratic size, due to the way that we chose

we can argue that
|C-B|=O(n)
. Indeed, recalling that
C=S^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime}\}
,
B=-P=-(P^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime}\})
and
P^{\prime}+S^{\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
, we have that

C-B=S^{\prime}+P^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime},2N^{\prime}\}\subseteq Q^{\prime}+\{0,N^{\prime},2N^{\prime}\}.

Since
|Q^{\prime}|=|Q|\leq n
, we have that
|C-B|\leq 3n
as claimed. This proves that a single call to the Partial Convolution oracle takes time
O(n\log^{c}N)
. Moreover, since the algorithm reaches recursion depth at most
\log N
, the total running time is
O(n\log^{c+1}N)
as claimed.
∎

In combination,
3.1
and
3.2
complete the proof of
Lemma

1.9
.
∎

Let

Let

be the indicator vector of

\displaystyle=-P\subseteq\mathbf{Z}/N\mathbf{Z},

Definition 4.2

An

The

z_{c}
(for
c\in T
) defined by

z_{c}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}.

The circuit has size
O(|T|\log^{2}|T|)
, and it takes time
O(|T|\log^{2}|T|+|T|\log N)
to construct the circuit.

Proof.

Let us assume that
A,B\subseteq T
; otherwise simply include the missing elements which does not increase the size of
T
asymptotically. We write
T=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{t}\}
where
t=|T|
. The circuit is constructed in several steps. Our first step is to precompute all powers
\omega^{c_{1}},\dots,\omega^{c_{t}}
. Note that these powers are pairwise distinct since
\omega
has multiplicative order at least
N
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{t}<N
. Writing

V=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
\omega^{c_{1}}&\omega^{c_{2}}&\cdots&\omega^{c_{t}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\omega^{(t-1)c_{1}}&\omega^{(t-1)c_{2}}&\cdots&\omega^{(t-1)c_{t}}\end{bmatrix},

by
Lemma

4.3
we can compute arithmetic circuits that respectively compute the matrix-vector products with
V
and
V^{-1}
. Using these circuits, our first step is to construct circuits computing the values
\widehat{x}_{i}
and
\widehat{y}_{i}
(for
i\in[t]
) defined by

\widehat{x}_{i}=\sum_{a\in A}\omega^{ia}\cdot x_{a},\quad\widehat{y}_{j}=\sum_{b\in B}\omega^{jb}\cdot y_{b}.

Indeed, these circuits follow immediately, since
\widehat{x}=(\widehat{x}_{0},\dots,\widehat{x}_{t-1})
is the result of the matrix-vector product
V
times the length-
t
vector with
i
-th entry
x_{c_{i}}
if
c_{i}\in A
and
0
otherwise (and similarly for
\widehat{y}
). Next, we compute for all
i\in[t]
the values

\widehat{z}_{i}=\widehat{x}_{i}\cdot\widehat{y}_{i}=\left\lparen\sum_{a\in A}\omega^{ia}\cdot x_{a}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{b\in B}\omega^{ib}\cdot y_{b}\right\rparen=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\end{subarray}}\omega^{i(a+b)}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}=\sum_{c\in T}\omega^{ic}\cdot z_{c},

where
z_{c}
is as in the lemma statement. Observe that similarly to before we can express
\widehat{z}=(\widehat{z}_{0},\dots,\widehat{z}_{t-1})
as the matrix-vector product
V\cdot(z_{c_{1}},\dots,z_{c_{t}})^{T}
. It follows that
(z_{c_{1}},\dots,z_{c_{t}})
can be computed via the matrix-vector product
V^{-1}\widehat{z}^{T}
for which we also have an arithmetic circuit available. This completes the description of the sparse convolution circuit.

It was constructed by appropriately composing three copies of the circuits constructed in
Lemma

4.3
all of which have size
O(t\log^{2}t)
and that can be constructed in the same time. The only other contribution to the running time was the precomputation of the powers
\omega^{c_{1}},\dots,\omega^{c_{t}}
that takes
O(t\log N)
by repeated squaring.
∎

4.3
Finding Large-Order Elements

A crucial ingredient of
Lemma

4.4
is that we need to provide a field element
\omega
with large multiplicative order. It is known that in any finite field
\mathbf{F}_{p}
, an almost-constant fraction of elements is primitive (i.e., has maximum multiplicative order
p-1
), and therefore we can find an element with large order in randomized time
\widetilde{O}(1)
by sampling. Unfortunately, the deterministic construction of such elements is known to be a notoriously hard problem. Even worse: For the field
\mathbf{F}_{p}
to contain an element with multiplicative order at least
N
, we have to find a prime
p
of size at least
N
. Again, this is in polylogarithmic randomized time, but the best deterministic algorithm takes time
N^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}

[
47
,
62
]
.

Luckily, a line of research has investigated how to construct large-order elements in finite fields of prime power order
q=p^{d}
with comparably small characteristic
p
; see e.g.
[
60
,
61
,
30
,
17
,
18
]
. Before we go into more details, let us first recap some basics on how to compute with finite fields in the RAM model.

Finite Field Arithmetic

Let
q=p^{d}
be a prime power. Recall that the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{p}
can be represented as
\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}
, the integer modulo
p
. The finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
can be represented as
\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]/\langle f\rangle
where
f\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]
is an irreducible polynomial of degree
d
. In the word RAM model with words storing numbers up to
p
, we can thus represent field elements from
\mathbf{F}_{q}
as length-
d
lists of coefficients from
\mathbf{F}_{p}
. Using basic polynomial arithmetic, we can compute the field operations (
{+},{-},{\times},{/}
) of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
in time
\widetilde{O}(\log q)
.

Constructing Large-Order Elements

Almost any of the results
[
60
,
61
,
30
,
17
,
18
]
is sufficient for our purposes. We will use a result of Cheng
[
18
]
, which gives the best running time in terms of lower-order factors
\log N
. See also
[
14
, Lemmas 8, 9]
for a compact proof.

4.5
Constructing Large-Order Elements
[
18
]
.

Let
p\geq 7
be a prime. In time
\widetilde{O}(p)
we can construct an irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]
of degree
p-1
, and an element
\omega\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]/\langle f\rangle=\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
of order at least
2^{p}
.

4.4
Partial Convolution

We are finally ready to give our algorithm for the Partial Convolution problem. Recall that in this problem, we consider three sets
A,B,C\subseteq[N]
and two vectors
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}^{N}
with
\supp(x)\subseteq A
and
\supp(y)\subseteq B
. The task is to compute the convolution vector
x\star y

restricted
to the positions in
C
.

We start to solve a relaxation of this problem; see the following
Lemma

4.6
. Here the goal is to compute the numbers
modulo
p
, for some prime
p
, and we can additionally assume that a small superset
T
of
A+B
is known. We remove these assumptions later in
Lemmas

4.7
and
4.1
.

4.6
Partial Convolution modulo Prime.

Let
A,B,C,T\subseteq[N]
and
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}^{N}
with
\supp(x)\subseteq A
,
\supp(y)\subseteq B
and
C-B\subseteq T
. Let
p>\log N
be a prime. Then we can compute
(x\star y)[c]\bmod p
for all
c\in C
in deterministic time
O((|A|+|T|)\log^{2}(N)\cdot p\polylog(p))
.

Proof.

As a first step, we set up an appropriate finite field containing an element of large multiplicative order. To this end, we apply
Lemma

4.5
to find an irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]
of degree
p-1
, and an element
\omega\in\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]/\langle f\rangle
whose multiplicative order is guaranteed to be at least
2^{p}
. Let us represent the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
by
\mathbf{F}_{p}[X]/\langle f\rangle
; then we have access to an element
\omega\in\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
with order at least
2^{p}>N
.

Next, we will use
\omega
to construct a sparse convolution circuit over
\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
. Applying
Lemma

4.4
with
-B
,
C
and
T
(note that we indeed have
-B+C=C-B\subseteq T
) yields an arithmetic circuit with inputs
y_{b}
(for
b\in B
) and
z_{c}
(for
c\in C
), and outputs
w_{a}
(for
a\in T
) computing the bilinear functions

w_{a}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
c\in C\\
-b+c=a\end{subarray}}y_{b}\cdot z_{c}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}y_{b}\cdot z_{c}.

We will modify this circuit in several steps. First, we add new input gates
x_{a}
(for
a\in A
) to the circuit. Then, by adding the appropriate wires and a new output gate
w
, we let the circuit compute

w=\sum_{a\in A\cap T}x_{a}\cdot w_{a}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\cap T\\
b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}\cdot z_{c}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}\cdot z_{c}.

We delete all output gates other than
w
, so that the circuit computes the single output
w
. This allows us to apply the Baur-Strassen theorem to construct an augmented circuit that computes the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial w}{\partial z_{c}}
for all
c\in C
. The description of the arithmetic circuit is complete. Note that it computes

\frac{\partial w}{\partial z_{c}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
c\in C\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}\cdot z_{c}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
b\in B\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}x_{a}\cdot y_{b}.

This is exactly the convolution function that we set out to compute. By plugging in the vector
x
and
y
into the same-named inputs
x_{a}
and
y_{b}
, we can therefore read of the convolution
(x\star y)[c]
from the outputs
\frac{\partial w}{\partial z_{c}}
. Recall that all computations are modulo
p
, and thus we in fact only have access to
(x\star y)[c]\bmod p
.

Let us finally analyze the running time. Constructing the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
as well as the large-order element
\omega
takes time
\widetilde{O}(p)
, which is negligible. The construction of the sparse convolution circuit takes time
O(|T|\log^{2}N)
and leads to a circuit of size
O(|T|\log^{2}N)
. All subsequent modifications, including adding outputs as well as applying the Baur-Strassen
Theorem

1.10
, do not increase the size of the circuit asymptotically and run in linear time in the circuit size,
O(|T|\log^{2}N+|A|)
. Finally, evaluating the circuit takes time
O(|T|\log^{2}(N)+|A|)
plus
O(|T|\log^{2}(N)+|A|)
field operations over
\mathbf{F}_{p^{p-1}}
. Recall that each field operation over a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{p^{d}}
takes time
d\polylog(d)
, and therefore the total running time is bounded by
O((|A|+|T|)\log^{2}(N)\cdot p\polylog(p))
as claimed.
∎

We will now remove the restriction of the previous lemma that it computes the outputs modulo some prime
p
, by applying it repeatedly for several primes
p
, and by using the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

4.7
Partial Convolution with Superset.

Let
A,B,C,T\subseteq[N]
and
x,y\in\mathbf{Z}^{N}
with
\supp(x)\subseteq A
,
\supp(y)\subseteq B
and
C-B\subseteq T
. Then we can compute
(x\star y)[c]
for all
c\in C
in deterministic time
O((|A|+|T|)\log^{4}(N\Delta)\polyloglog(N\Delta))
, where
\Delta
is the largest entry in
x,y
in absolute value.

Proof.

First, precompute a list of
r=\lceil\log(2N\Delta)\rceil
distinct primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
larger than
\log N
. By the Prime Number Theorem, we can pick primes
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
of size at most
O(\log(N\Delta)\log\log(N\Delta))
. Moreover, we can find these primes in time
O(\log(N\Delta)\polyloglog(N\Delta))
using for instance the Sieve of Eratosthenes.

We apply
Lemma

4.6

r
times, for the sets
A,B,C,T
and the primes
p=p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
respectively. We thereby compute, for each
c\in C
, the values
(x\star y)[c]\bmod p_{i}
(for
i=1,\dots,r
). Since
(x\star y)[c]
is at most
\Delta N
in absolute value, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem these modular equations uniquely determine the integer value
(x\star y)[c]
.

Finally, we analyze the running time.
Repeatedly calling
Lemma

4.6
for
r=O(\log(N\Delta))
times takes time
O((|A|+|T|)\log^{4}(N\Delta)\polyloglog(N\Delta))
. Afterwards, solving the congruences with the Chinese Remainder Theorem takes time
O(\log^{2}(\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}))=O(\log^{2}(N)\polyloglog(N))

[
64
]
for each element
c\in C
, and thus negligible time
O(|C|\log^{2}(N\Delta)\polyloglog(N\Delta))
in total.
∎

Finally, we can remove the assumption that a small superset
T
of
C-B
is known, simply by precomputing the set
T=C-B
exactly. To this end, we use the deterministic sparse convolution algorithm by Bringmann, Fischer and Nakos
[
14
]
that runs in time
O(|C-B|\log^{5}(N)\polyloglog(N))
. All in all, we obtain the claimed
Theorem

4.1
.

4.5
Corollary for 3SUM

As a direct consequence of
Theorem

4.1
we obtain an algorithm for the #AllNumbers3SUM problem, running in time
\widetilde{O}(n+\min\{|A+B|,|A+C|,|B+C|\})
(see
Theorem

1.11
).

Proof of
Theorem

1.11
.

It suffices to solve the problem in time
\widetilde{O}(|A+B|+|C|)
; the other running times can be obtained by exchanging the roles of
A,B,C
. The output of the problem consists of the following three types of queries:

1.

For all
a\in A
, compute
\lvert\{(b,c)\in B\times C:-c-b=a\}\rvert
.

2.

For all
b\in B
, compute
\lvert\{(a,c)\in A\times C:-c-a=b\}\rvert
.

3.

For all
c\in C
, compute
\lvert\{(a,b)\in A\times B:a+b=-c\}\rvert
.

It is known how to solve the third type using sparse convolution algorithms
[
14
]
: In time
\widetilde{O}(|A+B|)
we can compute the sumset
A+B
with multiplicities. Afterwards we can read off, for each
c\in C
, the multiplicity of
-c
in the sumset
A+B
.

The first and second types require our new technology. Specifically, for the queries of the first type, we apply
Theorem

4.1
with
A^{\prime}=-C
,
B^{\prime}=-B
and
C^{\prime}=A
, and let
x=\One_{-C}
and
y=\One_{-B}
be the indicator vectors of
A^{\prime}=-C
and
B^{\prime}=-B
, respectively. (Here, for simplicity we assume that vectors can be indexed by negative numbers. This requirement can easily be avoided by shifting all sets by a common large offset.) The output consists of
(x\star y)[a]
, for all
a\in C^{\prime}=A
. By construction, we have

(x\star y)[a]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbf{Z}\\
i+j=a\end{subarray}}\One_{-C}(i)\cdot\One_{-B}(j)=\lvert\{(b,c)\in B\times C:-c-b=a\}\rvert,

which is exactly as desired. The running time is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(|A^{\prime}|+|C^{\prime}-B^{\prime}|)=\widetilde{O}(|C|+|A+B|)
. Finally, the queries of the second type can be computed analogously (by exchanging
A
and
B
).
∎

Corollaries for Point and String Pattern Matching

By combining the previous two sections (
Lemmas

1.9
and
3.4
with
Theorem

4.1
), we have completed the proofs of our two Main
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
. Moreover, since
Lemma

3.4
works in the presence of
weighted
mismatches, we obtain the following result which summarizes our Main Theorems in their strongest form:

5.1
Deterministic Constellation with Mismatches.

Given sets
P,Q\subseteq[N]
of size at most
n
, positive integer weights
w:P\to\mathbf{Z}_{>0}
and
0\leq k\leq(1-\Omega(1))w(P)
, we can list all shifts
s
with
\sum_{p\in P:p+s\not\in Q}w(p)\leq k
in deterministic time
O(nk\polylog(N))
.

In the remainder of this section, we describe how to obtain
Corollaries

1.3
to
1.8
.

5.1
Preliminaries on Point Pattern Matching

We start with a systematic definition of the Point Pattern Matching problems that we consider. Consider the
d
-dimensional Euclidian space, and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denote the Euclidian norm. For two finite sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the
directed Hausdorff distance
from
P
to
Q
is defined as

h(P,Q)=\max_{p\in P}\min_{q\in Q}\lVert p-q\rVert,

and the
undirected Hausdorff distance
between
P
and
Q
is defined as

H(P,Q)=\max\{h(P,Q),h(Q,P)\}.

Throughout we fix a group of
transformations

T
. We consider two types of transformations: translations (in which case
T
is the additive group
\mathbf{R}^{d}
) and rigid motions—that is, translations, rotations and possibly reflections (in this case
T
is the Euclidian group). For a transformation
t\in T
, we write
t(p)
to denote image under the transformation
t
of some point
p\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, and for a set of points
P
we write
t(P)=\{t(p):p\in P\}
. Each Point Pattern Matching problem is defined for a group of transformations
T
, with the goal of returning all transformations that move a given set
P
close to another set
Q
. The exact meaning of “close” is formalized in the following four problem variants.

Problem 5.2

(Exact Point Pattern Matching)
.

Let
T
be a group of transformations. Given point sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{d}
, return
S=\{t\in T:t(P)\subseteq Q\}
.

Problem 5.3

(Approximate Point Pattern Matching)
.

Let
T
be a group of transformations. Given point sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}
and parameters
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\delta>0
, return a set
S\subseteq T
with:

•

Soundness:
For all
s\in S
,
h(s(P),Q)\leq(1+\epsilon)\delta
.

•

Completeness:
For all
t\in T
with
h(t(P),Q)\leq\delta
, there is some
s\in S
with
h(s(P),t(P))\leq\epsilon\delta
.

Problem 5.4

(Exact Point Pattern Matching with Mismatches)
.

Let
T
be a group of transformations. Given point sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{d}
and a parameter
0\leq k<|P|
, return
S=\{t\in T:|t(P)\setminus Q|\leq k\}
.

Problem 5.5

(Approximate Point Pattern Matching with Mismatches)
.

Let
T
be a group of transformations. Given point sets
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}
and parameters
0\leq k<|P|
and
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\delta>0
, return a set
S\subseteq T
with:

•

Soundness:
For all
s\in S
, there is some
P_{0}\subseteq P
with
|P_{0}|\geq|P|-k
and
h(s(P_{0}),Q)\leq(1+\epsilon)\delta
.

•

Completeness:
For all
t\in T
for which there is some set
P_{0}\subseteq P
with
|P_{0}|\geq|P|-k
and
h(t(P_{0}),Q)\leq\delta
, there is some
s\in S
with
h(s(P_{0}),t(P_{0}))\leq\epsilon\delta
.

As before we assume that the point sets
P
and
Q
have size at most
n
. Moreover, we let
N
denote the boundary of
P,Q
in the following sense. For integer points
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{d}
we let
N
denote the coordinate-wise maximum of all points in
P
and
Q
(in absolute value). For real points
P,Q\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}
we assume that all coordinates of all points are available up to some fixed precision using
\log N
bits. In particular, we assume that the maximum distance between any two points is
N
and that the minimum distance between any two points is
N^{-1}
.

For the exact Point Pattern Matching problem with mismatches (
5.4
), we will also consider a weighted version where, as before, we report
S=\{t\in T:\sum_{p\in P:t(p)\not\in Q}w(p)\leq k\}
. We need this problem as an intermediate step in the upcoming
Lemma

5.7
.

5.2
From Point Pattern Matching to Constellation

We now state three reductions from Point Pattern Matching to the Constellation problem (
Lemmas

5.6
,
5.7
and
5.8
). While these reductions are in spirit due to Cardoze and Schulman
[
15
]
, they at times relied on randomization. Fortunately, the use of randomization is not essential here and can be easily removed. For completeness, we include proofs for the two reductions that cannot be directly reused from
[
15
]
(
Lemmas

5.6
and
5.7
), and only treat the one reduction that is originally deterministic as a black-box (
Lemma

5.8
).

5.6
Exact Translations with Mismatches to Constellation.

If the Constellation problem with
k
(weighted) mismatches is in deterministic time
f(n,k,N)
, then the exact Point Pattern Matching problem with translations and
k
(weighted) mismatches is in deterministic time
O(f(n,k,(4N)^{d}))
.

We remark that in
[
15
]
, this reduction is achieved by mapping each high-dimensional point to an integer via a random linear combination of its coordinates. It is easy to prove that this preserves solutions and does not introduce false positives with good probability. Our work-around is standard: We instead encode all coordinates into one big integer.

Proof.

Let us start with the unweighted version, and assume without loss of generality that all coordinates are nonnegative (by shifting the sets
P,Q
if necessary, which increases
N
to at most
2N
). The idea is to encode points
p=(p_{0},\dots,p_{d-1})\in[2N]^{d}
as integers
\overline{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}p_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i}
. Construct the sets
\overline{P}=\{\overline{p}:p\in P\}
and
\overline{Q}=\{\overline{q}:q\in Q\}
, view
(\overline{P},\overline{Q})
as an instance of the Constellation problem and solve it using the oracle. The solution consists of several shifts
s^{\prime}
with
|(\overline{P}+s^{\prime})\setminus\overline{Q}|\leq k
. We say that such a shift
s^{\prime}
is
admissible
if we can express
s
as
s=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}s_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i}
, for integers
s_{0},\dots,s_{d-1}\in[2N]
. For each admissible shift
s^{\prime}
, we report
(s_{0},\dots,s_{d-1})\in[2N]^{d}
as a solution.

For the correctness argument, we first suppose that
s=(s_{0},\dots,s_{d-1})\in[2N]^{d}
satisfies that
|(P+s)\setminus Q|\leq k
. Then it is easy to check that
|(\overline{P}+\overline{s})\setminus\overline{Q}|\leq k
and that
\overline{s}
is admissible. Hence, the algorithm reports
s
. For the other direction, suppose that the algorithm reports
s=(s_{0},\dots,s_{d-1})\in[2N]^{d}
. It does so only whenever
\overline{s}
satisfies
|(\overline{P}+\overline{s})\setminus\overline{Q}|\leq k
. We claim that then also
|(P+s)\setminus Q|\leq k
. In fact, we prove the stronger statement that for any two points
p,q\in[2N]^{d}
,
\overline{p}+\overline{s}=\overline{q}
implies that
p+s=q
. The proof is by induction on
d
, where the case
d=1
is trivial. For
d>1
, we rewrite
\overline{p}+\overline{s}=\overline{q}
as

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}p_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}s_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}q_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i}.

Taking this equation modulo
4N
, we have that
p_{0}+s_{0}\equiv q_{0}\;\,({\bmod}\>4N)
. Recall that
0\leq p_{0},s_{0},q_{0}<2N
, and thus
p_{0}+s_{0}=q_{0}
. It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}p_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}s_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}q_{i}\cdot(4N)^{i-1},

and we continue by induction to prove that
p_{i}+s_{i}=q_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,d-1
. All in all, we have proven that
p+s=q
, as claimed.

Observe that the reduction is not affected by weights, as we construct a one-to-one mapping between
P
and
\overline{P}
.

Let us finally comment on the running time. The most significant contribution is calling the Constellation oracle on
(\overline{P},\overline{Q})
, which takes time
f(n,k,(4N)^{d})
. The pre- and post-processing runs in linear time
O(nd)
, which is dominated by
f(n,k,(4N)^{d})
, simply to read the input.
∎

5.7
Approximate Translations with Mismatches to Constellation.

If the Constellation problem with up to
k
weighted mismatches is in deterministic time
f(n,k,N)
, then the approximate Point Pattern Matching problem with translations and up to
k
mismatches is in deterministic time
O(f(\epsilon^{-O(d)}n,k,O(d^{-1/2}\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}N)^{d}))
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to reduce the approximate case to the exact case and then apply the reduction from
Lemma

5.6
. To this end, let
\alpha\in[\frac{\epsilon\delta}{8d^{1/2}},\frac{\epsilon\delta}{4d^{1/2}}]
be any number representable by
O(\log(d\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}))
bits. We construct the following two integer point sets
P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}
:

•

Let
P^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{d}
be the point set obtained from by rounding
\alpha^{-1}P
to the (approximately) closest integer points. We have that
H(\alpha^{-1}P,P^{\prime})\leq d^{1/2}
and therefore
H(P,\alpha P^{\prime})\leq\alpha d^{1/2}\leq\frac{\epsilon\delta}{4}
.

•

Let
Q^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{d}
be the set of points that are in Euclidian distance at most
\alpha^{-1}(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2})\delta
to some point in
\alpha^{-1}Q
, plus possibly some points with distance at most
\alpha^{-1}(1+\frac{3\epsilon}{4})\delta
. (This slack is necessary since we are working with finite-precision arithmetic.) In particular, we have that
H(Q,\alpha Q^{\prime})\leq(1+\frac{3\epsilon}{4})\delta
.

Moreover, we define the weight
w(p^{\prime})
of a point
p^{\prime}
to be the number of points in
P
that collided into
p^{\prime}
under the rounding. We view
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime},w,k)
as an instance of exact Point Pattern Matching with translations and up to
k
weighted mismatches, and solve it using the reduction in
Lemma

5.6
. The output is the set
S^{\prime}=\{s^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}:\sum_{p^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}:p^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\not\in Q^{\prime}}w(p^{\prime})\leq k\}
. Finally, we construct and report
S=\{\alpha s^{\prime}:s^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}\}
.

Let us first argue that this output is correct (i.e., satisfies the conditions in
5.5
):

•

Soundness:
Any shift
s\in S
has the form
\alpha s^{\prime}
where
s^{\prime}\in S
. It follows that there is some subset
P_{0}^{\prime}\subseteq P^{\prime}
with weight
w(P_{0}^{\prime})\geq w(P^{\prime})-k
satisfying that
P_{0}^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Let
P_{0}\subseteq P
denote a pre-image of
P_{0}^{\prime}
under the rounding. Since the weights count the number of points before the rounding, we have that
|P_{0}|\geq w(P^{\prime})-k=|P|-k
. Moreover, the rounding guarantees that
H(P_{0},\alpha P_{0}^{\prime})\leq\alpha d^{1/2}\leq\frac{\epsilon\delta}{4}
. Therefore, using the triangle inequality we have that

\displaystyle h(P_{0}+s,Q)

\displaystyle\leq H(P_{0}+s,\alpha P_{0}^{\prime}+\alpha s^{\prime})+h(\alpha P_{0}^{\prime}+\alpha s^{\prime},\alpha Q^{\prime})+H(\alpha Q^{\prime},Q)

\displaystyle\leq H(P_{0},\alpha P_{0}^{\prime})+\alpha\cdot h(P_{0}^{\prime}+s^{\prime},Q^{\prime})+H(\alpha Q^{\prime},Q)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\epsilon\delta}{4}+0+(1+\tfrac{3\epsilon}{4})\delta

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)\delta.

•

Completeness:
Suppose that there is a shift
t\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and a subset
P_{0}\subseteq P
of size
|P_{0}|\geq|P|-k
such that
h(P_{0}+t,Q)\leq\delta
. Let
s^{\prime}
be the integer shift obtained by rounding
\alpha^{-1}t
, and let
P_{0}^{\prime}
be the set obtained by rounding
\alpha^{-1}P_{0}
. By the rounding we have
h(\alpha P_{0}^{\prime}+\alpha s^{\prime},Q)\leq\delta+2\alpha d^{1/2}\leq(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2})\delta
, and by the construction of
Q^{\prime}
, we have that
P_{0}^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Since
w(P^{\prime}\setminus P_{0}^{\prime})=|P^{\prime}|-|P_{0}^{\prime}|\leq k
, it follows that
\sum_{p^{\prime}\in P:p^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\not\in Q^{\prime}}w(p^{\prime})\leq k
. Thus
s^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
and
s:=\alpha s^{\prime}\in S
. Finally, we have that
h(P_{0}+t,P_{0}+s)\leq\alpha d^{1/2}\leq\epsilon\delta
as desired.

It remains to analyze the running time. We have that
|P^{\prime}|\leq|P|
and
|Q^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon^{-O(d)}|Q|
. Moreover, assuming that the real points in
P,Q
are representable by
\log N
bits, it follows that the integer points in
P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}
have norm bounded by
O(\alpha^{-1}N)=O(d^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}N)
. Therefore, assuming that
f(n,k,N)
is the running time of Constellation with
k
mismatches, the running time of
Lemma

5.6
is
O(f(\epsilon^{-O(d)}n,k,O(d^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}N)^{d}))
. The pre- and post-processing steps run in time
\epsilon^{-O(d)}n
and are thus negligible.
∎

5.4
Corollary for Sparse Wildcard Matching

Recall the Sparse Wildcard Matching problem: Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet, let
0\not\in\Sigma
denote a designated character and let
*\not\in\Sigma
denote a wildcard symbol. Let the
text

T\in(\Sigma\cup\{0\})^{N}
and the
pattern

P\in(\Sigma\cup\{*\})^{M}
be two strings (with
M\leq N
). We assume that
T
contains at most
n
non-zero characters and
P
contains at most
n
non-wildcard characters. The task is to report all substrings of
T
that match
P
, where we understand that the wildcard
*
matches any character in
T
. As a corollary of
Theorem

1.1
, this problem can be solved in time
O(n\polylog(N))
:

Proof of
Corollary

1.8
.

Let
\Sigma(P)
denote the non-wildcard characters appearing in the pattern string
P
. We construct sets
P_{\sigma}=\{i:P[i]=\sigma\}
and
Q_{\sigma}=\{i:T[i]=\sigma\}
for each character
\sigma\in\Sigma(P)
. We then view each pair
(P_{\sigma},Q_{\sigma})
as an instance of the Constellation problem, and compute the sets
S_{\sigma}
of all shifts
s
with
P_{\sigma}+s\subseteq Q_{\sigma}
. Finally, we report
S=\bigcap_{\sigma}S_{\sigma}
(claiming that for each
s\in S
,
P
matches
T[s\,..\,s+M)
).

It is easy to check that this algorithm is correct: Whenever
P
matches
T[s\,..\,s+M)
, then for each non-wildcard character
\sigma
we have that
P_{\sigma}+s\subseteq Q_{\sigma}
. Conversely, whenever
P_{\sigma}+s\subseteq Q_{\sigma}
holds for all
\sigma
, then all non-wildcard characters in
P
match
T[s\,..\,s+M)
. Since the wildcards match by definition,
P
entirely matches
T[s\,..\,s+M)
.

By
Theorem

1.1
, the total running time is bounded by
O(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(P)}(|P_{\sigma}|+|Q_{\sigma}|)\polylog(N))
. Since the total number non-wildcard characters in
P
is at most
n
, we have that
\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(P)}|P_{\sigma}|\leq n
. Similarly, since
T
has at most
n
non-zero characters and since
0\not\in\Sigma(P)
, we have
\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(P)}|Q_{\sigma}|\leq n
. It follows that the total running time is
O(n\polylog(N))
.
∎
