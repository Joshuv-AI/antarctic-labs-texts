Mean-field limits for Consensus-Based Optimization and Sampling
Nicolai Jurek Gerber a,, Franca Hoffmann b,, Urbain Vaes c,

Abstract
For algorithms based on interacting particle systems that admit a mean-field description, convergence analysis is often more accessible at the mean-field level. In order to transfer convergence results obtained at the mean-field level to the finite ensemble size setting, it is desirable to show that the particle dynamics converge in an appropriate sense to the corresponding mean-field dynamics. In this paper, we prove quantitative mean-field limit results for two related interacting particle systems: Consensus-Based Optimization and Consensus-Based Sampling. Our approach requires a generalization of Sznitman’s classical argument: in order to circumvent issues related to the lack of global Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients, we discard an event of small probability, the contribution of which is controlled using moment estimates for the particle systems. In addition, we present new results on the well-posedness of the particle systems and their mean-field limit, and provide novel stability estimates for the weighted mean and the weighted covariance.

Introduction

Particle methods provide a powerful paradigm for solving complex global optimization problems leading to highly parallelizable algorithms.
Despite widespread and growing adoption, theory underpinning their behavior has been mainly based on meta-heuristics. In application settings involving black-box procedures, or where gradients are too costly to obtain, one relies on derivative-free approaches instead.

This work focuses on the rigorous derivation of quantitative mean-field limits for the recently proposed derivative-free algorithms Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) and Consensus-Based Sampling (CBS), as well as their variants.
The former, initially proposed in
[
53
]
, is an algorithm for global optimization tasks,
while the latter, proposed in
[
13
]
,
can be employed for both sampling and optimization purposes.
Both methods are based on an interacting particle system with only locally,
not globally, Lipschitz coefficients and interaction kernels,
which makes it challenging to rigorously prove propagation of chaos.

CBO and CBS methods are a novel family of algorithms providing an alternative to the popular particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm
[
40
,
43
]
that is found in many optimization toolboxes.
Recent studies have shown that CBO is competitive with PSO for a number of benchmark functions
[
56
]
,
and able to solve high-dimensional optimization problems arising from machine learning
[
15
,
26
]
.
For a detailed comparison between CBO and PSO,
and additional context on the connections between the two methods,
we refer the reader to the articles
[
34
,
33
,
39
]
.

By now, CBO and CBS have also been adapted for various other tasks and application settings,
including finance
[
4
]
, rare event simulation
[
2
]
and federated learning
[
14
]
,
to mention just a few.
Compared to PSO however, this family of algorithms is more amenable to theoretical analysis.
Since current theoretical guarantees for CBO and CBS are only available at the level of the mean-field description,
providing quantitative estimates for the distance between the evolution of the interacting particles and the corresponding mean-field probability distribution is essential.

) and Consensus-Based Sampling (
Section

),
giving pointers to the relevant literature. In
Section

we present a brief review of the literature on the use of coupling methods to prove propagation of chaos
for models with non-globally Lipschitz coefficients.
Finally,
we summarize the main contributions of this work in
Section

,
and introduce the notation used throughout the paper in

.

Since Consensus-Based Sampling can be viewed as a variant on Consensus-Based Optimization,
we focus predominantly on the former and then briefly discuss the latter.

1.1
Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO)

Given an objective function
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
to be minimized,
we consider the following version of Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{(j)}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\left(X^{(j)}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+S\left(X^{(j)}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}W^{(j)}_{t},\qquad t\geqslant 0,\qquad j\in,

(1.1)

where
J>1
is a natural number,
:=\{1,\dots,J\}
and
\mu^{J}_{t}:=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\delta_{X^{(i)}_{t}}
denotes the empirical measure associated with the particle system.
For a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
the notation
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)
denotes the mean of
\mu
after reweighting by
\e^{-\beta f}
.
Its precise definition, together with its expression when
\mu=\mu^{J}_{t}
,
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu):=\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}x\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu^{J}_{t}):=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J}X^{(j)}\e^{-\beta f\bigl(X^{(j)}_{t}\bigr)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\e^{-\beta f\bigl(X^{(k)}_{t}\bigr)}}.

(1.2)

The weighted mean
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu^{J}_{t})
may be viewed as an approximation of the particle position
X^{(j)}_{t}
such that
f(X^{(j)}_{t})
is minimal.
In (
1.1
), the processes
\bigl(W^{(j)}_{t}\bigr)_{j\in}
are independent
\mathbf{R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motions and

S\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
is a globally Lipschitz continuous function.
The two diffusion types commonly studied in the literature are
isotropic diffusion
[
53
,
12
,
27
]
, with
S(u)=\sqrt{2\theta}\lvert u\rvert\I_{d}
,
and anisotropic or component-wise
[
15
,
28
]
diffusion
with
S(u)=\sqrt{2\theta}\diag(u)
.
Here
\I_{d}\in\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
is the identity matrix and
\diag\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
the operator mapping a vector onto a diagonal matrix with the vector as its diagonal.
It was shown in
[
15
]
that CBO with anisotropic diffusion is well-suited for high-dimensional global optimization problems,
for example those arising in machine learning.
Commonly a constant coefficient is included in front of the drift term in (
1.1
),
but since this coefficient can be removed by rescaling time upon redefining the diffusion parameter
\theta
,
we choose to omit it.

By design, CBO allows passage to the corresponding mean-field description,
which is not the case for the PSO algorithm.
Indeed, since the particles in (
1.1
) evolve towards the weighted mean (
1.2
)
instead of the current best particle, as is the case for Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
[
44
]
,
it is possible to formally write down the corresponding mean-field equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{t}&=-\left(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+S\left(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\overline{\rho}_{t}&=\Law(\overline{X}_{t})\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

where the weighted mean
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)
for general measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
can be interpreted as a smooth approximation of the ‘current best’ that appears in PSO
[
34
]
.
The connection between the particle system (
1.1
) and the dynamics (
1.3
),
which is of McKean–Vlasov type and comparatively simpler to analyze mathematically,
stimulated theoretical research using tools from stochastic analysis and partial differential equations
[
38
,
12
,
27
]
.
In particular, consensus formation and convergence of the CBO mean-field dynamics (
1.3
) to a consensus point as
t\to\infty
has been proven in
[
12
]
.
While the consensus point does not necessarily coincide with the global minimizer of the cost function
f
,

[
12
, Theorem 4.2]
shows that the distance between the consensus point and the true global minimizer can be made arbitrarily small by choosing a large parameter
\beta
.
The proof in
[
12
]
covers also non-convex cost functions
f
.

The convergence, in an appropriate sense, of the empirical measure associated with the CBO finite-size particle system (
1.1
) to the solution of the mean-field equation (
1.3
)
in the large particle limit
J\to\infty
was first proved rigorously in a recent paper
[
38
]
using a compactness argument,
as we discuss further in
Section

1.3
.
However, the method of proof employed in
[
38
]
does not provide an explicit rate of convergence with respect to the number of particles.
Obtaining such an explicit rate of convergence is highly desirable,
as it would enable to transfer convergence results concerning the mean-field equation (
1.3
) to the finite ensemble size setting (
1.1
) by means of a triangle inequality argument,
modulo appropriate interfacing of the metrics.
This is the approach proposed in
[
27
, Theorem 13]
,
where the authors obtain a partial mean-field result in a set of high probability,
enabling them to prove convergence with high probability to the global minimizer in the finite ensemble size setting.
Here, we provide a stronger version of the mean-field limit result given in
[
27
, Proposition 15]
, extending to more general diffusion terms and
providing scope for global convergence results in a stronger metric than convergence in probability.

A number of extensions of CBO have been proposed in the literature.
As mentioned previously,
using an anisotropic diffusion term results in a scheme which is suitable for high-dimensional state spaces,
and has been applied successfully in the training of artificial neural networks
[
15
]
.
Using componentwise geometric Brownian motion in this way allows for proving consensus formation directly at the particle level,
which was achieved in
[
35
,
36
,
48
]
.
(On this subject, we point out that the results in
[
36
]
on the proximity of the consensus point to the global minimizer concern only the best case scenario;
a more general convergence result in probability or a different metric is still lacking.)
We also mention adaptations with mini-batching
[
41
]
,
memory effects
[
57
]
, and Lévy noise
[
42
]
,
as well as extensions to multi-objective optimization
[
47
,
9
]

and global optimization with constraints
[
10
,
16
,
17
,
25
,
6
,
26
,
24
,
4
]
.
Moreover,
a variant tailored for objective functions with several minima was proposed in
[
11
]
,
and results establishing a link between CBO and PSO in the so-called small inertia limit were obtained in
[
34
,
20
]
.
Many of the recent advances concerning the mathematical analysis of CBO and its extensions were recently overviewed in
[
55
]
.
Furthermore, a free software implementation of CBO and some of its extensions was recently published
[
5
]
.

1.2
Consensus-Based Sampling (CBS)

Consensus-Based Sampling is a sampling method inspired by the Ensemble Kalman Sampler (EKS)
[
30
]
and by consensus-based approaches such as CBO.
It is based on an interacting particle system with a noise term preconditioned by the square root of the empirical covariance.
The method in the finite ensemble size and continuous time setting reads

\mathrm{d}X^{(j)}_{t}=-\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}W^{(j)}_{t},\qquad j\in,

(1.4)

with parameters
\beta,\lambda>0
.
Here, for a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
the notation
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)
denotes the covariance of
\mu
after reweighting by
\e^{-\beta f}
;
see (
1.9
) in
Section

1.5
for the precise definition.
It is possible to formally write down the mean-field equation corresponding to (
1.4
):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{t}&=-\bigl(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\rho}_{t}\right)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\overline{\rho}_{t}&=\Law(\overline{X}_{t}).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

When the parameters satisfy
\lambda=(1+\beta)^{-1}
,
the interacting particle system (
1.4
) enables to generate approximate samples from the probability distribution with density proportional to
\e^{-f}
,
that is from

\rho_{f}:=\frac{\e^{-f}}{\mathcal{Z}},\qquad\mathcal{Z}:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-f(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

More precisely, the authors of
[
13
]
show that for uniformly convex
f
and Gaussian initial data
\overline{\rho}_{0}
,
the mean-field distribution
\overline{\rho}_{t}
converges exponentially in Wasserstein distance as
t\to\infty

to a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(m_{\infty},C_{\infty})
that can be made arbitrarily close to the Laplace approximation of
\rho_{f}
by choosing a sufficiently large parameter
\beta
.
In addition, it was noticed in
[
13
]
that,
with
\lambda:=1
, the dynamics (
1.4
) behaves as a global optimization method for finding the global minimum of
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
,
just like the Consensus-Based Optimization method proposed in
[
53
]
.
Since the convergence analysis in
[
13
]
is confined to the mean-field level,
it is of interest to prove rigorously that the particle dynamics (
1.4
) converges in an appropriate sense to the corresponding mean-field dynamics (
1.5
) as
J\to\infty
. This has so far been left as an open problem.
Recently, consensus-based sampling was extended to enable sampling of multi-modal distributions in
[
11
]
,
and for rare event estimation in
[
2
]
. It has also been shown that CBS,
together with other sampling methods based on interacting particle systems,
can be metropolized to correct the sampling error
[
54
]
.

1.3
Propagation of chaos

The CBO (
1.1
) and CBS (
1.4
) dynamics can both be recast as interacting particle systems of the form

\mathrm{d}X^{(j)}_{t}=b\left(X^{(j)}_{t},\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\left(X^{(j)}_{t},\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}W^{(j)},\qquad j\in\llbracket 1,J\rrbracket,

(1.6)

with drift coefficient
b
and diffusion coefficient
\sigma
depending on
\mu
through a convolution with appropriate kernels
K_{b}
and
K_{\sigma}
:

b(x,\mu)=\widetilde{b}(x,K_{b}\star\mu),\qquad\sigma(x,\mu)=\widetilde{\sigma}(x,K_{\sigma}\star\mu).

(1.7)

Although the consensus-based dynamics can be written in the form (
1.6
),
it is not possible to do so in such a way that
\widetilde{b},\widetilde{\sigma},K_{b}
and
K_{\sigma}
are all globally Lipschitz continuous and
at the same time
K_{b}
and
K_{\sigma}
are globally bounded.
Therefore, the classical McKean’s theorem
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
is not directly applicable,
and a different approach is needed.
We briefly discuss hereafter the existing mean-field results for CBO,
noting that no results have been obtained so far for CBS.

•

In
[
38
]
,
propagation of chaos for CBO is proved through a compactness argument based on Prokhorov’s theorem.
However, this method comes without convergence rates in terms of the number of particles
J
.

•

Later,
propagation of chaos by means of synchronous coupling was shown with optimal rates in
[
25
]

for a variant of CBO constrained to hypersurfaces.

•

Recent results in
[
27
]
also show a probabilistic mean-field approximation of the form

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{2}\mathsf{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{M}}\right]\leqslant\frac{C}{J},\qquad\Omega_{M}:=\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\max\left\{\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{4},\left\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{4}\right\}\geqslant M\right\}.

It is additionally proved in
[
27
]
,
by application of Markov’s inequality,
that the set of realizations
\Omega_{M}
has probability close to 0 for large
M
.
Specifically, it holds that
\mathbf{P}(\Omega_{M})=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1})
.

•

Shortly before completing this work,
we found out that propagation of chaos for a variant of CBO had been proved using a coupling approach very recently in
[
42
]
.
In that work, the lack of global Lipschitz continuity of the weighted mean is handled by using stopping times,
similarly to the approach we present in
Section

5.1
,
which was developed independently.
There are, however, several differences between our results and those presented in
[
42
]
.
Apart from the assumptions, which are slightly less restrictive in our work,
the main technical differences are that
(i) we improve existing Wasserstein stability estimates on the weighted mean (
Corollary

3.3
),
(ii) we rely on a powerful result from the statistics literature
[
1
,
21
]
to bound one of the terms in Sznitman’s argument (
Lemma

3.7
) and
(iii) we use a concentration inequality to bound the probability that empirical measures exit a compact set (
Section

2.3
).
Combined together, these ingredients enable to obtain mean-field result with the usual algebraic rate of convergence with respect to
J
,
whereas the error scales as
\ln(\ln(J))^{-1}
in the mean-field result obtained in
[
42
]
.

•

Finally,
we mention the recent preprint
[
49
]
,
which appeared a few months after this one and proves propagation of chaos for consensus-based sampling using a compactness argument,
similar to the approach used in
[
38
]
.

1.4
Our contributions

The main contributions of this paper are the following.

•

We obtain quantitative results on the mean-field limit for CBO and CBS.
More precisely, we prove results with an explicit rate of convergence with respect to the number
J
of particles.
Investigating the roles of the dimension and other problem parameters on the value of the constant prefactor is left for future work.

•

We present two approaches for dealing with the weighted mean (in the case of CBO) and the weighted covariance (in the case of CBS).
One of these approaches is based on the careful use of indicator sets,
whereas the other is based on stopping times.
The latter approach is inspired by a method initially proposed in
[
37
]

in order to handle local Lipschitz continuity issues
in the context of numerical analysis for the Euler–Maruyama method.

•

In order to prove mean-field results,
we generalize existing theorems and prove new results on the well-posedness of the particle systems and their mean-field limit.
We also present novel stability estimates for the weighted mean and the weighted covariance.

2.1
Main assumptions

We assume throughout this paper that the diffusion operator
S\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
appearing in the CBO dynamics (
1.1
) is globally Lipschitz continuous.
For the objective function
f
,
we use the following assumption.

Assumption H
.

We assume the following properties for the target function
f
.

(H1)

The function
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is bounded from below by
f_{\star}=\inf f

and satisfies

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad|f(x)-f(y)|\leqslant L_{f}\bigl(1+|x|+|y|\bigr)^{s}|x-y|,

(2.1)

for some constants
L_{f}>0
and
s\geqslant 0
.

(H2)

There exist constants
c_{\ell},C_{\ell},c_{u},C_{u}>0
and
u\geqslant\ell\geqslant 0
such that

\displaystyle\forall x\in\real^{d},\qquad f(x)-f_{\star}\leqslant c_{u}|x|^{u}+C_{u},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\real^{d},\qquad f(x)-f_{\star}\geqslant c_{\ell}|x|^{\ell}-C_{\ell}.

(2.2b)

In the rest of this paper,
we denote by
\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
the set of objective functions that satisfy
H
with these parameters.

Remark 2.1
.

A few remarks are in order.

•

The case
\ell=u=0
corresponds to bounded
f
.

•

If
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
, then necessarily
\ell\leqslant s+1
,
because the set
\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
is empty for
\ell>s+1
.

•

In the particular case where
s=1
and either
u=\ell=0
or
u=\ell=2
,
the assumptions in
H
coincide with those in
[
12
]
.
The well-posedness and mean-field results proved in this paper generalize beyond this setting, as explained below.

Our main results hold for
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,\ell)
,
i.e. under
H
with

2.3
Key preparatory lemma

We first show a key preparatory lemma which is needed for the mean-field results.
This result is a simple corollary of Markov’s inequality.

Lemma 2.5

(Bound on the probability of large excursions)
.

Let
(Z_{j})_{j\in\mathbf{N}^{+}}
be a family of i.i.d. -valued random variables such that
\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert Z_{1}\right\rvert^{r}\right]<\infty

for some
r\geqslant 2
.
Then for all
R>\mathbf{E}\bigl[\left\lvert Z_{1}\right\rvert\bigr]
,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\forall J\in\mathbf{N}^{+},\qquad\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}Z_{j}\geqslant R\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{r}{2}}.

Proof.

This follows from a generalization of Chebychev’s inequality
[
51
, Exercise 3.21]
.
Let

X=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}Z_{j}.

By the classical Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\bigl\lvert X-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr\rvert^{r}

\displaystyle\leqslant J^{-r}C_{\rm MZ}(r)\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Bigl\lvert Z_{j}-\mathbf{E}[Z_{j}]\Bigr\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant J^{-\frac{r}{2}}C_{\rm MZ}(r)\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Bigl\lvert Z_{j}-\mathbf{E}[Z_{j}]\Bigr\rvert^{r}\right]=J^{-\frac{r}{2}}C_{\rm MZ}(r)\mathbf{E}\left[\Bigl\lvert Z_{1}-\mathbf{E}[Z_{1}]\Bigr\rvert^{r}\right],

where we used the convexity of the function
x\mapsto x^{\frac{r}{2}}
in the second inequality.
Therefore,
using the Markov inequality,
we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[X\geqslant R\right]\leqslant\mathbf{P}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert X-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr\rvert^{r}\geqslant\bigl(R-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr)^{r}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\bigl\lvert X-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr\rvert^{r}}{\bigl(R-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr)^{r}}\right]\leqslant\frac{CJ^{-\frac{r}{2}}}{\bigl(R-\mathbf{E}[X]\bigr)^{r}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now present and prove the mean-field results, starting with CBO.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\mu^{J}_{s}\bigr)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\mathsf{1}_{\Omega_{J,s}}\right]\leqslant\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\mu^{J}_{s}\bigr)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\bigr)\right\rvert^{q}\right]\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}\left(\mathbf{P}\left[\Omega_{J,s}\right]\right)^{{}^{\frac{q-p}{q}}}\leqslant 2^{\frac{(q-1)p}{q}}\left(\kappa+\overline{\kappa}\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}J^{-\frac{q-p}{2p}}.

We have thus shown that

\mathbf{E}\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\mu^{J}_{s}\bigr)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\leqslant C\mathbf{E}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{s}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s}\right\rvert^{p}+CJ^{-\frac{q-p}{2p}},

which in turn by (
2.15
) implies that

\mathbf{E}\left\lvert b\Bigl(X_{s}^{(j)},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\leqslant C\mathbf{E}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{s}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s}\right\rvert^{p}+CJ^{-\frac{q-p}{2p}}.

Bounding the second term in (
2.14
)

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
2.14
),
we have by
(c)
(see
Lemma

3.7
) that

\mathbf{E}\left\lvert b\Bigl(\overline{X}_{s}^{(j)},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}_{s}^{(j)},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}=\mathbf{E}\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\bigr)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\bigl(\overline{\rho}_{s}\bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}.

Conclusion

Combining the bounds on both terms in (
2.14
), we deduce

\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\lvert b\Bigl(X_{s}^{(j)},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}_{s}^{(j)},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\min\left\{\frac{p}{2},\frac{q-p}{2p}\right\}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,s]}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{u}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{u}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Using that
S\colon\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
is globally Lipschitz continuous, we obtain analogously

\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X_{s}^{(j)},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}_{s}^{(j)},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\min\left\{\frac{p}{2},\frac{q-p}{2p}\right\}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,s]}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{u}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{u}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Substituting in (
2.13
),
we finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\bigl\lvert X_{s}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{s}^{(j)}\bigr\rvert^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant CJ^{-\min\left\{\frac{p}{2},\frac{q-p}{2p}\right\}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,s]}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{u}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{u}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

The conclusion follows from Grönwall’s lemma,
since
\frac{q-p}{2p^{2}}\leqslant\frac{q-(2\vee p_{\mathcal{M}})}{2(2\vee p_{\mathcal{M}})^{2}}

for the value of
p
considered.
∎

3.1
Wasserstein stability estimates for the weighted moments

In this subsection, we generalize

12

to the more general conditions in
H

Proposition 3.1

(Basic stability estimates for the weighted mean and the weighted covariance)
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
with
s\geqslant 0
and
u\geqslant\ell\geqslant 0
.
Then:

(a)

For all
R>0
and for all
p\geqslant p_{\mathcal{M}}(s,\ell)
,
where
p_{\mathcal{M}}(s,\ell)
is given in (
2.3
),
there exists
L_{\mathcal{M}}=L_{\mathcal{M}}(p,R)>0
such that

\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\rvert\leqslant L_{\mathcal{M}}\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

(3.1)

(b)

For all
R>0
and for all
p\geqslant p_{\mathcal{C}}(s,\ell)
,
where
p_{\mathcal{C}}(s,\ell)
is given in (
2.3
),
there exists
L_{\mathcal{C}}=L_{\mathcal{C}}(p,R)>0
such that

\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\Bigl\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)\Bigr\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant L_{\mathcal{C}}\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

(3.2)

Proof.

The proof for both
Item

(a)
and then
Item

(b)
relies on the technical result
Lemma

A.1
in the appendix.

Proof of
Item

(a)

In the notation of
Lemma

A.1
we can write

\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)=\frac{\mu[g]}{\mu[h]},\qquad g(x):=\e^{-\beta f(x)}x,\qquad h(x)\coloneq\e^{-\beta f(x)}.

Condition
Eq.

A.1
is satisfied for
\varsigma:=s+1
if
\ell=0
or
\varsigma:=0
if
\ell>0
.
Therefore, the claim follows from
Lemma

A.1
.

Proof of
Item

(b)

First observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)=\frac{\mu[g]}{\mu[h]}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu),\qquad g(x):=\e^{-\beta f(x)}(x\otimes x),\qquad h(x):=\e^{-\beta f(x)}.

By the triangle inequality,
we have that

\bigl\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)\bigr\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant\Bigl\lVert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\Bigr\rVert_{\rm F}+\left\lVert\frac{\mu[g]}{\mu[h]}-\frac{\nu[g]}{\nu[h]}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\,.

(3.3)

We shall use that for all
(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\lVert x\otimes x-y\otimes y\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert x(x-y)^{\mathsf{T}}+(x-y)y^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert x(x-y)^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}+\left\lVert(x-y)y^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\lvert x\rvert\cdot\lvert x-y\rvert+\lvert y\rvert\cdot\lvert x-y\rvert=\bigl(\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert\bigr)\lvert x-y\rvert.

(3.4)

Using this inequality,
that
p_{\mathcal{C}}\geqslant p_{\mathcal{M}}
and
Item

(a)
,
we obtain for the first term in (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\Bigl\lVert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\Bigr\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl(\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\right\rvert\Bigr)\cdot\bigl\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant L_{\mathcal{M}}\,\Bigl(\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\right\rvert\Bigr)\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leqslant L_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}\,\Bigl(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\delta_{0})+W_{p}(\nu,\delta_{0})\Bigr)\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant 2L_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}R\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

In order to bound the second term in (
3.3
),
we again use (
3.4
) to
deduce that the function
g
satisfies the local Lipschitz estimate

\forall x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad\bigl\lvert g(x)-g(y)\bigr\rvert\leqslant L\bigl(1+|x|+|y|\bigr)^{\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert,\qquad\varsigma:=\begin{cases}s+2\quad&\text{ if\penalty\ $\ell=0$},\\
0\quad&\text{ if\penalty\ $\ell>0$}.\end{cases}

We deduce that the functions
g
and
h
satisfy the assumption (
A.1
) of
Lemma

A.1
with this
\varsigma
.
Therefore, it holds that

\displaystyle\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\left\lVert\frac{\mu[\e^{-\beta f(x)}x\otimes x]}{\mu[\e^{-\beta f(x)}]}-\frac{\nu[\e^{-\beta f(x)}x\otimes x]}{\nu[\e^{-\beta f(x)}]}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant C\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2

(Basic stability estimate for the square root covariance)
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
with
s\geqslant 0
and
u\geqslant\ell\geqslant 0
.
Then for all
R>0
and all
p\geqslant 2p_{\mathcal{M}}(s,\ell)
,
there exists
L_{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}}=L_{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}}(p,R)>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant L_{\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}}\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

(3.5)

Proof.

It is sufficient to check the claim for measures
\mu
and
\nu
of the form

\mu^{J}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{X^{(j)}},\qquad\nu^{J}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{Y^{(j)}},\qquad J\in\mathbf{N}^{+}.

(3.6)

Indeed,
assume the claim holds for all such pairs of probability measures,
fix
R>0
and take any pair of probability measures
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}
.
By
[
60
, Theorem 6.18]
,
there exists a sequence
\bigl\{(\mu^{J},\nu^{J})\bigr\}_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{+}}
in
\mathcal{P}_{p}\times\mathcal{P}_{p}

such that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{J},\mu)\to 0
and
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\nu^{J},\nu)\to 0
in the limit as
J\to\infty
.
Since convergence in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
implies convergence of the moments of order up to
p
by
[
60
, Theorem 6.9]
,
we can assume, discarding the beginning of the sequence if necessary,
that
(\mu^{J},\nu^{J})\in\mathcal{P}_{p,2R}
for all
J
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu})-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu^{J})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu^{J})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu^{J})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}+\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu^{J})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}.

The first term is bounded from above by
C\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{J},\nu^{J})
by the base case,
while the other two terms converge to 0 in the limit as
J\to\infty
by
Proposition

3.1
.
Taking the limit
J\to\infty
, we deduce that

\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant C\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

Proof of the statement for empirical measures

Fix
R>0
.
By
[
61
, p.5]
,
the Wasserstein distance between empirical measures
\mu^{J}
and
\nu^{J}
of the form (
3.6
)
is equal to

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{J},\nu^{J})=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{J}}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert X^{(j)}-Y^{\sigma(j)}\right\rvert^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\mathcal{S}_{J}
denotes the set of permutations in
\{1,\dotsc,J\}
.
Thus, the claim will follow if we can prove that,
for any pair
(\mu^{J},\nu^{J})\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}
of the form (
3.6
),
it holds that

\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu^{J})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu^{J})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant C\,\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert X^{(j)}-Y^{(j)}\right\rvert^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(3.7)

We henceforth drop the superscript
J
in
\mu^{J},\nu^{J}
for simplicity of notation,
and write
\mathbf{X}=\left(X^{(1)},\dotsc,X^{(J)}\right)
and
\mathbf{Y}=\left(Y^{(1)},\dotsc,Y^{(J)}\right)
.
First note that

\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\Bigl(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\Bigr)\otimes\Bigl(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\Bigr),\qquad w_{j}(\mathbf{X}):=\frac{\e^{-\beta f(X^{(j)})}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\e^{-\beta f(X^{(k)})}}.

It is easily seen that
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)=M_{\mathbf{X}}M_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
where
M_{\mathbf{X}}
is the following
\mathbf{R}^{d\times J}
matrix

M_{\mathbf{X}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{w_{1}(\mathbf{X})}\left(X^{(1)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\right)&\ldots&\sqrt{w_{J}(\mathbf{X})}\left(X^{(J)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Proceeding in the same manner,
we construct a matrix
M_{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbf{R}^{d\times J}
such that

This yields

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\frac{\e^{-\gamma f(x)}x}{\sqrt{\mu\left[\e^{-\beta f}\right]}}-\frac{\e^{-\gamma f(y)}y}{\sqrt{\nu\left[\e^{-\beta f}\right]}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\frac{\e^{-\gamma f(x)}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu\left[\e^{-\beta f}\right]}}-\frac{\e^{-\gamma f(y)}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)}{\sqrt{\nu\left[\e^{-\beta f}\right]}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By continuity of
t\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
, we have
\lambda_{\rm min}\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\bigr)=0
for some
t\in[0,T]
.
Then
y^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})y=0
for some
y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
such that
|y|=1
.
In particular

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lvert y^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(x-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\Bigr)\right\rvert^{2}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\overline{\rho}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=0.

Thus, we obtain
y^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(x-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\bigr)=0
for
\overline{\rho}_{t}
-almost every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
and so
y^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})=y^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{M}(\overline{\rho}_{t})

by integrating against
\overline{\rho}_{t}
.
But then,

y^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{C}(\overline{\rho}_{t})y=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lvert y^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(x-\mathcal{M}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\Bigr)\right\rvert^{2}\,\overline{\rho}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lvert y^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(x-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\Bigr)\right\rvert^{2}\,\overline{\rho}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=0,

which is a contradiction.
Thus,
C_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\succ 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which implies the second inequality in (
3.14
).
∎

4
Proof of the well-posedness results

4.1
Well-posedness for the microscopic models

For a collection of particles
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ}
, the notation
\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert
denotes the usual Euclidean norm;
that is

\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert\coloneq\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert X^{(j)}\right\rvert^{2}}.

For the proof of
Theorem

2.2
we need the following stability result, which only requires local Lipschitz continuity of
f
,
in contrast to the results in
Section

3.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ}
it holds that

\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\rvert\leqslant\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\right\rVert\leqslant\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert^{2}.

(4.1)

Moreover, if
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is locally Lipschitz continuous,
then for each
R>0
and
J\in\mathbf{N}^{+}
there exists a constant
C_{J,R}>0
such that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})\rvert\leqslant C_{J,R}\lvert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}\rvert

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})}\right\rVert_{F}\leqslant C_{J,R}\lvert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}\rvert

(4.2)

for all
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ}
satisfying
\lvert\textbf{X}\rvert,|\mathbf{Y}|\leqslant R
.

Proof.

The bound on
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})
in (
4.1
) follows immediately from the definition:
We have that

\Bigl\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\Bigr\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})X^{(j)}\right\rvert^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\left\lvert X^{(j)}\right\rvert^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert X^{(j)}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert^{2},

where we used convexity of the square function and the
fact that the weights, given hereafter,
are bounded from above by 1:

w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\coloneq\frac{\e^{-\beta f\left(X^{(j)}\right)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\e^{-\beta f\left(X^{(k)}\right)}}.

Similarly, for the weighted covariance,
it holds by the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\Bigl(X^{(j)}\otimes X^{(j)}\Bigr)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\Bigl(X^{(j)}\otimes X^{(j)}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\left\lVert X^{(j)}\otimes X^{(j)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\left\lvert X^{(j)}\right\rvert^{2}\leqslant\left\lvert\mathbf{X}\right\rvert^{2}.

The local Lipschitz estimate on
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})
in (
4.2
) is
[
12
, Lemma 2.1]
.
In order to prove the local Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})
, fix
R>0
and
J>0
.
As in the proof of
Proposition

3.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}\left(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\right)-\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}\left(Y^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}\left(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})\right)-\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}\left(Y^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\right\rvert.

Using the triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}\left\lvert X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})-Y^{(j)}+\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\left(\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}-\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}\right)\left(Y^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\right\rvert.

Since
\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function by
[
12
, Lemma 2.1]
,
it suffices to prove that
\mathbf{X}\mapsto\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}
is locally Lipschitz as well.
To this end, observe that

\displaystyle\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}-\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}=\frac{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})-w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}{\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}+\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{Y})}}.

The denominator can be controlled by noting that

\forall\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert\leqslant R,\qquad w_{j}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{\e^{-\beta f\left(X^{(j)}\right)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\e^{-\beta f\left(X^{(k)}\right)}}\geqslant\frac{1}{J}\exp\left(-\beta\left(\max_{\lvert x\rvert\leqslant R}f(x)-\min_{\lvert x\rvert\leqslant R}f(x)\right)\right).

Therefore, there exists a constant
c_{R,J}>0
such that
\sqrt{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}\geqslant c_{R,J}
for all
\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert\leqslant R
.
Since
\mathbf{X}\mapsto{w_{j}(\mathbf{X})}
is locally Lipschitz continuous as a composition of locally Lipschitz continuous functions,
the claim follows.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

We prove only the claim for the CBS dynamics (
1.4
),
since the proof for CBO is similar.
We write the system (
1.4
) in the form

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=F(\mathbf{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+G(\mathbf{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}

where
\mathbf{X}_{t}\coloneq\Bigl(X^{(1)}_{t},\dots,X^{(J)}_{t}\Bigr)
.
The drift and diffusion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{X}_{t})\coloneq-\begin{pmatrix}X^{(1)}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\\
\vdots\\
X^{(J)}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ},

and

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{X}_{t})\coloneq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}}\operatorname{diag}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)},\dots,\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)}\right)\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ\times dJ}.

By
Lemma

4.1
,
the functions
F
and
G
have sublinear growth and are locally Lipschitz continuous.
Thus, by the non-explosion criterion from stochastic Lyapunov theory
[
45
, Theorem 3.5]
,
it suffices to find a function
\phi\in C^{2}\left(\mathbf{R}^{dJ},[0,\infty)\right)
such that

•

The function
\phi
is coercive:
\phi(\mathbf{X})\to\infty
as
|\mathbf{X}|\to\infty
and

•

There exists
c>0
such that

\forall\mathbf{X}\in\mathbf{R}^{dJ},\qquad\mathcal{L}\phi(\mathbf{X})\coloneq F(\mathbf{X})\cdot\nabla\phi(\mathbf{X})+\frac{1}{2}\mytrace\left(G^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{X})\operatorname{D}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{X})G(X)\right)\leqslant c\phi(\mathbf{X}).

Take
\phi(\mathbf{X})\coloneq\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathbf{X}|^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\phi(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\right)\cdot X^{(j)}+J\lambda^{-1}\mytrace\Bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\right)\cdot X^{(j)}+\lambda^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}(\mathbf{X})\left\lvert X^{(j)}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{t}\right)\right\rvert^{2}.

From (
4.1
) it follows that this is a valid choice,
concluding the proof.
∎

4.2
Well-posedness for the mean-field SDEs

We adapt the argument from
[
12
]
,
using the more general
H
and the improved stability estimates from
Section

3.1
.
We first prove
Theorem

2.4
,
and then comment on the necessary changes for proving
Theorem

2.3
in
Remark

4.3
. In the following, we endow the vector space
\mathcal{X}\coloneq\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
with the norm

\left\lVert(m,\Gamma)\right\rVert\coloneq\left({|m|^{2}+\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert^{2}_{\rm F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The Hölder space
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}\bigl([0,T],\mathcal{X}\bigr)
is given by

C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}\bigl([0,T],\mathcal{X}\bigr)\coloneq\left\{\varphi\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{X}:\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}<\infty\right\},

where

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}\coloneq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigl\lVert\varphi(t)\bigr\rVert+[\varphi]_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\varphi]_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}\coloneq\sup_{0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T}\frac{\bigl\lVert\varphi(t)-\varphi(s)\bigr\rVert}{|t-s|^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

In order to ensure that certain matrices are positive semidefinite,
we use the map
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d\times d}
defined by
\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)=\sqrt{\Gamma\Gamma^{\mathsf{T}}}
.
We will make use of the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
f
and
\overline{\rho}_{0}
as in
Theorem

2.4
, fix
(m,\Gamma)\in C([0,T],\mathcal{X})
and
y_{0}\sim\overline{\rho}_{0}
. Then the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-(Y_{t}-m_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{R}(\Gamma_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad Y_{0}=y_{0}\,,

(4.3)

admits a unique continuous strong solution
Y\in C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, denoting by
\rho_{t}\coloneq\Law(Y_{t})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
the pointwise law of
Y_{t}
, the functions
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
and
t\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
belong to
C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d\times d})
respectively.

Proof.

The existence of a unique continuous strong solution
Y\in C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d})
to (
4.3
) follows from classical theory of stochastic differential equations
[
52
, Theorem 5.2.1]
. It remains to prove the continuity statement for the weighted mean and covariance.
For any
0\leqslant r\leqslant t\leqslant T
,
it holds by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[r,t]}\left\lvert Y_{u}-Y_{r}\right\rvert^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl(2(t-r)\bigr)^{p-1}\int_{r}^{t}\mathbf{E}\bigl\lvert Y_{u}-m_{u}\bigr\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{\rm BDG}\,2^{p-1}\left(2\lambda^{-1}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}(t-r)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\int_{r}^{t}\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{R}\bigl(\Gamma(u)\bigr)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}u.

(4.4)

Taking
r=0
in this inequality and rearranging,
we obtain that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}\left\lvert Y_{u}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant C\left(\mathbf{E}\left\lvert Y_{0}\right\rvert^{p}+\lVert m\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}^{p}+\lVert\Gamma\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}^{\frac{p}{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\lvert Y_{u}\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}u\right),

for a constant
C
depending only on
(T,\lambda,p)
.
It follows from this inequality, by Grönwall’s lemma,
that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Y_{t}|^{p}\right]\leqslant C\left(\mathbf{E}\left\lvert Y_{0}\right\rvert^{p}+\lVert m\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}^{p}+\lVert\Gamma\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)\e^{CT}<\infty.

(4.5)

Therefore, by dominated convergence,
we have for any
t\in[0,T]
that

\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{r})=\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[Y_{r}\e^{-\beta f(Y_{r})}\right]}{\mathbf{E}\left[\e^{-\beta f(Y_{r})}\right]}\xrightarrow[r\to t]{}\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[Y_{t}\e^{-\beta f(Y_{t})}\right]}{\mathbf{E}\left[\e^{-\beta f(Y_{t})}\right]}=\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}),

implying that the function
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
belongs to
C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
An analogous reasoning shows that the function
t\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
belongs to
C([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d\times d})
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

Similarly to the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
,
we first construct a map

\mathcal{T}\colon C\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right)\rightarrow C\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right)

whose fixed points correspond to solutions of (
1.5
).

Step 1: Constructing the map
\mathcal{T}

Consider the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\colon C([0,T],\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle\rightarrow C([0,T],\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle(m,\Gamma)

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t}),\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})\bigr),

where
\rho_{t}\coloneq{\rm Law}(Y_{t})
and
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the unique solution to (
4.3
).
Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, the map
\mathcal{T}
is well-defined.
Fixed points of
\mathcal{T}
correspond to solutions of the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
1.5
).
The existence of fixed points follows from applying the Leray-Schauder Fixed Point Theorem
[
32
, Chapter 11]
in the space
C\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right)
,
once we have proved that
\mathcal{T}
is compact and that the following set is bounded:

\displaystyle\Bigl\{(m,\Gamma)\in C\bigl([0,T],\mathcal{X}\bigr):\exists\xi\in[0,1]\text{ such that }(m,\Gamma)=\xi\mathcal{T}\bigl((m,\Gamma)\bigr)\Bigr\}.

(4.6)

Step 2: Showing that
\mathcal{T}
is compact

In order to prove that
\mathcal{T}
is a compact operator,
fix
R>0
and consider the ball

B_{R}\coloneq\left\{(m,\Gamma)\in C([0,T],\mathcal{X}):\left\lVert(m,\Gamma)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T])}\leqslant R\right\}.

By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, we have a compact embedding

\displaystyle C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right)\hookrightarrow C\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right).

Thus, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{T}(B_{R})
is bounded in
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}\left([0,T],\mathcal{X}\right)
.
To this end, take
(m,\Gamma)\in B_{R}

and consider the corresponding solution
Y_{t}
of (
4.3
) with pointwise law
\rho_{t}
,
and let
\varphi=\mathcal{T}(m,\Gamma)
.
We first observe that

\bigl\lVert\varphi(0)\bigr\rVert^{2}=\bigl\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{0})\bigr\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}<\infty.

The right-hand side is independent of
m
and
\Gamma
,
and it is indeed finite by
Proposition

A.4
.
Given that

\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}\leqslant\left\lVert\varphi(0)\right\rVert+[\varphi]_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}\bigl(\sqrt{T}+1\bigr),

it only remains to show that
[\varphi]_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}
is bounded by a finite constant depending on
(m,\Gamma)
only through
R
.
In view of (
4.4
) and (
4.5
),
there is a constant
L=L(R)
such that

\mathbf{E}\left\lvert Y_{t}-Y_{r}\right\rvert^{p}\leqslant L\lvert t-r\rvert^{\frac{p}{2}}.

It follows that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\rho_{t},\rho_{r})\leqslant L^{\frac{1}{p}}\lvert t-r\rvert^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
which by
Proposition

3.1
implies
the Hölder continuity with exponent
\frac{1}{2}
of the functions
t\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
and
t\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
.
This completes the proof that
\mathcal{T}
is compact.

Step 3: Showing that the set (
4.6
) is bounded

To this end, let
(m,\Gamma)\in C([0,T],\mathcal{X})
be such that

(m,\Gamma)=\xi\mathcal{T}(m,\Gamma)

(4.7)

for some
\xi\in[0,1]
,
and let
(Y_{t})
denote the corresponding solution to (
4.3
).
By (
4.7
),
the stochastic process
(Y_{t})
is also a solution to

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-\Bigl(Y_{t}-\xi\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}\xi\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad\rho_{t}\coloneq{\rm Law}(Y_{t}).

But then, reasoning as in
Section

3.2
using a Grönwall-type argument and
Proposition

A.4
with
u=\ell
,
we find that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigl\lvert Y_{t}\bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant C\mathbf{E}\lvert Y_{0}\rvert^{p}=C\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\overline{\rho}_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

By
Proposition

A.4
,
it follows that
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})
and
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})

can be bounded uniformly in
[0,T]
in terms of
\overline{\rho}_{0}
,
implying that the set (
4.6
) is indeed bounded.
Thus, we established the existence of a fixed point of
\mathcal{T}
,
and we obtain as a byproduct the moment bounds in (

).

Step 4: Showing uniqueness

For uniqueness,
let
(m_{t},\Gamma_{t})
and
(\widehat{m}_{t},\widehat{\Gamma}_{t})
be two fixed points of
\mathcal{T}
with corresponding solutions
Y_{t},\widehat{Y}_{t}\in C([0,T],\mathcal{X})
of (
4.3
) and let
z_{t}\coloneq Y_{t}-\widehat{Y}_{t}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{T}
and since
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)
is symmetric and positive semidefinite for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
we have
\mathcal{R}\left(\Gamma_{t}\right)=\Gamma_{t}
and
\mathcal{R}\bigl(\widehat{\Gamma}_{t}\bigr)=\widehat{\Gamma}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Let
\rho_{t},\widehat{\rho}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
denote the marginal laws of
Y_{t}
and
\widehat{Y}_{t}
,
respectively.
By the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality,
we have for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,t]}|z_{r}|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left\lvert Y_{r}-\widehat{Y}_{r}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\rho_{r})+\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\widehat{\rho}_{r})\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{\rm BDG}T^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(2\lambda^{-1})^{\frac{p}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{r})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\widehat{\rho}_{r})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}r.

By
Proposition

3.9
,
we have that

\eta\coloneq\inf\left\{\lambda_{\rm min}\Bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{t})\Bigr):t\in[0,T]\right\}>0.

By virtue of the van Hemmen–Ando inequality
[
59
, Proposition 3.2]
,
it holds for any unitarily invariant norm,
thus in particular for the Frobenius norm, that

\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{r})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\widehat{\rho}_{r})}\right\rVert_{\rm F}\leqslant\eta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\rho_{r})-\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\widehat{\rho}_{r})\right\rVert_{\rm F}\,.

Using
Proposition

3.1
and noting that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\rho_{r},\widehat{\rho}_{r})^{p}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\lvert z_{r}\rvert^{p}
by definition of the Wasserstein distance,
we conclude that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,t]}\bigl\lvert z_{r}\bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,r]}\bigl\lvert z_{u}\bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}r.

By Grönwall’s lemma, and given that
z_{0}=0
,
it follows that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lvert z_{t}\rvert^{p}\right]=0,

which concludes the proof of uniqueness.

Step 5: Showing that
\overline{\rho}_{t}
solves the nonlocal Fokker–Planck equation (
2.7
)

This part of the proof is standard,
and we present only a formal derivation.
Let
(m,\Gamma)\in C([0,T],\mathcal{X})
denote the fixed point of
\mathcal{T}
,
in which case
\mathcal{R}(\Gamma_{t})=\Gamma_{t}
,
and let
\overline{Y}_{t}
denote the corresponding solution to (
4.3
).
Denote also by
\overline{\rho}_{t}
the law of
\overline{Y}_{t}
.
For a smooth function
\phi\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
with compact support,
it holds by Itô’s lemma that

\mathbf{E}\phi\left(\overline{Y}_{t}\right)-\mathbf{E}\phi\left(\overline{Y}_{r}\right)=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{r}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{u}\phi\left(\overline{Y}_{u}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u\right],

(4.8)

where

\mathcal{L}_{u}\phi(y)\coloneq-\bigl(y-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\rho}_{u}\right)\bigr)\cdot\nabla\phi(y)+\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\rho}_{u}\right):\operatorname{D}^{2}\phi(y).

It follows from (
4.8
) that
\overline{\rho}_{t}
satisfies,
for all smooth compactly supported
\phi\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\to\mathbf{R}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,\overline{\rho}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{t}\phi\,\overline{\rho}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x),

(4.9)

which is indeed a weak formulation of the Fokker–Planck equation (
2.5
).
For rigorous uniqueness and existence results for the Fokker–Planck equation (
4.9
)
as well as a more precise discussion of its connection with (
4.3
),
see for example
[
29
]
.
∎

By, among other things, replacing
\sqrt{2\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{R}(\Gamma_{t})}

S\left(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{t})\right)
,

. See also

]
, where a weaker version of

2.3

p=4
and

.

3.1
.

This section is organized as follows.
First, in
Section

,

Theorems

2.6
and
2.10
.
Then, in
Section

5.2
,
we show how the stopping time approach presented in
Section

5.1
can be employed in order to obtain a mean-field result in the setting where
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
with
u>\ell>0
,
in which the improved stability estimates in
Corollary

3.3
are no longer valid.

5.1
Proving mean-field results using stopping times

In this section, we present an alternative approach for proving
Theorem

2.6
and
Theorem

2.10
.
This approach is based on stopping times,
and inspired by a method employed in
[
37
]
to handle local Lipschitz continuity issues in the context of numerical analysis for the Euler–Maruyama method.
A similar approach was employed in
[
42
]
to obtain a non-quantitative mean-field estimate,
and more recently in
[
58
]
to prove sharp propagation of chaos estimates for an ensemble sampler related to the ensemble Kalman filter
[
30
,
31
]
.
Before presenting the proof,
we briefly discuss the main differences between this approach and the indicator set approach presented in
Section

2
.

•

The main advantage of the approach used in
Section

2
is that the event
\Omega_{J,t}
for which our stability estimates do not apply
can be time-dependent.
For the proof of
Theorem

2.6
,
this flexibility is in fact not necessary,
as we could have employed instead of
\Omega_{J,t}
in (
2.16
) the event

\Omega_{J}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\frac{1}{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}(\omega)\right\rvert^{p}\geqslant R\right\}.

(5.1)

In certain contexts, however,
being able to exclude a time-dependent event could be very useful.

•

The stopping time approach presented hereafter does not enjoy this flexibility,
but is convenient in settings where moments of both
\mu^{J}
and
\overline{\mu}^{J}
need to be controlled in order to apply the stability estimates obtained in
Proposition

3.1
for the weighted moments.
This is illustrated in
Section

5.2
.

We illustrate the stopping times approach only for
Theorem

2.10
,
noting that
Theorem

2.6
can be proved in exactly the same manner.
Furthermore, we assume for simplicity that
\overline{\rho}_{0}
has infinitely many moments.

Alternative proof of
Theorem

2.10
.

For
R>0
, let
\theta_{J}(R)
be the stopping time

\theta_{J}(R):=\inf\Bigl\{t\geqslant 0:\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{p}\geqslant R\Bigr\},

(5.2)

Note that the event
\{\theta_{J}\leqslant T\}
coincides with the event
\Omega_{J}
given in (
5.1
).
By Hölder’s inequality,
it holds for all
q>p
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Big\rvert^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Big\rvert^{p}\mathsf{1}_{\theta_{J}(R)>T}\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Big\rvert^{p}\mathsf{1}_{\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}\Big\rvert^{p}\right]+\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Big\rvert^{q}\right]\right)^{\frac{p}{q}}\Bigl(\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]\Bigr)^{\frac{q-p}{q}}.

(5.3)

Bounding the first term in (
5.3
)

We again employ an adapted version of Sznitman’s argument.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\left\lvert X_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}\right\rvert^{p}\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}b\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\rvert^{p}

\displaystyle+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\sigma\Bigl(X_{s}^{(j)},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right\rvert^{p}.

Let us introduce the martingale

M_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma\Bigl(X_{s}^{(j)},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

By Doob’s optional stopping theorem
[
37
, Theorem 3.3]
,
see also
[
23
, Equation 2.29, p.285]
,
the stopped process
(M_{t\wedge\theta_{J}})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a martingale,
with a quadratic variation process given by the stopped quadratic variation process of
M_{t}
,
i.e. by
(\langle M\rangle_{t\wedge\theta_{J}})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
Therefore, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality,
we have for all
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\bigr\rvert^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant(2T)^{p-1}\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\left\lvert b\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{\rm BDG}2^{p-1}T^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5.4)

Applying the triangle inequality gives that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\bigl\lvert b\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\bigr\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left\lvert b\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left\lvert b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-b\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rvert^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5.5)

Similarly, for the diffusion term,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5.6)

Next, we need to bound the terms on the right-hand side of (
5.5
) and (
5.6
).
Since the drift terms and the diffusion terms are bounded in a similar manner, we only present the bounds for the diffusion terms.

•

For the first diffusion term,
we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}

\displaystyle=\left(2\lambda^{-1}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\biggl\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\right)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\right)}\biggr\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}.

Using
Corollary

3.3
, together with the fact

\mathbf{E}\Bigl[\mathcal{W}_{p}\bigl(\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\bigr)^{p}\Bigr]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\left\lvert X^{(k)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(k)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\right\rvert^{p}\right]=\mathbf{E}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\right\rvert^{p},

we deduce that

\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\mu^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\leqslant C\mathbf{E}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\right\rvert^{p}.

•

For the other diffusion term,
we use
Lemma

3.8
.
The assumptions of this lemma are satisfied,
since it holds that
\overline{\rho}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for all
q>0
by
Theorem

2.4
,
and additionally
\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\overline{\rho}_{s})\succ 0
by
Proposition

3.9
.
We deduce that

\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}=\left(2\lambda^{-1}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\mu}^{J}_{s}\right)}-\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\left(\overline{\rho}_{s}\right)}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}.

Combining the bounds on both terms,
we deduce that

\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\left\lVert\sigma\Bigl(X^{(j)}_{s},\mu^{J}_{s}\Bigr)-\sigma\Bigl(\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s},\overline{\rho}_{s}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}s\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,s]}\left\lvert X^{(j)}_{u\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{u\wedge\theta_{J}}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

Applying a similar reasoning for the drift terms,
we finally obtain from (
5.4
) that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{s\wedge\theta_{J}}\Big\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{u\in[0,s]}\Bigl\lvert X_{u\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{u\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

By Grönwall’s inequality,
this implies that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}.

Bounding the second term in (
5.3
)

We have by
Lemma

3.5
and (
2.6
)
that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\big\rvert^{q}\right]\leqslant 2^{q-1}\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}\big\rvert^{q}\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\big\rvert^{q}\right]\right)\leqslant 2^{q}\kappa.

In addition, by
Lemma

2.5
, it holds for any sufficiently large fixed
R
,
that for all
\alpha>0
there is
C_{\alpha}
such that

\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{p}\geqslant R\right]\leqslant C_{\alpha}J^{-\alpha}.

Conclusion

We finally obtain from (
5.3
) that

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big\lvert X^{(j)}_{t}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Big\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant C\left(J^{-\frac{p}{2}}+J^{-\frac{\alpha(q-p)}{q}}\right),

which concludes the proof since
q
and
\alpha
can be taken arbitrarily large.
∎

5.2
Handling the case where
u>\ell>0
in
H

Our main results stand only when
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,\ell,u)
with
u=\ell
.
This is a convenient setting because it then holds by
Proposition

A.4
that
the
p
-th moment of the reweighted measure
L_{\beta}\mu
is controlled by the
p
-th moment of
\mu
,
which is key in order
to prove well-posedness of the mean-field system,
to obtain moment estimates for empirical measures,
and, most importantly, to improve the Wasserstein stability estimates for the weighted mean and weighted covariance,
see
Corollary

3.3
.
In the interest of simplicity, we refrained from stating precise results for the cases
u>\ell=0

and
u>\ell>0
.
We do not address the former setting,
but note that a quantitative mean-field estimate can still be obtained in the latter.
The main ingredients to this end are the following:

•

Moment estimates for the empirical measure and well-posedness of the mean-field equation
can be obtained by using
Proposition

A.4
together with the Bihari–LaSalle inequality instead of Grönwall’s inequality,
using the same reasoning as in
Lemma

3.5
and
Section

4
,
respectively.
In contrast with the setting
\ell=u
,
these results hold only up to some finite time
T<T_{+}
, where
T_{+}\in(0,\infty)
is the time at which the bounds given by the Bihari–LaSalle inequality become singular.

•

In order to obtain a mean-field result up to some time
T<T_{+}
,
the approach presented in
Section

5.1
can be used,
but with the stopping time
\theta_{J}:=\tau_{J}\wedge\overline{\tau}_{J}
,
where

\displaystyle\tau_{J}(R)

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigl\{t\geqslant 0:\mu_{t}^{J}\bigl[\lvert x\rvert^{p}\bigr]\geqslant R\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}_{J}(R)

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigl\{t\geqslant 0:\overline{\mu}_{t}^{J}\bigl[\lvert x\rvert^{p}\bigr]\geqslant R\Bigr\}.

Employing this stopping time instead of (
5.2
) enables to use the basic stability estimates for the weighted moments of
Proposition

3.1
,
which are valid even when
\ell<u
,
instead of the improved stability estimates in
Corollary

3.3
,
which require that
\ell=u
.
It is then simple to obtain a propagation of chaos estimate for the stopped particle system,
of the form

\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}-\overline{X}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}^{(j)}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}.

In order to complete the proof as in
Section

5.1
,
it remains to bound the probability
\mathbf{P}\left[\theta_{J}\leqslant T\right]
,
which can be achieved by noticing that

\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]=\mathbf{P}\left[\tau_{J}(R)\leqslant T<\overline{\tau}_{J}(R)\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[\overline{\tau}_{J}(R)\leqslant T\right].

Using the almost sure continuity of the solution to the interacting particle system by
Theorem

2.2
,
together with the triangle inequality,
we bound the first probability as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\tau_{J}\leqslant T<\overline{\tau}_{J}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr\rvert^{p}=R\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{2^{p-1}}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Bigr\rvert^{p}+\frac{2^{p-1}}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant R\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant\frac{R}{2^{p}}\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant\frac{R}{2^{p}}\right],

where we used that
\mathbf{P}[A+B\geqslant k]\leqslant\mathbf{P}[A\geqslant k/2]+\mathbf{P}[B\geqslant k/2]

for any two real-valued random variables
A
and
B
,
because
\{A+B\geqslant k\}\subset\{A\geqslant k/2\}\cup\{B\geqslant k/2\}
.
A similar trick, of bounding the probability of an event associated with a stochastic system
in terms of a similar event associated with a simpler system and the discrepancy between the two systems,
is employed in
[
22
, Theorem 2.10]
.
The probability
\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}\leqslant T]
can then be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left[\tau_{J}(R)\leqslant T<\overline{\tau}_{J}\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[\overline{\tau}_{J}(R)\leqslant T\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant\frac{R}{2^{p}}\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant\frac{R}{2^{p}}\right].

Using Markov’s inequality for the second term,
we then obtain

\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]\leqslant 2\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\geqslant\frac{R}{2^{p}}\right]+\frac{2^{p}}{R}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Bigl\lvert X^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t\wedge\theta_{J}}\Bigr\rvert^{p}\right].

(5.7)

By
Lemma

2.5
,
For any
\alpha>0
there exists
C_{\alpha}>0
such that
the first term is bounded from above by
C_{\alpha}J^{-\alpha}
for all
J\in\mathbf{N}^{+}
,
whereas the second term can be bounded by
CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}}
as in the proof presented in
Section

5.1
.
Thus, the probability
\mathbf{P}[\theta_{J}(R)\leqslant T]
scales as
J^{-\min\left\{\alpha,\frac{p}{2}\right\}}
,
instead of
J^{-\alpha}
in the case where
u=\ell
.
As a consequence,
we obtain a mean-field result of the type

\forall J\in\mathbf{N}^{+},\qquad\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lvert X_{t}^{(j)}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant CJ^{-\frac{p}{2}\left(\frac{q-p}{q}\right)}.

Since
q
was arbitrary given the assumption that
\overline{\rho}_{0}
has infinitely many moments,
it follows that for every
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

\forall J\in\mathbf{N}^{+},\qquad\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lvert X_{t}^{(j)}-\overline{X}^{(j)}_{t}\right\rvert^{p}\right]\leqslant C_{\varepsilon}J^{-\frac{p}{2}\left(1-\varepsilon\right)}.

Hence, we recover almost the classical Monte Carlo rate.
By using the same method as in
[
58
]
,
it is in fact possible to prove that this statement holds also for
\varepsilon=0
,
but for conciseness we do not present the details here.

Appendix A
Further technical results

A.1
Stability estimates for ratios of integrals

Lemma A.1
.

For a real finite-dimensional vector space
\mathcal{V}
with norm
\left\lVert\mathord{\color[rgb]{0.67,0.67,0.67}\bullet}\right\rVert
,
let
g\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
and
h\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be functions such that the following condition is satisfied for some
\varsigma\geqslant 0
and
L>0
:

\forall x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad\bigl\lVert g(x)-g(y)\bigr\rVert\vee\bigl\lvert h(x)-h(y)\bigr\rvert\leqslant L\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert.

(A.1)

Then for all
p\geqslant\varsigma+1
and all
R>0
,
there exist a constant
C=C(p,R)
such that

\displaystyle\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\real^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\real^{d}),\qquad\left\lVert\frac{\mu[g]}{\mu[h]}-\frac{\nu[g]}{\nu[h]}\right\rVert\leqslant C\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).\qquad

Proof.

Fix
p\geqslant\varsigma+1
and
R>0
.

Step 1

Let us first generalize
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
by showing that there exists a constant
\eta=\eta(p,R)>0
such that

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geqslant\eta.

(A.2)

Define
B_{K}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}:\lvert x\rvert\leqslant K\}
where
K\coloneq 2^{1/p}R
.
By Markov’s inequality, we have

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\mu\left(B_{K}\right)\geqslant 1-\mu\left(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus B_{K}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{R^{p}}{K^{p}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore we obtain

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geqslant\int_{B_{K}}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\min\bigl\{h(x):x\in B_{K}\bigr\}=:\eta>0,

since
h
is continuous by
Eq.

A.1
.
This proves (
A.2
).

Step 2

Fix probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}
.
We omit the integration domains for conciseness.
For any coupling
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
,
we have by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{\mu[g]}{\mu[h]}-\frac{\nu[g]}{\nu[h]}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\frac{\iint g(x)-g(y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)}{\mu[h]}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\int g\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\rVert\cdot\left\lvert\frac{\iint h(x)-h(y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)}{\mu[h]\nu[h]}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant L\left(\eta^{-1}+\eta^{-2}\int\left\lVert g\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\iint\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y).

By (
A.1
), there exists
C>0
such that
\left\lVert g(x)\right\rVert\leqslant C\bigl(1+|x|\bigr)^{\varsigma+1}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
The claim then follows by first applying Hölder’s inequality to obtain the bound

\iint\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\leqslant\left(\iint\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\frac{\varsigma p}{p-1}}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\left(\iint\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

noting that
\frac{\varsigma p}{p-1}\leqslant p
under the assumption that
p\geqslant\varsigma+1
,
and then infimizing over
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
g\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{m\times n}
,
h\colon\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{S}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}

be functions such that the following conditions hold.

(a)

There is
\varsigma\geqslant 0
and
L>0
such that

\forall x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad\bigl\lVert g(x)-g(y)\bigr\rVert\vee\bigl\lvert h(x)-h(y)\bigr\rvert\leqslant L\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert.

(b)

The map
\mathcal{S}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
satisfies

\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\real^{d})\times\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\real^{d}),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{S}(\mu)-\mathcal{S}(\nu)\rvert\leqslant L_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

Then, for all
R>0
and
p\geqslant 2\varsigma+2
,
there exist a constant
C>0
such that for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}

and all couplings
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left(\iint_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left\lVert\frac{g(x)\mathcal{S}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{g(y)\mathcal{S}(\nu)}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right\rVert^{2}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/2}\leqslant C\,\left(\int\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/p}.

(A.3)

Proof.

Fix
p\geqslant 2\varsigma+2
and
R>0
.
Again, there is a constant
\eta=\eta(R,p)>0
such that (
A.2
) holds.
From the triangle inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{g(x)\mathcal{S}(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{g(y)\mathcal{S}(\nu)}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\frac{g(x)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}\Bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mu)-\mathcal{S}(\nu)\Bigr)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}+\left\lVert\Bigl(g(x)-g(y)\Bigr)\frac{\mathcal{S}(\nu)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\lVert{g(y)\mathcal{S}(\nu)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)},

where, by a slight abuse of notation,
we wrote
g(x)
and
g(y)
in order to refer to the functions
(x,y)\mapsto g(x)
and
(x,y)\mapsto g(y)
,
respectively.
In the rest of the proof,
we denote by
C
any constant depending only on the parameters
(R,L,\varsigma,\eta,p)
.

Bounding the first term

It follows from
Item

(a)
that
\left\lVert g(x)\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant C(1+|x|)^{2\varsigma+2}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, for a constant
C
.
Using
Item

(b)
, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\left(\iint\left\lVert\frac{g(x)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}\bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mu)-\mathcal{S}(\nu)\bigr)\right\rVert^{2}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\mu[\lVert g(x)\rVert^{2}]}{\mu[h]}}\bigl\lvert\mathcal{S}(\mu)-\mathcal{S}(\nu)\bigr\rvert\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{C}{\eta}}L_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu).

Bounding the second term

For the second term, we use
Item

(a)
to find

\forall x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad\lvert g(x)-g(y)\rvert^{2}\leqslant L^{2}\left(1+|x|+|y|\right)^{2\varsigma}\lvert x-y\rvert^{2}.

Using Hölder’s inequality,
we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\bigl\lVert g(x)-g(y)\bigr\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leqslant L\left(\int\Bigl(1+|x|+|y|\Bigr)^{\frac{2\varsigma p}{p-2}}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2p}}\left(\int\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(\int\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where we used that
\frac{2\varsigma p}{p-2}\leqslant p
since
p\geqslant 2+2\varsigma
.
In addition, from
Item

(b)
we have

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p,R}(\real^{d}),\qquad\big\lvert\mathcal{S}(\mu)\big\rvert\leqslant\big\lvert\mathcal{S}(\delta_{0})\big\rvert+L_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\delta_{0},\mu)\leqslant\big\lvert\mathcal{S}(\delta_{0})\big\rvert+L_{\mathcal{S}}R,

(A.4)

and so we conclude that

\left\lVert\Bigl(g(x)-g(y)\Bigr)\frac{\mathcal{S}(\nu)}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leqslant C\left(\int\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/p}.

(A.5)

Bounding the third term

Using again (
A.4
),
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert{g(y)\mathcal{S}(\nu)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}\left\lvert\mathcal{S}(\nu)\right\rvert\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right)\leqslant C\left\lvert\frac{\sqrt{\mu[h]}-\sqrt{\nu[h]}}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right\rvert.

Since
\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}=(a-b)/(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})
for all
a,b>0
,
we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lVert{g(x)\mathcal{S}(\nu)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu[h]}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu[h]}}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\eta^{-{\frac{3}{2}}}\iint\bigl\lvert h(x)-h(y)\bigr\rvert\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\iint\bigl(1+|x|+|y|\bigr)^{\varsigma}|x-y|\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y).

By employing Hölder’s inequality, the integral can be bounded by the right-hand side of (
A.5
) up to multiplication by a constant.

Conclusion

The claim follows then by combining the bounds on the three terms,
and noting,
for the first term,
that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)
is bounded from above by the bracket on the right-hand side of (
A.3
)
by definition of the Wasserstein distance.
∎

A.2
Bound on weighted moments

If the function
f
is bounded from below and above,
it is trivial to show that

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}\leqslant C\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

for a constant
C
depending only on
\beta
and
f
.
When
f
is unbounded,
proving an inequality of this type is more involved.

To this end,
we first state and prove the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma A.3
.

For all
p,q,\beta>0
,
there is
C=C(p,q,\beta)
such that

\forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbf{R}^{+}),\qquad\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{p}\,\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}\leqslant C\left(1+\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{q}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{p}{q}},

(A.6)

where
\mathbf{R}^{+}\coloneq[0,\infty)
.
The constant is given by

C(q,p,\beta)=\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{q}}\right)^{-1}(p\beta^{-1})^{p}\e^{2\beta-p}.

Proof.

Fix
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{+})
.
If the right-hand side of (
A.6
) is infinite,
then the inequality (
A.6
) holds with any constant
C
,
so we assume from now on that

\lambda\coloneq\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{q}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty.

Step 1

Suppose first that
\lambda\leqslant 1
.
Then, by Markov’s inequality,
it holds that

\nu\bigl([0,2]\bigr)=1-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}\setminus[0,2]}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2^{q}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{q}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2^{q}}.

Using this inequality and the bound
y^{p}\e^{-\beta y}\leqslant(p\beta^{-1})^{p}\e^{-p}
,
which is valid for all
y\in\mathbf{R}^{+}
and obtained by maximization of
y\mapsto y^{p}\e^{-\beta y}
,
we have that

\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{p}\,\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}\leqslant\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}(p\beta^{-1})^{p}\e^{-p}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{[0,2]}\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{q}}\right)^{-1}(p\beta^{-1})^{p}\e^{2\beta-p}.

It follows that (
A.6
) is satisfied in this case.

Step 2

Assume now that
\lambda\geqslant 1
,
and let
\pi
denote the pushforward of
\nu
under the contraction
x\mapsto\lambda^{-1}x
.
Then, applying a change of variable,
using
Step 3
and using
Step 1
step,
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}y^{p}\,\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta y}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{p}\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}z^{p}\,\e^{-\beta\lambda z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta\lambda z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda^{p}\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}z^{p}\,\e^{-\beta z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}\leqslant\lambda^{p}C(p,q,\beta),

(A.7)

which is the required inequality.

Step 3

In the first inequality of (
A.7
),
we used that,
for any increasing function
g\colon\mathbf{R}^{+}\to\mathbf{R}
,
the function

F(\gamma)\coloneq\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}g(z)\,\e^{-\gamma z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\gamma z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}

is a non-increasing function of
\gamma
.
This follows from
[
13
, Lemma A.3]
,
and could also be proved by showing that
F^{\prime}(\gamma)
is non-positive for all
\gamma\geqslant 0
.
A simple calculation indeed gives that

F^{\prime}(\gamma)=-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}g(z)\left(z-\overline{z}\right)\,L_{\gamma}\pi(\mathrm{d}z),\qquad L_{\gamma}\pi(\mathrm{d}z)\coloneq\frac{\e^{-\gamma z}\pi(\mathrm{d}z)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\gamma z}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)},\qquad\overline{z}\coloneq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}z\,L_{\gamma}\pi(\mathrm{d}z).

By decomposing the integration domain into the union
[0,\overline{z})\cup\{\overline{z}\}\cup(\overline{z},\infty)
,
and using the fact that
g
is non-decreasing,
we have

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\int_{[0,\overline{z})}g(z)\left(z-\overline{z}\right)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z)-\int_{(\overline{z},\infty)}g(z)\left(z-\overline{z}\right)\,L_{\gamma}\pi(\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle\leqslant-g(\overline{z})\int_{[0,\overline{z})\cup(\overline{z},\infty)}\left(z-\overline{z}\right)\,L_{\gamma}\pi(\mathrm{d}z)=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Armed with this result,
we are ready to prove the following generalization of
[
12
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Proposition A.4
.

Suppose that
f
is bounded from below by
f_{\star}
and that
there are real numbers
u\geqslant\ell>0
and positive constants

c_{u},C_{u},c_{\ell},C_{\ell}
such that

\forall x\in\mathbf{R}^{d},\qquad\left\{\begin{aligned} f(x)-f_{\star}\leqslant c_{u}|x|^{u}+C_{u}\\
f(x)-f_{\star}\geqslant c_{\ell}|x|^{\ell}-C_{\ell}.\end{aligned}\right.

(A.8)

Then for all
p,q>0

there are constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
depending on
(p,q,u,\ell,c_{u},C_{u},c_{\ell},C_{\ell})
such that

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}\leqslant\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{q}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{pu}{q\ell}}

(A.9)

Furthermore, if
u=\ell
and
p\leqslant q
,
then this statement holds with
C_{1}=0
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
f_{\star}=0
.
By the second inequality in (
A.8
), we have that

\lvert x\rvert^{p}=\left(\lvert x\rvert^{\ell}\right)^{\frac{p}{\ell}}\leqslant\left(\frac{f(x)+C_{\ell}}{c_{\ell}}\right)^{\frac{p}{\ell}}.

(A.10)

For convenience,
we denote by
C
any constant depending only on
(p,q,\ell,u,c_{\ell},C_{\ell},c_{u},C_{u})
.
From (
A.10
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\bigl(f(x)+1\bigr)^{\frac{p}{\ell}}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}

\displaystyle=C\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\bigl(y+1\bigr)^{\frac{p}{\ell}}\,\e^{-\beta y}\,f_{\sharp}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\e^{-\beta y}\,f_{\sharp}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)},

where
f_{\sharp}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{+})
is the pushforward of
\mu
by
f
.
Using
Lemma

A.3

and then the first inequality in (
A.8
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left(1+\int_{\mathbf{R}^{+}}\lvert y\rvert^{\frac{q}{u}}\,f_{\sharp}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{\frac{pu}{q\ell}}

\displaystyle=C\left(1+\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\bigl\lvert f(x)\bigr\rvert^{\frac{q}{u}}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{pu}{q\ell}}\leqslant C\left(1+\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\bigl\lvert x\bigr\rvert^{q}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{pu}{q\ell}},

which completes the proof of (
A.9
).

Proof that (
A.9
) holds with
C_{1}=0
when
\ell=u
and
q\geqslant p

The case
q>p
follows from the case
q=p
,
so we assume the latter.
Suppose for contradiction that this was not true.
Then there would exist a sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{N}}
of probability measures such that

\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}\geqslant i\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x).

(A.11)

By the first part of the proof,
this inequality implies that

\frac{C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}\geqslant i,

and so the integral in this expression necessarily converges to 0 in the limit as
i\to\infty
.
In turn, this implies that the sequence
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbf{N}}
converges in distribution to the Dirac measure at
0
when
i\to\infty
.
But then it holds that

\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{-1}\leqslant\frac{\e^{-\beta f_{\star}}}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}\xrightarrow[i\to\infty]{}\e^{-\beta\bigl(f_{\star}-f(0)\bigr)},

which contradicts (
A.11
).
∎

Remark A.5
.

When
p=q>0
,
Proposition

A.4
implies that

\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\qquad\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}\leqslant\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert^{p}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{\frac{u}{\ell}}.

(A.12)

We motivate in this remark that the exponent
\frac{u}{\ell}
is optimal.
Indeed, let
p=1
and consider the function
f\colon\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{R}
given by
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\ell}+\lvert x_{2}\rvert^{u+\varepsilon}
,
for any
u\geqslant\ell>0
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then for the probability measures

\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{i^{u}}\delta_{\left(i^{u},0\right)}+\left(1-\frac{1}{i^{u}}\right)\delta_{\left(0,i^{\ell}\right)},

it holds that

\frac{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\lvert x\rvert\,\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\e^{-\beta f(x)}\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)}\sim i^{u}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\int_{\mathbf{R}^{2}}\lvert x\rvert\,\mu_{i}(\mathrm{d}x)\sim i^{\ell}\qquad\text{ as $i\to\infty$},

where
\sim
signifies asymptotic equivalence.
This example suggests that
Lemma

A.3
is sharp.

A.3
Generalized Wasserstein stability estimates

By adapting the proof of
Proposition

3.1
,
it is simple to prove Wasserstein stability estimates with different values of
p
on the left and right sides of the inequality.
More precisely, we have the following statement.

Lemma A.6
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{A}(s,0,u)
.
Then

•

for all
R>0
and
p>1
, there is
C=C(p,R)
such that

\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{\varrho,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{\varrho,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{M}_{\beta}(\nu)\rvert\leqslant C\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\varrho\coloneq\frac{(s+1)p}{p-1}

(A.13)

•

for all
R>0
and
p>1
, there is
C=C(p,R)
such that

\forall(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{\varrho,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr)\times\mathcal{P}_{\varrho,R}\bigl(\real^{d}\bigr),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\mu)-\mathcal{C}_{\beta}(\nu)\rvert\leqslant C\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu),\qquad\varrho\coloneq\frac{(s+2)p}{p-1}

(A.14)

Remark A.7
.

For
p=s+2
, the bound (
A.13
) coincides with (
3.1
),
and for
p=s+3
, the bound (
A.14
) coincides with (
3.2
).
