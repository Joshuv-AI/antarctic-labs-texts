Exact capacity of the wide hidden layer treelike neural networks with generic activations
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

Recent progress in studying
treelike committee machines
(TCM) neural networks (NN) in
[
36
,
40
]
showed that the Random Duality Theory (RDT) and its a
partially lifted
(pl RDT) variant are powerful tools that can be used for very precise networks capacity analysis. The initial considerations from
[
36
,
40
]
, related to the famous
sign
activations, were then extended to more general activations in
[
42
]
, where particularly elegant results were obtained for
any
even number of the
quadratically
and
ReLU
activated hidden layer neurons,
d
. While the results of
[
42
]
are in principle applicable to any type of activations a significant amount of numerical work is often needed to make them practically usable. Here, we consider
wide
hidden layer networks and uncover that certain aspects of such difficulties miraculously disappear. In particular, we employ recently developed
fully lifted
(fl) RDT to characterize the
wide
(
d\rightarrow\infty
) TCM nets capacity. We obtain explicit, closed form, capacity characterizations for a very generic class of the hidden layer activations. While the utilized approach significantly lowers the amount of the needed numerical evaluations, the ultimate fl RDT usefulness and success still require a solid portion of the residual numerical work. To get the concrete capacity values, we take four very famous activations examples:
ReLU
,
quadratic
,
erf
, and
tanh
. After successfully conducting all the residual numerical work for all of them, we uncover that the whole lifting mechanism exhibits a remarkably rapid convergence with the relative improvements no better than
\sim 0.1\%
happening already on the 3-rd level of lifting. As a convenient bonus, we also uncover that the capacity characterizations obtained on the first and second level of lifting precisely match those obtained through the statistical physics replica theory methods in
[
53
]
for the generic and in
[
2
]
for the ReLU activations.

Index Terms: Wide TCM neural networks; Capacity; Fully lifted random duality theory; ReLU, quadratic, erf, tanh
.

1
Introduction

Development of machine learning (ML) and neural networks (NN) concepts experienced a rapid progress over the last 15-20 years. Larger than ever need for efficient handling and interpretation of huge data sets stimulated the invention of many fundamental algorithmic NN breakthroughs. Such an algorithmic progress necessarily dictated advancement of the accompanying analytical/theoretical justification methodologies. Along the paths of both algorithmic and theoretical advancements, many new concepts have been developed and many old ones have been revisited and brought to practical usability. We are here interested in, possibly, the most important of them all, namely the so-called, network’s
memory capacity
(see, e.g.,
[
26
,
9
,
49
,
48
,
47
,
8
,
22
,
16
,
45
,
3
]
). As is well known, studying the network capacity has two key components:
(i)
the
theoretical
one which attempts to provide the engineering practitioners with the mathematically precise description of the ultimate underlying network architecture usefulness; and
(ii)
the
practical/algorithmic
one which attempts to provide the users with the concrete (hopefully efficiently implementable) computational methodologies to indeed utilize the network architectures to their ultimate potential. Here we continue the trend established in the recent literature, focus on the first one, and provide a strong theoretical progress on several important capacity related questions. To be able to properly present the technical contributions and to adequately contextualize them within the relevant prior work, we find it convenient to first introduce the needed mathematical formalisms that best describe the underlying NN models.

1.1
Architecture of the
wide
hidden layer generically activated NNs

We start with a generic architecture description of the multi-input single-output feed-forward neural networks with
L-2
hidden layers and
d_{i}
(
i\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
) nodes (neurons) in the
i
-th layer. For the notational convenience, we add two artificial layers, indexed by
i=1
and
i=L
, which correspond to the network’s input and output, respectively. Although they are artificial, we refer to them as networks layers to ensure the consistency of the overall indexation. The network operates through the specification of the activation functions vectors,
{\bf f}^{(i)}(\cdot)=[{\bf f}_{1}^{(i)}(\cdot),{\bf f}_{2}^{(i)}(\cdot),\dots,{\bf f}_{d_{i+1}}^{(i)}(\cdot)]^{T}
, where each activation function
{\bf f}_{j}^{(i)}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
describes how the
j
-th neuron in layer
i
operates. One effectively has that the outputs of the nodes in layer
i
are taken as the inputs of the nodes in layer
i+1
and then transformed into the new outputs (of the nodes in layer
i+1
) via
{\bf f}_{j}^{(i)}(\cdot)
and matrix of weights,
W^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}\times d_{i+1}}
. Setting
{\bf d}=[d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{L}]
(with
d_{1}=n
and
d_{L}=1
) and denoting by
{\bf b}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}}
the so-called activation thresholds vectors and by
{\bf x}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}
and
{\bf x}^{(i+1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}}
the inputs and outputs of the neurons in layer
i
, one
has the following:

Mathematical formalism of NN with architecture
A({\bf d}\text{; }{\bf f}^{(i)})
:

\mbox{{input:}}\triangleq{\bf x}^{(1)}\quad\longrightarrow

{\bf x}^{(i+1)}={\bf f}^{(i)}(W^{(i)}{\bf x}^{(i)}-{\bf b}^{(i)})

\longrightarrow\quad\mbox{{output:}}\triangleq{\bf x}^{(L+1)}
.

(1)

Clearly, the network’s architecture,
A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(i)})
, is fully specified by the vectors
{\bf d}
and
{\bf f}^{(i)}
(we may on occasion write
A({\bf d};{\bf f})
instead of
A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(i)})
when
{\bf f}^{(i)}
are identical).

As is the case for the single neurons, one of the most fundamental features of any neural net is their ability to properly store/memorize a large amount of data. To see how the above formalism works in that regard, one can assume, for example, the existence of
m
data pairs
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, with
{\bf x}^{(0,k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
being the
n
-dimensional data vectors and
{\bf y}^{(0,k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
being their corresponding labels. Finding weight matrices
W^{(i)}
such that

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(L+1)}={\bf y}^{(0,k)}\qquad\forall k,

(2)

is then sufficient to relate given data vectors,
{\bf x}^{(0,k)}
, to their associated labels,
{\bf y}^{(0,k)}
. The
memory capacity,
C(A({\bf d},{\bf f}^{(i)}))
of the given architecture
A({\bf d},{\bf f}^{(i)})
is then defined as the largest sample size,
m
, such that (
2
) holds for any collection of data pairs
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
with certain prescribed properties. Given the relevance of the capacity in understanding the limits of NNs functioning, determining its precise theoretical value (together with the development of the corresponding computationally efficient algorithmic procedures that achieve it), is of utmost importance. We below provide a collection of results that enable full capacity characterization for many well known architectures
A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(i)})
.

We state below several technical and structural assumptions that facilitate the presentation. As many of them are aligned with the ones discussed in
[
36
,
40
,
42
]
, we avoid unnecessarily repeating them and instead opt for briefly recalling on the most important ones. We, however, do place a particular emphasis on those that substantially differ and are of particular relevance for the considerations of main interest in this paper.

1.2
Assumptions related to architecture and data

As the assumptions that we rely on are rather common and prevalent in the existing literature, we avoid discussing them in deep details and instead focus on precisely stating them.

Architecture assumptions:
We consider
generic
zero-threshold activation functions in the hidden layer with the following properties:
(i)
We take
L=3
,
{\bf b}^{(i)}=0
,
W^{(1)}=I_{n\times n}
, and
W^{(3)}={\bf w}^{T}
, where
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}\times 1}
(in other words,
W^{(3)}
is a
d_{2}
-dimensional row vector).
(ii)
We set
d\triangleq d_{2}
and
\delta\triangleq\delta_{1}=\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}=\frac{n}{d}
. While we ultimately consider
d\rightarrow\infty
scenario, many of the presented mathematical concepts hold for any even
d
. Along the same lines, whenever
d
is not emphasized as infinite, we assume that it is any given even natural number.

(iii)

Identity
neuronal functions,
{\bf f}^{(1)}({\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf x}^{(1)}
, are considered in the first (artificial) layer. In the hidden layer, we take
generic
zero-threshold activations
{\bf f}_{j}^{(2)}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}}\rightarrow R
with
{\bf f}_{j}^{(2)}(\cdot)={\bf f}_{k}^{(2)}(\cdot)
for any
j\neq k
. As typical for the TCMs, at the output, the zero-threshold sign activations,
{\bf f}^{(3)}(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)}-{\bf b}^{(3)})=\mbox{sign}\left(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)}\right)
, are assumed. We denote this architecture by
A({\bf d};[{\bf f}^{(1)},{\bf f}^{(2)},{\bf f}^{(3)}])=A({\bf d};[I,{\bf f}^{(2)},\mbox{sign}])\triangleq A({\bf d};{\bf f}^{(2)})
.
(iv)

The matrix of the hidden layer weights,
W^{(2)}
, can be full or with a particular sparse structure. Both options have been considered previously throughout the literature. For example, a particular sparse structuring with the support of
W^{(2)}
’s
j
-th row,
\mbox{supp}\left(W_{j,:}^{(2)}\right)
, satisfying

\mbox{supp}\left(W_{j,:}^{(2)}\right)={\mathcal{S}}^{(j)}
, with
{\mathcal{S}}^{(j)}\triangleq\{(j-1)\delta+1,(j-1)\delta+2,\dots,j\delta\}
, makes the above architecture correspond to the
treelike committee machines
(TCM) which are of our main interest in this paper (alternatively, full
W^{(2)}
makes the architecture correspond to the
fully connected committee machines
(FCM)).

Data assumptions:

(i)
We assume the typical
binary
labeling
{\bf y}_{i}^{(0,k)}\in\{-1,1\}
(in addition to being the most standard type of labeling, it is also nicely complemented by the
sign
neuronal choice at the network’s output).
(ii)
Data sets that are inseparable are not allowed. This, for example, means that indistinguishable or contradictory pairs, such as
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
and
({\bf x}^{(0,k)},-{\bf y}^{(0,k)})
, can not appear.
(iii)
We focus on statistical datasets and particularly focus on
{\bf x}^{(0,k)}
being comprised of iid standard normals. This follows the trend established in the classical single perceptron references (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
16
,
29
,
9
,
49
,
48
,
47
]
) and is expected to allow for a fairly universal statistical treatment. To provide universal capacity upper bounds, it is, however, perfectly sufficient to consider any type of data set (including even nonstatistical ones).

1.3
Contextualization within relevant prior work

Given that the problems of our interest are well known and have been studied for longer than a half of century, the underlying related literature is rather vast. As surveying all of it here is infeasible (and way better suited for general review papers), we below focus on the results, which, in our view, are the most relevant and closest to our own.

The first memory capacity considerations started with the spherical perceptrons in the early sixties of the last century. A close connection to several fundamental integral geometry problems was observed and the early results were directly related to some of the classical geometrical/probabilistic works (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
9
,
47
,
22
]
). The most famous of them establishes that
C(A(1;\mbox{sign}))\rightarrow 2n
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which effectively means that, in a large dimensional statistical context, the spherical
sign
perceptron capacity basically
doubles the dimension
of the data ambient space,
n
. After being initially proven as a remarkable combinatorial geometry fact in
[
26
,
9
,
49
,
48
,
47
,
8
,
22
]
, decades later, it was reproved in various forms in a host of different fields ranging from machine learning and pattern recognition to probability and information theory (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
45
,
10
,
28
,
11
,
12
,
16
,
29
,
33
]
).

Sign perceptrons hidden layer activations:
Extending the single neuron capacity results to the corresponding network ones is not easy. Particularly scarce are results related to TCM NNs. On the other hand, a bit more is known about the FCM ones, but a direct connection between the two is not very apparent. For example, the FCM capacities trivially upper-bound the corresponding TCM ones, but a way more appropriate appears to be viewing the TCM capacities as roughly the FCM ones divided by
d
. An overwhelming majority of the known capacity results are of the
scaling
type and indicate an unavoidable relation to the total number of the network weights,
w=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}d_{i}d_{i+1}
. For example, the famous VC-dimension provides the upper-bounding scaling
O(w\log(w))
. For the NNs with 1-hidden layer, one has
w=d_{1}d_{2}+d_{2}=(n+1)d
for the FCM and
w=d_{1}+d_{2}=n+d
for the TCM, which, for large
d_{i}
’s and huge
n
, gives the “
division by
d
” FCM – TCM capacity relation. When it comes to the corresponding lower bounding,
[
7
]
argued that the capacity of a shallow 3-layer net scales as
O(nd)
. A bit stronger version was obtained recently in
[
46
]
, where, for the networks with more than three layers, the capacity was shown to scale at least as
O(w)
.

Scaling versus non-scaling:
Obtaining the capacity results that are precise and of the
non-scaling
type is a much harder challenge. Given the simplicity and elegance of the single spherical perceptron capacity, this initially might seem as a bit surprising. However, after recognizing that several decades of a strong effort did not produce much of the analytical progress, the level of difficulty becomes clearer. In fact, after
[
15
]
provided simple multi-perceptron extension of the combinatorial considerations of
[
9
,
49
,
48
,
47
,
8
,
22
]
, the progresses completely stalled until the very recent appearance of
[
40
,
36
,
42
]
. Relying on the Random duality theory (RDT),
[
36
]
developed a generic framework for the analysis of TCM networks and obtained strong capacity upper bounds for any given (odd) number of the hidden layer neurons.
[
40
]
went then a bit further, utilized a partially lifted (pl) RDT variant and substantially lowered the bounds proven in
[
36
]
.

Different hidden layer activations:
The above discussion highlighted the analytical hardness as the first of the two key obstacles one typically faces when trying to transition from the single to multi neuron architectures. The second one is of the algorithmic type and relates to the
sign
perceptrons being noncontinuous functions. Namely, it is usually not very easy to design computationally provably efficient network training strategies for such objects. This amplifies the need for potentially less simple but easier to use activation factions. Since the discreteness is usually perceived as the main source of the sign perceptrons analytical and algorithmic hardness, consideration of continuous activations positions itself as a promising alternative. Many of such activations have already found their place in various NN architectures, include the ReLU, quadratic, tanh, erf and so on. As things are, at least, algorithmically a bit more favorable when such activations are in place, a little bit more is known regarding their capacities as well. For example,
[
51
]
first suggested for deep nets, and
[
20
]
later on proved for 4-layer nets, that the capacity is at least
O(w)
for sigmoids.
[
54
,
19
]
then showed similar results for ReLU while keeping an additional number of nodes restriction which was later on removed in
[
52
]
for both tanh and ReLU. After
[
40
,
36
]
first introduced a generic framework for precise
non-scaling
capacity analysis of the
sign
hidden layer activations,
[
42
]
extended the framework so that it can handle various different activations. It then particularly focused on three types of activations, linear, quadratic, and ReLU. For the linear,
[
42
]
determined the exact value of the capacity and showed that, for
any
width (the number of the hidden layer neurons), it matches the corresponding one of the single spherical perceptron. On the other hand, for the quadratic and ReLU, it obtained the plain and the pl RDT capacity upper bounds. All considerations from
[
40
,
36
,
42
]
were done for the networks with any given (even) number of hidden layer neurons,
d
.

Statistical physics – Replica methods:
Notorious difficulty that moving from the

scaling
(say, of the
O(\cdot)
type) to the
precise
capacity descriptions imposes was already recognized in the early eighties of the last century. As at that time there were no available powerful mathematical techniques that could handle such a move, statistical physics replica methods positioned themselves as an excellent (and basically only known) alternative. Despite their analytical non-rigorousness, they produce expectedly
precise
final results. The foundational replica concepts within the analysis of the NN capacities were laid out in the pioneering works
[
17
,
16
]
, where various single perceptron forms were discussed. Utilizing those concepts, a few
years later,
[
14
,
5
]
studied the same TCM architecture that we study here (as well as the related FCM one). Considering the sign activations, and the so-called replica symmetry formalism, they obtained the capacity predictions for any number of the neurons in the hidden layer,
d
. They also established the corresponding large
d
scaling behavior. Each of these results was proven as mathematically rigorous capacity upper bound, the first one in
[
36
]
and the second one in
[
40
]
.
[
14
,
5
]
, however, went a step further and showed that their replica symmetry large
d
predictions violate the mathematically rigorous ones of
[
15
]
. To remedy such a contradiction,
[
14
,
5
]
then proceeded by studying the first level of replica symmetry breaking (rsb) and showing that it lowers the capacity. For both the committee and the so-called parity machines (PM), relevant large
d
scaling rsb considerations were presented in
[
24
]
as well (for earlier PM related replica considerations see also, e.g.,
[
6
,
4
]
). On the other hand, for the FCM architecture,
[
44
,
50
]
obtained a bit later the large
d
scaling that matches the upper-bounding one of
[
15
]
. Of particular relevance, however, to our work are two very recent lines of work
[
2
,
53
]
where the
wide
hidden layer TCM architectures were considered. In
[
2
]
the 1rsb capacity predictions for the ReLU activations were obtained. A bit later,
[
53
]
moved things further and obtained analogous 1rsb predictions for several other activations, including the linear, ReLU, quadratic, erf, and tanh among others.

Practical achievability:
We also mention another line of work that attracted a strong interest over the last several years. It relates to the design and analysis of the efficient network training algorithms that could potentially approach the capacity. After it was empirically observed that the simple gradient based methods perform reasonably well in this context while requiring only the so-called mild over-parametrization (moderately larger number of free parameters,
w
, compared to the data set size,
m
), a lot of effort was put in providing theoretical justifications of such a phenomenon. More on a solid progress made in these directions in recent years can be found in, e.g.,
[
13
,
18
,
1
,
21
,
23
,
25
,
43
,
27
,
55
]
. While these results are mostly oriented towards the FCMs, they are also extendable to the TCMs as well.

The

n

{\bf f}^{(2)}
, in the

\alpha_{c}(\infty;{\bf f}^{(2)})\triangleq\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{c}(d;{\bf f}^{(2)})\triangleq\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C(A([n,d,1];{\bf f}^{(2)}))}{n},

(3)

For all the considered activations, we successfully conduct the needed numerical evaluations and obtain the concrete capacity values as well.
(iv)
We observe a rather rapid convergence of the lifting mechanism with the relative improvement no better than
\sim 0.1\%
achieved already on the third level of full lifting. Moreover, we uncover that the first and second level of lifting characterizations match the ones obtained through the replica symmetry and 1rsb analyses in

Mathematical formalism of network functioning

To ensure the easiness of writing and overall presentation, we set
W\triangleq W^{(2)}
, recall on
W^{(1)}=I
and
W^{(3)}={\bf w}^{T}
, and write for any
k\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(2)}={\bf f}^{(1)}(W^{(1)}{\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf f}^{(1)}({\bf x}^{(1)})={\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)},

(4)

and

{\bf x}^{(2)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(3)}={\bf f}^{(2)}(W^{(2)}{\bf x}^{(2)})={\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)}),

(5)

and

{\bf x}^{(3)}={\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(4)}={\bf f}^{(3)}(W^{(3)}{\bf x}^{(3)})={\bf f}^{(3)}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf x}^{(3)})=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(6)

After connecting beginning in (
4
) and end in (
6
), one obtains the following explicit relation between the network’s input and output

{\bf x}^{(1)}={\bf x}^{(0,k)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\bf x}^{(4)}=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(7)

The necessary and sufficient condition for network to operate properly, is then the following

{\bf y}^{(0,k)}=\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(W{\bf x}^{(0,k)})).

(8)

Moreover, after setting

{\bf y}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\bf y}^{(0,1)}&{\bf y}^{(0,2)}&\dots&{\bf y}^{(0,m)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad X\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}^{(0,1)}&{\bf x}^{(0,2)}&\dots&{\bf x}^{(0,m)}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(9)

it is not that difficult to see that (
8
) can be rewritten in generic matrix form as

\left(\exists W\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times n}|\|{\bf y}^{T}-\mbox{sign}({\bf w}^{T}{\bf f}^{(2)}(WX^{T}))\|_{2}=0\right)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\left(\left(X,{\bf y}\right)\mbox{is memorized}\right),

(10)

where the
k
-th data pair,
\left({\bf x}^{(0,k)},{\bf y}^{(0,k)}\right)
, are the
k
-th row of
m\times n
matrix
X
and the
k
-th element of
m\times 1
column vector
{\bf y}
. One then has the following (alternative to (
10
)):

Algebraic memorization characterization of the
{\bf f}^{(2)}
-activated hidden layer TCMs:

\displaystyle\hskip-21.68121pt0=\xi\triangleq\min_{W,Q}

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743pt\|{\bf y}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743ptXW^{T}=Q

(11)

\Longleftrightarrow
Data set
\left(X,{\bf y}\right)
is properly memorized.

As it will soon be clear, the above is the key mathematical problem on the path towards characterizing the network memorization capabilities. We find it useful for what follows to slightly reformulate the above optimization. To that end we first observe that it can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\xi=\min_{Z,Q}

\displaystyle\|{\bf y}-\mbox{sign}({\bf f}^{(2)}(Q){\bf w})\|_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle XZ=Q,

(12)

where cosmetic change,
Z=W^{T}
, is only for facilitating overall writing. We continue the trend of
[
40
,
36
,
42
]
and consider the TCM architecture, with a sparse
Z
that ensures the
treelike
architecture. In other words, we consider
Z
with the only nonzero elements in the

2.1
Connecting network functioning and (partially reciprocal)
free energy

Free energies
are well known and almost unavoidable objects in many statistical physics considerations. Here, we view them as purely mathematical objects. We below give a bit of a preview related to the importance of these objects in studying neural networks capacities. To introduce their mathematical representation relevant to the problems of our interest here, we start by defining the following, so-called,
bilinearly summed Hamiltonian

partially reciprocal
partition function

(19)

It is important to note right here at the beginning that for
d=1
one gets the usual
bilinear
Hamiltonian. Indexing and summing over
j_{w}
will effectively correspond to the extension of the width of the hidden layer – the key component of the considered network architecture. For starters, we take
{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}
in (
19

One also notes that the inner summation factors in in a reciprocal fashion making the overall partition function appear as seemingly different from the typically seen counterparts in the statistical physics literature. The thermodynamic limit of the average of such “
partially reciprocal
” free energy is then

\displaystyle f_{sq}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\log{(Z_{sq}(\beta,G^{(j_{w})})})}{\beta\sqrt{n}}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\log\left(\sum_{{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}}\left(\sum_{{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}}e^{\beta{\cal H}_{sq}(G^{(j_{w})})}\right)^{-1}\right)}{\beta\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\log\left(\sum_{{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}}\left(\sum_{{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}}e^{\beta\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\right)^{T}G^{(j_{w})}{\bf x}^{(j_{w})})}\right)^{-1}\right)}{\beta\sqrt{n}}.

\displaystyle f_{sq}(\infty)\triangleq\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}f_{sq}(\beta)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta,n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\log{(Z_{sq}(\beta,G^{(j_{w})})})}{\beta\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\max_{{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}}-\max_{{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}}\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\right)^{T}G^{(j_{w})}{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\min_{{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}}\max_{{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}}\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\right)^{T}G^{(j_{w})}{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}}{\sqrt{n}}.

One can then also trivially rewrite (
) as

\displaystyle-f_{sq}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{G^{(j_{w})}}\min_{{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(j_{w})}}\max_{{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(j_{w})}}\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf y}^{(j_{w})}\right)^{T}G^{(j_{w})}{\bf x}^{(j_{w})}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(22)

Connecting

j_{w}

{\bf y}^{(j_{w})}

hints that the network functioning may indeed be related to the above introduced partially reciprocal free energy. However, quite a few of additional preliminaries need to be addressed before one can definitely make such a connection. One particular thing from (

The above hints at the potential role that the free energies can play in studying the networks capacities. Still, to be able to fully exploit such a potential connection, one would need to develop a mechanism to study the free energies themselves. Such a mechanism is precisely what we discuss next.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}\left(z_{i}^{(1)}\left(\bar{{\bf g}}^{(2)};\max\left({\bf q}^{(net)},0\right)\right)\right)}.

As discussed in
[
42
]
, solving the optimization in (
74
) is not an easy task in general. For a couple of small (even) values of
d
it was done analytically. For larger values of
d
the analytical solutions required additional numerical simulations to complete the needed evaluations. Here, however, we uncover that when
d
is large, i.e. when
d\rightarrow\infty
, the numerical evaluations miraculously sufficiently simplify so that they can ultimately be done. For the simplicity of writing, we assume large even
d
and, due to the nonnegativity of ReLU,

{\bf w}=\begin{bmatrix}-{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 1}\end{bmatrix},

(75)

where
{\bf 1}
is the
\frac{d}{2}
-dimensional vector of all ones.

To handle
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}\left(z_{i}^{(1)}\left(\bar{{\bf g}}^{(2)};{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)\right)\right)
, we start by writing

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(1)}\left(\bar{{\bf g}}^{(2)};{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)\right)=\min_{q^{(net)}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf g}_{j_{w}}^{(2)}-{\bf q}^{(net)}_{j_{w}}\right)^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}\geq 0.

(76)

After further writing the Lagrangian and utilizing the Lagrangian duality one obtains

z_{i}^{(1)}\left(\bar{{\bf g}}^{(2)};{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)\right)=\min_{{\bf q}^{(net)}}\max_{\nu\geq 0}{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)\geq\max_{\nu\geq 0}\min_{{\bf q}^{(net)}}{\mathcal{L}}(\nu),

(77)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{d}\left({\bf g}_{j_{w}}^{(2)}-{\bf q}^{(net)}_{j_{w}}\right)^{2}-2\nu{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}=\left\|{\bf g}^{(2)}-{\bf q}^{(net)}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}.

(78)

Taking the derivative with respect to
{\bf q}^{(net)}
, we further find

\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}=-2\left({\bf g}^{(2)}-{\bf q}^{(net)}\right)-2\nu\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}\circ{\bf w},

(79)

where
\circ
stands for the component-wise multiplication. Equalling the above derivative to zero gives

\displaystyle{\bf q}^{(net)}={\bf g}^{(2)}+\nu\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}\circ{\bf w}.

(80)

Plugging this back into (
78
), one then finds

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu^{2}\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}\circ{\bf w}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu^{2}\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)^{T}{\bf w},

with
{\bf q}^{(net)}
as given in (
80
). For the time being we assume
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\nu\rightarrow 0
, and for the
{\bf q}^{(net)}
from (
80
), we write

\displaystyle{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)={\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)+\left(\nu\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)}{d{\bf q}^{(net)}}\circ{\bf w}\right)\circ\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}+o(\nu).

(82)

Combining (
81
) and (
82
), one further finds

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu^{2}\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}\circ{\bf w}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\nu{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}+o(\nu).

(83)

Taking the derivative of
{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)
with respect to
\nu
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathcal{L}}(\nu)}{d\nu}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle-2\nu\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)^{T}{\bf w}.

(84)

Equalling the above derivative to zero and keeping in mind that
\nu\geq 0
, one then finds

\displaystyle\nu^{(opt)}=\frac{\max\left(-{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)^{T}{\bf w},0\right)}{\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

(85)

Plugging this value of
\nu
back into (
83
), one obtains

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\nu^{(opt)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\max\left(-{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)^{T}{\bf w},0\right)\right)^{2}}{\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

(86)

A combination of (
4.1.1
), (
77
), (
78
), and (

) together with concentrations gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}z_{i}^{(1)}\left(\bar{{\bf g}}^{(2)};{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf q}^{(net)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}{\mathcal{L}}(\nu^{(opt)})

(87)

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}\left(\max\left(-{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)^{T}{\bf w},0\right)\right)^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}^{(2)}}\left\|\frac{d{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}^{(2)}\right)}{d{\bf g}^{(2)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

We first write

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}_{j_{w}}^{(2)}\right)-\sum_{j_{w}=\frac{d}{2}+1}^{d}{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}_{j_{w}}^{(2)}\right).

Utilizing concentrations and the central limit theorem, one further has

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{w}=1}^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf f}^{(2)}\left({\bf g}_{j_{w}}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle g_{c,1}^{(2)},

(89)

where

is a Gaussian variable with mean
\mu_{2,1}
and variance

, i.e.,

Analogously, one also has

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle g_{c,2}^{(2)},

(91)

where
g_{c,2}^{(2)}
is a Gaussian variable with mean
\mu_{2,2}
and variance
\sigma_{2,2}^{2}
, i.e.,
g_{c,2}^{(2)}
is a Gaussian variable given by

As

and

are independent one also has

We then also have

(ii) Handling

5
Conclusion

We studied the memory capacity of the
wide
treelike committee machines (TCM) neural networks with generic hidden layer activations.
[
36
,
40
]
recently showed that the Random Duality Theory (RDT) and its a
partially lifted
(pl RDT) variant can be used to create very powerful frameworks for precise networks capacity analysis. The
sign
activations considerations from
[
36
,
40
]
were then extended to more general ones in
[
42
]
, where particularly elegant results were uncovered for
any
even number of the
quadratically
and
ReLU
activated hidden layer neurons,
d
. The machinery of
[
42
]
was designed to work for any type of activations. However, it often requires a significant amount of numerical work to become practically usable. Here, we consider very popular
wide
hidden layer networks and uncover that a strong portion of the numerical difficulties magically disappears.

In particular, we employ recently developed
fully lifted
(fl) RDT to characterize the
wide
(
d\rightarrow\infty
) TCM nets capacity. We obtain explicit, closed form, capacity characterizations for generic hidden layer activations functions. Even though the obtained forms allow significantly less computationally intensive evaluations, they ultimately still requite one to perform a substantial residual numerical work to ensure that the whole machinery can indeed be made practically relevant and useful. We successfully conducted all of it. Moreover, for four very famous activations, ReLU, quadratic, erf, and tanh, we uncovered that the whole lifting mechanism exhibits a remarkably fast convergence with the relative improvements no better than
\sim 0.1\%
happening already on the 3-rd level of lifting. As an additional bonus, we also observe that the capacity characterizations obtained on the first and second level of lifting exactly match those obtained through the statistical physics replica theory methods in
[
53
]
for the generic and in
[
2
]
for the ReLU activations.

Various extensions are possible as well. We here discussed in details some of the most famous NN activations functions. Depending on the context of application, many other well known ones are of interest and can be handled as well. Also, more complex network architectures including both multi-layered TCM and FCM or PM based ones are as relevant. We will discuss all of these in detail in separate papers.
