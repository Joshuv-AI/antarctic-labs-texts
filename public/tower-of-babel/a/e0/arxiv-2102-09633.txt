Abstract
. Suppose a committee consisting of three members has to match n n candidates to n n different positions. Each member of the committee proposes a matching, however the proposed matchings totally disagree, i.e., every candidate is matched to three different positions according to three committee members. All three committee members are very competitive and want to push through as many of their suggestions as possible. Can a committee always find a compromise — a matching of candidates to positions such that for every committee member a third of all candidates are assigned according to that committee member suggestion? We will consider an asymptotic version of this question and several other variants of similar problem. As an application we will consider an embedding problem — in particular which configurations large Steiner systems always need to contain.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the following question.

Question 1.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
2n
vertices that is a union of
k
edge-disjoint perfect matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
. For which sequences
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\leq n
we can always find a new matching
M
in
G
such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\geq a_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
?

Question
1.1
, in particular, is related to the famous Ryser conjecture, which is equivalent to asking if in every proper edge-colouring of
K_{n,n}
there is a rainbow matching, i.e. if for any
n
disjoint perfect matchings
M_{1},...,M_{n}
of
K_{n,n}
there is a matching
M
, such that
|M\cap M_{i}|=1
for all
i\in[n]
.

Keevash, Pokrovskiy, Sudakov and Yepremyan
[
5
]
proved that if
K_{n,n}
is decomposed into
n
perfect matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
, then there is a matching
M
(a rainbow matching) such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\leq 1
for all
i\in[n]
and
M
has size at least
n-O(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
.

Note that the result of Keevash et.al.
[
5
]
implies that relaxed version of Question
1.1
with
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{n}=1
has affirmative answer: if
K_{n,n}
is decomposed into
n
disjoint perfect matchings, then there are
k=n-O(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
of them, say
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
and a matching
M
such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\geq 1
for all
i\in[k]
.

The following is a central definition for this paper that formalizes a relaxation of Question
1.1
that we are considering.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a sequence of integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy if there is an integer
\ell\in[k,(1+\varepsilon)k]
such that for any
\ell
disjoint perfect matchings of
K_{2n}
there are
k
of them
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
and a matching
M
such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\geq a_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
.

We show that a relaxed version of Question
1.1
has an affirmative answer for a wide variety of sequences, namely we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is
n_{0}
, such that for all integers
n\geq n_{0}
the following holds.
Let
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a sequence of positive integer such that
\sum a_{i}<(1-\varepsilon)n
and either

(i)

a_{i}\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
for all
i
, or

(ii)

a_{i}\geq n^{\varepsilon}
for all
i
.

Then sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

While the proof of case (i) of Theorem
1.3
is based on the probabilistic argument the proof of case (ii) of Theorem
1.3
relies on Alon’s necklace theorem
[
2
]
.

We will find convenient to divide the proof of Theorem
1.3
by formulating two separate statements.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is
n_{1}
such that for any
n\geq n_{1}
any sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
with each term at most
n^{1-\varepsilon}
and such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<(1-\varepsilon)n
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

We deduce Theorem
1.4
from a slightly more technical Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there is
n_{2}
such that for any
n\geq n_{2}
any sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
with each term at least
n^{\varepsilon}
and such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<(1-\varepsilon)n
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
relies on Theorem
1.4
and Alon’s necklace theorem
[
2
]
and is presented in Section
4
.

For sequence
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
such that
a_{i}\geq\alpha n
for some real
\alpha
, it can be deduced from Theorem
1.5
that
\mathcal{A}
is
(0,n)
-happy (see Corollary
5.1
). In particular for the problem stated in the abstract, Corollary
5.1
of Theorem
1.5
implies that for any
\varepsilon>0
and large enough
n
, if disjoint perfect matchings
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
of
K_{n,n}
are given, then there is a matching

such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\geq(\frac{1}{3}-\varepsilon)n
.

In Section
5
we show how Theorem
1.3
is related to a problem of finding large hypertree in a Steiner triple system. A hypertree is a connected, simple

‐uniform hypergraph in which every two vertices are joined by a unique path. A Steiner triple system is a

‐uniform hypergraph in which every pair of vertices is contained in exactly one edge. Easy greedy-type argument shows that any hypertree with at most

vertices. Elliot and the second author
[

conjectured that the same is true even for larger hypertrees.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
there is
n_{0}
, such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
, any hypertree
T
of order
n
and any Steiner triple system of order at least
(1+\varepsilon)n
,
S
contains

as a subhypergraph.

In
[
3
]
authors proved Conjecture
1.6
for subdivision hypertree. With the proof based on Corollary
5.1
, in Section
5
we will verify the Conjecture

for another infinite family of hypertrees, which we call “turkeys”.

We start with proving the following quick consequence of Theorem

Claim 2.4
.

For any constant

there is

such that the following holds for any

,
\Delta=K\sqrt{D\ln D}
and integer

. Let
G
be a graph that is a union of edge disjoint matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{b}
such that:

(a)

deg(v)=D\pm\Delta
for all
v\in V(G)
.

(b)

|M_{i}|=Dm\pm\Delta m/2
for all

Then there are is a matching

in
G
containing at least
mb\left(1-O(\frac{\ln^{3/2}D}{D^{1/2}})\right)
edges such that
|M\cap M_{i}|\leq m
for all
i\in[b]
.

Proof of Claim
2.4
.

Let
D_{0}
be provided by Theorem
2.2
with a constant
K
as input. Assume
D_{0}
is large enough so that
D>4\Delta
. Let
G
be a graph with vertex set that is a union of edge disjoint matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{b}
. Number of edges in
G
is equal to
|X|(D\pm\Delta)/2
(by assumption (a)) and to
\sum_{i=1}^{b}|M_{i}|=mb(D\pm\Delta/2)
(by (b)). Hence we may conclude that
|X|=2mb\pm mb
.

For a given

construct a 3-uniform hypergraph
H
by arbitrarily splitting edges of each
M_{i}
into
m
groups
C_{i}^{1},\ldots,C_{i}^{m}
with
(D\pm\Delta)
edges in each group (recall (b)). Note, that splitting
Dm\pm\Delta m/2

m
groups of size
(D\pm\Delta/2)
might not be possible, as it may be the case that
|M_{i}|=Dm-\frac{\Delta m}{2}
is an integer, but

is not.

For each such group

add a new vertex
y^{j}_{i}
and construct
C^{j}_{i}
hyperedges (triples) by extending each edge in
C^{j}_{i}
by the vertex
y_{i}^{j}
. Let
Y
be a set of new vertices, then
|Y|=mb
(as each
M_{i}
produces
m

H
constructed in this way has
N=|X|+|Y|=\Theta(mb)
vertices, is linear and each hyperedge has nonempty intersection with
Y
. Degree of each vertex of

is
D\pm\Delta
, as every
y_{i}^{j}
has degree

and degrees of vertices of
X
in

are same as in
G
.

Apply Theorem
2.2
to hypergraph

(with
N=\Theta(mb)
) to obtain a 3-uniform matching

that misses at most
O(mbD^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}D)

misses at most
O(mbD^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}D)

. Recall that
|Y|=mb
and each hyperedge of

, so

has at least
mb-O(mbD^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}D)
edges. Let
M
be a (2-uniform) matching obtained from

by deleting a vertex in

|M|=|M^{\prime}|=mb-O(mbD^{-1/2}\ln^{3/2}D).

For each

, matching

intersects

m
edges, as edges of

are extended to a union of

. Therefore,

is a matching such that contains at least

for all

.
∎

Now, to prove Lemma
2.3
we first apply Claim
2.4
to the graph
G
and obtain a matching
M
such that
|M|=mb\left(1-O(\frac{\ln^{3/2}D}{D^{1/2}})\right)

.

Let
A_{i}=M\cap M_{i}
, and for each

we define a “deficit” to be

. The total deficit of

We now will find many sets

with small deficit.

We say that set

is “bad” if it has size smaller than

, or in other words if its deficit

is more than
\delta m/(1+\delta)
. Since the total deficit is

, the number of “bad”

’s is at most

\left.O\left(mb\frac{\ln^{3/2}D}{D^{1/2}}\right)\middle/\frac{\delta m}{1+\delta}\right.=O\left(b\frac{\ln^{3/2}D}{D^{1/2}}\right).

Finally, we deduce that there is a collection of at least
b^{{}^{\prime}}=b-O(b\frac{\ln^{3/2}D}{D^{1/2}})

, say
A_{1},\ldots,A_{b^{{}^{\prime}}}
, such that

for

. This means that for matching

and matchings

we have
|M\cap M_{i}|\geq\frac{m}{1+\delta}
, finishing the proof of the Lemma.
∎

.

Assume
\varepsilon
is given, we will specify
n_{1}
implicitly. Let

-happy then it is also

-happy for all

. Consequently it is sufficient to prove Theorem

for

We may also assume that

All together, we may assume that

and a sequence

Therefore, if

, sequence

-happy.

For the rest of the proof assume

. The sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
might not satisfy condition (d) of Theorem

, so we will construct a new sequence
\{a_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{i=1}^{k}
that satisfies assumptions (a)-(d) of Theorem

and such that

for all

. Define

\delta=\varepsilon/100,\qquad\lambda=\varepsilon/400.

Let

(1)

Now

3
), and we have

(2)

Claim 3.2
.

Sequence
\{a^{{}^{\prime}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{\prime}_{i}<(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})n.

(b)

a^{\prime}_{i}\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}\text{ for all }i\in[t].

|A_{i}|\geq m/(1+\delta)
for
i\in[b^{\prime}]
. It remains to notice that
\ell\geq k\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{\varepsilon}
,
p\geq\frac{1}{3}
and so
D=p\ell\geq\frac{1}{9}n^{\varepsilon}
. So for large enough

we have
b^{\prime}\geq k
. Therefore matchings

are such that
|A_{i}|\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}\geq a_{i}
for all

and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
is a matching.

For a general sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
we want to adopt the approach of the previous “simple” case when all the

’s are almost equal.
To this end we will group
a_{i}
’s in

sets

, so that inside each set

the elements do not differ much (this is done in Step 1 of formal proof). This way we will have for each group similar starting position
as in the “simple” case. Let

be the number of elements of sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in
I_{j}
. For notation convenience let the elements of
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in

form a sequence
\{a^{i}_{j}\}_{i=1}^{n_{j}}
.

Next, we define two sequences
m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}
(in Step 1) and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
(in Step 2) that will be used as an input values for

and
b
in Lemma
2.3
. Some technical but crucial properties of those sequences are:

(a)

\lceil m_{j}/(1+\delta)\rceil\geq\max\{x:x\in I_{j}\}
for all

.

(b)

b_{j}\geq n_{j}+m_{j}^{-1/2}n^{\lambda+1/2}
if
n_{j}\neq 0
.

Here we also choose
\ell=\sum b_{j}
and in Claim
3.8
we show that
\ell\leq(1+\varepsilon)k
.

Finally in Step 3 of the proof, for
G
that is a union of
\ell
matchings on set
X
we construct subgraphs

of
G
in the following way. We first partition collection of matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\ell}
into sub-collections
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{t}
so that each
|\mathcal{M}_{j}|=b_{j}
. We also set, after relabeling,
\mathcal{M}_{j}=\{M_{j}^{1},\ldots,M_{j}^{b_{j}}\}
. Then we partition vertex set
X
into sets
X_{0},\ldots,X_{t}
(vertices of
X_{0}
are leftover vertices that will not be used). Finally
G_{j}
is a defined to be a graph induced by edges of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
on
X_{j}
.

In Claim
3.11
it is shown that we can find partition
X
so that every
G_{j}
satisfies all conditions necessary to apply Lemma
2.3
. Claim
3.11
is proved by considering a random partition of
X
. We then apply Lemma
2.3
to a graph
G_{j}
with
m=m_{j}
,
b=b_{j}
and
D=D_{j}=b_{j}^{2}m_{j}/n
.

An application of Lemma
2.3
to each
G_{j}
yields the following: there are at least
b_{j}^{\prime}=b_{j}\left(1-O(\frac{\ln^{3/2}D_{j}}{D_{j}^{1/2}})\right)
matchings, say
M^{1}_{j},\ldots,M^{b_{j}^{\prime}}_{j}
and sets
A^{i}_{j}\subseteq M^{i}_{j}
for
i\in[b_{j}^{\prime}]
such that
|A^{i}_{j}|\geq\frac{m_{j}}{1+\delta}
fo all
i\in[b_{j}^{\prime}]
and that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{b_{j}^{\prime}}A^{i}_{j}
is a matching in
G_{j}
.

We will prove, that our choice of
b_{j}
(see (b) above) will guarantee for large enough
n
, that
b^{\prime}_{j}\geq n_{j}
for each
j\in[t]
. As mentioned above there are
b^{\prime}_{j}
sets
A_{j}^{i}
’s. On the other hand,
n_{j}
is equal to the number of elements of sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in set
I_{j}
(also recall that those elements were labeled as
\{a^{1}_{j},\ldots,a_{j}^{n_{j}}\}
). So every element
a^{i}_{j}\in I_{j}
with

can be put in correspondence with the matching

.

By Lemma
2.3
we have that
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq m_{j}/(1+\delta)
. Since
a_{j}^{i}\in I_{j}
, it follows from property (a) that
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq a^{i}_{j}
for all

,

. Finally,

is a desired matching since all

’s are vertex disjoint.

3.3.
Formal proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Recall that the proof of Theorem
3.1
is divided into three steps: construction of sets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}
and sequence
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
; construction of sequence
\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
; and construction of subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
with a subsequent application of Lemma
2.3
to each of
G_{j}
.

Step 1:
In this step we will construct sets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}
and a sequence
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}\}
.
Let
I
be the set of integers in
[1,n^{1-\varepsilon}]
, we cover
I
by sets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}
. Recall that
\delta=\varepsilon/100
,
\lambda=\delta/4
and choose an integer

C=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil.

There will be a special treatment of integers that are smaller than
C
.

Definition 3.3
.

Define the sets

I_{j}=\{j\}\text{ for }j\in[C]

and for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
define

I_{C+j}=\left(C(1+\delta)^{j-1},C(1+\delta)^{j}\right]\cap\mathbb{N}.

Define
t
to be the smallest integer such that
C(1+\delta)^{t-C}\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}
.
Notice that the choice of
C
guarantees that every
I_{j}
for
j\in[t]
is nonempty. Choice of
t
guarantees that
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}
cover
I=[1,n^{1-\varepsilon}]
and that
t=O(\ln n)
.

Definition 3.4
.

Define
m_{j}=j
if
j\leq C
. If
j>C
define

m_{j}=\min\{x\in\mathbb{N}:x\in I_{j+2}\}.

Notice that
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
is an increasing sequence. We also need the following observation about sequence
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
.

Claim 3.5
.

If
j\in[C+1,t]
and
x\in I_{j}
, then

m_{j}\in[(1+\delta)x,(1+\delta)^{3}x].

If
x\in I_{j}
with
j\leq C
, then
m_{j}=x=j
.

Proof.

If
x\in I_{j}
with
j>C
, then

C(1+\delta)^{j-C-1}\leq x\leq C(1+\delta)^{j-C},

C(1+\delta)^{j-C+1}\leq m_{j}\leq C(1+\delta)^{j-C+2}.

So if
x\in I_{j}
for
j>C
then

(1+\delta)x\leq m_{j}\leq(1+\delta)^{3}x.

The second statement of the Claim is trivial since
I_{j}=\{j\}
and
m_{j}=j
when
j\leq C
.
∎

Step 2:
The second step of the proof consists of constructing sequences
n_{1},\ldots,n_{t}
and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
.

Definition 3.6
.

Define
n_{j}
to be the number of elements of
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in set
I_{j}
for all
j\in[t]
. In other words

n_{j}=|\{i\in[k]:a_{i}\in I_{j}\}|.

Define a sequence
\{a^{i}_{j}\}_{i=1}^{n_{j}}
to consist of all elements of
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
that are in
I_{j}
.

Notice that according to this definition, a sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is partitioned into
t
sequences
\{a^{i}_{j}\}_{i=1}^{n_{j}}
with
j\in[t]
. Additionally we have

(5)

k=\sum_{j=1}^{t}n_{j}.

Now we define sequence
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
. Recall that
\lambda=\delta/4
.

Definition 3.7
.

For
j\in[t]
, set
b_{j}=n_{j}+\lceil m_{j}^{-1/2}n^{\lambda+1/2}\rceil
if
n_{j}\neq 0
, and set
b_{j}=0
if
n_{j}=0
.

Finally, we set

(6)

\ell=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}.

The following Claim proves that
\ell
is at most
(1+\varepsilon)k
.

Claim 3.8
.

We have
\ell-k\leq\varepsilon k
.

Proof.

First,
\ell-k
is equal to
\sum_{j=1}^{t}(b_{j}-n_{j})
by (
5
) and (
6
). For all
j\in[t]
, according to Definition
3.7
,

b_{j}-n_{j}\leq m_{j}^{-1/2}n^{\lambda+1/2}+1.

Case 1.
Consider the case
k\geq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
first. Since each
m_{j}
is a positive integer,

\ell-k=\sum_{j=1}^{t}{b_{j}-n_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}1+n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{-1/2}\leq t+tn^{\lambda+1/2}.

Recall that
t=O(\ln n)
and
\lambda<\varepsilon
, so for large enough
n
,

\ell-k=O(\ln n)n^{\lambda+1/2}\leq\varepsilon n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon k.

This finishes the proof of the Claim for case
k\geq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
.

Case 2.
Now we consider the case
k<n^{1/2+\varepsilon}
. In this case,
k^{-1}n^{1-\lambda}\geq n^{1/2-\varepsilon-\lambda}
and sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
satisfies property (
4
), so for all
i\in[k]
we have

(7)

a_{i}\geq n^{1/2-\varepsilon-\lambda}.

Next we will define an integer
j_{0}
and show that
n_{j}=0
for all
j<j_{0}
.

To this end consider the smallest
j_{0}\in[t]
, such that

(8)

m_{j_{0}}\geq(1+\delta)k^{-1}n^{1-\lambda}.

In order to observe that such
j_{0}
exists we notice that
k\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{\varepsilon}
(by (
3
)) and so by Definition
3.4
we have for sufficiently large
n

m_{t}\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}\geq(1+\delta)k^{-1}n^{1-\lambda}.

Therefore there is
j_{0}\leq t
that satisfies (
8
).

For
i\in[k]
, by (
7
) we have
a_{i}\geq n^{1/2-\varepsilon-\lambda}
, so for large enough
n
,
if
a_{i}\in I_{j}
, then
j>C
(see Definition
3.3
).

Therefore if
a_{i}\in I_{j}
, by applying Claim
3.5
with
x=a_{i}
, we get

m_{j}\geq(1+\delta)a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{ineq:scase})}}}{{\geq}}(1+\delta)k^{-1}n^{1-\lambda}.

Comparing this inequality with definition of
j_{0}
(
8
) we get that if
a_{i}\in I_{j}
for some
i\in[k]
, then
j\geq j_{0}
.

Therefore for all
j<j_{0}
there are no elements of
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in sets
I_{j}
, which implies that
n_{j}=0
and therefore
b_{j}=0
(by Definition
3.7
) for all
j<j_{0}
.

Finally, since
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
is increasing, provided
n
is large enough

\ell-k=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}-n_{j}=\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{t}b_{j}-n_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Def }\ref{def:bj}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{t}1+n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{-1/2}\leq t+tn^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j_{0}}^{-1/2}.

Using
t=O(\ln n)
and inequality (
8
), for large enough
n

\ell-k=O(\ln n)n^{\lambda+1/2}(k^{1/2}n^{\lambda/2-1/2})=O(\ln n)k^{1/2}n^{3\lambda/2}\leq k^{1/2}n^{3\varepsilon/8}.

Now, recall that by (
3
) we have
k\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{\varepsilon}
, so

\ell-k\leq 3k^{1/2}k^{3/8}<\varepsilon k

for large enough
n
. So
\ell-k\leq\varepsilon k
in both cases.

∎

The next Claim will be used in Step 3 of the proof.

Claim 3.9
.

For large enough
n
,

\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}m_{j}\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)n.

Proof.

According to Definition
3.7

\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{j}m_{j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{j}m_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(1+n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{-1/2}\right)m_{j}.

Let
S_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{j}m_{j}
and
S_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(1+n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{-1/2}\right)m_{j}
.

We now estimate
S_{1}
. Claim
3.5
implies that if for some
i\in[k]
we have
a_{i}\in I_{j}
, then
m_{j}\leq(1+\delta)^{3}a_{i}
. Since each
I_{j}
contains exactly
n_{j}
elements of sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
(by Definition
3.6
), we infer

n_{j}m_{j}=\sum_{i\in[t],\;a_{i}\in I_{j}}m_{j}\leq\sum_{i\in[t],\;a_{i}\in I_{j}}(1+\delta)^{3}a_{i}.

Since every member of
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is in some
I_{j}
, we have

S_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}n_{j}m_{j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+\delta)^{3}a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{ineq:many})}}{{\leq}}(1+\delta)^{3}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)n\leq\left(1-\frac{3\varepsilon}{8}\right)n.

Now we estimate
S_{2}
. Recall that sequences
\{m_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
is increasing. We also have
n^{1-\varepsilon}\in I_{t}
, so Claim
3.5
implies that
m_{t}\leq(1+\delta)^{3}n^{1-\varepsilon}
. So for all
j\in[t]
, we have
m_{j}\leq(1+\delta)^{3}n^{1-\varepsilon}
. Therefore

S_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(1+n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{-1/2}\right)m_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}m_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}n^{\lambda+1/2}m_{j}^{1/2}=O(tn^{1-\varepsilon})+O(tn^{\lambda+1/2}n^{1/2-\varepsilon/2}).

Recall that
t=O(\ln n)
, so for large enough
n
we have

S_{2}=o(n)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8}n.

Finally, combining the upperbounds on both sums we get

\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{j}m_{j}\leq S_{1}+S_{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{3\varepsilon}{8}\right)n+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}n=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)n.

∎

Step 3.
Now, let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\ell}
be disjoint perfect matchings on
K_{2n}
. Assume that
G
is a graph formed by
\ell
disjoint perfect matchings

on vertex set

of size

.

Definition 3.10
.

For a collection of matchings
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{b}\}
and a set

define
G[X^{\prime},\mathcal{M}]
to be the subgraph of
G
formed by the edges
e=xy
, where
x,y\in X^{\prime}
and
xy\in M
for some
M\in\mathcal{M}
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{t}
be a partition of a set
\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}\}
such that
|\mathcal{M}_{j}|=b_{j}
for all
j\in[t]
. Recall that elements of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
were also labeled by
M_{j}^{i}
, so that
\mathcal{M}_{j}=\{M^{1}_{j},\ldots,M^{b_{j}}_{j}\}
for all
j\in[t]
.

The following Claim allows us to construct vertex disjoint subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
of
G
, to which we will subsequently apply Lemma
2.3
.

To this end, for
j\in[t]
let

D_{j}=b_{j}^{2}m_{j}/n\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\Delta_{j}=K\sqrt{D_{j}\ln D_{j}}.

Claim 3.11
.

There is partition of
X
into parts
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}
, such that for graphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
, defined by
G_{j}=G[X_{j},\mathcal{M}_{j}]
, the following holds for all

•

deg_{G_{j}}(v)=D_{j}\pm\Delta_{j}
for all

.

•

|M_{j}^{i}\cap E(G_{j})|=D_{j}m_{j}\pm\frac{\Delta_{j}m_{j}}{2}
for all

.

Proof.

Define
p_{j}=m_{j}b_{j}/n
for
j\in[t]
. Then
D_{j}=p_{j}b_{j}
for all
j\in[t]
. Note that Claim
3.9
implies that
\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\leq 1-\varepsilon/4
, so each

.
Set
p_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}
. Consider random partition of
X
into parts
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}
such that each vertex
x\in X
ends up in set
X_{j}
with probability
p_{j}
independently of other vertices.

For all
j\in[t]
and

, degree of

and

.

Now, for any edge, probability that both of its end point belong to

Notice that if

Calculating the expectation of binomial random variable gives for all

and

•

\mathbb{E}(deg_{G_{j}}(v))=b_{j}p_{j}=b_{j}^{2}m_{j}/n=D_{j}

Recall that if

So for any

, so

\mathbb{P}(||M_{j}^{i}\cap E(G_{j})|-m_{j}D_{j}|>\Delta_{j}m_{j}/2)\leq 2e^{-K^{2}\ln D_{j}/16}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{ineq:Dj})}}{{\leq}}2n^{-\frac{K^{2}\lambda}{8}}.

Finally, let

be the probability that a random partition satisfies the conclusion of the Claim for all graphs

with

. There are at most

random variables

and at most

Recall that

and

, so for

.

Therefore the random partion above satisfies the conclusion of Claim

with high probability.
∎

Let
X
be partitioned as in Claim

, then the corresponding graphs

with

.

So, for each

,

,

, matchings

and

. We obtain at least

for

,

Next, we show the for each

than there are elements of

in

.

Claim 3.12
.

For all

and large enough

, we have

.

Proof.

Recall that,

b^{{}^{\prime}}_{j}=b_{j}\left(1-O\left(\frac{\ln^{3/2}D_{j}}{D_{j}^{1/2}}\right)\right)=b_{j}-O\left(\frac{b_{j}\ln^{3/2}D_{j}}{D_{j}^{1/2}}\right).

∎

Recall that
n_{j}
is equal to the number of elements of sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
in set
I_{j}
and those elements were also labeled as
\{a^{1}_{j},\ldots,a_{j}^{n_{j}}\}
.

We proved that for all
j\in[t]
there are
b_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
sets
\{A_{j}^{1},\ldots A_{j}^{b_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}}\}
that satisfy (L1), (L2), (L3). Consequently, by Claim
3.12
, for any
j\in[t]
and
i\in[n_{j}]
set
A_{j}^{i}
exists.

Claim 3.13
.

For every
j\in[t]
and
i\in[n_{j}]
we have
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq a_{j}^{i}
.

Proof.

By (L2) we have
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq m_{j}/(1+\delta),
so

|A_{j}^{i}|\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{j}}{1+\delta}\right\rceil.

Also recall that
a_{j}^{i}\in I_{j}
.

If
j\geq C+1
, since
a_{j}^{i}\in I_{j}
, Claim
3.5
yields that
\frac{m_{j}}{1+\delta}\geq a^{i}_{j}
. Hence
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq a^{i}_{j}
in this case.

If
j\leq C
, then
m_{j}=j=a_{j}^{i}
. In Step 1 we defined
C=\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\rceil
, so due to
j\leq C
we have
\frac{j}{1+\delta}>j-1
. Therefore in case
j\leq C
we have
\lceil\frac{j}{1+\delta}\rceil=j
, and so

|A_{j}^{i}|\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{j}}{1+\delta}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{j}{1+\delta}\right\rceil=j=a_{j}^{i}.

∎

To summarize our steps toward the proof of Theorem
3.1
, for a given sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
we split it into
t
sub-sequences
\{a_{j}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{j}}
with
j\in[t]
. Then, for given
\ell
disjoint perfect matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{\ell}
on set
X
of size
2n
we found
k
perfect matchings, labeled
\{M_{j}^{i}\}_{j=1,i=1}^{t,n_{j}}
, and submatchings
A_{j}^{i}\subseteq M_{j}^{i}
.

From Claim
3.13
it follows that for all
j\in[t]
and
i\in[n_{j}]
we have
|A_{j}^{i}|\geq a_{j}^{i}
.

Notice that for a given
j\in[t]
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{j}}A_{j}^{i}
is a matching (guaranteed by (L3) and Claim
3.12
) so any two
A_{j}^{i}
and
A_{j}^{i^{\prime}}
are vertex disjoint. Also all vertices of each
A_{j}^{i}
belong to
V(G_{j})
by (L1). Since
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
are vertex disjoint we get that
A_{j}^{i}
and
A_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}
are also vertex disjoint for
j\neq j^{\prime}
. Therefore, all
A_{j}^{i}
’s are vertex disjoint matchings and so
M=\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n_{j}}A_{j}^{i}
is a matching. This finishes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

Proof of Theorem
1.5
relies on Theorem
1.4
and an application of the following Necklace Theorem by Alon
[
2
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider an open necklace (a string of beads) in which beads are of
\ell
different colors and the number of beads of each color is divisible by
q
.

Then a group of
q
thieves can cut the necklace between the beads in at most
\ell(q-1)
places and distribute the obtained interval pieces between themselves in such a way that each thieve will get the same number of beads of each color.

For
\varepsilon>0
and sufficiently large
n
, let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
be a sequence of integers such that
\sum a_{i}<(1-\varepsilon)n
and
a_{i}\geq n^{\varepsilon}
for all
i\in[k]
.

We may assume that
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is non-decreasing, and that

(10)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\geq\frac{n}{3},

Indeed, in case
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<\frac{n}{3}
, for any
k
matchings
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
we can find desired subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
greedily.

The goal of this section is to show that sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy. Note that Theorem
1.4
implies that for any
\delta>0
, provided
n
is large, every positive sequence
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
that satisfies
\sum b_{i}\leq(1-\delta)n
and
b_{i}\leq n^{1-\delta}
is
(\delta,n)
-happy.

4.1.
Proof plan

The proof idea Therorem
1.5
is to modify initial sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
by a series of ‘non-happiness’ preserving operations until we obtain a happy sequence, this would imply that initial sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is also happy.
More specifically, let
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{a_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, we recursively define new non-decreasing sequences
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{s}
(here
s<k
) such that each sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j}
consists of
k-j
elements for
j\in[0,s]
and the last sequence
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

The recursive definition goes as follows: assume that non-decreasing sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j}=\{a_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{k-j}
for some
j\in[0,s-1]
is constructed with the sum of elements at most
(1-\varepsilon/2)n
. In what follows,
\delta
is a constant much smaller than
\varepsilon
.

If
a_{k-j,j}
is at most
n^{1-\delta}
, then we stop the construction and set
s=j
. The sequence
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is
(\delta,n)
-happy by Theorem
1.4
. For that, we assume that
n
is at least
n^{\prime}
, where
n^{\prime}
is the number guaranteed by Theorem
1.4
for
\delta
. Finally, as
\delta<\varepsilon
, sequence
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is also
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

If
a_{k-j,j}
is larger than
n^{1-\delta}
, we construct new sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}
such that the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}
are taken to be all but the last element of
\mathcal{A}_{j}
rescaled by some factor (see (
14
)).

This rescaling factor is chosen so that the sum of elements of
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}
is larger then sum of elements of
\mathcal{A}_{j}
by roughly a factor of
(1+\lambda)
, independent of
j
(Claim
4.2
(c)). In turn,
\lambda
is chosen so that the constructed sequences
\mathcal{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{s}
all have a sum at most
(1-\varepsilon/2)n
(Claim
4.3
(a)).

Construction of sequences
\mathcal{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{s}
stops when the last sequence
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is
(\delta,n)
-happy, which will happen eventually as the sequence consisting of single element
\{b\}
with
b<n
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy by definition.

Finally, Claim
4.6
shows that if
\mathcal{A}_{j}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy then
\mathcal{A}_{j-1}
is also
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy. Since the construction ends with a sequence
\mathcal{A}_{s}
that is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy, this implies, by repeated application of Claim
4.6
, that all previous sequences
\mathcal{A}_{j}
with
j\in[0,s-1]
, in particular
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, are also
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy. Claim
4.6
is a corollary of a rather general Lemma
4.4
that allows us to verify that a sequence is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy, provided some other sequence is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy, and the main ingredient of proof of Lemma
4.4
is Theorem
4.1
.

4.2.
Formal proof

Define the auxiliary variables

(11)

\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{10},\qquad\mu=\frac{\varepsilon}{2(1-\varepsilon)},\qquad S=\lceil 2(1+\mu)n^{\delta}\ln n\rceil,\qquad\lambda=\frac{\ln(1+\mu)}{S}.

To explain the choice of variables,
(1+\lambda)
will determine how much
\sum_{i=1}^{k-j+1}a_{i,j-1}
is smaller than
\sum_{i=1}^{k-j}a_{i,j}
for all
j\in[s]
(Claim
4.2
(c)). Constant
\mu
is chosen to guarantee that

(12)

(1+\lambda)^{S}\leq(1+\mu)=\frac{1-\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon}.

Inequality (

) follows easily from inequality

and is used in proving Claim
4.3
(a). Finally,

will be shown to be an upper bound on

in Claim
4.3
(c)

Provided

is large enough, the following inequalities hold:

(13)

n^{-2\delta}\leq\lambda\leq 1,\qquad\qquad n^{\delta}\leq S\leq n^{2\delta}.

Construction of sequences
\mathcal{A}_{j}
.
Recall that we assumed that
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is non-decreasing. We now construct
s+1
non-decreasing sequences
\mathcal{A}_{j}=\{a_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{k-j}
with

. Set
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and assume that sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j}=\{a_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{k-j}
was constructed for some integer
j
. Set

\sigma_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-j}a_{i,j}.

If
a_{k-j,j}>n^{1-\delta}
, then define

m_{j}=\frac{a_{k-j,j}}{\sigma_{j}},

and for
i\in[k-j-1]
define the terms of sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j+1}=\{a_{i,j+1}\}_{i=1}^{k-j-1}
by

(14)

a_{i,j+1}=\left\lfloor\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}a_{i,j}\right\rfloor.

If
a_{k-j,j}\leq n^{1-\delta}
or

, the construction stops and we set

.

We start with proving a few straightforward, but slightly technical observations about constructed sequences.

Claim 4.2
.

For the sequences
\mathcal{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{s}
we have the following:

(a)

a_{i,j}\leq a_{i,j+1}
for all
j\in[0,s-1]
and
i\in[k-j-1]
.

(b)

For each
j\in[0,s]
sequence
\mathcal{A}_{j}
is non-decreasing.

(c)

For each
j\in[0,s-1]

\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}\leq\sigma_{j+1}\leq(1+\lambda)\sigma_{j}.

Proof of Claim.

The proof is straightforward and uses equation (
14
) and the fact that
n
is sufficiently large.

Part (a).

The proof is by induction on
j
. The base case
j=0
and inductive step are proved simultaneously. Assume that for some
h\in[0,s-1]
we proved the statement for all values of
j<h
, that is assume that for all
j\in[h]
we have
a_{i,j}\leq a_{i,j+1}
for all
i\in[k-j+1]
. Then for all
i\in[k-h]
we have

a_{i,h}\geq a_{i,h-1}\geq\ldots\geq a_{i,0}\geq n^{\varepsilon}

and so

a_{i,h+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\geq}}\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{h}}a_{i,h}-1\geq(1+\lambda)a_{i,h}-1\geq a_{i,h}+(\lambda n^{\varepsilon}-1).

Then, by inequality (
13
) and provided
n
is sufficiently large

a_{i,h+1}\geq a_{i,h}+(n^{\varepsilon-2\delta}-1)>a_{i,h}.

Part (b).
Again, the proof is by induction on
j
. The base case
j=0
holds because
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{a_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is assumed to be non-decreasing. For some
j\in[s]
, assume that
a_{i,j-1}\leq a_{i+1,j-1}
holds for all
i\in[k-j]
.

Our goal is to show that
a_{i,j}\leq a_{i+1,j}
for all
i\in[k-j-1]
. If for some
i\in[k-j-1]
we have that
a_{i,j-1}=a_{i+1,j-1}
, then
a_{i,j}=a_{i+1,j}
by (
14
).

So we may assume that
a_{i+1,j-1}\geq a_{i,j-1}+1
. Then

a_{i+1,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\geq}}\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}a_{i+1,j-1}-1\geq\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}(a_{i,j-1}+1)-1\geq\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}a_{i,j-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\geq}}a_{i,j}.

Therefore for all
i\in[k-j-1]
we have
a_{i,j}\leq a_{i+1,j}
.

Part (c).
Note that
\frac{1}{1-m_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{k-j-1}a_{i,j}
is equal to
\sigma_{j}
by definition of

. Then,

\sigma_{j+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i=1}^{k-j-1}\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}a_{i,j}=(1+\lambda)\sigma_{j}.

Similarly, for the lower bound,

\sigma_{j+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{i=1}^{k-j-1}\left(\frac{(1+\lambda)}{1-m_{j}}a_{i,j}-1\right)=\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sigma_{j}-(k-j-1).

Now, Claim
4.2
(a) implies that
a_{i,j}\geq a_{i,0}\geq n^{\varepsilon}
for all
j\in[0,s]
,
i\in[k-j]
. Using inequality (
13
), provided

is sufficiently large, we get

\sigma_{j+1}\geq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}+\frac{n^{-2\delta}}{2}(k-j)n^{\varepsilon}-(k-j-1)\geq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}.

∎

The next Claim, in particular, shows that the construction of sequences
\mathcal{A}_{j}
’s ends after at most

steps.

Claim 4.3
.

Let the sequences
\mathcal{A}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{s}
and auxiliary

be constructed as described above, then

(a)

\sigma_{j}\leq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})n
for all
j\in[0,s]
.

(b)

m_{j}\geq n^{-\delta}
for all
j\in[0,s-1]
.

(c)

s<S
.

Proof.

We start with proving parts (a) and (b) with additional assumption that
j\leq S
. Then we prove part (c), which in turn implies parts (a), (b) for all
j\leq s
.

Part (a).
Assume
j\leq S
, then by repeatedly applying Claim
4.2
(c) and inequality (
12

\sigma_{j}\leq(1+\lambda)^{j}\sigma_{0}=(1+\lambda)^{S}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{a_{i,0}}\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon/2}{1-\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon)n=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)n.

Part (b).
Assume
j\leq S
. If
m_{j}
is defined, then

so by Claim

(a)

m_{j}=\frac{a_{k-j,j}}{\sigma_{j}}\geq\frac{n^{1-\delta}}{n}\geq n^{-\delta}.

Part (c).
Assume contrary, that is that the sequence
\mathcal{A}_{S}=\{a_{i,S}\}_{i=1}^{k-S}
is defined. For
h\in[0,S-1]
and
j\in[0,h]
define

f_{h,j}=\frac{a_{k-h,j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k-h}a_{i,j}}.

Note that
f_{h,h}=m_{h}
. In what follows we will show that
f_{h,h}\leq(1+\mu)f_{h,0}
for all
h\in[0,S-1]
and will use this to show that
a_{k}>n
, deriving a contradiction.

We will make use of a lower bound for each
a_{i,j}
with
j\in[S]
,
i\in[k-j]
. Claim
4.2
(a) implies that
a_{i,j}\geq a_{i,0}\geq n^{\varepsilon}
and so

a_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{eq:ii4})}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1+\lambda}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{i,j-1}-1\geq\frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{2}}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{i,j-1}+\frac{\lambda}{2}a_{i,j}-1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(\ref{ineq:ii3})}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{2}}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{i,j-1}+\frac{n^{-2\delta}}{2}n^{\varepsilon}-1.

Therefore, for large

,

(15)

a_{i,j}\geq\frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{2}}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{i,j-1}.

Then for any
h\in[0,S-1]
,
i\in[0,h]
by (

) and definition of

(

),

f_{h,j}=\frac{a_{k-h,j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k-h}a_{i,j}}\leq\frac{\frac{1+\lambda}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{k-h,j-1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k-h}\frac{1+\frac{\lambda}{2}}{1-m_{j-1}}a_{i,j-1}}=\frac{1+\lambda}{1+\frac{\lambda}{2}}f_{h,j-1}.

Therefore,
f_{h,j}\leq(1+\lambda)f_{h,j-1}
for all
h\in[0,S-1]
and

. Applying this inequality

m_{h}=f_{h,h}\leq(1+\lambda)^{h}f_{h,0}.

Now, for
h\in[0,S-1]
, by inequality (

),

m_{h}\leq(1+\mu)f_{h,0}.

Therefore,
f_{h,0}\geq\frac{1}{1+\mu}m_{h}
and by Claim

(b) we get that

for all

\displaystyle a_{k}

.
∎

We now show that if

is happy, than the preceding sequence

is also happy. To show that we prove a rather more general lemma that allows to verify that sequence of length

is happy, provided a certain sequence of length

is happy.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that a sequence
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy and that

is a positive proper fraction. If
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}
is a sequence of integers that satisfies

and

, then
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}
is also
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy.

Proof.

Let
\ell\in[t,(1+\varepsilon)t]
be the number of disjoint perfect matchings provided by the definition of
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
being
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy. We will show that
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy with
\ell^{\prime}=\ell+1\in[t+1,(1+\varepsilon)(t+1)]
.

In order to show that sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy, we need to show that for any
\ell^{\prime}
disjoint perfect matchings there are
t+1
of them

and vertex-disjoint matchings

such that
|A_{i}|\geq a_{i}
for all
i\in[t+1]
(then matching

can be taken to be
M=\bigcup A_{i}
).

Let
\ell^{\prime}
disjoint perfect matchings of
K_{2n}
be given and
M_{t+1}
be one of the matchings. Among
\ell=\ell^{\prime}-1
remaining matchings, due to
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
being
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy, there are
t
of them
M_{1},\ldots,M_{t}
and subsets
B_{i}\subseteq M_{i}
for
i\in[t]
such that all
B_{i}
’s are vertex disjoint and
|B_{i}|\geq b_{i}
for all
i\in[t]
.

In what follows we will find vertex disjoint matchings
A_{1},\ldots,A_{t+1}
such that
A_{t+1}\subseteq M_{t+1}
,
A_{i}\subseteq B_{i}
for
i\in[t]
and

(17)

|A_{i}|\geq\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)b_{i}-q\;\text{for all}\;i\in[t],\;\text{and}\;|A_{t+1}|\geq\frac{p}{q}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}\right)-3(t+1)q.

Existence of such sets

implies that the sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}
is
(\varepsilon,n)
-happy. Indeed, if
A_{1},\ldots,A_{t+1}
are such that (

) holds, then
|A_{i}|\geq a_{i}
for all
i\in[t+1]
.

In order to satisfy divisibility condition in Theorem
4.1
, for all
i\in[t]
let
B^{\prime}_{i}
be a largest subset of
B_{i}
such that
q
divides
|B^{\prime}_{i}|
, and
B^{\prime}_{t+1}
be a largest subset of
M_{t+1}
such that
q
divides
|B^{\prime}_{t+1}|
. Let
b^{\prime}_{i}=|B^{\prime}_{i}|
for
i\in[t+1]
, then

(18)

b^{\prime}_{i}\geq b_{i}-(q-1)\;\text{for}\;i\in[t]\;\text{and}\;b^{\prime}_{k+1}\geq n-(q-1).

We want to find sets
A_{i}\subseteq B_{i}^{\prime}
such that
|A_{i}|\geq a_{i}
for all
i\in[t+1]
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t+1}A_{i}
forms a matching. Since
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}
is a matching by our assumption, we only need to take care of incidences between edges of

and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}^{\prime}
. In what follows we will call the edges of
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}B_{i}^{\prime}

new.

The necklace needed to apply Theorem
4.1
is constructed in the following four steps.

Step 1.

We start with noticing that for

6.
Concluding remarks

Note that one may modify the proof of Theorem
1.5
allowing to prove a stronger form of Corollary
5.1
, namely that for any rational
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\leq 1
there are integers

and
n_{0}
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
and any

disjoint perfect matchings

of

there is a matching
M
such that
|M_{i}\cap M|\geq\alpha_{i}n-K
.

Finally, some of the authors of this paper believe that for any
\epsilon>0
there is
n_{0}
, such that if
n\geq n_{0}
, then any sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
that satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}<(1-\epsilon)n
is
(\epsilon,n)
-happy. The only sequences for which we know this to be true are covered by Theorem
1.3
.
