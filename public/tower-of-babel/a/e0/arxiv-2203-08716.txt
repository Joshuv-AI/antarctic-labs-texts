Quantum graphs: different perspectives, homomorphisms and quantum automorphisms
Matthew Daws

Abstract
We undertake a study of the notion of a quantum graph over arbitrary finite-dimensional C ∗ C^{*} -algebras B B equipped with arbitrary faithful states. Quantum graphs are realised principally as either certain operators on L 2 ​ ( B ) L^{2}(B) , the quantum adjacency matrices, or as certain operator bimodules over B ′ B^{\prime} . We present a simple, purely algebraic approach to proving equivalence between these settings, thus recovering existing results in the tracial state setting. For non-tracial states, our approach naturally suggests a generalisation of the operator bimodule definition, which takes account of (some aspect of) the modular automorphism group of the state. Furthermore, we show that each such “non-tracial” quantum graphs corresponds to a “tracial” quantum graph which satisfies an extra symmetry condition. We study homomorphisms (or CP-morphisms) of quantum graphs arising from UCP maps, and the closely related examples of quantum graphs constructed from UCP maps. We show that these constructions satisfy automatic bimodule properties. We study quantum automorphisms of quantum graphs, give a definition of what it means for a compact quantum group to act on an operator bimodule, and prove an equivalence between this definition, and the usual notion defined using a quantum adjacency matrix. We strive to give a relatively self-contained, elementary, account, in the hope this will be of use to other researchers in the field.

Notational conventions

We shall attempt to introduce terminology and technical background results, as needed, rather than collecting
them up-front. However, let us fix some conventions here. The inner-product on a Hilbert space
H
will be
written
(\cdot|\cdot)
and is assumed linear in the
right
variable. Write
\mathcal{B}(H),\mathcal{B}_{0}(H)
for
the algebras of bounded, respectively compact, operators on
H
. The identity on
H
is denoted by
1_{H}=1
, while
the identity map on other spaces is denoted by
\operatorname{id}
.

For
\xi,\eta\in H
, denote by
\omega_{\xi,\eta}
the functional
\mathcal{B}(H)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

x\mapsto(\xi|x(\eta))
. Then
\omega_{\xi,\eta}
is a normal functional, and the closed span of such functionals gives the space of trace-class operators, which we denote by
\mathcal{B}(H)_{*}
. Denote by
\theta_{\xi,\eta}
the rank-one operator
H\rightarrow H;\zeta\mapsto(\xi|\zeta)\eta
. Then
\theta_{\xi_{1},\eta_{1}}\circ\theta_{\xi_{2},\eta_{2}}=(\xi_{1}|\eta_{2})\theta_{\xi_{2},\eta_{1}}

which is a little awkward, but it means we are consistent in our convention that sesquilinear maps are linear on
the right.
We denote by
{\operatorname{Tr}}
the non-normalised trace on
\mathcal{B}(H)
, so
{\operatorname{Tr}}(\theta_{\xi,\eta})=(\xi|\eta)
, and
if
H\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
then
{\operatorname{Tr}}(1_{H})=n
.

Let
\overline{H}
be the
conjugate Hilbert space
to
H
, so vectors in
\overline{H}
are
\{\overline{\xi}:\xi\in H\}
with the same addition, but with the complex-conjugate scalar multiplication
\lambda\overline{\xi}=\overline{\overline{\lambda}\xi}
. For
x\in\mathcal{B}(H)
we define
x^{\top},\overline{x}\in\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})

by

x^{\top}(\overline{\xi})=\overline{x^{*}\xi},\quad\overline{x}(\overline{\xi})=\overline{x\xi}\qquad(\overline{\xi}\in\overline{H}).

Thus
(x^{\top})^{*}=(x^{*})^{\top}=\overline{x}
. The map
x\mapsto x^{\top}
is a linear anti-homomorphism
\mathcal{B}(H)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})
. If
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis for
H
then
(\overline{e}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

is an orthonormal basis for
\overline{H}
, so with respect to these bases we have that
\mathcal{B}(H)\cong\mathbb{M}_{n}

and
\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})\cong\mathbb{M}_{n}
, and
x\mapsto x^{\top}
is simply taking the transpose of a matrix.

Throughout the paper,
B
will denote a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra, and
\psi
will be a state on
B
,
always assumed faithful, with further conditions imposed if necessary.

Quantum adjacency matrices

We introduce the notion of a quantum adjacency matrix. We first briefly study finite-dimensional

C^{*}
-algebras. Any finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra
B
has the form

B\cong\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{M}_{n_{k}}.

The addition and multiplication in
B
respects this direct sum decomposition, and so often it suffices
to consider just a matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{n}
. We write
e_{ij}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
for the matrix units, so
a typical matrix
a=(a_{ij})
is
a=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}e_{ij}
, and the trace satisfies
{\operatorname{Tr}}(e_{ij})=\delta_{i,j}
the Kronecker delta. We write
a^{\top}
for the transpose of
a
(compare with Section
1.1
).

For
\psi\in\mathbb{M}_{n}^{*}
there is
Q\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
with
\psi(a)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa)
for each
a\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
;
indeed, set
Q_{ij}=\psi(e_{ji})
.
Then
\psi
being positive corresponds to
Q
being positive. If
\psi
is positive, then
\psi
being faithful
corresponds to
Q
being invertible, and
\psi
is a state exactly when
{\operatorname{Tr}}(Q)=1
. The same remarks hence apply
to
B
, choosing
Q
in each matrix factor.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
B,\psi
as throughout the paper, we shall denote by
Q\in B
the unique positive invertible element with

\psi(a)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa)
for each
a\in B
. Let
L^{2}(B)=L^{2}(B,\psi)
denote the GNS space for
\psi
.

Thus
L^{2}(B)
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Let
\Lambda:B\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
be the GNS map, which
in this situation is a linear isomorphism. If necessary, we write
\pi:B\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
for the GNS
representation, but we shall often suppress this. Hence

\pi(a)\Lambda(b)=a\Lambda(b)=\Lambda(ab),\quad\Lambda(a)=a\Lambda(1),\quad(\Lambda(a)|\Lambda(b))=\psi(a^{*}b)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa^{*}b)\qquad(a,b\in B).

Thus also
\Lambda\otimes\Lambda
is a linear isomorphism between
B\otimes B
and
L^{2}(B)\otimes L^{2}(B)
.
The multiplication map
m:B\otimes B\rightarrow B
may hence be regarded as a (bounded) operator

L^{2}(B)\otimes L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
. The identity
1\in B
induces a linear map
\mathbb{C}\rightarrow B;\lambda\mapsto\lambda 1
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
m:L^{2}(B)\otimes L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
be the multiplication map, and
m^{*}:L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)\otimes L^{2}(B)
its Hilbert space adjoint. Let
\eta:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
be induced by the identity
of
B
, and let
\eta^{*}:L^{2}(B)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be its Hilbert space adjoint.

Hence
\eta:\lambda\mapsto\lambda\Lambda(1)
and
\eta^{*}(\xi)=(\Lambda(1)|\xi)
for
\xi\in L^{2}(B)
, so

\eta^{*}(\Lambda(a))=\psi(a)
for

. Similarly,
m(\Lambda(a)\otimes\Lambda(b))=\Lambda(ab)
.

We can now proceed with the definition of a quantum adjacency matrix.

Definition 2.4
.

A
quantum adjacency matrix
is a self-adjoint operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
satisfying:

1.

m(A\otimes A)m^{*}=A
;

2.

(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(m^{*}\eta\otimes 1)=A
;

3.

m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=1
.

By symmetry we also define variations of these axioms:

4.

(\eta^{*}m\otimes 1)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(1\otimes m^{*}\eta)=A
;

5.

m(1\otimes A)m^{*}=1
.

Notice that, by taking the adjoint, axiom (
2
) holds for
A
if and only if axiom (
4
) holds for
A^{*}
. Hence, if
A
is self-adjoint, then axioms (
2
) and (
4
) are equivalent; in fact, these axioms are equivalent for any
A
, see Proposition
5.4
below.
We will show later that, in the presence of the other axioms,
also (
3
) and (
5
) are equivalent, see
Proposition
5.25
. To our knowledge, the only discussion of these “variations” is
in
[
33
, Section 2.2]
; in particular, here (
4
) is termed
A
being
“self-transpose”.

While the terminology is to call
A
a “matrix”, it is of course merely a linear map, as
L^{2}(B)
usually
carries no canonical choice of basis, and in our approach, we shall not think of
A
as an actual matrix,
except in the classical situation which motivates the more general theory.

There is some further discussion of variations of these axioms in
[
33
, Definition 2.19]
, and
we single out the following for later study.

Definition 2.5
.

Consider an operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
. Let

A_{0}:B\rightarrow B
be the linear map induced by
A
, if we regard
B
and
L^{2}(B)
as being linearly
isomorphic. We say that
A

A_{0}(a^{*})=A_{0}(a)^{*}

.

Let us see what happens in the classical situation, where
B=\ell^{\infty}(V)=C(V)
for a finite set

V
, and
\psi
is given by the uniform measure on
V
.
Let
(\delta_{v})_{v\in V}

\psi(\delta_{v})=1

Also

is given by the vector
\Lambda(1)=\sum_{v}\Lambda(\delta_{v})
, and

\eta^{*}\Lambda(\delta_{v})=1

m(A_{1}\otimes A_{2})m^{*}(e_{v})=m(A_{1}(e_{v})\otimes A_{2}(e_{v}))=\sum_{u,w}A^{(1)}_{u,v}A^{(2)}_{w,v}m(e_{u}\otimes e_{w})=\sum_{u}A^{(1)}_{u,v}A^{(2)}_{u,v}e_{u},

. Thus

is the

. Thus axiom

being real,
Definition
2.5

Similarly, we calculate that

\displaystyle(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)

\displaystyle(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(m^{*}\eta\otimes 1)(e_{v})=\sum_{u}(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(e_{u}\otimes e_{u}\otimes e_{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,w}(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)(e_{u}\otimes A_{w,u}e_{w}\otimes e_{v})=\sum_{u}e_{u}\otimes\eta^{*}(A_{v,u}e_{v})=\sum_{u}A_{v,u}e_{u}=A^{\top}(e_{v})

Thus axiom (
2
) is equivalent to

. In this situation, axiom
(

) gives the same condition. Finally,

\displaystyle m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}(e_{v})

\displaystyle=m(A(e_{v})\otimes e_{v})=A_{v,v}e_{v}

and so axiom (

4
Operator bimodules

The following definitions make perfect sense in the infinite-dimensional setting, and so we shall briefly work
in more generality. We follow
[

and
[

]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a von Neumann algebra. A

on

is a

-bimodule over

, that is, a weak

-closed subspace

We also call such an

. As stated, this definition depends on

and not just on

. However, the definition is essentially independent of the choice of

, compare

[
49
, Theorem 2.7]
. This will also follow from Theorem

.

Definition 4.2
.

A quantum relation
S
on

is
reflexive
if
M^{\prime}\subseteq S
, and is
symmetric
if

S^{*}=\{x^{*}:x\in S\}
equals

.

Notice that as
M^{\prime}SM^{\prime}\subseteq S
, we have that
S
is reflexive if and only if
1\in S
. Recall that an
operator system
is a unital, self-adjoint subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H)
, for some Hilbert space
H
.
[
29
,
49
]
define a
quantum graph
on
M\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
to be a reflexive, symmetric quantum relation on
M
. That is, a weak
∗
-closed operator system which is an operator bimodule over
M^{\prime}
.

We shall now specialise to the finite-dimensional setting, and also introduce some terminology of our own, separating out the conditions.

Definition 4.3
.

Given a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra

, a
quantum graph
on

is given by an embedding

for some finite-dimensional

which is an
operator bimodule over

. We define further that

is:

1.

when

is self-adjoint;

2.

has all loops
when

.

There is no dependence on our functional
\psi
.
Notice that it is rather easy to give examples: for example, when

we can choose

, and so

. Hence a quantum graph is simply a
(self-adjoint, unital) subspace of

.
There are two extremal choices if we assume undirected and that we have all loops.
We shall see later in Section

that this definition agrees with

2.8
and
2.10
.

Definition 4.4
.

Given

and any

is

.
The

is

.

In the classical situation, when

has basis

. Thus, given an operator bimodule

Proposition 4.5
.

With

biject with graphs on vertex set

is undirected if and only if the graph is, and the quantum graph has all loops if and only if the graph has a

We have already established a bijection between operator bimodules

over

and subsets

. Then

is undirected when it is self-adjoint, which clearly

. Further,

has all loops when

,
so that

for each

for all

.
∎

This result also holds for infinite

[

.

This notion of a quantum graph arose both from Weaver’s work in

]

5
Equivalence of definitions

We present a unified way to move between quantum adjacency matrices and the operator bimodule notion of a quantum
graph in the tracial case, and also show how to obtain a correspondence in the general setting, by adjusting
the definition of an operator bimodule to take account of (some aspect of) the functional
\psi
. Once we have
these results, we obtain a further link with the usual definition of an operator bimodule,
Section
5.3
. We then also look at super-operators.
We make some remarks about the existing literature.

We start by looking at a “toy version” of Tomita–Takesaki theory, which in our finite-dimensional situation
is easy: all of the following claimed properties may be verified by direct computation.
Recall the GNS construction
(L^{2}(B),\Lambda)
, and our invertible positive operator
Q\in B
. We use
functional calculus to define
Q^{z}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. We define
\sigma_{z}(a)=Q^{iz}aQ^{-iz}
for

a\in B
. This is the (analytic extension of the) modular automorphism group for
\psi
. Indeed, for

a,b,c\in B
,

\displaystyle(\Lambda(a)|\Lambda(bc))

\displaystyle=\psi(a^{*}bc)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa^{*}bc)={\operatorname{Tr}}(cQa^{*}b)={\operatorname{Tr}}(QQ^{-1}cQa^{*}b)=(\Lambda(aQc^{*}Q^{-1})|\Lambda(b))

\displaystyle=(\Lambda(a\sigma_{-i}(c^{*}))|\Lambda(b)).

Notice that
\sigma_{z}(a)^{*}=\sigma_{\overline{z}}(a^{*})
and
\sigma_{w}(\sigma_{z}(a))=\sigma_{w+z}(a)
for
each
a,z,w
. The
modular conjugation
is the anti-linear operator
J:L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
defined by

J\Lambda(a)=\Lambda(\sigma_{i/2}(a)^{*})=\Lambda(\sigma_{-i/2}(a^{*}))\qquad(a\in B).

One can easily check that
(J\xi|J\eta)=(\eta|\xi)
for each
\xi,\eta\in L^{2}(B)
and that
J^{2}=1
, so that

J
is an isometry. Furthermore,

\displaystyle JaJ\Lambda(b)

\displaystyle=J\Lambda(a\sigma_{-i/2}(b^{*}))=\Lambda(\sigma_{-i/2}(\sigma_{-i/2}(b^{*})^{*}a^{*}))

\displaystyle=\Lambda(\sigma_{-i/2}(\sigma_{i/2}(b)a^{*}))=\Lambda(b\sigma_{-i/2}(a^{*}))\qquad(a,b\in B)

(1)

and so
JaJ
is the operation of right multiplication by
\sigma_{i/2}(a)^{*}
. In fact,
B^{\prime}=JBJ

inside
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
. Notice that

J\theta_{\xi,\eta}J(\alpha)=J\big((\xi|J(\alpha))\eta\big)=(J(\alpha)|\xi)J(\eta)=(J(\xi)|\alpha)J(\eta)=\theta_{J(\xi),J(\eta)}(\alpha)\qquad(\xi,\eta,\alpha\in L^{2}(B)).

Recall that the
modular operator
is
\nabla\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))^{+}
given by

\nabla\Lambda(a)=\Lambda(\sigma_{-i}(a))=\Lambda(QaQ^{-1})
. More generally,

\nabla^{z}\Lambda(a)=\Lambda(\sigma_{-iz}(a))=\Lambda(Q^{z}aQ^{-z})
.

We now define two bijections which will be central to our arguments.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
B^{\operatorname{op}}
be the
opposite
algebra to
B
, so
B^{\operatorname{op}}
is the same vector space as
B
, and has the
same
*
-operation, but has the reversed product
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{op}}}}{{\cdot}}b=ba
. When it is clear from
context, we shall write the multiplication in
B^{\operatorname{op}}
just by juxtaposition.

As
L^{2}(B)
is finite-dimensional, the linear span of the operators
\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}
,
as
a,b\in B
vary, gives all of
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
. Given
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define bijections from

\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
to
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
by

\Psi_{t,s}=\Psi:\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}\mapsto\sigma_{it}(a)^{*}\otimes\sigma_{is}(b),\qquad\Psi^{\prime}_{t,s}=\Psi^{\prime}:\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}\mapsto\sigma_{it}(b)\otimes\sigma_{is}(a)^{*}.

As
\theta_{\cdot,\cdot}
is anti-linear in the first variable, and linear in the second, we see that

\Psi
is itself linear, and so extends by linearity to a bijection
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))\rightarrow B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
. Similarly,
\Psi^{\prime}
is linear. We work with
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
to match the existing literature,
which is natural for
\Psi^{\prime}
, but we shall shortly see that when working
with
\Psi
, it might have been more natural to consider
B^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes B
.

Definition 5.2
.

We write
\sigma
for the tensor swap map
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}\rightarrow B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}};a\otimes b\mapsto b\otimes a
, which is an anti-
*
-homomorphism.

We now see how the bijections
\Psi
and
\Psi^{\prime}
interact with the constructions which arise in
Definition
2.4
. We let the modular automorphism group act also on
B^{\operatorname{op}}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The bijection
\Psi=\Psi_{t,s}
gives the following correspondences between

and

1.

A^{*}
corresponds to
(\sigma_{i(s-t)}\otimes\sigma_{i(s-t)})\sigma(e^{*})

2.

(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(m^{*}\eta\otimes 1)

(\sigma_{-i(s+t)}\otimes\sigma_{i(t+s-1)})\sigma(e)
.

(\eta^{*}m\otimes 1)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(1\otimes m^{*}\eta)
corresponds to

(\sigma_{i(1-t-s)}\otimes\sigma_{i(s+t)})\sigma(e)
.

4.

we have that
\Psi(m(A_{1}\otimes A_{2})m^{*})=e_{2}e_{1}
for
e_{i}=\Psi(A_{i})
,

.

Proof.

By linearity, it suffices to consider the case when
A=\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}
and so

. Then
A^{*}=\theta_{\Lambda(b),\Lambda(a)}
which
corresponds to

as claimed for (
1

Set
T=(1\otimes\eta^{*}m)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)(m^{*}\eta\otimes 1)
. Let

, so that

for each

\displaystyle(\Lambda(x)|T\Lambda(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big(\Lambda(x)\otimes\Lambda(1)\big|(1\otimes m)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)\xi_{i}\otimes\eta_{i}\otimes\Lambda(y)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big(\Lambda(x)\otimes\Lambda(1)\big|(1\otimes m)\xi_{i}\otimes\Lambda(b)\otimes\Lambda(y)\big)(\Lambda(a)|\eta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(\Lambda(x)|\xi_{i})(\Lambda(1)|\Lambda(by))(\Lambda(a)|\eta_{i})

\displaystyle=(\Lambda(xa)|\Lambda(1))(\Lambda(1)|\Lambda(by))=(\Lambda(x)|\Lambda(\sigma_{-i}(a^{*})))(\Lambda(b^{*})|\Lambda(y)).

Hence

).

Similarly, if we now set

\displaystyle(\Lambda(x)|T\Lambda(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big(\Lambda(1)\otimes\Lambda(x)\big|(m\otimes 1)(1\otimes A\otimes 1)\Lambda(y)\otimes\xi_{i}\otimes\eta_{i}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\big(\Lambda(1)\otimes\Lambda(x)\big|\Lambda(yb)\otimes\eta_{i}\big)(\Lambda(a)|\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(\Lambda(1)|\Lambda(yb))(\Lambda(x)|\eta_{i}\big)(\Lambda(a)|\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=(\Lambda(\sigma_{-i}(b^{*}))|\Lambda(y))(\Lambda(x)|\Lambda(a^{*})).

Thus

Given

∎

5.1
From projections to subspaces

In this section,
H
will be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space with
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
(which is equivalent,
of course, to there being an injective
*
-homomorphism
B\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
which allows us to identify
B

with its image in
\mathcal{B}(H)
). Then
B^{\operatorname{op}}
is naturally a subalgebra of
\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})
where we identify

a\in B^{\operatorname{op}}
with
a^{\top}\in\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})
. Consequently,
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H\otimes\overline{H})
. For the following, notice that
(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}
is also naturally a subalgebra of

\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})
, where we identify
x\in(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
with
x^{\top}
. A simple calculation
shows that then
(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}=(B^{\operatorname{op}})^{\prime}
as subalgebras of
\mathcal{B}(\overline{H})
. Hence
B^{\prime}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H\otimes\overline{H})
.

Lemma 5.8
.

The commutant of
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H\otimes\overline{H})
is
B^{\prime}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}
.

Proof.

The result follows from Tomita’s theorem,
[
42
, Theorem 5.9, Chapter IV]
:
(B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}})^{\prime}=B^{\prime}\otimes(B^{\operatorname{op}})^{\prime}\cong B^{\prime}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}
.
∎

As
H
is finite-dimensional, every operator on
\mathcal{B}(H)
is in the linear span of the rank-one operators,
and so there is a linear isomorphism

\mathcal{B}(H)\cong H\otimes\overline{H};\quad\theta_{\xi,\eta}\mapsto\eta\otimes\overline{\xi}\qquad(\xi,\eta\in H).

Indeed, this is simply the GNS map for the functional
{\operatorname{Tr}}
on
\mathcal{B}(H)
, or equivalently, comes from
identifying
\mathcal{B}(H)
with the Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
H
.

Remark 5.9
.

We make links with the existing literature, and in particular
[
49
, Proposition 2.23]
where a bijection
between quantum relations
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
and left ideals in
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
is established (when
B
is
finite-dimensional). A key technical
tool for Weaver is the action
\Phi
of
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
on
\mathcal{B}(H)
given by

\Phi_{a\otimes b}(x)=abx\qquad(a\otimes b\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}},x\in\mathcal{B}(H)).

Once we identify
\mathcal{B}(H)
with
H\otimes\overline{H}
, we find that

(a\otimes b)\cdot(\eta\otimes\overline{\xi})\cong\Phi_{a\otimes b}(\theta_{\xi,\eta})=a\theta_{\xi,\eta}b=\theta_{b^{*}(\xi),a(\eta)}\cong a(\eta)\otimes b^{\top}(\overline{\xi}).

That is, we obtain the natural action of
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
on
H\otimes\overline{H}
.

Elsewhere in the literature, links with completely bounded maps (and implicitly with the (extended) Haagerup tensor
product) are made, compare
[
26
]
, or
[
25
, Section 2.4]
.
In contrast, our perspective in this
section is that it is Hilbert Space techniques which are vitally important, for which
H
being finite-dimensional
seems essential.
∎

An elementary result from von Neumann algebra theory is that invariant subspaces for a von Neumann algebra
M

biject with projections in the commutant
M^{\prime}
. We would like to have a version of this result for (possibly)
non-self-adjoint idempotents.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
M\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
be a von Neumann algebra. Let
e\in\mathcal{B}(H)
be an idempotent,
e^{2}=e
, and let

H_{0}={\operatorname{Im}}(e),H_{1}=\ker(e)
. Then
H=H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
(not necessarily orthogonal). Furthermore,

e\in M
if and only if
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
are invariant subspaces for
M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Any
\xi\in H
is written as
\xi=e(\xi)+(\xi-e(\xi))\in H_{0}+H_{1}
, and if
\xi\in H_{0}\cap H_{1}
then

\xi=e(\xi)=0
. Thus
H=H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
as a direct sum of Banach spaces (that is, a not necessarily
orthogonal direct sum). If
e\in M
then given
n\in M^{\prime}
, for
\xi\in H_{0}
we see that
n(\xi)=ne(\xi)=en(\xi)
so
n(\xi)\in H_{0}
, while for
\xi\in H_{1}
we have that
en(\xi)=ne(\xi)=0
so
n(\xi)\in H_{1}
.
Conversely, if
H_{0},H_{1}
are
M^{\prime}
-invariant, then to show that
e\in M
, by the bicommutant theorem, it
suffices to show that
ne=en
for each
n\in M^{\prime}
. Given
\xi\in H
write
\xi=\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}\in H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
, so
ne(\xi)=n(\xi_{0})=en(\xi_{0})
as
n(\xi_{0})\in H_{0}
, while
n(\xi)=n(\xi_{0})+n(\xi_{1})\in H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
so
en(\xi)=en(\xi_{0})
because
en(\xi_{1})=0
. Hence
ne(\xi)=en(\xi)
as required.
∎

We now collect some definitions which will allow us to talk about properties of subspaces of
H\otimes\overline{H}

which correspond to the properties of idempotents
e\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
which occurred in the previous section.

Definition 5.11
.

Given
H=H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
not necessarily orthogonal, we shall say that the idempotent
e\in\mathcal{B}(H)

with
{\operatorname{Im}}(e)=H_{0},\ker(e)=H_{1}
is the
projection onto
H_{0}
along
H_{1}
.

We define an anti-linear map

J_{0}:H\otimes\overline{H}\rightarrow H\otimes\overline{H};\quad\eta\otimes\overline{\xi}\mapsto\xi\otimes\overline{\eta}.

This corresponds to the adjoint on
\mathcal{B}(H)
, and is in fact the modular conjugation with respect to the
functional
{\operatorname{Tr}}
on
\mathcal{B}(H)
.

Let
\hat{1}\in H\otimes\overline{H}
correspond to the identity operator
1\in\mathcal{B}(H)
.

Let
(e_{i})
be some orthonormal basis for
H
. As
1=\sum_{i}\theta_{e_{i},e_{i}}
we find that

\hat{1}=\sum_{i}e_{i}\otimes\overline{e_{i}},

and that this is independent of the choice of
(e_{i})
.

Theorem 5.12
.

There is a bijection between idempotents
e\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
and decompositions

where

and
W
are

-invariant subspaces of

being the projection onto

along

1.

e=\sigma(e^{*})
if and only if

and
J_{0}(W)=W
;

2.

m(e)=1
if and only if
\hat{1}\in V
. (Here

is the multiplication map).

Proof.

The bijection follows from Lemma
5.10
combined with Lemma
5.8
.

When
e=a\otimes b
acting as
a\otimes b^{\top}
on
H\otimes\overline{H}
, so that
e(\eta\otimes\overline{\xi})=a(\eta)\otimes\overline{b^{*}(\xi)}
, we see that
\sigma(e^{*})=b^{*}\otimes a^{*}
, so that

\sigma(e^{*})(\eta\otimes\overline{\xi})=b^{*}(\eta)\otimes\overline{a(\xi)}=J_{0}eJ_{0}(\eta\otimes\overline{\xi}).

Thus in general
\sigma(e^{*})=J_{0}eJ_{0}
, and so

e=\sigma(e^{*})
if and only if
eJ_{0}=J_{0}e
. If this holds, then

J_{0}(V)=J_{0}e(H\otimes\overline{H})=eJ_{0}(H\otimes\overline{H})=V
, while also if
\xi\in W
then

eJ_{0}(\xi)=J_{0}e(\xi)=0
so
J_{0}(\xi)\in\ker(e)=W
and hence
W=J_{0}(W)
(as
J_{0}^{2}=1
).

Conversely, if
J_{0}(V)=V
and
J_{0}(W)=W
, then given
\xi\in H\otimes\overline{H}
take the decomposition

\xi=v+w\in V\oplus W
. By assumption,
J_{0}(\xi)
has decomposition
J_{0}(v)+J_{0}(w)\in V\oplus W
.
Hence
e(\xi)=v
and
eJ_{0}(\xi)=J_{0}(v)
, and so

\sigma(e^{*})(\xi)=J_{0}eJ_{0}(\xi)=J_{0}J_{0}(v)=v=e(\xi).

Hence

, and we have shown (

Again, when
e=a\otimes b
, and with
(e_{i})
some orthonormal basis of

,

\displaystyle e(\hat{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a(e_{i})\otimes\overline{b^{*}(e_{i})}=\sum_{i,j,k}(e_{j}|a(e_{i}))e_{j}\otimes(\overline{e_{k}}|\overline{b^{*}(e_{i})})\overline{e_{k}}=\sum_{i,j,k}(a^{*}(e_{j})|e_{i})(e_{i}|b(e_{k}))e_{j}\otimes\overline{e_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}(a^{*}(e_{j})|b(e_{k}))e_{j}\otimes\overline{e_{k}}=\sum_{j,k}(e_{j}|m(e)(e_{k}))e_{j}\otimes\overline{e_{k}}.

Thus, if
m(e)=1
then
e(\hat{1})=\hat{1}
. Conversely, if
e(\hat{1})=\hat{1}
then
(e_{j}|m(e)(e_{k}))=\delta_{j,k}
for each

so that

. So (
2
) holds.
∎

In the orthogonal case, we recover, in particular,
[
49
, Proposition 2.23]
.

Corollary 5.13
.

There is a bijection between
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodules

and projections

,
under which

is self-adjoint if and only if

, and

if and only if

Under the bijection between

and

, we see that

and

in the previous theorem.

If
J_{0}(S)=S
then for

and

∎

5.4
Adjacency super-operators to matrices

In this section we show that Definitions
3.1
and
2.4
are equivalent.
This was shown in
[
14
, Section 1.2]
in the case when
B=\mathbb{M}_{n}
, using the
Choi matrix

of a completely positive map. We shall give an account of how to extend this formalism to general
B
,
which might be of independent interest. As such, we proceed with a little more generality than strictly needed.
We shall also use the language of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, see
[
31
]
for example, but we shall not
use any deep results from this theory.

In this section, let
C
be an arbitrary
C^{*}
-algebra, and let
B,\psi
be as usual.
In the usual way, regard
B^{*}
as a bimodule over
B
. In particular, for
a\in B
, we denote by

\psi a\in B^{*}
the functional
b\mapsto\psi(ab)
. Every
f\in B^{*}
is of the form
\psi a
for some
a\in B
.
This follows, as if not, by Hahn-Banach, there is a non-zero
b\in B^{**}=B
with
0=(\psi a)(b)=\psi(ab)

for all
a
. Choosing
a=b^{*}
shows that
\psi(b^{*}b)=0
so
b=0
, contradiction. Furthermore, notice that

B\rightarrow B^{*},a\mapsto\psi a
is injective, as
(\psi a)(a^{*})=\psi(aa^{*})=0
only when
a=0
, again using
that
\psi
is faithful.

As
B
is finite-dimensional,
\mathcal{B}(B,C)
is spanned by finite-rank operators, that is, the span of operators of
the form
\theta_{\psi a,c}
for
a\in B,c\in C
, where
\theta_{\psi a,c}
is the map
B\ni b\mapsto\psi(ab)c\in C
. In fact, it will be profitable to again introduce the modular automorphism group,
and consider the map

\Psi^{\prime}:\mathcal{B}(B,C)\rightarrow C\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}};\quad\theta_{\psi a,c}\mapsto c\otimes\sigma_{-i/2}(a).

Theorem 5.36
.

\Psi^{\prime}
restricts to a bijection between the completely positive maps
B\rightarrow C
, and positive
elements
e\in C\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
.

The “usual” Choi matrix, compare
[
12
, Proposition 1.5.12]
associates a CP map
A:\mathbb{M}_{n}\rightarrow C
with the positive operator
(A(e_{ij}))\in M_{n}(C)
. Let us see this in our generalisation.
Set
B=\mathbb{M}_{n}
and
\psi={\operatorname{Tr}}
.
We identify
B^{\operatorname{op}}
with
\mathbb{M}_{n}
using the matrix transpose, and so our bijection identifies

A=\theta_{\psi a,c}
with
u=c\otimes a^{\top}\in C\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}\cong M_{n}(C)
.
Then
A(e_{ij})=\psi(ae_{ij})c=a_{ji}c=(a^{\top})_{ij}c=u_{ij}
, exactly as we expect.

To prove the theorem,
we shall use the language of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, in particular, the KSGNS construction, which we now
briefly recall from
[
31
, Chapter 5]
. Given a completely
positive map
A:B\rightarrow C
, we consider the algebraic tensor product
B\odot C
, and define on this a
(in general, degenerate) positive
C
-valued form by

\big(b_{1}\otimes c_{1}\big|b_{2}\otimes c_{2}\big)=c_{1}^{*}A(b_{1}^{*}b_{2})c_{2}.

The separation completion is denoted by
B\otimes_{A}C
, and we continue to denote by
b\otimes c
the
equivalence class in
B\otimes_{A}C
defined by this simple tensor. Almost by definition,
B\otimes_{A}C

is a (right) Hilbert
C^{*}
-module over
C
, and there is a
*
-homomorphism
\pi:B\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(B\otimes_{A}C)
given by
\pi(b)(b^{\prime}\otimes c)=bb^{\prime}\otimes c
. Finally, there is

V\in\mathcal{L}(C,B\otimes_{A}C)
defined by
V(c)=1\otimes c
, and then

A(b)=V^{*}\pi(b)V\qquad(b\in B),

giving a canonical dilation for the CP map
A
.

Proof of Theorem
5.36
.

Suppose that
A:B\rightarrow C
is completely positive, and set
e=\Psi^{\prime}(A)
. Perform the KSGNS construction
to form
E=B\otimes_{A}C,\pi
and
V
. We wish to describe
E
in terms of
e
. For the moment
suppose that
A=\theta_{\psi a,c}
, so that
e=c\otimes\sigma_{-i/2}(a)
, and

\displaystyle\big(b_{1}\otimes c_{1}\big|b_{2}\otimes c_{2}\big)_{E}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{*}A(b_{1}^{*}b_{2})c_{2}=\psi(ab_{1}^{*}b_{2})c_{1}^{*}cc_{2}=\psi(\sigma_{i/2}(b_{2})\sigma_{-i/2}(ab_{1}^{*}))c_{1}^{*}cc_{2}

\displaystyle=\psi(\sigma_{i/2}(b_{2})\sigma_{-i/2}(a)\sigma_{i/2}(b_{1})^{*})c_{1}^{*}cc_{2}

\displaystyle=(\psi^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes\operatorname{id})\big((\sigma_{i/2}(b_{1})\otimes c_{1})^{*}\sigma(e)(\sigma_{i/2}(b_{2})\otimes c_{2})\big),

where the final product is formed in
B^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes C
and
\sigma
is again the swap map, and
\psi^{\operatorname{op}}
is
as in Lemma
5.32
. By linearity, this relationship holds for general

.

Let
\mu
be a state on
C
, with GNS construction
(L^{2}(C,\mu),\Lambda_{\mu})

\displaystyle(u|\sigma(e)u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\big(\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}}(b_{i})\otimes\Lambda_{\mu}(c_{i})\big|\sigma(e)\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}}(b_{j})\otimes\Lambda_{\mu}(c_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(\psi^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes\mu)\big((b_{i}\otimes c_{i})^{*}\sigma(e)(b_{j}\otimes c_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mu\big((\sigma_{-i/2}(b_{i})\otimes c_{i}|\sigma_{-i/2}(b_{j})\otimes c_{j})_{E}\big)\geq 0.

As this holds for all

in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B^{\operatorname{op}})\otimes L^{2}(C,\mu))

was arbitrary, this shows that

We now consider the converse, assume that

is positive, and aim to show that

is
completely positive. We copy the KSGNS construction, but now
define

a sesquilinear form on

by

\big(u_{1}\big|u_{2}\big)=(\psi^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes\operatorname{id})\big((\sigma_{i/2}\otimes\operatorname{id})(u_{1})^{*}\sigma(e)(\sigma_{i/2}\otimes\operatorname{id})(u_{2})\big)\qquad(u_{1},u_{2}\in B^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes C).

be the separation completion (as

We shall repeatedly move between

We shall write the product in

.
We now show that

Notice that working in

, and so

\|\pi(b)(u)\|_{E}^{2}=(\psi^{\operatorname{op}}\otimes\operatorname{id})\big((\sigma_{i/2}(b)^{*}\otimes 1)(\sigma_{i/2}\otimes\operatorname{id})(u)^{*}\sigma(e)(\sigma_{i/2}\otimes\operatorname{id})(u)(\sigma_{i/2}(b)\otimes 1)\big).

.

, say

\displaystyle\|\pi(b)(u)\|_{E}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\psi^{\operatorname{op}}\big(\sigma_{i/2}(b)^{*}b_{i}^{*}b_{j}\sigma_{i/2}(b)\big)c_{i}^{*}c_{j}=\sum_{i,j}\psi\big(\sigma_{i/2}(b)\cdot b_{j}\cdot b_{i}^{*}\cdot\sigma_{i/2}(b)^{*}\big)c_{i}^{*}c_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\psi\big(\sigma_{-i/2}(b_{i})^{*}\cdot b^{*}\cdot b\cdot\sigma_{-i/2}(b_{j})\big)c_{i}^{*}c_{j}.

is well-defined.

is a

-homomorphism.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose that

6.2
From Quantum Channels

We now explore a class of examples (of quantum graphs in the sense of Definition
4.3
)
which come from quantum channels, see
[
16
,
21
,
22
,
41
]
. These examples are amongst the
first which arose, and motivate much of the theory. We shall generalise the constructions to arbitrary
finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebras, and at the same time streamline them, using the Stinespring representation
theorem directly, rather than passing via Kraus operators.

Quantum Channels are usually considered to be trace-preserving, completely positive maps between
matrix algebras. From an operator algebraic perspective, this means they are most naturally considered to
be maps between (pre)duals of algebras: namely trace-class operators. As such, we prefer to work with
the Banach space adjoints, and instead look at unital, completely positive maps. We shall make comments
below where this leads to slightly different conventions.

Remark 6.3
.

Classically we consider the following situation. Let
X,Y
be finite sets, and for each
x\in X
imagine
we send
x
down a noisy communication channel, so that
y\in Y
may be received with probability
p(y|x)\in[0,1]
. Thus
\sum_{y}p(y|x)=1
for each
x
. This gives a map
\ell^{1}(X)\rightarrow\ell^{1}(Y)
, say
sending a unit basis vector
\delta_{x}
to
\sum_{y}p(y|x)\delta_{y}
. The adjoint is a (completely) positive,
unital map
\theta:\ell^{\infty}(Y)\rightarrow\ell^{\infty}(X)
given by

\theta(f)(x)=\sum_{y}p(y|x)f(y)\qquad(f\in\ell^{\infty}(Y)).

We say that
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
are “confusable” if there is some
y
with both
p(y|x_{1})>0
and
p(y|x_{2})>0
,
that is, non-zero chance that
y
can be received if either of
x_{1}
or
x_{2}
is sent. This establishes
a symmetric, reflexive relation on
X
, that is, a graph structure on
X
. The associated

\ell^{\infty}(X)
-bimodule is

S=\operatorname{lin}\{e_{x_{1},x_{2}}:\exists\,y\in Y,\ p(y|x_{1})p(y|x_{2})>0\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(X)).

We shall see shortly how to express this
S
using
\theta
directly.
∎

We shall use a form of the Stinespring representation theorem, which follows
[
42
, Theorem 3.6, Chapter IV]
.
For
C^{*}
-algebras
B,C
with
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
, given a UCP map
\theta:C\rightarrow B
there is a Hilbert space

K
, a non-degenerate
*
-homomorphism
\pi:C\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K)
, a normal unital
*
-homomorphism
\rho:\theta(C)^{\prime}\rightarrow\pi(C)^{\prime}
, and an isometric linear operator
V:H\rightarrow K
with

\theta(c)=V^{*}\pi(c)V\quad(c\in C),\qquad\rho(x)V=Vx,\quad(x\in\theta(C)^{\prime}).

As
\theta(C)\subseteq B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
, we can, and will, always restrict
\rho
to
B^{\prime}
. If we have
the minimality condition that
K=\overline{\operatorname{lin}}\{\pi(c)V\xi:c\in C,\xi\in H\}
, then this construction
is unique up to unitary conjugation in the obvious way. When (as is our case)
B,H
are finite-dimensional,
a minimal
K
is also finite-dimensional.

The following will be immediate from more general considerations below in Section
7
,
see Remark
7.6
, but we give the proof, as it will in part motivate the
more general constructions to be given later.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
C
be some
C^{*}
-algebra, let
B
as usual be a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra with
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)

faithfully represented. Given a UCP map
\theta:C\rightarrow B
, construct
V,\rho,\pi,K
as above, and define

\hat{\theta}:\pi(C)^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
by
\hat{\theta}(y)=V^{*}yV
. Then
S=\hat{\theta}(\pi(C)^{\prime})
is a
quantum graph over
B
in the sense of Definition
4.3
. This definition of
S
is independent
of the choice of
V,\rho,\pi,K
(so in particular,
K
is not required to be minimal).

Proof.

As
V
is an isometry,
V^{*}V=1
, and so
\hat{\theta}
is UCP, from which it immediately follows that

S
is a self-adjoint unital subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H)
. Given
x_{1},x_{2}\in B^{\prime}
, as
\rho(x_{1}),\rho(x_{2})\in\pi(C)^{\prime}
,
we find that

x_{1}V^{*}y_{1}Vx_{2}=V^{*}\rho(x_{1})y\rho(x_{2})V\in S\qquad(y\in\pi(C)^{\prime}).

Thus
S
is a
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodule.

To show that
S
does not depend upon the Stinespring dilation chosen, we shall suppose that
K
is minimal,
pick another dilation
\tilde{V},\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\pi},\tilde{K}
, and show that the resulting
\tilde{S}

agrees with
S
. We claim that we may define
u:K\rightarrow\tilde{K}
by
\pi(a)V\xi\mapsto\tilde{\pi}(a)\tilde{V}\xi
. As

(\tilde{\pi}(a)\tilde{V}\xi|\tilde{\pi}(b)\tilde{V}\eta)=(\xi|\tilde{V}^{*}\tilde{\pi}(a^{*}b)\tilde{V}\eta)=\theta(a^{*}b)=(\pi(a)V\xi|\pi(b)V\eta),

(or more actually, repeating this calculation with linear spans)
it follows that
u
is well-defined, and an isometry. By minimality,
u
is defined on all of
K
(but
u

may not be surjective). Notice that
uV=\tilde{V}
, and it is easily verified that
\tilde{\pi}(c)u=u\pi(c)
for

c\in C
, and hence also
u^{*}\tilde{\pi}(c)=\pi(c)u^{*}
, as
\pi,\tilde{\pi}
are
*
-homomorphisms.
Given
y\in\tilde{\pi}(C)^{\prime}
we find that

\pi(c)u^{*}yu=u^{*}\tilde{\pi}(c)yu=u^{*}y\tilde{\pi}(c)u=u^{*}yu\pi(c)\qquad(c\in C).

Thus
u^{*}yu\in\pi(C)^{\prime}
and so
S\ni V^{*}u^{*}yuV=\tilde{V}^{*}y\tilde{V}
, and hence
\tilde{S}\subseteq S
.
For the converse, consider now
x\in\pi(C)^{\prime}
and set
y=uxu^{*}
. Similarly, we show that
y\in\tilde{\pi}(C)^{\prime}
, and
as
u
is an isometry,
u^{*}u=1
so
u^{*}\tilde{V}=V
, hence
\tilde{S}\ni\tilde{V}^{*}y\tilde{V}=V^{*}xV
,
so that
S\subseteq\tilde{S}
.
∎

Remark 6.5

Continuing Remark

Remark 6.6
.

Suppose that

6.3
The no-loops condition

In this section, we compare the “loops” condition
m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=1
with the “no loops” condition

m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=0
. We also see what this means for the operator bimodule picture of quantum graphs.
Into such considerations also comes the non-commutative generalisation of taking a graph complement, which
we consider first.

Recall that, when we view
B
as a direct sum of matrix algebras, the operator
mm^{*}
is a direct sum of scalar
multiples of the identity (equivalently,
mm^{*}
is in the centre of
B
) and is invertible, compare the
discussion around Definition
2.10
. We state the following for axioms
(
3
) and (
6
) of Definition
2.4
, but we know that in the
presence of the other axioms, these are equivalent to axioms (
5
) and
(
7
) respectively.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be self-adjoint and define
A^{\prime}=A-(mm^{*})^{-1}
which is also self-adjoint.
The following are equivalent:

1.

A
satisfies axioms (
1
) and
(
2
) of Definition
2.4
, and (
3
), namely
m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=1
;

2.

A^{\prime}
satisfies axioms (
1
) and
(
2
), and (
6
), namely
m(A^{\prime}\otimes 1)m^{*}=0
.

Proof.

We know that
(mm^{*})^{-1}
is itself a quantum adjacency matrix, see after Definition
2.10
.
As the axiom (
2
) is linear in
A
, clearly
A
satisfies axiom
(
2
) if and only if
A^{\prime}
does. Similarly,
m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=1
if and only if

m(A^{\prime}\otimes 1)m^{*}=0
; and
m(1\otimes A)m^{*}=1
if and only if
m(1\otimes A^{\prime})m^{*}=0
. Recall that in
the presence of the other axioms,
A
satisfies
m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}=1
if and only if
m(1\otimes A)m^{*}=1
.

It hence remains to consider axiom (
1
). As
B
is a direct sum of
n
matrix
algebras, also
L^{2}(B)
is the direct sum of Hilbert spaces
(H_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
, and
m,m^{*}
and hence
(mm^{*})^{-1}

all respect this decomposition. Let
\xi\in H_{k}
, so
m^{*}(\xi)\in H_{k}\otimes H_{k}
. There is a scalar

\delta_{k}>0
with
(mm^{*})^{-1}
equal to
\delta_{k}^{2}
on
H_{k}
. Hence

\displaystyle m(A^{\prime}\otimes A^{\prime})m^{*}(\xi)

\displaystyle=m(A\otimes A)m^{*}(\xi)-m((mm^{*})^{-1}\otimes A)m^{*}(\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-m(A\otimes(mm^{*})^{-1})m^{*}(\xi)+m((mm^{*})^{-1}\otimes(mm^{*})^{-1})m^{*}(\xi)

\displaystyle=A(\xi)-\delta_{k}^{2}m(1\otimes A)m^{*}(\xi)-\delta_{k}^{2}m(A\otimes 1)m^{*}(\xi)+\delta_{k}^{2}\xi

as
mm^{*}(\xi)=\delta_{k}^{-2}\xi
. Thus, if (
1
) holds,
then also
m(1\otimes A)m^{*}=1
, so
m(A^{\prime}\otimes A^{\prime})m^{*}(\xi)=A^{\prime}(\xi)
for each
\xi\in H_{k}
, for each
k
.
Thus (
2
) holds.

Conversely, let (
2
) hold. By Proposition
5.26
, also

m(1\otimes A^{\prime})m^{*}=0
. Using that
A=A^{\prime}+(mm^{*})^{-1}
an analogous calculation to the above shows that

m(A\otimes A)m^{*}(\xi)=A^{\prime}(\xi)+\delta_{k}^{2}m(1\otimes A^{\prime})m^{*}(\xi)+\delta_{k}^{2}m(A^{\prime}\otimes 1)m^{*}(\xi)+\delta_{k}^{2}\xi,

and so this equals
A^{\prime}(\xi)+\delta_{k}^{2}\xi
, for
\xi\in H_{k}
. As
k
was arbitrary,
m(A\otimes A)m^{*}=A
,
and so (
1
) holds.
∎

The previous proposition shows that we can move between “all loops” and “no loops”. We next look at the
notion of a “complement”, which classically would take an “all loops” graph to a “no loops” graph. We
will discuss an intermediate process, which preserves “all loops”, respectively, “no loops”, below.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be self-adjoint and define
A^{\prime\prime}=\theta_{\Lambda(1),\Lambda(1)}-A
which is also
self-adjoint. The following are equivalent:

1.

satisfies axioms (
1
) and
(
2
) of Definition
2.4
.

2.

A^{\prime\prime}
satisfies axioms (
1
) and
(
2
).

Furthermore,
A
satisfies axiom (
3
) if and only if
A^{\prime\prime}
satisfies axiom
(
6
); and
A
satisfies axiom (
6
) if and only if
A^{\prime\prime}

satisfies axiom (
3
).

Proof.

As remarked after Definition
2.8
,
A_{0}=\theta_{\Lambda(1),\Lambda(1)}
is itself a quantum
adjacency matrix. By linearity of the axioms (
2
) through
(
7
), we need only consider axiom (

).
Now observe that

m(A^{\prime\prime}\otimes A^{\prime\prime})m^{*}=m(A_{0}\otimes A_{0})m^{*}-m(A_{0}\otimes A)m^{*}-m(A\otimes A_{0})m^{*}+m(A\otimes A)m^{*},

where we know that

. For any

,

m(A_{0}\otimes\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)})m^{*}=m(\theta_{\Lambda(1),\Lambda(1)}\otimes\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)})m^{*}=\theta_{\Lambda(1a),\Lambda(1b)}=\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)},

.
∎

Proposition 6.12

. Give

which allows us to form the orthogonal

1.

Proof.

Using Theorem

we associate
A
with a projection

, and
S
is the image
of
e
(once we identify
\mathcal{B}(H)
with its GNS space

). Let
(mm^{*})^{-1}

correspond to projection
e_{\min}
, which from Proposition

is the minimal projection
fixing
u_{0}
, and corresponds to the bimodule

Consider (
1
), where
A

(

Now consider (

∎

To be precise,

-operator bimodule, so is

is. When
\psi

.

Remark 6.13
.

This is one place where Theorem
5.29
does not interact as we might wish.
Recall that we associate
S
to the “tracial” quantum graph
S_{0}=Q^{1/2}S
. A simple calculation shows that

(Q^{1/2}S)^{\perp}=Q^{-1/2}S^{\perp}
. Hence

(S^{\prime})_{0}=Q^{1/2}S^{\prime}=Q^{1/2}S\cap Q^{1/2}(Q^{-1/2}B^{\prime})^{\perp}=S_{0}\cap Q(B^{\prime})^{\perp},

and not
S_{0}\cap(B^{\prime})^{\perp}
as we might hope. Similarly,
(S^{\prime\prime})_{0}=Q^{1/2}S^{\perp}=(Q^{-1/2}S)^{\perp}=(Q^{-1}S_{0})^{\perp}=QS_{0}^{\perp}
, and
(S_{c})_{0}=Q^{1/2}S^{\perp}+B^{\prime}=QS_{0}^{\perp}+B^{\prime}
, and finally

(S_{nc})_{0}=Q^{1/2}S^{\perp}\cap Q(B^{\prime})^{\perp}=QS_{0}^{\perp}\cap Q(B^{\prime})^{\perp}
. See Remark

below for an example suggesting that we cannot improve this.

However, we do note that if
QSQ^{-1}=S
then the same is true of

, as given

, we

{\operatorname{Tr}}(y^{*}QxQ^{-1})={\operatorname{Tr}}(Q^{-1}y^{*}Qx)={\operatorname{Tr}}((QyQ^{-1})^{*}x)=0

. So

preserves all the properties of Theorem

.
∎

6.4
Small examples

A careful classification of quantum adjacency matrices over
B=\mathbb{M}_{2}
is made in
[
33
, Section 3]
.
Let us see how one part of our work intersects with the non-tracial case,
[
33
, Section 3.3]
.
Using Theorem
5.17
, we in particular wish to study subspaces
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)

with
QSQ^{-1}\subseteq S
, or equivalently
V\subseteq H\otimes\overline{H}
with
(Q\otimes(Q^{-1})^{\top})(V)\subseteq V
. With this in mind, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.14
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, let
T\in\mathcal{B}(H)^{+}
be invertible, and let
V\subseteq H
be
a subspace. Then
T(V)\subseteq V
if and only if
V
has a basis of eigenvectors of
T
.

Proof.

We need only show the “only if” clause.
From Lemma
5.16
we know that
T(V)=V
, and so also
(tT)^{n}(V)=V
for any

t>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. At
T
is self-adjoint, we can write
T=\sum_{i}t_{i}P_{i}
where
\{t_{i}\}
are the
eigenvalues of
T
, and each
P_{i}
is the projection onto the eigenspace associated to
t_{i}
. Thus the
\{P_{i}\}

are mutually orthogonal, and
\sum_{i}P_{i}=1
. Suppose
t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots
, and let
\xi\in V
. Then

(t_{1}^{-1}T)^{n}(\xi)=P_{1}(\xi)+\sum_{i\geq 2}(t_{1}^{-1}t_{i})^{n}P_{i}(\xi),

which converges to
P_{1}(\xi)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus
P_{1}(\xi)\in V
. Now repeat the argument,
applying
(t_{2}^{-1}T)^{n}
to
\xi-P_{1}(\xi)
, to conclude that also
P_{2}(\xi)\in V
. Continue, and
conclude that
P_{i}(\xi)\in V
for each
i
.

Thus
P_{i}(V)\subseteq V
for each
i
, and so
V
is the orthogonal direct sum of the subspaces
P_{i}(V)

(some of which may be
\{0\}
). The result follows.
∎

Given
Q
, we can diagonalise, and so find a unitary
u\in B
and a (component-wise) diagonal
Q_{d}\in B

with
uQu^{*}=Q_{d}
. If
S
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
5.17
for

Q_{d}
, then
T=u^{*}Su
satisfies the conditions for
Q
. As
u\in B
, it is easy to see that this
procedure correctly interacts with Theorem
5.29
; it also interacts smoothly
with taking complements etc., Proposition
6.12
. Finally, the notion of
“equivalence” is compatible with the notion of automorphism we explore in Section
8
,
and with the obvious notion of “isomorphism” between two quantum graphs, as mentioned elsewhere in
the literature. Thus, there is no loss of generality in working with
Q_{d}
.

When
B=\mathbb{M}_{2}
, say acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, we have that
B^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}
, and any
Q
is similar to
a Powers density

Q_{q}=\frac{1}{1+q^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&q^{2}\end{pmatrix},

with
0<q\leq 1
, compare
[
33
, Section 3.3]
. We will not consider the tracial case, so assume

q\not=1
. With Lemma
6.14
in mind, the eigenspaces to consider are

\operatorname{lin}\Big\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\Big\},\quad\mathbb{C}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbb{C}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

As
S
should also be self-adjoint, there are just four quantum graphs over
\mathbb{M}_{2}
with
Q_{d}SQ_{d}^{-1}=S

and
Q_{d}^{-1/2}\in S
(this from Proposition
5.25
), namely

\displaystyle S_{1}=\mathbb{C}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1/q\end{pmatrix},\qquad S_{3}=\mathbb{M}_{2},

\displaystyle S_{2A}=\operatorname{lin}\Big\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1/q\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\Big\},\quad S_{2B}=\operatorname{lin}\Big\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\Big\}.

Here
S_{1}
is the empty quantum graph,
S_{3}
the complete quantum graph, and
S_{2A},S_{2B}
lie strictly
between these, and are incomparable. A simple computation shows that taking the complement,

S\mapsto S_{c}
from Proposition
6.12
, interchanges
S_{1},S_{3}
and

S_{2A},S_{2B}
. If we apply Theorem
5.29
, then
(S_{1})_{0}
is the span of
the identity,
(S_{2A})_{0}
is the span of the identity and the off-diagonal matrices, and
S_{2B},S_{3}

remain unchanged. Thus, in this special case, Theorem
5.29
interacts as we might
hope with the complement.

Remark 6.15
.

We present an example where Theorem
5.29
really does not interact with the
complement. Let
B=\mathbb{M}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{M}_{2}
acting on
H=\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so that
B^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}
. Pick
0<t<1/2
and set

Q_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&1-t\end{pmatrix},\quad Q_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1-t&0\\
0&t\end{pmatrix},\quad Q=Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}.

We identify
\mathcal{B}(H)
with
2\times 2
matrices of operators in
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\cong\mathbb{M}_{2}
.
Set

x_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{M}_{2},\quad x=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{0}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(H).

Finally let
T=\operatorname{lin}\{x,x^{*}\}
, so
T
is a self-adjoint,
B^{\prime}
-bimodule. Then

Q_{1}x_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\
1-t&0\end{pmatrix}\not\in\mathbb{C}x_{0}\quad\implies\quad Qx=\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{1}x_{0}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

while

Q_{1}x_{0}Q_{2}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\
1-t&0\end{pmatrix}Q_{2}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=x_{0}\quad\implies\quad QxQ^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{1}x_{0}Q_{2}^{-1}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in T,

from which it follows that
QTQ^{-1}=T
.

By the final part of Remark
6.13
,
S_{0}=T^{\perp}
also satisfies the required conditions,
and so by Theorem
5.29
,
S=Q^{-1/2}S_{0}
satisfies all the conditions of
Theorem
5.17
. Towards a contradiction, suppose that
(S_{c})_{0}=(S_{0})^{\perp}+B^{\prime}
. By Remark
6.13
, always
(S_{c})_{0}=Q(S_{0})^{\perp}+B^{\prime}
, so we obtain that

(S_{0})^{\perp}+B^{\prime}=Q(S_{0})^{\perp}+B^{\prime}
, that is,
T+B^{\prime}=QT+B^{\prime}
, that is,
QT\subseteq T+B^{\prime}
.
However, given the form of
Qx
computed above, it is clear that
Qx\not\in T+B^{\prime}
, giving the required
contradiction.
∎

7
Homomorphisms

In this section, we look at various notions of “homomorphism” between quantum graph. We note that there
have been notions of a “quantum homomorphism” between classical graphs (see
[
36
, Section 4]

or
[
34
, Theorem 4.14, Remark 5.5]
for example) which can further be extended to quantum homomorphisms
between quantum graphs. These notions are linked to the quantum automorphisms we discuss in
Section
9
below, and
are discussed in detail in
[
27
, Section 4.4]
,
[
33
, Section 2.3]
, for example.
In contrast, in this section we look at what might be thought of as “classical homomorphisms” between
(possibly) quantum graphs. Such notions seem to be less firmly established than other definitions made in
this paper, so we compare and contrast them, without offering a definitive definition.

We start with two ideas from Weaver in
[
50
]
; again we work with quantum graphs in the sense of Definition
4.3
. For exactly what we mean by “Kraus form” see the discussion below.
It is not at all clear that the following definition is independent of the choice of Kraus form, but this
follows from
[
50
]
, or our, different, argument to follow.

Definition 7.1

(See
[
50
, Theorem 7.4, Theorem 8.2]
)
.

Let
B_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{i})
for
i=1,2
for finite-dimensional Hilbert space
H_{i}
. Let
\theta:B_{2}\rightarrow B_{1}
be a UCP map with Kraus form
\theta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{*}xb_{i}
for some operators

b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(H_{1},H_{2})
. Let
S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{1})
be a quantum graph. The
pushfoward
of

S_{1}
along
\theta
, denoted
\overrightarrow{S_{1}}
is the
B_{2}^{\prime}
-operator bimodule generated by

\{b_{i}xb_{j}^{*}:x\in S_{1}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{2}).

Let
S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{2})
be a quantum graph. The
pullback
of
S_{2}
along
\theta
is,
denoted by
\overleftarrow{S_{2}}
is the
B_{1}^{\prime}
-operator bimodule generated by

\{b_{i}^{*}xb_{j}:x\in S_{2}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{1}).

Again here we have translated from trace-preserving CP maps to UCP maps, equivalently, have “reversed
the arrows”, compare with Remark
6.3
above. We notice that the pullback
is always unital if
S_{2}
is unital, but the pushfoward may not be, in accordance with what happens in
the classical situation. While we stated this for quantum graphs, obviously the definition works for any
quantum relation. The reader who would like some motivation can see Remark
7.15
below.

The paper
[
50
]
develops an “intrinsic” way to characterise quantum graphs (making use of results
from
[
49
]
), and then shows that the pushfoward and pullback only depend on
\theta

(and not the Kraus form chosen) when recast using this intrinsic characterisation of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.
We shall show how to use Stinespring dilations (and a duality argument) to give a different approach to this fact. A biproduct of our construction is that in the above definition of the pullback, it suffices to take the linear span of the set given: it is automatically operator bimodule over the relevant commutant. We also give a link between pushforwards and pullbacks via a duality argument.

For most of this section, we work with possibly infinite-dimensional operator bimodules, as this is not much more work. Let us recall how to construct the Kraus form of a normal CP map between arbitrary von Neumann algebras.
This is surely known, but we are not aware of a canonical reference. Let
M,N
be von Neumann algebras
acting on Hilbert spaces
H_{M},H_{N}
respectively. We start by observing that the usual
Stinespring dilation of a normal CP map
\theta:M\rightarrow N
between von Neumann algebras, say
V,\rho,\pi,K

automatically has that
\pi
is normal. Consider now an arbitrary dilation (so we do not assume minimality).
By the structure theorem for normal
*
-homomorphisms between
von Neumann algebras (for example,
[
42
, Theorem 5.5, Chapter IV]
) there is an index set
I
and an isometry

W:K\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes\ell^{2}(I)
with
W^{*}(x\otimes 1)W=\pi(x)
for
x\in N
.
Furthermore, the projection
WW^{*}
is in
M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(I))
. Then

\theta(x)=V^{*}\pi(x)V=V^{*}W^{*}(x\otimes 1)WV\qquad(x\in M),

and there is a family of operators
(b_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{N},H_{M})
with
WV\xi=\sum_{i}b_{i}\xi\otimes\delta_{i}

and hence
\theta(x)=\sum_{i}b_{i}^{*}xb_{i}
gives us a Kraus representation.

So far, it is not clear how the property that
\theta
maps into
N
(and not just
\mathcal{B}(H_{N})
) is
reflected in the Kraus representation.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\theta:M\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{N})
be a normal CP map with some Stinespring dilation
V,\pi,K
, meaning that

\pi:M\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K)
is a normal unital
*
-homomorphism,
V:H_{N}\rightarrow K
an operator,
and
\theta(x)=V^{*}\pi(x)V
for
x\in M
; no minimality assumption is made. The following are equivalent:

1.

\theta
maps into
N
;

2.

there is a normal unital
*
-homomorphism
\rho:N^{\prime}\rightarrow\pi(M)^{\prime}
with
Vx^{\prime}=\rho(x^{\prime})V
for
x^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\theta
maps into
N
.
Let
V_{0},\pi_{0},K_{0},\rho_{0}
be the minimal Stinespring dilation for
\theta
. We may define
u:K_{0}\rightarrow K
by
u\pi_{0}(x)V_{0}\xi=\pi(x)V\xi
for
x\in M,\xi\in H_{N}
. As

(\pi(x)V\xi|\pi(y)V\eta)=(\xi|\theta(x^{*}y)\eta)=(\pi_{0}(x)V_{0}\xi|\pi_{0}(y)V_{0}\eta)\qquad(x,y\in M,\xi,\eta\in H_{N}),

it follows that
u
is well-defined, an isometry, and by minimality, extends by linearity and continuity
to all of
K_{0}
. By construction,
u\pi_{0}(x)=\pi(x)u
for
x\in M
, and
uV_{0}=V
.
Define
\rho(x^{\prime})=u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})u^{*}
which is a normal
*
-homomorphism, as
u^{*}u=1
. It follows that

\rho(x^{\prime})V=u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})u^{*}(uV_{0})=u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})V_{0}=uV_{0}x^{\prime}=Vx^{\prime}\qquad(x^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}).

Furthermore, for
x^{\prime}\in N^{\prime},x\in M
, we calculate that
\rho(x^{\prime})\pi(x)=u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})u^{*}\pi(x)=u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})\pi_{0}(x)u^{*}=u\pi_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})u^{*}=\pi(x)u\rho_{0}(x^{\prime})u^{*}=\pi(x)\rho(x^{\prime})
.
Thus
\rho
maps into
\pi(M)^{\prime}
.

Conversely, if we have such a
\rho
then for
x^{\prime}\in N^{\prime},x\in M
, we see that
\theta(x)x^{\prime}=V^{*}\pi(x)Vx^{\prime}=V^{*}\pi(x)\rho(x^{\prime})V=V^{*}\rho(x^{\prime})\pi(x)V=x^{\prime}V^{*}\pi(x)V=x^{\prime}\theta(x)
. Thus
\theta(x)\in N^{\prime\prime}=N
,
as claimed.
∎

As there is a bijection between dilations
V,\pi,K
with
K=H_{M}\otimes\ell^{2}(I)
and
\pi(x)=x\otimes 1
,
and Kraus forms
\theta(x)=\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}^{*}xb_{i}
, this lemma can be translated into a (slightly
cumbersome) condition on a Kraus form.

Weaver shows in
[
49
, Theorem 2.7]
that the definition of a Quantum Relation, Definition
4.1
,
is independent of the ambient Hilbert space
H
upon which our von Neumann algebra
M
acts. We will study
the ideas of this proof, and separate them out into a number of useful constructions. Let
M\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{M})

be a von Neumann algebra, let
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{M})
be a quantum relation on
M
, and let
H
be some other
Hilbert space. Let
\pi:M\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{M}\otimes H);x\mapsto x\otimes 1
and define

S_{\pi}=S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H),

the weak
∗
-closed linear span of
\{x\otimes a:x\in S,a\in\mathcal{B}(H)\}
in
\mathcal{B}(H_{M}\otimes H)
. It is routine
that
\pi(M)^{\prime}=M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
, and so
S_{\pi}
is a quantum relation on
\pi(M)
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Every quantum relation on
\pi(M)
is of the form
S_{\pi}
for some quantum relation
S
on
M
, and the
map
S\mapsto S_{\pi}
is an order-preserving bijection, and preserves the properties of
S
being self-adjoint,
respectively,
S
being unital.

Proof.

The only non-obvious thing to check is that every quantum relation on
\pi(M)
is of the form
S_{\pi}
.
Let
T
be a weak
∗
-closed operator bimodule for
\pi(M)^{\prime}=M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
. For
x\in T
, consider

(1\otimes\theta_{\xi_{1},\eta_{1}})x(1\otimes\theta_{\xi_{2},\eta_{2}})=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\omega_{\xi_{1},\eta_{2}})(x)\otimes\theta_{\xi_{2},\eta_{1}}\in T\qquad(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in H).

Let
S=\{(\operatorname{id}\otimes\omega)(x):x\in T,\omega\in\mathcal{B}(H)_{*}\}
a weak
∗
-closed subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H_{M})
which is
seen to be an
M^{\prime}
-bimodule. By linearity, and that
T
is weak
∗
-closed, it follows that
y\otimes a\in T

for each

. Furthermore, we can approximate
x\in T
in the weak

-topology by
multiplying on the left and right by

for suitable finite-rank operators
\theta
, and so

is in
S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
. Thus
T=S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)=S_{\pi}
, as required.
∎

Now let
\theta:M\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K)
be a normal unital
*
-homomorphism, not assumed injective. Again by the
structure theorem for such homomorphism,
[
42

, there is some

H
and an isometry
u:K\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H
such that
u^{*}(x\otimes 1)u=\theta(x)
for
x\in M
,
and with
uu^{*}\in M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
; equivalently,
(x\otimes 1)u=u\theta(x)
for
x\in M
. Define

S_{\theta}=u^{*}S_{\pi}u=\{u^{*}\alpha u:\alpha\in S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K),

where
\pi(x)=x\otimes 1
as before. Notice that the definition of
S_{\theta}
generalises the definition of

S_{\pi}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

The definition of
S_{\theta}
is independent of the choice of
u,H
, and
S_{\theta}
is a quantum relation
on
\theta(M)
. The map
S\mapsto S_{\theta}
is order preserving, and preserves the properties of
S
being
self-adjoint, respectively,
S
being unital.

Proof.

We show independence of
u,H
. Let
u_{1}:K\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H_{1}
be an isometry with

u_{1}\theta(x)=(x\otimes 1)u_{1}
for each

S_{1}=u_{1}^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H_{1}))u_{1}
.

Set
H_{0}=H\oplus H_{1}
and let
\iota:H\rightarrow H_{0}
be the inclusion. For
x\otimes a\in S\otimes\mathcal{B}(H_{0})
the algebraic tensor product, clearly
x\otimes\iota^{*}a\iota\in S\otimes\mathcal{B}(H)
.
Thus by weak
∗
-continuity,
((1\otimes\iota)u)^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H_{0}))(1\otimes\iota)u\subseteq S_{\theta}
.
However, the same reasoning shows that

((1\otimes\iota)u)^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H_{0}))(1\otimes\iota)u=S_{\theta}
.

with

, and similarly
for

. So consider

as maps

With these identifications, still

, and hence

Define

is a
\theta(M)^{\prime}
-operator

To show that

is weak
∗

[

for example, which tells us that it suffices to show that the (norm closed) unit ball of

is

∗

converging weak
∗

to

is a bounded net, but for each

. Of course,

The remaining claims are clear.

The following shows how to compute the pullback from a Stinespring dilation directly (not using the Kraus
form) and shows that even the Kraus form definition is already an

-operator bimodule.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let

along

-closed

-operator bimodule generated by elements

∗
-closed subspace generated by elements

-operator bimodule;

2.

\theta

\overleftarrow{S}
is the weak
∗
-closure of

V^{*}S_{\pi}V

While these results show that
\overleftarrow{S}
is independent of the choice of Kraus representative of
\theta:M\rightarrow N

[

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
\theta:M\rightarrow N
and
S
be as in Theorem
7.5
. Let
K_{M}
be a (possibly) different Hilbert space with
M\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K_{M})
, and let
S_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K_{M})
be the quantum relation corresponding to
S
. Then
\overleftarrow{S}=\overleftarrow{S_{K}}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi:M\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K_{M})
be the formal inclusion, an injective normal
*
-homomorphism. Then
S_{K}=S_{\phi}
in the sense of Lemma
7.4
. Indeed, choose
H
and an isometry
u:K_{M}\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H
with
(x\otimes 1)u=u\phi(x)
for
x\in M
, and then
S_{\phi}=u^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H))u
.

By Theorem
7.5
, if
V,\pi,K
is any Stinespring dilation of
\theta
, then
\overleftarrow{S}
is the weak
∗
-closure of
V^{*}S_{\pi}V
. By the discussion above, we can choose
K=H_{M}\otimes K^{\prime}
and
\pi(x)=x\otimes 1
for
x\in M
. Then
S_{\pi}=S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime})
. We now consider
\theta^{\prime}:\phi(M)\rightarrow N
given by
\theta^{\prime}=\theta\circ\phi^{-1}
. It is natural to consider
\pi^{\prime}=\pi\circ\phi^{-1}
; we find a dilation for
\pi^{\prime}
. Let
\xi_{0}\in H
be some unit vector, and define
\iota:H_{M}\otimes K^{\prime}\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H\otimes K^{\prime}
by
\xi\otimes\xi^{\prime}\mapsto\xi\otimes\xi_{0}\otimes\xi^{\prime}
.
Then let
U
be the composition

H_{M}\otimes K^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\iota}H_{M}\otimes H\otimes K^{\prime}\xrightarrow{u^{*}\otimes 1}K_{M}\otimes K^{\prime}.

Then for
x\in M
, as
uu^{*}\in M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
,

\displaystyle(\phi(x)\otimes 1)U

\displaystyle=(u^{*}(x\otimes 1)u\otimes 1)(u^{*}\otimes 1)\iota=(u^{*}\otimes 1)(x\otimes 1\otimes 1)(uu^{*}\otimes 1)\iota

\displaystyle=(u^{*}uu^{*}\otimes 1)(x\otimes 1\otimes 1)\iota=(u^{*}\otimes 1)(x\otimes 1\otimes 1)\iota

\displaystyle=(u^{*}\otimes 1)\iota(x\otimes 1)=U(x\otimes 1)=U\pi^{\prime}(\phi(x)).

Hence
(y\otimes 1)U=U\pi^{\prime}(y)
for

, so we have a dilation for
\pi^{\prime}
, and so
V,\pi^{\prime},K
is a dilation for
\theta^{\prime}
. Hence
\overleftarrow{S_{K}}=\overleftarrow{S_{\phi}}
is the weak
∗
-closure of

\displaystyle V^{*}(S_{\phi})_{\pi^{\prime}}V

\displaystyle=V^{*}U^{*}(S_{\phi}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))UV=V^{*}U^{*}(u^{*}\otimes 1)(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))(u\otimes 1)UV

\displaystyle=V^{*}\iota^{*}(uu^{*}\otimes 1)(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))(uu^{*}\otimes 1)\iota V

\displaystyle\subseteq V^{*}\iota^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))\iota V

because

and

is a

-bimodule. As
\iota^{*}z\iota=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\omega_{\xi_{0}}\otimes\operatorname{id})z
for

, we find that

\displaystyle V^{*}\iota^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))\iota V=V^{*}(S\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(K^{\prime}))V=V^{*}S_{\pi}V\subseteq\overleftarrow{S}.

We have shown that
\overleftarrow{S_{K}}\subseteq\overleftarrow{S}
, and reversing the roles of
H_{M}
and

shows the reverse inclusion, hence giving equality, as claimed.

While these results gives a rather pleasing form for the pullback at the level of operator bimodules, we
have not been able to formulate a simple result for the associated projections and quantum adjacency matrices.

We now consider pushforwards. Let
\theta:M\rightarrow N
be a normal CP map with Kraus form
\theta(x)=\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}^{*}xb_{i}
, and now let
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{N})
be a quantum relation over
N
. In the infinite-dimensional setting, we define
\overrightarrow{S}
to be the weak
∗
-closed
M^{\prime}
-bimodule generated by
\{b_{i}xb_{j}^{*}:x\in S\}
, which we shall denote by
\text{w${}^{*}$-$M^{\prime}$-bimod }\{b_{i}xb_{j}^{*}:x\in S\}
. Let
U:H_{N}\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes\ell^{2}(I)
be
\xi\mapsto\sum_{i}b_{i}(\xi)\otimes\delta_{i}
, so that
\theta(x)=U^{*}(x\otimes 1)U
for
x\in M
. Then

(\operatorname{id}\otimes\omega_{\delta_{i},\delta_{j}})(UxU^{*})=b_{i}xb_{j}^{*}\qquad(x\in S,i,j\in I).

Hence
\overrightarrow{S}=\text{w${}^{*}$-$M^{\prime}$-bimod }(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(I))_{*})(USU^{*})
, an alternative form which is useful in calculations. We now proceed to show that the definition of
\overrightarrow{S}
is independent of the various choices made.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
\theta:M\rightarrow N
be a normal CP map, and let
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{N})
be a quantum relation over
N
. The definition of
\overrightarrow{S}
is independent of the choice of Kraus form for
\theta
.

Proof.

Notice that we are free to enlarge the index set
I
, by setting “extra” Kraus operators
b_{i}
to be zero. Thus, without loss of generality, two different dilations of
\theta
may be assumed to be given by
U:H_{N}\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H
and
V:H_{N}\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H
for the same Hilbert space
H
. Let
K
be the closed linear span of
\{(x\otimes 1)U\xi:\xi\in H_{N},x\in M\}\subseteq H_{M}\otimes H
. We may define
u:K\rightarrow H_{M}\otimes H
by
u:(x\otimes 1)U\xi\mapsto(x\otimes 1)V\xi
, and linearity and continuity. Indeed, we calculate that

((x\otimes 1)V\xi|(y\otimes 1)V\eta)=(\xi|\theta(x^{*}y)\eta)=((x\otimes 1)U\xi|(y\otimes 1)U\eta)\qquad(\xi,\eta\in H_{N},x,y\in M).

This calculation shows that
u
is well-defined and an isometry. Extend
u
to a partial isomerty defined on all of
H_{M}\otimes H
. By construction, for
x\in M
we have that
u(x\otimes 1)=(x\otimes 1)u
on
K
, and as
K
is invariant for
M\otimes 1
, it follows that
u
commutes with
x\otimes 1
on all of
H_{M}\otimes H
, so that
u\in M^{\prime}\bar{\otimes}\mathcal{B}(H)
. By definition,
uU=V
, so

(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{B}(H)_{*})(VSV^{*})=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{B}(H)_{*})(uUSU^{*}u^{*})\subseteq\text{w${}^{*}$-$M^{\prime}$-bimod }(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{B}(H)_{*})(USU^{*}).

Reversing the roles of
U
and
V
gives the other inclusion, hence equality, which shows that using
U
or
V
gives the same definition of
\overrightarrow{S}
.
∎

We now show the analogue of Proposition
7.8
for pushforwards.

.

Let
\theta:M\rightarrow N
be a normal CP map, and let

. Let
N\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K_{N})

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
be finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebras equipped with faithful traces
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
. Let
\theta:B_{2}\rightarrow B_{1}
be a completely positive map. Define
\hat{\theta}:B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}
by

\psi_{1}(a\theta(b))=\psi_{2}(\hat{\theta}(a)b)\qquad(a\in B_{1},b\in B_{2}).

Then
\hat{\theta}
exists, and is completely positive.
\hat{\theta}
is UCP if and only if
\theta
is trace
preserving:
\psi_{1}\circ\theta=\psi_{2}
.

Proof.

As
\psi_{2}
is faithful, any functional
f
on
B_{2}
arises from a unique
b_{0}\in B_{2}
as
f(b)=\psi_{2}(b_{0}b)

for
b\in B_{2}
, compare with the discussion at the start of Section
5.4
.
Applying this to the functional
b\mapsto\psi_{1}(a\theta(b))
gives the existence and
uniqueness of
\hat{\theta}(a)
. Assuming that
\theta
is positive, for
a\geq 0
,

\psi_{2}(b^{*}\hat{\theta}(a)b)=\psi_{2}(\hat{\theta}(a)bb^{*})=\psi_{1}(a\theta(bb^{*}))=\psi_{1}(a^{1/2}\theta(bb^{*})a^{1/2})\geq 0\qquad(b\in B_{2}).

This shows that
\hat{\theta}(a)\geq 0
, so
\hat{\theta}
is positive.

Consider now
\hat{\theta}\otimes\operatorname{id}:B_{1}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}\rightarrow B_{2}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}
.
Giving
\mathbb{M}_{n}
the trace
{\operatorname{Tr}}
and hence
B_{i}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}
the faithful trace
\psi_{i}\otimes{\operatorname{Tr}}
,
it follows readily that
\hat{\theta}\otimes\operatorname{id}
and
\theta\otimes\operatorname{id}
are related in the same way, and hence
the previous argument shows that
\hat{\theta}\otimes\operatorname{id}
is positive when
\theta\otimes\operatorname{id}
is. It follows that

\theta
CP implies that
\hat{\theta}
is CP.

Finally,
\psi_{2}(\hat{\theta}(1)b)=\psi_{1}(\theta(b))
and so
\hat{\theta}(1)=1
if and only if
\theta
is
trace preserving.
∎

Lemma 7.12
.

Let

As everything respects the direct sum decomposition, it suffices to prove this in the case

with
\psi=n{\operatorname{Tr}}
. We identify the GNS space

with
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\overline{\mathbb{C}^{n}}

, as then

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Tr}}_{L^{2}(B)}(a\otimes 1)

Proposition 7.13

For

b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(H_{1},H_{2})
. Let
(x_{j})_{j=1}^{m}\subseteq B_{2}
be such that
(\Lambda(x_{j}))
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(B_{2})
.
Then
\hat{\theta}
has Kraus form
\hat{\theta}(y)=\sum_{i,j}Jx_{j}Jb_{i}yb_{i}^{*}Jx_{j}^{*}J
for
y\in B_{1}
, where
J
is the modular conjugation on
L^{2}(B_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
U:L^{2}(B_{1})\rightarrow L^{2}(B_{2})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
be
\xi\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\xi)\otimes\delta_{i}
, so that
\theta(x)=U^{*}(x\otimes 1)U
for
x\in B_{2}
. As
\psi_{2}
is a trace, we have in particular that
Jb^{*}J\Lambda(a)=\Lambda(ab)=a\Lambda(b)
for
a,b\in B_{2}
. Define

V:L^{2}(B_{2})\rightarrow L^{2}(B_{1})\otimes L^{2}(B_{2})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n};\quad\xi\mapsto\sum_{i,j}U^{*}(Jx_{j}^{*}J\xi\otimes\delta_{i})\otimes\Lambda(x_{j})\otimes\delta_{i}.

For
b,c\in B_{2}
and
a\in B_{1}
we see that

\displaystyle\big(\Lambda(b)\big|V^{*}(a\otimes 1)V\Lambda(c)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\big(U^{*}(b\Lambda(x_{j})\otimes\delta_{i})\big|aU^{*}(c\Lambda(x_{j})\otimes\delta_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=({\operatorname{Tr}}_{L^{2}(B_{2})}\otimes{\operatorname{Tr}}_{n})\big((b^{*}\otimes 1)UaU^{*}(c\otimes 1)\big)

\displaystyle={\operatorname{Tr}}_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\big(aU^{*}(cb^{*}\otimes 1)U\big)={\operatorname{Tr}}_{L^{2}(B_{1})}(a\theta(cb^{*}))=\psi_{1}(a\theta(cb^{*}))

\displaystyle=\psi_{2}(\hat{\theta}(a)cb^{*})=\psi_{2}(b^{*}\hat{\theta}(a)c)=\big(\Lambda(b)\big|\hat{\theta}(a)\Lambda(c)\big),

here using Lemma
7.12
.
Hence
V^{*}(a\otimes 1)V=\hat{\theta}(a)
.
The Kraus operators are hence given by

c_{i,j}(\xi)=U^{*}(Jx_{j}^{*}J\xi\otimes\delta_{i})=b_{i}^{*}Jx_{j}^{*}J(\xi)\qquad(\xi\in L^{2}(B_{2})),

which gives
\hat{\theta}(a)=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}^{*}ac_{i,j}=\sum_{i,j}Jx_{j}Jb_{i}ab_{i}^{*}Jx_{j}^{*}J
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 7.14
.

Let
\theta:B_{2}\rightarrow B_{1}
be a CP map, and let
S
be a quantum graph over
B_{1}
. Form
\hat{\theta}:B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}
using the Markov traces on
B_{1},B_{2}
, and use this to form the pullback
\overleftarrow{S}
. Then
\overrightarrow{S}=\overleftarrow{S}
.

Proof.

We continue with the notation of Proposition
7.13
. By Corollary
7.7
, Proposition
7.8
and Proposition
7.13
,

\overleftarrow{S}=\operatorname{lin}\{Jx_{l}Jb_{k}yb_{i}^{*}Jx_{j}^{*}J:y\in S\}.

As
Jx_{j}J\in B_{2}^{\prime}
for each
j
, and as
(x_{j})
is a basis for
B_{2}
, we see that

\overleftarrow{S}=\text{$B_{2}^{\prime}$-bimodule generated by }\{b_{k}yb_{i}^{*}:y\in S\}=\overrightarrow{S},

as claimed. In this final step, we use Proposition
7.10
which tells us that we are free to represent
B_{1}
on
L^{2}(B_{1})
without changing
\overrightarrow{S}
, and use Proposition
7.9
to use a Kraus representation of our choice.
∎

We have not discussed quantum graphs which arise from quantum adjacency matrices with respect to a non-tracial

, this is equivalent to studying quantum graphs

with the

QSQ^{-1}=S

7.1
Further notions

We give a quick summary of some of the other suggestions for what a “homomorphism” for a quantum graph
should be, and related ideas.

In
[
50
, Section 6]
given a quantum graph
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
over
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
and a
projection
p\in B
, the
restriction

S_{p}
is
pSp
, the quantum graph over
pBp\subseteq\mathcal{B}(p(H))
.
The map
\theta:B\rightarrow pBp,a\mapsto pap
is UCP with Stinespring dilation
\pi=\operatorname{id},K=H,V=p
, and so

pSp
is simply the pullback of
S
along this UCP map.

In
[
41
, Definition 7]
, given quantum graphs
S_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H_{i})
over
\mathcal{B}(H_{i})
for
i=1,2
,
a
homomorphism

S_{1}\rightarrow S_{2}
is defined to be a UCP map
\theta:\mathcal{B}(H_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H_{1})

with Kraus form
\theta(x)=\sum_{i}b_{i}^{*}xb_{i}
, with the property that
b_{i}S_{1}b_{j}^{*}\subseteq S_{2}
for
each
i,j
. This is equivalent to
\overrightarrow{S_{1}}\subseteq S_{2}
, which agrees with Weaver’s notion
of a
CP morphism
,
[
50
, Definition 7.5]
, once we recall that we always work with UCP maps,
not TPCP maps. In the case
B=\mathbb{M}_{n}
, links between these ideas and TRO equivalence are
explored in
[
24
, Section 7]
.

We should note that here Stahkle works with trace-free quantum graphs, see the remarks after
Proposition
6.12
. If we look instead at the quantum graphs
S_{i}^{\perp}
, then
the condition becomes
b_{i}^{*}S_{2}^{\perp}b_{j}\subseteq S_{1}^{\perp}
for each
i,j
. That is,
\overleftarrow{S_{2}^{\perp}}\subseteq S_{1}^{\perp}
. However, it seems to the author that these notions make sense in general, regardless of
whether we work with unital, trace-free, or more general quantum relations.

Remark 7.15
.

We follow
[
41
]
and motivate this definition from the classical situation.
Consider a classical graph
G
with associated
S_{G}=\operatorname{lin}\{e_{u,v}:(u,v)\in E(G)\}
over
C(V(G))
.
Similarly for a graph
H
. Let
f:G\rightarrow H
be a homomorphism, so
f
is a map
V(G)\rightarrow V(H)

with
(u,v)\in E(G)\implies(f(u),f(v))\in E(H)
.

For the moment, let
f:V(G)\rightarrow V(H)
be any map. We obtain a unital
*
-homomorphism
\theta:C(V(H))\rightarrow C(V(G))
given by
\theta(a)(u)=a(f(u))
for
a\in C(V(H)),u\in V(G)
. Then
\theta

is in particular a UCP map, and we can construct a Stinespring dilation as follows.

Let
n=|V(G)|
and
define
V:\ell^{2}(V(G))\rightarrow\ell^{2}(V(H))\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
as follows. Define an equivalence relation
on
V(G)
by setting
u\sim v
if
f(u)=f(v)
. For
u\in V(G)
fix an enumeration of the equivalence class

[u]=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{k}\}
, where
k\leq n
, and define
V(\delta_{u})=\delta_{f(u)}\otimes\delta_{i}
where

v_{i}=u
. This construction ensures that
V
is an isometry, and that

V^{*}(a\otimes 1)V\delta_{u}=a(f(u))\delta_{u}\qquad(u\in V(G),a\in C(V(H))).

The Kraus form is obtained by setting
V(\delta_{u})=\sum_{i}b_{i}(\delta_{u})\otimes\delta_{i}
, so that

b_{i}(\delta_{u})=\delta_{f(u)}
if
u
is the
i
th vertex which maps to
f(u)
, and
0
otherwise.
For
(u,v)\in E(G)
, so that
e_{u,v}\in S_{G}
, we see that

b_{i}e_{u,v}b_{j}^{*}=\theta_{b_{j}(\delta_{v}),b_{i}(\delta_{u})}=e_{f(u),f(v)},

or
0
, depending on
i,j
(and that there is some choice of
i,j
which gives
e_{f(u),f(v)}
).
Hence

\overrightarrow{S_{G}}=\operatorname{lin}\{e_{f(u),f(v)}:(u,v)\in E(G)\}

and so
\overrightarrow{S_{G}}\subseteq S_{H}
if and only if
f
is a homomorphism.

Thus
V^{*}(a\otimes 1)V=\theta(a)
for each
a
, and as the dilation has the correct form, it follows that

\overleftarrow{S_{H}}=V^{*}(S_{H}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n}))V.

A calculation shows that then
\overleftarrow{S_{H}}
is the linear span of “blocks” of the form

\sum\{e_{u,v}:f(u)=x,f(v)=y\}
as
(x,y)\in E(H)
vary. This indeed corresponds to a “pullback” of
H
along

f
to a graph on
G
, where we expand each edge in
H
to a clique in
G
.
∎

Stahkle shows further in
[
41
]
that given any CP morphism
C(V(H))\rightarrow C(V(G))
, there is
some graph theoretic homomorphism
G\rightarrow H
. However, this construction is not a bijection, but
rather an existence result (which motivates Weaver to use the new terminology of “CP morphism”).
Nevertheless, for many graph theory questions, we are merely interested in the
existence
of any
homomorphism between two graphs, and for this, the notion of a CP morphism exactly extends this to
quantum graphs (which is indeed Stahkle’s motivation). This notion of homomorphism is recast into the
theory of
non-local games
in
[
45
, Section 10.2]
, see also
[
11
, Section 5]
.

[
22
]
(see Section II) and
[
41
]
(see after Proposition 9) give tentative definitions for what
subgraphs or induced subgraphs should be. Gromada in
[
27
, Section 2.2]
looks at two notions of a
“quantum subgraph”, which are defined using the formalism of projections
e\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
. Firstly,

e_{1}
is a
subgraph made by removing edges
of
e_{2}
if
e_{1}\leq e_{2}
, which is clearly equivalent
to containment of the related operator bimodules. When we have
q:B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}
a surjective

*
-homomorphism, then
e_{q}
the
induced subgraph
of

along

is given by

.
It seems tricky to give a simple description of the associated operator bimodules.
Finally,
[
27
, Section 2.4]
looks at more general notions of “quotient”, which requires the
consideration of a more general object, a
weighted quantum graph
.

8
Automorphisms of Quantum graphs

As a warm-up to looking at quantum automorphisms, we look at (classical) automorphisms of quantum graphs.
Let us quickly discuss some well-known results about automorphisms of
B
.
By
\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
we mean the automorphisms of
B
, the bijective
*
-homomorphisms
B\rightarrow B
, which
leave
\psi
invariant. As
B
is finite-dimensional, it is easy to understand automorphisms of
B
. We give
a quick proof of the following well-known result, for completeness.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
B=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{M}_{n_{k}}
, and let
\theta:B\rightarrow B
be an automorphism.
Then
\theta
restricts to a bijection from each block
\mathbb{M}_{n_{k}}
to a block of the same dimension,
and each such
*
-isomorphism
\mathbb{M}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{m}
is of the form
x\mapsto uxu^{*}
for
some unitary
u\in\mathbb{M}_{m}
.

Proof.

It is well known that a
*
-isomorphism
\mathbb{M}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{m}
is of the form stated, so
we just shown the first part of the claim. For projections
e,f
we have by definition that
e\leq f
if and
only if
ef=e
, and so an automorphism preserves this partial ordering. Clearly also
\theta
maps the centre
of
B
to itself. Thus
\theta
gives a bijection when restricted to the set of minimal central projections.
The minimal central projections of
B
are exactly the units of the matrix blocks.
Fix some matrix block
\mathbb{M}_{n_{k}}
and let the unit be
e
. Let
(e_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n_{k}}
be the matrix
units of this block, so
ee_{ij}=e_{ij}e=e_{ij}
for each
i,j
. If
\theta(e)
is the unit of the matrix
block
\mathbb{M}_{n_{l}}
, then we see that
\theta(e_{ij})\leq\theta(e)
for each
i,j
, and so each

\theta(e_{ij})
is in this block, as required.
∎

As
\psi
is given by the positive invertible
Q\in B
, we can work out which automorphisms are in

\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\theta
be an automorphism of
B
. Then
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
if and only if
\theta(Q)=Q
,
and in this case,
\theta
and
(\sigma_{t})
commute.

Proof.

We first show that
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
commutes with
(\sigma_{t})
. We use the uniqueness property
of
(\sigma_{t})
, see for example
[
43
, Theorem 1.2, Chapter VIII]
, which says that
\psi\circ\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{t}
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and for every
a,b\in B
there is a continuous function
F=F_{a,b}

on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq{\operatorname{Im}}(z)\leq 1\}
which is holomorphic on the interior, and with

F(t)=\psi(\sigma_{t}(a)b)
and
F(t+i)=\psi(b\sigma_{t}(a))
. These properties determine
(\sigma_{t})

uniquely.

As
\psi\circ\theta=\psi
, also
\psi\circ\theta^{-1}=\psi
, so defining
\sigma^{\theta}_{t}=\theta\circ\sigma_{t}\circ\theta^{-1}
, we have that
\psi\circ\sigma^{\theta}_{t}=\psi
. Also, for
a,b\in B
,
let
F^{\prime}=F_{\theta^{-1}(a),\theta^{-1}(b)}
, and it is easily verified that
F^{\prime}
satisfies the condition
for
(\sigma^{\theta}_{t})
. By uniqueness,
\sigma_{t}=\sigma^{\theta}_{t}
for all
t
, equivalently,
\theta

and
(\sigma_{t})
commute.

By analytic continuation, also
\theta
commutes with
\sigma_{-i}
, and so
\theta(QaQ^{-1})=\theta(\sigma_{-i}(a))=\sigma_{-i}(\theta(a))=Q\theta(a)Q^{-1}
for each
a\in B
. Thus
\theta(Q)\theta(a)\theta(Q)^{-1}=Q\theta(a)Q^{-1}
, equivalently,
Q^{-1}\theta(Q)\theta(a)=\theta(a)Q^{-1}\theta(Q)
for each
a\in B
. So
Q^{-1}\theta(Q)
is central in
B
, and so on each matrix block of
B
is
a scalar multiple of the identity. Thus, with
Q=(Q_{k})
, there are scalars
q_{k}
with
\theta(Q_{k})=q_{k}Q_{k}
.
As
\psi(a)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa)
and
\psi(\theta(a))=\psi(a)
, we see that
{\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Q\theta(a))
for
a\in B
.
Taking
a=Q_{k}^{z}
(in the
k
th block, and
0
in other blocks) yields
{\operatorname{Tr}}(Q_{k}^{z+1})=q_{k}^{z}{\operatorname{Tr}}(Q_{k}^{z+1})

as
\theta(Q_{k}^{z})=q_{k}^{z}Q_{k}^{z}
. Setting
z=-1
shows that
q_{k}=1
. Thus
\theta(Q)=Q
.

Conversely, if
\theta(Q)=Q
, then notice that it follows from Proposition
8.1
that

{\operatorname{Tr}}\circ\theta={\operatorname{Tr}}
, and so
\psi(\theta(a))={\operatorname{Tr}}(Q\theta(a))={\operatorname{Tr}}(\theta(Qa))={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qa)=\psi(a)
, so

\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
as claimed.
∎

Definition 8.3
.

Given
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
we define
\hat{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
by

\hat{\theta}\Lambda(b)=\Lambda(\theta(b))\qquad(b\in B).

Notice that
(\Lambda(\theta(b))|\Lambda(\theta(c)))=\psi(\theta(b)^{*}\theta(c))=\psi(\theta(b^{*}c))=\psi(b^{*}c)=(\Lambda(b)|\Lambda(c))
and so
\hat{\theta}
is an isometry. As
\theta
preserves
\psi

also
\theta^{-1}
does, and so

(\Lambda(b)|\hat{\theta}^{*}\Lambda(c))=(\Lambda(\theta(b))|\Lambda(c))=\psi(\theta(b)^{*}c)=\psi(\theta^{-1}(\theta(b^{*})c))=\psi(b^{*}\theta^{-1}(c))=(\Lambda(b)|\widehat{\theta^{-1}}\Lambda(c)).

Thus
\hat{\theta}^{*}=\widehat{\theta^{-1}}
, and it is now easy to see that
\theta\mapsto\hat{\theta}
is
a unitary representation of
\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
on
L^{2}(B)
.

Shortly
A
will denote a quantum group, and so in this section we shall always write
A_{G}
for a quantum
adjacency matrix.
In the following, we are deliberately a little vague as to which exact axioms we need
A_{G}
to satisfy.

Definition 8.4
.

Let
A_{G}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be a quantum adjacency matrix. An
automorphism of
A_{G}
is
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)

such that
A_{G}
and
\hat{\theta}
commute.

Remark 8.5
.

Let
B=C(V)
and let
A=A_{G}
be the adjacency matrix of a classical (perhaps directed) graph, so
A_{ij}=1

exactly when there is an edge from
j
to
i
. Let us denote that there is an edge from
j
to
i
by

j\rightarrow i
. Any automorphism
\theta
is given by some
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(V)
,
and notice that the uniform measure on
V
is automatically preserved. Then
L^{2}(B)\cong\mathbb{C}^{V}

and
\hat{\theta}
is also induced by
\sigma
, in that
\hat{\theta}(\delta_{i})=\delta_{\sigma(i)}
. As

A(\delta_{i})=\sum_{j}A_{ji}\delta_{j}=\sum_{i\rightarrow j}\delta_{j},

we see that

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}A(\delta_{i})=\sum_{\{j:i\rightarrow j\}}\hat{\theta}(\delta_{j})=\sum_{\{j:i\rightarrow j\}}\delta_{\sigma(j)}=\sum_{\{k:i\rightarrow\sigma^{-1}(k)\}}\delta_{k},\qquad A\hat{\theta}(\delta_{i})=A(\delta_{\sigma(i)})=\sum_{\{j:\sigma(i)\rightarrow j\}}\delta_{j}.

Thus
\hat{\theta}A=A\hat{\theta}
if and only if, for each
i
, the sets
\{j:i\rightarrow\sigma^{-1}(j)\}

and
\{j:\sigma(i)\rightarrow j\}
agree. This is equivalent to the statement that
i\rightarrow k
if and
only if
\sigma(i)\rightarrow\sigma(k)
, which we observe by setting
k=\sigma^{-1}(j)
. That is,
\sigma
is
an automorphism of the (directed) graph.
∎

As a final remark, we notice that it is not really necessary to work with
L^{2}(B)
here. Instead, we could
interpret the quantum adjacency matrix
A_{G}
as a linear map on
B
(compare Section
5.4
), and then
require that
A_{G}
and
\theta
commute; this is
[
27
, Definition 2.4]
for example.

So far, we have been discussing well-known definitions. To our knowledge, however, there is little or no discussion
in the literature around what an automorphism of an operator bimodule might be. We shall follow
Theorem
5.17
. Let
A_{G}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be a quantum adjacency matrix, satisfying
axioms (
1
) and (
2
). Then
A_{G}
corresponds to a
projection
e\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
with
e=\sigma(e)
and with
e
invariant under
(\sigma_{t}\otimes\sigma_{t})
.
Notice that when
\theta
is an automorphism of
B
, also
\theta
is an automorphism of
B^{\operatorname{op}}
.

Proposition 8.6
.

We have that
A_{G}\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}A_{G}
if and only if
(\theta\otimes\theta)(e)=e
.

Proof.

We can check that
e^{\prime}=(\theta\otimes\theta)(e)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
5.17

and so corresponds to
A_{G}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
which satisfies axioms (
1
)
and (
2
) of Definition
2.4
. We will show that
A_{G}^{\prime}=\hat{\theta}A_{G}\hat{\theta}^{*}
, and so

A_{G}\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}A_{G}
if and only if
A_{G}^{\prime}=A_{G}
if and only if
e=e^{\prime}
, as claimed.

Using
\Psi_{0,1/2}:\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))\rightarrow B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
we see that

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}\hat{\theta}^{*}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\hat{\theta}\Lambda(a),\hat{\theta}\Lambda(b)}=\theta_{\Lambda(\theta(a)),\Lambda(\theta(b))}

\displaystyle\mapsto\theta(a)^{*}\otimes\sigma_{i/2}(\theta(b))=(\theta\otimes\theta)(a^{*}\otimes\sigma_{i/2}(b))=(\theta\otimes\theta)\Psi_{0,1/2}(\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}),

and so
\hat{\theta}A_{G}\hat{\theta}^{*}
maps to
e^{\prime}
, showing that
A_{G}^{\prime}=\hat{\theta}A_{G}\hat{\theta}^{*}
,
as claimed.
∎

Consider now
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
for some Hilbert space
H
. Let
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
be an automorphism of

\mathcal{B}(H)
, and suppose that
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
restricts to
B
giving some
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
. Notice that
for
a\in B,x\in B^{\prime}
we have that
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\theta(a)=\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(xa)=\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(ax)=\theta(a)\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
. As
\theta
is an automorphism, it follows that
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)\in B^{\prime}
,
so
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
restricts to an endomorphism of
B^{\prime}
. The same argument applies to
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}
,
and so
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
restricts to an automorphism of
B^{\prime}
. As all automorphisms of
\mathcal{B}(H)
are inner, there
is some unitary
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{B}(H)
with
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=u_{\theta}xu_{\theta}^{*}
for
x\in\mathcal{B}(H)
.
Thus
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
preserves
{\operatorname{Tr}}
on
\mathcal{B}(H)
. Using the GNS map
\mathcal{B}(H)\rightarrow H\otimes\overline{H}
,
the automorphism
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
corresponds to the unitary
u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}}
on

H\otimes\overline{H}
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
be a
B^{\prime}
-bimodule satisfying the conditions of Theorem
5.17
.
Then
S_{\theta}=\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(S)
is also a
B^{\prime}
-bimodule satisfying the conditions of
Theorem
5.17
. The associated projection in
B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
is

e_{\theta}=(\theta\otimes\theta)(e)
.

Proof.

As
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
restricts to an automorphism of
B^{\prime}
, it follows that
B_{\theta}
is a self-adjoint
B^{\prime}
-bimodule.
From Lemma
8.2
, we know that
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(Q)=Q
, and so
QSQ^{-1}=S
implies that

QS_{\theta}Q^{-1}=S_{\theta}
. Let
S
corresponds to
V\subseteq H\otimes\overline{H}
, and similarly

S_{\theta}
correspond to
V_{\theta}
. As noted above,
V_{\theta}=(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})(V)
.
Let
e\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
be the projection onto
V
, so that

V_{\theta}=(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})e(H\otimes\overline{H})=(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})e(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})^{*}(H\otimes\overline{H}),

and so
e_{\theta}=(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})e(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})^{*}

is the orthogonal projection onto
V_{\theta}
. However, then
e_{\theta}=(u_{\theta}\otimes\overline{u_{\theta}})e(u_{\theta}^{*}\otimes u_{\theta}^{\top})=(\theta\otimes\theta)(e)
, as claimed. (Here we use that, acting on

\overline{H}
, we have that
\theta(b)^{\top}=(u_{\theta}bu_{\theta}^{*})^{\top}=\overline{u_{\theta}}b^{\top}u_{\theta}^{\top}
.)
∎

Thus we might define an automorphism of
S
to be such a
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
with
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(S)=S
,
equivalently, with
(\theta\otimes\theta)(e)=e
. However, there are a number of problems here. Firstly,
different
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
might give the same
\theta
, and hence the same automorphism of
S
. Secondly,
we would wish there to be a bijection between automorphisms of a quantum adjacency matrix
A_{G}
and automorphisms
of the associated
S
, but it is not clear that every suitable
\theta
arises from some
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
.
Finally, when studying operator bimodules
S
, there is no real dependence on
H
, but it does not appear to
be the case that any
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}
on
\mathcal{B}(H)
, say restricting to
\theta
on
B
, with give rise to some
automorphism of
\mathcal{B}(K)
whenever
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K)
.

With a special choice of
H
, we can say something. Set
H=L^{2}(B)
and given
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
form

\hat{\theta}
as in Definition
8.3
. We now define
\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=\hat{\theta}x\hat{\theta}^{*}

for
x\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
, an automorphism which restricts to
\theta
on
B
.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
A_{G}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be a quantum adjacency matrix, satisfying axioms (
1
) and
(
2
) of Definition
2.4
. Then
\theta\in\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
is an automorphism of
A_{G}
if and only if

\theta_{\mathcal{B}}(S)=S
, where
S
is the
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodule associated with
A_{G}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
8.6
and Lemma
8.7
by
thinking about the projection associated to
A_{G}
and
S
.
∎

9
Quantum automorphisms of Quantum graphs

We now look at how (compact) quantum groups act on (quantum) graphs. For compact quantum groups acting
on classical graphs, this goes back to work of Banica in
[
2
]
. In the subsequent years, there has been
much activity, with the quantum automorphisms of some small graphs computed, and much interest in the question
of which graphs have a non-commutative (that is, not arising from a classical group) quantum automorphism group. In this direction, let us just mention
one recent paper
[
37
]
which constructs a finite graph which has a non-commutative, yet finite-dimensional,
quantum automorphism group. More recently quantum automorphisms of quantum graphs have been defined and
studied,
[
34
, Section V, Part C]
and more completely,
[
8
, Section 3]
, compare

[
27
, Section 4]
,
[
33
, Section 2.3]
.

We find some of the literature in this area to be quite hard to follow, and so we shall both motivate the
definitions from the classical situation, and develop certain aspects of compact quantum group theory
to allow us to make broader definitions than available elsewhere.
We will assume the reader is familiar with the basics of compact quantum group theory, for example,

[
35
,
44
]
.

We start again from the classical situation, which while well-known, we feel provides interesting motivation.
Let
G=\operatorname{Aut}(B,\psi)
and let
A=C(G)
the group algebra, with coproduct

\Delta:C(G)\rightarrow C(G)\otimes C(G)=C(G\times G);\quad\Delta(f)(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=f(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})\qquad(f\in C(G),\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in G).

The action of
G
on
B
gives a coaction of
C(G)
on
B
by

\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes C(G)\cong C(G,B);\quad\alpha(b)=\big(\theta(b)\big)_{\theta\in G}.

Let us check that the coaction equation holds. For
b\in B
we identify
(\alpha\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)\in B\otimes C(G)\otimes C(G)
as a member of
C(G\times G,B)
. Indeed, for arbitrary
f\in C(G,B)\cong B\otimes C(G)
we have that
(\alpha\otimes\operatorname{id})f
evaluated at
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is

\alpha(f(\theta_{2}))(\theta_{1})=\theta_{1}(f(\theta_{2}))
, while
(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)f
at
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

is
f(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})
. Thus

\displaystyle(\alpha\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\theta_{1}\big(\alpha(b)(\theta_{2})\big)=\theta_{1}(\theta_{2}(b))=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)\alpha(b)(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),

showing that indeed
(\alpha\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)\alpha
. Notice that with these conventions,
we are considering
right
coactions.

One can also verify the Podlés density condition, that
\operatorname{lin}\{(1\otimes a)\alpha(b):b\in B,a\in C(G)\}

is dense in
B\otimes C(G)
. Even in this setting, verifying this condition does not appear to be totally trivial
to do by direct calculation, and we point the reader to
[
39
, Section 2]
for an interesting discussion.

That
G
is a group of automorphisms which preserve
\psi
means that
(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)=\psi(b)1

for each
b\in B
. If we regard a quantum adjacency matrix
A_{G}
as acting on
B
, then that
G
is a group
of automorphisms of
A_{G}
is equivalent to
\theta(A_{G}(b))=A_{G}(\theta(b))
for each
b\in B
, that is,

(A_{G}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha=\alpha A_{G}
. If we, as is usual, regard
A_{G}
as acting on
L^{2}(B)
, then we have to
work harder to interpret this at the coaction level, see below.

We now consider an arbitrary, possibly non-commutative, compact quantum group
(A,\Delta)
.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
(A,\Delta)
be a compact quantum group. A
coaction
of
(A,\Delta)
on
B
is a unital

*
-homomorphism
\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A
with:

1.

(\alpha\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\Delta)\alpha
;

2.

\operatorname{lin}\{(1\otimes a)\alpha(b):a\in A,b\in B\}
is dense in
B\otimes A
.

A coaction
\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A
of
(A,\Delta)
on
B

preserves the state
\psi
when

(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)=\psi(b)1
for each
b\in B
.

We often think of (compact) quantum groups as being abstract objects represented by related algebraic objects (for example, a
C^{*}
-algebraic compact quantum group, or a Hopf
*
-algebraic compact quantum group). In the quantum group literature, one then speaks of the (abstract object) quantum group
acting
on some object if, at the level of the algebra in question, there exists a coaction. We shall mostly stick to considering concrete algebraic objects
(A,\Delta)
and hence will mostly speak about coactions of
(A,\Delta)
.

The condition of preserving
\psi
was first considered by Wang in
[
48
]
(in fact, only the case

\psi={\operatorname{Tr}}
is worked out in
[
48
, Section 6]
, but as remarked there, the same ideas work in general).
We write
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
for the maximal compact quantum group faithfully coacting on
B
and preserving
\psi
. This
means that if
(A,\Delta)
is any compact quantum group coacting on
B
and preserving
\psi
, there there is
a unique
*
-homomorphism
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)\rightarrow A
intertwining the coproducts and the coactions. That
such a maximal quantum group exists is shown in
[
48
, Theorem 6.1]
, where it is also show that

\operatorname{QAut}(B)
does not exist: there is no maximal compact quantum group coacting on
B
, unless
B
is commutative. Here we stated the maximality condition as a universal property, but faithfulness can also be described directly, see
[
48
, Definition 2.4]
.

Let us think more about coactions. The second condition is often called the
Podleś density

condition after Podleś’s early work in this area. Let
\mathcal{O}_{A}
be the dense Hopf
*
-algebra of
A

formed from the coefficients of irreducible unitary corepresentations of
A
. There is a
*
-algebra

\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}\subseteq B
(the
Podleś subalgebra
or
algebraic core
of
B
)
which is dense, and such that
\alpha
restricts to a
*
-homomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{A}
. In fact, this restriction is a Hopf
*
-algebraic coaction; in particular, it
is injective. A modern reference for these results is
[
18
]
.

For us,
B
is finite-dimensional, so
\mathcal{O}_{B}=B
, and hence
\alpha
actually maps into
B\otimes\mathcal{O}_{A}
.
A modern approach to constructing
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
makes use of the following observation.
Let
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an algebraic basis of
B
such that
(\Lambda(e_{i}))
is an orthonormal basis
of
L^{2}(B)
. For any linear map
\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A
there is a matrix
v=v_{ij}\in M_{n}(A)
with

\alpha(e_{i})=\sum_{j}e_{j}\otimes v_{ji}
for each
i
. When
\alpha
is a coaction, our observations show
that already
v_{ij}\in\mathcal{O}_{A}
for each
i,j
.

We follow here
ideas of Banica, see
[
3
, Section 4]
, or
[
4
, Section 1]
for the case when
\psi
is a trace.
Given unitary matrices
v\in M_{n}(A)\cong M_{n}\otimes A
and
w\in M_{m}(A)\cong M_{m}\otimes A
we define

v\boxtimes w=v_{13}w_{23}\in M_{n}\otimes M_{m}\otimes A\cong M_{nm}(A)
, here using the common
leg-numbering notation. Define
\hom(v,w)
to be the space of linear maps
t:M_{n}\rightarrow M_{m}
with

(t\otimes 1)v=w(t\otimes 1)
, an equation we can interpret simply using matrix multiplication. These
definitions should of course be familiar to the reader who knows about corepresentations of compact quantum
groups.

With respect to the basis
(\Lambda(e_{i}))
, write the
multiplication map
m:B\otimes B\rightarrow B
as an
n\times n^{2}
matrix, and write the unit map

\eta:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow B
as a
n\times 1
matrix. Also write the projection
\eta\circ\eta^{*}

as a
n\times n
matrix. The following proposition makes links between properties of a linear map

\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A
, and properties of
v
.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
A
be a compact quantum group. Under this bijection between
\alpha
and
v
, the following hold:

1.

\alpha
is multiplicative if and only if
m\in\hom(v\boxtimes v,v)
;

2.

\alpha
is a unital map if and only if
\eta\in\hom(1,v)
;

3.

assuming
\alpha
is unital,
\alpha
is
\psi
-preserving if and only if
\eta\circ\eta^{*}\in\hom(v,v)
;

4.

\alpha
satisfies the coaction equation if and only if
v
is a corepresentation.

Assume now that
\alpha
is multiplicative and unital. If
v
is unitary, then
\alpha
is a
*
-map.
If
\alpha
is a
*
-map, satisfies the coaction equation, and is
\psi
-preserving, then
v
is unitary.

Proof.

Most of this follows by simple direct calculation, and is rather similar to
[
4
, Lemma 1.2]

or
[
27
, Lemma 4.5]
(which both just considers the case when
\psi
is a trace).
However, we prove the final claim, to check that it works
for a non-trace, and to indicate that this step does not follow from direct calculation, but rather from
quite a lot of theory.

Suppose that
\alpha
is a unital
*
-homomorphism and is
\psi
-preserving. Then consider, for
i,j
,

\displaystyle(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(e_{i}^{*}e_{j})

\displaystyle=(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})(\alpha(e_{i})^{*}\alpha(e_{j}))=\sum_{s,t}\psi(e_{s}^{*}e_{t})v_{si}^{*}v_{tj}=\sum_{s,t}(\Lambda(e_{s})|\Lambda(e_{t}))v_{si}^{*}v_{tj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}v_{ki}^{*}v_{kj}=(v^{*}v)_{ij}.

As
\alpha
preserves
\psi
, this is equal to
\psi(e_{i}^{*}e_{j})1=\delta_{i,j}1
and so
v^{*}v=1
.

As we know that actually
v
is a matrix in
M_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{A})
, Hopf
*
-algebra theory would show that
v

is invertible, as it has a left inverse. An alternative way to show this is to check that
v
is

non-degenerate
and then to appeal to
[
51
, Proposition 3.2]
.
(We note here that we use “non-degenerate” in the sense of
[
51
, page 630]
, which is weaker than
the usual Hopf
*
-algebra definition.) Either way, we conclude that
v
is invertible,
so necessarily also
vv^{*}=1
, so
v
is unitary.
∎

Remark 9.3
.

The condition that
\eta\circ\eta^{*}\in\hom(v,v)
is often omitted, which is perhaps surprising.
In fact, it is automatic in the presence of the other properties.
Indeed,
\eta\in\hom(1,v)
means that
\eta\otimes\operatorname{id}=v(\eta\otimes\operatorname{id})
so also
\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}=(\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id})v^{*}
and hence

v(\eta\circ\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id})v^{*}=v(\eta\otimes\operatorname{id})(\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id})v^{*}=(\eta\otimes\operatorname{id})(\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id})=(\eta\circ\eta^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}).

If
v
is assumed unitary, this is implies that
\eta\circ\eta^{*}\in\hom(v,v)
.
∎

It is now easy to see how to define the
quantum automorphism group
of
B
which preserves
\psi
,
written as
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
. We define
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
to be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the
coefficients of an
n\times n
unitary matrix
v=(v_{ij})
with the conditions

m\in\hom(v\boxtimes v,v),\qquad\eta\in\hom(1,v).

The coproduct on
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
is defined
to satisfy
\Delta(v_{ij})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{ik}\otimes v_{kj}
, and exists by universality.
We define
\alpha
by
\alpha(e_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\otimes v_{ji}
, and the proposition shows that

\alpha
is a coaction.

As
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
is generated by elements of the matrix
v
, which is by definition
a (unitary) corepresentation, in fact
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
is a compact matrix quantum group. One point in this
definition which is far from obvious to the author is why the axioms of a compact quantum group hold, equivalently,
why the transpose of
v
is an invertible matrix (an alternative axiom in the case of a compact matrix quantum group).
We find it helpful to look at
[
5
, Section 3]
which explains links with the Tannaka–Krein reconstruction theorem.
Also not considered in this construction is the Podleś density condition for
\alpha
,
but this follows easily once we know that
v^{t}
is invertible. In fact, as we shall need the following later,
we shall prove by a more direct argument that
v^{t}
is invertible.

Lemma 9.4
.

Define
X\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
by
X_{ij}=(\Lambda(e_{i})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))=\psi(e_{i}^{*}e_{j}^{*})
.
Then
X^{-1}=\overline{X}
, and
v=(X\otimes 1)\overline{v}(\overline{X}\otimes 1)
where
\overline{v}

is the matrix
(v_{ij}^{*})
(the transpose of the adjoint). Furthermore,
\overline{v}
is invertible
with inverse
(\overline{X}\otimes 1)v^{*}(X\otimes 1)
, and so also
v^{t}
is invertible.

Proof.

Notice that
X
is nothing but the matrix representing the antilinear map
\Lambda(x)\mapsto\Lambda(x^{*})
, an observation which helps to explain the formulae.
As
\alpha
is a
*
-map, and expanding using the orthonormal basis
(\Lambda(e_{j}))
, we see that

\displaystyle\alpha(e_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\Lambda(e_{j})|\Lambda(e_{i}^{*}))\alpha(e_{j})=\sum_{j,l}e_{l}\otimes(\Lambda(e_{j})|\Lambda(e_{i}^{*}))v_{lj}

\displaystyle=\alpha(e_{i})^{*}=\sum_{j}e_{j}^{*}\otimes v_{ji}^{*}=\sum_{j,l}e_{l}\otimes(\Lambda(e_{l})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))v_{ji}^{*},

and so
\sum_{j}(\Lambda(e_{j})|\Lambda(e_{i}^{*}))v_{lj}=\sum_{j}(\Lambda(e_{l})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))v_{ji}^{*}

for each
l
. Thus
\sum_{j}v_{lj}X_{ji}=\sum_{j}X_{lj}v_{ji}^{*}
or
v(X\otimes 1)=(X\otimes 1)\overline{v}
.
As
\psi(b)={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qb)
for each
b\in B
,

(\Lambda(a^{*})|\Lambda(b))={\operatorname{Tr}}(Qab)={\operatorname{Tr}}(bQa)={\operatorname{Tr}}(QQ^{-1}bQa)=(\Lambda(Qb^{*}Q^{-1})|\Lambda(a))\qquad(a,b\in B).

Hence, for each
i,j
,

\displaystyle(\overline{X}X)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\overline{X_{ik}}X_{kj}=\sum_{k}\overline{(\Lambda(e_{i})|\Lambda(e_{k}^{*}))}(\Lambda(e_{k})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))=\sum_{k}(\Lambda(Qe_{i}^{*}Q^{-1})|\Lambda(e_{k}))(\Lambda(e_{k})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))

\displaystyle=(\Lambda(Qe_{i}^{*}Q^{-1})|\Lambda(e_{j}^{*}))=(\Lambda(e_{j})|\Lambda(e_{i}))=\delta_{i,j}.

As
X
is a scalar matrix, it follows that
X^{-1}=\overline{X}
, and so
v=(X\otimes 1)\overline{v}(X^{-1}\otimes 1)=(X\otimes 1)\overline{v}(\overline{X}\otimes 1)
, as claimed.
As
v^{*}v=vv^{*}=1
we see that
u=(\overline{X}\otimes 1)v^{*}(X\otimes 1)
is the inverse of
\overline{v}
,
and hence
u^{*}=(X^{*}\otimes 1)v(X^{t}\otimes 1)
is the inverse of
v^{t}
.
∎

We shall make use of this construction, and our parenthetical comments, below.
We hope also that this quick sketch of the construction will aid the reader in understanding links between
our presentation and, for example,
[
8
, Definition 3.7, Proposition 3.8]
. See also the informative

[
27
, Section 4]
.

9.1
Unitary implementations

In this section, let
(A,\Delta)
be a compact quantum group with a coaction
\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A

which preserves
\psi
. Consider
L^{2}(B)
with GNS map
\Lambda:B\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
, and let
A
be faithfully
and non-degenerately represented on a Hilbert space
K
. Define an operator
U
on
L^{2}(B)\otimes K
by

U(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi)=\alpha(b)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)\qquad(b\in B,\xi\in K).

Then

\displaystyle\big(U(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi)\big|U(\Lambda(d)\otimes\eta)\big)

\displaystyle=(\psi\otimes\omega_{\xi,\eta})\alpha(b^{*}d)=\psi(b^{*}d)(\xi|\eta)=\big(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi\big|\Lambda(d)\otimes\eta\big),

using that
\alpha
is
\psi
-invariant. A similar calculation holds with arbitrary tensors, which shows that

U
is well-defined, and a co-isometry. The density condition
on
\alpha
(and that
A
acts non-degenerately on
K
) shows that
U
is unitary. Then

U(b\otimes 1)U^{*}U(\Lambda(d)\otimes\xi)=U(\Lambda(bd)\otimes\xi)=\alpha(bd)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\alpha(b)U(\Lambda(d)\otimes\xi),

and thus
U(b\otimes 1)U^{*}=\alpha(b)
, acting on
L^{2}(B)\otimes K
.

We remark that we could instead have worked in the language of Hilbert
C^{*}
-modules, which would remove the
need to consider
K
, at the cost of more technicalities. The following now shows how
\alpha
commuting with

A_{G}
makes sense at the level of
U
.

Lemma 9.5
.

Let
A_{0}:B\rightarrow B
be a linear map which induces the linear map
A_{G}:L^{2}(B)\rightarrow L^{2}(B)
,
that is,
A_{G}\circ\Lambda=\Lambda\circ A_{0}
.
Then
\alpha A_{0}=(A_{0}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha
if and only if
(A_{G}\otimes 1)U=U(A_{G}\otimes 1)
.

Proof.

Given
b\in B,a\in A,\xi\in K
, we see that
(b\otimes a)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\Lambda(b)\otimes a\xi
.
As
A
acts non-degenerately on
K
, it follows that
u\in B\otimes A
has
u=0
if and only if

u(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in K
. We hence see that

\alpha A_{0}=(A_{0}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha
if and only if

\displaystyle\alpha(A_{0}(b))=(A_{0}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)

\displaystyle(b\in B)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle\alpha(A_{0}(b))(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=(A_{0}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(b)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle(b\in B,\xi\in K)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle U(\Lambda(A_{0}(b))\otimes\xi)=(A_{G}\otimes 1)U(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle(b\in B,\xi\in K)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle U\big((A_{G}\otimes 1)(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi)\big)=(A_{G}\otimes 1)U(\Lambda(b)\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle(b\in B,\xi\in K)

using that
A_{G}\Lambda(d)=\Lambda(A_{0}(d))
for
d\in B
. This is equivalent to
U(A_{G}\otimes 1)=(A_{G}\otimes 1)U
, as claimed.
∎

We shall call
U
the
unitary implementation of
\alpha
, compare with the much more general
construction of
[
46
]
.
Recall from Proposition
9.2
that there is a bijection between coactions
\alpha

and certain (unitary) matrices
v\in M_{n}(A)
. The following clarifies the relation between
v
and the
unitary
U
.

Lemma 9.6
.

The unitary
U\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B)\otimes K)
is equal to the image of
v\in M_{n}(A)\cong M_{n}\otimes A
acting on
L^{2}(B)\otimes K
.

Proof.

We simply calculate that for
a\in A
,

U\big(\Lambda(e_{i})\otimes\xi\big)=\alpha(e_{i})(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\sum_{j}\Lambda(e_{j})\otimes v_{ji}\xi.

Thus the matrix of
U
equals
(v_{ij})
, acting on
L^{2}(B)\otimes K
, as claimed.
∎

In particular,
U
is a corepresentation (which also follows by direct calculation).

The following definition should now be clear, given our motivation above from the case of classical
groups, and Lemma
9.5
.

Definition 9.7
.

Let
(A,\Delta)
be a compact quantum group, and let
\alpha:B\rightarrow B\otimes A
be a coaction which
preserves
\psi
. Let
A_{G}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
be a quantum adjacency matrix associated to the linear map
A_{0}

on
B
. Then
\alpha
is a
coaction on
A_{G}
when
\alpha A_{0}=(A_{0}\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha
, equivalently,
when
A_{G}
and
v
commute, where
v
is the unitary corepresentation given by
\alpha
.

Remark 9.8
.

Let us consider the complete quantum graph, compare Proposition
6.1
, so that

A_{G}=\theta_{\Lambda(1),\Lambda(1)}
. Then
A_{G}=\eta\circ\eta^{*}
, and so by
Remark
9.3
, as
\alpha
is
\psi
-preserving, automatically
v
commutes with

A_{G}
.

Similarly, consider the empty quantum graph, compare Proposition
6.2
, so that

A_{G}=(mm^{*})^{-1}
. As
\alpha
is a coaction, by Proposition
9.2
,

(m\otimes 1)v_{13}v_{23}=v(m\otimes 1)
. Thus also
v_{23}^{*}v_{13}^{*}(m^{*}\otimes 1)=(m^{*}\otimes 1)v^{*}
, and so as
v
is unitary, also
(m^{*}\otimes 1)v=v_{13}v_{23}(m^{*}\otimes 1)
.
Hence

v(mm^{*}\otimes 1)=(m\otimes 1)v_{13}v_{23}(m^{*}\otimes 1)=(mm^{*}\otimes 1)v,

and so also
A_{G}
commutes with

.

Hence, in agreement with intuition, as soon as
\alpha
is a
\psi
-preserving coaction on
B
, it is
automatically a coaction on both the empty and complete quantum graphs.
∎

We next show that there exists a universal compact quantum group which acts on
A_{G}
, and which we can then be
justified in calling the
quantum automorphism group of
A_{G}
. We shall compare this definition to
others in the literature in Remark
9.10
.

Proposition 9.9
.

Consider
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
as constructed above from a generating unitary corepresentation
v
.
Let
A
be the quotient formed by imposing the extra conditions that
A_{G}v=vA_{G}
. Then
\Delta
drops
to
A
making
(A,\Delta)
into a quantum subgroup of
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
, which is the quantum automorphism
group of
A_{G}
: that is, universal amongst all compact quantum groups acting on
A_{G}
.

Proof.

Let
J
be the ideal generated by the relations coming from
A_{G}v=vA_{G}
, that is,
J
is the ideal
generated by the element

\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{G}_{ik}v_{kj}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{G}_{kj}v_{ik}\qquad(1\leq i,j\leq n).

If we apply
\Delta
to one of these elements, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}A^{G}_{ik}v_{kl}\otimes v_{lj}-\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}A^{G}_{kj}v_{il}\otimes v_{lk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{G}_{ik}v_{kl}-v_{ik}A^{G}_{kl}\Big)\otimes v_{lj}+\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{ik}A^{G}_{kl}\Big)\otimes v_{lj}-\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}A^{G}_{kj}v_{il}\otimes v_{lk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{G}_{ik}v_{kl}-v_{ik}A^{G}_{kl}\Big)\otimes v_{lj}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v_{ik}\otimes A^{G}_{kl}v_{lj}-v_{ik}\otimes A^{G}_{lj}v_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}A^{G}_{ik}v_{kl}-v_{ik}A^{G}_{kl}\Big)\otimes v_{lj}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v_{ik}\otimes\Big(A^{G}_{kl}v_{lj}-A^{G}_{lj}v_{kl}\Big)

\displaystyle\in J\otimes\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)+\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)\otimes J.

In the first line we add in and subtract the same element, but notice that the element in the brackets
is manifestly in
J
. In the second step we re-index, and the re-arrange in the third step so
both brackets are now clearly members of
J
.

This calculation and a simple continuity argument shows that
\Delta
gives a well-defined map

A\rightarrow A\otimes A
, where
A=\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)/J
. By definition,
A
coacts on
A_{G}
.

Now let
A_{1}
be some other compact quantum group which coacts on
A_{G}
. Then, by
Proposition
9.2
for any orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(B)
there is a unitary corepresentation

v\in M_{n}(A_{1})
which defines the coaction. If necessary replacing
A_{1}
by the
C^{*}
-subalgebra generated by
the elements of
v
, we may suppose that
A_{1}
is a compact matrix quantum group generated by
v
.

Consider now
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
with generating corepresentation
w
say. As
A_{1}
coacts in a
\psi
-preserving way,

v
satisfies all the relations which are satisfied by
w
, and so by universality, there is a
*
-homomorphism

\theta:\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)\rightarrow A_{1}
which intertwines the coproducts and which sends
w
to
v
. Hence
\theta

is a surjection. As
A_{G}v=vA_{G}
it follows that
\theta(J)=\{0\}
, and so
\theta
drops to a morphism from

A
, which is necessarily uniquely defined. This has hence demonstrated the required universal property for
A
.
∎

We shall write
\operatorname{QAut}(A_{G})
for the resulting compact quantum group.

Remark 9.10
.

Let us compare this definition/construction with
[
8
, Definition 3.7]
. There,
\mathcal{O}(A_{G})
is
defined to be the universal

From the discussion above where we sketch the construction of

∎

Remark 9.11
.

As alluded to a number of times, much of the existing work on quantum graphs restricts to the case when
\psi

is a
\delta
-form (tracial, or not). There is some motivation for this if we consider
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
, as
a result attributed to Brannan,
[
19
, Proposition 20]
, describes
\operatorname{QAut}(B,\psi)
in terms of a
free product of a decomposition of

into the coarsest direct sum
B=\bigoplus B_{i}
where
\psi
restricts
to a
\delta
-form on each
B_{i}
. Thus for many questions, it suffices to look at each factor
B_{i}

separately.

We might wonder if
\operatorname{QAut}(A_{G})
factors similarly. This does not appear to be the case, as there appears to
be no reason why
A_{G}
should restrict to each factor
B_{i}
. To find a counter-example,
let
B=\mathbb{M}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{M}_{2}
where we equip each
\mathbb{M}_{2}

factor with differently normalised traces, say
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
with
\psi=\psi_{1}\oplus\psi_{2}
.
Then
\psi
is not a
\delta
-form, but each
\psi_{i}
is.

Let
B
act on
H=\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
so that
B^{\prime}\cong\ell^{\infty}_{2}
, say the span of

e_{1},e_{2}
where
e_{i}
is the projection onto the
i
th
\mathbb{C}^{2}
component. As usual, we identify

\mathcal{B}(H)
with
\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2}))\cong\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{M}_{2})
. Let

S=\operatorname{lin}\{e_{1},e_{2},x_{0},x_{0}^{*}\},\quad\text{with}\quad x_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

for some non-zero
t:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then
S
is an operator system, and so as
B

has a tracial state,
S
corresponds to a quantum adjacency matrix
A_{G}
say, and to a projection in

B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
, say
e_{G}
.

Towards a contradiction, suppose that
A_{G}
commutes with the projections onto each factor of
B
. As

A_{G}
is self-adjoint, it follows that
A_{G}
can be written as a sum of rank-one operators of the form

\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}
where
a,b
both come from the same factor of
B
. It follows that

is the span of tensors
b\otimes a^{*}
where
a,b
both come from the same factor of
B
. Thus
the projection of
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))
onto

is a sum of maps of the form
\theta\mapsto b\theta a^{*}

where
a,b
both come from the same factor of
B
. However,

\begin{pmatrix}b&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}x_{0}\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&bt\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=0,

and similarly if

come from the 2nd factor. This is a contradiction, as the projection onto

should preserve

.
∎

Examples of quantum automorphism groups of quantum graphs are considered in
[
27
]
, and in
particular in
[
33
, Section 4]
, where all quantum graphs over

are considered.

Remark 9.12
.

A possible definition of

Lemma 9.14

With the definition above,
\alpha^{\operatorname{op}}_{U}

\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(B))

\alpha^{\operatorname{op}}_{U}(B^{\prime})\subseteq B^{\prime}\otimes A

It is standard that

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\Lambda(e_{i})\otimes\overline{\Lambda(e_{j})}\otimes U_{is}(x_{0}^{-1}U^{*}x_{0})_{tj}\xi.

Compare this to

\displaystyle U_{13}U_{23}^{\top\otimes R}(\Lambda(e_{s})\otimes\overline{\Lambda(e_{t})}\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,l}e_{ik}\Lambda(e_{s})\otimes e_{jl}\overline{\Lambda(e_{t})}\otimes U_{ik}R(U_{lj})\xi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\Lambda(e_{i})\otimes\overline{\Lambda(e_{j})}\otimes U_{is}R(U_{tj})\xi.

We hence need to show that
(x_{0}^{-1}U^{*}x_{0})_{tj}=R(U_{tj})
for each
t,j
.

To show this, we use some compact quantum group theory. Let
S
be the antipode on
\mathcal{O}_{A}
, which has
decomposition
S=R\tau_{-i/2}
where
(\tau_{t})
is the scaling group, and
\tau_{-i/2}
is the analytic
extension. Recall the definition of the
operator
\rho_{U}
from
[
35
, Section 1.4]
, and from before
[
35
, Definition 1.4.5]
recall that

\rho_{U}
is the unique (up to a positive scalar) operator with
((\rho_{U}^{t})^{1/2}\otimes 1)\overline{U}((\rho_{U}^{t})^{-1/2}\otimes 1)
is unitary. From Lemma
9.4
, we know that
U=X\overline{U}X^{-1}
,
and so
\rho_{U}^{1/2}
is
|X|^{t}
, up to a scalar. As
P=X^{*}X
, we see that
|X|^{t}=(P^{t})^{1/2}=x_{0}
.
From after
[
35
, Remark 1.7.7]
we have that

(\operatorname{id}\otimes\tau_{i/2})(U)=(\rho_{U}^{-1/2}\otimes 1)U(\rho_{U}^{1/2}\otimes 1)
which thus equals

x_{0}^{-1}Ux_{0}
. Hence

R(U_{tj})=S\tau_{i/2}(U_{tj})=S((x_{0}^{-1}Ux_{0})_{tj})=(x_{0}^{-1}U^{*}x_{0})_{tj},

as required.
∎

This previous result should be compared with
[
47
, Corollary 2.6.3]
(which itself follows quickly
from
[
46
, Proposition 4.2]
). We do not invoke this directly, as we will need to use the exact form
of the unitary
w
, and it seems about as much work to check that
w
really is the intertwining unitary
as it does to give the above lemma (which is also of independent interest).

Recall the functional
\psi^{\operatorname{op}}
on
B^{\operatorname{op}}
studied in Lemma
5.32
, where we identify

L^{2}(B^{\operatorname{op}})
with
\overline{L^{2}(B)}
. Thus
L^{2}(B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}})
is identified with
H\otimes\overline{H}
,
with GNS map
\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}}
.
As usual, we use Theorem
5.17
to link
A_{G}
to a projection

e_{G}=\Psi^{\prime}_{1/2,0}(A_{G})
, so explicitly, we assume
A_{G}
is self-adjoint satisfying axioms
(
1
) and (
2
) of Definition
2.4
.

Lemma 9.17
.

Let
A_{G}
be a quantum adjacency matrix associated to
projection
e_{G}\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
.
Then:

1.

w\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})((\sigma_{-i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G}))
;

2.

w\tilde{\Lambda}(aTb)=(a\otimes\sigma_{i/2}(b)^{\top})w\tilde{\Lambda}(T)
for
T\in\mathcal{B}(H),a,b\in B
;

Let
S_{0}
be the
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodule generated by
A_{G}
, and set
S_{0}^{\prime}=\kappa(S_{0})
.
Then:

3.

S_{0}^{\prime}
is the
B
-operator bimodule generated by
A_{G}
;

4.

the orthogonal projection
f
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(H),\tilde{\psi})
onto
\tilde{\Lambda}(S_{0}^{\prime})

satisfies
wfw^{*}=(J\otimes\overline{J})e_{G}(J\otimes\overline{J})
;

5.

fw^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})=\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})
.

Proof.

Let
T=\theta_{\Lambda(a),\Lambda(b)}
for
a,b\in B
, so

\displaystyle w\tilde{\Lambda}(T)=\Lambda(b)\otimes\overline{x_{0}\Lambda(a)}=\Lambda(b)\otimes\overline{\nabla^{1/2}\Lambda(a)}=\Lambda(b)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(\sigma_{-i/2}(a))}=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(b\otimes\sigma_{-i/2}(a)^{*}).

As
\Psi^{\prime}_{1/2,0}(T)=\sigma_{i/2}(b)\otimes a^{*}
, we see that

\displaystyle w\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})

\displaystyle=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})\big((\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})\Psi^{\prime}_{1/2,0}(A_{G})\big)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})\big((\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(e_{G})\big)=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})((\sigma_{-i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G})),

as
e_{G}=(\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{-i/2})(e_{G})
.

With
T=\theta_{\xi,\eta}
and
a,b\in B
we see that

w\tilde{\Lambda}(aTb)=w\tilde{\Lambda}(\theta_{b^{*}\xi,a\eta})=a\eta\otimes\overline{\nabla^{1/2}b^{*}\xi}=a\eta\otimes\overline{(\nabla^{-1/2}b\nabla^{1/2})^{*}\nabla^{1/2}\xi}=(a\otimes\sigma_{i/2}(b)^{\top})w\tilde{\Lambda}(T),

as claimed.

As
\kappa
restricts to a bijection
B\rightarrow B^{\prime}
, and
\kappa(A_{G})=JA_{G}^{*}J=A_{G}
because

A_{G}
is self-adjoint, and using Proposition
5.23
, it follows that

S_{0}^{\prime}
is the
B
-operator bimodule generated by
A_{G}
.
It now follows that
w\tilde{\Lambda}(S_{0}^{\prime})=(B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}})(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})((\sigma_{-i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G}))
.

Set
f_{0}=(\sigma_{-i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G})\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
, so that

\displaystyle(J\otimes\overline{J})(\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(f_{0}^{*})(J\otimes\overline{J})

\displaystyle=(J\otimes\overline{J})(\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G})(J\otimes\overline{J})

\displaystyle=(J\otimes\overline{J})(\sigma_{i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(e_{G})(J\otimes\overline{J})

\displaystyle=(J\otimes\overline{J})e_{G}(J\otimes\overline{J})\in B^{\prime}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\operatorname{op}}.

However, for
u\in B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}}
, with reference to Lemma
5.32
, we see that

(J\otimes\overline{J})(\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(f_{0}^{*})(J\otimes\overline{J})(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(u)=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(uf_{0})=u(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(f_{0}).

Thus letting
u
vary shows that the image of the projection
(J\otimes\overline{J})e_{G}(J\otimes\overline{J})

is equal to
(B\otimes B^{\operatorname{op}})(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(f_{0})=w\tilde{\Lambda}(S_{0}^{\prime})
.
Thus necessarily
wfw^{*}=(J\otimes\overline{J})e_{G}(J\otimes\overline{J})
.
Hence

\displaystyle wfw^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})

\displaystyle=(J\otimes\overline{J})e_{G}(J\otimes\overline{J})(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})

\displaystyle=(J\otimes\overline{J})(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})(e_{G})=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})((\sigma_{-i/2}\otimes\sigma_{i/2})(e_{G}))

\displaystyle=(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda^{\operatorname{op}})((\sigma_{-i}\otimes\operatorname{id})(e_{G}))=w\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G}).

Thus
fw^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})=\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})
.
∎

Again, in the following we assume that
A_{G}
is self-adjoint satisfying axioms
(
1
) and (
2
) of Definition
2.4
.

Theorem 9.18
.

Let
\alpha
be a coaction of a compact quantum group
(A,\Delta)
on
B
, which preserves
\psi
, and has unitary
implementation
U
. Let
A_{G}
be a quantum adjacency matrix, and let
S_{0}
be the
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodule
generated by
A_{G}
. The following are equivalent:

1.

\alpha
acts on
A_{G}
, that is,
UA_{G}=A_{G}U
;

2.

\alpha_{U}
leaves
S_{0}^{\prime}=\kappa(S_{0})
invariant, that is,
\alpha_{U}(S_{0}^{\prime})\subseteq S_{0}^{\prime}\otimes A
;

3.

\alpha_{U}^{\operatorname{op}}(S_{0})\subseteq S_{0}\otimes A
.

Proof.

We have already seen that (
2
) and (
3
) are
equivalent.

Suppose that
\alpha_{U}(S_{0}^{\prime})\subseteq S_{0}^{\prime}\otimes A
, and let
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K)
.
By definition of the unitary implementation
\tilde{U}
, given
s_{0}\in S_{0}^{\prime}

and
\xi\in K
, we see that

\tilde{U}(\tilde{\Lambda}(s_{0}^{\prime})\otimes\xi)=\alpha_{U}(s_{0}^{\prime})(\tilde{\Lambda}(1)\otimes\xi)\in(S_{0}^{\prime}\otimes A)(\tilde{\Lambda}(1)\otimes\xi)=\tilde{\Lambda}(S_{0}^{\prime})\otimes A\xi.

Let
f
be the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(H),\tilde{\psi})
onto
\tilde{\Lambda}(S_{0}^{\prime})
, so this calculation
shows that
\tilde{U}(f\otimes 1)=(f\otimes 1)\tilde{U}(f\otimes 1)
. A non-trivial result about compact quantum
groups (see the proof of
[
32
, Proposition 6.2]
for example) shows that then

\tilde{U}(f\otimes 1)=(f\otimes 1)\tilde{U}
.

From the previous lemma, it hence follows that for
\xi\in K
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{U}(A_{G})(\tilde{\Lambda}(1)\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}(\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})\otimes\xi)=\tilde{U}(fw^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=(f\otimes 1)\tilde{U}(w^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)})\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=(fw^{*}\otimes 1)U_{13}U^{\top\otimes R}_{23}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)}\otimes\xi),

(5)

in the final step using Lemma
9.16
.

For
\xi\in K
, we have that
U(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\alpha(1)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi
, from the definition of
U
. Then for
a\in B,\xi_{1}\in K
, also

(U^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)|\Lambda(a)\otimes\xi_{1})=(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi|\alpha(a)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi_{1}))=(\xi|(\psi\otimes\operatorname{id})\alpha(a)\xi_{1})=\psi(a)(\xi|\xi_{1}),

as
\alpha
is
\psi
-preserving. Thus
U^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi
.
Let
U=\sum_{i}x_{i}\otimes a_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(H)\otimes A
, so that
\sum_{i}\Lambda(x_{i}^{*})\otimes a_{i}^{*}(\xi)=U^{*}(\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi)=\Lambda(1)\otimes\xi
. It is always possible to choose
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K)

in such a way that there is an involution
j:K\rightarrow K
with
R(a)=ja^{*}j
for
a\in A
.
(For example, let
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}(K)
be the universal representation.) Then

U^{\top\otimes R}(\overline{\Lambda(1)}\otimes\xi)=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\overline{\Lambda(1)}\otimes R(a_{i})(\xi)=\sum_{i}\overline{\Lambda(x_{i}^{*})}\otimes ja_{i}^{*}j(\xi)=\overline{\Lambda(1)}\otimes\xi.

It hence follows that, continuing the calculation from (
5
),

\displaystyle\alpha_{U}(A_{G})(\tilde{\Lambda}(1)\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=(fw^{*}\otimes 1)(\Lambda(1)\otimes\overline{\Lambda(1)}\otimes\xi)=\tilde{\Lambda}(A_{G})\otimes\xi=(A_{G}\otimes 1)(\tilde{\Lambda}(1)\otimes\xi).

As
\alpha_{U}(A_{G})\in\mathcal{B}(H)\otimes A
, and
\tilde{\Lambda}:\mathcal{B}(H)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(H))
is injective,
and is the map
x\mapsto x\tilde{\Lambda}(1)
, the previous calculation shows that
\alpha_{U}(A_{G})=A_{G}\otimes 1
. This is equivalent to
U(A_{G}\otimes 1)U^{*}=A_{G}\otimes 1
, that is, that
U
and
A_{G}

commute. We have hence shown that (
2
) implies (
1
).

Conversely, if
A_{G}
and
U
commute, then
\alpha_{U}(A_{G})=A_{G}\otimes 1
. As
\alpha_{U}
extends
\alpha

in the sense that
\alpha_{U}(b)=\alpha(b)
for
b\in B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
, we see that

\alpha_{U}(aA_{G}b)=\alpha(a)\alpha_{U}(A_{G})\alpha(b)\in(B\otimes A)(A_{G}\otimes 1)(B\otimes A)=S_{0}^{\prime}\otimes A\qquad(a,b\in B),

and so
\alpha_{U}(S_{0}^{\prime})\subseteq S_{0}^{\prime}\otimes A
, showing that (
1
) implies
(
2
).
∎

Given the discussion before, we might have hoped instead to find that
UA_{G}=A_{G}U
was equivalent to

\alpha(\kappa(S))\subseteq\kappa(S)\otimes A
, but it just seems that this is not so. However,
working with
S_{0}
and
\kappa(S_{0})
instead of
S
and
\kappa(S)
is not so unnatural in light of
Remark
5.35
, because
S_{0}
is exactly the “tracial” quantum graph which arises from

S
using Theorem
5.29
. The dependence on the state
\psi
arises from the
fact that we always assume that
(A,\Delta)
coacts on
B
in a way which preserves
\psi
.

In the tracial case we have a more direct result.

Corollary 9.19
.

Let
\psi
be a trace, and let
A_{G}
correspond to the
B^{\prime}
-operator bimodule
S
. Then
\alpha

acts on
A_{G}
if and only if
\alpha_{U}^{\operatorname{op}}
leaves
S
invariant.

In these final two results, we seem to be working with
\alpha_{U}
and
S_{0}^{\prime}
(or equivalently
\alpha_{U}^{\operatorname{op}}
on
S
) where of course
S_{0}^{\prime}
is a
B
-bimodule, not a
B^{\prime}
-bimodule. However, this appears to simply be an artifact of having fixed our Hilbert space as
L^{2}(B)
, so that
B
becomes anti-isomorphic to
B^{\prime}
.

We leave for future work an investigation of whether there is a quantum group analogue of the ideas
discussed at the end of Section
8
. That is, if we have a coaction of
(A,\Delta)

on
\mathcal{B}(H)
, and
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
with a quantum graph
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
, is it possible to
place conditions on the coaction so as we obtain a coaction on
S
(whatever this might mean, at this level
of generality). For more on coactions on
\mathcal{B}(H)
see, for example,
[
20
, Section 3]
.
